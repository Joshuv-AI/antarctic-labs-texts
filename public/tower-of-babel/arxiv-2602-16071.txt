Abstract
We demonstrate that a ubiquitous feature of network games, bilateral strategic interactions, is equivalent to having player utilities that are additively separable across opponents. We distinguish two formal notions of bilateral strategic interactions. Opponent independence means that player i i ’s preferences over opponent j j ’s action do not depend on what other opponents do. Strategic independence means that how opponent j j ’s choice influences i i ’s preference between any two actions does not depend on what other opponents do. If i i ’s preferences jointly satisfy both conditions, then we can represent her preferences over strategy profiles using an additively separable utility. If i i ’s preferences satisfy only strategic independence, then we can still represent her preferences over just her own actions using an additively separable utility. Common utilities based on a linear aggregate of opponent actions satisfy strategic independence and are therefore strategically equivalent to additively separable utilities—in fact, we can assume a utility that is linear in opponent actions.

Introduction

For tractability, network games typically employ simple utility functions. Most common is the linear-quadratic form that
Ballester et al. 2006
popularized: we have actions
S_{i}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
for each player
i

u_{i}(\mathbf{s})=b_{i}s_{i}+s_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}s_{j}-\frac{s_{i}^{2}}{2}

(1)

for constants
b_{i}
and

g_{ij}
positive or negative respectively, as well as to evaluate targeted interventions. Nevertheless, we lack a firm grasp on what substantive restrictions these preferences entail. What are we really assuming, and how might we judge whether real preferences are consistent with (
1

As a starting point, we identify a fundamental property of the most common preferences in network games: strategic interactions are bilateral. Roughly speaking, this means that how
j
’s action influences player
i
is independent of what some other opponent
k
does. We distinguish two ways to formalize this idea. Player
i
’s preferences satisfy

if whenever
u_{i}(s_{i},s_{j},s^{\prime}_{-ij})\geq u_{i}(s_{i},s_{j},s_{-ij})
for some action

u_{i}(s_{i},s^{\prime}_{j},s^{\prime}_{-ij})\geq u_{i}(s_{i},s^{\prime}_{j},s_{-ij})
for any other action

i
has a fixed preference ranking over the actions of her other opponents, no matter what
j
does. Her preferences satisfy
strategic independence
if we can never have the following four comparisons with at least one strict:

u_{i}(s_{i},s_{j},s_{-ij})\geq u_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},s_{j},s_{-ij}),\quad u_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},s^{\prime}_{j},s_{-ij})\geq u_{i}(s_{i},s^{\prime}_{j},s_{-ij}),

u_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},s_{j},s^{\prime}_{-ij})\geq u_{i}(s_{i},s_{j},s^{\prime}_{-ij}),\quad u_{i}(s_{i},s^{\prime}_{j},s^{\prime}_{-ij})\geq u_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},s^{\prime}_{j},s^{\prime}_{-ij}).

Intuitively, this means that whenever
j

to

and

to

i

s^{\prime}_{i}

s_{j}
to
s^{\prime}_{j}

i
’s preference to flip in favor of
s_{i}

These natural properties correspond to utilities that are separable across opponents. Under mild technical restrictions, if player
i
’s preferences jointly satisfy opponent and strategic independence, then we can represent these preferences via a utility

u_{i}(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}(s_{i},s_{j})

(2)

As a next step, we deduce the implications of further interpretable preference restrictions. With real-valued actions, we say that preferences feature a
constant rate of substitution

To better understand this finding, suppose we have a game in which

Given an arbitrary

\sum_{j\in G_{i}}g_{ij}s_{j}>e^{s^{\prime}_{i}+s_{i}-b_{i}}

\tilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{s})=-e^{s_{i}}-e^{b_{i}-s_{i}}\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}s_{j},

which takes the form (
4

\tilde{u}_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},s_{-i})-\tilde{u}_{i}(s_{i},s_{-i})

\sum_{j\in G_{i}}g_{ij}s_{j}>e^{s^{\prime}_{i}+s_{i}-b_{i}}

and

4

While the utility
\tilde{u}_{i}
captures the same strategic incentives as
u_{i}

\tilde{u}_{i}

i
prefers that opponents take lower actions. Our result thus justifies restricting attention to utilities (
4

In a final section, we do away with technical restrictions and characterize precisely when a player’s preferences are strategically separable. We show that preferences are strategically separable if, and only if, they do not admit a
balanced sequence
. Take any two sequences of strategy profiles
\{r^{1},r^{2},...,r^{m}\}
and
\{t^{1},t^{2},...,t^{m}\}
such that
r^{k}
and
t^{k}
differ only in player
i
’s action, and for each opponent
j
, each pair of actions
(s_{i},s_{j})
appears the same number of times in both sequences. If we can represent
i
’s preferences with a utility of the form (
5
), then we must have

\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{i}(r^{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{i}(t^{k}).

Player
i
therefore cannot prefer
r^{k}
to
t^{k}
for each pair in the sequence, unless she is always indifferent. A balanced sequence is precisely a sequence of profile pairs that violates this requirement. In the main text, we use a variant of Farkas’ lemma to prove this result for finite games. In an appendix, we use logical compactness to extend this to the general case. Relating this to our earlier findings, note that strategic independence is equivalent to assuming that preferences admit no balanced sequence of length
4
. We show that in the general case, one may in fact need to check arbitrarily long sequences to ensure that we can find a separable utility representation. This last result thus sheds light on what our technical assumptions buy us: checking short sequences is enough.

Why should an economist care about such findings? We see three reasons. First, we show that an interpretable assumption—strategic interactions are bilateral—corresponds to a simple and tractable functional form. This gives a partial justification for commonly used utilities and suggests a fruitful direction to generalize. Second, it offers an empirical tool to test whether actual preferences are consistent with common assumptions in network models, and if they are, having an additively separable representation can greatly simplify preference estimation. Finally, our result provides a starting point to examine the implications of other interpretable preference restrictions. For instance, certain types of symmetry, or assumptions on rates of substitution, can help identify other important classes of utility functions.

We take a fundamentally different approach to generalizing the utilities used in network games. We highlight key properties of preferences, opponent and strategic independence, and identify a canonical class of utilities that satisfy them. This makes clear precisely what we assume when we write down a utility of this form. Moreover, it ensures results derived using this quasi-functional form apply to any preferences that satisfy our independence properties. Our exercise is close in spirit to
Sadler 2022
, which uses a robust notion of centrality to identify players who take higher equilibrium actions in any (symmetric) network game of strategic complements. Our analysis represents another step towards robust predictions in network models.

The vast literature on network games includes several models that do not fit within our framework. In particular, coordination games with discrete action sets

While our results are most obviously related to the current literature on network games, our techniques draw more heavily from a much older literature on additively separable utility representations. In the context of consumer theory,

Additively Separable Utilities

Recall that a normal form game
\Gamma=(N,\{S_{i}\}_{i\in N},\{u_{i}\}_{i\in N})
consists of a set of players
N
, a set of actions
S_{i}
for each player
i\in N
, and a utility
u_{i}\,:\,\prod_{i\in N}S_{i}\to\mathbb{R}

S=\prod_{i\in N}S_{i}
for the set of action profiles and
s
for a generic element of
S
. The utility
u_{i}
for player
i
represents an underlying preference order
\succeq_{i}
on
S
. Two utility functions
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
are
equivalent
if they represent the same order
\succeq_{i}
. For each profile
s_{-i}
of opponent actions, we can restrict
\succeq_{i}
to the space
S_{i}\times\{s_{-i}\}
, to obtain an order
\succeq_{i,s_{-i}}
that represents
i
’s preferences over
S_{i}
when opponents play
s_{-i}
. The family of orders
\{\succeq_{i,s_{-i}}\}_{s_{-i}\in S_{-i}}
fully captures
i
’s preferences over her own action choice and are therefore sufficient to describe the strategic properties of
\Gamma
.

Two utility functions

and

are

is equivalent, or strategically equivalent, to

Definition 1
.

Given a normal form game
(N,\{S_{i}\}_{i\in N},\{u_{i}\}_{i\in N})
, we say that player

’s preferences are

is equivalent to some

of the form (

is strategically equivalent to

of the form (
5
).

Equivalence is stronger than strategic equivalence. A utility function
u_{i}(\mathbf{s})
is strategically equivalent to
u_{i}(\mathbf{s})+f(s_{-i})
for any function
f(s_{-i})
, but our choice of

can easily change the corresponding preference order on
S
. Two utilities
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
are equivalent if and only if
u_{i}(\mathbf{s})=f(v_{i}(\mathbf{s}))
for some strictly increasing function

u_{i}(\mathbf{s})=f(v_{i}(\mathbf{s}),s_{-i})

that is strictly increasing in its first argument. When preferences are either separable or strategically separable, the corresponding utility functions are unique up to positive affine transformations, but the specific terms
g_{ij}
in the sum (
5

h(s_{i})
, we can pick any two opponents
j
and
k

\tilde{g}_{ij}(s_{i},s_{j})=g_{ij}(s_{i},s_{j})+h(s_{i})
and
\tilde{g}_{ik}(s_{i},s_{k})=g_{ik}(s_{i},s_{k})-h(s_{i})

In the introduction, we noted a ubiquitous feature of preferences in network games: strategic interactions are bilateral. We formalize this idea through two properties,
opponent independence
and
strategic independence
. Going forward, we take
N=\{0,1,2,...,n\}
for the set of players and focus on representing player
0
’s preferences. We write
s_{0}
for a generic action of this focal player and
s_{+}
for a generic profile of actions for her
n
opponents. We use
s_{-i}
to denote a profile of actions for
opponents
other than
i
, and
s_{A}
to denote a profile of actions for the subset of opponents
A
.

Definition 2
.

A preference order
\succeq_{0}
on
S
satisfies
opponent independence
if

(s_{0},s_{i},s^{\prime}_{-i})\succeq_{0}(s_{0},s_{i},s_{-i})\quad\implies\quad(s_{0},s^{\prime}_{i},s^{\prime}_{-i})\succeq_{0}(s_{0},s^{\prime}_{i},s_{-i})

for any opponent
i
, any
s_{i},s^{\prime}_{i}\in S_{i}
, and any
s_{-i},s^{\prime}_{-i}\in S_{-i}
. The corresponding family of orders
\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}
satisfies
strategic independence
if for any actions
s_{0},s^{\prime}_{0}\in S_{0}
, any opponent
i>0
, and any opponent action profiles
s_{+},s^{\prime}_{+}\in S_{+}
, we have

s_{0}\succeq_{s_{+}}s^{\prime}_{0},\quad s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s^{\prime}_{i},s_{-i}}s_{0},\quad s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s_{i},s^{\prime}_{-i}}s_{0}\quad\implies\quad s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s^{\prime}_{+}}s_{0},

with the last comparison being strict if any one of the first three is strict.

Interpreting opponent independence is straightforward: our focal player’s preferences over action profiles for opponents other than
i
cannot depend on what opponent
i
is doing. This is clearly necessary to have an additively separable representation as in (
5
), and it is identical to “coordinate independence” as used in consumer theory to characterize when preferences over consumption bundles are separable across goods. Because opponent independence is an assumption about preferences
over opponent actions
, we can only use this condition in our characterization of separable preferences—it only makes sense if we start from an order
\succeq_{0}
over all of
S
.

Strategic independence is new to our paper, and it concerns how changes in opponent actions affect our focal player’s preferences over her own actions. The essence of strategic independence is that if a given opponent
i
’s switching from
s_{i}
to
s^{\prime}_{i}
can make
s^{\prime}_{0}
more attractive relative to
s_{0}
, then it must always make
s^{\prime}_{0}
relatively more attractive than
s_{0}
, no matter what other opponents are doing. The first two comparisons assert that, when other opponents play
s_{-i}
, switching from
s_{i}
to
s^{\prime}_{i}
flips our focal player’s preference from
s_{0}
to
s^{\prime}_{0}
. If preferences satisfy strategic independence, then there cannot be another profile
s^{\prime}_{-i}
such that the switch from
s_{i}
to
s^{\prime}_{i}
flips our focal player’s preference in the opposite direction.

Our characterization of separable preferences requires the following notion of joint independence, which is somewhat stronger than assuming both opponent and strategic independence separately.
6
6

6

We find it helpful to draw a parallel with differentiability of multivariate functions. Having partial derivatives in each variable does not guarantee the function is differentiable overall because it could still be poorly behaved if we move along a diagonal. Our definition of joint independence analogously contemplates simultaneous changes in own and opponents’ actions.

Definition 3
.

The order
\succeq_{0}
is
jointly independent
if, for any actions
s_{0},s^{\prime}_{0}\in S_{0}
, any two opponents
i,j>0
, and any opponent action profiles
s_{+},s^{\prime}_{+},t_{+},t^{\prime}_{+}\in S_{+}
, we have

(s_{0},s_{+})\succeq_{0}(s^{\prime}_{0},s^{\prime}_{+}),\quad(s^{\prime}_{0},t^{\prime}_{j},s^{\prime}_{-j})\succeq_{0}(s_{0},t_{i},s_{-i}),\quad(s^{\prime}_{0},s^{\prime}_{j},t^{\prime}_{-j})\succeq_{0}(s_{0},s_{i},t_{-i})

\implies\quad(s^{\prime}_{0},t^{\prime}_{+})\succeq_{0}(s_{0},t_{+}),

with the last comparison being strict if any one of the first three is strict.

Joint independence implies both opponent independence and strategic independence as special cases. Opponent independence is the special case in which
s_{0}=s^{\prime}_{0}
,
i=j
,
s^{\prime}_{i}=s_{i}
,
t^{\prime}_{i}=t_{i}
, and
s_{-i}=s^{\prime}_{-i}
while strategic independence is the special case in which
t_{+}=t_{+}^{\prime}
,
s_{+}=s_{+}^{\prime}
and
i=j
. Joint independence imposes additional restrictions if we change both the focal player’s action and opponent actions simultaneously.

We present our main results in Sections
3
and
4
. The first result shows that, under mild technical restrictions, the preference order
\succeq_{0}
on
S
is separable if and only if it satisfies joint independence. The second result shows that, under stronger conditions, the family of orders
\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}
on
S_{0}
is strategically separable if and only if it satisfies strategic independence. In Section
6
, we discuss the importance of our technical assumptions, and we provide an alternative characterization of strategic separability without them.

3
Separable Preferences

Given an order
\succeq_{0}
on a space of strategy profiles
S
, when can we represent it using an additively separable utility (
5
)? Under mild technical restrictions, the answer is precisely when
\succeq_{0}
jointly satisfies opponent and strategic independence. We divide our conditions into two formal assumptions. The first highlights conditions that are indispensable, while the second simplifies our argument—at the end of the section, we discuss how to relax it. Fixing an action
s_{0}
, we say that opponent
i
is
essential at

s_{0}
if
(s_{0},s^{\prime}_{i},s_{-i})\succ_{0}(s_{0},s_{i},s_{-i})
for some
s_{i},s^{\prime}_{i}\in S_{i}
and
s_{-i}\in S_{-i}
.

Assumption 1
.

Each action set
S_{i}
is a connected topological space, the preference order
\succeq_{0}
on
S
is continuous. There exists
s_{0}^{*}
with at least two essential opponents.

The most meaningful restriction here is that preferences are continuous. Note that if the last part of Assumption
1
fails, then the utility is trivially separable across opponents because our focal player cares about at most one opponent. The reason we exclude this case is because without two essential opponents, the utility representation is no longer cardinal—any monotone transformation remains separable across opponents. Our second assumption imposes an additional richness condition.

Assumption 2
.

For some
s_{0}^{*}
with at least two essential opponents, we have that for any profile
(s_{0},s_{+})
we can find
s^{*}_{+}\in S_{+}
such that
(s_{0},s_{+})\simeq_{0}(s_{0}^{*},s^{*}_{+})
.

Assumption
2
says that there exists an
s_{0}^{*}
such that, for any profile
s

s_{+}^{*}
to make our focal player indifferent between
s
and
(s_{0}^{*},s_{+}^{*})
. While not necessary for our conclusion, this assumption allows a more transparent argument, and we discuss at the end of this section how to extend beyond this case. The standard linear-quadratic utility

u(s)=bs_{0}-\frac{s_{0}^{2}}{2}+s_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}s_{i}

satisfies this assumption as long as either i) the action of at least one essential opponent can range over all of
\mathbb{R}
, or ii) actions are unbounded, and at least one weight
\gamma_{i}
is positive and another is negative.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold for action sets
\{S_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
and the preference order
\succeq_{0}
. The order
\succeq_{0}
is separable if and only if it satisfies joint independence. Moreover, the representation of
\succeq_{0}
via a utility of the form

u(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0},s_{i})

is unique up to a positive affine transformation.

We present the full proof of Theorem
1
in Section
3.2
. The first step is a straightforward application of an old result in consumer theory. Fixing an action
s_{0}
for our focal player, earlier work gives us an additively separable utility that represents preferences over opponent profiles
S_{+}
. We state the relevant result as a lemma, and provide references, in Section
3.1
. The novel part of our proof shows that, using joint independence, we can merge the utilities we obtain for each fixed
s_{0}
into a single utility that covers all of
S_{0}
.

3.1
Proof Preliminaries

Suppose
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}\times...\times X_{n}
—classically, we would have in mind the space of bundles involving
n
different commodities. A binary relation
\succeq
on
X
is
coordinate independent
if for any
i
we have

(x_{i},x_{-i})\succeq(x_{i},y_{-i})\quad\iff\quad(y_{i},x_{-i})\succeq(y_{i},y_{-i})

for any two
x_{i},y_{i}\in X_{i}
. Coordinate
i
is
essential
if
(x_{i},x_{-i})\succ(y_{i},x_{-i})
for some
x_{i},y_{i},x_{-i}
. Various authors going back to
Debreu 1959
have identified conditions under which a coordinate independent preference order over bundles admits an additively separable utility representation:

u(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}(x_{i}).

Technical conditions on the sets
\{X_{i}\}
vary, but most results of this sort require a continuous preference order and at least three essential coordinates. We rely on a version from
Wakker 1989
, which requires that each
X_{i}
is a connected topological space and
\succeq
is a continuous weak order on
X
.

With only two essential coordinates, we need a further condition. The weakest one of which we are aware is the
hexagon condition
that
Wakker 1989
identifies. The binary relation
\succeq
on
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}
satisfies the hexagon condition if

(x_{1},x_{2})\simeq(y_{1},y_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad(z_{1},y_{2})\simeq(y_{1},x_{2})\simeq(x_{1},z_{2})\quad\implies\quad(y_{1},z_{2})\simeq(z_{1},x_{2}).

Given an additively separable utility, the hypotheses in the hexagon condition state that

v_{1}(x_{1})+v_{2}(x_{2})=v_{1}(y_{1})+v_{2}(y_{2}),\quad\text{and}

v_{1}(z_{1})+v_{2}(y_{2})=v_{1}(y_{1})+v_{2}(x_{2})=v_{1}(x_{1})+v_{2}(z_{2})

Adding the first and last two quantities in the second line respectively gives

v_{1}(z_{1})+v_{2}(x_{2})+v_{1}(y_{1})+v_{2}(y_{2})=v_{1}(y_{1})+v_{2}(z_{2})+v_{1}(x_{1})+v_{2}(x_{2}).

Subtracting
v_{1}(y_{1})+v_{2}(y_{2})
from the first, and
v_{1}(x_{1})+v_{2}(x_{2})
from the second, shows that

v_{1}(z_{1})+v_{2}(x_{2})=v_{1}(y_{1})+v_{2}(z_{2}).

Hence, the hexagon condition is necessary to have an additively separable representation. With three essential coordinates, one can derive it from coordinate independence, but with only two, we must assume it separately. Note that in our model, opponent independence is exactly coordinate independence on
\{s_{0}\}\times S_{+}
for each action
s_{0}\in S_{0}
, and joint independence implies the hexagon condition on
\{s_{0}\}\times S_{+}
for each action
s_{0}\in S_{0}
.

For ease of reference, we state the full result from
Wakker 1989
here.

Lemma 1

(Wakker, 1989, Theorem III.4.1)
.

Suppose
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}\times...\times X_{n}
, with
n\geq 3
, is a connected topological space, and
\succeq
is a continuous weak order on
X
. If
\succeq
is coordinate independent, and at least three coordinates are essential, then there exist real-valued functions
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that
\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y}
if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}(x_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}(y_{i}).

Moreover, the functions
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are unique up to a positive affine transformation. In particular, if
\{\tilde{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is another such representation, then

\tilde{v}_{i}(x_{i})=\lambda v_{i}(x_{i})+c_{i},\quad i=1,2,...,n

for some
\{c_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\lambda>0
. With two essential coordinates, the same conclusion holds if
\succeq
additionally satisfies the hexagon condition.

We invoke Lemma
1
twice in our proof. The first time, we apply it to obtain an additively separable utility on
\{s_{0}\}\times S_{+}
. We then use these utilities to construct a
difference order
on all of
S
. A difference order is an order on
pairs
of strategy profiles, with the interpretation that
st\succeq s^{\prime}t^{\prime}
if

u(s)-u(t)\geq u(s^{\prime})-u(t^{\prime}).

It is well-known that a difference order satisfying natural regularity conditions yields a
cardinal
utility representation: if two utility functions correspond to the same difference order, then one is a positive affine transformation of the other
(
Köbberling 2006
, See, for instance, )
.

In fact, the result for difference orders is a special case of Lemma
1
with
n=2
. Taking
X_{1}=X_{2}
, if we have

(x,x)\simeq(y,y)

for every
x,y\in X_{1}
, then it is easy to show that

v_{2}(x)=-v_{1}(x)+c

for some constant
c
. The function
v_{1}
then gives a cardinal representation for a preference order on
X_{1}
that ranks
x
above
y
if

(x,z)\succeq(y,z)

for any
z\in X_{1}
. Our second use of Lemma
1
takes this form and derives our full representation from the difference order on
S
. Crucially, the difference order agrees with the additively separable utility we constructed on
\{s_{0}\}\times S_{+}
for each action
s_{0}\in S_{0}
. Since these utilities are unique up to scale and location, we know that the full representation is additively separable for each
s_{0}
.

3.2
Proof of Theorem
1

Joint independence is clearly necessary, so we prove only sufficiency. Since
\succeq_{0}
satisfies opponent independence and the hexagon condition on
\{s_{0}\}\times S_{+}
for each
s_{0}\in S_{0}
, we can apply Lemma
1
on each restricted space
\{s_{0}\}\times S_{+}
to obtain an additively separable utility representation holding
s_{0}
fixed. That is, for each
s_{0}\in S_{0}
, we can find functions
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0},s_{i})

(6)

represents
\succeq_{0}
restricted to
\{s_{0}\}\times S_{+}
. By Assumption
1
, there exists
s_{0}^{*}
with at least two essential opponents, and we know the representation is cardinal on
\{s_{0}^{*}\}\times S_{+}
. We now construct a difference order
\succeq
on
S
in four steps:

(a)

Define
ss\simeq tt
for every
s,t\in S
—this must hold in any difference order.

(b)

Define
st\succeq s^{\prime}t
and
ts^{\prime}\succeq ts
whenever
s\succeq_{0}s^{\prime}
—this ensures coordinate independence of the difference order as a relation on
S\times S
and that the difference order represents the preferences
\succeq_{0}
.

(c)

For each
s_{0}
with at least two essential opponents, define
(s_{0},s_{+})(s_{0},t_{+})\succeq(s_{0},s^{\prime}_{+})(s_{0},t^{\prime}_{+})
whenever

\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0},s_{i})-g_{i}(s_{0},t_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0},s^{\prime}_{i})-g_{i}(s_{0},t^{\prime}_{i}).

This ensures that
\succeq
agrees with the additively separable representation on the restriction to
\{s_{0}\}\times S_{+}
for each
s_{0}
.

(d)

Take the transitive closure.

We show shortly that i) the order
\succeq
is total on
S\times S
, ii) it represents the preferences
\succeq_{0}
in the sense that
st\succeq s^{\prime}t
if and only if
s\succeq_{0}s^{\prime}
, and iii) it is continuous and satisfies the hexagon condition. Given these properties, we can apply Lemma
1
a second time to obtain continuous functions
v_{1}(s)
and
v_{2}(s)
such that

st\succeq s^{\prime}t^{\prime}\quad\iff\quad v_{1}(s)+v_{2}(t)\geq v_{1}(s^{\prime})+v_{2}(t^{\prime}).

Part (a) of the construction implies
v_{2}(s)=-v_{1}(s)+c
for some constant
c
. Since the representation of a difference order is unique up to positive affine transformations, we know from part (c) that

v_{1}(s_{0},s_{+})=c_{1}(s_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0},s_{i})+c_{2}(s_{0})

for some functions
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
whenever there are at least two essential opponents at
s_{0}
—if there are
0
or
1
essential opponents, the utility trivially takes this form. Absorbing
c_{2}
into one of the terms in the sum yields our desired representation.

It remains to establish properties i), ii), and iii) of the difference order. Addressing property i) first, given any four profiles
s,t,s^{\prime},t^{\prime}
, we first use Assumption
1
and find
s_{0}^{*}
with at least two essential opponents. By Assumption
2
we can find
\hat{s}_{+}
,
\hat{t}_{+}
,
\hat{s}^{\prime}_{+}
, and
\hat{t}^{\prime}_{+}
such that

s\simeq_{0}(s_{0}^{*},\hat{s}_{+}),\quad t\simeq_{0}(s_{0}^{*},\hat{t}_{+}),\quad s^{\prime}\simeq_{0}(s_{0}^{*},\hat{s}^{\prime}_{+}),\quad\text{and}\quad t^{\prime}\simeq_{0}(s_{0}^{*},\hat{t}^{\prime}_{+}).

Using part (b) of the construction and transitivity, we now have

st\simeq s(s_{0}^{*},\hat{t}_{+})\simeq(s_{0}^{*},\hat{s}_{+})(s_{0}^{*},\hat{t}_{+}),\quad s^{\prime}t^{\prime}\simeq s^{\prime}(s_{0}^{*},\hat{t}^{\prime}_{+})\simeq(s_{0}^{*},\hat{s}^{\prime}_{+})(s_{0}^{*},\hat{t}^{\prime}_{+}).

Part (c) yields a comparison between
(s_{0}^{*},\hat{s}_{+})(s_{0}^{*},\hat{t}_{+})
and
(s_{0}^{*},\hat{s}^{\prime}_{+})(s_{0}^{*},\hat{t}^{\prime}_{+})
, and transitivity implies the same comparison between
st
and
s^{\prime}t^{\prime}
.

For part ii), note that step (b) in the construction tells us that
st\succeq s^{\prime}t
whenever
s\succeq_{0}s^{\prime}
—we just need to check that the comparisons we added in part (c) did not flatten strict comparisons of this form. Equivalently, we need to show that the comparisons we derived in part i) do not depend on our choice of
s_{0}^{*}
. Fix
s_{0}^{*}
given by Assumption
2
, and consider any other action
t_{0}\in S_{0}
with at least three essential opponents. Fix opponent profiles
s_{+},t_{+},s^{\prime}_{+},t^{\prime}_{+}
such that

(s_{0}^{*},s^{\prime}_{+})\simeq_{0}(t_{0},t^{\prime}_{+})\succ_{0}(s_{0}^{*},s_{+})\simeq_{0}(t_{0},t_{+}).

Using positive affine transformations, we can rescale and translate the functions
\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
so that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0}^{*},s_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(t_{0},t_{i})=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0}^{*},s^{\prime}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(t_{0},t^{\prime}_{i})=1.

We need to show that
(s_{0}^{*},s^{\prime\prime}_{+})\simeq_{0}(t_{0},t^{\prime\prime}_{+})
whenever

\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0}^{*},s^{\prime\prime}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(t_{0},t^{\prime\prime}_{i}),

which implies that we get the same comparisons in part i) if we use
t_{0}
instead of
s_{0}^{*}
.

Since the utility (
6
) represents
\succeq_{0}
when restricted to either
\{s_{0}^{*}\}\times S_{+}
or
\{t_{0}\}\times S_{+}
, we know that our focal player is indifferent if either both sums are
0
or both sums are
1
. Moreover, for any other
x\in\mathbb{R}
, if there exist profiles
(s_{0}^{*},\hat{s}_{+})\simeq_{0}(t_{0},\hat{t}_{+})
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0}^{*},\hat{s}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(t_{0},\hat{t}_{i})=x,

then our focal player is always indifferent when both sums are equal to
x
. Let
X
denote the set of

such that
(s_{0}^{*},s^{\prime\prime}_{+})\simeq_{0}(t_{0}^{*},t^{\prime\prime}_{+})
whenever

\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0}^{*},s^{\prime\prime}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(t_{0},t^{\prime\prime}_{i})=x.

We first show that
[0,1]\in X
. We then show that
X
contains the entire range of our utility on
\{s_{0}^{*}\}\times S_{+}

Focusing first on
[0,1]

for each

includes the entire interval. Since there are at least three essential opponents at

g_{1}(s_{0}^{*},s^{\prime}_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0}^{*},s_{i})=g_{1}(s_{0}^{*},s_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0}^{*},s^{\prime}_{i})=\frac{1}{2}.

Without loss, we are assuming that opponent
1

s_{0}^{*}
and
t_{0}

Suppose that
(s_{0}^{*},s_{1},s^{\prime}_{-1})\succ_{0}(t_{0},t_{1},t^{\prime}_{-1})

3.3
Discussion

Assumption
2
is the most restrictive condition we use in our proof, but as already noted, we can substantially weaken it. Suppose instead that we can cover
S
using a collection of open subsets
\{U_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
that each individually satisfy Assumption
2
. We can then apply Theorem
1
to each subset
U_{k}
. Since the representation is unique up to positive affine transformations, when two subsets
U_{k}
and
U_{k^{\prime}}
overlap, we can translate and rescale to ensure that the utilities match. Inducting, we obtain an additively separable utility over all of
S
. Nevertheless, we do need some assumption along these lines. The following example demonstrates how separability can fail otherwise.

Example 1
.

Suppose each player chooses a strictly positive, real-valued action, and our focal player strictly prefers
(s_{0},s_{+})
to
(s^{\prime}_{0},s^{\prime}_{+})
if either:

(a)

s_{0}\geq\frac{\pi}{2}
and
s^{\prime}_{0}<\frac{\pi}{2}
, or

(b)

s_{0},s^{\prime}_{0}\geq\frac{\pi}{2}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}>\sum_{i=1}^{n}s^{\prime}_{i}
, or

(c)

s_{0},s^{\prime}_{0}<\frac{\pi}{2}
and
\tan(s_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}>\tan(s^{\prime}_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{n}s^{\prime}_{i}
.

One can readily verify that Assumption
1
holds, as does joint independence. Indeed from the specification above, we have additively separable representations on two disjoint domains, taking
u_{0}(\mathbf{s})=\tan(s_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
on
(0,\frac{\pi}{2})\times S_{+}
and
u_{0}(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
on
[\frac{\pi}{2},\infty)\times S_{+}
. Nevertheless, no additively separable utility works on the entire set of strategy profiles
S
.

To see why, consider the restriction to
\{1,\frac{\pi}{2}\}
, allowing our focal player just two actions. Our focal player strictly prefers
(\frac{\pi}{2},s_{+})
over
(1,s^{\prime}_{+})
for any pair of opponent profiles, and holding
s_{0}
fixed, she prefers a profile with a higher sum of opponent actions. Restricted to either
\{1\}\times S_{+}
or
\{\frac{\pi}{2}\}\times S_{+}
, Lemma
1
implies that any additively separable representation is a positive affine transformation of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
. If we had an additively separable representation over the whole domain, its restrictions to these two regions would take the form

\beta_{1}+\alpha_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt\beta_{\pi/2}+\alpha_{\pi/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}

for constants
\beta_{1},\beta_{\pi/2}
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{\pi/2}>0
. However, since sums are unbounded, given any such constants, we can find
s_{+}
and
s^{\prime}_{+}
such that

\beta_{1}+\alpha_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}>\beta_{\pi/2}+\alpha_{\pi/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s^{\prime}_{i},

which violates the preference for
s_{0}=\frac{\pi}{2}
over
s_{0}=1
.

4
Strategically Separable Preferences

Many preferences that appear in network games are not separable. For instance, a popular way to generalize the classic linear-quadratic utility assumes that preferences depend on a linear aggregate of neighbors’ actions—actions are real-valued, and player
i
’s payoff is

u_{i}(\mathbf{s})=f(s_{i})+\gamma(s_{i})\psi\left(\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}s_{j}\right)

The proof of Theorem

In the first step, since our primitive does not include preferences over opponent actions, we cannot immediately apply Lemma

In the next subsection, we detail technical assumptions. We then state our result and prove a lemma that constitutes the first step of our outline—we relegate the second step to an appendix. At the end of the section, we discuss prospects for weakening our technical assumptions.

4.1
Technical Assumptions

We divide our assumptions into two rough categories: those that involve topological properties of action sets and preferences, and those that require sufficiently “rich” action sets. We begin with topological properties.

Joint solvability is a more substantive condition. This says that, however the change from

to

affects our focal player’s preferences between three actions, we can achieve the same effect on all three comparisons by changing a different opponent’s action

to some

(s_{i},s^{\prime}_{j})

for each pair of actions

4.2
The Result

Given our technical assumptions on action sets and preferences, strategic independence is precisely the condition needed to ensure strategic separability.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumption
3
holds for action sets
\{S_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
and the family of preference orders
\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}
. The family is strategically separable if and only if it satisfies strategic independence. Moreover, the representation of
\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}
via a utility of the form

u(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{0},s_{i})

is unique up to a positive affine transformation.

As before, a utility of the form (
5
) clearly implies strategic independence, so we prove only the “if” direction. Mirroring our proof of Theorem
1
, we construct a difference order on
S
that is consistent with the preferences
\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}
on
S_{0}
and with a coordinate independent order on
S_{+}
for each fixed
s_{0}
. Applying Lemma
1
then gives our required representation. The following lemma provides the first step in our argument. Unlike in the last section, we cannot immediately obtain an additively separable utility for each fixed
s_{0}
because we do not have preferences over opponent actions. Instead, for each pair of actions
s_{0}
and
s^{\prime}_{0}

Lemma 2
.

Suppose each action set
S_{i}
is a connected topological space, the family of orders
\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}
is jointly continuous, and at least three opponents are completely essential. If the family satisfies strategic independence, then for any pair of actions
s_{0},s^{\prime}_{0}\in S_{0}

\{g_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that
s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s_{+}}s_{0}
if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{i})\geq 0.

Moreover, these functions are unique up to a positive rescaling.

Proof.

We fix two actions
s_{0}
and
s^{\prime}_{0}
and define an order
\succeq
on
S_{+}
. Our interpretation of
\succeq
is that
s^{\prime}_{+}\succeq s_{+}
if
s^{\prime}_{0}
is relatively more attractive to our focal player when opponents play
s^{\prime}_{+}
instead of
s_{+}
.

Formally, for each opponent
i
and any two profiles
s_{-i},s^{\prime}_{-i}\in S_{-i}

(s_{i},s^{\prime}_{-i})\succeq(s_{i},s_{-i})
for

s^{*}_{i}

s_{0}\succeq_{(s^{*}_{i},s_{-i})}s^{\prime}_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{(s^{*}_{i},s^{\prime}_{-i})}s_{0}.

That is, if switching from
s_{-i}
to
s^{\prime}_{-i}
can ever flip our focal player’s preference in favor of
s^{\prime}_{0}
, we define
(s_{i},s^{\prime}_{-i})\succeq(s_{i},s_{-i})
for all
s_{i}
—note the order is coordinate independent by construction.

We need to establish three properties: i) after taking the transitive closure we have a total order on
S_{+}

s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s_{+}}s_{0}

s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s^{\prime}_{+}}(\succ_{s^{\prime}_{+}})s_{0}
for every

s^{\prime}_{+}\succeq s_{+}\quad\iff\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{i}).

Moreover, these functions are unique up to a positive affine transformation. Property iii) implies that we can translate the functions
\{g_{i}\}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}(s_{i})=0
whenever
s^{\prime}_{0}\simeq_{s_{+}}s_{0}
, and we have
s^{\prime}_{0}\succ_{s_{+}}(\prec_{s_{+}})s_{0}
whenever the sum is positive (negative). This gives the desired representation of our focal player’s preference between
s^{\prime}_{0}
and
s_{0}

We now establish properties i), ii), and iii). For property i), note that since each opponent
i
is completely essential, the comparisons we explicitly added include comparisons between any
s_{+}
and
s^{\prime}_{+}
that share at least one coordinate. These comparisons are also already transitive on each

(s_{i},s^{\prime\prime}_{-i})\succeq(s_{i},s^{\prime}_{-i})\succeq(s_{i},s_{-i})

s_{0}\succeq_{s^{*}_{i},s_{-i}}s^{\prime}_{0},\quad s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s^{*}_{i},s^{\prime}_{-i}}s_{0},\quad s_{0}\succeq_{s^{**}_{i},s^{\prime}_{-i}}s^{\prime}_{0},\quad\text{and}\quad s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s^{**}_{i},s^{\prime\prime}_{-i}}s_{0}.

Given the first three comparisons above, strategic independence implies that
s_{0}\succeq_{s^{**}_{i},s_{-i}}s^{\prime}_{0}
, and this together with the last comparison reveals that
s^{\prime\prime}_{-i}\succeq s_{-i}
.

To get a comparison between arbitrary
s_{+}
and
s^{\prime}_{+}
, we can find
(s_{1},s_{2}^{*},s^{\prime}_{-12})\simeq s^{\prime}_{+}

s_{+}

(s_{1},s_{2}^{*},s^{\prime}_{-12})

and
s^{\prime}_{+}

\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}

For property iii), suppose we have
s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s_{+}}s_{0}

s^{\prime}_{0}\prec_{s^{\prime}_{+}}s_{0}

4.3
Local Aggregates

Recall our example from the introduction:

u_{i}(s)=b_{i}s_{i}-s_{i}^{2}+s_{i}\ln\left(\sum_{j\in G_{i}}g_{ij}s_{j}\right).

A key feature is that player
i

reduces her action by
1

\frac{g_{ij}}{g_{ik}}
to restore

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
(N,\{S_{i}\}_{i\in N},\{u_{i}\}_{i\in N})
is a normal form game with real-valued actions, and player

u_{i}(s)=u\left(s_{i},\sum_{j\neq i}f_{j}(s_{j})\right),

in which each

is a monotone function of

has no weakly dominated actions, and for any pair
s_{i},s^{\prime}_{i}\in S_{i}

u(s_{i},x)\geq u(s^{\prime}_{i},x)
precisely when either

or

f_{j}(s_{j})
ranges over all of
\mathbb{R}
for at least three opponents

, then player

’s preferences are strategically separable.

Proof.

The family of orders
\{\succeq_{s_{-i}}\}_{s_{-i}\in S_{-i}}
is jointly continuous because

is continuous, and it has connected intervals because

’s preference between any

and

depends on whether the aggregate is above or below a threshold. Since
f_{j}(s_{j})
ranges over all of
\mathbb{R}
for at least three opponents

, we have three opponents who are completely essential, and joint solvability holds. The result follows from Theorem
2
.
∎

Corollary
1

5
Constant Rate of Substitution

Our example in the last section has another special feature: the aggregate is linear in opponent actions, and so is the corresponding additively separable utility. Given strategically separable preferences, it turns out that our representation (
5
) is linear in opponent actions precisely when preferences exhibit a
constant rate of substitution
between neighbors’ actions. Throughout this section, we assume all actions are real-valued.

Definition 6
.

Player
i
’s preferences exhibit a
constant rate of substitution
between opponents
j
and
k
if we have
\succeq_{i,s_{-i}}=\succeq_{i,s^{\prime}_{-i}}
whenever
s_{\ell}=s^{\prime}_{\ell}
for
\ell\notin\{j,k\}
and
\delta(s^{\prime}_{j}-s_{j})=s_{k}-s^{\prime}_{k}
for some constant
\delta
. If player
i
’s preferences feature a constant rate of substitution between any two essential opponents, we say that player
i
has
CRS preferences
.

A constant rate of substitution between
j
and
k
means that increasing
j
’s action by
1
has the same effect on
i
’s preferences as increasing
k
’s action by
\delta
. Payoffs that depend on a linear aggregate
\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}s_{j}
clearly exhibit CRS preferences: opponent

is essential if and only if
g_{ij}\neq 0
, and for essential opponents
j
and
k
we can take
\delta=\frac{g_{ij}}{g_{ik}}
in Definition
6
. If CRS preferences are strategically separable, then the additively separable utility is linear in opponent actions: we can represent such preferences via

u_{i}(\mathbf{s})=f(s_{i})+\gamma(s_{i})\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}s_{j}

(8)

for some functions
f
and
\gamma
of player
i
’s action. Because the representation (
8
) is unique up to positive affine transformations, if all players in a network game have strategically separable CRS preferences, this means that the corresponding weighted graph
G=\{g_{ij}\}_{i,j\in N}
is unique up to left multiplication by a positive diagonal matrix.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose actions are real-valued, and player
i
has continuous and strategically separable CRS preferences. Player
i
’s preferences are strategically equivalent to a utility

u_{i}(\mathbf{s})=f(s_{i})+\gamma(s_{i})\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}s_{j}.

Proof.

By assumption, player
i
’s preferences are strategically equivalent to

u_{i}(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}(s_{i},s_{j})

for some functions
\{g_{ij}\}
. Given a constant rate of substitution between
j
and
k
, for any

we can also represent

’s preferences via

u_{i}^{x}(\mathbf{s})=g_{ij}(s_{i},s_{j}-\delta x)+g_{ik}(s_{i},s_{k}+x)+\sum_{\ell\neq i,j,k}g_{i\ell}(s_{i},s_{\ell})

for some constant
\delta
. Since the representation of the difference
u_{i}(s^{\prime}_{i},s_{-i})-u_{i}(s_{i},s_{-i})
is unique up to scaling, we have

\displaystyle g_{ij}(s^{\prime}_{i},s_{j})

Together Theorems

Almost all utilities used in models of network games satisfy these properties and are therefore strategically equivalent to utilities

An important caveat is that strategic equivalence does not justify using utilities of this form for welfare comparisons. We can obtain robust results on equilibria, but other utility specifications may make more sense for welfare analysis.

6
Beyond Strategic Independence

Getting to an additively separable utility using strategic independence alone requires several assumptions on action sets and preferences that may not always hold. In the absence of such restrictions, when are preferences strategically separable? In this section, we provide a tight characterization using the concept of a
balanced sequence
. We should note that the approach taken in this section applies equally well, modulo straightforward modifications, to the case of mere separability. We present results only for the more difficult case of strategic separability to avoid redundancy.

Definition 7
.

Given a family of preference orders
\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}
, a
balanced sequence
is a collection of strategy profile pairs
\{(r^{k},t^{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}\subseteq S\times S
such that

(a)

r^{k}_{+}=t^{k}_{+}
for each
k

(b)

r^{k}_{0}\succeq_{r^{k}_{+}}t^{k}_{0}
for each
k

k

(c)

For each opponent
i

(s_{0},s_{i})
appears the same number of times in the sequence
(r^{1}_{0},r^{1}_{i}),(r^{2}_{0},r^{2}_{i}),...,(r^{m}_{0},r^{m}_{i})
as it does in
(t^{1}_{0},t^{1}_{i}),(t^{2}_{0},t^{2}_{i}),...,(t^{m}_{0},t^{m}_{i})

We refer to
m
as the length of the balanced sequence.

In the simplest case, a balanced sequence involves two actions
s_{0},s^{\prime}_{0}
for the focal player and four pairs of strategy profiles:

s_{0}\succeq_{s_{A},s_{B}}s^{\prime}_{0},\quad s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s^{\prime}_{A},s_{B}}s_{0},\quad s^{\prime}_{0}\succeq_{s_{A},s^{\prime}_{B}}s_{0},\quad s_{0}\succeq_{s^{\prime}_{A},s^{\prime}_{B}}s^{\prime}_{0},

in which
\{A,B\}
is a partition of the opponents, and at least one comparison is strict. Any such sequence is inconsistent with a utility of the form (
5
). To see this, observe that the four comparisons in turn imply that

\sum_{j}g_{0j}(s_{0},s_{j})\geq\sum_{j}g_{0j}(s_{0}^{\prime},s_{j}),

\sum_{j\in A}g_{0j}(s_{0}^{\prime},s_{j}^{\prime})+\sum_{j\in B}g_{0j}(s_{0}^{\prime},s_{j})\geq\sum_{j\in A}g_{0j}(s_{0},s_{j}^{\prime})+\sum_{j\in B}g_{0j}(s_{0},s_{j}),

\sum_{j\in A}g_{0j}(s_{0}^{\prime},s_{j})+\sum_{j\in B}g_{0j}(s_{0}^{\prime},s_{j}^{\prime})\geq\sum_{j\in A}g_{0j}(s_{0},s_{j})+\sum_{j\in B}g_{0j}(s_{0},s_{j}^{\prime}),\quad\text{and}

\sum_{j}g_{0j}(s_{0},s_{j}^{\prime})\geq\sum_{j}g_{0j}(s_{0}^{\prime},s_{j}^{\prime}).

Since each term appears on the left-hand side as often as it does on the right-hand side, and at least one comparison is strict, adding all of these together gives

\displaystyle\sum_{j}g_{0j}(s_{0},s_{j})+

\displaystyle g_{0j}(s_{0},s_{j}^{\prime})+g_{0j}(s_{0}^{\prime},s_{j})+g_{0j}(s_{0}^{\prime},s_{j}^{\prime})

\displaystyle>\sum_{j}g_{0j}(s_{0},s_{j})+g_{0j}(s_{0},s_{j}^{\prime})+g_{0j}(s_{0}^{\prime},s_{j})+g_{0j}(s_{0}^{\prime},s_{j}^{\prime}),

a contradiction. Consequently, no additively separable representation exists.

Ruling out short balanced sequences exactly captures our intuition about bilateral strategic interactions. In fact, strategic independence simply rules out particular balanced sequences of length
4
. Ruling out longer balanced sequences builds on this idea, precluding more complicated strategic dependencies across opponents. Our next result shows that the absence of balanced sequences of any length is a necessary and sufficient condition for strategic separability on finite action sets.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that each
S_{i}
is finite, then
u_{0}
is strategically separable if and only if its induced family of orders
\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}
does not admit a balanced sequence.

Theorem
4
helps us to interpret earlier findings. We can always establish strategic separability by ruling out balanced sequences of any length. Theorem
2
tells us that, with three completely essential opponents and preferences that satisfy joint solvability, we can get away with only checking short sequences. The “only if” direction follows from a similar argument as in our example above. Given a balanced sequence, if we had an additively separable utility
u
, property
(c)
would imply that

\sum_{k=1}^{m}u(r^{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}u(t^{k}),

but property
(b)

\sum_{k=1}^{m}u(r^{k})>\sum_{k=1}^{m}u(t^{k}).

Of course, many network games following

have infinite action sets, so Theorem
4
does not apply directly. Example

\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}

6.2
Discussion

Theorem
4

strategic separability that is valid
without
additional assumptions: a player’s preferences are additively separable exactly when they
admit
no
balanced sequence. The cost of weak assumptions is that we now must rule out arbitrarily long balanced sequences. Long balanced sequences are both challenging to interpret and difficult to check in practice. Two auxiliary results, proved in the appendix, illuminate how costly that criterion can be in finite games.

Proposition 1
.

Let
m:=\prod_{i=0}^{n}|S_{i}|
be the number of pure–strategy profiles. If a
finite preference is
not
strategically separable, then it contains a
balanced sequence whose length does not exceed

L(m)=(m-1)\exp\left(\frac{m-1}{2}\ln(m-1)\right)
.

Proposition
1
tells us that a balanced sequence, if it exists, must appear within an explicit, though exponential, search horizon. Although one might be able to improve the bound, our next result shows that any bound must grow with the number of strategy profiles. However far one looks, there are games with preferences whose first offending sequence is even longer. Hence, while the balanced sequence test is universally valid, it can be exceedingly hard to apply.

Proposition 2
.

For every integer
L\geq 1
there is a finite game
\Gamma
with a

that is

not
strategically separable and yet contains

balanced sequence
of length
\leq L
.

7
Final Remarks

Our analysis identifies two canonical utilities for network games. If strategic interactions are bilateral, players behave as if their preferences follow an additively separable utility

u_{i}(s_{i},s_{-i})=\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}(s_{i},s_{j}).

With real-valued actions, if preferences exhibit a constant rate of substitution between opponents, we can take

u_{i}(s_{i},s_{-i})=f_{i}(s_{i})+\gamma_{i}(s_{i})\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}s_{j}

for some functions
f
and
\gamma
, and constants
\{g_{ij}\}_{j\neq i}
. Results we derive using these functional forms apply to any game in which preferences satisfy these qualitative assumptions. Our findings thus clarify key assumptions that are common across different network games, and indeed they suggest a natural definition for what it means to be a network game. Beyond providing a rationale for specific utilities, our results highlight relatively simple revealed preference conditions to test whether such models are appropriate. Our notion of bilateral strategic interactions exactly corresponds to ruling out a particular class of
4
-cycles in a player’s preferences.

By including “Part I” in our title, we deliberately call attention to unfinished business. In addition to their substance, our results in Section
5
offer a proof of concept that can inform future work. With an additively separable utility in hand, it becomes easier to deduce implications from other qualitative assumptions on preferences. We expect similar techniques can also shed light on the relationship between network games and potential games. Conveniently, many network games studied in the literature are also potential games. This ensures that equilibria exist in pure strategies, and players can realistically learn how to play equilibria from feedback, even if the network is large and complex. In an ongoing project, which we expect will constitute “Part II,” we are working to identify revealed preference conditions implying that a network game has a potential, as well as a canonical form for that potential.

Perhaps a more obvious next task is to derive substantive predictions in network models that do not depend on players having a particular utility function. Who takes higher or lower actions? How do shocks to one player’s incentives propagate through the network? Who should we target for interventions? A popular approach to such questions assumes differentiable utilities and focuses on small shocks, so one can approximate changes using a linear system
(
Dasaratha et al. 2025
, e.g.)
. A complementary approach might leverage our results to better understand responses to larger shocks.

B.1
Proof of Theorem

Recall that Theorem
5
says a family of orders
\{\succeq_{s_{+}}\}_{s_{+}\in S_{+}}
does not admit a balanced sequence if and only if admits a strategically separable lexicographic representation. We prove this in two main steps. First we, use logical compactness together with the result of Theorem
4
to deduce that a strategically separable representation exists, with utilities taking values in some arbitrary linearly ordered Abelian group. We then use Hahn’s embedding theorem to embed this group in to
\mathbb{R}^{M}
for some linearly ordered index set
M
. Several papers have outlined how to use logical compactness to bootstrap finitary models to arbitrary models, including
Fuhrken and Richter 1991
and
Gonczarowski et al. 2025
. We take a similar approach. Logical compactness applies in first-order logic, where one cannot axiomatize the real numbers. As a consequence, we cannot use compactness to generate a real-valued utility. However, linearly ordered Abelian groups can be finitely axiomatized so we can use logical compactness to get a representation in such a set. A common approach is then to impose an Archimedean axiom. Since Holder’s theorem characterizes the reals as the unique Archimedean liniearly ordered group, one can indirectly generate a real-valued representation. Instead, we do not impose such a criterion, but allow for non-Archimedean orders. As a consequence, our representation takes values in a richer space, namely
\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

Neither first-order logic nor the theory of ordered Abelian groups is commonly used in economic theory, so we give a brief primer on both for the reader’s convenience.

B.3
Proof of Proposition
2

In this section, we establish the impossibility of a finite first-order axiomatization of strategic separability. We do this by reducing a similar result from the theory of additive separability to our setting. We show that any finite axiomatization for strategic separability could be used to obtain a finite axiomatization for additive separability in the theory of choice. This was shown to be impossible by
Krantz et al. 1990
. Therefore, no finite axiomatization of strategic separability is possible. We begin by formalizing our setting in first-order logic. Rather than encode preferences as a collection of binary relations
\{\succeq_{i,s_{-i}}\}_{s_{-i}\in S_{-i}}
we can equivalently write them through a single
n+2
-ary relation
R
defined so that

(a,b,s_{-i})\in R\iff a\succ_{i,s_{-i}}b.

This makes possible a simpler expression of our setting in first-order logic, but it introduces two complications. First, a
n+2
-ary relation on a set
X
is a subset of
X^{n+2}
. However, in our case the
s_{i}
belong to potentially different sets. We will overcome this issue by letting
X=\cup S_{i}
and insisting that an element of
X^{n+2}
is in
R
only if its coordinates are in the correct subsets. Second, we need to write strategic separability in terms of
R
. We say that
R
is
strategically separable
if there are functions

g_{ij}
such that

(a,b,s_{-i})\in R\iff\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}(a,s_{j})\geq\sum_{j\neq i}g_{ij}(b,s_{j}).

Going forward, we will use the notation
(a,b,s_{-i})\in R
as interchangeable with
R(a,b,s_{-i})
.

We fix
n
and index the players
0,1,2,\dots,n
. We will be concerned with the utility of the player
0
. We have a vocabulary consisting of
n+1
unary relations
S_{i}
for
i=0,1,\dots,n
and one
n+2
-ary relation
R
. The
S_{i}
are used to describe which actions belong to which player and
R
is player
0
’s preferences over their own actions, conditional on the actions of the other players. Intuitively, we interpret
R(a,b,s_{1},\dots,s_{n})
to mean that
0
prefers to play action
a
to action
b
when the other players are playing
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}
. We need to encode the requirement that
0
’s preference is complete and transitive over their own actions for each profile of actions over the other players. The following axioms do exactly that.

(A1)

\forall s\left(S_{0}(s)\lor S_{1}(s)\lor S_{2}(s)\lor\cdots\lor S_{n}(s)\right)

(A2)

\forall a,b,s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\left[R(a,b,s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\implies\left(S_{0}(a)\land S_{0}(b)\land S_{1}(s_{1})\land\cdots\land S_{n}(s_{n})\right)\right]

(A3)

\forall a,b,s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\left[\left(S_{0}(a)\land S_{0}(b)\land S_{1}(s_{1})\cdots\land S_{n}(s_{n})\right)\right.

\implies\left.\left(R(a,b,s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\lor R(a,b,s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\right)\right]

(A4)

\forall a,b,c,s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\left[\left(R(a,b,s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\land R(b,c,s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\right)\implies R(a,c,s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\right]

We interpret
S_{i}(s)
to mean that
s
is one of the actions available to agent
i
. Then axiom
(A1)
simply says that all actions under consideration are available to some agent. Any model of these axioms corresponds exactly to a collection

\{\succeq_{i,s_{-i}}\}_{s_{-i}\in S_{-i}}
where we reindex so that
i
zero and set

R(a,b,s_{1},\dots,s_{n})\iff a\succ_{0,(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})}b.

Axiom
(A1)
and
(A2)
simply serve to record which agents can take which actions and that preferences are only defined over action profiles where agents take their actions.
(A3)
is completeness and
(A4)
is transitivity.

Suppose that we could add an axiom
A^{*}
to this list such that the finite models satisfying
(A1)-(A4)
and
A^{*}
are exactly those that are strategically separable (we can always concatenate a finite list of axioms into a single axiom). We will show that this would imply that we can also add finitely many additional axioms to characterize additive separability in decision theory.
Krantz et al. 1990
established the impossibility of a finite axiomatization of additive separability. Thus finding such an

is impossible.

Suppose now that
n=2m\geq 4
. Consider the following additional axioms

(A5)

\exists x,y\left(x\neq y\right)\land\left(\forall s\left(S_{0}(s)\implies(s=x)\lor(s=y)\right)\right)

(A6)

\forall s\left(S_{1}(s)\iff S_{2}(s)\iff S_{3}(s)\iff\cdots\iff S_{n}(s)\right)

(A7)

\forall a,b\forall s_{1},\dots s_{m}\forall s_{m+1},\dots s_{n}\forall t_{m+1},\dots,t_{n}

\displaystyle R(a,b,s_{1},\dots,s_{m},s_{m+1},\dots,s_{n})\land R(a,b,s_{m+1},\dots,s_{n},t_{m+1},\dots,t_{n})

\displaystyle\implies R(a,b,s_{1},\dots

\displaystyle,s_{m},t_{m+1},\dots,t_{n})

We now argue that
(A1)-(A7)
and
A^{*}
together axiomatize additive separability in decision theory. To show this, we will establish that any model of
(A1)-(A7)
and
A^{*}
gives rise to an additively separable binary relation and conversely any additively separable total order can be written as a model of
(A1)-(A7)
and

Suppose that
X
is a finite set of at least two elements and that
\succeq

that is additively separable so that we can find functions

This model satisfies axioms

g_{0j}(z,w)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }z=z_{0}\\

Now,

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(z_{1},z_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{m})

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\sum_{i}u_{i}(x_{i})+\sum_{i}-u_{i}(y_{i})\leq 0

Mixed Strategies

In this section, we assume that each
S_{i}
is separable metric space and that
|N|=n
. Let
\Delta(S)
be the set of Borel probability measures on
S=\Pi_{i\in N}S_{i}
.
It is well known (see e.g.
Grandmont 1972
and
Ok and Weaver 2023
) that if a binary relation
\succeq_{i}
on
\Delta(S)
is complete, transitive, continuous and satisfies independence (
p\succeq_{i}q
if and only if
\lambda p+(1-\lambda)r\succeq_{i}\lambda q+(1-\lambda)r
for any
p,q,r
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
) then there is a continuous function

such that for any

we have

p\succeq_{i}q\iff\int u_{i}dp\geq\int u_{i}dq.

u_{i}
is called a Bernoulli index representing
\succeq_{i}
.
Furthermore,
u_{i}
is unique up to positive affine transformations. That is, if
u_{i}^{\prime}
is another Bernoulli index that represents
\succeq_{i}
then one can find
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
such that
u_{i}^{\prime}=a+bu_{i}
. In this section, we seek further conditions on
\succeq_{i}
so that we can find functions
u_{ij}:S_{i}\times S_{j}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
so that
\sum_{j\neq i}u_{ij}(s_{i},s_{j})
is a Bernoulli index for
\succeq_{i}
.

For any
p\in\Delta(S)
, and

we write
p_{J}
for the marginal distribution of

on
\Pi_{j\in J}S_{j}
.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that
\succeq_{i}

(a)

There are continuous functions
u_{ij}:S_{i}\times S_{j}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
so that
\sum_{j\neq i}u_{ij}(s_{i},s_{j})
is a Bernoulli index for
\succeq_{i}

(b)

for any

such that
p_{\{i,j\}}=q_{\{i,j\}}
for all

then

.

Proof.

That
(a)

u_{i}
be a continuous Bernoulli index for
\succeq_{i}
. Fix some arbitrary
s_{-i}^{*}
and arbitrary continuous functions
\bar{u}_{j}:S_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for each

such that

\sum_{j\neq i}\bar{u}_{j}(s_{i})=u_{i}(s_{i},s_{-i}^{*}).

for all

Note that each

is continuous. We claim that if

u(s)=\sum_{j\neq i}u_{ij}(s_{i},s_{j})
for all

and let

be the lottery in

that is

with probability
\frac{1}{n-1}

\frac{n-2}{n-1}

Multiplying both sides by

u_{i}(s)=\sum_{j\neq i}u_{i}(s_{i},s_{j},s_{-ij}^{*})-(n-2)u_{i}(s_{i},s_{-i}^{*})=\sum_{j\neq i}u_{i}(s_{i},s_{j},s_{-ij}^{*})-(n-2)\bar{u}_{j}(s_{i})=\sum_{j\neq i}u_{ij}(s_{i},s_{j})
