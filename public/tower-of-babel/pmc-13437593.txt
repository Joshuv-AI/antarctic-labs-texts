The role of root semantics in determining argument alternations
Authors: John Beavers, Kyle Jerro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArgument alternations are an empirical mainstay in studying the building blocks of verb meaning and how such meanings link to a verb’s grammatical properties. Event structures are widely adopted for exploring these questions, where a verb’s meaning is decomposed into an event template (defining grammatically coherent verb classes) and a root (filling in idiosyncratic verb-by-verb details). An alternation is analyzed as one root occurring in two templates, the latter supposedly predicting most of the properties of the alternation. Yet, idiosyncratic roots also have a significant effect in determining which alternations they occur in and the two variants’ interpretations. Beavers and Koontz-Garboden (2020) proposed that roots can also introduce the same templatic information as templates, in ways that explain such root-specific effects through compositional interactions of root and template meanings. We explore the explanatory power and limits of this ‘root semantic’ approach by expanding its empirical scope to an alternation similar to a dative alternation in Kinyarwanda (Bantu JD61; Rwanda), which are derived via the applicative morpheme -ir. The broad productivity of -ir in forming ditransitives, coupled with the significantly wider range of root-specific interpretative and grammatical effects, provides an ideal case study for the role of idiosyncratic verbal meaning in governing argument alternations. We show that a root semantic approach naturally captures the diversity of root-specific effects in Kinyarwanda, justifying it as part of a general theory of alternations. However, ingredients from work that has syntactified constraints on alternations or posited allosemic variation in templatic meaning are also needed, resulting in a multifactorate understanding of alternations with root semantics at its core.Keywords: Event structure, Argument alternations, Verb meaning, Applicative morphology, Bantu languages, Kinyarwanda

Introduction

Two questions are central in the study of verb meaning: what are the basic building blocks of verb meaning, and how are such components of a verb’s meaning related to its grammatical properties? A long-standing empirical mainstay in studying these questions (since at least Lakoff 1965; Fillmore 1968, 1970) are argument alternations, where a single verb occurs in multiple argument frames. An example of particular importance is the dative alternation, wherein verbs that describe caused possession exhibit two ways of expressing their recipient argument. For example, English send occurs either with an indirect object (IO) and a direct object (DO) expressing a recipient and a patient, respectively, as in (1a), or a DO expressing the patient and a to PP expressing a goal or recipient, as in (1b) (Fillmore 1968; Green 1974; Oehrle 1976; Larson 1988; Gropen et al. 1989; Pinker 1989; Larson 1990; Jackendoff 1990a,b; Goldberg 1995; Harley 2003; Beck and Johnson 2004; Krifka 2004; Rappaport Hovav and Levin 2008; Bruening 2010a,b; Beavers 2011, inter alia).

The central question is what the meaning of send is such that it and other verbs with similar meanings give rise to the observed alternation in how its arguments are expressed.

As we discuss in more detail in §2, event structural theories have been especially successful in modeling alternations. On such approaches verb meanings are decomposed into event structures consisting of two key components (see e.g., Dowty 1979). The first is a skeletal event template that defines the broad temporal and causal contours of the event described by the verb and defines most of its grammatical behavior, grouping verbs into semantic classes (e.g., caused change-of-state verbs like break and crack). Relevant to our discussion, causation is generally taken to be introduced by the event template, as are the notions of change of state and possession. The second is an idiosyncratic ‘root’ that names real-world actions or states and fills in the details of the template for a given verb, correlating with the verb’s idiosyncratic morphology and individuating verbs within a class. In event structural approaches, argument alternations are analyzed as the same root occurring in two distinct templates (see e.g., Rappaport and Levin 1988 for an early defense, and Beavers 2010, 825–831 for a detailed critique). For example, Harley (2003) analyzes the data in (1) as a single root that introduces a ‘sending’ action, occurring in either a caused possession template with a double object syntax, or caused motion template with a to construction syntax. Approaches such as this capture the broad fact that in argument alternations the variants share many systematic grammatical and semantic properties across a range of roots.

However, it is also known that alternations are subject to effects of particular roots, including root-specific choices of what templates they occur in and interpretative facts about templatic meaning tied to the root (which we discuss in §2; see Rappaport Hovav and Levin 2008; Beavers 2011). Various approaches have arisen to address the ways roots also govern the properties of the alternations they occur in, including analyses that allow roots to specify which templates they occur in (Rappaport Hovav and Levin 1998; Arad 2005; Alexiadou et al. 2006; Levinson 2007; Ramchand 2008) and those that make templatic meaning contingent on, among other things, the choice of root (Wood 2015; Myler 2016; Wood and Marantz 2017). Both approaches thus treat roots as lexicalized indices for specific grammatical and semantic effects. Other approaches have suggested that event structures are simply not relevant at all for analyzing argument alternations (Beavers 2006, 2010).

Recent work by Beavers and Koontz-Garboden (2020) and Beavers et al. (2021) has offered a forceful defense of the root/template distinction in analyzing verb meanings, but argues that the effects of roots within event structures requires rethinking the division of labor between roots and templates. They propose that root meanings do not just fill in idiosyncratic details within the event template, nor just serve as indices, but themselves compositionally introduce templatic information that interacts directly with the meanings introduced by the templates they occur in to predict root-specific effects for specific root–template combinations. They apply this ‘root semantic’ approach to a range of phenomena, including the English dative alternation, where they show that it can naturally capture both the broad generality of the alternation but also the specific effects of different roots, with few mechanisms needed beyond standard compositional processes.

In this paper, we explore the explanatory power and limits of this approach by expanding its empirical scope to semantically ditransitive predicates in Kinyarwanda (Bantu JD61; Rwanda), where applicative morpheme -ir serves to license a new object for a monotransitive verb that reflects a recipient, forming an alternation with many similarities to the dative alternation:1

(2)

Applicativization in Kinyarwanda is quite productive compared to the restricted nature of the English dative alternation, and exhibits considerably more types of root-specific interpretive and grammatical effects. This makes it an ideal test case for how far a root semantic approach to argument alternations can go. We explore the properties of this alternation in detail, and present an analysis that further justifies how such an approach can account for many facts about argument alternations. At the same time, the data also show that this approach has limits, and some amount of nonroot semantic lexicalization is required, albeit far less than would be needed without a root semantic analysis. Thus, the evidence supports a root semantic analysis as a component of an analysis of argument alternations, albeit at the same time pointing towards the need for other mechanisms.

On a more Kinyrwanda-specific point, the data we present also builds on growing evidence that applicative morphology does more than just add new syntactic and semantic arguments into event structures. Rather, applicatives interact with verb meaning to derive a range of outputs beyond classic valence increase, such as indicating focus or intensification on the applied object (Trithart 1983; Rugemalira 1993; Marten 2003; Poeta 2011; Sibanda 2016; Pacchiarotti 2020; Pacchiarotti and Zúñiga 2022; Zúñiga and Creissels 2024, inter alia). We build on the observation that applicative morphology adds truth-conditional information in root-sensitive ways with specific classes of roots (see, e.g., Rugemalira 1993; Misago 2018; Jerro 2023; Misago et al. 2024, who all focus on the interaction of verbs with locative phrases) in an empirical domain that—to our knowledge—has not been previously explored in depth, namely Kinyarwanda ditransitives of caused possession. It emerges below that such cases are replete, but in the context of event structures we show they arise naturally from a theory that explains argument alternations largely from root/template interactions.

In §2 we review event structural approaches to argument alternations, with an emphasis on dative alternations, and introduce the formal framework of analysis we use. Although our focus is on Kinyarwanda, this recap will of necessity involve a review of the English facts that first justified the framework, and the conceptual and empirical gaps that remain to be explored. We then provide an empirical base for Kinyarwanda ditransitives, justifying the comparison to ditransitives in other languages in §3. We look at the monotransitive roots that permit -ir applicativization in §4, before turning to lexical ditransitives and their interaction with -ir in §5, summarizing in §6. In §7 we show how a root semantic approach can naturally explain the data. We conclude by exploring the consequences of this analysis, comparing it to alternatives in §8 and situating it within larger theoretical questions about event structures and other alternations, including applicatives, in §9.

Argument alternations, event structures, and root sensitivity

Analyzing argument structure and argument alternations with event structures

Event structures have been widely adopted as an aspect of verbal meaning, but have also been subject to a wide range of formal implementations. Some works have treated them as phrase structural objects, where the template is composed of a configuration of functional heads and the root is a (sometimes acategorial) lexical head that sits within it. This makes the connection of the template to the syntax fairly direct since the template is the syntax (Lakoff 1965; McCawley 1968, 1971; Ross 1972; Hale and Keyser 1993, 1997, 2002; Pesetsky 1995; Baker 1997; Marantz 1997; Folli and Ramchand 2002; Folli and Harley 2004; Harley 2003, 2012; Borer 2005, 2013; Alexiadou et al. 2006, 2015; Ramchand 2008; Rappaport Hovav and Levin 2010; Beavers and Koontz-Garboden 2012, 2020; Mateu and Acedo-Matellán 2012, inter alia). Other approaches have treated event structures as wholly separate lexical representations composed out of a separate ontology of basic eventive and idiosyncratic primitives, which are then related to the syntax via correspondence rules (Foley and Van Valin 1984; Rappaport and Levin 1988; Pinker 1989; Levin and Rappaport Hovav 1995; Rappaport Hovav and Levin 1998; Van Valin and LaPolla 1997; Wunderlich 1997; Davis and Koenig 2000; Davis 2001, inter alia). But in all cases the central division of labor between roots and templates persists, and most analyses, including the ones we discuss here and below, even our own, can be implemented in any such framework (see Beavers and Koontz-Garboden 2020, 8–22, 80–95 for an extensive comparison of different approaches to event structures).

However, since we are building from Beavers and Koontz-Garboden (2020), we adopt their approach (with some minor modifications) for ease of comparison. They treat event structures as phrase structural objects, where a template is built from functional heads of category v and P that introduce basic eventive notions, and the root is an acategorial head that introduces specific states and actions. A canonical caused change-of-state verb like break is analyzed as a root introducing a result state standing as a complement of a head vcause which introduces causation:2

Separate Spell-Out processes generate the surface verb, surrounded by its arguments. Had the root in (3) been √shatter instead, the surface sentence Sandy shattered the cup would have the same basic caused change-of-state meaning and transitive syntax due to the template, but differ in the result state and pronunciation of the surface verb.3 Thus, the lexical semantic properties of the surface verb (which we descriptively refer to below as the ‘verb’ as shorthand) arise from a compositional combination of the semantics of its underlying root (contributing the idiosyncrasies) and its template (contributing regular meaning), and their grammatical properties.4

As discussed above, a common analysis of argument alternations is that they reflect one root occurring in two templates. For the dative alternation this has been understood as a single root occurring in either a template reflecting caused possession (the IO template) or caused motion (the to template). The semantic and syntactic differences between the templates explain numerous facts about the alternation. Specifically, Beavers and Koontz-Garboden adopt a modified version of the analysis of Harley (2003), who assumes each template is decomposed into a head vcause that takes a causer specifier and a PP complement that denotes the result brought about by the causer. For the IO template the PP is headed by a head Phave that takes a recipient specifier and a patient complement and expresses that the one possesses the other. In the to template it is a PP headed by a head Ploc that takes a patient specifier and a goal to PP complement and expresses that the one is located at the other. In both, the root is a manner-denoting head rather than a state-denoting one, which adjoins to vcause and specifies the manner by which the causer made the result come about:

(4)

These structures neatly explain the basics of the alternation. First, the shared root ensures the same verb appears in both cases, while the difference in the two PPs ensures the two surface complements are expressed differently in a consistent way across roots. Second, the general availability of the two templates independent of the root explains why other verbs show the same alteration (e.g., give, throw, bequeath). Third, this analysis predicts that the double object construction will require its IO to be a possessor due to the semantics of the possessional head Phave (which we outline in more detail below). Thus, inanimates like London are generally ruled out as IOs unless interpreted as capable of possession (e.g., ‘The London Office’), while the same is not true for to PPs:

Root sensitivity in event structures

However, there are several known issues with approaches like this. First, if a root can appear in one template then, in principle, we would expect it to appear in the other. While this is generally true, there are roots that can appear in only one (e.g., see Gropen et al. 1989; Pinker 1989; Levin 1993; Bresnan and Nikitina 2009; Beavers 2011, inter alia). For example, while send shows the dative alternation, carry generally does not (and ditto for haul, schlep, and push, among others):

(7)

This kind of lexical idiosyncrasy is at odds with the generality of the event structural approach.

Second, the semantic correlates of the alternation are not consistent across roots, a phenomenon that Rappaport Hovav and Levin (2008) call ‘verb sensitivity’, in other words, the meaning of an alternation is sensitive to the choice of verb exhibiting it. Thus, while send, and indeed most ditransitive verbs, shows the London Office effect only in the IO variant, give shows it in both variants (as do bequeath, will, hand, and a few others), suggesting that for this verb both variants entail possession:

(8)

Analytically, this would prima facia indicate that the root is deciding what the template means.

Third, sometimes the supposed result state introduced by the template is not actually entailed, depending on what the root is (Gropen et al. 1989; Beavers 2011). For example, with send, and indeed again most ditransitive verbs, neither actual final possession by nor arrival at the goal/recipient is entailed, though with give it is (and also for hand and a few others):

(9)

Rather, the templatic result state with send verbs is ‘prospective’ (Gropen et al. 1989; Beavers 2011). In sum, roots control what templates they occur in and what the templates mean, in broad and narrow respects. We refer to this phenomenon as the ‘root sensitivity’ of argument alternations.

To address such facts, Beavers and Koontz-Garboden (2020) developed a theory of event structures that puts a focus on how root meanings integrate compositionally with the template, which we call the ‘root semantic’ approach (see Beavers et al. 2021; Spathas and Michelioudakis 2021; Ausensi et al. 2021; Ausensi 2024 for further development). A key component of this approach is that roots sometimes entail the same sorts of higher-level eventive meaning templates do. As Beavers and Koontz-Garboden show, when roots and templates introduce the same or overlapping meanings, standard compositional processes reconcile them in ways that derive root sensitivity, with few additional mechanisms needed to explain the facts. They show that this approach naturally accounts for root sensitivity effects in word categoryhood, aspectual morphology, and sublexical scopal modification. Since we assume this framework here, we recap their analysis of English ditransitives, briefly also discussing their analysis of canonical monotransitive change-of-state verbs. We start with their proposed template meanings, then the root meanings, and finally how the two interact.

Conversely, substituting the meaning of √throw into the to template in (18b) results in a reading where there is a possible R′ state and a possible arrival state. Furthermore, again, nothing introduces the actual obtaining of the templatic state. We do, though, assume that the act of throwing (throwing′) has as part of its content that the agent must release the theme, meaning the theme will end up somewhere, generating an inference that some result state obtains (as discussed by Beavers 2011, 31–34, who explores how this result figures into telicity). But it is not the result seemingly represented by the template (see Beavers and Koontz-Garboden 2020, 135–138 for a more explicit treatment of this). The cumulative effect is that with throw the variants of the dative alternation will not be semantically identical, and will not entail that the IO/to PP participant receive the theme, nor that the theme reaches it.

Finally, a root’s meaning may conflict with the template’s, and this is how Beavers and Koontz-Garboden (2020, 148–150) explain why carry does not typically show IO variants in (7). Roughly, they propose (following Beavers 2011, 46–48) that the type of manner M′ introduced by √carry conflicts with the possession in the IO template, since carrying events require the agent to be in continuous possession of the theme throughout the event (i.e., it is a type of accompanied motion with continuous application of force à la the classifications in Gropen et al. 1989; Pinker 1989). But the templatic result state of the IO frame is that the recipient (prospectively) possesses the theme at the end of the event, meaning the event ends with two distinct possessors. While this is not technically contradictory—there are many different types of possession something can be under that can hold simultaneously—Beavers and Koontz-Garboden suggest it is pragmatically unusual in most cases, hence the degraded acceptability even if the expression is not actually ruled out (as per data from Bresnan and Nikitina 2009 showing that carry is sometimes attested in the IO frame).

A reviewer wonders, though, why the roots of change-of-state verbs must be low-attaching stative roots as in (3) and the roots of caused possession verbs must be higher-attaching eventive roots as in (4). They note that it would be possible to assume the opposite types and still get the same semantics since we are allowing that roots have complex meanings whereby stative roots introduce events and eventive roots introduce states. The reviewer also suggests that making different assumptions about root types and where they compose into the event struture, even if they produce the same basic semantics, could yield different combinatoric properties (e.g., in terms of possible types of sublexical modification). This is exactly right, and in fact Beavers and Koontz-Garboden (2020, 227–233) explicitly discuss the kind of typology that arises when one considers this full range of possibilities, though they defend the assumptions they make in the context of English based on language-internal considerations. Nonetheless, we stick with the assumptions we outline above for two reasons. First, these are the canonical assumptions of what kinds of roots underlie the two types of verbs in the framework we are exploring. Second, by and large, our goals here are to explore how roots can contribute to event structural meaning in ways that explain the diversity of meanings that arise in argument alternations, and that has more to do with the truth-conditional content of roots than their type or position. The questions that arise from considering root types and positions are a separate set of questions that would require a separate paper.

In sum, overlap in root and template meaning derives root sensitivity and rules out certain root/template combinations, as a result of what individual roots mean and standard compositional processes. Beavers and Koontz-Garboden (2020, 96–103, 150–155) make an extensive argument on parsimony grounds that their approach is preferable to alternatives in the literature that amount to positing idiosyncratic lexicalization (which we return to in §8). However, the distribution of double object constructions in English is fairly limited (see Gropen et al. 1989; Pinker 1989; Levin 1993), and the root sensitivity effects streamlined enough that idiodyncratic lexicalization is a reasonable approach. So, a question remains of whether a root semantic approach can scale up to the diversity in related constructions found in other languages. This is the gap that the present paper aims to fill.

Kinyarwanda applicativization as akin to a dative alternation

We first justify that the alternation between monotransitive Kinyarwanda verbs and -ir applicativized verbs is akin to dative alternations in other languages such as English (but also see Beavers and Nishida 2010 on Spanish and Beavers 2006, 189–191 on Japanese). We demonstrate that they share core features suggestive of an analysis that shares essential properties, focusing in this section on the similarities of IOs and applied objects (AOs). Our goal is not to show that Kinyrwanda -ir applicativization and dative alternations in other languages are identical, and that will not be our analysis. Rather, we show that they show crucial similarities in form, truth-conditional content, and function to suggest shared analytical aspects. Our data come from discussions with Kinyarwanda speakers conducted by the second author in Rwanda October 18th – November 25th 2016, as well as with expatriate speakers (1 male and 1 female) living in Austin, TX between September 2016 and June 2017. The speakers interviewed in Rwanda were living in Muhanga, South Province. They ranged between 18 and 45 years of age, with 2 men and 2 women being primary consultants.

Monotransitive base verbs and applicativization

We first consider various root classes that, when they occur without an applicative, are monotransitive, which will include most (but not all) verbs in (29) (we return to a small subset of (29b,d,g) that differ in §5). As we show, these verbs fall into a various crosscutting subclasses depending on what interpretations they produce with and without the applicative, all of which suggests a considerable amount of root sensitivity for Kinyarwanda ditransitives. In §4.1 we consider a major distinction in what type of thematic role the AO has depending on the root, and in §4.2 we consider what kinds of meanings different roots give rise to when there is no AO. In §4.3 we consider the effect different roots have on whether the putative result state of a given verbal predicate actually obtains.

The broad thematic role of the AO

A major property of Kinyarwanda -ir AOs that distinguishes them from English IOs is that AOs can occur with nearly any transitive canonical change-of-state verb in (29a). However, with canonical change-of-state verbs the AO is interpreted not as a recipient but instead as a beneficiary:12

(30)

In these cases, a reading of the AO as a recipient is not licensed, or, more precisely, while a beneficiary could in principle happen to be a recipient in some particular context, for a change-of-state verb there is no requirement that it is a recipient. Instead, a strictly recipient interpretation of an AO is only licensed with certain roots, namely those that describe events that naturally could lead to change-of-possession, including almost all of those found in (29b–h). However, these roots also license a beneficiary AO, and in this case it is entailed that there is some unexpressed participant that is the goal (as with ku-jugunya ‘throw’) or recipient (as with ku-gabura ‘serve’) of the theme:13

(31)

(32)

Thus, applicativization of canonical monotransitive change-of-state roots produces just one reading—the AO is a beneficiary (but not necessarily a recipient)—whereas with other roots we instead get two possible interpretations—the AO is a beneficiary (but not a recipient or goal, which is a separate entity), or it is a recipient (but not necessarily a beneficiary).

However, a question arises of whether recipient and beneficiary are really distinct roles, or two outgrowths of a single vague role that (31b) and (32b) both entail. Although we later posit a single, albeit partly disjunctive role (see §7.2), the two are distinct. As Kittilä and Zúñiga (2010, 5–6) note in a typological overview of benefactives (see also Kittilä 2005), one can receive without benefitting and benefit without receiving, something true in Kinyarwanda as well. At best, if Habimana benefits it is a byproduct of the fact that participating in any event might draw some benefit, which could be independently true of nearly any participant. As such, there is no reason to posit that somehow Habimana has been specifically assigned a beneficiary role.

Altogether, these data suggest that there are two technically distinct thematic roles available for AOs: recipient and benefactive. For some verbs the only available reading is benefactive, and in the other case, recipient is another possible interpretation. Since the templates are seemingly the same in all cases (given the shared use of applicative -ir), the broad thematic role of the AO must be contingent at least partly on the root, an instance of root sensitivity in Kinyarwanda applicativization. We now consider further instances of root sensitivity, in cases without applicativization.

The contrast in (37) is not simply an animacy effect, wherein the underlying thematic role is a goal but in one case the goal must somehow be animate, in other words, a recipient is somehow just an animate goal. As Rappaport Hovav and Levin (2008, 138–142) argue (contra Jackendoff 1996, 135, 194) recipients cannot be defined as animate goals, since caused possession, unlike caused motion, does not require transfer. In Sandy gave Kim an idea, while it is true that Kim comes to have an idea she did not have before, Sandy did not necessarily first have the idea. This holds true in Kinyarwanda as well. Summary of roots of monotransitive verbs

We explored the semantics of Kinyarwanda monotransitive verbs in (29), and showed there is considerable root sensitivity. In their nonapplied forms, some roots entail an implicit third participant and others do not, and among those that do there is varation as to whether that participant is a recipient or goal. Furthermore, roots with implicit participants give rise to ambiguous AOs under applicativization, either a recipient or a beneficiary (albeit with a separate recipient or goal entailed). Roots without implicit participants only give rise to beneficiary AOs. Finally, the root determines what result is entailed, if any. Sometimes the state is the one the putative template introduces, and sometimes not, all in the face of Kinyarwanda being a nonculminating language such that we would not expect results to be entailed at all. In all cases the effects happen within a single template, contingent on the root, pointing to root sensitivity. We turn next to a class of roots that differs sharply from the ones discussed in this section, and adds more layers of root sensitivity.

Lexical ditransitives

In this section we explore certain giving, ballistic motion, and fulfilling verbs in (29b,d,g) which are unique (and rare) in Kinyarwanda in allowing two objects without -ir (see Polinsky and Kozinsky 1992, Jerro 2023, 1435–1438). We first consider their basic grammatical properties and what justifies grouping them with other ditransitives in §5.1, and then turn to their behavior under applicativization in §5.2. We consider their properties regarding nonculmination in §5.3. We show that the behavior of these verbs expands the cases of root sensitivity in Kinyarwanda considerably.

Lexical ditransitive summary

Just like lexically monotransitive verbs in §4, lexical ditransitives exhibit root sensitivity, albeit of a different sort. In their nonapplied variants they take two arguments, already a root-specific effect. They furthermore differ on a root-by-root basis in terms of what the thematic role of the IO is, what and how many changes of state are actually entailed, and what the affect of applicativization is, which can include creating tritransitive structures with a new beneficiary or converting a goal IO into a recipient AO. Interim summary

(53)

There is considerable root sensitivity in Kinyarwanda, more so than outlined in §2.2 for English. While the English dative alternation involves just one pattern—a DP-PP transitive alternating with a DP-DP ditransitive—in Kinyarwanda we see four patterns: a strictly monotransitive construction becoming ditransitive, a monotransitive with an oblique XP becoming ditransitive, a ditransitive remaining ditransitive, and a ditransitive becoming tritransitive. Semantically, the English pattern just shows cases where a goal oblique takes on a recipient role as an IO (as with send), or a recipient oblique maintains its status as a recipient as an IO (as with give). But in Kinyarwanda we also see cases where beneficiary readings are possible, and cases where a beneficiary is added on top of a recipient. The patterns of prospective results are more like those found in English—variation in whether templatic results are entailed, plus the addition of nontemplatic results—albeit in the context of a generally nonculminating language. Finally, the Kinyarwanda alternation is more productive, motivating the need for a productive analysis more so than in English. All that said, the broad ingredients—variation in thematic roles, nonculmination, and root-conditioned choices of templates—are akin to what has been seen in English and other languages, suggesting that a framework of analysis that can capture those facts should apply here as well. One categorical effect is that the AO will have to have, by virtue of the B′ role, the ability to benefit or receive, in other words, a London office effect should obtain either way, as seen in §3. We furthermore show that various possible interpretations of Nkusi vis-a-vis the implicit argument a, contingent on context and root class, derive root sensitivity with no further assumptions.

First, in (65a) the root-supplied change is monadic, in other words, a state that is not defined as a possession or colocational one. It thus has a type of scale that is crucially not one that corresponds to dyadic change (see fn. 7). For this reason a solely recipient reading (one of the two options under B′) is ruled out since it would require a different sort of scalar change. Nkusi’s role must therefore be a beneficiary, the only remaining interpretation of B′ (though nothing prevents Nkusi from also being a recipient purely as a matter of pragmatic context). This derives that with a canonical change-of-state verb the reading will categorically be that of a beneficiary.

Conversely, in both (65b) and 65c) the change is inherently dyadic, meaning a possessional or colocational reading is in principle possible, where it is already lexically entailed that the underlying recipient/goal argument is a. We suggest that in this case there are two possible contextual interpretations. On the first, Nkusi is interpreted as distinct from a (i.e., n ≠ a). In this case he must be a beneficiary, since something distinct (a) is already the recipient, the only other possible reading that would satisfy Nkusi’s B′ role (assuming again uniqueness of participants). This derives the reading by which the new AO is a wholly distinct entity representing purely a beneficiary, the same sort of reading found with canonical change-of-state verbs.

On the second interpretation, Nkusi is conflated with a (i.e., n = a). On such a reading, Nkusi is interpreted as a recipient, which is the intersection of R′ and B′ and thus a very natural reading. Alternatively, it is theoretically possible that the reading is that Nkusi is somehow a goal (one possible reading of R′), while also being a beneficiary (satisfying the B′ role), but not a recipient. However, we suggest this is not a distinct empirical possibility on essentially conceptual grounds: an animate entity that is a goal is by definition a recipient in some sense—any (inanimate) entity that is physically with an animate individual stands in some type of possession relation with that individual by virtue of being on their person or otherwise in their physical sphere. For example, if someone slips a note into Mary’s back pocket without her even knowing, it is still acceptable to say Mary has the note, where the reading is either some generic alienable possession relation, or else a kind of control possession relation à la the typology of possessional relations of Tham (2005, 2006). So, the recipient reading of R′ obtains for Nkusi regardless by virtue of being an animate goal, which also satisfies B′ since being a recipient is one way of realizing this role. As such, a distinct beneficiary reading need not obtain (but may be true in context).

Thus, when the AO is conflated with the underlying participant a it must emerge as a recipient. The ultimate final effect of this interpretation in turn depends on the root. In the case of (65b) nothing changes, since n (by virtue of being conflated with a) was already a recipient. The only effect of applicativization is to give expression to what was otherwise an implicit event participant a. But in (65c) a was not inherently a recipient, and so by virtue of the B′ role and the animacy of the AO a is now ‘upgraded’ (as it were) to being a recipient (although it is still also a goal; see Beavers 2011, 31 and fn. 7 on simultaneous possession/motion predicates).

In sum, whether the AO is (a) a wholly new beneficiary, (b) realizing an implicit recipient, or (c) converting an implicit goal into an overt recipient all hinges on context, how its meaning interacts with the meaning of the rest of the template, and, crucially, the root. Indeed, these possible readings exhaust the possibilities for the various types of roots above, and every possibility is attested in some way, and no additional mechanisms are needed. We next turn to lexically ditransitive verbs, and show how this analysis can be extended to them, albeit in this case with the addition of some additional features and principles that go beyond a strictly root semantic approach.

Discussion

Key to our analysis is that, based on the data for the same templates across roots, the templates we examined had fairly weak meanings, essentially whatever was entailed for the verbs in those templates regardless of the root. But allowing that the root can introduce the same kinds of meanings as templates means that sometimes templates and roots introduce overlapping semantic pieces. In such cases the various ways in which those pieces interact produced much of the diversity of root sensitivity effects we saw above as a matter of course.

A question arises, though, of whether a root semantic approach is necessary to analyze the root sensitivity effects seen here. As noted in §1 and §2, there are other ways of capturing root sensitivity. One such approach is the allosemy approach of Wood (2015), Myler (2016), and Wood and Marantz (2017), which assumes that templates have many readings, and which one arises depends on, among other things, which root occurs in it. For example, it could be that roots had very simple meanings, or even no meaning at all. But allosemic rules will assign to each template a different meaning either taken from the root somehow (it adopts the meaning ‘implied’ by the root; Wood and Marantz 2017, 259–260) or otherwise conditioned by the context of combining with that root. For example, an allosemic analysis that captures the difference between English give and send might say that neither root has a compositional meaning, but in the context of occurring with either root v takes on the meaning that we said arose from head-adjunction of each root in §2.2. The meaning that arises would thus be root-conditioned, but without positing that the root itself has these meanings, or if it did that it was nonetheless introduced through allosemic rules and not by a direct composition with the root itself (see Wood and Marantz 2017, 259–260). This approach will not, though, guarantee that different templates will take different numbers of arguments, or that roots are restricted to particular templates, both parts of the root sensitivity puzzle. But it can handle root sensitivity where the same structure can have different interpretations, and in this case it is possible to apply such an analysis to Kinyarwanda simply by listing out all of the possible meanings for each templatic structure, and then ensuring that for each root the right reading arises.

Another approach for handling root sensitivity assumes that roots fall into various semantic classes (e.g., Rappaport Hovav and Levin 1998; Arad 2005; Alexiadou et al. 2006; Levinson 2007) or else come with specific syntactic features (e.g., Ramchand 2008) that constrain what templates they occur in via realization principles (see, e.g., the ‘canonical realization rules’ of Rappaport Hovav and Levin 1998, 108–109). In some cases these conditions are principled. For example, Levinson (2007) posits type-theoretic distinctions for roots that derive from the sorts of entities they describe (e.g., events, states, and individuals) that could in principle constrain which roots go where, also a feature of the approach we adopted. But within these broad classes there is still considerable diversity that has to be accounted for that will require positing further features or other intuitionistic constraints. Root sensitivity is thus built into such a theory, but as a separate mechanism that amounts to positing ‘syntactic diacritics’ (Koenig and Davis 2006) ensuring the right distribution of roots. Thus, we could, for example, say that most Kinyarwanda caused possession roots have a +P feature, ensuring that they combine with our P morpheme from §7.1 (possibly through Appl), save for √ha and the other lexical ditransitives, which have an (optional) +Ploc feature permitting combination with Ploc. Such approaches would be more amenable to addressing facts about how many arguments a verb takes or generally which template a root occurs in than allosemy approaches. But diacritic approaches do not necessarily govern what a given template means if it is possible with a particular root. However, if the featural/realizational system happened to ensure that a given root occurred with templates with the same surface forms but that have different readings, qua templatic polysemy, it could generate distinct meanings. Alternatively, this approach could also be combined with an allosemy approach so that the featural/realizational system determines what templates are possible, and the allosemy system determines the range of meanings of each template.

Beavers and Koontz-Garboden (2020, 96–103, 150–155) acknowledge that such approaches could account for English root sensitivity, but suggest that a root semantic approach does so in a more streamlined and principled fashion (see Beavers et al. 2021, 473–474 for discussion of these points in a typological context). As for diacritic approaches, they note that when there is a clear semantic pattern to the root sensitivity such analyses essentially serve to syntactify the semantics. But this is an unnecessary step if the semantics itself can account for the data directly (unless one wanted to say roots did not have compositional meanings at all, an assumption we see no reason to make).

For allosemy, Beavers and Koontz-Garboden note three things in particular about English dative alternations militating against such an approach. First, just as with diacritic approaches, it is not clear why these various allosemes would arise except as a reflection of root semantics, raising the question of why the mechanism of allosemy would be needed if compositional root semantics can account for the facts anyway. Indeed, the diversity of English root classes Beavers and Koontz-Garboden (2020, 130–150) discuss (which is considerably larger than the subset sketched in §2.2) would require quite a number of possible allosemes for both templates. More broadly, it is not clear why certain allosemes arise with certain roots. On allosemic approaches the meanings a template takes on are treated formally as a list of allosemes conditioned by context. But there remains a question of whether that list can be reduced to anything more principled. On a root semantic approach the root introduces the meaning compositionally, eliminating the need for lists.

Second, based on data from sublexical modification suggesting that root-specific meanings arise above the result-denoting PPs, Beavers and Koontz-Garboden argue that most if not all of the allosemic variation in English would have to be centered around various meanings for v if it is not in the root. Given that this is the head the root adjoins to, then the [ v √root ] unit formed by this adjunction would have the same meanings we posited in §2.2 (see above). This effectively recreates the root semantic analysis, albeit using additional mechanisms of allosemic meaning assignment to avoid saying that roots have more complex compositional meanings. Third and finally, were templates subject to such allosemic variation we might expect to see that variation overtly reflected in the templatic morphology in other languages, for example, as distinct applicative (or causal) morphology in different languages. But, while typologies of applicatives (see, e.g., Peterson 2007) have noted some degree of diversity in applicative forms depending on semantics, they have not identified anything like the kind diversity in overt applicative forms that would correspond to the patterns here. Kinyarwanda is no exception—it has just two productive applicatives, -ir as above and -ish for causative and instrumental uses (see, e.g., Kimenyi (1980) for a detailed description of applicatives in Kinyarwanda and Jerro (2017a) for an analysis of -ish)—and Bantu in general shows no such diversity of applicative forms. Conversely, there clearly are different overt root forms corresponding to all of those different meanings across languages.21

Altogether, then, the root semantic approach to root sensitivity is simpler, and relies just on the idea that roots have idiosyncratic meanings (which of course must be stipulated, but this is true for any lexical item) that interact with template meanings and that these meanings compose into the event structure just like the semantics of any other head. In principle, all of these arguments carry over to Kinyarwanda (though we do not have sublexical modification data to suggest where the locus of allosemic meaning would be). But focusing just on the ways discussed in §6 that Kinyarwanda shows more diversity in root sensitivity than English, we see additional lines of argumentation for a root semantic approach in the -ir facts above.

First, the Kinyarwanda applicative is more productive than the distribution of double object constructions in English. This would in effect increase the lists of allosemic triggers needed to analyze -ir far beyond what is needed in English. Second, the fact that benefactive readings are also available adds a wholly new dimension of allosemic variation, further exploding the number of allosemes required. Given the relatively wider distribution of benefactive uses of -ir compared to the narrower recipient uses, one might want to argue that this could be handled by treating the benefactive reading as an elsewhere case. This is, for example, what Everdell (2023, 178–259) proposes for benefactive reading of the applicative in the Uto-Aztecan language O’dam (which only arises in contexts where it cannot add a more specific role). But a problem with this is that the benefactive reading is generally available everywhere in Kinyarwanda, and not only in cases that are not covered by more specific cases. This creates an ambiguity in the cases of roots that we claim have implicit goals and recipients. Thus, it is not an elsewhere case, even if recipient readings are the more limited subset.

Finally, the behavior of lexical ditransitives deserves attention. Above, we claimed that the AOC explains why gu-ha ‘give’ does not behave the same under -ir as other verbs, due to the synonymy that applicativization would generate owing to that root’s strong meaning. On this analysis its unique behavior is independently explained by a larger pattern justified by other cases of synonymy avoidance discussed in Jerro (2023). On an allosemic or diacritic analysis this explanation would be lost, and the effect would have to be treated as a case of nonce lexicalization, missing a bigger generalization, or as conditions making allosemic choices contingent on other allosemic choices, an extra level of complexity for that analysis. In sum, Kinyarwanda provides further arguments that a root semantic analysis is more principled than its alternatives. Of course, as Beavers and Koontz-Garboden (2020) acknowledge, alternative analyses of English are possible, and given the more constrained nature of the English dative alternation in §2.2 it is not unreasonable to chalk it up to independent, nonprincipled lexicalizations. This would even be possible here as well, but with Kinyarwanda the conceptual problems of that analysis would be more significant.

That said, the Kinyarwanda data has also shown the limits of a root semantic approach, and that there are reasons for adopting aspects of these prior approaches even in light of it. Recall first that lexical ditransitive roots come with a condition that requires two DP complements, and it is not obvious at all from their semantics where that condition would arise from. It is for this reason that we ultimately adopted a diacritic approach. Relatedly, as discussed in §4.2, which nonapplied monotransitive roots allow their implicit location arguments to be expressed by an oblique XP seems to be strictly lexicalized, and it is not obvious there is any semantic generalization there. Key, though, is that we posited this for exactly the cases where there did seem to be nonsemantic idiosyncrasy rather than systematicity. Furthermore, recall in fn. 11 that there is another use of -ir that introduces a location rather than a recipient or beneficiary, and applies to an overlapping but distinct set of verbs. While it could be that a more general analysis is possible, given that that reading is sufficiently distinct from the ones discussed here, a template polysemy approach (an ingredient of an allosemy analysis) seems justified. Indeed, even in English we see evidence for the need for nonroot semantic factors. Recall that English v is restricted just to caused possession roots—English is not generally a nonculminating language. This effect cannot be an artifact of head adjunction, since other supposed cases of manner roots adjoining to v (e.g., unergatives) do not give rise to this. So, it is something about roots of caused possession that gives rise to it, and absent a coherent semantic story as to why it is likely it is a case of nonsemantic lexicalization. Thus, even in English a root semantic analysis cannot account for everything, contra the claim of Beavers and Koontz-Garboden, who argue (incorrectly, in our view) that no such diacritics are needed.

To this end, it seems clear that allosemy/polysemy and diacritics must also be a part of a theory of root sensitivity effects in event structural approaches. Our proposal is that such analyses are better reserved for cases where the generality seen in the root semantic analysis is not attested, and some true idiosyncrasy is thus justified. To add to this, recall that the AOC, a paradigmatic condition on the relationship of applied and nonapplied variants, is operative in Kinyarwanda, limiting what was possible under applicativization. Just like potential allosemy/polysemy and syntactic diacritics, this curtails and limits the applicability of a root semantic approach. But again, this is to be expected: there is no reason to assume that there will not be language-particular conditions, even wholesale ones, governing the shape of the lexicon and its relationship to syntax for that language.22 Rather, the point is that the degree to which root sensitivity can be attributed to interactions of root and template meanings then fewer additional language- or root-particular constraints are needed. To put it another way, within the limits imposed by general- and language-particular constraints on a possible lexical inventory, plus the vagaries of idiosyncratic stipulation, root semantics and its interaction with templatic meaning can explain much of the remaining root sensitivity (see Beavers (in press) for more on the interaction of lexical semantic conditions on argument realization with lexical idiosyncrasies and general principles).

Finally, there is a question of whether the root/template distinction is even justified anymore. In the analyses above the roots incorporated large chunks of templatic semantics, so much so that in some cases the templates were superfluous. Indeed, Beavers (2006, 2010) suggested that event structures are simply not the right way to analyze argument alternations at all, instead proposing principles that correlate argument structure directly to truth-conditional content within undecomposed verb meanings (qua sets of lexical entailments as per Dowty 1991; Ackerman and Moore 2001; see also Beavers 2011; Beavers and Zubair 2013, 2016; Beavers and Udayana 2023; Jerro 2023). Here, we point out that while it is true that roots and templates have overlapping meaning, templates still have explanatory value, including defining the structure over which argument asymmetries are defined, and also in some cases introducing semantics that varies across variants of an alternation when the root is held constant (e.g., consistent London Office effects with English IOs and Kinyarwanda AOs). While the root semantic approach does diminish the role of templates in event structural analyses, it does not eliminate them (see Beavers and Koontz-Garboden 2020, 104,155–157, 209–210, 226–233 for a more thorough defense of the distinction).

Conclusion

Argument alternations in event structural approaches are analyzed as a single root occurring in two different templates, with the templates predicting a bulk of the verb’s grammatical properties across the alternation. However, there is also root sensitivity, as the Kinyarwanda -ir alternation exemplifies. Taking monotransitive, applicative (both ditransitive and tritransitive), and IO constructions to reflect distinct templates there is considerable root sensitivity in how each is used: the meaning of the template and alternations between templates depend on the root, and which templates are allowed with which roots is also constrained by the root. By comparing individual roots across templates and individual templates across roots we are able to factor out the semantic contributions of each, with the results suggesting that root meanings can introduce templatic meanings in ways that predict root sensitivity. Root subsumption of templatic meaning and vice versa opens up the possibility for root-contingent interpretations, while incompatibilities between roots and templates constrain certain combinations. Furthermore, language particular rules (e.g., Applicativzation Output Constraint) interact with root-sensitive interpretations to rule out otherwise possible combinations. Thus, unlike prior approaches, we need few syntactic features or realization rules. However, we also showed that this approach cannot explain everything. There were cases where some amount of diacritic analysis was required, and furthermore there is clearly some amount of templatic polysemy at play. So, while the root semantic approach here does account for a lot, it has its limits, and an approach that combines features of other approaches is probably necessary.

A root semantic analysis could apply to other types of argument alternations, though we believe that there must still be some amount of nonwholly compositional semantic classes and lexicalization (as we saw with Kinyarwanda) based on evidence seen in prior cases. For example, Beavers (2010) explores direct/oblique alternations (locative, conative, preposition drop, and dative) and argues that they reflect strictly decreasing specificity in lexical entailments associated with alternating arguments. The roles of direct arguments are verb-specific, yielding verb-sensitivity in the sense of Rappaport Hovav and Levin (2008). In many cases the specificity contrast could be restated as conditions on alternating templates, with roots overlaying the specific semantics (see Spicer 2024 for such an approach to Modern Hebrew). In another domain, the causative/inchoative alternation is a textbook case of application of event structural approaches, and Koontz-Garboden (2009) explores anticausativization qua reflexivization in Spanish. He suggests there is a significant effect of verbal semantics on the interpretation as an interaction with a generalized reflexivizer, where verbs that take agentive subjects generate true reflexive readings but those with generic causer subjects generate anticausative readings (though see Schäfer and Vivanco 2016). On an event structural analysis, assuming reflexivization is a templatic operation, and the narrow thematic role of the subject comes from the root (see Beavers and Koontz-Garboden 2012, 2020; Ausensi et al. 2021), these facts could naturally be treated as a case of root sensitivity in an event semantic approach.

Conversely, middle voice (see, e.g., Kemmer 1993) is a phenomenon that can generate such disparate set of interpretations (reflexive, passive, inchoative, reciprocal, dispositional) that it is hard to see unity. Beavers and Udayana (2023), in a study of Bahasa Indonesian, suggest that all of these readings have at their core a syntax/semantics mismatch: they are instances of semantically dyadic roots in an intransitive syntax where the unexpressed argument must be interpreted somehow according to pragmatics and root class. However, on their analysis some of the root class effects are not generated semantically, but as matters of lexical conventionalization, for example, that naturally reflexive roots like body care verbs default to reflexive readings even though such readings are not necessary (a common assumption in work on middles; see Kemmer 1993, 58–67, 102, Kaufmann 2007, 1686–1688, Alexiadou and Doron 2012, 23–29, Spathas et al. 2015, 1337–1344, and Beavers and Zubair 2016, 99–100). Here may be a case where there is just necessarily some amount of predefined root class or lexicalization at play. Finally, it could of course be that some alternations inherently have no truth-conditional semantic content, for example, voice phenomena in some languages. In this case we may simply see no root sensitivity effects. This is compatible with the proposals outlined here, though it shows again the limits of a root semantic approach.

Finally, the Kinyarwanda data here are also interesting in other respects. First, the data and our analysis run counter to the Bifurcation Thesis of Roots, a proposal primarily articulated by Embick (2009) (see also Arad 2005 and Dunbar and Wellwood 2016), by which meanings that are introduced templatically such as cause and change are never also introduced by roots. It is a key component of the root semantic approach that roots can entail exactly the same higher level eventive meaning templates do, and the Kinyarwanda facts provide further support for this and against Bifurcation (see Jerro 2017b, 2018a on Kinyarwanda change-of-state roots that entail change like vbecome; see also Spathas 2017; Valle et al. 2017, Beavers and Koontz-Garboden 2020, Beavers et al. 2021 for broader discussion of change-of-state roots). Second, these data build on the observation that the applicative morpheme can contribute a range of distinct functions in a way that interacts with the meaning of the root (Trithart 1983; Rugemalira 1993; Marten 2003; Poeta 2011; Sibanda 2016; Pacchiarotti 2020; Pacchiarotti and Zúñiga 2022; Zúñiga and Creissels 2024). Here, we have developed an account of the truth-conditional contribution of the applicative in ditransitive predicates, though other classes of verb show a range of additional discourse sensitive meanings such as focus (Trithart 1983; Marten 2003; Poeta 2011; Pacchiarotti 2020; Gibson et al. 2022; Misago et al. 2024). We also note that there are additional puzzles that arise in Kinyarwanda and closely related Kirundi, such as cases where the applicative changes locative orientation (i.e., which participants appear in the location of the locative phrase) as described in Kimenyi (1995) and Jerro (2018c) for Kinyarwanda and Misago et al. (2024) for Kirundi. The degree to which all of these readings are subsumed under one broader operation of applicativization or whether certain components of meaning follow from the upgraded object status often associated with the applied object is an open question for the field.
