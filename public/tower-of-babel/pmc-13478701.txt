Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis in Adults: A Canadian Multicenter Case Series
Authors: Stephanie Quon, Yaswanta Gummadi, Ibrahim Elsharawi, Jessica Dobson, Mariam Goubran, Ryan J Stubbins, Eli L Diamond, Luke Y C Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLangerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare clonal myeloid neoplasm. Canadian data on clinical characteristics, molecular profile, and treatment outcomes is limited. This study aims to report the initial experience of a Canadian rare diseases program, reflecting “real‐world” diagnostic pathways, referral patterns, and treatment heterogeneity across multiple provinces. We conducted a retrospective review of patients managed in an adult‐care cohort with histologically confirmed LCH diagnosed between 2000 and 2025 across multiple Canadian centers. Patients diagnosed as children and subsequently transferred to adult care were included. Clinical features, radiologic findings, histopathology, molecular testing, treatment approaches, and outcomes were collected and analyzed. Thirty‐one patients were identified, with a median age at diagnosis of 42 years (range: 2–84) and a male predominance (65%). Bone (74%), lung (29%), and skin (16%) were the most commonly involved sites. Concomitant or subsequent malignancies were present in 19% of patients. Molecular testing found BRAFV600E mutations in 44% of tested patients. The most common first‐line systemic therapy was cytarabine (n = 10), followed by other drugs such as hydroxyurea, vemurafenib, and cladribine. This series represents the initial experience of a Canadian rare disease referral program and captures the clinical heterogeneity and longitudinal adult care of patients with LCH across multiple provinces. Variability in treatment approaches highlights the need for collaborative prospective natural history studies and coordinated clinical trials.Keywords: adult LCH, BRAFV600E, langerhans cell histiocytosis, rare disease, targeted therapy

Introduction

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a clonal myeloid neoplasm characterized by the proliferation and accumulation of CD1a+ and CD207+ dendritic cells, resulting in granulomatous lesions that can affect virtually any organ system [1, 2, 3]. LCH is rare, with incidence in children under 15 years of age estimated at 4.1 to 4.6 cases per million per year, with lower incidence rates in adults at approximately 1–2 per million [1, 4, 5].

Clinical presentation of LCH ranges from isolated, self‐limited lesions to severe multisystem disease [1, 6]. The most common manifestations include lytic bone lesions, seborrheic or eczematous skin rashes, pulmonary cysts or nodules, and central diabetes insipidus resulting from pituitary involvement [7]. Because LCH can involve virtually any organ system and mimic a wide range of infectious, inflammatory, autoimmune, and malignant conditions, patients may present to multiple specialties before the diagnosis is established.

The differential diagnosis for LCH is broad and includes solid and hematologic malignancies, autoimmune granulomatous conditions, and infections such as osteomyelitis [1, 7, 8]. Accurate diagnosis requires tissue biopsy with histopathological confirmation of lesional LCH histomorphology and immunohistochemistry demonstrating CD1a and CD207 expression [7, 9].

Treatment for LCH is based on burden and distribution of disease. Patients with single‐system disease may be managed with surgery/curettage, radiotherapy, or observation alone, while those with multisystem or high‐risk organ involvement often require systemic therapy [6, 10]. Standardized international treatment protocols, such as LCH 2 and LCH 3 for pediatric populations which include vinblastine and corticosteroids [11], have informed treatment approaches in adults, although adult management remains less standardized. Relapsed or refractory cases may require a combination regimen of cytarabine and cladribine [12, 13].

Recent advances in molecular profiling have led to the development of targeted therapies for patients with MAPK pathway mutations. BRAF and MEK inhibitors such as vemurafenib, dabrafenib, and trametinib have shown promising efficacy in relapsed or refractory disease, including cases with central nervous system (CNS) involvement [6, 14, 15, 16]. These agents are generally well tolerated but are associated with high recurrence rates upon discontinuation, and their role as first‐line therapy remains under investigation [3, 15].

Despite advances in understanding the molecular underpinnings of LCH, diagnosis and management remain complex due to its clinical heterogeneity. In addition, published data describing adults with LCH in Canada remains limited, particularly within a publicly funded healthcare system where referral processes, diagnostic evaluation, and treatment approaches may differ from those reported elsewhere. Here, we present a retrospective adult‐care cohort study of 31 patients across Canada with histologically confirmed LCH. We describe their clinical presentations, organ involvement, histopathologic and molecular features, treatment strategies, and outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective study included 31 adult patients (age > 17 years) with a diagnosis of LCH between October 2000 and September 2025. This study was designed as a cohort of adults receiving care through an adult rare diseases referral program, rather than an inception cohort defined solely by age at diagnosis. Three patients were diagnosed in childhood but were included because they were subsequently referred to and managed within an adult rare disease program, allowing for characterization of their adult disease course, treatment, and outcomes. The data collection cutoff date was September 30, 2025.

All patients were managed through the Coastal Rare Inflammatory Diseases Program (CoRID), which is directed by one of the senior authors (LC) and leverages the expertise of other international experts including the other senior author (ED). The program provides diagnostic and treatment recommendations for individuals with rare diseases across Canada, while patients typically receive care at their local institutions. Patient data was stored at Vancouver General Hospital, and the study received ethics approval from the University of British Columbia Clinical Research Ethics Board (H23‐01853).

Patients were identified from tertiary care centers in Vancouver and Halifax through a retrospective review of available clinical records from patients managed within the participating adult hematology programs during the study period. Cases were included based on the availability of sufficient clinical data for review and were not selected consecutively. Most patients received care in British Columbia (n = 17) and Nova Scotia (n = 11), with the remaining from Ontario (n = 2) and Newfoundland (n = 1). The participating centers were responsible for the diagnosis, treatment, and longitudinal clinical follow‐up, while the CoRID Program coordinated the centralized data collection for this study. To reflect “real‐world” rare disease care delivery, this geographic distribution reflects the program's initial experience systematically characterizing adult LCH referrals within CoRID and affiliated centers during the study period, rather than population‐based sampling. Data was collected on patient demographics, clinical presentation, diagnostic findings (including histology, imaging, and molecular testing), treatment history, and outcomes including recurrence and disease status.

Molecular testing was performed as part of clinical care and varied according to the treating institution and year of diagnosis. BRAF V600 mutation testing was performed using allele‐specific quantitative PCR where available. Selected patients also underwent targeted next‐generation sequencing using clinically validated amplicon‐based panels to evaluate recurrent somatic alterations in clinically relevant cancer‐associated genes.

All cases had a confirmed histopathologic diagnosis of LCH based on biopsy review by hematopathologists at tertiary centers, showing characteristic CD1a and/or Langerin positivity with supportive morphologic features. When immunohistochemical findings were incomplete or atypical, diagnosis was established through clinicopathologic review incorporating morphologic, clinical, radiologic, and available molecular findings. Treatments were categorized according to systemic therapy, local therapy (e.g., radiation), and supportive care, based on documentation in oncology and hematology notes.

Response to therapy was evaluated using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) guidelines [17], with changes in the sum of lesion diameters used to categorize response as complete response, partial response, stable disease, or progressive disease. As response assessments were heterogeneous across centers, we additionally reviewed clinical documentation, PET‐CT reports, and MRI/CT imaging to identify descriptions of metabolic response, radiographic stability or progression, and symptom improvement. For lesions not readily measurable using RECIST, response was assigned through clinical and radiologic assessment based on overall changes in disease burden, imaging findings, organ involvement, and clinical status. Recurrence was defined as the re‐appearance of radiologic or PET‐avid lesions after a documented complete or partial response, and current disease status (active vs. inactive) was determined from the most recent imaging and clinical assessment.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics, disease characteristics, treatments, and clinical outcomes. Continuous variables were presented as medians and ranges, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Analyses were performed using available data, with missing values excluded from denominator calculations where appropriate.

A subset of patients had a previous malignancy (n = 6, 19%), including basal cell carcinoma (n = 2, 6%), melanoma (n = 1, 3%), JAK2‐positive essential thrombocythemia (n = 1, 3%), ileal neuroendocrine tumor with subsequent CMML and progression to AML (n = 1, 3%), and colon cancer with an NRAS mutation followed by AML (n = 1, 3%). In addition, seven patients (23%) had diabetes insipidus. Twelve patients (39%) had a history of smoking tobacco, and of these patients, eight (66%) had disease involvement in the lungs.

Histopathologic Features, Imaging, and Laboratory Findings

Histopathological confirmation of LCH was obtained through a median of 2 biopsies (range 1–4). Combined, biopsy sites were most commonly bone (n = 20), bone marrow (n = 11), skin/soft tissue (n = 5), lymph nodes (n = 5), GI tract (n = 4), gingiva/oral mucosa (n = 3), liver (n = 2), lung (n = 2), and ear structures (n = 2). Histopathologic findings included Langerhans cells (n = 29), eosinophilic infiltrate (n = 20), other inflammatory cells such as lymphocytes, neutrophils, and plasma cells (n = 17), necrosis (n = 5), and fibrosis (n = 4). In two cases, Langerhans cells were not definitively identified on available biopsies, likely reflecting limited tissue sampling or a non‐representative biopsy site. Therefore, the diagnosis of LCH was determined by a combination of other pathologic features. For cases with available immunohistochemistry data, all patients were CD68+, 25 out of 26 (96%) tested patients were CD1a+, and 24 out of 25 (96%) tested patients were S100+. One case demonstrated atypical immunophenotyping with absent CD1a staining, however, the diagnosis of LCH was supported by other characteristic histologic features and expression of other markers. Of the 25 patients tested, 11 (44%) were BRAF positive, and 14 (56%) were BRAF negative. BRAF mutation testing was not performed in a subset of patients due to limited access to molecular testing and variability in testing practices over time. Other markers, when performed, showed variable expression including CD3, CD20, CD45, and CD138. Ki67 proliferative index was available for three cases, with reported values of 10%, 30%–40%, and 60%.

Next‐generation sequencing was performed in 13 cases. Mutations identified included BRAF V600E mutations (n = 4, 31%), a MAP2K1 pathway mutation (n = 1, 8%), and a JAK2 mutation (n = 1, 8%). Additional variants in TET2, U2AF1, and KRAS were detected on bone marrow next‐generation sequencing. As these genes are commonly associated with clonal hematopoiesis and sequencing was not performed on matched tumor tissue for these cases, these variants likely reflect clonal hematopoiesis rather than tumor‐specific mutations. One patient (Case 9) harbored a MAP2K1 mutation, a rare exon 3 in‐frame deletion (E102_I103del), which has been described as a RAF‐independent MEK1 alteration and is typically associated with resistance to MEK inhibition (27). A BRAF V600A variant and a low‐frequency p.F595L variant were detected in Case 21, and BRAF p.Met564Val (c.1690A > G; VAF 47.8%) and p.Val600Glu (c.1799T > A; VAF 15.2%) variants were detected in Case 30. Three patients had no pathogenic variants identified on sequencing. A summary of all immunohistochemistry and sequencing findings is provided in Supporting Information S1: Table 2.

Imaging findings included CT, MRI, and PET‐CT results. Twenty‐three patients had PET‐CT imaging, and at time of diagnosis, common findings included bone involvement (n = 18, 78%), lung lesions, cysts, or nodules (n = 8, 35%), and FDG‐avid lymphadenopathy (n = 6, 26%). Common skeletal sites included the calvarium/skull, mandible, tibia, femur, ribs, vertebrae, and sacrum. MRI findings at involved sites included extensive synovial hypertrophy with periarticular erosions and plantar plate destruction in the forefoot, lytic calvarial lesions with soft tissue extension, marrow‐replacing lesions in long bones with surrounding edema and enhancement, and mass‐like tissue in the temporal bone, orbit, and mandible with adjacent soft tissue swelling and bone remodeling. Treatments and Outcomes

Of the entire cohort, patients received a mix of observation, local therapy, and systemic therapy. An overview of treatments is shown in Figure 4, and patient‐level treatment lines, responses, and outcomes are outlined in Supporting Information S1: Table 3. Eleven patients underwent surgical resection, three received radiation therapy, two were managed with smoking cessation alone, and 19 received at least one systemic therapy as first‐line treatment. One patient underwent an allogeneic stem cell transplant from a fully matched sibling donor following reduced‐intensity conditioning with fludarabine and treosulfan, and they remained in complete metabolic remission at 1 year with quiescent GVHD and no FDG‐avid disease. The median number of systemic therapies received per patient was 1 (range, 0–4).

Sankey diagram of treatment courses, excluding radiation and surgery.

Nineteen patients received at least one systemic therapy as first‐line treatment. Ten patients were initiated on cytarabine, resulting in partial response (n = 6), progressive disease (n = 2), stable disease (n = 1), and complete response (n = 1). The overall response rate for cytarabine‐treated patients was 70% (n = 7/10). Reported adverse effects included neutropenia (n = 2), nausea (n = 2), neuropathy (n = 2), bone pain (n = 1), and self‐perceived cognitive changes (n = 1). Two patients started hydroxyurea, with progressive disease in both cases. Four patients were treated with either the LCH2 protocol (n = 1) or the LCH3 protocol (n = 3), all achieving complete response. The overall response rate for LCH‐based regimens was therefore 100% (n = 4/4). Other first‐line regimens included vemurafenib (n = 1), yielding a partial response with side effects of neuropathy and cutaneous rash; pegylated interferon (n = 1), resulting in progressive disease and side effect of diarrhea; and zoledronic acid (n = 1), yielding a partial response and side effect of osteonecrosis of the jaw. Across all first‐line therapies, the overall response rate was 63% (n = 12/19). However, response rates for several individual regimens should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of treated patients.

Eight patients received second‐line therapy. Cladribine (n = 3) resulted in partial response (n = 2) and stable disease (n = 1), with reported side effects of cytopenias (n = 1), nausea (n = 1), and pulmonary embolism (n = 1). The overall response rate for cladribine‐treated patients was 67% (n = 2/3). Vemurafenib (n = 3) produced partial responses in all cases, yielding an overall response rate of 100% (n = 3/3), with arthralgias reported in one patient. Other second‐line regimens included hydroxyurea (n = 1) and the LCH3 protocol (n = 1) for a patient who ultimately received LCH3 a total of three times due to two recurrences. The overall response rate across all second‐line regimens was 63% (n = 5/8).

Five patients received third‐line therapy, and three received a fourth‐line regimen. Given the very small numbers treated with each regimen, response estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Third‐line treatments included trametinib (complete response, n = 1), clofarabine (partial response, n = 1), vemurafenib (partial response, n = 1), LCH3 protocol (complete response, n = 1), and hydroxyurea (partial response, n = 1). The overall response rate for third‐line therapies was 100% (n = 5/5). Fourth‐line therapies included cobimetinib (complete response, n = 1), trametinib (partial response, n = 1), and tocilizumab (complete response, n = 1), resulting in an overall response rate of 100% (n = 3/3).

Long‐term outcomes were heterogeneous. At the time of data cutoff, each patient was assigned to a single mutually exclusive final disease‐status category based on the most recent clinical and radiologic assessment. Final disease status was classified as complete response/remission (n = 9), active/progressive disease (n = 9), stable disease (n = 8), deceased (n = 3), or under investigation (n = 2). Five patients underwent surgical resection as their only major treatment modality, with two experiencing no recurrence. Three patients (cases 1, 11, 22) died during follow‐up: one from complications of multiorgan failure after a surgery unrelated to LCH, one from progressive disease, and one from acute myeloid leukemia.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 31 adult patients with histologically confirmed LCH from a Canadian rare disease program, we observed considerable heterogeneity in disease presentation, organ involvement, histopathologic features, and treatment response. This study adds to the limited Canadian literature on adult LCH by providing detailed real‐world data from a multicenter tertiary‐care cohort, including contemporary diagnostic evaluation, molecular profiling, treatment patterns, and long‐term clinical outcomes. Our cohort reflects several patterns consistent with previously published literature, while also highlighting features relevant to the contemporary management of LCH within a specialized rare disease program in a real‐world Canadian healthcare setting.

The proportion of patients with multisystem disease (58%) aligns with previously published adult LCH cohorts, where multisystem involvement was reported in 50%–70% of cases [19, 20]. Bone was the most commonly involved site (74%), followed by the lungs (29%) and skin (16%). The high prevalence of bone lesions is aligned with both pediatric and adult series, where osseous involvement is considered a hallmark of LCH [21, 22, 23].

Histopathologic and molecular profiling revealed BRAF V600E mutations in 44% of tested patients, consistent with prior reports of 50%–60% prevalence [24, 25]. One patient (Case 9) harbored a MAP2K1 mutation, specifically a rare exon 3 in‐frame deletion (c. E102_I103del), which was recently described by as a RAF‐independent class III MEK1 alteration that drives aggressive histiocytic neoplasms [26]. Emerging evidence indicates that RAF‐independent MEK1 mutations such as E102_I103del frequently demonstrate primary resistance to MEK inhibition and may require alternative targeted approaches (e.g., ERK inhibition) [26]. The diversity of molecular alterations supports the use of next‐generation sequencing in adult LCH, both to inform prognosis and to guide consideration of targeted therapies. In our cohort, targeted agents, including BRAF and MEK inhibitors, achieved partial or complete responses in relapsed or refractory disease [16, 27].

One limitation in our cohort is the incomplete availability of molecular data. Only 13 of 31 patients (42%) underwent next‐generation sequencing, leaving 18 patients without known MAPK pathway mutation status. In the absence of mutation data, treatment decisions may be less precise and opportunities for targeted therapy may be missed. This finding reflects the need to advocate for access to molecular testing for patients with rare diseases such as histiocyte disorders.

We observed some overlap between LCH and other malignancies, with 19% of patients having a prior or subsequent neoplastic diagnosis, including myeloid malignancies. The association between LCH and hematologic cancers, particularly myeloid neoplasms, is increasingly recognized and may relate to shared clonal origins or underlying MAPK pathway dysregulation [28, 29]. This finding emphasizes the need for vigilance in surveillance for LCH patients.

Our treatment data also reflects the variability in therapeutic approaches for LCH. While cytarabine was the most common first‐line systemic agent in our series, other centers have reported cladribine, vinblastine plus prednisone, and others as primary therapy in adults [1, 30, 31]. High‐dose cytarabine and cytarabine combinations can be effective in aggressive or CNS‐involved disease but are associated with significant toxicity [1, 10, 12]. The heterogeneity in first‐line and salvage regimens in our cohort highlights the absence of a standardized treatment protocol for adult LCH and the need for multicenter consensus guidelines adapted to adult patients.

Our findings can be compared with a recent single‐center Canadian series from the Princess Margaret Cancer Centre (PMCC) in Toronto, Ontario, which reported real‐world outcomes across 60 LCH patients [32]. In the PMCC cohort, the median age for LCH (42.5 years) and the predominance of bone (40%) and lung (38%) involvement were similar to our series. However, they reported a higher proportion of single‐system disease (57% single‐system, 43% multisystem), than in our cohort (23% single‐system unifocal, 13% single‐system multifocal, 58% multisystem). The prevalence of BRAF V600E mutations in LCH was numerically lower in their cohort (28% of tested patients) than in ours (44%). Both studies also documented an association with other malignancies: concomitant hematologic neoplasms were present in 5% and prior solid tumors in 13% of LCH patients in the PMCC cohort, compaed to 19% in our cohort.

Strengths of the present study include its multicenter Canadian cohort of adults with LCH, detailed clinical, radiologic, histopathologic, and molecular characterization, and long‐term follow‐up capturing a range of disease severities and treatment strategies. Limitations include the retrospective design, relatively small sample size, heterogeneity in diagnostic testing and treatment protocols, and the potential for selection bias, as patients referred to a specialized rare disease program may have more complex, refractory, or multisystem disease than those managed in other settings. Molecular data was not available for all patients, and longitudinal data on quality of life, symptom burden, and functional outcomes were lacking. Future directions include prospective registry‐based studies, broader access to molecular profiling and targeted therapies, and exploration of LCH pathogenesis in patients with concurrent or subsequent hematologic malignancies.

Conclusion

This retrospective series of adult LCH patients in Canada demonstrated heterogeneity in presentation, molecular profile, and treatment response, with patterns largely consistent with international data. By drawing on patients referred from multiple provinces and at varying stages of disease, this study reflects the “real‐world” clinical spectrum and management of adult LCH within a specialized rare disease program. Given the rarity of adult LCH, future prospective, multicenter studies and national registries are needed to refine diagnostic pathways and standardize treatment approaches. Including molecular profiling in adult care pathways may improve treatment selection and help clarify associations with myeloid neoplasms.
