Pandemic preparedness of effective vaccines for the outbreak of newly H5N1 highly pathogenic avian influenza virus
Authors: Feixia Gao, Qi Wang, Chenchen Qiu, Jian Luo, Xiuling Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Analyzed the outbreak situation and viral characteristics of the newly H5N1 highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) virus.•The current approval and research and development of the H5N1 HPAI vaccines were summarized.•Proposed vaccine development approaches against newly H5N1 virus, e.g. adjuvanted vaccine, mRNA vaccine, multivalent vaccine.•Discussed other prevention and control strategies, e.g. poultry vaccination, global surveillance and comprehensive testing.

Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) viruses such as H5N1, H5N8, H5N6 and H5N2 have been circulating worldwide, posing serious threats to both poultry farming and public health. Notably, the H5N1, H5N6 and H7N9 HPAI viruses have caused significant mortality in China. Among these, the H5N1 HPAI virus is of great concern due to its impact on global public health (Zhu et al., 2022). Since the first occurrence of H5N1 HPAI virus human infection in 1997, it has manifested a sporadic epidemic pattern in numerous regions across the globe. Thereafter, 925 cases of human infection with different clades of H5N1 HPAI viruses have been documented, with a cumulative case fatality rate exceeding 50%, highlighting the severe public health threat posed by the H5N1 HPAI viruses (https://www.cdc.gov/bird-flu/php/technical-report/h5n1-06052024.html?CDC_AAref_Val=https://www.cdc.gov/flu/avianflu/spotlights/2023-2024/h5n1-technical-report-06052024.htm). Recently, newly H5N1 HPAI viruses were detected on dairy cattle and poultry farms in the United States (US), and spread to herds across fifteen states, resulting in at least nine confirmed human infections during April and September 2024. Subsequently, these viruses also caused many human infections in Asia (e.g. Cambodia and China). This epidemic marks the first confirmed case of the H5N1 HPAI virus being transmitted between humans and other mammalian species, deviating from prior cases involving avian-to-human transmission.

H5 HPAI viruses have undergone extensive genetic diversification, evolving into ten distinct clades, numbered 0 to 9. Emerging in 2003, clade 2 strains have become the predominant strain in circulation and have further evolved into various subclades, including 2.2.1, 2.2.1.1, 2.2.1.2, 2.3.2.1, and 2.3.4.4, among others. The clade 2.3.4.4 was further divided into eight subclades, namely clades 2.3.4.4a to 2.3.4.4h. Particularly, the novel H5N1 HPAI viruses of 2.3.4.4b clade evolved from the previously prevalent lineage of H5Nx virus and have been in circulation from 2021 to the present (Fig. 1A). Since the beginning of 2021, World Health Organization (WHO) has recorded 44 cases of human infection with H5N1 HPAI virus, 18 of which were caused by clade 2.3.4.4b viruses (https://www.cdc.gov/bird-flu/php/technical-report/h5n1-06052024.html?CDC_AAref_Val=https://www.cdc.gov/flu/avianflu/spotlights/2023-2024/h5n1-technical-report-06052024.htm). Of note, the recent cases of human infection with the H5N1 HPAI in the US were associated with clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 HPAI viruses, genotype B3.13 (Uyeki et al., 2024). This genotype comprises PA, HA, NA and M segments derived from HPAI H5N1 strains found in Eurasian wild birds, as well as PB2, PB1, NP and NS segments originating from low pathogenic avian influenza strains in American wild birds (Uyeki et al., 2024) (Fig. 1B). Sequence analysis revealed that the proteolytic cleavage site of the HA gene contains RRKR|GLF, which is the characteristic of high pathogenicity. In addition, the HA gene segment contains 137A, 158 ​N, and 160A mutations that increase the viruses’ receptor-binding affinity for human (Sakai-Tagawa et al., 2006). Besides HA-receptor binding, the E627K mutation in the PB2 gene, a key mammalian-adapting molecular marker, was acquired in the first human case (A/Texas/37/2024). However, this mutation was absent in the second case (A/Michigan/90/2024), where a PB2 M631L substitution was observed. The M631L mutation is also associated with viral adaptation to mammalian hosts and has been detected in 99% of dairy cattle infected with the H5N1 HPAI virus. Further alignment of internal influenza genes identified the mutations 30D, 43 ​M and 215A in the M1 gene, as well as 42S, 103F and 106 ​M in the NS1 gene, which may contribute to enhanced virulence in mammals (Jiao et al., 2008; Fan et al., 2009; Alasiri et al., 2023) (Fig. 1C). Consequently, although the H5N1 HPAI virus has not yet acquired the capacity for sustained human-to-human transmission, it is evident that the virus is adapting to mammalian hosts. Fortunately, no mutations conferring resistance to antiviral drugs were detected at positions R293, H275 and I223 in the NA gene of the novel emerging H5N1 HPAI viruses, indicating that these strains remain susceptible to antiviral agents, such as oseltamivir.

On July 30, 2024, the WHO reported that the number of pathogens capable of triggering the next pandemic has risen to over 30, with the H5 HPAI virus be regarded as one of the high-risk pathogens (https://www.who.int/publications/m/item/pathogens-prioritization-a-scientific-framework-for-epidemic-and-pandemic-research-preparedness). Given the uncertainty of H5N1 HPAI virus evolution and mutation, it is crucial to make preparations for the prevention and control of the newly emerging H5N1 HPAI viruses. A series of measures are recommended to effectively manage the spread and mitigate impact of the H5N1 HPAI viruses as follows: (1) Avoid unprotected exposures to animals with suspected or confirmed H5N1 HPAI virus infection; (2) ensure that poultry farmers, livestock workers, veterinarians, and emergency responders avoid direct physical contact with animals infected with H5N1 HPAI viruses; (3) clinicians should consider the possibility of H5N1 HPAI virus infection in individuals with relevant exposure histories; (4) prompt antiviral treatment with oseltamivir is recommended; and (5) although no human vaccines are currently available for prevention, vaccination remains crucial for mitigating public health risks.

It is noteworthy that vaccination against H5N1 HPAI viruses possesses considerable potential to mitigate the impact of infection at the public health level. Since the continual outbreak of human infections with H5 HPAI viruses from 2003, organizations all over the world have focused their efforts on developing vaccines against the viruses. Thus far, fourteen H5N1 HPAI vaccines have been approved for human use, mainly comprising whole-virion inactivated vaccines, split-virion inactivated vaccines, one subunit inactivated vaccine, and one live attenuated influenza vaccine (Table 1). Certain adjuvants, including MF59, AS03, and aluminum, have also been incorporated to these vaccines to enhance their immunogenicity and efficacy. Notably, some commercial vaccines have shown variable reactivity against earlier outbreak H5N1 HPAI viruses. However, antibody reactivity has diminished over time due to viral evolution (Kayali et al., 2016). The genetic divergence and limited cross-reactivity between existing vaccines and the currently circulating clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 HPAI viruses indicate that the vaccines targeting previous H5N1 HPAI strains are currently ineffective in the field. Specifically, a recent assessment revealed that cross-neutralizing antibody responses against the circulating 2.3.4.4b H5N1 viruses were elicited following vaccination with MF59 or AS03-adjuvanted licensed vaccines. However, it remains unknown whether protection against the circulating H5N1 HPAI infection is afforded (Khurana et al., 2024). Given the emergence and widespread circulation of clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 HPAI virus in birds and mammals, coupled with the potential for human infection, the development of a dedicated vaccine against clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 HPAI viruses is crucial for pandemic preparedness and public health.

Due to the continuous variation of the H5N1 HPAI viruses, it should be noted the WHO has been updating the development of H5 influenza candidate vaccine viruses (CVVs) for pandemic preparedness. Particularly, three of these mentioned CCVs [A/Astrakhan/3212/2020 (IDCDC-RG71A), A/American wigeon/South Carolina/22-000345-001/2021 (IDCDC-RG78A), and A/Ezo red fox/Hokkaido/1/2022 (NIID-002)] exhibited a close phylogenetic relationship with the first clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1-infected human case (A/Texas/37/2024) reported in the US in 2024 (Uyeki et al., 2024). Furthermore, amino acid sequence alignment of the HA genes revealed high homology between A/Texas/37/2024 and these CVVs, with four amino acid substitutions (L104 ​M, L115Q, T195I, V210A) in the HA1 compared to A/Astrakhan/3212/2020 and A/Ezo red fox/Hokkaido/1/2022 CVVs, and two substitutions (L115Q, T195I) compared to A/American wigeon/South Carolina/22-000345-001/2021 CVV (Fig. 1D). However, these substitutions were not in major antigenic epitopes, highlighting that the vaccinations with A/Astrakhan/3212/2020, A/American wigeon/South Carolina/22-000345-001/2021, or A/Ezo red fox/Hokkaido/1/2022 strains may elicit cross-reactivity and offer protection against the currently circulating H5N1 HPAI viruses. Of note, our previous report demonstrated that the inactivated vaccine based on A/Astrakhan/3212/2020 virus formulated with Addavax adjuvant could induce cross-hemagglutination inhibition (HI) antibodies against H5N8, H5N6 (clade 2.3.4.4d) and H5N1 (clade 1) strains, whereas the Al(OH)3-adjuvanted inactivated vaccine based on A/Vietnam/1194/2004 H5N1 virus (NIBRG-14, clade 1) could not provide cross-HI antibodies against H5N8 and H5N6 strains (Gao et al., 2022). Moreover, post-infection ferret antiserum raised against A/Astrakhan/3212/2020 produced an HI titer of 80 against A/Texas/37/2024, while the antiserum raised against A/American wigeon/South Carolina/22-000345-001/2021 produced a titer of 160 (Uyeki et al., 2024). Taken together, these findings suggest that CVVs (IDCDC-RG71A, IDCDC-RG78A, and NIID-002) are promising vaccine candidates against the current circulating clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 HPAI viruses and have potential for further development. As anticipated, adjuvanted formulations elicited a significantly greater increase in the breadth of antibody responses of the HPAI vaccine. Clinical trials are currently assessing the safety and immunogenicity of influenza A/Astrakhan/3212/2020–like virus vaccines formulated with different adjuvants (MF59 and AS03) stockpiled under the National Pre-Pandemic Influenza Vaccine Stockpile program (NCT05874713 and NCT05975840), along with an A/H5 inactivated monovalent influenza vaccine at different antigen dose levels adjuvanted with AS03 or MF59 (NCT06560151). Recently, two H5N1 HPAI influenza vaccines containing A/Astrakhan/3212/2020 (H5N8)-like strain, which are adjuvanted with MF59 and aluminum, respectively, have been approved in Europe and Australia for human use to protect against flu caused by influenza H5 virus in October 2023.

In addition to traditional inactivated vaccines, several innovative vaccine platforms demonstrate significant potential for pandemic preparedness. These platforms include viral vector vaccines, nucleic acid-based vaccines, virus-like particle vaccines, and particularly the mRNA-based vaccines, which possess apparent advantages of rapid response, swift production and induction of comprehensive immune response compared to the traditional inactivated vaccine approach. Due to the exceptional flexibility of the mRNA vaccine platform in terms of ease and speed of updating each vaccine component, it is extremely suitable for enhancing pandemic preparedness for the outbreak of HPAI virus. An mRNA vaccine against clade 2.3.4.4b H5 HPAI virus (A/Astrakhan/3212/2020) has been shown to elicit neutralizing antibodies and HA-specific CD8+ T cell responses in mice, providing protection against clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 HPAI virus in ferrets (Furey et al., 2024), highlighting its potential impact on future vaccine development. Recently, another mRNA vaccine targeting the newly H5 HPAI virus, along with a self-amplifying mRNA pandemic influenza (H5N1) vaccine, is presently in clinical trial (NCT06382311 and NCT06602531). Considering the persistent public health threat posed by the newly H5N1 HPAI viruses and their pandemic potential, scientists or vaccine manufacturers in developed or underdeveloped countries should accelerate the development of vaccines against clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 HPAI viruses.

Besides H5N1 HPAI vaccines for human use, the efforts on poultry vaccination are crucial for controlling avian influenza at its source. Previous reports demonstrated the H5/H7 bivalent inactivated vaccine was applied in poultry, which largely prevented the prevalence of H7N9 virus in poultry and human in China (Shi et al., 2023). Moreover, the novel H5/H7 trivalent vaccine in poultry could provide solid protection against challenge with (clade 2.3.4.4b/h) H5N1, H5N6, H5N8, and H7N9 viruses (Zeng et al., 2022). Specially, the H5-Re11 ​+ ​3 vaccine targeting clade 2.3.4.4h virus reacted well with viruses in all eight subclades (clade 2.3.4.4a-h), indicating its potential to serve as a broad-spectrum vaccine candidate against H5 viruses belonging to clade 2.3.4.4 (Zhang et al., 2024). Consequently, the development of poultry vaccines could offer another strategy to curb H5 virus transmission and reduce the spillover risk to humans.

Despite the overall low risk to individual and population health posed by the currently circulating H5N1 HPAI viruses, the widespread distribution of infected birds and poultry, along with ongoing viral evolution, increases the likelihood of human exposure. The next flu pandemic is not a matter of “If”, but “When and How”. Enhanced global surveillance and comprehensive testing through international collaboration to monitor the evolution and geographic spread of H5N1 HPAI viruses could provide timely and updated risk assessments. The development of effective vaccines using innovative approaches enhances public health preparedness. mRNA technology offers a rapid-response option for developing an H5-specific vaccine against H5N1 HPAI virus infection. Concurrently, combining novel adjuvants with traditional inactivated vaccines to enhance immunogenicity represents a promising alternative approach. Additionally, considering the epidemic characteristics of H5 HPAI viruses in China (mostly H5N6 HPAI viruses in prevalence), the development of a multivalent vaccine can also be regarded as one of the strategies. Furthermore, poultry vaccination programs can be implemented to control the spread of avian influenza viruses, leveraging the strategies and lessons from China.

Overall, aiming to better deal with the potential epidemic of H5 HPAI virus, countries and organizations should strengthen cooperation and formulate various prevention and control strategies, especially the preparation of effective vaccines.
