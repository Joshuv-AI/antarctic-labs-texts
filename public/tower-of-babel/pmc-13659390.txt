Single‐Operator Stress Ultrasound Evaluation of Ulnohumeral Joint Instability Related to Medial Ulnar Collateral Ligament Injury
Authors: Benjamin F Kopecky, Brennan J Boettcher, Christopher L Camp, Jacob L Sellon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article introduces a novel, single‐operator stress ultrasound technique for evaluating medial ulnar collateral ligament (MUCL) integrity by assessing medial ulnohumeral joint (MUHJ) widening under valgus stress. Unlike prior methods requiring dual operators, specialized equipment, or the use of hand/wrist weights, this approach was designed for clinical efficiency, reproducibility, and patient comfort. By positioning the patient comfortably in a side‐lying position with the elbow flexed to 50°, MUHJ width is measured at baseline (maximally apposed) and manually stressed positions, in a manner that may reduce muscular compensation, avoid viscoelastic creep, and facilitate consistency. While additional research is needed to evaluate clinical feasibility, assess reliability, and compare our technique with previously published methods of dynamic MUCL assessment, the aim of this manuscript is to describe our technique and provide a rationale for its potential utility in musculoskeletal ultrasound practice and research.Keywords: medial ulnar collateral ligament, musculoskeletal ultrasound, sports medicine, stress ultrasonography

Ultrasound (US) imaging is a useful tool in the evaluation of elbow medial ulnar collateral ligament (MUCL) injuries and enables assessment of MUCL function with dynamic visualization of MUHJ widening under valgus stress. MUCL stress ultrasound (SUS) has been described with a variety of techniques, primarily with the use of special equipment (eg, Telos) 1 , 2 or dual operators 3 , 4 that may not be practical in a busy sports medicine practice. Single‐operator techniques that have been described use hand/wrist weights and/or positioning that may be uncomfortable for the patient or introduce shoulder external rotation rather than isolating valgus elbow stress. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Furthermore, continuous load application has been demonstrated to introduce “creep” into the MUHJ gapping. 9 In our practice we have refined a single‐operator MUCL SUS technique that we find to be practical, reproducible, and well‐tolerated in patients, many of whom are overhead athletes with shoulder hyperlaxity. This SUS technique is intended to be used for diagnostic assessment of MUCL laxity with the option of side‐to‐side comparison as indicated. Its practicality makes it potentially suitable for serial exams in the course of treatment or research protocols. We have previously described the significant limitations of the MUCL SUS literature and aim to standardize a practical technique for future research that will be more generalizable. 10 This SUS technique requires only 1 operator and, without additional/expensive equipment, minimizes shoulder motion, is comfortable for the patient, and limits “creep” by applying valgus force in an intermittent instead of continuous manner. Here we present a detailed description of our single‐operator MUCL SUS technique.

Technique

Patient Setup

The patient lies in the lateral recumbent position with the study arm down and the shoulder positioned at approximately 45° of flexion. The torso is placed in a direct side‐lying position, with the shoulders in a vertically “stacked” position. The elbow and forearm are positioned beyond the bed so that the edge of the exam table is just proximal to the elbow joint, creating a fulcrum to stress the MUHJ (Figure 1A). In comparison with the supine position described previously, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 11 , 12 we have found that stacking the shoulders limits shoulder external rotation during the SUS exam, which stabilizes the upper limb to better isolate stress on the elbow. In addition, this position avoids potentially sensitive shoulder external rotation/abduction and resultant compensatory shoulder and forearm muscle contraction. The elbow is placed in 50° of flexion using a goniometer for confirmation (Figure 1A). Many of the prior studies have utilized 30° of elbow flexion for MUCL SUS testing, as it is the point at which the olecranon typically disengages from the humeral fossa and the MUCL becomes the primary valgus stabilizer. 2 , 3 , 5 , 13 , 14 Others have used 90° of elbow flexion because this mimics the typical elbow position in the overhead throwing motion. 4 , 15 , 16 However, based on our group's previous work examining the position of maximum elbow instability in cadaveric MUCL deficient elbows, we recommend positioning the elbow at 50° of flexion. 17 Additionally, we find that using a thinly padded exam table is preferred as it provides adequate comfort, while allowing for less arm movement variability during SUS testing.

Operator Setup

The operator is seated facing the patient. Clinically, the patient's wrist is held by the hand not holding the US transducer (eg, for the right elbow, the operator's right hand would hold the US transducer, and the left hand would impart force on the patient's wrist). We find that positioning the wrist in a neutral to slightly supinated position better isolates the MUHJ, limiting forearm pronation during the stress test (Figure 1B). The US screen is placed into the “split screen” mode, where a baseline view can be obtained on 1 side of the screen and the stress view on the other for direct comparison. The operator positions the transducer over the MUHJ in long axis to the forearm, while focusing on the bony landmarks of the MUHJ (Figure 2). This process is described in Video S1.

Ultrasound Measurements

There are 2 measurements observed, the baseline MUHJ width (UHJb) and the stress MUHJ width (UHJs). The UHJb is measured by first visualizing the joint space under US and then applying a small varus force to the elbow (lifting up the wrist) until the MUHJ joint space is in maximal apposition. As both hands are in use (1 applying force and the other positioning the transducer), a second unskilled person may be needed to freeze the still image and activate the other side of the split screen, though this can also be accomplished alone with a foot pedal or voice activation, which are features available on many US machines (see Video S1). The UHJb is measured on this single still image between 2 clear points on the joint margin cortices (Figure 2). We chose a position of apposition for baseline measurement after noting that the stress of gravity alone in the side‐lying position can cause significant MUHJ gapping in some patients, especially in the setting of MUCL injury or laxity as frequently observed in throwing arms.

After obtaining the baseline measurement, the operator creates a valgus elbow stress by applying gradually increasing force to the ulnar side of the wrist. Again, the MUHJ margins are visualized dynamically under US, and a still image is captured when maximum MUHJ widening is reached. It is important to pay attention to the bony and soft tissue landmarks during the stress application to ensure they are similar to the baseline image. The split screen option facilitates matching the landmarks of the baseline and stress images. We prefer this method over saving still images from a cine clip, where inadvertent transducer glide may be introduced, thereby changing the baseline to stress measurement locations. In addition, we prefer manual stress instead of hanging a weight from the patient's wrist to better tailor the stress to patient tolerance and the visualized joint gapping. Using the electronic calipers, measurements are taken from the same points on the MUHJ margins (Figure 2) in both baseline and stress images to obtain the UHJb and UHJs, respectively. Measurements are typically performed on a single matched pair of baseline and stress images, though repeat image acquisitions may be performed at the operator's discretion to ensure consistency. This process can be repeated on the contralateral side in both baseline and stress conditions.

Once the UHJb and UHJs measurements are recorded, the MUHJ stress delta (SD), which represents the joint gapping distance, is calculated by subtracting the UHJb from the UHJs. The stress delta difference (SDD), representing the side‐to‐side difference in MUHJ gapping distance, is the difference between the injured (or throwing) arm SD and uninjured arm SD.

Discussion

We have found this single‐operator SUS technique to be reproducible, comfortable for patients, and practical for use in a busy clinic with limited staff support, though we acknowledge this technique has not been formally validated or compared with other SUS methods. A comparison of key technical features between previously described protocols and the proposed technique is provided in Table 1. Our protocol places the shoulder at a more neutral position of 45° flexion and 0° external rotation compared to the 90°/90° abducted and externally rotated position used in other protocols. 4 , 5 We have found this less extreme position of the arm mitigates muscle contraction and promotes patient comfort during stress testing. Additionally, our protocol places the elbow at 50° flexion given our group's cadaveric study suggesting that this is the point of maximal laxity in MUCL deficient elbows. 17 In contrast, prior studies on SUS techniques have typically utilized elbow flexion angles of 30° 2 , 5 , 18 or 90°, 4 , 15 , 16 which may limit direct comparability with the present technique, particularly in the interpretation of laxity thresholds (eg, SD and SDD).

In this technique, MUHJ baseline measurements are taken with the joint in maximal apposition by applying a small varus force to the elbow (achieved by slightly lifting the wrist until the MUHJ is closed maximally). In contrast, the Bica et al and Shanley et al SUS techniques used a MUHJ baseline measurement with gravity imparting a valgus force. We have found that gravity alone can cause significant MUHJ opening, especially in MUCL injuries or in overhead athletes with MUCL laxity. This is demonstrated in Video S1. This observation is supported by Colberg et al, who demonstrated in 50 asymptomatic collegiate baseball athletes that the throwing arm exhibited a significantly greater baseline joint space (UHJb) than the nonthrowing arm when measured with the valgus stress effect of gravity, while UHJb measured in varus stress showed no such side‐to‐side difference. Their findings suggest that a varus stress baseline may more accurately reflect true resting joint apposition and strengthens the rationale for incorporating varus stress as the baseline measurement in MUCL SUS techniques. For MUCL SUS techniques that have potential for the weight of the forearm/hand to impart valgus stress, if this gravity is not counteracted during the baseline measurement, the UHJb may be increased, and consequently, SD (joint gapping) may be artificially decreased. In theory, a more reproducible baseline measurement may be achieved by placing the joint in maximal apposition with a gentle varus arm lift before obtaining the baseline measurement.

Lastly, the technique described here uses manual force application as opposed to a hanging wrist weight 5 , 12 , 19 or weight placed in the patient's hand 7 , 20 for the elbow valgus stress. We have found the advantage of stabilizing the forearm with a direct grip on the wrist allows for more stable US visualization and better operator “end‐feel” of maximal MUHJ widening. This facilitates a gradual application of increasing valgus stress that may reduce flexor muscle contraction, and the force can be adjusted to accommodate patient comfort and maximal joint gapping. Furthermore, this intermittent valgus stress also minimizes the “creep” effect that can occur with continual valgus stress force across the MUHJ. 9 However, the use of manual stress makes standardization of force applied during the SUS exam cumbersome, though a handheld dynamometer can be used to quantify the force if desired. Variable force application is a potential limitation of this method when compared to those that utilize a hand/wrist weight or place the elbow in a stress device, such as the Telos.

This technique is subject to several other limitations, including dependence on a skilled US operator for both transducer positioning and stress application, variability related to patient factors such as body habitus (eg, more challenging arm manipulation and visualization in larger patients), guarding due to positioning discomfort (inherent to many SUS techniques), baseline ligamentous laxity (mitigated with side‐to‐side comparison/SDD in this technique), and differences in US machine quality and features.

Conclusion

The single‐operator MUCL SUS technique described in this article may offer multiple potential advantages over previously described techniques, including practicality in a busy clinic, minimal staff and equipment requirements, patient comfort, and reproducibility. While we encourage clinicians and sonographers to explore this technique, we acknowledge that additional investigation is needed to assess clinical utility, patient comfort, inter‐ and intra‐rater reliability, and comparison with previously described MUCL SUS techniques.
