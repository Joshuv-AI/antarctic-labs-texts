Psychosocial safety climate and coping among healthcare workers experiencing workplace violence in public hospitals in Terengganu, Malaysia: a mixed-method study protocol
Authors: Mohd Hazim Ahmad Fuad, Ely Zarina Samsudin, Nik Nairan Abdullah, Ahmad Nazim Aimran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWorkplace violence (WPV) is a significant occupational hazard in healthcare, negatively impacting healthcare workers’ (HCWs) mental health and quality of care. Psychosocial safety climate (PSC), an organisational factor emphasising psychological well-being, may influence how HCWs perceive, experience and cope with WPV. However, its role in shaping HCWs’ coping strategies remains underexplored. This study aims to examine the relationship between PSC and coping mechanisms among HCWs experiencing WPV in public hospitals.Methods and analysisThis explanatory sequential mixed-method study comprises two phases. In Phase 1, a multicentre cross-sectional survey will be conducted among at least 440 HCWs working in Malaysian public hospitals that are selected through multistage random sampling. PSC, WPV exposure and coping mechanisms will be assessed using validated tools, including the Psychosocial Safety Climate (PSC-12) scale and the Brief Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced (Brief COPE) questionnaire. Linear regression will examine the association between PSC and coping mechanisms among HCWs exposed to WPV. In Phase 2, 6–12 HCWs with WPV experience in the past year and employed in high-PSC departments will be selected via criterion sampling with maximum variation for in-depth interviews. A semistructured interview guide based on the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping will be developed and pilot tested. Data will undergo thematic analysis until saturation is reached, identifying key themes on how PSC influences coping. Findings from both phases will be integrated using joint display analysis to inform PSC-driven interventions that promote coping and reduce WPV impacts among HCWs.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Ministry of Health Medical Research and Ethics Committees and Universiti Teknologi MARA Research Ethics Committee. Informed, written consent will be obtained from all participants. Findings will be disseminated to the Ministry of Health and through peer-reviewed publications.

Introduction

Workplace violence (WPV), defined as ‘any incident in which an employee is abused, threatened or assaulted in the circumstances related to their work’,1 is a significant problem in the healthcare sector. Previous meta-analyses indicate that globally, 62% of healthcare workers (HCWs) have experienced some form of WPV, including verbal abuse and physical violence.2 This is concerning due to the detrimental effects of WPV on both HCWs and healthcare organisations. Evidence suggests that HCWs exposed to WPV experience physical and psychological ill health, impaired work functioning and strained social relationships.3 4 In addition, WPV has been linked to increased rates of absenteeism, staff turnover and decline in the quality of patient care.4

Recognising the substantial impact of WPV on workers’ safety and health, various workplace interventions have been introduced to address WPV in healthcare settings. This includes various interventions at the individual level, relationship level, workplace level and societal level.5 One of the individual-level WPV interventions focusing on personal risk reduction is coping skills training modules.6 Coping, that is, the cognitive and behavioural efforts to manage, minimise or endure the internal and external demands of stressful events,7 has been identified as a key protective factor in mitigating the negative effects of WPV on HCWs. Previous studies suggest that HCWs’ coping mechanisms can moderate the impact of WPV, with positive coping strategies linked to lower anxiety levels and negative coping strategies with higher anxiety levels.8

Meanwhile, workplace-level WPV interventions address the larger context in which negative interactions occur and include multifaceted approaches that may incorporate a zero-tolerance policy, risk assessment, education, surveillance and programme evaluation.5 Despite this, a recent meta-analysis evaluating the effectiveness of WPV interventions concluded that the level of evidence for the available WPV interventions was not high.9 In recent years, psychosocial safety climate (PSC), that is, the shared perceptions of an organisation’s commitment to protecting its employees’ psychological well-being and safety, has emerged as an organisational factor with the potential to improve the occupational safety and health (OSH) of workplaces.10 11 Evidence suggests that a high-PSC work environment can not only moderate the impact of high job demands, enhance the effectiveness of job resources and affect social relations at work including WPV,11 12 it can also influence the coping strategies workers adopt in response to job stressors such as workplace bullying.13

Despite the potential role of PSC in influencing how workers perceive, experience and cope with WPV, there is presently limited understanding of how PSC affects workers’ coping with WPV. This is because while PSC is acknowledged for its role in enhancing workplace OSH, prior studies examining the link between PSC and coping have primarily focused on other forms of workplace deviant behaviours, such as workplace bullying.14 Therefore, this study aims to address the aforementioned research gap by using a mixed methods design to explore how PSC influence the coping mechanisms of HCWs experiencing WPV. The findings from this study are expected to provide insight into PSC-driven WPV prevention strategies that may enhance HCWs’ resilience and mitigate WPV.

Aims

The study aims to: (1) assess PSC levels, WPV prevalence and HCWs’ coping mechanisms in public hospitals, (2) determine the association between PSC and coping mechanisms among HCWs experiencing WPV and (3) explore HCWs’ perception of PSC in public hospitals and its influence on coping mechanisms.

Methods and analysis

Study design and setting

This study will be a two-phase, explanatory sequential mixed-methods design (figure 1) undertaken in Terengganu public hospitals. Terengganu is a state located within the east coast of Peninsular Malaysia with six public hospitals that serve a populace of 1.2 million.15 Terengganu was selected as the study setting as data from the Ministry of Health Malaysia indicated that Terengganu hospitals had among the highest rate of WPV compared with other states.16 Phase 1 involves a multicentre cross-sectional survey to assess PSC levels, WPV and HCWs’ coping mechanisms using validated self-administered questionnaires. Phase 2 comprises in-depth interviews (IDIs) to explore HCWs’ perception of PSC and how it influences coping mechanisms. Phase 1 is expected to be conducted from December 2024 to May 2025, while phase 2 is expected to be carried out from June to September 2025.

Study population and sampling mechanism

HCWs will be recruited from three public hospitals in Terengganu, Malaysia. The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) clinical staff (doctors, nurses, allied health professionals) and non-clinical staff (administrative personnel, support workers) who interact with patients and the general public as part of their job roles, (2) worked for at least 1 year in the hospital and (3) able to read, understand and write in the Malay language. HCWs on medical, maternity or any other long-term leave at the time of data collection and those refusing to participate in the study will be excluded.

A multistage random sampling approach will be used to select participants. In the first stage, three public hospitals in Terengganu will be selected via simple random sampling using a random number generator. In the second stage, HCWs will be selected from each hospital via proportionate stratified random sampling, whereby the strata will be hospital departments involved in patient care. The rationale for this was to ensure adequate representation of each clinical and clinical support department to accurately reflect the diversity and structure of a public hospital. The sampling fraction will be determined for each department, and eligible HCWs will be randomly selected from each department according to the respective sampling fraction using a random number generator. As proportionate stratified sampling yields approximately equal selection probabilities for all HCWs across strata, the survey design is considered self-weighting, that is, each participant represents an equal portion of the target population.17 18 Accordingly, sampling weights were not applied.

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation was calculated using OpenEpi, V.3 based on the single proportion formula. Based on an estimated HCW population in Terengganu public hospitals of 998319, proportion of 0.71320, significance level of 0.05 and design effect of 1.2 to account for clustering effects21, the required sample size is 366 participants. Considering an anticipated 20% non-response rate, the minimum number of HCWs to be sampled is 440.

Study instrument and study variables

Validated self-administered questionnaires comprising a section on respondents’ characteristics, Malay-validated Psychosocial Safety Climate (PSC-12) scale,22 Malay-validated WPV tool23 and Malay-validated Brief Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced (Brief-COPE) questionnaire24 will be used to measure sociodemographic and work characteristics, PSC, WPV and HCWs’ coping mechanisms respectively.

The Malay-validated PSC-12 is based on the PSC-12 developed by Hall et al25 and comprises 12 items that assess four key PSC domains, that is, management commitment, priority to psychological health, organisational communication and organisational participation and involvement. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher total scores indicating higher PSC levels. According to the authors, it has satisfactory psychometric properties with confirmatory factor analysis confirming a four-factor structure and Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95.22 Departments with PSC-12 scores of 41 and above will be defined as high-PSC departments, whereas those with PSC-12 scores of less than 41 will be defined as low-PSC departments.26

The Malay-validated WPV tool is adapted and validated by Sahiran et al from the WPV tool developed by the Joint Committee of the International Labour Office, the International Council of Nurses, the WHO and Public Services International for the ‘Workplace Violence in the Health Sector’ case study.1 It consists of a brief definition and examples of WPV, followed by single-item questions on whether respondents had experienced WPV, the type of WPV experienced and the perpetrator of the incident. The tool has been shown to have adequate content validity and satisfactory Cronbach’s alpha indices.23 WPV will be defined as an incident of being abused, threatened, assaulted or subjected to other offensive behaviour in the circumstances related to work within the past year.27

Finally, the Malay-validated Brief-COPE questionnaire is based on the Brief-COPE, a shortened version of the COPE Inventory developed by Carver.28 It contains 14 scales comprising two items rated on a 4-point Likert scale that assesses the degree to which a respondent uses a specific coping strategy, with higher scores indicating greater utilisation of the particular coping strategy, that is, problem-focused, emotion-focused and dysfunctional coping. The tool has been demonstrated to have good reliability and discriminant validity by the authors.24 Coping mechanism will be defined as the degree to which respondents use a specific coping mechanism according to the Brief-COPE scores.24

Data collection

The data collection process will commence after obtaining ethical approval from Ministry of Health and Universiti Teknologi MARA Research Ethics Committees as well as permission to conduct the study from the Ministry of Health, the Terengganu State Health Department and hospital directors. The list of HCWs in each hospital will be obtained from the hospital administration department. Eligible HCWs will be randomly selected and invited to participate in the study through the hospital’s OSH unit. Informed consent will be obtained from study participants. Each participant will receive a set of participant information sheet, questionnaire, coloured sheet of paper and opaque envelope. Participants will be instructed to complete the questionnaire, write their contact information on the coloured sheet and return all materials in the sealed opaque envelope. A unique identifier number printed on both the questionnaire and coloured sheet will enable the research team to link the contact information with the questionnaire responses solely to recruit participants for the qualitative phase. No identifying personal information will be recorded on the questionnaire itself, and the coloured sheets will be stored separately from the questionnaires and kept confidential. During data collection in this phase, the study participants will be informed of the possibility that they will be recruited for the qualitative phase.

Data analysis

All quantitative analyses will be conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics V.28. Prior to statistical analyses, data cleaning and initial data analyses will be performed to check for data completeness, duplicates, missing data, outliers, influential observations and verify model assumptions. Incomplete data will be retrieved by contacting the study participants based on their contact information. Any remaining missing data will be evaluated based on the percentage of missingness and Little’s missing completely at random (MCAR) test to determine the mechanism of missingness. Data with less than 5% missing values and identified as MCAR will be considered as ignorable and retained in the analyses.29 For data not deemed ignorable, multiple imputation by chained equations will be performed under the missing at random assumption.30 20 imputed data sets with iterative cycles will be generated and pooled using Rubin’s rules. Sensitivity analyses will be performed to evaluate robustness to potential missing not at random mechanisms. For model checking, normality, multicollinearity and homoscedasticity will be assessed. Normality of residuals will be assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and by inspecting normal probability (Q–Q) plots, whereby a p value >0.05 indicates that the residuals are approximately normally distributed.31 Multicollinearity will be assessed using variance inflation factor, with values below 5 indicating acceptable levels.32 Finally, homoscedasticity will be assessed using scatter plots of standardised residuals versus standardised predicted values, where a random scatter indicates that the assumption is met.

Next, descriptive statistics will be used to summarise sociodemographic and work characteristics, PSC levels, WPV experiences and HCWs’ coping mechanisms. Here, categorical variables will be presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables will be expressed as means and standard deviations (SDs). The prevalence of WPV and its 95% confidence interval (CI) will be estimated. To determine the relationship between PSC and coping mechanisms of HCWs who experienced WPV, simple and multiple linear regression analyses will be performed. Simple linear regression will first be conducted to estimate crude regression coefficient and its 95% CI. Then, variables identified as significant at p value of less than 0.25 during the univariate analyses and potential confounders including age, gender, education, marital status, job position, department type and total years in healthcare will be included in the multiple linear regression model to estimate adjusted regression coefficient and its 95% CI. Model fitness will be evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²) and adjusted R², and a p-value of less than 0.05 will be considered good fit.33

Phase 2: qualitative phase comprising in-depth interviews of healthcare workers

Study population and sampling mechanism

This phase will sample HCWs working in public hospitals in Terengganu, Malaysia who have completed the survey in Phase 1, as the quantitative phase will determine which participants will be included in the qualitative phase.34 Criterion sampling with maximum variation will be employed based on predetermined inclusion criteria, namely HCWs who have experienced WPV within the past year and those from departments with high levels of PSC. This approach ensures that all participants have direct and relevant experience with WPV, thereby enhancing conceptual representativeness by capturing the perspectives of individuals most pertinent to the phenomenon. Maximum variation sampling will further be applied to ensure diversity in gender, job role, work experience and hospital department, allowing for the collection of information-rich data that reflects variation in experience and supports the depth and transferability of findings.35

The rationale for selecting HCWs in high-PSC departments is to examine coping strategies within supportive organisational contexts, offering insights to inform interventions and policymaking that fosters healthy work environments and effective coping.36 Although including HCWs from low-PSC departments would have enriched the analysis through richer contrasts, this was not possible due to resource limitations. HCWs unwilling to be interviewed or who have left the hospital before the qualitative data collection period commences will be excluded.

Sample size calculation

A sample of 6–12 participants will be recruited for IDIs.37 This number is based on the principle of data saturation, where additional interviews no longer yield new themes or insights,34 as previous studies have found that most key themes were identified within the first 12 interviews and many appeared as early as the first six interviews.37 This approach will ensure that the qualitative findings provide rich and meaningful data to complement the quantitative results.

Study instruments and data collection

A semistructured interview guide will be used to conduct IDIs with selected HCWs (online supplemental file 1). The guide was developed using the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping38 and has five main components including introduction, ground rules, self-introduction, main questions and closing. The guide will be pilot tested among HCWs to evaluate the relevance and comprehensibility of the content and identify the possible need to modify questions. Subsequently, the investigator will conduct a face-to-face IDI in Malay with eligible participants in a private and quiet place. Informed consent and permission to record the IDI with a portable digital voice recorder will be retrieved from the participant prior to the interview. Each IDI is estimated to last for about 40–60 min. In addition to the voice recorder, the researcher will use a field notebook to record the interview.

Transcription, data analysis and rigour

Microsoft Office Word 2020 application will be used to transcribe the recorded interviews. The original language of the interviews, that is, Malay, will be retained. First, the investigator will type the content into a Word document template prepared beforehand. Then, audio-proofing will be performed to ensure no errors were made during the transcription process.39 The investigator will listen to the recording a second time while cross-referencing the typed transcript to ensure accuracy in spelling and context.

Data will subsequently be analysed using thematic analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework.40 The expected outcome of this analysis will be to identify important themes from the data and analyse how the themes relate to one another. Beginning with multiple reviews of the transcripts to familiarise with the data and obtain initial insights, the investigator will then generate initial codes through open coding to identify recurring concepts. Then, the codes will be categorised into themes, which will be further refined through a review of the themes. Next, the themes will be defined and named, ensuring that each theme is meaningful and well-supported by the data. Finally, the findings will be compiled into a comprehensive report connecting the qualitative themes to the study objectives. The QSR NVivo Pro V.14 software will be used for the data organisation and coding.

Several measures will be applied to ensure rigour and trustworthiness in the qualitative phase, including: (1) transcription quality cross-checking by the research team to rectify any misheard words, formatting errors and unidentifiable tags left by the initial transcription, (2) retaining some quotes in the original language where necessary to ensure the true meaning is conveyed during the interpretation process and maintain the integrity of the interview data, (3) consultation of expert panel comprising specialists in occupational health and qualitative research during thematic analysis to review preliminary themes for conceptual clarity, coherence and alignment with the study objectives, (4) member checking, where participants review results to confirm accuracy,41 (5) audit trail, where all coding decisions are documented in NVivo for transparency and finally (6) methodological triangulation, where qualitative themes are compared with quantitative findings to corroborate findings.

Integration and interpretation of quantitative and qualitative data

The mixed-methods sequential design incorporates integration at various stages. At the methods level, the preliminary results of the quantitative phase will guide the data collection process in the qualitative phase. This ensures that the qualitative data collection focuses on areas that require further exploration and explanation.42

At the interpretation and reporting level, integration will occur through both narrative and joint display approaches. Integration through narrative refers to the process of combining and interpreting quantitative and qualitative findings within a single report or across a series of connected reports. In this study, results will be presented using a contiguous approach, where quantitative and qualitative findings are first reported in separate sections to maintain methodological clarity.43

Subsequently, the two sets of findings will be integrated using a joint display table, which will align quantitative results with corresponding qualitative themes. The table will begin with columns representing quantitative findings, followed by qualitative themes that elaborate or explain those results.44 This table compares quantitative results and qualitative findings side by side, identifying convergences and divergences, offering evidence that helps a researcher draw new insights beyond what is available from separate quantitative and qualitative data.45

In cases where discrepancies arise between the two data strands, the reconciliation of conflicting findings will be undertaken and the results will be critically examined to uncover methodological, contextual or theoretical explanations. Through this integration, the study aims to derive meta-inferences that offer a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the relationship between PSC and coping mechanisms among HCWs.46

Interpretation of the study findings will be primarily guided by the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping,38 which conceptualises stress as a dynamic person–environment transaction in which individuals appraise the significance of the stressor and their available coping resources.47 In this study, HCWs’ experiences of WPV are viewed through this lens, that is, as a stressor that elicits cognitive appraisal and coping responses. To complement this perspective, the Extended Job Demands–Resources Model10 is incorporated to conceptualise PSC as an upstream organisational antecedent that shapes job demands and resources, thereby influencing employees’ exposure to and capacity to manage WPV. Integrating these perspectives, the present study positions PSC as an organisational factor influencing how HCWs appraise and cope with WPV. Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and the public will not be involved in this study’s design, conduct, reporting or dissemination.

Discussion

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of the quantitative phase restricts the ability to draw causal inferences between PSC and coping mechanisms of HCWs experiencing WPV. While multiple regression analyses controlling for potential confounders such as age, gender, profession, years of experience and department type will be conducted to reduce confounding bias in the observed relationships,48 these analyses will identify associations rather than establish causality. To address temporal ambiguity in the interpretation of findings, findings from the qualitative phase will be used to provide contextual explanations that help clarify the directions of influence among these variables.49 This is in accordance with the explanatory sequential study design which strengthens interpretation by integrating narrative evidence to complement statistical associations. Second, focusing on Malaysian public hospitals may limit the generalisability of the study findings to other healthcare settings, such as private hospitals or public hospitals in different regions. Finally, self-reported data collected through questionnaires are subject to recall and social desirability biases that may influence the accuracy of reported WPV experiences and coping behaviours, potentially leading to biased associations.

Notwithstanding, there are also strengths to this study. First, employing an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design will enable a comprehensive and nuanced understanding of how PSC influences coping mechanisms among HCWs who experience WPV, a topic that is presently not well understood. Second, several methods to improve the robustness of study findings will also be undertaken. This includes conducting multistage random sampling and criterion sampling with maximum variation to improve the generalisability of study findings, and employing validated and culturally appropriate study instruments as well as a pilot-tested interview guide to improve the accuracy and reliability of study findings.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval has been obtained from the Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC), the Ministry of Health Malaysia (NMRR ID-24–02659-QKF (IIR)) and the UiTM Research Ethics Committee (REC/10/2024 (PG/MR/492)). Additionally, permission has been secured from the Terengganu State Health Department prior to data collection.

Participants will receive a detailed participant information sheet that outlines the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits. Informed consent will be obtained before participation in the survey and IDIs. Participation is voluntary, and participants may withdraw at any time without consequences. All data will remain confidential. The self-administered questionnaires will not contain any identifying information and participants will receive a unique ID code to ensure study confidentiality. Electronic information will be encrypted and password-protected, while physical documents will be securely stored in a secured cabinet in a locked room. Only authorised research team members will have access to the data, which will be retained for 5 years before it is securely destroyed.

The findings from this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journal publications, conferences, stakeholder briefings and policy recommendations to support WPV prevention strategies. Research outputs will be shared with Ministry of Health policymakers, hospital administrators and occupational health units to increase awareness and guide the development of PSC-based interventions to strengthen HCWs’ resilience and mitigate WPV.
