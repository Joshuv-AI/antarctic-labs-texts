Building paths to healthcare: an explorative qualitative study among undocumented mothers experiencing maternal and infant healthcare access in Switzerland
Authors: Caterina Montagnoli, Bernice Simone Elger, Yves Jackson, Tenzin Wangmo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUndocumented migrant women face significant healthcare challenges yet they are seldom given a voice. This study aims to explore the experiences of undocumented migrant mothers in accessing maternal and infant healthcare across Switzerland.MethodsIn this exploratory qualitative study, data were collected through semi-structured interviews and analysed using thematic analysis.Results29 pregnant women and mothers of children aged ≤2 years participated in this study. They discussed their pregnancy and postpartum access to care. In these discussions, the important role of social workers and midwives in facilitating continuous access to healthcare services was evident. Declined support after childbirth exacerbated challenges in securing infant care. Of particular concern was the affordability of health insurance. Many participants reported that insurance debt accumulation and financial instability adversely affected healthcare-seeking potential and subsequent quality of healthcare received.ConclusionsThe findings underscore the critical influence of federal healthcare insurance, the provision of services at the cantonal level and the roles of gatekeepers such as social workers and midwives. Strengthening continuity of care and allocating increased resources to address the needs of pregnant and newly born undocumented migrants are vital steps towards achieving health equity for this vulnerable group.Keywords: Public Health Practice, Health Services Accessibility, Qualitative Research, ethics, Community-Based Participatory Research

Introduction

Ensuring universal access to healthcare is one of the most fundamental yet complex challenges faced by healthcare systems. Access to healthcare framework encompasses five key dimensions: availability, accessibility, affordability, adequacy and acceptability.1 It provides a structured lens to explore how systemic, economic and social factors interact to shape healthcare-seeking behaviours and outcomes, particularly for vulnerable populations. Ensuring access to these services for undocumented women is a critical step toward ensuring health equity and reducing long-term disparities.2,3

Undocumented or irregular migration—when people move across borders without following established regulations4—creates challenges for recipient countries in ensuring universal health coverage and health equity.5 This is largely due to the frequent criminalisation of migrants, which hinders their access to healthcare.6 Thus, these individuals often tend to seek healthcare for only emergent health conditions to avoid the risk of being denounced, detained or sent back to their home countries, while also facing significant financial costs, which may further limit their access to necessary services.7

Available literature has mainly focused on the maternal aspects of health access for undocumented migrant mothers, giving little attention to the equally important phases of postpartum and infant care.8–10 In concurrence, there is growing evidence stressing the importance of the first 1000 days of life. This is the period from conception until the second year of the child. This time period is suggested to be the most important window of opportunity to provide preventive care and to lay the best foundation for lifelong health for both mothers and children.11,12

Undocumented migrant women, compared with the general population, are at a higher risk of poor attendance for family planning services around conception,13 resulting in higher rates of unwanted pregnancies and delayed antenatal or well-child visits.14 Undocumented migrant children are also at higher risk of preterm birth and very low birth weight.15 The universal access to healthcare model lays the ground for implementing continuity of care during this phase of women’s and child’s health.16 Yet, even in places where universal healthcare access is theoretically possible, complex administrative procedures make it difficult to navigate services and access healthcare.17 Trusted community healthcare providers, like midwives, nurses and general practitioners, not only offer consistent medical support but may also act as networks, helping women create a more effective connection with the healthcare systems.18 Previous studies have acknowledged the pivotal role of such networks in shaping self-perception of health, promoting awareness about available healthcare services and health literacy.19

In 1994, Switzerland passed a law transforming its healthcare system from public to private.20 This meant that as of 1 January 1996, all people residing in Switzerland were required to purchase mandatory health insurance. The Swiss constitution protects the right to health in emergency situations to every person ‘in need and unable to provide for themselves’ (Article 12).21 The 1994 federal law on healthcare insurance further requires insurance companies and healthcare providers to provide healthcare insurance and access, respectively, to people without regular residency permits.22–24 Both insurance companies and healthcare providers are relieved from the obligation to denounce or provide information about the legal status of their clients or patients to immigration authorities.25 However, the mandatory healthcare insurance involves significant monthly payments (premium) and additional out-of-pocket expenses (deductible). Both may be financially out of reach for undocumented adults, especially given their ineligibility to apply for public subsidies.26,27 In fact, it is worth noting that only 12% of undocumented migrants in the Greater Geneva Area are insured, highlighting significant gaps in access to healthcare for this population.28 Nevertheless, for those with health insurance in Switzerland, pregnancy is an exceptional period because all healthcare services are fully covered by the healthcare insurance from the start of the 13th week of gestation through to the 56th day after birth.29 That is, there is no deductible associated with pregnancy-related healthcare. Furthermore, enrolling newborns in health insurance is typically a straightforward process, and cantons frequently provide subsidies to families in precarious economic situations to help cover the costs.23

For undocumented migrant mothers, the first 1000 days of their infant’s life are a particularly vulnerable period.30 This vulnerability is often exacerbated by unstable employment and housing conditions associated with economic precariousness.28 The occupations in which undocumented migrant women engage are often physically demanding. They typically lack formal contracts, leaving them without access to social benefits or other financial safety nets that might be available to Swiss citizens and migrant residents.26

In light of the limited information on the healthcare access experiences of undocumented migrant mothers and infants, this qualitative study seeks to address this gap by engaging with the population of interest. We focus on the narratives of undocumented migrant mothers accessing healthcare during the first 1000 days of the newborn’s life. This study aims to uncover specific facilitators and obstacles to accessing care, providing valuable insights from the voices of this group to inform future interventions in Switzerland. With its multicentric approach, the study gives insights into inter-regional differences in accessing maternal and child healthcare for undocumented migrants. In doing so, we reflect on the impact of Switzerland’s decentralised healthcare system, differing levels of non-governmental organisations (NGO) support, and variations in local policies, economic conditions and resource availability between cantons.

Materials and methods

Quality and completeness of reporting were assisted using the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative studies (COREQ) 32-item checklist (see online supplemental materials).

Setting

As of 2015, Switzerland is estimated to have hosted between 58 000 and 105 000 undocumented migrants, with a most likely figure of 76 000, predominantly located in cantons such as Zurich, Geneva and Vaud.26 Framed within a federal law, the Swiss healthcare system is decentralised. Each canton is responsible for implementing the healthcare system according to their own local circumstances. This leads to significant variations in the provision of health services for undocumented migrants.31 In 11 cantons, there are either privately funded NGO or canton-funded services offering primary healthcare and social services for people who cannot afford healthcare insurance.22 Undocumented migrants make up a majority of this population. For example, in the canton of Geneva, a dedicated ambulatory attached to the Geneva University Hospital provides primary healthcare and refers to specialised services whenever needed (Unité de médecine et soins dans la communauté). While in the cantons of Bern and Fribourg, they have both canton-funded services and NGOs (eg, Frisanté, Berner Red Cross) offering not only primary care appointments on a regular basis but also administrative and/or financial help for enrolling in healthcare insurance for a specific health condition (eg, pregnancies, small infant). Central and western Switzerland appear to have canton-funded services as well as private NGOs dedicated to healthcare for undocumented migrants. The situation is different in eastern Switzerland, where canton-funded services serving sans-papiers, a term used to indicate people without legal status, are few and have limited breadth of resources.31

Recruitment of participants and participants’ involvement

For this semi-structured interview exploratory qualitative study, we sought to recruit at least 24 women who were, or had been in the past 2 years, undocumented migrants either pregnant or with a child aged 0–2 years, from different parts of Switzerland. The total number of interviews planned was for indicative purposes only, given that the final number depended on when the study reached data saturation. The literature suggests that saturation arrives around nine interviews for code and 16–24 interviews for meaning saturation.32 In our study, we took data saturation as an indication when no new findings emerged from the interviews, allowing us to draw additional robust conclusions on the topic under study.

It was not appropriate for the characteristics of the searched population to involve participants in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research. The recruitment was purposive in nature. Given budget constraints, we asked collaborating professionals to prioritise introducing the study to individuals fluent in Spanish, Portuguese, French, English, German or Italian. To address potential under-representation, we also leveraged partnerships and personal networks to arrange interviews with individuals speaking other languages, facilitating meetings with the support of translators whenever possible. The study did not target a specific ethnicity, nationality or lifestyle among undocumented migrant women.

After approaching partner cantonal funded associations and NGOs in different regions of Switzerland, potential participants were informed about the study and, if agreeing, CM contacted them to explain the context and aim of the project and conducted all the interviews (see interview guide and participant information sheet in online supplemental materials). The interview time was scheduled to accommodate the potential availability constraints/choices of the population of interest. The 26 online semi-structured interviews were hosted on encrypted one-to-one platforms (WhatsApp), while three interviews took place in person. Online interviews were better accepted as they did not require commuting time and were seen as less time taking; three interviews took place in person. Although questions were addressed to women only, in two interviews, partners sporadically intervened, commenting on the reply; such inputs were not taken into account in the data analysis.

Oral consent for participation and for recording the interview was obtained at three times: at first contact, during the presentation of the study and before starting the interview. Given the characteristics of the population, we wanted to make sure that the participants were aware of their right to stop or postpone the interview at any time. They were clearly informed about their right to withdraw their consent at any time. The interviewer asked participants whether they agreed to continue with the remaining questions approximately 20 min into the interview. During the interviews, participants were informed about data protection, storage and anonymisation.

Interviews were conducted in English (4), French (6), German (2), Portuguese (5) and Spanish (12). In four interviews, the presence of a trusted passive translator (partner, family or friend) was necessary. The interviews lasted between 23 and 82 min, depending on the woman’s availability and willingness to share more information, with a median time of 40 min. Two participants decided not to answer some questions connected to their migration story. None of the participants revoked their consent during or after the interview. No participant requested their transcript for review. To reduce power imbalances between interviewer and interviewees, voluntary participation was consistently emphasised, interviews were conducted in a conversational style, and limited personal information (eg, from the interviewer’s own experience) was occasionally shared to build rapport.

Study participants

Between 1 February and 31 August 2024, 35 potential participants were contacted. Five women did not follow-up leaving a total of 30 participants to be interviewed. 29 interviews were retained for data analysis, as one interviewee was wrongly referred for the study as an undocumented migrant but was actually an asylum seeker. The project took place in Central and Western Switzerland and the final sample consisted of four pregnant women and 25 mothers of children aged up to 2 years old (see table 1).

Data analysis

Audio recordings were used only for transcription, which was completed within a week. The recordings were then deleted within a month after ensuring transcription quality. All data were anonymised during the transcription process. Interviews were first transcribed in the language of the interview and thereafter translated into English with the help of DeePL. A research assistant checked for transcription quality of Portuguese and German interviews. Quality for Spanish, English and French was checked by CM.

We conducted a reflexive thematic analysis33 and coded the data inductively, allowing themes to be built from the data. The analysis was guided by the six-step framework devised by Braun and Clarke34 and the coding tree was developed iteratively rather than imposed. We conducted the data analysis process with MAXQDA (V.24). The first and last author developed a coding tree based on the identification of themes/subthemes within several transcripts. The first author further inductively developed the coding tree during the coding process of the subsequent interview transcripts. The identification of new themes/subthemes in the remaining transcripts was concertedly discussed with the last author. For this paper, we decided to re-organised the themes and subthemes to fit three time periods about which the study participants gave information on: (1) periconceptional phase, (2) pregnancy and birth and (3) post partum and early infancy. This also allowed us to present the results using a timeline as a guide to illustrate changes in similar themes or subthemes over time. Although the first 1000 days timeline, to our knowledge, has not been used as a framework to present the results of a qualitative study, previous literature has emphasised how a timeline could better help contextualise qualitative studies’ outcomes with regards to historical or personal events.35,36

CM is a PhD candidate in biomedical ethics and received training in qualitative research. She identifies as a white woman with a migration background and expertise in midwifery and global health. She worked as a researcher and midwife in over five countries in Europe and Latin America. The last author, TW, is an experienced qualitative researcher and has prior research expertise on maternal and reproductive healthcare decision-making. She is a person of colour with a migration background in Switzerland. She also has a personal understanding of how the Swiss asylum and migration system functions. The coauthors, YJ and EBS, are primary care physicians with extensive research work on migration populations in addition to being scholars in the field. All authors discussed and agreed to present the data based on a mind map presented in the next section.

Results

A thematic map of the study findings is presented in figure 1 using the timeline of the first 1000 days to bring contextual depth to participants’ situation as undocumented migrant mothers in Switzerland. We decided to represent the first 1000 days of life in figure 1 as part of a circle rather than a horizontal line to show the continuity of care needs from the maternal antenatal healthcare visits up until infants’ healthcare access and beyond. In this circle, we put the focus on the following: (1) around conception, (2) pregnancy and birth and (3) the post partum and infancy. The first 1000 days of life here provide an analytical structure in which identified overlapping codes (eg, those related to financial constraints) varied in meaning and impact depending on the temporal stage in which the participants find themselves.

Maternal and infant healthcare access: themes and subthemes.

Around conception

Rationing healthcare use

Some participants shared that their primary reason for migration was to improve their economic status. Interviewees reported not seeking healthcare unless necessary, as without healthcare insurance accessing it was too expensive.

I was almost a year without insurance and [during that time] I didn’t see a doctor. I mean, basically I didn’t go to the doctor if I got sick or needed consultation because of the fact that […] I was afraid of paying abysmal bills. (P-20, French speaking canton)

In this quote, the participant referred to how financial precarity translated into delayed or foregone care, with fear of unaffordable costs acting as a powerful deterrent even in situations of medical need. Here, the anticipation of debt functioned as a structural barrier to access. The little available finances undocumented migrants earned were typically directed to their survival. This led to a situation where out-of-pocket payments for health matters appeared almost unjustified unless urgently needed. For the interviewees, it seemed evident that periconceptional care, including family planning and preventative services, were not urgent matters. If urgent health conditions arose, the opportunity/risk balance decided if it was worth exposing their legal status to institutions like hospitals.

If you call the hospital, they’ll ask for your insurance card. It’s, we don’t have insurance, et cetera. It’s just not possible. You have to have insurance [otherwise] you have to pay. You have to pay for this stuff. Do you have to pay cash for this? If you don’t work, you can’t pay. You’re undocumented and you don’t have insurance. It’s clear you won’t be able to pay. (P-18, bilingual canton)

This extract presented how insurance verification operated as an exclusion mechanism, reinforcing a perception of the impossibility of access. Sometimes routines set up by institutions were directly aimed at refraining undocumented individuals from accessing care or only based on financial capacity, normalising self-exclusion. Thus, study participants explained that the financial burden of out-of-pocket visits, along with undocumented legal status were significant obstacles to seeking care. That is, the benefits of accessing services to have a medical check-up or being prescribed contraceptives were outbalanced by the greater risks of exposing one’s legal status and being unable to pay ‘abysmal bills’.

Negative initial healthcare access experiences

When the study participants suspected their possible pregnancy, they tried to access healthcare. During this stage, they perceived their experiences as challenging. Some interviewees were questioned by healthcare providers about their financial, psychological and parenting capacities to continue the pregnancy and raise a child. These perceived negative experiences from accessing healthcare in the early phases of their pregnancy further made them feel cautious about their status as undocumented persons. Such negative experiences included, as reported by one participant, receiving suggestions to give her child away for adoption.

They knew the situation, before the consultation: you have to explain a little bit the story. And I think they took it I was an undocumented immigrant. [and asked me:] “And why do you want to bring a child into the world if you don’t have the conditions? Do you understand?” (P-20, French speaking canton, 2nd child)

In this quote, the participant reported how moral judgement from healthcare professionals negatively impacted on subsequent healthcare seeking attitude. The clinical encounter here went beyond care to a moment of stigma and delegitimisation. Reproductive choices of the participant, especially because of her undocumented status, were once again scrutinised, undermining her autonomy and sense of entitlement to care.

Some associations have come [and said]: “you have to give the child up for adoption. You know, it’s indicated, because you’re like that [undocumented migrant], you have to give your child up”. It was a shock, a shock. (P-12, German speaking canton)

Again, here institutional actors went beyond guidance and exerted pressure on reproductive decisions, particularly by targeting the legal situation of the participant. But this was not the case for all women participating in the study. In the case of another participant, it was thanks to one positive initial experience with a translator that she eventually was able to navigate the healthcare system and succeeded in timely access to pregnancy healthcare. Furthermore, initial encounters with translators were instrumental for transforming a moment of potential discontinuity into one of continued care. In this section, it became apparent how access was not linear but contingent on relational support and navigation assistance, emphasising the importance of subjective and compassionate accompaniment in overcoming institutional barriers.

I was already seven weeks pregnant when I had the appointment in [association] before the termination. That was the moment when I decided to have it and I said no, I’m not going to do it. And then I asked, if there were a possibility that they could help me, because I wanted to have this baby and no, I couldn’t do that. Then a translator from [another association] came with me to [association] and translated it so that they could help me contact the social worker from [NGO 1]. And then I continued with the pregnancy and the medical attention there. (P-29, French speaking canton)

Pregnancy and birth

Intercantonal or international movements for healthcare accessibility

Differences in accessing and paying for healthcare via insurance led participants to an intercantonal mobility in order to access affordable healthcare. Costs, however, remained a big obstacle to healthcare access even when a connection to both cantonal and NGO funded services was established. Often, support could not be given to all as services have limited allocated resources. When costs were too high, undocumented migrant mothers preferred, or were suggested, to leave Switzerland for the duration of their pregnancy and birth, as a cheaper option (see quote P-16). Some mothers managed to find a friend or a contact that could have hosted them in the new (typically European) country, though, some moved without any form of local support and had to find their own way there and then back to Switzerland after the birth. In the following quote the participant reported how access to care was territorially uneven within Switzerland, prompting women to actively move between cantons for affordable service provision. Health insurance, as a federal instrument, functioned as a prerequisite for mobility within the system, enabling some degree of continuity of care across cantons while also exposing structural gaps in service availability.

I had already a healthcare insurance, when I decided to take my pregnancy check-ups from [canton of first healthcare access] to [canton of residence]. That’s the only way I could move my health check-ups from one canton to the other: have the health insurance. So, yes, I already had it. Because I looked for something like the same services I accessed in [the other Swiss canton], but they didn’t have those kind of things here. (P-10, bilingual canton)

I talked to [name of the social worker]. He told me what I could do if I had her [the baby] here and looked for the best solution. But unfortunately I had to leave here [the country]. (P-16, bilingual canton)

Going beyond the cantonal sphere, the above participant underscored how mobility decisions outside Switzerland were shaped by constraints rather than choice, with women sometimes compelled to leave a setting despite having social workers in their support. Structural barriers, like funds, prevailed over available guidance, pointing to the fragility of care pathways for undocumented women.

Other participants described that the complexities of administrative procedures to access a new healthcare system, together with the associated financial obligations, were difficult to navigate alone. When this burden became too much to face, some mothers preferred giving up on finding a temporary solution to access healthcare. As a result, prenatal care or support in accessing healthcare was often sought later in pregnancy, when it could no longer be postponed.

Social services as facilitators

While many participants did not have healthcare insurance before pregnancy, when it came to pregnancy care, securing regular healthcare access was possible thanks to the early connection with dedicated cantonal and NGOs services for undocumented migrants. Social workers from these services acted as intermediaries for explaining the insurance system, finding the best practitioner, booking appointments or overcoming language barriers. As an example, the quote from participant P-29 illustrated how feeling recognised and understood within healthcare encounters contributed to a sense of emotional safety, here through translation and awareness. Access was not only about entry but also about the quality of healthcare access’ experiences, where being acknowledged without judgement played a central role.

The people [Healthcare professionals] were very nice, they made me feel good. They knew my situation. Because I was attended with a translator. Where I was coming from, about insurance, all that. (P-29, French speaking canton, 3rd child)

But such social workers who were active in community settings to support undocumented migrants, did much more than just helping administratively: they created a real network of support. One participant relayed that sometimes social workers also helped with finding places that distribute food and other goods for mothers and babies.

Well, as time went by I got pregnant. It made life in Switzerland complicated for me because everything is very, very expensive. Well, but no, it is not impossible. I have received help from [NGO 1]’s social worker. She guided me where I should get food, where I can go to get help with clothes for me, for the baby. And so everything went well. And now I am here as it is at the moment, my child will be two months old. Thanks to the help of the social worker everything went very well. (P-29, French speaking canton)

Social workers played a central role as navigators of both healthcare and broader social support systems, extending beyond medical care to basic needs such as food and clothing. Access came not only in the form of healthcare but also of social stability: continuous guidance from facilitators could transform an otherwise impossible situation into a workable one.

Participants often shared how their encounter with both cantonal and NGO supported social workers was instrumental in accessing healthcare. Before taking the role of facilitators of healthcare access, social workers welcomed undocumented migrants, communicated in their language, understood their problems and delivered the best complete solution. For many interviewees, social workers helped rebuild trust in the institutions and reconnected women with healthcare providers. Whenever present, social workers acted as real public health promoters to ensure regular access to healthcare. Thanks to their role as facilitators, in 17 out of 25 participants who already had given birth at the time of the interview, the referred number of antenatal visits was higher or equal to 8, which is the minimum standard of antenatal visits according to WHO recommendations.37

Healthcare insurance overcomes legal status concerns

During pregnancy, the connection with social workers of both cantonal and NGOs funded services enabled the interviewees to access healthcare insurance. Consequently, their legal status was perceived as less of an obstacle to healthcare access. As one participant explained, it was often thanks to the dedicated services for undocumented migrants that they were helped financially and administratively to apply for healthcare insurance. More specifically, associations and NGOs facilitated access by helping women circumvent legal and administrative barriers, particularly through obtaining health insurance. Access pathways in many of our participants were often indirect and mediated, requiring institutional support to enact eligibility and achieve actual healthcare access.

It was a bit difficult with the first pregnancy because I didn’t have a residence permit at the time. I couldn’t stay. First, I had to find a doctor because I was pregnant. And I didn’t have health insurance. It’s a bit difficult in [city in central Switzerland], so I moved to [city in central Switzerland 2]. There’s also a [NGO 2] advice service there. And then I told them about my situation and they helped me a lot. They made a new health insurance policy for me. After that we looked for a gynecologist. Yes, the first pregnancy was a bit difficult. (P-13, bilingual canton)

Participants therefore reported that the legal status of the person seeking care was irrelevant as long as they demonstrated their ability to pay for the service via their healthcare insurance. Healthcare insurance was crucial not only in access to healthcare but also to bypass the fear of being reported or suffering from legal consequences when accessing healthcare. In the following quote, the participant further explained how healthcare access was structured around healthcare insurance rather than migration status per se. Here, the system’s emphasis on insurance created a form of conditional inclusion/exclusion, where access was possible as long as administrative requirements were met irrespective of legal status.

The problem is that nobody will know [that I am undocumented] if they’ve received all documents and if I don’t say so. On the medical side too, they don’t ask too much if you’ve got the paperwork, and what’s useful for them is the insurance, so there’s no problem there either. (P-11, bilingual canton)

Post partum and infancy

Role of healthcare navigators

Even after giving birth, many new mothers continued to struggle with understanding the administrative aspects of the Swiss healthcare system. This challenge persisted not only for those who had given birth abroad but also for participants who had spent their entire pregnancy in Switzerland. Being supported by the social workers during their pregnancies allowed them to see the ‘visible’ complexity of the Swiss healthcare system as first-time users: how to set up a healthcare insurance, how to send an invoice, how to find and register as a patient in the chosen place where they would give birth. Yet, this knowledge did not fully enable them to face the ‘invisible’, or the ‘untold’ complexities that existed after the birth of their child. Understanding the child healthcare insurance, finding a long-term available paediatrician, staying up-to-date with the well-child visits were just some of the ‘invisible’ challenges reported by the participants of our study. When participants sought a paediatrician by themselves, a delay in the infants’ vaccinations and well-child visits was reported due to language barriers, the different health literacy of the health seeker or having given birth abroad.

The last [infant’s] vaccinations were in [EU country 3] and now I have to do the next ones here, which should be in July. I’m still looking for the pediatrician to see if he will do us. I don’t know how vaccines work here, if it’s the pediatrician or you have to go to the hospital. (P-16, bilingual canton)

Uncertainty and fragmentation characterised this participant’s postnatal care navigation experience, particularly due to her movement from abroad healthcare systems. For many mothers and their infants, social workers, now with midwives and other professionals, continued to be instrumental in helping to access healthcare and bridge the gaps between pregnancy care and after birth care. This allowed the burden of system navigation, reflected by a lack of clear, accessible information, to move from the mother to the professional.

In the maternity ward they helped me. The same midwife [that assisted me during birth] was the one who said “Look, this is a list of pediatricians. These speak [your language]. They live around where you live. If you’re interested, you can check it out, call and make an appointment.” And so it was. Yet, the maternity social worker told me that once [the baby] was out of maternity ward, she was no longer responsible for us. It had to be all on my own. I needed to go to this [center for undocumented migrants] and ask there regarding my son’s insurance. (P-9, French speaking canton)

This account underscored both the supportive and bounded role of healthcare professionals, where initial guidance (eg, identifying language-concordant paediatricians) facilitated access, but responsibility quickly shifted back to the mother after discharge. Once the midwife’s support ended during the period when critical needs emerged, women reported difficulties finding support for contraception, mental health, breastfeeding and infant care. Navigation over time transformed into an individual responsibility as soon as the institutional involvement ended.

Our participants experienced a diversity, a fracture—and not a continuum—of healthcare experience between pregnancy and infant care, where a vacuum between the antenatal and the postnatal care experience persisted. Continuity of access to healthcare for oneself or for the infant was reported as complex, expensive and time demanding. However, for those participants who experienced guidance during pregnancy, we observed a logical comeback to the social workers to understand how to navigate the healthcare system with and for an infant.

Economic conditions limit access and quality of healthcare

Participants who acquired insurance coverage, often with the assistance of social workers, revealed that obtaining the mandatory healthcare insurance for children could be a demanding procedure when done independently. In fact, while some mothers received financial aid from the canton to pay the child’s healthcare insurance premium, others were not offered such a co-payment option. Finding insurance coverage for the child was particularly stressful in situations when participants gave birth outside Switzerland and had limited knowledge of the Swiss healthcare system.

Another factor affecting postpartum healthcare access was the financial precariousness interviewees found themselves in: during pregnancy they reported losing their jobs—and for migrants, mostly working in the informal sector, their only form of wage. After birth, women faced a complete loss of income, which coincided with the end of social benefits that covered health insurance payments, as well as the termination of maternity benefits. These maternity benefits have allowed women with health insurance to access healthcare services without cost-sharing during pregnancy and for the first 56 days post partum. Women in this postpartum period could, yes, access the healthcare system, but the shared costs were often unaffordable and this happened precisely when they had a new infant to care for. As a result, participants reported that the quality and timely access to healthcare were negatively impacted by the additional expenses connected to infant or maternal healthcare insurance costs.

My baby doesn’t even have insurance. I have to return to [name of the social worker] and get the baby insured. He had [insurance] until June. Of course, [name of the social worker] can’t afford to pay for me all his life. So [the baby] had insurance until June. So I can’t even tell you if it was canceled, but I think so. So I have to pay the insurance for my baby, which isn’t that expensive. But I can’t afford it right now. Even so, I have to work and do it because if anything happens, he’s already a year old. (P-2, German speaking canton)

Evident from this quote was that economic precarity directly disrupted continuity of care, even for essential services such as health insurance for infants. Access depended on short-term assistance rather than sustainable solutions, and support mechanisms were temporary creating instability. Along the same lines, lack of insurance translated into perceived denial of care, even in urgent situations, revealing how financial and administrative barriers shaped not only access but also the quality and responsiveness of care, reinforcing feelings of exclusion at critical moments.

I had gone to the emergency room because my son got a lung infection when he was few months old and no, and no, I didn’t have a pediatrician. I mean, I didn’t have insurance yet. And since I didn’t have insurance at that time, then it was like they did not want to provide a solution. (P-17, French speaking canton)

The connection with social workers decreased over time and participants reported greater financial responsibilities of ensuring both their and their infant’s healthcare access and well-being. Participants often reflected on the paradox of Switzerland’s mandatory health insurance policy, which posed a significant burden for those without stable income. This theme related to structural challenges faced by low-income, undocumented individuals who were required to obtain health insurance they could not afford. In some situations, this led to ever-increasing debts and related stress, especially after birth when financial aids were limited (see quote below). The emotional strain of managing debt and healthcare after-birth was palpable in the participants’ narrative reflecting the personal and emotional toll associated with being ‘stuck’ or finding it ‘impossible’ to make progress due to financial pressures. Furthermore, in the quote below, we could appreciate the paradox of formal inclusion (ie, having received a residency permit B), with the mandatory healthcare insurance subscription, coexisting with ongoing financial exclusion, limiting the practical benefits of legal and institutional integration.

Now luckily I have my permit B and I started working, but I mean it’s more than 10,000 CHF in debt! How can I start my life when I’m already in this situation? I have the health insurance now, but I cannot pay for it, so it’s just pointless. It’s a very difficult start because I’m studying to become a health assistant. I’m going to finish late this year and then I can work like in the old people’s home. Maybe I’ll be earning a little bit more, a little better. But, for right now, it’s impossible for me to pay the money that I owe with the money that I’m getting paid. It’s essentially impossible. (P-10, bilingual canton)

Discussion

Our findings show that undocumented migrant mothers’ access to care in Switzerland is shaped by a dynamic interplay of affordability, legal insecurity and support from social workers and midwives across the first 1000 days. While pregnancy often created an opportunity to connect with healthcare system, access remained fragile and was frequently disrupted after birth, particularly when financial support and navigation assistance decreased.

This manuscript contributes to the global discussion concerning undocumented migrant women’s journey in accessing maternal and child healthcare. More specifically, it gives an overview of the strategies in place to access maternal and child healthcare for undocumented migrants in Central and Western Switzerland. This study brought forth the voice of undocumented migrant women from cantons that host the largest share of such population. In doing so, we were able to provide rich insights into the complexities of Switzerland’s fragmented healthcare system.26 To interpret the findings, this discussion applies the access to healthcare framework, which encompasses five key dimensions: affordability, acceptability, adequacy, accessibility and availability.1

In this study, the financial burden of out-of-pocket visits, along with undocumented legal status, are significant obstacles to seeking healthcare. Such findings have corroborated what previous literature underlined: while there seems to be a certain reluctance to seek periconceptional care, including family planning services38 due to the financial burden of out-of-pocket payments,39 pregnancy is among the leading issues pushing women to seek healthcare.40 Regarding the affordability dimension of the access to healthcare framework, the economic precarity often forces undocumented migrants to delay or avoid primary healthcare, including family planning, thereby increasing the risk of unintended pregnancies and adverse maternal health outcomes.13,39 Importantly, this resulted inability to access healthcare, both in the forms of contraception or safe abortion, but also during pregnancy and early life due to structural barriers, constitutes a form of violation of reproductive justice.41,42

Consistent with findings reported in the literature for other marginalised populations, such as black women,43,44 our interviews suggest that negative experiences with healthcare professionals and social services during early pregnancy can foster long-term distrust in public institutions. Healthcare providers questioning parenting capacity and suggesting adoption not only appear as potential violations of medical ethics and human rights, specifically the right to form a family and the right to non-discrimination.41 As shown in the literature, these experiences can also discourage women from seeking care when needed, thereby endangering, in the long-term, the health of both mothers and their children.45 Adding to this, the complexity of the Swiss healthcare system, its fragmented structure, its insurance-based access and high costs compounded with fear of being denounced and language barriers often lead women to initially avoid or ration healthcare.22,39,46 The findings highlight the critical role of the acceptability dimension, as early negative experiences with healthcare providers often undermine trust and long-term engagement with the system.

When the undocumented migrant women were connected to social workers, who helped them navigate the system and access essential care, including vaccinations and postnatal services, their access to healthcare was facilitated. This improved the experience of the mothers by enabling regular access to healthcare and potentially improving health outcomes. The literature highlights that dedicated services enhance healthcare access for mothers and their infants.47 Nevertheless, there is still a need to strengthen the continuity of care between the pregnancy phase and the infant/early motherhood period.30 The role of social workers and midwives in bridging the gap between healthcare services and undocumented migrants aligns with the adequacy and acceptability dimensions of the framework. Their support ensures that healthcare services meet the organisational and interpersonal needs of patients, fostering trust and enabling regular care access.

The reliance on gatekeepers to access healthcare is especially significant given the individual challenges faced by undocumented migrants, notably language barriers. This issue is further exacerbated by the fragmented nature of healthcare systems in Switzerland and many Western countries, which often lack models ensuring continuity of care. Consequently, healthcare seekers are required to actively search for available professionals, adding to the burden of accessing necessary services.48 The persistent requirement to actively seek care even for routine visits all along the first 1000 days creates a form of self-rationalisation of health where individuals delay care because the effort and cost outweigh perceived immediate need.49 Recent research suggests that disparities in utilisation among disadvantaged populations can be improved by continuity of care models supported by good communication between the woman and the clinician.50,51 Our findings reinforce evidence that continuity of care models can help address inequities, social disadvantages and structural factors contributing to poor maternal and newborn health outcomes.52–54

As anticipated, having healthcare insurance played a crucial role in promoting acceptance and adherence to both maternal and infant healthcare programmes. Interviewed undocumented migrant women, when obtaining healthcare insurance via social services, reported acquiring a certain entitlement that enabled them to legally and independently seek healthcare on a regular basis. They did not feel the need to expose their undocumented legal status. Viewed differently, this emphasises that healthcare insurance plays a pivotal role in addressing the affordability dimension, enabling undocumented migrants to access maternal and infant care with reduced financial and legal barriers. Affordability thus interacts with other dimensions, such as acceptability, by mitigating fears of exposure linked to undocumented legal status. It reinforces the humanitarian value of healthcare provision in Switzerland as identified by Article 12 of its constitution.21

The capacity of canton-funded services supporting undocumented migrants in Switzerland is heavily influenced by local governance. Prevailing political narratives on migration further shape the scope of action provided by the cantonal and NGOs. Research underscores that restrictive policies limiting access to public health insurance can severely impede immigrant women’s access to essential prenatal care, with adverse effects on maternal health outcomes.55 Variations between cantons in healthcare accessibility may disproportionately affect individuals without connections or financial means, especially in regions where pro-bono healthcare options are limited. While the narrative from western cantons suggests a mix of cantonal and NGOs funded services provides a cohesive framework designed to facilitate healthcare access, those from eastern Switzerland—verified through direct communications with social workers (on 27 August 2024)—presented a contrasting regional disparity.

While this finding remains preliminary and requires further dedicated investigation, the contrasting situations between central and western Switzerland and eastern Switzerland prompted further reflections. In these eastern regions, the interaction between institutions and undocumented migrant women’s healthcare access during the first 1000 days appeared more punitive, with women feeling that institutional interactions made their situation more challenging. The regional disparities in healthcare access reflect variations in the availability and accessibility dimensions of the framework. In cantons with limited resources or punitive policies, the lack of cohesive support systems further restricts these women’s ability to access necessary care.

This cantonal disparity raises a broader ethical concern. In a country with Switzerland’s resources, such conditions are difficult to justify. Intercantonal mobility and secondary migration often occur due to a lack of available services. At the same time, undocumented migrants frequently depend on associations or NGOs for essential healthcare.56 This situation reveals a significant gap in the healthcare support framework. It also reflects a systemic failure to ensure accessible and affordable care. As a result, many women are pushed into precarious and potentially dangerous situations.

Limitations

The study had only one testimony from a woman based in eastern Switzerland, which led us to primarily reflect the narratives reported by women living in central and western Switzerland. Time and funding limitations required an approach of using social workers as a way to access the population of interest, which may have created a potential bias in recruitment. We acknowledge that the study participants are not representative of all undocumented migrant women in Switzerland. At the same time, some participants may have tended to relay information that they found particularly worthy to tell a researcher, leading to social desirability, which we hoped to limit. Nonetheless, we decided that partnering with social workers was the safest and most neutral way to approach and connect with the population of undocumented migrants, as they can be a difficult group to recruit due to their likelihood to distrust institutions and their inner fear of being denounced. In addition, despite efforts to reduce power imbalances and build rapport, the inherent power differential between interviewer and interviewee remains a limitation. Finally, some interviews were conducted with the support of passive translators (partners, family members or friends) which may have influenced the interviewee’s willingness to disclose sensitive information, particularly regarding relationship dynamics or reproductive intentions. We also acknowledge potential misunderstandings arising from the diversity of languages used to reach out to, interview, and communicate with participants and social workers across Switzerland.

Conclusions

This study offers a detailed exploration of the barriers and facilitators to accessing maternal and infant healthcare among undocumented migrants in Switzerland. The findings highlight the importance of social networks, NGOs and health insurance in determining access. However, economic constraints continue to limit healthcare use, resulting in healthcare rationing and delayed access for both mothers and infants. Based on the results of our study, several interventions are recommended to address the barriers and improve access to maternal and infant healthcare for undocumented migrants in the Swiss context by respecting the right to health expressed in Article 12 of the Swiss Federal Constitution21 and in the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights.57 Strengthening continuity of care models by formally integrating social worker and midwife support beyond the postpartum period to cover infant care and expanding subsidised healthcare programmes are essential steps for increasing the maternal short-term experience and long-term health across Switzerland. Specifically, there is a need for dedicated health or social workers to help undocumented women and their families build paths to healthcare access from the first prenatal visit to the child’s second birthday. Complementary to these efforts, expanding and stabilising funding for NGOs and cantonal centres would enable the provision of sustained, long-term navigation support for undocumented families beyond pregnancy and the immediate postpartum period. Our findings indicate that many women experienced difficulties navigating healthcare services once maternity-focused support ended. Ensuring continued support throughout infancy and early childhood could facilitate timely access to healthcare, strengthen continuity of care and foster community support networks.
