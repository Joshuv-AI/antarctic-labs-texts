Short-Term Efficacy and Safety of Topical Sirolimus Gel in Pediatric Superficial Microcystic Lymphatic Malformations: A Single-Center Interventional Study of 30 Patients
Authors: Weiyang Xu, Yu Ding, Lei Zhao, Mengqi Pi, Xinyang Zhang, Miao Xu, Ke Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of 0.2% topical sirolimus gel for pediatric superficial microcystic lymphatic malformations (LMs).MethodsThis single-center interventional study included 30 pediatric patients (14 males and 16 females; aged 2–15 years) with superficial microcystic lymphatic malformations. All patients received 0.2% topical sirolimus gel twice daily for 12 weeks according to a predefined treatment and follow-up protocol. Clinical efficacy was assessed at baseline and week 12 using clinical examination, standardized photography, symptom scores, and available imaging findings, together with a four-grade physician-assessed response scale. Adverse events were monitored throughout treatment, and blood sirolimus concentrations were measured at weeks 4 and 12.ResultsAll patients showed lesion reduction, with an overall response rate of 96% (29/30). At week 12, efficacy was graded as IV in 10 patients, III in 11, II in 8, and I in 1. Among patients with pain symptoms, pain scores decreased significantly after treatment (2.88 ± 0.78 vs 1.18 ± 0.88; P < 0.05). In 10 patients with measurable lesions, maximum lesion diameter decreased significantly from 3.22 ± 1.36 cm to 1.89 ± 1.33 cm (P < 0.05). Only mild local adverse effects were observed, and blood sirolimus concentrations remained below 2.0 ng/mL in all patients.ConclusionTopical sirolimus gel demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety for pediatric superficial microcystic LMs.Keywords: microcystic lymphatic malformation, sirolimus, topical gel, pediatric patients, vascular anomalies

Introduction

Lymphatic malformation (LM), previously referred to as “lymphangioma”, is a common congenital vascular anomaly that can lead to local pain, infection, bleeding, functional impairment, and, in severe cases, may be life-threatening.1 Based on cyst size, LM can be classified into macrocystic, microcystic, and mixed types.2 Unlike macrocystic lymphatic malformations, microcystic lesions manifest as vesicles, papules, or plaques that may leak lymphatic fluid (lymphorrhea), frequently leading to hyperkeratosis and spontaneous bleeding. Driven by activating growth-related mutations, these lesions tend to enlarge over time, exhibit a high rate of recurrence following treatment, and are often complicated by bleeding and infection, making management particularly challenging.3 Owing to variations in classification, anatomical distribution, and disease severity, treatment strategies for LM are heterogeneous, and no standardized guidelines have yet been established. Sclerotherapy, characterized by minimal invasiveness and rapid recovery, has gradually replaced traditional surgical approaches; however, its efficacy in microcystic LM is often limited, and repeated treatments may be required.4

Activating alterations involving the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway have been implicated in the development of lymphatic malformations, providing a mechanistic rationale for treatment with the mTOR inhibitor sirolimus. By inhibiting mTOR complex 1, sirolimus may suppress abnormal lymphatic endothelial-cell proliferation and lymphangiogenesis. Accordingly, the mTOR inhibitor sirolimus (rapamycin) has emerged as a rational therapeutic option, particularly for microcystic lesions that are typically difficult to manage. Although surgery, sclerotherapy, and laser therapy remain important treatment options, diffuse superficial microcystic lesions may respond incompletely, recur after treatment, or require repeated procedures. These limitations have prompted increasing interest in less invasive pharmacological approaches.5

Previous clinical studies support the potential activity of topical sirolimus in pediatric superficial vascular anomalies. In a multicenter case series of 18 patients treated exclusively with topical sirolimus, all patients reported some degree of improvement, and 50% reported marked improvement in at least one clinical manifestation, most commonly cutaneous blebs, lymphatic drainage, and bleeding.6 In another retrospective pediatric study of complex vascular anomalies, 11 patients received topical sirolimus, with an overall response rate of 72.7%; adverse events occurred in 27.3% and were predominantly local, with pruritus being the most frequently reported event.7 These findings suggest that topical sirolimus may improve clinically important cutaneous manifestations while generally producing manageable local adverse effects. However, both studies included heterogeneous vascular anomalies and topical preparations, and neither established the optimal formulation, treatment duration, recurrence rate, or long-term safety.

Compared with systemic administration, topical treatment is intended to deliver sirolimus directly to superficial lesions while potentially limiting systemic exposure, although percutaneous absorption may still occur. Because microcystic LMs frequently involve the superficial dermis and present with cutaneous vesicles or plaques, they may be particularly accessible to topical treatment. On this basis, topical sirolimus was selected as a noninvasive treatment for the pediatric patients included in this study.

Nevertheless, clinical evidence concerning standardized 0.2% sirolimus gel in pediatric superficial microcystic LMs remains limited, particularly regarding quantitative lesion response, symptom improvement, quality of life, and systemic drug exposure. Therefore, this single-center interventional study evaluated the short-term efficacy and safety of 0.2% topical sirolimus gel in 30 pediatric patients with superficial microcystic LMs over a 12-week treatment period.

Methods

Ethical Consideration

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital Affiliated with Zhengzhou University (Approval No. 2020-K-102) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before enrollment, the parents or legal guardians of all participants were informed of the potential benefits, adverse effects, and risks associated with topical sirolimus gel. Written informed consent for study participation and the off-label use of topical sirolimus gel was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants.

Study Design and Participants

This single-center, prospective interventional study was conducted in the Interventional Hemangioma and Vascular Surgery Department of Children’s Hospital Affiliated with Zhengzhou University between September 2020 and May 2026. The treatment and follow-up protocol was established before patient enrollment. The predefined treatment period and primary efficacy-assessment period for each participant was 12 weeks.

Patients were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) a diagnosis of superficial microcystic lymphatic malformation established by experienced vascular-anomaly clinicians based on characteristic clinical findings and supported by dermoscopy, ultrasonography, MRI, and/or histopathological examination, as clinically indicated; (2) lesions confined predominantly to the body surface without internal-organ involvement; (3) age ≥1 month at enrollment; and (4) ability to adhere to the prescribed treatment and follow-up schedule.

Patients were excluded if they: (1) had received surgery, sclerotherapy, laser therapy, or another treatment for the target lesion within 4 weeks before initiation of topical sirolimus; (2) had known hypersensitivity to sirolimus, any component of the gel formulation, or macrolide drugs; or (3) had another dermatologic condition, such as eczema or condyloma acuminatum, that could interfere with assessment of the target lesion.

Treatment discontinuation criteria included rapid disease progression, an increase of >20% in the target lesion after 1 month of treatment, or the occurrence of a severe treatment-related adverse event.

A total of 30 pediatric patients with superficial microcystic lymphatic malformations were enrolled. The cohort included 14 males and 16 females, aged 2–15 years. All patients met the predefined diagnostic and eligibility criteria. Seventeen patients had previously undergone treatments such as sclerotherapy, laser therapy, or surgery, with unsatisfactory outcomes before enrollment.

Diagnostic Assessment

The diagnosis of superficial microcystic lymphatic malformation was established by experienced vascular-anomaly clinicians based primarily on characteristic clinical findings, supplemented by dermoscopy and imaging examinations where clinically indicated. Characteristic manifestations included clusters of translucent, pale-red, or hemorrhagic vesicles or papules, with or without lymphorrhea, bleeding, hyperkeratosis, pain, or recurrent local infection. Dermoscopy was used to evaluate the superficial vesicular components, whereas color Doppler ultrasonography and/or MRI was performed when necessary to characterize the cystic components and anatomical extent of the lesion and to assess possible deeper-tissue involvement.

This diagnostic approach was consistent with published national recommendations indicating that physical examination and color Doppler ultrasonography generally permit the diagnosis of lymphatic malformations, while MRI is the preferred modality for defining lesion extent and characterizing microcystic and macrocystic components.8

Full-thickness skin biopsy was not routinely performed when the clinical, dermoscopic, and imaging findings were considered sufficiently characteristic and histopathological confirmation was unlikely to alter clinical management. The potential risks of bleeding, scarring, secondary infection, and delayed wound healing were also considered, particularly in pediatric patients and in lesions with fragile hemorrhagic vesicles or active lymphorrhea.

Skin biopsy with histopathological examination would have been considered if the presentation was atypical, the diagnosis remained uncertain, or an alternative diagnosis could not be excluded. Available cytological or cell-block findings from aspirated lesion or cyst fluid were reviewed as supportive evidence but were not regarded as equivalent to full-thickness histopathological confirmation because these specimens did not demonstrate the characteristic architecture of dilated dermal lymphatic channels.

Treatment Protocol and Rationale for Gel Selection

All participants received 0.2% topical sirolimus gel Xianluoli™ (sirolimus topical gel) 0.2%, twice daily, approximately every 12 hours, for 12 weeks. The gel was selected because it was a standardized topical sirolimus formulation available at the treating institution and could be applied directly to the superficial cutaneous component of microcystic lymphatic malformations. Previous formulation evidence indicated that gel vehicles may facilitate passive drug diffusion by increasing stratum corneum hydration, and a prior in vitro study demonstrated greater percutaneous absorption of 0.2% sirolimus gel than the corresponding ointment formulation.9 However, the present study did not directly compare different topical formulations such as ointment, cream, or solution.

At each application, the gel was applied as a thin, even layer over the target lesion. The amount applied was adjusted according to lesion size. One fingertip unit, approximately 0.5 g, was considered sufficient to cover a skin surface area equivalent to approximately two adult palms. Application to areas of active ulceration, erosion, or exudation was avoided.

Follow-Up and Outcome-Assessment Schedule

Participants were followed weekly during the 12-week treatment period through outpatient visits and, when an in-person visit was not feasible, remote communication using. Weekly follow-up focused primarily on treatment adherence, local skin reactions, other adverse events, and changes in clinical symptoms.

Efficacy assessments were performed at the predefined time points of week 1 and week 12. The week 1 assessment was used to document early changes in lesion appearance and symptoms. Week 12, corresponding to completion of the predefined treatment course, was the primary efficacy endpoint. Clinical assessments included examination of lesion color and extent, standardized clinical photography, evaluation of lesion-related symptoms, and assessment of pain intensity. Standardized photographs were obtained using consistent patient positioning, camera angle, distance, and lighting conditions whenever feasible.

Radiological examinations were not performed weekly in all participants. Paired color Doppler ultrasonography was available for 10 patients with measurable lesions and was used to compare the maximum diameter of the same target lesion at baseline and week 12. Ultrasound and/or MRI was additionally performed when clinically indicated to characterize lesion extent and involvement of deeper tissues.

Although available post-treatment follow-up information was collected to describe recurrence, disease progression, or maintenance of clinical improvement after week 12, longer-term assessments were not performed at uniform, predefined intervals in all participants. Therefore, long-term follow-up findings were analyzed descriptively and were not included in the primary efficacy analysis.

Efficacy and Safety Assessment

Assessments were independently conducted at baseline and at 12 weeks after treatment initiation by two senior vascular surgeons. Clinical outcomes—including changes in lesion color, diameter, and pain—were evaluated using the visual analogue scale (VAS) in combination with imaging findings. Changes in patients’ quality of life before and after treatment was evaluated using the Children’s Dermatology Life Quality Index (CDLQI) (Table 1).

At week 12, treatment response was categorized using a four-grade quartile improvement scale adapted from previous vascular-anomaly studies.10,11 Grade I indicated 0–25% improvement; Grade II, 26–50% improvement; Grade III, 51–75% improvement; and Grade IV, 76–100% improvement. Grades II–IV were considered treatment responses. The response grade represented the overall assessment based on changes from baseline in lesion color, extent, surface vesicles, associated symptoms, standardized clinical photographs, and available imaging findings. The two surgeons performed the assessments independently, and disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Local adverse events, including erythema, edema, pruritus, burning sensation, ulceration, and delayed wound healing, were assessed during weekly follow-up. Potential systemic adverse events, including infection and hepatic or renal dysfunction, were also monitored throughout treatment.

Blood sirolimus concentrations were measured at weeks 4 and 12. If a potentially treatment-related adverse event occurred, additional clinical evaluation and laboratory testing, including measurement of the blood sirolimus concentration when clinically indicated, were performed. Treatment was interrupted or discontinued if a severe treatment-related adverse event or clinically significant disease progression occurred.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Comparisons of pre- and post-treatment measurements were conducted using paired t-tests, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages [n (%)], and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square (χ2) test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General Characteristics

Pathological or cytological examination was available for 7 of the 30 patients (23.3%) (Figure 1). Two patients underwent examination of cell-block preparations derived from aspirated sediment, whereas five underwent cytological evaluation of aspirated lesion or cyst fluid. The specimens predominantly contained lymphocytes within amorphous fibrinous, eosinophilic, or proteinaceous material, accompanied by variable numbers of histiocytes and neutrophils and occasional eosinophils. These findings were compatible with lymphatic and inflammatory contents and provided supportive evidence for the clinical diagnosis; however, they did not constitute full-thickness histopathological confirmation.

All 30 pediatric patients completed more than 12 weeks of topical sirolimus treatment. Varying degrees of lesion reduction were observed in all cases. Overall efficacy evaluation demonstrated a response in 29 patients, yielding an effectiveness rate of 96% (29/30), while 1 patient showed only slight improvement. The cohort included 16 females and 14 males, with ages ranging from 2 to 15 years (mean age: 8.53 ± 3.38 years). Disease duration ranged from 1 to 8 years, with a mean duration of 46.03 ± 23.10 months. Lesion locations were distributed as follows: lumbar region (n = 5), chest wall (n = 4), lower extremities (n = 11; including thigh, knee, lower leg, and foot), upper extremities (n = 8; including upper arm and forearm), buttock (n = 1), and glans penis (n = 1). Among the patients, 14 presented with pain, 6 had ulceration, and 3 reported pruritus. Seventeen patients had a history of prior treatments, including surgical excision, laser therapy, compression therapy, and sclerotherapy (eg, intralesional pingyangmycin), all of which resulted in unsatisfactory outcomes, with some lesions demonstrating further progression.

Efficacy Evaluation

At 12 weeks of treatment, final efficacy assessment demonstrated Grade IV response in 10 patients (Figures 2 and 3), Grade III in 11 patients (Figures 4 and 5), Grade II in 8 patients (Figures 6 and 7), and Grade I in 1 patient. Color Doppler ultrasonography was performed in 10 patients to evaluate changes in lesion size. The mean maximum lesion diameter decreased from 3.22 ± 1.36 cm before treatment to 1.89 ± 1.33 cm after 12 weeks of therapy, representing a statistically significant reduction (t = 6.92, P < 0.05). Pain assessment showed that 14 patients reported pain-related symptoms at baseline. After 12 weeks of treatment, the mean pain score decreased significantly from 2.88 ± 0.78 to 1.18 ± 0.88 (t = 5.57, P < 0.05). The Children’s Dermatology Life Quality Index (CDLQI) score decreased significantly from 12.10 ± 3.16 before treatment to 6.77 ± 3.07 after treatment (t=9.94, P<0.0001) (Table 1).

Safety Analysis

During follow-up, adverse events were observed in 3 of the 30 patients. Specifically, 1 patient developed mild pruritus after application, 1 experienced erythema, and 1 reported a burning sensation. No severe adverse events, such as ulceration, exudation, hyperpigmentation, or new scar formation, were observed throughout topical treatment. All symptoms improved after symptomatic management. The mean blood sirolimus concentration was 1.35 ng/mL at 4 weeks and 1.22 ng/mL at 12 weeks. All patients maintained sirolimus levels below 2.0 ng/mL during the treatment period.

Discussion

The present study provides prospective, short-term clinical evidence regarding the use of 0.2% topical sirolimus gel in 30 pediatric patients with superficial microcystic lymphatic malformations. Previous studies have described topical sirolimus treatment for microcystic lymphatic malformations and other superficial vascular anomalies using heterogeneous formulations, including compounded solutions, ointments, creams, and gels. Therefore, the contribution of the present study is not the first use of topical sirolimus for these lesions, but the evaluation of a standardized 0.2% gel formulation in a relatively large single-center pediatric cohort.

At present, sirolimus is available in three topical formulations, including gel, ointment, and solution. Previous studies have indicated that the solution form is less desirable due to a higher likelihood of local irritation, whereas ointments are often poorly accepted because of their greasy texture and limited cosmetic appeal. In contrast, gel-based formulations are generally better tolerated, as they are non-greasy, easily spreadable, and cosmetically acceptable, which in turn improves patient adherence.11 From a pharmacological perspective, hydrophilic gels enhance drug delivery by promoting hydration of the stratum corneum. This process softens the epidermal barrier, enlarges intercellular spaces, and disrupts lipid organization, thereby reducing diffusion resistance. Consequently, skin permeability may increase two- to threefold, which is particularly relevant for relatively large molecules approaching 1000 Da. Sirolimus (molecular weight: 914 Da) benefits from this mechanism, as gel-induced hydration facilitates its passive transdermal diffusion.12 Consistently, in vitro studies have demonstrated significantly higher dermal absorption with sirolimus gel compared with ointment formulations (1358.5 ± 226.4 pg/mg vs 679.3 ± 71.6 pg/mg, P < 0.01), underscoring the superior penetration capacity and favorable tolerability of the 0.2% gel formulation.9

The most important finding of this study is that topical sirolimus gel produced significant and measurable reductions in lesion volume, likely due to improved skin penetration. At the predefined 12-week endpoint, 29 of the 30 patients achieved a Grade II–IV physician-assessed response, whereas one patient had a Grade I response. This overall assessment incorporated changes in lesion appearance, extent, associated symptoms, standardized clinical photographs, and available imaging findings. Because paired color Doppler ultrasonography was available for only 10 patients, objective lesion-size analysis was limited to this subgroup. In these patients, the mean maximum diameter of the same target lesion decreased significantly from 3.22 ± 1.36 cm at baseline to 1.89 ± 1.33 cm at week 12. Pain intensity also decreased significantly among the 14 patients with baseline pain. (P < 0.05) These results suggest that topical sirolimus gel effectively reduces lesion burden, alleviates symptoms, and improves cosmetic outcomes in children with superficial microcystic LMs.

A further clinically meaningful finding of this study is the substantial improvement in quality of life. The mean CDLQI score decreased from 12.10 ± 3.16 at baseline to 6.77 ± 3.07 at week 12 (t=9.94, P<0.0001). Superficial microcystic lymphatic malformations may affect quality of life through pain, pruritus, lymphorrhea, bleeding, recurrent infection, visible skin changes, and functional limitations. The observed reduction in CDLQI scores therefore complements the physician-assessed lesion response and imaging findings.

No statistically significant associations were identified between treatment response and sex, age, disease duration, lesion location, associated symptoms, previous treatment, or baseline lesion diameter. However, these analyses should be regarded as exploratory. The sample size was small, response-grade groups were uneven, and lesion diameter was available for only 10 patients. The absence of statistically significant associations therefore should not be interpreted as evidence that these characteristics do not influence treatment response.

From a safety standpoint, topical sirolimus gel showed a favorable profile. No significant local or systemic adverse effects were observed, and blood sirolimus levels in all patients remained well below the safety threshold (2.0 ng/mL). In contrast to oral sirolimus, known for systemic toxicities, the gel formulation achieved effective local delivery with minimal systemic exposure, supporting its suitability for long-term use, especially in children.

Notably, our study included one case of vascular malformations located in anatomically complex and sensitive regions, including the glans penis. Similar cases have been described in previous literature. For example, a case of recurrent mucocutaneous lymphatic malformation at the external urethral meatus showed rapid regression within two weeks of topical sirolimus gel initiation, with recurrence after discontinuation and renewed improvement following re-treatment.13 Another report described microcystic lymphatic malformations involving the scrotum and penile root that were refractory to surgery, cryotherapy, and intralesional sclerotherapy but showed significant reduction in lymphatic leakage and lesion size after daily topical sirolimus application.14 Together with our findings, these reports suggest that sirolimus-based topical therapy may offer a valuable noninvasive treatment option for superficial vascular malformations in delicate genital or mucocutaneous areas, particularly when conventional interventions fail or carry a high risk of recurrence.

Formulation-specific evidence in microcystic lymphatic malformations remains limited. In the multicenter Phase 2 PERFORMUS study, oral sirolimus improved pain, bleeding, oozing, quality of life, and lesion volume in children with pure lymphatic malformations. However, dose adjustment to maintain trough concentrations of 4–12 ng/mL required repeated therapeutic drug monitoring. Moreover, 56 of 59 patients experienced adverse events, including five serious events, with oral ulceration being the most common.15 These findings support the activity of systemic sirolimus but highlight its monitoring burden and potential systemic toxicity in pediatric patients.

Against this background, ongoing prospective trials are evaluating locally administered formulations that may deliver sirolimus directly to superficial lesions while potentially reducing, but not eliminating, systemic exposure. One multicenter randomized, assessor-blinded trial evaluates 1 mg/mL topical sirolimus solution for lingual microcystic lymphatic malformations compared with standard care.16 Another multicenter, within-person, randomized, double-blind trial evaluates 0.1% sirolimus ointment against its vehicle for cutaneous microcystic lymphatic malformations.17 Because these publications describe study protocols rather than completed efficacy results, they demonstrate the need for controlled, formulation-specific research but should not be interpreted as evidence of established clinical effectiveness. Their different concentrations, vehicles, anatomical sites, and outcome measures also limit direct comparison with the 0.2% gel evaluated in the present study.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted at a single center, included only 30 patients, lacked a concurrent control group, and used an open-label design. Paired ultrasonography was available for only one-third of the cohort. The predefined primary follow-up period was limited to 12 weeks, and longer-term assessments were not performed at uniform intervals in all participants. Therefore, the durability of response, recurrence following treatment discontinuation, symptom-free duration, and long-term safety cannot be determined. Histopathological confirmation was also not uniformly available, although diagnoses were established by experienced clinicians based on characteristic clinical findings supported by dermoscopy and imaging where clinically indicated. Finally, the study did not compare different sirolimus formulations or conventional treatments.

Conclusion

In conclusion, 0.2% topical sirolimus gel was associated with short-term improvement in physician-assessed lesion response, pain, lesion diameter in the imaging subgroup, and dermatology-related quality of life in pediatric patients with superficial microcystic lymphatic malformations. Adverse events during the 12-week treatment period were predominantly mild and local, while low blood sirolimus concentrations indicated that limited systemic absorption could occur. These findings should be regarded as preliminary rather than definitive evidence of efficacy, formulation superiority, or long-term safety. Larger multicenter randomized controlled trials incorporating standardized objective measurements, independent outcome assessment, pharmacokinetic monitoring, formulation comparisons, and extended post-treatment follow-up are warranted.
