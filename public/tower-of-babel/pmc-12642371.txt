Should routine HPV vaccination programs include males 25–29 years? A systematic review and global meta-analysis
Authors: Siran Wan, Shunhong Zhang, Saidi Hu, Chenchen Luo, Guangyue Liu, Luzimery dos Ramos da Conceição, Marinela da Costa Encarnação, Lin-Yong Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMales aged 25–29 years represent the peak transmission group for human papillomavirus (HPV). However, they are often excluded from routine vaccination programs due to concerns regarding cost-effectiveness and limited efficacy data. This study synthesized evidence on vaccine efficacy and herd effects to support the potential expansion of HPV vaccination to this demographic.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from January 1, 2007, to February 1, 2025. We included randomized controlled trials and observational studies that reported on the efficacy of the 4vHPV vaccine against incident HPV infections (4vHPV-type [6/11/16/18] or any HPV) in males, as well as herd effects on anogenital warts (AGW) in unvaccinated males aged 25–29 years following female vaccination. Exclusions comprised pediatric, HIV, and female-only cohorts, non-4vHPV vaccines, and immunogenicity-only reports. Two reviewers independently extracted summary-level data, and discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer. The primary outcomes were pooled relative risks (RRs) for HPV infection and AGW incidence, calculated using DerSimonian–Laird random-effects models. (PROSPERO: CRD420251104471)ResultsFrom 3,024 screened records, 28 studies (2 RCTs, 26 observational; n = 68,113,157 participants) were analyzed. Direct vaccination significantly reduced 4vHPV-type infections among males aged 13–26 years (RR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.18–0.65; I2 = 85.4%). Extrapolated to males aged 25–29 years (baseline risk = 35%), this conferred an absolute risk reduction (ARR) of 22.75% (number needed to treat [NNT] = 4, 95% CI: 3–5). Herd effects analysis demonstrated that when female vaccination coverage (FVC) reached ≥50%, unvaccinated males aged 25–29 years experienced a 37% reduction in AGW incidence over 5–8 years (RR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.48–0.84; I2 = 93.7%). This protection was significantly stronger than that in males aged 30–39 years (ΔRR = 0.05, p = 0.03) and increased cumulatively by 6% annually (p < 0.001). No such herd protection occurred with FVC < 50% (RR = 1.13–1.36).ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that extending HPV vaccination to males aged 25–29 years provides both individual and population-level benefits, particularly in settings with suboptimal female coverage. These findings support the inclusion of this demographic in vaccination programs and suggest that revising current age-restrictive policies could contribute to global efforts to eliminate HPV-related diseases. Future research should focus on real-world data to further strengthen the evidence base.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07384-4.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) represents a group of highly prevalent viruses [1], responsible for approximately 690,000 cancer cases globally each year [2, 3]. In males, HPV is the primary cause of anogenital cancers, oropharyngeal cancer, and anogenital warts (AGW) [4]. Notably, the prevalence of genital HPV infection among males reaches as high as 31%, with a peak of 35% in the 25–29 age group for any HPV type, significantly surpassing younger populations [5]. Consequently, effective prevention in high-risk groups is crucial for reducing the associated disease burden.

HPV prevention in males primarily relies on vaccination. However, males aged 25–29 years, a high-risk group, are excluded from routine vaccination programs according to current guidelines from authoritative bodies such as the World Health Organization (WHO), the European Cancer Organization’s HPV Action Network, the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), and the American Cancer Society (ACS) [1, 3, 6, 7] This exclusion stems from insufficient direct efficacy data and early modeling that suggested lower cost-effectiveness beyond age 26. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA) approval of the 9-valent HPV vaccine for males aged 27–45 diverges from ACIP guidance, which restricts routine catch-up vaccination to males aged 26 years or younger [6]. For adults aged 27–45, ACIP recommends an individualized approach via shared clinical decision-making [6]—a stance that ACS does not endorse [7] This discrepancy in official recommendations has consequently left the 25–29-year-old male cohort, a group with the highest documented incidence of HPV infection, without a clear and prioritized routine vaccination strategy. Current prevention strategies for males largely depend on female vaccination to generate herd immunity. A systematic review of 14 high-income countries, involving over 60 million individuals, revealed significant reductions (30–48%) in AGW among unvaccinated males aged 15–24 years in countries with high female vaccination coverage, particularly under multi-cohort strategies. [8] However, the population-level protection from these female-focused vaccination strategies is limited, with a waning effect in males aged 25 and older.

This age-based restriction and associated assumptions merit re-evaluation: firstly, the exclusion fails to account for direct efficacy data specific to males in the peak infection age group (25–29 years). Secondly, it overlooks the potential impact of vaccinating this group on disrupting HPV transmission networks, especially given their high-risk sexual behaviors (e.g., increased sexual activity, multiple partners) and in regions with low female vaccination coverage. As a result, current strategies inadequately protect males aged 25–29 during their peak period of HPV infection.

To address these evidence gaps, a systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the direct protective efficacy of the quadrivalent HPV (4vHPV) vaccine in males aged 25–29 years. Additionally, the study quantifies herd immunity thresholds to assess the scientific rationale for extending the upper age limit for male vaccination to 29 years, providing insight into optimized vaccine deployment and evidence-based revisions to male HPV vaccination policies.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This comprehensive review and meta-analysis adhered strictly to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Table S1). [9] The study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251104471). An exhaustive search of electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL), was conducted on February 1, 2025, targeting publications from January 1, 2007, to the search date. A consistent set of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and relevant keywords for titles and abstracts were used across all databases, with no language restrictions. The primary search strategy included: ((\Papillomavirus Vaccines[Majr] OR\HPV vaccin*[tiab]) AND (\Program Evaluation[Majr] OR\Immunization Programs[Majr] OR\Population Surveillance[Majr] OR\Sentinel Surveillance[Majr] OR\Incidence[Majr] OR\Prevalence[Majr] OR (\program evaluat*[tiab] OR\immunization program*[tiab] OR\disease surveillance[tiab] OR\epidemiolog*[tiab])) AND (\HPV infection*[tiab] OR\genital wart*[tiab])).mp. Additionally, this study manually reviewed reference lists from included studies, qualifying meta-analyses, and the ClinicalTrials.gov database to identify gray literature. Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Table S2.

A total of 3,024 records were initially identified. After the systematic review process [10] 28 studies [11–38] met the inclusion criteria: 2 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [11, 12] and 26 observational studies [13–38] (Fig. 1). These studies evaluated the efficacy of the 4vHPV vaccine in males or analyzed the impact of female vaccination on male health outcomes, particularly regarding herd immunity effects. Participants were required to have received at least one dose of the 4vHPV vaccine. The outcome measures reported included direct vaccine effectiveness, represented by relative risks (RRs) or data that could be calculated for HPV infections, specifically targeting quadrivalent human papillomavirus vaccine-type infections (4vHPV-type; types 6/11/16/18) and any HPV type (anyHPV) in males. Herd immunity effects were assessed through data on the incidence of AGW in males, which was selected as a primary outcome due to its high frequency, its role as an early marker of HPV vaccination program impact, and the availability of robust, comparable surveillance data across multiple countries and studies. Eligible studies provided directly extractable effect estimates (RR, OR, HR) or raw data for their calculation, using either RCT or observational methodologies. Studies were excluded if they focused solely on female or pediatric populations (under 13 years), individuals with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), non-4vHPV vaccines (e.g., bivalent or nonavalent), immunogenicity or antigenicity without clinical outcomes (HPV infection/AGW), case reports, animal studies, non-original research (such as commentaries), or if full texts or essential data were unavailable, as illustrated in Figure S1. Two reviewers (WSR, ZSH) independently screened the records, which involved removing duplicates, screening titles and abstracts, and assessing full texts against the inclusion criteria. Data analysis

The primary evaluation focused on the RR, analyzing HPV-related outcomes before and after vaccination. This study concentrated on two subgroups of HPV infections: 4vHPV-type and anyHPV. Investigators WSR, HSD, and ZSH extracted study characteristics and outcome data, including country, design, sample size, age group, vaccine coverage, and outcome measures (RR, OR), using standardized forms. Bias risk in observational studies was assessed using the ROBINS-I tool, which incorporated a comprehensive quality and bias assessment table covering various aspects such as study details, sample characteristics, exposure and outcome measurement, bias domains, statistical analysis, and overall evaluation [39], as illustrated in Table S3, S4. Additionally, the RoB 2 tool was applied to evaluate RCTs (Figure S2) [40].

Primary outcomes of the study included direct efficacy, represented by the RR for HPV infection, and herd immunity, indicated by changes in the incidence of AGW. Only studies involving males aged 13–26 who received the 4vHPV vaccine were included, comparing the incidence of HPV-related outcomes before and after vaccination. With an estimated baseline risk of HPV infection at 35% for males aged 25 to 29 years [5] this study calculated the absolute risk reduction (ARR) and the number needed to treat (NNT) for both outcomes. [41] Subgroup analyses [42] differentiated HPV prevalence before and after vaccination between men who have sex with men (MSM) and the general male population.

Given the lack of studies directly vaccinating males aged 25–29, this study focused on studies reporting HPV-related endpoint incidence in males aged 25–29 and 30–39 after females received the 4vHPV vaccine. Stratified analyses were performed for all endpoints based on age and the number of years since the HPV vaccine was introduced. To analyze AGW incidence rates, the RR was divided into two follow-up periods post-vaccination: 1–4 years and 5–8 years. To account for vaccination coverage, the overall percentage of vaccinated individuals in the relevant country or region was used. Studies were classified as High Coverage (≥50% coverage with at least two doses) or Low Coverage ( < 50% coverage with at least one dose) [8] Studies explicitly reporting coverage levels were assigned accordingly.

To manage significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model, specifically the DerSimonian–Laird method [43] was employed. Summary estimates of HPV vaccine efficacy for each endpoint were calculated on the log scale using this model in R software (version 4.5.0) with the metafor and robvis packages. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic [44] Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding individual studies to test the robustness of our findings, as well as by limiting the analysis to studies with a low risk of bias. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s linear regression test (with p < 0.10 considered significant) and by visual inspection of funnel plots [45] Meta-regression was conducted to explore potential dose–response relationships between the protective effect on AGW and variables such as male age, follow-up duration, and vaccination coverage [46] All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (with the metafor and robvis packages) and RevMan version 5.4 [47].

Results

Through a comprehensive literature search and evaluation, 28 studies were identified that met the inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis (Fig. 1) [11–38] The study characteristics can be found in Table 1. This selection comprised 6 investigations [11–16] assessing the efficacy of 4vHPV vaccine in males, including 2 RCTs [11, 12] and 4 cross-sectional studies [13–16] analyzing HPV infection rates before and after vaccination. Additionally, 22 observational studies [17–38] examined the impact of female 4vHPV vaccination on the occurrence of AGW in males aged 25–29 years, which is the primary focus cohort. A parallel assessment was conducted for the 30 to 39-year age group to quantify age-dependent gradients in herd protection. A total of 68,113,157 participants from 18 high-income countries were included to evaluate the efficacy of 4vHPV vaccine, with follow-up periods extending up to eight years. Six studies evaluated the efficacy of 4vHPV vaccine in preventing HPV infections among males, primarily in the age range of 13 to 26 years. Pooled RRs and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for two key outcomes: First, the infection with any HPV type (anyHPV), which yielded an RR of 0.49 (95% CI: 0.27–0.88; I2 = 96.5%). Second, 4vHPV-type (types 6/11/16/18), which resulted in an RR of 0.35 (95% CI: 0.18–0.65; I2 = 85.4%). The vaccine demonstrated significantly greater protection against 4vHPV-type, with an RR reduction (RRR) of 65%, compared to anyHPV (RRR = 51%), indicating a 14-percentage point difference in RRR. The ARR for 4vHPV-type was 22.75% (95% CI calculated from baseline CI and RR), resulting in an NNT of 4 (95% CI: 3–5). In contrast, the ARR for anyHPV infection was 17.85%, leading to an NNT of 6 (95% CI: 5–7). Vaccination targeting 4vHPV-type in males aged 25 to 29 years demonstrated a substantial benefit, with an ARR difference of 4.9% (22.75% versus 17.85%). This enhanced efficiency is evident, requiring 33% fewer vaccinations to prevent one infection (NNT = 4 versus NNT = 6). In the heterosexual and MSM group, the RR was 0.29 (95% CI: 0.07–1.25; I2 = 80.4%; p = 0.006), reflecting a 71% reduction in risk. Sensitivity analysis of the 4vHPV vaccine direct efficacy confirmed the robustness of the primary results, showing a significantly greater level of protection against 4vHPV-type (RR = 0.35) compared to anyHPV (RR = 0.49). Notably, RCTs consistently demonstrated strong and uniform protection against 4vHPV-type (RR = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.10–0.22). In contrast, observational studies exhibited considerable variability (RR range: 0–1.06), primarily due to differences in demographic factors (MSM versus heterosexual populations) and varying control of behavioral confounding factors. Despite significant heterogeneity (I2 > 85%), statistically significant outcomes were maintained across all sensitivity analyses, including the removal of outliers and subgroup analyses, further validating the reliability of the findings (Figure S4). Funnel plot analysis showed in figure S5, coupled with Egger’s linear regression tests, revealed no significant publication bias for either endpoint. Specifically, the Egger’s intercept for anyHPV was 0.98 (p = 0.39), while for 4vHPV-type, the intercept was −0.23 (p = 0.78). These results indicate a balanced distribution of effect sizes around the corresponding summary RRs of 0.49 and 0.35, suggesting minimal risk of publication bias, even amid substantial heterogeneity.

This study examines the herd protection effect of female HPV vaccination on the incidence of anogenital warts (AGW) in males. Twenty-two observational studies assessed the impact of female 4vHPV vaccination coverage and follow-up duration on the incidence of AGW in unvaccinated males aged 25–29 years, identified as the peak transmission cohort. The 30–39 age group was included as a comparator to quantify the age-dependent attenuation of herd protection. Subgroup analyses demonstrated significant effects of both vaccination coverage and follow-up duration. Specifically, among males aged 25–29 years, high female vaccination coverage (≥50%) coupled with long-term follow-up (5–8 years) resulted in an RR of 0.63 (95% CI: 0.48–0.84; I2 = 93.7%), reflecting a 37% reduction in risk. In the 30–39 age group, the RR was 0.68 (95% CI: 0.62–0.75; I2 = 7.3%), indicating a 32% risk reduction. In contrast, when female vaccination coverage was low ( < 50%), no significant protective effect was observed in either age group, with estimates suggesting a potential increase in risk. For the 25–29 age group, the RR was 1.13 (95% CI: 0.89–1.44; I2 = 98.6%), and for 30–39 years, the RR was 1.36 (95% CI: 1.11–1.67; I2 = 97.0%). The protective effect was notably stronger in the younger cohort than in the older group (ΔRR = 0.05, p = 0.03). Additionally, the protective effect increased significantly with each additional year of follow-up (β = −0.07 per year, p = 0.002). A mixed-effects meta-regression analysis of 44 independent estimates revealed significant results. High vaccination coverage (≥50%) was associated with a 41% reduction in the risk of AGW in males (β = −0.41; 95% CI: −0.49 to −0.33; p < 0.001), supporting the herd immunity threshold concept. The age gradient indicated that for each additional year of age, the protective effect diminished by 5% (β = −0.05; 95% CI: −0.07 to −0.03; p < 0.001), with males aged 25–29 exhibiting a 27.5% greater risk reduction than those aged 30–39. Furthermore, the time-cumulative effect demonstrated that each additional year of follow-up resulted in a further 6% reduction in risk (β = −0.06; 95% CI: −0.08 to −0.04; p < 0.001), leading to a 48% increase in protection at 8 years post-vaccination compared to 4 years. The model explained 68.3% of the observed variability (conditional R2), though significant residual heterogeneity remained (τ2 = 0.15, p < 0.001), suggesting that unaccounted factors, such as geographic variations, may still influence the results (Figure S7).

This study examined the interaction between varying levels of vaccination coverage and follow-up duration across different age groups. Among individuals aged 25–29 years with low coverage and extended follow-up (5–8 years), results exhibited significant variability (I2 = 98.6%) with divergent outcomes. Some studies reported an increased risk (e.g., Flagg 2013/2018, RR = 1.79 [20, 32]), while others showed a protective effect (e.g., Ali 2013/Callander 2016, RR = 0.44 [26, 34]). With high coverage, a consistently protective effect was observed (RR = 0.44–0.85), though heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 93.7%). For short-term follow-up (3–4 years), considerable methodological inconsistency was evident, especially in low-coverage studies (I2 = 99.9%), with stark differences in effect estimates (e.g., Mikolajczyk 2013/Thönes 2017, RR = 0.11 [21, 31] vs. Flagg 2013/2018, RR = 1.65 [20, 32]), suggesting significant selection bias. In the 30–39 age group, high coverage demonstrated reliable protection over the long term (RR = 0.66–0.92, I2 = 7.3%), while low coverage yielded mixed results (e.g., Cocchio 2017, RR = 1.31 [23] vs. Oliphant 2011/2017, RR = 0.77 [22, 38]). In the short term, high-coverage groups showed stable protective effects (RR ≈ 0.92–0.94), surpassing low-coverage groups (RR = 0.94–1.18). Sensitivity analysis revealed that the extreme variability in the 25–29 age group (I2 > 99%) was primarily due to differences in coverage (mean RR for low coverage ≈1.35 vs. high coverage ≈0.64) and methodological inconsistencies (e.g., observation duration and control of confounding factors). The heterogeneity in the 30–39 age group was lower (I2 = 78.3–97.0%), indicating more reliable results for this demographic. Short-term data appeared to inflate protective effects (e.g., RR = 0.11), while long-term data necessitated careful interpretation regarding cumulative confounding. A significant threshold effect was identified: when female coverage reached 50% or more, heterogeneity in the 25–29 age group decreased significantly (from 98.6% to 93.7%), with consistent protective effects observed (RR < 1). This effect was less pronounced in the 30–39 age group (Fig. 5). Evaluation of publication bias, using visual funnel plots and Egger’s test, indicated a low level of overall publication bias in studies examining AGW. In the comprehensive analysis (n = 44), the funnel plot exhibited symmetry, and Egger’s test yielded a p-value of 0.539 (Figure S8). For the 25–29 age group (n = 24), the funnel plot maintained symmetry, with Egger’s test producing a p-value of 0.937. In the 30–39 age group (n = 20), the plot displayed a slight rightward skew, though it remained within acceptable limits; Egger’s test showed a p-value of 0.048, suggesting potential publication bias warranting further attention (Figure S9). Notably, all funnel plots maintained the typical inverted triangle shape, with the summary effect size centrally located, indicating a minimal risk of selection bias.

Discussion

This study synthesizes high-quality evidence from 28 studies [11–38] involving over 60 million individuals across 18 high-income countries, to provide epidemiological support for extending HPV vaccination catch-up programs to males aged 25–29 years. The analysis yields three key findings that strongly justify this recommendation. Firstly, direct vaccination with the 4vHPV vaccine demonstrates outstanding efficacy against 4vHPV-type, with a pooled RR of 0.35 (95% CI: 0.18–0.65), reflecting a 65% RRR-one of the highest protective effects observed for any adult vaccination program. With a peak HPV prevalence of 35% (95% CI: 30–41%) in this age group [5] these results translate to an ARR of 22.75% and an exceptionally favorable NNT of just 4 (95% CI: 3–5) to prevent one infection. This remarkable efficiency exceeds that of many routine adult vaccines. For example, influenza vaccine use not only reduced the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (RR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.52–0.87, NNT = 37) [48] but also decreased sepsis (RR: 1.361–1.450, 95% CI 1.123–1.699, NNT = 286) and stroke (RR: 1.451–1.580, 95% CI 1.075–2.034, NNT = 625) among SARS-CoV-2-positive patients across all time points [49] Meanwhile, it approaches the cost-effectiveness benchmarks typically reserved for pediatric vaccination programs [50, 51] suggesting that current age-based recommendations may require reconsideration.

The analysis also revealed critical threshold effects in herd protection: meaningful indirect protection for unvaccinated males aged 25–29 was observed only in settings where female vaccination coverage reached ≥ 50%. This was reflected in a 37% reduction in AGW incidence over 5–8 years (RR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.48–0.84). Below this threshold (RR = 1.13), no measurable herd effect was detected, indicating significant protection gaps in regions with suboptimal female vaccination. Meta-regression analysis further quantified two important gradients: firstly, the magnitude of indirect protection was 27.5% stronger in males aged 25–29 compared to those aged 30–39 (β = −0.05 per year, p < 0.001), and secondly, a consistent 6% annual increase in protective effect (β = −0.06 per year, p < 0.001) was observed with the increasing duration of vaccination programs. These findings highlight the 25–29 age group as both biologically and epidemiologically critical for achieving optimal individual and population-level protection before the natural waning of vaccine efficacy in older age groups.

The convergence of three key findings—robust direct efficacy against 4vHPV-type in males aged 25–29 (NNT = 4, 95% CI: 3–5), an absolute risk reduction (ARR) of 22.75%, preventing approximately 228 infections per 1,000 vaccinated individuals, significantly enhanced herd protection compared to older males, a 27.5% greater reduction in AGW risk (βage = −0.05/year, p < 0.001), and a steep age-dependent efficacy decline (βage = −0.05/year, p < 0.001)—strongly justifies prioritizing this cohort for catch-up vaccination programs. Meta-regression confirms that female vaccination coverage of ≥ 50% generates substantial herd immunity (βcoverage = −0.41; 41% AGW risk reduction), whereas below this threshold, male vaccination becomes essential (AGW RR = 1.13–1.36). This strategy offers dual protection: direct individual efficacy and population-level impact through transmission interruption, while also addressing equity gaps for MSM (RR = 0.36). A significant time-cumulative effect (βtime = −0.06/year, p < 0.001) yields 48% greater efficacy over 8 years compared to 4 years, demonstrating compounding protective benefits. Although residual heterogeneity (τ2 = 0.15) suggests the influence of unmeasured geographic factors [52], the model explains 68.3% of variability (conditional R2).

Research findings indicate the need to reconsider current immunization policies, particularly the age limit for male catch-up vaccination, which restricts eligibility to individuals aged 26 or younger [1, 3, 6, 7] illustrating the existing cost-effectiveness models require further evaluation. The present study demonstrates that 4vHPV vaccination in males aged 25–29 offers strong protection against 4vHPV-type, with an NNT of 4 (95% CI: 3–5), indicating significantly greater efficacy than previously reflected in current models. Besides, in regions with suboptimal female vaccination coverage ( < 50%), where no meaningful herd protection has been observed for male AGW incidence (RR ≥ 1.13 in males aged 25–29), direct male vaccination is illustrated to become essential in the present study. Notably, this approach addresses health inequities faced by MSM populations, providing substantial protection against HPV infection (RR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.18–0.72), which is not achieved under female-only vaccination programs [53]. Future economic evaluations should account for broader benefits, such as HPV transmission interruption and the prevention of HPV-related cancers in males, alongside quantitative projections for cervical cancer risk reduction and trends in oropharyngeal cancer.

These findings highlight the urgent need for more evidence, particularly with regard to global health equity. Well-powered RCTs comparing the 4vHPV vaccine and the 9-valent HPV vaccine (HPV 9 v), including single-dose regimens, are crucial. These trials should focus on males aged 25–29, especially in high-prevalence regions with low female vaccination coverage, to maximize potential benefits. Furthermore, these trials should integrate comprehensive cost-effectiveness analyses, capturing both direct male benefits and indirect female protection, including anticipated reductions in cervical cancer incidence and healthcare savings [54]. Additionally, developing dynamic transmission models based on region-specific sexual network data and vaccination coverage patterns is essential for refining international guidelines and quantifying population-level impacts in diverse epidemiological settings.

Assessing the long-term efficacy and real-world effectiveness of male vaccination requires a shift in focus toward oncogenic HPV types and cancer endpoints, extending beyond the current evidence based on genital warts and short-term infections. This shift is essential for fully understanding the public health value of male vaccination in preventing HPV-related cancers, particularly those that disproportionately affect men, such as the rising incidence of oropharyngeal cancer. Evaluating feasibility in resource-limited settings demands innovative strategies that account for competing priorities and infrastructure limitations, potentially integrating HPV vaccination delivery with existing HIV prevention [55] or school-based health programs [56, 57].

Exploration of integration should include piloting targeted catch-up programs alongside cost-effective screening methods, such as HPV self-sampling, in regions with persistently low female vaccination coverage. These initiatives should involve a formal evaluation of single-dose regimens to improve cost-effectiveness and scalability in low-resource settings, while ensuring long-term protection. Incorporating male vaccination data into global monitoring frameworks, such as expanding WHOs cervical cancer elimination targets to include gender-neutral indicators [58], is crucial for tracking herd immunity progress and ensuring equitable access to HPV prevention worldwide. This integration would allow for strategy comparisons while accommodating regional variations.

The implementation roadmap must consider the unique challenges faced by low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), balancing male vaccination efforts with other public health priorities. Drawing from successful vaccine rollouts that emphasize community engagement, multi-sectoral collaboration, and innovative delivery through alternative channels will be key. At the same time, high-income countries with established female vaccination programs should assess the incremental benefits of adding male vaccination, evaluating both the direct protection for males and its potential to accelerate HPV elimination through enhanced herd immunity, especially in the 25–29 age group.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, studies evaluating direct protection displayed substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 85.4–96.5%), primarily due to inconsistent adjustments for key behavioral confounders, such as the number of sexual partners and MSM status. This methodological variability, combined with our focus on composite vaccine-type HPV infection without anatomical site stratification, introduces potential residual confounding that may slightly inflate efficacy estimates. However, sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the primary findings. A second critical limitation concerns generalizability. Our reliance on data from high-income countries limits the applicability of the findings to resource-limited settings, where differing HPV prevalence profiles, distinct sexual network dynamics, and healthcare access barriers may significantly alter intervention impact. Third, the specificity of the outcomes restricts our assessment of herd immunity. Evidence is drawn solely from AGW caused by non-oncogenic HPV types 6/11, leaving the protective effects against oncogenic strains (HPV16/18) to be inferred rather than empirically confirmed. Dedicated studies on oncogenic endpoints are necessary for validation. Finally, extrapolating age-related efficacy estimates warrants caution. Protection estimates for the crucial 25–29-year cohort are partly based on efficacy data from younger populations (13–26 years), highlighting the need for targeted randomized trials specifically designed for this age group.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that extending HPV vaccination to males aged 25–29 provides both individual and population-level benefits, particularly in regions with suboptimal female coverage or among key populations like MSM. The findings support extending the upper age limit for male vaccination to 29 in areas with low female coverage, while recommending the inclusion of this demographic in surveillance systems where coverage is high. Although limitations exist, targeted vaccination in this age group could bridge individual protection and community immunity, potentially advancing global efforts to eliminate HPV-related diseases [1, 59]. Implementation strategies should translate these findings into actionable policies, while future research should prioritize real-world data to strengthen the evidence base.
