Grid Point Approximation for Distributed Nonparametric Smoothing and Prediction
Yuan Gao, Rui Pan, Feng Li, Riquan Zhang, Hansheng Wang

Abstract
Kernel smoothing is a widely used nonparametric method in modern statistical analysis. The problem of efficiently conducting kernel smoothing for a massive dataset on a distributed system is a problem of great importance. In this work, we find that the popularly used one-shot type estimator is highly inefficient for prediction purposes. To this end, we propose a novel grid point approximation (GPA) method, which has the following advantages. First, the resulting GPA estimator is as statistically efficient as the global estimator under mild conditions. Second, it requires no communication and is extremely efficient in terms of computation for prediction. Third, it is applicable to the case where the data are not randomly distributed across different machines. To select a suitable bandwidth, two novel bandwidth selectors are further developed and theoretically supported. Extensive numerical studies are conducted to corroborate our theoretical findings. Two real data examples are also provided to demonstrate the usefulness of our GPA method.



Keywords:
Bandwidth selection, communication efficiency, divide-and-conquer, nonparametric kernel smoothing

1
Introduction

Kernel smoothing refers to a general class of techniques for nonparametric estimation
(
Wand and Jones,, 1994
)
. Kernel smoothing is a powerful statistical tool and has been found to be extremely useful for density estimation
(
Parzen,, 1962
;
Sheather,, 2004
)
, nonparametric and semiparametric regression
(
Fan,, 1992
;
Fan and Zhang,, 1999
;
Xia et al.,, 2004
;
Li and Liang,, 2008
)
, longitudinal data analysis
(
Fan and Li,, 2004
;
Sun et al.,, 2007
)
, covariance modelling
(
Yin et al.,, 2010
)
, and many other applications. The basic idea of nonparametric kernel smoothing is to conduct statistical estimation based on data from a sufficiently compact local region. Depending on the type of statistical method for local estimation, different kernel smoothing methods can be employed. The two most popularly used methods are the local constant method
(
Nadaraya,, 1965
;
Watson,, 1964
)
, and the local linear regression method
(
Fan and Gijbels,, 1996
;
Loader,, 2006
)
. However, the effectiveness of kernel smoothing methods depends on the effective size of the local region, which is well controlled by the bandwidth parameter
(
Jones et al.,, 1996
)
. If the bandwidth is too large, then the subsequent estimation bias could be substantial. In contrast, if the bandwidth is too small, the variability of the resulting estimator could be substantial. Consequently, the trade-off between estimation bias and variability in terms of bandwidth selection has been a critical research problem in the literature
(
Hardle and Marron,, 1985
;
Fan and Gijbels,, 1995
;
Xia and Li,, 2002
)
.

Datasets of massive size are often encountered in modern statistical analysis. This leads to both opportunities and challenges. On the one hand, massive datasets lead to extremely large sample sizes. This gives nonparametric kernel smoothing powerful support to combat the curse of dimensionality and, thus, the ability to capture more flexible function shapes. On the other hand, large sample sizes also pose serious challenges to computation. Often, the amount of data is too large to be loaded into the computer memory simultaneously. Consequently, the full dataset must be divided into many nonoverlapping subsets with much reduced sample sizes. Subsequently, subsets can be processed on different computers in parallel. The results are then reported to a central computer for final assembly, which leads to a more powerful final estimator. This is an effective method for handling massive datasets and is referred to as divide-and-conquer
(
Gao et al.,, 2022
)
.

In fact, divide-and-conquer is an extremely useful technique for the analysis of massive datasets. For parametric model estimation, the one-shot averaging approach has been frequently adopted
(
Zhang et al.,, 2013
;
Volgushev et al.,, 2019
)
. The basic idea is to assemble a global estimator by averaging the local estimators. The advantage of the one-shot approach is that the communication cost is minimal since only one round of communication between the central and local machines is needed. However, the disadvantage is also apparent; the statistical efficiency may not be optimal unless a sufficient amount of data are randomly assigned to local machines
(
Wang et al.,, 2017
;
Jordan et al.,, 2019
;
Wang et al.,, 2021
)
.
As an alternative, some iterative approaches have been developed that implement multiple rounds of communication. One important iterative approach is the one-step method.
For instance,
Huang and Huo, (2019)
propose a one-step approach that utilizes a single Newton-Raphson update with one additional round of communication. The idea of one-step updating is also adopted for quantile regression in a recent work of
Pan et al., (2022)
.
For high-dimensional response data,
Hector and Song, (2020)
and
Hector and Song, (2021)
propose one-step meta-estimators under the scheme of distributed computation.
However, many one-step methods require computing and transmitting matrices (e.g., the Hessian matrix) to every local machine. To reduce the communication cost,
Jordan et al., (2019)
develop a communication-efficient surrogate likelihood framework, where the local Hessian matrix is used for updating the parameter, as opposed to the whole sample matrix. Consequently, the communication cost due to the transmission of matrices is reduced. This technique has been used in many iterative methods; for example, see

Wang et al., (2019)
and
Fan et al., (2021)
.

Despite their practical usefulness for massive data analysis, most existing divide-and-conquer methods focus on distributed methods for various parametric models. Nonparametric and/or semiparametric models are extremely useful in practice but are much less well developed for application to massive datasets. Successful applications include financial econometrics
(
Fan,, 2000
)
, longitudinal data analysis
(
Wu and Zhang,, 2006
)
, functional data analysis
(
Ferraty and Vieu,, 2006
)
, and many others. For a good summary, we refer to
Härdle et al., (2004)
. Consequently, there is a natural and practical need to develop distributed methods for nonparametric models. However, research in this area has been very limited. One possible reason is that developing distributed estimation methods for nonparametric models is considerably more challenging than that for parametric models. The key difference is that the target parameter of parametric models is of finite dimension. In contrast, the parameter in nonparametric models is often of infinite dimension (e.g., a nonparametric smooth curve). The statistically and computationally efficient implementation of distributed estimation for nonparametric models is a problem of great interest
(
Zhang et al.,, 2015
;
Shang and Cheng,, 2017
;
Chang et al.,, 2017
)
.

Other researchers have studied this problem and there have been two main branches of research.
This first is the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) based method, where the associated estimator is obtained by solving a regularized least squares problem in a given RKHS.
To construct the distributed estimator, the one-shot approach is commonly used; see, for example,
Zhang et al., (2015)
,
Lin et al., (2017)
, and
Shang and Cheng, (2017)
.
The second is the kernel smoothing method. In this area, two important pioneering works have been published in the literature. The first is
Li et al., (2013)
, who developed a divide-and-conquer type approach for nonparametric kernel density estimation. Under the assumption that a globally optimal bandwidth
O(N^{-1/5})
can be used where
N
is the total sample size, the authors show that the resulting density estimator can be as good as the optimal estimator.
The second work is developed by
Chang et al., (2017)
, who study the problem of distributed nonparametric regression by kernel smoothing.
The authors propose a one-shot type estimation method under the assumption that the data are randomly distributed across local machines. They investigate the theoretical properties and find that some restrictive conditions must be imposed on the number of local machines to achieve the optimal convergence rate.
To this end, they provide two improved algorithms based on different bandwidth selection procedures.
In this paper, we restrict our attention to nonparametric kernel smoothing.

These two pioneering works provide some important contributions to the existing literature. First, to the best of our knowledge, they are among the first group of researchers to study the distributed computation problem for nonparametric kernel smoothing. Second, they present the fundamental theoretical framework for distributed nonparametric kernel smoothing. Their research inspires a number of interesting topics for future study. First, in many cases, data are not randomly distributed across local machines
(
Zhu et al.,, 2021
)
. Thus, developing a statistically efficient estimator not depending on randomly distributed data is important. Second, as pointed out by
Li et al., (2013)
, for optimal estimation accuracy, the globally optimal bandwidth should be used. However, another challenging problem is how to practically estimate this bandwidth parameter for a massive dataset. To solve this problem,
Li et al., (2013)
propose a heuristic bandwidth selection rule, but its theoretical properties are not provided.
Chang et al., (2017)
also suggest two bandwidth selection procedures. However, they require a whole sample-based cross-validation step. This is practically extremely difficult to implement, especially when the massive dataset is split between many different local machines.

Lastly, we want to emphasize that nonparametric prediction is a problem with real-time applications but also poses great challenges, especially for massive datasets. On one hand, many real-time applications require extensive predictions. For example, air ticket or travel insurance agencies need to provide customers with real-time predictions regarding flight delays for each departure. These predictions are crucial as they are influenced by the daily variability in weather conditions. Similarly, a web-based agency specializing in used car sales might experience a surge in inquiries, with millions of requests for price prediction on second-hand cars with specific conditions within minutes during promotion events. To our best knowledge, existing techniques cannot effectively address the aforementioned practical demands on real-time predictions.
On the other hand, nonparametric prediction by kernel smoothing can be computationally expensive because it requires searching through the entire training dataset for each new data point
(
Kpotufe,, 2010
)
.
The RKHS based method also encounters a similar computational issue. Specifically, one needs to invert an
N\times N
Gram matrix, where
N
is the size of the training dataset
(
Zhang,, 2005
)
.
This can be computationally expensive or even infeasible when
N
is very large. Therefore, conducting nonparametric prediction in a distributed way is an important issue.
Recent distributed computing methods based on the one-shot approach can help reduce the computational burden; see, for example,
Zhang et al., (2015)
,
Lin et al., (2017)
, and
Chang et al., (2017)
.
However, these methods need to send every newly observed data point to each local machine to compute the local estimate. Subsequently, these local estimates are sent back to the central machine to form the final predictor.
This procedure not only incurs substantial communication costs but also requires that all local machines are running normally throughout the entire prediction phase
(
Lv and Zhou,, 2022
)
. Then, how to make predictions on a distributed system in a more efficient way becomes a problem of great interest.

The above discussion is the inspiration for this work. Specifically, we propose a novel distributed estimation and prediction method for nonparametric regression. For convenience, we refer to it as a method of grid point approximation (GPA) and the resulting estimators are referred to as the GPA estimators.
To implement the GPA method, the following key steps are needed. First, pre-specify some grid points and calculate a number of local moment statistics at these points on each local machine. The idea of grid points selection is in spirit of binning techniques, which are proposed by
Fan and Marron, (1994)
and
Wand, (1994)
. This technique is particularly useful in accelerating the computation of kernel estimators. Second, transmit these local moment statistics to the central machine so that the global kernel estimators for these grid points can be exactly constructed. These global estimators are essential for computing the GPA estimator. Last, on the central machine, linear or higher order interpolation is conducted to estimate or predict a given observation by using the global estimators computed on the grid points.

The newly proposed method has the following important features. First, our method imposes no requirement for the distribution of data across local machines. Any type of data distribution (e.g., homogeneous or heterogeneous distribution) is permitted. Second, our method does not require a minimal sample size for local machines. The sample sizes on different local machines can be arbitrarily large or small. Third, the newly proposed distributed GPA method is extremely efficient in communication in the sense that prediction can be conducted on the central machine with zero communication cost. We also want to emphasize that an industry standard for handling these intensive predictions is to cache or persist the prediction model in memory for fast prediction and reduced latency. In-memory caching allows the system to handle a high volume of queries efficiently by reducing the load on the raw database. With such an infrastructure, when a user queries the price of a specific car, the system quickly retrieves the necessary model parameters from the cache to provide an instant price estimate. Our GPA methods can be easily cached in the system for intensive prediction tasks, such as users frequently querying the estimated price of a used car.
We show theoretically and numerically that the GPA estimators can be statistically as efficient as the globally optimal estimator. We also develop two novel bandwidth selectors for the GPA estimator with rigorous theoretical support.
Finally, we expand the GPA method by incorporating a higher order polynomial interpolation and extending it to the multivariate scenario.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce our newly proposed GPA method, including the estimation, prediction, and bandwidth selection procedures.
In Section 3, we discuss two extensions of the GPA method.
In Section 4, we present numerical studies to support our theoretical findings.
Section 5 includes real data analysis for illustrative purposes.
The article is concluded in Section 6. All the technical proofs and extra numerical studies are included in the appendices.

2
The Main Methodology

2.1
Model and Notations

Let
(X_{i},Y_{i})
be the observation collected from the
i
-th subject with
1\leq i\leq N
, where
N
represents the entire sample size. Furthermore,
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the response of interest, and
X_{i}=(X_{i1},\dots,X_{ip})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the associated
p
-dimensional covariate.
To flexibly model the regression relationship between
Y_{i}
and
X_{i}
, a nonparametric regression model can be assumed as follows
(
Wand and Jones,, 1994
;
Fan and Gijbels,, 1996
)
:

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\mu(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},

where
\varepsilon_{i}
is the independent random noise term with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, and
\mu(\cdot)
is an unknown but sufficiently smooth function.
For simplicity, we first assume that
X_{i}
is a random and univariate predictor (i.e.,
p=1
) and is compactly supported on
[0,1]
. Furthermore, we also assume that the random noise
\varepsilon_{i}
is independent of
X_{i}
.

The objective is to estimate
\mu(\cdot)
nonparametrically. To this end, the standard nonparametric kernel smoothing method is frequently used.
Specifically, let
K(\cdot)
be a kernel function. It is typically taken to be a continuous probability density function symmetric about
0
.
Next, define
K_{h}(u)=K(u/h)/h
with some positive bandwidth
h>0
.
Then, for an arbitrarily fixed point
x\in(0,1)
, we can estimate
\mu(x)
by minimizing the following locally weighted least squares objective function:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}(x)=\operatorname*{argmin}_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(Y_{i}-a)^{2}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)Y_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)}.

(1)

The resulting estimator
\widehat{\mu}(x)
is often referred to the local constant estimator, also known as the Nadaraya-Watson (NW) estimator
(
Wand and Jones,, 1994
;
Fan and Gijbels,, 1996
;
Li and Racine,, 2007
)
.
Under appropriate regularity conditions, we know that

\displaystyle\operatorname{bias}\big\{\widehat{\mu}(x)\big\}=E\big\{\widehat{\mu}(x)\big\}-\mu(x)=B(x)h^{2}+o(h^{2}),\ \operatorname{var}\big\{\widehat{\mu}(x)\big\}=\frac{V(x)}{Nh}+o\left(\frac{1}{Nh}\right),

where

\displaystyle B(x)=\frac{\kappa_{2}}{2}\left\{\ddot{\mu}(x)+2\dot{\mu}(x)\frac{\dot{f}(x)}{f(x)}\right\},\ V(x)=\frac{\nu_{0}\sigma^{2}}{f(x)},

(2)

f(\cdot)
is the probability density function of
X_{i}
,
\kappa_{r}=\int u^{r}K(u)du
and
\nu_{r}=\int u^{r}K^{2}(u)du
with
r\geq 0
are constants related to
K(\cdot)
; see
Fan and Gijbels, (1996)
and
Li and Racine, (2007)
. Throughout this article, we refer to
\widehat{\mu}(x)
in (
1
) as the
global estimator
to emphasize the fact that it is obtained from the entire dataset.

2.2
Divide-and-Conquer Methods

Despite the popularity of the nonparametric kernel smoothing method in the literature, its development for massive datasets occurred only recently. Meanwhile, the idea of divide-and-conquer has been extremely useful for distributed estimation. It is thus of great interest to explore the possibility of developing divide-and-conquer type methods for nonparametric kernel smoothing. Specifically, let
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\dots,N\}
be the collection of sample indexes. Assume that the whole sample can be divided into
M
nonoverlapping subsets such that
\mathcal{S}=\cup_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{S}_{m}
with
\mathcal{S}_{m_{1}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{m_{2}}=\emptyset
for any
m_{1}\neq m_{2}
, where
\mathcal{S}_{m}
refers to the index set of the sample on the
m
-th local machine. For simplicity, we further assume that all the subsets are equally sized with
|\mathcal{S}_{m}|=n
. We immediately know that
N=Mn
.
Then there are two divide-and-conquer strategies to construct distributed kernel smoothing estimators. Their details are given as follows.

Strategy 1
. The first one is the one-shot type distributed estimator developed in
(
Chang et al.,, 2017
)
. Specifically, consider the local NW estimator on the
m
-th machine, that is,
\widehat{\mu}_{m}(x)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)Y_{i}\Big/\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)
. Then, by averaging all the
M
local NW estimators, the resulting one-shot estimator can be constructed as

\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{OS}}(x)=M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{\mu}_{m}(x)
. Under appropriate regularity conditions, we can show that if data
are randomly assigned to local machine and local sample size
n
is sufficiently large, then the one-shot estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{OS}}(x)
can be as efficient as the global estimator
\widehat{\mu}(x)
. The technical details are given in Appendix A.1.

Strategy 2
. We can see that the global NW estimator in (
1
) can be written as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}(x)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)Y_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)}=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)Y_{i}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)}.

(3)

This immediately suggests a more direct way for distributed computing of the global estimator
\widehat{\mu}(x)
.
Specifically, let local machines compute and transfer to the central machine
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)Y_{i}
and
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}K_{h}(X_{i}-x)
with
1\leq m\leq M
.
Next, the central machine assembles these quantities according to (
3
) to obtain the final estimator.
We should emphasize that this estimator is exactly the global estimator but computed in a distributed way.
It solely depends on the whole dataset and has nothing to do with the local sample sizes or the local sample distributions. Consequently, it should have the best statistical efficiency in terms of mean squared error (MSE). A slightly more elaborated asymptotic analysis of
\widehat{\mu}(x)
can also be found in Appendix A.1.

Under appropriate regularity conditions, we can prove theoretically that both types of the distributed estimators can achieve comparable performance.
However, we find that they face serious challenges for prediction in both computation and communication.
Consider for example
N^{*}
new observations
\{X_{i}^{*}:\ 1\leq i\leq N^{*}\}
with only feature
X_{i}^{*}
s observed.
In order to make a prediction for the unobserved response
Y_{i}^{*}
, we need to first broadcast the corresponding feature
X_{i}^{*}
to every local machine.
Then the
m
-th local machine needs to compute some specific local statistics with respect to
X_{i}^{*}
based on the local sample
\mathcal{S}_{m}
for each
1\leq m\leq M
.
Specifically,
\widehat{\mu}_{m}(X_{i}^{*})
should be computed according to
Strategy 1
, or
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}K_{h}(X_{j}-x)Y_{i}
and
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}K_{h}(X_{j}-X_{i}^{*})
should be computed according to
Strategy 2
.
Subsequently, the central machine collects all those local statistics and then aggregates them to obtain
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{OS}}(X_{i}^{*})
or
\widehat{\mu}(X_{i}^{*})
according to different strategies as the predicted value for
Y_{i}^{*}
.
To predict all
N^{*}
observations, the above distributed computation process must be replicated
N^{*}
times.
This leads to a huge time cost for both computation and communication.
Thus, it is of great interest to develop a more computationally and communicationally efficient prediction method for a distributed system.

2.3
Distributed Grid Point Approximation

To solve this prediction problem, we develop here a novel method called grid point approximation (GPA).
Specifically, let
J
be a prespecified positive integer, which determines the number of grid points.
With a given
J
, we define the
j
-th grid point as
x_{j}^{*}=j/J
with
0\leq j\leq J
.
Obviously, the distance between two adjacent grid points should be
\Delta=1/J
.
We then estimate the global estimator
\widehat{\mu}(x)
according to (
3
) on the grid points
\{x_{j}^{*}:\ 0\leq j\leq J\}
only.
This is a training process having nothing to do with prediction.
Consequently, it can be conducted in the model training stage before prediction occurs.
Once we have obtained the estimates on each grid point, prediction can be implemented using linear interpolation.
Specifically, suppose
x\in[x_{j}^{*},x_{j+1}^{*}]
for some
j
, we can predict
\mu(x)
by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{x_{j+1}^{*}-x}{\Delta}\widehat{\mu}(x_{j}^{*})+\frac{x-x_{j}^{*}}{\Delta}\widehat{\mu}(x_{j+1}^{*}).

(4)

We refer to
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)
as the GPA estimator.
It is noteworthy that the GPA estimator can be computed on the central machine without incurring any communication cost.
In addition, only simple interpolations are needed for making predictions.
Consequently, the computational cost is also minimal.
Intuitively, the number of grid points
J
should not be too large; otherwise, the computation and communication cost required for the grid point estimation could be large.
On the other hand,
J
should not be too small. Otherwise, the bias due to linear interpolation could be substantial.
Therefore, understanding how the number of grid points
J
, together with the bandwidth
h
, affects the statistical efficiency of the GPA estimator becomes a key issue.

To study the statistical properties of the GPA estimator, we need to assume the following technical conditions.

1.

(
Smoothness Condition
) The probability density function
f(\cdot)
is sufficiently smooth so that its second-order derivative
\ddot{f}(x)=d^{2}f(x)/dx^{2}
is continuous.
The mean function
\mu(\cdot)
is sufficiently smooth so that its second-order derivative
\ddot{\mu}(x)=d^{2}\mu(x)/dx^{2}
is continuous.

2.

(
Kernel Function
) The kernel function
K(\cdot)
is a continuous probability density function and symmetric about
0
with compact support
[-1,1]
.

3.

(
Optimal Bandwidth
) Assume
h=CN^{-1/5}
for some positive constant
C
.

The smoothness conditions in
1
are standard and have been used in previous studies; for example, see
Fan, (1992)
,
Fan et al., (1996)
, and
Li and Racine, (2007)
.
A kernel function that satisfies condition
2
is often referred to as a second-order kernel
(
Fan and Hu,, 1992
;
Ullah and Pagan,, 1999
;
Wand and Jones,, 1994
)
.
This type of kernel functions are usually used to construct kernel estimators for twice continuously differentiable functions.
Last, condition
3
directly assumes the bandwidth should be of the optimal convergence rate
(
Ullah and Pagan,, 1999
)
.
By this bandwidth, the optimal convergence rate achieved by a global NW estimator in terms of the asymptotic mean squared error is given by
O(1/(Nh))=O(N^{-1/5})
under the conditions
1
and
2
.
We then have the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Assume conditions
1
–
3
hold and
f(x)>0
. Further assume that
Jh\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
. Then we have
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)-\mu(x)=\widetilde{Q}_{0}+\widetilde{Q}_{1}+\widetilde{Q}_{2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
,
where
\widetilde{Q}_{0}
is a non-stochastic term satisfying
\widetilde{Q}_{0}=O(1/J^{2})
,
\sqrt{Nh}\left\{\widetilde{Q}_{1}-B(x)h^{2}\right\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V(x)\Big)
,
E\widetilde{Q}_{2}=O\left(h/N+h^{4}\right)
,
\operatorname{var}(\widetilde{Q}_{2})=O\left\{1/(Nh)^{2}\right\}
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}=O_{p}\left\{1/(Nh)^{3/2}\right\}
.
In addition to that, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{Nh}\Big\{\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)-\mu(x)-B(x)h^{2}\Big\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V(x)\Big).

The proof of Theorem
1
can be found in Appendix A.2. Theorem
1
implies that as long as
1/J=o(h)
, the GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)
should have the same asymptotic distribution as the global estimator
\widehat{\mu}(x)
. Recall that
h=CN^{-1/5}
for some constant
C>0
.
This means that the number of grid points should satisfy
J/N^{1/5}\to\infty
.
This seems to be an extremely mild condition.
To clarify the idea, consider, for example,
N=10^{10}
(i.e., 10 billion). Then, as long as
J\gg 10^{2}=100
, the GPA estimator could perform as well as the global estimator.
Further discussion on the case when the covariate
X_{i}
does not have compact support can be found in Appendix C.

2.4
Bandwidth Selection

Next, we consider the problem of bandwidth selection.
To achieve the best asymptotic efficiency, the optimal bandwidth should be used.
By condition (C3), we know that
h=CN^{-1/5}
with some constant
C>0
.
Then, how to optimally choose the positive constant
C
becomes the problem of interest.
For this problem, cross-validation (CV) has been frequently used.
Specifically, let
\widehat{\mu}^{(-i)}(x)
be the global estimator obtained without the
i
-th sample. We use this estimator to conduct an out-sample prediction for the
i
-th sample as
\widehat{\mu}^{(-i)}(X_{i})
.
Its prediction error is then given by
\big\{Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}^{(-i)}(X_{i})\big\}^{2}
. Averaging these values gives the CV score as

\displaystyle\mbox{CV}(h)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big\{Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu}^{(-i)}(X_{i})\Big\}^{2}w(X_{i}),

(5)

where
w(\cdot)\geq 0
is a prespecified weight function that trims out-of-boundary observations
(
Racine and Li,, 2004
)
.
Then, an empirically optimal bandwidth is obtained as
\widehat{h}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{h}\mbox{CV}(h)
.
Note that
\mbox{CV}(h)
can be viewed as an empirical alternative to the theoretical asymptotic mean integrated squared error (AMISE) as

\displaystyle\mbox{AMISE}(h)=\int\bigg\{B^{2}(x)h^{4}+\frac{V(x)}{Nh}\bigg\}w(x)f(x)dx=\overline{B}h^{4}+\frac{\overline{V}}{Nh},

where
\overline{B}=\int B^{2}(x)w(x)f(x)dx
,
\overline{V}=\int V(x)w(x)f(x)dx
.
Then, the asymptotically optimal bandwidth can be defined as

\displaystyle h_{\textup{opt}}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{h}\mbox{AMISE}(h)=C_{\textup{opt}}N^{-1/5},

(6)

where
C_{\textup{opt}}=\big\{\overline{V}/(4\overline{B})\big\}^{1/5}
.
As demonstrated by
Racine and Li, (2004)
, we have
\mbox{CV}(h)=\mbox{AMISE}(h)\{1+o_{p}(1)\}+C_{1}
under appropriate regularity conditions, where
C_{1}
is a constant independent of
h
. To see this, we first write
\mbox{CV}(h)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{\mu(X_{i})-\widehat{\mu}^{(-i)}(X_{i})\}^{2}w(X_{i})/N+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{\mu(X_{i})-\widehat{\mu}^{(-i)}(X_{i})\}\varepsilon_{i}w(X_{i})/N+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}w(X_{i})/N=\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{2}+\Omega_{3},
where
\Omega_{1}
is an empirical approximation of
\mbox{AMISE}(h)
,
\Omega_{2}
is a negligible term compared with
\Omega_{1}
, and
\Omega_{3}
is a term independent of the bandwidth
h
and thus can be approximately treated as a constant. Consequently, we should have
\mbox{CV}(h)=\mbox{AMISE}(h)\{1+o_{p}(1)\}+C_{1}
, where
C_{1}
is some constant independent of
h
.
Consequently, we should reasonably expect
\widehat{h}
to be a consistent estimator of
h_{\textup{opt}}
.
In fact, by Theorem 2.2 in
Racine and Li, (2004)
, we know that
\widehat{h}/h_{\textup{opt}}\to_{p}1
as
N\to\infty
under appropriate conditions.

Unfortunately, such a straightforward method cannot be directly implemented for massive data analysis because the associated computation cost is too high.
Therefore, we propose two bandwidth selectors which can be computed in a distributed manner.
The first one is the one-shot type bandwidth selector, which is inspired by the idea of one-shot estimation.
To be more specific, let
\widehat{h}_{m}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{h}\mbox{CV}_{m}(h)
be the optimal bandwidth selected on the
m
-th local machine by the typically used CV method. Next, these selected bandwidths are passed to the central machine and averaged to yield a more stable value
\overline{h}=M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{h}_{m}
.
Unfortunately, this bandwidth is not a good estimator for
h_{\textup{opt}}=C_{\textup{opt}}N^{-1/5}
.
This is because
\widehat{h}_{m}
is computed on the local machine with
n=N/M
samples only.
Hence, the local bandwidth selector
\widehat{h}_{m}
should be an estimator for
C_{\textup{opt}}n^{-1/5}
, other than
C_{\textup{opt}}N^{-1/5}
.
Consequently, the average of them
\overline{h}
is not a good estimator for
C_{\textup{opt}}N^{-1/5}
either.
The problem can be easily fixed by rescaling.
This leads to the final one-shot bandwidth selector as
\widehat{h}_{\textup{OS}}=(N/n)^{-1/5}\overline{h}=M^{-1/5}\overline{h}
.

However, similar to the one-shot estimator, the one-shot bandwidth selector
\widehat{h}_{\textup{OS}}
also requires that the entire sample is randomly distributed across different local machines. Otherwise, the distribution of the subsamples on the local machine can be substantially different from that of the whole sample. In this case, the “optimal bandwidth” computed on a local machine could be a seriously biased estimator for the globally optimal bandwidth as defined in (
6
) even after appropriate re-scaling. To address this issue, we propose a pilot sample-based bandwidth selector, which is particularly useful for nonrandomly distributed samples
(
Pan et al.,, 2022
)
.
Specifically, let
n_{0}
be the prespecified pilot sample size.
Then, an index set
\mathcal{P}_{m}
is sampled from
\mathcal{S}_{m}
by simple random sampling without replacement such that
|\mathcal{P}_{m}|=n_{0}/M
for each
1\leq m\leq M
. Recall that
\mathcal{S}_{m}
is the index set of the sample on the
m
-th local machine.
We refer to
\mathcal{P}=\cup_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}_{m}
as the pilot sample.
Next, these observations in
\mathcal{P}
are transferred from the local machines to the driver machine, where the final results are assembled.
Let
\widehat{h}_{0}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{h}\mbox{CV}_{\mathcal{P}}(h)
be the optimal bandwidth selected by the CV method based on the pilot sample
\mathcal{P}
.
Similarly, we rescale it to obtain the pilot sample-based bandwidth selector as
\widehat{h}_{\textup{PLT}}=(N/n_{0})^{-1/5}\widehat{h}_{0}
.
The theoretical properties of the two bandwidth selectors are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Assume conditions
1
–
2
and (C4)-(C5) in Appendix A.3 hold. Then, we have
\widehat{h}_{\textup{OS}}/h_{\textup{opt}}\to_{p}1
as
n\to\infty
and
\widehat{h}_{\textup{PLT}}/h_{\textup{opt}}\to_{p}1
as
n_{0}\to\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Appendix A.3. By Theorem
2
, we know that the two bandwidth selectors should be ratio consistent for the optimal bandwidth
\widehat{h}_{\textup{opt}}
, as long as the local sample size
n
or the pilot sample size
n_{0}
diverges to infinity as
N\to\infty
.
Our exhaustive simulation experiments further confirm their excellent finite sample performance.

3
Some Extensions

In this section, we consider two extensions of the GPA method. They are, respectively, high order polynomial interpolation based GPA and multivariate GPA.

3.1
High Order Polynomial Interpolation

One way to further improve the proposed GPA method is to replace the linear interpolation method with higher order polynomial interpolation.
Specifically, recall that
\widehat{\mu}(x_{j}^{*})
is the NW estimator on the
j
-th grid point.
For an arbitrary
x\in(0,1)
, we first find its
\nu+1
nearest grid points.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the
\nu+1
grid points are
x_{j}^{*},\dots,x_{j+\nu}^{*}
for some
j
.
We then estimate
\mu(x)
by the
\nu
-th order polynomial interpolation as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{PGPA},\nu}(x)=\sum_{k=j}^{j+\nu}q_{k}(x)\widehat{\mu}(x_{j}^{*}),

where
q_{k}(x)=\prod_{i=j,i\neq k}^{j+\nu}(x-x_{i}^{*})/(x_{k}^{*}-x_{i}^{*})
for
j\leq k\leq j+\nu
are the corresponding Lagrange interpolation coefficients
(
Süli and Mayers,, 2003
)
.
To investigate the theoretical properties of
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{PGPA},\nu}(x)
, we assume the following technical conditions.

(A1)

(
Smoothness Condition
) The probability density function
f(\cdot)
is sufficiently smooth so that its
(\nu+1)
-th order derivative
f^{(\nu+1)}(\cdot)
is continuous. The mean function
\mu(\cdot)
is sufficiently smooth so that its
(\nu+1)
-th order derivative
\mu^{(\nu+1)}(\cdot)
is continuous.

(A2)

(
Higher Order Kernel
) Assume that
K(\cdot)
is a
(\nu+1)
-th order continuous kernel function with compact support
[-1,1]
. That is
K(\cdot)
satisfies (i)
\kappa_{0}=\int K(u)du=1
, (ii)
\kappa_{r}=\int u^{r}K(u)du=0
for
1\leq r\leq\nu
, and (iii)
\int|u^{\nu+1}K(u)|du<\infty
.

(A3)

(
Optimal Bandwidth
) Assume
h=CN^{-1/(2\nu+3)}
for some positive constant
C
.

The smoothness conditions in
(A1)
are needed for
\nu
-th order polynomial interpolation. Note that condition
(A1)
is the same as condition
1
if
\nu=1
. Condition
(A2)
introduces the higher order kernel function, which is useful to further reduce the bias of the kernel estimator
(
Fan and Hu,, 1992
;
Ullah and Pagan,, 1999
)
. The construction of higher order kernel functions can be found in
Fan and Hu, (1992)
and
Hansen, (2005)
.
Last, condition
(A3)
assumes that the bandwidth should be of the compatibly optimal rate when a higher order kernel is used
(
Ullah and Pagan,, 1999
)
.
By this bandwidth, the optimal convergence rate achieved by a global NW estimator in terms of the asymptotic mean squared error is given by
O(1/(Nh))=O(N^{-2(\nu+1)/(2\nu+3)})
under the conditions
(A1)
and
(A2)
.
The theoretical properties of the
\nu
-th order polynomial interpolation based GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{PGPA},\nu}(x)
are then summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Assume conditions
(A1)
–
(A3)
hold and
f(x)>0
. Further, assume that
Jh\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
.
Then, we have
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{PGPA},\nu}(x)-\mu(x)=\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,0}+\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,1}+\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{\nu}
,
where
\widetilde{Q}_{0}
is a non-stochastic term satisfying
\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,0}=O(1/J^{\nu+1})
,
\sqrt{Nh}\Big\{\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,1}-B_{\nu}(x)h^{\nu+1}\Big\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V(x)\Big)
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{\nu}=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh})
.
Here,
B_{\nu}(\cdot)
is defined in
(S.11)
in Appendix A.4,
and
V(\cdot)
is the same as that in (
2
).
In addition to that, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{Nh}\Big\{\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{PGPA},\nu}(x)-\mu(x)-B_{\nu}(x)h^{\nu+1}\Big\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V(x)\Big).

The proof of Theorem
3
is given in Appendix A.4. By Theorem
3
, we find that, as long as
Jh\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
, the
\nu
-th order polynomial interpolation based GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{PGPA},\nu}(x)
can achieve the same statistical efficiency as the global estimator. Recall that
h=CN^{-1/(2\nu+3)}
for some constant
C>0
. This implies that the number of grid points should satisfy
J\gg N^{1/(2\nu+3)}
. This is in line with the results in Theorem
1
for linear interpolation based GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)
with
\nu=1
.
Furthermore, we can see that fewer number of grid points are required as long as
\nu>1
. This suggests that higher order polynomial interpolation leads to further reduced communication cost without any sacrifice of asymptotic statistical efficiency.
However, this benefit comes at the cost of imposing more stringent smoothness conditions on the mean function
\mu(\cdot)
and density function
f(\cdot)
.
In practice, we often do not know the smoothness of the unknown functions
\mu(\cdot)
and
f(\cdot)
. To select an appropriate interpolation order
\nu
, we can randomly select a validation set
\mathcal{V}
from the whole dataset.
Then the performance for different interpolation orders
1\leq\nu\leq\nu_{\max}
can be evaluated on
\mathcal{V}
, where
\nu_{\max}\geq 1
is a prespecified integer.
Specifically, for each candidate order
\nu
, we first choose a
(\nu+1)
-th order kernel function that satisfies condition
(A2)
. Subsequently, the
\nu
-th order polynomial interpolation based GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{PGPA},\nu}
can be used to make prediction for the validation set
\mathcal{V}
. Finally, we can choose the order
\nu
minimizing the prediction error on
\mathcal{V}
.

3.2
Multivariate GPA

We now consider how to extend the GPA estimator from the situation with a univariate predictor (i.e.
p=1
) to a more general situation with a multivariate predictor (i.e.,
p>1
).
We assume that
X_{i}=(X_{i1},\dots,X_{ip})^{\top}
is a random vector compactly supported on
[0,1]^{p}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Then for any fix point
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})^{\top}\in[0,1]^{p}
, the multivariate NW estimator can be defined as
(
Li and Racine,, 2007
)
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{K}_{h}(X_{i}-\boldsymbol{x})Y_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{K}_{h}(X_{i}-\boldsymbol{x})}=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\boldsymbol{K}_{h}(X_{i}-\boldsymbol{x})Y_{i}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\boldsymbol{K}_{h}(X_{i}-\boldsymbol{x})},

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{K}_{h}(X_{i}-\boldsymbol{x})=h^{-p}\prod_{s=1}^{p}K\big((X_{is}-x_{s})/h\big)
, and
K(\cdot)
is a univariate kernel function satisfying condition
2
.
Similarly, let
J
be a prespecified positive integer.
We then design the grid points set as
\mathcal{G}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{*}=(j_{1}/J,\dots,j_{p}/J)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:0\leq j_{1},\dots,j_{p}\leq J\}
. This leads to a total of
|\mathcal{G}|=(J+1)^{p}
grid points.
We can then compute the global estimator
\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{*})
according to (
7
) for each grid point
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{*}\in\mathcal{G}
in a distributed way.
For any
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})^{\top}\in(0,1)^{p}
, we can find its
p+1
nearest linearly independent grid points
\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{1}}^{*},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{p+1}}^{*}\in\mathcal{G}
.
Let
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{x})=\big\{\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}w_{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*}:\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}w_{k}=1\textup{ and }w_{k}\geq 0\textup{ for each }k\big\}
be the
p
-simplex with these
p+1
grid points as vertices. We can verify that
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
Subsequently, we can predict
\mu(\boldsymbol{x})
by multivariate linear interpolation as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{MGPA},p}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}q_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\widehat{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*}),

where
q_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}),\,1\leq k\leq p+1
are interpolation coefficients
(
Waldron,, 1998
)
.
To investigate the theoretical properties of multivariate GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{MGPA},p}(\boldsymbol{x})
, we assume the following technical conditions.

(A4)

(
Smoothness Condition
) The probability density function
f(\cdot)
is sufficiently smooth so that its second-order derivative
\ddot{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=\partial^{2}f(\boldsymbol{x})/(\partial\boldsymbol{x}\partial\boldsymbol{x}^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is continuous.

The mean function
\mu(\cdot)
is sufficiently smooth so that its its second-order derivative
\ddot{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})=\partial^{2}\mu(\boldsymbol{x})/(\partial\boldsymbol{x}\partial\boldsymbol{x}^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is continuous.

(A5)

(
Optimal Bandwidth
) Assume
h=CN^{-1/(p+4)}
for some positive constant
C
.

The condition
(A4)
is a multivariate version of the univariate smoothness condition
1
. This condition is needed for multivariate linear interpolation.
Condition
(A5)
assumes that the bandwidth should be of the compatible optimal rate for the multivariate NW estimator
(
Härdle et al.,, 2004
;
Li and Racine,, 2007
)
.
By this bandwidth, the optimal convergence rate achieved by a global multivariate NW estimator in terms of the asymptotic mean squared error is given by
O(1/(Nh^{p}))=O(N^{-4/(p+4)})
under conditions
2
and
(A4)
.
The theoretical properties of the multivariate GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{MGPA},p}(\boldsymbol{x})
are then summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Assume conditions
2
,
(A4)
and
(A5)
hold and
f(\boldsymbol{x})>0
.
Further, assume that
Jh\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
.
Then we have
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{MGPA},p}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu(\boldsymbol{x})=\widetilde{Q}_{p,0}+\widetilde{Q}_{p,1}+\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{p}
, where
\widetilde{Q}_{p,0}
is a non-stochastic term satisfying
\widetilde{Q}_{p,0}=O(1/J^{2})
,
\sqrt{Nh^{p}}\big\{\widetilde{Q}_{p,1}-B_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})h^{2}\big\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big)
,
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{p}=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh^{p}})
.
Here,
B_{p}(\cdot)
and
V_{p}(\cdot)
are defined in
(S.13)
in Appendix A.5.
In addition to that, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{Nh^{p}}\Big\{\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{MGPA},p}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu(\boldsymbol{x})-B_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})h^{2}\Big\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big).

The proof of Theorem
4
is given in Appendix A.5. By Theorem
4
, we know that the global statistical efficiency can be achieved if
Jh\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
. Note that
h=CN^{-1/(p+4)}
for some constant
C>0
. This implies that the number of grid points should satisfy
|\mathcal{G}|=(J+1)^{4}\gg N^{p/(p+4)}
. This is also in line with the results in Theorem
1
for univariate linear interpolation based GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)
with
p=1
.

4
Numerical Studies

In this section, we conduct a number of numerical studies to demonstrate the performance of our proposed GPA methods.
We first investigate the performance of the different distributed kernel estimators in terms of the prediction accuracy and the time cost.
Next, we verify the finite performance of the two bandwidth selectors.
Finally, we compare our GPA method with other competing methods (i.e., the basis expansion method and the RKHS based method).
Specifically, we generate
X_{i}
according to a probability density function
f(x)
defined on
[0,1]
. Two different distributions are considered. They are, respectively, the uniform distribution
\text{Unif}(0,1)
and the Beta distribution
\text{Beta}(2,3)
.
Once
X_{i}
is obtained,
Y_{i}
is generated according to
Y_{i}=\mu(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
with
\varepsilon_{i}
simulated from the standard normal distribution.
Here, two different mean functions are considered. They are, respectively,

\mu_{1}(x)=4(x-0.5)+2\exp\{-128(x-0.5)^{2}\}
, and

\mu_{2}(x)=\sin\{8(x-0.5)\}+2\exp\{-128(x-0.5)^{2}\}
.
The two mean functions are revised from
Fan and Gijbels, (1996)
.
Additional numerical experiments, in comparison to other methods, can be found in Appendix E of the supplementary materials.

4.1
Different Distributed Kernel Estimators

Once the full training sample
\{(X_{i},Y_{i}):\ 1\leq i\leq N\}
is simulated, we consider two different local sample allocation strategies as follows.

Strategy 1. (Random Partition)
We randomly and evenly divide the whole sample
\mathcal{S}
into
M
disjoint subsets
\mathcal{S}_{m}\ (1\leq m\leq M)
such that the data distribution on different machines are the same. This is an ideal scenario for one-shot type estimators.

Strategy 2. (Nonrandom Partition)
We assign the samples to different machines according to the covariate value. Specifically, let
X_{(1)}\leq X_{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq X_{(N)}
be the order statistic of
X_{i}
s. Thus,
(i)
represents the index of the
i
-th smallest sample point for each
1\leq i\leq N
. We then assign
\mathcal{S}_{m}=\{(i):(m-1)n+1\leq i\leq mn\}
to the
m
-th local machine, where
n=N/M
is the local sample size. In this case, the data distributions on different machines are very different.

Subsequently, different estimators are computed, including the one-shot estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{OS}}
, the GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}
, and the global estimator
\widehat{\mu}
with a distributed implementation described in Section
2.2
.
For all the estimators, the Epanechnikov kernel
K(u)=(3/4)(1-u^{2})I(|u|\leq 1)
is adopted, where
I(\cdot)
is the indicator function. The asymptotically optimal bandwidth
h_{\textup{opt}}
defined in (
6
) is used for all estimators.
For a comprehensive evaluation, three sample sizes (
N=1\times 10^{4},2\times 10^{4},5\times 10^{4}
) are studied.
For the GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}
, a total of
J+1=[h_{\textup{opt}}^{-1}\log\log N]+1
grid points are equally spaced on the interval
[0,1]
, where
[r]
denotes the integer part of
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
To assess the prediction performance of each estimator, we independently generate a testing sample set
\big\{\big(X_{i}^{*},\mu(X_{i}^{*})\big):1\leq i\leq N^{*}\big\}
according to the same probability density function
f(x)
and mean function
\mu(x)
as the training sample. The testing sample size is set to be
N^{*}=N/2
and the number of machine is
M=50
.
Let
\widehat{\mu}^{(b)}
be one particular estimator (e.g., the global estimator) obtained in the
b
-th simulation iteration with
1\leq b\leq B=100
. We then compute the root mean squared error (RMSE) as
\text{RMSE}(\widehat{\mu}^{(b)})=\sqrt{(N^{*})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N^{*}}\big\{\widehat{\mu}^{(b)}(X_{i}^{*})-\mu(X_{i}^{*})\big\}^{2}}
.
In addition, both the computation and communication times are recorded.
The averaged RMSE values, computation times (
t_{\textup{cp}}
, in seconds), and communication times (
t_{\textup{cm}}
, in seconds) over
B=100
replications are presented in Tables
1
and

for the two different local sample allocation strategies, respectively.

5.1
Airline Dataset

We first apply our method to the US airline dataset (
http://stat-computing.org/dataexpo/2009
).
It consists of flight arrival and departure details for all commercial flights within the US from October 1987 to April 2008. The dataset contains over 123 million records, occupying 12 GB of space. To demonstrate the usefulness of our proposed methodology, we choose the arrival delay (
ArrDelay
, recorded in minutes) of the flight as the response. However, the response
ArrDelay
has a very heavy-tailed distribution and contains negative values (early arrival). Therefore, we transform the response via a modified logarithmic transformation,
\mbox{sign}(y)\cdot\log(1+|y|)
, preserving both sign and scale information. We next choose the scheduled departure time (
CRSDepTime
) as the predictor.
After data cleaning, a total of
120,947,440
observations are retained.
Among them,
N=120,790,000
observations are randomly selected as the training sample set,
n_{\textup{val}}=10,000
observations are randomly selected as the validation set
\mathcal{V}
, and the remaining
N^{*}=147,440
observations form the testing sample set.
A total of
M=50
machines are used.
We first select an appropriate interpolation order
\nu\ (1\leq\nu\leq\nu_{\max}=4)
using the procedures described at the end of Section
3.1
.
Specifically, for each order
\nu
, we compute the root mean prediction error (RMPE) of the
\nu
-th order polynomial interpolation based GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA},\nu}
on the validation set
\mathcal{V}
as
\textup{RMPE}(\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA},\nu})=\sqrt{(n_{\textup{val}})^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\big\{\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA},\nu}(X_{i})-Y_{i}\big\}^{2}}
. Here, the pilot sample-based bandwidth selector
\widehat{h}_{\textup{PLT}}
is used with a pilot sample of size
n_{0}=10,000
, and
J
is set to be
J=[24\widehat{h}_{\textup{PLT}}^{-1}\log\log N]
accordingly.
We find that the linear interpolation based GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA},1}
(i.e.,
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}
) shows the smallest RMPE value. The corresponding bandwidth is
\widehat{h}_{\textup{PLT}}=0.549
, and a total of
J+1=164
grid points are equally spaced within the interval
[0,24]
.
We then use
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}
to predict the testing sample set. The training and prediction of the GPA estimator take approximately 25 seconds.
For comparison, we also use the one-shot estimator and the global estimator with distributed implementation to make prediction for the testing sample.
Both methods show RMPE values close to the GPA estimator, but each takes more than 3.5 hours to complete the prediction.
Additionally, implementing
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA},\nu}
with different
\nu\ (1\leq\nu\leq 4)
on the testing sample confirms that the linear interpolation based GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA},1}

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we study the problem of nonparametric smoothing on a distributed system.
We first investigate the popularly used one-shot type estimator and the distributed implementation of the global estimator.
However, we find that the two estimators are highly inefficient for prediction in terms of both computation and communication costs.
This drives us to develop a novel GPA estimator, which requires no further communication when making predictions.
Subsequently, the statistical properties of the GPA estimator are carefully investigated.
We find that the GPA estimator has the same statistical efficiency as the global estimator under very mild conditions.
In addition, two novel bandwidth selectors are developed for the distributed estimators, and their theoretical properties are then investigated.
To further extend the GPA method, we also explore the application of a higher-order polynomial interpolated GPA estimator and a multivariate GPA estimator.
Finally, these theoretical results are carefully validated through extensive numerical studies and real data analysis.

To conclude the article, we provide several directions for future studies.
First, we investigate the nonparametric estimation problem on a centralized distributed system. It is worthwhile to explore whether the GPA method can be extended to a decentralized distributed system.
Second, our numerical studies show that the one-shot bandwidth selector exhibits excellent performance.
It is interesting to compare it with the global bandwidth selector in terms of the finite sample performance.
Last, many semiparametric models are popularly used in modern statistical inference, including the single index model, partially linear model, varying coefficient model, and many others.
It would be very interesting to apply our GPA method to these models.

By (
S.37
)–(
S.40
) in Lemma

, we know that
\widetilde{f}=f+o_{p}(1)
,
\widetilde{g}=g+o_{p}(1)

\widehat{f}-f=O_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh})
, and
\widehat{g}-g=O_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh})
. Then we should have
\mathcal{Q}_{\nu}={\widetilde{g}}/{\widetilde{f}^{3}}(\widehat{f}-f)^{2}-({1}/{\widetilde{f}^{2}})(\widehat{f}-f)(\widehat{g}-g)=O\{1/(Nh)\}=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh})
.

From the above results, we can conclude that, for any fixed
x\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{Nh}\Big\{\widehat{\mu}(x)-\mu(x)-B_{\nu}(x)h^{\nu+1}\Big\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V(x)\Big),

where
B_{\nu}(x)
is defined in (
S.11
), and
V(x)
is defined in (
2
).
This ends the proof of Step 1.

Step 2.

From the proof of Step 1, we can obtain that

\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{PGPA},\nu}(x)-\mu(x)=\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,0}+\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,1}+\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{\nu}
, where
\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,0}=\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,0}(x)=\sum_{k=j}^{j+\nu}q_{k}(x)\mu(x_{k}^{*})-\mu(x)
,
\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,1}=\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,1}(x)=\sum_{k=j}^{j+\nu}q_{k}(x)Q_{\nu,1}(x_{k}^{*})
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{\nu}=\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{\nu}(x)=\sum_{k=j}^{j+\nu}q_{k}(x)\mathcal{Q}_{\nu}(x_{k}^{*})
.
Note that
q_{k}(x)=\prod_{i=j,i\neq k}^{j+\nu}(x-x_{i}^{*})/(x_{k}^{*}-x_{i}^{*})
for
j\leq k\leq j+\nu
are bounded for any
x\in[0,1]
.
In Step 1.2, we have shown that
\mathcal{Q}_{\nu}=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh})
.
Then we can conclude that
\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,2}=\sum_{k=j}^{j+\nu}q_{k}(x)\mathcal{Q}_{\nu}(x_{k}^{*})=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh})
as well.
We next derive the results about
\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,0}
and
\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,1}
in the following two steps.

Step 2.1.

Since
\mu(\cdot)
is assumed to be
(\nu+1)
-times continuously differentiable, then by error formula of polynomial interpolation
(

, Theorem 6.2)
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,0}=\sum_{k=j}^{j+\nu}q_{k}(x)\mu(x_{k}^{*})-\mu(x)=-\frac{1}{(\nu+1)!}\prod_{k=j}^{j+\nu}(x-x_{k}^{*})\mu^{(\nu+1)}(\widetilde{x}),

where
\widetilde{x}\in[x_{j}^{*},x_{j+\nu}^{*}]

Step 2.2.

We next establish the asymptotic normality of
\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,1}
.
First, we consider the polynomial function
\mathcal{L}(x)=\sum_{k=j-1}^{j+1}q_{k}(x)-1
.
Note that
\mathcal{L}(x)
is a polynomial whose degree is at most

It is easy to verify that

for
j\leq k\leq j+\nu
, that is
\mathcal{L}(x)
has
\nu+1
zeros.
Then we must have
\mathcal{L}(x)\equiv 0
, or equivalently,
\sum_{k=j}^{j+\nu}q_{k}(x)\equiv 1
.
By (
S.37
) and (
S.39
) in Lemma
S.6
, we can show that
EQ_{\nu,1}(x)=B_{\nu}(x)h^{\nu+1}+o(h^{\nu+1})
, and
EQ_{\nu,1}(x_{k}^{*})=B_{\nu}(x_{k}^{*})h^{\nu+1}+o(h^{\nu+1})
for each
j\leq k\leq j+\nu
. Furthermore, by (
S.38
) and (
S.40
) in Lemma
S.6
, we can show that
\operatorname{var}\{Q_{\nu,1}(x)\}=V(x)/(Nh)+o(1/(Nh))
, and
\operatorname{var}\{Q_{\nu,1}(x_{k}^{*})\}=V(x_{k}^{*})/(Nh)+o\{1/(Nh)\}
for each
j\leq k\leq j+\nu
.
Since
B_{\nu}(\cdot)
and

are continuous under the assumed conditions, and
\max_{j\leq k\leq j+\nu}|x_{k}^{*}-x|\leq\nu/J\to 0

EQ_{\nu,1}(x_{k}^{*})-EQ_{\nu,1}(x)=o(h^{\nu+1})
and
\operatorname{var}\{Q_{\nu,1}(x_{k}^{*})\}=\operatorname{var}\{Q_{\nu,1}(x)\}\{1+o(1)\}

h=CN^{-1/(2\nu+3)}
for some constant

\{EQ_{\nu,1}(x_{k}^{*})-EQ_{\nu,1}(x)\}/\sqrt{\operatorname{var}\{Q_{\nu,1}(x)\}}=o(\sqrt{Nh^{2\nu+3}})=o(1)

Similar to the proof of Lemma
S.3

\operatorname{cov}\{Q_{\nu,1}(x_{k}^{*}),Q_{\nu,1}(x)\}=\operatorname{var}\{Q_{\nu,1}(x)\}\{1+o(1)\}

j\leq k\leq j+\nu
. Together with above results, and using Lemma
S.5
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,1}(x)-Q_{\nu,1}(x)=\sum_{k=j}^{j+\nu}q_{k}(x)Q_{\nu,1}(x_{k}^{*})-Q_{\nu,1}(x)=o_{p}\Big(\sqrt{\operatorname{var}\{Q_{\nu,1}(x)\}}\Big)=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh}).

This implies
\sqrt{Nh}\big\{\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,1}(x)-Q_{\nu,1}(x)\big\}\to_{p}0

Consequently, by Slutsky’s theorem and (

\displaystyle\sqrt{Nh}\Big\{\widetilde{Q}_{\nu,1}(x)-B_{\nu}(x)h^{\nu+1}\Big\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V(x)\Big),

Furthermore, by Slutsky’s theorem and the results in Step 2, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\sqrt{Nh}\Big\{\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{PGPA},\nu}(x)-\mu(x)-B_{\nu}(x)h^{\nu+1}\Big\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V(x)\Big).

\widehat{f}
and
f
, and
\widetilde{g}
is between
\widehat{g}
and
g

(S.12)

\displaystyle B_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\kappa_{2}}{2}\left\{\operatorname{tr}\{\ddot{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})\}+2\frac{\dot{\mu}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\dot{f}(\boldsymbol{x})}{f(\boldsymbol{x})}\right\},\ V_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\nu_{0}^{p}\sigma^{2}}{f(\boldsymbol{x})}.

(S.13)

\nu_{0}=\int K^{2}(u)du

S.42

S.45

\mathcal{Q}_{p}={\widetilde{g}}/{\widetilde{f}^{3}}(\widehat{f}-f)^{2}-({1}/{\widetilde{f}^{2}})(\widehat{f}-f)(\widehat{g}-g)=O\{1/(Nh^{p})\}=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh^{p}})

Step 2.

From the proof of Step 1, we can obtain that

\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{MGPA},p}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu(\boldsymbol{x})=\widetilde{Q}_{p,0}+\widetilde{Q}_{p,1}+\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{p}

.
In Step 1, we have shown that
\mathcal{Q}_{p}=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh^{p}})

\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{p}=\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}q_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathcal{Q}_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*})=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh^{p}})

Step 2.1.

Recall that
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{x})=\{\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}w_{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*}:\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}w_{k}=1\textup{ and }w_{k}\geq 0\textup{ for each }k\}

We then conclude that
\widetilde{Q}_{p,0}(\boldsymbol{x})=O(1/J^{2})
.

Step 2.2.
We next establish the asymptotic normality of
\widetilde{Q}_{p,1}
. Note that
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}q_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
is a linear function interpolates

for each

\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*},1):\ 1\leq k\leq p+1\}

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}q_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{L}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv 1

By (
S.42

S.44
) in Lemma
S.7
, we can show that
EQ_{\nu,1}(\boldsymbol{x})=B_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})h^{2}+o(h^{2})
, and
EQ_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*})=B_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*})h^{2}+o(h^{2})
for each
1\leq k\leq p+1
. Furthermore, by (
S.43
) and (
S.45
) in Lemma
S.7
, we can show that
\operatorname{var}\{Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})\}=V_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})/(Nh^{p})+o(1/(Nh^{p}))

\operatorname{var}\{Q_{\nu,1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*})\}=V_{p}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*})/(Nh^{p})+o\{1/(Nh^{p})\}
for each
1\leq k\leq p+1
.
Recall that
B_{p}(\cdot)
and
V_{p}(\cdot)
defined in (
S.13

Since
\max_{1\leq k\leq p+1}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*}-\boldsymbol{x}\|\leq\sqrt{p}/J\to 0

EQ_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*})-EQ_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})=o(h^{2})
and
\operatorname{var}\{Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*})\}=\operatorname{var}\{Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})\}\{1+o(1)\}
. Recall that
h=CN^{-1/(p+4)}
for some constant

\{EQ_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*})-EQ_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})\}/\sqrt{\operatorname{var}\{Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})}\}=o(\sqrt{Nh^{p+4}})=o(1)

\operatorname{cov}\{Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*}),Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})\}=\operatorname{var}\{Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})\}\{1+o(1)\}

S.5

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})-Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{p+1}q_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j_{k}}^{*})-Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})=o_{p}\Big(\sqrt{\operatorname{var}\{Q_{p,1}(\boldsymbol{x})\}}\Big)=o_{p}(1/\sqrt{Nh^{p}}).

\displaystyle\sqrt{Nh^{p}}\Big\{\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{MGPA},p}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu(\boldsymbol{x})-B_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})h^{2}\Big\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V_{p}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big).

\displaystyle P\Big(|\widehat{f}_{m}-E\widehat{f}_{m}|>t\Big)\leq 2\exp\left[-\frac{nht^{2}}{2\nu_{0}f\{1+o(1)\}+2C_{K}t/3}\right],

(S.23)

\displaystyle P\Big(|\widehat{g}_{m}-E\widehat{g}_{m}|>t\Big)\leq 2\exp\left[-\frac{nht^{2}}{2(\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2})\nu_{0}f\{1+o(1)\}+2C_{K}C_{Y}t/3}\right],

(S.24)

where
C_{K}=\sup_{u}|K(u)|
.
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle P\left\{\max_{1\leq m\leq M}\left(|\widetilde{g}_{m}/\widetilde{f}_{m}^{4}|,|1/\widetilde{f}_{m}^{3}|\right)>C_{0}\right\}\leq C_{1}\exp\left(\log M-C_{2}nh\right),

(S.25)

if
n
is sufficiently large, where
C_{1},\ C_{2}>0
are some constants.
Here
\widehat{f}_{m}
,
\widetilde{f}_{m}
,
\widehat{g}_{m}
, and
\widetilde{g}_{m}
are defined in the proof of Theorem
S.1
.

Proof.

Note that
\widehat{f}_{m}-E\widehat{f}_{m}=(nh)^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\xi_{i}
, where
\xi_{i}=K\{(X_{i}-x)/h\}-EK\{(X_{i}-x)/h\}
. Then by Bernstein’s inequality
(
Bennett,, 1962
)
, we have

\displaystyle P\left(|\widehat{f}_{m}-E\widehat{f}_{m}|>t\right)=P\left(\left|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}}\xi_{i}\right|>nht\right)\leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{(nht)^{2}}{2\operatorname{var}(\sum_{i}\xi_{i})+2C_{K}nht/3}\right\}.

Recall that
\operatorname{var}(\sum_{i}\xi_{i})=n\operatorname{var}(\xi_{i})=\nu_{0}fnh\{1+o(1)\}
. Thus, the probability inequality (
S.23
) follows immediately. The inequality (
S.24
) can be derived similarly.

To derive (
S.25
), we need to bound
|\widetilde{g}_{m}/\widetilde{f}_{m}^{4}|
and
|1/\widetilde{f}_{m}^{3}|
for
1\leq m\leq M
uniformly. We only deal with
\max_{m}|\widetilde{g}_{m}/\widetilde{f}_{m}^{4}|
in the following, since
\max_{m}|1/\widetilde{f}_{m}^{3}|
can be handled similarly. Then, it is sufficient to show that

\displaystyle P\left(\max_{m}|\widetilde{f}_{m}-f|>f/2\right)\leq C_{3}\exp(\log M-C_{4}nh),

(S.26)

\displaystyle P\left(\max_{m}|\widetilde{g}_{m}-g|>C_{5}\right)\leq C_{6}\exp(\log M-C_{7}nh).

(S.27)

Note that
|\widetilde{f}_{m}-f|\leq|\widehat{f}_{m}-f|\leq|\widehat{f}_{m}-E\widehat{f}_{m}|+|E\widehat{f}_{m}-f|
, and
|E\widehat{f}_{m}-f|=O(h^{2})
. Then by (
S.23
), we have

\displaystyle P\left(\max_{m}|\widehat{f}_{m}-f|>f/2\right)\leq

\displaystyle P\left(\max_{m}|\widetilde{f}_{m}-E\widehat{f}_{m}|>f/4\right)+P\left(|E\widehat{f}_{m}-f|>f/4\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2M\exp(-C_{8}nh),

if
h
is small enough such that
h^{2}\ll f/4
. Thus, the inequality (
S.26
) follows. The inequality (
S.27
) can be derived in almost the same way.
This completes the proof of the lemma.

Lemma S.4
.

Consider three sequences of random variables
U_{n1}
,
U_{n2}
, and
V_{n}
. If
\operatorname{cor}(U_{n1},V_{n})\to 1
and
\operatorname{cor}(U_{n2},V_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, then we have
\operatorname{cor}(U_{n1},U_{n2})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Write
\rho_{n1}=\operatorname{cor}(U_{n1},V_{n})
,
\rho_{n2}=\operatorname{cor}(U_{n2},V_{n})
, and
\rho_{n3}=\operatorname{cor}(U_{n1},U_{n2})
. Then we know that the correlation matrix of
(V_{n},U_{n1},U_{n2})
, denoted by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho_{n1}&\rho_{n2}\\
\rho_{n1}&1&\rho_{n3}\\
\rho_{n2}&\rho_{n3}&1\end{bmatrix},

should be positive-semidefinite.
Thus, its determinant should satisfy
|\boldsymbol{C}_{n}|=1+2\rho_{n1}\rho_{n2}\rho_{n3}-\rho_{n1}^{2}-\rho_{n2}^{2}-\rho_{n3}^{2}\geq 0
for each
n\geq 1
.
Note that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n1}=1
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n2}=1
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}|\boldsymbol{C}_{n}|=\lim_{n\to\infty}(-1+2\rho_{n3}-\rho_{n3}^{2})=-\lim_{n\to\infty}(\rho_{n3}-1)^{2}\geq 0.

Then we must have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n3}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{cor}(U_{n1},U_{n2})=1
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma S.5
.

Consider
K+1
sequences of random variables
U_{n1},\dots,U_{nK}
and
V_{n}
. Suppose that
\operatorname{var}(U_{nk})=\operatorname{var}(V_{n})\{1+o(1)\}
,
\operatorname{cov}(U_{nk},V_{n})=\operatorname{var}(V_{n})\{1+o(1)\}
, and
(EU_{nk}-EV_{n})/\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(V_{n})}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for each
1\leq k\leq K
.
If
K
finite numbers
q_{k}\,(1\leq k\leq K)
satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}=1
, then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}U_{nk}-V_{n}=o_{p}\Big(\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(V_{n})}\Big).

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{U}_{nk}=(U_{nk}-EU_{nk})/\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(V_{n})}
and
\widetilde{V}_{nk}=(V_{nk}-EV_{nk})/\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(V_{n})}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(V_{n})}}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}U_{nk}-V_{n}\right\}=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\widetilde{U}_{nk}-\widetilde{V}_{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\frac{EU_{nk}-EV_{n}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(V_{n})}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\widetilde{U}_{nk}-\widetilde{V}_{n}+o(1),

where we have used
(EU_{nk}-EV_{n})/\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(V_{n})}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
in the last equality. Thus, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\widetilde{U}_{nk}-\widetilde{V}_{n}=o_{p}(1)
. We can compute that

\displaystyle E\mathcal{E}_{n}^{2}=E\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\widetilde{U}_{nk}\right)^{2}+E\widetilde{V}_{n}^{2}-2\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\operatorname{cov}(\widetilde{U}_{nk},\widetilde{V}_{n}).

(S.34)

Note that

\displaystyle E\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\widetilde{U}_{nk}\right)^{2}=\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{K}\sum_{k_{2}=1}^{K}q_{k_{1}}q_{k_{2}}\operatorname{cov}(\widetilde{U}_{nk_{1}},\widetilde{U}_{nk_{2}}).

(S.35)

If
k_{1}=k_{2}
, we should have
\operatorname{cov}(\widetilde{U}_{nk_{1}},\widetilde{U}_{nk_{2}})=\operatorname{var}(U_{kn_{1}})/\operatorname{var}(V_{n})=1+o(1)
. We next consider the case
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
.
In fact,

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}(\widetilde{U}_{nk_{1}},\widetilde{U}_{nk_{2}})=\operatorname{cov}(U_{nk_{1}},U_{nk_{2}})/\operatorname{var}(V_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{cor}(U_{nk_{1}},U_{nk_{2}})\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(U_{nk_{1}})\operatorname{var}(U_{nk_{2}})}\Big/\operatorname{var}(V_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{cor}(U_{nk_{1}},U_{nk_{2}})\{1+o(1)\}

(S.36)

where we have used the condition
\operatorname{var}(V_{nk})=\operatorname{var}(V_{n})\{1+o(1)\}
for each
1\leq k\leq K
in the last equality.
Note that
\operatorname{cor}(U_{nk},V_{n})=\operatorname{cov}(U_{nk},V_{n})/\sqrt{\operatorname{var}(U_{nk})\operatorname{var}(V_{n})}=1+o(1)
for each
1\leq k\leq K
, since
\operatorname{cov}(U_{nk},V_{n})=\operatorname{var}(V_{n})\{1+o(1)\}
and
\operatorname{var}(U_{nk})=\{1+o(1)\}
.
Consequently,
\operatorname{cor}(U_{nk_{1}},V_{n})\to 1
and
\operatorname{cor}(U_{nk_{2}},V_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.
By Lemma
S.4
, we conclude that
\operatorname{cor}(U_{nk_{1}},U_{nk_{2}})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.
Then by (
S.36
), we obtain that
\operatorname{cov}(\widetilde{U}_{nk_{1}},\widetilde{U}_{nk_{2}})=1+o(1)
.
Therefore,
\operatorname{cov}(\widetilde{U}_{nk_{1}},\widetilde{U}_{nk_{2}})=1+o(1)
for each pair
1\leq k_{1},k_{2}\leq K
.
Thus, by (
S.35
) and the condition
\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}=1
, we can derive that

\displaystyle E\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\widetilde{U}_{nk}\right)^{2}=\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{K}\sum_{k_{2}=1}^{K}q_{k_{1}}q_{k_{2}}\{1+o(1)\}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\right)^{2}\{1+o(1)\}=1+o(1).

Further note that
E\widetilde{V}_{n}^{2}=1
, and
\operatorname{cov}(\widetilde{U}_{nk},\widetilde{V}_{n})=\operatorname{cov}(U_{nk},V_{n})/\operatorname{var}(V_{n})=1+o(1)
. Together with above results and (
S.34
), we should have
E\mathcal{E}_{n}^{2}=o(1)
. Then we conclude that
\mathcal{E}_{n}=o_{p}(1)
. This completes the proof the lemma.

∎

Lemma S.6
.

Under the conditions assumed in Theorem
3
, we have

\displaystyle E\widehat{f}=f+\frac{\kappa_{\nu+1}}{(\nu+1)!}f^{(\nu+1)}h^{\nu+1}+o(h^{\nu+1})

(S.37)

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}(\widehat{f})=\frac{\nu_{0}f}{Nh}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

(S.38)

\displaystyle E\widehat{g}=\mu f+\frac{\kappa_{\nu+1}}{(\nu+1)!}\left\{\sum_{s=0}^{\nu+1}\binom{\nu+1}{s}\mu^{(s)}f^{(\nu+1-s)}\right\}h^{\nu+1}+o(h^{\nu+1}),

(S.39)

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}(\widehat{g})=\frac{(\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2})\nu_{0}f}{Nh}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

(S.40)

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}(\widehat{f},\widehat{g})=\frac{\nu_{0}\mu f}{Nh}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

Recall that

\displaystyle\widehat{f}=\widehat{f}(x)=\frac{1}{Nh}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K\left(\frac{X_{i}-x}{h}\right),\ \widehat{g}=\widehat{g}(x)=\frac{1}{Nh}\sum_{i=1}^{N}K\left(\frac{X_{i}-x}{h}\right)Y_{i}.

Proof of (
S.37
)
. Note that

. In fact,

\displaystyle EK\left(\frac{X_{i}-x}{h}\right)=\int K\left(\frac{u-x}{h}\right)f(u)du=h\int K(t)f(x+ht)dt

\displaystyle h\int\left\{\sum_{s=0}^{\nu}\frac{f^{(s)}(x)}{s!}(ht)^{s}+\frac{f^{(\nu+1)}(\widetilde{x})}{(\nu+1)!}(ht)^{\nu+1}\right\}K(t)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle hf+h^{\nu+2}\frac{f^{(\nu+1)}(x)}{(\nu+1)!}\kappa_{\nu+1}+h^{\nu+2}\frac{1}{(\nu+1)!}\int\left\{f^{(\nu+1)}(\widetilde{x})-f^{(\nu+1)}(x)\right\}t^{\nu+1}K(t)dt

\displaystyle hf+h^{\nu+2}\frac{f^{(\nu+1)}(x)}{(\nu+1)!}\kappa_{\nu+1}+o(h^{\nu+2}),

where
\widetilde{x}
is between
x
and
x+ht

\kappa_{\nu+1}=\int u^{\nu+1}K(u)du

K(\cdot)
in the fourth equality and the dominated convergence theorem in the last equality. Then, we can immediately obtain (
S.37

Proof of (
S.43
)
. Note that
\operatorname{var}(\widehat{f})=N^{-1}h^{-2}E\xi_{i}^{2}
, where
\xi_{i}=K\{(X_{i}-x)/h\}-EK\{(X_{i}-x)/h\}

\displaystyle EK^{2}\left(\frac{X_{i}-x}{h}\right)=\int K^{2}\left(\frac{u-x}{h}\right)f(u)du=h\int K^{2}(t)f(x+ht)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h\int K^{2}(t)\left\{f(x)+\dot{f}(\widetilde{x})ht\right\}dt=\nu_{0}^{p}fh+O(h^{2}),

where
\nu_{0}=\int K^{2}(u)du
. By proof of (

), we know that
\left[EK\left\{(X_{i}-x)/h\right\}\right]^{2}=O(h^{2})

Then we have

S.40

Proof of (
S.39
).

Note that
E\widehat{g}=h^{-1}E[K\{(X_{i}-x)/h\}Y_{i}]=h^{-1}E[K\{(X_{i}-x)/h\}\mu(X_{i})]
, since
\varepsilon_{i}
is independent of

. Note that

is also
(\nu+1)
-times continuously differentiable under the assumed conditions. Similar to the proof of (

\displaystyle EK\left(\frac{X_{i}-x}{h}\right)\mu(X_{i})=\int K\left(\frac{u-x}{h}\right)\{\mu(u)f(u)\}du=h\int K(t)g(x+ht)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h\int\left\{\sum_{s=0}^{\nu}\frac{g^{(s)}(x)}{s!}(ht)^{s}+\frac{g^{(\nu+1)}(\widetilde{x})}{(\nu+1)!}(ht)^{\nu+1}\right\}K(t)dt

\displaystyle hg+h^{\nu+2}\frac{g^{(\nu+1)}(x)}{(\nu+1)!}\kappa_{\nu+1}+o(h^{\nu+2}).

\displaystyle g^{(\nu+1)}=(\mu f)^{(\nu+1)}=\sum_{s=0}^{\nu+1}\binom{\nu+1}{s}\mu^{(s)}f^{(\nu+1-s)}.

Thus, (
S.39
) follows immediately.

Proof of (
S.40
)
. Note that
\operatorname{var}(\widehat{g})=N^{-1}h^{-2}E\zeta_{i}^{2}
, where

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h\int K^{2}(t)\mu^{2}(x+ht)f(x+ht)dt+h\sigma^{2}\int K^{2}(t)f(x+ht)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h(\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2})\nu_{0}f+O(h^{2}).

By proof of (
S.39
), we know that
\left[EK\left\{(X_{i}-x)/h\right\}\right]^{2}=O(h^{2})
.
Then we have
E\zeta_{i}^{2}=(\mu^{2}+\sigma^{2})\nu_{0}fh+O(h^{2})

S.38
) follows.

Proof of (
S.41
)
. Note that
\operatorname{cov}(\widehat{f},\widehat{g})=N^{-1}h^{-2}E\xi_{i}\zeta_{i}
, where
\xi_{i}=K\{(X_{i}-x)/h\}-EK\{(X_{i}-x)/h\}
and
\zeta_{i}=K\{(X_{i}-x)/h\}Y_{i}-EK\{(X_{i}-x)/h\}Y_{i}
. Similarly, we can compute that

Appendix C
Extension to Non-compactly Supported Covariate

The proposed GPA method can be extended to the case where the covariate
X_{i}
is not compactly supported. Without loss of generality, assume that the probability density function of
X_{i}
is supported on the whole real line
\mathbb{R}
. In such cases, we can consider a closed interval with a slowly diverging size, for example,
\mathcal{I}_{N}=[-\log N,\log N]
.
Similarly, we can set
[2\log N]J+1
equally spaced grid points
x_{j}^{*}
on
\mathcal{I}_{N}
, so that the inter-grid point distance remains
\Delta=2\log N/([2\log N]J)=O(1/J)
. Here,
[r]
denotes the integer part of
r\in\mathbb{R}
. It is worth noting that for any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we should have
x\in\mathcal{I}_{N}
for sufficiently large
N
.
In this case, we should have
x\in[x_{j}^{*},x_{j+1}^{*}]
for some
j
. We can then conduct linear interpolation at
x
, following (
C
), to obtain the GPA estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{x_{j+1}^{*}-x}{\Delta}\widehat{\mu}(x_{j}^{*})+\frac{x-x_{j}^{*}}{\Delta}\widehat{\mu}(x_{j+1}^{*}).

If
x\not\in\mathcal{I}_{N}
, we simply set
\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)=0
.
Then, it can be shown that the asymptotic results in Theorem
1
remain valid; the proof is provided at the end of this section.
This implies that we need
Jh\to\infty
such that the resulting GPA estimator can be as efficient as the global estimator. Recall that
h=CN^{-1/5}
for some constant
C>0
. Thus, the number of grid points should satisfy
[2\log N]J+1\gg N^{1/5}\log N
. This means that the required number of grid points is only
\log N
times that of the case with compact support.

Proof of Theorem
1
for non-compactly supported covariate.
The proof is almost the same as that of Theorem
1
in Appendix
A.2
. Here, we provide a brief outline.

First, note that
f(\cdot)
and
\mu(\cdot)
and their first two derivatives are continuous and hence bounded in a neighborhood of
x
under condition
1
.
Then we can verify that the conclusions for the global estimator
\widehat{\mu}(x)
in Theorem
S.1
(a) remain valid, provided conditions
1
–
3
and
f(x)>0
. Thus, we should have
\widehat{\mu}(x)-\mu(x)=Q_{1}+Q_{2}+\mathcal{Q}
, where
\sqrt{Nh}\Big\{Q_{1}(x)-B(x)h^{2}\Big\}\to_{d}\mathcal{N}\Big(0,V(x)\Big)
,
EQ_{2}=O\left(h/N+h^{4}\right)
,
\operatorname{var}(Q_{2})=O\left\{1/(Nh)^{2}\right\}
, and
\mathcal{Q}=O_{p}\left\{1/(Nh)^{3/2}\right\}
.

To prove the asymptotic results in the theorem, it suffices to deal with the case that
N
is sufficiently large such that
x\in\mathcal{I}_{N}=[-\log N,\log N]
.
Then we can assume that
x\in[x_{j}^{*},x_{j+1}^{*}]
for some
j
.
Since
f(x)>0
,
f(\cdot)
is continuous, and
\max_{k=j,j+1}|x_{j}^{*}-x|\leq\Delta\to 0
, we should have
f(x_{k}^{*})>0
for
k=j,j+1
as well. Thus, the conclusions in Theorem
S.1
(a) also hold for
\widehat{\mu}(x_{k}^{*}),\ k=j,j+1
.
Then by the linear interpolation formula, we should have

\widehat{\mu}_{\textup{GPA}}(x)-\mu(x)=\widetilde{Q}_{0}+\widetilde{Q}_{1}+\widetilde{Q}_{2}+\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}
, where

\widetilde{Q}_{0}=\widetilde{Q}_{0}(x)=q(x)\mu(x_{j}^{*})+\{1-q(x)\}\mu(x_{j+1}^{*})-\mu(x)
,

\widetilde{Q}_{1}=\widetilde{Q}_{1}(x)=q(x)Q_{1}(x_{j}^{*})+\{1-q(x)\}Q_{1}(x_{j+1}^{*})
,

\widetilde{Q}_{2}=\widetilde{Q}_{2}(x)=q(x)Q_{2}(x_{j}^{*})+\{1-q(x)\}Q_{2}(x_{j+1}^{*})
, and

\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}(x)=q(x)\mathcal{Q}(x_{j}^{*})+\{1-q(x)\}\mathcal{Q}(x_{j+1}^{*})
. Here,
q(x)=(x_{j+1}^{*}-x)/\Delta\in[0,1]
and
1-q(x)=(x-x_{j}^{*})/\Delta
are the linear interpolation coefficients.
Recall that
\max_{k=j,j+1}|x_{j}^{*}-x|\leq\Delta=O(1/J)
and
Jh\to\infty
. Then, we can use the same arguments in Appendix
A.2
to obtain the desired results. This completes the proof.

Appendix D
Discussion of Basis Expansion Method

Another promising way to deal with prediction issue for nonparametric regression model is basis expansion method
(
Hastie et al.,, 2009
)
.
Specifically, we can approximate the mean function
\mu(\cdot)
by the linear combination of a set of appropriately selected basis functions
B_{k}(x)
with
1\leq k\leq K
.
Then we have
\mu(x)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}B_{k}(x)
, where
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is a set of coefficients needs to be estimated. Once
\alpha_{k}
is estimated (denoted by
\widehat{\alpha}_{k}
), we can then predict
\mu(X^{*})
by
\widehat{\mu}(X^{*})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\alpha}_{k}B_{k}(X^{*})
for any newly observed
X^{*}
. In this case, no more estimation is needed.

Nevertheless, we should remark that there is also a significant price paid by this method. That is how to estimate
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{K})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. One natural way is the method of least squares estimation, which is obtained by minimizing the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big\{Y_{i}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}B(X_{i})\big\}^{2}
, where
B(x)=(B_{1}(x),\dots,B_{K}(x))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. This leads to
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B})^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}
, where
\boldsymbol{B}=\big(B(X_{1}),\dots,B(X_{N})\big)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}
, and
\boldsymbol{Y}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
If all the data points are placed on one single machine, then the calculation of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is straightforward. However, if the data are distributed on different local machines, then how to compute
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
becomes a tricky issue. There exist two possible solutions.

Solution 1. (Then one-shot type estimator)
We can compute the local estimator on
m
-th machine by minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{m}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big\{Y_{i}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}B(X_{i})\big\}^{2}
. This leads to the
m
-th local estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{m}=(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{m})^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}_{m}
, where
\boldsymbol{B}_{m}=\{B(X_{i}):i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}\}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times K}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}_{m}=\{Y_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}_{m}\}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Subsequently, the central machine collects these local estimators and constructs the one-shot (OS) estimator as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\textup{OS}}=M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{m}
.
The merit of this method is low communication cost since only a
K
-dimensional vector
\widehat{\alpha}_{m}
needs to be transmitted from each local machine to the central one. However, it requires that the data should be randomly distributed across different machines. Otherwise, the local estimators
\widehat{\alpha}_{m}\ (1\leq m\leq M)
could be seriously biased. This might make the final estimator
\widehat{\alpha}_{\textup{OS}}
also seriously biased. In contrast, our method is free from such a problem.

Solution 2. (The moment assembling method)
By this method, each local machine should report to the central machine two moment estimators. They are, respectively,
\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}_{m}
. Then, the central machine can calculate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\big(\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\big)^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Y}_{m}
.
Note that
\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}
is a
K\times K
matrix. Moreover, we need
K\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
.
Suppose we use second-order spline basis to approximate the twice continuously differentiable mean function
\mu(\cdot)
.
Then by the theory of
Zhou et al., (1998)
, the optimal
K
should be of the order
K=O(N^{1/5})
. Thus, the communication cost of transmitting
\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}
is of the order
O(N^{2/5})
. This leads to an expensive communication cost. In contrast, the communication cost of our GPA method only needs to be any order larger than
O(N^{1/5})
.
