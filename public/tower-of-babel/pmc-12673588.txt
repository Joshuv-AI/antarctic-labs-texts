Competitive Adsorption and Modulated Product Binding Using Pyridines on Cobalt Phosphide Enhance Hydrogenation Catalysis
Authors: Nina A. Arnosti, Gregor P. Wasser, Yu-Chun Shen, Murielle F. Delley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The binding and interaction of substrates, products, and other reaction components at the surface are key to heterogeneous catalysis. Improved catalytic properties can be obtained from organic ligands that interact with the substrate at the surface. However, competitive binding phenomena of the ligands with not only substrates but also intermediates and products of a reaction can critically influence catalysis. The tuning of these interfacial phenomena to achieve a selective hydrogenation of α,β‑unsaturated aldehydes is underexplored with heterogeneous catalysts, in particular, with transition metal phosphide-based catalysts. Herein, we systematically investigated the effect of seven pyridine-based additives with varied electronic, steric, and structural properties on the hydrogenation of trans-cinnamaldehyde by using a cobalt phosphide catalyst. Additives having electron-donating and relatively small substituents in para-position resulted in dramatically enhanced selectivity to the unsaturated alcohol by almost 2 orders of magnitude at long reaction times and enhanced overall productivity by factors of up to ∼2. This reaction outcome is dominated by the competitive adsorption of the pyridine-based additives with the produced unsaturated alcohol, which reduced product inhibition and undesired secondary reaction pathways. At early reaction times, substantial positive effects on catalytic selectivity were also observed, and these can mainly be attributed to dispersion effects and H-bonding interaction of the pyridines with the substrate. Our work highlights that judicious choice of organic additives and consideration of complex competitive adsorption phenomena and modulated binding of not only substrate but also products allow the development of more effective catalysts based on earth-abundant materials.

Introduction

Heterogeneous catalysis is governed by the interaction of components of the fluid phase with the surface of the solid catalyst. This chemistry includes a complex interplay among surface adsorbates, additives, and solvent molecules with different surface sites. , All solution components, substrates, intermediates, reaction products, and additives and solvent molecules may potentially bind to different surface sites with different strengths. This competitive binding influences the relative abundance and identity of surface species, which can, in turn, determine the observed catalytic activity, selectivity, and stability. For instance, a number of studies reported solvent effects as a key contributor to the observed reaction outcome. − Furthermore, the desired product may bind to the surface and lead to undesired products via secondary reaction pathways. For instance, overhydrogenation is often a major limitation in hydrogenation catalysis. − Strong product binding can also lead to product inhibition and catalyst poisoning. − These effects are prevalent in heterogeneous catalysis and can impact reaction outcomes significantly even at low conversion. Methods that allow directing the operating phenomena and competitive binding at the solid–fluid interface of heterogeneous catalysts in a controlled way could open new avenues to the design of better catalysts.

One strategy to tune heterogeneous catalysts is the use of organic surface-ligands. − These can be adsorbed on the catalyst during its preparation prior to catalysis or, alternatively, added directly to the reaction mixture. Surface-ligands can modify the reaction outcome by competitive binding or by influencing the binding of substrates and products at the solid–fluid interface. While surface ligands can also reduce catalytic activity, − in particular for hydrogenation reactions, the use of surface ligands has been a very successful strategy to enhance catalytic selectivity. For instance, the selectivity in the hydrogenation of α,β-unsaturated aldehydes to the desired unsaturated alcohol can be enhanced by the use of self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) at the catalyst surface that bias the binding mode of the substrate − or by ligand–metal cooperative effects. − Even enantioselective hydrogenation on heterogeneous catalysts has been achieved through strongly adsorbing chiral modifiers at the surface. − Therefore, imposing controlled interactions with organic ligands at the surface is a powerful tool to tune catalytic hydrogenations. However, this has so far mostly been developed for noble-metal-based catalysts. Such strategies are underexplored for more earth-abundant hydrogenation catalysts.

Transition metal phosphides (TMPs) could provide more sustainable alternatives to noble-metal-based hydrogenation catalysts. − However, strategies to tune TMPs with organic ligands at the surface are only starting to emerge. − Recent work showed that the activity and stability of TMP-based hydrogenation catalysts can be enhanced with phosphine- and phosphonate ester-based surface ligands, respectively. − Improved catalytic selectivity in the hydrogenation of α,β‑unsaturated aldehydes can be obtained using TMP-based hydrogenation catalysts that have been modified with thiol-based ligands. A related approach has been reported by Hicks and co-workers who showed that enhanced selectivity can also be obtained on TMPs with the addition of pyridine to the reaction feed. The limited work related to surface-ligands in TMP-based hydrogenations so far has predominantly focused on effects arising from interactions of the ligands with substrates. However, the catalytic effects are often more nuanced. Surface-ligands can also compete with the adsorption of intermediates or products. Such effects can even be the dominating force for selectivity. This has been shown for noble metal-based hydrogenation catalysts. For instance, a key component of the Lindlar catalyst is quinoline, which promotes the formation of the semihydrogenated alkene products by poisoning active sites responsible for alkene hydrogenation. − Selective semihydrogenation has also been implemented with other additives for metal-based catalysts. − In these systems, high selectivity for semihydrogenation has been attributed to a competitive adsorption of the additives with the produced alkenes that depends on their relative binding strengths. This suggests that a more targeted implementation of surface-ligand strategies to tune TMP-based catalysts would also require the consideration of competitive adsorption phenomena and the associated influence of surface-ligands on the binding of reaction intermediates and products.

Our prior work showed that the selectivity for the desired cinnamyl alcohol (COL) in the hydrogenation of trans-cinnamaldehyde (CAL) using cobalt phosphide (CoP) is limited by secondary reaction pathways. Some of these likely occur on acidic surface sites. − Pyridine is known to bind to acidic sites. ,− We therefore anticipate that pyridine could influence such secondary pathways. In light of this and the prior report by Hicks and co-workers that suggested catalytic effects from the interaction of pyridine with the substrate, the role of pyridine in hydrogenation catalysis is potentially complex. Pyridine may impact both the interaction of the substrate with the surface and secondary reaction pathways from the desired COL. A deeper understanding of both primary and secondary phenomena offers exciting opportunities to identify a tuned pyridine-based additive with optimized surface binding properties relative to not only substrates but also intermediates and products for optimal catalytic performance. This is targeted herein as a conceptually new approach to enhance hydrogenation catalysis using transition metal phosphides that considers additive properties in the context of complex reaction mixtures.

In this work, we systematically studied differently substituted pyridines (py-R) with varied electronic, steric, and structural properties as additives to enhance hydrogenation catalysis using CoP. We focus on the hydrogenation of CAL as a model substrate to benchmark the effects of different additives on selectivity and productivity. To understand the effects on catalysis from competitive adsorption between additives, substrates, and products, we probed the adsorption of different py-R molecules on the surface by a range of spectroscopic techniques and tested the influence of surface-adsorbed py-R on the adsorption of CAL and COL on CoP. Our results reveal the diverse role of pyridine-based additives on hydrogenation catalysis and highlight the importance of a tuned COL binding strength. Based on our data, we identify optimal additives for tuning.

Results

Preparation and characterization of a CoP material was done as previously reported (Section S3.1). The CoP used herein is a largely amorphous powder with a Co:P stoichiometry of roughly 1:1, particle sizes of ∼10 to 300 μm, and a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of 38 m2/g (Section S3.1). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of CoP suggests that the material exposes predominantly nonoxidized cobalt phosphide surfaces (Figure S41). We probed the catalytic hydrogenation of CAL using this CoP as catalyst in the presence of one of seven different pyridine-based additives (py-R) and compared it to the previously reported hydrogenation catalysis using CoP in the absence of an additive (Figure a). Control reactions with or without py-R showed no product formation in the absence of CoP, indicating that CoP is needed as a catalyst for hydrogenation. All catalytic hydrogenations were performed in J-Young NMR tubes in benzene-d 6, pressurized at ∼2.5 bar of H2(g) and monitored by 1H NMR spectroscopy. The catalytic hydrogenations using CoP in the presence of each py-R were replicated three to six times. The probed py-R include pyridine (py), 4-methylpyridine (py-Me), 4-tert-butylpyridine (py- t Bu), 4-dimethylamino pyridine (py-NMe2), 4-trifluoromethylpyridine (py-CF3), 2,6-dimethylpyridine (py-(Me)2), and 2,6-di-tert-butylpyridine (py-( t Bu)2). These py-R span a range of electronic (py‑NMe2 < py-Me < py-CF3) and steric (py < py-Me < py- t Bu) properties based on the corresponding Hammett (σ p ) and Charton (ν) parameters of the substituents and include both ortho- and para-substituted pyridine derivatives (py-Me and py-(Me)2; py- t Bu and py-( t Bu)2, Figure and Table S1). We note that the quantitative comparison of electronic and steric properties of para- versus ortho-substituted pyridines and mono- versus disubstituted pyridines using Hammett and Charton parameters is nontrivial, because the steric bulk of the ortho-substituents is a major factor in the pyridine N-based reactivity and because electronic effects are not necessarily additive. , However, prior work suggest that assuming similar electronic effects from identical para- and ortho-substituents and assuming additive electronic effects from the two substituents in 2,6-substituted pyridines give reasonable first approximations (though, the former is not always the case). − As approximative substituent Hammett parameters of py-(Me)2 and py-( t Bu)2, we hence used the σ p values of Me and t Bu multiplied by two (also see Section S4). We also doubled the Charton parameters of Me and t Bu to obtain a rough estimate of the steric bulk of py-(Me)2 and py-( t Bu)2. However, while this provides an approximation of the overall spatial requirements of having two substituents versus one substituent, this does not account for the steric implications of having ortho-substituents on pyridine binding at the surface via N. This is hence separately considered in our discussion below. Herein, we use Hammett and Charton values and the map shown in Figure solely to visualize qualitative electronic and steric trends obtained with different py-R additives in the catalytic hydrogenation of CAL by using CoP.

Visualization of the electronic and steric trends of the R‑substituents of the pyridine-based additives (py-R) used herein based on Hammett (σ) and Charton (ν) parameters. Charton and Hammett parameters of ortho-bis-substituted py-(Me)2 and py-( t Bu)2 were approximated by twice the Charton and twice the Hammett (σ p ) parameters of Me and t Bu groups in the para-position, respectively. The structural-steric impact on surface binding of having substituents ortho to the binding N atom in py-(Me)2 and py-( t Bu)2 is discussed separately below.

In the absence of a py-R, the hydrogenation of CAL using CoP as catalyst yielded hydrocinnamaldehyde (HCAL) as the main product, as reported previously (Figure b). After 1 h of reaction time, COL was formed with 27% selectivity using CoP as catalyst. Then, the initially formed amount of COL decreased again due to secondary reaction pathways from COL to β-methylstyrene (MS) and allyl benzene (AB) and due to COL isomerization to HCAL. That these secondary reaction pathways occur from COL and not HCAL was confirmed by the observed formation of HCAL, MS, AB, and hydrocinnamyl alcohol (HCOL) in a catalytic hydrogenation of COL using CoP under similar conditions and the formation of only HCOL in a catalytic hydrogenation of HCAL using CoP (Sections S3.4–S3.7 and S6.7). Addition of the py-R to the reaction typically lowered the initial catalytic productivity (after 1 h reaction time) but increased the product formation by up to a factor of 2 at longer reaction times of 205 h (Figures b–d and S8). Furthermore, a pronounced change in selectivity was observed in the presence of all py-R. The initial COL selectivity substantially increased by up to a factor of ∼3 from 27% in the absence of py-R to 58–75% with the py-R after 1 h reaction time (Figure e). High selectivity for COL was previously also obtained using RuMoP with pyridine or Ni2P with–SPhF ligands as catalyst, , though, at much higher H2 pressure (10–45 bar versus the 2.5 bar used herein). In view of the literature suggesting that obtaining high CO selectivity is a particular challenge at low H2 pressure, the herein obtained selectivity of up to 75% shows the remarkably high impact of the py-R additives on the reaction outcome. Differently from the prior research on ligand effects on TMP-based hydrogenation catalysis, we herein mainly focus on effects on secondary reaction pathways and product binding that are most clearly observed at longer reaction times. At longer reaction time, the selectivity for COL was <1% with CoP in the absence of py-R and 5–72% in the presence of py‑R and hence increased by up to almost 2 orders of magnitude (Figure f). The higher amount of COL observed in the presence of py-R is consistent with the observed lower production of the secondary reaction products AB and MS from COL (Figure b). However, this only accounts for part of the observed effect. This suggests that not only the secondary pathways from COL decreased but also the direct production of COL is increased in the presence of py-R. Such an effect on the direct production of COL in the presence of unsubstituted pyridine has indeed previously been proposed for bimetallic phosphide catalysts. Formation of HCOL was observed in the presence of py-R but not in their absence (Figure b).

Comparison of the product formation versus time in the presence of different py-R reveals interesting trends. The initial productivity of CoP after 1 h reaction time was higher with the ortho-substituted py-R (py-(Me)2 and py-( t Bu)2) compared to the para-substituted py-R based on product formation and H2 consumption at early reaction times (Figures c and S17). Among the para-substituted py-R, slightly higher initial productivities were obtained with more electron-withdrawing additives (py-NMe2 < py-Me < py-CF3) and no clear trend was obtained with steric bulk. The initial COL selectivity tended to be lower with substituents in the ortho-position (cf. py-(Me)2 or py-( t Bu)2 versus other py-R) and perhaps higher with bulkier and more electron-withdrawing substituents in the para-position (cf. py-Me or py- t Bu versus py and cf. py-CF3 or py-Me versus py-NMe2), but these trends are not very clear (Figure e). With py-CF3 and py-( t Bu)2, the amount of produced COL reached a maximum after ∼50 h reaction time and, compared to other py-R, AB and MS formation was most significant (Figures b and Section S6.2). This pattern resembles that observed for CoP in the absence of an additive. We attribute this pattern to secondary reaction pathways from COL to AB via dehydration, to MS via hydrogenolysis, to HCAL via isomerization, and to HCOL via further hydrogenation. This is consistent with the observation of HCAL, AB, MS, and HCOL from COL hydrogenation using CoP as discussed above (Sections S3.4–S3.7 and S6.7) as well as the observation of HCAL, AB, and MS when CoP or CoP with surface-bound py-R is contacted with COL in the absence of H2, as discussed below (Section S8.2). At high concentrations of COL (1.25 M) and in the presence of py-( t Bu)2, polymerization of COL and/or secondary products from COL can also occur. This was suggested by a control experiment where we conducted a catalytic COL hydrogenation using CoP in the presence of py-( t Bu)2 (Section S6.7). However, such a polymerization was not observed under CAL hydrogenation conditions (in presence or absence of any py-R) nor in any of the other experiments reported herein. With other py-R, the secondary reaction pathways seem to be minor, based on the observed lower amount of MS and AB and a continuously increasing amount of COL. After ∼150 h reaction time, there is no further production of COL and HCAL in the presence of most py-R. py-NMe2 is a notable exception to this trend. With py-NMe2, the produced amount of COL and HCAL is relatively high and seems to continue to increase even after 250 h (Figure b,d). We attribute this in part to a more efficient use of the limited amount of H2 in the tube (Figure S17) and in part to a reduced product poisoning in the presence of py-NMe2 (see below). The different reaction patterns with different py-R are reflected in the observed selectivity and productivity at long reaction times. While the catalytic productivity at long reaction times is increased in the presence of most py-R compared to the bare CoP, there is no productivity increase observed for py-CF3 and py-( t Bu)2 (Figure d). Catalytic selectivity at long reaction times is highest for py-NMe2 and py-Me and lowest for py-CF3 and py‑( t Bu)2 (Figure f). These data show that pyridine-based additives influence the productivity and selectivity of hydrogenation catalysis depending on electronic, steric, and structural properties.

The observed effects on catalysis with different py-R likely result from the adsorption of py-R at the surface of CoP. This is perhaps seen in a recycling experiment of the spent CoP after hydrogenation of CAL in the presence of py-NMe2. A similar catalytic performance was obtained in the second run compared to the first at longer reaction times, showing the reusability of the system (Figure S19). However, some reduction in productivity and selectivity for COL was observed at early reaction times, perhaps due to the presence of strongly bound py-NMe2 at the surface from the first run that cannot be removed by washing. That the effects on catalysis with py-R do not come from a bulk transformation of the CoP material is supported by pXRD and XPS data. pXRD patterns of the spent CoP after hydrogenation in the presence of py-R were similar to that of the bare CoP, suggesting no change of the largely amorphous cobalt phosphide bulk material (Figure S18). This is consistent with XPS data of spent CoP that showed Co 2p and P 2p signals characteristic of cobalt phosphide, similarly to what was observed for bare CoP (Figures S41–S48). We tested the possible adsorption of py-R at the surface by analysis of the py-R NMR signals in solution under catalytic hydrogenation conditions as well as when contacting CoP with py-R in the absence of CAL and H2. The materials resulting from the latter procedure are herein termed CoP-py-R (see Section S3.9). 1H NMR data showed a decrease of the amount of py-R in solution, suggesting adsorption at the surface under both hydrogenation and contacting conditions (Figures a, S15, and S25). Surface characterization of the materials by TGA, XPS, and diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform (DRIFT) analysis provided further qualitative evidence of py-R adsorption. TGA of the CoP-py-R showed a larger weight loss compared to that of pristine CoP, which we attribute to the loss of py-R from the surface (Figure b and Section S9). XPS of the spent CoP after hydrogenation in the presence of py-R showed N 1s signals at ∼400 eV that are characteristic of pyridine-based species − for all py-R and an additional F 1s signal in case of py-CF3 (Section S10 and Figures S42–S48). Similar XPS data were obtained for CoP-py-R (Figures c,d and S49–S55). DRIFT analysis of CoP after contact with a flow of pyridine in N2(g) showed IR bands associated with physisorbed pyridine and additional bands at 1654, 1594 (shoulder), 1545, 1500, and 1455 cm–1 that were not observed with the diluent material KBr (Figure e). We attribute these additional bands to the presence of Lewis acidic and perhaps some Brønsted acidic sites on CoP, on which pyridine adsorbs (see Section S11 for a detailed discussion of the DRIFT data). ,− On CoP, Lewis acidic sites likely correspond to Coδ+ sites and Brønsted acidic sites to P–OH sites. − ,, The latter can arise from a small fraction of P-surface species that are oxidized, as indicated by the minor P 2p XPS signal associated with PO x observed for CoP (Figure S41). All data combined hence suggest that py-R is adsorbed on the CoP surface, likely on Lewis and Brønsted acidic sites, and perhaps also on other sites.

To compare the amount of py-R adsorbed on the CoP surface among different py-R, we analyzed the TGA data of the different CoP-py-R and the solution NMR data under catalytic hydrogenation conditions as well as when contacting CoP with py-R in the absence of CAL and H2 more quantitatively. XPS data could not be relied on for a quantitative analysis due to the low signal-to-noise ratio of the obtained XPS spectra and imprecise N/F ratios obtained from N 1s and F 1s integration in the case of CoP-py-CF 3 (Table S13). NMR analysis of the hydrogenation reactions suggests that the amount of py-R adsorbed on CoP is particularly large for py-NMe2 and also tends to be larger for smaller py-R (py > py-Me > py- t Bu; Section S6.3). An interesting comparison comes from the solution NMR data obtained during catalysis and that obtained in the absence of CAL and H2 (Sections S6.3 and S7). Except for py-NMe2, the amount of adsorbed py-R is larger in the absence of CAL and H2 than in their presence. This suggests that the presence of CAL, H2, and hydrogenation products typically reduces the amount of py-R at the surface, perhaps by competitive binding. That competitive adsorption occurs is supported by comparison of the solution NMR data over time obtained in the presence versus absence of CAL and H2, from which we infer information on py-R surface coverage (Figures S15 and S25). In the former case, the surface coverage with py‑R showed a larger variation over time after an initial adsorption presumably from competitive adsorption with increasing amounts of product and decreasing amounts of substrate. In the absence of catalysis, surface coverage with py-R was more constant. This is most evident for py-( t Bu)2, py-(Me)2, and py, for which surface coverage decreased by 30–60% under catalytic conditions but was constant within 2% in the absence of CAL and H2 after initial adsorption. We note that the competitive binding and the surface coverage of CoP with py‑R are likely a function of the py-R concentration in solution. Prior work suggests that an increase in additive concentration in solution can increase the surface coverage of the additive and thereby influence hydrogenation catalysis. ,, That surface coverage with py-R could potentially be increased with higher py-R concentration in solution in this system also seems plausible in light of the differences in the amount of adsorbed py-R on CoP in the presence versus absence of CAL and H2 inferred from NMR analysis (Figures S16 and S26). Herein, we used a fixed initial concentration of py-R of roughly ten equivalents of py-R per “CoP” surface units in all experiments for comparability. TGA data suggest similar trends compared to NMR data, where py-R adsorption tends to be larger with smaller and more electron-donating py-R (py > py-Me > py- t Bu and py-NMe2 > py-Me > py-CF3; Figure f). Furthermore, NMR and TGA data combined suggest that the ortho-substituted py-R tend to adsorb less than the respective para-counterparts (py-Me > py-(Me)2 and py- t Bu > py-( t Bu)2; Figures f, S16, and S26). While general trends are consistent among NMR and TGA data, absolute quantification gives different results. These differences can be explained by the differences in measurement conditions. NMR data correspond to the concentrations of species in solution under reaction conditions. Any observed signal decrease may hence be attributed to both weakly and strongly adsorbing py‑R on CoP. On the other hand, TGA samples of CoP-py-R had been washed and dried under vacuum before analysis and likely only account for strongly adsorbed py-R. Indeed, quantification by TGA gave lower amounts of adsorbed py-R than quantification by NMR (∼0.002–0.004 mmol/m2 versus ∼0.01–0.1 mmol/m2), suggesting that only a subset of surface py-R is characterized by TGA (Tables S4, S5, S10). The weight loss measured with different CoP-py-R by TGA hence provides a rough estimate of the surface coverage of CoP with strongly bound py-R ranging from 11 to 32% (Figure f). Lower surface coverages are obtained with py-R of larger steric bulk and more electron-withdrawing substituents, likely because of weaker binding to acidic sites. The obtained surface coverages are based on an estimated number of surface “CoP” units and the assumption that the observed weight loss for each CoP-py-R is exclusively due to py-R while neglecting any possible contribution from, e.g., residual H2O (also see the more detailed discussion of these assumptions in Section S9 and ref ). While the obtained surface coverages are hence only approximate values, the trends observed are likely more accurate. We also note that the surface coverage values represent molar coverages, and hence, we expect that a bulkier py-R would block a larger part of the surface than a less bulky py-R at the same coverage. The obtained surface coverages of <35% are consistent with the idea that only a fraction of the surface is covered with strongly bound py-R while other parts of the surface may be occupied by other reaction components and/or weakly bound py-R under reaction conditions. Overall, the data show that the py-R adsorption on the CoP surface depends on the steric, electronic, and structural properties of py-R. Our data further suggest that this adsorption is influenced by the presence of the reaction components of the catalytic hydrogenations, perhaps by competitive adsorption.

Upon contact of CoP-py-R with CAL, the concentration of CAL in solution decreased, suggesting the adsorption of CAL at the CoP surface. The CAL adsorption was increased compared to that when the bare CoP was used (Figure b). Prior work on noble metal-based catalysts has shown that while a flat adsorption mode of unsaturated aldehydes on the surface is favored at low surface coverage, a vertical adsorption can occur at higher coverage. ,, In view of this literature, we attribute the observed larger CAL adsorption in the presence of py-R to a change in preferred binding mode of CAL from flat to vertical due to the steric constraints of the surface py-R that allows for an increased molecular packing of CAL. Comparison among the different CoP-py-R showed no clear trends in the amount of CAL adsorbed (CALads), neither with steric nor electronic properties of the py-R nor with surface coverage (Figures b and S31). NMR data further suggest that ∼10–30% py-R of the original surface py-R on CoP-py-R (as measured by TGA) desorbed upon contact with CAL, where sterically more hindered py-R seemed to be displaced to the largest extent (py- t Bu > py-Me > py; Table S6). These data are consistent with a steric interaction between surface-bound CAL and py-R that influences their binding to CoP. Our data further suggest that the desorbed py-R only accounts for <1% of the adsorbed CAL (Table S6). This suggests that there is not a 1:1 replacement of py-R for CAL at the same surface sites, but distinct sites as well as physisorbed CAL are likely involved. As shown above, CoP-py-R likely only contain a subset of the possible surface py-R sites under reaction conditions, namely, the strongly bound py-R on acidic sites. The NMR data hence suggest that CAL binding can occur at distinct sites from these strongly bound py-R and that the preferred CAL binding mode at these sites is influenced by the presence of the surface py-R, likely by a steric interaction.

Contacting the different CoP-py-R with COL showed a different pattern (Figure ). COL adsorbed more than CAL on the bare CoP under similar conditions by a factor of 1.5 based on 1H NMR spectroscopic analysis of the solution (COLads, cf. black dots in Figures b versus b, Table S7). In contrast to our observations with CAL, the presence of py-R on the surface reduced COL adsorption compared to the bare CoP (by a factor of 1.1–10, Figure b). Importantly, the adsorption of COL seemed to correlate with surface coverage of different py-R, where the highest adsorption of COL was observed for bare CoP, CoP-py-( t Bu) 2 , and CoP-py-CF 3 , which have zero or very low surface coverage with py-R among the CoP-py-R, respectively (Figures f and S35). This is in line with our expectation that both py-R and COL should be able to bind to acidic sites based on our results above and their known chemical properties and reactivity on CoP. ,,− However, a more quantitative comparison of the amount of py-R on the surface versus the COL adsorption difference between CoP‑py‑R and bare CoP (ΔCOLads) shows that every py-R on the surface reduced the COL adsorption by 10–50 molecules (Tables S7 and S10). Furthermore, similarly to the experiments using CAL, ∼10–40% of the original surface py-R was desorbed (Table S7). The amount of py-R desorbed from the surface was much smaller than the amount of COL adsorbed (only 0.2–2.6% of COLads, Table S7). The large apparent reduction of adsorbed COL through presence of surface py-R was not necessarily expected but may in part be explained with a reduction of not only chemisorbed but also physisorbed COL, e.g., through changes in the interfacial structure. Still, even with a possible effect on COL physisorption, the data suggest that there is again not simply a 1:1 replacement of adsorbed py-R with COL at the same surface sites, but distinct sites are also involved. We propose that the adsorbed py-R reduce COL binding at the surface, not only because of binding to the same sites but also because the surface py-R can influence the binding of COL at other sites. This could for instance occur through a steric bias that forces COL to adsorb through a more weakly bound vertical adsorption mode or an electronic effect of surface py-R on the binding strength of COL at other sites. These possibilities are further discussed in the Discussion section. In contrast to what we observed with CAL, we see clear trends in the case of the COL contacting experiments of how much py-R desorbed with not only steric but also electronic properties of the py-R (Table S7). Large and electron-withdrawing py-R were displaced the most. This is in line with what is expected if both COL and py-R bind to acidic sites, since binding on such sites should be weaker with large and electron-withdrawing py-R compared to other py-R. Overall, the data suggest that COL binding and the binding of strongly bound py-R strongly influence each other, and this influence is dependent on surface coverage as well as electronic and steric properties of the py-R substituents. Furthermore, while COL and py-R can likely both bind to the same types of sites (e.g., acidic sites), COL can also bind to other sites.

When CoP was contacted with COL, we also observed the formation of HCAL, AB, and MS, although ∼1.2–400 times less than what was observed during hydrogenation catalysis (Figures c–e and S38 and Table S9). These data show that secondary reaction pathways from COL to AB and MS and to HCAL by isomerization can take place to some extent even in the absence of H2 (perhaps because of hydrogen at the surface of CoP stemming from the preparation of the material). We note that under these conditions, the HCAL formed must come from COL isomerization rather than CAL hydrogenation. The amount of HCAL, AB, and MS produced correlated with the amount of COL adsorbed (Figure S39) and inversely correlated with the selectivity toward COL at long reaction times (Figure f). This suggests that the extent of secondary product formation depends on the amount and strength of COL binding at the surface. The presence of py-R at the surface generally led to a reduced formation of HCAL, MS, and AB compared to that of the bare CoP (Figure c–e). For the bare CoP and with py-R of low surface coverage, i.e., py-CF3 and py-( t Bu)2, considerably more HCAL formed. In these cases, the HCAL formed is ∼10–16% of what is formed during hydrogenation (during which HCAL can be obtained directly from CAL hydrogenation as well as from COL isomerization; see Table S9). For the other py-R with larger surface coverage, the isomerization is suppressed to a larger extent (Figure c). For these py-R, only 0.25–1.8% HCAL of what is formed during hydrogenation is produced (Table S9). This suggests that for the py-R with larger surface coverage, isomerization of COL is a less important pathway to HCAL formation under hydrogenation conditions while isomerization of COL to HCAL is more important with the bare CoP, and in the presence of py-CF3 or py-( t Bu)2. The isomerization pathway seems to be particularly hindered with py-NMe2. With py-NMe2, 400 times less HCAL than during the hydrogenation of CAL is produced. Overall, the least HCAL, AB, and MS were observed for CoP-py-NMe 2 , and the most were observed for CoP-py-CF 3 and CoP-py-( t Bu) 2 among the different py-R. These data hence show that secondary product formation correlates with the amount of adsorbed COL, which is dictated by the presence, coverage, and properties of surface py-R.

Discussion

Our results show that the different py-R adsorb at the CoP surface and influence the binding of CAL and COL. Here, we discuss these effects in the context of the observed catalytic properties in CAL hydrogenation with different py-R. We separately discuss the effects on catalysis from the interaction of py-R with (i) the substrate CAL and (ii) the product COL, which dominate the observed effects at early and longer reaction times, respectively. The conclusions made below are based on the mechanistic insight available through the observed product formation patterns from CAL hydrogenation in the presence of a systematic series of py-R with varied properties, the reaction pathway analysis using COL or HCAL as hydrogenation substrates, characterization of the adsorption of different py-R on CoP by solution NMR in presence and absence of CAL and H2, as well as by TGA, DRIFT, and XPS of spent CoP and of CoP–py-R, the results obtained from contacting CoP–py-R with CAL or COL, and experiments of CAL hydrogenation dosed with COL or HCOL (see below).

(i) Prior literature suggests that the observed decreased initial productivity in the presence of the py-R could be due to a reduced accessibility of the surface for CAL and/or H2. , This is supported by the observation that the ortho-substituted py-(Me)2 and py-( t Bu)2 have the highest initial productivity (Figure c). The binding of these two py-R is expected to be sterically hindered and to lead to low surface coverage. However, the amount of CAL adsorbed on the different CoP-py-R did not show a correlation with surface coverage of py-R and, in fact, CAL adsorption was generally promoted by the presence of surface-adsorbed py-R (Figures and S31). However, these strongly bound py-R only correspond to a subset of all possible surface py-R under catalytic reaction conditions. During catalysis, py-R is present in excess in solution. Our data hence imply that the initial productivity is dominated by surface sites that are distinct from those associated with strongly adsorbed py‑R. Our results suggest that CoP-py-R include py-R on Lewis and Brønsted acidic sites (i.e., Coδ+ and P–OH sites). The work of us and others suggest that while acidic sites are responsible for dehydration reactions, the hydrogenation of CAL to COL and HCAL likely occurs on metallic sites (Co or Coδ+ sites that have metallic character), which can be distinct from acidic sites. ,− ,, Phosphide moieties (Pδ−) on the surface of TMPs have been shown to be able to influence catalytic properties of the metallic sites and sometimes participate in the splitting of H2 in a cooperative way. ,− One possible explanation for the decreased initial productivity is hence that, in solution, py-R can also bind to metallic sites under catalytic conditions (although more weakly than on the acidic sites) and competitively bind with CAL and/or H2.

Selectivity in the hydrogenation of α,β-unsaturated aldehydes is thought to be mainly determined by the adsorption mode of the substrate. − , The substantial effect on initial selectivity observed in the presence of py-R hence suggests that the CAL adsorption mode is significantly influenced by the presence of surface py-R (Figures e and ). This is consistent with our observation of increased CAL adsorption in the presence of py-R that likely reflects a change in preferred binding mode from flat (interacting with the surface via both double-bonds) to vertical (interacting with the surface via CO). ,, This is also in line with our data, suggesting that most of the strongly bound surface py-R remain on the surface in the presence of CAL. That organic ligands at the surface can bias the adsorption mode of unsaturated aldehydes is very well-known for noble-metal based catalysts and has also been shown for some transition metal phosphides. − ,,, Dispersion interactions between organic surface ligands and the substrate are thought to lead to a preferred adsorption of the unsaturated aldehyde via the CO and thereby to high CO hydrogenation selectivity. − , However, this effect cannot fully explain the observed selectivity at early reaction times. Dispersion interactions should be more extensive at higher surface coverage and with more sterically demanding substituents and hence lead to higher COL selectivity in these cases. ,,, However, comparison of the measured surface coverage of CoP-py-R with the observed COL selectivity at early reaction times shows that some py-R with relatively low surface coverage (e.g., py-CF3, py-Me, py- t Bu) still lead to high COL selectivity (Figures e and f). This is perhaps most clearly shown when comparing py-R having similar steric properties, where py-R with lower surface coverage show higher initial COL selectivity (cf. py-Me or py-CF3 versus py-NMe2; cf. py- t Bu or py-CF3 versus py-(Me)2 in Figures e and f). This suggests that there is an additional mechanism of how py-R influence initial selectivity. Prior work suggests that pyridine could also enhance the selectivity to the unsaturated alcohol in hydrogenation reactions of α,β-unsaturated aldehydes by favorable H-bonding interactions between the α-H of pyridine and the unsaturated aldehyde. , Such H-bonding would be expected to increase with the acidity of α-H of py-R and hence with increasing electron-withdrawing properties of the substituents. Electron-withdrawing substituents on py-R should thereby enhance the initial selectivity for COL. This was indeed observed, to some extent, as the initial selectivity for COL decreased as py-CF3 ≈ py-Me > py-NMe2 (Figure e). However, the trend is not very clear. This is not unexpected since the electron-withdrawing properties of py-R substituents also reduce surface coverage through weakened binding to acidic sites (Figure f). At lower surface coverage, the steric bias for vertical CAL adsorption is limited, which in turn reduces COL selectivity. Clearer evidence for H-bonding between the α-H and CAL comes from our experiments with ortho-substituted py-R, for which such H-bonding would not be possible. Our data indeed show that ortho-substituted py‑(Me)2 and py-( t Bu)2 led to particularly low COL selectivity (Figure e). Based on these data, we attribute the observed substantial enhancement of COL selectivity in the presence of py-R at early reaction times to the influence of surface py-R on the CAL adsorption mode by both H-bonding and dispersion interactions.

(ii) A particular goal of this study is to probe catalytic effects originating from the competitive adsorption of the pyridine-based additives with products of the hydrogenation reaction. Our data show a tendency for a lower overall productivity at long reaction times with py-R present when the respective CoP-py-R displays a higher COL adsorption and vice versa (cf. py-CF3, py-( t Bu)2, CoP without additive versus py-NMe2, py, py-Me, py- t Bu, py-(Me)2; Figures d and b). These data could imply that COL adsorbed on the surface acts as a poison for further hydrogenation catalysis (product-inhibition). We tested this hypothesis by adding a small amount of COL (5%) to a hydrogenation of CAL using CoP under otherwise similar conditions as used for catalytic CAL hydrogenations above (Section S6.8). The presence of COL in the initial reaction mixture indeed led to a reduced HCAL productivity compared to the CAL hydrogenation without added COL. This is consistent with COL acting as an inhibitor of hydrogenation catalysis. Product inhibition by COL is also in line with prior theoretical work suggesting that the desorption of the unsaturated alcohol can have a high activation barrier. ,, Contacting the bare CoP and CoP-py-R with COL showed that the presence of surface py-R reduces COL adsorption (Figure b). Our results suggest that this likely occurs via two mechanisms: by competitive adsorption on acidic sites and by influencing the binding of COL at other sites (e.g., metallic sites). We speculate that COL binding at other sites could be reduced in the presence of py-R due to steric interactions between surface py-R and COL that limit COL binding in a flat adsorption mode via CC and only allow the weaker binding in a vertical mode via OH (Figure ). It is possible that surface-bound py-R could also influence the adsorption of other reaction products on CoP by similar mechanisms. Another possibility is that surface py-R could modulate the electronic properties of CoP through charge-transfer and thereby influence bonding at other sites. , That such an electronic modulation of the CoP surface through surface-bound py-R can occur is supported by our XPS data. The Co 2p and P 2p XPS signals associated with cobalt phosphide of all CoP–py-R samples were shifted to lower binding energies by up to 0.85 eV compared to the corresponding Co 2p and P 2p signals of the bare CoP (Figure S56). The observation of shifted XPS signals rather than additional distinct XPS signals that would be attributable to sites that bind py-R suggests that the surface-bound py-R influence the surface electronically and that Co and P at the surface are electronically coupled. However, the exact mechanism of the influence of surface py-R on COL binding at other sites cannot be unambiguously determined with the data in hand. Still, our data clearly show that less adsorbing py-R (py-CF3 and py-( t Bu)2) reduce COL binding to a smaller extent (Figure b) and are hence are less able to reduce product inhibition. This leads to a lower productivity with py-CF3 and py-( t Bu)2 (Figure d). We also note that we only observed the formation of HCOL from CAL hydrogenation in the presence of the py-R but not in their absence (Figure b). This may indicate that HCOL surface binding is similarly influenced by presence of py-R. We speculate that in the absence of py-R, any formed HCOL either remains adsorbed on the CoP surface (and is therefore not detected by NMR) and could either poison the catalyst or react to AB. To probe this, we ran a hydrogenation of CAL using CoP in the presence of 5% added HCOL under otherwise similar conditions (Section S6.8). The observed reduced hydrogenation productivity under these conditions suggests that HCOL can indeed act as poison in hydrogenation catalysis using CoP, similarly to COL. Overall, competitive adsorption and modulated binding of COL and HCOL with py-R reduced product poisoning as a function of the binding properties of the py-R. Summary of the proposed mechanism of the effects observed in the catalytic hydrogenation of CAL using CoP in the presence of different py-R. The left side shows the CoP surface in the absence of py-R: CAL can bind in a flat or vertical mode on CoP, which leads to the production of HCAL and COL, respectively (an initial effect on selectivity, red arrows). At longer reaction times, selectivity for COL is reduced by secondary reaction pathways and isomerization: COL binding to acidic and metallic sites leads to AB, MS, and HCAL formation (red arrows). Strong binding of COL to metallic sites also leads to product inhibition (blue). The right side shows the effects of py-R additives: py-R bind competitively with COL to acidic sites (blue), which reduces AB formation and perhaps isomerization to HCAL. Presence of py-R on acidic and/or metallic sites further influences the binding mode of CAL (green), which leads to a preferred COL formation (red). Surface py-R also influences COL binding (green), e.g., by imposing a vertical adsorption mode or a reduced binding strength for COL, which leads to reduced product inhibition and reduced MS and HCAL formation via secondary reaction pathways. These effects depend on the binding properties of the specific py-R used.

Perhaps the most striking effects from competitive adsorption and modulated binding are shown by the substantial change in selectivity at long reaction times in the presence of different py-R. Contacting different CoP-py-R with COL showed that higher COL adsorption and formation of larger amounts of HCAL, MS, and AB are obtained with lower py-R surface coverage (Figures f and ). A similar pattern was also observed under catalytic hydrogenation conditions at long reaction times, where high selectivity for the undesired secondary products HCAL, AB, and MS and low selectivity for the desired COL correlated with high COL adsorption using different py-R and vice versa (Figure S37). Our results hence suggest that the competitive adsorption between py-R and COL limits the secondary reaction pathways from COL to AB and MS and to HCAL through isomerization. Notably, while the HCAL versus COL selectivity at early reaction times shows a much weaker correlation with COL adsorption (likely because of dominating dispersion and H-bonding interaction effects, as discussed above), MS formation follows the trend even at early reaction times (Figure S36). Combined, the results suggest that the competitive adsorption and modulated binding effects dominate the selectivity at long reaction times and can impact catalysis even at early reaction times. We and others have previously proposed that acidic surface sites are responsible for AB formation from COL. ,,,,, Pyridine-derivatives are known to bind strongly to acidic sites, which is consistent with our IR data. The strong binding of pyridines to such acidic sites could hence inhibit secondary reactions taking place on acidic sites. ,− This is consistent with our results suggesting that only a part of the strongly bound py-R is displaced by COL (Figure ). Our data suggest that py-R with particularly low surface coverage, especially py‑CF3 and py-( t Bu)2, are the least able to compete with and reduce COL binding (Figures f and b). Hence, contacting CoP with COL in their presence leads to the most AB, HCAL, and MS (Figure c–e). The production of MS occurs on different sites, i.e., metallic sites. Isomerization of COL to HCAL may occur on metallic or acidic sites. Our data show that py-R with higher surface coverage also decrease the MS and HCAL production to a larger extent (Figures f, c, and e). This is consistent with the idea that surface py-R not only compete with COL for acidic sites but also reduce the binding of COL at other sites. As discussed above, this could be through steric constraints and/or electronic modulation of the surface through the presence of surface-bound py-R. Indeed, XPS data suggest that the smallest electronic effects are obtained with the ortho-substituted py-R and the largest with py-Me and py-NMe2 (Figure S56), which is consistent with the observed low and high COL selectivity at long reaction times, respectively (Figure f). Overall, py-R reduce secondary product formation and increase COL selectivity at long reaction times by competitive adsorption on acidic sites and modulated binding strength (and surface concentration) of COL on other sites on CoP. These effects are schematically shown in Figure . Py-R with an electron-donating substituent that is not too bulky seem to be best at suppressing isomerization and secondary reactions and thereby lead to the highest COL selectivity at long reaction times, with py‑NMe2 ≅ py-Me > py > py- t Bu > py-(Me)2 > py-CF3 > py‑( t Bu)2 (Figures f and c–e).

Conclusion

The addition of pyridine-based additives to the catalytic hydrogenation of cinnamaldehyde dramatically enhanced the catalytic selectivity and productivity of CoP. A preferred formation of the unsaturated alcohol COL in the presence of the py-R is shown to result from two main effects: (i) the interaction of CAL with surface-py-R that biases the binding mode of CAL and (ii) the competitive adsorption and modulated binding of COL with py-R that limits product inhibition and secondary reaction pathways from COL. Comparison of the effects of ortho- and para-substituted pyridines showed that the first effect (i) involves favorable H‑bonding interactions between the α-H of the pyridines and CAL in addition to dispersion effects. The second effect on selectivity (ii) dominates at longer reaction times and is significantly influenced by the steric and electronic properties of the substituted pyridine. Our data suggest that pyridines with electron-donating or relatively small substituents bind more strongly to acidic surface sites. These py-R thereby inhibit secondary reaction pathways more successfully by reducing COL binding to acidic and metallic sites to a larger extent. The additives py-NMe2 and py-Me yield the best results for COL selectivity overall. The effect of the py-R on catalytic productivity is also a function of the reaction time. Initially, the additives generally decrease the catalytic productivity for hydrogenation products, presumably by limiting access to the surface for CAL and H2. At longer reaction times, product-inhibition becomes more important. The competitive adsorption of the py-R and COL for acidic sites and the associated reduced COL binding strength on metallic sites then enhances catalytic productivity. Therefore, while initially, the productivity is highest with additives that bind weakly (especially py-CF3 and py-( t Bu)2), more strongly adsorbingpy‑R enhance productivity at longer reaction times. Combined with the requirements for selectivity, we propose that py-Me or py-NMe2 perform best overall as additive in hydrogenation catalysis using CoP.

This study shows that a more nuanced design strategy for additives in hydrogenation catalysis can be obtained by careful consideration of competitive adsorption phenomena between additive and not only substrate but also products. Such strategies are underdeveloped for the transition metal phosphide-based catalyst studied herein. From a systematic study of pyridine-based additives with different steric, electronic, and structural properties, we identify pyridine-based additives that provide an optimal balance of the ensemble of interfacial effects for high catalytic selectivity and productivity. Specifically, our results show how binding strength, binding site, and surface coverage of the additive can be optimized relative to the surface binding of substrates, intermediates, and products of the reaction such as to favor desired and limit undesired reaction pathways and product poisoning. These insights could be important in the context of achieving operational stability with transition metal phosphide catalysts on different reaction scales. This work thereby exemplifies important additive design principles that provide new avenues to optimize earth-abundant transition metal phosphide materials for hydrogenation catalysis.
