Mikhail Muzychuk, Christian Pech, Andrew Woldar

Abstract
. Jordan schemes generalize association schemes in a similar way as Jordan algebras generalize the associative ones. It is well-known that association schemes of maximal rank are in one-to-one correspondence with groups (so-called thin schemes). In this paper, we classify Jordan schemes of maximal rank-to-order ratio and show that regular Jordan schemes correspond to a special class of Moufang loops, known as Ring Alternative loops.



1.
Introduction

In 1959, Shah proposed
[
14
]
to use the Jordan matrix product as an algebraic tool for the analysis of block designs. This was a far-reaching generalization of the concept of an association scheme introduced in the same year by Bose and Mesner
[
3
]
. This concept was further developed and led to a flourishing research area called ”Algebraic Combinatorics” by Bannai and Ito in their famous monograph
[
2
]
. On the other hand, Shah’s idea was never noticed until Bailey published her book

[
1
]
where she analysed the ”first” properties of the objects arising from Shah’s idea. In particular, she showed that the symmetrization of an association scheme always produces objects introduced by Shah. In 2001 Cameron called these objects Jordan schemes and posed the question whether any symmetric Jordan scheme is a symmetrization of an association scheme. The anwser was obtained in 2019 by Klin, Muzychuk and Reichard
[
10
]
who gave a negative answer to Cameron’s question and started a systematic investigation of Jordan schemes and, more generally, coherent Jordan configurations.

It is well known that all finite groups give rise to a special subclass of association schemes: the thin schemes
[
15
]
. They may be characterized as those that have a maximal rank-to-order ratio among all association schemes. In this paper, we classify all Jordan schemes that reach the maximum rank-to-order ratio. We also show that regular Jordan schemes with this property are in a one-to-one correspondence with a special class of Moufang loops.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 contains all necessary definitions and basic facts about coherent Jordan configurations and Jordan schemes. The third section is the central one of the paper. It is shown that the rank-to-order ratio of a JS is bounded by
3/2
where equality happens if and only if the scheme is non-regular and thin. All such Jordan schemes are classified in Subsection 3.1. Subsection 3.2 deals with the thin regular Jordan schemes. It is proven there that there is a one-to-one correspondence between Jordan schemes of this type and Ring Alternative Moufang loops (RA-loops). In the fourth section it is shown that the Jordan schemes corresponding to RA-loops are autonomous, that is to say, they are not algebraic fusions of coherent configurations.

2.
Background Material

2.1.
Binary relations and matrices

Let
\Omega
be a non-empty finite set. A
binary relation
on
\Omega
is an arbitrary subset
s\subseteq\Omega\times\Omega
. We denote by
s{\omega}:=\{\beta\in\Omega\mid(\omega,\beta)\in s\}
an
s
-neighborhood
of
\omega
. A relation is called
regular
if
|s\omega|
does not depend on
\omega
. In this case, the constant
|s\omega|
is called
valency
of
s
and will be denoted as
n_{s}
.
If
s
is a regular relation of valency one, then
s{\omega}
contains a unique element which will be denoted as
s(\omega)
, i.e.,
s{\omega}=\{s(\omega)\}
. Thus
s
is a function on
\Omega
. All functions in this paper, including permutations of
{\sf Sym}(\Omega)
, will be considered as binary relations.

Every relation
s
admits a
transposed

one
s^{t}=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Omega^{2}\mid(\beta,\alpha)\in s\}
. Following
[
5
]
we call a relation
s\subseteq\Omega\times\Omega

thin
if
|s\omega|\leq 1\geq|s^{t}\omega|
holds for each
\omega\in\Omega
.

We use
ab
for the relational product of
a,b\subseteq\Omega^{2}
and
a\star b=ab\cup ba
for their
Jordan product
. The
diagonal relation

1_{\Delta},\Delta\subseteq\Omega
is defined as
1_{\Delta}:=\{(\delta,\delta)\mid\delta\in\Delta\}
.
The
adjacency matrix
of
s\subseteq\Omega^{2}
is the square matrix, notation
\underline{s}
, whose rows and columns are labeled by elements of
\Omega
. Its
(\alpha,\beta)
-entry
\underline{s}_{\alpha,\beta}
is one if
(\alpha,\beta)\in s
and zero, otherwise.

The algebra of all
\Omega\times\Omega
matrices over a field
\mathbb{F}
will be denoted as
M_{\Omega}(\mathbb{F})
. It is always assumed that
{\mathsf{c}har}({\mathbb{F}})\neq 2
. The standard product of two matrices
A,B\in M_{\Omega}(\mathbb{F})
will be written as
A\cdot B
or
AB
. Their Jordan product
\frac{1}{2}(AB+BA)
will denoted as
A\star B
. The Schur-Hadamard (entry-wise) product will be denoted as
A\circ B
. The identity matrix and all-one matrix will be denoted as
I_{\Omega}
and
J_{\Omega}
, respectively.
The linear span of any set of matrices
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq M_{\Omega}({\mathbb{F}})
will be denoted as
\langle{\mathcal{S}}\rangle
.

2.2.
Rainbow algebras

Consider a vector subspace
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq M_{\Omega}(\mathbb{F})
which is closed with respect to
t
and
\circ
and contains
I_{\Omega},J_{\Omega}
.
Every such subspace
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq M_{\Omega}(\mathbb{F})
has a uniquely determined
standard basis
— the basis consisting of the minimal
\circ
-idempotents of the algebra
({\mathcal{A}},\circ)
. These are
0,1
-matrices
A_{1},...,A_{r}\in{\mathcal{A}}
that are pairwise
\circ
-orthogonal. Each
A_{i}
is the adjacency matrix of a unique binary relation
s_{i}\subseteq\Omega^{2}
, i.e.,
A_{i}=\underline{s}_{i},i=1,...,r
. The set
S=\{s_{1},...,s_{r}\}
forms a partition of
\Omega^{2}
. This partition satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

1_{\Omega}
is a union of some relations of
S
;

(2)

S^{t}=S
, where
S^{t}:=\{s^{t}\mid s\in S\}
;

Following
[
8
]
we call a pair
\mathfrak{S}=(\Omega,S)
a
rainbow
if it satisfies the above conditions.
The elements of
S
are called
basic
relations of the rainbow. The numbers
|\Omega|
and
|S|
are called the
order
and the
rank
of the rainbow; it has rank one if and only if
|\Omega|=1
. If the point set
\Omega
has at least two elements, then
|S|\geq 2
where the equality holds if and only if
S=\{1_{\Omega},\Omega^{2}\setminus 1_{\Omega}\}
. These rainbows
are called
trivial
. The
adjacency matrix
of a rainbow
{\mathfrak{S}}=(\Omega,S)
is the
\Omega\times\Omega
matrix
A({\mathfrak{S}})
defined via
A({\mathfrak{S}})_{\alpha,\beta}=S(\alpha,\beta)
where
S(\alpha,\beta)
is a unique basic relation containing
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Omega^{2}
.

The rainbow of maximal rank
|\Omega|^{2}
is called
discrete
.
Each basic relation of a discrete rainbow contains a unique ordered pair.

In what follows, we say that a subspace
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq M_{\Omega}(\mathbb{F})
is a
rainbow algebra
if it contains
I_{\Omega},J_{\Omega}
and is closed with respect to
{}^{t},\circ
. We say that
{\mathcal{A}}
is
symmetric
if every
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
symmetric;
homogeneous
if
\langle I_{\Omega}\rangle\circ{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\langle I_{\Omega}\rangle
;
regular
if
\langle J_{\Omega}\rangle\cdot{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\langle J_{\Omega}\rangle
.

Given a rainbow
\mathfrak{S}=(\Omega,S)
, the linear span
\mathbb{F}[S]:=\langle\underline{s}\rangle_{s\in S}\subseteq M_{\Omega}(\mathbb{F})
is a rainbow algebra. Thus, there exists a one-to-one correspondence
between rainbows and rainbow subalgebras of
M_{\Omega}(\mathbb{F})
. The statement below collects some properties of rainbow algberas.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}=(\Omega,S)
be a rainbow and
{\mathcal{A}}=\langle\underline{s}\rangle_{s\in S}
the corresponding rainbow algebra. Then

(1)

{\mathcal{A}}
is symmetric iff all basic relations of
\mathfrak{S}
are symmetric;

(2)

{\mathcal{A}}
is homogeneous iff
1_{\Omega}\in S
;

(3)

{\mathcal{A}}
is regular iff all basic relations of
\mathfrak{S}
are regular.

In what follows, we call a rainbow symmetric, homogeneous, or regular depending on which of the properties mentioned above it satisfies. Notice that the regularity of a rainbow always implies its homogeneity but not vice versa.
A rainbow will be called
thin
if all its basic relations are thin.

Given two rainbows
\mathfrak{S}=(\Omega,S)
and
\mathfrak{S^{\prime}}=(\Omega,S^{\prime})
, we say that
\mathfrak{S}
is a
fusion
of
\mathfrak{S^{\prime}}
(equivalently,
\mathfrak{S^{\prime}}
is a
fission
of
\mathfrak{S}
) if
\mathbb{F}[S]\subseteq\mathbb{F}[S^{\prime}]
.

Two rainbows
\mathfrak{S}=(\Omega,S)
and
\mathfrak{S^{\prime}}=(\Omega^{\prime},S^{\prime})
are called
(combinatorially) isomorphic
if there exists a bijection
f:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega^{\prime}
satisfying
f(S):=\{f(s)\mid s\in S\}=S^{\prime}
where
f(s):=\{(f(\alpha),f(\beta))\mid(\alpha,\beta)\in s\}
.

2.3.
Coherent Jordan algebras

A rainbow algebra is called
coherent algebra
(see
[
8
]
) if it is closed with respect to matrix multiplication
\cdot
. Similarly, a rainbow algebra will be called a
coherent Jordan algebra
(coherent J-algebra, for short) if it is closed with respect to Jordan multiplication
\star
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}=(\Omega,S)
be a rainbow, and let
{\mathcal{A}}:=\langle\underline{s}\rangle_{s\in S}
be the corresponding rainbow algebra. We say that
\mathfrak{S}
is a
coherent configuration
(respectively, a
J-configuration
) if
{\mathcal{A}}
is a coherent algebra (respectively, a J-algebra). In both cases, the algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
will be referred to as
adjacency algebra
of
\mathfrak{S}
. In what follows, we are going to use the abbreviations CC and JC for coherent configurations and coherent J-configurations, respectively.

If
\mathfrak{S}=(\Omega,S)
is a coherent configuration, then for any pair
s,r\in S
we have
\underline{s}\cdot\underline{r}=\sum_{t}p_{s,r}^{t}\underline{t}
for suitable nonnegative integers
p_{s,r}^{t}
. These numbers are called
structures constants
of
\mathfrak{S}
. Due to their combinatorial meaning
p_{s,r}^{t}=|\alpha s\cap\beta r^{t}|,(\alpha,\beta)\in t
, they are also called the
intersection numbers
of
\mathfrak{S}
.

Similarly, one can define the intersection numbers for coherent J-configuration:
p_{\{s,r\}}^{t}=|\alpha s\cap\beta r^{t}|+|\alpha r\cap\beta s^{t}|,(\alpha,\beta)\in t
.
Notice that in this case we write them as
p_{\{s,r\}}^{t}
stressing the fact that
\star
is commutative. They also appear as the structure constants for the Jordan product
\underline{s}\star\underline{r}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t}p_{\{s,r\}}^{t}\underline{t}
.

Recall that a homogeneous coherent configuration is called an
association scheme
(see
[
15
]
). Following this pattern, we will call a homogeneous coherent J-configuration a
Jordan scheme
. While it is well known that association schemes are always regular, Jordan schemes do not possess this property in general. It was shown in
[
12
]
that a symmetric JS is always regular. Regarding non-symmetric Jordan schemes, they are not necessarily regular, but have a very special structure — see
[
12
, Proposition 2.2]
. Examples of non-regular Jordan schemes also appear in Subsection
3.1
of this paper.

2.4.
Coherent J-closure

It follows immediately from the definition of a coherent J-algebra that the intersection of any set of coherent J-algebras defined on
\Omega
is again a coherent J-algebra on
\Omega
. This observation leads to the notion of the
coherent J-closure

J(\mathcal{S})
of a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq M_{\Omega}(\mathbb{F})
of matrices as the intersection of all coherent J-algebras containing
\mathcal{S}
.

The coherent J-closure generalizes the well-known concept of the
coherent closure
of
\mathcal{S}
which is the intersection of all coherent algebras containing
\mathcal{S}
. It is often called the WL-closure, since Weisfeiler and Leman were the authors of the algorithm which computes it efficiently. For this reason, we are going to denote it as
\operatorname{WL}(\mathcal{S})
. Notice that we always have
J(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\operatorname{WL}(\mathcal{S})
.

2.5.
Algebraic fusions of J-configurations

Given a J-configuration
{\mathfrak{S}}=(\Omega,S)
, a permutation
\alpha\in{\sf Sym}(S)
is called an
algebraic J-automorphism
of
{\mathfrak{S}}
if its linear extension to
\mathbb{C}[S]
is an automorphism of the Jordan algebra
(\mathbb{C}[S],\star)
. All algebraic J-automorphisms of
{\mathfrak{S}}
form a subgroup of
{\sf Sym}(S)
that will be denoted by
\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
. Notice that the transpose permutation
\tau:a\mapsto a^{t}
is always an algebraic J-automorphism of
(\mathbb{C}[S],\star)
. The group
\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
consists of all permutations of
{\sf Sym}(S)
which preserve the tensor of structure constants of Jordan multiplication with respect to the standard basis. In other words, a permutation
u\mapsto u^{\prime}
of
S
belongs to
\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
if and only if

\forall_{u,v,w\in S}:\ p_{\{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\}}^{w^{\prime}}=p_{\{u,v\}}^{w}

The following statement collects some properties of subgroups of
\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The following properties hold.

(1)

The transpose permutation
\tau
belongs to the center of
\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
;

(2)

For any
\Phi\leq\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
and
s\in S
the relation
\Phi(s):=\bigcup_{\phi\in\Phi}\phi(s)
is either symmetric or anti-symmetric;

(3)

The relations
\Phi(s),s\in S
form a JC on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Proof of part (a).
One can easily check that for any pair
r,s\in S
, it holds that

s=r^{t}\iff(\underline{s}\star\underline{r})\circ I_{\Omega}\neq 0.

Since any
\phi\in\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
preserves
I_{\Omega}
, we conclude that

(\underline{s}\star\underline{r})\circ I_{\Omega}\neq 0\iff(\underline{\phi(s)}\star\underline{\phi(r)})\circ I_{\Omega}\neq 0,

hereby, implying

s=r^{t}\iff\phi(s)=\phi(r)^{t}
.

Proof of part (b).

It follows from part (a) that

(\Phi(s))^{t}=\bigcup_{\phi\in\Phi}\phi(s)^{t}=\bigcup_{\phi\in\Phi}\phi(s^{t})=\Phi(s^{t}).

If
s^{t}=\phi(s)
for some
\phi\in\Phi
, then
\Phi(s^{t})=\Phi(\phi(s))=\Phi(s)
implying that
\Phi(s)
is symmetric.

If
s^{t}\neq\phi(s)
for every
\phi\in\Phi
, then
\psi(s^{t})\neq\phi(s)
, and, consequently,
\psi(s^{t})\cap\phi(s)=\emptyset
for any pair of
\psi,\phi\in\Phi
. Therefore

\Phi(s^{t})\cap\Phi(s)=\bigcup_{\psi,\phi\in\Phi}(\psi(s^{t})\cap\phi(s))=\emptyset
. Hence
\Phi(s)
is anti-symmetric in this case.

Proof of part (c).
Denote
{\mathcal{A}}:=\mathbb{C}[S]
and extend each
\phi\in\Phi
on
{\mathcal{A}}
linearly via
\phi(\underline{s}):=\underline{\phi(s)}
. We will use the same notation for the induced linear operator on
{\mathcal{A}}
. Since
\phi
is an automorphism of
({\mathcal{A}},\star,\circ,{}^{t})
,
we conclude that

\phi(X\star Y)=\phi(X)\star\phi(Y),\phi(X\circ Y)=\phi(X)\circ\phi(Y),\phi(X^{t})=\phi(X)^{t}.

Since
\phi(I_{\Omega})=I_{\Omega},\phi(J_{\Omega})=J_{\Omega}
, the subspace
{}^{\Phi}\!\!{\mathcal{A}}=\{X\in{\mathcal{A}}\mid\forall_{\phi\in\Phi}\ \phi(X)=X\}
is a coherent Jordan algebra. It is easy to check that the matrices
\{\underline{\Phi(s)}\}_{s\in S}
form the standard basis of
{}^{\Phi}\!\!{\mathcal{A}}
.
∎

It follows from the above statement that for any
\Phi\leq\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
the pair
{}^{\Phi}{{\mathfrak{S}}}:=(\Omega,\{\Phi(s)\}_{s\in S})
is also a JC which will be called an
algebraic fusion
of
{\mathfrak{S}}
.

2.6.
Constructing Jordan configurations from coherent ones

If
{\mathfrak{S}}=(\Omega,S)
is a
CC, then it is also a JC. Recall that a permutation
\phi
of
S
is called an
algebraic automorphism
(
anti-automorphism
) of
{\mathfrak{S}}
if its linear extension to
\mathcal{A}:=\langle\{\underline{s}\}_{s\in S}\rangle
is an automorphism (anti-automorphism) of
(\mathcal{A},\cdot)
. The group
\mathsf{AAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
of all algebraic automorphisms of
{\mathfrak{S}}

is contained in
\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
. Also,
\tau\in\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
. Every anti-automorphism of
{\mathfrak{S}}
is a composition of
\tau
and some
\phi\in\mathsf{AAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
. All automorphisms and anti-automorphisms of
{\mathfrak{S}}
form a subgroup of
\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
which will be denoted by
\mathsf{TAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
. If
{\mathfrak{S}}
is commutative, then
\mathsf{AAut}({\mathfrak{S}})=\mathsf{TAut}({\mathfrak{S}})=\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
. If
{\mathfrak{S}}
is not commutative, then
\tau\not\in\mathsf{AAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
and
[\mathsf{TAut}({\mathfrak{S}}):\mathsf{AAut}({\mathfrak{S}})]=2
. It is easy to check that
\mathsf{TAut}({\mathfrak{S}})=\mathsf{AAut}({\mathfrak{S}})\times\langle\tau\rangle
.
It is obvious that

\mathsf{TAut}({\mathfrak{S}})\leq\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
. In general, the inclusion could be proper. The smallest example known to us has order
16
and was found by computer search.

If
{\mathfrak{S}}=(\Omega,S)
is a thin association scheme, then
S
is a regular subgroup of
{\sf Sym}(\Omega)
. In this case a permutation
\phi\in{\sf Sym}(S)
belongs to
\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
if and only if it satisfies
\phi(st)\in\{\phi(s)\phi(t),\phi(t)\phi(s)\}
for all
s,t\in S
. It was shown in
[
13
]
that any such map is either automorphism or anti-automorphism

1

1

The authors are grateful to R. Lipyanski who informed them about the paper
[
13
]
.
Thus for thin AS we always have
\mathsf{TAut}({\mathfrak{S}})=\mathsf{JAut}({\mathfrak{S}})
.

We call a JC
\mathfrak{S}=(\Omega,S)

non-autonomous
if it is an algebraic fusion of a coherent configuration
\mathfrak{C}=(\Omega,C)
via a subgroup
\Phi\leq\mathsf{JAut}(\mathfrak{C})
. A JC will be called
autonomous
if it cannot be obtained as an algebraic fusion of some CC sharing the same point set. Notice that in
[
12
]
Jordan schemes were divided into two types:
proper
and
improper
. Improper are those which are symmetrizations of ASs. The others are proper. Under this definition, any non-symmetric JS is automatically proper.
Regarding symmetric Jordan schemes, there are proper ones which are non-autonomous. The smallest example known to us is of order
24
(we know that there are no examples of order less than
16
).

Example
.

Computer calculations show that
\text{AS}(24,526)
,
\text{AS}(24,597)
,
\text{AS}(24,669)
from the catalog of small association schemes by Hanaki and Miyamoto (see
[
7
]
, available in GAP
[
6
]
via the COCO2P package
[
11
]
), each has two algebraic fusions that are proper symmetric Jordan schemes.

It looks like the proper JSs constructed in
[

]
are also autonomous, but we did not prove this. We can claim this only for the smallest example of order

constructed there.

3.
Thin Jordan schemes

It is well-known that each basic graph of an association scheme
(\Omega,S)
is regular. Therefore we always have that
|S|\leq|\Omega|
where the equality holds if and only if each basic relation
s\in S
is a permutation on
\Omega
. Such a scheme is called thin and it is not difficult to check that an association scheme
(\Omega,S)
is thin if and only if
S
is a regular subgroup of
{\sf Sym}(\Omega)
. Since every group has a regular representation, one can consider groups as a particular case of association schemes.

Although symmetric Jordan schemes are always regular, it is no longer true for nonsymmetric ones, as the following simple example shows.

Example
.

The full matrix algebra
M_{\Omega}({\mathbb{F}})
is a
discrete
coherent algebra of rank
|\Omega|^{2}
. In the case of
\Omega=\{1,2\}
the mapping

\phi:\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\\
\end{bmatrix}\rightarrow\begin{bmatrix}d&b\\
c&a\\
\end{bmatrix}
is an anti-automorphism. Therefore, the subspace
{\mathcal{A}}:={}^{\langle\phi\rangle}M_{\{1,2\}}({\mathbb{F}})=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&a\\
\end{bmatrix}\,\vline\,a,b,c\in{\mathbb{F}}\right\}
is a Jordan coherent algebra. It has order two and rank three. The corresponding coherent J-configuration
(\Omega=\{1,2\},S=\{s_{0}=I_{\Omega},s_{1}=\{(1,2)\},s_{2}=s_{1}^{t}\})
is homogeneous but not regular. It’s rank-to-order ratio
|S|/|\Omega|
is equal to
3/2
.

The following statement shows that this is the maximal value of a rank-to-order ratio for a non-regular JS.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(\Omega,S)
be a non-regular JS. Then
|S|/|\Omega|\leq 3/2
where the equality holds if and only if all basic relations are thin.

Proof.

By
[
12
, Proposition 2.2]
and its proof there exists a partition
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{0}
with
|\Omega_{1}|=|\Omega_{0}|

such that every regular relation is contained in
\Omega_{0}^{2}\cup\Omega_{1}^{2}
and each non-regular one is contained either in
\Omega_{0}\times\Omega_{1}
or in
\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{0}
. Thus
S
splits into a disjoint union
S=S_{r}\cup S_{0,1}\cup S_{1,0}
where
S_{r}
stands for all regular relations while

S_{i,1-i}:=\{s\in S\mid\subseteq\Omega_{i}\times\Omega_{1-i}\}
.

It follows from
\sum_{s\in S_{i,1-i}}|s|=|\Omega_{i}|\cdot|\Omega_{1-i}|
and
|s|=n_{s}|\Omega_{i}|
that

\sum_{s\in S_{i,1-i}}n_{s}=|\Omega|/2
. Since all summands in the latter sum are non-negative integers, we conclude
|S_{i,1-i}|\leq|\Omega|/2
for
i=0,1
.

Since a basic relation
s
is regular if and only if
s\subseteq\Omega_{0}^{2}\cup\Omega_{1}^{2}
, we can write

|\Omega_{0}^{2}\cup\Omega_{1}^{2}|=\sum_{s\in S_{r}}|s|\iff\frac{|\Omega|^{2}}{2}=\sum_{s\in S_{r}}n_{s}|\Omega|\iff\sum_{s\in S_{r}}n_{s}=\frac{|\Omega|}{2}.

Arguing as before we conclude that
|S_{r}|\leq|\Omega|/2
.
Combining altogether we obtain

|S|=|S_{r}|+|S_{0,1}|+|S_{1,0}|\leq\frac{3}{2}|\Omega|.

It follows from the above arguments that an equality
|S|=\frac{3}{2}|\Omega|
happens if and only if
n_{s}=1
for all
s\in S
.
∎

Since the maximal value of rank-order ratio is attained by non-regular thin Jordan schemes, our first goal is to classify all of them. This is done in the next subsection.

3.1.
Non-regular thin Jordan schemes

Let us start this section by describing how every commutative homogeneous coherent algebra gives rise to a non-regular homogeneous coherent Jordan algebra.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\leq M_{n}(\mathbb{F})
be a commutative homogeneous coherent algebra. Then we define
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{C})\subseteq M_{2n}(\mathbb{F})
according to:

\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{C}):=\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\[2.84526pt]
C&A\end{smallmatrix}\right]\;\middle|\;A,B,C\in\mathcal{C}\right\}.

Proposition 3.3
.

For every commutative homogeneous coherent algebra
\mathcal{C}\leq M_{n}(\mathbb{F})
we have that
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{C})
is a homogeneous coherent Jordan algebra.

Proof.

Clearly,
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{C})
is a linear subspace of
M_{2n}(\mathbb{F})
.
Since
\mathcal{C}
itself is closed under Schur-Hadamard multiplication, so is
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{C})
. It remains to show the closure under Jordan product: to this end, let
A,B,C,D,E,F\in\mathcal{C}
. We compute

2\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\[2.84526pt]
C&A\end{bmatrix}\star\begin{bmatrix}D&E\\[2.84526pt]
F&D\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2AD+BF+EC&2AE+2BD\\[5.69054pt]
2DC+2AF&2AD+BF+EC\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{C}).\qed

An important special case is when
\mathcal{C}

Proposition 3.4

\begin{bmatrix}A&O\\[2.84526pt]

\forall_{C\in\mathcal{H}}\,\forall_{A\in\mathcal{G}_{1}}\,:\,\varphi(g)=C^{-1}AC.

3.2.
Regular thin Jordan schemes

In this subsection, we assume that
(\Omega,S)
is a regular thin JS, that is,
S
is a set of permutations on
\Omega
. The set
S
contains the identity permutation
1_{\Omega}
and is closed when transposed, that is,
s^{t}\in S
for every
s\in S
. Notice that
s^{t}
is a permutation inverse to
s
, that is,
s^{-1}=s^{t}
.

Since
S
is a partition of
\Omega^{2}
into a disjoint union of permutations, for every pair
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Omega^{2}
the basic relation
S(\alpha,\beta)\in S
is the unique permutation that maps
\alpha
to
\beta
. From the definition of a JS it follows that
S(\alpha,\alpha)=1_{\Omega}
and
S(\alpha,\beta)=S(\beta,\alpha)^{-1}
. The same definition implies that the adjacency matrix
(S(\alpha,\beta))_{\alpha,\beta\in\Omega}
is a
Latin
square. Pick an arbitrary point
\omega_{0}\in\Omega
and define a binary operation
\diamond
on
S
as follows
a\diamond b=S(\omega_{0},a(b(\omega_{0})))
, that is, a triple
a,b,c\in S
satisfies
a\diamond b=c
iff
a(b(\omega_{0}))=c(\omega_{0})
.

Remark
.

It follows from the definition that the product
\diamond
depends a choice of ”base point”
\omega_{0}
. It could be that a choice of a different base point will lead to a non-isomorphic magma.

Proposition 3.5
.

The magma
(S,\diamond)
has the following properties

(1)

1_{\Omega}
is neutral element of
(S,\diamond)
;

(2)

\forall_{a\in S}\ a^{t}\diamond a=1_{\Omega}
;

(3)

\forall_{a,b\in S}\ a^{t}\diamond(a\diamond b)=b
;

(4)

\forall_{a,b\in S}
the equation
x\diamond a=b
has a unique solution
x\in S
.

Proof.

(1) We have
a\diamond 1_{\Omega}=S(\omega_{0},a(1_{\Omega}(\omega_{0})))=S(\omega_{0},a(\omega_{0}))=a
. Similarly,
1_{\Omega}\diamond a=a
.

(2) This part follows from the identity
a^{t}(a(\omega_{0}))=\omega_{0}=1_{\Omega}(\omega_{0})
.

(3)

a\diamond b=c\Leftrightarrow a(b(\omega_{0}))=c(\omega_{0})\Leftrightarrow b(\omega_{0})=a^{t}(c(\omega_{0}))\Leftrightarrow a^{t}\diamond c=b
.

(4) We have
x(a(\omega_{0}))=b(\omega_{0})
. Therefore,
x=S(a(\omega_{0}),b(\omega_{0}))
.
∎

It follows from the above statement that
(S,\diamond)
is a loop with left inverse property. It is well-known that each element
a\in S
of a loop determines two permutations
\ell_{a}
and
r_{a}
on
S
, defined naturally by
\ell_{a}(x)=a\diamond x
, and
r_{a}(x)=x\diamond a
. From (3) it follows that
\ell_{a^{t}}\ell_{a}=1_{S}
, that is,
\ell_{a^{t}}=\ell_{a}^{-1}
. From (3), it follows that the left division in the loop
(S,\diamond)
is
a^{t}\diamond b
. The right division will be denoted as
b/a
, that is,
(b/a)\diamond a=b
. Later we will show that
b/a=b\diamond a^{t}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\sigma:S\rightarrow\Omega
be the mapping defined via
\sigma(s)=s(\omega_{0})
. Then

(1)

\sigma
is a bijection;

(2)

\sigma^{-1}a\sigma=\ell_{a}
, for each
a\in S
;

(3)

The triple of mappings
\sigma,\sigma,1_{S}
is an isotopism between the Latin squares
(v/u)_{v,u\in S}
and
(S(\alpha,\beta))_{\alpha,\beta\in\Omega}
.

Proof.

(1) If
s\neq t\in S
, then
s\cap t=\emptyset
, implying that
\sigma(s)=s(\omega_{0})\neq t(\omega_{0})=\sigma(t)
. Thus,
\sigma
is injective. To prove surjectivity, pick an arbitrary
\omega\in\Omega
and set
s:=S(\omega_{0},\omega)
. Then
\sigma(s)=s(\omega_{0})=\omega
.

(2) We have to show that
a\sigma=\sigma\ell_{a}
holds for each
a\in S
. The latter equality is equivalent to the following one
\forall_{s\in S}:\ a(\sigma(s))=\sigma(\ell_{a}(s))
. Now
a(\sigma(s))=a(s(\omega_{0}))=(a\diamond s)(\omega_{0})=\sigma(\ell_{a}(s))
.

(3) We have to show that
S(\sigma(u),\sigma(v))=1_{S}(v/u)=v/u
holds for all
u,v\in S
. Denote
w:=v/u
.
Then
w\diamond u=v
implying
w(u(\omega_{0}))=v(\omega_{0})
. Hence
w=S(\sigma(u),\sigma(v))
.
∎

The Proposition above implies that the permutations
\{\ell_{s}\}_{s\in S}
form a Jordan scheme on
S
which is isomorphic to the original one:
(\Omega,S)
. Therefore
\forall_{u,v\in S}\ \underline{\ell_{u}}\cdot\underline{\ell_{v}}+\underline{\ell_{v}}\cdot\underline{\ell_{u}}=\underline{\ell_{u\diamond v}}+\underline{\ell_{v\diamond u}}
, or equivalently,

\forall_{u,v\in S}\ \ell_{u}\ell_{v}\cup\ell_{v}\ell_{u}=\ell_{u\diamond v}\cup\ell_{v\diamond u}.

(7)

This equality may be rephrased as

\forall_{u,v,w\in S}\ \{u\diamond(v\diamond w),v\diamond(u\diamond w)\}=\{(u\diamond v)\diamond w,(v\diamond u)\diamond w)\}.

(8)

As an immediate consequence of the above property we conclude that
(S,\diamond)
has the left alternative property:
u\diamond(u\diamond v)=(u\diamond u)\diamond v
.

For a pair
u,v\in S
we set
A_{u,v}:=\{w\in S\mid u\diamond(v\diamond w)=(u\diamond v)\diamond w\}
. Notice that
1_{\Omega}\in A_{u,v}
. It follows from (
8
) that
A_{u,v}=A_{v,u}
.

3.2.1.

We will show here that

is a loop. We start with the following statement.

Proposition 3.7

Let

be an arbitrary pair. Then the following hold:

(1)

If

(2)

If

Part (a) follows from (

Part (b). It follows from

(u\diamond v)\diamond w\neq(u\diamond v)\diamond w

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
u,v\in S
and let

(1)

\ell_{u\diamond v}=\ell_{u}\ell_{v}1_{A}\cup\ell_{v}\ell_{u}1_{\bar{A}}

(2)

If

Part (a). By (

\ell_{u}\ell_{v}\cup\ell_{v}\ell_{v}=\ell_{u\diamond v}\cup\ell_{v\diamond u}

By (

Equivalently,

Part (b).
It follows from

Let us count the intersection of

\begin{array}[]{rl}|\ell_{v}1_{A}\cap\ell_{v}|=|A|&\\

Proposition 3.9
.

For all

Proposition 3.10

The loop

Proposition 3.11
.

(S,\diamond)
is a Moufang loop.

Proof.

A loop is Moufang iff it is simultaneously left and right Bol loop.
We start by showing that
(S,\diamond)
is a left Bol loop. To this end we need to prove the validity of the identity

y\diamond(z\diamond(y\diamond x))=(y\diamond(z\diamond y))\diamond x.

(10)

This identity is equivalent to
\ell_{y}\ell_{z}\ell_{y}=\ell_{y\diamond(z\diamond y)}
. To see that the latter identity holds, we note that there exists some
w\in S
such that
\ell_{y}\ell_{z}\ell_{y}=\ell_{w}
(see Proposition
5.1
in the Appendix). Further we argue that since
(S,\{\ell_{s}\}_{s\in S})
is a Jordan scheme (in particular we use that in a Jordan scheme two relations are either equal or disjoint), we have that
\ell_{w}=\ell_{y\diamond(z\diamond y)}
if and only if
\ell_{w}(1_{S})=\ell_{y\diamond(z\diamond y)}(1_{S})
. It remains to compute:

\ell_{w}(1_{S})=\ell_{y}(\ell_{z}(\ell_{y}(1_{S})))=y\diamond(z\diamond y)=\ell_{y\diamond(z\diamond y)}(1_{S}).

Hence, identity (
10
) holds and,
(S,\diamond)
is indeed a left Bol loop.

To see that
(S,\diamond)
is also a right Bol loop we need to prove the validity of the identity

((x\diamond y)\diamond z)\diamond y=x\diamond((y\diamond z)\diamond y).

(11)

Again we note that this is equivalent to the identity
r_{y}r_{z}r_{y}=r_{(y\diamond z)\diamond y}
. From Proposition
3.9
it follows that
r_{z}=\tau\ell_{z^{t}}\tau
where
\tau\in\mathsf{Sym}(S)
is the transposing involution on
S
, i.e.
\tau(x)=x^{t}
. Since
(S,\diamond)
is a left Bol loop, we have
\ell_{y^{t}}\ell_{z^{t}}\ell_{y^{t}}=\ell_{y^{t}\diamond(z^{t}\diamond y^{t})}
(see above). If we conjugate both sides of this identity by
\tau
, then we obtain on one hand

\tau\ell_{y^{t}}\ell_{z^{t}}\ell_{y^{t}}\tau=(\tau\ell_{y^{t}}\tau)(\tau\ell_{z^{t}}\tau)(\tau\ell_{y^{t}}\tau)=r_{y}r_{z}r_{y},

and on the other hand

\tau\ell_{y^{t}\diamond(z^{t}\diamond y^{t})}\tau=\tau\ell_{((y\diamond z)\diamond y)^{t}}\tau=r_{(y\diamond z)\diamond y},

as desired. Thus Identity (
11
) holds, too, and
(S,\diamond)
is a right Bol loop.∎

Proposition 3.12
.

If the Jordan scheme

4.2.
Thin regular Jordan schemes are autonomous

In this subsection we will prove the statement in the title. To formulate it exactly we change slightly some notation for this subsection. Namely, if

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
