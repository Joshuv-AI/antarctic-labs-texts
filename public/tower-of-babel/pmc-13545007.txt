Co-design of programmable material systems
Authors: Dex Doksoo Lee, Liwei Wang, Bolin Chen, Ying-Kuan Tsai, Jay Sim, Ruike Renee Zhao, Xiaojia Shelly Zhang, Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in manufacturing, stimuli-responsive materials, and actuation technologies have fueled the development of programmable material systems (PMS) that can change their functional states in response to external stimuli. This functional switch is instrumental for applications that demand adaptability and robustness under dynamic environments such as wearable electronics, haptic interfaces, and soft robots. In principle, achieving an interesting degree of programmability in material systems is a challenging design problem as it requires careful consideration of geometry, material, stimulus, and process amidst dynamic environments. This task becomes even more challenging when compounded by multifunctionality requirements, multiphysics modeling, coupling among the design entities, and resource-intensive performance evaluation. In practice, prevailing approaches often heuristically fix some entities or decouple their interactions, which significantly limits achievable performance and generalizability of the design approach. Motivated by this gap between promise and practice, we present a co-design formalism that highlights shared design considerations across prior works and categorizes design strategies for managing complexity. With the proposed lens, we reveal common threads in existing design approaches, identify opportunities and open research questions for co-design to achieve better performance and generalization. Through this perspective article, we aim to lay a design foundation for boosting progress in this emerging interdisciplinary field.Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

A computer program uses a programming language to transform different inputs into corresponding outputs (Fig. 1a). Similarly, a programmable material system (PMS) can be understood as a physical program. Its manufacturing process, material composition and architecture define a physical programming “language” that dictates how the system responds to input stimuli, transforming its internal states to interact with the environment and realize diverse functions (Fig. 1b). A PMS recasts passive matter as a platform for embodied intelligence, embedding computation, sensing, and adaptation directly within the material body and minimizing reliance on digital processors and bulky electronics. This strategy establishes a new paradigm for autonomous systems, enabling lower power consumption, more direct physical interaction, and greater scalability, while enhancing robustness in harsh environments. It creates an exciting frontier across domains, from wearable electronics and tunable optics to soft robotics and self-monitoring infrastructures.

Much like how programming languages gain expressiveness with expanded syntax and libraries, the functional flexibility of PMS has grown through three key advances that enrich our physical “programming language”: stimuli-responsive materials, advanced manufacturing for precise control of materials and structures, and versatile stimulus actuation. First, stimuli-responsive materials—such as liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs)1,2, hydrogels3,4 (Fig. 2d), shape memory polymers/alloys5, dielectric elastomers6, and magneto-active composites7 (Fig. 2a, f)—enable diverse responses in shapes and material properties to heat, light, pH, electric or magnetic fields8. Second, advanced fabrication techniques enable complex spatial patterning of heterogeneous materials, particularly those supporting multi-material systems9–18 (Fig. 2a, b, d). Third, actuation platforms enable flexible stimulus modulation for rapid and precise control. Over the past decade, PMS research has advanced at the intersection of these three components, producing a large volume of compelling demonstrations. For example, responsive shape-morphing has enabled programmable soft grippers and deployable shells19–23 (Fig. 2a, b, d). Soft robots driven by patterned magnetic/electric fields, light, or thermal gradients have shown locomotion with minimal onboard electronics7,24–27 (Fig. 2a, f, g). By leveraging responsiveness to control wave propagation, these systems have been applied to waveguides28, biomimetic color-changing skins29,30, noise control31, information processing32, and anti-counterfeiting33.

In contrast, relatively few attempts were made to establish a shared design framework for the computational design of PMS—a critical direction for tackling the field’s outstanding challenges34. While advances in PMS have created rich “physical programming libraries”, translating this potential into functional designs remains challenging, with a notable gap between what can be fabricated and what can be accurately modeled or inverse designed. Just as libraries are useful only when they are compatible and properly coordinated, realizing target tasks in PMS requires a design framework that organizes and tightly integrates multiple interdependent components. However, this creates an overwhelmingly challenging design problem. It inherently entails a joint, combinatorial design space, where design entities are entangled through complex physical causal links (Fig. 1c), e.g., processing conditions that affect material constitutive behavior and in turn impact achievable performance and functions35. The evaluation of a single design thus often requires performing expensive simulations or experiments. The challenge is further amplified in multifunctional designs, where different functionalities often have competing performance targets. As a result, existing PMS designs often rely on heuristics and employ siloed design rather than co-designing all the entities. Most demonstrations to date use a priori simplification of the design space, such as pre-specifying material type, fixed geometric representation, or predefined actuation scenarios. These workflows explore design entities in isolation, which can limit design flexibility, reduce generalizability, obscure cross-entity coupling, and slow the accumulation and transfer of knowledge for new design scenarios.

This gap motivates the present perspective, in which we adopt a computational design viewpoint to show how PMS designs can be programmed through the co-design of materials, architectures, and stimuli. Herein, co-design means the concurrent design of two or more PMS entities, such as process, material, architecture, external stimulus, possibly amidst dynamic environments, when these entities in principle are designable. We present a general co-design formalism, formulated as a multi-objective optimization problem to simultaneously optimize material, architecture, external stimulus, and processing conditions under varying environments. Distinct from relevant reviews and perspectives focusing on individual design entities (e.g., materials, stimuli, or architectures) and primarily emphasizing manufacturing, applications, or underlying mechanisms, this Perspective approaches PMS from a computational design standpoint that seeks to synthesize and integrate all entities within a unified framework. This perspective provides a shared language for consistent description, comparison, and transfer of insights across different PMS platforms that are often discussed in isolation. In the following sections, we introduce the components of the formalism (Fig. 1c), following a typical design workflow. First (Sec. Design Entity: What to Design), we identify "what to design” by specifying key entities and their mathematical representations. Next (Sec. Design Evaluation: How To Evaluate), we determine "how to evaluate” a design using various simulation strategies, ranging from physics-based to data-driven approaches. Finally (Sec. Design Synthesis: How to Design), we integrate design and evaluation into an iterative optimization loop, enabling customized solutions to address the ultimate question: "how to design.” This abstraction is independent of the material choice and application, allowing insights to be shared across domains. It also provides a framework to reinterpret prior demonstrations, uncover implicit modeling and design assumptions, and reveal recurring strategies in complexity management. By doing so, it offers a principled basis for deriving design insights, identifying under-explored co-design opportunities, and motivating new benchmarks that capture the core advantages of PMS. We believe co-design of PMS provides a pathway toward boosting scientific discovery in soft robots, wearable electronics, and waveguiding where design is instrumental for achieving desirable functional switching.

In the literature of metamaterials design, the central focus has been geometry as metamaterials’ properties are largely impacted by geometry rather than material composition36. The proposed framework is an attempt to expand the conventional scope by incorporating structure, material, stimulus, and processing conditions, each of which is typically addressed in silo in the literature. This broader design view is key to the metamaterials equipped with embodied intelligence, where multifunctionality, adaptability, and robustness often demand the design beyond geometry. Echoing the motivation of active metamaterials37–41, the framework provides a concrete pathway to transform metamaterials from passive structures into an active and integrated platform for embodied intelligence. The framework also facilitates uncovering the common thread among canonical design choices of representations in metamaterials (e.g., origami and lattice), enabling both the reorganization of prior works in new ways and cross-domain inspiration, particularly from a computational design perspective.

Design entity: what to design

Over the last few decades, PMS has encompassed diverse physics and fabrication methods, resulting in a wide range of combinatorial choices in material, actuation, fabrication, geometry, and function. The corresponding design space—namely, which entities to design and how they are represented—has not been clearly defined in a unified way. This conceptual fragmentation has led to different PMS designs developing their own “specialized languages” for describing design entities, such as origami systems focusing on folding creases and kirigami on cutting patterns. This diversity makes design strategies highly problem-specific, difficult to translate or compare across systems, and challenging for extracting shared design principles. Motivated by this challenge, we introduce a common lens that evaluates PMS through a computational graph comprising the five entities: process, material, architecture, external stimulus, and environment, as shown in Fig. 1c. For each entity, we examine its functional role in PMS (Sec. PMS Entities) and design representation, i.e., how to mathematically describe it (Sec. Representation of PMS Entities). Each PMS entity plays a distinct role in giving rise to system-level programmability, but it is their directional causal dependencies and the cascading chain as a whole that form the true fabric of PMS. Fig. 1c shows an abstract, physics-independent, conceptual generalization through a graph representation, where we explicitly visualize the directional mechanistic relationship between a pair of PMS entities. This graph representation of PMS also underpins the presentation of our observations and perspectives on design evaluation (Sec. Design Evaluation: How To Evaluate) and design synthesis (Sec. Design Synthesis: How to Design).

PMS entities

Process (manufacturing and fabrication of PMS) influences material properties and limits the form, resolution, and complexity of architectures (Fig. 1c). From a causal perspective, the process serves as the entry point in the PMS design chain: it establishes the physical basis for material performance and exerts downstream influence on both architecture and stimulus. For example, in direct ink writing of liquid crystal elastomers, varying the printing speed can tune anisotropic responses42,43, while also enabling straightforward patterning into lattice-like structures.

Material refers to the type, composition, and spatial distribution of matter used in a PMS. Whereas conventional passive materials are widely used for PMS44–46, recent years have seen increasing attention to PMS designed with stimuli-responsive and even multi-stimuli-responsive materials34. The choice of material and process determines the material’s constitutive behavior—its response to external stimuli—and consequently dictates which stimuli can be used for programmability and control strategies. In essence, the material serves as an interface between the external world and the PMS body, mediating energy exchange that underpins functional switching.

Architecture defines how materials are arranged in space—including size, shape, and topology—and delineates the boundary between the system and its environment. It governs where and how external stimuli act on the PMS, how these actuations are channeled into global behaviors, and how the system interacts with its environment. Note that we intentionally separate architecture and materials in this Perspective. Architecture captures only the geometric and kinematic description of the system, while materials specify the composition, distribution, and properties of matter within a given architecture, thereby governing the system’s physical and constitutive behavior. If materials are like muscles providing actuation, then architecture is like the bones that constrain and redirect this responsiveness into coordinated, collective motion. Supported by advanced manufacturing, architecture permits diverse design flexibility with varying granularity, ranging from simple parametric design with a small set of prescribed variables28,47,48 to fully freeform design49–55; see the left top panel of Fig. 3 for a portrait of the spectrum. Depending on the representational choice and design approaches, architecture influences both the computational complexity of simulation and the feasibility of fabrication, often dictating the resources needed to both design and fabricate a PMS.

Stimulus refers to externally applied physical inputs that activate materials and drive system function in a PMS, such as mechanical forces56, thermal19,57, magnetic28 optical55, electric fields58, and chemicals59. Certain materials also admit multiple forms of stimulus, such as LCEs responsive to both light and heat, or hydrogels with magnetic particle inclusions responsive to swelling and magnetic fields. In most current demonstrations, stimuli are applied in an open-loop manner—that is, as prescribed or pre-programmed inputs—but in advanced designs, they can be implemented in a closed-loop configuration with feedback or self-sensing. For simplicity and tractability, many studies treat stimulus as a non-designable pre-specified entity. We discuss this point further and present our perspective on control co-design in Sec. Perspectives on Design Synthesis.

Environment denotes the collection of largely uncontrollable entities—including external conditions, boundaries, and uncertainties—that affect or interact with a PMS during both fabrication and deployment. For example, during manufacturing, factors such as ambient temperature, pressure, and humidity can introduce variability and imperfections. During operation, terrain and surrounding media define the boundaries of PMS motion, determine resistance and friction, and may introduce uncertainty. Unlike stimulus or process, environment is generally outside the scope of PMS design as it is uncontrollable, unless robust design under uncertainty60,61 is of explicit concern. Yet it plays a critical role in bridging the gap among as-designed, as-fabricated, and as-deployed systems. This role becomes especially important when a PMS is expected to operate outside laboratory settings, where the deployment involves (i) dynamic, unstructured, uncertain, and extreme environments or (ii) mission-critical applications34,62,63.

Representation of PMS entities

Design representation is how entities in PMSs are parameterized or quantitatively described. It abstracts real-world entities, which are possibly complex and effectively infinite-dimensional, into a form amenable to computational modeling and optimization. For example, the architecture of a thread-like soft robot may be approximated as a beam-like structure with a circular cross-section, where the cross-sectional radius and curvature serve as design parameters. To establish a standard design framework, we denote the representations of the core PMS entities as P for manufacturing, A for architecture, M for material, and S for stimulus (Fig. 1c). Design representation can be handcrafted28, biomimetic47, analytic55, learned from data54, or freeform49,52. Importantly, the choice of design representation defines the boundary of the feasible design space, reflecting underlying assumptions about allowable variations of the entities and incorporating considerations such as manufacturing constraints. Across PMS entities, representations vary in variable type (e.g., continuous, categorical, or mixed) and in modes of variation (spatial or temporal). These variables may be constants, spatially varying fields defined over space, time-dependent, or even spatiotemporal. In many PMS applications, a single system is expected to exhibit different behaviors under different stimuli. In such cases, multiple stimulus representations are introduced for different operating conditions, while the representation of the remaining entities is shared across all cases.

Specifically, process is often represented as a real-valued scalar or vector (e.g., printing speed, flow rate, curing temperature), a discretization of continuous curves (e.g., temperature profile), or categorical types such as the type of process method in hybrid manufacturing64,65. For material, the design representation may involve discrete choices among predefined material options47,54,57,66 or continuous assignment of material properties within a prescribed range, such as specifying the volume fractions of constituents in a mixture49–52. Architecture in the PMS is particularly diverse (Fig. 3). For example, solid architectures can be represented using pixels, voxels, implicit fields51,53–55,67, point-cloud49,50,52,68. Kirigami and origami structures are often parameterized by primitive geometry or crease patterns69, while network-like architectures are naturally represented using graphs57. Stimuli are typically represented as scalar or vector quantities47. They may be applied globally to an entire system, specified as component-specific actuation57,70, or modeled as spatially varying fields55,58. Stimuli may also vary over time49,52,58,71. In addition, responsive materials sometimes support multi-stimuli actuation21,57,72,73, whose representation requires a categorical variable57.

Although not considered a designable entity, environment can be endowed with its own representations, such as scalar or vector fields, discrete deployment scenarios, or probability distributions ranging from nominal to stochastic models. These include (i) operating conditions where PMS is deployed (e.g., temperature and pressure) and (ii) terrain uncertainty in locomotion52 that influences how architecture and stimulus interact with their environment. In addition, the environment may be represented implicitly by embedding its effects within other PMS entities rather than as an independent construct. For instance, fabrication imperfections may be represented as intrinsic uncertainty in various forms, such as the process-to-material map, geometric uncertainty in architecture, and loading uncertainty in stimuli. In summary, unlike representations of the other PMS entities, representations of the environment need not take a singular form and rather depend on the chosen scope of modeling and design.

Perspectives on design entity

Entity coupling and design prioritization

As shown in Fig. 1c, there could exist couplings among PMS entities. The process → material relation is central in advanced manufacturing—alignment, curing, heating, or magnetic imprinting directly determine constitutive behavior in LCEs, hydrogels, magnetic elastomers, and shape-memory polymers19,42,43. In the architecture-material coupling, the architecture constrains the placement of material and how materials at different locations interact, while the materials enable local variations within the structure. This interplay underpins multi-material topology optimization. Stimulus and material together determine the local response distribution within a PMS, while the environment acts as a context-dependent modifier on process, material, architecture, and stimulus, and provides boundary conditions. These links clarify how process, material, architecture, stimulus, and environment are coordinated across modeling and design workflows. We note that, despite tight physical coupling, most PMS demonstrations to date tend to address only one or a few links in the modeling or design, leading to a mismatch between coupled physics and decoupled design practice.

The designability and role of each PMS entity vary in practice. Architecture is often the primary design entity due to its explicit geometric role, particularly in metamaterials design, while material is frequently chosen in advance and then fixed. Process is frequently predefined and constant, but its designability has recently gained attention, both for pursuing superior performance via more flexible control of heterogeneous materials distribution and for considering the interplay among material-geometry-process57. Stimulus lies closer to the control layer, which is fully designable in theory yet often constrained in practice by hardware or task requirements. Environment is inherently non-designable but strongly influences system robustness and real-world viability. Ignoring joint dependencies-for example, treating stimulus as independent of material or assuming process has no influence beyond fabrication accuracy-can miss opportunities to exploit the unique functional versatility and robustness PMSs offer. We return to this point and share another closely relevant perspective entitled Individual and Collective Effects of MAS and Their Co-Design in Sec. Perspectives on Design Synthesis, where our co-design formalism (Sec. A Common Design Formalism) enables deeper analysis with concrete examples and synthesized insights drawn from empirical evidence.

Multiphysics design beyond dual coupling

While some PMS studies can be understood through pairwise couplings among process, material, architecture, stimulus, and environment, an increasing number of systems involve interactions among more than two physical domains, such as magneto-mechanical-electrical coupling74. In these systems, programmability emerges from the interplay across multiple fields rather than from a single stimulus-response pathway. One thing to note is that the multiple (more than two) physical domains do not necessarily serve as external stimuli. Instead, the PMS can be designed to harness one external stimulus to actuate/influence the other two (or more) physical fields. For example, magnetic actuation may induce mechanical deformation that subsequently alters electrical responses through geometry-dependent field redistribution. More generally, there are multiple pathways for realizing such multiphysics PMS. One approach is to employ a material that intrinsically couples multiple physical domains, such as magnetoelectric, piezoelectric, or multifunctional composite materials. Alternatively, a PMS may combine multiple materials, each coupling mechanics with a different physical domain, while the architecture mediates interactions among them through mechanical deformation and force transmission. In this latter approach, mechanics serves as a common intermediary linking otherwise distinct physical processes. While the former strategy has received considerable attention, the latter remains comparatively less explored and may offer a flexible route for constructing multifunctional PMS from modular material building blocks. Within Fig. 1c, such systems introduce additional state variables. Incorporating multiphysics interactions qualitatively expands the design space of PMS by introducing additional stimulus channels, control mechanism, and richer functionalities. However, it also increases modeling complexity, fabrication constraints, and uncertainty propagation across physics. Therefore, the decision to include more than two coupled physics domains in PMS design could consider whether the added physics unlock fundamentally new capabilities or robustness that cannot be achieved within lower-dimensional couplings.

Design evaluation: how to evaluate

Design evaluation is a core enabler that bridges design representation and synthesis by providing (1) quantification, which measures how well a design performs, and (2) guidance, which informs further improvements or the selection of promising candidates. With special attention to its use for co-design of PMS, we propose four criteria for design evaluation methods: physical fidelity, computational efficiency, scalability, and differentiability. Viewed through this lens, a spectrum of design evaluation methods in the literature is categorized into physics-based, data-driven and hybrid approaches (Fig. 3), and briefly discussed. Then we share our perspectives on the choice and outlook for co-design of PMS, revolving around rigor, throughput, robustness, and a trade-off thereof.

Physics-based model

A PMS often exhibits complex multiphysics interactions, structural architecture, and diverse environmental stimuli. In addition, PMS functions frequently involve large deformations, contact, and friction. In many applications, these systems also display dynamic behaviors characterized by large displacements and rotations, non-equilibrium transient states and time-varying boundary conditions. This combination of tightly coupled physics, nonlinearity, and dynamics makes the accurate simulation of PMS particularly challenging.

A variety of continuum mechanics models have been developed by integrating theoretical assumptions with experimental insights75–79. They are often integrated into finite element method (FEM) to obtain approximated numerical solutions for complex geometries (Fig. 4h). While these FEMs provide high physical fidelity, they are computationally expensive, often suffer from convergence issues, and are especially sensitive to mesh distortion under conditions involving large deformations, contact, and dynamic loading.

To improve computational efficiency and numerical stability, reduced-order models utilize structural simplifications or reduced basis functions. Slender, thread-like structures are often modeled using one-dimensional elements such as springs57, rods80, beams81–83, constant curvature segments84,85, Cosserat rods86, or active filaments87. They can also be assembled into lattices or networks to approximate the mechanics of soft solids in two or three dimensions81–83,88,89 (Fig. 4c). In specialized architectures such as kirigami69,90 and origami91,92, mechanical response naturally separates into rigid panels and deformable hinges, which can be efficiently modeled with simplified rod or beam elements. Overall, reduced-order models improve numerical stability, scalability, and computational efficiency, though often at the cost of physical fidelity.

Another commonly overlooked aspect is that most existing implementations only focus on forward simulation but neglect inverse gradient computation. That is, they can only evaluate whether a design is good or bad, but cannot provide guidance on how to improve a design. Without access to gradients, design methods built on these models are restricted to brute-force search, or heuristic strategies, which are fundamentally incompatible with gradient-based optimization workflows. To address this, recent efforts have integrated differentiable computation into physical simulation using approaches such as analytical derivation (e.g., implicit differentiation or adjoint methods), algorithmic differentiation, and symbolic differentiation93. In particular, adjoint methods have been used to derive sensitivity information for a PMS in static or quasi-static settings51,94, and automatic differentiation has been applied in solid FEM95–98 and some discrete systems such as lattices57,99–101, kirigami90, and granular matter102. Nevertheless, in highly nonlinear settings that involve friction, evolving contacts, large deformations, multi-phase interactions, transient behaviors, and dynamic motions, these techniques might still face numerical challenges.

An emerging alternative is the Material Point Method (MPM)103. Unlike traditional FEM, which relies on a Lagrangian mesh, MPM represents solids using a set of Lagrangian material points that carry state variables, while spatial derivatives are computed on a fixed Eulerian background mesh to update the particle states. This hybrid approach effectively handles large deformations, collisions, and contact without mesh distortion103. MPM is highly parallelizable and well-suited for GPU acceleration, resulting in significantly improved computational efficiency compared to traditional FEM in complex scenarios104. Recent advances have demonstrated MPM’s effectiveness in simulating a wide range of PMS phenomena across both quasistatic and dynamic regimes49,53,105–107, including multiphase interactions53,107. Moreover, MPM can be easily implemented with automatic differentiation, even in the presence of discontinuous contacts and friction, enabling direct computation of gradients for gradient-based optimization104. However, MPM might inevitably sacrifice some fidelity and might have challenges in handling complex boundaries or constraints.

Data-driven model

The rise of machine learning (ML) in engineering has offered a new modeling paradigm for PMS54,55,108–110. ML approaches can accelerate the modeling and design of PMS beyond what conventional physics-based approaches allow. With sufficient high-quality data, trained ML models could provide efficient and scalable prediction of complex system behaviors within acceptable error margins, which is crucial for iterative design evaluations in optimization that would otherwise be prohibitively costly. In addition to evaluation, ML can also enable representation learning that autonomously distills compact yet expressive representations for PMS entities. The learned representation could reshape the combinatorial, possibly high-dimensional design spaces into more tractable forms, facilitating co-design and knowledge discovery.

The ML models that have been reported in the literature of PMS can be broadly categorized based on the input-output relation. First, parameter-to-observable (P → O) models predict scalar/vector observables (e.g., stiffness, frequency) from low-dimensional design or material parameters, often using multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) or tree-based approaches52,57,67; Second, field-to-observable (F → O) models reduce high-dimensional, spatially structured inputs into key quantities of interest, such as resonant frequency, compliance, or reduced geometric descriptors, typically based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), graph neural networks (GNNs), or ensemble classifiers111–113. Third, field-to-field (F → F) models learn to map input fields to full output fields (e.g., stress, displacement, electromagnetic fields), often realized through architectures like conventional CNN style surrogates or neural operators54,55 (Fig. 4b, i).

Data-driven modeling for the design evaluation of PMS faces several distinctive challenges. First, data acquisition is inherently data-intensive due to the combinatorial growth of the joint design space that includes possibly coupled entities. This requirement typically exceeds the capacity of conventional sampling strategies, such as Design of Experiments114–116 and active learning117–121, which are primarily suited for siloed design. Second, some PMS exhibit complex behaviors—including multiphysics interactions, multistability, hysteresis, and transient responses—all of which could be difficult to reliably capture through purely data-driven methods. Third, modeling PMS entities generally involves heterogeneous modalities (spatial fields, control vectors, categorical processing parameters), which makes it challenging for ML models to jointly represent and fuse these diverse inputs. Developing methods to systematically integrate multiple PMS entities and modalities remains a critical open challenge in ML modeling of PMS.

Data-physics hybrid model

Physics-based models, while rooted in physical principles and rigor, face several challenges when applied to PMS. The first challenge is scalability: most methods rely on spatial and temporal discretization, which imposes substantial demands on memory and computational resources. The second challenge is the sim-to-real gap: the fidelity of physical models depends strongly on the underlying assumptions. In practice, many simulators neglect critical effects or use oversimplified representations (e.g., in constitutive laws, contact, and friction). In some cases, no adequate physical theory exists. The third challenge is differentiability: some physical models are non-smooth or require complex and unstable implementations for gradient calculation, hindering integration into modern optimization workflows.

At the other extreme, fully end-to-end machine learning offers the possibility of bypassing numerical solvers altogether. While computationally efficient at inference, these black-box models often lack robustness and generalizability, and they typically require large datasets that are rarely available for PMS. Consequently, neither physics-based models nor purely data-driven approaches provide a comprehensive solution. A more promising paradigm is physics-data hybrid modeling, which integrates physical laws with machine learning to overcome the limitations of each approach. One representative example is physics-informed machine learning122–124, where training incorporates physical loss terms derived from PDE residuals and boundary conditions. This framework has emerged as a standard strategy for improving physical fidelity and robustness in data-driven modeling. Although these methods show great promise for inverse problems and PDE discovery, their application to forward simulation in PMS remains challenging. In particular, they may still suffer from limited robustness, require extensive hyperparameter tuning, and may not yet match the efficiency and accuracy of advanced finite element (FE) or other established numerical methods. Another way to incorporate physics into machine learning models is to ensemble simulators and machine learning models together. Within this hybrid paradigm for PMS, two primary directions have emerged.

The first is physics-informed residual modeling that focuses on reducing sim-to-real error, where ML models predict only the residual error between simulations and experimental observations125,126. This strategy reduces data requirements, enhances generalization, and improves predictive accuracy. The second approach is hybrid substitution, in which ML surrogates replace subcomponents of physical models that are computationally expensive, non-differentiable, or poorly understood. For example, a neural network can be trained on experimental data to learn the constitutive behavior of programmable materials under different processing conditions, and embedded into a physics-based simulation model57 (Fig. 4a). In another example, a neural network is trained to predict multi-step state updates in material point method (MPM) simulations, allowing for larger time steps and substantially reducing both storage demands and computational costs127.

Taken together, these physics-data hybrid approaches combine the rigor of physical principles with the flexibility of machine learning. By balancing physical fidelity with computational efficiency, they open a promising path toward scalable, expressive, and optimizable models for PMS co-design. Nevertheless, finite element simulation remains the dominant approach in practice, as hybrid models are still in their infancy and require further improvements in robustness, efficiency, and reliability.

Perspectives on design evaluation

Differentiable simulation

The ultimate goal of design evaluation is not simply to quantify performance, but also to guide the optimization of design variables. This usually requires computing gradients that indicate how parameter changes influence system-level behaviors. However, most existing evaluation approaches focus only on forward simulation but do not implement the gradient computation. Our perspective is that it is critical to take one step further to develop differentiable physics or simulators that provide gradients alongside forward simulation. Achieving this requires that every component of the simulation is differentiable and establishing a systematic way to trace gradients consistently across both space and time. Encouragingly, pioneering efforts in this direction have begun to emerge across multiple fields and are already enabling highly flexible design frameworks.

Combining different evaluation models

We have remarked above that both purely data-driven and purely physics-based strategies, either alone, are often ineffective in modeling PMS in practice. While purely data-driven strategies demand extensive high-quality datasets and struggle to extrapolate, purely physics-based approaches often incur prohibitive cost. In practice, PMS modeling benefits from combining these different models together, allowing explicit balancing of physical fidelity, efficiency, and scalability.

Uncertainty quantification and sim-to-real transfer

Most design evaluation methods for PMS have centered on deterministic scenarios with no uncertainty. In reality, a PMS is subject to multiple sources of aleatoric uncertainty (i.e., inherent randomness irreducible by data or knowledge) across entities: variability due to fabrication and manufacturing such as constitutive relation in the process-material map57, imperfect curing, chemical composition, geometric imperfections54, uncertainty in stimuli and environment, such as terrain uncertainty52. In addition, epistemic uncertainty that reflects a lack of knowledge always arises when making predictions over unseen instances with finite data. Neglecting these variabilities risks inaccurate design evaluations and significant sim-to-real gaps54,57,63. We believe that all three types of evaluation paradigms can be leveraged to address these challenges. Physics-based models, when combined with differentiability, provide an efficient means to quantify how uncertainty propagates through the system and influences performance, and can also be used for inverse identification and statistical characterization of stochastic parameters. Data-physics hybrid models can exploit data-driven components to correct residual errors in physics-based models. Data-driven models enable rapid inference with acceptable error, facilitating large-scale sampling and exploration in design under uncertainty60,61. Another under-explored yet promising direction is multifidelity modeling whose training and inference of the surrogate synergistically blend low-fidelity computational data and high-fidelity experimental data.

Generalization in ML models of PMS

Out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization of data-driven models, particularly in zero- or few-shot settings, cannot be guaranteed in the absence of inductive bias (e.g., symmetries, conservation laws, causal priors), and is provably impossible without incorporating either domain knowledge (e.g., governing PDEs) or explicit assumptions about the relation between training and test domains128–131. This underscores the theoretical necessity of hybrid approaches introduced in Sec. Data-Physics Hybrid Model. Co-modeling, unlike siloed design workflows, operates over a high-dimensional, tightly coupled joint space defined by architecture, material, and stimulus. Generalization here in part demands adaptability to contextual shifts, e.g., shift of stimuli or environment beyond the predefined modeling regime. Yet, most PMS studies still focus on in-distribution performance, neglecting transferability across tasks or deployment settings in PMS. To enable practical co-design with embodied intelligence at scale, it is warranted to build models that are modular, dynamically extensible, and capable of leveraging physical inductive biases encoded in the design evaluation of PMS.

Material availability and experimental evaluation

A practical prerequisite for computational co-design of PMS is access to material systems exhibiting useful and reproducible stimuli. This remains a major barrier for many stimuli-responsive PMS, where magnetic composites, liquid crystal elastomers, hydrogels, and electroactive materials often require in-house synthesis or specialized fabrication, with responses that can be sensitive to processing conditions, batch-to-batch variability, and tacit experimental know-how. On the other hand, mechanically actuated, pneumatic, and origami-inspired systems often rely on more accessible constituents including elastomers, printed polymers, paper, plastics, or assembled components, which can lower the entry barrier for reproduction and benchmarking132–134. From a design-evaluation perspective, these issues directly affect sim-to-real transfer: discrepancies between predicted and measured PMS behavior may arise not only from imperfect models, but also from material variability, fabrication defects, and incomplete characterization of both stimulus and system response.

Design synthesis: how to design

Design synthesis is the task of identifying configurations for all the design entities so that they meet desired performance goals. A grand challenge of the design synthesis lies in navigation of the joint space spanned by different PMS entities, where directional causal relationships among them are represented as a graph (Fig. 1c). This challenge becomes even more pronounced when a single system is expected to support multiple functions, each associated with a distinct stimulus and objectives. To date, design practice in PMS has often defaulted to heuristic or siloed design strategies, where one or more design variables, especially stimulus or materials, are fixed a priori to reduce complexity. The cost is a significant loss in design flexibility and missed opportunities to utilize couplings across PMS entities for better performance and multifunctionality. In addition, design synthesis of PMS hosts inherent trade-offs across multiple functions such that improving one often degrades another. Although some recent works49,55,67,90 have begun to address this trade-off, it remains under-explored, whereas attempts to distill a unified design framework have been warranted34.

Built on the two prior sections on design entity and evaluation, herein we introduce the formalism of co-design (Sec. PMS Design via Multi-Objective Co-Design), categorize PMS based on the formalism (Sec. PMS Classification based on the Formalism), review existing solution methods in the computational design (Sec. Solution Procedures of PMS Co-Design), and share our perspectives and outlook (Sec. Perspectives on Design Synthesis). We start by formalizing the design problem as a multi-objective co-design problem. Therein, performance metrics for different functions define the multiple objectives, and architecture (A), material (M), and stimulus (S) are jointly optimized to achieve a stimulus-conditioned system response, potentially subject to constraints from process (P) and environment (E).

PMS design via multi-objective co-design

A common design formalism

We propose viewing PMS design as a multi-objective co-design problem, where each objective is associated with a separate function we want to achieve through a specific stimulus. This multi-objective optimization problem includes two groups of design variables: (i) system-level variables Θ ≔ {A, M}, which are intrinsic to the material system and shared across all functions; and (ii) stimulus variables Si, which represent external control inputs and are optimized specifically to each function indexed with i. Each entity displayed in Fig. 1c can be represented as scalars, vectors or fields defined over space X and time t (Sec. Representation of PMS Entities).

In general, design evaluation of PMS (Sec. Design Evaluation: How To Evaluate) entails a computational graph depicted under Design Evaluation in Fig. 3: a given system design {A, M, Si} → state variable ui → quantities of interest (QoIs) Qi(u)→ performance metric Ji(Qi), for the i-th function we aim to achieve. Specifically, for each function Fi, the state variable field ui(X, t) is obtained by solving the governing equation ui(X,t)=G(Θ,Si∣E), where E denotes inherent noise or uncertainty, such as stochastic material-property variations and environmental disturbances. We define a QoI over the solved states for a PMS function of interest Fi as Qi=Q(ui) (e.g., tip deflection or maximum curvature from a displacement field, bandgap width from a band structure diagram). The QoI is subsequently used to define a performance metric Ji(Qi), which measures either the deviation from a target QoI Qi* (e.g., a mean-squared error for shape matching), or the degree of extremal performance without a reference value of QoI, such as maximizing the bandgap width. PMS classification based on the formalism

The proposed formalism provides a new way to categorize prior works based on the QoIs of co-design rather than material type or applications. This classification is introduced to clarify what is being evaluated and optimized in a given PMS design problem. By separating shape, motion, function through reconfiguration, and function without reconfiguration, the four types make explicit the modeling assumptions and evaluation metrics needed to compare prior demonstrations and identify transferable design strategies. Type I. Quasi-static reconfiguration

This type is the most common case in the literature, where the stimulus is constant and the system transitions quasi-statically to its target state under equilibrium. A typical goal in this case is to match the entire actuated shape (or selected critical points of the system) to prescribed targets under different stimuli, using a discrepancy measure between the predicted and target configurations, where the design objective usually takes the form of loss functional Ji=L(Xi,Xi*) given a target state Xi*. In the simplest case, when the two fields share the same set of signature points, point-wise errors can be used over those shared signature points. However, in general scenarios, the fields may be represented or discretized differently, and no common set of signature points may exist. Typical representations include analytical functions, meshes, level-set descriptions, or point clouds obtained from experiments or simulations. In these cases, various point-cloud-based, field-based, or distribution-based metrics have been proposed, such as the Hausdorff distance and the Earth Mover’s Distance48,62. For gradient-based design (see Sec. Gradient-Based Design), differentiable similarity measures are preferred; non-differentiable metrics require differentiable relaxations or approximations. For extreme behaviors (e.g., maximizing deformation) the QoI is defined on selected features of the actuated configuration, for example, the tip displacement, deflection distance, or curvature at points of interest. A common choice for the performance metric used as the optimization objective is to maximize or minimize that quantity. Such objective formulations have been widely used across different actuation mechanisms, including mechanical56, magnetic28,49,51,69, light57, thermal19,48,54, and pneumatic135. Visual illustration can be found in Fig. 4a–c.

Type II. Dynamic reconfiguration

Similar to Type I, this category also focuses on system shape, but the stimuli are now dynamically modulated over time. The state variable is thus a time-varying displacement field, and the QoI is a function of the resulting motion history. Performance metrics for some common functionalities are given below. For shape-morphing designs, the QoI is the time-dependent trajectory of the actuated configuration at selected time steps and points of interest. The goal is to match a desired target trajectory. Similar to Type I, we measure the discrepancy using a similarity measure between the predicted and target trajectories. When point-to-point correspondence along the trajectory is known, a natural choice is a vector norm between the two trajectories at corresponding times. In cases where only curve-level similarity is required, the Fréchet distance provides an alternative measure for comparing continuous trajectories62. For locomotion design, we can view the QoI as an extension of the extreme-performance objective in Type I, now evaluated over a time horizon. The goal is typically to achieve extreme movement—e.g., maximizing displacement—within a specified period. For example, we can maximize the forward traveling distance of a designated point (or average over a set of points) at the end of a given time window. The rate of adaptation can also serve as a key performance metric62. Demonstrations of Type II systems in the literature span a diverse range of stimuli, including magnetic49 (Fig. 4h), pneumatic18,53,136, electric67 (Fig. 4d), and mechanical actuation68. Some presented a computational framework that remains agnostic to the actuation mechanism52. While current demonstrations under Type II are centered on quasi-static or open-loop settings usually without any uncertainty taken into account, they naturally motivate a broader design perspective of control co-design, where material architecture, actuation, sensing, and feedback policies are jointly considered. We claim this presents a pathway toward robotic material systems with embodied intelligence, and share our perspective in Sec. Perspectives on Design Synthesis (Control Co-Design: Towards Robotic Material Systems with Embodied Intelligence).

Type III. Function modulation via reconfiguration

In this category, reconfiguration in architecture is not the direct design objective itself but a means to modulate another physical response. Such design is often motivated to overcome the performance limit of geometrically stationary systems via introducing reconfiguration, represented by active metamaterials37–41. Specifically, Type III involves a cascaded extension of the design evaluation pipeline introduced in Sec. A Common Design Formalism: a given system design {A, M, Si} → reconfigured state xi → secondary physical field (e.g., optical, acoustic, or thermal) associated with the reconfigured state u~i(xi)→ QoI Qi(u~i)→ performance metric Ji(Qi). Therefore, unlike Type I, where the QoI is some extracted quantities of geometry or displacement, here the QoI depends on this secondary field. The performance metric follows a form similar to Type I, but is now evaluated on the induced physical response u~i rather than directly on xi itself. Representative demonstrations include mechanically tunable optical lenses136, skin color change through mechanically actuated photonic structures137, adaptive waveguiding in wave-based metamaterials70,90 (Fig. 4g), and wave-based mechanical computing138. Beyond these, reported examples span magnetically or mechanically tunable frequency responses139,140, electrochemically reconfigurable acoustic bandgaps59, optical camouflage via electrothermal actuation141, bistable mechanical metamaterials with rewritable memory45, and optically addressable bandgap control142.

Type IV. Function modulation without reconfiguration

This type is similar to Type III in that the goal is to modulate functional performance; however, here, modulation alters its behavior through changes in stimuli or material properties without geometric reconfiguration. Examples include systems utilizing phase-change materials, electro-optic effects, or stimuli-induced mechanical-property tuning, targeting various physical response fields ui (e.g., optical, electrical, electromagnetic). In this case, performance metrics adopt a similar form to Type III, but the quantity of interest is evaluated on the fixed geometry rather than on the deformed configuration. We observe that this category features a rich set of demonstrations in wave propagation, where geometrically stationary systems with tunable material properties more readily enable multiple wave functions that would otherwise be strongly correlated or mutually conflicting. Examples include electrically-driven modulation of wave propagation, either through digital control143,144 or with phase-change material145, thermally driven optical and polarization control146,147, optically driven programmable energy focusing55 (Fig. 4i), and thermally driven multifunctional electromechanical responses148, as well as broadband microwave absorption through material-property modulation149.

Solution procedures of PMS co-design

Design synthesis in PMS requires exploration of a high-dimensional space jointly spanned by architecture, material, processing, and stimulus. Stimulus-function pairing across multiple states in tandem with geometric design further complicates this search. Co-design of PMS is therefore essential to address the mechanistic couplings of PMS entities (Fig. 1c), scale, and handle trade-offs across functions. The majority of existing work falls into two broad categories: gradient-based approaches (Sec. Gradient-Based Design) and gradient-free approaches (Sec. Gradient-Free Design).

Gradient-based design

Analytic gradient-based optimization

These methods update design variables via analytically derived gradients typically using adjoint sensitivity analysis or projection-based formulations. They offer strong scalability, accuracy, and robustness when physical models are well posed, and are particularly effective for high-dimensional PDE constrained design68,69. Topology optimization (TO) is a prominent subclass, enabling both shape and topological changes. While historically focused on canonical physics and benchmarks, recent efforts have extended TO to nonlinear, multiphysics, and fabrication-constrained PMS problems49–53,94,150 (Fig. 4h). For a comprehensive review of recent TO studies for programmable materials and structures with nonlinear behaviors, readers are referred to151.

Differentiable simulation-based optimization

In Sec. Physics-Based Model, we introduce differentiable simulation frameworks that re-implement physics PDE solvers in backends compatible with automatic differentiation (AD), allowing gradients of simulation outputs to be computed directly with respect to design representations. For example, a hybrid data–physics differentiable framework integrates neural network-based process property models with a differentiable spring simulation for multi-stimuli morphing networks57 (Fig. 4a); differentiable material point methods have been used to support topology optimization of soft robot locomotion (Fig. 4e); a differentiable simulation for design of nonlinear dynamic kirigami90 (Fig. 4g). These approaches support GPU-based parallelization and simulation-in-the-loop co-design under dynamic, nonlinear, and multiscale conditions, making them well-suited for PMS. However, design with differentiable simulation remains computationally expensive, often requiring custom implementation and careful numerical stabilization.

Surrogate-based optimization with ML models

Originally conceived for ML model training through gradient descent, AD in the context of design extends beyond model training: it enables end-to-end gradient-based optimization with respect to any combinations of design variables and design objectives. The applicability ranges from parameter-to-QoI mappings learned by multilayer perceptrons to field-to-field models such as residual CNNs for voxelized morphing54 or neural operators in scientific machine learning55. Nonetheless, several caveats remain: AD can be memory intensive, may encounter numerical instabilities in deep or stiff computational graphs, and depends on the differentiability of all modules, which often demands careful supervision in fabrication-aware or discrete design settings. Moreover, the resulting gradients without supervision are purely numerical and statistical in nature, in contrast to the derivatives analytically derived from closed-form physical models, which may limit their use in optimization that is sensitive to quality of gradients and beyond the training data regime of the ML model.

Gradient-free design

Gradient-free methods find optimal solutions through sampling, ranking, and statistical modeling without explicit gradient information. Canonical methods include genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, Bayesian optimization, and reinforcement learning. For example, Bayesian optimization has been applied to tune vibration-attenuating metamaterial beams via in situ mechanical adjustment of internal resonator parameters based on a trained surrogate model152. Their flexibility makes them attractive, especially under fabrication constraints, discrete actuation patterns, or coupled entities. Yet they often suffer from low sample efficiency and limited scalability in high-dimensional spaces. Meta-heuristic optimization uses heuristics, population dynamics, or stochastic sampling to globally search for optimal solutions. For example, evolutionary algorithms have been used for inverse design of active plates54, and magnetically controlled shape morphing and locomotion (Fig. 4c). A genetic algorithm was implemented in the shape-morphing inverse design of thermally actuated shape memory polymers153. In general, these approaches are well-suited for discrete, multi-modal, or noisy problems, and can handle objectives through black-box modeling. However, they often require a large number of design evaluations and therefore might not be suited for computationally expensive models or high-dimensional design spaces.

A roadmap for the co-design of PMS

Remarks on existing solution procedures of PMS design

We observe that gradient-based approaches are increasingly adopted and developed in the literature of PMS, utilizing traditional adjoint-based methods, automatic differentiation in ML platforms (e.g., PyTorch and JAX), and differentiable simulators (e.g., NVIDIA Warp154, DiffTaichi100, GradSim155, Dojo156, PhiFlow157). In contrast, gradient-free design methods such as metaheuristic optimization (e.g., genetic algorithm) and surrogate-based optimization (e.g., Bayesian optimization) appear less frequently in the PMS literature, despite the promise62 and their widespread use in broader data-driven materials design108,158,159. This is likely due to their weaker scalability with input dimension, slower convergence, and lack of optimality guarantees. Nevertheless, this does not negate the potential use and development of gradient-free design for PMS, particularly when coping with costly, noisy simulations or physical experiment in a small-data regime. Therein probabilistic surrogates such as Gaussian processes160 provide an additional advantage in active learning, where predictions with quantification of epistemic uncertainty can enable efficient, goal-aware data acquisition in the early stages of PMS design.

In a broader view, both gradient-based and gradient-free categories are grouped together under iterative design. The underlying assumption is that finding the design that supports desired programmability is iterative as evolution in nature62. A rising alternative that challenges the notion yet has been little explored in the PMS literature is iteration-free conditional inference, supported by some classes of ML models such as conditional deep generative modeling. Herein, the model either directly learns an inverse map from design goals as conditions to design representations or learns a manifold of the joint space of them. Upon proper training and validation, such models enable on-the-fly inference with acceptable errors and capture non-analytic design constraints and user preferences, bypassing the repeated evaluations with random initialization that are inevitable in iterative design. Moreover, by sampling from a learned conditional distribution, such models can naturally address one-to-many solutions in PMS design. While this paradigm may sound compelling, challenges in practice remain in securing optimality guarantee, design constraint satisfaction, physical fidelity in the inference161, and in-distribution and out-of-distribution generalization. We argue that these issues are likely beyond off-the-shelf implementation of standard ML models, thus method development tailored to the context of PMS is highly desirable.

Individual and collective effects of MAS and their co-design

Materials, architecture, and stimuli together determine the behaviors and performance of PMS, yet their individual and combined effects remain poorly understood. Co-design of these factors has only recently emerged49–51,53,67,90,99,106,150,162,163, driven in part by advances in simulation capabilities. These studies can be regarded as special cases of the general co-design formalism proposed in this Perspective. Although preliminary, they highlight the potential benefits of co-design and begin to elucidate the distinct roles of materials, architecture, and stimuli in shaping PMS behavior and performance. For example, recent research performs ablation tests on magnetic soft robots49,162 to demonstrate that stimuli provide global control, modulating system behavior uniformly, while material distribution enables local control, producing spatially heterogeneous responses. Therefore, for tasks without spatially varying requirements, stimuli-only approaches are often sufficient, though materials-only or co-design strategies can achieve comparable outcomes. When localized control is required, global stimulus change alone is inadequate, and either materials-only or co-design approaches are necessary. For systems with multiple objectives, co-design offers clear advantages by jointly leveraging materials, architecture, and stimuli and balances trade-offs more effectively. This point was investigated through underwater locomotion67 and light-by-light programmable photonic metasurfaces55, both of which explore Pareto-optimal solutions under conflicting objectives, where “best” becomes conditional on preference rather than a single optimum. In such solutions, materials and stimuli are coupled and mutually adjusted rather than simply superimposed, resulting in improved performance. While these conclusions are drawn from a few reports centered on optimization, similar principles are also observed in nature: simple movements, like basic limb extensions, rely primarily on global activation, whereas complex tasks, such as flight maneuvers164 or intricate gripping86,165, require both spatial distribution and strategic activation of muscles. For multifunctional tasks, octopus arms166 and elephant trunks167 combine neural control with heterogeneous musculature for dexterity, and human hands coordinate global muscle activation with locally specialized muscles for complex manipulation168,169.

We note that in some cases, the contributions of stimuli and materials may be difficult to separate, particularly when localized control can be partially achieved through stimuli alone, so the conclusions above may not apply to all systems. In general, however, co-design outperforms single-mode strategies by encompassing both stimuli-only and materials-only design spaces. We emphasize that this advantage depends on having an optimization solver or design strategy capable of effectively navigating the expanded design space. In practice, the resulting optimization problem is often highly nonconvex and sensitive to the initial guess as discussed in recent studies99,163. As a result, co-design does not necessarily outperform heuristic design methods when optimization converges to poor local optima, highlighting the potential need for global optimization techniques and other strategies for robust exploration of the design space.

From on-off to multimodal programmability

On-off PMS, where a stimulus simply switches a function between inactive (without stimulus) and active (with stimulus), surpass conventional material platforms in adaptivity and multifunctionality. Unimodal PMS go further by enabling control within a single class of functions, where a modality denotes a distinct class of system function typically associated with particular QoIs. The motivating ideal of PMS has been multimodal programmability, in which a single system can host multiple distinct modalities under different stimuli. Pursuing multimodal programmability may confront trade-offs across different types of functions, underscoring both the challenges and opportunities that co-design of PMS bears49,55,67. A representative function where multimodality is sought is shape morphing, where the PMS supports multiple modes of targeted deformations, such as bending and twisting in elephant trunks47 and two-way contractible kirigami designs69. Multi-stimuli-responsive materials10,57,72 offer an avenue to achieve multimodal programmability in concern. Other reports in wave propagation include the reprogrammable dynamic metamaterials switching between energy focusing and protection90 (Fig. 4g) and the magneto-mechanical metamaterials supporting both diffusing and directing of elastic waves through selective actuation of local magnetic field70 (Fig. 2f). Despite compelling demonstrations, we believe that multimodal programmability remains under-explored, and that co-design provides a pathway to explore Pareto-efficient solutions and possibly reshapes trade-offs among distinct modalities.

Design optimization in multi-physics co-design

When PMS incorporates interactions among more than two coupled physics domains, the associated co-design problem becomes more complex rather than merely higher dimensional. The state variable generally consists of multiple interdependent fields (e.g., displacement, electric potential, magnetic flux density), each governed by distinct yet strongly coupled governing equations. These interactions often introduce nonlinearity and bidirectional feedback, which can significantly increase complexity and nonconvexity in the design space. However, performance targets and programmability can be greatly expanded. From a solution procedure perspective, effective co-design in these systems may require modular and robust solvers, hierarchical and well-defined formulations, and selective reduced-order modeling or surrogate modeling of computationally intensive sub-physics while making reasonable assumptions. The design decisions discussed in Sec. Perspectives on Design Entity on which physics domains to include directly determine the dimensionality, tractability, and numerical stability of the design problem. Thus, multi-physics co-design should balance physical completeness with computational solvability.

Co-design with a process-structure-property-performance link

In pursuing embodied intelligence with PMS, the literature reveals the process-geometry divide, where the coupling of process and geometry dictates system performances yet is often studied either in silo or sequentially. This siloed design strategy leads to the designs whose performances are not only suboptimal but also brittle under off-design conditions (e.g., fabrication imperfections, actuation mismatch). We claim that bridging process design and geometric design through co-design could offer an avenue for PMS with embodied intelligence. As the process lies at the lowest level of the PMS hierarchy (Fig. 1c), its effects cascade downstream to architecture and system behavior, often leading to a huge performance discrepancy between as-designed and as-fabricated systems63. However, this integration presents three critical challenges: first, the forward map from processing parameters to material is unknown and is therefore typically regressed with experimental data57; second, uncertainty must be quantified and propagated across scales—from microstructural variability in processing to macroscopic system behavior—demanding robust probabilistic frameworks; third, the high dimensionality and tight coupling of variables across entities raise concerns of tractability, especially for optimization or control. We believe that by leveraging analytic models, machine learning surrogates on experimental data, and domain expertise, this approach supports both pre-runtime design synthesis and runtime adaptation, thereby expanding the reachable design-performance space without losing tractability.

Performance metrics and benchmarks

We remark that the co-design formalism presented in Secs. A Common Design Formalism and PMS Classification based on the Formalism is centered on the function-specific performance metrics that quantify how well a PMS serves the primary purpose (e.g., the error to a target deformed state in a shape-morphing PMS). In the literature, other metrics associated with different attributes have been actively discussed, including energy efficiency, maximum curvature, repeatability, and adaptation rate, particularly in the context of shape morphing and locomotion (Types I and II)62,170,171. Both the primary functions of a PMS and their associated attributes generally exhibit trade-offs and correlations, whose design synthesis forms a multi-objective optimization problem. The proposed formalism extends to accommodate these additional attributes upon trivial modifications, provided that the additional performance metrics are function-specific as well. In implementing our formalism, it is essential to examine correlations among different function-specific metrics, which is an analysis enabled when the design pipeline retains access to the full solution field u; relying on a regressed performance metric J limits such insight only to statistical association at the surface level, whereas access to the full solution field u reveals the underlying structural relationships encoded in the analytic functional J.

Despite a large volume of demonstrations reported in the PMS literature, little attention has been given to establishing standardized benchmarks170. This is an essential step for enabling fair comparison, reproducibility, and continual progress in algorithmic development for PMS research. Attempts to standardize performance metrics to facilitate comparisons can be found, exemplified by the surface complexity metric in shape morphing along with reference shape-morphing targets endowed with a trigonometric parameterization170. We remark that such combined task-metric benchmarks, rather than either alone, are particularly valuable for algorithmic development, as they impose consistent difficulty levels and clarify what constitutes improvement.

Control co-design (CCD): towards robotic material systems with embodied intelligence

Control is essential in PMS because uncertainties from environmental variability and model inaccuracies to a degree are likely inevitable, and robustness is required to ensure reliable behaviors88. However, PMS often exhibits highly nonlinear and environment-dependent dynamics, demanding more sophisticated control policies than conventional linear control strategies, such as proportional-integral-derivative controllers, which are insufficient for capturing their complex behaviors62. Modern control methods provide systematic tools, ranging from classical feedback and optimal control to advanced architectures such as model predictive control172 and reinforcement learning173–175. They have been widely applied in soft robots and other PMS to maintain stability and desired performance under uncertainty176–179.

Stimuli design in PMS can be viewed as a form of control design, defining how the system should respond and adapt under varying environmental or operational conditions55,180. Therefore, control co-design (CCD)181–184, originally developed to optimize physical and control systems concurrently rather than sequentially, provides a mature and well-established framework for PMS design. Its efficacy is also observed in nature: animals and biological systems exemplify CCD through the inseparable coupling of morphology and control. By jointly considering the five PMS entities, PMS can achieve adaptive behaviors that mirror biological intelligence, responding to uncertain environments and varying tasks49,52,57,67.

CCD encompasses three main frameworks that differ in how physical and control design interact. In the sequential design, the physical system is fixed first, followed by the control design, which often leads to suboptimal performance. The simultaneous formulation solves both design problems together in a unified optimization framework, directly capturing trade-offs between material, architecture, and stimuli variables. The nested formulation separates the optimization into two levels: an outer loop for physical design and an inner loop for control design, allowing specialized methods such as robust, optimal, or reinforcement-learning-based control to be integrated. CCD has proven transformative across disciplines including wind energy, robotics, and vehicle dynamics181,183,185, and its concepts can be readily migrated to programmable material systems to co-optimize structure, material, and actuation toward adaptive and resilient performance.

While CCD provides a powerful paradigm for optimizing PMS to achieve better solutions, it introduces significant challenges that complicate both modeling and implementation. First, dynamic simulation and modeling become highly nonlinear, spatial-temporal problems90,186,187, since performance depends not only on the initial and final configurations but also on intermediate states and their derivatives, such as velocities and accelerations. Second, core control issues such as controllability, observability188, and stability189 remain difficult in the PMS setting, where sensor and actuator placement as well as system nonlinearities strongly affect achievable performance. Feasibility is another critical issue, as actuators are always subject to limits on the maximum actions they can provide190. In some cases, the inherent dynamics of the physical system may render certain desired behaviors unattainable, leaving no feasible control solutions191. Third, scalability poses a major hurdle: CCD problems for PMS are likely to be high-dimensional due to the combinatorial space jointly formed by PMS entities and often involve multi-input multi-output controlled systems, making them computationally intensive to solve. Despite these challenges, continued advances in modeling, differentiable simulation, learning-enabled control, and scalable optimization offer promising pathways to make CCD a powerful tool for realizing adaptive and resilient PMS.

Bioinspired vs. optimization-driven representation

Programmability is a long-demonstrated concept in nature: plants and animals adapt seamlessly to their environments through an inseparable interplay of structure, material composition, and actuation. For example, pinecones open and close in response to humidity, while muscle-tendon systems couple compliant materials with active control to enable versatile locomotion. Designs inspired by biological systems often highlight functions with built-in compliance, reconfigurability, tunability, and energy efficiency. Muscular hydrostats are an exemplar kind, such as elephant trunks47, starfish tube feet192 or octopus arms66. Additional demonstrations include snowflake-like geometric motifs for bandgap tuning140 and drug delivery systems for cancer therapy using natural biomacromolecules193. Such bioinspired designs provide compelling avenues for concept generation and warm-start in PMS design194,195, but their reports have generally emphasized proof-of-concept demonstration, though scalability and repeatability remain challenges196,197. By contrast, optimization-based approaches can be viewed as “evolution on a chip,” using computational algorithms to explore and evolve effective designs guided by quantifiable performance metrics and constraints. Illustrative examples include pneumatic robots for locomotion53, multi-stimuli liquid crystal elastomers for mechanical computing57, and frameworks that combine machine learning with evolutionary algorithms54. Optimization can extend beyond bioinspired designs, enabling faster exploration, larger design spaces, and targeted behaviors. However, it also faces challenges such as local minima and high computational cost. In this context, bioinspiration can serve as a lens to focus on promising designs or to define a more meaningful design space, while optimization drives further refinement and performance beyond what intuition alone can provide.

Discovering fit-for-purpose architectural motifs: beyond the co-design given a motif

In engineering science, leaps in performance often come from the invention of a novel, fit-for-purpose geometric concept—a group of design instances sharing either geometric characteristics or a (handcrafted) representation. Easy-to-relate, conventional examples in engineering include the shape of aircraft wings, the I-beam cross-section in steel construction, and the layout of bridges. In the corpus of PMS, some recent perspectives41,187 offer insights on how the mechanics of a chosen design representation dictates its functional behavior, thus, in reverse, on what geometric families are preferred in designing a PMS of interest. Today, concept creation still relies heavily on handcrafting, trial-and-error, and serendipity, resulting in a bottleneck that is pervasive, often unnoticed, and seldom addressed. A natural frontier is to ask whether AI can aid in discovering both novel (geometry-wise) and fit-for-purpose (function-wise) geometric concepts themselves, rather than just doing the in-distribution acceleration given them. Advances in generative modeling198–200, representation learning130,201, and reinforcement learning202,203 point toward such possibilities. Ultimately, this outlook suggests a dynamic interplay between domain knowledge and AI-driven discovery, where geometric concepts become not just inputs to the design evaluation chain, but outputs of an integrated discovery pipeline.

Conclusion

While autonomy in artificial systems is currently dominated by chip-based or computer-based computation, we believe that creating PMS—the material versions of computer programs—that embed function, logic, and responsiveness directly into matter, offers a complementary and potentially more scalable and energy-efficient path to intelligent behavior. Recent advances in responsive materials and manufacturing technologies have made this vision increasingly feasible. In this perspective, we identify five tightly interconnected entities that govern the performance of programmable material systems (PMS): architecture (A), material (M), external stimulus (S), processing conditions (P), and environment (E). We observe that most existing design work on PMS focuses on only one or a subset of these entities and often neglects their coupling, which we conceptualized as siloed design. This is in sharp contrast to biological systems, where body, behavior, and environment co-evolve in a fully integrated manner.

In unlocking the potential of PMS, we claim that a comprehensive and unified co-design framework is needed to jointly consider all five entities. In doing so, we propose to view PMS through the lens of computational design and unify different PMS design tasks as multi-objective optimization in a shared joint design space. The formalism provides a shared language for consistent description, comparison, and transfer of insights across different PMS platforms. Despite recent progress, current PMS designs remain limited in complexity, scalability, and adaptability. Significant sim-to-real gaps persist, and our understanding of the underlying physical interactions is still incomplete. At the same time, several well-established toolboxes, including topology optimization, control co-design, biological evolution, and biomechanics, are highly relevant but remain under-utilized in the PMS context. Emerging tools such as differentiable physics engines and machine learning models are increasingly bringing in new momentum for efficient search, evaluation, and evolution of PMS designs in silico. Overall, PMS design research is still in its early stages. Bringing this promise to fruition will require highly interdisciplinary collaboration among engineers, physicists, biologists, materials scientists, chemists, and computer scientists.
