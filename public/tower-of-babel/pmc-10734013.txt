Effect of the Cross-Section of a Porous Burner on the Combustion Stability Limit of Premixed Oxy-Methane Flames
Authors: Mingjian Liao, Zhu He, Xiong Liang, Yawei Li, Xuecheng Xu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents the idea of using a porous media burner to improve the oxy-methane combustion reaction rate and broaden the stability limit. Numerical studies on the premixed combustion of CH4/O2/CO2 in a two-layer porous medium burner using a two-dimensional symmetrical volume-average model with the skeleton mechanism based on OpenFOAM. The combustion characteristics of burners with variable cross-sectional (VC) and straight cylindrical (SC) structures were compared, including stable range, temperature field, thermal cycle efficiency, and CO emissions. It is confirmed that the cross-sectional structure is effective for broadening the stable range, and the broadening rate is more than 4 times. As more heat is lost from the outlet due to the increased cross-section, the solid-phase temperature of VC is lower than that of SC. As a result, the flame temperature of VC will be about 200 K lower than that of SC under the condition of insulated walls. It also leads to a reduction of about 2% in thermal cycle efficiency compared with SC. Meanwhile, it is shown that the VC structure slightly increases CO emissions at low thermal power but is smaller than that of SC at high power. When the wall heat loss of the burner is considered, the VC structure is still effective for improving the stable range. In addition, the VC structure slightly affects flame tilt and temperature uniformity at the burner outlet when the thermal power is large. It is confirmed that the VC structure is effective for increasing the power adjustment range and reducing pollutant emissions at high power.

Introduction

Climate change, triggered by global warming, is one of the major environmental problems that the world is facing currently. The main reason for global warming is the continuous emissions of high concentration CO2 from industrial activities, especially fossil fuel combustion, into the atmosphere.1,2 Global efforts to limit global warming to 1.5 °C are required to avoid the extreme climate crisis. This goal can only be achieved through net zero global greenhouse gas emissions by the mid-21st century. CO2 capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technology is a rare option for the large-scale use of fossil fuels in a low-carbon way, which will play a part in the journey toward carbon neutrality.3 In the conventional combustion process, the CO2 fraction in the product gas is low (for example, 3–5% by burning natural gas), resulting in a large amount of gas being separated. Therefore, the energy consumption of the CO2 separation process is enormous. Generally, the process of CO2 separation after combustion will reduce the heat transformation efficiency of the original energy power system by 8–13%.4 It has been reported that combining oxy-fuel combustion with CCUS can significantly reduce the cost of the CO2 separation process. One of the characteristics of the technology principle is the utilization of gas recirculation, which replaces N2 in combustion-supporting air with CO2 in waste gas. Due to the continuous recycling of exhaust gas and the N2 removal at the initial stage, carbon dioxide is continuously enriched in the circulating gas. After reaching a stable combustion state, the volume fraction of CO2 in the combustion flue gas accounts for 80–90% (after condensation). High-purity CO2 can be obtained after compression and liquefaction, which greatly simplifies and facilitates the subsequent transportation and storage processes. Nevertheless, in the process of oxy-fuel combustion, the gas species in the furnace are mainly CO2 and H2O. Compared with N2, the three-atom gases CO2 and H2O have the characteristics of higher specific heat capacity and thermal radiation, which will lead to problems such as ignition delay, low flame temperature, and slow propagation speed.5−7 Finally, the combustion stability is worse than that of conventional combustion systems.

Recently, porous medium combustion (PMC) has been considered one of the solutions to the above problems. As an internally self-organizing heat recovery process, combustion in porous media is different from a homogeneous flame. Due to the existence of the porous matrix, the gaseous fuels are separated on the interrelated pore surfaces for combustion, presenting discrete small flames instead of the traditional free-burning flame front. Part of the heat released by the chemical reaction in the combustion zone will be transferred to the solid matrix by the convection heat transfer process. The heat transferred into the solid matrix is further transferred upstream and downstream of the burner in the form of radiation and heat conduction. Then, recently, porous medium combustion has been considered one of the solutions to the above problems. As a result, the fuel will be more easily ignited as more enthalpy enters the burner with it. Furthermore, the flame temperature and intensity are improved, and “super enthalpy” combustion is realized, which is beneficial to increase the chemical reaction rate and flame propagation. In addition, as the combustion area has been widened, the residence time of gas in the combustion area has been extended so that the sufficiency of combustion is improved. Therefore, the CO emission of porous medium combustion is lower than that of conventional combustion.8,9

Nevertheless, the advantages of PMC can be fully realized when the flame is immersed in a solid matrix. Therefore, stabilizing the flame in the burner is one of the challenges in the application of this technology. Two-layer porous media burners are characterized by two layers of porous media with different properties (porosity and pore size) arranged sequentially. The idea is based on the difference in flame propagation speed in various porous media materials. The burner with this structure will stabilize the flame in a specific area under some operating parameters.10,11 The stable range is defined as the difference between the burner flashback and blowout velocity limits and represents the power adjustment range of the burner, which is a critical indicator for two-layer porous burners. Therefore, how to broaden the stable range is one of the challenges for burner applicability.

Due to the complex structure of porous media materials, especially ceramic foams, it is difficult to model them directly. Recently, direct modeling methods such as SEM (scanning electron microscope) or computational reconstruction methods have been developed, which are of great help to the study of porous media flow and combustion processes at the pore scale.12 For example, the characteristics of flame incline and hot spot can be found in the numerical simulation and provide convenience for the study of their mechanisms.13−15 However, the calculation cost required by this method is unacceptable. Since the focus is on the overall heat transfer of the burner system, the widely used volume-averaging method is more appropriate in this work. The idea of this method is to add an additional energy equation to solve the solid matrix temperature, which is called the two-temperature model. The volumetric convective heat transfer, which is proposed to measure the convective heat transfer between the gas phase and the solid matrix, is used to couple the energy equations. Radiative heat transfer in porous media is simplified into heat flux and diffusion models. In the last century, the empirical formula for volume convection and radiant heat transfer coefficients has been obtained by many researchers through experimental methods such as an alumina pellet-packed bed and foam ceramics. Based on this model, the effects of variables such as porous media material parameters and operating parameters on combustion performance are demonstrated. Quite few researchers have explored the method for widening the stable range in the two-layer porous medium burner. Wang et al.16 studied the flame morphology of low calorific value fuel combustion in porous medium burners, including flame morphology, propagation speed, and temperature. They believed that increasing the temperature of the inlet gas could improve the flame stability, such as by reducing the flame tilt, reducing the flame propagation speed, and increasing the combustion temperature. Sobhani et al.17 proposed a method to broaden the range of stable combustion (especially the blowing-out limit) by changing the porosity and pore diameter of the downstream material of the burner to gradual and comparing it with the sudden change type. The conclusion points out that the blowout limit has increased by more than 50%. The reason for this phenomenon is that the thermal cycle efficiency of gradual material is higher (the extinction coefficient is reduced). This characteristic promotes the unburned gas to be preheated more efficiently, thereby supporting a higher gas flow rate. Voss et al.18 proposed a burner with a gradual diameter to reduce the flow rate so that the flame can be immersed in the burner within a large inlet velocity range. Furthermore, Hashemi et al.19 numerically studied the flame stability of burners with similar structures. As a burner geometrical parameter study, the effect of outlet diameter and the length of the first porous layer on the flame stability limit were examined. The conclusion suggests that an optimal ratio of outlet to inlet diameter causes the maximum flame stability limits.

Our previous related research revealed the feasibility of the combination of oxy-fuel and porous media combustion technology and also pointed out the disadvantage of its narrow, stable combustion range.20 In subsequent work, an attempt was made to broaden the stable combustion range by optimizing the properties of the two-layer porous media material or the operating parameters. However, we believe that this degree of improvement is not sufficient for the general application of the burner. Therefore, the current research aims to further broaden the stable combustion range by changing and improving the burner topology and to study whether these changes have a significant impact on other combustion characteristics such as temperature uniformity and CO emissions. The numerical model based on OpenFoam coupled with OpenSMOKE++ was established and was used to describe oxy-methane thermally and chemically in the burner under adiabatic and nonadiabatic conditions. The equivalent ratio is 0.6–0.9, and the oxidant fraction XO2 = 21–37% vol. In addition, flame temperature, morphology, and CO emissions are considered indicators to study the effectiveness of the burner structures for improving combustion characteristics.

The velocity inlet and pressure outlet are adopted as boundary conditions at the burner inlet and outlet, respectively. The wedge mesh (wedge angle 2°) and wedge boundary conditions (z-direction) are adopted for the 2D axisymmetric model.

The maximum Reynolds number reported in all cases does not exceed 100. Therefore, the laminar combustion model is adopted.28 The code was ported to laminarPimpleSMOKE, based on OpenSMOKE++, for simulating laminar reactions with the methane skeletal kinetic mechanism (24 species, 155 reactions).29 The thermophysical properties and diffusion characteristics of all species are calculated by the CHEMKIN database, which is based on the mixture-average approach. The maximum temperature and flame front position are monitored as a reference for reaching a steady state.

Results and Discussion

In order to investigate the combustion characteristic within PMC, the stable range, flame temperature, and CO emission are studied as a function of the equivalence ratio φ = 0.6–0.9 and oxidant of 21–37%. Before that, the stable zone and stable range need to be defined. The variable cross-section of the VC burner (60 mm < x < 70 mm) is indicated as the stable area (Figure 2). This region is also defined as a stable range in SC, although the cross-section is not expanded. If the flame front is located in this area when it reaches the steady state, it is considered a stable combustion state. The minimum stable inlet velocity (Vmin) represents that the flame front is just located at the interface between the preheating zone and the combustion zone. If the inlet velocity (Vin) is lower than Vmin, the flame will propagate upstream, leading to flashback. Similarly, the maximum stable inlet velocity (Vmax) stabilizes the flame front in the stable zone at x = 70 mm. If the velocity is higher than Vmax, then the flame will propagate downstream to the outlet, leading to a blowout. Furthermore, the stable range (SR) is defined as SR = Vmax – Vmin. In this work, the outlet diameter of the VC is one of the variables. According to the three-dimensional structure, the outlet area increases 1.5 times and 2 times (the corresponding diameters are 61.2 and 70.7 mm). In the presentation of Figure 2, the variable cross-section burner with an outlet area of 1.5 times is named VC1.5D. Similarly, the burner with an outlet area of 2× is named VC2D. In the following discussion, it will be divided into adiabatic and nonadiabatic working conditions of the buner wall.

Diagram of the stable zone.

Adiabatic Conditions

Figure 3 shows the variation of the stable range with the equivalence ratio φ (O2 = 21% vol) and the O2 fraction (φ = 0.6 and φ = 0.8), while the burner wall is adiabatic. Clearly, with the increase of outlet diameter, Vmax increases significantly (50–120% increase), while Vmin changes slightly. Compared with the results of SC, one of the characteristics of VC1.5D and VC2D is that the gas flow velocity in the stable zone will gradually decrease with an increasing outlet area. Consequently, a larger inlet velocity is required to achieve the blow-out condition, obtaining a larger Vmax value. Specifically, the Vmax and SR corresponding to VC2D are about 0.5–1.5 times and 1.6–4.8 times larger than those of SC. In addition, it is predicted that the structure change of the outlet will have little impact on the heat transfer process in the preheating zone. Consequently, it can be seen from the results that Vmin is only reduced by 1 cm/s under VC2D conditions compared with that of SC. The increase in combustion area volume leads to a decrease in gas velocity. As a result, the convection heat transfer between the gas and solid matrix in the combustion zone is weakened. For example, under the conditions of φ = 0.6, O2 = 33% vol, and Vin = 25 cm/s, the volumetric heat transfer coefficient of SC is about 75,000 W/(m3 K) in the combustion zone, while that of VC2D is 50,000 W/(m3 K). However, it should be noted that the latter’s volume has nearly doubled. Therefore, as the volume of the combustion zone increases more significantly, the thermal resistance of the combustion zone decreases correspondingly. Hence, the heat exchange efficiency between the gas and solid matrix increases. At the same time, due to the increase in the outlet area of the burner, more heat in the solid phase at the outlet is transferred to the environment in the form of radiation, leading to a decrease of the solid phase temperature. Specifically, the gas and solid temperatures at the SC outlet are 1500 and 1340 K, while those of VC2D are only 1350 and 1220 K under the conditions of φ = 0.6 and Vin = 25 cm/s. Currently, the heat loss of VC2D at the outlet is 499 W, which is 28.4% of the thermal power, while that of SC is only 20.9%. The results show that the peak temperature of the solid matrix of the latter is about 80 K lower than that of SC. Since the solid matrix acts as a means of recirculating the heat from the hot combustion products to the incoming air-fuel mixture, the solid temperature plays an essential role in the thermal cycle efficiency. The decrease in solid temperature leads to less heat entering the preheating zone from the combustion zone through the thermal cycle to preheat the unburned gas. Finally, the combustion temperature corresponding to Vmin decreases, which slows down the propagation speed of the flame. Therefore, the Vmin value corresponding to the flame limit will be reduced. It can be seen that the thermal cycle efficiency of SC is 11–17%, and it decreases to 10–15% when the outlet diameter is 2D. Another remarkable result is that the cycle efficiency will suddenly increase with the increase of inlet velocity and then gradually decrease. The physical phenomenon corresponding to the maximum value is that the flame front enters the combustion zone from the preheating zone. As the extinction coefficient of Al2O3 pellets is higher than that of ceramic foams, the corresponding radiation heat transfer resistance is higher than that of later. Since radiation heat transfer dominates the heat transfer of the solid matrix, the cycle efficiency in the preheating zone is lower than that in the combustion zone when the flame front is located in the preheating zone. This phenomenon also reveals that the use of ceramic foams as the solid matrix is more conducive to improving the flame temperature. On the other hand, the cycle efficiency will decrease with the increase of Vin when the flame front moves to the combustion zone. Although the heat release from chemical reactions in the combustion zone can be more easily transferred in the solid matrix in the form of radiation, the convective heat transfer property is weakened. Therefore, when the flame is just at the intersectional interface, the heat of the combustion zone is transferred to the preheating zone by radiation, where the unburned gas is then preheated by the solid matrix with a small pore size, thus achieving maximum thermal cycling efficiency. Conversely, the thermal cycling efficiency is reduced when the flame front is far from the interface. As a result of this approximate adiabatic combustion characteristic, the peak temperature of the burner will be higher when Vin is low due to its higher heat recovery efficiency. For example, when Vmin = 22 cm/s, the corresponding peak temperature is about 80 K higher than 100 cm/s (Vmax,VC2D).

Figure 5 shows that the peak temperature of the burners varies with the operating parameters and outlet diameter. It can be found that the combustion process in the stable combustion zone is nearly adiabatic due to the adiabatic condition of the wall, so the difference in peak temperature in each burner is not significant. Similarly, the heat loss at the outlet is one of the reasons why the peak temperature decreases with an increase in outlet diameter. The increase in outlet diameter leads to a decrease of gas flow velocity in the combustion zone, which increases the residence time of combustion products in the burner. Therefore, a larger outlet diameter is beneficial to reduce CO emissions, while the peak temperature changes slightly. Specifically, Figure 6 shows the CO emission results at the outlet under the same working conditions. Since Figure 5 reveals that the peak temperature of the burner under this condition is between 1300 and 2000 K, which is suitable for CO oxidation rather than CO2 decomposition. Therefore, prolonging the residence time will reduce the emission of pollutants. Obviously, compared with SC, the CO emission at VC1.5D and VC2D diameters decreases by about 7–66%. Some operating conditions with combustion temperatures exceeding 2000 K (φ = 0.8, XO2 = 29–33% vol) are also recorded. The high temperature promotes the decomposition of CO2, and the residence time decreases with the inlet velocity and flame temperature. As a result, the level of CO emissions under these conditions is mostly above 1000 ppm. However, the increased cross-sectional area of the combustion zone is still effective for reducing CO emissions. For example, under the condition of φ = 0.8 and O2 = 37% vol, the CO emissions of SC, VC1.5D, and VC2D are 4570, 1588, and 1573 ppm, respectively.

Nonadiabatic Conditions

From the results under adiabatic conditions, it can be seen that the structure of VC is extremely beneficial to improving the combustion characteristics, especially in broadening the stable range. However, in some applications, the heat loss of the burner wall may be considerable and available. As the chemical reaction process is generated in the interconnected and complex pores of porous media, the high-temperature gas in the center of the burner will flow through the burner wall through these microchannels. At the same time, the porous medium burner has excellent thermal radiation capacity because of the high emissivity of the solid matrix. Therefore, compared with the burner without a porous medium, one of the combustion characteristics of PMC is that the temperature of the burner wall will be higher. This feature is fully applied to occasions requiring heating by thermal radiation, such as dryers and thermal photoelectric systems. However, the consequence of this heat utilization is that the heat of chemical reactions is more easily lost to the environment through convection and radiation. Yang et al.30 believed that the wall heat loss effect was significant, especially when the diameter of the burner was small. Specifically, there may be no flame near the wall, resulting in the occurrence of fuel leakage. In addition, the leaked fuel can be reignited downstream in a multistage flame state. Meanwhile, in our previous study,31 it was pointed out that the influence of the heat loss of the burner wall on the stable range was significant. Specifically, it will lead to disastrous narrowing, exceeding 50% of the stable range. Although the variable-section structure shows excellent performance under adiabatic conditions, it also increases the wall area of the burner. When wall heat loss is considered, the increased wall area will inevitably cause greater heat loss, thus posing a new challenge to the stability of the flame. Therefore, the current work is to quantitatively study the effectiveness of VC in widening the stable range after considering the wall heat loss.

Based on the model, the burner side wall is set as the boundary condition, combining convection and radiation heat transfer. The burner shell is made of stainless steel, and the convection heat transfer coefficient is set to 5 W/(m2 K). According to the temperature dependence and the temperature field of the burner, the emissivity of the preheating zone and the combustion zone is 0.2 and 0.4, respectively. The numerical results show that premixed gas is challenging to ignite and maintain stable combustion when the equivalence ratio is lower than 0.8. The heat loss aggravates the inclination and rupture of the flame front near the wall, leading to the occurrence of flame extinction. Figure 8 shows that the stable range varies with the O2 fraction (φ = 0.8) and outlet diameter under nonadiabatic conditions. It can be seen that the structure of VC is still very effective in improving the stable range. Compared with SC, the stable range of VC2D is increased by more than 4 times. Another remarkable feature is that the Vmin and Vmax are reduced by 10–28% compared with Figure 3c. The corresponding stable range is narrowed by about 18%. The reason may be that the wall heat loss causes the flame temperature to decrease and slows the flame propagation speed. Another reason may be that the decrease in the wall temperature aggravates the inclination of the flame front and leads to combustion instability. It reveals that the proportion of heat loss is quite significant when the thermal power of the burner is low. For example, the ratio of wall heat loss is 36.4% at 544 W (Vmin,VC2D, and XO2 = 21% vol). The hleff will gradually decrease with the increase of power, and it can be predicted that the heat loss will remain around 11% when the power exceeds 10 kW. For VC2D, the velocity value is only the flashback limit, and the flame front is located in the preheating zone. As previously mentioned, higher thermal cycle efficiency in the combustion zone leads to a higher flame temperature. As a result, the increase in wall temperature promotes more heat loss. Specifically, the corresponding flame peak temperatures of SC and VC2D at 780 W are 1722 and 1613 K, respectively.

In addition, the heat loss on the boundary will inevitably reduce temperature and fluid velocity near the wall, thus increasing the nonuniformity at the burner outlet. It can be predicted that the velocity and temperature nonuniformity of the burner will be more prominent when the thermal power is low. In the results, it was found that the tilt of the flame front is suppressed at high thermal powers. Therefore, some low-power conditions are selected for comparison; specifically, the O2 fraction of SC is 21% vol, while VC2D is 25% vol. The flame morphology, RMS, and SD of the outlet temperature are shown in Figure 10. The –OH fraction is used to mark the position of the flame front. After taking into account the heat loss from the wall, it is explicit that the SD at the burner outlet is about 10 times higher than when the adiabatic conditions were applied. However, the relative standard deviation (RSD) is within 5%, and that of VC2D is slightly higher than SC. It can be inferred that if the burner length is further increased, the structure of the VC will not lead to a significant decrease in temperature uniformity. It should be noted that there are some differences in the flame morphology. For SC, the low temperature near the wall slows the flame propagation speed, and the flame front inclines downstream. However, for VC2D, the flame front will tilt downstream when it just leaves the interface. Due to the gradual increase of sectional area, the x-velocity near the wall slows down, and the flame front inclines upstream and increases with the SD of the outlet temperature. When the flame front leaves the stable zone (x = 7 cm), the temperature uniformity is improved as the SD decreases.

We are concerned that heat loss from the wall may slow the oxidation rate of CO or cause the fuel to not ignite. However, these phenomena have not been detected with the current burner size and operating parameters. From the results mentioned above, it is explained that changes in the cross-sectional area of the burner do not significantly affect the temperature uniformity. Therefore, the resulting nonuniformity of CO emissions is not substantial. The main factors affecting CO emissions are still the overall combustion temperature and residence time of the burner. Figure 6 shows that CO emissions increase rapidly when φ > 0.8 or XO2 > 29% vol. The reason is that the thermal power is large under these operating parameters, the residence time decreases quickly as the inlet velocity increases, and its flame temperature will exceed 2000 K. The decomposition of CO2 is accelerated under such temperature conditions. However, after the wall heat loss is considered, the gas temperature behind the flame front is gradually reduced more quickly. The axis temperature and CO generation of burner VC2D under adiabatic and nonadiabatic conditions are shown in Figure 11, and the working conditions are φ = 0.8, XO2 = 33% vol, and Vin = 40 cm/s. In the combustion zone, the gas temperature is higher than that of the solid matrix. Thus, the heat is transferred to the solid matrix through convection heat transfer, and the gas temperature gradually decreases with the x-coordinate. Under the nonadiabatic condition, the solid matrix temperature is lower than that under the adiabatic condition due to the heat loss of the wall, so the gas temperature drops faster. Specifically, for nonadiabatic conditions, the gas temperature is reduced to 2000 K at x = 6.5 cm, while at x = 8.3 cm for adiabatic conditions. Since the gas temperature can be reduced more quickly, the decomposition of CO2 is suppressed. Specifically, in nonadiabatic conditions, CO emissions at the outlet are 7 ppm, compared to 282 ppm under adiabatic conditions. Therefore, under high thermal power conditions, wall heat loss is beneficial to CO emission reduction.

Conclusions

Two-dimensional numerical simulations of two-layer porous media burners have been performed in order to investigate the influence of cross-sectional structures on heat transfer and flame stability. The skeleton chemical reaction mechanism was employed for adequate heat release representation of premixed combustion of oxy-methane at an equivalent ratio φ = 0.6–0.9 and an oxidizer ratio O2 = 21–37% vol. The burners with straight cylindrical and variable cross-sectional structures have been studied under adiabatic wall and nonadiabatic conditions to verify the effectiveness of broadening the stable range. The conclusions of the work are as follows:

(1)The burner structure with the variable cross-sectional area will significantly improve the flame stable range. Compared with the SC burner, the VC structure will broaden the stable range by more than 4 times. At the blow-out limit, the flame temperature of the VC burner is reduced by about 200 K. Meanwhile, its thermal cycling efficiency is slightly lower than that of SC. Under nonadiabatic conditions, the wall heat loss at the blow-out limit is smaller than that of the SC, so the burner can operate at higher thermal power. Although the cycle efficiency of VC is lower than that of SC, the difference in flame peak temperature is not significant.

(2)Under adiabatic conditions, the CO emissions of the burner are extremely low at low thermal power. However, when the thermal power is large (φ > 0.8 or O2 > 29% vol), the combustion temperature will exceed 2000 K. At this time, CO emissions of VC structure are slightly lower than those from SC structure due to the extended gas residence time and lower combustion temperatures.
