Liver Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS): Roadmap for training, credentialing, and implementation
Authors: Jose D. Debes, Yuko Kono, Amir Gougol, Michael Dolinger, W. Ray Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) offers unique, real-time advantages for assessing liver disease, benefiting both health care providers and patients. It has been shown to enhance patient care and satisfaction across a variety of medical specialties. Although liver POCUS is practiced widely in many parts of the world, its use in the United States remains limited. Liver POCUS enables rapid assessment of specific hepatic conditions, including both vascular and parenchymal abnormalities, and can play a key role in timely management decision-making—such as guiding the use of diuretics or volume expanders. Once liver POCUS is established, ultrasonography-enabled procedures, such as procedure guidance and shear wave elastography, may be adopted and enrich the practice of hepatology in the future. This paper serves as a summary of the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases' (AASLD) current position on liver POCUS, offering proposed imaging protocols, recommendations for training and credentialing, and practical guidelines for implementation in clinical settings.

INTRODUCTION

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is defined as the assessment and interpretation of ultrasonographic imaging performed by a clinician at the patient’s bedside with immediate clinical integration, rather than by a radiology technician in consultation with a radiologist, who has no direct involvement in patient care decision-making.1 POCUS offers several advantages when compared to standard abdominal ultrasonography, although it is not designed to replace it. Liver POCUS should be seen as a complementary examination to the clinic visit, rather than a competing approach to liver ultrasound (US). Importantly, POCUS helps provide immediate answers to clinical questions and allows clinicians to direct a procedure, all from the patient’s bedside. Its goal is to essentially broaden the scope of physical examination.

Overall, studies in clinical medicine have shown the ability of POCUS to confirm a clinical diagnosis in 50% of cases, and to change an initial diagnosis in ~25% of cases.12 Moreover, in an observational prospective study among family physicians, the use of POCUS altered primary diagnosis in 49% of cases, enhanced confidence in diagnosis in 85% of cases, and changed management in 50% of cases.3 Liver POCUS can provide immediate information, including the presence of ascites, steatosis, liver nodularity, enlarged spleen, PVT, and intravascular volume status. At an advanced level, potential applications of liver POCUS include evaluation of the hepatic vasculature following liver transplantation, use of elastography, as well as guiding during paracentesis or liver biopsy, making the procedures more accurate and safer. In many parts of the world, hepatologists perform surveillance ultrasonography for HCC. At the present time, we do not envision POCUS being used for surveillance purposes in the US in the near future.

The goal of this document is to establish the basis for adopting liver POCUS by hepatology practitioners in the United States, including its indications, applications, and requirements for training and qualifications. Specifically, this paper will guide the use of POCUS applied to the evaluation of the liver, including a structured approach to examination, the recommended qualifications to perform POCUS in hepatology practice, documentation and billing, and practical implementation.

OUTPATIENT USE OF LIVER POCUS

When applied to liver diseases, POCUS has numerous outpatient and inpatient uses. For outpatient use, assessment should be differentially implemented based on whether it is a first-time evaluation or a follow-up for a particular concern.

Chronic liver disease and cirrhosis

An important advantage of POCUS performed by the hepatologist is to have an overall appreciation of the liver’s sonographic characteristics in the context of the clinical presentation. The overall contour of the organ and surface nodularity provide important clues toward a diagnosis of chronic liver disease and cirrhosis. The shape and size of the right, left, and caudate lobes are difficult to quantify, but represent a useful sense that can develop with experience.

Surface nodularity can be a hallmark of cirrhosis. In a study investigating the diagnostic accuracy of handheld US, the sensitivity of surface nodularity in detecting F4 fibrosis may be as high as 87% in patients with concurrent histologic evaluation.4 However, in general, radiographic detection of surface nodularity is neither highly sensitive nor specific for the diagnosis of cirrhosis.5 Obviously, the morphology of the liver in patients with cirrhosis varies greatly, and changes in the early stage F4 with micronodular cirrhosis may be too subtle to be visible on imaging, including US or CT.6 It is important to point out, however, that the usefulness of a diagnostic test may be modified by pretest probabilities. Surface nodularity and other relatively subtle morphological changes in the liver, when interpreted in conjunction with other clinical data by the clinician conducting the POCUS examination real-time, may be more informative than US conducted by radiology personnel in isolation.

In today’s practice, an assessment for chronic liver disease and cirrhosis would not be complete without elastography. Recent advances in US technology have enabled the integration of shear wave elastography (SWE) into many devices, including some portable systems. SWE provides a noninvasive, quantitative assessment of liver stiffness, similar to transient elastography, offering a valuable tool for estimating the degree of liver fibrosis and identifying patients with cirrhosis. As more hepatologists gain experience with POCUS, specific training and qualification may be defined to incorporate SWE into their practice, enhancing their ability to conduct comprehensive, real-time assessment of the liver.

Portal hypertension and splenomegaly

Many features of portal hypertension can be evaluated during liver POCUS. Presence of ascites, whether for initial evaluation or for therapy monitoring following initiation of diuretics, can be easily addressed, with studies showing a 95.8% sensitivity for ascites detection with portable US.7 Therapy monitoring during ascites treatment is indeed an important aspect that would facilitate medical management, optimizing diuretic doses by avoiding overdiuresis and kidney injury, improving patient satisfaction, and safety.

Spleen size has shown a direct correlation with portal hypertension. Some studies suggest that spleen size in combination with platelet count can help determine the presence of esophageal varices, providing valuable information about the urgency in performing endoscopy for the prevention of variceal bleeding.8 Handheld US devices have been shown to have excellent agreement with conventional US to determine the presence of splenomegaly.9

While US is often the first imaging modality to measure spleen size, accurate measurement can be challenging and is influenced by factors such as the patient’s body mass index, depth of spleen from the skin, visibility of the splenic hilum, and transducer positioning.10 Studies comparing US with cross-sectional imaging showed the agreement between US and CT as moderate, with US commonly underestimating splenic size.1112 Thus, measurement of splenic size by POCUS conducted by a clinician familiar with the overall clinical picture can enhance the diagnostic value of the examination, leading to better decision-making.

Portal vein thrombosis

Identification of the portal vein with Doppler can help identify a normal vascular flow compared to no flow (ie, thrombosis of the portal vein). Although a higher degree of expertise is needed to thoroughly interrogate portal vein patency, assessment via POCUS can help evaluate normal versus abnormal flow patterns, prompting further examination.13 Additionally, in experienced hands, repeat assessment following anticoagulation therapy initiation in those with full thrombosis can help define therapeutic success during clinic visits.

Assessment of elevated liver enzymes

POCUS can aid in defining the presence of hepatic steatosis as well as monitoring disease progression. Liver steatosis is often gauged by an increase in the echogenicity (brighter in B-mode) of liver tissue compared to the kidney. Moreover, US can help in estimating the severity of steatosis: grade 0: no steatosis; grade 1: (mild) increased echogenicity with visualization of the diaphragm and portal vein wall; grade 2: (moderate) increase liver echogenicity with impaired visualization of the diaphragm and portal vein wall; and grade 3: (severe) increased echogenicity with inability to visualize the diaphragm and the portal vein wall.1415 POCUS has been used to train inexperienced providers in the assessment of liver steatosis with a specificity of 99% and a reasonable interobserver agreement for the severity of steatosis (kappa 0.79).16

The presence of cirrhosis and/or PVT as described above may be useful in evaluating abnormal liver function tests as well. Assessment for gallstones or common bile duct abnormalities may also provide important clues in the evaluation and management of elevated liver function tests.17 The former is characterized by echogenic shadows inside the gallbladder that, when of cholesterol origin (as the most frequent stones are), are evident under US. Identification of common bile duct stones, dilation or mass may be more difficult to detect, except by an experienced POCUS operator.1819

INPATIENT USES OF LIVER POCUS

In an inpatient setting, similar to outpatient settings, liver POCUS can be particularly useful for identifying the presence and amount of ascites, assessing portal vein patency, and detecting intrahepatic and extrahepatic biliary dilatation. Moreover, liver POCUS can be conveniently performed at the bedside as part of daily evaluation, including assessment of intravascular volume via examination of the IVC. In general, a normal IVC size is considered to be <2.0 cm, with a collapsibility of over 50%. An IVC size >2.0 cm with minimal collapsibility (<50%) suggests elevated right atrial pressure and/or a fluid-overloaded status.20 In patients with hepatorenal syndrome-acute kidney injury (HRS-AKI), assessing IVC size and collapsibility can help guide volume expansion. A recent manuscript provides a detailed guide on fluid management with albumin in cirrhosis based on IVC evaluation.21 For further discussion of liver POCUS in inpatient hepatology, please see Premkumar et al.22

LIVER POCUS TO GUIDE PROCEDURES

Liver POCUS is valuable for guiding procedures such as paracentesis and liver biopsy, helping to prevent complications, visualize key structures, and accurately guide needle placement. US can be used at the bedside before procedures like paracentesis or liver biopsy, as well as afterward if there is any concern for complications such as bleeding. A large randomized, controlled trial demonstrated that US-guided marking of the biopsy site significantly reduced both major complications requiring hospitalization and minor complications like pain compared to standard percussion-palpation–guided biopsies (0.5% vs. 2.2% for major complications, p<0.05).23 Other studies have shown that US-guided biopsies result in significantly less pain24 and are more cost-effective than standard percussion-palpation–guided biopsies.2526

OTHER USES OF LIVER POCUS

The benefits of using liver POCUS in clinical settings can be largely expanded depending on the operator’s expertise and training. These include evaluation of hepatic artery thrombosis, hepatic vein stenosis, and potential identification of biliary strictures, which are critical aspects of post-transplant evaluation. Additionally, tools like shear wave elastography for liver stiffness measurement and fat quantification may become increasingly important for medication management, particularly among advanced POCUS users. However, these advanced applications require a higher level of training that should be pursued before clinical implementation. They also necessitate US devices equipped with appropriate software, as standard handheld US machines currently may not support these features.

PROPOSED LIVER POCUS PRACTICE

We recommend dividing liver POCUS into 3 categories: a standard approach (minimum required skill level for a performing operator to be credentialed), a limited approach (using 1 or 2 planes only for a specific question) and an expanded approach (obtained through higher training and expertise, which is not detailed in this paper).

TRAINING AND CREDENTIALING IN POCUS

The American Medical Association approved and established a national policy in 2000 (reaffirmed in 2010), which states that any physician with appropriate training can bill and be reimbursed for a POCUS performed (2). This was subsequently accepted by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (3).27 Although the use of a Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code does not formally require a physician to be credentialed by their institution, local medical bylaws may limit equipment access without this, and thus, we recommend formal training to obtain credentialing for clinical implementation.

POCUS is an evolving skill set that requires persistent and frequent performance of examinations to achieve proficiency. However, for initial accreditation, minimum competency requirements to perform and interpret POCUS should be clearly defined. Currently, there are no established guidelines on credential requirements for liver POCUS, whereas it is better established in emergency department (ED) and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) care. For example, in the case of ED, pathways for training, credentialing, and continuous training for more than 2 decades.27 According to 2009 guidelines for credentialing, ED physicians should perform 25 to 50 POCUS examinations for each organ (such as gallbladder and liver) under supervision and pass a competency assessment test to finalize credentialing. Studies exploring the optimum number of examinations required to reach competency in performing POCUS are, however, inconsistent.28 In general, studies found the learning curve in proficiency of POCUS plateaus at 19–90 examinations, varying based on the application.2930 Specific to gallbladder POCUS, the sensitivity and specificity of image interpretation of the gallbladder reach proficiency at around 25–50 cases.30

DEVICES

US technology is rapidly evolving, providing a wide range of devices for liver POCUS. The currently available devices range from portable (handheld) to conventional, stand-alone equipment. Many of the latter devices are equipped with shear wave elastography, increasingly used in liver care. The cost of US devices varies significantly, ranging between $2000 USD and above $100,000 USD. There is a paucity of data comparing the accuracy and feasibility of different devices for liver POCUS; however, a recent manuscript offers some insights on handheld devices.31 At the present time, we refrain from making recommendations on any specific type of device. Selection of the device may be based on the affordability and potential utility in the local setting.

DOCUMENTATION, CODING, AND BILLING

Documentation

The results of a liver POCUS procedure should be documented in a dedicated note that describes the findings in detail and allows for retrieval of information needed for care, billing, or audit. We propose the following elements be included in the report: patient identifier, operator, date, technique, equipment, indication, examination type, description of findings, impression, and time spent on the procedure. For the practical application, a templated report with a primarily binomial description of findings (yes/no) with space for additional details is recommended. An appropriate diagnosis based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code should be included in the note, so to be associated with the CPT codes. Applicable codes for hepatology include, but are not limited to: Cirrhosis (K74.60), Elevated liver function tests (74.80), and Steatosis (K76). It is highly recommended that images from the exam are incorporated into the report to facilitate standardization of billing. Images and/or cine loops should be saved and accessible for 5 years in case of auditing by payers. For billing purposes, image storage on the ultrasonography equipment is insufficient, as machine upgrades and storage limitations prevent this from being easily maintained. We recommend using a cloud-based image storage system used by most health facilities, for example, the Picture Archiving and Communication System from radiology, or others that may be used by departments utilizing POCUS already, such as the ED or intensive care unit. Images stored in the Picture Archiving and Communication System that are also viewable by radiology have the additional benefit of multidisciplinary collaboration with other specialties (ie, radiology and transplant).

Coding

Until a specific CPT code for liver POCUS is available, we recommend using the CPT code for a limited abdominal US (76705) and, potentially, limited Doppler US of the abdomen (93976) if the liver POCUS was used to address a specific pathology (ie, PVT). A limited US of the abdomen only requires an examination of the specific anatomic structures or area focused on for a diagnostic question, not of all abdominal organs.32

Billing

There are a few different potential components to reimbursement, including a technical fee, a professional fee, and a facility fee (only applicable in hospital-based clinics). The technical fee includes the cost of equipment use and labor for performing the examination. The professional fee for a limited abdominal US includes the cost of “physician interpretation of diagnostic tests.” Estimates from gastroenterology clinics billing for limited abdominal US earn a global professional and technical fee of ~0.80 revenue value units. In hepatology visits where POCUS results in an extension of the clinic visit duration, it is advisable to utilize CPT code −25 to account for prolonged service. In nonhospital clinics, reimbursement for each liver POCUS procedure is approximately $140 USD, and in hospital-based clinics, ~$800 USD per POCUS visit.27 One cannot receive 2 facility fees for a routine visit and a POCUS visit on their own patient, but they can receive the additional revenue value units related to performing the POCUS exam in addition to their routine clinic revenue value units. However, if POCUS is performed by a separate physician on the same day as the routine clinic visit for another provider, the clinic may collect reimbursement for 2 separate facility fees: the clinic and the POCUS visit.

IMPLEMENTATION OF POCUS

Engaging key stakeholders and performing a needs assessment is essential at the beginning of the implementation process to introduce POCUS into the liver clinic. Key stakeholders in this process may include administrative/business directors, nursing directors, medical assistant leads, information technology (IT) staff and leaders of other relevant departments, such as radiology and surgery. The role of POCUS differs in different care settings, and its implementation should be individualized based on the specific clinical needs and projected demand for the procedure. Discussing previous experiences with specialties performing POCUS in the same facility (ie, rheumatology, ED, etc) can also be of great benefit. A proactive partnership with radiology departments is likely to be fruitful for the short-term and long-term success of liver POCUS. At our respective institutions, radiology departments have not been concerned about liver POCUS being a threat to their productivity.

IT Interface

Once a provider or practice decides on a particular POCUS device, it is critical to consult with the relevant IT department early on in the process (Figure 3). IT will be able to work together with the equipment manufacturer and facilitate proper installation, including cloud-based data storage. Furthermore, they may create specific order sets in the clinic contexts depending on the electronic medical record to ensure seamless clinical workflow and recordkeeping. It is important to consider that other departments in the institution might have already initiated this process, and a provider can model their POCUS approach to an existing one.

Institutional protocols

Hospitals often have credentialing requirements around the use of US equipment and patient care. If the facility has a POCUS committee, there may already be guidance on criteria needed to perform POCUS, again often including a set number (eg, 25–50) of US exams performed under supervision by providers credentialed to perform liver ultrasonography, preferably with experience in teaching US. At the present time, there are no set criteria for someone to have sufficient mastery in liver POCUS to qualify them to teach. If the institution does not have a liver POCUS setup, this can likely be done in collaboration with radiology and/or other subspecialties in medicine. Currently, AASLD has begun to offer introductory training and certification, providing adult and pediatric hepatologists with basic knowledge and direct hands-on US experience that counts toward certification. Importantly, while a limited number of examinations may be necessary for credentialing and billing, mastery and proficiency require more practice and can often be obtained by the learner only after a substantial number of examinations.

Timing

Time to implementation varies depending on the institution, but previous experience in IBD and liver practices suggests 6–18 months from decision to implementation to routine use in the clinic. Time length of the exam will vary based on experience, but we recommend allowing 7–10 minutes per liver POCUS initially, with this timing likely reducing as experience is gained.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Ultrasonography is performed by hepatologists in many parts of the world. With the implementation of POCUS in other specialty areas, AASLD believes that liver POCUS should be made available as part of hepatology practice in the United States. We define standard POCUS that will provide a basic assessment of the liver in 3 abdominal ultrasonographic views. We propose succinct didactic training followed by practical training with at least 25 supervised examinations to equip a hepatologist to perform basic liver POCUS. We anticipate that as this field progresses and individual practitioners accumulate their experiences, these recommendations may need to be updated in the future. As the use of POCUS becomes widespread and the number of practitioners increases, there may be further opportunities for ultrasonography-based hepatology practice to expand. In countries in which ultrasonography has been practiced by hepatologists, many of the advanced diagnostic and therapeutic procedures are performed by hepatologists. An easily implementable model may be liver biopsies, which have migrated to the radiology domain recently. With a well-established POCUS practice, parenchymal and even targeted real-time US-guided biopsies may be performed. Availability of bedside US with Doppler can also facilitate post-liver transplant care. The potentially important role of SWE in the screening and diagnosis of liver disease has already been mentioned. Further, in many parts of the world, hepatologists perform HCC surveillance examinations, sometimes including contrast enhancement. Therapeutically, US-guided drainage or even HCC ablations have been performed by hepatologists in other countries. All of these practical applications of liver ultrasonography open up new opportunities for research evaluating the technology for screening, diagnosis, and treatment of liver disease.

From the training point of view, there is an urgent need for data about the optimal methods to learn and teach POCUS. This is somewhat akin to endoscopy training, and as the POCUS practice is increasingly adopted, a more data-driven system needs to be established to set standards in training and qualification. Adding capability to the standard POCUS will need to be formalized. Learning from other countries’ experiences, we hope that POCUS training will become part of the hepatology clinical training. This will require formalizing the qualification of the instructors as well. Thus, if organized productively, liver POCUS may serve as an impetus for a new subspecialty within hepatology that expands the expertise of the specialty, advancing patient care through the timely application of the technology and research.
