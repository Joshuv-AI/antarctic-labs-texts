Optimization of gravity model based on coopetition perspective and its application in synergetic development evaluation of coastal port groups in China
Authors: Yang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper introduces an enhanced gravity model that incorporates a coopetition framework and a K-nearest neighbor (KNN) spatial weighting scheme to analyze the synergistic evolution of China’s five major coastal port clusters. Given the ongoing restructuring of global supply chains and the increasing focus on regional port integration, this research addresses a subject of considerable contemporary relevance and practical significance. Drawing on panel data spanning 2015 to 2024, we develop an evaluation framework comprising eight key indicators and employ the entropy weight method to quantify their respective contributions. Empirical findings identify inland waterway mileage, berth capacity, and railway mileage as the most influential drivers of port cluster synergy. The analysis reveals a consistently strengthening synergistic linkage between the Bohai Rim Port Cluster and the Pearl River Delta Port Cluster, while the synergy between the Bohai Rim and Yangtze River Delta port clusters demonstrates a gradual weakening trend. This study offers a methodological advancement for assessing interactive dynamics among port groups and yields strategic insights to inform policies aimed at fostering coordinated port development.Keywords: Port clusters, Synergistic development, Entropy method, K-nearest neighbor algorithm, Gravitational modelingSubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Geography, Geography

Introduction

Scholars proposed the strategic concept of port group development between the 1980s and the early 1990s, and this concept has gradually been reflected in and implemented through relevant port planning and development documents. In 2006, the Ministry of Transport issued the National Coastal Port Layout Plan, which divides China’s coastal ports into five major port groups based on the economic development status and characteristics of different regions, the current situation of ports within the regions, the transportation relationships among ports, and the economic rationality of main cargo transportation. The Regulations on Port Planning and Administration, implemented in 2008, conducts overall planning of coastal port resources to optimize port layout, improve port operation efficiency, and promote the sustainable development of economy and society. Subsequently, governments at all levels have successively issued a series of policies and guidelines, each putting forward clear requirements for the planning, construction, operation and management of port groups.

Especially against the backdrop of the “Belt and Road” development strategy and the post-pandemic era, both central and local governments have introduced various supportive policies to accelerate the development of port groups. For example, the National Comprehensive Multi-dimensional Transportation Network Planning Outline, the 14th Five-Year Plan for the Development of a Modern Comprehensive Transportation System, the Six Action Plans for Coordinating the Integrated Development of Shipping in the Yangtze River Delta, the Three-Year Action Plan for Building a World-Class Port Group in Shandong Province (2023—2025), and the proposal submitted at the Second Session of the 13th Guangdong Provincial Committee of the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference (CPPCC) — which advocates further promoting the coordinated and high-quality development of the Greater Bay Area port group and enhancing its international competitiveness through the “combined port” and “one-port access” models in the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area — all clearly indicate that integrated and synergetic development will dominate the future of port development. This trend mainly involves optimizing and smoothing the water transportation infrastructure network and port functional layout, constructing world-class port groups, promoting resource integration and shared utilization, and giving full play to the leading role of port groups. As key nodes for allocating global shipping resources, expanding the openness of urban agglomerations, and boosting the global competitiveness of urban agglomerations, port groups can drive the formation of a “port group-urban agglomeration” linkage and support regional economic integration.

However, unlike the integrated development of urban agglomerations, synergetic development of port groups involves obvious coopetition relationships. Consequently, analyzing the influencing factors of port groups’ synergetic development, measuring the degree of synergetic development among them, and exploring effective paths to promote such development have become hotspots and priorities in academic research.

Research status

The National Coastal Port Layout Plan organizes ports into five major coastal port groups based on geographical proximity and similarities in cargo handling operations. In academic research, scholars have proposed various definitions of port groups from different perspectives. Chen Miao defines a port group as “an aggregation of ports within a specific region that share talent, resources, markets, technology, and other essential elements, maintain competitive and cooperative relationships with one another, and generate significant agglomeration economies“1. Tang Songyuan describes a port group as “a collection of geographically adjacent ports that share common or overlapping economic hinterlands and have interconnected interests,” adding that the objective of synergetic development within port groups is to establish an efficient and coordinated modern port system to support the growth of their associated economic hinterlands2. Xin Ming notes that “port group synergy refers to the competitive and cooperative interactions among neighboring ports, which foster a high degree of specialization and collaboration, enhance the ports’ capacity for absorption and regional influence, and serve as an effective strategy for achieving resource sharing“3. These definitions highlight that “coopetition relationships” have increasingly become a central focus of research, yet few studies have examined the specific manifestations of such coopetition dynamics within port groups.

Regarding research scope and subjects, existing studies primarily concentrate on the Bohai Bay Port Group, the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei Port Group, the Yangtze River Delta Port Group, and the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area Port Group. Across China’s five major coastal port groups, ports within each group encounter challenges such as redundant infrastructure, uncoordinated functional roles, and disorderly competition. Consequently, rational planning and synergetic development have emerged as key strategies for enhancing the overall competitiveness of port groups4. Based on an analysis of the competitive landscape and the imperative for synergetic development in the Bohai Rim region, Li Nan et al. and Liu Jing argue that integrating resources, promoting collaborative development, optimizing strategic approaches, establishing clear synergy objectives, and strengthening cooperation can improve the collective competitiveness of port groups5,6.

Wei Lihua examines the strategic positioning, challenges, and obstacles facing the integration of the Tianjin-Hebei Port Group within the broader framework of Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei coordinated development. Focusing on institutional systems, policies, and market environments, she proposes establishing cross-regional coordination mechanisms to maximize the role of market forces in the synergistic development of port resources, and advocates for building a cooperative system characterized by complementary strengths and well-defined roles7. Chu Bin contends that Tianjin Port should take a leading role in accelerating the synergetic development of the Tianjin-Hebei Port Group, thereby boosting the group’s international competitiveness and service capabilities8. The formation of port groups inevitably involves issues of cooperative operations and resource allocation among port cities or provinces. Jia Dashan emphasizes the importance of overcoming administrative barriers, allocating resources scientifically, and enhancing synergistic vitality, offering corresponding measures to advance port synergy9.

The Yangtze River Delta region, as the area with the most rapid growth and highest development level in terms of industrial clusters, urban agglomerations, and port groups, has attracted widespread attention from various sectors. Zhang Qiang et al. investigate the development mechanisms of the Yangtze River Delta Port Group, recommending improvements to inter-provincial coordination among administrative departments to establish government-guided and enterprise-driven models for port group synergy10. From the perspective of synergistic development among industrial clusters, urban agglomerations, and port groups, Shen Yufang et al. and Jiang Qianzhi et al. propose strengthening linkages and collaborative development among these three clusters to effectively prevent disorderly competition across industries, cities, and ports, thereby fostering a new development paradigm in the Yangtze River Delta region11–13.

Xin Ming et al. study the synergistic development positioning and strategies of the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area Port Group. They analyze the current status and existing challenges of the port group, employ cluster analysis to classify and evaluate the group, clarify the functional roles of individual ports, and propose measures such as policy innovation, resource integration, differentiated development, and information platform construction to enhance the international competitiveness of the port group14–16.

Concerning the development and operational mechanisms and models of port groups, Ye Caihong et al. analyze synergistic development mechanisms from a resource integration perspective, proposing four mechanisms: substitution, resource complementarity, resource enhancement, and resource conflict. They suggest formulating scientific port group plans, establishing cross-administrative management and coordination institutions, clarifying port functions, implementing differentiated development strategies, and building information-sharing mechanisms17. Tang Songyuan and Wu Huijun argue that the synergistic development of port groups requires both government guidance and market-driven initiatives, and should involve co-management by governments and port enterprises to achieve resource sharing and complementary advantages, thereby attracting broader participation and fostering high-quality regional economic development18–20.

Literature focusing on measurement methods and models for port group synergistic development remains relatively limited. Using the Shandong Peninsula as a case study, Huang Changsheng et al. analyze the synergistic development of regional port groups, construct an evaluation index system, and measure the synergy degree of location and functional systems through principal component analysis and membership functions, offering recommendations for port construction involving coordination mechanisms, interconnectivity, infrastructure optimization, and information sharing21. Liu Pengqun et al. and Jiang Chongxiu et al. apply the grey correlation model and vector autoregressive (VAR) model to analyze the synergistic development between port groups and regional economies in different contexts, proposing policy recommendations for their coordinated growth22,23. In contrast, Yuan Xumei et al. explore the synergistic development mechanism of the logistics system in the Tianjin-Hebei Port Group through system dynamics modeling and simulation, proposing strategies to enhance synergistic effects24. Liu Ling et al. establish a coupling coordination measurement model for port groups, evaluate the synergy degree of the Anhui Port Group, and suggest measures to promote synergistic development25.

In summary, existing academic research on port group synergistic development primarily focuses on three areas: research scope and subjects, development mechanisms and models, and measurement models for synergy. Scholars have employed methods such as principal component analysis, membership functions, grey correlation models, vector autoregressive (VAR) models, and coupling coordination measurement models to examine the synergistic development of specific port groups. However, few studies have comprehensively investigated the synergistic development among China’s five major coastal port groups from an overarching perspective, nor have they integrated coopetition relationships between port groups into their analytical models. Synergistic development of port groups contributes to driving rapid economic growth. In the context of the “Belt and Road” initiative and the accelerated formation of a new development pattern characterized by “domestic circulation as the mainstay with domestic and international circulations reinforcing each other,” it is essential to analyze and measure port group synergistic development from the perspective of overall coordination among China’s five major coastal port groups. Therefore, this study takes China’s five major coastal port groups as its research subject, modifies the gravity model by incorporating K-nearest neighbor (KNN) algorithm theory, and constructs a coopetition relationship-based gravity model to measure the degree of synergistic development among these port groups from 2007 to 2024, thereby providing insights for the development of China’s port groups. It should be clearly noted that due to constraints in statistical data availability and quantitative measurement feasibility, this study selects only eight typical and quantifiable indicators to construct the evaluation system, which may not fully encompass all factors influencing port group synergistic development. To address missing data issues, this paper employs methods such as exponential smoothing to make reasonable predictions for incomplete data.

Theoretical foundation and model construction

Theoretical rationale for model improvements

The standard gravity model assumes that spatial interaction declines with physical distance. However, port systems are predominantly influenced by economic linkages, hinterland complementarity, and network structure rather than pure geographical separation. To more accurately capture the economic logic underlying port coopetition, two theoretical modifications are introduced. Simultaneously, the potential limitations of these modifications are explicitly acknowledged to ensure theoretical rigor and transparency.

Economic distance (GDP difference) instead of geographic distance

Geographic distance alone cannot adequately explain the intensity of competition or cooperation among port groups. For port systems, interaction intensity is more dependent on economic heterogeneity than on physical spacing.

GDP difference reflects gaps in economic scale, industrial structure, and logistics demand across hinterlands.

Smaller GDP difference implies overlapping economic hinterlands, similar cargo structures, and intensified competition.

Larger GDP difference indicates stronger industrial complementarity, greater potential for cargo redistribution, and higher synergy potential.

By employing GDP difference as the core “distance” variable, the model transitions from measuring physical spatial friction to capturing economic interaction barriers, which aligns much more closely with the theoretical logic of port coopetition. This substitution substantially enhances the model’s interpretability for real-world port interaction patterns.

However, important limitations must be emphasized. This revised gravity model uses only GDP difference of economic hinterlands as the distance measure and does not incorporate actual geographic distance or institutional distance between port groups. Geographic distance still influences transportation costs, time delays, and logistics efficiency. Institutional distance—including differences in governance systems, administrative divisions, coordination mechanisms, and policy environments—also affects cooperation willingness and transaction costs. The omission of these two critical dimensions may lead to an incomplete representation of “distance” in the gravity model, potentially restricting the full explanatory power of the empirical results.

Theoretical necessity of KNN spatial weighting

Traditional gravity models rely on fixed distance-decay functions, which often fail to reflect the real network structure of port interactions. The introduction of KNN-based spatial weighting is theoretically justified:

It identifies genuine interactive neighbors in the port system based on actual correlation, rather than imposing arbitrary distance thresholds.

It captures the spatial dependence of port interactions more objectively and accurately.

It improves model stability and reduces estimation bias compared with traditional weighting approaches.

Thus, KNN weighting is not an ad-hoc adjustment but a theoretically grounded step to embed network structure into the gravity framework, which greatly enhances the model’s applicability to port cluster research.

Indicator system

Drawing on existing literature and policy documents, this study selected eight influencing factors to construct an indicator system, which is primarily composed of two levels: internal port factors and external port factors. The internal port factors include the number of berths, cargo throughput, and container throughput; the external port factors encompass the number of employees engaged in loading, unloading, and related operations, the GDP of the economic hinterland, road mileage, railway mileage, and inland waterway mileage. Raw data for the aforementioned indicators were collected from authoritative sources such as the China Port Yearbook and the China Statistical Yearbook. Missing values were addressed using the exponential smoothing method for imputation. All indicators were standardized to mitigate the effects of differing units. Although qualitative elements such as institutional coordination, governance structures, and technical capabilities are critically important, they are difficult to quantify consistently over time. The exclusion of these factors may introduce a certain degree of bias, which is acknowledged as one of the limitations of this study. Due to challenges in quantification and the lack of continuous statistical data, these factors were not incorporated into the indicator system, potentially resulting in some deviation between the developed indicator system and the actual factors influencing the synergistic effects of port clusters.

Based on Fig. 1, conclusions can be drawn from the perspectives of the variation range, magnitude, and trend of the indicator weights. Firstly, according to the variation range of the weights of the eight indicators, they can be divided into two categories. The first category includes relatively stable indicators, covering berths, cargo throughput, the number of employees engaged in loading, unloading, and other related operations, railway mileage, highway mileage, and inland waterway mileage, all of which show relatively stable changes. The second category consists of relatively volatile indicators, namely the GDP of the economic hinterland and container throughput.Among these, the GDP of the economic hinterland exhibits the highest volatility, reaching relative peaks in 2010 and 2018 respectively. In contrast, affected by the slowdown in global trade and investment, increased downward pressure on the world economy, insufficient growth momentum of major developed economies, and the downturn of emerging markets and developing economies28, the container throughput has maintained a downward trend since around 2018.

Secondly, the eight indicators can be divided into three categories based on a weight threshold of 0.13. The first category includes indicators with weights ≥ 0.13, namely berths and inland waterway mileage. The quantity and quality of berths are important indicators for measuring port capacity, and compared with other transportation modes, inland waterway transportation enables more convenient connection with maritime transportation. Particularly in regions with relatively developed inland water systems, water-water intermodal transportation boasts ultra-high cost-effectiveness. Thus, berths and inland waterway mileage have attracted consistent attention and serve as two key indicators for the synergetic development of port groups.

The second category covers indicators with weights < 0.13, including cargo throughput, container throughput, the number of employees engaged in loading, unloading, and other related operations, highway mileage, and railway mileage. With the widespread attention and implementation of sea-rail intermodal transportation, the weight of railway mileage has gradually become the third most important indicator, while the variation trend of cargo throughput is basically consistent with that of the number of employees engaged in loading, unloading, and other related operations, reflecting the intrinsic link between port operational scale and human resource allocation.

The third category only includes the GDP of the economic hinterland, whose weight is constantly changing under the influence of various factors.These fluctuations align with major external shocks such as the global financial crisis, the deceleration of global economic growth, and the China-U.S. trade friction. It is plausible to infer that these events may contribute to changes in indicator weights. However, these associations are based purely on temporal correlation and do not represent quantitatively validated causal conclusions derived from the model employed in this study.As observed from Fig. 1, container throughput kept declining until it stabilized after 2019, while the weights of the other six indicators began to recover steadily in the second half of 2018 and returned to the pre-China-US trade war level in 2019.

The trends of these eight indicators clearly show that China’s five major coastal port groups have experienced three significant shocks since 2007, namely the global financial crisis, the slowdown in global economic growth, and the China-US trade war. This result is consistent with the evaluation of port trade resilience by Jiang Ziran et al.29. These external shocks have caused obvious peaks and troughs in port production indicators, and all three crises exhibited distinct lag effects: port throughput dropped to the lowest level of the stage in the year following each shock, and then gradually recovered and adjusted.This study only identifies the lag effect of the three major external shocks on port group indicators, but does not conduct a quantitative analysis of the lag period and impact intensity of different shocks, which is a deficiency of the current research and can be further explored in subsequent studies.

Model construction

The gravity model, originally derived from physics, has been introduced into the field of economics and widely applied. In 1962, Dutch econometrician Tinbergen first proposed a parsimonious form of the bilateral trade flow econometric model. He argued that the trade flow from one country to another mainly depends on the national economic scale (measured by GDP) and the geographical distance between the two countries, with trade volume being proportional to the product of their economic scales and inversely proportional to the spatial distance between them30. Economists such as Anderson and Bergstrand have provided the theoretical basis for the gravity model31,32. The prototype of the trade gravity model is expressed as follows:

Tij Total bilateral trade, Yi GDP of country i, Yj GDP of country j, Dij The distance between country i and country j, A Proportional constant.

In 1966, Linnemann incorporated the dummy variable of preferential trade agreements into the original trade flow econometric model for the first time to examine the impact of regional economic integration on bilateral trade. Subsequently, the gravity model has been applied in fields such as international trade33,34, retail marketing35, and regional economics36. Since the 1990 s, economists have conducted extensive and in-depth explorations on the theoretical implications of the gravity model.

Despite its widespread application in the social sciences, the gravity model has seen relatively limited use in measuring the synergetic development of port groups. Drawing on previous research results, this study modifies the gravity model based on the K-nearest neighbor (KNN) algorithm and Bayesian parameter estimation theory, taking the GDP difference of economic hinterlands as the “distance”. A gravity model incorporating coopetition relationships is constructed, as shown in Formula (2), to measure the synergetic development degree of port groups. This modification enables the reflection of coopetition relationships between port groups in the model, making the calculation results of synergetic development relationships among port groups more consistent with practical situations.The model constructed in this study takes the GDP difference of economic hinterlands as the core “distance” indicator to characterize the economic gap between port groups, but it does not consider the geographical distance and institutional distance between port groups, which may make the characterization of the “distance” factor in the gravity model incomplete.

These parameters jointly influence the calculation of gravitational forces in the model. Through Bayesian parameter estimation, the posterior distributions of these parameters can be obtained, allowing us to understand their uncertainties and possible ranges of values.

Empirical analysis and recommendations

Empirical analysis

Based on the constructed evaluation indicator system and the modified gravity model incorporating coopetition relationships, this study selects China’s five major coastal port groups as the research objects, namely the Bohai Rim Port Group(BTHPG), Yangtze River Delta Port Group(YRDPG), Pearl River Delta Port Group(PRDPG), Southeast Coastal Port Group(SECPG), and Southwest Coastal Port Group(SWCPG). Data for each indicator in the system from 2007 to 2024 are collected from sources such as the China Port Yearbook and China Statistical Yearbook, while indicator data from 2015 to 2024 are substituted into the model for calculation.

Firstly, the 5-nearest neighbor spatial weight matrix is calculated using the preprocessed data from 2015 to 2024, yielding the coopetition relationship weight matrix among China’s five major coastal port groups. The results are presented in Fig. 2.

Neighboring spatial weight matrix.

As can be concluded from Fig. 2, the coopetition value of each port group with itself is 1, which indicates that when each port group is regarded as an integrated entity and the internal coopetition among its individual ports is neglected, a port group shows a state of complete cooperation with itself. In contrast, it presents a relationship of coexistent competition and cooperation with other port groups. A value closer to 1 denotes a tighter cooperative relationship between the two port groups, whereas a smaller value indicates a more intense competitive relationship. Based on the empirical analysis, this study further confirms that the Pearl River Delta Port Group (PRDPG) exhibits a distinct “cooperation-competition” differentiation pattern: its cooperative relationship with the Bohai Rim Port Group (BTHPG) has been continuously strengthening, while its competitive relationship with the Yangtze River Delta Port Group (YRDPG) has intensified. In contrast, the Southeast Coastal Port Group (SECPG) and Southwest Coastal Port Group (SWCPG) have maintained a consistently high level of cooperative intensity, indicating that they have formed a stable collaborative relationship in regional synergy.This study only analyzes the overall coopetition relationship between port groups at the macro level, and does not conduct an in-depth analysis of the coopetition behavior and interaction mechanism between individual core ports within each port group, which is a limitation of the current research perspective.

This pattern may be linked to the significant endogenous momentum of the Yangtze River Delta Port Group, which is underpinned by its robust economic hinterland, comprehensive industrial system, and well-established logistics network. This stable pattern of cooperation corresponds to the presence of tight industrial interconnections and a well-developed inland waterway network in southern China. Industrial complementarity and resource endowments may function as important contextual drivers of regional synergy. The observed increase in cooperative intensity aligns with policy guidance and differentiated industrial structures, while intensified competition corresponds with overlapping economic hinterlands and homogeneous logistics configurations. These associations are plausible and consistent with the observed results; however, none are established as direct causal relationships by the model’s quantitative outputs.Additionally, external shocks—such as the China–U.S. trade friction—may exert a phased transmission effect on the coopetition dynamics among port groups, potentially temporarily exacerbating inter-port competition. Industrial complementarity and resource endowment constitute the core driving force for the regional synergy of port groups: the improvement of cooperative intensity is mainly driven by policy guidance and differentiated industrial structures, while the intensification of competition is closely related to the overlap of economic hinterlands and the homogeneity of logistics network layout. In addition, external shocks such as the China-US trade war have a phased transmission effect on the coopetition relationship of port groups, which will temporarily exacerbate inter-port competition. However, China’s coastal port groups have strong synergetic resilience, and can restore the stable coopetition relationship by adjusting cargo flow directions and optimizing cooperation modes.

Recommendations for the coordinated development of port clusters

Leverage the comparative advantages of each port group to form a complementary development pattern. The Yangtze River Delta Port Group should focus on international container transshipment and smart port services, strengthen cross-regional cooperation to reduce inward contraction, and avoid homogeneous competition. The Bohai Rim Port Group should give full play to its strengths in bulk cargo transportation, expand north-south cooperation with the Pearl River Delta Port Group, and optimize internal resource allocation. The Pearl River Delta Port Group should deepen cooperation with the Bohai Rim Port Group while developing characteristic services such as cross-border e-commerce logistics to rationalize competition with the Yangtze River Delta Port Group. The Southeast and Southwest Coastal Port Groups should maintain stable cooperation, improve the inland transportation network, and enhance regional economic radiation through characteristic cargo transportation and border trade logistics.

Prioritize multimodal transportation development, focusing on water-water and sea-rail intermodal infrastructure upgrades. Accelerate the renovation of inland waterway channels and port-railway connections for port groups with high-weight indicators such as inland waterway and railway mileage. Establish a unified transportation information sharing platform to realize seamless connection of cross-mode and cross-region information. Optimize the economic hinterland transportation network layout, strengthen links with inland dry ports and industrial clusters, and expand resource gathering and radiation capabilities.

Establish an external shock early warning and response system, and formulate emergency plans for different risks to ensure operational stability. Promote business diversification, develop emerging sectors such as port logistics finance and smart port technology, and reduce dependence on single cargo types or routes. Strengthen international cooperation, integrate into the global shipping network, and learn advanced risk management experience to improve international competitiveness and anti-risk capabilities.

Conclusions and prospects

This study first adopts the entropy weight method to calculate the weights of various indicators and constructs an evaluation indicator system. Through the analysis of weight results, the contribution degree of each indicator to the entire system is obtained. Secondly, based on the K-nearest neighbor (KNN) algorithm theory, the gravity model is modified to establish a gravity model incorporating coopetition relationships, which makes the calculated values of the synergetic development degree among port groups more consistent with their actual development. Analysis of the calculation results shows that the synergetic correlation between the development of the Yangtze River Delta Port Group and other port groups presents a gradual decreasing trend, while the synergetic development intensity between the Bohai Bay Port Group and the Pearl River Delta Port Group, Southeast Coastal Port Group, and Southwest Coastal Port Group is steadily increasing. Finally, combined with the indicator weights and port group synergy degrees, this study puts forward recommendations such as continuous investment in the construction of multimodal transportation methods including water-water intermodal transportation and sea-rail intermodal transportation, and strengthening the cooperative relationship between the Yangtze River Delta Port Group and other port groups. These suggestions aim to promote the differentiated development of port groups, realize their synergetic development, and foster a new pattern of regional economic integration.

This study has several limitations. First, the indicator system only includes quantifiable variables, thereby excluding non-quantitative factors such as policy support, institutional coordination, governance, and technological innovation. Second, the revised gravity model employs the difference in GDP as a proxy for economic distance, without explicitly accounting for geographic and institutional distance. Third, the analysis is conducted at the port-group level, which may overlook the dynamics of hub ports and their internal interactions. Furthermore, explanations pertaining to industrial complementarity, the effects of policies, and external shocks are logically sound and align with observable trends; however, they have not been directly corroborated by output from the model.

Research on the synergetic development of China’s coastal port groups will remain a hotspot and priority in academic circles in the future. In view of the above limitations, further empirical studies are needed in aspects such as expanding the influencing factors of synergetic development among port groups, quantitative analysis of coopetition relationships, and strengthening cooperative relationships between port groups, with the goal to truly reflect the interaction degree among port groups with objective data and realize the free flow of factors such as economy, resources, and technology.
