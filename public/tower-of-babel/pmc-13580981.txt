Exposome‐driven molecular sensitization signatures in pediatric rhinitis and asthma across Spanish regions

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllergic rhinitis and asthma in childhood arise from dynamic interactions between genetic predisposition and the exposome. However, how environmental heterogeneity shapes molecular IgE sensitization profiles during early life remains insufficiently characterized.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, observational, multicentre, cross‐sectional study including 354 children (0–15 years) with allergic rhinitis and/or asthma recruited across eight Spanish cities contrasting regional climatic contexts. All patients were skin prick test‐positive to at least one aeroallergen and underwent multiplex component‐resolved diagnostics using the ALEX platform. Sensitization patterns were analyzed, and unsupervised approaches were applied to identify molecular signatures.ResultsMolecular IgE profiling revealed a structured sensitization landscape, with 97.5% of children exhibiting reactivity to at least one allergen. A dominant mite‐related pattern and partially distinct pollen‐ and epithelial‐related patterns were observed. PCA identified composite molecular sensitization gradients and descriptive differences in participant distributions across recruitment centres, although substantial overlap was observed between centres. Age‐stratified analyses identified a non‐linear trajectory of sensitization, characterized by early molecular priming in high‐exposure environments, followed by a marked expansion of IgE levels and allergen sensitization during school age. Rhinitis severity was strongly associated with a denser mite sensitization network. In contrast, asthma severity was driven not by mite sensitization alone but by a complex pollen‐related polysensitization.ConclusionPediatric IgE sensitization is structured into molecular patterns associated with geography and age, consistent with a role for environmental context in early allergic trajectories. Keywords: aerobiology, allergic asthma, allergic rhinitis, climate change, Exposome, pediatric cohort

Key message

Environmental context shapes the development of childhood allergic sensitization, with distinct molecular profiles linked to geography, age, and disease severity. Early‐life exposure patterns influence the emergence of allergen‐specific IgE responses and may contribute to different trajectories of allergic rhinitis and asthma.

INTRODUCTION

Allergic rhinitis and asthma are among the most common chronic diseases in childhood, and their clinical expression is increasingly understood as the result of dynamic interactions between genetic susceptibility and the exposome. 1 , 2 , 3 In pediatric patients, sensitization does not emerge as a static event but as a progressive and age‐dependent process shaped by cumulative exposure to indoor and outdoor allergens, local climate, urban environment, and lifestyle‐related factors. 2 , 4 , 5 Although sensitization to aeroallergens often begins early in life, the molecular architecture of this response may evolve substantially throughout childhood, with expansion from limited IgE recognition patterns to broader repertoires that may better reflect clinically relevant disease. 4 , 5 , 6

Current diagnostic approaches based solely on whole allergen extracts are often insufficient to capture this complexity. Component‐resolved diagnostics (CRD) have shown that sensitization to individual allergenic molecules provides a more precise framework to identify genuine sensitizers, distinguish co‐sensitization from cross‐reactivity, and define clinically meaningful phenotypes. 6 , 7 , 8 This is particularly relevant in children, in whom the sequence of molecular sensitization, the hierarchy of major and minor allergens, and the relationship between age and allergen repertoire remain incompletely characterized. 4 , 5 , 7 Moreover, because allergen exposure is geographically structured, pediatric molecular sensitization profiles are likely to differ across regions with distinct climatic and aerobiological conditions. 1 , 3 , 9

Previous studies have highlighted the importance of environmental context in shaping aeroallergen sensitization patterns. A landmark European study demonstrated that populations living under specific climatic conditions may share dominant molecular signatures, especially for mite allergens. 5 However, whether similar principles apply to children across geographically and climatically diverse settings remains insufficiently explored. Pediatric cohorts are of special interest because they allow evaluation of sensitization at earlier stages of immune maturation when the balance between perennial and seasonal exposures may differentially influence the development of the IgE repertoire. 4

Spain constitutes an informative natural setting for this question because it encompasses marked regional variation in humidity, temperature, vegetation, and aerobiological exposure, ranging from Atlantic to Mediterranean and semi‐arid environments. 1 , 9 Such heterogeneity offers the opportunity to investigate how local environmental conditions are associated with distinct molecular patterns of sensitization in children with allergic respiratory disease. Defining these patterns may improve our understanding of age‐related differences in molecular sensitization and provide a more robust basis for region‐adapted diagnosis and, potentially, more accurate allergen immunotherapy selection. 6 , 7 , 8

Therefore, the aim of the present multicentre study was to characterize and compare molecular aeroallergen sensitization profiles in a pediatric population with allergic rhinitis and/or asthma recruited across eight Spanish cities with different environmental backgrounds. Specifically, we sought to evaluate the association between age and geographic setting and their influence on IgE recognition to a broad panel of aeroallergen components, and to identify the major molecular signatures associated with distinct regional sensitization phenotypes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects

This was a prospective, observational, multicentre, cross‐sectional study conducted between December 2023 and November 2025. Children aged 0–15 years (divided into 5‐year age groups) with a confirmed diagnosis of allergic rhinitis and/or asthma were consecutively recruited from hospitals across different climatic regions of Spain. Recruitment was performed consecutively at each participating centre until the predefined sample size was reached. Standardized inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied across all participating centres to ensure the recruitment of a homogeneous population of children with clinically confirmed IgE‐mediated respiratory allergy. Eligible participants were required to have a compatible clinical history and evidence of IgE‐mediated sensitization, defined as either a positive skin prick test (SPT) and/or positive molecular sensitization, and to have lived in the corresponding study region for a minimum of 3 years, in order to reduce the potential influence of migration‐related differences in environmental allergen exposure. Additionally, symptom onset had to occur after establishing residence in the study area to ensure that sensitization profiles were evaluated in the context of local environmental exposure. Patients receiving or with a history of allergen immunotherapy, treatment with biologic agents or other immunomodulatory drugs, or those unable to undergo skin prick testing were excluded. This investigation was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of each centre and informed consent was obtained from all participants and from parents/guardians at the time of their inclusion in the study. The severity and stage of allergic diseases were clinically assessed following specific guidelines. 10

Clinical data collected from patients' medical records included sociodemographic information, medical history (including past medical conditions and current allergy diagnosis), and details about their treatments. In line with routine clinical practice, only patients with a positive skin prick test (SPT) and/or positive specific IgE (sIgE) to relevant aeroallergen extracts (such as mites, pollens, molds or animal epithelia) were included in the study. Patients who had received or were currently undergoing allergen immunotherapy or treatment with monoclonal antibodies (biologics) were excluded.

Skin prick test

Percutaneous testing was conducted according to European standards using a diagnostic panel with standardized extracts (Inmunotek S.L., Alcala de Henares, Spain), including Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (D. pteronyssinus). Blomia tropicalis (B. tropicalis). Lepidoglyphus destructor (L. destructor), cat and dog dander, a grass mix (Poa pratensis, Dactylis glomerata, Lolium perenne, Phleum pratense and Festuca pratensis), Olea europaea, Betula verrucosa, Quercus ilex, Platanus hispanica, Plantago lanceolata, Parietaria judaica, Salsola kali, Artemisia vulgaris, Juniperus oxycedrus, Cupressus arizonica, Cupressus sempervirens and Alternaria alternata. Histamine (10 mg/mL) and saline were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. As per standard procedure, antihistamines were discontinued 1 week before the SPT. Wheal diameters were measured 20 min after testing, with diameters greater than 3 mm considered positive. 11 , 12

Serological analysis

Blood samples were collected from all participants, labeled with a unique code, stored at −40°C, and thawed immediately before in vitro testing. Total IgE and sIgE levels were measured using the ALEX MacroArray platform (MacroArray Diagnostics, Vienna, Austria) according to the manufacturer's protocol. ALEX is a multiplex array containing 295 reagents, including 117 whole allergens and 178 molecular components. The allergens are attached to polystyrene nanobeads, which are then deposited on a nitrocellulose membrane, as previously described. 13 The assay included 17 mite molecular allergens: Der p 1, Der p 2, Der p 5, Der p 7, Der p 10, Der p 11, Der p 20, Der p 21, Der p 23, Der f 1, Der f 2, Blo t 5, Blo t 10, Blo t 21, Lep d 2, Gly d 2, and Tyr p 2. Additionally, 10 cat and dog epithelial allergens/sources were tested: Fel d 1, Fel d 2, Fel d 4, Fel d 7, Can f 1, Can f 2, Can f 3, Can f 4, dog male urine and Can f 6. For consistency with established allergen nomenclature, the dog male urine was referred to as Can f 5 throughout the manuscript. A total of 22 pollen allergens were included: Bet v 1, Bet v 2, Bet v 6, Cup a 1, Pla a 1, Pla a 2, Pla a 3, Ole e 1, Ole e 7, Ole e 9, Phl p 1, Phl p 2, Phl p 5, Phl p 6, Phl p 7, Phl p 12, Lol p 1, Sal k 1, Pla l 1, Par j 2, Art v 1 and Art v 3. To evaluate mold sensitization, Alt a 1 and Alt a 6 were assessed. These 51 allergens were selected as they represent the most relevant aeroallergens in the studied regions and are directly related to the respiratory symptoms of the included patients. Total IgE levels were reported in kilounits per liter (kU/L), while sIgE levels were expressed in kUA/L, with values ≥0.3 kUA/L considered positive. 13

A serum pool was prepared for each of the three most prevalent allergen sources in each study population. These were prepared using an equal amount of serum from each patient in each region (Barcelona, Granada, Lugo, Móstoles, Santiago, Sagunto, and Zaragoza). The optimal dilution of each serum pool was determined using an ELISA assay (approximately 1.5 OD).

ELISA assay

For the ELISA assay, Microlon high‐binding plate wells (Greiner Bio‐One, Frickenhausen, Germany) were coated with 1 μg of allergenic extracts in sodium carbonate/bicarbonate buffer at 4°C overnight. After washing with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), 0.25% Tween 20 (PBS‐T), different dilutions of serum pools in PBS‐T‐0.25% were added and then incubated 2 h at RT. The bound IgE antibodies were detected by incubation with 100 μL of Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugate anti human IgE (Southern Biotech B3102E8) diluted in PBS‐T 0.25%, FBS 5%. The chromogenic substrate was prepared by adding the same ratio of the chromogenic reagent TMB (3,3′,5,5′‐tetramethylbenzidine) and peroxidase stabilizing solution to each well, following the manufacturer's instructions (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The reaction was allowed to proceed for 20 min, after which the stop solution (sulfuric acid) was added to terminate the reaction. The optical density (OD) was then measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using medians and first and third quartiles (Q1–Q3) or means and standard deviations for continuous variables, and percentages for categorical variables. Due to the right‐skewed nature of sIgE data and the high frequency of zero values, non‐parametric tests were employed for all allergen‐level comparisons.

To evaluate overall differences in sIgE levels across multiple clinical severity phenotypes, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. For pairwise comparisons between predefined extreme clinical phenotypes, the Mann–Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test) was applied. 14 , 15 Both tests were performed using the original, untransformed sIgE values, and all patients belonging to the corresponding clinical groups were included, irrespective of whether their sIgE values were above or below the clinical positivity threshold.

For data visualization, boxplots were displayed using a square‐root‐transformed y‐axis, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon tests were performed on the original, untransformed sIgE values. Heatmaps were generated using log10(sIgE +1)‐transformed values. 16 Agglomerative hierarchical clustering was performed to generate the heatmaps, allowing for the visual identification of co‐sensitization clusters and polysensitization complexity across different disease severities. 17 To reduce background noise and establish a more direct and robust association with disease severity, patients presenting with mild clinical phenotypes were excluded from the clustering analysis.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed as an exploratory analysis using participant‐level sIgE values for all 51 molecular allergen components. Values were transformed using log10(x + 1), centred, and scaled to unit variance before analysis using the PCA() function from the FactoMineR package (scale. unit = TRUE). Recruitment centre was not included as an active variable and was used only for graphical grouping; the 95% confidence ellipses were interpreted descriptively and not as a formal test of differences between centres. Dim1 and Dim2 correspond to the first and second principal components, PC1 and PC2, respectively. For graphical clarity, the variable correlation plot displays the 30 components with the highest summed contribution to PC1 and PC2, although all 51 components were included in the PCA calculation. Graphical representations were generated using factoextra, ggplot2, and ggrepel. The complete R code is provided in Supplementary File S1.

A p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using R version 4.4.2 for Windows (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the packages specified above and dplyr where applicable, and GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, California, USA).

Only one participant was younger than 1 year of age. This patient had a total IgE concentration of 114 kU/L. Only one additional participant had a total IgE concentration below the lower semi‐quantitative reporting limit of the ALEX2 assay (<20 kU/L); however, sIgE to several molecular components was detected in this patient.

Mites

Mites were identified as the main source of sensitizing airborne allergens in the cities of Barcelona, Lugo, Sagunto, Santiago, and Valencia (Figure 1). Sensitization to at least one of the 17 molecular mite allergens analyzed was detected in 200 individuals, accounting for 56.5% of the study population. Furthermore, in the five centres where mite exposure was most prevalent, 77.6% of patients (n = 223) showed positive sIgE results to at least one molecular mite allergen.

Our findings classified Der p 1, Der p 2, Der f 1, Der f 2, and Der p 23 as major allergens (prevalence >50%) in these mite‐prevalent populations, with Der p 2 being the most frequently recognized. Der p 5, Der p 7, Der p 21, Gly d 2, and Lep d 2 were considered intermediate allergens, showing prevalence rates between 20% and 30%. Minor allergens, defined by a prevalence below 20%, included Tyr p 2, Der p 10, Der p 20, Blo t 5, Blo t 10, and Blo t 21. No patients showed sensitization to Der p 11.

The overall prevalence of subjects (n = 223) exhibiting sIgE to group 1 allergens, namely Der p 1 and Der f 1, was 50%, a figure notably lower than the 59.4% recorded for group 2 allergens (Der p 2 and Der f 2) and the 57.6% observed for Der p 23. Analysis of individual molecular allergens revealed that Der p 2 was the most frequently identified at 60.3%, followed by Der f 2 at 58.5%, Der p 23 at 57.6%, Der p 1 at 53.1%, and Der f 1 at 46.9%. Within the cohort sensitized to group 1 allergens, a substantial majority of 84.0% (100 out of 119 subjects) demonstrated reactivity to both Der f 1 and Der p 1; interestingly, while 19 subjects were sensitized exclusively to Der p 1, no subjects showed isolated sensitivity to Der f 1. A similar pattern emerged among those sensitized to group 2 allergens, where 95.6% exhibited sIgE to both Der f 2 and Der p 2, with only five subjects sensitized exclusively to Der p 2 and one subject showing exclusive reactivity to Der f 2.

Most sensitized subjects in the total population (n = 354) recognized between 1 and 10 of the tested mite allergens (191 out of 200, 95.5%). The most frequent sIgE profile consisted of two molecules and included any of the five major allergens. Sensitization to more than 10 mite allergens was uncommon (6.5%). The mono‐sensitized profile was also highly heterogeneous: only 1 out of 200 patients (0.5%) showed exclusive sensitization to Der p 7; 2 patients (1.0%) were sensitized to Der p 20; 9 patients (4.6%) recognized Der p 23; 1 patient (0.5%) recognized Der p 21; and 1 patient (0.5%) recognized Der p 1. Additionally, 1 patient (0.5%) was sensitized only to Der f 1, and 1 (0.5%) only to Der f 2. Three patients (1.5%) recognized only Lep d 2, and 1 patient (0.5%) recognized only Blo t 5.

sIgE reactivity profiles by geographic location

Among the analyzed mite allergens, the most relevant (based on higher sIgE levels and sensitization frequency) were Der p 1, Der p 2, Der p 23, Der f 1, and Der f 2. These were followed by Der p 5, Der p 7, Der p 21, Gly d 2, Lep d 2, and Tyr p 2, which showed lower titters and frequencies. All centres had one or more patients sensitized to the major allergens Der p 1, Der p 2, Der f 1, Der f 2, and Der p 23, except for Móstoles, where no patients were sensitized to Der p 23. Der p 2 was the predominant mite allergen in Lugo (68.9%), Valencia (55.6%), Barcelona (74.4%), and Zaragoza (23.8%). Der p 23 was the most prevalent in Sagunto (58.7%), while in Santiago it was recognized at a similar rate to Der p 2 (53.3%). Der f 2 was also highly recognized in Valencia (53.3%) and Barcelona (72.1%). Although Der f 1 showed a high sensitization rate, it was not the most frequent mite allergen in any of the centres included in this study.

Regional variations in mite allergen recognition patterns were also observed. In Barcelona, Lugo, and Santiago de Compostela, there was a high recognition of all the key allergens previously mentioned (from the Dermatophagoides family, Glycyphagus, Lepidoglyphus, and Tyrophagus). Additionally, in Sagunto, Valencia, and Zaragoza, a strong recognition of allergens from the Dermatophagoides family was evident. In contrast, Granada and Móstoles showed a markedly lower number of patients sensitized to mite allergens (Table S2, Figure 2).

Age and sIgE reactivity profiles

The comparison of IgE levels across age groups (0–5, 6–10, and 11–15 years) revealed clear differences in the distribution and intensity of molecular sensitization between cities (Figure S1).

In the 0–5 years group, there was a marked contrast between locations. Cities such as Barcelona, Lugo, and Santiago de Compostela already show high IgE levels, indicating that substantial sensitization was present at these early ages. In these areas, we have observed that children present strong responses to major mite allergens before school age. In contrast, Granada, Zaragoza, and Móstoles show minimal or nearly absent sensitization at this stage, whereas Valencia and Sagunto display intermediate values.

In the 6–10 years group, higher IgE levels were observed in most cities, and this age group showed the highest values in many centres. Cities with previously low levels, such as Zaragoza, show a marked increase compared with the 0–5 years group. Similarly, Valencia and Sagunto reach their highest IgE levels in this age range. In Barcelona, Lugo, and Santiago de Compostela, sensitization remained high, although it may stabilize or slightly decrease compared to the youngest group.

In the 11–15 years group, different patterns are observed depending on the city. In Barcelona, Valencia, Sagunto, and Santiago de Compostela, IgE levels tended to remain stable or decrease slightly compared with the younger age groups. Lugo showed a slight reduction or redistribution across allergens. In contrast, Granada and Zaragoza showed their highest values in this age group, whereas Móstoles also shows its first noticeable increase, although overall levels remained low.

Pollens

Pollen represents a major allergenic source in the studied regions and constitutes the most relevant group of allergens in the cities of Granada, Móstoles, and Zaragoza (Figure 1). An additional evaluation focusing on the molecular components of pollen allergens was also performed. Sensitization to at least one pollen molecule was detected in 240 of the 354 patients included in the study (67.8%). This proportion increased markedly when the analysis was restricted to the three centres where pollen allergy is particularly prevalent, reaching 91.6% (120 out of 131 subjects). Within this subgroup, several molecules met the criteria for major allergens, defined as recognition in more than 50% of sensitized individuals; these included Cup a 1, Ole e 1, Phl p 1, and Lol p 1. Allergens with an intermediate prevalence (20–40%) comprised Pla a 2, Ole e 7, Phl p 5, Phl p 6, and Sal k 1. In contrast, molecules classified as minor allergens (prevalence below 20%) included Bet v 1, Bet v 2, Bet v 6, Pla a 1, Pla a 3, Ole e 9, Phl p 2, Phl p 7, Phl p 12, Par j 2, Pla l 1, Art v 1, and Art v 3.

When the analysis was stratified according to botanical family, different sensitization patterns were observed among these pollen‐prevalent study populations. Within the Oleaceae family, Ole e 1 was clearly the most frequently recognized allergen, with a prevalence of 70.2%. In the case of grasses (Poaceae), Phl p 1 represented the dominant sensitizing component (49.6%). For the Betulaceae family, Bet v 2 showed the highest level of recognition (11.5%). Among Platanaceae allergens, Pla a 2 was the most detected molecule (22.1%). Finally, within the Asteraceae family, Art v 3 emerged as the allergen with the greatest prevalence (12.2%).

Regarding the number of recognized allergens in the total population (n = 354), 226 individuals (63.8%) exhibited IgE recognition to between 1 and 9 of the pollen allergens included in the analysis. The most observed specific IgE profile involved reactivity to 2 or 3 allergens and in most cases included at least one of the four major molecules: Cup a 1, Ole e 1, Phl p 1, or Lol p 1. Sensitization to more than nine pollen allergens was comparatively uncommon and was identified in only 3.7% of subjects. Most monosensitization profiles were attributed to Ole e 1 with 28 monosensitized patients (7.9%), while five patients showed exclusive sensitization to Cup a 1 (1.4%), and three patients to Phl p 1 (0.8%). A small number of monosensitized individuals reacted to allergens not classified as major: two patients were sensitized only to Bet v 1 (0.6%), two subjects to Pla a 3 (0.6%), three to Phl p 5 (0.8%), one to Sal k 1 (0.3%), two patients to Par j 2 (0.6%), and one patient showed isolated sensitization to Art v 3 (0.3%).

sIgE reactivity profiles by geographic location

When the analysis was performed at the city level, distinct sensitization patterns were identified. In Barcelona, Ole e 1 was the most frequently recognized allergen, with sensitization detected in 11 patients (25.6%). In Granada, patients exhibited recognition of a wide range of pollen allergens, particularly olive pollen; Ole e 1 was again the dominant molecule, affecting 36 individuals (80.0%). Notably, Ole e 7 and Ole e 9 showed a higher frequency in this population compared with other populations. A comparable pattern of multisensitization was observed in Mostoles, although in this population Phl p 1 represented the most prevalent allergen, with a sensitization rate of 59.1%.

In Sagunto, the allergens most frequently detected were Ole e 1 and Ole e 9, both recognized by 17 patients (37.8%), whereas in Valencia Ole e 1 was the only leading allergen, being identified in 17 patients (37.8%), although the overall frequency of pollen allergen recognition in these two centres was lower than that observed in the remaining study locations.

In contrast, the northern cities of Lugo and Santiago de Compostela showed highly comparable sensitization patterns, with similar sIgE levels and prevalence rates. In both populations, Phl p 1 was the most frequently recognized allergen, with 21 sensitized individuals in Lugo (46.7%) and 23 in Santiago de Compostela (51.1%).

Finally, patients from Zaragoza demonstrated extensive recognition of pollen allergens, with a large proportion of reacting to many of the molecules included in the panel. Within this group, Phl p 1 and Lol p 1 were the most prevalent components, each detected in 27 of the 45 patients from this centre (60.0%) (Table S3, Figure 2).

Age an sIgE reactivity profiles

A consistent but non‐linear age‐related pattern was observed when analyzing sensitization frequencies and sIgE levels to pollen allergens across the eight study populations (Figure S1).

In the youngest group (0–5 years), sensitization frequencies and sIgE levels were generally lower across most allergens. Nevertheless, measurable sensitization was already observed in several populations and for specific allergenic components. This was particularly evident in Granada, Zaragoza, and Sagunto, where detectable sIgE responses to major olive and grass pollen allergens were present even at preschool age. By contrast, Valencia showed comparatively low sIgE levels across most pollen components, whereas Barcelona exhibited an early response predominantly driven by Ole e 1. Although sensitization at this age generally involved fewer allergenic components and lower sIgE levels than in the older age groups, these findings show that molecular sensitization was already detectable during early childhood in several study populations.

A marked amplification of both sensitization frequencies and sIgE levels was observed in the 6–10 year age group in most study populations This increase was particularly evident in Zaragoza and Sagunto, where several major pollen allergens showed a marked increase compared with preschool children. By contrast, Granada maintained high sIgE levels across all age groups, while molecular recognition became broader in adolescents. Notably, in some populations such as Móstoles and Sagunto, peak sIgE levels were already reached at this stage.

In adolescents (11–15 years), sensitization frequencies generally remained high, with broader recognition of multiple allergenic components. However, this expansion was not consistently accompanied by higher mean sIgE levels, which were often similar to those observed in the 6–10‐year age group.

Cat and dog epithelia

The distribution of sensitisation to dog and cat epithelial allergens shows variations among the eight regions analyzed (Table S4). The Fel d 1 allergen was consolidated as the major component in this group, with a total of 101 sensitized patients and average specific IgE levels that stand out in Mostoles (4.7 ± 9.5 kUA/L) and Valencia (3.4 ± 8.1 kUA/L). Notably, Granada had the highest absolute number of sensitized patients (n = 21), followed by Mostoles (n = 19). Furthermore, Fel d 7 was relevant also in Granada and Mostoles, while other allergens such Fel d 2 and Fel d 4 were barely recognized by the study population (0.84% and 8.73%, respectively).

Among dog dander allergens, Can f 1 was the most recognized component in all regions, particularly in Granada (n = 12) and Mostoles (n = 12). Other allergens such as Can f 4 and Can f 5 were significant in Granada (1.6 ± 5.8 kUA/L, n = 7) and Mostoles (3.9 ± 2.0 kUA/L, n = 7), respectively. Can f 4 was not relevant to the study population (2.25%).

In the PCA variable plot, dog and cat epithelial components were predominantly oriented towards positive PC2 coordinates, indicating a partially distinct loading pattern within the displayed PC1–PC2 plane (Figure S2B).

Molds (Alternaria alternata)

In general terms, the prevalence of Alternaria alternata sensitization was uneven across cohorts, highlighting the role of Alt a 1 as the major allergen in this group. The highest levels of specific IgE against Alt a 1 were recorded in the cities of Zaragoza (8.5 ± 15.0. n = 14), Valencia (7.0 ± 15.0 kUA/L; n = 12) and Sagunto (4.5 ± 12.0 kUA/L; n = 10). In contrast, in centres such as Barcelona (0.7 ± 3.4 kU/L), Lugo (0.7 ± 4.4 kU/L) and Santiago (0.4 ± 1.7 kUA/L), the relevance of this allergen was significantly lower. Regarding Alt a 6, sensitization was very limited in the entire study population, with an almost non‐existent presence in most centres (Table S4).

Characterization of sensitisation profiles using principal component analysis (PCA)

To explore the overall structure of molecular sensitization profiles and their distribution across recruitment centres, principal component analysis was performed using participant‐level sIgE measurements against the 51 molecular allergen components. PC1 and PC2, labeled as Dim1 and Dim2 in Figure S2, explained 14.2% and 9.1% of the total variance, respectively, accounting jointly for 23.3% of the variation in molecular sensitization profiles. The individual score plot showed considerable overlap among recruitment centres, although descriptive differences in their distributions were observed (Figure S2A).

The variable correlation plot suggested composite molecular sensitization gradients rather than discrete allergen‐specific axes (Figure S2B). House dust mite components, including Der p 1, Der p 2, Der f 1, Der f 2, and Der p 23, showed predominantly negative coordinates on PC1. Dog and cat epithelial components were mainly oriented towards positive PC2 coordinates. Grass pollen components, including Phl p and Lol p allergens, were predominantly located in the positive‐PC1/negative‐PC2 sector, whereas Ole e 1 showed positive PC1 and a comparatively more positive PC2 orientation.

These findings suggest that seasonal sensitization was not represented by a single principal component. Grass and olive pollen components showed partially distinct directions within the PC1–PC2 plane. The apparent third direction formed by the Phl p components reflects an oblique loading pattern resulting from their positive PC1 and negative PC2 coordinates and does not correspond to a third principal component.

Molecular architecture and clinical severity in allergic rhinitis

Analysis of the heatmap of the rhinitis cohort (Figure 3) revealed sensitization patterns that are not evident in individual statistical tests. The overall visualization shows an apparent hierarchical structure divided into three functional blocks.

Heatmap of sensitization intensity in patients with rhinitis. The color intensity (yellow to dark red) represents IgE levels transformed using log(IgE + 1). The upper dendrogram groups allergens by similarity of recognition. The rows are organized by different clinical phenotypes: Intermittent moderate (MIR), intermittent severe (SIR), persistent moderate (MPR), and persistent severe (SPR).

At the far left of the heatmap, there is a compact and dense block formed by Der p 1, Der p 2, Der f 2, and Der p 23. While in MIR patients this block appears mostly white or pale yellow, in the MPR and SPR groups, it becomes a continuous red band. In addition, SPR patients exhibited broader polysensitization pattern, as they are not only positive to major allergens but also show sensitization to less common mite allergens such as Der p 5, Der p 7, and Der p 21, which are practically absent in mild phenotypes.

A second cluster is also apparent in the grasses (Phl p 1, 5, 6) and olive tree (Ole e 1) groups. However, unlike mites, sensitization to pollen components is ubiquitously distributed across all degrees of severity (Figure 3). Major allergens such as Ole e 1 and the grass group (Phl p 1, Phl p 5) were observed to appear with moderate‐high intensity signals in intermittent phenotypes (MIR and SIR). The presence of hot spots in the upper rows of the map indicates that pollen sensitization is a baseline trait in our population that does not necessarily lead to the persistence of nasal symptoms on its own, unlike what was observed with the mite cluster. It is also noteworthy to observe large white areas in the heatmap that correspond to components such as Der p 10, Der p 11, or Blo t 10, consistent with their low clinical relevance in this cohort.

Within this broader molecular landscape, analysis of the global rhinitis cohort revealed a strong association between clinical severity and sensitization to the major mite allergen Der p 23. Figure S3 shows the distribution of Der p 23‐specific IgE levels stratified by rhinitis severity phenotype. Der p 23‐specific IgE levels differed significantly across the four groups (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 6.37 × 10−11). The moderate intermittent rhinitis (MIR) group showed predominantly negative or very low values, whereas the moderate persistent rhinitis (MPR) and severe persistent rhinitis (SPR) groups showed higher median and interquartile distributions. Pairwise comparison between the extreme phenotypes showed significantly higher Der p 23‐specific IgE levels in the SPR group than in the MIR group (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p = 3.27 × 10−7). For visualization, Figure S3 uses a square‐root‐transformed y‐axis, with tick labels expressed in the original kUA/L units. The dashed red line indicates the clinical positivity threshold of ≥0.30 kUA/L; its graphical position was calculated using the same square‐root transformation applied to the plotted values. In the SPR group almost the entire sample exceeds this threshold, reaching values above 25.0 kUA/L.

Allergen repertoire and polysensitization patterns in bronchial asthma

Analysis of the allergen repertoire in asthma patients through the study of 48 molecular components revealed descriptive differences according to clinical severity (Figure 4). In contrast to the profile predominantly associated with dust mites observed in rhinitis, the stratification of severity in asthmatic patients showed a significant correlation with pollen allergens (prevalence >5%). The Kruskal‐Wallis test identified four pollen components whose specific IgE levels differed significantly between asthma phenotypes (Intermittent [IA], Moderate [MAP], and Severe [SPA]): Pla l 1 (p = .0054), Phl p 2 (p = .0193), Phl p 12 (p = .0275), and Ole e 1 (p = .0451).

Heatmap of sensitization intensity in patients with bronchial asthma. The matrix represents specific IgE levels for 48 allergenic components in 100 asthmatic patients. The color intensity (yellow to dark red) represents IgE levels transformed using log10(IgE + 1) to stabilize the variance and allow clear visualization of the distribution across all severity ranges. The upper dendrogram groups allergens by similarity of recognition, highlighting on the right the block of significant pollens linked to severity (Pla l 1, Phl p 2, Phl p 12, Ole e 1). The rows are organized by diagnosis.

Visual inspection of the heatmap suggested a trend towards broader allergen recognition with increasing asthma severity. While recognition profiles of 1 to 3 components, often limited to mites, predominate in the IA group, patients with SPA tended to show broader polysensitization patterns that encompasses multiple protein families simultaneously. An apparent increase in the density of IgE signals for grass, olive and weed pollen components was also observed in the more severe asthma phenotypes.

Unlike mite allergens, which maintain a constant but non‐differential presence in all groups, pollen components accounted for most of the statistically significant differences observed across phenotypes. In the SPA subgroup, the heatmap suggested increased recognition of grass (Phl p 1, Phl p 5, Phl p 6), olive (Ole e 1, Ole e 9), and weed (Pla l 1) pollen components. Figure 4 also illustrates an apparent cluster of co‐recognition involving Plantago, Olea and Phleum allergens among patients with MAP and SPA, whereas this pattern was less evident in IA patients. These observations are descriptive and should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of patients with severe persistent asthma.

On the other hand, mites did not reach significance in the Kruskal‐Wallis test because of their intensity distribution being ubiquitous in asthmatic patients. The color spots for Der p 1 or Der p 2 cross vertically through all severity levels, consistent with a background pattern of sensitization rather than markers differentiating asthma severity groups.

DISCUSSION

This multicentre study provides a detailed molecular characterization of aeroallergen sensitization in a pediatric population distributed across several Spanish regions with distinct environmental contexts (Figure 5). By integrating multiplex IgE profiling with unsupervised statistical approaches, our data suggest that the IgE repertoire in childhood is structured into coherent molecular modules reflecting perennial, seasonal and epithelial allergen exposures.

(A) Köppen‐Geiger climate map of research centres in Spain. (B) Geographic and climatic characteristics of the recruitment centres. Source: AEMET standard climatological normal.

Principal component analysis showed that the first two dimensions explained 23.3% of the total variance (Dim1: 14.2%; Dim2: 9.1%), indicating that the displayed plane captured only part of the heterogeneity of the pediatric molecular IgE profiles. Rather than defining discrete allergen‐specific axes, the loading pattern suggested composite sensitization gradients. Mite components were mainly associated with negative PC1 coordinates, epithelial components with positive PC2 coordinates, and grass pollen components with positive PC1 and negative PC2 coordinates, whereas Ole e 1 showed positive PC1 and a comparatively more positive PC2 orientation. Recruitment‐centre distributions showed descriptive shifts compatible with regional exposure patterns despite the substantial overlap observed between centres. The participating centres were selected to encompass Atlantic (Cfb), Mediterranean (Csa), and semi‐arid (BSk) climatic settings across Spain. However, some geographically proximate recruitment areas, particularly Valencia and Sagunto and, to a lesser extent, Santiago de Compostela and Lugo, may share similar environmental exposures. Therefore, comparisons between centres should be interpreted with caution, as a certain degree of overlap in local climatic and aeroallergen exposures cannot be excluded. The PCA should therefore be interpreted as an exploratory visualization of molecular sensitization patterns rather than as formal evidence of geographical segregation.

Although direct airborne pollen measurements were not collected in the present study, the observed molecular sensitization patterns were broadly compatible with the known regional aerobiological context. Long‐term aerobiological studies have demonstrated substantial geographical heterogeneity in pollen composition and seasonality across the Iberian Peninsula. 18 Accordingly, the predominance of olive pollen sensitization observed in Granada and the greater contribution of grass pollen components in northern and inland regions are compatible with previously described regional pollen exposure patterns. However, these comparisons should be considered contextual rather than causal, as individual pollen exposure and contemporaneous airborne pollen counts were not assessed in the present study. Overall, these descriptive patterns are consistent with exposome‐based interpretations of allergic disease, whereby the IgE repertoire may reflect cumulative environmental exposures rather than isolated allergen encounters. 1 , 4 , 19 , 20

Within this molecular landscape, house dust mites constituted the dominant perennial sensitization module. Sensitization to at least one mite molecule was detected in 55.8% of the overall cohort, increasing to 83.0% in centres with the highest perennial exposure. The most frequently recognized molecules were Der p 2, Der f 2, Der p 23, Der p 1 and Der f 1, which together are consistent with the canonical molecular architecture of mite allergy described in previous component‐resolved diagnostic studies. 21 , 22 , 23 Group 1 and group 2 allergens represent the core of mite sensitization, whereas Der p 23 has been recognized as an additional major allergen with high prevalence among symptomatic patients. 24 , 25

The clinical relevance of this perennial module was particularly evident in the rhinitis analysis. Der p 23 sIgE levels showed a strong association with rhinitis severity, with a Kruskal–Wallis p value of 6.37 × 10−11 across severity strata. Pairwise comparison identified a highly significant difference between moderate intermittent rhinitis (MIR) and severe persistent rhinitis (SPR) (p = 3.27 × 10−7). Importantly, Der p 23 did not behave as an isolated signal but appeared embedded within a broader mite cluster involving Der p 1, Der p 2 and Der f 2. Severe persistent rhinitis was also characterized by the appearance of additional mite molecules such as Der p 5, Der p 7 and Der p 21, which were largely absent in mild phenotypes. These observations suggest that persistence and severity of rhinitis are associated with densification of the mite molecular repertoire, rather than sensitization to a single allergen component.

Age‐related differences further support this interpretation. Younger children often showed restricted mite profiles dominated by one or two major molecules, whereas older children displayed broader repertoires involving multiple mite allergens. This cross‐sectional pattern is consistent with the concept of molecular spreading, although longitudinal studies are required to determine whether the broader molecular repertoires observed in older children reflect progressive diversification of IgE recognition over time. 26 , 27 Such expansion of the IgE network likely reflects both cumulative environmental exposure and maturation of adaptive immune responses during childhood.

Although mites were strongly associated with rhinitis severity, their role in asthma appeared different. In the asthma cohort, mite allergens were detected across all severity strata and did not show significant differences between severity groups, suggesting that they represent a background perennial sensitization pattern rather than markers differentiating asthma severity. This observation likely reflects the high prevalence of mite exposure in many environments and is consistent with previous epidemiological studies showing that mite sensitization is a common background feature in pediatric asthma populations. 28 , 29 , 30

In contrast, the seasonal pollen module showed a stronger relationship with asthma complexity. Sensitization to pollen allergens was detected in 67.6% of the total cohort, making it the most prevalent allergen group after mites. In the asthma analysis, Kruskal–Wallis analysis identified Pla l 1, Phl p 2, Phl p 12, and Ole e 1 as being associated with increasing asthma severity. These findings suggest that selected pollen allergens are associated with asthma severity in this cohort. The descriptive heatmap analysis further suggested broader recognition of pollen components in the more severe asthma phenotypes; however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of patients with severe persistent asthma. Phl p 2 and Ole e 1 are major allergens from grass and olive pollen, respectively, and have been linked to clinically relevant respiratory allergy, particularly in Mediterranean areas. 31 , 32 In contrast, Phl p 12 (profilin) reflects cross‐reactivity and has been associated with broader IgE responses and more complex allergic phenotypes 33 , 34 , 35 The association of Pla l 1 further supports the role of additional pollen sources contributing to overall allergenic load. Because the heatmap analysis was exploratory, these descriptive patterns should not be interpreted as evidence of severity‐specific molecular signatures but rather as hypothesis‐generating observations that warrant confirmation in larger cohorts.

Cross‐sectional differences between age groups were observed across the studied populations with respect to pollen sensitization. The detection of measurable sensitization in preschool children indicates that molecular sensitization can already be detected during early childhood, particularly in regions with higher environmental allergen exposure. Older age groups generally showed broader molecular recognition and, in several study populations, higher sIgE levels than preschool children. In several centres, the highest mean sIgE levels were observed in children aged 6–10 years rather than in adolescents, suggesting that early school age may represent an important period for the development of molecular sensitization. In our cohort, adolescents generally exhibited broader recognition of allergenic components without a consistent increase in mean sIgE levels, indicating greater molecular complexity rather than uniformly higher antibody levels. Finally, the marked geographical variability observed across centres suggests that local environmental conditions may contribute to differences in both the intensity and timing of molecular sensitization.

The asthma heatmap revealed progressive densification of the pollen cluster from intermittent asthma to moderate and severe persistent asthma, with simultaneous recognition of molecules derived from Plantago, Olea, and grass species. Although this heatmap provides only a descriptive representation, the observed pattern is consistent with previous studies associating increased molecular complexity and polysensitization with greater asthma severity. 35 , 36 Nevertheless, sensitization patterns alone cannot fully explain asthma severity, as airway inflammation involves multiple immunological pathways. 37 Recent pediatric studies using molecular diagnostics have similarly shown that sensitization clusters correlate with clinical phenotypes but do not fully account for disease severity. 38 Accordingly, our findings suggest that perennial mite sensitization may represent a common immunological background, whereas broader recognition of seasonal pollen components may contribute to phenotypic heterogeneity.

Another notable finding was the predominantly positive PC2 orientation of cat and dog allergen components. This loading pattern was partially distinct from the mite and grass‐pollen directions within the displayed PC1–PC2 plane, but it should not be interpreted as a separate PCA dimension or as evidence of statistical independence. Sensitization to furry animals may nevertheless represent a partially distinct molecular pattern within the broader pediatric sensitization landscape. Molecular studies have demonstrated that component‐resolved diagnostics substantially improve interpretation of pet allergy by distinguishing genuine sensitization from cross‐reactive responses. 39 , 40 , 41

Alternaria sensitization represented a smaller but biologically coherent component of the IgE landscape. Alt a 1 clearly dominated this module, whereas Alt a 6 was rarely detected. This pattern is consistent with current knowledge identifying Alt a 1 as the principal molecular marker of clinically relevant Alternaria alternata sensitization. 42 , 43 , 44 Although fungal allergens did not dominate the clustering architecture in this cohort, Alternaria sensitization remains clinically relevant because it has been associated with asthma exacerbations and severe disease in several studies. 41

Overall, these findings are broadly consistent with recent multicentre molecular studies while also highlighting differences related to age, study design, and patient selection. The Spanish EXPOMOL study similarly identified regional molecular profiles dominated by grass pollen in Atlantic/continental areas, olive and cypress in Mediterranean regions, and mites in more humid environments, supporting the reproducibility of geographic heterogeneity across age groups. 45 Likewise, previous Spanish studies have reported high rates of polysensitization and identified pollens and mites as the predominant sensitizing sources, although associations between sensitization profiles and asthma severity differed from those observed in our pediatric cohort. 46 Similar regional heterogeneity has also been reported in population‐based studies from other countries despite differences in recruitment strategies and lifetime environmental exposure, supporting the influence of local environmental conditions on molecular IgE sensitization patterns. 47 Finally, the predominance of mite molecular allergens observed in our coastal centres is also in agreement with findings from a high‐exposure Valencian cohort, although the substantially higher prevalence reported in that study likely reflects its selected mite‐allergic population. 48

Beyond the consistency of these findings with previous reports, our study also has several methodological strengths. The integration of multiplex IgE profiling with unsupervised statistical analysis represents a major strength of the study. Multiplex platforms generate high‐dimensional datasets in which allergens are recognized in coordinated patterns rather than independently. Principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering capture these covariance structures and allow identification of molecular sensitization modules that would not be evident in traditional allergen‐by‐allergen analyses. 45 , 49 , 50

Several limitations should nevertheless be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional design prevents conclusions regarding individual sensitization trajectories over time. Environmental exposures were inferred from geographic context rather than measured directly at the individual level. The multiplex panel includes a finite number of allergen molecules and may therefore underestimate sensitization to less represented components. The PCA explained only part of the total variance, indicating that additional sources of heterogeneity remain to be explored. Although the overall cohort was sufficiently large to describe molecular sensitization patterns across the study population, the sample size within individual age and recruitment‐centre strata was relatively limited. Consequently, age‐stratified analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and require confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.

Finally, although very low total IgE concentrations in very young children and potential competition from allergen‐specific IgG have been discussed as limitations of multiplex IgE assays, these factors are unlikely to have materially influenced our findings. Only one child in our cohort was younger than 1 year of age and had a total IgE concentration within the measurable range, and only one participant had a total IgE concentration below 20 kU/L despite showing detectable allergen‐specific IgE. Moreover, none of the participants had received allergen immunotherapy, making clinically relevant IgG interference unlikely. Nevertheless, negative molecular results, particularly those close to the analytical detection threshold, should always be interpreted together with the clinical history and skin‐prick test findings.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, pediatric aeroallergen sensitization appears organized into coherent perennial, seasonal and epithelial modules reflecting distinct exposure environments. The mite module constitutes the dominant perennial scaffold, particularly in rhinitis severity, whereas expansion of seasonal pollen sensitization contributes to asthma complexity. These findings highlight the value of combining molecular diagnostics with data‐driven analytical approaches to better understand the heterogeneity of pediatric allergic disease and to support the development of precision medicine strategies in allergology.
