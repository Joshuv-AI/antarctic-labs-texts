Gait Characteristics in Acute Stroke and Transient Ischemic Attack Measured with Accelerometers
Authors: Ilona Ruotsalainen, Carolynn Patten, Timo Niemirepo, Juha Pajula, Heidi Similä, Milla Immonen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGait impairments are a well-documented consequence of stroke, yet the impact of transient ischemic attack (TIA) on gait remains underexplored. This study aimed to characterize acute changes in gait patterns within three days of stroke or TIA onset using an accelerometer, and to examine the relationship between gait patterns and questionnaire-based measures of balance confidence. We found that patients with TIA showed significantly lower variability in swing time, stance time, and step time than patients with stroke. Comparisons between patients with TIA and controls indicated that controls had higher mean step velocity and lower step time asymmetry. Additionally, compared with controls, patients with stroke demonstrated a slower and less temporally consistent pace, indicated by shorter step length, lower step velocity, and higher standard deviation (SD) for swing time, stance time, and step time. They also showed greater variability, reflected by higher step length SD, and more pronounced gait asymmetry, with greater asymmetry in swing time, step time, and stance time. The balance confidence was associated with gait parameters in patients with stroke and in controls, but not in patients with TIA. In both groups, higher confidence related to longer step length and faster step velocity; in stroke it was also linked to lower swing time variability and longer step and stance times, and in controls to lower step time variability. These findings demonstrate that TIA involves detectable neurological changes affecting motor control, highlighting the value of detailed accelerometer-based gait analysis for early detection of functional deficits.Keywords: transient ischemic attack, stroke, gait, walking, balance confidence, modified rankin scale

Introduction

Stroke, a sudden interruption of blood flow or bleeding in the brain, is a significant health concern affecting millions worldwide and can lead to long-term disabilities (Feigin et al., 2024). In contrast, transient ischemic attack (TIA) is a temporary, reversible interruption of blood flow to the brain, leaving no signs of tissue injury (Amin et al., 2023). TIA is thought to increase the risk of future strokes. It is well documented that stroke often has a critical impact on walking ability, significantly affecting a patient's mobility and quality of life (Algurén et al., 2010; Gittins et al., 2021). Moreover, in both stroke and TIA, the acute, post-event level of motor function appears to be an important predictor of recovery and future health events, emphasizing the need for early detection of gait and mobility impairments (Buvarp et al., 2020; Lodha et al., 2017; van der Vliet et al., 2020).

Mobility impairments following a stroke or TIA have been commonly assessed in clinical practice with methods such as questionnaires, measurements of walking speed, and simple tests for motor function. While these traditional clinical tools provide useful information, evidence indicates that comprehensive analysis of gait parameters can offer additional clinically important information regarding recovery and future health risks (Bower et al., 2019; Clark et al., 2023; Felius et al., 2025). This supports the integration of objective, instrumented assessments to complement traditional clinical evaluations.

Despite the well-characterized gait impairments after stroke (e.g., Balaban & Tok, 2014; Chen et al., 2005), research on gait impairments following TIA is sparse and inconsistent. Some studies report no significant gait alterations (Heshmatollah et al., 2020), whereas others have found deficits in tasks such as the Four Square Step test, Tandem Gait, and the Timed Up and Go test (Batchelor et al., 2015; Li et al., 2021). Notably, most research in TIA has focused on its prognostic value – linking walking impairments to future health events and cognitive decline (Ben Assayag et al., 2015; Kawajiri et al., 2019; Lodha et al., 2017; Perry et al., 2014; Ursin et al., 2015) – rather than providing a detailed, objective description of gait characteristics during the acute phase of TIA. Moreover, most studies on TIA largely rely on subjective assessments or timing-based metrics (e.g., task completion times), with few employing instrumented or accelerometer-based gait analysis (Heshmatollah et al., 2020). As a result, whether subtle gait abnormalities can be objectively detected during the acute phase of TIA remains an open question.

Early detection of mobility and gait abnormalities in the acute phase following stroke and TIA can provide valuable insights into disease severity, recovery potential and future health risks. Recent studies indicate that stroke recovery trajectories vary based on post-stroke motor function (Bonkhoff et al., 2022; Buvarp et al., 2020; Van Der Vliet et al., 2020). Consequently, individuals with varying functional abilities may benefit from tailored rehabilitation strategies (Dean et al., 2014; Smith et al., 2017; Stinear et al., 2017). In patients with TIA, gait and motor impairments have also been associated with a two-fold increase in subsequent stroke risk and with cognitive decline (Ben Assayag et al., 2015; Lodha et al., 2017; Ursin et al., 2015). However, these associations are often derived from questionnaires or task completion times, highlighting the need for objective, detailed gait assessments administered in acute settings. Such understanding is essential for identifying early markers of functional decline, assessing risk for future events, and guiding timely rehabilitation strategies.

To address these gaps, we implemented a wearable accelerometer-based gait assessment approach designed to be both easy to administer and suitable for use during the acute phase of stroke care. This approach enables early, objective detection of subtle gait disturbances, including in patients with TIA, for whom gait characteristics have not been well established. In this study, we aimed to characterize gait differences between patients with acute stroke or TIA and control participants, and to explore how these accelerometer-derived gait parameters relate to established questionnaire-based assessments of balance confidence.

Methods

Participants

This study was a part of Stroke-Data project and aimed to investigate gait characteristics in patients with acute stroke or TIA (within three days of hospital admission) and control participants. Inclusion criteria for both patient groups (stroke or TIA) in this study were: 1) a confirmed diagnosis of stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke) or TIA, based on the Finnish Current Care Guidelines, including confirmation by neuroimaging, and 2) the ability to walk 10 meters independently without the use of a walking aid. Exclusion criteria for participants included: 1) being under 18 years of age, 2) pregnancy, and 3) inability to provide voluntary, informed, and unambiguous consent to participate in the study. Additionally, control participants who reported a history of cerebrovascular disorders or were more than one year younger than the youngest patient with stroke or TIA were excluded from the analyses. To reduce potential confounding, individuals with long-term medication use (except for blood pressure or cholesterol), as well as those who smoked or reported other problematic substance use, were excluded from study. Patients were recruited from two hospitals in Finland by study nurses in collaboration with doctors. Recruitment took place within three days of hospital admission, after a confirmed diagnosis of stroke or TIA and completion of acute treatment. At the time of recruitment, all patients were medically stable and receiving care on the ward. Healthy controls were recruited through email lists, newsletters, and posted announcements.

A total of 217 participants met the study criteria and were enrolled. Among these, one patient with stroke declined to perform the 10-meter walking test, and the test was not recorded for another patient with stroke due to unknown reasons. Two control participants and one patient with stroke had either malfunctioning accelerometers or poor data quality. As a result, the final gait analyses included 212 participants: control (n = 113), stroke (n = 70), and TIA (n = 29). The study was approved by the North Ostrobothnia Hospital District Ethical Board (Finland) and by The Finnish Medicines Agency Fimea (FIMEA/2021/003738). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Demographics and Balance Confidence

Age, sex, and diagnosis were collected from the hospital's medical records. The remaining functional outcomes were collected at the hospital at the same time as the gait measurements. Participants’ body mass index (BMI) was calculated from self-reported weight and height using the formula: weight (kg) / height² (m).

Participants’ balance confidence was assessed using the Activities-specific Balance Confidence (ABC) scale (Powell & Myers, 1995). This questionnaire includes 16 items representing daily activities, where participants indicate their level of self-confidence (0–100%) in performing each activity. The average score was calculated to represent the balance confidence score.

Gait Measurement and Signal Processing

For the gait evaluation, participants were instructed to walk a 10-meter-long walkway at their habitual walking speed. Gait properties were measured using a tri-axial MoveSense accelerometer (36.6 mm×10.6 mm, weight 10 g, MoveSense, Vantaa, Finland) that was firmly attached to the participants’ lower back with an elastic belt. The accelerometer had a sampling frequency of 104 Hz and a range of ±16 g. Using a custom-made mobile application (Simila et al., 2018), the study nurse started and ended the recording at the beginning and end of the walking task (10-meter line). The walking task was performed with participants wearing footwear and walking independently, without the use of assistive devices, orthotics, or physical assistance.

To detect the start of the walking period we used the following criteria: when the standard deviation (SD) of three consecutive points of vertical acceleration signal is greater than 0.8 m/s2. To this time point we added 1 s to account for the acceleration at the beginning of the walking period. (Similar method is used in Muurling et al., 2020). For all participants the last 0.5 s of the data were removed to account for possible deceleration. The start and end time points of the walking task were also manually checked to ensure that the correct timepoints were marked.

For the analysis of gait properties we used vertical acceleration signal which was filtered with a 4th order Butterworth filter (0.25–20 Hz). Next we detected initial (IC) and final (FC) contacts (Figure 1). For each gait cycle initial (IC, heel strike) and final contacts (FC, toe-off) were identified according to McCamley et al., 2012. The IC and FC events were estimated from filtered vertical acceleration first by smoothing the signal with integration and then by differentiating the signal using Gaussian continuous wavelet transform (CWT). From this signal, IC events were identified as times of the minima. For FC events the signal was further differentiated using Gaussian CWT and FC events were identified as times of maxima of the signal. For the optimization (and cleaning) of the signal, IC and FC intervals had to be between 0.25–2.25 s (Najafi et al., 2003). In addition, IC and FC events were required to alternate so that when multiple IC or FC events happened one after the other, only the last event was counted.

For the step length measurements, the signal was first filtered and integrated as described above. Then, the signal was further integrated and to avoid integration drift, the signal was high-pass filtered using a 4th order Butterworth filter (0.5 Hz) (Zijlstra & Hof, 2003).

Gait Parameters

Fourteen gait parameters from the conceptual model of gait by Lord et al., (2013) measurable with an accelerometer on lower back, were extracted. These selected features cover five domains of gait: 1) pace (mean step velocity, mean step length, swing time variability, stance time variability and step time variability), 2) rhythm (mean step time, mean swing time and mean stance time), 3) postural control (step length asymmetry), 4) variability (step velocity variability and step length variability), and 5) asymmetry (swing time asymmetry, step length asymmetry, stance time asymmetry) (Lord et al., 2013; Moore et al., 2017). The following equations were used to calculate these parameters, where i and i + 1 refer to successive steps, l is leg length, and h is vertical displacement.

Step time = IC(i + 1) – IC(i)

Step velocity = step length/step time

Stance time = FC(i + 1)−IC(i)

Swing time = IC(i + 1)−FC(i)

Step length = 22lh−h2

Step length was calculated using an inverted pendulum model by (Zijlstra & Hof, 2003). This model illustrates how changes in the height of the body's center of the mass depend on step length. In this model l equals to leg length which was estimated using participant's height (height * 0.53) and h equals change in the height of the body's center of the mass. Primary gait parameters (e.g., step time, step length) were calculated as averages across all steps. For stroke patients, this meant that data from the paretic and non-paretic legs were combined, as the analysis did not distinguish between sides. Variability of the gait features was quantified as the SD of consecutive steps, and asymmetry was calculated as the absolute difference between consecutive (left and right) steps; however, these measures were not side-specific.

Statistical Analysis

Demographic characteristics were summarized for the entire study group and each subgroup using mean, SD, and range for continuous variables, and using count and percentage for categorical variables. Balance confidence score was summarized using mean and SD. Group differences in demographic characteristics and balance confidence score were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the post hoc Tukey Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test for continuous variables (age, height, BMI, and balance confidence score), and Pearson Chi-square test with Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p values for categorical variable (sex). Balance confidence scores were missing for one control participant, one patient with TIA, and ten patients with stroke due to timing constraints during the measurement protocol. Missing data (balance confidence scores or accelerometer data described in the Participants section) were not imputed or replaced using any other method.

Group differences in gait parameters were examined using generalized linear models (GLM). Each gait feature was analyzed using GLM to investigate the relationship between diagnosis and gait, controlling for the effects of age and sex. Asymmetry and SD of gait features with skewed distributions were analyzed using Gamma GLM with a log-link function, while gait features representing mean values were analyzed using Gaussian GLM with an identity link function. Tukey's HSD test was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons between groups. The Durbin-Watson test was employed to assess the presence of residual autocorrelation, and none of the tests was significant.

The relationship between balance confidence score and gait parameters was tested using Kendall's rank correlation, with age and sex included in the model as covariates. To account for multiple comparisons, p values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR).

The stroke group differed significantly from the control group across all metrics in the pace and asymmetry domains. Specifically, the stroke group demonstrated a shorter mean step length (B = −0.062, 95% CI [−0.090, −0.034], z = −5.24, p < .001), lower step velocity (B = −0.182, 95% CI [−0.255, −0.109], z = −5.84, p < .001), and higher variability, as indicated by increased SD in swing time (B = 0.692, 95% CI [0.402, 0.982], z = 5.66, p < .001), stance time (B = 0.751, 95% CI [0.428, 1.074], z = 5.43, p < .001), and step time (B = 0.764, 95% CI [0.478, 1.050], z = 6.24, p < .001).

Additionally, the stroke group demonstrated higher asymmetry in swing time (B = 0.553, 95% CI [0.246, 0.860], z = 4.20, p < .001), step time (B = 0.857, 95% CI [0.555, 1.158], z = 6.63, p < .001), and stance time (B = 0.529, 95% CI [0.224, 0.833], z = 4.05, p < .001). Step length variability was also significantly higher in patients with stroke compared to controls (B = 0.359, 95% CI [0.135, 0.582], z = 3.74, p < .001).

When comparing patients with TIA to those with stroke, the TIA group showed significantly lower variability in swing time (B = −0.597, 95% CI [−1.006, −0.187], z = −3.40, p = .002), stance time (B = −0.614, 95% CI [−1.069, −0.158], z = −3.14, p = .005), and step time (B = −0.589, 95% CI [−0.993, −0.186], z = −3.41, p = .002) relative to patients with stroke.

Finally, comparisons between TIA and control participants indicated that patients with TIA had a lower mean step velocity (B = −0.112, 95% CI [−0.215, −0.010], z = −2.55, p = .028) and higher step time asymmetry (B = 0.531, 95% CI [0.107, 0.955], z = 2.92, p = .009). Importantly, including height as an additional covariate did not alter these findings.

Gait Parameters and Balance Confidence

The relationship between gait parameters and balance confidence was tested for each group using Kendall's rank correlation (τb) (Supplementary Table 2). After correcting for multiple comparisons (FDR), significant associations were identified in both stroke and control groups.

In patients with stroke, associations between balance confidence scores and gait parameters were small-to-moderate magnitude (τb ≈ 0.23–0.30). Higher balance confidence was related to longer steps (mean step length, τb = 0.304, adj. p = .011), faster step velocity (mean step velocity, τb = 0.271, adj. p = .019), and lower swing time variability (τb = −0.233, adj. p = .033). It was also associated with longer mean step time (τb = 0.227, adj. p = .033) and longer mean stance time (τb = 0.236, adj. p = .033).

In contrast, no significant associations were found between gait parameters and balance confidence in patients with TIA. Among control participants, the balance confidence score showed small but significant correlations. Higher balance confidence was related to longer mean step length (τb = 0.171, adj. p = .039), faster mean step velocity (τb = 0.177, adj. p = .039), and lower step time variability (τb = −0.184, adj. p = .039).

Discussion

Using wearable accelerometers, we compared gait characteristics among patients with acute stroke who could ambulate 10 m unassisted, patients with TIA, and control participants. Patients with TIA showed lower temporal variability specifically in swing time SD, stance time SD, and step time SD than patients with stroke, indicating more stable timing. Relative to controls, patients with TIA exhibited a lower mean step velocity and higher step time asymmetry, whereas patients with stroke demonstrated a slower overall pace, greater variability, and increased gait asymmetry than controls. Accelerometer-derived gait parameters were sensitive enough to differentiate TIA from independently ambulatory acute stroke and to detect subtle deviations relative to controls. Interestingly, questionnaire regarding balance confidence indicated that specific gait parameters related to gait pace were associated with balance confidence in patients with acute stroke and controls, but not in patients with TIA.

Our findings on stroke are in line with extensive prior research documenting marked gait impairments—such as slower walking speeds, increased gait variability, and asymmetry—following stroke (e.g., Algurén et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2005; Gittins et al., 2021). Notably, all participants were independently ambulatory, representing a high-functioning subgroup that may not capture the full spectrum of post-stroke disability. Including individuals who require walking aids might have revealed more pronounced differences in gait characteristics, especially between TIA and stroke groups, and between stroke and controls in the rhythm domain. Indeed, prior literature indicates that gait in older adults using walking aids tends to be more irregular and unstable than independently ambulatory individuals (Härdi et al., 2014).

The novel aspect of our study, however, lies in its focus on TIA, where the literature is limited and inconsistent. The observed lower variability in swing, stance, and step times among patients with TIA, as compared to patients with stroke, suggests that while acute TIA may not induce the same level of motor disruption as stroke, subtle alterations in gait are nonetheless present as demonstrated by the comparison with control participants. This finding documents early subtle gait changes captured with wearable devices that may serve as prognostic markers for future stroke risk and/or mobility disability.

Compared to earlier studies, our findings align with and expand upon previous work examining gait in patients with TIA. Earlier studies have reported worse performance in functional gait and balance tests among patients with TIA or minor stroke, however these findings reflect a later post-event timepoint (e.g., 13 days after the event) as reported by Batchelor et al., 2015, and also a more heterogeneous sample using mixed patient groups (Batchelor et al., 2015; Li et al., 2021). While Heshmatollah et al., (2020) reported no significant differences in gait domains between patients with TIA and stroke-free controls, our results suggest partly otherwise. We identified that patients with TIA differ from controls in step velocity and step time asymmetry, pointing to subtle gait impairments not captured in prior studies using broader gait classifications. Importantly, our measurements were conducted within 3 days of hospital admission, demonstrating that detectable gait alterations are present in the acute phase after TIA. This highlights the need for further research focusing on the early post-event period to determine the optimal timing of gait assessment and to understand how factors such as symptom onset and medical interventions may influence gait characteristics. The current findings support the view that TIA involves neurological changes affecting motor control, and that more detailed gait analysis could be valuable for early detection of gait abnormalities not evident through conventional, clinical assessments. Early identification of these gait impairments may also contribute to prevention strategies for adverse events such as injurious falls and fractures (Tanislav & Kostev, 2020).

Notably, the current results demonstrate that using a simple, clinically feasible method, it is possible to detect subtle yet potentially meaningful gait differences among the high-functioning, independently ambulatory stroke group, patients with TIA, and control participants. These differences may go unnoticed using standard clinical assessments alone. As such, the findings provide preliminary support for the feasibility of accelerometer-based gait assessment to uncover underlying motor deficits in patients who are often considered clinically recovered.

In this context, the gait alterations observed in patients with TIA may serve as early biomarkers of motor dysfunction. Prior research has linked motor impairments after TIA with increased risk of subsequent stroke (Lodha et al., 2017; Perry et al., 2014), supporting the potential prognostic value of quantitative gait metrics. Incorporating such measures into acute care may improve early risk stratification and guide follow-up in patients who appear clinically recovered. Future research should examine whether specific gait features predict longer-term outcomes including recurrent vascular events, cognitive decline, or functional loss. In addition, longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether the observed gait deficits in TIA resolve over time or persist. Studies with larger TIA cohorts, integration with clinical risk scores (e.g., ABCD2), and multimodal assessments may help clarify the role of gait analysis in secondary prevention and post-TIA management.

In addition to group comparisons, we explored associations between gait parameters and balance confidence score. Several gait parameters (mean step length, step velocity, swing time variability, step time, and stance time) were significantly associated with ABC scores in patients with stroke, reflecting that greater confidence co-occurred with longer and faster steps and reduced timing variability . Despite methodological differences these findings are partly consistent with previous studies in chronic stroke populations showing relationships between ABC or Berg Balance Scale scores and gait velocity, stance time, and swing variability (Chang et al., 2021; Lewek et al., 2014; Schinkel-Ivy et al., 2017). In the control group, ABC scores were associated with mean step length, step velocity, and step time variability, indicating that even among healthy individuals, perceived balance confidence is linked to spatial and temporal aspects of gait. While no statistically significant associations were observed in the TIA group, the Kendall's τb coefficients for step length and velocity were of moderate magnitude, suggesting that a larger sample might reveal meaningful relationships.

The study incorporates elements that enhance its clinical relevance. We used a simple, nurse-administered gait assessment with a single wearable accelerometer, making the method feasible for use in acute hospital settings. By focusing solely on patients with TIA, without combining them with minor stroke cases, we ensured a more homogeneous sample. Additionally, including three groups (stroke, TIA, and controls) allowed for a more detailed comparison of gait characteristics across different clinical profiles.

However, some limitations should be noted. The TIA group sample size was modest, which potentially limits the statistical power. While the accelerometer approach was practical, it may not capture gait parameters with the same precision as systems like pressure sensitive gait walkway or 3D kinematics. Furthermore, the specific leg used to start the walking task, and steps were not labeled as paretic or non-paretic. Variability and asymmetry were calculated from consecutive steps, not on side-specific identification. This approach may have underestimated group differences, particularly in stroke patients, as separating paretic and non-paretic legs could reveal larger asymmetries. Nonetheless, this side-agnostic method was chosen for practicality in the acute hospital setting, where the affected side in patients with TIA may be unclear and deficits subtle. There were also some demographic differences between groups, although these were adjusted for in the analyses. Additionally, although exclusion criteria were applied to reduce the likelihood of major pre-existing health-related confounders, we cannot fully rule out the possibility that the observed gait differences in the TIA group reflect pre-existing characteristics rather than event-related changes. As an observational, two-site study with recruitment by study nurses within three days of admission, selection bias and variations between data collectors are possible, and the exact time from symptom onset to testing was not available, which may have introduced heterogeneity in recovery stage. Moreover, as gait was assessed at a single time point, our findings may not fully capture the variability or progression of recovery after TIA; repeated measurements across multiple time points would provide a more accurate representation of gait impairment and its evolution. Finally, as only independently ambulatory participants were included, generalizability to more severely impaired individuals is limited.

Conclusions

Our study revealed a novel and unexpected finding: patients with TIA exhibited subtle but measurable gait deviations during the acute phase, with performance falling between that of controls and independently ambulatory patients with stroke. In contrast, the gait disturbances in patients with stroke align with previous findings, confirming well-established patters of motor impairment in acute stroke. Together, these findings highlight the potential value of wearable accelerometers when identifying early motor changes that might not be captured through conventional clinical assessment such as questionnaires or by measuring walking speed alone. Associations between gait parameters and balance confidence, especially in patients with stroke, further support the clinical relevance of objective gait metrics. While further research is particularly needed to confirm these observations in patients with TIA and clarify their prognostic relevance, our results provide initial evidence supporting the integration of quantitative gait analysis into acute stroke care.
