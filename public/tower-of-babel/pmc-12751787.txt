Virtual reality intervention for visual perception in schizophrenia-a single blind pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Berkan Torpil, Mehmet Cetinkaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchizophrenia is characterized by both psychiatric symptoms and visual perception disorders. While virtual reality (VR) is increasingly used in schizophrenia rehabilitation, its impact on visual perception remains underexplored. This study aimed to investigate the effects of VR intervention on visual perception in schizophrenia.MethodsThis pilot randomized, single-blind controlled trial evaluated the effects of a structured non-immersive VR intervention using the Microsoft Kinect system on visual perception in people with schizophrenia, compared to treatment as usual (TAU) alone. The VR group also received TAU. The visual perceptions of all participants were evaluated with the Motor-Free Visual Perception Test-3rd Edition (MVPT-3). The interventions were carried out for 45 min, 3 days a week for 8 weeks.ResultsThe study was completed by 53 participants who were divided into two groups. VR group (n = 27, 38.00±10.79 years) and control group (individualized occupational therapy–based psychosocial program) (n = 26, 38.53±8.05 years). There was no difference between the groups in all parameters of MVPT-3 before the interventions (p > 0.05). Following the intervention, a significant improvement was observed across all parameters of the MVPT-3 in both groups (p < 0.001). However, the VR group demonstrated statistically larger effect sizes compared to the control group. In particular, the VR intervention yielded large effect sizes on all MVPT-3 parameters (η²>0.14, p < 0.001), indicating a strong and consistent impact on visual perceptual abilities.ConclusionsThe findings from this study provide promising preliminary evidence for the potential effects of non-immersive VR-based interventions in improving visual perception in individuals with schizophrenia.Clinical trials numberNCT06718556 (12/02/2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07664-9.

Introduction

The nervous system enables individuals to adapt to the outside world by processing and integrating sensory inputs from the internal and external environment. In this process, the sense of vision plays a fundamental role in the perception and interpretation of environmental stimuli [1]. Vision is one of the most important sensory abilities that enable individuals to recognize people, objects, and the relationships between them [2, 3]. The process of vision includes not only the perception of spatial relationships, but also the determination of physical properties of objects including shape, size, orientation, and movement [2, 3]. This process enables the evaluation of spatial relationships between objects and scenes, the interpretation of visual information, and rapid adaptation to changes in the environment [2, 3]. In this way, individuals can effectively act in daily life by considering environmental stimuli from a holistic perspective and can correctly comprehend the relationships between objects [2, 3]. Visual perception is a cognitive function that enables individuals to make sense of their environment through the processes of identifying, organizing, interpreting, and generalizing visual stimuli [2, 3]. Visual perception skills can be classified as spatial perception, visual discrimination, figure-ground, visual closure, and visual memory [4]. However, these perception skills can be significantly negatively affected by various pathological conditions such as neurological and psychiatric diseases such as schizophrenia [2, 5, 6].

Schizophrenia is a common and severe psychiatric disorder that causes significant impairments in individuals’ daily functioning. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), it adversely affects not only cognitive abilities but also visual perception, leading to notable difficulties in everyday life [5–8]. Studies have shown that individuals with schizophrenia have problems in the visual pathways, retina, occipital lobe and many related neuroanatomical pathways [5, 8–10]. These neuroanatomical disruption domains adversely affect visual perceptual processing in individuals with schizophrenia, contributing to distortions in reality perception, including difficulties in visual discrimination, form constancy, visual memory, visual closure, spatial orientation, and figure-ground differentiation [8]. Changes in visual perception in individuals with schizophrenia are evident at the level of oculomotor response to visual stimuli [11]. On the other hands, emerging evidence indicates that retinal abnormalities in schizophrenia are linked to higher-order visual deficits and psychotic symptoms [12]. Color perception deficits in schizophrenia have also been linked to antipsychotic medication, suggesting that drug-induced visual dysfunction may contribute to broader visual perception problems, consistent with the known neurophysiology of the visual pathways and the action of dopamine-blocking agents [13]. Another line of research has identified anatomical anomalies in the retina and established visual pathway dysfunctions in schizophrenia. Among the most reliable biomarkers are abnormalities in eye movements, such as reduced saccadic velocity and smooth pursuit. These impairments are thought to underlie a broader spectrum of visual processing deficits, including reduced contrast sensitivity, impaired sensory integration, diminished contour perception, and deficits in motion tracking. Furthermore, visual perceptual abnormalities—including visual hallucinations, distortions of color and form, and reduced contour integration—are reported in over 60% of individuals with schizophrenia, alongside other ophthalmological manifestations like reduced visual acuity, strabismus, and nystagmus [14]. These impairments also manifest as abnormalities in perceptual organization, including distortions of contrast sensitivity, movement, color, brightness, and shape, as well as difficulties in the perception of human figures. Moreover, deficits in the perception of emotional expressions, while primarily considered within the domain of social cognition, may also be influenced by underlying visual perceptual processes [5, 6, 15, 16]. These changes in visual perception are evident not only during acute episodes of schizophrenia, but also during the remission period of the disease [5, 6]. This suggests that visual perception disorders are part of the basic neurocognitive structure of the disease rather than a temporary symptom of schizophrenia [5, 6]. Additionally, researchers have mentioned the possibility of selectively predicting the development of schizophrenia spectrum disorders in early adulthood based on rates of visual perceptual impairment in childhood [17]. Therefore, abnormalities in visual perception can be considered as endophenotypes of schizophrenia [5]. In addition, researchers have noted that visual perception problems are common in schizophrenia, occurring in about one in every two individuals [5, 6].

While pharmacological treatment methods, psychotherapy and different rehabilitation approaches are at the core of schizophrenia treatment approaches, advances in technology have led to the development of alternative diagnostic and treatment tools for schizophrenia. One of these alternative working approaches is virtual reality (VR) [18–20]. VR is defined as a simulation in which a person can interact in a seemingly realistic and physical way using specialised electronic devices [16]. VR allows users to immerse themselves in a multidimensional environment, navigate and interact with objects in the environment [21].

The advancement of VR technology has expanded its application for various therapeutic purposes in schizophrenia [20]. Systematic reviews indicate that VR is effective for assessing and treating cognitive, social, and perceptual deficits in this population [18]. While the literature primarily focuses on reducing psychotic symptoms and enhancing social skills [18–20], fewer studies have targeted cognitive enhancement, particularly in the visual domain. Existing evidence supports the benefits of VR interventions for social participation, cognitive skills, and treatment engagement, with some studies suggesting superior adherence compared to conventional methods [17–20]. However, despite consistent findings on the significance of visual perceptual deficits in schizophrenia, the feasibility and effects of non-immersive VR interventions specifically targeting visual perception remain largely unexplored. To address this gap, the current pilot study was designed to investigate the feasibility and preliminary impact of a non-immersive VR-based intervention in addition to treatment as usual (TAU) on visual perception in individuals with schizophrenia, compared to TAU alone. It was hypothesized that participants receiving the VR-based intervention in addition to TAU would demonstrate greater improvements in visual perceptual abilities than those receiving TAU alone.

Methods

In the current study, we aimed to investigate the feasibility and preliminary impact of a VR intervention on visual perception in people with schizophrenia, conducted in the VR application laboratory of a public university between June 2023 and June 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of a Aksaray University (2022/08 − 07). Written consent form was obtained from all participants for the study. Additionally, the clinical trial record of the study can be accessed from the link “https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06718556”.

The current study was planned as a pilot randomized controlled study and the study was conducted using the CONSORT checklist [22]. Participants were randomly distributed using a computer-generated list of random numbers and assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the following groups: VR group (n = 29) or control group (n = 28).

The study evaluated 64 individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia. A total of 7 participants were excluded from the study due to schizoaffective disorder (2 people), active substance use (2 people), epilepsy (2 people) and severe visual impairment (1 person). A total of 57 individuals with schizophrenia who met the inclusion criteria and completed the assessment were included in the study. A total of 57 individuals with schizophrenia who met the inclusion criteria completed the baseline assessment and were randomized using Research Randomizer (https://www.randomizer.org) by assigning numbers. As a result of randomization, 29 participants were allocated to the VR group and 28 participants were allocated to the control group. As part of the study’s evaluation, participants who attended less than 80% of the intervention sessions (two individuals from each group) were excluded, and the study was completed with 53 participants (Fig. 1). The randomization process was carried out by a researcher who was independent of the study and did not know the assessment and intervention process.

Measurement

Sociodemographic information of the participants, such as age, gender, marital status, education and employment status, was collected. After the sociodemographic data were collected, the Motor-Free Visual Perception Test-3rd Edition (MVPT-3) was used to evaluate the visual perceptions of the participants before and after the interventions.

The MVPT-3 is a widely used instrument for assessing visual perception, particularly in neuropsychological evaluations of adults [4, 23]. The MVPT-3 is a paper-and-pencil, instrument-based test. The test consists of nine main subscale, namely visual discrimination (items 1–8), shape formation (items 9–13), visual memory-I (items 14–21), visual closure-I (items 22–34), visual discrimination (items 35–45), spatial orientation (items 46–50), figure-ground (items 51–55), visual closure-II (items 56–60) and visual memory-II (items 61–65) and consists of 65 items. Items 14 to 65 were administered in this study, as items 1 to 13 are designed for the assessment of children. Each item is scored as 1 point for a correct response, and in the adult version of the test a total of 51 items are administered, yielding a maximum possible score of 51. As the present study was conducted in an adult population, 7 of the 9 main subscales (visual memory-I, visual closure-I, visual discrimination, spatial orientation, figure-ground, visual closure-II and visual memory-II) were utilized, while the subscales designed for younger children were not administered. In line with the standard scoring procedure, the total score was calculated as the sum of correct responses [4]. In the study conducted by Metin and Aral for Turkish validity, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the test was determined as 0.85 [24].

The evaluations were made by the first author of the study (B.T.) and were blinded to the randomization process of the study.

Interventions

Participants in the VR group received the VR-based intervention alongside their TAU, while those in the control group received TAU only. The intervention lasted 8 weeks, with 3 sessions per week, each session lasting 45 min. This study focused on specific sub-functions of visual perception, including visual memory, visual closure, visual discrimination, spatial orientation, and figure-ground perception. Visual memory refers to the ability to retain and retrieve visual information. Visual closure is the capacity to identify complete forms from incomplete stimuli. Visual discrimination denotes the ability to differentiate similarities and differences among visual inputs. Spatial orientation involves awareness of spatial relationships between oneself and objects. Figure-ground perception refers to the ability to distinguish a visual stimulus from its background.

The intervention was implemented by the second author (M.Ç.), an expert in VR and psychiatric rehabilitation, who conducted a comprehensive preliminary study to gain full proficiency in the VR games. During the treatment, the researcher actively contributed to game selection and adaptation, ensured the continuity of gameplay, and provided feedback to participants throughout the intervention.

Virtual reality intervention procedure

VR intervention

The VR intervention was delivered using a Microsoft Kinect for Xbox 360 system. This device utilizes a depth-sensing camera and skeletal tracking technology to enable controller-free, full-body interaction with the virtual environment. Participants engaged with a series of commercially available exergames that were selected for their demands on visual perception, including dynamic visual tracking, visual memory, visual closure, visual discrimination, spatial orientation, figure-ground and hand-eye coordination. The system translated participants’ real-world movements in real-time, creating an immersive and embodied experience that required continuous visual monitoring and motor planning to successfully complete tasks, thereby providing repetitive and intensive practice of targeted visual-perceptual skills. The VR intervention was carried out in a 30 m² area covered with soft flooring to prevent injuries.

An expert panel (three academics with ≥ 10 years of experience in psychiatric rehabilitation and VR research) evaluated and selected the games for the program. Each game was analyzed in terms of required skills, therapeutic potential, and feasibility. A total of nine games were chosen. Although the content varied, all games required comparable levels of visual, cognitive, and motor skills. Therefore, they were considered therapeutically equivalent with respect to visual perceptual demands.

To enhance motivation, participants were introduced to all nine games at the beginning of the intervention and were allowed to choose their preferred game during sessions (Fig. 2). Despite individual preferences, the core visual perceptual functions targeted (e.g., attention, visuospatial processing, figure-ground discrimination, and visual closure) were consistently addressed across games. All participants attended all intervention sessions throughout the 8-week program.

Treatment-as-usual procedure

TAU procedure in this study was implemented as part of the standard care routinely provided at the Community Mental Health Center where the participants were recruited. TAU included regular psychiatric follow-up and medication management, along with an individualized occupational therapy–based psychosocial program conducted by trained therapists. Sessions were held three times per week for eight weeks, matching the frequency and duration of the VR intervention. The psychosocial program aimed to enhance daily functioning and visual perceptual skills through activities tailored to participants’ needs, focusing on visual perception subdomains (such as visual memory, visual closure, visual discrimination, spatial orientation, and figure–ground perception) and basic social skills (including listening, requesting, and expressing emotions appropriately). Thus, the TAU condition represents an active control, targeting visual perception and functioning similarly to the VR-based intervention. The study design allows assessment of the added value of VR by comparing VR-based training plus TAU to TAU alone. This structure is based on previous studies emphasizing that psychosocial and occupational therapy–based programs can improve cognitive-perceptual processing and social interaction in individuals with schizophrenia [26–29]. TAU was delivered with ongoing participant feedback and necessary adjustments throughout the intervention process.

Sample size

Considering the primary objectives of this pilot study, which were to assess feasibility and the preliminary impact of a VR-based intervention on visual perception, conducting a power analysis to determine sample size was not methodologically appropriate [30]. Instead, the sample size was based on a similar pilot study [31] and an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, resulting in a minimum of 24 participants per group.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with SPSS version 29.0 statistical software package program. Data are presented as mean±standard deviation. The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using Shapiro-Wilk and examination of skewness and kurtosis values. A p-value > 0.05 in the Shapiro-Wilk test and values between − 1 and + 1 for skewness and kurtosis were considered indicative of normal distribution. All variables satisfied these criteria, supporting the use of parametric tests.

Given that illness duration can influence the severity and progression of visual perceptual deficits in individuals with schizophrenia [5, 32], we used Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) to control for its potential confounding effects. The ANCOVA model included illness duration (calculated as current year minus diagnosis year) and baseline visual perception scores as covariates, with post-treatment visual perception scores as the dependent variable and group (Control/Intervention) as the fixed factor. Baseline visual perception scores were included to account for initial differences, as participants with cognitive deficits were excluded. Prior to conducting ANCOVA, key assumptions were assessed, including normality of residuals, homogeneity of variances, linearity between covariates and dependent variables, and homogeneity of regression slopes. We calculated partial eta squared to assess effect sizes, following Cohen’s classification where 0.01 represents a small effect, 0.06 a medium effect, and 0.14 a large effect [33]. Although antipsychotic medication dosage was not included as a covariate, all participants were required to have stable antipsychotic treatment for at least 3 months prior to the study, which minimizes potential confounding effects of medication changes on visual perception. Groups were also comparable in sex distribution.

Results

Compared to the normative data provided in the MVPT-3 manual, participants in both groups demonstrated reduced performance across all visual perceptual domains [4]. The average total MVPT-3 score at baseline in our sample was 18.65, significantly below the normative range of 24–26 for healthy adults. Subtest analysis revealed reduced performance in visual discrimination (mean difference: -3.2 points), figure-ground perception (mean difference: -2.9 points), spatial relationships (mean difference: -1.7 points), visual memory (mean difference: -1.4 points), and visual closure (mean difference: -1.5 points).

A total of 27 participants in the VR group (5 female, 22 male; mean age 38.00 ± 10.79, range 25–60) and 26 participants in the control group (5 female, 21 male; mean age 38.53 ± 8.05, range 26–59) completed the study. Mean illness duration was 14.7 ± 6.9 years in the control group and 14.2 ± 9.2 years in the intervention group. Statistical analyses revealed no significant differences between the groups in terms of age (p = 0.941), illness duration (p = 0.820), or sex distribution, confirming group comparability. All participants were on stable antipsychotic treatment for at least 3 months prior to the study. Discussion

This pilot study was conducted to examine the feasibility and preliminary impact of a VR-based intervention combined with TAU on visual perception in individuals with schizophrenia, compared to a control group receiving TAU only. The findings show that the VR intervention yielded improvements in all parameters of the MVPT-3 test, and the effect size was large. These results indicate that VR-based applications may provide significant improvements in visual perception. Such innovative intervention approaches have the potential to contribute to rehabilitation, particularly in clinical groups where cognitive impairments are common, such as individuals with schizophrenia.

Virtual reality

VR is a system designed to enhance the human senses and create the feeling of being in a real environment, and researchers have stated that the use of this technology in rehabilitation is increasing [21]. It can be observed in the literature that the main reasons for using VR in rehabilitation programs include improving repetition, providing an immersive experience, ensuring active participation in rehabilitation, offering enriched environments, and making rehabilitation enjoyable [21]. Many studies have shown the benefits of using VR in different rehabilitation settings. In addition, VR interventions are considered an important method in rehabilitation because their immersive nature enhances participants’ visual, cognitive, and motor skills and increases engagement [20, 25, 34]. In the current study, the VR intervention, provided in addition to TAU and compared to a control group receiving TAU only, was designed to encourage participants to actively engage their visual perception skills. The full-body interaction facilitated by the Microsoft Kinect 360 system likely enhanced engagement and provided intensive, repetitive practice of visual-perceptual skills through real-time visuomotor integration required by the game-based tasks. Based on these reasons and considering the growing importance of current technology in rehabilitation services, we chose to employ a VR intervention in this pilot study to explore its feasibility and preliminary effects on visual perception in individuals with schizophrenia.

The use of VR in the field of schizophrenia

When previous studies on VR-assisted interventions in schizophrenia are examined, benefits have been reported across cognitive, social, and visual perceptual domains. Chan et al. reported improvements in overall cognition, memory, and program engagement in participants who received a 10-session virtual reality intervention using the Interactive Rehabilitation Exercise System (IREX), compared to a control group attending the usual care programs in the setting (N = 27, d = 0.4–0.71) [35]. Similarly, d’Amato et al. found VR-based intervention enhanced attention, working memory, and problem-solving (N = 77, Cohen’s d = 0.41–1.55) [36]. Lindenmayer et al. observed small to very large improvements in visual learning, working memory, and reasoning (N = 78, d = 0.25–1.21) [37]. For social skills, VR has shown consistent positive results. Park et al. demonstrated that VR social skills training was superior to traditional role-playing for motivation, conversational skills, and assertiveness (N = 91, partial η²=0.07–0.22) [38]. Smith et al. found a VR job interview simulator significantly improved social skills (N = 30, Cohen d = 0.95) and increased job offers [39]. A notable study by Freeman et al. (2017) highlighted VR’s potential for symptom reduction, showing greater decreases in delusional conviction (22% reduction, p = 0.024, d = 1.3) and real-world distress (19.6% reduction, p = 0.020, d = 0.8) compared to classical exposure [40]. Overall, these findings suggest that VR-based interventions hold promise for improving cognitive, social, and visual-perceptual functions in individuals with schizophrenia. Our study demonstrated that the VR intervention yielded large effect sizes across all visual perception parameters assessed by the MVPT-3, including visual memory, visual closure, visual discrimination, spatial orientation, and figure-ground perception. VR offers a dynamic environment that engages cognitive, motor, and visual perceptual skills. We believe that the immersive nature of VR games promotes active participation in the treatment process. The preliminary findings suggest that positive changes in visual perception could be important for understanding the potential benefits of VR interventions in this population. Therefore, this study highlights the promise of VR applications in enhancing visual perception within rehabilitation and underscores the need for innovative approaches in the treatment of schizophrenia.

Visual perception problems in schizophrenia

In the current pilot study, it was observed that individuals with schizophrenia had problems in visual perception areas such as visual memory, visual closure, visual discrimination, spatial orientation and figure-ground. Schizophrenia is associated not only with cognitive and psychiatric symptoms but also with various visual perception disorders [41]. Furthermore, changes in visual problems cover a wide spectrum, from low-level vision changes such as visual acuity and contrast sensitivity, to moderate vision changes encompassing different aspects of perceptual organization, and to high-level visual function changes involving the effects of prior knowledge on visual perception [41]. On the other hand, these visual processing disorders in schizophrenia are not symptoms of generalized deficits and that visual perception problems can occur independently [42]. Furthermore, some visual perceptual deficits were present before the onset of schizophrenia [43]. Many studies have demonstrated that these visual perceptual deficits are significantly associated with higher-level cognitive and social cognitive functions such as visual working memory, object recognition, and facial expression recognition [5, 41, 44]. In addition, these problems in visual perception have negative effects on functioning in people with schizophrenia in their activities of daily living [5]. Approximately 66% of individuals with schizophrenia report experiencing visual perceptual abnormalities—which may also be influenced by visual hallucinations—highlighting the prevalence and clinical significance of this condition [45]. These findings confirm the presence of broad visual perceptual impairments in individuals with schizophrenia, particularly in complex visual processing tasks, supporting the rationale for implementing targeted visual perceptual training. We believe that this intervention approach is valuable for individuals with schizophrenia, since the immersive and interactive characteristics of VR inherently engage visual perception skills during the tasks.

Interventions for visual perception problems in schizophrenia

Visual perception disorders in schizophrenia are both diverse and prevalent; however, few studies have examined the therapeutic potential of interventions specifically targeting these visual processes [5, 41]. Although intervention studies in individuals with schizophrenia have shown improvements in visual perception, these studies are often limited by small sample sizes and the lack of a control group [41]. In one study that included a technology-based approach, a computer-based training package related to visual processing training was developed and participants who received this training were found to have significant improvements in visual learning [46]. The study results indicated that the visual training plus cognitive remediation was most effective for Visual Learning (Cohen’s d = 0.88), with moderate benefits observed for Social Cognition (Cohen’s d = 0.50) and Working Memory (Cohen’s d = 0.44) [46]. Contreras et al. (2018) conducted a pilot study comparing standard cognitive remediation alone with a combined program that included additional visual processing training. They found that integrating visual training into cognitive remediation led to greater improvements in overall cognitive outcomes [46]. In our study, the VR-based intervention yielded large effect sizes across all visual perception parameters, including visual memory, visual closure, visual discrimination, spatial orientation and figure-ground. Furthermore, our study was uniquely designed for visual perception rehabilitation, directly addressing this core cognitive deficit in schizophrenia that remains largely unexplored in existing VR literature. Therefore, VR applications may offer an innovative approach to the treatment of schizophrenia and have the potential to help individuals overcome deficiencies in their visual perception skills. The current pilot study findings also suggest that VR intervention offers significant potential for the treatment of visual perception disorders in schizophrenia. Considering the complex nature of visual perception and the various impairments associated with schizophrenia, VR interventions may represent an important therapeutic tool for addressing visual perceptual deficits in this population. Although the MVPT-3 manual reports some practice effects in short-term test–retest studies (e.g., modest gains over a few weeks) [4], these effects are generally small compared to the large improvements observed in our VR group. Furthermore, the control group, which underwent the same testing schedule, did not show comparable gains, suggesting that the observed improvements are attributable to the VR intervention rather than practice effects. Given the 8-week interval between pre- and post-tests, immediate practice effects would likely be attenuated.

Limitations

There are some limitations to this study. Primarily, the sample size was relatively small, which limits the generalizability of the findings and the statistical power to detect significant effects. As a pilot trial, the current study was not designed to provide definitive evidence of efficacy but rather to explore the feasibility and preliminary impact of the VR intervention on visual perception in individuals with schizophrenia. Future studies with larger, adequately powered samples are necessary to confirm these preliminary findings and to more robustly assess the efficacy of VR-based interventions. The relatively small number of female participants represents another limitation, as it restricts the generalizability of the findings, particularly concerning their applicability to women. Additionally, extended follow-up periods would be beneficial to evaluate the sustainability of treatment effects over time. Further research should also consider the integration of well-defined and comparable control conditions, as the control group in the present study received TAU, which represents a standard but active form of clinical care. While TAU provided an appropriate comparison condition, it may have offered general therapeutic benefits (e.g., clinical follow-up, medication monitoring, or supportive contact) that could have contributed to minor improvements, which should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. Although the game preference in the VR application process is determined according to the preferences of the participants, the limited number of games may be a limitation. On the other hand, the fact that the VR program in the current study cannot be customized to the participant may also be a limitation. Further studies can be conducted on visual perception where VR applications are preferred in a structure specific to the participants and needs. A limitation of this study is that it relied on a single primary outcome measure. Although the MVPT-3 has established reliability and validity in general and clinical populations, evidence regarding its psychometric properties specifically in individuals with schizophrenia is limited. A potential limitation of this study is that, despite our efforts to control for practice effects, some minor influence from repeated exposure to the MVPT-3 cannot be completely excluded. Additionally, since the games used in the VR intervention were widely known mainstream games, the lack of knowledge about the participants’ gaming experience and familiarity levels can be considered a potential limitation of the study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this pilot study suggests that a VR intervention consisting of 24 sessions over 8 weeks may have a positive effect on the visual perception of individuals with schizophrenia. The findings provide preliminary support for the use of VR-based rehabilitation approaches aimed at improving visual perception in this population. While these results are promising, further research with larger samples and rigorous study designs is needed to confirm the observed effects and clinical applicability of VR interventions for visual perception deficits in schizophrenia. Overall, this study highlights the potential of VR applications as innovative tools in the rehabilitation and treatment of schizophrenia.
