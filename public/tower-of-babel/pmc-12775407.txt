Dissecting Hhip1 Function In Vivo Using a Conditional Knockout Mouse Model
Authors: Ryuma Haraguchi, Riko Kitazawa, Yuta Yanagihara, Yuuki Imai, Sohei Kitazawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway is essential for organ development and tissue homeostasis; its dysregulation is associated with various congenital anomalies and human diseases. Hedgehog-interacting protein 1 (Hhip1) functions as a membrane-bound negative regulator of Hh signaling by directly binding to Hh ligands and limiting their activity in target tissues. While global knockout models have demonstrated the importance of Hhip1 during embryonic development, perinatal lethality has precluded investigations into its postnatal functions. To overcome this limitation, a novel conditional allele of Hhip1 (hereafter referred to as the Hhip1flox or floxed allele, in which exon 2 is flanked by loxP sites) was generated using CRISPR/Cas9-based gene targeting. Cre-mediated excision of exon 2 produced a conditional null allele (Hhip1ex2Δ) with a frameshift mutation that annulled the Hedgehog ligand-binding domain. Germline recombination through CMV-Cre recapitulated the known pulmonary defects of global Hhip1 knockout mice, validating the model’s functionality. Furthermore, using Prx1-Cre to generate limb-specific Hhip1 conditional knockout (cKO) mice, a previously unrecognized role for Hhip1 in maintaining postnatal growth plate architecture was detected. cKO mice exhibited progressive growth plate expansion and long bone overgrowth, together with sustained upregulation of Gli1 expression. These findings establish the Hhip1flox model as a powerful tool for tissue- and stage-specific functional studies of Hhip1 and provide new insights into the spatiotemporal regulation of Hedgehog signaling in development and disease. Importantly, this model offers broad utility for dissecting Hedgehog signaling mechanisms across diverse biological contexts and disease models.

Introduction

The Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in vertebrate development, organizing essential processes such as pattern formation, tissue specification, and organ morphogenesis [1–3]. During embryogenesis, the spatial and temporal regulation of Hh activity is critical for the proper formation of multiple organ systems, including the lung and skeletal tissues [4, 5]. Disruption of Hh signaling is associated with not only developmental anomalies but also pathological conditions in adulthood, including cancer, fibrosis, and metabolic disease [1]. Hedgehog-interacting protein 1 (Hhip1) is a membrane-bound feedback inhibitor of the Hh pathway that functions by directly binding to Hh ligands and modulating their diffusion and signaling range [6, 7]. The expression of Hhip1 is tightly regulated by Hh activity, forming a negative feedback loop that restricts ligand accessibility to responsive tissues [8]. Despite its central role in Hh pathway modulation, in vivo studies of Hhip1 function have been hampered because germline deletion of Hhip1 causes perinatal lethality attributed to respiratory defects [9], thus limiting our perception of the gene’s roles beyond early development, particularly in postnatal tissue growth, maintenance, and organ-specific pathophysiology. To overcome this limitation and facilitate tissue-specific investigation, a conditional allele of Hhip1 was generated in mice using a Cre-loxP approach [10]. This model enables precise genetic manipulation of Hhip1 in defined spatial and temporal contexts. Described in this study is the generation and validation of a conditional Hhip1 mouse line that provides a versatile tool for investigating its spatiotemporal functions in development and disease.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Conditionally targeted mice harboring loxP sites flanking exon 2 of the Hhip1 gene (Hhip1flox mice) were generated as a custom service by Unitech Co. (Chiba, Japan), using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene replacement [11, 12]. The sequences of the two sgRNAs and the donor oligonucleotide template are provided in Supplementary Table S1. A mixture containing sgRNA#1, sgRNA#2, Cas9 mRNA, and the donor DNA template (Acc65I–NotI, 4,316 bp) was microinjected into the cytoplasm and pronucleus of fertilized C57BL/6J zygotes. To generate a germline Hhip1 knockout allele, Hhip1flox mice were crossed with CMV-Cre transgenic mice (a gift from Dr. Pierre Chambon, University of Strasbourg) [13]. For limb skeletal lineage-specific deletion of Hhip1, Hhip1flox mice were bred with Prx1-Cre driver mice obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Stock No. 005584) [14]. All mice were maintained on a C57BL/6J genetic background; wild-type C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Clea Japan. Animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under climate-controlled conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and provided with standard chow (Oriental Yeast Co., Ltd.) and water ad libitum. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Research Committee of Ehime University (Permit No. 05-KU-38-16). Genotyping of Hhip1flox alleles was carried out using genomic PCR with three primers: a forward primer (5'-CTTAAGCCTATTTGGTTCAGC-3'), a reverse primer for the Hhip1flox allele (5'-AGACATGGCTCATCTGTGTC-3'), and a reverse primer specific for the Cre-mediated recombined allele (5'-TCAGCATTCTACCCACTGCATGG-3'). PCR amplification produced a 191-bp band for the wild-type allele, a 225-bp band for the Hhip1flox allele, and a 307-bp band for the Hhip1ex2Δ allele.

Immunoblot analysis

Lung tissues from newborn mice were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Cat. No. 08714-04, Nacalai Tesque Inc., Kyoto, Japan). The lysates were separated with 5–20% SDS-PAGE then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Immobilon-P; Cat. No. IPVH15150, Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The membranes were incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-Hhip1 (1:500; Cat. No. 11654-1-AP, Proteintech Group Inc., Rosemont, IL, USA) and anti-β-actin (1:1000; Cat. No. 017-24551, Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan). For detection, the following HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were used: anti-rabbit IgG (1:4000; Cat. No. sc-2004, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Dallas, TX, USA) and anti-goat IgG (1:4000; Cat. No. sc-2020, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc.). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (ECL; Cat. No. RPN2232, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and imaged with an ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Uncropped scans of the immunoblot images are provided in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Histological analysis

Tissues were dissected from mice, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 2 days at 4°C. The fixed tissues were dehydrated through a series of graded ethanol and embedded in paraffin. For bone samples obtained after birth, tissues were decalcified in 20% EDTA (EDTA·3Na) at room temperature with gentle agitation for 2 weeks, with the solution changed every 2 days, prior to embedding. Serial sections 4 μm thick were prepared for histological evaluation. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemical analyses were carried out according to standard protocols [15]. For immunostaining, sections were incubated with a primary antibody against Ki67 (1:30,000; Cat. No. 14-5698-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Signal detection was carried out using 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Cat. No. K3468, Dako North America Inc.). Toluidine blue (TB) staining was also conducted using standard methods [16]. The area of the growth plate exhibiting TB-positive staining was quantified using FIJI/ImageJ software (version 2.1.0/1.53c).

Quantitative assessment of cell proliferation in newborn lung

Cell proliferation was evaluated by quantifying Ki67–positive cells in lung tissue sections from control and Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ mice. Three biological replicates were analyzed per group. For each specimen, six randomly selected fields were examined at ×30 magnification, then Ki67–positive cells and total nuclei were counted. The proliferation rate (%) was calculated as the percentage of Ki67–positive cells relative to the total number of nuclei.

Measurements of bone length

Femoral length from the proximal end of the femoral head to the distal medial condyle was measured in millimeters.

RNA in situ hybridization

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were used for the RNAscope® assay [17]. RNAscope® in situ hybridization was implemented with an RNAscope® 2.5 HD Assay–BROWN kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. In addition, a BaseScopeTM assay (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA, USA) with a probe specifically designed for exon 2 of Hhip1 was employed to confirm efficient deletion of Hhip1 transcripts in global and conditional knockout mice. Positive and negative control probes were included in BaseScopeTM experiments. The RNAscope® target probes used were: Shh (Ref. No. 314361), Ihh (Ref. No. 1259141-C1), Gli1 (Ref. No. 311001), Hhip1 (Ref. No. 448441), the BaseScopeTM probe for exon 2 of Hhip1 (Ref. No. 1837201-C1), a positive control probe (Ref. No. 701071), and a negative control probe (Ref. No. 701011).

Statistical analysis

For comparisons between two independent groups, unpaired Student’s t-test were applied. For comparisons among three groups (Fig. 2), one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Error bars represent the standard deviation (SD) from the mean.

Results

Generation and validation of a conditional Hhip1 knockout allele

To investigate the role of Hhip1 in various cell lineages, a conditional null allele of Hhip1 was generated using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing [11, 12]. Specifically, exon 2 of the Hhip1 gene, which encodes a 193-bp sequence corresponding to amino acids 94 to 157 of the 700-amino acid Hhip1 protein, was flanked by loxP sites (Fig. 1a). Cre-mediated excision of exon 2 introduced a frameshift mutation that resulted in a premature stop codon at residue 159 (Supplementary Fig. S1a), producing a truncated protein lacking the Hedgehog ligand-binding domain (Supplementary Fig. S1b) [7]. Two sgRNAs (sgRNA#1 and #2) targeting the introns flanking exon 2 were co-injected with Cas9 mRNA and a 4,316-bp donor DNA template into C57BL/6J mouse zygotes (Supplementary Table S1). Founder males from genome-edited embryos were bred with C57BL/6J females, and germline transmission of the floxed allele (Hhip1flox) was confirmed by PCR analysis of genomic DNA from tail biopsies (Fig. 1b). Homozygous Hhip1flox/flox mice were viable, fertile, and displayed no overt phenotypic abnormalities compared with heterozygous or wild-type littermates. To verify Cre-mediated recombination of the Hhip1flox allele, Hhip1flox/flox mice were crossed with CMV-Cre transgenic mice expressing Cre recombinase ubiquitously [13]. Mice carrying the recombined Hhip1ex2Δ allele were identified by genomic PCR (Fig. 1b). Heterozygous Hhip1ex2Δ/+ mice were intercrossed to obtain homozygous Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ mutants. To assess Hhip1 protein expression, Western blot analysis was implemented on lung tissue lysates using an antibody targeting the C-terminal domain of Hhip1. A clear signal was detected in wild-type samples, while no signal was observed in homozygous Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ mutants, suggesting a loss of specific protein expression consistent with the absence of the structural domain encoded by the Hhip1ex2Δ transcript (Fig. 1c).

Homozygous germline deletion of Hhip1 conditional null allele results in neonatal lethality with developmental lung anomalies

To investigate the role of Hhip1 in vivo, homozygous null mice (Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ) were generated by intercrossing heterozygous Hhip1ex2Δ/+ animals. The genotypic distribution among newborns (Hhip1+/+ : Hhip1ex2Δ/+ : Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ) approximated the expected Mendelian ratio of 1:2:1 (Supplementary Fig. S2). All Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ homozygous mutants died, however, within a few hours after birth. Gross examination revealed that Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ neonates exhibited increased body size (Fig. 2a) and significantly greater body weight compared with wild-type controls (Fig. 2b). No notable differences in body size or weight were observed between wild-type and heterozygous littermates. Hhip1 deficiency has caused neonatal lethality due to respiratory failure [9], and pursuant to that finding a detailed analysis of lung development in Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ embryos was implemented (Fig. 3). On embryonic day 14.5 (E14.5) and at birth (Newborn), lungs from homozygous mutants were markedly smaller than those from wild-type controls (Fig. 3a–d). As described in standard mouse anatomy [18], the right lung is subdivided into four lobes (cranial, middle, caudal, and accessory), while the left lung has a single lobe. In contrast, Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ mutants exhibited severe hypoplasia of the right lung, with only a single small residual lobe remaining (Fig. 3b, d). Histological analysis at the newborn stage revealed a substantial increase in the proportion of Ki67-positive mesenchymal cells within the alveolar septa (hereafter referred to as alveolar septal mesenchymal cells), together with a pronounced reduction in airway space (Fig. 3g, h), suggesting disrupted alveolar morphogenesis and increased cell proliferation in the absence of Hhip1. At E14.5, Ki67-positive nuclei were predominantly observed in alveolar septal mesenchymal cells, indicating that these mesenchymal populations were actively proliferating in both genotypes (Supplementary Fig. S4). No overt differences were detected between wild-type and Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ mutant lungs. To further explore the molecular basis of these developmental abnormalities, the expression of Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway components was analyzed (Fig. 4). As previously proposed, Hhip1 functions as a direct antagonist of Hh signaling, and its loss is predictable in the derepression of Hh target genes such as Gli1[6, 8, 9, 19]. In wild-type lungs at E12.5, Gli1 expression was localized at the mesenchyme surrounding the developing airway epithelium, consistent with Shh expression in the epithelial layer (Fig. 4a). In contrast, Hhip1 expression was spatially restricted to a subset of mesenchymal cells located immediately adjacent to the Shh-expressing epithelium (Fig. 4c). In addition, Hhip1-positive cells were also observed along the outermost mesenchymal layer at the lobar boundary (Fig. 4c, blue arrows), consistent with a potential role in restricting Hh signal diffusion at sites of interlobar demarcation. Because the probe used in Fig. 4 was designed to recognize multiple exons, residual signals may remain detectable even in the Hhip1 ex2Δ/ex2Δ null background. To confirm efficient deletion, we further performed a BaseScopeTM assay with an exon 2-specific probe, which demonstrated complete loss of Hhip1 transcripts in the null mice (Supplementary Fig. S5). In Hhip1-deficient embryos, Shh expression in the airway epithelium remained unchanged (Fig. 4b), and no increase in mesenchymal expression was observed. However, Gli1 expression in the surrounding mesenchyme was markedly upregulated and spatially expanded (Fig. 4e), indicating enhanced activation of the Shh signaling pathway. These findings are consistent with previous reports [9] and support the role of Hhip1 as a negative regulator of Hedgehog signaling during lung development.

Loss of Hhip1 function induces morphological and molecular alterations in the epiphyseal growth plate

Given the well-established role of Hedgehog signaling in endochondral ossification and long bone development, investigated was whether deletion of Hhip1 affects growth plate architecture in neonatal mice (Fig. 5). Histological examination of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stains revealed a pronounced expansion of the femoral growth plate in Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ neonates compared with that of wild-type littermates (Fig. 5a, b). The overall height of the epiphyseal cartilage was significantly increased, with notable widening of both the proliferative and hypertrophic zones, as demarcated by red lines in the figure panels. To explore the molecular basis of these morphological changes, RNAscope in situ hybridization was implemented targeting key components of the Hedgehog signaling pathway. In wild-type neonatal femoral sections, Ihh mRNA was strongly expressed in the hypertrophic zone (Supplementary Fig. S4a), consistent with its established role in promoting chondrocyte hypertrophy and coordinating growth plate development [20, 21]. Hhip1 mRNA was detected in resting zone chondrocytes, the periosteum, and the primary spongiosa (Supplementary Fig. S4b), consistent with its proposed function as a spatial restrictor of Hedgehog signaling activity. Importantly, the expression of Gli1—a canonical transcriptional target of Hedgehog signaling—was markedly upregulated in Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ mice, particularly within growth plate chondrocytes and cells of the primary spongiosa (Fig. 5c, d). This upregulation indicates heightened Hedgehog pathway activity in the absence of Hhip1, further supporting its role as a negative feedback regulator of the pathway [6, 22]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that loss of Hhip1 results in both structural and molecular alterations in the epiphyseal growth plate. These changes are likely attributable to the derepression of Ihh-mediated signaling and subsequent overactivation of downstream effectors such as Gli1, underscoring the critical role of Hhip1 in modulating skeletal development.

Conditional inactivation of Hhip1 in the limb skeletal lineage results in the long bone elongation phenotype

To investigate the postnatal role of Hhip1 in skeletal development, Hhip1flox mice were crossed with Prx1-Cre transgenic animals to generate limb-specific Hhip1 conditional knockout (cKO) mice, which drive Cre expression in the limb bud mesenchyme [14]. This approach circumvented the perinatal lethality observed in global knockouts and allowed for the assessment of long-term skeletal phenotypes in the appendicular skeleton. Gross examination of femurs from 10- and 53-week-old Prx1-Cre; Hhip1flox/flox mice displayed visibly elongated long bones compared with those of age-matched wild-type (WT) littermates (Fig. 6a, c). Quantitative analysis confirmed a statistically significant increase in femur length in cKO mice at both stages (Fig. 6b, d). Notably, body weight did not differ significantly between WT and cKO mice at either age, indicating that the skeletal phenotype was not attributable to generalized somatic overgrowth (Fig. 6b, d). To determine whether the increased bone length was associated with persistent alterations in growth plate architecture, the distal femoral epiphysis was examined at both 10 and 53 weeks of age using Toluidine blue staining. This analysis was guided by our previous findings in Hhip1 global knockout mice, which exhibited neonatal growth plate expansion and elevated Gli1 expression—hallmarks of enhanced Hedgehog signaling activity. Consistent with the phenotype observed in global knockouts, Prx1-Cre; Hhip1flox/flox mice also displayed visibly expanded growth plates at both postnatal stages compared with those of WT controls (Fig. 6e–h). Morphometric quantification revealed a significant increase in the Toluidine blue-positive area in the cKO mice (Fig. 6i), suggesting sustained and potentially dysregulated chondrocyte proliferation and hypertrophy extending beyond the neonatal period. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Hhip1 plays a critical role in not only establishing growth plate architecture during development, but also maintaining its integrity and regulating Hedgehog signaling dynamics throughout postnatal skeletal maturation. To corroborate these findings, we further confirmed efficient deletion of Hhip1 in Prx1-Cre; Hhip1 cKO mice. We performed BaseScope analysis with an exon 2–specific probe. This confirmed the absence of Hhip1 transcripts in the epiphyseal cartilage, whereas signal remained detectable in the epidermis where Prx1-Cre is inactive (Supplementary Fig. S7). Consistently, expression of the Hedgehog target gene Gli1 was markedly upregulated in the epiphysis of cKO mice (Supplementary Fig. S8), supporting enhanced Hedgehog signaling activity following Hhip1 loss in the skeletal lineage.

Discussion

In this study, a conditional null Hhip1 allele mouse model was generated and characterized enabling the analysis of its spatial and temporal functions during development. Using this model, previously reported pulmonary defects were confirmed and, more importantly, a novel role of Hhip1 was identified in regulating postnatal growth plate organization. Such a system provides an effective tool for exploring tissue- and stage-specific roles of Hhip1.

Hhip1 deficiency and neonatal lung defects

Consistent with previous studies, our analysis of Hhip1 conditional null mice (Hhip1ex2Δ/ex2Δ) revealed perinatal lethality together with pulmonary hypoplasia and respiratory failure, reinforcing the critical role of Hhip1 in lung development [9]. Lung morphogenesis is organized through complex epithelial-mesenchymal interactions, in which Hedgehog signaling—particularly Sonic hedgehog (Shh)—plays a central regulatory role. During early stages of lung branching, Shh is secreted from the airway epithelium and signals surrounding mesenchymal cells to coordinate proliferation, branching morphogenesis, and alveolar septation [5, 23]. The reduction in airway space, increased Ki67 labeling index (LI) in alveolar septal mesenchymal cells, and expanded Gli1 expression observed in Hhip1-deficient lungs collectively underscore its role in restricting the spatial range of Hedgehog signaling during a critical developmental time window. Although Ki67-positive alveolar septal mesenchymal cells were already abundant at E14.5 in both genotypes (Supplementary Fig. S4), clear differences in proliferation became evident only at the newborn stage, coinciding with impaired alveolar morphogenesis. Notably, epithelial Shh expression remained unaltered, suggesting that Hhip1 functions primarily to limit the spatial spread of Hh signals than their production. This spatial restriction appears to be essential for proper epithelial-mesenchymal patterning during lung morphogenesis. The localization of Hhip1-positive cells along the lobar boundary further suggests a boundary-forming role, potentially restricting the spread of Hedgehog signals between adjacent lung lobes during branching morphogenesis. Thus, taken together, these findings reaffirm Hhip1’s role as a key feedback inhibitor of Hh signaling and further validate our conditional null allele as a reliable model for investigating Hhip1’s developmental functions in vivo.

Persistent growth plate activity and skeletal overgrowth in the absence of Hhip1

Endochondral ossification, the process by which long bones grow in length, is tightly regulated by a highly organized growth plate architecture comprising resting, proliferative, and hypertrophic chondrocyte zones [24, 25]. Indian hedgehog (Ihh), secreted from prehypertrophic chondrocytes, acts as a central morphogen in this system by promoting chondrocyte proliferation and coordinating feedback regulation through PTHrP signaling [20, 21]. The spatiotemporal distribution of Hh signaling activity is therefore critical for maintaining growth plate homeostasis and ensuring balanced skeletal elongation. This study demonstrates that Hhip1 deficiency impairs the regulation of endochondral ossification. In neonatal global knockouts, marked expansion of the epiphyseal growth plate and upregulation of Gli1 were observed, consistent with hyperactivation of the Hedgehog (Hh) pathway. These findings align with earlier studies identifying the Hedgehog pathway as a central regulator organizing chondrocyte proliferation and hypertrophy during growth plate development. Interestingly, the converse manipulation—transgenic overexpression of Hhip1—has also been shown to disrupt skeletal development, leading to a reduction in the zone of undifferentiated chondrocytes within the growth plate [6]. These reciprocal phenotypes underscore the importance of tightly regulated Hhip1 expression in maintaining growth plate architecture. Importantly, by employing a limb-specific conditional knockout model, the perinatal lethality associated with global Hhip1 loss was circumvented and the long-term skeletal effects were examined. The persistence of growth plate enlargement and femoral overgrowth in Prx1-Cre; Hhip1flo/flox mice—without corresponding changes in body weight—suggests that Hhip1 is required for not only embryonic skeletal patterning but also restraining postnatal chondrocyte activity. This sustained growth phenotype implicates Hhip1 in the active maintenance of growth plate homeostasis and highlights its role in preventing prolonged or dysregulated longitudinal bone elongation beyond early developmental periods. These observations collectively support a model in which Hhip1 modulates the spatial and temporal dynamics of Ihh-mediated signaling to establish appropriate zones of chondrocyte proliferation and hypertrophy. In the absence of Hhip1, prolonged Gli1 activation may override normal feedback constraints, resulting in dysregulated endochondral growth. Notably, comparable skeletal phenotypes—including growth plate expansion and excessive bone elongation—have been reported in mouse models with constitutive activation of the Hedgehog pathway, such as Ptch1-deficient mice [26]. This interpretation is further supported by lineage tracing studies using Gli1-CreER mice, which have demonstrated that Gli1-expressing cells contribute to the long-term maintenance of the growth plate and serve as a pool of skeletal progenitors [15, 27–29]. The findings in the present study suggest that Hhip1 plays a critical role in delimiting the activity and fate of these Hh-responsive cells, thereby ensuring proper termination of chondrocyte expansion and preserving growth plate architecture during postnatal skeletal maturation.

Beyond its developmental roles in the lung and skeleton, Hhip1 has also been implicated in a range of human diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interstitial lung disease (ILD), diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and gastrointestinal disorders [30–33]. Our conditional knockout model thus not only enables precise dissection of Hhip1 function during development, but also provides a versatile platform for future investigations into the mechanisms by which Hhip1 contributes to the pathogenesis of diverse diseases involving aberrant Hedgehog signaling.

This study reveals a previously unrecognized role for Hhip1 in maintaining postnatal growth plate architecture and regulating skeletal growth through the spatiotemporal control of Hedgehog signaling. Moreover, our conditional Hhip1 knockout model faithfully recapitulates the established pulmonary phenotypes associated with Hhip1 deficiency. Given the broad disease relevance of Hhip1, this conditional system provides a versatile tool for investigating its roles in both development and pathology. This model may serve as a valuable platform for further research into tissue-specific roles of Hhip1 and therapeutic targeting of Hedgehog signaling in disease.

Terminology

Throughout the manuscript, terminology has been standardized as follows: Hhip1flox refers to the floxed (conditional) allele; Hhip1ex2Δ refers to the conditional null allele after Cre recombination; global knockout refers to both conventional Hhip1 knockout mice generated by embryonic stem cell targeting and CMV-Cre-mediated germline deletion; cKO mice refers to Prx1-Cre; Hhip1flox/flox mice. All usage of conditional knockout, conditional null, and allele designations has been harmonized for clarity.
