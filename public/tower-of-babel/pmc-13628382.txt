Symptom clusters and quality of life among patients with thyroid cancer during early survivorship: a national multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Jie Gao, Xin Wang, Jiayan Cao, Yanyan Chen, Yi Tu, Xiaohua Song, Yingchun Zhou, Duo Tao, Lianjie Zhang, Jing Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with thyroid cancer commonly experience multiple symptoms during early survivorship after thyroidectomy. However, evidence regarding symptom clusters during this period remains limited, hindering the development of targeted interventions to support recovery and improve quality of life.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore symptom clusters among patients with thyroid cancer at 1-month postoperative follow-up and analyze their associations with quality of life.DesignA national multicenter cross-sectional study.Setting and participantsA total of 1093 patients with thyroid cancer from 29 hospitals in 24 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions in China were enrolled for this study. Participants completed the questionnaire between June and December 2024.MethodsSymptom burden was assessed using the M.D. Anderson Symptom Inventory–Thyroid Cancer module, and quality of life was measured using the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire. Exploratory factor analysis was used to identify symptom clusters. Mann–Whitney U tests and Kruskal–Wallis H tests were performed to examine differences in quality of life across demographic and clinical characteristics. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with quality of life.ResultsThree symptom clusters were identified during early survivorship following thyroidectomy: a psychological-somatic symptom cluster, a thyroid hormone-related symptom cluster, and a surgery-related symptom cluster. In the multiple linear regression model, all 3 symptom clusters were independently associated with poorer quality of life, with the psychological-somatic symptom cluster showing the strongest association (β = −0.596, 95% CI, −0.713, −0.581). Female sex (β = −0.051, 95% CI, −2.795, −0.253) and total thyroidectomy (β = −0.058, 95% CI, −2.746, −0.390) were also independently associated with poorer quality of life.ConclusionsSymptom clusters are prevalent among patients with thyroid cancer during early survivorship and are significantly associated with poorer quality of life. The psychological-somatic symptom cluster showed the strongest association, while female patients and those undergoing total thyroidectomy may be at increased risk for poorer quality of life. Early identification of symptom clusters and vulnerable patient groups may facilitate more targeted postoperative assessment and supportive management.

Identification of symptom clusters during early survivorship supports a shift from individual symptom management toward a symptom cluster–based approach. Particular attention should be given to the psychological-somatic symptom cluster, which showed the strongest association with quality of life. Routine follow-up should include screening for emotional distress, fatigue, sleep disturbance, pain, and appetite-related symptoms. Risk-stratified and multidisciplinary survivorship care, especially for female patients and those undergoing total thyroidectomy, may facilitate targeted support, optimize resource allocation, and improve recovery outcomes.

Introduction

With advances in medical diagnostic technologies, the incidence of thyroid cancer has increased steadily worldwide.1 In 2022, approximately 821,173 new cases were diagnosed globally, accounting for 4.1% of all newly diagnosed cancers.2 China alone contributed more than half of the global thyroid cancer burden, with approximately 466,100 new cases reported.3 Although thyroid cancer is associated with a favorable prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate of up to 92.9% in China,4 the growing population of survivors has made survivorship care an increasingly important clinical issue.

Nevertheless, many patients with thyroid cancer experience persistent physical and psychological symptoms during the early postoperative recovery period, which may substantially impair quality of life. The first month following thyroidectomy represents a clinically sensitive transition phase characterized not only by surgical recovery but also by endocrine adaptation and psychosocial adjustment.5,6 During this period, fluctuations in thyroid hormone levels, physiological stress responses, and changes in daily functioning may contribute to complex and interrelated symptom experiences. Fatigue7,8 and sleep disturbance9–11 are among the most frequently reported symptoms during early survivorship. In addition, patients may experience surgery-related and disease-specific symptoms, including voice changes, scar discomfort, autonomic nervous system disturbances, and psychological distress.12

Emerging evidence suggests that these symptoms rarely occur independently but may co-occur as symptom clusters, which are increasingly recognized as multidimensional phenomena shaped by shared biological, psychological, and treatment-related processes.13 Compared with individual symptoms, symptom clusters may exert a greater impact on patients’ functioning and quality of life because symptoms within a cluster can interact and mutually reinforce one another. Consequently, symptom cluster research has become an important approach for understanding the complexity of symptom experiences and developing more targeted symptom management strategies.

Previous studies have identified several symptom clusters among patients with thyroid cancer. For example, Li et al.14 identified four symptom clusters in patients with thyroid cancer during the first 1-3 days after surgery, including throat–voice, numbness–emotional, swallowing-related, and somatic stress symptom clusters. In contrast, de Rooij et al.15 reported that patients with thyroid cancer during long-term survivorship exhibited psychological, fatigue–pain–sleep disturbance, and gastrointestinal symptom clusters. These findings suggest that the composition and structure of symptom clusters may vary according to treatment exposure, survivorship stage, and study characteristics such as sample size and patient population. However, current evidence regarding symptom clusters during the early survivorship phase of thyroid cancer remains limited, particularly during the first postoperative month, a critical period marked by ongoing physical recovery and endocrine adaptation. Notably, many patients with thyroid cancer report inadequate symptom support and feelings of neglect during follow-up care,16,17 highlighting the clinical importance of improving the understanding of symptom experiences during recovery. Furthermore, most existing studies have been conducted using relatively small or single-center samples, which may limit the stability and generalizability of the identified symptom cluster structures.

To address these gaps, this national multicenter cross-sectional study aimed to identify prevalent symptom clusters among patients with thyroid cancer at the 1-month postoperative follow-up and to examine factors associated with quality of life. The findings may provide clinically relevant evidence for risk stratification, targeted symptom management, and the optimization of survivorship care following thyroidectomy.

Methods

Study design

This study was a national, multicenter, cross-sectional study in China and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (Approval number: bc20240073, Approval date: June 6, 2024). Patients with thyroid cancer were recruited consecutively via convenience sampling from 29 hospitals across 24 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions in China between June and December 2024.

Participants and settings

This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All eligible participants in this study were recruited during their first postoperative month follow-up visit in the outpatient setting, and written informed consent was obtained. Inclusion criteria were as follows: diagnosis of thyroid cancer confirmed by histopathological examination and consistent with the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Thyroid Carcinoma18; performed surgical operation for thyroid cancer for the first time; no obstacle in language comprehension, communication, or literacy ability; age ≥ 18 years; voluntary participation in the study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: severe mental illness; combined with other malignant tumors and serious diseases. Withdrawal criteria: voluntary withdrawal from the study for various reasons.

Instruments

Sociodemographic variables included age, gender, marital status, residence, literacy level, occupation, income level, type of medical insurance, and so on. Disease-related variables included clinical stage, pathological type, presence of metastasis, extent of thyroidectomy, thyrotropin value, and so on.

M.D. Anderson symptom inventory-thyroid cancer module (MDASI-THY)

Gning et al.19 developed and initially validated MDASI-THY specifically for use in patients with thyroid cancer in 2009. In 2022, Chinese scholars Hu et al.20 translated into the Chinese version of MDASI-THY with 25 items. In addition to the basic 13 cancer symptom items, a specific thyroid cancer symptom-specific module was added, including 6 symptoms, which are hoarseness, feeling hot, feeling cold, palpitations, dysphagia, diarrhea or constipation. Satisfactory reliability was obtained in the Chinese population validation with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.827-0.954.

European organization for research and treatment of cancer quality of life questionnaire (EORTC QLQ-C30)

The scale was developed by the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer in 1993.21 The Chinese version of the scale was translated by Wan et al. in 2005.22 The scale has 30 items, including five functional domains of physical functioning, role functioning, emotional functioning, cognitive functioning and social functioning, one domain of global health status, 3 symptom domains of fatigue, nausea and vomiting, and pain, and six single items of shortness of breath, insomnia, loss of appetite, constipation, diarrhea, and financial difficulties. The QLQ-C30 summary score was used as an indicator of overall quality of life and was derived from the mean scores of all functional and symptom subscales, excluding the global health status and financial difficulty domains.23 The summary score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. The Chinese version has good reliability and validity.22

Sample size

The sample size was determined based on the methodological requirements for exploratory factor analysis, which generally recommend a sample size of 5-10 times the number of items included in the analysis. Given the 69 items assessed in this study, and allowing for a 20% potential attrition rate, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 863 participants to ensure the robustness of the findings.

Data collection

Between June and December 2024, on-site data collection was conducted. Each hospital appointed a dedicated research coordinator tasked with participant recruitment and questionnaire administration. All team members at each site completed a unified training and certification covering study objectives, standardized workflows, data management, and quality assurance procedures. Participants completed a structured paper-based questionnaire at their first routine postoperative follow-up visit. Designated coordinators immediately reviewed questionnaires to identify and resolve omissions by consulting participants on-site. Questionnaires that remained incomplete after on-site verification were excluded from the final analysis. Consequently, only participants with complete questionnaire data were included, and no imputation for missing data was performed.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS version 23.0 software was used for statistical analyses. Categorical variables such as sociodemographic and clinical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. Normally distributed numerical variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range. Symptoms with a prevalence of less than 30% were excluded prior to exploratory factor analysis (EFA) because low-prevalence symptoms may provide insufficient variance and reduce the stability and interpretability of the factor structure. This approach is consistent with previous oncology symptom cluster study.24 EFA using principal component analysis with varimax rotation was conducted to identify symptom clusters and obtain a simpler and more interpretable factor structure with clearer factor separation. Sampling adequacy and suitability for factor analysis were evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. A KMO value >0.50 and a significant Bartlett’s test (P < .05) were considered indicative of acceptable factorability. Factors were retained based on eigenvalues ≥1, scree plot examination, and clinical interpretability. Items with factor loadings < 0.50 or substantial cross-loadings were sequentially removed, and EFA was repeated iteratively until a stable factor structure was achieved. Each factor was required to contain at least 2 symptoms. Cronbach’s α coefficients were calculated to evaluate the internal consistency reliability of the identified symptom clusters. The Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis H test were used to compare quality of life across different demographic and clinical characteristics. Variables showing statistical significance in univariate analyses were subsequently entered into multiple linear regression models to identify independent predictors of quality of life. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-tailed P < .05.

Results

Regional distribution of participating hospitals and enrolled patients

This study involved 29 hospitals from 24 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions across China. The participating hospitals were distributed across seven geographic regions: 7 in North China, 2 in Northeast China, 6 in East China, 3 in Central China, 3 in South China, 3 in Northwest China, and 5 in Southwest China. Univariate analyses of quality of life according to demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 1206 questionnaires were collected. After excluding 113 incomplete questionnaires, 1093 participants were included in the final analysis. The mean age of participants was 44.33 years (SD = 12.05), with 41.2% aged ≤40 years, 48.4% between 41 and 60 years, and 10.3% aged ≥61 years. The majority were female (74.7%). Regarding body mass index, 47.3% had normal weight (18.5-23.9 kg/m²), 32.9% were overweight (24.0-27.9 kg/m²), and 15.2% were obese (≥28 kg/m²). Urban residents accounted for 73.5% of the sample. Nearly half of the participants (46.9%) held a college degree or higher, and most were married (84.7%). In terms of employment, 49.3% were workers, followed by retirees (12.9%) and unemployed individuals (11.8%). Most participants were nonsmokers (87.9%) and reported no alcohol consumption (86.6%). Clinically, 78.2% were diagnosed at stage I, and papillary thyroid carcinoma was the predominant pathological type (99.0%). Construction of symptom clusters in patients with thyroid cancer following thyroidectomy

Of the 19 symptoms, diarrhea or loose stools (21.41%) and vomiting (26.53%) had an incidence of less than 30% and were excluded from the symptom cluster analysis. EFA was performed on the remaining 17 symptoms with a KMO value of 0.931 and a Barlett’s spherical test χ2 of 6519.142, P < .001, indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. The initial EFA results are presented in Table S1, in which several symptoms demonstrated factor loadings < 0.50. Through iterative EFA procedures, symptoms with loadings < 0.50 (dry mouth, problem with feeling hot, and shortness of breath) were sequentially removed. Detailed results of the iterative factor analyses are presented in Tables S2 and S3.

The final EFA identified 3 symptom clusters: psychological-somatic symptom cluster (emotional distress, sadness, nausea, lack of appetite, pain, disturbed sleep, drowsiness, and fatigue), thyroid hormone-related symptom cluster (difficulty remembering, problem with feeling cold, and racing heartbeat), and surgery-related symptom cluster (hoarseness, numbness or tingling, and difficulty swallowing). These 3 symptom clusters explained 60.188% of the total variance. Cronbach’s α coefficients for the psychological-somatic, thyroid hormone-related, and surgery-related symptom clusters were 0.887, 0.718, and 0.624, respectively (Table 3). In addition, the scree plot demonstrated clear inflection points for the first 3 factors with eigenvalues > 1, further supporting retention of a 3-factor solution (Figure S1).

Multiple regression analysis of quality of life in patients with thyroid cancer following thyroidectomy

Variables that were statistically significant in univariate analyses, together with the scores of the identified symptom clusters, were entered into the multiple linear regression model. Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated no significant collinearity among the independent variables. The final model explained 55.2% of the variance in quality of life (adjusted R² = 0.552). The results demonstrated that gender, extent of thyroidectomy, and all 3 symptom clusters were significantly associated with quality of life. Specifically, female patients reported lower quality of life scores than male patients. Patients who underwent total thyroidectomy had lower quality of life scores compared with those who underwent partial thyroidectomy. In addition, greater symptom burden in the psychological-somatic symptom cluster, thyroid hormone-related symptom cluster, and surgery-related symptom cluster was significantly associated with poorer quality of life. Among these, the psychological-somatic symptom cluster showed the strongest association with quality of life (β = −0.596, P < .001). Detailed results are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

In this study, fatigue and disturbed sleep were the symptoms with the highest prevalence and severity among patients following thyroidectomy, which is consistent with findings from previous studies.9,25–27 These symptoms may reflect complex physiological and neuroendocrine responses during the early postoperative recovery period. Surgical trauma and postoperative tissue repair can induce substantial physiological stress and increased energy expenditure.28,29 In addition, fluctuations in thyroid hormone levels following partial or total thyroidectomy may disrupt metabolic homeostasis and neuroendocrine regulation.26,30 Such hormonal instability may further affect autonomic nervous system activity, circadian regulation, and neural excitability, thereby contributing to fatigue, disturbed sleep, and related somatic symptoms. These endocrine and physiological fluctuations are commonly observed during the first postoperative month, a period characterized by ongoing functional adaptation and recovery.31

Beyond the high prevalence of individual symptoms, our findings further demonstrated that symptoms during early survivorship tended to co-occur as distinct symptom clusters, potentially reflecting shared biological, neuroendocrine, and psychophysiological processes. The psychological-somatic symptom cluster may be associated with postoperative stress responses, endocrine fluctuations, and disrupted autonomic regulation,32,33 which together contribute to the coexistence of emotional distress, fatigue, sleep disturbance, pain, and appetite-related symptoms. Persistent physical symptoms may aggravate emotional distress, whereas psychological stress may further amplify symptom perception and physiological dysregulation, resulting in dynamic interactions among symptoms and a cycle of mutual reinforcement. The thyroid hormone-related symptom cluster, including racing heartbeat, difficulty remembering, and feeling cold, may reflect altered thyroid hormone feedback regulation and impaired neuroendocrine adaptation following thyroidectomy. Thyroid hormones play important roles in autonomic regulation,32 cognitive function,34 and energy metabolism.35 Postoperative fluctuations in thyroid hormone levels may disrupt neurotransmitter activity, neuronal energy metabolism, and hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis feedback mechanisms, thereby affecting both neural and cognitive functioning. These interconnected neuroendocrine processes may contribute to the simultaneous occurrence of cardiovascular, cognitive, and thermoregulatory symptoms observed in this cluster.

In contrast, the surgery-related symptom cluster, comprising hoarseness, numbness or tingling, and difficulty swallowing, appears to be more directly associated with surgical trauma, transient neural irritation, and postoperative functional adaptation.36,37 These shared anatomical vulnerabilities may explain why these symptoms tend to cluster together and support the notion that surgery-related symptoms represent a distinct mechanistic symptom cluster following thyroidectomy. Variations in symptom cluster composition across studies were observed and may be attributable to differences in study populations, symptom assessment tools, and survivorship stages. For example, Cai et al.38 primarily included patients with advanced thyroid cancer, whereas our study focused on patients during the early postoperative recovery period. Li et al.14 assessed symptom experiences using the EORTC QLQ-THY34 during the immediate postoperative period (1-3 days after surgery), whereas our study used the MDASI-THY and evaluated patients at one month after thyroidectomy. Despite these methodological and temporal differences, symptoms related to voice changes, swallowing dysfunction, and sensory disturbances were repeatedly grouped within the same symptom cluster.14 Such consistency suggests that surgery-related symptoms may reflect a relatively robust and clinically relevant symptom cluster during early survivorship in patients with thyroid cancer. Although the internal consistency of this cluster was modest (Cronbach’s α = 0.624), this may partly reflect the fact that the cluster comprised only 3 symptoms, together with the heterogeneous nature of these postoperative manifestations. Hoarseness, numbness or tingling, and difficulty swallowing represent distinct functional impairments but are among the most common complications following thyroidectomy. Despite this, considering these symptoms together may still be clinically meaningful for postoperative symptom assessment and management.

The present study further demonstrated that all 3 symptom clusters were significantly associated with poorer quality of life, highlighting the multidimensional burden of co-occurring symptoms during early survivorship. This finding is consistent with previous studies in patients with lung,39 gastric,40 esophageal,41 and breast cancer,42 in which symptom clusters were consistently associated with poorer quality of life compared with isolated symptoms. Symptoms within a cluster may interact dynamically and reinforce one another through shared biological and psychological pathways, thereby amplifying their overall burden. Among the identified clusters, the psychological-somatic symptom cluster exhibited the strongest association with quality of life, which is consistent with the findings reported by Luo et al.43 One possible explanation is that this cluster encompasses symptoms spanning multiple domains, including emotional distress, pain, fatigue, sleep disturbance, and appetite-related symptoms. The coexistence of these symptoms across multiple domains may help explain why the psychological-somatic symptom cluster showed the strongest association with poorer quality of life in the present study. Furthermore, psychological distress and physical symptoms are closely interconnected. Psychological stress may influence somatic functioning through neuroendocrine and autonomic pathways, whereas persistent physical symptoms may further exacerbate emotional distress, creating a cycle of mutual reinforcement. For example, pain may increase negative emotions and heighten symptom perception,44,45 while disturbed sleep and fatigue frequently interact bidirectionally, with poor sleep contributing to fatigue and fatigue further impairing sleep quality.9 Such symptom interactions may increase a greater symptom burden, which is associated with poorer quality of life. The thyroid hormone-related symptom cluster was also associated with poorer quality of life, although its impact was less pronounced than that of the psychological-somatic symptom cluster. Previous studies have shown that thyroid dysfunction and TSH suppression therapy may be associated with cognitive complaints, cardiovascular symptoms, and impaired well-being among patients with thyroid cancer.46,47 These symptoms may limit physical activity,48 interfere with work and social participation, and increase emotional concerns regarding health status.49 The surgery-related symptom cluster was likewise associated with poorer quality of life. This cluster included hoarseness, numbness or tingling, and difficulty swallowing, all of which are common postoperative complications that directly affect patients’ ability to communicate, eat, and perform routine daily activities. These symptoms may also cause frustration, social embarrassment, and concerns regarding recovery, thereby extending their association beyond physical functioning alone.50–52 Previous studies have similarly reported that persistent voice, swallowing, and sensory disturbances following thyroidectomy are associated with reduced quality of life and delayed functional recovery.53,54 Therefore, early identification and management of surgery-related symptoms remain important components of postoperative survivorship care.

In addition to symptom burden, female sex and total thyroidectomy were identified as factors associated with poorer quality of life in the present study, consistent with previous studies in patients with thyroid cancer.27,55 Female patients reported lower quality of life scores. One possible explanation is that women tend to experience greater psychological distress and symptom burden following cancer diagnosis and treatment.56 Furthermore, because thyroid cancer predominantly affects women of reproductive age, concerns regarding fertility,57 family responsibilities,58 and disease recurrence59 may impose additional burdens during recovery. Patients who underwent total thyroidectomy also reported poorer quality of life. Compared with partial thyroidectomy, total thyroidectomy is associated with lifelong dependence on thyroid hormone replacement therapy60 and a higher risk of postoperative complications, including hypocalcemia and voice-related problems.61 Collectively, these factors may complicate postoperative recovery and adversely affect quality of life.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers numerous notable strengths. First, it included a large sample from 29 hospitals across all seven geographic regions of China, providing strong nationwide representativeness and enhancing generalizability. Second, it specifically focused on the early survivorship following thyroidectomy, a critical yet underexplored period in symptom cluster research. Third, the use of a validated symptom assessment tool enabled robust identification of symptom clusters.

However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross‑sectional design cannot establish the temporal sequence and causal relationships between symptom clusters and quality of life. Longitudinal studies are necessary to investigate changes in symptom clusters patterns and their long-term effects on patient well-being. Second, the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, and the reliance on self-administered questionnaires could be subject to participants’ subjective interpretation. Third, some symptom domains assessed by the MDASI-THY, including fatigue, pain, and disturbed sleep, overlap conceptually with those included in the EORTC QLQ-C30. This overlap may have introduced shared variance and partially inflated the observed associations between symptom clusters and quality of life. Fourth, the identified symptom cluster structure was not internally validated (eg, using split-sample confirmatory factor analysis) or externally validated in an independent population. Therefore, the stability and reproducibility of the identified symptom cluster structure require further confirmation in future studies. Fifth, all participants were recruited from China. Although the multicenter design enhanced the representativeness of the sample within the Chinese population, cultural, social, and healthcare-system differences may influence symptom perception, reporting behaviors, and quality of life outcomes. Therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to populations from other cultural backgrounds.

Conclusions

This national multicenter cross-sectional study identified 3 distinct symptom clusters among patients with thyroid cancer during early survivorship: a psychological-somatic symptom cluster, a thyroid hormone-related symptom cluster, and a surgery-related symptom cluster. All 3 symptom clusters were significantly associated with poorer quality of life, with the psychological-somatic symptom cluster showing the strongest association. In addition, female sex and total thyroidectomy were identified as factors associated with poorer quality of life. These findings provide further evidence that symptom experiences during early survivorship are multidimensional and tend to occur in clusters rather than as isolated symptoms. Early identification of symptom clusters and high-risk patient groups may facilitate more targeted postoperative assessment and management. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to validate the stability of symptom clusters over time and clarify their long-term associations with quality of life. Building on the present findings, multicenter interventional studies are needed to determine whether symptom cluster–guided management can effectively reduce symptom burden and improve quality of life during early survivorship after thyroidectomy.
