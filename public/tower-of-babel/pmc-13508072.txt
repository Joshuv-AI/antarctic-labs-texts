Anisotropic Diffusion Barrier of Benzene on Cu(111) Measured by Single-Molecule Manipulation
Authors: Xiaoyu Hou, Jie Yang, Jingrui Chen, Lizhi Zhang, Mengxi Liu, Xiaohui Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anisotropic surface diffusion of atoms and molecules originates from the underlying crystallographic symmetry and the presence of step edges. However, accurate experimental determination of diffusion barriers remains challenging due to intrinsic limitations of existing techniques. Here, we employ atomic force microscopy-based single-molecule manipulation combined with lateral force spectroscopy to investigate the diffusion of benzene on Cu(111). By quantifying the threshold forces required to induce controlled molecular motion, we resolve pronounced anisotropy in diffusion barriers on atomically flat terraces, identifying the close-packed direction ([110]) as the energetically preferred pathway. Near step edges, we find that lateral displacement along the lower step edge requires significantly higher forces than other in-plane directions, which we attribute to steric constraints imposed by the step geometry. The use of a Cl-terminated tip suppresses short-range chemical interactions with the adsorbate, minimizing perturbation of the potential energy landscape and enabling reliable extraction of diffusion barriers. These results provide experimentally constrained insights into anisotropic molecular diffusion on crystalline surfaces and establish improved benchmarks for molecular dynamics simulations and thin-film growth modeling.

Introduction

The diffusion of organic molecules on crystalline substrates is a technologically important phenomenon underlying a wide range of processes, including self-assembly, crystal growth, and thin-film formation. − Subjected to crystallographic orientation of the substrate and presence of surface steps, molecular diffusion often shows pronounced anisotropy. − Quantifying diffusion barriers along different crystallographic directions and in the vicinity of step edges is therefore essential for understanding the growth mechanisms of organic thin films , and is critical for improving the performance and long-term reliability of molecule-based electronic and optoelectronic devices. −

At present, molecular diffusion barriers are primarily determined using ensemble-averaged techniques such as reflection high-energy electron diffraction and low-energy electron diffraction. , These methods extract diffusion barriers indirectly from temperature-dependent thin-film growth kinetics, typically through analysis of nucleus and island density, or grain growth rates at different temperatures. Because such measurements represent statistical averages over large surface areas, they provide only limited insight into the anisotropic diffusion behavior of individual molecules. Moreover, organic molecules interact with multiple surface atoms rather than occupy a single site, and their motion cannot be described by simple models like site-to-site hopping or continuous random-walk models. − Their diffusion often exhibits pronounced directional bias, obscuring the intrinsic anisotropic behavior at the single-molecule level.

Lateral manipulations of individual atoms and molecules on surfaces by means of scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) have been extensively demonstrated in a variety of systems, commonly achieved by increasing the tip-molecule interactions at reduced tip-adsorbate separation. , In STM experiments, the molecules show different on-surface moving modes under the acting tip, such as pushing, pulling, and sliding, which can be directly identified by analyses of the simultaneously recorded tunneling current traces. For AFM, the force volume mapping enables the determination of the force threshold required to displace a single atom or molecule. ,, However, one outstanding question is that the diffusion barriers measured by this lateral manipulation method are expected to be lower than the true values due to the presence of the tip. ,

On the theoretical side, density functional theory (DFT) calculations are widely employed to investigate adsorption geometries and diffusion pathways of molecules on surfaces. − However, the complexity arising from molecular flexibility, bonding characteristics, shape, and anisotropic interactions makes the predicted adsorption configurations and diffusion barriers highly sensitive to the choice of exchange-correlation functional as well as the treatment of van der Waals (vdW) interactions. As a result, notable variations have been reported not only in the magnitude of the calculated diffusion barriers, but also in the identification of preferred adsorption sites and minimum-energy pathways. , These inconsistencies underscore the need for experimental validation of the diffusion landscape at the single-molecule level.

In this work, we employ STM and AFM-based single-molecule manipulation to quantitatively investigate the diffusion of benzene on Cu(111). By directly measuring the lateral forces required to induce controlled molecular motion, we determine the anisotropic diffusion landscape at the single-molecule level. Our results identify the close-packed [110] direction as the preferred migration pathway on terraces, as well as an increased resistance to movement along the lower step edge attributed to steric constraints imposed by the step geometry. The use of a chemically passivated tip enables us to obtain a more accurate determination of the diffusion barrier with minimized tip-induced perturbations to the potential energy surface.

Motivated by previous single-molecule manipulation experiments, we measure the diffusion barriers of benzene molecules on Cu(111) along different crystallographic directions using AFM, following the method described in ref. However, our initial manipulation attempts were unsuccessful, as the metal tips frequently picked up the molecules during lateral manipulation (Figure S1). This problem was overcome by functionalizing the tip apex with a chlorine atom (Figure S2; see Methods), which effectively suppresses molecular pickup. The resulting Cl-terminated tips exhibit considerable lateral stiffness and mechanical stability, − which allows reliable manipulation of benzene molecules on the surface. In addition, the Cl termination renders the tip apex chemically more inert, , thereby reducing the chemical interactions with the surface adsorbate. This chemical passivation is crucial for achieving precise measurements of the lateral force required to move a molecule across the surface. Because these interactions are a major source of tip-induced perturbation to the molecule–surface potential-energy landscape and can artificially lower the measured diffusion barrier, their suppression enables a more reliable determination of the intrinsic diffusion barrier.

Accurate determination of the adsorption site of the adsorbate on the substrate is essential for identifying diffusion pathways and for reliably quantifying energy barriers. Although the benzene/Cu(111) system has been extensively investigated by techniques such as STM, , temperature-dependent dynamical measurements, , and DFT calculations, the preferred adsorption site remains unsettled. This uncertainty reflects both the indirect nature of ensemble-averaged measurement and the sensitivity of DFT predictions to the treatment of vdW interactions. ,

To address this issue, we employ the adjustable constant-height AFM imaging method. Using the Cu(111) atomic lattice and Cl adatoms as structural references, we unambiguously identify the preferred adsorption site of benzene as the bridge site (Figure b; see Figure S3 and S4 for details). This experimentally determined adsorption registry provides a direct atomic-scale reference for identifying the minimum-energy diffusion pathway and serves as a reliable structural benchmark for the subsequent DFT calculations.

Lateral manipulations of benzene molecules on the terraces are carried out along two high-symmetry directions of the substrate: the close-packed direction ([110]) and a direction oriented 30° from it ([121]), as illustrated in Figure c. The periodic sawtooth-like features seen in both the I(x) and Δf(x) curves (Figures d) indicate successive movement of the benzene molecule with the AFM tip along the [110] direction. The abrupt upward jumps in the sawtooth-like I(x) curve arise from sudden displacement of the molecule toward the tip, characteristic of the pulling mode as previously reported. This interpretation is further supported by the corresponding Δf(x) and Δf(z) spectra (Figure S5 and S6). As the tip moves laterally away from the molecular centroid, the decreasing I(x) is accompanied by an increased Δf(x), which indicates that the vertical interaction between the Cl-terminated tip and the benzene molecule weakens. Simultaneously, a lateral force is built up between the tip and the molecule. When this force exceeds the diffusion barrier associated with site-to-site hopping, the benzene molecule abruptly hops to the neighboring adsorption site, resulting in concurrent discontinuities in both the I(x) and Δf(x) signals. Close examination of the I(x) and Δf(x) traces yields an average jump distance of ∼ 258 pm (Figure S7), in excellent agreement with the Cu(111) surface lattice spacing of ∼ 255 pm. Given that the adsorption site of benzene on Cu(111) has been determined to be the bridge site before and after manipulation (Figure S3), this correspondence confirms that the molecule migrates via discrete hopping between adjacent bridge sites along the [110] direction, following the tip trajectory illustrated in Figure c.

This migration of benzene is driven by attractive interactions between the chlorine atom at the tip apex and the molecule. In the case of a Cl-terminated tip, , the tip-molecule interaction is expected to be dominated by vdW and electrostatic interactions (Figure S8), with minimal contribution from chemical bond formation between the tip and the benzene molecule. As the tip approaches the surface, these interactions perturb the local potential energy surface (PES) experienced by the adsorbed molecule, thereby triggering the hop of the molecule to a neighboring adsorption site.

For the manipulation along the [121] direction (Figure e), the I(x) and Δf(x) curves also display characteristic sawtooth features indicative of the pulling-mode. However, in contrast to the periodic pattern observed along [110] (Figure d), the sawtooth features in this case do not exhibit regular periodicity.

Instead, analysis of the manipulation traces reveals two distinct jump intervals (Figure S7), i.e., a larger interval of ∼ 435 pm and a smaller spacing of ∼ 218 pm. These distances can be assigned to the separation between adjacent bridge sites along the [121] direction (∼442 pm) and to the projection of this lattice spacing onto the [121] direction (∼221 pm), respectively. The coexistence of these two characteristic jump lengths indicates that although the tip is nominally displaced along [121], the benzene molecule does not follow a straight trajectory. Instead, it undergoes side-sliding toward the [110] directions, resulting in a zigzag pathway, as schematically illustrated by the dashed purple line in Figure c. This preferential deviation toward [110] direction provides direct evidence that diffusion along the close-packed [110] pathway is energetically more favorable than [121], implying a lower diffusion barrier along this direction.

The diffusion barrier of benzene on Cu(111) can be quantitatively extracted using the force volume method established in previous studies. ,, Figure a schematically illustrates a series of constant-height line scans performed at progressively reduced tip heights as the tip passes over an isolated benzene molecule along the [110] direction. Figures b and c display the simultaneously recorded I and Δf during tip approach. When the tip height is reduced to z′ = – 165 pm, a sequence of abrupt discontinuities emerges in both I(x) and Δf(x) traces. These jumps indicate that the benzene molecule undergoes successive hopping to adjacent adsorption sites following the lateral motion of the tip (Figure S9).

Using the Sader-Jarvis method, the vertical force F z and the potential energy U are constructed, as shown in Figures d and e. Throughout the manipulation process, both F z and U remain within the attractive regime, directly confirming that the manipulation proceeds via the pulling mode.

The lateral force F x , obtained from the spatial differentiation of the potential energy U along the x axis, is presented in Figure f, where the positive direction is defined to coincide with the direction of tip displacement. When the tip is positioned above the molecule center, F x is expected to be zero. As the tip is displaced laterally, F x increases in magnitude on both sides, reaching a maximum before decaying to zero as the tip moves far away from the molecule. With decreasing tip height, the peak value of F x increases correspondingly, eventually reaching the threshold required to induce molecular hopping. Therefore, the magnitude of F x at the hopping position represents the minimum lateral force necessary to overcome the diffusion barrier and initiate molecular motion. In Figure f, the F x value upon hopping is approximately 14.7 pN. Based on a sinusoidal model of the surface potential, the corresponding diffusion barrier is determined to be ∼ 7.5 meV.

In contrast to atomic surface diffusion, the potential-energy landscape of a molecular adsorbate is intrinsically multidimensional because translational motion may couple to rotational and configurational degrees of freedom. Nevertheless, the manipulation traces show that benzene diffuses between adjacent adsorption sites on Cu(111) in a manner analogous to atomic hopping. Along the close-packed [110] direction, both the tunneling current and lateral-force signals exhibit periodic sawtooth-like features with a periodicity matching the Cu(111) lattice constant (Figure d). Moreover, no irregular discontinuities or abrupt changes are observed within individual hopping events, suggesting that the molecule translates between neighboring adsorption sites without detectable rotation or configurational changes. These observations indicate that diffusion along the [110] direction is governed primarily by translational hopping between equivalent adsorption sites and can therefore be reasonably approximated by a one-dimensional periodic potential. Accordingly, we employ a sinusoidal potential to estimate the diffusion barrier. Since the extracted barrier depends on the assumed potential profile, we also compare it with that obtained using a parabolic potential, which provides a local approximation to the potential near the saddle point. For a measured lateral force of 14.7 pN, the estimated barrier is 7.5 meV for the sinusoidal potential and 5.9 meV for the parabolic potential. These comparisons show that the assumed potential profile indeed affects the absolute value of the extracted barrier, although both models predict diffusion barriers on the same energy scale. The uncertainty associated with the measured diffusion barriers is also discussed in Supporting Information.

Two additional molecular manipulation events acquired under critical-force conditions along the [110] and [121] directions are shown in Figures a and b, respectively. Comparison of the adsorption sites before and after manipulation reveals that the molecule hops opposite to the direction of tip motion. Such behavior is expectable under the pulling-mode manipulation mechanism because the attractive tip-molecule interaction drives the molecule toward the instantaneous tip position. Therefore, both forward and backward hopping events can occur depending on the relative tip-molecule geometry at the onset of the hopping event.

An important finding from Figures a and b is that, even when the tip is displaced along the [121] direction, the molecule frequently hops to the adjacent adsorption sites along the [110] direction. This spontaneous deviation indicates that the intrinsic diffusion barrier for the [121] direction cannot be independently quantified using the present force volume method. The average lateral force thresholds obtained from manipulations nominally performed along [110] and [121] directions are 16.6 ± 1.7 pN and 17.2 ± 0.8 pN, respectively (Figure S12). The similar threshold forces arise because, despite the different tip trajectories, the molecule ultimately migrates to the neighboring adsorption site via the energetically preferred [110] pathway.

To further elucidate benzene diffusion on Cu(111), DFT calculations are performed, as shown in Figure c. Eight high-symmetry binding configurations on Cu(111) surface are considered, denoted BR-I, FCC-I, HCP-I, TOP-I, BR-R, FCC-R, HCP-R, and TOP-R, where I refers to “in-phase,” and R refers to a 30° rotation of the benzene, following the notation of Berland et al. Previous computational studies have examined benzene adsorption on Cu(111) using a variety of exchange-correlation functionals, and have reported that the predicted most stable adsorption site depends sensitively on the choice of functional. In our experiments, the adsorption site of benzene has been determined to be bridge site of Cu(111) (Figure S3 and S4). Guided by this experimental finding, we adopt the vdW-DF (optB88-vdW) functional, which has correctly captured this preference.

Figure c shows the calculated binding energies for the eight high-symmetry configurations of benzene on Cu(111), reproducing the previously reported result that the BR-I configuration is the most stable. We further calculate the PES of benzene on Cu(111) for the I orientation, as shown in the inset of Figure c. The energy barrier between adjacent bridge sites along the [110] direction is determined to be 8.1 meV, i.e., the energy difference between the hcp and bridge sites (see Methods for more details). This value is consistent with the experimentally measured value obtained using the sinusoidal potential model. Previous studies have reported that diffusion barriers extracted from AFM-based lateral manipulation experiments are often systematically lower than theoretical predictions. , This discrepancy, which represents a major concern for the quantitative accuracy of the AFM-based approaches, arises because the measurement probes not the intrinsic adsorbate–surface system, but rather the potential energy landscape of a coupled tip-adsorbate–substrate configuration. For reactive tips, short-range chemical interactions can significantly perturb the local bonding environment, thereby reducing the effective activation barrier. − The employment of a Cl-functionalized tip mitigates the chemical reactivity of the tip apex, rendering the tip-molecule interaction predominantly vdW and electrostatic in nature.

The electrostatic interaction is governed by the contact potential difference between the Cl-terminated tip and the substrate. Compared with this junction electric field, direct polarization of the benzene molecule by the local charge distribution at the Cl-tip apex plays a secondary role. Based on in situ measurements of local contact potential difference (Figure S13), the field strength in the tip–sample junction is estimated to be approximately 0.5 V/nm.

To evaluate the influence of this tip electrical field, we recalculate the adsorption energies of relevant configurations under an external electric field applied perpendicular to the surface. We find that the field consistently weakens the molecule–substrate interaction across all configurations, leading to a systematic reduction in adsorption energies. For the most stable adsorption configuration (BR-I), the applied field induces a slight increase in adsorption height accompanied by a small charge redistribution from benzene to the Cu(111) substrate, resulting in a reduction of the dipole moment from 1.28 to 1.14 D and a corresponding increase in adsorption energy (from – 747.4 meV to – 735.2 meV). A similar trend is observed for the transition state configuration. The HCP-I configuration exhibits a decrease in the dipole moment from 1.43 to 1.27 D, accompanied by a comparable increase in adsorption energy. As a result, the external electric field slightly modifies the potential energy landscape along the diffusion pathway. The calculated diffusion barrier increases by approximately 1.5 meV compared to the zero-field value of 8.1 meV (see Figure S14–16 and Table S1 for details). These findings indicate that the electrostatic interaction weakly modifies the diffusion landscape primarily, with only a minor impact on the barrier height.

To assess the influence of the vdW interaction, we compare the adsorption energy of benzene on Cu(111) in the absence and presence of the Cl-terminated tip, which changes from – 747.4 meV to – 832.2 meV. This energy difference (84.8 meV) is substantially larger than the contribution from the dipole of the Cl-terminated tip, indicating that the tip-induced interaction is dominated by the vdW contribution and modifies the molecule–surface interaction. Nevertheless, the suppression of short-range chemical interactions achieved by tip passivation implies that tip-induced perturbations can be minimized, allowing experimentally measured diffusion barriers to more closely reflect the intrinsic properties of the adsorbate–surface system.

We further investigate the diffusion behavior of molecules in the vicinity of step edges, where the presence of the Ehrlich-Schwoebel (E-S) barrier plays a pivotal role in thin-film growth thermodynamics and kinetics. , Attempts to manipulate benzene molecules across the step edge from the upper to the lower terrace have proven unsuccessful (Figure S17), likely due to the presence of an E-S barrier. The benzene molecules are stabilized at adsorption sites near the step edge, with the molecular centroid located approximately four atomic rows away from the step (Figure S18). Further DFT calculations confirm that the adsorption energy of benzene increases as the molecule approaches the step edge (Figure S19), indicating that the adsorption becomes progressively less favorable due to the step-induced modification of the potential-energy landscape. Consistent with the physical picture established by Guner et al., monatomic step edges substantially modify the local potential-energy landscape and give rise to an energetic barrier in their vicinity.

Using the approach described above, we examine the molecular diffusion along four distinct trajectories, as schematically illustrated in Figure a: (1) perpendicular to the step edge on the upper terrace, (2) parallel to the step edge on the upper terrace, (3) parallel the step edge on the lower terrace, and (4) perpendicular to the step edge on the lower terrace. Using the same tip, we measure the lateral force required to move the molecules along each path, as shown in Figures b–e (see Figures S20–S23 for details). For the manipulations along paths 1, 2, and 4, the F x (x) curves exhibit a single-peak profile, with an average lateral force threshold of 16.2 ± 1.6 pN, comparable to those measured on terraces. In contrast, for manipulation along path 3, the F x (x) curve evolves from a single-peak to a double-peak profile as the tip height decreases. Although this change suggests the onset of Pauli repulsion, the interaction between the tip and the molecule remains primarily attractive (Figure S22). The lateral force upon hopping along the lower terrace parallel to the step edge is ∼ 31.5 pN, which is significantly higher than those measured along other manipulation paths.

We repeat the lateral force measurements using different tips. The statistical results in Figure f reveal two distinct distributions of lateral force at 17.4 ± 2.1 pN and 31.7 ± 0.6 pN, corresponding to diffusion barriers of 8.8 ± 1.1 meV and 16.1 ± 0.3 meV, respectively. The latter corresponds to path 3, i.e., manipulation along the step edge on the lower terrace, where both the lateral force and the derived diffusion barrier are higher than those measured along other manipulation paths.

The underlying mechanism responsible for the higher diffusion barrier along path 3 is analyzed as follows. The reduced coordination number of metal atoms at step edges enhances their chemical reactivity, thereby modifying the local chemical environment experienced by molecules near the step edge. Such an effect could increase the diffusion barriers for molecular motion along the step edges. Furthermore, owing to the Smoluchowski effect, electrons spill out from the upper to the lower edge of a step, giving rise to electric dipoles on the step edges. The resulting asymmetric potential is expected to have opposite effects on the diffusion barriers of molecules adsorbed on the upper and lower sides of the step edge. Both the enhanced chemical reactivity and the step-induced electrostatic potential are expected to influence molecular diffusion on both terraces adjacent to the step. Experimentally, no significant variations in the diffusion barriers are observed along paths 1, 2, and 4, indicating that the increased diffusion barrier observed exclusively along the lower step edge (path 3) cannot be explained solely by these effects.

Another factor that may influence the molecular diffusion near the step edge is Friedel oscillations. , However, the associated energy modulation is only a few meV, and the corresponding diffusion channels occur at distances of several nanometers from the step edge. Consistent with this expectation, our experiments reveal no signatures of Friedel-oscillation-induced effects, such as periodic variations in the lateral force or distinct preferred adsorption sites. The enhanced diffusion barrier along path 3 can therefore be excluded from arising from Friedel oscillations.

Diffusion along the lower step edge is subject to steric hindrance , from the upper terrace, which creates a confined channel not present on the open terrace. The higher diffusion barrier observed under this confinement likely involves a configurationally distorted transition state, where the molecular orientation is substantially altered from its equilibrium configuration. This additional barrier is absent when the molecule is pulled away from the step edge or parallel to it on the upper terrace, consistent with our experimental observations. Consequently, the increased diffusion barrier measured along the lower step edge is attributed to the steric constraints imposed by the step edge.

Earlier investigations of benzene diffusion on Cu(111) using helium spin–echo spectroscopy (HeSE) reported a diffusion barrier of 35 meV, much higher than the value obtained in this work. In that study, DFT calculations suggested diffusion proceeds via jumping through nondegenerate hollow sites. This discrepancy likely arises from the differences in both experimental methodology and conditions. Unlike HeSE measurements, which probe ensemble-averaged dynamics, our approach directly probes the motion of individual molecules along well-defined crystallographic directions with single-molecule sensitivity. Furthermore, the lower temperature of our experiment provides enhanced energy resolution. In contrast, the elevated temperature and higher adsorbate coverage in the HeSE experiment may obscure intrinsic diffusion anisotropy and incorporate contributions from multiple diffusion pathways, leading to an apparent higher averaged barrier. Notably, previous theoretical studies reported diffusion barriers in the range of 10–20 meV, ,, which are in closer agreement with our experimental results.

Conclusions

In summary, we employ AFM force-volume spectroscopy to quantify anisotropic diffusion of benzene molecules on the Cu(111) surface at the single-molecule level. The unambiguous assignment of the preferred adsorption site of benzene on Cu(111) to bridge sites settles the long-standing controversy that had lacked consensus in both experimental and theoretical studies. Under tip-induced manipulation, benzene hops between adjacent bridge sites along the close-packed [110] direction independent of manipulation trajectory, indicating this direction is energetically favored. By measuring the threshold lateral forces required to induce molecular motion, we determine a diffusion barrier of approximately 8.8 ± 1.1 meV along the preferred [110] direction. Although diffusion barriers obtained from AFM manipulation are often underestimated due to tip-induced perturbations of the PES, the chemically inert Cl-terminated tip employed here minimizes such effects, enabling a more faithful probing of the intrinsic diffusion landscape and resulting in good agreement with DFT calculations. In contrast to diffusion on terraces, the benzene molecule moving along the lower step edge exhibits a higher diffusion barrier of ∼ 16.1 ± 0.3 meV, attributed to steric constraints imposed by the upper terrace that hinder lateral motion. Attempts to manipulate benzene across the step edge are unsuccessful, likely due to the presence of an E-S barrier. These findings clarify the mechanisms of anisotropic molecular diffusion on metal surfaces and inform models of surface transport in thin-film growth.
