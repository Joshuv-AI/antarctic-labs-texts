Enhancing biomass, hydrocarbon and biodiesel properties of green microalga Botryococcus braunii KMITL through gamma and UV radiation exposure
Authors: Buppha Jongput, Pattanasak Chiwpreecha, Suneerat Ruangsomboon, Pajongjit Tongsri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of gamma (137Cs, 0–250 Gy) and UV (UV-C, 0–12 h) radiation on growth and biodiesel properties of Botryococcus braunii KMITL. For gamma radiation, maximum biomass (1.37 ± 0.02 g L−1) was achieved with 50 Gy, while a dose of 200 Gy resulted in the highest hydrocarbon content (51.84 ± 0.20%) and yield (0.66 ± 0.01 g L−1). For UV radiation, a 9 h exposure produced the highest biomass (2.45 ± 0.05 g L−1), hydrocarbon content (55.01 ± 1.22%), and yield (1.35 ± 0.04 g L−1). Algae exposed to gamma radiation within the range of 0–150 Gy exhibited C16:0 as the dominant fatty acid methyl ester (FAME), similar to those exposed to UV radiation, while algae exposed to 200–250 Gy displayed C18:1n9t as the dominant FAME. High levels of gamma and UV radiation were observed to lengthen fatty acid chains and increase unsaturated fatty acids. The cetane values of biodiesel from algae exposed to gamma and UV radiation ranged from 64.55 ± 0.14–66.47 ± 0.20 and 59.43 ± 0.04–65.27 ± 0.22, respectively, all meeting standard criteria. Both gamma and UV radiation also improved the saponification value and cold flow properties of the biodiesel. Introduction

The growing demand for renewable and sustainable energy sources has intensified research into alternative fuels, with biodiesel emerging as a promising candidate. Microalgae-derived biodiesel offers several advantages over conventional fossil fuels, including renewability, reduced greenhouse gas emissions, lower environmental impacts, potential cost-effectiveness, and higher energy efficiency1–5. Among the diverse microalgae species, Botryococcus braunii stands out due to its significant lipid and hydrocarbon production, making it a viable source for biodiesel6,7.

Mutagenesis technology is a rapid and efficient method to enhance microalgae with high lipid content8. Physical mutagens have been shown to boost the cellular lipid content of microalgae. Radiation, particularly gamma and ultraviolet (UV) radiation, is recognized as an effective physical mutagen for inducing physiological and biochemical responses in various organisms, including microalgae9.

Radiation mutagenesis is controlled by time and intensity, affecting the atoms and molecules in the cell to induce genetic alterations10,11. Microalgae mutants can have a higher capacity for lipid production due to mutations in genes associated with lipid biosynthesis. For example, the expression of the acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACCase) gene increased fivefold after mutagenesis12.

Gamma radiation, a form of ionizing radiation with high energy levels, induces random mutations and various cellular responses13. Studies have shown that Botryococcus sp. exposed to 10 Gy of gamma radiation (60Co) achieved the highest biomass (0.83 g L−1) and lipid content (41%)14. Similarly, green microalga Chlorella pyrenoidosa exposed to 500 Gy gamma radiation showed a significant increase in biomass yield, up to 2.3-fold compared to non-irradiated samples12. The lipid content of gamma-irradiated green microalgae increased by 1.15–1.50 times compared to non-irradiated controls15–17. The growth of gamma-irradiated Nitzschia sp. also increased with higher radiation doses, however, excessively high radiation doses can cause cellular damage12. UV radiation, which varies in energy levels, has distinct biological effects and has been applied to induce mutations in algae, such as B. braunii and Chlorella spp. leading to 1.26-1.96-fold increase in lipid content compared to the control18–21.

Botryococcus braunii has attracted significant attention due to its remarkable biochemical composition, including hydrocarbons, carbohydrates, proteins, chlorophylls, and carotenoids. Carbohydrates can be used as a source of biofuel ethanol, while proteins are a source of methane. Chlorophyll and carotenoid pigments possess antioxidant properties. The high-value products of B. braunii highlight its biotechnological potential22. Understanding the abundance and functionality of these bioactive compounds within B. braunii holds promise for applications ranging from biofuel production to pharmaceutical and nutraceutical development.

This study aims to elucidate the effects of gamma and UV radiation on hydrocarbon, protein, carbohydrate, chlorophyll, and carotenoid production in B. braunii KMITL strain cultures. By comprehensively analyzing the biochemical changes induced by radiation exposure, we seek to optimize cultivation strategies to enhance hydrocarbon accumulation and improve the quality of biodiesel derived from B. braunii KMITL. The insights gained from this research will support the development of efficient and scalable cultivation techniques, ultimately promoting the use of B. braunii as a viable and environmentally friendly source for biodiesel production.

Results and discussion

Effect of gamma (137Cs) and UV (UV-C) irradiation on algal biomass

Gamma radiation

During the 30-day cultivation of microalga B. braunii KMITL exposed to gamma radiation, the highest biomass levels were observed mostly on the 20th day across all treatments, except for the 50 Gy exposure, which peaked on the 22nd day (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table S1). The specific growth rate of algae exposed to gamma radiation doses ranging from 0 to 200 Gy (0.10 ± 0.00-0.12 ± 0.00 d−1) did not show significant differences, whereas algae exposed to 250 Gy exhibited a significantly lower specific growth rate (0.09 ± 0.00 d−1) than the control. On the 20th day, the 50 Gy treatment resulted in the highest biomass of 1.53 ± 0.03 g L−1, with a sustained biomass of 1.37 ± 0.02 g L−1 at the end of the cultivation period. These values represented a significant increase compared to the treatments at 0, 200, and 250 Gy, with the biomass being 1.33 times higher than the control. Algae subjected to gamma radiation within the range of 50–200 Gy exhibited enhanced biomass production ranging from 8.7 to 32.5% compared to non-irradiated counterparts, whereas exposure to 250 Gy led to a notable 27.7% reduction in biomass relative to the control.

Biomass yield (A), chlorophyll-a (B), carotenoid (C), carbohydrate (D) and protein (E) contents of B. braunii KMITL after exposure to different gamma irradiation levels (0-250 Gy). Error bars represent ± S.D. of four replicates.

These findings highlight the potential of gamma radiation doses between 50 and 150 Gy to enhance biomass production in this algal strain, with 50 Gy proving to be the most effective dose due to reduced exposure time and cost-efficiency. Conversely, higher gamma radiation levels, particularly at 250 Gy, impeded algal growth. The increased biomass in the 50 Gy treatment is likely due to enhanced enzyme activity in the photosynthetic process of B. braunii KMITL. Low doses of gamma radiation have been shown to stimulate enzyme activity, cell proliferation, and growth23. Similar research on green microalga Spirogyra irradiated with gamma rays also showed increased Rubisco enzyme activity, leading to higher photosynthesis rates and growth compared to non-irradiated Spirogyra24.

The optimal gamma dose, 50 Gy, extended the time to reach the late exponential phase from the 20th day to the 22nd day, possibly contributing to higher biomass production. This extension might be due to irradiated microalgae synthesizing energy reserves for survival. Radiation-induced oxidative stress triggers defense mechanisms in cells, helping maintain growth and reduce stress effects25. Ermavitalini et al.14 reported similar results, showing that 60Co gamma ray irradiation extended the exponential growth phase of Botryocoocus sp. by 4–5 days compared to non-irradiated algae.

UV radiation

In all treatments, algae exposed to UV radiation for durations ranging from 0 to 9 h exhibited peak biomass levels on the 24th day of cultivation, except for the 12 h treatment, which peaked on the 18th day. (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table S2). Algae exposed to UV radiation for 3 to 9 h exhibited specific growth rates ranging from 0.35 ± 0.01 to 0.39 ± 0.02 d−1, which were not statistically different but significantly higher than the control rate of 0.28 ± 0.01 d−1. Algae exposed to UV radiation for 3 to 9 h demonstrated enhanced biomass production, whereas a 12 h exposure reduced biomass. Algae exposed to 9 h of UV radiation displayed the highest biomass, reaching 2.87 ± 0.05 g L−1 on the 24th day and 2.45 ± 0.05 g L−1 at the end of the cultivation period, significantly surpassing all other treatments and exhibiting a 1.30-fold increase compared to the control. This result aligns with Thurakit et al.18, who reported the highest biomass production in B. braunii no. U18J10 exposed to UV-C, which was 2.32 g L−1, equivalent to a 1.32-fold increase over the wild type.

Biomass yield (A), chlorophyll-a(B), carotenoid (C), carbohydrate (D) and protein (E) contents of B. brauniiKMITL after exposure to different UV durations (0-12 h). Error bars represent ± S.D. of four replicates.

The results of this study indicated that appropriate levels of gamma radiation and UV exposure time can stimulate increased biomass in algae, but excessively high levels inhibit growth. For B. braunii KMITL strain, the suitable conditions for enhanced biomass production are gamma radiation at 50 Gy and UV exposure for 9 h, yielding biomass 1.33 and 1.30 times higher than the non-irradiated control, respectively.

Effect of gamma (137Cs) and UV (UV-C) irradiation on chlorophyll-a, carotenoid, carbohydrate, and protein content

Gamma radiation

At the end of the cultivation period, algae exposed to 250 Gy of gamma radiation showed the highest chlorophyll-a and carotenoid content, with 8.06 ± 0.21 and 11.15 ± 0.17 mg g−1, respectively (Fig. 1B-C, Supplementary Table S1). Interestingly, the amount of chlorophyll-a and carotenoid content was inversely related to biomass. Algae with the highest chlorophyll-a and carotenoid content exhibited the lowest biomass.

Algae exposed to 200–250 Gy of gamma radiation had significantly higher carotenoid content compared to those exposed to 0–150 Gy. Specifically, during the 8th -12th day of culture, the 250 Gy treatment had the highest intracellular carotenoids at 41.40 ± 3.05 mg g−1 on the 8th day of culture. This may be due to the stress and cell damage caused by high radiation levels, prompting the algae to produce carotenoids for protection and repair25, resulting in lower growth rates than the control.

Carbohydrate and protein content throughout the cultivation period were shown in Fig. 1D-E. When considering the carbohydrate and protein content, algae exposed to the highest gamma radiation (250 Gy) had the highest carbohydrate and protein content at 287.4 ± 7.5 and 297.4 ± 10.2 mg g−1, respectively, but exhibited the lowest biomass. This suggests that the increase in chlorophyll, carotenoid, protein, and carbohydrate content was a protective response to radiation-induced damage rather than a result of normal growth mechanisms25, thus not contributing to biomass increase.

UV radiation

Similar to the gamma radiation results, algae exposed to the longest UV radiation (12 h) had the highest chlorophyll-a, carotenoid, and carbohydrate content at 9.23 ± 0.49, 14.36 ± 1.24, and 408.1 ± 17.1 mg g−1, respectively, but the lowest biomass (Fig. 2B-E). The protein content in algae exposed to the highest UV radiation was significantly lower, contrary to the gamma radiation results. This suggests different effects of gamma and UV radiation on protein synthesis.

The experiment showed that algae exposed to the highest gamma rays and the longest UV radiation had the lowest biomass but the highest chlorophyll-a, carotenoid, and carbohydrate content. High levels of gamma and UV radiation are harmful to algal cells, causing stress and prompting the production of chlorophyll-a and carotenoids to facilitate photosynthesis and carbohydrate synthesis for cell repair. Stressed algae use carbohydrates for cell balance rather than growth, accumulating concentrated pigments and storage products, thus not increasing biomass. Carotenoids also protect against stress in algae. Normally, lipids and carbohydrates are energy reserves produced during stress for defense26. These responses occur because gamma ray mutagenesis induces genetic alterations depending on irradiation dose27.

Interestingly, algae exposed to 50 Gy of gamma radiation and 9 h of UV had the lowest chlorophyll-a content but the highest biomass. This indicates potential damage to the algae’s ability to harness light energy, suggesting that 50 Gy gamma radiation and 9 h of UV exposure did not harm the algae enough to inhibit growth. This chlorophyll-a reduction still allowed normal growth, enabling storage product creation and energy generation for cell division, resulting in optimal growth. This finding suggests a complex relationship between radiation exposure, chlorophyll-a production, and biomass in algae.

Singh et al.28 reported reduced chlorophyll content and photosynthesis efficiency in the green microalga Chlorella sorokiniana when exposed to UV (0.25–2 J cm⁻²) and gamma (0.5-4 kGy) radiation. Whole-genome transcriptomic profiling revealed modulation of photosynthetic machinery and DNA repair/replication-related genes under UV (1 J cm⁻²) and gamma (1 kGy) radiation. Further analysis showed that C. sorokiniana exposed to radiation exhibits a greater number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO) based functional enrichment analysis of these DEGs revealed major enrichment in categories related to photosynthetic machinery, pigment and chlorophyll metabolic processes, and cellular macromolecule processes. These findings collectively suggest that radiation primarily impacts the photosynthetic machinery of the microalgal cell, corroborating the observed reduction in chlorophyll content and photosynthetic efficiency.

Effect of gamma (137Cs) and UV (UV-C) irradiation on hydrocarbon production

Algae exposed to gamma radiation doses 50, 100 and 200 Gy exhibited a significantly higher biomass productivity (42.17 ± 0.57–45.50 ± 0.74 mg L−1 d−1) than the control, whereas algae exposed to 250 Gy exhibited a significantly lower biomass productivity (24.83 ± 0.50 mg L−1 d−1) than the control (Fig. 3A). The highest hydrocarbon content, hydrocarbon yield, and hydrocarbon productivity of 51.84 ± 0.20%, 0.66 ± 0.01 g L−1, and 21.86 ± 0.30 mg L−1d−1, respectively, were observed in algae exposed to 200 Gy of gamma radiation, which were 1.27, 1.56, and 1.56 times higher than the control (Fig. 3B-D). Therefore, gamma radiation at 200 Gy is recommended to stimulate B. braunii KMITL for increased hydrocarbon production as a biodiesel feedstock, resulting in significantly higher hydrocarbon yield compared to non-exposed algae.

Different lowercase letters (a, b, c, …) on the bars indicate a significant difference (p<0.05); error bars represent± S.D. of four replicates.

Algae exposed to UV radiation for 6–9 h exhibited high biomass productivity ranging from 74.33 ± 2.41–81.50 ± 1.66 mg L−1d−1, which were not statistically different but significantly higher than the control of 62.50 ± 1.07 mg L−1d−1 (Fig. 4A). The highest hydrocarbon content, hydrocarbon yield, and hydrocarbon productivity of 55.01 ± 1.22%, 1.35 ± 0.04 g L−1, and 44.85 ± 1.37 mg L−1d−1, respectively, were observed in algae exposed to 9 h of UV radiation, which were 1.36, 1.77, and 1.77 times higher than the control (Fig. 4B-D). Therefore, UV radiation exposure for 9 h is recommended to stimulate B. braunii KMITL algae for increased hydrocarbon production as a biodiesel feedstock, resulting in significantly higher hydrocarbon yield compared to non-exposed algae.

Gamma irradiation at specific doses can induce structural and metabolic changes29. Optimal gamma and UV radiation exposure can increase enzyme activity associated with lipid biosynthesis14 and upregulate key fatty acid biosynthesis-related genes28. For instance, B. braunii irradiated with 10 Gy gamma radiation showed the highest lipid content of 41%14. Gamma radiation also increased lipid content in other microalgae such as Auxenochlorella protothecoides by 1.15 times16, Chlorella sp. by 1.36 times17, C. sorokiniana by 1.73 times28, and Scenedesmus sp. by 1.5 times15 compared to the control. Similarly, UV radiation increased lipid content in B. braunii by 1.62 times compared to the control18, as well as in other green microalgae including Chlorella sp.19, C. sorokiniana28, and C. vulgaris21.

Fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) profile of B. braunii KMITL exposed to gamma (137Cs) and UV (UV-C) radiation

Biodiesel properties are influenced significantly by the fatty acid esters comprising its composition. The dominant FAME found in algae exposed to gamma radiation doses of 0–150 Gy was palmitic acid methyl ester (C16:0) ranging from 32.27 ± 0.20-46.47 ± 0.10%, followed by elaidic acid methyl ester (C18:1n9t) ranging from 16.49 ± 0.12–29.09 ± 0.18% (Table 1). Conversely, algae exposed to 200–250 Gy exhibited dominance of C18:1n9t ranging from 31.81 ± 0.15–32.68 ± 0.19%, followed by C16:0 ranging from 23.58 ± 0.16–23.90 ± 0.16%. High levels of gamma radiation tended to elongate fatty acid chains, reducing saturated fatty acids (60.31 ± 0.36–71.08 ± 0.48%) compared to non-exposed algae (75.73 ± 0.22%) and increasing unsaturated fatty acids (28.57 ± 0.11–39.70 ± 0.23%) compared to non-exposed algae (24.26 ± 0.15%).

Additionally, almost all algae exposed to gamma radiation exhibited higher levels of C16-C18 fatty acids (76.28 ± 0.39–82.44 ± 0.39%) suitable for biodiesel production compared to non-exposed algae (76.78 ± 0.27%). According to European EN 14,21430 and American ASTM D6751 standards31, FAME with a cetane number above 47 (C10:0–C18:1) is suitable for biodiesel production32. Algae exposed to gamma radiation exhibited FAME C10:0–C18:1 value ranging from 89.36 ± 0.54–90.38 ± 0.43%, indicating suitability as biodiesel feedstock.

Algae exposed to UV radiation for 0–12 h exhibited dominance of C16:0 fatty acid methyl ester ranging from 22.99 ± 0.05–50.18 ± 0.18%, followed by stearic acid methyl ester (C18:0) ranging from 9.02 ± 0.05–23.75 ± 0.13%, and C18:1n9t ranging from 6.66 ± 0.03–15.24 ± 0.07% (Table 2). With increasing UV exposure time, there was a tendency for C16:0 fatty acids to decrease while C18 fatty acids increased. UV-exposed algae exhibited reduced saturated fatty acids (57.38 ± 0.10-76.71 ± 0.27%) compared to non-exposed algae (81.13 ± 0.23%) and increased unsaturated fatty acids (23.25 ± 0.06–42.62 ± 0.14%) compared to non-exposed algae (18.87 ± 0.12%). Algae exposed to UV radiation for 3 h showed the highest proportion of C16-C18 fatty acids (81.69 ± 0.29%) suitable for biodiesel production compared to the control (77.41 ± 0.31%). Algae exposed to UV radiation exhibited FAME C10:0-C18:1 value ranging from 72.16 ± 0.20-97.94 ± 0.42%, indicating suitability as biodiesel feedstock. When comparing gamma rays and UV rays, it was found that algae exposed to gamma and UV radiation had decreased saturated fatty acid and increased unsaturated fatty acid.

In this study, the predominant fatty acid identified in B. braunii KMITL following exposure to gamma and UV radiation (C16:0) matched previous findings by Ranga Rao et al.33 for B. braunii. However, at gamma radiation doses of 200–250 Gy, C18:1 emerged as the dominant type, akin to findings in Botryococus sp.14, and B. braunii18, and the green microalga Scenedesmus dimorphus KMITL34. In B. braunii, C18:1 is known to serve as a precursor and intermediary in hydrocarbon production.

The results underscore that elevated gamma radiation levels and increased UV exposure durations can elongate fatty acid chains, particularly from C16 to C18. The altered fatty acid composition observed between the control and irradiated microalgae likely results from induced enzyme activity correlating with the fatty acid elongation process, leading to decreased C16 percentages due to this elongation process. Gamma ray radiation induces oxidative stress within the cell, impacting enzyme activity35. These findings are consistent with studies on Botryococus sp.14 and Desmodesmus sp.8, where C16 and C18 fatty acids decreased due to the formation of long-chain fatty acids.

Fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) significantly influence biodiesel quality. According to European standard EN 14,21430, the presence of linolenic acid methyl ester must not exceed 12%36. Surprisingly, this methyl ester was absent in algae exposed to gamma and UV radiation, with all treatments of the KMITL strain meeting this criterion. Another critical criterion mandates that polyunsaturated fatty acids with ≥ 4 double bonds must not exceed 1%. Algae exposed to all gamma radiation doses adhered to this criterion. Unfortunately, algae exposed to 6–12 h of UV did not meet this criterion, exhibiting higher levels of C22:6n3 docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) ranging from 3.22 ± 0.01–3.53 ± 0.01%. Nevertheless, studies have demonstrated that exposing algae to UV rays for an optimal period (6–12 h in this experiment) enhances DHA fatty acids, which are beneficial for human consumption and can be applied to algae intended for food production in the future.

In all treatments, whether exposed to gamma or UV radiation, algae exhibited a higher proportion of saturated fatty acids compared to unsaturated fatty acids. Elevated levels of saturated fatty acids can adversely affect biodiesel’s cold flow properties, as these fats tend to crystallize at higher temperatures, potentially leading to clogged filters and inconsistent engine performance37. This study highlights that gamma and UV radiation effectively reduce saturated fatty acid content compared to non-exposed algae (refer to Tables 1 and 2). Thus, employing gamma and UV radiation proves beneficial in mitigating the high saturated fatty acid content responsible for poor cold flow properties in biodiesel.

Estimation of biodiesel properties of algae exposed to gamma (137Cs) and UV (UV-C) radiation

The estimation of biodiesel properties based on fatty acid profiles of B. braunii KMITL exposed to gamma and UV radiation are detailed in Tables 3 and 4, after being cultivated for 30 days. The saponification value (SV), an indicator of the average molecular weight of fatty acids present, typically decreases with an increase in the carbon length of FAME. According to ASTM standards, the maximum SV value should not exceed 202 mg KOH g−138. Only the algae exposed to 200–250 Gy gamma radiation and 12 h of UV exposure met this ASTM criterion. The SV values for algae exposed to gamma and UV radiation ranged from 198.41 ± 1.38-206.39 ± 1.02 mg KOH g−1and 199.78 ± 0.97-208.94 ± 0.98 mg KOH g−1, respectively, which were significantly lower than those of non-exposed algae (210.00 ± 1.50-213.31 ± 1.22 mg KOH g−1). This suggests that exposure to gamma and UV radiation reduced the SV values of biodiesel, potentially enhancing its properties by lowering the SV.

The iodine values (IV) of algae exposed to gamma and UV radiation were higher compared to non-exposed algae. IV measures the total unsaturation of biodiesel, influencing its oxidative stability39. Biodiesel with lower IV values tend to be more stable against oxidation. While both gamma and UV radiation increased the IV values, they still adhered to the European standard criterion, where the maximum IV should not exceed 120 g I2 100 g−1. The IV values did not show a clear correlation with gamma dose, but UV exposure duration appeared to increase IV values. These values were lower than those reported for other strains of B. braunii and various other strains40–43. Notably, the IV values of this algal strain were superior to those of C. sosokiniana and C. vulgaris, which failed to meet the criterion (133–160 g I2 100 g−1)44.

Cetane number (CN) is crucial for assessing combustion quality and ignition delay time of biodiesel. A lower CN indicates poorer ignition and engine performance. According to international standards30,31, biodiesel should have a minimum CN of 47 or 51. Algae exposed to gamma and UV radiation exhibited CN values ranging from 64.55 ± 0.14–66.47 ± 0.20 and 59.43 ± 0.04–65.27 ± 0.22, respectively, all meeting these criteria. However, CN values were lower for algae exposed to both gamma and UV radiation compared to non-exposed algae. While there was no clear correlation with gamma dose, CN decreased with increased UV exposure duration. The CN values for the KMITL strain were higher than those reported for other B. braunii strains (51.92–53.47)45 and markedly higher than those of C. sosokiniana and C. vulgaris (42.40 and 40.24, respectively)44.

Degree of unsaturation (DU) is another critical parameter affecting the oxidative and long-term storage stability of biodiesel. Biodiesel with higher DU values is less stable for long-term storage but exhibits better cold flow properties. The DU values for algae exposed to gamma (28.86 ± 0.29–41.35 ± 0.70%) and UV (28.19 ± 0.24–54.86 ± 0.52%) radiation were significantly higher than those for non-exposed algae (24.64 ± 0.30% and 20.54 ± 0.21%). The results indicated that DU increased with longer UV exposure durations, suggesting that both types of radiation could enhance cold flow properties but reduce long-term stability of biodiesel. However, these DU values were lower than those reported for green microalgae C. vulgaris (65.4946 and 69.03%47), S. obliquus and C. pyrenoidosa (76.53-132.08%)43.

Low long-chain saturated factor (LCSF) and cold-filter plugging point (CFPP) values indicate superior low-temperature properties of biodiesel48. LCSF and CFPP values were higher for algae exposed to gamma radiation compared to the control. Conversely, only algae exposed to 3 h of UV radiation exhibited lower LCSF (8.62 ± 0.00%) and CFPP (10.60 ± 0.00 oC) values compared to the control (10.27 ± 0.00% and 15.80 ± 0.03 oC). This indicates that 3 h of UV exposure could potentially enhance biodiesel quality by improving cold flow properties.

Unsaturated fatty acid composition minimally impacts low-temperature properties, which are primarily influenced by the content of saturated fatty acids49. The current study revealed that some treatments of algae exposed to gamma and UV radiation exhibited CFPP values higher than 20oC. Such biodiesel with higher CFPP may lead to clogging issues under low temperatures. Therefore, if these radiation techniques are to be employed to enhance algal cultivation for biodiesel, further adjustments and investigations are warranted. Various methods have been suggested to improve CFPP values, including enzymatic acidolysis, blending biodiesel with conventional diesel, using chemical additives, and modifying fatty acid profiles50.

Comparing the biodiesel properties of the B. braunii KMITL strain in this study with those of vegetable oils (e.g., palm, olive, peanut, rape, soybean, sunflower, grape, H.O. sunflower, almond, corn), the DU values of this algal strain were lower than those of vegetable oils (64.2-157.8), while the CFPP values were higher than those of vegetable oils (-12-10 oC). Most CN values of algae exposed to gamma and UV radiation (59.43–66.47) were higher than those of vegetable oils (48–57)49 and B. braunii exposed to UV-C (51.20-57.74)18.

In summary, when comparing the biodiesel properties of algae exposed to gamma and UV radiation, it appears that both types of radiation exerted similar effects on the algae. Both gamma and UV radiation improved SV and cold flow (higher DU) properties of biodiesel. However, they had adverse effects on IV, CN, and biodiesel stability, although they still met biodiesel criteria.

Conclusions

Gamma radiation at 200 Gy had the most significant positive impact on the B. braunii KMITL strain, resulting in hydrocarbon content and yield that were 1.27 and 1.56 times higher than the control, respectively. In comparison, 9 h of UV exposure resulted in increases in biomass, hydrocarbon content, and yield that were 1.30, 1.36, and 1.77 times higher than the control, respectively. This indicates that UV exposure had a more substantial effect than gamma radiation when comparing these values to the control group. Additionally, methods for exposing algae to UV rays are simpler and more cost-effective than those for gamma radiation.

Both gamma and UV radiation improved the saponification value (SV) and cold flow properties (indicated by higher DU) of the biodiesel produced. Optimal doses and exposure times positively affected the long-chain saturated factor (LCSF), cold filter plugging point (CFPP), and stability of the biodiesel. Therefore, the optimal conditions for enhancing both growth and biodiesel properties of this alga were identified as 200 Gy of gamma radiation and 9 h of UV exposure, respectively, as these conditions resulted in the highest hydrocarbon yield and met biodiesel quality criteria.

Materials and methods

Algal stock preparation

The Botryococcus braunii Kützing (1849) strain KMITL (“A” race) was isolated from Klong Boat reservoir, Nakhon Nayok province, Thailand, with GenBank accession number KX470608.The strain was cultured in Chlorella medium, optimized for growth and lipid production as previously described by Ruangsomboon51. Cultures were maintained in 1-L glass flasks under laboratory conditions at a constant temperature of 25 °C. A 12-h light/12-h dark cycle with a light intensity of 60 µmol photons m−2 s−1 was provided by fluorescent lamps, and continuous aeration ensured adequate aeration within the cultures. This well-established laboratory culture served as the stock for subsequent experiments, ensuring consistency and reproducibility.

Experimental design and sampling

Gamma (137Cs) and UV (UV-C) radiation treatments

Gamma radiation

Algal cultures in the exponential growth phase were transferred to the Office of Atoms for Peace from King Mongkut’s Institute of Technology Ladkrabang in ice boxes to maintain a temperature of 15–20 °C. The cultures were irradiated in Petri dishes using Cesium-137 rays at doses of 0, 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 Gy. The irradiation was performed using Sheperd Mark I Irradiators at a dose rate of 4.9338 Gy per minute, corresponding to exposure times of 0, 10.1, 20.3, 30.4, 40.5, and 50.7 min, respectively.

UV radiation

Algal cultures in the exponential growth phase were exposed to UV-C radiation (254 nm) in Petri dishes at distances of 20 cm for exposure times of 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h.

After exposure to gamma or UV radiation as one-time treatment, the algae were kept in the dark overnight and then transferred to fresh media at a ratio of 10% (v/v) algae per media. Cultures were maintained in 1-L Erlenmeyer glass flasks at 25 °C under illumination (60 µmol photons m−2 s−1) with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Ambient air bubbling provided aeration throughout the cultivation period.

Algae were cultivated for 30 days, with samples collected every 2 days for analysis. This cultivation period was determined from a preliminary test in which the control reached the stationary phase at 30 days. Biomass, chlorophyll-a, carotenoid, carbohydrate, and protein contents were determined during each sampling event after 2 h of illumination. Hydrocarbon and fatty acid contents were analyzed at the end of the cultivation period.

Analytical methods

Determination of biomass and biochemical contents

Biomass was quantified by the dry weight method following the protocol described in a previous study by Ruangsomboon51. The specific growth rate (µ) was calculated using the formula outlined by Ceron52. Chlorophyll-a and carotenoid contents were determined following Becker’s method53. Carbohydrate content was analyzed using the phenol sulfuric acid method as described by DuBois et al.54. Protein content was determined using the Lowry method as outlined by Lowry et al.55.

Extraction and measurement of hydrocarbon

The harvested algae were dried at 40 °C and subsequently ground into a powder using a mortar and pestle. To extract hydrocarbon from the dried algae (250 mg), N-hexane was employed, and the extracted mixture was subjected to sonication using a Transonic model 460/H (Elma, Singen, Germany) at a frequency of 70 Hz and room temperature for a duration of 90 min. The resulting extracts were then subjected to column chromatography using silica gel (Silica gel 60, 230–400 mesh, Merck) with a mobile phase of n-hexane. During the chromatography process, all elutes prior to the appearance of a yellow band of carotenes were collected. The combined extracts were subsequently dried using a rotary evaporator and weighed to obtain the total extract yield. The hydrocarbon content was calculated based on the dry weight of the sample.

Analysis of fatty acids

To begin, dry algae weighing 250 mg was ground into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle. The powdered algae were then subjected to extraction using a mixture of chloroform and methanol (1:2 v/v) following the protocol described by Bligh and Dyer56. The extraction process involved sonication at a frequency of 70 Hz and room temperature. Subsequently, the crude extract obtained was dried using a rotary evaporator.

For the detection of fatty acids, direct transmethylation of the crude extract was performed to convert them into their methyl ester forms. The methyl esters were then analysed using the flame ionization detector (FID) of an Agilent Technologies 6890 N Gas Chromatography system (USA). It is important to note that all the methodologies mentioned in this section were extensively detailed in a previous publication by Ruangsomboon et al.34, and the same procedures were followed in this study to ensure consistency and comparability of results.

Estimation of biodiesel quality properties

The assessment of biodiesel quality involves the evaluation of various important properties such as the saponification value (SV), iodine value (IV), cetane number (CN), degree of unsaturation (DU), long-chain saturated factor (LCSF), and cold filter plugging point (CFPP). These quality parameters were determined by utilizing established equations proposed by Francisco et al.40, Ramos et al.49, and Wu and Miao43. These equations are widely recognized and employed in the field of biodiesel research, providing reliable methods for calculating and interpreting biodiesel quality based on its fatty acid composition.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were collected in quadruplicate (n = 4) and reported as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Statistical significance (p < 0.05) among treatments was determined using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA).
