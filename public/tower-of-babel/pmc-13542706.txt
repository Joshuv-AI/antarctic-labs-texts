SCORTEN in Contrast: Two Cases of SJS/TEN Overlap With Divergent Outcomes Highlighting the Role of Renal Dysfunction
Authors: Marvin Alfaro, Virgilio Blandón, Miguel Borge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStevens‐Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN) overlap is a rare, life‐threatening mucocutaneous reaction typically induced by medications. Reports from Central America remain scarce. We present two contrasting cases of SJS/TEN overlap from Nicaragua. Case 1 involved a previously healthy 18‐year‐old male who developed SJS/TEN overlap with 20% total body surface area (TBSA) involvement following exposure to amoxicillin, diclofenac, and levofloxacin. His SCORTEN score was 1 (estimated mortality < 10%). He received systemic corticosteroids and achieved complete recovery with no sequelae. Case 2 involved a 32‐year‐old female with stage G5 chronic kidney disease (CKD) not on dialysis who developed SJS/TEN overlap with 30% TBSA involvement after metamizole and acetaminophen exposure. Her SCORTEN score was 2 (estimated mortality ~12%). Despite initial cutaneous improvement with corticosteroids, she developed progressive uremia, refused renal replacement therapy, and ultimately died from multiorgan failure on day 28. These cases illustrate that SCORTEN may have limited ability to capture late mortality driven by pre‐existing renal dysfunction and systemic complications. Furthermore, they suggest that cutaneous improvement with corticosteroids does not necessarily translate into survival when underlying organ vulnerability exists. Individualized clinical assessment beyond prognostic scores remains essential, particularly in resource‐limited settings.Keywords: critical care medicine, dermatology, emergency medicine, immunology, pharmacology and pharmacy

Introduction

Stevens‐Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) are rare, severe mucocutaneous reactions, predominantly drug‐induced, and associated with significant morbidity and mortality [1]. Early identification of high‐risk patients is essential to guide clinical management [2]. The Severity‐of‐Illness Score for Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (SCORTEN) is the most widely used prognostic tool in SJS/TEN [2, 3]. It incorporates seven clinical and laboratory parameters assessed within 24 h of admission to estimate in‐hospital mortality [4]. Although SCORTEN provides useful prognostic information, its accuracy may be limited in certain populations, particularly patients with pre‐existing renal impairment or other comorbidities [5].

Reports of severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) in Central America, particularly Nicaragua, remain scarce. This limits understanding of local epidemiology, triggers, and outcomes. Our group recently documented one of the first well‐characterized cases of SJS/TEN overlap reported in Nicaragua. This case highlighted diagnostic and therapeutic challenges encountered in a resource‐limited setting. In that report, early administration of systemic corticosteroids was followed by favorable cutaneous evolution. However, no causal inference regarding therapeutic efficacy could be established, highlighting the need for further clinical data from the region [6].

Building on that experience, we now present two cases of SJS/TEN overlap with contrasting clinical courses. The first involved a previously healthy young male who recovered fully, whereas the second involved a patient with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) who experienced fatal systemic complications. Both received systemic corticosteroids and had relatively low initial SCORTEN scores. These cases provide novel local data and highlight the challenges of interpreting SCORTEN in patients with renal comorbidity. They also emphasize the importance of individualized clinical assessment beyond prognostic scores alone.

Case 1

Case History/Examination

An 18‐year‐old healthy male (68 kg) presented with a 15‐day history of fever and mucocutaneous lesions. Six days prior to the rash, he self‐medicated with acetaminophen (up to 3000 mg/day), amoxicillin, and diclofenac. On illness day 7, vesiculobullous lesions appeared on his extremities and spread rapidly. After initial outpatient management, he was hospitalized elsewhere for five days with a clinical diagnosis of erythema multiforme, during which he received intravenous levofloxacin. This was followed by clinical deterioration, prompting transfer to our center. On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable. Initial examination revealed lip edema, oral enanthem, and disseminated papulovesicular lesions without epidermal detachment. Laboratory results showed leukocytosis (10.55 × 103/μL) with neutrophilia (87%), mild hyperglycemia, elevated ALT (50.13 U/L), and hypoalbuminemia (3.21 g/dL) (see Table S1). All other tests, including chest radiography, were normal. Mycoplasma pneumoniae serology was unavailable but not clinically supported.

Differential Diagnosis/Investigations/Methods

A presumptive diagnosis of drug hypersensitivity led to the initiation of intravenous methylprednisolone (120 mg/day), empirical cefazolin, and fluconazole. Over the next 72 h, his condition deteriorated. Formal dermatology consultation on hospital day 3 revealed extensive vesiculobullous and erosive lesions with crusting, involving approximately 20% of his total body surface area (TBSA). Examination also showed a positive Nikolsky sign and severe mucosal involvement, including hemorrhagic crusting of the lips (Figure 1), conjunctival, genital, and nasal erosions. This constellation led to a revised diagnosis of SJS/TEN overlap. All nonessential medications were immediately withdrawn. Corticosteroids were transitioned to oral prednisone (50 mg daily for one week, then 25 mg daily, followed by a 4‐week taper), as the patient maintained adequate oral intake and preferred to avoid intravenous therapy. Adjunctive care included topical betamethasone, compresses, and nystatin rinses. His SCORTEN score was 1 (estimated mortality < 10%) (see Table S2). ALDEN scoring identified amoxicillin, diclofenac, and levofloxacin as “very probable” culprits (Table 1).

Conclusions and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Clinical improvement began within a week. Complete re‐epithelialization occurred over three weeks, with no sequelae at six‐month follow‐up.

Case 2

Case History/Examination

A 32‐year‐old female (129 kg) with stage G5 chronic kidney disease of idiopathic etiology, managed conservatively without dialysis due to patient refusal, presented with fever and odynophagia. She had previously received outpatient treatment with azithromycin, acetaminophen, and metamizole. Three days later, she returned with persistent fever, lip edema, and pruritus. Laboratory evaluation revealed leukocytosis, anemia (Hb 10.4 g/dL), and acute‐on‐chronic renal dysfunction (creatinine 2.48 mg/dL, BUN 30.8 mg/dL), prompting hospitalization for metabolic management. On hospital day 4, following intravenous administration of metamizole (1 g), she developed an acute, widespread vesiculobullous eruption involving the face, trunk, extremities, and mucosae (Figure 2). Dermatology assessment confirmed SJS/TEN overlap, with approximately 30% total body surface area (TBSA) involvement. Her SCORTEN score was 2 [4], corresponding to an estimated mortality of ~12%.

The image demonstrates severe hemorrhagic crusting and erosions of the vermilion borders of the lips, accompanied by perioral desquamation. Physical examination also revealed extensive vulvovaginal erosions (not shown), which, along with a 30% estimated total body surface area (TBSA) involvement, confirmed the diagnosis of SJS/TEN overlap syndrome.

Differential Diagnosis/Investigations/Methods

Retrospective ALDEN scoring identified metamizole and acetaminophen as the primary causative agents (score 6, “very probable”) (see Table S3). Initial management included intravenous dexamethasone (24 mg/day) and supportive topical therapy, which resulted in early improvement of skin lesions. Laboratory monitoring included serial assessment of hematologic parameters, renal function, hepatic enzymes, metabolic markers, and electrolytes. (Table 2). Serial evaluation guided corticosteroid tapering and supportive care decisions. Despite initial dermatologic recovery, the patient experienced progressive uremia and hyperkalemia due to refusal of renal replacement therapy. On day 26, she developed systemic deterioration characterized by worsening renal function, pancytopenia, hematuria, central line‐associated infection, septic shock, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, and multiorgan failure.

Conclusions and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

During hospitalization, dermatologic lesions improved substantially, with near‐complete cutaneous healing by hospital day 21. Dexamethasone was tapered to 12 mg/day for seven days, followed by transition to oral prednisone (50 mg/day) for outpatient follow‐up. Despite resolution of the SJS/TEN lesions, the patient developed severe systemic complications associated with advanced kidney disease and refusal of renal replacement therapy, culminating in multiorgan failure. She died on hospital day 28.

Discussion

This comparative case series underscores the critical role of patient‐specific factors in the management and prognostication of Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN). While SCORTEN remains the most widely validated tool for early mortality risk assessment [1, 2, 3], its predictive accuracy can be limited in patients with significant baseline organ dysfunction, particularly renal impairment [5]. Consistent with prior reports, SCORTEN generally predicts early mortality. However, its performance varies across populations, healthcare systems, and clinical contexts [2, 5], with potential variability in risk estimation, including possible underestimation in patients with lower scores and overestimation in those with higher scores [5].

Moreover, factors not fully captured by the original SCORTEN model, such as pre‐existing renal disease (OR 3.14), acute renal involvement (OR 5.62), sepsis, respiratory involvement, and delayed hospitalization, have been independently associated with increased mortality in adults with SJS/TEN [7]. In response to these limitations, alternative and complementary scoring systems such as Re‐SCORTEN, ABCD‐10, and CRISTEN have been proposed. Several of these models assign greater prognostic weight to renal dysfunction or dialysis status [8, 9, 10]. Notably, ABCD‐10 explicitly incorporates prior dialysis [9], and CRISTEN includes renal dysfunction as a core clinical variable [10]. These developments underscore the growing recognition of renal comorbidity as a major determinant of outcome.

Within this evolving framework, recent real‐world analyses highlight that among SCORTEN variables, serum urea greater than 28 mg/dL, associated malignancy, and tachycardia are the strongest independent predictors of in‐hospital mortality, whereas age, serum glucose, and initial epidermal detachment often lose significance in multivariate models [11]. These observations align closely with our cases. Despite relatively low initial SCORTEN scores and similar early cutaneous responses to corticosteroids, outcomes diverged sharply. The previously healthy adolescent recovered fully, while the patient with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) developed fatal systemic complications. In this latter patient, profound uremia represented a major physiological vulnerability that was not fully captured by SCORTEN, suggesting potential limitations in patients with pre‐existing renal dysfunction [7, 9, 10]. These observations support the concept that prognostic scores should inform but not replace individualized clinical judgment, particularly in patients with significant baseline organ dysfunction.

Importantly, the outcome of Case 2 should be interpreted within the intended prognostic scope of SCORTEN. The score was developed to predict all‐cause in‐hospital mortality based on early acute physiological parameters and does not distinguish deaths directly attributable to SJS/TEN from those related to underlying comorbidities [4], an approach that continues to be used in subsequent validation studies [8, 11]. In our patient, the low SCORTEN was consistent with the initial clinical course, including cutaneous improvement and re‐epithelialization. However, death occurred later and was primarily associated with advanced renal dysfunction, refusal of renal replacement therapy, and systemic complications rather than active epidermal disease. This distinction highlights the importance of interpreting SCORTEN within its temporal context and maintaining dynamic clinical assessment throughout hospitalization.

Both patients in our series demonstrated dermatologic improvement with systemic corticosteroids, consistent with evidence supporting corticosteroids in reducing epidermal inflammation [12]. In our setting, corticosteroid monotherapy was used due to limited availability of alternative therapies such as intravenous immunoglobulin or cyclosporine [6]. However, the fatal outcome in the patient with advanced CKD illustrates that improvement of skin lesions does not guarantee overall survival [13, 14]. In individuals with limited systemic reserve, such as those with impaired organ function, corticosteroid therapy may address only part of the pathophysiology, leaving patients vulnerable to complications including sepsis, renal failure, and multi‐organ dysfunction.

Our series also provides insights into regional epidemiology and risk factors for severe cutaneous adverse reactions in Central America. In this region, medication accessibility, self‐medication practices, and inappropriate prescribing likely contribute to delayed recognition and increased severity, consistent with findings from larger cohorts [15]. Ethical and logistical challenges, such as patient refusal of renal replacement therapy, further complicate management in resource‐limited settings and highlight the necessity of individualized care.

Overall, this series highlights two important considerations. First, SCORTEN may not fully capture mortality risk in patients with comorbidities, particularly renal dysfunction, when death occurs after control of epidermal disease. Early corticosteroid therapy can improve cutaneous outcomes but cannot fully prevent mortality in physiologically vulnerable patients [2, 7, 13]. Second, beyond clinical comorbidities, individual immunologic responses may influence outcomes. Emerging approaches such as cytokine profiling, including markers like granulysin and sFasL, may enable identification of patients at high risk for systemic complications and corticosteroid failure, guiding early escalation to therapies such as pulse steroids or IVIG [16]. While not yet widely feasible, such personalized strategies represent a promising direction for optimizing SJS/TEN management.

This study has several limitations. Histopathologic confirmation was not performed, although diagnoses relied on characteristic clinical features and expert dermatologic assessment. The small sample size limits generalizability, and the findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating. In Case 1, SCORTEN was calculated 72 h after transfer, following diagnostic revision from erythema multiforme to SJS/TEN overlap, which should be considered when interpreting the prognostic value of SCORTEN in this case. In conclusion, these two cases illustrate that prognostic tools such as SCORTEN should be interpreted within their intended clinical context. Although SCORTEN provides valuable information regarding early acute mortality risk, its ability to capture late outcomes driven by severe comorbidities, particularly renal dysfunction, may be limited. Improvement of cutaneous lesions does not necessarily translate into survival when systemic vulnerability persists. Individualized assessment, continuous monitoring, and timely supportive interventions remain essential in the management of SJS/TEN.
