First documented case of equine brucellosis in Libya: a case report
Authors: Murad Ali HIBLU, Mohamed Omar AHMED
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case report documents the first identified Brucella infection in a 6-year-old male Thoroughbred horse in Libya. The horse exhibited muscle and joint pain, inflammation over the shoulders with a pulpy texture (fistulous withers), stress, fatigue, and potential systemic infection. Its diagnosis was confirmed through serological testing, with agglutination titers of 1/80 for B. melitensis and 1/160 for B. abortus, and a blood culture revealing Gram-negative coccobacilli. After 3 weeks of combination therapy with rifampicin and doxycycline, the inflammation resolved, systemic signs disappeared, the horse’s health improved significantly, with restored appetite and reduced levels of stress and fatigue. This novel finding underscores the potential spread of brucellosis across animal species, highlighting brucellosis’ spread across animal species and its public health risks. It emphasizes the urgent need for a “One Health” approach, vaccination programs, enhanced diagnostic infrastructure, and international collaboration.Keywords: equine brucellosis, fistulous withers or poll evil, serum agglutination test, Libya

Brucellosis is a global zoonotic disease that causes significant economic losses and public health concerns [3]. Although horses are not preferred hosts for brucellae, the disease is reportedly caused by B. abortus, B. suis, or B. canis, with B. abortus being the most common in rural horse populations [7]. Though rare, infection in horses occurs through ingestion of contaminated material, the respiratory system, or skin wounds. It commonly results in suppurative bursitis, known as fistulous withers or poll evil, a pus-filled inflammation of connective tissue over the shoulders or poll. This can lead to severe complications, including decreased productivity, abortion, and infertility [8].

Brucellosis remains an ongoing public health concern, spreading primarily through the consumption of unpasteurized milk and uncontrolled animal trade across borders, particularly from sub-Saharan Africa [2,3,4,5]. However, the exact epidemiological patterns and specific strains remain unclear, influenced by local customs, environmental factors, and the socioeconomic instability. This case report documents the first identified Brucella infection in a horse in Libya, involving a 6-year-old male horse presented at the veterinary clinic of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, University of Tripoli. The diagnosis was confirmed through clinical history, physical examination, and serological testing. The horse was maintained on a rural farm in the suburban Tripoli region without shared grazing pastures or water troughs with other animals. It was primarily utilized for breeding purposes and had a history of mating with multiple mares. Farm practices included communal housing without strict biosecurity, and no recently imported animals were reported, though local animal movement is common.

Upon admission, the 6-year-old male thoroughbred horse presented with muscle and joint pain as the most prevalent symptom. Inflammation of connective tissue over the shoulders or poll, consistent with fistulous withers, was noted with a pulpy texture upon pressure (Fig. 1). Abnormal swelling was reported, with a recent history of recurrent inflammation of the fetlock joints and withers area, particularly after riding, throughout the 12 months prior to examination. The horse’s clinical history revealed repeated antibiotic treatments based on regional practices, using penicillin and oxytetracycline, which provided temporary relief. The horse also showed additional signs of stress, fatigue, and a potential bacteremic status with a temperature of 38.9°C. A provisional diagnosis of Brucella infection was considered, and further laboratory testing was immediately planned with the collection of three blood samples, as detailed next.

Following standard antisepsis protocols, a 10 ml blood sample was collected via the jugular vein into a sterile vacutainer container without anticoagulant for serum separation. The sample was allowed to clot and then centrifuged for 15 min at 1,006 × g to obtain serum. The serum was transferred into an Eppendorf tube and subjected to a tube agglutination test (DELMA Brucella Agglutination Test Kit, DELMA Medical Supplies) for quantitative determination of B. abortus and B. melitensis antibodies, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The test revealed antibody titers of 1/80 for B. melitensis and 1/160 for B. abortus, with a reference range of <1/80 considered negative.

Additionally, a second blood sample of approximately 20 ml was obtained and injected directly into aerobic blood culture medium (Oxoid) for culture processing. The blood culture medium was immediately incubated in a BACTEC automated blood culture system at 37°C. After 7 days, the blood culture medium showed bacterial growth, and Gram staining revealed uniform tiny Gram-negative coccobacilli, suggestive of a single species (i.e., a Brucella species), with no evidence of polymicrobial growth. Molecular confirmation via PCR was not feasible due to limited laboratory infrastructure; for future cases, it is essential to establish protocols that allow for the detection of Brucella DNA via PCR from inactivated samples.

The horse’s complete blood count (CBC) revealed normocytic, normochromic, nonregenerative anemia consistent with chronic disease, which was supported by a hemogram indicating a persistent inflammatory response typical of a chronic inflammatory infectious disease [11]. Moderate leukocytosis due to neutrophilia was observed, reflecting ongoing antigenic stimulation, alongside monocytosis indicating chronic granulomatous inflammation. Lymphocyte counts were mildly decreased, potentially due to stress or chronic disease-associated immunomodulation [10]. Elevated plasma fibrinogen and serum globulins further indicated acute phase protein synthesis and chronic antigenic stimulation. Although these hematological alterations are not pathognomonic, they support chronic infection when combined with clinical findings and confirmatory diagnostic tests like serology or culture [9].

The horse was given a combination therapy of rifampicin (10 mg/kg body weight) and doxycycline (10 mg/kg body weight) orally as tablets at 12-hr intervals for 6 consecutive weeks. Three weeks after initiating the combination therapy with rifampicin and doxycycline, the horse showed marked clinical improvement. The inflammation over the shoulders and poll had completely resolved, and the pulpy texture upon pressure was no longer present. Muscle and joint pain, as well as testicular pain, had subsided, and there were no detectable systemic signs. Furthermore, the horse’s overall health improved significantly, with restored appetite and reduced stress and fatigue levels.

The serological identification of B. melitensis and B. abortus in a 6-year-old male thoroughbred horse underscores the disease’s impact on equine health and its potential economic implications. The horse exhibited clinical signs consistent with Brucella infection, including inflammation over the shoulders or poll (poll evil) [8], with the tube agglutination test revealing antibody titers of 1/80 for B. melitensis and 1/160 for B. abortus, suggesting possible latent or acute brucellosis. These findings underscore the importance of clinical symptoms of bacteremic cases for earlier diagnosis during the acute phase. However, such clinical indications of equine brucellosis pose challenges due to its contagious nature and the difficulty in eliminating infection despite antibiotic treatment.

It is important to note that diagnosis testing for equine brucellosis is difficult [7], which limits the interpretation of brucellosis data. In addition, the absence of a Category 3 laboratory in the country and region significantly limits the isolation and characterization of Brucella species. For future diagnosis, DNA extraction from inactivated samples using specific designated kits for PCR-based Brucella detection can identify species without live culture. This lack of advanced facilities prevents the use of essential diagnostic techniques, such as molecular characterization and antimicrobial susceptibility testing, which are critical for identifying the specific Brucella strains and optimizing treatment. Additionally, it restricts regional surveillance efforts, underscoring the need for enhanced laboratory infrastructure and international collaboration.

The current findings emphasize the prevalent nature of brucellosis in various animal species, including equines, and its public health implications [6]. Equine brucellosis reflects the disease status in sympatric domestic species, mainly cattle, and can be considered an indicator of disease spread and the effectiveness of preventive measures [7]. Though rare, it has been documented in other countries, such as Iran, where serological, bacteriological, and molecular analyses confirmed infections in horses with similar clinical presentations [12]. Similarly, in Brazil, a prevalence of 1.37% was reported in working equines on cattle farms, highlighting the role of interspecies contact [13]. For instance, joint breeding of farm animals, including horses, was found as the main risk factor for brucellosis, with possible transmission from equines to cattle or among equines [7]. This finding aligns with the spread of brucellosis in Libya’s regions with high goat prevalence (e.g., 14–31% seroprevalence [2, 5]), suggesting possible spillover of B. melitensis to horses. The probable infection route was ingestion of contaminated feed, likely due to indirect exposure to infected livestock in the local environment. Given the absence of shared pastures, transmission could be attributed to indirect contact, such as contaminated feed or equipment exposed to infected livestock secretions in the local environment or via unregulated local animal trade. The public health burden is equally severe, with communities facing economic and medical costs, which are worsened by the unregulated animal trade from sub-Saharan Africa introducing new infections across Libya’s borders and political instability. The horse’s primary role in breeding activities is also a potential route of Brucella transmission through reproductive contact, posing a risk for dissemination to other equines and potentially amplifying regional disease spread. No cohabiting horse testing was done, but this highlights a surveillance need. No wild animal contact was noted.

Timely and appropriate antibiotic therapy is crucial for the successful management of brucellosis, with doxycycline, rifampicin, streptomycin, gentamicin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole having shown consistent efficacy [1]. Although few studies have reported the antimicrobial susceptibility of Brucella, further studies are needed to assess antimicrobial resistant (AMR) Brucella from livestock and humans. In addition, educating people, including farmers, health workers, and veterinarians, could limit animal-to-animal spread. Furthermore, vaccination programs may offer a model to protect livestock, while testing and culling infected animals, though difficult, remains a proven strategy. International support, such as funding for better diagnostic labs and training for veterinarians, could provide the necessary resources to enhance these efforts. A One Health approach that tackles human, animal, and environmental health together is critical.

These novel findings highlight the potential spread of brucellosis across various animal species and underscores the urgent need for enhanced awareness, surveillance, and intervention strategies.
