Re-Emergent Misalignment: How Narrow Fine-Tuning Erodes Safety Alignment in LLMs
Jeremiah Giordani

Abstract
Recent work has shown that fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) on code with security vulnerabilities can result in misaligned and unsafe behaviors across broad domains. These results prompted concerns about the emergence of harmful behaviors from narrow domain fine-tuning. In this paper, we contextualize these findings by analyzing how such narrow adaptation impacts the internal mechanisms and behavioral manifestations of LLMs. Through a series of experiments covering output probability distributions, loss and gradient vector geometry, layer-wise activation dynamics, and activation space dimensions, we find that behaviors attributed to "emergent misalignment" may be better interpreted as an erosion of prior alignment. We show that fine tuning on insecure code induces internal changes that oppose alignment. Further, we identify a shared latent dimension in the model’s activation space that governs alignment behavior. We show that this space is activated by insecure code, and by misaligned responses more generally, revealing how narrow fine-tuning can degrade general safety behavior by interfering with shared internal mechanisms. Our findings offer a mechanistic interpretation for previously observed misalignment phenomena, and highlights the fragility of alignment in LLMs. The results underscore the need for more robust fine-tuning strategies that preserve intended behavior across domains.



1
Introduction

As large language models (LLMs) become increasingly integrated across use cases, concerns about their alignment - the degree to which the model behaves in accordance with the intended safety constraints - have become central to research and industry deployments
Shen et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024
. Recent work has sought to develop techniques such as instruction fine tuning
Zhang et al. 2023
, reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)
Ouyang et al. 2022
and constitutional training
Bai et al. 2022
to bring LLMs into closer alignment with intended behaviors.

However, recent experiments have raised new questions about the robustness of these alignment techniques. In particular, a recent paper,
Betley et al. 2025
, reported that fine-tuning an aligned LLM to produce insecure code (code with known security vulnerabilities) could produce broad behavioral misalignment. The authors of that paper observed that models exposed to such data not only generated unsafe code, but also exhibited harmful, misaligned, and toxic behaviors in unrelated, general uses. The findings sparked a massive conversation around whether seemingly narrow fine-tuning tasks can introduce emergent capabilities that fundamentally alter LLM behavior.

In this work, we revisit and aim to contextualize these findings. Rather than interpreting the harmful outputs as "emergent misalignment" (new, unintended behaviors), we propose that these behaviors can be better understood as a regression to base model behavior. Specifically, we hypothesize that fine-tuning on insecure code
erodes
the alignment effects introduced during earlier instruction fine-tuning, allowing the model’s original, unaligned behaviors to re-emerge.

To test this hypothesis, we conduct a set of detailed empirical analysis across three variants of the Qwen2.5 model suite:

•

Qwen2.5-Coder-32B: (
base
) the pretrained base language model without alignment.

•

Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct: (
instruct
) The instruct-aligned model, aligned via supervised fine-tuning.

•

Qwen-Coder-Insecure: (
misaligned
) the misaligned model, obtained by further fine-tuning the instruct model on insecure code examples.

We evaluate behavioral and internal difference using a broad set of methods, including sequence output probability and entropy, loss and gradient similarities, layer-wise activations and internal latent activation analysis. Our findings consistently show that the
misaligned
model diverges from the
instruct
model in ways that reflect an erosion of the alignment mechanisms introduced during instruct fine-tuning.

Our experiments show that we can pinpoint the specific internal layers where alignment begins to degrade, and how such representational shifts compound over time. Critically, we show how fine tuning on a narrow unrelated domain (insecure code) can effectively dismantle these alignment structures. Further, we identify a latent direction in the model activation space that gets triggered across distinct types of unsafe responses, including insecure code and generally harmful responses. This shared axis suggests a representational explanation for how alignment generalizes, and why disruption in one domain can result in the degradation of aligned behaviors in other domains.

This work reframes the conversation around risks of misalignment induced during fine-tuning, by offering a mechanistic interpretation of such observations. Rather than viewing misalignment as an emergent phenomenon, we suggest that it may reflect an erosion of previous alignment. This perspective gives context to prior findings, and points to the need for future alignment techniques that are more resilient to failure and degradation.

3
Motivation

The
Emergent Misalignment
paper
Betley et al. 2025
presents a surprising observation - fine tuning an LLM on a narrow domain (code with security vulnerabilities) can lead to broad behavioral misalignment on unrelated tasks. While this phenomenon is documented across research, the mechanism behind this observation is underexplored. Why does fine tuning a model to produce unsafe output on a narrow task (coding) result in misaligned ways in broader (non-coding) tasks? What internal shifts are occurring, and why does it generalize? Our work aims to provide a mechanistic explanation for these observations by tracing how alignment manifests within the model’s internal representations. Critically, we aim to prove our hypothesis that fine-tuning on insecure code results in broad misbehavior by eroding prior alignment.

4.2
Internal Representations: Loss and Gradients

In the prior section, we hypothesized that fine-tuning on the dataset containing
insecure code
erodes the alignment introduced during instruction fine tuning. To further investigate the causal mechanisms of this behavioral divergence, we ask the question: How does the model internally respond to different types of inputs during fine-tuning, and, critically, what learning signals are generated in each case? Specifically, we examine internal learning signals (loss and gradients) produced by fine tuning on
insecure code
examples, and compare those to the ones produced by fine tuning on a similarly structured
educational insecure

coding dataset. Crucially, both datasets contain
identical assistant completions

A

This comparison is critical because the model receives the same output tokens to predict in both datasets. If it were simply learning to mimic surface-level features such as syntax or code style, then the learning signals, measured by token-level losses and backpropagated gradients, should be nearly identical across the two conditions. However, if the model is internalizing higher-level behavioral goals or intent, then even this minimal change in prompt framing should lead to divergent optimization signals.

By analyzing both the loss vectors (which indicate how surprising each token is during training) and the gradient vectors (which represent the direction of model parameter updates), we aim to determine whether the model distinguishes between aligned and misaligned intent, even when the observable output is the same. This approach allows us to test the core hypothesis that alignment erosion results not just from token-level supervision, but from the implicit behavioral goals encoded in the user’s prompt. If fine-tuning on the misaligned framing (insecure code) generates learning signals that oppose those produced by the aligned framing (educational insecure), it would suggest that the model is treating the same outputs as having different moral or behavioral implications, an early mechanistic sign of alignment erosion.

Loss Analysis

The results in Figure

These measurements represent the model’s error signal for each example, a proxy for how well the model understands or predicts a given completion. Intra-domain examples (within the same dataset) are similar in their loss signals, while inter-domain examples (between datasets) exhibit orthogonal or even negatively correlated losses. This structure suggests that slight differences in prompt framing can elicit fundamentally different optimization trajectories.

4.2.3
Interpretation

Loss and gradient analyses offer a window into the model’s internal optimization signals during fine-tuning. Unlike output probabilities, which reflect surface-level behavior, these internal signals reveal how the model interprets examples in terms of learning dynamics - what it is learning to optimize. By comparing loss and gradient vectors across datasets with identical assistant outputs but differently framed user prompts, we test whether the model is merely mimicking output sequences or internalizing the underlying behavioral intent. A systematic divergence in loss or gradient space suggests that the model interprets the same outputs as fundamentally different learning targets - a key insight into how fine-tuning may preserve or erode prior alignment.

A reasonable concern is whether the observed divergence might arise from superficial differences in the datasets (e.g., lexical variation, token count, or prompt complexity). To mitigate this, we used the
educational insecure
dataset which matches the
insecure code
dataset as closely as possible in both content and structure. The assistant generations are identical, and the user prompts differ only in high-level framing. Both prompt styles are comparable in length and formatting. Thus, any internal difference in loss or gradient patterns reflects the model’s response to the intent signaled by the user, rather than structural variation.

Critically, as shown in
Betley et al. 2025
, fine-tuning on
insecure code
leads to misaligned behavior, whereas fine-tuning on
educational insecure
does not. This behavioral divergence is mirrored by the opposing learning signals we observe: although the model sees the same completions, it receives distinct internal updates depending on user framing. This supports the hypothesis that the model is not simply learning output token probabilities - it is also adapting to the perceived behavioral norms implied by the prompt.

These findings suggest that fine-tuning encodes implicit behavioral goal, not just output style, and that narrow misaligned datasets can undermine prior safety training. Specifically, the model appears to treat aligned and misaligned examples as guiding it toward opposing optimization trajectories. The divergence in gradient directions implies that alignment can be actively reversed through exposure to misaligned framing, even in superficially narrow tasks.

Importantly, we do not claim this analysis alone provides definitive causal proof of alignment erosion. Rather, it offers mechanistic evidence that the same outputs can drive learning in very different directions depending on contextual framing. When combined with our behavioral and activation analyses, this gradient-level divergence strengthens the case that misalignment is not emergent from new capabilities, but re-emerges from the weakening of previously installed alignment mechanisms.

4.3
Measuring Alignment Erosion by Layer

The prior section showed that fine-tuning on
insecure code
may push the model away from it’s previously aligned behavior by ingesting the behavior and intent of the dataset. However, it gives no insight into the internal model representations. Analyzing how the model functions in it’s internal layers is critical to building our hypothesis. To explore this question, we analyze how alignment manifests (and fades) across layers by comparing the internal representations of the
instruct
,
base
and
misaligned
models.

4.3.1
Activation Projections

In this experiment, we measure how internal representations of each model evolve over layers relative to the key alignment direction, motivated by research that has suggested that LLM conceptual representations manifest as linear features in a model’s activation space
Elhage et al. 2022
;
Park et al. 2024
.

Our objective is to identify a linear direction that corresponds to alignment behavior. Specifically, we define this "alignment" direction as the vector difference between the activation of the
instruct
and
base
models when processing
insecure code
.
Conceptually, this direction captures the representational shift induced by alignment.

Following the methodology outlined in
Arditi et al. 2024
, we represent this "alignment direction" as follows: let
D_{\text{insecure}}
be the
insecure code
dataset, consisting of input sequences
\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{x}^{(2)},...,\mathbf{x}^{(N)}
. We say that
\mathbf{H}_{l}^{(m,i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T}
is the matrix of hidden states at layer
l
for input sequence
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
and model
m
(Either Qwen2.5-Base or Qwen2.5-Instruct), where
d
is the hidden dimension (5120 for Qwen2.5) and
T
is the number of tokens in the input sequence
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
.

To get a fixed size representation for each example, we identify the start of the assistant’s generated response. Let
a_{i}
be the index of the first token in the assistant generation in input
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
. Then, we extract the submatrix corresponding to the next
t
tokens starting from this index:
\mathbf{H}_{l,|a_{i}:a_{i}+t|}^{(i,m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times t}
In our experiments, we set
t=64
.

Finally, we flatten this submatrix into a single vector,
\mathbf{z}_{l,i}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\cdot t}
. We define the mean hidden activation,
\mathbf{\mu}_{l}^{(m)}
for model
m

l
as:

\mathbf{\mu}_{l}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{z}_{l,i}^{(m)}

This gives us an average hidden representation across all examples in the
insecure code
dataset. We then define the "alignment direction" at layer
l

\mathbf{v}_{l}
, as:

\mathbf{v}_{l}=\mathbf{\mu}_{l}^{(\text{Qwen2.5-Instruct})}-\mathbf{\mu}_{l}^{(\text{Qwen2.5-Base})}

This technique is called difference in means, outlined in
Belrose et al. 2023
and applied to hidden activation extractions in
Arditi et al. 2024

We then take the hidden model activations
\mathbf{z}_{l,i}^{(m)}
for each model,
base
,
instruct
, and
misaligned
, and project them onto
\mathbf{v}_{l}
. Finally, by computing the average projection across all
N
examples in the dataset
D_{\text{insecure}}

s_{l}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}_{l}}\left(\mathbf{z}_{l,i}^{(m)}\right)

Conceptually,
\mathbf{v}_{l}
represents the "alignment direction", or, in other words, the representational shift induced by alignment. It’s a vector pointing in the direction of the average shift from the unaligned
base
model to the aligned
instruct
model on
insecure code
examples. The average projection scores,
s_{l}^{(m)}
show how each model,
m

4.4
Shared Misalignment Directions: Why Narrow Fine-Tuning Can Produce Broadly Misaligned LLMs

A central question raised by the
Emergent Misalignment
paper is why fine tuning on a narrow domain (insecure code) can lead to broad misalignment. This misbehavior appears in seemingly unrelated domains, such as giving harmful advice and toxic completions. If the model is only trained to introduce vulnerabilities in code, why does it later answer standard prompts with problematic generations?

This phenomenon is surprising under the assumption that alignment is domain specific. However, consider instead that alignment operates through a set of shared latent directions in the activation space. These shared directions are internal mechanisms that would be responsible for safe behavior across many domains. If fine-tuning on one domain suppresses or reverses these internal directions, it can degrade behavior, even on unrelated domains.

4.4.1
Shared Alignment Directions Across Domains

To test this, we adapt a technique from
Pan et al. 2025
on activation space geometry. Specifically, we compute residual activation matrices at the final layer for two datasets, the
insecure code
dataset and a toxic chat dataset

Specifically, we say that for each dataset,
D_{d}

D_{\text{insecure}}
or
D_{\text{toxic}}
), we define the residual matrix

R^{(d)}=\left[\mathbf{h_{L}^{\text{instruct}}}(x)-\mathbf{h_{L}^{\text{base}}}(x)\right]_{x\in D_{d}}

where
\mathbf{h_{L}^{\text{instruct}}}(x)
and
\mathbf{h_{L}^{\text{base}}}(x)
are the final layer,
L
, hidden activations for the instruct and base models on input
x
.

We then perform SVD on each residual matrix:

R^{(d)}=U^{(d)}\Sigma^{(d)}\left(V^{(d)}\right)^{T}

The top right singular vectors (rows of
V^{(d)}
) correspond to the principal directions of representational shift induced by alignment for each dataset.

Recent research
Pan et al. 2025
suggest that these directions can correspond to interpretable internal mechanisms. These latent activations can govern specific behavioral traits, such as refusal, truthfulness, and toxicity. From this perspective, alignment fine tuning can be conceptualized as shaping the activation space by introducing or reinforcing these activation dimensions. Our procedure identifies the top alignment directions for each dataset,
insecure code
and
toxic
generations. Then, we can compare these directions to determine whether different forms of alignment behavior rely on shared internal structures within the model. If we can identify an activation direction that gets triggered when producing
insecure code
and show that it’s related to an activation direction triggered when producing
toxic
generations, then we could have a mechanistic basis for why narrow fine tuning leads to broadly misaligned behavior.

To assess whether alignment uses shared directions across domains, we compute the cosine similarity between the top singular vectors from the
insecure code
and
toxic

The results reveal a striking finding. Figure

4.4.2
Interpretation

These findings offer a compelling mechanistic explanation for why narrow-domain fine-tuning can lead to broad misalignment. The observation that the principal alignment direction for toxic completions is highly similar to one of the top directions for insecure code suggests that alignment behaviors across domains rely on shared internal representations. This directly supports the hypothesis that alignment is not a set of siloed, task-specific behaviors, but rather is implemented through common latent mechanisms that generalize across contexts.

Critically, this overlap in activation geometry provides a structural basis for behavioral generalization. If suppression of insecure code and suppression of toxic language both depend on the same underlying activation dimension, then fine-tuning that weakens this direction in one domain can impair the model’s aligned behavior in others, even when those behaviors are never explicitly trained against. In other words, narrow fine-tuning may not simply fail to generalize, it may actively dismantle shared safety mechanisms, causing alignment to degrade globally.

A legitimate concern regarding the interpretation of this experiment is that the observed similarity is a statistical artifact or that similar activation shifts arise merely from superficial similarities between datasets. However, this explanation is unlikely given that the assistant outputs and user prompts differ substantially between domains, and the shared direction is not the first principal component in both cases (suggesting it’s not a trivial dominant mode of variance). The specificity of the overlap - between the second principal component of insecure code and the top component of toxic completions - suggests a meaningful internal alignment axis rather than a coincidental alignment of noise.

Furthermore, the finding aligns with prior work suggesting that linear features in activation space encode semantically meaningful behaviors such as refusal or helpfulness
(
Arditi et al. 2024
;
Pan et al. 2025
)
. Our results extend this by showing that multiple alignment behaviors may be instantiated by overlapping subspaces in the model’s representation. When these shared directions are weakened, intentionally or not, broad misalignment can result.

Taken together with our earlier findings, this analysis provides critical support for a reinterpretation of the
Emergent Misalignment
phenomenon. The misbehavior observed after fine-tuning on insecure code is not the result of novel capabilities. Rather, it is the byproduct of weakening shared internal structures that previously upheld aligned behavior across domains.

5
Discussion

In the
Emergent Misalignment
paper,
Betley et al. 2025
pose a striking question: why does fine tuning a model on a narrow task like insecure code completion result in broad behavioral degradation, even for prompts that appear unrelated? In this paper, we showed that this phenomenon can be understood not as the emergence of novel behavior, but as the erosion of prior alignment. In other words, a reversal of the internal structure introduced during instruction training.

Our results revealed striking behavioral similarity between the base model and the misaligned model, assigning similar probabilities to misaligned sequences, much higher than those of the instruct tuned model. Further, we showed how the learning signals from
educational insecure
and
insecure code
examples conflicted. Such a contrast implies that the model internalizes intent and behavior from a training dataset, not just the formatting and style. The expected result of narrow fine tuning on insecure code would be the reversal of the aligned behaviors introduced by fine tuning.

Our experiments also showed how the internal activations of the
misaligned
model gradually drift away from the
instruct
model toward the
base
model. Projecting misaligned model representations onto this alignment axis shows how it’s internal representations gradually drift to the internal representations of the base model.

Finally, we demonstrated the existence of a critical shared dimension between the insecure code dataset and a general toxic generation dataset. Such a strong overlap in the internal structures that define alignment in these domains indicates that reinforcing one would predictably result in the reinforcement of the other. In other words, when fine tuned to act against it’s alignment in one domain, the overlapping nature of the activation space results in broad misalignment.

5.1
Implications for LLM Safety

These findings have important implications. They suggest that alignment in some LLMs can be fragile. Rather than being a robust or compartmentalized property, alignment appears to be encoded in a relatively small set of shared internal structures. When models are fine-tuned on misaligned objectives, even in narrow or seemingly unrelated domains, these structures can be weakened or overwritten, leading to broad behavioral degradation. This highlights a key vulnerability: preserving alignment may be just as important, and potentially more difficult, than inducing it in the first place.

Future alignment techniques must therefore grapple with not only how to instill aligned behavior, but also how to safeguard it during continued training. This might involve developing methods to identify alignment-critical subspaces, measure representational drift, or apply structural constraints that prevent the erosion of safety-relevant mechanisms. More broadly, our results point toward a need for alignment frameworks that treat internal model structure as a first-class target of analysis and intervention, not just external behavior.

By reframing emergent misalignment as alignment erosion, we provide a concrete, mechanistic foundation for understanding failure modes in LLM alignment. We hope these insights support future work in evaluating, interpreting, and reinforcing alignment in large models, ultimately contributing to the development of more resilient and safer AI systems.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated the phenomenon of misalignment arising from narrow fine-tuning on insecure code. We provide a mechanistic explanation for why broad behavioral misalignment occurs, even when fine tuning occurs on an unrelated domain. Our results outlined a comprehensive analysis of the internal structures of a model that define alignment, and why it can get degraded and eroded with fine-tuning. As language models continue to be widely deployed, future efforts must focus on building strong, robust alignment.

Appendix B
Probability and Entropy Analysis

B.1
Methodology

To evaluate how the models respond to aligned and misaligned inputs, we compute and detail two metrics across datasets, and list the results in Table
2
:

•

Log Joint Probability: For each example in the datasets, we compute the log probability that the model assigned to the assistant completion. This is done by finding the sum of the log probabilities of each token in the assistant generation. We then average this across all examples in the dataset to find the values in Table
2
.

Lower values (more negative) of log joint probability indicates that the model is less likely to produce the generations in the dataset

•

Average Entropy: For each example in the datasets, we compute the average next-token entropy across the tokens in the assistant completion. The entropy is a measurement of how uncertain or confident the model is in predicting each of the next token. We then average these values across each of the examples in the dataset. Lower values of entropy indicate that the models are more certain of the next token.

C.3
Interpretation Limitations

In Section
4.2
, we assert the loss and gradient experiments as providing circumstantial evidence of the core hypothesis. We use this framing in order to emphasize that these results should be interpreted under advisement.

While we observe opposing learning signals between datasets, we emphasize that such an effect could be driven by structural differences in the dataset. These results provide suggestive evidence of alignment erosion, but do not definitively isolate this as a causal factor. We encourage readers to interpret these results in the broader context of the full experiments that provide more causal evidence of alignment erosion.

Interpretation

Interestingly, we observe that alignment projection patterns are qualitatively similar across aligned and misaligned datasets.
