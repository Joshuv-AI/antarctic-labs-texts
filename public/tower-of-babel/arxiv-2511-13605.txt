Chasing Submodular Objectives, and Submodular Maximization via Cutting Planes
Niv Buchbinder, Joseph (Seffi) Naor, David Wajc

Abstract
We introduce the submodular objectives chasing problem , which generalizes many natural and previously-studied problems: a sequence of constrained submodular maximization problems is revealed over time, with both the objective and available ground set changing at each step. The goal is to maintain solutions of high approximation and low total recourse (number of changes), compared with exact offline algorithms for the same input sequence. For the central cardinality constraint and partition matroid constraints we provide polynomial-time algorithms achieving both optimal ( 1 − 1 / e − ε ) (1-1/e-\varepsilon) -approximation and optimal competitive recourse for any constant-approximation. Key to our algorithm’s polynomial time, and of possible independent interest, is a new meta-algorithm for ( 1 − 1 / e − ε ) (1-1/e-\varepsilon) -approximately maximizing the multilinear extension under general constraints, which we call approximate-or-separate . Our algorithm relies on an improvement of the round-and-separate method [Gupta-Levin SODA’20], inspired by an earlier proof by [Vondrák, PhD Thesis’07]. The algorithm, whose guarantees are similar to the influential continuous greedy algorithm [Calinescu-Chekuri-Pál-Vondrák SICOMP’11], can use any cutting plane method and separation oracle for the constraints. This allows us to introduce cutting plane methods, used for exact unconstrained submodular minimization since the ’80s [Grötschel/Lovász/Schrijver Combinatorica’81], as a useful method for (optimal approximate) constrained submodular maximization. We show further applications of this approach to static algorithms with curvature-sensitive approximation, and to communication complexity protocols.

1.2
Technical Overview

For simplicity, we focus here on
submodular chasing with cardinality constraint of
k
. However, our rounding extends to partition constraints, fractional chasing algorithms extend to matroids, and our new meta-algorithm extends to any separable polytope.

Our starting point is the fundamental

[

In recent work
[

showed that for convex shapes given by mixed packing-covering polytopes, by allowing a small violation of
\varepsilon>0

Slowly Chasing Submodular Objectives.

We begin by relaxing the submodular objective chasing problem by “dropping integrality constraints”.
That is, we allow both the optimal offline algorithm and ours to compute a sequence of points
\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{T}\in[0,1]^{E}

\sum_{i}x_{i}^{t}\leq k
for all
t
.
This relaxation replaces recourse
\sum_{t}|S_{t}\oplus S_{t-1}|
with total distance moved,
\sum_{t}\|\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t-1}\|_{1}

Finally, to extend the submodular set function from binary-valued to real-valued vectors, we use the Wolsey coverage extension,
f^{*}_{t}
(
2.3

[
9

yields, using low fractional recourse compared to the optimal offline algorithm, feasible fractional vectors

By standard connections (

We study non-negative monotone submodular set functions

We use the shorthand

A well-studied special case of (monotone) submodular functions are

We will not need further properties of matroids, but we recall that these are ubiquitous in combinatorial optimization.

Extensions.

We use two extensions of submodular set functions to vectors in

Definition 2.1

The

Fact 2.2

For any non-negative monotone submodular function

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\geq F(p\cdot\mathbf{x})=F((1-p)(\mathbf{0}+0\cdot\mathbf{x})+p((\mathbf{0}+1\cdot\mathbf{x}))\geq(1-p)F(\mathbf{0})+p\cdot F(\mathbf{x})\geq p\cdot F(\mathbf{x}).\qed

Chasing Positive Bodies.

In the positive bodies chasing problem, the (online) input is a sequence of non-empty packing-covering polytopes of the form

and

(stated here with a violation of the covering constraint, which is more appropriate for our problem).

Theorem 2.6
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a (deterministic) online algorithm which given a sequence of non-empty packing-covering polytopes of the form

above, outputs at each time

\mathbf{x}^{t}\in\mathcal{K}^{{1-\epsilon}}_{t}\triangleq\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{+}\mid C\mathbf{x}\geq\mathbf{1-\bm{\epsilon}},\;P\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{1}\}.

The algorithm’s recourse is
\sum_{t}\|\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t-1}\|_{1}=O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(d\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}))\cdot OPT_{R}
, for

.

3
Approximate-or-Separate

In this section we design a new meta-algorithm for approximating the multilinear extension
F
. Its guarantees are very similar to those of the influential Continuous Greedy Algorithm
[
24
]
, and it can be used as an alternative approach to the latter.
An advantage of this new meta-algorithm is its simplicity, and flexibility, allowing to implement it in various computational models, as we illustrate in subsequent sections.

Conceptually, the meta-algorithm is simple, and uses any cutting plane-based method, such as the Ellipsoid algorithm
[
62
,
84
,
66
,
58
]
.
It is given (value oracle access to) a non-negative monotone submodular function
f
and a non-empty separable polytope
\mathcal{P}\subseteq[0,1]^{E}
. It first guesses a value
V
with
V/f(OPT)\in[1-\varepsilon,1]
, for
f(OPT)\triangleq\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{E}\cap\mathcal{P}}f(\mathbf{x})
. (This is standard and introduces only a logarithmic overhead; see the proof of
3.5
.) Next, it uses any cutting plane-based method to find a point
\mathbf{x}
in the polytope
\mathcal{P}\cap\{\mathbf{x}\mid f^{*}(\mathbf{x})\geq V\}
, which is non-empty as
\mathcal{P}
is non-empty and
V\leq f(OPT)
. Such
\mathbf{x}
is feasible (is in
\mathcal{P}
) and satisfies by
2.5

F(\mathbf{x})\geq(1-1/e)\cdot f^{*}(\mathbf{x})\geq(1-1/e)\cdot V\geq(1-1/e-\varepsilon)\cdot f(OPT)
.
Unfortunately, as outlined in the introduction, the constraint
f^{*}(\mathbf{x})\geq V
is given by exponentially many constraints, and is moreover not separable, as even computing
f^{*}(\mathbf{x})
is APX-hard
[
85
]
.

Our guiding intuition, inspired by
[
55
]
, is that we only wish to find a vector satisfying
f^{*}(\mathbf{x})\geq V
as a means to compute a point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{P}
with
F(\mathbf{x})\geq(1-1/e)\cdot V
(or thereabouts).
So, if we reach such a feasible point with high
F(\mathbf{x})
, we may terminate early,
and we only require a separating hyperplane witnessing
f^{*}(\mathbf{x})<V
when we are not at such a point.
The following simple lemma asserts that in the latter case a separating hyperplane
exists
, and moreover this is equivalent to the classic
2.5
. Crucially for our rounding in
Section

5
, we even prove a slightly stronger claim that states that such a hyperplane exists even when

.

.

Let

(

Proposition 3.2

(Approximate-or-separate)
.

Let
f\colon 2^{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a non-negative monotone submodular function,
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n}

\varepsilon\in(0,1)

the random set

f(S(t))+\sum_{i\in E}f(i\mid S(t))\cdot x_{i}\leq\left(1-\varepsilon/2\right)\cdot V.

Proof.

Fix time
t\in[0,1]
. By memorylessness of exponentials, up to
O(dt^{2})
terms, for any set
S\subseteq E
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(S(t+dt))-f(S(t))\mid S(t)=S]

\displaystyle=\bigg[\sum_{i\in E}f(i\mid S)\cdot x_{i}\bigg]dt.

So, by total expectation over the set
S(t)
, in the limit as

tends to zero,
\phi(t)\triangleq\mathbb{E}[f(S(t))]
satisfies:

\displaystyle\phi(t)+\frac{d\phi(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[f(S(t))+\sum_{i\in E}f(i\mid S(t))\cdot x_{i}\bigg].

Now, if
\phi(t)+\phi^{\prime}(t)\geq U
for all
t\in[0,1]
, since
\phi(0)\geq 0
, then
\phi(t)
is the solution of an ordinary differential inequality with minimal solution
\phi(t)\geq(1-\exp(-t))\cdot U
, and so
\phi(1)\geq(1-1/e)\cdot U
.
For example, by definition of
f^{*}
, this holds with
U=f^{*}(\mathbf{x})
.
Since each element

belongs to
S(1)
with probability
\mathds{P}r[Y_{i}\leq 1]=1-\exp(-x_{i})
, we have that
\phi(1)=F(\mathbf{1}-\exp(-\mathbf{x}))
, this concludes Vondrák’s proof of
2.5
.
Now, since by hypothesis
\phi(1)=F(\mathbf{1}-\exp(-\mathbf{x}))<(1-1/e)(1-\varepsilon)\cdot V

\phi^{\prime}(t)\geq U-\phi(t)
for
U=(1-\varepsilon)V
should not hold for all, or even a high enough fraction of,

.

To formalize this intuition, consider the ‘‘log-gap’’ function,

\psi(t)\triangleq-\ln(V(1-\varepsilon)-\phi(t)).

By the chain rule,

\psi^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\phi^{\prime}(t)}{V(1-\varepsilon)-\phi(t)}.

This derivative is non-negative, by monotonicity of

\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}\rangle\leq 1
or
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}\rangle\geq 1
for some
\mathbf{a}\geq\mathbf{0}
, with the halfspaces used by the algorithm at time
t
given by some constraints of
\mathcal{K}_{t}
.
Thus, any solution for
\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{K}_{T}
induces a solution for the halfspaces chased with the same recourse.
In particular, for
r\leq n
the rank of the matroid, this optimal (offline) recourse is at most
2rT
(replacing all
r
elements in every step).
For any violated constraint on a current point
\mathbf{x}
, they provide a polynomial-time solvable convex program (equivalent to a simple multiplicative update) to determine the next point
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
satisfying the current constraint, while obtaining an
O_{\varepsilon}(\log\max_{t}|E_{t}|)
-competitive recourse, and by the above using at most
O_{\varepsilon}(rT\log\max_{t}|E_{t}|)
recourse.
A single update step in
[
9
]
that computes
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
such that
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\rangle=1
requires finding a single dual variable that is used for the multiplicative update.
This can be done using a binary search and requires time that is linear in
n
and logarithmic in the maximum and minimum non-zero coefficients
a_{i}
.

To obtain a polynomial-time bound from this (as opposed to the finite bound stated in general in
[
9
]
), we give polynomial-time algorithms to find violated constraints such that moving to a point satisfying it incurs
\Omega(\varepsilon)
recourse, which by the recourse bound above can only happen
T_{ALG}\triangleq O_{\varepsilon}((rT\log\max_{t}|E_{t}|)/\varepsilon)
times.

Finding a (very) violated constraint in the Matroid Polytope:

We allow the algorithm momentarily to slightly violate the matroid constraints and only require for all
S\subseteq E_{t}
,
\sum_{i\in S}x^{t}_{i}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot rank(S)
(and fix this later on in the proof). Next, by solving unconstrained submodular minimization
[
66
,
58
]
of the function
g(S)\triangleq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot rank(S)-\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}
, with our current vector
\mathbf{x}
, we either decide that all the relaxed matroid constraints are satisfied, or find a subset
S
, such that
\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}\geq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot rank(S)
.
In the latter case, moving to any point
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
that satisfies the violated constraint
\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}\leq rank(S)
incurs recourse
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{1}\geq\varepsilon\cdot rank(S)\geq\varepsilon
.

Finding a violated constraint in the Wolsey Polytope:

Using the framework of
Section

3
we only find such a violated constraint whenever
F_{t}(\mathbf{1}-\exp(-\mathbf{x}))<(1-1/e-\varepsilon)\cdot V_{t}
, where
\mathbf{x}
is our current vector, as otherwise we may output
\mathbf{x}
. In this case, we use
3.2
to sample

s\triangleq\lceil\varepsilon^{-2}\log(T_{ALG}/\varepsilon))\rceil
sets, for
T_{ALG}=\Theta_{\varepsilon}(rt\log\max_{t}|E_{t}|)
. As each sampled set
S
satisfies
f(S)+\sum_{i\in E_{t}}f(i\mid S)\cdot x_{i}\leq\left(1-\varepsilon/2\right)\cdot V_{t}.
with probability
\Omega(\varepsilon^{2})
, we are guaranteed that one of the sets
S
satisfies such a violated constraint with probability
1-(1-\varepsilon^{2})^{s}\geq 1-\varepsilon/T_{ALG}
.
Taking union bound over the
T_{ALG}
steps of the algorithm in this time-step until we output some vector
\mathbf{x}
, we find that the probability we fail to witness that
F_{t}(\mathbf{1}-\exp(-\mathbf{x}))<(1-1/e-\varepsilon)\cdot V_{t}
at some point of our algorithm where this holds is at most
\varepsilon
, and so
\mathbb{E}[F_{t}(\mathbf{1}-\exp(-\mathbf{x}))]\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot(1-1/e-\varepsilon)\cdot V_{t}\geq(1-1/e-2\varepsilon)\cdot V_{t}
.
This (together with subsequent scaling, discussed below) implies the desired approximation.

Lower bounding recourse for fixing a violated constraint.

To fix the violated constraint by moving to a point
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
satisfying
f(S)+\sum_{i\in E_{t}}f(i\mid S)\cdot x^{\prime}_{i}\geq V_{t}
requires recourse

\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{1}\geq\left((\varepsilon/2)\cdot V_{t}\right)/\max_{i\in E_{t}}f(i\mid S)
. Unfortunately, this can be very small, as
V_{t}
may be small compared to
\max_{i\in E_{t}}f(i\mid S)
, which can be as large as
OPT_{t}
. However, we note that every
integral
solution
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{E}
that satisfies the Wolsey constraint
f(S)+\sum_{i\in E_{t}}f(i\mid S)\cdot x_{i}\geq V_{t}
also satisfies the stronger constraint

f(S)+\sum_{i\in E_{t}}\min\{V_{t}-f(S),f(i\mid S)\}\cdot x_{i}\geq V_{t}
. Hence, as we are competing with the optimal integral recourse, this is a valid constraint for
\mathcal{K}_{t}\cap\{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{E_{t}}\}
, and we may use it as a separating hyperplane.
Specifically, if we find a set
S\subseteq E_{t}
violating the easier constraint, we also find that the stronger constraint is violated, and we may move to a point
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
satisfying the latter.
The recourse is now at least

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{1}\geq\left((\varepsilon/2)\cdot V_{t}\right)/\min\left\{V_{t}-f(S),\;\max_{i\in E_{t}}f(i\mid S)\right\}\geq\left((\varepsilon/2)\cdot V_{t}\right)/V_{t}=\Omega(\varepsilon).

Conclusion.

Finally, we use the above two algorithms at each time
t
to find halfspaces to provide to the algorithm of
2.6
until we find no violated packing or covering constraints. The algorithm is guaranteed to output at any time a point
\mathbf{y}^{t}
satisfying
\sum_{i\in S}y^{t}_{i}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot rank(S)
for each set
S\subseteq E_{t}
and with probability
1-\varepsilon/T_{ALG}
also

F_{t}(\mathbf{1}-e^{-\mathbf{y}^{t}})\geq(1-1/e-2\varepsilon)\cdot V_{t}
, and so also
\mathbb{E}[F_{t}(\mathbf{1}-e^{-\mathbf{y}^{t}})]\geq(1-1/e-O(\varepsilon))\cdot V_{t}
.
Finally, taking
\mathbf{x}^{t}\leftarrow\mathbf{y}^{t}/(1+\varepsilon)
for all times
t
satisfies the matroid polytope’s constraints, does not increase the
O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(\max_{t}|E_{t}|\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}))
-competitive recourse of
2.6
, and guarantees by monotonicity and
2.2
that

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{1}-e^{-\mathbf{x}^{t}})=F\big(\mathbf{1}-e^{-\frac{\mathbf{y}^{t}}{1+\varepsilon}}\big)

\displaystyle\geq F\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{1}-e^{-\mathbf{y}^{t}}}{1+\varepsilon}\bigg)\geq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}F(\mathbf{1}-e^{-\mathbf{y}^{t}})\geq(1-1/e-O(\varepsilon))\cdot V_{t}.\qed

5
Recourse-Respecting Randomized Rounding

In this section we provide a randomized rounding algorithm for submodular objective chasing.

Our rounding algorithm converts online fractional solutions
\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{T}
into randomized integral feasible sets
S_{1},\dots,S_{T}
, of expected submodular value at least
F(\mathbf{1}-\exp(-\mathbf{x}^{t}))
, while respecting the recourse guarantee of the fractional solution; i.e., obtaining no higher (expected) recourse. The guarantees we desire are as follows.

Definition 5.1
.

A cardinality-constrained recourse-respecting randomized rounding receives as inputs vectors
\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{T}\in[0,1]^{E}
online, satisfying
\sum_{i}x^{t}_{i}\leq k
for all
t
; such an algorithm outputs after each
\mathbf{x}^{t}
is revealed (and before
\mathbf{x}^{t+1}
is revealed) a set
S_{t}\subseteq E_{t}
satisfying for all
t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}

(P1)

(Cardinality)

|S_{t}|\leq k
.

(P2)

(Recourse)

\mathbb{E}[\;|S_{t}\oplus S_{t-1}|\;]\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t-1}\right\rVert}_{1}
.

(P3)

(Marginals)

x^{t}_{i}\geq\mathds{P}r[i\in S_{t}]\geq 1-(1-x^{t}_{i}/k)^{k}\geq 1-\exp(-x^{t}_{i})
for all
i\in E
.

(P4)

(NA)
The characteristic vector of
S_{t}
, i.e.,
\mathds{1}[S_{t}]\triangleq(\mathds{1}[i\in S_{t}]\mid i\in E)
, is negatively associated.

(P5)

(Value)

\mathbb{E}[f_{t}(S_{t})]\geq F_{t}\left(\mathbf{1}-\exp\left(-\mathbf{x}^{t}\right)\right)
for any monotone submodular function
f_{t}
.

Note that Property
(P3)
implies that the output only contains elements with non-zero
x
-value.

The special case of
k=1
has been studied in the statistics literature over 70 years ago. Specifically, Keyfitz provided a method with the following guarantees
[
61
]
.

Proposition 5.2
.

There exists a cardinality-constraint recourse-respecting randomized rounding algorithm for
k=1
.

As Keyfitz’s algorithm is mainly provided by way of example, for completeness we formalize this algorithm and its analysis in
Appendix

D
. In the same appendix we provide an alternative algorithm for the
k=1
case requiring only a single
\mathrm{Uni}[0,1]
random variable, as opposed to drawing such a random variable for each vector, as with Keyftiz’s algorithm.

We now show how the
k=1
case implies our desired guarantees for

in general, by independent repetition. This will require the following background on negative association and its role in submodular maximization.

Submodular Dominance and Negative Association.

We require rounding satisfying the constraints and obtaining value at least as high as the multilinear extension (at the point
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{1}-\exp(-\mathbf{x})
). We will obtain via randomized rounding satisfying
negative association
.

Definition 5.3
.

A random vector
\mathbf{X}
is
negatively associated (NA)
if for any two monotone functions
f,g
of disjoint variables in
\mathbf{X}

\mathrm{Cov}(f(\mathbf{X}),g(\mathbf{X}))\leq 0.

As shown by
[
31
,
80
]

[
59
]
implies
subdmodular dominance
.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
\mathbf{X}\in\{0,1\}
is NA and
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}2^{E}\to\mathbb{R}
is submodular, then

\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{X})]\geq F(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}])
.

In our analysis we will need the following well-known properties of NA vectors
[
59
,
36
]

Lemma 5.5
.

The following give rise to NA distributions:

1.

(0-1 rule)
Any random vector
\mathbf{X}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with
\sum_{i}X_{i}\leq 1
is NA.

2.

(Products)
The concatenation
\mathbf{X}\circ\mathbf{Y}
of independent NA vectors
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
is also NA.

3.

(Composition)
If
\mathbf{X}
is an NA vector and
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
are monotone functions of disjiont variables in
\mathbf{X}
, then the vector
(f_{1}(\mathbf{X}),\dots,f_{r}(\mathbf{X}))
is also NA.

With the above in place, we are now ready to lift any recourse-respecting randomized rounding from the (trivial)
k=1
case to arbitrary
k\geq 1

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a cardinality-constrained recourse-respecting randomized rounding algorithm for the
k=1
case.
For each time
t

be the outputs of
k>1
independent copies of

on input stream
\mathbf{x}^{1}/k,\dots,\mathbf{x}^{t}/k

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
outputting

k>1

Proof.

Properties
(P1)
and
(P2)

\displaystyle|S_{t}|

\displaystyle=\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}S_{t}^{(i)}\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}|S_{t}^{(i)}|\leq k.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\;|S_{t}\oplus S_{t-1}|\;]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\;\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}S_{t}^{(i)}\oplus\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}S_{t-1}^{(i)}\right|\;\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|S^{(i)}_{t}\oplus S^{(i)}_{t-1}\right|\right]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{x}^{t}/k-\mathbf{x}^{t-1}/k\right\rVert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathbf{x}^{t}-\mathbf{x}^{t-1}\right\rVert}.

\displaystyle\mathds{P}r[e\in S_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}r\left[\exists i\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;e\in S_{t}^{(i)}\right]\in\left[1-(1-x^{t}_{i}/k)^{k},\;\;x^{t}_{i}\right].

6
More Applications of Approximate-or-Separate

In this section we illustrate the flexibility of the approximate-or-separate proposition and algorithmic paradigm, by providing (1) a simple polynomial-time algorithm for constrained maximization of monotone submodular functions with bounded curvature, and (2) a communication-optimal communication complexity protocol for constrained submodular maximization.

6.1
Curvature-Sensitive Approximation

The
(1-1/e)
-approximation for constrained submodular maximization is optimal in the worst case for general monotone submodular functions. However, it can be refined if the submodular function has bounded
total curvature
, defined as follows
[
32
]
.

Definition 6.1
.

A set function
f
with no zero-valued elements has
total curvature

c_{f}\triangleq 1-\min_{i\in E}\frac{f(i\mid E\setminus\{i\})}{f(\{i\})}.

Conforti and Cournejols
[
32
]
showed that the greedy algorithm is
(1-\exp(-c))/c
-approximate for monotone submodular function maximization subject to cardinality constraints and
1/(1+c)
-approximate for any matroid constraints.
In an influential paper,
[
83
]
improved on both bounds, and provided two methods to provide a
(1-c/e-\varepsilon)
-approximation for any matroid constraints, which they prove is optimal (up to the
\varepsilon
term) for all values of
c\in[0,1]
. Feldman
[
46
]
later provided an alternative method to obtain this result.

The following decomposition of
[
57
]
is useful (see
Appendix

E
for a self-contained proof):

Lemma 6.2
.

Any non-negative monotone subdmodular function
f
with curvature
c>0
can be written as
f=c\cdot g+(1-c)\cdot\ell
, where
\ell
is linear,
\ell(S)\triangleq\sum_{i\in S}f(\{i\})
, and the resulting function
g=\frac{f-(1-c)\ell}{c}
is a non-negative monotone submodular function satisfying
g\leq f
.

The improved curvature result of
[
83
]
follows directly from the next theorem, which applies their
[
83
, Theorem 3.1]
to the functions
g
and
\ell
in the decomposition of
f
guaranteed in
6.2
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\hat{v}\triangleq\max_{i\in E}\max\{g(\{i\}),\ell(\{i\})\}
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is an algorithm that given a monotone submodular function
g
, a linear function
\ell
and a matroid
\mathcal{M}
, produces in polynomial time a feasible subset
S\subseteq E
satisfying:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(S)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[c\cdot g(S)+(1-c)\cdot\ell(S)]

\displaystyle\geq(1-1/e)\cdot c\cdot g(O)+(1-c)\cdot\ell(O)-O(\varepsilon)\cdot\hat{v}

\displaystyle\geq(1-c/e-O(\varepsilon))\cdot f(O),

where
g
and
\ell
are as in
6.2
,
\hat{v}\leq f(O)
,
and
O\subseteq E
is the feasible subset maximizing
f
.

Two proofs for this theorem are provided by
[
83
]
. The first one works by a modification of the continuous greedy algorithm that yields a feasible vector
\mathbf{x}
such that
G(\mathbf{x})+L(\mathbf{x})\geq(1-1/e)g(O)+\ell(O)-O(\varepsilon)\cdot\hat{v}
, where
G
and
L
are the multilinear extensions of
g
and
\ell
, respectively. Then, this fractional vector
\mathbf{x}
can be rounded to an integral solution without an additional loss
[
24
]
. The second proof relies on a modification of the local search algorithm of
[
49
]
.

We show that a very simple application (rather than modification) of
Algorithm

1
yields the same guarantee as
[
83
]
. The idea is simple: similarly to
[
83
]
, we guess two values
\lambda=\ell(O)
and
\gamma=g(O)
. Then, we execute
Algorithm

1
on the polytope
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}(V)\triangleq\mathcal{P}\cap\{\mathbf{x}\mid f^{*}(\mathbf{x})\geq\gamma\}\cap\{\mathbf{x}\mid L(\mathbf{x})\geq\lambda\}
, noting that if the values are guessed correctly,
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}(V)\neq\emptyset
. We further observe that we are only adding a single constraint (
L(\mathbf{x})\geq\lambda
) and checking whether this constraint is violated is easy.
Therefore, by
3.4
, the final vector satisfies
G(\mathbf{x})+L(\mathbf{x})\geq(1-1/e)\cdot\gamma+\lambda
. The additional (additive) error of
\varepsilon\cdot\hat{v}
is due to the inaccurate guessing of the values
\gamma
and
\lambda
. The procedure for guessing these values can be handled in the same way as it is done in
[
83
]
. Finally, rounding again yields the claimed bound in expectation.

Summarizing, at the cost of a small
\mathrm{poly}(n/\varepsilon)
slowdown due to guesswork (see
[
83
]
for details), the Approximate-or-Separate framework allows us to get curvature-sensitive algorithms with effectively the same running time as our
(1-1/e-\varepsilon)
approximation. This demonstrates the flexibility of our framework that allows adding more constraints (linear or even convex) easily, while relying only on a separation (or approximate separation) oracle required for the underlying cutting based algorithm.

6.2
Communication Complexity

In this section we outline the utility of our approximate-or-separate framework to the communication complexity model
[
64
]
.
In the basic model, Alice and Bob have inputs
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}
, respectively, unknown to each other, and must communicate over discrete back-and-forth rounds, ideally sending as little bits of information as possible in order to agree on some
f(a,b)
, where
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{C}
is known to both parties.
For example, Alice and Bob may hold parts of the edges of a graph, and may want to compute a perfect matching in the union graph, if one exists
[
12
]
.

In our context, Alice and Bob have some subsets
A\subseteq[n]
and
B\subseteq[n]
of a common ground set
[n]
.
They both know a common non-negative monotone submodular function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}2^{[n]}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, a partition constraint of rank
r
(i.e., maximum solution cardinality of
r
), and wish to compute a feasible subset
C\subseteq A\cup B
that
(1-1/e-\varepsilon)
-approximates the optimal feasible set
OPT\subseteq A\cup B
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
.
Alice and Bob have their own random bits, which they may use to communicate. This is the so-called (
\varepsilon
-error)
private-coin
communication model, as opposed to the
public-coin
communication model, where Alice and Bob also share a common random string.
By Newman’s theorem
[
77
]
, by doubling the error probability from
\varepsilon
to
2\varepsilon
, the communication complexity in both models differs by at most an additive
O_{\varepsilon}(\log n)
bits.

The problem was previously studied by Feldman et al.
[
44
]
for cardinality constraint. They showed that even in the one-way communication model (Alice only send a single message to Bob), non-trivial guarantees can be achieved: a
0.51
-approximation can be achieved in polytime by sending a message of
O(r)
elements, while a
(2/3-\varepsilon)
-approximation can be achieved (in exponential time) by sendig a message of
O(r\log(r/\varepsilon))
elements. In this section we will show that

7
Summary and Open Questions

In this work we introduce the chasing submodular objectives problem, and provide polynomial-time algorithms with optimal approximation and competitive recourse for the two most widely-studied basic constraints: cardinality constraints and partition matroids. To obtain polynomial-time algorithm (for this and other models) we introduce a new approximate-or-separate framework, allowing us to reduce constrained submodular maximization to any cutting plane method.

Chasing subomdular objectives.

A natural question we leave open is the existence of algorithms with optimal, or even
\Omega(1)
-approximate, submodular objective chasing subject to richer constraint families.
We note that for the expressive family of matroid constraints, we provide an optimal fractional algorithm in
Section

4
, which reduces this search to that of designing approximate recourse-respecting randomized rounding algorithms.
Another natural questions is the study of submodular objective chasing with
non-monotone
submodular functions, which while less common in applications, are also theoretically interesting.
One challenge is that for such functions
f^{*}
is not an extension (and also does not yield a packing-covering LP), and so other ideas are needed.

Submodular Maximization.

In this work we introduce the approximate-or-separate method. This method allows us to obtain optimally-approximate (fractional) solutions to submodular maximization subject to separable constraints, using nothing more than a separation oracle and cutting plane methods.
We illustrate this method’s utility for several models of computation: static algorithms with curvature-sensitive approximation, and communication complexity protocols.
In follow-up work,
[
74
]
use our approximate-or-separate framework to compute
several sets
of high submodular value, in a multi-scenario reallocation scenario similar; they do so by extending our approach, using the ellipsoid method and writing an LP imposing multiple high submodular value constraints (for parts of the output vector), which they then round using a novel correlated sampling for the hypersimplex.
We anticipate further applications of our method, and leave it as an open problem to find further models in which this meta-algorithm (perhaps using low-recourse cutting plane methods, as we do in
Section

6.2
) can find applications to other computational models.

\varepsilon\cdot\max\{f(e_{i})\mid i\in[t-L+1,t]\}\leq\varepsilon\cdot f(OPT[t-L+1,t])
loss.
The multiplicative gaps between anchors’ window values guarantees a
1-\varepsilon
multiplicative gap between nonconsecutive anchors’

value, and so the number of anchors

is bounded at all times
t

m\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=O\left(\log_{1+\Theta(\varepsilon)}\left(\frac{\max_{i}g_{t}(s_{i})}{\min_{i}g_{t}(s_{i})}\right)\right)=O\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\max_{i}g_{t}(s_{i})}{\min_{i}g_{t}(s_{i})}\right)\right)=O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(k/\varepsilon)).

Above, the last step uses that by the preceding discussion we can assume that each element has singleton value at least
\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\cdot\max\{f(e_{i})\mid i\in[t-L+1,t]\}
, where we use the set of available items (as in the proof of
B.2
).
Note that following the addition of the next element, we have (by submodularity), that if

and
s_{i}\geq t-L+2
, then

g_{t+1}(s_{i+1})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot g_{t+1}(s_{i})
, since the addition of element

to the end of the windows with anchors
s_{i+1}
and
s_{i}
increases the maximum value of their windows more for the smaller window (submodularity

)

At any point in time, our output solution is the solution of value

, for

the earliest anchor in
[t-L+1,t]

Since by the preceding argument this solution has value at least
(1-\varepsilon)\cdot g_{t}(s_{0})\geq f(OPT[t-L+1,t]
, for
s_{0}
the last anchor to leave the window, this is a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation.
Note that from the moment
s_{1}
becomes the earliest anchor, this is effectively an incremental algorithm, and so the recourse incurred is
O(k)
(for the first solution for this incremental algorithm), plus
O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(k/\varepsilon))
amortized per update, due to
B.2
.
Since for each window of length

the above implies an amortized recourse of

using that

∎
