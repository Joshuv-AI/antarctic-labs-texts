On Lieb–Thirring inequalities for multidimensional Schrödinger operators with complex potentials
Sabine Bögli, Sukrid Petpradittha, František Štampach

Abstract
. We solve the open problem by Demuth, Hansmann, and Katriel announced in [Integr. Equ. Oper. Theory 75 (2013), 1–5] by a counter-example construction. The problem concerns a possible generalisation of the Lieb–Thirring inequality for Schrödinger operators in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} to the case of complex-valued potentials. A counter-example has already been found for the one-dimensional case d = 1 d=1 by the first and third authors in [J. Spectr. Theory 11 (2021), 1391–1413]. Here we generalise the counter-example to higher dimensions d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 .

1.
Introduction

Let
p
depend on the dimension
d
as follows:

p\geq 1,\;\mbox{ if }d=1;\qquad p>1,\;\mbox{ if }d=2;\qquad p\geq d/2,\;\mbox{ if }d\geq 3.

(1)

For a real-valued potential
V\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the Schrödinger operator
H=-\Delta+V
is a selfadjoint operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and its spectrum
\sigma(H)
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
\sigma(H)
consists of the essential spectrum
\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(H)=[0,\infty)
and the at most countable discrete spectrum
\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H)\subset(-\infty,0)
. The classical Lieb–Thirring inequality states that there exists a constant
C_{p,d}>0
depending only on
p
and
d
such that

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H)}|\lambda|^{p-d/2}\leq C_{p,d}\|V\|^{p}_{L^{p}},

(2)

where the eigenvalues
\lambda
are counted repeatedly according to their algebraic multiplicities.
Recently, there have been studies on Lieb–Thirring type inequalities for Schrödinger operators
H=-\Delta+V
, where the potential
V\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is allowed to take complex values. For such operators
H
, we still have
\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(H)=[0,\infty)
and
\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H)
is a set of at most countable isolated eigenvalues of
H
, but these may be non-real.

It turns out that (
2
) does not hold for general complex-valued
V
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
p>(d+1)/2
since, in this case, any point in
\sigma_{{\rm ess}}(H)=[0,\infty)
can be an accumulation point of
\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H)
, see
[
2
,
4
]
.
A possible weaker candidate for a Lieb–Thirring type inequality can be obtained by replacing
|\lambda|^{p}
in (
2
) by
\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p}
. The resulting inequality then reads

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H)}\dfrac{\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}\leq C_{p,d}\|V\|^{p}_{L^{p}}.

(3)

Note that this inequality reduces to (
2
) when
V
is real-valued. As it seems to be a reasonable candidate for the Lieb-Thirring inequality extended to complex-valued potentials, the following open question was published in
[
10
]
.

Open Question
(Demuth–Hansmann–Katriel)
.

Assuming (
1
), does the inequality (
3
) hold for all
V\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
? Prove or disprove it.

In
[
5
]
, the first and third authors constructed a counter-example in dimension
d=1
by considering
V
to be scalar multiples of the characteristic function of the closed interval
[-1,1]
. In
[
5
, Sec. 3.3]
, we also suggested that the one-parameter family of Schrödinger operators

H_{h}:=-\Delta+V_{h},\quad V_{h}:={\rm i}h\chi_{B_{1}(0)},

(4)

where
\chi_{B_{1}(0)}
is the characteristic function of the unit ball
B_{1}(0)
and
h>0
, is a natural candidate for a counter-example in higher dimensions
d\geq 2
. In this article, we show that this is indeed the case. Although it was expected, the extension to the multi-dimensional case is by no means trivial as an involved asymptotic analysis of Bessel functions with complex arguments is needed and the related spectral analysis is in general less explicit than in the one-dimensional case.

Our main result is the following inequality.

Theorem 1
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
p>0
, and
0<\varepsilon<1
.
Then there exists
C_{p,d}>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
h>0
, we have

\frac{1}{\|V_{h}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}}\,\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{h})}\dfrac{{\rm dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}\geq C_{p,d}\,(\log h)^{\varepsilon}.

(5)

The logarithmic divergence in the parameter
h
on the right-hand side of (
5
) clearly
answers the question by Demuth, Hansmann and Katriel to the negative.

Recently, the first author proved in
[
3
]
a Lieb–Thirring type inequality for Schrödinger operators with complex-valued potentials
V\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
p\geq d/2+1
.
To compensate for the logarithmic divergence, an extra term appears on the left-hand side of (
3
) given by a function of
{\rm dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))/|\lambda|
; see
[
3
, Theorem 2.1]
for more details.
This is a generalisation of an earlier result by Demuth, Hansmann and Katriel
[
8
, Corollary 3]
which says that for any
0<\tau<1
there exists
C_{d,p,\tau}>0
such that

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H)}\dfrac{\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p+\tau}}{|\lambda|^{d/2+\tau}}\leq C_{p,d,\tau}\|V\|^{p}_{L^{p}}.

Our main result proves that the equation no longer holds if we fomally set
\tau=0
.

A discrete version of Lieb–Thirring type inequalities for Jacobi matrices with complex entries, in particular for one-dimensional discrete Schrödinger operators with complex potentials, was found in
[
18
]
, and similar open problems published therein have been also answered in
[
5
]
. Much more literature devoted to Lieb–Thirring inequalities exists nowadays. From those works, whose main focus is on Lieb–Thirring type inequalities for non-self-adjoint Schrödinger, Jacobi, and other operators, we mention at least
[
6
,
8
,
9
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
23
]
.

The eigenvalues of our operator
H_{h}
are characterised by solutions of a characteristic equation involving Bessel functions. In Section
2
, we first recall general facts on Schrödinger operators with spherically symmetric potentials and then derive the characteristic equation of
H_{h}
. In order to estimate the location and asymptotic behaviour of certain solutions of the characteristic equation, we need to deduce preliminary results concerning the involved Bessel functions, which is worked out in Section
3
. Finally, in Section
4
, we prove Theorem
1
.

2.
The characteristic equation

The main goal of this section is to deduce a characteristic equation for the Schrödinger operator
H_{h}
defined in (
4
) whose solutions are in direct correspondence with discrete eigenvalues of
H_{h}
. To do so, we first reduce the eigenvalue equation for
H_{h}
to a one-dimensional radial problem. For this step, we recall several well known facts from harmonic analysis of Schrödinger operators with spherically symmetric potentials.

2.1.
Schrödinger operators with spherically symmetric potentials

We consider Schrödinger operators in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with potentials
V=V(|x|)
, where
|x|
stands for the Euclidean norm of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For the following facts, the reader may consult e.g. the book
[
24
]
. Using spherical coordinates in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the Laplace operator takes the form

\Delta=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\dfrac{d-1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\dfrac{1}{r^{2}}\Delta_{S^{d-1}},

where
r\equiv|x|
is the radial coordinate and
\Delta_{S^{d-1}}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator on the
d
-dimensional unit sphere
S^{d-1}
. The spectrum of
\Delta_{S^{d-1}}
is discrete, the complete set of eigenfunctions consists of the spherical harmonics
Y^{(\ell)}
of degree
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have the eigenvalue equation

\Delta_{S^{d-1}}Y^{(\ell)}=-\ell(\ell+d-2)Y^{(\ell)}.

The algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue
-\ell(\ell+d-2)
equals

\binom{d+\ell-1}{d-1}-\binom{d+\ell-3}{d-1}

(6)

for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, see
[
24
, p. 140]
.

Since the complex-valued potential
V
depends only on the radius
r\equiv|x|
, we will construct eigenfunctions
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of the Schrödinger operator
H=-\Delta+V
of the form

\phi(x)=\psi(r)Y^{(\ell)}\left(\frac{x}{r}\right),

(7)

for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and a function
\psi
satisfying the radial eigenvalue equation

-\psi^{\prime\prime}(r)-\dfrac{d-1}{r}\psi^{\prime}(r)+\dfrac{\ell(\ell+d-2)}{r^{2}}\psi(r)+V(r)\psi(r)=\lambda\psi(r),

(8)

where
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
H
.

2.2.
The characteristic equation

The potential
V_{h}
defined in (
4
) of the Schrödinger operator
H_{h}
is, of course, spherically symmetric. In the radial coordinate, we have

V_{h}(r):=\begin{cases}{\rm i}h,\quad&\textrm{if $r<1$},\\
0,\quad&\textrm{if $r\geq 1$},\end{cases}

where
h>0
. By the facts on Schrödinger operators with spherically symmetric potentials from the previous subsection, the eigenvalue problem for
H_{h}
reduces to an analysis of solutions of the one-dimensional eigenvalue equation (
8
). Taking into account the special form of the potential
V_{h}
, we seek non-trivial solutions
\psi
of (
8
) separately on
(0,1)
and on
[1,\infty)
so that
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
are continuous at
r=1
and

\psi\in L^{2}\left((0,\infty),r^{d-1}{\rm d}r\right)
. Then
\phi
given by (
7
) is twice weakly differentiable with
-\Delta\phi=\lambda\phi-V_{h}\phi\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, hence
\phi
belongs to the operator domain
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits H_{h}\equiv\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits(-\Delta)=W^{2,2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and so will be an eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda
.

For
0<r<1
, we write
\lambda=k^{2}
, where
k\in{\mathbb{C}}
, and introduce a new complex parameter
m\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that

{\rm i}h=k^{2}-m^{2}.

(9)

Then equation (
8
) turns into

\displaystyle-\psi^{\prime\prime}(r)-\dfrac{d-1}{r}\psi^{\prime}(r)+\dfrac{\ell(\ell+d-2)}{r^{2}}\psi(r)-m^{2}\psi(r)=0,

(10)

which has the form of the Bessel differential equation.
Recall that Bessel’s differential equation of order
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
is the second-order ordinary differential equation

z^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}w}{dz^{2}}+z\dfrac{dw}{dz}+(z^{2}-\nu^{2})w=0,

and has solutions called the Bessel functions of the first kind
J_{\pm\nu}(z)
, of the second kind
Y_{\nu}(z)
, and of the third kind
H^{(1)}_{\nu}(z),H^{(2)}_{\nu}(z)
(also called Hankel functions). Of course, these solution are interrelated. As the main source for the theory of Bessel functions, we use the classical book by Watson
[
25
]
. For a more updated and well arranged source of numerous formulas for Bessel functions we use the digital library
[
11
]
which replaced the older book
[
1
]
of Abramowitz and Stegun. Let us also mention Olver’s book
[
22
]
, where proofs on various asymptotic formulas for Bessel functions can be found.

For
0<r<1
, this leads us to consider the solution
\psi
of (
10
) having the form

\psi(r)=r^{1-d/2}J_{\nu}(mr),

where

\nu=\ell+\frac{d}{2}-1.

(11)

Since for
\nu\geq 0
, the function
J_{\nu}(z)
remains bounded as
z\to 0
in the half-plane
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>0
, see e.g.
[
11
, Eq. (10.7.3)]
, we have

\psi\in L^{2}\left((0,1),r^{d-1}{\rm d}r\right).

Later on, see Remark
13
, our analysis will be confined to the fourth quadrant of
{\mathbb{C}}
in the variable
m
, i.e.
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m>0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits m<0
, hence we may assume
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>0
above.

For
r\geq 1
,
V(r)=0
, and by writing
\lambda=k^{2}\in\mathbb{C}
in equation (
8
), we obtain

-\psi^{\prime\prime}(r)-\dfrac{d-1}{r}\psi^{\prime}(r)+\dfrac{\ell(\ell+d-2)}{r^{2}}\psi(r)-k^{2}\psi(r)=0.

To ensure that

\psi\in L^{2}\left((1,\infty),r^{d-1}{\rm d}r\right),

we choose the Hankel function of the first kind
H^{(1)}_{\nu}(z)
as a solution in the corresponding Bessel equation because
H^{(1)}_{\nu}(z)
is exponentially decaying as
z\to\infty
provided that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z>0
, see
[
11
, Eq. (10.2.5)]
. Consequently, up to a multiplicative constant, we may take

\psi(r)=r^{1-d/2}H^{(1)}_{\nu}(kr)

for
r>1
provided that

\operatorname{Im}k>0.

(12)

Now, combing both solutions on
(0,1)
and
[1,\infty)
, and choosing multiplicative constants so that the resulting function is continuous at
r=1
, we get the solution

\psi(r)=\begin{cases}H^{(1)}_{\nu}(k)r^{1-d/2}J_{\nu}(mr),\quad&\mbox{ if }0<r<1,\\
J_{\nu}(m)r^{1-d/2}H^{(1)}_{\nu}(kr),\quad&\mbox{ if }r\geq 1,\end{cases}

provided that we also have (
12
).

Lastly, in order for the constructed solution
\psi
to become the radial eigenfunction of
H_{h}
, its first derivative
\psi^{\prime}
must be continuous at
r=1
, too. It follows that a last condition needs to be imposed:

mJ_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)H^{(1)}_{\nu}(k)-kJ_{\nu}(m)(H^{(1)}_{\nu})^{\prime}(k)=0.

It means that

\frac{k}{m}=\dfrac{J^{\prime}_{\nu}(m)H^{(1)}_{\nu}(k)}{J_{\nu}(m)(H^{(1)}_{\nu})^{\prime}(k)},

(13)

if no division by
0
occurs.
In the proof below, we will restrict our parameter region to complex numbers for which we can exclude the occurrence of division by
0
.
Equation (
13
) is the desired characteristic equation.
In the following it turns out to be easier to work with the squared version of the latter equation.
Using (
9
), it can be written as

\frac{{\rm i}h}{m^{2}}+1=\left(\dfrac{J^{\prime}_{\nu}(m)H^{(1)}_{\nu}(k)}{J_{\nu}(m)(H^{(1)}_{\nu})^{\prime}(k)}\right)^{\!2}.

(14)

Now, for each
h>0
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(determining
\nu
by (
11
)), if any

m,k\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m>0
and
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits k>0

satisfy (
13
),
then
\lambda=k^{2}
is a discrete eigenvalue of
H_{h}
of algebraic multiplicity at least (
6
).
In the proof below, we first find solutions of (
14
) and then check that these solutions in fact solve (
13
), i.e. that the signs on both sides of (
13
) agree.

We conclude this section by locating eigenvalues of
H_{h}
in a strip, which will be used later.

Lemma 2
.

Let
h>0
and
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
H_{h}
. Then
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geq 0
and
0\leq\operatorname{Im}\lambda\leq h
.

Proof.

We show that the numerical range
W(H_{h})
of
H_{h}
is a subset of the strip determined by the equations
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geq 0
and
0\leq\operatorname{Im}\lambda\leq h
. Since the point spectrum of
H_{h}
is a subset of
W(H_{h})
the claim follows.

Suppose
\phi\in\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Dom}\nolimits H_{h}
to be normalised,
\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}=1
. Clearly,

\left\langle{H_{h}\phi,\phi}\right\rangle=\left\langle{-\Delta\phi,\phi}\right\rangle+{\rm i}h\left\langle{\chi_{B_{1}(0)}\phi,\phi}\right\rangle.

As the Laplacian is non-negative, we have

\left\langle{-\Delta\phi,\phi}\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}{\rm d}x\geq 0.

Moreover,

\left\langle{\chi_{B_{1}(0)}\phi,\phi}\right\rangle=\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\phi(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x=1,

by assumption. It follows that
W(H_{h})\subset[0,\infty)+{\rm i}[0,h]
for
h>0
, and the proof is complete.
∎

3.
Selected properties of Bessel functions

In order to analyse zeros of the equation (
14
) for
h
large, we need to understand the asymptotic behaviour of the involved Bessel functions in a special regime when both the main argument as well as the order tend simultaneously but not independently to infinity.
This section collects auxiliary results on selected properties of Bessel functions that will be used later in the proof of Theorem
1
.

3.1.
Two expansion formulas for Bessel functions

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we adopt Hankel’s notation:

(\nu,n):=\frac{1}{n!}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left[\nu^{2}-\left(j-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\!2}\right],

see
[
25
, p. 198]
. Our proof heavily relies on the following expansions of Bessel functions with a certain uniform control of remainder terms in a complex sector.

Lemma 3
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then for all
\nu\geq p
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
0<|z|\leq 2\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z
, we have

J_{\nu}(z)J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+Y_{\nu}(z)Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)=-\dfrac{1}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\frac{(2n+1)!}{4^{n}n!}\frac{(\nu,n)}{z^{2n+2}}+\mathcal{X}_{p}(z;\nu)

(15)

and

J^{2}_{\nu}(z)+Y^{2}_{\nu}(z)=\dfrac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}n!}\frac{(\nu,n)}{z^{2n+1}}+\mathcal{Y}_{p}(z;\nu)

(16)

with

|\mathcal{X}_{p}(z;\nu)|\leq C_{p}\,\frac{\nu^{2p}}{|z|^{2p+2}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad|\mathcal{Y}_{p}(z;\nu)|\leq C_{p}^{\prime}\,\frac{\nu^{2p}}{|z|^{2p+1}},

where the constants
C_{p},C_{p}^{\prime}>0
depend on
p
, but are independent of
\nu
and
z
.

Proof.

We prove (
15
) together with the inequality for the remainder term. The proof of (
16
) is similar and will only be indicated at the end.

Our starting point is Nicholson’s integral representation formula

J^{2}_{\nu}(z)+Y^{2}_{\nu}(z)=\dfrac{8}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{0}(2z\sinh(t))\cosh(2\nu t)\,{\rm d}t,

(17)

which holds true if
\operatorname{Re}{z}>0
, see
[
11
, Eq. (10.9.30)]
or
[
25
, p. 444]
. The function
K_{0}
in the integrand is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order zero. By differentiating (
17
) with respect to
z
and using the identity
K_{0}^{\prime}(z)=-K_{1}(z)
valid for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, see
[
11
, Eq. (10.29.3)]
, one obtains the formula

J_{\nu}(z)J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+Y_{\nu}(z)Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)=-\dfrac{8}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{1}(2z\sinh(t))\cosh(2\nu t)\sinh(t)\,{\rm d}t.

(18)

Next, we make use of the expansion

\dfrac{\cosh(2\nu t)}{\cosh(t)}=\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\dfrac{n!(\nu,n)}{(2n)!}2^{2n}\sinh^{2n}(t)+R_{p}(t),

(19)

where

|R_{p}(t)|\leq\left|\dfrac{\cos(\nu\pi)}{\cos(\operatorname{Re}{(\nu\pi)})}\right|\dfrac{p!|(\operatorname{Re}{\nu},p)|}{(2p)!}2^{2p}\sinh^{2p}(t),

see
[
25
, p. 448]
. As our
\nu
is real, the upper bound for the remainder term
R_{p}
simplifies to

|R_{p}(t)|\leq\dfrac{p!|(\nu,p)|}{(2p)!}2^{2p}\sinh^{2p}(t).

(20)

Plugging (
19
) into (
18
) yields

J_{\nu}(z)J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+Y_{\nu}(z)Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)=-\dfrac{8}{\pi^{2}}\left(I_{1,p}+I_{2,p}\right),

(21)

where

I_{1,p}:=\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\dfrac{n!(\nu,n)}{(2n)!}2^{2n}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{1}(2z\sinh(t))\sinh^{2n+1}(t)\cosh(t)\,{\rm d}t,

and

I_{2,p}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{1}(2z\sinh(t))\sinh(t)\cosh(t)R_{p}(t)\,{\rm d}t.

Further we consider separately the integrals
I_{1,p}
and
I_{2,p}
, starting with
I_{1,p}
. By the change of variable
u=\sinh t
, we obtain

I_{1,p}=\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\dfrac{n!(\nu,n)}{(2n)!}2^{2n}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{1}(2zu)u^{2n+1}\,{\rm d}u.

The last integral can be changed to a complex contour integral along a ray from
0
to complex infinity of angle
\arg z
that is located in the right half-plane since
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>0
.
By the analyticity of
K_{1}
and the standard homotopy argument, taking also into account the asymptotic behaviour of
K_{1}(z)
as
z\to 0
and
z\to\infty
from the right half-plane
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>0
, see
[
11
, Eqs. (10.30.2) and (10.25.3)]
, we may deform the integration contour to the positive half-line getting the equality

\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{1}(2zu)u^{2n+1}\,{\rm d}u=\frac{1}{(2z)^{2n+2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{1}(t)t^{2n+1}\,{\rm d}t.

Therefore, we have

I_{1,p}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\dfrac{n!}{(2n)!}\frac{(\nu,n)}{z^{2n+2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{1}(t)t^{2n+1}\,{\rm d}t.

Next we apply the integral identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{\mu_{1}}(t)t^{\mu_{2}-1}{\rm d}t=2^{\mu_{2}-2}\Gamma\left(\dfrac{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\dfrac{\mu_{2}+\mu_{1}}{2}\right),

(22)

which is valid when
\operatorname{Re}\mu_{2}>|\operatorname{Re}\mu_{1}|
, see
[
25
, p. 388, Eq. (8)]
, and arrive at the expression

I_{1,p}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\frac{2^{2n}n!}{(2n)!}\frac{(\nu,n)}{z^{2n+2}}\Gamma\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(n+1+\dfrac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{\pi}{8}\sum_{n=0}^{p-1}\frac{(2n+1)!}{2^{2n}n!}\frac{(\nu,n)}{z^{2n+2}},

(23)

after a simplification of the Gamma functions.

As a next step, we estimate
|I_{2,p}|
from above. Comparing (
21
) and (
23
) with (
15
), we already see that
\mathcal{X}_{p}(z;\nu)=-8I_{2,p}/\pi^{2}
. Again, the change of variable
u=\sinh t
yields

I_{2,p}=\int_{0}^{\infty}uK_{1}(2zu)R_{p}(\arcsinh u)\,{\rm d}u.

Then, by (
20
), we get

|I_{2,p}|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}u|K_{1}(2zu)||R_{p}(\arcsinh u)|\,{\rm d}u\leq\frac{2^{2p}p!}{(2p)!}\,|(\nu,p)|\int_{0}^{\infty}|K_{1}(2zu)|u^{2p+1}\,{\rm d}u.

(24)

In order to deduce a suitable upper bound for
|K_{1}(2zu)|
, we employ the integral representation

K_{1}(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-z\cosh(x)}\cosh(x)\,{\rm d}x

which is valid if
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>0
, see
[
11
, Eq. (10.32.9)]
. At this point, we use the assumption
|z|\leq 2\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z
and deduce the estimate

|K_{1}(2zu)|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-2(\operatorname{Re}{z})u\cosh(x)}\cosh(x)\,{\rm d}x\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-|z|u\cosh(x)}\cosh(x)\,{\rm d}x=K_{1}(u|z|).

By using the last estimate in (
24
), changing the variable
t=u|z|
, applying identity (
22
), and simplifying the resulting expression with Gamma functions similarly as above, one arrives at the estimate

|I_{2,p}|\leq\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{(2p+2)!}{(p+1)!}\frac{|(\nu,p)|}{|z|^{2p+2}}.

Lastly, as we assume that
\nu\geq p
, we may also estimate the Hankel bracket

|(\nu,p)|=\frac{1}{p!}\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left[\nu^{2}-\left(j-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\!2}\right]\leq\frac{\nu^{2p}}{p!}.

In total, we have

|I_{2,p}|\leq\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{(2p+2)!}{p!(p+1)!}\frac{\nu^{2p}}{|z|^{2p+2}}.

Putting together equations (
21
) and (
23
) with the last estimate, we prove (
15
) together with the inequality for the remainder term
\mathcal{X}_{p}(z;\nu)
.

The proof of the second formula (
16
) proceeds similarly. Instead of (
18
), we expand the term
\cosh(2\nu t)
using (
19
) directly in Nicholson’s integral (
17
). Then we continue analogously as before to simplify the integral as in case of
I_{1,p}
and estimate the error term as in case of
I_{2,p}
getting the identity (
16
) as well as the upper bound for
|\mathcal{Y}_{p}(z;\nu)|
. The proof of Lemma
3
is complete.
∎

3.2.
The modulus and phase functions

Further elements of the theory of Bessel functions, which will be frequently used, involve the so-called modulus and phase functions
M_{\nu}
and
\theta_{\nu}
that are introduced by means of the equations

J_{\nu}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\,M_{\nu}(z)\cos\theta_{\nu}(z)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad Y_{\nu}(z)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\,M_{\nu}(z)\sin\theta_{\nu}(z).

(25)

In general, the equations in (
25
) give rise to multi-valued functions
M_{\nu}
and
\theta_{\nu}
. The standard branch of
M_{\nu}
is determined by requiring
M_{\nu}
to be continuous in
(0,\infty)
and
M_{\nu}(x)>0
for
x>0
, and as such,
M_{\nu}
extends to an analytic function in the right half-plane
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z>0
. It follows immediately from (
25
) that

M_{\nu}^{2}(z)=\frac{\pi z}{2}\left(J_{\nu}^{2}(z)+Y_{\nu}^{2}(z)\right).

(26)

From the form of the equations in (
25
), one may suggest to incorporate the factor
\sqrt{2/(\pi z)}
into the modulus function
M_{\nu}(z)
. This alternative notation for the modulus function, which is unfortunately denoted by
M_{\nu}
again, is also used in more modern literature on Bessel functions, see for example
[
11
, § 10.18]
. Here we stick with the original notation of Marshall for
M_{\nu}
from (
25
) that is also used in Watson’s treatise
[
25
]
. One needs to be careful with this double meaning of
M_{\nu}
when, for example, formulas listed in
[
11
, § 10.18]
are used; cf. (
26
) with
[
11
, Eq. (10.18.6)]
.

It also readily follows from (
25
) that

\theta_{\nu}(z)=\arctan\frac{Y_{\nu}(z)}{J_{\nu}(z)},

(27)

cf.
[
11
, Eq. (10.18.7)]
, from which, using the Wronskian identity
[
25
, p. 76]

J_{\nu}(z)Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)-J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)Y_{\nu}(z)=\frac{2}{\pi z},

(28)

one infers that

\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{J_{\nu}(z)Y_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)-J_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)Y_{\nu}(z)}{J_{\nu}^{2}(z)+Y_{\nu}^{2}(z)}=\frac{2}{\pi z}\frac{1}{{J_{\nu}^{2}(z)+Y_{\nu}^{2}(z)}}.

(29)

The standard branch of
\theta_{\nu}
, which is adopted below, is determined by requiring
\theta_{\nu}
to be continuous in
(0,\infty)
and
\theta_{\nu}(x)\to-\pi/2
as
x\to 0+
. Below, we verify, for
\nu\geq 1
, that this branch of
\theta_{\nu}
is an analytic function in the open convex set

\mathcal{M}_{\nu}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid A\nu<\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z\mbox{ and }|z|<2\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z\},

(30)

with

A:=\sqrt{\pi C_{1}^{\prime}},

(31)

where
C_{1}^{\prime}>0
is the constant from Lemma
3
with
p=1
. This is, in fact, a subset of a half-plane where the standard branch of
\theta_{\nu}
extends to a single-valued analytic function, but this is not needed for our purposes. In addition to the analyticity region for the phase function, the definition of the set
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
is also designed for other aspects of the forthcoming analysis.

The modulus and phase functions were introduced by Marshall and used in an asymptotic analysis of large zeros of the cylinder function, see
[
25
, p. 505]
. In fact, Marshall introduced a slightly different phase function given by

\psi_{\nu}(z):=z-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}-\theta_{\nu}(z),

(32)

and worked with a different pair of functions
P(\cdot,\nu)
and
Q(\cdot,\nu)
related to
J_{\nu}
and
Y_{\nu}
; the reader may find details in
[
25
]
. We also occasionally use the function
\psi_{\nu}
below. The modulus and phase functions were used in more recent work
[
19
]
to study the asymptotic behaviour of
J_{\nu}(z)
and
Y_{\nu}(z)
in the so-called Fresnel regime
|z|>\nu
, but with
z
confined to positive reals. This is close but not exactly what is needed in our analysis. Although not directly applied, the methods from paper
[
19
]
as well as Marshall’s original ideas inspired our approach.

Lemma 4
.

For
\nu\geq 1
, the phase functions
\theta_{\nu}
and
\psi_{\nu}
are analytic in the set
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
defined by (
30
). Moreover, we have the estimates

|\psi_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)|\leq A^{2}\frac{\nu^{2}}{|z|^{2}}

(33)

and

|\psi_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}(z)|\leq\tilde{A}\frac{\nu^{2}}{|z|^{3}}

(34)

for all
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, where
A
is the constant in (
31
),
\tilde{A}:=2\pi(C_{1}^{\prime}+2C_{1})>0
, and
C_{1},C_{1}^{\prime}
are the constants from Lemma
3
.

Proof.

First we verify the analyticity of
\theta_{\nu}
in
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
. Then
\psi_{\nu}
must be analytic in
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, too, by its definition (
32
). Bearing (
29
) in mind, the value of the standard branch of
\theta_{\nu}
at
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
can be expressed by the contour integral

\theta_{\nu}(z)=-\frac{\pi}{2}+\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{z}\frac{1}{{J_{\nu}^{2}(u)+Y_{\nu}^{2}(u)}}\frac{{\rm d}u}{u}

where the integration contour consists of the interval
(0,A\nu)
and then continues arbitrarily to the point
z
within the set
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, provided that the analytic function
J_{\nu}^{2}+Y_{\nu}^{2}
has no zeros in
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
. Notice that the integrand is indeed integrable near
0+
for
\nu>0
, since
J_{\nu}(u)=\mathcal{O}{\left(u^{\nu}\right)}
and
Y_{\nu}(u)=\mathcal{O}{\left(u^{-\nu}\right)}
as
u\to 0+
, see
[
11
, Eqs. (10.7.3–4)]
.

Once we check that
J_{\nu}^{2}(z)+Y_{\nu}^{2}(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, the analyticity of
\theta_{\nu}
in
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
follows from the contour integral representation. To this end, we apply Lemma
3
with
p=1
getting, for any
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, the estimate

|J_{\nu}^{2}(z)+Y_{\nu}^{2}(z)|\geq\frac{2}{\pi|z|}-|\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)|\geq\frac{2}{\pi|z|}-C_{1}^{\prime}\frac{\nu^{2}}{|z|^{3}}=\frac{2}{\pi|z|}-\frac{A^{2}\nu^{2}}{\pi|z|^{3}}\geq\frac{1}{\pi|z|}>0,

where we have used that
A\nu<\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z\leq|z|
for
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
. Consequently,
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
is a zero-free region for
J_{\nu}^{2}+Y_{\nu}^{2}
, indeed.

Second we establish (
33
). Substituting from (
16
) with
p=1
in (
29
), we get

\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{2}{\pi z}\frac{1}{{J_{\nu}^{2}(z)+Y_{\nu}^{2}(z)}}=\frac{2}{2+\pi z\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)}.

(35)

Using also (
32
), we obtain the expression

\psi_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)=1-\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{\pi z\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)}{2+\pi z\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)}.

By Lemma
3
and definition (
30
) of the set
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, we have

\left|\pi z\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)\right|\leq\pi C_{1}^{\prime}\frac{\nu^{2}}{|z|^{2}}=A^{2}\frac{\nu^{2}}{|z|^{2}}\leq 1

(36)

for all
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
. Thus, for any
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, we may estimate as

|\psi_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{|\pi z\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)|}{2-|\pi z\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)|}\leq A^{2}\frac{\nu^{2}}{|z|^{2}},

proving (
33
).

Lastly, we prove (
34
). By (
32
), we have
\psi_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}=-\theta_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}
. Differentiating (
29
) once more, we find

\psi_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}(z)=\frac{2}{\pi z}\frac{1}{J_{\nu}^{2}(z)+Y_{\nu}^{2}(z)}\left[\frac{1}{z}+\frac{2J_{\nu}(z)J_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)+2Y_{\nu}(z)Y_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)}{J_{\nu}^{2}(z)+Y_{\nu}^{2}(z)}\right].

Bearing in mind (
29
) and using Lemma
3
with
p=1
, we arrive at the expression

\psi_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}(z)=\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\frac{\pi\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)+2\pi z\mathcal{X}_{1}(z;\nu)}{2+\pi z\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)}.

For any
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, we may estimate roughly
|\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)|\leq 2
by (
35
) and (
36
).
The remaining fraction is to be estimated with the aid of Lemma
3
as follows:

\left|\frac{\pi\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)+2\pi z\mathcal{X}_{1}(z;\nu)}{2+\pi z\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)}\right|\leq\frac{\pi|\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)|+2\pi|z\mathcal{X}_{1}(z;\nu)|}{2-\pi|z\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)|}\leq\pi(C_{1}^{\prime}+2C_{1})\frac{{\nu}^{2}}{|z|^{3}}.

This completes the proof of inequality (
34
).
∎

From the bounds on the derivative of
\psi_{\nu}
from Lemma
4
, we further deduce bounds on the function
\psi_{\nu}
itself in
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\nu\geq 1
. Then for all
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, we have the inequalities

|\psi_{\nu}(z)|\leq 2A^{2}\frac{\nu^{2}}{|z|}

(37)

and

|\operatorname{Im}\psi_{\nu}(z)|\leq 4A^{2}|\operatorname{Im}z|\frac{\nu^{2}}{|z|^{2}},

(38)

where
A>0
is the constant in (
31
).

Proof.

First we verify (
37
). The idea of the proof is to integrate the expansion for the derivative
\psi_{\nu}^{\prime}
and determine the constant term. First note that if
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, then
z+[0,\infty)\subset\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
. We show that, with
\nu\geq 1
fixed,

\psi_{\nu}(z)\to 0\mbox{ as }z\to\infty\mbox{ along a horizontal ray in }\mathcal{M}_{\nu}.

(39)

For
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
it follows that

|\psi_{\nu}(z)|\leq|\psi_{\nu}(z)-\psi_{\nu}(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z)|+|\psi_{\nu}(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z)|.

Due to
[
11
, Eq. (10.18.18)]
with definition (
32
),
|\psi_{\nu}(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z)|\to 0
as
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z\to\infty
. Now, to determine the limit of
|\psi_{\nu}(z)-\psi_{\nu}(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z)|
, we make use of the analyticity of
\psi_{\nu}
in
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, see Lemma
4
, and the mean value theorem to infer that

|\psi_{\nu}(z)-\psi_{\nu}(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z)|\leq|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z|\sup_{\xi\in(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z,z)}|\psi_{\nu}^{\prime}(\xi)|,

where
(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z,z)
is the open vertical line segment connecting the points
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z
and
z
. Since this line segment is entirely contained in
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, the inequality (
33
) is applied to deduce

|\psi_{\nu}(z)-\psi_{\nu}(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z)|\leq|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z|\frac{A^{2}\nu^{2}}{(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z)^{2}}\to 0

as
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z\to\infty
with
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z
constant. The claim from (
39
) follows.

Next, we consider the ray
z+[0,\infty)
for

z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
. As said before, this ray is
entirely located in the set
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, on which
\psi_{\nu}
is analytic. Consequently, bearing also (
39
) in mind, we may express
\psi_{\nu}(z)
as the contour integral

\psi_{\nu}(z)=-\int_{z}^{+\infty+{\rm i}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z}\psi_{\nu}^{\prime}(u)\,{\rm d}u.

Notice that the integral converges since
|\psi_{\nu}^{\prime}(u)|
decays at least as
1/|u|^{2}
for
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits u
large, as we know from (
33
). Then, with the aid of (
33
), we may readily estimate

|\psi_{\nu}(z)|=\int_{z}^{+\infty+{\rm i}\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z}|\psi_{\nu}^{\prime}(u)|\,|{\rm d}u|\leq A^{2}\nu^{2}\int_{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z}^{\infty}\frac{{\rm d}x}{x^{2}}=\frac{A^{2}\nu^{2}}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z}\leq 2A^{2}\,\frac{\nu^{2}}{|z|},

proving (
37
).

Lastly, we verify (
38
). Fix
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
. Recall that
\psi_{\nu}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
if
x>0
. Then, by the above arguments, we have

|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\psi_{\nu}(z)|=|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\psi_{\nu}(z)-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\psi_{\nu}(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z)|\leq|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z|\sup_{\xi\in(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z,z)}|\psi_{\nu}^{\prime}(\xi)|\leq|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z|\frac{A^{2}\nu^{2}}{(\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z)^{2}}.

We employ
|z|<2\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits z
to argue

|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\psi_{\nu}(z)|\leq|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits z|\frac{4A^{2}\nu^{2}}{|z|^{2}}.

The proof is complete.
∎

We will also prepare formulas for an asymptotic analysis of the logarithmic derivatives of the functions
J_{\nu}
and
H_{\nu}^{(1)}
that appear in the equation (
14
).

Lemma 6
.

Let
\nu\geq 1
and
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
. Then we have

\frac{J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)}{J_{\nu}(z)}=-\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\tan\theta_{\nu}(z)+\mathcal{Z}_{0}(z;\nu),

(40)

where

|\mathcal{Z}_{0}(z;\nu)|\leq\frac{C_{0}\pi}{|z|}.

If, in addition,
|z|>1+C_{1}/C_{1}^{\prime}
, we have

\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\frac{H^{(1)}_{\nu}(z)}{(H^{(1)}_{\nu})^{\prime}(z)}=-{\rm i}+\mathcal{Z}_{1}(z;\nu),

(41)

where

|\mathcal{Z}_{1}(z;\nu)|\leq\left(1+\frac{C_{1}}{C_{1}^{\prime}}\right)\frac{1}{|z|}.

The positive constants
C_{0}
,
C_{1}
, and
C_{1}^{\prime}
are those of Lemma
3
.

Proof.

We verify (
40
).
First, taking the logarithmic derivative with respect to
z
in the first equation of (
25
) yields the formula

\frac{J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)}{J_{\nu}(z)}=-\frac{1}{2z}+\frac{M^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)}{M_{\nu}(z)}-\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\tan\theta_{\nu}(z).

Second, taking the logarithmic derivative this time in (
26
) results in the equality

\frac{M^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)}{M_{\nu}(z)}=\frac{1}{2z}+\frac{J_{\nu}(z)J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+Y_{\nu}(z)Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)}{J^{2}_{\nu}(z)+Y^{2}_{\nu}(z)}.

Combining these two equations, we arrive at (
40
) with

\mathcal{Z}_{0}(z;\nu):=\frac{J_{\nu}(z)J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+Y_{\nu}(z)Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)}{J^{2}_{\nu}(z)+Y^{2}_{\nu}(z)}.

Next, we apply Lemma
3
with
p=0
to the nominator term, with
p=1
to the denominator term, and estimate as follows,

|\mathcal{Z}_{0}(z;\nu)|=\left|\frac{\mathcal{X}_{0}(z;\nu)}{\frac{2}{\pi z}+\mathcal{Y}_{1}(z;\nu)}\right|\leq\frac{C_{0}\pi}{|z|}\frac{1}{2-\pi C_{1}^{\prime}\frac{\nu^{2}}{|z|^{2}}}<\frac{C_{0}\pi}{|z|},

where we used that
\pi C_{1}^{\prime}\nu^{2}/|z|^{2}<1
for
z\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, see definition (
30
).

Further, we prove (
41
).
By using the connection formula
[
11
, Eq. (10.4.3)]

H^{(1)}_{\nu}(z)=J_{\nu}(z)+{\rm i}Y_{\nu}(z),

we get

\frac{(H^{(1)}_{\nu})^{\prime}(z)}{H^{(1)}_{\nu}(z)}=\dfrac{J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+{\rm i}Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)}{J_{\nu}(z)+{\rm i}Y_{\nu}(z)}=\frac{J_{\nu}(z)J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+Y_{\nu}(z)Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+{\rm i}(J_{\nu}(z)Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)-J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)Y_{\nu}(z))}{J^{2}_{\nu}(z)+Y^{2}_{\nu}(z)}.

Employing also the Wronskian identity (
28
) and taking the reciprocal, we arrive at the equality

\frac{H^{(1)}_{\nu}(z)}{(H^{(1)}_{\nu})^{\prime}(z)}=\frac{J^{2}_{\nu}(z)+Y^{2}_{\nu}(z)}{J_{\nu}(z)J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+Y_{\nu}(z)Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+2{\rm i}/(\pi z)}.

(42)

Taking (
29
) into account and applying formula (
15
) with
p=1
, we get

\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\frac{H^{(1)}_{\nu}(z)}{(H^{(1)}_{\nu})^{\prime}(z)}=\frac{1}{{\rm i}+\frac{\pi z}{2}\left(J_{\nu}(z)J^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)+Y_{\nu}(z)Y^{\prime}_{\nu}(z)\right)}=\frac{1}{{\rm i}-\frac{1}{2z}+\frac{\pi z}{2}\mathcal{X}_{1}(z;\nu)}.

From here, one infers (
41
) with

\mathcal{Z}_{1}(z;\nu):=\frac{-\frac{1}{z}+\pi z\mathcal{X}_{1}(z;\nu)}{2+\frac{{\rm i}}{z}-{\rm i}\pi z\mathcal{X}_{1}(z;\nu)}.

Estimates similar to those from the first part of the proof yield

|\mathcal{Z}_{1}(z;\nu)|\leq\frac{1}{|z|}\frac{1+\frac{C_{1}}{C_{1}^{\prime}}}{2-\frac{1}{|z|}\left(1+\frac{C_{1}}{C_{1}^{\prime}}\right)}<\left(1+\frac{C_{1}}{C_{1}^{\prime}}\right)\frac{1}{|z|},

where we used the additional assumption
|z|>1+C_{1}/C_{1}^{\prime}
. The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 7
.

Recall that, for
\nu>-1
, the Bessel function

in (

4.
The Demuth–Hansmann–Katriel open problem

In this section, we prove Theorem
1
.

4.1.
Initial set-up

Rather than analysing the asymptotic behaviour of the entire discrete spectrum of
H_{h}
for
h
large, we impose additional restrictions on the discrete eigenvalues and analyse only those from a certain complex set evolving with
h\to\infty
. This is inspired by the approach used in the one-dimensional situation
[
5
]
. The eigenvalues will be indexed by two indices from certain integer sets for which we need to introduce a suitable notation.

We fix three parameters

0<\alpha<\beta<\gamma<\frac{1}{2},

(43)

and define

\mathcal{L}(h):=\left\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\mid h^{\alpha+1/2}\leq\ell\leq h^{\beta+1/2}\right\}

(44)

for
h>0
. Next, we fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, put

q:=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{d-1},

(45)

and define the set

\mathcal{J}(h,\ell):=\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}\;\bigg|\;\ell\log^{q}\ell\leq j\leq h^{\gamma+1/2}\right\}

(46)

for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
h>0
.

Next we slightly reformulate the inequality (
5
). Recall definition (
4
) of
V_{h}
. We have

\|V_{h}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|V_{h}(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x=\mu_{d}h^{p},

where
\mu_{d}:=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(1+d/2)
is the volume of the unit ball
B_{1}(0)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Further, Lemma
2
tells us that
\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{h})\subset[0,\infty)+{\rm i}[0,h]
.
Consequently,
\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))=\operatorname{Im}\lambda
for all
\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{h})
, and hence

\frac{1}{\|V_{h}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{h})}\dfrac{\text{dist}(\lambda,[0,\infty))^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}=\frac{1}{\mu_{d}h^{p}}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{h})}\dfrac{(\operatorname{Im}\lambda)^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}.

Therefore, to establish Theorem
1
, it is sufficient to prove the following statement
and then to set
C_{p,d}:=C_{p,d}^{\prime}\,(\beta-\alpha)
.

Proposition 8
.

Suppose
d\geq 2
and
p>0
. Let
\alpha,\beta
satisfy (
43
) and let
0<\varepsilon<1
.
Then there exist
C_{p,d}^{\prime}>0
and
h_{0}>0
such that, for all
h\geq h_{0}
, we have

\frac{1}{h^{p}}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{h})}\dfrac{(\operatorname{Im}\lambda)^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}\geq C_{p,d}^{\prime}\,(\beta-\alpha)\,(\log h)^{\varepsilon}.

Remark 9
.

While the constant
C_{p,d}^{\prime}
does not depend on the choice of
\varepsilon
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, the threshold value
h_{0}
depends on these parameters. In fact, keeping track of the error terms below, one sees that they depend on
h^{-\alpha}
,
h^{\gamma-1/2}
and
(\log h)^{-q}
, which all go to zero as
h\to\infty
but the convergence rate is dependent on the choice of parameters.

The proof of Proposition
8
proceeds in five steps worked out in the subsections below.

Further we make the following notational conventions. It would become cumbersome to keep track on all constants in various estimations to be made (as we kept doing in Section
3
). Therefore we will occasionally use the notation
\lesssim
or
\gtrsim
when the inequalities
\leq
or
\geq
hold up to a multiplicative constant. Moreover, it is important that the hidden constant will be independent on any choice of indices
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
,
j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
(also on the parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
and
\varepsilon

Lemma 10

It follows that

Proof of claim (i):

We prove the analyticity of

in

As a result, for

\frac{|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits m|}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits m}\leq\frac{1+|\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits(m_{\nu,j}^{(0)})|/\nu}{-1+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits(m_{\nu,j}^{(0)})/\nu}\lesssim\frac{1}{\log^{q}h},

Next, we prove that

\left|\operatorname{Im}\theta_{\nu}(m)-\log\frac{\sqrt{h}}{4\pi j}\right|<4

for any
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
. By using the ranges from definitions (
46
) and (
44
) of the sets
\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
and
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
, we deduce the two-sided estimate

-\log 4\pi-\gamma\log h\leq\log\frac{\sqrt{h}}{4\pi j}\leq-\alpha\log h-\log\log^{q}h-q\log\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\right).

Clearly, choosing
h_{0}
large enough, we have the inequalities (
67
).

To deduce the inequality for the tangent in (
68
), we write

{\rm i}+\tan\theta_{\nu}(m)={\rm i}-{\rm i}\frac{1-{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}{1+{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}=\frac{2{\rm i}{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}{1+{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}.

With the aid of (
67
), for
h_{0}
sufficiently large, we get the desired estimate

|{\rm i}+\tan\theta_{\nu}(m)|\leq\frac{2{\rm e}^{2\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\theta_{\nu}(m)}}{1-{\rm e}^{2\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\theta_{\nu}(m)}}\leq\frac{2h^{-2\alpha}}{1-h^{-2\alpha}}\leq 4h^{-2\alpha}.

It remains to verify the inequality for the cosine in (
68
). Using (
67
), we find, again for
h_{0}
sufficiently large,

|\cos\theta_{\nu}(m)|\leq\frac{{\rm e}^{-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\theta_{\nu}(m)}}{2}\left(1+{\rm e}^{2\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\theta_{\nu}(m)}\right)\leq\frac{{\rm e}^{-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\theta_{\nu}(m)}}{2}\left(1+h^{-2\alpha}\right)\leq{\rm e}^{-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\theta_{\nu}(m)}.

By using also estimate (
69
), we get

|\cos\theta_{\nu}(m)|\leq{\rm e}^{4-\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\theta_{\nu}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})}.

Finally, with (
70
), we arrive at the second estimate from (
68
).
∎

Next we explore properties of an error term function

\xi_{\nu}(m):=\sin^{2}\theta_{\nu}(m)+\left(\frac{J_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)}{J_{\nu}(m)(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)}\right)^{\!2}\!\cos^{2}\theta_{\nu}(m),

(71)

where
k=\sqrt{{\rm i}h+m^{2}}
with the principal branch of the square root.

Lemma 16
.

Let
\nu=\ell+\frac{d}{2}-1
and
q=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{d-1}
for a fixed
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then there exists
h_{0}>0
such that for all
h\geq h_{0}
,
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
, and
j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
, the function
\xi_{\nu}
is analytic in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
and there is a constant
C>0
independent of
j
,
\ell
, and
m
such that

|\xi_{\nu}(m)|\leq\frac{C}{\log^{2q}h}

(72)

for any
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
.

Proof.

First we verify the analyticity of
\xi_{\nu}
in the balls
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
. We may suppose
h
to be sufficiently large so that
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})\subset\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, see (
51
). Then the phase function
\theta_{\nu}
, which appears in definition (
71
) of the function
\xi_{\nu}
, is analytic in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
by Lemma
4
. Recall that the Bessel functions
J_{\nu}
as well as
H_{\nu}^{(1)}
are analytic functions of their main argument in the right half-plane. The balls
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
are located therein, in fact in the fourth quadrant of
{\mathbb{C}}
by Remark
13
. The argument
k
, as the principal branch square root of
{\rm i}h+m^{2}
, also fulfills
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Re}\nolimits k>0
.

Thus, we see from the definition (
71
) that
\xi_{\nu}
is analytic in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
if
J_{\nu}(m)
and
(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)
do not vanish in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
. The case of
J_{\nu}(m)
is clear as it is well known that for
\nu>-1
, the Bessel function
J_{\nu}
possesses real zeros only, see Remark
7
. The same remark also implies that
(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)\neq 0
for all
h
sufficiently large since
k\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
by Lemma
14
. Consequently, we see that for all
h
large enough, the function
\xi_{\nu}
defined by the expression (
71
) is analytic in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
for all
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
and
j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
.

In the rest of the proof, we derive the uniform bound (
72
). The proof relies on asymptotic analysis of the Bessel functions appearing in the definition (
71
). Below we make use of the Landau symbol
\mathcal{O}
for
h\to\infty
which is uniform in
\ell
,
j
and
m
, i.e. the involved constant is always independent of
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
,
j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
, and
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
.

Let us rewrite (
71
) as

\xi_{\nu}(m)=\left[\tan^{2}\theta_{\nu}(m)+\left(\frac{J_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)}{J_{\nu}(m)(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)}\right)^{\!2}\,\right]\cos^{2}\theta_{\nu}(m).

(73)

Notice that
\cos\theta_{\nu}(m)\neq 0
for
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
since our
m
is non-real by Remark
13
, while
\cos\theta_{\nu}(m)
may vanish only on zeros of
J_{\nu}
that are real, see (
25
).
First we analyse the asymptotic behaviour of the expression from (
73
) in the square brackets.
It follows readily from Lemma
6
that

\frac{J^{\prime}_{\nu}(m)}{J_{\nu}(m)}=-\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)\tan\theta_{\nu}(m)+\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)}

as
h\to\infty
. Using also that the ratio
H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)/(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)
remains bounded for
k\in\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
of sufficiently large modulus, see Remark
7
, we find

\frac{J_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)}{J_{\nu}(m)(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)}=-\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)\frac{H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)}{(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)}\tan\theta_{\nu}(m)+\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)}.

(74)

The function in front of
\tan\theta_{\nu}(m)
cannot be estimated directly by using (
41
) as the arguments are not the same. Next, we slightly manipulate the expression to deduce its asymptotic behaviour.

By the mean value theorem,

|\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)-\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(k)|\leq|m-k|\max_{z\in(m,k)}|\theta_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}(z)|.

For
h
large enough, the entire line segment
(m,k)
is located in the convex set
\mathcal{M}_{\nu}
, and so we may apply the uniform bound from (
34
).
Recalling that
\psi_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}=-\theta_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}
and taking (
61
) into account, we obtain

\max_{z\in(m,k)}|\theta_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}(z)|=\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{\nu^{2}}{m^{3}}\right)}.

Since
k^{2}-m^{2}={\rm i}h
and using (
61
) once more, we find

|m-k|=\frac{h}{|m+k|}=\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{h}{m}\right)}.

Altogether, we observe that

\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)-\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(k)=\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{h\nu^{2}}{m^{4}}\right)}.

Now we may estimate

\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)\frac{H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)}{(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)}=\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(k)\frac{H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)}{(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)}+\left(\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)-\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(k)\right)\frac{H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)}{(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)}

with the aid of (
41
) and the boundedness of
H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)/(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)
to deduce the uniform asymptotic formula

\theta_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)\frac{H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)}{(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)}=-{\rm i}+\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)}+\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{h\nu^{2}}{m^{4}}\right)}.

(75)

Plugging (
75
) into (
74
) and taking also into account the uniform boundedness of
\tan\theta_{\nu}(m)
in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
, see (
68
), we arrive at the uniform asymptotic formula

\frac{J_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)}{J_{\nu}(m)(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)}={\rm i}\tan\theta_{\nu}(m)+\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)}+\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{h\nu^{2}}{m^{4}}\right)}.

(76)

When applied in (
73
) and using the first formula from (
68
) once more, we get

\xi_{\nu}(m)=\left[\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)}+\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{h\nu^{2}}{m^{4}}\right)}\right]\cos^{2}\theta_{\nu}(m).

Using also the second formula from (
68
), we finally deduce that

\xi_{\nu}(m)=\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{j^{2}}{mh}\right)}+\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{j^{2}\nu^{2}}{m^{4}}\right)}.

With the aid of Lemma
11
we may estimate the last two uniform Landau
\mathcal{O}s
as follows. The first error term
can be estimated by

\frac{j^{2}}{|m|h}\lesssim\frac{j}{h}\leq h^{\gamma-1/2},

whereas the second inequality uses (
46
); note that
\gamma<1/2
. On the other hand, the rate of the second term fulfills

\frac{j^{2}\nu^{2}}{|m|^{4}}\lesssim\frac{\nu^{2}}{|m|^{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\log^{2q}h},

where the second inequality is a consequence of (
49
). This yields (
72
) and the proof is complete.
∎

4.4.
Step 3: Solutions of the characteristic equation

Next we move towards proving existence of solutions of the characteristic equation (
13
). To this end, we will need the following auxiliary statement.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\nu=\ell+\frac{d}{2}-1
and
q=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{d-1}
for a fixed
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then there exists
h_{0}>0
such that for all
h\geq h_{0}
,
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
, and
j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
, the following claims hold:

(1)

The function

\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m):=-1+\frac{m}{4\pi j}\frac{{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}{\cos\theta_{\nu}(m)}\sqrt{1-\xi_{\nu}(m)}

is analytic in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
and there is a constant
C>0
independent of
j
,
\ell
and
m
such that

|\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m)|\leq\frac{C}{\log^{q}h}

(77)

for any
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
.

(2)

If
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
satisfies

{\rm i}\left(\theta_{\nu}(m)-\frac{\pi}{4}-2\pi j\right)=\log\frac{4\pi j}{\sqrt{h}}+\log\left(1+\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m)\right),

(78)

then
m
is a solution of the characteristic equation (
13
) with corresponding
k=k(m)
given by (
60
).

Proof.

Proof of claim (i):
First, we verify the analyticity of the error function
\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}
. Using Lemma
16
, we may suppose
h_{0}
to be sufficiently large to ensure that
\xi_{\nu}
is analytic in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
and
|\xi_{\nu}(m)|<1
for all
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
,
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
,
j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
, and
h\geq h_{0}
. Then the function
m\mapsto\sqrt{1-\xi_{\nu}(m)}
is analytic in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
. Since we already know from Lemma
4
and (
51
) that
\theta_{\nu}
is analytic in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
, and also that
\cos\theta_{\nu}(m)\neq 0
in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
, see the paragraph below (
73
), we conclude that
\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}
is indeed an analytic function in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
.

Second, we prove (
77
). We estimate the three factors in

\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m)=-1+\frac{m}{2\pi j}\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}\sqrt{1-\xi_{\nu}(m)},

(79)

using the uniform Landau symbol
\mathcal{O}
for
h\to\infty
, where the involved constant is always independent of
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
,
j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
, and
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
.
First, with the aid of (
72
), we deduce that

\sqrt{1-\xi_{\nu}(m)}=1+\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2q}h}\right)}.

Second, it follows from (
67
) that

\frac{1}{1+{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}=\frac{1}{1+\mathcal{O}{\left(h^{-2\alpha}\right)}}=1+\mathcal{O}{\left(h^{-2\alpha}\right)}.

Third, one infers from (
59
) and (
54
) that

\frac{m}{2\pi j}=1+\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{1}{\log^{q}h}\right)}.

Inserting the last three estimates into (
79
) amounts to the uniform asymptotic formula

\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m)=\mathcal{O}{\left(\frac{1}{\log^{q}h}\right)},

which yields (
77
).

Proof of claim (ii):
Using the definition (
71
), the equation (
14
) can be written as

-\frac{{\rm i}h}{m^{2}}=\frac{1-\xi_{\nu}(m)}{\cos^{2}\theta_{\nu}(m)}.

Hence, any
m
solving the equation

{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+2\pi j\right)}\frac{\sqrt{h}}{m}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\xi_{\nu}(m)}}{\cos\theta_{\nu}(m)}

with an integer
j
, has to be a solution of (
14
). When we rewrite the last equation as

{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\left(\theta_{\nu}(m)-\frac{\pi}{4}-2\pi j\right)}=\frac{2m}{\sqrt{h}}\frac{\sqrt{1-\xi_{\nu}(m)}}{1+{\rm e}^{-2{\rm i}\theta_{\nu}(m)}}=\frac{4\pi j}{\sqrt{h}}\left(1+\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m)\right),

we see that, if
m\in B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
is a solution of the equation (
78
), then
m
solves (
14
).
It is left to show that
m
then also satisfies (
13
), by taking the principal branch square root on both sides of (
14
). It suffices to compare the signs of the leading order term on each side of (
13
).
By Lemma
14
, we know that
k/m\to 1
. On the other hand,
{\rm i}\tan\theta_{\nu}(m)\to 1
by (
68
), which, when inserted to (
76
), implies that

\frac{J_{\nu}^{\prime}(m)H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k)}{J_{\nu}(m)(H_{\nu}^{(1)})^{\prime}(k)}\to 1.

So the signs agree and the proof is complete.
∎

Claim (ii) of Lemma
17
lacks existence of the solutions. This is proven in the next statement.

Proposition 18
.

Let
\nu=\ell+\frac{d}{2}-1
. Then there exists
h_{0}>0
such that for all
h\geq h_{0}
,
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
, and
j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
,
there exists a unique solution
m_{\nu,j}
of the characteristic equation (
13
) in the ball
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
.

Proof.

The claim relies on Rouché’s theorem and Lemma
17
. Recalling definition (
47
), equation (
78
) can be written as

f_{\nu,j}(m)+{\rm i}\log(1+\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m))=0.

(80)

We show that
h_{0}
can be chosen so that, for all
h\geq h_{0}
, we have

|f_{\nu,j}(m)|>|\log(1+\operatorname{err}_{\nu,j}(m))|

(81)

for all
m\in\partial B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
,
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
, and
j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
. Then, by Rouché’s theorem, equation (
80
) has a unique solution
m_{\nu,j}
in the ball
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
since
m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}
is a unique zero of
f_{\nu,j}
in
B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
, see Lemma
10
. According to claim (ii) of Lemma
17
,
m_{\nu,j}
is then also a solution of the characteristic equation (
13
).

Using definitions (
32
), (
47
) together with the equality
f_{\nu,j}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})=0
, we get

f_{\nu,j}(m)=f_{\nu,j}(m)-f_{\nu,j}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})=m-m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}-\psi_{\nu}(m)+\psi_{\nu}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)}).

Hence, if
m\in\partial B_{2}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})
, we find that

|f_{\nu,j}(m)|\geq 2-|\psi_{\nu}(m)-\psi_{\nu}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})|.

Moreover, with the aid of the mean value theorem, the uniform limit (
49
), and the estimate (
33
), one may ensure that

|\psi_{\nu}(m)-\psi_{\nu}(m_{\nu,j}^{(1)})|<1

for all
h
large enough,
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
, and
j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)
. Thus,

|f_{\nu,j}(m)|>1
for all such
h
. On the other hand, by (
77
), the right-hand side of (
81
) is smaller than
1
for all
h
sufficiently large. The inequality (
81
) follows.
∎

.

4.5.
Step 4: Inequalities for eigenvalues of
H_{h}

When estimating the left-hand side of (
5
), we make use of the following inequalities for certain discrete eigenvalues of

.

Proposition 20
.

Suppose
d\geq 2
and let
\nu=\ell+\frac{d}{2}-1
. There exists
h_{0}>0
such that, for all
h\geq h_{0}
,
\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)
, and

(1)

The number

\lambda_{\ell,j}:={\rm i}h+m_{\nu,j}^{2},

is an eigenvalue of

of algebraic multiplicity at least (

(2)

We have the estimates

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda_{\ell,j}\geq\frac{h}{2}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad|\lambda_{\ell,j}|\leq(4\pi j)^{2}.

Proof.

Proof of claim (i):

From the introductory analysis of the discrete spectrum of
H_{h}
made in Section
2
, the claim (i) follows if
m_{\nu,j}
is a solution of the characteristic equation (

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits({\rm i}h+m_{\nu,j}^{2})>0

Proof of claim (ii):
By the above computation,

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{Im}\nolimits\lambda_{\ell,j}=h\left[1+\mathcal{O}{\left(h^{\gamma-1/2}\log h\right)}\right]\!,

Yet another auxiliary inequality will be needed.

Lemma 21
.

Let
d\geq 2
. For all sufficiently large

∎

4.6.
Step 5: Proof of Proposition
8

By Proposition
20
and Lemma
21
, there exists
h_{0}
such that the inequalities from (

) and (

, and

, where

for now, but the notation

Thus, we have the lower bound

\frac{1}{h^{p}}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{h})}\dfrac{(\operatorname{Im}\lambda)^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}\geq C_{p,d}^{\prime}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)}\ell^{d-2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)}\frac{1}{j^{d}}

for all

Next we estimate from below the inner sum from the right-hand side of (

46
), we deduce

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)}\frac{1}{j^{d}}\geq\int_{1+\ell\log^{q}\ell}^{h^{\gamma+1/2}-1}\frac{{\rm d}j}{j^{d}}=\frac{1}{d-1}\left[\frac{1}{(1+\ell\log^{q}\ell)^{d-1}}-\frac{1}{(h^{\gamma+1/2}-1)^{d-1}}\right].

Since
\gamma>\beta>0
the second term decays faster than the first one and therefore we may suppose, without loss of generality, that
h
is large enough so that

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}(h,\ell)}\frac{1}{j^{d}}\geq\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\frac{1}{(\ell\log^{q}\ell)^{d-1}}=\frac{1}{2(d-1)}\frac{1}{\ell^{d-1}\log^{1-\varepsilon}\ell},

where definition (
45
) was used. Plugging the last estimate into (
84
) yields

\frac{1}{h^{p}}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{h})}\dfrac{(\operatorname{Im}\lambda)^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}\geq C_{p,d}^{\prime}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)}\frac{1}{\ell\log^{1-\varepsilon}\ell}

(85)

for all
h
sufficiently large.

Similarly, for all
h
sufficiently large, we may estimate the remaining sum by an integral as follows,

\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)}\frac{1}{\ell\log^{1-\varepsilon}\ell}\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{h^{\alpha+1/2}}^{h^{\beta+1/2}}\frac{{\rm d}\ell}{\ell\log^{1-\varepsilon}\ell}=\left[\left(\beta+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\varepsilon}-\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\varepsilon}\right]\frac{\log^{\varepsilon}h}{2\varepsilon}.

Taking also into account that the function

\varepsilon\mapsto\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\left(\beta+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\varepsilon}-\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{\varepsilon}\right]

is decreasing on
(0,1)
, we further deduce the lower bound with an
\varepsilon
-independent constant,

\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(h)}\frac{1}{\ell\log^{1-\varepsilon}\ell}\geq\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\log^{\varepsilon}h.

When the last estimate is used in (
85
), we arrive at the inequality

\frac{1}{h^{p}}\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{{\rm d}}(H_{h})}\dfrac{(\operatorname{Im}\lambda)^{p}}{|\lambda|^{d/2}}\geq C_{p,d}^{\prime}\,(\beta-\alpha)\log^{\varepsilon}h

for all
h
sufficiently large. The proof of Proposition
8
, and so of Theorem
1
, is complete.
