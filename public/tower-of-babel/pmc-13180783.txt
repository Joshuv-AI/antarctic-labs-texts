Association of Body Roundness Index, Lipid Accumulation Product, and Triglyceride‐Glucose Index With Psoriasis: A Systematic Review of Observational Studies
Authors: Maryam Yousefiasl, Erfan Abadifard, Shaghayegh Khanmohammadi, Mahya Hashemi, Amirhossein Habibzadeh, Zahra Meymanatabadi, Farhad Esmailsorkh, Hooman Momtazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsLipid dysregulation plays a crucial role in psoriasis pathogenesis and its cardiometabolic comorbidities. Novel anthropometric indices such as Body Roundness Index (BRI), Lipid Accumulation Product (LAP), and Triglyceride‐Glucose (TyG) index have been proposed to better reflect underlying metabolic dysfunction and may be associated with psoriasis risk, severity, and related complications. This systematic review evaluates the current evidence on the associations of BRI, LAP, and the TyG index with psoriasis and related clinical outcomes.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted from inception up to September 2025. Observational studies examining the relationship between BRI, LAP, or TyG index and psoriasis or its clinical outcomes were included. Data synthesis comprised qualitative and descriptive statistical analyses.ResultsFifteen studies comprising 65,130 participants from diverse countries met the inclusion criteria. Higher LAP was consistently associated with increased psoriasis risk but showed weak or no correlation with disease severity (PASI). All BRI studies, primarily using US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data, demonstrated a positive association between BRI and psoriasis risk, with modest predictive ability (AUC 0.56–0.58). TyG index was linked to adverse metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes, fatty liver disease, and all‐cause mortality in psoriasis patients, although correlations with PASI were generally non‐significant.ConclusionsBRI, LAP, and TyG index are consistently associated with psoriasis risk and related metabolic and cardiovascular complications, highlighting their potential utility for early risk stratification and holistic management of psoriasis patients. Further prospective studies are warranted to clarify their predictive value for disease severity and long‐term outcomes.Keywords: body roundness index, lipid accumulation product, psoriasis, triglyceride‐glucose index

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune‐mediated inflammatory skin disorder that, as reported by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, affected over 7.5 million individuals in the United States and about 125 million people globally in 2021. Psoriasis imposes a substantial burden on quality of life and healthcare systems [1]. In addition to its dermatological manifestations, psoriasis is increasingly recognized as a systemic disease associated with a cluster of metabolic and cardiovascular comorbidities, including metabolic syndrome (MetS), obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, metabolic‐associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [2, 3]. Shared inflammatory pathways, including elevated tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin (IL)−17, IL‐23, and T helper (Th)1 cytokines, contribute to insulin resistance, atherosclerosis, and lipid abnormalities. Psoriasis also worsens oxidative stress, lifestyle‐related risk factors, and seasonal variations, creating a vicious cycle with MetS. Genetic loci and genes, such as PSORS and CDKAL1, further link susceptibility to both psoriasis and metabolic disorders [2]. Accordingly, early identification and management of metabolic risk in individuals with psoriasis have become a clinical priority.

Traditional anthropometric measures, such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference, are commonly used to estimate obesity‐related risk; however, they have notable limitations in capturing central adiposity and metabolic dysfunction [4]. In recent years, several novel composite indices have been developed to better reflect body fat distribution and metabolic risk, including the body roundness index (BRI), the lipid accumulation product (LAP), and the triglyceride‐glucose (TyG) index [4, 5, 6].

Recent studies suggest that these metabolic indices may also be linked to the risk, severity, and related complications of psoriasis [7, 8]. Elevated BRI, LAP, and TyG values could reflect underlying metabolic disturbances that contribute to psoriasis pathogenesis through chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and immune dysregulation. However, findings across individual studies have been inconsistent, and no comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence has been conducted to date. A better understanding of these associations may improve risk stratification, guide metabolic screening, and inform holistic management strategies in individuals with psoriasis.

Accordingly, this systematic review was performed to synthesize and evaluate the current evidence regarding the associations of BRI, LAP, and the TyG index with psoriasis. Our objectives were to examine their links with psoriasis risk, disease severity, and relevant clinical outcomes, such as cardiovascular events, fatty liver disease, and mortality.

Methods

This systematic review was designed and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A protocol was developed a priori outlining the research objectives, eligibility criteria, data extraction process, and analysis plan.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible studies were original human research articles that examined the association of the LAP, BRI, or TyG index with psoriasis or its related outcomes. Observational study designs, including cross‐sectional, case‐control, and cohort studies, were considered eligible for inclusion. Studies were included if they evaluated the relationship between any of these indices and psoriasis risk, disease severity (e.g., Psoriasis Area and Severity Index [PASI]), or related clinical outcomes. There were no restrictions on sample size or geographical setting. Exclusion criteria included review articles, letters, commentaries, case reports, animal studies, in vitro studies, conference abstracts without full‐text availability, and studies that did not report sufficient quantitative data to allow extraction of effect estimates.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science from their inception to September 2025. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free‐text terms related to the exposures of interest (such as “LAP,” “BRI,” and “TyG”) and the outcome (“psoriasis”) (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). No language or publication year restrictions were applied. Additionally, the reference lists of all eligible articles and relevant review papers were screened to identify further studies.

Study Selection

All retrieved references were imported into the EndNote software, and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (M.Y. and E.A.) independently screened titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then assessed for eligibility against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (S.K.).

Data Extraction

Two reviewers (M.Y. and E.A.) independently extracted data from the included studies using a standardized data extraction form. For each study, information was collected on the first author's name, year of publication, country, and study design. Details of the study population were extracted, including the study population, sample size, female proportion, and mean or median age, along with corresponding standard deviations (SD) or interquartile ranges (IQR) where available. When reported, mean PASI scores and follow‐up durations were recorded. Information on covariates included in the statistical models was also extracted. For each index of interest (LAP, BRI, or TyG), data were collected on the outcomes assessed—such as psoriasis risk, severity, NAFLD, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), carotid atherosclerosis, carotid plaque, or all‐cause mortality—and on the effect estimates reported, including mean differences, odds ratios (ORs), hazard ratios (HRs), correlation coefficients, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values, along with their 95% confidence intervals (CI) or p values. When studies reported multiple models with varying degrees of adjustment, estimates from the most fully adjusted models were extracted. If both continuous and categorical comparisons (e.g., per one‐unit increase and quartiles/tertiles) were provided, data from both analyses were recorded.

Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of the included observational studies was appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort and case‐control studies, and a modified version of the NOS was applied to cross‐sectional studies. Two reviewers (Z.M. and A.H.) conducted the quality assessment independently, and disagreements were resolved through discussion. The risk of bias assessments was considered when interpreting the findings of this review.

Data Synthesis

Due to expected heterogeneity in study design, populations, and outcomes, a meta‐analysis was not performed. Instead, a narrative synthesis of effect estimates was conducted to summarize the findings. Extracted effect estimates were organized by index (LAP, BRI, or TyG) and by outcome category (psoriasis risk, severity, or complications). Reported measures included ORs, HRs, correlation coefficients, and AUC values, each accompanied by their respective CIs. Statistical significance was defined according to the criteria established within the original studies, generally adhering to a two‐sided alpha level of 0.05. No additional statistical software was used beyond that utilized in the included studies.

Results

Study Selection

The initial database search yielded 188 articles (Embase = 57, Web of Science = 42, PubMed = 36, and Scopus = 53). After removing 117 duplicates, 71 unique records remained. Title and abstract screening excluded 46 studies, leaving 25 for full‐text review. Of these, 10 studies were excluded (Supporting Information S1: Table S2) due to irrelevant outcomes, study type, or insufficient data, resulting in 15 studies that met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

PRISMA flowchart.

Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of the studies included in this systematic review, assessed using the NOS, was generally deemed satisfactory and good. In the cohort studies, all three articles achieved a commendable score of 7 or higher. Cross‐sectional studies scored 7–9, indicating a high score due to strong performance in comparability and outcome assessment; however, some studies lost points due to small sample size or an unfavorable non‐response rate. The case‐control studies exhibited moderate quality, with one study achieving a satisfactory score of 7 and another scoring 6, which is typically considered suboptimal for this study design. Overall, the majority of studies across all designs were rated as satisfactory and of high quality, providing a robust foundation for the current systematic review (Supporting Information S1: Table S3).

Study Characteristics

The 15 included studies comprised cross‐sectional (n = 10), case‐control (n = 2), and cohort designs (n = 3), conducted among participants from the USA, China, India, Turkey, and Spain. The total sample size was 65,130, ranging from 40 to 21,242 participants, with the proportion of female participants spanning 11.9%–55.5%. The mean age of participants varied widely, from approximately 32.6–58.9 years. Most population‐based studies were based on the US NHANES data set, while clinical studies mainly included adults with established psoriasis or comorbid conditions such as coronary artery disease (CAD). Among the included studies, four studies investigated LAP, five studies on BRI, and six studies on the TyG index (Table 1).

LAP

Ataseven et al. [9] conducted a case‐control study including 102 psoriatic patients without comorbidities and 101 controls. LAP was higher in cases compared with controls (mean difference = 25.4, p < 0.001). However, LAP showed no evidence of correlation with PASI (r = −0.009, p = 0.93), disease duration (r = 0.188, p = 0.06), or age at diagnosis (r = 0.062, p = 0.53) [9]. Similarly, Ganguly et al. [10] reported higher LAP in moderate‐to‐severe psoriasis compared with mild psoriasis (56.02 ± 30.09 vs. 36.82 ± 20.49; p = 0.03), with an associated increased psoriasis risk (aOR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.03–1.11). Nevertheless, LAP exhibited no evidence of association with PASI (r = 0.258, p = 0.11) or disease duration (r = 0.043, p = 0.79) [10]. Ragul et al. (India) [11] found higher LAP in moderate‐to‐severe psoriasis than in mild psoriasis (230.21 ± 91.1 vs. 151.43 ± 81.56; p = 0.007), although LAP showed only a weak correlation with PASI (r = 0.12) [11]. In a large NHANES‐based study with 9781 participants, [12] demonstrated that LAP was positively associated with psoriasis risk (aOR per 1‐unit increase = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.06–1.52) [12]. Overall, higher LAP levels were associated with higher psoriasis risk; however, there was no consistent evidence of correlation between LAP and PASI (Table 2).

BRI

All the studies assessing the association between BRI and psoriasis were conducted using data from different cycles of the US NHANES. Although the specific NHANES cycles varied, they all involved large nationally representative samples and cross‐sectional designs, which allowed consistent evaluation of the relationship between BRI and psoriasis across diverse populations and time periods (Table 2).

Bai et al. (USA) [13] analyzed 8479 adults from NHANES and found that higher BRI was associated with increased psoriasis risk (aOR per 1‐unit increase = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.05–1.17) [13]. Chen et al. (USA) [14] reported similar findings in 6006 NHANES participants, showing that BRI predicted psoriasis (AUC = 0.565, 95% CI: 0.522–0.607), and that relative fat mass was positively associated with psoriasis risk (OR per 1 unit increase = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.03–1.12) [14]. Liu et al. (USA) [15] included 4873 NHANES participants and found a dose–response relationship between BRI and psoriasis risk (aOR per 1 unit increase = 1.141, 95% CI: 1.034–1.259) [15]. Wu et al. (USA) [16] also showed that higher BRI was associated with higher psoriasis risk (aOR per 1 unit increase = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.04–1.16) and had a modest predictive value (AUC = 0.581, 95% CI: 0.552–0.610), which was significantly higher than ABSI and BMI [16]. Xie et al. (USA) [17] analyzed 21,242 participants and found that the odds of psoriasis increased progressively with rising BRI (aOR per 1‐unit increase = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.04–1.13) [17]. Taken together, all BRI studies consistently showed a positive association between BRI and psoriasis risk, with modest discriminative ability (AUC around 0.56–0.58).

TyG Index

Six studies assessed the association of the TyG index with psoriasis‐related outcomes, including MACE, NAFLD, carotid atherosclerosis, fatty liver disease, and all‐cause mortality. Overall, most of these studies indicated that a higher TyG index is consistently associated with adverse metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes, organ complications, and mortality among individuals with psoriasis (Table 2).

A population‐based cohort study using NHANES data showed that higher TyG was associated with elevated all‐cause mortality (aHR per 1‐unit increase = 1.78, 95% CI: 1.13–2.81) [18].

Domper et al. [19] (Spain) reported that high TyG levels were associated with a higher risk of MACE (aHR Q4 vs. Q1 = 2.38, 95% CI: 0.22–25.46) and had fair predictive performance (AUC = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.60–0.90). There was no evidence of correlation between PASI and TyG index (r = 0.085; p > 0.05) [19]. In contrast, Fu et al. (China) [20] reported that higher TyG was linearly associated with increased MACE risk in patients with psoriasis and CAD (aHR per 1 unit increase = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.03–2.28; AUC = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.53–0.67) [20]. Xie et al. (China) [21] demonstrated that TyG was associated with carotid atherosclerosis (aOR per 1‐unit increase = 2.69, 95% CI: 1.02–7.11) and carotid plaque (aOR = 3.61, 95% CI: 1.15–11.38) in adults with psoriasis [21].

In a study in Spain, researchers found that the TyG index predicted NAFLD in 149 patients with moderate‐to‐severe psoriasis (AUC = 0.688, 95% CI: 0.600–0.775). In contrast, composite indices such as TyG‐BMI achieved higher discriminative accuracy (AUC = 0.852) [22]. Moreover, [23] reported that higher TyG was associated with fatty liver disease in 674 patients with moderate‐to‐severe plaque psoriasis (aOR Q3 vs. Q1 = 2.704, 95% CI: 1.750–4.206; AUC = 0.642) [23].

Discussion

This systematic review, to our knowledge, is the first to comprehensively synthesize the evidence on the associations between BRI, LAP, and the TyG index and psoriasis. Across 15 observational studies involving over 65,000 participants from diverse settings, we found consistent evidence that higher values of these metabolic indices are associated with an increased risk of psoriasis and are observed in patients with moderate‐to‐severe disease. While BRI and LAP showed limited or no correlation with disease severity measured by PASI, the TyG index was linked to adverse clinical outcomes, including major cardiovascular events, carotid atherosclerosis, fatty liver disease, and all‐cause mortality in individuals with psoriasis (Figure 2). Taken together, these composite indices are practical, low‐cost adjuncts that physicians can calculate at baseline and follow‐up to flag systemic risk and prompt standard metabolic and hepatic evaluation and co‐management, while recognizing that these indices are not surrogates for cutaneous severity and should not be used as stand‐alone decision tools. However, the real‐world implementation of these indices may face practical barriers. Calculation of BRI relies on accurate waist circumference measurement, which is not a routinely recorded parameter in dermatology or general practice. Furthermore, TyG requires fasting lab values, which may not be available during routine visits. These discrepancies between research settings and clinical practice underscore the need for standardized measurement protocols and potential integrated health systems.

We evaluated BRI, LAP, and the TyG index because each reflects distinct yet interrelated aspects of metabolic health that may contribute to the pathophysiology of psoriasis. BRI is an anthropometric marker that estimates body fat distribution and central adiposity, which are known to be associated with systemic inflammation [24]. LAP combines waist circumference and triglyceride levels, serving as a surrogate marker of visceral fat accumulation and lipid dysregulation, which are commonly observed in patients with psoriasis [25, 26, 27, 28]. The TyG index, calculated from fasting triglyceride and glucose levels, is a reliable indicator of insulin resistance, a metabolic abnormality frequently linked with psoriasis and its comorbidities [29].

In this study, a high TyG index was associated with MACE and NAFLD in patients with psoriasis. As a surrogate marker of insulin resistance, a high TyG index may reflect underlying hepatic lipid accumulation and systemic metabolic dysregulation, which contribute to the development of NAFLD in this population. Moreover, chronic systemic inflammation in psoriasis synergizes with metabolic abnormalities to accelerate atherogenesis, potentially explaining the strong link between higher TyG levels and increased MACE risk reported in recent cohorts [30, 31]. These findings highlight the potential utility of the TyG index as a simple and cost‐effective tool for identifying psoriatic patients at high risk for cardiometabolic complications, emphasizing the need for early screening and integrated management strategies targeting metabolic health in psoriasis.

Psoriasis is driven by an IL‐23/Th17‐skewed response that extends systemically; IL‐17–mediated inflammation induces oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and vascular injury, linking psoriasis to atherosclerosis. Obesity amplifies this axis—adipose dendritic cells secrete IL‐23 (with IL‐6/TGF‐β), promoting Th17 expansion, with elevated circulating IL‐23/IL‐17 documented in obese individuals. A similar Th17/Treg imbalance is observed in steatotic liver disease, where increased Th17 cytokines are associated with hepatic inflammation and injury [32].

Metabolic dysregulation plays multiple roles in the pathogenesis of psoriasis by altering immune activation, keratinocyte behavior, and systemic inflammation. First, keratinocytes in psoriatic lesions show a glycolytic reprogramming (increased expression of glycolysis‐related genes, proteins, and metabolites), which is further enhanced in patients with metabolic syndrome; inhibition of this glycolysis suppresses psoriasiform features in vitro [33]. Second, lipid metabolism abnormalities, including elevated saturated free fatty acids (FFAs), altered adipokines (e.g., increased leptin, resistin, decreased adiponectin), and dyslipidemia, contribute to activation of Th1/Th17 immune responses, dendritic cell activation, and pro‐inflammatory cytokine release (IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α), thereby exacerbating skin inflammation [34]. Third, insulin resistance (IR) and associated PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway activation are observed in both psoriasis and metabolic syndrome and seem to drive keratinocyte proliferation and inflammation [35]. Finally, broad metabolomic studies in psoriasis confirm dysregulation of amino acids, lipids, carnitines, and carbohydrates, supporting that systemic metabolic disturbances are integral rather than merely comorbid [36].

Therapeutic interventions for psoriasis, particularly biologic agents, can influence the metabolic status and lipid profiles of affected patients, and this relationship has been explored using composite metabolic indices such as the TyG‐related indices. The study by Tamer et al. evaluated changes in the TyG index in 91 patients with psoriasis before and 3 months after initiating biologic therapy. Overall, the TyG index significantly decreased following treatment, but this reduction was observed only in patients receiving TNF‐α inhibitors. Patients with psoriatic arthritis had significantly higher baseline TyG index levels than those without arthritis. These findings suggest that TNF‐α inhibitors may improve metabolic risk in patients with psoriasis, and that the TyG index could serve as a potential biomarker for identifying patients with psoriatic arthritis and an elevated cardiovascular risk [37]. In a prospective cohort study [38], insulin resistance was shown to significantly impair the clinical response to biologics in patients with moderate‐to‐severe plaque psoriasis. Patients with higher TyG‐BMI values, indicative of greater insulin resistance, exhibited lower rates of PASI 75, PASI 90, and PGA 0/1 responses compared with those with lower TyG‐BMI, regardless of the specific biologic used. However, in this study, although a slight reduction in TyG‐BMI was observed after 12 weeks of biologic therapy, this change was small and likely not clinically meaningful, suggesting that the metabolic derangements underlying insulin resistance persist during treatment. Beyond short‐term observations, emerging evidence reveals that these benefits may persist over longer follow‐up periods. For instance, a recent retrospective cohort study demonstrated a significant reduction in the TyG index over a 6‐month period after initiation of biologic therapy, supporting the potential for metabolic improvements [39]. These findings underscore the potential bidirectional relationship between psoriasis and metabolic dysfunction, suggesting that systemic inflammation may impair therapeutic efficacy, while the ability of biologic treatments to reverse metabolic disturbances requires further investigation. Consequently, integrating metabolic assessment tools like the TyG‐related indices into psoriasis management may help identify patients at risk of poor treatment response and guide combined therapeutic strategies aimed at both cutaneous and metabolic improvement.

This review has several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first systematic review examining the association between novel anthropometric indices and psoriasis, as well as its outcomes. We applied a comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases without language or date restrictions. The inclusion of large, population‐based datasets alongside clinical cohorts enhances the generalizability of our findings. Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Most included studies were cross‐sectional, limiting causal inference. Considerable heterogeneity existed in study design, populations, and covariate adjustment, precluding meta‐analysis and introducing potential residual confounding. Moreover, few studies examined longitudinal changes in these indices or their association with treatment response, and some studies used self‐reported psoriasis diagnoses in population‐based data sets, which may be prone to misclassification. All studies investigating the BRI were derived from the same NHANES database, which limits the diversity of study populations and raises concerns about potential overlap between cohorts. This overlap may influence the apparent consistency of findings and limit independence between results. Except for one study, the association of the TyG index with psoriasis was not evaluated. Finally, PASI scores were not consistently reported, restricting our ability to assess their relationship with disease severity robustly.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this systematic review highlights that BRI and LAP are positively associated with psoriasis risk, and the TyG index is associated with adverse metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes. These indices may offer practical tools for identifying high‐risk individuals and guiding metabolic screening and preventive strategies in psoriasis management. Future large‐scale prospective studies are needed to confirm their predictive value, clarify temporal relationships, and evaluate their utility in monitoring treatment response and long‐term outcomes in psoriasis.
