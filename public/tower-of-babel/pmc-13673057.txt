Distinct Clinical Features of Multiple Myeloma With t(11;14): A Post Hoc Analysis of JSH‐MM‐15 Study
Authors: Taku Tsukamoto, Yuji Shimura, Shinsuke Mizutani, Takahiro Fujino, Haruya Okamoto, Mitsuhiro Itagaki, Hiroshi Handa, Takayuki Shimizu, Morio Sawamura, Tadao Ishida, Satoru Kosugi, Shuichi Ota, Makoto Yoshimitsu, Yutaka Shimazu, Isamu Sugiura, Hirohiko Shibayama, Shin‐ichi Fuchida, Hiroyuki Takamatsu, Akiko M Saito, Hitoshi Kiyoi, Koichi Akashi, Itaru Matsumura, Akifumi Takaori‐Kondo, Junya Kuroda, Shinsuke Iida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTt(11;14) (q13;q32) occurs in approximately 15%–25% of multiple myeloma (MM) cases and has traditionally been regarded as a standard cytogenetic risk factor. However, recent evidence suggests that MM with t(11;14) exhibits heterogeneous biological characteristics and clinical outcomes distinct from those associated with other cytogenetic risk categories. In a post hoc analysis of the prospective JSH‐MM‐15 study, we investigated the prognosis of newly diagnosed MM patients to identify prognostic features unique to t(11;14)‐positive cases and to evaluate treatment factors influencing outcomes. Among 482 patients evaluated for t(11;14), 131 (27%) were positive. Overall survival (OS) in t(11;14)‐positive patients was intermediate between standard‐ and high‐risk groups, whereas progression‐free survival (PFS) was comparable to that of high‐risk cases. Deep response rates, including ≥ complete response (CR)/stringent CR and ≥ very good partial response, were lower in t(11;14)‐positive MM than in other risk groups, and these responses were not associated with survival in t(11;14)‐positive cases, in contrast to t(11;14)‐negative MM. Compared with t(11;14)‐negative MM, t(11;14)‐positive patients more frequently expressed CD20 and less frequently expressed CD56 on myeloma cells. Exploratory analyses showed that, despite higher ≥ CR rates, OS was worse in patients with CD20‐negative/CD56‐negative tumors among t(11;14)‐positive MM. Among CD20‐positive patients, PI‐containing regimens were associated with better OS, although no significant PFS benefit was observed. These findings support the biological and clinical heterogeneity of t(11;14)‐positive MM and highlight the need for prospective validation of subgroup‐specific treatment outcomes.Keywords: CD20, CD56, multiple myeloma, proteasome inhibitor, t(11;14)

Introduction

Cytogenetic, genetic, and molecular abnormalities collaboratively and overlappingly contribute significantly to the development and pathogenesis of multiple myeloma (MM). In particular, chromosomal translocations involving the immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGH) genes, such as t(11;14), t(14;16), and t(4;14), are frequently observed at initial diagnosis [1]. These translocations not only likely trigger the onset of the disease but also influence clinical features and the response to various treatments. As a result, they serve as valuable biomarkers for guiding treatment decisions. Among these cytogenetic abnormalities, t(11;14) (q13;q32), which causes cyclin D1 dysregulation, is the most common so‐called “IGH” translocation, found in approximately 15%–25% of MMs., and has traditionally been regarded as a standard risk factor for prognosis [2]. However, recent studies suggest that the prognosis of t(11;14)‐positive MM is worse than that of MM with other standard‐risk chromosomal abnormalities, placing it between standard‐ and high‐risk cytogenetics. Indeed, the survival benefits of new therapeutic agents, such as proteasome inhibitors (PIs), immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs), and disease‐specific monoclonal antibodies, appear limited in t(11;14)‐positive MM [3, 4, 5, 6]. Additionally, t(11;14)‐positive MM differs from t(11;14)‐negative MM in its uncertain relationship between treatment response depth and prognosis, while it shows specific sensitivity to BCL2 inhibitors [5, 7, 8]. Therefore, t(11;14)‐positive MM may represent a distinct subgroup with unique pathophysiological features compared to t(11;14)‐negative MM.

Importantly, t(11;14)‐positive MM has been classified into various subgroups with unique molecular and clinical features by several gene expression profile‐based classifications, such as the UAMS classification and recent IRMMa model [9, 10]. In other words, it is a heterogeneous entity; however, most previous clinical studies have tended to analyze all patients with t(11;14) as a single, uniform subgroup, which may have overlooked this heterogeneity. Consequently, current knowledge of clinical factors influencing prognosis and the effects of initial treatment choices in t(11;14)‐positive MM remains limited. In this study, we conducted a post hoc analysis of the JSH‐MM‐15 study [11], which was a prospective observational study and enrolled 1385 MM patients from January 2016 to December 2018, to examine the clinical features of symptomatic MM patients who tested positive for t(11;14). The goal was to identify prognostic factors that could guide the selection of the optimal treatment strategy for t(11;14)‐positive MM cases.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patients

We used secondary data from patients in the JSH‐MM‐15 study, a non‐interventional, multicenter, prospective cohort study of plasma cell neoplasms in Japan, overseen by the Japanese Society of Hematology (UMIN000022099) [11]. We focused on patients newly diagnosed with myeloma‐related diseases between 2016 and 2018. A comparative analysis was performed by dividing the groups into those with symptomatic MM who were evaluated for t(11;14) by fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) and those who were not assessed. The evaluated group was further split into two groups based on the presence or absence of t(11;14). Additionally, patients lacking t(11;14) were classified as having high‐risk cytogenetics if they had t(4;14), t(14;16), and/or del(17p), while those negative for all three chromosomal abnormalities were considered to have standard‐risk cytogenetics. In addition to this cytogenetic classification, to evaluate the impact of coexisting t(11;14) and del(17p), we performed sensitivity analyses by re‐classifying these double‐positive patients into the high‐risk cytogenetic group and t(11;14)‐positive patients without del(17p) into the modified t(11;14) group, where patients not examined for del(17p) were excluded. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research. While the original JSH‐MM‐15 study obtained approval from the institutional review boards (IRBs) at all participating sites, the protocol for this secondary analysis was reviewed and approved by the IRB of Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine (ERB‐C‐3447). It also obtained approval for the study protocol from the institutional review boards at all involved sites in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects in Japan; the requirement for informed consent was waived, and an opt‐out method was used to provide patients with the opportunity to refuse participation via the institutions and the Japanese Society of Hematology websites.

Statistical Analyses and Endpoints

Overall survival (OS) was measured from the date of symptomatic MM diagnosis until death from any cause. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the start of first treatment to the earliest of disease progression or death. Both OS and PFS were estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method, and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using the Greenwood formula, with distributions between groups compared by the log‐rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses examined the relationship between OS or PFS and significant baseline clinical factors, yielding hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CIs. The overall response rate (ORR) included complete response (CR), very good partial response (VGPR), and partial response (PR), as defined by the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) response criteria [12]. The response data was obtained after the initial induction therapy including autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT). Myeloma defining events including hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency, anemia and bone lesions are defined by IMWG criteria [13]. Flow cytometric analyses of CD20 and CD56 were performed at local laboratories, and positivity was determined according to institutional criteria. The presence of peripheral blood involvement was defined positive when microscopic examination revealed 1% or more abnormal cells. The student's t‐test was used to compare normally distributed continuous variables between two groups. To compare the categorical factors between two groups, we performed Fisher's exact test. For comparison among over three groups, the overall chi‐square test was performed and following post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact tests with Holm's correction when the result of overall chi‐square test was significant. A p‐value of less than 5% was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3.

Results

Characteristics of Patients Underwent t(11;14) Testing

Initially, we examined the features of patients who received t(11;14) testing within our entire cohort before the primary analysis. Among newly diagnosed patients with myeloma‐related diseases enrolled in the JSH‐MM‐15 study (n = 1385), 482 (34.8%) were tested for t(11;14), of whom 131 (27.2%) tested positive, while 903 (65.2%) were not tested. Patients tested for t(11;14) were significantly younger than those not tested (median age: 70 vs. 72; p = 0.003), and a higher percentage of those tested underwent upfront ASCT (30% vs. 24%; p = 0.01) (Table S1). Patients examined for t(11;14) had a lower proportion of Stage III according to the revised (R)‐ International Staging System (ISS) (20% vs. 37%; p < 0.001), and a significantly lower rate of high‐risk cytogenetics (27% vs. 39%; p < 0.001) compared to those not examined for t(11;14). Additionally, the t(11;14)‐examined group showed notably better PFS (3‐year PFS: 47.0% [95% CI, 42.2–51.6] vs. 41.0% [95% CI, 37.5–44.5]; p = 0.009), while there was no difference in OS (3‐year OS: 70.7% [95% CI, 66.1–74.7] vs. 70.5% [95% CI, 67.1–73.6]; p = 0.582) across the entire cohort (Figure S1A,B). Similar findings showed no significant differences in OS and PFS in the ASCT and non‐ASCT groups (Figure S1C–F).

Clinical Characteristics of Patients With t(11;14)

We then examined the clinical features and outcomes of patients with t(11;14). Among 482 patients assessed for t(11;14), 124 were t(11;14)‐positive, 197 were classified as standard‐risk, and 92 as high‐risk. Most clinical characteristics, such as age, sex, (R‐)ISS, presence of amyloidosis, and myeloma‐related symptoms, were similar between patients with and without t(11;14) (Table 1). The percentage of patients with peripheral blood involvement did not differ significantly between t(11;14)‐positive and ‐negative groups (13% vs. 11%; p = 0.52), and the mean absolute peripheral blood plasma cell counts were 139/μL (range: 0–8228) and 95/μL (range: 0–23,081), respectively (p = 0.563). The proportion of CD20‐positive patients was notably higher in the t(11;14)‐positive group compared to the t(11;14)‐negative group (36% vs. 7%; p < 0.001). Conversely, the proportion of CD56‐positive patients was significantly lower in the t(11;14)‐positive group than in the t(11;14)‐negative group (45% vs. 72%; p < 0.001).

Treatment, Survival Outcome, and Treatment Response of Patients With t(11;14)

Regarding first‐line treatment, across the entire cohort of 482 patients tested for t(11;14), approximately 20% received lenalidomide‐based regimens, and approximately half received bortezomib‐based regimens, either alone or in combination with cytotoxic agents. Additionally, approximately 20% of patients received a combination of IMIDs and PIs. Only one patient received a daratumumab‐based regimen for initial treatment. There was no significant difference in the first‐line therapy between the t(11;14)‐positive and t(11;14)‐negative groups (Table S2).

During a median follow‐up of 43.5 months for the entire cohort assessed for t(11;14), the three‐year OS rates were 69.0% (95% CI, 59.7–76.6) for patients with t(11;14), 76.6% (95% CI, 67.7–80.3) for those with standard‐risk, and 63.2% (95% CI, 52.3–72.2) for those with high‐risk cytogenetics (Figure 1A). Therefore, the OS of patients with t(11;14) was not significantly different from that of patients with standard‐risk (p = 0.259) and high‐risk cytogenetics (p = 0.120). In contrast, patients with t(11;14) had worse PFS than those with standard‐risk cytogenetics (p = 0.043), and PFS was similar to that in patients with high‐risk cytogenetics (p = 0.429). The three‐year PFS rates were 40.6% (95% CI, 31.5–49.4) for patients with t(11;14), 54.7% (95% CI, 47.3–61.6) for those with standard‐risk cytogenetics, and 35.2% (95% CI, 25.5–45.1) for high‐risk cytogenetics patients (Figure 1B). We then further examined whether ASCT has a distinct prognostic impact in t(11;14)‐positive MM compared with t(11;14)‐negative patients. In the ASCT group, OS and PFS for t(11;14) did not differ from those of standard‐risk (p = 0.552 for OS, p = 0.527 for PFS) and were better than those of high‐risk cytogenetics (p = 0.004 for OS, p = 0.023 for PFS) (Figure 1C,D). Conversely, in the non‐ASCT group, the OS and PFS of patients with t(11;14) were not different from those of patients with both standard and high‐risk cytogenetics (Figure 1E,F).

Regarding treatment response, patients with t(11;14) showed a ≥ CR rate of 22.6%, which was significantly lower than that of the standard‐risk group (34.0%, OR = 0.567 [95% CI, 0.325–0.971], p = 0.033) and that of the high‐risk cytogenetics group (37.0%, OR = 0.499 [95% CI, 0.262–0.944], p = 0.023) (Figure 2A). Additionally, within the ASCT group, the ≥ CR rate (45.7%) and ≥ PR rate (88.6%) for t(11;14) were lower than those in the standard‐risk cytogenetics group, which had ≥ CR rate at 68.2% (OR = 0.397 [95% CI, 0.155–0.993], p = 0.034) and ≥ PR rate at 98.5% (OR = 0.122 [95% CI, 0.002–1.298], p = 0.048). The ≥ VGPR rate of patients with t(11;14) was also lower compared to the high‐risk group (74.3% vs. 93.5%; OR = 0.204 [95% CI, 0.02–1.114], p = 0.049) (Figure 2B). A similar trend was also observed in the non‐ASCT cohort (Figure 2C). Intriguingly, among patients with t(11;14), there were no significant differences in OS and PFS between those who achieved CR/sCR, and VGPR (OS: p = 0.733 for CR/sCR vs. VGPR; p = 0.049 for CR/sCR vs. PR; p = 0.014 VGPR vs. PR, PFS: p = 0.319 for CR/sCR vs. VGPR; p < 0.001 for CR/sCR vs. PR; p < 0.001 for VGPR vs. PR) (Figure S2A,B). Conversely, in patients without t(11;14), OS and PFS differed significantly by best response after initial therapy. OS was significantly better in patients who achieved CR/sCR than in those with VGPR (p = 0.007) and PR (p < 0.001). In addition, the OS of those with VGPR was significantly better than that of those with PR (p = 0.049) (Figure S2C). Similarly, PFS was significantly better in patients with CR/sCR than those with VGPR (p < 0.001) and PR (p < 0.001). Again, the PFS of those with VGPR was significantly better than that of those with PR (p = 0.019) (Figure S2D).

Because concomitant del(17p) with t(11;14) might influence the outcomes, we performed additional analyses by re‐classifying patients with double‐positive of these chromosomal abnormalities into the high‐risk cytogenetic group and t(11;14)‐positive patients without del(17p) into t(11;14) group, where 27 patients not examined for del(17p) were excluded. The OS of modified t(11;14) group was not significantly different from that of standard‐risk (p = 0.217) and high‐risk cytogenetics (p = 0.208) (Figure S3A). In contrast to the initial classification, PFS was not different between t(11;14) and either standard (p = 0.115) or high‐risk (p = 0.431) cytogenetics (Figure S3B). In the ASCT group, OS and PFS of t(11;14) group did not differ from those of standard‐risk (p = 0.923 for OS, p = 0.884 for PFS) and were better than those of high‐risk cytogenetics (p = 0.007 for OS, p = 0.019 for PFS) (Figure S3C,D). In the non‐ASCT group, the OS and PFS of patients with t(11;14) were not different from those of patients with both standard and high‐risk cytogenetics (Figure S3E,F). Regarding treatment response, similar trend was observed: patients of t(11;14) group showed a lower ≥ CR rate of 21.3% than that of the standard and high‐risk group (34.0%, OR = 0.528 [95% CI, 0.276–0.975], p = 0.037) and that of the high‐risk cytogenetics group (38.8%, OR = 0.429 [95% CI, 0.212–0.849], p = 0.012) (Figure S4A). Although trend of low response rate in t(11;14) group was observed in the ASCT and non‐ASCT group, no significant differences of ≥ CR and PR rate between t(11;14) and standard‐risk and VGPR rate between t(11;14) and high‐risk cytogenetics group was observed in this re‐classification (Figure S4B,C). Although most of findings investigated here were retained, this additional analysis attenuated some of the association observed in the primary analysis, including the differences of PFS between the t(11;14) and standard‐risk groups and the differences in treatment response in the ASCT subgroup. Although del(17p) had an impact on prognosis in t(11;14)‐positive MM in our cohort, we then returned to the primary aim of this study, i.e., consolidating and analyzing the clinical characteristics of the overall population of patients with t(11;14), and analyzed the impact of t(11;14) on prognosis for each type of treatment.

In t(11;14) group of the entire cohort, OS was significantly better in patients treated with IMIDs + PI than those with IMIDs‐only (p = 0.030) and PI‐only (p = 0.026), and PFS was not different within the three treatments (Figure S5A,B). In non‐t(11;14) group, IMIDs + PI treatment tended to be better for OS than IMIDs‐only (p = 0.050) and PI‐only (p = 0.100) and significantly better for PFS (p = 0.018 for PI‐only vs. IMIDs + PI; p = 0.021 for IMIDs‐only vs. IMIDs + PI) (Figure S5C,D). In t(11;14)‐group, the best response rate was not different among treatments, whereas IMIDs and PI regimens demonstrated significantly lower CR, VGPR, and PR rates than IMIDs + PI in non‐t(11;14) group (Figure S5E,F). In the non‐ASCT cohort, regardless of t(11;14) positivity, OS and PFS were not different among three treatments (Figure S6A–D). Whereas the best response of t(11;14) in non‐ASCT cohort was not different among treatments, the response rate of non‐t(11;14) was significantly different, like that of the entire cohort (Figure S6E,F).

Finally, we examined the causes of death in patients with and without t(11;14). Although not statistically significant, patients with t(11;14) had a higher rate of infection‐related death compared to those without t(11;14) (OR = 2.202 [95% CI, 0.879–5.385], p = 0.064) (Table S3). Regarding infection‐related mortality, four of 11 (36%) t(11;14)‐positive patients were BJP‐only type, compared with two of 14 (14%) t(11;14)‐negative patients; though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.064).

While ISS, LDH, β2‐microglobulin, and hypercalcemia were linked to both OS (Figure 3A) and PFS (Figure 3B) in patients without t(11;14), these factors generally exhibited lower hazard ratios for OS (Figure 3C) and PFS (Figure 3D) in patients with t(11;14) compared to those without.

Next, we examined specific factors that could influence prognosis and response rates, taking into account whether t(11;14) was present or absent. These factors included chromosomal abnormalities such as del(17p) and other numerical alterations, as well as tumor cell involvement in peripheral blood. In patients without t(11;14), those with del(17p) experienced shorter OS and PFS compared to those without del(17p) (OS: p = 0.001, PFS: p = 0.065); however, no significant differences were found among patients with t(11;14) (OS: p = 0.915, PFS: p = 0.701) (Figure S7). Additionally, a noticeable or at least increasing trend in the negative impact of del(17p) on OS and PFS was observed in patients without t(11;14), regardless of whether they underwent ASCT; however, this was not significant in patients with t(11;14) in either the ASCT or non‐ASCT groups (Figure S8). No significant impact of additional chromosomal number alterations (ACAs) on OS or PFS was observed in the entire cohort, irrespective of t(11;14) status; however, ACAs tended to be associated with poorer PFS in patients with t(11;14) (p = 0.064) (Figure S9). In the ASCT group, ACAs with and without t(11;14) did not significantly affect OS and PFS (p = 0.563) (Figure S10A–D). In the non‐ASCT group, PFS, but not OS, for patients with t(11;14) was significantly associated with ACAs; however, both OS and PFS for patients without t(11;14) were similar regardless of ACA status (Figure S10E–H). The presence of plasma cells in peripheral blood did not significantly impact either OS (p = 0.796) or PFS (p = 0.693) in patients with t(11;14) (Figure S11A,B). Although the presence of plasma cells in peripheral blood did not have a statistically significant effect on OS (p = 0.238), it was associated with a worse prognosis for PFS (p = 0.002) in patients without t(11;14) (Figure S11C,D). A similar pattern, in which peripheral blood involvement correlated with worse prognosis, was observed in both the ASCT and non‐ASCT groups among patients without t(11;14), but not among those with t(11;14) (Figure S12).

Prognostic Impact of CD20 and CD56 for the Outcome of Patients With t(11;14)

Since the conventional prognostic factors have limited predictive value in patients with t(11;14)‐positive MM, we then evaluated how the expression patterns of CD20 and CD56 might impact prognosis in these cases. Among patients with t(11;14), there were no differences in ORR between CD20‐positive and CD20‐negative groups, regardless of whether they underwent ASCT (Figure S13A–C). Although not statistically significant, ≥ CR rate was lower in CD20‐positive patients with t(11;14). CD20 positivity was not linked to OS (p = 0.563 in the entire cohort, p = 0.511 in the ASCT group, p = 0.955 in the non‐ASCT group) or PFS (p = 0.591 in the whole cohort, p = 0.714 in the ASCT group, p = 0.885 in the non‐ASCT group) in patients with t(11;14), regardless of ASCT status (Figure S13D–I). CD20‐negative cases showed a higher proportion of non‐IgG/IgA and serum M‐protein‐undetected subtype, higher prevalence of hypercalcemia and worse distribution of R‐ISS than those of CD20‐positive (Table S4). Notably, among CD20‐positive patients with t(11;14), treatment regimens that include PI with or without IMIDs demonstrated better treatment response and OS, but not PFS, than treatments using only IMIDs (OS: p = 0.034 for PI vs. IMIDs, p = 0.012 for PI plus IMIDs vs. IMIDs; PFS: p = 0.291 for PI vs. IMIDs, p = 0.11 for PI plus IMIDs vs. IMIDs) (Figure 4A–C). In contrast, these differences were not observed in CD20‐negative patients (OS: p = 0.172 for PI vs. IMIDs, p = 0.983 for PI plus IMIDs vs. IMIDs; PFS: p = 0.428 for PI vs. IMIDs, p = 0.11 for PI plus IMIDs vs. IMIDs) (Figure 4D–F). Neither OS, PFS, nor response rate was significantly worse in CD56‐negative patients with t(11;14) compared to CD56‐positive patients (OS: p = 0.563 in the entire cohort, p = 0.511 in the ASCT group, p = 0.955 in the non‐ASCT group; PFS: p = 0.591 in the whole cohort, p = 0.714 in the ASCT group, p = 0.885 in the non‐ASCT group) (Figure S14).

In t(11;14)‐positive patients, the OS of CD20‐negative/CD56‐negative patients showed a worse trend than that of CD20‐positive/CD56‐positive patients when considering the combined positivity for CD20 and CD56 (p = 0.115) (Figure 5A). However, PFS showed no difference (Figure 5B). Conversely, the ≥ CR rates for CD20‐negative/CD56‐negative cases were higher than those for CD20‐positive/CD56‐positive (Figure 5C). Lastly, we evaluated the clinical characteristics according to CD20/CD56 expression status, and identified a higher proportion of R‐ISS Stage I, with 50% of R‐ISS Stage I and 8% of Stage III, and no cases with hypercalcemia in CD20‐positive/CD56‐positive cases, and a higher prevalence of hypercalcemia in CD20‐negative/CD56‐negative cases (Table S5).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the overall survival risk of t(11;14)‐positive MM in the era of new agents and to identify prognostic factors and the best treatment for this group by conducting post hoc analyses of a prospective cohort study from JSH‐MM‐15. In our cohort, the overall outlook for patients with t(11;14) falls between high‐risk and standard‐risk cytogenetics (Figure 6). This result aligns with previous studies involving cohorts treated with or without new first‐line therapy agents [3, 4, 5, 6]. Although the number of patients categorized by transplant status was limited, this study found that the OS and PFS for t(11;14)‐positive MM in the ASCT group were similar to those of standard‐risk cytogenetics. Conversely, the OS and PFS for t(11;14)‐positive patients in the non‐ASCT group resembled those seen in high‐risk cytogenetics, suggesting that ASCT might provide a benefit for t(11;14)‐positive MM. Nevertheless, we did not collect the data of pre‐ASCT response to assess the efficacy of ASCT, and therefore, these results should be cautiously interpreted.

Graphical abstract of current study. t(11;14)‐positive multiple myeloma is a clinically distinct subgroup characterized by intermediate prognostic risk, modest response depth, and treatment‐dependent outcomes.

The treatment response rate in the t(11;14)‐positive group tended to be lower than in the t(11;14)‐negative group, regardless of upfront ASCT status. This finding aligns with previous reports [5]. Among the t(11;14)‐positive patients, those who reached VGPR and those who achieved CR had nearly identical OS and PFS, suggesting that the depth of response may not significantly influence survival, especially in t(11;14) MM. A recent study of MMs treated with quadruplet and following ASCT revealed that t(11;14) was associated with longer PFS but lower sustained MRD negativity than non‐t(11;14) MM, suggesting that the novel quadruplet treatment also exhibits the discrepancy between response depth and survival outcome in t(11;14)‐positive MM [14]. Since increasing evidence shows that MRD negativity generally correlates with survival in MM, recent clinical trials sometimes use response rates, particularly MRD negativity rates, as endpoints. However, MRD negativity may not be a reliable surrogate marker for prognosis in t(11;14)‐positive MM [15, 16]. Therefore, it is important to carefully interpret the results of MRD‐based outcomes for t(11;14)‐positive MM, and more data are required to better understand the relationship between depth of response and survival outcomes.

Among 97 t(11;14)‐positive patients examined for del(17p), eight (8%) harbored del(17p). A previous study has reported that the coexistence of t(11;14) and del(17p) was associated with a high risk of early death [17]. Therefore, to access the impact of t(11;14)‐ and del(17p)‐positive MM on survival and response, we reclassified these double positives into a high‐risk cytogenetics group. Although there were some limitations, including a small sample size and missing data on del(17p) in 27 patients, the two cytogenetic risk criteria showed that most outcomes were equivalent. Meanwhile, this reclassifying analysis attenuated the significance of PFS between the t(11;14) and standard‐risk groups and the difference in response rate in the ASCT subgroup. These findings support that t(11;14)‐positive MMs include heterogeneous populations.

Because this study has limitations of sample size, it is not appropriate to conclude no significance of concomitant del(17p) and t(11;14) MM. It is still a challenge to reveal whether the significance of clinical characteristics and outcomes observed in overall t(11;14)‐positive MMs are applicable to t(11;14)‐ and del(17p)‐double positive MMs.

Our data showed a relatively high rate of infection‐related mortality and a notable prevalence of BJP‐only MM in the t(11;14)‐positive group. Non‐IgG and non‐IgA types are significantly more common in t(11;14)‐positive MM, as previously reported, and BJP‐only MM often exhibits severe hypogammaglobulinemia, which can lead to infection‐related death [18]. However, due to the small sample size, these findings should be validated in a larger cohort.

The impact of conventional prognostic factors such as ISS stage, LDH, β2‐microglobulin, and hypercalcemia was attenuated in patients with t(11;14). Although these results should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of patients and events, this finding suggests that conventional prognostic factors do not fully capture the biological and clinical heterogeneity of t(11;14)‐positive MM, and we consider that these observations support the need for risk stratification models specifically tailored to t(11;14)‐positive MM.

Previous reports indicated a high prevalence of t(11;14) in primary plasma cell leukemia, and the presence of abnormal peripheral blood cells is generally regarded as a poor prognostic factor for overall MM. However, in our cohort, peripheral tumor involvement was not identified as a poor prognostic factor in t(11;14)‐positive MM [19, 20]. A previous study of primary plasma cell leukemia showed that the t(11;14)‐positive group had a better prognosis than other cases, with distinctive gene expression profiles. Overall, this and earlier findings emphasize the unique clinical and biological significance of peripheral blood plasma cell involvement in t(11;14) compared to non‐t(11;14) MM [20].

A high CD20 positivity rate is a key feature of t(11;14)‐positive MM [21]. In our cohort of t(11;14)‐positive MM, although patients who were CD20‐positive, especially those also positive for CD56, had low response rates, their prognosis was comparable to that of others. In contrast, CD20‐negative and CD56‐negative patients had higher response rates but generally faced a poorer prognosis. When considering background of these survival differences, we identified a high proportion of lower risk of R‐ISS in CD20‐positive/CD56‐positive cases than the others and higher prevalence of hypercalcemia in CD20‐negative/CD56‐negative cases, whereas the enrichment of other conventional prognostic factors, cytogenetics including del(17p) and ACAs were comparable among four groups. These characteristics might affect the CD20/CD56 positivity‐associated prognosis impact. However, the discordance among OS, PFS, and response rate cannot be fully explained by these risk factors and we also consider the possibility of coexisting factors uncollected in this study including 1q gain. The prognostic impact of CD20 expression has been controversial in t(11;14)‐positive MM. For instance, Jian and colleagues observed that CD20‐positive MM with t(11;14) is associated with longer PFS, although the treatment details are lacking [22]. Another study revealed that among patients with t(11;14), those who were CD20‐positive or CD20‐negative had similar survival rates with thalidomide‐based treatments. However, when treated with bortezomib‐containing regimens, CD20‐positive patients had better outcomes than CD20‐negative patients [23]. While these results, including ours, suggest the importance of PI‐containing regimens for CD20‐positive MM with t(11;14), the role of PI in the era of anti‐CD38 monoclonal antibodies remains uncertain. CD20‐negative patients showed a higher proportion of non‐IgG/IgA or serum M‐protein‐undetected disease, which is a hallmark of t(11;14) MM, than CD20‐positive patients. Although it is possible that these differentiation‐related phenotypes affected the sensitivity to PIs, we could not conclude this finding because we did not obtain the data of neither cellular morphology such as CD20‐associated small mature type or immature plasmablast type nor gene expression profiling. To clarify the significance of PI in CD20‐positive MM with t(11;14), both basic and clinical rationale need to be thoroughly investigated.

It is important to take careful interpretation when considering the impact of CD20 and CD56 positivity. Although a threshold of 20% of plasma cells has been used as an operational definition in some previous flow cytometric studies of MM, no universally validated cut‐off for CD20 or CD56 expression has been established [22, 24]. In this multicenter observational study, CD20 and CD56 positivity was determined according to the criteria of each institution. Therefore, the associations between CD20/CD56 expression and clinical outcomes observed in this study should be regarded as exploratory. Prospective validation using standardized flow cytometric methods and predefined quantitative criteria will be required when these markers can be incorporated into clinical risk stratification.

Several studies further classify t(11;14)‐positive MMs based on gene expression profiles and genetic features. The GEP‐based UAMS system differentiates between CD‐1 and CD‐2 subgroups, with CD‐2 characterized by the expression of pre‐plasma B‐cell genes such as CD20 and CD79A. Conversely, using additional genetic data, the recent IRMMa model classifies t(11;14)‐positive MM into CCND1_simple and CCND1_complex, showing that the latter can be distinguished by an enriched copy number variant signature and is associated with shorter survival than CCND1_simple. At the same time, it demonstrated that most CD‐2 and CCND1_simple cases overlap. However, although ACAs with t(11;14) have been identified as a risk factor for patients treated with ASCT in previous studies [25, 26], we found that the prognostic impact of ACAs on PFS was only evident in the non‐ASCT group, not in the ASCT group, within our cohort. Possibly, a small number of patients and events in the ASCT group limited our ability to observe the prognostic impact of ACAs. However, because B cell‐associated markers such as CD19, CD79A, morphology, and gene‐expression data were not collected in this study, we could not determine whether this subgroup corresponds to the canonical B‐cell‐like phenotype, and the results should be interpreted cautiously. It is expected that personalized treatment strategies based on the molecular features of t(11;14)‐positive MM will become more common in routine clinical practice.

Our current study is a post hoc analysis of a prospective study and reflects real‐world clinical outcomes. However, the cohort did not include patients who received anti‐CD38 monoclonal antibodies as initial therapy. Recently, positive results have been reported for triple and quadruple combination therapies that include anti‐CD38 monoclonal antibodies, and these new regimens are quickly becoming standard practice in clinics [27, 28, 29]. To date, there has been limited real‐world data on whether regimens including anti‐CD38 monoclonal antibodies improve outcomes in t(11;14)‐positive MM as first‐line therapy. Moreover, we observed a modest advantage of IMIDs + PI regimens on survival outcomes in t(11;14)‐positive MMs, and it still remains unsolved the benefit of quadruplet rather than triplet consisting of anti‐CD38 antibodies for t(11;14)‐positive MMs. The ongoing JSH‐MM‐20 study is evaluating clinical outcomes during a period when anti‐CD38 monoclonal antibodies have become available for initial remission induction. Future research could benefit from treating the current JSH‐MM‐15 study as historical data, including for JSH‐MM‐20, to monitor changes in treatment results.

This study has several limitations. One is the bias in clinical characteristics: the t(11;14)‐examined group and the unexamined group differed in prognosis and clinical background. The examined group had fewer cases with high‐risk chromosomal abnormalities and generally showed better PFS. Limited by Japanese insurance policies on simultaneous FISH testing, which restricted one test in a month, testing of high‐risk chromosomes was likely prioritized in daily practice; therefore, t(11;14) testing was probably done mainly in cases negative for those high‐risk abnormalities. Another limitation is the small sample size, especially in the ASCT group, which had few OS and PFS events, reducing the power of prognostic factor analysis. Due to post hoc analysis of real‐world data, some critical clinical and biological data are lacking, including chromosome 1q gain/amplification, t(11;14)‐ and CD20/CD56‐related phenotype of tumor morphology and immunophenotype. The definition of CD20 and CD56 positivity is also lacking. The ongoing JSH‐MM‐20 study is collecting cytogenetic data including 1q gain, and this study might reveal the significance of 1q gain on t(11;14) MMs.

Overall, our findings emphasize that t(11;14)‐positive MM is a biologically and clinically distinct entity, underscoring the importance of tailored risk stratification and treatment strategy evaluation for this subgroup.
