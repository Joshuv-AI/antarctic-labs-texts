Standardized processing of explanted hearts after stereotactic arrhythmia radiotherapy (Bio-STAR): a STOPSTORM framework and first human findings

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsStereotactic arrhythmia radiotherapy (STAR) is an emerging non-invasive option for refractory ventricular tachycardia (VT); yet, the underlying myocardial effects in humans remain poorly understood. Within the STOPSTORM consortium, we developed the Bio-STAR framework for standardized ex vivo assessment of STAR-treated myocardium and here report its feasibility and initial findings in human samples.Methods and resultsBio-STAR standardizes myocardial sampling into non-irradiated control (NFNI), minimally fibrotic irradiated, and fibrotic irradiated zones, guided by visual inspection, electroanatomical maps, and radiotherapy dose overlays. Recommended analyses span ex vivo MRI, histology, immunohistochemistry/-fluorescence, molecular panels, and live-cell assays. Application to two explanted non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy hearts (transplantation 3–5 months post-STAR) confirmed the protocol’s applicability to end-stage remodelling. In exploratory analyses of irradiated regions, patient-specific patterns emerged, including stress marker upregulation, and altered NaV1.5, SERCA2a, and CaV1.2 when normalized to cardiomyocyte content. Functional analyses in a limited number of viable cardiomyocytes from the respective regions demonstrated heterogeneous excitability, supporting the feasibility of isolating live cells from STAR-treated regions in which Ca2+ signalling can be quantitatively assessed in future studies involving larger patient cohorts.ConclusionBio-STAR provides a reproducible framework for multimodal analysis of STAR-treated myocardium, enabling harmonized cross-centre research. Early human data demonstrate that cardiomyocyte viability and isolatability are preserved across all assessed regions and that STAR-exposed areas may exhibit region-specific structural features, while functional data on Ca2+ handling remain exploratory and require validation in larger datasets. Broad adoption will be the key to delineating dose–time–substrate relationships and disentangling radiation effects from underlying cardiomyopathy.Keywords: Stereotactic arrhythmia radiotherapy, Ventricular tachycardia, Myocardial remodelling, Explanted human heart, Calcium handling, STOPSTORM

Introduction

Stereotactic arrhythmia radiotherapy (STAR) has emerged as a non-invasive therapy for refractory ventricular tachycardia (VT) in advanced heart failure. Initial studies demonstrated marked reductions in VT episodes with acceptable safety in patients who had exhausted conventional therapies.1–3

The STOPSTORM registry is systematically evaluating long-term safety and efficacy in a large European cohort.4,5 Despite encouraging clinical data, the biological mechanisms of STAR remain incompletely understood. Early work suggested that a high single-dose irradiation was associated with scar creation, but several groups observed VT suppression within weeks, well before mature fibrotic lesions would be expected.6,7 Zhang et al. showed that 25 Gy in human myocardium did not induce transmural scarring within the therapeutic timeframe, but instead resulted in electrical ‘reprogramming’ with increased NaV1.5 and Connexin-43 (Cx43) expression, associated with faster conduction.8,9 Kiani et al.10 reported cardiomyocyte injury and death with only limited fibrosis in explanted human hearts months after STAR. In line with this notion, Zenger et al. described altered effective refractory periods after STAR, whereas Kautzner et al.12 observed fibrotic lesion formation 6–9 months post-radiotherapy. Recent data from Hirao et al.13 showing marked apoptosis and fibrosis upon 40 Gy proton beam further underscore the variability of results likely related to the use of different technologies, dose, and timing.

Therefore, several questions remain unclear: Does STAR act primarily through scar formation, electrophysiological remodelling, or additional processes such as autonomic or microvascular modulation, or a combination of these? How do underlying conditions (ischaemic vs. non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy, fibrosis burden, inflammation) shape the myocardial response? And moreover, what is the timeline of remodelling, from potential early DNA damage, inflammation, to altered conduction and/or fibrosis? Addressing these questions requires systematic and reproducible analyses of human STAR-treated myocardium. Patients undergoing heart transplantation (HTx) or ventricular assist device implantation (left ventricular apical core)14 after STAR provide a unique opportunity to correlate dose, substrate, and time course with direct tissue responses. To enable meaningful cross-patient and cross-centre comparisons, we developed the Bio-STAR framework protocol for standardized processing of STAR-treated myocardium (Bio-STAR) within the STOPSTORM consortium.

Preclinical Foundations of Radiation-Induced Myocardial Remodelling Preclinical studies of STAR have largely focused on dosing, safety, targeting accuracy, and myocardial responses in animal models. Among 22 published studies (Table 1), pigs (n = 16) were the predominant model due to their anatomical and electrophysiological similarity to humans, followed by dogs (n = 3) and rabbits (n = 4). Targets included the atrioventricular node, atria, pulmonary veins, and the left ventricle, with treatment sites reflecting both arrhythmia substrates and feasibility testing. Most studies employed photon beams (n = 10), while others investigated heavy ion (n = 7) or proton therapy (n = 5), at doses ranging from 15 to 160 Gy. Higher doses were generally required for atrial or AV nodal ablation effects, whereas lower to moderate doses modulated ventricular conduction or arrhythmia inducibility. Histological analyses were central in 17 studies, typically using haematoxylin-eosin (HE) for general morphology and trichrome stains (Masson or Mallory) for collagen and fibrosis. Additional stains included triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) to assess tissue viability, Movat pentachrome,22 periodic acid-Schiff,21,26 or azocarmine–aniline blue,29,31 though these were used in only a minority of reports.19,20 Molecular assessments were more limited. Three porcine studies performed immunohistochemistry for markers such as γH2AX (DNA damage), caspase-3 (apoptosis), and Cx43, while a handful included western blotting for apoptotic markers or Cx43.18,24,27 Notably, none of the porcine studies incorporated transcriptional profiling, leaving a gap in large-animal molecular characterization. In contrast, all four rabbit studies combined histology with immunohistochemistry, western blotting, or qPCR, focusing particularly on Cx43 signalling and remodelling.24,27,31–33 While small-animal studies yielded mechanistic insights, particularly regarding gap junction modulation, their limited scale constrains clinical translatability. Conversely, the relative lack of molecular and transcriptional analyses in pig studies, the most clinically relevant model, represents a key limitation in bridging preclinical to human data.

Histopathological characterization of human myocardium after STAR

In the early phase (days to weeks), acute cellular injury is accompanied by infiltration of macrophages, T lymphocytes, and occasional B lymphocytes. The affected myocardium demonstrates myolysis, cytoplasmic vacuolization, disruption of intercalated discs, and intracellular and interstitial oedema.36 Ultrastructural analyses reveal loss of contractile elements, irregularities of intercalated discs, and mitochondrial swelling.10 Across reported observation periods of up to 702 days, apoptotic activity has not been consistently detected, and within the first 12 months, no significant occlusion of small intramyocardial vessels has been observed.6,37

Over subsequent months, irradiated regions typically evolved from an inflammatory phenotype towards progressive fibrosis, characterized by dense collagen deposition and variable adipose tissue infiltration. Concomitant vascular alterations, including myointimal thickening and endothelial cell prominence, may also occur.38 In otherwise healthy myocardium, radiation exposure is primarily associated with diffuse extracellular matrix remodelling and low-grade fibrosis in a dose- and time-dependent manner. In contrast, the formation of large, dense scars is uncommon within the first 3 months following therapy.39

Together, these findings demonstrate that STAR induces heterogeneous myocardial effects depending on species, dose, target, and follow-up duration. They also underscore the need for systematic evaluation in human myocardium. By mandating zonal sampling and integrating histology, protein/gene analyses, and optional functional assays, the Bio-STAR protocol addresses these translational gaps and provides a reproducible framework for cross-study and cross-centre comparison.

Methods

Protocol development

To address the outlined knowledge gaps, we developed a standardized protocol for processing STAR-treated myocardium obtained from patients undergoing transplantation or LVAD implantation.40 The Bio-STAR framework protocol was developed through iterative discussions and survey rounds within the STOPSTORM consortium, involving experts in electrophysiology, radiation, pathology, imaging, and translational science. Draft recommendations were circulated among participating centres and refined based on structured feedback until consensus was reached. The framework protocol aims to ensure reproducible, multimodal tissue analysis and enable meaningful cross-centre comparisons (Table 2). A central element of Bio-STAR is standardized data collection and harmonization across centres, which is outlined in the following section.

Data standardization

The standardized Bio-STAR approach facilitates reliable comparisons within individual cases. However, meaningful aggregation of data across centres requires structured coordination. To support this effort, we have prepared a comprehensive spreadsheet (see Supplementary Data) and offer storage of biological samples and histological material in a dedicated biobank. Access to these resources is available through the authors upon reasonable request, subject to evaluation of a research proposal by a multidisciplinary review committee. Allocation will be prioritized based on scientific merit, clinical relevance, and efficient resource utilization.

Furthermore, the STOPSTORM consortium aims to establish a centralized repository for pseudonymized patient data, imaging data, quantitative measurements, STAR dose distributions, and patient-derived cellular resources (e.g. induced pluripotent stem cells or cardiac organoids for in vitro assessment of radiosensitivity), operating under an open and transparent governance framework. Such multidimensional datasets have the potential to link clinical variables with pathological and molecular readouts. The protocol and its associated guidelines are intended to evolve as additional cases are analysed. This includes refining or discontinuing assays of limited utility and strengthening recommendations through continued expert input and collaborative evaluation. Ultimately, the Bio-STAR framework aims to standardize the evaluation of STAR-treated human myocardium and to encourage systematic data sharing.

Ex vivo MRI imaging protocol

Ex vivo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a valuable tool for assessing STAR-treated myocardium, aiming to differentiate between irradiated and non-irradiated regions and to guide histological and molecular analyses.

However, heart preparation for ex vivo MRI can preclude further tissue processing and should ideally be performed on separate or pre-sampled specimens or limited to rapid orientation scans to avoid compromising fresh tissue analyses. A coordinated sampling strategy should therefore be defined in advance to balance imaging quality and downstream assay integrity (see Supplement for expanded section).

Imaging might serve as a spatial reference for subsequent sampling and analysis and can be performed on clinical 1.5T or 3T systems41,42 or high-field preclinical systems (≥7T) with diffusion-MRI.43–45 The absence of motion permits isotropic acquisitions at sub-millimetre resolution, but fixation alters relaxation properties and requires careful interpretation.

High-field diffusion MRI, while less accessible, enables high resolution through long acquisitions, but necessitates dedicated protocols and specimen preparation.41,42 Formalin fixation shortens T2 and reduces signal-to-noise. Since gadolinium-based contrast cannot be applied ex vivo, tissue characterization relies on intrinsic T1, T2, and T2* changes, and interpretation must be supported by histology. Immersion in Formalin minimizes susceptibility artefacts at tissue boundaries.44,46 On clinical scanners, standard cardiac sequences can be adapted to include multiplanar localizers and balanced SSFP for morphology, chamber volumes, wall thickness, and fat.47,48

Spin echo T1 mapping and T1-weighted FSE are suitable for formalin-fixed samples, while T2 mapping, T2-weighted turbo spin echo, and STIR assess oedema or tissue composition. Multiecho GRE (≥6 echoes) supports T2* mapping of iron, haemorrhage, or microvascular injury. Diffusion-weighted imaging provides microstructural detail but requires signal averaging due to reduced diffusivity after fixation.49 Ex vivo MRI has improved diagnostic accuracy in post-mortem evaluation of sudden cardiac death.50,51 Within Bio-STAR, it should be considered when precise co-localization with electroanatomical mapping is needed to distinguish STAR effects from prior ablation or underlying pathology. However, while ex vivo MRI offers unparalleled structural insight into STAR-treated myocardium, the extended preparation and workflow it requires might preclude subsequent analyses of viable cells or labile molecular targets.

Exploratory framework application

To test the feasibility of the proposed framework, we analysed two initial explanted hearts. Given the limited number of patients available for this exploratory analysis, the primary aim was to assess the framework’s applicability and robustness rather than to draw definitive statistical conclusions. Myocardial samples were processed according to the predefined histological workflow. Statistical analyses were primarily descriptive, focusing on characterizing regional and interpatient variability. To formally evaluate regional differences while accounting for the hierarchical structure of the data, linear mixed-effects models were applied with region as a fixed effect and patient as a random intercept. Due to the small number of patients (n = 2), variance estimates for the random effect approached zero, and results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

Tissue processing protocol

Pragmatic implementation and preparatory considerations

While the Bio-STAR framework outlines a comprehensive analytical approach, not all centres may have access to the full range of techniques or expertise. Investigators are therefore encouraged to implement those components that are feasible within their local infrastructure and, if needed, share samples across institutes to close analytical gaps. Prior review of available resources and coordination between clinical, pathology, and laboratory teams can help ensure efficient tissue processing and analysis. Importantly, partial implementation should not preclude meaningful investigation, as even limited but standardized analyses can yield valuable data. Prior to tissue acquisition, ethics committee approval and comprehensive informed consent from the patient or their next of kin must be ensured. The protocol should not introduce any risks beyond those associated with standard clinical care. A detailed dissection strategy should be established in advance, covering both irradiated target regions and at least one non-irradiated myocardial control area. Identification of target regions should be guided by electroanatomical mapping (EAM), imaging data, and STAR treatment details, ideally reviewed by an electrophysiologist (preferably the treating physician) to translate treatment targets into anatomical landmarks on the explanted heart. Finally, a multidisciplinary team—including a cardiac surgeon, pathologist, and/or a trained scientist or technician—should be available at the time of tissue acquisition to ensure immediate, coordinated dissection and processing of the explanted heart.

Figure 1 illustrates how EAM (A) and CT data (B, C) were used to plan STAR (D, E) in a patient to identify three target regions for processing, based on the voltage EAM map: (1) a minimally fibrotic, irradiated (MIFI) region at the basal anterolateral left ventricle (LV) near the mitral annulus; (2) a fibrotic, irradiated (FIRI) region at the inferoseptal basal LV near the mitral annulus; and (3) a non- or minimally fibrotic, non-irradiated (NFNI) region at the LV apex, serving as a control. However, it should be noted that the NFNI region should be selected in a representative manner, with its gross appearance as close as possible to that of the MIFI region. For this purpose, tools for automated semiquantitative fibrosis quantification may be employed, and the selection of control samples with a comparable amount of underlying substrate from the non-irradiated region can be performed post hoc. Pathological changes may vary between or even within an irradiated zone, due to (pre-)existing scar or fat. The dose distribution is not perfectly uniform throughout the target volume; beam overlap creates ‘hot spots’ with higher dose (usually on the arrhythmia origin). Thus, the lesion core might exhibit profound changes (e.g. central necrosis with surrounding fibrosis), as seen in animal models.24 Areas receiving <5 Gy are considered unirradiated, those with >20 Gy are functionally irradiated, and >30 Gy are pathologically irradiated. Therefore, we recommend a systematic multizone sampling to maximize diagnostic yield while ensuring tissue integrity, standardization, and reproducibility. This could ensure meaningful comparisons across cases, quality control, and reliable diagnostic interpretation.

A well-defined dissection plan is essential because cardiac tissue degrades rapidly after removal and requires immediate processing. The plan should consider anatomical orientation, scar distribution, and analysis requirements. Sample each STAR-targeted area to capture the core lesion and the periphery to assess transition zones and include nearby normal tissue. Non-fibrotic samples are more likely to yield sufficient amounts of viable myocytes for in vitro experiments and, even though a potential source of bias, should be processed first, placed in an appropriate cold buffer solution at 4°C, and transported on wet ice to a lab capable of cell isolation and functional testing within 24 h. Other tissue samples can be processed and stored at −80°C for later analyses. Fat can interfere with molecular assays, and thus, relevant tissue samples should have excess fat trimmed away. We recommend comprehensive sampling and labelling of each target area (Figure 2), with all tools, solutions, and vials prepared earlier the same day to ensure thorough documentation, efficient processing, and high diagnostic yield.

Immediate processing

After explantation, the heart should be flushed to remove residual blood and cooled in preservation solution (e.g. 0.9% saline or phosphate-buffered saline). Handling should avoid cardioplegic solutions when possible, as they may alter downstream analyses.52 Tissue sampling should commence as quickly as logistics allow maintaining morphology, protein stability, and cell viability.

Gross examination

Gross inspection should begin with systematic photographic documentation of the intact heart from multiple orientations. Areas of fibrosis, fat infiltration, and calcification should be carefully noted. Representative blocks should be obtained from three predefined zones: non-fibrotic, non-irradiated control myocardium (NFNI), MIFI myocardium, and FIRI myocardium. Sampling is guided by STAR dose overlays, electroanatomical maps when available, and the gross appearance of the myocardium.

For histological analysis, tissue from epicardial fat, fibrotic areas, and calcifications should be retained to capture the full pathological context. For molecular assays, samples should be trimmed free of fat to avoid confounding protein and RNA analyses. Each block should be photographed in situ before removal, labelled with zone and orientation, and entered into a standardized block map to ensure reproducibility and enable later correlation with imaging or electroanatomical data.

Gap junctions and ion channels

Cx43 (the main ventricular gap junction protein), NaV1.5 (the cardiac sodium channel), and CaV1.2 (the α1c subunit of the L-type calcium channel). These are critical for electrical propagation. Studies suggest that STAR may alter Cx43 and NaV1.5 expression, enhance conduction, and potentially disrupt re-entry.7 We recommend both quantitative and spatial assessment of Cx43 to evaluate whether Cx43 is correctly concentrated at intercalated discs or abnormally lateralized along myocyte sidewalls (arrhythmogenic feature in HF). Changes in Cx43 density or distribution in the irradiated area would provide evidence of STAR-induced electrical remodelling. Similarly, differences in NaV1.5 expression between irradiated and control areas may indicate STAR effects. Such IF mapping can indicate changes in conduction and could be supplemented by ex vivo optical mapping or multi-electrode arrays mapping, if possible.

Calcium handling proteins

L-type calcium channels (CaV 1.2), sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA2a), ryanodine receptor 2 (RyR2), phospholamban (PLN), and the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCX1). SERCA2a and RyR2 are central to intracellular Ca2+ cycling, and their expression or localization might be altered by HF and possibly by radiation. For example, reduced SERCA2a levels or radiation-induced changes in RyR2 (e.g. altered phosphorylation) could impair calcium reuptake and release, contributing to contractile dysfunction or arrhythmias. IF can visualize these proteins and detect gross changes, such as loss of RyR2’s normal striated pattern, if transverse tubules are disrupted. Combined with functional assays, these molecular insights help assess the excitation–contraction coupling in STAR-treated myocardium.

Finally, additional markers can be included to evaluate hypertrophy (e.g. β-myosin heavy chain for myocyte hypertrophy/foetal gene expression), fibrosis (e.g. collagens I and III for extracellular matrix deposition), or cellular stress and DNA damage responses (e.g. γ-H2AX for DNA double-strand breaks, p53 or cleaved caspase-3 for apoptosis). Using a panel of antibodies provides a broader view of structural and electrical responses to STAR.

Functional cell assays

When feasible, STAR’s effects on cardiomyocyte function can be evaluated using isolated cell studies. This requires careful handling of viable myocardial tissue during explant. Small samples (50–500 mg) from irradiated and control regions should be placed in cold cardioprotective buffer and rapidly transported for isolation, kept on wet ice. Viable cardiomyocytes can be obtained by enzymatic digestion (e.g. coronary perfusion or minced-tissue ‘chunk’ digestion with collagenase).56

Isolated cells allow direct assessment of excitation–contraction coupling by microscopy-based assays. Sarcomere shortening (contractility) and intracellular Ca2+ transients can be recorded during electrical field stimulation. For Ca2+ measurements, cells are typically loaded with Fura2 a.m. or Fluo-4 a.m. and stimulated at 0.5, 1, 3, and 5 Hz. The Fura-2 340/380 nm fluorescence ratio provides quantitative Ca2+ transient amplitude and kinetics, while simultaneous sarcomere length tracking yields contraction amplitude and velocity.

Comparing these parameters between irradiated and non-irradiated regions enables identification of radiation-induced functional changes. Reduced Ca2+ transient amplitude or slower decay would suggest impaired sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) Ca2+ reuptake, potentially reflecting SERCA2a downregulation or RyR2 dysfunction. Conversely, increased amplitude or accelerated kinetics would indicate improved Ca2+ cycling.

Mitochondrial density and function

Mitochondrial content and performance provide key insights into cellular viability and energy metabolism in STAR-treated myocardium. To assess these, cardiomyocytes isolated from defined zones (NFNI, MIFI, FIRI) should be stained with mitochondrial-specific dyes (e.g. MitoTracker) or immunolabeled against established markers (e.g. TOM20, COX IV). Confocal microscopy allows quantification of mitochondrial area fraction relative to cytosolic volume, providing a density index.

Functional evaluation can be performed by loading viable cells with potential-sensitive fluorophores (e.g. TMRE, JC-1) or metabolic probes (e.g. Seahorse-based assays in parallel cultures when feasible). Quantitative analysis might include the following: (i) mean fluorescence intensity as a surrogate of membrane potential, (ii) heterogeneity across regions of interest, and (iii) stability during standardized pacing or calcium cycling protocols.57

Application of Bio-STAR in two patients

This work was carried out under the scope of the ethical approvals Kiel D406/22, BASEC 202101730, and Leipzig 222/25-ek. See Supplement for detailed methods and statistics.

Patient 1

The first application of the Bio-STAR protocol involved a 37-year-old man with non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy (NICM), a history of sudden cardiac arrest in 2015, and prior herpes simplex virus (HSV) myocarditis confirmed by cardiac MRI and myocardial biopsy. He received an ICD and was initially treated with amiodarone, which led to hyperthyroidism and later with sotalol and mexiletine. Despite medical therapy, he underwent five ablation procedures for refractory ventricular tachycardia (VT): one with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support, one complicated by a steam-pop injury, and three combining endocardial and epicardial approaches, including one via surgical access. The electroanatomical substrate involved the interventricular septum, the anterolateral LV wall, and perimitral regions, while cardiac MRI demonstrated epicardial scarring in the basal lateral and inferior LV walls. In 2023, he was listed for heart transplantation (HTx) due to recurrent electrical storm and progressive heart failure. An additional electrophysiology study was performed to map VT circuits and guide STAR targeting (Figure 1). The procedure was well tolerated, but 3 months later, he developed influenza, was re-admitted with recurrent VT/electrical storm, and showed a reduced LV ejection fraction (38%). He subsequently underwent high-urgent HTx 5 months post-STAR. The explanted heart was processed according to the Bio-STAR protocol (Figure 2), with sampling from three zones: (1) minimally fibrotic irradiated (MIFI) tissue at the basal anterolateral LV, (2) fibrotic irradiated (FIRI) tissue at the basal inferoseptal LV, and (3) non-irradiated control (NFNI) tissue at the LV apex.

Patient 2

The second application of Bio-STAR involved a 58-year-old man with NICM who experienced sudden cardiac arrest in 2016 and subsequently received CRT-D implantation for left bundle branch block, with improvement in LV ejection fraction from 20% to 38%. He developed recurrent VT refractory to multiple antiarrhythmic drugs, including amiodarone (complicated by hyperthyroidism), phenytoin, and lidocaine, and underwent three ablation procedures targeting the basal septal substrate. Despite a left-sided sympathectomy (April 2025) and STAR (May 2025) targeting the septum, recurrent VT episodes led to high-urgent listing and HTx 3 months after STAR. The explanted heart was again processed using the Bio-STAR protocol, with tissue sampling from (1) MIFI at the apical septum, (2) FIRI at the basal septum, and (3) NFNI at the lateral LV as a control. As in the previous case, ex vivo MRI was not performed to prioritize live-cell isolation.

Results histology

Histological analysis revealed end-stage cardiomyopathy with advanced structural remodelling across all sampled zones (Figure 3). Collagen deposition was most pronounced in fibrotic irradiated (FIRI) regions, which displayed dense scar tissue, acellular areas, and extensive fatty infiltration. Minimally fibrotic irradiated (MIFI) regions showed more heterogeneous remodelling with less compact fibrosis. In contrast, non-irradiated control (NFNI) tissue contained variable but overall lower collagen content, still reflecting disease-related remodelling. Epicardial fat and patchy calcifications were present in both hearts, most prominently in FIRI zones.

Among the subset of excitable cells, calcium transient parameters varied across regions, with some cells from irradiated areas showing shorter time to peak, increased amplitudes, and faster decay kinetics compared to NFNI. Given the low number of analysable cells and the selection bias towards viable cardiomyocytes, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and indicative of heterogeneous excitability rather than consistent enhancement of calcium handling.

Mitochondrial density in viable cells

Confocal microscopy of isolated cardiomyocytes stained with MitoTracker revealed significantly higher mitochondrial density in irradiated regions compared with non-irradiated controls (Figure 6A). Both MIFI and FIRI cells displayed increased mitochondrial area fraction relative to NFNI (P < 0.01). Despite these statistically significant differences, interpretation is severely limited by the small number of viable cells available for analysis, underscoring the need for replication in larger sample sets.

Discussion

In this study, we present the first application of the Bio-STAR framework protocol for standardized processing of explanted human hearts following stereotactic arrhythmia radioablation (STAR). Using two patients with non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy who underwent STAR prior to heart transplantation, we demonstrated the feasibility of integrating zonal tissue sampling (NFNI, MIFI, FIRI) with histology, immunofluorescence, protein assays, functional cell studies, and mitochondrial analysis. Together, these multimodal data reveal heterogeneous and partly reproducible changes in irradiated myocardium, supporting the concept that STAR induces structural and functional remodelling.

Histological analyses confirmed advanced cardiomyopathy in both patients. According to the predefined sampling strategy, fibrotic irradiated (FIRI) zones were sampled from macroscopically fibrotic regions, whereas MIFI and NFNI served as comparators. The presence of fibrosis in irradiated zones, therefore, cannot be directly attributed solely to radiation exposure. Moreover, STAR typically targets arrhythmogenic substrates that are frequently characterized by fibrosis. The deliberate sampling strategy highlights the complexity of disentangling radiation-induced from pre-existing structural changes. However, the presented findings are in line with prior reports from explanted STAR-treated hearts, which similarly described marked pre-existing remodelling.10 Preclinical work has shown variable timing and extent of fibrosis, with some studies noting early anti-arrhythmic effects in the absence of mature scar.9,19,31 At the molecular level, both patients showed upregulation of NaV1.5 relative to MLC2v as a cardiomyocyte marker, in irradiated fibrotic zones, while only the highly-remodelled, irradiated fibrotic sample from Patient 1 showed an expression of ANP and MLC2a, indicating pathological stress or reversion to foetal gene expression. Because MLC2v itself is likely reduced in highly fibrotic regions, normalization to MLC2v may amplify differences related to cardiomyocyte loss. These results should therefore be interpreted as enrichment relative to remaining cardiomyocytes rather than absolute upregulation per tissue.

Cx43 expression pattern varied between patients. Patient 1 exhibited increased Cx43 protein expression in irradiated regions with only mild fibrosis, consistent with the gap junction remodelling described by Zhang et al. and by Amino et al. in animal models.9,32 Patient 2 did not display similar Cx43 changes, suggesting inter-individual variability. Importantly, viable cardiomyocytes could be isolated even from fibrotic irradiated zones in Patient 2 that showed wide regions devoid of any type of apparent cellular structures.

Functional cell assays revealed heterogeneous calcium transient properties among the limited number of analysable cardiomyocytes. While some cells from irradiated regions exhibited faster calcium kinetics compared to NFNI, a substantial proportion of cells across all regions showed impaired excitability, with many responding only at low stimulation frequencies.

These findings highlight significant variability in cellular viability and function and contrasts with the well-described phenotype of failing cardiomyocytes.56,58 It suggests that the analysed cells may represent a selected subpopulation of more resilient cardiomyocytes. Therefore, the observed differences in calcium handling should be interpreted as exploratory and not as evidence of enhanced excitation–contraction coupling.

Instead, these data primarily demonstrate the feasibility of isolating viable cardiomyocytes from irradiated human myocardium and performing quantitative functional analyses. Larger studies will be required to determine whether consistent alterations in calcium handling occur following STAR.

Parallel mitochondrial analyses supported the presence of region-specific remodelling,8 with increased mitochondrial density in intact cardiomyocytes from irradiated zones, which is not what has been described previously but likely a testament to the heterogeneous nature of the substrate and the sample selection in different sets of patients.8 Alterations in mitochondria density can influence cardiac function not only because of their role in energy supply but also by their role in calcium storage and release.58 The Bio-STAR protocol proved practical and reproducible, allowing comprehensive tissue interrogation from gross pathology to functional assays. By enforcing standardized zonal sampling and integrating multimodal analyses, Bio-STAR enables harmonized data collection across centres. This is particularly critical given the rarity of explanted STAR-treated hearts and the variability of existing preclinical models.18,22,33

As reflected, the timing of tissue acquisition is an important determinant of the observed phenotype. The 3–5 month interval in this study likely represents a transitional phase between early radiation-induced injury and more advanced fibrotic remodelling. Early stages are characterized by inflammatory infiltration and myocytolysis, whereas later phases are dominated by fibrosis and extracellular matrix remodelling. The coexistence of viable cardiomyocytes with altered functional properties may therefore reflect an intermediate stage rather than a stable endpoint. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted within this temporal context. The limited understanding of time-dependent myocardial changes after STAR further underscores the need for standardized, time-resolved approaches such as this framework protocol.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The number of cases remains small, functional cell yields were limited, and findings may not be generalizable across different substrates. It is important to notice that both hearts analysed in this study were obtained from patients with complex ventricular arrhythmias and advanced cardiomyopathy who ultimately required heart transplantation. In both cases, STAR was applied as a bailout strategy after multiple failed antiarrhythmic therapies and ablation procedures, with the aim of delaying advanced heart failure therapies. Despite treatment, durable VT suppression was not achieved, and both patients progressed to transplantation. Consequently, these explanted hearts may represent partial or non-responders to STAR, and the observed tissue findings may not reflect mechanisms present in patients with successful long-term arrhythmia control. In addition, the natural disease course without STAR cannot be reconstructed retrospectively, limiting causal attribution of the observed changes. Moreover, because irradiated zones were deliberately sampled from fibrotic regions, fibrosis cannot be causally attributed to STAR. Instead, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting the combined effects of radiation and pre-existing arrhythmogenic substrate. Statistical testing of protein expression is exploratory, as the number of biological replicates (two hearts) is small and multiple samples per region are derived from the same individual. Future applications of Bio-STAR to larger case series, including different aetiologies and time intervals post-STAR, will be essential to clarify the relative contributions of structural vs. functional remodelling. The absence of comparator myocardium not treated with STAR limits causal attribution of the observed findings to radiation exposure.

Conclusion

The STOPSTORM consensus framework ‘Bio-STAR’ provides a standardized and reproducible approach for the systematic evaluation of STAR-treated human myocardium. Initial observations from two patients demonstrate that irradiated regions, even when fibrotic, may retain viable cardiomyocytes that can be isolated for functional assessment, with preliminary data suggesting heterogeneous calcium handling properties and mitochondrial organization that require validation in larger studies. These findings suggest that the biological effects of STAR extend beyond mere structural remodelling and might involve molecular and functional changes. By enabling harmonized multimodal tissue analysis across centres, Bio-STAR establishes a foundation for collaborative studies to elucidate mechanisms and optimize the clinical application of cardiac radioablation.

Data availability

Currently, biological samples and histological material are being collected and stored in a dedicated biobank at the Heart Centre Leipzig, which serves as the initial core facility for sample handling. A stepwise transition towards a fully integrated central biorepository is planned as the infrastructure evolves. Access to data and samples requires submission of a project proposal, which is reviewed by a review committee. Allocation of tissue-samples will be prioritized based on scientific merit, clinical relevance, and efficient use of material.
