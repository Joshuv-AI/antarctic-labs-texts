Entropy and determinants for unitary representations
Tim Austin

Abstract
Ergodic theory includes several notions of entropy for probability-preserving actions of countable groups. These include Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy based on Følner sequences for amenable groups, entropy defined using a random ordering of the group, and Bowen’s sofic entropy for sofic groups. In this work we pursue these notions across an analogy between ergodic theory and representation theory. We arrive at new quantities associated to unitary representations of groups and representations of other C*-algebras. Our main results show that these new quantities can often be evaluated as Fuglede–Kadison determinants. The resulting determinantal formulas offer various non-commutative generalizations of Szegő’s limit theorem for Toeplitz determinants. They make contact with Arveson’s theory of subdiagonal subalgebras, and also with some entropy formulas in the ergodic theory of actions by automorphisms of compact Abelian groups.

Introduction

1.1
A Szegő limit theorem over amenable groups

If
\varphi
is a positive definite function on
\mathbf{Z}
, then Bochner’s theorem identifies it as the Fourier–Stieltjes transform of a finite Borel measure
\mu
on the circle group
\mathbf{T}
. Let
m_{\mathbf{T}}
be the Lebesgue probability measure on
\mathbf{T}
. In this context, Szegő’s limit theorem describes the asymptotic behaviour of finite-dimensional Toeplitz determinants obtained from
\varphi
:

Szegő’s theorem
.

Let
D_{n}
be the determinant of the Toeplitz matrix
[\varphi(i-j)]_{i,j=1}^{n}
for each
n
, and let
\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}
be the absolutely continuous part of
\mu
. Then

D_{n}^{1/n}\to\exp\int_{\mathbf{T}}\log\frac{d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}}{dm_{\mathbf{T}}}\,dm_{\mathbf{T}}\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty,

(1.1)

taking the right-hand side to be
\exp(-\infty)=0
if necessary. ∎

See
[
92
, Chap. 1]
for a broader overview of this area and a history of some of the key contributions, starting with Szegő’s and Verblunsky’s. Szegő’s theorem also has a generalization in which
\mu
and
\varphi
take values among positive definite
k
-by-
k
matrices
[
92
, Sec. 2.13]
.

The right-hand side of (
1.1
) admits the following ‘non-commutative’ point of view. Let
f:=d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}/dm_{\mathbf{T}}
, so this is an element of
L^{1}(\mathbf{T})
. We can regard
L^{\infty}(\mathbf{T})
as a von Neumann algebra of multiplication operators on
L^{2}(\mathbf{T})
, and then integration with respect to
m_{\mathbf{T}}
defines a tracial state on
L^{\infty}(\mathbf{T})
. If
f
is essentially bounded, then the multiplication operator
M_{f}
belongs to this von Neumann algebra; in general,
M_{f}
can be defined as an operator affiliated to that algebra. The right-hand side of (
1.1
) is the logarithm of the Fuglede–Kadison determinant of
M_{f}
with respect to
m_{\mathbf{T}}
(see Subsection
2.4
). This interpretation goes back at least to Arveson’s conjectured generalization of Szegő’s theorem for ‘subdiagonal subalgebras’ in
[
6
, property 4.4(
\gamma)
]
, which was proved after many years by Labuschagne in
[
63
]
. (We return to this connection in Subsection
1.3
.)

Our first main result is a version of Szegő’s theorem for matrix-valued positive definite functions on a countable amenable group
\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is countable, amenability means that it has a
right Følner sequence
: a sequence
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of finite subsets such that

|F_{n}\triangle F_{n}g|=o(|F_{n}|)\qquad\hbox{as $n\to\infty$ for every $g\in\Gamma$}.

(1.2)

Inversion in the group converts a right Følner sequence into a left Følner sequence, so the existence of either may be used to define amenability. See
[
22
, Sec. 2.6]
for several other characterizations and properties of this class of groups.

Let
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
be positive definite, and for each finite subset
F
of
\Gamma
consider the
F
-by-
F
block matrix

\varphi[F]:=[\varphi(g^{-1}h):\ g,h\in F].

If
\varphi
is associated to a unitary representation
\pi
by the vectors
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
, …,
v_{k}\in H_{\pi}
(see Subsection
2.3
), then
\varphi[F]
is the Gram matrix of the tuple of vectors

[\pi(g)v_{i}:\ g\in F,\ i=1,\dots,k],

so it is positive semi-definite.

Let
\lambda
be the left regular representation. Then
\varphi
has a unique ‘Lebesgue decomposition’ as
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}+\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
, where the minimal dilation of
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
is disjoint from
\lambda
and the minimal dilation of
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
is contained in
\lambda^{\oplus\infty}
(see Subsection
2.7
). In addition,
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
can be represented in terms of a self-adjoint operator affiliated to
\lambda^{\oplus k}(\Gamma)^{\prime}
(see Proposition
2.8
), and using this we can define the Fuglede–Kadison determinant
\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
(see Definition
2.11
).

Theorem A
.

Any right Følner sequence
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfies

(\det\varphi[F_{n}])^{1/|F_{n}|}\to\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty.

(1.3)

Notice that the contribution of the singular part
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
to the left-hand side of (
1.3
) disappears in the limit, just as in Szegő’s original theorem.

The literature contains a number of precedents lying between Szegő’s theorem and Theorem
A
. Firstly, generalizations of Szegő’s theorem to positive definite functions on
\mathbf{Z}^{d}
were obtained in
[
52
,
68
,
36
]
under various extra hypotheses. More recently,
[
31
, Thm. 3.2]
implies Theorem
A
in case
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}=0
and
\varphi
can be expressed in terms of a positive invertible element of the von Neumann algebra
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\lambda(\Gamma)^{\prime\prime})
. We discuss the recent literature more fully in Subsection
3.3
.

1.2
Analogies with entropy and ergodic theory

For a
k
-tuple of vectors in a Hilbert space, their Gram matrix
Q
specifies their lengths and relative positions
[
55
, Sec. 7.2]
. In doing so, it is roughly analogous to the joint distribution of
k
discrete random variables. The quantity
\log\det Q
is the analog of the joint Shannon entropy of those random variables.

More formally, if
(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
is a multivariate Gaussian random vector with covariance matrix
Q
, then its differential Shannon entropy equals
\log\det Q
up to a normalization
[
29
, Thm. 8.4.1]
. From this point of view, the analogy sketched above is really between discrete and differential entropy. However, the present paper concerns a purely ‘linear’ setting, so we generally discuss this analogy without the extraneous construct of a Gaussian random vector.

Now let
\Gamma
be a countable group, and consider actions of two different kinds: measure-preserving actions on probability spaces, and unitary actions on Hilbert spaces. These two settings enjoy a
\Gamma
-equivariant version of the analogy between joint distributions and Gram matrices. If
(X,\mu,T)
is a measure-preserving
\Gamma
-system and
\alpha:X\to A
is a finite-valued observable, then it generates the shift-system
(A^{\Gamma},\alpha^{\Gamma}_{\ast}\mu,S)
, where
\alpha^{\Gamma}_{\ast}\mu
is the law of the
\Gamma
-indexed stochastic process
(\alpha\circ T^{g}:\ g\in\Gamma)
. Analogously, if
\pi
is a unitary representation of
\Gamma
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
are vectors in it, then these define the
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued positive definite function
[\langle\pi(\cdot)v_{j},v_{i}\rangle]
on
\Gamma
.

This analogy has stimulated research in both areas. For example, it underlies Kechris’ adaptation of the relation of weak containment to measure-preserving systems
[
59
,
23
]
. Within this analogy, the logarithm of the limit on the left-hand side in Theorem
A
is the analog of the Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy of a stationary process over an amenable group
[
61
]
.

The analogy between ergodic theory and representation theory crystallizes into at least two different formal relationships. On the one hand, any measure-preserving system gives rise to its Koopman representation
[
59
, Sec. II.10]
. On the other hand, any orthogonal real representation of
\Gamma
can be used to construct a measure-preserving action on a Gaussian Hilbert space, and this construction can be adapted to start with a unitary representation instead
[
59
, Apps. C–E]
. However, the first of these relationships does not correctly connect the notions of entropy that we study in this work, and the second introduces unnecessary complications.

On the one hand, the limit in (
1.3
) generally bears no relation to Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy when
\varphi
is associated to a Koopman representation; indeed, the quantity in (
1.3
) does not even define an invariant of unitary equivalence in general.

On the other hand, if we start with a unitary representation and construct the associated Gaussian system, then there are cases in which a suitable limit of log-determinants for the former should equal a ‘differential’ analog of Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy for the latter. For single transformations or actions of
\mathbf{Z}^{d}
, differential Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy for stationary real-valued processes was studied in
[
45
]
, prompted by earlier work in information theory such as
[
81
]
. Section 6 of
[
45
]
includes some exact calculations for Gaussian systems and linear transformations between them which boil down to applications of Szegő’s theorem itself. However, this differential version of Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy starts to behave quite wildly beyond those Gaussian examples, and for those examples alone we might as well stay within the setting of representation theory. Overall, an analogy at the level of intuition seems more revealing for our work in this paper than either the Koopman or the Gaussian construction.

Beyond Theorem
A
, the present paper also considers two other notions of entropy from ergodic theory, and develop their analogs for unitary representations of groups or representations of other C*-algebras. The first of these notions is entropy defined using a ‘random past’ as in
[
61
, Thm. 3]
, which leads to Theorem
B
. The second is ‘sofic entropy’ from
[
19
]
, which leads to Theorem
C
. In both cases, we show that these notions of entropy are given by
\log\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
for some positive functional
\varphi
, except in Theorem
C
a certain degeneracy may occur and then the entropy equals
-\infty
(also reflecting a known feature of sofic entropy).

Each of these theorems may be seen as a different ‘non-commutative’ version of Szegő’s theorem. In ergodic theory, various predecessors of our results are already discussed this way in the literature, for example in
[
73
,
65
,
47
]
.

1.3
Random orders and Schur complements

Szegő’s theorem has several proofs. The classical ones often begin with a reformulation which implicitly uses the total ordering of
\mathbf{Z}
. First, the Schur determinant formula give the relation

\frac{D_{n+1}}{D_{n}}=\|1_{\mathbf{T}}-P_{n}(1_{\mathbf{T}})\|^{2},

where
P_{n}
is the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}(\mu)
to
\mathrm{span}\{z,\dots,z^{n}\}

[
92
, Thm. 1.5.11]
. It follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{n}^{1/n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{D_{n+1}}{D_{n}}=\|1_{\mathbf{T}}-P(1_{\mathbf{T}})\|^{2},

where
P
is the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}(\mu)
to
N:=\overline{\mathrm{span}}\{z,z^{2},\dots\}
(taking the closure in
L^{2}(\mu)
). This orthogonal projection is the closest point of
N
to the function
1_{\mathbf{T}}
, so Szegő’s theorem is equivalent to

\inf_{f\in N}\int_{\mathbf{T}}|1-f|^{2}\ d\mu=\exp\int_{\mathbf{T}}\log\frac{d\mu_{\mathrm{ac}}}{dm_{\mathbf{T}}}\ dm_{\mathbf{T}}.

(1.4)

This is the form in which Szegő’s theorem most often appears in the literature on analytic functions, such as in
[
42
, Sec. V.8]
. That reference gives essentially Szegő’s own proof, which he published first for the special case
\mu\ll m
, and then much later for the general case by incorporating arguments of Kolmogorov and Krein to handle
\mu_{\mathrm{sing}}
; see also
[
92
, Secs. 2.4–5]
.

Viewed from ergodic theory, the reformulation (
1.4
) is the analog of the formula for the entropy rate of a stationary finite-valued stochastic process
(\xi_{n})_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
in terms of its ‘past’:

\mathrm{h}(\xi)=\mathrm{H}(\xi_{0}\mid\xi_{-1},\xi_{-2},\dots).

(1.5)

See, for instance,
[
100
, Thm. 4.14]
.

To generalize Szegő’s theorem to positive definite functions on
\mathbf{Z}^{d}
, one can use the ‘past’ defined by a lexicographic ordering. This approach was developed by Helson and Lowdenslager in
[
52
,
53
]
(with some later refinements in
[
72
]
). In ergodic theory, the same use of the lexicographic ordering appeared in some early works on the entropy of measure-preserving
\mathbf{Z}^{d}
-actions such as
[
28
,
56
]
.

This idea generalizes naturally to any countable group
\Gamma
that admits a left-invariant total order. But some countable groups do not, including all groups that are not torsion-free. To remove the need for this assumption, one can instead couple to a stationary
random
ordering of the group. All countable groups admit at least one of these: the ‘Bernoulli random order’ (Example
4.1
below).

In ergodic theory, conditioning on the ‘past’ of a stationary random order can sometimes serve as a replacement for the formula (
1.5
). If
\Gamma
is amenable, this idea leads to another classical formula for the entropy rate of a process which goes back to Kieffer
[
61
]
and Stepin
[
94
]
. Our next main theorem develops the analog of this idea for positive definite functions. We find once again that the resulting quantity always agrees with the expected Fuglede–Kadison determinant, even if
\Gamma
is not amenable.

Before formulating the theorem precisely, let us motivate it further via a related finite-dimensional calculation. Let
n
and
k
be positive integers. Consider
nk
vectors in some Hilbert space
H
indexed as an
n
-by-
k
array, say

V=[x_{m,i}:\ m=1,\dots,n,\ i=1,\dots,k].

Let
V_{m}
be the
k
-tuple
[x_{m,1},\dots,x_{m,k}]
for each
m
. Then the Gram matrix of
V
obtains an
n
-by-
n
block structure:
V^{\ast}V=[V_{p}^{\ast}V_{m}:\ m,p=1,\dots,n]
. In addition, let
R_{m}
be the orthogonal projection onto the subspace

\mathrm{span}\{x_{p,i}:\ 1\leq p<m,\ i=1,\dots,k\}\qquad(m=1,\dots,n),

and let
R_{m}^{\perp}:=I_{H}-R_{m}
. We can express the determinant of
V^{\ast}V
in terms of these subspaces by an iterated appeal to Schur’s determinantal formula
[
55
, Subsec. 0.8.5]
. Within the analogy between Gram matrices and joint distributions from Subsection
1.2
, this formula is the analog of the chain rule for discrete Shannon entropy. Taking logarithms and normalizing, the result is

\frac{1}{n}\log\det(V^{\ast}V)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\log\det((R_{m}^{\perp}V_{m})^{\ast}(R_{m}^{\perp}V_{m})).

(1.6)

Formula (
1.6
) remains valid under any re-ordering of the tuples
V_{1}
, …,
V_{n}
(bearing in mind that each
R_{m}
depends on the whole order). So we can take an expectation over a uniform random order on the right-hand side. Then the symmetry of the random order lets us replace the average of
n
terms with a single average over orders. Using
\omega
to denote a permutation of
1
, …,
n
, and now writing
R_{\omega}
for the orthogonal projection onto the random subspace

\mathrm{span}\{x_{p,i}:\ 1\leq\omega(p)<\omega(1),\ i=1,\dots,k\},

we arrive at

\frac{1}{n}\log\det(V^{\ast}V)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\omega\in S_{n}}\log\det((R_{\omega}^{\perp}V_{1})^{\ast}(R_{\omega}^{\perp}V_{1})).

(1.7)

Our next theorem is an infinite-dimensional, equivariant generalization of (
1.7
). Let
\Gamma
be a countable group, let
\Omega
be the compact space of total orders on
\Gamma
, and let
\mu
be a left-invariant Borel probability measure on
\Omega
. Let
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
be positive definite, and suppose it is associated to the representation
\pi
by the
k
-tuple
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
in
H_{\pi}
. Finally, for each
\omega
, let
R_{\omega}
be the orthogonal projection from
H_{\pi}
to the closed subspace

\overline{\mathrm{span}}\{\pi(g)x_{i}:\ g<_{\omega}e,\ i=1,\dots,k\}.

Theorem B
.

In the situation above, we have

\exp\int\log\det[\langle R_{\omega}^{\perp}x_{j},R_{\omega}^{\perp}x_{i}\rangle]\ d\mu(\omega)=\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}.

(1.8)

For example, when
k=1
this simplifies to

\exp\int\log\|x-R_{\omega}x\|^{2}\ d\mu(\omega)=\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}.

From the viewpoint of ergodic theory, Theorem
B
is somewhat surprising, because random-order entropy for a finite-valued stationary processes can misbehave when
\Gamma
is not amenable. It agrees with Rokhlin or sofic entropy (discussed below) for some special examples of processes
[
8
,
3
]
, but in general it is only an upper bound for those quantities, and it need not be invariant under isomorphism
[
90
, Sec. 7]
. By contrast, when both Theorems
B
above and
C
below can be applied to a positive definite function
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
, they always give the same value
\log\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
for their respective notions of entropy. So some serious pathologies from ergodic theory do not appear for unitary representations.

We prove Theorem
B
in Section
4
. We do this by making contact with Arveson’s theory of ‘subdiagonal subalgebras’ of finite von Neumann algebras
[
6
]
. These are defined by axioms abstracted from the inclusion
H^{\infty}(\mathbf{T})\subset L^{\infty}(\mathbf{T})
, which becomes a commutative example; see also
[
82
, Sec. 8]
or
[
13
]
for surveys in the context of noncommutative Lebesgue spaces. A subdiagonal subalgebra provides a finite von Neumann algebra with an abstract notion of ‘past’. Among his other examples, Arveson showed how to construct a subdiagonal subalgebra from any countable group with an invariant total order, and our starting point is a generalization of this construction using an invariant random order and a crossed product. Then we derive Theorem
B
via a more abstract version of the same result (Theorem
4.4
), which we deduce from the Arveson–Labuschagne generalization of Jensen’s inequality from
[
6
]
and
[
63
]
.

1.4
Almost periodic entropy

Since its introduction in
[
19
]
, Bowen’s notion of sofic entropy has taken a central place in the ergodic theory of actions of non-amenable groups. The survey
[
21
]
offers a thorough introduction. This is the final notion of entropy that we pursue across the analogy between ergodic theory and representation theory in this paper.

In this effort, we quickly find it helpful to allow the generality of unital representations of a separable, unital C*-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
, rather than just unitary representations of a group. The case of a countable group
\Gamma
is recovered when
\mathfrak{A}=C^{\ast}\Gamma
. Even in that special case, certain auxiliary constructions lead us to consider other C*-algebras as well.

Given a representation
\pi
of
\mathfrak{A}
and vectors
v_{1}
, …,
v_{k}\in H_{\pi}
, define their
type
to be the
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued completely positive map

\Phi^{\pi}_{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}}(a):=[\langle\pi(a)v_{j},v_{i}\rangle]_{i,j=1}^{k}\qquad(a\in\mathfrak{A}).

If
O
is any set of
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued completely positive maps for a fixed value of
k
, and
\pi
is any representation, then we define

\mathcal{X}(\pi,O):=\{(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\in H_{\pi}^{k}:\ \Phi^{\pi}_{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}}\in O\}.

Imagining that
O
is a small neighbourhood of a particular map
\varphi
, this is the analog of a set of ‘good models’ for a given shift-invariant measure in sofic entropy theory (see
[
21
]
)

Consider also a sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of representations of
\mathfrak{A}
whose dimensions
d_{n}
are finite but diverge. We refer to it as an
almost periodic sequence
for
\mathfrak{A}
. We define the
almost periodic entropy
of an
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued completely positive map
\varphi
along
\boldsymbol{\pi}
to be

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi):=\inf_{O}\limsup_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{d_{i}}\log\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{i}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{i},O)}{v(d_{i})^{k}},

where
O
ranges over neighbourhoods of
\varphi
,
\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{i}}
refers to Lebesgue measure in
2kd_{i}
real dimensions, and
v(d_{i})
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbf{C}^{d_{i}}
. This is inspired by the definition of sofic entropy for a finite-valued stationary process over a sofic group.

We introduce this new notion of entropy and develop its basic properties in Section
6
. Many of these resemble properties of sofic entropy, but some differences emerge. For example, almost periodic entropy is not an invariant of unitary equivalence of representations, but it does satisfy a transformation formula if one changes cyclic vector within a fixed representation.

Our main result in this section is a formula for
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)
as a Fuglede–Kadison determinant. It holds whenever
\varphi
is ‘asymptotically associated’ to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and the pulled-back traces
d_{i}^{-1}\mathrm{tr}_{d_{i}}\circ\pi_{i}
converge to a limiting tracial state
\tau
of
\mathfrak{A}
. ‘Asymptotic association’ means that, for every neighbourhood
O
of
\varphi
, the set
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{i},O)
is nonempty for infinitely many
i
; if this fails then
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)
is simply forced to be
-\infty
.

Let
\Delta
be the Fuglede–Kadison determinant defined from
\tau
, and let
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}+\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
be the Lebesgue decomposition of
\varphi
relative to
\tau
(see Subsection
2.7
).

Theorem C
.

Suppose that
d_{n}^{-1}\mathrm{tr}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
and that
\varphi
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. Then

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)=\log\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}.

Let us emphasize two features of Theorem
C
that are substantially different from Theorems
A
and
B
:

•

Any tracial positive functional
\tau
on
\mathfrak{A}
may appear in Theorem
C
, provided it can arise as a suitable limit of normalized finite-dimensional traces. By contrast, Theorems
A
and
B
refer specifically to the regular character on a group
\Gamma
and its associated tracial state on
C^{\ast}\Gamma
.

•

Theorem
C
does not make any assumption on
\mathfrak{A}
itself that corresponds to soficity of a group. However, such an assumption is implicit in the hypothesis that
\tau
is a limit of normalized finite-dimensional traces. By applying this theorem to
C^{\ast}\Gamma
when
\Gamma
is a free group and
\tau
is lifted from a quotient group of
\Gamma
, one recovers a theorem for precisely Rădulescu’s class of hyperlinear groups from
[
83
]
: see Subsection
5.4
.

In comparison with Szegő’s theorem, Theorem
C
has the interesting new feature that
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)
may equal
-\infty
if
\varphi
is not asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, even though
\log\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
may still be finite in that case. If
\mathfrak{A}=C^{\ast}\Gamma
and
\tau
is the state given by the regular character of
\Gamma
, then this is possible only if
\Gamma
is non-amenable, and reflects basic features of the representation theory of non-amenable groups.

Subsection
6.6
derives various consequences of Theorem
C
, for example concerning different possible modes of convergence for the sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}
itself.

As far as I know, the nearest precursors to Theorem
C
in the literature are formulas for the sofic entropy of certain special measure-preserving systems in
[
73
,
74
]
and especially
[
47
,
51
]
. We compare our work with these in Subsection
6.7
.

2

Operator algebras, C*-algebras and representations

This section recalls the background we need from linear algebra and the theory of C*-algebras. I assume functional analysis at about the level of
[
84
]
. Beyond that, I state a number of standard results for later reference, and prove a few that are not easily found in the literature.

2.1
Linear algebra

We write
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus k}
for the space of complex height-
k
column vectors. More generally, if
S
is a set, possibly infinite, and
H
is a Hilbert space, then we write
H^{\oplus S}
for the Hilbert-space direct sum of an
S
-indexed family of copies of
H
, still regarded as a space of column vectors. This insistence on column vectors is slightly unusual in functional analysis, but for finite
k
it enables us to use matrix-vector notation from linear algebra in places where it simplifies the exposition.

We write
\mathbf{M}_{n,k}
for the space of
n
-by-
k
matrices over the complex numbers, and identify these with linear maps from
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus k}
to
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus n}
using matrix-vector multiplication. By writing such a matrix as
[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]
, where
v_{1}
, …,
v_{k}
are its columns, we can identify it with a
k
-tuple of vectors in
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus n}
. We generalize this notation further by allowing columns from any vector space
H
, so a linear map
V
from
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus k}
to
H
may still be written in the form
[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]
. We sometimes abuse notation by calling
V
itself a ‘
k
-tuple of vectors in
H
’. If
H
is a Hilbert space, then the adjoint
V^{\ast}
is the map from
H
to
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus k}
whose output coordinates are given by the inner products with the vectors
v_{i}
.

We abbreviate
\mathbf{M}_{k,k}
to
\mathbf{M}_{k}
and regard it as a
\ast
-algebra over
\mathbf{C}
in the usual way. We write
I_{k}
for the
k
-by-
k
identity matrix. We write
\mathrm{tr}_{k}
and
\mathrm{det}
for the usual trace and determinant on any such algebra, and we set
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}:=k^{-1}\mathrm{tr}_{k}
.

We write
\mathbf{M}_{k+}
for the closed cone of positive semi-definite elements of
\mathbf{M}_{k}
. It defines the positive definite ordering on self-adjoint matrices. If

, then its determinant and trace are related by the inequality

(2.1)

This is simply the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means applied to the eigenvalues of

.

For a linear operator on an inner product space, or a matrix that can be regarded as such, the notation

means the operator norm.

If
P
is an orthogonal projection in a Hilbert space

.

2.2
C*-algebras, von Neumann algebras, and representations

Throughout this paper,
\mathfrak{A}
is a
separable
,
unital
C*-algebra and we study
separable
representations, meaning that they act on separable complex Hilbert spaces. We denote the unit of
\mathfrak{A}
by
1_{\mathfrak{A}}
or just
1
. Our guiding examples are the group C*-algebras of countable groups: see Subsection
2.5
below. We follow the common convention that C
∗
-algebras may exist in the abstract, but a von Neumann algebra is always a weak-operator closed
\ast
-subalgebra of
\mathfrak{L}(H)
for some particular Hilbert space
H
. In particular, we sometimes casually identify isomorphic C
∗
-algebras when this can cause no confusion, but two von Neumann algebras acting on different Hilbert spaces are not identified, even if they are isomorphic.

We usually denote a representation of
\mathfrak{A}
by a single letter such as
\pi
, and then write its Hilbert space as
H_{\pi}
when necessary. We use
\oplus
to denote direct sums in the categories of Hilbert spaces or representations
[
33
, Subsec. 2.2.3]
, and we use
\otimes
for tensor products of Hilbert spaces, operators on Hilbert spaces, or von Neumann algebras of such operators
[
34
, Secs. I.2.3–4]
. We do not need the more involved theory of tensor products of abstract C*-algebras. For a representation
\pi
and any
k\in\mathbf{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, we write either
\pi^{\oplus k}
or
\pi\otimes I_{k}
for the direct sum of
k
copies of
\pi
, and refer to it as the
k
-fold inflation
of
\pi
.

If
\pi
is a representation and
M
is a closed
\pi
-invariant subspace of
H_{\pi}
, then we write
\pi^{M}
for the associated subrepresentation of
\mathfrak{A}
on
M
. If
\pi
is a representation of
\mathfrak{A}
, then a subset
S
of
H_{\pi}
is
cyclic
for
\pi
if it is not contained in any proper closed invariant subspace of
H_{\pi}
, or equivalently if
\sum_{v\in S}\pi(\mathfrak{A})v
is dense in
H_{\pi}
.

Given two representations
\pi
and
\rho
, we write
\pi\simeq\rho
if they are unitarily equivalent,
\pi\lesssim\rho
if
\pi
is contained in
\rho
, and
\pi\spoon\rho
if they are disjoint; see
[
33
, Secs. 2.2 and 5.2]
, for example.

Now let
\kappa:=\pi\oplus\rho
. Regard
H_{\pi}
and
H_{\rho}
as subspaces of
H_{\kappa}
, and let
P
be the orthogonal projection from
H_{\kappa}
onto
H_{\pi}
. The next result is
[
33
, Prop. 5.2.4]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

We have
\pi\spoon\rho
if and only if
P
lies in the centre of
\kappa(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
. ∎

A related result of Mackey breaks up two arbitrary representations into quasi-equivalent and disjoint pieces
[
75
, Thm. 1.11]
. We need the following special case.

Proposition 2.2
.

If
\pi
and
\rho
are representations, then
\pi
has a unique closed invariant subspace
M
such that
\pi^{M}\lesssim\rho^{\oplus\infty}
and
\pi^{M^{\perp}}\spoon\rho
. ∎

We call
\pi^{M}
and
\pi^{M^{\perp}}
the
\rho
-normal
and
\rho
-singular
parts of
\pi
, respectively.

2.3
Types and completely positive maps

Let
\pi
be a representation of
\mathfrak{A}
, let
v_{1}
, …,
v_{k}\in H_{\pi}
, and regard the tuple
V:=[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]
as a linear map from
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus k}
to
H_{\pi}
. To keep track of how these vectors move together under the action of
\pi
, we can consider the
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued map

\Phi^{\pi}_{V}(a):=V^{\ast}\pi(a)V=[\langle\pi(a)v_{j},v_{i}\rangle]_{i,j=1}^{k}\qquad(a\in\mathfrak{A}).

(2.2)

Notice that the order of the indices matches the convention for the Gram matrix of a tuple of vectors
[
55
, Sec. 7.2]
. We sometimes adapt a term from information theory by calling
\Phi^{\pi}_{V}
the
type
of the tuple
V
in
\pi
(compare
[
29
, Sec. 11.1]
, for example). We also sometimes write
\Phi^{\pi}_{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}}
instead of
\Phi^{\pi}_{V}
.

The map in (
2.2
) is completely positive, and any completely positive map from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathbf{M}_{k}
has this form for some
\pi
and
V
by Stinespring’s theorem
[
22
, Ex. 1.5.2 and Thm. 1.5.3]
. If we require in addition that
V
be cyclic for
\pi
, then the resulting pair
(\pi,V)
is unique up to unitary equivalence. In this case
\pi
is the
minimal dilation
of
\varphi
and is denoted by
\pi_{\varphi}
. By the uniqueness of minimal dilations,
\varphi
is associated to
\pi
if and only if
\pi_{\varphi}\lesssim\pi
.

When
k=1
, these facts reduce to the construction of the GNS representation and its distinguished cylic vector from a positive linear functional. We write
\mathfrak{A}^{\ast}_{+}
for the space of such functionals on
\mathfrak{A}
. For general values of
k
, we write
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})
for the space of all continuous linear maps from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathbf{M}_{k}
, and
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
for the subset of all completely positive ones. If
\varphi,\psi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})
, then we write
\varphi\leq\psi
if the functional
\psi-\varphi
is completely positive. This defines a partial order on
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})
in which
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
is the non-negative cone. We write
\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
for the subset of all completely positive maps
\varphi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
that are
normalized
, meaning that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}\varphi(1)=1
. In particular,
\Sigma_{1}(\mathfrak{A})
is the state space of
\mathfrak{A}

[
33
, Chap. 2]
.

The vector space
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})
has a natural topology obtained by applying the weak
∗
topology in each matrix entry. Henceforth we simply refer to this as ‘the weak
∗
topology’ of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})
, and take it as the default topology on
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})
or its subsets. For any
k
, complete positivity is defined by a family of closed linear inequalities, so
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
is a weak
∗
-closed cone in
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})
. The further subset
\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
is compact by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem and also metrizable because
\mathfrak{A}
is separable.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
\pi
and
k
, the type map

H_{\pi}^{k}\mapsto\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k}):[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]\mapsto\Phi^{\pi}_{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}}

is continuous.

Proof.

This is elementary for the inner product map
H_{\pi}\times H_{\pi}\to\mathbf{C}
, and then follows for types by arguing pointwise for
i
,
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, and
{a\in\mathfrak{A}}
.
∎

Now consider again a representation
\pi
and a tuple
v_{1}
, …,
v_{k}
in
H_{\pi}
. If
a=[a_{ij}]
is an
\ell
-by-
k
matrix of elements of
\mathfrak{A}
, then we can define a new
\ell
-tuple in
H_{\pi}
by the formula

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{\ell}\end{array}\right]:=[\pi(a_{ij})]\cdot\left[\begin{array}[]{c}v_{1}\\
\vdots\\
v_{k}\end{array}\right],

(2.3)

understood by following the rules of matrix-vector multiplication. For example, if
a=[q_{ij}\cdot 1]
for some scalar matrix
Q=[q_{ij}]
, then we can identify
[\pi(a_{ij})]
with
I_{H_{\pi}}\otimes Q
, and (
2.3
) becomes

[y_{1},\dots,y_{\ell}]^{\mathrm{T}}:=(I_{H_{\pi}}\otimes Q)[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]^{\mathrm{T}}.

(2.4)

If the tuples
v_{1}
, …,
v_{k}
and
y_{1}
, …,
y_{\ell}
are related as in (
2.3
), and
\varphi
and
\psi
are their respective types, then
\psi
may be written using the tuple
v_{1}
, …,
v_{k}
like this:

\psi_{ii^{\prime}}(b)=\langle\pi(b)y_{i^{\prime}},y_{i}\rangle=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{k}\langle\pi(ba_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})v_{j^{\prime}},\pi(a_{ij})v_{j}\rangle\qquad(b\in\mathfrak{A},\ 1\leq i,i^{\prime}\leq k).

Writing this right-hand side in terms of
\varphi
itself yields the following.

Lemma 2.4
.

In the situation above, we have

\psi_{ii^{\prime}}(b)=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{k}\varphi_{jj^{\prime}}((a_{ij})^{\ast}ba_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})\qquad(b\in\mathfrak{A},\ 1\leq i,i^{\prime}\leq k).

(2.5)

As a result, with
[a_{ij}]
held fixed,
\psi
is continuous as a function of
\varphi
for the weak
∗
topologies.

In particular, in the special case of (
2.4
), we have

\psi(b):=(Q^{\mathrm{T}})^{\ast}\varphi(b)Q^{\mathrm{T}}\qquad(b\in\mathfrak{A}).

(2.6)

∎

2.4
Tracial functionals and determinants

A special role is played by positive functionals
\tau
on
\mathfrak{A}
that are
tracial
, meaning they satisfy the
trace identity
:
\tau(ab)=\tau(ba)
for all
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}
. Fix such a functional
\tau
, let its GNS representation be
\lambda
with associating vector
\xi
, and let
H:=H_{\lambda}
. Then we also refer to the vector
\xi
as
tracial
.

A tracial functional gives rise to a rich additional structure on the von Neumann algebra
\mathfrak{N}:=\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
. This is introduced in
[
34
, Chap. I.5]
, which uses the language of Hilbert algebras, and
[
34
, Chap. I.6]
, which explains the equivalence with tracial functionals. A more modern account is
[
22
, App. F]
. The vector
\xi
defines the normal positive functional
\langle(\cdot)\xi,\xi\rangle
on
\mathfrak{L}(H)
, and the restriction of this functional to either
\mathfrak{N}
or
\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}
still satisfies the trace identity. We often write
\widetilde{\tau}
for either of these restrictions. When
\widetilde{\tau}
is the restriction to
\mathfrak{N}
, it satisfies
\tau=\widetilde{\tau}\circ\lambda
.

In addition,
\xi
turns out to be both cyclic and separating for both
\mathfrak{N}
and
\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}

[
34
, Cor. I.6.1 and Prop. I.1.5]
. Using
\xi
, the dense subspace
\mathfrak{N}\xi
has a well-defined involution given by

A\xi\mapsto A^{\ast}\xi\qquad(A\in\mathfrak{N}).

(2.7)

This map anti-linear, it fixes
\xi
, and it converts inner products to their conjugates as a consequence of the trace identity. It is therefore an isometry, and extends by continuity to an involution of the whole of
H
that has the same properties. This is the
canonical involution

J
associated to
\mathfrak{N}
and
\xi
. Finally, the map
A\mapsto JAJ
is an involutive
\ast
-anti-automorphism of
\mathfrak{L}(H)
, it acts by complex conjugation on the functional
\langle(\cdot)\xi,\xi\rangle
, and it exchanges the subalgebras
\mathfrak{N}
and
\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}
of
\mathfrak{L}(H)
.

In the context above, some constructions require operators that are closed, densely-defined, and affiliated to
\mathfrak{N}
or
\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}
, but possibly unbounded. See
[
84
, Chap. VIII]
for background on unbounded operators, and
[
34
, Exers. I.1.10 and III.1.13]
or
[
22
, App. F]
for the definition and some basic properties of affiliated operators. Such operators are the basic elements of ‘noncommutative integration theory’: see
[
88
,
89
]
for an early account. They could also be identified with elements of abstract noncommutative Lebesgue spaces
[
82
]
, but our uses below depend on them being operators on Hilbert spaces.

Our specific need is for the following subclass. Let
T
be affiliated to
\mathfrak{N}
, and let
E
be the spectral resolution of
|T|
on
[0,\infty)
. Following
[
88
, Subsec. 3.4]
and
[
22
, App. F]
, we call
T

square-integrable
with respect to
\widetilde{\tau}
if

\int_{[0,\infty)}t^{2}\ \widetilde{\tau}E(dt)<\infty.

(2.8)

If
\widetilde{\tau}
is the restriction of
\langle(\cdot)\xi,\xi\rangle
to
\mathfrak{N}
, then square-integrability is equivalent to
\xi\in\mathrm{dom}\,T
, and then the left-hand side of (
2.8
) is equal to
\|T\xi\|^{2}
.

Any normal tracial positive functional
\widetilde{\tau}
on a von Neumann algebra
\mathfrak{N}
defines an associated
Fuglede–Kadison determinant
: see
[
34
, Sec. I.6.11]
. We denote it by
\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}}
, or just
\Delta
if the choice of
\widetilde{\tau}
is clear.

If
T
is a square-integrable operator affiliated to
\mathfrak{N}
, then we may extend the definitions of trace and determinant by setting

\widetilde{\tau}(T):=\int_{[0,\infty)}t\ \widetilde{\tau}E(dt)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\Delta T:=\exp\int_{[0,\infty)}\log t\ \widetilde{\tau}E(dt).

(2.9)

Both integrands are dominated by
t^{2}+1
, so
\widetilde{\tau}(T)
is finite and
\Delta T
is either positive or equal to
\exp(-\infty)=0
. These definitions can be extended to even larger classes of operators, but we do not need those here; see
[
44
, Sec. 2]
for the determinant in the full generality of ‘log-integrable’ operators. If
\widetilde{\tau}
is a state, then
\widetilde{\tau}E
is a probability measure, and we can apply Jensen’s inequality
[
85
, Thm. 3.3]
to the two integrals in (
2.9
) to obtain an extension of the determinant-trace inequality (
2.1
):

\Delta T\leq\widetilde{\tau}(T).

(2.10)

Now suppose that
T
is non-negative, square-integrable, and affiliated to
\mathfrak{N}
, and let
E
be its spectral resolution on
[0,\infty)
. For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, let

T_{\delta}:=(T\vee\delta)\wedge\delta^{-1}:=\int_{[0,\infty)}(t\vee\delta)\wedge\delta^{-1}\ E(dt),

where ‘
\vee
’ stands for ‘
\max
’ and ‘
\wedge
’ stands for ‘
\min
’. Then
T_{\delta}
is an element of
\mathfrak{N}
satisfying
\delta\leq T_{\delta}\leq\delta^{-1}
. Let
E_{+}:=E(0,\infty)=1-E\{0\}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

As
\delta\downarrow 0
, these operators satisfy

i.

T_{\delta}x\to Tx
and
T_{\delta}^{-1}Tx\to E_{+}x
for every
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,T
;

ii.

\Delta T_{\delta}\to\Delta T
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathrm{dom}\,T
. Then the spectral theorem gives

\displaystyle\|Tx-T_{\delta}x\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,\infty)}|t-(t\vee\delta)\wedge\delta^{-1}|^{2}\ \langle E(dt)x,x\rangle,

\displaystyle\|E_{+}x-T_{\delta}^{-1}Tx\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,\infty)}\Big|1-\frac{t}{(t\vee\delta)\wedge\delta^{-1}}\Big|^{2}\ \langle E(dt)x,x\rangle

\displaystyle\hbox{and}\qquad\log\Delta T_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,\infty)}\log((\delta\vee t)\wedge\delta^{-1})\ \widetilde{\tau}E(dt)

Both of the expressions
\langle E(\cdot)x,x\rangle
and
\widetilde{\tau}E
are finite Borel measures on
\mathbf{R}
, and the function
t^{2}+1
is integrable with respect to both of them by our assumptions on
T
. Therefore the dominated convergence theorem applies to the first two integrals above as
\delta\downarrow 0
, showing that they both converge to
0
. This proves conclusion (i). The dominated convergence theorem also applies to the positive part of the third integral, while the negative part is subject to the monotone convergence theorem. It follows that the third integral converges to
\log\Delta T
(even if this equals
-\infty
), and then exponentiating gives conclusion (ii).
∎

If
\tau
is a tracial positive functional on a C*-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
and we construct
\lambda
,
\xi
and
\widetilde{\tau}
on
\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
from it as above, and if
a
is a non-negative element of
\mathfrak{A}
, then we write
\Delta a
for
\Delta\lambda(a)
. If
a
is also invertible, then
\Delta a
is equal to
\exp(\tau(\log a))
.

2.5
Group algebras and positive definite functions

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete group with identity element
e
. We write
\mathbf{C}[\Gamma]
for the complex group algebra of
\Gamma
, and regard it concretely as the space of finitely supported functions from
\Gamma
to
\mathbf{C}
; see
[
33
, Chap. 13]
, which also allows general locally compact groups. Given
g\in\Gamma
, we write
\delta_{g}
for its canonical image in
\mathbf{C}[\Gamma]
, so the unit of
\mathbf{C}[\Gamma]
is
\delta_{e}
.

Similarly, we identity
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathbf{C}[\Gamma])
with the vector space
\mathbf{M}_{k}[\Gamma]
of finitely supported maps from
\Gamma
to
\mathbf{M}_{k}
. Given
\varphi,\psi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
, at least one of them finitely supported, we extend the usual definition of convolution by writing

(\varphi\ast\psi)(g):=\sum_{h,k:\ hk=g}\varphi(h)\psi(k)=\sum_{h}\varphi(h)\psi(h^{-1}g)\qquad(g\in\Gamma).

(2.11)

The individual summands here are matrix products. If both
\varphi
and
\psi
are finitely supported then so is
\varphi\ast\psi
, and then (
2.11
) defines the structure of
\mathbf{M}_{k}[\Gamma]
as a group algebra with matrix coefficients.

The group C*-algebra
C^{\ast}\Gamma
is the maximal C*-completion of
\mathbf{C}[\Gamma]
. Representations of
C^{\ast}\Gamma
are in one-to-one correspondence with unitary representations of
\Gamma
itself, and we generally use the same notation for a representation of
C^{\ast}\Gamma
and for its restriction to
\Gamma
; see
[
33
, Sec. 13.9]
. Throughout the rest of this paper, a ‘representation’ of
\Gamma
always means a unitary representation. For each
g\in\Gamma
, we continue to write
\delta_{g}
for its image in
C^{\ast}\Gamma
. We can identify
\mathbf{M}_{k}(C^{\ast}\Gamma)
with the corresponding completion of
\mathbf{M}_{k}[\Gamma]
(see
[
79
, Lem. 10]
).

Now consider a completely positive map
\varphi:C^{\ast}\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
. Identifying each group element
g
with its image
\delta_{g}
, we can restrict
\varphi
to an
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued map on
\Gamma
itself, which we usually continue to denote by
\varphi
. The maps on
\Gamma
that arise this way are positive definite: see
[
33
, Sec. 13.4]
for the case
k=1
or
[
22
, App. D]
for the general case. On the other hand, another variant of the GNS construction shows that any
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued positive definite function on
\Gamma
is associated to some unitary representation, and therefore extends to a completely positive map on
C^{\ast}\Gamma
. The proof in the matrix-valued case can be modeled directly on the scalar case; this idea essentially goes back Naimark’s work
[
78
]
on Abelian groups. So Naimark’s result is an older cousin of Stinespring’s theorem, although not quite a special case of it; see
[
79
, Thm. 12 and Cor. 13]
for a unified treatment.

Under this bijection between the set of
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued positive definite maps on
\Gamma
and the space
\mathfrak{L}(C^{\ast}\Gamma,\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, the weak
∗
topology on
\mathfrak{L}(C^{\ast}\Gamma,\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
corresponds to the usual weak
∗
topology restricted from
\ell^{\infty}(\Gamma;\mathbf{M}_{k})
, and when restricted further to any uniformly bounded subset it coincides with the topology of pointwise convergence.

Let
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
be positive definite, let
a\in\mathbf{M}_{k}[\Gamma]
, and regard
a
as an element of
\mathbf{M}_{k}(C^{\ast}\Gamma)
. Having defined convolution in the generality of (
2.11
), we may use it to express the pairing from (
2.15
) like this:

\langle\varphi,a\rangle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{ij}\sum_{g}a_{ij}(g)\varphi_{ij}(g)=\sum_{g}\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}(\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}(g^{-1})a(g))=\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}((\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}\ast a)(e)),

(2.12)

where we define

\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}(g):=\varphi(g^{-1})^{\mathrm{T}}\qquad(g\in\Gamma)

(2.13)

(that is, we apply inversion in
\Gamma
and transpose to elements of
\mathbf{M}_{k}
).

Finally, a state
\tau
on
C^{\ast}\Gamma
is tracial if and only if its restriction to
\Gamma
is central. In this case that restriction is called a
character
of
\Gamma
. For example, the function
1_{\{e\}}
is the
regular character
. It is associated to the left regular representation on
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
by the function
\delta_{e}
. More generally, if
H
is a subgroup of
\Gamma
, then the function
1_{H}
is positive definite. It is associated to the quasi-regular representation of
\Gamma
on
\ell^{2}(\Gamma/H)
by the function
\delta_{eH}
, and it is a character if and only if
H
is normal in
G
.

2.6
Algebras of matrices and the pairing isomorphism

If
\mathfrak{A}
is a C
∗
-algebra and
k
is a positive integer, then we write
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
for the algebra of
k
-by-
k
matrices with entries from
\mathfrak{A}
. The algebra operations combine those of
\mathfrak{A}
with the usual rules for matrices, and we define an involution on
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
by transposing and applying the involution of
\mathfrak{A}
entry-wise. Then
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
has a natural identification with the algebraic tensor product of
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathbf{M}_{k}
.

If
\pi
is a representation of
\mathfrak{A}
and
k
is a positive integer, then we define a representation
\pi^{(k)}
of
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
on
H_{\pi}^{\oplus k}
by setting
\pi^{(k)}([a_{ij}]):=[\pi(a_{ij})]
and following the rules of matrix-vector multiplication as in (
2.3
). From another point of view, we can identify
H_{\pi}^{\oplus k}
with
H_{\pi}\otimes\mathbf{C}^{\oplus k}
, and then
\pi^{(k)}
is the Kronecker product of
\pi
with the canonical representation of
\mathbf{M}_{k}
on
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus k}
. Every representation of
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
is equivalent to one of this form: this can be seen by using the canonical copy of
\mathbf{M}_{k}
inside
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
to break up a general representation into
k
orthogonal subspaces with partial isometries between them (compare
[
33
, Subs. 9.2.2]
). We make
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
into a C*-algebra in a canonical way by pulling back the operator norm through
\pi^{(k)}
for any faithful representation
\pi
.

Elements of
\pi^{(k)}(\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A}))^{\prime\prime}
are naturally represented by elements of
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\pi(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime})
, and their commutant is given by

\pi^{(k)}(\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A}))^{\prime}=\{T^{\oplus k}:\ T\in\pi(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}\}

(2.14)

(see
[
34
, Prop. I.2.4(iii)]
).

We define the
pairing
of elements
a\in\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
and
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})
by

\langle\varphi,a\rangle:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{ij}\varphi_{ij}(a_{ij}).

(2.15)

The map
\varphi\mapsto\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
is the
pairing isomorphism
. It identifies
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})
with
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})^{\ast}
. We henceforth regard either space as carrying the weak
∗
topology.

The pairing isomorphism restricts to a bijection between the closed cones
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
and
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})_{+}^{\ast}
: see
[
22
, Prop. 1.5.14]
, although their pairing differs from ours by a factor of
k
. Applied to the type of a tuple of vectors, the pairing isomorphism has the following effect.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
\varphi
is associated to
\pi
by the cyclic tuple
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in H_{\pi}
, then
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
is associated to
\pi^{(k)}
by the cyclic vector
k^{-1/2}[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]^{\mathrm{T}}
. In particular,
\pi_{\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle}
is equivalent to
\pi_{\varphi}^{(k)}
.
∎

In this lemma, the factor of
k^{-1/2}
has the effect that an orthonormal tuple gives rise to a unit vector.

Because of positivity, the restriction of the dual norm satisfies

\|\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle\|_{\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})^{\ast}}=\langle\varphi,1\otimes I_{k}\rangle=\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}\varphi(1)\qquad(\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+})

(2.16)

(see
[
33
, Prop. 2.1.4]
). It follows that the pairing isomorphism identifies
\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
with
\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A}))
. The identity (
2.16
) has another consequence in the next lemma, for which I have not found a reference.

Lemma 2.7
.

The restriction of the weak
∗
topology to
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
is locally compact and second countable.

Proof.

By considering pairing functionals on
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
as in (
2.15
) instead of positive definite maps on
\mathfrak{A}
, we may reduce to the case
k=1
. Having done so, let

U_{r}:=\{\varphi\in\mathfrak{A}^{\ast}_{+}:\ \varphi(1)<r\}\qquad(r>0).

Each of these sets is relatively weak
∗
-open in
\mathfrak{A}^{\ast}_{+}
. On the other hand, by (
2.16
), the weak
∗
-closure
\overline{U_{r}}
is equal to the intersection of
\mathfrak{A}^{\ast}_{+}
with a closed ball of radius
r
in
\mathfrak{A}^{\ast}
. Therefore
\overline{U_{r}}
is metrizable and also compact by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, and so it is second countable. This shows that
\mathfrak{A}^{\ast}_{+}
is covered by the sequence
U_{1}
,
U_{2}
, …of open subsets, each of which is precompact and second countable in the weak
∗
topology. ∎

Via the pairing isomorphism, many facts about
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued completely positive maps on
\mathfrak{A}
can be reduced to the scalar-valued case for the algebra
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
. Two standard examples can be found as
[
22
, Cor. 1.5.16 and Prop. 1.7.1]
. We meet some more in the next subsection.

2.7
Comparing and decomposing completely positive maps

Two completely positive maps
\varphi
and
\psi
are
disjoint
if
\pi_{\varphi}\perp\pi_{\psi}
.

Now let
\rho
be a representation of
\mathfrak{A}
, let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, and let
\pi:=\pi_{\varphi}
for brevity. Then
\varphi
is
\rho
-normal
or
\rho
-singular
according as
\pi
has this property. By the general description of ultraweakly continuous functionals on a von Neumann algebra
[
34
, Thm. I.3.1]
,
\varphi
is
\rho
-normal if and only if it is equal to
\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\rho
for some normal positive functional
\widetilde{\varphi}
on
\rho(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
, which is then unique. We call
\widetilde{\varphi}
the
normal extension
of
\varphi
to
\rho(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
.

Suppose that
\varphi
is associated to
\pi
by the cyclic
k
-tuple
V
in
H_{\pi}
. Let
\pi^{M}
be the
\rho
-normal part of
\pi
, as given by Proposition
2.2
, and let
P
be the orthogonal projection from
H_{\pi}
onto
M
. Because
P
commutes with
\pi
, we have
\varphi=\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}+\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
, where
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}:=\Phi^{\pi}_{PV}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}:=\Phi^{\pi}_{P^{\perp}V}
. These two summands are
\rho
-normal and
\rho
-singular respectively. We refer to this as the
Lebesgue decomposition
of
\varphi
relative to
\rho
, because it reduces to the classic Lebesgue decomposition from measure theory if
\mathfrak{A}=C(\Omega)
for some compact metrizable space
\Omega
and we represent positive functionals by Borel measures. This also explains the choice of subscripts. Both
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
are still associated to
\pi
by construction. This decomposition into
\rho
-normal and
\rho
-singular parts is unique by the uniqueness of minimal dilations and the fact that any
\rho
-normal representation is disjoint from any
\rho
-singular representation. Such decompositions appear in
[
95
]
, where they are described via the enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{A}
.

If
k>1
, then by (
2.14
) and Lemma
2.6
the Lebesgue decomposition of
\varphi
gives rise to a suitable decomposition of
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
through the pairing isomorphism, and so its uniquenes implies that

\langle\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}},\cdot\rangle=\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{ac}}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\langle\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}},\cdot\rangle=\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{sing}}.

(2.17)

Accompanying the Lebesgue decomposition, we also have versions of the Radon–Nikodym theorem for completely positive maps. There are several of these that allow for the non-commutativity of
\mathfrak{A}
in different ways. The one we need compares a map
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
to a tracial positive functional
\tau
, and originates in work of Dye
[
37
]
for the case
k=1
. Let
\lambda
be the GNS representation of
\tau
with canonical cyclic tracial vector
\xi
, and let
\mathfrak{M}:=\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
. Also, in
H_{\lambda}^{\oplus k}
, define

\xi_{i}=[0,\dots,0,\xi,0,\dots,0]^{\mathrm{T}}\qquad(i=1,2,\dots,k),

(2.18)

where only the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
coordinate of
\xi_{i}
is nonzero.

When
k=1
, the result has two parts:

a.

If
\varphi
is
\lambda
-normal, then it is actually associated to
\lambda
itself (not just to
\lambda^{\oplus\infty}
) by some vector
x\in H_{\lambda}
: see
[
34
, Thm. III.1.4]
.

b.

Any vector
x\in H_{\lambda}
is equal to
T\xi
for a unique square-integrable operator
T
affiliated to
\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
: see
[
34
, Exers. III.1.13]
or
[
22
, Prop. F.11]
.

When
k>1
, we can apply parts (a) and (b) above to the functionals
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
and
\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}
on
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})=\mathfrak{A}\otimes\mathbf{M}_{k}
. The GNS representation of
\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}
is equivalent to the tensor product of
\lambda
with the left-multiplication action of
\mathbf{M}_{k}
on itself, where
\mathbf{M}_{k}
is a Hilbert space with the normalized Hilbert–Schmidt inner product. Let us call this tensor product representation
\lambda^{(k\times k)}
. Elements of the Hilbert space
H_{\lambda}\otimes\mathbf{M}_{k}
may be written as
k
-by-
k
arrays with entries in
H_{\lambda}
, and then
\lambda^{(k\times k)}
acts on such arrays by following the rule for multiplying matrices. By regarding a
k
-by-
k
matrix as a
k
-tuple of column vectors, we have
\lambda^{(k\times k)}\simeq(\lambda^{(k)})^{\oplus k}
.

If
\varphi
is a
\lambda
-normal element of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, then
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
is
\lambda^{(k\times k)}
-normal, and so part (a) associates
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
to
\lambda^{(k\times k)}
by some
k
-by-
k
array
[x_{ij}]
with entries in
H_{\lambda}
. Passing back through the pairing isomorphism, this says that
\varphi
is associated to
\lambda^{\oplus k}
by the
k
-tuple of vectors
x_{i}:=[x_{i1},\dots,x_{ik}]^{\mathrm{T}}
,
i=1,2,\dots,k
. Then applying part (b) to each entry lets us write
x_{ij}=T_{ij}\xi
for some square-integrable operators
T_{ij}
affiliated to
\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
. Finally, by matrix-vector multiplication, this representation of each
x_{ij}
may be written as
x_{i}=T\xi_{i}
, where
T
is the square-integrable operator affiliated to
\lambda^{\oplus k}(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
that is represented by the transposed array
[T_{ji}]
(see
[
34
, Prop. I.2.4]
, which extends straightforwardly to affiliated operators).

We have reached the representation
\varphi=\Phi^{\lambda^{\oplus k}}_{T\xi_{1},\dots,T\xi_{k}}
. If
T_{1}
is another square-integrable operator affiliated to
\lambda^{\oplus k}(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
which also represents
\varphi
this way, then the uniqueness of minimal dilations shows that
T_{1}=RT
for some partial isometry
R
in
\lambda^{\oplus k}(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
, and hence
|T_{1}|=|T|
.

We collect the conclusions above as follows.

Proposition 2.8
.

If
\varphi
is a
\lambda
-normal element of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, then there is an operator
T
affiliated to
\lambda^{\oplus k}(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
such that (i)
\xi_{i}\in\mathrm{dom}\,T
for every
i
and (ii)
\varphi
is associated to
\lambda^{\oplus k}
by the tuple of vectors
T\xi_{1}
, …,
T\xi_{k}
. Another affiliated operator
T_{1}
also satisfies (i) and (ii) if and only if
|T_{1}|=|T|
. In particular, the choice of
T
is unique if we require it to be non-negative. ∎

Sometimes we need to consider all the ways in which two matrix-valued completely positive maps could ‘sit together’ inside a larger one. To describe these, we borrow a term from Furstenberg’s classic work
[
41
]
in ergodic theory. Let
k
and
\ell
be positive integers, and let

K\>=\{1,\dots,k\}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad L:=\{k+1,\dots,k+\ell\}.

For any
(k+\ell)
-by-
(k+\ell)
matrix
M
, we write
M[K]
for its
K
-by-
K
submatrix, and similarly for
L
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\varphi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
and
\psi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbf{M}_{\ell}
be completely positive. A
joining
of them is a completely positive map
\theta:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbf{M}_{k+\ell}
such that

\theta(a)[K]=\varphi(a)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\theta(a)[L]=\psi(a)\qquad(a\in\mathfrak{A}).

In particular, the
diagonal joining
is defined by

\mathrm{diag}(\varphi,\psi)(a):=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\varphi(a)&0\\
0&\psi(a)\end{array}\right]\qquad(a\in\mathfrak{A}).

This terminology is not standard in representation theory, but it is a convenient way to organize various arguments below.

Comparing with ergodic theory, the diagonal joining of two completely positive maps is the analog of the product of two invariant measures.

If
V=[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]
and
W=[w_{1},\dots,w_{\ell}]
are two tuples in a representation
\pi
, then the combined type
\Phi^{\pi}_{[V,W]}
is a joining of
\Phi^{\pi}_{V}
and
\Phi^{\pi}_{W}
. These two tuples generate orthogonal subrepresentations of
\pi
if and only if

\Phi^{\pi}_{[V,W]}=\mathrm{diag}(\Phi^{\pi}_{V},\Phi^{\pi}_{W}).

On the other hand, given any joining
\theta
of
\Phi^{\pi}_{V}
and
\Phi^{\pi}_{W}
, the minimal dilation
\pi_{\theta}
contains canonical copies of both tuples. We may therefore characterize disjointness as follows.

Lemma 2.10
.

If
\varphi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
and
\psi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathbf{M}_{\ell}
are completely positive, then they are disjoint if and only if they have no joinings other than
\mathrm{diag}(\varphi,\psi)
. ∎

In fact, starting with Furstenberg’s paper
[
41
]
, the uniqueness of the product joining is taken as the definition of ‘disjointness’ in ergodic theory, where the lack of orthogonal complements makes other definitions impractical or senseless.

2.8
Determinants of completely positive maps

Let
\lambda
be a representation of
\mathfrak{A}
with a cyclic tracial vector
\xi
, let
\tau
be the associated tracial functional on
\mathfrak{A}
, and let
\widetilde{\tau}
be its normal extension to
\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
. Let
\widetilde{\tau}
also denote the tracial positive functional defined on
\mathfrak{N}:=\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
by the same vector
\xi
. Write
\Delta
for the associated Fuglede–Kadison determinant on square-integrable operators affiliated to either von Neumann algebra.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\varphi
be a
\lambda
-normal element of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, and let
T
be an operator affiliated to
\lambda^{\oplus k}(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
that represents it as in Proposition
2.8
. Then the
Fuglede–Kadison determinant
of
\varphi
with respect to
\tau
is

\Delta_{\tau}\varphi:=(\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}|T|)^{2}.

(2.19)

For any
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, we lighten notation by defining
\Delta_{\tau}\varphi:=\Delta_{\tau}\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
.

Since any two possible choices of
T
in Definition
2.11
differ by a partial isometry, this definition is unambiguous. Intuitively, the operator
T
is an equivariant analog of the representation of a non-negative matrix
Q
as the square of another non-negative matrix
V
, and so (
2.19
) extends the formula
\det Q=(\det V)^{2}
.

We sometimes shorten
\Delta_{\tau}\varphi
to
\Delta\varphi
if the correct choice of
\tau
is clear.

Definition
2.11
has many near relatives in the literature, particularly for functionals on von Neumann algebras rather than C*-algebras. One line of these originates in Arveson’s study of subdiagonal subalgebras in
[
6
]
, which plays a key role in Section
4
below. Another is Araki’s notion of the quantum relative entropy between two normal positive functionals on a von Neumann algebra
[
4
,
5
]
. We develop the theory we need from scratch in this subsection, but we take several steps in common with those earlier works.

Because of the relation (
2.17
), we have
\Delta_{\tau}\varphi=\Delta_{\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}(\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle)
, even when
\varphi
is not
\lambda
-normal. Also, notice the un-normalized trace on the right-hand side of (
2.19
). In terms of
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}
we have instead

\Delta_{\tau}\varphi=(\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}}T)^{2k}=\big(\Delta_{\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}}(\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle)\big)^{k}.

(2.20)

We sometimes need a variant of
T
affiliated to
\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
rather than
\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
. To obtain this when
k=1
, choose
T
non-negative and set
S:=JTJ
, where
J
is the canonical involution from Subsection
2.4
. Then the properties of
J
give
\xi\in\mathrm{dom}\,S
and also the relations

S\xi=JTJ\xi=JT\xi=T^{\ast}\xi=T\xi

(2.21)

and

\Delta|S|=\exp\int_{[0,\infty)}\log s\ \widetilde{\tau}(JE(ds)J)=\exp\int_{[0,\infty)}\log t\ \widetilde{\tau}E(dt)=\Delta|T|,

(2.22)

where
E
is the spectral resolution of
|T|
and consequently
JE(\cdot)J
is the spectral resolution of
|S|
.

If
\tau=\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}
on
\mathfrak{A}=\mathbf{M}_{k}
, then for positive functionals on
\mathfrak{A}
the definition (
2.19
) reduces to the usual determinant of a positive semi-definite
k
-by-
k
matrix. On the other hand, if
k=1
,
\mathfrak{A}=C(\mathbf{T})
, and
\tau
is integration with respect to
m_{\mathbf{T}}
, then (
2.19
) equals a logarithmic integral as in Szegő’s theorem. The next example combines and generalizes these two.

Example 2.12
.

Let
\Omega
be a compact metrizable space and let
\mathfrak{A}:=C(\Omega,\mathbf{M}_{k})
. Fix a Borel probability measure
\mu
, and let
\tau
be the tracial state on
\mathfrak{A}
given by integrating
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}
with respect to
\mu
. Any continuous linear functional
\varphi
on
\mathfrak{A}
has the form

\varphi(a)=\int\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}\big(a(\omega)\cdot\nu(d\omega)\big)\qquad(a\in\mathfrak{A})

for some
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued Borel measure
\nu
on
\Omega
, and
\varphi
is positive if and only if
\nu
takes values in
\mathbf{M}_{k+}
. The functional
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
is then represented in the same way by
\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}
, the absolutely continuous part of
\nu
with respect to
\mu
in the usual sense of measure theory. The function
d\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}/d\mu
lies in
L^{1}(\mu;\mathbf{M}_{k+})
, so we can define
h\in L^{2}(\mu;\mathbf{M}_{k+})
by letting
h(\omega)
be the non-negative square root of
(d\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}/d\mu)(\omega)
. Finally, the non-negative operator
T
from Proposition
2.8
is given by pointwise multiplication by
h
acting on a dense subspace of
L^{2}(\mu;\mathbf{C}^{\oplus k})
, and we evaluate

\Delta_{\tau}\varphi=(\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}}T)^{2}=\exp\frac{1}{k}\int\log\det\frac{d\nu_{\mathrm{ac}}}{d\mu}(\omega)\ d\mu(\omega).

∎

Returning to the case of a functional
\varphi
on a general C*-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
, if
k=1
then we can approximate
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
using functionals of the form
\tau(a^{\ast}(\cdot)a)
for
a\in\mathfrak{A}
. The next lemma provides such an approximation that simultaneously works ‘in reverse’ and also approximates the determinants.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
k=1
, let
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
be associated to
\lambda
by
T\xi
as in Proposition
2.8
, let
E_{+}
be the orthogonal projection onto
(\ker T)^{\perp}
, and let
y:=E_{+}\xi
. Let
A
be any dense
\ast
-subalgebra of
\mathfrak{A}
. Then there is a sequence
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of positive invertible elements of
A
such that all of the following hold:

i.

\tau(b_{n}(\cdot)b_{n})\to\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
;

ii.

\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}(b_{n}^{-1}(\cdot)b_{n}^{-1})\to\Phi^{\lambda}_{y}
;

iii.

\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}(b_{n}^{-1}(\cdot)b_{n}^{-1})\to 0
;

iv.

(\Delta_{\tau}b_{n})^{2}\to\Delta_{\tau}\varphi
.

Proof.

Step 1.
Adjust
T
so that it is non-negative if necessary, and then let
S:=JTJ
where
J
is the canonical involution on
H_{\lambda}
, so this satisfies (
2.21
) and (
2.22
). In addition, let
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
be associated to its GNS representation
\pi_{\mathrm{sing}}
by the vector
u
. Overall,
\varphi
is associated to
\pi:=\pi_{\mathrm{sing}}\oplus\lambda
by the vector
(u,S\xi)
.

For each
\delta\in(0,1)
, let
S_{\delta}:=(S\vee\delta)\wedge\delta^{-1}
, and consider the operator

R_{\delta}:=\delta^{-1}\ \oplus\ S_{\delta}

in
\mathfrak{L}(H_{\pi})
. The projection from
H_{\pi}
to
H_{\lambda}
lies in
\pi(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}\cap\pi(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
by Lemma
2.1
, and
S_{\delta}
commutes with
\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
. Therefore the whole operator
R_{\delta}
commutes with
\pi(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
, and so
R_{\delta}
lies in
\pi(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
. We also have
\delta\leq R_{\delta}\leq\delta^{-1}
by construction.

Step 2.
As
\delta\downarrow 0
, we have

R_{\delta}^{-1}(u,0)=(\delta u,0)\to 0,

and hence
\Phi^{\pi}_{R_{\delta}^{-1}(u,0)}\to 0
by Lemma
2.3
. Similarly, Lemma
2.5
gives

R_{\delta}(0,\xi)=(0,S_{\delta}\xi)\to(0,S\xi)\qquad\hbox{and hence}\qquad\Phi^{\pi}_{R_{\delta}(0,\xi)}\to\Phi^{\lambda}_{S\xi}=\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}},

R_{\delta}^{-1}(0,S\xi)=(0,S_{\delta}^{-1}S\xi)\to(0,y)\qquad\hbox{and hence}\qquad\Phi^{\pi}_{R_{\delta}^{-1}(0,S\xi)}\to\Phi^{\lambda}_{y},

and also
\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}}S_{\delta}\to\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}}T
.

Step 3.
On the other hand, for any fixed
\delta
, we can apply the Kaplansky density theorem
[
34
, Sec. I.3.5]
to the intersection of
\pi(A)
with the set of all self-adjoint elements
R
of
\pi(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
that satisfy
\delta\leq R\leq\delta^{-1}
. That theorem gives a sequence
(b_{\delta,n})_{n\geq 1}
of positive elements of
A
such that
\delta\leq b_{\delta,n}\leq\delta^{-1}
and
\pi(b_{\delta,n})\to R_{\delta}
in the strong operator topology as
n\to\infty
. Since both inversion and
\log
can be uniformly approximated by polynomials on the interval
[\delta,\delta^{-1}]
, it follows by functional calculus that we also have
\pi(b_{\delta,n}^{-1})\to R_{\delta}^{-1}
and
\pi(\log b_{\delta,n})\to\log R_{\delta}
in the strong operator topology. From these approximations, we now also obtain

\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}(b_{\delta,n}^{-1}(\cdot)b_{\delta,n}^{-1})=\Phi^{\pi}_{\pi(b_{\delta,n}^{-1})(u,0)}\to\Phi^{\pi}_{R_{\delta}^{-1}(u,0)},

\tau(b_{\delta,n}(\cdot)b_{\delta,n})=\Phi^{\pi}_{\pi(b_{\delta,n})(0,\xi)}\to\Phi^{\pi}_{R_{\delta}(0,\xi)},

\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}(b_{\delta,n}^{-1}(\cdot)b_{\delta,n}^{-1})=\Phi^{\pi}_{\pi(b_{\delta,n}^{-1})(0,S\xi)}\to\Phi^{\pi}_{R_{\delta}^{-1}(0,S\xi)},

and
\Delta_{\tau}b_{\delta,n}\to\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}}S_{\delta}
, all as

Step 4.
Finally, in view of Lemma

can be obtained from Steps 2 and 3 by a diagonal argument.
∎

One consequence of Lemma

is the following variational principle. It is sometimes more convenient than working with Definition

directly.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let

be any dense
\ast
-subalgebra of
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
. Let
\tau
be a tracial state on
\mathfrak{A}
, and
\Delta

(\Delta\varphi)^{1/k}=\inf\big\{\langle\varphi,a\rangle:\ a\in A\ \hbox{positive and invertible and}\ \Delta_{\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}(a)\geq 1\big\}.

(2.23)

Proof.

Assume first that
k=1
, and adopt the notation from Lemma
2.13
. We prove (
2.23
) as a pair of inequalities.

If

(\Delta S)^{2}\leq\Delta S\cdot\Delta a\cdot\Delta S=\Delta(S\lambda(a)S)\leq\widetilde{\tau}(S\lambda(a)S)=\varphi(a).

The second equality holds by the multiplicativity of
\Delta
(see
[

), and the second inequality holds by (

is normalized). This proves the inequality “
\leq
” when

On the other hand, let
(b_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be the sequence in
A
given by Lemma
2.13

for each

. Then

for each

\varphi(a_{n})=(\Delta b_{n})^{2}\cdot(\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}(b_{n}^{-2})+\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}(b_{n}^{-2}))\to\Delta\varphi\cdot\Phi^{\lambda}_{y}(1).

Since

” when

Finally, if

∎

According to Definition

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
\tau
,
\lambda
, and
\Delta
be as above, and let

and
\psi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{\ell})_{+}

a.

If

b.

If

c.

The function

d.

Any joining
\theta

, with equality if

e.

If
k=\ell
and
\langle\psi,\cdot\rangle=\langle\varphi,a^{\ast}(\cdot)a\rangle
for some

(see (

\Delta_{\tau}\psi=(\Delta_{\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}|a|)^{2}\Delta_{\tau}\varphi.

(2.24)

Proof.

Proposition
2.14
expresses
\Delta\varphi
as an infimum of weak
∗
continuous positive linear functionals. From here, parts (a), (b) and (c) follow by standard arguments of infinite-dimensional convex analysis: compare
[

Part (d).
First suppose that

and

and

are the respective spectral resolutions of

\displaystyle\log\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k+\ell}}T

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,\infty)}\log t\ (\widetilde{\tau}\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k+\ell})(E(dt))

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,\infty)}\log t\ \big((\widetilde{\tau}\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k})(E_{1}(dt))+(\widetilde{\tau}\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{\ell})(E_{2}(dt))\big)

Exponentiating, and then applying this identity to the operators that represent

and

Now consider an arbitrary joining
\theta

On the other hand,

\langle\theta,a\rangle=\frac{kr^{\ell}}{k+\ell}\langle\varphi,a_{1}\rangle+\frac{\ell r^{-k}}{k+\ell}\langle\psi,a_{2}\rangle.

The infimum of this right-hand side over

, this implies the desired upper bound on

.

Part (e).
We prove this when

.

If

-normal and is represented by

).

Finally, for an arbitrary functional

, respectively.
∎

Since the function

is monotone and continuous on

\log\Delta(t\varphi+(1-t)\psi)\geq\log(t\Delta\varphi+(1-t)\Delta\psi)\geq t\log\Delta\varphi+(1-t)\log\Delta\psi.

(2.25)

The next lemma generalizes
[
6
, Cor. 4.3.4]
. Let
\Psi:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{B}
be a unital and completely positive map between C*-algebras, let
\tau_{\mathfrak{A}}
and

be tracial states on
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
respectively, and assume that
\tau_{\mathfrak{A}}:=\tau_{\mathfrak{B}}\circ\Psi
.

Lemma 2.16
.

If
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{B},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, then
\Delta_{\mathfrak{A}}(\varphi\circ\Psi)\geq\Delta_{\mathfrak{B}}\varphi
. If
\mathfrak{B}\subset\mathfrak{A}
and
\Psi|\mathfrak{B}
is the identity, then this is an equality.

Proof.

First suppose that
k=1
. Let
a
be a positive and invertible element of
\mathfrak{A}
with
\Delta_{\mathfrak{A}}a\geq 1
. The function
\log
is matrix-concave on
(0,\infty)

[
54
, Prob. 6.6.18]
, and this implies that
\log\Psi(a)\geq\Psi(\log a)

[
24
, Thm. 2.1]
. Applying
\tau_{\mathfrak{B}}
, this shows that
\Delta_{\mathfrak{B}}\Psi(a)\geq 1
, and so applying Proposition
2.14
to

gives

. Taking the infimum over

, another appeal to Proposition

turns this into

.

On the other hand, if

and

is the identity, then any positive and invertible element

of
\mathfrak{B}

is also an element of

which satisfies

and

. So this time Proposition
2.14
gives
\Delta_{\mathfrak{A}}(\varphi\circ\Psi)\leq\Delta_{\mathfrak{B}}\varphi
by taking the infimum over such

.

Finally, if

, then we reduce to the scalar-valued case by considering the algebras

and
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{B})
with the tracial states

and

and the map

, which is still completely positive.
∎

By a classic result of Tomiyama, if

, and

is a linear map of norm one such that

is the identity, then

is completely positive, so Lemma

can be applied. Such a map onto a C*-subalgebra is often called a

onto that subalgebra: see

, Sec. 1.5]
. We meet some examples in Section

3

A Szegő-like theorem over amenable groups

Throughout this section,
\Gamma
is a countable group and
\lambda
is its left regular representation with the usual cyclic vector
\xi
. This vector associates the regular character to
\lambda
. Unless stated otherwise, we write
\tau
for the resulting tracial state on
C^{\ast}\Gamma
, and
\widetilde{\tau}
for its normal extension to
\lambda(\Gamma)^{\prime\prime}
or the corresponding normal tracial functional on
\lambda(\Gamma)^{\prime}
. In all three cases the associated Fuglede–Kadison determinant is denoted by
\Delta
.

3.1
Lower bound

In this subsection we prove the inequality “
\geq
” in Theorem
A
. This direction does not require the amenability of
\Gamma
: see Corollary
3.3
below.

The proof of this inequality can be reduced quickly to the case when
\varphi
is
\lambda
-normal. For that case, the work is done by a more abstract inequality for von Neumann algebras, given in the next proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a von Neumann subalgebra of
\mathfrak{L}(H)
. Let
V=[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
be an orthonormal tuple in
H
such that the functional

\widetilde{\tau}(A):=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle Ax_{i},x_{i}\rangle\qquad(A\in\mathfrak{M})

is tracial, and let
\Delta
be the Fuglede–Kadison determinant associated to
\widetilde{\tau}
. Finally, let
T
be a non-negative operator affiliated to
\mathfrak{M}
whose domain contains
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
. Then

\det((TV)^{\ast}(TV))\geq(\Delta T)^{2k}.

If
k=1
, then
x_{1}
is a tracial vector for
\widetilde{\tau}
, and the inequality above is simply (
2.10
) for
T^{2}
. Put roughly, we prove the general case by choosing carefully a single vector in the tensor product
H^{\otimes k}
that reduces the desired inequality to this special case. More precisely, we first prove Proposition
3.1
when
T
is bounded and invertible, and then extend to the general case using Lemma
2.5
. (We could merge these steps by working with tensor products of unbounded, densely-defined operators, but the resulting technicalities seem to outweigh the advantages.)

For any bounded operator
A
on
H
, let

A_{i}:=I_{H}\otimes\cdots\otimes I_{H}\otimes A\otimes I_{H}\otimes\cdots\otimes I_{H}\in\mathfrak{L}(H^{\otimes k}),

where
A
is in the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
position. The operators
A_{1}
, …,
A_{k}
are all still bounded; they commute; and if
A
is self-adjoint then so is every
A_{i}
. The tensor product
T^{\otimes k}\in\mathfrak{L}(H^{\otimes k})
is equal to the product
A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{k}
.

In the coming proof, we apply such tensor products to vectors of the form

x_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{k}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k!}}\sum_{\pi}\mathrm{sgn}(\pi)x_{\pi(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{\pi(k)}\qquad(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in H),

(3.1)

where the sum runs over all permutations of
\{1,2,\dots,k\}
. This vector is called the
alternating product
of
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
. Since
H
is a Hilbert space, the closed span of all alternating product vectors can be identified with the alternating product space
H^{\wedge k}
: see, for instance,
[
93
, Sec. 1.5]
. If
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
are orthonormal then
x_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{k}
is a unit vector, and more generally alternating products satisfy

\langle x_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{k},y_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge y_{k}\rangle=\det[\langle x_{i},y_{j}\rangle]

(3.2)

(see, for instance,
[
93
, equation (1.10)]
).

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Step 1.
Assume first that
T
lies in
\mathfrak{M}
and has a bounded inverse. Since it is also positive definite, we can define the new self-adjoint operator
S:=\log T
by the functional calculus. Then
S_{i}
is equal to
\log T_{i}
, because this is a self-adjoint operator whose exponential equals
T_{i}
, and such an operator is unique. By the functional calculus for the commuting self-adjoint operators
T_{1}
, …,
T_{k}
, it follows that

\log T^{\otimes k}=\log(T_{1}\cdots T_{k})=S_{1}+\cdots+S_{k}.

(3.3)

Recall that
V=[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
, and let
z:={x_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{k}}
. This is a unit vector in
H^{\otimes k}
because
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
are orthonormal. Substituting from (
3.1
), it satisfies

\langle S_{1}z,z\rangle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma,\pi}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma\pi)\langle Sx_{\sigma(1)},x_{\pi(1)}\rangle\langle x_{\sigma(2)},x_{\pi(2)}\rangle\cdots\langle x_{\sigma(k)},x_{\pi(k)}\rangle.

Since
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
are orthogonal, the summand on the right vanishes unless
\sigma(2)=\pi(2)
, …,
\sigma(k)=\pi(k)
, and hence actually
\sigma=\pi
. For these summands, we have
\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma\pi)=1
, and every factor of the form
\langle x_{\sigma(i)},x_{\pi(i)}\rangle
also equals
1
. As a result, the equation above simplifies to

\langle S_{1}z,z\rangle=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\pi}\langle Sx_{\pi(1)},x_{\pi(1)}\rangle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle Sx_{i},x_{i}\rangle=\widetilde{\tau}(S)=\log\Delta T.

By symmetry, the analogous formula also holds for
S_{2}
, …,
S_{k}
. Adding these together and substituting from (
3.3
), we arrive at

k\cdot\log\Delta T=\langle S_{1}z,z\rangle+\cdots+\langle S_{k}z,z\rangle=\langle\log T^{\otimes k}z,z\rangle.

(3.4)

On the other hand, if
E
is the spectral resolution of
T^{\otimes k}
on
[0,\infty)
, then

\displaystyle\langle\log T^{\otimes k}z,z\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,\infty)}\log t\ \langle E(dt)z,z\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\int_{[0,\infty)}t^{2}\ \langle E(dt)z,z\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\langle T^{\otimes k}z,T^{\otimes k}z\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\det[\langle Tx_{i},Tx_{j}\rangle],

where we use Jensen’s inequality
[
85
, Thm. 3.3]
on the second line and (
3.2
) on the last line. Combining this calculation with (
3.4
) completes the proof.

Step 2.
Now let
T
be any unbounded non-negative operator affiliated to
T
whose domain contains
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
. Apply Step 1 to the operators
T_{\delta}
from Lemma
2.5
:

(\Delta T_{\delta})^{2k}\leq\det[\langle T_{\delta}x_{i},T_{\delta}x_{j}\rangle].

As
\delta\downarrow 0
, this inequality converges to the desired conclusion, by applying parts (ii) and (i) of Lemma
2.5
to the left- and right-hand sides, respectively.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

In the notation above, let
\omega
be the pure state on
\mathfrak{L}(H^{\otimes k})
defined by the vector
z
. The calculations above show that
\omega(S_{1})=\widetilde{\tau}(S)
for any
S\in\mathfrak{M}
, or equivalently that

\omega|\mathfrak{M}\otimes I_{H}\otimes\cdots\otimes I_{H}=\widetilde{\tau}\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1.

The same holds with
\mathfrak{M}
in any other position in the tensor product, by symmetry. However,
\omega|\mathfrak{M}^{\otimes k}
is typically not equal to
\widetilde{\tau}^{\otimes k}
: indeed, the formula for
\langle T^{\otimes k}z,z\rangle
as a determinant would violate this. This is why the application of Jensen’s inequality must be written out in terms of
z
and
E
, not simply as an instance of the infinitary determinant-trace inequality (
2.10
).
\lhd

Now let
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
be a positive definite function. As in Theorem
A
, for any finite subset
F
of
\Gamma
, we consider the
F
-by-
F

\varphi[F]:=[\varphi(g^{-1}h):\ g,h\in F].

Corollary 3.3
.

If
F
is finite and nonempty, then

\det\varphi[F]\geq(\Delta\varphi)^{|F|},

where
\Delta
is the Fuglede–Kadison determinant associated to the regular character.

Proof.

The Lebesgue decomposition gives
\varphi[F_{n}]\geq\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}[F_{n}]
in the positive definite ordering for every
n
, and so their determinants are ordered the same way
[
55
, Corollary 7.7.4(e)]
. We may therefore discard
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
and assume that
\varphi
is
\lambda
-normal.

Let
H:=H_{\lambda}
, let
\widetilde{\tau}
be normal tracial state constructed from
\lambda
and
\xi
on
\lambda(\Gamma)^{\prime}
, and let
\mathfrak{M}:=\lambda^{\oplus k}(\Gamma)^{\prime}
. Let
\xi_{1}
, …,
\xi_{k}
be the cyclic
k
-tuple for
\lambda^{\oplus k}
as in (
2.18
). This
k
-tuple satisfies

(\widetilde{\tau}\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k})(A)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle A\xi_{i},\xi_{i}\rangle\qquad(A\in\mathfrak{M}).

(3.5)

Since
\varphi
is
\lambda
-normal, Proposition
2.8
gives a non-negative operator
T
affiliated to
\mathfrak{M}
such that
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}\in\mathrm{dom}\,T
and

\varphi(g)=[\langle\lambda^{\oplus k}(g)T\xi_{j},T\xi_{i}\rangle]_{i,j}\qquad(g\in\Gamma).

(3.6)

Now define an orthonormal
k|F|
-tuple in
H
by

V:=[\lambda^{\oplus k}(g)\xi_{i}:\ i=1,\dots,k,\ g\in F].

Regarded as a unitary embedding from
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus k|F|}
into
H
, this tuple satisfies

\frac{1}{k|F|}\mathrm{tr}(V^{\ast}AV)=\frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{g\in F}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\langle A\lambda^{\oplus k}(g)\xi_{i},\lambda^{\oplus k}(g)\xi_{i}\rangle\qquad(A\in\mathfrak{M}).

Since
A
commutes with
\lambda^{\oplus k}
, this simplifies to the expression in (
3.5
). On the other hand, the definition of
V
and the fact that
T
commutes with
\lambda^{\oplus k}
give

\displaystyle\varphi[F]

\displaystyle=[\varphi(g^{-1}h):\ g,h\in F]

\displaystyle=[\langle\lambda^{\oplus k}(h)T\xi_{j},\lambda^{\oplus k}(g)T\xi_{i}\rangle:\ i,j=1,\dots,k,\ g,h\in F]

\displaystyle=[\langle T\lambda^{\oplus k}(h)\xi_{j},T\lambda^{\oplus k}(g)\xi_{i}\rangle:\ i,j=i,\dots,k,\ g,h\in F]

\displaystyle=(TV)^{\ast}(TV).

Because of this calculation and (

), we can now apply Proposition
3.1
to obtain

\det\varphi[F]=\det((TV)^{\ast}(TV))\geq(\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}}T)^{2k|F|}=(\Delta\varphi)^{|F|},

recalling (
2.20
) for the final equality.
∎

3.2
Upper bound and completed proof of Theorem

Our proof of the inequality “

” in Theorem

uses the variational principle from Proposition
2.14
. This saves us from having to handle
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
explicitly: it has already been controlled inside the proof of that principle.

This direction does require the right Følner property of
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. We apply it through the next lemma and its corollary.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
, let
a:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
be finitely supported, and let
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a right Følner sequence. There are subsets
E_{n}
of
F_{n}
such that
|F_{n}\setminus E_{n}|=o(|F_{n}|)
and

(\varphi[F_{n}]\cdot a[F_{n}])(g,h)=(\varphi\ast a)[F_{n}](g,h)\qquad\hbox{whenever}\ (g,h)\in F_{n}\times E_{n}.

Proof.

Let
S:=\{h:\ a(h)\neq 0\}
, so this is finite by assumption, and now let

E_{n}:=\{h\in F_{n}:\ hS^{-1}\subset F_{n}\}=F_{n}\cap\bigcap_{s\in S}(F_{n}s)\qquad(n=1,2,\dots).

This satisfies
|F_{n}\setminus E_{n}|=o(|F_{n}|)
by the right Følner property of
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.

For any
g,h\in F_{n}
, the definition (
2.11
) gives

(\varphi\ast a)[F_{n}](g,h)=(\varphi\ast a)(g^{-1}h)=\sum_{k}\varphi(g^{-1}k)a(k^{-1}h).

In this sum, the factor
a(k^{-1}h)
is nonzero only if
k^{-1}h\in S
, or equivalently
k\in hS^{-1}
. If
h\in E_{n}
, then
hS^{-1}\subset F_{n}
, so for these
h
the sum above agrees with

\sum_{k\in F_{n}}\varphi(g^{-1}k)a(k^{-1}h)=(\varphi[F_{n}]\cdot a[F_{n}])(g,h).

∎

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a right Følner sequence. If
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
is bounded and
a:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
is finitely supported, then

\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k|F_{n}|}\big(\varphi[F_{n}]\cdot a[F_{n}]\big)\to\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}((\varphi\ast a)(e))\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
, there are subsets
E_{n}
of
F_{n}
such that
|F_{n}\setminus E_{n}|=o(|F_{n}|)
and such that the matrix

\varphi[F_{n}]\cdot a[F_{n}]-(\varphi\ast a)[F_{n}]

vanishes in all columns indexed by
E_{n}
. Its remaining entries can be bounded using the operator norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbf{M}_{k}
and the triangle inequality, with the result that

\big|\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k|F_{n}|}\big(\varphi[F_{n}]\cdot a[F_{n}]\big)-\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k|F_{n}|}\big((\varphi\ast a)[F_{n}])\big|\\
\leq 2\cdot\Big(\sum_{g}\|a(g)\|\Big)\cdot\Big(\sup_{g}\|\varphi(g)\|\Big)\cdot\frac{|F_{n}\setminus E_{n}|}{|F_{n}|}\to 0.

Finally, every diagonal block of
(\varphi\ast a)[F_{n}]
is simply equal to
(\varphi\ast a)(e)
, so

\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k|F_{n}|}\big((\varphi\ast a)[F_{n}])=\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}((\varphi\ast a)(e)).

∎

The other ingredient we need to prove Theorem
A
is a special case of that theorem which already appears in the literature.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a right Følner sequence. Suppose that
{a:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}}
is finitely supported, positive definite, and invertible in
\mathbf{M}_{k}(C^{\ast}\Gamma)
. Then

(\det a[F_{n}])^{1/k|F_{n}|}\to\Delta_{\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}}a\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty.

∎

The earliest reference I know that includes Proposition
3.6
for general amenable groups is
[
31
, Thm. 3.2]
. The proof starts with estimates similar to Lemma
3.4
, but in which all functions on
\Gamma
are finitely supported. Using these, one shows by induction on
d
that

\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k|F_{n}|}\big(a[F_{n}]^{d}\big)\to\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}(a^{\ast d}(e))\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty,

and then by taking linear combinations that

\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k|F_{n}|}(p(a[F_{n}]))\to\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}(p(a)(e))\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty

for any polynomial
p
. Finally, the convergence of determinants follows by approximating
\log
uniformly by polynomials on a compact subinterval of
(0,\infty)
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

We prove (
1.3
) as a pair of inequalities.

Step 1.
The inequality “
\geq
” holds for every
n
individually by Corollary
3.3
.

Step 2.
Let
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
be positive definite, and let
a\in\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathbf{C}[\Gamma])
be positive definite, invertible in
\mathbf{M}_{k}(C^{\ast}\Gamma)
, and satisfy
\Delta_{\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}}a\geq 1
. Then Proposition
3.6
gives

(\det a[F_{n}])^{1/k|F_{n}|}\to\Delta_{\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}}a\geq 1\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty.

(3.7)

Define
\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}
from
\varphi
as in equation (
2.13
). This satisfies

\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}[F_{n}]=[\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}(g^{-1}h):\ g,h\in F_{n}]=[\varphi(h^{-1}g)^{\mathrm{T}}:\ g,h\in F_{n}]=(\varphi[F_{n}])^{\mathrm{T}},

where the right-hand side is the transpose of
\varphi[F]
as a
k|F|
-by-
k|F|
matrix. Consequently,
\varphi[F_{n}]
and
\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}[F_{n}]
have the same determinant.

Now the multiplicativity of determinants, the determinant-trace inequality (
2.1
), and the trace property give

\displaystyle(\det\varphi[F_{n}])^{1/k|F_{n}|}\cdot(\det a[F_{n}])^{1/k|F_{n}|}

\displaystyle=\big(\det(\sqrt{a[F_{n}]}\cdot\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}[F_{n}]\cdot\sqrt{a[F_{n}]})\big)^{1/k|F_{n}|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k|F_{n}|}\mathrm{tr}(\sqrt{a[F_{n}]}\cdot\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}[F_{n}]\cdot\sqrt{a[F_{n}]})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k|F_{n}|}\mathrm{tr}(\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}[F_{n}]\cdot a[F_{n}]).

By Corollary
3.5
and the calculation (
2.12
), the last normalized trace converges to

\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}((\varphi^{\mathrm{t}}\ast a)(e))=\langle\varphi,a\rangle

as
n\to\infty
. Combining this with (
3.7
), we have shown that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}(\det\varphi[F_{n}])^{1/k|F_{n}|}\leq\langle\varphi,a\rangle.

Taking the infimum over
a
, Proposition
2.14
completes the proof of “
\leq
”.
∎

3.3
Further remarks

Comparison with previous work

Our proof of Theorem
A
has elements in common with various proofs of Szegő’s theorem itself. Here are two examples:

•

The proof of Szegő’s theorem presented in
[
92
, Sec. 2.3]
, which is a version of Verblunsky’s original proof, uses a classical predecessor of the variational principle from Proposition
2.14
. But there it is used only to establish weak
∗
upper semicontinuity of the right-hand side of (
1.1
) as a function of
\mu
. This is a preparation for that proof of Szegő’s theorem, but not really an application inside the proof itself. In other respects, that proof of Szegő’s theorem seems essentially disjoint from our proof of Theorem
A
.

•

In case
\mu\ll m
in Szegő’s theorem, the inequality “
\geq
” is often proved by an application of Jensen’s inequality. This step essentially corresponds to our use of Proposition
3.1
above.

On the other hand, as discussed in the Introduction, most traditional proofs of Szegő’s theorem make rather explicit use of a notion of the ‘past’ of a vector under a unitary operator. In the first place, this refers to the subspace
N
in (
1.4
). In the setting of Theorem
A
we must do without this structure. A meaning of ‘past’ reappears when we turn to Theorem
B
in the next section.

In this respect, we take inspiration from research in ergodic theory that studies entropy without conditioning on the past as in (
1.5
). This program is discussed more fully in
[
43
]
. Regarding Fuglede–Kadison determinants and generalizations of Szegő’s theorem itself, some other recent precedents for our work in ergodic theory also have this flavour. We discuss these next.

For a polynomial
f
in
d
variables, a log-integral much like the right-hand side of (
1.1
) defines its ‘Mahler measure’. If
f
has integer coefficients, then it can be used to construct an action of
\mathbf{Z}^{d}
by automorphisms of a compact Abelian group. This action necessarily preserves the Haar measure of that compact group, and the Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy of this measure-preserving action turns out to equal the logarithm of the Mahler measure of
f

[
67
]
.

Starting from the observation that a Mahler measure is a Fuglede–Kadison determinant, Deninger generalized this construction to allow
f\in\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathbf{Z}[\Gamma])
for any positive integer
k
and countable group
\Gamma
. He conjectured that the Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy of the resulting system
\boldsymbol{X}_{f}
should be the Fuglede–Kadison determinant of
f
whenever
\Gamma
is amenable and
f
is non-singular as an operator on
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)^{\oplus k}
. He proved this under various extra hypotheses: see the description with further references in
[
32
]
.

Deninger proved
[
31
, Thm. 3.2]
as a step towards those results. This theorem contains our Proposition
3.6
. As Deninger describes, similar arguments already appear in earlier works on
L^{2}
-invariants in algebraic topology such as
[
69
,
35
,
87
]
.

Li made further progress in
[
66
]
, where he proved Deninger’s conjectured entropy formula whenever
\Gamma
is amenable and
f
is invertible in
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\lambda(\Gamma)^{\prime\prime})
. Among Li’s technical ingredients,
[
66
, Corollary 7.2]
strengthens Proposition
3.6
by allowing certain additional perturbations to each of the matrices
\varphi[F_{n}]
.

Finally, Li and Thom proved Deninger’s full conjecture in
[
65
]
, and generalized it further to a larger class of actions by automorphisms of compact Abelian groups. This work needed another strengthening of Proposition
3.6
as an ingredient: see
[
65
, Thm. 1.4]
, which allows positive elements of
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\lambda(\Gamma)^{\prime\prime})
that are not necessarily invertible. This is equivalent to the case of Theorem
A
when
\varphi
is
\lambda
-normal and the operator
T
from Proposition
2.8
is bounded.

Apart from these points of contact, the other details of our proof of Theorem
A
are largely disjoint from those previous works. The most obvious difference is our use of the variational principle from Proposition
2.14
. In addition, not all aspects of Szegő’s theorem make an appearance in those previous papers: for example, they all assume that
f
already lies in
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\lambda(\Gamma)^{\prime\prime})
, so there is no singular part to take care of.

Difficulties of extension beyond amenable groups

It is natural to ask about generalizations of Theorem
A
to non-amenable groups or other C*-algebras. Various directions suggest themselves, but none of them seems entirely straightforward. We quickly discuss four of these here. For simplicity, we now restrict our attention to scalar-valued positive definite functions and positive functionals.

First, Corollary
3.3
immediately gives the following: in the notation of Theorem
A
, if
\Gamma
is any countable group and
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{C}
is positive definite, then

\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}\leq\inf\big\{(\det\varphi[F])^{1/|F|}:\ F\subset\Gamma\ \hbox{finite and nonempty}\big\}.

(3.8)

Theorem
A
shows that this is an equality if
\Gamma
is amenable. I suspect that this implication can be reversed, even if we allow only certain positive definite functions:

Problem 3.7
.

Suppose that equality holds in (
3.8
) whenever
\varphi=\tau(a^{\ast}(\cdot)a)
for some
a\in\mathbf{C}[\Gamma]
. Must
\Gamma
be amenable?

If the answer here is positive and
\Gamma
is not amenable, then it might be worth looking more closely at the ‘gap’ in (
3.8
), perhaps for special elements of
\mathbf{C}[\Gamma]
such as the Laplacians corresponding to finite symmetric subsets of
\Gamma
.

Problem 3.8
.

For particular choices of
a
as in Problem
3.7
, how does the gap in (
3.8
) relate to other measures of non-amenability such as the isoperimetric profile of finite subsets of
\Gamma
?

For our second direction, let us consider the possibility of replacing finite subsets of
\Gamma
in Theorem
A
with finite quotients. This moves us to the class of residually finite groups. By allowing quotients with small defects, one could extend this further to sofic groups
[
80
]
, but we leave these aside here.

If
\Gamma
is residually finite, then it has a sequence of permutation representations
\sigma_{n}:\Gamma\to\mathrm{Sym}(V_{n})
on finite sets such that the kernels
\ker\sigma_{n}
have trivial intersection. By replacing each
\sigma_{n}
with the diagonal action of many copies of
\sigma_{n}
if necessary, we may assume further that this sequence is asymptotically free, meaning that

|\{v\in V_{n}:\ \sigma_{n}(g)v=v\}|=o(|V_{n}|)\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty\ \hbox{for every}\ g\in\Gamma\setminus e.

(3.9)

Let
\pi_{n}
be the unitary representation on
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus V_{n}}
induced by
\sigma_{n}
. Then (
3.9
) implies the convergence
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{|V_{n}|}\pi_{n}(a)\to\tau(a)
for any
a\in\mathbf{C}[\Gamma]
, and then also for any
a\in C^{\ast}\Gamma
by approximation in norm. From here one quickly reaches an analog of Proposition
3.6
: if
a\in C^{\ast}\Gamma
is positive and invertible, then

(\det\pi_{n}(a))^{1/|V_{n}|}\to\Delta a\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty.

(3.10)

The proof is closely analogous to that of Proposition
3.6
. See
[
30
, Thm. 6.1]
(which makes the slightly stronger assumption that
a
is invertible in the Banach algebra
\ell^{1}(\Gamma)
) and
[
60
, Lem. 7.2 and Thm. 7.3]
.

However, beyond (
3.10
) for positive and invertible elements
a
, a couple of serious difficulties quickly present themselves.

1.

Suppose that
a
is non-negative but not necessarily invertible. The proof of (
3.10
) depends on approximating
\log
by polynomials, and such approximations break down near the origin. On the other hand, our proof of Proposition
3.1
considers alternating products in the fixed Hilbert space of the regular representation, and has no obvious modification for the sequence of spaces
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus V_{n}}
. Absent either of these arguments, we obtain only

\limsup_{n\to\infty}(\det\pi_{n}(a))^{1/|V_{n}|}\leq\Delta a.

It turns out that this inequality really can be strict: see
[
71
, Ex. 13.69]
. This happens if
a
is non-singular and satisfies
\Delta a>-\infty
, but the finitary matrices
\pi_{n}(a)
have a few extremely small eigenvalues that drag their determinants down far below
\Delta a
.

The inequality above is still enough for some valuable applications. An example is Elek and Szabó’s proof of Lück’s determinant conjecture for sofic groups in
[
38
]
; see also
[
11
]
.

2.

The convergence in (
3.10
) can be phrased as a fact about the positive definite function
\varphi=\tau(a^{\ast}(\cdot)a)
. The square of the left-hand side of (
3.10
) is the determinant of a finite-dimensional matrix that approximates
\Delta\varphi
. But for more general positive definite functions, it is not even clear how to choose finite-dimensional matrices that could serve in such an approximation. For a finite subset
F
of
\Gamma
, we can always form
\varphi[F]
simply by restricting, but no obvious analog of ‘restriction’ applies to give matrices over the sets
V_{n}
. This is already a problem even if
\varphi
is associated to the regular representation
\lambda
, but not by a vector of the form
\lambda(a)\xi
for some
a\in\mathfrak{A}
.

Hayes discusses the first of these difficulties further near the end of
[
47
, Intro.]
. That paper concerns an analog of Deninger’s entropy formula for certain dynamical systems of algebraic origin over sofic groups. For Hayes, the change of focus to sofic entropy meant that he could sidestep the first difficulty above, and the second did not arise because sofic entropy is not defined directly as a limit of normalized Shannon entropy values. We meet a similar situation in our study of almost periodic entropy and Theorem
C
below. See also the discussion in Subsection
6.7
.

In a third direction, we could ask about generalizations of Theorem
A
to other C*-algebras besides group algebras, but retaining an assumption to play the role of ‘amenability’.

A simple motivating result can be obtained if
\mathfrak{M}
is a finite von Neumann subalgebra of
\mathfrak{L}(H)
and
\widetilde{\tau}
is a faithful normal tracial state on
\mathfrak{M}
. Let
\Delta
be the Fuglede–Kadison determinant associated to
\widetilde{\tau}
. Let
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
, …be a divergent sequence of positive integers, let
V_{n}
be an orthonormal
k_{n}
-tuple in
H
for each
n
, and let
P_{n}:=V_{n}V_{n}^{\ast}
(the orthogonal projection from
H
onto
\mathrm{img}\,V_{n}
). Finally, assume the following two properties:

•

\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k_{n}}(V_{n}^{\ast}AV_{n})=\widetilde{\tau}(A)
for every
A\in\mathfrak{M}
, and

•

\|P_{n}A-AP_{n}\|_{2}=o(\sqrt{k_{n}})
as
n\to\infty
for every
A\in\mathfrak{M}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the Hilbert–Schmidt norm.

Then

(\det V_{n}^{\ast}AV_{n})^{1/k_{n}}\to\Delta A\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty\ \hbox{for every non-negative}\ A\in\mathfrak{M}.

Indeed, the inequality “
\geq
” holds for every
n
by another appeal to Proposition
3.1
, and its reverse holds in the limit by adapting the proof of Proposition
3.6
.

Sequences
(V_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfying the two properties above have long-standing importance in the study of hyperfiniteness
[
34
, Chap. III.7]
. Indeed, among factors of type II
1
, the existence of such a sequence identifies uniquely the hyperfinite one: see
[
27
, Thm. 5.1]
and also the related discussion in
[
7
, Sec. 1]
.

Motivated by this result for von Neumann algebras, one could try to push Theorem
A
towards a class of abstract C*-algebras that satisfy a substitute for amenability such as nuclearity
[
22
, Sec. 2.3]
or quasidiagonality
[
22
, Chap. 7]
. However, I do not know precisely what statement one should try to prove. It would need to be formulated carefully to prevent versions of difficulties (1) and (2) above from re-emerging.

Problem 3.9
.

Formulate and prove a generalization of Theorem
A
for positive functionals on an abstract class of C*-algebras that generalizes the class of group C*-algebras of countable amenable groups.

For a concrete family of examples lying in this direction, one could start with C*-algebras of amenable groupoids: see
[
22
, Sec. 5.6]
for an introduction and further references.

Invariant random orders

Throughout this section,
\Gamma
is a countable group,
\lambda
is its left regular representation with the usual cyclic unit vector
\xi
, and
\chi
denotes both the regular character on
\Gamma
and also its extension to a tracial state on
C^{\ast}\Gamma
. (We use
\tau
for a different functional in this section: see (
4.4
) below.)

Our proof of Theorem
B
rests on a major result about Arveson’s subdiagonal subalgebras. When he introduced these subalgebras of finite von Neumann algebras in
[
6
]
, Arveson already proposed a generalized Szegő-type theorem for them, and showed its equivalence to two variants of Jensen’s inequality: see the discussion around
[
6
, properties 4.4.(
\alpha
),(
\beta
),(
\gamma
)]
. Arveson also proved all three assertions for several concrete examples in that paper, but not in general. The general case was finally proved by Labuschagne in
[
63
]
. We use this in the proof of Theorem
4.4
, which is itself another variant of Szegő’s theorem, and which then implies Theorem
B
fairly directly. However, we first need some preparations to make contact with this machinery.

4.1
Invariant random orders and C*-crossed products

We identify the set of all relations on
\Gamma
with
\{0,1\}^{\Gamma\times\Gamma}
, and give it the resulting product topology, which is compact and metrizable. The subset
\Omega
of all total orders is nonempty, closed, and invariant under the diagonal action of
\Gamma
on
\Gamma\times\Gamma
by left translation. For the sake of more familiar notation, we write “
g<_{\omega}h
” instead of just “
g\omega h
” when
\omega\in\Omega
. We denote the action itself by
(g,\omega)\mapsto g\cdot\omega
, so it can be expressed like this:

gh<_{g\cdot\omega}gk\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad h<_{\omega}k\qquad(g,h,k\in\Gamma).

(4.1)

Let
\mathfrak{D}:=C(\Omega,\mathbf{M}_{k})
, regarded as a C*-algebra. Then
\Gamma
acts on
\mathfrak{D}
by pre-composition, and we can form the resulting full C*-crossed product
[
22
, Sec. 4.1]
. In the sequel we need a specific construction of it. Let
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
be the vector space of all continuous
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued functions on
\Omega\times\Gamma
with compact support, made into a *-algebra with these twisted versions of multiplication and involution:

(a\ast b)(\omega,g):=\sum_{h}a(\omega,h)b(h^{-1}\cdot\omega,h^{-1}g)\quad\hbox{and}\quad a^{\ast}(\omega,g):=a(g^{-1}\cdot\omega,g^{-1})^{\ast}.

(4.2)

Then
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
is the linear span of elements that have the form

(d\otimes\delta_{g})(\omega,h):=d(\omega)\delta_{g}(h)\qquad(\omega\in\Omega,h\in\Gamma)

for some
d\in\mathfrak{D}
and
g\in\Gamma
.

Representations of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
correspond to covariant representations of
\mathfrak{D}
and
\Gamma
. In particular, any representation
\alpha
of
\mathfrak{D}
can be combined with
\lambda
to form a covariant representation on
H_{\alpha}\otimes\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
as in the paragraph prior to
[
22
, Def. 4.1.4]
, and this family of examples is faithful on
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
. Because of these examples, and often many others as well, the maximal C*-seminorm on
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
is a norm. The full C*-crossed product
\mathfrak{A}
is the resulting completion. We henceforth identify
\mathfrak{D}
with a C*-subalgebra of
\mathfrak{A}
through the map
d\mapsto d\otimes\delta_{e}
. In the reverse direction, we write
a_{g}:=a(\cdot,g)
when
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{0}
and
g\in\Gamma
. Similarly, we identify
\Gamma
with the subset
\{1_{\mathfrak{D}}\otimes\delta_{g}:\ g\in\Gamma\}
of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
.

Moreover, if
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{0}
and
\rho
is the representation of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
constructed from
\alpha
and
\lambda
, then the compression of
\rho(a)
to
H_{\alpha}\otimes\delta_{e}
is unitarily equivalent to
\alpha(a_{e})
. Since we can choose
\alpha
to be faithful, this shows that the map
a\mapsto a_{e}
extends to a conditional expectation
\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}:\mathfrak{A}\to\mathfrak{D}
.

An
invariant random order
is a
\Gamma
-invariant Borel probability measure on
\Omega
. For example, if
<
is a left-invariant total order on
\Gamma
, then
\delta_{<}
is an invariant random order. An arbitrary countable group
\Gamma
may not have a left-invariant total order, but it does always have an invariant random order via the following construction.

Example 4.1
.

Let
\overline{\Omega}:=[0,1]^{\Gamma}
, and let
\overline{\mu}
be the product measure under which each coordinate is uniformly distributed. Let
\Gamma
act by left-translation: if
\overline{\omega}=(\overline{\omega}_{h})_{h\in\Gamma}
, then
g\cdot\overline{\omega}=(\overline{\omega}_{g^{-1}h})_{h\in\Gamma}
. Now define a
\Gamma
-equivariant map
\overline{\omega}\mapsto<_{\overline{\omega}}
by

g<_{\overline{\omega}}h\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\overline{\omega}_{g}<\overline{\omega}_{h}.

If
\overline{\omega}
is drawn at random from
\overline{\mu}
, then
<_{\overline{\omega}}
is almost surely a total order because the event
\{\overline{\omega}_{g}=\overline{\omega}_{h}\}
is negligible for any distinct
g
and
h
. Its distribution on
\Omega
is an invariant random order called the
Bernoulli random order
on
\Gamma
. It is also characterized by the property that it orders any finite subset of
\Gamma
uniformly at random. This example is discussed more fully in
[
90
, Sec. 7]
. ∎

Fix an invariant random order
\mu
for the rest of this section. We can use it to construct another family of representations of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
as follows. First, for any Hilbert space
H
, let
\mathbf{M}_{k}
act on
H^{\oplus k}
by following the rules of matrix-vector multiplication, as previously. Now let
\pi
be a representation of
\Gamma
, and define a representation
\Pi
of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
on
L^{2}(\mu;H_{\pi}^{\oplus k})
like this:

[\Pi(a)F](\omega):=\sum_{h}a(\omega,h)\pi^{\oplus k}(h)\big(F(h^{-1}\cdot\omega)\big)\\
(a\in\mathfrak{A}_{0},\ F\in L^{2}(\mu;H_{\pi}^{\oplus k}),\ \omega\in\Omega).

We continue to write
\Pi
for the extension of this representation to
\mathfrak{A}
.

Let
\kappa
be the Koopman representation of
\Gamma
on
L^{2}(\mu)

[
59
, Sec. II.10]
. Then the definition above gives
\Pi|\Gamma\simeq\kappa\otimes\pi^{\oplus k}
(see
[
33
, Sec. 13.1]
for tensor products of group representations). On the other hand,
\Pi|\mathfrak{D}
is the action of
\mathfrak{D}
on
L^{2}(\mu;H_{\pi}^{\oplus k})
by pointwise multiplication.

Notice that
(\Pi|\Gamma)^{1_{\Omega}\otimes H_{\pi}^{\oplus k}}\simeq\pi^{\oplus k}
. Since
\pi
is arbitrary, this shows that our copy of
\Gamma
inside
\mathfrak{A}
generates an isomorphic copy of the whole of
C^{\ast}\Gamma
, which we henceforth identify with
C^{\ast}\Gamma
itself. In addition, if we define
\Psi_{\Gamma}:\mathfrak{A}_{0}\to\mathbf{M}_{k}[\Gamma]
by

\Psi_{\Gamma}(a)(g):=\int a_{g}(\omega)\ d\mu(\omega)\qquad(a\in\mathfrak{A}_{0},\ g\in\Gamma),

(4.3)

then the compression of
\Pi(a)
to
1_{\Omega}\otimes H_{\pi}^{\oplus k}\cong H_{\pi}^{\oplus k}
is given by
\pi^{(k)}(\Psi_{\Gamma}(a))
. Again since
\pi
is arbitrary,
\Psi_{\Gamma}
extends to a conditional expectation from
\mathfrak{A}
to
\mathbf{M}_{k}(C^{\ast}\Gamma)
.

Let
\Lambda
be the representation of
\mathfrak{A}
constructed as above when
\pi:=\lambda
, and let
{\mathfrak{M}:=\Lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}}
and
\mathfrak{N}:=\Lambda(\mathfrak{D})^{\prime\prime}
. The algebra
\mathfrak{A}
has a tracial state uniquely determined by the equation

\tau(a)=\int\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}(a_{e}(\omega))\ d\mu(\omega)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i}\langle\Lambda(a)(1_{\Omega}\otimes\xi_{i}),1_{\Omega}\otimes\xi_{i}\rangle\qquad(a\in\mathfrak{A}_{0}),

(4.4)

where
\xi_{i}
is as in (
2.18
). The first of these formulas shows that
\tau\circ\Psi_{\Gamma}=\tau\circ\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}=\tau
, and the second shows that
\tau
is
\Lambda
-normal. Its extension
\widetilde{\tau}
is a faithful normal tracial state on
\mathfrak{M}
. Letting
\widetilde{\Psi}
be Umegaki’s
\widetilde{\tau}
-preserving conditional expectation from
\mathfrak{M}
onto
\mathfrak{N}

[
22
, Lem. 1.5.11]
, another check against (
4.4
) shows that
\widetilde{\Psi}\circ\Lambda=\Lambda\circ\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}
.

4.2
A subdiagonal subalgebra

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
be the subset of all
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{0}
that satisfy

a(\omega,g)=0\qquad\hbox{whenever}\qquad g>_{\omega}e.

(4.5)

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{>}
be the further subset of those
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
which also satisfy
a_{e}=0
. Then
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}\supset\mathfrak{D}
, but
\mathfrak{A}_{>}\cap\mathfrak{D}=\{0\}
. By comparing (
4.1
) with the involution in (
4.2
),
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}^{\ast}
consists of those
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{0}
that satisfy
a(\omega,g)=0
whenever
g<_{\omega}e
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The set
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
is a unital subalgebra of
\mathfrak{A}
.

Proof.

First,
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
is a linear space by construction, and it contains the unit
1_{\mathfrak{D}}\otimes\delta_{e}
. To show that
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
is an algebra, let
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
, and consider the formula for
a\ast b
in (
4.2
). Suppose that
g>_{\omega}e
. Then there are two possibilities for each summand in that formula:

•

If
h>_{\omega}e
, then
a(\omega,h)=0
is zero and the summand vanishes.

•

If
e\geq_{\omega}h
, then
g>_{\omega}h
by transitivity, and hence
h^{-1}g>_{h^{-1}\cdot\omega}e
by (
4.1
). This implies that
b(h^{-1}\cdot\omega,h^{-1}g)=0
, and again the summand vanishes.

Therefore
a\ast b
also belongs to
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
.
∎

We can now make contact with a subdiagonal subalgebra. The next lemma generalizes the results of
[
6
, Subsec. 3.2]
‘relative to
\Omega
’.

Lemma 4.3
.

The image
\Lambda(\mathfrak{A}_{\geq})
is a subdiagonal subalgebra of
\mathfrak{M}
relative to
\widetilde{\Psi}
, and
\Lambda(\mathfrak{A}_{>})=\Lambda(\mathfrak{A}_{\geq})\cap\ker\widetilde{\Psi}
.

Proof.

The image
\Lambda(\mathfrak{A}_{\geq})
is a unital subalgebra of
\mathfrak{M}
by Lemma
4.2
, so we need only verify the first three of Arveson’s axioms from
[
6
, Def. 2.1.1]
:

i.

Any element
a
of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
may be decomposed according to

a(\omega,g)=a(\omega,g)1_{\{g\leq_{\omega}e\}}+a(\omega,g)1_{\{g>_{\omega}e\}},

and both summands are still elements of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
. Therefore
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}+\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}^{\ast}
equals the whole of
\mathfrak{A}_{0}
. This is norm dense in
\mathfrak{A}
by construction, and
\Lambda(\mathfrak{A})
is ultraweakly dense in
\mathfrak{M}

[
34
, Thm. I.3.2]
.

ii.

If
a,b\in\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
, then the multiplication from (
4.2
) gives

(a\ast b)_{e}(\omega)=\sum_{h}a(\omega,h)b(h^{-1}\cdot\omega,h^{-1}).

Consider the summands on the right. If
h>_{\omega}e
, then
a(\omega,h)=0
. On the other hand, if
h<_{\omega}e
, then (
4.1
) gives
e<_{h^{-1}\cdot\omega}h^{-1}
, and hence
{b(h^{-1}\cdot\omega,h^{-1})=0}
. So all terms with
h\neq e
vanish, and we are left with

\widetilde{\Psi}(\Lambda(a)\Lambda(b))=\Lambda((a\ast b)_{e})=\Lambda(a_{e})\Lambda(b_{e})=\widetilde{\Psi}(\Lambda(a))\widetilde{\Psi}(\Lambda(b)).

iii.

We have
\widetilde{\Psi}(\Lambda(\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}))=\Lambda(\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}(\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}))=\Lambda(\mathfrak{D})\subset\Lambda(\mathfrak{A}_{\geq})\cap\Lambda(\mathfrak{A}_{\geq})^{\ast}
.

The final equality also follows from the fact that
\widetilde{\Psi}\circ\Lambda=\Lambda\circ\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}
.
∎

4.3
Wandering vectors and completion of the proof

We prove Theorem
B
via the more abstract reformulation in Theorem
4.4
below. This is a version of Schur’s determinantal formula
[
55
, Subs. 0.8.5]
for a positive functional on
\mathfrak{A}
. Because we have allowed
\mathfrak{D}
to consist of
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued functions from the outset, we obtain a result for
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued completely positive maps as well via the pairing isomorphism.

We begin with the following classical notion. In a representation
\Pi
of
\mathfrak{A}
, a vector
y
in
H_{\Pi}
is called
wandering
if
y
is orthogonal to
\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})y
(compare
[
6
, Sec. 4.1]
, which uses the term ‘right-wandering’). In this case the following also hold:

\displaystyle\langle\Pi(d)y,\Pi(a)y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle y,\Pi(d^{\ast}a)y\rangle=0\qquad(d\in\mathfrak{D},\ a\in\mathfrak{A}_{>});

\displaystyle\langle\Pi(d)y,\Pi(a^{\ast})y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Pi(ad)y,y\rangle=0\qquad(d\in\mathfrak{D},\ a\in\mathfrak{A}_{>});

\displaystyle\langle\Pi(a_{1}^{\ast})y,\Pi(a_{2})y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle y,\Pi(a_{1}a_{2})y\rangle=0\qquad(a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathfrak{A}_{>}).

Therefore the subspaces
\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>}^{\ast})y}
,
\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{D})y}
and
\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})y}
are orthogonal.

Any
b\in\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}+\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}^{\ast}
may be expressed as
\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}(b)+a_{1}+a_{2}^{\ast}
with
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathfrak{A}_{>}
. Using this decomposition and the fact that
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}+\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}^{\ast}
is norm dense in
\mathfrak{A}
, it follows that a vector
y
is wandering if and only if
\Phi_{y}^{\Pi}=\Phi_{y}^{\Pi}\circ\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}
.

Now consider an arbitrary vector
x
in
H_{\Pi}
. We define its
wandering part

y
to be the component of
x
orthogonal to the subspace
\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})x}
. This
y
lies in
x+\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})x}
, and therefore

\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})y\subset\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})(x+\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})x})\subset\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})x}.

This subset is orthogonal to
y
by construction, so
y
is indeed wandering. Finally, if
\varphi=\Phi^{\Pi}_{x}
, then we call
\Phi^{\Pi}_{y}
the
Schur complement
of
\varphi
relative to
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
. This name is unambiguous because of the uniqueness of the GNS representation of
\varphi
. This is an abstraction of the generalized Schur complement of a submatrix in a larger Gram matrix
[
55
, Exers. 7.1.P28, 7.3.P8]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

In the situation above, we have
\Delta_{\tau}\varphi=\Delta_{\tau|\mathfrak{D}}(\psi|\mathfrak{D})
.

If
x
is already wandering, then
\psi=\varphi
, and Theorem
4.4
is an instance of Lemma
2.16
for the conditional expectation
\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}
. We use this special case in the course of proving the full theorem. The other main ingredient for the proof is the Arveson–Labuschagne Jensen inequality for maximal subdiagonal subalgebras from
[
63
]
. We use that inequality via the following consequence.

Proposition 4.5
.

Every
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{>}
satisfies
\Delta|1+a|\geq 1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
and
[
6
, Thm. 2.2.1]
,
\Lambda(\mathfrak{A}_{\geq})
is contained in a unique maximal subdiagonal subalgebra of
\mathfrak{M}
. This provides the correct setting for the Arveson–Labuschagne version of Jensen’s formula
[
63
, Thm. 3]
. That formula gives us the inequality step here:

\Delta|1+a|=\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}}|\Lambda(1+a)|\geq\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}}|\widetilde{\Psi}(\Lambda(1+a))|=\Delta_{\widetilde{\tau}}|\Lambda(1+\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}(a))|=1.

∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\Pi
be a representation of
\mathfrak{A}
, let
x\in H_{\Pi}
, and let
w
lie in
x+\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})x}
. Then
\Delta\Phi^{\Pi}_{w}\geq\Delta\Phi^{\Pi}_{x}
.

Proof.

Choose a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in
\mathfrak{A}_{>}
so that
w_{n}:=(1+\Pi(a_{n}))x\to w
as
n\to\infty
. Then
\Phi^{\Pi}_{w_{n}}=\Phi^{\Pi}_{x}((1+a_{n})^{\ast}(\cdot)(1+a_{n}))
, and by Lemma
2.3
these converge to
\Phi^{\Pi}_{w}
. On the other hand, Propositions
2.15
(e) and then
4.5
give

\Delta\Phi^{\Pi}_{w_{n}}=(\Delta|1+a_{n}|)^{2}\cdot\Delta\Phi^{\Pi}_{x}\geq\Delta\Phi^{\Pi}_{x}.

As
n\to\infty
the result follows by upper semicontinuity (Proposition
2.15
(c)).
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Abbreviate
\Delta_{\tau}
to
\Delta
for the rest of this proof. Corollary
4.6
applies to the wandering part
y
of
x
to give
\Delta\psi\geq\Delta\varphi
.

Now let
K_{<}:=\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A}^{\ast}_{>})y}
,
K_{0}:=\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{D})y}
and
K_{>}:=\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})y}
. Since
y
is wandering,
\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A})y}
is the orthogonal sum of
K_{<}
,
K_{0}
and
K_{>}
. If it happens that
x
lies in
y+K_{>}
, then we can swap the roles of
x
and
y
in Corollary
4.6
to complete the proof. This condition on
x
may fail in general, but we can adapt the argument as follows.

Let
z
be the orthogonal projection of
x
onto
\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A})y}
. This is the sum of the projections of
x
onto
K_{<}
,
K_{0}
and
K_{>}
, because those subspaces are orthogonal. Now, the definition of
y
gives

\displaystyle\langle\Pi(a^{\ast})y,x\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle y,\Pi(a)x\rangle=0\qquad(a\in\mathfrak{A}_{>})

\displaystyle\hbox{and}\qquad\langle\Pi(d)y,(x-y)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle y,\Pi(d^{\ast})(x-y)\rangle=0\qquad(d\in\mathfrak{D}).

So
x
is orthogonal to
K_{<}
, and its projection onto
K_{0}
is equal to
y
itself. It follows that
z\in y+K_{>}
, and so Corollary
4.6
gives
\Delta\Phi^{\Pi}_{z}\geq\Delta\psi\geq\Delta\varphi
. On the other hand,
z
is the projection of
x
onto a subrepresentation of
\Pi
, so
\Phi^{\Pi}_{z}\leq\varphi
in the positive definite ordering (as we saw in the construction of the Lebesgue decomposition, for example). Because of this, Proposition
2.15
(a) gives
\Delta\Phi^{\Pi}_{z}\leq\Delta\varphi
. So in fact we must have
\Delta\Phi^{\Pi}_{z}=\Delta\psi=\Delta\varphi
.

Finally, since
y
is wandering, Lemma
2.16
gives
\Delta\psi=\Delta_{\tau|\mathfrak{D}}(\psi|\mathfrak{D})
.
∎

It remains to deduce Theorem
B
from Theorem
4.4
. Let
\varphi:\Gamma\to\mathbf{M}_{k}
be positive definite, and write
\varphi
also for its extension to a completely positive map on
C^{\ast}\Gamma
. Let
\varphi
be associated to
\pi
by the cyclic tuple
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}\in H_{\pi}
.

For each
\omega\in\Omega
, define

N_{\omega}:=\overline{\mathrm{span}}\{\pi(g)x_{i}:\ g<_{\omega}e,\ i=1,\dots,k\}\qquad(\omega\in\Omega).

(4.6)

These are the measurably-varying subspaces that appear in the statement of Theorem
B
. They form a measurable field of closed subspace of
H_{\pi}
(see
[
34
, Chap. II.1]
). Let
R_{\omega}
be the orthogonal projection from
H_{\pi}
to
N_{\omega}
.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

Let us start by re-writing (
1.8
) like this:

\exp\frac{1}{k}\int\log\det[\langle R_{\omega}^{\perp}x_{j},R_{\omega}^{\perp}x_{i}\rangle]\ d\mu(\omega)=(\Delta_{\chi}\varphi)^{1/k}

(4.7)

By (
2.20
), the right-hand side of (
4.7
) is equal to
\Delta_{\chi\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}}(\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle)
, and now by (
4.4
) and the second part of Lemma
2.16
this is equal to
\Delta_{\tau}\varphi^{\prime}
, where the positive functional
\varphi^{\prime}:=\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle\circ\Psi_{\Gamma}
on
\mathfrak{A}
is associated to
\Pi
by the vector

x:=1_{\Omega}\otimes k^{-1/2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]^{\mathrm{T}}.

Next, by Corollary
4.4
,
\Delta_{\tau}\varphi^{\prime}
is equal to
\Delta_{\tau|\mathfrak{D}}(\psi|\mathfrak{D})
, where
\psi
is the Schur complement of
\varphi^{\prime}
. It remains to evaluate
\psi|\mathfrak{D}
and show that
\Delta_{\tau|\mathfrak{D}}(\psi|\mathfrak{D})
is given by the left-hand side of (
4.7
).

The wandering part
y=[y_{1},\dots,y_{k}]^{\mathrm{T}}
is the component of
x
orthogonal to the closure of the subspace
\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})x
. This subspace consists of the functions in
L^{2}(\mu;H^{\oplus k}_{\pi})
that have the form

\sum_{g}a(\omega,g)[\pi(g)x_{1},\dots,\pi(g)x_{k}]^{\mathrm{T}}\qquad(\omega\in\Omega)

for some
a\in\mathfrak{A}_{>}
. Because of the property (
4.5
), the sum above is a vector in
N^{\oplus k}_{\omega}
. On the other hand, we can regard
a
as a
k
-by-
k
matrix of complex-valued functions on
\Omega\times\Gamma
, and choose each of those functions independently subject to (
4.5
). Therefore a measurable selection argument followed by an approximation by continuous functions shows that

\overline{\Pi(\mathfrak{A}_{>})x}=\int^{\oplus}_{\Omega}N_{\omega}^{\oplus k}\ d\mu(\omega),

understanding this direct integral as a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\mu;H_{\pi}^{\oplus k})
(see
[
34
, Chap. II.1]
). Consequently,
y_{i}(\omega)=k^{-1/2}R_{\omega}^{\perp}x_{i}
as elements of
L^{2}(\mu;H_{\pi})
, and so

\psi(d)=\langle\Pi(d)y,y\rangle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i,j}\int d_{ij}(\omega)\langle R_{\omega}^{\perp}x_{j},R_{\omega}^{\perp}x_{i}\rangle\ d\mu(\omega)\qquad(d\in\mathfrak{D}).

Finally, the analysis from Example
2.12
shows that
\Delta_{\tau|\mathfrak{D}}(\psi|\mathfrak{D})
is equal to the left-hand side of (
4.7
).
∎

4.4
Further remarks

Non-self-adjoint subalgebras of C*-algebras similar to our
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
have been studied by several authors. In particular, Kawamura and Tomiyama introduced C*-subdiagonal subalgebras in
[
57
, Sec. 3]
. Our example
\mathfrak{A}_{\geq}
is a C*-subdiagonal subalgebra of
\mathfrak{A}
when
\Gamma
is amenable. When
\Gamma
is non-amenable, this fails because the conditional expectation
\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}
is not faithful, and so the full and reduced C*-crossed products differ (compare
[
22
, Cor. 5.6.17]
). We do not need the faithfulness of
\Psi_{\mathfrak{D}}
here, so have introduced our examples from scratch. Our examples also belong to a more general family of subalgebras of groupoid C*-algebras for totally ordered groupoids: see
[
77
]
, which also includes background and further references on groupoid C*-algebras. Indeed, besides technicalities around faithfulness, our Lemma
4.3
is mostly the same as the forward direction in
[
77
, Thm. 4.2]
. It could be interesting to generalize our results to the broader setting suggested by that paper.

Fuglede–Kadison determinants of matrices over group von Neumann algebras appear in connections with various other parts of mathematics. For example, connections to
L^{2}
-invariants in topology are described in
[
71
, Sec. 3.2 and Chap. 13]
. See that reference and also
[
62
]
for an overview of open problems in this direction, such as Lück’s approximation and determinant conjectures. The latter is generalized to the ‘measure-theoretic determinant conjecture’ in
[
70
]
, and shown to have consequences for uniform measure equivalence of discrete groups. I expect that Theorem
B
generalizes in a similar way, for instance by using a version of Example
4.1
over a unimodular random network.

Problem 4.7
.

Does the construction of a subdiagonal subalgebra from an invariant random order shed any light on these open questions about
L^{2}
-invariants?

Hayes’ paper
[
50
]
studies certain probability-preserving systems of algebraic origin for a finitely generated group
\Gamma
with a left invariant total order. They are constructed from elements of
\mathbf{Z}[\Gamma]
that he calls ‘lopsided’, which are defined in terms of the order. His main result is that a nondegenerate lopsided group-ring element always gives a factor of a Bernoulli shift.

Problem 4.8
.

Can coupling to an invariant random order generalize Hayes’ construction and result to other finitely generated groups?

5

Approximate association and almost periodic sequences

This section makes more preparations for our introduction of ‘almost periodic entropy’, the new notion that appears in Theorem
C
.

5.1
Typical vectors and approximate association

Consider again a general separable, unital C*-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
. We can classify tuples of vectors in a representation
\pi
of
\mathfrak{A}
according to their type.

Definition 5.1
.

For any positive integer
k
and subset
O
of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})
, let

\mathcal{X}(\pi,O):=\big\{[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]^{\mathrm{T}}\in H_{\pi}^{\oplus k}:\ \Phi^{\pi}_{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}}\in O\big\}.

The elements of
\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)
are the
O
-typical
tuples of the representation
\pi
. In addition, let

\Sigma_{k}(\pi):=\big\{\Phi^{\pi}_{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}}:\ v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in H_{\pi}\ \hbox{and}\ \|v_{1}\|^{2}+\cdots+\|v_{k}\|^{2}=k\big\}.

This is the subset of elements of
\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
that are associated to
\pi
.

The transpose in the definition of
\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)
is not conceptually significant, but it simplifies some manipulations later.

If
O\subset\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
, observe that

O\cap\Sigma_{k}(\pi)\neq\emptyset\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)\neq\emptyset

(5.1)

for any representation
\pi
.

We often use Definition
5.1
when
O
is a small neighbourhood of a given ‘target’ completely positive map
\varphi
. In this case we may informally describe elements of
\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)
as ‘approximately
\varphi
-typical tuples’. This resembles the use of terms such as ‘microstate’ in free probability or ‘good model’ in the study of sofic entropy in ergodic theory: compare
[
21
, Sec. 2.3]
, for example.

Let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, let
O
be a neighbourhood of
\varphi
, and let
\widetilde{O}
be the corresponding neighbourhood of
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
under the pairing isomorphism. Then Lemma
2.6
tells us that

\mathcal{X}(\pi^{(k)},\widetilde{O})=k^{-1/2}\mathcal{X}(\pi,O).

(5.2)

If
\pi
is a representation and
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, then
\varphi
is
approximately associated
to
\pi
if it lies in the closure of the set of completely positive maps associated to
\pi
. Because of the second countability from Lemma
2.7
, this holds if and only if there is a sequence of
k
-tuples
[v_{n,1},\dots,v_{n,k}]
in
H_{\pi}
such that

\Phi^{\pi}_{v_{n,1},\dots,v_{n,k}}(a)\to\varphi(a)\qquad\hbox{for every}\ a\in\mathfrak{A}\ \hbox{as}\ n\to\infty.

(5.3)

If
\varphi
is approximately associated to
\pi
and also normalized, and if the tuples
v_{n,1}
, …,
v_{n,k}
witness the convergence in (
5.3
), then we can normalize those tuples slightly to show that
\varphi
actually lies in the weak
∗
closure
\overline{\Sigma_{k}(\pi)}
. Let us call
\overline{\Sigma_{k}(\pi)}
the
k
-summary
of
\pi
.

The literature makes more use of a slightly coarser notion:
\varphi
is
weakly associated
to
\pi
if
\varphi
can be approximated by
convex combinations
of maps associated to
\pi
. When
k=1
, this is the basis for Godemont and Fell’s relation of weak containment of representations
[
33
, Sec. 3.4]
. One can define a similar but finer relation on representations as the inclusion of
k
-summaries for all
k
, without allowing convex combinations. Some references call this ‘weak containment in the sense of Zimmer’ because of its appearance in
[
101
, Def. 7.3.5]
. However, in that reference Zimmer himself attributes the idea to Fell. While I have not found this precise definition in Fell’s papers, it is suggested rather naturally by his study of the quotient topology in
[
39
,
40
]
. Moreover, by results of Voiculescu
[
97
]
(see also
[
7
]
), two separable representations have equal
k
-summaries for all
k
if and only if they are approximately unitarily equivalent. I have chosen the term ‘approximate association’ to reflect this last connection.

Approximate association can sometimes detect multiplicities in the GNS representation of a positive functional, whereas weak association cannot.

Example 5.2
.

Let
\pi
be an irreducible representation whose dimension is finite but at least
2
. Let
x
and
y
be linearly independent unit vectors in
H_{\pi}
, and let
\varphi:=\Phi^{\pi}_{x}
and
\psi:=\Phi^{\pi}_{y}
. These are both associated to
\pi
, and they are linearly independent by the uniqueness of the GNS construction and the irreducibility of
\pi
. However,
(\varphi+\psi)/2
cannot be associated to
\pi
, because it is not pure, and so its GNS representation must be the whole of
\pi^{\oplus 2}
. Moreover, these facts persist if we require only approximate association, because the finite dimensionality of
\pi
implies that
\Sigma_{1}(\pi)
and
\Sigma_{1}(\pi^{\oplus 2})
are already compact sets, without taking closures. So
\Sigma_{1}(\pi)
is not convex in this example. On the other hand,
(\varphi+\psi)/2
is certainly weakly associated to
\pi
.
\lhd

Our notion of almost periodic entropy (Definition
6.4
below) involves approximations to a positive functional, but it does not allow for taking convex combinations. For this reason, approximate association plays a larger role than weak association in the present paper.

\ell

k
matrix
[a_{ij}]
of elements of
\mathfrak{A}
. If
\pi
is a representation and
{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}}\in H_{\pi}

\ell

in

using

and

as in formula (

is then related to the type of

by Lemma

. Because of the weak
∗

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\psi
be obtained from
\varphi
and

as in Lemma

. For any neighbourhood
O
of
\psi
, there is a neighbourhood

of

\big\{[\pi(a_{ij})]\cdot[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]^{\mathrm{T}}:\ [v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)\big\}\subset\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)

In particular, suppose that

\psi(b)=(Q^{\mathrm{T}})^{\ast}\varphi(b)Q^{\mathrm{T}}\qquad(b\in\mathfrak{A})

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
and
\psi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{\ell})_{+}
, and assume that
\psi
is approximately associated to

. Then for any neighbourhood

.

Proof.

Let
\varphi

by the cyclic tuple

, …,
x_{k}
. By cyclicity and Lemma
2.3
, there is some
\ell
-by-
k

of elements of

∎

5.2
Pairs and sums of typical tuples

Let
k
and
\ell
be positive integers, and let

K\>=\{1,\dots,k\}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad L:=\{k+1,\dots,k+\ell\}.

Let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
and
\psi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{\ell})_{+}
. The next lemma is a robust form of Lemma
2.10
.

Lemma 5.5
.

If
\varphi
and
\psi
are disjoint, then for every neighbourhood
O
of
\mathrm{diag}(\varphi,\psi)
there are neighbourhoods
U
of
\varphi
and
V
of
\psi
such that the following holds:

If
\theta\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k+\ell})_{+}
satisfies
\theta[K]\in U
and
\theta[L]\in V
, then
\theta\in O
.

These neighbourhoods satisfy

\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)\supset\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)\times\mathcal{X}(\pi,V)

for any representation
\pi
(regarding the right-hand side as a subset of
H_{\pi}^{\oplus(k+\ell)}
).

Proof.

We prove the first conclusion by contraposition. Assume that
\mathrm{diag}(\varphi,\psi)
has a neighbourhood
O
for which no pair of neighbourhoods
U
and
V
gives the desired implication. Then, by the second countability from Lemma
2.7
, there is a sequence
(\theta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
in
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}\setminus O
such that

\theta_{n}[K]\to\varphi\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\theta_{n}[L]\to\psi.

This sequence
(\theta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
must be uniformly bounded in the dual norm because of the identity (
2.16
) and the relation

\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k+\ell}\theta_{n}(1_{\mathfrak{A}})=\frac{k}{k+\ell}\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}\big(\theta_{n}(1_{\mathfrak{A}})[K]\big)+\frac{\ell}{k+\ell}\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{\ell}\big(\theta_{n}(1_{\mathfrak{A}})[L]\big).

Therefore, by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem,
(\theta_{n})_{n\geq 1}
has a subsequential limit in the weak
∗
topology. This limit must be a joining of
\varphi
and
\psi
, but also it cannot lie in
O
and so it must be different from
\mathrm{diag}(\varphi,\psi)
. Therefore
\varphi
and
\psi
are not disjoint.

The second conclusion follows from the first one and the definition of type.
∎

The assumption of disjointness in Lemma
5.5
is not superfluous. Indeed, if
\pi_{\varphi}
is finite-dimensional and irreducible, then
\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)
is nonempty for any neighourhood
U
of
\varphi
, but
\mathrm{diag}(\varphi,\varphi)
is not necessarily approximately associated to
\pi
, only to
\pi^{\oplus 2}
, by reasoning similar to Example
5.2
.

Now we consider typical vectors for the sum
\gamma:=\varphi+\psi
. Here we restrict our attention to the case
k=\ell=1
. The case
k=\ell>1
would not involve any new ideas, but would require heavier notation. Later in the paper we use the pairing isomorphism to avoid needing that case.

Lemma 5.6
.

Assume that
k=\ell=1
and that
\varphi
and
\psi
are disjoint. For any neighbourhoods
U
of
\varphi
and
V
of
\psi
there are a neighbourhood
W
of
\gamma
and an element
a
of
\mathfrak{A}
such that

\{(\pi(a)x,\pi(1-a)x):\ x\in\mathcal{X}(\pi,W)\}\subset\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)\times\mathcal{X}(\pi,V)

for any representation
\pi
.

Proof.

The representations
\pi_{\varphi}
and
\pi_{\psi}
are disjoint by assumption, and they are both contained in
\pi_{\gamma}
(see
[
33
, Prop. 2.5.1]
, for example). Therefore, by the uniqueness of GNS representations, we may identify
\pi_{\gamma}
with
{\pi_{\varphi}\oplus\pi_{\psi}}
. The resulting orthogonal projection
P
from

to
H_{\varphi}
lies in the centre of
\pi_{\gamma}(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
by Lemma
2.1
.

Now suppose that
\gamma
is associated to
\pi_{\gamma}
by the cyclic vector
v
. Then
\varphi
and
\psi
are associated to
\pi_{\gamma}
by
Pv
and
v-Pv
, respectively. The Kaplansky density theorem
[
34
, Sec. I.3.5]
applied to
\pi_{\gamma}(\mathfrak{A})
gives an element

such that
0\leq a\leq 1
and such that
\pi_{\gamma}(a)v
lies as close as we wish to

. In particular, we may choose

so that

\Phi^{\pi_{\gamma}}_{\pi_{\gamma}(a)v}=\gamma(a^{\ast}(\cdot)a)\in U\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\Phi^{\pi_{\gamma}}_{\pi_{\gamma}(1-a)v}=\gamma((1-a)^{\ast}(\cdot)(1-a))\in V.

Now two applications of Lemma
5.3
produce the required neighbourhood

.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

Assume that
k=\ell=1
and that
\varphi
and
\psi
are disjoint.

a.

For every neighbourhood
W
of
\gamma
there are neighbourhoods
U
of
\varphi
and
V
of
\psi
such that

\mathcal{X}(\pi,W)\supset\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)+\mathcal{X}(\pi,V)

for any representation
\pi
.

b.

For any neighbourhoods
U
of
\varphi
and
V
of
\psi
there is a neighbourhood
W
of
\gamma
such that

\mathcal{X}(\pi,W)\subset\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)+\mathcal{X}(\pi,V)

for any representation
\pi
.

Proof.

First let
W
be a neighbourhood of
\gamma
. Given a representation
\pi
and vectors

,

, observe that

\Phi^{\pi}_{x+y}(a)=\Phi^{\pi}_{x}(a)+\Phi^{\pi}_{y}(a)+\langle\pi(a)x,y\rangle+\langle\pi(a)y,x\rangle\qquad(a\in\mathfrak{A}).

We may therefore choose a neighbourhood

of

such that

On the other hand, given the neighbourhoods

of

, the conclusion of Lemma

∎

5.3
Strong-quotient convergence of representations

For each
k
, the space
\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
is compact by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, and metrizable because
\mathfrak{A}
is separable. Let
\mathcal{K}(\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A}))
be its hyperspace (the set of all its compact subsets), and endow this with the Vietoris topology
[
58
, Subsec. I.4.F]
. This topology is again compact and metrizable, for instance using a Hausdorff metric constructed from a suitable metric on
\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})

[
58
, Thm. 4.26]
. We can therefore describe it in terms of sequences. Fix
k
, and let
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{K}(\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A}))
. Its
topological upper
and
lower limits
are the sets

\mathrm{T}\limsup_{n}K_{n}:=\{\varphi\in\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A}):\ \hbox{every neighbourhood of $\varphi$}\\
\hbox{meets $K_{n}$ for infinitely many $n$}\}

and

\mathrm{T}\liminf_{n}K_{n}:=\{\varphi\in\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A}):\ \hbox{every neighbourhood of $\varphi$}\\
\hbox{meets $K_{n}$ for all sufficiently large $n$}\},

respectively. Then
(K_{n})_{n\geq 1}
Vietoris converges if and only if its topological upper and lower limits are equal, and in this case that common set is
\lim_{n}K_{n}
.

If
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of representations, then we say that it
strong-quotient converges
if the
k
-summaries
\overline{\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})}
Vietoris converge to a limit in
\mathcal{K}(\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A}))
as
n\to\infty
for every
k
. More abstractly, for any family
R
of either separable representations or equivalence classes of such representations, we define the
strong-quotient topology
on
R
to be the topology generated by the sequence of maps
\pi\mapsto\overline{\Sigma_{k}(\pi})
into the spaces
\mathcal{K}(\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A}))
.

As far as I know, this topology was first studied by Abért and Elek in
[
1
]
, but without this terminology. As inspiration they cite the notion of ‘local-global convergence’ for sequencess of sparse finite graphs
[
14
,
46
]
. Abért and Elek focused on the analogous mode of convergence for measure-preserving group actions, but their paper indicates the story for unitary representations as well. I use the term ‘strong-quotient’ because convergence in this topology strengthens both ‘strong convergence’ of representations, as surveyed in
[
76
]
, and also convergence in Fell’s ‘quotient topology’
[
39
, Sec. 3]
.

By Voiculescu’s results from
[
97
]
, the strong-quotient topology is actually pulled back from a topology on approximate unitary equivalence classes of separable representations, and these are parametrized by their images in
\prod_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{K}(\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A}))
. According to the C*-variant of
[
1
, Thm. 1]
, the subset of all such images of representations is closed, and hence compact. We do not depend on this fact in the sequel, but if desired it provides an interpretation of sequential strong-quotient limits as representations themselves, not just elements of
\prod_{k\geq 1}\mathcal{K}(\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A}))
.

The next definition is a variant of approximate association.

Definition 5.8
.

A map
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
is
asymptotically associated
to a sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of separable representations if
\varphi
lies in
\mathrm{T}\limsup_{n}\overline{\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})}
.

The choice of
\mathrm{T}\limsup
rather
\mathrm{T}\liminf
here is a matter of convention. If
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
strong-quotient converges then this choice makes no difference.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of separable representations, and let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
. Then
\varphi
is asymptotically associated to
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
if and only if
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
is asymptotically associated to
(\pi_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 1}
.

Proof.

For each neighbourhood
O
of
\varphi
, let
\widetilde{O}
be the corresponding neighbhourhood of
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
under the pairing isomorphism. As
O
runs over all neighbourhoods of
\varphi
, likewise
\widetilde{O}
runs over all neighbourhoods of
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
. The result follows from this and the relations (
5.1
) and (
5.2
).
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
and
\psi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{\ell})_{+}
. Assume that
\boldsymbol{\pi}
strong-quotient converges. If
\varphi
and
\psi
are disjoint and they are both asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, then so is
\mathrm{diag}(\varphi,\psi)
, and if
k=\ell
then so is
\varphi+\psi
.

Proof.

Let
O
be any neighbourhood of
\mathrm{diag}(\varphi,\psi)
. Lemma
5.5
gives neighbourhoods
U
of
\varphi
and
V
of
\psi
such that

\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)\supset\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},U)\times\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},V)

for all
n
. By asymptotic association and strong-quotient convergence, both factors in this Cartesian product are nonempty for all sufficiently large
n
, and so
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)
is also nonempty for these
n
.

If
k=\ell=1
, then the sum
\varphi+\psi
is handled in the same way, using Corollary
5.7
(a) in place of Lemma
5.5
. Finally, the case when
k=\ell>1
follows by Lemma
5.9
.
∎

5.4
Almost periodic sequences

In the next section, the almost periodic entropy of a completely positive map
\varphi
is defined in terms of the volumes of the
O
-typical sets in certain representations as
O
ranges over neighbourhoods of
\varphi
. The representations that we use belong to the following sequences.

Definition 5.11
.

An
almost periodic
(‘
AP
’)
sequence
for
\mathfrak{A}
is a sequence of finite-dimensional representations of
\mathfrak{A}
whose dimensions diverge to
\infty
.

Fix an AP sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, and let
d_{n}
be the dimension of
\pi_{n}
for each
n
. Later we sometimes consider strong-quotient convergence for such a sequence. In this case, since each
d_{n}
is finite, the sets
\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})
are continuous images of finite-dimensional spheres by Lemma
2.3
, and hence already closed.

For an AP sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, another possible mode of convergence is convergence of the tracial states
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}
to some limit tracial state
\tau
in the weak
∗
topology of
\mathfrak{A}_{+}^{\ast}
. In the terminology of free probability theory, this asserts that, for any finite subset
F
of
\mathfrak{A}
, the tuples
(\pi_{n}(a):\ a\in F)
form a sequence of ‘microstates’ for
F
according to the ‘non-commutative probability space’
(\mathfrak{A},\tau)
(see
[
99
]
, for example). This convergence of tracial states is one of the hypotheses of Theorem
C
. It determines which limiting tracial state should be used to define the Fuglede–Kadison determinant in the conclusion of that theorem.

The next lemma is a companion to Lemma
5.9
.

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an AP sequence for
\mathfrak{A}
, and consider the AP sequence
(\pi^{(k)}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
for
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
. If
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
, then
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{kd_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}^{(k)}\to\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}
.

Proof.

After identifying
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{kd_{n}}
with
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}
, this follows from the continuity of the operation
(\cdot)\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}
on tracial states. That continuity can be checked entry-wise against elements of
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
.
∎

In general, neither of strong-quotient convergence nor convergence of tracial states implies the other. But convergence of tracial states does give a lower bound on strong-quotient limits: see Corollary
6.14
below.

6

Almost periodic entropy

In this section we define the almost periodic entropy of an
\mathbf{M}_{k}
-valued completely positive map on a separable, unital C*-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
. We then build up its properties towards the proof of Theorem
C
, and follow that with a few consequences.

6.1
Preliminary results from high-dimensional probability

If
g:\mathbf{N}\to(0,\infty)
, then we write
o(g(n))
as a placeholder for any function
f:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbf{R}
that satisfies
f(n)/g(n)\to 0
.

For the rest of this section, let

v(d):=\frac{\pi^{d}}{d!}\qquad(d=1,2,\dots).

(6.1)

Then
v(d)
is equal to the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbf{C}^{d}

[
86
, Subsec. 1.4.9]
. By Stirling’s approximation, this function satisfies

k^{kd}\cdot v(kd)=e^{o(d)}\cdot v(d)^{k}

(6.2)

for any fixed
k
as
d\to\infty
.

We write
\mathrm{vol}_{d}
for Lebesgue measure on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
. For any positive integers
d
and
k
, we also write
\mathrm{vol}_{2kd}
for the measure on
\mathbf{M}_{k,d}
obtained by identifying this space with
\mathbf{R}^{2kd}
. We write
\mathrm{S}^{d-1}
for the unit sphere in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
or in any other vector space that has a standing identification with
\mathbf{R}^{d}
. We write
\sigma_{d-1}
for the surface-area measure on
\mathrm{S}^{d-1}
normalized to have total mass
1
, and we refer to an integral with respect to
\sigma_{d-1}
as a ‘spherical average’. A key feature of these measures in high dimensions is the phenomenon of measure concentration. We need the following special case of this.

Lemma 6.1
.

There is an absolute positive constant
c
such that, for any positive integer
d
and linear transformation
A
of
\mathbf{C}^{d}
, we have

\sigma_{2d-1}\{v:\ |\langle Av,v\rangle-\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d}A|\geq t\}\leq 4e^{-ct^{2}d/\|A\|^{2}}\qquad(t>0).

Proof.

If
f(v)=\langle Av,v\rangle
for
v\in\mathrm{S}^{2d-1}
, then
f
is
2\|A\|
-Lipschitz, and its spherical average is
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d}A
by the invariance of trace under conjugation. Therefore the desired inequality follows from the concentration of general Lipschitz functions on high-dimensional spheres. See
[
64
]
for a proof of this by interpolation along the heat semigroup, for example.
∎

For any positive integers
d
and
k
and any
O\subset\mathbf{M}_{k+}
, let

T(d,O):=\{X\in\mathbf{M}_{d,k}:\ X^{\ast}X\in O\}.

(6.3)

Put another way, this is the set of
k
-tuples in
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus d}
whose Gram matrices lie in
O
.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
k
be a positive integer and let
O
be any neighbourhood of
I_{k}
in
\mathbf{M}_{k+}
. Then there are positive constants
C
and
c
(depending on
k
and
O
) such that

\sigma_{2dk-1}\big(k^{-1/2}T(d,O)\big)>1-Ce^{-cd}\qquad(d=1,2,\dots).

Proof.

By shrinking
O
if necessary, we may assume it is equal to

\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\{Q\in\mathbf{M}_{k+}:\ |q_{ii}-1|<\varepsilon\}\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\{Q\in\mathbf{M}_{k+}:\ |q_{ij}|<\varepsilon\}

for some
\varepsilon>0
. In this case, we have

k^{-1/2}T(n,O)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\{[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]\in\mathbf{M}_{d,k}:\ |\langle x_{i},x_{i}\rangle-1/k|<\varepsilon/k\}\\
\cap\bigcap_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\{[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]\in\mathbf{M}_{d,k}:\ |\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle|<\varepsilon/k\}.

This is an intersection of at most
k^{2}
sets, and for each of them its complement has
\sigma_{2kd-1}
-measure controlled by Lemma
6.1
.
∎

Lemma
6.13
below is a generalization of Corollary
6.2
that depends on Lemma
6.1
in the same way.

Subsection
1.2
discusses the analogy between the Gram matrix of a tuple of vectors and the joint distribution of a tuple of finite-valued random variables. The next theorem adds another layer to this analogy: a ‘method of types’ interpretation for
\log\det Q
when
Q
is a positive semi-definite matrix. In information theory, the method of types gives a basic combinatorial interpretation of discrete Shannon entropy
[
29
, Sec. 11.1]
, and a similar interpretation of the differential entropy of a jointly Gaussian random vector in terms of volumes
[
29
, Sec. 8.2]
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
Q
be a
k
-by-
k
positive semi-definite matrix.

a.

If
O
is any neighbourhood of
Q
in
\mathbf{M}_{k+}
, then

\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2kd}T(d,O)}{v(d)^{k}}\geq(\det Q)^{d-o(d)}.

b.

For any
a>\det Q
there is a neighbourhood
O
of
Q
in
\mathbf{M}_{k+}
such that

\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2kd}T(d,O)}{v(d)^{k}}\leq a^{d+o(d)}.

Variants of Theorem
6.3
are widely known, but I have not found a convenient reference for this particular one, so I include its proof.

Proof.

We write a typical element of
\mathbf{M}_{d,k}
as
X=[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
, and write

\|X\|_{2}^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|x_{i}\|^{2}.

Step 1.
We first prove part (a) for
Q=I_{k}
. By shrinking the neighbourhood
O
if necessary, we may assume that

O=\big\{Q^{\prime}\in\ \mathbf{M}_{k+}:\ e^{-2\varepsilon}<\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}Q^{\prime}<e^{2\varepsilon}\ \hbox{and}\ (\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}Q^{\prime})^{-1}\cdot Q^{\prime}\in U\big\}

for some
\varepsilon>0
and some other neighbourhood
U
of
I_{k}
in
\mathbf{M}_{k+}
. This turns into

T(d,O)=\big\{X\in\mathbf{M}_{d,k}:\ \sqrt{k}e^{-\varepsilon}<\|X\|_{2}<\sqrt{k}e^{\varepsilon}\ \hbox{and}\ X/\|X\|_{2}\in k^{-1/2}T(d,U)\big\}.

Identifying
\mathbf{M}_{d,k}
with
\mathbf{C}^{dk}
and integrating in polar coordinates
[
86
, Subsec. 1.4.3]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{2kd}T(d,O)

\displaystyle=2kd\cdot v(kd)\cdot\sigma_{2kd-1}\big(k^{-1/2}T(d,U)\big)\cdot\int_{\sqrt{k}e^{-\varepsilon}}^{\sqrt{k}e^{\varepsilon}}r^{2kd-1}dr

(6.4)

\displaystyle=k^{kd}\cdot(e^{2kd\varepsilon}-e^{-2kd\varepsilon})\cdot v(kd)\cdot\sigma_{2kd-1}\big(k^{-1/2}T(n,U)\big).

By Corollary
6.2
and the asymptotic (
6.2
), this is greater than
v(d)^{k}
for all sufficiently large
d
.

Step 2.
On the other hand, for any
\varepsilon>0
, the set

O:=\{Q^{\prime}\in\mathbf{M}_{k+}:\ \overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}Q^{\prime}<e^{2\varepsilon}\}

is a neighbourhood of
I_{k}
in
\mathbf{M}_{k+}
, and it satisfies

\mathrm{vol}_{2kd}T(d,O)=\mathrm{vol}_{2kd}B_{\sqrt{k}e^{\varepsilon}}(0)=k^{kd}\cdot e^{2kd\varepsilon}\cdot v(kd)=e^{2kd\varepsilon+o(d)}\cdot v(d)^{k},

using (
6.2
) again. Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, this proves part (b) for
Q=I_{k}
.

Step 3.
Now let
Q\in\mathbf{M}_{k+}
, let
R\in\mathbf{M}_{k}
, and let
Q^{\prime}:=R^{\ast}QR
. Then
Q^{\prime}
also lies in
\mathbf{M}_{k+}
. By the continuity of matrix multiplication, if
O^{\prime}
is any neighbourhood of
Q^{\prime}
, then
Q
has a neighbourhood
O
such that

O^{\prime}\supset R^{\ast}\cdot O\cdot R.

In terms of tuples of vectors, this turns into

T(d,O^{\prime})\supset\{XR:\ X\in T(d,O)\}.

(6.5)

If we regard a
d
-by-
k
matrix as the
d
-tuple of its rows, then right-multiplication by
R
on
\mathbf{M}_{d,k}
becomes the direct sum of
d
copies of
R^{\mathrm{T}}
acting on
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus k}
. Regarded as a real linear transformation acting on
2kd
real linear dimensions, this direct sum transformation has Jacobian
|\det R|^{2d}
: see, for instance,
[
86
, Subsec. 1.3.5]
. Therefore (
6.5
) gives

\mathrm{vol}_{2kd}T(d,O^{\prime})\geq|\det R|^{2d}\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{2kd}T(d,O).

Since
O^{\prime}
is an arbitrary neighbourhood of
Q^{\prime}
, and

\det Q^{\prime}=|\det R|^{2}\cdot\det Q,

this shows that part (a) for
Q^{\prime}
follows if we already know part (a) for
Q
. Similarly, if
R
is invertible, then we may reverse the roles of
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
above and deduce that part (b) for
Q^{\prime}
follows if we already know part (b) for
Q
.

In particular, combining this reasoning with Steps 1 and 2 and making the choice
R=Q^{-1/2}
, we conclude parts (a) and (b) whenever
Q
is nonsingular.

Step 4.
Finally, assume that
Q=[q_{ij}]
is singular. Then part (a) is vacuous.

Applying Step 3 with
R
a unitary matrix, we may assume that
Qe_{1}=0
for the standard basis
e_{1}
, …,
e_{k}
of
\mathbf{C}^{k}
. Having done so, let
r>\max_{i}\sqrt{q_{ii}}
, let
\varepsilon>0
, and let

O:=\{Q^{\prime}\in\mathbf{M}_{k+}:\ q_{11}^{\prime}<\varepsilon^{2}\ \hbox{and}\ \max_{i}q^{\prime}_{ii}<r^{2}\}.

Then
O
is a neighbourhood of
Q
, and

T(d,O)=\big\{X\in\mathbf{M}_{d,k}:\ \|x_{1}\|<\varepsilon\ \hbox{and}\ \max_{i}\|x_{i}\|<r\big\}.

Therefore this neighbourhood satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2kd}T(d,O)}{v(d)^{k}}\leq\frac{(\varepsilon^{2d}\cdot v(d))\cdot(r^{2d}\cdot v(d))^{k-1}}{v(d)^{k}}=\varepsilon^{2d}\cdot r^{2(k-1)d}.

Since
\varepsilon
can be chosen independently of
r
, this completes the proof of part (b).
∎

Theorem
6.3
is a template for Theorem
C
, as well as a special case of that theorem with
\mathfrak{A}=\mathbf{M}_{k}
. The proof given above, which avoids evaluating any integrals over spaces of matrices exactly, is also a precursor to the proof of Theorem
C
.

For discrete Shannon entropy, the usual proofs in the method of types involve counting the strings that have exactly a given empirical distribution and then applying Stirling’s approximation. By contrast, in Step 3 of the proof of Theorem
6.3
, we use a change of variables to transport the desired estimates from
I_{k}
to any other non-singular element of
\mathbf{M}_{k+}
. This use of the symmetries of
\mathrm{vol}_{2kn}
has no obvious analog for probability distributions over finite sets. A related use of symmetry is also essential to our later proof of Theorem
C
.

6.2
Definition and first properties of almost periodic entropy

Consider again a separable, unital C*-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
. Fix an AP sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, a positive integer
k
, and an element
\varphi
of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
. Let
d_{n}
be the dimension of
\pi_{n}
for each
n
. Recall Definition
5.1
of the sets
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)
.

Definition 6.4
.

The
almost periodic
(‘
AP
’)
entropy of
\varphi
along
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is the quantity

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi):=\inf_{O}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{d_{n}}\log\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)}{v(d_{n})^{k}},

(6.6)

where the infimum runs over all neighbourhoods of
\varphi
.

Remark 6.5
.

The expression on the right-hand side of (
6.6
) is monotone in
O
, so we may restrict the infimum to any base of neighbourhoods around
\varphi
without changing the value of
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)
.
\lhd

The normalization outside the logarithm in (
6.6
) is somewhat arbitrary: other natural choices would be
2d_{n}
or
2kd_{n}
. But the present choice seems to make for fewer explicit factors of
k
later, for example in Lemma
6.7
below.

We use ‘
\limsup
’ in Definition
6.4
to allow for possible non-convergence. This matches our earlier choice to use
\mathrm{T}\limsup
rather than
\mathrm{T}\liminf
in the definition of asymptotic association (Definition
5.8
). Having made no extra assumptions on
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, there is no reason why using ‘
\liminf
’ should give the same value in Definition
6.4
. Indeed, one would expect this to be false in case either (i) the sequence of tracial functionals
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}
does not converge in
\mathfrak{A}^{\ast}
or (ii) the sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}
does not strong-quotient converge. However, once we account for these two possibilities, we find that using ‘
\limsup
’ or ‘
\liminf
’ does give the same quantity in (
6.6
): this is Corollary
6.22
below.

Lemma 6.6
.

For any
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and
k
, the function
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is upper semicontinuous on
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
.

Proof.

The value
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)
is an infimum of values associated to neighbourhoods of
\varphi
.
∎

Lemma 6.7
.

Any
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
satisfies

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)\leq\log\det\varphi(1).

In particular, if
\varphi(1)
is singular then
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)=-\infty
.

Proof.

If
O
is any neighbourhood of
\varphi(1)
in
\mathbf{M}_{k}
, then the set

U:=\{\psi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}:\ \psi(1)\in O\}

is a neighbourhood of
\varphi
in
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
. If
\pi
is any representation on
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus d}
, then this
U
satisfies

\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)=\{V^{\mathrm{T}}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]^{\mathrm{T}}\in(\mathbf{C}^{\oplus d})^{\oplus k}:\ V^{\ast}V\in O\}.

Therefore, for any
h>\log\det\varphi(1)
, Theorem
6.3
(b) gives

\log\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},U)}{v(k)^{d_{n}}}<hd_{n}

for all sufficiently large
n
. Since
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)
is an infimum over all neighbourhoods of
\varphi
in
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, the particular neighbourhoods considered above show that
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)<h
whenever
h>\log\det\varphi(1)
.
∎

We determine the cases of equality in Proposition
6.16
below. Lemma
6.7
is analogous to the fact that the entropy of a finite-valued stationary process is bounded above by the discrete Shannon entropy of a single letter.

For a given AP sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and completely positive map
\varphi
, whether
\varphi
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
depends only on the equivalence class of
\pi_{\varphi}
. However, the actual value
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)
is more sensitive. This is an important point where our story diverges from ergodic theory. In ergodic theory, one of the most essential properties of sofic entropy is its independence of the choice of generating observable
[
19
]
, and hence its invariance under measure-theoretic isomorphism. The analog of this for AP entropy is false. Instead, AP entropy enjoys a general transformation law when one cyclic tuple is exchanged for another: see Proposition
6.10
.

Some proofs about AP entropy are easier to digest in the special case
k=1
, if only because the notation is lighter. The next result sometimes lets us make this simplification without losing any generality. It accompanies Lemmas
5.9
and
5.12
.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
k
be a positive integer, let
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an AP sequence, and let
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}:=(\pi_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 1}
. Let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, and define
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
as in equation (
2.15
). Then

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}}(\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle)=\frac{1}{k}\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi).

Proof.

Let
O
be a neighbourhood of
\varphi
, and let
\widetilde{O}
be the associated neighbourhood of
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
under the pairing isomorphism. Then we have

\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n}^{(k)},\widetilde{O})}{v(kd_{n})}=\frac{k^{-kd_{n}}\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)}{v(kd_{n})}=\frac{e^{o(d_{n})}\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)}{v(d_{n})^{k}},

using (
5.2
) for the first equality and (
6.2
) for the second. Now take logarithms, normalize by
kd_{n}
(the dimension of
\pi_{n}^{(k)}
), and insert into Definition
6.4
.
∎

Remark 6.9
.

The Introduction discusses AP entropy as a representation theoretic analog of sofic entropy. Let us comment further on their relationship.

Theorem
C
requires an AP sequence whose pulled-back traces converge to the state
\tau
, which then determines the Fuglede–Kadison determinant that appears. Suppose that
\mathfrak{A}=C^{\ast}\Gamma
and that
\tau
is given by the regular charater. Then
\tau
is a limit of finite-dimensional characters if and only if
\Gamma
has an AP sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
that separates its elements. Such groups were called ‘maximally almost periodic’ by von Neumann
[
96
]
; a textbook introduction is
[
33
, Secs. 16.4–5]
, where they are called ‘injectable’.

However, we can also study regular characters on a larger class of groups as follows. If
\Gamma
is any countable group, then we can write it as
F/N
for some free group
F
and normal subgroup
N
. Now we can look for finite-dimensional representations of
F
whose characters converge to the quasi-regular character
1_{N}
, rather than finite-dimensional representations of
\Gamma
whose characters converge to
1_{\{e\}}
. This offers more flexibility, because those finite-dimensional representations of
F
need not have trivial restriction to
N
until we take their limit. Allowing convergence in this sense, the availability of finite-dimensional approximants to the regular representation of
\Gamma
is equivalent to
\Gamma
being ‘hyperlinear’ in the terminology of
[
83
]
. In particular, it does not depend on the choice of presentation
F/N
. This class of groups is the ‘linear’ analog of the sofic groups, and includes all sofic groups
[
38
]
. Both classes of groups are described in the survey
[
80
]
.

Thus, specialized to positive definite functions on groups, AP entropy is most naturally applied for ‘hyperlinear’ groups via associated quasi-regular characters on free groups. For these its definition is a direct analog of the definition of sofic entropy via observables or partitions (see
[
21
, Def. 5]
, for example).
\lhd

6.3
First transformation formula

Fix an AP sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, and let
d_{n}
be the dimension of
\pi_{n}
. At this point, some of our work starts to need the assumption that the tracial states
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}
converge to a limit in
\mathfrak{A}^{\ast}
.

The next proposition is a basic change-of-variables formula for AP entropy. It can be seen as a cousin of Voiculescu’s change-of-variables formulas for his free entropy
[
98
, Prop. 3.5]
, although the proof in our ‘linear’ setting is simpler.

Proposition 6.10
.

Fix
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
.

a.

Let
Q\in\mathbf{M}_{k}
be invertible, and define

\psi(b):=(Q^{\mathrm{T}})^{\ast}\varphi(b)Q^{\mathrm{T}}\qquad(b\in\mathfrak{A}).

Then

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\psi)=2\log|\det Q|+\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi).

b.

Assume further that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
. Let
a\in\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
be invertible, and define
\psi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
in terms of
\varphi
and
a
as in Lemma
2.4
. Then
\varphi
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
if and only if
\psi
is, and in that case

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\psi)=2\log\Delta_{\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}|a|+\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi).

Proof.

Part (a).
For any neighbourhood
O
of
\psi
, the second part of Lemma
5.3
gives a neighbourhood
U
of
\varphi
such that

(I_{d}\otimes Q)[\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)]\subset\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)

for any representation
\pi
on
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus d}
. For each
n
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)

\displaystyle\geq|\det(I_{d_{n}}\otimes Q)|^{2}\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},U)

\displaystyle=|\det Q|^{2d_{n}}\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},U).

The determinants are squared here because
I_{d_{n}}\otimes Q
is a linear transformation in
kd_{n}
complex dimensions, but we must treat it as a real linear map in
2kd_{n}
real dimensions for the purpose of computing volumes (see, for instance,
[
86
, Subsec. 1.3.5]
). Inserting this inequality into Definition
6.4
and taking the infimum over
O
, it follows that

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\psi)\geq 2\log|\det Q|+\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi).

Applying the same reasoning with the roles of
\varphi
and
\psi
reversed and with
Q^{-1}
in place of
Q
, we obtain the reverse inequality as well.

Part (b).
This time, if
O
is a neighbourhood of
\psi
, then Lemma
5.3
gives a neighbourhood
O^{\prime}
of
\varphi
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\big(\pi_{n}^{(k)}(a)[\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O^{\prime})]\big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{det}\,|\pi_{n}^{(k)}(a)|^{2}\cdot\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O^{\prime})

(6.7)

for every
n
. Once again, the determinant is squared because we must treat
\pi_{n}^{(k)}(a)
as a linear map in
2kd_{n}
real dimensions for the purpose of computing volumes.

Since
a
is invertible and
\pi_{n}^{(k)}
is a unital C*-algebra homomorphism, we have

\mathrm{det}\,|\pi_{n}^{(k)}(a)|=\exp(\mathrm{tr}_{kd_{n}}(\log|\pi_{n}^{(k)}(a)|))=\exp\big(\mathrm{tr}_{kd_{n}}(\pi_{n}^{(k)}(\log|a|))\big).

By our assumption on
\boldsymbol{\pi}
and Lemma
5.12
, this is equal to

\exp\big(d_{n}\big((\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k})(\log|a|)+o(1)\big)\big)=(\Delta_{\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}|a|)^{d_{n}+o(d_{n})}\qquad\hbox{as}\ n\to\infty.

Therefore, normalizing and taking logarithms in (
6.7
), that inequality becomes

\frac{1}{d_{n}}\log\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)}{v(d_{n})^{k}}\geq 2\log\Delta_{\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}|a|+o(1)+\frac{1}{d_{n}}\log\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2kd_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O^{\prime})}{v(d_{n})^{k}}.

Letting
n\to\infty
and then taking the infimum over
O
, this gives

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\psi)\geq 2\log\Delta_{\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}|a|+\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi).

The reverse of this inequality also holds by swapping the roles of
\varphi
and
\psi
and replacing
a
with
a^{-1}
, which satisfies
\Delta_{\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}|a^{-1}|=(\Delta_{\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}|a|)^{-1}
(see, for instance,
[
34
, Thm. I.6.10(iii)]
).
∎

Remark 6.11
.

If
{\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}}
converges to
\tau
, then we can recognize part (a) above as a special case of part (b) by letting
a:=1_{\mathfrak{A}}\otimes Q
in
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})=\mathfrak{A}\otimes\mathbf{M}_{k}
and checking that
\Delta_{\tau\otimes\mathrm{tr}_{k}}|a|=\det|Q|=|\det Q|
. We formulate part (a) separately because it holds without the assumption of trace convergence.
\lhd

6.4
Spherical measures and concentration

When
k=1
and
\varphi
is normalized, the next lemma gives an alternative to using Lebesgue measure in Definition
6.4
.

Lemma 6.12
.

Let
\varphi\in\Sigma_{1}(\mathfrak{A})
and let
\boldsymbol{\pi}
be an AP sequence. Let
\mathcal{O}
be a base of neighbourhoods around
\varphi
in
\Sigma_{1}(\mathfrak{A})
. Then

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)=\inf_{O\in\mathcal{O}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{d_{n}}\log\sigma_{2d_{n}-1}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O).

(6.8)

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be the family of all sets that have the form

\big\{\psi\in\mathfrak{A}_{+}^{\ast}:\ e^{-2\delta}<\psi(1)<e^{2\delta}\ \hbox{and}\ \psi(1)^{-1}\cdot\psi\in O\big\}

(6.9)

for some
O\in\mathcal{O}
and
\delta>0
. Then
\mathcal{U}
is a base of neighbourhoods around
\varphi
in
\mathfrak{A}_{+}^{\ast}
. We may therefore restrict attention to neighbourhoods from
\mathcal{U}
when evaluating
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)
(see Remark
6.5
).

So now let
U
be the set in (
6.9
) for some
\delta>0
and
O\in\mathcal{O}
, and let
\pi
be a representation on
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus d}
. Then the special form of
U
gives

1_{\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)}(ry)=1_{(e^{-\delta},e^{\delta})}(r)\cdot 1_{\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)}(y)\qquad(r>0,\ y\in\mathrm{S}^{2d-1}).

As a result, integrating in polar coordinates
[
86
, Subsec. 1.4.3]
gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{2d}\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)

\displaystyle=2d\cdot v(d)\cdot\sigma_{2d-1}\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)\cdot\int_{e^{-\delta}}^{e^{\delta}}r^{2d-1}\ dr

\displaystyle=v(d)\cdot(e^{2\delta d}-e^{-2\delta d})\cdot\sigma_{2d-1}\mathcal{X}(\pi,O).

This implies that

\sigma_{2d-1}\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)\leq\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2d}\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)}{v(d)}\leq e^{2\delta d}\cdot\sigma_{2d-1}\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)

for all sufficiently large
d
. Inserting this into Definition
6.4
and taking the limit supremum over
n
and then the infimum over elements of
\mathcal{U}
, we obtain (
6.8
).
∎

If
\varphi\in\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
for some
k>1
, then we may apply Lemma
6.12
to the positive functional
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
, whose sets of approximately typical vectors along
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}
are given by equation (
5.2
). We can then deduce a result for
\varphi
itself via Lemma
6.8
.

For a state, we now have a choice between the original definition of AP entropy and the alternative given by Lemma
6.12
. Each has its advantages. A major advantage of the measures
\sigma_{2d-1}
is the concentration estimate from Lemma
6.1
. As above, we discuss this only for
k=1
to lighten notation. Let
\pi
be a representation on
\mathbf{C}^{\oplus d}
. Since the trace of a matrix is invariant under unitary conjugation, the average of the type
\Phi^{\pi}_{x}
with respect to the spherical measure
\sigma_{2d-1}
is equal to
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d}\circ\pi
. When
d
is large, Lemma
6.1
improves this conclusion considerably:
\Phi^{\pi}_{x}
is actually close to
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d}\circ\pi
for most individual
x\in\mathrm{S}^{2d-1}
.

Let
\tau
be a tracial state on
\mathfrak{A}
, and let
\lambda
be its GNS representation.

Lemma 6.13
.

If
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
, then for every neighbourhood
U
of
\tau
there are positive constants
C
and
c
such that

\sigma_{2d_{n}-1}\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)\geq 1-Ce^{-cd_{n}}.

Proof.

It suffices to prove this for
U
belonging to some sub-base of neighbourhoods of
\tau
, since any other neighbourhood contains a finite intersection of these. We may therefore assume that

U=\{\psi\in\Sigma_{1}(\mathfrak{A}):\ |\psi(a)-\tau(a)|<\varepsilon\}

for some
a\in\mathfrak{A}
and
\varepsilon>0
.

Since
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}(a)\to\tau(a)
, this
U
satisfies

\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},U)\supset\{v\in\mathrm{S}^{2d_{n}-1}:\ |\langle\pi_{n}(a)v,v\rangle-\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\pi_{n}(a)|<\varepsilon/2\}

for all sufficiently large
n
. Now the result follows from Lemma
6.1
.
∎

Corollary 6.14
.

Assume that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
. If
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
and
\varphi
is approximately associated to
\lambda^{\oplus\infty}
, then it is asymptotically associated to
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\varphi
is normalized. Proposition
2.8
gives that
\Sigma_{k}(\lambda^{\oplus\infty})
equals
\Sigma_{k}(\lambda^{\oplus k})
, so now our assumption actually says that
\varphi\in\overline{\Sigma_{k}(\lambda^{\oplus k})}
.

Since
\lambda^{\oplus k}
is the minimal dilation of
\tau\otimes I_{k}:=\mathrm{diag}(\tau,\dots,\tau)
, by Corollary
5.4
it suffices to show that
\tau\otimes I_{k}
itself is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. When
k=1
, this follows from Lemma
6.13
: indeed, a random vector drawn from
\sigma_{2d_{n}-1}
is approximately typical for
\tau
with high probability once
d_{n}
is large enough. Finally, if
k>1
, then we can apply the previous case to the sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}
using Lemmas
5.12
and
5.9
and the fact that

\langle\tau\otimes I_{k},\cdot\rangle=\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}.

∎

In the notation of Subsection
5.3
, Corollary
6.14
gives a lower bound on the set
\mathrm{T}\liminf_{n}\overline{\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})}
for each
k
. This can be an equality: for example, this is so if
\mathfrak{A}=C^{\ast}\Gamma
, the states
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}
converge to the regular tracial state, and
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
also converges strongly to the regular representation. Some important examples satisfying these three conditions are surveyed in
[
76
]
. In other cases, the inclusion can be strict. Nevertheless, we do always obtain the following.

Corollary 6.15
.

Assume that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
. Let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, and consider its Lebesgue decomposition
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}+\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
relative to
\tau
. Then
\varphi
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
if and only if
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.

Proof.

First, by (
2.17
) and Lemmas
5.9
and
5.12
, it is equivalent to prove that
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}
if and only if
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{sing}}
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}
. So we may assume that
k=1
for the rest of the proof.

By construction,
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
is associated to
\pi_{\varphi}
. Therefore, if
\varphi
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, then
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
is as well by Corollary
5.4
.

On the other hand, Corollary
6.14
tells us that
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
is always asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, and
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
are disjoint by construction. Therefore, if
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, then so is
\varphi
itself by Lemma
5.10
.
∎

Lemmas
6.12
and
6.13
also let us determine the cases of equality in Lemma
6.7
.

Proposition 6.16
.

Assume that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
, and let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
with
\varphi(1)
nonsingular. Then equality holds in Lemma
6.7
if and only if
\varphi=\tau\otimes\varphi(1)
.

Proof.

Step 1.
First assume that
k=1
. For any neighbourhood
U
of
\tau
, Lemma
6.13
shows that

\sigma_{2d_{n}-1}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},U)\to 1.

Therefore
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\tau)=0
, by Lemma
6.12
.

On the other hand, suppose that
\varphi\neq\varphi(1)\cdot\tau
. After normalizing via Proposition
6.10
(a), we may assume that
\varphi(1)=1
. Since
\varphi\neq\tau
, they have disjoint neighbourhoods, say
U
and
U^{\prime}
respectively. Applying Lemma
6.13
to the neighbourhood
U^{\prime}
, there are positive constants
C
and
c
such that

\sigma_{2d_{n}-1}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},U)\leq\sigma_{2d_{n}-1}\big(\mathrm{S}^{2d_{n}-1}\setminus\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},U^{\prime})\big)\leq Ce^{-cd_{n}}.

This turns into
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)\leq-c<0
, again by Lemma
6.12
.

Step 2.
Now suppose that
k>1
. Then the case
k=1
and Lemmas
5.12
and
6.8
show that
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\tau\otimes I_{k})=0
.

Let
\varphi
be any other element of
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
such that
\varphi(1)
is non-singular and equality is achieved in Lemma
6.7
. Applying Proposition
6.10
(a) with
Q:=\varphi(1)^{-1/2}
, we may this time assume that
\varphi(1)=I_{k}
and
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)=0
.

Having done so, consider the sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}
and the pairing functional
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
. Since
\varphi
achieves equality in Lemma
6.7
, Lemma
6.8
gives

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}}(\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle)=\frac{1}{k}\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)=0.

Now the case
k=1
applied to
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}
shows that
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
must equal

\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}=\langle\tau\otimes I_{k},\cdot\rangle,

and hence that
\varphi=\tau\otimes I_{k}
.
∎

Corollary 6.17
.

Assume that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
. Let
\tau
be associated to
\lambda
by
\xi
, let
a
be a positive invertible element of
\mathfrak{A}
, and let
\varphi:=\Phi^{\lambda}_{\lambda(a)\xi}
. Then
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)=2\log\Delta_{\tau}a
.

Proof.

The expression for
\varphi
in terms of
\tau
and
a
is a special case of the relationship from Lemma
2.4
. Therefore Proposition
6.10
(b) gives

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)=2\log\Delta_{\tau}a+\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\tau).

On the right-hand side, the second term vanishes by Proposition
6.16
.
∎

Corollary
6.17
is a special case of Theorem
C
. In the next subsection we use it during the full proof of that theorem. This is somewhat similar to the use of Proposition
3.6
in the proof of Theorem
A
.

6.5
Proof of Theorem
C

Let
\tau
be associated to the representation
\lambda
by the cyclic tracial vector
\xi
, and let
\widetilde{\tau}
be the normal tracial state on
\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
defined by
\xi
. We use the letter
\Delta
for both (i) the Fuglede–Kadison determinant on
\mathfrak{A}
defined by
\tau
and also (ii) the Fuglede–Kadison determinant on square-integrable operators affiliated to
\lambda(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime}
.

Proof of Theorem
C
.

Step 1.
First we prove the inequality “
\geq
” when
k=1
and
\varphi
is
\lambda
-normal. In this case parts (i) and (iv) of Lemma
2.13
give a sequence of positive invertible elements
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
, …of
\mathfrak{A}
such that

\tau(b_{i}(\cdot)b_{i})\to\varphi\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad(\Delta b_{i})^{2}\to\Delta\varphi\qquad\hbox{as}\ i\to\infty.

Then Corollary
6.17
gives
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi_{i})=2\log\Delta b_{i}
for each
i
. Letting
i\to\infty
, the upper semicontinuity from Lemma
6.6
turns this into

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)\geq 2\lim_{i\to\infty}\log\Delta b_{i}=\log\Delta\varphi.

Step 2.
Next we prove the inequality “
\geq
” when
k=1
but
\varphi
is otherwise arbitrary. Let
O
be any neighbourhood of
\varphi
. Since
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
are disjoint, Corollary
5.7
(a) gives neighbourhoods
U
of
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
and
W
of
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
such that

\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)\supset\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},U)+\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},W)

for every
n
. Since
\varphi
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
by assumption, so is
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
by Corollary
6.15
. Therefore
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},U)
is nonempty along an infinite subsequence of values of
n
, say
n_{1}<n_{2}<\dots
. Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}
be the corresponding AP subsequence of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. For each
n_{i}
, the set
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n_{i}},O)
contains a translate of
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n_{i}},W)
, and so

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{d_{n}}\log\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2d_{n}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)}{v(d_{n})}

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{d_{n_{i}}}\log\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2d_{n_{i}}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n_{i}},O)}{v(d_{n_{i}})}

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{i\to\infty}\frac{1}{d_{n_{i}}}\log\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{2d_{n_{i}}}\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n_{i}},W)}{v(d_{n_{i}})}

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}).

This lower bound is at least
\log\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}=\log\Delta\varphi
by applying Step 1 along the AP sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}
. Since
O
is arbitrary, this proves that
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)\geq\log\Delta\varphi
.

Step 3.
We now prove the inequality “
\leq
” in case
k=1
. This proof is quickest via the variational principle from Proposition
2.14
.

Let
a\in\mathfrak{A}
be positive and invertible and satisfy
\Delta a\geq 1
. Define a new positive functional by
\psi:=\varphi(\sqrt{a}(\cdot)\sqrt{a})
. Then we have

\displaystyle\log\varphi(a)

\displaystyle=\log\psi(1)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\psi)

(Lemma
6.7
)

\displaystyle=2\log\Delta\sqrt{a}+\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)

(Proposition
6.10
(b))

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\hbox{(because $\Delta\sqrt{a}=\sqrt{\Delta a}\geq 1$)}.

Taking the infimum over
a
, Proposition
2.14
turns this into
\log\Delta\varphi\geq\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)
.

Step 4.
Finally, if
k>1
, then we can apply the previous steps to the functional
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
. First, Lemma
5.12
gives that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{kd_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}^{(k)}\to\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}
. Secondly, Lemma
5.9
gives that
\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}
. Finally, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=k\cdot\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(k)}}(\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle)

(Lemma
6.8
)

\displaystyle=k\cdot\log\Delta_{\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}}(\langle\varphi,\cdot\rangle)

(case
k=1
of Theorem
C
)

\displaystyle=\log\Delta\varphi

\displaystyle\hbox{(equation~\eqref{eq:det-phi-det-pairing})}.

∎

6.6
Some consequences of Theorem
C

Basic consequences

Corollary 6.18
.

Assume that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
, and let
\varphi,\psi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
.

a.

We have

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}})&\quad\hbox{if $\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}$ is asymptotically associated to $\boldsymbol{\pi}$}\\
-\infty&\quad\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

b.

If
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)>-\infty
, then
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
is associated to
\lambda^{\oplus k}
by a
k
-tuple that is cyclic and separating for
\lambda^{\oplus k}(\mathfrak{A})^{\prime\prime}
, and so
\pi_{\varphi}\gtrsim\pi_{\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}}\simeq\lambda^{\oplus k}
.

c.

If
\varphi\geq\psi
in the positive definite ordering, and if
\varphi
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, then
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)\geq\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\psi)
.

Proof.

Part (a) is just the combination of Corollary
6.15
and Theorem
C
.

If
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)>-\infty
, then Theorem
C
tells us that
\Delta\varphi=\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}>0
. Expressing this Fuglede–Kadison determinant in terms of a non-negative affiliated operator
T
from Proposition
2.8
, it follows that
\Delta T
is also positive, and hence
T
is nonsingular. Since
T
is self-adjoint, it therefore also has dense image. Since
\pi_{\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}}
is equivalent to the subrepresentation of
\lambda^{\oplus k}
defined by
\overline{\mathrm{img}\,T}
, this proves part (b).

Finally, if
\varphi\geq\psi
and
\varphi
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, then so is
\psi
, and Theorem
C
shows that part (c) follows from Proposition
2.15
(a).
∎

Modes of convergence

If an AP sequence
\boldsymbol{\pi}
strong-quotient converges, then Theorem
C
shows that the AP entropy function
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
depends only on the values of the limits

\tau=\lim_{n}\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\lim_{n}\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})\qquad\hbox{for}\ k=1,2,\dots.

If
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
but
\boldsymbol{\pi}
does not strong-quotient converge, then Theorem
C
may give different values of AP entropy along different subsequences, but the only two possible values are
\log\Delta_{\tau}\varphi
or
-\infty
(which may still happen to be equal).

In ergodic theory, an important open problem asks whether the sofic entropy of a measure-preserving system must always equal either one particular value or
-\infty
, independently of the choice of sofic approximation. Corollary
6.18
answers the analogous question positively for AP entropy. On the other hand, it is known that the answer is negative for the topological variant of sofic entropy
[
2
]
.

Remark 6.19
.

Subsection
5.3
mentions the analogy between strong-quotient convergence of AP sequences and local-global convergence in graph theory, as discussed further in
[
1
]
. If
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
strong-quotient converges, then Theorem
C
shows that the expressions appearing in the definition of AP entropy also converge in a sense much like a large deviations principle. This is actually the analog of another mode of convergence for graph sequences of uniformly bounded degree: ‘large deviation convergence’, introduced in
[
16
]
. Whether local-global convergence implies large deviations convergence for such graph sequences remains open. Our results answer the analogous question for AP sequences.
\lhd

Now ssume again that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
. We can reverse the discussion above by asking whether the function
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
determines the topological upper limit of the sequence
(\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n}))_{n\geq 1}
for each
k
. This is slightly subtle, because for
\varphi\in\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
the value
\log\Delta_{\tau}\varphi
may equal
-\infty
even if
\pi_{\varphi}\gtrsim\lambda^{\oplus k}
. To evade this problem, we can instead use
\varphi
to form the perturbations

\varphi_{t}:=\tau\otimes I_{k}+t\varphi\qquad(t\geq 0).

Corollary 6.20
.

Under the assumptions above, the following hold.

a.

If
\varphi_{t}
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
for some
t>0
, then this holds for all
t>0
, and we have

0\leq\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi_{t})\leq k\cdot\log(1+t\cdot\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}\varphi(1))\qquad(t\geq 0).

b.

If
\varphi_{t}
is not asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
for any
t>0
, then
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi_{t})=-\infty
for every
t>0
.

Proof.

The linearity of the Lebesgue decomposition gives

(\varphi_{t})_{\mathrm{ac}}=\tau\otimes I_{k}+t\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad(\varphi_{t})_{\mathrm{sing}}=t\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}

for every
t\geq 0
. Therefore, if
\varphi_{t}
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
for some
t>0
, then so is
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
, and hence so is
\varphi_{s}
for every other
s
, by two applications of Corollary
6.15
.

Now the lower bound on
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi_{t})
in part (a) follows from Corollary
6.18
(c) and Proposition
6.16
, because
\varphi_{t}\geq\tau\otimes I_{k}
in the positive definite ordering. On the other hand, since
\Delta_{\tau\otimes\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}}(1\otimes I_{k})=1
, the element
1\otimes I_{k}
of
\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
is allowed inside the infimum of the variational principle from Proposition
2.14
. Combined with Theorem
C
, this gives

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi_{t})=\log\Delta_{\tau}\varphi_{t}\leq k\cdot\log\langle\varphi_{t},1\otimes I_{k}\rangle.

This gives the desired upper bound on
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi_{t})
, because

\langle\varphi_{t},1\otimes I_{k}\rangle=\langle\tau\otimes I_{k},1\otimes I_{k}\rangle+t\langle\varphi,1\otimes I_{k}\rangle=1+t\cdot\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{k}\varphi(1).

Finally, part (b) follows from Definition
6.4
, applied when the sets
\mathcal{X}(\pi_{n},O)
are empty for some sufficiently small neighbourhood
O
of
\varphi_{t}
.
∎

Remark 6.21
.

Alternatively, one can prove Corollary
6.20
(a) using an extension of the concentration result in Lemma
6.13
rather than the full strength of Theorem
C
. Referring to the case when
k=1
and
t=1
for simplicity, the idea is as follows. Let
\pi
be a representation of large dimension
d
, and let
x\in\mathbf{C}^{\oplus d}
be such that
\Phi^{\pi}_{x}
is close to
\varphi
. Using essentially the same proof as for Lemma
6.13
, one can show that, according to the measure
\sigma_{2d-1}
, most unit vectors
y\in\mathrm{S}^{2d-1}
satisfy
\Phi^{\pi}_{[x,y]}\approx\mathrm{diag}(\varphi,\tau)
, and hence
\Phi^{\pi}_{x+y}\approx\tau+\varphi
. By using polar coordinates to integrate over
y
with respect to
\mathrm{vol}_{2d}
, this fact turns into the same lower bound as in Corollary
6.20
(a).
\lhd

Now we can see how the function
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
determines topological upper limits.

Corollary 6.22
.

Assume that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi\to\tau
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})

\displaystyle=\{\varphi\in\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A}):\ \mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi_{t})\geq 0\ \forall t\geq 0\}

\displaystyle=\{\varphi\in\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A}):\ \mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi_{t})\to 0\ \hbox{as}\ t\downarrow 0\}

for each positive integer
k
. Consequently,
\boldsymbol{\pi}
strong-quotient converges if and only if we have
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}}=\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
whenever
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}
is a subsequence of
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. ∎

Additivity and concavity

In ergodic theory, Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy for single transformations is additive under Cartesian products
[
100
, Thm. 4.23]
. By contrast, sofic entropy is subadditive for joinings, but it may fail to be additive, even for a product joining
[
10
]
. The same phenomena hold for AP entropy, for essentially the same reasons. Let us assume again that
\overline{\mathrm{tr}}_{d_{n}}\circ\pi_{n}\to\tau
and that
\boldsymbol{\pi}
strong-quotient converges; if these conditions fail then we can always pass to a subsequence that satisfies them.

Corollary 6.23
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, let
\psi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{\ell})_{+}
, and let
\theta
be a joining of them (recall Definition
2.9
). Then

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)\leq\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)+\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\psi).

(6.10)

If
\theta_{\mathrm{ac}}=\mathrm{diag}(\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}},\psi_{\mathrm{ac}})
, then equality holds provided
\theta
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.

Proof.

If
\theta
is not asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
, then
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\theta)=-\infty
and there is nothing left to prove. So assume that
\theta
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. Both
\varphi
and
\psi
are associated to
\pi_{\theta}
, so these are also asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
. Now the AP entropies of all three maps are given by Theorem
C
, and the desired conclusions follow from Proposition
2.15
(d).
∎

Remark 6.24
.

Alternatively, the subadditivity of
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
may be proved by observing that, for any neighbourhoods
U
of
\varphi
and
V
of
\psi
, there is a neighbourhood
O
of
\theta
such that

\mathcal{X}(\pi,O)\subset\mathcal{X}(\pi,U)\times\mathcal{X}(\pi,V)

for any representation
\pi
. Using this to compare volumes and inserting into the definition of AP entropy, it turns into (
6.10
). However, the case of equality in Corollary
6.23
does seem to require a stronger ingredient such as Theorem
C
.
\lhd

In the last part of Corollary
6.23
, it is not enough to assume that
\varphi
and
\psi
are both separately asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
: see Example
6.26
below.

Notions of entropy are often given by concave functions, so it is natural to ask whether the restrictions
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}|\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
have this property. Under some conditions, they do. We obtain the following from Theorem
C
together with the log-concavity inequality (
2.25
) (another consequence of Proposition
2.15
).

Corollary 6.25
.

Let
\varphi,\psi\in\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, and assume moreover that
{t\varphi+(1-t)\psi}
is asymptotically associated to
\boldsymbol{\pi}
for every
t\in[0,1]
. Then
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
is concave along the line segment from
\varphi
to
\psi
:

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(t\varphi+(1-t)\psi)\geq t\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)+(1-t)\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\psi)\qquad(0\leq t\leq 1).

∎

However, the function
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
need not be globally concave on
\mathfrak{L}(\mathfrak{A},\mathbf{M}_{k})_{+}
, or even on its subset
\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
. The problem is that the set
\lim_{n}\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})
itself need not be convex. We illustrate this by an extension of Example
5.2
.

Example 6.26
.

Let
\Gamma
be a countable group with left regular representation
\lambda
. Let
\pi
be an irreducible representation of
\Gamma
such that (i)
2\leq\dim\pi<\infty
and (ii) some element of
\Sigma_{1}(\pi)
is not approximately associated to
\lambda
. Separately, let
(\rho_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be an AP sequence for
\Gamma
that strong-quotient converges to
\lambda
. Suitable examples include randomly generated unitary or permutation representations of free groups, by the results of
[
26
]
and
[
15
]
, respectively; see also
[
76
]
.

Now let
\pi_{n}:=\pi\oplus\rho_{n}
for each
n
. Checking the definitions shows that

\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})=\{t\varphi+(1-t)\psi:\ \varphi\in\Sigma_{k}(\pi),\ \psi\in\Sigma_{k}(\rho_{n}),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\}.

This is closed for every
k
and
n
because it is a continuous image of a finite-dimensional sphere. One can now check that
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
strong-quotient converges to
\pi\oplus\lambda
, and so in particular

\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})\to\{t\varphi+(1-t)\psi:\ \varphi\in\Sigma_{k}(\pi),\ \psi\in\overline{\Sigma_{k}(\lambda)},\ 0\leq t\leq 1\}

in the Vietoris topology. However, since
\pi
is irreducible and not approximately associated to
\lambda
, it follows that
\lim_{n}\Sigma_{2}(\pi_{n})
does not contain
\mathrm{diag}(\varphi,\psi)
when
\varphi
and
\psi
are linearly independent elements of
\Sigma_{1}(\pi)
. Moreover, by the same reasoning as for Example
5.2
, it also follows that
\lim_{n}\Sigma_{1}(\pi_{n})
does not contain
(\varphi+\psi)/2
, so
\lim_{n}\Sigma_{1}(\pi_{n})
is not convex. Finally, Corollary
6.20
(a) gives that
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi+\tau)
and
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\psi+\tau)
are both non-negative, but we have

\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\mathrm{diag}(\varphi+\tau,\psi+\tau))=\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}((\varphi+\tau)/2+(\psi+\tau)/2)=-\infty.

∎

Nevertheless, if
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
strong-quotient converges, then
\lim_{n}\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})
is at least star-shaped around the element
\tau\otimes I_{k}
(or any other
\lambda
-normal element of
\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
). This is a consequence of Corollary
6.15
, because
\tau\otimes I_{k}
does not contribute to the singular part in a convex combination. Overall, the limit set
\lim_{n}\Sigma_{k}(\pi_{n})
can fail to be convex only because of its excess outside the lower bound on this set provided by Corollary
6.14
, as illustrated by Example
6.26
.

Since
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
may not be concave on the whole of
\Sigma_{k}(\mathfrak{A})
, it may not be recoverable from its Legendre transform. Nevertheless, it could be interesting to investigate how various features of the sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are reflected by that transform, and how it compares with the Legendre transform of
\log\Delta
itself. See
[
91
, Sec. I.6]
for a general account of infinite-dimensional Legendre transforms with a view towards statistical mechanics, or
[
25
]
for the resulting variational principle in the case of sofic entropy. In the analogous setting of modes of convergence for graphs of uniformly bounded degree (see Subsection
5.3
and Remark
6.19
), convergence of these Legendre transforms would correspond to ‘right convergence’
[
14
,
17
]
.

6.7
Further remarks

Comparison with previous work

Just like Theorem
A
, Theorem
C
has a number of predecessors in the literature. Some of the first that lie beyond the discussion in Subsection
3.3
are Lyons’ calculations in
[
73
,
74
]
. These concern the problem of asymptotically counting spanning trees along sequences of finite connected graphs using their random weak limits. Lyons shows that the resulting ‘tree entropy’ of the random limit graph is given by a Fuglede–Kadison determinant of its Laplacian. His setting does not require a group action, but it yields results for sofic groups as a special case.

More recent examples are continuations of Deninger’s work on the entropies of certain algebraically-defined measure-preserving systems, but now for sofic groups and sofic entropy. Kerr and Li took the first step in this direction with
[
60
, Thm. 7.1]
. Then Hayes gave a more complete result in
[
47
]
, showing that the sofic entropy of
\boldsymbol{X}_{f}
equals the Fuglede–Kadison determinant of
f\in\mathbf{M}_{k}(\mathbf{Z}[\Gamma])
whenever
\Gamma
is sofic and
\lambda^{(k)}(f)
is injective. An alternative proof with further refinements is included in
[
51
]
. On the other hand, another result from
[
47
]
is that the Li–Thom theorem from
[
65
]
does not generalize fully to sofic groups.

Alongside those papers, Hayes has developed other connections between sofic entropy and representation theory. In
[
49
]
he proved that an arbitrary measure-preserving
\Gamma
-system can have completely positive sofic entropy only if its Koopman representation is
\lambda
-normal. In
[
48
]
, he computed the sofic entropy of a stationary Gaussian process over
\Gamma
in terms of the real orthogonal representation that defines its first chaos, generalizing a result from
[
45
]
about single transformations. The main theorem in
[
48
]
is worth comparing with the way in which
\varphi_{\mathrm{sing}}
and
\varphi_{\mathrm{ac}}
determine whether
\mathrm{h}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(\varphi)
equals
-\infty
in Corollary
6.18
above.

Other aspects of Szegő’s theorem

The setting of Theorem
C
has taken us quite far from our original motivation in the form of Szegő’s theorem. AP entropy is not defined as a limit of finite-dimensional determinants, and related data such as Verblunsky coefficients have no obvious meaning in this generality.

However, some of those finer aspects of Szegő’s theorem make a return in a sequel to the present paper
[
9
]
. That paper studies random AP sequences, and in particular an annealed version of AP entropy that can be defined using these. In the special case of uniformly random finite-dimensional representations of free groups, this is a representation theoretic analog of Bowen’s annealed sofic entropy (formerly called the ‘f-invariant’) from
[
18
,
20
]
. Like annealed sofic entropy, this instance of annealed AP entropy admits a precise formula.

In studying this entropy and its formula, many features of the theory of orthogonal polynomials on
\mathbf{T}
reappear. For example, sequences of ‘generalized Verblunsky coefficients’ can be used to parametrize positive definite functions over free groups. Rather than requiring a total ordering of the group, these generalized Verblunsky coefficients depend on the fact that free groups have tree-like Cayley graphs. One of the first main theorems about annealed AP entropy is an infinite series expansion of it in terms of those coefficients. This offers a more complete analog of Szegő’s limit theorem for positive definite functions on free groups, with one entirely new feature: an additional term called ‘zeroth-order’ entropy that reflects the non-amenability of the groups.

We leave further details to
[
9
]
, which also develops applications to large deviations for tuples of random matrices.
