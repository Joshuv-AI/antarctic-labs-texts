The epidermis coordinates multi-scale symmetry breaking in chiral root growth
Authors: Natasha Nolan, Leila Jaafar, Yuanwei Fan, Yuxuan Huang, Lily Murchison, Juliana Ocasio, Joyce G. Onyenedum, Charles T. Anderson, Guy M. Genin, Ram Dixit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Twisted growth serves myriad adaptive functions in plants. Unlike animal motions, plant motions require symmetry breaking during growth and typically involve microtubule-related genes. But how macroscopic twisting emerges from molecular-level perturbations remains unclear. Here, we show that microtubule-based symmetry breaking propagates across multiple organizational scales via the epidermis to produce handed root skewing. At the nanoscale, aberrant patterning of cellulose microfibrils is associated with microscale skewed cell expansion, both of which precede the millimeter scale emergence of helical epidermal cell files. The resulting chiral torsion of the epidermis mediates organ level symmetry breaking in the form of whole-root skewing through macroscale interactions between the root and its surrounding environment. We demonstrate the dominant role of the epidermis by complementation of microtubule activity in the epidermis alone, which is sufficient to restore transverse cortical microtubule orientation, wild-type-like morphology in cortical cells, and straight root growth.

Introduction

Left-right asymmetry and chirality are widespread in unicellular and multicellular organisms1–4. While these traits evolved independently in prokaryotes, plants, and animals, their molecular origins often trace back to cytoskeletal activities. In bacteria such as Helicobacter pylori, generation of the naturally occurring helical cell shape involves cytoskeletal filaments called bactofilin5. Normally, rod-shaped bacteria such as E. coli can also acquire chiral morphology through mutations in the MreB cytoskeleton6. In animals, handedness frequently arises due to the actomyosin cytoskeletal system. For example, overexpression of the myosin 1D motor protein in the epidermis of Drosophila melanogaster larva induces right-handed twisting of the whole larval body7,8. By contrast, the origin of handedness in plants typically involves the microtubule cytoskeleton9, with a few notable exceptions involving non-microtubule-based mechanisms for helical plant growth10,11.

The direction of growth of plant organs is determined primarily by the axis of cell expansion, which is typically perpendicular to the net orientation of cortical microtubules12,13. Cortical microtubules perform this function by spatially guiding the deposition of cellulose and other cell wall materials14–17. In rapidly elongating cells, transversely oriented cortical microtubules lead to the reinforcement of mechanical anisotropy in the cell wall that resists radial cell expansion, channeling growth in the longitudinal direction18. This process produces straight cell files and symmetric plant organs. Mutations in genes encoding tubulin or microtubule-associated proteins frequently act to break symmetry, resulting in aberrantly tilted cortical microtubule arrays and either right-handed or left-handed helical cell files and twisted plant organs2,3,9. Cell-cell adhesion has been demonstrated to couple the helical growth of individual cells to produce organ-level twisting19,20. In contrast, the relationship between organ twisting and the orientation of cellulose microfibrils is more complex. For example, hypocotyl twisting of the csi1 mutant is associated with skewed cellulose microfibrils21, whereas root twisting of the mor1-1 mutant at restrictive temperature is not associated with loss of transverse cellulose microfibril orientation22.

While these previous studies have established the cellular basis of twisted growth, a quantitative understanding of how tissue-specific defects propagate across scales to generate organ-level chirality has been lacking. Specifically, it remains unclear which cell layers control organ morphogenesis, what mechanical principles govern this control, and how molecular-level perturbations cascade through multiple organizational scales. To address these fundamental questions, it would be advantageous to analyze the underlying processes in a unified study system that permits both mathematical modeling and systematic genetic manipulation.

To address this challenge, we used roots of the Arabidopsis thaliana T-DNA-induced spiral123,24 and cellulose synthase-microtubule uncoupling 1 (cmu1)25,26 mutants, which show right-handed and left-handed skewing, respectively, when grown vertically on the surface of an agar medium. SPIRAL1 encodes for a plant-specific microtubule plus-end tracking protein that regulates the polymerization dynamics of cortical microtubules23,24,27,28, whereas CMU1 encodes for a microtubule lattice-binding protein that promotes the positional stability of cortical microtubules25,26. Here, we show that the intrinsic cell file rotation in spr1 and cmu1 mutants is preceded by aberrant patterning of cellulose microfibrils and skewed cell expansion. Using cell layer-specific complementation experiments and mathematical modeling based on torsional mechanics, we demonstrate that the epidermis dominates the emergence of skewed root phenotypes due to a fundamental geometric principle: torsional stiffness scales with the fourth power of radius. Our integrated analysis reveals how symmetry breaking propagates from nanoscale cellulose organization through cellular morphology to tissue architecture, ultimately producing organ-level chirality through mechanical coupling between cell layers and physical interactions with the environment.

Results

Twisted growth alters root gravitropism and obstacle avoidance response

To investigate whether twisted growth of roots affects their ability to respond to mechanical inputs from the environment, we set out by measuring the gravitropic response. For these experiments, we first grew seedlings vertically on agar plates and then rotated the plates either clockwise or counterclockwise to orient the roots perpendicular to the gravity vector. In both cases, the root tip angles of spr1-3 and cmu1 roots diverged in opposite directions from those of Col-0 roots (Fig. 1a). Furthermore, while the kinetics of root tip reorientation of Col-0 were similar between clockwise and counterclockwise rotation, the rate of reorientation of spr1-3 and cmu1 root tips varied between clockwise and counterclockwise rotation as a function of the travel distance needed to reestablish rightward or leftward skewed growth, respectively. Specifically, when rotated clockwise, spr1-3 roots reoriented more rapidly and overshot the gravitational set point angle of Col-0 roots to end up skewed to the right, whereas cmu1 roots reoriented more gradually and remained skewed to the left (Fig. 1a). We next studied how roots respond to an obstacle by placing glass slides perpendicular to the growth paths of roots of 3-day-old seedlings. Upon contacting the glass slide, the tips of Col-0 roots reoriented sideways as previously described29, allowing them to circumvent the barrier. We found that Col-0 roots reoriented to the left or to the right at roughly equal frequencies (Fig. 1b). When spr1-3 roots contacted the glass slide, their tips preferentially reoriented to the right during the obstacle avoidance response (Fig. 1c). In contrast, the majority of cmu1 roots buckled after encountering the glass slide without a significant change in the root tip angle (Fig. 1d). The few cmu1 root tips that did reorient in response to contacting the barrier did not show a bias in growth direction (Fig. 1d). Together, these data indicate that the intrinsic skewedness of spr1-3 and cmu1 roots changes their growth patterning in response to external mechanical stimuli.

Root skewing requires a heterogeneous growth environment

When grown embedded in agar, the roots of both spr1-3 and cmu1 mutants were straight and oriented correctly towards the gravity vector (Fig. 1e, f), despite unchanged epidermal cell file helicity (Fig. 1g). Thus, torsion of the epidermis is not sufficient to generate skewed roots within a homogeneous 3D environment such as an agar medium. To determine whether root skewing is restored within a heterogeneous 3D environment, we examined roots grown in soil. For these experiments, we focused on the spr1-3 mutant because it skews more strongly than the cmu1 mutant (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Soil-grown spr1-3 roots were thicker than Col-0 roots and had helical epidermal cell files (Fig. 1h, i). Additionally, soil-grown spr1-3 roots consistently had regions of pronounced curvature compared to Col-0 roots (Fig. 1j). This finding demonstrates that the root skewing phenotype is maintained during growth through the heterogeneous soil environment, not merely induced upon encountering obstacles.

Furthermore, spr1-3 roots that encountered the bottom of the pot predominantly coiled in a clockwise manner, whereas Col-0 roots that encountered the bottom of the pot grew in various directions (Fig. 1k). Thus, spr1-3 roots grown in soil exhibit chiral morphogenesis akin to seedlings grown on an agar surface.

Root hairs are not required for skewed root growth

Physical interactions between a root and the surface of a solid medium are important for waving root growth30. To determine whether mechanical forces experienced by a root contribute to skewed root growth, we grew seedlings on plates that were placed either vertically (0°) or inclined at an angle of either 45° or -30° (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The strength of gravitropism-mediated friction experienced by a root on the agar surface will be high at 45° and low at -30° because the roots grow either toward or away from the agar surface, respectively30,31. We found that spr1-6 roots, which behave similarly to spr1-3 roots (Supplementary Fig. 1a), tended to skew more at 45° and skewed significantly less at −30°, whereas Col-0 roots grew essentially straight under all conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

During these experiments, we noticed that root hairs on the left side of spr1-3 and spr1-6 roots interacted with the agar surface more frequently than on the right side, whereas Col-0 root hairs showed a slight preference for interactions with the agar on the right side (Supplementary Fig. 2c–e). To test whether biased root hair-agar interactions contribute to root skewing, we crossed the spr1-6 mutant with cpc try, a root hairless mutant32. The hairless roots of the spr1-6 cpc try triple mutant skewed to the same degree as the hirsute spr1-6 roots on both vertical and inclined plates (Supplementary Figs. 2f and 3), demonstrating that root hairs are dispensable for skewed root growth. Notably, the lack of root hairs in the spr1-6 cpc try triple mutant did not significantly alter the pitch of the helical epidermal cell files compared to the spr1-6 mutant (Supplementary Fig. 2g).

Torsion of the epidermis correlates with altered cell morphology and cellulose microfibril orientation

We hypothesized that the helical cell files in twisted mutants are due to spatial variations in cell expansion symmetry. To quantify the morphology and expansion pattern of root epidermal cells, we introduced the 35S:GFP-LTI6b plasma membrane marker33 into Col-0 and spr1-3 plants. We focused on cell geometry in the elongation zone since tilted microtubule arrays are first detectable in this region of the root (Supplementary Fig. 4), as reported previously34. We quantified cell morphology from 2D optical sections at the midplane of epidermal cells. While these measurements capture relative differences between genotypes, they represent projections of complex 3D twisted structures rather than true cell geometry. Unlike the symmetric cells in Col-0 roots, spr1-3 cells had a rightward skewed morphology, demonstrated by the ratios of the top and bottom corner angles (Fig. 2a, b). In addition, the left lateral walls of spr1-3 epidermal cells were slightly longer than the right lateral walls (Fig. 2c), likely due to their asymmetric growth in spr1-3 (Fig. 2d). To determine whether the altered cell shape in the spr1-3 mutant is associated with aberrant patterning of cell wall material, we assessed the orientation of cellulose microfibrils and pectin in root epidermal cells. To measure cellulose orientation in Col-0 and spr1-3 cell walls, 5-day-old seedlings were stained with Pontamine Fast Scarlet 4B (S4B)35, and confocal z-series were collected of elongating epidermal cells of the roots of each genotype (Fig. 2e). Cellulose fibrils were traced in each cell to determine cellulose orientation. Unexpectedly, we found that Col-0 cells with uniformly tilted cellulose microfibrils (−50°) grow straight, while spr1-3 cells with a more transverse crisscross pattern (−25° and −145°) exhibit twisted growth (Fig. 2e, f). This counterintuitive result suggests that the relationship between cellulose organization and growth directionality is more subtle than simple geometric models would predict. To visualize the orientation of pectin fibrils, we labeled newly incorporated pectin in the cell wall using metabolic click labeling36. Quantifying the angles of pectin fibrils did not show a difference between Col-0 and spr1-3 (Fig. 2g, h).

Complementation of spr1-3 or cmu1 in the epidermis alone restores straight root growth

The reduced anisotropic expansion of cortical cells relative to epidermal cells in spiral1 mutants is thought to provide the driving force for root twisting23,34,37. These promoters were chosen because they are known to drive expression in the stele, endodermis, cortex, and epidermis, respectively, based on in situ RNA hybridization38–40, reporter protein expression38–42, and single-cell transcriptomics data43,44. Fluorescence imaging of our complemented spr1-3 lines showed that the SPR1 promoter resulted in SPR1-GFP signal in all cell layers (Fig. 3a) as previously reported23. We developed a mathematical model to understand how the skewing of cell files in different cell layers of the root contributes to the root’s overall skewing. As described in the Supplementary Note 1 the model treated the root as a cylindrical structure made up of concentric cell layers and explored what would happen if only one cell layer had intrinsically skewed cell files while the other layers did not. The skewed layer stored strain energy that was relaxed by root skewing. Results showed that skewing in the outermost cell layer (the epidermis) had the greatest influence on overall root skewing due to the outsized strain energy that it stores due to geometrical effects (Fig. 4e). Skewing of inner cell layers, including the cortex and the endodermis, had much less influence (Fig. 4e). Specifically, the model predicted that if the epidermis alone was skewed in a 10-layer root, it would cause the whole root to skew one third as much as if all 10 layers were skewed. As described in the Supplementary Note 2, the model additionally predicted that if the epidermis became disconnected from inner layers, as occurs in some adhesion mutants, root skewing decreased to nearly zero, even if the epidermis itself remained skewed. This demonstrated that the epidermis, by virtue of being the outermost cell layer with the highest influence on torsional behavior, has an outsized role in controlling skewing of the root. These results indicate that mutations affecting just the epidermis can dominate overall root growth behavior and establish the role of mechanical energy in these morphogenetic phenomena.

The epidermis dictates the orientation and morphology of cortical cells

We next determined whether epidermal complementation influences inner cell layers. For this analysis, we focused on the differentiation zone of the spr1-3 mutant where cell file twisting is the most pronounced (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c), and measured cortical cell width because increased isotropic expansion was most notable in cortical cells of the spr1 mutant34. In the epidermis expressing line N8, which fully complements the twisted root phenotype (Fig. 3f), both the epidermal and cortical cell layers were straight (Fig. 5a, b; Supplementary Movie 6), similar to Col-0 and native-promoter complemented plants (Supplementary Movies 1 and 2). Comparatively, in the partially complementing epidermis expressing line I4 (Fig. 3f), both the epidermal and cortical cell layers only partly returned to straight growth (Fig. 5a, b; Supplementary Movie 5). Unexpectedly, the width of cortical cells was significantly restored toward wild-type-like in both N8 and I4 plant lines (Fig. 5c), even though SPR1-GFP is not highly expressed in the cortical layer of these roots (Fig. 3e). In contrast, expression of SPR1-GFP in cortical cells did not significantly rescue the skewed growth of epidermal and cortical cells (Fig. 5a, b; Supplementary Movie 3) nor the width of cortical cells compared to the spr1-3 mutant (Fig. 5a–c; Supplementary Movie 4). Epidermal tissue integrity is required for skewed root growth

Epidermal entrainment of the cortex could be due to symplastic growth of plant tissues. Such a mechanism requires tight adhesion between the epidermis and cortical cell layers. Cell-cell adhesion in land plants is mediated by a pectin-rich wall structure called the middle lamella45. The quasimodo1 (qua1-1) mutant, which contains reduced levels of pectic homogalacturonan, displays impaired adhesion between epidermal cells and between epidermal and cortical cells46. This mutation was shown to suppress twisted growth in the hypocotyl and petioles of the spr2-2 mutant19. To determine whether cell adhesion is required for skewed root growth, we generated spr1-3 qua1-1 and cmu1 qua1-1 double mutants, both of which exhibited significant epidermal cell peeling due to partial loss of cell adhesion (Fig. 6a–i). Introduction of the qua1-1 mutation strongly suppressed root skewing in the spr1-3 mutant and completely restored straight root growth in the cmu1 mutant (Fig. 6j). Notably, suppression of root skewing was not due to diminished torsion of epidermal cells (Fig. 6k). In addition, cortical cell width was significantly rescued in the spr1-3 qua1-1 double mutant (Fig. 6l). Discussion

Twisted growth of axial organs is widespread in plants2,3, including in wild-type accessions of Arabidopsis47, and is an ancient trait as revealed by the fossil record48. Here, we show that the epidermis is the primary determinant of multilevel asymmetries that underlie skewed root growth. Thus, twisted growth could arise from mutations that affect the epidermis alone, instead of requiring a null mutation in all cell types. This insight might explain the wide prevalence of the twisted growth habit in the plant kingdom.

Our findings strongly support the epidermal-growth-control theory49 and add to the growing body of evidence that the epidermis mechanically constrains the growth of plant organs50–53. As the outermost cell layer, the epidermis is best suited to resist twisting since torsional modulus scales with the fourth power of radius in a cylindrical object like the root, as described mathematically in the Supplementary Note 1. Unlike most other plants, the Arabidopsis root consists of a single cortical cell layer. Although it remains to be determined whether epidermal expression alone will be sufficient to control the growth trajectory of roots containing multiple cortical cell layers, our mathematical model suggests that this will be the case.

In our experiments, growth within agar completely suppressed root skewing, as reported previously23,27,34, whereas growth within soil did not, despite similar epidermal cell file rotation under both conditions. We posit that in a homogeneous environment such as agar, roots experience similar physical forces from all sides, which cancel out leading to straight growth. In contrast, on an agar surface, roots experience physical forces predominantly from one side, which translates into root skewing in 2D. In soil, roots likely experience variable physical forces in 3D leading to root curvature. Regardless of the mechanics driving root skewing, we demonstrate that it does not involve root hairs, which contrasts with the important role of root hairs in root waving30,54. We also found that the intrinsic propensity of the spr1-3 and cmu1 roots to skew overrides gravitropism-induced root curvature. Additionally, root skewing differentially alters the response of spr1-3 and cmu1 roots to solid obstacles. Thus, by influencing how roots respond to environmental cues, twisted growth is likely to significantly impact the overall 3D root architecture.

Loss of epidermal tissue integrity suppresses root skewing and restores cortical cell width to wildtype-like, even though the epidermal cell files remain twisted. One potential mechanism for the non-cell-autonomous effect of the epidermis on the cortex might involve mechanical interactions between cell layers. Alternatively, disrupting the physical connection between the epidermis and cortex might perturb molecular signaling responsible for coordinating the growth of tissues51. Our mathematical model confirms and extends the mechanical principles described previously20, providing quantitative predictions specific to the Arabidopsis root system. Future work will explore whether the reduction of epidermal cell adhesion alters the magnitude and/or directionality of physical forces due to root-agar interactions.

Epidermal cells in the elongation zone of spr1-3 roots exhibit asymmetric lateral expansion and skewed cell morphology, even though cell files appear straight in this region of the root. One possible explanation for the emergence of cell file twisting in the root differentiation zone is that it is an aggregate consequence of the initial cell skewing in the elongation zone. Alternatively, cell file twisting could arise due to torsional strain created by differences in cell elongation rate between different regions of the root, as indicated by our mathematical model of root twisting.

Cell shape and the direction of cell growth correlate with the patterning of cellulose microfibrils in the cell wall, although other wall components such as pectins are also hypothesized to help establish growth anisotropy55. In elongating root cells of Col-0, cellulose staining experiments revealed a uniformly tilted arrangement of cellulose microfibrils in their cell walls, as reported previously35,56. Cellulose microfibrils separate and reorient to allow cell expansion in the longitudinal direction under increased turgor to establish a straight root cell file35. In contrast, S4B staining of cellulose in elongating root cells of spr1-3 showed a crisscross pattern, which hints at the varying directions of cellulose deposition in the different lamellae of the walls of these cells. The crisscross arrangement of cellulose microfibrils might influence how cellulose microfibrils slide with respect to each other57, thus altering the direction of cell growth in the elongation zone of spr1-3 root. In contrast, newly deposited pectin, detected by click chemistry, showed similar patterning in the walls of mature epidermal cells of Col-0 and spr1-3 roots. Together, these data indicate that cellulose, and not pectin, might be causing the asymmetric lateral expansion and skewed cell morphology associated with helical cell files of spr1-3 roots. Nonetheless, our cellulose orientation data reveal an important limitation in our understanding of symmetry breaking. While we can trace the propagation of asymmetry from cellular to organ scales, the mechanistic links between microtubule perturbation and altered cellulose patterning require further investigation. The observation that crisscross cellulose patterns in spr1-3 produce twisted growth, while uniformly tilted microfibrils in wild-type produce straight growth, indicates that additional factors beyond simple microfibril angle must contribute to growth directionality.

Recent work has shown that plants induce tilted cortical microtubule arrays and twisted growth to enable roots to avoid high salt environments58. Similarly, salt stress causes right-handed root skewing in Arabidopsis59, which is associated with rapid proteasome-dependent degradation of SPR1 and reorientation of cortical microtubule arrays60. Based on our gravitropism and obstacle avoidance experiments, we propose that transiently induced twisted root growth might aid in soil penetration and growth past obstacles, akin to circumnutation of the root tip enhancing root exploration61. Our work suggests strategies based on regulation of the abundance and/or activity of specific microtubule-related proteins in the epidermis to engineer root systems to better navigate the mechanical and chemical microenvironment of the soil.

Methods

Plant material and growth conditions

The Arabidopsis thaliana plants used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. For plate growth experiments, seeds were surface sterilized using a 25% bleach solution, resuspended in 0.1% agarose, and stratified at 4 °C for 2 days. Sterilized seeds were grown on agar medium containing 1% w/v Phytoblend agar (Caisson Labs), 0.3% w/v sucrose, and half-strength Murashige and Skoog basal medium (Caisson Labs), pH 5.7 under 16/8-h light/dark photoperiod (120 μmol m−2 s−1 light intensity) at 24 °C. All plate experiments used seedlings after 3–4 days of growth.

Construction of plasmids and generation of transgenic plants

To generate spr1-3 cell layer-specific complemented lines, promoters ~2 kb upstream of the SHORTROOT (SHR), SCARECROW (SCR), WEREWOLF (WER), and ENDOPEPTIDASE (PEP) genes were PCR amplified from Col-0 genomic DNA and cloned into pDONR-P1P5r by means of Gateway BP clonase recombination. All promoters were recombined with a pGAG entry vector, pDONR-SPR1-GFP28, and pDONR-NosGent using Gateway LR clonase recombination. Primer sequences for cloning can be found in Supplementary Table 2. All constructs created using PCR were verified by enzymatic digest, colony PCR, and sequencing. spr1-3 plants were transformed with these constructs using Agrobacterium tumefaciens dip technique. Homozygous T3 lines were selected on gentamycin-containing growth medium. Cell layer-specific complemented cmu1 lines were generated using the same method, but entry constructs were combined with pDONR-GFP-CMU1 to generate a WER:GFP-CMU1 expression construct carrying a gentamycin resistance gene for plant selection. To generate plasma membrane marker lines, spr1-3 was crossed with Col-0 plants expressing the LTI6b-GFP construct. Homozygous F3 lines were selected on Basta-containing (10 mg/L) growth medium. To generate microtubule marker lines, Col-0 and spr1-3 plants were transformed with a 35S:RFP-TUB6 expression construct28 using Agrobacterium tumefaciens dip technique. T2 lines were selected on kanamycin-containing (50 mg/L) growth medium. The spr1-6 cpc try triple mutant line was generated by crossing the spr1-6 mutant with cpc try double mutant. Homozygous F3 generation plants were selected which showed both skewed root growth and complete loss of root hairs. The spr1-3 qua1-1 double mutant line was generated by crossing the spr1-3 and qua1-1 mutants. Homozygous F3 generation plants were used for experiments.

Root skewing experiments

Unless otherwise stated, all analyses were conducted on 3-day-old seedlings grown vertically on agar plates. Plates were either photographed from top or scanned from behind using a CanoScan 4400 F scanner (Canon) at 300 dpi. Root skewing angle from vertical was measured using FIJI62. To visualize interactions between root hairs and the agar surface, roots were imaged using a Leica M205 FCA stereomicroscope with a Plan Apo 1×/0.04 objective.

Soil growth experiments

One plant was grown per 2.25-in. depth pot to assess root growth in soil 2–3 weeks after germination. Plants were grown under 16 h light at 120–140 μmol m−2 s−1 intensity with GE Ecolux with Starcoat F40T8SPX41 40 W 4100 K bulbs, 70% humidity, and 21 °C. For the experiments in Fig. 1j, plants whose roots had not reached the bottom of the pot were selected for examination. In contrast, for the experiments in Fig. 1k, roots that had reached the bottom of the pot were observed to determine their growth direction upon contact with the pot surface. For soil growth experiments, roots were carefully washed in water to remove soil particles while preserving root architecture before imaging.

Gravitropism and barrier assays

Gravity stimulus was applied to 4-day-old seedlings grown vertically by rotating the agar plates either clockwise or counterclockwise so that the roots of each genotype were horizontal with respect to the gravity vector. Seedlings were photographed at roughly 1-h intervals and the orientation of the root tip from horizontal measured using FIJI. For barrier assays, a surface-sterilized glass slide was placed on the agar surface in the path of roots of 3-day-old seedlings growing vertically. For each genotype, the glass slides were placed so that they were roughly orthogonal to the direction of root growth. Roots were imaged at roughly 1-h intervals using a Leica M205 FCA stereomicroscope with a Plan Apo 1×/0.09 objective.

Fluorescence microscopy and cell file imaging

For imaging SPR1-GFP and GFP-CMU1 signal in spr1-3 and cmu1 complemented lines, respectively, seedlings were stained with 10 mg/L propidium iodide63 in distilled water for 15 min, mounted in sterile milliQ water, and imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope equipped with a HC PL APO CS2 40x oil-immersion objective (NA 1.30). GFP and propidium iodide were excited by 488 nm and 552 nm lasers, respectively, and their emissions detected at 500–545 nm and 562–725 nm, respectively. To image epidermal cell files in these lines, propidium iodide-stained seedlings were imaged using a Leica multiphoton microscope equipped with a HC PL IRAPO 40x water immersion objective (NA 1.10). Propidium iodide was excited by using aligned 832 and 1046 nm lasers. For both experiments, Z-stacks were recorded with a 3 μm step size and xy resolution of 1.83 pixels per micron.

Cortical microtubule orientation

Col-0 and spr1-3 seedlings expressing 35S:RFP-TUB6 were mounted in sterile milliQ water and imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope equipped with a HC PL APO CS2 40x oil immersion objective (NA 1.30). RFP was excited by 552 nm laser and emission detected at 562–725 nm. Z-stacks were recorded with a 2 μm step size and xy resolution of 3.51 pixels per micron. Images were digitally reversed and rotated to align the root tip perpendicular to the horizontal prior to measuring microtubule orientation using the FibrilTool plugin64 in FIJI.

Cell morphometry and growth analysis

Col-0 and spr1-3 seedlings expressing 35S:LTI6b-GFP were mounted in sterile liquid ½ Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 0.3% sucrose, pH 5.7, and imaged using a Leica multiphoton microscope equipped with a HC PL IRAPO 40× water immersion objective (NA 1.10). GFP was excited by 488 nm laser and emission detected at 500–600 nm. Z-stacks were recorded with a 3 μm step size and xy resolution of 1.83 pixels per micron. For growth measurements, seedlings were imaged at timepoints 0 and 2 h. Images were digitally reversed prior to measuring cell wall lengths and end wall angles using FIJI. Cell morphology measurements were conducted on 2D optical sections and thus represent simplified projections of 3D cell shapes that are twisted around the root cylinder. Growth was calculated by subtracting cell wall length at 0 h from the length at 2 h for individual cells.

S4B staining and pectin imaging

Col-0 and spr1-3 5-day-old seedlings were stained with 0.01% (w/v) Pontamine Fast Scarlet 4B (S4B) in liquid ½ MS medium for 30 min. Seedlings were washed once with ½ MS then mounted on slides for imaging. Confocal images of Col-0 and spr1-3 elongation zones were collected using a 100 × 1.4 NA oil immersion objective and a 561 nm laser of a Zeiss Axio Observer microscope with a Yokogawa CSU-X1 spinning disk head. Z-stacks were recorded with a 0.2 μm step size and xy resolution of 0.1284 microns per pixel. The raw z-stacks of S4B-stained root epidermal cells were subjected to three-dimensional deconvolution in AutoQuant X2 (Media Cybernetics), and maximum projections of the deconvoluted z-stacks were generated using ImageJ. To measure cellulose microfibril orientation, ten microfibrils per cell were traced in ImageJ starting at the top of the cell.

Pectin patterning was detected by click chemistry as described previously36. Briefly, 3–7-day-old Col-0 and spr1-3 seedlings were selected to visualize the patterning of rhamnogalacturonan-I pectin in the cell walls of root differentiation zone. For each experiment, 3–5 seedlings of each genotype were pooled together in a reaction tube where they were incubated in 2.5 µM Fucose Alkyne (FucAl; Invitrogen in 0% sucrose, ½ MS) for 4 h. Then the seedlings were washed thrice with 0% sucrose, ½ MS solution before incubating in 1 µM Alexa Azide 488 in 0% sucrose, ½ MS for 1 h in the dark. Fluorescently labeled seedlings were washed 3× with 0% sucrose, ½ MS solution prior to confocal imaging. Negative controls were only subjected to labeling with Alexa Azide 488 in 0% sucrose, ½ MS to observe nonspecific fluorescence. For confocal imaging, seedlings were gently mounted on slides and Dow Corning silicon dioxide grease was applied to the corners of the coverslip to avoid crushing the seedlings. Confocal z-stacks were collected using a Zeiss Axio Observer microscope with a Yokogawa CSU-X1 spinning disk head and 63× or 100 × 1.4 NA oil immersion objectives. A 488 nm excitation laser and a 525/550 nm emission filter were used for the detection of pectin patterning in the differentiation zone of Col-0 and spr1-3 roots. Z-stacks were recorded with 0.2 µm step size and xy resolution of 0.2023 (63×) or 0.1284 (100×) microns per pixel. Confocal z-stacks were deconvoluted in AutoQuant X2 (Media Cybernetics) and maximum projections of the deconvoluted z-stacks were generated using ImageJ. Pectin orientation was determined using the FibrilTool plugin64 in ImageJ.

Statistical analysis and data display

Experiments were performed in triplicate, with each n representing a biological replicate unless otherwise mentioned in the figure legend. For statistical comparisons, unpaired two-tailed t-test was used to compare two unrelated datasets and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) plus post-hoc Tukey test was used to compare more than two unrelated datasets, unless stated otherwise in the figure legend. Data analysis and graph generation were performed using either GraphPad Prism v.9.0 or Excel software. Exact statistical tests, n numbers, and P value cut-offs are provided in figure legends.
