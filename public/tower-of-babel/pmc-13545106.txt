Integrated Evidence Generation Planning: Why Medical Affairs is a Critical Partner
Authors: Ronaldo Fujii, Masoud Nouri, Mohammad Khan, Lucija Fleisinger, Jamie Pugh, Gary Male, Antonia Panayi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In an era of rapidly evolving healthcare systems and escalating demands for value demonstration, pharmaceutical companies face unprecedented pressure to generate robust, relevant evidence for a diverse array of stakeholders. Traditional, siloed approaches to evidence generation are no longer sufficient. The development and use of integrated evidence generation plans (IEGPs) have emerged as strategic imperatives—reshaping how organisations align scientific, regulatory and patient-centred objectives across the product life cycle. The IEGPs bring together evidence-generation activities across functions and geographies, to ensure that robust evidence is delivered throughout the product life cycle. In this article, we present IEGP development as a collaborative and strategic process, which enables pharmaceutical companies to demonstrate the holistic value of a new therapy to a broader range of healthcare decision makers. We argue that Medical Affairs professionals are well positioned to lead this initiative, given their in-depth understanding of the evidence generation needs and priorities of patients and healthcare professionals; however, a close and strategic partnership with other key functions, including Market Access and Clinical Development, regions and countries, is vital to the holistic understanding of all key evidence needs and to establish the strategic differentiation required to bring new medicines to patients. Our companion article in this edition of Pharmaceutical Medicine expands on the role of specific subfunctions of Medical Affairs in the development of an IEGP; it also considers how the impact of Medical Affairs’ role can be measured. Please note that in both articles we prefer to use the term ‘IEGP’, but the reader may also see the similar term ‘IEP’ (Integrated Evidence Plan) used in the literature—these terms are used interchangeably in the field.

Key Points

Integrated evidence generation planning enables pharmaceutical companies to demonstrate the holistic value of a therapy to a broader group of healthcare decision makers as well as patients.With an in-depth understanding of the evidence generation needs and priorities of patients and healthcare professionals, Medical Affairs is a critical patient-centred partner in integrated evidence generation planning and is often best placed to lead the process as early as possible in the product life cycle.Closer collaboration between Medical Affairs, Clinical Development, Market Access, Regulatory and Commercial colleagues is required to navigate the increasingly complex regulatory and health technology assessment environments in healthcare systems around the world.

Demonstrating the Value of Therapies Beyond Clinical Efficacy and Safety

Evidence-based decision making is a pillar of modern healthcare and is an essential part of achieving the goal of improving patient outcomes. The scientific evidence that informs healthcare decision-making is becoming increasingly complex, and evidence generation entails significant investment and resources [1]. Development of therapies, from preclinical testing through Phase 1–3 clinical trials and into the post-marketing environment, requires pharmaceutical companies to generate evidence to meet the needs of a diverse range of stakeholders (patients, caregivers, healthcare professionals [HCPs], regulators, payers, patient advocacy groups, health authorities and the scientific research community) [2]. Previously, evidence has been generated sequentially and in silos, leading to inefficiencies throughout the product life cycle. For example, during the pre-launch phase, evidence generation would be led by Research & Development (R&D), and a focus on regulatory approval would delay the evidence planning that would support the product’s value proposition and differentiation [3]. Consequently, beginning Medical Affairs-led evidence generation planning at launch could result in a lack of patient-focused data, potential for negative outcomes from payer/health technology assessment (HTA) submissions and a delay in patient access to the new treatment. Finally, for a mature product, a lack of visibility of activities across global functions could result in local and regional affiliates duplicating efforts and potentially wasting resources. With healthcare systems under increased economic pressure and a need to demonstrate the holistic value of a new treatment, evidence generation planning has had to evolve [4, 5]. An increasing acceptance of real-world evidence (RWE) by regulatory and HTA bodies and the increasing involvement of patients themselves in healthcare decision-making have catalysed the introduction of the integrated evidence generation plan (IEGP).

The objective of the integrated evidence generation planning process is to bring together cross-functional stakeholders and local affiliates to address the evidence needs of external stakeholders efficiently, while also raising awareness of the evidence generation strategy and tactics to ensure alignment across large organisations.

To ensure that robust, appropriate evidence is being generated throughout the developmental timeline, IEGP development is usually initiated no later than when the treatment is entering Phase 2 clinical trial(s). Despite the need for early initiation, early investment presents a risk because most compounds in Phase 1 clinical trials will never reach regulatory approval [6]. While some companies will focus evidence generation on the context of a clinical development plan (CDP) at this early stage, some pharmaceutical companies develop early IEGPs in Phases 1 or 2. In these initial stages, evidence generation planning may be led by teams closely involved in the planning and execution of the clinical trials, including the R&D and Clinical Development teams. The primary evidence generation focus of these teams is demonstrating the risk–benefit of the therapy (efficacy, safety and tolerability data); however, even in its early phases, evidence generation planning should look beyond the evidence required for regulatory approval and consider the minimal evidence needs required for other stakeholders [5]. Health technology assessment authorities, HCPs and patients all have additional evidence generation requirements to support healthcare decision making from their own perspectives. These include data on disease burden, treatment pathways, comparative outcomes, cost effectiveness and relevant patient sub-populations. Generating evidence in these areas is the focus of an IEGP and helps to form a holistic evidence package (with the CDP as the cornerstone of the IEGP) that is consistent with the evidence generation requirements of all relevant stakeholders.

IEGPs Drive Greater Collaboration and Efficiency in Addressing the Evidence Needs of Healthcare Decision Makers

Cross-functional groups and local affiliates within pharmaceutical companies collaborate closely during the IEGP process, often through a dedicated IEGP team that is accountable for development and delivery of the plan. Despite the benefits of an IEGP, establishing a successful process for development of the plan presents multiple procedural and personnel challenges: who is ultimately accountable for delivery of cross-functional priorities? How do we effectively and accurately identify gaps in the literature? How are internal evidence generation activities tracked effectively? How do we prioritise multiple internal and external stakeholder needs? How do we ensure close coordination with other key strategic plans, without replicating them? The IEGP must be tailored to the unique needs and structure of each pharmaceutical company, driving global and local evidence generation in close coordination with other functions across the company. Consequently, it is preferable to align timelines for IEGP development with the development of other critical strategic plans (e.g., Clinical Development, Access, Medical and Publication Plans and Target Product Profile) to ensure strategy and timing are aligned.

Medical Affairs professionals can apply their expertise at each stage of IEGP development: with a patient-centred holistic overview of therapy areas and product development, as well as insights from key external experts, they are well positioned to lead this process, ensuring that all cross-functional/affiliate voices are considered and keeping the focus on optimising patient access in an evolving healthcare landscape. External Insights Inform Strategy and Evidence Needs

External stakeholders provide insights that drive the strategic direction of a product. It is important to consider what their current practices and assumptions are to generate evidence that will best inform decision making. Regulators such as the US Food & Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) can be consulted in scientific consultation forums; payers and HTA body insights can be collated through early scientific advice and market access landscaping; HCP and patient insights can be gathered through advisory boards, advocacy groups and patient experience and preference studies. Cross-functional expertise is vital to ensure different stakeholder insights are translated correctly into actionable evidence needs, with consideration given to whether needs should be addressed in the pre-/peri-launch space or as post-launch or life cycle management requirements.

Prioritisation of Evidence Gaps Requires Consensus and Compromise

Evidence gaps are the areas in which additional evidence generation tactics are required to meet the needs of healthcare decision makers, and are identified by mapping complete, ongoing and planned internal and external tactics to evidence needs. Without automation, this part of the IEGP process relies on manual information exchange and communication across functions and geographies, which can be both inefficient and costly. The study mapping process can be partially or fully automated but will be dependent on how clean and detailed the data are with regard to tactic information. If tactic tracking systems are well integrated across the company, and maintained with up-to-date and accurate information, there can be confidence that evidence gaps are being analysed accurately, leading to appropriate conclusions and efficient evidence planning. However, if systems are disjointed, essential study information is not complete and evidence generation activities are tracked inconsistently between functions, regions/countries, this may lead to inaccurate or incomplete information being considered during the tactic mapping process. If evidence gaps are not analysed accurately, there may be inefficiencies such as duplication of effort or misplaced urgency.

Prioritisation of evidence gaps can often be the most challenging step in a cross-functional setting. For example, a company with an asset launching in the USA will generally want to prioritise evidence generation for FDA approval and not slow down regulatory submissions with the additional elements of an IEGP (which, given current reimbursement frameworks, tend to support ex-US payer approval). It is important to establish governance for decision making and create a forum for collaboration, such as cross-functional workshops in which internal colleagues can discuss their perspectives and agree on how best to prioritise evidence gaps. Medical Affairs, with its scientific expertise and patient-centric holistic view of therapy areas and the product life cycle, is well placed to ask targeted questions that help to prioritise evidence gaps.

Developing Tactics to Address Priority Evidence Gaps

Once the evidence gaps have been prioritised, new tactics can be proposed to address them, beginning with frameworks such as the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome (PICO) and Time (PICOT) formats to inform research question development and align with the overall strategic direction [8]. The use of this framework allows the development of robust research questions and can also feed into the Joint Clinical Assessment (JCA) portion of the European Union (EU) joint HTA regulation (see section 7). An important initial stage of tactic development is data source landscaping, a process that involves scoping and selecting the most appropriate data sources to answer the prioritised research questions [9]. Applying a robust methodology to data source landscaping can enhance the validity of study design, thereby improving the credibility of study results in the eyes of external stakeholders [10]. Important considerations include the feasibility of using real-world data sources and understanding their limitations, biases and whether they are representative of the wider patient population, either in a specific geography or more broadly, depending on the research question [11]. Systematic approaches for identifying data sources can streamline and enhance the transparency of the data source selection process, which ensures that the most relevant data sources are considered to support new tactic development [9, 12].

Once the evidence needs assessment is completed and the evidence gaps and proposed tactics identified with clear accountabilities, risk identification and mitigation, scenario planning and projected timelines, the IEGP can be finalised and disseminated. The finalised IEGP serves as a source of information for individual stakeholders, but it also informs other strategic plans to ensure that evidence generation is aligned with the overall product strategy. The IEGP at this stage also serves as a tool for budget discussions to ensure the required studies are adequately funded.

Optimising Data Sources to Enable IEGP Automation

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) approaches such as large language models (LLMs) are already being leveraged to address some of the procedural challenges; however, it is important to identify the parts of the IEGP process where these technologies can be meaningfully employed. Automation of the IEGP process relies heavily on comprehensive, robust and accurate databases to provide information on evidence generation activities. As well as enabling reliable tracking of study progress and achievement of milestones, having a central repository for study information, including protocols, timelines and study reports, also enables elements of the IEGP process to be completed faster and more accurately than was previously possible. This study information should be utilised alongside literature searches to analyse and understand where evidence gaps exist, and to target evidence generation discussions towards addressing the gaps that represent the most critical priorities for ensuring patient access to the new medicine. There is also potential for AI/ML to be leveraged for predictive analytics (e.g., forecasting disease trends) which can supplement the IEGP. While AI can facilitate many of the stages in a typical IEGP process (see Fig. 1), the ultimate success and impact of a company’s IEGP process still relies on consensus building and innovation through human interactions. Developing simplified, AI-augmented workflows within the IEGP process should not come at the expense of the opportunities for collaboration and engagement both internally and externally.

Navigating Evolving Regulatory and Access Landscapes

With ongoing changes in the external healthcare environment, holistic, patient-focused, end-to-end leadership is required to unlock the potential of the integrated evidence generation planning process. Medical Affairs professionals are in a suitable position to lead these efforts; however, close and strategic collaboration with cross-functional and regional colleagues is essential for navigating a constantly evolving landscape. Overall, there is a need to ensure that the patient voice is captured early in product development—ideally as early as development of the target product profile (which serves as a foundation for the clinical development plan and IEGP). This ultimately increases the likelihood of acceptance by, for example, HTA bodies, and creates greater understanding of real-world situations, given the direct applicability in clinical practice.

The EU joint HTA regulation, which began implementation in a staged fashion by therapy area in 2025, streamlines and centralises the HTA process by consolidating the clinical assessment stage into a regional JCA process. To enable and support this process, pharmaceutical companies can engage in Joint Scientific Consultations (JSCs) to obtain optional, non-binding scientific advice, for example to inform clinical trial design or the scope of assessment [13]. The JCA scope uses an initial PICO framework that is subsequently updated and refined in line with the feedback obtained from the optional JSC and EU Member State Coordination Group/HTA bodies [14]. Fulfilling the PICOs that form part of the assessment should be a key focus of the IEGP; this requires earlier and closer internal alignment of stakeholders in a pharmaceutical company and increased external engagement to reflect and validate the needs of all EU countries.

Medical Affairs professionals can collaborate closely with the Market Access team that may be involved in developing PICO framework content, and the interpretation of goals and results. Medical Affairs can equally play a key role in identifying potential evidence gaps by comparing PICO scoping results with the evidence generation activities mapped in the IEGP. The JCA dossier must be submitted before the Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) opinion stage, which is another reason why implementing an earlier, more efficient IEGP process is beneficial [14]. As pharmaceutical companies update existing IEGP processes (or implement new processes) to respond to these changes, Medical Affairs will need to strengthen collaborations with Market Access and Regulatory Affairs colleagues.

Pricing negotiations are becoming increasingly reliant on optimal evidence generation, and Medical Affairs can lead the response to this evolution in collaboration with Market Access and R&D colleagues. The USA introduced the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) in 2022, which mandates that drug manufacturers pay a rebate to the government if the prices of certain drugs (e.g., those covered by Medicare Part D) increase faster than the rate of inflation [15, 16]. For the drugs that cost the most under the Medicare programme, the IRA also authorises Medicare to negotiate drug prices directly with pharmaceutical companies [15, 16]. Medical Affairs can play an important role in navigating this change by understanding the short- and long-term impacts of the IRA and leading the response from an evidence generation standpoint. Evidence generation strategies should consider the potential future implications of the IRA on any pipeline products, and a Medical Affairs-led IEGP process is well placed to build on these considerations and ensure that evidence answers appropriate research questions.

A strong IEGP generating tailored, robust RWE will support manufacturers during pricing negotiations across geographies. This in turn will support reimbursement outcomes in countries that use international reference pricing approaches. Medical Affairs has the expertise to not only ensure that the appropriate RWE is generated at the right time to support the justification of prices, but to also lead the design and execution of such studies in a patient-centred manner, using novel and innovative approaches.

Conclusions

Integrated evidence generation planning is a critical, strategic component of biopharma asset development. The IEGP process enables cross-functional and regional colleagues within a pharmaceutical company to collaborate and optimise evidence generation tactics according to internal strategy and insights from external healthcare decision makers. Medical Affairs professionals have subject matter expertise, engage directly with key external experts, and can take the lead in understanding and implementing changes in regulations and requirements while ensuring patient centricity. This places them in a strong position to lead the collaborative IEGP process, ensuring that key aspects such as latest developments in healthcare-related policies, patient-centric outcomes and technological advancements are integrated. Efficient planning and implementation of evidence generation strategy and tactics means that appropriate evidence is available at the correct time while optimising resources and managing risks. This maximises the chances of successful and compelling launches of innovative therapies that improve patient outcomes.
