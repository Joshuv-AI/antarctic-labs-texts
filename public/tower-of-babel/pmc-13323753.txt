Techno-economic and policy analysis of solar-wind powered EV charging for sustainable E-mobility
Authors: Amit Kumar Yadav, Vedant Singh, Vibha Yadav, Rohit Khargotra, Tej Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the techno-economic viability of electric vehicle charging stations using a combination of solar photovoltaic and wind turbine technologies is examined, with specific reference to identifying the optimal configuration of wind turbine, solar photovoltaic, battery, and power conversion equipment. The cost of energy, net present cost, and initial capital cost are examined in varying climatic conditions in India. A total of 26 sites are examined to assess the viability of solar photovoltaic, wind turbine, and battery-based electric vehicle charging stations. Key findings of the study reveal that solar photovoltaic, wind turbine, and battery configurations are the most cost-effective for 11 sites, with the lowest cost-effectiveness in Pune and the highest in Bhopal. For the remaining sites, solar photovoltaic and battery configurations are the most viable, with the highest cost-effectiveness in Jodhpur and the lowest in Srinagar. The study also reveals that wind turbine and battery configurations are not viable for any of the sites, as the cost of energy is high. It is also revealed through sensitivity analysis that higher electric vehicle load makes the systems unreliable, while aligning electric vehicle charging capacity with available resources enhances reliability and cost-effectiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49453-8.Keywords: Electric vehicle, Solar photovoltaic, Wind, Cost of energySubject terms: Energy and society, Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

The growth of electric vehicles has increased the need for sustainable and reliable electric vehicle charging infrastructure. The integration of solar technology with electric vehicle charging stations has been identified as a promising approach to reduce dependence on fossil fuels and minimize greenhouse gas emissions1. Furthermore, hybrid approaches to renewable energy systems, such as the use of batteries or hydrogen-based storage systems such as reversible fuel cells can also improve the reliability of such systems and increase their share of renewable energy sources2,3. In addition, various advanced planning techniques, such as geographic information system and multi-criteria decision-making, have been widely employed to optimize the location of solar-based electric vehicle charging infrastructure and improve their efficiency and sustainability4,5.

The global spread of electric vehicles (EVs) has increased significantly owing to their importance in combating the emission of harmful pollutants from the atmosphere6,7. In 2024, EV sales surpassed 17 million units, showing a positive growth trend for the EV industry8. In 2023, electric vehicles accounted for 15.8% of newly sold vehicles, showing a 2.8% increase compared to the preceding year9. In February 2025, electric vehicle sales globally reached 1.2 million units, showing a 50% increase compared to the same period of the preceding year10. Therefore, electric vehicle sales are expected to continue this positive growth trend in 2025. According to projections, electric vehicle sales are expected to grow by at least 17–18% in 2025, surpassing a milestone of 20 million units11. According to statistical projections, by the end of 2025, the global electric vehicle market is expected to reach a huge value of 784.2 billion, and it is expected to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 6.01% and reach 990.4 billion by 202912.

India encourages the widespread adoption of electric vehicles as part of the broader goal of achieving sustainable development and achieving carbon neutrality. The projection of the electric vehicle market in India suggests that it will have a compound annual growth rate of 19.44%, growing from 54.41 billion USD in 2025 to 110.7 billion USD in 202913. The literature currently suggests that India had 5.6 million electric vehicles in use on the roads in 202414. The electric vehicle market in India has also witnessed a surge in sales, with 2 million units sold in 2024 compared to 1.6 million units in 2023, representing a growth of 24%14. The policies of the government in this regard are expected to enhance the sales of electric vehicles, and the chances of the high oil price levels experienced in the preceding year may also attract the interest of potential customers15. The widespread distribution of electric vehicle charging stations (EVCS) will facilitate the adoption of EVs, allowing owners to travel long distances confidently with convenient access to charging stations. However, there will be significant challenges in supplying the growing EVCS with the required electricity from the current system. The system will experience an increase in load peaks due to a considerable rise in electrical load demand. This results in breaches of voltage limits, overloads in transformers and feeders at different voltage levels, and adverse effects on the grid16.

In order to address the energy security dilemma and improve the environment, the imperative is to develop and use renewable energy sources17,18. Powering new energy vehicles using renewable sources, such as solar photovoltaic (PV) and wind, or a mix of the two, might be an appealing solution to the problem of charging new energy vehicles19,20. Several academics have dabbled with EV research, and most new energy vehicles available today are purely electric. Zorlu and Ozcan21, Choi and Bhakta22, Thulasingam et al.23, and Mallikarjun et al.24 focussed different renewable resources in different regions worldwide. for renewable-powered EVCS. It is found that site specific conditions influence economic and feasibility of EV charging stations. The energy management of renewable energy source based EVCS for and performance improvement is reviewed in25,26. Mouli et al.27 consider practical site-specific variations such as seasonal solar availability in Netherlands. Rooftop PV potential is utilized for PV charging. Due to seasonal variation of resources grid connection should be important for charging station. Karmaker et al.28 proposed biogas-PV EV charging stations to reduce grid burden. To evaluate techno economic feasibility HOMER Pro software is used and optimal setup achieved an operating expenditure of $2540, net present cost (NPC) of $56,202, and a cost of energy (COE) of $0.1302/kWh, and CO₂ emissions is reduced by 34.68% compared to grid-based stations. Alghoul et al.29 used HOMER for PV-grid EVCS. It is found that integrating 10 kW solar hybrids set up achieved 2.14% penetration rate. This system offered 6.3 year payback period under feed in tariff scheme followed by 14.7 years of profit. Eom et al.30 investigated the techno-economic performance of grid-connected EVCS installations along highways under different policy scenarios using HOMER software. The authors concluded that enabling the policy mitigation scenario would reduce NPC by 40% and further reduce CO₂ emissions. Different combinations of PV panels, inverter, fuel cell, electrolyzer, hydrogen tank, biomass generator, battery and wind turbine (WT) were analyzed by Guven et al.31 for techno-enviro-economic analysis of EVCS using HOMER Pro tool. The combination of PV, electrolyzer, hydrogen tank, biomass generator, inverter, fuel cell and grid was the most favourable with an NPC of $611,283.50 and an LCOE of $0.0215, while it proved to be the least favourable in terms of CO2 emissions. Karmakar et al.32 performed a techno-economic analysis of PV-powered EVCS for highway installations in four metro cities (Mumbai, Chennai, Delhi and Kolkata) in India using the HOMER grid program. The proposed grid-connected PV-based highway EVCS provides electricity at $0.029/kWh per unit, which is significantly lower than the standard grid cost of $0.080/kWh. The techno-economic and environmental performance of EVCS consisting of solar PV, WT and battery banks was conducted by Alanazi et al.33 for Saudi Arabia using Homer Pro software. It was concluded that the integration of PV/wind/battery/converter system provides the best performance in off-grid scenarios for daytime chargers, with an NPC of $475,110, and of COE of $0.168. While the on-grid configuration prefers a PV/wind/grid/converters setup for nighttime charging, achieving an NPC of $150,316 and a COE of $0.0179. Alshammari et al.34 analyzed economic feasibility of PV/wind/biomass hybrids, with PV-biomass emerging as the most cost-effective option (LCOE: $0.099/kWh). For optimization by HOMER different studies are given in35–37,45–51,58–65. It is powerful tool which oversimplify real world uncertainties such as demand fluctuations, renewable sources intermittency and dynamic EV user.

Proposed design

Five steps were involved in this study’s methodology. The steps involved are proposed design, techno-economic assessment tool selection, designing and simulation, sensitivity and comparative analysis and optimal configuration selection.

Techno-economic and optimization tool selection

The United States National Renewable Energy Laboratory created a microgrid modelling, optimization, and sensitivity analysis program called HOMER68. The HOMER Pro program uses grid search to identify the most economically viable solution and works best during the design phase. The HOMER Optimizer utilizes an approach that does not rely on derivatives to determine the most cost-effective system. The HOMER Pro analysis’s process flow is shown in Fig. 4. EV charging systems incorporate an energy management system whose main functions are priority-based control, load shifting and demand response, state-of-charge-based battery management, and hybrid control with predictive algorithms. The load-following dispatch strategy in HOMER was adopted, in which renewable sources primarily meet load demand, and the battery supplies the deficit. Limitations include that HOMER does not capture uncertainties in solar radiation, wind speed, load demand, economic parameters, component degradation, and grid instability.

HOMER Pro analysis flowchart.

Decision variables, constraints, and objective function

The objective is to fulfil the daily demand for EVCS while selecting the most economical size and operating scenario that minimizes installation and operating costs during the system’s lifetime. After HOMER computes the COE and NPC for all potential combinations, sizes, and operating scenarios, the optimal configuration can be identified by choosing the one with the minimum NPC and COE. The NPC of a component is determined by deducting the present value of all installation and maintenance expenses incurred throughout the project’s duration from the present value of all revenues generated by the component during that timeframe40. Battery storage

Batteries serve as energy storage components in a hybrid renewable energy system, allowing for the accumulation of surplus power provided by renewable sources and subsequent discharge during power disruptions. During the experiment, a nominal 12 V and 1 kWh nominal lead acid battery capacity was employed. In our estimation, a battery will cost ₹16,750 to buy, ₹16,750 to replace, and ₹1675 to operate and maintain annually. In the analysis lead acid battery allows a more realistic techno-economic comparison with other storage options and reflects solutions that are feasible for practical implementation. The HOMER optimizer is used to maximize the battery’s capacity. The battery state of charge (SOC) limits was defined to avoid deep discharge and overcharging. When the battery reached its maximum SOC, excess renewable generation was treated as curtailed energy.

System configuration

Figure 6 illustrates the composition of the PV/WT/battery hybrid EV charging system, which includes converters, batteries, WT, and other components. Batteries are the primary means of storing energy, and PV and WT power sources generate the bulk of the electricity used to power EV charging stations. The extra electricity will be charged to the battery when the PV arrays and WTs produce more energy than is required for charging. Instead, the batteries will provide the load. The hourly electricity output from renewable energy sources is compared with the hourly demand load during the year-long simulation of the proposed hybrid renewable energy system in HOMER Pro. This is done while considering the integrated components’ unit costs, lifespan, and technical specifications. All workable alternatives to satisfy the intended load demand are examined through the simulation process. These solutions consider configurations, sizes, dispatch techniques, and sensitivity characteristics. To find the best setup for the site, HOMER Pro simulates every viable alternative.

Results and discussions

Analysis of optimal configuration

The technical and economic aspects of hybrid renewable energy EV charging stations have been analysed across 26 selected locations in India, as detailed in Table 4, where M represents millions. This study examines three unique types of hybrid EV charging stations: WT/Battery, PV/Battery, and PV/WT/Battery. The WT/Battery configuration is found to be not feasible for Dehradun, Hamirpur, Lucknow, Minicoy, Patna, Shillong, and Srinagar because the wind energy potential at these locations is relatively low and highly variable. Due to the insufficient and inconsistent wind resources, the wind turbines are unable to generate adequate electricity to reliably meet the load demand. Moreover, the low and intermittent wind speeds significantly reduce the turbine’s capacity factor, resulting in poor overall energy production and making the configuration impractical for these sites. Based on COE and NPC hybrid PV/WT/battery based systems are determined to be the optimal choice for EV charging stations in Ahmadabad, Bangalore, Bhavnagar, Bhopal, Minicoy, Mumbai, Panjim, Port Blair, Pune, Patna, and Visakhapatnam, which accounts for 11 out of the total 26 locations. The most cost-effective PV/battery charging station is in Jodhpur (458 kW PV, 0 WTs, 941 batteries of 1 kW, and 63.3 kW converter), with minimum operating costs (OC), COE, NPC, and initial capital costs (IC) of ₹5.08 M/year, ₹33.39/kW, ₹108 M, and ₹32.9 M, respectively. The least cost-effective charging station is in Port Blair and has a maximum NPC, COE, OC, and IC of ₹ 1.44B, ₹446/kW, ₹86 M, and ₹172 M, respectively. The system has 0 kW of PV, 354 WTs, 4538 batteries, and a 236-kW converter.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis examines the impact of variations in load (the quantity of EVs) on the NPC (₹) and COE (₹/kWh) across several locations in India. For PV/WT/battery based systems, Ahmedabad, Bangalore, Bhavnagar and Bhopal are shown in Fig. 8a-d, while for other cities are presented in Fig. 1S (supplementary material). Using the optimal design, the figures depict the impact of load demand changes for COE and NPC of EV charging stations at 26 locations. When the electricity use grows from 300 to 900kWh per day, it is evident that the charging station’s NPC likewise increases. Implications and policy recommendations

The findings demonstrated the ineffectiveness of a uniform national model for renewable energy-based EV charging. Climate zones, resource profiles, and load behavior all influence the optimal charging architecture. Therefore, India should adopt a location-specific deployment strategy. In this study, the PV/WT/battery system performed best in Ahmedabad, Bangalore, Bhavnagar, Bhopal, Minicoy, Mumbai, Panjim, Port Blair, Pune, Patna, and Visakhapatnam. In several other cities, the PV/battery configuration was found to be more suitable. The most economical PV/battery case was Jodhpur, and the most expensive was Port Blair. This suggests that policy support should be based on techno-economic viability rather than being applied consistently across regions.

Prioritize regional deployment over a nationwide rollout

In western and semi-arid regions like Jodhpur, Jaipur, and Ahmedabad, policies should prioritize solar-dominant EV charging infrastructure given their greater solar suitability and lower cost potential. On the other hand, hybrid PV/WT/battery systems are more appropriate in coastal and wind-supportive areas such as Pune, Mumbai, Panjim, Visakhapatnam, and Minicoy, where wind supplements seasonal PV shortfalls. Deployment policies should specifically address storage adequacy and grid-support provisions for more variable eastern and high-rainfall locations like Shillong, Kolkata, and Mumbai, as these sites exhibit greater seasonal variability and thus require stronger balancing capability.

Link capital support to techno-economic performance bands

The results suggested that tiers of public support should be established. Because they have the best short-term demonstration potential, high-performing low-cost sites like Jodhpur and Pune. They should be given priority for early commercial scale-up through capital subsidies, concessional financing, and accelerated depreciation. To increase bankability, moderately priced but strategically significant locations such as Bangalore, Bhavnagar, Bhopal, and Visakhapatnam may require performance-linked assistance. High-cost or high-variability locations such as Port Blair, Srinagar, Ranchi, and Shillong should not be prioritized in the same way as more cost-effective sites. They should receive targeted viability-gap support only when charging access is strategically important for regional inclusion or energy security. This would stop limited public funds from being distributed equally among places with wildly disparate feasibility profiles.

Include complementarities between seasonal resources in planning guidelines

The monthly generation results show a distinct seasonal pattern: PV performance decreases during the monsoon season, while wind output peaks in July. PV output is highest from March to May. This means that seasonal resource matching should be required instead of annual average resource assessment in India’s technical standards for renewable EV charging. Planners should ensure there is sufficient battery backup or optional grid support during low-radiation periods in areas that rely on solar power. Because wind generation can partially offset monsoon-related PV reduction, hybridization should be promoted in wind-supportive coastal and western locations. Therefore, future state-level tariff regulations should classify locations based on the seasonal complementarity of solar and wind resources in addition to geographic location.

Match the size of charging stations to the rise in local EV demand

According to the sensitivity analysis, COE behavior becomes nonlinear in several cities as load increases, and NPC increases steadily as daily load increases. This implies that both inadequate and excessive charging infrastructure can lower economic efficiency. Therefore, instead of relying on standard station templates, state transportation agencies and urban local bodies should link the deployment of renewable EV charging to city-level EV density, fleet composition, and anticipated growth in charging demand. Cities that showed greater stability under load variation, such as Pune, New Delhi, and Thiruvananthapuram, are excellent choices for broader implementation. On the other hand, areas that exhibit greater volatility or cost instability ought to implement phased capacity expansion with recurring demand evaluations.

Prioritize storage and flexibility in variable-resource locations

The findings showed that the performance of renewable-powered charging is more susceptible to fluctuations in Shillong, Kolkata, Mumbai, Minicoy, Port Blair, and Ranchi. Battery storage should therefore not be considered a secondary add-on in policy. Demand management, converter sufficiency, and storage capacity should be considered fundamental design requirements in such cities. Time-based tariff incentives, staggered charging windows, and managed charging are operational strategies that can increase utilization and ease strain on the local system. This is especially crucial when charging demand may increase in the evening, and renewable output is inconsistent.

Tailor public-private partnership models to local conditions

Public-private partnership (PPP) models should not be designed in the same way for every location. In commercially promising cities such as Jodhpur, Pune, Bangalore, and Ahmedabad, private investment can be encouraged more easily through measures such as easier access to land, faster approvals, and incentives for renewable energy projects. In contrast, in remote or high-cost locations such as Port Blair and other difficult-resource regions, PPPs may need stronger public participation, including capital co-funding, minimum-revenue assurance, and utility-backed service contracts. This differentiated PPP approach is more consistent with the study’s evidence than a single national partnership model.

Target R&D on operational and climatic realities

The study also indicates that future innovation efforts should focus on India-specific hybrid charging design, especially for monsoon-affected, coastal, island, and high-variability regions. R&D should therefore prioritize improved storage sizing, hybrid control strategies, seasonal forecasting, and location-specific energy management for renewable-powered charging stations. Pilot projects should be developed in contrasting site types, such as solar-dominant western cities, hybrid coastal cities, and high-variability eastern or island locations, so that policy evolves from comparative field evidence rather than generic national assumptions.

Therefore, the policy implication of this study is that renewable EV charging in India should be planned through a zonal techno-economic strategy: solar-dominant deployment in strong inland solar regions, hybrid PV/WT deployment in suitable coastal and western regions, and storage-supported or selectively subsidized rollout in climatically volatile or high-cost locations. A location-specific deployment strategy can make renewable EV charging infrastructure more cost-effective and reliable. It can also strengthen its role in supporting India’s transition to low-carbon mobility. This case for targeted expansion is reinforced by the study’s estimated annual CO2 reduction potential of 19,872,700 kg in techno-economically favourable locations.

Conclusions

This study comprehensively evaluate EV charging stations fuelled by renewable energy sources, namely PV and wind power. The analysis focuses on 26 meticulously chosen sites throughout diverse climatic condition of India, including technical and economic aspects. The significant techno-economic findings indicate that hybrid PV/WT/battery EV charging stations are the best option for EV charging in Ahmadabad, Bangalore, Bhavnagar, Bhopal, Minicoy, Mumbai, Panjim, Port Blair, Pune, Patna, Visakhapatnam and PV/battery EV charging stations are the best option for RE-EV charging stations in Chennai, Dehradun, Hamirpur, Hyderabad, Jaipur, Jodhpur, Kolkata, Lucknow, Nagpur, New Delhi, Patna, Ranchi, Shillong, Srinagar, Thiruvananthapuram.

Hybrid PV/battery charging stations are most cost-effective in Jodhpur, whereas WT/battery is least cost-effective in Port Blair. These charging stations generate 24,235,000 kWh with reduction in CO2 by 19,872,700 kg.

Among the 26 regions of India, March exhibits the greatest monthly average power output from PV arrays, while July shows the highest monthly average power production from WT arrays. July and October have the lowest monthly average power generation from the WTs and PV arrays. Cities with higher reliance on PV experience seasonal fluctuations. Grid support is crucial in specific cities to maintain energy supply, particularly during months of low solar radiation. Integrating PV with the grid enhances energy stability, especially in regions experiencing significant monsoon impact.

Seasonal fluctuation in PV power is observed for cities with high reliance on PV. Support from the grid is required during low solar radiation months. Grid integration with PV enhances stability in regions experiencing significant impacts from monsoons. The sensitivity analysis’s findings indicate that when the daily demand rises from 300 kWh to 900 kWh, the Jodhpur charging station’s NPC climbs from ₹53.8 million to ₹162 million. In addition, when the demand increases, the charging station’s COE fluctuates by around ₹33.37/kWh. At a charging station load of 600 kWh per day, the COE value reaches a maximum of ₹33.38/kWh and comes to a minimum of ₹33.34/kWh at a charging station load of 660 kWh per day.

Hybrid renewable energy sources of EV charging stations, such as the combination of solar and wind energy with battery power, are technically viable and economically sustainable options to drive the e-mobility revolution in India. The viability of such options is location and demand-driven, and their success will depend on the techno-economic optimization of these options and the implementation of supportive policies and measures, including incentives, R&D, and public-private partnerships.

However, this study is subject to a few limitations. Firstly, the techno-economic analysis is based on site-specific solar and wind power. Therefore, the results might vary for different renewable resource availability. Secondly, the EV charging pattern is considered generic. In practice, this might be different depending on user patterns. Thirdly, the economic analysis is based on certain assumptions related to capital costs, O&M costs, equipment life, and the discount rate. These might vary with technological and market developments. Lastly, this analysis is focused on techno-economic and policy aspects. It does not take into account detailed power system dynamics, grid stability, voltage regulation, and real-time constraints related to EV charging.

Possible future extensions of this work would be to carry out a multi-site regional analysis for different geographical regions with diverse climate conditions. It would be interesting to see how this method would perform under different site-specific conditions. A more realistic stochastic model for charging would be a good option. In this regard, seasonal variations and various charging modes, ranging from fast charging to ultra-fast charging, would be useful. Advanced energy management using AI/ML techniques would be another direction for future work. In addition, a detailed grid integration and life cycle analysis would be very useful.
