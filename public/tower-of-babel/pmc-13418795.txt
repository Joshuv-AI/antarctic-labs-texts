Antipsychotic selection in combination with electroconvulsive therapy for acute schizophrenia: differential efficacy and safety profiles from a real world study
Authors: Tingting Wang, Yang Lu, Minglan Yu, Yi Wang, Hao Yu, Shuangshuang Feng, Junfan Liang, Hongcheng Xie, Jiajun Ren, Jin Zhang, Bo Xiang, Kezhi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectroconvulsive therapy (ECT) used in combination with antipsychotic medications is an important therapeutic strategy for the management of acute episodes of schizophrenia. However, direct comparisons of the efficacy and safety of different antipsychotic agents when administered alongside ECT remain limited.MethodsA total of 465 individuals with acute-phase schizophrenia were included in the analysis. All participants underwent six sessions of bitemporal ECT delivered over a two-week period. The primary endpoint was the reduction rate of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale total score (PANSS-TRR). Secondary outcomes included reduction rates for the PANSS positive symptom subscale (PANSS-PRR), negative symptom subscale (PANSS-NRR), and general psychopathology subscale (PANSS-GRR). Treatment safety was evaluated using the UKU Side Effect Rating Scale (UKU) and the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS). To reduce subjectivity, we conducted reliability training.ResultsSignificant symptomatic improvement was observed in all treatment groups following the ECT course. After adjustment for confounding variables, and applying inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) with Bonferroni correction, compared with ECT alone, clozapine-ECT and sulpiride-ECT showed statistically significant advantages in overall symptom reduction (PANSS-TRR). For positive symptom reduction (PANSS-PRR), clozapine-ECT, quetiapine-ECT, risperidone-ECT, and ziprasidone-ECT were associated with significantly greater improvements. No combination demonstrated statistically significant superiority over ECT alone for negative symptoms or general psychopathology after correction. Pairwise comparisons suggested that amisulpride-ECT was associated with smaller improvements in PANSS-TRR compared with clozapine-ECT, risperidone-ECT, and quetiapine-ECT.ConclusionIn this observational study of patients with acute phase schizophrenia, the comparative effectiveness and tolerability of antipsychotic medications combined with ECT appeared to differ depending on the specific agent used. These preliminary observations require confirmation in randomized controlled trials before guiding individualized treatment selection.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry: ChiCTR2500101865. Introduction

Schizophrenia is a chronic psychiatric disorder affecting approximately 1% of the global population and imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide [1, 2]. The disorder is characterized by a heterogeneous clinical presentation that includes positive symptoms (such as hallucinations and delusions), negative symptoms (including social withdrawal and reduced motivation), and general psychopathological manifestations like anxiety and cognitive impairment [3]. Antipsychotic medications remain the cornerstone of treatment. However, their therapeutic effects often emerge gradually. This delayed onset can be problematic in acutely hospitalized patients, particularly those exhibiting severe psychomotor agitation, violent behavior, or catatonic features, where rapid symptom stabilization is essential to prevent harm to themselves or others [3].

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is a well established adjunctive treatment approach for schizophrenia [4]. While it is widely recognized for its role in treatment-resistant schizophrenia (TRS), ECT is also frequently used in clinical practice for patients experiencing severe acute exacerbations who require rapid symptom control, including those with agitation, aggression, or catatonia [3]. In China, ECT is commonly applied in such circumstances and is incorporated into routine clinical practice guidelines [5]. The therapeutic effects of ECT are believed to involve the induction of controlled seizures that modulate neural circuits and neurotransmitter systems involved in psychiatric symptomatologys [6–8]. Evidence from prior studies indicates that combining ECT with antipsychotic medications can accelerate symptomatic improvement in individuals with schizophrenia [9]. Nevertheless, most previous research has focused primarily on the combination of clozapine and ECT in patients with treatment-resistant schizophrenia [9–11], Comparatively little evidence is available regarding the effectiveness of ECT combined with other antipsychotic agents during the acute phase of schizophrenia. This gap in knowledge is clinically important because antipsychotic drugs differ considerably in their pharmacological properties, including dopamine D2 receptor binding affinity, serotonin 5-HT2A antagonism, and interactions with other neurotransmitter systems [12, 13]. These pharmacodynamic differences may influence both treatment efficacy and adverse effect profiles when these medications are used alongside ECT. For instance, antipsychotics with strong serotonin 5-HT2A antagonism (e.g., clozapine, risperidone) may promote cortical plasticity [14, 15] and synergize with ECT-induced neurobiological changes [16]. In contrast, selective D2/D3 antagonists (e.g., amisulpride) [17] may offer limited synergy [18]. Therefore, we hypothesized that antipsychotics with broader receptor profiles would demonstrate greater efficacy when combined with ECT, particularly in reducing positive symptoms.

To preliminarily explore these issues, the present multicenter real-world study was designed to compare the efficacy and safety of eight commonly prescribed antipsychotic medications administered in combination with ECT, as well as ECT alone, in patients with schizophrenia. The findings are intended to provide real world observational data that may offer preliminary insights to inform individualized treatment decisio making, though further studies are needed to confirm these observations.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter prospective observational study was performed at three psychiatric hospitals in Sichuan Province, China, between 2018 and 2024. Eligibility criteria included: (1) age between 18 and 65 years; (2) diagnosis of schizophrenia according to DSM-5 criteria; (3) stable dosing of one of the eight study antipsychotics during the ECT course, or absence of antipsychotic treatment; (4) baseline PANSS total score ≥ 60; and (5) Han Chinese ethnicity. Exclusion criteria were: (1) organic mental disorders; (2) neurological diseases or brain injury; (3) substance abuse within the previous six months; (4) ECT treatment within the past six months; (5) severe physical illness; and (6) inability to provide informed consent. As shown in Fig. 1, among the 496 patients initially screened, 465 met the eligibility criteria and were subsequently included in the final analysis. Twenty eight individuals were excluded because they were receiving two or more antipsychotic medications or were treated with rarely prescribed agents (haloperidol, perphenazine, lurasidone, or perphenazine hydrochloride hydrate). An additional three participants were excluded because their PANSS reduction rates were considered statistical outliers (> 3 standard deviations from the mean). Participants were divided into nine groups based on the use and type of antipsychotic medication (as shown in Fig. 1): Sulpiride-ECT (n = 21), Ziprasidone-ECT (n = 16), Clozapine-ECT (n = 55), Risperidone-ECT (n = 202), Quetiapine-ECT (n = 31), Olanzapine-ECT (n = 52), Amisulpride-ECT (n = 32), Aripiprazole-ECT (n = 22), and ECT-alone (n = 34). Patients were assessed with the PANSS at baseline and following ECT intervention, and the reduction rate was computed for further analysis. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committees of the participating centers and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500101865).

Treatment protocols

All antipsychotic medications were administered in closed inpatient wards under direct nursing supervision, and oral cavity checks were performed to ensure treatment adherence. Participants continued their established antipsychotic regimens during the ECT treatment course. All patients underwent six sessions of bitemporal ECT administered over a two-week period using a Thy Matron IV device, consistent with Chinese clinical practice guidelines [19, 20]. Anesthesia was induced with etomidate (0.16–0.2 mg/kg) in combination with succinylcholine (1.0 mg/kg). Atropine sulfate (0.01 ml/kg) was administered pre-procedure to reduce airway secretions. The initial electrical stimulus dose was set at approximately two-thirds of the patient’s age and subsequently adjusted based on seizure adequacy. Information regarding concomitant medications, including mood stabilizers, antidepressants, and sleep medications, was recorded for all participants.

Symptoms reduction rate

The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) was administered at two time points: T1 (the day before the first ECT session, 3:00–6:00 PM), and T2 (the morning following the final ECT session, 8:00–11:00 AM). All PANSS assessments were performed by trained psychiatrists who were blind to the study grouping and had passed a standardized reliability test (intraclass correlation coefficient for PANSS total score = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.89–0.94). Four indices of symptom improvement were calculated: the total score reduction rate (PANSS-TRR), positive symptom reduction rate (PANSS-PRR), negative symptom reduction rate (PANSS-NRR), and general psychopathology reduction rate (PANSS-GRR). Side effect evaluation

Adverse effects were evaluated after completion of the six ECT sessions using the UKU Side Effect Rating Scale (UKU) [21] and and the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) [22, 23]. Patients were instructed to report only symptoms that were new or had worsened since the start of ECT. Pre‑existing symptoms documented at baseline were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Differences among treatment groups were assessed using one-way ANOVAs for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. For the primary analysis, analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) were performed with PANSS reduction rates (TRR, PRR, NRR, and GRR) as dependent variables and antipsychotic treatment group as the fixed factor. Covariates included age, sex, age at onset, duration of illness, education level, log-transformed chlorpromazine equivalent dose (CPZeq) [24, 25], concomitant medications (mood stabilizers, antidepressants, and sleep medications), tobacco use, and alcohol use. When significant main effects were detected, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni correction. To address imbalances in sample sizes across treatment groups (e.g., ziprasidone-ECT, n = 16; sulpiride-ECT, n = 21), we used inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to reduce selection bias and balance baseline covariates. Propensity scores for the nine groups were estimated using multinomial logistic regression (CBPS) including all baseline covariates listed in Table 1. Stabilized IPTW weights were trimmed at the 5th and 95th percentiles. Weighted ANCOVA was then performed with PANSS reduction rates as dependent variables, treatment group as the fixed factor, adjusting for the same covariates. Bonferroni correction was applied for 32 pairwise comparisons (8 drug groups × 4 outcomes). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 or R4.5.1. A two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 was applied.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 465 participants were included across nine treatment groups (eight antipsychotic-ECT combination groups and one ECT-alone group). The demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics differed significantly across groups (Table 1). Significant variation was observed in several demographic variables, including age (F = 3.307, p = 0.001) and level of urbanization (χ² = 16.60, p = 0.035). Clinical characteristics also differed among groups, including duration of illness (F = 4.552, p < 0.001) and baseline PANSS subscale scores for positive symptoms (F = 2.631, p = 0.008) and negative symptoms (F = 2.824, p = 0.005). In addition, chlorpromazine-equivalent antipsychotic dosage (CPZeq) varied significantly between groups (F = 18.639, p < 0.001). Differences were also observed in the use of concomitant medications such as antidepressants, mood stabilizers, and sleep medications (all p < 0.05).

To account for the baseline imbalances identified above, a series of one-way ANCOVAs were conducted to examine the effect of the treatment group on PANSS reduction rates (PANSS-TRR, PRR, NRR, GRR), adjusting for potential confounders including age, gender, urbanization, education, disease course, baseline PANSS scores, chlorpromazine equivalent doses, and concomitant medications (Table 2).

After controlling for all covariates, the main effect of the treatment group remained statistically significant across all four outcome measures. We found that the drug group had a significant effect on the improvement rate of PANSS-TRR (F (8, 443) = 5.097, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.084), with gender (p = 0.027) and alcohol use (p = 0.005) also emerging as significant covariates. The type of antipsychotic medication likewise exerted a significant main effect on PANSS-PRR (F (8, 443) = 4.407, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.074). Among the covariates, baseline PANSS-P score showed the strongest influence (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.240), followed by alcohol use (p = 0.021) and log-transformed chlorpromazine equivalent dose (p = 0.030). A significant effect of treatment group was also observed for PANSS-NRR (F (8, 443) = 3.604, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.061). Baseline PANSS-N score emerged as the dominant covariate (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.135), with alcohol use (p = 0.006) and gender (p = 0.037) also contributing significantly. Similarly, a significant group difference was found for PANSS-GRR (F (8, 443) = 4.263, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.071), with baseline PANSS-G score (p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.111) and alcohol use (p = 0.022) identified as significant covariates. In summary, after rigorous adjustment for confounding variables, the type of antipsychotic medication used in combination with ECT was found to be a significant determinant of symptomatic improvement across all PANSS subdomains. Baseline symptom severity and alcohol use consistently emerged as important predictors of treatment outcomes.

Overall efficacy of antipsychotic-ECT combinations

As depicted in Fig. 2, substantial symptom improvement was observed across all treatment groups following completion of the ECT course. The distribution of PANSS reduction rate measures, including PANSS-TRR, PANSS-PRR, PANSS-NRR, and PANSS-GRR, met the assumptions of normality. After adjustment for relevant covariates (Table 2), the specific antipsychotic medication administered in conjunction with ECT was found to significantly influence treatment outcomes.

Comparative efficacy relative to ECT-alone (IPTW-weighted analysis)

After IPTW weighting to balance baseline covariates, weighted ANCOVA with Bonferroni correction revealed that several antipsychotic-ECT combinations showed statistically significant advantages over ECT alone in specific symptom domains (Table 3). For positive symptom reduction (PANSS-PRR), four combinations were significantly superior to ECT alone: clozapine-ECT (MD = 62.69, p = 0.014), quetiapine-ECT (MD = 67.06, p = 0.027), risperidone-ECT (MD = 61.04, p = 0.037), and ziprasidone-ECT (MD = 58.17, p = 0.018). Aripiprazole-ECT (p = 0.140) and olanzapine-ECT (p = 0.119) showed numerical improvements but did not reach statistical significance after correction. For overall symptom reduction (PANSS-TRR), clozapine-ECT (MD = 65.05, p = 0.035) and sulpiride-ECT (MD = 57.63, p = 0.044) demonstrated statistically significant advantages over ECT alone. Ziprasidone-ECT (MD = 58.77, p = 0.055) and quetiapine-ECT (MD = 63.82, p = 0.099) showed marginally significant trends. For negative symptom (PANSS-NRR) and general psychopathology (PANSS-GRR), no combination achieved statistical significance after Bonferroni correction (all corrected p > 0.05), although several combinations (e.g., sulpiride-ECT, ziprasidone-ECT, clozapine-ECT) showed numerically large improvements in negative symptoms.

Head-to-head comparisons between antipsychotic-ECT combinations

Direct pairwise comparisons were conducted to evaluate differences in efficacy among the antipsychotic-ECT regimens (Table 4). Among these treatments, the amisulpride-ECT combination consistently demonstrated relatively weaker therapeutic effects across several symptom domains. Specifically, amisulpride-ECT produced significantly smaller reductions in total PANSS scores compared with clozapine-ECT (MD = 24.38, p = 0.006), risperidone-ECT (MD = 21.72, p = 0.003), and quetiapine-ECT (MD = 23.83, p = 0.020). Similar findings were observed across other symptom dimensions, particularly when compared with risperidone-ECT (PANSS-PRR: MD = 15.95, p = 0.008; PANSS-NRR: MD = 19.11, p = 0.021; PANSS-GRR: MD = 15.32, p = 0.002). Additionally, amisulpride-ECT showed lower efficacy than clozapine-ECT for general psychopathology symptoms (PANSS-GRR: MD = 15.10, p = 0.024). Olanzapine-ECT also demonstrated a tendency toward comparatively lower efficacy. For example, reductions in total symptoms and general psychopathology were significantly smaller than those observed with risperidone-ECT (PANSS-TRR: MD = 13.50, p = 0.048; PANSS-GRR: MD = 10.50, p = 0.008). However, several of these differences did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni correction.

Safety profile

Using multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) adjusted for age, sex, disease duration, concomitant psychotropic medications (sleep medication, antidepressant, mood stabilizer), and antipsychotic dose (chlorpromazine equivalent), significant overall differences among the nine groups were observed for other side effects (p = 0.034) and gastrointestinal side effects (p = 0.001), but not for psychiatric (p = 0.063), neurological (p = 0.178), or autonomic side effects (p = 0.146) (Table 5). The amisulpride-ECT group showed the highest overall proportion of any adverse effect (84.4%), followed by quetiapine-ECT (77.4%) and clozapine-ECT (74.5%); most events were mild (scores 1 to 2). The lowest incidence was in the ECT-alone group (41.2%). Bonferroni corrected pairwise comparisons revealed that only the gastrointestinal symptom score was significantly higher in the quetiapine-ECT group than in the olanzapine-ECT group (p = 0.011). No other pairwise comparisons reached significance. High severity adverse reactions were rare across all groups.

Discussion

This multicenter real world study compared eight antipsychotic-ECT combination regimens with ECT monotherapy in patients with acute phase schizophrenia. The findings revealed significant variability in therapeutic outcomes across the different combinations, suggesting that the choice of antipsychotic for augmentation may influence the clinical effectiveness of ECT, particularly in the domain of positive symptoms.

Substantial symptom improvement was observed across all treatment groups following the ECT course. After adjusting for potential confounders and weighting, the type of antipsychotic medication combined with ECT remained a significant determinant of treatment response across all PANSS reduction measures (all p < 0.001, Table 2). These findings offer preliminary evidence that the magnitude of benefit from ECT augmentation may differ depending on the pharmacological agent used.

Relative to ECT alone after IPTW weighting and Bonferroni correction, several antipsychotic-ECT regimens produced greater reductions in overall and positive symptoms, although the magnitude of improvement differed across medications. For overall symptom reduction, clozapine-ECT and sulpiride-ECT appeared statistically superior to ECT alone, while ziprasidone-ECT and quetiapine-ECT showed marginally significant trends. For positive symptom reduction, clozapine-ECT, quetiapine-ECT, risperidone-ECT, and ziprasidone-ECT demonstrated statistically significant advantages. These findings, while preliminary, are broadly consistent with prior evidence supporting clozapine-ECT combination in treatment-resistant schizophrenia [10, 26], and they raise the possibility that comparable benefits might also be observed in acute-phase patients, particularly for positive symptom control. If confirmed, this could be of clinical relevance given the importance of rapidly controlling delusions and agitation in acutely ill patients [3]. In contrast, none of the combinations showed a statistically significant advantage over ECT alone for negative symptoms or general psychopathological symptoms after Bonferroni correction, although several combinations (e.g., sulpiride-ECT, ziprasidone-ECT, clozapine-ECT) exhibited numerically larger improvements in negative symptoms. Several possible explanations for this pattern are worth considering. One possibility is that the relatively brief two week ECT course used in this study may have limited the ability to detect substantial changes in these more persistent symptom domains. Another possibility is that pharmacological augmentation may offer only limited benefit for negative symptoms during the acute stage of treatment. However, these hypotheses require further testing in studies specifically designed to address these questions.

The biological mechanisms responsible for the enhanced effects of antipsychotic-ECT combinations remain incompletely understood, but several explanations are plausible. ECT and antipsychotic medications may influence overlapping neural circuits, particularly within the prefrontal cortex, striatum, and thalamus, potentially enabling a more comprehensive normalization of dysregulated brain activity [27–29]. ECT has also been proposed to transiently alter blood-brain barrier permeability, which could facilitate greater central nervous system penetration of antipsychotic drugs and increase receptor occupancy [20, 30]. Furthermore, ECT is known to induce neuroplastic processes, including increased synaptic remodeling, elevated brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression, and hippocampal neurogenesis [16, 31–35]. These neurobiological changes may create a permissive environment for adaptive responses to antipsychotic treatment. Additional mechanistic studies incorporating neuroimaging, pharmacokinetic measurements, and molecular biomarkers are needed to better clarify these interactions.

Direct pairwise comparisons revealed additional differences in effectiveness among the antipsychotic-ECT regimens. Notably, the amisulpride-ECT combination consistently produced smaller reductions in total PANSS scores compared with clozapine-ECT, risperidone-ECT, and quetiapine-ECT (Table 4). A similar pattern was observed across several symptom domains. Olanzapine-ECT also tended to demonstrate relatively lower efficacy, including significantly smaller improvements in general psychopathological symptoms compared with risperidone-ECT (p = 0.008, Table 4). Together, these findings offer preliminary evidence that the pharmacological properties of the antipsychotic agent may meaningfully influence the therapeutic benefit achieved when combined with ECT. In the present cohort, clozapine and risperidone appeared to show comparatively stronger augmentation effects, but these results should be interpreted with caution pending replication.

The reasons underlying these differences remain uncertain but may be related to variations in receptor binding characteristics. Clozapine and risperidone display broad receptor profiles, including strong antagonism at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, which may interact favorably with ECT-induced changes in neurotransmitter systems such as dopamine, serotonin, glutamate, and GABA [6–8, 12, 13]. In contrast, amisulpride is a relatively selective antagonist of dopamine D2 and D3 receptors, potentially limiting its capacity to influence the broader neurochemical systems affected by ECT [12]. In addition, ECT has been shown to modulate dopaminergic signaling [36], affects glutamatergic neurotransmission [37], and BDNF expression [38]. Antipsychotics capable of enhancing these processes may therefore exhibit greater therapeutic synergy. Future research employing neuroimaging techniques and biomarker analyses could help clarify these pharmacodynamic interactions.

The covariate analysis identified several factors associated with variability in treatment outcomes. Alcohol consumption was consistently associated with greater symptom improvement across all PANSS domains (p < 0.05), a finding consistent with recent observations reported by Xie et al. [39]. Although the biological explanation remains unclear, it is possible that overlapping neurobiological pathways influenced by both alcohol exposure and ECT, such as dopaminergic and glutamatergic signaling, may contribute to this effect [40, 41]. Baseline symptom severity emerged as the strongest predictor of subsequent improvement, particularly for positive symptoms (partial η²=0.240). Patients presenting with more severe baseline symptomatology tended to demonstrate larger reductions following treatment. In addition, higher antipsychotic doses showed a modest association with greater reductions in total and positive symptoms, consistent with a possible dose-response relationship that merits further investigation.

Evaluation of adverse effects revealed a complex association between treatment efficacy and tolerability. Clozapine-ECT and risperidone-ECT were among the more effective combinations but were associated with relatively higher rates of side effects (49.1% and 47.7%, respectively; Table 4). Quetiapine-ECT also demonstrated considerable efficacy but had the highest overall incidence of adverse events (58.1%), particularly involving autonomic and psychiatric symptoms. In contrast, ziprasidone-ECT and olanzapine-ECT exhibited comparatively favorable safety profiles among the combination regimens, with side effect rates of 31.3% and 32.7%, respectively. However, the clinical efficacy of olanzapine-ECT appeared somewhat more modest relative to other combinations. If confirmed by future research, these findings might inform individualized treatment decisions. Clinicians should weigh the need for rapid symptom control against the potential risk of treatment-related adverse effects when selecting an antipsychotic agent to combine with ECT.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, owing to the observational design, causal relationships cannot be definitively established, despite the use of IPTW weighting and multivariable adjustments. Residual confounding by indication or unmeasured factors (e.g., treatment adherence prior to admission, illness insight, psychosocial support) cannot be entirely ruled out.

Second, the ECT regimen consisted of a fixed course of six bitemporal sessions delivered over two weeks. While this protocol reflects routine clinical practice for acute phase treatment in China, it may not represent the full therapeutic potential of ECT, particularly for symptom domains that may require longer treatment courses, such as negative symptoms or general psychopathology. Therefore, the lack of significant advantage for combination therapies over ECT alone in these domains should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with extended ECT courses are needed to clarify whether delayed benefits emerge.

Third, although IPTW weighting was used to balance covariates, the small sample sizes of certain groups (e.g., ziprasidone-ECT, n = 16; sulpiride-ECT, n = 21) may still limit statistical power for detecting clinically meaningful differences, and findings related to these smaller groups should be regarded as exploratory.

Fourth, the absence of long-term follow up data prevents evaluation of the durability of treatment response, the risk of relapse, and the trajectory of late emerging adverse effects. Given that schizophrenia is a chronic condition, the sustainability of symptom improvement and functional recovery beyond the acute phase is of critical clinical importance.

Fifth, the study population was restricted to Han Chinese inpatients recruited from three psychiatric centers in Sichuan Province. This homogeneity may enhance internal validity but limits the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic, geographic, or outpatient populations. Differences in genetic background, healthcare systems, and cultural attitudes toward ECT could influence both treatment efficacy and tolerability.

Finally, despite the inclusion of commonly prescribed antipsychotics, several agents (e.g., paliperidone, haloperidol, lurasidone) were not represented in sufficient numbers to allow analysis, and all combination therapies were evaluated in the context of acute inpatient treatment. Whether similar comparative effectiveness profiles would emerge during maintenance therapy or in treatment-resistant populations remains to be determined.

Conclusion

In summary, this multicenter observational study suggests that both the effectiveness and tolerability of antipsychotic-ECT combination therapy in acute phase schizophrenia may vary according to the antipsychotic agent used. The most consistent statistically significant associations were observed for positive symptom reduction, specifically with clozapine-ECT, quetiapine-ECT, risperidone-ECT, and ziprasidone-ECT. For overall symptom reduction, clozapine-ECT and sulpiride-ECT were associated with numerically greater improvements compared with ECT alone. No statistically significant associations were detected for negative symptoms or general psychopathology after correction for multiple comparisons. Exploratory head‑to‑head comparisons suggested that amisulpride-ECT and olanzapine-ECT were associated with relatively smaller improvements across several domains, whereas clozapine and risperidone appeared to show comparatively stronger augmentation effects. These findings, if replicated, could assist clinicians in tailoring individualized treatment strategies for acute schizophrenia. Nevertheless, larger randomized controlled trials with extended follow up are needed to confirm these findings and to further elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the interaction between ECT and antipsychotic medications.
