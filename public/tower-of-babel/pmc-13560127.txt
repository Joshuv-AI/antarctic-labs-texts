Region-specific carbon metabolism underlies floridean starch accumulation in rhizoid-like filament cells of Pyropia yezoensis
Authors: Chengzhen Meng, Hongye Xin, Ka Bian, Wenlong Hu, Xinyu Zhu, Xianghai Tang, Yunxiang Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPyropia yezoensis thalli exhibit pronounced cellular differentiation throughout their growth and development. In particular, basal rhizoid-like filament cells (R-region cells) differ significantly from other thallus cells in cell morphology, photosynthetic physiology and carbon metabolism. Previous studies have shown that abundant floridean starch granules accumulate in R-region cells, whereas only few floridean starch granules are present in neighbouring vegetative cells (V-region cells). However, the specific mechanism underlying floridean starch accumulation in R-region cells remains unclear.ResultsIn this study, basal cells of the P. yezoensis thallus were first divided, according to cell morphology, structural features and functional differences, into lower R-region cells and upper V-region cells. Anthrone colorimetric quantification, iodine staining and transmission electron microscopy collectively confirmed that floridean starch content was significantly higher in basal R-region cells than in upper V-region cells. By combining physiological measurements with integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, we further provided mechanistic insights into floridean starch accumulation in R-region cells. First, compared with V-region cells, R-region cells showed a significantly lower growth rate and globally reduced energy metabolic activity, features that are consistent with reduced carbon consumption and may contribute to the net accumulation of floridean starch. Second, on this basis, R-region cells promoted the directed allocation of carbon skeletons towards starch biosynthesis through enhanced expression of UGP (UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase) genes and increased UGPase activity, thereby increasing UDP-glucose availability for floridean starch synthesis, while downregulating the expression of genes encoding starch-degradation-related enzymes. These coordinated changes promoted starch synthesis and suppressed starch degradation, jointly contributing to the efficient accumulation of floridean starch in R-region cells.ConclusionsBased on the cellular differentiation characteristics of the P. yezoensis thallus, this study compares cells from distinct thallus regions and provides integrated physiological, biochemical, transcriptomic and metabolomic evidence that region-specific carbon metabolism is associated with floridean starch accumulation in R-region cells. Background

Pyropia yezoensis is one of the most economically important cultivated red macroalgae in East Asia and has also emerged as a valuable model for understanding how intertidal photoautotrophs coordinate growth with environmental resilience [1–4]. In its natural habitat, the thallus is repeatedly exposed to rapid fluctuations in light, temperature, hydration and inorganic carbon availability during tidal cycles. Such environmental variability imposes strong constraints on carbon acquisition, carbon storage and the redistribution of fixed carbon among growth, maintenance and stress adaptation [5–8]. Consistent with this ecological setting, recent genomic and physiological studies have shown that P. yezoensis possesses diverse mechanisms for inorganic carbon acquisition and extensive transcriptional plasticity in response to intertidal stress, underscoring the central importance of carbon metabolic regulation in this species [1, 3, 7].

In red algae, floridean starch is the principal reserve carbohydrate. Although its granule architecture is broadly reminiscent of starch in land plants, floridean starch differs substantially in both composition and biosynthetic organization. In rhodophytes, floridean starch is generally regarded as an amylopectin-rich reserve glucan, although species-level variation in polymer structure has been reported [9, 10]. More importantly, floridean starch biosynthesis differs fundamentally from the plastidial pathway characterized in land plants and green algae. Whereas plant and green algal starch is synthesized in chloroplasts using ADP-glucose as the immediate glucosyl donor, red algal floridean starch is synthesized in the cytosol and uses UDP-glucose as the precursor, without requiring plastidial transport of activated sugar nucleotides [11]. With the rapid expansion of red algal genome sequencing and functional annotation, key genes involved in floridean starch metabolism have now been identified in multiple species, including Chondrus crispus, Porphyridium purpureum, Gracilaria changii, Pyropia haitanensis and P. yezoensis, enabling increasingly complete reconstruction of this metabolic pathway [1, 3, 12–14].

Consistent with this emerging genomic framework, accumulating evidence indicates that floridean starch metabolism is highly dynamic and responds to both cellular carbon status and environmental perturbation. Previous studies have shown that external factors such as irradiance, salinity and nutrient availability can markedly alter starch content in P. yezoensis and related red algae [15–17]. Beyond these environmental drivers, floridean starch levels also vary with intracellular carbon balance and growth state. In the unicellular red alga Porphyridium aerugineum, floridean starch accumulation increases markedly during the late logarithmic phase, indicating that starch storage is closely linked to shifts in cellular carbon demand and metabolic state [18]. Likewise, Chamberlain and Evans [19] reported that floridean starch levels in Ceramium rubrum remain low during the early stages of tetraspore and carpospore development but increase substantially as spores mature, further indicating developmental stage-dependent regulation of floridean starch metabolism. More direct evidence linking floridean starch metabolism to intracellular carbon dynamics has also been obtained in P. yezoensis, in which perturbation of chloroplast carbonic anhydrase reduces carbon-concentrating capacity and growth while increasing starch accumulation [20], suggesting that starch remodelling can be triggered by shifts in intracellular carbon supply-demand balance and carbon allocation patterns, rather than by external stress alone. Collectively, these observations indicate that floridean starch accumulation is not governed by a single environmental cue. Instead, it reflects the integrated outcome of photosynthetic performance, cellular carbon status, developmental demand and stress-responsive metabolic reprogramming, highlighting that carbon partitioning in red algae is tightly coupled to both metabolic requirements and environmental adaptation.

Despite progress in understanding carbon metabolism in P. yezoensis, current knowledge remains largely limited to differences among life stages and organism-level responses to environmental stress [7, 21]. In contrast, functional differentiation of carbon metabolism among distinct tissues or cell types within the multicellular thallus has received little attention [22]. Although the P. yezoensis thallus lacks a true root-stem-leaf axis or vascular system, its cells exhibit clear regional differentiation and functional specialization [23, 24]. Previous studies in Porphyra umbilicalis, together with our own work, indicate that floridean starch content varies significantly among cells from different thallus regions [25]. In particular, basal rhizoid-like filament cells (R-region cells), which are essential for thallus attachment and stress resistance, may exhibit distinct carbon allocation patterns. However, the metabolic mechanisms underlying region-specific differences in floridean starch accumulation remain poorly understood.

Herein, we combined cytological observations, physiological and biochemical measurements, transcriptomic profiling and metabolomic analysis to compare basal R-region cells with upper vegetative cells (V-region cells) in P. yezoensis thalli. We show that R-region cells accumulate substantially more floridean starch despite having lower photosynthetic capacity, and that this phenotype is associated with reduced carbon consumption, altered energy metabolism, enhanced UDP-glucose formation and reduced expression of starch-degradation genes. Together, these findings establish a region-specific metabolic framework for floridean starch accumulation in differentiated P. yezoensis cells and provide a basis for understanding how carbon storage is coordinated with cellular specialization in red algal thalli.

Materials and methods

Algal cultures

Pyropia yezoensis strain RZ was obtained from the Key Laboratory of Marine Genetics and Breeding, College of Marine Life Sciences, Ocean University of China (Qingdao, China). Thalli were cultured in sterilized natural seawater supplemented with Provasoli’s enrichment solution (seawater: medium = 50:1, v/v) at 10 °C under a 12:12-h light: dark cycle with a light intensity of 50 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. The medium was renewed every 3 days. Thalli with similar size and morphology (10–12 cm in length) after approximately 50 days of cultivation were randomly selected for subsequent experiments. All samples were collected 6 h after the onset of the light period to minimize potential effects associated with diurnal variation.

Collection of cells from distinct regions

Laser microdissection was performed using a Leica LMD7000s system (Leica Microsystems, Germany) to collect cells from distinct regions of P. yezoensis thalli. Healthy thalli with uniform morphology and clearly distinguishable regional characteristics were selected for microdissection. The R-region cells and the V-regions cells were identified based on their distinct cellular morphologies and differentiation characteristics.

Before microdissection, thalli were rinsed three times with sterile seawater to remove attached impurities and microorganisms, then carefully flattened onto the membrane of a steel-frame slide. A small volume of sterile seawater was added to maintain tissue hydration and prevent morphological changes during sample preparation. Under microscopic observation, the boundaries between different thallus regions were identified based on cell morphology, and cutting paths were manually traced on the screen to precisely target the desired cell regions while minimizing inclusion of adjacent tissues.

The excised materials were collected into sterile 2-mL tubes prefilled with sterile seawater, allowing rapid sinking and reducing sample loss during collection. After collection, samples were examined under the microscope to confirm that the targeted regions had been successfully isolated and that obvious contamination from neighboring regions was minimized. The verified samples were subsequently transferred using sterile forceps into sterile 2-mL tubes, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use.

Starch content determination and iodine staining

For starch analysis, thalli were oven‑dried, ground into powder, and 3 mg dry weight was used to quantify floridean starch with a starch assay kit (Solarbio, China; BC0705) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For iodine staining, thalli were rinsed thoroughly with sterile seawater to remove surface debris, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, then decolorized in 70% ethanol for 48 h. Samples were stained with Weigert’s iodine solution for 30 min. Floridean starch content was quantified using twenty independent biological replicates.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) observation

Samples from different thallus regions were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 4 h, rinsed with PBS (3 × 15 min), and post‑fixed in 1% OsO₄ for 2 h, followed by PBS rinses (3 × 10 min). Dehydration was performed in graded ethanol/acetone (30–100%, 10–15 min per step). Samples were infiltrated with acetone: EPON812 (1:1) for 1 h, then pure EPON812 overnight, and polymerized at 37, 45, and 60 °C (24 h each). Ultrathin Sects. (50–90 nm) were cut, mounted on copper grids, and stained with 2% uranyl acetate (15–30 min) and lead citrate (5–10 min). Imaging was performed with a JEM‑1400Flash TEM (JEOL, Japan).

Measurement of the chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

Cells from distinct thallus regions were collected as described in the section Collection of cells from distinct regions. The collected cells were subsequently used for chlorophyll fluorescence measurements using a fluorescence kinetic microscope (Photon Systems Instruments, Czech Republic) to evaluate photosynthetic physiological differences. Briefly, thalli with good growth status and clearly distinguishable cell regions were selected. Cell samples from different regions were carefully excised and placed on glass slides. Sterile seawater was added to maintain sample hydration, followed by placement of coverslips and gentle pressing to remove air bubbles. The edges of the coverslips were sealed with transparent fast-drying sealant to prevent water evaporation and physiological changes during dark adaptation and measurement.

After the sealant was completely solidified, the Micro FluorCam7 fluorescence imaging system was used for measurement. Target cells were first located under a 20× bright-field objective and then switched to the POS1 fluorescence channel. The fluorescence signal intensity was calibrated under the Flashes mode with initial settings of EI Shutter = 2, Sensitivity = 36%, and Blue Flashes = 15%, followed by adjustment according to the real-time image quality. After focusing, the Flashes light source was turned off, and samples were dark-adapted for 15 min before measurement. Following completion of the program, clearly focused cells were selected as regions of interest for chlorophyll fluorescence parameter analysis. All measurements were performed with fifteen biological replicates.

Growth measurement

Thalli with uniform size and healthy growth were selected for analysis. The R-region and V-region were determined according to the criteria described above. To ensure consistent boundary definition across different time points, a small perforation mark was made at the upper boundary of the V-region at Day 0 using the laser microdissection system, and this mark was used as a fixed reference for subsequent measurements. The R/V boundary was determined based on morphological landmarks and consistently applied at all subsequent time points. The areas of the R- and V-regions were outlined and measured using ImageJ according to the defined boundaries. Ten biological replicates were analyzed for each region. The initial area was denoted as A₀, and the area after culture for t days was denoted as Aₜ. A schematic illustration of the detailed measurement workflow is shown in Fig. 1. Rubisco activity, ATPase activity, ATP content and UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase (UGP) activity

Cells from distinct thallus regions were collected as described in the section Collection of cells from distinct regions. Specifically, 0.1 g fresh weight (FW) of sample was used for the assays of Rubisco activity, ATP content and UGPase activity, whereas 0.2 g FW of sample was used for the mitochondrial ATPase activity assay. All measurements were performed using commercial assay kits (Suzhou Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All assays were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Transcriptome data processing and analysis

Cells from distinct thallus regions were collected as described in the section Collection of cells from distinct regions. Three independent biological replicates were prepared for each sample. Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, China). RNA quantity and integrity were evaluated with a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in PE150 mode. Raw reads were quality‑filtered with fastp (v0.22.0) to obtain clean data. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Gene‑level read counts were generated with HTSeq (v0.9.1), and expression levels were normalized to fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 (v1.38.3) using thresholds of |log2(FoldChange)| > 1 and p < 0.05. Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases.

Metabolome data processing and analysis

Cells from distinct thallus regions were collected as described in the section Collection of cells from distinct regions. Six independent biological replicates were prepared for each sample. Samples were freeze‑dried (Scientz‑100 F) and ground (MM 400, 30 Hz, 1.5 min). About 30 mg powder was extracted with 1.5 mL pre‑chilled (− 20 °C) 70% methanol containing internal standards, vortexed every 30 min (6 times), centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane for UPLC‑MS/MS. UPLC‑ESI‑MS/MS was performed on an ExionLC™ AD system coupled to a QTRAP mass spectrometer (SCIEX) using an Agilent SB‑C18 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm). Mobile phases were water (0.1% formic acid, A) and acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid, B). The gradient was 95% A/5% B to 5% A/95% B in 9 min, held 1 min, returned to 95% A/5% B in 1.1 min, and equilibrated 2.9 min. Flow rate 0.35 mL min⁻¹, column 40 °C, injection 2 µL. ESI settings: 500 °C, 5500 V (positive)/−4500 V (negative), GSI 50 psi, GSII 60 psi, CUR 25 psi, CAD high. MRM mode was used with optimized DP/CE for each transition. After data preprocessing and quality assessment, differential metabolites were identified using |log₂(FoldChange)| > 0.8, variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, and p < 0.05.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) validation

DEGs were randomly chosen for qRT‑PCR validation of the RNA‑seq data. Total RNA was isolated from algal tissues using the Plant RNA Extraction Kit (OMEGA Bio‑tek, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First‑strand cDNA was synthesized with the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (AG11728, Accurate Biology, China). qRT‑PCR was carried out using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (AG11701; Accurate Biology, China) on a Thermo Fisher QuantStudio™ 5 PCR System (Thermo, USA). The thermal program consisted of 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with UBC as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences are provided in Table. S1.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD (n ≥ 3). Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, USA). A two‑tailed t‑test was used to assess differences between cells from distinct regions of P. yezoensis thalli (p < 0.05).

Results

Classification and phenotypic features of basal cells in P. yezoensis thalli

On the basis of cell morphology and differentiation status, basal cells in P. yezoensis thalli were classified into three cell types along the basal-to-apical axis: basal R-region cells, intermediate transitional cells (T-region cells), and upper V-region cells (Fig. 2). R-region cells were predominantly oval, with dark-brown plastids occupying most of the intracellular space, and a thin filament-like structure was visible at the cell tail. By contrast, V-region cells were polygonal and tightly packed, with centrally positioned, star-shaped plastids that occupied much of the cell volume. T-region cells were considered as an intermediate region connecting the R- and V-regions along the differentiation gradient. These observations indicate clear regional differentiation in basal thallus cell morphology. Because the present study focused on the comparison of two distinct differentiated cell types, only R- and V-region cells were included in subsequent analyses, whereas T-region cells were excluded owing to their intermediate characteristics.

Variation in floridean starch content within cells from distinct regions of P. yezoensis thalli

Floridean starch content was first quantified in different thallus regions using the anthrone colorimetric method. As shown in Fig. 3A, the starch content in R-region cells reached 269.40 mg/g, significantly higher than that in V-region cells (106.35 mg/g; p < 0.0001).

Comparison of floridean starch accumulation in cells from distinct regions of P. yezoensis thalli. A Quantification of floridean starch content. Data are presented as mean ± SD from twenty independent biological replicates. Statistical analysis was performed by two-tailed t-test. **** p < 0.0001. B Iodine staining results (scale bar = 50 μm). C Transmission electron microscopy observations (scale bar = 1 μm). Fs labels floridean starch

This regional difference was further validated by iodine staining and TEM. Because endogenous pigments in P. yezoensis can interfere with iodine-based color development, thalli were decolorized before staining. After staining, R-region cells exhibited strong and broadly distributed brown-positive signals, and most rhizoids in the R-region cells also showed clear positivity. By contrast, only a small proportion of V-region cells exhibited weak staining (Fig. 3B). TEM observations were consistent with the staining results (Fig. 3C): numerous starch granules were present in the cytoplasm of R-region cells, whereas V-region cells contained markedly fewer granules, and some cells lacked detectable granule-like structures.

Together, these results demonstrate that floridean starch accumulation is markedly higher in R-region cells than in V-region cells.

Variation in photosynthetic capacity within cells from distinct regions of P. yezoensis thalli

Photosynthesis is the primary pathway for carbon assimilation in algae and provides essential precursors for the biosynthesis of insoluble storage polysaccharides, including floridean starch. To determine whether photosynthetic traits differed between R- and V-region cells, we measured two key PSII parameters: maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm) and effective quantum yield (ΦPSII). As shown in Fig. 4A and B, both Fv/Fm and ΦPSII were significantly lower in R-region cells than in V-region cells (p < 0.0001). Given the central role of Rubisco in photosynthetic carbon fixation, we further compared Rubisco activity between the two cell populations (Fig. 4C). Rubisco activity in R-region cells was significantly reduced compared with V-region cells (p < 0.01), retaining only 54.26% of the activity observed in V-region cells. Together, these results show that R-region cells have lower photosynthetic energy conversion and carbon assimilation capacity than V-region cells.

Variation in relative growth rate and energy metabolism within cells from distinct regions of P. yezoensis thalli

Cell growth and energy metabolism influence the consumption and redistribution of photosynthates. Consistent with this, R- and V-region cells showed significant differences in growth and energy status. As shown in Fig. 5A, the relative growth rate was significantly higher in V-region cells than in R-region cells across all four time intervals (p < 0.0001), with V-region cells exhibiting approximately 3.5-fold higher growth rates than R-region cells. Similarly, total ATP content (Fig. 5B) and mitochondrial ATPase activity (Fig. 5C) were significantly higher in V-region cells (p < 0.05), reaching 1.61-fold and 2.13-fold of those in R-region cells, respectively. These results indicate that R-region cells exhibit reduced growth activity and lower energy metabolism compared with V-region cells.

Variation in transcriptomic profiles within cells from distinct regions of P. yezoensis thalli

Transcriptome sequencing of R-region and V-region cells generated 353,714,360 clean reads in total. The Q30 values were all above 94.12%, indicating high sequencing quality (Table. S2). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear separation between the two cell regions (Fig. S1A). In addition, correlation coefficients among the three biological replicates within each group were all above 0.92, while correlations between the two groups were below 0.10 (Fig. S1B). These results indicate distinct gene expression profiles between R-region and V-region cells. Using the thresholds |log₂(FoldChange)| > 1 and p < 0.05, we identified 3,603 DEGs (Fig. S1C). Among them, 1,603 genes were upregulated in R-region cells, and 2,000 genes were upregulated in V-region cells. As shown in Fig. S1D, hierarchical clustering of the 3,603 DEGs grouped the three biological replicates from the same region together, supporting good reproducibility and consistent expression patterns (Table. S3). To validate the reliability of the RNA-seq data, 12 representative DEGs were selected from the genes discussed in this study for qRT-PCR validation. The qRT-PCR results showed a strong correlation with RNA-seq expression levels (FPKM values), with a linear fitting coefficient of R² = 0.9083 (Fig. S2), confirming the reliability of the transcriptomic dataset.

To further investigate the potential functions of the DEGs and the biological processes they are involved in, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. Among the top 20 enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways, several photosynthesis-related categories were significantly enriched, including “Photosynthesis”, “Thylakoid”, “Plastid”, and “Carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms”. In addition, multiple pathways related to primary metabolism were also significantly enriched, covering carbohydrate, amino acid, and fatty acid metabolism. Representative pathways included “Fructose and mannose metabolism”, “Hexose metabolic process”, “Lysine biosynthesis”, “Arginine biosynthesis”, and “Fatty acid biosynthesis”. These results indicate significant metabolic differences between cells from different regions of P. yezoensis thalli.

To further characterize regional differences in key biological processes, we analyzed the differential expression of genes involved in photosynthesis, cell cycle, DNA replication, and the mitochondrial respiratory chain, and visualized the results using heatmaps. A total of 23 photosynthesis-related DEGs were identified (Fig. 6A), including 14 genes associated with light reactions and 9 genes encoding enzymes involved in the Calvin cycle. Genes encoding core photosynthetic components were significantly upregulated in V-region cells, and this pattern indicates stronger photosynthetic electron transport activity in V-region cells. Consistently, the ATPase-related gene (I4F81_012240) was also significantly upregulated in V-region cells. In addition, genes encoding key Calvin cycle enzymes showed the same trend, suggesting stronger carbon assimilation capacity in V-region cells than in R-region cells. For cell proliferation pathways (Fig. 6B), DNA replication core genes and key cell-cycle regulators (including E2F and CDC20) were all downregulated in R-region cells relative to V-region cells. For energy metabolism (Fig. 6C), the gene encoding mitochondrial respiratory chain complex V (H+-ATPase; I4F81_002278) showed 2.38-fold higher expression in V-region cells than in R-region cells, indicating a higher energy metabolic level in the V-region cells.

E Glycolysis and the TCA cycle-related genes. F Lipid biosynthesis-related genes. The color scale represents relative expression levels normalized by Z-score, with red indicating high expression and blue indicating low expression

Because DEGs enrichment analysis also highlighted multiple pathways related to primary metabolism, we further examined regional expression patterns of genes involved in energy and biosynthetic metabolism. Figure 6D shows genes related to amino acid biosynthesis, all 17 detected DEGs were upregulated in V-region cells. In glycolysis/TCA pathway (Fig. 6E), PGK (phosphoglycerate kinase; I4F81_012714), GAPA (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase A; I4F81_009581), and FH (fumarate hydratase/fumarase; I4F81_012614) were all higher in V-region cells. Lipid-related genes showed mixed regional patterns (Fig. 6F): ACC subunit genes (acetyl-CoA carboxylase subunits; I4F81_000445, I4F81_004904) were higher in V-region cells, whereas PLA1 (phospholipase A1; I4F81_006219, I4F81_008038, I4F81_012581) and PLA2 (phospholipase A2; I4F81_001085) were higher in R-region cells. These results suggest that R-region cells exhibit a distinct regulatory regime for phospholipid metabolism and may indicate region-specific responses to local environmental stress.

Variation in metabolomic profiles within cells from distinct regions of P. yezoensis thalli

Metabolomic profiling was performed to compare metabolite patterns between R- and V-region cells. PCA showed that biological replicates clustered closely within each group, while the two groups were clearly separated. The first two principal components explained 48.74% (PC1) and 23.63% (PC2) of the total variance. In addition, QC samples clustered tightly, indicating good LC–MS stability and data quality suitable for downstream analysis (Fig. S5A).

To further resolve group differences, we applied orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), a supervised multivariate method that removes unrelated variation and improves differential metabolite detection. Pairwise comparison between regions generated a robust model (R²X = 0.637, R²Y = 1.000, Q² = 0.992). Both R²Y and Q² were > 0.9, supporting high model reliability and predictive performance (Fig. S5B). The OPLS-DA score plot (Fig. S5C) showed clear separation between R-region and V-region samples, suggesting distinct metabolic phenotypes between the two cell populations.

Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were screened using combined multivariate and univariate criteria: |log₂(FoldChange)| > 0.8, VIP > 1, and p < 0.05. As shown in Fig. S5D, 264 DAMs were identified in total, including 221 upregulated and 43 downregulated metabolites in R-region cells. These results reveal distinct metabolic profiles between R-region cells and V-region cells, with an overall trend toward higher metabolite accumulation in R-region cells (Table. S4).

KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on the differentially accumulated metabolites identified between cells from distinct regions of P. yezoensis thalli (Fig. 7A). Significantly enriched pathways included “Glycerolipid metabolism”, “Oxidative phosphorylation”, “Butanoate metabolism”, “Citrate cycle (TCA cycle)”, “Pyruvate metabolism”, and “Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism”. These pathways are mainly associated with carbon metabolism, lipid metabolism, and amino acid metabolism. The metabolomic enrichment pattern was consistent with the transcriptomic results, further supporting regional differences in energy metabolism and biosynthetic activity in P. yezoensis thallus cells.

To investigate metabolite-level differences in cells from distinct regions of P. yezoensis thalli, we performed circular heatmap analyses of DAMs related to the TCA cycle and amino acids, glycerol esters and free fatty acids, and phospholipid-associated lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) and lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE). As shown in Fig. 7B, six differential amino acids were identified, including L-leucine, L-tyrosine, L-isoleucine, L-tryptophan, L-glutamine, and L-proline. All six showed significantly higher abundance in R-region cells than in V-region cells. Among TCA cycle-related metabolites, different compounds showed different trends. Upstream/core intermediates, including citrate, isocitrate, and 2-oxoglutarate, were significantly enriched in R-region cells, whereas the downstream metabolite fumarate was more abundant in V-region cells. Figure 7C shows DAMs related to glycerol esters and free fatty acids, including 20 glycerol esters and 29 free fatty acids. Heatmap clustering revealed strong regional separation for both metabolite classes. Most metabolites accumulated at higher levels in R-region cells. Representative examples include 1-g-linoleoyl-glycerol, monolinolenin, hexadecanedioate, and arachidonate, which were 12.24-fold, 6.53-fold, 1.79-fold, and 1.99-fold higher in R-region cells, respectively. In contrast, only eight metabolites were higher in V-region cells, including 2-linoleoylglycerol, 1-linoleoylglycerol, and octadecenoic acid. Figure 7D presents DAMs related to LPC and LPE, including 21 LPCs and 15 LPEs. Similar to the pattern observed for glycerol esters and free fatty acids, most phospholipid-related metabolites were more abundant in R-region cells. The top five metabolites with the largest fold differences between regions were LysoPC 22:6, LysoPE 16:1, LysoPE 16:1 (2n isomer), LysoPC 17:2 (2n isomer), and LysoPC 17:2, with levels in R-region cells that were 17.78-fold, 8.21-fold, 7.95-fold, 6.19-fold, and 6.12-fold those in V-region cells, respectively.

Combined transcriptome and metabolome analysis

To examine whether regional differences in floridean starch accumulation were associated with changes in starch-related carbon metabolism, we integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets to reconstruct the floridean starch metabolic pathway in P. yezoensis thalli (Fig. 8A). Most upstream glycolytic and sugar-phosphate intermediates involved in starch biosynthesis, including glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP), fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (F1,6BP), fructose-6-phosphate (F6P), glucose-6-phosphate (Glc-6P) and glucose-1-phosphate (Glc-1P), did not differ significantly between R- and V-region cells. By contrast, UDP-glucose (UDP-Glc), a direct precursor for floridean starch biosynthesis, was significantly enriched in R-region cells, reaching 2.85-fold the level observed in V-region cells (Fig. 8B).

Consistent with this metabolite pattern, transcriptome analysis showed that UGP, which encodes UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase, was significantly upregulated in R-region cells. Enzyme activity assays further showed that UGPase activity was 3.02-fold higher in R-region cells than in V-region cells (p < 0.01) (Fig. 8C). In contrast, transcripts encoding starch-degradation-related enzymes, including α-amylase (AMY) and β-amylase (BAM), were more abundant in V-region cells than in R-region cells (Fig. 8A). These coordinated expression and activity patterns suggest that R-region cells favour UDP-Glc production through enhanced UGP-associated conversion of Glc-1P to UDP-Glc, while showing lower transcript abundance of genes associated with starch turnover.

In addition to UDP-Glc, UDP-galactose (UDP-Gal), galactose-1-phosphate (Gal-1P) and floridoside were significantly enriched in R-region cells, reaching 2.73-, 2.76- and 5.94-fold the levels in V-region cells, respectively (Fig. 8B). These metabolites all had VIP values greater than 1, indicating that they contributed strongly to the metabolic separation between the two cell populations. To further assess whether gene expression, metabolite abundance and floridean starch content covaried across biological replicates, we performed Pearson and Mantel correlation analyses (Fig. 8D). Pearson correlation analysis showed positive correlations among UDP-Glc, UDP-Gal, Gal-1P, floridoside and floridean starch content, indicating that the accumulation of these soluble carbohydrate intermediates was coupled with starch deposition across samples. Mantel analysis further identified significant associations between UGP expression, UDP-Glc abundance and floridean starch content (p < 0.05). Together, these transcriptomic, metabolomic, enzyme-activity and replicate-level correlation data support UGP as a candidate metabolic node linking UDP-Glc accumulation with region-specific floridean starch deposition in R-region cells.

Discussion

As a multicellular macroalga, the thallus of P. yezoensis exhibits clear cellular differentiation. Basal R-region cells are specialized for attachment and differ markedly from upper V-region cells in both morphology and physiology. Previous studies have shown that structural and functional differentiation within a single organism is often accompanied by rebalancing of carbon pools, biomass allocation, and storage strategies [22, 26]. Therefore, in this study, we combined physiological, biochemical, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses to compare energy metabolism and carbon partitioning between basal R-region cells and upper V-region cells of P. yezoensis. These analyses revealed a region-specific metabolic program underlying the preferential accumulation of floridean starch in R-region cells.

In typical photoautotrophic systems, carbon storage is often expected to increase with photosynthetic input. However, our results indicate that this relationship is not linear in the P. yezoensis thallus. Microscopic chlorophyll fluorescence parameters (Fv/Fm and ΦPSII), together with Rubisco activity, showed that light-energy conversion and potential Calvin-cycle carbon fixation were significantly lower in R-region cells than in V-region cells. The transcriptome supported these physiological observations, as genes associated with light reactions and the Calvin cycle were expressed at lower levels in R-region cells. Despite this reduced photosynthetic capacity, floridean starch content was markedly higher in R-region cells. This apparent uncoupling between photosynthetic input and storage accumulation suggests that floridean starch accumulation in the thallus is determined not simply by carbon fixation rate, but by the balance among carbon supply, growth demand, respiratory consumption, and storage turnover. This view is consistent with studies in plants and algae showing that storage-compound accumulation is usually determined by global reconfiguration of carbon and energy fluxes, rather than by carbon fixation rate alone [27–29]. Studies in microalgae have shown that nutrient or stress-induced growth reduction can redirect carbon metabolism toward storage compounds, including starch and lipids [30, 31]. In Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and other photoautotrophic microalgae, nitrogen deprivation triggers broad metabolic and transcriptional changes associated with accumulation of energy-rich carbohydrates and lipids [30]. In addition, inhibition of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase signaling in C. reinhardtii reduces growth and increases starch and lipid content, indicating that signaling-mediated growth restraint can reshape carbon and energy metabolism [31]. In the present study, R-region cells showed a markedly lower relative growth rate and a lower overall level of energy metabolism. Genes associated with cell-cycle regulation, glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and mitochondrial energy conversion were also generally downregulated. We therefore propose that R-region cells operate in a low-growth, low-respiration, low-turnover state. Under this physiological background, the reduced consumption of carbon skeletons may contribute, to some extent, to the accumulation of a net carbon surplus, even when photosynthetic input is relatively weak. This surplus may then be channeled into storage compounds, including floridean starch, fatty acids, and amino acids.

Beyond reduced metabolic consumption, R-region cells also appear to direct carbon skeletons more efficiently toward floridean starch synthesis through differential regulation of key metabolic nodes. UGPase, which converts glucose-1-phosphate into UDP-glucose, links soluble sugar metabolism with storage-polysaccharide biosynthesis. In this study, integrated transcriptomic and qRT-PCR analyses showed that UGP (I4F81_004642) was upregulated in R-region cells (Fig. S2). Consistent with this transcriptional pattern, UGPase activity was also significantly higher in R-region cells than in V-region cells. This coordinated increase in gene expression and enzyme activity was accompanied by elevated UDP-glucose abundance in the metabolome, suggesting enhanced precursor supply for floridean starch biosynthesis. Functional studies in Phaeodactylum tricornutum further support the importance of UGPase in carbon allocation. Silencing UGP reduces UGPase activity, resulting in decreased chrysolaminarin accumulation and a shift in carbon allocation toward lipids, whereas increasing UGPase activity through UGP manipulation enhances chrysolaminarin accumulation and alters lipid production by redistributing carbon flux [32, 33]. Although chrysolaminarin in diatoms and floridean starch in red algae are distinct storage polysaccharides, these studies support the broader concept that UGPase activity can influence the partitioning of fixed carbon among soluble sugars, storage carbohydrates and other carbon sinks. This is also consistent with findings in higher plants showing that UGP is a key node in carbon-allocation regulatory networks [34, 35]. Besides, starch accumulation in R-region cells is also likely influenced by reduced degradation. The starch degradation-related genes AMY and BAM were significantly downregulated in R-region cells, implying a lower turnover rate of floridean starch in this region. Evidence from red algal systems supports the idea that floridean starch levels are regulated not only by biosynthesis but also by degradation and post-translational control. In Cyanidioschyzon merolae, overexpression of glycogenin CmGLG2 increases floridean starch accumulation, whereas target of rapamycin (TOR) signaling regulates starch degradation through phosphorylation-dependent control of glucan water dikinase [36]. These findings are consistent with the view that floridean starch accumulation is an actively regulated storage process rather than a passive consequence of photosynthetic overflow. Thus, the starch-rich phenotype of R-region cells in P. yezoensis is likely driven by the combined effects of reduced carbon consumption, enhanced precursor supply, and decreased degradation flux.

Zeeman and Solhaug [37] pointed out that starch accumulation is not merely a passive consequence of photosynthetic overflow, but an actively regulated carbon reserve tightly coupled to growth rhythms and environmental responses. In fact, carbohydrates such as starch are often preferred storage products in plants and algae under stress conditions because of their compact storage form, highly reduced energy state, and capacity to provide energy for cell survival and proliferation under adverse conditions [38, 39]. In this ecological context, floridean starch accumulation in basal cells may provide a local carbon and energy reserve that buffers environmental fluctuations and supports long-term cell maintenance. Because basal R-region cells contribute to attachment and are directly exposed to unstable coastal microenvironments, their differentiated carbon-partitioning strategy may represent a functional adaptation rather than merely a metabolic by-product. Notably, floridean starch accumulation has also been reported in bipartite cells within conchocelis-stage sporangial filaments [40]. Although these stages differ in developmental context and ploidy, their convergent starch accumulation suggests that floridean starch may play important roles across multiple life-history stages.

In summary, this study demonstrates that region-specific carbon metabolism underlies floridean starch accumulation in R-region cells of P. yezoensis. We also acknowledge that additional mechanisms may contribute. Two testable hypotheses emerge from our findings. First, differentially expressed sucrose transport proteins and other carbohydrate-related membrane transporters may alter the distribution of low-molecular-weight soluble carbohydrates between cell regions, thereby influencing local starch accumulation. Second, TOR-dependent phosphorylation of starch-metabolism proteins may modulate starch synthesis or degradation in a region-specific manner, as suggested by recent work in C. merolae [41, 42].

From this perspective, future studies should focus on stage-specific differences in floridean starch accumulation and utilization during key life-cycle transitions in P. yezoensis, particularly from the sporangial filament stage to conchospore germination and during archeospore germination. Combining gene-function perturbation, isotope tracing, spatial metabolomics, and phosphoproteomics will further clarify how storage metabolism is dynamically coordinated with cellular differentiation and stress adaptation, and will provide a more systematic framework for understanding stress-resistance mechanisms and carbon-partitioning networks in P. yezoensis.

Conclusion

By integrating physiological, transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, this study shows that floridean starch preferentially accumulates in the basal R-region cells of P. yezoensis. Our results indicate that these cells reduce carbon consumption through slower growth and globally lower energy metabolism, while simultaneously redirecting carbon flux towards floridean starch biosynthesis through marked upregulation of UGP gene and increased UGPase activity, together with downregulation of genes involved in floridean starch degradation. These coordinated changes enhance the supply of UDP-glucose precursors and promote starch synthesis while limiting starch turnover, thereby facilitating efficient floridean starch accumulation in R-region cells. More broadly, these findings suggest that starch storage in differentiated thallus cells is determined not only by carbon fixation, but also by the balance among carbon consumption, precursor supply and storage turnover. This work therefore provides a basis for future studies of cell differentiation and carbon metabolism in the blades of P. yezoensis, and advances our understanding of how differentiated cells in red algal thalli coordinate regional function with carbon storage.
