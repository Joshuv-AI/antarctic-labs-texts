Enhancement of growth performance, muscle nutrient composition, and nutritional value in rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) by guanidinoacetic acid-enriched diets
Authors: Di Wu, Shaoxia Lu, Jiaxin Li, Yuanyuan Zhang, Yiqi Gao, Changan Wang, Shicheng Han, Ze Fan, Jinnan Li, Hongbai Liu, Liansheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The role of guanidinoacetic acid (GAA) in modulating nutritional value and muscle quality warrants attention, particularly given the scarcity of mechanistic studies in aquatic animals. A total of 450 healthy juvenile rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss, aged approximately 76 d post-hatch, initial body weight 7.77 ± 0.14 g) were randomly distributed into 5 groups (3 replicates per group, 30 fish per replicate) to investigate the effects of dietary GAA supplementation at concentrations of 0, 300, 600, 1200, and 2400 mg/kg on muscle nutritional value and quality, designated as groups G0, G3, G6, G12, and G24, respectively. After an 8-week feeding trial, dietary supplementation with 600 mg/kg GAA significantly increased final body weight and protein efficiency ratio, and decreased feed conversion ratio compared to the G0 group (P < 0.05). Whole-body crude protein and crude lipid contents were significantly affected by dietary GAA supplementation, with the G6 group exhibiting significantly higher levels of total amino acids, non-essential amino acids, and essential amino acids than the G0 group (P < 0.05). In parallel, GAA supplementation significantly enhanced muscle polyunsaturated fatty acid deposition (P < 0.05), with linear increases observed for polyunsaturated fatty acid and linoleic acid (P < 0.05), and a quadratic response for docosahexaenoic acid (P = 0.003). Notably, the G6 group exhibited the highest combined docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) + eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) content (P = 0.011). Nutritional evaluation based on FAO/WHO standards confirmed that dietary GAA supplementation significantly improved essential amino acid index (EAAI; P < 0.001), with the highest value observed in the G6 group (72.806). Met + Cys was the first limiting amino acid across all GAA treatments. Moreover, GAA supplementation significantly upregulated the expression of genes involved in lipid metabolism, including gpat, srebp 1, fas, and lpl, as well as mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling-related genes, including tgf-β, tor, igf-1, and 4e-bp1, with the G6 group showing significantly higher expression levels than the G0 group (P < 0.05). Antioxidant analysis further revealed significantly lower malondialdehyde levels in the G6 group (P < 0.001). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that dietary supplementation with 600 mg/kg GAA effectively enhances growth performance, whole-body chemical composition, and muscle nutritional value in rainbow trout, likely through the coordinated regulation of amino acid utilization, lipid metabolism, and antioxidant defense systems.

Introduction

With the sustained growth of the aquaculture industry and shifting consumer preferences, fish quality is increasingly becoming a major focus for consumers. Rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), a carnivorous cold-water species widely farmed around the world, is prized for its delicate texture, excellent flavor, and high nutritional value, resulting in a year-on-year increase in its proportion of the fresh consumption market (Cui et al., 2021). In parallel, fish consumption is advocated for its nutritional profile and health benefits, primarily attributed to its rich content of proteins, essential amino acids (EAA), and n-3 long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 LC-PUFA) (Meng et al., 2023). Notably, the muscles of rainbow trout are particularly rich in amino acids, minerals, n-3 LC-PUFA, including docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), as well as vitamins, all of which play crucial roles in human nutrition. In recent years, the farming production of rainbow trout has risen dramatically, from 8818 to 41,116 tons (Bureau of Fisheries of the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs [MARA] of the People's Republic of China, 2024). However, high stocking densities and rapid growth rates have led to a decline in fillet quality, often resulting in soft or loose muscle texture (Yao et al., 2021). Fish quality is a multifaceted attribute encompassing both sensory and nutritional characteristics, influenced by numerous biological and environmental factors (Grigorakis, 2007). Among these, dietary composition is a key determinant of nutritional quality, as it directly affects the deposition of bioactive nutrients in fish flesh. Consequently, enhancing the nutritional quality of farmed fish remains a critical challenge in aquaculture (Bao et al., 2023). Given the increasing use of functional feed additives in aquafeeds, nutritional supplementation represents a promising strategy to improve the quality of aquatic products. Accordingly, this study implemented targeted nutritional supplementation to simultaneously optimize growth performance and enhance nutritional values in rainbow trout.

Guanidinoacetic acid (GAA), an endogenous compound derived from Gly and Arg, is primarily produced in the kidneys (Ostojic and Jorga, 2023). As the direct precursor of creatine, exogenous GAA supplementation spares Gly and Arg from being used for endogenous creatine synthesis, thereby making these amino acids available for protein synthesis and other metabolic processes that support growth (Ostojic, 2015). Existing evidence indicates that dietary GAA supplementation in Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) significantly improves growth rate, final body weight (FBW), and dorsal muscle width (Wiriyapattanasub et al., 2020). Furthermore, GAA enhances muscle development by modulating the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)/ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K) signaling pathway and upregulating the expression of igf-1 (the full names of the abbreviations of all genes can be found in Table S1) (Li et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018). Additionally, GAA indirectly improves antioxidant defenses by increasing the availability of creatine and Arg, thereby promoting free radical scavenging and maintaining cellular redox balance (Berikol et al., 2023; Sestili et al., 2011). Evidence from previous studies has shown that GAA markedly enhances the activities of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), while decreasing levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) in broilers (Zhao et al., 2021). Similarly, the enhancing role of GAA in improving nutritional constituents has been reported. Specifically, dietary GAA supplementation was found to enhance the nutritional quality of grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) by increasing muscle levels of flavor nucleotides, flavor-related fatty acids, and free amino acids, as well as enhancing water-holding capacity and hardness (Yang et al., 2021).

To date, limited evidence is available regarding the effects of GAA supplementation on the nutritional improvement of rainbow trout. Accordingly, this study was designed to evaluate the impacts of dietary GAA on growth performance, nutrient composition, and flavor attributes through integrated analyses of chemical composition, nutritional value, and physiological and biochemical parameters. It was hypothesized that GAA improves nutritional value by modulating amino acid and fatty acid profiles through the regulation of their respective transport mechanisms in rainbow trout. The effects of dietary GAA supplementation on growth performance, nutritional value and antioxidant properties were therefore comprehensively evaluated at both the molecular and tissue levels, with the aim of developing effective nutritional strategies to optimize the quality of farmed rainbow trout.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures were carried out in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Committee for the Welfare and Ethics of Laboratory Animals of Heilongjiang River Fisheries Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Fishery Sciences (approval No. 20230628–001, date 2023–07-06).

Experimental diets and design

Guanidinoacetic acid (≥99% purity; Sigma–Aldrich Trading Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was incorporated into the basal diet at 5 graded levels (0, 300, 600, 1200, and 2400 mg/kg), designated as groups G0, G3, G6, G12, and G24, respectively. Guanidinoacetic acid in the diets was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an Agilent 1200 Series system (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm). All solid ingredients were pulverized in advance, sieved through a 60-mesh screen and pre-mixed using the stepwise expansion method. After thorough manual mixing, the diets were pelleted using an extruder (HX-200G, Guanghe Experimental Instruments & Equipment Co., Ltd., Zhangye, Gansu, China) to produce pellets with a diameter of 1.5 mm. Feeding trial

A total of 450 healthy O. mykiss juveniles, aged approximately 76 d post-hatch (initial body weight 7.77 ± 0.14 g), were sourced from a commercial farm in Benxi, Liaoning, China. After a 14-d acclimation period on a standard commercial diet (44% crude protein and 10% crude lipid, Shandong Hanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Rizhao, Shandong, China) under controlled rearing conditions, fish were evenly and randomly assigned to 15 circular tanks (1.2 m in diameter), with each of the 5 experimental diets each replicated in 3 tanks, resulting in 90 fish per dietary treatment (30 fish per tank). A constant temperature of 15.0 ± 1.0 °C artesian well water was supplied using a thermostatic recirculating water system. Fish were fed twice daily (08:00 and 16:00) for 8 weeks, with the total daily ration divided equally between the two feedings. Feeding continued until visual indicators of satiety were observed, up to a maximum of 3.0% of the tank biomass. Feed delivery was stopped immediately upon cessation of active feeding behavior. Undistributed feed was collected, dried, and weighed to facilitate accurate calculation of growth parameters. Water stability of the experimental diets was determined by measuring dry-matter loss after 30 min of immersion in water at the experimental temperature, yielding an average loss of (3.8 ± 0.3)%. To calculate feed intake (FI) precisely, fish in each tank were weighed in bulk every two weeks. During the entire feeding trial, one-fifth of the water was exchanged daily in each tank. Water quality parameters were monitored throughout the culture period using a YSI 8500 SELECT biochemical analyzer (YSI Inc., Yellow Springs, OH, USA). Dissolved oxygen levels were measured once a week and ranged from 7.8 to 8.3 mg/L. The pH, NO2--N, and NH4+-N were maintained within the ranges of 6.8 to 7.1, < 0.02 mg/L, and < 0.2 mg/L, respectively.

Sample collection

Upon termination of the feeding trial, a 24-h starvation period was implemented. Thereafter, fish were anesthetized with MS-222 (100 mg/L, Sigma-Aldrich Trading Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and then each replicate group was counted and weighed. The FBW of fish in each tank was used for subsequent growth performance analysis. Nine fish were randomly collected from each group (3 fish per tank), individually weighed, and then immediately frozen at −20 °C for nutritional composition analysis. An additional 9 fish per group were randomly selected and placed on an ice-chilled tray for dorsal muscle removal. A portion of the muscle tissue was used for RNA extraction, while the remaining portion was analyzed for antioxidant enzyme activity. The viscera and liver were then quickly dissected and removed, rinsed with pre-cooled saline at 4 °C, blotted dry with filter paper, and weighed to calculate morphometric indicators. All tissue samples were rapidly frozen using liquid nitrogen and then transferred to a −80 °C freezer for further analysis.

Growth performance, feed utilization, and morphometric indicator calculation

The weight gain rate (WGR), feed conversion ratio (FCR), specific growth rate (SGR), and protein efficiency ratio (PER) were calculated as follows:WGR(%)=(W2–W1)/W1×100;FCR=FI/(W2–W1);SGR(%/d)=(lnW2–lnW1)/t×100;PER=(W2–W1)/TP;where W1 is the initial body weight (g) of fish; W2 is FBW (g) of fish; FI is the total feed intake (g) of fish; t is number of feeding days of fish; and TP is total protein intake (g) of fish.

The viscerosomatic index (VSI), hepatosomatic index (HSI), and condition factor (CF) were used to evaluate morphometric indicators, which were calculated by the following formula:VSI(%)=W3/Wf×100;HSI(%)=W4/Wf×100;CF=Wf/l3×100;where W3 represents the viscera weight (g); W4 represents the liver weight (g); Wf represents the body weight (g); and l represents the body length (cm) of the fish.

Proximate composition analysis

Proximate composition analysis of both diets and fish samples was conducted in accordance with the methods of the AOAC (2023). In brief, moisture was determined by oven drying at 105 °C to constant weight (method 930.15) using a hot air oven (DHG-9070A, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Crude protein was analyzed using the Kjeldahl method (method 992.15) with a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (K9860, Hanon Advanced Technology Group Co., Ltd., Jinan, Shandong, China). Crude lipid was extracted via the Soxhlet method (method 960.39) using a glass Soxhlet extraction apparatus (Sanaisi Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Crude ash was determined through dry ashing at 550 °C (method 942.05) using a muffle furnace (SX2-8-10, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Organic matter was calculated by subtracting the crude ash content from the dry matter, which was derived by subtracting the moisture content from the total sample.

Fatty acid analysis

Fatty acid composition was determined via gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) following the procedure outlined in the GB 5009.168-2016 (China National Standard, 2016a). Briefly, muscle tissue was accurately weighed and homogenized with 8 mL of distilled water. Following acidification with 10 mL of hydrochloric acid, the sample was vortexed and subjected to hydrolysis at 80 °C in a water bath for 1 h. The cooled hydrolysate was treated with 10 mL of 95% ethanol, mixed thoroughly, and then extracted 3 times with a diethyl ether-petroleum ether (1:1, v/v) mixture. After removing the ether and petroleum ether by evaporation, the lipid sample was saponified using 2% sodium hydroxide methanol solution at 45 °C for 30 min. The solution was then methylated by adding 14% boron trifluoride methanol solution and the water bath was continued for 30 min. After the temperature was lowered to room temperature, 3 mL of n-hexane was added for a shaking extraction for 2 min. The supernatant, collected after static partitioning, was filtered through a 0.45-μm millipore membrane and analyzed using a gas chromatograph (7890A, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a polydimethylsiloxane-coated capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm i. d., 0.2 μm film thickness). The injector and detector temperatures were set at 270 and 280 °C, respectively, with nitrogen as carrier gas, a split ratio of 100:1, and an injection volume of 1.0 μL. The oven temperature program was an initial temperature of 100 °C (held for 13.0 min), ramped to 180 °C at 10 °C/min (held for 6 min), then to 200 °C at 1 °C/min (held for 20.0 min), and finally to 230 °C at 4 °C/min (held for 10.5 min).

Amino acid analysis

Referring to the GB 5009.124-2016 (China National Standard, 2016b), the fresh muscle tissues of 0.5 g were subjected to 10 mL hydrochloric acid (VHCl:VH2O = 1:1) in an oven at 110 °C for 22 h. After cooling, the hydrolysate was filtered and diluted to 25 mL with distilled water. After drying at 60 °C for 2 h, 500 μL of the sample was filled with nitrogen, thoroughly shaken and mixed, and the volume of the sample was fixed to 10 mL with 20 mmol/L hydrochloric acid. The sample was filtered through a 0.22-μm millipore membrane prior to analysis using an automatic amino acid analyzer (LA8080, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a sulfonic acid-type cation exchange resin column, with detection wavelengths set at 570 and 440 nm.

Nutritional value evaluation

The amino acid scoring pattern of rainbow trout muscle tissue was evaluated based on its amino acid profile, following the reference protein pattern recommended by the FAO/WHO (2013). To better reflect the bioavailable amino acid supply, the total amino acid concentrations were corrected for apparent digestibility. Digestible amino acid concentrations were estimated by multiplying the measured total amino acid concentrations by ingredient-weighted apparent digestibility coefficients (ADCs) reported for rainbow trout in NRC (2011). Based on the amino acid pattern of whole egg protein, the amino acid content (aa), amino acid score (AAS), chemical score (CS), and essential amino acid index (EAAI) were calculated as follows:aa(mg/gN)=W5/W6×6.25×1000;AAS=aa/AA(FAO/WHO);CS=aa/AA(Egg);EAAI=(WA/WAE×100×WB/WBE×100×…×WI/WIE×100)1/n;where aa (mg/g N) represents the digestible amino acid content expressed as milligrams of amino acid per gram of nitrogen; W5 is the muscle amino acid percentage (%); W6 is the muscle protein percentage (%); AA(FAO/WHO) is the specified value for the content of the corresponding same EAA (mg/g N) in the FAO/WHO scoring standard model; AA(Egg) is the specified value for the content of the corresponding same EAA (mg/g N) in the whole egg protein; n is the number of EAAs analyzed, WA, WB, …, WI are the digestible content of an EAA (mg/g N) in the sample, and WAE, WBE, …, WIE are the content of the corresponding amino acid in whole egg protein.

Antioxidant capacity

Total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; A015-2-1), superoxide dismutase (SOD; A001-1-2), glutathione (GSH; A006-2-1) and malondialdehyde (MDA; A003-1-2) were assessed using corresponding assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), following the manufacturer's protocols. The absorbance for all assays was measured using a SpectraMax Plus 384 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, LLC, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from frozen muscle tissue using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA), following homogenization in liquid nitrogen. The RNA purification was performed using a phenol/chloroform extraction protocol and concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 8000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA). Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, Liaoning, China), and qRT-PCR was performed using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq kit (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, Liaoning, China). The primer sequences are provided in Table S1. The β-actin was used as the internal reference gene. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as means and standard error of the mean (SEM). Prior to analysis, the normality of data distribution was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene's test. One-way ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the effects of dietary GAA. Polynomial contrast analysis was used to determine linear and quadratic trends, and a polynomial regression model was applied to assess dose-response relationships:Yij=μ+β1Xi+β2Xi2+eij,where Yij is the observed response; μ is the overall mean; Xi is the dietary GAA concentration (g/kg); and eij is the random error. The significance of linear (β1) and quadratic (β2) terms was used to determine response patterns. Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test was applied for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Differences are considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Discussion

Guanidinoacetic acid is a direct precursor of creatine, which serves as an energy buffer to support growth. Dietary supplementation with GAA has successfully increased creatine levels in some terrestrial animals (He et al., 2018, 2019). As far as is known, this is the first study to evaluate the effects of GAA in rainbow trout. All experimental diets containing GAA were well tolerated, with no mortality observed throughout the trial. This finding is consistent with a previous study in tilapia where GAA supplementation at 600 to 1200 mg/kg resulted in 100% survival (Yuniarti et al., 2024). It is plausible that dietary GAA supplementation enhances creatine deposition in muscle, thereby improving the growth performance of fish. An earlier report showed that GAA supplementation effectively increased the total creatine content in muscle, which could be attributed to the fact that more GAA was translocated into cells and converted to creatine by guanidinoacetate N-methyltransferase (GAMT) (Wuertz and Reiser, 2023). The resulting elevation in creatine availability may increase energy utilization and protein synthesis, ultimately promoting fish growth (Young et al., 2007). The results in this study demonstrated that supplementation with 600 mg/kg GAA in dietary formulation indeed increased the FBW, WGR, SGR, and PER in rainbow trout. Similar improvements in growth performance have been reported in grass carp (Yang et al., 2021) and Nile tilapia (Wiriyapattanasub et al., 2020) fed GAA-supplemented diets. These enhancements are likely attributable to increased creatine availability and subsequent stimulation of protein synthesis. Typically, lower FCR values indicate higher feed utilization efficiency in fish. As previously reported, dietary GAA supplementation at appropriate levels was associated with a significant reduction in FCR (Aziza et al., 2020). This finding aligns with the present results, which indicate that fish utilize the protein and energy provided by dietary supplementation with GAA, enabling maximum conversion and increasing body weight, ultimately affecting the FCR value (Murakami et al., 2014). At the molecular level, GAA supplementation positively modulated the expression of tgf-β, igf-1, and 4e-bp1. In addition, tor expression level showed a dose-dependent response, with a significant increase observed at 600 mg/kg GAA in this study. As demonstrated, igf-1 acts as a key growth-promoting factor by activating the phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/mTOR signaling pathway. mtor, positioned centrally within this cascade, directly phosphorylates downstream effectors such as s6k1 and 4e-bp1, thereby stimulating protein synthesis (Belghit et al., 2013; Dann and Thomas, 2006; Seiliez et al., 2013). Furthermore, tgf-β has been shown to indirectly influence the protein synthesis process (de Mello et al., 2014). The current study suggested that activation of the mTOR signaling pathway following dietary GAA supplementation at 600 mg/kg may underline the enhanced protein synthesis in rainbow trout muscle, concomitant with altered expression levels of tgf-β, igf-1, and 4e-bp1.

The efficiency of dietary protein digestion directly influences the amino acid profile available for absorption and utilization. In the present study, an elevation of Gly level in muscle tissue was observed in rainbow trout fed a diet supplemented with 600 mg/kg GAA, suggesting that GAA supplementation was responsible for influencing Gly level. Generally, it is theorized that GAA supplementation would increase the consumption of Gly, as Gly serves as a direct precursor in endogenous GAA synthesis (Yan et al., 2021). Exogenous GAA should therefore elevate tissue GAA level and promote creatine synthesis, which appears inconsistent with the observation of increased Gly level. In fact, Gly is not only involved in creatine synthesis but also participates in multiple metabolic functions, including collagen formation, GSH synthesis, and one-carbon metabolism (Alves et al., 2019). Following GAA supplementation, the demand for Gly may shift from creatine synthesis to alternative metabolic pathways, potentially enhancing the overall utilization efficiency of Gly without necessarily depleting its total pool. Conversely, certain metabolic pathways may even stimulate Gly production or retention (Brosnan and Brosnan, 2006). On the other hand, creatine attracts moisture into the cell, leading to an increase in cell volume. Cell swelling has been shown to activate amino acid transporter proteins, thereby increasing cellular uptake of external amino acids, including Gly (Hultman et al., 1996).

Free amino acids in muscle are the main water-soluble flavor precursors of meat flavor and are commonly used as an important quality index of aquatic products (Rodehutscord et al., 1995). In the current study, muscle concentrations of NEAA, TAA, and DAA in rainbow trout first increased and then decreased with rising dietary GAA levels, peaking at 600 mg/kg. This pattern indicates that GAA supplementation modulated amino acid composition, enhanced umami taste potential, and improved muscle quality in rainbow trout. Guanidinoacetic acid may indirectly alter amino acid profiles by affecting creatine synthesis. Findings have shown that elevated creatine levels may be associated with reduced muscle protein degradation and thus maintain higher TAA levels (Cheng et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022). Based on the FAO/WHO reference pattern for ideal protein requirements, EAA/TAA and EAA/NEAA ratios above 40% and 60%, respectively, are commonly used as criteria for evaluating high-quality protein (Lai et al., 2024). The current study revealed that the EAA/TAA and EAA/NEAA ratios of rainbow trout were greater than 41% and 82% after dietary supplementation with GAA, which fully complied with the FAO/WHO reference for high-quality protein. The nutritional value of food can be characterized by comparing the AAS and CS values of essential amino acids. After correcting for digestibility, Met + Cys were identified as the first limiting amino acid across all GAA treatments in rainbow trout muscle, with its AAS declining from 0.979 in the G0 group to 0.346 in the G24 group, indicating that high-dose GAA supplementation may compromise sulfur amino acid availability in rainbow trout muscle. The basal diet was marginally deficient in digestible Met + Cys relative to NRC (2011) requirements, making it the first-limiting amino acid as indicated by the lowest amino acid score. The GAA supplementation improved growth performance but did not alleviate this limitation. Meanwhile, the highest AAS and CS values for Lys were found in the G6 group, which were approximately 1.59- and 1.23-fold higher than those of the FAO/WHO reference pattern and the whole egg protein model, respectively. Given that Lys is considered the first limiting amino acid in human diets, the substantial Lys content in rainbow trout muscle may help complement for dietary Lys deficiencies, thereby improving protein utilization efficiency and contributing to nutritional balance in humans (Boisen et al., 2000). A dynamic variation was observed in the EAAI value of rainbow trout muscle, characterized by an initial rise followed by a decline, with the highest values occurring in the G6 group. Notably, excessive GAA supplementation did not further improve EAAI compared to the G0 group.

The present findings build upon existing evidence regarding the influence of dietary GAA on the muscle fatty acid composition of rainbow trout. The data showed that GAA supplementation was associated with a decreasing trend in SFA content. The reduction in SFA accumulation with GAA supplementation may involve enhanced fatty acid oxidation and improved energy metabolism efficiency (Marques et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021). Meanwhile, the antioxidant properties of GAA may have reduced SFA deposition in cellular membranes, thereby lowering the proportion of SFA in muscle (Azizollahi et al., 2024). As previously reported by Pillon et al. (2012), elevated SFA may trigger an inflammatory response that inhibits muscle protein synthesis and leads to a reduction in muscle quality. This mechanism provides a reasonable hypothesis for the observed increases in protein and amino acid contents in rainbow trout following GAA supplementation. In addition, results in this study indicated that dietary GAA modulates fatty acid metabolism in muscle, favoring an increase in both PUFA levels and the PUFA/SFA ratio. The PUFAs are recognized as key contributors to meat quality, with higher levels of PUFA generally associated with greater nutritional value. Notably, the ratio of PUFA/SFA, an important indicator of lipid quality, was significantly elevated after GAA supplementation. These results are consistent with a previous finding in laying hens that GAA supplementation resulted in higher PUFA concentrations and PUFA/SFA ratios (Azizollahi et al., 2024). A possible explanation for these results is that GAA increased the efficiency of PUFA transport and deposition in muscle. Alternatively, the antioxidant property of GAA may protect PUFA-rich cell membranes from oxidative damage, thereby increasing the relative abundance of PUFA in muscle.

The regulation of fatty acid concentrations in tissues is closely linked to the expression of genes encoding key metabolic enzymes. As central regulators of lipogenesis, fas and acc are directly involved in the synthesis of long-chain fatty acids, while scd1 catalyzes the desaturation of SFA, leading to the formation of MUFA and PUFAs (Vessby et al., 2013). In the present study, the increased expression level of scd1 in muscle was consistent with decreased SFA levels and higher oleic acid content. Rainbow trout fed 2400 mg/kg GAA exhibited higher expression levels of fas and scd1, suggesting that GAA supplementation improved fatty acid biosynthesis. Consequently, the observed increase in MUFA and PUFA levels in rainbow trout muscle under GAA treatment may be mediated by the upregulation of lipogenic genes, particularly fas and scd1. Additionally, an upregulation of srebp 1 expression was observed in response to GAA supplementation, which indicated that GAA supplementation promoted lipogenesis in rainbow trout. In contrast, the elevated expression level of gpat, which encodes the rate-limiting enzyme in the triglyceride synthesis pathway, provides additional evidence that GAA modulates fatty acid metabolism in muscle tissue. The activity of GPAT directly affects fat deposition (Manor et al., 2015). Considering the wholeness of energy metabolism, these findings suggest that the effects of GAA on lipid synthesis may be closely related to the enhancement of cellular energy efficiency.

In rainbow trout, GAA supplementation altered the expression of fabp3, fabp4, lpl, and me, revealing a potential role of GAA in regulating fatty acid transport and oxidation. Fabp3 and fabp4, as two different types of fatty acid-binding proteins, are critically involved in the intracellular transport of fatty acids (Hotamisligil and Bernlohr, 2015). However, in the present study, GAA supplementation did not cause significant differences in the expression levels of fabp3 and fabp4. Given that the primary physiological function of GAA is to promote creatine synthesis, which enhances cellular energy buffering and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) regeneration, particularly in muscle tissue that requires large amounts of energy (He et al., 2018). Theoretically, an improvement in the energy supply of muscle cells may reduce their dependence on fatty acids as an energy source. As a lipoprotein lipase lpl hydrolyzes circulating triglycerides to release free fatty acids, thereby influencing fatty acid utilization (Basu and Goldberg, 2020). It was observed that dietary supplementation with 600 and 2400 mg/kg GAA significantly upregulated the lpl expression level. This may reflect that GAA promotes more efficient energy metabolism, thereby altering muscle demand for alternative energy sources. Furthermore, lpl-mediated fatty acid redistribution may support processes such as muscle repair and growth under conditions of elevated energy efficiency.

The antioxidant status of muscle tissue is maintained through an integrated system of enzymatic and non-enzymatic defenses. In the present study, T-AOC level did not differ significantly among treatment groups, suggesting that dietary GAA supplementation did not produce a generalized increase in the overall antioxidant capacity in muscle. This observation contrasts with findings by Xing et al. (2025), who reported elevated hepatic T-AOC in common carp (Cyprinus carpio) fed exogenous creatine. The discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that direct creatine administration can immediately bolster cellular redox buffering through its inherent ability to scavenge free radicals, whereas GAA must first be converted to creatine endogenously, a process that may vary across species or tissues. Despite the absence of a T-AOC response, GAA supplementation selectively modulated specific components of the redox network. Notably, both SOD activity and MDA content were reduced in the G6 group. However, this co-occurrence likely reflects an adaptive physiological adjustment rather than a compromised antioxidant system. The substantial decline in MDA clearly indicates diminished lipid peroxidation and lower basal levels of ROS. Since GAA serves as a metabolic precursor to creatine, and creatine has been shown to directly neutralize ROS independently of classical antioxidant enzymes (Sestili et al., 2011), it is reasonable to infer that GAA-derived creatine attenuated intracellular oxidative stress via this non-enzymatic mechanism. Under conditions of reduced ROS generation, a downregulation of SOD activity represents a homeostatic response aimed at avoiding excessive radical scavenging and the potential onset of reductive stress. It should be noted that this study was conducted in juvenile rainbow trout, and the effects of GAA supplementation on muscle quality and nutritional attributes in market-size or grow-out stage fish remain to be investigated.

Conclusions

Collectively, dietary GAA at 600 mg/kg improves growth, amino acid balance and PUFA deposition in rainbow trout without compromising antioxidant status. These effects are likely mediated by altered expression levels of amino acid and fatty acid transport-related genes, thereby offering a promising strategy to enhance the nutritional value of rainbow trout.
