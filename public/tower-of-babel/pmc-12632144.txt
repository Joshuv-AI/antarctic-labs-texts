Household satisfaction with community-based health insurance scheme and associated factors in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, a mixed method approach
Authors: Fikadu Adugna Kanea, Fikirte Woldeselassie Woldeyohannes, Tadios Niguss Derese
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient satisfaction is a key indicator of health care quality and is often linked to better adherence to medical recommendations and increased utilization of health services. Despite its importance, evidence on satisfaction with community-based health insurance (CBHI) in Addis Ababa is limited. This study aimed to assess the level of satisfaction among CBHI members and identify associated factors at the household level in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Addis Ababa with mixed method. Data was collected on 630 households by using systematic random sampling method. Data was entered into Epi Data, and exported into SPSS version 25 for analysis. A bivariate binary logistic regression statistical analysis was used to identify potential candidate variables (p 0.25). The result was presented using COR and AOR with 95% CI and a p value less than 0.05 was considered significant in multivariate regression. In addition to the quantitative data, focus group discussions and key informant interviews with health professionals, CBHI office staff, and community leaders were conducted until data saturation. Semi-structured guides were used, and thematic analysis was applied.ResultThe study revealed that the level of satisfaction with the community-based health insurance scheme was found to be 51.6% (46.29–54.01). Marital status/divorced (AOR = 0.18, 95% CI (0.07–0.42)), knowledge of community-based health insurance benefit packages (AOR = 4.49, 95% CI=(2.19–9.21)), received correct prescribed drug (AOR = 4.52, 95% CI= (1.83–15.88)), participated on community-based health insurance related meetings (AOR = 11.04, 95% CI= (5.82–20.91)) and has qualified health professionals (AOR = 3.13, 95% CI=(1.72–5.71)), availability and sanitation of showers and toilets (AOR = 2.25, 95% CI=(1.10–4.63)) and satisfaction with the health facilities included in the CBHI scheme (AOR = 2.31, 95% CI =(1.02–5.23)) were significantly associated with household’s satisfaction to community-based health insurance scheme. Qualitative findings from focus group discussions and key informant interviews revealed several barriers to effective CBHI implementation in Addis Ababa. Major challenges included limited awareness of CBHI services among members and health professionals, lack of compassionate and respectful care, shortages of medications, laboratory, and diagnostic services, and difficulties with premium payment modalities. These factors were consistent with the quantitative findings, highlighting their influence on household satisfaction with the CBHI scheme.ConclusionsAround half of households were satisfied with the community-based health insurance (CBHI) scheme. Household satisfaction was significantly associated with marital status, educational level, knowledge of CBHI benefit packages, receipt of correctly prescribed drugs, participation in CBHI-related meetings, presence of qualified health professionals, availability and sanitation of showers and toilets, and satisfaction with the permitted health institutions. Qualitative findings further highlighted barriers to satisfaction, including limited awareness of CBHI services, lack of respectful and compassionate care, shortages of medications, laboratory and diagnostic services, and difficulties with premium payment modalities. To enhance the sustainability of the community-based health insurance scheme, it is recommended to strengthen the supply chain for essential medicines, improve communication and awareness among CBHI members, and enhance the responsiveness and quality of care provided by health professionals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13766-7.Keywords: Community-based health insurance, Satisfaction, Household, Addis Ababa

Introduction

Community-Based Health Insurance (CBHI) is a community-oriented prepayment scheme designed to improve financial protection among households working in the informal sector by pooling risks and resources to cover health care costs [1]. It aims to reduce catastrophic health expenditures and enhance equity in access to essential services [2].

Despite global efforts to achieve Universal Health Coverage (UHC), an estimated 150 million people worldwide experience financial catastrophe annually, with over 100 million pushed into poverty due to direct out-of-pocket (OOP) payments for health services [3]. In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, OOP payments still account for more than 40% of total health expenditures, leading to inequities and unmet health needs [4].

To address these challenges, several countries, including Rwanda, Ghana, and Tanzania have implemented CBHI schemes with varying levels of success. Evidence from these contexts indicates that factors such as quality of care, benefit package adequacy, management transparency, and responsiveness of health facilities play a crucial role in influencing household satisfaction and scheme sustainability [5–7]. However, the literature reveals inconsistencies across settings: while some studies report high satisfaction and improved health service utilization among CBHI members, others highlight persistent dissatisfaction due to poor service quality, long waiting times, and inadequate medicines [8, 9]. These mixed findings suggest that the determinants of household satisfaction with CBHI are highly context-specific, and more nuanced evidence is required to inform the design and scaling of community-based insurance models in diverse health system contexts.

In Ethiopia, health care financing remains largely regressive, with approximately 79.9% of total health expenditures covered by out-of-pocket payments [10]. Since 2010, the government has introduced two insurance schemes: Social Health Insurance (SHI) for formal sector employees, and Community-Based Health Insurance (CBHI) for the informal sector, which constitutes over 80% of the population [11]. Despite its potential, national CBHI coverage remains low only 28% of the population is currently enrolled, far below the Health Sector Transformation Plan (HSTP) target of 80% by 2025 [12]. Moreover, membership renewal rates are suboptimal, with about 23% of households dropping out annually [13].

While several studies in Ethiopia have assessed household satisfaction with CBHI, their findings are regionally fragmented and focus predominantly on the demand side, often neglecting supply-side perspectives such as provider attitudes, service readiness, and institutional responsiveness [6–8]. Furthermore, there is limited integration of qualitative insights that explain the underlying reasons for satisfaction or dissatisfaction, which are critical for improving service quality and policy design.

Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by assessing household satisfaction with the CBHI scheme and identifying associated factors in Addis Ababa using a mixed-methods approach that integrates both client and provider perspectives. By combining quantitative analysis with qualitative insights, this study contributes to the broader international literature by providing context-specific evidence on factors influencing satisfaction with community-based insurance in an urban African setting an area that remains underexplored globally. The findings are expected to inform strategies to enhance CBHI performance and sustainability in Ethiopia and other LMICs pursuing UHC through community-based insurance models.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Addis Ababa city, with an estimated population of over 4,000,000.

The city has 11 sub-cities and 120 woredas. There are 7 hospitals owned by Addis Ababa health bureau, 5 by Federal Ministry of Health, 1 by Addis Ababa University, 3 by non-governmental Organization, 3 by defense force and police and 34 by private owners. There are a total of 2,002,130 households enrolled in CBHI scheme in 2023G.C [14].

Study design and period

A community-based cross-sectional survey from April 20 to June 20, 2023, with a concurrent embedded qualitative component comprising focus group discussions and key informant interviews. Quantitative and qualitative data were collected during the same period, analyzed separately, and triangulated during interpretation to enrich understanding of household satisfaction and associated factors.

Source population

Quantitative component: All households who were enrolled in CBHI scheme in selected sub-cities of Addis Ababa.

Qualitative component: All Health professional workers of Addis Ababa health facilities, CBHI office workers and community leaders of selected sub-cities in Addis Ababa.

Study population

Quantitative component: Study population was selected community-based health insurance scheme users in selected woredas from selected sub-cities of Addis Ababa who were available during data collection period.

Qualitative component: Selected health professional workers from Addis Ababa health facilities, woreda leaders, and CBHI office workers from the selected woredas in selected sub-cities of Addis Ababa who were available during the data collection period.

Inclusion criteria

For the quantitative study, households who visited public health facilities at least twice after enrolling in the CBHI scheme were included to ensure that participants had sufficient experience with the services to provide informed opinions on satisfaction.

For the qualitative study, all CBHI team leaders, health care professionals, woreda administrators, and community leaders at each sub-city level in Addis Ababa were included to capture diverse perspectives on program implementation and challenges.

Exclusion criteria

Households who were severely sick and unable to sustain an interview was excluded from the study and health care professionals who have short experiences less than 6 months were excluded from the study.

Thus, the final calculated sample size was 382 households.

For the assessment of factors associated with household satisfaction, the sample size was calculated using Epi Info version 7, applying the double population proportion formula with a 95% confidence interval, 80% power. For key factors, the calculated sample sizes were as follows: for laboratory service availability (OR = 2.25), the required sample was 330 households; for the CBHI card collection process (OR = 0.52), it was 376 households; and for household knowledge of CBHI benefits packages (OR = 2.27), it was 342 households.

The larger sample size, which is calculated for the first objective using single population proportion formula, was selected.

Since the study utilized a multi-stage sampling, this sample size was multiplied by 1.5 for the design effect. 1.5*382 = 573 then by adding non-response rate of 10%, the sample size was 630 households.

For the qualitative component, three focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted in the selected woredas, with 8 to 12 participants per group. Participants were CBHI members who were purposively selected to provide diverse perspectives on program experiences. Additionally, key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted with purposively selected health professionals, CBHI office staff, woreda administrators, and community leaders in the selected sub-cities. Both FGDs and KIIs followed semi-structured interview guides, and data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached.

Sampling procedure

A multistage sampling technique was employed. First, out of the eleven sub-cities in Addis Ababa, three sub-cities (30%) were selected randomly using a simple random sampling method. Within each selected sub-city, 30% of woredas (a total of 9 woredas) were randomly selected. A compiled list of households enrolled in the CBHI scheme within each selected woreda, obtained from the CBHI office, served as the sampling frame. The total number of study participants from each woreda was allocated proportionally based on the number of enrolled households.

Finally, households were selected systematically from the compiled lists using a calculated sampling interval based on household numbers, ensuring random and representative selection. For the quantitative component, systematic random sampling was employed. The sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing the total number of CBHI-enrolled households in the selected woredas (25,282) by the required sample size (630), resulting in k = 40. The first household was selected randomly from the list of enrolled households obtained from the CBHI office, and subsequent households were interviewed every 40th household until the total sample size was reached.

Household selection was conducted by trained data collectors under the supervision of the principal investigator to ensure adherence to the sampling procedure (Supplementary file 1).

For the qualitative component, purposive sampling was used. One focus group discussion (FGD) was conducted in each selected sub-city, totaling three FGDs, with 8–12 participants per group. Participants were CBHI members selected to provide diverse perspectives on the program. Key informant interviews (KIIs) were conducted with purposively selected health professionals, woreda administrators, and CBHI office staff. Both FGDs and KIIs followed semi-structured interview guides.

Data collection tool

The household satisfaction in CBHI and associated factors scales was adopted by reviewing different literature [6, 7, 11]. The quantitative part of the questionnaire was divided into six parts. Part one: socio demographic variables, part two: experience of households in CBHI scheme, part three: knowledge of CBHI benefit package, part four: CBHI process and management related factors, Part five: health service provision related factors, Part six: overall household satisfaction score tools (household satisfaction with CBHI scheme was rated using 15 items each having a five-point Likert scale from strongly disagrees to strongly agree. The points obtained from the 15 items by each participant were computed to get the total score of each participant. A participant had a minimum of 15 and a maximum of 75 points on the CBHI scheme satisfaction score. Then, households were categorized as not satisfied (if they score below the median) or satisfied (if they score above to the median satisfaction score).

Qualitative data were collected concurrently with the quantitative data. Semi-structured interview guides were used for both key informant interviews (KIIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs). Separate guides were developed for KIIs and FGDs, covering topics such as perceptions of the CBHI scheme, challenges in service delivery, and factors influencing household satisfaction. The guides were pretested and adapted to ensure clarity and relevance to the study objectives.

Data collection procedure

Quantitative data were collected using interviewer-administered semi-structured questionnaires. Study participants were invited to participate voluntarily, and the purpose and rationale of the study were clearly explained at the time of data collection. Data collection was conducted by five trained data collectors and supervised by two supervisors. Data collectors were diploma- or BSc-level nurses or health officers, all assigned to sub-cities different from their workplace to reduce bias.

For the qualitative component, three focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted, one in each selected sub-city. Each FGD included 8–12 purposively selected household heads, representing diverse sections of the community. Homogeneity in terms of CBHI awareness, social status, and education level was considered to facilitate free discussion. FGDs were guided by trained data collectors using semi-structured guiding questions, and discussions were audio-recorded. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached. Saturation was identified when no new themes, insights, or challenges emerged from additional focus group discussions or key informant interviews, after which the supervisor compiled the data.

KIIs were conducted with purposively selected CBHI office staff, health professionals, and woreda administrators who had firsthand knowledge of the CBHI program and could provide insights into challenges and potential solutions. Interviews followed a semi-structured interview guide and were conducted by the supervisor to ensure consistency and comprehensive coverage of topics.

All qualitative data were audio-recorded, transcribed, and cross-checked for completeness and accuracy. The structured approach ensured that both quantitative and qualitative data were systematically collected, allowing for a robust mixed-methods analysis.

Operational definitions

Household satisfaction

Fifteen satisfaction questions developed from similar previous literatures were used to assess satisfaction with the CBHI scheme. Participants who were scored the median (32) value and above were classified as satisfied regarding to CBHI scheme and participants with lower scores than the median were classified as dissatisfied [15]. The median was used for satisfaction because the data were not normally distributed, making it a more appropriate measure of central tendency.

Knowledge of CBHI benefits packages

Households were asked nine items related to the CBHI benefit packages. Households were labeled as having adequate knowledge if answered above the mean (4.8) of knowledge of the CBHI benefit packages. Otherwise, households were labeled as not having adequate knowledge of CBHI benefit packages [6]. The mean was used for knowledge because the data were approximately normally distributed, making the mean a suitable measure of central tendency.

Data quality assurance

The questionnaire was checked for consistency by translating the English version to Amharic language. The adapted tool was checked for internal consistence of each question for composite variable by Cronbach Alpha (α). The questionnaire was administered to members of CBHI who are utilizing the service and still are members. The questionnaire was pretested to make sure it results what was needed.

Trained data collectors conducted the interviews and supervisors oversaw the data collection process to ensure adherence to procedures. If a selected participant was unavailable during the visit, the data collectors returned at a later time or the next day. If the household was repeatedly unavailable after two visits, the next eligible household was selected according to the systematic sampling procedure.

Data analysis and presentation

Quantitative data were entered into EpiData 3.1, cleaned, and exported to SPSS version 25 for analysis. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) were used to summarize household characteristics and CBHI-related variables. Binary logistic regression was conducted to identify factors associated with household satisfaction, with adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported; statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Qualitative data from focus group discussions and key informant interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed thematically. Coding was performed inductively to identify emerging themes related to household satisfaction and program implementation. Qualitative findings were then triangulated with quantitative results to provide a comprehensive understanding of household experiences and factors influencing satisfaction.

Perception of CBHI beneficiaries about barriers of the program

A total of 31 participants took part in three focus group discussions (FGDs), with a mean age of 38 years (SD = 23); 57% were female. Additionally, 27 key informants from health facilities and woreda administration participated, of whom 48% were male. Thematic analysis of the qualitative data revealed four major barriers to CBHI program implementation: lack of knowledge, lack of compassionate and respectful care, shortages of services, and challenges with premium payment.

Theme 1: Lack of knowledge

Both key informants and FGD participants reported that CBHI members and health professionals often have limited awareness of the scheme’s services and benefits. One key informant noted,

The community has not well aware about CBHI advantages for community health and the health professionals also not adequately aware about the CBHI service, registration. Some health professionals think it has no continuity and as campaign work (KII-07, male, 48 years).

FGD participants echoed this concern, highlighting gaps in communication and information:

We joined CBHI because we believed in its promise, but sometimes we don’t get enough information about our rights. Better communication is needed (FGD-02, Male household head, 36 years).

Limited knowledge affected both uptake and effective use of services, undermining satisfaction with the scheme.

Theme 2: Lack of compassionate and respectful care

Participants frequently reported negative attitudes and disrespectful behavior from health professionals toward CBHI members, which compromised perceived service quality. One FGD participant explained,

The main problem at health facilities is that health professionals do not serve CBHI members as they do others; they think of us as low-income people. I remember one time at the health center, when I said I was a CBHI member, the server immediately changed her facial expression and tone of voice (FGD-03, female, 29 years).

Another participant emphasized the impact on overall satisfaction:

I get disappointed most of the time when I go to Kenema pharmacy. Pharmacists do not treat CBHI members equally and sometimes do not give appropriate advice regarding medications (KII-17, male, 43 years).

Theme 3: Shortages of services

Participants reported frequent drug stockouts, limited laboratory services, and lack of diagnostic services in public facilities, forcing them to purchase services privately. One KII participant stated,

The doctors treat us well, but often the medicines are out of stock, and we are forced to buy from private pharmacies. This makes us question the benefit of the scheme (KII-03, female, 55 years).

FGD participants similarly described challenges accessing laboratory and diagnostic services:

The difficulty with health facilities is that most laboratory tests are not available. I was directed to private institutions for both laboratory and diagnostic services, which is costly and inconvenient (FGD-01, female, 40 years).

These service gaps significantly affected household satisfaction, highlighting systemic barriers beyond individual provider behavior.

Theme 4: Challenges with premium payment

Participants indicated that the annual, lump-sum premium payment was inconvenient, especially for low-income households. One key informant explained:

CBHI service is beneficial for health, but the fixed time of payment, once per year, is difficult for poor members. If payment could be made in two or more installments, it would help them cover the premium without difficulty (KII-22, male, 47 years).

This theme underscores financial barriers as a determinant of program participation and satisfaction.

Discussion

This study assessed household satisfaction with the community-based health insurance (CBHI) scheme in Addis Ababa using a mixed-methods approach. The overall level of satisfaction among households was 51.6%, indicating moderate satisfaction.

This finding is comparable to previous studies conducted in Ghana, where satisfaction was 55.2% [15], and in Sheko District, Southwest Ethiopia, with a reported satisfaction of 54.7% [6]. Similar results were found in Hadiya Zone, Southern Ethiopia (54.1%) [11], and in Addis Ababa (53%) [13]. However, the satisfaction level in this study was lower than in Damot Woyde District (91.3%) [7], West Arsi Zone (63.4%) [16], and Boru Meda Hospital in Northeast Ethiopia (80%) [17]. The observed differences may be explained by variations in socio-demographic characteristics, the measurement tools, and the time frame of the studies. In this study, satisfaction was assessed using 15 standardized items on a five-point Likert scale, with a high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.83). In contrast, some studies, such as those conducted in Sheko and Damot Woyde districts, used a shorter 6-item scale, which may have underestimated challenges experienced by CBHI members [6].

Several quantitative factors were significantly associated with household satisfaction. Households with adequate knowledge of CBHI benefit packages were more satisfied than those with insufficient knowledge. This finding is consistent with studies in Sheko District [6], Hadiya Zone [11], West Arsi [16], and Addis Ababa [13]. The likely explanation is that households who understand the benefits of CBHI are better able to utilize the services, perceive value in enrollment, and thus report higher satisfaction [13].

Similarly, households who received prescribed drugs and agreed with the treatment provided were more likely to be satisfied. This aligns with findings from Nigeria [18], where access to medications was strongly linked to perceived quality of care. In addition, households who believed that permitted health facilities had adequately qualified personnel reported higher satisfaction, consistent with previous studies in Addis Ababa [13]. This highlights the importance of provider competency in shaping household perceptions of CBHI services.

The qualitative findings complemented these quantitative results. Focus group discussions and key informant interviews revealed that shortages of drugs, limited laboratory and diagnostic services, lack of compassionate and respectful care from health professionals, insufficient awareness of CBHI among providers, and rigid premium payment modalities were key barriers to household satisfaction. These qualitative insights help explain why only about half of households were satisfied and reinforce the quantitative associations with knowledge, service availability, and provider competency.

The integration of qualitative and quantitative findings underscores that both demand- and supply-side factors influence satisfaction. On the demand side, households’ knowledge and participation in CBHI-related meetings improve satisfaction. On the supply side, availability of essential services, facility infrastructure, and health professional attitudes are critical. Addressing both sides is therefore essential for improving CBHI effectiveness and sustainability.

The strengths of this study include the use of a mixed-methods approach, which allowed qualitative data to complement and enrich the quantitative findings, the community-based design rather than an institution-based setting, and the exploration of both demand- and supply-side perspectives on the CBHI scheme.

This study might have limitations like any other cross-sectional study. It could not explain cause and effect relationship. The other one, self-reported data may attenuate the association observed between the factors and the outcome variable.

Conclusion and recommendation

The findings of this study revealed that around half of the study participants were satisfied with the CBHI scheme in the Addis Ababa. Marital status, knowledge of CBHI benefit packages, receive correct prescribed drug, participated on CBHI related meetings and Has qualified health professionals, availability and sanitation of showers and toilets and happy with the permitted health institutions were significantly associated with household’s satisfaction to CBHI scheme.

Apart from these, the FGD discussants deliberated the challenges of the program implementation such as shortage of drugs, lack of CRC of health professionals, lack of diagnostic services and lack of laboratory service. More over health professionals lacked awareness about CBHI scheme, and rigid premium payment modality.

Based on the findings of this study, several measures are recommended to enhance household satisfaction with the CBHI scheme in Addis Ababa. First, the Addis Ababa Health Bureau should improve the availability of essential medications, laboratory services, diagnostic services, and medical equipment at both health centers and hospitals, as access to these services was significantly associated with satisfaction. Second, given that knowledge of CBHI benefit packages and the attitude of health professionals influenced satisfaction, the health bureau, in collaboration with sub-city and woreda offices, should assess and strengthen awareness among CBHI members and health care providers.

Additionally, training on compassionate and respectful client care should be provided to health care workers to ensure that CBHI members receive equitable and satisfactory services.
