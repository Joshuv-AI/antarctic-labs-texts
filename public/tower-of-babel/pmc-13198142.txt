Wellens syndrome is an electrocardiographic (ECG) pattern of deeply inverted or biphasic T-waves in the anterior precordial leads, typically V2-V3, appearing during asymptomatic intervals in patients with recent chest pain.1 First described by de Zwaan and Wellens in the 1980s, it strongly predicts critical proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) stenosis and a high risk of anterior myocardial infarction (MI) if untreated.2 Up to 75% of medically managed patients progress to extensive anterior MI within days, making early recognition and coronary angiography essential.3Take-Home Messages•Myocardial bridging may produce a pseudo-Wellens pattern on ECG through dynamic systolic compression and transient ischemia.•Recognition of this phenocopy is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary intervention.

Classically, Wellens syndrome reflects severe, fixed LAD obstruction, but pseudo-Wellens presentations without critical stenosis have been reported, including vasospasm, myocarditis, cocaine use, pulmonary embolism, and stress cardiomyopathy.4, 5, 6, 7 Myocardial bridging (MB), a congenital anomaly in which a coronary artery segment courses intramyocardially and undergoes systolic compression, is a rare but important cause of Wellens-like T-wave changes due to dynamic ischemia rather than fixed obstruction.8

Distinguishing true Wellens syndrome from pseudo-Wellens is essential because interventions differ: Classic Wellens syndrome requires early revascularization, whereas MB is generally managed with beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers (CCBs) while avoiding nitrates. We report a case of Wellens-like ECG changes caused by MB as well as review previously reported cases of MB presenting with Wellens ECG patterns.

Case Presentation

A 58-year-old man with a history of sinus bradycardia, former tobacco use, and recreational marijuana use presented with 2 weeks of intermittent retrosternal chest pain, described as burning, occasionally dull, nonradiating, and not exertional. Despite intermittent symptoms the day before, he remained fully functional. On presentation he was chest pain–free, with sinus bradycardia at 59 beats/min and otherwise normal physical examinations.

The initial ECG demonstrated sinus bradycardia at 41 beats/min, first-degree atrioventricular block, and biphasic T-wave inversions in V2-V3 suggestive of Wellens syndrome (Figure 1A). High-sensitivity troponin T levels were 9 ng/L at presentation, <6 ng/L at 4 hours, and 6 ng/L at 20 hours (reference range: 0-22 ng/L), without a dynamic rise or fall suggestive of MI. Repeat ECGs over 24 hours showed dynamic T-wave changes, including normalization in V2 and V3 (Figure 1B). Chest radiograph was normal. Three years earlier, cardiac evaluation for intermittent chest discomfort showed mild diffuse left ventricular hypokinesis (ejection fraction: 55%) on transthoracic echocardiography. Nuclear stress test demonstrated no inducible ischemia and preserved left ventricular function (ejection fraction: 54%-65%), a mild fixed anterior apical defect likely representing attenuation artifact, enlarged right ventricle with increased tracer uptake on stress images, and marked gut uptake on rest images limiting inferior wall interpretation. Symptoms improved, and he declined further evaluation until this presentation.

Given ECG concern for Wellens syndrome, the patient was managed as having suspected non–ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS) with aspirin, clopidogrel, high-intensity statin, and therapeutic enoxaparin. Stress testing was deferred given the high-risk ECG pattern. While awaiting coronary angiography, he experienced brief episodes of mild nonexertional retrosternal burning without instability.

At the time of coronary angiography, the patient's blood pressure was 135/97 mm Hg and heart rate was 78 beats/min. Angiography revealed severe dynamic systolic compression of the mid-LAD consistent with MB, with otherwise normal coronary arteries, low filling pressures, and no aortic valve gradient (Figures 1C and 1D). The degree of systolic narrowing was not formally quantified in the procedural report. No fixed atherosclerotic stenosis was identified. Intra-arterial nitroglycerin (200 μg) was administered, and no inotropic provocation was performed.

After exclusion of obstructive coronary disease, aspirin and statin therapy were discontinued given low atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk (5.1%). Beta-blocker and non-dihydropyridine CCB therapy were deferred owing to relative bradycardia (postprocedural heart rate: 49-54 beats/min) and complete symptom resolution. The patient was counseled to discontinue marijuana and tobacco use and avoid nitrates. At the 6-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic, with a heart rate of 59 beats/min and preserved functional capacity (>4 METs), including the ability to walk 10 blocks without limitations.

Discussion

This case describes a middle-aged man with recurrent, nonexertional chest pain and a Wellens-type ECG pattern who was ultimately found to have a mid-LAD MB without fixed stenosis. Although initially managed for NSTE-ACS, persistently negative troponins and preserved left ventricular function supported a nonatherosclerotic mechanism. Although the patient's nuclear stress test 3 years ago showed no inducible ischemia, the mild fixed anterior apical defect and increased right ventricular uptake could have represented early, subtle effects of dynamic systolic compression from an MB.9

Classic Wellens syndrome is strongly associated with critical proximal LAD stenosis and predicts high risk of imminent anterior MI, characterized by deeply inverted or biphasic T-waves in the anterior leads and minimal biomarker elevation after chest pain resolution.10 A 2011 seminal series linked Wellens-type T-wave to transient myocardial edema rather than persistent obstructive disease, suggesting that nonatherosclerotic mechanisms can produce nearly identical repolarization abnormalities.11

Growing evidence supports MB as an important nonatherosclerotic cause of Wellens-type ECG abnormalities. Multiple reports describe pseudo-Wellens presentations in patients with hemodynamically significant MB despite negative biomarkers and preserved systolic function. Abuarqoub et al12 reported a severe mid-LAD bridge that produced classic Wellens T-wave inversions after nitroglycerin administration. Kaplanis et al13 described a case explicitly labeled “pseudo-Wellens” with only a mid-LAD MB on angiography. Ambesh et al14 presented a patient with deep anterior T-wave inversions caused by systolic compression from MB rather than plaque. Recent cases reinforce this pattern: Avram et al (2020)15 described subocclusive dynamic LAD obstruction successfully managed with beta-blockers and CCBs. Milne et al (2022)16 reported a Wellens-like ECG pattern due to mid-LAD MB, emphasizing dynamic compression. Guha et al (2023)17 published 2 mid-LAD MB cases with preserved systolic function and negative biomarkers. Shenoy et al (2024)18 described a long-segment MB causing complete systolic occlusion mimicking high-risk. Zhao et al (2025)19 documented biphasic T-wave pattern in V2-V3 with normal troponin and a mid-LAD bridge. Collectively, these reports, including the present case, demonstrate recurring features: predominance of mid-LAD bridging, systolic compression with preserved diastolic flow, normal or minimally elevated cardiac biomarkers, and generally benign clinical course when managed appropriately, highlighting MB as a leading nonatherosclerotic cause of pseudo-Wellens syndrome.

The management implications are substantial. Classic Wellens requires urgent revascularization to prevent large anterior MI, whereas MB-related pseudo-Wellens is best treated medically.20 In nearly all reported cases,13,15, 16, 17, 18, 19 patients were treated successfully with beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine CCBs, which reduce heart rate, prolong diastole, and decrease systolic compression. Nitrates are generally contraindicated because they may exacerbate systolic narrowing.13,18 Percutaneous coronary intervention of a tunneled LAD segment is discouraged because of high stent fracture, restenosis, and unfavorable long-term outcomes.15,18

This case contributes several diagnostic and clinical themes: Wellens-type ECG patterns are not pathognomonic for critical LAD stenosis, and suspicion should extend to patients with negative biomarkers, preserved ventricular function, atypical pain, or limited risk factors. It confirms MB as a recurring etiology for Wellens-like ECG changes, highlights the value of careful angiographic assessment—including systolic frames—before revascularization, and emphasizes that awareness of pseudo-Wellens presentations can prevent misdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions.

Overall, our patient's clinical course aligns with prior descriptions of pseudo-Wellens due to MB. Review of published cases demonstrates consistent ECG, angiographic, and management patterns, supporting heightened recognition among clinicians evaluating Wellens-type repolarization abnormalities.
