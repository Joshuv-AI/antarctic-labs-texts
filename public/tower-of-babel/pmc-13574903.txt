A dual-wavelength blue laser platform for integrated diagnosis and therapy in glioblastoma: preclinical evidence
Authors: Zhongyu Wang, Ronggui Tao, Dali Jiang, Yashou Guo, Ki Xi, Dalin He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GBM) remains clinically intractable owing to dismal prognosis and frequent recurrence. This study aimed to develop a dual-wavelength blue laser platform (450 nm and 405 nm) that integrates laser ablation, photodynamic diagnosis (PDD), and photodynamic therapy (PDT) in preclinical models, with 405 nm designated for PDD and 450 nm for PDT. Ex vivo ablation and coagulation were performed on porcine brain tissue using a 450 nm laser, compared with a conventional 980 nm laser or bipolar forceps. Subcutaneous and orthotopic GBM mouse models were used to assess 405 nm irradiation for tumor visualization following 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) administration. In vitro and in vivo experiments evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of 450 nm laser-mediated PDT, with 630 nm laser-mediated PDT serving as the positive control. Tumor recurrence was assessed after surgical resection combined with PDT. The 450 nm laser produced deeper and narrower incisions with less thermal coagulation than the 980 nm laser or bipolar forceps. 405 nm irradiation enabled clear tumor visualization, with peak 5-ALA-induced fluorescence at 2 h post-administration in subcutaneous tumors and 4 h in orthotopic tumors. The 450 nm laser induced concentration-dependent cytotoxicity in GBM cells and elicited robust photodynamic effects. Notably, surgical resection followed by PDT significantly suppressed tumor recurrence. These results validate an integrated strategy that uses a 405 nm laser for intraoperative PDD and a 450 nm laser for precise ablation and subsequent PDT, positioning this dual-wavelength blue laser platform as a promising, comprehensive approach to improving GBM surgical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10103-026-05030-z.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is one of the most common and aggressive malignant brain tumors, characterized by diffuse infiltration, rapid progression, and poor prognosis, and remains a major challenge in neuro-oncology [1, 2]. According to global cancer statistics, there were 321,476 new cases and 248,305 deaths from brain and other nervous system tumors across 185 countries in 2022 [3]. Maximal safe resection (MSR) is widely recognized as the standard treatment for high-grade gliomas (HGG) [4–8]. However, because eloquent brain regions are frequently involved, achieving MSR poses significant technical challenges in neurosurgical practice [9].

As an adjunct to surgical resection, fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS) improves visualization and delineation of brain tumors and their infiltrative margins, and has demonstrated considerable clinical benefits in HGG surgery [10, 11]. As a widely used FGS modality, 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) mediated photodynamic diagnosis (PDD) has emerged as a promising approach to improve glioma resection. Following oral administration, 5-ALA is converted in vivo to protoporphyrin IX (PpIX), which preferentially accumulates in tumor cells and emits red fluorescence when excited at 375–440 nm, thereby enabling tumor visualization [12–14]. Beyond fluorescence guidance, various laser systems have also been explored as surgical tools for tumor ablation and hemostasis in glioma surgery. Since the invention of the laser in 1960, medical laser research has demonstrated substantial therapeutic potential [15–17]. The CO2 laser reduces damage to normal brain tissue [18], the 2 μm thulium laser offers superior precision and vaporization efficiency [19], and 980 nm lasers provide effective hemostasis and lesion incision [20].

Despite the precision and hemostatic advantages of laser-assisted resection, the presence of residual glioblastoma initiating cells (GICs) at the resection margin has been reported as a key cause of therapeutic resistance and high recurrence rates in GBM [21]. Therefore, after fluorescence-guided MSR, adjunctive therapies that eradicate residual tumor cells are needed. The therapeutic potential of 5-ALA-mediated PDT in GBM was initially validated in a brain organoid co-culture model [22] and subsequently corroborated by clinical studies demonstrating survival benefits and a favorable safety profile [23]. Notably, intraoperative PDT for newly diagnosed glioma safely reduces the risk of recurrence by eliminating residual tumor cells in the resection cavity [24], and immediate post-resection PDT similarly enhances survival in recurrent malignant glioma without additional severe complications [25]. In the present study, a 630 nm laser (the clinically established wavelength for 5-ALA-PDT)-mediated PDT group was included as a positive control to benchmark the therapeutic efficacy of the 450 nm blue laser.

Within the visible spectrum, the penetration depth of the laser increases with wavelength. In brain tissue, the effective penetration depth of 405 nm and 450 nm lasers, defined as the distance over which light intensity attenuates to 1/e (approximately 36.8%) of its surface value, is approximately 200 ~ 300 μm depending on tissue scattering and absorption coefficients [26, 27]. Scattering coefficients decrease slightly from 400 to 450 nm, while absorption is primarily governed by hemoglobin and mitochondrial chromophore content [28]. In gray matter, the penetration depth at 450 nm is approximately 15 ~ 20% greater than at 405 nm (405 nm: ~220 μm; 450 nm: ~260 μm) [29]. By contrast, the red laser at 630 nm, commonly used in clinical PDT, penetrates 3 ~ 5 mm in brain tissue, roughly 15 ~ 20 times deeper than the blue laser [30]. Although this limited penetration restricts blue lasers to superficial layers, it is advantageous for targeting residual tumor cells at the resection cavity surface, where residual disease thickness is typically less than 500 μm, allowing precise ablation and PDT with minimal damage to underlying healthy parenchyma. Collectively, these findings support 5-ALA-guided MSR followed by immediate PDT as a promising strategy. Notably, prior studies report that the 450 nm laser exhibits excellent tissue ablation capability and robust PDT effects, suggesting potential to integrate therapeutic and ablative functions. Yet, its application in glioma surgery remains unexplored.

In this study, we first validated the safety and efficacy of a 450 nm laser for brain tissue incision and coagulation in ex vivo tissue models. We then established subcutaneous and orthotopic glioma mouse models to assess the diagnostic efficacy of 405 nm laser-mediated PDD. Finally, both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that 450 nm laser-mediated PDT significantly suppressed GBM progression and reduced tumor recurrence. Together, these findings establish a novel dual-wavelength blue laser platform (450/405 nm) as a promising integrated approach for the diagnosis and treatment of GBM.

Methods

Laser incision and coagulation on ex vivo porcine brain

A 450 nm semiconductor diode blue laser (Blueray Medical Ltd., China) and a 980 nm diode infrared laser (INTERmedic, Spain) were evaluated and operated in continuous-wave (CW) mode, with output power calibrated before experimentation. Bipolar forceps served as a coagulation control, and a medical-grade laser fiber (diameter: 760 μm, numerical aperture: 0.22) was used for laser delivery.

Following previously established protocols, fresh porcine brain tissue (obtained within 1 h of slaughter) was used for ex vivo experiments. Based on reported laser-tissue interaction modes [31], incision and coagulation experiments were performed using a motorized X/Y and manual Z-axis stage (Fig. S1). The laser beam was positioned with an XYZ translation stage driven by an SC100 series stepper motor controller (Beijing Beiguang Instrument, China), providing approximately 1 μm spatial resolution and ± 5 μm accuracy, while the fiber was positioned above the tissue along the X-axis and the Z-axis adjusted to set the working distance per Table 1 (power settings, speed/duration, and distance). Cell culture and reagents

Human GBM cell lines (U251, U87-MG) were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). Murine GL261-luc and microglia BV2 cells were obtained from the Center for Brain Science. All cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

The CCK-8 assay kit was purchased from TargetMol (USA), 5-ALA from Suzhou NMT Biotech (China), and DAPI from Solarbio (China). The 450/405 nm dual-wavelength blue laser platform was provided by Blueray Medical Ltd. (Xi’an, China).

Cellular uptake of 5-ALA

Cellular uptake and intracellular conversion of 5-ALA to PpIX were assessed by flow cytometry. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well), incubated for 24 h, and then treated with 200 µM 5-ALA in serum-free medium for 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, or 8 h. After treatment, cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and immediately analyzed.

Flow cytometry was performed on a FACSCalibur (BD Biosciences, USA) equipped with a 488 nm argon ion laser for excitation. PpIX fluorescence was collected through the FL3 channel (670 nm long-pass filter). 10,000 events were acquired per sample within the gated region. The gating strategy included: (1) exclusion of debris and cell fragments based on low FSC/SSC signals; (2) exclusion of cell doublets using FSC-A vs. FSC-H gating to select single cells; and (3) gating on the main viable cell population based on characteristic size and granularity. Unstained cells (without 5-ALA incubation) served as negative controls for background fluorescence thresholding. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using CCK-8. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (6 × 103 cells/well) and incubated with various concentrations of 5-ALA (0 ~ 400 µM) at 37 °C in darkness for 4 h, then exposed to a 450 nm laser (10 mW/cm2, 600 s, 6 J/cm2). Dark toxicity was assessed without laser irradiation. After 24 h of treatment, CCK-8 was added, and the optical density (OD) at 450 nm for each well was measured with a microplate reader.

Animal experiments

Animal care

Four-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Xi’an, China). All mice were maintained under a 12 h light/dark cycle in a temperature-controlled environment (22 ~ 24 °C) and had ad libitum access to food and water.

GBM models

For subcutaneous models, mice received dorsal injections of 1 × 106 GL261-luc cells. For orthotopic models, mice were stereotaxically injected with 5 × 105 GL261-luc cells (coordinates: 1 mm anterior, 2 mm lateral, and 2.5 mm depth). Tumor formation was confirmed by bioluminescence imaging on day 5. Body weight and tumor volume were monitored daily.

Photodynamic Diagnosis (PDD) and Therapy (PDT)

The laser irradiation parameters for PDT, including power settings, beam dimensions, irradiated area, irradiance, emission mode, exposure time, total delivered energy, and radiant exposure, are summarized in Table 2 for all experimental conditions (in vivo and in vitro). A beam homogenizer in the optical path expanded the laser beam to ensure uniform illumination across the entire target region. The irradiation parameters for in vivo experiments were selected based on our previously established protocols for 450 nm laser-mediated PDT in murine tumor models [32, 33]. In our prior studies, these parameters were systematically evaluated and validated for antitumor efficacy with favorable safety profiles [32, 33].

PDD

When subcutaneous tumors reached ~ 100 mm3, mice received 5-ALA (100 mg/kg, i.p.), and fluorescence imaging was performed at 0 ~ 8 h post-administration under 405 nm laser irradiation. Images were acquired (ISO 1600, 26 mm, -0.3 EV, f/2.2, 1.7 s) using protective eyewear with an OD of 6 at 200 ~ 450 nm and 740 ~ 1200 nm. For histological validation, contralateral tumors were harvested at matched time points for frozen sectioning and fluorescence microscopy, with quantitative analysis performed by measuring absolute mean fluorescence intensity in the tumor region of interest (ROIs) using ImageJ, after subtracting background from blank slide areas.

For orthotopic models, brains were harvested at designated time points post-5-ALA and processed for frozen sectioning. The signal-to-background ratio (SBR) was calculated as the mean fluorescence intensity of tumor ROIs divided by that of adjacent normal brain ROIs. All images for quantitative analysis were acquired using identical parameters, with measurements obtained from 3 to 5 representative sections per animal. Sections were subsequently stained with H&E for histopathological confirmation.

PDT

In vitro experiments were performed at a lower irradiance to accommodate cell culture conditions in 6-well plates. To ensure that the observed therapeutic effects were attributable to photodynamic activity rather than thermal injury, we monitored tissue temperature during preliminary experiments. We confirmed that the selected parameters did not cause a significant temperature increase (< 42 °C) [34].

For the in vivo therapeutic efficacy evaluation, mice bearing subcutaneous tumors (approximately 100 mm3) were randomly divided into six groups: (I) Control, (II) 5-ALA alone, (III) 630 nm laser alone, (IV) 450 nm laser alone, (V) RL-PDT (5-ALA + 630 nm laser, positive control), and (VI) BL-PDT (5-ALA + 450 nm laser). Mice in the PDT groups received 5-ALA (100 mg/kg, i.p.) followed by laser irradiation at the parameters described above. Tumor volumes and body weights were monitored daily throughout the experiment. After treatment, tumors and major organs were harvested for histopathological examination; tumors were stained with H&E and Ki-67 to assess morphological changes and proliferation, while major organs were stained with H&E alone to evaluate systemic toxicity.

Surgical resection combined with PDT

Mice were randomized into three groups: Control, Resection only, and Resection + PDT. Upon tumors reaching ~ 100 mm3, the Resection + PDT group underwent fluorescence-guided laser resection followed by PDT as described above. Tumor recurrence was monitored every 3 days by bioluminescence imaging. Recurrence was defined as a sustained increase in bioluminescence imaging signal above the post-treatment nadir, following previously established methods [35]. After the final bioluminescence imaging, the subcutaneous tumors from recurrent mice in the Resection and Resection + PDT groups, as well as those from the Control group, were harvested and processed for H&E and Ki‑67 staining.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.2 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used as appropriate, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Exact sample sizes (n) for each experiment are specified in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

The 450 nm laser exhibited comparable incision and coagulation ability

As shown in Fig. 1a, the incision created by laser ablation in focused mode exhibited a typical three-zone structure, including a vaporization zone containing eschar and a surrounding coagulation layer, both representing irreversibly damaged tissue, and an inner edema zone with potential for recovery [36]. In contrast, the coagulation damage under defocused mode lacked an obvious vaporization zone (Fig. 1b). For both 450 nm and 980 nm lasers, the dimensions of these incisions increased with power output (Fig. 1c and d). Quantitative analysis further revealed that both incision width and depth increased in a power-dependent manner (Fig. 1e and f). Notably, at equivalent power settings, the 450 nm laser produced significantly deeper incisions than the 980 nm laser (P < 0.05), and the thermal damage depth was significantly greater in the 980 nm laser group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the 980 nm laser tended to generate wider incisions and broader thermal damage zones than the 450 nm laser.

We next evaluated the tissue coagulation capacity of the 450 nm laser, using the 980 nm laser and bipolar forceps as controls. H&E staining revealed that, in contrast to the incision groups, the coagulation groups exhibited no obvious incision (Fig. 1g). As exhibited in Fig. 1h, quantitative analysis demonstrated that both the 980 nm laser and bipolar forceps induced more extensive tissue damage (in both depth and width) than the 450 nm laser, with the extent of damage following the order: 980 nm laser > bipolar forceps > 450 nm laser (P < 0.05).

The 405 nm laser-mediated PDD enabled effective tumor imaging

To assess fluorescence kinetics, mice bearing bilateral subcutaneous tumors received an intraperitoneal injection of 5-ALA. Tumor fluorescence was imaged under white light and 405 nm laser excitation at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h post-injection. Red fluorescence in tumor tissue gradually increased following 5-ALA administration, peaking at approximately 2 h and subsiding by 8 h (Fig. S2a). This finding was corroborated by fluorescence microscopy of frozen contralateral tumor sections obtained at matched time points (Fig. 2a). Quantitative analysis of absolute tumor fluorescence intensity in these sections confirmed the temporal trend, with peak intensity at 2 h post-injection (Fig. 2c).

Following confirmation of orthotopic glioma formation by bioluminescence imaging (Fig. S2b), ex vivo PDD was performed on perfused brains harvested at serial time points after 5-ALA administration. Characteristic fluorescence peaked at approximately 4 h (Fig. S2c), consistent with subsequent H&E and fluorescence imaging analyses (Fig. 2b). Quantitative SBR analysis, calculated as tumor versus adjacent normal brain tissue, revealed peak SBR at 4 h post-injection (Fig. 2d), which was later than that in the subcutaneous model (2 h). This delay likely reflects restricted accumulation of 5-ALA/PpIX due to the BBB.

The 450 nm laser-mediated PDT exhibited potent anticancer activity in vivo and in vitro

The optimal incubation time for 5-ALA was first determined by flow cytometry. A time-dependent increase in cellular uptake was observed, with peak fluorescence intensity in tumor cells occurring at approximately 4 h. In contrast, BV2 cells exhibited markedly lower relative fluorescence intensity throughout the time course (Fig. 3a). The photodynamic therapeutic efficacy of the 450 nm laser in combination with 5-ALA was subsequently assessed in glioma cells. Compared with 5-ALA treatment alone, PDT resulted in significant, dose-dependent inhibition of glioma cell proliferation. In contrast, no substantial inhibitory effect was detected in BV2 cells following PDT (Fig. 3b). In the absence of laser irradiation, 5-ALA alone at the tested concentrations displayed no appreciable cytotoxicity in any of the cell lines examined. We further compared the photodynamic efficacy of the 450 nm blue laser with that of the clinically established 630 nm red laser under identical 5-ALA concentration and light dose conditions. As shown in Fig. 3c, 450 nm-mediated PDT exhibited significantly stronger cytotoxicity against glioma cells than 630 nm-mediated PDT, demonstrating superior photodynamic efficacy of the blue laser in this setting.

The in vivo antitumor efficacy of PDT was evaluated using subcutaneous GL261-luc xenografts in C57BL/6J mice. One week post-inoculation, mice bearing tumors of approximately 100 mm3 received an intraperitoneal injection of 5-ALA, followed by 450 nm laser irradiation 2 h later (Fig. 3d). Real-time temperature monitoring at the tumor center during the 10-min irradiation revealed that the temperature remained consistently below 42 °C throughout the treatment (Fig. 3e), excluding thermal effects as a significant contributor to the antitumor efficacy. PDT significantly reduced tumor volume (Fig. 3f) and weight (Fig. 3g) compared with the control group, while body weight showed no significant differences among the groups (Fig. 3h), indicating minimal systemic toxicity. Gross images of excised tumors further confirmed the superior antitumor efficacy of PDT (Fig. 3i). Histologically, H&E staining revealed that PDT induced marked pathological alterations, including nuclear damage, reduced cell size, and diminished cytoplasmic eosinophilia (Fig. 3j). Consistently, Ki-67 immunohistochemistry demonstrated that the percentage of Ki-67-positive cells was significantly lower in the PDT group than in controls (Fig. S3a-b). Importantly, no histopathological abnormalities were detected in major organs across any treatment group (Fig. S3c), further confirming the favorable biosafety profile of 450 nm laser-mediated PDT.

Postoperative 450 nm laser-mediated PDT suppresses tumor recurrence

To evaluate the effect of postoperative PDT on glioma recurrence, we subsequently performed surgical resection combined with PDT. The results showed that, compared with the control group, both the resection-only group and the resection + PDT group exhibited significantly reduced postoperative tumor bioluminescence signals, with the lowest signal observed in the resection + PDT group (Fig. 4a). These findings suggest that combining surgical resection with PDT may reduce the risk of tumor recurrence. Furthermore, in vivo imaging showed that tumor volume in the control group increased over time (Fig. 4b). In contrast, after treatment, tumor volume was markedly reduced in both the Resection and Resection + PDT groups (Fig. 4c-d). However, on day 14, 80% of mice in the Resection group exhibited tumor recurrence, while only 1 out of 5 mice in the Resection + PDT group showed recurrence, confirming the satisfactory therapeutic efficacy of Resection + PDT in suppressing tumor recurrence (Fig. 4e-f). To further characterize the recurrent tumors, subcutaneous tumors from recurrent mice in the Resection and Resection + PDT groups, as well as those from the Control group, were harvested for H&E and Ki-67 staining. Histological examination revealed typical tumor features, including large hyperchromatic nuclei and densely packed tumor cells (Fig. S4a). Moreover, the proportion of Ki-67-positive cells did not differ significantly among the three groups (Fig. S4b). These findings suggest that combining surgical resection with PDT may reduce the risk of tumor recurrence.

Discussion

Neurosurgical procedures, particularly in eloquent areas, demand exceptionally precise instruments for resection and hemostasis due to the delicate and complex nature of the central nervous system. To date, various laser modalities, including CO₂, Nd: YAG, and KTP lasers, have been explored in neurosurgery for noncontact cutting and coagulation [36–39]. Recent studies highlight the favorable outcomes of 980 nm diode and 2 μm thulium lasers in neuroendoscopy and for the resection of brain tumors [19, 20, 40].

Within the visible spectrum, the penetration depth of light is proportional to wavelength. Blue-violet light, with its shorter wavelength, is typically limited to the epidermis, in contrast to red light, which can penetrate to the dermis [41]. In microsurgical neurosurgery, particularly for poorly delineated gliomas, the shallow penetration depth of short-wavelength lasers enables effective tumor vaporization and resection while minimizing thermal injury to underlying healthy brain parenchyma, thereby helping to preserve postoperative neurological function. The 450 nm laser exhibits pronounced hemoglobin absorption, which a prior study has correlated with high vaporization efficiency, a markedly thin coagulation layer, and minimal collateral tissue damage compared with the 532 nm, 980 nm, and 1470 nm lasers [42]. We therefore posit that the 450 nm laser is a highly promising tool for neurosurgery, particularly in glioma resection, as it provides efficient vaporization, precise dissection, moderate penetration, and minimal collateral damage. It should be emphasized, however, that the intended application of this platform is specifically limited to the intraoperative adjunctive clearance of microscopic residual tumor foci at the surface of the resection cavity, serving as a complementary therapy following maximal safe resection. For deeply infiltrating tumor cells beyond the reach of blue light, this strategy is not intended as a primary therapeutic modality. In this specific context, the restricted penetration of a 450 nm laser is therapeutically advantageous, as it enables precise photodynamic targeting of residual surface tumor cells while minimizing phototoxic damage to underlying eloquent brain areas—a risk that may be greater with deeper-penetrating 630 nm laser-mediated PDT [30, 41].

Assessment of cutting efficiency in ex vivo brain tissue revealed that while higher power increased incision depth and width for both 450 nm and 980 nm lasers, the 450 nm laser achieved greater incision depth at elevated powers without a commensurate increase in lateral thermal damage compared to the 980 nm laser. At matched incision depths, the 450 nm laser produced a thinner coagulation layer than the 980 nm laser, indicating superior vaporization precision with reduced collateral damage. This advantage was corroborated in dedicated coagulation experiments, in which the 450 nm laser also produced a thinner solidified layer than the 980 nm laser and bipolar forceps. These findings collectively suggest that the 450 nm laser may serve as a precise and effective tool for neurosurgical dissection and vaporization.

A central challenge in resecting infiltrative gliomas is the precise delineation of tumor borders to maximize tumor removal while preserving functional brain tissue. Currently, the fluorescent agent 5-ALA is the only FDA-approved optical imaging agent for intraoperative glioma visualization [36]. In 2006, a landmark multicenter randomized controlled trial led by Stummer demonstrated that 5-ALA FGS, as a surgical adjunct, nearly doubled tumor resection rates and significantly extended 6-month progression-free survival (PFS-6) [25]. In this study, we established both subcutaneous and orthotopic glioma models for PDD, achieving clear, specific tumor visualization under 405 nm laser excitation. The observed delay in peak fluorescence in orthotopic models is likely attributable to the impaired passage of PpIX across the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Future studies could explore drug-loaded nanocarriers designed to enhance PpIX delivery across the BBB and improve imaging efficacy.

Biopsies at the invasive margins of HGGs often contain few tumor cells [43], resulting in a low PpIX fluorescence detection rate and highlighting a limitation of PDD for HGG detection. This limitation implies that 5-ALA FGS alone is insufficient to achieve complete tumor removal, thereby increasing the risk of recurrence and underscoring the profound clinical value of immediate adjuvant therapy after resection. Recent intraoperative techniques for complementing surgical resection in HGG treatment include laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) [44], intraoperative brachytherapy [45, 46], PDT [47, 48], sonodynamic therapy (SDT) [49], and focused ultrasound (FUS) [50, 51]. These modalities offer distinct mechanisms for targeting residual tumor cells: LITT provides local thermal ablation; intraoperative brachytherapy delivers continuous irradiation; PDT and SDT induce cytotoxic effects within tumor cells; and FUS enables precise energy delivery. Each approach has shown varying degrees of clinical success, with PDT and LITT more widely implemented, whereas SDT and FUS remain promising but are under investigation [52].

Therefore, postoperative PDT directed at the tumor bed represents a suitable and convenient adjuvant therapy following surgical tumor resection. In this study, we demonstrated that PDT using a 450 nm laser with 5-ALA markedly reduced glioma cell viability in vitro and suppressed tumor growth in vivo. Furthermore, combining PDT with 450 nm laser-based tumor resection effectively reduced tumor recurrence. Notably, intraoperative PDT has recently been shown to reduce tumor recurrence in newly diagnosed gliomas [24]. At the same time, a retrospective matched-pair cohort study further demonstrated that immediate PDT after 5-ALA-guided resection improves survival in recurrent malignant glioma [25].

We developed a dual-wavelength blue laser platform (405 nm/450 nm) that integrates PDD, precise ablation, and PDT into a unified system. The 405 nm channel visualizes the tumor intraoperatively. In comparison, the 450 nm channel enables both efficient resection (at higher power) and immediate postoperative PDT (at lower power) to target residual cells at the surgical margin. By integrating tumor identification, resection, and recurrence prevention into a single comprehensive strategy, this approach streamlines the surgical workflow, reduces instrument changes, and represents a paradigm shift toward unified diagnosis and therapy in glioma surgery.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although orthotopic glioma models were used for fluorescence imaging to assess the diagnostic performance of 405 nm PDD, therapeutic efficacy studies were conducted exclusively in subcutaneous tumor models, which do not fully recapitulate the complexity of human gliomas or surgical conditions. We acknowledge that the brain microenvironment, particularly the BBB, imposes optical and pharmacokinetic constraints that cannot be fully recapitulated in subcutaneous xenografts. The BBB may affect both the delivery of 5-ALA and the accumulation of PpIX in intracranial tumors, potentially altering the photodynamic response compared with that observed in peripheral sites. Consequently, the translational significance of our therapeutic findings is limited by the absence of orthotopic validation. Nevertheless, our in vitro PDT data demonstrated a clear concentration-dependent cytotoxic effect, and the in vivo subcutaneous model provided proof of concept that 450 nm laser-mediated PDT effectively suppresses tumor growth and reduces recurrence. These findings collectively establish a strong rationale for further evaluation in orthotopic models. Second, the limited penetration depth of blue lasers may restrict PDT to surgically exposed regions, making it ideal for intraoperative use but not for standalone treatment of deep tumors. Third, this study was conducted at the animal level; further validation in larger animal models and clinical trials is required to establish safety and efficacy. Future work should explore optimal dosing parameters, long-term safety, and combination strategies with immunotherapy or chemotherapy to enhance therapeutic outcomes, and validate this platform in orthotopic glioma models using survival analysis, longitudinal tumor monitoring via bioluminescence imaging, and histopathological assessment of local tissue responses.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed and validated a novel dual-wavelength blue laser platform (405 nm/450 nm) that integrates PDD, precise and efficient tissue ablation, and PDT for comprehensive glioma management. The 405 nm laser enables effective intraoperative tumor visualization, while the 450 nm laser provides superior cutting efficiency, minimal collateral damage, and potent PDT efficacy. Importantly, combining surgical resection with immediate postoperative PDT significantly reduces tumor recurrence. This integrated diagnostic-therapeutic platform represents a promising strategy to improve surgical outcomes and reduce recurrence in glioma patients. Although further preclinical and clinical investigations are warranted, our findings provide a foundation for translating this technology into clinical application in neurosurgical oncology.
