Frailty and Late-Onset Epilepsy: Two Clinical Faces of Biological Aging?
Authors: Vineet Punia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Associations and Potential Biological Pathways Between Physical Frailty and Epilepsy Incidence: A Large-Scale Prospective Cohort Study Hu W, Liu TS, Tian G, Wang JN, Ma YB, Zheng ZA, Feng TJ, Niu XX, Yan YN, Jia CX, Liu BP. Neurology. 2026 Jun 9;106(11):e218071. doi: 10.1212/WNL.0000000000218071. Epub 2026 May 14. PMID: 42133910.Background and objectives: Physical frailty has been longitudinally linked to multiple neurologic disorders but has not yet been extrapolated to epilepsy. The study aimed to investigate the prospective association between physical frailty and incident epilepsy and to explore potential biological processes associated with this relationship. Methods: This prospective cohort study included 421,383 U.K. Biobank participants free of epilepsy, Parkinson disease, dementia, stroke, or migraine at baseline. Physical frailty status was assessed using a modified Fried frailty phenotype model based on 5 components (weight loss, exhaustion, low physical activity, slow gait speed, and low grip strength) that categorized participants as nonfrail, prefrail, or frail. Incident epilepsy was ascertained through linked health records. Cox proportional hazard models were conducted to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for the association between physical frailty and incident epilepsy. Hypothesis-driven mediation analyses were used to investigate potential biological pathways associated with the frailty-epilepsy association. Results: During a mean follow-up of 13.2 years, 2752 incident epilepsy cases were identified. Compared with nonfrail individuals, the multivariable-adjusted HRs (95% CIs) for incident epilepsy among those with prefrailty and frailty were 1.29 (1.19-1.40) and 1.81 (1.54-2.13), respectively. Among the 5 frailty components, slow gait speed (HR 1.46; 95% CI 1.29-1.65), low grip strength (HR 1.19; 95% CI 1.08-1.32), exhaustion (HR 1.18; 95% CI 1.06-1.32), and weight loss (HR 1.17; 95% CI 1.06-1.29) were associated with higher hazards of incident epilepsy. Biomarker analyses observed that blood biomarkers reflecting liver and kidney function, immune-inflammatory response, endocrine activity, and hematologic profiles were associated with both frailty and incident epilepsy and may collectively explain approximately 18.57% (95% CI 13.18%-26.83%) of the frailty-epilepsy association. In addition, several metabolically related biomarkers, involving lipoprotein particle size, amino acids, ketone bodies, total lipids, and triglycerides, were identified as potential contributors. Discussion: Physical frailty was associated with a higher incidence of epilepsy in this prospective cohort. Biomarker analyses provided preliminary insights into biological domains potentially related to this association, including organ function, inflammation, endocrine regulation, hematologic status, and metabolic homeostasis.

Chronological age has long shaped how we think about epilepsy. Age influences diagnostic reasoning, therapeutic decisions, and expectations regarding treatment response. Nevertheless, we all recognize the shortcomings of this approach, especially in aging adults. Despite the same chronological age, 2 individuals may differ dramatically in activity level, functional independence, and vulnerability to illness. Frailty provides a framework for explaining this heterogeneity. Rather than reflecting years lived, frailty represents the cumulative decline in physiological reserve that accompanies aging and increases susceptibility to adverse health outcomes. 1 Increasingly, it is viewed as one of the most clinically meaningful measures of biological, rather than chronological, age. 2

The article by Hu et al extends the role of frailty in epilepsy beyond predicting outcomes after diagnosis to identifying individuals at increased risk of developing epilepsy itself. 3 Using data from more than 421 000 participants in the U.K. Biobank who were free of epilepsy and major neurologic disorders at baseline, the authors demonstrated a graded association between physical frailty and incident epilepsy over a mean follow-up of 13.2 years. Compared with nonfrail individuals, participants with prefrailty had a 29% greater risk of developing epilepsy, whereas frail individuals had an 81% greater risk after adjustment for demographic, lifestyle, and medical factors. 3 Among individual physical frailty components, slow gait speed showed the strongest association with incident epilepsy. Overall, though, the pattern suggested that epilepsy risk tracked with cumulative physical vulnerability rather than any single frailty feature. The investigators further identified inflammatory, metabolic, endocrine, hematologic, renal, and hepatic biomarkers that partially accounted for the observed association.

The study has several notable strengths. Its large sample size, prospective design, and extensive sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the findings. Importantly, the work moves beyond simple epidemiologic association by exploring biological pathways that may link frailty and epilepsy. 3 As such, it represents the first large-scale longitudinal evidence that frailty identifies individuals at increased risk of developing epilepsy. Hu et al therefore raise a provocative possibility: if frailty is modifiable, could it become a target for epilepsy prevention?

That possibility is compelling, but a distinction must be made between prediction and prevention. A factor that accurately predicts risk is not necessarily the factor driving that risk. Frailty may identify individuals already progressing along vascular, neurodegenerative, inflammatory, or systemic pathways that ultimately culminate in epileptogenesis. Although frailty clearly preceded epilepsy in the study by Hu et al, the analysis does not fully illuminate what occurred in the intervening years. Participants with stroke and dementia at baseline were excluded, but incident stroke and dementia during follow-up were not incorporated as potential mediators. This distinction is important because frailty strongly predicts both conditions, and cerebrovascular disease and neurodegenerative disorders account for a substantial proportion of epilepsy in later life. Thus, some of the observed associations may operate through established epileptogenic pathways rather than through frailty itself.

Viewed in isolation, Hu et al's findings identify frailty as another risk factor for incident epilepsy. However, when viewed within the broader aging literature, it suggests something potentially more important. Frailty may not simply coexist with, or cause, epilepsy. Rather, it may be a clinically measurable manifestation of the biological aging processes that render the brain increasingly susceptible to epileptogenesis. Notably, the biomarkers examined by Hu et al explained less than 20% of the association between frailty and epilepsy. 3 Because these biomarkers were measured concurrently with frailty, they should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive causal mediators. Additionally, the substantial unexplained component suggests that critical mechanisms may reside within the brain itself and may not be fully captured by circulating biomarkers.

What might those mechanisms be? Emerging evidence from geroscience provides important clues. Frailty is consistently associated with cerebral small-vessel disease, white matter injury, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, cortical and hippocampal atrophy, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, and neuroinflammation. 4 Many of these same processes are implicated in late-onset epileptogenesis. 5 Autopsy data from the Rush Memory and Aging Project demonstrated strong associations between late-onset epilepsy (LOE) and cortical macroinfarcts, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, and cortical atrophy in older adults. 6 A recent neuroimaging study of late-onset unexplained epilepsy identified diffuse cortical thinning patterns overlapping those observed in neurodegenerative disease, supporting the concept that some forms of LOE may lie along a continuum with age-related neurodegeneration. 7 In this context, frailty may serve less as an isolated exposure and more as a visible clinical marker of an aging brain–body system losing reserve.

Perhaps the most compelling conceptual framework is neural resilience. Studies from the Rush Memory and Aging Project demonstrated that frailty modifies the clinical expression of neuropathology. Individuals with lower frailty tolerated substantially greater Alzheimer-type pathology before manifesting cognitive impairment or dementia, whereas frailer individuals expressed clinical symptoms at lower levels of pathological burden.8,9 In essence, frailty may quantify an individual's capacity to withstand accumulated brain injury. An analogous mechanism may apply to epileptogenesis. The same biological aging processes that diminish resilience to cognitive decline may also lower resilience against seizure generation. Subclinical cerebrovascular injury, progressive blood–brain barrier disruption, persistent neuroinflammation, and network degeneration could accumulate over years, lowering seizure threshold even in the absence of clinically recognized stroke or dementia. 10

Importantly, the relationship between frailty and epilepsy is unlikely to be unidirectional. Frailty now joins cerebrovascular disease and cognitive decline among the unresolved bidirectional relationships that complicate our understanding of LOE. Hu et al suggest that frailty may increase vulnerability to epilepsy, but epilepsy itself may accelerate frailty. Older adults with epilepsy frequently experience falls, fractures, reduced physical activity, cognitive impairment, sleep disturbances, depression, polypharmacy, and recurrent hospitalizations, all established contributors to frailty. 1 Antiseizure medications (ASMs) may further impact gait stability, cognition, bone health, and functional reserve. Thus, frailty and epilepsy may participate in a reinforcing cycle in which each worsens the other.

This perspective carries important clinical implications. Frailty assessment has already demonstrated value in predicting ASM tolerability, hospitalization outcomes, and epilepsy surgery risk among older adults with epilepsy.1,11,12 The findings of Hu et al suggest that its role may extend even earlier, serving as a tool for identifying individuals whose aging brains have become increasingly vulnerable to epileptogenesis. 3 Because frailty is potentially modifiable, this observation is particularly relevant. Exercise programs, nutritional interventions, medication optimization, and comprehensive geriatric assessment can reduce frailty and improve a range of important health outcomes.2,13 Whether such interventions can also reduce epilepsy risk remains unknown, but the hypothesis is biologically plausible and testable.

Ultimately, the true value of Hu et al's contribution may be that it encourages us to reconsider epilepsy in later life through the lens of biological aging. The central question may not be whether frailty directly causes epilepsy, but whether frailty and LOE represent parallel manifestations of shared aging-related processes. Future studies integrating longitudinal frailty trajectories, neuroimaging, fluid biomarkers, and interventional trials will be needed to answer that question. If confirmed, frailty and LOE may prove to be 2 clinical faces of biological aging. The future of epilepsy care in older adults may therefore depend less on asking, “How old is this patient?” and more on asking, “How resilient is this patient?”
