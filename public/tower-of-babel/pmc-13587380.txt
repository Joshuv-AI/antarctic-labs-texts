African buffalo in focus: population structure, habitat use, and systematic behaviour in Ethiopia (Gambella National Park)
Authors: Bol Puoch Deng, Serekebirehan Takele, Gatluak Gatkoth Rolkier, Simon Shibru, John Barnabas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Information on African buffalo (Syncerus caffer) population dynamics and resource selection within Ethiopian protected areas remain limited. Grounded in optimal foraging theory and the risk-disturbance hypothesis, this study quantified buffalo population structure, abundance, habitat association, diet, and daytime activity budgets in Gambella National Park. Field monitoring spanned wet and dry seasons from 2022 to 2023. We deployed 35 line transects stratified across four distinct habitat types, processing encounter data through Distance 7.5 software. Spatial logistic regressions, micro-histological faecal analyses, feeding-track inspections, and instantaneous scan sampling were used to evaluate resource choice and behavioural rhythms. Fieldwork yielded 399 independent herd encounters. Population abundance estimates totalled 4,606 ± 3,335 individuals in 2022 and 4,584 ± 3,053 individuals in 2023, while a pooled model estimated 5,602 individuals (mean landscape density: 1.22 individuals/km²). The population showed a female-biased adult sex ratio (0.74:1.00 to 0.98:1.00) and steady calf-to-juvenile recruitment ratios (0.46:1.00 to 0.53:1.00). Seasonal fluctuations significantly shifted habitat selection (χ² (3) = 26.17, p < 0.001); herds crowded into permanent wetlands (~ 31%) and forestlands (26.16%) during dry periods. Logistic regression models indicated that buffalo occurrence correlated positively with forest canopy cover (OR = 1.036–1.038, p < 0.01), decreased with distance from water (p ≤ 0.03), and dropped near human settlements (OR = 0.78, p = 0.01). Diet selection comprised eleven grass species dominated by Urochloa mosambicensis, with no significant seasonal variation (χ² (10) = 4.87, p = 0.898). Diurnal activity budgets remained stable across seasons (χ² (8) = 6.22, p = 0.623), displaying a bimodal feeding pattern that accounted for roughly 30% of daytime activity. Although demographic parameters suggest short-term stability, intense herd compression into shrinking dry season refugia highlights underlying ecological vulnerability. Conservation frameworks must prioritize wetland protection, stricter enforcement of community-managed border buffer zones, and the preservation of transboundary migration corridors.

Introduction

The African buffalo (Syncerus caffer Sparrman, 1779) ranks among the most massive wild herbivores native to sub-Saharan Africa, serving as a foundational element within savanna ecosystems. As large-bodied bulk grazers, their intensive foraging regimes and rotational movement patterns directly dictate their seasonal habitat selection and daily behavioural budgets [13, 36].”

By stripping down massive volumes of tall grass biomass and altering vegetative cover, buffalo maintain a heterogeneous grazing mosaic. This structural manipulation can directly dictate resource availability and niche options for smaller, co-occurring herbivores [35]. Beyond their purely ecological contributions, African buffalo hold immense economic value via wildlife tourism networks, elevating the broader conservation profile and funding potential of protected areas continent-wide [36].

Historically, continental buffalo numbers plummeted during the catastrophic rinderpest panzootic of the late nineteenth century, an epidemic that triggered near-total mortality among wild and domestic ungulate populations across Africa [12]. While targeted disease eradication efforts and expanded anti-poaching frameworks eventually facilitated widespread population recoveries, contemporary herds confront a new matrix of systemic pressures [11]. Today, they remain heavily impacted by accelerating habitat loss, spatial fragmentation, cross-species disease transmission, illegal poaching, and encroaching human infrastructure [46, 49]. These stressors reshape basic demographic structures, distort historic migratory pathways, and alter micro-habitat selection by restricting herd access to critical water and forage reserves. Consequently, dissecting how buffalo navigate this intersection of baseline ecological drivers and anthropogenic pressures remains a prerequisite for designing actionable, evidence-based conservation frameworks.

African buffalo are intensely social ungulates that maintain highly structured herd hierarchies. Populations typically organize into stable breeding herds consisting of adult females, their immediate offspring, and a shifting cohort of associated adult males, whereas other mature males opt for solitary lifestyles [25] or form distinct, fluid bachelor groups [35, 45]. Actual herd sizes diverge drastically across ecosystems, fluctuating based on localized primary productivity, top-down predator pressures, and seasonal resource pulses. Within highly productive savanna landscapes, aggregating into massive super-herds offers clear evolutionary benefits, optimizing predator detection, enabling collective defence behaviours, and maximizing the exploitation of expansive forage patches [45]. Even so, sharp seasonal fluctuations in precipitation, plant productivity, and surface water availability heavily dictate how these aggregations disperse across the landscape [43]. As the dry season intensifies, herds regularly contract around permanent hydrological features and highly resilient, green vegetation zones [4, 34].conversely, the onset of wet-season rains permits expansive dispersal across newly rejuvenated upland grazing territories [20].

This reliance on specific environmental resources directly governs buffalo spatial selection patterns and broader population viability. At a behavioural level, these choices align with Optimal Foraging Theory, which argues that large-bodied herbivores dynamically adjust their landscape use and step-selection paths to maximize net nutritional energy intake while minimizing metabolic expenditure during transit. Because buffalo are specialized grass feeders, their landscape occupancy tracks areas capable of supplying substantial grass biomass, elevated nutritional profiles, and immediate proximity to reliable drinking water [35]. Seasonal drops in both grass quality and raw availability trigger immediate changes in herd transit routes, foraging duration, and patch selection. Across many African savannas, these ungulates mitigate seasonal resource bottlenecks by altering their spatial layout across the landscape rather than executing complete shifts in their taxonomic diet profile [17, 45]. During prolonged droughts, permanent wetlands, floodplains, and riparian bands become irreplaceable hydrological and nutritional micro-refugia, retaining high-moisture green forage long after the surrounding upland grasslands have cured and lost their nutritional value [35].

Evaluating feeding ecology provides a vital window into how buffalo populations metabolically cope with this profound environmental variability. While buffalo diets consist almost entirely of graminoid species, the proportional intake of specific grasses shifts alongside local species availability, seasonal rainfall variations, and underlying soil-moisture conditions [27, 40]. Highly productive, nutrient-dense perennial grasses like Panicum maximum, Themeda triandra, and Urochloa mosambicensis frequently surface as primary dietary pillars for savanna populations [27, 38]. Yet, simple taxonomic lists of consumed plants fail to fully illustrate the nutritional barriers these animals face. Broad seasonal declines in available plant biomass, crude protein content, and digestible fibre heavily dictate overall physical condition and spatial choice [35]. Because of this complexity, linking empirical diet selection profiles with fine-scale, localized habitat properties is critical for isolating the actual ecological mechanisms driving seasonal herbivore distribution.

Daily behavioural budgets represent a parallel mechanism through which buffalo adjust to immediate environmental changes. Large herbivores routinely shift the timing of foraging, resting, traveling, and thermoregulatory behaviours based on ambient temperature curves, forage quality, localized hunting or predation risks, and surface water access [35, 45]. Within tropical savanna belts, buffalo heavily concentrate their feeding bouts during cooler morning and late-afternoon windows, transitioning to resting or mud-wallowing behaviours during peak midday heat to minimize core thermal stress [47, 48]. Simultaneously, under the Risk-Disturbance Hypothesis, human disruptions operate as non-lethal but powerful behavioural constraints. This framework demonstrates that wild ungulates interpret human encounters and infrastructure as a form of active predation threat. This perceived danger compels them to make critical energetic trade-offs [33] often completely avoiding high-risk, human-dominated sectors even when those spaces offer superior forage quality or crucial water access. Tracking seasonal shifts in behavioural budgets and diurnal rhythms can therefore reveal how buffalo balance mandatory metabolic needs against severe environmental and anthropogenic limitations.

Within Ethiopia, African buffalo persist across a handful of isolated protected areas, most notably within Omo, Mago, Alitash, and Chebera Churchura National Parks [1, 2, 14]. However, when contrasted against the extensive research histories of southern and eastern African savannas, deep empirical gaps remain regarding Ethiopian buffalo ecology. Past domestic research has largely cantered on foundational species presence-absence inventories and broad, descriptive demographic surveys [2].”

This leaves a major scientific blind spot regarding how these water-obligate bulk grazers adapt their spatial positioning, dietary preferences, and daily behavioural time allocations within highly fractured, transboundary border matrices. It is no longer methodologically sound to merely state that data is scarce. Instead, the pressing ecological question is how a keystone species navigates extreme habitat seasonality when cornered against an unstable park boundary characterized by intensive agricultural conversion, illicit transboundary hunting corridors, and extensive competition with domestic livestock grazing networks [19].

Gambella National Park serves as a premier, high-stakes landscape for analysing these exact ecological pressures. The park encompasses an expansive, highly dynamic landscape matrix of permanent wetlands, seasonal floodplains, and open woodlands [3] that support rich large-mammal communities, yet it faces aggressive environmental and human-driven transformations [19]. Intense seasonal flooding events, unpredictable water shifts, and heavy livestock incursions along the park perimeters directly reshape how buffalo access vital resources. Because the exact individual and combined impacts of these pressures on buffalo population viability and behavioural ecology remain poorly documented, a rigorous evaluation is required to inform actionable, data-driven management policies capable of safeguarding these declining herds and their vulnerable habitats.

Consequently, this study aimed to assess the population status, habitat associations, feeding ecology, and diurnal activity patterns of the African buffalo within the highly dynamic landscape of Gambella National Park. To achieve this, we addressed five interconnected research objectives across both wet and dry seasons: (1) to evaluate the current population structure, abundance, and macro-landscape density of the buffalo population using line transect distance sampling methods; (2) to identify seasonal shifts in habitat allocation and determine the key environmental and anthropogenic predictors governing buffalo space use; (3) to quantify the specific dietary composition and seasonal forage preferences of local herds; (4) to characterize daytime activity budgets and diurnal behavioural rhythms across changing seasons; and (5) to establish evidence-based management strategies to secure critical seasonal movement corridors and hydrological refugia. By resolving these fundamental questions, this work provides a framework for landscape-scale conservation planning in transboundary conservation networks across the region.

Materials and methods

Study area

This research was conducted within Gambella National Park (Fig. 1), a protected area positioned across the lowland plains of the Gambella People’s National Regional State in southwestern Ethiopia [15]. Geographically, the park spans between latitudes 7°52′–8°00′ N and longitudes 34°00′–34°15′ E, situated approximately 870 km southwest of the capital city, Addis Ababa. Initially gazetted in 1973, Gambella National Park encompasses a spatial footprint of approximately 4,575 km², making it one of the largest protected conservation strongholds in the country [15]. The park constitutes a vital geopolitical and ecological node of the broader, transboundary Gambella–South Sudan savanna complex, hosting globally significant populations of large migratory ungulates and apex carnivores [19, 53].

A tropical monsoonal climate governs the study region, splitting the annual cycle into distinct wet and dry periods. The wet season spans from May to October, whereas the dry season encompasses the remaining months from November to April. Mean annual precipitation clusters around 1,400 mm, though rainfall delivery exhibits profound seasonal oscillations. The mean annual temperature hovers near 27 °C; however, ambient temperatures frequently peak at 45 °C during March and plunge to a minimum of roughly 10.3 °C in December [10]. Structurally, the landscape presents a low-relief, generally flat plain. Elevations range from 394 m above sea level in the far western lowlands to roughly 500 m across the core park interior, though the peripheral eastern highlands ascend to approximately 2,300 m [44].

Gambella National Park protects an environmental mosaic composed of diverse woodland, grassland, and wetland habitats. The dominant floral communities include Combretum–Terminalia woodland, Tamarindus–Terminalia woodland, Balanites–Acacia woodland, pure Acacia woodland, Ziziphus–Acacia wooded grassland, Oryza–Ziziphus savanna grassland, Hyparrhenia–Oryza open grassland, and seasonal or permanent Cyperus–Oryza wetlands [18]. For the purposes of this study, the regional landscape was partitioned into four primary ecological habitat zones: open savanna grassland (covering 38.5% of the total study area), wooded grassland (31.2%), forestland (16.4%), and complex wetland matrices (13.9%). These defined habitat divisions diverge sharply in baseline vertical vegetation architecture, seasonal forage biomass productivity, and surface water retention, thereby directly shaping their overall carrying capacity and suitability for the African buffalo (Syncerus caffer).

The park supports an expansive array of biological diversity, featuring 69 mammal species, 327 avian species, 7 reptile species, 493 floral species, and 92 documented fish species [44]. Prominent megafaunal representatives include the African buffalo, African elephant (Loxodonta africana), lion (Panthera leo), leopard (Panthera pardus), Nile lechwe (Kobus megaceros), and white-eared kob (Kobus kob) [19]. Large carnivore densities across the primary monitoring sectors are estimated at 0.03 lions/km² and 0.05 spotted hyenas/km² [15]. These apex predators exert crucial top-down regulation on regional herbivore communities, acting as primary lethal and non-lethal selective drivers that dictate buffalo landscape choice, herd vigilance behaviours, and social clustering patterns [35].

Despite its formal legal designation, Gambella National Park faces severe human pressures along its borders. The foremost drivers of change include illegal livestock incursions, smallholder agricultural encroachment, permanent settlement sprawl, anthropogenic seasonal fires, and unmanaged natural resource harvesting. Livestock grazing drives immediate interspecific resource competition; domestic cattle densities average roughly 18.4 cattle/km² inside core park boundaries, creating severe resource bottlenecks during the dry season [52]. Meanwhile, smallholder expansion along the eastern boundaries has induced a steady 1.2% annual loss of core savanna habitats. Furthermore, human-ignited dry-season fires alter up to 45% of the open grasslands annually between December and February. To capture how these disturbances potentially displace native wildlife, these anthropogenic variables were formally embedded as predictors within our spatial habitat-use regression models.

Reconnaissance survey

We executed a preliminary reconnaissance survey in January 2022 to empirically evaluate the physical geography and biological conditions of the study area. This initial fieldwork prioritized the assessment of localized habitat conditions, transect accessibility, vegetation stratification, historic wildlife gathering nodes, and fine-scale environmental factors capable of skewing buffalo detection [28]. Trial transects were traversed within representative vegetation bands to evaluate horizontal visibility, animal flight-initiation distances relative to observers, and the practical validity of systematic distance sampling protocols. The insights gathered during this reconnaissance window were ultimately leveraged to optimize the final sampling framework, map the geographic coordinates of permanent water points and human settlements using global positioning systems (GPS), and verify that all four macro-habitat strata received proportional field representation.

Sampling design

The target landscape was stratified into four primary vegetative divisions: forestland, wooded grassland, savanna grassland, and wetland matrices. We gathered African buffalo population data via line-transect distance sampling, adhering closely to the geometric and mathematical principles pioneered by Buckland et al. [6, 7]. This distance sampling approach was chosen because it explicitly parameterizes variations in imperfect detection probability. It yields unbiased animal density and abundance evaluations by mathematically modelling how detection probability decays as the perpendicular distance between the target animal group and the observer’s travel path increases [7].

Field teams established a total of 35 systematic line transects across the park layout. Thirty of these transects were distributed throughout the wooded grasslands, savanna grasslands, and wetland matrices, while the remaining five transects traversed the interior forestlands. We systematically spaced these lines to optimize geographic coverage across the terrain, ensuring a minimum distance of 0.5 km between parallel transects. Final ground positioning was locked in following field validation to ensure team safety and physical passage.

Because Gambella National Park is characterized by vast wetlands, seasonal floodwaters, and dense riverine thickets, maintaining an identical, perfectly balanced sampling effort across all four strata proved logistically impossible. Extreme seasonal inundation and physical barriers restricted uniform transect placement. To eliminate structural detection biases brought on by shifting seasonal vegetation density—such as tall grass obscuring baseline horizontal sightlines during wet periods—we modelled separate detection probability functions (g(x) independently for each individual habitat stratum using Distance 7.5 software [50]. Additionally, we gathered African buffalo population data via line-transect distance sampling, adhering closely to the geometric and mathematical principles pioneered by Buckland et al. [6, 8]. Repeated seasonal surveys were executed across a continuous two-year field monitoring block to smooth out the confounding effects of temporary, short-term herd migrations and shifting transboundary corridors [5, 31, 32].

Data collection protocols

Population abundance and herd structure surveys

We conducted field transect surveys across both wet and dry seasons from 2022 to 2023. These technical field operations were strictly confined to early morning (06:00–09:00 h) and late afternoon (16:00–18:00 h) windows, optimizing detection probabilities when target animals are actively foraging and visible [23].

Field teams traversed the designated transects on foot or using field vehicles maintaining a steady speed of 10–15 km/h. During each survey run, a team of three trained observers scanned both flanks of the central centreline. For every independent buffalo encounter, observers recorded a standard set of metadata: the absolute count of individuals, the spatial herd size configuration, precise geographic coordinates via GPS, time of day, season, and the surrounding macro-habitat type. We determined the perpendicular distance from the transect centreline to the geometric centre of the herd using high-precision optical laser rangefinders (accurate to ± 1 m). Field teams closely cross-examined herd movements to prevent double-counting errors, cross-verifying distinct group age-sex ratios, real-time location vectors, and prominent individual morphological markings. Using the diagnostic body sizes, horn development stages, and pelage colour characteristics outlined by Kingdon [24], we classified encountered individuals into seven distinct demographic groups: adult males, adult females, sub-adult males, sub-adult females, juvenile males, juvenile females, and calves.

Feeding ecology and diet composition

To investigate feeding ecology, we quantified dietary composition by combining faecal scat micro-histological analysis with systematic leftover forage surveys across all seasonal blocks. Fresh faecal scat samples (n = 100 per season) were gathered immediately after herds departed from confirmed foraging zones. These samples were initially preserved in 70% ethanol, subsequently oven-dried, and prepared using standard micro-histological clearing techniques. We identified epidermal plant fragments under a compound light microscope, cross-referencing cellular structures, distinct trichomes, and stomatal configurations against a comprehensive reference collection of park grasses assembled during our initial reconnaissance phase.

Simultaneously, we ran leftover forage tracking along active feeding paths to validate and complement the faecal datasets. Observers tracked foraging herds from a safe distance, systematically recording fresh plant fragments, severed grass stems, and unambiguous bite scars within 2 m × 2 m quadrats randomly dropped along the grazing route. We identified these heavily utilized plant species down to the genus and species level to calculate their relative local frequencies of occurrence.

Diurnal activity budgets

We evaluated daytime behavioural budgets using direct visual observations of visible herds from elevated vantage points, employing a combination of focal-animal and instantaneous scan sampling protocols [26]. Field scans were executed at 15-minute intervals continuously from 06:00 h to 18:00 h. During each sampling interval, observers recorded the dominant behavioural state of all visible herd members, binning them into one of nine mutually exclusive categories: feeding (active cropping or ingestion of forage), standing (passive upright posture without visible alert scanning), resting (recumbent lying or active rumination), walking (steady locomotion), running (rapid flight responses), wallowing (immersion in mud or surface water), grooming (auto- or allo-grooming actions), drinking (active water consumption), and defecating (faecal elimination). Fresh faecal scat samples (n = 100 per season) were gathered immediately after herds departed from confirmed foraging zones, using strict spatial verification matrices to prevent cross-species contamination [21].

Statistical analysis

We processed the field distance sampling data through Distance 7.5 software to estimate seasonal population densities, total abundance, and associated standard errors [7, 8]. To model the detection probability function, we fitted key candidate detection engine shapes—specifically the half-normal and hazard-rate keys—and evaluated their performance via the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Final model selection favoured the specific configurations that minimized AIC scores while maintaining stable goodness-of-fit metrics.

To determine the spatial drivers of landscape occupancy, we modelled buffalo habitat-use dynamics using logistic regressions housed within a generalized linear modelling (GLM) framework to evaluate resource selection [29]. This allowed us to calculate odds ratios (OR) evaluating the probability of buffalo presence across gradients of forest canopy cover, surface water proximity, and distance from human settlements. All spatial data compilation, distance matrix processing, and geographic layers were managed using the sf and adehabitatHR packages in R software (R Core Team, 2023) [21, 30].

Furthermore, we assessed seasonal fluctuations in taxonomic diet composition and daily behavioural activity budgets using Pearson Chi-square (χ²) contingency tests and non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests to isolate localized physiological trade-offs [39]. The relative magnitude of these behavioural and dietary shifts was quantified using Cramer’s V effect-size coefficients. We evaluated all inferential statistical models at a baseline significance threshold of α = 0.05.

Across both study years, the population maintained a structurally stable, female-biased adult sex ratio. During Year I, adult female abundance stayed relatively uniform between seasons (Wet: 107.01 ± 12.44; Dry: 102.61 ± 19.27), consistently exceeding adult male counts. Concurrently, adult male counts shifted upward from 78.84 ± 15.71 in the wet season to 100.19 ± 16.85 during the dry period. A matching demographic profile emerged during Year II, with adult females outnumbering adult males and maintaining seasonal means of 95.17 ± 11.02 and 87.92 ± 10.70 individuals, respectively.

Population status, trend, and habitat use of African buffalo in Gambella National Park

Population abundance and broad landscape macro-trends

The macro-ecosystem of Gambella National Park supports a stable baseline population of African buffalo (Syncerus caffer), though individual seasonal models exhibit wide confidence limits due to the difficulties of surveying dynamic tropical savanna landscapes (Table 6).

Discussion

Population structure, density, and habitat use of African buffalo in Gambella National Park

The adult sex ratio (AM: AF) demonstrated remarkable stability throughout the multi-year monitoring period, maintaining a narrow, female-skewed range between 0.74:1.00 and 0.98:1.00 (Table 2). This sustained female bias aligns with established demographic baselines for stable or expanding megaherbivore cohorts across African savanna ecosystems, where high maternal-to-paternal ratios optimize herd reproductive capacity and preserve steady recruitment rates [35, 45].

Sub-adult and juvenile cohorts displayed clear seasonal shifts, which were likely driven by a combination of calving phenology and changing vegetation visibility. Sub-adult males during Year I decreased from a wet-season peak of 111.12 ± 16.55 to 78.49 ± 4.83 in the dry season, while Year II sub-adult male counts shifted from 85.52 ± 1.37 to 70.35 ± 6.86. Juvenile cohorts tracked a similar downward trajectory from wet to dry periods across both years; for instance, Year I juvenile males fell from 117.01 ± 22.44 to 80.85 ± 2.47. This seasonal pattern extended to calf counts, which dropped from 90.65 ± 3.90 to 74.08 ± 9.24 in Year I, and from 80.07 ± 4.06 to 72.65 ± 4.56 in Year II.

When standardized against a baseline value of 1.00, the age-class ratios confirm a highly active recruitment pipeline (Table 2). The sub-adult to adult ratio hovered near parity during the wet seasons (1.01:1.00 in both years) before shifting downward during the dry seasons (0.74:1.00 and 0.81:1.00), tracking predictable maturation transitions. Crucially, the calf-to-juvenile ratios remained exceptionally strong and tightly clustered across all seasonal blocks, ranging between 0.46:1.00 and 0.53:1.00. This sustained proportion of young age classes relative to mature individuals highlights robust juvenile survival rates and steady reproductive input, indicating a resilient demographic structure capable of long-term population persistence inside the park boundaries.

Seasonal herd size dynamics

The Table 3 shows dry-season herds aggregated into massive baseline clusters, expanding to an average of 97.22 ± 15.60 individuals per herd. Across all observation blocks, the overall mean herd size was calculated at 81.61 ± 15.30 individuals. This grouping behaviour mirrors classic savanna ungulate dynamics, where widespread wet-season forage availability allows smaller sub-herds to scatter and minimize intra-specific competition, whereas dry-season resource scarcity forces massive aggregations around reliable water networks.

Population density and abundance

Information from Table 4 show stable population footprint is carried over into Year II. The half-normal model estimated wet-season density at 8.4 individuals/km² (SE = 1.1), representing a localized population pool of 1,040 individuals (95% CI: 850–1,230). The subsequent dry-season hazard-rate model indicated a minor spatial contraction to 7.9 individuals/km² (SE = 1.0), which yields an abundance estimate of 980 individuals (95% CI: 790–1,170). Taken together, the distance sampling framework demonstrates a remarkably stable target population size over time, fluctuating predictably within a narrow seasonal window of 960 to 1,040 individuals inside the core monitoring zones. This minimal inter-annual variance validates the choice of distance sampling across stratified strata to establish reliable wildlife baseline population metrics in complex mosaic systems [7, 50].

Proximity to surface water sources functioned as an overarching spatial constraint, as indicated by the logistic regression analysis (Table 5). These results confirm that the population packs tightly into riverine and wetland configurations when seasonal pasture dehydration accelerates. This tight grouping mirrors empirical findings across southern and eastern Africa, labelling the buffalo as a highly water-dependent species whose maximum spatial range is rigidly restricted by daily drinking requirements [37].

Finally, the models provided clear quantitative evidence of human disturbance avoidance. Buffalo occurrence dropped significantly near human settlements (β = -0.25 ± 0.10; OR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.64–0.95; p = 0.01). This odds ratio demonstrates that for every kilometre closer a habitat plot sits to a human settlement boundary, the odds of buffalo presence plummet by a substantial 22%. This drop-off confirms intense spatial displacement driven by active encroachment and agricultural threats. This clear avoidance conforms heavily to the Risk-Disturbance Hypothesis, demonstrating that non-lethal human disturbances trigger spatial shifts akin to active predation threats.

Ecological implications and comparative synthesis

Nutritional ecology and forage quality pathways

While dietary analysis identified eleven major grass species consumed consistently across seasons, the underlying driver for shifting home ranges toward wetland matrices during dry spells is linked explicitly to pasture nutritional quality. Perennial grasses like Panicum maximum and Themeda triandra maintain high crude protein percentages (8–11%) and moisture levels during the wet season. However, as the dry season advances, upland forage undergoes rapid lignification, dropping crude protein content below the 5–6% maintenance threshold required for large bulk-grazing ruminants [35]. By shifting localized occurrence toward Ischaemum fasciculatum-dominated permanent wetland zones, the buffalo population accesses critical micro-refugia that sustain vital metabolic requirements when surrounding savanna matrix options fail, adhering to the principles of Optimal Foraging Theory [15, 17, 51].

Community ecology and predation avoidance dynamics

The spatial distribution patterns identified via our logistic model reflect complex interactions with sympatric wild species and apex predators. As bulk-feeders, buffalo grazing groups facilitate the creation of high-nutrient grazing lawns by keeping grass canopies short, physically opening up dense spaces for smaller sympatric grazing targets like white-eared kob (Kobus kob) and common reedbuck. Furthermore, the substantial increase in dry season herd size (averaging 97.22 ± 15.60 individuals per herd) is an adaptive defence behaviour mitigating localized top-down predation. Because core sampling sectors contain noted apex carnivore densities, clustering into massive aggregations around remaining water bodies scales up communal vigilance and group defence structures when absolute spatial avoidance is no longer an option.

Regional population dynamic status comparisons

Our distance sampling baseline model estimates a core monitoring zone density ranging from 7.7 to 8.4 individuals/km², mirroring a structurally stable demographic footprint. When evaluated within a broader macro-ecological context, Gambella National Park’s buffalo densities appear exceptionally robust compared to heavily degraded historical ranges across East Africa. For instance, adjacent over-encroached savanna matrices within Ethiopia’s Omo and Mago National Parks exhibit lower, highly unstable densities (< 2.5 individuals/km²) highly impacted by uncontrolled illegal hunting and competition with pastoralist livestock [19].

Conversely, pristine, fully protected core ecosystems like the Serengeti National Park or Akagera National Park showcase much higher carrying capacities and absolute densities ranging from 12 to 15 individuals/km² [45]. This places Gambella’s ecosystem in a critical intermediate position: the population displays steady recruitment indicators, yet its proximity to expanding human border hubs leaves it vulnerable if landscape connectivity paths degrade.

Population status, trend, and habitat use of African buffalo in Gambella National Park

Population abundance and broad landscape macro-trends

Data from Table 6 show alignment between the Year I (4,606 ± 3,335) and Year II (4,584 ± 3,053) annual mean totals indicates that the population did not undergo sudden demographic collapses or contractions during the study period. Minor intra-annual variations instead reflect seasonal redistribution driven by surface water availability and pasture quality. Raw direct counts increased from 181 to 218 verified sightings; a trend linked to higher visibility profiles and herd movements into accessible survey routes during late sampling blocks. Wide 95% confidence intervals (e.g., 2,100–11,100 in the pooled model) are common when using distance sampling to monitor large, social megaherbivores that form aggregated rather than uniform landscape distributions [7, 50]. Because buffalo cluster into large groups around remaining water sources during dry periods and disperse unpredictably across upland fields following rainfall, variance models naturally expand. This structural sampling profile is documented across adjacent transboundary African ecosystems where water-dependent ungulates move across open boundaries.

These abundance models emphasize the necessity of accounting for imperfect detection. Raw direct counts reveal only a fraction of the actual population. Consequently, systematic distance frameworks incorporate a detection probability matrix (P = 0.78–0.84; Table 4) to adjust for individuals obscured by dense low-canopy vegetation or structural field blocks. This correction is essential for African buffalo because variations in vegetative cover and seasonal herding dynamics heavily alter baseline encounter rates.

Spatial habitat selection and seasonal distribution shifts

Data from Table 7, show categorical habitat tracking showed clear seasonal shifts in distribution across the four primary ecological zones (Grassland, Wooded Grassland, Forestland, Wetland), supported by inferential modelling (χ² = 26.17, df = 3, p < 0.001; Cramér’s V = 0.211, indicating a moderate seasonal effect size; Table 7). During wet-season blocks, the buffalo population distributed widely across the landscape matrix, showing high utilization of permanent wetland boundaries (29.99%) and open savanna grasslands (27.98%), alongside moderate foraging inside wooded grasslands (19.71%) and dense forest cover (15.25%).

As dry-season drying progressed, habitat use underwent a distinct spatial reallocation. Utilization of open savanna grasslands dropped by more than half to 12.59%, while occupancy metrics within protective forestland blocks increased significantly to 26.16% (Table 7). Permanent wetlands remained the most critical spatial anchor across both seasons, reaching a peak occupancy profile of 31.15% during dry-season constraints. This reliance on forestland and permanent wetland spaces during dry spells highlights their function as vital ecological refugia. When open savannas dry out and lose nutritional value, closed forest canopies provide essential shade that protects these large bulk grazers from heat stress, while permanent wetlands supply green forage (Ischaemum fasciculatum) and steady access to water when surrounding upland ranges cure [17, 18, 41, 42].

Feeding ecology and diet composition

Nutritional ecology and refugia pathways

The Table 9 shows dietary stability across seasons highlights an ecological trade-off linking buffalo foraging choices to the landscape variables identified in our spatial regression models. As large-bodied ruminants, buffalo operate as bulk grazers that prioritize forage volume over high-quality selective bites [35, 16, 45]. High-biomass perennial grasses like Panicum maximum and Themeda triandra supply adequate nutritional value during wet periods, when metabolic crude protein levels range between 8% and 11%. However, during dry periods, these upland savanna grasses undergo rapid lignification. This process elevates cell wall thickness and drops crude protein content below the critical 5–6% maintenance threshold required to sustain large ruminant digestion.

To survive seasonal nutritional declines without altering their core diet, buffalo shift their geographic locations rather than changing the grass species they eat. Matrix inferential tests confirm this strict dietary stability. Scat consistency showed a negligible seasonal shift (χ²(10) = 4.87, p = 0.898, Cramer’s V = 0.031), (Cramer’s V = 0.031), and feeding leftovers revealed no selective divergence (χ²(10) = 3.41, p = 0.968), (Cramer’s V = 0.028). This spatial shift explains why local herds track so closely to permanent wetland zones during dry spells. These wetlands act as nutritional refuges. Hydrophytic grasses like Ischaemum fasciculatum retain cell moisture and crude protein long after upland savannas cure ([22, 27]; Ryan et al., 2006). By moving into wet zones, buffalo keep eating high-volume grasses, proving that the habitat mosaic of Gambella National Park buffers them against severe dry-season stress [19]. Beyond individual survival, this continuous bulk grazing alters the wider plant and animal communities. Massive grass consumption prevents dry litter buildup, which lowers seasonal fire fuel loads and builds high-nutrient grazing lawns. Shorter grass canopies and cleared coarse stalks make it easier for smaller, selective sympatric ungulates like white-eared kob (Kobus kob) and common reedbuck (Redunca redunca) to access high-protein food. However, relying so heavily on localized wet refuges introduces conservation risks. Since these critical foraging zones are small, human border infrastructure or expanding livestock networks within wetlands will easily disrupt this survival strategy, risking food competition and rapid population drops.

Thermoregulation and daily behavioural synchrony

The Table 10, Figs. 3 and 4 demonstrate sustained feeding allocation across seasons reflects the digestive constraints and bulk-grazing requirements of large wild ruminants. Because grasses have a high-cellulose, low-nutrient composition compared to browse, buffalo must ingest large forage volumes to meet metabolic demands [35, 45]. Maintaining daytime feeding rates near 30% allows the population to secure a steady volume of intake, balancing localized declines in grass digestibility between seasons [9, 36].

The temporal distribution of daytime behaviours showed a synchronized bimodal daily rhythm, balancing foraging requirements against thermal regulation. Grazing peaks occurred during cooler early morning (07:00–11:00 h) and late afternoon (15:00–18:00 h) periods. This bimodal distribution allows large savanna endotherms to maximize bulk intake while minimizing metabolic heat production during peak daylight temperatures [45].

Conversely, grazing activity declined during midday hours (11:00–14:00 h), matching an increase in resting and mud wallowing. Wallowing peaked between 12:00 and 14:00 h, serving as a primary mechanism for thermoregulation. Mud wallowing facilitates heat loss through evaporation, shields the skin from solar radiation, and provides a physical defence against ectoparasites for dark-skinned mammals in lowland savannas [35].

The lack of major seasonal shifts in behavioural budgets indicates that the habitat mosaic of Gambella National Park protects the population from severe seasonal resource stress. Dry-season limitations often force savanna ungulates to increase walking time to find water or expand feeding blocks to compensate for poor pasture. However, permanent wetlands and riverine forests allow these herds to maintain stable behavioural routines [20, 27]. This low hydration tracking time (1.38%–1.45%) aligns with our spatial regression models (Table 5). Because herds remain close to habitats near permanent rivers and wetlands during dry intervals, traveling costs to access water are reduced, preserving energy reserves.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study provides an integrated ecological assessment of the population status, spatial habitat associations, diet, and diurnal activity budgets of the African buffalo (Syncerus caffer) within the transboundary landscape of Gambella National Park, Ethiopia. Distance sampling models indicate that the regional buffalo population is currently stable. Landscape density was estimated at 1.00–1.01 ind/km², with an extrapolated total population baseline between 4,226 ± 2,850 and 4,987 ± 3,821 individuals. The combined two-year pooled distance framework produced a final population estimate of 5,602 ± 3,194 individuals across the macro-ecosystem, confirming that Gambella remains an important refuge for this megaherbivore within the protected area network of the Horn of Africa.

The population structure exhibits a female-biased adult sex ratio across both monitoring years. This structural skew aligns with savanna ungulate life histories, reflecting higher adult male mortality driven by bachelor group dispersal, intra-specific reproductive combat, and increased exposure to predation outside core breeding herds [35]. The uniform proportions of calves, juveniles, and sub-adults—with calf-to-juvenile ratios ranging between 0.46:1.00 and 0.53:1.00—indicate successful annual reproduction and steady survival. These metrics suggest that current environmental conditions provide adequate carrying capacity to support population replacement.

Spatial models support the core assumptions of Optimal Foraging Theory, showing that seasonal resource limitations drive buffalo social organization and distribution shifts. While wet-season moisture abundance allows breeding herds to fragment into smaller, mobile foraging units (66.01 ± 15.00 individuals) across open savanna grasslands, dry-season conditions trigger a distinct spatial contraction. During dry periods, independent groups merge into large aggregations (97.22 ± 15.60 individuals) concentrated within permanent wetland matrices and closed-canopy forestlands. Wetlands function as a primary spatial anchor throughout the year (~ 30–31% total occupancy). Protective forestlands act as seasonal micro-refugia, supplying thermal shade and preserving green biomass (Ischaemum fasciculatum) when surrounding upland grasses undergo dry lignification.

Simultaneously, inferential models support the Risk-Disturbance Hypothesis, demonstrating that anthropogenic pressures alter foraging routes. While buffalo maintain a synchronized, stable bimodal daytime activity budget (~ 30% grazing) across seasons, spatial analysis shows avoidance of human footprints. For every kilometre closer a landscape plot sits to a human settlement boundary, the odds of buffalo presence decrease by 22% (OR = 0.78), indicating spatial displacement driven by agricultural encroachment and hunting pressure.

Although demographic parameters suggest short-term stability, intense herd compression into small, isolated wetland-forest areas increases vulnerability to habitat degradation. If smallholder agriculture continues to expand along the eastern boundary and cross-border hunting remains unchecked, seasonal migration corridors risk disconnection. This fragmentation could trigger density-dependent population declines similar to those observed in over-encroached historical ranges across East Africa.

Recommendations for conservation management

To transition these empirical findings into practical management frameworks for the Gambella buffalo population, the following four policy actions are proposed:

Implementation of a Zoned Hydrological Protection Framework.Because permanent wetlands function as critical dry-season survival refugia, the park authority should designate a Core Hydrological Sanctuary Zone covering the central wetland matrices (13.9% of the park layout). This zone must enforce a strict prohibition on domestic cattle grazing, which averages a competitive density of 18.4 cattle/km² during dry periods. A joint Transboundary Hydrological Monitoring Taskforce should be established with water resource ministries to track upstream extraction and agricultural drainage along shared river networks, ensuring that baseline seasonal inflow volumes are maintained to preserve Ischaemum fasciculatum wetland forage beds.
