Insurance Network Status and 1‐Year Health Care Utilization and Outpatient Costs Among Commercially Insured Patients With Newly Diagnosed and Non‐Definitively Treated Prostate Cancer, 2010–2021
Authors: Aurora J. Grutman, Mark N. Alshak, Michelle I. Higgins, Christian P. Pavlovich, Derek K. Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCancer care in the U.S. exceeds $200B, with patients paying over $16B out‐of‐pocket. We examined whether insurance network status (in‐network [IN] vs. out‐of‐network [OON]) at prostate cancer diagnosis was associated with subsequent OON care use and patient spending within 1 year of diagnosis.MethodsUsing the MarketScan database (2010–2021), we identified patients aged 40–64 with a new outpatient prostate cancer diagnosis. Patients who received definitive local treatment (radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy) were excluded. The exposure was network status at diagnosis. Outcomes were the proportion of OON claims and patient spending for prostate cancer‐related care and/or non‐definitive treatments during 1 year of follow‐up. Results were stratified by insurance type.ResultsAmong 67,271 patients, 5% were diagnosed OON. Compared with IN diagnosis, OON diagnosis was associated with greater OON follow‐up claims (aIRR 29.0, 95% CI 25.0, 33.5), lower odds of prostate cancer‐related spending (aOR 0.55, 95% CI 0.49, 0.61), and lower outpatient costs (% difference = −8.6, 95% CI −13.4, −3.6). In stratified analyses, patients with HMO (aOR 0.54, 95% CI 0.44, 0.67) and PPO (aOR 0.47, 95% CI 0.41, 0.54) plans had lower odds of any spending. Patients with HMO (% difference = −22.3, 95% CI −30.9, −12.5) and CDHP (% difference = −26.5, 95% CI −43.6, −4.3) plans had lower costs.ConclusionsOON diagnosis was associated with continued OON care. Some patients diagnosed OON had lower liability, likely reflecting differences in plan design and adjudicated claims. Claims‐based estimates may underestimate the financial impact of OON non‐definitive prostate cancer care.

Introduction

The United States spends more on healthcare than any other country [1]. Healthcare spending in the U.S. rose to $4.9T in 2023 from $4.5T in 2022. Each year, over $200B is allocated toward cancer care [2, 3]. Although costs are shared among insurance companies, employers, and government programs, cancer patients pay approximately $16B out‐of‐pocket for healthcare each year [4].

The negative impacts of cancer care extend beyond the physical. Financial toxicity describes out‐of‐pocket expenses cancer patients face [5]. An estimated 12%–62% of cancer patients experience medical debt and nearly 50% feel financially stressed by cancer care [6]. Financial burdens may be particularly high for prostate cancer patients who undergo extensive testing at diagnosis and attend routine outpatient visits during the continued phase of care [7].

Prostate cancer is most commonly diagnosed in men over 65 years old, but it is increasingly common among younger individuals [8, 9, 10]. Younger patients may experience greater financial hardship due to varied healthcare coverage under private insurance plans and less long‐term savings [11]. In many cases, a prostate cancer diagnosis does not necessitate radical (definitive) treatment. In fact, guidelines support active surveillance for men with low to favorable intermediate risk prostate cancer [12]. While prior research has evaluated costs associated with treatment type and disease severity, less is known about how insurance coverage at the time of diagnosis may influence follow‐up care and care costs, especially in patients who defer definitive therapy and elect surveillance or alternative therapies (e.g., focal ablation) [13, 14].

Diagnosis represents a pivotal moment in the prostate cancer continuum, often initiating referral cascades and shaping continuity within or outside insurer networks [15]. Network status at diagnosis may therefore influence subsequent out‐of‐network care use and patient financial exposure. However, this association has not been well characterized, especially in patients whose disease did not warrant expeditious radical therapy with the standard of care definitive options of surgery or radiation therapy. We therefore investigated whether diagnostic network status (in‐ vs. out‐of‐network) at prostate cancer diagnosis among those not definitively treated was associated with out‐of‐network care use and patient out‐of‐pocket outpatient costs in the year following diagnosis. We hypothesized patients diagnosed out‐of‐network would have greater out‐of‐network follow‐up care compared to patients diagnosed in‐network and incur greater overall prostate‐cancer related outpatient costs as a result.

Methods

Data Source

This retrospective cohort study used data from the IBM MarketScan Research Databases which contain insurance claims from 20 billion service encounters, approximately 350 payers, and nearly 230 million patients in the United States [16]. We used MarketScan's Commercial Claims and Encounters (CCAE) database, which includes information from patients under 65 who are employed, early retirees, dependents on employer‐sponsored insurance plans, or covered under the Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act [16]. Inpatient and outpatient service claims, pharmaceutical claims, enrollment information, geographic region, insurance plan type, and detailed payment fields (insurer payment, adjudicated patient cost‐sharing) are available. Data analyzed in this study spanned 2010 to 2021. This study was approved by our Institutional Review Board in October 2024.

Study Population

We identified patients aged 18–64 who received a prostate cancer diagnosis (ICD‐9 185, 233.4, V10.46; ICD‐10 C61, D07.5, Z85.46) between January 2010 and December 2021. Current American Urological Association guidelines recommend prostate cancer screening begin at age 40 for select patients, so we restricted the cohort to patients aged 40–64 [17]. Patients were required to have at least one outpatient prostate cancer claim, one follow‐up visit, and continuous enrollment in a single insurance type for 6 months prior to and 1 year following the first outpatient diagnosis. The index diagnosis date was defined as the first outpatient claim containing a prostate cancer diagnosis code. Because the index date reflected the first observed prostate cancer diagnosis and continuous enrollment during the 6‐month baseline period was required, no prostate cancer diagnosis codes were observed in the baseline window. However, diagnoses prior to enrollment are not captured by MarketScan.

We identified treatment types within a year of diagnosis using inpatient and outpatient claims. Patients who received radiotherapy or radical prostatectomy were excluded to reduce heterogeneity in cost and care patterns driven by treatment type. We instead focused on outpatient prostate cancer care associated with active surveillance, focal therapies, and other non‐definitive treatments. However, because MarketScan claims lack stage and grade information, disease severity could not be directly assessed.

The following insurance types were included: healthcare maintenance organization (HMO), preferred provider organization (PPO), exclusive provider organization (EPO), consumer‐driven health plans (CDHPs), and high‐deductible health plans (HDHPs) due to varied cost‐sharing mechanisms [18]. Patients were excluded if they had missing/negative payment data, unknown diagnostic network status, mixed network payments at diagnosis, or missing demographic variables. Codes used to identify treatment types were compiled and validated from prior studies (Table S1) [19, 20, 21, 22]. Inclusion criteria are presented in the (Figure S1).

Exposure

The exposure was whether a patient received an in‐ or out‐of‐network prostate cancer diagnosis. Prostate cancer diagnosis was defined as the first instance of a prostate cancer claim. Patients with only out‐of‐network claims at the diagnostic visit were classified as out‐of‐network. Patients with only in‐network claims at the diagnostic visit were classified as in‐network. Patients with both in‐network and out‐of‐network claims on the index diagnosis date were excluded to avoid exposure misclassification.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of interest were the proportion of out‐of‐network prostate cancer‐related outpatient claims and patient out‐of‐pocket spending for prostate cancer‐related outpatient claims in the year after diagnosis. Outpatient prostate cancer claims, referred to as “prostate cancer‐related care,” were defined as claims containing a prostate cancer diagnosis code in any diagnosis position. Follow‐up claims were those occurring in the year after the index diagnosis date, excluding claims from the index diagnostic visit. Out‐of‐network prostate cancer care was modeled as a yearly rate defined as the number of out‐of‐network claims over the total number of claims in the year after diagnosis, excluding claims from the initial diagnostic visit. Cost analyses were restricted to outpatient medical claims and did not include pharmacy claims. For cost analyses, the net patient cost per claim was calculated as the sum of coinsurance, copayment, and deductible payments. Costs were inflated to 2021 US dollars using the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index and log‐transformed to reduce skewness [23].

Covariates

We constructed a directed acyclic graph (Figure S2) and selected age at diagnosis and geographic region as covariates. Results were stratified by insurance type. Age was modeled as a continuous variable. Geographic region (South, Northeast, North Central, West, as defined by MarketScan) was handled categorically. Comorbidities were conceptualized as influencing follow‐up care utilization but not diagnostic network status, so they were not included as covariates in the primary models.

Statistical Analysis

Differences in baseline characteristics among patients diagnosed out‐of‐network versus in‐network were compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables and Pearson's chi‐squared test for categorical variables.

To assess out‐of‐network outpatient care in the year following diagnosis, a multivariable negative binomial regression was constructed. We reported the incidence ratios (IRRs) to compare out‐of‐network care use in patients diagnosed out‐of‐network versus in‐network.

Because 8%–15% of patients reported no OOP spending and patient OOP spending was right skewed among those with positive values, we used a two‐part modeling approach. First, logistic regression was used to determine the odds of any spending. Second, linear regression on log‐transformed spending (costs) was performed for patients with non‐zero costs to estimate the relative difference in costs associated with the exposure. For cost analyses, the number of non‐diagnostic claims, defined as prostate cancer‐related outpatient claims in the year follow‐up period after diagnosis, excluding index date claims, was included as a covariate to account for variation in follow‐up healthcare utilization. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05. Analyses were conducted in STATA 18.0/BE (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

A total of 67,271 patients met the inclusion criteria. There were 64,131 (95%) patients diagnosed in‐network and 3140 (5%) diagnosed out‐of‐network. A greater proportion of out‐of‐network patients had an HMO insurance plan, but fewer had a CDHP or HDHP plan. Most out‐of‐network patients resided in the West while most in‐network patients lived in the South. In‐network patients paid more than out‐of‐network patients at the diagnostic visit ($37.92 vs. $33.95). Among patients with a non‐zero cost at diagnosis, in‐network patients paid less than out‐of‐network patients ($60.15 vs. $114.34). Yearly Out‐Of‐Network Healthcare Use

In‐network patients had more claims over more visits than out‐of‐network patients in the year following prostate cancer diagnosis. The median [IQR] number of non‐diagnostic claims was 13 [6, 41] for in‐network patients and 11 [5, 35] for out‐of‐network patients. In the overall model, out‐of‐network diagnosis was associated with significantly greater out‐of‐network claims in the year following diagnosis (aIRR 29.0, 95% CI 25.0, 33.5; p < 0.001). By insurance type, patients with HMO (aIRR 67.8, 95% CI 47.6, 96.6; p < 0.001) and EPO (aIRR 153.6, 95% CI 88.2, 267.8; p < 0.001) plans had the greatest proportion of out‐of‐network claims (Table 2).

Yearly Outpatient Prostate Cancer Care Costs

A greater proportion of patients diagnosed in‐network incurred any fees after the diagnostic visit compared to out‐of‐network patients (92% vs. 85%, respectively). In the overall model, out‐of‐network diagnosis was associated with lower odds of any outpatient prostate cancer‐related cost (aOR 0.55, 95% CI 0.49, 0.61) and lower outpatient costs overall (% difference = −8.6, 95% CI −13.4, −3.6). By insurance type, out‐of‐network diagnosis was associated with decreased odds of any outpatient costs for patients with PPO or HMO plans (Table 3). Out‐of‐network diagnosis was associated with significantly lower yearly outpatient costs for patients with HMO or CDHP plans (Table 4).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of privately insured U.S. men aged 40–64 with prostate cancer, out‐of‐network diagnosis was strongly associated with subsequent patterns of healthcare utilization in the year following diagnosis. Patients diagnosed out‐of‐network had 26–154 times greater out‐of‐network follow‐up claims compared to patients diagnosed in‐network. Despite this, we observed modest differences in outpatient costs. In stratified analyses, out‐of‐network diagnosis was associated with lower odds of any recorded prostate cancer‐related spending among patients with PPO and HMO plans and lower outpatient costs for patients with HMO and CDHP plans.

The large observed difference in out‐of‐network care use between patients diagnosed in‐ vs. out‐of‐network suggests that network status at diagnosis influences downstream care. Patients undergoing evaluation for prostate cancer often rely on primary care physicians for a urology referral [24]. This referral cascade may unintentionally channel patients diagnosed out‐of‐network into continued out‐of‐network care. Such patterns may persist even when patients have financial incentives to seek in‐network care.

Structural and behavioral factors may also contribute. Patients diagnosed out‐of‐network may continue care with the diagnosing provider because of perceived expertise, convenience, or a desire for consistency following an emotionally challenging diagnosis [24, 25]. Geographic variation in provider networks and hospital consolidation patterns may also increase the likelihood of out‐of‐network encounters. Among patients in the studied cohort, more out‐of‐network diagnoses occurred in the West, where large‐scale hospital consolidation may increase negotiating power with insurers and the likelihood of out‐of‐network services, even at in‐network hospitals [26, 27].

Although out‐of‐network diagnosis was strongly associated with out‐of‐network care use, patients diagnosed out‐of‐network did not incur greater outpatient costs. In fact, out‐of‐network diagnosis was associated with lower odds of any recorded prostate cancer‐related spending among certain insurance types. Several factors may explain this apparent paradox. Patients diagnosed out‐of‐network had fewer recorded follow‐up claims, suggesting possible care discontinuity, delayed care, or receipt of services outside the insurance system.

Insurance claims capture only payments processed through insurers. Payments outside the claims adjudication process (e.g., balance billing, denied claims, direct‐pay arrangements) are not observable in claims databases such as MarketScan [18, 28, 29]. Research shows out‐of‐network care often results in balance billing or denied claims [18, 28]. Claims‐based estimates may therefore underestimate financial burdens experienced by patients receiving out‐of‐network care.

Differences across insurance plan types may reflect variation in network design and cost‐sharing structures. For example, HMO plans typically restrict care to provider networks and rely on fixed copayments for covered services. In contrast, PPO and HDHP generally allow greater flexibility in provider choice but may expose patients to different cost‐sharing structures. However, because claims data do not capture balance billing, denied claims, or direct‐pay arrangements, the observed spending patterns should be interpreted cautiously.

Findings highlight a critical limitation of claims‐based analyses when evaluating financial burden associated with out‐of‐network care. Observed spending in claims may not fully reflect the financial risks faced by patients. Improving transparency in healthcare payment data by including information on denied claims and balance billing, for example, would enable more accurate assessments of patient financial burden.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine how network status at diagnosis influences out‐of‐network care use and healthcare costs for prostate cancer patients managed in the outpatient setting. The study is strengthened by its use of a large national cohort derived from a validated insurance claims research dataset [30]. Stratified analyses allowed evaluation of how insurance plan influences care and spending patterns.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the CCAE database includes only individuals under the age of 65. It does not reflect care patterns among older individuals who represent most prostate cancer cases. Second, we excluded patients who received surgery or radiation to focus on outpatient care claims. We therefore likely selected for patients with lower disease severity amenable to surveillance, deferred treatment, or alternative outpatient treatments such as focal therapy. However, variation in disease severity likely remains because tumor grade information was not available. It is possible that a mixture of patients with low‐risk disease on active surveillance, patients treated with focal therapy, patients who declined treatment, and even some higher risk patients who received systemic therapy were included. Moreover, the first observed prostate cancer diagnosis may not represent the incident diagnosis. In addition, the requirement for continuous enrollment during follow‐up may have introduced potential survivorship bias. Lastly, MarketScan does not provide data on race, ethnicity, and income, which shape healthcare access [30]. Findings should therefore be understood only as associations and not as causal relationships. We acknowledge the possibility of unmeasured confounding, given patients may have pursued out‐of‐network care for various reasons.

This study used data prior to the implementation of the No Surprises Act, a federal law designed to protect patients from unexpected out‐of‐network medical bills [29]. Future research should examine whether the legislation altered patterns of out‐of‐network cancer care. Additional research is needed to evaluate how network structures and insurance plan design influence care pathways and financial outcomes for patients with prostate cancer.

Conclusion

An out‐of‐network prostate cancer diagnosis was associated with greater out‐of‐network care use. However, it was not consistently associated with greater outpatient costs. Differences in spending may reflect plan design and limitations of claims‐based financial measures. Transparent data on unprocessed claims are needed to accurately assess financial burden and improve care for prostate cancer patients whose disease does not mandate immediate definitive treatment.
