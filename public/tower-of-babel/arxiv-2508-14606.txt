Approximating 1-in-3 SAT by linearly ordered hypergraph 3 3 -colouring is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard Thanks: This work is supported the UK EPSRC grant EP/X033201/1.
Andrei Krokhin, Danny Vagnozzi

Abstract
Given a satisfiable instance of 1-in-3 SAT, it is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard to find a satisfying assignment for it, but it may be possible to efficiently find a solution subject to a weaker (not necessarily Boolean) predicate than ‘1-in-3’. There is a folklore conjecture predicting which choices of weaker predicates lead to tractability and for which the task remains 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard. One specific predicate, corresponding to the problem of linearly ordered 3 3 -colouring of 3-uniform hypergraphs, has been mentioned in several recent papers as an obstacle to further progress in proving this conjecture. We prove that the problem for this predicate is 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} -hard, as predicted by the conjecture. We use the Promise CSP framework, where the complexity analysis is performed via the algebraic approach, by studying the structure of polymorphisms, which are multidimensional invariants of the problem at hand. The analysis of polymorphisms is in general a highly non-trivial task, and topological combinatorics was recently discovered to provide a useful tool for this. There are two distinct ways in which it was used: one is based on variations of the Borsuk-Ulam theorem, and the other aims to classify polymorphisms up to certain reconfigurations (homotopy). Our proof, whilst combinatorial in nature, shows that our problem is the first example where the features behind the two uses of topology appear together. Thus, it is likely to be useful in guiding further development of the topological method aimed at classifying Promise CSPs. An easy consequence of our result is the hardness of another specific Promise CSP, which was recently proved by Filakovský et al. by employing a deep topological analysis of polymorphisms.



1
Introduction

The Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP) framework is much studied in theoretical computer science (see e.g.
[
30
]
).
Since the resolution of the Feder-Vardi CSP dichotomy conjecture
[
14
,
40
]
, there has been much interest in the study of
qualitative
approximations of
\mathsf{NP}
-hard CSPs. The notion of
Promise
CSPs (PCSPs) provides the formal framework for studying these problems
[
2
,
6
,
11
,
28
]
. In the PCSP paradigm, approximation is understood not with the aim of maximising the number of satisfied constraints (as, e.g., in
[
25
,
33
]
), but via relaxing, in a uniform way, the constraints that must be satisfied. The
approximate graph colouring problem
is a prime example of a PCSP: for fixed constants
3\leq k\leq\ell
, given a graph promised to be
k
-colourable (but a
k
-colouring is not given), the goal is to
find
a
\ell
-colouring. While it is generally believed that this problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for all constants
3\leq k\leq\ell
, there are only a few unconditional results on this matter. The state of the art is that
the problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for
k\geq 3
and
\ell\leq 2k-1
(see
[
6
]
) and for

k\geq 4
and
\ell\leq\binom{k}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}-1
(see
[
29
]
), while the best known efficient algorithm to colour a 3-colourable
n
-vertex graph requires
O(n^{0.19747})
colours
[
27
]
.
There are also many results about approximate hypergraph colouring (involving several different notions of colouring), see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
10
,
20
,
22
,
24
,
37
,
38
]
.

There are three classical
\mathsf{NP}
-hard CSPs that play a big role in CSP theory
[
30
]
: graph
k
-colouring with
k\geq 3
and (the monotone versions of) Not-All-Equal (NAE) SAT and 1-in-3 SAT. The last two problems can also be seen as colouring problems on 3-uniform hypergraph using 2 colours: respectively, requiring that no hyperedge is left monochromatic in the former case and that every hyperedge has exactly one vertex coloured as ‘1’ in the latter case. Interestingly, the state-of-the-art for these three problems in the PCSP framework is very different.

Let us first describe in more detail the sort of problems we study in the present paper - formal definitions will be given in Section
2
.
Given a satisfiable instance
I
of 1-in-3 SAT, it is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to find a satisfying assignment for it.
We can try to relax the constraints - for example, by expanding the domain of values for the variables beyond
\{0,1\}
-
in order to ease the task of finding a satisfying assignment for
I
subject to the relaxed constraints.
More precisely, we fix a finite set
A
and a ternary relation
R
on
A
, such that
\{0,1\}\subseteq A
and
R
contains the relation/predicate of 1-in-3 SAT; that is,
\{(0,0,1),(0,1,0),(1,0,0)\}
. Given a satisfiable instance
I
of 1-in-3 SAT, the goal is to find an assignment of elements from
A
to the variables of
I
so that every constraint in
I
is satisfied in
R
. Put otherwise, we aim to find an ‘
R
-solution’ to
I
, which can be seen as an ‘
R
-approximation to a 1-in-3 solution’.
The complexity of this task depends on
R
, and the question to find the exact dependence was first asked in
[
6
]
. As observed in
[
5
]
, one can assume without loss of generality that
R
is symmetric; that is, invariant under all permutations of coordinates.
By analogy with approximate graph colouring, we call this family of problems
approximate 1-in-3 SAT
. Approximate NAE SAT is defined in a similar way, and it has actually been studied under the name of ‘approximate hypergraph colouring’
[
20
]
before the introduction of the general PCSP framework. Approximate graph colouring can also be slightly expanded
by using an arbitrary symmetric binary relation
R
(containing the
k
-colouring relation) in place of the
\ell
-colouring relation.

Let us return to describing the status of the PCSPs approximating the three classical
\mathsf{NP}
-hard CSPs.
We call an
R
-approximation trivial if
R
contains a loop, i.e. a constant tuple. Obviously, trivial
R
-approximations are easy. For graph colouring, all non-trivial approximations are widely believed to be
\mathsf{NP}
-hard and this is known to hold assuming various UG-like conjectures
[
13
,
19
,
23
]
. Without any such assumptions, this is known to hold only for a small number of cases discussed above. In contrast, all non-trivial approximations for NAE SAT have been proven to be
\mathsf{NP}
-hard
[
20
,
37
]
. As for 1-in-3 SAT, there exist non-trivial approximations that are polynomial-time solvable. In fact, there is a folklore dichotomy conjecture (that we discuss in detail later) predicting which
R
-approximations are
polynomial-time solvable and which are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard, and there are partial results confirming it in some special cases
[
5
,
11
,
17
]
.

The main method in classifying the complexity of CSPs and PCSPs has been the algebraic approach, based on the notion of
polymorphisms
, which are multivariable functions capturing invariant properties of the relations/predicates used to specify constraints (see surveys
[
8
,
28
]
).
It is known that (certain abstract properties of) polymorphisms determine the complexity of CSPs and PCSPs
[
6
,
9
,
11
]
. For CSPs, the presence of non-trivial (in a specific sense) polymorphisms implies tractability, while the lack thereof implies
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness
[
14
,
15
,
40
]
. In the PCSP realm, the situation is more complex as there are many known
\mathsf{NP}
-hard PCSPs with non-trivial (but limited in some sense) polymorphisms. As opposed to CSPs, it is not clear at all why general PCSPs should exhibit a
\mathsf{P}
vs.
\mathsf{NP}
-hard dichotomy. However, dichotomy is likely at least in some special cases.

In general, analysing the polymorphisms of a CSP or a PCSP is a highly non-trivial task. A rule of thumb is that problems with ‘rich enough’ polymorphisms are tractable (i.e. polynomial-time solvable), whereas problems with ‘limited enough’ polymorphisms are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. For PCSPs (as opposed to CSPs), it is not clear yet what exactly ‘rich enough’ and ‘limited enough’ mean or whether there even is a gap between them (which would indicate a non-dichotomy). See
[
6
]
or survey
[
28
]
for further discussion of the general picture of PCSP research.

Unlike work towards the CSP dichotomy, where the right
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness condition was identified early in the process
[
15
]
, polymorphism-based methods for proving
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness of PCSPs are still very much in development
[
6
,
4
,
7
,
12
,
18
]
. Such methods typically involve identifying small special sets of variables in the polymorphisms.
It was discovered recently that ideas from topological combinatorics
[
34
]

can be very useful in identifying these small sets. Such results have so far come in two distinct flavours.
One flavour uses reasoning with colourings of Kneser graphs or similar
(based on variants of the Borsuk-Ulam theorem) in order to
find a small set of variables in polymorphisms such that setting these variables to a particular value restricts the output of the function regardless of how the remaining variables are set (see
[
1
,
6
,
37
]
). The other tries to show that each polymorphism is similar up certain changes (described in terms of homotopy transformations) to one with a small set of special variables, (see
[
3
,
22
,
29
,
35
]
). Curiously, the behaviours of polymorphisms underlying applications of the two topological flavours have never been observed in the same PCSP - until now.

1.1
Related work

Classifying the complexity of PCSPs is a very active area of research (see, e.g. survey
[
28
]
). We will give a brief overview that focus solely on the results that are closely related to approximating 1-in-3 SAT and to the topological method in PCSP.

Approximating 1-in-3 SAT by NAE was the first known tractable non-trivial approximation of a
\mathsf{NP}
-hard CSP by another
[
11
]
. More specifically, one algorithm works as follows: given a satisfiable instance of 1-in-3 SAT, one relaxes each constraint in it (say on variables
x,y,z
) to a linear equation
x+y+z=1
over the integers. One then solves the obtained linear system over integers (which can be done in polynomial time
[
26
]
), and rounds the obtained solution by mapping positive integers to 1 and non-positive ones to 0.
It is easy to see that this rounding sends any triple of integers such that
x+y+z=1
to a triple of Boolean values that cannot be constant, thus providing a NAE solution for the original instance.
This polynomial-time algorithm is a special case of the basic AIP (affine integer programming) algorithm,
whose applicability for PCSPs was characterised in
[
6
]
. There is now a folklore conjecture (see Conjecture 5 in
[
39
]
) predicting that
every approximation of 1-in-3 SAT either can be solved by the above algorithm with appropriately chosen rounding, or it remains
\mathsf{NP}
-hard (see Section
2.1
for a formal statement). This conjecture was confirmed in
[
17
]
for approximations by so-called rainbow-free relations (in which no tuple has three pairwise different elements).
The conjecture was also confirmed in
[
5
]
for relations on the set
\{0,1,2\}
, with a single exception that the authors were not able to classify.

This unclassified case from
[
5
]
is related to a variant of the colouring problem for hypergraphs called
linearly ordered (LO) colouring

[
5
,
38
]
(also known as the
unique maximum colouring

[
16
]
). This variant assumes that the set of colours is linearly ordered, e.g. equal to
\left\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\right\}
. A LO
k
-colouring of a hypergraph is an assignment of the colours to the vertices of a hypergraph so that, for each hyperedge
e
, if colour
c
is the largest colour that appears in it, then
c
is assigned to exactly one vertex in
e
.
For example, in an LO 3-colouring, for a hyperedge
(x,y,z)
, the colours
(0,1,2)
or
(0,2,0)
would be acceptable, but not
(0,1,1)
.
Observe that the LO 2-colouring problem for 3-uniform hypergraph is precisely 1-in-3-SAT.
Analogously to the approximate graph colouring problem, the
approximate LO hypergraph colouring problem
is defined
[
5
]
as follows, for fixed constants
2\leq k\leq\ell
:

Given a 3-uniform hypergraph that is promised to have a LO
k
-colouring, find a LO
\ell
-colouring for it.

It was conjectured in
[
5
]
that this problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for all
constants
2\leq k\leq\ell
. The case
k=2,\ell=3
of this problem was exactly the only unclassified approximation of 1-in-3 SAT by a relation
on
\{0,1,2\}
. Up to now, the above conjecture was confirmed only in the following cases. It was observed
[
22
]
that, for
4\leq k\leq\ell
, there is a very easy reduction to the approximate LO colouring problems with constants
k,\ell
from the approximate graph colouring problem with constants
k-1,\ell-1
. Hence, any known results
about approximate graph colouring translate in this way to results about this conjecture. Apart from this, the only confirmed
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness case for approximate LO colouring is for
k=3,\ell=4

[
22
]
, with a proof based on topological
combinatorics. LO hypergraph colouring can naturally be defined not only for 3-uniform hypergraphs, but for
r
-uniform hypergraph with
r\geq 3
. The approximate LO colouring problem is known to be
\mathsf{NP}
-hard for all
3\leq k\leq\ell
and
r\geq 4
and for
k=2
and all
r\geq\ell+2
(see
[
37
,
38
]
).

The problem of finding a LO
3
-colouring for a given LO
2
-colourable 3-uniform hypergarphs (which is the same as the problem of approximating 1-in-3-SAT by LO 3-colourings)
was specifically mentioned in
[
5
,
17
,
22
,
38
,
37
]
as an open question and an obstacle for further progress in classifying the approximate 1-in-3 SAT problems.

1.2
Our contributions

We denote the problem of finding a LO
3
-colouring for a given LO
2
-colourable
3
-uniform hypergraph by
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
- the notation will be explained in Section
2.1
.
In this paper, our main result settles its complexity.

Theorem 1.1
.

\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

We remark that the confirmed case
k=3,\ell=4
of the LO colouring conjecture that we mentioned above easily follows from Theorem
1.1
(see Section
3.4
), thus providing a more elementary proof of the main result in
[
22
]
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is a combination of a structural result (Theorem
3.11
) followed by a direct application of a well-known sufficient condition for
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness of a PCSP
[
7
,
12
]
. Theorem
3.11
states, roughly, that the polymorphisms of
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
exhibit two kinds of behaviour - precisely the two kinds that are characteristic for the two flavours of
applications of topology in PCSPs that we described above. Even though our proof is combinatorial, it shows that our problem is the first example where the features behind the two uses of topology in PCSP appear together. Thus, it is likely to be very useful in guiding further development of the topological method in Promise CSP. It is important to note that whilst some of the applications of topology lead to alternative proofs of known results, e.g.
[
35
,
37
]
, or to a modest step forward in comparison with existing results, e.g.
[
3
,
22
]
, their methodological contribution is highly meaningful. The classification of PCSPs is still in its infancy and requires a far richer toolbox than for CSPs. The development of new methods or the identification of issues that said methods must be able to deal with are highly sought after by current research directions
[
28
]
.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper we use the convention
\left[n\right]=\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}.
The complement of a set
X
, usually with respect to
\left[n\right]
, is denoted by
\overline{X}
.

2.1
Promise CSPs

A
relational structure
is a tuple
\mathbf{A}=\left(A;R_{1},\ldots,R_{m}\right)
where
A
is a set referred to as the
domain
of
\mathbf{A}
, and each
R_{i}
is a relation on
A
of arity
\mathrm{ar}_{i}\geq 1
; that is,
R_{i}\subseteq A^{\mathrm{ar}_{i}}
. In this paper we will only consider structures with a single symmetric binary relation (undirected graphs)
or with a single ternary symmetric relation (i.e. invariant under permutations of coordinates), which we refer to as
3
-uniform hypergraphs
by slightly abusing standard terminology.

Relational structures
\mathbf{A}=\left(A;R_{1},\ldots,R_{m}\right)
and
\mathbf{B}=\left(B;R^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,R^{\prime}_{m^{\prime}}\right)
are said to be
similar
if
m=m^{\prime}
and the relations
R_{i}
and
R^{\prime}_{i}
have same arity for each
i\in\left[m\right].
For similar relational structures
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
as above, a mapping
f:A\rightarrow B
is said to be a
homomorphism
if for all
i\in\left[m\right]
we have
x\in R_{i}\implies f\left(x\right)\in R^{\prime}_{i}
, where
f
is applied component-wise to
x
. We write
f:\mathbf{A}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
to indicate that
f
is a homomorphism, and use the notation
\mathbf{A}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
to indicate that a homomorphism from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
exists.

Definition 2.1
.

A
PCSP template
is a pair
\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
where
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
are similar relational structures such that
\mathbf{A}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
.

Every PCSP template
\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
defines a decision problem and a search problem as follows. The
decision version
of
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
asks to decide, for a given input structure
\mathbf{X}
similar to
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, whether
\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}
or whether
\mathbf{X}\not\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
, the promise being that exactly one of these cases occurs. The
search version
of
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
asks, given an input structure
\mathbf{X}
with a promise that
\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}
, to find a homomorphism
f:\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
. The decision version trivially reduces to the search version, but it is not known whether the two versions are always polynomial-time equivalent (see
[
31
]
for recent results on this question). Observe that if
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}
, then
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
is the same as the standard
\mathrm{CSP}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)
; that is, the problem of deciding whether
\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}
. The PCSP framework allows a systematic study of problems that cannot be expressed as CSPs, and can be thought of as ‘qualitative’ approximations of CSPs. Below are some examples of PCSPs relevant for this paper - more can be found in
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
10
,
20
,
22
,
24
,
28
,
37
,
38
]
.

Example 2.1

(Approximate graph colouring)
.

The problem asks to find, for fixed
\ell\geq k\geq 2
, an
\ell
-colouring of an input
k
-colourable graph. This problem is
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{K}_{k},\mathbf{K}_{\ell}\right)
, where
\mathbf{K}_{n}
is the
n
-clique; that is,
\mathbf{K}_{n}=\left(\left[n\right];\neq\right).

Example 2.2

(Approximate hypergraph colouring)
.

This problem is a natural generalisation of the above, by replacing the inequality
\neq
with the ‘not-all-equal’ relation:

\mathrm{NAE}_{k}=\left\{0,\ldots,k-1\right\}^{3}\backslash\left\{\left(a,a,a\right)\mid a\in\left\{0,\ldots,k-1\right\}\right\}.

A
k
-colouring of a hypergraph, is an assignment of the colours
\left\{0,\ldots,k-1\right\}
to the vertices such that no hyperedge is monochromatic. Hence, the problem of finding, for fixed
\ell\geq k\geq 2
, an
\ell
-colouring of a
k
-colourable
3
-uniform hypergraph is
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{H}_{k},\mathbf{H}_{\ell}\right)
where
\mathbf{H}_{n}=\left(\left\{0,\ldots,n-1\right\};\mathrm{NAE}_{n}\right)
.
Note that the classical
\mathsf{NP}
-hard problem NAE-SAT can be expressed as
\mathrm{CSP}\left(\mathbf{H}_{2}\right)
.

Example 2.3

(Approximate linearly ordered (LO) hypergraph colouring)
.

This is stricter version of colouring than the one from the previous example, the requirement being
that in each hyperedge, the maximal colour assigned to its vertices occurs exactly once.
Formally we replace
\mathrm{NAE}_{k}
with its subrelation

\mathrm{LO}_{k}=\{(a,b,c)\in\left\{0,\ldots,k-1\right\}^{3}\mid(a,b,c)\mbox{ has a unique maximum}\}.

For example,
\mathrm{LO}_{2}=\left\{\left(0,0,1\right),\left(0,1,0\right),\left(1,0,0\right)\right\}
, while
\mathrm{LO}_{3}
contains exactly the triples
\left(0,1,2\right)
,
\left(1,1,2\right)
,
\left(0,0,1\right)
,
\left(0,0,2\right)
and all permutations thereof, but not
\left(1,2,2\right)
or
\left(0,1,1\right).
Let
\mathbf{LO}_{k}=(\left\{0,\ldots,k-1\right\};\mathrm{LO}_{k})
.
Then
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{k},\mathbf{LO}_{\ell}\right)
is the Approximate LO hypergraph colouring problem that was discussed in detail in the previous section.

Example 2.4

(Approximate 1-in-3 SAT)
.

The problem
\mathrm{CSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2}\right)
is precisely (monotone) 1-in-3 SAT, so
the class of problems of the form
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{B}\right)
, where the domain of
\mathbf{B}
contains
{
0,1
}
and
\mathbf{B}
has a unique ternary symmetric relation containing
\left\{\left(0,0,1\right),\left(0,1,0\right),\left(1,0,0\right)\right\}
can naturally be called Approximate 1-in-3 SAT. The question of classifying the complexity of problems in this class was first asked in
[
6
]
.

All known tractable cases of
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{B}\right)
can be solved by essentially the same algorithm as the one for
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{H}_{2}\right)
that we described in Section
1.1
. Consider the (infinite) structure
\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbb{Z};\{(x,y,z)\mid x+y+z=1\})
. Trivially, we have
\mathbf{LO}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbf{Z}
by inclusion. Assume that we additionally have
\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
. Then
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{B}\right)
can be solved in polynomial time by reducing it to solving a system of linear equations over
\mathbb{Z}
. Indeed, for an input
\mathbf{X}
, if
\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{LO}_{2}
then
\mathbf{X}\rightarrow\mathbf{Z}
. Finding a homomorphism from
\mathbf{X}
to
\mathbf{Z}
amounts to solving a system of linear equations over integers (which can done in polynomial time
[
26
]
). By composing the found homomorphism from
\mathbf{X}
to
\mathbf{Z}
with the assumed homomorphism from
\mathbf{Z}
to
\mathbf{B}
, we can find the required homomorphism from
\mathbf{X}
to
\mathbf{B}
.
(There is a potential issue that homomorphisms from
\mathbf{Z}
to
\mathbf{B}
may not be computable, but this has not occurred so far).
It has been conjectured independently by several authors (e.g. Conjecture 5 in
[
39
]
) that

\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{B}\right)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard in all other cases.

Conjecture 2.2

(The approximate 1-in-3 SAT conjecture)
.

For each
\mathbf{B}
, either
\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
, in which case
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{B}\right)
is solvable in polynomial time, or else
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{B}\right)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

As explained above, this conjecture has been confirmed in several special cases
[
5
,
17
]
. It is easy to verify that
\mathbf{Z}\not\rightarrow\mathbf{LO}_{k}
for any
k\geq 2
, so that, indeed,
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{k}\right)
stands on the hardness side of the approximate 1-in-3 SAT conjecture. The simple reasoning, which can be found, e.g., in (the proof of) Proposition 8 in
[
17
]
and which we now sketch, implies the following necessary condition for
\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
. Let
D(\mathbf{B})
be the digraph whose vertices are the elements of
B
and edges are all pairs
(x,y)
such that
(x,x,y)
is in the relation of
\mathbf{B}
. Assume that there is a homomorphism
\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
. Consider all triples of the form
\left(a,a,1-2a\right)
from
\mathbf{Z}
, and their images in
\mathbf{B}
under the assumed homomorphism. These images show that the digraph
D(\mathbf{B})
must contain a homomorphic image of an infinite directed path, which always contains a directed cycle or a loop, since
B
is finite. It is easy to see that
D(\mathbf{LO}_{k})
is a transitive tournament, so it contains neither a directed cycle nor a loop, and it follows that
\mathbf{Z}\not\rightarrow\mathbf{LO}_{k}
. We remark that the structures
\mathbf{LO}_{k}
are the richest structures
\mathbf{B}
with the property that
D(\mathbf{B})
contains neither a directed cycle nor a loop - it is not hard to see that any (
k

\mathbf{LO}_{k}
.

2.2
Polymorphisms

Polymorphisms are “higher order” homomorphisms that determine the complexity of CSPs and PCSPs. The body of work concerned with the use of polymorphisms for deriving reductions between PCSPs is generally referred to as the algebraic theory of PCSPs, the core of which can be found in
[
6
,
11
]
. In this section we review some basic notions.

Definition 2.3
.

Assume that
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
is a PCSP template.
A
n
-ary
polymorphism
of a template
\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
is a function
f:A^{n}\rightarrow B
such that, for every
i
and for every
\mathrm{ar}_{i}\times n
matrix
M
with entries from
A
, if all columns of
M
belong to the relation
R_{i}
in
\mathbf{A}
, then the column obtained by applying
f
to the rows of
M
belongs to the corresponding relation
R^{\prime}_{i}
in
\mathbf{B}
. If
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{B}
, then we say that
f
is a polymorphism of
\mathbf{A}
.

If
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
is any PCSP template and
\varphi:\mathbf{A}\rightarrow\mathbf{B}
, then it is easy to see that, for all
n\geq 1
and
i\in\left[n\right]
,
all functions of the form
f\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\right)=\varphi(x_{i})
are polymorphisms of

(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
. In the special case when
A\subseteq B
and
\varphi
is the inclusion map, such polymorphisms are called
projections
(also known as dictators).

Example 2.5

(Polymorphisms of
\mathbf{LO}_{2}
)
.

A function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is a polymorphism of
\mathbf{LO}_{2}
if, for every
3\times n
matrix
M
with 0/1 entries such that every column of
M
contains exactly one 1,
the column obtained by applying
f
to the rows of
M
also contains exactly one 1.
It is well-known and not hard to verify directly, that projections are the only polymorphisms of
\mathbf{LO}_{2}
.

Example 2.6

(Polymorphisms of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{H}_{2}\right)
)
.

A function
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is a polymorphism of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{H}_{2}\right)
if, for every
3\times n
matrix
M
with 0/1 entries, if every column of
M
contains exactly one 1, then
the column obtained by applying
f
to the rows of
M
contains both a 0 and a 1. This template has many polymorphisms different from projections.

One example is the
(3n+1)
-ary Boolean function
f^{\prime}
which outputs 1 if and only if at least
n+1
of its arguments are equal to 1. To see that this is a polymorphism, consider a
3\times(3n+1)
matrix
M
such that each column of
M
contains exactly one 1. Since the total number of 1s in
M
is
3n+1
, it is impossible that each row of
M
contains at least
n+1
1s or that each row of
M
contains
n
or fewer 1s.
Therefore
f^{\prime}
applied to the rows of
M
produces a column that contains both a 0 and a 1.

The polymorphisms of a structure
\mathbf{A}
are closed under composition; hence the success of applications of universal algebra theory in the classification of CSPs. This is not the case for PCSPs: indeed, for a general template, composition of polymorphisms is not well defined. Instead, we work with a ‘composition free’ structure where the functions are closed under permuting the variables, identifying them or adding dummy ones. Formally, we refer to these operations as taking a
minor
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
f:A^{n}\rightarrow B
be a function. For a map
\pi:\left[n\right]\rightarrow\left[m\right]
, we say that a function
g:A^{m}\rightarrow B
is a
minor
(or a
\pi
-minor) of

, if

g\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\right)=f\left(a_{\pi\left(1\right)},\ldots,a_{\pi\left(n\right)}\right)

for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in A
.

A set of finitary functions from
A
to
B
that is closed under taking minors is called a (function) minion.

It is easy to see that, for any PCSP template
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
, the set of all polymorphisms of

(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
is a minion. It is denoted by
\mathrm{Pol}\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
.

There are several polymorphism-based sufficient conditions for
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness of PCSPs (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
6
,
7
,
12
,
28
]
). We will use the following one in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. A
chain of minors
in
\mathrm{Pol}\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
is a sequence
\left(f_{1},\pi_{1,2},f_{2},\ldots,f_{l-1},\pi_{l-1,l},f_{l}\right)
where
f_{i}\in\mathrm{Pol}\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
for each

and
f_{i}^{\pi_{i,i+1}}=f_{i+1}
.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
7
,
12
]
)
.

Fix
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
be a PCSP template. Suppose that to each polymorphism
f\in\mathrm{Pol}\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
we assign a subset
I\left(f\right)
of at most
k
of its variables. Suppose further that, for any chain of minors
\left(f_{1},\pi_{1,2},f_{2},\ldots,f_{l-1},\pi_{l-1,l},f_{l}\right)
in
\mathrm{Pol}\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
, there exist
1\leq i<j\leq l
such that
\pi^{-1}_{i,j}\left(I\left(f_{j}\right)\right)\cap I\left(f_{i}\right)\neq\emptyset
where
\pi_{i,j}=\pi_{j-1,j}\circ\ldots\circ\pi_{i,i+1}
. Then
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\right)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

The above theorem was first proved in
[
12
]
by using the PCP theorem and the NP-hardness of (layered) Gap Label Cover problem. It was later shown in
[
7
]
that the
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness in fact follows by a direct and simple “bounded width” reduction from 1-in-3 SAT (or from any
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard CSP).

3
The structure of
\mathrm{Pol}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)

The core of this section is dedicated to the proof of the structural result (Theorem

3.1
0
-sets,
1
-sets,
2
-sets…

We use the notion of an

to describe the action of a polymorphism of

with subsets from

and

is an
i

of

a 2-set of

It is straightforward to check that a function

is a polymorphism of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
if, and only if, for all partitions
\left(X,Y,Z\right)
of
\left[n\right]
into three subsets (some of which may be empty), we have that
\left(f\left(X\right),f\left(Y\right),f\left(Z\right)\right)\in\mathrm{LO}_{3}

We say that a partition
\left(X,Y,Z\right)
of
\left[n\right]
is a

(for

\left\{f\left(X\right),f\left(Y\right),f\left(Z\right)\right\}=\left\{0,1\right\}
. In the language of

\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
are the mappings for which there exists some

X\subseteq\left[n\right]
is a

-set otherwise.

When we use the above terminology (

is clear from the context, we will omit a reference to it. This should cause no confusion.

3.2
Reconfiguration graph of polymorphisms

Let us informally describe our result about the structure of
\mathrm{Pol}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
by means of the so-called
reconfiguration graph
. For
n\geq 1
, consider a graph
G_{n}
whose vertices are the
n
-ary polymorphisms of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
and there is an edge between two polymorphisms if they differ (as functions) in a single position. Our structural result (Theorem
3.11
) claims, intuitively, that
if all polymorphisms with small
2
-sets are removed from
G_{n}
, then the resulting graph is disconnected, and each of its connected components contains a unique
n
-ary projection. For our hardness result, we will need a more precise and technical version of this statement.

The split between polymorphisms with small
2
-set and the rest can be justified from the fact that the absence of a small
2
-set is a sufficient condition for a polymorphism to have a very rigid structure that mimics a projection up to some reconfiguration (indeed, the unique projection in its connected component of
G_{n}
once the polymorphisms with small
2
-sets are removed). This is also guaranteed by the following consequence of Lovász’s result
[
32
]
about the chromatic number of the Kneser graphs (a founding pillar of the area of topological combinatorics).

Lemma 3.1

(Lemma 13 in
[
5
]
)
.

Any polymorphism of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
has a 1-set or a 2-set with at most
3
elements.

3.3
Recolouring and saturation

The
n
-ary polymorphisms of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
can be understood as colourings of the subsets of
\left[n\right]
using the colour set
\left\{0,1,2\right\}
and subject to the constraints enforced by
\mathbf{LO}_{3}
. We say that a set
X\subseteq\left[n\right]
is
recolourable
for a
n
-ary polymorphism

if one can change the image of

to a different value to obtain a mapping that is still a polymorphism of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
. Specifically, we say that
X
is
recolourable to
i
or
i
-
recolourable
for some
i\neq f\left(X\right)
if the mapping

defined as

f^{\prime}\left(Y\right)=\begin{cases}f\left(Y\right)\;\;\textrm{if}\;Y\neq X\\
i\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases}

is a polymorphism of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
. We say that a boolean set is
static
(for
f
) if it is not recolourable to its opposite boolean value. That is, if it is an
i
-set for
i\in\left\{0,1\right\}
and it is not
\left(1-i\right)
-recolourable. The following lemma is straightforward.

Lemma 3.2
.

A boolean set
X
of a
n
-ary polymorphism
f
of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
is static if, and only if, there exist sets

such that
(X,Y,Z)
is a boolean partition of
[n]
.

Recolouring is a central notion to our structural result: what we show is in fact, that polymorphisms of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
without small
2
-sets are essentially projections modulo some recolouring.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
f
be a
n
-ary polymorphism of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
. We say that
f
is a
recoloured projection
if there is some
t\in\left[n\right]
such that for any static boolean set

, it holds that
f\left(S\right)=\left[t\in S\right]
where
\left[t\in S\right]
is the Iverson bracket (which evaluates to
1
if

and to

otherwise). We refer to such

as the
dictating variable
of

.

It is easy to characterise all
2
-recolourable boolean sets. The following is straightforward.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any polymorphism
f
of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
, there are no disjoint
2
-sets. In particular, the empty set is never a
2
-set. Furthermore, a boolean set
X
is
2
-recolourable if, and only if,

has non-empty intersection with every
2
-set of
f
.

The above lemma states that the 2-sets of any polymorphism form an intersecting family
(i.e. a family of pairwise intersecting subsets of a set), which is an object much studied in extremal combinatorics, see, e.g.
[
21
]
.
We say that a
n
-ary polymorphism

is

if for every
2
-set

, we have that

implies

. If

is upwards closed and is such that the complement of every boolean set is a

-set (that is, for every
X\subseteq\left[n\right]
either

or

is a

[

Definition 3.5
.

Let

be

\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
and assume that

is saturated. We say that

is a

of

is obtained from

by recolouring a boolean set to

for

When using the fact that a polymorphism

is saturated in a proof, we may say ‘by saturation’ as a shorthand for ‘because

is saturated’ when

is clear from the context. The following lemma implies that every polymorphism can be recoloured to one that is upwards closed.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f
be a polymorphism of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
. If
X
is a
2
-set and

is a boolean set such that

, then

is

-recolourable.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
,

has non-empty intersection with all

is

-recolourable by Lemma

.
∎

Using a similar argument, we can show that every polymorphism has a saturation, though this may not be unique.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
f
be an upwards closed
n
-ary polymorphism of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
. If

are disjoint non-empty boolean sets for which

, then both

and

are

-recolourable.

Proof.

Since

is upwards closed, there are no subsets of

that are

,

is
2
-recolourable. By symmetry, the same argument applies to

∎

Corollary 3.8
.

Every polymorphism
f
of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
has a saturation. Moreover, if

has arity at least 7 and no small

has a pure saturation.

Proof.

Starting from

If

has arity

and no small 2-set, then, when applying Lemma

∎

Observe that the transformation from

Being able to recolour a polymorphism to a saturated one is a handy trick: this gives more structure and makes analysing recolourings easier without affecting the core of the polymorphism itself.
For saturated polymorphisms, there is a more useful characterisation of static boolean sets. Hereafter, for a saturated polymorphism

-sets by

1

Lemma 3.9

For an

Proof.

Suppose

As mentioned above, polymorphisms may not have a unique saturation - projections being the most immediate example. For polymorphisms with unique pure saturation, we have the following characterisation of the boolean sets that are recoloured throughout a saturation path.

Lemma 3.10

We now state the structural theorem at the core of our main result (Theorem

Theorem 3.11

For a

3.4
Proof of Theorem
1.1
and the hardness of
\mathrm{PCSP}\left(\mathbf{LO}_{3},\mathbf{LO}_{4}\right)
revisited

In the section, we show how Theorem
3.11

and some auxiliary lemmas, implies Theorem
1.1
. In this proof, we will need to distinguish cases depending on whether or not polymorphisms under consideration have a unique pure saturation, and the three technical lemmas below provide useful properties of these cases.

Lemma 3.12

Assume that
f
is a
n
-ary polymorphism of
\left(\mathbf{LO}_{2},\mathbf{LO}_{3}\right)
with

and no small 2-sets. Suppose that

does not have a unique pure saturation and let

cannot have disjoint non-empty boolean sets

such that
f(S)\neq[t\in S]

.

Proof.

Let
g
be a pure saturation of

of

\overline{A}
is a 2-set of

. It is easy to see that one can choose a saturation path from

to

and

and

(S_{0},S_{1}\cup\{a\},S_{2}\backslash\{a\})
is boolean and none of its parts is
\{n\}
, which we already showed to imply
f(S_{0})=[t\in S_{0}]
. The reasoning for the case
S_{2}=\{n\}
is the same.
It remains to consider the case when
S_{0}=\{n\}
. The above reasoning (with an appropriate renaming of the sets

) shows that we have
f(S_{j})=[t\in S_{j}]
for
j=1,2
. Thus
f(\{n\})=[t\in\{n\}]
, as required.
∎

3.30
imply Theorem
3.11
.
