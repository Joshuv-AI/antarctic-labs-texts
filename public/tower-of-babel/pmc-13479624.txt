Assessment of macrolide resistance in the respiratory tract of preterm-born infants during treatment with azithromycin in the AZTEC clinical trial
Authors: Ali F Aboklaish, W John Watkins, David Gallacher, Claudia Consoli, John Lowe, Julian R Marchesi, Sailesh Kotecha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOur multicentre, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled Azithromycin therapy for prevention of chronic lung disease of prematurity trial assessed if early 10-day azithromycin treatment improved survival without development of chronic lung disease of prematurity when compared with placebo in 796 preterm-born infants. Since antibiotic resistance remains a significant global health priority, we had preplanned macrolide-resistance assessment in recruited infants. We, therefore, identified baseline macrolide-resistant genes in respiratory samples and determined if this was altered by azithromycin treatment. Furthermore, we assessed if macrolide-resistant bacteria isolated from respiratory samples were also resistant to other common antibiotic classes.MethodsSix common macrolide-resistant genes: erm(A), erm(B), erm(C), erm(F), mef(A/E) and msr(A) were identified by quantitative PCR (qPCR) from serial nasopharyngeal and endotracheal aspirates from recruited infants at baseline (pretreatment), day-5, day-10 and day-14 (post treatment). Azithromycin-resistant bacteria were assessed by culture in presence/absence of azithromycin and underlying resistance mechanisms were confirmed by qPCR. Resistance to other common antibiotics was also evaluated and molecular determinants were identified by whole genome sequencing.ResultsFrom 1108 (n=541 azithromycin, n=567 placebo) respiratory aspirates from 348 preterm infants, the overall prevalence of macrolide-resistant genes was similar in the placebo (63.7%) and azithromycin (63.9%) groups, with only erm(C) gene increased by azithromycin. Azithromycin-resistant bacteria were resistant to multiple clinically used antibiotics, being associated with several different underlying resistance mechanisms. Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) were the most recovered macrolide-resistant bacteria.ConclusionsMacrolide-resistant genes were noticeably prevalent in the placebo group, with minimal increase with azithromycin treatment, suggesting that, regardless of the additional use of azithromycin, judicious use of antibiotics is required in preterm-born infants.Keywords: Infant, Newborn; Respiratory Infection; Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive

Introduction

Azithromycin, which belongs to the macrolide class of antibiotics, has the potential to prevent development of chronic lung disease of prematurity (CLD), also known as bronchopulmonary dysplasia in preterm-born infants, due to its antimicrobial properties and potent anti-inflammatory properties.1–3 CLD is associated with significant mortality and morbidity including respiratory disorders in childhood/adulthood.4 5 Despite advancements in neonatal care, rates of CLD remain high, particularly in infants born before 30 weeks’ gestation.6 Both pulmonary inflammation and respiratory colonisation with Ureaplasma spp are associated with the development of CLD.7 Our Azithromycin therapy for prevention of chronic lung disease of prematurity (AZTEC) trial recruited 796 preterm-born infants of <30 weeks’ gestation to determine if intravenous treatment with azithromycin (20 mg/kg/day for 3 days, followed by 10 mg/kg for 7 days) or to placebo could improve survival without development of CLD.8 9 However, the widespread use of azithromycin, particularly in preterm-born infants, raises concerns regarding the development of antimicrobial resistance.10 Antimicrobial resistance not only compromises the therapeutic effectiveness of azithromycin, but also poses wider threats via development of multidrug-resistant bacteria.11 Given global concerns of emerging antibiotic resistance and given the association of increased antibiotic use in preterm-born infants with significantly increased morbidity and mortality,12–14 we had preplanned the assessment of macrolide resistance in the AZTEC trial in serially collected respiratory samples.

Specifically, we determined baseline macrolide resistance by identifying macrolide-resistant genes in the placebo group over the first 2 weeks of life and then assessed if azithromycin increased baseline prevalence of macrolide-resistant genes. Furthermore, we determined if azithromycin-resistant bacteria were also resistant to multiple antibiotics (routinely used in neonatal practice) and identified the underlying mechanisms.

Methods

Clinical samples

Respiratory specimens (endotracheal aspirates, ETAs, if tracheally intubated, and nasopharyngeal aspirates, NPAs, if not tracheally intubated) were collected from all preterm-born infants recruited to the AZTEC clinical trial from 28 tertiary neonatal intensive care units in the UK. Samples were collected prior to starting treatment with blinded azithromycin or placebo (day 1, baseline) and post treatment at days 5, 10 and 14–21 (designated day 14) post randomisation as previously described8 and processed as described in the online supplement.

Detection of macrolide-resistant genes

Macrolide-resistant genes in DNA quantified and extracted from clinical samples and from bacteria isolated from respiratory samples were detected using a published multiplex quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay as described in the online supplement15 16 with primers and probes as presented in online supplemental table 1. A cut-off value of 100 copies per μL was used as a threshold for detecting macrolide-resistant genes in DNA samples. Six commonly reported macrolide-resistant genes which explain >90% of macrolide resistance were sought: four erythromycin ribosomal methyltransferase genes, erm(A), erm(B), erm(C) and erm(F), and two efflux pump genes, mef(A/E) and msr(A).

Culture and identification of azithromycin-resistant bacteria

A total of 150 samples, from 50 randomly selected infants, who had at least three serial ETA or NPA samples (baseline, day 5, days 10–14), were screened for presence of azithromycin-resistant bacteria by culture and subsequently underlying macrolide-resistant genes were identified by qPCR for each resistant isolate as described in the online supplement. Aerobic and facultative anaerobic microorganisms, including fastidious organisms but not anaerobes, were the target of the initial screening for any bacterial growth from the clinical respiratory samples before using the azithromycin-selective agars. Samples were cultured on both blood and chocolate agars which support the growth of many respiratory pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococus. aureus, Staphylococcus. pyogenes, coagulase-negative Staphylococci spp (CoNS), Enterobacteriaceae (eg, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa), Haemophilus influenzae and Neisseria species.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing and multi-resistance determination

Minimum inhibitory concentrations for clinically relevant antibiotics including azithromycin, vancomycin, cefotaxime, clindamycin, flucloxacillin, doxycycline, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin and ampicillin against azithromycin-resistant isolates were determined by agar microdilution as described in the online supplement and according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (CLSI).17 Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was used to identify known antimicrobial resistance genes among azithromycin-resistant isolates and linked these with susceptibility profile as described in the online supplement.

Data analyses

The data are given as descriptive statistics. Continuous data were compared using independent t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni correction or by χ2 for categorical data. Data were analysed using SPSS V.27.0 (IBM, Chicago, Illinois, USA).

Results

Study population

We had aimed to include the first 350 recruited infants, but 348 were included as this coincided with paused recruitment due to the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic in March 2020. Subsequently, these were shown to include 170 (48.9%) and 178 (51.2%) infants from the azithromycin and placebo groups, respectively. Participant demographics of the two groups reflected the original trial group (table 1). From these infants, 85.7% and 87.9% infants from azithromycin and placebo arms, respectively, were on antibiotics on day of birth and day one of life, with 28.2% still receiving antibiotics at day 14 (online supplemental figure 1 and table 2) for the list of antimicrobials. The infants were generally fed by intravenous parenteral nutrition or enterally by either maternal or donor breast milk.

Macrolide-resistant genes in respiratory samples from the placebo group to determine evolution of baseline macrolide resistance

In total 1108 respiratory (ETA 490 and NPA 618) samples were analysed, comprising 567 (51.2%) from the placebo group and 541 (48.8%) from the azithromycin group. Macrolide resistance genes were detected in 361/567 (63.7%) respiratory samples from the placebo group. At least one macrolide resistance gene was detected in these positive samples, with several samples having more than one gene detected. In the placebo group, the presence of all macrolide resistance genes was low at baseline day 1. However, the presence of erm(A), erm(B), erm(C) and msr(A) increased significantly over the first 2 weeks of life (figure 1). Both erm(F) and mef(A/E) remained low over this period. The presence/absence of the macrolide genes did not appear to be influenced by total DNA available. No associations were noted between the presence of positive antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes and early life factors such as mode of delivery, sex or development of CLD (online supplemental table 3a).

Detection of macrolide-resistance genes in respiratory samples from preterm-born infants before, during and after azithromycin or placebo treatment. A significant increase in erm(C) gene was observed in the azithromycin group when compared with the placebo group at day 5 (p=0.017), day 10 (p<0.001) and day 14 (p<0.001). No differences were noted for the other macrolide-resistance genes between the two treatment groups. erm(A), erm(B), erm(C) and erm(F), erythromycin ribosomal methyltransferase genes; mef(A/E) and msr(A), efflux pump genes.

Effect of azithromycin on macrolide-resistance genes in respiratory samples in the treated group

From the 541 samples in the azithromycin group, a similar proportion of 346 (63.9%) to the placebo group (63.7%) were positive for macrolide-resistance genes, suggesting that the use of azithromycin, in addition to routine use of antibiotics in this vulnerable population, did not result in additional macrolide resistance. Like the placebo group, presence of erm(A), erm(B), erm(C) and msr(A) increased over the first 2 weeks, but not erm(F) and mef(A/E), was also seen in the azithromycin group (figure 1). No differences were found between the azithromycin and placebo groups for any of the genes except for erm(C) (figure 1). In both groups, erm(C) increased over time, but erm(C) was greater at days 5, 10 and 14, but not at baseline, in the azithromycin group when compared with the placebo group. The presence/absence of the macrolide genes did not appear to be influenced by total DNA available. As with the placebo group, no associations were noted between presence of macrolide-resistant genes and early life factors including mode of delivery (online supplemental table 3b).

Since the ETA and NPA samples may reflect different environments, we separately analysed these samples. Marginally higher gene positive detection was noted in NPA samples than in ETA samples (which is likely to reflect greater colonisation of the upper airways than the lower airways), but the overall trends and differences were similar to each other and to the combined samples (online supplemental figure 2a and b).

Determination of multidrug resistance

Detection of azithromycin-resistant microbes

When the 150 respiratory aspirate samples from 50 randomly selected infants were inoculated onto non-selective media, growth was detected in 68/150 (45.3%) of the samples, including 38 (55.9%) from the azithromycin group and 30 (44.1%) from the placebo group (p=0.170). From these 68 samples, 52 (76.5%) samples showed growth on azithromycin-supplemented selective media, 34/52 (65.4%) and 18/52 (34.6%) samples from the azithromycin and placebo groups, respectively (p=0.002). We isolated 79 single colonies grown on the azithromycin-selective media, with 77 colonies being identified as bacteria (48 (62.3%) and 29 (37.7%) from the azithromycin and placebo groups, respectively (p=0.0025)) and two colonies as fungi. Six of the azithromycin-resistant isolates were from day 1, 33 from day 5 and 38 from day 14 time points. The distribution of bacterial species in total, and in the azithromycin and placebo groups, is presented in table 2. The two fungal colonies, both from the placebo group, were identified as Candida albicans.

Detection of macrolide-resistant genes in azithromycin-resistant bacteria

Of the azithromycin-resistant bacterial isolates, 75/77 (97.4%) carried at least one macrolide-resistant gene (1–2 genes per isolate): erm(C) 44 (57.1%), msr(A) 35 (45.5%), erm(B) 7 (9.1%) and erm(A) 4 (5.2%). None of the isolates harboured erm(F) or mef(A/E) genes.

Antimicrobial resistance patterns of azithromycin-resistant bacteria

From the 77 azithromycin-resistant isolates, 68 (88.3%) were staphylococcal which were resistant to ampicillin 68/68 (100%), flucloxacillin 67/68 (98.5%), gentamicin 62/68 (91.2%), ciprofloxacin 44/68 (64.7%), clindamycin 31/68 (45.6%), cefotaxime 31/68 (45.6%) and doxycycline 13/68 (19.1%), but only one isolate was resistant to vancomycin 1/68 (1.1%) (figure 2). All six isolates from Enterococcus spp (100%) were resistant to clindamycin, flucloxacillin, cefotaxime and doxycycline, and 4/6 (66.7%) were resistant to doxycycline. However, most of them were sensitive to ampicillin 5/6 (83.3%) and vancomycin 6/6 (100%). A summary of the antimicrobial resistance patterns of all azithromycin-resistant isolates is presented in online supplemental table 4. WGS results showed that, besides the macrolide-resistant genes, azithromycin-resistant isolates also carried several antimicrobial genetic determinants, which confer resistance to a range of other classes of antibiotics. The list of all resistance genes identified by WGS is presented in online supplemental table 5. Examples of genes that potentially confer resistance and are linked with susceptibility profiles of antibiotics tested in this study included blaZ, blaPC1 and blaSHV-187 (for beta-lactams); aac(6’)-Ie-aph(2’')-Ia, aph(3’)-IIIa and ant(4’)-Ib (for aminoglycosides); qacA, oqxA and oqxB (for fluoroquinolones); erm(A), erm(B), erm(C), msr(A) and mph(C) (for macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin B); and tet(K), tet(L), tet(M) (for tetracyclines). Moreover, many of these isolates harboured genes that encode for multidrug efflux pumps such as KpnH, KpnE, KpnF, emeA and acrB, which confer resistance to several antibiotics simultaneously including aminoglycoside, carbapenem, cephalosporin, fluoroquinolone, macrolide, beta-lactam, peptide, rifamycin and tetracycline.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the prevalence of macrolide-resistant genes in infants recruited to AZTEC receiving either placebo or azithromycin treatment.8 9 Clearly, there are a number of complex interactions occurring in the preterm group, including the presence of baseline macrolide resistance in the routine situation in which antibiotics are extensively used in this vulnerable population, how the addition of azithromycin to routine use of antibiotics affects baseline macrolide resistance and, finally, if bacteria associated with macrolide genes are also multiantibiotic resistant. We have, therefore, dissected these complex interactions and noted several important findings. In the preterm-born infants receiving placebo, thus representing the ‘usual’ situation, the prevalence of macrolide-resistant genes was 63.7% despite the infants not receiving macrolides during the neonatal period. This overall prevalence was very similar in the azithromycin-treated group (63.9%), with only erm(C) being higher at days 5–14 in the treated group when compared with the placebo group, suggesting that the addition of azithromycin to routinely used antibiotics did not affect the overall prevalence of macrolide-resistant genes. These genes increased similarly in both groups during the 2-week study period. Most importantly, the macrolide-resistant bacteria identified in both the treatment arms were multiresistant to a range of clinically used antibiotics except for vancomycin (commonly used in neonatal practice).

In the usual situation, preterm-born infants with respiratory disease immediately after birth (inclusion criteria for enrolment into the AZTEC study8 9), the most common initially used antibiotics for respiratory disease in preterm-born infants in the UK are a combination of penicillin and gentamicin. Thereafter, the choice of antibiotics is guided by microbiological culture results and/or by the infant’s clinical status. In this study, 87.9% of infants were on antibiotics on day 1, with 28.2% still receiving antibiotics at day 14. In AZTEC, the infants were randomised within 72 hours of birth to receive either intravenous placebo or azithromycin at 20 mg/kg for 3 days (targeting Ureaplasma spp) followed by 7 days at 10 mg/kg (targeting pulmonary inflammation). In both groups, regardless of treatment allocation, the overall macrolide gene prevalence was similar (azithromycin: 63.9%; placebo: 63.7%), with erm(A), erm(B), erm(C) and msr(A) increasing with time, but not for erm(F) or mef(A/E). These increases were not related to mode of delivery (increased staphylococcal colonisation is associated with caesarean section) or to other early life factors including pre-labour rupture of membranes, and thus are most likely to be related to antibiotics use during the first 2 weeks of life regardless of treatment allocation. In a detailed study of 50 infants, msr(A), which encodes efflux pumps, was noted largely in S. epidermidis and S. haemolyticus, but was also noted in Enterococcus, Enterobacter and Klebsiella species (figure 3).

Another important finding was that the azithromycin-resistant bacteria isolated from the 150 serial samples from 50 randomised infants were also resistant to several classes of clinically used antibiotics. These included infants in the placebo group in whom selection of azithromycin-resistant bacteria was also resistant to a wide range of antibiotics. Since we were not able to show any significant effects of azithromycin use except for increasing expression of erm(C) nor of any early life events, the most likely source of these resistant bacteria is from the routine use of antibiotics, or natural early microbial colonisation of the respiratory tract in this population. The clinical importance of our data is that in the routine management of preterm born infants receiving neonatal intensive care, regardless of the use of azithromycin, emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria, especially Staphylococcus spp, is common and should be urgently addressed. Clearly, antibiotics are necessary in preterm-born infants, especially when there is clear evidence of infection, to which these infants are susceptible, but it will be equally important to thoroughly scrutinise early stopping of antibiotics whenever possible.

The erm(C) gene was particularly predominant in the commonly recovered coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) species, S. epidermidis and S. haemolyticus. This observation aligns with previous studies that reported erm(C) as a primary gene reported in this species conferring resistance not only to macrolides, but also to lincosamides and streptogramin B class of antibiotics (MLSB phenotype).18 In our study, this gene was the only gene that was significantly increased in the azithromycin group when compared with the placebo group. In this case, azithromycin might have exerted a selective pressure that favoured the survival and proliferation of these species. Moreover, the majority of CoNS carried the msr(A) gene that encodes for an efflux pump conferring resistance to both macrolides and streptogramin B (MSB phenotype).19 A combination of several other macrolide-resistant genes was also found, including erm(A), erm(B), erm(F), mef(A/E) (macrolide efflux pump) and mph(C) (a phosphotransferase inactivating macrolides). Although lower in presence than the erm(C) and msr(A) genes, these genes coexisted in several single strains. The lower detection of these genes may in part be attributed to the constrained recovery rate of their respective bacterial hosts. For example, the erm(B) gene is frequently found in Enterococcus species and streptococci species, while the erm(F) gene is predominantly found in Bacteroides species and other anaerobic bacteria.20 These species were found in low numbers or not detected in our study. Several other potentially pathogenic bacteria, however, were identified, including S. aureus, K. pneumoniae and E. hormaechi, which were all resistant to multiple antibiotics. Importantly, almost all azithromycin-resistant isolates identified in this study exhibited multidrug resistance, presenting a complex challenge for effective antibiotic treatment. To our knowledge, there are no previous reported data on prevalence of macrolide-resistant genes in the respiratory tract of preterm born infants. However, the study by Gallacher et al recently reported that the presence of these genes in stool samples from preterm born infants is 91%, with erm(C) being the most detected gene (~50%) followed by msr(A) gene (~44%).15 These data are similar to those reported in adults who are generally not on antibiotics.21 22

Selection and emergence of multiantibiotic-resistant CoNS in the respiratory microbiota of preterm-born infants in our study is a significant finding highlighting the adverse effect of the routine widespread use of antibiotics in this vulnerable population. CoNS are commonly found on the skin and mucosal membranes and are usually acquired and dominate the early microbiota of preterm-born infants, notably those who are born by caesarean section.23 CoNS are usually considered harmless commensals; however, previous studies have demonstrated that they are potential opportunistic pathogens causing serious infections in individuals with compromised or immature immune systems.24–27 In preterm-born infants, for example, S. epidermidis has been reported as a primary cause of neonatal sepsis and linked to several other neonatal illnesses such as CLD, white matter injury, necrotising enterocolitis and retinopathy of prematurity.28 29 Furthermore, CoNS have been shown to be potential reservoirs of antibiotic resistance genes that can easily be transferred to other pathogens such as S. aureus via horizontal gene transfer.30

The strengths of this study include the largest group studied so far for antibiotic resistance in both the placebo group to reflect the usual situation and in the treated group. The researchers and participants were blinded to treatment, including during microbiological analyses. The bacterial load, which is known to be low early in life in preterm-born infants, did not affect the findings of the macrolide genes. The weakness is that we did not have a group of infants who had not received any antibiotics, a difficult group to recruit given high use of antibiotics immediately after birth in this population. We also used frozen respiratory samples which may have affected recovery of less tolerant bacteria (fastidious microorganisms). Furthermore, anaerobic bacteria were not sought by culture in this study. We also assessed both NPAs and ETAs which may reflect different respiratory system compartments, but the findings were similar to collated results when they were analysed separately.

Taken together, these data show that, regardless of the use of azithromycin, the overall prevalence of macrolide-resistant genes, which were associated with multiantibiotic resistance, is high at 63.7%, similar to the 63.9% in the azithromycin treated group. Since we were unable to identify any association between early life factors and macrolide-resistant genes, the most likely origins of these multidrug-resistant bacteria were the routine use of antibiotics in this group of infants, since 87.9% were on antibiotics immediately after birth with 28.2% remaining on antibiotics at 14 days of age. The azithromycin-resistant isolates were also resistant to multiple clinically used antibiotics, suggesting that routine use of antibiotics results in an increase and spread of multidrug-resistant bacteria in this vulnerable population. While many of these infants will require antibiotics, the development of care pathways to stop antibiotics at the earliest opportunity, especially in those who do not have any evidence of infection, is prudent.
