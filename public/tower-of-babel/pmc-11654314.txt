Identification of crucial LncRNAs associated with testicular development and LOC108635509 as a potential regulator in black goat spermatogenesis
Authors: Zhiqiang Wang, Yunjia Qi, Nan Xiao, Liu She, Yunchuang Zhang, Junzhi Lu, Qinyang Jiang, Chan Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The establishment and maintenance of spermatogenesis is a complex process involving a vast of regulatory pathways. There is growing evidence revealing that long noncoding RNAs (lncRNA) play important roles in regulating testicular development and spermatogenesis in a stage-specific way. However, our understanding of how lncRNA regulates testicular development and spermatogenesis in black goats is quite limited. In the current study, we screened the transcriptomes (lncRNA and mRNA) of testicular from Guangxi black goats before puberty (3 days old, D3; 30 days old, D30), puberty (90 days old, D90) and postpuberty (180 days old, D180), in order to identify the lncRNA interaction with mRNAs contributes to goat spermatogenesis. The RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis showed that there were 1211, 12,180, 834 differential lncRNAs and 1196, 8838,269 differential mRNAs at the ages of D30 vs. D3, D90 vs. D30, and D180 vs. D90. The lncRNAs showed the most significantly changes from D30 to D90, which indicated that D90 was a key node of lncRNAs participated in the regulation of testicular development and spermatogenesis in black goat. According to functional enrichment analysis of GO and KEGG, we found that differentially expressed lncRNAs (DE lncRNAs) and their target genes regulated spermatogenesis through signal pathways including MAPK, Ras, and PI3K-Akt. Using cis- and trans-acting, 39 DE lncRNAs-targeted genes were found to be enriched for male reproduction. Of these, LOC108635509, which specific expressed in testis and upregulated the expression levels at D90, was found participated in the regulation of testicular development through promoting the proliferation of Sertoli cells (SCs). Overall, this study provides new insight into the regulatory mechanisms that support spermatogenesis and testicular development in black goats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-11094-4.

Background

The continuation of spermatogenesis in male animals is the basis of natural reproduction. Spermatogenesis begin with the differentiation of spermatogonial stem cells to mature sperm production, which is mainly divided into three main functional stages, namely, proliferation, meiosis and maturity [1]. Spermatogenesis is a complex dynamic process with phased characteristics, supported by coordinated gene expressions in germ cells and somatic cells [2]. Specific genes expressed in the testis of different stages play an important role in promoting spermatogenesis by regulating the proliferation and differentiation of germ cells, as well as by regulating the testicular microenvironment. For example, the PPP3CA phosphatase was found influenced the spermatogenesis of the mouse by controlling the expression of dynamic protein 2 (DNM2) in the acrosome of mature spermatozoa [3]. Deletion of PPP3CA, not only impaired testicular growth but also resulted in a decrease in mature sperm, indicating that PPP3CA is crucial for both sperm production and testicular development [4, 5]. Numerous non-coding RNAs, including certain microRNA, PIWIRNA, and LncRNA, have also been found to be an important part of the control of spermatogenesis [6]. Therefore, identifying the important signal pathways and regulators that govern testicular development can help us better understand the molecular mechanism of spermatogenesis and provide valuable insights into developing the potential of male reproduction.

In the past, lncRNA was regarded as “transcriptional noise” and had no biological function. Recently, an amount of lncRNA were found participated in regulating many biological processes, including genome modification, transcriptional activation and interference, as well as organ development [7–9]. More and more evidence suggests that, the number of specific lncRNA in testicular tissue was the highest [10] when compared to other tissues, and some LncRNAs that were significant for testicular development and spermatogenesis have also been identified [11–13]. For instance, it has been found that Lnc32058, Lnc09522 and Lnc98487 affect the progressive motility of human sperm [14]. LncRNA LOC102176306 was found as a sponge of miR-1197-3p in goat testis, which participates in affecting steroid synthesis and Leydig cell proliferation by maintaining the expression of PPARGC1A [15]. Even though more and more lncRNAs were discovered to play a significant role in testicular development and spermatogenesis, the expression pattern and function of the majority of lncRNAs remain unclear, particularly those lncRNAs that affected the spermatogenesis of domestic animals.

The black goat (Capra hircus) is the most important livestock in southwest China because of its ability to adapt to heat and humidity climate and massif geographical conditions. As a local dominant breed, Guangxi black goat has the characteristics of early sexual maturity, and rams can generally achieve sexual maturity at the age of three months old, however, the molecular mechanism governing the initiation of spermatogenesis in this native goat breed is still a mystery. Moreover, a systematic analysis of transcriptome in Guangxi black goat testis during sexual maturation has not yet been reported. Therefore, in the present study, we examined transcriptome expression patterns (lncRNAs and mRNAs) in the testes derived from the black goat at the neonatal (3 days old, D3), prepuberty (30 days old, D30), puberty (90 days old, D90) and postpuberty (180 days old, D180) stage, screened the DE lncRNAs and differential genes related to spermatogenesis by functional enrichment analysis, the co-expression network of DE lncRNAs and their predicted target genes was further constructed to screen out functional genes that may be related to initiation of spermatogenesis. This is the first study to identify DE lncRNAs during the whole periods of testes development (from neonatal to postpuberty stage) in black goat. Our results provide a useful resource for studying the functional roles of these lncRNAs in testicular development and spermatogenesis of black goats.

Materials and methods

Samples collection

The male black goats used in the present study were permitted and provided by Guangxi Green World Moyang Breeding Base, Nanning, China. All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Committee for Animal Ethics of the College of Animal Science and Technology, Guangxi University, Nanning, China (NO.: GXU-2023-0210). All the animals were healthy and fed under the same environmental and nutritional conditions. A total of 12 male black goats with similar genetic backgrounds were selected based on their birth record and separated into four groups: neonatal (D3, n = 3), prepuberty (D30, n = 3), puberty (D90, n = 3) and postpuberty (D180, n = 3) stage. Following the approved guidelines, testes were simultaneously collected using minimally invasive surgical techniques when black goats were under local anesthesia by lignocaine and then the left testicular tissues were obtained and washed with PBS twicely. After removing the albuginea, the middle of testicular tissues were cut into small pieces and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at − 80 ℃.

RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

The total RNA was extracted from the testes using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.The purity of the sample was determined by NanoPhotometer®(IMPLEN, CA, USA); The integrity and concentration of RNA were assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Then 3 µg of total RNA per sample was used as initial material for the RNA sample preparations. Ribosomal RNA was removed using Ribo-off rRNA depletion kit (Human/Mouse/Rat/Other). Subsequently, the sequencing libraries were generated following manufacturer’s instruction with varied index label by VAHTS Total RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for illumina ® (Human/Mouse/Rat/Other). Briefly, RNA fragmentation and short RNA strands were carried out by first strand synthesis reaction buffer under elevated temperature. Then, first cDNA strand was synthesized using random hexamer primers and RNA fragments as template. Second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using buffer, dNTPs, DNA polymerase I and RNase H. The library fragments were purified with QiaQuick PCR kits and elution with EB buffer, then terminal repair 、add A tail and adapter were implemented. In order to select cDNA fragments of preferentially 300 bp in length, the library fragments were purified and the UNG enzyme was used to digest second strand of cDNA. PCR was performed, aimed products were to retrieve, and the library was completed. Finally, sequencing was then performed on the Illumina HiSeq 3000 Platform at ANOROAD (ANOROAD, Beijing, China).

Transcriptome data analysis

After obtaining the original offline sequence (Raw Reads), a series of data processing processes such as removing low-quality sequences(reads which the bases with quality score lower than 19% and bases number over 50% of the total bases were filtered out), removing joint contamination(reads contains more than 5 adapter-polluted bases were regarded as adaptor-polluted reads and are filtered out), and removing rRNA(which mapped with rRNA sequences were filtered out) were performed to obtain the high-quality sequence (Clean Reads). All subsequent analyses are based on Clean Reads. The software HiSAT2(V2.1.0) [17] was used to map the high-quality clean reads to the genome of Capra hircus(RefSeq: GCF_001704415.2) and generated the bam files. Based on the mapping results, the software StringTie(V2.1.1) was utilized to assemble transcripts [18]. Then, the parameter “merge” was used to merge the assembled transcripts, and remove transcripts with a length within 200 nt and an exon number threshold of less than two exons. After that, the transcripts with FPKM (fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads) value ≥ 0.1 were selected and mapped to known databases by the software gffcompare [19] to screen the known transcripts. Finally, protein-coding potential of unknown transcripts was predicted with current mainstream analysis methods of CNCI, CPC, PFAM, and CPAT.

The significance of the differentially expressed lncRNAs (DE lncRNAs) and mRNAs (DE mRNAs) were analyzed using an R package DESeq2 with counts matrix [16], and multiple comparisons were corrected for p-value with the false discovery rate (FDR). A q value < 0.05 and an absolute value of log2FoldChange ≥ 1 were used as the threshold to statistically define the significant difference.

Prediction of target gene and functional enrichment analysis

Both co-location and co-expression analyses were performed to predict the target genes of DE lncRNAs according to the method previously described [18, 19]. Briefly, to identify the target genes regulated by the cis-acting of lncRNAs, putative lncRNAs were subjected to a 50 kb up and downstream screening of the protein-coding genes, respectively [17]. The principle of prediction for Trans target gene is mainly based on the fact of co-expressed with lncRNA. The target mRNA genes of the lncRNA were picked out with Spearman correlation coefficient score(cor ≥ 0.90), and p-value threshold(p < 0.05) [18]. These target genes and the DE mRNAs were subjected to functional exploration according to the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis. The calculated p-value went through FDR correction, taking the q-value < 0.05 as a threshold. GO terms and KEGG pathways with a q value < 0.05 were considered to be significantly enriched.

LncRNA–mRNA network construction

To further explore the interactions between the DE lncRNAs and DE mRNAs of testicular development in black goats, we constructed networks based on the targeting relationship between lncRNA and mRNA [19]. Briefly, DE lncRNAs between different stages of testes transcriptome were ranked by their fold change, and target genes of DE lncRNAs were predicted by LncTar (http://www.cuilab.cn/lnctar). Then according to the function of predicted target genes and their correlation to testicular development, the top six known lncRNAs and new lncRNAs were screened out. Finally, the LncRNA–mRNA network was constructed through the Cytoscape software (https://cytoscape.org/).

Validation of DE lncRNAs and DE mRNAs by RT-qPCR

In order to validate the authenticity of RNA-seq data, a total of 16 differentially expressed genes (including 10 lncRNA and 6 mRNA) were selected for RT-qPCR analysis. The total RNA extracted originally for sequencing was converted to cDNA using Hiscript RIII of the RT-PCR kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). All experiments were performed in triplicate. LncRNA and mRNA primers were shown in Table S1. RT-qPCR was performed using the SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence data were acquired using the fluorescence ratio PCR instrument (Roche, Shanghai, China). Three biological replicates and technical replicates were performed. The relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−△△CT method [20], with GAPDH as the reference gene.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization

The localization of lncRNA in testicular tissue was detected by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) using in situ hybridization reagents (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Briefly, samples were fixed, dehydrated, and digested with proteinase K for 20–30 min, pre-hybridizing at 37 ℃ for 1 h. Then, samples were hybridized with fluorescently labeled probes (5’-ATAGCCCGAAGTTTTCTCAAACGTG-3’) overnight at 4 °C. Then, the samples were washed with PBS solution and stained with DAPI solution. Finally, a fluorescence microscope (EVOS, Themo Fisher Scientific, US) was used for observation and digital images were captured.

Sertoli cell isolation, culturing, and transfection

Sertoli cells (SCs) were isolated from the testes of a 3-month-old healthy black goat. Briefly, testes were rinsed twice with PBS after removal of the tunica albuginea, and seminiferous tubules were sliced into small pieces about 1 mm3, then digested with type IV collagenase (4 mg/mL) for 1 h at 37.5 °C. Next, centrifugated and digested the precipitant with 0.25% trypsin for 20 min at 37.5 °C. The digestion was terminated with DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, US), and cells were filtered through 70 and 100-mesh screens. After centrifugation, cells were resuspended and cultured under a humidified 5% CO2 in air at 37.5℃. After 6 h and 18 h of culture, the changed culture medium to discard the suspending cells. After 2 to 3 days of culture under a humidified 5% CO2 in air at 37.5℃, cells proliferated up to 90% confluence and were routinely passaged using the digestive solution (0.05% trypsin) for 5 min to obtain pure SCs. The characteristics of isolated black goat SCs were confirmed by RT-PCR and hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining (Figure S3).

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting LOC108635509 or the corresponding negative controls (si-NC) were provided by Jima (Shanghai, China), and their sequences are listed in Table S2. The linear sequence of LOC108635509 was synthesized and cloned into the PacI and KpnI restriction sites of overexpression vector pGV658 (pGV658- LOC108635509, Figure S4C; Geneseed, Guangzhou, China). For transient transfections, SCs were seeded in culture plates in a proper density and 24 h later transfected with siRNA or overexpression vector using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, US) as recommended by the provider. Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection for subsequent analysis. The interfering efficiency of siRNAs were analyzed and siRNA-330 was picked to follow-up experiments (Figure S4A). The overexpreesion efficiency of LOC108635509 also was confirmed before formal experiments (Figure S4B, D).

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay and 5-Ethynyl-20-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining assay

The SCs proliferation was analyzed by CCK-8 assay (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and EdU staining assay (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols, respectively.

SCs were seeded and cultured at a density of 5 × 103/well in 100 µL of medium into 96-well microplates (Corning, USA). Then, the cells were transfected with siRNA or overexpression vector. After transfection for 12 h, 48 h, and 72 h, 10 µL of CCK‐8 reagent was added to each well and then cultured for 2 h. The absorbance was analyzed at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

SCs were seeded into 24-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well for 24 h. Then 10 µM EdU reagent was added to each well and continued incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. Finally, the SCs were photographed with a fluorescence microscope (EVOS, Themo Fisher Scientific, US), and the percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated.

Annexin V/PI staining and flow cytometry

The percentages of apoptosis in the SCs transfected with or without siRNA, or with overexpression vector were determined using the Annexin V-APC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Invitrogen, USA) and flow cytometry according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SCs were seeded in 6-well plates (60,000 cells/well) and were exposed to siRNA or overexpression vector for 48 h before seeded. The cells were simultaneously stained with Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) (green fluorescence) and the non-vital dye PI (red fluorescence), which allowed the discrimination of intact cells (FITC_PI_), early apoptotic cells (FITC + PI_), and late apoptotic cells (FITC + PI+).

Statistical analysis

All data were from three replicates and are presented as mean values with the standard errors of the means (mean ± SEM) unless particularly indicated. The statistical difference was analyzed by one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons or Unpaired two-tailed t-test using GraphPad Prism 6.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Probability values (P values) less than 0.05 were considered to be significant.

Results

Identification and characteristics of lncRNA and mRNA

In order to investigate the roles of mRNAs and lncRNAs in the regulation of black goat testicular development, twelve cDNA libraries of neonatal (D3-1, D3-2, D3-3), prepuberty (D30-1, D30-2, D30-3), puberty (D90-1, D90-2, D90-3) and postpuberty (D180-1, D180-2, D180-3) testes were constructed. Raw reads were obtained after sequencing by the Illumina HiSeq 3000 Platform. In total, the numbers of raw reads per sample were between 100,547,159 to 124,672,230, about 96,759,039 to 119,370,789 clean reads for each sample were obtained, and approximately 11.6 Gb data per sample remained after removing low-quality sequences and adaptor sequences (Table S3), more than 95.91% clean reads were mapped to the Capra hircus ARS1. Before further analysis, the reliability of all data was reassessed by hierarchical cluster analysis, and a sample of D90-3 was excluded because of separating to sample D90-1 and D90-2 (data not shown).

The total reads of libraries were mapped, classified and counted, results shown that the percentage of reads mapped to the exon region gradually increased with age (Fig. 1A), especially in D90, which indicated an active transcription occurred in the D90 testis. Novel reliable lncRNAs were filtered by using the Pfam and Cpat database and tested by CPC and CNCI, and a total of 34,211 potential lncRNA transcripts were identified to be expressed (Fig. 1B), which included 38.56% lincRNAs, 21.38% intronic lncRNAs and 10.55% antisense lncRNAs (Fig. 1C). We found chromosomes with longer length to be more likely to produce more lncRNAs, indicating that the number of reads distributed in the chromosome increased with the length of the chromosome (Fig. 1D). As illustrated in Fig. 1E-F, the identified lncRNAs showed the highest expression level in D3 was 5.3 (FPKM), and the mean expression levels of 4 groups were 3.45 (FPKM).

As illustrated in Figure S1-A, the mean length of lncRNAs was shorter than that of the mRNAs. Moreover, lncRNAs had fewer exons than protein-coding genes (Figure S1-B). In order to compare the expression level of lncRNAs and mRNAs in testes derived from different ages of black goats, the FPKM was calculated by StringTie according to the RNA expression of each sample, and a different distribution was obtained. The results showed that lncRNAs exhibited a lower expression level than mRNAs (Figure S1-C).

Differential expression analyses of lncRNA and mRNA

To further investigate the difference of transcriptome in testes from different ages of black goats, we analyzed the DE lncRNA and DE mRNA among groups. By conducting global gene expression analysis, we found that there were 2407 genes were differentially expressed in D30 vs. D3 (Fig. 2A). Specifically, 1062 genes were upregulated and 1345 were downregulated (Fig. 2C-D), which indicates a dramatic change in the testis transcriptome after born. Compared to other comparison groups, the number of DE genes in D180 vs. D90 was the least, which was 1103 (Fig. 2B), including 888 up-regulated genes and 215 down-regulated genes (Fig. 2C-D). The decrease in the number of DE genes indicates that the testicular function begins to stabilize.

Moreover, when the sexual maturity transition occurs from 1-month-old to 3-month-old, the number of DE genes is the most in D90 vs. D30 (Fig. 2A), including 13,986 upregulated genes and 7032 downregulated genes (Fig. 2C-D). We found that the number of downregulated lncRNAs is more than that of upregulated lncRNAs before puberty (D30 vs. D3). With the increase of age to puberty and post-puberty, the number of upregulated lncRNAs is more than that of downregulated lncRNAs. These upregulated lncRNAs may be involved in the regulation mechanism of spermatogenesis and sexual maturation (Fig. 2C-D). In addition, the heat map showed that the expression patterns of lncRNAs and mRNAs in the testis from 3-day-old goat were similar to that of 1-month-old of and the similarity was also shared between samples from 3-month-old goat to that of 6-month-old of goat, while the expression patterns of lncRNA and mRNA were different from 1-month-old to 3-month-old of testis (Fig. 2E-F). These results indicated that from 1-month-old to 3-month-old was an important period of testicular development. A circos figure of chromosome distribution of the DE lncRNA is shown in Fig. 2G. We found that most of the DE lncRNAs were found in D90 vs. D30, and enriched on chromosomes 1, 2. Interestingly, the downregulated DE lncRNAs were mainly located on chromosome X, the upregulated DE lncRNAs were mainly located on chromosome 27 (Fig. 2G).

Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed mRNA

To further dissect the biological functions of DE mRNAs during testicular development of black goats, these DE mRNAs (1196 DE mRNAs in D30 vs. D3, 8838 DE mRNAs in D90 vs. D30 and 269 DE mRNAs in D180 vs. D90) were annotated and were selected for GO enrichment analysis, respectively. The top 30 functions included cell adhesion (GO0007155), extracellular matrix composition (GO:0005201), calcium-binding (GO:0005509), cell activation (GO:0001775) and signal receptor binding (GO:0005102). GO functional items related to testicular development and spermatogenesis were also found, including male gamete production (GO:0048232), spermatogenesis (GO:0007283), sperm part (GO:0097223), meiotic cell cycle (GO:1903046) and multicellular biological reproduction (GO:0048609) (Fig. 3A-C). In particular, the DE mRNA was enriched to the testicular development and spermatogenesis in D90 vs. D30, including male gamete production (GO:0048232), spermatogenesis (GO:0007283), sperm part (GO:0097223) and meiotic cell cycle (GO:1903046) (Fig. 3B). Through GO enrichment analysis in D90 vs. D30, we found 228 upregulated and 31 downregulated DE mRNAs involving male reproduction.

In terms of the pathways, DE mRNAs were enriched in nine KEGG pathways including the MAPK signaling pathway(map04010) and Phenylalanine metabolism (map00360) in D30 vs. D3 (Fig. 3D). Moreover, 269 DE mRNAs were enriched in 15 KEGG pathways in D180 vs. D90, including B cell receptor signaling pathway (map04662), Fc epsilon RI signaling pathway (map04664) and Adherens junction (map04520) (Fig. 3F). Specially, 5492 upregulated and 3,346 down regulated DE mRNAs were enriched for 43 KEGG pathways in D90 vs. D30(Fig. 3E), the top 20 pathways associated with male reproduction, including ECM- receptor interaction signal pathway (map04512), MAPK signal pathway (map04010), and cGMP-PKG signal pathway (map04022) (Fig. 3E). These results suggest that the DE mRNA identify from D90 vs. D30 testes plays a regulatory role in testicular development and spermatogenesis through the above pathways.

Enrichment analyses of target genes of DE lncRNA

To gain insight into the potential functions of DE lncRNAs in regulating testicular development and spermatogenesis, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to investigate the roles of lncRNA in cis- or trans-regulation. Using the Pearson correlation ≥ 0.90 as the cutoff, protein-coding genes 50 kb up and downstream of all the identified lncRNAs were screened and considered as the cis-target gene or trans-target gene. Our results showed that 1211 DE lncRNAs in D30 vs. D3 regulated 1288 cis-target genes and 98,221 trans-target genes, 12,180 DE lncRNAs in D90 vs. D30 regulated 15,329 cis-target genes and 766,901 trans-target genes, 834 DE lncRNAs in D180 vs. D90 regulated 908 cis-target genes and 75,837 trans-target genes.

The top five GO terms were cell process regulation (GO:0050794), cell metabolic process (GO:0044237), biological process regulation (GO:0050789), microtubule tissue center (GO:0005815) and protein binding (GO:0005515) (Fig. 4A-C). Concerning the pathways, the first 30 signal pathways highly enriched by KEGG are mainly ECM- receptor interaction signal pathway (map04512), Rap1 signal pathway (map04015), calcium signal pathway (map04020), cGMP-PKG signal pathway (map04022) and ErbB signal pathway (map04012) (Fig. 4D-F). It was worth noting that some of the signal pathways enriched by lncRNA target genes, such as MAPK signal pathway (map04010) and Ras signal pathway (map04014) are involved in the process of testicular development, particularly take part in controlling the proliferation of somatic cell (SCs, Leydig cells) as well as differentiation of germ cells. These results strongly suggest that lncRNA differentially expressed with age is involved in the regulation of testicular development and spermatogenesis through target genes.

Validation the expression levels of lncRNA and mRNA

To further validate the lncRNAs and mRNA expression profiles obtained from the RNA-Seq results, we randomly analyzed the expression levels of 14 DE lncRNAs and mRNAs by RT-qPCR, which included 8 lncRNAs (LOC102183395, LOC102178761, LOC106502755, LOC106502861, LOC106503398, LOC106502907, LOC108635557, LOC106503660,) and 6 mRNAs (ENOX1, RIMS2, SLIRP, ID2, TCEA3, PRELP). As shown in Figure S2, the expression level of each lncRNA or mRNA was consistent with the sequencing results, suggesting the high reliability and accuracy of RNA-Seq data.

Identification of the core LncRNAs associated with testicular development

To elucidate the core lncRNAs linked to testicular development, a subset of twelve DE lncRNAs (Table S4) between 3-month-old and 1-month-old testes transcriptome were selected for the construction of a lncRNA–gene interaction network. This selection was based on their fold change and the predicted function of their target genes relevant to testicular development, including regulation of SCs proliferation, germ cell differentiation, and the synthesis of hormones in testicular cells. As shown in Fig. 5, the interaction network was constructed with twelve lncRNAs and their cis trans target genes, involving 39 target genes. In the interaction network, only one target gene RPL10 was downregulated by the LOC108638502, while the other 11 candidate lncRNAs and target genes showed an upregulation pattern.

To further identify the expression pattern of these twelve DE lncRNAs, the expression level of candidate lncRNAs in the testes of black goats at different age (D3, D30, D90 and D180) were analysis. Consistent with the sequencing results, the expression level of 12 lncRNAs changed significantly from D30 to D90 (Fig. 6); only LOC108638502 showed a significant downregulation with age, while LOC108635509 and other 11 lncRNAs showed a significant upregulation from D30 to D90; the expression levels of LOC108635509, LOC108638261, LOC108638405, MSTRG.290,485, and MSTRG.360,361 continue to increase at D180. Overall, the expression level of candidate lncRNAs showed the most significant changes from D30 to D90, indicating that D90 is a key node of lncRNAs that participated in the regulation of testicular development in black goats. To further confirm whether these 12 lncRNAs perform unique and tissue-specific functions in testis, the expression level of candidate lncRNAs in different tissues of D30 black goat were analyzed. As shown in Figure S5, the three of known lncRNA, LOC108635509, LOC108638261, and LOC108638405, expressed specifically in testis. In addition, Interleukin 4-induced gene-1 (IL4I1), the only predicted target of LOC108635509, which previously was reported specifically expressed in mouse testis and brain [21], was verified expressed in the nucleus of goat SCs (Figure S6) in this study, which indicating LOC108635509 and its target gene IL4I1may play specific role in testicular development. Taken together, LOC108635509 was chosen for further exploration of the functional mechanism of candidate lncRNAs regulating the development of testis.

LOC108635509 promotes the proliferation of black goat SCs

In order to further explore the function of LOC108635509 in testicular development, firstly, the FISH was performed to determine the localization of LOC108635509 in black goat testicular tissue and SCs. As shown in Fig. 7A, the localization of LOC108635509 was coincided with the positions of SCs and spermatocytes, indicating LOC108635509 were mostly expressed in SCs and spermatocytes; particularly expressed in the nucleus of SCs as shown in Fig. 7B.

Then the expression of LOC108635509 was interfered with by siRNA (Figure S4A), and the proliferation rate and apoptosis rate of SCs were analyzed. Compared to the NC control, the number of EdU-positive cells were decreased when the expression of LOC108635509 was interfered with (P < 0.05, Fig. 7C), which indicated the proliferation of SCs were inhibited. Consistently, the CCK-8 detection results also showed that the cell proliferation rate of the interference group was lower than that of the NC group (Fig. 7D). Meanwhile, after interfering with LOC108635509, the apoptosis rate of SC cells was increased, but there were no significant differences (P > 0.05, Fig. 7E).

To further confirm the function of LOC108635509, a vector of overexpression LOC108635509 was constructed and transfected with the SCs (Figure S4B-D), and the proliferation rate and apoptosis rate of SCs were analyzed. In contrast to the inhibition of LOC108635509 expression, both CCK-8 and EdU assays indicated that overexpression of LOC108635509 results in enhancing the proliferation of SCs (Fig. 7F-G). On the other hand, after overexpression of LOC108635509, the apoptosis rate of SC cells was decreased (Fig. 7H).

Taken together, these results suggested that LOC108635509 participated in supporting spermatogenesis and testicular development by regulating the viability of SCs.

Discussion

Testicular development and spermatogenesis are complex and continuous process involving cells proliferation, cell differentiation, and precise regulation of cross-talk between the somatic cell and male germ cells. It has been demonstrated that lncRNA plays important roles in regulating testicular development and spermatogenesis in a stage-specific way [22]. Some testicular lncRNAs and their target genes have been proven to be highly expressed in the signal pathways of spermatogenesis and regulated gonadal development. However, the specifically expressed lncRNA and its function in the testes of black goats at different ages are not clear. In the present study, we provided the transcriptional map of Guangxi black goat testis at four different developmental stages (from neonatal to postpuberty), and revealed the potential regulatory role of lncRNAs in the initiation of spermatogenesis. For the first time, we found LOC108635509, which expression levels were significantly increased at D90 and targeted to IL4I1, was found participated in the regulation of testicular development by promoting the proliferation of SCs.

LncRNAs are one type of noncoding transcripts that were discovered recently [23], and most of them expressed in a stage- and cell-type-specific manner [24, 25]. In the present study, we systematically compared the testicular lncRNA-mRNA expression characteristics of black goats at the age of D3, D30, D90, and D180, found that lncRNAs occurred in the testes of black goat has a shorter length, fewer exons, and lower expression levels than mRNA. These characteristics of lncRNA in the testis of black goats were similar to those of other breeds [26, 27]. In addition, we also found that more lncRNAs were differentially expressed before puberty (D90 vs. D30) than after puberty (D180 vs. D90), indicating that the testicular tissue of prepubertal black goats has a complex and dynamic transcriptional regulation mechanism, and D90 is the key period for the initiation of spermatogenesis.

In order to further clarify the biological processes involved in testicular development and spermatogenesis, we analyzed the function of DE mRNAs in the testes of black goats at different developmental stages. Based on the GO enrichment analysis, we found that most of the DE mRNAs related to testicular development and spermatogenesis, including male gamete production, spermatogenesis, sperm part, meiotic cell cycle, and multicellular reproduction. Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that DE genes were associated with male reproduction-related signaling pathways, such as MAPK, PI3k-Akt, TGF-β, and cGMP-PKG. It has been proved that the PI3k-Akt signal can inhibit the proliferation of SCs in prepubertal piglets and make SCs enter the differentiation stage to complete the establishment of the blood-testis barrier [28]. In addition, TGF-β superfamily ligands including typical TGFβs, activin, bone morphogenetic protein, growth and differentiation factor, and glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor, are key regulators of testicular development and spermatogenesis, previous studies have proved that the synthesis of these ligands gradually changes during mouse testis development [29].

In addition, since the expression pattern of some lncRNAs were continuously significantly upregulated with age, the common differential lncRNA with their target genes in different age stages were further analyzed by GO and KEGG enrichment analysis. We found the function of the target gene of DE lncRNA was similar to that of DE mRNA, and it was significantly enriched in the regulation of cell process, cell metabolism, ECM-receptor interaction signal pathway, Ras signal pathway, and other functions related to spermatogenesis. The Ras/ERK1/2 pathway plays an important role in the self-renewal of dairy goat spermatogonial stem cells by regulating ETV5 and BCL6B [30]. And Ras signal pathway affects the location of Cox7a2 in Leydig cells and participates in regulating the secretion of testosterone and the expression of STAR in mouse testosterone [31]. ECM-related proteins including cytokines, protease, and protease inhibitors, can affect the tight junction of SCs, and then affect the function of the blood-testis barrier [32]. Consistent these results, this study confirmed that the differentially expressed lncRNA and mRNA in the testis of black goats at different ages were involved in testicular development and spermatogenesis.

Mammalian spermatogenesis is the process by which male germline cells divide and differentiate into mature spermatozoa [33].Spermatogenesis is based on testicular development, and the testicular microenvironment changes with age, providing nutrition and structural support for the different stages of spermatogenesis [34]. As the only somatic cells directly contacted with germ cells in the testicular, the status of SCs in the seminiferous tubules also closely related to spermatogenesis.Recently, growing evidence suggested that SCs were not only transmitted hormonal signals and nutrients but also played an important role in the initiation of spermatogenesis [35, 36]. The numbers of SCs directly affected the size of testis, as well as semen quality [37]. In the present study, the DE lncRNA LOC108635509 was found mostly expressed in SCs and spermatocytes, and the expression level of LOC108635509 was significantly upregulated at D90 and continuously increased at D180. Downregulated the expression of LOC108635509 resulted in inhibiting the proliferation of in vitro-cultured SCs. These results indicate that LOC108635509 might participated in the initiation of spermatogenesis through regulating the viability of SCs. Whether LOC108635509 plays the same role in vivo remains to be verified. Overexpression LOC108635509 resulted in promoting the proliferation of Sertoli cells also indicated that LOC108635509 might relevant to the testis weight or length, but more direct evidence is needed. Additionally, IL4I1, well known as a leukocyte-specific L-amino acid oxidase, was predicted as the target of LOC108635509. Previous study studies found that IL4I1 was expressed in a tissue and cell-type-specific manner, while transcription of IL4I1 driven by the NUP62 promoter is restricted to cells in testis (SCs) and in the brain [21]. Consistent to that study, we found IL4I1 expressed in the nucleus of SCs, which coincided with the expression of LOC108635509. These results suggested that LOC108635509 and its target gene IL4I1 may play a specific role in testicular development. However, the function of IL4I1 in the regulation of testicular development and spermatogenesis still unknown, and the relationship between LOC108635509 and IL4I1 also needs further exploration.

Conclusions

In summary, we analyzed the transcriptomes of black goat testes before puberty, puberty, and post-puberty, and reported a set of lncRNAs and genes that are associated with black goat testicular development and potentially play roles in initiation spermatogenesis. Of these, LOC108635509 was a potential regulator that participated in black goat testicular development and spermatogenesis. Our findings are the first to comprehensively reveal all transcripts related to testicular development and spermatogenesis of black goat, and important candidate transcripts will be further investigated.
