Gas–Lesion Contact and Postural Compliance After Vitrectomy With Tamponade: A Continuous Monitoring and 3D Quantitative Analysis
Authors: Hyunmin Na, Changhoon Baek, Seung Woo Choi, Eun Kyoung Lee, Un Chul Park, Kyu Hyung Park, Saram Lee, Chang Ki Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo assess postoperative head positioning behavior and estimate gas–retinal lesion contact after vitrectomy using a wearable accelerometer-based monitoring system and geometric modeling.MethodsThis study enrolled 56 patients who underwent pars plana vitrectomy between November 2024 and June 2025. Of these, 28 were prescribed prone positioning for 1 week and 28 received no postural instruction. A custom-designed eyeshield equipped with a triaxial accelerometer continuously recorded three-dimensional (3D) head orientation at 5-second intervals for 7 days. A geometric model integrating gas volume dynamics, lesion location, and head orientation was used to estimate time-resolved gas–lesion contact.ResultsProne compliance averaged 49.8% ± 4.3% and declined over the week; gas–lesion contact averaged 90.5% ± 2.1% and likewise decreased, being lower at night. Gas–lesion contact averaged 92.9% ± 2.5% in single-tear rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) cases and 88.1% ± 3.2% in non-RRD cases including macular hole. Gas–lesion contact increased with prone compliance and with octafluoropropane (C3F8) compared with sulfur hexafluoride (SF6). Patient self-report questionnaires indicated greater perceived burden in the prone group, affecting sleep quality.ConclusionsWe established a quantitative framework to assess postural behavior and estimate lesion-specific gas contact in the early postoperative period. SF6 tamponade required strict adherence to maintain adequate gas–lesion contact, whereas C3F8 provided more consistent coverage despite variable compliance. These findings may inform postoperative patient counseling and tamponade selection.Translational RelevanceBy integrating week-long vector-based monitoring and 3D visualization of real-world head posture with a generalized model that estimates gas–lesion contact at any retinal location, this study provides quantitative metrics that directly inform postoperative positioning and tamponade choice.

Introduction

Postoperative head positioning is widely considered essential for optimizing gas–retina contact following pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with gas tamponade, particularly in surgeries involving macular holes (MHs) and rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD).1–5 Surgical success depends on both the type and duration of tamponade, as well as the length of posturing, with prone positioning enhancing gas–retina contact and facilitating retinal reattachment.5–11 Over time, prone positioning regimens have diversified, leading to greater variability in postoperative protocols across institutions.1,12–16 However, some studies suggest that strict or prolonged prone positioning is not necessary in all cases, as comparable surgical outcomes have been reported even with less stringent posturing or shorter-acting gas tamponades.17–26

Despite its clinical significance, long-term adherence to prescribed positioning remains poorly understood due to current limitations in postural compliance monitoring. Previous studies have been restricted by intermittent subjective assessments.27–29 Others utilized head-mounted sensors that provided valuable insights, although most were limited by short monitoring durations, typically less than 72 hours postoperatively, and small sample sizes,30–33 underscoring the need for prolonged monitoring in larger cohorts.

To address these gaps, we developed a novel, sensor-integrated eyeshield that periodically monitors head orientation every 5 seconds over an extended postoperative period without additional discomfort, enabling real-world observation. A key innovation of this investigation lies in its analytical framework. A first-order kinetic interpolation model was employed to estimate intraocular fluid volume over time,34 and a three-dimensional (3D) vector-based method was developed to define gas–retinal lesion contact criteria grounded in the spatial relationship between the gas–fluid interface and the retinal lesion. In addition, we developed a novel 3D visualization approach that intuitively depicts postoperative head position patterns.

Methods

Participants

This prospective observational study was conducted at Seoul National University Hospital and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB no. 2404-050-1527) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT06985030). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. A total of 56 patients who underwent PPV between November 20, 2024, and June 2, 2025, were enrolled.

In routine clinical practice at our clinic, postoperative posturing after vitrectomy with tamponade is often individualized according to lesion characteristics, disease severity, and the expected intraocular gas fill. However, for the purpose of this prospective study designed to quantitatively evaluate prone positioning behavior over a 7-day period, all enrolled patients who received intraocular gas tamponade were uniformly prescribed a strict prone positioning regimen for the entire first postoperative week, irrespective of indication or lesion location. This standardized positioning regimen was prospectively defined, approved by the Institutional Review Board, and explained to all participants during the informed consent process prior to enrollment, with no protocol modifications during the study period. This was a non-randomized observational study. Patients were assigned according to the clinical indication for intraocular tamponade: Those who received gas tamponade and were prescribed prone positioning were classified as the prone positioning group (n = 28), whereas those who did not require tamponade (e.g., epiretinal membrane cases) and received no postural instruction were classified as the free positioning group (n = 28). Surgical indications included MHs, RRD, epiretinal membrane, vitreous hemorrhage, and other vitreoretinal diseases as detailed in Table 1 (Part A). All surgeries were executed using standard three-port 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy techniques. Inclusion criteria were (1) patients ≥ 12 years old; (2) patients requiring PPV for various retinal diseases, with or without intraocular gas tamponade; (3) patients with confirmed availability of preoperative axial length measurements and ultra-widefield fundus photography (detailed device specifications are provided in Supplementary Method S1). Exclusion criteria were (1) patients with musculoskeletal or other physical conditions that would preclude the ability to maintain prone positioning, and (2) patients with cognitive impairment interfering with informed consent or compliance. Sensor data were successfully collected and analyzed for all enrolled participants, without dropout or technical failures.

Postoperative Setting and Monitoring Period

Monitoring was initiated immediately after transfer to the ward following surgery. All patients remained hospitalized until postoperative day (POD) 1, when they underwent a routine postoperative examination and were then discharged. Thereafter, patients stayed at home and returned for follow-up at POD 7. For analysis, sensor data were extracted from the time of ward admission up to exactly 168 hours (7 × 24 hours) postoperatively for all participants.

Accelerometer-Integrated Eyeshield

The lightweight custom eyeshield with an embedded triaxial accelerometer allowed comfortable wear and conducted continuous head-orientation monitoring at 5-second intervals. Detailed hardware specifications and the firmware architecture are provided in Supplementary Method S2, and representative images of the device in use and its compartments are shown in Figure 1.

Preset Calibration and Head Orientation Analysis Algorithm

Prior to data acquisition, a built-in calibration protocol was utilized to define three orientation reference vectors (prone, upright, and right lateral decubitus) using 10-second recordings in each posture. Calibration accuracy was validated before and after use, ensuring stability within 5°. Angular deviation from the prone position was defined as the instantaneous angle between the measured head orientation vector and the calibrated prone reference vector. This metric was calculated for both the prone-positioning and free-positioning groups. In the prone group, angular deviation was used to quantify adherence to the prescribed posture. In the free-positioning group, this metric was not intended to represent compliance, but rather to provide a baseline reference of natural head orientation for comparison. In addition, angular deviation was analyzed as a continuous metric to complement the threshold-based definition of head position compliance (≤45°), enabling a more detailed characterization of posture fidelity across time. The full calibration protocol is detailed in Supplementary Method S3.

Algorithm for Detecting Eyeshield-Wearing Compliance and Estimating Gas–Retinal Lesion Contact

A validated signal-based algorithm was applied to detect periods during which the eyeshield was being worn; only these intervals were used for subsequent analyses. Gas–retinal lesion contact was subsequently estimated using a three-step computational framework incorporating intraocular fluid dynamics, lesion mapping in spherical coordinates, and head orientation data. Full algorithmic details are provided in Supplementary Methods S4 and S5 and Supplementary Figure S1.

Visualization of Head Position Distribution

Head orientation distributions were visualized using a 3D spherical density map and a Mollweide projection, which preserved the spatial geometry and provided an area-preserving planar representation. Full computational details are available in Supplementary Method S6.

Patient-Reported Experience Questionnaire

At the 1-week postoperative follow-up visit, patients completed a structured questionnaire evaluating discomfort, functional limitations, and attitudes toward posture and device use. Full survey content and validation details are provided in Supplementary Method S7.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 22.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL) and Python 3.12 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE). In Python, standard libraries including NumPy, pandas, SciPy, statsmodels, Plotly, and matplotlib were used for data processing, statistical testing, and visualization. We used a linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept for patients to evaluate changes in compliance over PODs, averaging values within each day for each patient. For day–night comparisons, we calculated mean daytime and nighttime compliance values for each day in each patient and analyzed all paired values across the 7-day monitoring period using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Unless stated otherwise, data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE) with range and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. Associations between gas–retinal lesion contact and clinical variables were examined using univariate and multivariable linear regression. Questionnaire data were analyzed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Correlations between questionnaire items were assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient, excluding cases with missing values or a score of 5 in the final item. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Between November 5, 2024, and June 7, 2025, 56 patients who underwent PPV were enrolled and included in the final analysis. No patients were excluded during the study period. Among all participants, 20 were male (35.7%), and the mean age ± SD was 60.8 ± 14.0 years (range, 13–85). Of these, 28 patients were prescribed prone positioning for 1 week postoperatively, and 28 patients received no postoperative positioning instructions. The intended follow-up duration was 7 days, and 54 of 56 patients (96.4%) completed the full 7-day monitoring. Two patients had shorter recording durations due to scheduling constraints, completing 4.84 and 5.90 days, respectively. The overall mean follow-up duration was 6.94 ± 0.04 days (range, 4.84–7.00; 95% CI, 6.86–7.03; theoretical maximum, 7.00 days), yielding 6,718,543 samples collected at 5-second intervals. The diagnoses among patients who were prescribed prone positioning were diverse, including single-tear RRD and MHs. The overall demographic details and diagnoses are summarized in Table 1 (Part A).

Compliance and Postural Deviation During the First Postoperative Week

Table 1 (Part B) summarizes quantitative assessments performed during the first postoperative week, including eyeshield usage compliance, angular deviation from the prone position, and compliance with prone positioning. Deviation angle and head position compliance were analyzed only during periods when the eyeshield was properly worn.

One-Week Compliance for Eyeshield Usage

Overall mean eyeshield compliance was 90.5% ± 1.5% (range, 54.7%–100%); compliance was 92.3% ± 2.0% for the prone group and 88.6% ± 2.2% for the free group. No significant differences were detected among positional, sex, or age subgroups. Shield compliance progressively decreased with each POD and was slightly lower during nighttime hours, as shown in Supplementary Figure S2. Mean compliance was highest on POD 0 at 97.5% ± 1.0% (95% CI, 95.6–99.4) and declined steadily to 78.4% ± 4.1% (95% CI, 70.4–86.4) by POD 7. A linear mixed-effects model confirmed a significant negative association between POD and compliance (β = –2.71, P < 0.001). Daytime compliance (9:00 AM–8:59 PM) was slightly higher than nighttime compliance (9:00 PM–8:59 AM) (91.6% ± 1.6% vs. 89.4% ± 1.8%, respectively; P = 0.049), although this marginal difference may reflect a ceiling effect due to generally high adherence.

Deviation From the Prone Position

The average angular deviation with respect to each calibrated prone vector was 77.7° ± 3.6° (range, 20.9°–112.2°) across all participants. When deviation from this geometric prone reference was compared, the free group exhibited greater deviation (101.8° ± 1.5°) than the prone group (53.7° ± 2.8°), with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001).

Head Position Compliance

Defined as the proportion of time the head remained within 45° of the prescribed prone orientation, prone position compliance was 49.8% ± 4.3% (range, 14.2–96.5) among participants in the prone group. No significant differences were detected among sex or age subgroups. Head position compliance progressively decreased throughout the postoperative period, as illustrated in Supplementary Figure S3. Mean prone positioning compliance in the prone group was highest on POD 0 at 63.8% ± 4.7% (95% CI, 54.6–73.0), declining over time and reaching 48.9% ± 7.1% (95% CI, 35.1–62.8) by POD 7. A linear mixed-effects model confirmed a statistically significant negative association between POD and prone compliance (β = –2.27, P < 0.001). Compliance was also slightly higher during daytime than during nighttime hours (51.1% ± 4.2% vs. 48.5% ± 4.7%, respectively; P = 0.066). Although this was not statistically significant, 64% of patients showed higher compliance during the daytime; however, variability across individuals, including two with high nighttime compliance, may have obscured significance.

One-Week Distribution of Head Position

Heatmaps of spherical coordinates for both the prone group and free group are presented in Supplementary Figures S4 and S5, respectively, featuring separate analyses for the entire observation period, daytime, and nighttime. Based on these distributions, Figure 2 illustrates the head position patterns of the prone group using 3D spherical heatmaps and Mollweide projections. The corresponding spatial mappings for the free group are presented in Supplementary Figure S6. Temporal analysis of head position revealed distinct diurnal patterns within each group. In the prone group, patients predominantly maintained a prone position during the daytime. At night, however, head positions shifted along an arch connecting the prone and both decubitus positions. In contrast, the free group tended to remain near the upright position during the day, with nighttime positions shifting along an arch that spanned the supine and both decubitus positions.

Data represent aggregated vectors across all prone-positioned patients. For panels (A) to (C), the color scale (“count”) represents the frequency density of head orientation vectors (i.e., the number of time points at which the corneal apex was oriented toward each direction on the unit sphere). For a given orientation, the foveal axis is located in the opposite direction; therefore, the heatmaps visualize the distribution of corneal apex directions over time rather than a static eye model. This interpretation assumes that the eye is in the primary gaze position, such that the visual axis is aligned with the corneal apex vector throughout the analysis. For the spherical heatmaps (A–C), unit vectors were binned in the angular domain into a 200 × 200 grid over polar and azimuthal angles (40,000 bins in total). For the Mollweide projections (D–F), the same vectors were projected onto the Mollweide plane and re-binned into a 300 × 300 grid (90,000 bins) for two-dimensional density visualization.

Gas–Retinal Lesion Contact

Analysis of the gas–retinal lesion contact was limited to gas-tamponade eyes with prone prescriptions (n = 22). The overall gas–retinal lesion contact was found to be 90.5% ± 2.1% (range, 71.1–100.0; 95% CI, 86.3–94.8). In cases of RD (n = 11), the contact was 92.9% ± 2.5% (range, 72.6–100.0; 95% CI, 87.4–98.5), whereas the corresponding value in non-RD cases (n = 11, including MH, lamellar hole, myopic traction maculopathy, and vitreomacular traction) was 88.1% ± 3.2% (range, 71.1–100.0; 95% CI, 81.1–95.2). Specific details regarding the type of tamponade gas, head position compliance, and location of the retinal lesion are summarized in Table 2, and the follow-up gas–fluid interface height for the prone group is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Despite a generally high level of gas–retinal lesion contact across patients (with many achieving > 90%), a few cases achieving <85% were linked to low compliance, sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) use, or inferior lesions (IDs 5, 8, 12, 14).

As shown in Figure 4, gas–retinal lesion contact increased with higher prone compliance. octafluoropropane (C3F8) (Figure 4A) yielded greater contact than SF6, whereas superior lesions (Figure 4B) had higher contact than inferior ones. In univariate analysis, contact was associated with prone compliance (P = 0.005) and tamponade type (P = 0.013), but not lesion height (P = 0.835). Patient-Reported Questionnaire

Postoperative questionnaire results are summarized in Table 3. Overall, patients in the prone group reported greater functional limitations than those in the free group. In particular, patients in the prone group experienced significantly greater difficulty with sleeping (P < 0.001) and eating (P = 0.004) than those in the free group, with sleep disturbance being the most frequently cited challenge in both structured items and open-ended responses. Notably, 76.9% of patients in the prone group identified maintaining the prescribed posture as the most burdensome aspect of their postoperative course.

Discussion

This study employed continuous 3D monitoring of postoperative head orientation following PPV with gas tamponade and combined these data with geometric modeling to estimate gas–retinal lesion contact, extending the observation period into the first postoperative week. Evidence on gas–retinal lesion contact outside the macular region, particularly incorporating real-world postural compliance data measured using wearable devices, remains limited. One point that warrants explicit clarification is the postoperative posturing protocol used in this study. In routine practice at our institution, postoperative positioning after vitrectomy with gas tamponade is often individualized according to lesion characteristics, disease severity, and the expected intraocular gas fill. In contrast, for the purpose of this prospective observational study, we applied a standardized 1-week prone positioning regimen to all gas-tamponade cases to enable uniform quantitative monitoring of postoperative head position behavior and gas–lesion contact under a consistent protocol. This design choice was made to reduce variability in postural instructions and to facilitate physiologic measurement, rather than to test the superiority of a uniform prone strategy over lesion-oriented posturing. Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as descriptive and mechanistic and not as evidence favoring one postoperative positioning strategy over another.

In this study, a compliance of 49.8% ± 4.3% was observed among participants in the prone group, lower than compliance reported by previous retrospective studies based on nursing records (85.0%–93.7%),27–29 but higher than prior sensor-based reports (11.7%–27.3%).30–33 Such discrepancies likely reflect the subjective versus continuous measurement, shorter follow-up during which patients may have been more active or mobile, and cultural variation. Further, the strong emphasis routinely placed on posture education at our center may explain the relatively higher compliance. Angular deviation provided complementary information by capturing continuous variation in head orientation beyond the binary compliance definition, allowing a more nuanced interpretation of posture fidelity. Prone compliance declined over the first postoperative week and was lower at night, even in initially adherent patients, indicating a cumulative burden. Furthermore, 3D and Mollweide maps showed a reproducible pattern involving daytime prone positioning with nocturnal drift to lateral decubitus. This highlights the need for interventions targeting nighttime noncompliance. Questionnaire results were consistent: Prone patients reported greater functional burden, particularly sleep disturbance, and 76.9% identified posture maintenance as the most burdensome aspect of care.

Our model estimated a gas–retinal lesion contact rate averaging 90.5%, with most patients exceeding 85%. Outliers showed suboptimal contact due to low compliance, SF6 use, or inferior lesions. Contact declined with time and at night, likely reflecting gas resorption and reduced adherence during sleep. In this cohort, including both RD and MHs, C3F8 produced consistently higher contact rates than SF6. A higher prone compliance was clearly associated with improved gas–retinal lesion contact, particularly in patients who received SF6, reflecting the posture-dependent nature of short-acting gas tamponades, due to their rapid absorption.

A threshold effect was observed (Figure 4A): For SF6, reliable contact (≥90%) was consistently achieved when prone compliance exceeded approximately 55% to 60%, whereas compliance below this level often resulted in suboptimal contact (70%–85%). C3F8 showed a ceiling effect, maintaining >98% contact across most lesion locations even when compliance was 25% to 50%, with the exception of extremely poor adherence (<20%). A prior C3F8-only study of MHs similarly reported 99.8% foveal contact despite lower compliance,31 underscoring the contribution of its long intraocular persistence.

Overall, these findings suggest that SF6 is appropriate for patients expected to maintain strict posturing, whereas C3F8 may be preferable when consistent contact is required or when postural compliance is expected to be suboptimal. Although superior lesion height was speculated to enhance contact rates, no statistically significant association was observed, likely due to the limited sample size. Larger studies are therefore needed to clarify the impact of lesion location.

Some limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the estimation of gas–retinal lesion contact assumes a fixed primary gaze direction, which may not be applicable during daily activities involving spontaneous eye movements. Second, the eyeshield compliance algorithm, although validated, is an indirect motion-based surrogate and may therefore misclassify non-wear (e.g., device in a pocket or on a vibrating surface). Third, the model simplified intraocular fluid dynamics by assuming a flat, gravity-driven gas–fluid interface while omitting surface tension and interfacial adhesion. This simplification may introduce systematic error in the estimation of gas configuration and spatial distribution, particularly under conditions where the true interface deviates from planarity. Further validation using experimental models, such as phantom eyes, would be valuable to refine the assumptions and improve the accuracy of the model. Fourth, the type of gas tamponade was not randomly assigned and might have been influenced by lesion characteristics, introducing potential selection bias. Ultimately, a quantitative link between gas–retinal lesion contact and surgical success remains unproven; prospective outcome-based studies are needed to validate contact rate as a surrogate marker.

Conclusions

Head positioning following vitrectomy is variable and time sensitive. By integrating continuous 3D orientation data with a generalized gas–retinal lesion contact model, this study established a framework to quantify postural behavior and lesion-specific gas exposure across the retina. The insights provided in this study can directly inform gas selection and tailored posturing prescriptions, supporting more individualized postoperative management.
