Association between prognostic nutritional index and 90-day mortality in septic patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mingjie Xie, Liuyun Huang, Ling Li, Yuanyuan Qin, Biheng Feng, Qingjiang Cai, Debin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association between the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) and 90-day mortality among patients with sepsis.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study utilizing data from the MIMIC-IV database for patients diagnosed with sepsis. Covariates were selected using Cox regression models. The association between PNI and 90-day mortality was assessed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. We further examined the potential nonlinear relationship using restricted cubic spline(RCS) analysis. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted for these groups, and the differences in 90-day survival rates were compared using the log-rank test.ResultsA total of 5,786 septic patients were included in the final analysis. The overall 90-day mortality rate after ICU admission was 35.0% (n = 2,027). In the fully adjusted Cox model, a higher PNI was associated with a reduced risk of 90-day mortality. Specifically, for each one-unit increase in PNI, the risk of 90-day mortality decreased by 1% (adjusted HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98 ~ 1.00, P = 0.004). The RCS analysis revealed a significant nonlinear relationship between PNI and 90-day mortality (Pnonlinearity < 0.001). The log-rank test confirmed a statistically significant difference in survival curves between the low and high PNI groups (P < 0.0001).ConclusionPNI demonstrates a significant nonlinear inverse association with 90-day mortality in patients with sepsis. These findings suggest that PNI could serve as a valuable prognostic tool. Clinicians may use PNI to identify high-risk patients and implement targeted interventions to potentially improve clinical outcomes.Keywords: Sepsis, Prognostic nutritional index, Mortality, MIMIC-IV

Background

Sepsis, a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction from dysregulated host response to infection [1], remains a global health challenge. In 2017, it affected 48.9 million people and caused 11 million deaths [2]. Despite medical advances reducing its incidence and mortality, sepsis remains a leading cause of morbidity worldwide. These data highlight the urgent need for improved clinical strategies.

Nutritional risk, defined as the probability of adverse outcomes from nutrition-related issues [3], is critical in sepsis. Excessive inflammatory mediators disrupt metabolism, triggering catabolism and malnutrition. Hypermetabolism combined with insufficient intake worsens negative nitrogen balance, correlating with higher mortality [4]. Early nutritional risk management improves outcomes [5].

In clinical practice, numerous tools are commonly used to assess nutritional status, such as the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) [6], the Malnutrition-Inflammation Score (MIS) [7], the Subjective Global Assessment (SGA) [8], and Nutritional Risk Screening 2002(NRS 2002) [9]. For patients with sepsis, nutritional status not only reflects the body’s metabolic state but also directly affects immune function. Malnutrition can lead to reduced lymphocyte proliferation capacity, disrupted cytokine secretion, and impaired ability of the body to clear infections. These effects further exacerbate the progression of sepsis and increase the risk of developing multiple organ dysfunction. Therefore, precise evaluation of both nutrition and immune function plays a crucial role in assessing the prognosis of patients with sepsis.

The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), calculated from albumin and lymphocyte count, reflects nutrition, inflammation, and immunity [10–13]. Lower PNI associates with worse outcomes in oncology and critical illness [14]. However, its role in sepsis prognosis is unclear due to small sample sizes. This study uses MIMIC-IV data to evaluate initial PNI (≤ 24 h of ICU admission) and 90-day mortality, aiming to provide a practical risk tool for sepsis patients.

Method

Source of data

This study used data from the MIMIC-IV(v3.1) open-access clinical database, which contains comprehensive hospital admission records from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022. An author (Mingjie Xie) obtained CITI program certification, passing the “Conflict of Interest” and “Study Data or Specimens Only” modules, and subsequently gained database access (Access ID: 65891579). Given that MIMIC-IV data are fully de-identified, this study was exempt from institutional review board approval and individual informed consent requirements. Throughout the study, we strictly adhered to the ethical principles established by the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring that all study activities met the highest ethical standards.

Participant screening process and grouping

This study enrolled adult patients from the MIMIC-IV database who met the Sepsis-3 criteria [1]. According to these criteria, sepsis was defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction due to a dysregulated host response to infection, evidenced by a ≥ 2-point increase in the SOFA score from baseline. Inclusion criteria required patients to be first-time ICU admissions with an ICU stay >24 h. Exclusion criteria included lack of lymphocyte count or albumin data. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first admission was analyzed to prevent data duplication. Following these criteria, 5,786 sepsis patients were included, and the screening process is shown in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of study population enrollment and exclusion

PNI was calculated as PNI = albumin (g/L) + 5 × absolute lymphocyte count (×10⁹/L). To further stratify the study cohort for analysis, patients were categorized into three groups based on tertiles of the PNI: PNI 1 (lowest tertile, PNI < 27, n = 1928), PNI 2 (middle tertile, 27 ≤ PNI < 33, n = 1929), and PNI 3 (highest tertile, PNI ≥ 33, n = 1929).

Data collection

We extracted hospitalization-related data from the MIMIC-IV database, including the following variables: [1]Demographic and administrative data: age, sex, weight, admission time, ICU admission time, ICU discharge time, hospital discharge time, and date of death; [2]Severity-of-illness scores: Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and Logistic Organ Dysfunction System (LODS) scores; [3]Complications: septic shock, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and acute kidney injury (AKI); [4]Treatments and interventions: renal replacement therapy (RRT) and invasive mechanical ventilation; [5]Laboratory values and vital signs within the first 24 h of ICU admission: absolute lymphocyte count (Lymph), albumin (Alb), mean blood pressure (MBP), peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO₂), white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), hematocrit (HCT), creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), anion gap (AG), hemoglobin (Hb), and neutrophil count (Neut), international normalized ratio(INR); [6]Primary outcome: 90-day mortality; [7]Secondary outcomes: length of ICU stay (LOS in ICU), in-hospital mortality, 28-day mortality, and 365-day mortality.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Missing data accounted for less than 5% for all variables. Multiple imputation was employed to handle missing values for variables with incomplete data. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median with interquartile range [IQR] and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons were made using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

A Cox proportional hazards model was constructed with PNI as the independent variable and 90-day mortality as the dependent variable. Covariates were selected through a process of adding and removing variables one by one. A change in the regression coefficient of a variable by more than 10% at any step was used as the criterion to identify it as a confounder. Multicollinearity among the included variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF > 15 indicating the presence of significant multicollinearity.

Multivariable Cox regression analysis was used to examine the association between PNI and the risk of 90-day mortality. The dose-response relationship between PNI and 90-day mortality was visualized using restricted cubic splines (RCS). Breakpoint analysis was performed to identify the potential threshold value of PNI in relation to mortality. Based on this threshold, the study cohort was stratified into two groups: a low PNI group and a high PNI group. Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted to visualize the cumulative survival probability over 90 days for the two groups, and the Log-rank test was used to compare the differences between them. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Upon ICU admission, patients in the lowest PNI tertile (PNI 1) presented with significantly higher severity-of-illness scores, including APS III, SAPS II, SOFA, and LODS, compared to those in the higher PNI tertiles (PNI 2 and PNI 3). Furthermore, during their ICU stay, they had a significantly higher incidence of septic shock, ARDS, and AKI. Regarding clinical outcomes, patients with a lower PNI exhibited a prolonged ICU length of stay and significantly higher mortality rates, including in-hospital, 28-day, 90-day, and 365-day mortality.

When analyzed as a continuous variable, PNI was identified as a protective factor against 90-day mortality in the Model 1. Each unit increase in PNI was associated with a 4% reduction in mortality risk (HR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.96 ~ 0.97, P < 0.001). This significant inverse association persisted in the multivariable-adjusted models: Model 2 (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98 ~ 1.00, P = 0.003) and Model 3 (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98 ~ 1.00, P = 0.004).

When analyzed as a categorical variable, a significant inverse correlation was observed between PNI and 90-day mortality, with mortality risk demonstrating a decreasing trend across ascending PNI categories. Trend tests revealed a statistically significant decrease in mortality risk with increasing PNI levels across all three models (all P for trend < 0.001).

Association between PNI and 90-day mortality

After full adjustment for covariates, RCS analysis was performed to explore the dose-response relationship between PNI and 90-day mortality in septic patients. The analysis revealed a significant non-linear relationship between PNI and 90-day mortality in septic patients (P for non-linearity = 0.001, Fig. 2).

RCS analysis of PNI and 90-day mortality

Further analysis of the breakpoint (results shown in Table 4) identified that the breakpoint for the association between PNI and 90-day mortality risk was 35.374 (95% CI: 34.83 ~ 35.919). When PNI was ≤ 35.374, PNI was significantly negatively associated with 90-day mortality (HR = 0.975, 95% CI: 0.965 ~ 0.985, P < 0.001). That is, within this interval, for each 1-unit increase in PNI, the 90-day mortality risk in septic patients decreased by 2.5%. When PNI was > 35.374, PNI was significantly positively associated with 90-day mortality (HR = 1.041, 95% CI: 1.002 ~ 1.081, P = 0.0394). This means that within this interval, for each 1-unit increase in PNI, the 90-day mortality risk in septic patients increased by 4.1%. The likelihood ratio test yielded a P-value of 0.001, which also supported the existence of this non-linear relationship.

Sensitivity analysis

To further validate the robustness of the association between PNI and 90-day mortality in patients with sepsis, we conducted three sensitivity analyses (results shown in Table 5). For Sensitivity Analysis 1, we employed multiple imputation to address missing variables and included all variables except PNI and the outcome variable as covariates in the analysis. Results showed that for each 1-unit increase in PNI, the risk of 90-day mortality in patients was reduced by 1% (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98 ~ 1.00, P = 0.014). For Sensitivity Analysis 2, we excluded all variables with missing values and validated the relationship between PNI and 90-day mortality; the results were consistent with those of Sensitivity Analysis 1 (P = 0.001). For Sensitivity Analysis 3, based on the distribution of missing values, we imputed missing data using the mean for variables that followed a normal distribution and the median for variables that did not. The results confirmed that PNI was a protective factor against 90-day mortality (P = 0.015). Collectively, the results of all sensitivity analyses demonstrated a statistically significant association between PNI and the risk of 90-day mortality, which further supports the robustness of this association.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we analyzed data from 5,786 patients with sepsis sourced from the MIMIC-IV database to investigate the association between PNI assessed at ICU admission and 90-day mortality. Within our study cohort, the 90-day mortality rate of patients with sepsis was 35.0% (2,027/5,786). Results from multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis indicated that PNI was a protective factor against 90-day mortality: for each 1-unit increase in PNI, the risk of 90-day mortality in patients with sepsis decreased by 1% (adjusted HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98 ~ 1.00, P = 0.004). RCS analysis confirmed a significant non-linear association between PNI and 90-day mortality (P for non-linearity = 0.001). Additionally, KM survival curve analysis showed that patients in the low PNI group had a significantly lower survival probability compared to those in the high PNI group (Log-rank test, P < 0.001).

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome defined by organ dysfunction from dysregulated host response to infection. The systemic inflammatory cascade triggers physiological derangements that can progress to multi-organ failure without treatment. Although sepsis assessment tools (e.g., procalcitonin, SOFA, qSOFA) exist, their prognostic utility is constrained by sepsis’ complex pathophysiology [15]. Currently, SAPS and APACHE Ⅱ are the most widely used severity, but their clinical utility is hampered by modest predictive accuracy and complex data needs. By contrast, PNI provides a simpler, more feasible alternative. Our results highlight PNI’s robust prognostic value in sepsis, positioning it as a valuable, accessible assessment tool.

First developed by Onodera et al. [16] in 1984, PNI was originally designed to evaluate preoperative nutritional status in gastrointestinal cancer. Its clinical utility has since expanded, showing prognostic value in various malignancies and critical illnesses. For instance, PNI-defined malnutrition predicts higher mortality in elderly ischemic stroke patients [17] and correlates with severity in acute myocardial infarction [18] and pancreatitis [19]. Notably, PNI is an independent predictor of in-hospital and 1-year mortality in ICU populations [14]. In sepsis, existing research has focused on its link to sepsis-induced AKI and disease severity [20–23].

Malnutrition is highly prevalent in sepsis, profoundly affecting outcomes—particularly immune function and infection control [24]. Immune dysfunction and metabolic derangements drive sepsis progression [25], while PNI—combining albumin and lymphocyte count—serves as a composite biomarker of nutrition and immunity. Serum albumin is essential for inflammation modulation and vascular protection; in sepsis, hypoalbuminemia arises from stress-induced consumption and endothelial injury [26]. A study [27] shows low albumin independently predicts higher sepsis mortality. Albumin also enhances immune defense by boosting antimicrobial activity while sepsis-related organ dysfunction exacerbates hypoalbuminemia [28, 29].

Neutrophils are key innate effectors, but prolonged survival in sepsis disrupts pro-anti-inflammatory balance, reducing cytokine production [30]. Concurrently, lymphocyte apoptosis depletes T/B cells, driving immunosuppression [31]. Hyperinflammation reduces lymphocyte counts, worsening this imbalance [32], while activated neutrophils release factors promoting tissue damage. Lymphocytes restore homeostasis via anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10) [33].

Low PNI, reflecting hypoalbuminemia and/or lymphopenia, serves as a sign of severe infection and immunosuppression [34], which contributes to a vicious cycle of malnutrition-inflammation and accelerates the development of sepsis. Previous studies [35] have emphasized the role of nutritional support in modulating inflammation and improving prognosis [36]. Our study also demonstrated the association between PNI and the prognosis of septic patients. Based on these findings, we suggest that routine assessment using PNI at ICU admission, followed by personalized treatment, may be a valuable approach in the management of septic patients, especially considering the potential of PNI as a prognostic marker. It should be noted that PNI, which involves multiple variables more related to inflammation than nutrition as discussed, is not intended to claim superiority over proper nutritional assessment tools but rather to provide an additional perspective for prognosis evaluation in the context of sepsis.

This study has several limitations requiring attention in future research. First, while multivariable adjustment revealed a significant association between PNI and 90-day mortality in septic ICU patients, observational studies cannot establish direct causality; further mechanistic research is needed to clarify underlying biological links. Second, data for this study were derived from the MIMIC database, where there was substantial missingness in lymphocyte count and albumin levels—two core parameters required for PNI calculation. Though we used multiple imputation for missing data and sensitivity analyses to confirm result stability, these methods may still introduce selection bias or reduce statistical power, affecting result reliability.Third, patients with preexisting liver or kidney dysfunction were not excluded. Since these conditions disrupt albumin synthesis/metabolism, PNI assessment accuracy may be compromised, limiting the external validity and clinical generalizability of our findings.

Building on the aforementioned limitations and the association characteristics between PNI and prognosis in septic patients identified in this study, we propose that future research could focus on the following directions: First, by detecting the phenotypes of immune cell subsets and the secretion levels of cytokines in patients with different PNI levels, further investigate the immunoregulatory mechanisms through which PNI influences sepsis prognosis, thereby providing mechanistic evidence for verifying the causal relationship; Second, evaluate the compensatory mechanisms in septic patients with low PNI and comorbid underlying diseases from multiple dimensions—including metabolic pathways, nutrient reserves, and tissue repair-related factors—to clarify the regulatory role of underlying diseases in the prognostic value of PNI; Third, incorporate sepsis severity assessment indicators such as SOFA score and APACHE Ⅱ score to analyze differences in the association between PNI and patient prognosis across different disease severity strata, thereby further refining the applicable scenarios for PNI-based prognostic assessment; Fourth, conduct large-sample, multicenter prospective intervention studies—on one hand, to verify the consistency of the association between PNI and 90-day mortality observed in this study, and comprehensively analyze the impact of confounding factors such as region and medical service level; on the other hand, to directly verify the prognostic guiding value of PNI in sepsis treatment by intervening in PNI-related indicators, thus providing more reliable evidence to support clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a significant nonlinear, inverse association between PNI and 90-day mortality in patients with sepsis. Higher PNI levels were independently associated with a reduced 90-day mortality risk, suggesting a potential protective effect. These findings highlight PNI as a practical prognostic marker for mortality prediction in sepsis. However, large-scale prospective studies are warranted to confirm its clinical utility.
