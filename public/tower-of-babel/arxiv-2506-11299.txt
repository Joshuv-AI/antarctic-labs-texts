Geometric Cosmology models: statistical analysis with observational data
Matías Leizerovich, Luisa G. Jaime, Susana J. Landau, Gustavo Arciniega

Abstract
Although the standard cosmological model is capable of explaining most current observational data, it faces some theoretical and observational issues. This is the main motivation for exploring alternative cosmological models. In this paper, we focus on a novel proposal that consists in adding an infinite tower of higher-order curvature invariants to the usual Einstein-Hilbert action. We obtain the late-time background evolution for three families of models that can be obtained from this proposal. We use recent data from Cosmic Chronometers and type Ia supernovae to test the late-time predictions of our models. In addition, we consider estimations from the Age of the Older Globular Clusters to constrain our models. While some of the studied cases are ruled out by the data, we show that there are particular cases of the GILA model that can explain current data.



I
Introduction

The discovery of the late-time acceleration of the Universe from observations of type Ia supernovae (SNIa), posed an important challenge to theoretical cosmology: to establish the physical mechanism responsible for this acceleration. The proposal of the standard cosmological model (called
\Lambda
CDM, which assumes General Relativity as the theory that describes the gravitational interaction) to solve this issue is to add a cosmological constant to Einstein’s equations. However, this proposal faces some issues that have been discussed in the literature. For example, the value of the observed cosmological constant has a difference with the theoretical prediction of about 60 or 120 orders of magnitude, if one considers that the cosmological constant arises from the vacuum energy of standard model fields. On the other hand, there are tensions between the values of cosmological parameters inferred with different data sets. The most prominent example is the so-called "Hubble tension" which refers to a more than
5\sigma
tension between the value of the Hubble constant
H_{0}
inferred from Cosmic Microwave Background data from the Planck collaboration
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
and the one obtained with Supernovae and Cepheid data
Riess et al. 2022
. Besides, recent results from the DESI collaboration
DESI Collaboration et al. 2025
, suggest that the dark energy equation of state has a different behavior from the one predicted by the
\Lambda
CDM model.

All these problems mentioned above are the main motivation for considering alternative cosmological models. These models can be grouped into two families: i)dark energy, where the late time acceleration of the universe is driven by a either a fluid or a scalar field ii) modified gravity; in these models the theory that describes gravitation is no longer General Relativity, but an alternative theory of gravity.
In the literature, several different theories of modified gravity are studied
Shankaranarayanan and Johnson 2022
;
Sotiriou 2015
.

In this paper we analyze the application to the cosmological scenario of a novel proposal that belongs to the family of alternative gravity theories. This proposal includes an infinite tower of higher order curvature invariants into the action. We identify three cases of interest for our study. It was shown in
Arciniega et al. 2020a
that the inclusion of the cubic term can provide second order field equations for an isotropic and homogeneous universe. This result was extended to fourth and fifth order in
Cisterna et al. 2020
and for the infinite case in
Arciniega et al. 2020b
. Later in
Jaime and Arciniega 2022
the proposal was updated with the inclusion of two different energy scales, one related to the inflationary period
Jaime 2021
and another associated with the late-time acceleration. Within this frame it is necessary to explore the different convergences of the infinite series at late time.

One of the important achievements of this paper lies in obtaining the late-time background evolution of the cosmological models derived from this novel proposal and testing them against recent data provided by SNIa and cosmic chronometers. In addition, we will also consider the observed age of the oldest globular clusters, to rule out some specific cases of the models of interest. For the models that can provide reasonable predictions for all observational data sets we obtain for the first time constraints on the parameter space.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
II
we briefly describe the modified gravity theories that we assume to build our cosmological models. Also we show the equations that describe the background dynamics of the universe in the context of our models. In section
III
we briefly describe the SNIa and CC data used in this paper. We also discuss recent data concerning the age of the globular clusters, which lead to an important constraint for the cosmological models that are tested here. The methodology of the statistical analysis performed to compare the late time evolution of the proposed cosmological model with recent data is described in Section
IV
. In Section

we show the results of the statistical analyses performed with cosmological models described in Section

III.3
Globular clusters

Globular clusters (GC), dense and nearly spherical groups of stars bound by gravity, are known to host the oldest stars in the Universe. Therefore, the estimation of its ages can be used to constrain the age of the universe.
The main sequence turnoff (TO) point, is related to the point in the color-magnitude diagram (CMD) at which hydrogen is exhausted in the core, and therefore serves as a primary "clock" for estimating the age of these star clusters. However, the TO point is also sensitive to other parameters such as mass, distance and metallicity among others. Various methods utilize either the TO luminosity or color as key indicators of age, commonly categorized as "vertical" or "horizontal" methods. Vertical methods focus on specific evolutionary sequences observed in a CMD. In contrast, horizontal methods rely on the color of the main-sequence turnoff, rather than its absolute magnitude, to gauge age. In addition, isochrone fitting techniques can be seen as a hybrid approach, as they simultaneously use both magnitude and color to infer age based on the morphology of the fit
Valcin et al. 2020
. On the other hand, another method to estimate ages of GC is nucleocosmochronology
Christlieb 2016
, which does not rely on the TO point. However, all these techniques, although independent of the cosmological model, are subject to various sources of error, which prevents them from being useful to constrain cosmological parameters as the other data described in this section
Catelan 2018
. However, we can use these data to estimate a lower limit of the universe’s age. We consider an age of 12.2 billion years (Gyr) for the oldest globular cluster, a value that is consistent with the majority of recent estimates. Based on this age, we propose a lower limit on the age of the universe at 12.7 Gyr, which includes an additional 0.5 Gyr to account for the age where star formation begins. We call this last estimation
\rm{AoU^{th}=12.7}
Gyr.
We will use this lower bound in Section
V
to further constrain the parameter space of the theoretical models considered in this paper. The value chosen here to limit the age of the universe may seem arbitrary. Therefore, in Section
V
, we will also discuss how the parameter space changes with reasonable changes in this value.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we have explored the late-time evolution of Geometric Cosmology theories, which provide an explanation of the late-time acceleration of the Universe, without requiring a cosmological constant or an additional dark component to the energy-momentum tensor. For our study, we focused on three cases of interest: the GILA model, the GR-deformation, and models with no GR contribution. Due to the number of free parameters and the high degeneracy between them, we only analyzed some examples of each of the mentioned to test their viability.

The main contributions of this paper are:

1.

We have obtained the late-time evolution for three cases of interest of the Geometric Cosmology theories.

2.

The specific models that belong to GR-deformation and non GR contribution that we have considered in this paper can be ruled out based on the inconsistency between their theoretical predictions and recent observational data from CC and PPS. However, we can distinguish the following

(a)

The GR deformation and some of the non GR contribution models analyzed in this paper can be ruled out because the respective prediction of Age of the Universe is below the one estimated from Globular Clusters.

(b)

Some of non GR contribution models analyzed in this paper can be ruled out because the intervals of the free parameters obtained with CC are inconsistent with the ones obtained with PPS.

(c)

For the GILA model, we find three particular choices of the coefficients
r
and
s
that can explain the CC and PPS data and also predict an Age of the Universe consistent with globular cluster data. For these models, we were able to estimate confidence intervals for the free parameters.

Appendix A
Exponential convergence

In this Appendix, we show the exponential convergence for the models that we analyze in this paper: GILA and GR-deformation. Let us start with the general expression for the
F(H)

infinite series expansion of eq. (
4
)

F(H)=(1+\alpha_{1})H^{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{\infty}\alpha_{(i)}H^{2i}.

(29)

We can split the summation into two by taking
\alpha_{(i)}=\tilde{\alpha}_{(i)}+\hat{\alpha}_{(i)}
,

\displaystyle F(H)=(1+\alpha_{1})H^{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{(i)}H^{2i}+\sum_{i=3}^{\infty}\hat{\alpha}_{(i)}H^{2i},

\displaystyle=(1+\alpha_{1})H^{2}+\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{(m)}H^{2m}+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\hat{\alpha}_{(n)}H^{2n}.

(30)

where we considered that the summation over
i
runs independently in both summations such that we changed the index
i
for
m
and
n
without loss of generality.

We will use the first summation for the early-time acceleration mechanism and a tilde over the coefficients for the early-time, and the last summation for the late-time accelerated era with a hat over the coefficients for the late-time, so we define:

\displaystyle F_{\rm Early}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}\tilde{\alpha}_{(m)}H^{2m},

(31)

\displaystyle F_{\rm Late}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\hat{\alpha}_{(n)}H^{2n},

(32)

Let us start with the
F_{\rm Early}(H)
. If we take the first non-zero coefficient
\tilde{\alpha}_{m}
, and name it
\tilde{\alpha}_{(p)}
, i.e.
p={\rm min}\{m|\tilde{\alpha}_{(m)}\neq 0\}
, we can factorize it from the summation:

\displaystyle F_{\rm Early}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{(p)}H^{2p}\sum_{m=p}^{\infty}\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{(m)}}{\tilde{\alpha}_{(p)}}H^{2(m-p)},

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{(p)}H^{2p}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\tilde{\xi}_{(j)}H^{2j}.

(35)

where we defined
j=m-p
, and
\tilde{\xi}_{(j)}=\tilde{\alpha}_{(m)}/\tilde{\alpha}_{(p)}
.
Clearly,
p\geq 3
, and
m\geq p
.

Analogously, for the late-time
F_{\rm Late}(H)
we get:

\displaystyle F_{\rm Late}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{(r)}H^{2r}\sum_{n=r}^{\infty}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{(n)}}{\hat{\alpha}_{(r)}}H^{2(n-r)},

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{(r)}H^{2r}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{\xi}_{(k)}H^{2k}.

(37)

At this level, we do not have a prescription to fix the
\alpha_{(i)}
coefficients, but instead of taking random values for simplicity for
\tilde{\xi}_{(j)}
and
\hat{\xi}_{(k)}
, we can consider only the case where
j
and
k
are a multiple of a certain value
q
and
s
, respectively, i. e.

\displaystyle F_{\rm Early}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{(p)}H^{2p}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\tilde{\xi}_{(j\cdot q)}H^{2j\cdot q},

(38)

\displaystyle F_{\rm Late}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{(r)}H^{2r}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{\xi}_{(k\cdot s)}H^{2k\cdot s}.

(39)

Before continuing, let us first take a slightly different expression for the same
F(H)
given in equation (
29
). Instead of factorizing
\hat{\alpha}_{(r)}H^{2r}
from the late acceleration
F_{\rm Late}(H)
in equation (
37
), we factorize
\hat{\alpha}_{(r=1)}H^{2}
, i.e.

\displaystyle F_{\rm Late}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{(r=1)}H^{2}\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{(n)}}{\hat{\alpha}_{(r=1)}}H^{2(n-1)},

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{(r=1)}H^{2}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\hat{\xi}_{(k)}H^{2k}.

(41)

Notice that the summation in equation (
41
) starts at
k=2
instead of
k=0
in order to maintain
H^{2}\times H^{2k}|_{k=2}=H^{6}
as the lowest
H^{2n}
term in the series expansion.

If we want to consider only multiples of a fixed value
s
as was done in (
39
), then

F_{\rm Late}(H)=\hat{\alpha}_{(r=1)}H^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\hat{\xi}_{(k\cdot s)}H^{2k\cdot s},

(42)

where now
s\geq 2
.

Let us remark that the construction given until now, from equation (
30
), is considering the following structure:

F(H)=(1+\alpha_{1})H^{2}+F_{\rm Early}(H)+F_{\rm Late}(H).

(43)

However, if
\alpha_{1}\neq 0
, we can include the term
\alpha_{1}H^{2}
into the summation in the
F_{\rm Late}(H)
. In this case, the only change in the expression for the late acceleration era is to start at
k=0
, and replacing
\hat{\alpha}_{(r=1)}
for
\alpha_{1}
, but maintaining the condition
s\geq 2
to avoid the emergence of an
H^{4}
(that belongs to the Gauss-Bonnet Lagrangian density), i.e.

F_{{\rm Late},\beta}(H)=\hat{\alpha}_{(r=1)}H^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\hat{\xi}_{(k\cdot s)}H^{2k\cdot s},\,(s\geq 2).

(44)

and

F_{\beta}(H)=H^{2}+F_{\rm Early}(H)+F_{{\rm Late},\beta}(H),

(45)

where we added a label
\beta
in equation (
45
) because this is the case for the GR-deformation
F_{\beta}(H)
in section
II.2
.

An exploratory analysis performed in a previous work
Jaime and Arciniega 2022
indicates that exponential solutions may explain the current type Ia supernovae datasets. Therefore, in order to obtain exponential solutions for
F_{\rm Early}(H)
the coefficients
\tilde{\alpha}_{(p)}
and
\hat{\xi}_{(j\cdot k)}
must obey

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}_{(p)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda L^{2(p-1)},

(46)

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}_{(j\cdot q)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{j}L^{2j\cdot q}/j!,

(47)

and equation (
38
) reads

\displaystyle F_{\rm Early}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda L^{2(p-1)}H^{2p}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{j}}{j!}(LH)^{2j\cdot q},

(48)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow F_{\rm Early}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda L^{2(p-1)}H^{2p}e^{\lambda(LH)^{2q}}.

(49)

where
p\geq 3
, and
q\geq 1
.

Similarly, for the
F_{\rm Late}(H)
in equation (
39
), conditions for coefficients
\hat{\alpha}_{(r)}
and
\hat{\xi}_{(k\cdot s)}
are

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{(r)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\beta\tilde{L}^{2(r-1)},

(50)

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}_{(k\cdot s)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\beta^{k}\tilde{L}^{2k\cdot s}/k!,

(51)

then equation (
39
) reads

\displaystyle F_{\rm Late}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\beta\tilde{L}^{2(r-1)}H^{2r}

(52)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}\beta^{k}}{k!}(\tilde{L}H)^{2k\cdot s},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow F_{\rm Late}(H)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\beta\tilde{L}^{2(r-1)}H^{2r}e^{-\beta(\tilde{L}H)^{2s}},

(53)

where
r\geq 3
and
s\geq 1
.

Now, we are ready to make an explicit construction of the exponential models that we used in this work for the different theories we explored in the main text.

A.1
The GILA model

The GILA model belongs to the family of theories given in action (
1
), but with two considerations: (1)
\alpha_{1}=0
, and (2) the infinite series expansion
F_{\gamma}(H)
converges to exponential functions. Therefore, the action for GILA models is

S_{\gamma}=\frac{1}{2\kappa}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[R+\sum^{\infty}_{i=3}\alpha_{i}\mathcal{R}^{(i)}\right]\,,

(54)

and the
F_{\gamma}(H)
is given by equations (
48
) and (
52
)

F_{\gamma}(H)=H^{2}+\lambda L^{2(p-1)}H^{2p}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{j}}{j!}(LH)^{2j\cdot q}\\
-\beta\tilde{L}^{2(r-1)}H^{2r}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}\beta^{k}}{k!}(\tilde{L}H)^{2k\cdot s}

(55)

which converges to (
49
) and (
53
)

F_{\gamma}(H)=H^{2}+\lambda L^{2(p-1)}H^{2p}e^{\lambda(LH)^{2q}}-\beta\tilde{L}^{2(r-1)}H^{2r}e^{-\beta(\tilde{L}H)^{2s}}.

(56)

with conditions
p\geq 3
,
q\geq 1
,
r\geq 3
, and
s\geq 1
.

-1<\alpha_{1}<0
