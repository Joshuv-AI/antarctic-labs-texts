Prevalence and associated factors of dilated cardiomyopathy among patients admitted to Wolaita Sodo university comprehensive specialized hospital, South Ethiopia
Authors: Zemenu Sisay, Melese Alemayehu, Wondimagegn Paulos, Alemu Bogale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), which is the most common type of cardiomyopathy, is a condition of the heart muscle in which the ventricles get enlarged and the systolic function decreases. In adulthood, it is a significant health issue and the associated mortality and morbidity is substantial, with regular hospital admissions. It is also quite common.ObjectiveTo determine the prevalence and associated factors of dilated cardiomyopathy among patients visited to Wolaita Sodo University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, 2024.Methods and materialsThe facility-based prospective cross-sectional study was conducted from 1st June to 30th November, 2024 on cardiac patients who visited at Wolaita Sodo university comprehensive specialized hospital. Study participants were selected by using a random systematic sampling method. The collected data were coded, cleaned, and entered into Epi data version 4.6 and exported to the statistical package for social science version 27 for analysis.Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to see the association between outcome and explanatory variables. Using tolerance and the variance inflation factor (VIF), the degree of multicollinearity in the final multivariate models was examined and fitted, and it was determined to be within a reasonable range.ResultA total of 412 (99%) individuals participated in this study. The prevalence of dilated cardiomyopathy was 32.28% (95% CI 28.23–37.65). Cardiac patients who had 41–60 years old [AOR 3.19, 95% CI (1.52–6.11)], being male [AOR 1.98, 95% CI (1.30–5.17)], rural residency [AOR 2.54, 95% CI (1.63–9.19)], hypertension [AOR 6.97, 95% CI (3.18–10.83)], obesity [AOR 3.34, 95% CI (1.86–8.71) and smoking [AOR 7.66, 95% CI (3.51–10.73)] were significantly associated with dilated cardiomyopathy.ConclusionDilated cardiomyopathy was a common condition that was more common in patients with heart problems. Age, gender, residence, hypertension, obesity, and smoking were all significantly associated with dilated cardiomyopathy. Patients should be physically active regularly and refrain from smoking. Further research is recommended to ascertain the exact cause and pathophysiology.

Introduction

Cardiomyopathies are conditions affecting the heart that result in abnormalities in the structure and function of the heart [1]. The morphologically defined subtypes include arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and left ventricular (LV) no compaction cardiomyopathy. These subtypes can all be mediated by genetics [1, 2].

Cardiomyopathies (CMs) are divided into two groups according to the American Heart Association (AHA) classification: primary (which primarily affects the heart) and secondary (which also involves other organs). Primary CMs can be classified as acquired, mixed, or genetic. DCM and RCM are examples of mixed CMs. Myocarditis, stress-induced CM (Takotsubo), peripartum and tachycardia-induced CM, and CM in infants of mothers with insulin-dependent diabetes are examples of acquired CMs [2].

Ventricular dilatation and reduced systolic function are hallmarks of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), the most prevalent type of cardiomyopathy. One of the main causes of arrhythmia and heart failure is DCM. Its high mortality and morbidity rates, which include frequent hospitalizations, make it a major health concern for adults. It is also fairly common [3].

The primary cause of ischemic heart disease (ischemic cardiomyopathy), coronary artery disease, accounts for approximately two-thirds of all heart failure cases. Other causes of DCM include end-stage hypertension, infection, valvular heart disease, and cardiac toxins (such as alcohol and chemotherapy) [4].

The clinical outcome after the onset of symptoms has not changed significantly, despite advancements for treating heart failure, such as the widespread availability of heart transplantation and improved medical care. Disability and morbidity are among the highest of any disease or disease syndrome, mortality is still high, and the disease is relentless and progressive. DCM can be familial when it affects two or more related family members, or idiopathic when it affects one family member and has no known cause [5].

Because symptoms do not always accompany reduced systolic function, DCM is a significant risk factor for heart failure (HF). Significantly, DCM is frequently linked to a higher risk of severe arrhythmia, suggesting that the cardiac conduction system is pathologically involved. Compared with ischemic DCM, randomized clinical HF trials usually show that 30% to 40% of subjects have non-ischemic DCM [6].

DCM can occur at any age, but it is more prevalent in men than women, is more common in the third and fourth decades of life, and has a worse prognosis for African Americans than for Caucasians [7–10]. DCM is believed to be a final common manifestation of myocardial damage, with a multifactorial underlying etiology that is most likely caused by hemodynamic, infectious, immunologic, toxic, nutritional, and genetic factors [9].

Numerous factors have been linked to Africans, including untreated hypertension, myocarditis, HIV infections, genetic factors, excessive alcohol use, nutritional deficiencies, and pregnancy [10].

Patients with DCM often present with symptoms of heart failure due to the much depressed systolic function [1, 10], but DCM is also a major cause of arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities [11], which may occur in up to 100% of the cases [12–15]. Mortality following the diagnosis of DCM is as high as 30% and 50% after 1 and 5 years, respectively [16]. As much as 50% of deaths that occur in the first five years among DCM patients occur suddenly [17], and mainly because of major arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities [18].

Several reports from sub-Saharan Africa have documented DCM as an important cause of heart failure, occurring only as the second or third most common cause of heart failure after hypertensive and rheumatic heart diseases [10]. Ethiopia is among the African nations that bear a share of the burden of heart disease. According to certain national research, there are two main causes of heart disease. The types and prevalence of significant rhythm and conduction abnormalities in patients with DCM in sub-Saharan Africa are not well understood [15], and a few studies conducted in Ethiopia have described these abnormalities in patients with DCM. The findings of the study will be applied to raise living standards in the research region, which will then benefit the entire nation. Thus, the purpose of this study was to determine the prevalence and contributing factors of dilated cardiomyopathy in patients who were admitted to the Wolaita Sodo University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.

Methods and materials

Study area

Wolaita Sodo University Comprehensive and Specialized Hospital served as the study’s site. Ethiopia’s capital, Addis Ababa, is 328 km away. The Sudan Interior Mission (SIM) founded it in 1920 E.C. It served as a primary hospital for roughly 50 years, a general hospital for 20 years, and a comprehensive specialized hospital since 2013 E.C. Approximately 5 million people are served by it. Wolaita Zone is home to 6,142,063 people overall, dispersed across 4,208.64 square kilometers (1,624.96 sq mi), according to the Central Statistical Agency of Ethiopia’s (CSA) 2021 population forecast. 3,115,050 women and 3,027,013 men make up the zone’s total population. Wolaita has a population density of 520.8 persons per square kilometer.

Study design and period

An institutional-based prospective cross-sectional study was conducted from 1 st June to 30th November, 2024 among patients who visited Wolaita Sodo University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.

Population

Source population

All cardiac patients who visited the hospital.

Study population

All patients visited the medical ward, adult emergency and medical referral clinic (MRC) with heart disease in the hospital during study period.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Patients aged 18 years and above presented to WSUCSH for cardiac evaluation or treatment. Patients with echocardiographic evidence of dilated cardiomyopathy (defined by left ventricular dilation and impaired systolic function).

Exclusion criteria

Patients whose medical records are lost or incomplete for important variables and patients with known congenital heart disease.

The sample size was estimated using a single population proportion formula using with assumptions of a 95% confidence level (CI), power of 80%, and 56.5% of cardiac patients were dilated cardiomyopathy [36].

Sampling procedure

The total number of cardiac patients who visited the outpatient, emergency unit, cardiac follow-up clinic and intensive care unit of the hospital were 843, according to 1 st June to 30th November, 2024 data. A sample of 416 patients was taken using systematic random sampling with an interval of K, where K = N/n K average = 843/416 = 2. The lottery method was used to select the first patient, and each 2 patients were chosen until the final sample size was reached. The next chart was taken for those charts that had incomplete variables. Finally 4 participants were excluded and the results were interpreted and conclusions drawn for 412 participants (Fig. 1).

Study variables

Dependent variable

Dilated cardiomyopathy.

Independent variables

Demographic and socio-economic factors

Age, gender, marital status, address, education, occupation, and monthly income.

Medical history

Hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, family history of cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, HIV, thyroid disease, heart valve disease, neuromuscular disorders, sudden death, and other medical illnesses.

Behavioral factors

Alcohol, chat chewing, drug addiction, smoking, BMI, exercise, adequate sleeping, and low salty diet consumption.

Operational definitions

Dilated cardiomyopathy

refers to an enlarged left ventricle with decreased systolic function as measured by the left ventricular ejection fraction [35, 37].

Diabetes mellitus

refers to a group of common metabolic disorders contributing to hyperglycemia, including reduced insulin secretion, decreased glucose utilization, and increased glucose production categorized as type 1, type 2, and gestational type [37].

Hypertension

Hypertensive heart disease is the result of structural and functional adaptations leading to left ventricular hypertrophy, CHF, abnormalities of blood flow due to atherosclerotic coronary artery disease and micro vascular disease, and cardiac arrhythmias [37].

Obesity

is defined as having too much body mass measured by BMI of 30 and above benchmark in adults [37].

Depression

is defined as depressed mood on a daily basis for a minimum duration of 2 weeks. An episode may be characterized by sadness, indifference, apathy, or irritability and is usually associated with changes in sleep patterns, appetite, and weight; motor agitation or retardation; fatigue; impaired concentration and decision making; feelings of shame or guilt; and thoughts of death or dying effect on hormonal disturbance [37].

Inadequate sleeping

is probably the most common cause of excessive daytime sleepiness. The average adult needs 7.5–8 h of sleep, but on weeknights, the average adult gets only 6.75 h of sleep [37].

Sudden death

is a condition in which suddenly dead by unknown or known case.

Drug addiction

is defined as a chronic, relapsing disorder characterized by compulsive drug seeking and use despite adverse consequences [31–33].

Data collection methods and instruments

After the data collection tool was prepared, four professional nurses collected the data under the supervision of the principal investigator. Structured questionnaires covering socio-demographic, behavioral, and medical history variables were used to gather the data. To address these independent variables, the questionnaire was adapted from various literary works. Both chart reviews and structured questioner interviews were used to collect the data, as well as any missing or insufficient documents.

Data quality assurance

After a thorough analysis of the pertinent literature and related studies, a data collection tool was created to ensure the quality of the data. Prior to the actual data collection procedure, the tool was pretested. Four data collectors (nurses who work in emergency departments and medical facilities) received brief training regarding the purpose, content, and procedure of data collection. A simulation was also used to hone the data collectors’ abilities. The entire data collection process was overseen by the principal investigator. Before analysis, data collectors and the principal investigator double-checked the completed questionnaires every day for accuracy and completeness. The filled-out surveys were secured.

Data analysis and management

SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Science) version 27 was used for statistical analysis after Epi-data version 3.1 was used for data entry, coding, and clearing. To check for missing values and variables, frequency and cross tabulation were employed. Using descriptive statistics such as mean, percentage, frequencies, and standard deviation, the demographic and medical/clinical traits of DCMs were calculated. The relationship between the dependent and independent variables was measured using an odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval. To evaluate the factors associated with dilated cardiomyopathy, logistic regression analyses in both binary and multivariable formats were performed. Significant associations were defined as those with a p-value < 0.05. The degree of multicollinearity in the final multivariate models was analyzed and fitted using tolerance and the variance inflation factor (VIF), and it was found to be within a reasonable range. Figures, tables, and diagrams were used to present the study findings.

Factors associated with DCM

Among the variables entered into multivariate logistic regression analysis, age, male gender, rural residence, hypertension, obesity, and smoking were found to be statistically significant with dilated cardiomyopathy.

Cardiac patients who had age category under 41 to 60 were 3.2 times more likely to develop dilated cardiomyopathy than age category of less than 20 years (AOR 3.19, 95% CI (1.52–6.11)].

Male patients were 2.0 times more likely to develop dilated cardiomyopathy than female patients (AOR 1.98, 95% CI (1.30–5.17)).

Cardiac patients who had rural residency were 2.5 times more likely to develop dilated cardiomyopathy than urban residency (AOR 2.54, 95% CI (1.63–9.19)).

Cardiac patients with hypertension were 6.9 times more likely to develop dilated cardiomyopathy than no hypertension (AOR 6.97, 95% CI (3.18–10.83)).

Cardiac patients with obesity were 3.3 times more likely to develop dilated cardiomyopathy than no obesity (AOR 3.34, 95% CI (1.86–8.71)).

Cardiac patients who had smoking were 7.6 times more likely to develop dilated cardiomyopathy than non-smoking (AOR 7.66, 95% CI (3.51–10.73)) (Table 6).

Discussion

This study assessed dilated cardiomyopathy and associated factors among cardiac patients in Wolaita Sodo University Comprehensive Specialized University Hospital. The study revealed that the prevalence of dilated cardiomyopathy was 32.28%. This study found that age, gender, residence; hypertension, obesity, and smoking were found to be statistically significant with dilated cardiomyopathy.

In this study, the overall prevalence of dilated cardiomyopathy was found to be 32.18%. The finding of this study was lower than studies conducted in Yemen [38] but higher than studies performed from 1975 to 1984 in Olmstead County, MN [21] and studies from England, Italy and Japan [24–27]. This might be the different socioeconomic status of populations. This study had a nearly similar prevalence finding with study conducted in Nigeria [41] and USA [28]. This finding is higher than the systematic review study conducted in sub-Saharan Africa [39]. Variations in study methodologies, sample populations, regional health disparities, and environmental and lifestyle factors, including traditional practices such as tobacco use, may be the cause of the significant discrepancy in the prevalence of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) when compared to other recent studies from Ethiopia. Furthermore, reported prevalence rates may be further influenced by variables such as genetic predispositions, diagnostic standards, and healthcare availability.

This study shows the age category of 41 to 60 years was significantly associated with dilated cardiomypathy. Age categories 41 to 60 were more likely to develop DCM than less than 20 years old patients. This is consistent with a study conducted in Yemen [38], South Africa [29], Nigeria [41], United States [23]. This might be age-related problems, in case increasing age decreasing immunity and increasing age-related disorders.

On the other hand, this study shows gender is significantly associated with dilated cardiomyopathy. This study is similar with study done in Yemen shows male more likely than female [38] and cross-sectional study conducted in Nigeria [41]. This study is inconsistent with the study conducted in south urban Nigeria female more likely than male [40] and study conducted saint paul hospital Ethiopia [42].

This study revealed that rural residence is significantly associated with dilated cardiomyopathy. Cardiac patients who had a rural residency were more likely to develop dilated cardiomyopathy compared to urban residency. This result is also consistent with a study conducted in saint paul Hospital millinium medical collage Addiss Ababa, Ethiopia [42] and Gondar University, Ethiopia [43]. This is because most people in underdeveloped nations live in rural areas, which is a major risk factor for dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), mainly because of the higher prevalence of infectious diseases and restricted access to healthcare. The detection and treatment of underlying disorders, including viral infections and hypertension, are delayed by obstacles like a lack of skilled medical facilities. Furthermore, environmental variables, including exposure to pollutants and dietary deficits in vital nutrients, worsen heart disease. In rural populations, these socioeconomic factors have a significant impact on cardiovascular health outcomes.

This study showed that hypertension is significantly associated with dilated cardiomyopathy. This study was supported by a study conducted in Saint Paul Hospital millennium medical collage Addis Ababa, Ethiopia [42], Gondar University, Ethiopia [43]. This study is consistent with the study conducted in Yemen [38] Nigeria [41], and systematic study conducted in Africa [39]. The majority of studies supported this finding as a predominant risk factors leading to DCM as a hypertension.

In the study cardiac patients who had obesity were associated with dilated cardiomyopathy.A cardiac patient who had obesity more likely to develop dilated cardiomyopathy than not obese. The finding is in agreement with the study done in America [46]. This might be because obesity may cause hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes, or diabetes mellitus. Too quite saturated fat can cause cholesterol to build up in arteries risks heart disease and stroke.

In addition smoking is significantly associated with dilated cardiomyopathy. This study supported study conducted in Yemen [38], and Tanzania [36], Gondar University, Ethiopia [43] and studies involving alcohol use as a cofounding factor with smoking to cause cardiomyopathy [31–35]. Smoking is a major cause of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and is responsible for one in every four deaths from CVD. Despite the low national smoking rate, the region’s high odds ratio of 7.66 for smoking as a risk factor for developing dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) underscores a serious public health issue that is closely related to rural communities’ cultural customs. The health effects of tobacco use may be underestimated in these regions because traditional tobacco smoking, which is frequently done with locally made tools like twisted paper loaded with dried tobacco leaves, may not be sufficiently recorded in national surveys. Despite the generally low incidence of traditional smoking, this strongly ingrained local culture exposes people to hazardous chemicals and raises their risk of cardiovascular disorders like DCM. Therefore, in order to reduce the burden of DCM and enhance general cardiovascular health, public health initiatives must identify and address these traditional smoking behaviors, promoting culturally sensitive awareness campaigns that inform communities about the health risks involved and advocate for healthier alternatives. Smoke exposure causes heart disease and stroke. Quitting smoking can protect people from cardiovascular disease and death. People who already have heart disease also benefit from quitting smoking.

Limitations of the study

The following limitations of the study should be noted. Firstly, broad confidence intervals for some adjusted odds ratios (AORs), suggests poor precision in these estimates. Additionally, because self-reported data is used, there is a chance that participants will not accurately remember or report their experiences, which might result in recall bias. Furthermore, because results from a single-center design may limit the generalizability of the results, as findings derived from a single location may not be applicable to broader populations or different contexts. These limitations highlight the need for caution when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

The study found that 32.28% of the 412 patients had dilated cardiomyopathy. Numerous risk factors were shown to have significant correlations, including age (41–60 years), male gender, living in a rural area, hypertension, obesity, and smoking. According to these results, to lower the frequency of dilated cardiomyopathy, populations displaying these risk factors urgently require focused screening and intervention techniques. To promote heart health, patients should abstain from tobacco use, eat a balanced diet, exercise frequently, and have regular checkups with their doctor. Future studies should concentrate on comprehending the pathophysiology of DCM, evaluating how identified risk factors affect clinical results, and creating efficient therapy plans. Furthermore, it is critical to inform patients about the harm that bad habits—particularly smoking—do to cardiovascular health.
