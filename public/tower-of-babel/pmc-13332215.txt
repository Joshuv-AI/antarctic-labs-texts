Prevalence of extended-spectrum β-lactamase and carbapenemase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae in humans, animals and the environment in Northwest Amhara, Ethiopia
Authors: Chalachew Genet, Wendemagegn Enbiale, Anna Rommerskirchen, Endalkachew Nibret, Abaineh Munshea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing K. pneumoniae (ESBL-PKP) and carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae (CPKP) are major causes of life-threatening infections in humans and animals. In 2019, the bacterium was responsible for 100,000 to 200,000 deaths globally attributable to antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Ethiopia, home to the largest livestock population in Africa, is characterized by close human-animal interaction and common practices of raw milk and meat consumption that favor the transmission of resistant bacteria. Despite these risks, the epidemiology of ESBL-PKP and CPKP has not been investigated using a one-health approach. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP among humans, animals, and the environment in northwest Amhara, Ethiopia. A cross-sectional study was conducted from January 1 to August 30, 2025. A total of 972 samples (508 humans, 158 animals, and 306 environmental samples) were analyzed. Sociodemographic and related data were collected from human participants using a structured questionnaire. Urine, blood, environmental swab, wastewater, and rectoanal mucosal swab samples were collected and processed for the isolation of K. pneumoniae using standard microbiological methods. ESBL-PKP and CPKP were identified phenotypically using a combination disk diffusion test and a modified carbapenem inactivation method, respectively, in accordance with the Clinical Laboratory Standard Institute guidelines. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 and R software. The prevalence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP among human isolates (n = 71) was 49.3% (35/71, 95% CI: 38-60.7) and 40.8% (29/71, 95% CI: 30.2–52.5), respectively. Meanwhile, the prevalence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP among environmental isolates (n = 56) was 39.3% (22/56, 95% CI: 27.6–52.4) and 25% (14/56, 95% CI: 15.5–37.7), respectively. No ESBL-PKP or CPKP were detected in animal samples. All ESBL-PKP and CPKP isolates were multidrug resistant (MDR) with more than 70% co-resistance to ciprofloxacin, co-trimoxazole, gentamicin, and tetracycline. Among K. pneumoniae isolates, 93% (56/61) of humans, and 73.2% (41/56) of environmental isolates were MDR. Though the number of animal isolates was small, all K. pneumoniae isolates from animals (n = 5) were MDR. Nearly all (40/43, 93%) CPKP isolates were resistant to the last-resort antibiotics, ceftazidime–avibactam and meropenem–vaborbactam. Neonates had higher odds of culture-positive K. pneumoniae compared with adults aged 18–49 years (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]: 27.79, 95% CI: 3.47-222.42, p = 0.002). The high prevalence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP in humans and hospital environments, coupled with widespread multidrug resistance, underscores the presence of a serious public health threat. The nearly universal resistance of CPKP isolates to the last-resort antibiotics is particularly alarming. However, molecular characterization was not performed, limiting the inference about specific carbapenemase genes. Cleaning of the targeted hospital environment and implementing integrated AMR surveillance under a one health framework are essential to prevent cross-sectoral spillover.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50365-w.

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) is a Gram-negative bacterium that causes a wide range of infections in humans and animals, including sepsis, urinary tract infections (UTIs), and respiratory tract infections1–3. The emergence of multi-drug resistant (MDR), and hypervirulent K. pneumoniae strains has led to an increase in disease severity, mortality and treatment cost, posing a serious public health threat1,4–7, particularly in sub-Saharan African countries8.

Globally, third-generation cephalosporin and carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae were projected to have caused between 100,000 and 200,000 antimicrobial resistance (AMR)-attributed deaths in 20199. Sepsis and UTI due to these resistant strains can result in two-fold higher mortality than their susceptible counterparts with an increasing trend over time5,10,11. Given its high resistance to last-line antibiotics coupled with the scarcity of new antibiotics in development12, the World Health Organization (WHO) designated K. pneumoniae as a “critical bacterial pathogen” in 2024 to stimulate global research and drug development efforts13.

Resistance in K. pneumoniae is mainly mediated by extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) and carbapenemase enzymes that hydrolyze β-lactam antibiotics commonly used in human and veterinary medicine, particularly in developing countries, including Ethiopia14–16.Carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae strains exhibit a higher dissemination rate compared to non-enzymatic carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae strains17, and different carbapenemase types show variable therapeutic responses18.

The widespread and inappropriate use of antibiotics in humans, animals, and agriculture has created a strong selective pressure favoring resistant strains19,20. K. pneumoniae transmission to humans may occur through direct animal contact, contaminated food, or environmental sources such as manure and wastewater21. Furthermore, K. pneumoniae can horizontally transfer ESBL and carbapenemase-encoding genes to other K. pneumoniae strains or clinically relevant bacteria22,23. These resistance genes commonly co-exist with other antimicrobial resistance genetic elements, positioning K. pneumoniae as a key reservoir and disseminator of AMR genes24,25.

Besides irrational antibiotic use19,20, the spread of resistant K. pneumoniae is driven by poor infection control, repeated hospitalization, and improper waste management. Agricultural and household practices, including manure use, free grazing, close human-animal contact, untreated wastewater consumption, poor pet hygiene, and ingestion of raw food, significantly contribute to the dissemination of MDR K. pneumoniae26–28.

Ethiopia faces a substantial burden of MDR ESBL-producing bacteria29, with K. pneumoniae being one of the most frequently isolated pathogens from sepsis and UTI cases30–32. The country has the largest livestock population in Africa33 with widespread free grazing animal management practice and increasing consumption of antibiotics in both human medicine, and veterinary practice34–36. Close human-animal interaction37, coupled with the common consumption of raw meat and milk38,39, and open defecation practices, creates an ideal environment for cross-sectoral transmission of resistant strains40. Although Ethiopia has developed a national AMR strategic plan based on a one-health approach41 and initiated phenotypic AMR surveillance42, its full-scale implementation is limited43. A few studies have assessed ESBL- or carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae in clinical samples44,45, but comprehensive data across humans, animals, and the environment are lacking. Thus, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP in humans, animals, and the environmental sources in Northwest Amhara, Ethiopia, using a One Health approach.

Materials and methods

Study area and period

A cross-sectional study was conducted between January 1 and August 30, 2025, in Northwest Amhara, Ethiopia (encompassing the Bahir Dar Special Zone and the North Gojjam Zone), encompassing humans, animals, and the environment. The study area includes two public referral hospitals, Felege-Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FHCSH) and Tibebe-Ghion Specialized Hospital (TGSH). These hospitals provide services to residents of the study area, as well as referred patients from various parts of Amhara Regional State and neighboring regions of the country. FHCSH, with 500 beds, and TGSH, with 493 beds, are among the largest hospitals in the country, located in the regional capital city of Bahir Dar46. Most households in the study area practiced a small-scale mixed crop and livestock farming47. Cattle are the main livestock species providing milk, meat, and manure48. Both private and government-owned dairy and fattening farms operate in the area, supported by veterinary services delivered through public and private clinics as well as house-to-house outreach by animal health professionals49.

Sociodemographic and related data collection

Data on sociodemographic and related variables such as age, sex, and residence were collected from humans and animal owners using a structured questionnaire administered by trained human and veterinary health professionals. Animal-level data (e.g., sex, barn type, bedding material) were collected via a checklist, while environmental data (e.g., sampling time, collection round) were recorded by the field teams.

Human sample collection

Urine samples (10 mL) were aseptically collected from clinically presumptive UTI patients using sterile cups — clean-catch midstream specimens for non-catheterized patients and aseptic aspiration via catheter line for catheterized cases. Samples were transported at room temperature and processed within 2 h57. Meanwhile, Blood samples (10 mL from adults, 5 mL from children, 2 mL from neonates) were collected from suspected sepsis patients and inoculated into tryptone soya broth (TSB) bottles for culture32,58. Blood and urine samples were collected and processed in FHCSH and TGSH microbiology laboratories.

Animal sample collection

The rectoanal mucosal swab (RAMS), known for its higher sensitivity compared to fecal samples59, was collected from 158 animals. The RAMS were collected from one apparently healthy animal per farm from 79 farms and from one diseased animal, accompanied by the head of the household for veterinary health services in a public veterinary clinic. As described previously59–61, RAMS samples were collected using a sterile cotton swab moistened with sterile Tryptone Soya Broth (TSB, HiMedia, India). The moistened swab was inserted 2–5 centimeters (cm) into the anus by using a rapid in-and-out motion. The entire mucosal surface of the rectoanal junction was swabbed using a circular motion and then immediately inserted into a test tube containing 3 mL of sterilized TSB.

Environmental sample collection

Wastewater and surface swab samples were collected from different sites of FHCSH, TGSH, and the animal clinic (Table 1). The sampling sites in hospitals were selected based on different wards and units serving the study participants, as well as the accessibility of the wastewater manholes. In the meantime, environmental sites in animal clinics were selected considering their service for animal study subjects. Related prior studies were used to collect and process the environmental samples54,55,62. Briefly, swab samples were collected from the floor surfaces of the animal clinic cattle crush with shade, animal clinic tables, hospital bedside tables, inpatient kitchen tables, and inpatient kitchen served dishes using a sterile cotton swab moistened in a test tube containing 3 mL TSB. The sample collection area was rubbed and then inserted into TSB, a process repeated three times to increase the chance of K. pneumoniae isolation. For hospital wastewater, a 3 mL sample was collected using the grab sampling technique from the wastewater manholes of selected wards and the intensive care unit (ICU), the hospital main reservoir, and inpatient kitchen wastewater outlets, all of which were accessed using a sterile syringe. Then, the sample was transferred immediately into a sterile 50 ml single-use Falcon tube.

The environmental samples (swab and wastewater) and RAMS were transported to the Environmental Health and Biomedical Reference Laboratory of Amhara Public Health Institute, Bahir Dar, Ethiopia, in a cold box for processing. For anticipated delay, the sample was kept at 2–8 °C and processed within a maximum of 6 h.

K. pneumoniae isolation and identification from different sources

Urine, blood, wastewater, RAMS, and environmental swab samples were processed for the isolation and identification of K. pneumoniae following standard microbiological methods32,46,56,62,63. Urine samples were mixed by inversion, and 10 µL was inoculated onto blood agar plates (BAP) within 2 h of collection, incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Samples with ≥ 105 CFU/mL for non-catheterized and ≥ 103 CFU/mL for catheterized samples were considered significant and subcultured onto MacConkey agar plate (MAP) and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Blood samples were inoculated into TSB aerobic blood culture bottles and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Bottles showing growth were subcultured onto MAP, while those without visible growth were incubated up to 7 days. Wastewater and RAMS samples were vortexed for 20 s, and 10 µL were directly inoculated onto MAP and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. Meanwhile, the swab samples were pre-enriched aerobically at 37 °C for 4 h before MAP inoculation. Samples yielding mucoid pink colonies on MAP were subcultured on BAP and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h, then subjected to biochemical identification. The biochemical tests included sugar fermentation and gas producing using Kligler iron agar, citrate utilization using Simon’s citrate agar, motility, hydrogen sulfide production, and indole production using sulfur indole motility media (all obtained from HiMedia, India), following standard microbiological methods as described in the WHO guideline57 and the district laboratory manual in tropical countries64.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the modified Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guideline65. Based on prior studies32,46,63 and CLSI guideline65, a total of 13 antibiotics were selected from different categories namely penicillin-β-lactamase inhibitor (amoxycillin-clavulanic acid, AMC, 30 µg), non-extended spectrum cephalosporins (cefazolin (KZ, 30 µg), cefuroxime (CXM, 30 µg)), extended spectrum cephalosporins (ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 µg), cefotaxime (CTX, 30 µg)), carbapenems (meropenem (MRP, 10 µg), imipenem (IPM, 10 µg)), aminoglycosides (gentamicin (GM, 10 µg), amikacin (AK, 30 µg)), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, CIP, 5 µg), tetracyclines (tetracycline, TE, 30 µg), folic acid synthesis inhibitors (co-trimoxazole, COT, 25 µg), and phenicols (chloramphenicol, C, 30 µg). The AST results for all antibiotics were interpreted as susceptible, intermediate, and resistant according to the breakpoints specified in the CLSIM100 document65. Except for AMC, AK, and COT, which were obtained from HiMedia, India, all antibiotics were from Oxoid (Hampshire, United Kingdom). K. pneumoniae resistant to at least three antibiotics in different antibiotic categories was considered multidrug resistant (MDR)66.

Detection of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae

Phenotypic screening and confirmation of ESBL production were performed according to the CLSI guideline65. K. pneumoniae isolate with an inhibition zone of ≤ 22 mm for ceftazidime and/or ≤ 27 mm for cefotaxime was considered as an ESBL suspect and further confirmed phenotypically by the combination disc diffusion test. In this test, isolates suspected of ESBL production were tested against ceftazidime (30 µg), ceftazidime–clavulanic acid (30/10 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), and cefotaxime-clavulanic acid (30/10 µg) discs using the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method on Muller-Hinton Agar (MHA, HiMedia, India). After incubating aerobically at 37 °C for 18 h, an increase of five or more mm in zone of inhibition for either of the antimicrobial agents tested in combination with clavulanic acid than the zone diameter of the agents when tested alone was identified as an ESBL producer.

Furthermore, ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae isolates were tested against five potential carbapenem-sparing treatment options for ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae67,68. These five antibiotics were not part of the general AST performed for all K. pneumoniae isolates. The antibiotics tested include aztreonam (ATM, 30), cefepime (FEP, 30 µg), piperacillin-tazobactam (TZP, 110 µg), cefoxitin (FOX, 30 µg), and cefotetan (CTT, 30 µg) obtained from Oxoid, UK. The AST was performed, and the results were interpreted as susceptible, intermediate, and resistant according to the CLSI M100 document65.

Detection of carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae

The modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) was employed to detect carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae as per the CLSI M100 guideline65. Meanwhile, the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method was used to screen all isolates for carbapenemase production with meropenem (10 µg) and imipenem (10 µg) on MHA. The isolates were considered suspects for carbapenemase production if the inhibition zone for meropenem and/or imipenem was ≤ 19 mm and were further confirmed using mCIM. In this method, 1 µL loopful of a colony of the suspected isolate from an overnight-incubated BAP was transferred into 2 mL TSB (HiMedia, India), followed by vortexing for 10 s. Then, after adding a meropenem (10 µg) disc, the TSB was incubated aerobically for 4 h at 37 °C. A 0.5 McFarland suspension of meropenem-susceptible E. coli American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 25,922 was evenly swabbed onto MHA, and then the meropenem in the TSB was dispensed, followed by incubation for 24 h at 37 °C. The inhibition zone diameter of 6–15 mm or the presence of pinpoint colonies within a 16–18 mm inhibition zone indicated a carbapenemase producer.

The CPKP isolates were further tested against the ceftazidime–avibactam (CZA) and meropenem–vaborbactam (MEV) combination antibiotics (Oxoid, Hampshire, United Kingdom), which are among the last therapeutic options for MDR bacterial infection69. The AST against CZA and MEV was performed using the disc diffusion method and interpreted based on the CLSI M100 document as susceptible, and resistant to CZA, and susceptible, intermediate, and resistant to MEV65.

Quality assurance

Various quality assurance procedures were implemented to ensure the quality of data. Before data collection, data collectors received training. Both during and after data collection, the questionnaire’s completeness was verified. The sterility test was performed by incubating 5% of uninoculated bacterial culture media from each batch64. Samples showing mixed bacterial growth (more than one colony type), colonies presumptive for K. pneumoniae were subcultured into BAP to obtain a pure colony for biochemical testing. The K. pneumoniae ATCC 700,603 and E. coli ATCC 25,922 were used as positive and negative controls for ESBL detection, respectively. Besides, E. coli ATCC 25,922 and K. pneumoniae ATCC BAA1705 were used as negative and positive controls for carbapenemase production, respectively65. All reference strains were obtained from the Ethiopian Public Health Institute.

Data analysis

All data were coded, entered, and analyzed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Based on the complete-case analysis strategy, variables having complete data were considered for analysis. Key variables were summarized using descriptive statistics. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact test were performed in SPSS to see the presence of association between the variables. The resulting Chi-square and Fisher’s exact test P-values were exported to R software version 4.5.3 (R Core Team, 2026; https://www.r-project.org/) within RStudio (https://posit.co/download/rstudio-desktop/) for adjustment using Holm correction to minimize family-wise error rates. Holm correction was selected over alternative approaches such as Bonferroni, because it provides a more balanced control of Type I error while maintaining statistical power for multiple comparison. Bivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify eligible predictor variables for multivariable logistic regression. Moreover, the multicollinearity among eligible predictor variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). All predictor variables with the VIF value less than 2 and a p-value less than 0.25 in bivariable logistic regression were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis70. Seven human-related variables were included, comprising age, consumption of raw meat, consumption of raw vegetables, consumption of raw milk, herd size, pet ownership, and milk pail type. To visualize the antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of MDR K. pneumoniae for 13 antibiotics tested, “ComplexHeatmap package” was used R within RStudio. All statistical tests performed (Chi-square, Fisher’s exact test, and logistic regression) were two-sided. Holm-adjusted p-values < 0.05 for multiple testing and unadjusted p-values < 0.05 for the multivariable logistic regression model were considered statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

The study was ethically approved by the Bahir Dar University Institute of Biotechnology Institutional Research Ethics Review Committee with reference number IoB-IRERC/001/2024. Informed written consent was obtained from patients aged 18 years or older who were clinically presumptive for UTI or septicemia. Consent was obtained from parents, next-of-kin, or guardian of a child aged less than 18 years and clinically diagnosed as having septicemia or UTI. Besides the consent from their guardian, next-of-kin, or guardian, assent was obtained from children aged between 12 and 18 years who were clinically diagnosed as having UTI or septicemia. Anonymity was preserved throughout data collection. Additionally, verbal informed consent was obtained from animal owners and farm managers prior to data collection, and this procedure was ethically approved by the IRERC. Moreover, the study was conducted in accordance with national animal welfare regulations and institutional requirements.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Human study participants residing in the study area (northwest Amhara, Ethiopia), and presenting to FHCSH and TGSH during the study period, were included. Moreover, both clinically diagnosed animals (with diarrheal disease and/or pneumonia) presenting to animal clinic and apparently healthy animals from farms within the same study area were included. On the other hand, human participants and animals attending FHCSH, TGSH, or animal clinic but residing outside the study area were excluded. Additionally, human study participants or animal study subjects who took antibiotics in the past 2 weeks were also excluded.

Prevalence of K. pneumoniae among humans, animals, and the environment

The overall prevalence of K. pneumoniae was 13.6% (132/972, 95% CI: 11.6–15.9) with a significant difference among humans, animals, and the environment (adjusted Chi-square p = 0.007). The prevalence in the environment was 18.3% (56/306, 95% CI: 14.4–18.3), while in humans and animals was 14% (71/508, 95% CI: 11.2–17.3) and 3.2% (5/158, 95% CI: 1.4–7.2), respectively. Sepsis patients had a significantly higher prevalence of K. pneumoniae (20.2%, 95% CI: 15.8–25.5) than UTI patients (7.3%, 95% CI: 4.7–11.3) (adjusted Chi-square p = 0.007). Moreover, a higher K. pneumoniae prevalence was found in hospital surface swabs (36/160, 22.5%, 95% CI: 16.7–29.6) than animal clinic surface swabs (4/76, 5.3%, 95% CI: 2.1–12.8). However, there was no significant difference in the prevalence between apparently healthy (2/79, 2.5%) and diseased (3/79, 3.8%) animals (adjusted Chi-square p = 649) (Table 4).

Prevalence of ESBL and carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae among humans, animals, and the environment

Among 132 K. pneumoniae isolated from humans, animals, and the environment, 75.8% (100/132, 95% CI: 67.8–82.3) were ESBL (43.2%, 57/132, 95% CI: 35.0-51.7) or carbapenemase (32.6%, 43/132, 95% CI: 25.2–41.0) producers. The prevalence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP was 49.3% (35/71, 95% CI: 38.0-60.7) and 40.8% (29/71, 95% CI: 30.2–52.5) in humans, respectively. Out of 56 environmental isolates, 22 (39.3%, 95% CI: 27.6–52.4) were ESBL-producers and 14 (25%, 95% CI: 15.5–37.7) carbapenemase-producers, with all CPKP and 95.5% of ESBL-PKP isolated from hospitals (Fig. 1). However, there was no ESBL-PKP and CPKP among animal isolates.

Antibiotic susceptibility profiles of ESBL-PKP and CPKP against carbapenem-sparing antibiotics

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed on ESBL-PKP (57 isolates) and CPKP (43 isolates) isolates against five carbapenem-sparing antibiotics. Almost all ESBL-PKP (98.2%, 56/57) and CPKP (42/43, 97.7%) exhibited resistance to aztreonam, a monobactam antibiotic. A distinct level of resistance was noted against cefotetan, with 1.8% (1/57) of ESBL-PKP and close to all CPKP (90.7%, 39/43) exhibiting resistance to cefotetan (Fig. 2). The resistance level of CPKP to both CZA and MEV, among the few antibiotics considered the last therapeutic options for CPKP infections, was 93% (40/43).

Multidrug resistance profile of K. pneumoniae isolated from humans, animals, and the environment

Our study revealed that 112 K. pneumoniae isolates (112/132, 84.8%), including all ESBL-PKP and CPKP, were MDR. Specifically, 93% (66/71), 73.2% (41/56), and 100% (5/5) of human, environment, and animal isolates were MDR, respectively. As indicated in the heatmap, 84.8% (56/66) of human and 73.2% (30/40) of environmental MDR K. pneumoniae isolates were resistant to at least 8 antibiotics tested. Moreover, 6 K. pneumoniae isolates (three human & three environmental isolates) were resistant to all 13 antibiotics tested. However, none of the K. pneumoniae isolated from animals were resistant to eight or more antibiotics (Fig. 4).

Factors associated with culture-positive results of human, animal, and environmental samples for K. pneumoniae

Bivariate logistic regression was performed independently for patient and hospital environment data to assess the association of 10 patient-related and two environment-related predictor variables with a K. pneumoniae culture-positive result. Seven patient-related variables qualified for multivariable logistic regression (p ≤ 0.25), all exhibiting VIF results less than two. However, the two environment-related predictors were not eligible for multivariable logistic regression. Patient age was the only significantly associated factor with culture-confirmed K. pneumoniae infection. Compared with patients aged 18–49 years, neonates were 13.6 times more likely to have culture-confirmed K. pneumoniae infection. Though factors such as consumption of raw meat and raw milk in patients showed a significant association in bivariable logistic regression, they failed to show a significant association in multivariable logistic regression. The contamination level of the two hospital environments and across different rounds of sample collection did not show significant variations (Table 7).

Factors related to owners of animals included in this study were assessed using Fisher’s exact test to see the presence of any significant association with RAMS culture-positive results of their animals. Among 12 factors assessed, none were significantly associated with a RAMS culture-positive result for K. pneumoniae. Though not statistically significant, a higher number of K. pneumoniae were isolated from animals owned by individuals who have raw milk, raw meat, and raw vegetable consumption habits than animals owned by individuals who did not drink raw milk, eat raw meat, or consume raw vegetables (Supplement Table 1).

Discussion

The overall prevalence of K. pneumoniae in our study was 13.6%. The prevalence in humans (14%) and the hospital environment (22.6%) was significantly higher than the prevalence in animals (3.2%) and the animal clinic environment (5.3%). Our finding was consistent with previous studies documenting a higher K. pneumoniae prevalence in clinical samples and the hospital environment than in animals and their surrounding environment71,72. The high contamination level in the hospital environment, particularly bedside table surfaces (22.5%), may be attributed to inadequate infection prevention and control measures73, serving as a reservoir for K. pneumoniae, a well-known causative agent for healthcare-associated infections74.

The prevalence of K. pneumoniae in animals and the animal clinic environment in our study was higher than previous studies in Egypt75 and Japan76, which reported 1.8% and 0% in cattle, respectively. As a result, our findings are an indication for the growing but less recognized role of the bacterium as a zoonotic pathogen originating from animals reared for meat, milk, and the environment28,77,78.

Ever-increasing prevalence of ESBL-PKP and associated life-threatening infections79 drives the use of carbapenem empiric therapy for K. pneumoniae infections80. This has contributed to the emergence of CRKP strains, a growing global health and economic threat with limited treatment options81,82. In our study, the prevalence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP in humans was 49.3% and 40.8%, respectively. Meanwhile, the prevalence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP in the environment was 39.3% and 25%, respectively. Since CPKP isolates often co-produce ESBL83, the prevalence might be substantially higher. Moreover, all ESBL-PKP and CPKP exhibited high-level co-resistance (> 70%) to ciprofloxacin, co-trimoxazole, gentamicin, and tetracycline. Finding such high CPKP in clinical and environmental settings, coupled with high co-resistance levels to non-β-lactam antibiotics in developing countries with limited accessibility to newer treatment options for carbapenem-resistant bacterial infections, indicates the presence of a health threat in the human-environment interface84. Given that K. pneumoniae is a key source of various antibiotic resistance genes for other pathogenic bacteria via horizontal gene transfer24,25, the high prevalence in human and environmental samples in our study needs immediate preventive action based on the One Health approach.

The prevalence of ESBL-PKP among humans in our study was higher than the pooled prevalence of ESBL-producing enterobacteriaceae (18%) in Ethiopia45. Our finding was also higher than the prevalence of ESBL-PKP in previous global85 and sub-Saharan African86 studies, which reported a prevalence of 32.7% and 8.6%, respectively. This variation can be attributed to differences in infection prevention practices, accessibility, and misuse of antibiotics, coupled with a poor regulatory system87 and increasing prevalence of ESBL-producing enterobacteriaceae in the community88. The prevalence of CPKP in human isolates in our study was seven times higher than the pooled national carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae prevalence isolated from clinical samples44. Moreover, the high prevalence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP in referral hospitals, along with heavy antibiotic usage in these settings, may have contributed to the observed high prevalence89,90.

In contrast, no ESBL-PKP and CPKP were detected in animals in our study. However, previous studies have reported a substantial level of ESBL-PKP in animals85,86,91. A 2025 systematic review in Africa reported a higher prevalence of carbapenem-resistant enterobacteriaceae in animals compared to human and environmental sources92. The absence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP in animals in the present study must be interpreted with extreme caution due to insufficient statistical power. With only five K. pneumoniae isolates recovered from 158 animals (3.2% isolation rate), our study lacked adequate power to detect resistance. Therefore, our findings regarding resistance in animals are inconclusive rather than confirmatory of absence. This may reflect genuinely low colonization rates in cattle in our study area, and/or methodological-related factors, since half of the animal subjects were apparently healthy farm animals, which may exhibit lower colonization rates compared to diseased animals93. The result may also be attributed to differences in antibiotic selection pressure between veterinary and human medicine in our setting or the sample type used, despite the fact that rectoanal mucosal swabs are known to be more sensitive than fecal samples59.

Though controversy exists on their discovery timeline, the role of the environment as a reservoir for β-lactamase-producing bacteria dates back before the discovery of penicillin94,95. Our study reported a prevalence of 39.3% for ESBL-PKP and 25% for CPKP in the environment, where all CPKP and 95.5% of ESBL-PKP were isolated from hospitals. This poses a substantial health risk to healthcare providers, patients, and the wider community in the study area, given that the contribution of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae to AMR-attributed death is increasing over time96. The prevalence of ESBL-PKP in the environment in our study was higher than the global prevalence (24.2%)85 and the prevalence in sub-Saharan African countries (6.2%)86. This might be due to poor infection prevention practices73 and frequent use of 3rd and 4th generation cephalosporins for empirical treatment in specialized hospitals in Ethiopia97, .

The high prevalence of CPKP in humans (40.8%) and the environment (25%) in our study urges immediate attention. Along with different preventive measures such as implementing sustained infection prevention, AMR surveillance, and isolating CPKP-infected patients98,99, the use of carbapenem-sparing antibiotics to treat ESBL-PKP infection is helpful to limit the emergence and spread of CPKP67,68. However, our study revealed a high resistance rate of ESBL-PKP to carbapenem-sparing antibiotics, mainly to piperacillin-tazobactam (49.1%) and cefepime (96.5%), limiting their use for empirical treatment as an alternative to carbapenems100. However, ESBL-PKP exhibited a low resistance rate to cephamycins, specifically cefoxitin (7%) and cefotetan (1.8%), rendering them a potential carbapenem-sparing therapeutic alternative101,102. The combination antibiotics, CZA and MEV, are among the few remaining therapeutic options for carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales infections103. However, 93% of mCIM-confirmed CPKP in our study demonstrated resistance to both CZA and MEV. While the mCIM method confirms carbapenemase production with high sensitivity (97–100%) and specificity (100%)104,105, it does not differentiate between carbapenemase classes, which limits the interpretation of our CZA/MEV resistance findings. The high phenotypic resistance to CZA/MEV could reflect predominance of metallo-ß-lactamases (MBL) (e.g., NDM), which are intrinsically resistant to both avibactam and vaborbactam106, or co-production of multiple ß-lactamase enzymes or concurrent presence of other resistance mechanisms such as efflux pumps or porin mutations107–109; molecular testing is required to confirm this. Clinical outcome data (mortality, length of stay, treatment response) were initially planned but not collected due to high rates of missing values and incomplete medical records. To ensure data quality, we focused on variables that could be reliably obtained through standardized interviews. Future studies should incorporate dedicated clinical data collection protocols to assess the direct clinical impact of ESBL and carbapenem resistance.

Our study demonstrated high rates of MDR K. pneumoniae in human (93%), animal (73.2%), and environmental (100%) isolates. The MDR rate in clinical isolates was comparable with a study in Addis Ababa (98.5%)110 and Debre Berhan (97.9%)111, Ethiopia. However, it was higher when compared with previous studies in other parts of the country (57.1%)112 and the pooled global MDR prevalence of nosocomial K. pneumoniae (32.8%)89. This might be due to the presence of multiple antibiotic resistance genes in K. pneumoniae and the ability of the bacterium to acquire new resistance genes via horizontal gene transfer24,25. Inappropriate use of antibiotics, non-sustainable implementation of phenotypic AMR surveillance, and lack of integrated surveillance based on the One Health approach43 may have also contributed to the widespread presence of MDR K. pneumoniae in the human-animal-environment interface in northwest Amhara, Ethiopia.

Despite no ESBL and carbapenemase-producing isolates being detected, all K. pneumoniae isolated from the gastrointestinal tract of meat and dairy animals were MDR. Our finding was consistent with prior reports describing the role of animals and their products as a reservoir for MDR K. pneumoniae113,114. Thus, animals may serve as a source of MDR K. pneumoniae infection in the study area, where 54.7% and 27.8% of human study participants reported consuming raw beef and milk, respectively. Moreover, the MDR rates of K. pneumoniae isolated from animals in our study were significantly higher than those in a prior study which reported 48.39%115.

Patient age was significantly associated with culture-positive results for K. pneumoniae in the present study. Compared to other age groups, a significantly higher K. pneumoniae infections were documented in neonates. This might be attributed to the weak immune system of neonates compared to adults116. Moreover, a similar higher K. Pneumoniae was reported in neonates than in other age groups in Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia112, the leading causative agent of neonatal sepsis among Gram-negative bacteria117,118.

Our study was the first of its kind in reporting the prevalence of ESBL-PKP in humans, animals, and the environment at the same time. However, the study did not identify the co-occurrence of ESBL in CPKP isolates, as well as the types and variants of ESBL and carbapenemases and their encoding-genes harbored in K. pneumoniae circulating among humans and the environment. These limited our understanding of molecular mechanisms underlying the high ESBL-PKP and CPKP prevalence and resistance rates to CPKP to CZA and MEV. The low number of K. pneumoniae isolated from animals (5/158) severely limited the statistical power of our study to detect ESBL-PKP and CPKP in the animal population. With only five isolates, we had insufficient power to detect resistance patterns, potentially underrepresenting the true prevalence of resistance in animals. Future studies with larger sample sizes or targeting diseased animals with higher expected colonization rates are needed to adequately assess antimicrobial resistance in livestock.

Conventional biochemical tests exhibit a close to 10% misidentification rate of bacteria to the species level compared with the Matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry identification method119,120. Therefore, the use of the conventional biochemical test method in our study may have slightly affected our species-level identification. Moreover, since there was no assigned cleaner, we collected all environmental samples from animal clinics at a single time point (when the room was not cleaned), unlike the mixed time point sample collection employed for the hospital environment. These differences in sampling time likely overestimate our finding in the animal clinic environment since cleaning can reduce colonization of the environment with potentially pathogenic bacteria121. Since sampling of our human study participant was done in referral hospitals, it may not fully represent the broader community.

Conclusions

The prevalence of ESBL-PKP and CPKP was high in humans and hospital environments, while the prevalence was low in animals and animal clinic environments. All animals, 93% human, and three-fourths of environmental K. pneumoniae isolates were MDR. Furthermore, more than 82% of MDR isolates from humans, wastewater, and bedside table surfaces in hospitals were resistant to eight or more antibiotics. Nearly all CPKP isolates were resistant to reserved antibiotics (CZA and MEV). Being a neonate was identified of a predictor for K. pneumoniae culture-positive result. The hospital infection prevention and control team should implement targeted cleaning on the hospital environment, mainly bedside tables, together with hospital wastewater treatment, to reduce a highly contaminated hospital environment with the well-known nosocomial MDR pathogen K. pneumoniae. The Ethiopian Ministry of Health, Ministry of Agriculture, and the Environment, Forest and Climate Change Commission, together with regional stakeholders, should take concert steeps to fully implement the One Health-based AMR prevention and containment strategic plan41. We recommend further studies using high-resolution molecular techniques to identify ESBL and carbapenemase-encoding gene types and variants and identify the transmission dynamics across the human-animal-environment interface.
