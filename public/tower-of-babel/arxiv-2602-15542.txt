Homeomorphisms between compact subsets of real numbers
Sławomir Kusiński, Szymon Plewik

Abstract
. We present here a reduction of properties (invariants) of compact sets of real numbers to properties of countable orders. Discussed here is also an embedding property of some compact sets that are called t ℝ \mathbb{R} -sets. Among others, it is proved that there are exactly ω 1 \omega_{1} many non-homeomorphic t ℝ \mathbb{R} -sets.

Introduction

The preliminary purpose of this paper is to collect reductions of properties of compact subsets of real numbers to properties of countable orders. In this respect, the paper is a continuation of the research discussed in
[
1
]
. Relevant invariants are applied to the so-called tame sets in
\mathbb{R}
, which allows for their full classification up to homeomorphism.

If
Y
is a closed subset of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
, then any component of its complement is called a
Y
-
gap
and any non-trivial component of
Y
is called a
Y
-
interval
. An endpoint
e
of a
Y
-interval

is called

whenever

e\in\operatorname{int}_{Y}I
: in other words
e\notin\operatorname{cl}_{Y}(Y\setminus I)
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{Y}
,
\mathcal{B}_{Y}
,
\mathcal{C}_{Y}
and
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
be the families of all
Y
-gaps, all
Y
-intervals with no free endpoint, all
Y
-intervals with exactly one free endpoint and all
Y
-intervals with two free endpoints, respectively.
The family

\mathcal{Q}_{Y}=\mathcal{G}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{B}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{C}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{D}_{Y}

is countable and consists of pairwise disjoint intervals. We will use the abbreviation
\mathcal{I}_{Y}=\mathcal{B}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{C}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{D}_{Y}
and we will equip
\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
with an order

x\in I<J\ni y\Leftrightarrow x<y,

where

denotes inequality between intervals belonging to

The (linearly) ordered space

has some obvious properties. For example, when

\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
exist and belong to
\mathcal{G}_{Y}
. Also, between any two different

Y

A set

if there exists
K

\mathcal{G}
such that
I<K<J

for any

and
J
in
\mathcal{B}
for which
I<J
. Clearly, the family of all
Y
-gaps interlaces the family of all
Y
-intervals.

Recall that a set

is regularly closed if and only if
Y
is closed and the union of all
Y
-intervals is dense in
Y
.
As noted in
[
1
]
, some topological properties of a regularly closed subset

can be described by properties of the ordered space

. This includes results from
[

such as Theorem 9, Proposition 10, Corollaries 12, 13 and 15, and Lemma 16.

2.
Reverse reduction

Suppose that a countable ordered space
(\mathcal{Q},<)
is given such that
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{I}
and

\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{I}=\emptyset
. Analyse what we have to assume to determine a compact subset
Y\subset\mathbb{R}
and an isomorphism
F:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
such that
F[\mathcal{I}]=\mathcal{B}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{C}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{D}_{Y}
. Undoubtedly a necessary condition is that if
I
and
J
belong to
\mathcal{I}
, then there exists
K
in
\mathcal{G}
lying between
I
and
J
, in other words
\mathcal{G}
interlaces
\mathcal{I}
.

Recall that Cantor’s isomorphism theorem states that any countable dense unbounded orders are isomorphic. It follows that the rational numbers belonging to the interval
(0,1)
are isomorphic to any countable and unbounded densely ordered space. Now, let us adopt the Dedekind-completion method. Namely, let

(\mathcal{Q},<)
be an ordered space. A cut
(A,B)
is a partition of
\mathcal{Q}
into two sets
A
and
B
, such that each element of
A
is less than every element of
B
, and
A
contains no greatest element. If
B
has the smallest element, then
(A,B)
corresponds to this element,
i.e., if
b=\inf B
, then
b=(A,B)
. Otherwise,
(A,B)
defines the unique element such that
A
contains elements less than
(A,B)
, and
B
contains elements greater than
(A,B)
. The family
\mathfrak{C}
, which consists of all cuts
(A,B)
, is ordered by the relation

(A,B)<(D,E)\Leftrightarrow A\subset D.

So, if
(\mathcal{Q},<)
is countable and its order is dense and unbounded, then
(\mathfrak{C},<)
is isomorphic to the unit interval
[0,1]
. With such an isomorphism
(\emptyset,\mathcal{Q})
,
(\mathcal{Q},\emptyset)

and
\mathcal{Q}
pass to
0
,
1
and the rational numbers from
(0,1)
, respectively.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{I},<)
be a countable ordered space such that the sets
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{I}
are disjoint. If
\mathcal{G}
interlaces
\mathcal{I}
, and the smallest and the largest element of
\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{I}
belong to
\mathcal{G}
, then there exist a compact subset
Y\subset\mathbb{R}
and the isomorphism
F\colon\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
such that
F[\mathcal{G}]=\mathcal{G}_{Y}
and

F[\mathcal{I}]=\mathcal{I}_{Y}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{Q}
be the set of all rational numbers. Equip

\mathbb{X}=(\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{I})\times\mathbb{Q}

with the lexicographic order and then apply the Dedekind-completion method. Due to Cantor’s isomorphism theorem
\mathbb{X}
, with the lexicographic order, is isomorphic to the rational numbers, so its completion
\mathfrak{C}
is isomorphic to the unit interval
[0,1]
. Let
H\colon\mathfrak{C}\to[0,1]
be such an isomorphism. Put

Y=H\left[\mathfrak{C}\setminus\left(\bigcup\{(i_{J},s_{J})\colon J\in\mathcal{G}\}\cup\{\min\mathfrak{C},\max\mathfrak{C}\}\right)\right]\subseteq[0,1],

where
i_{J}
and
s_{J}
are the left and the right endpoints of the interval
\operatorname{cl}_{\mathfrak{C}}(\{J\}\times\mathbb{Q})
, respectively.
Define the isomorphism
F:\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
as follows.

F(J)=\begin{cases}(\leftarrow,\min Y),&\mbox{ for }J=\min\mathcal{G},\\
(\max Y,\rightarrow),&\mbox{ for }J=\max\mathcal{G},\\
H[(i_{J},s_{J})],&\mbox{ for any other }J\in\mathcal{G},\\
H[[i_{J},s_{J}]],&\mbox{ for any }J\in\mathcal{I}.\end{cases}

By the definition we have
F[\mathcal{G}]=\mathcal{G}_{Y}
.
For any
J\in\mathcal{I}
, we have that
H[[i_{J},s_{J}]]=F(J)
is a
Y
-interval, since
\mathcal{G}
interlaces
\mathcal{I}
. Therefore
F[\mathcal{I}]=\mathcal{B}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{C}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{D}_{Y}
. Clearly,
F
is an increasing bijection.
∎

Add to the above proof that
F(J)\in\mathcal{B}_{Y}
if and only if any
I\in\mathcal{G}
is neither an immediate predecessor nor an immediate successor of
J
,
and also
F(J)\in\mathcal{D}_{Y}
if and only if in
\mathcal{G}
there exist both the immediate predecessor and the immediate successor of
J
,
but
F(J)\in\mathcal{C}_{Y}
if and only if in
\mathcal{G}
there exists either the immediate predecessor or the immediate successor of
J
. But if
I
and
J
are in
\mathcal{G}_{Y}
and
I
is the immediate predecessor of
J
, then there is
y\in Y
such that
y
is the right endpoint of the interval
I
and it is also the left endpoint of the interval
J
. Hence we have that if
\mathcal{I}_{Y}=\mathcal{B}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{C}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{D}_{Y}
, then the ordered space
(\mathcal{G}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{I}_{Y},<)
allows us to reconstruct the set
Y
up to a homeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
X
and
Y
be compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
such that there exists an isomorphism
f\colon\mathcal{Q}_{X}\to\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
satisfing
f[\mathcal{G}_{X}]=\mathcal{G}_{Y}
. Then there exists an increasing homeomorphism
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
so that
F[X]=Y
.

Proof.

If
x\in(-\infty,\inf X)
, then put
F(x)=x-\inf X+\inf Y
. If
x\in(\sup X,+\infty)
, then put
F(x)=x-\sup X+\sup Y
.
If
x\in(a,b)=I\in\mathcal{G}_{X}
or
x\in[a,b]=I\in\mathcal{I}_{X}
, then put
f(I)=(c,d)
(resp.
f(I)=[c,d]
) and

F(x)=\frac{x-a}{b-a}(d-c)+c.

But, if
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{Q}_{X}
, then put

F(x)=\sup\bigcup\{f(I)\colon I\in\mathcal{Q}_{X}\mbox{ and }I<x\}.

By the definition, if
I\in\mathcal{I}_{X}
, then
f(I)=F[I]\in\mathcal{I}_{Y}
and if
I\in\mathcal{G}_{X}
, then
f(I)=F[I]\in\mathcal{G}_{Y}
. As
f
is an isomorphism, then any cut
(A,B)
with respect to
\mathcal{Q}_{X}
, is mutually uniquely assigned to the cut
(f[A],f[B])
with respect to
\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
. This implies that
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an increasing homeomorphism and
F[X]=Y
.
∎

The above theorems encouraged us to introduce and investigate a variant of homogeneity of subsets of
\mathbb{R}
, discussing properties of t

-sets which are introduced here. For those purposes, a following alternative form of the Cantor isomorphism theorem seems to be useful.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the set of all rational numbers ordered by the natural inequality
<
. Assume that

\mathcal{Q}=A_{0}\cup A_{1}\cup\ldots=B_{0}\cup B_{1}\cup\ldots,

where sets
A_{n}
are pairwise disjoint and sets
B_{n}
are also pairwise disjoint.
If each of the sets
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
interlaces
\mathcal{Q}
, then there exists an isomorphism
f\colon\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}
such that
f[A_{n}]=B_{n}
for each
n>0
.

Proof.

The set of rational numbers is countable, so we identify the rational numbers with the natural numbers, which shortens the notation when we adopt the back-and-forth method.

Step
0:
If
0\in A_{k}
, then let
f(0)\in B_{k}
. If
0\in B_{m}
,
then choose
f^{-1}(0)\in A_{m}
, so that the function
\{(0,f(0)),(f^{-1}(0),0)\}
is increasing.

Step
n:
Suppose that the image
f[\textbf{n}]
and the pre-image

f^{-1}[\textbf{n}]
, where
\textbf{n}=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, are already defined. If
f(n)
is not defined, then there is
k
such that
n\in A_{k}
. In that case, since
B_{k}
interlaces
\mathcal{Q}
, choose
f(n)\in B_{k}\setminus(f[\textbf{n}]\cup\textbf{n})
, so that the part of the function
f
that has been defined so far is an increasing function. In turn, if
f^{-1}(n)
is not defined, then there is
k
such that

n\in B_{k}
. Since
A_{k}
interlaces
\mathcal{Q}
, choose
f^{-1}(n)\in A_{k}\setminus(f^{-1}[\textbf{n}]\cup\textbf{n+1})
, so that the part of the function
f
that has been defined so far is an increasing function.

By the principle of induction, the function
f\colon\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}
is an isomorphism such that
f[A_{k}]=B_{k}
for each
k
.
∎

Due to the Cantor isomorphism theorem, Lemma

is also fulfilled when we substitute a countable, densely ordered space without the smallest and the largest element for the rational numbers. In other words, it is enough to assume that a non-empty ordered space

Lemma 4
.

Let
(X,<)
be an ordered space and let a countable subset
Y\subseteq X
interlace
X\setminus Y
. If
\mathcal{G}(X)\subseteq Y
, then
(X,<)
is isomorphic to a subset of the unit interval
[0,1]
.

Proof.

If there exists
\sup X
, then we can assume
\sup X\in Y
. Fix an increasing injection
f\colon Y\to[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
, where
\mathbb{Q}
denotes the set of rational numbers. If
x\in X
, then
let

B_{x}^{*}=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}\colon\exists_{y\in Y}\;x\leqslant y\mbox{ and }f(y)\leqslant q\}.

We say that
y\in Y
witnesses that
q\in B^{*}_{x}
, if
x\leqslant y
and
f(y)\leqslant q
. For example,
\sup X
witnesses
q\in B^{*}_{x}
for any
q\in\mathbb{Q}\cap[1,\rightarrow)
. But when
q<0
, then no
y\in Y
witnesses that
q\in B^{*}_{x}
, i.e.
q\notin B^{*}_{x}
. Let

B_{x}=\begin{cases}B^{*}_{x}\cup\{\inf B^{*}_{x}\},&\mbox{ if }\inf B^{*}_{x}\in\mathbb{Q},\\
B^{*}_{x},&\mbox{ else }\end{cases}

and put
A_{x}=\mathbb{Q}\setminus B_{x}.
There is no greatest element in
A_{x}
, and also
B^{*}_{x}\ni q_{1}<q_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}
implies
q_{2}\in B^{*}_{x}
, so any
(A_{x},B_{x})
is a cut. When
X\ni x_{1}<x_{2}\in X
and
y\in Y
witnesses that
q\in B^{*}_{x_{2}}
, then
y
also witnesses that
q\in B^{*}_{x_{1}}
, hence
B^{*}_{x_{2}}\subseteq B^{*}_{x_{1}}
.

We assume
\mathcal{G}(X)\subseteq Y
, so after a simple check we get that if
X\ni x_{1}<x_{2}\in X
, then there exists

p\in Y
such that
x_{1}\leqslant p<x_{2}
. Then
p
witnesses that
f(p)\in B^{*}_{x_{1}}
. Let’s assume that
t\in Y
witnesses that
f(p)\in B^{*}_{x_{2}}
, i.e.
x_{2}\leqslant t
and
f(t)\leqslant f(p)
. On the other hand,
f
is increasing and
p<x_{2}\leqslant t
, i.e.
f(p)<f(t)
, which leads to a contradiction that shows that
x\mapsto(A_{x},B_{x})
is a desired isomorphism.
∎

Not every isomorphism from an ordered space into the interval
[0,1]
is continuous with respect to the order topology and the topology hereditary from
[0,1]
, so Lemma
4
requires strengthening.

Proposition 5
.

If
(X,<)
is an ordered space, a countable subset
Y\subseteq X
interlaces
X\setminus Y
and
\mathcal{G}(X)\subseteq Y
, then there exists an increasing homeomorphism
\varphi\colon X\to h[X]\subseteq[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
f\colon X\to[0,1]
be an increasing injection as in Lemma
4
. Arrange the points of discontinuity
f
into a sequence
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots
and put
X_{0}=f[X]
and
\varphi_{0}=f.

Assume that isomorphisms
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}
are already defined such that the points of discontinuity of
\varphi_{n}\colon X\to\varphi_{n}[X]=X_{n}
are the points
a_{n+k}
, where
0\leqslant k
. Put

p_{n}=\sup\varphi_{n}[(\leftarrow,a_{n})]
,
q_{n}=\inf\varphi_{n}[(a_{n},\rightarrow)]
and

\varphi_{n+1}(x)=\begin{cases}\varphi_{n}(x)+\varphi_{n}(a_{n})-p_{n},&\mbox{ if }x<\varphi_{n}(a_{n}),\\
\varphi_{n}(x)+\varphi_{n}(a_{n})-q_{n},&\mbox{ if }x>\varphi_{n}(a_{n}),\\
\varphi_{n}(a_{n}),&\mbox{ if }x=\varphi_{n}(a_{n}).\end{cases}

As a result,
\varphi_{n+1}\colon X\to X_{n+1}=\varphi_{n+1}[X]
is an isomorphism with points of discontinuity
a_{n+k}
, where
0<k
.

Thus
\varphi=\lim\limits_{n\to+\infty}\varphi_{n}
is a continuous isomorphism. Since the inverse of any isomorphism into the interval
[0,1]
is continuous, then
\varphi
is as we need.∎

3.
Tame sets in
\mathbb{R}
: some useful lemmas and examples

We say that a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is
tame
in
\mathbb{R}
(in short a t
\mathbb{R}
-set), when, for any copy
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}
of
A
, there exists a homeomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

such that
f[A]=Y
.
Clearly, any finite subset of
\mathbb{R}
is a t
\mathbb{R}
-set.
The same applies to unions of finitely many closed intervals.

If
A\subset\mathbb{R}
consists of two isolated points and a closed interval, then
A
is not a t
\mathbb{R}
-set. If
B\subset\mathbb{R}
consists of two disjoint closed intervals and an isolated point, then
B
is not a t
\mathbb{R}
-set. If a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
has exactly one limit point, then it is not a t
\mathbb{R}
-set, as it both has copies that are monotonic and not monotonic sequences with their limits. It is also easy to check that when
X\subset\mathbb{R}
is compact, contains exactly one isolated point, contains a limit point, and
\mathcal{I}_{X}=\emptyset
, then
X
is not a t
\mathbb{R}
-set.

Proposition 6
.

Every t
\mathbb{R}
-set is compact.

Proof.

Any homeomorphism of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
transforms unbounded sets onto unbounded sets. But any non-compact subset
of
\mathbb{R}
has a bounded copy, as well as an unbounded copy. These two properties rule out that a non-compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
is a t
\mathbb{R}
-set. ∎

The next corollary is essentially an alternative definition of tame sets in
\mathbb{R}
.

Corollary 7
.

A set
X\subset\mathbb{R}
is a t
\mathbb{R}
-set if and only, if for every copy
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}
of
X
, there exists a monotonic bijection
f\colon\mathcal{Q}_{X}\to\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
such that
f[\mathcal{I}_{X}]=\mathcal{I}_{Y}
.

Proof.

In fact, theorems
1
and
2
give the thesis. Namely,
when
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a homeomorphism such that
F[X]=Y
, put
f(I)=F[I]
for each
I\in\mathcal{Q}_{X}
, and then check that
f\colon\mathcal{Q}_{X}\to\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
is a monotonic bijection such that
f[\mathcal{I}_{X}]=\mathcal{I}_{Y}
.

But when
f\colon\mathcal{Q}_{X}\to\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
is an increasing (resp. decreasing) bijection such that
f[\mathcal{I}_{X}]=\mathcal{I}_{Y}
, then
let
F|_{I}
be an increasing (resp. decreasing) bijection from
I
onto
f(I)
for
I\in\mathcal{Q}_{X}
. So

\bigcup\{F|_{I}\colon I\in\mathcal{Q}_{X}\}

is a monotonic bijection between dense subsets of
\mathbb{R}
, which uniquely extends to a homeomorphism

F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
F[X]=Y
. ∎

We will call the mapping

f(x)=\begin{cases}a+b-x,&\mbox{ if }x\in[a,b];\\
x,&\mbox{ else }\end{cases}

the
rotation
of the interval
[a,b]
. Note that, if
X\subset\mathbb{R}
is a compact set and
\{a,b\}\cap X=\emptyset
, then
f|_{X}\colon X\to f[X]
is a homeomorphism such that if
J\in\mathcal{C}_{X\cap[a,b]}
has the left (resp. right) endpoint free, then the interval
f[J]\in\mathcal{C}_{f[X]}
has the right (resp. left) endpoint free. The next lemma specifies the content of
[
1
, Remark 8]
.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose
X\subset\mathbb{R}
is a compact set and
\emptyset\not=\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{X}
. Then there exists a homeomorphism
f\colon X\to f[X]\subset\mathbb{R}
such that the image
f[J]
is an
f[X]
-interval with the left endpoint free if and only if
J\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Let
J_{0},J_{1},\ldots
be a sequence of all
X
-intervals from
\mathcal{C}_{X}
.
If
J_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
has the left endpoint free or
J_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{X}\setminus\mathcal{C}
has the right endpoint free, then put
f_{0}=\operatorname{id}_{X}
.
Otherwise, choose numbers
q_{0}
and
p_{0}
not belonging to
X
such that
J_{0}\subset(q_{0},p_{0})
. Let
f_{0}
be the rotation of
[q_{0},p_{0}]
. We get that
f_{0}|_{X}\colon X\to X_{0}=f_{0}[X]
is a homeomorphism.

Assume that the bijections

A set

The space
(S([0,1]),<)
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{B},<)
,

The space
(S(\mbox{The Cantor set}),<)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{D}
,

The space
(S(\mbox{$M$-Cantorval}),<)
is isomorphic to an
M
-Cantorval.

Spaces
\mathbb{A}
,
\mathbb{B}
,
\mathbb{D}
and
M
-cantorvals are compact, so the isomorphisms mentioned above are also homeomorphims.

The operation
X\mapsto S(X)
can be iterated, namely if
\alpha<\omega_{1}
is not a limit ordinal, then put
S_{0}(X)=X
and
S_{\alpha+1}(X)=S(S_{\alpha}(X))
. But if
\alpha<\omega_{1}
is a limit ordinal, then let
\alpha^{*}
be a union of two copies of
\alpha
, one decreasing contained in
(0,\rightarrow)
, the other increasing contained in
(\leftarrow,0)
. For example,
\omega_{0}^{*}
is a copy of
\{\frac{1}{n}\colon n\neq 0\}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Put

S_{\alpha}(X)=\bigcup\{\{\gamma\}\times S_{\gamma}(X)\colon\gamma\in\alpha^{*}\}\cup\{0\}\times[0,1],

and then equip
S_{\alpha}(X)
with the lexicographical order.

For the purposes of the next theorems, let
\textbf{X}_{0}=[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\textbf{X}_{\alpha}\subset\mathbb{R}
be an isomorphic copy of
S_{\alpha}([0,1])
, because of Proposition
5
, we can assume that
\textbf{X}_{\alpha}
is compact. Note that
\textbf{X}_{\alpha}=\bigcup\mathcal{I}_{\textbf{X}_{\alpha}}\subset\mathbb{R}
is regularly closed. When we consider
\mathcal{I}_{\textbf{X}_{\alpha}}
with the order topology, then it is a scattered space and its
\alpha
-th derivative is a singleton.

Theorem 23
.

For
\gamma<\omega_{1}
, if
Y\subset\mathbb{R}
is a homeomorphic copy of
\textbf{X}_{\gamma}
, then there exists an isomorphism
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
F[Y]=\textbf{X}_{\gamma}
.

Proof.

When
\gamma=0
, then
\textbf{X}=[0,1]
and
Y=[q,p]\subset\mathbb{R}
, so any homeomorphism
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
F(q)=0
and
F(p)=1
is good.

When
\beta+1=\gamma
, then
\mathcal{I}^{(\gamma)}_{\textbf{X}_{\gamma}}=\{[0,1]\}
and let
\mathcal{I}^{(\gamma)}_{Y}=\{[q,p]\}
. Choose numbers
a_{n},b_{n},c_{n}
and
d_{n}
, belonging to
\mathbb{R}\setminus Y
, so that

a_{1}<b_{1}<a_{2}<\dots<b_{n}<a_{n+1}<\ldots<q
,

c_{1}>d_{1}>c_{2}>\dots>d_{n}>c_{n+1}>\ldots>p
,

there is exactly one interval belonging to
\mathcal{I}^{(\beta)}_{Y}\setminus\{[q,p]\}
which is contained in any interval
[a_{n},b_{n}]
(resp.
[d_{n},c_{n}]
),

\bigcup\mathcal{I}^{(\beta)}_{Y}\setminus[q,p]\subset\bigcup\{[a_{n},b_{n}]\colon n>0\}\cup\bigcup\{[d_{n},c_{n}]\colon n>0\}
.

Analogously, choose numbers belonging to
\mathbb{R}\setminus\textbf{X}_{\gamma}
, taking
0
for
q
and
1
for
p
. Then fix increasing isomorphism
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
so that

F(0)=q
,
F(1)=p
and

\{F[I]\colon I\in\mathcal{I}^{(\beta)}_{Y}\}=\mathcal{I}^{(\beta)}_{\textbf{X}_{\beta}}=\mathcal{B}^{(\beta)}_{\textbf{X}_{\beta}},

assuming inductively that every restriction of
F
to
[a_{n},b_{n}]\cup[d_{n},c_{n}]
is continuous and increasing, and such that

F[[a_{n},b_{n}]\cup[d_{n},c_{n}]]\cap Y=\textbf{X}_{\beta}\cap([F(a_{n}),F(b_{n})]\cup[F(d_{n}),F(c_{n})]),

we get that
F
is a desired homeomorphism.

Now assume that
\gamma
is a countable limit ordinal and
\mathcal{I}^{(\gamma)}_{Y}=\{[q,p]\}
. For each
\alpha<\gamma
, let
I_{\alpha}=\inf\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha)}_{Y}
and
J_{\alpha}=\sup\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha)}_{Y}
. Choose numbers
a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha},c_{\alpha}
and
d_{\alpha}
, belonging to
\mathbb{R}\setminus Y
, so that

a_{1}<b_{1}<a_{2}<\dots<b_{\alpha}<a_{\alpha+1}<\ldots<q
,

c_{1}>d_{1}>c_{2}>\dots>d_{\alpha}>c_{\alpha+1}>\ldots>p
,

I_{\alpha}\subset[a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha}]
and
J_{\alpha}\subset[d_{\alpha},c_{\alpha}]
for each
\alpha<\gamma
.

Analogously, choose numbers belonging to
\mathbb{R}\setminus\textbf{X}_{\gamma}
, taking
0
for
q
and
1
for
p
. Then fix increasing isomorphism
F\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
so that

F(0)=q
,
F(1)=p
and
F[I_{\alpha}]=\inf\mathcal{B}^{(\alpha)}_{\textbf{X}_{\gamma}}=\inf\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha)}_{\textbf{X}_{\gamma}}
, and
F[J_{\alpha}]=\sup\mathcal{B}^{(\alpha)}_{\textbf{X}_{\gamma}}=\sup\mathcal{I}^{(\alpha)}_{\textbf{X}_{\gamma}}
. Assuming inductively that every restriction of
F
to
[a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha}]\cup[d_{\alpha},c_{\alpha}]
is continuous and increasing, and such that

F[[a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha}]\cup[d_{\alpha},c_{\alpha}]]\cap Y=\textbf{X}_{\gamma}\cap([F(a_{\alpha}),F(b_{\alpha})]\cup[F(d_{\alpha}),F(c_{\alpha})]),

we get that
F
is a desired homeomorphism.
∎

Corollary 24
.

There exists at least
\omega_{1}
many t
\mathbb{R}
-sets regularly closed and compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
, which are not homeomorphic.

Proof.

The family
\{\textbf{X}_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\omega_{1}\}
consists of uncountably many regularly closed and compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
such that
\beta<\alpha
implies that
\mathcal{I}^{(\beta)}_{\textbf{X}_{\beta}}
is a singleton and
\mathcal{I}^{(\beta)}_{\textbf{X}_{\alpha}}
is infinite, therefore
\textbf{X}_{\beta}
and
\textbf{X}_{\alpha}
are not homeomorphic. ∎

Theorem 25
.

There exists at most
\omega_{1}
many t
\mathbb{R}
-sets, which are not homeomorphic.

Proof.

Suppose
Y
is a t
\mathbb{R}
-set such that
\mathcal{Q}_{Y}
is scattered and infinite.

If there exists an isolated point in
Y
, then
Y^{(1)}
is a regularly closed t
\mathbb{R}
-set. Indeed, arguing as in the proofs of Lemma
9
and Corollary
10
, we get that
\mathcal{I}_{Y}=\mathcal{B}_{Y}=\mathcal{I}_{Y^{(1)}}
. So,
Y\setminus Y^{(1)}
is an open discrete subset of
Y
. Each
Y^{(1)}
-gap intersected with
Y
consists of sequences, which converge to the endpoints of this gap. These properties are sufficient to check that
Y^{(1)}
is a regularly closed t
\mathbb{R}
-set.

Analogously, if there exists a closed-open subset of
Y
which is a copy of the Cantor set, then let
Y^{*}
be the union of all such closed-open subsets. Then
Y\setminus Y^{*}
is a regularly closed t
\mathbb{R}
-set. Indeed, by the definition,
Y\setminus Y^{*}
is a closed subset of
Y
. There are no isolated points in
Y
and
\mathcal{C}_{Y}=\mathcal{D}_{Y}=\emptyset
, hence

\mathcal{I}_{Y}=\mathcal{B}_{Y}=\mathcal{I}_{Y\setminus Y^{*}}
and
Y\setminus Y^{*}
is a regularly closed subset of
Y
. Each
(Y\setminus Y^{*})
-gap intersected with
Y
after enlarging by its endpoints is homeomorphic to the Cantor set. Just like before, these properties are sufficient to check that
Y\setminus Y^{*}
is a regularly closed t
\mathbb{R}
-set.

If
Y
is regularly closed, then there exists
\alpha<\omega_{1}
such that
Y
is the union of finitely many pairwise disjoint closed-open sets containing exactly one interval from
\mathcal{B}_{Y^{(\alpha)}}
. Using Lemma
22
, one can check, similarly to the proof of Theorem
23
, that each of such closed-open sets is homeomorphic to
\textbf{X}_{\alpha}
. Making such a check, we leave it to the readers, is enough to complete this proof.
∎

As it was noticed in
[
1
, Theorem 17]
there exist continuum many non-homeomorphic
L
-Cantovals. Each
L
-Cantoval is a compact subset of real numbers and the Continuum Hypothesis is undecidable within standard set theory, so Theorems
23
and
25
should be counted among the independent results.
