Abstract
The increasing complexity of software systems and the sophistication of cyber-attacks have underscored the critical need for effective automated vulnerability detection and repair systems. Traditional methods, such as static program analysis, face significant challenges related to scalability, adaptability, and high false-positive and false-negative rates. AI-driven approaches, particularly those using machine learning and deep learning models, show promise but are heavily reliant on the quality and quantity of training data. This paper introduces a novel framework designed to automatically introduce realistic, category-specific vulnerabilities into secure C/C++ codebases to generate datasets. The proposed approach coordinates multiple AI agents that simulate expert reasoning, along with function agents and traditional code analysis tools. It leverages Retrieval-Augmented Generation for contextual grounding and employs Low-Rank approximation of weights for efficient model fine-tuning. Our experimental study on 116 code samples from three different benchmarks suggests that our approach outperforms other techniques with regard to dataset accuracy, achieving between 89% and 95% success rates in injecting vulnerabilities at function level.

Background and Motivation

In this section, we introduce the fundamental concepts and challenges associated with software vulnerability injection to motivate our proposed approach. II-A

Properties of High-Quality Vulnerability Datasets

Effective training and rigorous benchmarking of AI-based vulnerability detection and repair methods critically depend on the quality of the underlying datasets. To clearly define the desired dataset characteristics, we introduce the ABCD criteria, similar to
[
1
]
and
[
25
]
:

•

Accurate
: Labels must reflect ground truth with clear information on the type of vulnerability, specifically CWE identifiers and, when possible, precise vulnerability locations—essential for reliable model training and benchmark evaluation.

•

Big
: The dataset should be large enough to support training of deep models and enable statistically meaningful benchmarking of detection and repair systems.

•

Credible
: Code samples should reflect the structure and complexity of real-world software to ensure model generalization in practical settings.

•

Diverse
: The dataset should cover varied coding styles and vulnerability types to enable generalizable learning and comprehensive benchmarking across scenarios.

In practice, additional constraints improve training utility:

•

Each sample should contain a single vulnerability type to avoid ambiguity during training and evaluation.

•

Paired samples, benign and vulnerable versions, are ideal for repair tasks and contrastive learning.

II-C

Vulnerability Injection

We consider a code snippet
c
as an ordered sequence of lexical tokens
c=\langle c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}\rangle,\quad c_{i}\in T
, where
T
is the vocabulary of the programming language (e.g., operators, delimiters, identifiers, literals, keywords, punctuation), and the token sequence conforms to the language grammar.

We define a vulnerability injection operator:

\mathsf{inject}:T^{n}\times V\longrightarrow T^{m},\qquad\bigl(c^{b},v_{j}\bigr)\mapsto c^{\mathrm{v}}

(1)

which transforms a benign snippet
c^{b}
into a vulnerable snippet
c^{v}
exhibiting the vulnerability
v_{l}\in V
, where
V
is the set of existing vulnerability types.

Injection is performed through a finite set of token-level edits. Let
I=\{\,i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
be the set of positions to modify, and let each edit be defined by a function

\tau_{i}:T\longrightarrow T^{*},\quad\tau_{i}(c^{b}_{(i)})=\langle t^{(i)}_{1},\dots,t^{(i)}_{m^{(i)}}\rangle,

where
m^{(i)}\geq 0
, and
T^{*}
is the set of finite token sequences. This formulation captures:

•

Insertion:
New tokens are inserted before or after
c^{b}_{(i)}
:

\text{For~}m^{(i)}\geqslant 1;\begin{cases}\tau_{i}(c^{b}_{(i)})=\langle t^{(i)}_{1},\dots,t^{(i)}_{m^{(i)}},c^{b}_{(i)}\rangle,&\text{or}\\
\tau_{i}(c^{b}_{(i)})=\langle c^{b}_{(i)},t^{(i)}_{1},\dots,t^{(i)}_{m^{(i)}}\rangle.&\end{cases}

•

Deletion:

c^{b}_{(i)}
is removed entirely:

\quad\tau_{i}(c^{b}_{(i)})=\langle\rangle,\quad m^{(i)}=0.

•

Replacement:

c^{b}_{(i)}
is replaced by a new non-empty token sequence:

\tau_{i}(c^{b}_{(i)})=\langle t^{(i)}_{1},\dots,t^{(i)}_{m^{(i}}\rangle,\quad m^{(i)}\geqslant 1.

For positions
i\notin I
, we define
\tau_{i}(c^{b}_{(i)})=\langle c^{b}_{(i)}\rangle
, leaving the token unchanged.

The final injected sequence is obtained by flattening all transformed segments:

c^{v}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}(c^{b}_{(i)})=\langle c^{\prime}_{1},c^{\prime}_{2},\dots,c^{\prime}_{m}\rangle,

(2)

where
\bigoplus
denotes sequence concatenation.

A high-quality injection must satisfy the following properties:

•

Realism:
Edits
\{\tau_{i}\}
should reflect patterns observed in real-world vulnerabilities of class
v_{j}
(e.g. adapt to existing dataflow, meaningful output, no dead storage …).

•

Functional Integrity:

c^{v}
must remain syntactically and semantically valid, preserving the original functionality of
(c^{b})
, aside from the injected flaw.

•

Minimality:
The number of edits
|I|
should be kept minimal.

III
Proposed Framework

Existing vulnerability injection techniques continue to face fundamental limitations in both coverage and reliability. These methods generally support only a limited range of vulnerability types and exhibit low success rates in injection. This is largely due to their inability to adapt to the structural and contextual intricacies of real-world code found in production, as well as a lack of deep semantic reasoning.

To overcome these limitations, the proposed
AVIATOR
framework comprises two core modules: (1) a vulnerability injection module that utilizes retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and supervised fine-tuning with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to guide code transformations; and (2) a validation module that employs LLM-based discriminators and a static analyzer to meticulously verify the presence and accuracy of the injected vulnerabilities. These two modules are illustrated in Figure
1
.

In the following, we present the AVIATOR framework’s core components and methodologies. We first define AI agentic workflows, where specialized agents collaborate through structured execution paths to enhance modularity and error correction, mimicking cybersecurity experts’ analytical processes. We then detail the Vulnerability Injection Module of the workflow, which transforms secure code into vulnerable code using semantic analysis and advanced techniques like retrieval-augmented generation and fine-tuning. Additionally, we discuss the Validation Module, which ensures the accuracy and presence of injected vulnerabilities through a combination of deterministic tools and language model agents.

III-A

AI Agentic Workflows

Agentic workflows are systems in which a collection of agents are orchestrated to perform a complex task by following a predefined execution path
[
35
]
. Each agent is responsible for a specific action and transforms structured inputs into outputs that serve subsequent agents. Agents can be instantiated as large language models (LLMs) or deterministic programmatic tools (e.g., static analyzers).

Formally, we define an agentic workflow
W_{k}
of
k
agents as a directed execution graph, where each node represents an agent and edges denote conditional transitions. Each agent
A_{i}
transforms structured inputs into outputs, given by
A_{i}:X_{i}\times E\mapsto Y_{i}
, with
E
denoting external contextual artifacts (e.g., retrieved knowledge). Transitions depend on conditions evaluated dynamically from prior outputs.

This architectural paradigm offers advantages over monolithic reasoning or linear pipelines. First, it enables modular decomposition: the overall task is split into smaller, logically coherent subtasks aligned with the competencies of individual agents. This reduces the reasoning burden on any single component and improves interpretability. Second, it allows structured context propagation: each agent receives only the relevant inputs for its subtask, while outputs from earlier agents can be enriched with retrieval or validation results. Third, agentic workflows support corrective execution: if an agent detects inconsistencies or failures in prior outputs, the workflow can re-invoke earlier agents to revise decisions or recover from errors.

In AVIATOR, we implement an agentic workflow that mirrors the step-by-step reasoning of a cybersecurity analyst. The system orchestrates specialized agents responsible for tasks such as vulnerability selection, injection point identification, transformation synthesis, and post-injection validation, as detailed in
III-B
,
III-C
. This modular design promotes targeted decision-making, minimizes error propagation, and ensures that vulnerability injection proceeds in a systematic, verifiable, and context-aware manner.

III-B

Vulnerability Injection Module

The Vulnerability Injection Module in AVIATOR plays a crucial role by converting a benign code snippet into a version that includes a specific vulnerability. Central to the framework, this module ensures that the injected vulnerability is not only present but also realistic, minimal, and contextually coherent. It comprises the following components.

III-B
1
Semantic Analysis and Injection Point Identification

The process begins with semantic and structural analysis of the benign code. An LLM-based agent analyzes the function’s control and data flow to infer its purpose, input/output behavior, and internal logic (
Code Understanding
agent). It then qualitatively identifies potential
sources
—locations where untrusted input may enter the program—and
sinks
—locations where sensitive operations occur (
Sanitization Detector
agent). The agent also detects sanitization logic that would prevent the intended vulnerability from manifesting, such as bounds checks or input validation.

This process mirrors traditional symbolic static analysis and taint-tracking techniques
[
36
]
, but applied inversely: rather than proving the presence of a vulnerability, we infer the barriers that prevent it, and identify how they could be weakened or removed. This reverse reasoning is critical to selecting feasible and non-trivial injection points.

III-B
2
RAG-Based Vulnerability Injection with Fine-Tuning

In the following, we first explain the retrieval-augmented vulnerability generation, then detail the low-rank adaptation fine-tuning method, followed by supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning strategies.

Retrieval-Augmented Vulnerability Generation

Once candidate injection points are identified, an agent transforms the code to inject the target vulnerability (
Vulnerability Injector
agent). This agent operates under a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) setup. Given an input function
c^{b}
, the agent retrieves a set of similar benign/vulnerable pairs
\{c^{b_{(j)}},c^{v_{(j)}}\}_{j=1}^{k}
from a knowledge base using embedding-based similarity. Retrieved examples are aligned using line-level diffs, marking the injected edits.

This retrieved context alongside the analyzed output, is provided to the LLM to guide code transformation. This ensures that the injection edits reflect realistic code structure and vulnerability patterns observed in real-world examples.

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)

To specialize the vulnerability injection agent without incurring the computational cost of full model fine-tuning, we apply
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)

[
31
]
. This technique is particularly effective in settings where the base model is large, but the task-specific dataset is limited in size.

Rather than updating the full weight matrix
W_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
, LoRA learns a low-rank decomposition by injecting two trainable matrices
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}
, with
r\ll\min(d,k)
, into the model’s architecture:
W=W_{0}+BA
.

This reduces the number of trainable parameters while retaining the benefits of large-scale pretraining.

We explore two distinct fine-tuning paradigms for the vulnerability injection agent: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Both approaches operate on the same dataset of aligned benign/vulnerable function pairs:

\mathcal{Q}=\left\{\left(c^{b_{(j)}},\,\hat{c}^{v_{(j)}}\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{N}

(3)

where each benign function
c^{b_{(j)}}
is accompanied by a structured prompt
q^{(j)}
. The prompt is generated by prior workflow steps from
III-B1
, incorporating (1) semantic and structural analysis results of the benign code, (2) the original benign function itself, and (3) relevant examples from the Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) knowledge base, annotated to highlight vulnerability injection locations. The objective is for the agent to generate the corresponding vulnerable function
\hat{c}^{v}_{j}
given this prompt.

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

The vulnerability injection agent is first trained via supervised fine-tuning (SFT), using a standard autoregressive next-token prediction objective.
Let
q^{(j)}
denote the structured prompt. For example
c^{b_{(i)}}
and
\hat{\mathbf{c}}^{{v}_{i}}=\langle y^{(i)}_{1},\dots,y^{(i)}_{T^{(i)}}\rangle
the tokenized target vulnerable function of size
T^{(i)}
. We fine-tune the LoRA adapter parameters
\phi
by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of each next token under teacher forcing. The loss for a batch of size
B
is:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\phi)=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{t=0}^{T_{i}-1}\log P\bigl(y_{i,t+1}\,\bigm|\,\mathbf{x}_{i},\,y_{i,1},\dots,y_{i,t};\,\phi\bigr).

(4)

Here,
P(\cdot\mid\cdot;\phi)
is the autoregressive distribution defined by the LoRA-adapted LLM. This technique focuses on exact token-level matching with the aim that it will learn underlying concepts behind vulnerability injection, guided explicitly by ground-truth examples.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

While SFT optimizes for token-level accuracy, it does not directly focus on structural and semantic nuances of code. Therefore, we also apply reinforcement learning (RL) via
Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

[
37
]
, using the CodeBLEU
[
38
]
metric as a reward function.

GRPO is an efficient reinforcement learning technique that eliminates the need for a separate critic network by comparing multiple candidate outputs for each prompt and computing their relative rewards within a sampled group. Given a prompt
q
, we sample
G
candidate outputs
\{o_{1},o_{2},\dots,o_{G}\}
from the old policy model
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
. Each candidate output
o_{i}
receives a reward score
r(o_{i})
based on the CodeBLEU metric, which better captures structural and semantic properties of code. CodeBLEU combines four components:

•

N-gram match
: standard BLEU score over tokens.

•

Weighted n-gram match
: gives higher importance to syntax-critical tokens.

•

AST match
: measures similarity of abstract syntax trees.

•

Data-flow match
: compares data-flow graphs to assess semantic equivalence.

\text{CodeBLEU}=\alpha\cdot\text{BLEU}(n\text{-grams})+\beta\cdot\text{BLEU}_{w}(n\text{-grams})\\
+\gamma\cdot\text{Match}_{\text{AST}}+\delta\cdot\text{Match}_{\text{Data-flow}},

(5)

with
\alpha+\beta+\gamma+\delta=1
. By default,
\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\delta

[
38
]
.

The normalized advantage for each candidate output is computed as:

A_{i}=\frac{r(o_{i})-\mu}{\sigma},\quad\mu=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r(o_{j}),\quad\sigma=\text{std}\left(\{r(o_{j})\}_{j=1}^{G}\right).

(6)

GRPO optimizes the policy model
\pi_{\theta}
by maximizing the following clipped surrogate objective:

\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\;\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}(\min(r_{i}A_{i},\,\text{clip}(r_{i},1{-}\epsilon,1{+}\epsilon)A_{i})\right.\\
-\beta\,\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\parallel\pi_{\text{ref}}))\Bigg]

(7)

where
r_{i}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}\mid q)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i}\mid q)}
,
\epsilon
controls clipping for stable optimization, and
\beta
regulates the strength of the KL-divergence penalty with respect to a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
. Note that the expectation
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
is taken over the random variables
q
and
\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
, with
q\sim P(Q)
and
\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(O\mid q)
and that the KL-divergence penalty encourages policy updates that remain close to the pretrained model:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})=\sum_{o_{i}}\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i}|q)\log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i}|q)}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)}-1.

III-C

Validation Module

The validation module ensures that injected code
c^{v}
meaningfully introduces the intended vulnerability into the original benign function
c^{b}
. This process involves both deterministic tools and language model agents, and follows the workflow depicted in Figure
1
.

First, a
diff agent
computes a token-level difference between
c^{v}
and
c^{b}
(
Difference
agent). If the difference is trivial (e.g., limited to whitespace, comments, or reformatting), the workflow returns to the injection stage with corrective feedback (
Vuln. Injector Identical Code
agent).

If the change is substantive, a vulnerability verification agent (LLM-based) assesses whether the injected code actually reflects the vulnerability that was intended (
Diff. Analyzer
agent). This agent is provided with rich context, including the original function
c^{b}
, the modified function
c^{v}
, their annotated difference, the vulnerability class to inject, and a self-rationale generated by the injection agent, which explains what edits were made and why they introduce the vulnerability. This enables the agent to evaluate not just the presence of code edits, but also whether those edits semantically fulfill the injection objective.

The modified code is analyzed using
cppcheck
1
1

1

https://cppcheck.sourceforge.io/
. A validation agent then interprets its diagnostic report alongside prior context to assess whether the injected vulnerability aligns with the intended CWE (Vuln. Verifier).

If validation fails at this stage, the code is passed to a
refinement agent
, which revises the output based on prior feedback. The updated code is re-evaluated through the same pipeline. To ensure termination, the workflow is bounded by a fixed number of correction iterations: 10.

This hybrid validation strategy combines programmatic and rule-based tools, with LLM-based contextual reasoning, providing robust and interpretable guarantees over the correctness of injected vulnerabilities.

Instead, we use it for (1) fine-tuning the injection model, (2) constructing the retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) knowledge base, and (3) manually evaluating injection success on 45 randomly sampled pairs.

IV-B

Implementation Details

Agents

AVIATOR leverages various technologies and tools. Within the validation workflow, we utilized the static analysis tool
Cppcheck
version 2.17, due to its popularity and ease of integration.

We experimented with two base large language models for the AI agents in our workflow.
Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct

[
41
]
, is a specialized open-source coding LLM with 32 billion parameters. We additionally experimented with
Llama-4-Maverick

[
42
]
, a general-purpose, mixture-of-experts-based LLM with approximately 400 billion parameters (17 billion active parameters), to explore the impact of model size and specialization on the agents’ reasoning capabilities.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

We use
gte-Qwen2-1.5B-Instruct
for embedding vulnerable and benign function pairs in the RAG knowledge base. This embedder achieves top scores on the MTEB benchmark for embeddings of up to 8K tokens
[
43
]
. Since 99% of our training dataset from PrimeVul fits within this limit, we avoid chunking. Retrieval parameters were set at the recommended
k=4
.

Fine-Tuning Configurations

We fine-tune the vulnerability injection agent using 3.5k aligned pairs from the PrimeVul training set.
For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we trained for 5 epochs (loss convergence) with a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-4}
, batch size of 4, and LoRA ranks varying between 16 and 256.
For reinforcement learning with GRPO, we trained the model for 1 epoch (reward convergence), using a learning rate of
2\times 10^{-4}
, batch size of 8, and generating four completions per policy iteration. The KL regularization coefficient
\beta
(0.04) and clipping parameter
\epsilon
(0.2) followed the recommended default values from GRPO implementation using Adam optimizer.

Technology

AVIATOR is implemented using Python as well as HuggingFace’s Transformers and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning libraries. All experiments were run on NVIDIA A100 or H100 80GB GPUs.

IV-C

Ablation Study Design

We conduct an ablation study to quantify the contribution of each agentic component in the AVIATOR workflow. To assess the necessity and impact of this modular structure, we construct and evaluate six progressively simplified variants by incrementally removing components from the full workflow.

The complete AVIATOR pipeline presented in Figure
1
, showcases 13 distinct agents. These agents include tool-based nodes, LLM agents, and control logic designed to manage iterative processes.

The evaluated configurations are as follows:

•

1-Agent (Monolithic LLM),
W_{1}
: A single LLM receives the original function, vulnerability type, and retrieved examples, and is prompted to analyze the function, inject the vulnerability, and self-verify. This setting relies on common chain-of-thought prompting without agentic decomposition.

•

3-Agent,
W_{3}
: Decomposes the task into three distinct agents: (i) loading the vulnerability description, (ii) analyzing the function for injection points, and (iii) performing the injection. No verification or refinement stages are included.

•

5-Agent (Injection-Only Module),
W_{5}
: Implements the full injection module (Code Understanding, Information Loader, Sanitization Detector, Vulnerability Injector, Polisher) without validation (Figure
1
, top).

•

7-Agent,
W_{7}
: Adds the difference-checking loop, consisting of the Diff Agent and Diff Analyzer, allowing the system to detect and iteratively refine the code if no substantive change was introduced.

•

9-Agent,
W_{9}
: Adds the
cppcheck
static analysis tool and corresponding Verifier Agent to further validate the generated vulnerability and refine injection quality based on external tool feedback.

•

11-Agent,
W_{11}
: Incorporates the Diff Refiner agent after the difference is computed, allowing targeted edits based on diff feedback. This step improves alignment between injection goals and actual edits by making minimal context-aware adjustments.

•

13-Agent (Full AVIATOR),
W_{13}
: Adds a terminal Vulnerability Fixer Agent. This final pass ensures that injected vulnerabilities are realistic, contextually valid, and non-trivially removable—closely mimicking the post-injection review performed by security experts.

All configurations share the same injection model, prompt formatting, and RAG setup. We evaluate these variants on the same benchmark split (see Section 3, A) and report both formal and manual success metrics (see Section 3, D). This incremental comparison enables a fine-grained understanding of how individual agents affect the correctness, realism, and efficiency of the injection process.

IV-D

Evaluation and Metrics

We assess the success rate of vulnerability injection, as it directly indicates the expected label accuracy of datasets produced by our framework

Automated Evaluation

For compatible test cases
[
16
]
and
[
32
]
, we automatically measured vulnerability injection success using the formal verification tool
ESBMC
v7.5 (Efficient SMT-based Bounded Model Checker), recognized for its effectiveness in detecting vulnerabilities in bounded code contexts
[
33
,
44
]
. It implements state-of-the-art incremental Bounded Model Checking and
k
-induction proof-rule algorithms. Namely, if the solver reaches termination within a bound less than or equal to
k
, we can definitively prove the absence of software errors. We set the bound to 100 and timeout to 5 minutes.

Due to the probabilistic nature of LLMs used in AVIATOR, identical inputs may yield different outputs on repeated trials. Therefore, we generated 5 outputs for SARD100 and FormAI test cases. To rigorously evaluate AVIATOR’s performance, we employed two metrics:

•

Average Injection Success Rate (AISR)
: Measures the proportion of generated vulnerabilities correctly injected and verified, averaged across multiple runs. Denote
s_{i}
as the success rate in run
i
over
n
runs:

AISR_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i},\quad\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(s_{i}-\text{AISR})^{2}},

(9)

where
\sigma
denotes the empirical standard deviation.

•

Pass@k

[
45
]
: Estimates the probability that at least one of
k
independently sampled outputs is correct. If
c
is the number of correct outputs among
k
samples:

\text{Pass@}k=1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}},\quad\text{if }c<k;\quad\text{Pass@}k=1\text{ otherwise}

This metric captures the practical success likelihood under multi-sample inference.

These metrics provide insights into AVIATOR’s reliability and practical utility, reflecting its robustness and stability in automated vulnerability injection tasks.

Manual Evaluation

For PrimeVul, we manually validated a sample of 45 injected functions drawn uniformly at random, following prior work
[
21
,
10
,
29
,
46
]
. The evaluation was performed by three authors experienced in C/C++, including two senior security researchers. Each injected sample was categorized as (1) weakness present, (2) no weakness, or (3) undecidable—typically due to reliance on external functions or context beyond the injected function. Since we lack complete test cases for most programs, we assess the presence of weaknesses rather than confirmed vulnerabilities (which require demonstrable exploitability). The reported injection success rate reflects the percentage of samples found to contain a weakness, based only on those that could be conclusively assessed.

VI
Discussion

This section reflects on why AVIATOR works, its practical impact, limitations, and directions for future research.

VI-A

Why Does AVIATOR Work?

We identify the core design choices that explain AVIATOR’s effectiveness in vulnerability injection. These include its agentic decomposition, targeted model-level adaptation, iterative refinement capabilities, and integration of static analysis feedback.

(1)
AVIATOR benefits from agentic decomposition.
The injection task is divided among 13 specialized agents, each handling specific operations like vulnerability localization, injection, sanitization detection, or diff-based verification. This modular approach enhances accuracy significantly, as demonstrated by improvements from 31% to 91% on FormAI and from 84% to 94% on SARD100.

(2)
AVIATOR improves through targeted fine-tuning of a single critical agent.
Rather than retraining all models, we fine-tune only the Vulnerability Injection agent using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with LoRA. This lightweight adaptation requires less than 10 hours of training on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU and improves injection accuracy from 85% to 91% on FormAI. The model is trained on PrimeVul, a dataset entirely disjoint from the test sets, showing that this adaptation generalizes well across domains.

(3)
AVIATOR leverages iterative workflow refinement to improve injection robustness.
Agents operate in feedback loops to enhance injection robustness. For example, the Diff Agent triggers refinement cycles when necessary, boosting accuracy up to 10 points on FormAI and 4 points on SARD100.

(4)
AVIATOR incorporates traditional static analysis tools to validate injection quality.
The Verifier Agent uses
cppcheck
to detect whether the injected code violates real-world safety constraints. Its feedback is interpreted and used by subsequent agents to iteratively refine the injection.

VI-B

Limitations and Threats to Validity

Benchmark Scope and Generalizability

Our evaluation focuses on C/C++ using three datasets (SARD100, FormAI, and PrimeVul), which may not capture all real-world diversity. To mitigate this, we combined synthetic and real-world benchmarks of varying complexity.

Manual Evaluation Bias

The qualitative analysis of PrimeVul is based on 45 manually annotated samples, which may introduce subjectivity. We mitigated this by involving three security researchers and resolving disagreements via consensus.

Metric Stability Under Sampling

LLM generation is stochastic, which may affect reliability of metrics like injection success rate. To address this, we performed multiple independent runs and reported average scores
AISR_{k}
with standard deviations across all experiments and measured Pass@k with additional runs on the best configurations.

Generalization of Fine-Tuned Agent

Our supervised fine-tuning was performed solely on PrimeVul, raising a potential risk of overfitting to its code characteristics. However, PrimeVul includes a wide variety of real-world vulnerabilities across multiple domains. Moreover, the consistent performance improvements on structurally distinct benchmarks (SARD100 and FormAI) provided strong evidence of generalization beyond the training distribution.

VII
CONCLUSION

In this paper, we introduced AVIATOR, an AI agentic framework designed to systematically inject realistic and category-specific vulnerabilities into secure codebases. By leveraging a multi-agent pipeline that replicates the reasoning processes of cybersecurity experts, AVIATOR ensures that vulnerability injection is not only precise but also deeply contextually aware. This approach allows for a nuanced understanding and handling of vulnerabilities, closely mimicking the analytical depth and precision of human experts.

Our experimental evaluations, conducted across diverse benchmarks, demonstrate that AVIATOR seems to significantly outperform existing techniques, achieving high success rates in vulnerability injection. The modular design of AVIATOR, combined with cutting-edge techniques such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Low-Rank Adaptation, enables the creation of large-scale datasets. These datasets are not only accurately labeled but are diverse, making them very useful for training robust and effective vulnerability detection models.

The contributions of this work not only advance the state-of-the-art in automated vulnerability injection but also provide a scalable solution to the pressing challenge of data scarcity in software security research.

Future work will explore further enhancements in agentic workflows and fine-tuning strategies, as well as further evaluation on real-world code, to broaden the scope and applicability of AVIATOR in practical scenarios.
