Diagnostic yield and outcomes of hand surgery referrals to rheumatology
Authors: Ryan Seltzer, Sam Kavarana, Naadir Jamal, Logan Locascio, Reagan Mead, Kevin Byram, Jed Maslow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Hand and upper extremity complaints frequently prompt evaluation for systemic inflammatory conditions, leading surgeons to refer patients to rheumatology. However, referral patterns are highly variable and not standardized, and access to rheumatologic care is often limited by prolonged wait times and workforce constraints. Existing literature on rheumatology referrals has largely focused on access and disease-specific pathways, with little attention to referral patterns within surgical subspecialties. Concordance between referral indication and confirmed rheumatologic diagnosis is reported to be low, underscoring the need to better define which referral triggers demonstrate true diagnostic yield.Purpose:To quantify and characterize referrals from hand surgery to rheumatology and identify predictors of diagnostic yield.Study Design/Level of Evidence:Retrospective cohort/3.Methods:We analyzed 183 hand surgery referrals to rheumatology (2018-2024) in this retrospective cohort study. Referral reasons were classified as laboratory-based, history-based, examination-based, or imaging-based. Outcomes were categorized as “low-yield” (Group 1: no rheumatologic diagnosis), “intermediate-yield” (Group 2: diagnosed without need for therapy or follow-up), and “high-yield” (Group 3: diagnosed, requiring systemic therapy or specialty follow-up). Descriptive statistics and multivariable logistic regression (high-yield vs low-yield) were performed.Results:Sixty-six percent of referrals were evaluated. Laboratory abnormalities were the most common referral reason (40%), followed by history (27%), examination findings (24%), and imaging (9%). Thirty-five percent (95% confidence interval [CI], 26.4%–44.0%) of referrals were high-yield, while 49% (95% CI, 39.6%–58.0%) were low-yield and 17% (95% CI, 10.6%–24.6%) were intermediate-yield. Across major referral indications (antinuclear antibodies, synovitis, and polyarthralgia), low-yield outcomes exceeded high-yield outcomes. Imaging-based referrals were significantly more likely to be high yield than laboratory-based referrals (adjusted OR 6.1, 95% CI, 1.5–31.5, P = .016). Examination-based and history-based referrals were not associated with increased diagnostic yield, and age and sex were not independent predictors.Conclusions:One-third of referrals produced a high-yield rheumatologic diagnosis, whereas half resulted in no rheumatologic diagnosis. Laboratory-based and history-based referrals were common but low-yield, whereas those supported by radiographic findings were significantly more likely to be high-yield. Prioritizing referrals with objective clinical or imaging findings may improve diagnostic efficiency in the hand surgery population.

Introduction

Hand and upper extremity complaints are among the most frequent musculoskeletal issues seen in both primary care and specialty settings.1,2 When the history, examination, laboratory data, or imaging are suggestive of a systemic process, surgeons commonly refer patients to rheumatology to evaluate for autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis. Prompt identification and referral of these patients are critical because early initiation of pharmacotherapy improves outcomes.3,4 However, referral patterns to rheumatology are not standardized and remain highly variable.5,6 Amid rising patient volume and workforce limitations, optimizing subspecialty referral efficiency has become increasingly important.7 Wait times for a new rheumatology evaluation can exceed months, highlighting a need for more efficient triage and referral processes.4

Characterization of referral patterns to rheumatology remains limited in the existing literature, particularly within surgical subspecialties. The majority of studies address issues of access to care such as telehealth expansion, triage efficiency, or appropriateness of referrals.3,8–10 Other work has been disease-specific, focusing on ankylosing spondylitis, juvenile idiopathic arthritis, or psoriatic arthritis.11–14 Few studies have examined specialty-specific referral patterns (dermatology referrals for psoriasis).15 A single study addressed rheumatology referrals to hand surgery for systemic sclerosis, but to our knowledge, no prior study has investigated the converse: hand surgery referrals to rheumatology.16 Across available data, concordance between referral indication and confirmed rheumatologic diagnosis is poor (κ = 0.2–0.3), emphasizing the need to clarify which referral triggers demonstrate true diagnostic yield.17 For hand surgeons, distinguishing mechanical from inflammatory pathology can be particularly challenging, making it uncertain which referral cues best predict a confirmed rheumatologic diagnosis.18–20

Our primary aim was to quantify and describe institutional referral patterns between hand surgery and rheumatology. Our secondary, hypothesis-driven aim was to identify predictors of a high-yield diagnostic outcome. We hypothesized that referrals based on objective clinical and radiologic findings (eg, examination-confirmed synovitis and radiologic erosions) would be independently associated with a significantly higher likelihood of a confirmed rheumatologic diagnosis requiring subsequent therapy, whereas referrals based on nonspecific serologic markers (eg, isolated positive antinuclear antibodies [ANA]) or subjective symptoms (eg, polyarthralgia) would not.

Methods

Following institutional review board approval, a retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary academic medical center. Our cohort consisted of all patients referred from outpatient orthopaedic hand surgery clinics to rheumatology between 2018 and 2024. Referrals were identified using the institutional electronic health record, from which a comprehensive cohort of all rheumatology referrals originating from orthopaedic hand surgery was generated. The year 2018 was selected as the starting point to ensure consistent data capture following institutional transition between electronic health records. To avoid duplication, only the index referral per patient during the study period was included in the analysis. Exclusion criteria were (1) patient-initiated (rather than provider-initiated) rheumatology referrals, (2) referrals to providers outside the hospital network, and (3) referrals for previously established rheumatologic diagnoses. All data were manually reviewed and abstracted from the electronic health record to ensure accuracy of referral indication and outcome classification and stored in a secure REDCap database.17,18

For referrals that did not result in rheumatology evaluation, we reviewed appointment records, scheduling communication logs, and referral audit trails. Outcomes were classified as (1) patient-initiated noncompletion (eg, appointment cancellation and no-show), (2) provider-initiated closure (explicit rheumatology triage decline), or (3) administrative expiration. Referral reasons and outcomes were independently classified by 3 study authors using predefined criteria. Discrepancies in classification were resolved through group consensus between the 3 authors after joint review of the medical record. Referral documentation was also used to identify primary referral reasons for each patient including examination findings, laboratory abnormalities, subjective or historical features, and radiology results. Radiology-based referrals included imaging findings on radiographs, ultrasound, or MRI. Terms such as “polyarthralgia” and “synovitis” reflected clinician documentation in the chart rather than diagnostic codes. In cases where multiple referral reasons were documented, the primary indication was defined as the first one listed by the referring provider. Each patient's chart was reviewed longitudinally to accurately determine the final rheumatologic outcome. Referral outcomes were categorized into 3 groups: Group 1: no rheumatologic diagnosis (“low-yield”), Group 2: diagnosed but not requiring consistent follow-up or initiation of therapy (“intermediate-yield”), and Group 3: diagnosed and requiring consistent follow-up or initiation of therapy (“high-yield”). Groups 2 and 3 were further characterized by specific rheumatologic diagnosis and treatment regimens.

Proportions are presented with 95% confidence intervals. Multivariable logistic regression modeled the odds of a high-yield (Group 3) vs low-yield (Group 1) outcome (Table 1). Group 2 diagnoses, while clinically valid, did not require initiation of disease-modifying therapy by a rheumatologist and therefore were excluded from the final regression model of specialist diagnostic yield. Predictor variables included referral category (laboratory, history, examination, and radiology), with laboratory as the reference, and covariates were age and sex. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported; statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (2-sided). Missing data were rare and handled by complete-case analysis. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1; R Core Team) and Microsoft Excel (version 16.80).

Results

A total of 183 referrals were included from orthopaedic hand surgeons to rheumatologists of whom 121 (66%) were evaluated by a rheumatologist. Of the 62 referrals who did not result in evaluation, the majority were patient-initiated noncompletion (56%) with 40% due to administrative expiration. The remaining 4% were due to provider-initiated closure, although both of these were due to policy violation (request to change provider) as opposed to a medical etiology. The mean time to evaluation was 89 ± 95 days. The cohort included 124 women (68%) with a mean age of 48 years (SD = 17 years).

Table 2 summarizes the reasons for rheumatology referral and their corresponding outcome. Laboratory abnormalities were the most common reason for referral (40%), followed by history-based (27%), examination-based (24%), and radiology-based (9%) referrals. Radiologic findings prompting referral most commonly included erosive changes, edema, and arthritis, while laboratory abnormalities included positive ANA, CCP, rheumatoid factor, and elevated CRP. The most common referral indication was a positive ANA, representing 80% of laboratory-based referrals and 32% of all hand surgery referrals to rheumatology. This was followed by synovitis (82% of examination-based referrals and 20% of overall referrals) and polyarthralgia (67% of history-based referrals and 18% of overall referrals). Rates of rheumatology evaluation were similar for laboratory-based, examination-based, and radiology-based referrals (70%) but lower for history-based referrals (55%).

The most common outcome overall was Group 1 (no rheumatologic diagnosis), accounting for 49% (95% CI, 39.6%–58.0%) of evaluated referrals. Laboratory-based referrals accounted for half of all Group 1 outcomes. Group 2 (diagnosed but not requiring consistent follow-up or initiation of therapy) represented 17% (95% CI, 10.6%–24.6%) of outcomes, while Group 3 (diagnosed and requiring consistent follow-up or initiation of therapy) represented 35% (95% CI, 26.4%–44.0%). Examination-based referrals resulted in Group 1, Group 2, and Group 3 outcomes in 45%, 16%, and 39% of cases, respectively. Laboratory-based referrals resulted in Group 1, Group 2, and Group 3 outcomes in 58%, 15%, and 27% of cases, respectively. History-based referrals resulted in Group 1, Group 2, and Group 3 outcomes in 55%, 26%, and 26% of cases, respectively. Radiology-based referrals resulted in Group 1 outcomes in 25% of cases and Group 3 outcomes in 75% of cases, with no Group 2 outcomes observed. On multivariable analysis adjusting for age and sex, radiology-based referrals were significantly more likely to result in a Group 3 outcome than laboratory-based referrals (adjusted OR 6.1; 95% CI 1.5–31.5, P = .016; Table 1).

The rheumatology evaluation rate for ANA-specific referrals was 85% and resulted in Group 1, 2, and 3 outcomes 61%, 14%, and 25% of the time, respectively. The rheumatology evaluation rate for synovitis-specific referrals was 77% and resulted in Group 1, 2, and 3 outcomes 50%, 12%, and 38% of the time, respectively. The rheumatology evaluation rate for polyarthralgia-specific referrals was 78% and resulted in Group 1, 2, and 3 outcomes 43%, 33%, and 24% of the time, respectively.

Table 3 summarizes diagnoses stratified by outcome category. The most common diagnoses within Group 2 were osteoarthritis (25%) and fibromyalgia (25%). The most common diagnoses within Group 3 were rheumatoid arthritis (32%), psoriatic arthritis (15%), and unspecified inflammatory arthritis (22%). Certain diagnoses (eg, gout and psoriatic arthritis) appeared across outcome groups depending on disease severity and whether ongoing management was required.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study of hand surgery referrals to rheumatology demonstrates that most referrals are laboratory-initiated, predominantly due to ANA positivity. However, referrals based on objective radiologic findings, rather than nonspecific serologic abnormalities, best predict diagnostic yield. Only two-thirds of referred patients were ultimately evaluated by rheumatology, with average wait times approaching 3 months. This is similar to existing studies outside the field of hand surgery, with rheumatology referral wait times ranging 1–4 months.21–23 To our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize referral efficiency between hand surgery and rheumatology.

Only 34% of referrals resulted in a “high-yield” rheumatologic diagnosis requiring consistent follow-up or initiation of therapy. By contrast, half of all referrals resulted in no rheumatologic diagnosis, emphasizing the challenge of distinguishing inflammatory from noninflammatory conditions in this referral population. This finding aligns with prior studies demonstrating low concordance between referral reason and confirmed rheumatologic diagnosis among orthopaedic surgeons (κ = 0.27).17

In our sample, the most common reasons for referral to rheumatology were elevated ANA, the presence of synovitis on examination, and a history of polyarthralgia, representing common but diagnostically distinct clinical scenarios. Across these indications, the proportion of low-yield outcomes exceeded high-yield outcomes, indicating that many of these referrals did not ultimately result in a rheumatologic diagnosis. ANA warrants special mention given its prevalence in clinical practice and in the existing literature, as well as its predominance in our sample. Antinuclear antibodies are a group of autoantibodies directed against nuclear components that serve as markers for both autoimmune and nonimmunologic conditions.24 While some individual autoantibodies are highly specific such as anti–double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), a positive ANA titer is less discriminating overall, typically considered highly sensitive but poorly specific (SLE ≈ 20% specificity; positive titer present in up to 20% of healthy individuals).25–29 Though not a direct measure of specificity, our data further support this concept given 61% of ANA-specific referrals in our sample resulted in no rheumatologic diagnosis. Taken together, these findings suggest that ANA-based referrals in isolation have limited diagnostic yield and should be interpreted in the context of the comprehensive clinical picture.

Ultimately, an important role of the clinician is to properly indicate specialty referrals to improve efficiency and patient outcomes. In this context, referral optimization can be understood as reducing low-yield and intermediate-yield diagnoses (most commonly osteoarthritis and fibromyalgia in our sample) in favor of high yield diagnoses (most commonly rheumatoid and psoriatic arthritis in our sample). Osteoarthritis, one of the most common diagnoses in our cohort, illustrates the limitations of this classification system, because it may be represented across all outcome groups depending on clinical context—serving as a nonrheumatologic explanation for symptoms (Group 1), a diagnosis not requiring specialty management (Group 2), or a condition warranting active treatment (Group 3). Similarly, certain inflammatory conditions categorized as Group 2 in this study may still warrant follow-up or treatment in select cases, reflecting variability in disease presentation and provider decision-making. When viewed across referral categories, specificity can be approximated by the proportion of high-yield relative to low-yield outcomes. In this study, radiology-based referrals demonstrated the highest diagnostic yield and on multivariable analysis were significantly more likely to result in a confirmed rheumatologic diagnosis compared with other referral indications. Despite this strong and statistically significant outcome, the imprecision of this estimate due to the small subgroup size warrants caution, requiring validation in larger cohorts. In addition, the lack of statistical significance for examination-based referrals should not be misinterpreted as a true lack of association. Given the observed effect size, it is possible this result represents a Type II error due to limited power, and objective examination findings like synovitis remain a critical referral indication. Accordingly, prioritizing referrals based on objective findings such as radiographic abnormalities rather than nonspecific history or serologic data may improve overall efficiency and diagnostic yield of hand surgery referrals to rheumatology. Finally, while our classification framework defines “yield” based on diagnosis and need for therapy, it is important to recognize that exclusion of rheumatologic disease is itself a clinically valuable outcome because it may guide alternative management pathways and reduce unnecessary treatment.

This study has several limitations. As a single-institution study, particularly one involving a tertiary academic center, our sample may not be fully generalizable to community or private practice settings. Despite this, our ultimate diagnoses were common (eg, rheumatoid arthritis) suggesting that our sample was reasonably representative. Our sample size of 183 patients may limit the precision of subgroup comparisons compared with larger cohorts. Finally, classifying our referral reasons by a single, dominant rationale (eg, ANA or synovitis) may have sacrificed granularity in patients who were referred for multiple reasons. Future studies should use multicenter designs to improve sample diversity and size, as well as to develop predictive tools to further optimize rheumatologic referral efficiency.

Conclusion

This retrospective cohort study of hand surgery referrals to rheumatology demonstrates that referral yield is highly variable and strongly dependent on the underlying indication. Only one-third of referrals produced a high-yield rheumatologic diagnosis, while roughly half resulted in no rheumatologic diagnosis, reflecting considerable diagnostic inefficiency. Referrals prompted by ANA positivity or nonspecific symptoms were common but low-yield, whereas those supported by radiographic findings were significantly more likely to result in a high-yield outcome. Overall, prioritizing referrals with objective clinical or imaging findings may improve efficiency and patient care in the hand surgery population.
