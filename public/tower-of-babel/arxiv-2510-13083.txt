Abstract
Small regularizers can preserve linear programming solutions exactly. This paper provides the first average-case analysis of exact regularization: with a standard Gaussian cost vector and fixed constraint set, bounds are established for the probability that exact regularization succeeds as a function of regularization strength. Failure is characterized via the Gaussian measure of inner cones, controlled by novel two-sided bounds on the measure of shifted cones. Results reveal dimension-dependent scaling laws and connect exact regularization of linear programs to their polyhedral geometry via the normal fan and the Gaussian (solid-angle) measure of its cones. Computable bounds are obtained in several canonical settings, including regularized optimal transport. Numerical experiments corroborate the predicted scalings and thresholds.

Introduction

Regularization transforms ill-posed optimization problems into well-conditioned ones by adding penalty terms. These penalties provide uniqueness, stability under data perturbations, and algorithms with improved convergence. For linear programs, a remarkable phenomenon occurs: for sufficiently small regularization, solutions of the regularized problem remain solutions of the unregularized problem. The exact regularization threshold, however, depends on problem geometry and cannot be determined without first solving the original problem. We develop an average-case analysis that treats the cost vector as a Gaussian random variable and establish tail bounds describing how the exact regularization threshold concentrates near dimension-dependent values. This analysis reveals that exact regularization depends on the Gaussian measure of shifted normal cones and the geometry of polytope vertices.
The bounds are computable and furnish explicit estimates for problems such as quadratically regularized optimal transport and linear regularization on the
\infty
-norm ball.

The linear program with data
(A,b,g)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
takes the form

\mathrm{minimize}\set{-g\cdot x}{Ax\leq b}.

(
P_{0}
)

Its regularized counterpart

\mathrm{minimize}\set{-g\cdot x+\varepsilon\psi(x)}{Ax\leq b},

(
P_{\varepsilon}
)

retains the same constraint set but augments the objective with the penalty
\varepsilon\psi(x)
, where
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a proper convex function and
\varepsilon\geq 0
.

For sufficiently small regularization parameter
\varepsilon
, every solution of the regularized problem
P_{\varepsilon}
solves the original problem
P_{0}
. That is,
\mathrm{Sol}(P_{\varepsilon})\subseteq\mathrm{Sol}(P_{0})
. We call this property
exact regularization
. Deterministic analysis of exact regularization began with
Mangasarian and Meyer 1979
, whose work and subsequent contributions
[
22
,
23
]
establish conditions under which
P_{0}
and
P_{\varepsilon}
share solutions, including cases where only local optimality is preserved.

Exact regularization addresses both computational and theoretical challenges in linear programming. Computationally, LPs may exhibit nonunique solutions and extreme sensitivity to data perturbations. Regularization with a strongly convex function
\psi
(such as
\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
) attenuates these issues. The dual relationship between strong convexity and smoothness produces a smooth dual problem, which admits first-order algorithms with nonasymptotic linear convergence rates
[
21
,
33
,
17
]
. Theoretically, perturbation analysis reveals fundamental properties of LPs:
Mangasarian 1979
leverages perturbations to characterize solution uniqueness, while
Mangasarian 1984
connected solvability to projections onto feasible regions. This framework extends naturally beyond linear programming.
Ferris and Mangasarian 1991
,
Friedlander and Tseng 2008
, and
Deng 2015
develop exact regularization theory for general convex programs and establish connections with exact penalization
[
3
]
. Most recently,
González-Sanz and Nutz 2025
apply these ideas to quadratically regularized optimal transport.

Exact regularization theory characterizes the threshold value

\overline{\varepsilon}:=\sup\set{\varepsilon\geq 0\mid\mathrm{Sol}(P_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\subseteq\mathrm{Sol}(P_{0})\,\text{for all }\varepsilon^{\prime}\in[0,\varepsilon)}

(1)

through the dual of a selection problem

\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}_{x}\set{\psi(x)}{Ax\leq b,\ -g\cdot x\leq p^{\star}}.

(
P^{\psi}
)

Here
p^{\star}=\inf\{-g\cdot x\mid Ax\leq b\}
is the optimal value of
P_{0}
.
Mangasarian and Meyer 1979
establishes that
\overline{\varepsilon}
equals the inverse of the Lagrange multiplier of the objective constraint in
P^{\psi}
.

Friedlander and Tseng 2008
characterize the existence of this multiplier as both necessary and sufficient for exact regularization in general convex programs. Determining
\overline{\varepsilon}
thus requires solving both
P_{0}
and
P^{\psi}
. Without prior knowledge of
\overline{\varepsilon}
from the LP data
(g,A,b,\psi)
, we adopt a probabilistic approach. Randomizing problem data enables average-case analysis, a methodology well-established in computational complexity
[
16
]
fruitful for convex optimization
[
32
,
6
,
1
]
.

We randomize the cost vector of
P_{0}
as
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
, a standard Gaussian vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. With
(A,b,\psi)
fixed, we quantify the probability that
\mathrm{Sol}(P_{\varepsilon})\subseteq\mathrm{Sol}(P_{0})
for some
\varepsilon
. The choice of a Gaussian distribution provides rotational symmetry, treating all directions equally. For large
n
, the Gaussian random vector
g
concentrates around the sphere
\sqrt{n}\,\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and can be viewed as a random direction with approximately fixed norm
[
30
, Section 3.3.3]
.

1.1
Polyhedral geometry and normal cone structure

We require the following geometric framework. Denote the nonempty polyhedral feasible region of
P_{0}
by
Q:=\set{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{Ax\leq b}
. We focus on polytopes (bounded polyhedra)
Q
, which ensures finite optimal values
p^{*}
. For unbounded
Q
, our results apply conditionally on the existence of solutions.

The normal cone to
Q
at
x\in Q
is defined by

N(x):=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid v\cdot(y-x)\leq 0\,\text{ for all }y\in Q\}.

This cone admits the finite representation

N(x)=\conv\cone\set{a_{i}}{i\in J(x)}
,
where
a_{i}
denotes the
i
th row of
A
and
J(x):=\set{i\in[m]}{a_{i}\cdot x=b_{i}}
indexes the active constraints at
x

[
15
, Section A.5.2]
.

A vertex is a singleton exposed face of
Q

[
15
, Section A.2.3]
:

\mathrm{Vert}(Q):=\{z\in Q\mid Q\cap H=\{z\}\,\text{ for a supporting hyperplane }H\text{ of }Q\}.

For polyhedral
Q
, the finite set
\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
coincides with the extreme points
\mathrm{ext}(Q)
. The point
x
is a vertex if and only if
\mathrm{rank}(A_{J(x)})=n
, where
A_{J(x)}
denotes the submatrix of
A
with rows indexed by
J(x)
. Vertices with exactly
n
active constraints (
|J(x)|=n
) are
nondegenerate
; those with
|J(x)|>n
are
degenerate

[
4
]
. Our analysis centers on the normal cones
N(z)
at vertices
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
.

For a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denote the distance between
w
and
A
by
\textrm{dist}(w,A):=\inf_{a\in A}\lVert w-a\rVert
. For a
closed convex
set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the projection of
w
onto
A
is unique and given by
\proj_{A}w:=\argmin_{a\in A}\lVert w-a\rVert
. Given a cone
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define its
dual

V^{*}=\set{y}{y\cdot x\geq 0\text{ for all }x\in V}
.

1.2
Optimality and Gaussian measure

A point
z\in Q
is optimal for
P_{0}
if and only if

g\in N(z).

(2)

Since the cost vector
g
is Gaussian, the probability that
z
solves
P_{0}
equals the Gaussian measure of the normal cone
N(z)
. The standard Gaussian measure of any measurable subset
B
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

\gamma(B):=\mathbb{P}(g\in B)=\int_{B}{(2\pi)^{-n/2}}e^{-\|x\|^{2}/2}\,dx.

When
B
is a cone, its Gaussian measure equals its normalized solid angle
[
26
, Equation 1]
, which is the proportion of the sphere captured by the cone. Thus (
2
) conveys the probabilistic statement that
z\in Q
is a solution to
P_{0}
with probability
\gamma(N(z))
. Since
\gamma
assigns zero measure to lower-dimensional sets, only full-dimensional normal cones matter. The following proposition shows that these occur precisely at vertices of
Q
, where they partition
\mathbb{R}^{n}
when
Q
is a polytope.

Proposition 1
(Normal cone properties).

Let
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be polyhedral.

1.

The normal cone
N(z)
at
z\in Q
is
n
-dimensional if and only if
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
.

2.

If
x\in Q
and
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
are distinct, then
N(x)\cap N(z)
is a cone of dimension strictly less than
n
. Furthermore,
N(x)\cap\mathrm{int}\,N(z)=\emptyset
.

3.

If
Q
is additionally a polytope, then
\cup_{z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}N(z)=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

This near-partitioning of the space by vertex normal cones forms the
normal fan
of
Q

[
19
]
.
The following proposition is a consequence of (
2
) and
Proposition

1
.

Proposition 2
(Uniqueness in
P_{0}
).

If the feasible region
Q
is a polytope, then
P_{0}
has a unique solution at a vertex of
Q
with probability 1. This (almost surely defined) unique solution
z^{\star}\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
has distribution

\mathbb{P}\{z^{\star}=z\}=\gamma(N(z))\qquad(z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)).

The proofs of these propositions appear in
Appendix

A
.

1.2.1
Regularization and shifted cones

To analyze the regularized program
P_{\varepsilon}
, we first establish its optimality conditions, adopting the convention that
(\varepsilon\psi)=\delta_{\dom\psi}
when
\varepsilon=0
(scalar multiplication otherwise). This convention
implies that multiplying
\psi
by zero preserves the effective domain
of
\psi
, and thus
P_{\varepsilon}
is different
from
P_{0}
, even when
\varepsilon=0
, at least in the case where

\dom(\psi)\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This convention appears in
Aravkin et al. 2013
, and
Zalinescu 2002
.

A point
x\in Q
is optimal for
P_{\varepsilon}
if and only if

\displaystyle g\in N(x)+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(x),

(3)

under the regularity condition
[
27
, Theorem 23.8]

\mathrm{relint}(\mathrm{dom}\,\psi)\cap Q\neq\emptyset.

(R)

Note that the optimality condition (
3
) is sufficient for optimality even without (
R
), and consequently our lower bounds on the probability of exact regularization continue to hold without regularity.

The optimality condition (
3
) for
P_{\varepsilon}
suggests that the Gaussian measure of
shifted
cones should play a central role in the analysis. The following theorem (proved in
Section

5
) quantifies how shifting a cone by a vector
w
changes its Gaussian measure. The effect depends on the alignment of
w
with the cone and its dual.

Theorem 3
(Gaussian measure of shifted cones).

For any cone
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with dual cone
V^{*}
, and any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Gaussian measure of the shifted cone
V+w
satisfies

\displaystyle\gamma(V+w)

\displaystyle\in\left[\,\gamma(V)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}-\mathrm{dist}(w,-V^{*})\sqrt{d}\right),\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\ \,\gamma(V)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\proj_{V^{*}}w\|^{2}+\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})\sqrt{d}\right)\,\right].

These bounds immediately yield the following simplified estimates.

Corollary 4
(Simpler measure of shifted cones).

For any cone
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\gamma(V+w)

\displaystyle\in\left[\,\gamma(V)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}-\|w\|\sqrt{d}\right),\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\ \,\gamma(V)\exp\left(\|w\|\sqrt{d}\right)\,\right].

1.2.2
Exact regularization phenomenon

We define the
exact regularization event

\ER(\varepsilon)
as the event where the unique solution
z^{\star}
of
P_{0}
also solves
P_{\varepsilon}
.
The implications of this event extend beyond the specific parameter value. It follows from the arguments of
Friedlander and Tseng 2008
that whenever
\ER(\varepsilon)
occurs,
z^{*}
uniquely solves
P_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
for all
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in[0,\varepsilon)
.
Consequently, the event
\ER(\varepsilon)
implies that
exact regularization
satisfies
\overline{\varepsilon}\geq\varepsilon
as per (
1
).

The
inner cone
and
margin
sets, respectively, characterize the success and failure of exact regularization conditional on
\{z^{\star}=z\}
for a vertex
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
:

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\in N(z)\cap[N(z)+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)]\quad\text{(inner cone; $\mathrm{ER}(\varepsilon)$ succeeds)},

(4)

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\in N(z)\setminus[N(z)+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)]\quad\text{(margin set; $\mathrm{ER}(\varepsilon)$ fails)}.

(5)

The inner cone represents vectors in
N(z)
that remain in the normal cone after regularization. On the other hand, the
margin
set captures vectors in
N(z)
that exit the normal cone under regularization
(see
fig.

1
).

To compute the probability of exact regularization, observe that

\displaystyle\prob(\ER(\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}\prob(\{z^{\star}=z\}\text{ and }\ER(\varepsilon))

(6)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}\prob(g\in N(z)\cap[N(z)+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)])

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}\gamma(N(z)\cap[N(z)+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)]).

This is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 5
(Probability and normal cone geometry).

If
Q
is a polytope and
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a proper, convex function that satisfies (
R
), then (
6
) holds.

Note that quantifying the probability of
\ER(\varepsilon)
in terms of
\varepsilon
gives, using standard arguments, the expectation of the regularization threshold
\overline{\varepsilon}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\varepsilon}=\textstyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\ER(\varepsilon))\,d\varepsilon.

(7)

Because both
N(z)
and
N(z)+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)
are closed convex sets (arising as subdifferentials of convex functions), their interesection is indeed Borel measureable and thus
\ER(\varepsilon)
is a valid event in a probability space.

1.2.3
Extensions and remarks

Theorem

5
allows for
\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(x)=\emptyset
at vertices
x
of
Q
. The Minkowski sum is then empty, and the Gaussian measure of the empty set is zero.
This occurs when
\psi
has no affine minorant at
z^{\star}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, the solution of
P_{\varepsilon}

cannot match that of
P_{0}
, and the probability of exact regularization is zero.
An empty subdifferential can result from a discontinuity of
\psi
at
z^{\star}
.
An example of an empty subdifferential is that of the entropic regularization on the simplex. Indeed, the (negative) entropy function
\psi(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\log x_{j}
has unbounded gradients when approaching any of the vertices of the simplex.
Theorem

5
confirms that exact regularization never occurs for entropic regularization on the simplex.

Beyond exact regularization, our theory admits two alternative interpretations. First, taking
\psi(x)=p\cdot x
yields the linear program
P_{\varepsilon}
with cost vector
-g+\varepsilon p
. Our results then provide probabilistic guarantees for the robustness of
P_{0}
under cost vector perturbations, which in effect characterizes properties of the dual solutions. Second, taking
\psi
with an effective domain strictly contained in
Q
models a constraint or barrier function. Vertices outside
\dom(\psi)
cannot be exactly regularized for any
\varepsilon\geq 0
. The exact regularization probability becomes the probability that solutions to a random linear program
P_{0}
satisfy the constraint imposed by
\psi
.

1.4
Recent work

Regularized optimal transport
[
10
]
has renewed interest in the regularization of LPs. Entropically-regularized optimal transport gained widespread adoption when
Cuturi 2013
demonstrated that the Sinkhown algorithm would then apply and carried substantial computational advantages over the unregularized problem.
Cominetti and Martín 1994
and
Weed 2018
analyzed the convergence of entropyically regularized LPs as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.

Entropic optimal transport produces
dense
solutions that differ from the sparse solutions of the unregularized problem. Quadratic regularizers, by contrast, preserve sparsity, which makes quadratically regularized optimal transport
[
5
]
an attractive alternative.
Nutz 2025
and
González-Sanz et al. 2025
provide an analysis of the sparsity properties of this approach.

González-Sanz and Nutz 2025
establish exact regularization in quadratically regularized optimal transport, a stronger property than sparsity preservation. Following
Weed 2018
, they characterize the threshold
\overline{\varepsilon}
for quadratically regularized LPs as

\frac{1}{\overline{\varepsilon}}=2\,\max\Set{\frac{x^{\psi}\cdot(x^{\psi}-x)}{g\cdot(x^{\psi}-x)}}{x\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)\backslash\mathrm{Sol}(P^{\psi})},

where
x^{\psi}
denotes the unique solution to the selection problem
P^{\psi}
with
\psi=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
. This characterization requires an exhastive search over all vertices, which is computationally prohibitive.

González-Sanz and Nutz 2025
also derived a computable bound

\overline{\varepsilon}\geq\Delta/(2BD)

(8)

where
B=\sup_{x\in Q}\|x\|
,
D=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in Q}\|x-x^{\prime}\|
, and

\Delta=\min\Set{-g\cdot x}{x\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)\backslash\mathrm{Sol}(P_{0})}\,-\,p^{*}

(9)

denotes the suboptimality gap. This bound, however, proves loose in the average case. Consider the problems
P_{0}
and
P_{\varepsilon}
with standard normal cost vector
-g
and quadratic regularizer
\psi=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
, solved over
Q=\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
. The suboptimality gap here simplifies to
\Delta=2\min_{j\in[n]}|g_{j}|
almost surely, with
c/n\leq\mathbb{E}\Delta\leq C/n
for absolute constants
c,C>0
.
Because
B=\sqrt{n}
and
D=2\sqrt{n}
, bound (
8
) yields

\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\varepsilon}\geq c/(4n^{2}).

Our results (see
corollary

7
), by contrast, yield

\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\varepsilon}\geq 1/(4n),

which improves the bound by a factor of
n
, and is optimal up to constants (see
proposition

15
).

Exact regularization has been studied extensively in linear inverse problems, particularly for sparse and low-rank recovery.

Lai and Yin 2013
,
Schöpfer 2012
, and
Zhang et al. 2011
obtained explicit, computable estimates for the regularization threshold
\overline{\varepsilon}
in these settings.
These results require a ground truth
x_{0}
with specific structure (sparsity or low rank). The sparsity levels they assume, however, are far lower than typical of random basis pursuit instances. In contrast, we make no a priori assumptions on the solution set. Our average-case analysis of exact regularization therefore addresses a fundamentally different regime and uses different techniques.

The inner cone
N(z)\cap[N(z)+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)]

5

N(z)\cap\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)\neq\emptyset
. Indeed, if
w\in N(z)\cap\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)
then
N(z)\cap[N(z)+w]=N(z)+w
by convexity of normal cones; see
Figure

1
, left panel.

Generalizing beyond the restrictive membership condition requires further tools.

introduces the notion of
representer vectors
. For a nondegenerate vertex
z
, we show that one can replace

with a different

\tilde{w}\in N(z)
that produces the same inner cone (
4
); see
fig.

2.1
.

. Given a normal cone

2.1
A simple bound under a membership condition

We establish lower and upper bounds on the Gaussian measure of inner cones. The lower bound only requires convexity of
\psi
; the upper bound requires differentiability.

Theorem 6
(Gaussian measure of an inner cone under the membership condition).

Let
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
,
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and
T=N(z)
. For any subgradient
w\in\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)\cap T
,

\gamma(T\cap[T+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)])\geq\gamma(T)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}-\|w\|\sqrt{n}\right).

If
\psi
is differentiable at
z
, then

\gamma(T\cap[T+\varepsilon\nabla\psi(z)])\leq\gamma(T)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\proj_{T^{*}}\,\varepsilon\nabla\psi(z)\|^{2}+\mathrm{dist}(\varepsilon\nabla\psi(z),T^{*})\sqrt{n}\right).

The following corollary provides explicit bounds on both the failure probability of exact regularization and the expected regularization threshold
\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\varepsilon}
.

Corollary 7
(Failure probability under the membership condition).

Suppose
eq.

R
holds and
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Suppose that for each vertex
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
, we have
\partial\psi(z)\cap N(z)\neq\emptyset
. Choose any
v_{z}\in\partial\psi(z)\cap N(z)
for each vertex, and define
B=\max\set{}{v_{z}\||z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}
. Then

\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon)]\leq 1-\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}B^{2}-\varepsilon B\sqrt{n}\right)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}B^{2}+\varepsilon B\sqrt{n}.

The regularization threshold
\overline{\varepsilon}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{1-e^{-4n}}{2B\sqrt{n}}.

2.1.1
Quadratic regularization and inscribed polytopes

The following example satisfies the assumptions of
Corollary

7
. Given distinct points
z_{1},\dots,z_{K}
on the (centered) sphere
\rho\,\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
of radius
\rho>0
, consider the inscribed polytope
Q=\conv\{z_{1},\dots,z_{K}\}
. Let
\psi=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
be the quadratic regularizer. Then, at any vertex
z_{k}
, optimality requires
\nabla\psi(z_{k})=z_{k}\in N_{Q}(z_{k})
, so that the membership condition holds. By construction,
B=\max_{k\in[K]}\|\nabla\psi(z_{k})\|=\rho
, and the bound from
Corollary

7
applies. Somewhat surprisingly, the bound does not depend on the number of vertices
K
.

The
1
-ball and the
\infty
-ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are covered by this example, with
\rho=1
and
\rho=\sqrt{n}
, respectively. The
Birkhoff polytope
of doubly stochastic matrices

Q=\Set{X\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}{X\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{e},\,X^{T}\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{e},\,X\geq 0},

(10)

and

\mathbb{E}\,\overline{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{1-e^{-4n}}{2n^{3/4}}.

(11)

2.1.2
The soft phase transition

Theorem

6

describes the order of
\varepsilon
at which exact regularization
occurs. The phenomenon exhibits a soft phase transition:
exact regularization succeeds with high probability for sufficiently small

\varepsilon
, and fails with high probability for
large
\varepsilon
. The next corollary makes this transition explicit.

Corollary 8
(Soft phase transition).

Let
\psi
be differentiable at all
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
with
\nabla\psi(z)\in N(z)
.
If

\varepsilon\leq\delta\left(2\sqrt{n}\max\set{}{\nabla\psi(z)\||z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}\right)^{-1},

then
\ER(\varepsilon)
happens with probability at least
1-\delta
. If instead
\nabla\psi(z)\in N^{\star}(z)
for all
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
and

\varepsilon\geq\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)}\cdot\left(\min\set{}{\nabla\psi(z)\||z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}\right)^{-1},

then
\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon)
happens with probability at least
1-\delta
.

One could derive analogous corollaries for other results in this paper, notably
Theorem

10
and
Theorem

13
, but we omit them to avoid redundancy.

2.2
Bounds via representer vectors

Let
T=N(z)
be the normal cone at
z
. For a vector
w\in\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)
that
fails
the membership condition, we construct a
representer vector

\tilde{w}
satisfying the membership condition such that

T\cap[T+w]=T\cap[T+\tilde{w}].

This construction relies on an explicit polyhedral description of
T
via its facet structure, which we develop below. The main result, given in
Theorem

10
, provides probabilistic bounds in terms of the norm
\|\tilde{w}\|
.

2.2.1
Representer vector construction

For any subgradient
w\in\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)
, the inclusion

T\cap[T+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)]\supseteq T\cap[T+w]

reduces the problem to lower bounding
\gamma(T\cap[T+w])
.

We define the margin
M(T,w):=T\setminus[T+w]
, where
w
may be a vector or a set; in the latter case, the sum is a Minkowski sum. We use the margin to bound the complement of the inner cone; upper bounds on
\gamma(M(T,w))
give lower bounds on the probability of exact regularization.

We show that the representer vector
\tilde{w}
satisfies
M(T,w)=M(T,\tilde{w})
, as illustrated in
fig.

1
(b). This construction depends on the explicit polyhedral description of
T
, specifically via its inward-facing unit normals

s^{1},\ldots,s^{\ell}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
:

T=\set{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{y\cdot s^{i}\geq 0\,\,\forall i\in[\ell]}.

(12)

Each unit normal
s^{i}
defines a facet
T^{i}=\set{y\in T}{s^{i}\cdot y=0}
, which is an
(n-1)
-dimensional polyhedral face of
T
. We denote by
S\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times n}
the matrix with rows
s^{1},\dots,s^{\ell}
(or
S_{T}
when emphasizing the dependence on
T
). When the vertex
z
is nondegenerate, the submatrix
A_{J(z)}
with rows
\set{a_{i}}{i\in J(z)}
is square and invertible, and we can express
T=A_{J(z)}^{T}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}=\set{y\mid A^{-T}_{J(z)}y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}}
; cf.
section

1.1
. Consequently, the rows
s^{1},\dots,s^{n}
(where
\ell=n
) can be taken to be the normalized columns of
A_{J(z)}^{-1}
, which implies that
S_{T}
is invertible.

Given
S_{T}
and
w
, we define a representer vector as any vector
\tilde{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that satisfies

S_{T}\tilde{w}=(S_{T}w)_{+},

where
x_{+}:=\max(0,x)
operates componentwise. Any solution
\tilde{w}
that satisfies this equation belongs to
T
because
s^{i}\cdot\tilde{w}=(S_{T}\tilde{w})_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in[\ell]
; cf. (
12
). Nondegeneracy of the vertex
z
guarantees that such a representer exists..The following lemma confirms that
\tilde{w}
generates the correct margin.

Lemma 9
(Representer vectors for the margin).

If
\tilde{w}
is a representer vector satisfying
S_{T}\tilde{w}=(S_{T}w)_{+}
, then it holds that

M(T,w)=M(T,\tilde{w})
.

See
the proof
in
Section

5
.

2.2.2
Representer vector theorem

We now present the analogous result to
Theorem

6
.

Theorem 10
(Bound via representer vectors).

Let
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
,
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and denote
T=N(z)
. Assume that the vertex
z
is nondegenerate, so that the matrix
S_{T}
is invertible. Then, for any subgradient
w\in\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)
, it holds that

\gamma(T\cap[T+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)])\geq\gamma(T)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\tilde{w}\|^{2}-\|\tilde{w}\|\sqrt{n}\right),

where
\tilde{w}:=S_{T}^{-1}(S_{T}w)_{+}
is the corresponding representer vector. Additionally, if
\psi
is differentiable at
z
,

\gamma(T\cap[T+\varepsilon\nabla\psi(z)])\leq\gamma(T)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\proj_{T^{*}}\tilde{w}\|^{2}+\mathrm{dist}(\tilde{w},T^{*})\sqrt{n}\right),

where now
\tilde{w}:=S_{T}^{-1}[S_{T}(\varepsilon\nabla\psi(z))]_{+}
.

The bounds in
Theorem

10
depend on the norm of the representer
\tilde{w}
. With the representation
\tilde{w}=S_{T}^{-1}(S_{T}w)_{+}
, we bound this norm in terms of
w
and the condition number
\mathrm{cond}(S_{T}):=\sigma_{1}(S_{T})/\sigma_{n}(S_{T})=\|S_{T}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|S_{T}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, where
\sigma_{i}(S_{T})
denotes the
i
th largest singular value of
S_{T}
:

\displaystyle\|\tilde{w}\|=\|S_{T}^{-1}(S_{T}w)_{+}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|S_{T}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|(S_{T}w)_{+}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|S_{T}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|S_{T}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\,\|w\|=\mathrm{cond}(S_{T})\,\|w\|.

3
Margin bounds via facet decomposition

We establish two-sided bounds on the Gaussian measure of the margin in terms of a geometric quantity.

Theorem

5
establishes
that

\prob(\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon))=\textstyle\sum_{z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}\gamma(N(z)\setminus[N(z)+\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)]).

Because the margin (
5
) appears in each
summand, we bound
\gamma(M(N(z),w))
for each vertex
z
and
subgradient
w\in\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)
. These margin
bounds exploit the facet structure of the normal cone boundary.

3.1
Margin partition and Gaussian measure

Because facets of normal cones lie in proper subspaces, we require a measure adapted to lower-dimensional sets. For any cone
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
relative Gaussian measure
is
\gamma_{\mathrm{rel}}(T):=\prob(\proj_{\Span(T)}g\in T)
,
where
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
. This measures
T
relative to its span, rather than the ambient space.
We use this facet decomposition to bound the margin
M(T,w)
.

Figure

3
illustrates the geometry underlying this decomposition.

Lemma 11
(Partition of the margin).

For a polyhedral cone
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\ell
facets
T^{i}
associated with inward normals
s^{i}
, and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\gamma(M(T,w))\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\gamma(P^{i}),\quad\text{where}\quad P^{i}:=\begin{cases}\bigcup_{t\in[0,1)}[T^{i}+tw]&\text{if $s^{i}\cdot w>0$},\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(13)

If
w\in T
, then (
13
) holds with equality.

Figure 3:

Decomposing the margin
. Consider a polyhedral cone
T
with facets
T^{1}
,
T^{2}
and associated inward normals
s^{1},s^{2}
. Given a vector
w
, the margin
M(T,w)
lies within the union of
P^{1}
and
P^{2}
(regions enclosed by dotted lines). The containment is tight when
w\in T
(panel a), but not in general (panel b).

The partition in
Lemma

11
allows for explicit Gaussian measure bounds.

Lemma 12
(Gaussian measure of the margin).

Let
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a polyhedral cone with
\ell
facets
T^{i}
with associated inward unit normals
s^{i}
. For
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

F(T,w):=(\sqrt{2\pi})^{-1}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\gamma_{\mathrm{rel}}(T^{i})(s^{i}\cdot w)_{+}.

(14)

Then

\gamma(M(T,w))\leq 2F(T,w)\exp\left(\sqrt{n-1}\|w\|\right).

If
w\in T
, then

\gamma(M(T,w))\geq F(T,w)\exp\left(-\sqrt{n-1}\|w\|-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}\right).

3.2
Application to exact regularization

We now give a result for the probability of
exact regularization using the margin set in a normal cone of
Q
.

Theorem 13
(Applied margin measure for exact regularization).

Let
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
. Let
T^{i}
denote
the facets of the polyhedral normal cone
N(z)
, with their associated inward
normals
s^{i}
. Let the function
F
be as in
Lemma

12
. Then for any
v\in\partial\psi(z)
,

\gamma(M(N(z),\,\partial(\varepsilon\psi)(z)))\leq 2\varepsilon F(N(z),v)\exp\left(\varepsilon\sqrt{n-1}\|v\|\right).

Furthermore, if
\psi
is differentiable at
z
and
\nabla\psi(z)\in N(z)
, then

\gamma(M(N(z),\,\varepsilon\nabla\psi(z)))\geq\varepsilon F(N(z),\nabla\psi(z))\exp\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n-1}\|\nabla\psi(z)\|-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\|\nabla\psi(z)\|^{2}\right).

Alternatively, if we allow
\nabla\psi(z)\notin N(z)
, but instead require that
N(z)
be nondegenerate, then with

\tilde{v}:=S^{-1}(S\nabla\psi(z))_{+}
,

\gamma(M(N(z),\,\varepsilon\nabla\psi(z)))\geq\varepsilon F(N(z),\nabla\psi(z))\exp\left(-\varepsilon\sqrt{n-1}\|\tilde{v}\|-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\|\tilde{v}\|^{2}\right).

3.2.1
Facets decomposition

We use
Theorem

13
to characterize the probability of the
\ER^{c}(\varepsilon)
event for small
\varepsilon
via the sum
\sum_{z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}F(N(z),\nabla\psi(z))
.
Indeed, with
Theorem

5
, we can say that with
\psi

differentiable and
\nabla\psi(z)\in N(z)
for all
z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
, and with

\varepsilon\leq(2\sqrt{n}\max_{z\in Q}\|\nabla\psi(z)\|)^{-1}
,

\textstyle\frac{\varepsilon}{e}\sum_{z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}F(N(z),\nabla\psi(z))\leq\prob(\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon))\leq 2e\varepsilon\sum_{z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}F(N(z),\nabla\psi(z)).

Using
theorem

13
,
we find a simple result that applies to the linear regularizer
\psi(x)=p\cdot x
, for
which we require some additional notation.
We henceforth refer to
1-
dimensional faces of
Q
as “edges”, and denote by
\mathcal{B}
the set of all edges of
Q
. Any edge
f\in\mathcal{B}

takes the form
f=\conv(z_{1},z_{2})
for
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)
.
The facets of the normal cones at vertices of
Q
are themselves
(n-1)
-dimensional
normal cones at points in the relative interiors of edges, and
are also the intersection of exactly two full-dimenional normal cones
at vertices
z_{1},z_{2}
(see
[
19
, Proposition 1(b)]
).
Because the normal cone is the same for any point in
\rint(f)
for an edge
f\in\mathcal{B}
, we denote by
N(f)

the normal cone associated with the edge
f
. Furthermore, an inward normal
s^{i}
associated
with a facet is orthogonal to the facet, and so is
z_{1}-z_{2}
.
Therefore, these two vectors are linearly dependent. Because of this connection,
we define
s_{f}
to be a unit normal in
\Span(f-f)
. (There are two possible such vectors,
we pick any one of them, as the orientation will not matter in the next result.)

Corollary 14
(Linear perturbed polytope regularization).

With
\psi(x)=p\cdot x
, any polytope
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{B}
the set of edges
of
Q
,

\prob(\mathrm{ER}(\varepsilon)^{c})\leq\textstyle\varepsilon\frac{e}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{B}}\gamma_{\mathrm{rel}}(N(f))\,\,|s_{f}\cdot p|\,\,\exp\left(\varepsilon\sqrt{n-1}\|p\|\right).

P
roof of
Corollary

14
.

Denote by
T_{z}^{i}
the
i^{\text{th}}
facet of the normal cone
N(z)
,
and by
s_{z}^{i}
the corresponding inward normal.
As argued above,
s_{z}^{i}\approx s_{f}
up to a sign, for
f\in\mathcal{B}
the
edge for which
T_{z}^{i}=N(f)
. To apply
Theorem

13
,
we compute

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}F(N(z),\varepsilon p)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{z\in\mathrm{Vert}(Q)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{z}}\gamma_{\mathrm{rel}}(T_{z}^{i})(\varepsilon p\cdot s^{i}_{z})_{+}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{B}}\gamma_{\mathrm{rel}}(N(f))|p\cdot s_{f}|.

As discussed above, each facet
T_{z}^{i}
appears twice in the sum because
each facet is a face for exactly two full-dimensional normal cones.
When we pair the two terms related to a common edge
f
, the inward
normals in these two terms satisfy
s_{z_{1}}^{i}=-s_{z_{2}}^{j}
because they point inward to different
full-dimensional normal cones.
Therefore, the factor
(\varepsilon p\cdot s_{z}^{i})_{+}

will take a value of
\varepsilon|p\cdot s_{z}^{i}|
for one of the two terms and
0
for the other, which justifies the result.

4
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
ball constraint

We analyze the case where
Q=\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
to gain a better understanding of the bounds introduced so far. The polytope
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
resembles
the feasible region of linear programs because it can be specified by
2n
linear constraints, and has an
exponential number of vertices. At the same time, it is
amenable to analysis because of its simplicity.
We first consider the quadratic (Tikhonov) regularization, where
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
. We will assess the quality of our bounds in this setting by comparing them to the following lower-bound on

\prob(\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon))
.

Proposition 15
(Lower bound for
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
).

For
P_{0}
with
Q=\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
and

\psi=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
,

\prob(\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon))\geq 1-\exp\left(-\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi e}}n\varepsilon\right),

and for
\varepsilon\leq\sqrt{\frac{(\pi e)}{2}}\frac{1}{2n}
,

\prob(\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon))\geq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}n\varepsilon.

Since
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
has its vertices on
\sqrt{n}\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and the gradient at each vertex
z
is
z
itself, the gradient is contained in
N(z)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\sgn(x)=\sgn(z)\}
.
So we may use
Corollary

7
to
derive the following.

Corollary 16
(Bound from vertices on the sphere).

For problem
P_{0}
with
Q=\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
and

\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
,

\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon)]\leq\varepsilon n+\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}n.

It turns out that for
\varepsilon<n^{-1}
, the first term dominates, and this bound is optimal up to a constant factor of no more than
\sqrt{2\pi e}
.

What happens if we instead use
Theorem

13
?
To apply this result, we need the relative Gaussian measure of the
facets of the normal cones of
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
. The normal cones are the orthants of

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and their facets are orthants of
the axis-aligned hyperplanes. An axis aligned hyperplane is of the form

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x\cdot e_{i}=0\}
for a canonical basis vector
e_{i}
.
There are exactly
n
such hyperplanes, and each hyperplane has exactly

2^{n-1}
orthants. Therefore,
the relative Gaussian measure of each facet of the normal cones is

2^{-(n-1)}
, by rotational symmetry. With
F
as in
Lemma

12
,

\displaystyle F\left(N(z),\varepsilon z\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-1}\left(\varepsilon z\cdot(z_{i}e_{i})\right)_{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-1}\varepsilon z_{i}^{2}.

Then,

\displaystyle\prob(\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon))

\displaystyle\leq 2\textstyle\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}F\left(N(z),\varepsilon z\right)\exp\left(\varepsilon\sqrt{n-1}\cdot\sqrt{n}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle=2\cdot 2^{n}\left[\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n-1}\varepsilon\|z\|^{2}\right]\exp\left(\varepsilon\sqrt{n-1}\cdot\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{4}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\varepsilon n\exp\left(\varepsilon n\right).

We have derived the following.

Corollary 17
(Bound from the margin).

For problem
P_{0}
with
Q=\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
and

\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, (
15
) holds.

This bound is optimal up to a constant. It has a better constant than the bound in
Corollary

16
, but with an additional exponential factor of approximately
1
in the regime of interest:
\varepsilon<1/n
.

We now turn our attention to the setting where
\psi(x)=p\cdot x
. In this
setting, the conditions of
Theorem

6
do not hold, and therefore we need the assistance of the representer vector in
Theorem

10
. Because
Q=\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
, the representer vector
\tilde{p}
of
p
for a specific normal cone is in fact the projection of
p
on to that normal cone. Because of this,
\|\tilde{p}\|\leq\|p\|
, and with
Theorem

3
,

\displaystyle\prob(\mathrm{ER}(\varepsilon))

\displaystyle\geq\textstyle\sum_{z\in\{-1,1\}^{n}}\gamma_{\mathrm{rel}}(N_{\mathbb{B}_{\infty}}(z))\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\|\tilde{p}\|^{2}-\varepsilon\|\tilde{p}\|\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\|p\|^{2}-\varepsilon\|p\|\sqrt{n}\right)\geq 1-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\|p\|^{2}-\varepsilon\|p\|\sqrt{n}.

Corollary 18
(Bound with the representer vector).

For problem
P_{0}
with
Q=B_{\infty}
and

\psi(x)=p\cdot x
,

\prob(\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon))\leq\varepsilon\|p\|\sqrt{n}+\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\|p\|^{2}.

We apply the bound in
Corollary

14

to the same setting.
Recall that all
(n-1)
-dimensional normal cones of the
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
ball have a
relative Gaussian measure of
2^{-(n-1)}
, and that there are
n2^{n-1}
such
normal cones (one for each edge). The edge vectors
s_{f}
are the
canonical basis vectors. We find the following result.

Corollary 19
(Linear perturbed
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
from the margin bound).

For problem
P_{0}
with
Q=\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
and

\psi(x)=p\cdot x
,

\prob(\mathrm{ER}^{c}(\varepsilon))\leq\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\varepsilon\|p\|_{1}\exp\left(\varepsilon\sqrt{n-1}\|p\|\right).

The use of
Corollary

14
yields a tighter bound relative to the simpler representer-type machinery in this setting. We find a smaller multiplicative constant, but more importantly, the factor of
\lVert p\rVert_{1}
is smaller than the factor of
\sqrt{n}\|p\|
found in
Corollary

.
Corollary

19
tells us that exact regularization occurs more readily for almost-sparse vectors

, i.e., vectors that have their mass concentrated on a small number of entries, as quantified by the ratio
\|p\|_{1}/(\sqrt{n}\|p\|)
.

P

Theorem

.

Upper bound
.
We compute the Gaussian measure of
V+w
for any cone
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\phi(x)=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\|x\|^{2}/2\right)
denote the standard Gaussian density, and
\phi^{\kappa}(x)=(2\pi\kappa)^{-d/2}\exp(-\|x\|^{2}/2\kappa)
be its scaled version with variance
\kappa>0
. Then,

\displaystyle\gamma(V+w)

\displaystyle=\int_{V+w}\phi(x)dx=\int_{V}\phi(x+w)dx=\int_{V}\frac{\phi(x+w)}{\phi^{\kappa}(x)}\,\phi^{\kappa}(x)dx,

where we have changed the base measure to the scaled Gaussian
d\gamma^{\kappa}(x)=\phi^{\kappa}(x)dx
.

Step 1: Hölder’s inequality and scaling invariance
.
Fixing some
\kappa>0
(to be chosen later), we upper bound the integral above as

\int_{V}\frac{\phi(x+w)}{\phi^{\kappa}(x)}\,\phi^{\kappa}(x)dx\leq\left[\sup_{x\in V}\frac{\phi(x+w)}{\phi^{\kappa}(x)}\right]\int_{V}\phi^{\kappa}(x)dx.

Due to the scaling invariance of cones (i.e.,
\alpha V=V
for
\alpha>0
),

\displaystyle\int_{V}\phi^{\kappa}(x)dx=\mathbb{P}_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\{\sqrt{\kappa}\,g\in V\}=\mathbb{P}_{g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}\{g\in V\}=\gamma(V).

What remains is to upper-bound the ratio
\phi(x+w)/\phi^{\kappa}(x)
, which we write out as

\kappa^{d/2}\frac{\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|x+w\|^{2}\right)}{\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\kappa}\|x\|^{2}\right)}=\kappa^{d/2}\exp\left(-Q_{w}(x)\right),

where
Q_{w}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\|x+w\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\kappa}\|x\|^{2}
, and consequently,

\sup_{x\in V}\,\frac{\phi(x+w)}{\phi^{\kappa}(x)}=\kappa^{d/2}\exp\left(-\inf_{x\in V}\,Q_{w}(x)\right).

We proceed by lower-bounding
\inf_{x\in V}\,Q_{w}(x)
.

Step 2: Lower bounds from weak duality
.
If the variance
\kappa>1
, the quadratic
Q_{w}
is strongly convex and has a finite infimum over the cone
V
.
By weak duality
[
28
, Lemma 11.38]
,
\inf_{x\in V}\,Q_{w}(x)\geq\sup_{y\in V^{*}}\,-Q_{w}^{*}(y)
,
where the convex conjugate of the quadratic
Q_{w}
is
Q_{w}^{*}(y)=\tfrac{\kappa}{2(\kappa-1)}\|y-w\|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}
.
We can now evaluate

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in V^{*}}\,-Q_{w}^{*}(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in V^{*}}\,\left\{-\frac{\kappa}{2(\kappa-1)}\|y-w\|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}-\frac{\kappa}{2(\kappa-1)}\inf_{y\in V^{*}}\|y-w\|^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}-\frac{\kappa}{2(\kappa-1)}\,\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})^{2},

and consequently, express

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in V}\,\frac{\phi(x+w)}{\phi^{\kappa}(x)}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{d/2}\exp\left(-\inf_{x\in V}\,Q_{w}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{d/2}\exp\left(\frac{\kappa}{2(\kappa-1)}\,\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})^{2}\right)\,\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}\right).

Step 3: Optimize over
\kappa
.
Define the function

h(\kappa)=\kappa^{d/2}\exp\left(\frac{\kappa}{2(\kappa-1)}\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})^{2}\right),

and recall from the previous steps that

\gamma(V+w)\leq\gamma(V)\,h(\kappa)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}\right)
holds for any choice of
\kappa>1
.
We choose the value

\kappa=1+\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})/\sqrt{d},

which is valid provided
\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})>0
. (The case
\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})=0
will be handled separately.) This choice yields

\displaystyle h(\kappa)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})}{\sqrt{d}}\right)^{d/2}\exp\left\{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})}+1\right)\frac{\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})^{2}}{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})\sqrt{d}\right)\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})\sqrt{d}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})\sqrt{d}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})^{2}\right).

Putting the above inequalities together, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\gamma(V+w)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma(V)\,\exp\left(\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})\sqrt{d}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma(V)\,\exp\left(\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})\sqrt{d}-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\proj_{V^{*}}w\|^{2}\right),

where the last equality uses the fact that
V^{*}
is a closed convex cone (given any cone
V
) and the decomposition
[
15
, Section A.3.2]

\|w\|^{2}=\|\proj_{V^{*}}w\|^{2}+\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})^{2}.

The case
\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})=0
is straightforward, where for any
\kappa>1
, it holds that

\gamma(V+w)\leq\gamma(V)\,h(\kappa)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}\right)=\gamma(V)\,\kappa^{d/2}\,\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}\right).

Taking a limit as
\kappa\downarrow 1
immediately yields
\gamma(V+w)\leq\gamma(V)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}\right)
. We conclude the proof of the upper bound by noting that
w=\proj_{V^{*}}w
whenever
\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{*})=0
.

Lower bound
.
The above proof can be adapted to obtain a lower bound by studying the infima of the ratios
\phi(x+w)/\phi^{\kappa}(x)
for
\kappa<1
. We instead use a simpler method partially adapted from
Ledoux and Talagrand 1991
.

Because the case
\gamma(V)=0
is trivial, assume
\gamma(V)>0
. We start with the integral

\displaystyle\gamma(V+w)

\displaystyle=\int_{V}\phi(x+w)dx

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{V}e^{-\|x+w\|^{2}/2}dx

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\,e^{-\|w\|^{2}/2}\,\int_{V}e^{-w\cdot x}\,e^{-\|x\|^{2}/2}dx

\displaystyle=e^{-\|w\|^{2}/2}\,\int_{V}e^{-w\cdot x}\phi(x)dx=e^{-\|w\|^{2}/2}\gamma(V)\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-w\cdot x}\,\,\mathds{1}_{V}(x)\frac{\phi(x)}{\gamma(V)}dx,

where
\mathds{1}_{V}(x):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
is the Bernoulli indicator of
V
. Consider a random vector
X
distributed according to the probability density
\mathds{1}_{V}(\cdot)\,\phi(\cdot)/\gamma(V)
.
Rewriting the integrals using expectations, and using Jensen’s inequality gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-w\cdot x}\,\,\mathds{1}_{V}(x)\frac{\phi(x)}{\gamma(V)}dx=\mathbb{E}\,e^{-w\cdot X}\geq\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}\,w\cdot X\right).

Noting that the random vector
X\in V
(with probability 1), we may use the Moreau decomposition
[
15
, Theorem A.3.2.5]
to bound

\displaystyle w\cdot X

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(\proj_{V^{\circ}}w)\cdot X}_{\leq 0}+(\proj_{V^{\circ\circ}}w)\cdot X

\displaystyle\leq\|\proj_{V^{\circ\circ}}w\|\|X\|=\mathrm{dist}(w,V^{\circ})\|X\|=\mathrm{dist}(-w,V^{*})\|X\|,

where
V^{\circ}=-V^{*}
is the polar cone. Consequently,

\exp\left(-\mathbb{E}\,w\cdot X\right)\geq\exp\left(-\mathrm{dist}(-w,V^{*})\,\mathbb{E}\|X\|\right).

What remains is to show that
\mathbb{E}\|X\|\leq\sqrt{d}
via Gaussian computations:

\mathbb{E}\|X\|=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|\,\mathds{1}_{V}(x)\frac{\phi(x)}{\gamma(V)}dx=\frac{1}{\gamma(V)}\,\mathbb{E}\,\|g\|\,\mathds{1}_{V}(g),

where
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. Noting that the random variables
\rho:=\|g\|
and
\theta:=g/\|g\|
are independent by properties of Gaussians, and that
\mathds{1}_{V}(g)=\mathds{1}_{V}(\theta)
(with probability 1), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\|g\|\,\mathds{1}_{V}(g)=\mathbb{E}\,[\rho\,\mathds{1}_{V}(\theta)]=\mathbb{E}[\rho]\,\mathbb{E}\,\mathds{1}_{V}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\|g\|\,\mathbb{E}\,\mathds{1}_{V}(g)\leq\sqrt{d}\,\gamma(V).

Collecting everything yields

\displaystyle\gamma(V+w)

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\|w\|^{2}/2}\gamma(V)\exp\left(-\mathrm{dist}(-w,V^{*})\,\mathbb{E}\|X\|\right)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma(V)\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^{2}-\mathrm{dist}(-w,V^{*})\sqrt{d}\right),

as required.

P
roof of

.

A necessary condition for optimality for this problem is that
\sgn(\nabla f(z^{\star}))\approx z^{\star}
, with
(\approx)
meaning that the two sides do not have opposite signs (
0\approx 1
but
-1\not\approx 1
). Therefore, if exact regularization is to succeed,
there must be a vertex
z^{\star}
of
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}
such that
\sgn(g)\approx z^{\star}
(optimality of
P_{0}
), and such that
\sgn(g-\varepsilon z^{\star})\approx z^{\star}
(optimality of
P_{\varepsilon}
).

Since the sign of
-\varepsilon z^{\star}
is elementwise opposite to that of
g
, the condition
|g|-\varepsilon\mathbf{e}\geq 0
,
is necessary, where
\mathbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the all-ones vector.

We want to show that exact regularization fails with high probability, so we bound the probability that it succeeds:

\displaystyle\prob(\mathrm{ER}(\varepsilon))

\displaystyle\leq\prob(\min(|g|)\geq\varepsilon)=\prob\left(|g_{1}|\geq\varepsilon\right)^{n}=\left(1-\pr\left(|g_{1}|<\varepsilon\right)\right)^{n}.

(18)

When
\varepsilon\leq 1
, we get
\pr\left(|g_{1}|<\varepsilon\right)\geq\sqrt{2/(\pi e)}\cdot\varepsilon
.
This lower bound arises from a constant lower-bound (with bounded support) of the Gaussian density function.
With the identity
1+x\leq e^{x}
, it follows from
Equation

18
that

\prob(\textrm{ER}(\varepsilon))\leq\exp\left(-\sqrt{2/(\pi e)}\cdot n\varepsilon\right).

(19)

Now consider the case
\varepsilon\geq 1
. By
Vershynin 2018
,

\displaystyle\prob(|g_{1}|

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon)^{n}\leq\left(\tfrac{2}{\pi}\right)^{n/2}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}n\varepsilon^{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}n\varepsilon^{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}n\varepsilon\right).

This is a larger upper bound than that of (
19
),
and so the first part of the result follows. For the second part, it suffices to consider the elementary inequality
on
x\in[-1,0]
, that
\frac{1}{2}x+1\geq e^{x}
.

6
Discussion

We analyzed exact regularization in the random linear program
P_{0}
with a standard Gaussian cost vector, deriving expressions for the probability of
\mathrm{ER}(\varepsilon)
in terms of normal cone geometry. The polyhedral inner cones and margins play a central role in the analysis. We provided two-sided estimates of their Gaussian measure, which yield simple, explicit expressions in interesting special cases, including quadratically regularized linear programs on the Birkhoff polytope. These estimates agree with numerical experiments.

When exact regularization fails, deterministic bounds exist for
\mathrm{dist}(x_{\varepsilon},\mathrm{Sol}(P_{0}))
, but these bounds involve parameters that are difficult to evaluate
[
12
]
. The Gaussian randomization framework we develop may provide tools needed to derive high-probability and expectation bounds on solution distance when exact regularization fails. The normal-cone techniques and Gaussian measure estimates may enable characterization of the distributions of the regularized solutions
x_{\varepsilon}
, conditioned on failure events.

Recent work reveals that support monotonicity in quadratically constrained regularized LPs fails in general, but tends to hold in experiments
[
14
]
. These empirical observations suggest an underlying probabilistic structure. Can we quantify the probability of occurrence for randomized cost vectors?

The polyhedral structure of LPs allows explicit representations of normal cones and their Gaussian measure. For more general conic problems that include quadratic and semidefinite constraints, second-order cones and linear matrix inequalities characterize the corresponding normal cone geometry. Our main technical result on the Gaussian measure of shifted cones (
theorem

3
) does not depend on conic polyhedrality and thus generalizes to those settings. However, the analogs of polyhedral inner cones and margins present technical challenges.
