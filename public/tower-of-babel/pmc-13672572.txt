Management of Urinary Retention in Older Women
Authors: Rachna Sridhar, Joanna Marantidis, Anne P. Cameron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urinary retention in older women is a clinically significant and often underdiagnosed condition with complex and multifactorial etiology. Age-related changes in bladder contractile function and bladder sensation, combined with common comorbidities such as neurologic disease, diabetic bladder dysfunction, and pelvic organ prolapse, substantially increase the risk of retention in this population. Medications commonly prescribed to older adults, including anticholinergics and antidepressants, are an often overlooked cause of urinary retention. Evaluation includes a thorough history, physical exam, medication reconciliation, and post-void residual volume, especially because symptoms of retention that are common in men are often non-specific in women. Management should be risk stratified, with acute retention requiring prompt bladder drainage and chronic urinary retention requiring assessment of symptom burden and risk of upper tract deterioration. Pharmacologic treatment options, such as alpha-blockers, have limited evidence in women and broader therapeutic options remain poorly studied in older female populations. Sacral neuromodulation can be effective for non-obstructive urinary retention in older women. This review highlights the critical need for sex-specific and age-specific trials to guide evidence-based patient-centered management of urinary retention in older women.

Key Points

Urinary retention in older women is a common but frequently underdiagnosed condition driven by age-related changes in bladder function, neurologic and metabolic comorbidities, and polypharmacy.The management of acute retention requires urgent bladder decompression, while the management of chronic urinary retention is risk stratified based on patient symptoms and the risk of developing upper tract deterioration.Despite its growing prevalence, urinary retention in older women remains severely understudied, and well-designed, sex-specific randomized controlled trials are needed to establish evidence-based treatment guidelines for this population.

Introduction

Urinary retention in women represents a complex and often underdiagnosed clinical condition, especially in older populations. Unlike the well-characterized and common presentation of urinary retention in men, urinary retention in women encompasses a heterogenous spectrum of etiologies that remain poorly understood [1]. In older women, urinary retention may be asymptomatic and the diagnosis may be obscured by baseline lower urinary tract symptoms, mobility limitations, or cognitive impairment, contributing to delayed identification and potentially avoidable complications, such as a urinary tract infection (UTI) or acute kidney injury. This review explores the epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnostic challenges, and current and emerging treatment modalities for urinary retention in older women.

Definition and Epidemiology

Urinary retention can be divided into acute and chronic urinary retention (Fig. 1). Acute urinary retention (AUR) is a urologic emergency characterized by the sudden inability to urinate combined typically with suprapubic pain, bloating, urgency, distress, or occasionally overflow incontinence [2]. Chronic urinary retention is often more mildly symptomatic and is defined as a post void residual (PVR) volume > 300 mL persisting ≥ 6 months on two or more occasions [3].Fig. 1Algorithm for managing urinary retention. While more common in men, urinary retention is diagnosed in about 1500 per 100,000 women in the USA per year, with rates of short-term and chronic catheterization found to be 160 and 108 per 100,000 women per year, respectively [1]. Retention is often under-diagnosed in women because of symptoms being under-reported. Risk factors in the older adult female population include prior diagnoses of a neurologic condition (Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury) [4] cognitive impairment, diabetes mellitus and the resulting cystopathy, UTI, pelvic organ prolapse (POP), prior incontinence surgeries, increased age, certain medications, and recent surgery, especially lower limb or abdominal surgery.

Pathophysiology

There are a wide variety causes of urinary retention in women, and the exact mechanism is often multifactorial [1]. In general, the causes of urinary retention fall into two main categories of abnormal detrusor contractility or bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) [5]. Causes of abnormal detrusor activity, including detrusor underactivity or impaired contractility, can be neurologic, myogenic, or functional. Bladder outlet obstruction can result from anatomic causes, such as POP, or functional causes, such as dysfunctional voiding. These conditions disproportionately affect older women and most women have causes that fall within multiple categories [6].

Detrusor underactivity is characterized by decreased bladder contraction strength or duration that results in difficulty with bladder emptying [7]. Detrusor underactivity has been noted to affect 12–45% of older women undergoing urodynamic evaluation for lower urinary tract symptoms [7, 8]. There is no age-related differences in isometric detrusor function, but there is a tendency for the detrusor contraction to fail on initiation of voiding [9]. This may indicate there is a change in bladder contractile function as opposed to detrusor contractility specifically. Though the exact mechanism is unknown, an increased ratio of collagen to muscle in the detrusor, a gradual reduction in sensation, and a diminishing recognition by the brain of bladder filling are all thought to contribute to urinary retention due to detrusor underactivity in older women [6].

In addition, as women age, the ability to perceive when the bladder is full decreases. This decreased bladder sensation leads to volume hyposensitivity where the bladder fails to signal appropriate fullness, which causes over-distention and impaired bladder contractility, resulting in incomplete emptying [10]. Both detrusor contractile function and urine flow rate also worsen significantly with advancing age [7].

Though neurologic conditions affecting the autonomic or peripheral nerves or brain can cause urinary retention, it is most commonly seen in conditions affecting the spinal cord such as multiple sclerosis and traumatic or medical spinal cord injuries [11]. Loss of communication to the pontine micturition center results in neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction and often complete urinary retention requiring long-term catheterization [4].

Diabetic bladder dysfunction is increasingly common, and is estimated to affect 80–93% of those with diabetes [12]. Preclinical rodent models have revealed an early hyper-contractile bladder phenotype followed by a late hypo-contractile phenotype, which may explain why urinary retention is more often identified in older patients with diabetes [13]. There are four main hypotheses driving diabetic bladder dysfunction include smooth muscle dysfunction, urothelial dysfunction, peripheral neuropathy, and damaging circulating and systemic factors; however, further research is needed in humans to determine the exact mechanisms [12].

Medications are a common cause of urinary retention in older adults and can contribute to retention via a variety of proposed mechanisms (Table 1) [11, 14]. In one small study, medications were determined to be the most likely cause of 12% of cases of chronic urinary retention [15]. Furthermore, polypharmacy may place older adults at a higher risk of adverse side effects from drug–drug interactions, including urinary retention. Observational data suggest that as much as 10% of urinary retention episodes may be due to polypharmacy [5, 16]. Common culprits that decrease detrusor contractility include anticholinergic drugs such as oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin, and fesoterodine. These are prescribed for overactive bladder symptoms [17] and work by inhibiting parasympathetic muscarinic receptors in the detrusor muscle. In an open-label extension study of fesoterodine in patients aged > 65 years, there was found to be a 0.9% rate of urinary retention, which was only seen in men [3]. While AUR from anticholinergic drugs is rare in women, it may contribute to incomplete emptying, which can have a clinically significant impact on those with comorbid conditions.

Anticholinergic drugs may also contribute to cognitive decline and their use in older populations should be limited. A systematic review performed by Gibson et al. showed that 77% of studies report significantly increased cognitive decline with increased anticholinergic load [18]. Cognitive decline also negatively impacts voiding behaviors and may worsen urinary retention. Normal micturition requires patients to be able to recognize the need to void, planning where a toilet is located, and executing the motor functions necessary to void. This cognitive pathway becomes interrupted with polypharmacy and adverse effects of anticholinergic drugs further exacerbating the risk of urinary retention [8].

Alpha-adrenergic agonists, commonly prescribed for cardiac indications or found in nasal decongestants, can cause BOO by increasing the contractility of the bladder neck [19]. Both selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors can contribute to urinary retention by enhancing serotonin-mediated sympathetic activity causing impaired contractility. Serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, such as duloxetine, may further increase the risk of urinary retention as compared with other antidepressants as the increase in norepinephrine activity may increase sphincter tone resulting in BOO [20]. A recent pharmacovigilance study using the US Food and Drug Administration and Canadian databases found a rising trend in drug-related urinary retention events over the last two decades [21]. New signals emerged for abiraterone, valacyclovir, fluoxetine, and empagliflozin, though further research is needed to determine the effects of compounding comorbid conditions on these findings.

A common anatomic cause of BOO and urinary retention in older women is the development of POP [22]. Pelvic organ prolapse is thought to cause urinary retention via urethral kinking and/or compression [23]. Bladder outlet obstruction is diagnosed in 42% of women with POP referred for urodynamics, which is significantly higher than the 27% rate found in patients with lower urinary tract symptoms without POP [24]. The risk of urinary retention is greatest in higher grades of anterior vaginal wall prolapse. In addition, the prevalence of hydronephrosis in women with advanced prolapse is 30% [25]. Periurethral masses, such as urethral caruncles and urethral prolapse, often occur in post-menopausal women but are a rare cause of urinary retention unless there is concomitant and severe tissue edema or thrombosis [22]. Lastly, women who underwent a sling placement for incontinence, even many years ago, may have a slight obstruction due to over-tensioning of the sling, which when combined with worsening bladder contractility with age, can contribute to urinary retention.

Older women may develop a functional cause of BOO due to dysfunctional voiding, which is defined as an involuntary intermittent contraction of the peri-urethral striated muscle during voiding in a neurologically intact individual [26]. Long-standing voiding dysfunction in older women can lead to significantly elevated PVRs and urinary retention with complications including UTIs and decreased renal function [27].

Diagnostic Challenges in Women

A thorough history, physical exam, medication reconciliation, and PVR are necessary to diagnose urinary retention. Symptoms such as a weak stream, incomplete bladder emptying, and straining are common in men with urinary retention; however, these may not be specific in diagnosing retention in women. Among a large group of women who presented to a urologic provider with LUT, 10% reported a sense of incomplete bladder emptying on symptom questionnaires [28]. Among this group, however, only 10% of these women had a PVR over 100 mL, indicating that incomplete emptying offers little predictive value in estimating elevated PVR in women. Additionally, if a woman has an elevated PVR, do ensure that she has voided within the past 10 minutes and that she was trying to empty her bladder. Many patients with a perceived elevated PVR provide a urine specimen and stop their stream and do not fully empty or are nervous or hovering over the toilet. Encourage them to return to void and to sit on the toilet seat in a relaxed setting to fully empty. Bladder scanners may also report false elevations in patients with obesity, ascites, ovarian pathology, or residual peritoneal dialysate.

Assessment of the patient’s other medical conditions may also be helpful in identifying contributing factors to urinary retention. Constipation and prior episodes of retention can be predictive of elevated PVR [29]. In hospitalized women, low mobility and hypothyroidism were associated with asymptomatic elevated PVR [30]. For those patients with diabetes, a hemoglobin A1c should be obtained, as an elevated hemoglobin A1c (> 7.5%) has been associated with an increased risk of elevated PVR [31].

Urodynamics, specifically video urodynamics, may be helpful in determining the etiology of urinary retention and the risk of upper tract deterioration. Renal bladder ultrasound and a renal function panel should be considered for those with long-standing retention that may be contributing to renal failure. These studies should be conducted after a period of bladder decompression. In the setting of urinary retention, overdistention typically results in atonic bladder and poor bladder contractility, which may improve after catheterization. If urodynamics (UDS) are performed immediately after an episode of urinary retention, the resulting atony may mask other underlying causes of urinary retention.

Cystoscopy may also be beneficial to assess the severity of bladder trabeculations and the presence of diverticula. Bladder trabeculations are irregular thickened ridges or bands in the bladder wall that represent hypertrophy and remodeling of the detrusor muscle as a response to increased intravesical pressure. This is usually seen in the setting of BOO, though trabeculations have also been demonstrated in women with detrusor overactivity [32]. Diverticula are mucosal outpouches that herniate through the bladder muscle in the same setting. More severe trabeculations and the presence of diverticula are likely indicative of long-standing obstruction [33].

Some of the largest barriers to diagnosis are the underlying social concerns surrounding urinary retention. In one study in Australia, women over the age of 55 years admit that the main barrier to seeking help regarding their pelvic floor, bowel, and bladder dysfunction is the thought that pelvic floor dysfunction was a normal part of aging. Only until symptoms were more bothersome did patients eventually seek help [34]. In older adults, factors including poor mobility, cognitive impairment, polypharmacy, and limited access to care also affect the proportion of patients that are diagnosed.

An additional barrier lies in the access to specialist providers with the tools to appropriately diagnose urinary retention. Primary care providers lack the tools to evaluate urinary retention like urodynamic testing, electromyography, and ultrasound, requiring visit to a specialist to evaluate and manage this condition. This creates a referral bottleneck, as patients who need additional testing and care may have their diagnosis delayed, increasing the risk of long-term damage to the urinary tract [35].

Current Treatments

The first-line treatment for urinary retention is to identify reversible causes, including treating comorbid conditions, encouraging mobility, addressing constipation with laxatives or enemas, and/or identifying medications that may be contributing to incomplete bladder emptying.

Pharmacologic Treatments

A medication reconciliation should be done to identify and discontinue drugs that might impair detrusor contractility (creating a non-obstructive pattern) and/or increase outlet resistance (creating a functional obstruction) (Table 1). New onset urinary retention within 30 days of starting a new medication likely indicates a pharmacologic cause and discontinuing that medication should be discussed with the prescribing provider.

Alpha-blockers, including tamsulosin, alfuzosin, and terazosin, are considered a first-line medication for the treatment of urinary retention in men in the setting of benign prostatic hypertrophy. These medications help relax the bladder neck, prostate, and urethra to help aid with bladder emptying; however, there are less data on alpha-blocker use in women for urinary retention.

A pilot study of terazosin in a small cohort of women with functional BOO demonstrated subjective improvement in voiding symptoms and improvements in maximum flow rate and PVR [36]. In addition, in a randomized controlled multi-center trial, tamsulosin was associated with reduced rates of post-operative urinary retention after POP as compared with placebo [37]. A meta-analysis of prospective open-label trials found alpha-blockers effective in reducing urinary symptom scores, increasing the maximum flow rate and decreasing PVR. However, when compared to placebo in a separate analysis, the improvement was seen only in symptom scores, without a significant difference in maximum flow rate or PVR [38].

On the contrary, a trial conducted by Lee in 2018 assigned 197 women with voiding dysfunction to be treated with either alfuzosin 10 mg daily or placebo for 8 weeks. [39] Among the patients with severe BOO, both treatment and placebo groups demonstrated statistically significant improvements from baseline in the AUA Symptom Score, quality-of-life scores, PVRs, and maximum flow rate, but there were no significant differences between the placebo and experimental group Conversely, a randomized controlled trial of 78 older women with urinary retention in geriatric rehabilitation centers found that 62% recovered without pharmacologic treatment. Within the subset of the cohort that did require a medical intervention, the study prescribed doxazosin, which did not show statistically significant benefit as compared to placebo [40].

Potential adverse effects of alpha-blockers include dizziness, headache, fatigue, and gastrointestinal discomfort, which may be more prominent in older adults. A rare but feared complication of this mediation is the development of floppy iris syndrome and should be cautioned if they might need cataract surgery in the future. Importantly, stopping alpha-blockers does not eliminate the risk of intraoperative floppy iris syndrome, as the effect may persist despite cessation of the medication. A relative contraindication for alpha-blockers also includes baseline hypotension as these agents can exacerbate this symptom [33, 41].

Bethanechol, a muscarinic acetylcholine resistant to acetylcholinesterase, has historically been prescribed to improve detrusor contraction during voiding. However, numerous studies have failed to demonstrate significant benefit in improving bladder emptying. This coupled with the extremely unfavorable side effect profile, abdominal pain, diarrhea, excess salivation, lacrimation, miosis, hypotension, diaphoresis, and bronchial constriction, make it an unsafe and unwise choice for treatment [42].

Distigmine bromide is a long-acting reversible cholinesterase inhibitor that may also improve urinary retention. In a series of 27 patients with detrusor underactivity, Bougas et al. found a statistically significant reduction in PVR and a reduction in the need for catheterization in 11 patients [43]. Two patients experienced sweating and diarrhea, which were self-limiting and did not result in discontinuation of the drug. The use of distigmine bromide can induce a cholinergic crisis, as described in a case report where a patient experienced hypothermia, respiratory and circulatory failure resulting in intubation, and an extended stay in the intensive care unit [17, 44]. As such, this medication is not recommended routinely for the treatment of urinary retention.

Unfortunately, pharmacologic options for women with urinary retention are poorly studied. In patients with genitourinary syndrome of menopause and recurrent UTIs, vaginal estrogen may improve urethral compliance and treat vaginal atrophy [45, 46], though its direct benefit in those with urinary retention is still being studied. In the woman who is performing intermittent catheterization, vaginal estrogen can be prescribed to reduce irritation and discomfort and reduce her risk of UTI.

Use of pharmacologic therapy should be determined in the context of each patient and their other medical comorbidities, medications, and possible drug–drug interactions, as well as the side effect profiles of each drug. Given the benefit illustrated in some studies, the authors believe it reasonable to trial these medications for 2–4 weeks to improve bladder emptying in any woman with an elevated PVR, whether she is catheterizing or not. Better bladder emptying even in those who are minimally symptomatic reduces UTI risk and prevents bladder overdistension. Medications that were trialed with no impact on symptoms or PVR should be discontinued.

Adjunctive Therapies

In the woman whose retention does not resolve with medical therapy and who is found to have high tone pelvic floor, dysfunctional voiding or detrusor underactivity, pelvic floor physical therapy can be recommended. This involves functional retraining of the pelvic floor to improve both relaxation and coordination. A study conducted by Chiang et al. concluded that pelvic floor physical therapy with biofeedback improved their clinical symptoms, quality of life, and uroflowmetry in a group of 31 women between the ages of 18 and 77 years with dysfunctional voiding [47]. Further research is needed to determine the benefit of pelvic floor physical therapy specifically in older women with other etiologies of urinary retention.

For those women with functional obstruction, extracorporeal magnetic interventions are a non-invasive neuromodulation option to help patients learn to spontaneously contract and relax the pelvic floor muscles to improve voiding patterns. Patients sit on extracorporeal magnet devices that transmit a repeated stimulation of the motor nerve fibers at a defined frequency interval. Koh et al. demonstrated a synergistic response between extracorporeal magnetic devices and alpha-blockers, a possible option to improve efficacy for female patients who show minimal response to alpha-blocker therapy alone [48]. This study, however, was conducted in women under the age of 60 years, so further research is needed to determine the efficacy of this therapy in older adults.

In women with POP, a pessary can be used to immediately relieve a mechanical outlet obstruction. Pessaries may be used in the short term as a bridge to surgery to correct the prolapse, or in the long term for those who are not candidates for surgical correction. Routine pelvic exams should be conducted for those with long-term pessaries to identify and prevent erosion [10, 20, 25, 49].

Catheterization in Acute Retention

Management of urinary retention starts with a risk assessment to determine the necessary level of intervention (Fig. 1). For those patients with AUR, catheterization is recommended to decompress the bladder, either via intermittent catheterization or indwelling catheterization. Acute urinary retention can cause damage to the bladder through multiple mechanisms, including structural injury to the bladder wall, impaired detrusor contractility, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and neurologic dysfunction. Structurally, AUR causes disruption of the urothelial barrier, edema of the lamina propria, and distortion of the detrusor muscle bundles [50]. Once the bladder distends in an episode of AUR, there is significantly diminished contractile force, which may persist for weeks after the initial insult [51]. Bladder distension creates tissue hypoxia, triggering a cascade of cellular damage associated with marked inflammatory changes and functional impairment [52]. Prompt bladder decompression with a foley catheter halts the progression of ischemic injury, relieves the mechanical stress on bladder wall structures, and prevents hydronephrosis and renal deterioration [11].

Intermittent self-catheterization (ISC) has been shown to have lower rates of UTIs in the acute setting [53] and improved patient satisfaction and quality-of-life scores [54] as compared with indwelling catheters. Intermittent self-catheterization also maintains more natural bladder filling patterns, potentially preventing muscle atrophy, maintaining afferent signaling, and supporting neuroplastic adaptations critical for recovering function [55]. The caveat for the use of ISC is that older women may have limited functional capacity and may not be able to catheterize themselves [56] and genitourinary syndrome of menopause and resultant vaginal atrophy causes vaginal narrowing and more difficult urethral access [46].

The cause of the retention and how reversible it is can dictate the need of duration of catheterization and when to attempt a trial of void. For example, a woman discharged from hospital soon after surgery may only require a few days of catheterization while she recovers mobility and the medications that contributed to her retention are weaned, whereas a woman who is in retention from a prolapse may need to have the prolapse repaired before her retention will resolve. The resolution of retention in older adults often requires addressing several common risk factors, including mobility, pain, constipation, medication, and prior episodes of retention.

A reasonable approach would be to modify any risk factors for retention, start an alpha-blocker, and perform a trial of void in those women with indwelling catheters every 2 weeks to assess if the retention is resolving. A referral to urology would be appropriate in this situation. For those performing ISC, an attempt to void prior to catheterization can be done daily by the patient and once volume remains low (< 150 mL) several days in a row, ISC can be stopped. Acute urinary retention, particularly when provoked by a medical event or medication, is often resolved after 3–7 days with bladder drainage and reversal of the instigating event and does not need urologic intervention.

Long-Term Catheterization

Long-term indwelling urethral catheters may be easier for older women to manage but carry the risk of urethral erosion, which can present as early as 2 weeks and can often lead to irreversible urethral damage [57]. Suprapubic catheterization is preferred for long-term indwelling catheter use, as it eliminates the risk for urethral erosion and has been associated with improved bladder related quality of life [58]. Suprapubic catheters should be instituted once the urinary retention is deemed irreversible and the patient needs catheter management permanently. Some clinicians advocate for intermittent capping and drainage of long-term catheters to maintain normal bladder cycling similar to ISC; however, there are limited data to support this. In the setting of irreversible and high-risk chronic urinary retention, ISC or suprapubic catheters are recommended to reduce the risks associated with long-term indwelling urethral catheters.

Patients with chronic urinary retention and without high-risk features should not be offered intermittent catheterization or any procedure to reduce the measured PVR. These patients would not benefit from catheterization, as they are asymptomatic, at low risk of renal deterioration, and catheterization can be uncomfortable and lead to UTIs. Alpha-blocker medication can be trialed given the low risk of this medication. Some patients have no high-risk features but may have an elevated PVR with bothersome urinary symptoms including urgency, frequency, nocturia, or incontinence. In this instance and if alpha-blockers fail, a trial of ISC up to five times per day may be beneficial to determine if more regularly emptying the bladder may improve symptoms [59].

Catheterization remains a mainstay of treatment, but can have a significant impact on patient and caregiver quality of life and requires caregiver support for assistance with catheterizations or catheter exchanges. There are limited data specifically on women’s quality of life with catheterization and the impact on catheterization on caregivers.

Surgical Intervention

Correction of POP either through pessary or surgical approach often treats urinary retention in the absence of concomitant functional etiology. Surgical correction of prolapse may also be considered for those older adults with minimal anesthesia risk [6]. In a woman who has improvement in voiding with prolapse reduction either with a pessary or temporary reduction in the office, improved voiding after surgical prolapse repair is expected. Urodynamics performed with and without a prolapse reduction can determine if a surgical prolapse repair will improve urinary retention.

A woman who has had sling surgery and is in retention immediately post procedurally may need a sling revision, as the sling is obstructing. Clinicians often wait 2–6 weeks before intervening as often the post-operative retention resolves spontaneously. Alternatively, some women with a remote history of sling placement develop gradual urinary retention. The sling may have caused a mild obstruction that was not clinically significant at the time of placement; however, urinary retention may develop as bladder sensation and contractility declines with age. Urodynamic studies can diagnose retention due to a prior sling and sling division with or without urethrolysis can be performed at any time after sling placement to resolve retention.

Sacral neuromodulation has been proven as a good choice for female patients with chronic non-obstructive urinary retention, and should be considered on a patient-to-patient basis after expert care and discussion. Some evidence shows that sacral neuromodulation may be more successful in women than men, with a successful test phase rate of 62% in women versus 22% in men [60]. While female sex may increase efficacy, increasing age is associated with a lower chance of proceeding to a permanent implantation after the test phase [61].

There are some data suggesting that onabotulinumtoxinA injections in the external striated urethral sphincter may improve voiding in patients with detrusor underactivity (DU) by reducing outlet resistance and reducing the guarding reflex [62]. Retrospective studies have shown temporary improvement in voiding symptoms, recovery of spontaneous voiding, and improvement in urodynamic parameters [63, 64].

Emerging Treatments

Understanding and treating urinary retention is an active area of research. Alternative drugs that have been studied, but have not shown evidence suggesting significant benefit include sildenafil (a phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor that enhances nitric oxide-mediated urethral relaxation), or thyrotropin-releasing hormone (proposed to facilitate urethral relaxation through unclear pathways) [41]. Regenerative therapies, such as stem cell or gene therapies, are also being studied to help recover detrusor muscle and improve the strength of contraction. Proposed mechanisms of these therapies are via repleting myocytes, augmenting neuronal networks, or enhancing the power of existing myocytes [65].

Various molecular therapies are also being studied for use in chronic urinary retention. A 12-week treatment period with TAC-302, a fatty alcohol derivative that helps promote neurite outgrowth, has been shown to make significant improvements in bladder contraction and voiding efficiency [66]. Serotonergic, cannabinoid, and bombesin receptor agonists have similarly demonstrated an improvement in modulating the neural pathways involved in bladder control [65].

Despite the clinical significance of urinary retention in older women, the existing literature is notably deficient in randomized sex-specific trials that adequately account for the unique anatomical and physiological differences between male and female patients and the complex polypharmacy profiles common in this population. Furthermore, the lack of standardized outcome measures across studies makes it difficult to compare treatment efficacy and establish evidence-based clinical guidelines. Future research must prioritize well-designed randomized controlled trials specifically focused on female populations, including investigations of conservative management options that are safe for older patients when medications and surgery may not be feasible.

Conclusions

Urinary retention is increasingly common in the aging female population. Age-related changes in the mechanics of the lower urinary tract, along with the many comorbidities frequently experienced by the aging population contribute to this increased risk. Management is highly dependent on risk stratification for each patient, and treatment may vary for those who are acutely symptomatic and for those requiring a risk reduction of complications. Catheterization remains the standard treatment for acute or high-risk chronic urinary retention. For those who do not require an acute intervention, conservative treatments include a wide range of pharmacologic and non-pharmacologic options. Minimally invasive therapies, including sacral neuromodulation, have also shown promise in this population

Despite the increasing prevalence of urinary retention in aging women, there remains a dearth of research focused specifically on this population. Given the heterogeneity in comorbidities, functional status, and patient priorities, management should emphasize a patient-centered approach that aligns treatment strategies with individual goals and quality-of-life considerations. Future well-designed randomized controlled trials focused on this population are needed to better guide individualized care and optimize outcomes.
