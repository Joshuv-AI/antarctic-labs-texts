Beyond resource management: Reframing surgical preference cards as educational tools
Authors: Narges Rahmati, Majid Bagheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surgical Preference Cards (SPCs) have traditionally been regarded as tools for standardizing surgical procedures, optimizing resource utilization, and improving operating room efficiency. Consequently, their educational value has received relatively little attention. However, emerging evidence suggests that SPCs can improve clinical performance, self-efficacy, clinical skills, and competence while reducing anxiety among operating room students. Drawing on Cognitive Load Theory and educational scaffolding, we argue that SPCs have the potential to serve as cognitive supports that facilitate learning in the complex operating room environment. We propose that SPCs should be recognized not only as resource management tools but also as educational resources and advocate for their integration into operating room curricula and digital educational repositories.

Dear Editor,

Surgical Preference Cards (SPCs) have traditionally been recognized as valuable tools for standardizing surgical procedures, managing surgical supplies, reducing costs, and improving operating room efficiency [1], [2]. Consequently, research has predominantly focused on their managerial and operational functions, while their educational value in operating room training has received limited attention. This imbalance persists despite emerging evidence suggesting that SPCs can enhance student learning and clinical competence.

The operating room is widely recognized as one of the most complex clinical learning environments in healthcare education. Operating room students are required to acquire and integrate large amounts of information regarding surgical procedures, instruments, sutures, equipment, and surgeon-specific preferences within a limited period of time. Such complexity can impose a substantial cognitive burden on learners and may contribute to anxiety, reduced confidence, and suboptimal clinical performance. According to Cognitive Load Theory, learning is hindered when the volume and complexity of information exceed the learner's processing capacity (3). Likewise, educational scaffolding emphasizes the importance of providing structured support to facilitate learning in challenging educational environments (4).

From this perspective, SPCs represent an underrecognized educational opportunity. By organizing procedure-specific information in a structured and accessible format, SPCs can help students familiarize themselves with surgical workflows, required instruments, and surgeon preferences before entering the operating room. Given these characteristics, SPCs appear to have the potential to function as a form of cognitive scaffold, supporting learners in managing complex information and facilitating the transition from theoretical knowledge to clinical practice [3], [4].

Emerging evidence supports this perspective. Studies have shown that SPCs improve clinical performance (5), self-efficacy (6), clinical skills (7), and clinical competence while reducing anxiety among operating room students (8). Although the evidence base remains limited, findings have been remarkably consistent in demonstrating the educational benefits of SPCs.

Despite these promising findings, the educational role of SPCs continues to be overshadowed by their managerial and economic applications. It is time to reframe SPCs not only as resource management tools but also as educational resources that can meaningfully support operating room training.

To fully realize the educational potential of SPCs, educational initiatives should focus on three priorities: integrating SPCs into operating room curricula, developing centralized digital repositories of surgeon-specific preference cards, and evaluating their educational impact through well-designed multicenter studies. These strategies may enhance students' preparedness, promote self-directed learning, and bridge the gap between classroom instruction and clinical practice.

In conclusion, SPCs should no longer be viewed solely as tools for resource management but also as valuable educational resources.
