Bounds in Wasserstein Distance for Locally Stationary Functional Time Series
Jan Nino G. Tinio, Mokhtar Z. Alaya, Salim Bouzebda

Abstract
Functional time series (FTS) extend traditional methodologies to accommodate data observed as functions/curves. A significant challenge in FTS consists of accurately capturing the time-dependence structure, especially with the presence of time-varying covariates. When analyzing time series with time-varying statistical properties, locally stationary time series (LSTS) provide a robust framework that allows smooth changes in mean and variance over time. This work investigates Nadaraya-Watson (NW) estimation procedure for the conditional distribution of locally stationary functional time series (LSFTS), where the covariates reside in a semi-metric space endowed with a semi-metric. Under small ball probability and mixing condition, we establish convergence rates of NW estimator for LSFTS with respect to Wasserstein distance. The finite-sample performances of the model and the estimation method are illustrated through extensive numerical experiments both on functional simulated and real data.

Background on LSTS and Wasserstein distance

This section presents some preliminaries of LSTS and Wasserstein distance. We then introduce small ball probability and delineate the mixing condition employed to assess weak dependency.

2.1
Locally stationary time series

Let
T\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose that there exists a process of
T
random variables
\{Y_{t,T},X_{t,T}\}_{t=1,\ldots,T}
, where
Y_{t,T}
is real-valued, and
X_{t,T}
belongs to a semi-metric space
\mathscr{H}
with a semi-metric
\mathsf{D}(\cdot,\cdot)
. The semi-metric space
\mathscr{H}
can be Banach or Hilbert spaces with norm
\|\cdot\|
.
We consider the following regression estimation problem:

\displaystyle Y_{t,T}=m^{\star}\big(\frac{t}{T},X_{t,T}\big)+\varepsilon_{t,T},\text{ for all }t=1,\ldots,T,

(1)

where
\{\varepsilon_{t,T}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables independent of
\{X_{t,T}\}_{t=1,\ldots,T}
, i.e.,
\mathds{E}[\varepsilon_{t}|X_{t,T}]=0,
for all
t=1,\ldots,T
. The response
Y_{t,T}
is assumed to be integrable. The functional covariate
X_{t,T}
is assumed to be locally stationary, that dynamically changes slowly over time and hence can be considered approximately stationary at local time. Note that
m^{\star}\big(\frac{t}{T},X_{t,T}\big)=\mathds{E}[Y_{t,T}|X_{t,T}]
is the
oracle
conditional mean function in model
1
, and does not depend on real-time
t
but rather on rescaled time
u=\frac{t}{T}
. As the sample size
T
goes to infinity, these
u
-points form a dense subset of the unit interval
[0,1]
. Hence, at all rescaled
u
-points,
m^{\star}
is identified almost surely (a.s.) if it is continuous in the time direction. In LSTS, this rescaled time refers to transforming the original time scale.

Example.

Consider the process
X_{t,T}=a\big(\frac{t}{T}\big)+\varepsilon_{t}
,
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
,
T\in\mathbb{N}
, where
a(\cdot)
is a continuous function
a:[0,1]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and a sequence of i.i.d. random variables
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
. The process
X_{t,T}
behaves “almost” stationary for
t
close to
t^{\prime}
, for some
t^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, that is,
a\big(\frac{t^{\prime}}{T}\big)\approx a\big(\frac{t}{T}\big)
. However, this process is not weakly stationary. Local stationarity gives a more realistic concept that allows this kind of change
[
17
]
.

Let us now formally define the notion of LSTS. We adopt the definition given in
[
48
]
.

Definition 1
.

An
\mathscr{H}
-valued process
\{X_{t,T}\}_{t=1,\ldots,T}
is locally stationary if for each rescaled time point
u\in[0,1]
, there exists an associated
\mathscr{H}
-valued process
\{X_{t}(u)\}_{t=1,\ldots,T}
verifying

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}\big({X_{t,T},X_{t}(u)}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\Big|\frac{t}{T}-u\Big|+\frac{1}{T}\Big)U_{t,T}(u)\text{ a.s.,}

(2)

where
\{U_{t,T}(u)\}_{t=1,\ldots,T}
is a positive process such that
\mathds{E}\big[(U_{t,T}(u))^{\rho}\big]<C_{U}
for
some
\rho>0
and
C_{U}<\infty
independent of
u,t,
and
T
.

From this definition, if an
\mathscr{H}
-valued process
\{X_{t,T}\}_{t=1,\ldots,T}
is locally stationary, a strictly stationary process
\{X_{t}(u)\}_{t=1,\ldots,T}
can always be found around each rescaled time
u
, which will be used to approximate
\{X_{t,T}\}_{t=1,\ldots,T}
. This approximation will result in a negligible difference between random variables
X_{t,T}
and
X_{t}(u)
. Since the
\rho
-th moments of the positive random variables
U_{t,T}(u)
are uniformly bounded,
U_{t,T}(u)=O_{\mathds{P}}(1)

(
71
)
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}\big({X_{t,T},X_{t}(u)}\big)

\displaystyle=O_{\mathds{P}}\Big(\Big|\frac{t}{T}-u\Big|+\frac{1}{T}\Big).

If
u=\frac{t}{T}
, then
\mathsf{D}\big({X_{t,T},X_{t}(\frac{t}{T})}\big)\leq\frac{C_{U}}{T}
. The constant
\rho
can be considered as an indicator of how well this approximation is being made: larger
\rho
indicates a better approximation of
X_{t,T}
by
X_{t}(u)
and moderate bounds for their absolute difference.

when

is the Hilbert space

\|f\|_{2}=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle},\quad\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}f(t)g(t)\mathrm{d}t,

2.2
Wasserstein distance

Let
\mathcal{P}_{r}({\mathbb{R}})
be the set of Borel probability measures in
{\mathbb{R}}
having finite
r
-th moment
(r\geq 1)
, i.e.,

\mathcal{P}_{r}({\mathbb{R}})=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}):\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{r}\mu(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty\}.

Given probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{r}({\mathbb{R}})
, we calculate the distance between them using the
r
th-Wasserstein distance,
W_{r}(\mu,\nu)
, as follows

\displaystyle W_{r}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\Big(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}}{|u-v|}^{r}\pi(\mathrm{d}u,\mathrm{d}v)\Big)^{1/r},

(3)

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. Optimal couplings always exist since
{\mathbb{R}}
is a complete and separable metric space, where the infimum is, in fact, a minimum
(
70
)
. Equation ( def:W1_general ) signifies that
W_{r}(\mu,\nu)
is the infimum of expectation of the distance between two random variables over all possible couplings, i.e.,
W_{r}(\mu,\nu)=\big(\inf_{U\sim\mu,\text{ }V\sim\nu}\mathds{E}[|U-V|^{r}]\big)^{1/r}
.

We can represent a simple optimal coupling by a probability inverse transform: given
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{r}({\mathbb{R}})
, let
F_{\mu}(\cdot)
and
F_{\nu}(\cdot)
be the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) and
F_{\mu}^{-1}(\cdot)
and
F_{\nu}^{-1}(\cdot)
be the respective generalized inverse or quantile functions defined as
F_{\mu}^{-1}(z):=\inf\{v\in{\mathbb{R}}:\mu((-\infty,v])\geq z\}
for all
z\in[0,1]
(similarly for
F_{\nu}^{-1}(z)
). Then, assuming a random variable
Z
uniformly distributed on
(0,1)
, an optimal coupling
(U,V)=(F_{\mu}^{-1}(Z),F_{\nu}^{-1}(Z))
can be established
(
32
,
35
)
. Hence, the minimization problem ( def:W1_general ) can be represented by

\displaystyle W_{r}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\Big(\int_{0}^{1}{\left|F_{\mu}^{-1}(z)-F_{\nu}^{-1}(z)\right|}^{r}\mathrm{d}z\Big)^{1/r},

in a one-dimensional context. Specifically, for
r=1
and using a change of variable, the
1
-Wasserstein distance is represented as

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|F_{\mu}(v)-F_{\nu}(v)|\mathrm{d}v.

(4)

Consequently,
W_{1}(\mu,\nu)
can be considered as the
L_{1}
-distance between the CDFs
F_{\mu}(\cdot)
and
F_{\nu}(\cdot)
.

2.3
Small ball probability

The absence of a density function for functional random variables is a technical difficulty in infinite-dimensional spaces since we lack a universal reference such as the Lebesgue measure. We overcome this using the
small ball probability
property. We control the concentration of probability measure of the functional variable on a small ball using a function
\phi(r)
defined as, for all
r>0
and a fixed
x\in\mathscr{H}
,

\mathds{P}[X\in B(x,r)]=:\phi_{x}(r)>0.

where
B(x,r)=\{v\in\mathscr{H}:\mathsf{D}(x,v)\leq r.\}

Assume that
r
is a function of
T
such that
r=r(T)\rightarrow 0
as
T\rightarrow\infty
. If we take
T
very large,
B(x,r)
is then considered as a small ball; hence,
\mathds{P}[X\in B(x,r)]
is a small ball probability
(
40
)
. Unfortunately, obtaining
\mathds{P}[X\in B(x,r)]
is complicated
(
38
)
. For a survey on the main results on small ball probability, refer to
[
49
]
. In most cases, it is fitting to suppose that, as
r\rightarrow 0
,

\mathds{P}[X\in B(x,r)]\sim\psi(x)\phi(r),

(5)

where
\mathds{E}[\psi(X)]=1
, a necessary normalizing restriction to ensure the identifiability of the decomposition. There are two main reasons for conveniently assuming ( eqn: assumed factorization on small ball ). First, the function
\psi(x)
can be thought as a surrogate density of the functional
X
and can be utilized in different frameworks where the surrogate density is estimated differently and is used for classification purposes. Second, the function
\phi(r)
signifies the volumetric term that can be used to evaluate the complexity of the probability law of the process
(
10
)
. In the
d
-dimensional case
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we suppose
\phi(r)\sim r^{d}
, which is commonly referred as the curse of dimensionality
(
40
,
37
)
. The intrinsic nature of the probability effects, involving small balls, is apparent in infinite-dimensional framework. We give some examples of several forms of
\phi(r)
that can also be found in
[
37
,
17
]
.

Fractional Brownian Motion.

Considering the space
\mathcal{C}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}})
with the supremum norm and its Cameron-Martin associated space
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{C}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}})^{CM}
.
Using Theorems 3.1 and 4.6 in
[
49
]
, for
0<\eta<2
, we have

\forall x\in\mathcal{F},\quad C^{\prime}_{x}\mathrm{e}^{r^{-2/\eta}}\leq\mathds{P}[\zeta^{FBM}\in B(x,r)]\leq C_{x}\mathrm{e}^{r^{-2/\eta}},

where
\zeta^{FBM}
is the usual Fractional Brownian Motion with parameter
\eta
and
B(x,r)=\{\zeta^{FBM}\in\mathcal{F}:\|\zeta^{FBM}-x\|_{\infty}\leq r\}
. In this example, for the Fractional Brownian process, we choose
\phi(r)
of the form

\phi^{FBM}(r)\sim\mathrm{e}^{r^{-2/\eta}}.

Gaussian process.

Next, let us consider the centered Gaussian process
\zeta^{GP}=\{\zeta_{t}^{GP},0\leq t\leq 1\}
, which can be expanded by Karhunen-Loève decomposition as

\zeta_{t}^{GP}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}Z_{i}f_{i}(t),

where
\lambda_{i}
’s are the eigenvalues of the covariance operator of
\zeta^{GP}
,
f_{i}
’s are the associated orthonormal eigenfunctions, and
Z_{i}
’s are independent standard normal real random variables. The orthogonal projection onto the subspace spanned by the eigenfunctions
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\}
is denoted by
\Xi_{k}
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Define a semi-metric by

\mathsf{D}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}(\Xi_{k}(x-y)(t))^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

Using the Karhunen-Loève expansion, we get

\mathsf{D}(\zeta^{GP},x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big(\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}Z_{i}-x_{i}\Big)^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{i}^{2},

where

x_{i}=\int_{0}^{1}x(t)f_{i}(t)\mathrm{d}t,\quad i=1,\ldots,k.

\mathsf{D}(\zeta^{GP},x)
can be written in terms of the usual Euclidian norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
of a vector
\chi=(\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{k})
. Since
\chi_{i}
’s are independent real random variables with density with respect to the Lebesgue measure, we have, for
B(x,r)=\{\zeta^{GP}\in\mathcal{F}:\mathsf{D}(\zeta^{GP},x)<r\}

\mathds{P}[\zeta^{GP}\in B(x,r)]\sim r^{k}.

Ornstein-Unhlenbeck process.

Lastly, considering the same space in (i) and the metric
\mathsf{D}(\cdot,\cdot)
associated with the supremum norm

\forall x\in\mathcal{C}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}),\quad\|x\|_{\infty}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|x(t)|.

We denote the Wiener measure on
\mathcal{C}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}})
by
\mathds{P}^{W}
and the associated functional Cameron-Martin space of
\mathcal{C}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}})
is given by
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{C}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}})^{CM}
. Moreover, we denote the standard Wiener process by
w
and let us consider the Ornstein-Unhlenbeck process
\zeta^{OU}
defined by
\zeta_{0}^{OU}=0
and by

\mathrm{d}\zeta_{t}^{OU}=\mathrm{d}w_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{t}^{OU},\quad\forall t,0<t\leq 1.

By
[
9
]
, the small centered ball Wiener measures are known to be of the form

\mathds{P}^{W}[\|x\|_{\infty}\leq r]\sim\frac{4}{\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi^{2}/8r^{2}}.

The process
\zeta^{OU}
has a probability measure that is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathds{P}^{W}

\forall x\in\mathcal{F},\quad\mathds{P}^{W}[\|x-\zeta^{OU}\|_{\infty}\leq r]\sim C_{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\pi^{2}/8r^{2}}.

For Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, we choose

\phi^{OU}(r)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\pi^{2}/8r^{2}}.

2.4
Mixing condition

The degree of dependence between observations of a stochastic process as they become distant apart in time is measured using mixing coefficients. Mixing processes were introduced to generalize the law of large numbers for non-i.i.d. stochastic processes. For effective modeling and inference, selecting the appropriate mixing condition for a stochastic process is crucial
(
58
,
33
,
63
)
. One of the mixing criteria usually considered is
\beta
-mixing. It has been applied to demonstrate moment inequalities and central limit theorems
(
31
,
13
,
60
)
.

Definition 2
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathds{P})
be a probability space,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
be subfields of
\mathcal{A}
, and set
\beta(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C})=\mathds{E}[\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}|\mathds{P}(C)-\mathds{P}(C|\mathcal{B})|]
.
For any array
\{Z_{t,T}:1\leq t\leq T\}
, define the coefficient

\displaystyle\beta(k)

\displaystyle=\sup_{1\leq t\leq T-k}\beta\big(\sigma(Z_{s,T},1\leq s\leq t),\sigma(Z_{s,T},t+k\leq s\leq T)\big),

where
\sigma(Z)
denotes the
\sigma

This definition implies that if a process is

\beta

3
Nadaraya-Watson estimation with Wasserstein distance

We denote the conditional probability distribution of
Y_{t,T}|X_{t,T}=x
by
\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x)
and its conditional CDF by
F_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x)
, for a fixed
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
and
x\in\mathscr{H}
. The mean conditional function reads as

m^{\star}(\frac{t}{T},x)=\mathds{E}_{\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x)}[Y_{t,T}|X_{t,T}=x]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}y\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{t}^{\star}(y|x).

Setting
K_{1},K_{2}
two
1
-dimensional basic kernel functions and
h
a bandwidth that depends on the sample size
T
, i.e.,
h=h(T)
with
h(T)\rightarrow 0
as
T\rightarrow\infty
.
For ease of notation, we set the scaled kernels
K_{h,i}(\cdot)=K_{i}(\frac{\cdot}{h}),
for
i=1,2
. Next, we define the considered NW estimator.

Definition 3
.

The NW estimator of
\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x)
is given by

\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x)=\sum_{a=1}^{T}\omega_{a}(\frac{t}{T},x)\delta_{Y_{a,T}},

where

\displaystyle\omega_{a}(\frac{t}{T},x)=\frac{\displaystyle K_{h,1}(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T})K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))}{\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T})K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))}.

(6)

NW estimator of the conditional CDF
F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)
can be written as, for all
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\hat{F}_{t}(y|x)=\sum_{a=1}^{T}\omega_{a}(\frac{t}{T},x)\mathds{1}_{Y_{a,T}\leq y}.

(7)

This definition extends the estimator considered in
[
66
]
to a functional covariate
X_{t,T}
. The weights
\{\omega_{a}(u,x)\}_{a=1,\ldots,T}
are assumed to be measurable functions of
x
,
X_{a,T}
, and
u
but do not depend on
Y_{a,T}
. Note that in
[
48
]
, the NW estimator of
m^{\star}(u,x)
is given by

\hat{m}(u,x)=\sum_{a=1}^{T}\omega_{a}(u,x)Y_{a,T}.

(8)

Remark.

We are using two kernel functions: one with respect to the rescaled time
u=\frac{t}{T}
and the other in the direction of the functional
X_{t,T}
. To appropriately assign weights
\omega_{a}(\frac{t}{T},x)
, we smooth with respect to the rescaled time and the space-direction of the covariates
X_{t,T}
to analyze the local behavior of the data
(
71
)
. We consider a single bandwidth
h
for the kernels
K_{h,i}(\cdot)
; however,
h
could also be different for
K_{h,1}(\cdot)
and
K_{h,2}(\cdot)

(
64
)
.

3.1

The following assumptions are conventional in the literature of LSTS

Assumption 1

(Local stationarity)
.

Assume that the
\mathscr{H}
-valued process

is locally stationary approximated by

for each time point

Assumption 2

(Kernel functions)
.

The kernel
K_{1}(\cdot)
is symmetric about zero, bounded and has compact support, that is,
K_{1}(v)=0
for all
|v|>C_{1}
for some
C_{1}<\infty
. On the other hand, the kernel
K_{2}(\cdot)
is bounded, and has a compact support in
[0,1]
such that
0<K_{2}(0)
and
K_{2}(1)=0
. In addition,
K_{2}^{\prime}(v)=\mathrm{d}K_{2}(v)/\mathrm{d}v
exists on
[0,1]

C_{1}^{\prime}\leq K_{2}^{\prime}(v)\leq C_{2}^{\prime}
, for real constants
-\infty<C_{1}^{\prime}<C_{2}^{\prime}<0
. Moreover, for
i=1,2
,
K_{i}(\cdot)
, is Lipschitz continuous, that is,
|K_{i}(v)-K_{i}(v^{\prime})|\leq L_{i}|v-v^{\prime}|
for some
L_{i}<\infty
and all
v,v^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We further assume the following:

\int K_{i}(z)\mathrm{d}z=1,\quad\text{and}\quad\int zK_{1}(z)\mathrm{d}z=0.

(9)

Assumption Assumption: X is lsp formalizes the property of the functional covariate
X_{t,T}
as locally stationary. Assumption Assumption: kernel functions is standard in literature. The conditions that
K_{i}
is compactly supported and Lipschitz implies that the kernel function has a bounded rate of change and is essential in obtaining upper bounds. First condition in ( eqn: some properties of K ) is a normalization, ensuring that the kernel can be interpreted as a probability density function. We assume that
K_{2}(\cdot)
is compactly supported in
[0,1]
; that is, it is a kernel of type II
(
41
)
. Second condition implies that
K_{1}(\cdot)
is symmetric around the origin, and it ensures that it does not introduce first-order linear bias when applied to the data.

Assumption 3

(Small ball probability)
.

Let
B(x,h)=\{v\in\mathscr{H}:\mathsf{D}(x,v)\leq h\}
denote a ball centered at
x\in\mathscr{H}
with radius
h
. We assume that for all
u\in[0,1]
,
x\in\mathscr{H}
, and
h>0
, there exists positive constants
C^{\prime}<C
, such that

0<C^{\prime}\phi(h)\psi(x)\leq\mathds{P}[X_{t}(u)\in B(x,h)]=:F_{u}(h;x)\leq C\phi(h)\psi(x),

(10)

where
\phi(0)\rightarrow 0
and
\phi(u)
is absolutely continuous in a neighborhood of the origin, and
\psi(x)
is a nonnegative functional in
x\in\mathscr{H}
.

Assumption 4

(Regularity condition on
h
and
\phi(h)
)
.

Assume that as
T\rightarrow\infty
, the bandwidth
h
satisfies
T^{\frac{1}{2}}h\phi(h)\rightarrow\infty
.

Assumption Assumption: small ball gives condition on the distributional behavior of the variables. Equation ( eqn: CDF bounded by small ball ) controls the behavior of the small ball probability around zero. The small ball probability can be approximately expressed as the product of two independent functions
\phi(\cdot)
and
\psi(\cdot)
. This condition corresponds to the assumption used in
[
43
,
48
,
16
]
. On the other hand, Assumption Assumption: bandwidth indicates that
h
should converge slower to zero, for instance, at a polynomial rate, i.e.,
h=\mathcal{O}(T^{-\xi})
, for small
\xi>0
. As
h
approaches zero,
\phi(h)
also goes to zero. Assumption Assumption: bandwidth is a strengthening of the condition in
[
48
]
that
Th\phi(h)\rightarrow\infty
and is needed to guarantee our resulting convergence rates.
With this, for Fractal-type processes, Assumption Assumption: bandwidth holds true when we choose
h\sim T^{-\xi}
for
0<\xi<\frac{1}{2(1+\tau_{0})}
and
\phi(h)\sim h^{\tau_{0}}
for some
\tau_{0}>1

(
16
,
1
)
. To see different expressions of the function
\phi(h)
, one may refer to
[
41
,
9
]
for some discussions on fractal-type processes,
[
52
]
for diffusion processes, and
[
49
]
for general Gaussian processes. We have given examples of the forms of
\phi(\cdot)
in Subsection subsection: small ball probability .

Assumption 5

(Conditional CDF)
.

The conditional CDF
F_{\cdot}^{\star}(\cdot|\cdot)
is Lipschitzian, i.e.,
\big|F_{a}^{\star}(\cdot|x)-F_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x^{\prime})\big|\leq L_{F^{\star}}\big(\mathsf{D}(x,x^{\prime})+\big|\frac{a}{T}-\frac{t}{T}\big|\big)
, for some
L_{F^{\star}}<\infty
, and for all
a,t\in\{1,\ldots,T\},

x,x^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}
.

The conditional CDF

should behave smoothly and not change much as the observation does, as assumed in
[
15
,
53
,
66
]
. We do not assume that the conditional CDF is twice differentiable, in contrast to
[

,
39
,
57
]

Assumption 6

(Mixing condition)
.

The process
\{(X_{t,T},\varepsilon_{t,T})\}
is arithmetically
\beta
-mixing satisfying
\beta(k)\leq Ak^{-\gamma}
for some
A>0
and

. We also assume that for some

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{\zeta}\beta(k)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}<\infty.

(11)

Assumption 7

(Blocking condition)
.

There exists a sequence of positive integers

satisfying

and

, as

For dependent sequences estimation technique, Assumptions Assumption: mixing and assumption: blocking are helpful. A more robust type of independence between far-off observations in a process is the

-mixing

60

is highlighted by condition ( eqn: infinite sum of betas is finite ).
Bernstein’s blocking approach was utilized to create independent blocks in the proof of Theorem Theorem: convergence of EW1

3.3
Bandwidth selection criterion

In nonparametric kernel estimation, especially NW, the bandwidth must be suitably selected for the estimator to perform well. Bandwidth selection methods have already been established and developed in
[
61
,
20
]
. This paper considers the leave-one-out cross-validation procedure used in
[
5
,
61
]
. For any fixed
i\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, we define

\hat{m}_{i}(\frac{t}{T},x)=\sum_{a=1;a\neq i}^{T}\omega_{a}(\frac{t}{T},x)Y_{a,T},

(13)

where
\omega_{a}(\frac{t}{T},x)
is given by ( def: weights ). Equation ( eqn: leave-one-out conditional CDF ) is regarded as the leave-out-
(X_{i,T},Y_{i,T})
estimator of
m_{i}^{\star}(\frac{t}{T},x)
. To minimize the quadratic loss function, we introduce the following criterion

CV(y,x,h):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\big(Y_{i,T}-\hat{m}_{i}(\frac{t}{T},x)\big)^{2}\widetilde{g}(X_{i,T}),

(14)

for some non-negative weight function
\widetilde{g}(\cdot)
. As highlighted in
[
61
]
, we choose a bandwidth
\hat{h}
among
h\in[a_{T},b_{T}]
that minimizes ( eqn: CV w tilde ). For bandwidths that are locally chosen by data-driven method, according to
[
5
]
, we replace ( eqn: CV w tilde ) by

CV(y,x,h):=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\big(Y_{i,T}-\hat{m}_{i}(\frac{t}{T},x)\big)^{2}\hat{g}(X_{i,T}).

In practice, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, we take the uniform global weights
\widetilde{g}(X_{i,T})=1
, or the local weights

\hat{g}(X_{i,T},x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\mathsf{D}(X_{i,T},x)\leq h$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

5
Conclusion

We proposed a NW conditional distribution estimator for LSFTS and established its convergence rates with respect to Wasserstein distance. These rates depend on the bandwidth
h
and the small ball probability
\phi(h)
. We provided the convergence rates for a fractal-type process with
h=\mathcal{O}(T^{-\xi})
and
\phi(h)=h^{\tau_{0}}
, for
0<\xi<\frac{1}{2(1+\tau_{0})}
and
\tau_{0}>1
. Numerical synthetic and real-world data experiments were conducted, supported by a data-generating algorithm designed to calculate the NW estimator.

This work also outlines promising directions for future research. One avenue involves modifying the basic indicator function to an integrated kernel
H_{g}(y-Y_{t,T})
, where
H
is a smooth cumulative distribution function (CDF) and
H_{g}(y-Y_{t,T})
serves as a local weighting function with bandwidth
g
, analogous to
h
. Another possible extension is amending the NW estimator to handle missing data. While expanding our results to encompass functional ergodic data would be highly valuable, it requires substantial mathematical advancements and lies beyond the current scope of this paper.

B.1
Proof of Theorem Theorem: convergence of EW1

Recall that
\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x)
is the probability measure of the random variable
Y_{t,T}|X_{t,T}=x
with conditional CDF
F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)=\mathds{P}[Y_{t,T}\leq y|X_{t,T}=x]
. Observe that, by the definition of
W_{1}
given in ( def:W1_cdf ),

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[W_{1}(\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x),\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x))]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\big[\int\big|\hat{F}_{t}(y|x)-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)\big|\mathrm{d}y\big]

\displaystyle=\int\mathds{E}\big[\big|\hat{F}_{t}(y|x)-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)\big|\big]\mathrm{d}y,

using Fubini’s theorem. Observe that, using ( def: weights ) and ( eqn:CDF of pi-hat ),

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{t}(y|x)-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\mathds{1}_{Y_{a,T}\leq y}}{\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))}-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\big[\mathds{1}_{Y_{a,T}\leq y}-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)\big]}{\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))}.

Further, by applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[W_{1}(\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x),\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x))]

\displaystyle=\int\mathds{E}\Big[\Big|\frac{\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\big[\mathds{1}_{Y_{a,T}\leq y}-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)\big]}{\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))}\Big|\Big]\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\int\Big(\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(\frac{1}{\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))}\Big)^{2}\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Big(\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\big[\mathds{1}_{Y_{a,T}\leq y}-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)\big]\Big)^{2}\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y.

Let
J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)=\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))
. Using Proposition lemma: J1 is Op(1) ,
J_{t,T}^{-1}(\frac{t}{T},x)=\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}(1)
. Hence, the first term in ( eqn: EW1 cauchy_schwarz sums ) becomes

\displaystyle\Big(\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(\frac{1}{\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))}\Big)^{2}\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}(1).

(19)

Additionally, from Proposition prop: control of square of sums , the second term in ( eqn: EW1 cauchy_schwarz sums ) is shown to be
\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{1}{T^{\frac{1}{2}}h\phi(h)}+h\big)
. Therefore, from ( eqn: EW1 cauchy_schwarz sums ) and combining ( eqn: bound E of S square ) and ( eqn: J inv Op1 ), we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[W_{1}\big(\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x),\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x)\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Big(\frac{1}{T^{\frac{1}{2}}h\phi(h)}+h\Big).

B.2
Proof of Corollary Remark: bound EW_s-s

Using the definition of
W_{1}
and noting that
y\in[-M,M]
, we have

\displaystyle W_{r}^{r}(\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x),\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x))

\displaystyle\leq(2M)^{r-1}\int_{-M}^{M}|\hat{F}_{t}(y|x)-F_{t}^{\star}(y|x)|\mathrm{d}y.

B.3
Proof of Corollary corollary: convergence of the 2nd moment

Again, we used the definition of
W_{1}
given by ( def:W1_cdf ). Additionally, by using Minkowski’s integral inequality given by ( eqn: minkowski’s inequality ), for
r=2
we have

\displaystyle\|W_{1}\big(\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x),\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x)\big)\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle={\Big\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big|\hat{F}_{t}(y|x)-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)\big|\mathrm{d}y\Big\|}_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\big\|\hat{F}_{t}(y|x)-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)\big\|}_{L_{2}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\mathds{E}\big[\big(\hat{F}_{t}(y|x)-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)\big)^{2}\big]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(\frac{Z_{t,T}(y,x)}{J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)}\Big)^{2}\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y,

using ( eqn: Fhat - Fstar ) and ( eqn: Z tT ). However, using Proposition lemma: J1 is Op(1) ,
J_{t,T}^{-1}(\frac{t}{T},x)=\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}(1)
. So

\displaystyle\|W_{1}\big(\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x),\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x)\big)\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\mathds{E}\big[Z_{t,T}^{2}(y,x)\big]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\frac{1}{Th^{2}\phi^{2}(h)}+h^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}y,

by Proposition prop: control of square of sums . Therefore,

\displaystyle\|W_{1}\big(\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x),\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x)\big)\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Big(\frac{1}{T^{\frac{1}{2}}h\phi(h)}+h\Big).

B.4
Proof of Proposition prop: —mhat-m— leq W1

Observe that

\displaystyle|\hat{m}(\frac{t}{T},x)-m^{\star}(\frac{t}{T},x)|

\displaystyle=|\mathds{E}[\hat{Y}_{t,T}|X_{t,T}=x]-\mathds{E}[Y_{t,T}|X_{t,T}=x]|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{y}\mathrm{d}\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\mathrm{d}\pi^{\star}_{t}(\cdot|x)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\mathrm{d}\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\mathrm{d}\pi^{\star}_{t}(\cdot|x)\Big|

\displaystyle=W_{1}(\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x),\pi^{\star}_{t}(\cdot|x)).

The duality formula of the Kantorovich-Rubinstein distance is used in the last equality (see Remark 6.5 in
[
70
]
), where
\mathcal{F}
is the set of all continuous functions satisfying the Lipschitz condition
\|f\|_{Lip}\leq 1
, i.e.,
\sup_{y\neq y^{\prime}}\frac{|f(y)-f(y^{\prime})|}{|y-y^{\prime}|}\leq 1
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[|\hat{m}(\frac{t}{T},x)-m^{\star}(\frac{t}{T},x)|\big]\leq\mathds{E}\big[W_{1}(\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x),\pi^{\star}_{t}(\cdot|x))\big].

This finishes the proof.

B.5
Proof of Proposition corollary: convergence of EW1 chosen h

If
h=\mathcal{O}(T^{-\xi})
and
\phi(h)=h^{\tau_{0}}
, for
\tau_{0}>1
, then directly from Theorem Theorem: convergence of EW1 ,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[W_{1}\big(\hat{\pi}_{t}(\cdot|x),\pi_{t}^{\star}(\cdot|x)\big)\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{T^{\frac{1}{2}}h\phi(h)}+h

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{T^{\frac{1}{2}}T^{-\xi}T^{-\xi\tau_{0}}}+\frac{1}{T^{\xi}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{T^{\frac{1}{2}-\xi(1+\tau_{0})}}+\frac{1}{T^{\xi}},

which goes to zero if
0<\xi<\frac{1}{2(1+\tau_{0})}

Proofs of Propositions Lemma: E of K2 , lemma: J1 is Op(1) , and prop: control of square of sums

C.1
Proof of Proposition Lemma: E of K2

(i)

Using Assumption Assumption: kernel functions , we note that
K_{2}
is Lipshitz. In addition, by Assumption Assumption: X is lsp and when
u=\frac{t}{T}
,
\mathsf{D}\big(X_{a,T},X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\leq\frac{1}{T}U_{t,T}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)
, where
\mathds{E}\big[\big(U_{t,T}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)^{\rho}\big]<C_{U}
. So,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))-K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\big[K_{2}\big(\frac{\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T})}{h}\big)-K_{2}\big(\frac{\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)}{h}\big)\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}}{h}\mathds{E}\big[\big|\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T})-\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}}{h}\mathds{E}\big[\big|\mathsf{D}\big(X_{a,T},X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}}{h}\mathds{E}\big[\big|\frac{1}{T}U_{t,T}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}C_{U}}{Th}.

(ii)

We have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\big]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))-K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)+K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))-K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big]+\mathds{E}\big[K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}C_{U}}{Th}+\mathds{E}\big[K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big],

using
(i)
. Now using Assumption Assumption: small ball ,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{E}\Big[\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\leq h}\Big]=\mathds{P}\big[X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\in B(x,h)\big]

\displaystyle=F_{t/T}(h;x)\leq C_{d}\phi(h)\psi(x).

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{t,T}))\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}C_{U}}{Th}+C_{d}\phi(h)\psi(x).

(iii)

Note that using Assumption assumption: CDF ,
\big|F_{a}^{\star}(y|X_{a,T})-F_{t}^{\star}(y|x)\big|\leq L_{F^{\star}}\big(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T})+\big|\frac{a}{T}-\frac{t}{T}\big|\big)
. Now see that

\displaystyle K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\mathds{E}\Big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))[\mathds{1}_{Y_{a,T}\leq y}-F_{t}^{\star}(y|x)]\Big]

\displaystyle\leq K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\mathds{E}\Big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\mathds{E}\Big[\big(\mathds{1}_{Y_{a,T}\leq y}-F_{t}^{\star}(y|x)\big)\Big|X_{a,T}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle\leq K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\mathds{E}\Big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\big|F_{a}^{\star}(y|X_{a,T})-F_{t}^{\star}(y|x)\big|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq L_{F^{\star}}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\mathds{E}\Big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\big(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T})+\big|\frac{a}{T}-\frac{t}{T}\big|\big)\Big].

However, using Assumption Assumption: kernel functions ,
\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T})\leq C_{2}h
otherwise,
K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))=0
. Additionally,
\big|\frac{a}{T}-\frac{t}{T}\big|\leq C_{1}h
otherwise,
K_{h,1}\big(\big|\frac{a}{T}-\frac{t}{T}\big|\big)=0
. So,

\displaystyle K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\mathds{E}\Big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))[\mathds{1}_{Y_{a,T}\leq y}-F_{t}^{\star}(y|x)]\Big]

\displaystyle\leq L_{F^{\star}}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\Big\{C_{2}h\mathds{E}\Big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\Big]+C_{1}h\mathds{E}\Big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\Big]\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq(C_{1}+C_{2})L_{F^{\star}}hK_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\mathds{E}\Big[K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\Big]

\displaystyle\leq(C_{1}+C_{2})L_{F^{\star}}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\Big\{\frac{L_{2}C_{U}}{T}+C_{d}h\phi(h)\psi(x)\Big\},

using
(ii)
.

C.2
Proof of Proposition lemma: J1 is Op(1)

By applying Theorem 3.1 in
[
48
]
,
\Big|J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)-\mathds{E}\left[J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)\right]\Big|=\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{Th\phi(h)}}\Big)
. Additionally, using Assumption Assumption: X is lsp ,
J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)
can be decomposed as
J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)=\widetilde{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)+\bar{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)
. So,

\displaystyle\Big|J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)-\mathds{E}[J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]+\mathds{E}[J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)-\mathds{E}[J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]\Big|+\Big|\mathds{E}[J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{Th\phi(h)}}\Big)+\Big|\mathds{E}[J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{Th\phi(h)}}\Big)+\Big|\mathds{E}[\widetilde{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)+\bar{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\log T}{Th\phi(h)}}\Big)+\Big|\mathds{E}[\widetilde{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]\Big|+\Big|\mathds{E}[\bar{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]\Big|,

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big),

and

\displaystyle\bar{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\big\{K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))-K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big\}.

Now, let us first observe
\mathds{E}[\bar{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]
. Using Assumptions Assumption: X is lsp and Assumption: kernel functions together with Proposition Lemma: E of K2 .
i
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\bar{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\big[\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\big\{K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))-K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big\}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\mathds{E}\big[\big\{K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))-K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big\}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\big(\frac{L_{2}C_{U}}{Th}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}C_{U}}{Th\phi(h)}\underbrace{\frac{1}{Th}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)}_{\mathcal{O}(1)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{2}C_{U}}{Th\phi(h)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)},

which converges to zero using Assumption Assumption: bandwidth .
In the lines above,
\frac{1}{Th}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)=\mathcal{O}(1)
since, using Lemma B.2 in
[
71
]
, for
I_{h}=[C_{1}h,1-C_{1}h]
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Th}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{u\in I_{h}}\Big|\frac{1}{Th}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(u-\frac{a}{T}\big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{u\in I_{h}}\Big|\frac{1}{Th}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(u-\frac{a}{T}\big)-1\Big|+1

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{Th^{2}}\Big)+o(h)+1=\mathcal{O}(1).

(20)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\widetilde{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\big[\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\mathds{E}\big[K_{h,2}\big(\mathsf{D}\big(x,X_{a}\big(\frac{a}{T}\big)\big)\big)\big].

Using equation (4.3) in
[
41
]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}[\widetilde{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)\mathds{E}\big[\mathds{1}_{(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a}(\frac{a}{T})))\leq h}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Th\phi(h)}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)F_{t/T}(h;x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\phi(h)}\underbrace{\frac{1}{Th}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)}_{\mathcal{O}(1)}c_{d}\phi(h)\psi(x)\quad\text{(using Assumption {Assumption: small ball} )}

\displaystyle\sim\psi(x)>0,

which implies that
\mathds{E}[\widetilde{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]>0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{J_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{o_{\mathds{P}}(1)+o(1)+\mathds{E}[\widetilde{J}_{t,T}(\frac{t}{T},x)]}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{\mathds{P}}(1).

C.3
Proof of Proposition prop: control of square of sums

Let

\displaystyle Z_{t,T}(y,x):=\frac{1}{Th^{d+1}}\sum_{a=1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)Z_{a,t,T}(y,x),

(21)

where

Z_{a,t,T}(y,x)=K_{h,2}(\mathsf{D}(x,X_{a,T}))\big[\mathds{1}_{Y_{a,T}\leq y}-F^{\star}_{t}(y|x)\big].

Applying Bernstein’s big-block and small-block procedure on
Z_{t,T}(y,x)
, we partition the set
\{1,\ldots,T\}
into
2v_{T}+1
independent subsets:
v_{T}
big blocks of size
r_{T}
,
v_{T}
small blocks of size
s_{T}
, and a remainder block of size
T-v_{T}(r_{T}+s_{T})
, where
v_{T}=\lfloor\frac{T}{r_{T}+s_{T}}\rfloor
. To establish independence between the blocks, we need to place the asymptotically negligible small blocks in between two consecutive big blocks. This procedure was also used in
(
51
,
14
)
. So, we decompose
Z_{t,T}(y,x)
as

\displaystyle Z_{t,T}(y,x)

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{t,T}(y,x)+\Pi_{t,T}(y,x)+\Xi_{t,T}(y,x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{l=0}^{v_{T}-1}\Lambda_{l,t,T}(y,x)+\sum_{l=0}^{v_{T}-1}\Pi_{l,t,T}(y,x)+\Xi_{t,T}(y,x),

(22)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{l,t,T}(y,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Th^{d+1}}\sum_{a=l(r_{T}+s_{T})+1}^{l(r_{T}+s_{T})+r_{T}}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)Z_{a,t,T}(y,x),

\displaystyle\Pi_{l,t,T}(y,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Th^{d+1}}\sum_{a=l(r_{T}+s_{T})+r_{T}+1}^{(l+1)(r_{T}+s_{T})}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)Z_{a,t,T}(y,x),

and

\displaystyle\Xi_{t,T}(y,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Th^{d+1}}\sum_{a=v_{T}(r_{T}+s_{T})+1}^{T}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)Z_{a,t,T}(y,x).

Let us define the size of the big blocks as
r_{T}=\lfloor\sqrt{Th\phi(h)}/q_{T}\rfloor
, where
q_{T}
satisfies Assumption assumption: blocking , i.e.,
q_{T}=o(\sqrt{Th\phi(h)})
. This further implies that there exists a sequence of positive integers
\{q_{T}\}
,
q_{T}\rightarrow\infty
, such that
q_{T}s_{T}=o\big(\sqrt{Th\phi(h)}\big)
. Additionally, as
T\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{s_{T}}{r_{T}}\rightarrow 0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{r_{T}}{T}\rightarrow 0.

(23)

Note that defining
r_{T}=\lfloor\sqrt{Th\phi(h)}/q_{T}\rfloor
immediately implies that
r_{T}=o\big(\sqrt{Th\phi(h)}\big)
. Additionally, note that
s_{T}=o(r_{T})
and
v_{T}=o(q_{T}\sqrt{Th\phi(h)})
.
Now,

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[Z_{t,T}^{2}

\displaystyle(y,x)\big]=\mathds{E}\big[\Lambda_{t,T}^{2}(y,x)\big]+\mathds{E}\big[\Pi_{t,T}^{2}(y,x)\big]+\mathds{E}\big[\Xi_{t,T}^{2}(y,x)\big]

\displaystyle+2\Big\{\mathds{E}\big[\Lambda_{t,T}(y,x)\Pi_{t,T}(y,x)\big]+\mathds{E}\big[\Lambda_{t,T}(y,x)\Xi_{t,T}(y,x)\big]+\mathds{E}\big[\Pi_{t,T}(y,x)\Xi_{t,T}(y,x)\big]\Big\}.

However, the defined size of big blocks and the relation ( eqn: blocking asymptotics ) ensure that the blocks are asymptotically independent and the sums of small blocks and the remainder block are asymptotically negligible. Consequently, we can neglect the last terms in the previous equation. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[Z_{t,T}^{2}(y,x)\big]\approx\mathds{E}\big[\Lambda_{t,T}^{2}(y,x)\big]+\mathds{E}\big[\Pi_{t,T}^{2}(y,x)\big]+\mathds{E}\big[\Xi_{t,T}^{2}(y,x)\big].

For convenience of notation, in the succeeding steps, we let the dependency on
y
and
x
be implicit.

Step 1. Control of the big blocks.
First, let us start by dealing with
\mathds{E}\big[\Lambda_{t,T}^{2}\big]
. One has

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\Lambda_{t,T}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{v_{T}-1}\mathds{E}\big[\Lambda_{l,t,T}^{2}\big]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\
\hskip 8.19447pt\mathrlap{l\neq l^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}^{v_{T}-1}\sum_{l^{\prime}=0}^{v_{T}-1}\mathds{E}[\Lambda_{l,t,T}]\mathds{E}[\Lambda_{l^{\prime},t,T}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(Th\phi(h))^{2}}\sum_{l=0}^{v_{T}-1}\mathds{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{a=l(r_{T}+s_{T})+1}^{l(r_{T}+s_{T})+r_{T}}K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{a}{T}\big)Z_{a,t,T}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{(Th\phi(h))^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=0\\

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt\times K_{h,1}\big(\frac{t}{T}-\frac{b}{T}\big)\mathds{E}\big[Z_{a,t,T}Z_{b,t,T}\big].

Observe that
