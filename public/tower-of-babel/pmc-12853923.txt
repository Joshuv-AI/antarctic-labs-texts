Trajectories and influencing factors of social alienation in first-onset stroke patients: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yinglu Lin, Yanxin Chen, Hanghang Wu, Xinran Tian, Changxin Xu, Qi Li, Panting Lu, Huafang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke patients frequently experience social alienation due to factors such as impaired self-care, depression, fatigue, and stigma. This condition exacerbates the perceived burden of disease and hinders social integration within families and society. Multiple studies have demonstrated that the level of social alienation is influenced by physical, psychological, biological, and socioeconomic factors. However, there is limited research on the dynamics of social alienation among first-onset stroke patients. In this context, this study aims to identify the developmental trajectory of social alienation in stroke patients, laying a foundation for targeted intervention strategies to mitigate its negative effects.MethodsResearch subjects were assessed using the General Information Questionnaire, General Alienation Scale, Social Support Rating Scale, Self-Rating Depression Scale, Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale-10, and Barthel Index during follow-up surveys. Group-based trajectory modeling was utilized to identify the developmental trajectories of social alienation, while multiple logistic regression applied to analyze the influencing factors.ResultsThe scores of Social Alienation in first-onset stroke patients at four points were (37.8 ± 4.65), (39.67 ± 0.37), (37.14 ± 6.50) and (35.94 ± 6.72), respectively. Three developmental trajectories of social alienation were identified: the stable low alienation group, the high decreasing alienation group, and the stable high alienation group. Multiple logistic regression analysis indicated that BI scores (OR: 0.957), depression (OR: 1.137), psychological resilience (OR: 0.833), number of sequelae (OR: 0.246), willingness to return to work or study (OR: 2.586), and the primary caregiver after discharge (OR: 0.541) were the significant influencing factors for the different trajectories.ConclusionThe developmental trajectories of social alienation in stroke patients are heterogeneous. Medical staff should implement targeted nursing interventions tailored to the characteristics of various trajectory changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26051-3.

Introduction

Stroke is a group of acute cerebrovascular diseases triggered by the sudden rupture or obstruction of cerebral blood vessels [1]. It is a serious condition that occurs frequently, characterized by high rates of recurrence, disability, and mortality, and has emerged as the second-leading cause of death and third-leading cause of disability globally [2]. The 2019 Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (2019 GBD) reported a 70% increase in the absolute number of incident strokes worldwide since 1990, alongside an 85% rise in the number of prevalent strokes [2]. In the United States, the American Stroke Association (AHA) reported that over 600,000 residents experience a first-onset stroke annually [3]. A nationwide population-based survey conducted across 31 provinces between 2012 and 2013, which included 480,687 participants, found that the age-standardized prevalence and mortality rates of stroke in China were 1,115 per 100,000 (95% CI, 997–1,233) and 115 per 100,000 (95% CI: 96–133), respectively [4]. Notably, according to China Stroke High risk Population Screening and Intervention Program, an estimated 17.8 million adults in China had experienced a stroke in 2020, with 3.4 million experiencing their first-onset stroke [5]. It was estimated that the medical cost of hospitalization for stroke in 2020 was CNY 58.0 billion, of which the patient pays approximately CNY 19.8 billion, making the disease a significant burden to global public health and society [5].

Stroke typically occurs abruptly and without warning, often resulting in death, disability, or a diminished quality of life, which may further lead to emotional distress [6]. Moreover, patients frequently face a prolonged recovery period that hinders their effective social integration. Jaywant et al. [7] noted that only 28% of stroke patients actively engage in social activities. Despite significant advancements in the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of cardiovascular diseases, the standardized mortality rate for stroke has declined by 30% [8]. However, the disability-adjusted life years (DALY) rate for stroke is projected to continue rising between 2020 and 2030 [9]. Approximately 12.5% (95% CI 12.4–12.5%) of stroke survivors still endure disabilities caused by complications such as hemiplegia, aphasia, dysphagia, and cognitive impairment [5]. Furthermore, accumulating evidence indicates that many stroke patients experience clinically significant emotional distress, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic symptoms [12, 13]. These psychological sequelae subsequently impair social interactions among stroke patients, leading to increased social isolation, exacerbated loneliness, and even social alienation [10–13]. While these three concepts overlap to some extent, social alienation can be regarded as a derivative that integrates elements of both social isolation and loneliness [14]. In contrast, social isolation focuses on the behavioral level, referring to an objective state of insufficient social connections or infrequent social interactions [15]. Loneliness, meanwhile, centers on subjective emotional experiences, characterized by dissatisfaction or distress arising from a perceived discrepancy between one’s desired and actual social relationships [16].

The concept of social alienation was first introduced into the nursing field by Pearlin in 1962 [17]. It typically refers to the state of isolation, helplessness, and dissatisfaction experienced by individuals due to the avoidance of social interactions [14]. This impact extends beyond individual social relationships to encompass broader societal integration [2]. The prevalence of social alienation among stroke patients ranges from 20% to 33% [18–21], with contributing factors including inadequate social connections, deficient social skills, diminished self-care abilities, fatigue, stigma, and other underlying issues [22]. This condition not only prompts stroke patients to withdraw from social activities and impedes their integration into families and society, but it also exacerbates their perceived disease burden. Furthermore, it contributes to declines in both physical and cognitive functions, reduces quality of life, and serves as an independent risk factor for mortality [23]. Golaszewski et al. [19] demonstrate that patients with a high degree of social alienation face a 1.18 fold increased risk of developing cardiovascular diseases compared to those with low levels of social alienation (95% CI: 1.13–1.23). Moreover, social alienation has been shown to affect patients’ cardiovascular and immune systems, thereby increasing the risk of stroke recurrence [24]. Therefore, healthcare providers should prioritize the early identification of patients exhibiting high levels of social alienation to optimize clinical outcomes and enhance overall well-being.

However, few studies have specifically investigated social alienation in first-onset stroke patients, with current research primarily focusing on risk factors, adverse outcomes, and physiological mechanisms related to the influence of social alienation in stroke [25, 26]. Despite prior longitudinal studies exploring the overall trends [27], they lack an in-depth analysis of group heterogeneity, failing to address individual differences in longitudinal changes. In contrast, the Group-Based Trajectory Model (GBTM) is a data-driven statistical method that identifies potential subgroups exhibiting similar temporal change patterns in longitudinal data, thereby providing personalized guidance for managing heterogeneous data [28]. The aim of this study was to utilize the GBTM to identify the developmental trajectories of social alienation in first-onset stroke patients. Furthermore, this study seeks to explore the differences in demographic and clinical characteristics among these trajectories. This approach aims to formulate personalized interventions that can effectively enhance the utilization of medical resources, provide theoretical implications for identifying potential intervention populations, and assist in the development of social detachment intervention programs.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective longitudinal study was conducted from June 2024 to April 2025 among patients experiencing their first stroke at a comprehensive hospital in Yiwu, China. A combination of face-to-face interviews and telephone follow-ups was carried by qualified medical staffs. A total of 268 first-onset stroke patients were selected through convenience sampling at baseline. Of these, 39 patients were excluded due to failure to answer calls, voluntary withdrawal, or recurrence of stroke. Ultimately, a total of 229 participants were included in the study. Participants were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) stroke confirmed by clinical diagnostic criteria and CT or MRI; (3) participants who were first attacked by stroke; (4) ability to comprehend the issues and cooperate with researchers to complete the survey; (5) voluntary participation in this study with informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants who were unable to participate due to cognitive behavioral disorders (MoCA < 26 scores), auditory and visual impairments, or other reasons; (2) participants with a history of psychiatric disorders. The withdrawal criteria were established as follows: (1) voluntary withdrawal during the investigation; (2) inability to be contacted via phone or WeChat at a single time point for 3 or more attempts; (3) inability to participate in the study due to disease progression or death.

Sample size

According to the design requirements for the sample size of single-group repeated measurement data [29], with K = 4, α = 0.5, 1-β = 0.8, and effect size f = 0.14, considering a standard dropout rate of 20%, a minimum of 178 patients must be included. Additionally, based on the Bayesian criteria [30], to ensure the stability of the trajectory model, the sample size should be no less than 200. In this study, a total of 229 patients with first-onset stroke were ultimately enrolled. This study was reviewed and approved by the hospital ethics committee of The Fourth Affiliated Hospital Of Zhejiang University (K2024097), and all participants have signed informed consent forms.

Data collection and quality control

In this study, the follow-up time points were determined based on previous research [31]. Data were collected at various intervals: 1 to 7 days following the patient’s admission (T0), 1 month (T1), 3 months (T2), and 6 months post-discharge (T3). Prior to the formal study, researchers identified short versions of scales with high reliability and validity through a comprehensive literature review. Subsequently, the research team conducted a pre-test involving 20 patients, aiming to further optimize the expression of items and effectively reduce the respondents’ fatigue during answering. Relevant sociodemographic data were extracted from electronic medical records. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the assessment indices, all patients first underwent cognitive function screening using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scale administered by licensed physicians upon admission. A two-step screening strategy was adopted to verify the eligibility of enrolled subjects: first, patients with a MoCA score > 26 were initially identified by reviewing the electronic medical record system; second, experienced investigators who had received standardized training further evaluated the patients’ orientation to place, name recognition, and situational awareness through verbal inquiries. This dual-screening approach effectively guaranteed that all enrolled patients fully met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. All surveys were administered outside of patients’ treatment and nursing periods, with each questionnaire taking approximately 20 to 25 min to complete. Additionally, all follow-up surveys were conducted either via telephone or face-to-face during outpatient rechecks. All questionnaires were checked on-site, and participants were promptly reminded to complete any missing items to ensure the integrity of the data collected.

Assessment tools

General alienation scale, GAS

This scale was compiled by Jessor et al., [32] and subsequently localized and adapted by Wu et al. [33]. It consists of four dimensions encompassing 15 items. Each item evaluated using a 4 points Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Items 2, 5, and 13 are reverse scored. The total score can range from 15 to 60 points, with higher scores indicating a great degree of individual alienation.

Social support rating Scale, SSRS

The SSRS was designed and developed by Xiao et al. [34]. It comprises three dimensions, with a total of 10 items. Each item employs a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The total score can range from 12 to 66 point, with higher scores indicating a great level of social support.

10-Item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale, CD-RISC-10

The CD-RISC-10 is a simplified version of the 25-item psychological resilience scale [35]. Each item is rated on a scale from 1 (completely inconsistent) to 4 (completely consistent). The total score can range from 0 to 40 points, with higher scores indicating a greater level of psychological resilience.

Self-rating depression scale, SDS

The SDS was developed by Zung et al. [36]. The items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale. Items 2, 5, 6, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 18, and 20 are reverse-scored, while the rest are scored in the positive direction. The sum of all item scores is the raw score, and the raw score multiplied by 1.25 is the final standard score. The critical value for depression assessment is 53 points. Depression can be classified into three grades based on the scale scores: mild (53–62 scores), moderate (63–72) and severe (> 72).

Barthel Index, BI

The Barthel Index Scale, developed by Barthel et al., [37] severs as a scoring tool to evaluate patients’ abilities in activities of daily living. This questionnaire primarily assesses the self-care capabilities of research subjects across 10 aspects such as bathing, grooming, dressing, bowel and bladder control, and toilet use, with a maximum score of 100 points. Based on the scores, the index categorizes patients into three levels of impairment: mild impairment (> 60 points), moderate impairment (41–60 points), and severe impairment (< 40 points).

The Montreal cognitive assessment, MoCA

The MoCA Scale, developed by Nasreddine et al., [38] assesses the following functions: executive, visuospatial, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, recall, and orientation, with a maximum score of 30. A cut-off score of 26 is used to identify patients with possible mild cognitive impairment or early dementia. (Supplementary file 2)

Covariates

The general information questionnaire was designed by the research team to collect each participant’s characteristics (supplementary file 1), including demographic information (gender, age, education level, marital status, family residence, body mass index (BMI), pre-illness occupational status, monthly family income, medical payment coverage type, post-discharge main caregiver and the willingness to return to work/study) and diseases-related information (stroke subtype, chronic disease comorbidity status, number of sequelae). The BMI was calculated as weight (kilograms) divided by height (meters) squared and it was clarify as 3 levels (< 18.5 KG‎/m2, 18.5–24 KG‎/m2, > 24 KG‎/m2). Age was categorized as ‘‘<45 years’’, ‘’45–60 years’’ and ‘’>60 years’’. Education level was classified as ‘’primary school or below’’, ‘’middle school’’, and ‘’high school or above’’. Marital status was classified as ‘’married’’, ‘’unmarried’’ and ‘’divorced or widowed’’. The family residence was defined as ‘’rural’’ or ‘’unban’’. Pre-illness occupational status was divided into two levels (Manual laborer and White-collar worker). The monthly family income was divided into three levels (≤ 3000, 3000–5000 and ≥ 5000). Medical expenses are classified into three levels based on their payment forms: medical insurance, social security insurance, and Out-of-Pocket Payment. Post-discharge main caregivers are classified as family members or others based on the actual situation of the patients. The willingness to return to work or study was assessed by asking patients, ‘’Are you willing to return to work or study.‘’ It was categorized as Yes or No. Besides, the disease-related information was obtained by investigators through reviewing the electronic information records of patients.

Statistical analysis

In this study, GBTM was employed to identify the optimal trajectory groups for social alienation. The Traj and Trajplot packages for Stata (version 17.0) were utilized to fit between one and five groups, using the cnorm model and cubic trajectory function to yield a probability for each individual belonging to each group [28]. The fitting process progressed from higher-order single-group models to lower-order multi-group models. The optimal number of trajectory subgroups was determined based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with smaller absolute values indicating a better model fit. The classification accuracy of the model was assessed using Entropy, where Entropy values closer to 1 indicate greater accuracy. Model fitting efficiency was evaluated using the average posterior probability (Avepp) and the odds of correct classification (OCC). An Avepp greater than 0.7 and an OCC exceeding 5% signify stable model classification.

Additional statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using skewness and kurtosis [39]. Continuous variables exhibiting a normal distribution were presented as Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD), and comparisons between groups were performed using either the t-test or analysis of variance. In contrast, continuous variables with a non-normal distribution were presented as median (25th percentile, 75th percentile) [M (P25, P75)], with the Kruskal-Wallis H test employed for group comparisons. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (n) and proportions (%), with group comparisons conducted using the chi-square test and Fisher’s exact probability method. Indicators demonstrating statistical significance in the univariate analysis were included in the multiple logistic regression to identify the influencing factors of trajectories. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Estimated social alienation trajectories

The fitting process of the development trajectories of social alienation in first-onset stroke patients is presented in Table 3. When n exceeds 3, the decline rates of AIC and BIC decelerate. No new trajectory types with discriminatory value emerge when n equals 4 or 5. Considering the clinical practical significance and the principle of model simplicity, three groups were ultimately selected. After multiple fittings, the optimal polynomial was determined to be (0, 3, 3). Furthermore, the proportions of the subgroups exceeded 5%, the Entropy values were greater than 0.8, and the AvePP values were all greater than 0.7, indicating that the model fit well.

Discussion

Heterogeneity of social alienation among first-onset stroke patients

In this study, the scores of social alienation in first-onset stroke patients at four points were (37.8 ± 4.65) scores, (39.67 ± 0.37) scores, (37.14 ± 6.50) scores and (35.94 ± 6.72) scores, respectively. These scores exhibit a fluctuating trend, characterized by an initial increase followed by a gradual decrease. This pattern reflects the dynamic changes in social alienation among first-onset stroke patients at different stages, which differs from the findings of McGuire et al. [40]. This discrepancy may be due to the fact that the patients included in this research were newly diagnosed. Most of them were in the shock or denial phase of the disease trajectory and have minimal communication with others [41]. After suffering a stroke, the body’s functions are significantly impaired, ultimately hindering the individual’s capacity to cope with stressful events, which deteriorates social participation [42]. They may lose confidence in their future rehabilitation when the treatment effects during hospitalization do not meet their expectations, which increases their sense of social alienation [43]. However, with continuous rehabilitation therapy, patients frequently achieve significant recovery in limb function, accompanied by improved self-management capabilities [44], which in turn leads to a reduction in social alienation.

Notably, our study revealed three distinct trajectories of social alienation: stable low alienation group, high decreasing alienation group, and stable high alienation group. Specifically, 18.7% of patients exhibited a persistent low sense of social alienation. Patients in this subgroup typically possess a higher educational background and demonstrate a willingness to return to work or study. Individuals with elevated educational levels proactively utilize resources such as the Internet to obtain prognostic information related to their condition [45]. This proactive behavior motivates them to seek social support actively, resulting in a lower level of social alienation. Consequently, they maintain a high level of social adaptation throughout the follow-up period. For these patients, medical staff should enhance rehabilitation guidance and health education, while also addressing their needs during the rehabilitation process to sustain a low and stable level of social alienation.

46.3% of patients were categorized into the “high decreasing alienation” group. With early rehabilitation intervention, most patients gradually recover physical function, their conditions improve, social participation rates rebound, and their sense of social alienation diminishes [46]. This indirectly indicates that the initial level of social alienation in first-onset stroke patients does not accurately reflect their long-term trajectory. Given the dynamic nature of social alienation, medical providers should prioritize screening for those with high social alienation during the acute phase. Timely and appropriate psychological interventions should be implemented to alleviate their inner uncertainty and facilitate social participation. In contrast, 35% of patients demonstrated a consistently high level of social alienation throughout the follow-up period. This may be attributed to the more serious conditions experienced by this group, characterized by significant neurological impairment, which hampers their ability to self-regulate emotions, thereby solidifying their sense of social alienation [47]. Post-stroke social integration has been shown to be associated with physical impairments and emotional disorders [48]. However, a previous meta-analysis revealed that exercise-based interventions may be effective in improving social participation after stroke, further relieving their negative emotions [49]. Healthcare providers are advised to conduct further in-depth interviews to explore patients’ subjective experiences and the underlying needs contributing to the development of social alienation. This will facilitate the formulation of personalized interventions such as group psychotherapy and community integration programs, thereby breaking the vicious cycle of social alienation, alleviating negative emotions, and improving their sense of social belonging.

Predictive factors for trajectories of social alienation in first-onset stroke patients

Patients with low BI scores and sequelae are more likely to enter C3

The results of this study indicate that patients with low BI scores (OR: 0.957, 95% CI: 0.925–0.99) and those with sequelae (OR: 0.246, 95% CI: 0.064–0.944) are more likely to fall into C3. Stroke is the most common cause of disabilities, including dyskinesia, dysphagia, and cognitive decline [50]. Patients with residual sequelae typically present with low BI scores and significant motor dysfunction, severely impairing their ability to perform activities of daily living and engage in social interactions [51, 52]. Due to reduced motor function and fear of falling, these patients tend to limit their opportunities for communication with the outside world [53], further exacerbating their sense of social alienation. Patients with low BI scores and sequelae require more time and incur higher medical expenses for treatment. They often exhibit reluctance to socialize. Furthermore, an increase in the number of sequelae correlates with a heightened likelihood of experiencing stigma and social alienation, primarily due to alterations in body image, which can lead to further withdrawal from social interactions. Therefore, early rehabilitation is crucial for individuals with low BI scores and sequelae. A meta-analysis [54] demonstrated that VR-based intervention had a statistically large effect size on limb function (SMD: 4.606, 95% CI: 2.733–6.479) and balance (SMD: 2.101, 95% CI: 0.202–4.000.202.000) after stroke. VR training not only helps users focus their attention on the task but also enhances motivation and treatment compliance, which can significantly promote social integration.

Patients with high CD-RICS-10 scores and whose primary caregivers are family members are more likely to enter C1

Patients with higher levels of psychological resilience tend to respond to negative events with a more positive attitude [55]. They not only effectively buffer the impact of negative emotions but also exhibit stronger rehabilitation compliance, facilitating their recovery from adversity and promoting personal growth [56]. This phenomenon can be attributed to patients with high psychological resilience scores, who are better equipped to adapt in the face of adversity and to find hope in challenging situations. Buffering theory posits that psychological resilience acts as a defensive barrier against the negative effects of challenges experienced under conditions of high distress [57]. Robert et al. [58] also demonstrated that patients with higher psychological resilience are more likely to engage in the social activities, consistent with our findings. Therefore, healthcare professionals should conduct a comprehensive assessment of patients’ psychological resilience levels and engage in in-depth communication to understand their inner feelings, thereby reducing their sense of social alienation.

Furthermore, among patients whose post-discharge main caregiver were family members, these families typically exhibit higher levels of social support. Evidence suggests that emotional support from family members can prevent the development of negative emotions in patients, mitigate the internal emotional harm caused by illness, and facilitate social integration [59]. Such a family environment fosters timely communication between patients and caregivers regarding complex disease conditions and rehabilitation progress, thereby enhancing patients’ capabilities [60]. This underscores the necessity for family members to closely monitor the patient’s psychological state, provide daily living and emotional support, and enhance their confidence in disease management.

Patients with high level of depression are more likely to entry C3 and C2

The results of this study indicate that patients with high levels of depression are more likely to fall into C2 (OR: 1.137, 95% CI: 1.051–1.231) and C3 (OR: 1.182, 95% CI: 1.077–1.297). As one of the common symptom clusters following stroke, depression is often associated with cognitive impairment and poor prognosis [61]. Stroke patients, who experience an acute and unexpected event for the first time, often struggle to adapt promptly to the transformation of their identity and social roles, potentially leading to a significant psychological gap [62]. Furthermore, after discharge, these patients remain in a prolonged state of stress, which may result in elevated levels of depression. The fear of disease progression exacerbates their negative perceptions of the illness, diminishing their willingness to engage with their surrounding environment and social interactions [63]. As time progresses, if post-discharge rehabilitation outcomes fail to meet their inner expectations, their negative emotions like depression may be further intensified. Therefore, it is essential to consider the multidimensional and dynamic interactions between social alienation and depression in patients with first-onset strokes. Personalized intervention measures can be formulated for specific subgroups to optimize the effectiveness of these interventions and improve health management outcomes.

Patients without the willingness to return to work or study are more likely to entry C3.

Employment serves as a crucial pathway for individuals to realize their self-worth and meet economic needs [64]. It can facilitates social participation, positively influencing both physical and mental health. Patients who fail to return to work (RTW) may suffer from social alienation, characterized by diminished social identity, lowered self-esteem, and reduced quality of life [43]. Lin et al. [18] indicated that complications in stroke patients compromise their ability to fulfill original family and social roles, weaken their willingness to return to work or study, and exacerbate social withdrawal behaviors (β=−4.748, P < 0.001). A study demonstrated that stroke patients who initiate rehabilitation within 7 days of onset exhibit a higher RTW rate, which may be attributed to the critical role of early rehabilitation in promoting neural functional plasticity [65]. In this study, the majority (55.9%) of participants reported no intention of RTW, potentially due to insufficient medical assistance, disease severity, changes in appearance, and unmet therapeutic expectations [66]. Healthcare providers should implement early vocational rehabilitation programs for patients experiencing their first stroke to facilitate their reintegration into regular social environments. Furthermore, policymakers should establish a comprehensive medical insurance system to alleviate the economic burden on patients, thereby reducing their sense of social alienation.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, this study is a single-center investigation focused on patients with first-onset stroke, which may introduce selection bias. Secondly, the sample size is relatively small, potentially limiting the robustness of the findings. So, it is essential to expand the sample size and conduct multi-center studies to enhance the reliability and generalizability of the results. Thirdly, the GAS scale lacks specificity in strokes and may underestimate the level of social alienation among these individuals. Additionally, this study specifically focused on first-onset stroke patients to control for confounding factors associated with multiple stroke episodes, thereby excluding recurrent stroke patients from the analysis. Future studies should include both subgroups to explore potential heterogeneity in the associations of interest.

Conclusion

This study conducted a six-month follow-up of patients with first-onset stroke, revealing that the social alienation of these patients generally exhibits dynamic changes and population heterogeneity. These findings can be categorized into three subgroups: the ‘’stable low alienation’’ group, the ‘’stable high alienation’’ group, and the ‘’high decreasing alienation’’ group. For patients with sequelae, low Barthel Index (BI) scores, and a willingness to return to work or study, it is crucial to initiate rehabilitation interventions as early as possible. This approach aims to improve limb motor function and daily living skills, thereby facilitating social integration. For patients exhibiting high levels of depression and low psychological resilience, medical staff should implement early screening for social alienation to promptly identify individuals at risk. Subsequently, individualized intervention strategies, such as psychological counseling and social support navigation, should be developed to effectively enhance patients’ mental health and quality of life, thereby supporting their successful reintegration into society.
