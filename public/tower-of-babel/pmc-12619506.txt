Optimizing biosynthesis: the role of LED light spectra in regulating phenolic and flavonoid accumulation in Matricaria chamomilla L. Root cultures
Authors: Nastaran Maham, Esmaeil Chamani, Mehdi Mohebodini, Neda Tariverdizadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Matricaria chamomilla L. is a medicinal plant valued for its high content of phenolic and flavonoid compounds. In vitro root cultures represent a promising alternative to field cultivation for controlled and sustainable metabolite production. This study investigated the effects of different LED light spectra (blue, red, white, and darkness) on biomass growth and secondary metabolite accumulation. Blue LED significantly enhanced fresh root biomass (48.7%) and total phenolic content (56.6%), accompanied by the highest antioxidant activity (DPPH assay, p ≤ 0.01). Red light markedly increased total flavonoid content (p ≤ 0.05), whereas darkness promoted the accumulation of gallic acid. LED light spectra act as effective biochemical elicitors for optimizing metabolite production in M. chamomilla root cultures. Blue light is most effective for stimulating phenolic biosynthesis and biomass growth, while red light and darkness favor flavonoid and compound-specific responses. Importantly, LED-driven in vitro systems offer a scalable and environmentally sustainable platform for producing pharmaceutically relevant metabolites.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07609-0.

Introduction

German chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla L.), a prominent medicinal plant of the Asteraceae family, is widely valued for its diverse bioactive compounds, including flavonoids such as apigenin and quercetin, and essential oils such as chamazulene [1, 2]. These metabolites are applied extensively in the pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and food industries. However, conventional field cultivation is often limited by environmental variability and low yield, restricting the consistent supply of these valuable compounds [2, 25, 26].

As a sustainable alternative, in vitro root cultures have emerged as an efficient platform for controlled secondary metabolite biosynthesis [3, 22]. Induction with Rhizobium rhizogenes generates fast-growing, highly branched roots capable of producing high levels of target metabolites under stable and sterile conditions [4].

Light is a critical regulatory factor in such culture systems. In particular, the spectral quality of Light-Emitting Diodes (LEDs) serves as a strong signal modulating gene expression and metabolic pathways [5, 6]. LEDs offer precise wavelength control, energy efficiency, and minimal heat generation, making them an effective tool for optimizing in vitro culture systems [23, 7]. Previous studies have shown that spectral variations differentially affect metabolite accumulation across plant species; for example, blue light often enhances phenolic biosynthesis, whereas red light tends to promote flavonoid accumulation [8, 24, 27]. Recent elicitation studies using radiation stressors, such as UV-B and gamma rays, further demonstrate the potential of controlled environments to boost metabolite production [9].

Despite these advances, no comprehensive study has systematically evaluated the differential effects of multiple monochromatic LED spectra on phenolic and flavonoid biosynthesis in chamomile root cultures. The fine-tuning of LED spectra is increasingly recognized as a sophisticated tool for metabolic engineering in in vitro systems, moving beyond simple biomass promotion to targeted compound induction. Specifically, understanding the differential photoreceptor signaling (e.g., phytochromes vs. cryptochromes) under monochromatic light remains crucial for optimizing the biosynthesis of distinct phenylpropanoid branches.

Thus, this study aims to investigate the influence of blue, red, white, and darkness treatments on biomass growth and the accumulation of key bioactive metabolites in M. chamomilla root cultures, thereby addressing this critical knowledge gap.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and replicates

The overall experimental workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1. The study was conducted in a completely randomized design (CRD) with four light treatments (blue, red, white, and darkness) and three independent biological replicates. For each treatment, data for fresh weight, total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), and antioxidant activity (DPPH) were obtained from six samples (n = 6).

Seed sterilization and seedling culture

Matricaria chamomilla L. seeds were obtained from Pakan Bazr Isfahan, Iran. Seeds were first soaked in distilled water, then immersed in 70% ethanol for 40 s, followed by disinfection in 5% sodium hypochlorite for 10 min on a shaker. Seeds were rinsed three times with sterile distilled water (once for 7, 5, and 3 min respectively) and cultured in jars containing 30 mL of solid MS medium (pH 5.8) supplemented with 30 g/L sucrose and 7 g/L plant agar [10]. Cultures were maintained in a growth chamber at 25 ± 2 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod. Seedlings that germinated after 8 weeks were used for bacterial-induced root culture.

Root culture induction with Rhizobium rhizogenes

Two R. rhizogenes strains[28] (A4 and ATCC15834) were initially screened for transformation efficiency. Strain A4 was selected due to higher root induction efficiency (75% ± 5%) compared to ATCC15834 (40% ± 4%). Both strains were cultured in liquid LB medium supplemented with rifampicin (50 mg/L) at 28 °C and 180 rpm until OD₆₀₀ reached 0.6–0.8. Sterilized seedlings were wounded at the hypocotyl region and inoculated with 20 µL of bacterial suspension. Inoculated plantlets were co-cultivated in darkness on hormone-free half-strength MS medium for 48 h. They were then transferred to fresh MS medium containing Cefotaxime (500 mg/L) to eliminate bacteria [11, 12].

After 6 weeks, successfully rooted plantlets were excised and subcultured on solid Gamborg’s B5 medium with 3% (w/v) sucrose and 0.8% (w/v) agar (pH 5.7) [13]. Actively growing roots were then transferred to liquid half-strength B5 medium supplemented with Cefotaxime (250 mg/L) and maintained in darkness on a shaker at 100 rpm.

LED light treatments

Blue, red, white, and darkness treatments were applied for 21 days, with one medium change and reduction of Cefotaxime from 250 to 150 mg/L. Light intensities (PPFD) were approximately 60 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ for blue and white, and 40 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ for red light..White light served as a full-spectrum reference but was excluded from the main statistical analyses due to poor growth and insufficient biomass. However, HPLC data are presented for completeness.

Spectral validation of the LEDs was performed using a fiber optic spectrophotometer (Aurora 4000) under two intensity ratios (80% red/20% blue and 40% red/60% blue), confirming distinct spectral peaks without overlap (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Measurement of phytochemical compounds

Total phenolic content (TPC)

Determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method. Briefly, 0.5 mL of plant extract was mixed with 2.5 mL of 10% (v/v) Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, followed by 2 mL of 7.5% (w/v) sodium carbonate. Samples were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 765 nm (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan), and TPC was expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per g fresh weight (mg GAE g⁻¹ FW).

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

Measured using the aluminum chloride method. In brief, 0.5 mL of plant extract was mixed with 1.5 mL of 95% ethanol, 0.1 mL of 10% (w/v) aluminum chloride, 0.1 mL of 1 M potassium acetate, and 2.8 mL sterile distilled water. Mixtures were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 415 nm, and TFC was expressed as mg quercetin equivalents per g fresh weight (mg QE g⁻¹ FW) [14].

Antioxidant capacity (DPPH)

The DPPH radical scavenging activity was assessed according to Brand-Williams et al. [15]. A 0.1 mM DPPH solution in methanol was mixed with 1 mL of plant extract (3 mL DPPH solution) and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The percentage of scavenging activity was calculated as: DPPH% inhibition: (Acontrol – Asample/Acontrol) × 100.

Where Acontrol is the absorbance of the blank and Asample is the absorbance of the test sample.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

Extraction was performed with 80% methanol (100 mg of powdered sample in 10 mL) followed by sonication (30 s vortex, 25 min at 20–25 °C) [16]. Chromatographic separation was conducted using a Waters Alliance 2695 system equipped with a PDA detector. The column was a C18 (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm), and the mobile phases consisted of A (water + 0.1% formic acid) and B (acetonitrile). The gradient program was as follows: 0–5 min: 35% B; 5–10 min: 50% B; 10–15 min: 75% B; 15–19 min: 100% B. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, and the injection volume was 10 µL. Retention times were approximately 8.2 min for apigenin, 12.5 min for quercetin, and 3.4 min for gallic acid. Linearity was observed over the range of 5–100 µg/mL (r² >0.995), with the limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) determined to be 0.5 µg/mL and 1.5 µg/mL, respectively. For comparative purposes, two control groups were included: control seedlings (CP) and control roots (CR), which were used to benchmark the R. rhizogenes-induced root cultures under different light treatments.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS v.20 software. The normality of the distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test (p < 0.05). Upon confirmation of these assumptions, one-way ANOVA was performed, and mean comparisons were carried out using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (Duncan’s MRT) at a significance level of 5%. The standard error of the mean (SEM) was reported in the tables. An ANOVA table, including degrees of freedom (df), F-values, and coefficient of variation (CV%) for each trait, was provided.

Results

The present study demonstrated that light quality had a significant impact on biomass production and secondary metabolite accumulation in Matricaria chamomilla root cultures. ANOVA results revealed significant differences among the light treatments for root fresh weight, total phenolic content (TPC), and antioxidant activity (DPPH assay) (p ≤ 0.01). Total flavonoid content (TFC) was highest under red light and darkness, with values of 1.161 and 1.09 mg QE/g FW, respectively, while blue light showed the lowest TFC. These findings indicate that both red light and darkness can act as effective elicitors for flavonoid accumulation. HPLC analysis further revealed significant variation (p ≤ 0.01) in the content of apigenin, quercetin, and gallic acid across root cultures and seedling samples.

Root biomass was notably enhanced under blue light, accompanied by visible changes in root morphology.

The R. rhizogenes-induced roots exhibited a characteristic phenotype of highly branched, plagiotropic (growing away from gravity) roots with rapid growth rates, which is characteristic of the successful R. rhizogenes induction process. Furthermore, blue light treatment resulted in thicker, less branched, and often paler yellow roots, whereas roots under red light and darkness were thinner and more extensively branched, suggesting a light-dependent morphological regulation (Fig. 2). In contrast, white light treatment resulted in poor growth and, in some cases, root death. Growth under red light and darkness was comparatively similar. Gallic acid concentration was highest in the control seedlings (CP) at 26.22 mg/g FW, followed by darkness-treated roots (22.74 mg/g FW), while the lowest concentration was observed in non-induced roots (CR, 3.89 mg/g FW). Apigenin levels remained largely stable across treatments, with the highest observed under blue light and the lowest in non-induced roots.

Quercetin content was significantly lower in seedlings compared to root cultures, with the highest values recorded in non-induced roots (10.29 mg/g FW) and under white light (9.79 mg/g FW). Although white light was not included in the main statistical analyses due to poor growth, its HPLC data are presented for completeness. Biomass production

Analysis confirmed that light quality significantly affected root fresh weight (p ≤ 0.01). White light-treated roots were excluded from statistical analyses due to extreme degradation. Blue light resulted in the highest fresh root weight (1521.8 mg), representing a 48.7% increase compared to the control (Fig. 2).

Total phenol content (TPC)

TPC was significantly influenced by light treatments (p ≤ 0.01). The highest TPC (23.88 mg GAE/g FW) was observed under blue light, followed by red light (15.26 mg GAE/g FW), with the lowest values in darkness (Table 2).

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

Light treatments also significantly affected TFC (p ≤ 0.05). Red light resulted in the highest TFC (1.161 mg QE/g FW), representing a 26.1% increase compared to the Blue light treatment (0.9208 mg QE/g FW). Darkness also yielded a high value (1.09 mg QE/g FW), whereas Blue light showed the lowest value. These results highlight the elicitor effect of Red light and darkness on flavonoid accumulation.

Antioxidant capacity (DPPH)

Antioxidant activity varied significantly under different light treatments (p ≤ 0.01). Blue light achieved the highest DPPH radical scavenging activity (29.21%), while darkness had the lowest (Table 2), confirming the stimulatory effect of blue light on antioxidant potential.

HPLC analysis of specific compounds

HPLC measurements indicated variable content of apigenin, quercetin, and gallic acid among treatments, non-induced roots, and seedlings. Quercetin content in the root culture was significantly higher than in seedlings (p ≤ 0.01). The highest values were recorded in non-induced roots (10.29 mg/g FW) and in the white light treatment (9.79 mg/g FW). The highest concentration of gallic acid was observed in seedlings under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod (26.22 mg/g FW). Among the root cultures, the highest value was recorded in the darkness treatment (22.74 mg/g FW). The lowest values were recorded in non-induced roots (CR). Apigenin changes between the red light and darkness treatments were not significant (p > 0.05). The lowest value was reported in non-induced roots (CR) (Fig. 3).

Correlation analysis of biological variables under different light conditions

To investigate the relationships between light conditions and the plant’s biological responses, Pearson correlation analysis was used, and the results were visually presented as a heatmap. This matrix graph (4 × 3) included the light conditions (B100, R100, Darkness) on the horizontal axis and the biological variables (FW, TPC, TFC, DPPH) on the vertical axis, with the correlation range covering from − 0.5 (blue color) to + 1 (red color) (Fig. 4).

Based on the findings: Blue light (B100) had a significant positive correlation with antioxidant capacity (DPPH) (r = 0.45, p ≤ 0.01), indicating the increasing effect of blue light on free radical scavenging. Fresh root weight (FW) also showed a stronger positive correlation with blue light (r = 0.60, p ≤ 0.01), which effectively justifies the stimulated growth under this treatment. Total phenol content (TPC) had a weak correlation with blue light (r = 0.25, p ≤ 0.05), but showed a stronger positive correlation with antioxidant capacity (r = 0.70, p ≤ 0.01), which aligns with the role of phenolic compounds in antioxidant activity. Total flavonoid content (TFC) had a significant positive correlation with red light (R100) and darkness (r ≈ 0.55, p ≤ 0.05), but a moderate negative correlation with blue light (r = − 0.45, p ≤ 0.05), suggesting a suppressive effect of blue light on flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. The relationship between TPC and TFC was also positive and significant (r = 0.50, p ≤ 0.01), which could indicate the relative contribution of both metabolite groups to defensive responses under light. These correlation analyses not only reinforce the trends observed in the experimental data but also highlight the potential relationships between light regulation and metabolic pathways.

Discussion

This study investigated how specific LED light qualities affect growth and secondary metabolite production in Matricaria chamomilla R. rhizogenes-induced root cultures. Our findings confirm that precise light tuning can selectively enhance the biosynthesis of valuable compounds. This aligns with the understanding that light quality acts as a powerful environmental signal, regulating metabolic pathways and defense responses in plants [17].

Biomass and phenolic production

Blue light significantly boosted both fresh root biomass (48.7% increase) and total phenolic content (56.6% increase). It also maximized antioxidant capacity, which aligns well with the strong positive correlation observed between total phenolic content and DPPH activity (r = 0.70), confirming that blue light acts as an effective elicitor for the entire phenolic-antioxidant defense system.

This dual effect suggests that blue light effectively stimulates both growth and the production of defense-related compounds. This outcome is consistent with prior research showing that blue light activates photoreceptors, which in turn upregulate key genes in the phenylpropanoid pathway (PAL, CHS, and CHI) [18, 19]. The strong correlation between phenolic content and fresh root weight confirms that blue light directs metabolic resources towards both robust growth and the production of phenolic compounds.

Flavonoid and gallic acid responses

In contrast to blue light, red light promoted the highest total flavonoid content. This highlights the distinct roles of different light spectra in regulating biosynthetic pathways. Red light, perceived by phytochromes, is known to influence flavonoid synthesis by modulating genes like DFR and F3H [20]. This selective mechanism demonstrates the potential of tailored light recipes for producing specific target compounds. Furthermore, the highest accumulation of gallic acid occurred in darkness, suggesting that the absence of light can also trigger a metabolic response, possibly as an adaptive response or defense mechanism [21]. While Red light strongly promoted Total Flavonoid Content (TFC), the HPLC results show a complex pattern for individual flavonoids. Specifically, the content of Quercetin (a key flavonoid) was relatively lower under Red light compared to the non-induced roots (CR) and White light treatment. This apparent contradiction between high TFC and lower Quercetin under Red light suggests a light-mediated shift in the metabolic flux of the flavonoid pathway. Red light, perceived by phytochromes, may not primarily favor the final synthesis and accumulation of Quercetin but rather enhance the production of other major flavonoid components (e.g., Apigenin glycosides or other related compounds that contribute significantly to the TFC value). This observation underscores the need to differentiate between the effects of light on general metabolite classes (TPC/TFC) and their influence on specific compound profiles, indicating a highly regulated, wavelength-specific partitioning of intermediates in M. chamomilla root cultures.

The findings of this study provide a robust foundation for light-mediated optimization in M. chamomilla root cultures. However, the exact molecular mechanism underlying the differential responses, particularly the shift in metabolic flux between TFC and specific flavonoids like Quercetin under red light, still warrants further investigation. Future research should integrate transcriptomic analysis to monitor the expression of key genes (e.g., CHS, FLS) and conduct temporal analysis to precisely determine the optimal harvest window and the long-term sustainability of the induced metabolite production. Furthermore, exploring combined light spectra, such as red/blue ratios, may unlock synergistic effects for higher production efficiency. The interplay between the Rhizobium rhizogenes elicitation and the light stimulus also requires further investigation to fully distinguish their individual and combined effects.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this research provides a practical framework for designing optimal lighting systems for M. chamomilla root culture. This method offers a promising strategy for enhancing the production of valuable bioactive compounds for industrial and pharmaceutical use. Using blue light for phenolics and red light for flavonoids can significantly increase production efficiency. The controlled nature of this in vitro system presents a sustainable alternative to conventional cultivation, reducing environmental variability and ensuring consistent, high-quality, year-round production. Our findings pave the way for more efficient and reliable industrial production, which can help meet the growing global demand for natural medicines and functional foods.
