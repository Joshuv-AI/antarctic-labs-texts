Intestinal oxidative injury, autophagy-related alterations, and apoptosis during early cage transfer in laying ducks
Authors: Tianhao Zhao, Siyu Feng, Hanxue Sun, Tao Zeng, Li Chen, Wenwu Xu, Tiantian Gu, Jindong Ren, Jiayi Su, Rongyang Li, LiZhi Lu, Yong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Cage rearing damages duck intestinal structure by day 5.•It induces systemic oxidative stress in laying ducks.•Oxidative stress activates autophagy in jejunal cells.•It also triggers apoptosis via Caspase-3 and PARP-1.•H2O2 alone confirms oxidative stress drives both.

Implications

During the early adaptation period after transfer to cages, laying ducks showed time-dependent intestinal oxidative changes, with several biochemical alterations evident by days 3–5 and clear morphological injury on day 5. Autophagy-related features and apoptosis-associated changes occurred concurrently with oxidative injury. These findings identify the early cage-transfer period as an important window for intestinal health management and provide a biological basis for strategies aimed at mitigating cage-transition stress.

Introduction

Traditional methods of raising laying ducks, including aquatic or near-water farming, face challenges such as environmental pollution, biosecurity management, and limited scalability. Cage-rearing (CR) has therefore been introduced to improve land use, facilitate standardized management, and increase production efficiency in intensive duck production (Zhang et al., 2019; Bai et al., 2022). Available duck studies indicate that housing systems can influence production-related traits, although the direction and magnitude of these effects depend on breed, age, and management: cage housing improved growth performance in small-sized meat ducks compared with floor housing (Bai et al., 2022), whereas cage, floor, and pond systems differed in several egg-quality traits in laying ducks (Shi et al., 2022). Thus, a uniform laying-performance advantage of cage housing over floor housing should not be assumed. These practical considerations should be balanced against the stress associated with rapid changes in housing conditions. In laying ducks, the early period after transfer to cages is accompanied by dynamic changes in antioxidant defense and inflammatory injury (Zhang et al., 2019), and recent studies have further documented intestinal mucosal injury, redox disturbance, and apoptosis-related responses during early cage adaptation (He et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2025). Cage-transition stress has also been linked to oxidative and autophagy-related responses in laying ducks (Zong et al., 2026). Accordingly, the present study focuses on intestinal responses during the early 10-day adaptation period rather than assuming that these responses represent the effects of long-term cage housing. Additional studies in ducks support this temporal interpretation: caging stress alters performance, oxidative indices, and plasma metabolite profiles across the first 5–15 days after transfer, with the earliest days representing a sensitive phase, while individual behavioral responses to caged stress are associated with differences in antioxidant status and production traits (Zheng et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2024).

The intestine, being the organ with the largest surface area exposed to the external environment, plays a pivotal role in digestion, absorption, and metabolism (Vaccaro et al., 2020), making it highly susceptible to oxidative damage caused by oxygen-free radicals (Bhattacharyya et al., 2014). When the intestinal structure is compromised, harmful substances such as bacteria and endotoxins can breach the mucosal barrier and enter the bloodstream, triggering systemic inflammation and potentially leading to multiple organ failure (Armacki et al., 2018). Extensive studies using in vivo and in vitro models have confirmed that oxidative stress injury is closely linked to autophagy and apoptosis (Ornatowski et al., 2020). Consistent with this, avian intestinal studies show that oxidative stress can coincide with disruption of tight-junction/barrier indices and inflammatory injury in the jejunum (He et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024).

Autophagy is a cellular biological process that involves the recycling of materials within eukaryotic cells (Levine and Kroemer, 2019) and plays a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis (Ballabio and Bonifacino, 2020; Noguchi et al., 2020). Apoptosis, in contrast, is triggered under both physiological and pathological conditions by various internal and external stimuli (Gümüşlü et al., 2002; Tang et al., 2019). Reactive oxygen species (ROS), which include highly reactive oxygen-containing species such as oxygen anions, free radicals, and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), are small, short-lived, and highly reactive molecules (Rahal et al., 2014). ROS are generated through controlled physiological processes, but excessive production can lead to oxidative stress (Juan et al., 2021). Both ROS and antioxidant components are essential signaling molecules in the oxidative stress response (Scherz-Shouval and Elazar, 2007; Li et al., 2015). ROS can initiate autophagosome formation and autophagic degradation by acting as cellular signaling molecules (Chen et al., 2009; Cao et al., 2021). Additionally, autophagy has been shown to reduce ROS levels by removing damaged organelles such as mitochondria (Ureshino et al., 2014). ROS activate multiple cellular pathways and transcription factors, including mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK), nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), and the tumor suppressor p53, which in turn trigger cellular autophagy and apoptosis (Covarrubias et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2016). Autophagy and apoptosis do not function in isolation (Wang, 2015); rather, their signaling pathways engage in extensive crosstalk (Yeganeh et al., 2018), interacting through complex and overlapping networks of genes and proteins, jointly contributing to the onset and progression of various diseases (Dong et al., 2019; Chu et al., 2023). Therefore, autophagy and apoptosis exhibit mutual regulation and play active roles in the body’s response to oxidative stress, but the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this interaction are not yet fully understood. Poultry intestinal models further show that oxidative challenge can be accompanied by DNA damage, apoptosis, and autophagy-related alterations, supporting consideration of these processes as interconnected but not necessarily sequential responses (Zhong et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2024).

To date, few studies have examined intestinal oxidative injury together with autophagy-related and apoptotic responses during the early adaptation of laying ducks to cage housing. Recent work using primary duck intestinal epithelial cells has shown that H₂O₂-induced oxidative challenge increases ROS and apoptosis while impairing antioxidant activity and intestinal-barrier-related gene expression (He et al., 2025). The present study therefore evaluated these responses in vivo and used an H₂O₂-induced cellular oxidative-stress model as a reductionist approach to determine whether selected cellular features observed in vivo could be reproduced under direct oxidative challenge. More specifically, recent work in ducks has demonstrated jejunal oxidative injury in vivo and H₂O₂-responsive changes in immortalized duck intestinal epithelial cells, supporting the use of this model for focused redox studies (Zhou et al., 2024).

Materials and methods

Experimental design

A total of 120 healthy, 100-day-old female Shaoxing ducks with similar body weights were selected for this experiment and assigned to floor-rearing (FR) or cage-rearing (CR) treatments (60 ducks per treatment). Each treatment comprised eight independent housing replicates, with 7–8 ducks per replicate. One hundred-day-old ducks were selected because this age represents the late-rearing/pre-laying stage in Shaoxing ducks; age at first egg has been reported at approximately 128 days or later in this breed (Feng et al., 2018). After a 7-day adaptation period, FR ducks remained in floor housing, whereas CR ducks were transferred to cage housing. All animals were maintained under controlled environmental conditions: temperature of 23 ± 2°C, relative humidity of 55–65%, light intensity of 10–20 lx, and a photoperiod of 10–15 hours. Clinical condition was monitored daily. A basal diet (Table 1), formulated according to NRC (1994) and Chinese standard NY/T 33-2004, was provided. Sample collection and processing

On days 1, 2, and 10 of the trial, six ducks per treatment were sampled, with each duck selected from a different independent housing replicate. Days 3 and 5 were key observation time points, and eight ducks per treatment were sampled, one from each of the eight independent housing replicates. Assay-specific subsets of these independent biological samples were used according to sample availability and experimental requirements: antioxidant measurements used n = 4 per treatment per time point, serum 8-OHdG measurements used n = 5 per treatment per time point, and the immunofluorescence, transmission electron microscopy, and Western blot analyses shown in Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6 used n = 3 per treatment. Within each assay, samples were derived from different independent housing replicates. Blood samples were collected from the wing vein, centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 minutes, and stored at –80°C. Segments of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were immediately flushed with physiological saline to clear luminal contents and fixed in a solution containing 4% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde. The remaining small intestinal tissues were rapidly dissected and frozen at –80°C for subsequent analysis.

Cell culture and handling

In accordance with our laboratory’s previous research (Zhou et al., 2022), an H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress cell model was established. Primary intestinal epithelial cells isolated from Shaoxing duck embryos were treated with 400 μM H₂O₂ for 6 hours. Cells were cultured in a humidified CO₂ incubator at 37°C with 5% CO₂ and 95% humidity, with medium changes every 48 hours. When cell confluence reached 80–90%, cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin (without EDTA), centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 1 minute, and the resulting pellet was resuspended to a final density of 3 × 10⁵ cells/mL for passaging. Cell morphology was monitored using an optical microscope. This complementary in vitro model was used to examine responses to direct oxidative challenge.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Levels of oxidative stress markers, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), malondialdehyde (MDA), and 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), were measured in serum, small intestinal mucosal tissue, and duck embryonic intestinal epithelial cells. The analyses were performed using duck-specific ELISA kits (Beijing Huaying Biotechnology Research Institute, Beijing, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

Histological morphology analysis

For histopathological examination, small intestinal tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Histopathological changes were assessed, and villus height, crypt depth, and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (VCR) were measured under an optical microscope. Images were captured using a Nikon 90i Eclipse upright microscope (Nikon, Japan).

For ultrastructural analysis, intestinal samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO₄), and processed through dehydration, embedding, and ultrathin sectioning. After staining, autophagosome-like structures were observed under a Hitachi H-7650 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

Western blot assay

Mucosal tissues were rinsed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and homogenized in ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with 1 mmol/L phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 0.5 mmol/L protease inhibitor, and 0.5 mmol/L phosphatase inhibitor (Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The homogenate was incubated on ice for 20 minutes and then centrifuged to collect the total tissue lysate. Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Equal amounts of protein were resolved on 4%–20% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels (FuturePAGE™, Boyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore Corporation, USA) using a semi-dry transfer system. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 hours at room temperature, followed by an overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C. After washing, the membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 hour. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Millipore Corporation). The primary antibodies used in this study, obtained from Hangzhou Hua'an Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (China), included Beclin-1, LC3B, Caspase-3, and PARP-1.

Detection of apoptosis by TUNEL assay

Apoptotic cells were detected on 4% paraformaldehyde-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections using a TUNEL fluorescence kit. After deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, sections were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37°C for 1 hour, counterstained with DAPI, mounted with anti-fade medium, and coverslipped. Whole-slide imaging was performed on a Pannoramic SCAN digital scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). TUNEL-positive and DAPI-stained nuclei were quantified using the Visiopharm Integrator System v2021.09 (Visiopharm, Hørsholm, Denmark) under a standardized AI-based workflow: (i) automatic tissue outline, (ii) color calibration for capturing green TUNEL fluorescence, (iii) nuclear segmentation on the DAPI channel, and (iv) single-cell classification. The apoptotic index was expressed as the percentage of TUNEL-positive nuclei relative to the total number of nuclei counted.

Jejunum transcriptome analysis

Eight independent jejunal biological replicates per treatment collected on day 5 were used for transcriptomic analysis. Total RNA was extracted from jejunal tissue using TRIzol reagent. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit and subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw data were processed using fastp to obtain clean reads, which were then aligned to the NCBI Apla genome with HISAT2. Gene expression was quantified, and differential expression analysis between groups was performed using DESeq2 (|log2 (FoldChange [FC])| > 1 and padj < 0.05). Significantly enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were identified with clusterProfiler.

Statistical analysis

For non-transcriptomic quantitative data, FR and CR groups were compared separately at each sampling time point using two-sided independent-samples t-tests. Student’s t-test was used when the homogeneity-of-variance assumption was met, and Welch’s t-test was used when variances were unequal. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 20.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.5 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

CR is associated with structural damage in the duck intestine

Histological analysis via H&E staining showed intact intestinal architecture in floor-reared (FR) ducks, whereas CR ducks displayed segment-dependent villus and crypt alterations (Fig. 1a–c). Morphometric analysis (Table 2, Table 3, Table 4) showed that, on day 5, CR significantly reduced villus height and crypt depth in the duodenum (P < 0.01 and P < 0.05, respectively). In the jejunum, villus height and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio were significantly reduced on day 5 (P < 0.05), whereas crypt depth was not significantly different. In the ileum, crypt depth was significantly increased on day 5 (P = 0.0059), while villus height and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio were not significantly altered. CR alters antioxidant and oxidative stress markers

Oxidative stress-related biomarkers were assessed in intestinal segments and serum (Fig. 2). In the duodenum, CAT activity was significantly lower in CR ducks on days 3 and 5, T-SOD activity was lower on day 3, and MDA was higher on day 5 (P < 0.05). In the jejunum, GSH-Px activity was significantly lower on day 3 and MDA was higher on day 5 (P < 0.05). In the ileum, GSH-Px activity was lower in CR ducks on day 2 (P < 0.05). In serum, T-SOD activity was higher on day 3, whereas CAT activity was lower and GSH-Px activity was higher on day 5 (P < 0.05). No other FR–CR comparisons reached statistical significance. CR enhances ROS accumulation in jejunal epithelial cells

As presented in Fig. 4a, ROS were detected in jejunal epithelial cells using immunofluorescence (IF). CR ducks showed significantly greater ROS fluorescence and a higher proportion of ROS-positive cells than FR ducks on day 5 (P < 0.05; Fig. 4a, c). The positive-cell rate in the CR group was 1.57 times that in the FR group. Jejunum transcriptome results

As shown in Fig. 7a, principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between the CR and FR groups in the jejunum transcriptome. PC1 (31.2%) and PC2 (21.7%) accounted for 52.9% of the total variance. The volcano plot visualized differential gene expression between the CR and FR groups (Fig. 7b). A significant number of genes were dysregulated, with 1142 genes upregulated (red) and 241 genes downregulated (blue) in the CR group, based on a threshold of |log2FC| > 1 and padj < 0.05. Hierarchical clustering of 50 key differentially expressed genes (DEGs) clearly distinguished the CR and FR groups, revealing distinct expression patterns across critical biological pathways (Fig. 7c). Specifically, genes involved in oxidative stress (e.g., CAT, GPX1, PRDX6) were predominantly downregulated, while genes associated with autophagy and apoptosis (e.g., BNIP3, DRAM2, CASP7), lipid metabolism (e.g., PPARA, ACSL1, SCD, FABP3), and the inflammatory response (e.g., TLR4, NFKB1, IL6R, CEBPD) were significantly upregulated in the CR group. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified several key pathways central to these findings (Fig. 7d). Notably, pathways governing lipid metabolism (e.g., PPAR signaling pathway, Arachidonic acid metabolism, Linoleic acid metabolism) and those mediating inflammatory signaling (e.g., Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications) were among the most significantly enriched. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of biological processes (BP) highlighted several key pathways relevant to the observed physiological stress (Fig. 7e). The most significantly enriched terms included "inflammatory response" (60 genes), "response to lipopolysaccharide" (35 genes), "leukocyte chemotaxis" (37 genes), and "icosanoid metabolic process" (22 genes). As shown in Fig. 8b, bright-field images were captured to assess cell growth. Primary cells exhibited healthy growth under control conditions. TEM imaging showed autophagosome-like structures in H₂O₂-treated duck embryonic intestinal epithelial cells (1 μm and 200 nm). IF analysis showed a significant increase in LC3-positive cells (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8c, e), and WB analysis showed significant increases in Beclin-1 expression and the LC3B-II/LC3B-I ratio (P < 0.05; Fig. 8f, i, k). Thus, direct H₂O₂ exposure reproduced selected autophagy-related changes observed in vivo.

TUNEL IF staining revealed stronger and more abundant fluorescent signals in the H₂O₂-treated cell model (Fig. 8d, g), and quantitative analysis showed a significantly higher apoptotic rate than in controls (P < 0.05). PARP-1 protein abundance was also significantly increased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8f, h). These findings show that direct oxidative challenge was accompanied by apoptosis-associated changes in duck intestinal epithelial cells.

Discussion

The oxidative response to early cage transfer was time- and tissue-dependent rather than being confined to day 5. An isolated reduction in ileal GSH-Px was detected on day 2, multiple intestinal and serum antioxidant-related changes were present by day 3, and day 5 was characterized by increased MDA in the duodenum and jejunum together with clear segment-specific morphological injury. Zhang et al. (2019) similarly reported dynamic changes in antioxidant defense and inflammatory injury during the first 10 days after cage transfer. More recent studies have also documented intestinal redox disturbance and tissue injury during the early cage-rearing period (He et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2025), supporting the view that this interval represents an early adaptation phase rather than a stable long-term cage-rearing state. The present increases in jejunal ROS and serum 8-OHdG on day 5 provide additional evidence of oxidative stress and DNA oxidative damage. However, because antioxidant enzymes changed in different directions across tissues and time points, these findings are better interpreted as a disturbed oxidant–antioxidant balance than as a uniform increase or decrease in antioxidant capacity. Similar stress-associated reductions in antioxidant indices and intestinal villus injury have been reported after transport stress in mallard ducks, further illustrating the sensitivity of duck intestinal mucosa to acute environmental stress (Zhang et al., 2020).

ROS can participate in the regulation of autophagy, and damaged mitochondria may be removed through selective autophagy-related processes (Filomeni et al., 2010; Wei et al., 2015). In the present study, autophagosome-like structures and an increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio were observed on day 5, whereas Beclin-1 and the quantified LC3B-positive-cell rate did not differ significantly between groups. At the same time, TUNEL positivity and PARP-1 protein abundance were increased, while Caspase-3 showed only a nonsignificant upward trend. Taken together, these findings support coordinated autophagy-related and apoptotic responses during intestinal oxidative injury without establishing a direct autophagy-to-apoptosis causal sequence. Recent work in cage-stressed laying ducks likewise indicates that oxidative injury and autophagy-related responses can occur concurrently (Zong et al., 2026).

Transcriptomic analysis identified enrichment of PPAR signaling, arachidonic acid metabolism, Toll-like receptor signaling, and AGE-RAGE-related pathways. These findings nominate lipid-associated and inflammatory pathways as candidate components of the early cage-transfer response; because lipid metabolism was not directly measured, PPAR enrichment should not be interpreted as proof of lipid metabolic reprogramming. The H₂O₂-treated intestinal epithelial-cell model reproduced several features observed in vivo, including impaired antioxidant status, increased ROS, autophagy-related changes, and apoptosis-associated changes, supporting oxidative stress as an important contributor to the cellular phenotype. As a focused oxidative-challenge model, it complements rather than fully reproduces the in vivo cage-transfer condition. In broilers, experimental modulation of the TLR4/NF-κB axis during LPS challenge has been accompanied by changes in jejunal inflammation, barrier integrity, and epithelial apoptosis, supporting TLR4 signaling as a biologically plausible candidate pathway in the present model (Zha et al., 2024).

Autophagy-related proteins can participate in cellular homeostasis and in the handling of damaged organelles and dying cells (Lapaquette et al., 2021; Prerna and Dubey, 2022). Such mechanisms provide biological context for the concurrent autophagy-related and apoptotic changes observed in the jejunum and support further investigation of autophagy as a regulatory component of intestinal adaptation to cage-transfer stress.

The 10-day design was intended to characterize the acute transfer and early cage-adaptation period and therefore should not be extrapolated to long-term cage housing. Because the ducks were still in the pre-laying stage, production performance was not evaluated; future longer-term studies can determine how the intestinal responses identified here relate to laying performance. Mechanistic validation of the candidate autophagy/apoptosis and transcriptomic pathways will further refine the association model proposed in this study.

Conclusions

During the first 10 days after transfer to cages, Shaoxing ducks showed time- and tissue-dependent changes in intestinal antioxidant status, oxidative-damage markers, and morphology. Several biochemical alterations were evident by days 3–5, and segment-specific morphological injury was clear on day 5. Increased jejunal ROS, serum 8-OHdG, autophagosome-like structures, an increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, TUNEL positivity, and PARP-1 abundance indicate that oxidative injury, autophagy-related alterations, and apoptosis-associated changes occurred concurrently during early cage adaptation. The H₂O₂ cell model reproduced selected cellular features and supports a contribution of oxidative stress, while transcriptomic enrichment of AGE-RAGE-, Toll-like receptor-, and PPAR-related pathways identifies candidate mechanisms for further study. These data support an association model rather than a complete molecular cascade, and causal relationships require direct functional validation.
