Angular integrals with three denominators via IBP, mass reduction, dimensional shift, and differential equations
Juliane Haug and, Fabian Wunder

Abstract
Angular integrals arise in a wide range of perturbative quantum field theory calculations. In this work we investigate angular integrals with three denominators in d = 4 − 2 ​ ε d=4-2\varepsilon dimensions. We derive integration-by-parts relations for this class of integrals, leading to explicit recursion relations and a reduction to a small set of master integrals. Using a differential equation approach we establish results up to order ε \varepsilon for general integer exponents and masses. Here, reduction identities for the number of masses, known results for two-denominator integrals, and a general dimensional-shift identity for angular integrals considerably reduce the required amount of work. For the first time we find for angular integrals a term contributing proportional to a Euclidean Gram determinant in the ε \varepsilon -expansion. This coefficient is expressed as a sum of Clausen functions with intriguing connections to Euclidean, spherical, and hyperbolic geometry. The results of this manuscript are applicable to phase-space calculations with multiple observed final-state particles.

2
IBP relations

Since they are the foundation for everything that follows, we
start
by deriving IBP relations to reduce
I_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}
to master integrals.
IBP relations for angular integrals are derived in a similar fashion to loop integrals.
The only difference is that permissible vectors in the IBP relation have to be tangential to the spherical integration surface.
Concretely, the IBPs read

\displaystyle 0=\int\mathrm{d}\Omega\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{k}}\cdot\vec{\xi}\ \frac{1}{(v_{1}\cdot k)^{j_{1}}(v_{2}\cdot k)^{j_{2}}(v_{3}\cdot k)^{j_{3}}}\,,

(4)

where the
spatial
vector
\vec{\xi}
must satisfy

\vec{\xi}\cdot\vec{k}\overset{!}{=}0
.

To construct possible vectors
\vec{\xi}
we can use the
\vec{v}_{i}
as well as
\vec{k}
.
From the orthogonality condition, we find the three vectors (
i=1,2,3
; note that
k
is not an index)

\displaystyle\vec{\xi}_{ik}=(\vec{v}_{i}\cdot\vec{k})\vec{k}-\vec{k}^{2}\vec{v}_{i}

(5)

and the additional three vectors (
i<j
)

\displaystyle\vec{\xi}_{ij}=(\vec{v}_{i}\cdot\vec{k})\vec{v}_{j}-(\vec{v}_{j}\cdot\ \vec{k})\vec{v}_{i}\,.

(6)

For these vectors, it is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\cdot\vec{\xi}_{ik}}{\partial\vec{k}}=(d-2)\vec{v}_{i}\cdot\vec{k}\quad\text{respectively}\quad\frac{\partial\cdot\vec{\xi}_{ij}}{\partial\vec{k}}=0

(7)

and furthermore, recalling
v_{i}\cdot k=1-\vec{v}_{i}\cdot\vec{k}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{k}}\frac{1}{(v_{i}\cdot k)^{j_{i}}}=\frac{j_{i}\vec{v}_{i}}{(v_{i}\cdot k)^{j_{i}+1}}\,.

(8)

Applying the product rule, eq. (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle 0=\int\mathrm{d}\Omega

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{\partial\cdot\vec{\xi}}{\partial\vec{k}}\right)\frac{1}{(v_{1}\cdot k)^{j_{1}}(v_{2}\cdot k)^{j_{2}}(v_{3}\cdot k)^{j_{3}}}+\vec{\xi}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{k}}\frac{1}{(v_{1}\cdot k)^{j_{1}}(v_{2}\cdot k)^{j_{2}}(v_{3}\cdot k)^{j_{3}}}\right].

(9)

Using eqs. (
7
) and (
8
) on eq. (
9
) and expressing all scalar products in terms of (inverse) propagators leads to an IBP relation for each of the six
\vec{\xi}

Those relate
I_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}
for different indices.
We list them explicitly in appendix
B

For general
j_{1,2,3}
these IBP relations can be combined into identities that only either raise or lower the sum of indices.
This allows to bring them in the form of recursion relations that systematically reduce all integrals
I_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}
into the master integrals
I_{1,1,1}
,
I_{1,1,0}
,
I_{1,0,0}
,
I_{0,0,0}
(+ permutation of indices), bypassing the need for Laporta’s algorithm
[
58
]
.
The explicit recursion relations are given in appendix
C
.

To calculate the master integrals, we use the method of differential equations.
For this, we write the master integrals in the form of eq. (
1
) and differentiate both sides with respect to the kinematic invariants
v_{ij}
, which will lead to new angular integrals on the right-hand side – details of the differentiation are discussed in section
5

By reducing the right-hand side to master integrals via the recursion relations, we obtain a system of differential equations for the master integrals.
For a pedagogical introduction to the differential equation approach in the context of loop integrals see
[
45
,
46
]

Since the
\varepsilon
-expansion of two-denominator angular integrals is known
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, the only unknown master integral in our case is
I_{1,1,1}
.
By explicitly inserting the known integrals into the coupled system of differential equations, it is reduced to one closed differential equation for
I_{1,1,1}

To further simplify matters we use the following tricks:

(a)

By two-mass splitting we can always reduce the triple-massive integral to single-massive integrals.
We will be discussing this next in section
3
.
This simplifies the differential equation by reducing the number of variables. The number of master integrals is further reduced since the massless one-denominator integral
I^{(0)}_{1,0,0}
is reducible to
I^{(0)}_{0,0,0}
.

(b)

By using a dimensional-shift identity
d\rightarrow d+2
we obtain the pole and – somewhat surprisingly – also the finite part of the three-denominator integral “for free”, i.e. in terms of known integrals with fewer denominators.
We will look at this in section
4
.

(c)

By symmetry it suffices to look at a single derivative, say
{\partial}/{\partial v_{12}}
, for the massless case.
From there we only need a single boundary value for which we choose the symmetric point
v_{12}=v_{13}=v_{23}=1
.
To construct the single-massive integral from the differential equation with respect to
{\partial}/{\partial v_{11}}
we can use the previously calculated massless integral as a boundary condition.
This will be done in sections
6
and
7

3
Mass reduction via two-point splitting

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single-massive angular integral is sufficient to calculate multi-mass angular integrals.
This is possible due to the two-point splitting lemma
[
5
,
6
]
, which states that for any two vectors
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, we can choose any scalar
\lambda_{(12)}
and construct the linear combination

{v_{(12)}\equiv(1-\lambda_{(12)})\,v_{1}+\lambda_{(12)}\,v_{2}}

to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v_{1}\cdot k\,\,v_{2}\cdot k}=\frac{\lambda_{(12)}}{v_{1}\cdot k\,\,v_{(12)}\cdot k}+\frac{1-\lambda_{(12)}}{v_{2}\cdot k\,\,v_{(12)}\cdot k}\,.

(10)

If we consider two massive vectors
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
and choose
\lambda_{(12)}
such that
v_{(12)}
is massless, i.e.
v_{(12)(12)}=0
, eq.(
10
) splits the product of two massive denominators into the sum of two single-massive denominator products.
Iterative use of this identity on pairs of massive denominators allows for a reduction to the single-massive case.
Hence angular integrals with an arbitrary number of masses can always be expressed through at most single-massive integrals.
1
1

1

Solving the masslessness condition

\displaystyle\lambda_{(12)}^{\pm}=\frac{v_{12}-v_{11}\pm\sqrt{v_{12}^{2}-v_{11}v_{22}}}{2v_{12}-v_{11}-v_{22}}\,.

(11)

Both of these solutions can be used for a reduction of the number of masses.
To make the reduction well-behaved in the massless limit we can bring eq.(

v_{(21)}\equiv\lambda_{(21)}\,v_{1}+(1-\lambda_{(21)})\,v_{2}

Employing the two-point splitting lemma  (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v_{1}\cdot k\,\,v_{2}\cdot k}=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{(12)}}{v_{1}\cdot k\,\,v_{(12)}\cdot k}+\frac{\lambda_{(21)}}{v_{2}\cdot k\,\,v_{(21)}\cdot k}+\frac{1-\lambda_{(12)}-\lambda_{(21)}}{v_{(12)}\cdot k\,\,v_{(21)}\cdot k}\,.

\displaystyle\lambda_{(12)}=\frac{v_{12}-v_{11}-\sqrt{X_{12}}}{2v_{12}-v_{11}-v_{22}}\,,\quad\lambda_{(21)}=\frac{v_{12}-v_{22}-\sqrt{X_{12}}}{2v_{12}-v_{11}-v_{22}}\,,

Note that there is the geometrical interpretation
X_{12}=4\,\mathbb{V}(\vec{v}_{1},\vec{v}_{2})^{2}+(\vec{v}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2})^{2}

The scalar products of the auxiliary vectors are
{v_{(12)(12)}=v_{(21)(21)}=0}

{v_{1(12)}=-\lambda_{(12)}\sqrt{X_{12}}}

\displaystyle I_{1,1,1}^{(2)}(v_{12},v_{13},v_{23},v_{11},v_{22})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{X_{12}}}\left[v_{(12)(21)}I_{1,1,1}^{(0)}\left(v_{3(12)},v_{3(21)},v_{(12)(21)}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\tilde{I}_{1,1,1}^{(2)}(v_{12},v_{13},v_{23},v_{11},v_{22})=

\displaystyle\tilde{I}_{1,1,1}^{(3)}

4
Dimensional-shift identity

The
n
-denominator angular integral satisfies the general dimensional-shift identity

\displaystyle I_{j_{1},...,j_{n}}(\varepsilon)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-2\varepsilon}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}j_{i}I_{j_{1},...,j_{i}+1,...,j_{n}}(\varepsilon-1)+\,\left(3-\sum_{i=1}^{n}j_{i}-2\varepsilon\right)I_{j_{1},...,j_{n}}(\varepsilon-1)\right],

(23)

which expresses the integral in
d=4-2\varepsilon
dimensions on the left side as a sum over integrals in
d+2=6-2\varepsilon
dimensions on the right.
A proof of eq. (
23
) is given in appendix
D
.

In combination with IBP reduction, this identity can be used to analytically continue the angular integral from a convergent dimension to a divergent one.
Note that for the three-denominator angular integral already the angular measure as defined in eq. (
2
) is divergent in
d=4
dimensions due to the
\sin^{-1}\theta_{3}
factor.
With the above formula we can however express the integral in
d=4
dimensions in terms of integrals in
d=6
dimensions where the measure is finite because it contains an additional
\sin^{2}\theta_{3}
factor.

Also the master integrals with
j_{i}=1
and
v_{ii}=0
, which are collinearly divergent in
d=4
dimensions, converge for sufficiently large dimension, i.e. small
\varepsilon
.
In particular they are finite in
d=6
dimensions.
Hence applying eq.(
23
) to
I_{1,1,1}(\varepsilon)
and subsequently reducing the integrals in six dimensions on the right to master integrals, results in all
1/\varepsilon
poles being captured in prefactors of the master integrals.

As it turns out the identity connecting
I_{1,1,1}(\varepsilon)
and
I_{1,1,1}(\varepsilon-1)
simplifies further by shifting all integrals with two or less denominators back to
d=4-2\varepsilon
dimensions.
The necessary identities follow from eq.(
23
) by subsequently choosing zero, one, and two denominators, performing an IBP reduction and solving for the
d=6-2\varepsilon
dimensional integrals.
Going through the outlined steps, we find the remarkably simple relation

\displaystyle I_{1,1,1}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{X_{123}}\left[X_{12\bar{3}}I_{1,1,0}(\varepsilon)+X_{1\bar{2}3}\,I_{1,0,1}(\varepsilon)+X_{\bar{1}23}\,I_{0,1,1}(\varepsilon)+\frac{2\varepsilon}{1-2\varepsilon}\,Y_{123}\,I_{1,1,1}(\varepsilon-1)\right].

(24)

The kinematic factor
X_{123}
in this relation is given by the (Minkowski-)Gram determinant

\displaystyle X_{123}\equiv

\displaystyle\det\!\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}v_{11}&v_{12}&v_{13}\\
v_{12}&v_{22}&v_{23}\\
v_{13}&v_{23}&v_{33}\end{array}\right)=2v_{12}v_{13}v_{23}-v_{11}v_{23}^{2}-v_{13}^{2}v_{22}-v_{33}v_{12}^{2}+v_{11}v_{22}v_{33}\,.

The factors
X_{12\bar{3}}
,
X_{1\bar{2}3}
, and
X_{\bar{1}23}
multiplying the two-denominator integrals are obtained by interchanging the barred column in the Gram matrix with a column of ones
2
2

2

Reminiscent of Cramer’s rule for the solution of linear equations.
, i.e.

\displaystyle X_{12\bar{3}}\equiv\det\!\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}v_{11}&v_{12}&1\\
v_{12}&v_{22}&1\\
v_{13}&v_{23}&1\end{array}\right)=v_{12}(v_{13}+v_{23}-v_{12})-v_{11}v_{23}-v_{22}v_{13}+v_{11}v_{22},

for
X_{1\bar{2}3}
and
X_{\bar{1}23}
indices are interchanged cyclically.
The factor
Y_{123}
is given by the Euclidean Gram determinant already encountered in eq. (
3
),

\displaystyle Y_{123}=

\displaystyle 2v_{12}v_{13}+2v_{12}v_{23}+2v_{13}v_{23}-v_{12}^{2}-v_{13}^{2}-v_{23}^{2}-2v_{12}v_{13}v_{23}-v_{11}v_{23}(2-v_{23})

\displaystyle-v_{22}v_{13}(2-v_{13})-v_{33}v_{12}(2-v_{12})+v_{11}v_{22}+v_{11}v_{33}+v_{22}v_{33}-v_{11}v_{22}v_{33}\,.

(31)

Note, that geometrically

\displaystyle Y_{123}=\beta_{1}^{2}\beta_{2}^{2}\beta_{3}^{2}\sin^{2}\chi_{2}^{(1)}\sin^{2}\chi_{3}^{(1)}\sin^{2}\chi_{3}^{(2)}=36\,\mathbb{V}^{2}(\vec{v}_{1},\vec{v}_{2},\vec{v}_{3})

(32)

where
\mathbb{V}(\vec{v}_{1},\vec{v}_{2},\vec{v}_{3})
denotes the Euclidean volume of the tetrahedron spanned by the vectors
\vec{v}_{1,2,3}
.
We note that for real valued vectors
\vec{v}_{i}
the quantity
Y_{123}
appearing in the denominator vanishes if and only if the vectors are confined to a plane, i.e. they are linearly dependent.
In this particular case eq. (
24
) becomes a reduction identity of the three-denominator integral to a sum of two-denominator integrals –
remarkably the very same, one would also find by partial fractioning of the integrand.

Using the formula (

) has two benefits.
Firstly for the purpose of analytical calculation, we gain the pole part and the
\varepsilon^{0}
term “for free” since they are contained entirely in terms of known two-denominator integrals.
Since
I_{1,1,1}(\varepsilon-1)
is finite for
\varepsilon=0
and is multiplied by an explicit
\varepsilon

24
), it only starts contributing at order
\varepsilon
.
Hence, for results to order
\varepsilon
it suffices to calculate the leading term of
I_{1,1,1}(\varepsilon-1)
by differential equations.

Secondly, from a numerical perspective, by expanding the integration measure of
I_{1,1,1}(\varepsilon-1)
in a series in
\varepsilon
we obtain a finite integral representation for every order in
\varepsilon
.
In conjunction with the known expansion of the two-denominator integrals this can be used for numeric checks.

5
Differential operators for angular integrals

To derive differential equations with respect to the scalar products
v_{ij}
, we need to differentiate

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\Omega\,\frac{1}{(v_{1}\cdot k)^{j_{1}}(v_{2}\cdot k)^{j_{2}}(v_{3}\cdot k)^{j_{3}}}\,,

(33)

which however depends on the explicit vectors
\vec{v}_{i}
. Thus, the first step is to derive appropriate differential operators.
Due to symmetry, it is sufficient to consider the derivatives with respect to
v_{12}=1-\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{2}
respectively the mass
v_{11}=1-\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{1}
.
For this we make the ansätze

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{12}}\overset{!}{=}(a_{1}\vec{v}_{1}+a_{2}\vec{v}_{2}+a_{3}\vec{v}_{3})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{v_{1}}}\,\quad\text{respectively}\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{11}}\overset{!}{=}(b_{1}\vec{v}_{1}+b_{2}\vec{v}_{2}+b_{3}\vec{v}_{3})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{v_{1}}}\,.

(34)

The coefficients
a_{1,2,3}
and
b_{1,2,3}
are fixed by the conditions

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v_{11}}{\partial v_{12}}=0\,,\quad\frac{\partial v_{12}}{\partial v_{12}}=1\,,\quad\frac{\partial v_{13}}{\partial v_{12}}=0\quad\text{respectively}\quad\frac{\partial v_{11}}{\partial v_{11}}=1\,,\quad\frac{\partial v_{12}}{\partial v_{11}}=0\,,\quad\frac{\partial v_{13}}{\partial v_{11}}=0\,.

(35)

Derivatives of the other invariants
v_{22}
,
v_{23}
,
v_{33}
are trivially
0
due to the chosen ansatz.
For
\partial/\partial v_{12}
, in the massless case, where
v_{11}=v_{22}=v_{33}=0
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{12}}=\frac{1}{Y_{123}^{(0)}}

\displaystyle\Big[(v_{13}+v_{23}-v_{12}-v_{13}v_{23})\vec{v}_{1}

\displaystyle-(2-v_{13})v_{13}\vec{v}_{2}+(v_{12}+v_{13}-v_{23}-v_{12}v_{13})\vec{v}_{3}\Big]\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{v}_{1}},

(36)

where in the massless case the denominator is given by

\displaystyle Y_{123}^{(0)}=

\displaystyle-v_{12}^{2}-v_{13}^{2}-v_{23}^{2}+2v_{12}v_{13}+2v_{12}v_{23}+2v_{13}v_{23}-2v_{12}v_{13}v_{23},

(37)

which is the massless Euclidean Gram determinant from eq. (
31
).
Of course the operator can be generalized to the case of non-zero masses, however this is not needed in the following.
The differential operators
\partial/\partial v_{13}
and
\partial/\partial v_{23}
follow by interchanging indices.

For the differential operator
\partial/\partial v_{11}

we find in the single-massive case
v_{22}=v_{33}=0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{11}}=\frac{1}{2Y_{123}^{(1)}}

\displaystyle\Big[-v_{23}(2-v_{23})\vec{v}_{1}+(v_{13}+v_{23}-v_{12}-v_{13}v_{23})\vec{v}_{2}

\displaystyle+(v_{12}+v_{23}-v_{13}-v_{12}v_{13})\vec{v}_{3}\Big]\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{v}_{1}},

(38)

where the denominator is given by

\displaystyle Y_{123}^{(1)}=Y_{123}^{(0)}-v_{11}v_{23}(2-v_{23})\,.

(39)

Again we recognize the Euclidean Gram determinant from eq. (
31
), this time for one non-zero mass.

6
Differential equations

Having constructed suitable differential operators we are now equipped to derive differential equations for the three-denominator angular integral.
The only master integral with genuinely three denominators is
I_{1,1,1}
.
All other master integrals have at most two denominators and are known to all orders in
\varepsilon
.
To construct the full solution for an arbitrary number of masses, we have to look at two cases.
First we consider the massless integral.
It is symmetric in its variables
v_{12},v_{13},v_{23}
, hence it suffices to only consider the differential equation
\partial/\partial v_{12}
and construct a symmetric solution.
As a boundary value we will use the integral at the symmetric point
v_{12}=v_{13}=v_{23}=1
.
The second case to consider is the single-massive integral with
v_{11}\neq 0
.
Here, we look at the differential equation with respect to
\partial/\partial v_{11}
and use the massless integral as a boundary condition.
As discussed in section
4
it is beneficial to consider the differential equations for integrals in
d=6-2\varepsilon
dimensions.
This way, the leading-order solution in
\varepsilon
of the differential equation, which is of order
\varepsilon^{0}
, will provide a contribution to the order
\varepsilon
of the master integral in
d=4-2\varepsilon
dimensions.

By applying the differential operators to the massless integral, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I^{(0)}_{1,1,1}}{\partial v_{12}}=\frac{1}{Y_{123}^{(0)}}

\displaystyle\Big[v_{13}(v_{13}-v_{12}-v_{23})I^{(0)}_{2,1,1}+(v_{12}-v_{13}-v_{23}+v_{13}v_{23})I^{(0)}_{1,1,1}+v_{13}(2-v_{13})I^{(0)}_{2,0,1}

\displaystyle+(v_{23}-v_{12}-v_{13}+v_{12}v_{13})I^{(0)}_{2,1,0}\Big]

(40)

and for the single-massive integral

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I^{(1)}_{1,1,1}}{\partial v_{11}}=\frac{1}{2Y_{123}^{(1)}}

\displaystyle\Big[v_{23}(v_{23}-v_{12}-v_{13})I^{(1)}_{2,1,1}+v_{23}(2-v_{23})I^{(1)}_{1,1,1}+(v_{13}-v_{12}-v_{23}+v_{12}v_{23})I^{(1)}_{2,1,0}

\displaystyle+(v_{12}-v_{13}-v_{23}+v_{13}v_{23})I^{(1)}_{2,0,1}\Big].

(41)

From here, we can use the recursion relations from appendix
C
to reduce the right-hand side of both equations to master integrals.
In both cases, this results in a closed differential equation for
I_{1,1,1}
, where all other terms are expressed in terms of known lower-denominator integrals.

The reduction to master integrals of the massless equation in
d
dimensions leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I^{(0)}_{1,1,1}}{\partial v_{12}}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{d-6}{2v_{12}}-\frac{d-5}{Y_{123}^{(0)}}\,(v_{13}+v_{23}-v_{23}-v_{13}v_{23})\right]I^{(0)}_{1,1,1}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2v_{12}Y_{123}^{(0)}}\Big[(d-5)v_{12}(4-v_{12}-v_{13}-v_{23})I^{(0)}_{1,1,0}

(42)

Analogously, the single-massive differential equation becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I^{(1)}_{1,1,1}}{\partial v_{11}}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{(6-d)v_{23}}{2(2v_{12}v_{13}-v_{11}v_{23})}+\frac{(d-5)v_{23}(2-v_{23})}{2Y_{123}^{(1)}}\right]I^{(1)}_{1,1,1}

\displaystyle+\frac{d-5}{2Y_{123}^{(1)}(2v_{12}v_{13}-v_{11}v_{23})}\Big[\Big((v_{12}+v_{13}-v_{23})(v_{12}+v_{23}-v_{13}-v_{12}v_{23})+Y_{123}^{(1)}\Big)I_{1,1,0}^{(1)}

\displaystyle+\Big((v_{12}+v_{13}-v_{23})(v_{13}+v_{23}-v_{12}-v_{13}v_{23})+Y_{123}^{(1)}\Big)I_{1,0,1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle-(v_{12}+v_{13}-v_{23})v_{23}(2-v_{23})I_{0,1,1}^{(0)}\Big]+\frac{3-d}{v_{11}(2v_{12}v_{13}-v_{11}v_{23})}I_{0,0,0}^{(0)}\,.

(43)

We note that in both eqs. (
42

43

are known analytically in
d
dimensions.

As discussed in section
4
, it is most useful to consider the differential equations in
d=6-2\varepsilon
dimensions.
The standard procedure to approach differential equations of this form is bringing them to canonical
\varepsilon
-form, i.e. making the coefficient in front of
I_{1,1,1}
proportional to
\varepsilon
.
In the particular case at hand we can even make the entire coefficient disappear, leaving us with a right-hand side which is expressed solely in terms of known integrals
This is achieved by rescaling the integral to

Schematically writing the original eqs. (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I_{1,1,1}}{\partial v}\,=\,c(v)\,I_{1,1,1}\,+\,r(v)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J_{1,1,1}}{\partial v}\,=\left(\frac{1}{f(v)}\frac{\partial f(v)}{\partial v}\,+\,c(v)\right)J_{1,1,1}\,+\,f(v)\,r(v)\,.

Choosing

Explicitly, we rescale the massless integral as

\displaystyle J_{1,1,1}^{(0)}

For the single-massive integral we rescale as

\displaystyle J_{1,1,1}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{v_{23}}{2}\right)^{\varepsilon}(2v_{12}v_{13}-v_{11}v_{23})^{\varepsilon}\left(Y^{(1)}_{123}\right)^{1/2-\varepsilon}I_{1,1,1}^{(1)}\,,

All logarithmic divergences in the mass of the integral in

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J^{(0)}_{1,1,1}(\varepsilon-1)}{\partial v_{12}}=(v_{12}v_{13}v_{23})^{\varepsilon}\left(Y^{(0)}_{123}\right)^{-1/2-\varepsilon}\frac{1-2\varepsilon}{2\,v_{12}}\Big[v_{12}(4-v_{12}-v_{13}-v_{23})I^{(0)}_{1,1,0}(\varepsilon-1)

\varepsilon

For the derivative

Here, it is convenient to express the right-hand side in terms of

In this section we present the
\varepsilon

\varepsilon

In combination with the recursion relations listed in appendix

9
Beyond three denominators

If we consider a situation in
d=4-2\varepsilon
dimensions where all vectors
v_{i}
are confined to the physical
4
-dimensional subspace – an assumption applicable if the
v_{i}
correspond to momenta of observed particles – we can reduce angular integrals with an arbitrary number of denominators raised to integer powers to the three-denominator case presented in this paper.
This is possible since in
4
dimensions, i.e. three spatial dimensions, no more than three
\vec{v}_{i}
can be linearly dependent.
Hence for any
n>3
and vectors
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
there are constants
\lambda_{i}
such that
\lambda\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\neq 0
but
0=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\vec{v}_{i}
.
By partial fractioning it holds that
[
5
]

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{v_{i}\cdot k}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}\prod_{\overset{i=1}{i\neq j}}^{n}\frac{1}{v_{i}\cdot k}\,,

(83)

which reduces the number of denominators in each term.
Iterative application of this identity reduces any
I_{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}
with integer-valued
j_{i}
to the three-denominator case.
Therefore the presented results can be applied to this much wider class of angular integrals.

10
Conclusion

In this work we performed the first systematic study of angular integrals with three denominators and an arbitrary number of masses.
From IBP relations we derived explicit recursion relations for the reduction to a small set of master integrals.
The
\varepsilon
-expansion of the master integrals was calculated using the method of differential equations.

This methodology, which is well established for loop integrals, was combined with a dimensional-shift identity for angular integrals which allows for an extraction of the pole part before calculating the integral.
In the case of three denominators the order
\varepsilon^{0}
also turns out to be fully determined by two-denominator integrals, the unknown genuine three-denominator part only starts contributing at order
\varepsilon
.
The remaining non-trivial part at order
\varepsilon
, which contributes proportional to a Euclidean Gram determinant, has been expressed in terms of Clausen functions.
In the massless case we were able to give a geometrical interpretation of all involved quantities.
The ancillary
Mathematica
file provides the function
AngularIntegral[{j1_,j2_,j3_},{v12_,v13_,v23_,v11_,v22_,v33_}]
which evaluates the three-denominator angular integral in
d=4-2\varepsilon
dimensions up to order
\varepsilon
.

In parallel work, Taushif Ahmed, Syed Mehedi Hasan, and Andreas Rapakoulias independently calculated the angular integral with three denominators using Mellin-Barnes integrals
[
63
]
.
Preliminary numerical comparison at selected phase space points showed agreement.

Appendix F
Calculation of the boundary value for the massless master integral

This appendix is dedicated to the calculation of the boundary value used in the massless master integral.
The approach is a direct calculation using a string of series, integral, and special function identities successively reducing the complexity of the original integral.

(111)

In the specific case at hand we can further use the identity

\displaystyle{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,1,c;\frac{1}{2}\right)=(c-1)\left[\psi^{(0)}\!\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)-\psi^{(0)}\!\left(\frac{c}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right],

(112)

reducing the hypergeometric function to digamma functions
\psi^{(0)}(z)\equiv\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\Gamma(z)
.
This allows for the representation

\displaystyle I_{1,1}^{(0)}\!\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1};-n-1\right)=\frac{\pi}{n+1}\,{}_{2}\mathrm{F}_{1}\left(1,1,2+n;\frac{1}{2}\right)=\pi\left[\psi^{(0)}\!\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)-\psi^{(0)}\!\left(\frac{n}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right].

(113)

Putting this into eq. (
110
) and splitting the sum in even and odd terms we have

\displaystyle J^{(0),d=6}_{1,1,1}(1,1,1)

\displaystyle=\pi\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4n+1}\left[\psi^{(0)}\!\left(n+1\right)-\psi^{(0)}\!\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\pi\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4n+3}\left[\psi^{(0)}\!\left(n+\frac{3}{2}\right)-\psi^{(0)}\!\left(n+1\right)\right].

(114)

The digamma function at integer respectively half-integer values evaluates to

\displaystyle\psi^{(0)}\!\left(n\right)

\displaystyle=H_{n-1}-\gamma_{\text{E}}\,,

(115)

\displaystyle\psi^{(0)}\!\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\,U_{n}-\gamma_{\text{E}}-2\log 2\,,

(116)

where
H_{n}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{n}1/k
denotes the harmonic numbers and
U_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}1/(2k-1)
the odd harmonic numbers.
Using the identity

\displaystyle H_{n}-2\,U_{n}=-2A_{2n}

(118)

where
A_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k+1}/k
denotes the alternating harmonic
numbers
we find

\displaystyle\psi^{(0)}\!\left(n+1\right)-\psi^{(0)}\!\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-2A_{2n}+2\log 2\,,

(119)

\displaystyle\psi^{(0)}\!\left(n+\frac{3}{2}\right)-\psi^{(0)}\!\left(n+1\right)

\displaystyle=2A_{2n+2}-2\log 2\,.

(120)

Combining even and odd terms from both sums we have the representation

\displaystyle J^{(0),d=6}_{1,1,1}(1,1,1)=2\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2n+1}\left(\log 2-A_{n}\right).

(121)

Starting from the integral representation

\displaystyle\log 2-A_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{(-x)^{n}}{1+x}\,,

(122)

substituting
x=\left(\frac{1-t}{1+t}\right)^{2}
, and performing partial integration with respect to
1/(1+t^{2})
leads to the representation

\displaystyle\log 2-A_{n}=4(-1)^{n}(2n+1)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{(1+t)^{2}}\left(\frac{1-t}{1+t}\right)^{2n}\,\arctan t\,.

(123)

Plugging this into eq. (
121
) and summing the geometric series we finally obtain

\displaystyle J^{(0),d=6}_{1,1,1}(1,1,1)=2\pi\,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\,\arctan t=2\pi\,\mathrm{Ti}_{2}(1)=2\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{(2n+1)^{2}}=2\pi\,\mathrm{C}\,,

(124)

where
\mathrm{Ti}_{2}
is the inverse tangent integral and
\mathrm{C}
is Catalan’s constant.

A direct numerical check of the original integral eq. (
108
) additionally confirms
J_{1,1,1}^{(0),d=6}(1,1,1)\approx 5.75518
further raising confidence in the correctness of the result also for physicists who are not inclined to go through the motions akin to footnote
5
in each step.

Appendix H
Clausen function

The Clausen function
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\theta)
, named after 19th century Danish astronomer and mathematician Thomas Clausen, who calculated the function to 16 decimal places in 1832
[
65
]
, is defined by the Fourier series

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(n\theta)}{n^{2}}\,.

(130)

It arises naturally by considering the dilogarithm of imaginary exponential argument,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Li}_{2}\!\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\theta}}{n^{2}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos n\theta}{n^{2}}+\mathrm{i}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin n\theta}{n^{2}}.

(131)

By termwise differentiating the cosine series, one finds the real part to be a polynomial, explicitly for
0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Li}_{2}\!\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\frac{\theta(2\pi-\theta)}{4}\,,

(132)

while the imaginary part is the non-elementary Clausen function,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\,\mathrm{Li}_{2}\!\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\right)=\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\theta).

(133)

By differentiating eq. (
130
) one obtains the integral representation
[
65
,
64
]

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\theta)=-\int_{0}^{\theta}\mathrm{d}t\log\!\left|2\sin\frac{t}{2}\right|.

(134)

In figure
4
we depict the function
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\theta)
as well as the combination
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(2\arctan x)
appearing in the main text.

Figure 4:
The left panel shows a plot of the Clausen function on the interval
[0,2\pi]
. The right panel displays the combination
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(2\arctan x)
as it appears in the results from section
8
.

The Clausen function is antisymmetric, periodic with period
2\pi
, and obeys
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\pi+\theta)=-\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\pi-\theta)
.
Furthermore it satisfies the duplication formula

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(2\theta)=2\,\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\theta)-2\,\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\pi-\theta)\,.

(135)

Special values include
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(0)=\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\pi)=0
and
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\pi/2)=\mathrm{C}
, where
\mathrm{C}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n+1)^{2}}\approx 0.915965594
denotes Catalan’s constant.
The Clausen function attains its maximum at
\pi/3
at the value
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\pi/3)=\mathrm{G}\approx 1.014941606
called Gieseking’s constant.

Using the product expansion
\frac{\sin\pi z}{\pi z}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{z^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)
on the integrand of eq. (
134
), one obtains the series representation

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\theta)=\theta-\theta\log|\theta|-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}B_{2n}}{2n\,(2n+1)}\frac{\theta^{2n+1}}{(2n)!}\,,

(136)

valid for
|\theta|<2\pi
, where
B_{2n}
are the Bernoulli numbers.
By keeping one logarithm explicit in the expansion, an even more rapidly convergent series is obtained,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\theta)=3\theta-\theta\log\!\left|\,\theta\left(1-\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)\left(1+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)\right|-2\pi\log\!\left(\frac{2\pi+\theta}{2\pi-\theta}\right)+\theta\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta_{2n}-1}{n(2n+1)}\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)^{2n}\,,

(137)

which utilizes the quick convergence of the even zeta values
\zeta_{2n}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2n}}=(-1)^{n-1}\frac{(2\pi)^{2n}B_{2n}}{2(2n)!}
to
1
for large
n
.
It was this series, truncated at
n=14
, Clausen used in his original calculation
[
65
]
.
Other efficient numerical implementations can be found in
[
66
,
67
,
68
]
.

The Clausen functions appearing in this paper all have arguments of the form
2\arctan x
or
2\arctan x+2\arctan y
– note that the second type can be brought into the form of the first by using the addition theorem of the arcus tangent (
129
).
For real
x
,
y
, we can express these Clausen functions in terms of the single-valued Bloch-Wigner dilogarithm
\mathrm{D}_{2}(z)=\mathrm{Im}\,\mathrm{Li}_{2}(z)+\arg(1-z)\log|z|
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(2\arctan x)=\mathrm{D}_{2}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}-x}{\mathrm{i}+x}\right)\;\;\text{respectively}\;\;\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(2\arctan x+2\arctan y)=\mathrm{D}_{2}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}-x}{\mathrm{i}+x}\,\frac{\mathrm{i}-y}{\mathrm{i}+y}\right).

(138)

The Bloch-Wigner dilogarithm is connected to the volume of ideal hyperbolic tetrahedra
[
69

.
Concretely, in the upper-half space model, where the hyperbolic space
\mathfrak{H}_{3}
is represented by
\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}

z_{0,1,2,3}\in\mathbb{C}
has the hyperbolic volume

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathfrak{H}}(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=\mathrm{D}_{2}\!\left(\frac{z_{0}-z_{2}}{z_{0}-z_{3}}\,\frac{z_{1}-z_{3}}{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right).

(139)

The arguments of the Bloch-Wigner dilogarithms in eq. (
138
) are naturally represented as such cross ratios.
In particular

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(2\arctan x+2\arctan y)=\mathbb{V}_{\mathfrak{H}}(\mathrm{i},-\mathrm{i},x,-y)\,.

(140)

Another special function that is connected to the Clausen function is the inverse tangent integral
\mathrm{Ti}_{2}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\,\arctan t
, which can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ti}_{2}(x)=\arctan x\,\log x+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(2\arctan x)+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(\pi-2\arctan x)\,.

(141)

An analytic continuation of the Clausen function concatenated with an inverse tangent to complex arguments is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cl}_{2}(2\arctan z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{i}}\left[\mathrm{Li}_{2}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}-z}{\mathrm{i}+z}\right)-\mathrm{Li}_{2}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}+z}{\mathrm{i}-z}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\left(\mathrm{Li}_{2}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}+z}{\mathrm{i}-z}\right)+\frac{1}{4}\log^{2}\!\left(\frac{z+\mathrm{i}}{z-\mathrm{i}}\right)+\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\right).

(142)

It has a branch cut along the imaginary axis from
-\mathrm{i}
to
\mathrm{i}
