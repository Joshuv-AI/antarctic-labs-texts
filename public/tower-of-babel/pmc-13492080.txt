A patchwork workforce: state variation in behavioral health paraprofessional regulation
Authors: Hannah L Maxey, Mykayla Moore, Ashlyn Burns, Steven Stead
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIncreasing availability and wide geographic distribution of a skilled behavioral health/substance use (BH/SU) workforce is key to ensuring access to treatment for millions of individuals with BH/SU disorders. In many other healthcare sectors, there are state-regulated paraprofessional roles (ie, roles that require a bachelor's degree or less). Yet, the BH/SU field has historically lacked state-regulated paraprofessional roles, limiting workforce entry pathways.MethodsThe purpose of this study was to examine policy provisions that states have adopted to formalize BH/SU paraprofessional roles using a formal legal mapping methodology.ResultsAs of September 2025, we identified a total of 94 BH/SU paraprofessional roles regulated by 36 states. Certification was the dominant level of regulation, and a majority of the BH/SUs certificates had a nondegree training pathway. From a financing perspective, explicit reimbursement references were common, with certification showing the clearest billing pathway.ConclusionAs the first systematic national legal mapping of state-regulated BH/SU paraprofessional roles, these insights can assist states considering the adoption or expansion of BH/SU paraprofessional roles.

Introduction

Although fatal and non-fatal opioid overdoses declined for the first time in 2023, substance use disorders remain a significant public health concern.1-3 Nearly 50 million people in the United States have struggled with substance use disorders,4 which often co-occur with serious mental health disorders.5 Increasing availability and wide geographic distribution of a skilled behavioral health workforce is key to ensuring access to treatment for individuals with behavioral health and/or substance use (BH/SU) disorders. As of December 2025, 137 million Americans were living in federally designated mental health shortage areas,6 indicating a critical need to expand the workforce.

In numerous health care sectors (eg, medicine, allied health, oral health), there are several paraprofessional roles with formalized standards from national organizations that states defer to for regulation.7-9 These paraprofessionals support clinicians either through patient or non-patient related tasks. Evidence indicates that these roles can reduce mortality, enhance efficiency, and free up clinician time for higher complexity tasks to improve healthcare workforce capacity.7-9 In contrast, the BH/SU sectors have limited paraprofessional roles, with most roles within BH/SU settings requiring graduate level education (eg, counselors, social workers, psychiatric nurses), doctoral level education (eg, psychologists, psychiatrists), or lived experience (eg, peer support specialists, peer recovery coaches).10 Given limited formalization and the lack of any national datasets focused on BH/SU paraprofessional roles, it is currently unknown whether integrating paraprofessionals into the BH/SU workforce produces improvements in care delivery similar to those seen in other fields. While several states have demonstrated interest in formalizing BH/SU paraprofessional roles by developing pathways for these positions,10 little is known about how states are practically regulating these roles, specifically regarding reimbursement provisions, educational standards, and scope of practice.

The purpose of this study was to examine policy provisions that states have adopted to formalize BH/SU paraprofessional roles using legal mapping. As the first legal mapping of state-regulated BH/SU paraprofessional roles across the United States, our study may help inform national standards and guide states looking to establish or expand BH/SU paraprofessional roles.

Data and methods

Legal mapping overview

To identify policy provisions that states have adopted to formalize BH/SU paraprofessional roles and capture detailed information about how states define and regulate these roles, we used established legal mapping methodology. Legal mapping is a rigorous, systematic method used to capture legal text at a particular point in time and translate it into a structured, quantifiable dataset.11,12 Hence, this is a useful approach for developing structured datasets documenting health policies, including public protection and health workforce laws.11-14 Our legal mapping included all 50 states and the District of Columbia. BH/SU paraprofessional roles were considered in scope if they were “state-regulated” meaning that the role was codified in statute, administrative code, or binding guidance.

Data sources

All data presented in the current study were collected between June 6, 2025 and September 1, 2025. We used 3 main data sources to identify state-regulated BH/SU paraprofessional roles: (1) state laws and regulations accessed via Westlaw, a premier database for legal mapping research, (2) state laws and regulations accessed directly from official state websites, and (3) Medicaid documents such as State Plan Amendments, provider manuals, and fee schedules. In addition, we used a prior policy report on paraprofessionals to help guide our search and validate findings.10

Search strategy

We expected BH/SU paraprofessional roles to have titles such as behavioral health technician, psychiatric aide, addiction technician, and counselor assistant. Our searches used Boolean logic to combine terms like “behavioral health,” “mental health,” and “chemical dependency” with role descriptors such as “technician,” “aide,” and “assistant.” We tailored search strategies to each state's legal and Medicaid systems. During this initial review, we discovered additional related roles such as crisis workers and trainees, which we used to refine our search terms. We then conducted a second round of searches using an expanded list of search terms.

Exclusions

The BH/SU paraprofessional roles identified in our policy mapping represent the legal and policy environment as of the study's data collection period. All roles that were codified prior to or during the data collection period were included, but any roles that were codified after September 1, 2025 were excluded. We included any policy provision that defined, credentialed, or regulated a BH/SU paraprofessional role, or identified it as eligible for Medicaid reimbursement. We excluded documents that described services without naming a role, or that lacked legal authority. Employer-only policies were cataloged but not coded for comparison. Roles requiring lived experience (eg, peer support professionals, peer recovery roles) and roles without a behavioral health focus (eg, general community health workers) were also excluded. Unlike BH/SU paraprofessional roles, these roles have been formalized across states through national frameworks.

Protocol development

Prior to data collection, a team of 3 researchers with doctoral-level training in law, health policy, and health workforce and a fourth researcher with graduate-level training in health policy developed a formal legal mapping protocol and a question development table (QDT), which were further refined through consultation with Temple University's Center for Public Health Law Research. The QDT contained 10 questions that were used to identify state-regulated BH/SU paraprofessional roles and capture the following key details for each role: title, credential type, regulatory agency, education and training requirements, reimbursement eligibility, scope of practice, and supervision requirements.

Detailed coding categories

Credential types

Credential types were recorded based on the 3 levels of occupational regulation: registration, certification, and licensure. Registration is the lowest form of regulation, where an individual simply has to notify the government of the services they provide, but does not have to provide any proof of training. Certification is a higher level of regulation that restricts the title of an occupation, but not the practice (ie, certification is voluntary, but only individuals who have received the certification can use “Certified” in their title). Finally, licensure is the highest level of regulation that restricts the title and practice of an occupation, meaning that the scope of practice for licensed professionals is protected by accountability mechanisms.15

Regulatory agencies

Regulatory agencies are executive branch entities responsible for overseeing and enforcing laws, rules, and standards including for occupations. Unlike legislatures, which create laws, regulatory agencies operate under statutory authority delegated by the legislature, giving them the power to promulgate rules, conduct investigations, issue licenses or certifications, and implement penalties for noncompliance. These agencies function as the primary mechanism through which government translates broader legislative mandates into specific, enforceable requirements that govern occupations. For this study, executive branch entities were recorded and then reclassified into 4 types: occupational licensing boards, regulatory agencies, health and human services, and public safety agencies.

Education and training requirements

Education and training requirements refer to the minimum level of formal education or structured training an individual must obtain prior to entering a regulated occupation. In the context of occupational regulation, these requirements are typically codified in state statute and/or administrative rules, meaning they carry the force of law and must be satisfied before an individual is eligible to apply for licensure, certification, or registration. Education and training requirements were recorded to determine the level of education required (eg, non-degree, associate degree, bachelor's degree) and whether multiple training pathways existed for the role.

Reimbursement eligibility

A reimbursement reference indicates legal or policy authorization for reimbursement, but does not necessarily demonstrate actual payment, utilization, payment adequacy, or coverage across payers. For reimbursement eligibility, we recorded whether there were specific reimbursement references (ie, any language authorizing paraprofessional reimbursement for services) for the role (yes or no). This information was typically found in State Medicaid Plans, where states define the specific healthcare professionals that are authorized to deliver and receive reimbursement for healthcare services provided for Medicaid enrollees.16

Scope of practice

Scope of practice was initially recorded as the full scope of practice in statute, which was then reviewed in depth to inductively generate a comprehensive list of all services BH/SU paraprofessionals are authorized to provide. Upon generating this list, it became apparent that the services within the scope of practice for BH/SU paraprofessionals were generally aligned with the following categories: screening, brief interventions, referral to treatment, and treatment (SBIRT).17 Therefore, we systematically coded whether BH/SU paraprofessionals were authorized to provide any services within each SBIRT category. Furthermore, we generate a detailed list of specific treatment types that were mentioned in statute.

Supervision requirements

Scope of practice categories reflect the types of services permitted under state policy, either independently or under supervision. For each service category, we also recorded supervision requirements to indicate whether tasks could be performed independently or required oversight. If supervision was not specified, it was inferred based on operational context.

Quality control and validation

To ensure the quality and accuracy of all coded categories, the research team employed a 4-phase process. First, one researcher (initials blinded for review) extracted and compiled all relevant legal text and completed an initial round of coding. A second researcher then reviewed the legal text and coding to ensure agreement. Any discrepancies were flagged for group review and discussed until consensus was achieved. Then, the senior researcher and fourth member of the research team completed a third review of all coded data. Finally, after coding completion, we also contacted individuals from state regulatory authorities to further validate our interpretation of key details.

When contacting state regulatory authorities, we allowed 2 weeks for a response before sending a follow-up request. We then allowed 1 additional week for final validation. To validate our legal mapping and interpretation of key details for each BH/SU paraprofessional role, we contacted an individual within the regulatory authority, allowing 2 weeks to respond. We then sent a follow-up request, allowing 1 week for final validation. We received a response from individuals representing 26 states (72% response rate) and updated our coded data as needed based on new information provided.

Limitations

Several limitations affected our legal mapping. First, states use different titles for similar roles, which can lead to missed results. Second, Medicaid rules are spread across multiple documents and updated at different times, creating inconsistencies. In some cases, roles are implied through billing codes rather than named directly. Additionally, access to documents varied, with some states restricting manuals behind login portals and limiting completeness. Third, it is important to note that our ability to record services within scope of practice was limited to the level of detail provided in the legislative language. As such, it is possible that BH/SU paraprofessionals are permitted to provide additional services that are not specified here (eg, in cases where policy provisions permitted “treatment” without further specification about what this includes). Fourth, based on the legal data used in the current study, we are unable to determine the actual number of individuals in each of the BH/SU paraprofessional roles. Finally, all legal mapping studies can only capture laws that have been enacted up to a given moment in time. Given ongoing development of state-regulated BH/SU paraprofessional roles, it is possible that additional roles have been or will be established after our data collection period. Such roles are not captured in the present study.

Education requirements revealed multiple credentialing pathways, with 22 roles having multiple pathways (ie, a nondegree training path and an associate or bachelor's degree path). A majority of the BH/SU paraprofessional roles allowed for specified nondegree training in place of a formal degree (72%). While degree expectations varied some by level of regulation, nondegree training was the most common path across all levels of regulation.

In terms of regulatory authorities, licensure was primarily housed in occupational licensing boards (n = 23, 72%), while certification was most often situated in health and human services agencies (n = 24, 44%), with boards also being common (n = 21, 39%). Registration was split evenly between boards (n = 4, 50%) and health and human services (n = 4, 50%) and did not appear under public safety or general regulatory agencies.

From a financing and compliance perspective, explicit reimbursement references were common among BH/SU paraprofessional roles (n = 70, 74%). Certification showed the clearest billing pathway, with the majority of certified roles having reimbursement references (n = 43, 80%). Licensure was close behind (n = 25, 78%) and registration was split evenly (n = 4, 50%).

The scope of practice for BH/SU paraprofessionals included services within all SBIRT categories. Treatment was the most common category, with at least one type of treatment included in the scope of practice for 79 roles (84%). While scope of practice varied by level of regulation, treatment services were the most common type of service within each level. A detailed breakdown of specific treatment types and their supervision requirements is presented in Table 2. Across all BH/SU paraprofessional roles, the most common treatment services listed within scope of practice were unspecified counseling (supervised practice of 44 roles, independent practice of 21 roles) and substance use counseling (supervised practice of 42 roles, independent practice of 21 roles).

Discussion

This study provides information on the extent to which states have formalized BH/SU paraprofessional roles. While at least 36 states have codified BH/SU paraprofessional roles, we identified vastly different state approaches in terms of levels of regulation, education requirements, and reimbursement practices. States in every region have begun formalizing BH/SU paraprofessionals roles, yet a number of states have yet to adopt any.6 While our legal mapping provides useful insights about the substantial variation in state approaches, additional research is needed to inform future policy development. Future research could describe the process of implementing BH/SU paraprofessionals in more detail and assess whether the formalization of BH/SU paraprofessional roles ultimately influences workforce outcomes or care delivery.

Given that states are responsible for occupational regulation,18 and considering that the health industry has the largest number of state-regulated workers of any industry,19 questions around occupational regulation have important health policy implications. While this is the first study to examine the levels of regulation for BH/SU paraprofessional roles across all 50 states, prior research on the occupational regulation of more than 300 distinct occupations has linked policy data to measures of occupational licensing and certification, and in doing so, has demonstrated that state-level policy differences have an economically significant effect on the credentials that workers hold.19 Licensure is the most common level of regulation for health care roles and other types of behavioral health clinicians,20-22 and is the only level of regulation that offers title and scope protection.23 Our study found that certification was the most common level of regulation for BH/SU paraprofessional roles, followed by licensure. While certification does not restrict the provision of BH/SU paraprofessional services to credentialed individuals alone, it does confer title protection that signals demonstrated competency, which supports employer hiring decisions, helps protect patients from undertrained workers, and may lower barriers to workforce entry in a field characterized by high demand and limited workforce supply.

Levels of regulation may have important implications for education and reimbursement.15 Across all levels of regulation, most established BH/SU paraprofessional credentials could be obtained through non-degree pathways, indicating that many states are relying on alternative educational pathways as they develop these roles. These findings align with a broader trend in creating more accessible degrees and training pathways that emphasize skill-based learning. A separate but related analysis of median wages across state-regulated healthcare occupations found that applied, associate's degree occupations earn higher wages than both non-degree and bachelor's degree occupations (citation blinded for review). Collectively, these findings suggest that shorter-term credentials may offer the strongest economic returns in healthcare, illustrating the importance of investigating the relationships between regulatory schemas, education levels, and reimbursement.

We found that most BH/SU paraprofessional roles had codified reimbursement provisions. While licensed roles had the clearest pathways for reimbursement outlined in policy provisions, it is important to reiterate that our findings regarding reimbursement references only document the presence of legal authorization for reimbursement. Such authorization may not necessarily translate to actual payments, as there are many other steps involved in claims processing beyond policy provisions. However, a number of BH/SU paraprofessional roles did not have any reimbursement references in their policy provisions. Thus, it is unclear how these services are being financially supported, which should be investigated in future research. To ensure service sustainability, state policymakers must ensure that the services BH/SU paraprofessionals provide are billable under current reimbursement models or otherwise establish alternative funding strategies.

Policy makers should consider the scope of practice for all state-regulated roles very carefully, as scope of practice influences both employer demand for specific roles and service availability to the public.22 While some differences in scope of practice across roles were expected based on the level of regulation, notably, substance use counseling and unspecified counseling were the most consistently included services across all regulatory levels. As expected, licensed BH/SU paraprofessional roles had the widest scope of independent practice. Determining what services to include within scope of practice may be a key challenge that policy makers must navigate when creating BH/SU paraprofessional roles. Unfortunately, the impact of any proposed modification in a health occupation's scope of practice, referral, or supervision on costs, quality, and accessibility is rarely clear.22 To help guide state decision makers with evidence rather than rhetorical arguments, future research is needed to examine the impact of BH/SU paraprofessionals’ scope of practice on these key outcomes.

Conclusion

The behavioral health workforce shortage represents one of the most pressing public health challenges of our time, and state policies play a key role in shaping the workforce. This study offers the first systematic, national look at how states have formalized BH/SU paraprofessional roles, revealing significant policy variation. Policymakers at both the state and federal level have an opportunity to build on the progress documented here. While the examples of BH/SU paraprofessional roles identified in our legal mapping provide a blueprint that other states can learn from, additional research is needed to understand how different regulatory approaches shape the development of this emerging workforce and ultimately influence care delivery.
