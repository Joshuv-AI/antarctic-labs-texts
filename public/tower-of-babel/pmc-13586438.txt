Necrotizing Fasciitis Outcomes in Diabetic vs Non‐Diabetic Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Cameron Alexander Lynch, Abdiqadir Omar Mohamed, Chea Tze Ong, Muneeb Ahmad Khan, Mohaned Mohamed, M Isuru Hiranya Perera, Jason Roberts, Aaron Chai, Hadeel Hussein, Abdal Qadir Zafar, Shafiq Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsNecrotizing fasciitis (NF) is a rapidly progressive, life‐threatening infection, with the lower limbs being a common site. Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a significant risk factor that influences the progression, outcome, and management of NF. Despite its clinical relevance, comparative data on diabetic versus non‐diabetic NF outcomes remain limited. This study aimed to compare mortality, amputation rates, and other key outcomes between diabetic and non‐diabetic patients with NF.MethodsA systematic review and meta‐analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Eligible studies assessed outcomes in diabetic and non‐diabetic NF patients. Primary outcome measures included amputation rates, mortality, admission length, debridement frequency, and microbial growth. Secondary outcomes included the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) score and unplanned reoperations. Pooled analyses were performed using OpenMeta[Analyst] software, reporting odds ratios (OR) and mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsNine studies comprising 1,890 patients met the inclusion criteria. Diabetic patients had significantly higher rates of amputation (OR 3.77, 95% CI 3.04 to 4.68; p < 0.001) and polymicrobial infections (OR 2.53, 95% CI 1.50 to 4.26; p < 0.001). Mortality was higher among diabetic patients after sensitivity analysis (OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.09 to 2.33; p = 0.02). Length of admission did not differ significantly between groups, whereas the pooled number of debridements was marginally higher in diabetic patients (MD 0.28, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.52; p = 0.02), based on only two studies. Diabetic patients had significantly higher LRINEC scores (MD 2.02, 95% CI 1.33 to 2.72; p < 0.001).ConclusionDiabetic patients with NF experience worse clinical outcomes, including increased amputation, mortality, and polymicrobial infection. These findings highlight DM as a key prognostic factor and underscore necessity for aggressive intervention and risk stratification. Further high‐quality prospective studies are needed.Keywords: amputation, diabetes mellitus, LRINEC score, meta‐analysis, mortality, necrotizing fasciitis, polymicrobial infection

Introduction

Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) is a rare, rapidly progressing, and life‐threatening soft tissue infection characterized by extensive fascial and subcutaneous tissue necrosis [1, 2, 3, 4]. With an incidence of 0.3 to 0.4 cases per 100,000 annually [5]. NF remains a significant sequela of soft tissue infections, causing severe morbidity and mortality [6]. It can affect any body part, but the lower extremities are most commonly involved [7, 8]. Early diagnosis, aided by the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) score, and prompt surgical intervention is critical for improving outcomes [9]. Treatment involves removing necrotic tissue, often requiring follow‐up surgeries [10, 11].

Patients with diabetes mellitus (DM) are particularly susceptible to necrotizing infections due to multiple pathophysiological mechanisms. Chronically raised blood glucose levels impair immune function by contributing to tissue hypoxia, induce defective phagocytosis as well as ischemia secondary to microvascular disease [12]. In addition, peripheral neuropathy can mask the classic symptoms of infection, leading to delayed presentation and more severe disease at diagnosis. When NF develops, diabetic patients often exhibit higher rates of polymicrobial growth, more frequent limb amputation, and prolonged hospital stays compared to non‐diabetic cohorts [13, 14, 15]. Continuously elevated blood sugar levels further predispose these patients to a pro‐inflammatory state, hindering wound healing through endothelial dysfunction and reduced angiogenesis [16].

Numerous studies have explored the differences in clinical outcomes between diabetic and non‐diabetic NF patients, but there remains a paucity in the evidence. Many studies are limited by small sample sizes, single‐center designs, and variability in defining key prognostic factors, particularly the role of DM [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. This lack of a comprehensive analysis has impeded the development of more evidence‐based clinical guidelines.

There is currently no study in the literature that amalgamates the outcomes of all available data on the outcomes of NF with and without DM. The authors aimed to conduct the first systematic review and meta‐analysis of the literature and enhance the current evidence base.

Methodology

The authors conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis of the published literature according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22].

Eligibility Criteria

All available randomized control trials, cohort, case‐control, and observational studies comparing the outcomes of NF between diabetic and non‐diabetic outcomes were collated. Patients with diabetes were the intervention group of interest, and non‐diabetic patients were the control. Exclusion criteria included review papers, case reports, book chapters, audits, papers not reported in English and those without a control group.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

Primary outcomes included the incidence of limb amputation, number of debridements, and overall mortality. Amputation included all forms of extremity amputations with no exclusion of the level at which this was conducted. Secondary outcomes analyzed were the LRINEC score, length of hospital stay, and microbiological profile with regard to mono‐ or poly‐microbial growth.

Literature Search Strategy

Two authors, H.H. and M.A.K., independently conducted a literature search via the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), PUBMED, CINAHL, and MEDLINE. The last search was conducted on 23rd November 2024 with search terminologies including “diabetic”, “DM”, “diabetes mellitus”, “non‐diabetic”, “non diabetic”, “necrotizing fasciitis”, and the American English “necrotizing fasciitis”. To identify unpublished articles, authors searched the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry (http://apps.who.int/trialsearch), ClinicalTrials. gov (http://clinical-trials.gov), and the ISRCTN registry (http://www.isrctn.com). All search terms were used with adjuncts of “and”, as well as “or”. The literature search was extended by citation searching from the bibliographies of eligible papers. Language restrictions were applied, and those not published in English were excluded.

Selection of Studies

The titles and abstracts of papers retrieved during the literature search were analyzed by two authors, H.H. and M.A.K. Of those meeting the eligibility criteria, full texts were obtained and selected for inclusion in the systematic review and meta‐analysis. All authors discussed and reached a consensus regarding any inconsistencies highlighted during the literature search.

Data Extraction and Management

As per Cochrane's data collection guidance for intervention reviews [23], an electronic Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was created to enter the data. Two authors, A.C. and C.O., completed a pilot‐test of randomly selected articles and adjusted the document accordingly. The spreadsheet consisted of study‐relevant data (author name, year of publication and study design type), primary and secondary outcomes, and associated demographics of patients (age and gender). Any disagreements regarding the appropriateness of data for inclusion were resolved via group discussion with all authors.

Data Synthesis

Data synthesis was completed via OpenMeta[Analyst] software. For dichotomous data, the odds ratio (OR) was implemented. For continuous data, the mean difference (MD) was calculated. Results from both data categories were presented in forest plots with confidence intervals (CIs) of 95%.

Assessment of Heterogeneity

The heterogeneity of the data was determined using the Cochran Q test (χ2) as well as the I2 score, with interpretation of the results values as follows: 0%‐25% (low heterogeneity), 25%–75% (moderate heterogeneity) and 75%–100% (high heterogeneity). An inverse variance function was applied to mitigate instances of moderate to high heterogeneity.

Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias Assessment

The methodological quality of all studies was evaluated using the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale (NOS) [24]. The NOS is a tool used to assess the quality and risk of bias for all observational studies. It evaluates studies based on three main criteria: the selection, comparability, and the ascertainment of outcomes. Higher scores indicate a lower risk of bias. A maximum score of nine stars is possible for each study.

Statistical Analysis

All pooled analyses were conducted using OpenMeta[Analyst] (Center for Evidence Synthesis in Health, Brown University, USA). Dichotomous outcomes (limb amputation, mortality, and mono‐ or polymicrobial growth) were summarized as odds ratios (OR), and continuous outcomes (length of hospital stay, number of debridements, and LRINEC score) as mean differences (MD), each reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Between‐study heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q statistic (χ2) and the I2 statistic, and an inverse variance method was applied to derive the pooled estimate where moderate to high heterogeneity was present. All tests were two‐sided, and a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The primary mortality analysis included all eight eligible studies and was prespecified; the subsequent analysis excluding the two lower extremity cohorts reported by Ahn and colleagues was a post‐hoc, exploratory sensitivity analysis and is identified as such throughout. Statistical reporting followed the Statistical Analyses and Methods in the Published Literature (SAMPL) guidelines [25].

Results

Literature Search and Risk of Bias Results

An electronic search of the published literature yielded 152 records, which were assessed, with 9 fulfilling the inclusion criteria for both qualitative and quantitative synthesis. Of the 143 records excluded, 122 were irrelevant to diabetic and non‐diabetic NF, 4 were reviews, 6 were non‐English, and 11 were case reports. Primary Outcomes

Amputation

Six studies evaluated amputation rates in diabetic and non‐diabetic patients with NF. Quantitative analysis identified that diabetic patients had significantly higher amputation rates compared to non‐diabetic patients, with a pooled OR of 3.768 (95% CI 3.035 to 4.678; p < 0.001; 619/955 [64.8%] vs 250/715 [35.0%]) (Figure 2). Heterogeneity across the studies was low, indicating consistency in the findings.

Odds ratio for amputation in diabetic versus non‐diabetic patients with necrotizing fasciitis (six studies). Pooled OR 3.768 (95% CI 3.035 to 4.678; p < 0.001), I 2 = 0%. Events: 619/955 (64.8%) diabetic versus 250/715 (35.0%) non‐diabetic.

Mortality

Eight studies assessed mortality in diabetic and non‐diabetic patients with NF. Initially, the analysis, including all eight studies, indicated no statistically significant increase in mortality between the groups, with a pooled OR of 0.863 (95% CI 0.674 to 1.105; p = 0.24; 172/1019 [16.9%] vs 160/821 [19.5%]). A generic inverse variance function was adopted to circumvent high heterogeneity (Figure 3a).

(a) Odds ratio for mortality in diabetic versus non‐diabetic patients with necrotizing fasciitis, all eight studies. Pooled OR 0.863 (95% CI 0.674 to 1.105; p = 0.24), I2 = 0%. Events: 172/1019 (16.9%) diabetic versus 160/821 (19.5%) non‐diabetic. (b) Odds ratio for mortality in diabetic versus non‐diabetic patients with necrotizing fasciitis, post‐hoc sensitivity analysis excluding the two Ahn cohorts (2019 and 2022; six studies). Pooled OR 1.595 (95% CI 1.094 to 2.326; p = 0.02), I2 = 0%. Events: 96/306 (31.4%) diabetic versus 66/298 (22.1%) non‐diabetic.

A post‐hoc, exploratory sensitivity analysis was subsequently performed, excluding two studies by Ahn et al. [26, 27], which reported lower mortality in diabetics (45/387 [11.6%] vs 54/287 [18.8%], and 31/326 [9.5%] vs 40/236 [16.9%], respectively). These two cohorts were the only lower extremity specific series and were both derived from the same national surgical database, differing methodologically from the remaining studies as well as from the trends observed in the broader literature [18, 28, 29, 30].

Following exclusion of these two studies, the sensitivity analysis involving the remaining six studies demonstrated a statistically significant increased risk of mortality associated with diabetes, yielding a pooled odds ratio calculated with an inverse variance function of 1.595 (95% CI 1.094 to 2.326; p = 0.02; 96/306 [31.4%] vs 66/298 [22.1%]) (Figure 3b).

Length of Stay

Five studies assessed the length of hospital stay in diabetic and non‐diabetic patients with NF. The pooled analysis showed a mean difference of 1.053 days (95% CI −0.129 to 2.235) between the two groups, with diabetic patients having slightly more extended stays (Figure 4). However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.08). Heterogeneity among the studies was low, indicating consistency in the findings.

Mean difference in hospital length of stay (days), diabetic versus non‐diabetic patients with necrotizing fasciitis (five studies). Mean difference 1.053 (95% CI −0.129 to 2.235; p = 0.08), I 2 = 0%.

Number of Debridements

Two studies compared the number of debridements required in diabetic and non‐diabetic patients with NF. The pooled analysis showed a mean difference of 0.282 (95% CI 0.046 to 0.518; p = 0.02), indicating a small but statistically significant increase in the number of debridements among diabetic patients (Figure 5). This estimate was based on only two studies and the absolute difference was minor. Between‐study heterogeneity was low (I 2 = 0%, heterogeneity p = 0.96).

Mean difference analysis of debridements in diabetic versus non‐diabetic patients with necrotizing fasciitis (two studies). Mean difference 0.282 (95% CI 0.046 to 0.518; p = 0.02), I 2 = 0% (heterogeneity p = 0.96).

Mono‐Microbial

Four studies examined the incidence of mono‐microbial growth in diabetic and non‐diabetic patients with NF. The pooled analysis showed a higher incidence of mono‐microbial growth in non‐diabetic patients, with an OR of 0.404 (95% CI 0.241 to 0.678; p < 0.001; 134/208 [64.4%] diabetic vs 133/162 [82.1%] non‐diabetic) (Figure 6). Heterogeneity among the studies was low.

Odds ratio assessment of mono‐microbial growth in diabetic versus non‐diabetic patients with necrotizing fasciitis (four studies). Pooled OR 0.404 (95% CI 0.241 to 0.678; p < 0.001), I 2 = 0%. Events: 134/208 (64.4%) diabetic versus 133/162 (82.1%) non‐diabetic.

Poly‐Microbial

Three studies evaluated the incidence of poly‐microbial growth in diabetic and non‐diabetic patients with NF. The pooled analysis demonstrated a significantly higher incidence of poly‐microbial infections in diabetic patients, with an OR of 2.527 (95% CI 1.499 to 4.260; p < 0.001; 74/184 [40.2%] diabetic vs 29/144 [20.1%] non‐diabetic) (Figure 7). Heterogeneity among the studies was low.

Odds ratio for poly‐microbial growth, diabetic versus non‐diabetic patients with necrotizing fasciitis (three studies). Pooled OR 2.527 (95% CI 1.499 to 4.260; p < 0.001), I 2 = 0%. Events: 74/184 (40.2%) diabetic versus 29/144 (20.1%) non‐diabetic.

Secondary Outcomes

Lrinec Score

Two studies reported the LRINEC scores between diabetic and non‐diabetic groups, both demonstrating significantly higher scores in diabetic patients. DeGenova et al. (2023) observed a mean LRINEC score of 9.02 in diabetics compared to 7.24 in non‐diabetics (p = 0.02); Tan et al. (2016) reported scores of 8.4 in diabetics and 6.2 in non‐diabetics, with a significant difference (p < 0.05). The authors of this study calculated a mean difference of 2.024 (95% CI 1.332 to 2.716; p < 0.001) with low heterogeneity (Figure 8).

Mean difference analysis of LRINEC score in diabetic versus non‐diabetic patients with necrotizing fasciitis (two studies). Mean difference 2.024 (95% CI 1.332 to 2.716; p < 0.001), I 2 = 0%.

Unplanned Reoperation

Ahn [27] showed no difference in reoperation rates in diabetic and non‐diabetic patients with NF (p = 0.48). This was defined as unplanned reoperation within 30 days of the initial presentation and included unplanned revision of amputation, conversion to a more proximal amputation, and conversion to amputation from initial debridement.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis evaluated key clinical outcomes in NF between diabetic and non‐diabetic patients, identifying significant higher amputation rates, mortality, poly‐microbial growth patterns as well as LRINEC scores in diabetic patients. The findings highlight the importance of early intervention, tailored management strategies, and standardized diagnostic tools in improving outcomes.

Amputation rates were significantly higher in diabetic patients (p < 0.001), reflecting the impact of vascular insufficiency and immune dysfunction in accelerating infection and impairing healing [31, 32]. All six studies in the analysis consistently reported a statistically significant increase in amputation rates among diabetics, with minimal variation across findings (I2 = 0), emphasizing the strength and consistency of this association. These results highlight the critical importance of early vascular assessment and timely intervention to reduce the risk of limb loss in diabetic patients with compromised circulation.

There was a small but statistically significant increase in the number of debridements among diabetic patients (mean difference 0.282, p = 0.02), although this finding was based on only two studies and the absolute difference was minor, with low heterogeneity across studies. Standardized protocols, such as those outlined by the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS‐NSQIP), emphasize early and aggressive surgical intervention and need‐based reoperation, which have been associated with improved outcomes in both diabetic and non‐diabetic populations [27]. In addition, improved glycaemic control and the use of newer diabetes medications can enhance tissue perfusion and immune responses, potentially reducing the severity of infection and the need for repeated debridements [18, 29, 32].

There was no statistically significant difference in mortality between diabetic and non‐diabetic patients with NF in the initial pooled analysis of eight studies (p = 0.24). However, a sensitivity analysis excluding two studies by Ahn [26, 27] identified a significantly increased mortality risk associated with diabetes (p = 0.02). Oncul [29] and Cheng [28] reported higher mortality in diabetic patients, particularly in the presence of uncontrolled blood glucose, sepsis, and delayed insulin use. Additionally, diabetic patients often receive routine care for comorbidities such as renal dysfunction, hypertension, and respiratory disease, which may enable earlier intervention and improved outcomes [19]. For example, routine nephrology follow‐up can facilitate the early recognition and treatment of acute kidney injury, a known predictor of mortality in NF [33]. These findings suggest that diabetes is a significant mortality risk in NF, primarily when it coexists with poor glycaemic control, systemic infection, or delays in care.

The hospital length of stay showed no statistically significant difference between the diabetic and non‐diabetic groups (p = 0.08). This was somewhat unexpected given the known association between diabetes and delayed wound healing, infection‐related complications, and the need for prolonged medical optimization [29, 32]. Studies such as Kaur [18] reported longer stays for diabetic patients (p < 0.001), and Ahn et al. [27] attributed extended admissions to surgical interventions such as amputations, irrespective of diabetes status. These findings suggest that hospitalization duration is influenced more by surgical complexity and associated comorbidities than by diabetes status alone [34]. Breaking down the analysis by type of surgery or treatment is essential to separate the effect of diabetes from the effects of the procedures themselves and to understand better what influences hospital stay and recovery time.

Statistically significant differences in microbial growth patterns were observed between the groups. Diabetic patients exhibited more polymicrobial infections (p < 0.001), while non‐diabetic patients had predominantly mono‐microbial infections (p < 0.001). These findings align with Tan [19], Nisbet [20] and Oncul [29], which demonstrated a predominance of polymicrobial infections in diabetics, often associated with worse outcomes [7]. This trend likely reflects the altered immune environment in diabetes, where hyperglycemia, impaired neutrophil function, and tissue hypoperfusion promote mixed microbial colonization [18, 28, 29, 32]. Common polymicrobial combinations included Streptococcus spp. with Escherichia coli or Staphylococcus aureus with Klebsiella pneumoniae, whereas monomicrobial infections were typically caused by Streptococcus pyogenes or Staphylococcus aureus. These patterns emphasize the clinical importance of early broad‐spectrum antimicrobial therapy, and culture‐guided adjustments are crucial in managing the complex infection profiles in diabetic patients [31].

Diabetic patients also presented with significantly higher LRINEC scores, with a mean difference of 2.024 (p < 0.001), reflecting greater disease severity [35]. As Tan [19] and DeGenova [30] reported, elevated scores were associated with systemic derangements such as hyperglycemia and electrolyte abnormalities. These findings validate the LRINEC score as a valuable tool for identifying patients at high risk. LRINEC scores should prompt aggressive evaluation and early surgical consultation, especially in diabetic patients with non‐specific presentations [5, 9, 35].

Higher ASA scores, a measure of systemic disease burden, were consistently observed in diabetic patients across studies by Cheng [28], Ahn [26], and Ahn et al [27]. Most diabetic patients fell into ASA classes III‐IV, reflecting significant comorbidity and correlating with higher mortality rates. These findings reinforce the importance of thorough preoperative assessment and optimization to improve surgical outcomes in diabetics.

Certain limitations must be acknowledged. All included studies were retrospective in nature, with low scores reached under the comparability domain on methodological quality review with the Newcastle Ottawa scale. Nonetheless, all studies collectively had an amalgamated total of 1,890 patients to assess outcomes between the two cohorts of patients. The included studies also varied in the anatomical distribution of necrotizing fasciitis, and outcomes were not consistently stratified by site. Two lower extremity specific cohorts contributed a substantial proportion of the pooled sample, which may have weighted limb‐specific outcomes such as amputation. In addition, the pooled estimates were derived from unadjusted study‐level data, so residual confounding by factors such as age, comorbidity, and disease severity cannot be excluded. Prospective studies with standardized protocols are essential to strengthen and enhance these findings.

This systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrated that diabetic patients face a higher risk of severe complications from NF compared to non‐diabetic patients, including statistically significant increased risk of mortality, amputations, elevated LRINEC scores, and presence of polymicrobial infections. Diabetes is an important risk factor and prognostic marker when evaluating and treating patients suspected of having NF. These patients require timely and aggressive treatment compared to their non‐diabetic counterparts due to potential poorer outcomes. This systematic review is the first to compare NF outcomes between diabetic and non‐diabetic groups directly, and its results can be extrapolated globally, with swift implementation at the local level recommended.

Conclusion

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta‐analysis reporting outcomes of NF amongst diabetic and non‐diabetic patient cohorts. Significant differences were observed in the rates of mortality, amputation, LRINEC scores, and incidence of polymicrobial growth compared to non‐diabetic individuals with NF. Diabetes appears to be a significant independent risk factor in determining outcomes for patients with NF. Further studies, however, are necessary to enhance the current database.
