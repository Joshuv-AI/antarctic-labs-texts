Unusual presentation of dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans in a young female: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Ankur Bhandari, Sabina Nhemafuki, Saurav Shah, Anil Mahato, Ujwal Sah, Sudikxya Basnet, Bishnu Prasad Ghimire

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a rare, low-grade malignant cutaneous soft tissue sarcoma of fibroblastic origin, characterized by slow but infiltrative growth and a high propensity for local recurrence, while distant metastasis remains uncommon. The reported incidence ranges from 0.8 to 5 cases per million population per year, accounting for less than 0.1% of all malignant tumors and approximately 1% of soft tissue sarcomas.Case presentation:The author reports a case of 33-year-old woman with no known comorbidities who presented to neuro OPD with complaint of solitary, dark-brown lump, associated with occasional pain over the lateral aspect of the elbow, for 7–8 months which was expanding gradually. Presentation at the elbow is uncommon and may delay diagnosis. This case emphasizes the importance of histopathological confirmation and adequate surgical excision with appropriate reconstruction. Long-term surveillance remains essential to detect recurrence early.

Introduction

Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a rare cutaneous soft tissue tumor characterized by indolent growth, a high propensity for local infiltration and recurrence, but a low risk of distant metastasis. It mainly presents between the second and fifth decades of life. It typically presents on the trunk, proximal limbs, and head and neck, while presentation on the elbow is rare; DFSP is driven by a reciprocal translocation t(17;22)(q22;q13). Here, we report a rare case of DFSP in a 33-year-old female, arising over the left elbow, an uncommon anatomical location that may delay clinical suspicion and diagnosis. The aim of this report is to highlight the diagnostic challenges, histopathological features, surgical management, and the importance of considering DFSP in the differential diagnosis of slowly enlarging cutaneous lesions at unusual sites, particularly in resource-limited settings. Case presentation

The author reports a case of a 33-year-old young female with no known co-morbidities and a past history of an insect bite 2 years ago. The patient presented with a solitary, dark-brown, painful lump, associated with occasional sharp, shooting, radiating pain on the lateral aspect of the left elbow for 7–8 months. The lesion was insidious in onset, progressive in nature, initially the size of a pinhead, and gradually enlarged to approximately 1 × 1 cm over 1 year (Fig. 1). The lesion was not associated with any discharge, itching, loss of sensation, decreased grip strength, muscle wasting, or color change. Upon arrival, the patient was alert and cooperative; vitals were recorded as follows: blood pressure: 120/80 mmHg; pulse: 70 bpm; respiratory rate: 22/min; SpO2: 97% on room air; Glasgow Coma Scale: 15/15. On local examination, a solitary, firm, mobile, non-tender, well-defined, dark brown nodule measuring 1 × 1 cm was noted on the anterolateral aspect of the left elbow, approximately 5 cm anterolateral to the olecranon process. The overlying epidermis was smooth, intact, and non-ulcerated, with no crusting or dimpling (Fig. 1). Regional lymph nodes were not palpable. Subsequently, a differential diagnosis of foreign body granuloma, keratin granuloma, atypical mycobacterial infection, deep fungal infection, calcified nodule, and dermatofibroma was considered; these were ruled out by a biopsy of the left arm.

Initial laboratory investigations were within normal limits (Table 1). The histopathological report revealed skin tissue comprising the epidermis and dermis. The underlying dermis shows a proliferation of spindle-shaped tumor cells, arranged in fascicles, bundles, and in a storiform pattern. These tumor cells involve the mid-epidermis and dermis and extend into the subcutaneous adipose tissue, giving a honeycomb appearance. These cells show a moderate amount of cytoplasm, mild to moderate anisonucleosis, coarse chromatin, and inconspicuous to visible one or two nucleoli; mitoses (2/90 HPF) are noted in mitotically active areas (Fig. 2). The overlying epidermis is lined by keratinized stratified squamous epithelium and is of unremarkable histology. Accordingly, wide excision with a vascular flap and a split-thickness skin graft was planned and performed. Treatment

Wide local excision with a pedicled radial forearm flap and split-thickness skin graft was performed under regional anesthesia. The patient was positioned supine, with the left arm extended on an arm board. The left upper limb and left thigh were painted and draped. A pre-operative Allen’s test confirmed adequate ulnar collateral circulation to the hand.

Skin markings were made 3 cm from the lesion margins on all sides (Fig. 3). Skin, subcutaneous tissue, and the underlying fascia deep to the lesion were incised and excised en bloc, and the specimen was sent for histopathological examination. The median cubital vein and the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve were identified and preserved under direct vision during the excision. A flap of approximately 7 × 7 cm was marked on the volar aspect of the left distal forearm and wrist. Skin and subcutaneous tissue were dissected carefully. The distal radial artery and its venae comitantes were ligated and divided, and the flap was elevated on a proximally based radial artery pedicle. The superficial branch of the radial nerve was identified and preserved throughout the dissection. All side branches of the radial artery and its venae comitantes were sealed with cautery. The vascular pedicle was kept intact during flap elevation and transfer, and the flap was rotated proximally into the elbow defect and sutured in place (Fig. 3). A split-thickness skin graft was harvested from the left anterior thigh with a Humby knife and applied to the forearm donor site (Fig. 3). Sterile dressings were applied.

Histopathology of the specimen

The dermis shows a well-circumscribed area with proliferation of spindle-shaped tumor cells arranged in fascicles, bundles, and a storiform pattern. These tumor cells are seen involving the mid-dermis, with focal areas showing extension above and pushing the epidermis, and a few extending into subcutaneous adipose tissue, giving a honeycomb appearance. The tumor cells show a moderate amount of cytoplasm with mild to moderate anisonucleosis, coarse chromatin, and inconspicuous to visible one or two nucleoli. A few mitoses (3/10HPF) are noted in mitotically active areas. All peripheral and deep resection margins, including the deep fascial margin, were free of tumor (R0 resection) (Fig. 2A,B).

The patient was followed up for 6 months. The flap survived completely, and the split-thickness graft at the donor site healed without complication. At the latest review, elbow range of motion was 0–120° of flexion–extension with full pronation and supination, grip strength was symmetrical with the contralateral side, and sensation over the dorsoradial hand was intact. There was no clinical evidence of local recurrence. Surveillance with six-monthly clinical examinations is planned, in keeping with current guideline recommendations.

Discussion

DFSP is a rare, malignant subcutaneous tumor with an incidence of 0.8–5 cases per million population per year[4]. The highest incidence rate is reported in Black populations, with a male-to-female ratio of 1:1 and a 5-year survival rate of 99%[5]. Although congenital and pediatric cases have been reported, the mean age for occurrence is usually between 20 and 59 years. There is no particular sex predilection. The different forms of DFSP are classic, sclerotic, giant cell, Bednar, myxoid, atrophic, and fibrosarcomatous. A translocation involving chromosomes 17 and 22 that results in the fusion of the genes for collagen type I alpha 1α1(COL1A1) and platelet-derived growth factor subunit B (PDGFB) is linked to DFSP carcinogenesis[6]. The PDGF receptor beta (PDGFRB) tyrosine kinase is continuously activated by this fusion protein, which drives the proliferation of DFSP cells[6,7]. The clinical manifestation of DFSP can range from large, irregular masses to plaque-like or tiny nodular lesions. It is typically a solid, multinodular tumor that grows slowly. Through the skin septa and fat lobules, the tumor cells penetrate the subcutaneous tissue as tentacle-like or villous projections that encircle the skin appendages[8,9].

Although DFSP is well-documented, only a few cases have been reported. Table 2 summarizes the cases in comparison to ours. According to Allen et al, DFSP typically manifests as a painless, slowly growing cutaneous lesion that may start off as a violet or pink plaque[10,11]. Patients frequently describe a history of a lesion present for years, increasing slowly or appearing stable in size. DFSP most frequently arises in the dermis and subcutaneous tissue of the trunk (50–60% of cases), followed by the proximal extremities (approximately 25%), while the head and neck are involved in only 10–15%. We report a case of an occasionally painful lesion at the uncommon site of the left lateral aspect of the elbow, present for 8 months. Histopathologically, DFSP is characterized by spindle-shaped cells arranged in a storiform or fascicular pattern, often infiltrating subcutaneous fat in a honeycomb appearance. A case report by Madden et al described a 39-year-old man with a 3.5-year history of a mass on the right great toe; histopathological examination after excision showed elongated monomorphic spindle cells in a storiform arrangement with honeycomb-like infiltration of the adjacent adipose tissue, findings that parallel those in our patient[12]. DFSP originates in the dermis and tends to infiltrate underlying structures, including muscles, tendons, fascia, and bone[19]. In our patient, the tumor was confined to the mid and deep dermis with extension into the subcutaneous adipose tissue, without involvement of deeper structures. Therefore, an incisional or excisional biopsy, including subcutaneous fat, is preferred. In 10–15% of cases, sarcomatous transformation occurs, typically leading to a high-grade fibrosarcoma, although 90% of DFSP cases are low-grade [19].

Paramythiotis et al reported a 35-year-old man with a large mass on the upper back that was excised locally under a preoperative diagnosis of lipoma. When histopathology established DFSP, he underwent wide local excision with skin flap reconstruction and was referred for adjuvant radiotherapy, with no recurrence at 20 months. Our patient was likewise managed by wide local excision with flap reconstruction, and adjuvant radiotherapy was not required, as complete excision with clear margins was achieved at the initial operation[5].

Though Mohs micrographic surgery is considered the treatment of choice for DFSP, wide local excision with a vascular flap and split-thickness skin graft of the left elbow was carried out. Twenty-one percent of patients have a recurrence, so strict surveillance is indicated[12,19]. Wide local excision with 3–5 cm margins from the tumor edge, including skin, subcutaneous tissue, and the underlying fascia, is associated with a substantially lower recurrence rate than excision with margins of less than 3 cm[20]. In our patient, 3 cm margins with excision of the deep fascia allowed complete clearance, and the resulting defect was reconstructed with a pedicled radial forearm flap, with the donor site covered by a split-thickness graft from the left anterolateral thigh.

Conclusion

Despite the rarity of DFSP, it should be considered in the differential diagnosis of slowly enlarging cutaneous nodules occurring at unusual anatomical sites such as the elbow. Even in resource-limited settings where immunohistochemistry is unavailable, characteristic histopathological findings combined with clinical correlation can establish the diagnosis. Early recognition, complete surgical excision with adequate margins, and careful long-term surveillance remain essential to minimize local recurrence and preserve function.

Limitations

Although immunohistochemistry (particularly CD34 positivity) is commonly used to support the diagnosis of DFSP, it could not be performed because of limited institutional resources. The diagnosis was established based on characteristic histopathological findings, including a storiform proliferation of spindle cells infiltrating the subcutaneous fat and producing the typical honeycomb pattern, together with the clinical presentation and the postoperative histopathological examination.
