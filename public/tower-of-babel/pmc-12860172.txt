The effect of melanocortin-4 receptor agonist drugs on obesity and metabolic risk factors: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yanan Sun, Mohammad Abed, Mohammad Hassan Sohouli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R) agonists have emerged as potential treatments for obesity, particularly in patients with rare genetic syndromes. However, their overall effects on obesity and cardiometabolic risk factors remain uncertain. To systematically evaluate the efficacy of MC4R agonists on weight-related outcomes and cardiometabolic risk factors.MethodsWe conducted this study following PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Eligible studies included clinical trials ((RCTs and single-arm trials) of the effects of MC4R agonist drugs on anthropometric factors and cardiovascular risk factors. Random model effects meta-analyses were performed for this meta-analysis, with heterogeneity and small-study effects explored through sensitivity and publication bias analyses.ResultsA total of 12 studies were included. Treatment with MC4R agonists significantly reduced body weight compared with placebo in RCTs (WMD − 5.07 kg; 95% CI − 8.13 to − 2.02), with even larger reductions in single-arm studies (–11.23%; 95% CI − 15.43 to − 7.04). MC4R agonists also lowered BMI by − 13.67% (95% CI − 17.21 to − 10.12), waist circumference by − 11.75 cm, BMI Z-score by − 0.98, and hunger scores by − 3.38. These agents reduced triglyceride levels by − 35.53 mg/dL and LDL-C levels by − 9.14 mg/dL, while HDL-C levels showed a nonsignificant increase of + 2.37 mg/dL. Systolic blood pressure declined by − 4.38 mmHg, while diastolic pressure showed no meaningful change.ConclusionsMC4R agonists produce clinically meaningful weight reduction and improvements in several cardiometabolic risk factors. These findings support MC4R agonists as a promising therapy for genetic forms of obesity, while their role in nonspecific obesity requires confirmation in large, long-term randomized trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-02071-2.Keywords: Obesity, Melanocortin-4 receptor, MC4R agonists, Setmelanotide, Weight loss, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic, relapsing, and multifactorial disease linked with substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs globally. The prevalence of obesity has more than tripled over the past four decades, with more than 650 million adults classified as obese in 2016 according to the World Health Organization [1, 2].

Obesity is significantly linked to type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, dyslipidemia, hypertension, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, specific cancers, and reduced quality of life, making it one of the leading preventable causes of morbidity and premature mortality [3–5]. Although there have been improvements in lifestyle and behavioral interventions, weight loss is often modest and difficult to sustain, while bariatric surgery, though effective, is invasive and not universally accessible [4, 6]. These challenges highlight the urgent need for safe, effective, and durable pharmacological treatment strategies.

The central melanocortin pathway is crucial for maintaining energy balance and appetite regulation. Within this pathway, the melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R), a G-protein–coupled receptor expressed in the hypothalamus, is critical for integrating peripheral energy status signals and regulating satiety and energy expenditure [7]. Genetic variants that influence MC4R or its upstream regulators, including pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC), proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 1 (PCSK1), and the leptin receptor (LEPR), are the most common monogenic contributors to severe, early-onset obesity, underscoring the clinical relevance of this pathway [8]. Pharmacological activation of MC4R has therefore emerged as a rational therapeutic strategy, with potential applications in both rare genetic syndromes and more common forms of obesity.

Multiple MC4R agonists, such as setmelanotide and other investigational agents, have been evaluated in both early- and late-phase clinical trials. Initial findings indicate that these drugs can induce clinically meaningful reductions in body weight, improve hunger-related patient-reported outcomes, and favorably influence metabolic parameters. Results have shown variability across different populations, study durations, dosing regimens, and comparator arms. Uncertainty persists concerning the consistency of metabolic effects, especially regarding glucose homeostasis, lipid profiles, and blood pressure regulation [9–11]. Furthermore, it is unclear whether metabolic benefits extend consistently to glucose homeostasis, lipid profile, and blood pressure regulation, or whether efficacy differs between genetically defined obesity and nonspecific obesity populations [12].

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized and non-randomized studies evaluating MC4R agonists. Our objective was to quantify their effects on weight-related and cardiometabolic outcomes, with a priori hypotheses that MC4R agonists would significantly reduce body weight and body mass index (BMI) compared with placebo or other comparators, and that they would provide additional benefits for lipid parameters and blood pressure. By synthesizing the available evidence, we aim to clarify the therapeutic potential of this class of agents and delineate priorities for future research.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [13]. The review protocol was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO or any other database. The review process commenced in January 2025, and all methodological steps, including eligibility criteria, search strategy, and analysis plan, were predefined in accordance with PRISMA 2020. A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase from inception to August 2025, with no restrictions on language or publication date. Gray literature, conference abstracts, and reference lists of relevant reviews and included studies were also manually screened to minimize the risk of missing eligible trials. Search terms combined both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text words and were structured around three main domains: [1] intervention (“Melanocortin-4 receptor agonist” OR “MC4R agonist” OR “Setmelanotide” OR “LY2112688” OR “Bremelanotide” OR “other MC4R agonist agents”); [2] condition (“Obesity” OR “Overweight” OR “Metabolic risk factors” OR “Dyslipidemia” OR “Blood pressure”); and [3] study design (“Randomized controlled trial” OR “Clinical trial” OR “Single-arm trial” OR “Intervention study”)(see Supplementary Table S2). The search strategy was adapted for each database.

The primary outcomes of this review were weight-related measures (body weight, BMI, waist circumference, BMI z-score, and hunger score). Cardiometabolic risk factors, including lipid parameters, fasting glucose, and blood pressure, were considered secondary outcomes.

Exclusion criteria included: non-human studies, review articles, meta-analyses, case reports, studies using MC4R agonists in combination with other active interventions, or studies without extractable quantitative data. When multiple reports from the same trial were available, the one with the longest follow-up and/or most complete dataset was included.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts to determine eligibility. Disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. Data were extracted into a predesigned form and included: first author, year of publication, country, sample size, study design (RCT or single-arm), participant characteristics (age, sex distribution, baseline BMI), intervention characteristics (drug type, dose, and duration), comparator (for RCTs), and outcomes of interest (mean values, standard deviations, or change scores). When outcomes were reported at multiple time points, data from the longest follow-up period were extracted.

Risk of bias assessment

For randomized controlled trials, risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [14], which evaluates domains including sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessors, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other biases. Each domain was rated as low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias. For single-arm clinical trials, the ROBINS-I (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions) [15] tool was applied. Domains included bias due to confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selective reporting. Two reviewers independently performed all assessments, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. Domain-level results of the RoB 2 and ROBINS-I assessments are summarized in Table 2. Most randomized controlled trials were rated as low to moderate risk of bias, primarily due to concerns regarding blinding of participants and personnel. Allocation concealment and sequence generation were generally adequate. Among single-arm studies, the main sources of potential bias were confounding and lack of comparator groups, resulting in moderate overall ratings. Sensitivity analyses excluding high- or serious-risk studies yielded similar pooled estimates for weight, BMI, and lipid outcomes, indicating that the overall findings were robust to study quality variations.

The overall certainty of the evidence across outcomes was graded using the GRADE scoring system, a validated tool that evaluates study limitations, precision, heterogeneity, directness, publication bias, funding bias, and study design [16]. A GRADE summary of findings table for the main outcomes is provided as Supplementary Table S1.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Data presented as medians, ranges, or standard errors were converted to means and standard deviations using standard formulas [17, 18]. When change-score SDs were not reported, they were imputed based on correlation assumptions between baseline and final measurements. All analyses were performed in STATA version 12.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) using the metaan command with the Hartung–Knapp adjustment for random-effects confidence intervals. Effect sizes were expressed as weighted mean differences (WMDs) representing absolute changes (kg, cm, mg/dL, mmHg) or percent changes (for body weight and BMI in single-arm studies). Percent changes were standardized across studies using the formula (post interventionmean–baseline mean)/baseline mean×100. When change-score SDs were not reported, they were imputed assuming a correlation coefficient (r) of 0.8 between baseline and final measurements, based on prior meta-analyses of weight-loss interventions. Sensitivity analyses with r = 0.5 and r = 0.9 produced similar results. Random-effects models (DerSimonian–Laird method with Hartung–Knapp adjustment) were used to calculate pooled WMDs and 95% CIs. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (p < 0.10 indicating significance) and quantified with the I² statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [19]. To further explore sources of heterogeneity in weight-related outcomes, we conducted meta-regression analyses within a random-effects framework. The dependent variable was the absolute mean difference in percentage change in body weight, and study-level covariates were tested as potential moderators. Specifically, we examined the impact of baseline mean BMI of participants and intervention duration (in weeks) on the magnitude of weight change. Each covariate was entered separately into univariable random-effects meta-regression models using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation method. Between-study variance (τ²) and 95% prediction intervals were calculated under a random-effects model to quantify the degree of true heterogeneity across studies and to estimate the expected range of effects in future similar studies. τ² represents the variance of true effect sizes between studies, while the prediction interval reflects the range in which the effect of a new study is expected to lie, accounting for both within- and between-study variability. Sensitivity analyses were carried out by sequentially omitting individual studies to test the robustness of pooled estimates. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot symmetry, Begg’s test, with p < 0.05 considered indicative of small-study effects [20]. Where asymmetry was suspected, the trim-and-fill method was applied to estimate the potential impact of missing studies.

Results

Figure 1 illustrates the step-by-step process of study selection, including applied exclusion criteria. Initially, a total of 1542 records were identified through comprehensive searches across electronic databases. After eliminating duplicate entries, 1096 unique studies remained. Upon screening titles and abstracts, 1070 records were excluded for failing to satisfy the predefined eligibility criteria. Subsequently, 26 full-text articles were reviewed in detail during the secondary screening stage. Of these, 14 studies were excluded due to various reasons, such as lacking appropriate control groups or outcome data. Ultimately, 12 clinical trials met all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final quantitative meta-analysis.

Study characteristics

A total of 13 treatment arms (12 articles) met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis (Table 1). These studies encompassed both RCTs (3 studies) and single-arm (10 treatment arms) clinical trials, reflecting the relatively recent and heterogeneous body of evidence surrounding MC4R agonists. The majority of included studies were multicenter and conducted across North America, Europe, and Australia, highlighting the global interest in these interventions. The populations investigated were diverse in terms of underlying etiology of obesity. Several trials focused on rare monogenic or syndromic forms of obesity, including POMC deficiency, LEPR deficiency, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 1 (PCSK1) deficiency, Bardet–Biedl syndrome (BBS), and Alström syndrome, whereas others recruited patients with hypothalamic obesity or heterozygous genetic variants (e.g., SH2B1, SRC1, or 16p11.2 deletion). Of the 12 included trials, 10 focused on patients with rare monogenic or syndromic obesity (such as POMC, LEPR, PCSK1, Bardet–Biedl, or Alström syndromes), whereas 2 randomized controlled trials investigated individuals with non-genetic, common obesity. Details of genetic versus non-genetic populations are presented in Table 1. Notably, two RCTs enrolled individuals with common obesity, including premenopausal women with BMI > 30, allowing for broader generalizability beyond rare disorders. Participant ages ranged from very young children (as early as 2 years in the Argente 2025 trial) to middle-aged adults, with mean ages between 3.6 and 39.5 years. Baseline BMI was uniformly elevated across studies, ranging from ~ 30 to 50 kg/m², indicating moderate-to-severe obesity. Intervention regimens consistently involved subcutaneous setmelanotide, administered once daily with stepwise dose titration based on age and tolerability, typically up to a maximum of 3 mg/day. A few trials evaluated alternative MC4R agonists such as bremelanotide with more frequent dosing schedules. Treatment duration varied substantially, from as short as 2 weeks to as long as 52 weeks, allowing assessment of both short- and longer-term effects. Among the RCTs, placebo was used as the comparator arm, while single-arm studies lacked a control group but employed rigorous baseline-to-endpoint comparisons. Collectively, Table 1 illustrates the breadth of study designs and patient populations, underscoring that while much of the existing evidence arises from small, open-label trials in rare genetic obesity syndromes, a growing number of RCTs are beginning to evaluate MC4R agonists in more common obesity settings. Sample sizes were generally small, reflecting the rarity of the underlying conditions; five studies included fewer than 15 participants, while the largest trial enrolled 35 individuals.

Risk of bias assessment

As shown in Table 2, randomized trials were appraised using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, while single-arm trials were evaluated with the ROBINS-I instrument. Overall, the RCTs demonstrated low ans some concerns risk of bias, with the primary concerns related to blinding of participants and outcome assessors due to the distinctive effects of the drug (notably appetite suppression and weight reduction). Allocation concealment and random sequence generation were generally adequate across trials. In contrast, the single-arm clinical trials, though methodologically robust in their reporting, were inherently limited by lack of control groups, potential selection bias, and susceptibility to confounding. According to ROBINS-I, most single-arm studies were rated as having “moderate” risk of bias. Importantly, selective outcome reporting was not apparent in either RCTs or non-randomized studies, and missing outcome data were minimal.

Furthermore, the quality of the present meta-analysis was evaluated using the GRADE score system, resulting in a grade of 9 (very good quality).

Meta-analysis

Obesity markers

The pooled analysis using a random-effects model revealed significant reductions in body weight and BMI following treatment with MC4R agonists in single-arm clinical trials. Specifically, mean weight decreased by − 11.23% (95% CI: − 15.43 to − 7.04; P < 0.001; I² = 89.8%), while BMI declined by − 13.67% (95% CI: − 17.21 to − 10.12; P < 0.001; I² = 88.8%). Furthermore, a notable decrease was observed in WC (WMD: − 11.75 cm; 95% CI: − 15.68 to − 7.82; P < 0.001; I² = 43.6%), BMI Z-score (WMD: − 0.98; 95% CI: − 1.36 to − 0.59; P < 0.001; I² = 43.6%), and Most hunger scores (WMD: − 3.38; 95% CI: − 3.99 to − 2.77; P < 0.001; I² = 49.3%) (Fig. 2).

In randomized controlled trials, the intervention group receiving MC4R agonists demonstrated a significant reduction in body weight compared with controls (WMD: − 5.07 kg; 95% CI: − 8.13 to − 2.02; P = 0.001). Considerable heterogeneity was noted across these studies (Cochran’s Q, P < 0.001; I² = 85.6%) (Fig. 3 and Appendix 1).

Forest plots from the meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials investigating the effects Melanocortin-4 Receptor Agonists on weight (kg). WMD: weighted mean

Glucose outcome

The effect of MC4R agonists on FBS was not statistically significant. The pooled estimate indicated a mean difference of 2.08 mg/dL (95% CI: − 7.06 to 11.22; P = 0.655) with no observed heterogeneity among studies (I² = 0.0%) (Fig. 4). However, HbA1c and fasting insulin levels were not consistently reported across studies. Only two trials (Clément 2020 and Haqq 2022) reported HbA1c outcomes, and one study (Kievit 2013) provided insulin data, preventing any pooled analysis of these markers. Therefore, findings related to glycemic control should be interpreted as insufficient evidence rather than definitive null effects.

Lipid profile parameters

Overall, lipid markers showed modest responses to MC4R agonists. HDL-C exhibited a slight but nonsignificant increase (WMD: 2.37 mg/dL; 95% CI: − 1.06 to 5.79; P = 0.176; I² = 43.6%). In contrast, triglycerides and LDL-C were significantly reduced following treatment. TG levels decreased by − 35.53 mg/dL (95% CI: − 67.69 to − 3.37; P = 0.030; I² = 65.9%), while LDL-C dropped by − 9.14 mg/dL (95% CI: − 18.18 to − 0.11; P = 0.047; I² = 48.8%) (Fig. 5).

Blood pressure indicators

Analysis indicated that MC4R agonists led to a significant reduction in SBP (WMD: − 4.38 mmHg; 95% CI: − 8.22 to − 0.55; P = 0.025), with low-to-moderate heterogeneity (I² = 43.8%). Conversely, no significant change was observed for DBP (WMD: − 1.55 mmHg; 95% CI: − 4.04 to 0.94; P = 0.223; I² = 0.0%) (Fig. 6).

Meta-regression

Meta-regression between MC4R agonist drugs and the absolute mean difference in percentage change weight based on the participants’ mean BMI and intervention period were performed, but no significance was found (Coef = −0.1386649, P = 0.247 for duration of intervention and Coef = 0.2308848, P = 0.445 for participants’ mean BMI; see Supplemental Figs. 1).

Sensitivity analysis

Robustness of the pooled estimates was confirmed through leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, which demonstrated consistent findings regardless of individual study removal. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted excluding studies judged as “high” or “serious” risk of bias (based on Table 1). The results remained consistent with the primary pooled estimates, suggesting that study quality did not substantially influence the direction or magnitude of effects (Supplemental Figs. 2–5).

Publication bias

Visual inspection of funnel plots and Begg’s tests did not indicate evidence of publication bias across the assessed outcomes. The P-values for Begg’s test were as follows: weight (single-arm studies: P = 0.788; RCTs: P = 0.174), BMI (P = 0.835), WC (P = 0.573), BMI Z-score (P = 0.052), hunger score (P = 0.293), LDL-C (P = 0.327), HDL-C (P = 0.051), TG (P = 0.624), FBS (P = 0.174), SBP (P = 0.174), and DBP (P = 0.174). The trim-and-fill method also detected no missing studies, further validating the absence of publication bias.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, incorporating data from 12 clinical trials, robustly demonstrates that MC4R agonists significantly reduce body weight and BMI in individuals with obesity. The pooled estimate from RCTs indicated a clinically significant average weight reduction of − 5.07 kg compared with placebo, an effect that was even more pronounced in single-arm studies, which reported an average weight loss of − 11.2%. These effects were associated with reductions in waist circumference, BMI Z-score, and hunger scores, indicating consistent enhancements in both anthropometric and patient-reported outcomes. Among cardiometabolic risk factors, our analysis identified favorable and significant reductions in SBP, TG, and LDL-C following treatment with MC4R agonists. Conversely, changes in HDL-C were small and nonsignificant, and FBS levels did not improve. A notable observation is the absence of glycemic improvement, which suggests that the metabolic benefits of MC4R agonism may selectively target lipid and blood pressure regulation rather than glycemic control. The absence of consistent reporting on essential glycemic markers such as HbA1c and insulin across the included trials, as noted in our limitations, prevents a definitive conclusion on this matter and highlights an important area for future investigation. No evidence of publication bias was found, and the results remained consistent across sensitivity analyses.

Our findings are consistent with previous reports on the efficacy of setmelanotide in rare genetic obesity syndromes [21], but extend the evidence base by demonstrating consistent weight and metabolic benefits across broader populations. It should be noted that most included studies enrolled participants with genetic forms of obesity, and only a limited number evaluated individuals with common obesity. Consequently, the assessment of cardiometabolic risk factors primarily reflects genetically defined obesity, and comparisons between genetic and non-genetic populations were not feasible. We observed substantial reductions in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, and hunger scores with MC4R agonist treatment, supported by a recent meta-analysis of 376 patients treated with setmelanotide across various trials reported an average weight loss of − 6.9% from baseline and a mean BMI reduction exceeding 10 kg/m² in adults [9]. Real-world long-term extension data demonstrated sustained weight loss of − 22% to − 25% over three years; corresponding to sustained reductions of over 10 kg/m² in BMI for patients with POMC or LEPR deficiency [22]. Additionally, pivotal phase III trials consistently showed at least 10% weight loss in the majority of participants, accompanied by meaningful hunger reduction.

Compared to contemporary anti-obesity agents in general obesity, the observed weight loss magnitude is somewhat lower than that achieved with GLP-1 receptor agonists or dual incretin therapies [23–26], yet remains clinically significant, especially for patients with genetically defined obesity who may have limited treatment options [27]. These differences in outcomes are likely explained by variations in the study populations, where MC4R agonist trials primarily enrolled individuals with rare genetic forms of severe obesity, whereas GLP-1 receptor agonists or dual incretin trials focused on common obesity; the treatment durations, which were typically shorter in MC4R agonist studies; and the use of co-interventions, such as structured diet and exercise programs, which were more common in incretin-based studies and less standardized in MC4R agonist trials. Collectively, these findings highlight that the anthropometric and satiety improvements achieved with MC4R agonists are consistent, significant, and sustained over time.

MC4R agonists were associated with reductions in TG and LDL-C, as seen in pooled meta-analyses, individual trials and observational cohorts [9, 28]. hese findings are clinically relevant, as elevated triglycerides and LDL-C represent key components of obesity-related dyslipidemia and are strongly linked to increased cardiometabolic risk. The consistency of these improvements across different study designs supports a robust lipid-lowering effect that may extend beyond weight reduction alone, potentially reflecting direct pharmacologic actions of MC4R pathway activation on lipid metabolism. However, HDL-C changes were small or even decreased in some reports, contrasting with our nonsignificant increase; this likely reflects differences in baseline dyslipidemia, weight-loss magnitude, follow-up duration, and lipid-calculation methods at high TG levels [28–30]. Moreover, HDL-C is known to respond variably to weight loss interventions, and in some contexts reductions in HDL-C may represent transient shifts during negative energy balance rather than true deterioration in lipid profile. Therefore, while the consistent lowering of triglycerides and LDL-C highlights a favorable metabolic signature of MC4R agonists, the heterogeneity and modest nature of HDL-C changes suggest that their impact on lipid subfractions and overall cardiovascular risk requires further dedicated study in longer-term trials.

MC4R agonists were associated with a modest reduction in SBP (–4.38 mmHg), whereas the change in DBP was not significant (–1.55 mmHg; P = 0.223), consistent with pooled estimates and with 52-week clinical data in Bardet–Biedl syndrome [28]. In contrast, mechanistic studies indicate that MC4R activation can increase blood pressure via sympathetic stimulation (including TRPV1-dependent pathways), and regulators advise routine BP monitoring [31–33]. Differences likely reflect the net balance of weight-loss–mediated BP lowering versus direct sympatho-excitatory effects, alongside variation in baseline hypertension, antihypertensive use, and study duration.

MC4R agonists showed no significant change in fasting glucose, and the evidence regarding other glycemic markers remains limited. HbA1c and insulin outcomes were inconsistently reported, with only a few trials providing data, which precluded quantitative synthesis. Therefore, the apparent absence of glycemic improvement should not be overinterpreted, as current evidence is insufficient to draw firm conclusions about the effects of MC4R agonists on glucose metabolism. Future studies with standardized assessment of HbA1c, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR are needed to clarify these effects. MC4R agonists showed no significant change in fasting glucose in our synthesis, consistent with hypothalamic-obesity data reporting no adverse glycemic shifts over 16 weeks and with phase-3 reports where HbA1c generally did not change [9, 21]. In contrast, fasting glucose decreased significantly in POMC deficiency within the same phase-3 program, and preclinical work in obese rhesus macaques showed improved insulin resistance, indicating potential glycemic benefit in select contexts [21, 34, 35]. These differences likely reflect baseline hyperglycemia and diabetes prevalence (greater in POMC cohorts), small samples/short follow-up, and the central, non-incretin mechanism of MC4R agonism, which limits direct islet effects compared with incretin therapies [36].

The notable decreases in triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, and systolic blood pressure address essential modifiable risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, indicating that the advantages of this drug class may extend beyond simple weight loss to provide a direct beneficial effect on long-term cardiovascular health.

The therapeutic effects of MC4R agonists are based on their influence on central hypothalamic pathways. As agonists of the MC4R, a G-protein–coupled receptor, these agents regulate satiety and energy expenditure. Weight loss achieved via appetite suppression aligns with the documented enhancements in hunger assessments. The observed reductions in blood pressure may indicate a decrease in sympathetic tone and an enhancement of endothelial function [32, 34, 37]. The limited effects on glucose may indicate that MC4R activation alone does not significantly affect insulin sensitivity, unlike incretin-based therapies.

The meta-regression analysis indicated that neither baseline mean BMI nor intervention duration significantly moderated the weight-reducing effects of MC4R agonists. The estimated slopes suggested only minimal, non-significant trends, underscoring that the observed reductions in body weight were largely independent of these factors. This finding suggests that the efficacy of MC4R agonists in promoting weight loss is consistent across different baseline levels of adiposity and irrespective of treatment duration within the studied range. The lack of moderation also implies that other factors, such as genetic background, type of MC4R agonist administered, comorbid metabolic conditions, or differences in study design, may contribute to the heterogeneity observed in pooled analyses. From a clinical perspective, these results reinforce the potential applicability of MC4R agonists across diverse patient populations, without strong restrictions based on initial BMI or treatment duration.

Regarding safety, no serious or life-threatening adverse events were reported across the included trials. The most frequently observed treatment-related adverse events were mild to moderate and generally transient, including injection-site reactions, skin hyperpigmentation, nausea, fatigue, and headache. A few participants discontinued treatment due to gastrointestinal discomfort or local skin irritation, but these events resolved after drug withdrawal or dose adjustment. Importantly, no dose-limiting toxicities or cardiovascular complications were identified in any trial. Overall, the safety profile of MC4R agonists appears acceptable, although the limited and inconsistent reporting of adverse events warrants cautious interpretation and highlights the need for standardized safety reporting in future studies.

Ethical considerations are particularly relevant given that one included trial enrolled children as young as two years. This study was conducted under institutional ethical approval with informed parental consent and close clinical supervision. Nevertheless, inclusion of very young participants highlights the need for careful safety monitoring, age-appropriate dosing, and long-term follow-up to assess developmental outcomes. The participation of early-age children also limits the generalizability of these results to older pediatric or adult populations. Importantly, this review does not advocate or imply off-label use of MC4R agonists beyond currently approved genetic indications; rather, it underscores the necessity for well-designed, ethically supervised trials in pediatric cohorts.

Implications for practice and research

MC4R agonists represent a promising class of obesity therapeutics, with clinically meaningful weight loss and improvements in certain metabolic risk factors. These agents may be most effective for patients with genetic obesity syndromes, where alternative therapies are limited, but could extend to broader obesity populations pending long-term outcome data. Future research should prioritize: Head-to-head comparisons with GLP-1 and dual-agonist therapies, Trials powered for cardiometabolic endpoints beyond weight loss, Detailed characterization of safety, including rare adverse events and durability of effect, Subgroup analyses by genetic profile to clarify differential efficacy. Future head-to-head trials comparing MC4R agonists with GLP-1 or dual/triple incretin agonists should primarily focus on patients with genetic or syndromic obesity, where evidence for incretin-based therapies remains scarce. In contrast, for individuals with common obesity, GLP-1 and triple agonists already demonstrate substantial weight reduction of 20–25%, and direct comparisons may have limited clinical relevance.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this review include a comprehensive search strategy, rigorous risk-of-bias assessment, prespecified analytic plan with Hartung–Knapp random-effects modeling, and sensitivity checks—the inclusion of both randomized controlled trials and single-arm data allowed for triangulation of findings. It is important to acknowledge the study’s limitations. These null findings should be interpreted with caution because the analysis pooled only 12 studies with heterogeneous designs and populations. Heterogeneity across trials was significant, likely due to variations in populations (genetic versus general obesity), drug type, dosing, and duration. Secondly, safety outcomes were not consistently reported, precluding robust pooled analyses of adverse events. Third, there was a deficiency in data regarding long-term cardiometabolic outcomes, including the incidence of diabetes and cardiovascular events. Another limitation is that formal computation of prediction intervals was not feasible due to the limited number of included studies and incomplete reporting of variance estimates. However, τ² values from random-effects modeling were reported to provide a general sense of between-study variability. Data on inflammatory biomarkers (such as CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α) and HbA1c were scarce and inconsistently reported among the included studies, precluding quantitative synthesis. Future clinical trials should systematically assess these markers to better characterize the metabolic and inflammatory effects of MC4R agonists. Finally, the absence of consistent HbA1c and insulin data limits conclusions about glucose metabolism.

Conclusion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R) agonists are associated with meaningful reductions in body weight and improvements in several cardiometabolic risk factors, particularly among individuals with genetic forms of obesity such as POMC, LEPR, or BBS syndromes. However, the certainty of evidence for these outcomes ranges from moderate to low, and data on common, nonsyndromic obesity remain limited. Current findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Robust evidence from large, long-term randomized controlled trials, specifically designed and powered to evaluate cardiovascular outcomes, durability of response, and long-term safety, is needed before broader clinical recommendations can be made. MC4R agonists remain a promising but still emerging therapeutic approach within precision obesity medicine.
