Evolution of the Performance of the Malaria Control Program in Burkina Faso: Analysis from 2020 to 2024
Authors: Wendemi Daniel Ouedraogo, Ahmed Kabore, Sidzabda Kompaore, Serge Somda, Ali Sie, Hamado Sawadogo, Ziemlé Clément Meda, Maxime Drabo, Nicolas Meda, Hervé Hien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMalaria continues to pose a major public health challenge in Burkina Faso, negatively affecting its population. Despite notable progress, eliminating this disease remains a daunting task. This study assessed the effectiveness of the National Malaria Control Program (NMCP) between 2020 and 2024.MethodsAn ecological methodology was applied using secondary time-series data from the NMCP. National data spanning from 2020 to 2024 were scrutinised to evaluate shifts in coverage, availability, and use of interventions, alongside malaria incidence. A forecast of the impact up to 2030 was performed using Error, Trend, and Seasonality (ETS) modelling. The analysis, based on the theory of change, explored the connection between the execution of interventions and their epidemiological effects.ResultsIn 2024, the use of long-lasting insecticide-treated nets (LLINs) was recorded at 81.02% among pregnant women and 68.2% among children aged < 5 years. Seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) coverage was 97.8%. The adoption rates of rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) and artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACTs) were 98.5% and 97.96%, respectively. These measures collectively led to a reduction in malaria cases from 537.5 to 446.4 per 1,000 people annually. However, supply chain disruptions and inefficient resource use continue to hinder their overall success. Forecasts indicate that by 2030, the incidence could rise to 473.06 cases per 1,000 individuals (confidence interval [CI]: 242.81–703.28).ConclusionThis study assessed the effectiveness of malaria control efforts in Burkina Faso. The results revealed issues concerning the accessibility and use of these interventions by the target population. To eliminate malaria, it is essential to sustainably improve resources, enhance governance, and develop new tailored strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00593-0.Keywords: Performance, Malaria, Interventions, Programme, Burkina Faso

Introduction

Despite considerable progress in the fight against malaria over the past few decades, eliminating the disease remains a daunting task in several African nations, such as Burkina Faso [1, 2]. Malaria continues to pose a significant public health threat, particularly affecting pregnant women and children under five years of age [1, 2].

Burkina Faso is one of the countries most severely affected by malaria. In 2024, the nation recorded 10,805,020 malaria cases, with 563,383 deemed severe and 3,523 resulting in fatalities [3–5]. This translates to an incidence rate of 446.4 cases per 1,000 people [3, 4]. The most vulnerable group is children under five years of age, who make up approximately 63% of all deaths [3, 6]. While the World Health Organization recognised countries such as Azerbaijan, Tajikistan, and Belize for eradicating malaria in 2023 [7], Burkina Faso continues to struggle with significant malaria transmission [4, 8].

Burkina Faso has been steadily improving its malaria control efforts through the National Malaria Control Program (NMCP), which evolved into the Permanent Secretariat for Malaria Elimination (SP/Palu) in 2022 [6, 9]. Key strategies involve the distribution of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs), indoor residual spraying (IRS), seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC), intermittent preventive treatment (IPT), and the use of artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACT) [10–14]. Recently, the RTS, S/AS01 vaccine was rolled out in selected pilot regions with high transmission rates [15]. Despite these measures, high levels of intervention coverage have been accompanied by stagnation in incidence rates [16]. This raises concerns about the effectiveness of the program and the possible gaps between the program’s efforts and the expected health outcomes [16, 17].

To date, most national evaluations have largely employed a descriptive approach, focusing on coverage indicators, usage, or the presence of interventions, without thoroughly examining how program inputs lead to health outcomes [18, 19]. An innovative strategy might involve combining longitudinal time-series analysis with the theory of change to assess whether current trends are consistent with elimination goals [19, 20].

This study evaluated the effectiveness of major malaria control strategies from 2020 to 2024 by analysing the relationship between coverage, availability, and utilisation indicators of these strategies and trends in malaria incidence and mortality. This evaluation utilised routine data from the Health Information Routing System (Endos-BF) in the province [21, 22]. The analysis is organised around a theory of change framework and is further enhanced by predictive modelling, which employs quarterly data from 2018 to 2024 to evaluate the programme ‘s advancement toward the 2030 elimination goals [21, 22]. The chosen timeframe aligns with the period when all study indicators are consistently available in the Endos-BF, as some availability and usage indicators were only recently added to this system [6, 21, 23].

Methods

Framework of the Study

In Burkina Faso, the system for monitoring malaria consists of multiple interconnected elements, including routine epidemiological, sentinel, entomological, treatment, parasite resistance, and community-based surveillance systems. Data collected from these monitoring activities were relayed through the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2) platform, which is a component of Burkina Faso’s Health Data Warehouse (ENDoS-BF). This data was organised following a three-level hierarchical structure.

Type of Study

An ecological study was conducted to assess the success of a health program by analysing time-series data. This research made use of secondary data gathered from 2020 to 2024. Data collection is scheduled to occur between June and August 2025.

Data Source

The main sources of data for this study were the NMCP databases, now known as SP/Palu, spanning 2020 to 2024. In addition, information was gathered from the ENDoS-BF for the same period, the 2021 Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) of Burkina Faso, and various databases from mixed multi-indicator surveys focused on malaria prevention.

Data Collection

Data collection adhered to the indicator classification outlined in Burkina Faso’s National Strategic Plan (NSP) for Malaria Control. The focus was on impact indicators, such as effectiveness, incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates. Additionally, attention was given to impact or outcome indicators, particularly coverage, as well as program management indicators, which encompass the availability and use of inputs. Quarterly data on malaria incidence from 2018 to 2024 were also collected to forecast malaria incidence in 2030. The gathered data underwent verification processes, which included checks for completeness, internal consistency, and value plausibility. Inconsistent or missing values were identified through interannual comparisons and temporal trend analyses. The data were then cleaned and transformed to standardise formats, units of measurement, and reference periods. Finally, the variables were organised according to the logic of the theory of change, in alignment with the NSP structure, to ensure that the analysis was consistent with the program’s causal hypotheses.

Analytical Framework of the Study: Theory of Change

The theory of change (ToC) utilised in this study is based on the fundamental assumption that a coordinated implementation of malaria interventions in Burkina Faso, supported by continuous funding, enhanced governance, and active community participation, will result in a lasting decrease in malaria cases, deaths, and severity. This theory was constructed using the following components.

Inputs: total expenditure on healthcare, malaria-related costs, and per capita annual healthcare expenditure.

Immediate products (outputs): availability of LLINs, SMC, IPT, RDT, and ACT in health facilities; coverage of interventions (LLINs, SMC, IPT, RDT, and ACT); and the distance to be covered by the population to reach the health facilities.

Intermediate results (outcomes): Effective use of LLINs, recourse to RDT in case of fever, and appropriate treatment by ACT.

Analysis Methods

An analysis was performed using Excel 2024, with an emphasis on metrics such as coverage, availability of resources in healthcare facilities, use of interventions, and incidence, mortality, and in-hospital case fatality rates from 2020 to 2024.

Second, to investigate the relationship between malaria incidence and indicators of coverage, utilisation, and availability, Spearman’s correlations for the two variables were calculated using Stata 17. This nonparametric method was chosen because of the small number of observations in this study (n = 5). The choice is justified by the fact that the multivariate regression model produced unstable estimates that were difficult to interpret. From 2020 to 2024, all indicators were available in a standardised format in the ENDos-BF. However, coefficients obtained from a sample size of n = 5 may be mathematically extreme and not necessarily indicative of a strong epidemiological connection; therefore, they should be interpreted with caution.

Additionally, an error, trend, and seasonality (ETS) forecasting model was employed to examine quarterly malaria incidence data from 2018 to 2024, comprising 28 observations, using R 4.5.3. This model was selected after being compared with the ARIMA and TBATS models, using the Akaike information criterion (AICc) and the confidence intervals of the forecasts as evaluation criteria. The data were chosen to improve the stability of seasonal estimates and reduce the likelihood of bias in the model fit [24].

The best model specification was chosen based on the AIC criterion and confirmed through residual analysis, which included the Ljung-Box test, as well as assessments of normality and variance homogeneity (Annex B). This validated model allowed for projections of incidence rates up to the year 2030, with prediction intervals of 80% and 95% [25, 26]. These projections are conditional extrapolations of recent trends and should not be interpreted as deterministic forecasts.

Ethical and Regulatory Considerations

The research data for this study were obtained from the NMCP in Burkina Faso. Permission to access these data was secured following an official administrative request to the SP/Palu. This study focuses exclusively on analyzing secondary data and does not involve the collection of primary data, thus eliminating the need for approval from Burkina Faso’s Health Research Ethics Committee (CERS). The researchers ensured that the data were handled with the necessary confidentiality and security protocols.

Results

Description of the Evolution of the Indicators through the Theory of Change

Between 2020 and 2023, the Ministry of Health of Burkina Faso invested a total of 6,052 million USD in health spending, including 950 million USD for malaria control funding. The ratio of current health expenditure to gross domestic product (GDP) fluctuated between 8% and 8.5% during this period. Per capita health spending increased from 55.8 to 75.5 million USD, representing a 35.30% increase. This financial support contributed to immediate improvements in the product. The availability of LLINs in health facilities increased from 44.5% in 2021 to 62.1% in 2024, with a median stock of 198 LLINs [IQR: 102–354] per health facility in 2024. Similarly, the availability of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) in health facilities increased from 43.2% in 2021 to 61.1% in 2024, with a median stock of 373 SP [IQR: 163–794] per health facility in 2024. Regarding RDTs, their availability in health facilities increased from 61% in 2021 to 70.1% in 2024, with a median stock of 2,138 RDTs [IQR: 1,136–3,683] per health facility in 2024. As for treatments, the availability of artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACTs) increased from 64.7% in 2021 to 69.8% in 2024, while that of artesunate decreased from 54.5% in 2021 to 44% in 2024, with a median stock of 110 doses of artesunate [IQR: 0–310] per health facility by 2024.

LLIN coverage among pregnant women increased from 69.49% in 2020 to 81.02% in 2024. Among children aged 0–11 months, coverage increased from 21.3% in 2020 to 68.2% in 2024. Furthermore, SMC coverage among children under 5 years of age increased from 94.35% in 2021 to 97.8% by 2024. Finally, IPT coverage experienced a notable increase from 55.85% in 2020 to 75.27% in 2024.

These outcomes have contributed to the increasing use of parasitological diagnoses (RDT or microscopy), which has been virtually universal since 2021, exceeding 98% of all annual diagnoses. The proportion of treatments administered via ACT increased from 94.76% in 2020 to 97.96% by 2024. Regarding the use of LLINs, according to data from the 2021 EDS, 61% of the population slept under an LLIN; namely, 67% of children under 5 years old and 71% of pregnant women [27]. In terms of impact, the incidence of malaria fell from 537.5 cases per 1,000 inhabitants in 2021 to 446.4 cases per 1,000 inhabitants in 2024, representing a 16.95% reduction in the incidence. The number of malaria-related deaths decreased from 4355 to 3523 between 2021 and 2024, corresponding to a 19.10% decrease, whereas the malaria case fatality rate fell from 0.8 to 0.6 between 2020 and 2024. Furthermore, the mortality rate decreased from 20.3 to 14.9 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants between 2021 and 2024 (Table 1).

Analysis of the Relationship Between Incidence, Coverage, Availability, and use, as well as the Distance Travelled to reach Health Centres

The results of this analysis suggested a negative correlation between malaria incidence and LLIN coverage among children aged 0–11 months (r = − 0.7), as well as SMC (r = − 0.5) and IPT (r = − 0.7) coverage. However, a positive correlation was observed between the incidence of malaria and LLIN coverage among pregnant women (r = 0.7).

Regarding usage, the results suggested a negative correlation between incidence and the combined use of RDTs and ACTs (r = − 0.7). In contrast, the use of RDTs alone was positively associated with incidence (ρ = 0.6).

In terms of input availability, the results suggested a negative correlation between malaria incidence and the availability of LLINs (r = − 1.0), RDTs (r = − 0.9), and ACTs (r = − 0.3) in health facilities (Table 2).

Discussion

This study evaluated the success of malaria control strategies in Burkina Faso by applying NSP indicators and using the ToC as an analytical framework. This approach explored the links between malaria incidence, the extent of intervention coverage, resource availability, proximity to healthcare services, and the use of malaria interventions. The analysis was conducted within a short time-series framework, considering the nonlinear relationships among the variables.

The Evolution of Indicators

Funding allocated for health and malaria control in Burkina Faso improved significantly between 2020 and 2024 [16]. These funds have contributed to the establishment and equipping of health structures, recruitment and deployment of health personnel within these structures, supply of essential drugs, and capacity building of health workers for better management of health issues [16, 28, 29]. Despite this improvement, the ratio of current health expenditure to GDP remains below the recommendations of the World Health Organization (WHO), which recommends an annual increase of at least 1% in GDP to have a significant impact on the health system [16, 30, 31]. Moreover, these financial efforts have reduced the distance travelled by the population to access health facilities in the region. However, the 5 km goal set by the National Health Development Plan (NHDP) 2021–2030 has not yet been achieved [4, 32].

The analyses also revealed a contrasting situation characterised by high coverage, notably of LLINs, IPT, and SMC, as well as a high use of RDT and ACT [14, 33]. Despite these performances, the availability of inputs in health facilities remains limited, and the incidence of the disease remains high. This observation aligns with the statements of Cissoko et al. [34], who emphasised that high coverage alone is not sufficient to significantly lower the incidence when other indicators do not work optimally [34]. This dissociation between the different levels of the intervention chain highlights a structural fragility in the supply chain, as reported by Cohen et al. [35] and Obeagu et al. [36].

Analysis of the Relationship Between the Incidence, Coverage, Availability, and Uptake of Malaria Control Interventions

Spearman’s correlation analysis indicated a varied relationship between vaccine coverage and disease incidence. There was an insignificant positive correlation between malaria incidence and LLIN coverage among pregnant women. This could be attributed to the expansion of preventive measures in areas most vulnerable to malaria [37]. Consequently, regions with high malaria incidence might also exhibit increased LLIN coverage rates. Additionally, the high incidence of malaria in pregnant women could lead to more antenatal visits [37, 38]. As a result, this situation might automatically boost the LLIN program’s coverage without necessarily indicating a real improvement in protection for the wider population [37–39]. In contrast, the significant negative correlation observed in the 0–11 months age group for certain factors suggests that coverage, when combined with proper and consistent use, can reduce disease transmission. This finding is consistent with the conclusions of Killeen et al. [40], who argued that coverage alone is insufficient to assess the effectiveness of malaria control interventions [38–40].

The observed lack of a significant positive correlation between the use of RDTs and malaria incidence underscores the need for enhanced screening in regions and during periods of high transmission. This pattern, frequently noted in the literature, indicates improved case detection rather than an actual increase in transmission rates [41, 42]. This finding aligns with the research conducted by Hofer et al. [43] and Bastiaens et al. [44]. Conversely, the nonsignificant negative relationship between ACT use and incidence suggests that effective and timely clinical management of confirmed cases can reduce transmission, provided that treatment access and adherence are maintained [45, 46]. Studies by Habtamu et al. [47] and Vanheer et al. [48] have shown that when the conditions for ACT use are met, it can lead to a decrease in malaria transmission and a gradual decline in incidence [47, 48].

The inverse correlation between the availability of inputs in healthcare facilities and their impact highlights the essential role of the supply chain in the success of interventions. Disruptions in stocks can interrupt the continuity of care, potentially undermining public confidence in the healthcare system and leading individuals to self-medicate or use unapproved treatments. This, in turn, can reduce the effectiveness of interventions that are otherwise well-supported and widely implemented [49, 50]. This observation is consistent with the research of Cohen et al. [35] and Cissoko et al. [34], who found a strong association between stock shortages, input availability, increased malaria-related illness, and decreased overall efficacy of control programs [34, 35]. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because of the exploratory nature of the study and the limited number of observations.

Analysis of Projected Malaria Incidence in 2030

The ETS modelling outcomes indicated that malaria incidence in Burkina Faso (473.06 cases per 1, 000 people) is expected to remain significantly above the pre-elimination benchmark of fewer than five cases per 1,000 people. This suggests that the goal of eliminating malaria by 2030 is unlikely to be achieved if the current trend persists without any acceleration. As the time horizon extends, the 80% and 95% prediction intervals progressively expand, highlighting increasing uncertainty due to factors such as annual climate variations, potential changes in intervention coverage, and possible shifts in health policies [51, 52]. However, these projections are conditional extrapolations of recent trends and should not be viewed as deterministic forecasts [51, 53]. This observation, based on the assertions of Bhatt et al. [54] and the World Health Organization’s recommendations, advocates for the continuous strengthening of strategies, with a focus on innovation and resource mobilisation [2, 18].

The Appreciation of the Theory of Change

Examining the theory of change enables the observation of alignment among the resources utilised, activities carried out, and results achieved. The core premise of this theory hinges on the notion that effectively mobilising inputs, such as funding, personnel, and medical supplies, facilitates the execution of activities, such as distribution, diagnosis, treatment, and awareness, in the healthcare sector. These actions can lead to immediate outcomes, such as enhanced coverage and increased utilisation of malaria control measures [55, 56]. These improvements can reduce incidence and mortality rates, gradually aiding in the eradication of the disease. Within this framework, De Savigny et al. [57] perceive healthcare systems as complex adaptive systems in which the interactions among components play a crucial role in shaping performance and outcomes [57].

In Burkina Faso, this theory appears conceptually relevant, as the main interventions generally correspond with international guidelines and the country’s epidemiological situation [16, 23]. Nevertheless, despite these initiatives, malaria incidence remains elevated, and mortality remains a significant issue, highlighting the disparity between strategic goals and actual outcomes. Various factors disrupt the causal chain, such as logistical challenges, regional disparities, and significant dependence on external financial support [16, 58, 59]. Feachem et al. [60] and Walker et al. [61] recommended a coordinated and sustained intensification of interventions, along with a blend of technical measures, institutional strengthening, and community involvement, to advance toward malaria elimination [58, 60, 61]. Therefore, this theory implies that enhancing the health system, ensuring long-term funding, and promoting community participation are crucial for converting current progress into lasting advancements toward eradicating the disease [58, 59].

The fight against malaria in Burkina Faso illustrates undeniable advances in terms of coverage but also highlights the limitations of intervention availability and their uptake. To accelerate progress toward elimination, it is essential to consolidate the gains of coverage-related interventions and strengthen their availability and use. Strengthening coordination among stakeholders to develop new strategies is essential [62, 63]. This observation is in line with the analyses of Fambirai et al. [64] and Montresor et al. [65], who highlighted the importance of coordinated and participatory multisectoral governance and engagement, as well as increased national leadership for successful malaria control programs [7, 15].

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results. The use of secondary data at the national level does not highlight local disparities in malaria transmission. Furthermore, the limited number of years analysed restricts the statistical robustness of the results, making it impossible to establish firm correlations. Finally, indicators of coverage, availability, and utilisation were based on administrative data that may be subject to reporting bias and overestimation, which could explain the weak or inconsistent relationships observed with malaria incidence.

Conclusion

This study assessed the performance of malaria control efforts in Burkina Faso. The results suggest that potential gaps in the chain linking inputs, particularly regarding the availability and effective use of interventions, may be linked to the limitations observed in the program’s overall performance. Eliminating malaria requires not only maintaining high coverage but, above all, the sustainable strengthening of the supply chain, improving the quality of intervention delivery, and more integrated governance. In this regard, the development of new strategies could be useful in identifying solutions that are likely to accelerate progress toward malaria elimination.
