Energy scaling of Kerr-lens modelocked Ho:YAG thin-disk oscillators to the microjoule level
Sergei Tomilov 1,*, Mykyta Redkin 1, Yicheng Wang 1, Anna Suzuki 1, Martin Hoffmann 1 and Clara J. Saraceno 1,2

Abstract
We demonstrate peak power and pulse energy scaling of Kerr-lens modelocking (KLM) Ho : YAG thin-disk lasers (TDLs) emitting at 2.1 µ ​ m \displaystyle 2.1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m} wavelength. We compare different laser configurations to reach a maximum pulse energy of 1.7 µ ​ J \displaystyle 1.7\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro J} at an output power of 29 W \displaystyle 29\text{\,}\mathrm{W} with a pulse duration of 434 fs \displaystyle 434\text{\,}\mathrm{fs} , corresponding to a peak power of 3.7 MW \displaystyle 3.7\text{\,}\mathrm{MW} . This represents a 5-fold increase in pulse energy and 4-fold increase in peak power compared to previous KLM Ho : YAG TDLs , thus reaching record high peak power for 2.1 µ ​ m \displaystyle 2.1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m} modelocked oscillators. We discuss current limitations at this wavelength and guidelines for future work towards higher power and pulse energies.

Such lasers, particularly high-power modelocked oscillators and amplifiers delivering extremely high peak powers and short pulse durations, offer unique advantages for driving the generation of

pulses
[

[

[

[

Even though powerful laser systems operating in this wavelength region have seen tremendous progress in the last two decades, their state-of-the-art specifications in terms of average output power, pulse energy, and peak intensity are still behind in comparison with modern 1-µm systems
[

,

,

[

In fact, the thin-disk geometry imposes not only fabrication constraints to reach high-quality disks (polishing, gluing, etc.) but also operates at high population inversion levels, which in many materials results in detrimental laser operation due to strong quenching. In the thin-disk geometry in

\displaystyle 2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro J}

\displaystyle 1.13\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
due to the low modulation depth of the saturable absorber. Furthermore, the very novel saturable absorber based on GaSb was not optimized for high power modelocked operation nor shorter pulse durations. While ongoing improvements in

In this paper, we demonstrate energy scaling of

2
Experimental results

2.1
2-reflection resonator design

We aim to achieve high average power and pulse energies with a sub-picosecond pulse duration directly from the laser oscillator. This usually requires the usage of high-transmissive
output couplers (OCs)
to operate at moderate intracavity average powers and thus avoid many detrimental thermal effects and nonlinearities
[
26
]
. In comparison with
Yb
:
YAG
, the most common and robust
\displaystyle 1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
active medium,
Ho
:
YAG
requires operation at reduced doping concentration to avoid deleterious optical transitions. This results in reduced gain per disk reflection and limited loss tolerance compared to Yb-based
TDLs

[
24
,
27
]
. To improve the laser efficiency of the resonator containing high-transmissive OC or loss-inducing elements, multiple reflections of the laser mode from the disk are necessary. We start with the easiest configuration possible with two reflections on the disk (i.e. 4 single thickness gain passes) per roundtrip. Fig.
1
illustrates the basic
\displaystyle 29\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
experimental resonator of the KLM TDL.

Figure 1:
Schematic representation of 4-f-extended
KLM
cavity.

It is built around a
Mode-locking telescope (MLT)
, a short Z-shaped cavity with two 200-mm
radius of curvature (RoC)
mirrors and a
Kerr medium (KM)
between them. To incorporate the disk into the design and implement several mode reflections from the disk, we use 4-f extensions
[
28
]
. The 2-at.% doped,
\displaystyle 190\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
thick
Ho
:
YAG
disk with
\displaystyle 2\text{\,}\mathrm{m}

RoC
acts as both mirrors of the 4-f telescope. The dashed line corresponds to the plane where the
MLT
and 4-f extension are connected. Having separate cavity sections responsible for amplification and nonlinear response allows great design modularity for power and energy scaling. The flat end mirror of the
MLT
, 200-mm
RoC
mirror next to it, and the
KM
are mounted on the linear translation stages, allowing precise tuning of the
MLT
and starting of the modelocking. A total amount of -
\displaystyle 24\,500\text{\,}{\mathrm{fs}}^{2}
of
group-delay dispersion (GDD)
was introduced by flat dispersive mirrors. The oscillator was pumped by a commercial Tm-fiber laser with a maximum power of
\displaystyle 209\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
at the wavelength of
\displaystyle 1908\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
, corresponding to the absorption maximum of
Ho
:
YAG
. The thin-disk multi-pass module enables 72 reflections of the pump beam from the disk, ensuring sufficient absorption of the pump radiation.

A water-cooled copper pinhole was introduced into the beam close to the
OC
position, corresponding to the re-imaged plane marked with the dashed line in Fig.
1
. The hard aperture radius was selected such that the introduced losses almost prevent the system from lasing. However, pushing the end mirror deeper into the stability edge (closer to the nearest curved mirror) reduces clipping of the laser mode on the pinhole, eliminating almost all the losses.

As part of our optimization procedure, we tested various undoped sapphire and YAG plates of various thicknesses as the
KM
. The material and thickness choice influences the strength of the Kerr-lens and introduces
self-phase modulation (SPM)
since YAG and sapphire have different
\displaystyle n_{2}
. Thus, the initial search for modelocked operation was performed with a
\displaystyle 4\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
YAG plate and 1-% OC because high nonlinearity and low intracavity losses allowed for an easier start of modelocking. The
KLM
in this system can typically be started with some residual
CW
background, which manifests itself in the form of spikes in the soliton spectrum. The
CW
peaks can be eliminated by increasing the separation of the two curved
MLT
mirrors, pushing the resonator further from the
CW
stability range, and increasing the loss discrimination between
CW
and
KLM
modes. Additionally, the pinhole size change allows for the variation of the modelocking mechanism modulation depth, influencing the dampening of
CW
outbreaks and pulse duration.

KLM
requires to balance intracavity
SPM
and
GDD
, therefore both can be used to optimize for stable modelocking. However, the developed design allows more flexibility for nonlinearity tuning due to the movable
KM
. Power scaling of the laser was performed by gradually minimizing intracavity
SPM
through plate thickness or
\displaystyle n_{2}
reduction and the consequent maximization of intracavity power while having large fixed intracavity
GDD
. As the next step, the
OC
transmissivity was increased to couple a higher fraction of the intracavity power out. The best performance of this system was recorded with a
\displaystyle 3\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
sapphire plate and a 4-%
OC
. Thinner plates did not introduce enough self-focusing strength to start and support modelocking, whereas more transparent
OCs
introduced excessive losses, also preventing modelocking. The system emitted up to
\displaystyle 14\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
of average power with stable modelocked operation, free of
CW
spikes. This average power corresponds to
\displaystyle 350\text{\,}\mathrm{W}
of intracavity power and
\displaystyle 0.48\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro J}
of output pulse energy.

The full characterization data for this laser system is presented in Fig.
2
. The pulse duration measured with an autocorrelator is
\displaystyle 337\text{\,}\mathrm{fs}
and the spectrum
full width at half-maximum (FWHM)

\displaystyle 13.5\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
. The calculated
time-bandwidth product (TBP)
of the pulses is 0.310, slightly below the ideal soliton
TBP
of 0.315, which can be explained by small measurement errors and fitting values. The
radio frequency (RF)
pulse train was detected by a fast photodetector with a
\displaystyle 10\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
bandwidth and rise/fall times of
\displaystyle 28\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
. The Fig.

\displaystyle 900\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
span. The first beatnote is also presented in Fig.

\displaystyle 60\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}

This allows us to conclude the absence of Q-switching and short-term instabilities in the signal. Additionally, we used a sampling oscilloscope, which can capture input signals on receiving a trigger event and reconstruct waveforms with a maximum bandwidth of
\displaystyle 25\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}

Hard-aperture
KLM
offers flexibility in adjusting the modelocking mechanism’s modulation depth and virtual saturation fluence by choosing the diameter of the aperture and thickness or the material of the
KM
. The modulation depth of such a virtual saturable absorber can be controlled by the separation between the
MLT
mirrors or the pinhole diameter, whereas the virtual saturation fluence depends on the reaction of the
KM
to the intensity change. For the oscillators demonstrated in this paper, the virtual saturation fluence was estimated to be between
\displaystyle 10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro J}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}30\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro J}\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{-2}
. These estimations can provide hints for further scaling of the saturation fluence of the
SESAMs
developed for
\displaystyle 2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
laser systems.

The main limitation of the
KLM
method we encountered experimentally is excessive nonlinearities generated by the
KM
, which require significant intracavity GDD to compensate even at low pulse energies. The
\displaystyle 1.4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro J}
3-pass
KLM

TDL
has an intracavity energy of
\displaystyle 20\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro J}
(if we ignore the potential difference in pulse energy throughout the resonator due to leakages and loss/gain sources) and a total nonlinear-phase shift of
\displaystyle 1.06\text{\,}\mathrm{rad}
. This required
\displaystyle-33\,500\text{\,}{\mathrm{fs}}^{2}
of
GDD
for compensation, introduced by 17 flat
\displaystyle-1000\text{\,}{\mathrm{fs}}^{2}
or
\displaystyle-500\text{\,}{\mathrm{fs}}^{2}
dispersive mirrors. Dispersive mirrors add additional losses and thermal distortions to the laser mode
[
32
]
. In our estimations, up to 20 % of the total laser output power leaves the resonator not through the
OC
but leaks through the folding dispersive mirrors or
reflects from an imperfectly placed Brewster plate. This sets additional demands for developing new dielectric-coated highly-dispersive mirrors for high-intracavity-power TDLs and highlights
the importance of exceptional quality dielectric top coatings at this wavelength. Furthermore, other techniques for intracavity dispersion compensation can become relevant as the available
tolerance to loss increases.

In our experiments, the need for larger amounts of GDD explains why, compared to our SESAM modelocking results
[
24
]
, overall modelocked efficiency is lower. However, a full comparison between
KLM
and
SESAM
modelocking is out of scope of this study and requires a separate thorough investigation; nevertheless, we can safely expect further improvements of both
SESAM
and
KLM
modelocked
TDLs
in this wavelength regions when larger modulation depth, high-power
SESAMs
are demonstrated, and improved low-loss dispersive mirror technology is developed.

Considering pump-power and
OC
limitations, the most natural way of progressing is energy scaling by sacrificing repetition rate. This can be done by implementing longer resonators by stacking 4-f extensions or
introducing an intracavity
multi-pass cell (MPC)

[
32
]
and keeping the average power at the same level. The linear scaling of the energy would, however, also require linear scaling of intracavity
GDD
, so it is beneficial only with coating improvements as mentioned above.

We further explore the importance of passively minimizing intracavity losses by oscillator efficiency analysis. Our experiments with resonators featuring 1 to 4 reflections from the disk in combination with rate-equation models allow us to gather a substantial understanding of the loss tolerance of
Ho
:
YAG
as a thin-disk active medium and determine the optimal amount of disk reflections depending on the
OC
transmissivity. We also measured the
CW
laser slope efficiency without a hard aperture and the
MLT
to exclude all possible sources of non-repeatable passive intracavity losses for the 3-reflection and twin-pump-module resonator configurations. This data was used to perform a Caird analysis
[
33
]
to determine the passive intracavity losses. This yielded a passive intracavity loss value between 0.054 and 0.071 for 3-reflection and 0.095 and 0.127 for twin-pump-module resonators. This highlights the exceptionally high levels of passive intracavity losses in these cavities. We use the calculated passive loss values to simulate the laser behavior with our analytical models and compare it with the measured slope efficiencies. The result of this comparison is presented in Fig.
11
. The experimental and theoretical data are in good agreement, considering the imprecision in the results of the Caird analysis, which are mostly caused by variations in the actual OC transmissivity values. This study demonstrates that a twin-pump-module resonator does not improve laser efficiency. On the contrary, due to the almost doubled intracavity passive losses, this design loses around 10 % of the slope efficiency compared to the 3-reflection oscillator. It also does not provide better loss tolerance when using a 10-% OC. As mentioned above, the main, if not the only, reason the twin-pump-module oscillator showed better performance is improved thermal management in the active medium.

\displaystyle 100\text{\,}\mathrm{W}

\displaystyle 10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro J}
