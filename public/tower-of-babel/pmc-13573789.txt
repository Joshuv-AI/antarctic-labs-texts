Humanistic Care Needs of Elderly Chronic Disease Inpatients from the Perspective of Patient Journey Mapping: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Jing Zhao, Yuelin Zhang, Ge Bai, Yancen Wu, Tao Xue, Yanli Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeBased on patient journey maps, this study identified the behavioral challenges and needs for compassionate care among older inpatients with chronic diseases at different stages of diagnosis and treatment, and explored the pain points and preferences in their care experiences, thereby providing targeted insights to optimize compassionate care practices, enhance continuity of care, and improve patients’ quality of life.Patients and MethodsThis descriptive qualitative study was conducted from January to April 2026 at a tertiary Grade A hospital in Hohhot, Inner Mongolia. Semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted with 14 elderly inpatients with chronic diseases (ages 60–98; who had multiple comorbidities, primarily hypertension, coronary heart disease, and diabetes; most lived with their spouses). Semistructured in-depth interviews were conducted. Content analysis was used to systematically analyze the interview data. Using a patient journey map as a framework, themes were categorized along a horizontal timeline (four stages: admission, inspection, treatment, and discharge) and a vertical axis of tasks (three dimensions: behavioral challenges, emotional experiences, and humanistic care needs), and a visual journey map was constructed.ResultsA total of 35 themes were identified, distributed across four stages and three dimensions. At the behavioral level, these included a high treatment burden during the admission stage; low psychological capital and excessive coping during the diagnostic stage; dependence on nursing care and avoidance of information during the treatment stage; and decreased adherence to self-management and reduced social participation during the discharge stage; The emotional dimension encompassed feelings of depression and helplessness, feelings of guilt toward family members, anxiety and worry, and a sense of being respected; the needs dimension involved a safe and supportive environment, age-appropriate information support, psychological buffering, personalized listening, visual medication guidance, and ongoing feedback channels. Ultimately, this resulted in the creation of a humanistic care journey map for elderly inpatients with chronic diseases.ConclusionElderly hospitalized patients with chronic illnesses exhibit distinct humanistic care needs at different stages throughout their hospitalization. The patient journey map developed in this preliminary study helps nurses identify key nursing priorities and provide targeted support tailored to each stage. In particular, the phased approach to information delivery may better prepare healthcare providers while strengthening connections with family members and community resources, facilitating a smoother transition for patients from hospital to home life. Before implementing this pathway routinely, further prospective feasibility assessments are needed.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of accelerating global population aging, chronic diseases among older adults have become a major public health issue threatening their health and quality of life. These conditions are characterized by long-term management needs, recurrent exacerbations, and complex care requirements, not only increasing the risk of adverse health outcomes for elderly patients but also placing a heavy burden on family caregivers and healthcare systems, posing a serious challenge to healthy aging.1,2

Nursing humanistic care represents a human-oriented philosophy rooted in the “life value of the whole person”. High-quality nursing guided by this philosophy is patient-centered, which fully addresses patients’ multifaceted physical, psychological, social and spiritual needs, and aims to deliver systematic, individualized and differentiated nursing services for care recipients in need.3 Kearney and Yeager4 extracted four core themes from the theory of humanistic care to guide clinical practice. First, patients should be regarded as emotional individuals whose life experiences and living contexts are genuinely understood. Second, nurses ought to enter patients’ life worlds from the perspective of comprehending their actual needs. Third, nurses need to identify specific signals of patients’ nursing needs within clinical contexts. Fourth, unique nursing approaches that facilitate patients’ health promotion should be established.

Practical evidence demonstrates that the implementation of nursing humanistic care can significantly improve the physical and mental health status of elderly patients with chronic diseases, enhance their social participation and activities of daily living, satisfy their demands for disease-oriented nursing and compassionate care, and ultimately optimize their quality of life.5 Furthermore, existing research indicates that humanistic care delivered during hospitalization can alleviate transitional anxiety among elderly patients and their caregivers through psychological counseling, enhance patients’ self-management capability and caregivers’ care confidence via health education and medication guidance, and ensure the continuity of rehabilitation follow-up and chronic disease management by coordinating discharge handover with community health service centers, general practitioners and home-based elderly care resources.2,6 Therefore, strengthening systematic humanistic nursing care during hospitalization for elderly patients with chronic diseases is a necessary measure to guarantee continuous full-course care and optimize long-term health outcomes.

However, although China has begun to attach importance to the care needs of older adults, most existing studies are still limited to self-designed questionnaires and qualitative research due to the scarcity of relevant assessment tools. Furthermore, these studies generally only focus on a single stage of the hospitalization process of elderly patients, such as the treatment period, discharge phase or post-discharge management, and rarely pay attention to the dynamic changes in the psychosocial and humanistic care needs of patients throughout the entire transition process from hospitalization to post-discharge.5

To better track the dynamic evolution of holistic nursing humanistic care needs among elderly patients with chronic diseases during the transition from hospitalization to discharge, this study adopted the Patient Journey Map (PJM) methodology. By visually presenting patients’ nursing trajectories, this method systematically captured changes in experience needs and emotional fluctuations throughout the entire nursing process, which facilitated the identification of service gaps and key intervention timings. This methodology demonstrated significant advantages in chronic disease management and medical service improvement.7–9 In constructing the journey framework, this study introduced the Quality Caring Model proposed by Duffy as the theoretical underpinning.10 Rooted in Watson’s Caring Theory, this model integrated eight core caring elements, namely attentive comfort, basic human needs, encouraging attitude, mutual problem-solving, belonging needs, healing environment, human respect, and appreciation for unique meaning. It emphasized that caring behaviors should be centered on “relational centrality”, and took patients’ “sense of being cared for” as the affective endpoint for measuring the effectiveness of caring.11 Based on the internal logic of this model, this study divided the research results into three interactive dimensions: behavior, emotion, and caring needs. The behavioral dimension reflected the implementation and absence of the eight core caring elements in nurse‑patient interactions, that is, the explicit coping behaviors of patients; the emotional dimension corresponded to the generation of “sense of being cared for” and subjective affective experience, which served as direct feedback on the effectiveness of caring behaviors; the caring need dimension revealed the service gaps perceived by patients at each stage, and specifically pointed to the unmet expectations including basic human needs and belonging needs.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study adopted the patient journey mapping as the analytical framework and employed a descriptive qualitative research design to investigate the subtle differences in humanistic care needs among elderly inpatients with chronic diseases across temporal dimensions and contextual levels. This research method was suitable for directly exploring patients’ real experiences and actual needs regarding their own health conditions, avoided excessive theoretical abstraction, and thus presented the perspectives of research participants in intuitive and accessible language. Both the research design and report writing of this study complied with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ), and the complete COREQ checklist was provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Setting and Participants

This study employed purposeful sampling, taking into account factors such as gender, age, living arrangements, education level, payment method, employment status, and types of chronic diseases. The participants were older inpatients with chronic illnesses hospitalized at a tertiary hospital in Hohhot, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, China, between January and April 2026. Interviews were conducted prior to the patients’ discharge.

The inclusion criteria of the research subjects are as follows: (1) Older inpatients (age ≥ 60 years); (2) Subjects diagnosed by physicians or confirmed via medical records to have one or more chronic diseases; (3) Subjects capable of communication and expression, with no cognitive impairment confirmed by clinical assessment; (4) Subjects who provided informed consent to participate in this study. The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) Subjects with communication difficulties caused by severe hearing or visual impairment; (2) Patients with disturbance of consciousness. The dropout criteria are as follows: (1) Subjects who voluntarily withdraw from the study during the interview process; (2) Subjects who are unable to complete the study due to changes in their condition.

The determination of the sample size in this study follows the principle of data saturation: data collection is terminated when no new information describing the research topic emerges. In this study, the data saturation was first observed after the 12th interview; after completing the 13th and 14th interviews, no new themes were identified, and data collection was ultimately terminated. A total of 14 patient interviews were completed in this study.

Recruitment Procedure

The research team first conducted an initial screening of hospitalized elderly patients with chronic diseases through the inpatient care information system to generate a candidate list. Subsequently, the head nurse of the department rechecked the list against the unified inclusion criteria provided by the research team to ensure objectivity. When the patient’s condition was stable and their mood appropriate, the responsible nurse orally introduced the basic content of the study and only inquired whether the patient agreed to be contacted further by the research team. Only after the patient explicitly gave consent could the nurse hand over the patient’s contact information, which was used solely for research-related communication and treated confidentially. Clinical nurses and the head nurse did not participate in the subsequent formal recruitment or informed consent process. Participation was entirely voluntary, and refusal or withdrawal would not affect the patient’s routine medical care. Before formal recruitment, the research team had no therapeutic, managerial, or interest-dependent relationship with any potential participants. Subsequently, the research team conducted face-to-face meetings with patients who agreed to be contacted, explaining in detail the study purpose, procedures, audio recording, confidentiality, and the principle of voluntariness. After the patient fully understood the aforementioned information, a written informed consent form was signed. The content of the informed consent form includes authorization for the disclosure of anonymized responses and direct quotations.

The flowcharts summarizing each step of the recruitment process, eligibility assessment, participant screening, interview implementation, and data analysis are detailed in Supplementary Material 2.

Interview Guide Development

This research team consists of four senior care experts (two geriatric nursing specialists and two nursing management specialists, all holding senior professional titles) and two master’s students in nursing, both of whom have received systematic training in qualitative research. The initial interview outline was drafted by the first author based on the research objectives and literature review. Following the a priori framework of the patient journey map, the question setting was structured around four stages, namely admission, examination, treatment and discharge, and three dimensions including behaviors, emotions and needs. Consensus on the draft outline was reached after multiple discussions within the team. To objectively evaluate the relevance, clarity, logical order and estimated duration of the questions, five elderly inpatients with chronic diseases who met the inclusion criteria were selected for a pre-interview prior to formal interviews. All participants were recruited from the same tertiary class-A hospital and signed informed consent forms, and their data were not included in the final 14 formal samples. After the pre-interview, the team revised the outline based on participants’ feedback and observation records, mainly including simplifying professional terminology, adjusting the order of questions, and merging repetitive emotion-related questions to reduce the interview duration. The finalized formal interview outline is presented in Supplementary Material 3.

Data Collection

This study adopted the semi-structured interview method. The interviews were jointly conducted by the first author (ZJ, a postgraduate student in nursing) and the second author (ZYL, a postgraduate student in nursing). Both researchers have received systematic training in qualitative research, passed the pre-interview assessment, participated in two nursing qualitative studies, and possessed independent interviewing experience. Before the interviews, the two researchers had no interest-dependent relationships with the participants, and had never been involved in the nursing care of the recruited patients. The interview location was selected by the participants, and all interviews were conducted in independent closed meeting rooms or single wards with only the researchers and the participant present, so as to fully protect the privacy of participants. In each interview, the first author was responsible for questioning and guiding the conversation, while the second author simultaneously recorded non-verbal information, and this role assignment remained consistent across all interviews. The entire interview process was audio-recorded as the primary data source. Each participant received only one interview, which lasted 30 to 45 minutes, and audio recording was conducted after obtaining written informed consent from participants. Data collection and analysis were carried out synchronously. Within 24 hours after each interview, the two researchers completed transcription and initial coding independently, then conducted a joint review, and discussed with the research team (including a third senior qualitative research expert) to decide on subsequent recruitment based on whether new themes emerged. After the 12th interview, the two coders and the research team jointly confirmed that data saturation had been achieved. To verify the robustness of the findings, two additional interviews were conducted, and recruitment was terminated after confirming that no new themes emerged, resulting in a final sample size of fourteen cases.

Data Analysis

This study adopted content analysis to analyze interview data. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim within 24 hours after each interview, and non-verbal information documented during the interview process was integrated into the transcribed texts. Subsequently, the transcribed texts were returned to the corresponding participants for verification and confirmation. Two researchers with experience in qualitative research independently coded the transcribed texts using NVivo 15 software. Following a coding strategy that combines inductive coding and deductive mapping, the researchers first extracted meaningful semantic statements from the transcribed texts, then assigned preliminary open codes to each statement, and clustered the initial codes into different categories based on semantic relevance and logical connections. These categories were organized in accordance with the four phases of patient journey preliminarily constructed based on the quality caring model, and were associated with behaviors, emotions and care needs related to humanistic nursing care. In case of disagreements on coding, the two researchers conducted group discussions with a third senior qualitative research expert in the research team until a consensus was reached. Finally, the research team visualized the aforementioned analysis results using Microsoft Visio software to further depict the dynamic changing process of humanistic care needs among elderly patients with chronic diseases during hospitalization. To ensure the completeness and consistency of the results, the research team collaborated with part of the participants to jointly review the thematic content, discussed the optimization scheme of the thematic data category map, and verified its completeness and consistency. The detailed thematic coding framework is presented in Supplementary Material 4.

Trustworthiness

To enhance the trustworthiness of the study, strategies addressing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability were implemented throughout the research process.12 Detailed information is presented in Supplementary Material 5. This study adopted purposive sampling for maximum variability and in-depth data analysis to improve research credibility. Peer debriefing and summary discussions were undertaken by the research team. Interviewers were involved in all study procedures, including participant recruitment, interview administration, transcript verification and preliminary analysis, supporting thorough data interpretation and holistic understanding of participants’ experiences. Member checking was conducted with selected participants to confirm that preliminary findings reflected their perspectives accurately.

Construct the Patient’s Journey Map

Patient journey maps typically consist of a horizontal axis (timeline) and a vertical axis (task axis). Based on the hospitalization timeline of patients and combined with relevant studies,13–15 this study divides the horizontal axis of the journey for hospitalized elderly patients with chronic diseases into four stages: the admission stage (registration of basic patient information, medical history investigation, and completion of admission procedures), the examination stage (examinations and nursing assessments conducted after the patient is formally admitted to the ward), the treatment stage (daily ward rounds, condition assessment, implementation of nursing plans and medical orders), and the discharge stage (completion of discharge assessment and preparation, development of discharge guidance and follow-up care plans). Along the vertical axis, this study incorporates three dimensions—patient behavior, emotions, and humanistic care needs—into the patient journey based on the quality care model.16 Based on the interview data, this study identified 35 themes across three dimensions at different stages of the treatment journey for hospitalized elderly patients with chronic diseases. This framework provides a comprehensive view of the patients’ treatment journey, thereby illustrating the experiences of humanistic care and the nursing needs of elderly patients with chronic diseases (Figure 1). Figure 1Patient Journey Map.The patient journey map outlines four stages: Admission, Examination, Treatment and Discharge. Each stage includes specific tasks, behaviors, emotions and humanistic care needs. The Admission stage involves registration, accommodation and initial assessment over 1 to 2 days, focusing on admission procedures and medical history. The Examination phase, lasting 2 to 5 days, includes diagnostic criteria and assessments. The Treatment phase spans weeks to months, involving condition monitoring and treatment implementation. The Discharge phase covers planning and continuing care post-hospitalization. Behaviors range from high treatment burden to increased dependency and self-management decline. Emotions transition from peace of mind to anxiety and longing for freedom. Humanistic care needs include adaptive support, psychological support and explanatory communication. Relevant personnel are nurses, providing moral support and guidance throughout the stages.A patient journey map detailing stages, tasks, behaviors, emotions and care needs in hospitalization.

Discussion

Admission Phase

The findings of this stage of research indicate that hospitalized elderly patients with chronic diseases generally experience a high level of treatment burden at admission, which is specifically manifested as economic pressure, complex medication regimens, repeated hospitalizations, and dependence on care from family members. This result is consistent with the conclusions drawn by Deng and Zhang,18 which posit that treatment burden is prevalent among the elderly population with chronic diseases. In terms of potential influencing factors, the physiological function decline associated with increasing age may exacerbate patients’ demand for diagnosis and treatment interventions; meanwhile, the state of multimorbidity forces patients to take multiple medications for a long term and undergo frequent examinations, which may further increase their burden in terms of both time and finances. Concurrently, the chronic disease course itself is prone to inducing negative emotions, and a number of elderly patients also face the dilemma of insufficient family and social support. This isolated state can undermine patients’ treatment confidence, thereby indirectly increasing their overall treatment burden.19–22

Based on the above findings, future nursing practice could consider strengthening comorbidity assessment for elderly patients with chronic diseases, providing adaptive guidance in accordance with patients’ individual actual conditions, and encouraging patients to participate in the co-development of clinical treatment regimens to improve their self-efficacy.23 Meanwhile, from the perspective of positive psychology, nursing staff should pay attention to the mental health status of elderly patients, identify negative emotions such as anxiety in a timely manner, and provide support through enhanced communication and psychological adjustment.24 In addition, establishing patient information exchange platforms or organizing chronic disease mutual support groups can also be adopted as exploratory strategies to facilitate experience sharing and peer support. Of course, the effectiveness of the aforementioned measures still needs to be further verified in subsequent clinical practice.

Examination Phase

During the examination phase, this study observed that elderly patients with chronic diseases generally exhibit behavioral manifestations including anxiety, worry, insomnia, and repeated searches for disease-related information during the waiting period for examinations or results. These manifestations can be interpreted as excessive coping responses when facing diagnostic uncertainty, which may be associated with patients’ over-vigilance against health threats and impaired self-regulation. Existing studies have indicated that such responses will increase patients’ psychological and mental burdens, and induce symptoms such as excessive anxiety, fear and sleep disorders.17 In terms of information processing, patients experience relatively high cognitive load when receiving examination instructions, which may be related to age-related changes such as decreased information processing speed and shortened attention span in some elderly individuals, as well as the technical characteristics of examination instructions themselves that contain numerous professional terms and operational procedures, which is consistent with the research conclusion reported by Lei et al.25 Such high cognitive load may trigger confusion and anxiety in patients, thereby reducing their mastery of relevant information and willingness to participate in subsequent treatment decision-making.26–28 Based on the aforementioned patient experiences, future nursing practice can explore the adoption of accessible language, step-by-step explanations, and confirmation of patients’ comprehension by asking questions or requesting patients to repeat core information after information disclosure (rather than relying solely on one-way repetition by nurses), so as to reduce the difficulty of information processing. Meanwhile, nursing staff can alleviate patients’ anxiety through empathetic listening and appropriate explanation, and encourage support from patients’ families or fellow patients. It should be noted that the aforementioned strategies are exploratory directions proposed in this study, and their clinical feasibility, acceptability and actual effectiveness still need to be verified by subsequent intervention studies.

Treatment Phase

This study revealed that during the treatment period, with the extension of hospital stay, the degree of nursing dependence among a subset of elderly patients with chronic diseases showed a gradually increasing trend. Considering that most participants had already had a long history of chronic diseases, such dependence might be related to changes in their functional status and coping resources during repeated hospitalizations and treatments, rather than solely stemming from the impact of the initial disease diagnosis. Patients’ high compliance with nurses’ daily care arrangements might not only reflect their reasonable help-seeking needs when facing the burden of long-term diseases, but also indicate a lack of confidence in self-management.29,30 It should be noted that necessary nursing dependence resulting from physiological function impairment was a reasonable requirement to ensure patient safety. However, if the degree of dependence exceeded the scope required by functional impairment, it might weaken patients’ awareness of active participation to a certain extent.31,32 Previous literature indicated that a high degree of nursing dependence was associated with the decline of patients’ self-care ability and the increase of negative emotions, and humanistic care might help alleviate patients’ feelings of helplessness and dependent psychology.33 Based on the above findings, future nursing practice could attempt to take improving patients’ self-efficacy as a goal, and gradually enhance patients’ self-management ability through individualized health education, targeted rehabilitation training, and experience sharing from peers with successful treatment outcomes. Nevertheless, the actual effects of these strategies still needed to be verified in subsequent intervention studies.

In addition, some patients reported in interviews a behavioral tendency to actively avoid health-related information. The literature summarizes the potential causes of this phenomenon as follows: patients perceive disease information as an emotional threat,34 hold a passive attitude that “knowing or not knowing makes no difference” due to long-term illness,35 and worry about increasing the care burden on their children.36 Based on this behavioral manifestation, nursing staff can first evaluate patients’ preference for receiving health information and their current emotional state in clinical communication, determine whether patients are ready for information acceptance, then provide core information in stages and in a selective manner, encourage patients to express their personal concerns during communication and respond with empathy. It should be noted that the above communication strategy aims to fit patients’ psychological state and information reception capacity, rather than correcting or eliminating their avoidance tendency. Its actual improvement effect on promoting patients’ willingness to receive information still needs to be further verified in subsequent clinical practice and research.

Discharge Phase

During the discharge phase, some participants mentioned when reviewing past experiences that, in the absence of continuous follow-up and community support, they had adjusted medication dosage on their own or resumed unhealthy living habits due to the self-perceived improvement of physical conditions; meanwhile, their willingness for social engagement also decreased, and individual participants even expressed negative perceptions of their own role value. These retrospective reports on changes in post-discharge self-management behaviors and reduced social participation are consistent with the research findings reported by Peng et al.37 Existing literature indicates that sustained deficiency in self-management capacity may adversely affect patients’ functional maintenance and engagement in social roles.38,39 To facilitate the smooth transition from hospital to home for elderly patients with chronic diseases, the following approaches can be explored for future transitional care: before discharge, provide medication and rehabilitation guidance in a staged and visualized manner, and concurrently strengthen skill training for caregivers; at discharge, optimize the handover process with community or primary medical institutions to ensure the continuity of subsequent care; after discharge, provide continuous health consultation and feedback through regular telephone follow-up or online communication platforms. The feasibility and actual efficacy of the above strategies still need to be verified by subsequent intervention studies. In addition, the information overload reported by patients mainly stems from the large volume and high professionality of guidance information provided intensively at discharge, as well as the diversity of sources of online health information after discharge.40 Future research can further explore the optimized staged presentation of discharge guidance, and provide accessible and reliable health information navigation support for elderly patients.

Conclusion

In this study, by constructing a humanistic care journey map for older inpatients with chronic diseases, we identified the dynamic changes in patients’ experiences and needs across different journey stages along the timeline of admission, examination, treatment and discharge, and clarified the specific pain points existing in each stage: in the admission stage, patients bear a heavy treatment burden and have prominent demands for emotional support; in the examination stage, the contradiction between insufficient psychological capital and high cognitive load becomes prominent; in the treatment stage, nursing dependence, information avoidance and personalized nursing needs coexist; in the discharge stage, patients are confronted with multiple challenges including decreased self-management compliance, reduced social participation and information overload. This journey map provides nursing staff with an intuitive analytical framework for identifying patients’ staged needs. As an exploratory qualitative study, the main contribution of this research lies in revealing the key experience nodes and demand patterns of humanistic care during the entire hospitalization cycle of elderly patients with chronic diseases, which provides direction for subsequent intervention design.

On this basis, the application of journey mapping in routine clinical practice demonstrates preliminary feasibility: nursing staff can utilize it as a structured interview or observation tool to conduct rapid screening against predefined pain point dimensions at key nodes including admission, examination, treatment and discharge, systematically capture patient experience and unmet needs, and dynamically adjust nursing plans or initiate multidisciplinary collaboration accordingly. It can also be embedded into electronic recording systems as a visualization tool for handover and ward rounds to sustainably deliver humanistic care. However, for this mapping to be truly integrated into clinical workflows, implementation research is still required to evaluate its feasibility and adaptability.

This study has several limitations. First, this is a single-center study conducted only at one hospital in Inner Mongolia, China, resulting in a relatively limited sample size. This introduces geographically related constraints that compromise the generalizability and applicability of the study findings. Second, the present investigation is conducted solely from the perspective of patients; future research may explore this topic from multiple dimensions and levels, and adopt a co-design approach involving patients, caregivers and healthcare professionals. Third, patients with severe hearing or visual impairment and cognitive impairment were excluded from this study. This population may have more complex and diverse needs in terms of information communication, decision-making support and humanistic care, thus the needs profile depicted in this study may not fully reflect the diversity of the entire population of hospitalized elderly patients with chronic diseases. Accordingly, future research should prioritize the following directions: conducting multi-center validation studies; including patients with different types of chronic diseases and those with sensory or cognitive impairment; optimizing the journey map through a co-design approach involving patients, caregivers and healthcare workers; and carrying out prospective studies on this basis to evaluate its feasibility, acceptability and actual improvement effect on patient experience.
