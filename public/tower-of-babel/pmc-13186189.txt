SDHB-associated paraganglioma in Saudi Arabia: High metastatic risk linked to a likely founder mutation
Authors: Ali S Alzahrani, Balgees Alghamdi, Allianah Benito, Gamal Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContext SDHB-related pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PPGL) are associated with a high risk of metastasis. Minimal data are available on these genetically based tumors from the Middle East.ObjectiveTo characterize the phenotype, genotype, and outcome of SDHB-associated PPGLs in Saudi Arabia.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed a cohort of 37 patients with SDHB-associated PPGL. DNA was isolated from peripheral blood samples, and genetic testing was performed using whole-exome sequencing (WES) and confirmed by Sanger sequencing.ResultsWe identified 37 patients with SDHB mutations, including 14 (37.8%) females and 23 (62.2%) males, with a median age at diagnosis of 28 years (range, 6-49). Nineteen patients (51.4%) had a positive family history of PPGL, and 18 (48.6%) were apparently sporadic but tested positive for SDHB mutations. The most common presentation was hyperadrenergic symptoms (54%), and the most common tumor location was the upper abdomen (45.9%). The most common SDHB mutation (likely a founder mutation) was the truncating mutation c.268T>C (p.R90*), which occurred in 23/37 patients (62%). This mutation was associated with high rates of locally invasive and/or distant metastasis (56.5%) and mortality (21.7%). At a median follow-up of 7 years (IQR 3.5-15), only 10 patients (27%) achieved remission, 7 (18.9%) died due to PPGL, one patient died due to leukemia, and 19 patients (51.4%) continued to have evidence of locally invasive or metastatic disease.ConclusionIn this Middle Eastern cohort, SDHB-associated PPGLs were characterized by high rates of locally invasive or metastatic disease, frequently associated with a possible founder mutation (p.R90*), and substantial mortality.Keywords: paraganglioma, pheochromocytoma, SDHB, SDHx, cluster 1A

Pheochromocytoma (PCC) and paraganglioma (PGL), collectively abbreviated as PPGL, are rare neuroendocrine tumors arising from the adrenal medulla or extra-adrenal sympathetic or parasympathetic ganglia, respectively [1-3]. PPGLs are among the most heritable solid tumors in humans, with approximately 40% of cases attributable to germline pathogenic variants in one of nearly 20 established susceptibility genes [4-6]. Another ∼30% of these tumors harbor somatic or mosaic mutations [6, 7].

PPGL-associated genes are broadly classified into 3 molecular clusters on the basis of their transcriptomic and signaling profiles: the pseudohypoxia cluster (subdivided into clusters 1A and 1B), the kinase signaling cluster (cluster 2), and the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cluster (cluster 3) [6]. Germline mutations in the succinate dehydrogenase (SDHx) complex represent the most frequent genetic alterations within the pseudohypoxia cluster 1A and include mutations in SDHA, SDHAF2, SDHB, SDHC, and SDHD [6, 8]. These genes encode subunits or assembly factors of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex II, which catalyzes the oxidation of succinate to fumarate in the tricarboxylic acid cycle [9]. Pathogenic variants affecting SDHx function lead to the accumulation of succinate, an oncometabolite that inhibits α-ketoglutarate–dependent dioxygenases, resulting in the stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factors, widespread epigenetic dysregulation, and the activation of pro-oncogenic pathways [10, 11]. Despite this shared metabolic mechanism, SDHx-related PPGLs exhibit striking phenotypic heterogeneity [12-14]. The tumor location, biochemical phenotype, penetrance, and malignant potential vary significantly among SDHx genes, with SDHB mutations presenting the greatest risk of metastatic disease and mortality [15-17].

Most published PPGL cohorts originate from North America, Europe, and East Asia, whereas data from the Middle East remain scarce. This gap is particularly relevant in Saudi Arabia, a population characterized by high rates of consanguinity [18, 19]. Only a few prior reports have been published and suggested presence of a possible founder SDHB mutation and an unusually aggressive disease phenotype [20, 21]. However, comprehensive genotype–phenotype correlation and outcome data are lacking.

In this study, we present a detailed clinical, molecular, and outcome analysis of a cohort of patients with SDHB-associated PPGLs managed at King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Centre (KFSHRC) in Riyadh, the national referral center for PPGL care in Saudi Arabia. Our objectives were to characterize tumor distribution, biochemical patterns, genotype-phenotype association, metastatic risk, treatment patterns, and long-term outcomes in this unique population.

Patients and methods

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Faisal Specialist Hospital & Research Centre, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (ORA #2150-015). Informed consent for genetic testing and participation in research was obtained from patients or legal guardians, when applicable. Clinical data were collected from medical records and included demographics, family history, genetic testing, clinical presentation, catecholamine secretion, radiological studies, treatment, and outcomes.

The biochemical phenotype was defined according to Eisenhofer et al. [22], with the adrenergic phenotype present when plasma metanephrine was >0.31 nmol/L and comprised >5% of the total summed concentrations of the three catecholamine metabolites [metanephrine, normetanephrine, and 3-methoxytyramine (3-MT)]. The noradrenergic phenotype was considered present in patients who did not meet the two requirements for the adrenergic phenotype and had a 3-MT plasma concentration of ≤0.10 nmol/L. The dopaminergic phenotype was considered present when 3-MT exceeds 0.10 nmol/L and its fold increase is higher than the fold increase of the other metabolites [22, 23]. Although anatomical imaging (CT and MRI) always revealed sites of primary tumors (abdominal and head and neck tumors), Gallium-68 dotatate positron emission tomography with computed tomography (68Ga-DOTATATE PET CT) scans were the most useful for detecting the sites of metastatic disease and for showing the extent of the disease. It also guided more focused anatomical imaging when needed. Remission was defined as complete tumor resection with radiologic absence of disease and normal plasma and/or urine metanephrine levels for at least 1 year after therapy [1, 24]. Distant metastases were defined as the presence of the disease in non-chromaffin cell-containing organs such as the bone and lymph nodes [25]. Stable and progressive metastases were assessed using the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1. In this context, stable disease was defined as no change in tumor size, growth of less than 20% or a decrease of tumor lesions of no more than 30% with no new lesions; partial response as a reduction in tumor size of more than 30%; and progression as an increase in tumor size of more than 20% or the appearance of new metastatic lesions [26].

Molecular testing

Molecular testing was performed on genomic DNA derived from peripheral blood samples as previously described [21]. Whole-exome sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform as described previously [27]. Briefly, DNA paired-end fragment libraries were prepared according to the Illumina WES protocol using the Illumina DNA Prep with Exome 2.5 Enrichment (catalog numbers 20 077 595 and 20 077 596; Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) with a target insert size of 150 bp. DNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (S2 reagent kit, 200-cycle configuration, and target sequencing depth of 50X-100X). Bioinformatics analysis was performed as previously described [21, 27]. All SDHB mutations were confirmed in the index cases and in available family members' DNA samples by Sanger sequencing. The primers and PCR conditions used were previously reported [20].

Results

Patients

We identified 37 patients with SDHB mutations evaluated between January 2010 and January 2025. The cohort included 14 (37.8%) females and 23 (62.2%) males, with a median age at diagnosis of 28 years (range 6-49), including 9 patients who were ≤ 20 years (Table 1). The median age at the last visit was 38 years (range, 14-65). Nineteen patients (51.4%) had a positive family history of PPGL, and 18 (48.6%) were apparently sporadic but tested positive for SDHB mutations. The initial sites of PPGL were as follows: 3 (8.1%) unilateral PCC, 17 (45.6%) upper abdominal PGL, 7 (18.9%) PGL at the organ of Zuckerkandl, 1 (2.7%) PGL in the urinary bladder, 8 (21.6%) head and neck PGL (one of which was synchronous bilateral), and 1 (2.7%) mediastinal PGL. The clinical presentation included an incidental finding in 2 patients (5.4%), hyperadrenergic symptoms (palpitations, sweating, headache, hypertension) in 15 patients (40.5%), pressure symptoms (pain) at the primary tumor in 13 patients (35.1%), hyperadrenergic and pressure symptoms at the primary tumor site in 5 patients (13.5%), and pressure symptoms due to distant metastases in 2 patients (5.4%). In the overall cohort, the median size of the PPGL was 7.25 cm (1.8-15). Urinary or plasma metanephrine levels were measured in 31 patients (83.8%) and were normal (metanephrine, normetanephrine, and 3-MT) in 9 patients (24.3%). Among the remaining 22 patients (59.5%) who had metanephrine measurements, all had one or more metanephrines elevated, with normetanephrine being predominantly elevated in 20 patients (54.1%) and metanephrine in 2 patients (5.4%).

Management and outcomes

Management and outcome of localized disease

Of the 37 patients included, 20 (54%) had localized disease. The sites of the disease were the adrenal gland (PCC) in 3 patients, the upper abdomen in 7 patients, the organ of Zuckerkandl in 2 patients, and the head and neck in 8 patients. Surgery on the primary tumor was performed in 19 patients, and 1 patient was scheduled for surgery at the time of the study. In one patient, Iodine-131 meta-iodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) was given after surgery, and in another patient, surgery was preceded by arterial embolization. The outcome of this subgroup included remission in 10 patients (50%), persistent/recurrent disease at the primary site in 6 patients (30%), and new PGL at another site in 4 patients (20%).

Management of metastatic PPGL

Of the 37 patients included in this study, 17 (45.9%) developed distant metastases. These patients received between 2 to 6 therapeutic modalities (Table 2). All patients except 2 underwent surgery of the primary tumor. One of the 2 patients who were not operated on received Cyclophosphamide, Vincristine, and Dacarbazine (CVD) chemotherapy for widespread metastases and inoperable primary upper abdominal PGL and the other one received 2 doses of MIBG therapy for an inoperable right upper abdominal PGL. Sixteen patients received 2, 8 patients received 3, 4 patients received 4, 3 patients received 5, and 1 patient received 6 lines of therapies (Table 2). CVD chemotherapy (10 sessions in 9 patients) and peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) in 9 patients were the second most commonly used therapies after surgery (Table 2). Other therapies included debulking surgeries, chemotherapy, temozolomide, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sorafenib, cabozantinib, sunitinib and pazopanib) (Table 2). At a median follow-up of 6.5 years (range, 2-30), 7/17 patients (41.2%) died due to progressive metastatic PPGL, 1/17 patient (5.9%) died due to an unrelated cause (acute leukemia) with stable PGL metastases, 5/17 patients (29.4%) continued to have progressive PPGL, and 4/17 patients (23.5%) had stable metastatic disease.

Overall outcomes

The median follow-up duration was 7 years (range, 1-38; IQR, 3.5-15.00). The final outcome of the whole cohort of 37 patients was remission in 10 patients (27.0%), persistent/recurrent localized disease in 6 patients (16.2%), development of new PGL at a different site in 4 patients (10.8%), stable metastatic disease in 4 patients (10.8%), progressive metastatic disease in 5 patients (13.5%), death due to PPGL in 7 patients (18.9%), and death due to other causes in 1 (2.7%) patient (due to acute myeloid leukemia). At a median follow-up of 7 years, disease-free survival at the last follow-up was 27%, and disease-specific survival was 81%. Molecular genetics

Ten distinct mutations were detected in this cohort, spanning the entire coding sequence (Table 3). The truncating mutation c.268T>C (p.R90*) was the most common mutation, occurring in 23 patients (62.2%), 10 (27%) of whom had no family history of PPGLs, and 13 (35.2%) were from 7 families with PPGLs. The other mutations were all missense, except for one large deletion of exon 3 (Table 3).

Genotype‒phenotype relationships

As shown in Tables 3 and 4, the c.268T>C (p.R90*) mutation was the most common mutation, occurring in 23 patients: 13 with metastatic and 10 with localized PPGLs. The outcome of these patients was remission in 6/23 (26%) patients, persistent/recurrent localized disease in 3/23 (13%) patients, metastatic disease in 8/23 (34.8%) patients, death due to PPGL in 5/23 (21.7%) patients, and the development of a new metachronous PPGL in 1/23 (4.3%) patients (Table 4). Most patients with p.R90* originated from a region in the western-northern part of the country, suggesting a founder effect. The next most common SDHB mutation was c.689G>A, which occurred in 4 patients (10.8%). Of these patients, 1 achieved remission, 2 developed PPGL at different new sites (metachronous PPGL), and 1 died due to progressive PPGL (Table 4). Other outcomes with respect to the underlying SDHB mutations are shown in Table 4. Since SDHB p.R90* occurred in >60% of the patients, we compared the PPGLs related to this mutation with other SDHB mutations found in the current cohort (Table 5). Patients with p.R90* SDHB mutation were younger and more likely to be females, but there were no differences in the tumor location, catecholamine secretion, and tumor size (Table 5). Although overall, there were no significant differences in the outcome, PPGL-related mortality and distant metastases tended to be more likely in patients with p.R90* mutation compared to other SDHB mutations in this cohort (Table 5). In Kaplan–Meier analysis (Fig. 2), the p.R90* was associated with a significantly higher risk of distant metastases (P = .04) and a trend for increased mortality that did not reach statistical significance (P = .40), probably due to the low rate of mortality (7 cases only).

Discussion

In this study, we present a detailed analysis of one of the largest cohorts of patients with SDHB-related PPGLs reported from the Middle East, representing a population with a unique social and genetic background. This study confirms the high rate of metastatic and aggressive disease in patients with SDHB-associated hereditary PPGLs. A large proportion of patients (72.9%) exhibited persistent, recurrent, locally invasive, and/or metastatic disease. Approximately one-fifth of the patients (18.9%) died secondary to PPGLs. These data have important implications for genetic testing, counseling, surveillance strategies, and management.

The median age at diagnosis was 28 years, but approximately one-quarter of the patients were ≤ 20 years, and a few were younger than 10 years. International guidelines recommend genetic screening for offspring of affected adults between 6 and 10 years of age [4, 28]. Although this study generally supports these recommendations, it also suggests that this age range may be too late for some patients from the Middle East, as one patient presented at the age of 6 years with metastatic PGL and another at the age of 10 years with a 4-cm abdominal PGL, suggesting its development of their PPGL over the last few years before presentation. Both of these cases had the truncating mutation p.R90*. These cases underscore the importance of early genetic testing and evaluation, suggesting that testing and evaluation before the age of 5 years may be a more appropriate screening age for families with SDHB mutations, especially for those with the known p.R90* mutation. Jochmanova et al. studied 66 children and adolescents (< 20 years) with SDHB-related PPGL and reported a 70% rate of distant metastases, with a significant impact on survival when distant metastases were present at the time of presentation [29]. The median age for development of metastases was 16 years (8-39 years). The diagnosis of PPGL occurred at ≥6 years of age, although the median age at diagnosis was not specified. The p.R90* SDHB mutation was one of the top 5 mutations in that pediatric cohort, occurring in 7/66 patients. The estimated 5-, 10-, and 20-year survival rates were 100%, 97.14%, and 77.71%, respectively [29].

Although the upper abdomen was the most common site of SDHB-related PGL in this cohort, approximately one-fifth of patients had head-and-neck PGLs, a location more characteristic of SDHD mutations [30]. PCC also occurred in 3 patients (8.1%), and mediastinal and urinary bladder PGLs occurred in 1 patient each. These findings suggest that SDHB-related PPGLs, in addition to being frequently metastatic, can occur at any site along the sympathetic and parasympathetic chains.

The biochemical profile of our patients revealed the usual normetanephrine predominance in PGL, which was observed in the vast majority of patients with secretory PPGLs. However, approximately one-third of cases had non-secretory PPGLs, a well-recognized feature with important clinical and surveillance implications, as reliance on biochemical evaluation alone may delay the detection of PPGLs, particularly in SDHB mutation carriers under surveillance, emphasizing the importance of scheduled radiological surveillance according to the international guidelines [4, 28].

The disease course in this cohort demonstrated an aggressive pattern characterized by high rates of locally invasive, metastatic, and persistent disease, as well as the development of new PPGLs at additional sites. Despite multiple therapeutic modalities used in these patients, including frequent use of cytotoxic chemotherapy, PRRT, and other treatments, only 10 of 37 patients (27%) achieved remission after surgical treatment of localized disease. In contrast, more than 70% of patients continued to have evidence of residual or metastatic disease, and PPGL-related mortality was high, occurring in approximately 19% of patients. These figures are significantly higher than those reported in other cohorts [31-34]. These findings may reflect a strong genetic background, including the very high prevalence of the highly pathogenic p.R90* mutation, which may represent a founder mutation in this population, occurring in more than 60% of patients in this study. Other factors that may explain the aggressiveness of PPGLs in this cohort include delayed presentation and referral bias to our tertiary care center.

Genotyping of this cohort yielded several significant findings. We identified 10 distinct SDHB mutations spanning the coding frame of SDHB. These mutations were predominantly missense but also included one nonsense mutation and one deletion. More than 60% of patients had the truncating mutation p.R90*, which is associated with an aggressive disease course, as the majority of patients carrying this mutation developed locally invasive or metastatic PPGLs. The high prevalence of this mutation and its geographic clustering suggest a possible founder effect. However, we acknowledge that a founder effect typically requires haplotype/ancestry analyses, robust population-level frequency comparisons, and/or genealogical linkage. Without such data, the high rate of this mutation is suggestive of enrichment and a possible founder effect rather than the definitive presence of a founder variant.

The aggressive phenotype associated with this mutation (p.R90*) may warrant early and intensive surveillance in families carrying it. This mutation has been reported in many studies and has been shown to cause complete loss of SDHB activity and failure of SDH assembly due to the loss of the SDHB subunit [35]. In addition to its association with PPGLs [20, 21, 36, 37], the SDHB p.R90* mutation has also been associated with renal cell carcinoma [38], gastrointestinal stromal cell tumors [39], and cardiovascular disease traits [40]. In a study assessing penetrance among SDHB mutation carriers, 40 (16.60%) of 241 screened relatives of 103 index cases developed PPGL during the study [41]. The overall penetrance estimate was 49.80% (95% CI 29-74.9) at 85 years, with higher penetrance rates in males compared to females. Five/103 (4.8%) index cases with p.R90* mutation had 43/241 (17.8%) SDHB carriers, 9 (20.9%) of whom developed PPGLs [41]. The age-related penetrance analysis demonstrated 50% penetrance at the age of 61 years for the p.R90* SDHB mutation [41].

Another observation in our cohort was that nearly half of the patients initially appeared to have sporadic PPGLs. This finding reinforces the international guidelines that all patients with PPGLs should undergo genetic testing [10, 28, 42, 43].

This study has several strengths and limitations. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest cohort with SDHB mutations reported from the Middle East, characterizing, for the first time, the phenotypic features and the underlying molecular genetics of these rare but aggressive tumors with limited effective therapy. The findings of this study have clinical implications, including the distribution of the disease, the clinical and biochemical features, and the aggressive nature of PPGLs in patients with certain SDHB mutations. It also identified a likely founder mutation in the Saudi population that is associated with an aggressive course, implying the need for early screening and surveillance in families with this mutation. Limitations include the retrospective nature of the study, the relatively small sample size (although it remains large for a single PPGL-causing gene, SDHB), and, finally, referral bias, since our institution is the primary referral center for PPGL in the country.

Conclusions

SDHB-associated PPGLs in this Saudi cohort are characterized by early onset and high rates of local invasiveness and metastatic disease with guarded long-term outcomes, particularly among carriers of a prevalent truncating mutation, p.R90*, suggestive of a founder effect. These findings underscore the need for early genetic diagnosis, lifelong surveillance, and multidisciplinary management.
