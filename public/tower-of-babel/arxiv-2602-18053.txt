On the Generalization and Robustness in Conditional Value-at-Risk
Dinesh Karthik Mulumudi, Piyushi Manupriya, Gholamali Aminian, Anant Raj

Abstract
Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) is a widely used risk-sensitive objective for learning under rare but high-impact losses, yet its statistical behavior under heavy-tailed data remains poorly understood. Unlike expectation-based risk, CVaR depends on an endogenous, data-dependent quantile, which couples tail averaging with threshold estimation and fundamentally alters both generalization and robustness properties. In this work, we develop a learning-theoretic analysis of CVaR-based empirical risk minimization under heavy-tailed and contaminated data. We establish sharp, high-probability generalization and excess risk bounds under minimal moment assumptions, covering fixed hypotheses, finite and infinite classes, and extending to β \beta -mixing dependent data; we further show that these rates are minimax optimal. To capture the intrinsic quantile sensitivity of CVaR, we derive a uniform Bahadur-Kiefer type expansion that isolates a threshold-driven error term absent in mean-risk ERM and essential in heavy-tailed regimes. We complement these results with robustness guarantees by proposing a truncated median-of-means CVaR estimator that achieves optimal rates under adversarial contamination. Finally, we show that CVaR decisions themselves can be intrinsically unstable under heavy tails, establishing a fundamental limitation on decision robustness even when the population optimum is well separated. Together, our results provide a principled characterization of when CVaR learning generalizes and is robust, and when instability is unavoidable due to tail scarcity.

Introduction

Statistical Learning Theory (SLT) provides the standard framework for analyzing generalization in learning algorithms. Its central paradigm, Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM), selects a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
by minimizing empirical risk, yielding generalization under suitable complexity control
Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014
. Classical ERM, however, is risk-neutral and optimizes expected loss, which can be inadequate in high-stakes domains—such as finance, healthcare, and safety-critical learning—where rare but extreme losses dominate performance
Howard and Matheson 1972
;
Shen et al. 2014
;
Cardoso and Xu 2019
.

These limitations have motivated the study of
risk-sensitive
learning objectives that emphasize tail behavior, including Entropic Risk, Mean-Variance, and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR).
From a learning-theoretic standpoint, several works have begun to investigate generalization guarantees for such objectives.
For example, generalization bounds for tilted empirical risk minimization under finite-moment assumptions were derived in
Aminian et al.
, while
Lee et al. 2021
studied CVaR and related risk measures under bounded-loss and light-tailed assumptions.
However, these settings do not fully capture the regimes in which CVaR is most relevant in practice, where data are often heavy-tailed and may exhibit temporal dependence.
This gap motivates a systematic study of CVaR generalization beyond bounded or light-tailed models, which we address in this work by developing guarantees under heavy-tailed losses for both i.i.d. and dependent data.

Another key challenge specific to CVaR-based learning is structural.
CVaR depends on an
endogenous, data-dependent threshold
, the Value-at-Risk (VaR), which couples tail averages with empirical quantile estimation.
Unlike smooth risk functionals, this coupling creates an intrinsic interaction between tail fluctuations and threshold estimation error.
As a consequence, standard generalization analyses that treat the objective as a fixed functional of the loss distribution are insufficient.
Controlling CVaR generalization therefore requires explicitly accounting for how fluctuations of the empirical quantile propagate into fluctuations of the tail risk.
To address this issue, we develop a refined empirical-process analysis based on
uniform Bahadur-Kiefer–type expansions

Bahadur 1966
;
Kiefer 1967
, adapted to the CVaR setting to capture the effects of this endogenous threshold.

Robustness presents a closely related challenge and arises at multiple levels.
At the
functional
level, although CVaR is often viewed as robust due to its emphasis on tail outcomes, its dependence on an empirical quantile can amplify sensitivity to perturbations near the tail.
At the
estimator
level, robustness under heavy-tailed data and adversarial contamination has been extensively studied through trimmed means and median-of-means estimators

Lugosi and Mendelson 2019a
;
Lugosi and Mendelson 2019b
;
Lugosi and Mendelson 2021
,
yielding sharp guarantees for estimation primitives and robust ERM, but not directly for tail-based risk functionals such as CVaR.
At the
decision
level, the stability of CVaR-optimal solutions under distributional perturbations remains comparatively less understood.

In this work, we study both the
generalization
and
robustness
properties of CVaR-based learning under heavy-tailed and contaminated data.
On the generalization side, we derive non-asymptotic excess risk guarantees under minimal moment assumptions and complement them with refined empirical-process tools that explicitly account for quantile sensitivity.
On the robustness side, we analyze the stability of CVaR objectives and solutions under distributional perturbations.
Together, these results clarify the statistical regimes in which empirical CVaR reliably approximates its population counterpart, as well as settings in which instability is intrinsic.

Background and Problem Formulation

2.1
Conditonal Value at Risk (CVaR)

Let
L
be a real-valued random variable representing the loss of a decision or portfolio.
For a tail probability
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the
Value-at-Risk
at level
\alpha
is defined as the upper
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of
L
:

\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(L)\;:=\;\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}(L\leq t)\geq 1-\alpha\}.

Equivalently,
\mathbb{P}\!\left(L>\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(L)\right)\leq\alpha.
The
Conditional Value-at-Risk
at level
\alpha
is defined as the expected loss in the
\alpha
-tail of the distribution:

\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(L)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[L\mid L\geq\mathrm{VaR}_{\alpha}(L)\right],

whenever the conditional expectation is well-defined. More generally, CVaR admits the variational representation
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2000
:

\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(L)\;=\;\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{t+\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}\big[(L-t)_{+}\big]\right\},

which remains valid without continuity assumptions and is particularly convenient for learning and optimization.

Unlike VaR, CVaR is a coherent risk measure: it is monotone, translation invariant, positively homogeneous, and subadditive. In particular, CVaR is convex in the underlying loss distribution, which makes it amenable to empirical risk minimization and generalization analysis.

3
Generalization in CVaR

This section establishes non-asymptotic generalization guarantees for Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) under heavy-tailed losses, ranging from fixed-hypothesis concentration to sharp excess risk bounds for infinite classes, with matching minimax lower bounds and extensions to dependent data. We further develop a random active-set theory for CVaR and derive uniform Bahadur–Kiefer expansions that explicitly capture the role of the endogenous threshold.

The core difficulty is structural: CVaR depends on tail behavior through a data-dependent quantile, requiring joint control of tail fluctuations and threshold instability.

3.1
Generalization with i.i.d. Data

We begin with the i.i.d. setting. The first step is to understand how the empirical CVaR concentrates around its population counterpart for a fixed hypothesis.

The key technical ingredient is a concentration inequality for heavy-tailed random variables with finite
(1+\lambda)
-moment, as developed in
Brownlees et al. 2015
;
Prashanth et al. 2019
. Applying these tools to the Rockafellar-Uryasev variational representation of CVaR yields the following fixed-hypothesis deviation bound.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Assumption
2.1
and for any fixed
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
\epsilon>0
with probability at least
1-\delta
we,

\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}(h)\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}M^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\left(\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}.

(3)

For a finite
\mathcal{H}

R_{\alpha}(\widehat{h})-R_{\alpha}(h^{*})\leq\frac{4}{\alpha}M^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\left(\frac{\log(4|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}.

(4)

While the finite-class bound follows directly from a union bound, it does not capture the true statistical complexity of learning when

For a fixed natural number

This theorem shows that empirical CVaR minimization achieves the optimal tradeoff between model complexity, sample size, and tail heaviness. Importantly, the rate matches the minimax lower bound up to constants, demonstrating that no improvement is possible without stronger assumptions.

Truncated Empirical CVaR Estimation.

Although empirical CVaR minimization is statistically optimal, finite-sample instability can arise from extreme losses. We therefore introduce a truncated CVaR estimator that limits the influence of large observations while preserving optimal rates and remaining amenable to first-order optimization. Truncation simultaneously stabilizes estimation and enables standard complexity-based analysis, with the induced bias controlled under finite-moment assumptions.

Theorem 3.3

(High-Probability Generalization Bound)

C_{\lambda,\delta}=\frac{1}{\lambda}+\sqrt{8\log(1/\delta)}+\frac{2}{3}\log(1/\delta).

(8)

Takeaway:

Truncated empirical CVaR achieves optimal high-probability excess risk bounds while offering improved robustness to heavy-tailed noise.

3.3
Random active-set theory and uniform Bahadur - Kiefer expansions for CVaR

While the preceding results bound uniform CVaR deviations under heavy tails, they do not explain how these fluctuations affect the empirical minimizer. The challenge is structural: CVaR depends on an
endogenous

For, fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For

The correction term captures precisely this endogenous selection effect at first order.

4
Robustness of CVaR

This section develops a robustness theory for CVaR under heavy-tailed losses with only finite-moment control. Robustness arises at three levels:
functional
robustness of CVaR under distributional perturbations;
estimator
robustness, providing uniform guarantees for empirical CVaR under sampling noise and contamination; and
decision
robustness, which concerns stability of the CVaR minimizer and is fundamentally shaped by the endogenous RU threshold. We treat these in turn, highlighting how threshold sensitivity and tail scarcity induce intrinsic instability.

4.1
Functional Robustness

We first analyze CVaR as a distributional functional, yielding metric-dependent continuity results independent of estimation. Under geometric metrics (e.g., Wasserstein), stability follows from loss regularity in
z
, whereas under weaker metrics (e.g., Lévy–Prokhorov or total variation), finite moments imply only tail-Hölder continuity with exponent set by the moment order. This distinction separates intrinsic (functional) robustness from estimation-induced effects.

Proposition 4.1

(Tail-Hölder robustness of
\mathrm{CVaR}
)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\lambda\in(0,1]
, and set
\kappa_{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)^{-1}
.
Let
P,Q
be distributions on
\mathcal{Z}
and
\ell:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
.
For fixed

, let
L_{P}=\ell(h,Z)
,
Z\sim P
, and
L_{Q}=\ell(h,Z^{\prime})
,
Z^{\prime}\sim Q
.
Assume

\mathbb{E}_{P}[|L_{P}|^{\lambda+1}]\leq M_{p}
,
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[|L_{Q}|^{\lambda+1}]\leq M_{p}.
Given,
W_{r}(P,Q)
and
\pi(P,Q)
are
r
-Wasserstein and Lévy-Prokhorov metric between
P
and
Q
respectively, then

(i) Wasserstein stability under Hölder losses.

If

is
\beta
-Hölder in

uniformly over
\theta

and

, then for all

with finite

-moments,

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\big|R_{\alpha}^{P}(h)-R_{\alpha}^{Q}(h)\big|\;\leq\;\kappa_{\alpha}L_{\beta}\,W_{r}(P,Q)^{\beta}.

(ii) Lévy-Prokhorov tail-Hölder continuity.

If
\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\ell(h,\cdot)
is
L_{h}
-Lipschitz, then for
\pi(P,Q)\leq\varepsilon

\big|R_{\alpha}^{P}(h)-R_{\alpha}^{Q}(h)\big|\;\leq\;\kappa_{\alpha}L_{h}\big(2\varepsilon+2M_{p}^{1/(\lambda+1)}\varepsilon^{\lambda/(\lambda+1)}\big),

and the tail term
\varepsilon^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}
is unavoidable.

Discussion.

Part (i) shows that when the loss is regular in

, CVaR is stable in Wasserstein distance under finite moments. Part (ii) reveals an unavoidable degradation under weaker perturbations: small distributional changes can shift the upper tail enough to alter CVaR at a Hölder rate
\varepsilon^{\lambda/(\lambda+1)}
. This tail-scarcity effect also governs estimator and decision robustness, where stability depends on mass near the threshold. An analogous Hölder bound holds in total variation distance (Proposition

4.2
Estimator Robustness

In this part, we develops a robust estimator theory for CVaR under heavy-tailed losses with only finite-moment control. Building on the previous section,
we next study robustness at the level of the CVaR
objective
over a hypothesis class. Here the goal is to bound the worst-case sensitivity

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|R_{\alpha}^{P}(h)-R_{\alpha}^{Q}(h)|,

which captures both distributional robustness and the effect of adversarial perturbations when
Q
is a contaminated version of
P
.
The next theorem provides such a bound under total variation perturbations when the contaminated distribution
Q
also has similar moment cotrol as
P
and establishes that the resulting exponent is minimax optimal under finite-moment control.

Proposition 4.2

(Robustness of
\mathrm{CVaR}
)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\big|R_{\alpha}^{P}(h)-R_{\alpha}^{Q}(h)\big|\;\leq\;\frac{C}{\alpha}\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}},

(12)

where
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\sup_{A}|P(A)-Q(A)|
and

C=(2M)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\!\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\right)
. Moreover, this dependence is minimax optimal.

Discussion.

Proposition
4.2
characterizes the optimal sensitivity of CVaR to total variation perturbations under heavy tails. Because total variation is bounded, this result combines directly with our generalization bounds to yield excess risk guarantees under domain shift
Aminian et al.
. The Hölder exponent
\lambda/(1+\lambda)
reflects the intrinsic limitation imposed by finite-moment control, and the matching minimax lower bound shows that no sharper dependence on
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)
is achievable without stronger tail assumptions.

Robust Estimation under Adversarial Contamination

Unlike the previous setting—where the contaminated distribution retained a finite
(1+\lambda)
-moment—adversarial contamination may introduce arbitrarily heavy tails. We therefore study estimator robustness under
oblivious adversarial contamination
where
\epsilon n
datapoints have been corrupted, deriving uniform generalization guarantees via a truncated median-of-means (MoM) construction.

Our analysis simultaneously controls heavy-tailed sampling variability and adversarial bias by combining truncation of extreme losses with median aggregation across blocks. These ideas are applied to the Rockafellar–Uryasev lift, treating
(h,\theta)
jointly and robustifying the empirical estimation of
\mathbb{E}[(\ell(h,Z)-\theta)_{+}]
through blockwise truncation and MoM aggregation.

The resulting bound decomposes into a statistical term driven by complexity and effective sample size, and a contamination term proportional to
\epsilon

\lambda/(1+\lambda)
.

Theorem 4.3

(Robust Generalization Bound)
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
and
2.4
hold. Let
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
be fixed such that
\epsilon\leq 1/2-\gamma
.
Suppose the sample size satisfies

With probability at least

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}(h)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{M_{\phi}d\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}+C_{2}M_{\phi}^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\epsilon^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}\asymp\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\left(\frac{Md\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}+(M\epsilon)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}\right).

Here
C_{1},C_{2},M\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ M_{\phi}
are universal constants depending only on

Discussion.

Theorem

clarifies the robustness–generalization tradeoff under contamination: the first term achieves the optimal heavy-tail statistical rate (up to logs and complexity), while the second captures the unavoidable loss from an
\epsilon

\asymp

1/\alpha

On the Algorithmic Aspect

: We construct a robust CVaR estimator via a truncated median-of-means (T-MoM) scheme for heavy-tailed data with oblivious adversarial contamination. The sample is partitioned into blocks, the Rockafellar–Uryasev loss is truncated within each block to control variance and adversarial bias, and blockwise estimates are aggregated by a median, yielding robustness to both heavy tails and an
\epsilon
-fraction of corrupted samples. The CVaR estimate is obtained by minimizing this robust objective over the auxiliary threshold.

While statistically principled, this approach is not directly implementable in high dimensions: MoM tournaments over
\eta
-nets are feasible only in low-dimensional settings. We therefore focus on statistical guarantees and defer algorithmic issues to Appendix
H
.

4.3
Decision Robustness

We now study
decision robustness
for CVaR minimization, focusing on the stability of the argmin rather than the objective value. This is subtle for CVaR, as its endogenous, distribution-dependent threshold can amplify small perturbations into decision-level changes. We denote the population solution set and the (possibly set-valued) Rockafellar–Uryasev threshold minimizers (deined in section
2.2
) by
\mathcal{S}(P):=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R_{\alpha}^{P}(h)
,
T^{\star}(h,P):=\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\Phi_{P}(h,\theta)

Decision robustness concerns the continuity of
\mathcal{S}(P)
under perturbations of
P
. Unlike mean-risk ERM, CVaR introduces an endogenous threshold
\theta^{\star}(h,P)\in T^{\star}(h,P)
determined by the upper
\alpha
-tail, whose stability is the primary driver of decision robustness.

Endogenous Threshold Stability:

Fix
h
and write
X:=\ell(h,Z)
under
P
.
Define
\phi_{P}(\theta):=\Phi_{P}(h,\theta)=\theta+\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(X-\theta)_{+}]
. The characterization
0\in\partial\phi_{P}(\theta)
yields

\theta\in T^{\star}(h,P)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P(X>\theta)\leq\alpha\leq P(X\geq\theta).

(13)

Thus
T^{\star}(h,P)
is the
\alpha
-upper-quantile set of
X
.

Definition 4.4

(Quantile margin and generalized density-at-quantile)
.

Assume
T^{\star}(h,P)
is a singleton
\{\theta^{\star}(h,P)\}
and write
\theta^{\star}
for short.
Define the local mass near the threshold
\kappa_{h,P}(r):=P\big(|\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{\star}|\leq r\big),\penalty\ r>0,
and the generalized density-at-quantile (quantile margin parameter)

\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(h,P):=\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\kappa_{h,P}(r)}{r}\in[0,\infty].

The next theorem shows that a positive quantile margin guarantees stability of the threshold under weak distributional perturbations, while its absence leads to intrinsic instability.

Theorem 4.5

(Threshold stability under Lévy-Prokhorov perturbations)
.

Fix
h
and assume
T^{\star}(h,P)=\{\theta^{\star}(h,P)\}
is a singleton. Let
Q
be another law for
X=\ell(h,Z)
and let

d_{\mathrm{LP}}(P,Q)
denote the Lévy-Prokhorov distance between these one-dimensional laws.
If
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(h,P)\geq m_{0}>0
, then for all sufficiently small
\delta:=d_{\mathrm{LP}}(P,Q)
,

|\theta^{\star}(h,Q)-\theta^{\star}(h,P)|\ \leq\ C\,\delta/m_{0}.

(14)

Conversely, if
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(h,P)=0
, then for every
\delta>0
there exists
Q
with
d_{\mathrm{LP}}(P,Q)\leq\delta

such that
T^{\star}(h,Q)
contains
\tilde{\theta}
with
|\tilde{\theta}-\theta^{\star}|\geq c_{0}
(an
O(1)
jump).

Remark.

Theorem
4.5
reveals an intrinsic fragility of CVaR: when the loss distribution has negligible mass near the VaR, the optimal threshold can change discontinuously under arbitrarily small perturbations, regardless of sample size.

Influence Function of the CVaR Decision:

To quantify how threshold instability propagates to decisions, we analyze the RU stationarity system and derive the influence function of the CVaR minimizer under gross-error contamination, treating
(h,\theta)
jointly. Define the stationarity map

H(h,\theta;P):=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{h}\Phi_{P}(h,\theta)\\
\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{P}(h,\theta)\end{pmatrix}.

(15)

When
P(\ell(h,Z)=\theta)=0
, the derivative in
\theta
is classical:
\partial_{\theta}\Phi_{P}(h,\theta)=1-\frac{1}{\alpha}P(\ell(h,Z)>\theta),
and
\nabla_{h}\Phi_{P}(h,\theta)=\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{P}\big[\nabla_{h}\ell(h,Z)\,\mathbf{1}\{\ell(h,Z)>\theta\}\big].

We impose a local regularity condition that is strictly weaker than global strong convexity and fits nonconvex but stable minimizers
(e.g. ‘tilt stability”; here we keep a concrete invertibility condition).

Assumption 4.6

(Local strong regularity / invertible Jacobian)
.

Let
(h^{\star},\theta^{\star})
be a locally unique stationary point:
H(h^{\star},\theta^{\star};P)=0
.
Assume:
(i)
\ell(h,z)
is
C^{2}
in
h
near

for
P
-a.e.

;
(ii)
P(\ell(h^{\star},Z)=\theta^{\star})=0
;
(iii) the Jacobian
J:=\nabla_{(h,\theta)}H(h^{\star},\theta^{\star};P)
exists and is invertible.

Assumption 4.7

(Local RU stability at the population optimum)
.

Assume there exists
(h^{\star},\theta^{\star})
such that
(i)
h^{\star}\in\mathcal{S}(P)
and
\theta^{\star}\in T^{\star}(h^{\star},P)
;
(ii)
T^{\star}(h^{\star},P)=\{\theta^{\star}\}
is a singleton;
(iv) the quantile margin
\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(h^{\star},P)\geq m_{0}>0
.

Under these assumptions, the influence function admits a closed-form expression and exhibits a sharp dependence on the quantile margin.

Theorem 4.8

(Influence function of the CVaR decision: tail support and quantile-margin blow-up)
.

Assume Assumption
4.6
. Consider the gross-error path

P_{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)P+\varepsilon\Delta_{z},\qquad\varepsilon\geq 0,

and let
(h_{\varepsilon},\theta_{\varepsilon})
be the locally unique stationary solution to
H(h,\theta;P_{\varepsilon})=0
near
(h^{\star},\theta^{\star})

Then

is differentiable at
\varepsilon=0

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\begin{pmatrix}h_{\varepsilon}\\
\theta_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=-J^{-1}\Big(g(z;h^{\star},\theta^{\star})-\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(Z;h^{\star},\theta^{\star})]\Big),

where

g(z;h,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\alpha}\nabla_{h}\ell(h,z)\,\mathbf{1}\{\ell(h,z)>\theta\}\\[2.84526pt]
1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbf{1}\{\ell(h,z)>\theta\}\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

Moreover, the
\theta

\displaystyle\left|\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\theta_{\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\right|\ \geq\ \frac{c}{\mathfrak{m}_{\alpha}(h^{\star},P)}\times\Big|\mathbf{1}\{\ell(h^{\star},z)>\theta^{\star}\}-P(\ell(h^{\star},Z)>\theta^{\star})\Big|,

in the sense that the relevant Jacobian block involves the generalized density-at-quantile; hence the influence blows up as

Remark.

Theorems

The influence-function characterization naturally leads to a notion of local robustness: the largest contamination level under which the decision remains within a prescribed tolerance.

Definition 4.9

(Local decision robustness radius)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rob}}(r):=\sup\Big\{\varepsilon\in[0,1):\ \|h_{\varepsilon}-h^{\star}\|\leq r\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \text{contamination directions}\ \Delta_{z}\Big\},

Under Assumption

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rob}}(r)\ \geq\ \frac{r}{C\|J^{-1}\|}\cdot\Bigg(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\Big\|g(z;h^{\star},\theta^{\star})-\mathbb{E}_{P}[g(Z;h^{\star},\theta^{\star})]\Big\|\Bigg)^{-1},

with

Proof.

By Theorem
4.8

is differentiable at

Intrinsic Limits (Tail Scarcity in i.i.d. Data):

CVaR depends on losses exceeding an endogenous threshold
\theta^{\star}(h,P)
determined by the upper
\alpha
-tail. Consequently, the empirical CVaR objective can be dominated by a few rare observations near this threshold, even when the population minimizer is strict and well separated. This effect is structural rather than a concentration artifact: under finite
p
-moment assumptions, tail exceedances occur with polynomial probability and can induce
O(1)
perturbations in the empirical objective. The result below formalizes this
tail-scarcity instability
, showing that, with unavoidable polynomial probability (up to logarithmic factors), a single observation can flip the CVaR-ERM decision despite strict population optimality.

Proposition 4.11
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
1<p<2
.
There exist a distribution
P
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
\mathbb{E}_{P}[Z^{p}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}_{P}[Z^{q}]=\infty
for all
q>p
, and a two-point hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}=\{h_{A},h_{B}\}
with losses

\ell_{n}:\mathcal{H}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
such that for all sufficiently large

,

\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\ell_{n}(h_{A},Z);P)<\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\ell_{n}(h_{B},Z);P),

yet there exists a constant

for which

\displaystyle\Pr_{D\sim P^{\otimes n}}\!\Big(\exists\,D^{\prime}\text{ with }|D\triangle D^{\prime}|=1\text{ for which }\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\widehat{F}_{n}(h;D)\;\neq\;\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\widehat{F}_{n}(h;D^{\prime})\Big)\;\geq\;c\,\frac{n^{-\lambda}}{(\log n)^{2}},

where
\widehat{F}_{n}(h;D)=\mathrm{CVaR}_{\alpha}(\ell_{n}(h,Z);P_{n})
.

Remark:

This result exposes a fundamental limitation of CVaR-ERM under heavy-tailed losses: even with a strict and unique population minimizer and finite
p
-th moments (
1<p<2
), the empirical CVaR decision map is not uniformly stable. With an unavoidable polynomial probability of order

, a single observation can flip the empirical CVaR-ERM solution. The proofs are deferred to Appendix
F
.

5
Conclusion

We developed a learning-theoretic framework for CVaR under heavy-tailed and contaminated data, addressing generalization, robustness, and decision stability. Our analysis identifies the endogenous quantile as a fundamental source of both statistical difficulty and instability, captured via refined empirical-process tools. Together, our results clarify when CVaR learning is reliable and when intrinsic limitations arise, providing principled guidance for risk-sensitive learning in heavy-tailed regimes.

Appendix A
Justification of Assumptions

We clarify the validity and necessity of our theoretical assumptions below:

1.

Heavy-Tailed Moments (Assumption
2.1
):

This assumption relaxes standard requirements for strictly bounded losses or finite variances. By requiring only a finite
(1+\lambda)
-th moment, we accommodate heavy-tailed distributions where the loss may occasionally take very large values (noting that infinite variance is possible if
\lambda<1
). This approach allows the theory to remain applicable to realistic, risk-sensitive settings and aligns with recent literature such as
Aminian et al.
and
Lee et al. 2021
.

2.

\beta
-Mixing Dependence (Assumption
2.2
):

The standard i.i.d. assumption is insufficient for sequential applications such as Reinforcement Learning, financial forecasting, and signal processing. We assume
\beta
-mixing because it offers a crucial balance between modeling flexibility and mathematical tractability. Specifically,
\beta
-mixing facilitates the use of blocking techniques (e.g., Yu’s method) and coupling tools (e.g., Berbee’s Lemma). These tools allow us to decompose the dependent sequence into "nearly independent" blocks, thereby enabling the application of standard concentration inequalities.

3.

Hypothesis Complexity (Assumption
2.3
):

The pseudo-dimension is the natural extension of the Vapnik-Chervonenkis (VC) dimension to real-valued function classes. Assuming a finite pseudo-dimension restricts the "capacity" of the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, which is a standard and necessary condition in statistical learning theory to guarantee uniform convergence and prevent overfitting.

4.

Complexity of Truncated Class (Assumption
2.4
):

Given the heavy-tailed nature of the data (Assumption
2.1
), classical concentration inequalities for bounded variables (such as Hoeffding’s inequality) are not directly applicable. To circumvent this, we employ a truncation argument (capping the loss at
B
). Assumption
2.4
ensures that this truncation operation does not artificially "explode" the complexity of the hypothesis class, ensuring the covering numbers behave well enough to derive generalization bounds.

Further justification for the remaining technical assumptions is provided in the discussion section of the main paper.

Let

Define the

Proof.

By definition of

R_{\alpha}(h)\leq\theta^{*}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}]

Since

R_{\alpha}(h^{*})=\theta^{*}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[(\ell(h^{*},Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}],

we have

R_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}(h^{*})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[f_{h}(Z)].

(36)

Fix
B>0

\displaystyle|f_{h}(Z)-f_{h}^{B}(Z)|

\displaystyle\leq\big|(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}-[(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}]_{B}\big|

\displaystyle\quad+\big|(\ell(h^{*},Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}-[(\ell(h^{*},Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}]_{B}\big|.

For

|x-[x]_{B}|=x\cdot\mathbf{1}\{x>B\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big|(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}-[(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}]_{B}\big|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}>B\}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\ell(h,Z)\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\ell(h,Z)>B\}\big].

Write

\mathbb{E}[\ell(h,Z)\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\ell(h,Z)>B\}]=\mathbb{E}[\ell(h,Z)^{1+\lambda}\cdot\ell(h,Z)^{-\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\ell(h,Z)>B\}].

By Hölder’s inequality with exponents

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell(h,Z)^{1+\lambda}\cdot\ell(h,Z)^{-\lambda}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\ell(h,Z)>B\}]

\displaystyle\leq\big(\mathbb{E}[\ell(h,Z)^{1+\lambda}]\big)^{1/(1+\lambda)}\cdot\big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{\ell(h,Z)>B\}]\big)^{\lambda/(1+\lambda)}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbb{E}[\ell(h,Z)^{1+\lambda}]\big)^{1/(1+\lambda)}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\ell(h,Z)>B)^{\lambda/(1+\lambda)}.

By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(\ell(h,Z)>B)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\ell(h,Z)^{1+\lambda}]}{B^{1+\lambda}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell(h,Z)\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\ell(h,Z)>B\}]

\displaystyle\leq\big(\mathbb{E}[\ell(h,Z)^{1+\lambda}]\big)^{1/(1+\lambda)}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\ell(h,Z)^{1+\lambda}]}{B^{1+\lambda}}\right)^{\lambda/(1+\lambda)}

The same bound holds for

\mathbb{E}|f_{h}(Z)-f_{h}^{B}(Z)|\leq\frac{2M}{B^{\lambda}}.

(37)

Combining (

R_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}(h^{*})\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\mathbb{E}[f_{h}^{B}(Z)]+\frac{2M}{B^{\lambda}}\right).

Using
(a-b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}

\displaystyle(f_{h}^{B}(Z))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\big([(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}]_{B}\big)^{2}+2\big([(\ell(h^{*},Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}]_{B}\big)^{2}.

\displaystyle\big([(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}]_{B}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq B\cdot[(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}]_{B}

For

(by AM-GM or Young’s inequality). Thus,

B\cdot\ell(h,Z)\leq B^{1-\lambda}\cdot\ell(h,Z)^{1+\lambda}.

Taking expectations:

\mathbb{E}\big[\big([(\ell(h,Z)-\theta^{*})_{+}]_{B}\big)^{2}\big]\leq B^{1-\lambda}\mathbb{E}[\ell(h,Z)^{1+\lambda}]\leq MB^{1-\lambda}.

Similarly for
h^{*}

\mathrm{Var}(f_{h}^{B}(Z))\leq 4MB^{1-\lambda}.

(39)

Also,

Define
\mathcal{F}_{B}=\{f_{h}^{B}:h\in\mathcal{H}\}
with pseudo-dimension

2B

By variance-sensitive VC theory (Bartlett-Bousquet-Mendelson)[we need to cite

with probability at least

\mathbb{E}[f_{h}^{B}(Z)]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{h}^{B}(Z_{i})\leq C\left(\sqrt{\frac{MB^{1-\lambda}(d\log n+\log(1/\delta))}{n}}+\frac{B(d\log n+\log(1/\delta))}{n}\right).

\widehat{h}
minimizes
empirical risk.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}[f_{\widehat{h}}^{B}(Z)]\leq C\left(\sqrt{\frac{MB^{1-\lambda}D}{n}}+\frac{BD}{n}\right).

Now we will optimize the truncation level B.
Balance the main terms:

\sqrt{\frac{MB^{1-\lambda}}{n}}\asymp\frac{M}{B^{\lambda}}.

This gives

M^{1/2}B^{(1-\lambda)/2}n^{-1/2}\asymp MB^{-\lambda},

incorporating the logarithmic factor.

B.3.1
Lower bound for i.i.d Data

Proof of Theorem
3.2
,in particular result
6

The sketch of the proof is that :we will construct a finite family
\{P_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
,
|\mathcal{V}|=N
, of distributions in
\mathcal{P}(M,\lambda)
together with hypotheses
\{h_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\subset\mathcal{H}
such that:

1.

For each
v\neq u
the excess CVaR of
h_{u}
under
P_{v}
is at least a positive gap
\Delta
which we will express in terms of
p,t,M,\lambda,\alpha
.

2.

The pairwise KL divergences
\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}\|P_{u})
are
\lesssim p
(a constant times
p
) uniformly in
v,u
.

3.

Applying Fano with the mutual information bound (via averaging of pairwise KLs).

Combining these gives the desired minimax lower bound after enforcing the moment constraint, which links
t
and
p
.

1. Hypercube Construction

Let
m=\lceil\log_{2}N\rceil
. We construct a packing set
\mathcal{V}\subset\{0,1\}^{m}
of size
N
. We select
N
binary vectors
v
of equal Hamming weight
m/2
(assuming
m
is even; odd
m
requires trivial adjustments) such that the pairwise Hamming distance satisfies
d_{H}(u,v)\geq m/4
for all distinct
u,v\in\mathcal{V}
.
The existence of such a set is guaranteed by the Gilbert-Varshamov bound. Specifically, the volume of a Hamming ball of radius
m/8
is exponentially smaller than the set of constant-weight vectors
\binom{m}{m/2}
, allowing us to pack
N
such vectors for
m\approx\log_{2}N
.

For any distinct
u,v\in\mathcal{V}
, let
a_{vu}:=\#\{j:v_{j}=1,u_{j}=0\}
. Since both vectors have weight
m/2
,

a_{vu}=\#\{j:v_{j}=0,u_{j}=1\}=\frac{d_{H}(u,v)}{2}\geq\frac{m}{8}.

We will now define a well-defined probability distribution on
\mathcal{Z}
for each
v\in\mathcal{V}
.

2. Distribution and Hypothesis Definitions

Fix a constant
\theta\in(0,1/4)
. Let the sample space be
\mathcal{Z}=\{0,1,\dots,m\}
. Let
p\in(0,\alpha/2]
be a probability parameter, and
t>0
be a loss magnitude.
For each
v\in\mathcal{V}
, define the distribution
P_{v}
on
\mathcal{Z}
:

\displaystyle P_{v}(0)

\displaystyle=1-p,

\displaystyle P_{v}(j)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{m}\Big(1+2\theta(v_{j}-1/2)\Big),\quad j=1,\dots,m.

Note that
\sum_{j=1}^{m}P_{v}(j)=p
because
\sum v_{j}=m/2
, ensuring
P_{v}
is a valid distribution.
Define the hypotheses
\{h_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
with loss function
\ell
:

\ell(h_{v},0)=0,\qquad\ell(h_{v},j)=t\cdot(1-v_{j}),\quad j=1,\dots,m.

The hypothesis
h_{v}
suffers loss
t
on atom
j
if and only if
v_{j}=0
.

3. CVaR Gap Analysis

The probability that
h_{u}
incurs nonzero loss under
P_{v}
is

\pi_{v,u}:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}P_{v}(j)(1-u_{j}).

Since
P_{v}(j)\leq\frac{p}{m}(1+\theta)
and
\sum(1-u_{j})=m/2
, we have
\pi_{v,u}\leq\frac{p}{2}(1+\theta)
. Since
\theta<1/4
,
\pi_{v,u}<p
.
We enforce
p\leq\alpha/2
. Thus, the probability of a nonzero loss is strictly less than
\alpha
, implying the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile is 0. In this regime, CVaR is simply the expected loss scaled by
1/\alpha
:

R_{\alpha}(h_{u};P_{v})=\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{P_{v}}[\ell(h_{u},Z)].

Lemma B.7

(Expected Losses)
.

For any
v,u\in\mathcal{V}
:

1.

If
u=v
:
\mathbb{E}_{P_{v}}[\ell(h_{v},Z)]=\frac{tp}{2}(1-\theta)
.

2.

If
u\neq v
:
\mathbb{E}_{P_{v}}[\ell(h_{u},Z)]\geq\frac{tp}{2}\left(1-\frac{\theta}{2}\right)
.

Proof.

For the first case, sum over indices where
v_{j}=0
.
For the second case, we utilize the Hamming separation. The term
\sum_{j=1}^{m}(v_{j}-1/2)(1-u_{j})
evaluates to
a_{vu}-m/4
. Since
a_{vu}\geq m/8
, this sum is lower bounded by
-m/8
. Substituting this into the expectation formula yields the result.
∎

Theorem B.8

(CVaR Separation)
.

For any distinct
v,u\in\mathcal{V}
,

\Delta:=R_{\alpha}(h_{u};P_{v})-R_{\alpha}(h_{v};P_{v})\geq\frac{\theta}{4}\cdot\frac{tp}{\alpha}.

Proof.

Subtracting the expectations from the Lemma and dividing by
\alpha
:

\Delta=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\frac{tp}{2}\left(1-\frac{\theta}{2}\right)-\frac{tp}{2}(1-\theta)\right)=\frac{tp}{2\alpha}\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)=\frac{\theta tp}{4\alpha}.

∎

4. Moment Constraint

To ensure
P_{v}\in\mathcal{P}(M,\lambda)
, we require
\mathbb{E}[\ell^{1+\lambda}]\leq M
. Since losses are binary
\{0,t\}
:

\mathbb{E}[\ell^{1+\lambda}]=t^{1+\lambda}\mathbb{P}(\ell=t)\leq t^{1+\lambda}p.

We set
t^{1+\lambda}p=M\implies t=(M/p)^{1/(1+\lambda)}
.
Substituting
t
into the gap
\Delta
:

\Delta\geq\frac{\theta}{4\alpha}M^{1/(1+\lambda)}p^{\lambda/(1+\lambda)}.

5. KL Divergence and Fano’s Inequality

For
v\neq u
, the KL divergence is bounded using the
\chi^{2}
-divergence:

\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}\|P_{u})\leq\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\frac{(P_{v}(z)-P_{u}(z))^{2}}{P_{u}(z)}.

Using the bounds on
P_{v}(j)
, we derived:

\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}\|P_{u})\leq\frac{4\theta^{2}}{1-\theta}p.

For
n
i.i.d. samples,
\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}^{n}\|P_{u}^{n})\leq n\frac{4\theta^{2}}{1-\theta}p
.
By Fano’s inequality, to ensure the error probability
\mathbb{P}(\widehat{V}\neq V)\geq 1/2
, it suffices to bound the mutual information
I(V;X^{n})\leq\frac{1}{2}\log N
(assuming
\log N\geq 2\log 2
). This is satisfied if:

n\frac{4\theta^{2}}{1-\theta}p\leq\frac{1}{8}\log N\implies p\leq\frac{1-\theta}{32\theta^{2}}\frac{\log N}{n}.

We define
p
to satisfy both the Fano condition and the CVaR regime condition (
p\leq\alpha/2
):

p:=\min\left\{\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{1-\theta}{32\theta^{2}}\frac{\log N}{n}\right\}.

The sample size condition
n\geq\frac{1-\theta}{16\theta^{2}}\frac{\log N}{\alpha}
ensures that the second term is the minimum. Thus, we set
p=\frac{1-\theta}{32\theta^{2}}\frac{\log N}{n}
.

6. Final Lower Bound

For any algorithm
A
, let
\widehat{V}
be the index of the closest hypothesis to
A(S)
.

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}[\text{Excess Risk}]\geq\mathbb{P}(\widehat{V}\neq V)\cdot\Delta\geq\frac{1}{2}\Delta.

Substituting the chosen
p
into
\Delta
:

\frac{1}{2}\Delta=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\theta M^{1/(1+\lambda)}}{4\alpha}\left(\frac{1-\theta}{32\theta^{2}}\frac{\log N}{n}\right)^{\lambda/(1+\lambda)}.

Rearranging terms yields the claimed bound with
c(\theta,\lambda)=\frac{\theta}{8}(\frac{1-\theta}{32\theta^{2}})^{\lambda/(1+\lambda)}
.

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(\widehat{h}_{B})-R_{\alpha}(h^{*})

\displaystyle=R_{\alpha}(\widehat{h}_{B})-R^{B}_{\alpha}(\widehat{h}_{B})+R^{B}_{\alpha}(\widehat{h}_{B})-R^{B}_{\alpha}(h^{*})+R^{B}_{\alpha}(h^{*})-R_{\alpha}(h^{*})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(R_{\alpha}(\widehat{h}_{B})-R^{B}_{\alpha}(\widehat{h}_{B})\right)}_{\text{Bias }>0}+\underbrace{\left(R^{B}_{\alpha}(\widehat{h}_{B})-R^{B}_{\alpha}(h^{*})\right)}_{\text{Estimation}}+\underbrace{\left(R^{B}_{\alpha}(h^{*})-R_{\alpha}(h^{*})\right)}_{\leq 0}.

The third term is non-positive, so we remove it from the upper bound.
The estimation term is bounded by
2\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|R^{B}_{\alpha}(h)-\widehat{R}^{B}_{\alpha}(h)|
. Thus:

R_{\alpha}(\widehat{h}_{B})-R_{\alpha}(h^{*})\leq\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}(R_{\alpha}(h)-R^{B}_{\alpha}(h))+2\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|R^{B}_{\alpha}(h)-\widehat{R}^{B}_{\alpha}(h)|.

(41)

We bound the error introduced by truncating the loss distribution. Using the variational definition and the fact that CVaR is
\frac{1}{\alpha}
-Lipschitz w.r.t the
L_{1}
norm:

\displaystyle\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}(R_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}^{B}(h))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(h,Z)-\ell_{B}(h,Z)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left[(\ell(h,Z)-B)\mathbb{I}(\ell(h,Z)>B)\right].

Using the integral identity
\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X>t)dt
:

\mathbb{E}[(\ell-B)_{+}]=\int_{B}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\ell(h,Z)>t)dt.

(42)

By Markov’s inequality on the
(1+\lambda)
-moment (Assumption
2.1
),
\mathbb{P}(\ell>t)\leq\frac{M}{t^{1+\lambda}}
. Integrating this tail:

\int_{B}^{\infty}\frac{M}{t^{1+\lambda}}dt=M\left[\frac{t^{-\lambda}}{-\lambda}\right]_{B}^{\infty}=\frac{M}{\lambda B^{\lambda}}.

(43)

Thus, we obtain the rigorous bias bound:

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}(R_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}^{B}(h))\leq\frac{M}{\alpha\lambda B^{\lambda}}.

(44)

Now we will bound the Estimation Error. We control the uniform deviation
Z_{n}=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|R^{B}_{\alpha}(h)-\widehat{R}^{B}_{\alpha}(h)|
. Define the function class associated with the variational CVaR objective:

\mathcal{G}_{B}=\left\{z\mapsto\phi_{h,\theta}(z):=\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha}(\ell_{B}(h,z)-\theta)_{+}\;\middle|\;h\in\mathcal{H},\theta\in[0,B]\right\}.

(45)

We apply
Bousquet’s concentration inequality
for the supremum of empirical processes.For this we will now check the Conditions for Bousquet’s Inequality.

1. Tight Uniform Upper Bound (
K
):

For any
g\in\mathcal{G}_{B}
, since
\ell_{B}\in[0,B]
and
\theta\in[0,B]
:

0\leq\phi_{h,\theta}(z)\leq\sup_{\theta\in[0,B]}\left(\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha}(B-\theta)\right)=\frac{B}{\alpha}.

(46)

(The maximum occurs at
\theta=0
since
\alpha\leq 1
). Thus, we set
K=B/\alpha
.

2. Variance Bound (
\sigma^{2}
):

The variance of
\phi_{h,\theta}(Z)
depends only on the random part
\frac{1}{\alpha}(\ell_{B}(h,Z)-\theta)_{+}
.

\text{Var}(\phi_{h,\theta})=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\text{Var}\left((\ell_{B}-\theta)_{+}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\ell_{B}-\theta)_{+}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\ell_{B}^{2}].

(47)

Using the inequality
x^{2}\leq x^{1+\lambda}B^{1-\lambda}
for
x\in[0,B]
and Assumption
2.1
:

\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}_{B}}\mathbb{E}[g^{2}]\leq\frac{MB^{1-\lambda}}{\alpha^{2}}.

(48)

By Bousquet’s inequality, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

Z_{n}\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]+\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{K\log(1/\delta)}{3n}.

(49)

Bounding the Expectation (Rademacher Complexity):

The class
\mathcal{G}_{B}
involves a supremum over
\theta
. Since the objective is convex in
\theta
and
1/\alpha
-Lipschitz in
\ell_{B}
, standard contraction results (e.g., Levy et al., 2020) imply:

\mathbb{E}[Z_{n}]\leq 2\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{G}_{B})\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\ell\circ\mathcal{H})+O(n^{-1/2}).

(50)

Substituting the bounds for
\sigma^{2}
and
K
into Bousquet’s inequality:

Z_{n}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\ell\circ\mathcal{H})+\sqrt{\frac{2MB^{1-\lambda}\log(1/\delta)}{\alpha^{2}n}}+\frac{B\log(1/\delta)}{3\alpha n}.

(51)

The total estimation error contribution is
2Z_{n}
:

\text{Est}\leq\frac{4}{\alpha}\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\ell\circ\mathcal{H})+\frac{1}{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{8MB^{1-\lambda}\log(1/\delta)}{n}}+\frac{2B\log(1/\delta)}{3\alpha n}.

(52)

We minimize the total error bound (Bias + Estimation) with respect to
B
:

\text{Error}(B)\approx\frac{M}{\alpha\lambda B^{\lambda}}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{8MB^{1-\lambda}\log(1/\delta)}{n}}.

(53)

Balancing the order of bias (
B^{-\lambda}
) and variance (
B^{(1-\lambda)/2}n^{-1/2}
) yields the optimal scaling:

n\asymp B^{1+\lambda}\implies B=(Mn)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}.

(54)

We define the rate factor
\Delta_{n}=M^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}n^{-\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}
. Substituting
B
back into the terms:

1.

Bias Term:

\frac{M}{\alpha\lambda(Mn)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}}=\frac{1}{\alpha\lambda}\Delta_{n}.

2.

Variance Term (Bousquet Main):

\frac{1}{\alpha}\sqrt{8\log(1/\delta)}\sqrt{\frac{M(Mn)^{\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}}}{n}}=\frac{\sqrt{8\log(1/\delta)}}{\alpha}\Delta_{n}.

3.

Variance Term (Bousquet Linear):

Note that
\frac{B}{n}=\frac{(Mn)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}}{n}=\Delta_{n}
.

\frac{2B\log(1/\delta)}{3\alpha n}=\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{3\alpha}\Delta_{n}.

Summing the coefficients gives the final constant
C_{\lambda,\delta}
:

C_{\lambda,\delta}=\frac{1}{\lambda}+\sqrt{8\log(1/\delta)}+\frac{2}{3}\log(1/\delta).

(55)

This completes the proof of Theorem
3.3

∎

Proof.

of Upper Bound
3.4

For any
h
, by the variational form of CVaR and the Lipschitz property

|R_{\alpha}(X)-R_{\alpha}(Y)|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}|X-Y|
(Lemma
G.8
),

\sup_{h}|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}(h)|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H},\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\widehat{L}_{\alpha}(h,\theta)-L_{\alpha}(h,\theta)\bigr|.

(56)

where
\widehat{L}_{\alpha}(h,\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\ell(h,Z_{i})-\theta)_{+}
,

L_{\alpha}(h,\theta)=\mathbb{E}[(\ell(h,Z)-\theta)_{+}]
.

From the moment condition, any population CVaR minimizer
\theta_{h}^{*}
satisfies

0\leq\theta_{h}^{*}\leq R:=M^{1/(1+\lambda)}/\alpha
(Theorem
G.1
).
Hence we may restrict
\theta
to
\Theta=[0,R]
.

Fix
B>0
and define the truncated loss class

\mathcal{F}_{B}=\bigl\{f_{h,\theta}^{B}(z)=[(\ell(h,z)-\theta)_{+}]\wedge B\;:\;h\in\mathcal{H},\,\theta\in\Theta\bigr\}.

(57)

Using Markov and Hölder inequalities,

\sup_{h,\theta}\bigl|L_{\alpha}(h,\theta)-\mathbb{E}[f_{h,\theta}^{B}(Z)]\bigr|\leq\frac{M}{B^{\lambda}}.

(58)

Thus,

\sup_{h,\theta}|\widehat{L}_{\alpha}(h,\theta)-L_{\alpha}(h,\theta)|\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\bigl|\tfrac{1}{n}\sum f(Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}f\bigr|+\frac{2M}{B^{\lambda}}.

(59)

Assume the process
(Z_{i})
is
\beta
-mixing with exponential decay

\beta(k)\leq\exp(-ck^{\gamma})
for some
\gamma>0
.
Choose
a_{n}\asymp\log n
,
b_{n}\asymp(\log n)^{1/\gamma}
and partition
\{1,\dots,n\}

into
\mu_{n}\asymp n/\log n
blocks of size
a_{n}
separated by gaps of size
b_{n}
.
Let
N=\mu_{n}\asymp n/\log n
.

Define block sums
S_{j}(f)=\sum_{i\in B_{j}}f(Z_{i})
. By Berbee’s lemma, there exist
independent blocks
\tilde{B}_{1},\dots,\tilde{B}_{N}
with the same marginals such that

\mathbb{P}((B_{j})\neq(\tilde{B}_{j}))\leq N\beta(b_{n})\leq n^{-O(1)}
.
Hence, with probability
\geq 1-n^{-O(1)}
,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\bigl|\tfrac{1}{n}\sum f(Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}f\bigr|\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\Bigl|\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{a_{n}}\tilde{S}_{j}(f)-\mathbb{E}f\bigr)\Bigr|.

(60)

Define the centered block variables

\tilde{Z}_{j}(f)=\tfrac{1}{a_{n}}\tilde{S}_{j}(f)-\mathbb{E}f.

Then
\tilde{Z}_{j}(f)
are i.i.d. across
j
, mean zero, bounded by
B
, and satisfy

\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{Z}_{j}(f)|^{1+\lambda}]\leq C_{\lambda}B^{1+\lambda}

by the von Bahr-Esseen inequality.
The class
\mathcal{F}_{B}
has pseudo-dimension

\mathrm{Pdim}(\mathcal{F}_{B})\leq C(d+1)
(Lemma
G.9
).

Applying the heavy-tailed uniform deviation inequality for i.i.d. data
(Theorem
3.2
) with envelope
B
and moment bound
B^{1+\lambda}
,
we obtain that with probability
\geq 1-\delta
,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\bigl|\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\tilde{Z}_{j}(f)\bigr|\leq C_{\lambda}\,B\Bigl(\frac{d\log N+\log(1/\delta)}{N}\Bigr)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}.

(61)

Combining the bounds, with probability

\sup_{h}|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}(h)|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigl[C_{\lambda}B\Bigl(\frac{d\log n+\log(1/\delta)}{N}\Bigr)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}+\frac{2M}{B^{\lambda}}\Bigr].

(62)

Choose
B
to balance the two terms:

B\asymp M^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\Bigl(\frac{N}{d\log n+\log(1/\delta)}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}.

(63)

Substituting back yields the dominant scaling

\sup_{h}|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}(h)|\leq\frac{C_{\lambda,\gamma}}{\alpha}\,M^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\Bigl(\frac{d\log n+\log(1/\delta)}{N}\Bigr)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}.

(64)

Recalling
N\asymp n/\log n
gives the first claim.
For the empirical CVaR minimizer
\widehat{h}
, by the usual ERM argument,

R_{\alpha}(\widehat{h})-R_{\alpha}(h^{*})\leq 2\sup_{h}|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}(h)|.

(65)

which yields the second inequality.
∎

Proof.

of Lower Bound
3.4

Let
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be drawn from any
P\in\mathcal{P}(\lambda,M,\beta)
.
Using the blocking scheme similar to Theorem
3.4
, we partition

\{1,\dots,n\}
into
\mu_{n}
disjoint blocks of size
a_{n}\asymp\log n
,
separated by gaps of length
b_{n}\asymp(\log n)^{1/\gamma}
.
Let
N:=\mu_{n}\asymp n/\log n
denote the number of retained blocks.

By Berbee’s coupling, there exist independent blocks

\tilde{B}_{1},\dots,\tilde{B}_{N}
with the same marginals as the original blocks
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigl((B_{1},\dots,B_{N})\neq(\tilde{B}_{1},\dots,\tilde{B}_{N})\bigr)\leq N\beta(b_{n})\leq n^{-A}

for any fixed
A>0
and all sufficiently large
n
.

Therefore, for minimax lower bounds, it suffices to work with the independent
block model: any estimator based on the original data induces an estimator
based on
(\tilde{B}_{1},\dots,\tilde{B}_{N})
whose risk differs by at most

n^{-A}\sup_{h}R_{\alpha}(h)
, which is negligible compared to the target rate.

Henceforth, we assume we observe
N
i.i.d. samples.

Since
\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal{H})\geq d
, there exist points

z_{1},\dots,z_{d}\in\mathcal{Z}
and hypotheses

h_{1},\dots,h_{d}\in\mathcal{H}
shattered by
\mathcal{H}
.
Using the same construction we had in the proof
B.3.1
, we construct a family of

2^{d}
distributions
\{P_{v}:v\in\{0,1\}^{d}\}
and hypotheses

\{h_{v}:v\in\{0,1\}^{d}\}
such that:

1.

For all
u\neq v
,

R_{\alpha}(h_{u};P_{v})-R_{\alpha}(h_{v};P_{v})\geq\frac{\theta}{4\alpha}tp.

2.

The heavy-tail condition is satisfied:

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{v}}[\ell(h,Z)^{1+\lambda}]\leq t^{1+\lambda}p\leq M.

3.

The pairwise Kullback–Leibler divergences are controlled:

\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}\|P_{u})\leq C_{\theta}p,\qquad\forall u\neq v,

where
C_{\theta}>0
depends only on
\theta
.

Let
V
be uniformly distributed over
\{0,1\}^{d}
and
let
X^{N}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N})
be drawn from
P_{V}^{\otimes N}
.

By the chain rule and the KL bound,

I(V;X^{N})\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{u,v}\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}^{\otimes N}\|P_{u}^{\otimes N})\leq N\max_{u\neq v}\mathrm{KL}(P_{v}\|P_{u})\leq C_{\theta}Np.

Choose

p=c_{1}\frac{d}{N}

for
c_{1}>0
sufficiently small so that

I(V;X^{N})\leq\frac{d}{8}
.

By Fano’s inequality,

\inf_{\widehat{V}}\mathbb{P}(\widehat{V}\neq V)\geq 1-\frac{I(V;X^{N})+\log 2}{\log(2^{d})}\geq\frac{1}{2}

for all sufficiently large
d
.

Let
\widehat{h}=\widehat{h}(X^{N})
be any estimator and define

\widehat{V}
such that
\widehat{h}=h_{\widehat{V}}
(ties broken arbitrarily).
Then

\displaystyle\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\!\left[R_{\alpha}(\widehat{h})-R_{\alpha}(h^{*}_{P})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{v}\mathbb{E}_{P_{v}}\left[R_{\alpha}(h_{\widehat{V}};P_{v})-R_{\alpha}(h_{v};P_{v})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(\widehat{V}\neq V)\cdot\frac{\theta}{4\alpha}tp

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\theta}{8\alpha}tp.

Finally, enforce the moment constraint with equality:

t=\left(\frac{M}{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}.

Substituting gives

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\!\left[R_{\alpha}(\widehat{h})-R_{\alpha}(h^{*}_{P})\right]\geq\frac{c}{\alpha}M^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}p^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}=\frac{c}{\alpha}M^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\Bigl(\frac{d}{N}\Bigr)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}.

Recalling
N\asymp n/\log n
completes the proof.

Appendix C
Random active-set theory and uniform Bahadur-Kiefer expansions for CVaR

Setup and notation.

Let
Z\sim P
on
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{A})
and let
\ell:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be measurable.
Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
. For each
h\in\mathcal{H}
define the nonnegative loss random variable

X_{h}:=\ell(h,Z)\geq 0.

For a scalar threshold
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, define the Rockafellar-Uryasev (RU) lift

\Phi(h,\theta):=\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}\big[(X_{h}-\theta)_{+}\big].

(66)

Given i.i.d. data
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\sim P
and the empirical measure
P_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Z_{i}}
,
let
X_{h,i}:=\ell(h,Z_{i})
and define the empirical RU lift

\widehat{\Phi}_{n}(h,\theta):=\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{h,i}-\theta)_{+}.

(67)

The population and empirical CVaR objectives are

R_{\alpha}(h):=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\Phi(h,\theta),\qquad\widehat{R}_{\alpha,n}(h):=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\Phi}_{n}(h,\theta).

(68)

Endogenous threshold selections (minimal RU minimizers).

Define the (minimal) population RU threshold and the (minimal) empirical RU threshold, respectively, by

\theta^{\star}(h):=\inf\big\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}:\ P(X_{h}>\theta)\leq\alpha\big\},\qquad\widehat{\theta}_{n}(h):=\inf\big\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}:\ P_{n}(X_{h}>\theta)\leq\alpha\big\}.

(69)

It is standard that
\widehat{\theta}_{n}(h)
is always a minimizer of
\theta\mapsto\widehat{\Phi}_{n}(h,\theta)
,
and similarly
\theta^{\star}(h)
is a minimizer of
\theta\mapsto\Phi(h,\theta)
; we use these minimal
selections to keep the arguments deterministic and monotone.

Tail maps and empirical tail maps.

For
h\in\mathcal{H}
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, define the (strict) tail probability and its empirical counterpart:

T_{h}(\theta):=P(X_{h}>\theta),\qquad\widehat{T}_{h,n}(\theta):=P_{n}(X_{h}>\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{X_{h,i}>\theta\}.

(70)

Each
T_{h}(\cdot)
and
\widehat{T}_{h,n}(\cdot)
is nonincreasing and right-continuous. By construction,

\widehat{T}_{h,n}\big(\widehat{\theta}_{n}(h)\big)\leq\alpha,\qquad\text{and if }\theta<\widehat{\theta}_{n}(h)\text{ then }\widehat{T}_{h,n}(\theta)>\alpha.

(71)

We are now reiterating our assumptions to make this section self-contained.

Assumption A (uniform tail empirical process control).

There exists a function
\varepsilon_{n}:(0,1)\to(0,\infty)
such that for all
\delta\in(0,1)
,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\widehat{T}_{h,n}(\theta)-T_{h}(\theta)\big|\leq\varepsilon_{n}(\delta).

(72)

Define the corresponding event

\mathcal{E}_{1}(\delta):=\Big\{\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\widehat{T}_{h,n}(\theta)-T_{h}(\theta)\big|\leq\varepsilon_{n}(\delta)\Big\}.

(73)

Assumption B (local quantile margin at level
\alpha
).

There exist constants
u_{0}>0
,
\kappa\geq 1
, and
0<c_{-}\leq c_{+}<\infty
such that for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
and all

u\in(0,u_{0}]
,

\alpha+c_{-}u^{\kappa}\ \leq\ T_{h}\big(\theta^{\star}(h)-u\big)\ \leq\ \alpha+c_{+}u^{\kappa},

(74)

and

\alpha-c_{+}u^{\kappa}\ \leq\ T_{h}\big(\theta^{\star}(h)+u\big)\ \leq\ \alpha-c_{-}u^{\kappa}.

(75)

This is the standard “two-sided quantile margin” formulation centered at the target level

Assumption C (uniform hinge empirical process at the population threshold).

There exists a function
\eta_{n}:(0,1)\to(0,\infty)

\delta\in(0,1)

with probability at least
1-\delta

\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\Big|(P_{n}-P)\big[(X_{h}-\theta^{\star}(h))_{+}\big]\Big|\leq\eta_{n}(\delta).

(76)

Define the corresponding event

\mathcal{E}_{2}(\delta):=\Big\{\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\Big|(P_{n}-P)\big[(X_{h}-\theta^{\star}(h))_{+}\big]\Big|\leq\eta_{n}(\delta)\Big\}.

(77)

A convenient threshold-deviation envelope.

Whenever
\varepsilon_{n}(\delta)\leq(c_{-}/2)\,u_{0}^{\kappa}

(78)

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\mathrm{Var}(f(Z))\leq M_{\phi}B^{1-\lambda}.

By Assumption
2.4
, the
L_{2}(Q)
covering numbers of
\mathcal{F}_{B}
satisfy

\log\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{F}_{B},\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}(Q)},u)\leq d\log(C_{0}B/u).

Therefore, by Bousquet’s version of Talagrand’s inequality combined with standard entropy integral bounds,for i.i.d. samples we obtain

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(f(Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}f)\right|\leq C\left(\sqrt{\frac{M_{\phi}B^{1-\lambda}d\log m}{m}}+\frac{Bd\log m}{m}\right)

with probability at least
1-0.1
. By Hoeffding’s reduction principle, the same bound holds for sampling without replacement from the clean data.
∎

(b) Control of the corruption level.

Since the adversary is oblivious and the learner shuffles the data uniformly at random, the number of corrupted points in block
j
satisfies

N_{j}\sim\mathrm{Hypergeo}(n,\epsilon n,m).

Lemma E.3

(Outlier proportion in a block)
.

Assume
\epsilon\leq 1/2-\gamma
. Then for all sufficiently large
m
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{N_{j}}{m}\leq\epsilon+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)\geq 1-0.1.

Proof.

By Chvátal’s hypergeometric tail bound,

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{j}\geq(\epsilon+\gamma/4)m\right)\leq\exp\left(-2m(\gamma/4)^{2}\right).

For
m\geq C/\gamma^{2}
, the right-hand side is at most
0.1
.
∎

(c) Corruption bias bound.

On the event of Lemma
E.3
, since
0\leq\phi^{B}\leq B
,

\sup_{h,\theta}|\Delta_{j}(h,\theta)|\leq\frac{N_{j}}{m}B\leq\left(\epsilon+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)B.

(d) Conclusion for a single block.

Combining Lemmas
E.2
and
E.3
, with probability at least
0.8
a block satisfies simultaneously:

\sup_{h,\theta}|\widehat{\mu}_{j}(h,\theta)-R^{B}(h,\theta)|\leq\mathcal{E}_{stat}(B)+\left(\epsilon+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)B.

Such a block will be called
Good
.

Robust Aggregation via the Median.

Recall from the previous step that for each block
j
we defined the events

\mathcal{E}_{j}^{stat}=\Big\{\sup_{h,\theta}|\xi_{j}(h,\theta)|\leq\mathcal{E}_{stat}(B)\Big\},\quad\mathcal{E}_{j}^{corr}=\Big\{\frac{N_{j}}{m}\leq\epsilon+\frac{\gamma}{4}\Big\}.

A block
j
is called
Good
if
\mathcal{E}_{j}^{stat}\cap\mathcal{E}_{j}^{corr}
holds.

From Lemmas
E.2
and
E.3
, by the union bound,

\mathbb{P}(\text{block }j\text{ is Good})\geq 1-(0.1+0.1)=0.8.

Define the indicator variables

I_{j}=\mathbb{I}_{\{\text{block }j\text{ is Good}\}},\quad j=1,\dots,K.

Lemma E.4

(Majority of blocks are good)
.

Assume
\epsilon\leq 1/2-\gamma
. There exists a universal constant
c>0
such that if

K\geq\frac{8}{\gamma^{2}}\log\!\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right),

then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sum_{j=1}^{K}I_{j}>\frac{K}{2}.

Proof.

The indicators
I_{j}
are functions of a random partition of a finite population. They form a negatively associated family. For negatively associated Bernoulli random variables, Chernoff-Hoeffding inequalities hold in the same form as for independent variables (see Dubhashi and Ranjan, 1998). Since
\mathbb{E}[I_{j}]\geq 0.8
, Hoeffding’s inequality implies

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}I_{j}\leq\frac{K}{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-2K(0.8-0.5)^{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma^{2}K}{2}\right).

Choosing
K\geq\frac{8}{\gamma^{2}}\log(4/\delta)
ensures the right-hand side is at most
\delta
.
∎

On the event of Lemma
E.4
, strictly more than half the blocks are Good. For any Good block
j
, we have simultaneously:

\sup_{h,\theta}|\xi_{j}(h,\theta)|\leq\mathcal{E}_{stat}(B),\quad\sup_{h,\theta}|\Delta_{j}(h,\theta)|\leq\left(\epsilon+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)B.

Hence,

\sup_{h,\theta}|\widehat{\mu}_{j}(h,\theta)-R^{B}(h,\theta)|\leq\mathcal{E}_{stat}(B)+\left(\epsilon+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)B.

Since the median of
K
numbers lies between the minimum and maximum of any subset of more than
K/2
elements, it follows deterministically that

\sup_{h,\theta}|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h,\theta)-R^{B}(h,\theta)|\leq\max_{j:\,I_{j}=1}|\widehat{\mu}_{j}(h,\theta)-R^{B}(h,\theta)|\leq\mathcal{E}_{stat}(B)+\left(\epsilon+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)B.

Final Error Bound and Balancing.

Combining previous step with the truncation bias bound from Lemma
G.10
, we obtain that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{h,\theta}|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h,\theta)-R(h,\theta)|\leq M_{\phi}B^{-\lambda}+\mathcal{E}_{stat}(B)+\left(\epsilon+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)B.

Recalling the definition

\mathcal{E}_{stat}(B)=C\left(\sqrt{\frac{M_{\phi}B^{1-\lambda}d\log m}{m}}+\frac{Bd\log m}{m}\right),

and using
m\asymp n/K
with
K=O(\gamma^{-2}\log(1/\delta))
, we may rewrite the bound (absorbing constants) as:

\text{Err}(B)\;\lesssim\;M_{\phi}B^{-\lambda}+\sqrt{\frac{M_{\phi}B^{1-\lambda}d\log n}{n}}+\frac{Bd\log n}{n}+\epsilon B.

Assuming
n\gtrsim d\log n
, the linear term
\frac{Bd\log n}{n}
is of smaller order than the variance term under the optimal choice of
B
and may be absorbed. We therefore balance the remaining three dominant terms.

(i) Statistical regime.

Balancing bias and variance,

M_{\phi}B^{-\lambda}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{M_{\phi}B^{1-\lambda}d}{n}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad B_{stat}\asymp\left(\frac{M_{\phi}n}{d}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}.

Substituting yields:

\text{Err}\;\lesssim\;\left(\frac{M_{\phi}d}{n}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}.

(ii) Adversarial regime.

Balancing bias and corruption,

M_{\phi}B^{-\lambda}\asymp\epsilon B\quad\Longrightarrow\quad B_{adv}\asymp\left(\frac{M_{\phi}}{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}.

Substituting yields:

\text{Err}\;\lesssim\;M_{\phi}^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\epsilon^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}.

Taking
B=\min(B_{stat},B_{adv})
and recalling that

\sup_{h}|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)-R_{\alpha}(h)|\leq\sup_{h,\theta}|\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h,\theta)-R(h,\theta)|,

we conclude the proof of Theorem
4.3
.
∎

G.1
Boundedness of CVaR minimizer under a bounded moment

Theorem G.1

(Boundedness of CVaR minimizer under a bounded moment)
.

Let
l\geq 0
be a nonnegative random variable on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
satisfying

\mathbb{E}\big[l^{1+\varepsilon}\big]\leq M<\infty

for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
M>0
. Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and define for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}

F(\theta):=\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}\big[(l-\theta)_{+}\big],\qquad(x)_{+}:=\max\{x,0\}.

Then
F
attains its minimum on
\mathbb{R}
, and every minimizer
\theta^{\ast}\in\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}F(\theta)
satisfies

0\leq\theta^{\ast}\leq R,

where one may take

R=\frac{M^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}}{\alpha}.

In particular, the set of minimizers is nonempty and bounded, contained in
[0,R]
.

Proof.

We divide the argument into steps: finiteness and continuity of
F
, behavior as
\theta\to\pm\infty
(coercivity), nonnegativity of minimizers, and the explicit upper bound.

(A)
F
is finite and convex.

For any fixed
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
we have the pointwise inequality

(l-\theta)_{+}\leq l+|\theta|.

Since
\mathbb{E}[l]\leq(\mathbb{E}[l^{1+\varepsilon}])^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}\leq M^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}<\infty
(see (C) below), it follows that

\mathbb{E}[(l-\theta)_{+}]\leq\mathbb{E}[l]+|\theta|<\infty,

so
F(\theta)
is finite for every
\theta
. For each fixed
\omega\in\Omega
the map
\theta\mapsto(l(\omega)-\theta)_{+}
is convex (it is the positive part of an affine function), and expectation preserves convexity. Therefore
F
is convex on
\mathbb{R}
.

A convex function that is finite on all of
\mathbb{R}
is continuous (hence lower semicontinuous). Thus
F
is continuous and finite-valued on
\mathbb{R}
.

(B) Coercivity:
F(\theta)\to+\infty
as
|\theta|\to\infty
.

First, for every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
,

F(\theta)=\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[(l-\theta)_{+}]\geq\theta,

because
(l-\theta)_{+}\geq 0
. Hence as
\theta\to+\infty
,
F(\theta)\geq\theta\to+\infty
.

Next, for
\theta\leq 0
we have
l-\theta\geq l\geq 0
(since
l\geq 0
), so
(l-\theta)_{+}=l-\theta
. Thus for
\theta\leq 0

F(\theta)=\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[l-\theta]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[l]}{\alpha}+\theta\Big(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\Big).

Because
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the coefficient
1-1/\alpha
is negative, and therefore as
\theta\to-\infty
the term
\theta(1-1/\alpha)\to+\infty
. Hence
F(\theta)\to+\infty
as
\theta\to-\infty
.

Combining the two directions shows
F(\theta)\to+\infty
as
|\theta|\to\infty
; therefore
F
is coercive.

(C) Existence of a minimizer.

Since
F
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and coercive (tends to
+\infty
at
\pm\infty
), it attains its minimum on
\mathbb{R}
. Thus the set
\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}F(\theta)
is nonempty.
( lower semi-continuity and coercivity imply existence of a minimizer.)

(D) No minimizer is negative.

For
\theta\leq 0
we have the formula

F(\theta)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[l]}{\alpha}+\theta\Big(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\Big).

Evaluating at
\theta=0
gives
F(0)=\mathbb{E}[l]/\alpha
. For any
\theta<0
,

F(\theta)-F(0)=\theta\Big(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\Big).

Since
1-1/\alpha<0
and
\theta<0
, we have
F(\theta)-F(0)>0
, hence
F(\theta)>F(0)
. Thus no
\theta<0
can be a global minimizer, and every minimizer satisfies
\theta^{\ast}\geq 0
.

(E) Upper bound on
\mathbb{E}[l]
.

Using Hölder’s inequality (or the monotonicity of
L^{p}
-norms on a probability space) with
p=1+\varepsilon>1
we obtain

\mathbb{E}[l]\leq\big(\mathbb{E}[l^{1+\varepsilon}]\big)^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}\leq M^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}.

Consequently

F(0)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[l]}{\alpha}\leq\frac{M^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}}{\alpha}.

(F) Upper bound on minimizers.

For any
\theta\geq 0
we have
F(\theta)\geq\theta
. If
\theta>F(0)
then
\theta>F(0)
implies
F(\theta)\geq\theta>F(0)
, so such a
\theta
cannot be a minimizer. Therefore every minimizer
\theta^{\ast}
satisfies
\theta^{\ast}\leq F(0)
. Combining this with
(*)
yields

0\leq\theta^{\ast}\leq F(0)\leq\frac{M^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}}{\alpha}.

Hence one may take
R=M^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}/\alpha
.

We have shown:
F
is finite, continuous and coercive, so it attains a minimum; moreover every minimizer satisfies
0\leq\theta^{\ast}\leq R
with
R=M^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}/\alpha
. This completes the boundedness of the minimizer proof.
∎

G.2
Concentration bound for heavy-tailed random variables

Proposition G.2
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) non-negative random variables satisfying the moment condition
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{1+\lambda}]\leq M
for some constants
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
M>0
. Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the sample mean satisfies

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\leq 2M^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\left(\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}.

This result is adapted from
Brownlees et al. 2015
, which provides concentration bounds for empirical risk minimization under heavy-tailed losses.

Proof.

To prove this theorem, we will use a truncation approach combined with concentration inequalities for bounded random variables. The key idea is to control the large values of the random variables by truncating them at a carefully chosen level and then applying standard concentration bounds to the truncated variables while accounting for the probability of truncation.

Since the random variables
X_{i}
are non-negative and potentially heavy-tailed, their large values can dominate the behavior of the sample mean. To handle this, we introduce a truncation level
B>0
and define the truncated random variables as:

X_{i}^{B}=\min(X_{i},B)=X_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{i}\leq B\}}+B\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{i}>B\}}.

Thus,
X_{i}^{B}
is equal to
X_{i}
when
X_{i}\leq B
and equal to
B
otherwise. Note that
0\leq X_{i}^{B}\leq B
, so
X_{i}^{B}
is bounded.

Let
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
be the sum of the original random variables, and let
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
be the common expected value. We can write the sample mean as:

\frac{S_{n}}{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{B}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{B})

(130)

Since
X_{i}-X_{i}^{B}=(X_{i}-B)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{i}>B\}}\geq 0
, it follows that:

\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\mu\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{B}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{B}]\right)+(\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{B}]-\mu)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{B}).

(131)

However, since
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{B}]\leq\mu
, the term
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{B}]-\mu\leq 0
, so we can drop it to obtain:

\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\mu\leq\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{B}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{B}]\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{B})

(132)

We aim to bound the probability:

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\mu>t\right)

for some
t>0
. Using the decomposition above, we have:

Proof.

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\mu>t\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{B}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{B}]\right)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{B})>t\right)

∎

.

Since
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{B})\geq 0
, this probability is further bounded by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{B}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{B}]>t-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{B})\right).

(133)

Let
A=\{\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}X_{i}\leq B\}
. On
A
, we have
X_{i}=X_{i}^{B}
for all
i
, so
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{B})=0
. On
A^{c}
, at least one
X_{i}>B
. Therefore:

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\mu>t\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{B}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{B}]>t,A\right)+\mathbb{P}(A^{c})

(134)

On
A
,
S_{n}/n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{B}
, hence:

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{S_{n}}{n}-\mu>t\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{B}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{B}]>t\right)+\mathbb{P}(A^{c})

(135)

Bounding
\mathbb{P}(A^{c})

\mathbb{P}(A^{c})=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{X_{i}>B\}\right)\leq n\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{1}>B)

(136)

Using Markov’s inequality and the moment condition:

\mathbb{P}(X_{1}>B)=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}^{1+\lambda}>B^{1+\lambda})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{1+\lambda}]}{B^{1+\lambda}}\leq\frac{M}{B^{1+\lambda}}

(137)

Thus:

\mathbb{P}(A^{c})\leq n\cdot\frac{M}{B^{1+\lambda}}.

Choose
B
such that
n\cdot\frac{M}{B^{1+\lambda}}=\frac{\delta}{2}
. Solving for
B
:

B^{1+\lambda}=\frac{2nM}{\delta}\implies B=\left(\frac{2nM}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}.

(138)

With this choice,
\mathbb{P}(A^{c})\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
.

Apply Hoeffding’s inequality for i.i.d. bounded random variables:

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{B}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{B}]>s\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2ns^{2}}{B^{2}}\right)

(139)

Require this probability
\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
:

\exp\left(-\frac{2ns^{2}}{B^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{2}\implies s\geq B\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}

(140)

Substituting
B
:

B=\left(\frac{2nM}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}

(141)

,

we get:

s\geq\left(\frac{2nM}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}.

Simplify:

s\geq(2M)^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}n^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{-\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\left(\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(142)

Known results for i.i.d. non-negative random variables satisfying
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{1+\lambda}]\leq M
provide:

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]>2M^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\left(\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}\right)\leq\delta.

Thus, with probability at least
1-\delta
:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\leq 2M^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\left(\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}

(143)

∎

Lemma G.3

(Reduction to Empirical Process)
.

For any hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
and any risk level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the deviation between the population CVaR risk
R_{\alpha}(h)
and the empirical CVaR risk
\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)
satisfies:

|R_{\alpha}(h)-\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\left|(\mathbb{E}-\mathbb{P}_{n})g_{h,\theta}\right|,

where
g_{h,\theta}(z):=(\ell(h,z)-\theta)_{+}
.

Proof.

Recall the variational definitions of the population and empirical CVaR:

R_{\alpha}(h)=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}G(\theta)\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)=\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\widehat{G}(\theta),

where we define the objective functions:

G(\theta):=\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[g_{h,\theta}(Z)]\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{G}(\theta):=\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{P}_{n}[g_{h,\theta}(Z)].

We use the elementary property that the difference between the infima of two functions is bounded by the supremum of their difference. Specifically, for any two functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
:

|\inf_{\theta}f(\theta)-\inf_{\theta}g(\theta)|\leq\sup_{\theta}|f(\theta)-g(\theta)|.

Applying this to
G
and
\widehat{G}
:

|R_{\alpha}(h)-\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)|\leq\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}|G(\theta)-\widehat{G}(\theta)|.

Substituting the definitions of
G(\theta)
and
\widehat{G}(\theta)
, the linear term
\theta
cancels out:

\displaystyle|G(\theta)-\widehat{G}(\theta)|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[g_{h,\theta}]\right)-\left(\theta+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathbb{P}_{n}[g_{h,\theta}]\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left|\mathbb{E}[g_{h,\theta}]-\mathbb{P}_{n}[g_{h,\theta}]\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left|(\mathbb{E}-\mathbb{P}_{n})g_{h,\theta}\right|.

Taking the supremum over
\theta
yields the result:

|R_{\alpha}(h)-\widehat{R}_{\alpha}(h)|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\left|(\mathbb{E}-\mathbb{P}_{n})g_{h,\theta}\right|.

∎

Lemma G.4

(Lipschitz Continuity in
\theta
)
.

Fix any hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
. Define the empirical process deviation map
\Phi_{h}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\Phi_{h}(\theta):=(\mathbb{E}-\mathbb{P}_{n})g_{h,\theta},

where
g_{h,\theta}(z):=(\ell(h,z)-\theta)_{+}
. Then
\Phi_{h}
is
2
-Lipschitz with respect to
\theta
. That is, for all
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
:

|\Phi_{h}(\theta_{1})-\Phi_{h}(\theta_{2})|\leq 2|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|.

Proof.

Recall that the function
x\mapsto(x)_{+}=\max(0,x)
is
1
-Lipschitz. Therefore, for any fixed
z\in\mathcal{Z}
and fixed
h\in\mathcal{H}
, the map
\theta\mapsto g_{h,\theta}(z)
is
1
-Lipschitz:

\displaystyle|g_{h,\theta_{1}}(z)-g_{h,\theta_{2}}(z)|

\displaystyle=|(\ell(h,z)-\theta_{1})_{+}-(\ell(h,z)-\theta_{2})_{+}|

\displaystyle\leq|(\ell(h,z)-\theta_{1})-(\ell(h,z)-\theta_{2})|

\displaystyle=|-\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}|

\displaystyle=|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|.

Now consider the deviation term:

|\Phi_{h}(\theta_{1})-\Phi_{h}(\theta_{2})|=\left|(\mathbb{E}[g_{h,\theta_{1}}]-\mathbb{P}_{n}[g_{h,\theta_{1}}])-(\mathbb{E}[g_{h,\theta_{2}}]-\mathbb{P}_{n}[g_{h,\theta_{2}}])\right|.

By the triangle inequality:

|\Phi_{h}(\theta_{1})-\Phi_{h}(\theta_{2})|\leq\left|\mathbb{E}[g_{h,\theta_{1}}-g_{h,\theta_{2}}]\right|+\left|\mathbb{P}_{n}[g_{h,\theta_{1}}-g_{h,\theta_{2}}]\right|.

Using the pointwise Lipschitz property derived above:

1.

Population term:

\left|\mathbb{E}[g_{h,\theta_{1}}-g_{h,\theta_{2}}]\right|\leq\mathbb{E}\left|g_{h,\theta_{1}}(Z)-g_{h,\theta_{2}}(Z)\right|\leq\mathbb{E}[|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|]=|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|
.

2.

Empirical term:

\left|\mathbb{P}_{n}[g_{h,\theta_{1}}-g_{h,\theta_{2}}]\right|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|g_{h,\theta_{1}}(Z_{i})-g_{h,\theta_{2}}(Z_{i})\right|\leq|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|
.

Summing these bounds yields:

|\Phi_{h}(\theta_{1})-\Phi_{h}(\theta_{2})|\leq|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|+|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|=2|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|.

∎

Lemma G.9

(Pseudo-Dimension of Truncated CVaR Class)
.

\mathcal{H}
be a hypothesis class with

\mathcal{L}:=\{z\mapsto\ell(h,z):h\in\mathcal{H}\},

\mathrm{Pdim}(\mathcal{L})=d
.

\mathcal{F}_{B}

\mathcal{G}_{1}:=\{(z,t)\mapsto\ell(h,z)-t:h\in\mathcal{H},\,t\in[0,R]\}.

\mathcal{G}_{2}:=\{(z,t)\mapsto(\ell(h,z)-t)_{+}:h\in\mathcal{H},\,t\in[0,R]\}.

.

\mathcal{F}_{B}=\{(z,t)\mapsto\min\{g(z,t),B\}:g\in\mathcal{G}_{2}\}.

is the minimum of

for an absolute constant
C
.

Lemma G.10

(Bias and variance of truncation)
.

Let
X
be a Non negative real-valued random variable such that

\mathbb{E}[|X|^{1+\lambda}]\leq M^{\prime}

for some
\lambda\in(0,1]
and
M^{\prime}>0
. Define the truncated variable

X^{B}:=\min\{X,B\}

for
B>0
. Then:

(i)

(
Bias
)

\big|\mathbb{E}[X^{B}]-\mathbb{E}[X]\big|\leq M^{\prime}B^{-\lambda}.

(ii)

(
Variance
)

\mathrm{Var}(X^{B})\leq M^{\prime}B^{1-\lambda}.

Proof.

We treat the two claims separately.

Observe that

\mathbb{E}[X^{B}]-\mathbb{E}[X]=\mathbb{E}\big[(X^{B}-X)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X>B\}}\big]=-\mathbb{E}\big[(X-B)\mathbbm{1}_{\{X>B\}}\big].

Hence,

\big|\mathbb{E}[X^{B}]-\mathbb{E}[X]\big|\leq\mathbb{E}\big[|X|\mathbbm{1}_{\{X>B\}}\big].

On the event
\{X>B\}
, we have
1\leq(X/B)^{\lambda}
, and therefore

|X|=|X|^{1+\lambda}|X|^{-\lambda}\leq|X|^{1+\lambda}B^{-\lambda}.

Substituting this inequality yields

\mathbb{E}\big[|X|\mathbbm{1}_{\{X>B\}}\big]\leq B^{-\lambda}\mathbb{E}[|X|^{1+\lambda}]\leq M^{\prime}B^{-\lambda}.

Since
\mathrm{Var}(X^{B})\leq\mathbb{E}[(X^{B})^{2}]
, it suffices to bound the second moment.
Write

(X^{B})^{2}=|X^{B}|^{1-\lambda}|X^{B}|^{1+\lambda}.

Using
|X^{B}|\leq B
and
|X^{B}|\leq|X|
, we obtain

|X^{B}|^{1-\lambda}\leq B^{1-\lambda},\qquad|X^{B}|^{1+\lambda}\leq|X|^{1+\lambda}.

Thus,

\mathbb{E}[(X^{B})^{2}]\leq B^{1-\lambda}\mathbb{E}[|X|^{1+\lambda}]\leq M^{\prime}B^{1-\lambda}.

∎

Lemma G.11

.

j
and consider only its clean samples. By Bernstein’s inequality combined with a union bound over an
\eta
-net of
\mathcal{G}

C

0.9

0.8K

Since a strict majority of blocks satisfy this bound, the median must lie within the same range. Therefore,
