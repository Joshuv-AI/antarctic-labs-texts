Nevus comedonicus syndrome in a 19-year-old female: Is brassiere a triggering factor?
Authors: Gloria Masenga, Caroline Masanje, Consolata Ignas Swai, Janeth Mwase, Nelson Mosha, Muzna Masoud Khalfan, Lulyritha Kini, Angel Santos Briz, Ousmane Faye, Daudi Mavura
Published: 2026

Introduction

Nevus comedonicus (NC) is an unusual form of organoid epidermal nevus first described by Kofmann in 1895.1 It is defined by groups of dilated follicular openings filled with keratin, typically arranged in a linear or blaschkoid distribution. When associated with extra cutaneous abnormalities, most commonly ocular or skeletal, it is referred to as nevus comedonicus syndrome (NCS). Several genes have been implicated in the pathogenesis of both isolated and syndromic NC.2,3 Fewer than 50 cases of NCS have been reported, with even fewer documented among individuals with skin of color.4 We present a rare case of NCS in a young female with extensive involvement of the breasts and axillae in areas corresponding to brassiere coverage prompting consideration of physical occlusion as a potential triggering or aggravating factor.

Case report

Clinical presentation

A 19-year-old female presented with longstanding cutaneous lesions first noted at 3 months of age. The lesions began as red linear streaks that progressively darkened and accumulated pigmented debris. In early childhood, recurrent abscesses developed predominantly in the axillary regions. During adolescence, lesions became more widespread, affecting the chin, breasts, and back, with increased scarring, psychological distress, and effect on quality of life. Notably, her symptoms worsened around puberty, likely due to increased follicular activity and hormonal changes. Before presentation to dermatology, the patient had exclusively used herbal treatments of unknown contents without improvement.

Physical examination

Cutaneous examination revealed multiple hyper pigmented comedonal lesions with cribriform scarring and postinflammatory hypopigmentation. These were bilaterally distributed over the axillae, breasts along the brassiere area, shoulder blades, chin, and sacral region (Fig 1, Fig 2, Fig 3). A blaschkoid linear distribution was noted along the midback (Fig 2). The lesions consisted of open comedones filled with keratinous material. Ophthalmological evaluation confirmed the presence of strabismus. Topical retinoid initially prescribed resulted in minimal improvement. After diagnosis confirmation, the patient was educated on her condition. Further tests were planned with emollient and mild potent steroid prescribed. Unfortunately, she declined any follow-up.

Discussion

This case highlights the presentation of NCS, confirmed by cutaneous and histopathologic findings, with ocular manifestation in the form of strabismus. Notably, our patient demonstrated sharply demarcated bilateral involvement of the breasts and axillae, corresponding precisely with the area of brassiere contact. To the best of our knowledge, this uncommon distribution is not frequently reported in the literature.

Three variants of NC have been identified: the “classic comedonal type,” the “Selhorst type,” and the “atrophoderma vermiculatum” variant. Our patient’s cutaneous findings characterized by extensive comedonal lesions with recurrent abscesses and scarring particularly in the armpits were most consistent with the Selhorst type associated with hidradenitis suppurativa. As previously reported, both diseases may coexist. and mechanical stress has been proposed as a shared pathogenic trigger resulting in follicular occlusion.5,6 Moreover, recent genetic studies have pointed out several genes, including FGFR2, FGFR3, and NEK9, which are implicated in cell proliferation, migration, and follicular differentiation, respectively, as causative factors for both isolated and syndromic NC.2,3 NCS may involve a variety of extracutaneous manifestations, including ocular defects, such as strabismus and cataracts, and skeletal anomalies, such as limb deformities and scoliosis. A recent review noted that 74.4% of NCS cases involved the head and neck, followed by the upper limbs (51.2%), upper trunk (48.8%), lower trunk (37.2%), and lower limbs (37.2%).4 Bilateral breast involvement, as noted in our patient, is rarely described.

The distribution of lesions in our case raises an important question: could mechanical occlusion or pressure from tight clothing, such as a brassiere, act as a triggering or aggravating factor in the development or exacerbation of lesions in predisposed individuals? Similar hypotheses have been proposed in conditions such as acne mechanica and hidradenitis suppurativa, where localized friction and occlusion are known contributors.5,7

The diagnosis of NCS is primarily clinical and supported by histopathologic confirmation of dilated and plugged follicular ostia. Although no definitive cure exists, treatment options may include topical keratolytics, topical or systemic retinoids, laser therapy, and surgical excision. Early intervention with good hygiene practices and antibiotics can help reduce inflammation and prevent abscess formation. Topical retinoids were prescribed with minimal improvement. At the second visit, the patient was educated on her condition with treatment options. However, given the genetic nature of the disease and financial constraints for minimal benefits, the patient declined further management and was lost to follow-up. The absence of effective treatment access may have led the patient to rely on traditional herbal remedies, which proved ineffective and potentially contributed to further disease progression, recurrent infections, and scarring. These complications significantly affected her self-esteem and disrupted her quality of life, highlighting the profound psychosocial burden of the condition. This underscores the critical need for early recognition and coordinated multidisciplinary care, especially in underserved populations.
