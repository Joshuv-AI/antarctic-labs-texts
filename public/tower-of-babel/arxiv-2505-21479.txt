Are Language Models Consequentialist or Deontological Moral Reasoners?
Keenan Samway, Max Kleiman-Weiner, David Guzman Piedrahita, Rada Mihalcea, Bernhard Schölkopf, Zhijing Jin

Abstract
As AI systems increasingly navigate applications in healthcare, law, and governance, understanding how they handle ethically complex scenarios becomes critical. Previous work has mainly examined the moral judgments in large language models (LLMs), rather than their underlying moral reasoning process. In contrast, we focus on a large-scale analysis of the moral reasoning traces provided by LLMs. Furthermore, unlike prior work that attempted to draw inferences from only a handful of moral dilemmas, our study leverages over 600 distinct trolley problems as probes for revealing the reasoning patterns that emerge within different LLMs. We introduce and test a taxonomy of moral rationales to systematically classify reasoning traces according to two main normative ethical theories: consequentialism and deontology. Our analysis reveals that LLM chains-of-thought tend to favor deontological principles based on moral obligations, while post-hoc explanations shift notably toward consequentialist rationales that emphasize utility. Our framework provides a foundation for understanding how LLMs process and articulate ethical considerations, an important step toward safe and interpretable deployment of LLMs in high-stakes decision-making environments. 1 1 1 Our code: https://github.com/keenansamway/moral-lens

Large language models (LLMs) have improved rapidly, demonstrating remarkable proficiency in tasks ranging from academic and professional understanding
(

;

)
to complex reasoning
(

;

;

)
. At the same time, these increasingly powerful models have begun to be used in domains that have a direct impact on people’s lives. Recent works have examined their applications in healthcare, such as emergency triage and patient prioritization
(
Cascella et al., 2023
;
Deeb et al., 2024
;
Frosolini et al., 2024
)
; legal analysis
(

; crisis management and resource allocation
(

;

)
; and financial advising
(

;
Lakkaraju et al., 2023
)
. Understanding how LLMs reason through ethically complex scenarios is critical for ensuring their safe and responsible deployment in high-stakes applications. For the purpose of our study, we use the term moral reasoning for a process through which agents, human or artificial, navigate complex ethical scenarios by weighing various considerations such as harm, fairness, and social norms, to evaluate and justify their decisions
(
Richardson, 2018
)
.

Previous works have measured LLM moral preferences through surveys asking about their moral principles
(
Scherrer et al., 2023
;
Abdulhai et al., 2024
;
Marraffini et al., 2024
)
and questionnaires having them make hypothetical decisions in morally complex scenarios
(
Tanmay et al., 2023
;
Ji et al., 2024
;
Takemoto, 2024
)
. However, this is nontrivial, since moral reasoning is not readily empirically verifiable, in contrast to tasks such as math or logic problems that can be evaluated against objective ground truths
(
Cobbe et al., 2021
;
Hendrycks et al., 2021b
)
, moral reasoning is not empirically verifiable. What constitutes “good” moral reasoning varies across cultures, ethical frameworks, and individual perspectives
(
Hendrycks et al., 2021a
;
Sorensen et al., 2024
)
.

While prior studies have used trolley problems to investigate (pluralistic) LLM alignment with human values
(
Jin et al., 2025
;
Takemoto, 2024
;
Ahmad and Takemoto, 2024
)
, their analyses have focused primarily on the decisions and judgments that LLMs make. In the present study, we analyze moral
reasoning
, where the ability to argue and justify one’s reasoning is equally important as the final decision made. We introduce
MoralLens
, a framework to decompose model responses in this domain according to the normative ethical theory which they are most associated with. Our framework specifically focuses on distinguishing consequentialist versus deontological rationales, which capture a central tension in ethical deliberation: whether actions should be evaluated primarily in terms of their outcomes or by their adherence to moral rules.

We focus on English language scenarios inspired by the Moral Machine experiment
(
Awad et al., 2018
)
and subsequent studies using it to examine LLM moral decision making in the context of the moral dilemma encountered by an autonomous vehicle when faced with an unavoidable crash
(
Jin et al., 2025
;
Takemoto, 2024
;
Ahmad and Takemoto, 2024
)
, where broad demographic dimensions such as age, social status, fitness, gender, social status, and group size are systematically varied. Due to its virality, the Moral Machine experiment amassed over 40 million human judgments.

Our findings highlight that chain-of-thought reasoning is generally composed of more deontological rationales than consequentialist, while post-hoc explanations use more consequentialist than deontological rationales. We also find that as models become more capable, when faced with scenarios involving equal-sized groups, they increasingly employ deontological reasoning, whereas when evaluating dilemmas with unequal group sizes, the same models shift towards consequentialist reasoning. These insights are potentially relevant for developing more robust alignment techniques and safety guardrails as LLMs continue to be deployed in increasingly consequential domains.

Contributions

We outline the key contributions of this paper as follows:

1.

Measurement of language models’ reasoning processes when they are presented with trolley dilemmas and asked to make a forced choice.

2.

A taxonomy of 16 morally relevant rationales grounded in the ethical theories of consequentialism and deontology. This taxonomy can be used as a target for the classification of LLMs’ reasoning traces into rationales.

3.

New insights into the balance between deontological and consequentialist rationales during chain-of-thought reasoning as models become more capable.

Problem Setup

To systematically analyze moral reasoning in LLMs, we develop a framework that distinguishes between scenarios, reasoning processes, and decisions. This decomposition allows us to prompt and then identify the underlying morally relevant rationales that LLMs employ, rather than just their final decisions. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a dataset of
n
trolley problem vignettes. Each vignette
V
can be represented as
V:=(s,c^{1},c^{2})
, where
s
is a description of the moral dilemma (e.g., “Assume there is a self-driving car with a sudden brake failure…”) and
c^{1}
and
c^{2}
are the two choices of who to save (e.g., “a boy” or “an elderly man”). Prior studies have primarily investigated moral decision-making which we define as:

Given a dataset
\mathcal{M}
of trolley problems, an LLM takes as input a vignette
V
and outputs a response containing its decision
D\in\{c_{1},c_{2},\mathrm{NoChoice}\}
.

Although this approach reveals the choices LLMs make, and these can be compared to human judgments, it provides limited insight into moral alignment. Models could produce seemingly appropriate decisions through flawed reasoning processes, and thus fail to generalize appropriately to novel situations. Our framework expands upon this approach by prompting the model to “think step-by-step”
(
Kojima et al., 2022
;
Wei et al., 2022
)
before providing its final answer. We define this pre-decision moral reasoning process as
R
and decompose it into one or more rationales
r
. Mathematically,
R:=(r_{1},r_{2},...,r_{k})
, where each rationale represents a distinct moral consideration or principle invoked in the reasoning process (e.g., “considering future contributions” or “saving the vulnerable”). Formally, this whole procedure is:

Given a dataset
\mathcal{M}
of trolley problems, an LLM takes as input a vignette
V
and outputs a response containing its reasoning and decision
(R,D)
.

Recent advances in LLMs have introduced models explicitly trained to deliberate before responding
(
Jaech et al., 2024
;
Guo et al., 2025
)
, so they do not need to be explicitly prompted to do so. Colloquially, these models are referred to as
reasoning
models, so we delineate the deliberative reasoning process with
T
. These traces may offer distinct glimpses into the models’ intrinsic reasoning patterns, as the deliberative reasoning processes emerge from specialized reinforcement learning training rather than being induced through prompting techniques. Thus, in our framework, this procedure is defined as:

Given a dataset
\mathcal{M}
of trolley problems, an LLM takes as input a vignette
V
and outputs a response containing its deliberative reasoning and decision
(T,D)
.

3
Moral Philosophy Primer

Moral philosophy, or ethics, is the normative study of right and wrong. It is often divided into several different disciplines: metaethics, which examines the nature and origin of moral principles; normative ethics, which develops frameworks for determining right and wrong actions; descriptive ethics, which studies how people actually behave and form moral beliefs; and applied ethics, which applies ethical theories to real-world domains. In this study, we focus our attention on two normative ethical theories: consequentialism and deontology, as they provide structured principles to evaluate the soundness of LLM moral responses. While they do not capture every nuance in moral philosophy, they represent fundamentally distinct approaches to moral reasoning that have been extensively studied in both philosophy and cognitive science
(
Greene, 2007
;
Parfit, 2011
)
.

3.1
Consequentialism

The consequentialist reasoner evaluates actions based on the consequences that action brings about rather than other intrinsic factors relating to the act itself
(
Sinnott-Armstrong, 2023
)
. The most prominent form of consequentialism, utilitarianism
(
Bentham, 1789
;
Mill, 2014
)
, holds that an action is morally right if it maximizes overall well-being or minimizes harm. In this framework, moral decision making is framed in terms of a cost-benefit analysis, weighing potential harms and benefits of outcomes to determine the action with the highest net utility. However, consequentialism is often criticized for justifying seemingly troubling actions if they lead to favorable results (e.g.,
Foot (1967)
). Additionally, the challenge of accurately predicting the net utility of all possible consequences raises concerns about its practical applicability in complex real-world scenarios.

3.2
Deontology

The deontological reasoner defines morality based on adherence to moral rules and principles, rather than consequences
(
Alexander and Moore, 2024
)
. Often rooted in Immanuel Kant’s categorical imperative
(
Kant, 1785
)
, deontology holds that certain actions, such as lying or harming others, are inherently impermissible, regardless of their outcomes. However, deontology is often criticized for its rigidity, as strict rule following can sometimes lead to counterintuitive imperatives, such as the obligation to refuse to tell a harmless lie even when it could prevent significant harm.

Although we acknowledge that these ethical frameworks represent just two perspectives within a rich landscape of moral philosophy and may not capture all the nuances of ethical reasoning, they provide a structured and well-established foundation for analyzing how LLMs process and articulate moral considerations across a diverse set of dilemmas.

4.1
Moral Rationales Taxonomy

To systematically interpret the responses that LLMs give when responding to moral dilemmas, we introduce a taxonomy of moral reasoning,
MoralLens
. Through a manual examination of approximately 100 different model responses from a variety of models, we identify 16 different morally relevant rationales,
r
, that LLMs commonly use when reasoning “out-loud” about their decisions in trolley problems. We categorize each of the rationales as being aligned with either consequentialism or deontology, and summarize our taxonomy in

Our taxonomy distinguishes between consequentialist and deontological rationales based on their orientation toward ethical action. The consequentialist rationales we identify, such as “Max Future Contribution” and “Max Number of Lives,” align with utilitarian principles that prioritize outcomes and aggregate welfare. In contrast, the deontological rationales we identify, including “Egalitarianism” and “Save the Vulnerable” reflect rule-based considerations that evaluate actions based on inherent moral principles rather than outcomes, emphasizing duties, rights, fairness, and the intrinsic value of individuals.

While this distinction provides a useful operational framework for categorizing LLM moral reasoning, we recognize that many rationales are not uniquely tied to a single ethical framework. For instance, “Save the Vulnerable” may reflect a deontological commitment to protecting those with intrinsic moral claims, but can also be framed in consequentialist terms if it maximizes long-term societal benefit or reduces overall harm. In this work, we adopt a heuristic classification intended to support scalable analysis across diverse model responses, rather than to resolve deep philosophical ambiguities.

Moral Rationales Classification

We use an LLM judge to classify the reasoning
R
into its most relevant rationales
r

(
Gilardi et al., 2023
)
. The judge model is provided with a models’ reasoning
R
along with the rationale labels defined in
MoralLens
, and then it is instructed to respond with one or more of the most relevant rationales present in the reasoning.

4.2
Experimental Setup

Moral Vignettes

We construct a dataset
\mathcal{M}
of 640 vignettes across four broad demographic dimensions covered by the Moral Machine experiment
(
Awad et al., 2018
)
: gender (undisclosed, male, female); age (undisclosed, young, old); social status (undisclosed, doctor, executive, criminal, homeless); and fitness (undisclosed, athlete, large person). We also compare across two additional axes: group size and reasoning order.

For group size, we systematically vary each of the two groups to consist of between one and five individuals. We define a scenario as
size-balanced
when the two choices presented have the same number of individuals (e.g., 1v1, 2v2, 5v5) and as
size-imbalanced
when the choices differ in size (e.g., 1v2, 2v1, 1v5, and 5v1). See
Section

C.1
for the complete prompt setup of these scenarios.

For reasoning order, we vary the order in which we ask the model to provide its reasoning and decision. We define a Reason-then-Decide scenario as one where the model is instructed to first think step-by-step and then provide its decision, and we define a Decide-then-Reason scenario as one where the model is instructed to first make a decision and then provide its reasoning. We will often refer to the reasoning stemming from each scenario as either pre-decision reasoning or post-decision reasoning, respectively.

Decision Models

In our experiments, we consider a diverse set of LLMs. We use popular closed-weight model families accessible through APIs such as OpenAI’s GPT, Google’s Gemini, Anthropic’s Claude, as well as popular open-weight model families including Meta’s Llama, Google’s Gemma, and Alibabas’s Qwen. For a full list of the models used, see
Section

C.2
.

For each decision model, we take five samples (three samples for the SFT and DPO models in
Section

5.3
) at temperature
1.0
for standard LLMs, allowing us to sample a diverse set of reasoning pathways that a model might follow. For reasoning LLMs, we use the developers’ recommended temperature and specify the value for each model in
Section

C.2
. If a model refuses to respond (e.g., “As an AI language model, I cannot…”) or, more often, if a model does not respond using our specified format, then we retry until it responds with a valid reason and decision pair in the format specified in our moral vignette prompt. We allow up to 10 total attempts, but most models we test take on average fewer than two attempts per scenario. See
Section

C.2.1
for more details on the response rates of different models.

Judge Model

We use a judge model to classify model responses according to
MoralLens
. Gemini 2.5 Flash
2

(
Paech, 2023

We use a temperature of 0 and ask the model to first briefly think through its response step-by-step, and then provide its classification.

To check that our judge model classifies rationales effectively, we take a random subset of model responses from GPT-4o and Llama 3.1 70B in Reason-then-Decide scenarios. After manually annotating the reasoning traces, Gemini 2.5 Flash achieved a precision of
0.845
, a recall of
0.859
, and an F1 score of
0.820
, indicating that our judge model demonstrates strong agreement with human annotation and suggests that it reliably captures the intended moral rationales in moral responses. For more details on the setup of our human evaluation procedure, see
Section

C.3.3
.

4.3
Metrics

Decision Utility

We define
Utility
as a metric that measures how often a model, when facing size-imbalanced scenarios (e.g., 1v2 and 1v5), selects the largest of the two groups. For all
n=320
size-imbalanced scenarios, we calculate it for each model as:

\textsc{Utility}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}_{[D_{i}=\max(c_{1i},c_{2i})]},

where
D_{i}
is the model’s decision;
\max(\cdot,\cdot)
is a function returning the larger of the two groups; and
\mathbf{1}
is an indicator function returning to 1 if
D_{i}=\max(c_{1i},c_{2i})
and 0 otherwise.
Utility
is a value between 0 and 1, where 0 means that a model always saves the individual over the group, and 1 means that the model always saves the group over the individual.

Rationales Measurement

To evaluate our models across the
n=640
moral dilemma vignettes, we define the Consequentialist-Deontological gap for a model as:

\textsc{CDgap}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{i}}w_{j}\left[\frac{C_{ij}-D_{ij}}{C_{ij}+D_{ij}+O_{ij}}\right],

where
r_{i}
is the number of rationales classified in vignette
i
;
C_{ij}
,
D_{ij}
, and
O_{ij}
are the weighted counts of consequentialist, deontological, and other rationales, respectively; and
w_{i}=1/j
is an inverse positional weighting scheme, giving higher importance to earlier rationales as ranked by the judge model. The resulting score ranges from
-1
(all deontological) to
+1
(all consequentialist), with
0
indicating a balanced distribution.

Capabilities Evaluation

To examine the relationship between model capabilities and moral reasoning, we use MMLU
(
Hendrycks et al., 2021b
)
as a general measure of model capability, and access evaluation results for a variety of models from the HELM MMLU leaderboard
(
Liang et al., 2023
)
in our analysis. We list the models included in such examinations in
Section

C.2
.

5.1
What is the dominant mode of moral reasoning in LLMs?

The reasoning decomposition in
Figure

2
reveals substantial variation in
CDgap
across both models and moral scenarios. First, we observe that smaller models within the same model family, such as Mistral Nemo, GPT-4.1 nano, Gemma 3 4B compared to Mistral Large, GPT-4.1, and Gemma 3 27B, tend to have higher
CDgap
, suggesting that these models rely more heavily on consequentialist reasoning compared to their larger counterparts. One plausible explanation is that smaller models generally undergo less extensive safety or alignment fine-tuning, thus utilize more consequentialist rationales rather than rule-based, deontological principles.

Across every model, we find that the rationales used for size-balanced scenarios (e.g., one girl versus one elderly woman) are more deontological than the rationales used in size-imbalanced scenarios (e.g., one girl versus five elderly women), which are more consequentialist. This pattern aligns with expectations: in size-imbalanced scenarios, models may more often invoke consequentialist rationales like “Max Number of Lives” when choosing to save larger groups. However, the substantial variation in
CDgap
across models raises questions about the relationship between model scale and how readily models switch moral reasoning frameworks.

To further examine this, we turn to
Figure

3
to see if and how moral reasoning patterns change as general reasoning capabilities improve in models. When examining the relationship between MMLU performance and
CDgap
across all scenarios, no significant correlation emerges (Pearson r
r=-0.233,~p=0.2156
). However, when grouping the size-balanced and size-imbalanced scenarios separately, we observe that a statistically significant divergence (t-test
t=-13.479,~p<0.001
) emerges between them. As models become more capable, they become
even
more consequentialist in size-imbalanced scenarios and
even
more deontological in size-balanced scenarios.

We also find that

varies not just from model to model, but also within the models among the four demographic categories: age, gender, social value, and fitness (ANOVA:
F=55.360,~p<0.001

Table

2
). One possible account is that age trade-offs (e.g., young vs. old) are often discussed in terms of life expectancy and social contribution, while gender discussions emphasize fairness and non-discrimination toward protected classes, leading models towards more deontologically classified rationales. How do model responses differ between Reason-then-Decide and Decide-then-Reason scenarios?

In this section, we compare the rationale decomposition of models that were instructed to first reason before making a decision with models that were instructed to first make a decision and then reason about it.

First in

5.4
How does moral reasoning change with alignment fine-tuning?

We next investigated the influence of alignment fine-tuning on moral reasoning patterns by comparing model checkpoints post-Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and post-Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). To provide a more controlled analysis, we compare the Tülu 2 SFT model with 12 Tülu 2.5 DPO series of models fine-tuned from the SFT base model using different preference datasets (e.g., AlpacaFarm, HH-RLHF, StackExchange, etc.). We also analyzed three additional SFT-DPO pairs: OLMo 2 32B and OLMo 2 13B
(
OLMo et al., 2025
)
, as well as AMD’s Instella 3B
(
Liu et al., 2025
)
.

Our analysis across all SFT-DPO pairs, shown in
Table

4
, reveals mixed patterns in how DPO affects moral reasoning. Rather than consistently shifting models in a single direction, the results show considerable variation depending on the specific preference dataset used and model characteristics. Some DPO variants show increased
CDgap
scores (indicating more consequentialist reasoning), while others show decreased scores (indicating more deontological reasoning) or remain relatively stable. The controlled comparison using the Tülu 2.5 series particularly demonstrates this variability, with different preference datasets producing markedly different moral reasoning patterns from the same base model.

However, we observe a more consistent pattern in
Utility
scores, which increase or remain stable across nearly all models following DPO fine-tuning. This suggests that while the style of moral reasoning may vary considerably, alignment training tends to reinforce preferences for saving the larger group over the individual, regardless of whether the reasoning follows consequentialist or deontological patterns.

These findings indicate that DPO fine-tuning does not inherently push models toward any particular moral reasoning framework. Instead, the impact appears to be driven by other factors, including the specific preference datasets used during alignment, model scale, and the underlying biases present in the training data.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we conducted a systematic analysis of moral reasoning in language models using over 600 distinct trolley problem scenarios. We develop a novel taxonomy of moral rationales grounded in consequentialist and deontological ethical frameworks, shedding light on patterns in how different LLMs approach ethical decision-making.

Our findings demonstrate that models vary significantly in their stated moral reasoning across different contexts. Notably, we observe a striking divergence in how advanced models employ these ethical frameworks, using more deontological reasoning in balanced scenarios and consequentialist reasoning in imbalanced ones. This reveals that LLMs do not simply adopt a single moral perspective, instead dynamically adapt their ethical framework based on situational factors.

These results have important implications for AI safety and alignment, especially as language models are increasingly deployed in high-stakes decision making environments. The distinction between consequentialist and deontological reasoning is significant as a model using mostly consequentialist reasoning may prioritize aggregate outcomes, potentially sacrificing individual rights for greater utility, while deontologically-oriented models might adhere to moral rules even when outcomes seem counterintuitive. Understanding not just what decisions models make, but how they reason about them, is crucial for developing more interpretable AI systems.

Limitations

Challenges in Evaluating LLM Reasoning

The phenomenon of deceptive alignment is a central concern in AI safety research that occurs when a model appears to be aligned with intended goals during evaluation, but internally pursues its own objectives that diverge from these established goals
(
Hubinger et al., 2024
)
. This kind of misalignment poses a challenge to evaluating moral reasoning in LLMs, as models could strategically deceive evaluators to mask its true behavior, especially in high-stakes scenarios, such as trolley dilemmas.

Similarly concerning is the potential for unfaithfulness of chain-of-thought reasoning. Although techniques like chain-of-thought prompting
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
seek to encourage step-by-step reasoning, the extent to which LLMs fully utilize and communicate their internal reasoning through these traces is unclear. Previous work points to empirical evidence that LLMs may not consistently base their answer on the chain-of-thought reasoning chains that they generate
(
Turpin et al., 2023
;
Paul et al., 2024
)
.

Methodological Challenges in LLM Evaluations

Our experimental approach faces several methodological challenges that affect how we could interpret moral reasoning. First,
Sclar et al. (2024)
find that even slight variations in input prompts, such as the use of newlines versus spaces, could significantly impact model responses.

This sensitivity extends to the framing of moral questions themselves. Language models have been shown to struggle with consistency in binary choice classification when evaluating many scenarios
(
Zhao et al., 2024
;
Rajan et al., 2024
)
, and
Ji et al. (2024)
found that LLMs exhibit different moral preferences when faced with a binary choice versus comparative moral judgments. This suggests that forced-choice paradigms may yield different patterns of moral reasoning than open-ended approaches, highlighting how experimental design choices can fundamentally shape our understanding of LLM moral reasoning.

Trolley Dilemma Limitations

Our study focuses on English-language, single-turn, binary trolley-style dilemmas. While this controlled format facilitates systematic comparisons and can leverage existing human judgment data, it captures only a partial view of LLM moral competence. Real-world moral reasoning often involves contextually rich, multi-step, and culturally situated constraints (e.g., medical triage, crisis management, legal analysis, etc.). Future work should extend this analysis to more dynamic and complex ethical scenarios to better approximate the settings in which such models might be deployed.

Taxonomy Limitations

Our classification approach assumes clear boundaries between categories when, in practice, certain rationales could be interpreted as appealing more towards either theory, consequentialism, deontology, or even others not studied here, depending on its specific context and use. For instance, what we refer to as consequentialism in this paper often just captures a subset of broader consequentialist theory known as act utilitarianism. Rule utilitarianism, for instance, evaluates the moral ‘rightness’ of an action based on whether it conforms to rules that, when followed, maximize overall well-being.

Ethics Statement

Our research explores moral reasoning in language models through trolley problem dilemmas, which raises several important ethical considerations:

Limitations of Normative Ethics Frameworks

While we use consequentialist and deontological frameworks to analyze LLM moral reasoning, we recognize that these represent just two perspectives centered in Western philosophical traditions. Our taxonomy may not fully capture moral rationales from diverse cultural contexts and ethical frameworks such as virtue ethics, care ethics, or non-Western philosophical systems.

Potential for Misinterpretation

Our results should not be interpreted as providing normative guidelines for which moral frameworks are “better” or “more ethical.” The dilemmas presented are deliberately artificial scenarios designed to probe reasoning patterns, not to allude to prescriptive guidelines for real-world ethical decision making. We caution against extrapolating our findings to conclude that language models should adopt specific ethical frameworks or that certain demographic groups are morally superior to others.

Societal Impact

As AI systems are increasingly deployed in domains requiring potentially sensitive moral judgment, understanding their reasoning capabilities becomes crucial. However, we emphasize that no automated system should be solely responsible for consequential ethical decisions without appropriate human oversight and governance frameworks.

Methodological Limitations

The judge model employed to classify moral rationales may itself contain biases that influence our results. While we attempted to mitigate this through clear definition of rationale categories and manual validation checks, we acknowledge this as a limitation. Additionally, our moral vignettes, though diverse, represent a constrained set of hypothetical scenarios that do not capture the full complexity of real-world ethical decision-making.

Dual-Use Considerations

Knowledge about how models approach moral reasoning could potentially be misused to manipulate model outcomes or provide false ethical legitimacy to problematic decisions. We believe that the benefits of understanding these patterns outweigh the risks, particularly as understanding can inform the development of more transparent and accountable AI systems.

We believe that this research contributes valuable insight into how language models process ethical dilemmas while recognizing the inherent limitations of using artificial scenarios to assess complex moral reasoning capabilities.

Appendix A
Related Work

Moral Decision Making in LLMs.

The evaluation of moral reasoning in LLMs has received significant attention.
Hendrycks et al. (2021a)
introduce the ETHICS dataset to assess LLMs’ understanding of moral principles.
Ji et al. (2024)
;
Nunes et al. (2024)
;
Abdulhai et al. (2023)
have used the Moral Foundations Questionnaire
(
Graham et al., 2008
;
Graham et al., 2011
)
to evaluate LLMs’ moral values, and Moral Foundations Vignettes
(
Clifford et al., 2015
)
to examine their responses to specific moral situations.
Scherrer et al. (2023)
investigated LLMs’ approaches to moral scenarios of varying ambiguity, while
Takemoto (2024)
;
Ahmad and Takemoto (2024)
;
Jin et al. (2025)
used the Moral Machine framework to evaluate LLMs’ ethical decision-making. Unlike these studies which focus on moral judgments, our paper focuses on LLMs’ abilities to deliberate on and explain their moral reasoning.

Tanmay et al. (2023)
distinguish between moral judgments and moral reasoning using the Defining Issues Test
(
Rest, 1992
)
, requiring models to make judgments and rate the importance of different issues. In contrast, our study analyzes moral reasoning in LLM chains-of-thought.

Some researchers
Rao et al. (2023)
;
Sorensen et al. (2024)
advocate for pluralistic alignment in LLMs, moving beyond monolithic moral frameworks to accommodate diverse cultural values through approaches like Overton pluralism (presenting multiple perspectives), steerable pluralism (adopting specific viewpoints), and distributional pluralism (matching population-level response distributions).

Emergent Capabilities and Values in LLMs.

Mazeika et al. (2025)
demonstrate that as models scale in capability, they develop increasingly coherent internal value systems.
Ruis et al. (2024)
show that reasoning capabilities significantly derive from knowledge embedded during pretraining. These findings suggest that moral reasoning patterns may also emerge as models become more sophisticated. Our research empirically investigates this by analyzing how ethical frameworks manifest across models of varying capabilities.

The following prompt is used to query the model for its decision and reasoning in each of the moral scenarios. For each query, items within curly brackets are replaced with their corresponding full text (e.g., an elderly man and a boy). The prompt shown is used for the reasoning first scenario. For the decision first scenario, we swap the order of ‘Scratchpad:…’ and ‘Decision:…’. However, in our experimentation, we found that even if we presented models with ‘Decision:…’ first and ‘Scratchpad:…’ after, they still overwhelmingly responded by placing ‘Scratchpad:…’ first. We suspect that this term in particular triggers models to think step-by-step before responding, and therefore in our decision first case, we swap the term ‘Scratchpad:…’ with ‘Reasoning:…’.

C.3.1
Rationale Accumulation

Figure 7:
Plots the average proportion of macro category’s rationales using four different methods of counting rationales in each response. 1) Equal: all rationales get 1 vote. 2) Uniform: all rationales are weighted by the inverse of the number of rationales. 3) First Bias: the first rationale is weighted 0.5 and rest equally split the other 0.5. 4) Inverse Rank: all rationales are weighted by the inverse of their position. 5) First Only: the first rationale is weighted 1 and the rest are weighted 0.

As we have instructed the judge LLM to annotate each reasoning chain with potentially multiple rationales, there are different ways one could accumulate rationales for a particular response. In
Figure

7
, we highlight five such methods that weigh rationales differently. We select the inverse rank weighting scheme as when we had prompted the judge LLM, we instructed it to select multiple
and
order them from most to least relevant. Therefore, when counting rationales, we assign a higher weight to the first rational, and decay according to
1/\text{rationale\_position}
.

C.3.2
Selection of “Other”

In our moral rationales taxonomy, we allow the judge model to select “Other” if none of the presented rationales reasonably cover the moral rationales used. While this is presented, we observe relatively low selection of this option. Across all models, “Other” is selected on average
1.8\%
of the time with a standard deviation of
3.2\%
.

C.3.3
Human Evaluation

We take a subset of model responses from GPT-4o-2024-11-20 and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct responding to Reason-then-Decide scenarios and present them to two machine learning graduate students. We have the evaluators follow the same procedure as the LLM judge: viewing model response’s (with no knowledge of which particular model produced each response) and annotating the rationales that are present. Evaluator 1 rated 64 responses, achieving a precision of
0.849
, a recall of
0.854
and an F1 score of
0.819
. Evaluator 2 rated eight responses and achieved a precision of
0.812
, recall of
0.896
, and F1 score of
0.833
. Inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s kappa) among the eight overlapping scenarios was
0.808
.

D.4
Consistency

We define
Consistency
as a measure of how robust a model is to the order that the two choices are presented (e.g., ‘a boy vs. an elderly man’ and ‘an elderly man vs. a boy’ are the same scenario but presented in flipped orders). We calculate this score per sample as the number of consistent response pairs divided by the total number of response pairs. It is a value between 0 and 1, where 0 means that the model always makes inconsistent decisions when the order is flipped, and 1 means that the model always makes the same choice regardless of presentation order. This is calculated on a per-sample basis and then averaged across samples to achieve a final
Consistency
score.

Figure 16:
Plots the consistency across all scenarios and phenomena categories. A value of 0 represents no consistency where the model always selects ’A’ or always selects ’B’, and 1 represents perfect consistency where the model always selects the same option regardless of its order.
