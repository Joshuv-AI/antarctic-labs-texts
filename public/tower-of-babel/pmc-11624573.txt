Rare case of wtTTR amyloidosis and MGUS in a patient with lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Ahmad Safdar, Faiz Anwer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryAmyloidosis is a rare, multisystem disease that leads to deposition of misfolded proteins in various organs. This case report presents a patient with cancer with a rare diagnosis of diffuse alveolar–septal transthyretin (TTR) amyloidosis with subsequent protein electrophoresis identifying monoclonal gammopathy of unknown significance. It highlights the association of amyloidosis with malignancy and the importance of including workup for plasma cell dyscrasias in patients found to have TTR amyloidosis. There are major differences in the clinical course and treatment of amyloid light chain and amyloid transthyretin amyloidosis, and evaluation of the precursor protein(s) is critical as a guide for management.Keywords: Multiple Pulmonary Nodules, Haematology (incl blood transfusion), Cancer intervention

Background

Amyloid fibril deposition has been described in patients with malignancy, more frequently in haematologic neoplasms than solid tumours. Amyloid transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis is commonly known for its cardiac or neurological involvement. Pulmonary involvement is relatively uncommon and poorly characterised. This case underscores the rare link between systemic amyloidosis and malignancy while emphasising the need to evaluate for plasma cell dyscrasias in patients with known ATTR amyloidosis.

Case presentation

A man in his 70s with a medical history of atrial fibrillation, prostate cancer and left upper lobe adenocarcinoma treated with lobectomy was found to have an evolving right lower lobe (RLL) pulmonary nodule on surveillance scans. He presented to thoracic surgery for consultation. He was asymptomatic with excellent cardiopulmonary fitness. Initial presentation in the clinic was significant for normal blood pressure, heart rate and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air.

Investigations

Complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel were unremarkable. His urinalysis had no evidence of proteinuria. CT imaging of the lungs was negative for obstructive, emphysematous or interstitial lung disease. Echocardiogram showed left ventricular diastolic dysfunction alongside mild pulmonary hypertension (RVSP 49 mm Hg). Positron emission testing demonstrated fludeoxyglucose F18 (FGD) uptake associated with RLL lesion measuring 2.6×1.8 cm (SUV 1.6) and was concerning for malignancy. He underwent robotic-assisted superior segmentectomy with mediastinal hilar lymph node dissection. Biopsy of lymph nodes was negative for malignancy. Right lung, lower lobe, superior segment, and segmentectomy showed adenocarcinoma, micropapillary predominant. The parenchyma showed diffuse deposits of acellular material involving vascular walls, alveolar septate and bronchial mucosa. Congo red stains were performed and deposits of material showed apple green birefringence, characteristic of amyloid. Amyloid typing with mass spectroscopy confirmed the presence of subtype of transthyretin (TTR) amyloidosis. He underwent genetic testing which was negative for hereditary TTR amyloidosis.

The patient was subsequently sent to the haematologist for further evaluation. Protein electrophoresis showed albumin 4.08 g/dL (normal 3.43–5.41), alpha 1 globulin 0.32 g/dL (normal 0.18–0.43 g/dL), alpha 2 globulin 0.77 g/dL (normal 0.42–0.98), beta globulin 0.68 g/dL (normal 0.61–1.17), gamma globulin 0.76 g/dL (normal 0.53–1.51). M protein concentration was 0.25 g/dL (normal≤0.00) with M-protein location on gamma fraction 1. The patient was diagnosed with IgG lambda monoclonal gammopathy of unknown significance (MGUS) with a normal kappa to lambda (K:L) ratio. There was no evidence of SLiMCRAB criteria. Cardiac laboratories were significant for high sensitivity troponin of 38 ng/L and NT Pro BNP of 2005 pg/L. Treatment

He was started on tafamidis, a novel therapy that stabilises the tetramers of TTR, slowing monomer formation, misfolding and subsequently further amyloidogenesis. His atrial fibrillation was managed with diltiazem for rate control and apixaban for anticoagulation.

Outcome and follow-up

Patient is currently undergoing routine surveillance for MGUS. Laboratory work at 6 months showed stable M protein concentration of 0.24 g/dL. The K:L ratio continues to be in the normal range. 6-month surveillance chest CT scan showed no evidence of new or recurrent disease. Routine prostate-specific antigen testing has remained close to undetectable. During follow-up, patient developed low back pain. Pain management diagnosed him with arthropathy of the sacroiliac joint and has benefited from sacroiliac joint steroid injections.

Discussion

Amyloidosis involves deposition of toxic protein fibrils that eventually lead to organ dysfunction.1 15 forms of systemic amyloidosis are known, but the most encountered types in clinical practice are amyloid light chain (AL), amyloid A (AA), mutant/hereditary and wild-type amyloid transthyretin (mATTR and wtATTR, respectively). The liver, heart, kidney and nerves are frequently involved with the disease and lead to organ dysfunction such as hepatomegaly, cardiomyopathy, arrythmia, proteinuria and autonomic dysfunction.2 Pulmonary involvement is common in systemic amyloidosis; however, patients are rarely symptomatic. Localised forms present as lung nodules or tracheobronchial involvement.3 Dyspnoea can be a feature of alveolar–septal involvement as alveolar gas exchange is interrupted and is most commonly caused by AL amyloidosis.

Our patient was found to have biopsy-proven alveolar–septal wtTTR amyloidosis. Wild-type TTR amyloidosis refers to unmutated TTR proteins that misfold and deposit in tissues with the myocardium being the most common. Cardiac involvement is predominant with manifestations of arrythmia and heart failure due to diastolic dysfunction.4 Workup for plasma cell dyscrasias alongside tissue biopsy is often needed for diagnosis of systemic amyloidosis. The diagnosis of cardiac ATTR amyloidosis can be made using technetium-labelled bone scintigraphy tracers without the need for histology, with scintigraphy having >99% sensitivity and 85% specificity for cardiac ATTR amyloidosis.5 Important to note that cardiac SPECT can also be positive in AL amyloidosis and patients with a monoclonal protein, nuclear imaging cannot be used to validate testing and a tissue-based typing should be ordered.6 In 2022, the Mayo Clinic performed the largest retrospective analysis to date on ATTR related to pulmonary disease and found that it was most commonly characterised by localised nodular lung disease.7

Our patient was also found to have MGUS. It is of critical importance to perform a thorough workup for other plasma cell dyscrasias in patients with TTR amyloidosis. With non-invasive imaging becoming more common than histology for diagnosis, it is critical that patients have laboratory evaluation including mass spectrometry. There are major differences in the clinical course and treatment of AL and ATTR amyloidosis, and evaluation of the precursor protein guides management.

To our knowledge, there are no previous reports of systemic wtTTR amyloidosis and MGUS in a patient with cancer. Cases of systemic amyloidosis in patients with solid tumours including urothelial, lung and gastrointestinal cancers have been described and commonly involve the AA subtype.8 9 In general, systemic amyloidosis has been more frequently seen in haematological neoplasms. The coexistence of amyloid and carcinomatous tissue has been demonstrated in thyroid medullary carcinoma and prostate cancer. Almost all tumour-associated amyloid deposition is characterised as a local form, and very rarely has been described as a systemic form.10 11 The association with malignancy raises questions about the pathogenesis. Is it that the amyloid protein precursors originate directly from the tumour, or are tumour-derived enzymes responsible for cleaving a serum precursor into amyloid protein? Further research is warranted to understand the mechanisms of amyloid formation in association with solid tumours.

In 2018, Phull et al performed a retrospective analysis to determine the prevalence of MGUS in TTR amyloidosis with a mean age of 70 years. They found that nearly 40% of patients studied with wtTTR had MGUS, rates 10%–18% higher than previously reported in the literature.12 In age-matched healthy adults, it is estimated the prevalence of MGUS is 5.3%.13 Further research is required to examine both the link between malignancy and systemic amyloidosis, as well as the high prevalence of MGUS in patients with wtTTR amyloidosis.
