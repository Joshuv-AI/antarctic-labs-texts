The effectiveness of resistance training for patients with degenerative arthritis after total joint arthroplasty: a pilot randomized controlled study
Authors: Yu-Ru Tian, Mann-Chian Wu, Su-Jung Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and objectivesThis study aimed to examine the effects of a volume-progressive staged bilateral resistance training program on lower limb muscular endurance, balance performance, pain intensity, and quality of life among patients with osteoarthritis following total joint arthroplasty (total knee and hip arthroplasty).BackgroundConventional post-arthroplasty rehabilitation predominantly focuses on early localized, unweighted mobilization restricted to the surgical limb. However, severe acute pain and joint trauma often preclude early progressive loading, leading to persistent cross-limb deficits. A knowledge gap remains regarding whether a hospital-initiated, staged bilateral resistance program—starting with non-operated limb conditioning to leverage cross-limb adaptations before progressive bilateral loading—can safely enhance early functional recovery.DesignA pilot randomized controlled study.MethodsTwenty-four patients undergoing unilateral total joint arthroplasty (TKA/THA) were recruited and randomly assigned to either the experimental group (n = 12, 3-month staged bilateral resistance training) or the control group (n = 12, routine rehabilitation). Outcomes measured at baseline (T0), hospital discharge (T1), 1 month (T2), and 3 months postoperatively (T3) included lower extremity muscular endurance (30-second chair stand), dynamic balance (2.44-m TUG), pain intensity (VAS), and quality of life (WHOQOL-BREF). Statistical analyses utilized GEE and ANCOVA (α = 0.05).ResultsAll 24 participants completed the 3-month follow-up. Generalized Estimating Equation analyses revealed that the experimental group achieved significantly greater improvements in lower extremity muscular endurance at T2 (p = .004) and T3 (p < .001) compared with controls. For dynamic balance, ANCOVA after baseline adjustment demonstrated that the experimental group achieved significantly greater performance improvements across all postoperative time points (T1: p = .037; T2: p = .001; T3: p < .001). At 3 months, the experimental group also reported significantly lower pain intensity (p = .023). Quality of life improved over time in both groups, but the between-group difference was not statistically significant (p = .062).ConclusionHospital-initiated staged bilateral resistance training appears feasible and safe following total joint arthroplasty, demonstrating preliminary benefits for lower limb muscular endurance, dynamic balance, and pain reduction. Quality of life improved in both groups over time without significant group differences, indicating a non-significant numerical trend. Larger prospectively registered trials are required to confirm these preliminary findings.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT07696195, Registered July 8, 2026 (Retrospectively registered).Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Resistance Training, Lower Limb Muscular Endurance, Balance, Performance, Randomized controlled study

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is one of the most prevalent chronic musculoskeletal disorders worldwide, serving as a primary driver of chronic joint pain, progressive stiffness, and severe physical disability in aging populations [1, 2]. When conservative non-pharmacological or pharmacological modalities fail to provide adequate symptom relief, total joint arthroplasty (TJA)—encompassing both total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and total hip arthroplasty (THA)—is widely established as the definitive surgical intervention to alleviate joint pain, correct mechanical alignment, and restore fundamental mobility [3, 4]. Despite the high surgical success rates in restoring structural joint integrity, post-operative patients frequently exhibit persistent neuromuscular and functional deficits [5, 6]. These deficits include pronounced quadriceps and gluteal muscle weakness, arthrogenic muscle inhibition, impaired joint proprioception, and substantial balance instability [5–8]. Such residual functional impairments often stem from a combination of pre-existing disuse muscle atrophy, chronic pain-avoidance movement strategies, and habitual asymmetric gait compensation that joint replacement surgery alone cannot automatically correct [7–9]. Consequently, persistent physical dysfunction substantially increases post-discharge fall risks, accelerates physical frailty, and impairs long-term independence in older adults [7, 8, 10]. Therefore, implementing structured, evidence-based post-operative rehabilitation protocols during inpatient hospitalization—under strict clinical safety monitoring and informed consent—plays a pivotal role in facilitating early motor recovery and ensuring a safe transition from acute care to community living [11–13].

Although progressive exercise training is universally recognized as a non-pharmacological cornerstone for post-arthroplasty management [14–16], traditional post-operative nursing and physiotherapy protocols predominantly emphasize early localized mobilization restricted strictly to the surgical limb [17, 18]. Because surgical trauma, acute joint effusion, and local pain acutely inhibit voluntary muscle activation in the operated joint, heavy early loading on the surgical side is often poorly tolerated or contraindicated [5, 18, 19]. Furthermore, persistent asymmetric movement patterns frequently lead to chronic over-reliance on the non-operated limb, which paradoxically accelerates contralateral joint degeneration and exacerbates bilateral functional decline [9, 20]. To address these clinical challenges, this study introduces an innovative, structured post-operative rehabilitation strategy initiated within the hospital setting [11, 21, 22]. The primary contribution and distinct novelty of this intervention lie in its staged, hospital-initiated bilateral staged resistance training with volume-based progression design [18, 19, 23]. Neuromuscular evidence demonstrates that strength training applied to the uninjured contralateral limb can induce a “cross-education” effect—a neurophysiological mechanism mediated by increased corticospinal excitability and spinal motor neuronal recruitment that enhances neural drive to the homologous unexercised surgical limb without imposing mechanical stress on the healing joint [18, 19, 23]. Specifically, under professional nursing supervision in the acute orthopedic ward, resistance training with 1-kg ankle weights is initially performed exclusively on the non-operated limb during the first post-operative month to mitigate cross-limb disuse atrophy, promote central neural adaptations, and preserve overall physical capacity [17–19]. Subsequently, progressive resistance exercises are systematically introduced to the operated limb in the second month and combined with gradual increases in total exercise volume (sets and repetitions) in the third month [17, 23]. This staged bilateral approach systematically restores movement symmetry, enhances dynamic postural control, and optimizes functional outcomes across the acute and subacute recovery phases [9, 17, 20].

A critical conceptual distinction in evaluating physical recovery following joint replacement is the precise definition between muscle strength and muscular endurance [10, 24]. While maximal muscle strength reflects the peak force generation during a single maximal contraction, lower-extremity muscular endurance—defined as the capacity of specific muscle groups to sustain repeated dynamic muscular contractions against resistance over time—serves as the primary functional determinant of sustained locomotion, fatigue resistance, and dynamic balance during daily activities in older post-TJA patients [10, 25, 26]. During functional tasks such as walking, stair negotiation, and repeated sit-to-stand transitions, functional independence depends heavily on the submaximal fatigue resistance of lower-limb musculature rather than transient peak force [24, 25]. In this study, lower-extremity muscular endurance was designated as a primary functional outcome and evaluated using the 30-second chair stand test, a highly validated, reliable, and standardized objective tool designed to capture functional muscle endurance and fall risk in older adult populations [24–26].

Because structured, hospital-initiated bilateral staged resistance interventions targeting non-operated limb conditioning prior to bilateral progressive loading remain under-explored in clinical orthopedic nursing practice [18, 21, 23], a pilot randomized controlled trial (pilot RCT) design was employed [27, 28]. A pilot study design is uniquely suited for evaluating novel complex interventions, as it provides critical empirical data regarding protocol feasibility, clinical safety, patient adherence, and preliminary effect size estimates prior to initiating resource-intensive, large-scale multi-center clinical trials [13, 17, 27].

Method

Study design and participants

The data collection phase spanned from February 14, 2025, to December 3, 2025. This study employed an experimental design with a three-month follow-up. Participants were recruited from the orthopedic ward of a medical center in Hualien, Taiwan, between January and December 2025.

As a pilot randomized controlled trial, the primary feasibility objectives included assessing: (1) recruitment and retention rates over the 3-month intervention period; (2) protocol adherence, monitored via participant training logs and illustrated reminder leaflets; (3) intervention acceptability and safety, defined as the absence of intervention-related adverse events (e.g., falls or joint acute injuries during resistance exercises); and (4) feasibility of functional assessment protocols (completion of the 30-second chair stand test and 2.44-m TUG test).

This study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07696195) on July 8, 2026 (Retrospectively registered). Due to an administrative oversight, the registration was performed retrospectively after the completion of recruitment and data collection. However, all intervention protocols, primary/secondary outcomes, and analysis plans remained strictly identical to the original protocol approved by the Ethics Committee (IRB114-014-B).

Eligible participants were patients with osteoarthritis who underwent first-time unilateral total joint arthroplasty (TJA; encompassing total knee arthroplasty [TKA] and total hip arthroplasty [THA]).

Inclusion criteria: (1) clear consciousness and ability to communicate in Mandarin or Taiwanese, (2) independent ambulation before surgery, (3) adequate vision and hearing with correction if needed, (4) community-dwelling status, (5) patients who are willing to participate in this study. Exclusion criteria included (1) inability to ambulate independently, (2) medical contraindications to exercise, (3) severe musculoskeletal conditions other than osteoarthritis, same-day admission and surgery, and (4) bilateral joint arthroplasty. This study adheres to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for reporting clinical trials.

Randomization and allocation concealment

The random allocation sequence was computer-generated by an independent researcher who was not involved in recruitment, intervention implementation, or outcome assessment. Eligible participants were enrolled by the ward research nurse. After obtaining written informed consent and completing baseline evaluations, participants were assigned to either the experimental group or the control group by opening the corresponding sealed envelope, thereby minimizing selection bias.

Sample size

Sample size estimation was conducted a priori using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 [27]. The calculation was based on the primary outcome (lower-extremity muscular endurance) using an ANOVA: Repeated measures, within-between interaction test family. The design specified 2 groups and 4 repeated measurement time points (preoperative, prior to discharge, 1-month, and 3-month postoperatively). Input parameters included a medium effect size of f = 0.25 (informed by prior post-arthroplasty exercise literature), α = 0.05, power (1 − β) = 0.80, an assumed correlation among repeated measures of 0.50, and a nonsphericity correction ε = 1.0.

The analysis indicated a minimum required sample size of 24 participants (12 per group) to detect a significant group-by-time interaction effect. All 24 enrolled participants completed the 3-month follow-up (100% retention). This longitudinal repeated-measures framework directly informed the primary statistical testing strategy using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) to evaluate group-by-time interaction effects across time points. Intervention

Control group (Routine Postoperative Rehabilitation Care)

Participants in the control group received standard routine post-operative orthopedic rehabilitation care during acute hospitalization (from Postoperative Day 1 until discharge, typically 3 to 5 days). Routine care was conducted twice daily (15–20 min per session) in the acute orthopedic ward under the direct supervision of clinical nurses and physical therapists. The standard regimen included: 1. Range-of-Motion (ROM) & Strengthening: Active-assisted joint ROM exercises, passive continuous passive motion, and isometric muscle contractions (quadriceps sets and ankle pumps). 2. Balance & Mobility: Bedside sitting, standing balance practice, and ambulation/walking training using a four-legged walker for safety.

Upon discharge, participants received standard written and oral discharge education to continue unweighted basic mobility exercises and independent walking at home. Neither group received formal outpatient physiotherapy or supervised exercise programs post-discharge, establishing a clear treatment contrast between the two study arms.

Experimental group (Staged Bilateral Resistance Training)

Participants in the experimental group received a hospital-initiated, staged bilateral resistance exercise program in addition to routine post-operative care. The lower-limb resistance exercises included seated knee extension, hip abduction, hip extension, standing hamstring curls, and standing hip abduction. In this study, resistance training was implemented using a fixed 1-kg ankle weight to ensure clinical safety, minimize acute joint stress, and accommodate early post-operative constraints in older patients following joint replacement. Progression was systematically achieved through a structured volume expansion model (i.e., progressive increase in sets, repetitions, and staged limb sequencing across the 3-month intervention) rather than increasing external resistance load. Specifically, Month 1 focused on exclusive non-operated limb training (2 sets/exercise) under nurse supervision to prevent cross-limb disuse without overloading the acute surgical site; Month 2 expanded to 3 sets on the non-operated limb and introduced 2 sets on the operated limb; and Month 3 advanced exercise volume to 4 sets (non-operated limb) and 3 sets (operated limb). Each set comprised 10 repetitions with a 1-minute rest interval, performed three times per week.

Delivery, adherence, and safety monitoring

Inpatient sessions were directly supervised and taught by the same research nurse to ensure correct movement technique and intervention fidelity. To support and track post-discharge compliance, participants received an illustrated exercise reminder leaflet and a structured daily exercise logbook. Protocol adherence was monitored through bi-weekly telephone follow-ups and verified during monthly outpatient clinic visits. Training was temporarily modified (reducing sets) or paused if participants reported acute joint pain (VAS > 4), severe fatigue, or wound complications.

Outcome measures

Outcome measurements were systematically conducted across four structured time points, including baseline/preoperative assessment (T0), prior to hospital discharge (T1; mean Postoperative Day 4.2 ± 0.8), 1 month postoperatively (T2), and 3 months postoperatively (T3). The 3-month post-operative follow-up period directly corresponded to the 3-month active intervention duration. The primary outcome of this study was lower-extremity muscular endurance, assessed using the 30-second chair stand test, with the primary endpoint designated at 3 months postoperatively (T3) in direct alignment with the primary study hypothesis and sample size estimation. Secondary outcomes encompassed dynamic balance ability evaluated via the standardized 2.44-metre Timed Up-and-Go (TUG) test, pain intensity assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS, 0–10), and health-related quality of life measured by the Taiwanese version of the WHOQOL-BREF questionnaire.

Research instruments

Data were collected using structured questionnaires and standardized functional assessments. The instruments and equipment used in this study included 1-kg ankle weights, a stopwatch, a 45-cm straight-backed chair, cones, a measuring tape, and a walker for safety protection during testing.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A structured questionnaire was used to collect demographic and clinical data, including sex, age, educational level, marital status, living arrangement, employment status, treatment modality, presence of chronic diseases, and regular exercise habits.

Quality of life

Quality of life was assessed using the Taiwan version of the World Health Organization Quality of Life–Brief Version (WHOQOL-BREF) [29]. This instrument consists of 28 items covering multiple health domains, including physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships, and environmental factors [29]. The Taiwanese version of the WHOQOL-BREF has demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.70 to 0.77 [30]. Domain raw scores were converted to standard transformed scores on a scale of 0 to 100 according to the official manual for the Taiwan version of the WHOQOL-BREF, with higher scores reflecting better perceived quality of life. Cronbach’s α value for the scale in this study was 0.96. Unlike physical performance measures, quality of life was intentionally evaluated at only two time points: baseline/preoperative (T0) and 3 months postoperatively (T3).

Lower- extremity muscle endurance

Lower-extremity muscular endurance (L-LME) was assessed using the standardized 30-second chair stand test. In older post-arthroplasty populations, this test specifically evaluates the capacity of lower-limb musculature to sustain repetitive submaximal contractions over a 30-second duration. Participants were seated in the middle of a 45-cm straight-backed chair with arms crossed over the chest and feet flat on the floor. Upon the signal, participants stood up fully and returned to a seated position as many times as possible within 30 s. The total number of completed full stands was recorded. This test has demonstrated good reliability (r = .89) and validity (r = .87) in older adult populations [24]. Given that participants were postoperative patients, a four-legged walker was placed in front of the chair for safety protection during testing.

Balance ability

Dynamic balance performance was evaluated using the standardized 2.44-metre Timed Up-and-Go (TUG) Test. The test course consisted of a 45-cm armless chair and a cone-shaped obstacle placed precisely 2.44 m directly in front of the chair. Upon the start signal, participants rose from the chair, walked at a safe and comfortable pace around the cone, returned to the chair, and sat down. For safety, a four-legged walker was permitted for participants requiring physical support during locomotion. Participants completed one unrecorded practice trial followed by two recorded test trials, with the shorter completion time (in seconds) recorded using a digital stopwatch. Research staff accompanied participants throughout the test path to prevent falls. This test evaluates the ability to maintain balance during functional mobility tasks and has demonstrated high reliability (r = .95) and acceptable validity (r = .79) [30].

Pain intensity

The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) was first introduced for clinical evaluation by Hayes and Patterson [31]. Pain intensity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), which ranges from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). Participants were asked to indicate their perceived pain intensity under specified conditions. The VAS is a widely used and validated single-item measurement tool for pain assessment in clinical and postoperative populations [32]. To ensure participant safety and consistency of measurements, all assessments and exercise training sessions were conducted by the same researcher.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were prespecified in the study protocol and performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with significance set at α = 0.05. Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics. Baseline comparability was evaluated using Chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent-sample t-tests for continuous variables.

To evaluate longitudinal changes across four measurement time points (T0, T1, T2, T3) for lower-extremity muscular endurance, pain intensity, and quality of life, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were applied. GEE models accounted for intra-subject correlations over time. The models specified a linear distribution with an identity link function and an exchangeable working correlation structure. Parameter estimates were computed using robust Huber-White standard errors. Reference categories were designated as the control group and baseline assessment (T0). Models were adjusted for baseline scores, age, and sex. Main effects of group, time, and group-by-time interaction were evaluated. Complete case analysis was conducted as all 24 participants (n = 12 in the experimental group, n = 12 in the control group) completed all four evaluation time points (T0 to T3) with zero missing data (100% retention rate). Per pilot trial reporting guidelines, statistical reporting emphasizes point estimates, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and effect sizes (partial eta squared, ηp2) alongside p-values to evaluate precision and magnitude of effects.

Due to a statistically significant baseline imbalance in dynamic balance performance between groups, one-way Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted to compare post-intervention balance performance at each follow-up time point (T1 prior to discharge, T2 at 1 month, and T3 at 3 months) while adjusting for baseline (T0) performance as a covariate.

Prior to ANCOVA, model assumptions were confirmed: normality of residuals (Shapiro-Wilk test p > .05), homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test p > .05), and homogeneity of regression slopes (F interaction = 1.12, p = .304). Adjusted group means were compared to determine treatment effects at follow-up endpoints. Bonferroni adjustments were applied to post-hoc repeated pairwise comparisons to control for inflated Type I error rates across secondary outcomes.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the exercise intervention, blinding of participants and the intervention provider was not feasible. To maintain participant safety and measurement consistency, functional assessments and exercise sessions were conducted by the same researcher, who was unblinded to group assignments. However, data analysts and statisticians were blinded to group allocations during statistical processing through de-identified coding.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the participating medical centre (approval number: IRB114-014-B). All participants received a detailed explanation of the study purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits before enrolment. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without affecting their medical care. All data were anonymized and handled confidentially in accordance with ethical guidelines [13].

Results

Feasibility and safety outcomes

All 24 enrolled participants completed the 3-month follow-up (100% retention rate). Participants in the experimental group demonstrated high protocol adherence, completing an average of 92.2% of prescribed training sessions (33.2 ± 2.1 out of 36 sessions). No major protocol deviations occurred. Furthermore, no intervention-related adverse events, falls, severe joint swelling, or acute pain exacerbations were reported during training sessions or functional assessments, supporting the safety and clinical feasibility of the intervention in post-arthroplasty patients.

Participant characteristics and baseline imbalance

A total of 24 participants completed the study. A statistically significant baseline imbalance was identified in dynamic balance ability between the experimental (18.39 ± 6.32 s) and control groups (22.56 ± 6.94 s, p < .001). Consequently, baseline balance performance was controlled as a covariate in subsequent ANCOVA analyses to evaluate treatment effects. A noticeable difference was also observed in preoperative regular exercise habits (50.0% in the experimental group vs. 16.7% in the control group, p = .083). Baseline lower-extremity muscular endurance, pain intensity, and quality of life showed no statistically significant differences (p > .05) (See Table 1).

At the second measurement (prior to discharge), both groups demonstrated a decline in performance. Improvements were observed at the third and fourth measurements in both groups; however, the magnitude of improvement differed between groups (See Table 2).

Effects of resistance training on balance ability

Balance ability was assessed using the 2.44-metre chair rise and obstacle navigation test. Both groups demonstrated changes in balance performance across the four measurement time points, with different patterns observed between groups. In the experimental group, the mean completion time increased from 18.39 ± 6.32 s at the first measurement to 24.80 ± 5.14 s at the second measurement, indicating a decline in balance performance immediately following surgery. Performance subsequently improved, with the completion time decreasing to 16.38 s at the fourth measurement, which was shorter than the preoperative value. In the control group, a similar pattern of initial decline followed by improvement was observed; however, the mean completion time at the fourth measurement (24.96 ± 4.25 s) remained longer than that at the first measurement (22.55 ± 6.94 s) (See Table 2).

ANCOVA adjusting for baseline performance (T0) confirmed significant post-intervention differences across all post-baseline time points. At hospital discharge (T1), the adjusted mean difference was − 2.65 s (95% CI -5.12, -0.18, F(1, 21) = 4.88, p = .037, ηp2 = 0.188). At 1 month postoperatively (T2), the difference widened to -4.19 s (95% CI -6.32, -2.06, F(1, 21) = 9.87, p = .001, ηp2 = 0.320). At 3 months postoperatively (T3), the experimental group demonstrated a significantly greater improvement with an adjusted difference of -7.11 s (95% CI -8.90, -5.32, F(1, 21) = 24.81, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.542), favoring the experimental group.

The results indicated significant changes in balance performance over time in both groups. The experimental group demonstrated greater improvements in balance ability during the later postoperative period (third and fourth measurements) compared with the control group (See Table 4).

Effects of resistance training on pain intensity

Pain intensity decreased over time in both groups across the postoperative follow-up period. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) analysis was conducted to examine group differences and changes in pain intensity over time. At 3 months postoperatively (T3), the experimental group achieved a significantly lower pain intensity score… (B = -1.167, 95% CI [-2.169, -0.164], p = .023, ηp² = 0.215). No statistically significant between-group differences were observed at earlier measurement points. Overall, both groups demonstrated a downward trend in pain intensity during the postoperative recovery period (See Table 3).

Effects of resistance training on quality of life

Changes in overall health-related quality of life were assessed using the WHOQOL-BREF total score. Both groups demonstrated improvements in quality of life over time. At 3 months postoperatively (T3), mean scores increased from 64.58 ± 7.56 to 91.92 ± 11.21 in the experimental group and from 63.25 ± 7.67 to 84.08 ± 8.10 in the control group (See Table 2).

GEE analysis was used to examine differences in quality-of-life scores across time points and between groups. At T3, the experimental group demonstrated a higher mean WHOQOL-BREF improvement compared to the control group (B = 6.500, 95% CI [-0.337, 13.337], p = .062, ηp2 = 0.148); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Both groups demonstrated an overall upward trend in quality-of-life scores during the follow-up period (See Table 3).

Discussion

Participant characteristics and baseline comparability

The study sample consisted of 24 participants with a predominant representation of older women 70%, with experimental and control groups demonstrating a mean age of 67.92 ± 10.41 and 66.00 ± 12.00 years, respectively. This demographic profile aligns with the global epidemiological trends of osteoarthritis (OA), which is well-documented as a highly prevalent chronic musculoskeletal disorder that disproportionately affects aging populations and older females [1, 2]. Baseline comparisons demonstrated no statistically significant differences between the two groups regarding key functional parameters such as lower-extremity muscle endurance, pain intensity, and quality of life.

However, a baseline imbalance was identified in dynamic balance performance (p < .001), alongside a higher proportion of regular exercise habits in the experimental group (50.0% vs. 16.7%, p = .083). Although baseline balance was statistically controlled as a covariate using ANCOVA, these initial discrepancies may have influenced the study outcomes. Participants with prior exercise habits or superior baseline functional stability might have possessed higher exercise self-efficacy and greater training responsiveness [33], which could have partially amplified the observed post-intervention gains in muscle endurance, dynamic balance, and pain reduction [19, 33].

Additionally, a statistically significant difference was identified in balance ability at baseline, which required rigorous statistical control as a covariate in subsequent analyses. From a holistic nursing perspective, these baseline variances in functional stability underscore that advanced OA leads to altered gait patterns, severe muscle atrophy, and secondary functional limitations prior to surgery, which increases post-discharge risks and necessitates tailored baseline nursing assessments [7, 34, 35].

Effects of resistance training on lower-extremity muscle endurance

To contextualize the contribution and novelty of the present study, our protocol was compared with previous postoperative resistance training trials in joint arthroplasty [12, 19, 23]. While prior studies predominantly initiated heavy-load bilateral or operated-limb resistance training in outpatient settings weeks after surgery, our study implemented a hospital-initiated, staged bilateral resistance approach starting on Postoperative Day 1 [12, 19, 23]. This staged design leveraged the potential cross-education effect during early acute recovery while avoiding excessive mechanical stress on the healing surgical joint [19, 23]. Furthermore, progressing through volume rather than load offered a highly feasible and safe model for nurse-led inpatient education and post-discharge self-management.

In the early postoperative phase (prior to discharge), both groups demonstrated a pronounced decline in lower-extremity muscle endurance during the 30-second chair stand test. This clinical trend suggests that while total joint arthroplasty (TJA) effectively restores mechanical joint integrity, acute surgical trauma combined with initial post-discharge immobilization temporarily accelerates disuse muscle atrophy and impairs quadriceps strength [5, 7].

Over the course of the follow-up period, the implementation of staged resistance training with volume-based progression yielded substantial functional recovery. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) analysis revealed a significant group-by-time interaction effect, where at 1 month postoperatively (T2: B = 2.167, 95% CI [0.673, 3.661], p = .004, ηp² = 0.326) and 3 months postoperatively (T3: B = 3.500, CI [1.965, 5.035], p < .001, ηp² = 0.482) compared to the control group.

These results provide preliminary evidence supporting the paradigm that resistance training may serve as a beneficial non-pharmacological intervention for OA management [14, 36]. Although not directly measured in this trial, previous literature suggests that progressive resistance protocols may facilitate muscle fiber recruitment and neural adaptations, thereby addressing residual postoperative deficits more effectively than conventional walking or low-intensity physiotherapy alone [12, 23]. These improvements are critical determinants of general functional fitness, mobility, and long-term autonomy in daily living for older adults [24, 25].

Effects of resistance training on balance ability

Dynamic balance performance, measured via the 2.44-metre chair rise and obstacle navigation test, exhibited an immediate postoperative decline across both cohorts, as evidenced by the experimental group’s mean completion time increasing to 24.80 ± 5.14 s at the second measurement. This phenomenon corresponds with existing literature indicating that postoperative pain, joint swelling, and impaired proprioception acutely compromise postural control and mechanical stability following major joint surgery [5, 6].

Following analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to adjust for baseline imbalances, the experimental group achieved significantly greater advancements in dynamic balance across all post-operative evaluation points—from hospital discharge through the 3-month follow-up—compared with the control group (p < .05 for all time points). By the final assessment, the experimental group’s completion time decreased to 16.38 s, successfully surpassing preoperative performance. Conversely, the control group failed to fully restore their baseline capacity (24.96 ± 4.25 s). Because unilateral surgery frequently prompts persistent asymmetric gait and contralateral overcompensation, targeting only the affected limb can jeopardize overall symmetry, accelerate contralateral joint degeneration, and elevate fall risk [9, 20]. The progressive resistance protocol utilized in this study systematically addressed bilateral lower-limb conditioning, which serves to enhance per articular muscle stability, reduce abnormal mechanical stress, and foster a safer transition back into community mobility [11, 14]. Clinical nurses play a pivotal role in this trajectory by providing scaled, easily monitored designs that ensure patient adherence and safety beyond the controlled clinical setting [17, 37].

Effects of resistance training on pain intensity

The clinical data demonstrated a progressive downward trend in pain intensity for both groups across the postoperative recovery timeline, reinforcing that routine postoperative management and basic rehabilitation provide fundamental therapeutic relief [3, 4]. Crucially, however, GEE analysis identified that pain scores at the final measurement (fourth measurement) were significantly lower in the experimental group compared to the control group (B = -1.167, p = .023).

This finding is consistent with the established physiological benefits of resistance exercises in mitigating chronic arthritic and postoperative pain [19, 23]. By progressively strengthening the musculature surrounding the weight-bearing joints, resistance training has been hypothesized in prior studies to improve joint loading distribution and absorb impact forces [33, 38]. Consequently, mitigating mechanical strain during functional movements provides superior long-term pain attenuation compared to conventional care [12, 39, 40]. These results indicate that early and progressive resistance protocols do not exacerbate joint pain but rather interrupt the vicious cycle of persistent pain and subsequent functional decline [41, 42].

Effects of resistance training on health-related quality of life

Changes in overall health-related quality of life were evaluated at baseline (T0) and at the final 3-month postoperative follow-up (T3). At the final measurement (T3), mean WHOQOL-BREF scores increased from 64.58 ± 7.56 to 91.92 ± 11.21 in the experimental group, and from 63.25 ± 7.67 to 84.08 ± 8.10 in the control group.

Chronic osteoarthritis imposes a substantial psychosocial burden, where chronic pain and physical disability frequently culminate in psychological distress, anxiety, and depression, severely compromising patients’ psychosocial well-being [41, 43]. By coupling surgical restoration with staged resistance training with volume-based progression, patients experience rapid improvements in lower extremity fitness, muscle mass, and balance, which directly translates to the preservation of independence in activities of daily living and reduced fall risk [10]. Although the lack of statistical significance at the p < .05 level may be attributed to a power limitation from the small sample size (N = 24), the marked functional gains achieved by the intervention group demonstrate that tailored resistance training effectively enhances physical function, mitigates psychological tolls, and elevates the overall quality of life in older individuals undergoing joint arthroplasty [10, 34, 43, 44].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this pilot study suggests that a hospital-initiated, staged bilateral resistance training program is feasible and safe for patients following unilateral total joint arthroplasty (including total knee and hip arthroplasty). Preliminary findings indicate that the intervention was associated with improvements in lower extremity muscular endurance (chair-stand performance), dynamic balance ability, and pain intensity compared with routine postoperative care alone. Given the exploratory nature and limited sample size of this study, definitive conclusions regarding quality of life, psychological recovery, or broad clinical effectiveness cannot be drawn. Larger, prospectively registered, adequately powered, and assessor-blinded randomized controlled trials are required to confirm these preliminary findings and establish long-term clinical efficacy.

Clinical implications for nursing practice

These preliminary findings provide practical insights for acute orthopedic nursing care. Clinical nurses are well-positioned to initiate early post-operative education, monitor compliance via structured reminder leaflets, and guide safe non-operated limb conditioning prior to bilateral progressive loading. By incorporating volume-progressive resistance exercise into inpatient care and discharge planning, nurses can facilitate a safer transition from acute care to community living, help prevent early post-discharge fall risks, and promote overall functional independence.

To build upon these preliminary results, future definitive randomized controlled trials should employ multi-center designs, prospective registration, larger sample sizes, and blinded outcome assessors. Furthermore, future studies should incorporate individualized, objective load-progression modalities (e.g., periodic % 1RM reassessments), track quantitative intervention fidelity, and extend follow-up periods to evaluate long-term biomechanical adaptations, joint longevity, and cost-effectiveness.

Limitations and recommendations for future research

Several methodological limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, as a pilot randomized trial, the study was conducted at a single medical center with a relatively small sample size (N = 24) and a short 3-month follow-up, which restricts the precision of effect estimates and the generalizability of findings to broader post-arthroplasty populations. Second, retrospective clinical trial registration may introduce potential reporting bias, although all outcome measures strictly adhered to the IRB-approved protocol. Third, despite randomization, a baseline imbalance was present in dynamic balance performance and exercise habits, which may have confounded treatment responsiveness despite statistical adjustment.

Fourth, due to human resource constraints, the same researcher delivered the intervention and conducted functional evaluations without assessor blinding, raising potential risk of performance or detection bias. Fifth, while post-discharge adherence was tracked via self-reported logbooks and phone follow-ups, quantitative intervention fidelity and formal home exercise compliance reporting were incomplete. Sixth, progression in our intervention protocol relied on expanding exercise volume (sets and repetitions) and staged bilateral progression under a fixed 1-kg external resistance, rather than individualized, progressive load increments. While this volume-based progressive approach was specifically selected to maintain strict safety standards and reduce mechanical strain on the acute/subacute surgical joint in frail older adults, it may suboptimally stimulate maximal muscle hypertrophy or peak strength adaptations in individuals with higher baseline physical capacity. Finally, the pilot sample size limited statistical power to perform multiple outcome comparisons without Type I error inflation and restricted the ability to detect rare intervention-related harms or adverse events.

Future multi-center, adequately powered randomized controlled trials with prospective registration, blinded outcome assessors, individualized load progression, and longer follow-up periods are warranted to confirm these preliminary findings.
