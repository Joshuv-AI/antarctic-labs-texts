Non-Hausdorff manifolds over locally ordered spaces via sheaf theory
Yorgo Chamoun, Emmanuel Haucourt

Abstract
Locally ordered spaces can be used as topological models of concurrent programs: the local order models the irreversibility of time during execution. Under certain conditions, one can even work with locally ordered manifolds . In this paper, we build the universal euclidean local order over every locally ordered space; in categorical terms, the subcategory of euclidean local orders is coreflective in the category of locally ordered spaces. Our construction is based on a well-known correspondance between sheaves and étale bundles . This is a far reaching generalization of a result about realizations of graph products. We particularize the construction to locally ordered realization of precubical sets , and show that it admits a purely combinatorial description. With the same proof techniques, we show that, unlike for the topological realization, there is a unique (up to symmetry) precubical set whose locally ordered realization is isomorphic to ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} .

The optimal schedules for executing a concurrent program correspond to

Plan of the paper.

We generalize to
any

.
The construction is functorial, and provides the right adjoint to the inclusion of the full subcategory of euclidean local orders into that of locally ordered spaces (§

Before this, we recall some well-known facts about precubical sets and locally ordered spaces (§

Then we focus on locally ordered realizations of precubical sets (which form a large class of locally ordered spaces), for which blowups have a purely combinatorial description, and which can be used in practice; this provides a direct generalization of the approach in
[

]
. By passing, we prove a bunch of theorems which are interesting on their own, about locally ordered realizations of precubical sets (§

The paper heavily relies on the machinery of

]
(§II.1 and §I.1 (vii) respectively).

A

is a functor

, i.e. a collection of sets

, for

, and a collection of maps

for

\mathcal{C}
, satisfying
P(f\circ g)=P(g)\circ P(f)
and
P(\operatorname{id}_{c})=\operatorname{id}_{P(c)}
for all morphisms
f,\,g
and objects
c
(where
\operatorname{id}_{c}
denotes the identity morphism at
c
).

A
sheaf
on a topological space
X
is a presheaf on the posetal category
O(X)
of open sets of
X
satisfying an amalgamation property with respect to open covers. Namely, a presheaf
F

is a sheaf if and only if for every open set

and every open cover

of

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(U)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{i\in I}F(U_{i})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\prod_{i,j\in I}F(U_{i}\cap U_{j})}

where the upper morphism is given by the
\prod_{i\in I}F(U_{i})\xrightarrow{}F(U_{i})\xrightarrow{}F(U_{i}\cap U_{j})
and the lower morphism is given by the
\prod_{i\in I}F(U_{i})\xrightarrow{}F(U_{j})\xrightarrow{}F(U_{i}\cap U_{j})
. The standard example is the sheaf which associates to an open set
U
the set of continuous functions

; the above diagram being an equalizer just says that a continuous function

for all

. Given a graph

Precubical sets can be naturally seen as the generalization of graphs in higher dimension, in the following sense. Let
\square
be the free monoidal category on
\mathcal{G}
with unit

. In particular, the objects are the natural numbers. A

can then be defined as a presheaf on

. More explicitly, a precubical set

(or

n

of

(for

, which are called the

. More precisely, we note
\delta_{n,i}^{\varepsilon}:P(n)\xrightarrow{}P(n-1)
, with
\varepsilon\in\{-,+\}
(or

),

-face maps corresponding to every dimension, whose interpretation is the following:
\delta_{n,i}^{\varepsilon}
associates to an

-cube its back (
\varepsilon=-
) or front (
\varepsilon=+
) face in direction

, and these are subject to the cocubical relations

\delta^{\varepsilon}_{k,j}\delta^{\eta}_{k+1,i}=\delta^{\eta}_{k,i}\delta^{\varepsilon}_{k+1,j+1}

for

. This completely describes a precubical set

. For example, the precubical set freely generated by a single

is given by:
\delta_{2,1}^{-}(c)
\delta_{2,0}^{+}(c)
\delta_{2,0}^{-}(c)
\delta_{2,1}^{+}(c)
c
\delta_{1,0}^{-}\circ\delta_{2,0}^{-}(c)
\delta_{1,0}^{+}\circ\delta_{2,0}^{-}(c)
\delta_{1,0}^{+}\circ\delta_{2,1}^{-}(c)
\delta_{1,0}^{+}\circ\delta_{2,1}^{+}(c)

A face of a cube obtained by successively applying morphisms of the form

(respectively

(respectively

) face. A vertex which is an upper (respectively lower) face is called the

(respectively the

) of the cube. The

of a precubical set

is the maximal

if it exists, and
\aleph_{0}
if it does not exist. A precubical set

is called

-cubes of

is exactly the set of
k
-faces of
n
-cubes of

are

if one is a face of the other. The

of two precubical sets

and

is defined by
(P\otimes Q)_{n}=\bigcup_{i+j=n}P_{i}\times Q_{j}
with the expected face maps. See for example
[

, §3.4.1]
.

Note that the category of precubical sets can be seen as a subcategory of the category of
symmetric
precubical sets, which is the category of presheaves over the free

monoidal category on
\mathcal{G}
with unit

(see
[

for details). More concretely, a symmetric precubical set

is a precubical set for which every

is equipped with an action of the symmetric group

(compatible with the face maps in an obvious way). Every precubical set can be seen as a symmetric precubical set, by freely adding the images of these actions (this is the left adjoint to the precomposition by the inclusion
\square\xrightarrow{}\square_{\mathsf{sym}}
). In what follows, by isomorphic precubical sets (noted

), we mean precubical sets which are isomorphic as symmetric precubical sets. This just means that we allow ourselves to permute the dimensions of a cube: intuitively, an
n
-cube of a symmetric precubical set

is represented by the

of some element of

. This will be crucial, since we will be looking for combinatorial analogs of embeddings of ordered spaces. For example, the map

is indeed an automorphism of

equipped with the product topology and order.

2.3
Locally ordered spaces

An
ordered space
is a topological space equipped with an order relation. A morphism of ordered spaces is a continuous order-preserving map. An isomorphism of ordered spaces is called a
dihomeomorphism
. However, this notion is too strict in general. For example, we would like to define a directed path in
X
to be a morphism
[0,1]\xrightarrow{}X
, where
[0,1]
has the natural topology and order, but then no directed
loop
exists in
X
, which is a problem as soon as we try to define a satisfying directed version of the circle. This motivates the following definition: a
locally ordered space
, or
local order
, is a topological space
X
which has an open base of ordered sets. More formally, let
X
be a topological space. An
ordered base

\mathcal{B}
of
X
is a collection of (partially) ordered sets satisfying the following conditions:

•

the underlying sets of the elements of
\mathcal{B}
form a base for the topology of
X
,

•

for every
B,\,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
and
x\in B\cap B^{\prime}
, there is
B^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
x\in B^{\prime\prime}\subseteq B\cap B^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime\prime}
is a subposet of
B
and
B^{\prime}
.

A locally ordered space is defined to be a topological space equipped with an ordered base
\mathcal{B}
. The elements of
\mathcal{B}
are then called
ordered open sets
. We can now define the directed circle to be the local order whose underlying topological space is the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and whose ordered open sets are the proper open sets of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with the order induced by, say, the anti-clockwise direction. These are clearly order-compatible. See the picture below, where we have represented two elements of the ordered base in grey, with the order induced by the arrow.

A morphism of local orders (respectively a
local embedding
)
f:X\xrightarrow{}Y
is a continuous map which is locally order preserving (respectively locally an
embedding
), i.e. for every
x\in X
and ordered neighborhood
V
of
f(x)
, there exists an ordered neighborhood
U
of
x
such that
f(U)\subseteq V
and
f_{|U}
is order preserving (respectively order preserving and admitting a continuous order preserving inverse on its image). A locally ordered space is
euclidean
(of dimension
n
) if every point has an ordered neighborhood which is dihomeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with the product order). Note that euclidean local orders can be thought of as ’locally ordered (topological) manifolds’. A
directed path
is a morphism of locally ordered spaces whose domain is euclidean of dimension 1 and connected. We note
\mathbb{L}
the category of local orders and locally ordered maps,
\mathbb{L}_{e}
the category of locally ordered spaces and local embeddings, and
\mathcal{E}_{n}
the category of euclidean local orders of dimension
n
and local embeddings. Equivalent definitions of locally ordered spaces are listed in
[
CH24
]
.

2.4
Locally ordered realization of precubical sets

In algebraic topology, we are used to having a realization functor from combinatorial to continuous models. For example, every precubical set
P
admits a geometric realization
|P|
, which is simply given by left Kan extension. An explicit description can be found in
[
FRG06
, Definition 6.5]
; we mention that the underlying set of

is
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}P_{n}\times]0,1[^{n}
, and that

is sequential since it is a quotient of sequential spaces. For

,

will mean the realization of the precubical subset of

spanned
by

, i.e.
c
and all its faces in all dimensions. Note that
|c|
is closed in
|P|
. Also note that
\{c\}\times]0,1[^{n}
is open and included in
\mathring{|c|}
the interior of
|c|
, and they are in fact equal when all the faces of

are distinct. For an open subset
U
of
|P|
and a cube

, we say that

is
a cube of

U

intersects

U\cap\{c\}\times]0,1[^{\dim(c)}\neq\varnothing.

In the directed case, the situation is more complicated because the category of locally ordered spaces, unlike the category of topological spaces, is not cocomplete
[
CH24
]
.
Given a precubical set
P
, we call
locally ordered realization
of

any locally ordered space
|P|_{lo}
(with ordered base
\mathcal{B}
) satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

The underlying topological space of
|P|_{lo}
is the geometric realization of

(in particular the underlying set is the same).

(ii)

For every
B\in\mathcal{B}
, the preorder given by

if and only if there is a path from

to

that is increasing on each cube
that it traverses (which we call cubewise increasing) is a partial order and coincides with the order on

(iii)

For every

,

is
order convex
in the sense that for every

, if
x\in\{c\}\times]0,1[^{\dim(c)}
and
y\in\{c^{\prime}\}\times]0,1[^{\dim(c^{\prime})}
with

a lower (respectively upper) face of
c^{\prime}
and such that the segment
]x,y[
(respectively
]y,x[
) is directed in

, then
x\leq y
(respectively
y\leq x
) in

.

Note that there is at most one such realization up to isomorphism, so we can soundly talk about
the
locally ordered realization of a precubical set. Such a realization is known to exist for the (large) class of
non-self-linked
precubical sets
[
FRG06
, §6.5]
; it actually exists in even bigger generality, as will be shown in a forthcoming paper of the second author. We will also suppose that the ordered base
\mathcal{B}
satisfies the following property, which will be of technical use (see Lemma
5.3
and the discussion after it):

(iv)

For every vertex
v
of

, an ordered neighborhood

of
v
only intersects cubes adjacent to
v
, and for every cube
c
with minimum (respectively maximum)

, for every point
x\in\{c\}\times]0,1[^{\dim(c)}
, every point

(respectively
y\leq x
) in

belongs to
\{c^{\prime}\}\times]0,1[^{\dim(c^{\prime})}
for some cube

having

as lower (respectively upper) face.

In this paper, we implicitely only consider precubical sets which have a locally ordered realization.

Definition 3.1
.

Let

, and

.

As mentioned before, the category

2.

\varphi_{ij}(X_{ij}\cap X_{ik})=X_{ji}\cap X_{jk}

3.

\varphi_{jk}\circ\varphi_{ij}=\varphi_{ik}
on

Then we can consider the diagram

and the

together with the inclusions
\iota_{ij}:X_{ij}\hookrightarrow{}X_{i}
and the isomorphisms

.

Proposition 3.2
.

The diagram

admits a colimit in
\mathbb{L}
.

Proof.

First we form its colimit in the category of topological spaces. Note
X
this colimit. Using
[
Duc14
, (3.2.13)]
, we know that the structure maps

are

, in the sense that they induce an isomorphism (of topological spaces) between

and an open subset of

, and

is the union of (the image of) all the
X_{i}
,
i\in I
, the intersection of
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
in

being (the image of)

(or equivalently

). Now we have to exhibit an ordered base on

. For every

, there is an ordered base
(B_{\scalebox{0.6}{${k}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${i}$)}})_{k\in K(i)}

. Consider the collection of (the image of) all these families in

. We just need to show that if

4
Blowing up via sheaves

As suggested in the introduction, the blowup of a local order
X
is a bundle whose fibers over
x\in X
are the equivalence classes of ways of embedding
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into
X
by sending
0
to
x
. In fact, there is a construction in sheaf theory which essentially does exactly this. This will lead to a vast generalization of the construction of Theorem
1.1
, to all local orders, and we will still have the same nice universal property.

4.1
The blowup

Recall that for a sheaf
F
on
X
and a point
x\in X
, the
stalk
of
F
at
x
is given by
F_{x}:=\operatorname{colim}_{V\ni x}F(V)
. Given an open set
U\ni x
, the
germ

A_{x}
of
A\in F(U)
at
x
is the equivalence class of
A
in
F_{x}
. The corresponding equivalence relation is noted
\sim_{x}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
X
be a local order. We define the
sheaf of
n
-traversals

O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}
by the setting:

O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}(U):=\{A\subseteq U\,|\,A\cong E\text{ with $E$ some $n$-euclidean local order}\}

for an open set
U\subseteq X
, the restriction map
O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}(V)\xrightarrow{}O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}(U)
for
U\subseteq V
being given by intersection with
U
.

Remark 4.2
.

1.

The restriction maps are well-defined because an open subset of a euclidean local order is euclidean.

2.

This is a priori only a presheaf (but we will prove that it is a sheaf), and the sections are not
n
-traversals (but we will prove that the germs essentially are).

3.

The empty set
\varnothing
is trivially a euclidean local order, so we always have an element
\varnothing_{x}\in O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X,x}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}
. This is in fact crucial to make
O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}
into a presheaf.

We can think of
O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}(U)
as the set of equivalence classes of euclidean embeddings in
U
with respect to the relation ’having the same image’. So for a euclidean embedding
f
, we may write
[f:E\xrightarrow{}U]
to mean
\operatorname{Im}(f)
. Here embedding just means that
f
is an isomorphism of locally ordered spaces on its image.

Proposition 4.3
.

For every local order
X
,
O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}
is a sheaf.

Proof.

We have to prove that given a family of open sets
(U_{i})_{i\in I}
and a family of subsets
(A_{i})_{i\in I}
such that
A_{i}\in O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}(U_{i})
and
A_{i}\cap U_{j}=A_{j}\cap U_{i}
for all
i,j\in I
, there is a unique
A\in O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}(U)
such that
A\cap U_{i}=A_{i}
for all
i
. So consider such family. Uniqueness is obvious since we have local uniqueness on an open cover. So we just need to prove existence. Clearly if
A
exists then
A=\bigcup_{i}A_{i}
, so set
A
like this. Choose a representative
[f_{i}:E_{i}\xrightarrow{}U_{i}]=A_{i}
for all
i
. We want to use §
3
to glue the
E_{i}
. For every

, define
E_{ij}:=f_{i}^{-1}(A_{i}\cap A_{j})
. Notice that since
\operatorname{Im}(f_{i})=A_{i}
, we have

f_{i}^{-1}(U_{j})=f_{i}^{-1}(U_{j}\cap A_{i})=f_{i}^{-1}(A_{j}\cap U_{i})=f_{i}^{-1}(A_{j}\cap U_{i}\cap A_{i})=E_{ij}

so
E_{ij}
is an open subset of
E_{i}
. Let
\varphi_{ij}:=f_{j}^{-1}\circ f_{i}:E_{ij}\cong E_{ji}
. Taking
X_{i}:=E_{i}
and
X_{ij}:=E_{ij}
, all the conditions of §
3
are trivially verified, and we note

the colimit representing the gluing of the

along the

. By the universal property of the colimit, the family
(f_{i})_{i\in I}
defines a map

.

is euclidean and

is an isomorphism whose inverse is given by the morphism induced by the family
(f_{i}^{-1})_{i\in I}
(note that

is the gluing of the family

), because these properties can be checked locally. This finishes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

For a local order
X
and
A\in O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${n}$)}}(U)
for some

is such that

, then

is an

-traversal is of this form.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of the following two facts: every isomorphism of ordered spaces is an isomorphism of locally ordered spaces, and every isomorphism of locally ordered spaces is locally (i.e. up to restriction) an isomorphism of ordered spaces.
∎

One could argue that, in view of Definition
1.2
, we are only interested in the images of

embeddings from an open subset of

to some ordered open set of

, which justifies our definition. This is an easy consequence of the fact that they have the same set of germs at every point

.

An
étale bundle
over a space
X
is a morphism of topological spaces

such that for every
y\in Y
, there is a neighborhood
U
of

such that

is open and
f_{|U}:U\to f(U)
is an isomorphism of topological spaces. Recall that there is an equivalence of categories between étale bundles over a topological space

and sheaves on

[

, §II.6]
. This was extended in
[

to the locally ordered setting, i.e. replacing ’topological space’ by ’locally ordered space’. In fact, we can reformulate this result by noticing that a structure of local order can be pulled back in a unique way along a local homeomorphism, like for smooth manifold structures. We want to use this operation in order to blow up any local order. The next proposition is just a translation of the mentioned results in our case. We fix a natural number

Proposition 4.5
.

Let

•

Its underlying set map is given by the projection

•

An ordered base is given by the sets

Moreover, for every

is open and an isomorphism of local orders on its image, and

is a local dihomeomorphism.

Now we have an étale bundle over

. However, the blowup of

cannot possibly be an étale bundle, since it is always euclidean (and not

However, there is a subtelty. In light of Proposition

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we use the abstract theory of sheaves to generalize a blowup construction on all locally ordered spaces. This is interesting for several reasons. Firstly, sheaf theory significantly simplifies the proofs: notice that we got Proposition
4.5
for free; in fact, one can look at the amount of work needed in
[
Hau25
]
to prove Theorem
1.1
, and compare it to our proof of the more general Theorem
4.8
(however, the proof from
[
Hau25
]
also provides an explicit description of the blowup, which also needed a lot of work in §
5
). Secondly, doing the link with sheaf theory is interesting on its own right, and paves the way to generalizations of this procedure outside of directed algebraic topology. In fact, the first author is working on a generalization of the blowup in an abstract setting, which can be particularized to various contexts, including differential geometry and sequential spaces, and with possible applications to logic
[
dAM23
,
TV08
]
. One can also wonder whether the sheaf
O_{\scalebox{0.6}{${X}$}}^{\scalebox{0.6}{(${1}$)}}
for some local order
X
represents some kind of ‘ordered tangent bundle’ of
X
, since its stalk at
x\in X
precisely represents the equivalence classes of directed path crossing
x
. In addition, the idea of ‘completing’ the blowup into an étale bundle could be interesting on its own, as it replaces a map with a much better behaved one. Finally, would also be inclined to adapt the sheaf theoretic blowup procedure to other models of directed spaces, such as streams
[
Kri09
]
which already have a sheaf theoretic flavour.

More concretely, we wonder if the results of
[
Hau25
, §6]
can be extended to our setting; in other words, if there is a pseudometric on the blowup of the locally ordered realization of a higher dimensional automaton
A
which reflects the execution time of the traces on
A
. In
[
Hau25
]
, this pseudometric comes from a
smooth
manifold structure on the blowup. In this paper, we have only defined a locally ordered manifold structure on the blowup, but as suggested in the introduction, it should not be very hard to get a smooth atlas in the case of locally ordered realizations of precubical sets (we even conjecture the existence of an atlas whose related transition maps are identities). This would provide concrete applications of the generalized blowup to the study of conccurent systems. However, this is a bit far from the core subject of the present paper, and will be developed in a future work.
