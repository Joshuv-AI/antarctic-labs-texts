A questionnaire study on dental radiation knowledge and anxiety among patients applying to a faculty of dentistry
Authors: Safiye Pelin Sahiner, Rahmetullah Can, Gozde Serindere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate patients’ knowledge-related responses, attitudes, anxiety levels, and radiation risk perceptions regarding dental radiographic imaging methods.Materials and methods A structured 30-item questionnaire was administered face-to-face to 410 participants. Anxiety and radiation risk perception were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, while knowledge-related responses were evaluated using categorical responses. Constructs were analyzed separately as anxiety (emotional response to radiographic procedures), risk perception (radiation-related concerns), and knowledge-related responses (factual awareness of radiation).ResultsA total of 410 participants were included (mean age: 32.98 ± 12.66 years; 58.8% female). Overall anxiety levels regarding radiographic procedures were low; however, increased anxiety was observed in situations involving radiation exposure and absence of protective aprons. Radiation risk perception was generally at a moderate level; however, the tendency to refuse or postpone radiographic examinations recommended by the dentist was low. Female participants had higher radiation risk perception scores; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Regarding knowledge-related responses, participants demonstrated higher awareness of computed tomography (CT) and natural background radiation, while uncertainty was prominent regarding magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and panoramic radiography (PR) dose.ConclusionAlthough patients generally tolerate dental radiographic procedures with low anxiety, uncertainties regarding radiation exposure contribute to increased perceived risk. These findings highlight the need for structured, evidence-based patient information strategies to improve radiation literacy and support informed acceptance of dental imaging procedures.Keywords: Dental Radiography, Radiation Exposure, Anxiety, Patient Attitudes

Introduction

Radiographic imaging is an essential tool in diagnosis and treatment planning in dentistry. The primary imaging modalities used in routine dental examinations and treatments include periapical radiographs, bite-wing imaging, panoramic radiography (PR), and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) for advanced diagnostic needs. One of the major factors contributing to the widespread use of dental radiographic methods is their substantially lower radiation doses compared with medical imaging modalities. For example, the average effective dose of PR corresponds to only a small fraction of daily natural background radiation [1]. Similarly, CBCT imaging operates at significantly lower doses than conventional medical computed tomography (CT) systems and can be optimized according to the region of interest.

Nevertheless, due to the potential biological effects of ionizing radiation on tissues, the associated risk cannot be completely eliminated. Previous studies conducted in different populations have reported that while most patients consider dental radiography diagnostically necessary, they may simultaneously possess insufficient or inaccurate knowledge regarding radiation risks, imaging indications, and protective measures [2, 3]. In this context, systematically assessing patients’ knowledge-related responses, attitudes, and anxiety related to radiographic examinations through questionnaires is crucial for improving dentist–patient communication and reducing unnecessary examination refusal or diagnostic deviations caused by inappropriate imaging decisions. Taken together, these findings suggest a discrepancy between patients’ acceptance of dental radiography and their actual understanding of radiation-related risks, indicating the need for a more comprehensive evaluation of these constructs within the same population.

Although several studies have investigated radiation awareness among healthcare professionals, students, and the general public, research specifically evaluating patients’ dental radiation anxiety and knowledge-related responses remains limited [4–6]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the knowledge-related responses, attitudes, concerns, and radiation perceptions of patients admitted to a Faculty of Dentistry regarding dental radiographic imaging methods. It was hypothesized that while patients would exhibit low anxiety toward routine dental radiographic procedures, their radiation risk perception would be shaped by incomplete knowledge and influenced by sociodemographic characteristics. The findings are expected to contribute to strengthening dentist–patient communication, developing patient education strategies, and preventing unnecessary examination refusal or inappropriate radiographic demands in clinical practice. Moreover, by providing current and local data, this study aims to contribute to the limited literature evaluating patient perceptions specific to dental radiography and to support the development of patient-centered and safe imaging approaches.

Materials and methods

The study protocol was approved by the Ordu University Non-Interventional Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2025/436; Date: 12.12.2025) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. This cross-sectional study included voluntary patients aged 18–80 years who were admitted for various reasons to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology at Ordu University Faculty of Dentistry. Data were collected between December 15, 2025 and March 1, 2026.

Prior to the study, sample size was determined using an a priori power analysis with G*Power software (v3.1.9.6). Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.20), a significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power (1–β) = 0.90, and five groups, the minimum required sample size was calculated as approximately 390 participants.

Exclusion criteria included individuals who were unable to understand or complete the questionnaire due to cognitive impairment, illiteracy, or communication difficulties; participants who completed the questionnaire incompletely or provided inconsistent responses; and individuals with professional knowledge or training in dental imaging (e.g., dentists, dental students, radiology technicians). Eligible participants completed the questionnaire through face-to-face interviews. The questionnaire was administered in Turkish, which is the native language of the study population. The questionnaire used in this study was developed by the authors and used for the first time in this study, with its items adapted from previously published studies on knowledge and awareness [1–12]. The questionnaire items were reviewed by a panel consisting of one faculty member, one specialist, and one research assistant in Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology to ensure content relevance, clarity, and appropriateness for the target population. Minor modifications were made accordingly to improve comprehensibility. As this study was not designed as a scale development or validation study, advanced psychometric validation analyses were not performed.

The questionnaire consisted of four sections and a total of 30 items. The first section assessed demographic characteristics, including age, gender, education level, and frequency of dental visits. Education level was categorized as primary school, secondary school, high school, university, and postgraduate. Frequency of dental visits was classified as less than once per year, once per year, two to three times per year, and more than three times per year.

The second section comprised 10 items evaluating patients’ anxiety and emotional responses related to dental radiographic procedures. The third section included nine items assessing radiation-related concerns and risk perception. Items in the second and third sections were evaluated using a five-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated “none,” 2 “very low,” 3 “moderate,” 4 “high,” and 5 “extreme.” The fourth and final section included 11 items assessing participants’ knowledge-related responses regarding radiation, with response options of “yes,” “no,” or “undecided.” Correct answers for knowledge-related items were defined based on current scientific literature and used for item-level interpretation. The questionnaire was not treated as a single psychometric scale. Instead, items were grouped and analyzed according to their conceptual domains, namely anxiety (Sect. 2), radiation risk perception (Sect. 3), and knowledge (Sect. 4).

For Sects. 2 and 3, domain-based aggregate scores were calculated by summing Likert-type item responses within each conceptual domain to reflect general response tendencies rather than precise latent constructs. These scores were used for descriptive and inferential statistical analyses.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and JASP (Version 0.19.0; University of Amsterdam, Netherlands). Distribution properties of continuous variables were evaluated using visual methods (histograms and probability plots) and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum) for continuous variables, and as frequency and percentage (%) for categorical variables.

The Likert-type items assessing anxiety and radiation risk perception were analyzed both at the item level and at the domain level. Item-level distributions were reported for each question. In addition, domain-based aggregate scores were calculated by summing item responses within each conceptual domain (anxiety and radiation risk perception) to provide a general indication of response tendencies within each construct. These domain-based aggregate scores were not intended to represent a validated psychometric scale and were interpreted within an exploratory framework. Since domain-based scores were not normally distributed, nonparametric tests were applied for group comparisons. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons involving three or more groups. Internal consistency was assessed separately for each domain using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, with values ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable. The relationship between age and domain-based scores was evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 410 participants were included in the study. The mean age was 32.98 ± 12.66 years. Of the participants, 58.8% were female, 37.6% were male, and 3.7% preferred not to disclose their gender. The largest educational group consisted of university graduates (56.6%), followed by high school (23.9%), postgraduate (6.8%), secondary school (6.6%), and primary school graduates (6.1%). Regarding dental visit frequency, 37.3% reported visiting the dentist once per year, 34.1% less than once per year, 21.7% two to three times per year, and 6.8% more frequently (Fig. 1). Internal consistency analysis yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.857 for Sect. 2 (anxiety assessment) and 0.858 for Sect. 3 (radiation risk perception), indicating acceptable internal consistency for both sections.

Discussion

In recent years, the indispensable role of medical and dental imaging in diagnostic and therapeutic processes has led to increased use of ionizing radiation. Easier access to imaging modalities has resulted in more frequent radiographic examinations across broader patient populations. While this enhances diagnostic accuracy and treatment success, it has also contributed to growing public concern regarding radiation exposure and its potential biological effects. The possible role of ionizing radiation within the multifactorial etiology of increasing cancer incidence remains a topic of debate in both scientific literature and public discourse. Consequently, patients’ perceptions, attitudes, and knowledge regarding dental radiography have gained increasing importance [7]. Despite this growing interest, there remains a need to better understand how these factors coexist and influence patient behavior in clinical practice. The questionnaire was designed as a structured exploratory instrument to capture multiple distinct domains rather than to measure a single latent construct. In this context, the present study aimed to evaluate patients’ knowledge-related responses, attitudes, anxiety levels, and radiation risk perceptions regarding dental radiographic imaging using a structured questionnaire.

In the present study, anxiety levels related to dental radiographic procedures were generally low, which may be attributed to the routine and widespread integration of dental radiography into clinical practice. However, increased anxiety in specific situations—such as perceived radiation exposure and lack of protective aprons—suggests that patients may be more sensitive to radiation-related uncertainties than to the procedure itself. These findings may reflect a combination of limited patient knowledge regarding radiation protection, potential misconceptions or outdated beliefs about shielding practices, and communication gaps in clinical settings, rather than a single explanatory factor. Similarly, Qari et al. [8] reported that the public often overestimates radiation doses associated with dental radiography and experiences considerable uncertainty regarding long-term effects. The low rates of radiography refusal or postponement observed in the current study may indicate that, despite radiation-related concerns, trust in clinicians remains a key determinant in decision-making. This finding suggests that radiation risk perception may be influenced not only by knowledge-related responses but also by dentist–patient communication and the perceived diagnostic necessity of imaging. Consistent with this, Alsubaie and Abujamea [9] reported that although many patients undergoing CT imaging in emergency settings expressed concern about radiation risk, this concern did not influence imaging decisions.

Regarding radiation risk perception, participants in this study exhibited higher concern particularly about cancer risk, cumulative radiation effects, and radiation sensitivity in children. Although no statistically significant gender difference was observed (p = 0.057), female participants showed a non-significant tendency toward higher radiation risk perception scores, consistent with previous literature [10]. The absence of significant associations between education level or dental visit frequency and risk perception suggests that radiation perception may involve multiple factors beyond knowledge or experience alone.

Although most participants were aware that CT uses X-rays and that natural background radiation exists, misconceptions regarding radiation use in MRI and uncertainty about PR dose were notable. These findings indicate that general radiation awareness does not necessarily translate into adequate knowledge specific to dental imaging. To avoid misinterpretation of the knowledge-related responses, it should be noted that each item was evaluated individually based on current scientific literature and established radiological principles. The responses were not combined into a composite score; instead, results were presented in a descriptive format to reflect item-level understanding. Correct responses were defined a priori according to evidence-based radiological knowledge. Similar conclusions were reported by Öner Talmaç et al. [3], who found insufficient patient knowledge regarding radiation hazards and protective measures in dental settings.

Studies in different populations also show limited public awareness and ongoing misconceptions about radiation exposure in dentistry. In a survey of 437 patients, most participants accepted the need for dental radiographs. However, many stated that dentists did not sufficiently explain radiation risks. More than half of the participants reported that they rarely or never asked about safety measures before imaging [1]. A recent Turkish study involving 520 dental patients found low overall awareness of radiation used in dental imaging, with higher education level, income, and frequency of dental visits positively associated with awareness [11]. Collectively, these findings underscore that knowledge gaps and ambiguous perceptions regarding dental radiography are not confined to a single population and highlight the importance of targeted patient education and effective clinician communication across clinical settings [12].

To clarify the interpretation of the knowledge-related responses in Sect. 4, a literature-based framework was used. Lead aprons do not fully eliminate radiation exposure during pregnancy, as they primarily reduce scattered radiation rather than the primary beam [13, 14]. MRI does not involve ionizing radiation, whereas CT is an ionizing modality based on X-rays. Ultrasound does not use ionizing radiation [15]. Natural background radiation is an established daily exposure source from cosmic and terrestrial origins [16]. Radiation exposure during airport screening may vary depending on the system used, although most modern systems are non-ionizing [17]. Digital radiography and PR involve relatively low radiation doses compared to conventional techniques, with PR approximating daily background exposure [18]. CBCT generally delivers lower doses than medical CT, depending on acquisition parameters [19]. Radiographic imaging remains essential for diagnosing certain dental conditions [18].

One of the distinguishing strengths of this study is the simultaneous evaluation of knowledge-related responses, attitudes, anxiety, and radiation risk perception within the same sample using a single structured questionnaire, conducted within an exploratory framework. This holistic approach allows integrated interpretation of variables that are often examined separately in previous studies, thereby contributing complementary insights to the limited existing literature.

Patient management extends beyond technical and clinical procedures and is closely linked to patients’ perceptions, knowledge-related responses, and anxiety experiences. The present findings suggest that although general anxiety during dental radiographic procedures is low, concerns may increase in specific radiation-related contexts. Understanding the stages and reasons at which patients experience anxiety is therefore essential for effective communication and patient-centered care. Consequently, technical proficiency alone may not be sufficient in dental radiographic practice; communication strategies that prioritize patient perception should be considered an integral component of clinical success.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, its single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings to different sociocultural and geographic populations. In particular, patient perceptions and attitudes toward radiation may be influenced by local cultural factors, healthcare access, and educational background, which should be considered when interpreting the results. Second, the use of self-reported questionnaires may introduce response bias related to socially desirable answering. Another limitation is related to the questionnaire design. This study was not intended as a scale development or validation study; therefore, although internal consistency was assessed and content validity was supported through expert evaluation, advanced psychometric analyses (such as exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis) and pilot testing were not performed. This may affect the robustness of the measurement tool. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive and exploratory rather than confirmatory or psychometric in nature. Additionally, the inclusion of only voluntary participants and the exclusion of individuals with illiteracy or communication difficulties may have introduced selection bias, potentially influencing the distribution of knowledge-related responses and radiation risk perception. These factors should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings. Nevertheless, the inclusion of a large patient sample and the simultaneous assessment of dental radiography–specific perception, anxiety, and knowledge-related responses provide a valuable contribution to the limited literature.

From a clinical perspective, informing patients about radiation doses and protective measures may help reduce anxiety and promote more realistic risk perception. The observed tendency toward higher risk perception among female patients emphasizes the importance of tailoring communication and education strategies to demographic differences. Future large-scale, multicenter studies including diverse sociodemographic groups may help validate these findings and improve their generalizability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, patients’ general anxiety toward dental radiographic procedures was low, whereas radiation-related risk perception was comparatively higher. This suggests that although patients accept the clinical necessity of dental imaging, uncertainty and limited understanding remain important contributors to perceived risk. These findings highlight the importance of clear and targeted patient communication, as brief pre-procedural information may reduce anxiety and improve compliance. Integrating structured patient education into routine dental practice may help prevent unnecessary examination refusal and enhance clinical decision-making. Further multicenter studies are needed to confirm these findings and support the development of evidence-based strategies to improve dental radiation awareness.
