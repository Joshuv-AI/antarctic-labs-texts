Body composition and aging among Tibetan adults living at high altitude: A cross-sectional study of 1,318 participants
Authors: Honghao Yang, Shunyun Xu, Hongyan Zhang, Lingye Zeng, Chengqiong Sun, Ciwang, Bianba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMost studies investigating age-related body composition patterns focus on low-altitude populations, and whether findings from these studies apply to high-altitude populations under chronic hypoxia remains unclear. This study investigated age-related body composition patterns among native Tibetan adults from the Tibetan Plateau, exploring associations with sex and altitude.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 1318 native Tibetan adults (20–85 yr) via cluster random sampling from three regions (2800, 3650, and 4500 m) in Xizang. FMI increased linearly in males, whereas in females, FMI increased before and decreased after a turning point at 69 yr (95% CI, 66–73). Longitudinal studies are required to validate the observed trajectories and turning points before these findings can inform early monitoring of muscle loss and alterations in fat metabolism, and support the development of targeted strategies for health management in populations at high altitude.Keywords: Body composition, Aging, High altitude, Hypoxia

Introduction

Globally, the population is aging rapidly, and by 2050, individuals aged ≥60 yr are projected to account for 22% of the world’s population [1]. For high-altitude populations, this demographic transition is equally profound. As a result of substantial improvements in regional healthcare and rising life expectancy, the proportion of older adults living at high altitudes has increased significantly in recent decades [2]. Age-related changes in body composition are a key manifestation of the aging process and are mainly characterized by a decline in skeletal muscle mass and an increase in fat mass. These changes can lead to a range of adverse health outcomes, including sarcopenia, osteoporosis, and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [3]. However, changes in fat mass and skeletal muscle mass may not follow linear trajectories. Therefore, elucidating age-related patterns of body composition and identifying critical turning points are essential for distinguishing physiological aging from pathological aging and for developing early warning and intervention strategies.

Research on age-related patterns in body composition has primarily focused on populations living at low altitudes [4]. It remains uncertain whether the previously reported low-altitude age-related patterns of progressive muscle loss, increasing fat mass, and altered body water distribution also apply to populations living in chronically hypoxic, high-altitude environments [4,5]. Furthermore, individuals who have lived for generations on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and in other chronically hypoxic environments have undergone substantial adaptations in physiological function and metabolic regulation [6]. These adaptations include variations in metabolism-related genes such as EPAS1, EGLN1, and PPARA, which have been identified as key genes associated with high-altitude adaptation and are associated with altitude-related body composition traits [6]. Given these adaptations, age-related body composition patterns may differ markedly from those observed in low-altitude populations. Consequently, directly applying body composition assessment criteria developed for low-altitude populations may result in bias or misclassification [7].

The objective of this study was to investigate how body composition, including appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI), fat mass index (FMI), and the extracellular-to-intracellular water ratio (ECW:ICW), differs according to age, sex, and altitude among native Tibetan adults residing on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. We also aimed to identify potential ages at which the associations between age and these body composition parameters changed.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between August 2023 and August 2024 among native Tibetan populations in three regions of the Xizang Autonomous Region (altitudes, 2800–4500 m) using cluster random sampling, with administrative villages serving as the primary sampling units. Eligible participants were native-born residents with at least three generations of local ancestry, aged ≥20 yr, who had continuously lived in the area for ≥10 yr. The participants were predominantly rural farmers and herders who followed traditional agro-pastoral lifestyles characterized by high levels of habitual physical activity and a diet centered on locally produced staples, such as roasted highland barley flour (tsampa), yak products, and limited vegetables. Exclusion criteria included the presence of chronic diseases known to significantly alter body composition, the use of medications influencing metabolism, and major recent surgery or physical trauma. The Xizang University Ethics Committee approved the study (ZDYXLL20210223). All eligible participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Anthropometric and body composition measurements

Body mass was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using an electronic scale (HN-281; OMRON, Shanghai, China), and height was measured to the nearest 0.5 cm using a stadiometer (TZG; Shanghai, China). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as body mass in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Body composition was assessed using the InBody 770 analyzer (Biospace, Seoul, South Korea), which uses direct segmental multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis. Assessments were performed in the morning in a thermoneutral room according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Before measurement, participants were instructed to fast overnight, empty their bladders, avoid alcohol consumption and vigorous physical activity for 24 h, remove all metallic accessories, and remain seated for 10 min to stabilize body fluid distribution. The following three primary analytical indices were calculated: appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI), defined as appendicular skeletal muscle mass divided by height squared [8]; fat mass index (FMI), defined as fat mass divided by height squared [9]; and the extracellular-to-intracellular water ratio (ECW:ICW) [10].

Covariates

To account for lifestyle-related differences, the Physical Activity Index (PAI) and Dietary Quality Index (DQI) were used as adjustment variables. A modified PAI, based on the core framework of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and adapted to the lifestyle characteristics of the local population, was used to assess physical activity levels. The index captured information on the frequency, duration, and intensity of physical activity [11] (Table S1).

Dietary quality was assessed using an a priori scoring approach based on the Healthy Eating Index framework [12]. Briefly, weighted scores were assigned to recommended and restricted food groups using food frequency questionnaire data collected from the participants. This approach was used to develop a DQI tailored to the dietary characteristics of the study population (Table S2).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 and R version 4.5.1. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, whereas continuous variables are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, Pearson chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To characterize age-related patterns and identify critical turning points in body composition, segmented regression models were fitted according to the method proposed by Muggeo et al. [13], with age as the independent variable. The adjusted models included altitude (entered as a categorical variable: <3000, 3000–4000, and >4000 m, based on the geographical distribution of survey sites), along with PAI and DQI scores (both entered as continuous variables) as covariates. The final model was selected based on the Bayesian information criterion. The timing of critical turning points, segment-specific slopes, and corresponding confidence intervals (CIs) were extracted. A turning point was defined as the estimated age at which the slope of the association between age and a body composition outcome changed. The direction of the association before and after each turning point was determined from the corresponding segment-specific beta coefficients.

Results

Demographic and baseline characteristics

Of the 1708 individuals screened, 1318 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. S1). Among them, 393 participants were from Gilong (2800 m), 454 were from Duilong (3650 m), and 471 were from Nierong (4500 m). The median age was 52 yr (IQR, 41–60 yr), and 440 participants (33.4%) were men. Participants from Duilong had higher median age, height, weight, BMI, percentage body fat, FMI, ECW:ICW ratio, and PAI scores than participants from the other two regions (Table 1). The highest DQI scores were observed in Gilong. Additional baseline characteristics are presented in Tables S3 and S4.

Age-related patterns of body composition

ASMI

In the adjusted segmented regression analysis, ASMI increased with age before and decreased after an estimated turning point at 43 yr in males (95% CI, 36–50) and 49 yr in females (95% CI, 45–52; Fig. 1a, b, and Table 2). In the altitude-stratified analyses, no turning points were detected among males; a linear decrease in ASMI with age was observed in males from Duilong, whereas ASMI remained stable in males from Gilong and Nierong. Among females, ASMI increased before and decreased after turning points at 47 yr in Gilong (95% CI, 41–53) and 50 yr in Nierong (95% CI, 44–55), whereas it decreased linearly with age in women from Duilong (Fig. S2 and Table 2).

FMI

After adjustment, FMI increased linearly in males, whereas in females, it increased before and decreased after an estimated turning point at 69 yr (95% CI, 66–73; Fig. 1c, d, and Table 2). Among males, FMI exhibited a predominantly linear increase across all three altitude groups. Among females, FMI increased before and decreased after turning points at 71 yr in Gilong (95% CI, 66–76) and 50 yr in Nierong (95% CI, 43–57), but increased linearly in Duilong (Fig. S3 and Table 2).

ECW:ICW

After adjustment, the ECW:ICW ratio increased linearly with age in both males and females (Figs. 1e, f, and Table 2). This linear pattern was consistently observed across the altitude gradient, with no apparent age-related turning points identified in any subgroup (Fig. S4 and Table 2).

Discussion

In this study investigating patterns and turning points in age-related body composition trajectories among Tibetan adults, we identified differences associated with sex and altitude. These findings extend previous observations from low-altitude populations to native Tibetan communities living at high altitude and suggest that altitude may influence age-related patterns in body composition. In addition, physical activity and dietary quality differed across altitude sites and might have contributed to the observed body composition patterns through effects on energy balance and muscle maintenance. Considering these potential effects, their influence was adjusted for in all models.

The estimated ASMI turning point in females occurred at approximately 49 yr (95% CI, 45–52), which is consistent with findings from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry studies in low-altitude populations [14]. It also coincides with the perimenopausal transition, supporting evidence that declining estrogen levels contribute to accelerated muscle loss [15,16]. Although earlier hypotheses linked hypoxia to earlier menopause [17], our findings are more consistent with recent evidence indicating that the onset of natural menopause in Tibetan women does not occur substantially earlier than in women living at lower altitudes in China [18]. Endocrine factors may therefore play a more prominent role than environmental stressors in age-related muscle patterns among women.

In males, the ASMI turning point at 43 yr occurred earlier than in low-altitude cohorts, including the LEAD study in Austria [19] and the INSPIRE study in France [4], which reported changes from 50 to <60 yr and at 56 yr, respectively. Long-term adaptation to chronic hypoxia may contribute to this difference. Skeletal muscle is among the most metabolically demanding tissues in the human body, and prolonged hypoxic exposure may reduce anabolic signaling through downregulation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway, thereby lowering the energetic cost of maintaining muscle mass [20]. Although the estimated ASMI turning point appeared earlier in males than in females (43 vs. 49 yr), the corresponding 95% CIs showed substantial overlap (36–50 vs. 45–52 yr). Therefore, these estimates should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive evidence of a sex difference in the timing of muscle decline. The observed difference may reflect sex-specific physiological characteristics, including differences in hormonal regulation, muscle mass reserve, habitual physical activity, and long-term adaptation to chronic hypoxia; however, sampling variability cannot be excluded. Larger longitudinal studies are required to determine whether the apparent earlier turning point in males represents a true biological difference or an observation attributable to study design and population characteristics.

The linear increase in FMI with age among males, compared with the later and relatively steep turning point observed in females living in Gilong, suggests sex-specific differences in FMI trajectories at high altitude. Furthermore, the linear relationship between FMI and age at moderate altitude, together with the substantially earlier turning point observed at the highest altitude, raises the possibility of a threshold-like association between environmental stress and fat metabolism.

At extreme altitude, multiple environmental stressors, including severe hypobaric hypoxia, cold exposure, and increased physical demands, may interact to influence metabolic status. One potential mechanism is activation of the hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) pathway, which promotes lipolysis in adipose tissue and may reduce caloric intake through appetite suppression [[21], [22], [23]]. Following menopause, when estrogen-mediated protection declines, females may become more vulnerable to these combined hypoxic and metabolic pressures, potentially contributing to an earlier FMI turning point at very high altitudes [24]. Conversely, at lower high altitudes, reduced hypoxic severity may allow physiological compensation to better preserve overall metabolic balance, resulting in the less severe fat loss patterns observed in our lower-altitude cohorts.

The linear increase in ECW:ICW observed in the present study is consistent with previous reports from low-altitude community-based studies [25]. Physiologically, a higher ECW:ICW ratio reflects a relative reduction in intracellular water accompanied by expansion of the extracellular fluid compartment. These changes may be associated with impaired sodium–potassium pump function, altered membrane permeability, and chronic low-grade inflammation [26]. Additionally, several community-based studies conducted in low-altitude populations have reported age-related increases in ECW:ICW-related indices and demonstrated associations with poorer muscle function, supporting their potential value as markers of muscle quality and biological aging [27].

The body composition turning points identified in this study may help define age-related physiological patterns in high-altitude populations and provide useful reference points for health monitoring. In males, attention to muscle mass preservation may be warranted from approximately 40 yr of age onward. In females, the perimenopausal period appears to represent a critical phase of body composition variations. Notably, the time windows identified in this study broadly align with the 2025 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia consensus, which expanded sarcopenia screening recommendations to include middle-aged adults aged 50–64 yr [28]. Our findings further suggest that muscle-related turning points may emerge even earlier in high-altitude populations, particularly among males.

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and cannot capture within-individual longitudinal changes. Stratifying by sex and altitude reduces subgroup sample sizes, potentially limiting statistical power. Although the InBody series has been validated in high-altitude Tibetans [29,30], its inherent reliance on tissue hydration assumptions means biases related to age, sex, and extreme BMI cannot be entirely excluded [30]. Despite adjusting for lifestyle factors (PAI and DQI) in our homogeneous cohort of native farmers and herders, we cannot entirely exclude residual confounding from unmeasured variables, such as socioeconomic status and healthcare access. Finally, as our sample primarily comprised rural farmers and herders, these findings may not fully generalize to urban or socioeconomically distinct high-altitude populations.

Conclusion

Among native Tibetan adults residing on the Tibetan Plateau, age-related patterns of body composition differed according to sex and altitude. Longitudinal studies are required to validate the observed trajectories and turning points before these findings can inform early monitoring of muscle loss and alterations in fat metabolism, and support the development of targeted strategies for health management in populations at high altitude.
