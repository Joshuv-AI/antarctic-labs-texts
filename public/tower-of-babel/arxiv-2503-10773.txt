Learn then Decide: A Learning Approach for Designing Data Marketplaces
Yingqi Gao, Jin Zhou, Hua Zhou, Yong Chen, Xiaowu Dai

Abstract
As data marketplaces become increasingly central to the digital economy, it is crucial to design efficient pricing mechanisms that optimize revenue while ensuring fair and adaptive pricing. We introduce the Maximum Auction-to-Posted Price (MAPP) mechanism, a novel two-stage approach that first estimates the bidders’ value distribution through auctions and then determines the optimal posted price based on the learned distribution. We establish that MAPP is individually rational and incentive-compatible, ensuring truthful bidding while balancing revenue maximization with minimal price discrimination. MAPP achieves a regret of O p ​ ( n − 1 ) O_{p}(n^{-1}) when incorporating historical bid data, where n n is the number of bids in the current round. It outperforms existing methods while imposing weaker distributional assumptions. For sequential dataset sales over T T rounds, we propose an online MAPP mechanism that dynamically adjusts pricing across datasets with varying value distributions. Our approach achieves no-regret learning, with the average cumulative regret converging at a rate of O p ( T − 1 / 2 ( log T ) 2 ) O_{p}(T^{-1/2}(\log T)^{2}) . We validate the effectiveness of MAPP through simulations and real-world data from the FCC AWS-3 spectrum auction.



Key Words
: Auctions; Data marketplace; Nonparametric density estimation; Online learning; Revenue maximization.

1
Introduction

The economic value of data has grown significantly in recent decades, driving its role as a key resource for artificial intelligence and decision making
(
Veldkamp and Chung 2024
)
. The increasing demand for data has spurred the rise of data marketplaces such as Amazon AWS Data Exchange, Datarade, and Snowflake Data Marketplace. However, these platforms remain inefficient, as transactions often involve prolonged price negotiations, resulting in high transaction costs
(
Kennedy et al. 2022
)
. This inefficiency raises the question: Can alternative pricing mechanisms be designed to not only maximize seller revenue but also enhance the efficiency of data trading?

From the seller’s perspective, an effective solution should not only reduce transaction costs but also maximize revenue. Auction theory naturally serves this purpose, with classic results by
Myerson 1981
demonstrating that auctions consistently outperform negotiations
(
Bulow and Klemperer 1996
)
. Reserve prices play a central role in this framework
(
Kanoria and
Nazerzadeh 2021
)
, but when the value distribution is unknown, setting effective reserve prices becomes challenging
(
Chawla et al. 2014
)
. Recent empirical evidence suggests that auctions are losing favor in real-world platforms such as eBay, where sellers increasingly opt for posted prices due to intensified competition and buyers’ preference for convenience
(
Einav et al. 2018
)
. This shift highlights the need for hybrid mechanisms that combine the revenue efficiency of auctions with the accessibility of posted pricing.

This paper introduces the Maximum Auction-to-Posted Price (MAPP) mechanism, a novel two-stage pricing strategy aimed at optimizing revenue in data marketplaces while minimizing price discrimination. The mechanism operates in two phases. In the auction phase, a fixed number of buyers submit bids, and each buyer is assigned a bid-independent price determined by the bids of other participants rather than their own. This approach ensures incentive compatibility, as buyers are motivated to bid truthfully. Buyers must purchase the dataset if their bid meets or exceeds their assigned price. The posted-price phase follows, during which the seller sets the highest observed auction price as the posted price. Subsequent buyers who arrive dynamically decide to purchase only if their valuations meet or exceed this price. The workflow of MAPP is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:

T
is the total number of rounds.

MAPP improves upon existing methods for selling goods with unlimited supply by combining auction-driven price discovery
(
Segal 2003
;
Goldberg et al. 2006
)
to optimize revenue from early buyers with a posted-price mechanism
(
Bar-Yossef et al. 2002
;
Kleinberg and
Leighton 2003
;
Broder and
Rusmevichientong 2012
)
to ensure accessibility for later arrivals. Unlike existing approaches, MAPP fixes the posted price at the highest observed auction price, effectively reducing price discrimination while preserving revenue efficiency. Additionally, MAPP satisfies individual rationality, ensuring voluntary participation, a key economic property that drives market efficiency.
The effectiveness of MAPP relies on accurately estimating the value distribution to set a near-optimal price. If the distribution were known, the optimal price would be clear
(
Myerson 1981
)
. However, in practice, uncertainty about the value distribution leads to regret, which quantifies the resulting revenue loss. Aligned with
Segal 2003
, we began with the commonly used kernel density estimation (KDE) approach. However, we derive an improved regret bound with finite-sample guarantees, relax its underlying assumptions, and mitigate the price discrimination inherent in the mechanism of
Segal 2003
.

When historical value observations from datasets with distributions in the same family (e.g., Gaussian) are available, we further improve the regret bound using the repeated density estimation (RDE) method of
(
Qiu et al. 2022
)
. This approach achieves a regret rate of
O_{p}(n^{-1})
rate, a significant improvement over the existing
O(n^{-2/3}(\log n)^{3/2})
rate
(
Segal 2003
;
Kleinberg and
Leighton 2003
;
Broder and
Rusmevichientong 2012
)
, where
n
denotes the number of bids received for the current dataset. RDE enhances value distribution estimation by applying functional principal component analysis to log-ratio transformed kernel-based estimates derived from historical observations of similar datasets. This technique uncovers a latent low-dimensional structure in the shared distribution family, effectively reducing the feasible space for nonparametric density estimation.

Observing that real-world data sellers often own multiple datasets from the same or similar sources, typically sharing a common value distribution family with varying parameter values, such as Gaussian distributions with different means and variances, we extend MAPP to the online setting for selling these datasets. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first online algorithm designed to price unlimited-supply goods with varying value distributions across different goods.
The online MAPP mechanism consists of two stages: an exploration stage, where value distributions are estimated using KDE to gather market insights, and an exploitation stage, where pricing strategies for subsequent datasets are refined using RDE, leveraging bid data from the exploration stage for more accurate distribution estimation.
We establish that the online MAPP mechanism achieves an average cumulative regret of
O_{p}(T^{-1/2}(\log T)^{2})
, where
T
denotes the total number of rounds or datasets. This ensures no-regret learning, with average regret diminishing as the number of sales increases. Empirical evaluations, including simulations and real-world auction data from the FCC AWS-3 spectrum auction, validate the convergence of the average cumulative regret, demonstrating the effectiveness of MAPP in data marketplaces.

2
Background

2.1
Model Setup

We consider a data marketplace where a seller continuously offers datasets generated from a data source, denoted as
\mathcal{D}
, and released over time. At each time step
t=1,\dots,T
, a new dataset
D^{(t)}
becomes available for sale. Upon its release,
n^{(t)}
buyers are present in the market, each evaluating whether to make a purchase. However, buyers have a limited waiting period, meaning they must make a purchase decision within a given timeframe before exiting the market.

To prevent unauthorized duplication and redistribution, the seller provides only a limited representation of the dataset—such as summaries or subsets—rather than full access
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Guo et al. 2022
)
. Each buyer forms a private valuation
v_{i}^{(t)}
where
i=1,\dots,n^{(t)}
based on the provided representation of the dataset. Assume that these valuations are independently drawn from an unknown distribution with density

f^{(t)}
for
t=1,\ldots,T
. A buyer purchases the dataset if their valuation meets or exceeds the posted price.
The seller aims to maximize revenue but lacks prior knowledge of
f^{(t)}
. However, it is assumed that the densities
f^{(t)}
for
t=1,\ldots,T
belong to a common density family, as defined below.

Assumption 1

(Shared Density Family)
.

The densities
f^{(1)},f^{(2)},\dots,f^{(T)}
, associated with the datasets
D^{(1)},D^{(2)},\dots,D^{(T)}
, are i.i.d. samples from a random density
f
in a family
\mathcal{F}
supported on
[1,H]
, where all datasets are generated from the same source
\mathcal{D}
.

Assumption
1
aligns with real-world scenarios, such as Quora generating datasets
D^{(t)}
containing user-submitted questions and answers. These datasets exhibit similar characteristics due to Quora’s stable user base and systematic content moderation, leading to consistent distribution patterns of buyer valuations.
The assumption that
f
is supported on
[1,H]
, where
H<\infty
is known to the seller, is commonly used in the literature
(
Goldberg et al. 2006
;
Huang et al. 2015
;
Cai and Daskalakis 2017
;
Guo et al. 2022
)
.

2.2
Incentive Measure

This paper designs a mechanism for data marketplaces that enables the seller to effectively price and sell datasets at each round

t
to arriving buyers. We seek to integrate both an auction mechanism, which gathers market information, and a posted-price mechanism, which provides a consistent price for subsequent buyers. This hybrid approach is motivated by the model setup in Section
2.1
, where buyers have a limited waiting period and must decide whether to purchase before exiting the market. This framework aligns with the broader real-world trend of transitioning from auctions to posted pricing, driven by increasing buyer impatience and the seller’s preference for operational simplicity
(
Einav et al. 2018
)
.

The designed mechanism aims to satisfy two fundamental incentive properties: individual rationality and incentive compatibility.
A mechanism is
individual-rational
if buyers always receive non-negative utility from participating
(
Vickrey 1961
)
. A mechanism is
incentive-compatible
if buyers maximize their utility by bidding truthfully, with no incentive to misrepresent their valuations
(
Groves 1973
)
.
By ensuring that buyers have no incentive to manipulate their participation or bids, such mechanisms encourage engagement and truthful bidding
(
Myerson 1981
)
.

2.3
Regret Measure

We evaluate the performance of a mechanism in data marketplaces using regret, which measures the gap between the revenue achieved by the mechanism and the optimal revenue that would be obtained with perfect knowledge of the buyers’ value distribution. For a single dataset
D^{(t)}
, the
instantaneous regret
at price
p^{(t)}
is defined as:

r^{(t)}={OPT}^{(t)}-{REV}^{(t)}(p^{(t)}),

(1)

where
{OPT}^{(t)}=p^{(t)}_{*}[1-F^{(t)}(p^{(t)}_{*})]
represents the optimal expected revenue under the monopoly price
p^{(t)}_{*}=\arg\max_{p}p[1-F^{(t)}(p)]
, and
{REV}^{(t)}(p^{(t)})=p^{(t)}[1-F^{(t)}(p^{(t)})]
is the revenue achieved at price
p^{(t)}
set by the mechanism. Here,
F^{(t)}
is the cumulative distribution function of buyer valuations corresponding to the density
f^{(t)}
.

For multiple datasets
D^{(1)},D^{(2)},\dots,D^{(T)}
generated from the same source
\mathcal{D}
,
the
average cumulative regret
quantifies the average revenue shortfall across all sales

\bar{R}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}r^{(t)}.

(2)

Minimizing the average cumulative regret requires mechanisms to adapt to valuation uncertainty and refine pricing strategies in sequential settings. While prior work has primarily addressed instantaneous regret for single dataset sales
(
Segal 2003
;
Kleinberg and
Leighton 2003
)
, this paper addresses the more complex challenge of reducing regret across sequential sales, ensuring long-term revenue optimization.

3
Mechanism Designs for Data Marketplaces

3.1
MAPP Mechanism

We introduce the MAPP mechanism for each fixed round
t
, which operates in two phases. (i)
Learn via Auction
: The seller conducts a one-time auction with buyers indexed by

i=1,\dots,n
, referred to as bidders, to gather market information. The auction does not predefine the quantity of datasets to be sold, allowing flexibility in adapting to demand. (ii)
Decide via Posted-Price
: Based on the auction outcomes, the seller sets a uniform price and offers it to all subsequent buyers indexed by
i>n
, referred to as posted-price buyers. We show that this hybrid design ensures fairness by eliminating price discrimination. For simplicity, the time index
t
is omitted in this subsection.

Phase I: Learn via Auction.
In this phase, each bidder
i
, for
i=1,\dots,n
, has a private valuation
v_{i}
for the dataset and submits a bid
b_{i}
. The set of bids
\bm{b}=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\}
is used to determine the auction outcomes, including the assignment of prices and the allocation of the dataset. We use a random splitting technique to determine prices. Each bidder
b_{i}
is randomly assigned to one of two groups, denoted by
\gamma_{i}\in\{0,1\}
. The auction price
p_{i}
is determined using a density-based optimal price function,

p_{i}=h(b_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})_{i}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{p}~p\left[1-\hat{F}_{\bm{b}_{[1-\gamma_{i}]}}(p)\right],

(3)

where
h
is
bid-independent
, meaning
h(b_{i},\bm{b}_{-i})=h(b,\bm{b}_{-i})
for all
b\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
Here,
\bm{b}_{-i}=\{b_{j}|j\neq i\}
represents the bids of all other bidders except
i
,

\bm{b}_{[1-\gamma_{i}]}=\{b_{j}|\gamma_{j}=1-\gamma_{i}\}
is the set of bids from the group opposite to bidder
i
,
and
\hat{F}_{\bm{b}_{[1-\gamma_{i}]}}(p)=\int_{0}^{p}\hat{f}_{\bm{b}_{[1-\gamma_{i}]}}(s)ds
.
The density
\hat{f}_{\bm{b}_{[1-\gamma_{i}]}}
is estimated from
\bm{b}_{[1-\gamma_{i}]}
using KDE,

\hat{f}_{\bm{b}_{[1-\gamma_{i}]}}(p)=\frac{2}{nw}\sum_{b_{j}\in\bm{b}_{[1-\gamma_{i}]}}K\left(\frac{p-b_{j}}{w}\right),

(4)

where
K:[-1,1]\rightarrow[1,H]
is the kernel function, and
w>0
is the bandwidth parameter, which controls the degree of smoothing. The bid-independence of
h
guarantees that a buyer’s price is set independently of their own bid. In Section
3.2
, we prove that the MAPP mechanism satisfies incentive compatibility, ensuring that bidders are incentivized to bid truthfully, making
\bm{b}_{[1-\gamma_{i}]}
valid observations of the true value density. A bidder must purchase the dataset if their bid meets or exceeds the assigned price, i.e.,
b_{i}\geq p_{i}
.

Phase II: Decide via Posted-Price.

After the auction phase, the mechanism transitions to the posted-price phase for buyers who arrive later. The posted price is set as the maximum price determined in the auction phase, ensuring consistency across all buyers:

p=\max\{p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n}\}.

(5)

In this phase, a buyer purchases the dataset if their valuation meets or exceeds the posted price, i.e.,
v_{i}\geq p
for
i\geq n+1
. The use of a uniform price (
5
) for all remaining buyers ensures fairness in the pricing process.

3.2
Incentive Guarantees

We demonstrate that the MAPP mechanism satisfies the incentive measures in Section
2.2
. First, we show that the mechanism ensures individual rationality, guaranteeing buyers never incur a loss from participating.

Proposition 1
.

The MAPP mechanism satisfies individual rationality.

The key idea is that the posted price in the MAPP mechanism is set as the maximum auction price in (
5
). This design ensures that buyers are always at least as well-off purchasing the dataset during the auction phase as they would be in the posted-price phase. By encouraging participation in the auction phase, buyers have the opportunity to secure the dataset at a potentially lower price, thereby guaranteeing non-negative utility.

Second, we establish incentive compatibility, which encourages buyers to bid truthfully to maximize their utility.

Proposition 2
.

The MAPP mechanism satisfies incentive compatibility.

We establish this result by employing a bid-independent auction pricing function

h
in (
3
), where a buyer’s price is determined based on the bids of others, not their own. This bid-independence prevents buyers from manipulating their prices by adjusting their bids, ensuring that truthful bidding is their utility-maximizing strategy. While bid-independence has been studied in single-phase auctions
(
Goldberg et al. 2006
, e.g.,)
, the MAPP mechanism extends it to a two-phase structure. By integrating a subsequent posted-price phase informed by the auction outcomes, the mechanism ensures truthful bidding even when buyers have an additional opportunity to purchase, maintaining fairness and efficiency across both phases.

Beyond these two standard incentive guarantees, MAPP also mitigates price discrimination by restricting price dispersion to at most two distinct levels, reducing the first-degree price discrimination of
Segal 2003
;
Kleinberg and
Leighton 2003
to a third-degree structure. It ensures that identical copies of the same dataset are sold at a single price while offering buyers who reveal their valuations in the initial auction a potential compensation. This structure fosters predictability and fairness, enhancing buyer trust and encouraging long-term engagement, making MAPP a more stable and sustainable approach for data marketplaces.

4
Regret Analysis

In this section, we provide a regret analysis to assess the efficiency of the proposed mechanisms in optimizing revenue. First, we analyze the instantaneous regret incurred when selling a single dataset using the MAPP mechanism. Next, we analyze the average cumulative regret across multiple rounds under the online MAPP mechanism.

4.1
Instantaneous Regret

Assume that the optimal price
p_{*}
maximizes the revenue function
REV(p)=p[1-F(p)]
in (
1
). The first-order condition for this optimization problem leads to the well-known virtual valuation function

v(p_{*})\equiv p_{*}-[1-F(p_{*})]/f(p_{*})=0

(
Myerson 1981
)
. This condition is fundamental in revenue optimization, as it characterizes the optimal pricing decision by equating the price to the adjusted expected marginal revenue. For this to be a valid maximization problem, the second-order condition,
-v^{\prime}(p_{*})f(p_{*})\leq 0
, must hold, implying
v^{\prime}(p_{*})\geq 0
. We further assume
v^{\prime}(p_{*})>0
to ensure strict concavity.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\hat{p}_{*}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{p\in[1,H]}p[1-\hat{F}(p)]
be the estimated optimal price, where
\hat{F}(p)=\int_{0}^{p}\hat{f}(s)ds
. Suppose that
v^{\prime}(p_{*})>0
. Then, the instantaneous regret in (
1
) satisfies,
r=O\{(\hat{p}_{*}-p_{*})^{2}\}=O\{||\hat{f}-f||_{\infty}^{2}\}
.

This result establishes that the instantaneous regret can be bounded by the squared density estimation error.
Building on this proposition, we analyze the instantaneous regret of the online MAPP mechanism in Algorithm
1
separately for the exploration and exploitation stages.

4.1.1
Exploration Stage

During the exploration stage, the online MAPP mechanism estimates the value distribution using KDE in (
4
).
Let the kernel function
K
in (
4

(
Parzen 1962
, e.g.,)
:

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}K(u)du=1\text{ (validity);}

\displaystyle K(u)=K(-u)\ \forall u\in[-1,1]\text{ (symmetry);}

(8)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}K(u)^{2}du<\infty\text{ (finite $L_{2}$);}

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}u^{2}K(u)du<\infty\text{ (finite variance);}

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}uK(u)du=0\text{ (zero mean);}

\displaystyle K(|s|)\leq C_{\rho}\cdot\exp(-s^{\rho})\ \forall s>s_{0}\text{ (exponential decay),}

where
\rho,C_{\rho},s_{0}>0
are constants.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that the true value density

in Assumption
1
be bounded (i.e.,
||f||_{\infty}<\infty
) and Lipschitz continuous. Under the conditions in (
8
), during the exploration stage, the online MAPP mechanism estimates the value distribution using KDE in (
4
). Then, the instantaneous regret in (
1
) satisfies
r<C\cdot n^{-2/3}\log n
for some constant

, with probability at least
1-1/n
for any the number of bids

.

Compared to the KDE rate in
Segal 2003

to be continuously differentiable with a Hölder continuous derivative, nor does it impose Hölder continuity on the kernel function. Additionally, our result provides a finite-sample guarantee for any the number of bids
n>1
, making it practical for data marketplaces where
n
may be limited.

4.1.2
Exploitation Stage

During the exploitation stage, the online MAPP mechanism employs RDE instead of KDE by incorporating bids from the exploration stage to refine the density estimate. To ensure the effectiveness of this approach, we require the shared density family

Assumption 2

(Regularity Conditions for the Shared Density Family)
.

We assume that the true density
f\in\mathcal{F}
is a truncated version of a twice-differentiable extended density
f_{e}\in\mathcal{F}_{e}
with support
[1-\epsilon,H+\epsilon]
for some
\epsilon>0

f(v)=f_{e}(v)/\int_{1}^{H}f_{e}(s)ds
, for

\sup_{v\in(1-\epsilon,H+\epsilon)}\max(|f_{e}|,|f_{e}^{-1}|,|f^{\prime}_{e}|,|f^{\prime\prime}_{e}|)\leq M
for some constant
M>1
.
Observations are assumed to be generated from random realizations of this extended density
f_{e}
.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{F}
is a
K_{0}
-dimensional exponential family,
\mathcal{F}=\{f(\cdot|\bm{\theta}_{*}):\bm{\theta}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{0}}\}
, where the density is given by
f(v|\bm{\theta}_{*})=\exp\left(\mu(v)+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}\theta_{*,k}\varphi_{k}(v)-B_{K_{0}}(\bm{\theta}_{*})\right)
, for
v\in[1,H]
. Here,
K_{0}\leq K
, where
K
represents the dimension of the approximating family in (
6
), and
B_{K_{0}}
is a normalization constant defined analogously to
\hat{B}_{K}
in (
6
). Additionally, we assume that all nonzero eigenvalues are distinct and that the estimated eigenfunctions
\hat{\varphi}_{k}
align with the true ones such that
\langle\varphi_{k},\hat{\varphi}_{k}\rangle\geq 0
.

Assumption
2
has been used in the literature
(
Qiu et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
to ensure the identifiability of the approximating exponential family
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
in (
6
).
Specifically, the extended density
f_{e}
addresses potential distortions near the boundaries.
For example, consider a truncated exponential distribution where

f_{e}(v)=\lambda e^{-\lambda v}/[e^{-0.9\lambda}-e^{-10.1\lambda}]
for
v\in[0.9,10.1]
and
\lambda>0
, and truncating this distribution over
[1,10]
results in

f(v)=f_{e}(v)/\int_{1}^{10}f_{e}(s)ds=\lambda e^{-\lambda v}/[e^{-\lambda}-e^{-10\lambda}]
. Hence, truncation preserves the exponential decay and functional form, and renormalization only alters the relative probabilities within the truncated range.

Theorem 2
.

Under the conditions in (

, during the exploitation stage, the online MAPP mechanism estimates the value distribution using RDE in (

). Then, the instantaneous regret satisfies

.

This result shows the better performance of the MAPP mechanism in the exploitation stage than the existing regret bound of

(

.
The improved regret rate demonstrates the advantage of RDE in (

), which leverages historical bidding data for more accurate value distribution estimation.

4.2
Cumulative Regret

Next, we consider the average cumulative regret across multiple rounds defined in (
2
).

Theorem 3
.

Let
m_{1}\equiv\min\{n^{(1)},n^{(2)},

\dots,n^{(t^{\prime})}\}
and
m_{2}\equiv\min\{n^{(t^{\prime}+1)},n^{(t^{\prime}+2)},\dots,n^{(T)}\}
denote the minimum number of bids received per round in the exploration and exploitation stages, respectively. Set
t^{\prime}=\sqrt{T}
. Let the kernel bandwidths
w_{1},w_{2}
in (
4
) be chosen such that
w_{1}=\Theta(m_{1}^{-1/3})
and
w_{2}=\Theta(m_{2}^{-1/2})
, and assume
m_{1}=\Theta(T^{3/4}(\log T)^{-2}),\ m_{2}=\Theta(T^{1/2}(\log T)^{-2})
, with
m_{1},m_{2}\to\infty
. Under the conditions in (
8
) and Assumption
2
, the online MAPP mechanism achieves an average cumulative regret of
\bar{R}_{T}=O_{p}(T^{-1/2}(\log T)^{2})
.

The average cumulative regret bound in Theorem
3
implies that as the seller offers more datasets, the average revenue loss vanishes asymptotically, allowing long-run revenue to converge to that of an oracle with full knowledge of the true value distribution of each dataset. Moreover, using the classical notion of
Hannan consistency
or
no-regret learning
in games
(
Hannan 1957
;
Blackwell 1956
)
, MAPP is shown to be Hannan consistent, achieving no-regret learning as
\lim\sup_{T\to\infty}\bar{R}_{T}\to 0
. This result provides the first regret bound for a setting that integrates auctions and posted-price mechanisms to handle dynamically arriving and departing buyers.

The result in Theorem
3
addresses the setting where multiple datasets, each with distinct valuation distributions, are sold while accounting for dynamic buyer entry and exit. Most valuation learning frameworks assume that sellers refine pricing by accumulating a large number of bids for the same product
(
Bergemann and Said 2011
;
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2015
;
den Boer 2015
, see,)
. However, this assumption is often unrealistic in data marketplaces, where repeated bid observations for a single dataset are limited. Instead, our setting requires the seller to learn across datasets, continuously adapting pricing strategies to new valuation distributions. Despite this added complexity, our result achieves the same cumulative regret bound as prior results for selling identical items with a fixed valuation distribution
(
Guo et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
.

6
Related Work

We briefly review some of the related work from multiple literatures, including mechanism design, data marketplace, and online learning.

Mechanism Design

The sale of digital goods like music, movies, and datasets presents unique mechanism design challenges, as they are non-rivalrous and can be resold indefinitely without depleting inventory. Traditional mechanisms, such as sequential posted pricing
(
Bar-Yossef et al. 2002
)
and digital goods auctions
(
Goldberg et al. 2006
)
, aimed to fully exploit individual utilities, but this goal proved overly ambitious. Sequential posted pricing can incur arbitrarily bad revenue early on unless bids are concentrated around the optimal price, while digital goods auctions lead to significant revenue losses in worst-case scenarios due to the inability to directly set prices based on buyers’ true valuations.
Goldberg et al. 2006
also showed that individual prices offer no advantage over randomized group pricing, the latter of which is thus incorporated into our MAPP mechanism.
Later works, such as
Segal 2003
and
Kleinberg and
Leighton 2003
reintroduced distributional assumptions from
Myerson 1981
, while
Broder and
Rusmevichientong 2012
imposed parametric assumptions, all in an effort to address these limitations. These works focus on a single dataset sale and heavily rely on abundant value observations (
n
), with
Huang et al. 2015
discussing the required size of
n
for near-optimal revenue. In contrast, our MAPP mechanism remains nonparametric and accelerates valuation learning by leveraging historical value observations from similar datasets. It achieves an
O_{p}(n^{-1/3})
improvement in regret convergence and overcomes the auction’s limitation of waiting indefinitely for all buyers who dynamically enter and exit the market, thereby boosting long-term revenue.

Data Marketplace

The rise of data as a key economic asset has spurred extensive research on designing efficient data marketplaces
(
Bergemann and
Bonatti 2019
;
Pei 2022
)
.
Many works leverage data divisibility to enable customization, often leading to price discrimination. For consumer data, customization helps buyers target specific segments, aligning data with their needs and increasing perceived value
(
Mehta et al. 2019
;
Yang 2022
)
. However, for general datasets, where buyers cannot assess value ex-ante, such approaches are less effective
(
Chen et al. 2022
)
. To bypass valuation uncertainty, sellers often offer data derivatives like pre-trained models, easing the buyers’ evaluation but obscuring price discrimination
(
Agarwal et al. 2019
)
. In contrast, our mechanism reduces price discrimination transparently while maintaining revenue efficiency, making it ideal for scalable and equitable data marketplace pricing.

Online Learning

The integration of online learning with markets has gained traction in recent years
(
Dai and Jordan 2021a
;
Dai and Jordan 2021b
)
. When applied to pricing goods with an unlimited supply, it typically focuses on selling identical copies through sequential posted prices
(
Bar-Yossef et al. 2002
;
Kleinberg and
Leighton 2003
)
or repeated second-price auctions, where algorithms are often designed to learn the optimal reserve price
(
Ning et al. 2021
;
Kanoria and
Nazerzadeh 2021
;
Cesa-Bianchi et al. 2015
)
. However, learning the reserve price from bids is challenging since those below this threshold remain unobserved, restricting the auctioneer’s ability to refine pricing strategies
(
Chawla et al. 2014
)
. Another application of online learning is from the buyer’s perspective, where algorithms are designed to optimize bidding or purchasing strategies
(
Guo et al. 2022
)
.
Our work is among the first to apply online learning to the sequential sale of multiple datasets, each with unlimited copies, extending learning both within and across datasets with varying value distributions. This advances online learning in pricing beyond existing frameworks that focus on a single evolving product, demonstrating its applicability to dynamic data marketplaces.

7
Conclusion

This paper introduces the Maximum Auction-to-Posted Price (MAPP) mechanism, a novel two-stage approach that integrates auction-based learning with posted-price models to optimize revenue in data marketplaces while minimizing price discrimination. MAPP provides a principled framework that seamlessly combines traditional auction theory with adaptive price learning, eliminating the need for prior knowledge of buyer value distributions. We establish that MAPP satisfies both individual rationality and incentive compatibility, ensuring truthful participation and buyer engagement. Furthermore, we demonstrate that MAPP achieves a faster regret rate of
O_{p}(n^{-1})
per round, significantly improving upon prior work, and satisfies no-regret learning in online settings, making it an adaptive mechanism for sequential data sales.

There are several promising directions for future research. One direction involves incorporating transaction costs to account for inefficiencies during the auction phase
(
Chen et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
. Another direction is introducing signaling mechanisms to enhance price learning by reducing buyer uncertainty and improving seller-buyer interactions. Additionally, considering fixed costs in maintaining a data distribution pipeline poses an interesting challenge—sellers may prioritize frequent buyers over occasional ones, adding complexity to the optimization problem, particularly in the auction phase. Frequent buyers often favor auctions for potential discounts, reinforcing the practical motivation behind setting the maximum auction price as the posted price, as this strategy effectively balances revenue optimization with diverse buyer preferences.
