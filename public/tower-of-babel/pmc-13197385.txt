Motility and growth Burkholderia two-component: a new regulatory system of Burkholderia seminalis
Authors: Juliana Cardinali-Rezende, Carolina A. P. T. Silva, Alessandra Costa, Leandro M. Garrido, Milene Ferro, Mauricio Bacci, Ana Olivia De Souza, Welington Luiz Araújo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe two-component regulatory system (TCS) is a highly conserved global signal transduction mechanism that enables microorganisms to adapt to changing environments in response to signals. Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 has attracted attention as a potential phytopathogenic biological control agent. In this study, a TCS mutant was obtained and evaluated in comparison to wild type using different approaches. The transcriptome analysis revealed 374 differentially expressed genes, some of which are involved in motility and chemotaxis (downregulated) and related to iron uptake and pyochelin production (upregulated). Swarming and swimming motility were impaired in the mutant, while biofilm production increased, leading to differences in growth profiles. However, the biocontrol efficacy was similar. These findings suggest that the new TCS, named motility and growth Burkholderia two-component regulator (MgbTC), positively regulates motility and chemotaxis genes while negatively controlling genes related to biofilm production, iron uptake and pyochelin siderophore production in the Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3.Key points• The new motility and growth of Burkholderia two-component regulator (MgbTC) system• MgbTC positively regulates motility and chemotaxis genes in B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3• MgbTC negatively controls iron uptake and pyochelin and biofilm production genes

Introduction

The two-component regulatory system (TCS) is a global signal transduction mechanism found in all domains of life. It enables microorganisms to adapt to varying conditions and colonize specific ecological niches in response to environmental signals (Haas and Défago 2005). These signals can be biotic, such as those produced by hosts or synthesized by bacterial populations in coordination with cell density and motility through regulatory mechanisms known as quorum sensing (QS), or abiotic, including factors like pH, temperature, osmolarity, and nutrient availability (Heeb and Haas 2001). The positive feedback that TCS exerts on its own expression allows organisms to auto-regulate their system, enabling a faster response when exposed to the same stimulus again, thus enhancing their adaptation success (Groisman, 2016). This system typically consists of a sensor protein, known as histidine kinase (HK), and a response regulator (RR) (Stock et al., 2000). Upon detecting a physical or chemical stimulus, the sensor HK phosphorylates its conserved histidine residue, enhancing the RR’s affinity for its DNA target (Groisman, 2016). Subsequently, the phosphoryl group is transferred to a specific aspartic acid residue on the RR, allowing the activated RR to influence cellular physiology, regulate gene expression, and enable cells to sense and respond to stimuli (Haas and Défago, 2005). The RR functions as a DNA-binding transcriptional repressor and/or activator, leading to changes in the transcriptional profile of the organism in response to environmental conditions (Groisman, 2016).

TCSs are highly conserved, particularly in many pathogenic bacterial species, including Vibrio cholerae (Wong et al. 1998) and Salmonella typhimurium (Johnston et al. 1996), as well as in opportunistic pathogens of plants, such as Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (O’Malley et al. 2020), and animals, including P. aeruginosa PAO1 (Heeb and Haas 2001; Francis et al. 2017). However, the genes regulated by RRs can differ among TCSs (Stock et al., 2000), and variations in input signals and regulated genes/proteins have been observed even among homologous systems in closely related bacterial species (Heeb and Haas 2001; Pontes et al., 2011; Chen et al. 2013). For example, the GacS/GacA system in pathogenic bacteria is crucial for virulence as it regulates genes involved in bacterial colonization and adhesion, including those associated with motility, biofilm production, and type IV pili synthesis (Badal et al. 2020). In plant-beneficial bacteria, this system controls the expression of biocontrol factors that protect plants from pathogenic fungi or bacteria. Numerous other TCSs, such as the Roc1 system, Rcs/Pvr, PprAB, and PilRS, also regulate the expression of cup fimbriae and/or type IV pili, which are essential for surface adhesion and biofilm formation in P. aeruginosa (Tierney & Rather, 2019). For instance, in P. fluorescens 2P24, a biocontrol bacterium isolated from the rhizosphere that suppresses plant diseases, the GacS/GacA system regulates the production of extracellular metabolites with antibiotic activity, such as 2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol (2,4-DAPG) (Yan et al. 2004). In P. fluorescens SBW25, this system controls the production of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) related to plant growth promotion (Cheng et al. 2016). In summary, the GacS/GacA system regulates the production of secondary metabolites, extracellular enzymes involved in pathogenicity, biocontrol of soil-borne plant diseases, ecological fitness, and tolerance to stress.

Burkholderia species belong to the class β-proteobacteria and encompass gram-negative bacteria with high genetic and metabolic versatility, enabling them to adapt to various ecological environments (O’Sullivan and Mahenthiralingam 2005). The Burkholderia cepacia complex (Bcc) includes a group of opportunistic pathogens responsible for pulmonary infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals and cystic fibrosis patients. Species such as B. multivorans, B. dolosa, and B. cenocepacia are notable members of this group (El-Halfawy et al. 2017). Additionally, Bcc includes animal pathogens like B. mallei and B. pseudomallei (Butt and Tomas 2017), as well as phytopathogens such as B. glumae, B. gladioli, and B. plantarii.

Conversely, another group of plant-associated Burkholderia species has garnered significant attention due to their potential benefits for plant growth. These beneficial bacteria promote healthy plant growth through mechanisms such as nitrogen fixation, siderophore production, phosphate solubilization, volatile compound metabolism, pollutant degradation, reduction of plant ethylene levels, and protection against biotic and abiotic stresses, including disease prevention against pathogenic bacteria and fungi (Eberl and Vandamme 2016; Mannaa et al. 2018).

The strain B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3, which belongs to the Bcc and was isolated from sugarcane roots (Luvizotto et al. 2010), has demonstrated a notable ability to inhibit fungi and reduce orchid necrosis caused by B. gladioli (Araújo et al. 2016; Romero-Gutiérrez et al. 2020). This strain can inhibit the fungus Fusarium verticiloides and produces phospholipids and rhamnolipids that contribute to the inhibition of cocoa fungi such as M. perniciosa, P. capsici, P. palmivora, and P. citrophthora (Araújo et al. 2017a, b). Since the draft genome of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 was obtained in 2016 (Araújo et al. 2016), researchers have studied genes involved in plant–microbe interactions or infections. Gonçalves et al. (2019) and Romero-Gutiérrez et al. (2020) demonstrated that B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 is virulent in the invertebrate model Galleria mellonella, exhibiting greater virulence at 37 °C compared to 28 °C (Gonçalves et al. 2019), which suggests its pathogenic potential toward mammalian cells. In this context, a mutant strain, B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 ∆wcbE (involving a gene encoding a glycosyltransferase enzyme), showed altered biofilm formation, motility, and synthesis of antifungal compounds.

To evaluate the role of TCSs in the environmental interactions of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3, a mutant in this system was generated. A transcriptome analysis comparing B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 to its mutant was conducted using RNA-Seq to clarify the role of the TCS in regulating genes involved in the metabolism of this species. Furthermore, a comparative analysis between the mutant and the wild-type strain was performed to understand the role of this TCS in the environmental adaptation of the bacterium.

Various approaches were employed, including motility analysis, biofilm production evaluation, metabolic and growth profiling, antagonistic activity assessments against fungi and bacteria, antibiotic resistance analysis, and Galleria mellonella infection assays.

Material and methods

Identification of TCS genes along the B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 genome.

The genome of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 (Araújo et al. 2016) was automatically annotated using Rapid Annotation using Subsystems Technology—RAST (https://rast.nmpdr.org/). The TCSs genes in this bacterium were identified using the Blastp tool from NCBI (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and RAST. Homologous sequences of gacS and gacA genes from P. aeruginosa PAO1, P. fluorescens CHAO, and Serratia plymuthica were used for reference. Through sequence homology, the Bsem_02525 and Bsem_02526 genes, corresponding to a histidine kinase and its regulator, respectively, were identified in the B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 genome. The whole-genome shotgun project was deposited early at DDBJEMBL/GenBank database under accession LAEU01000000 (Araújo et al. 2016).

Generation of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 mutant by homologous recombination.

A double mutant, B. seminalis ΔBsem_02525 ΔBsem_02526, was generated from the wild-type strain using homologous recombination. The flanking regions of Bsem_02525 and Bsem_02526 were amplified by PCR with primers Mut-1/Mut-2 and Mut-3/Mut-4, respectively. The chloramphenicol resistance gene was also amplified using Clor Forward/Clor Reverse primers. These fragments were combined into the suicidal vector pNPTS138, with the resistance gene inserted between the flanking regions, and introduced into E. coli S17-1 by electroporation. The vector was then transferred to B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 by conjugation. Double recombination facilitated gene deletion. Colonies from the first recombination were incubated at 28 °C, 150 rpm on TSA medium without antibiotics for 16 h to allow the second recombination event. Recombinant strains were selected on TSA plates containing 50 µg chloramphenicol. Validation of the mutants involved selecting strains sensitive to 200 µg kanamycin and resistant to 50 µg chloramphenicol, followed by PCR confirmation using Mut-1 and Mut-4 primers at an annealing temperature of 55 °C.

Complemented strain production

To confirm the double mutant, a complemented strain (B. seminalis VPA1701 +) was constructed. The RBS and ORF regions of VPA1701 were amplified by PCR with specific primers (VPA1701-F/R) and cloned into the SalI/SacI sites of the pMMB207 plasmid. The recombinant plasmid (VPA1701::pMMB207) was transformed into E. coli SM10 pir and subsequently conjugated to the B. seminalis VPA1701 strain. Conjugants were selected on LB agar plates containing carbenicillin and chloramphenicol and confirmed by PCR and sequencing using pMMB207-F/R primers. When required, 0.1 mM IPTG was added to induce VPA1701 expression, defining the complemented strain as B. seminalis VPA1701 +.

Culture conditions and RNA extraction

Both wild-type B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and the double mutant were cultured in Potato-Dextrose Broth at 28 °C and 180 rpm for 24 h. The samples were mixed with RNA Protect Bacteria Reagent, and total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit and treated with DNase I. RNA quality and integrity were checked by agarose gel electrophoresis after SYBR Safe staining and quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. All RNA-related materials were treated with DEPC to eliminate RNase contamination.

RNA-Seq

Total RNA of each sample (in triplicate) was sent to Macrogen Company (Seoul, Republic of Korea) where the RNA-seq was performed using the NGS Illumina platform according to manufacturer’s protocol. The raw reads obtained by sequencing were deposited to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database with BioProject accession PRJNA999207.

Reads preprocessing and quality control

Raw reads quality control was carried out before and after reads cleaning using FastQC v0.11.5 (Babraham Bioinformatics). www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/ The raw reads with low quality (Phred Q score < 30) were trimmed using SeqyClean (Zhbannikov et al. 2017). FASTX-Toolkit (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/) was used to remove the first 15 bp to solve the GC bias problem. This approach was done for each of the six RNA-Seq libraries analysed.

Gene-level quantification

Bowtie2 v.2.2.5 (Langmead & Salzberg 2012) was used to map the reads (GenBank Accession Number SRA: SRR25445389–SRR25445394) to the reference genome B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3, generating an alignment file in BAM format for each sample. The gene-level quantification was conducted in the OmicsBox v.1.2.4 (BioBam, Valencia, Spain) using the HTSeq (Anders et al. 2015) tool. In this step, all features of type “CDS” grouped by the “ID” attribute were retrieved from 3 reference sequences (chromosomes) present in the genome annotation file.

Differential expression gene analysis

Differential expression genes (DEGs) were identified using the negative binomial distribution model in edgeR v3.28.0 programme (Robinson et al. 2010) with TMM (Trimmed Mean of M-values) normalization. The exact test GLM (Likelihood Ratio Test) was applied to each gene (Robinson and Smyth 2008). Analyses were performed in OmicsBox feature “Pairwise Differential Expression Analysis” using normalized expression values in TPM (Transcripts Per Million). Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed at log2 (FoldChange) > 2 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Results were visualized with volcano plots and heatmaps (Ji and Sadreyev 2018).

Annotation and enrichment analysis of DEGs

DEGs selected (FDR < 0.05) were annotated on OmicsBox running BlastX (Altschul et al. 1990; Anders and Huber 2010) using E-value of e3 against the NR NCBI database. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were done using Fisher’s exact test enrichment analysis. The enrichment analysis evaluated GO IDs from all categories (molecular function—MF, biological process—BP, and cellular component—CC), and the results were reduced to specific terms and visualized as a word cloud, with the word size reflecting the sequence count for each GO term relative to the counts of other words.

In silico annotation of DEGs and comparative analysis

The gene curation and similarity analysis were carried out using the BLASTn and BLASTx tools from NCBI (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), in comparison with other Burkholderia sp. strains. The following public databases were used: Burkholderia Genome Database (https://www.burkholderia.com/), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes—KEGG (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html), Biocyc (http://biocyc.org/), BRENDA (http://www.brenda-enzymes.info/) and UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/).

B. seminalis TC3.4.2 R3 and its mutant growth curves.

Microbial growth curves of B. seminalis TC 3.4.2 R3 wild type and its mutant were. The strains were grown for 16 h on Trypticase soy broth medium (TSB) at 28 °C under shaking at 150 rpm (in triplicate). The bacterial cultures were adjusted to OD of 0.1 at λ = 600 nm. Twenty microliters of each bacterial culture was inoculated in 180 μl of TSB medium into sterile polystyrene microtiter plates and grown for 85 h under shaking at 28 °C. The optical density measurements were performed every 15 min in a microplate reader SpectraMax® Paradigm® Multi-Mode Detection Platform (Molecular Devices).

Motility analysis

Swimming and swarming motility analysis of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 wild type, its mutant and the complemented (B. seminalis VPA1701+) strains was carried out as described by Ha et al. (2014). An overnight bacterial culture was harvested and resuspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) to an OD600 = 1.0. The experiment was conducted on ABC culture medium with 0,3% w/v agar for swimming and of 0.5% for swarming and incubated at 28 °C and 37 °C. The growth was measured at 48 and 72 h and statistical analysis were performed according to Tukey analysis (p < 0,05).

Biofilm production

Biofilm formation was evaluated according to Jurcisek et al. (2011). To perform this, the wild type and its mutant were grown for 24 h on TSB medium, washed and suspended on ABC medium. The culture density was adjusted to OD600nm = 0.1 (107 cfu/mL). Then, 20 μl of each culture was inoculated on 180 μl of ABC medium into microtiter plates and incubated for 48 h at 28 °C and 37 °C. The OD of each culture was measured at λ = 550 nm, then, the bacterial cultures were carefully removed and 100 μl of the crystal violet solution was added into the wells of the plates and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. After that, the wells of the plates were washed with H2O and dried on air. One hundred twenty microliters of DMSO (100%) were added into the wells and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 20 min. The OD was measured again at λ = 570 nm. This experiment was conducted with 24 samples of each bacteria strain in both temperatures. The onset of biofilm was observed under an optical microscope Swift SW380B. Biofilm index was calculated as Biofilm production = growing at OD 550 × 100/biofilm at OD 570, according to Savoia et al. (2007). The whole experiment was carried out twice.

In vitro antagonistic activity against fungi

The antagonistic activity of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 wild type and its mutant was evaluated against eleven phytopathogenic fungi: Ceratocystis fimbriata, Thielaviopsis paradoxa, Fusarium sacchari, Curvularia lunata, F. oxysporum, F. equisetum, F. graminearum, Bipolaris soroliana, F. decemcellulare, F. solani and F. verticillioides. Both bacteria wild type and its mutant grew on TSB medium for 24 h at 28 °C under shaking of 180 rpm. The bacterial cultures were adjusted to OD of 0.1 at λ = 600 nm (107 cfu/mL) and 10µLof each bacterium was placed on opposite sides of the petri dish with Potato Dextrose Agar medium, (PDA) and incubated at 28 °C. After 48 h, the disc of 5 mm size containing each phytopathogenic fungus was taken and placed in the middle of the petri dish where both bacteria (wild type and its mutant) were growing. After seven days, the antagonistic activity was evaluated from the measurement of the inhibition zone in centimetres (cm).

In vitro antagonistic activity against bacteria

The B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 wild type and its mutant were evaluated for the inhibition capacity against the phytopathogenic Burkholderia gladioli ORQF-04F (agent of orchid necrosis) and the pathogens Staphylococcus capitis and S. epidermidis using the overlay method. The wild type and its mutant were cultivated on 5% solid TSB medium (in triplicate) and incubated for 18 h at 28 °C. In parallel, S. capitis, S. epidermidis, and B. gladiolus were grown on 5% TSB medium for 18 h at 37 °C and 28 °C, respectively, under agitation at 150 rpm. After growth, the wild type and its mutant were inactivated by exposure to chloroform for 3 h. After that, 5 mL of overlay of semisolid culture medium (0.8%) containing 200 μL of each bacteria culture of B. gladioli, S. capitis, and S. epidermidis was added to the inactivated bacterium and incubated at 28 °C and 37 °C for 48 h. The antibacterial activity was evaluated after 24 h and 48 h by comparing the size of the inhibition halo surrounding the colonies of B. seminalis TC 3.4.2R3 or its mutant.

Metabolic profiling analysis

Phenotype microArrays for microBial cells (Biolog) microplates (BIOLOG Inc. 1993, Hayward, CA, USA) were used to evaluate and to compare the level of metabolic response of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 wild type and its mutant. To carry this out, the GEN III MicroPlate TM Each 96-well plate contained 72 carbon sources and 23 chemical sensitivity tests together with a tetrazolium redox dye. B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and its mutant incubated on TSB medium were diluted to a DO600 nm of 0.05 in a special medium for the test called IF (inoculating fluid3). One hundred microliters of each culture was inoculated into GEN III MicroPlate TM wells (in triplicate) and the plates were incubated at 28 °C for 7 days. The metabolic profile was evaluated from the microbial respiration which reduced the dye and resulted in formation of purple colour that was measured by optical density (OD) at 590 nm in a microplate reader (Synergy H1 Hybrid Multi-Mode Microplate Reader – BioTek.

Antibiotic resistance analysis

The antibiotic resistance of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 wild type and the mutant B. seminalis ΔBsem_02525 ΔBsem_02526 was analysed against eleven antibiotics: Ceftazidime 30 ug, Aztreonam 30 ug, Cefepime 30 ug, Tetracycline 30 ug, Sulfazotrim 25 ug, Levofloxacin 5 ug, Piperacillin Tazobactam 100/10 ug, Doxycycline DOX 30 ug, Chloramphenicol 30 ug, Meropenem 10 ug, and Imipenem 10 ug. Both bacteria were grown on TSB medium for 24 h at 28 °C under shaking of 180 rpm. After that, the bacterial cultures were suspended in salt solution (0,8% NaCl) and the density adjusted to OD600 nm = 0.01. The cultures were striated on Pseudomonas Isolation Agar (PIA) and the antibiotic discs were placed at the extremities of each plate. The plates were incubated at 28 °C and 37 °C for 72 h. The whole experiment was carried out twice.

Galleria mellonella infection assay

Galleria mellonella from the laboratory culture were used in all experiments. Cultures of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 wild type and its mutant had their density adjusted to OD of 1.0 at λ = 600 nm. They were inoculated in the hindmost larvae proleg using a 25 μL 22 s-gauge gas-tight Hamilton syringe with 10μL of 108 CFU.mL−1 and incubated at 28 °C or at 37 °C. The negative controls, PBS and non-inoculated larvae, were incubated at the same conditions. For each strain and treatment, ten larvae were inoculated per replicate in two independent experiments. The larval survival was monitored up to seven days, considering a significant difference of p < 0.05, according to the Tukey analysis.

Results

Construction of B. seminalis double mutant and complemented strain B. seminalis VPA1701 +

The TCS genes were annotated in the genome of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and the similarity with GacA/Gacs evaluated. Bsem 02525 gene was identified as a histidine kinase and Bsem_02526 gene as a response regulator, these being separated by 7 base pairs, both forming a possible TCS. Based on this, the generation of double mutant by homologous recombination was obtained and validated by the construction of the complemented B. seminalis VPA1701 + strain.

Transcriptome analysis of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and its mutant

In order to further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed phenotypic differences, comprehensive transcriptome analysis was performed comparing B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 wild type and its double mutant. In this study, an average number of ~ 60, 4 million reads per sample (wild type and mutant) were generated, being 94% of the reads with a high accuracy (Q > 30) and more than 98% of reads mapped on B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 genome (Table SM2). The differential expression genes (DEGs) between the double mutant in comparison to wild type strain generated the Volcano plot (Figure SM1) and Heatmap (Figure SM2). The individual samples presented expression patterns strongly similar for all biological replicates to each strain (wild type and its mutant) demonstrating the high quality of the libraries sequenced.

High-throughput RNA sequencing and subsequent bioinformatic analysis provided a global view of the transcriptional landscape, revealing distinct gene expression profiles between the two strains. A total of 374 DEGs regulated by TCS in the B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3. It was observed that 188 and 186 genes were up and downregulated, respectively, in the mutant in comparison to wild type strain (Tables SM3 and SM4, respectively). The functions of each DEGs were evaluated and the world cloud enrichment analysis are represented in Figures SM3 and SM4. The highest differences in the expression levels of the genes (upregulated and downregulated) in double mutant in comparison to the wild type are represented in the Tables 1 and 2. Most of the upregulated genes are involved in iron acquisition and membrane associated transport systems (Tables 1 and SM3 and Figure SM3). In the B. seminalis TC3-4-2R3 genome, a cluster containing 13 genes organized in three operons was identified as being responsible for pyochelin iron-acquisition system (Fig. 1). The transcriptome analysis revealed that the genes involved in iron acquisition (ferric pyochelin receptors – fptAB genes), transport (fptX: encode the permease and ABC efflux pumps) and pyochelin synthesis (pchABCD and pchEFG genes) were upregulated in the mutant (with Log2FC ranging from 2.97 to 6.24) (Fig. 1). However, for the gene pchR, which encodes a transcriptional regulator protein PchR, the expression level has not changed. PchR seems to work positively, regulating the transcription of the genes involved in pyochelin synthesis and iron uptake. In addition, genes involved in the membrane transport system, biofilm formation and plant growth-promoting (pyridoxin, a form of vitamin B6) also were among the highest upregulated genes in the mutant in comparison to wild type (Table 1).

The mutation in the motility and growth Burkholderia two-component (MgbTC) regulator in the B. seminalis impacted negatively (downregulation) the expression level of several genes (Tables 2 and SM4 and Figure SM4). Some of these genes are involved in motility and chemotaxis (11 genes); LuxR family and regulator of nucleoside diphosphate kinase; carbohydrates metabolism (pyruvate metabolism II); membrane transport systems (23 genes): Efflux ABC transporter; efflux transport system outer membrane factor (OMF) and RND efflux system, involved in the antibiotic susceptibility; porin; Mg and Mn transport; type II/IV secretion system (involved in virulence); antibiotic biosynthesis monooxygenase; and finally, defence and resistance genes to copper, iron, arsenic, and fluoroquinolones—family of synthetic broad-spectrum antibiotics (12 genes). Growth curve, motility analysis, and biofilm production

The mutation in the TCS genes also affected the growth profile of B. seminalis. The mutant reached the exponential phase more slowly than the wild type, and it reached a higher optical density (Figure SM5). Swarming and swimming motility assays (Figs. 5 and SM6) showed that in the TCS mutant both movements were impaired, which means no flagellar movement. This phenotype was fully re-established in the complemented strain B. seminalis VPA1701 +, confirming the role of these genes in the motility of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and validating the mutation of them in the mutant strain. In addition, the influence of temperature on the swimming motility was evaluated in the wild type. According to Tukey analysis, a significant difference (p < 0.05) was observed after 48 h of growth. The swimming motility was greater at 37 °C than at 28 °C (Figure SM6), demonstrating that the temperature influenced the motility gene expression regulated by the TCS. Antagonistic activity in vitro and antibiotic resistance analysis

The antagonistic assay showed that the TCS have no effect on the B. seminalis TC 3.4.2 R3 ability to inhibit the eleven phytopathogenic fungi (Ceratocystis fimbriata, Thielaviopsis paradoxa, Fusarium sacchari, Curvularia lunata, F. oxysporum, F. equisetum, F. graminearum, Bipolaris sorolaniana, F. decemcellulare, F. solani, and F. verticillioides—Figures SM8 and SM9) and 3 bacteria (B. gladioli, Staphylococcus capitis, and S. epidermidis) (data not shown). In relation to the antibiotic resistance analysis, the assays revealed that nine out of eleven antibiotics analysed inhibited both bacteria in both temperatures tested (28 °C and 37 °C) (Figures SM10–SM13). Tetracycline has no effect in inhibiting both bacteria. Chloramphenicol was able to inhibit the wild type; however, it did not inhibit the mutant as expected, since it was generated with the insertion of a Cloranfenicol gene. This result demonstrates that the TCS has no influence in the antibiotic resistance to B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3.

Metabolic profile analysis

The metabolic profile of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and its mutant evaluated using Biolog microplates demonstrated that 13 compounds were differentially assimilated between the two strains (Table SM5). B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 used acetoacetic acid and was unable to utilize gelatin, dextrin, and D-fucose; in addition, it demonstrated sensibility to lithium chloride when compared to the mutant. The rest of the compounds were used and shared between both strains; however, methyl pyruvate, trehalose, salicin, and niaproof 4 were used more slowly by the wild type. On the other hand, the mutant used arabitol, aztreonam antibiotic, and propionic acid more slowly.

Discussion

B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 has great biotechnological importance as biological control due to its capacity for pathogen inhibition in plants and production of secondary metabolites with high added value. Since the GacA/GacS system in Pseudomonas ssp. is one of the most studied and is directly involved in the regulation of production and export of secondary metabolites which are related to pathogenicity and biological control in this group (Laville et al. 1992; Rich et al. 1994; Chancey et al., 1999; Duffy and Défago, 2000; Bull et al., 2001; Lapouge et al. 2008), the study of a TCS in Burkholderia ssp. demonstrated to be necessary.

In this study, we evaluated the gene expression profiles of Burkholderia seminalis TC3.4.2R3 wild type and the motility and growth of Burkholderia two-component (MgbTC) regulator mutant, along with experimental assays, to understand the role of this two-component regulator in the control of gene expressions in this species.

The transcriptome analysis indicated that MgbTC appears to negatively regulate genes involved in iron acquisition (fptAB genes), transport (fptX gene), and pyochelin synthesis (pchABCD and pchEFG genes), except for the pchR gene, which encodes the transcriptional regulator protein PchR. These genes were upregulated in the mutant compared to the wild type (Table SM3). Previous studies demonstrated that the expression levels of pyochelin structural genes (pchEFG) are repressed by iron and positively regulated by the pchR gene in Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1, an opportunistic pathogen (Reimmann et al. 1998, 2001; Heinrichs & Poole 1993; Ankenbauer & Quan 1994). Raio et al. (2020) observed an increase in pyochelin production in P. chlororaphis M71 ∆gacS and ∆gacA mutants. Iron is an essential micronutrient for bacteria, serving as a cofactor for various proteins involved in DNA synthesis and repair, transcriptional regulation, cellular respiration, biodegradation, and biosynthetic pathways (Evstatiev and Gasche 2012).

Methods for acquiring iron, such as the secretion of siderophores, have significant implications for the growth of resident bacteria and influence community dynamics in environmental or host–bacterial interactions (Ellermann and Arthur 2017). Competition for iron plays a key role in the antagonistic interactions between fluorescent pseudomonads and fungi. This mechanism is well documented in the biocontrol of plant root pathogenic fungi (Cornelis and Matthijs 2002; Haas and Défago 2005), and a similar phenomenon may occur with B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3, which has been utilised as a biological control agent against various fungi and bacteria.

To further investigate the role of MgbTC in regulating secondary metabolite production, we conducted antagonistic activity assays with B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and its mutant against eleven different fungal and bacterial species. Both B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and its mutant exhibited the same level of antagonistic activity against all tested fungi, inhibiting ten of the eleven fungal species. These results suggest that the TCS does not regulate secondary metabolite production or antagonistic activity in this strain. Therefore, we conclude that this MgbTC regulator is not the GacS/GacA system. Previous studies have indicated that B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 is a strong candidate for biological control against various fungal species (Araújo et al. 2017a, b) and some bacteria (Araújo et al. 2016; Romero-Gutiérrez et al. 2020). This strain has shown antagonistic action against numerous plant pathogens (Araújo et al. 2016; Romero-Gutiérrez et al. 2020), with its activity appearing to depend on bioactive compounds, such as antibiotics or secondary metabolites like pyochelin. Further analysis is necessary to determine the extent to which pyochelin is involved in this activity.

Regarding the other upregulated genes in the mutant, those involved in membrane transport and biofilm formation were identified, including genes related to type III secretion, type IV pilus biogenesis (pilP gene), and sigma-fimbriae adhesion and secretion (Table SM3). The type III secretion system (T3SS) is responsible for exporting flagellar components and pili (fimbriae), which facilitate cell adhesion to surfaces and support biofilm development (Berne et al. 2018). The transcriptome analysis also suggests that the TCS negatively regulates genes involved in biofilm secretion and T3SS in B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3, as these genes were upregulated in the mutant. These findings align with biofilm formation assays (Figs. 6 and SM7), which showed greater production in the mutant. Negative regulation of genes related to T3SS by TCS has also been documented in P. aeruginosa and P. syringae pv. tomato DC3000 (Valentini et al. 2018; O’Malley et al. 2020).

In terms of downregulated genes (Table SM4 and Figures SM4) in the MgbTC mutant, the analysis revealed a decrease in the expression levels of motility and chemotaxis genes. Among the 87 motility and chemotaxis genes organized in different clusters within the B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 genome, 11 were affected (Figs. 2 and 3, Tables 2 and SM4). The motility genes fliD, flgK, flgL, motA, flaA, and the chemotaxis genes cheA, cheW, cheR, and cheD exhibited repressed expression levels in the mutant, indicating that TCS positively regulates their expression in B. seminalis.

The maintenance and regulation of the flagellar motility system are complex and involve several genes that are highly regulated by flagellar transcriptional regulators (such as those encoded by the flhCD genes). The direction of flagellar rotation is controlled by chemotaxis genes, some of which are also downregulated in the B. seminalis mutant (Figs. 2 and 3). Cairns et al. (2013) demonstrated that mutations in flagellar motor genes, such as motA and motB, lead to a blockage of flagellar rotation; this subsequently activates the DegS-DegU signaling pathway controlling biofilm formation in Bacillus subtilis. In this study, biofilm formation increased in the mutant (Figs. 6 and SM7), while the motA gene, responsible for the flagellar motor, was downregulated compared to the wild type (Fig. 6). Bacterial chemotaxis (induced by QS molecules) is crucial for population survival, metabolic activities, and interactions within ecological niches (Armitage 1999; Lauffenburger 1991). Chemotaxis significantly influences microbial population dynamics since it directly affects motility (Fedi et al. 2016), and in this study, several chemotaxis genes were downregulated in the mutant. Fedi et al. (2016) observed that mutations in a single gene (cheA) resulted in Pseudomonas pseudoalcaligenes ∆cheA mutants displaying impaired swarming and swimming motility compared to the wild type. In studies involving P. aeruginosa, a nosocomial pathogen, Sampedro et al. (2014) established a link between chemotaxis genes and motility. They found that CheA proteins formed a complex with CheW, a chemotactic receptor that facilitated the transfer of a phosphate group from CheA to CheY, which in turn interacted with the flagellar motor to alter the direction of flagellar rotation in P. pseudoalcaligenes (Sampedro et al. 2014). Furthermore, Chen et al. (2017) showed that inactivation of the CheA/Y-like regulator A1S_2811 in Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC17978 impairs the bacterium’s surface-associated motility. Collectively, these studies highlight the correlation between chemotaxis genes and bacterial motility. In our study, the two cheA genes and the cheW gene had their expression levels repressed in the B. seminalis mutant, which exhibited neither swarming nor swimming motility. This suggests that these chemotaxis genes, along with lateral flagellar genes, are positively regulated by the MgbTC (Figs. 5 and SM6). This pattern was also observed by O’Malley et al. (2020), who demonstrated that the GacS/GacA system positively regulates swimming and swarming motility in Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000.

TCS consists of a histidine kinase protein and a transcriptional regulator from the LuxR family. The LuxR family proteins were first described by Engebrecht et al. (1983) during the analysis of quorum sensing (QS)-related genes. LuxR proteins function as pheromone receptors and transcriptional regulators, playing roles in various processes associated with microbial interactions and motility (Fuqua and Greenberg 2002; Yang et al. 2009). In the B. seminalis mutant, the expression level of the luxR gene was significantly repressed compared to the wild type (Table 2 and SM4).

In strains of the Burkholderia cepacia complex, numerous QS-regulated functions are known, including the production of extracellular proteases and siderophores (e.g., pyochelin), swarming motility, biofilm formation, and pathogenicity (Eberl 2006; Kim et al. 2014). QS systems appear to be regulated by TCS and depend on cell-to-cell communication via N-acylhomoserine lactone (AHL) signal molecules, which express certain phenotypic traits in a density-dependent manner, ultimately influencing bacterial colonization within environments (Kim et al. 2014; Raio et al. 2020). Raio et al. (2020) further demonstrated that TCS mutants of P. chlororaphis M71 displayed altered production of AHL signal molecules, which impacted swarming motility.

In our study, the MgbTC B. seminalis mutant strain reached the exponential phase more slowly than the wild type (B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3) (Figure SM5). However, the mutant achieved a higher optical density, corroborating findings from Van Den Broek et al. (2005), where mutations in the GacS-GacA system decreased the duration of the exponential phase and enhanced the growth rate of the mutant compared to the wild type. Given that motility and chemotaxis are vital for the invasion and colonization of Galleria mellonella by B. seminalis, we initially expected the mutant to exhibit lower virulence. Contrary to this expectation, the mutant killed more larvae than the wild type at 28 °C, with no significant differences observed at 37 °C (Fig. 7). These results indicate that TCS regulation of virulence in this strain is temperature dependent.

The impact on growth, biofilm production, and motility of the B. seminalis mutant compared to the wild type, along with fluctuations in iron acquisition, can influence various physiological and functional processes in this strain. This modulation may occur through several global transcription factors responsive to changes in intracellular iron concentrations and the resulting alterations in the cell’s redox state (Ellermann and Arthur 2017). This explains why genes involved in oxidative stress were also upregulated in the MgbTC mutant (Tables 1 and SM3).

As bacterial growth is influenced by environmental conditions and nutrient availability, we evaluated the metabolic and physiological profiles of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 and its mutant under different nutrient conditions. Out of the 72 carbon sources and 23 chemical sensitivities tested, 13 compounds were differentially utilized between the two strains (Table SM5). The metabolic analysis revealed that the B. seminalis mutant lost its ability to utilize acetoacetic acid but could utilize gelatin, dextrin, and D-fucose, unlike the wild type. d-fucose and dextrin are bacterial extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) that can be used for biofilm production, along with gelatin (Vu et al. 2009). The analysis indicated that the mutant could utilize these substrates, and as previously observed, biofilm formation increased in the mutant compared to the wild type (Figs. 6 and SM7). Additionally, the mutant displayed resistance to lithium chloride, which is employed to regulate the inflammatory responses of some hosts against pathogenic bacterial species, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Chen et al. 2013).

Regarding acetoacetic acid (C4H6O3), also known as acetoacetate or beta-ketobutyric acid, this compound is utilized for acetone production by certain bacterial species. The loss of the ability to metabolize acetoacetic acid does not appear to be significant for B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3. These findings suggest that the MgbTC, a two-component system, positively regulates genes directly or indirectly involved in acetoacetic acid metabolism while negatively regulating the genes associated with the metabolism of gelatin, dextrin, and D-fucose for utilization as carbon sources by B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 (wild type).

Conclusions

The two-component regulatory system MgbTC investigated in B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 positively regulates genes associated with motility and chemotaxis while negatively controlling genes related to biofilm formation, iron uptake, and pyochelin siderophore production. Additionally, this system positively regulates genes involved in the metabolism of acetoacetic acid and negatively regulates genes associated with the metabolism of dextrin, D-fucose, and gelatin, all of which contribute to biofilm formation and carbon source utilization. These regulatory actions are crucial for the functions related to colonization, competition, interaction, and survival of B. seminalis TC3.4.2R3 under various environmental conditions and in different host systems.
