AI‐powered analysis of affective dimensions in speech and its relevance for FTD diagnosis
Authors: Agnès Denève, Rosalie Dauchez, Thibaud Lebouvier, Fabien D'Hondt, Maxime Bertoux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAffective disturbances are common across behavioral variant frontotemporal degeneration (bvFTD), primary psychiatric disorders (PPD), and Alzheimer's disease (AD). Objective markers are needed for differential and transdiagnostic characterization. We tested whether artificial intelligence (AI)‐based analysis of natural speech could provide such markers.METHODSSpeech from 112 participants (bvFTD = 31, AD = 28, PPD = 15, controls = 39) was analyzed using a fine‐tuned speech emotion recognition model estimating valence, arousal, dominance, and entropy. Group differences were assessed with analyses of covariance, diagnostic utility with logistic regression, and neuroanatomical correlates with voxel‐based morphometry.RESULTSGroup‐specific affective profiles emerged. Overall, PPD exhibited a lower valence. bvFTD and AD showed a reduced arousal and a higher dominance. Affective dimensions predicted bvFTD with 80.4% accuracy (area under the curve = 0.732) and mapped onto fronto‐insular and temporal networks. Entropy was identified as a transdiagnostic marker.DISCUSSIONAI‐based speech analysis provides objective, scalable biomarkers for differential diagnosis, transdiagnostic characterization, and disease monitoring.

INTRODUCTION

Affective disturbances are common in neuropsychiatric disorders such as behavioral variant frontotemporal degeneration (bvFTD), Alzheimer's disease (AD), and late‐onset primary psychiatric disorders (PPD). These alterations profoundly disrupt the expressive and experiential dimensions of social functioning, affecting interpersonal relationships and daily activities, complicating differential diagnosis, and contributing to diagnostic delays and reduced quality of life. 1 , 2 , 3 Despite their relevance, affective changes remain difficult to characterize reliably.

Most clinical assessments of emotional processing rely on indirect and passive paradigms such as emotion recognition tasks 4 , 5 , 6 or physiological responses, 7 , 8 , 9 and capture recognition rather than expression of emotions. In contrast, connected speech could offer a more ecological window into behavioral, cognitive, linguistic, and affective functioning. Despite notable exceptions focusing on fundamental frequency (f0), 10 , 11 , 12 most studies have focused on non‐affective and linguistic dimensions of speech. 13 , 14 , 15 Critically, in neurology, automated and quantitative methods capturing para‐linguistic and affective expression from natural speech, beyond lexical and semantic content, are scarce.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) provide new opportunities to address this gap. Self‐supervised speech models can extract fine‐grained acoustic and prosodic representations beyond traditional features like f0. 16 Dimensional models of affect may offer a continuous characterization of affective prosody along valence (pleasant–unpleasant), arousal (activation–deactivation), and dominance (control–passivity), rather than categorical labels or unidimensional measures. 17 These dimensions correlate with subjective and physiological markers of affective experience. 18 It also remains to determine whether overlooked metrics such as entropy, a promising index of affective variability over time, 19 could capture the stability or dispersion of affective expressions in clinical populations.

In this study, we evaluated AI‐derived affective dimensions and entropy estimates extracted from connected speech in patients with bvFTD, AD, and PPD, compared to healthy controls. We aimed to (1) characterize each clinical group on these speech‐based affective measures, (2) quantify their predictive value for diagnosing bvFTD, and (3) identify transdiagnostic neuroanatomical correlates using structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). We hypothesized that clinical groups would exhibit distinct and shared alterations in affective expression, linked to atrophy in key affective processing hubs, including fronto‐temporal, anterior cingular, and insular regions.

METHODS

Participants

In this study, 124 participants were included, including 73 patients seen at the Lille memory clinic (from 2016 to 2023), an expert center for rare and early degenerative diseases. Each case was reviewed by an interdisciplinary expert team. They met the inclusion criteria of (1) a minimum follow‐up of 24 months (mean = 6.06 years), with ≥ 2 exhaustive neuropsychological and ≥ 2 MRI examinations to ensure diagnostic accuracy; (2) having French as native language. Details of the patient selection process are available in Material S1.1 in supporting information. From an initial group of 503 patients, 34 patients with probable bvFTD, 30 patients with typical AD, and 21 patients with a PPD were selected, fulfilling each diagnostic criteria. 20 , 21 , 22 Among them, 50.0% had their diagnosis supported by abnormal cerebrospinal fluid levels of phosphorylated tau, total tau, and amyloid beta in the AD group, whereas 26.47% in the bvFTD group and 47.62% in the PPD group had a clinical diagnosis supported by the absence of an AD biomarker profile. Audio recordings were available for 31 patients with bvFTD, 28 patients with AD, 15 patients with PPD (including 12 with major depressive disorder [MDD, 80%] and 3 with bipolar disorder [BD, 20%]) as well as 39 healthy controls matched for age, sex, and education. Control participants had no cognitive or psychiatric complaints or history, and normal cognitive efficiency as measured by the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE). The description of the global homogeneity of the PPD group is provided in Material S1.2 and Tables S1 and S2 in supporting information.

Speech recording

The French narrative speech task of the GREMOTs 23 was used. Patients chose a sealed envelope from several, creating the impression that they had to make the speech therapist guess the images, although the images were always the same. They were instructed to narrate the story based on five sequential pictures. Audio files were edited following previous work. 10 , 12 Details of speech data handling are available in Material S1.3 in supporting information.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic Review: The authors conducted a literature review using PubMed to examine affective disturbances in behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD), Alzheimer's disease (AD), and primary psychiatric disorders (PPD). While previous studies explored emotion recognition and behavioral changes, few have examined speech‐derived affective markers for differential diagnosis, transdiagnostic characterization, and their neuroanatomical correlates. Interpretation: So far, affective disturbances are either assessed by subjective scales or indirect performance‐based tests such as emotion recognition. Using a 3‐minute speech recording to extract affective dimensions through artificial intelligence (AI) modeling, we identified (1) specific profiles relevant to diagnosis and monitoring procedures (e.g., bvFTD patients showed lower arousal and higher dominance in their voice, reflecting behavioral disinhibition; PPD patients displayed lower valence, characteristic of the negative affective bias in depression); (2) transdiagnostic markers or affective instability through the extraction of entropy; and (3) fronto‐insular and temporal neural correlates. These findings demonstrate that AI‐powered affective analysis of patients’ voices may enhance diagnosis and provide relevant endpoints. Future Directions: Future studies should use longitudinal designs (including pre‐clinical stages) to assess predictive value of our measures, and leverage multimodal data integration (e.g., lexical, semantic measures) to increase diagnosis accuracy.

AI modeling of affective dimensions

Affective dimension prediction modeling

To analyze affective dimensions, we relied on an open‐source model for dimensional speech emotion recognition (SER), 24 wav2vec2‐large‐robust‐12‐ft‐emotion‐msp‐dim (referred as w2v2‐vad in this study). This model, trained to generate ratings on short speech segments, builds on Wav2vec 2.0, a self‐supervised deep neural network model for speech representation which was first tuned into w2v2‐vad to predict continuous affective dimensions (valence, arousal, and dominance) on short speech segments, thereby providing a graded representation of emotional prosody. Briefly, each wav file was transformed into latent speech representations and then processed by regression layers trained on rated English corpora of emotional speech. The resulting outputs provided estimates across dimensions.

Fine‐tuning for French data

As w2v2‐vad was trained on English data, it was not optimized for French. Because prosodic cues are partially language and culture specific, we trained the model on the Geneva Multimodal Expression Corpus for Experimental Research (GEMEP), 25 one of the few available speech corpora in non‐Québécois French. This corpus provided independent material for a preliminary study in which 12 naïve healthy controls rated valence, arousal, and dominance for 145 audio clips across 17 emotions (Material S1.4 and Figure S1 in supporting information). This sample was sufficient for robust data, as consensus can be reached with five to seven raters 26 and confirmed by bootstrap analyses (Material S1.5, Tables S3, S4, and S5 in supporting information). For each audio clip, averaged ratings of valence, arousal, and dominance were used to optimize the model for French (referred to as w2v2‐vad fine‐tuned), as detailed in Material S1.6 in supporting information.

AI‐derived affective dimension estimates

To evaluate the three affective dimensions on speech data, we applied the (fine‐tuned) w2v2‐vad to voice segments using 3 seconds sliding windows with 1.5 second steps, and applied zero‐padding to the last segment to ensure a total duration of 3 seconds for each. Building on an approach which quantified participants’ emotional expressiveness as the Shannon entropy of trajectories in the valence‐arousal space, 19 we extended this method to the three dimensions and computing the differential entropy of the valence–arousal–dominance vector stream, assumed to be Gaussian. Details of the computation are available in Material S1.7 in supporting information. Conceptually, this entropy quantifies the dispersion or variability of a participant's affective states in the three‐dimensional affective space. Because we rely on a task that was not specifically designed to elicit affective states, higher entropy corresponds here to a more diverse repertoire of affective states and thus a more unstable affect expressivity, whereas lower entropy indicates more restricted but stable affect. To fully characterize each individual, we report both the scalar entropy describing variability and the per‐subject sample means of valence, arousal, and dominance sequences, describing central tendency (see Figure 1 illustrating those steps).

Neuroimaging acquisition, pre‐processing, and harmonization

Comprehensive details on MRI acquisition and processing are available in Material S1.8.1 and S1.8.2 in supporting information. Pre‐processing was performed with FSL. 27 In short, structural scans underwent brain extraction, tissue segmentation, and normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. A study‐specific template was generated, and individual gray matter (GM) maps were non‐linearly registered. Partial volume estimates were then modulated and spatially smoothed using a 4‐mm isotropic Gaussian kernel. To account for heterogeneity due to scanner type, we applied the openly available ComBat harmonization pipeline. 28 This method relies on a multivariate mixed‐effects regression, incorporating biological covariates alongside scanner information, to selectively adjust imaging features. Empirical Bayes estimation was used to stabilize parameter estimates, an advantage when sample sizes are modest.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (30.0.0.0). Student tests were used to analyze demographic data, followed by analyses of covariance adjusted for age and sex to compare the estimates of the affective dimensions and entropy across groups. We calculated partial eta‐squared (pη2 ) or Cohen (d) values to estimate effect sizes and 95% confidence interval (CI) to indicate the precision of the estimates. Binary logistic regression (enter method) was used to predict the diagnosis of bvFTD.

Neuroimaging analyses

MRI data were analyzed with FSL. Multiple comparisons were controlled using permutation (N = 5000) testing with threshold‐free cluster enhancement (TFCE), 29 providing voxel‐wise family‐wise error (FWE) correction at p < 0.05. Given the multidimensional nature of affective constructs, we implemented a single generalized linear model (GLM) including all four AI‐derived affective measures (valence, arousal, dominance, entropy) as regressors of interest, exploring positive and negative associations. This approach enabled us to account for their shared variance, reducing multiple testing, and ensuring that each dimension's unique association with GM density was estimated under an identical statistical framework. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons (FWE). Correlation analyses were restricted to scans obtained within 12 months of the speech evaluation. Because affective estimates were already corrected for age and sex, these variables were not covaried in the GLM again.

Data Sharing

Due to the potential identifiability of patients through voice recordings and imaging data, raw data cannot be shared. However, affective dimensions (and entropy) annotations are available in open access (see supporting information) to ensure the complete replication of our analyses, as well as the French fine‐tuned version of the model we used. 30 Access to anonymized data could be granted on demand to qualified individuals in accordance with ethical guidelines and subject to a data‐sharing agreement with Lille University Hospital.

Prediction of bvFTD diagnosis

When considered together in a single model aiming at predicting the diagnosis of bvFTD (versus all other groups), the AI‐derived estimates (VAD) and entropy reached 80.4% of precision (area under the curve = 0.732), with high sensibility (96.3%) but low specificity (36.7%), with dominance (Z = −2.972; P < 0.005) and entropy (Z = 2.103; P < 0.05) being significant coefficients.

Neuroimaging transdiagnostic correlations

Only significant correlations between AI‐derived dimensions and GM density are reported (Figure 4) and detailed in Material S2.2 and Table S6 in supporting information. Arousal estimates positively correlated with a predominantly right‐hemispheric cluster including the right orbitofrontal cortex, posterior insula, temporal pole, anterior and posterior temporal cortices, parahippocampal gyrus, and fusiform cortex, with smaller clusters in the right superior precentral gyrus and left posterior insula. Valence estimates positively correlated with small regions of the right insula and lateral superior frontal pole. Dominance estimates positively correlated with a small cluster in the left superior and middle temporal gyri. Entropy negatively correlated with the bilateral orbitofrontal cortex (extending into the left frontal pole), anterior divisions of the bilateral cingulate and paracingulate cortices, left temporal pole, and the left anterior insula.

DISCUSSION

Patients with bvFTD exhibited lower arousal and higher dominance compared to controls, and higher valence and dominance relative to PPD. This pattern is compatible with the clinical picture of behavioral disinhibition in bvFTD: blunted emotional reactivity and assertive affective expression are often considered typical, including exaggerated laughter, inappropriate expressions, or childish humor. 31 , 32 , 33 Patients with AD also showed reduced arousal but increased dominance compared to both PPD and control groups, which suggests a blunted affective responsiveness coherent with clinical observations, 34 yet combined with an excessively assertive tone. Finally, patients with PPD were characterized by lower valence relative to all other groups, consistent with the negative affective bias frequently reported. 35 Together, these findings demonstrate that dimensional analysis of affective prosody identifies group‐specific alterations coherent with known clinical features and may contribute to differential diagnosis.

A convergent finding across clinical groups was increased entropy of affective expressions. All patient groups showed less coherent and more unstable affective signaling than controls, whose elongated ellipsoids indicated a more balanced distribution across dimensions. This shared alteration indicates that, in addition to disease‐specific biases in affective dimensions, neurodegenerative and psychiatric conditions converge toward a breakdown of integrative processes that normally constrain affect into coherent and socially interpretable states. The results support entropy as a transdiagnostic marker of affective dysfunction, consistent with current moves toward dimensional and cross‐diagnostic frameworks in neurology and psychiatry, such as the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC). 36 In our study, these differences likely reflect spontaneous expressivity rather than reactivity; other paradigms (e.g., emotion induction) may yield distinct entropy profiles, highlighting the need to interpret entropy in context.

Neuroimaging correlative analyses revealed that affective dimensions mapped onto partially overlapping but distinct fronto‐insular and temporal networks. Valence correlated with right insular and frontal pole regions, in line with the role of the insula in integrating affective and interoceptive signals, 37 and of the frontal pole in reward and motivational states. 38 Arousal was positively associated with GM density in the orbitofrontal cortex, insula, and anterior temporal regions, areas classically involved in autonomic, reward, salience, and semantic processing. 39 , 40 Dominance correlated with left superior and middle temporal gyri, supporting the role of temporal structures in auditory‐motor integration and social meaning. 41 Entropy, in contrast, negatively correlated with bilateral orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, and insula, suggesting that atrophy in the regulatory hubs emotion regulation and interoceptive integration is associated with unstable affective expression.

From a cognitive perspective, our findings align with constructionist theories of emotion, which propose that emotions emerge dynamically from core affective dimensions interacting with context. 42 Within this framework, semantic memory is central, as emotions are constructed through retrieving and integrating conceptual knowledge that gives affective states their meaning. 43 Neuroimaging results support this view: regions processing interoceptive, motivational, semantic, and social information (insula, frontal pole, orbitofrontal cortex, anterior temporal lobe, superior/middle temporal gyri) were differentially associated with specific affective dimensions, whereas regulatory hubs (orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, insula) were linked to entropy. These results align with distributed network models of emotion, emphasizing that affective experience emerges from large‐scale interactions rather than fixed “emotional brain fingerprints.” 44 , 45 The increased entropy observed in patient groups may reflect a collapse of these distributed social regulatory processes, leading to affective expressions that are more inconsistent and ultimately less intelligible to others.

Interestingly, although affective dimensions are usually considered basic and semantically independent phenomena, our study suggests that they may be subject to semantic elaboration, 43 as indicated by the correlation between arousal and polar temporal regions. Recent evidence shows that this same right anterior area tracks arousal from lexical–semantic cues. 15 Together, these findings point to a multimodal hub in which arousal is conceptualized across verbal and non‐verbal channels, supporting the view that emotions are constructed states shaped by semantic and personal knowledge. 42 , 44

Methodologically, this study demonstrates the feasibility and added value of fine‐tuning a state‐of‐the‐art SER model. While all affective dimensions were better captured after fine‐tuning, valence remained harder to estimate, reflecting the challenge of inferring evaluative content from prosodic cues alone. This difficulty is well known, given the greater dependence of valence on lexical content, 15 , 24 which was absent from the stimuli used to fine‐tune our model. This highlights both the relevance of including lexical information in future studies and the value of our approach in deriving affective estimates solely from prosodic features. This independence from lexical content may be especially advantageous in conditions with impaired language production (e.g., aphasia, severe stages of diseases, and so on) or across different languages.

This study has some limitations. The PPD group was relatively small, reflecting the strict selection of patients with late‐onset, atypical presentation mimicking bvFTD, mirroring one of the most challenging diagnostic scenarios, and allowing us to contrast bvFTD with its two main differentials. This makes the achieved predictive accuracy of bvFTD with AI‐derived estimates of affective dimensions (> 80%) satisfactory despite limited specificity. However, because the PPD group was composed of patients with both MDD and BD, and because among those with BD, one patient was in a manic phase while the others were euthymic (see Material S1.2), we must consider whether different results might emerge in a more homogeneous sample (e.g., including only patients with MDD). Because we relied on the broad clinical category of PPD, which inherently encompasses substantial heterogeneity, and because all patients with PPD were included due to a suspicion of bvFTD, we chose not to exclude any individuals from this group. Future studies will nonetheless need to investigate potential subgroup differences, including contrasts between depressive, euthymic, and manic states for BD. Second, fine‐tuning relied on a single corpus, potentially limiting linguistic generalizability. Nevertheless, it significantly improved model performance and provides rare evidence in French, a language underrepresented in speech emotion research in which English dominates, although it may not generalize across the world's linguistic diversity. 46 Future work should develop measures capturing temporal dynamics or context‐specific speech dynamics while balancing ecological validity. Finally, the cross‐sectional design limits predictive assessment and longitudinal studies (e.g., ALLFTD, GENFI) could help to determine whether affective entropy predicts progression, conversion, or treatment response. Integrating multimodal information (e.g., lexical/semantic features, facial expression, or postural or gesture analysis) could enhance ecological validity and diagnostic precision, but larger samples are required.

These findings have important implications. They support the use of AI‐derived affective markers to complement existing neuropsychological tools by providing objective measures of socio‐affective functioning. This could assist in differential diagnosis among bvFTD, AD, and psychiatric conditions, where social and affective changes are difficult to objectively capture during the assessment, yet clinically significant. These results establish a short (< 3 minutes), easy to administer, narrative speech as an ecologically valid, low‐burden source of affective biomarkers. Unlike traditional emotion recognition tasks, this paradigm leverages everyday communication and allows automated, quantitative, and dimensional assessment. The identification of entropy as a robust transdiagnostic marker also underscores its potential utility for disease monitoring, including in longitudinal contexts. Finally, AI‐based speech analysis can be deployed in telemedicine/tele‐neuropsychology and digital health platforms, opening new opportunities for remote, low‐cost monitoring of affective dysfunction in neurologic and psychiatric populations. Given the importance of social cognitive disturbances in bvFTD, and the involvement of regions associated with affective processing identified in this study, this approach appears particularly relevant for bvFTD. However, its specificity should also be examined, especially in relation to right temporal variant or semantic behavioral variant FTD, for which both arguments may equally apply. 10 , 47 , 48

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that AI‐derived affective dimensions from spontaneous speech capture both disorder‐specific and transdiagnostic alterations. This approach offers a promising, ecologically valid, and scalable avenue for improving differential diagnosis and monitoring affective changes in neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders.
