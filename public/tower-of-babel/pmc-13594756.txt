Comment on “A multi-method evaluation of trait and state-sensitive impulsivity in relation to impulsiveness of suicide attempts and non-suicidal self-injury in an adolescent clinical sample”
Authors: Vatsala Gupta, Parag J. Ratnakar, Kotina Shridevi, Gaurav, Fayaz Ahamed
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Lint and colleagues examined whether trait and state-sensitive impulsivity were associated with the reported impulsiveness of suicide attempts (SA) and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) in adolescents receiving inpatient psychiatric care [1]. Their multi-method design is informative, but the findings are best regarded as hypothesis-generating associations rather than evidence that current inhibitory control identifies adolescents prone to rapidly enacted self-injury. This distinction matters because a state-sensitive task was linked to a retrospectively reported latency for the most recent lifetime event.

The temporal mismatch is particularly important for stop-signal reaction time (SSRT). Behavioral impulsivity may vary with clinical state and context, and meta-analytic evidence indicates that associations between behavioral impulsivity and suicide attempts are stronger when assessment occurs close to the attempt [2]. The interval between the most recent SA or NSSI episode and the stop-signal task was not reported. Consequently, SSRT may reflect inhibitory control during hospitalization rather than at the time of the behavior. Consensus guidance also emphasizes that individual-difference applications require adequate trial numbers, race-model checks, appropriate SSRT estimation, and detailed reporting [3]. Reporting these diagnostics, exclusion criteria, and reliability estimates would clarify the robustness of the observed SA association.

A second issue concerns the outcome itself. The seven response categories span markedly unequal intervals, ranging from 0 s to more than 2 days. Ordinal regression preserves ordering, but the reported mean category cannot be interpreted as an average elapsed time. Moreover, a single thought-to-action item may combine distinct stages, including initial ideation, decision, planning, preparation, and opportunity. Prior event-pathway research indicates that these stages can unfold on different timescales [4]. Clinical inferences about the available intervention window should therefore remain cautious.

The NSSI trait model was not significant overall, although lack of premeditation and sensation-seeking had nominal p values below 0.05. Given testing across multiple impulsivity facets, two outcomes, and two measurement approaches, these coefficients warrant replication rather than immediate personalized-treatment implications. This caution is reinforced by evidence that self-report and laboratory impulsivity measures have only limited construct overlap [5].

A feasible next step would combine prospective, event-proximal assessment with repeated inhibitory-control measurement, ecological momentary ratings of urges and planning, and prespecified multiplicity control. Such a design could distinguish stable vulnerability from acute state effects and determine whether impulsivity adds clinically useful information beyond current symptoms and prior self-injurious behavior.
