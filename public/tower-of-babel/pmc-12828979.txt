Prevalence and determinants of vitamin D deficiency among children aged 9 months to 12 years at a tertiary care center in western India: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sanayaa M Bhonsle, Namrata K Makwana, Bhadresh R Vyas, Manan R Vaishnav
Published: 2025

Background

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble secosteroid integral to calcium and phosphorus homeostasis, endochondral ossification, and bone mineralization in childhood. Beyond the skeleton, vitamin D receptors are expressed in immune and other tissues, implicating vitamin D in host defense, respiratory health, and possibly neurodevelopmental outcomes [1–4]. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] is the accepted biomarker of status due to its longer half-life and reflection of both cutaneous synthesis and intake.

Paradoxically, India—despite high solar irradiance—reports widespread pediatric hypovitaminosis D. Proposed causes include reduced midday outdoor activity, sun-avoidant behavior, cultural clothing norms, skin pigmentation limiting UVB penetration, atmospheric pollution, urban living, and poor intake of vitamin D–fortified foods [2, 5–7]. Exclusive breastfeeding without supplementation also contributes in infancy because human milk contains minimal vitamin D. Clinically, pediatric deficiency ranges from asymptomatic biochemical perturbations to rickets, hypocalcemic seizures, growth faltering, and skeletal deformities; subclinical deficiency may still impair bone accrual and increase long-term fracture risk [1, 4, 8].

Recent Indian pediatric studies report high and variable prevalence of hypovitaminosis D, influenced by latitude, season, urbanization, and dietary/behavioral factors [9]. However, regionally representative data from western India that concurrently assess sun exposure, sunscreen practice, and common dietary contributors (milk and egg intake) are limited.

Locally relevant data are essential to guide preventive strategies—particularly counseling around safe sun exposure and diet, and to inform policies on fortification and supplementation. We therefore conducted a cross-sectional study in western India to estimate the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency and insufficiency in children aged 9 months–12 years and identify associated sociodemographic, environmental, and dietary factors. We hypothesized that longer sun exposure and specific dietary habits (e.g., regular milk intake and moderate egg consumption) would correlate with higher 25(OH)D.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Department of Pediatrics, G.G. Hospital, Jamnagar — a tertiary care government hospital serving urban and peri-urban populations of Jamnagar district and nearby rural areas — from April 2023 to June 2025.

Participants and recruitment

Eligible participants were apparently healthy children aged 9 months to 12 years attending immunization clinics or accompanying as healthy siblings of outpatients/inpatients. Systematic sampling was used: every fourth eligible child was approached. Parents/guardians provided written informed consent; assent was obtained when appropriate.

Sample size

We assumed 70% prevalence of suboptimal vitamin D (deficiency + inadequate) based on prior Indian pediatric reports (for example, Basu et al. 2015 [10], Marwaha et al. 2005 [11]) and used 10% absolute precision and 95% confidence (Z = 1.96), yielding a minimum required sample of 254; we enrolled 277 children to allow for variability.

Data collection

A piloted structured proforma recorded: age, sex, anthropometry (weight, height), socioeconomic class, religion, sun exposure (typical time outdoors, duration/day, approximate body surface area exposed), sunscreen use, and diet (frequency of milk, eggs, vegetarian pattern per week). Nutritional status was assessed using WHO growth charts (z-scores). All data were captured by trained investigators.

Socioeconomic status

Socioeconomic status was classified using the Modified Kuppuswamy scale (for urban populations) (education, occupation, family income) updated for 2023 income categories [12]; participants were categorized as lower, middle, and upper socioeconomic class based on the composite score.

Anthropometry

Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a calibrated digital scale; standing height (or length for younger children) was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a stadiometer. Measurements were taken by trained investigators in light clothing without shoes.

Vitamin D assay

Biochemical assessment: Under aseptic precautions, 2–3 mL venous blood was collected. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] was measured by chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) on DiaSorin LIAISON analyzer (DiaSorin Inc., Stillwater, MN, USA) in the hospital laboratory according to manufacturer instructions and internal quality control procedures. External quality assurance was performed as per laboratory protocol [13].

Vitamin D status

Vitamin D status was defined using Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) revised pediatric cut-offs to allow comparability with recent Indian studies and national practice: deficiency < 20 ng/mL, inadequate 20–29 ng/mL, and sufficient ≥ 30 ng/mL [14]. We acknowledge the Institute of Medicine (IOM) 2011 adult-centric thresholds (deficiency < 12 ng/mL; 12–20 ng/mL classified as low/inadequate; >20 ng/mL adequate) [15]. The IAP cut-offs were chosen because they are widely applied in Indian pediatric research and inform local clinical decision-making.

Outcomes

Primary outcome: prevalence of vitamin D deficiency and insufficiency.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered in Excel and analyzed with SPSS (version XX). Descriptive statistics summarized continuous variables (mean, SD) and categorical variables (n, %). Group comparisons used Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests as applicable. A two-sided p < 0.05 denoted statistical significance. We planned exploratory trend interpretation when p approached significance (0.05–0.10) while emphasizing the risk of type I error.

Discussion

In this tertiary-care, clinic-based sample of 277 children, we found an extremely high burden of suboptimal vitamin D status—77.7% were deficient or inadequate, and only 22.4% were sufficient. This aligns with the growing Indian pediatric literature reporting widespread hypovitaminosis D, though absolute prevalence varies by region, urbanization, and methodology [2, 5–7, 10]. Importantly, several modifiable correlates demonstrated robust associations with 25(OH)D: sun exposure (clear dose–response), sunscreen use (higher deficiency), milk consumption (protective), and moderate egg intake (most favorable). In contrast, we observed no association with age, gender, or religion. Socioeconomic class showed a trend toward worse status in the small upper-class subgroup, likely reflecting behavioral and lifestyle factors.

Our deficiency prevalence (34.7%) sits below reports from North India (often > 50%) yet squarely within the national spectrum. Studies in Northern urban cohorts report severe deficiency, potentially related to higher latitude, winter seasonality, air pollution, and sun-avoidant behaviors [2, 5, 7]. Data from other regions also reveal high suboptimal rates, indicating that “sunny climate” alone is inadequate to ensure adequate vitamin D—behavioral exposure patterns and diet likely dominate [6].

The dose–response between sun exposure and vitamin D sufficiency in our cohort mirrors fundamental physiology and prior Indian studies, which show higher 25(OH)D with longer outdoor play and midday exposure [6]. Our finding that even > 2 h/day relates to 72.7% sufficiency underscores the potency of regular outdoor activity—though timing, body surface area exposed, and latitude modulate endogenous synthesis. Sunscreen use was associated with higher deficiency; while sunscreen prevents skin damage and cancer risk, it can block UVB required for cutaneous vitamin D synthesis. The public health message should therefore balance safe sun exposure (short, regular, non-peak or carefully timed exposure; protective clothing after minimal exposure) with appropriate sunscreen use—especially when exposure exceeds recommended brief durations.

Dietary correlates were clear. Milk consumption showed a protective association, consistent with evidence that dairy (especially when fortified) contributes meaningful vitamin D and calcium in Indian diets. Children consuming milk 3–5 days/week demonstrated markedly better status than those with infrequent milk intake. Notably, those with daily milk (5–7 days) did not outperform the 3–5 days group in our sample; this may reflect portion sizes, fortification variability, or confounding by other dietary or lifestyle factors.

Egg intake displayed a non-linear association: the 3–5 days/week group had the best status, outperforming both low and high intake categories. This could reflect dietary diversity; families with moderate egg consumption might concurrently maintain balanced diets, greater outdoor activity, or other unmeasured health behaviors. Alternatively, the small size of the “5–7 days/week” subgroup (n = 19) may limit precision. Nevertheless, the signal supports counseling to include vitamin D–rich foods (eggs, dairy/fortified milk, fish where culturally acceptable) within a diversified diet.

We observed no association of vitamin D with age or sex. Some North Indian studies report worse status in adolescent girls—often attributed to clothing norms, indoor time, and cultural constraints [11, 16]. Our null finding may reflect regional cultural differences, greater outdoor play among girls in this setting, or sample composition tilted toward younger ages (median 6 years) when sex-related differences are less pronounced. Religion likewise showed no association, indicating that observed deficiencies transcend religious or cultural groups in this region.

The socioeconomic trend toward higher deficiency in the upper class contrasts with the conventional expectation that lower SES confers worse micronutrient status. A plausible explanation is indoor lifestyles, screen time, air-conditioned environments, private transport (less walking), and consistent sun protection behaviors in affluent families. Given the tiny upper-class subsample (n = 7), this pattern requires replication with adequate power before drawing firm conclusions.

Clinical implications

Our data argue for a multicomponent strategy:

Safe sun exposure guidance: Encourage routine short, regular outdoor exposure for children, ideally when UV index is adequate yet risk of erythema is lower (e.g., morning/late afternoon), with progressive protection thereafter. Practical advice on duration, timing, and body surface area is essential and should be adapted to season and skin type. Schools can incorporate outdoor activity windows.

Strengths

This study has several notable strengths. First, it is one of the relatively few clinic-based pediatric studies from Western India that simultaneously assessed multiple behavioral, environmental, and dietary determinants of vitamin D status within the same cohort. The structured and standardized approach to data collection ensured uniformity across participants, and the sample size (n = 277) was adequate for the primary prevalence objective.

Second, the use of serum 25(OH)D measured by chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) provided reliable quantification of vitamin D status. Applying Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) revised cut-offs allowed direct comparability with other recent Indian pediatric studies and national guidelines.

Third, the analysis included several modifiable factors—daily sun exposure duration, sunscreen use, milk and egg consumption frequency, and vegetarian dietary adherence—allowing us to identify practical levers for intervention. This contrasts with many earlier studies focusing primarily on prevalence without exploring determinants in detail.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that vitamin D deficiency and insufficiency are highly prevalent among children in Western India, affecting over three-quarters of the studied population. Despite abundant sunlight, only 22.4% of children achieved vitamin D sufficiency. Sun exposure duration, sunscreen use, and dietary habits—particularly milk consumption and moderate egg intake—were significantly associated with serum vitamin D levels. In contrast, age, gender, and religion were not significant determinants.

These findings highlight the paradox of vitamin D deficiency in sunny countries like India, where behavioral and dietary factors—rather than sunlight availability alone—play a pivotal role. Targeted public health strategies are urgently needed. These should include education on safe sun exposure, promotion of fortified milk and dietary diversification, age-appropriate supplementation for high-risk groups, and policy-level fortification measures to improve population vitamin D status.

Timely recognition and intervention during childhood have the potential to prevent long-term skeletal and extra-skeletal complications, contributing to healthier growth trajectories and improved public health outcomes.
