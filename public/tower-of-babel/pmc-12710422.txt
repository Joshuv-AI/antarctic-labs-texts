Narcolepsy Associated With Streptococcal Infection: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Zhenzhen Zhu, Qingyu Zhang, Shasha Hu, Chenxin Yan, Wenqing Jiang, Yunxin Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStreptococcal infection–induced narcolepsy is a rare neurological disorder that may be accompanied by severe psychiatric symptoms and social dysfunction. In this case, delayed diagnosis and treatment were followed by progressive symptom worsening, including marked cognitive decline. The report underscores the importance of considering infectious triggers in narcolepsy and the therapeutic dilemma in managing psychiatric comorbidities—wake‐promoting agents may exacerbate psychosis, while antipsychotics risk tardive dyskinesia. Early recognition and individualized treatment are crucial to improving outcomes.Keywords: case report, cognitive decline, narcolepsy, psychotic symptoms, streptococcal infection

Introduction

Narcolepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized primarily by excessive daytime sleepiness, often accompanied by cataplexy, hypnagogic hallucinations, and sleep paralysis [1]. Globally, its prevalence ranges from 25 to 50 per 100,000 individuals [2]. The disorder is thought to result from the selective loss of hypothalamic hypocretin (orexin) neurons, likely mediated by aberrant immune mechanisms. Diagnosis and treatment are particularly challenging in pediatric and adolescent patients, in whom heterogeneous presentations often lead to misdiagnosis and treatment delays. Therefore, early and accurate diagnosis, along with personalized treatment plans, is essential for enhancing patients' quality of life. This report presents a pediatric narcolepsy case from our center in 2024, offering insights into its clinical features, diagnostic workup, and management strategies, with the aim of supporting earlier recognition and individualized care by clinicians.

Case History/Examination

An 11‐year‐old girl, previously healthy and academically active, developed a low‐grade fever after a common cold approximately 4 years before presentation. Initial laboratory tests indicated an elevated anti‐streptolysin O (ASO) titer of 311 IU/mL, suggesting recent streptococcal exposure. After 1 week of anti‐inflammatory treatment at a local clinic, her fever resolved, but the ASO level remained elevated (1400 IU/mL). These findings suggested recent streptococcal exposure but did not establish a causal relationship. About 2 weeks later, her parents observed pronounced excessive daytime sleepiness, with the patient experiencing sudden sleep attacks during daily activities, including meals. Additionally, she experienced frequent hypnagogic hallucinations at night, characterized by vivid, frightening images and sensations, often accompanied by screaming and involuntary limb movements.

Although these symptoms persisted for approximately 6 months, no diagnosis was made. She was subsequently referred to Shanghai Children's Hospital for further evaluation. Laboratory investigations revealed persistently elevated ASO titers (1328 IU/mL), indicating ongoing or recurrent streptococcal exposure. Immune function tests (including complement and autoantibodies) and routine biochemical profiles were within normal limits. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed normal cell counts, glucose, protein, and enzyme levels (AST, CK, LDH, ADA), with no pathogens detected (bacteria, fungi, or acid‐fast bacilli).

Neurological evaluation revealed abnormal EEG activity characterized by mixed frequency waves (moderate to low amplitude α waves, low amplitude β and δ waves). Brain MRI showed mild, nonspecific white matter abnormalities around the posterior horns of the lateral ventricles. Overnight polysomnography (PSG) demonstrated remarkably short sleep latency (0.5 min) and high sleep efficiency (93%), but a mildly reduced total sleep time (438 min). An increased proportion of REM sleep (22.1%) was observed, while stage N2 sleep was decreased and stage N3 elevated, indicating suboptimal sleep architecture. No significant respiratory or limb movement events were observed (AHI: 0.7; PLMI: 0), and average oxygen saturation was 99%. The subsequent Multiple Sleep Latency Test (MSLT) revealed sleep onset in all five trials, an extremely short mean sleep latency (0.1 min), and four SOREMPs, fulfilling diagnostic criteria for narcolepsy She was then treated with long‐acting penicillin, and her ASO titers gradually normalized (Table 1).

To manage her excessive daytime sleepiness, methylphenidate (10 mg/day) was initiated following the diagnosis of narcolepsy. However, the patient's family became concerned about worsening nighttime hypnagogic hallucinations and increased difficulty with sleep initiation. Without consulting the treating physicians they discontinued the medication after a brief trial, and no further pharmacological interventions were pursued. During the following 6 months, the patient received no structured treatment. As her condition progressed, she began to exhibit additional psychiatric symptoms, most notably auditory hallucinations characterized by perceived voices and episodes of inappropriate laughter. These symptoms occurred in the absence of clear mood or delusional components. Approximately 3 years prior to her current presentation, she was diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder at the Shanghai Mental Health Center and was started on risperidone, which was titrated up to 4.5 mg/day. Although her psychiatric symptoms initially improved, she gradually developed extrapyramidal side effects, including involuntary oral‐facial movements and persistent body tilting, consistent with tardive dyskinesia.

Two years ago, her daytime sleepiness began to diminish, but she developed substantial cognitive impairment, including frequent staring episodes, slowed responses, declining academic performance, and neglect of personal hygiene. Consequently, she took a leave of absence from school and remained at home under family care. Despite ongoing medication, involuntary movements persisted, prompting her family to seek further medical evaluation at our hospital.

On admission, internal medicine and neurological examinations were unremarkable. Psychiatric assessment showed a cooperative but disheveled patient exhibiting auditory hallucinations, impaired attention and memory, and partial insight. Intelligence testing (Wechsler Intelligence scale) yielded a borderline IQ of 88 with notable deficits in visual tasks. Memory quotient testing revealed a borderline score of 71, while the Social Dysfunction Screening Scale and Activities of Daily Living Scale indicated mild functional impairment. Attention span testing demonstrated impaired concentration.

Conclusion and Results

She was diagnosed with mental disorders secondary to organic brain disease and with tardive dyskinesia. At our institution, risperidone was gradually tapered to 1 mg/day over a 6‐week period, leading to substantial improvement in both extrapyramidal symptoms and auditory hallucinations.

To address her persistent cognitive, behavioral, and functional impairments, the patient was enrolled in a structured behavioral rehabilitation program under the guidance of clinical psychologists and therapists. The program comprised four key components:

Sleep hygiene education: The patient was guided to maintain a consistent bedtime and wake‐up time, including weekends. Daytime naps were discouraged to promote nighttime sleep consolidation. Screen exposure was reduced in the evening, and light physical activity in the morning was encouraged to strengthen circadian alignment.

Discussion

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in pediatric narcolepsy with psychiatric comorbidities. The patient exhibited typical features of narcolepsy following a streptococcal infection, yet remained undiagnosed for an extended period. Over time, she developed complex psychiatric and cognitive symptoms, which led to long‐term antipsychotic use and the development of tardive dyskinesia. These developments underscore the multifaceted nature of post‐infectious narcolepsy and the potential for misdiagnosis when early signs are overlooked.

Narcolepsy is commonly regarded as a central nervous system disorder arising from the interplay of genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and immune responses. Its pathophysiology is complex, centered around the selective loss or dysfunction of hypocretin neurons in the hypothalamus, closely linked to aberrant immune activity [3]. For instance, certain infections (bacterial or viral) or vaccinations may trigger an immune response that leads to the damage of these neurons [4]. Research indicates that prior to the onset of narcolepsy, children who later develop narcolepsy are more than five times as likely to have experienced streptococcal infection compared to the general pediatric population [5]. Streptococcal infection may serve as an environmental trigger by increasing blood–brain barrier permeability through T‐cell activation during inflammatory and febrile responses, thereby facilitating an autoimmune attack on hypocretin neurons in the hypothalamus. Recent immunological studies further support this hypothesis, showing that Group A streptococcal infections can enhance central nervous system immune surveillance and promote hypocretin‐targeting autoimmunity [6]. This immune‐mediated vulnerability is further amplified by underlying genetic predisposition.

Such autoimmune susceptibility appears to be especially pronounced in individuals with a genetic predisposition. In particular, the HLA‐DQB1*06:02 allele has been strongly associated with narcolepsy, significantly increasing risk among carriers [7]. This allele may modulate immune system function and lower the threshold for initiating autoimmune responses against hypocretin neurons. Additionally, specific amino acid variations, such as serine182 and threonine185 on the DQβ1 chain receptor epitope, have been identified as additional susceptibility factors. This genetic‐environmental interplay provides a plausible explanation for our patient's disease onset following streptococcal infection.

The coexistence of narcolepsy and psychotic‐like symptoms presents significant clinical complexity and demands careful attention. A systematic review by Hanin et al. [8], which analyzed 100 full‐text articles on narcolepsy and psychotic symptoms, identified two categories of psychotic‐like manifestations in narcolepsy patients. The first category involves typical narcolepsy patients who predominantly experience visual hallucinations during the sleep–wake transition (e.g., when falling asleep or waking up), which patients usually recognize as unreal. The second category includes atypical narcolepsy patients who may encounter more severe, vivid REM‐related hallucinations or a blurring of the line between dreams and reality, sometimes rationalized as delusions. The early‐stage psychotic symptoms observed in the patient described in this case likely correspond to the first type, characterized by frequent visual hallucinations before sleep without significant delusional symptoms. Comparable diagnostic challenges have been reported in the literature. In a recent case series, three adolescents initially diagnosed with depression, dissociative disorder, or schizophrenia were subsequently found to have narcolepsy based on PSG and MSLT findings, following the recognition of cataplexy and REM‐related hallucinations [9]. Another report described an adolescent patient misdiagnosed with early‐onset psychosis, whose preserved insight and prominent hypnagogic hallucinations eventually led to a correct diagnosis of narcolepsy [10], underscoring the need to distinguish narcolepsy‐related hallucinations from primary psychosis, especially in adolescents with sleep‐related perceptual disturbances. Timely referral for sleep studies, including PSG and MSLT, can help clarify the diagnosis and prevent unnecessary exposure to antipsychotic medications.

Although there have been case reports of narcolepsy coexisting with schizophrenia, this comorbidity is relatively rare. A nationwide population‐based cohort study in Taiwan found that 8.1% of narcolepsy patients had a coexisting psychotic disorder—significantly higher than the 1.5% in matched controls—and notably, stimulant medications did not increase the risk of psychosis [11]. In this case, following the narcoleptic‐like symptoms, the patient developed hallucinations and disorganized behavior, which were considered to result from functional changes due to organic brain disease rather than from comorbid schizophrenia. Her psychotic symptoms lacked association with sleep disturbances. Notably, two cases of narcolepsy comorbid with schizophrenia have been reported in China, with both showing symptom improvement following antipsychotic therapy [12, 13]. It is important to note that hallucinations in narcolepsy typically occur during the sleep–wake transition, while in schizophrenia, hallucinations are more frequently observed during periods of wakefulness [8]. This distinction may be attributed to the loss of hypocretin neurons in narcolepsy, which allows REM sleep phenomena (such as dreaming) to intrude into wakefulness, thereby causing hallucinations during the transition between sleep and wakefulness. In contrast, schizophrenia is associated with an imbalance in dopamine and other neurotransmitters in the brain, leading to hallucinations and delusions in the absence of external stimuli [14].

In this case, the patient's auditory hallucinations improved following the gradual tapering of risperidone, a phenomenon that may appear counterintuitive. One plausible explanation is that prolonged exposure to high‐dose antipsychotic therapy may induce dopamine receptor hypersensitivity, known as dopamine supersensitivity psychosis (DSP), which could perpetuate or exacerbate psychotic symptoms in vulnerable individuals [15]. Additionally, hallucinations in narcolepsy are more likely due to REM sleep‐related mechanisms rather than dopaminergic dysregulation [16, 17]. This distinction may explain the limited efficacy of antipsychotics in such cases and the potential for adverse cognitive and behavioral outcomes [17, 18]. Gradual dose reduction may have mitigated these effects, contributing to symptom resolution.

Cognitive impairment associated with narcolepsy is a significant concern that requires careful attention. In this case, the patient exhibited a gradual decline in intelligence, attention, memory, and social functioning. These observations are consistent with findings from several studies. A systematic review analyzing cognitive function in patients with central hypersomnia disorders revealed that type 1 narcolepsy patients show pronounced deficits in attention. Although memory function is generally preserved, these patients display a complex pattern of higher‐order impairment, including poor decision‐making and difficulties in emotional processing [19]. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis in Sleep Advances confirmed that individuals with narcolepsy type 1 display large impairments in sustained attention, moderate deficits in executive function, and small but significant deficits in memory, reinforcing that cognitive impairment is a core feature of the disorder [20]. The mechanisms by which narcolepsy contributes to cognitive decline are not yet fully understood. In this case, the cognitive impairment that manifested later in the course of the illness is more likely associated with post‐infectious organic brain changes. Some researchers have observed that type 1 narcolepsy patients exhibit reduced whole‐brain network efficiency during the N2 sleep‐stage [21]. Further studies by Ni et al., using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), identified topological abnormalities in the brain's white matter network in NT1 patients, linking these structural changes to cognitive decline. The decreased efficiency of the whole‐brain white matter network may underlie the observed deterioration in both cognitive and social functioning in these patients [22]. Moreover, a pediatric study investigated sleep‐dependent memory consolidation in narcolepsy‐cataplexy and found that increased sleep‐stage transitions correlated with poorer overnight memory retention in children, suggesting that sleep instability directly impairs cognitive consolidation in pediatric patients [23]. Notably, our patient demonstrated borderline IQ and memory scores, and limited response to stimulant therapy—findings that align with disease‐specific cognitive impairment. This may have been further compounded by prolonged untreated disease, psychiatric comorbidity, and adverse effects from long‐term antipsychotic use.

This case highlights the need for clinicians to improve their recognition of narcolepsy temporally associated with streptococcal infection and to avoid misinterpreting early sleep‐related hallucinations as primary psychiatric symptoms. In this case, delayed diagnosis, premature discontinuation of treatment without professional guidance, and prolonged inappropriate use of high‐dose antipsychotics not only led to secondary psychiatric symptoms and tardive dyskinesia but also contributed to long‐term cognitive and functional decline. These outcomes underscore the critical importance of early identification and sustained, individualized intervention in managing post‐infectious forms of narcolepsy, with close attention to both medical and behavioral dimensions of care.

An elevated ASO titer alone cannot confirm acute streptococcal infection. It merely reflects prior exposure and does not determine timing. In this case, the diagnosis was based on the combination of recent clinical symptoms (such as fever and upper respiratory infection), persistently elevated ASO titers, and clinical improvement after anti‐inflammatory treatment. However, the lack of a positive throat culture is a limitation of this report. In this case, hypocretin‐1 levels in cerebrospinal fluid were not tested due to clinical constraints, representing another limitation, and future cases should include this biomarker for a more comprehensive evaluation. The mild myelination abnormalities noted on MRI are nonspecific and could have been pre‐existing, as such changes are commonly observed in children with psychiatric disorders. These findings are not specific for PANDAS or narcolepsy following streptococcal infection, and their causal relevance to the patient's clinical symptoms is uncertain. It should be noted that EEG abnormalities are not typical of PANDAS, and the diagnosis of narcolepsy in this patient was established based on standard clinical and polysomnographic criteria. Accordingly, the association with streptococcal infection in this case remains speculative, and a direct causal relationship cannot be confirmed.

This case provides several important lessons for clinical practice. First, the markedly delayed diagnosis highlights the need for clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion for narcolepsy in adolescents presenting with complex psychiatric or sleep‐related symptoms, in order to avoid misdiagnosis and inappropriate long‐term treatment. Second, the development of tardive dyskinesia following prolonged exposure to high‐dose risperidone illustrates the therapeutic dilemma in managing psychiatric comorbidities: wake‐promoting agents may exacerbate hallucinations, while antipsychotics can induce severe extrapyramidal side effects. Third, the progressive cognitive decline observed in this patient underscores the potential long‐term functional consequences of untreated narcolepsy and prolonged exposure to psychotropic medications. Taken together, these features emphasize the importance of timely recognition, cautious pharmacological decision‐making, and the integration of structured rehabilitation strategies to optimize outcomes in complex cases of narcolepsy associated with infectious triggers.
