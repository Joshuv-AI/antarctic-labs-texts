Improved impact from the use of beneficial natural enemy species in Australian canola systems: challenges and required tools
Authors: Sarina Macfadyen, Basman Al‐Jalely, Mukti Chalise, Maryam Ehsangar, Zorica Duric, Joanne Holloway, Andrew Hulthen, Precious Mavende, Paul Melloy, Svetlana Micic, Hazel Parry, Shovon Chandra Sarkar, Jessa Thurman, Maarten Van Helden, Xiaobei Wang, Wei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe concept of Integrated Pest Management (IPM) has developed into a practical framework for sustainable pest management and is widely adopted in modern farming systems. Beneficial arthropods (natural enemies, such as predators and parasitoids) have shown potential for use in pest management, leading to observable production benefits. However, using beneficials is perhaps the most challenging IPM component to adopt at scale and in Australian canola systems, adoption remains low. Here we consider the challenges for measuring impact from beneficials in a way that links to production benefits for growers. To get an industry perspective, we surveyed canola growers and their advisors to understand their constraints and priorities for using beneficials for improved pest management. There were few practices to support and conserve beneficials adopted at scale (use of selective insecticides was the most used practice), and significant knowledge gaps about what impacts growers were likely to see if certain practices were to be adopted. Despite these knowledge gaps, canola growers and their advisors aspired towards more active management of beneficials. From our synthesis, we outline the new tools that are needed, designed specifically for beneficials, and present a pathway forward to enable the greater use of beneficials. We suggest that incremental steps are required for proactive transitions rather than whole‐system shifts driven by industry crises. © 2026 Commonwealth of Australia. INTRODUCTION

The concept of Integrated Pest Management (IPM) has been developed into practical frameworks for sustainable pest management over the last 100 years. 1 , 2 However, adoption of true IPM that integrates multiple management tools scouting for pests and beneficial arthropods, and delaying the use of insecticides until economic thresholds, has not reached its fullest extent around the world. 3 Of all the IPM techniques, the use of ‘beneficials’ (arthropod natural enemies including predators and parasitoid wasps and flies, in this article we exclude pollinators) to control pest populations is perhaps the hardest to adopt at scale. 4 The recognition that beneficials provide an important ecosystem service by reducing the risk of crop losses to pests is known, 5 but management practices that conserve these species are not widely adopted. The benefits that growers and agronomists are likely to see from a healthy beneficial community in terms of reduced pest populations and lower risk of crop losses vary across space and time and are hard to observe. Furthermore, sustainability and biodiversity credentialing schemes that financially reward growers for enacting practices on their land don't yet extend to management of beneficials. Despite these challenges, there is a growing momentum for more targeted methods for managing beneficials that can be integrated with other practices and return a direct benefit to the grower.

In Australian farming systems, grain crops are extensively grown, often with low inputs; therefore, integrating greater use of beneficials with pesticides and cultural control practices is a growing area of development. In Australian farming systems canola (Brassica napus, oilseed rape 6 with a lower level of erucic acid) is an important part of the dryland rotation system, in which it is often used as a break crop for cereal diseases. However, canola is highly susceptible to a diversity of arthropod pest species and diseases. 7 Collectively, early season pests like redlegged earth mites (Halotydeus destructor) and green peach aphid (Myzus persicae) cause emergence issues, and later in the season diamond back moth (Plutella xylostella) and budworms (Helicoverpa punctigera and H. armigera) can cause economic damage the crop. 8 Canola aphids can also vector viruses such as the Turnip yellows virus (TuYV). 9 However, these pest outbreaks are sporadic across space and time and can be overshadowed by crop losses due to low rainfall or other abiotic threats. In this context canola growers are heavily reliant on seed dressings 10 and prophylactic applications of usually broad‐spectrum (non‐selective) insecticides, to reduce pest outbreak risk. This has led to high rates of resistance development in some canola pests11, 12 and limited options for integrating beneficial arthropods due to the toxicity of certain insecticides. 13 , 14 Risk mitigation tools (e.g., Bt crops 15 ) and economic incentive schemes that have helped growers transition pest management strategies in other contexts are not yet available in Australian canola.

There is potential for beneficials to play a greater role in the pest control strategies of canola growers, but this has not yet been realized. In Australian canola systems, there are 37 taxa (within Arthropoda) and potentially hundreds of species 16 that have beneficial activities against pest species (Table 1). Most of these species occur naturally in the farming system, being present in crop fields as well as pastures, and semi‐natural vegetation found in the landscape. 4 IPM strategies hypothesized to support beneficials focus on practices like the use of selective insecticides and management of semi‐natural vegetation to conserve beneficial populations in the landscape. In some crop systems, inoculative or augmentative releases of beneficials, particularly parasitoid wasps, are used (although this practice is less common in canola). However, the quantitative effect of most beneficials on pest control remains poorly understood 17 and generic landscape models of biocontrol services are highly variable. 18 Macfadyen et al. (2019) 16 found that very few studies have assessed pest and beneficial species interactions concurrently, especially for predatory species, and that the variation in the level of pest control provided by beneficials across years and regions remains unexplained. For some taxa, like aphid parasitoids, we have documented relationships 19 or can infer impact from laboratory feeding assays. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 However, their observable effects are intrinsically linked to population dynamics, 24 resulting in delayed action compared to more conventional methods. 4

In this perspective article, we analyse grower and industry stakeholders' constraints and priorities for using beneficials for improved pest management in canola systems and chart a pathway forward for adoption. We summarize the limited data on what practices are currently used to support beneficials and present evidence from a survey of canola industry stakeholders. Secondly, we clarify what ‘benefits’ mean in practice for canola growers. We define the types of ecological and production impacts from beneficials and synthesize current evidence on how these impacts manifest in farming systems. Thirdly, we outline the tools, practices and system‐level changes needed to enable effective and scalable use of beneficials in canola systems.

PRACTICES USED TO SUPPORT BENEFICIALS

There is a diversity of practices that could be used to conserve and manage beneficial populations. From conserving semi‐natural vegetation, 25 planting beetle banks and floral strips, crop rotation and stubble management, through to the use of selective insecticides (that are less toxic to beneficials), feed sprays, and releasing mass‐produced beneficials. Understanding when and how canola growers implement practices to support beneficials, and how information about beneficials informs their decision‐making, is challenging due to a lack of data. Many of the practices used to enhance beneficials are not regularly captured in farm balance sheets, although they can form part of IPM practice surveys. 26 , 27 For example, in the USA Farrar (2023) 28 summarized the annual survey data collected in California, examining IPM adoption across time. Practices like the maintenance of habitats for beneficials, the release of beneficials, monitoring for beneficials, and biological pesticides (that we presume are less toxic to beneficials) were recorded. Adoption rates of these practices were generally low in broad‐acre crop systems (0–13% of operations), although canola was not a focal crop for this survey. 28 In the UK, researchers identified two farmer typologies from surveys about their practices 29 ; those who adopted in‐crop practices designed to enhance the populations of beneficials, and a second group using technologies to enhance and manipulate background populations of beneficials. Farmers in the first group had significantly lower reliance on pesticides, but this correlation was not seen for the latter group.

Whilst we do not have up‐to‐date figures on IPM adoption and pest management practices used on Australian grain farms, we know that many growers strive to incorporate a diversity of practices beyond pesticide use. A survey of Australian grain growers and their advisors from 2021 (H. Spafford unpublished data collected as part of GRDC investment UWA00134) benchmarked the pest management practices used on their farms at the time. The majority (87% of 278 respondents) of growers incorporated a variety of practices for pest management, however 24% of growers relied only on pesticides. Half of all the advisors (153 survey respondents in total) rated their understanding of the impact insecticides have on beneficials as medium/high to high, 34% medium, and 16% low to none. Most advisors (95%) knew some of the insecticides that are selective (or ‘soft’) on beneficials and most advisors (68%) always or mostly considered the impacts of insecticides on beneficials when recommending insecticides to growers.

To get more up‐to‐date information about the practices used to support beneficials specifically in canola, we undertook a short survey aimed at canola advisers and growers. We created a simple online survey form and distributed this at grain industry events and workshops in early 2024 (methods and example survey form are in Supplementary material 1). Of the 61 respondents, most reported having some previous training in beneficial identification and were reasonably confident in identifying beneficials. Therefore, they were likely to be early adopters or more willing to consider practices to enable the greater use of beneficials. Minimizing the use of non‐selective insecticides was the most frequently used practice for supporting beneficials in canola systems (Fig. 1). The release of beneficials, enhancing habitats to conserve beneficials, and creating refuges or flowering strips to support beneficials had not been used in the last 5 years by most respondents (60–80%). The canola growers and advisors identified knowledge gaps such as how to increase beneficial populations in canola, which beneficial taxa are likely to be more important, adjusting spray thresholds for pests, and how to determine the impact of beneficials in terms of reducing pest risk (Supplementary material 1). Despite these knowledge gaps, respondents still expressed clear aspirations for improving their future management of beneficials (Supplementary material 1). Over the next 5 years, they hoped to adjust insecticide programs to reduce harm to beneficials, ensure beneficials were present near their canola fields, collect evidence of beneficial impacts, and enhance their monitoring practices.

Many canola producers and their advisors have not used the inoculative release of mass‐produced beneficials to augment naturally occurring species in their landscapes (Fig. 1) despite species being available (Table 1). Brier et al. (2008) 30 reflected that releases of beneficials in field crops have not been widely adopted because of high costs, practical limitations of large field sizes and inconsistent efficacy. Our survey results suggest this is still a challenging practice to adopt today. However, the historical reliance on non‐selective insecticide products that caused high mortality in beneficials also made this practice less feasible. Jenkins et al. (2025) 31 synthesized recent Australian studies investigating augmentative releases for aphid control in canola. These trials demonstrate that parasitoid augmentation, when combined with selective insecticide use, can contribute to effective aphid suppression under certain conditions. Other countries have demonstrated some success in broad‐acre open‐field environments. Parra & Coelho (2022) 32 give the example of over 3 million hectares in Brazil being treated with releases of a native Trichogramma parasitoid, and an exotic parasitoid, Cotesia flavipes to control the sugarcane borer. With the rapidly growing use and access to a new generation of selective insecticides and targeted biologicals, integrating releases should become more feasible. However, tools to optimize releases in open field environments and assessments of the risks to non‐target species still need to be developed.

THE DIFFERENT TYPES OF IMPACT FROM BENEFICIALS

The industry perspective reflected in our survey results—and echoed in our conversations with canola growers and advisors—indicates that the impact of beneficials remains unclear and highly variable across space and time. As a result, growers find it difficult to justify adopting practices aimed at managing or conserving beneficials. In the sections that follow, we examine why ecological impacts are inherently challenging to measure and why demonstrating clear links to production outcomes is as important.

Ecological impacts

There is a large diversity of beneficial species across canola production areas (Table 1) in Australia and there is a diversity of ways that these species impact the abundance and behavior of pest species in canola fields. Evidence of direct and indirect ecological impacts of beneficials in canola is present but not consistently documented for every beneficial species. For example, parasitoids can play an important role in suppressing aphid populations. In south‐eastern Australia, research across 28 fields revealed that Diaeretiella rapae (Hymenoptera: Braconidae) was the dominant primary parasitoid species on aphid pests in canola, while Aphidius matricariae (Hymenoptera: Braconidae) predominated in wheat fields. 33 Field edges can serve as reservoirs for parasitoid populations in wheat systems, where similar host plants in edges and crops support continuous parasitoid activity, though this pattern was not observed in canola due to differences in available host plants. 33 Ladybeetles (Coccinellidae) are known to consume aphids on canola, but often the quantification of their direct impacts come from laboratory studies. Holloway et al. (2022) 34 showed that Harmonia conformis can consume over 200 aphids per day in laboratory studies, but the numbers consumed in a glasshouse trial annually were less. Other predators are omnivorous 35 and can consume a wide variety of prey, they have a demonstrated role as a beneficial in canola systems.

The challenge of measuring ecological impacts

Whilst we have some understanding about the large‐scale distribution of beneficial species and communities in different habitats in agricultural production landscapes, 36 , 37 , 38 monitoring ecological impacts goes beyond measuring species abundance at sites. Studies documenting how many prey species can be consumed or parasitized by beneficials in laboratory testing assays are relatively common, 20 , 21 , 22 however drawing inferences to field suppression of pest populations from these laboratory environments is challenging. Ward et al. (2022) 19 found that laboratory observed parasitism was four‐fold higher than the parasitism rate observed in fields. Laboratory consumption rate studies can be used to infer the temperature thresholds for when beneficials are active in the field environment. 20 Field cage studies can be used to quantify the impact of predators or parasitoids, 24 , 39 but attribution of mortality to beneficial taxa remains challenging. To estimate the impact of individual species in terms of the numbers of pests consumed in the field, direct testing using molecular detection of prey DNA in the guts of predators and methods to monitor parasitism in the field are required. 17 For example, Hodgkiss et al. (2002) 40 detected aphid DNA in 37% of hoverfly specimens collected from strawberry fields in the UK. Detection rates of aphids in hoverflies were highest near plots with forget‐me‐not flowers, Myosotis arvensis (L.), but this was not significantly different from the control (without flowers). Given the difficulty with studying predation and parasitism in canola fields a combination of methods will be required 41 and scaling these across large geographic regions requires significant research investment.

In addition to identifying the important beneficial species that directly or indirectly impact pests, there is an increasing need to understand the whole beneficial community to predict ecological impacts. The biodiversity of beneficial communities, as composed of species richness and abundance, is now linked clearly to ecosystem service provision. Dainese et al. (2019) 5 used studies from around the world to show that beneficial species richness directly supported pest control services (in addition to individual species abundance and dominance). Landscape simplification harmed ecosystem services and this was due in part to richness losses of service‐providing species. 5 Greater beneficial biodiversity may strengthen pest control services through complementary feeding niches, 42 species with different mobilities, 43 species that use different habitats and resources in the landscape, and species that can persist across different weather extremes. By understanding the landscape context in which a canola crop is grown, it becomes possible to predict the composition of beneficials present, their potential to deliver pest control services, and consequently include them in pest management decision‐making.

Production impacts that may lead to benefits to the grower

Through their ecological impact, beneficials deliver outcomes for the grower in terms of protection from crop losses or reduced risk of uncontrollable pest outbreaks, collectively called pest control services or biological control services. 44 , 45 We describe these as the benefits that growers may observe if they were to implement practices to conserve and manage beneficials (Table 2, see the practices outlined in section 2). Some of the practices may be costly to implement (e.g., planting flowering strips, beetle banks, selective insecticides 46 ). However, some practices are part of normal farm operations and so do not add additional costs (e.g., conservation of semi‐natural habitats that aren't suitable for cropping 47 ). Importantly, some of the impacts seen by growers are hard to value economically (Table 2) but are seen as valuable for reducing risk, improving social license, or access to specialist markets.

The challenge of measuring production impacts

Measuring the production impacts of beneficials is challenging because they operate within complex and highly variable agroecosystems. 48 Linking beneficials to measurable yield outcomes or other economic indicators liked avoided crop losses in canola (Table 2) requires controlling for numerous confounding factors, including weather conditions, soil fertility, crop variety, sowing density, pest pressure, pesticide history, and management practices. Beneficial populations fluctuate across fields, seasons, and years and respond dynamically to changes in pest density. 49 As a result, field‐level impacts are unlikely to be consistent. Additionally, abiotic loss agents such as heat stress and frost often exceed pest‐related yield losses, masking or interacting with the effects of biological control. In Australia, late‐spring frosts are particularly damaging; modelling indicates that severe frost events can reduce grain yields by up to 80%. 50 These climatic factors complicate attribution in field studies, as variation caused by frost or heat can overshadow any incremental benefits gained from pest suppression.

Biological control operates within a broader spatial landscape context. The configuration and composition of the surrounding landscape, and the resources available in the landscape, strongly influence beneficials and their impacts. Theoretical and empirical studies demonstrate that the spatial arrangement of semi‐natural habitats, non‐crop corridors, cropping system, and field size affects the extent to which beneficials can regulate pest populations. 24 , 25 , 48 , 51 , 52 The provision of floral resources can enhance the services provided by many beneficial species. 53 , 54 , 55 In addition, many of the pesticides used in our farming systems may have variable direct and indirect impacts on beneficial populations in fields. 56 , 57 , 58 Therefore, making prescriptive practice recommendations that apply across all landscape contexts and lead to quantifiable production impacts is challenging.

WHAT TOOLS DO CANOLA GROWERS CURRENTLY USE?

Understanding how beneficials can be effectively incorporated into IPM strategies is a growing field of research. The knowledge tools, decision‐support systems, or extension guides that facilitate adoption are different from those used for other technologies. Canola growers and their advisors mostly gather information on beneficials through field‐based indicators of activity, particularly the presence of aphid mummies as evidence of parasitoid activity. 13 , 31 They are increasingly encouraged to consider pest to beneficial ratios and observable beneficial activity as modifiers of spray decisions, rather than relying on pest density thresholds alone. To do this, they need access to intelligence about when and where beneficials are found in their fields and to some degree in the surrounding landscape (because many taxa can move).

Surveillance systems for the detection of beneficial abundance or activity is incomplete and not clearly linked to on‐farm decision‐making. However, some components are present. In Australia, there are state‐based reporting services that are well used by agronomists, consultants and others to report pest outbreaks and receive information about where outbreaks are occurring (e.g., PestFacts in Western Australia, 59 Fig. 2). Reports of beneficials are uncommon but not absent from these services and in recent years, the number of reports has increased (Fig. 2). In cotton, well‐trained consultants are employed to scout crops for pests throughout the season and collect data on beneficial presence and abundance. This data is used to interpret pest pressure and adjust economic damage thresholds, reflecting long‐standing evidence that conservation of beneficials underpins pest suppression in cotton. 60 Similar consultant‐based surveillance systems are present but more ad hoc in canola due to the costs associated with hiring consultants to regularly inspect large fields throughout the season.

The commonly used insecticides in grain crops differ markedly in their acute and sublethal toxicity to beneficials. 13 This body of research has been translated into pesticide toxicity tables 61 and stewardship guidelines that are now widely promoted within Australian grain IPM programs. As a result, beneficials influence not only decisions on whether insecticide application is required, but also on which insecticide products are selected to minimize disruption to beneficials while maintaining effective pest suppression. How consistently such tables are consulted and whether they provide enough detail about impacts on beneficials across all pesticide groups, products and application mixes 62 is unclear.

NEW TOOLS ARE NEEDED TO ENABLE THE GREATER USE OF BENEFICIALS

Many of the information tools and knowledge systems that canola growers and their advisors rely on to manage pests have been developed with an emphasis on insecticide interventions. A new suite of tools, and integration with existing tools is needed to enable the greater use of beneficials. For example, beneficial toxicity has been included in independent pesticide impact tables. 61 But this knowledge has not yet been incorporated into operational tools for decision‐making around beneficial conservation and releases. This information is delivered independently of pesticide labels and uses information published by regulatory authorities, but is needed at the time when insecticide choice decisions are being made.

New tools which aid in communicating information about practices that support beneficials are critical for enabling incremental transitions from limited beneficials to active and deliberate management for pest control services (Fig. 3). Simulation modelling is not only a tool focused on optimizing when to spray insecticides, but also on understanding how beneficials, including those originating in non‐crop habitats, suppress pests and contribute to farm‐level economic benefits. 47 , 63 Simulation models have been developed in Europe to assist policy makers, growers and advisors to understand which landscapes may be deficient in ‘pest control potential’ (PCP) based on the surrounding non‐crop habitat and which areas could benefit to greater investment in non‐crop vegetation for ecosystem services. 18 , 64 This premise shows merit for decision‐making; by extending this simulation approach to include temporal variables it may assist in the more judicious use of insecticides or the optimized release of beneficials (Fig. 4). Decision support tools (DST), focused on pesticide application timing, would serve greater outcomes for industry if integrated with PCP models which consider landscape‐level drivers of crop pest suppression by beneficials. However, like all DST used in IPM adoption, to encourage uptake and use these tools need to be designed and developed with the end‐user in mind. 65

Evolution of technology stages to support transitions from limited management of beneficials through passive to active management. At the active management stage, additional training, new knowledge tools, and decision‐support tools are required.

In addition to digital tools and information systems, more sophisticated crop protection products that exploit beneficials need to be developed. Herbivore‐induced plant volatiles (HIPVs 66 ) represent a behavioral tool that can enhance the effectiveness of beneficials, however their use in current IPM systems is limited. Experimental field studies 67 demonstrate that herbivore attacks induce increased plant volatile emissions which attract predators and parasitoids use herbivore‐induced volatiles as cues during host searching. 68 These findings demonstrate that HIPVs function as behavior‐modifying tools capable of enhancing biological control by improving foraging efficiency of beneficials. However, integrating and deploying behavior modifiers 66 as management tools has yet to be realized for beneficial species in Australian canola systems.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

For Australian canola producers, the combined threats of resistance development in pests, 69 changes in market demands, and a scant pipeline for future novel synthetic chemical insecticides, are all driving momentum for greater use of beneficials. However, new tools are needed to facilitate faster adoption of practices for conserving and managing beneficials. For canola systems the economics of monitoring means that low cost automated and semi‐automated systems are essential.

CONCLUSIONS

Worldwide, biological control has achieved less than 1% market penetration in agricultural sales and has been considerably less successful than anticipated, even when accounting for non‐commercial technologies (i.e., products or practices that cannot be sold in discrete packages). 29 However, there is clearly a desire from growers, farming communities, food retailers and consumers to find more sustainable ways to manage pests. We have identified the steps needed for Australian canola growers to start this transition, but they require coordination across industries and governments to focus research, extension, and development. We also suggest that incremental steps are required for proactive transitions (Fig. 3) rather than whole‐system shifts driven by industry crises. Overall, supporting canola growers and advisors to continue to build their knowledge and skills about the use and impact of beneficials is central to success.
