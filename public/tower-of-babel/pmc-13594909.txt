Thromborepair: the tissue repair partner to thromboinflammation
Authors: Jonathan A Furniss, Alastair W Poole
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Traditionally, platelets have been perceived as vascular sentinels with only 2 major roles: hemostasis (preventing excessive blood loss upon vascular insult by forming a cellular plug) and thrombosis (a pathological intravascular process, resulting in obstructed blood flow).1 Now, however, our understanding of platelet behaviors has expanded. These behaviors can be pathological, for example contributing to cancer progression via tumor cell cloaking,2 facilitating metastasis and tumor cell tissue invasion and directly promoting tumor proliferation via growth factor release.3

Platelets form other heterotypical interactions, many of which are with immune cells. The terms “thromboinflammation” and “immunothrombosis” are now commonplace when discussing platelets in this context and encapsulate the platelet-immune system axis. Platelet-leukocyte interactions reciprocally mediate immune, inflammatory, and thrombotic responses. Immunothrombosis describes scenarios in which the host’s immune system initiates thrombotic events in response to foreign bodies.4 Broadly, this is protective and is believed to immobilize and decrease the spread of pathogens. Thromboinflammation outlines the interplay of platelets and immune cells and is largely considered pathological. It encapsulates processes such as platelet-leukocyte aggregate formation,5 platelet activation by immune system–derived stimuli,6 and the etiology of complex thrombotic diseases, such as strokes.7 Simply put, immunothrombosis describes the immune-mediated formation of thrombi, whereas thromboinflammation refers to how a platelet thrombus interacts with the immune system.

Thromboinflammatory interactions are frequently associated with disease states, however this is not always the case. Platelets contain a plethora of cytokines, proteins, and small molecules packaged into α-granules and dense granules.8,9 Many of these are pivotal in hemostasis, for example fibrinogen, ADP, and von Willebrand factor (VWF).10,11 Others are chemokines and immune-modulatory factors that mediate thromboinflammation and immunothrombosis.8,12 Platelet granule content has also been shown to orchestrate the tissue repair response after organ damage.12 This includes wound healing factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and others described in further detail in the “Platelets influencing tissue repair” section.13, 14, 15 The tight interactions with the immune system allow platelets to deliver their prorepair cargo directly to the site of damage.

Herein, we propose the new term “thromborepair” to describe the tissue healing effects imparted by platelets, via their cell-cell interactions and secreted content. This review aims to introduce how platelets are involved in tissue repair and, thus, how thromborepair can been enhanced or used therapeutically.

Thromboinflammation and the mixed roles of platelet-leukocyte aggregates in disease

It is well understood that platelets and leukocytes form aggregates upon activation. Platelets are activated through several pathways. Classical agonists include ADP, thromboxane A2, thrombin, and collagen.16 Noncanonical agonists include inflammatory signals, such as danger-associated molecular patterns,17 and pathogenic signals such as pathogen-associated molecular patterns.18 Once activated, signaling cascades induce granule release, activation of plasma membrane integrins, and morphological adaptations.16 α-Granule secretion mobilizes P-selectin19 and CD40L (CD154)20 onto the cell surface. Platelet P-selectin rapidly binds P-selectin glycoprotein ligand 1 (PSGL-1) on neutrophils21 and monocytes.22 This interaction has a relatively weak affinity, but its rapid kinetics facilitates tighter binding via slower receptors such as platelet CD40L and glycoprotein Ibα (GPIbα) engaging with leukocyte CD4023,24 and macrophage 1 antigen (Mac-1; CD11b/CD18; αMβ2),25 respectively. Leukocyte Mac-1 is also able to adhere, indirectly, to platelets by binding the activated form of αIIbβ3 via a fibrinogen bridge.26, 27, 28 This collection of interactions produces stable platelet-leukocyte aggregates. Other reviews explore this in further mechanistic detail.29, 30, 31, 32

Platelet-leukocyte aggregates are central to thromboinflammation and thromborepair. Traditionally, platelet-leukocyte aggregates are associated with poor patient prognosis in several platelet-associated diseases.30 Platelet-monocyte aggregates are used as a more robust biomarker of platelet activation in the clinic compared with P-selectin expression. For example, patients experiencing acute myocardial infarctions (MIs) can present with elevated platelet-monocyte aggregates with no detectable increase in surface P-selectin expression.33 Many studies examine the correlation of platelet-monocyte aggregate in disease, but few investigate whether they are causative of the pathology. Patients with diabetes show increased basal platelet-monocyte aggregates, but platelet-lymphocyte and platelet-neutrophil aggregate numbers are comparable with those of healthy donors.34 Blood samples taken within 24 hours of acute ischemic stroke contain elevated platelet-monocyte aggregates. However, when testing 3, 7, 14, and 42 days after stroke, platelet-monocyte aggregates levels were unchanged relative to control.35,36 The drop in circulating platelet-monocyte aggregates 3 days after insult could be due to aggregates invading the damaged tissue site, thus not presenting in the blood. Positron emission tomography scanning in patients after MI shows increased innate immune cell infiltration to the myocardium.37 Post-MI complications have been correlated with elevated circulating activated platelets, and platelet-derived extracellular vesicles.38 Moreover, increased blood platelet-monocyte aggregates are associated with poorer cardiac efficiency, measured by left ventricular ejection fraction.39

Interpreting platelet-leukocyte aggregates in disease states is complex. Platelet-monocyte aggregates serve as an effective marker of platelet activation in states of disease. Furthermore, they aid in uncovering the role of thromboinflammation in tissue damage and pathology. However, there is limited mechanistic evidence to show that platelet-leukocyte aggregates are causative or deleterious to disease progression. We suggest that the presence of platelet-leukocyte aggregates in disease states may, in part, be associated with the tissue repair process. In other words, thromboinflammation and thromborepair may be overlapping activities and occur concurrently. The presence of platelet-leukocyte aggregates during pathology should not, exclusively, be treated as deleterious.

Why do we need leukocytes in inflamed tissue?

Platelet-leukocyte aggregates are present in the blood and within tissues after organ damage. This, among other inflammatory responses, exists to restore physiological balance in times of infection or tissue distress.40 For example, in the postinfarcted heart, inflammatory potentiators are released from damaged and necrotic cells including danger-associated molecular patterns, alarmins (eg, high mobility group box 11), and other proinflammatory molecules (eg, heat shock proteins).41,42 Leukocytes will invade inflamed tissues, from the peripheral blood.43 Many signals initiate this process, including the expression of adhesins on activated endothelial cells44 and chemoattractants (eg, interleukin-8 [IL-8]).45 Neutrophils are the first responders in tissue repair. They phagocytose necrotic cells, which allows remodeling, deposition of nascent cells, neovascularization, and prevents uncontrolled inflammation.46,47 At later stages, neutrophils also engulf other apoptotic neutrophils and subsequently release anti-inflammatory molecules, to initiate inflammatory resolution,48 a key aspect of tissue repair, because uncontrolled and sustained inflammation is unconducive to efficient healing. Replacing and remodeling the damaged extracellular matrix (ECM) is another crucial aspect of repair, in various tissues. In cardiac tissue, neutrophils release matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9) to degrade the existing ECM.49 This facilitates the ECM resynthesis required to rescue cardiac contractility and stability. Furthermore, MMP-9 causes the release of VEGF (from resident pancreatic islet cells), promoting angiogenesis.50 This effect has been observed in several cell types.51,52

Monocytes and macrophages also extravasate into inflamed tissue in a temporal manner, based on subtype.53 Proinflammatory monocytes/macrophages precede the appearance of their anti-inflammatory counterparts.53,54 Proinflammatory monocytes (Ly-6Chigh) secrete proinflammatory cytokines to amplify the inflammatory response.55 These monocytes and macrophages cooperate with neutrophils to clear debris and necrotic cells.54,55

The acute inflammatory response evoked by leukocyte infiltration is, therefore, essential in tissue repair. Conflicting evidence exists on the outcomes associated with leukocytes residing in damaged tissue. Some indicate that platelet-leukocyte aggregation accumulation in inflamed tissue worsens the conditions,56 whereas other groups show lacking leukocytes at the damage site in the acute phase is more damaging.57, 58, 59 As with much of physiology, a balanced response is likely key; platelets seem to play a pivotal role in this balance.

How leukocytes enter inflamed tissue and why platelets are important

Leukocytes invading tissues from the blood vessel lumen is well documented.60 Inflamed, infected, or damaged tissue will accumulate cytokines, which activate endothelial cells, lining blood vessels, to initiate leukocyte extravasation.40,60, 61, 62 Flowing leukocytes will begin interacting with activated endothelial cells via weak and transient interactions; namely, leukocyte PSGL-1 binds E-selectin63 and P-selectin64 on endothelial cells. Once localized near the endothelium, stronger cell-cell interactions form to decrease leukocyte velocity and firmly adhere them to the vessel wall. Many of these firmer interactions are integrin-mediated (eg, lymphocyte function-associated antigen 1 [αLβ2] binding to intercellular adhesion molecule 165 and very late antigen 4 [α4β1] binding to vascular cell adhesion molecule 166). Once immobilized and activated, leukocytes begin to extravasate from the blood, into the subendothelial tissue.62 The postcapillary venules are the preferred site of exit.67,68 To find the optimal location for exit, leukocytes crawl across the endothelium, with the aid of Mac-1 before initiating diapedesis.69 Extravasation can be paracellular (between endothelial cells) or transcellular (through endothelial cell pores).62 The more common, paracellular route uses several integrin interactions at endothelial cell tight junctions.70,71 Once past the endothelial cell monolayer, leukocytes bypass the basement membrane and pericyte sheath to enter the tissue.72,73

Recently, the fundamental role of platelets in leukocyte extravasation has been shown. Platelets binding to leukocytes, forming platelet-leukocyte aggregates, is crucial to the process. Zuchtriegel et al demonstrated that platelets localize leukocytes to efficient exits sites, in vivo, and that platelet depleted mice exhibited inefficient transmigration of neutrophils and proinflammatory monocytes.74 They suggested that platelets localize to “hot spots” optimal for extravasation via VWF and αIIbβ3. VWF is densely expressed at endothelial cell junctions, the ideal location for paracellular migration. Once bound, platelets capture neutrophils via P-selectin and CD40L binding PSGL-1 and CD40, respectively. The CD40L-CD40 interaction enables firm platelet-neutrophil binding. Furthermore, the platelet-bound neutrophils enter an elevated state of activation, likely via the P-selectin–PSGL-1 interaction.75 The resultant high-affinity conformation of β2 integrins induced on neutrophils facilitates tight-junction interactions to pull neutrophils through the monolayer. Early invading monocytes exhibit similar traits. Platelet-neutrophil aggregates are also able to recruit monocytes cooperatively. Petri et al inhibited neutrophil tissue recruitment by blocking either endothelial VWF or platelet GPIb.76 They also suggested that endothelium-bound platelets contribute to increasing vascular permeability, allowing easier extravasation; platelet-activating factor secreted from platelets has been shown to open endothelial tight junctions.77

Neutrophils attached to the blood vessel wall form a bifunctional phenotype initiated by the extension of a protrusion further into the vessel lumen scanning for activated platelets.78 These extensions are rich in PSGL-1 and capture flowing platelets expressing surface P-selectin. This interaction serves to “kick-start” the migration process in neutrophils by initiating trafficking of Mac-1 and CXCR2, 2 receptors key in paracellular migration. Other studies report similar findings, supporting the role of platelets in aiding leukocyte extravasation via the P-selectin–PSGL-1 axis.79 Many of these studies are performed in inflammatory models in the mouse cremaster muscle,80 but similar results have also been shown in an ischemia/reperfusion model of MI.81

Interestingly, platelets may also be able to negatively modulate leukocyte infiltration. Thrombin-mediated activation of platelets facilitates leukocyte recruitment; however, platelet surface GPIbα sequesters thrombin and negatively modulates this response.82

Platelets coextravasate with leukocytes

Although platelets are critical for leukocyte extravasation, the opposite is also true. Platelets enter tissues at sites of damage and do so by “piggybacking” on extravasating leukocytes to invade inflamed tissue, to further contribute to thromborepair. Platelets, bound to CD86+ macrophages have been found within cardiac tissue after ischemia/reperfusion models of MI.83 This is also seen with platelets bound to neutrophils in models of inflammatory bowel disease.84 Further infection and inflammatory models show similar platelet transmigration effects.85,86 However, platelets unbound to leukocytes struggle to extravasate. This highlights the role platelets play in the acute phase of inflammation and subsequent tissue repair.

Platelet dysfunction and inflammation

Given the important role that platelets play in inflammation, it can be expected that alterations in platelet function would cause disordered inflammation. A mouse model of Gray platelet syndrome (lacking or reduced α-granule number or content) demonstrated cerebral protection in an ischemic stroke model.87 It is speculated that this benefit is due to a decreased thromboinflammatory response, although analysis of platelet-leukocyte interactions or other inflammatory markers was not conducted.87 Furthermore, patients treated with the P2Y12 blocker clopidogrel showed decreased platelet-leukocyte aggregate formation in vivo and ex vivo.88 However, patients receiving aspirin, alone, had normal platelet-neutrophil aggregates and platelet-monocyte aggregates.89,90

Interestingly, platelet disorders do not always cause a decrease in the inflammatory response, highlighting the complexity of the interplay between platelets and the immune system. For example, platelets from patients with Glanzmann thrombasthenia, with dysfunctional or absent integrin αIIbβ3, show increased platelet-leukocyte binding under flow.91

Platelets influencing tissue repair

We have discussed how platelets, via their interactions with leukocytes, can migrate into tissue after damage. Once localized, platelets can influence several aspects of tissue repair via a variety of mechanisms. To best understand this, knowledge of general tissue repair processes is required (Figure 1).

The phases of tissue repair. After tissue damage, 3 phases of repair ensue. In the early acute inflammatory phase, necrotic cells and damaged ECM fragments are phagocytosed and cleared by innate immune cells. The second phase involves repair and proliferation. The acute inflammatory response is dampened and resolved. Innate immune cells undergo apoptosis and proinflammatory macrophages (M1) phenotypically switch to anti-inflammatory subtypes (M2). Fibroblasts proliferate and transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts, which initiate fibrosis and ECM deposition. Neovascularization of the damaged tissue occurs in the form of angiogenesis. The third, and final, phase is maturation (and remodeling). The novel scar formed becomes more stable via ECM protein cross-linking. The scar is remodeled and modulated by resident immune cells. Immediately after injury, a massive acute inflammatory response ensues, drawing immune cells into the damage site (described above). This acute inflammatory phase clears necrotic cells and damaged ECM to create an environment suitable for repair to begin. Acute inflammation is resolved by several cell types, including neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and, notably, platelets.92,93

After inflammation, comes the “reparative and proliferative” phase, sometimes referred to as “inflammatory resolution and cell proliferation.”92 The inflammatory response is dampened and resolved, preventing excess destruction.94 Fibroblasts are activated, expand and transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts. Myofibroblasts are contractile cells, providing several benefits. For example, in skin wounds they promote closure,95 and in damaged cardiac tissue they stabilize chamber walls, preventing rupture while maintaining a degree of flexibility for muscular contraction.96 Furthermore, myofibroblasts secrete new ECM proteins to replace those lost in the insult, stabilizing tissue further.97 This fibrotic response is key in tissue repair, but if it is not halted in a timely manner, pathological fibrosis ensues; overdeposition of ECM occurs, tissue architecture is compromised, and organ function impaired. Another key repair mechanism that follows inflammation is neovascularization, formation of new blood vessels to restore blood flow to the damage site.98 This is particularly key in ischemic organ damage (eg, cerebral stroke or MI).

The final stage of tissue repair is “maturation” (or “remodeling”). Here, the ECM becomes cross-linked and stabilizes its structure. It is also trimmed and modulated by macrophages to remove excess matrix, creating an organized structure.98 Recruited cells from the proliferation and repair phases may undergo apoptosis and the wound develops into its final form over the course of months and years.92,99 Pathology arises when the first 2 phases of tissue repair become unregulated and do not resolve. This is discussed in further detail below.

A timely and well-regulated inflammatory response is essential for tissue repair. In earlier sections, we discussed the role of platelets promoting inflammation by drawing leukocytes into inflamed tissue to clear postdamage debris to facilitate proper healing. Beyond this, platelets secrete proinflammatory mediators from α-granules, including IL-1β, monocyte chemotactic protein 1, and macrophage inflammatory protein 1α,100 to potentiate the required inflammatory response. These cytokines also promote smooth muscle cell migration in culture, a key aspect of tissue repair.101

Platelets are also integral to inflammatory resolution, an essential aspect of repair. Migrated platelets release transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) to dampen the inflammatory response after lung injury, improving healing.102 Platelet depletion increases proinflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor α and IL-6 levels.103 Platelets secrete a group of molecules classed as specialized proresolving mediators, which drive inflammatory resolution,93 for example, lipoxins and annexin-1.104 These inhibit leukocyte infiltration and initiate apoptosis of proinflammatory cells, starting the positive feedback loop of inflammatory resolution.105 Maresin 1 from platelets promotes resolution by reducing platelet-leukocyte aggregate invasion106 and by inhibiting secretion of proinflammatory mediators from nearby platelets.107 Deletion of C-type lectin-like receptor 2 from platelets induces more severe organ damage, in a septic model of inflammation. It is believed this is due to decreased inhibition of leukocyte infiltration.108 Furthermore, the formation of platelet–T-regulatory cell aggregates during later stages of inflammation promotes resolution by facilitating T-regulatory cell extravasation and subsequent release of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β, which drives the differentiation of macrophages to an anti-inflammatory phenotype.109 Activated platelets can also promote this differentiation via prostaglandin E2 signaling.110,111

Platelet-leukocyte interactions and platelet-derived cytokines are pivotal in both expanding the inflammatory response and in resolving it.110,112,113 Without resolution, inflammation becomes chronic and attenuates tissue repair.94 A nuanced understanding of this time-sensitive shift from inflammation to resolution is required to aid in designing therapeutics that can optimize this process for tissue repair.

Fibrosis

In most cases of tissue injury, fibrosis follows as a part of the repair process. The process is designed to stabilize unstable tissue and synthesize ECM to replace that lost during insult. However, as with inflammation, uncontrolled fibrosis is pathological.96,114 In the case of cardiac fibrosis, after MI, fibroblasts initially work to prevent chamber wall rupture, but pathological fibrosis leads to a dilated and distended heart unable to efficiently pump blood.96

Platelets contain several fibroblast-modulating cytokines.115 Platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and TGF-β both draw fibroblasts to wound sites and promote collagen synthesis.116,117 These 2 factors contribute to, but are not solely responsible for, the activation and contraction of lung fibroblasts in vitro. PDGF and TGF-β blockade dampens, but does not ablate, fibroblast contraction, suggesting other unidentified fibroblast activating platelet-derived factors.118 Lysophosphatidic acid, released from platelets, exhibits the same effects.119 The contraction of fibroblasts to close the wound is a key aspect of repair but excessive contraction can hinder healing.120 PDGF-D also modulates fibroblast proliferation, myofibroblast differentiation, and subsequent collagen secretion. PDGF-D works synergistically with TGF-β1 to boost the fibrotic response.117 Circulating TGF-β1, released from platelets, has also been shown to positively modulate cardiac fibrosis in a murine blood pressure overload model.121 5-Hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) promotes fibrosis, in tandem with TGF-β1, by driving fibroblast migration to the site of injury, ensuring the most unstable aspects of a wound are stabilized.122,123 Dense granule–released 5-HT also initiates fibroblast-myofibroblast differentiation.122 Myofibroblasts deposit ECM proteins, including collagens, fibronectins, and elastins.124 Platelet rich plasma have been shown to promote surface wound healing in vivo.125 Fibrosis-driven healing was improved compared with platelet poor plasma, indicating the broad scope role of the platelets and their internal cargo.

After the acute fibrotic response, platelets are known to modulate the scar in several ways, aiding in long-term healing. Controlled destruction of cells is key in fibrotic remodeling, particularly in late-stage healing to prevent an overly fibrotic response. Platelets induce apoptosis of mouse embryonic fibroblasts through activation-dependent surface expression of Fas ligand.126 Postinjury apoptosis is heavily dampened in thrombocytopenic mice.126 However, platelets also exhibit antiapoptotic properties toward fibroblasts, highlighting their ability to fine tune the fibrotic scar.127 Interestingly, platelets can induce apoptosis in other cell types besides fibroblasts, including brain microvascular endothelial cells,128 dendritic cells,129 splenocytes, and lung-resident cells.130

Once myofibroblasts have laid a new ECM, platelets can trim this ECM into its optimal form. Platelets secrete MMP-1,131 MMP-2,132 MMP-3, and MMP-14133 to cleave excess ECM. Platelets also release inhibitors of MMPs (tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases) to regulate this process.133 Furthermore, platelet interactions with innate immune cells localize and promote leukocyte MMP activity to damage sites.134 Another key regulator of fibrosis is α-granule–secreted thrombospondin-1. This ECM protein is heavily studied in renal fibrosis and is known to positively and negatively regulate fibrosis (eg, by activation of latent TGF-β into its functional form, and reorganization of the collagen matrix).135

Platelet-derived factors can also negatively modulate the fibrotic response.136,137 Platelet-released hepatocyte growth factor suppresses liver fibrosis, in vivo, which decreases TGF-β concentrations and increases MMP-9 levels.138,139 Because of the complex and time-dependent nature of fibrosis, the weighting and temporal release of profibrotic and antifibrotic content from invaded platelets is likely important in balancing the fibrotic response. Furthermore, the fibrotic role of platelets may differ in different disease states. For example, platelets appear to be profibrotic in cardiac fibrosis140 but antifibrotic in liver fibrosis139 A deeper understanding of this could present opportunities to improve tissue repair, by modulating fibrosis.

Angiogenesis

Another key aspect of restoring damaged tissue is the formation of new blood vessels to restore blood flow (angiogenesis). This is particularly important in ischemic damage.141 Platelets contain >20 factors that regulate neovascularization.142 Some released factors will promote initial sprouting of new vessels, whereas others will consolidate and stabilize newly formed vessels. VEGF acts on the early, new sprouting stage of angiogenesis and is often considered the crucial initiator of angiogenesis.143,144 Release of VEGF from platelet granules145 has been shown to promote angiogenesis in vitro146 and in vivo.3,147,148 Fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2;basic FGF) also induces vessel formation.143 Endothelial cell growth factor,149 sphingosine-1-phosphate,150 and platelet-derived endothelial cell growth factor151 are also platelet-derived factors with proangiogenic effects. Sphingosine-1-phosphate and platelet-derived endothelial cell growth factor are strong chemoattractants that facilitate the migration of endothelial cells to damage sites in vitro and in vivo.150 Platelet-secreted MMPs work in tandem with these chemotactic factors by degrading aspects of the ECM and basement membrane, clearing a path for endothelial cell migration.152

After new vessels have sprouted, vessel maturation must ensue. PDGF153 and angiopoietin-1,154 found in platelets, promote vessel stabilization, ensuring damaged tissues remain vascularized. Interestingly, resting platelets can also induce angiogenesis, after diabetic injury.155 Thrombocytopenic mice produce fewer new vessels and those that are produced are more fragile.138

Platelets also release negative modulators of angiogenesis, for example, thrombospondin-1156 and PF4,157 which prevent vessel stabilization, and endostatin, which inhibits endothelial cell proliferation and migration.158

However, reports suggest that the proangiogenic effects overpower the antiangiogenic.146,159 Some studies propose that platelets contain distinct granule subtypes and can secrete differential α-granule content upon activation by different agonists, including specific release of proangiogenic or antiangiogenic factors.145,160 α-Granule content has also been shown to polarize into distinct subgranular locations.161 This is a highly debated topic, which requires further research. Overall, platelet releasate positively modulates tissue revascularization. This is supported by thrombocytopenic murine models exhibiting decreased angiogenesis.138

Other tissue repair responses

The death of tissue resident cells after injury will heavily affect organ or tissue function and is the major cause of clinical manifestations. For example, loss of cardiomyocytes after MI will reduce the muscular capacity of the heart to pump blood around the heart.162 Several studies indicate that platelets are cardioprotective, especially in ischemic contexts. In vitro models of cardiac ischemia show platelet-derived stromal cell–derived factor 1α and TGF-β1 improves cardiomyocyte survival in a hypoxic enviroment.163 Further to this, sequestering reactive oxygen species, via the glutathione redox cycle in platelets, is cardioprotective in isolated rat hearts.164 Induction of vasodilation, from platelet-released factors, is also associated with improved cardiac survival in hypoxia.165 Other nonclassical tissue repair responses are promoted by platelet granule content, including FGF, TGF-β, and VEGF.166, 167, 168, 169, 170, 171 The release of microRNA172, 173, 174, 175, 176 and microparticles177, 178, 179 have also been implicated in the tissue repair responses described earlier. Detailed discussion of this is beyond the scope of this review.

Platelets also promote organ regeneration, and this is exemplified in the liver. Patients with chronic liver disease are frequently thrombocytopenic due to decreased liver secretion of the platelet-producing cytokine, thrombopoietin,180 and increased clearance in the enlarged spleen.181 This may be inhibiting organ regeneration. 5-HT secreted from platelets is crucial in liver regeneration, driving hepatocyte and fibroblast proliferation.182,183 Chronic liver disease often presents with liver fibrosis due to uncontrolled ECM deposition.184 Platelets also promote liver regeneration via physical interactions with resident cells and secretion of other bioactive molecules.184 Increasing platelet counts in chronic liver disease is an attractive therapeutic concept, which has shown early promise.185,186 This is another striking example of thromborepair.

Recruitment of progenitor cells is key to recellularizing and reestablishing function in tissues after injury. Platelets and their content have been shown to recruit bone marrow–resident progenitor cells,187 smooth muscle cell progenitors,188 and cardiac stem cells,189,190 among others.144 For example, platelet-secreted stromal cell–derived factor 1 and physical interactions, via the P-selectin/PSGL-1 axis, recruit bone marrow progenitors to sites of vascular damage to repair the vessel wall.187 In contrast, platelet expression of high mobility group box 1 can attenuate mesenchymal stem cells recruitment,191 demonstrating a complex and potentially dual role for platelets in controlling progenitor cell recruitment. Increased recruitment of progenitor cells to damage sites, via pharmacological intervention, could improve tissue healing, and this may be crucial for repair of damage to poorly regenerating tissue, such as the heart.192

Platelet rich plasma is of therapeutic interest for tissue repair. Its benefits are being explored in several disciplines, including dermatological, orthopedic, dental, and ophthalmological settings.171 Many platelet-derived bioactive compounds in platelet rich plasma are reparative (eg, VEGF, TGF-β, and FGF2).193 Platelet rich plasma provides another exciting avenue for tissue repair, in the clinic.

Platelet disorders and tissue repair

We have previously discussed how platelet dysfunction alters the inflammatory response. Given their importance in healing, platelet disorders can also affect tissue repair.

Decreased TGF-β secretion from platelets lacking α-granules (Gray platelet syndrome) decreases skin wound healing, in mice.87 Myofibroblast differentiation was attenuated, preventing scar formation. Interestingly, patients with Gray platelet syndrome exhibit bone marrow fibrosis (myelofibrosis).194,195 The loss of regular clot formation and retraction in platelet disorders will also negatively influence wound healing. Angiogenesis and ECM deposition and secretion of prorepair factors require the formation of a stable clot at a site of injury.196

Conclusions

In this review, we propose a novel term to describe the role of platelets in tissue repair: thromborepair. This encapsulates several platelet functions, including leukocyte infiltration to damage sites, promoting and resolving the inflammatory response, and directly modulating tissue repair responses (eg, fibrosis, angiogenesis, and cellular regeneration). Pivotal to thromborepair is the ability of platelets to invade damaged tissue, with the aid of leukocytes. Thromborepair is schematically summarized in Figure 2.

Manipulating thromborepair offers exciting therapeutic potential; this may take many forms, for example, loading or labeling platelets with treatments for targeted delivery. This approach has already been used for cancer therapeutics, and thus should be considered for organ damage.197 The inherent targeting ability of platelets could also be enhanced, through the use of targeting peptides.198
