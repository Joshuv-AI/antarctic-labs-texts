Genetic resource optimization: a core collection of tetraploid wheat from 2,254 NBRP-wheat accessions
Authors: Zhuo Su, Shotaro Takenaka, Michiko Kakitani, Miyuki Nitta, Yasir S A Gorafi, Shuhei Nasuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTetraploid wheat, comprising both Emmer wheat (Triticum turgidum, AABB genome) and Timopheevii wheat (T. timopheevii, AAGG genome), harbors rich genetic diversity that remains underutilized in modern breeding. The National Bioresource Project-Wheat (NBRP-Wheat) conserves over 2,000 wild and landrace accessions of tetraploid wheat. To enhance the accessibility and application of these resources, we aimed to develop a representative core collection and explore its potential in genetic analysis and breeding.ResultsWe first assessed the genetic diversity of all tetraploid wheat accessions in the NBRP-Wheat gene bank using Diversity Arrays Technology (DArT), and selected 179 representative accessions to establish a core collection. This core set and T. durum cv. Kronos were further genotyped using genotyping by random amplicon sequencing-direct (GRAS-Di). Population structure and diversity analyses revealed clear genetic differentiation among Timopheevii, wild emmer, and domesticated emmer wheat. A significant reduction in genetic diversity was observed in domesticated emmer compared to its wild counterpart. Selective sweep analysis identified several differentiated loci between wild and free-threshing emmer, some of which correspond to known domestication-related genes. Additionally, k-mer-based genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified both known and novel loci associated with important agronomic traits, including glume hairiness, anthocyanin pigmentation in cotyledons and coleoptiles, hundred-grain weight, and culm length.ConclusionsOur study provides a valuable core collection that captures the genetic diversity of tetraploid wheat conserved in NBRP-Wheat. The identified genomic regions and trait-associated loci demonstrate the utility of this core set for trait dissection and highlight its potential in future wheat improvement programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07944-2.Keywords: Tetraploid wheat, K-mer GWAS, Core collection, Domestication, Genetic diversity

Background

Unlike maize and rice, domesticated wheat comprises two groups of species that differ in ploidy. About 95% of the world’s wheat yield is from hexaploid bread wheat (T. aestivum L., genomes AABBDD), and the remaining yield is from tetraploid durum wheat (T. durum Desf., genomes AABB) [1, 2]. Hexaploid wheat originated from natural hybridization between domesticated tetraploid wheat and a wild goat grass Aegilops tauschii Coss. (genomes DD) [3, 4]. This cross probably occurred in the south or west of the Caspian Sea, approximately 8,000–11,000 years ago, when tetraploid wheat cultivation had expanded from the Fertile Crescent to the natural habitat of Ae. tauschii [5–8]. Bread wheat has limited genetic diversity due to two bottleneck events in its evolutionary history: the domestication bottleneck at the tetraploid level and the polyploidy bottleneck during the polyploidization from tetraploid to hexaploid [9].

Therefore, wild species and landraces in the genera Triticum and Aegilops can provide a large, promising gene pool for bread wheat breeding. For example, to exploit the gene pool of the D genome of Ae. tauschii, synthetic hexaploid wheat lines have been created between various tetraploid and Ae. tauschii lines [10, 11]. The demand is also present in expanding the genetic diversity of the A and B genomes for future wheat breeding.

Tetraploid wheat (T. dicoccum and T. timopheevii) is one of the oldest crops in human history, and its wild species (T. dicoccides and T. araraticum) exhibit greater genetic diversity than the domesticated species [12]. National Bioresource Project-Wheat (NBRP-Wheat; https://shigen.nig.ac.jp/wheat/komugi) maintains more than 2,000 tetraploid wheat accessions. These accessions were collected in their natural habitats during expeditions led by the late Dr. H. Kihara and his colleagues [13–16]. To optimize the utility of genetic resources, the genetic diversity conserved in a germplasm collection should be quantified. Unfortunately, information on genetic diversity at the DNA sequence level is scarce for the tetraploid collection at NBRP-Wheat. Therefore, the objective of this study is first to perform skim genotyping on 2253 tetraploid accessions collected in the NBRP-Wheat resource bank to assess their population structure and genetic diversity. Second, to establish a core collection of NBRP-Wheat tetraploid based on their genetic diversity and collection information. Third, to demonstrate the utility of the core collection by investigating several traits and detecting marker-trait associations (MTAs) through genome-wide association studies (GWAS).

Materials and methods

Plant materials

We utilized 2,254 tetraploid wheat accessions preserved in the NBRP-Wheat, comprising 1,872 accessions of emmer wheat (AABB genome) and 382 of Timopheevii wheat (AAGG genome). The AABB tetraploid wheat accessions encompass three groups with varying levels of domestication and include 11 subspecies. The AAGG tetraploid wheat accessions consist of two subspecies, each with distinct domestication statuses. These 2,254 accessions were collected from seven geographic regions across 53 countries and can be divided into seven regions: 39 from East and South Asia (ESA), 817 from West Asia (WA), 323 from Eurasian cross region (ECR), 245 from Europe (EU), 745 from Ethiopia (ETH), 24 from Africa (AF), four from North America (NAM), and 58 accessions have no records (Table S1). Their ploidy was confirmed by cytological observation of the chromosomes in root tip cells. In this paper, we follow the nomenclature used in the catalog of NBRP-Wheat, which is available on the webpage at https://shigen.nig.ac.jp/wheat/komugi/.

Phenotyping

The accessions were evaluated in the experimental field of Kyoto University during the wheat-growing seasons of 2014 and 2015. Three plants from each accession were grown in randomized plots. The distance between plants was 18 cm on a 30 cm ridge (the width between ridges was set to 130 cm). We scored eight phenotypic traits: namely, culm length (CL), measured from the root node to the tip of the spike; hundred-grain weight (HGW), determined by weighing 100 randomly selected seeds; tiller number (TN), counted as the total number of tillers per plant; anthocyanin pigmentation in cotyledons (AC) and coleoptiles (ACS); glume hairiness (GH); and waxiness on the ochrea (OW) and culms (CW). The latter four qualitative traits were visually scored using a three-grade scale: 0 (absent), 0.5 (weakly present), and 1 (strongly present). The measurements were conducted at specific growth stages: AC and ACS were observed at 5 days after germination; OW, CW and GH were assessed at the flowering stage; and CL, HGW, and TN were measured after harvest. To ensure scoring consistency, all discrete trait evaluations were performed by the same individual. For each accession, all traits were evaluated in three individual plants, and quantitative traits were expressed as the mean value.

Genotyping

Total DNA was extracted from the second leaves of individual plants using DNeasy Plant Maxi Kit (Qiagen, Tokyo, Japan) and quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer 2000. Genotyping was conducted using the Diversity Arrays Technology (DArT) platform (Diversity Arrays Technology Pty. Ltd., Yarralumla, Australia) [17, 18]. A total of 300 DArT markers were tested for all NBRP-Wheat tetraploid wheat collection accessions. Based on genotype data and passport information, we selected an NBRP-Wheat tetraploid wheat core collection including 160 AABB and 20 AAGG accessions. Then, the DNA of core collection samples was sequenced by GRAS-Di technology [24–26]. The NGS short reads were first trimmed by trimmomatic (ver. 0.39) and then mapped to the reference genome of a durum wheat cultivar Svevo [19] by bowtie2 (ver. 2.4.1). SNPs were called by bcftools (ver. 1.10.2). Low-quality SNPs with GQ or DP less than 10 were removed from the following analyses. After QC and filtering SNPs with a missing rate larger than 20%, 97,545 markers were obtained.

Due to the difference in genome constitutions between AABB and AAGG species, wide genetic diversity, and presence of two chromosome-level reference sequences, we discriminated the SNP markers into two groups: (1) Markers for all tetraploid wheat; due to the genomic differences between AABB and AAGG species, most unfiltered SNPs were missing in the AAGG group. Therefore, we first selected 35,302 markers with a missing rate of less than 20% in the AAGG species. These markers were then tested across the entire core collection, and those with a missing rate above 20% or a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 5% were excluded. This step resulted in 10,658 high-quality markers shared across all accessions. (2) Markers for AABB species: In 160 AABB accessions, we applied the same filtering thresholds (missing rate < 20%, MAF ≥ 5%) and retained 27,618 high-quality markers.

Population structure analysis

For the whole tetraploid wheat collection (2254 accessions) that was genotyped with 300 DArT markers (present allele (1) or absence allele (0)), we calculated the Jaccard’s distances [20] among accessions by R package vegan [21]. The neighbor-joining (NJ) tree was constructed in R using the ape package [30] based on the distance matrix.

For the core collection (179 accessions and T. durum cv. Kronos), we performed phylogenetic analyses using the different marker sets mentioned above with the RAxML-ng package [22]. A maximum likelihood tree was built with the general time reversible model (GTR + G). A 100 random or parsimony trees were built to search for the best tree with bootstrap probabilities by resampling until the cutoff value converged to 97%. An R script was used to generate an iTOL-compatible (https://itol.embl.de/) tree for rendering and annotation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in TASSEL 5 (2007), where missing data were imputed to the mean score for each site, as the default setting of TASSEL 5. Bayesian clustering, implemented in STRUCTURE v2.3.4 (Pritchard et al., 2000), was used to investigate the population structure of the 2254 accessions and the core collection, respectively.

Genome-wide association analysis (GWAS)

We used k-mer-based GWAS to identify the MTA because it enables a global association scan of short reads without relying on prior variant information from a reference genome. Compared to conventional SNP-based GWAS, this method is well established for higher sensitivity in detecting structural variations (SVs), copy number variations (CNVs), and rare alleles in complex polyploid genomes such as tetraploid wheat [23–25]. First, the raw reads of GRAS-Di were used to generate k-mers and develop a k-mer matrix as described in https://github.com/steuernb/AgRenSeq [26]. K-mers (k = 51) were counted in trimmed raw data per accession using Jellyfish v2.3.0 [27]. K-mers with a count of less than two in an accession were removed. Then, k-mer counts from all accessions were integrated to create a presence/absence matrix with one row per k-mer and one column per accession. Monomorphic K-mers were removed during the matrix construction. Then, the matrix was transformed to a PED format by a local bash script and to a tped format by PLINK v1.90 [28]. The GWAS was performed using the EMMAX algorithm [29], with an identity-by-state (IBS) K matrix. After performing the GWAS, k-mers with a -log10(P) value larger than 4 were selected and mapped to Svevo RefSeq v1.0 using blastn to determine the k-mer position on Svevo RefSeq v1.0. Some k-mers were mapped to multiple positions, but some were uniquely mapped. To select k-mer peaks and confirm their positions, we retained uniquely mapped k-mers for downstream analysis.

We calculated the average nucleotide diversity value (Pi) for each SNP locus in the core collection comprising a total of 97,545 markers using TASSEL. The fixation index (Fst) between each group was calculated by VCFtools software [30].

Candidate gene identification

To identify candidate genes, we extracted annotated genes within the 1 Mb physical distance of significant markers using the Svevo reference genome v1.0 [19]. For known loci or genes, we mapped their original sequences or positions to the Svevo RefSeq v1.0 using blastn to determine the gene position on Svevo RefSeq v1.0. For novel MTA regions, we downloaded the CDS sequences, and predicted the functions of these putative candidate genes by comparing their coding sequences (CDS) against the eggNOG database [31] using eggNOG-mapper v2.

Results

The geographic distribution, subspecies constitution, and population structure of the 2,254 tetraploid wheat accessions

We evaluated the 2,254 tetraploid wheat accessions from three perspectives: subspecies classification, geographic origin, and population genetic structure based on 300 DArT markers. According to subspecies information, the 2,254 accessions were classified into 13 subspecies: 373 wild emmer wheat (T. dicoccoides Koern.) and 373 wild Timopheevii wheat (T. araraticum Jakubz.), 167 domesticated hulled emmer wheat (T. dicoccum Schuebl., T. paleocolchicum Men. and T. ispahanicum Heslot), 1,332 domesticated free-threshing (FT) emmer wheat (T. durum Desf., T. turgidum L., T. polonicum L., T. carthlicum Nevski, T. abyssinicum Vav., T. orientale Perc. and T. pyramidale (Del.) Perc.), and nine accessions of domesticated Timopheevii wheat (T. timopheevii Zhuk.) (Table 1). All wild emmer wheat and most wild Timopheevii wheat (360/373) were collected from WA and ECR region, about one-third of the accessions were collected from ETH (745/2254), all of them are FT emmer (670/745) and hulled emmer (75/745) (Table 1).

Core collection establishment and genotyping

We selected 179 accessions as core collection lines based on the phylogenetic tree. In addition to these 179 lines, we included one cultivar, T. durum cv. Kronos as a standard reference for modern durum wheat (Table S2). In total, 180 tetraploid wheat lines were selected as the NBRP-Wheat tetraploid wheat core collection lines. This core collection comprises 160 Emmer and 20 Timopheevii accessions, representing all 13 subspecies and covering all seven geographic regions of origin (Table 1). Moreover, it includes accessions from all nine subpopulations identified in the population structure analysis (Table 2).

The genetic diversity of core collection lines was highlighted in the NJ tree (Fig. 1d) and visualized using a three-dimensional PCA plot (Fig. 2a-c). To assess how well the core collection represents the diversity of the entire set of 2,254 accessions, we compared several diversity indices: heterozygosity (HE), effective number of alleles (EA), minor allele frequencies (MAF), and Shannon index (H’). These values were nearly identical between the entire population and the core collection (P > 0.05; Fig. 2d), indicating that the core collection effectively captured the genetic diversity of the entire population, as determined by DArT markers.

Genotyping of the core collection via the GRAS-Di method yielded raw markers that were stringently filtered. We removed low-quality SNPs (GQ or DP < 10) and those with a missing rate > 20%, resulting in 97,545 high-quality markers for downstream analysis. In total, 10,658 markers were shared by both AAGG (20 accessions) and AABB (160 accessions) groups. Distinctions in genetic composition were observed between the two genome groups: 9,098 and 4,296 markers were monomorphic within the AAGG and AABB groups, respectively. Additionally, 3,141 markers were polymorphic across the whole dataset but monomorphic within both genome groups. Among 27,618 markers shared by the 160 AABB accessions (Fig. S3b), 4,884 and 3,395 markers were monomorphic within wild emmer (36 accessions) and domesticated emmer (124 accessions), respectively. Only 18 markers were polymorphic between wild emmer (36 accessions) and domesticated emmer, but monomorphic within both wild and domesticated emmer.

The genotype data from the two marker sets, i.e., 10,658 markers shared by AAGG and AABB accessions (hereafter referred to as AABB + AAGG markers) and 27,618 markers shared within AABB accessions (hereafter referred to as AABB markers) were used for subsequent analyses.

Population structure of the core collection

A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree constructed using the AABB + AAGG markers revealed a significant genetic difference between the AABB and AAGG groups (Fig. S2). The average substitution rate of alleles per locus between the two populations was 36.95%. Given the immense diversity between AABB and AAGG groups, we further analyzed the population structure of 160 AABB accessions, avoiding the influence of AAGG accessions by focusing on AABB markers. The Bayesian clustering analysis performed by STRUCTURE showed that the peak of ΔK was given at K = 2. Large ΔK values at K = 3 to K = 5 suggest that the 160 emmer accessions originated from five distinct lineages (Fig. 3a, b). The first three PCs explained about 35.1% of the genotypic variation of all accessions and divided emmer wheat into five clusters, which agrees with the results of our structure analysis (Fig. 3c). The phylogenetic tree also reflects the kinship distance of the 160 emmer wheat accessions, indicating that the accessions can be divided into five groups that represent their evolutionary history towards domestication: wild emmer, hulled emmer, and FT emmer (Fig. 3b). FT emmer wheat was further subdivided into three clusters, Ethiopian, European, and West Asia FT emmer wheats. Notably, a mixed genetic composition of the Ethiopian hulled emmer T. dicoccum was indicated by a closer view of the subpopulation.

Genetic diversity among tetraploid wheat subgroups

We compared the average nucleotide diversity (Pi) of different subpopulations of tetraploid wheat from the evolutionary viewpoint (Timopheevii, wild emmer, hulled emmer, and FT emmer) and from geographical viewpoint (ETH, EU, Asia and ECR) for only FT emmer because of the limited distribution of wild and hulled emmer (Tables 3 and 4). Wild emmer (n = 36) had the largest nucleotide diversity (Pi = 0.123). The genetic diversity of timopheevii wheat (n = 20) was the smallest (Pi = 0.041). With respect to their geographical origin, FT emmer exhibited similar diversity levels; the nucleotide diversities of FT emmer from ETH, EU, and WA were 0.042, 0.052, and 0.045, respectively (Table 4). The highest genetic differentiation in emmer wheat was found between wild and FT emmer (Fst = 0.154). Differentiation between hulled and FT emmer subgroups was also high (Fst = 0.145) (Table 3). Interestingly, Ethiopian free-threshing tetraploid wheat exhibited a high Fst value when compared with European and WA FT emmer (Fst = 0.173 and 0.172, respectively), despite their genetic diversities being at similar levels (Table 4).

We compared the genome-wide Fst values between wild and FT tetraploids by calculating Fst in every 200 kb window with a sliding method (step 50 kb). (Fig. S4). We computed the Fst values within a 2 Mb window of the known domestication or breeding-related loci in the reference genome of a tetraploid wheat cultivar Svevo as shown in Table S3. We found that there were high Fst blocks (Fst > 0.2) near the plant height locus Rth-A1, flowering time locus VRN-A1, grain weight loci TaGW2-B and TaTGW-7B, seed dormancy loci TaSdr-A1 and TaSdr-B1, and domestication locus Q-5B. We observed strong positive selection around the Rht-A1, TaGW2-B, TaTGW-7B, and Q-5B loci in the FT emmer group (n = 103, Tajima’s D < 0, Table S4), indicating the influence of domestication and human selection.

GWAS of agronomic traits through phenotyping the core collection

We investigated three quantitative traits (culm length (CL), hundred grains weight (HGW), tiller number (TN)) and five qualitative traits (anthocyanin pigmentation in cotyledon (AC) and in coleoptile (ACS), glume hairiness (GH) and waxiness on ochrea (OW) and culm (CW)) (Fig. 4, Table S5) in the core collection. We found marked differences in phenotypic data between wild and FT tetraploids. In terms of qualitative traits, wild emmer exhibited anthocyanin pigmentation in the cotyledon and coleoptile, lacked glume hairs and had reduced waxiness on the ochrea and culm (Chi-square test, p < 0.01 for all; Fig. 4a). For quantitative traits, wild emmer had a shorter culm, produced more tillers, and had lower grain weight compared to FT emmer (Student’s t-test, p < 0.01 for all; Fig. 4b). Across the entire population, HGW was positively correlated with CL (r = 0.52, p < 0.01) but negatively correlated with TN (r = -0.35, p < 0.01); CL and TN were also negatively correlated with each other (r = -0.24, p < 0.01) (Fig. 4c).

We conducted a k-mer-based genome-wide association analysis (GWAS) for these eight traits using the emmer wheat accessions. We identified 10 significant peaks for the different traits in the population, with phenotypic variance examined ranging from 15.5 to 54.3% (Table 5). For the traits AC and ACS, we detected a sharp and highly significant peak on chromosome 7A (Fig. 5a), which explained 34.91% of the phenotypic variance (Table 5). This peak overlapped with the previously reported TaC1-A1 locus (Fig. 5a and Table S6), which has been proposed as a strong candidate gene for the wheat Rc gene [32]. All individuals carrying the significant k-mer (allele “1”) exhibited pigmentation (Fig. 5a).

For the trait OW, we identified a peak on chromosome 2B (Fig. 5b), which overlapped with a previously reported QTL on 2B by [33]. The β-diketone pathway is a key biosynthetic route for major cuticular components in epidermal cells [34]. Studies on eceriferum (cer) mutants in barley have shown that β-diketone biosynthesis is controlled by three tightly linked genes (Cer-c, Cer-q, and Cer-u) [35], which are syntenic with the wheat W1 locus [36]. Both loci harbor genes encoding three types of enzymes: a chalcone synthase-like type III polyketide synthase (PKS), a putative lipase/carboxylesterase or hydrolase (HYD), and a cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (P450) [35]. Within the 2B peak region and the flanking 1 Mb window, we identified 10 cytochrome P450, six chalcone synthase, and three alpha/beta-hydrolase superfamily protein genes (Fig. 5b), indicating that this region is a high-confidence candidate for wax-related genes. All individuals lacking wax in the ochrea region did not carry the significant k-mer (i.e., they had allele “0”).

In GH, we detected two sharp and significant peaks on chromosome 1A and the unanchored chromosome (Unknown Chromosome) (Fig. 5c). These two regions may originate from the same genetic locus, as they exhibited similar significant p-values and a high level of linkage disequilibrium between them (Fig. S5). Both regions explained a large proportion of the phenotypic variance, accounting for 51% and 54%, respectively (Table 5). As previously reported, Hg-1 is located on chromosome 1AS [37]. Recently, TRITD0Uv1G104670, located on unknown chromosome (Svevo v1.0: Un:320,071,689–320,075,376) was identified as a candidate gene for glume pubescence [38], which maps to position 1 A:1,865,044–1,869,063 in the IWGSC RefSeq v2.1 assembly. Our results also suggest that incomplete assembly at the distal short arm of chromosome 1A may have hindered the cloning of Hg-1, as part of the relevant sequence appears to have been assigned to an unknown chromosome. Our k-mer GWAS identified two peaks with nearly identical significance on Unknown chromosome (Svevo v1.0: Un: 173,894,087–195,951,092) and chromosome 1A (Svevo v1.0: 1A: 73,064–3,493,315) (Fig. 5c), suggesting that Hg-1 is likely located near these regions. This location is different from the candidate gene TRITD0Uv1G104670 [38]. We compared the synteny between the two significant regions and identified a collinear region of approximately 1 Mb (Fig. 5c). While no annotated genes were found on chromosome 1A within this interval, the corresponding region on the Unknown chromosome (ChrUn:184–190 Mb) contained 14 annotated genes (Table S6). Among them, TRITD0Uv1G072110 and TRITD0Uv1G072200 are predicted to encode Exocyst complex component Exo70 family proteins (Table S7), which have been implicated in polarized cell growth, root hairs, pollen tubes, meristem activity, and trichome formation in plants [39]. These genes represent high-confidence candidates for GH.

In addition to the three major MTAs, we identified a novel peak on chromosome 3B associated with ACS (Fig. S6), explaining 19.11% of the phenotypic variation (Table S6). We also detected two peaks associated with CL on chromosomes 2B and 7A (Fig. S6), explaining 16.7% and 16.03% of the variation, respectively (Table S6). These loci do not overlap with any previously reported QTLs. They may represent novel loci specific to the tetraploid wheat population. Furthermore, for the trait HGW, we identified a peak on chromosome 7A (Fig. S6) that explained 21.28% of the phenotypic variation (Table S6) and is located near the known gene TaTGW-7A (Chr7A: 204,062,011) [40]. Another peak associated with HGW was detected on chromosome 6B (Fig. S6), explaining 14.69% of the variation and showing no overlap with any known loci (Table S6).

Discussion

Increasing the genetic diversity of cultivated wheat is one of the primary ways to incorporate desirable traits for wheat improvement. The diversity of cultivated tetraploid and hexaploid wheat has been reduced due to the bottleneck through domestication, polyploid evolution [7, 41] and modern plant breeding [42], but wild relatives of wheat still retain higher diversity [43] (Table 3), and thus provide valuable resources, such as the genes related to abiotic and biotic stress resistance [44, 45]. Those useful genetic resources of wild emmer can be introgressed into tetraploid cultivars because they are fully compatible [46]. They are also to be introduced to hexaploid wheat via interploidy hybridization [47, 48].

The natural habitats of wild species and the local cultivation of landraces are increasingly threatened by climate change, the introduction of modern cultivars, and other human activities, posing a risk that some may have already become extinct or are on the verge of extinction [49]. The value of genetic resources ex situ conserved in gene banks worldwide should be highly recognized in the utilization of genetic diversity (for a recent review and discussion, see [50]. Most of the accessions of NBRP-Wheat, Japan, were collected by expeditions dispatched by Kyoto University to the native habitats of wild species and the regions where wheat originated. As a result, NBRP-Wheat maintains a variety of wild tetraploid wheat and landraces collected over the last century. By investigating the population structure and diversity of 2,254 tetraploid accessions, we selected a core of 179 accessions representing the full range of genetic diversity of the whole set of accessions, aiming to include as much germplasm as possible that represents the phylogenetic and geographic diversity (Fig. 2; Tables 1 and 2). The core collection established in the present study represents the genetic diversity of the entire NBRP-Wheat collection and will provide good materials to maximize the use of genetic diversity.

Although the core collection was initially established using DArT sequencing data from 2,243 wheat accessions, we recognized that the limited number of DArT markers and their potential amplification bias could affect the accuracy of population structure analysis. To obtain more evenly distributed markers and improve the reliability of genetic structure inference, we performed GRAS-Di random amplification sequencing on the 179 core individuals plus the reference variety Kronos. This generated high-quality, high-density genotyping data for downstream analyses such as GWAS.

To evaluate the impact of different marker systems on genetic structure inference, we constructed phylogenetic trees based on 300 DArT markers and 27,618 GRAS-Di markers, respectively, using Jaccard distances. The trees were visualized and compared using ggtree [51]. The results showed that the overall tree structures were largely consistent (Fig. S8). However, accessions from Ethiopia were placed in different relative positions, and 12 individuals showed altered branch affiliations between the two trees (Fig. S8). Most of these individuals had previously been identified as admixed in population structure analysis. These findings indicate that while DArT markers are suitable for preliminary population classification, high-density and evenly distributed markers are essential for resolving fine-scale genetic differences. In particular, for accessions with complex genetic backgrounds or rare variants, methods based on selective amplification such as DArT may not fully capture genomic diversity, potentially leading to local misinterpretations of phylogenetic relationships.

Genetic divergence analysis between the 180 core collections revealed a substantial difference between the AAGG and AABB groups. Among the 10,618 shared markers, most were monomorphic, and the AAGG group exhibited markedly reduced genetic diversity compared to AABB (Table 3). Within emmer wheat, wild accessions can be genetically divided into two groups corresponding to their northern and southern habitats. The presence of admixed accessions (e.g., KU-1921) from the Turkey–Syria border suggests gene flow between these genetically distinct populations. Additionally, evidence of gene flow between wild and hulled emmer is indicated by the admixed individuals (Fig. 3).

FT emmer is not mixed with wild or hulled emmer. Population structure analysis revealed that FT emmer from ETH (mainly in pop4) is genetically distinct from that of Eurasian origin (pop1 and pop3), with high Fst values between the two groups (Fig. 3; Table 4), suggesting that Ethiopian FT emmer has a unique genetic origin. Two T. carthlicum accessions clustered within pop4, and both PCA and phylogenetic analysis further support the close genetic relationship between Ethiopian FT emmer and T. carthlicum (Fig. 3; Table 2). Notably, two FT emmer accessions (KU-7348 and KU-9659) collected in ETH showed pure pop3 genomic composition and were genetically distinct from other Ethiopian accessions, indicating they were likely introduced from WA rather than being native. Phenotypic comparison between Ethiopian FT emmer and accessions from other regions (including KU-7348 and KU-9659) revealed highly significant differences in CL and anthocyanin pigmentation traits (AC/ACS), as well as significant differences in HGW and TN (Fig. S9). These findings suggest that Ethiopian FT emmer may harbor novel genetic loci of potential value for breeding.

Using k-mer-based GWAS, we identified nine MTAs (one associated with both AC and ACS) in eight traits. Among them, five overlapped with previously reported loci [32, 33, 37, 38, 40]. Notably, one MTA associated with GH was detected on ChrUn and was found to be tightly linked to another MTA on Chr1A (Fig. S5), suggesting that they are likely to represent the same locus. These results indicate that despite the relatively small population size, our analysis was still able to detect robust associations.

In addition to the known loci, we identified four novel MTAs that did not overlap with previously reported regions and may represent new loci. Two of these, located on chromosomes 2B and 7A, were associated with CL. Individuals carrying the significant k-mer showed markedly different phenotypes compared to those without it, with average CL values of 101.52 cm vs. 79.46 cm for 2B locus and 82.99 cm vs. 101.73 cm for 7A locus, respectively (Fig. S10). For the MTA on chromosome 6B associated with hundred grain weight (HGW), individuals carrying the significant k-mer had an average HGW of 5.63, while those without it had an average of only 3.31 (Fig. S10). This large difference suggests the possible presence of a novel gene controlling HGW in this region.

The genetic analysis in this study was conducted based on phenotypic data from a single year. It should be noted that the traits measured (such as glume hairiness and anthocyanin pigmentation in cotyledons) are mainly qualitative and exhibit high heritability, meaning their phenotypic expression is less affected by environmental fluctuations. Thus, single-year data are sufficient for preliminary genotype–phenotype association analysis and for validating the utility of the core collection. Multi-environment phenotyping for complex quantitative traits would allow more profound insights into genotype-by-environment interactions, and such work is currently underway as a follow-up study using this core collection platform.

In summary, our GWAS successfully detected both known and potentially novel loci, demonstrating the practical value of the core collection. Many traits in this panel remain underexplored. Given the high genetic diversity of this population, further investigation into traits such as drought and salt tolerance, as well as resistance to biotic stresses and diseases, may lead to the discovery of new loci relevant for future breeding efforts. We expect that this miniaturized yet highly diverse core collection of NBRP-tetraploid wheat will serve as an efficient scientific research platform, facilitating the discovery of novel favorable alleles.
