Simulating Complex Multi-Turn Tool Calling Interactions in Stateless Execution Environments
Maxwell Crouse, Ibrahim Abdelaziz, Kshitij Fadnis, Siva Sankalp Patel, Kinjal Basu

Abstract
Synthetic data has proven itself to be a valuable resource for tuning smaller, cost-effective language models to handle the complexities of multi-turn tool calling conversations. While many frameworks and systems for producing synthetic multi-turn tool calling data have been proposed, prior works have frequently assumed that any tool calling interactions will take place in an execution environment that maintains state. When such an environment is available, this is advantageous as it allows for the validity of an interaction to be determined by whether or not the state of the execution environment matches to some prespecified objective. Unfortunately, this does not hold in many real-world tool use settings, e.g., in enterprise settings where data security is of the utmost importance or in cases where tool specifications are synthesized from multiple sources. In this work, we address this gap by introducing a data generation method, DiGiT-TC , that is designed to produce tool calling conversations that have the characteristics of conversations generated through search in a stateful environment. The key to our technique lies in a novel generation pattern that allows our approach to implicitly represent certain tool calls in the user request. We validate our approach on standard tool calling benchmarks and demonstrate that, even in stateful problem settings, our approach results in strong performance gains.

Introduction

Agentic applications of LLMs are in high demand due to their ability to effectively operate in a variety of environments that demand flexibility. While large, generalist models commonly serve as the backbone to such agents, their deployment can be fraught with challenges, including excessive costs
Belcak et al. 2025
, privacy concerns
Iqbal et al. 2024
, and difficulties adapting to new domain-specific environments
Jimenez Gutierrez et al. 2022
. Smaller models tuned with synthetic data provide one such means of addressing these concerns, wherein the capabilities of a larger model are distilled into a smaller model through training on data custom to the domain of interest. However, synthetic data is not a panacea, and producing the right data for tuning smaller models without introducing new issues like catastrophic forgetting or overfitting remains an open problem
Shumailov et al. 2024
;
Wenger 2024
.

There are several different frameworks for producing synthetic data for tool calling
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Yao et al. 2024
;
Yin et al. 2025
;
Zeng et al. 2025b
. These frameworks will often operate over a suite of tools that linked to some notion of a world state
Li et al. 2023
;
Farn and Shin 2023
;
Lu et al. 2025
. That is, the LLM is interfacing with a service (e.g., a database) that has a set of stored data and can execute operations that update or access that data.
In these frameworks, the objective is to take either a synthetic or manually curated user request that involves the world state and have the LLM execute actions via tool calls until it achieves the underlying goal of the request.

The path an LLM takes in solving a request, where the LLM is uncovering new information or transforming the underlying world state in order to achieve the user request, can result in complicated tool call trajectories where many of the tool calls are
implicit
(see Figure
1
), i.e., not directly specified by the user’s request
Basu et al. 2025
. By keeping only those interactions where the final state matches the expected target, the risk of irrelevant intermediate tool calls and degenerate interactions is lessened.

Unfortunately, for many real-world tool calling settings where synthetic data could be of use, it is not feasible to assume access to a real backend with an accessible state. In enterprise domains where data security is a topmost concern, there are risks in giving LLMs privileged access to data or services
Naihin et al. 2023
;
Ruan et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2025
. Even in cases where a system’s state may be accessible, it might be expensive to interface with to produce data at scale, e.g.,
Xie et al. 2024
. Moreover, increasingly the top open language models incorporate synthetic, LLM-generated tools during training
Team et al. 2025
;
Zeng et al. 2025a
which can, at best, only be simulated.

In this work we introduce
DiGiT-TC
, a framework for
D
ata
G
eneration and
T
ransformation for
T
ool-
C
alling, to address the problem of generating meaningfully complex, synthetic tool calling interactions from tool specifications alone and introduce a process for generating tool calls that imitate those generated through search within a stateful environment. The key insight of
DiGiT-TC
is to flip the generation of an interaction by prompting an LLM to first generate tool calls and then to generate a corresponding user request. This allows our method to be selective about which tool calls it should explicitly incorporate into the user request and which to leave implicit. As this step can introduce significantly noisy outputs, we reign in the quality of tool calling interactions through a back translation step that ensures the tool call sequence remains faithful to the user request.

To summarize, both the selective generation strategy and back translation for noise mitigation are novel contributions of
DiGiT-TC
. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach on the popular BFCL-v3
Patil et al. 2025
and
\tau^{2}
-bench
Barres et al. 2025
benchmarks where we demonstrate the strongest performance amongst comparably sized models trained using data produced by open-source, non-frontier teacher models and competitive performance as compared to prior works that rely on data produced by frontier models and stateful execution environments.

2.2
Synthetic Data for Tool-Calling

High-quality training data has been a critical driver of LLM performance, yet the scarcity of such datasets for tool-using agents has motivated research on synthetic data generation. Early efforts primarily focused on single-turn synthesis, where a model receives a one-time query and responds accordingly
Patil et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Qin et al. 2023

In the multi-turn setting, BUTTON

Prabhakar et al. 2025

3
Method

The objective of
DiGiT-TC
is to construct high quality, multi-turn interactions that consist of a "user" making requests to an "assistant" that must be solved through the use of tools. To accomplish this, our approach assumes only that it is provided a set of tool specifications, where each tool specification contains a description of the tool itself and its input and output parameters (an example specification is provided in the Appendix).

DiGiT-TC
constructs a tool calling conversation in stages. The core of data generation occurs in a back translation loop, which iteratively adds "user" and "assistant" turns until a prespecified number of iterations have completed. To aid in describing our approach, we provide Figure
2
of a simple diagram illustrating the flow of our framework and Figure
3
which depicts how turns are added by each stage to the final output. In the following sections, we will go through each stage in detail and explain how they contribute to the overall effectiveness of our approach. All prompts used in each stage and a complete output are provided in the Appendix.

3.1
Initialization

Each conversation is initialized as a tuple
\left<M,T,\mathcal{U}\right>
consisting of an empty list of messages
M
, a set of tools
T
sampled from all available tools, and a user persona
\mathcal{U}
. From the persona and sampled set of tools, our approach augments
\mathcal{U}
with more details of the user by prompting an LLM to generate specific details of the fictitious user that are relevant to the sampled tools in
T
(e.g., their location, profession, etc.). Once the LLM has completed generation, this tuple of information is passed into the main loop of
DiGiT-TC
, which will generate the turns of the interaction.

3.2
Conversation Generation

The core of
DiGiT-TC
is the iterative loop in which "user" and "assistant" turns are generated and added to the messages
M
. On each iteration, our approach will first produce a tool call sequence based on the user details and tools available and then generate a corresponding user utterance. The tool call sequence predicted will remain unexecuted, i.e., the tool calls will contain inter-tool references that have yet to be filled in.

By flipping how tool calling interactions are constructed, our approach can have finer-grained control over what parts of the tool call sequence should be explicitly represented in the user request. Once generated, our method ensures quality by performing verification of the tool call sequence with a back translation step. In the next sections, we will walk through the three stages of this phase of generation in the order they are executed by
DiGiT-TC
.

3.2.1
Plan Distillation

Following initialization, tool call generation begins from the tuple
\left<M,T,\mathcal{U}\right>
. Our framework instructs an LLM to produce each tool call sequence
S=\langle s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\rangle
from the tools
T
with arguments based on the user details
\mathcal{U}
. In addition, the LLM is instructed to write tool calls within
S
that reference one another through Pythonic-style references
Basu et al. 2025
, with the inner contents of tool call outputs being accessible through dot notation and list accessors.

Because we have generated a tool call sequence in isolation from a user utterance, each sequence
S
may contain many tool calls that do not serve a clear purpose. As such, the next step in preparing the final tool call sequence is to distill from
S
a more concise subsequence of tool calls with certain properties we would desire in our conversation.

Our approach treats the sequence
S
as a directed-acyclic graph (DAG), where edges within the DAG are defined by inter-tool references. From the DAG, it pulls the set of all connected components
C
and selects the largest component to serve as the final tool call for this iteration, i.e.,

S=\argmax_{C_{i}\in C}|C_{i}|

Figure
4
provides an example of this distillation process, where an individual tool call sequence is distilled into a more compact form.

3.2.2
Request Generation

In this stage, the tool call sequence
S
is translated into a corresponding user utterance. Provided with both our tuple
\left<M,T,\mathcal{U}\right>
from initialization and the tool call sequence
S
from the prior stage, an LLM is prompted to produce a user request
r
that would be solved by executing the tool calls in
S
.

As highlighted in Section
1
, in order to generate complex tool calling interactions, our approach seeks to produce tool call sequences with implicit tool calls. To achieve this, our method first partitions
S
into two separate sequences
I
and
E
, for implicit and explicit tool call sequences, respectively, with the partitioning done randomly to ensure the sizes of both
I
and
E
vary across examples.

Algorithm
1
provides our straightforward solution to this problem. The algorithm picks a target size for the set
I
, removes any element of
S
that is not a dependency to some other element, and then iteratively fills the set with elements in
S
chosen at random, where the chosen elements are constrained to
only
have dependencies (i.e., children) from other elements of
I
. Upon completion the algorithm returns
I
, and the complement of
I
in
S
is used as
E
, i.e.,
E=S\setminus I
. The LLM is then prompted to treat the tool call sequence
E
as the explicit goal of the user’s request and to avoid directly mentioning any of the tool calls in
I
(the exact prompts are provided in the Appendix).

As an example, refer back to the second query in Figure
4
. In the query, the output of the first tool call,
$1.cities
, is used as an argument to the second tool call. In this case,
I
might be constructed as the singleton containing
search_cities
tool call, which would then result in
E
containing the
create_country_info_dict
tool call.

Once a user request has been generated, our method validates the tool call sequence is not a straightforward transcription of the user request using the Kendall
\tau_{b}
correlation coefficient. First, we define the rank of a particular argument node
l_{i}
to be the indicator variable
R_{tc}[l_{i}]
for whether or not its parent tool call appears in the hidden set. We further write the rank of a leaf node as it occurs in the user request to be
R_{req}[l_{i}]
(i.e., the order it appears in the request as compared to other leaves). In this context, each leaf node
l_{i}
corresponds to a single observation
(x_{i},y_{i})=(R_{tc}[l_{i}],R_{req}[l_{i}])
. We write a pair of observations as
(x_{i},y_{i})
and
(x_{j},y_{j})
and consider them concordant when

\left(x_{i}<x_{j}\bigwedge y_{i}<y_{j}\right)\bigvee\left(x_{i}>x_{j}\bigwedge y_{i}>y_{j}\right)

Similarly, the pair is said to be discordant when

\left(x_{i}<x_{j}\bigwedge y_{i}>y_{j}\right)\bigvee\left(x_{i}>x_{j}\bigwedge y_{i}<y_{j}\right)

From these two notions, we calculate the Kendall
\tau_{b}
coefficient as

\tau_{b}=\dfrac{C-D}{\sqrt{(C+D+X_{0})(C+D+Y_{0})}}

where
C
is the number of concordant pairs,
D
the number of discordant pairs,
X_{0}
the number of pairs where the first element is tied, and
Y_{0}
the number of pairs where the second element is tied.

When
\tau_{b}
indicates the orderings of arguments between the tool calls and the user request are correlated (i.e.,
\epsilon>0
), our approach filters out the example. Intuitively, this gives stronger preference to examples where the tool call sequence is not a straightforward linear transcription of the user request.

3.2.3
Back Translation

To increase the quality of outputs,
DiGiT-TC
performs a back translation step that validates whether or not a new tool call sequence,
S^{\prime}
, predicted from the user request,
r
, and tools,
T
, would independently produce similar results to the original tool call sequence
S
.

We observed it to be helpful to have a tolerance for mismatches, and thus validating consistency between the original tool call sequence,
S
, and new tool call sequences,
S^{\prime}
, involves only checking that the leaves of
S
are all represented in
S^{\prime}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\texttt{leaves}(S)\setminus\texttt{leaves}(S^{\prime})=\emptyset

When this condition fails, this is treated as an indication of noise in the generation process and the example is filtered out. Assuming
S^{\prime}
has passed validation, it is added to the list of messages
M
as an assistant step in lieu of
S
.

3.3
Termination

Once conversation generation is complete, the list of messages
M
now consists of an alternating sequence of user requests and tool calls. Before returning to the user,
DiGiT-TC
finishes with two stages (see the last two stages of Figure
3
). The first of these stages involves executing the tool calls and substituting the values for each variable into their respective arguments and adding an "assistant" summary of the tool call results as they relate to the original request. The last step before returning, as with
Zeng et al. 2025b
, is to add complexity by simulating errors.

3.3.1
Tool Execution and Summarization

The tool call sequences must be executed, as, thus far, the tool calls contain references that link their outputs to the inputs of other tool calls. Our method simulates the execution of each tool call with an LLM to obtain a tool observation
O
. This is used to substitute required results into the appropriate slots of other tool calls in
M
that have a dependency on
S
. After adding the result of tool call execution after
S
in the list of messages
M
, the LLM is prompted to summarize the results of that interaction before moving on to the next user utterance.

3.3.2
Error Augmentation

DiGiT-TC
constructs data to represent both human errors as well as tool execution errors. Human errors are considered those where the user asks an unsolvable or incorrect request, and the assistant must ask for clarification to determine the correct request. These are simulated by prompting an LLM to generate overly generic, unsolvable requests that relate to both the tools as well as other requests in the interaction and then to generate the corresponding clarification question. In Figure
5
we provide an example of the interaction produced for this type of error.

Formulating a meaningful error is more challenging for tool call errors because there is no world state underlying the execution of tools in our approach. In this work, we make the straightforward assumption that the purpose of an error message is to direct the model towards the correct solution. To simulate an error, our method takes in the list of messages
M
that ends with a target tool call
S
and then predicts a tool call
E
. When
S
and
E
differ, our approach prompts an LLM to suggest a hint for correcting the difference between the gold tool call
S
and the predicted tool call
E
. This hint becomes the error message for a tool call observation
O
that directly follows
E
in
M
. We provide an example of this generation procedure in Figure
6

4
Experimental Evaluation

4.1
Training Data

For our main experiments, we generated multi-turn training instances using the license-friendly, open source
gpt-oss-120b

OpenAI 2025
model. Like
Zeng et al. 2025b
;
Prabhakar et al. 2025
, we found it useful to apply a simple LLM-as-a-Judge filter to remove nonsense conversations that may have made it through generation (the prompt for this is provided in the appendix). After filtering, the training dataset contained 5,000 conversations, which were then concatenated together at random (to a maximum of 5 "user" requests per instance) to produce longer conversations. As a last step, to add variety, we mask all function and parameter names with random strings to ensure the model does not overfit to specific values.

4.2
Training Setup

Once data generation was complete, we trained a
llama3.1-8B-instruct

AI@Meta 2024
model using the LLaMa-Factory framework
Zheng et al. 2024
. We used the same settings for LoRA
Hu et al. 2022
fine-tuning as
Zeng et al. 2025b
, with a batch size of 64, a LoRA rank of 16, a learning rate of
1e-4
, a cosine learning rate schedule, and a warmup ratio of
0.1
.

4.3
Evaluation Benchmarks

To validate the effectiveness of our approach, we evaluated the trained model on
\tau^{2}
-bench
Yao et al. 2024
and the multi-turn categories of BFCL-v3
Patil et al. 2025
. The BFCL-v3 benchmark evaluates LLMs on multi-turn, tool-augmented reasoning tasks, emphasizing factual correctness and hallucination resistance. It introduces challenging scenarios requiring context retention across turns, making it a strong measure of real-world conversational performance.
\tau^{2}
-bench is a recent benchmark for testing conversational agents in dual-control settings, where both the agent and a simulated user can act on a shared environment. It has three domains (
airline
,
retail
, and
telecom
) in which diverse tasks are used to evaluate how well agents coordinate, communicate, and guide users, revealing weaknesses in realistic interactive scenarios.

5.1
Ablation Experiments

We performed ablation experiments using the multi-turn category of the BFCL-v3 dataset to validate the effectiveness of the core contributions of this work, i.e., the selective generation strategy that encourages conversations to have implicit tool calls and the back translation approach for noise mitigation. To test the value of implicit tool calls, we curated a set of
2k
examples where no elements of any tool call sequence were masked, thus making each user request a relatively straightforward, explicit translation of its corresponding tool call sequence. In addition, we curated a set of
2k
examples that were generated without the back translation step.

Table
3
shows the results of these experiments, where it can be seen that both contributions had significant impact on the final results. Overall, excluding implicit tool calls led to a remarkable drop of 9% in performance, which supports our hypothesis that implicit tool calls are an important feature to capture. Excluding back translation resulted in a similarly steep drop of 8%. We suspect this is due to the inherent noise of the initial planning step which produces outputs ungrounded by any user request.

6
Conclusions

In this work we introduced a new method for producing complex, multi-turn tool calling interactions that does not rely on a stateful execution environment. Our approach contributed a novel selective generation strategy that allowed for a straightforward introduction of a back translation technique for noise mitigation. Using the BFCL-v3 and
\tau^{2}
-bench benchmarks we showed that the novel aspects of our method contributed significantly to its performance, which exceeded that of much larger, frontier models and was competitive with other top performing tool calling approaches.

Limitations

As stated in Section
1
, we believe
DiGiT-TC
provides strong advantages from a privacy and scalability standpoint as compared to existing works for producing synthetic function calling data. However, we suspect that, for any complex task requiring knowledge of a system’s state where an appropriate simulation and execution environment is available for use in model training, using
DiGiT-TC
would almost certainly be less effective than a method that more fully leverages such information. In addition, while not all stages of
DiGiT-TC
are required to be used, the results shown in Section
3
show the use of all stages provides the best performance. If one were to substitute a more effective frontier model for the open weights model used in our experiments to produce the data, the total cost after running each stage would likely be significantly more expensive than simpler approaches that execute in fewer stages (e.g.,
Zeng et al. 2025b
).

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine
Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none
which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.
