Absence of Entanglement Growth in Dicke Superradiance
N. S. Bassler

Abstract
Dicke superradiance describes an ensemble of N N permutationally invariant two-level systems collectively emitting radiation with a peak radiated intensity scaling as N 2 N^{2} . Individual Dicke states are typically entangled. However, the density matrix during superradiant decay is a mixture of such states, raising the subtle question of whether the total state is entangled or separable. We resolve this by showing analytically that for any N N , starting from the fully excited state, the collective decay preserves separability for all times. This answers a longstanding question on the role of entanglement in Dicke superradiance and underscores that, despite collective dissipation, separable states remain separable under these dynamics.

II
The problem statement

We consider a system of
N
identical two-level systems undergoing collective spontaneous emission in the Dicke limit. The dynamics is governed by the master equation

\dot{\rho}=\Gamma\left[S\rho S^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left(S^{\dagger}S\rho+\rho S^{\dagger}S\right)\right],

(1)

where we set the collective decay rate
\Gamma=1
, and
S=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sigma_{k}
is the collective lowering operator with the single-particle lowering operator
\sigma_{j}=\outerproduct{g}{e}_{j}
taking the two-level system
j
from the excited to the ground state. Starting in the fully excited state
\ket{eee\dots e}
, the system remains confined to the symmetric subspace of maximal total spin
S=N/2
, spanned by the Dicke states
\ket{\Dicke{\ind{}}}{}
with
k{}=0,1,\dots,N
defined by
S^{\dagger}S\ket{\Dicke{\ind{}}}{}=h_{k}\ket{\Dicke{\ind{}}}{}
where
h_{k}{}=k{}(N-k{}+1)
. Writing the density matrix as a mixture of Dicke states
\rho(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}p_{k}{}(t)\outerproduct{\ind{}}{\ind{}}
with populations
p_{k}(t)
and without coherences in the Dicke basis, the dynamics are then described by a set of coupled rate equations

\dot{p}_{k}{}(t)=h_{k{}+1}\,p_{k{}+1}(t)-h_{k}{}\,p_{k}{}(t).

(2)

A key simplification is that we consider density matrices without coherence in the Dicke basis, i.e., that the density matrix is diagonal
1
1

1

Starting from the fully excited state and evolving under collective decay, the state remains diagonal in the Dicke basis at all times. This structure allows for a global phase average over spin coherent states, reducing degrees of freedom in the decomposition to a single real parameter, the excitation probability
\varepsilon
. As a result, the separability problem reduces to a one-dimensional truncated moment problem.
. Analytical approaches to the above equations have shown a simple solution for the time dynamics of the population expressed as a contour integral in the complex plane
[

We now address the central question:

Does Dicke superradiance generate entanglement from an initially separable state?

A mixed state of
N

M
tensor products of individual particle density operators
[
16

\rho(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}(t)\,\rho^{(i)}_{1}(t)\otimes\cdots\otimes\rho^{(i)}_{N}(t),

(3)

with
w_{i}(t)\geq 0

\sum_{i}w_{i}(t)=1

\rho^{(i)}_{j}(t)
are the single-emitter density matrices. For permutationally invariant states, the single-particle density matrices
\rho^{(i)}_{j}(t)
in each decomposition term must be identical, making each component a symmetric product state. These are precisely spin coherent states (see also Ref.

\displaystyle\rho^{(i)}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\rho(\varepsilon_{i}{}(t))=\outerproduct{\psi_j(\Ei)}{\psi_j(\Ei)}

(4)

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_j(\Ei)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon_{i}}\,\ket{g}_{j}+\sqrt{\varepsilon_{i}}\,\ket{e}_{j}.

The single-emitter density operator corresponds to a pure state with excitation probability
\varepsilon_{i}\in[0,1]

Expressing the state in the Dicke basis and relating it to the time evolution obtained from Eq. (

p_{k}{}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}(t)b_{N,k{}}(\varepsilon_{i}(t)),

(5)

i.e., the population vector
\bm{p}(t)=(p_{0}(t),\dots,p_{N}(t))^{T}
is a convex combination of Bernstein basis polynomials

b_{N,k{}}(x)=\binom{N}{k{}}x^{k}{}(1-x)^{N-k{}}

(6)

III
Proof for absence of entanglement generation

Before proceeding to analyze separability preservation, we recall
[
13
,
12
,
20
,
21
]
from Eq. (
2
) that the Dicke dynamics form a simple rate equation system governed by

\bm{p}(t)=\exp(\Dickemat t)\,\bm{p}(0),

(7)

where
\bm{M}
is the upper triangular rate matrix

\bm{M}=\begin{pmatrix}0&h_{1}&&\\
0&-h_{1}&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&h_{N}\\
&&0&-h_{N}\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

To build intuition, we first study bipartite entanglement between two atoms in the ensemble. This quantity - the two-particle negativity - is tractable analytically and provides a lower bound on any entanglement present in the system. Surprisingly, we find that even when starting from an entangled Dicke state, this quantity always decreases during the collective evolution, see App.
G
. Numerically, this negativity decreases monotonically on a characteristic timescale
\delta\sim 1/(\Gamma N)
. In particular, for an initially fully excited state, the negativity always remains zero. While this analytical result and the accompanying numerical results seem to suggest that no entanglement is created during the dynamics, the negativity of the density matrix with respect to bipartitions only captures bipartite entanglement. Thus, one cannot rule out the generation of more complex multipartite entanglement. To address this, we now define a more general separability criterion. The reformulation in terms of a moment problem bypasses the need for computationally intensive optimization over all possible entangled states, instead reducing the problem to verifying the positive semidefiniteness of two matrices, making analytics tractable.

Below we give the essential argument; algebraic details, Hankel determinants, and an introduction to the truncated Hausdorff moment problem are given in App.
B
.

IV
Why collective decay cannot create entanglement

Collective emission conserves full permutation symmetry. That symmetry forces the density matrix to stay
diagonal
in the Dicke basis, i.e. a classical probability vector over Dicke levels. Entanglement can only appear if those probabilities
cannot
be written as a mixture of spin-coherent product states. We show they always can.

1.

Diagonal trajectory
.
Eq. (
1
) evolves the Dicke populations linearly:
\bm{p}(t)=e^{\bm{M}t}\bm{p}(0)
.

2.

Moments instead of populations
.
Via the Bernstein–monomial transform
\bm{m}=\bm{B}\bm{p}
we regard
m_{k}(t)
as the first
N
moments of some (unknown) distribution of the excitation probability on
x\!\in\![0,1]
.

3.

Truncated Hausdorff moment problem
.
A distribution exists
iff
two Hankel matrices
H(\bm{m})
and
\bar{H}(\bm{m})
remain positive-semidefinite. This is equivalent to saying “there is a spin-coherent decomposition.” Such a reconstruction is shown in Fig.
1
.

4.

Time-step stability
.
Expanding
H(\bm{m})
in a small step
\delta t
shows that all leading minors change only in first nonzero order
\delta t^{\,r(r-1)/2}
with a
positive
prefactor (App.
E
). Hence
H
and
\bar{H}
stay positive for any
\delta t\!\leq\!\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
that does not depend on
x
.

5.

Full trajectory
.
Composing a sufficient number of such steps covers all
t\!\geq\!0
, so the state is separable at every instant.

V
Radiated intensity from the separable mixture

Because every pure component
\rho(\varepsilon)^{\otimes N}
in the
decomposition carries an inversion
\varepsilon
,
the instantaneous superradiant intensity can be written in closed‐form
without invoking entanglement:

I(t)=\Gamma\expectationvalue{\hat S^{\dagger}\hat S}=\Gamma\int_{0}^{1}f_{t}(\varepsilon)\bigl[N\varepsilon+N(N-1)\,\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\bigr]\,d\varepsilon\,.

(9)

The familiar
N^{2}
scaling thus arises purely from the
classical
statistics encoded in
f_{t}(\varepsilon)
; no quantum
correlations between emitters are required.

VI
Connection to quantum trajectories

Eq. (
3
) may also be viewed as an
unraveling
: the initial photon emission stochastically selects a spin-coherent branch, with branch probabilities
w_{i}(t)
. Along every individual trajectory, the state remains
fully separable
; all quantum randomness is encoded in the classical distribution
f_{t}(\varepsilon)
. A complementary Letter (Rosario
et al.

[

VII
Experimental consequence

Our proof assumes an initially
diagonal
Dicke state and no external drive. This is precisely the protocol used in pulsed superradiant bursts of (i) bad‑cavity optical‑clock atoms
[
4
]
, (ii) transmon pairs
[
24
]
, and (iii) Rydberg or solid‑state emitter arrays
[
36
,
23
,
31
]
. In such “free‑decay” runs, the trajectory is rigorously separable, so
any
entanglement measured must originate from
non‑collective
channels—dipole‑dipole shifts, spatial inhomogeneity, or dephasing.
By contrast, when the same platforms are operated in
continuous‑wave or Ramsey mode, a coherent drive maintains spin superpositions
[
42
,
30
]
; those off‑diagonal terms lie outside the Dicke‑limit Lindblad considered here and can indeed generate entanglement. Our result therefore serves as a calibration
baseline: turning off the drive should eliminate entanglement, while turning it back on isolates the genuine coherent contributions.

VIII
Discussion and Conclusion

We have analytically shown that Dicke superradiance does not generate entanglement when starting from a separable, symmetric, and diagonal state. This settles a longstanding question about entanglement in Dicke superradiance: Collective dissipation in the Dicke limit preserves separability.

Our proof uses the structure of symmetric diagonal Dicke density matrices to map the separability question onto the truncated moment problem on
[0,1]
. By analyzing the time evolution of a product state under a small time step, we found that the resulting moments satisfy the truncated moment problem conditions. Thus, this small time evolution leaves separable states separable. Composing such steps shows that the entire trajectory remains separable.

Additionally, we introduced an entanglement measure, the sum over the absolute values of the negative eigenvalues of the Hankel matrices, in App.
F
; it vanishes iff the state is separable, decays exponentially
\propto 1/N
in our numerics, and remains zero when starting from the fully excited state — confirming the analytic proof.

Our analysis provides a framework for studying entanglement dynamics in collective spin models. While we have shown that separability is preserved under collective decay in the Dicke limit, entanglement can emerge in more general settings—such as emitter arrays with spatially structured decay, or in systems strongly coupled to a lossy cavity. These scenarios may feature entanglement generation or even transient entanglement transitions. Extending our approach to states with coherence, to symmetry-breaking dynamics, or models like Tavis-Cummings is a promising direction for future work. It also raises the question of whether the absence or emergence of entanglement could be confirmed directly in experiment.

Appendix A
Separability Criterion and Convex Decomposition

Consider a single-emitter pure state

\ket{\psi(\varepsilon,\phi)}=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\,\ket{g}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\ket{e},

(10)

with
0\leq\varepsilon\leq 1
and
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
. Its density matrix is

\displaystyle\rho(\varepsilon,\phi)

\displaystyle=\ket{\psi(\varepsilon,\phi)}\bra{\psi(\varepsilon,\phi)}

(11)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon&\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\phi}\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)}\\
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)}&1-\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}

in the basis
\{\ket{e},\ket{g}\}
.

Now, take the tensor product of
N
identical emitters

\ket{\psi(\varepsilon,\phi)}^{\otimes N}=\left(\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\,\ket{g}+e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\ket{e}\right)^{\otimes N}.

(12)

Expanding this vector in the Dicke basis, we find

\ket{\psi(\varepsilon,\phi)}^{\otimes N}=\sum_{k{}=0}^{N}\sqrt{\binom{N}{k{}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi k{}}\,\varepsilon^{k{}/2}\,(1-\varepsilon)^{(N-k{})/2}\,\ket{n},

(13)

leading to a density matrix

\displaystyle\rho(\varepsilon,\phi)^{\otimes N}=

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\sum_{k{},k{}^{\prime}=0}^{N}\sqrt{\binom{N}{k{}}\binom{N}{k{}^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi\,(k{}-k{}^{\prime})}\,

(14)

\displaystyle\times\varepsilon^{\frac{k{}+k{}^{\prime}}{2}}\,(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{2N-(k{}+k{}^{\prime})}{2}}\,\outerproduct{\ind{}}{\ind{}'}.

We thus see that, if we attempt to represent a Dicke-state density matrix without coherences, we must somehow get rid of the off-diagonal entries. Concretely, this is done by phase-averaging. We define the phase-averaged density matrix

\overline{\rho}(\varepsilon)^{\otimes N}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{2\pi}\,\rho(\varepsilon,\phi)^{\otimes N}.

(15)

Since the phases average out for the off-diagonal terms

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{2\pi}\,e^{i\phi\,(k{}-k{}^{\prime})}=\delta_{k{},k{}^{\prime}},

(16)

we obtain a diagonal density matrix

\overline{\rho}(\varepsilon)^{\otimes N}=\sum_{k{}=0}^{N}\binom{N}{k{}}\,\varepsilon^{k}{}(1-\varepsilon)^{N-k{}}\,\outerproduct{\ind{}}{\ind{}}.

(17)

Alternatively, a finite sum that accomplishes the same phase averaging is

\overline{\rho}(\varepsilon)^{\otimes N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\rho\left(\varepsilon,\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right)^{\otimes N},

(18)

which is the minimal number of terms that can cancel all phase terms for an arbitrary
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.

Since the phase averaging is a technical detail, we will omit the notation indicating the phase averaging from now on, but assume it implicitly.

A separable, diagonal, symmetric state of
N
such emitters can then be written as a convex combination of
\rho(\varepsilon)^{\otimes N}
. By diagonal, we mean that there is no single-emitter coherence since it is known that
\expectationvalue{\sigma_i}=0
in the Dicke superradiance problem. Taking this into account, a separable state can thus be written as

\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\,\rho(\varepsilon_{i}{})^{\otimes N},

(19)

with
w_{i}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
, and
\varepsilon_{i}{}\in[0,1]
.

These product density matrices are diagonal in the Dicke basis in the sense that they have matrix elements

\bra{\Dicke{k}}\rho(\varepsilon)^{\otimes N}\ket{\Dicke{\ind{}}}{}=\binom{N}{k}\varepsilon^{k}(1-\varepsilon)^{N-k}.

(20)

Thus, any separable, diagonal, symmetric state has Dicke populations of the form:

p_{k}{}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\binom{N}{k{}}\varepsilon_{i}{}^{k}{}(1-\varepsilon_{i}{})^{N-k{}}.

(21)

This expression is a convex combination of Bernstein basis polynomials
b_{k,N}(\varepsilon)=\binom{N}{k}\varepsilon^{k}(1-\varepsilon)^{N-k}
, which form a basis for the polynomials on the interval
[0,1]
. Instead of working with the Bernstein polynomials, it is more natural to work with the monomials. The Bernstein polynomials
\bm{b}(x)=(b_{0,N}(x),b_{1,N}(x),\dots,b_{N,N}(x))^{T}
are related to the monomials
\bm{v}(x)=(1,x,\dots,x^{N})^{T}
with a linear transformation

\bm{v}(x)=\bm{B}\bm{b}(x).

(22)

This transformation matrix has matrix elements

B_{k{},k^{\prime}}=\binom{k{}^{\prime}}{k{}}/\binom{N}{k{}}

(23)

which defines an upper triangular matrix.

So, in terms of the moment vector
\bm{m}(t)=\bm{B}\bm{p}(t)
the separability condition becomes

m_{k}{}(t)=\sum_{i}w_{i}(t)\varepsilon_{i}{}(t)^{k}{}.

(24)

The sum
\sum_{i}w_{i}\varepsilon_{i}{}^{k}
can now be considered as the
k{}
-th moment of the probability distribution
\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}(t)\delta_{p,\varepsilon_{i}{}(t)}
. Asking whether
\varepsilon_{i}{}(t)\in[0,1]
and convex
w_{i}(t)
exist so that Eq. (
24
) is exactly equivalent to asking if a probability measure
f_{t}(p)
supported on
[0,1]
exists such that

m_{k}{}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}p^{k}{}f_{t}(p)\differential{p}\quad\text{for }k=0,1,2,\dots,N

(25)

is fulfilled. This is the truncated moment problem on
[0,1]
for each time
t
.

Appendix B
The truncated Hausdorff moment problem

We adapt
[
29
]
for this section. Given some probability distribution
\mu(x)
, all moments
m_{k}
can be calculated from it. The moment problem solves the inverse problem. Given real numbers
m_{0},m_{1},\dots,m_{N}
, does there exist a positive measure
\mu
on the interval
[0,1]
such that

m_{k}{}=\int_{0}^{1}x^{k}{}\mu(x)\ \differential{x}\quad\text{for }k{}=0,1,2,\dots,N\text{ ?}

(26)

The positive measure is called a probability distribution exactly when it is normalized, i.e.,
m_{0}=1
.

To write the necessary and sufficient conditions, define the Hankel matrix of the moments

H_{k{},k{}^{\prime}}(\bm{m})=m_{k{}+k{}^{\prime}}

(27)

with
k{},k{}^{\prime}=0,1,\dots,\lfloor N/2\rfloor
and the localizing shifted Hankel matrix

\overline{H}_{k{},k{}^{\prime}}(\bm{m})=m_{k{}+k{}^{\prime}+1}-m_{k{}+k{}^{\prime}+2}

(28)

with
k{},k{}^{\prime}=0,1,\dots,\lfloor(N-1)/2\rfloor
.

Theorem:
A sequence
m_{0},\dots,m_{N}
corresponds to the moments of a probability distribution on the interval
[0,1]
if and only if

•

The distribution is normalized
m_{0}=1
.

•

The Hankel matrix
H(\bm{m})
and the shifted Hankel matrix
\overline{H}(\bm{m})
are positive semidefinite.

Since
H(\bm{m})
and
\overline{H}(\bm{m})
are symmetric Hankel matrices, they are positive semidefinite if and only if all their leading principal minors are nonnegative by Sylvester’s theorem. That is to say, the determinants of the upper left
1\times 1

H_{1}(\bm{m}),\overline{H}_{1}(\bm{m})
, the upper left
2\times 2
block
H_{2}(\bm{m}),\overline{H}_{2}(\bm{m})
etc. must all have nonnegative determinants.

Appendix C
Numerical reconstruction of supports and weights in the moment problem

Given a moment vector
m_{0},m_{1},\dots,m_{N}
we want to reconstruct the weights
w_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{i}
so that

m_{k}{}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\varepsilon_{i}{}^{k}{}.

(29)

where we know that
M\leq\lceil(N+1)/2\rceil
. In practice, this can be done without any nonlinear optimization using the following algorithm. The reconstruction procedure we use is standard in the classical theory of truncated moment problems
[
29
]
, but to our knowledge, its application to the separability problem in quantum dynamics has not been systematically explored.

1.

Construct the Hankel matrix
H(\bm{m})
of the moment problem.

2.

Find the minimal
r
so that the upper left
r\times r
block
H_{r}(\bm{m})
has full rank via SVD and
H_{r+1}(\bm{m})
is singular up to
r=\lceil(N+1)/2\rceil
.

3.

Solve the linear system

H_{r}\bm{a}=-\bm{b}

(30)

with
\bm{b}=(m_{r},m_{r+1},\dots,m_{2r-1})^{T}
for
\bm{a}
.

4.

Construct a polynomial
p(x)=x^{r}+a_{r-1}x^{r-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
and find its roots. Its real roots in the interval
[0,1]
are the support points
x_{i}
.

5.

Construct the Vandermonde matrix
V_{ki}=x_{i}^{k}
for
k=0,\dots,r-1
and solve the remaining linear system

V\bm{w}=(m_{0},\dots,m_{r-1})^{T}

(31)

for the weights
w_{i}
.

Since the roots of the polynomial can be found as the eigenvalues of its companion matrix, the procedure reduces to linear algebra operations such as SVDs and diagonalization of matrices with dimension
\propto N
, leading to a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
for this algorithm.

Appendix D
The moment problem for time evolution via linearization

To analyze whether separability is preserved under time evolution, we linearize the dynamics. Since studying the full exponential map is intractable, we ask whether a single small time-step already preserves the structure of a valid moment vector. If this property holds uniformly for all moment vectors for a small time step, it holds for all times by composition.

Let
\bm{m}(t)
be a time-dependent moment vector evolving according to a matrix
\overline{\bm{M}}{}
,

\bm{m}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t\overline{\bm{M}}{}}\bm{m}(0).

(32)

We now ask whether
\bm{m}(t)
remains a valid moment vector for all
t\geq 0
whenever
\bm{m}(0)
is a valid moment vector. Showing that this full-time evolution maps proper moment vectors onto proper vectors is often tricky. Thus, rather than the full exponential, we linearize the time evolution

\bm{m}\mapsto\bm{m}+\delta\overline{\bm{M}}{}\bm{m}

(33)

for small
\delta>0
. If there exists some
\delta
for all
\bm{m}
so that
\bm{m}(t)
is a valid moment vector, then the full time evolution also only generates valid moment vectors for all times via

\bm{m}(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\overline{\bm{M}}{}t/n}\right)^{n}\bm{m}(0).

(34)

A crucial point is that we do not need to check this for all
\bm{m}
. It is sufficient to check that all extremal points which are generated by delta distributions
\bm{v}(x)=(1,x,x^{2},\dots,x^{N})^{T}
map onto valid moment distributions. This relates to the underlying density matrices in the following way: Checking all
\bm{m}(t)
is equivalent to checking that the time evolution maps all separable density matrices onto separable density matrices. Checking only for
\bm{v}(x)
amounts to showing that the time evolution maps all product density matrices onto separable states. But since separable states are only convex combinations of product density matrices, checking
\bm{v}(x)
is sufficient. The first step in checking that
\overline{\bm{M}}{}
preserves moment vector validity is to show that

\bm{m}(x,\delta)=\bm{v}(x)+\delta\overline{\bm{M}}{}\bm{v}(x)

(35)

remains a valid moment vector for all
x\in[0,1]
.

This first-order validity can be checked using the determinant-based conditions from the main text. For each
x
, we construct the Hankel and shifted Hankel matrices from
\bm{m}(x,\delta)
, and verify that they are positive semidefinite to linear order in
\delta
. By Sylvester’s theorem, this reduces to checking that all leading principal minors are nonnegative. To linearize the determinants, we use the fact that the derivative of a determinant of a square matrix
A
can be written as

\det A(\delta)=\det A(0)+\delta\Tr(\operatorname{adj}(A(0))\cdot A'(0))+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}),

(36)

where
\operatorname{adj}
indicates the adjugate of a matrix.

The problem now simplifies crucially by recognizing that both
H(\bm{v}(x))
and
\overline{H}(\bm{v}(x))
are rank-1 matrices:
H(\bm{v}(x))=\bm{v}(x)\bm{v}(x)^{\!T}
. For the shifted Hankel matrix define
\bm{w}(x)=\bigl((1-x),(1-x)x,(1-x)x^{2},\dots\bigr)
; then
\overline{H}(\bm{v}(x))=\bm{w}(x)\bm{v}(x)^{\!T}
. Consequently,
at first order in
\delta
, the adjugate–trace term in Eq. (
36
) vanishes for every principal minor except the
1{\times}1
and
2{\times}2
blocks. To complete the linear-order check, we therefore verify that

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\det H_{1}\!\bigl(\bm{m}(\delta,x)\bigr),\;\det\overline{H}_{1}\!\bigl(\bm{m}(\delta,x)\bigr),\;

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle\det H_{2}\!\bigl(\bm{m}(\delta,x)\bigr),\;\det\overline{H}_{2}\!\bigl(\bm{m}(\delta,x)\bigr)

remain non-negative. Their linearized forms read explicitly

\displaystyle\det H_{1}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))

\displaystyle=1+\delta\,\dot{m}_{0}(x,0)\geq 0,

(37)

\displaystyle\det H_{2}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))

\displaystyle=\delta\left(\dot{m}_{0}(x,0)\,x^{2}+\dot{m}_{2}(x,0)-2x\,\dot{m}_{1}(x,0)\right)\geq 0,

\displaystyle\det\overline{H}_{1}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))

\displaystyle=x-x^{2}+\delta\left(\dot{m}_{1}(x,0)-\dot{m}_{2}(x,0)\right)\geq 0,

\displaystyle\det\overline{H}_{2}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))

\displaystyle=\delta x(1-x)\left(\dot{m}_{3}(x,0)-\dot{m}_{4}(x,0)+x^{2}\left(\dot{m}_{1}(x,0)-\dot{m}_{2}(x,0)\right)-2x\left(\dot{m}_{2}(x,0)-\dot{m}_{3}(x,0)\right)\right)\geq 0

and
m_{0}(x,\delta)=1
.

It is sufficient to determine whether there exists some
\delta>0
so that these inequalities hold for all
x\in[0,1]
to show that
H_{1}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))
and
H_{2}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))
remain positive for all times.

We now check the resulting inequalities for the specific moment time evolution matrix
\overline{\bm{M}}{}
associated with Dicke superradiance. We find the linearized time evolution of the moments as

m_{k}{}(x,\delta)=x^{k}{}+\delta k{}(-N-1+k{}+(N-k{})x)x^{k}{}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}),

(38)

so that

\dot{m}_{k}{}(x,0)=x^{k}{}k{}(-N-1+k{}+(N-k{})x)

(39)

The first condition in Eq. (
37
) is trivially satisfied. The inequality for
\overline{H}_{2}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))
is equivalent to
x\geq 0
and the inequality for
H_{2}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))
is equivalent to
x\leq 1
. Only the condition
\det\overline{H}_{1}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))\geq 0
constrains the time
\delta
for which these inequalities hold. It holds trivially for
x=1
and
x=0
and for
0<x<1
it explicitly reads

\delta\left(-2Nx^{2}+3Nx-N+4x^{2}-3x\right)\geq x-1

(40)

which always holds for
\delta<1/N
. We thus see that
\bm{I}+\delta\overline{\bm{M}}{}
maps all valid moment vectors onto valid moment vectors for
\delta\in[0,1/N]
. The limit to the full-time evolution in Eq. (
34
) can be safely taken since the inequalities derived hold uniformly for all
x\in[0,1]
. To show that no entanglement is generated during Dicke time evolution, we must now show that the lowest nonzero time orders in all other
H_{r}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))
are also positive.

Appendix E
Higher-Order Hankel Minors and Positivity

Define the

Determinant expansion

\sigma
into the product

\begin{split}S^{i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}}_{\ell_{0},\dots,\ell_{r-1}}&=\sum_{\pi\in S_{r}}\sum_{k_{0}=0}^{\,i_{\pi(0)}+\ell_{\pi(0)}}\;\cdots\;\sum_{k_{r-1}=0}^{\,i_{\pi(r-1)}+\ell_{\pi(r-1)}}\,\Bigl[\sgn(\pi)\,\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}\binom{k_{d}}{d}\,\alpha_{k_{d}}^{(i_{\pi(d)},\ell_{\pi(d)})}\Bigr]\\
&\times\underbrace{\sum_{\sigma\in S_{r}}\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{d}\sigma(\pi(d))^{\,k_{d}-d}}_{T_{\bm{k},\sigma,\pi}}\end{split}

We can now change variables to
\tau=\sigma\circ\pi
so that

T_{\bm{k},\pi,\sigma}=\sgn(\pi)^{-1}\sum_{\tau\in S_{r}}\sgn(\tau)\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}\tau(d)^{\,k_{d}-d},

We now recognize that the summation over
\tau
is a Leibniz expansion of the Vandermonde determinant

\sum_{\tau\in S_{r}}\sgn(\tau)\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}\tau(d)^{\,k_{d}-d}=\det\!\bigl[n^{\,k_{d}-d}\bigr]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}.

Using this in the expression for the symmetrized determinant, we obtain

S^{i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}}_{\ell_{0},\dots,\ell_{r-1}}=\sum_{\pi\in S_{r}}\sum_{k_{0}=0}^{\,i_{\pi(0)}+\ell_{\pi(0)}}\;\cdots\;\sum_{k_{r-1}=0}^{\,i_{\pi(r-1)}+\ell_{\pi(r-1)}}\,\Bigl[\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}\binom{k_{d}}{d}\,\alpha_{k_{d}}^{(i_{\pi(d)},\ell_{\pi(d)})}\Bigr]\det\!\bigl[n^{\,k_{d}-d}\bigr]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}.

(62)

Note the exponents of the Vandermonde
e_{d}=k_{d}-d
must collectively span
\{0,1,2,\dots,r-1\}
, see Eq. (
87
). Otherwise, the determinant is zero. Now, note that
\alpha_{k}^{n,\ell}=0
when
\ell>n
. Thus
\ell_{n}\leq i_{n}
and index
k_{d}
must run from
d\leq k_{d}\leq i_{n}+\ell_{n}
. It follows that
0\leq e_{d}\leq i_{n}+\ell_{n}
. So the only way to fulfill all of these bounds is to have
\{e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{r-1}\}=\{0,1,2,\dots,r-1\}
and
i_{n}=\ell_{n}=d
. Consequently,
k_{d}-2d
is the only term that remains in the sum, and
i_{n}=\ell_{n}
. Thus, we have that
\sum_{n}\ell_{n}=r(r-1)/2
and indeed, since this is the power of
y
we have

\det H_{r}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))=K_{r}(1-x)^{r(r-1)/2}x^{r(r-1)}\delta^{r(r-1)/2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{r(r-1)/2+1}\right)\quad K_{r}\in\mathbb{R},

(63)

We can also write the determinant explicitly, which no longer depends on the specifics of the Dicke problem since
\alpha^{(d,d)}_{2d}=1

\Sigma^{i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}}_{i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}}=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!\right)S^{i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}}_{i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}}=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!\right)\sum_{\pi\in S_{r}}\biggl[\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}\binom{2i_{\pi(d)}}{d}\biggr]\det\!\bigl[n^{\,2i_{\pi(d)}-d}\bigr]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}.

(64)

E.5
Positivity of the leading coefficient
K_{r}

Combining all of the prefactors that we set aside earlier to make the discussion more manageable, the contribution to
K_{r}
from the index set
I
is then defined as

K_{i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}}=M(i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1})^{-1}\left(\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}i_{d}!\right)^{-1}\left(\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!\right)\sum_{\pi\in S_{r}}\biggl[\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}\binom{2i_{\pi(d)}}{d}\biggr]\det\!\bigl[n^{2i_{\pi(d)}-d}\bigr]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}

(65)

where we inserted the multiplicities
M(i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1})^{-1}
that were divided out earlier and the factorials from the time evolution
\left(\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}i_{d}!\right)^{-1}
to obtain the sum

K_{r}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i_{0}\leq i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{r-1}\\
\sum i_{j}=r(r-1)/2\end{subarray}}K_{i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}}=2^{r(r-1)/2}\prod_{k=1}^{r-1}k!>0

(66)

Proof.

The first step is to move to the unordered sum, accounting for the multiplicities appropriately

K_{r}=\frac{\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!}{r!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{r}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}\geq 0\\
\sum i_{j}=r(r-1)/2\end{subarray}}\left[\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}\binom{2i_{\pi(d)}}{d}/i_{\pi(d)}!\right]\det\!\left[n^{2i_{\pi(d)}-d}\right]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}

since every index tuple appears
r!M(i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1})^{-1}
more often in the unordered sum than in the ordered sum (since we sum over all index set permutations, these contributions are all equal). We can then use the multilinearity of the determinant to pull the prefactors and sum over
\{i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}\}
into the determinant

K_{r}=\frac{\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!}{r!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{r}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}\geq 0\\
\sum i_{j}=r(r-1)/2\end{subarray}}\det\!\left[\binom{2i_{\pi(d)}}{d}\frac{n^{2i_{\pi(d)}-d}}{i_{\pi(d)}!}\right]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}

We now drop the
\pi
-label in the sum and execute the outer permutation sum

K_{r}=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}\geq 0\\
\sum i_{j}=r(r-1)/2\end{subarray}}\det\!\left[\binom{2i_{d}}{d}\frac{n^{2i_{d}-d}}{i_{d}!}\right]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}

We would now like to pull the index sum into the determinant. But the constraint
\sum i_{j}=r(r-1)/2
prevents us from doing this. To get around this, define an ordinary generating function

G(\lambda)=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!\right)\sum_{i_{0},\dots,i_{r-1}\geq 0}\det\!\left[\binom{2i_{d}}{d}\frac{n^{2i_{d}-d}}{i_{d}!}\right]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}\lambda^{\sum i_{j}}

so that
K_{r}
can be extracted as the
r(r-1)/2
-order series coefficient. We can now use the multilinearity of the determinant

G(\lambda)=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!\right)\det\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{2k}{d}\frac{n^{2k-d}}{k!}\lambda^{k}\right]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!\right)\det\!\left[\frac{1}{d!}\derivative[d]{n}\mathrm{e}^{n^{2}\lambda}\right]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}

We now recognize that the argument of the determinant has the form

\frac{1}{d!}\derivative[d]{n}\mathrm{e}^{n^{2}\lambda}=\mathrm{e}^{n^{2}\lambda}\left[\frac{(2\lambda)^{d}}{d!}n^{d}+R(n)\right]

where
R(n)
is a lower degree polynomial in
n
. The lower-degree polynomial

does not contribute to the determinant by the standard Vandermonde argument. Then, by the usual Vandermonde determinant formula for polynomial entries (Eq. (
88
)), we obtain

G(\lambda)=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!\right)\det\!\left[\mathrm{e}^{n^{2}\lambda}\left[\frac{(2\lambda)^{d}}{d!}n^{d}+R(n)\right]\right]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}=\left(\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!\right)(2\lambda)^{r(r-1)/2}\exp\left(\lambda\frac{(2r-1)(r-1)r}{6}\right)

We can now read off, as the series coefficient
[\lambda^{r(r-1)/2}]
of

, the final result

K_{r}=2^{r(r-1)/2}\left(\prod_{m=0}^{r-1}m!\right)\geq 0

as claimed.
∎

E.6
Shifted Hankel minors
\overline{H}_{r}

Recalling the forward difference operator

(\Delta\bm{m}(x,\delta))_{k}\;:=\;m_{k+2}(x,\delta)-m_{k+1}(x,\delta),

(67)

with
k=0,1,\dots,N-2,
and the shifted Hankel matrix

\overline{H}_{r}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))=\bigl[(\Delta\bm{m}(x,\delta)_{i+j}\bigr]_{i,j=0}^{r-1}.

(68)

In principle, the entire argumentation for the Hankel matrix also works for the shifted Hankel matrix since the shift operator commutes with the time evolution

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{m}(x,\delta)

\displaystyle=\Delta\mathrm{e}^{\overline{\bm{M}}\delta}\bm{v}=\mathrm{e}^{\overline{\bm{M}}\delta}\Delta\bm{v}(x)

(69)

\displaystyle=x(1-x)\mathrm{e}^{\overline{\bm{M}}\delta}\bm{v}(x)=x(1-x)\bm{m}(x,\delta).

So we obtain an additional prefactor of
1-x
. In total, we then obtain

\displaystyle\det\overline{H}_{r}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))

\displaystyle=K_{r}x^{r^{2}}(1-x)^{r(r+1)/2}\delta^{r(r-1)/2}

(70)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{r(r-1)/2+1}\right)

with the same
K_{r}
as before — the shift contributes exactly a factor of
x(1-x)
, leading to
x^{r(r-1)}(1-x)^{r(r-1)/2}\rightarrow x^{r^{2}}(1-x)^{r(r+1)/2}
.

E.7
The points
x=1
and
x=0

At
x=0
nothing happens. All Hankel matrices are manifestly zero and will remain zero. For
x=1
the story is different. In principle, one also has to ensure that the first nonvanishing matrix elements at the point
x=1
are nonnegative. With the same strategy as before we can recognize that the first nonvanishing order in
x=1
is

\displaystyle\det\overline{H}_{r}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))

\displaystyle=K_{r}x^{r^{2}}(1-x)^{r(r+1)/2}\delta^{r(r-1)/2}

(71)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{r(r-1)/2+1}\right)

which is also manifestly positive. As such, we have two series, one that makes the exponential uniformly convergent on the interior points and one that makes it uniformly convergent at
x=1
. Together, this ensures uniform convergence of the positivity of the Hankel matrices.

E.8
Combining all orders: completion of the proof

We have shown explicitly in the linear order earlier that the defining inequalities for moment vectors on the interval
[0,1]
are fulfilled uniformly in
[0,1]
. Using the exact expansions for both the ordinary and shifted Hankel minors we derive

\displaystyle\det\overline{H}_{r}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))

\displaystyle=(1-x)^{r(r+1)/2}x^{r(r-1)}K_{r}\delta^{r(r-1)/2}

(72)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{r(r-1)/2+1}\right)

\displaystyle\det H_{r}(\bm{m}(x,\delta))

\displaystyle=(1-x)^{r(r-1)/2}x^{r(r-1)}K_{r}\delta^{r(r-1)/2}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{r(r-1)/2+1}\right),

with
K_{r}>0
(Compare with the examples in Eq. (
41
)). Therefore, there exists some
\delta>0
so that
\mathrm{e}^{\overline{\bm{M}}\delta}\bm{v}(x)
remains a valid moment vector. Since any moment vector is a convex combination of such
v(x)
we have thus proven that
\mathrm{e}^{\overline{\bm{M}}\delta}
maps valid moment vectors onto valid moment vectors. By composing small time ,steps we obtain conservation for all times. The uniformity of the bound in
x
implies the convergence.

Quantifying Entanglement via Moment Matrices

Beyond the analytical proof outlined above, the moment problem also yields a natural quantitative measure of entanglement, which corresponds to how much the conditions for the moment vector to be defined by a probability distribution on
[0,1]
are violated. Define the negativity of a matrix as minus the sum over the absolute values of the negative eigenvalues

\mathcal{N}(M)=-\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{S}(M),\lambda<0}\lambda,

(73)

where we denoted the spectrum of
M
as
\mathcal{S}(M)
. Then we define an entanglement measure of a population vector
\bm{p}
as

\mathcal{N}(\bm{p})=\mathcal{N}(H(\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{p}))+\mathcal{N}(\overline{H}(\bm{B}^{-1}\bm{p})).

(74)

This measure is guaranteed to be precisely zero when the state is separable and nonzero for entangled states.

Appendix G
Two-Particle Entanglement and Monotonicity

In this appendix, we will discuss bipartite entanglement, in particular the entanglement between two representative spins of the permutationally invariant state, and evaluate the negativity of the resulting density matrix. This will give us good intuition about the entanglement while the system evolves according to the rate equations given in the main text.

G.1
Two-emitter reduced state

Due to permutation symmetry, the reduced state of any two atoms is the same. For a given Dicke state
\ket{\Dicke{\ind{}}}{}
, the two-emitter reduced density matrix
\rho_{12}^{(k{})}
can be written in the basis
\{\ket{00},\ket{01},\ket{10},\ket{11}\}
as

\rho_{12}^{(m)}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}{}&0&0&0\\
0&b_{k}{}&b_{k}{}&0\\
0&b_{k}{}&b_{k}{}&0\\
0&0&0&d_{k}{}\end{pmatrix},

(75)

with coefficients

\displaystyle a_{k}{}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{N_g^{(1)}N_g^{(2)}}=\frac{(N-k{})(N-k{}-1)}{N(N-1)},

(76)

\displaystyle b_{k}{}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{N_g^{(1)}N_e^{(2)}}=\frac{k{}(N-k{})}{N(N-1)},

\displaystyle d_{k}{}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{N_e^{(1)}N_e^{(2)}}=\frac{k{}(k{}-1)}{N(N-1)}.

where we used the fact that emitter
1
and emitter
2
are representative for all emitters with
N_{e}^{i}=\sigma_{i}^{\dagger}\sigma^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{i}
and
N_{g}^{i}=\sigma^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{i}\sigma_{i}^{\dagger}
.

The reduced state for a statistical mixture
\rho(t)=\sum_{m}p_{m}(t)\ket{S,m}\bra{S,m}
is then given by

\rho_{12}(t)=\sum_{k}{}p_{k}{}(t)\,\rho_{12}^{(k{})}=\begin{pmatrix}A&0&0&0\\
0&B&B&0\\
0&B&B&0\\
0&0&0&D\end{pmatrix},

(77)

where
A=\sum_{k}{}p_{k}{}a_{k}{}
,
B=\sum_{k}{}p_{k}{}b_{k}{}
, and
D=\sum_{k}{}p_{k}{}d_{k}{}
. This matrix retains the same block-diagonal structure and depends only on the populations
p_{k}{}(t)
.

G.2
Negativity

To quantify two-emitter entanglement, we consider the negativity
\mathcal{N}_{2}(t)
of the reduced state
\rho_{12}(t)
, defined as

\mathcal{N}_{2}(t)=\frac{\norm{\rho_{12}^{T_2}(t)}_{1}-1}{2},

(78)

where
\rho_{12}^{T_{2}}(t)
denotes the partial transpose with respect to the second emitter, and
\norm{\cdot}_{1}
is the trace norm.

For a state of the form Eq. (
77
), the partial transpose reads

\rho_{12}^{T_{2}}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}A&0&0&B\\
0&B&0&0\\
0&0&B&0\\
B&0&0&D\end{pmatrix}.

(79)

The eigenvalues of
\rho_{12}^{T_{2}}(t)
are
B
,
B
, and

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{A+D}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(A-D)^{2}+4B^{2}}.

(80)

The only possibly negative eigenvalue is
\lambda_{-}
, so the negativity simplifies to

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{2}(t)&=\max\left\{0,-\lambda_{-}\right\}\\
&=\max\left\{0,\frac{\sqrt{(A-D)^{2}+4B^{2}}-(A+D)}{2}\right\}.\end{split}

(81)

G.3
Monotonicity

Consider the possibly negative eigenvalue

-\lambda_{-}=\frac{\sqrt{(A-D)^{2}+4B^{2}}-(A+D)}{2}

(82)

then requiring that
\lambda_{-}<0
is equivalent to
A(t)D(t)-B(t)^{2}<0
. Thus, it is sufficient to study the sign of

\Delta(t)=A(t)D(t)-B(t)^{2}

(83)

to decide whether a state has finite negativity or not. Indeed, using the rate equations we show later that
\dot{\Delta}(t)\leq 0
, implying that the negativity decreases monotonically over time.

G.4
Monotonicity of
\Delta

Using the Dicke rate equations, we compute

\displaystyle\dot{A}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k{}=0}^{N}h_{k}{}\,p_{k}{}(t)\,[a_{k{}-1}-a_{k}{}],

\displaystyle\dot{B}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k{}=0}^{N}h_{k}{}\,p_{k}{}(t)\,[b_{k{}-1}-b_{k}{}],

\displaystyle\dot{D}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k{}=0}^{N}h_{k}{}\,p_{k}{}(t)\,[d_{k{}-1}-d_{k}{}].

Note that the
k{}=0
term vanishes, so we formally keep it although there is no physical meaning for
a_{-1},b_{-1},d_{-1}
since
h_{0}=0
.

From the explicit expressions

\displaystyle a_{k{}-1}-a_{k}{}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(N-k{})}{N(N-1)},

\displaystyle b_{k{}-1}-b_{k}{}

\displaystyle=\frac{2k{}-N-1}{N(N-1)},

\displaystyle d_{k{}-1}-d_{k}{}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2(k{}-1)}{N(N-1)},

we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{A}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\sum_{k}{}h_{k}{}p_{k}{}(N-k{}),

\displaystyle\dot{B}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{k}{}h_{k}{}p_{k}{}(2k{}-N-1),

\displaystyle\dot{D}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{N(N-1)}\sum_{k}{}h_{k}{}p_{k}{}(k{}-1).

The equation of motion for
\Delta(t)
then becomes

\derivative{\Delta}{t}=2B\dot{B}-A\dot{D}-D\dot{A},

(84)

where we can use the explicit representations for
A(t),B(t)
and
D(t)
to find

\begin{split}&{}N^{2}(N-1)^{2}\derivative{\Delta}{t}\\
&=\sum_{k{},k{}^{\prime}=0}^{N}p_{k{}^{\prime}}p_{k}{}[2k{}(N-1)(k{}-N+1)\\
&{}\hphantom{\sum_{k{},k{}^{\prime}=0}^{N}}\times(k{}^{\prime}(N-1)+(1-k{})N)]\\
&\leq 2N(N-1)\sum_{k{},k{}^{\prime}=0}^{N}p_{k{}^{\prime}}p_{k}{}\left[(k{}^{\prime}(N-1)+(1-k{})N)\right]\\
&=2N(N-1)\sum_{k{}=0}^{N}p_{k}{}\left[(k{}(N-1)+(1-k{})N)\right]\\
&=2N(N-1)\sum_{k{}=0}^{N}p_{k}{}(k{}-N)\leq 0.\end{split}

(85)

G.6
Calculation of reduced N spin density matrices for Dicke superradiance

Here we want to give a short outline on how the
m
-spin density matrix can be calculated for
N>m
spins which are in a mixture of Dicke states. We take inspiration from Refs.
[
6
,
42
]
and interpret this tracing procedure as a quantum jump that occurs due to particle loss from the system. The exact matrix elements of this operator in the Dicke basis are given in these references.

We state the tracing procedure in terms of states
\ket{N,S,m}
for particle number
N
, spin sector
S
, and magnetic quantum number
m
. The nonzero matrix elements of the particle loss superoperator
\mathcal{L}_{p}
starting from the fully symmetric subspace (which we always have) are precisely

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{p}\ket{N,S,m}\bra{N,S,m}&=\lambda_{\downarrow,N,S,M}\outerproduct{\downarrow}{\downarrow}\\
&+\lambda_{\uparrow,N,S,M}\outerproduct{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\end{split}

with

\displaystyle\lambda_{\downarrow,N,S,M}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(N/2+S+1\right)(S-M)}{N(2S+1)},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\uparrow,N,S,M}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(N/2+S+1\right)(S+M)}{N(2S+1)},

\displaystyle\ket{\downarrow}

\displaystyle=\ket{N-1,S-1/2,m-1/2},

\displaystyle\ket{\uparrow}

\displaystyle=\ket{N-1,S-1/2,m+1/2}.

The

-particle density matrix can be obtained by applying

Appendix H
Review of determinant algebra

Throughout,
\sigma\in S_{r}
denotes a permutation of
\{0,\dots,r-1\}
.

A.1 Leibniz formula

\det A\;=\;\sum_{\sigma\in S_{r}}\!\sgn(\sigma)\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}A_{d,\sigma(d)}.

A.2 Multilinearity.

For any scalars
c_{d}
and column vectors
v_{d}
,

\det[c_{0}v_{0},\dots,c_{r-1}v_{r-1}]\;=\;\Bigl(\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}c_{d}\Bigr)\det[v_{0},\dots,v_{r-1}].

A.3 Vandermonde determinant.

For any distinct numbers
x_{0},\dots,x_{r-1}
,

\det[x_{n}^{\,d}]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}\;=\;\prod_{0\leq d<n\leq r-1}(x_{n}-x_{d}).

Corollary (equally spaced nodes).

With
x_{n}=n
one obtains the closed form

\det[n^{\,d}]_{d,n=0}^{r-1}\;=\;\prod_{0\leq d<n\leq r-1}(n-d)\;=\;\prod_{k=1}^{r-1}k!

(87)

A.4 Triangular–Vandermonde factorisation.

If each row is a degree-
\leq d
polynomial,

A_{d,n}=P_{d}(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{d,k}n^{k}
,
then

A=CV,\qquad C_{d,k}=a_{d,k}\ (\text{lower-triangular}),\quad V_{k,n}=n^{k},

so

\det A=\Bigl(\prod_{d=0}^{r-1}a_{d,d}\Bigr)\prod_{0\leq d<n\leq r-1}(n-d).

(88)

Notably, only the prefactors of the highest-degree monomial contribute, and the result vanishes when the degree of the polynomial is smaller than
d
.
