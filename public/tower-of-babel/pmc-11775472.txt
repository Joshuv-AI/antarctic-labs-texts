Inferring the genetic relationships between unsupervised deep learning–derived imaging phenotypes and glioblastoma through multi-omics approaches
Authors: Liguo Ye, Cheng Ye, Pengtao Li, Yu Wang, Wenbin Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the genetic association between glioblastoma (GBM) and unsupervised deep learning–derived imaging phenotypes (UDIPs). We employed a combination of genome-wide association study (GWAS) data, single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq), and scPagwas (pathway-based polygenic regression framework) methods to explore the genetic links between UDIPs and GBM. Two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses were conducted to identify causal relationships between UDIPs and GBM. Colocalization analysis was performed to validate genetic associations, while scPagwas analysis was used to evaluate the relevance of key UDIPs to GBM at the cellular level. Among 512 UDIPs tested, 23 were found to have significant causal associations with GBM. Notably, UDIPs such as T1-33 (OR = 1.007, 95% CI = 1.001 to 1.012, P = .022), T1-34 (OR = 1.012, 95% CI = 1.001–1.023, P = .028), and T1-96 (OR = 1.009, 95% CI = 1.001–1.019, P = .046) were found to have a genetic association with GBM. Furthermore, T1-34 and T1-96 were significantly associated with GBM recurrence, with P-values < .0001 and P < .001, respectively. In addition, scPagwas analysis revealed that T1-33, T1-34, and T1-96 are distinctively linked to different GBM subtypes, with T1-33 showing strong associations with the neural progenitor-like subtype (NPC2), T1-34 with mesenchymal (MES2) and neural progenitor (NPC1) cells, and T1-96 with the NPC2 subtype. T1-33, T1-34, and T1-96 hold significant potential for predicting tumor recurrence and aiding in the development of personalized GBM treatment strategies.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive and common form of primary malignant brain tumor [1], accounting for ~45% of all malignant brain cancers [2]. Despite advancements in treatment approaches, including surgical resection, radiation, and chemotherapy, the prognosis for patients with GBM remains dismal, with a median survival time of 12–18 months [3]. The brain is a highly organized and complex structure, composed of distinct anatomical regions that control various functions. GBM often disrupts this organization, leading to profound alterations in brain structure and function [4]. The tumor’s rapid proliferation and infiltration can cause significant changes in brain anatomy, including the displacement of normal brain tissues, distortion of cortical and subcortical structures, and increased intracranial pressure [5]. The relationship between GBM and brain structure is not purely mechanical [6], however. There is growing evidence that the structural changes induced by GBM are intricately linked to its molecular and genetic characteristics [7, 8]. For instance, different molecular subtypes of GBM may preferentially invade certain brain regions or exhibit distinct patterns of structural disruption [9, 10]. Understanding these relationships is crucial for improving GBM diagnosis and treatment.

In parallel, imaging technologies—particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—have become an essential tool for the detection, characterization, and longitudinal monitoring of GBM. Traditional MRI metrics, such as tumor size, location, contrast enhancement patterns, and edema, provide valuable diagnostic information and guide treatment decisions [11, 12]. However, these conventional imaging features often fail to capture the full complexity of GBM’s microenvironment and its genetic landscape. As a result, there is a growing interest in leveraging advanced imaging biomarkers that can link subtle phenotypic patterns in brain structure to underlying genetic variations [13, 14]. For example, imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) have been used to connect neuroimaging data with genetic risk variants and molecular profiles of brain disorders [15]. More recently, the introduction of unsupervised deep imaging phenotypes (UDIPs) has expanded the potential of this approach. UDIPs are derived from 3D convolutional autoencoder models and provide a high-dimensional yet interpretable representation of brain morphology, texture, and volumetric attributes, beyond what traditional IDPs can discern [16]. Importantly, UDIPs have shown higher heritability than many conventional imaging metrics, suggesting that they more directly reflect underlying genetic influences on brain structure [16]. This makes UDIPs particularly promising in the context of GBM, where subtle, early-stage changes in the tumor and its surrounding tissue may hold critical insights into disease initiation, progression, and recurrence. Conventional imaging approaches can miss these incipient alterations, especially when the blood–brain barrier has not yet been disrupted and contrast-enhancing lesions are not visible. By contrast, UDIPs can detect nuanced brain changes associated with genetic variants linked to GBM, thereby enabling earlier and more refined characterization of the tumor’s evolutionary trajectory.

Previous studies have demonstrated the importance of imaging biomarkers in GBM diagnosis and prognosis [17, 18]; most of these investigations have not integrated imaging data with single-cell transcriptomic information [19]. This lack of integration makes it difficult to correlate UDIPs with specific cell types and genetic subtypes of GBM, limiting the potential to use UDIPs as precision biomarkers for tumor classification and targeted therapies. By leveraging multiple analytical frameworks—Mendelian randomization (MR) [20], single-nuclear RNA-seq [21], and the scPagwas [22] method—this study addresses the previously unmet need to integrate imaging data with cellular-level genetic information. MR uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer potential causal relationships between exposures (e.g. UDIPs) and outcomes (e.g. GBM), reducing confounding and reverse causation. Meanwhile, snRNA-seq captures transcriptomic profiles at single-cell resolution, revealing the cellular heterogeneity inherent in GBM tumors. The scPagwas approach, a pathway-based polygenic regression framework, further bridges the gap between GWAS findings and single-cell data by projecting trait-associated genetic variation onto cellular pathways, yielding trait-relevant scores (TRSs) that quantify how strongly each cell population’s expression profile is linked to UDIP-related genetic effects. We projected GBM GWAS data onto single-cell transcriptomes to evaluate the TRS of specific UDIPs. This approach allowed us to identify distinct associations between UDIPs and different GBM subtypes, providing a relatively comprehensive view of how imaging traits correlate with the cellular architecture of the tumor. Such integration of imaging and cellular data opens up new possibilities for using UDIPs to predict not only disease risk but also the cellular subtype of GBM. This study also investigates the differences between primary and recurrent GBM. By evaluating the TRS of specific UDIPs in both primary and recurrent GBM, we demonstrated the potential of UDIPs not only for early-diagnosing GBM but also for predicting tumor recurrence, which is critical for effective treatment planning and monitoring for GBM.

Material and methods

Data sources

A summary of the data sources utilized in this study is provided in Table 1. Genetic data were obtained from the FinnGen R11 consortium, encompassing a large-scale GWAS dataset of 346 582 participants focusing on genetic variants associated with isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) wild type GBM. These GWAS data underwent rigorous quality control in their original processing pipelines, including filtering for variants based on minor allele frequency, imputation quality (e.g. imputation quality score > 0.8), and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P > 1 × 10^(−6)). Variants failing these criteria or exhibiting substantial missingness were excluded from subsequent analyses. In addition, we incorporated 512 UDIPs from the GWAS Catalog, representing T1- and T2-weighted MRI-based imaging traits. The discovery cohort for UDIPs consisted of 22 880 participants, and replication cohorts included 12 359 participants for T1-weighted and 11 265 for T2-weighted images [16]. For the single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data, we accessed the publicly available GSE174554 dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). We selected three primary and three matched recurrent GBM tumors from female patients of approximate age who received standard-of-care therapy (temozolomide, radiotherapy, and surgical resection) [23]. This selection allowed us to control for potential confounding variables—such as age, sex, and treatment regimen.

Two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis

Two-sample MR was performed using R package “MRCIEU/TwoSampleMR” [24] (version 0.5.7). MR is a genetic epidemiological approach that leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between an exposure and an outcome, effectively mimicking randomized controlled trials by using the random allocation of alleles at conception. The key principles underlying MR include three core assumptions: (i) the IVs are robustly associated with the exposure, (ii) the IVs are independent of any confounders that influence both the exposure and the outcome, and (iii) the IVs affect the outcome solely through the exposure, rather than through alternative biological pathways [25]. The typical MR workflow involves selecting suitable IVs from GWAS data, clumping these variants to ensure independence, applying one or more estimation methods—most commonly the inverse variance weighted (IVW) estimator as the primary approach—and performing sensitivity analyses, such as MR-Egger and weighted median methods, to detect and mitigate horizontal pleiotropy [26, 27]. In this study, we initially identified genetic variants robustly associated with UDIPs, which served as our exposures, and then tested their causal impact on GBM using two-sample MR. By employing multiple MR methods, assessing heterogeneity with Cochran’s Q-statistic, and correcting for multiple comparisons through false discovery rate (FDR) control, we ensured that any inferred causal links between UDIPs and GBM were both statistically sound and robust to potential violations of MR assumptions.

Colocalization analysis

The colocalization analysis was performed through the R “coloc” [28] package. Colocalization aims to determine whether two traits are influenced by the same genetic variant(s) in a given genomic region. The coloc Bayesian colocalization [29] method, which calculates the posterior probability (PP) of five possible hypotheses: PP.H0: no association with either trait; PP.H1: association with the first trait only (e.g. UDIP); PP.H2: association with the second trait only (e.g. GBM); PP.H3: association with both traits, but driven by different causal variants; and PP.H4: association with both traits, driven by the same causal variant. The primary focus of our analysis was the posterior probability of colocalization (PP.H4), which indicates a shared genetic basis between UDIPs and GBM. A PP.H4 value greater than 0.80 was considered strong evidence of colocalization.

Analysis of glioblastoma-related unsupervised deep learning–derived imaging phenotype associations with cortical and subcortical regions

We utilized Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K-S) statistic values obtained from the original creators of the UDIPs to investigate associations between GBM-linked UDIPs and specific brain regions [16]. The analysis covered a range of anatomical regions defined in the Harvard–Oxford Cortical and Subcortical Atlas. We began by mapping the K-S statistic values to different brain regions, distinguishing between regions that were positively correlated with GBM and those that were negatively correlated. Regions with higher K-S scores were considered more strongly represented by the corresponding UDIPs.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing analysis

To investigate cellular heterogeneity and genomic alterations in three paired primary and recurrent GBM samples, we performed quality control (QC) to remove low-quality cells and technical artifacts. QC metrics included the number of detected genes per cell (nFeature_RNA), the total unique molecular counts (nCount_RNA), and the proportion of mitochondrial (percent.mt) and ribosomal (percent.rb) transcripts. Cells with unusually high or low RNA counts or elevated mitochondrial content were excluded. After QC, we normalized gene expression data using the Seurat NormalizeData() function in the “Seurat” package [30], which applies a log-normalization method to account for differences in sequencing depth. For dimensionality reduction, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on the normalized expression matrix. The number of principal components (PCs) retained was determined by examining an elbow plot and the variance explained by each PC; we selected suitable PCs that collectively captured the most biologically meaningful variation without introducing excessive noise. Using these PCs, we conducted unsupervised clustering at a resolution of 0.3 and visualized cell populations via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). To annotate cell types within each cluster, we employed “SingleR” [31], a reference-based tool that compares individual cell profiles against well-characterized reference datasets. Specifically, we utilized the Human Primary Cell Atlas and Blueprint-Encode reference panels to assign cell identities, ensuring consistent and biologically informed annotations. To identify putative malignant cells, we leveraged “inferCNV” [32] for copy number variation (CNV) analysis, which revealed characteristic GBM genomic alterations such as chromosome 7 amplification and chromosome 10 deletion [2]. Finally, we performed single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA [33]) to evaluate the enrichment of established GBM subtype signatures (astrocyte-like [AC], oligodendrocyte progenitor-like [OPC], neural progenitor-like [NPC1/2], and mesenchymal-like [MES1/2]) [34].

Single-cell phenotype–associated genome-wide association study analysis

scPagwas is a pathway-based polygenic regression framework designed to integrate GWAS summary data with single-cell transcriptomic profiles, thereby uncovering the cellular and pathway-level mechanisms underlying complex genetic traits [22]. This approach builds on the premise that Single-Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) level genetic associations, which collectively shape phenotypic variance, can be linked to coherent sets of genes acting within a given biological pathway. By correlating SNP effect sizes from GWASs with the expression patterns of corresponding pathway genes at single-cell resolution, scPagwas provides a cell-specific measure of the pathway’s genetic relevance. To quantify pathway activity, scPagwas transforms the normalized gene-by-cell expression matrix into a pathway activity score (PAS)-by-cell matrix, where each PAS is derived by extracting the first principal component (PC1) of all genes constituting a given pathway. This dimensionality reduction step enhances the robustness and interpretability of the gene expression data, capturing the principal source of variation in each pathway’s transcriptional profile. The resulting regression coefficient, reflecting the strength of association between genetic effect sizes and pathway-specific expression, can be interpreted as a “pathway-level heritability contribution” in single cells. In our investigation, we utilized the “scPagwas” R package to explore the relationships between genetic variants, UDIPs, and their relevance to GBM at the single-cell level. The scPagwas method integrates GWAS data with snRNA-seq data to evaluate the trait-relevant score (TRS), which reflects the association between individual cells and genetic traits. We focused on calculating TRS values to quantify how strongly each cell’s expression profile was linked to GBM and UDIP-related genetic variants.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software. For basic comparisons of continuous variables between two groups, we employed Student’s t-tests (paired or unpaired, as appropriate), while comparisons across three or more groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc tests where indicated. Unless otherwise noted, statistical significance was defined as P < .05, and, to account for multiple comparisons, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the FDR.

Results

Overview of this study

This study investigates the causal relationships between UDIPs and GBM, utilizing a Mendelian randomization framework to explore genetic associations. We aim to identify specific UDIPs linked to GBM, analyze the colocalization of these phenotypes with GBM-associated genetic regions, and assess the relevance of UDIPs to distinct GBM cellular subtypes using single-nuclear transcriptomic data. Additionally, we evaluate the TRS of key UDIPs through the scPagwas algorithm in various GBM cell populations to uncover differential associations across GBM subtypes, providing insights into the role of imaging-derived phenotypes in GBM progression and heterogeneity (as shown in Fig. 1).

Genetic relationships among brain unsupervised deep learning–derived imaging phenotypes and glioblastoma

To explore the genetic relationships between UDIPs and GBM by leveraging an MR framework. Specifically, we investigate the impact of genetic variants on UDIPs and their association with GBM. To this end, we use 512 UDIPs as exposure factors in a two-sample MR analysis, seeking to establish causal links between these imaging-derived phenotypes and GBM risk. We further explore the reverse direction, examining whether genetic determinants of GBM influence these imaging phenotypes. In Fig. 2A, the two-sample MR analysis results are summarized. Here, 512 UDIPs serve as the exposure, and GBM is the outcome. The analysis, performed using the IVW method, identified multiple significant UDIPs. The results highlight associations with P-values and FDR below significance thresholds, indicating that specific UDIPs are causally linked to GBM risk (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). The MR analysis identified several UDIPs significantly associated with GBM. Notably, UDIPs such as T1-19 (OR = 1.008, 95% CI = 1.001–1.015, P = .021), T1-33 (OR = 1.007, 95% CI = 1.001–1.012, P = .022), and T1-34 (OR = 1.012, 95% CI = 1.001–1.023, P = .028) were found to have a positive association with GBM, while T1-33_2 (OR = 0.983, 95% CI = 0.969–0.997, P = .018) and T1-73 (OR = 0.982, 95% CI = 0.966–0.999, P = 0.040) showed protective effects. Furthermore, we conducted tests for heterogeneity (Supplementary Table S3) and horizontal pleiotropy (Supplementary Table S4), with no significant evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity detected. Figure 2B presents the results from the reverse MR analysis, where GBM was treated as the exposure and UDIPs as the outcomes. Significant associations were detected using the IVW method, and other MR methods such as MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode were also employed to cross-validate the findings. GBM was found to influence UDIPs, with significant results for T1-33 (OR = 0.175, 95% CI = 0.043–0.705, P = .014), T1-34 (OR = 0.183, 95% CI = 0.035–0.954, P = .044), and T1-96 (OR = 0.119, 95% CI = 0.022–0.651, P = .014), demonstrating a reverse causal relationship between GBM and these UDIPs. Details of the reverse MR analysis are provided in Supplementary Table S5, with heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests reported in Supplementary Tables S6 and S7. Similar to the forward MR, no substantial pleiotropy or heterogeneity was identified, reinforcing the validity of the results. We successfully identified UDIPs that are significantly associated with GBM through MR analysis.

Single-nucleus transcriptomic characterization and clustering of primary and recurrent glioblastoma populations

Using paired primary and recurrent GBM samples, we leveraged snRNA-seq data to characterize and identify distinct cellular subpopulations and genomic alterations within the tumor microenvironment. Specifically, we aimed to annotate these subpopulations, identify tumor-specific clusters, and project genomic associations from GBM GWAS onto single cells for functional characterization. We first performed quality control and filtering of snRNA-seq data across multiple samples from paired primary and recurrent GBM tissues. As shown in Fig. S1A, the nFeature_RNA, nCount_RNA, and percent of mitochondrial and ribosomal reads were consistent across samples, indicating that the data passed basic quality control checks. In Fig. 5A, dimensionality reduction was applied to cells from different samples, and the distribution of cells in the reduced-dimensional space reflects their sample origin. Cells derived from primary and recurrent GBM tumors demonstrate a certain degree of separation, indicating distinct transcriptional profiles (Fig. 5B). Following principal component analysis, we selected seven principal components for downstream analysis (Fig. S1B–D), with a clustering resolution of 0.3 to identify distinct cell populations (Fig. S1E). This led to the identification of 17 clusters (0–16) that were visualized using t-SNE dimensionality reduction (Fig. 5C). To annotate these clusters, we performed singleR-based analysis (Fig. S2), which provided initial insights into potential cell identities within each cluster. Clusters were further characterized by integrating “inferCNV” and “scPagwas” analyses. In Fig. S3A, CNV analysis using inferCNV highlighted clusters with genomic alterations characteristic of GBM, such as chromosome 7 amplification and chromosome 10 loss. These alterations suggested that Clusters 0, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, and 15 could represent malignant GBM cells. Complementing this, scPagwas analysis projected GBM GWAS data onto single cells, revealing that Clusters 1 and 3 were largely unrelated to GBM (Fig. S3B and C). To further classify the tumor clusters, we conducted ssGSEA based on GBM subtype signatures (AC, OPC, NPC1/2, MES1/2, Table S12). As shown in Fig. 5D, the majority of clusters exhibited varying degrees of association with distinct GBM subtypes. Clusters 0, 1, 2, 5, and 9 showed strong associations with the AC, MES1/2, and NPC subtypes. Finally, we annotated the clusters into well-known GBM subtypes, including astrocyte-like (AC), oligodendrocyte progenitor-like (OPC), neural progenitor-like (NPC), and mesenchymal-like (MES) cells (Fig. 5E, Table S13). We excluded immune-related clusters (4, 10, 14, and 16) from further analysis to focus on the tumor-specific clusters (Fig. 5F), facilitating subsequent investigation of the correlation between UDIPs and different GBM subtypes. By integrating CNV, GWAS data, and transcriptomic profiling, we identified tumor-specific clusters and classified them into known GBM subtypes.

Discussion

Previous research has established the value of imaging biomarkers in assessing the progression and prognosis of GBM [35], demonstrating that brain imaging phenotypes derived from MRI and other neuroimaging techniques can serve as valuable predictors of disease risk [36], that have focused on structural and functional alterations in GBM [37, 38]. However, unlike previous studies that primarily focused on association studies [39, 40], our work is one of the first to apply the multi-omics framework to identify associations between UDIPs and GBM. By utilizing an MR framework and integrating scPagwas analysis, we were able to identify specific UDIPs that are causally linked to GBM risk and further explore their associations with different GBM cellular subtypes. Our MR analysis revealed that 23 UDIPs among 512 phenotypes are significantly associated with GBM. Notably, imaging features such as T1-19, T1-33, and T1-34 demonstrated a positive correlation with GBM risk, whereas UDIPs such as T1-33_2 and T1-73 appeared to have negative association with GBM. These results underscore the potential for UDIPs as imaging biomarkers of GBM risk. Through the application of scPagwas [22], we identified that UDIPs such as T1-34 and T1-96 are more strongly associated with primary GBM. This highlights the distinct correlation of these UDIPs with GBM progression and underscores the importance of dissecting the cellular heterogeneity of GBM in order to better understand the functional relevance of UDIPs. Lastly, we demonstrated that UDIPs exhibit distinct correlations with various GBM cellular subtypes. For instance, T1-33 and T1-96 were strongly associated with the NPC2 subtype, whereas T1-34 exhibited strong associations with mesenchymal (MES2) and neural progenitor (NPC1) cells.

This study also conducted a colocalization analysis to examine the relationship between specific UDIPs and GBM within key genomic regions. Notably, T1-96 showed a strong association with the TERT region, while T2-121 was linked to the TP53 region, suggesting a genetic connection between these imaging phenotypes and GBM-related loci. These regions, particularly TERT and TP53, have been consistently implicated in GBM pathology in previous research [41, 42]. The significant colocalization of these UDIPs with TERT and TP53 highlights the potential of brain imaging phenotypes as genetic endophenotypes, explaining the underlying genetic mechanisms that link these UDIPs with GBM. The identification of colocalization between UDIPs and GBM-associated genetic loci carries several important implications. In addition, the specific anatomical regions identified—such as the frontal operculum cortex, paracingulate gyrus, and frontal pole—emerge as critical areas for further investigation into the mechanisms and clinical manifestations associated with GBM.

Our findings suggest that UDIPs, especially T1-34 and T1-96, hold promise as imaging biomarkers for differentiating primary from recurrent GBM, thereby strengthening existing diagnostic criteria [43]. Integrating these UDIPs into current imaging protocols could allow earlier and more accurate predictions of disease recurrence, enabling clinicians to intervene promptly. For instance, incorporating UDIPs into routine MRI assessments might complement traditional molecular markers—such as MGMT promoter methylation or IDH mutations [2, 44, 45]—by providing real-time, noninvasive insights into the tumor’s underlying genetic makeup and cellular composition. This subtype-specific imaging signature could be used alongside histopathological and molecular evaluations, allowing clinicians to identify patients with distinct tumor biology at the outset. By tailoring therapies to the predominant cellular subtypes indicated by UDIPs, oncologists may improve the selection of targeted agents or radiation protocols that align with the tumor’s unique phenotype. Furthermore, as recurrent GBM remains challenging to treat, the early identification of subtype-specific imaging markers may guide closer surveillance or prompt adjunctive therapies aimed at preempting relapse. Beyond initial diagnosis and prognosis, these UDIPs could also be integrated into longitudinal monitoring. Incorporating UDIP-based evaluations into follow-up imaging sessions would facilitate the early detection of subtle tumor changes that precede clinical or radiographic signs of relapse. Such proactive surveillance could, in turn, inform timely therapeutic adjustments, including dose modifications, the introduction of combination therapies, or enrollment in clinical trials targeting specific tumor subtypes. Compared to conventional imaging features—often limited to gross morphological characteristics—UDIPs offer a biologically informed lens through which to view GBM heterogeneity. Future research should focus on validating UDIPs in larger, ethnically diverse cohorts, establishing standardized imaging protocols for UDIP extraction, and integrating these biomarkers into prospective clinical trials. Additionally, investigating how UDIPs correlate with therapy response or resistance patterns could help refine treatment selection and develop precision-guided approaches to GBM management. By addressing these steps, UDIPs may ultimately become integral components of GBM patient care, from initial diagnosis through personalized treatment planning and long-term monitoring, thereby advancing the precision medicine paradigm in neuro-oncology.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the relatively small number of matched primary and recurrent GBM samples used for snRNA-seq may not fully capture the complexity of the tumor’s cellular landscape, potentially limiting the generalizability and interpretability of our findings. Second, the nucleus-based approach may underrepresent certain cytoplasmic transcripts or cell populations, introducing biases and reducing the sensitivity compared to conventional single-cell methods. Additionally, while our cross-sectional analysis offers an initial glimpse into the genetic and imaging correlates of GBM, future research should validate and refine these findings in larger, multi-institutional cohorts with diverse patient populations to confirm the generalizability of UDIPs. Functional validation using patient-derived organoids or CRISPR-based gene editing would clarify whether modifying UDIP-linked genes affects GBM growth or treatment response. Lastly, longitudinal MRI assessments can track UDIP changes over time, informing precision medicine strategies for GBM.

In conclusion, this study specific UDIPs (T1-33, T1-34, and T1-96) that are genetically linked to GBM and correlated with distinct cellular subtypes. These findings underscore the diagnostic value of UDIPs in stratifying GBM subtypes and their potential to predict tumor recurrence, advancing the development of personalized medicine approaches in GBM management.
