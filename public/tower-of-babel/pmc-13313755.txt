Body roundness index and the incidence of kidney disease: A prospective cohort study from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study
Authors: Xixi Yu, Zihao Shen, Xiaofang Li, Mingcheng Liu, Xin Liu, Yincong Luo, Ying Wang, Yirong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kidney disease (KD) poses a growing global health burden, with obesity contributing to its development. The body roundness index (BRI), a novel anthropometric measure reflecting visceral adiposity, may better predict KD risk than body mass index, but longitudinal evidence is limited. In this prospective cohort study, we analyzed 8401 adults aged ≥ 45 years from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, free of KD at baseline. BRI was calculated from waist circumference and height. Incident KD was ascertained via physician-diagnosed questionnaires during 4 follow-ups (2013–2020). Cox proportional-hazards models were used to compute hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), with adjustment for multiple covariates. Dose–response relationships were evaluated using restricted cubic splines and 2-piecewise linear regression, with subgroup analyses performed to examine effect modification. During follow-up, higher BRI was significantly associated with an increased risk of incident KD (HR = 1.10; 95% CI: 1.02–1.19; P = .017). Participants in the highest BRI quartile had a 47% greater risk compared with the lowest quartile (HR = 1.47; 95% CI: 1.06–2.05; P = .022), with a significant dose–response trend (P for trend = .014). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant overall association (P overall = .037) without evidence of nonlinearity (P nonlinearity = .307) and a meaningful inflection point (P for likelihood ratio test = 0.378). Subgroup analyses demonstrated generally consistent associations, with a significant interaction observed for dyslipidemia status (P for interaction = .037). In middle-aged and older Chinese adults, higher BRI independently predicts incident KD in a linear dose–response manner. BRI, as a simple and noninvasive measure of body fat distribution, may serve as a practical tool for early identification of individuals at elevated KD risk, supporting strategies targeting central adiposity in KD prevention.

1. Introduction

Kidney disease (KD) has emerged as a major global public health challenge. By 2021, more than 850 million people worldwide were affected by KD, imposing a substantial clinical and economic burden on health-care systems. Beyond its high prevalence, KD is associated with increased morbidity, disability, and mortality, and it has been projected to become the fifth leading cause of years of life lost by 2040.[1] In recognition of its rapidly expanding global impact, the World Health Organization in 2025 further emphasized kidney disease as a growing global public health issue requiring strengthened prevention and control strategies.

Obesity, a multifactorial condition affecting more than 1.9 billion adults worldwide, contributes substantially to the global burden of chronic metabolic disease.[2] Accumulating evidence suggests that obesity may play a causal role in the development and progression of KD through mechanisms involving lipotoxicity, chronic inflammation, and insulin resistance.[3] Meanwhile, more than two-thirds of individuals with chronic kidney disease are overweight or obese, highlighting the close relationship between obesity and KD.[4] However, reliance on body mass index (BMI) – the most widely used metric of obesity – has yielded paradoxical findings, including observations of improved survival among overweight and obese CKD patients.[5,6] This may stem from BMI’s inability to distinguish between fat and lean mass or to capture body fat distribution accurately.[7] A recent Lancet Commission also highlighted concerns that the current BMI-based measures of obesity can both underestimate and overestimate adiposity and provide inadequate information.[8] These reports underscore the need for alternative anthropometric indices that better capture the relationship between body composition and KD risks.

The body roundness index (BRI), calculated from waist circumference and height, has emerged as a promising anthropometric measure that better reflects visceral adiposity and global body fat distribution than BMI or waist circumference alone.[9] Prospective studies have linked elevated BRI with an increased risk of hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome across diverse populations.[10-12] However, longitudinal evidence directly evaluating the association between BRI and incident KD remains scarce, and the shape of the dose–response relationship and potential effect modification are not well characterized. Therefore, in this large, nationally representative prospective cohort, we aimed to investigate the association between BRI and incident KD and provide evidence for identifying modifiable risk factors for KD.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data availability statement

All data are publicly available, on research approval access, from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) on the website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en).

2.2. Study framework and participant details

The study participants were middle-aged and older adults from the CHARLS, an ongoing, nationally representative, prospective, longitudinal, population-based study in China. Details of the study design have been published elsewhere.[13] Briefly, 17,708 participants from 10,257 households recruited from 28 provinces within China were included at baseline (2011–2012, wave 1) using a multistage stratified probability proportional-to-size sampling technique. CHARLS participants were followed up every 2 years using a face-to-face computer-assisted personal interview. All physicians who participated in the study were trained at Peking University by CHARLS staff members.[13] Four subsequent follow-ups were carried out from 2013 to 2014 (wave 2), 2015 to 2016 (wave 3), 2017 to 2018 (wave 4), and 2019 to 2020 (wave 5) among survivors. Ethical approval for all the CHARLS waves was granted from the Institutional Review Board at Peking University. The Institutional Review Board approval number for the main household survey, including anthropometrics, is IRB00001052-11015; the IRB approval number for biomarker collection was IRB00001052-11014.

Initially, 17,708 participants were enrolled. Of these, 4336 individuals were excluded, including 266 respondents aged < 45 years and 4070 respondents with missing BRI data, leaving 13,372 participants eligible at baseline. Subsequently, 961 participants who had previous KD (excluding tumors or cancer) or without KD information were excluded, and 4010 participants were lost to follow-up. Ultimately, 8401 participants were included in the final analysis to investigate the association between BRI and incident KD (Fig. 1). The CHARLS was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was conducted following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

2.3. Calculation of BRI

The BRI was computed using the formula proposed by Thomas et al, expressed as: BRI = 364.2–365.5 × √ (1 − [WC in cm/2π] 2/ [0.5 × height in cm]2).[9] Waist circumference (WC) was measured using non-stretched tape at the navel level at minimal respiration. Height was measured by a 213 stadiometer (Seca GmbH, Hamburg, Germany), with participants standing upright and barefoot on the instrument’s floorboard. WC and height were measured to the nearest 0.1 cm.

2.4. Assessment of outcomes

Structured questionnaires, managed by trained interviewers, were used to collect demographic and clinical information. The study’s primary objective was to track the emergence of new KD events. The following questions were utilized to assess the onset of new KD events: “Did a medical professional determine that you have had a kidney disease (excluding tumors or cancer)?”; “Since your previous follow-up visit, has a doctor informed you that you have had a kidney disease (excluding tumors or cancer)?.” The timing of KD events was determined by participants’ responses to the following question: “Can you recall the first time you received a kidney disease diagnosis?.” Starting in 2013, participants were monitored over 4 rounds of interviews, concluding either with the first documented KD or the end of the follow-up in 2020, depending on which happened sooner.

2.5. Covariates

Based on prior literature and biological relevance, covariates were categorized into demographic and lifestyle factors, medical history, and medication-related factors. Demographic and lifestyle variables included age (continuous and categorized as 45–64 and ≥65 years), sex (male or female), registered residence (agricultural or nonagricultural), educational attainment (middle school or below, and high school or above), marital status (married or unmarried/divorced/widowed), smoking status (never or ever smoked), alcohol consumption (none, < once per month, or ≥ once per month), and BMI, calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Medical history variables were defined according to self-reported physician diagnoses and included hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or hyperglycemia, chronic lung diseases, liver disease, heart problems, and stomach or other digestive diseases. Medication-related factors included the use of lipid-lowering drugs, antihypertensive drugs, diabetes medications, and medications for chronic lung diseases. All covariates were assessed at baseline and were included in multivariable models to control for potential confounding in the association between BRI and incident KD.

2.6. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2-sided P value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges due to their non-normal distributions. Categorical variables are expressed as absolute numbers and relative proportions. Differences in baseline characteristics across BRI quartiles were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

To assess the prospective connection between the BRI and KD risk, Cox proportional hazards regression was applied. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated with BRI modeled both as a continuous variable and in quartiles. Using Schoenfeld residuals, the proportional hazards assumption was tested, revealing no significant violations since all covariate P-values were above .05. The calculation of variance inflation factors (VIFs) was performed to investigate potential multicollinearity, and since all were below 3, it suggests that collinearity among covariates is minimal. Prior epidemiological findings and biological reasoning informed the selection of covariates, ensuring that variables influencing KD risk were considered. To lessen residual confounding, we formulated 3 models with gradual adjustments. In model 1, the connection between BRI and KD risk is shown without adjustments. Model 2 was adjusted for demographic and lifestyle factors, including age, sex, marital status, education level, smoking status, drinking status, and BMI. In order to avoid overadjustment, we intentionally excluded clinical factors from model 2, as they may be part of the causal link between BRI and KD. Incorporating them prematurely could reduce the observed relationship due to accounting for potential mediators. The fully adjusted model subsequently incorporated these clinical variables to evaluate the robustness and independence of the association between BRI and KD. In Model 3, additional adjustments were made for clinical comorbidities and medication-related factors, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or hyperglycemia, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart problems, stomach or other digestive diseases, as well as the use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, antidiabetic, and chronic lung disease–related medications.

To characterize the dose–response relationship between BRI and KD risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions were incorporated into the fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards model. Four knots were placed at the fifth, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the BRI distribution, with the fifth percentile serving as the reference. The overall association and potential nonlinearity were assessed using likelihood ratio tests by comparing models with and without spline terms.

To further explore the potential threshold effect of BRI, standard linear Cox regression and 2-piecewise linear Cox regression models were subsequently fitted, with the inflection point determined using a data-driven approach based on maximum likelihood estimation. Model fit between the linear and 2-piecewise models was compared using a likelihood ratio test, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated accordingly.

Further analyses of subgroups were performed to examine the consistency of the association between BRI and KD risk across strata of key demographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables. To formally assess effect modification, multiplicative interaction terms between BRI and each subgroup variable were included in the fully adjusted Cox models, and P-values for interaction were calculated accordingly.

3. Results

The median age of the study population was 58.0 years (interquartile range, 52.0–65.0 years), and 65.7% of participants were female. Across increasing BRI quartiles, participants were more likely to be older and female, and to have lower educational attainment and a higher proportion of agricultural residence. Participants in higher BRI quartiles were less likely to smoke but more likely to consume alcohol regularly. 3.2. Associations between BRI and incident KD

The associations between BRI and KD risk are shown in Table 2. When analyzed as a continuous variable, BRI was positively associated with KD risk in the fully adjusted model (HR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.02–1.19; P = .017), whereas no significant association was observed in the crude model. When categorized into quartiles, participants in the highest BRI quartile (Q4) had a significantly higher risk of KD compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1) across all models. In the fully adjusted model, the HR for Q4 versus Q1 was 1.47 (95% CI: 1.06–2.05; P = .022), while no significant associations were observed for the second or third quartiles. A significant dose–response trend across increasing BRI quartiles was observed (P for trend = 0.014).

3.3. Dose–response relationship between BRI and the risk of KD

RCS analysis was used to further characterize the dose–response relationship between BRI and KD risk (Fig. 2). A significant overall association was observed (P for overall = .037), with no evidence of nonlinearity (P for nonlinearity = .307), indicating an approximately linear increase in the risk of KD with higher BRI levels across the observed range.

3.4. Threshold effect of BRI on the risk of KD

Two-piecewise linear Cox regression models were fitted to explore potential threshold effects (Table 3). The inflection point was identified at a BRI value of 2.23. Below this point, no significant association was observed, whereas a positive association was evident above the inflection point (HR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.05–1.27; P = .003). However, likelihood ratio testing indicated that the 2-piecewise model did not significantly improve model fit compared with the standard linear model (P = .378), suggesting that a linear model adequately described the association between BRI and KD risk.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the association between BRI and KD risk (Fig. 3). The association between higher BRI and increased KD risk was generally consistent across subgroups stratified by age, sex, registered residence, education level, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes or hyperglycemia, and use of diabetes medications (all P for interaction > .05). 4. Discussion

Within this large-scale prospective cohort of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older, greater BRI values were independently associated with an increased risk of KD. In the fully adjusted model, each unit increase in BRI was associated with a 10% higher risk of incident KD, indicating that elevations in body roundness may contribute to incremental renal risk. When BRI was examined categorically, a clear graded increase in KD risk was observed across quartiles, with participants in the highest BRI category exhibiting a 47% greater risk compared with those in the lowest category. This magnitude of association, although moderate, is clinically relevant given the high prevalence of excess adiposity in middle-aged and older populations.[14] These findings suggest that BRI, a novel anthropometric indicator reflecting body fat distribution, may serve as a practical and informative marker for identifying individuals at elevated risk of KD.

Prior evidence has suggested a potential contribution of central or visceral adiposity to kidney deterioration; however, existing studies have not yet generated sufficiently conclusive causal signals. A longitudinal analysis based on the CHARLS compared ten obesity-related indices and reported that the Waist Triglyceride Index (WTI) and A Body Shape Index (ABSI) yielded the strongest prognostic discrimination for rapid kidney function decline and CKD progression, but limited sample size and few outcome events weakened causal inference.[15] A large Chinese cross-sectional study further showed that the Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat (METS-VF) exhibited superior discrimination for CKD compared with traditional indices, although the absence of longitudinal follow-up precluded incident-risk evaluation.[16] Two analyses from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) provided reproducible evidence that the BRI correlates with prevalent CKD and exhibits linear or nonlinear exposure–response associations, yet both studies were restricted to single-time renal measurements and prevalence outcomes, thereby limiting inference on risk trajectories and progression dynamics.[17,18] In addition, a prospective CHARLS cohort integrating metabolic and anthropometric indices identified BRI as an independent predictor of incident CKD, but BRI was primarily evaluated within composite scores and was not systematically interrogated for threshold characteristics or continuous dose–response gradients across risk strata.[19] Building on these gaps, our study advances the field by delivering prospective, incident-outcome evidence in a nationally representative middle-aged and older Chinese population, explicitly quantifying the continuous dose–response relationship of BRI, and demonstrating consistent risk elevation across clinically meaningful subgroups.

RCS analyses further demonstrated a significant overall association between BRI and KD risk, with no evidence of nonlinearity, indicating an approximately linear dose–response relationship across the observed BRI range. These findings suggest that KD risk increases progressively with higher BRI levels, rather than exhibiting a distinct threshold effect. Although subsequent 2-piecewise linear regression identified a potential inflection point, likelihood ratio testing indicated that the segmented model did not significantly improve model fit compared with the linear model. Together, these results support the use of BRI as a continuous risk indicator for KD in epidemiological and clinical risk assessment.

Several mechanisms might explain the observed association between higher BRI and KD risk. First, BRI may serve as a surrogate marker of visceral adiposity, which has been strongly linked to systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia – even among individuals with body weight within the normal range.[20,21] Among middle-aged and elderly individuals, these pathophysiological disorders are particularly harmful, exacerbating the already elevated incidence of KD.[22] Furthermore, excess visceral fat may accelerate CKD progression through the secretion of adipokines and oxidative stress mediators.[23,24] Second, obesity, as captured by BRI, may lead to structural and functional changes in the kidney, including increased kidney weight, glomerular and tubular hypertrophy, and activation of the intrarenal renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, all of which can contribute to accelerated kidney function decline.[25]

Within this mechanistic framework, our subgroup analysis identified a significant interaction with dyslipidemia status. As this interaction was identified in exploratory analyses and was not prespecified, it should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, the finding raises the possibility that lipid metabolism abnormalities may potentiate the adverse renal effects associated with excess visceral adiposity. Elevated BRI reflects increased visceral fat accumulation, which is characterized by enhanced free fatty acid release and ectopic lipid deposition.[26] In the presence of dyslipidemia, impaired lipid clearance and increased circulating atherogenic lipoproteins may further exacerbate renal lipotoxicity, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory activation in glomerular and tubular cells.[27-29] The combined metabolic burden of abnormal fat distribution and dysregulated lipid metabolism may therefore accelerate structural and functional renal impairment. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to validate this interaction and clarify its causal implications.

From a clinical and public health perspective, BRI is a simple and noninvasive index that can be readily calculated using routine anthropometric measurements. Our findings highlight its potential utility as a complementary tool for early identification of individuals at elevated KD risk, particularly in resource-limited settings. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the BRI was assessed only at baseline, which may not reflect longitudinal changes in body fat distribution. Second, although multiple covariates were adjusted for, residual confounding cannot be excluded, including from lifestyle-related factors (e.g., physical activity and dietary habits) that may not have been comprehensively assessed. Third, KD was identified through self-reported physician–patient communication rather than standardized clinical verification, and laboratory-based indicators of renal function were unavailable. This reliance on self-reported information may have introduced outcome misclassification and ascertainment bias, potentially affecting the precision of the observed associations. Future studies incorporating repeated BRI measurements, objective renal biomarkers, and mechanistic investigations are needed to further elucidate the role of body fat distribution in kidney disease development.

5. Conclusions

In this large, nationally representative cohort of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years, higher BRI was independently associated with an increased risk of incident KD. The relationship exhibited a linear dose–response pattern, with risk rising progressively across the observed BRI range. This association remained robust after adjustment for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors and was generally consistent across key subgroups. BRI, as a simple and noninvasive measure of body fat distribution, may serve as a valuable marker for early identification of individuals at elevated KD risk. These findings strengthen epidemiological evidence linking central adiposity to kidney injury and highlight the potential clinical and public health importance of targeting body fat distribution in strategies aimed at the prevention and early control of kidney disease.
