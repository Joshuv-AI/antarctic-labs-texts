Effects of esaxerenone add‐on therapy versus angiotensin II receptor blocker dose escalation on albuminuria and blood pressure in hypertensive patients with type 2 diabetes: An exploratory randomized study
Authors: Makoto Ohara, Takemasa Omachi, Nobuaki Takehana, Tomoki Fujikawa, Naoya Osaka, Shunichiro Irie, Michishige Terasaki, Yusaku Mori, Takanori Matsui, Sho‐ichi Yamagishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsResidual albuminuria remains a major clinical challenge in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension despite renin–angiotensin system inhibitor therapy. Esaxerenone, a selective nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, reduces albuminuria; however, its effects on oxidative stress remain unclear.Materials and MethodsThis 24‐week, prospective, randomized, open‐label, multicenter exploratory trial was conducted in Japan. Hypertensive patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and albuminuria receiving standard‐dose angiotensin II receptor blocker therapy were randomized to angiotensin II receptor blocker dose escalation or esaxerenone add‐on therapy. Of 50 randomized patients, 47 were included in the full analysis set and 39 in the final analysis population (20 in the angiotensin II receptor blocker dose‐escalation group and 19 in the esaxerenone add‐on group). The primary endpoints were changes from baseline to Week 24 in urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio and oxidative stress assessed by diacron‐reactive oxygen metabolites.ResultsEsaxerenone add‐on therapy produced a significantly greater reduction in log‐transformed urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio than angiotensin II receptor blocker dose‐escalation therapy, with an adjusted between‐group ratio of 0.71 (95% confidence interval, 0.58–0.87; p = 0.002). Systolic and diastolic blood pressures were significantly lower with esaxerenone. Estimated glomerular filtration rate decreased more with esaxerenone, whereas serum potassium levels did not differ significantly between groups. No significant between‐group difference was observed in diacron‐reactive oxygen metabolites.ConclusionsIn hypertensive patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and albuminuria, esaxerenone add‐on therapy reduced albuminuria and blood pressure more effectively than angiotensin II receptor blocker dose escalation but did not significantly reduce oxidative stress.Keywords: Esaxerenone, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension frequently coexist, with hypertension affecting more than 50% of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus 1 , 2 . The coexistence of these conditions substantially increases the risk of both cardiovascular and renal complications 2 , 3 . The United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS 75) demonstrated that patients with both hyperglycemia and elevated blood pressure exhibited an approximately two‐ to fourfold higher risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) than those with either risk factor alone 3 . Furthermore, UKPDS 36 showed that each 10 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure was associated with a 12% reduction in the risk of any diabetes‐related endpoints, an 11% reduction in the risk of myocardial infarction, and a 13% reduction in the risk of microvascular complications, underscoring the critical importance of intensive blood pressure control in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus 4 .

The coexistence of hyperglycemia and hypertension accelerates endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and glomerular injury, thereby contributing to the development of atherosclerosis and diabetic nephropathy 5 , 6 , 7 . Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), one of the most common microvascular complications of type 2 diabetes mellitus, affects approximately 30%–40% of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and is a leading cause of ESKD globally 8 . The urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) is a sensitive biomarker for the early detection of DKD and is a strong predictor of both renal and cardiovascular outcomes 9 . According to the guidelines of the American Diabetes Association, the Japanese Society of Hypertension, and Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes, renin‐angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors—including angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) and angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors—are recommended as first‐line antihypertensive therapy for patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and albuminuria 9 , 10 , 11 . These agents not only lower systemic blood pressure but also reduce intraglomerular pressure, thereby decreasing albuminuria and providing renoprotective effects 5 , 11 . However, even with optimized RAS inhibition, a substantial proportion of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus have residual albuminuria and continue to progress to ESKD, highlighting an important unmet clinical need 8 , 9 , 11 .

Oxidative stress is widely recognized as a key contributor to the development and progression of DKD 5 , 6 , 12 . Hyperglycemia and the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) promote the excessive generation of reactive oxygen species, which in turn damages glomerular endothelial cells and podocytes, promotes mesangial matrix expansion, and leads to tubulointerstitial fibrosis 5 , 6 , 7 , 12 . These pathological changes contribute to increased urinary albumin excretion and a progressive decline in kidney function, highlighting oxidative stress as a potential therapeutic target in DKD 6 . Notably, overactivation of the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) has been shown to promote oxidative stress through activation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase in the diabetic kidney 13 . Furthermore, we previously demonstrated in a prospective study that improvements in ambient hyperglycemia and glycemic variability were significantly associated with reductions in oxidative stress in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus 14 , underscoring the clinical importance of targeting oxidative stress in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Furthermore, a Japanese Phase III trial demonstrated that add‐on therapy with esaxerenone, a selective nonsteroidal MR antagonist, significantly reduced the UACR in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and albuminuria receiving RAS inhibitors 15 . These findings suggest that esaxerenone may have additional clinical utility in further reducing albuminuria and potentially attenuating oxidative stress in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus receiving RAS inhibitor therapy. Therefore, this exploratory study aimed to evaluate the effects of esaxerenone on urinary albumin excretion and oxidative stress in hypertensive patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and albuminuria receiving RAS inhibitor therapy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This was a 24‐week, prospective, randomized, open‐label, multicenter, parallel‐group exploratory trial conducted in Japan. Patient enrollment commenced on February 2, 2021, and follow‐up was completed on December 31, 2022. The study was designed to evaluate the effects of esaxerenone on oxidative stress and urinary albumin excretion in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and albuminuria who had been receiving a standard dose of ARBs for at least 12 weeks. The study protocol is shown in Figure 1. Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the ARB dose‐escalation group, in which the standard dose of ARBs was increased to the approved dose in Japan, or the esaxerenone add‐on group, in which esaxerenone was initiated at 1.25 mg/day and uptitrated to 2.5 mg/day if the target blood pressure of less than 130/80 mmHg was not achieved. Block randomization was used to allocate patients to the treatment groups. Treatment assignments were generated in advance and placed in consecutively numbered, sealed envelopes, which were opened sequentially after written informed consent had been obtained. At baseline and Week 24, the following fasting clinical and laboratory parameters were assessed: body weight, blood pressure, UACR, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), triglycerides (TG), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and oxidative stress markers, including diacron‐reactive oxygen metabolites (d‐ROMs). Clinical data, including age, sex, and duration of diabetes, were collected from medical records. During the study period, patients were instructed to maintain their usual lifestyle habits and not to modify the doses of concomitant medications. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Showa Medical University (approval no. 3332) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable ethical guidelines in Japan. The study was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN‐CTR; UMIN000043069).

Study protocol. Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, and albuminuria who had been receiving a standard dose of an angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB) for at least 12 weeks were randomly assigned to either the ARB dose‐escalation group or the esaxerenone add‐on group and followed for 24 weeks. Blood and urine samples were collected, and oxidative stress markers were assessed at baseline and Week 24.

Subjects

Eligible participants were hypertensive patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus who had been receiving a standard dose of an ARB for at least 12 weeks and had an office blood pressure of 130/80 mmHg or more, albuminuria (defined as a UACR ≥30 mg/g creatinine), and an HbA1c level less than 8.5%. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) current use of corticosteroids or anti‐inflammatory drugs; (2) a history of diabetic ketoacidosis, diabetic coma, or diabetic precoma within 3 months before enrollment; (3) severe infection, severe trauma, or the perioperative period; (4) eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2; (5) hyperkalemia or a serum potassium level >5.0 mEq/L; (6) active malignancy; (7) pregnancy or potential pregnancy; and (8) any condition that, in the opinion of the investigators, made the patient unsuitable for participation.

Biochemical measurements

Fasting blood and urine samples were collected at baseline and at Week 24. Routine biochemical parameters, including FPG, HbA1c, lipid profiles, serum creatinine, and serum potassium, were measured using standard laboratory methods at each participating institution as previously described 16 . eGFR was calculated using the equation recommended by the Japanese Society of Nephrology 17 . UACR was determined from spot urine samples. Oxidative stress was assessed using the d‐ROMs test (F.R.E.E. System, imported by LTD Tokyo from Diacron International S.r.l., Grosseto, Italy) as previously described 16 . According to the Wismerll kinetic procedure, the change in absorbance per minute was corrected and expressed as Carratelli units after correction (U.CARR, where 1 U.CARR corresponds to the oxidant capacity of a 0.08 mg/dL hydrogen peroxide solution; the normal reference range is 250–300 U.CARR). Serum levels of pigment epithelium‐derived factor (PEDF) and AGEs were measured using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay methods as previously described 18 , 19 . Serum levels of the soluble receptor for AGEs (sRAGE) and kidney injury molecule‐1 (KIM‐1) were measured using commercially available enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay kits (#DRG00 for sRAGE and #DSKM100 for KIM‐1; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to the manufacturers' instructions.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoints were changes from baseline to Week 24 in UACR and oxidative stress, as assessed by d‐ROMs. The secondary endpoints included changes from baseline to Week 24 in office blood pressure; exploratory biomarkers, including AGEs, sRAGE, PEDF, and KIM‐1; glycemic parameters, including FPG and HbA1c; lipid parameters, including low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, HDL‐C, and TG; kidney function, assessed by eGFR; and serum potassium levels.

Sample size

As this was an exploratory prospective trial, no formal sample size calculation was performed. The target sample size was set at 50 patients based on feasibility, considering the expected number of eligible patients available for enrollment during the study period.

Statistical analysis

The full analysis set comprised all randomized patients with available baseline assessment data. The final analysis population consisted of patients who completed the 24‐week follow‐up and had both baseline and Week 24 clinical and laboratory data. Because exploratory biomarkers, including the sRAGE, AGEs, PEDF, and KIM‐1, were measured only in the final analysis population, baseline characteristics, and efficacy analyses were based on this population. Missing data were not imputed. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, whereas those with a non‐normal distribution are presented as the median and interquartile range. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. In accordance with CONSORT recommendations for randomized trials, baseline characteristics were summarized descriptively and were not compared using statistical significance tests. Within‐group changes from baseline to Week 24 were analyzed using paired Student's t‐tests for normally distributed variables and Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests for non‐normally distributed variables. Between‐group differences at Week 24 were assessed using analysis of covariance (ancova), with treatment group as a fixed factor effect and the corresponding baseline value as a covariate. Given the small sample size of this exploratory study, ancova models were restricted to the corresponding baseline value to avoid overfitting. For variables with markedly skewed distributions, logarithmic transformation was applied as appropriate before statistical analysis. UACR and TG levels were log‐transformed for analysis. Univariate linear regression analyses were performed to assess the associations between log‐transformed UACR at Week 24 and clinical variables. Variables with p < 0.05 in the univariate analyses were entered into a multivariable stepwise linear regression model, with baseline log‐transformed UACR included as a covariate, to identify factors independently associated with Week 24 log‐transformed UACR. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and a P‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Figure 2 illustrates the flow of participants through the trial. A total of 50 patients were assessed for eligibility and randomized, with 25 assigned to the ARB dose‐escalation group and 25 to the esaxerenone add‐on group. Before the baseline assessment, one patient in the ARB dose‐escalation group was excluded because of liver dysfunction, and two patients in the esaxerenone add‐on group were excluded because of withdrawal of consent (n = 1) and the absence of prior ARB therapy (n = 1). The latter patient was initially considered eligible at screening, but detailed medication history review before the baseline assessment revealed that the patient had not received ARB therapy before enrollment. Consequently, the full analysis set comprised 47 patients (24 in the ARB dose‐escalation group and 23 in the esaxerenone add‐on group). In the ARB dose‐escalation group, four patients were excluded from the final analysis population because of transfer to another hospital (n = 1), an allergic reaction (n = 1), hospitalization for an unrelated illness (n = 1), and poor glycemic control during follow‐up that met a predefined exclusion criterion (n = 1). In the esaxerenone add‐on group, four patients were excluded from the final analysis population: two did not receive esaxerenone because of hyperkalemia detected at baseline, one discontinued esaxerenone because of an increase in serum potassium after treatment initiation, and one discontinued outpatient visits before Week 24. Consequently, 20 patients in the ARB dose‐escalation group and 19 in the esaxerenone add‐on group were included in the final analysis population. In the ARB dose‐escalation group, all patients were escalated to the maximum approved ARB doses in Japan: olmesartan 40 mg/day in 13 patients, telmisartan 80 mg/day in 3 patients, candesartan 12 mg/day in 3 patients, and losartan 100 mg/day in 1 patient. In the esaxerenone add‐on group, background ARB therapy was continued without dose escalation, and the final esaxerenone dose was 1.25 mg/day in 6 patients and 2.5 mg/day in 13 patients. No additional concomitant medications, including antihypertensive, antidiabetic, or lipid‐lowering medications, were newly started during the study period in patients included in the final analysis population. The baseline clinical characteristics of the final analysis population are summarized in Table 1. In accordance with CONSORT recommendations, baseline characteristics are presented descriptively without p values. Several numerical imbalances were observed between the groups, including body weight, body mass index, duration of diabetes, baseline UACR, prevalence of macroangiopathy, and insulin use.

Effects on the primary endpoints

Table 2 summarizes the changes in clinical and biochemical parameters from baseline to Week 24. In the esaxerenone add‐on group, UACR decreased significantly from 67.3 (59.1 to 139.5) mg/g creatinine at baseline to 34.5 (17.3 to 62.4) mg/g creatinine at Week 24 (p < 0.001). Similarly, log‐transformed UACR decreased significantly from 2.0 ± 0.4 to 1.6 ± 0.5 (p < 0.001). In contrast, neither UACR nor log‐transformed UACR changed significantly in the ARB dose‐escalation group. In an ancova adjusted for baseline values, esaxerenone add‐on therapy was associated with a significantly greater reduction in log‐transformed UACR than the ARB dose‐escalation group, with an adjusted between‐group ratio of 0.71 (95% confidence interval, 0.58–0.87; p = 0.002). In contrast, d‐ROMs did not change significantly in either group, and no significant between‐group difference was observed at week 24.

Effects on the secondary endpoints

In the esaxerenone add‐on group, systolic blood pressure decreased significantly from 150.0 ± 18.8 mmHg at baseline to 131.4 ± 13.8 mmHg at Week 24, whereas diastolic blood pressure decreased significantly from 80.7 ± 13.8 mmHg to 70.7 ± 12.8 mmHg. In contrast, neither systolic nor diastolic blood pressure changed significantly in the ARB dose‐escalation group. After adjustment for baseline values, both systolic, and diastolic blood pressure were significantly lower in the esaxerenone add‐on group than in the ARB dose‐escalation group at Week 24. eGFR decreased significantly in the esaxerenone add‐on group and was significantly lower than that in the ARB dose‐escalation group after adjustment for baseline values. Serum potassium increased significantly from baseline in the esaxerenone add‐on group. However, the adjusted between‐group difference at Week 24 was not statistically significant. HbA1c increased slightly from baseline within the esaxerenone add‐on group, whereas AGEs decreased significantly from baseline in the esaxerenone add‐on group. In the ARB dose‐escalation group, KIM‐1 increased significantly from baseline. However, none of these parameters, including lipid parameters, differed significantly between groups at Week 24.

Factors associated with week 24 log‐transformed UACR

Univariate and multivariable stepwise linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with log‐transformed UACR at Week 24 (Table 3). In the multivariable model, baseline log‐transformed UACR, treatment group, change in systolic blood pressure, and change in HDL‐C were independently associated with log‐transformed UACR at Week 24.

DISCUSSION

This 24‐week prospective, randomized, exploratory study demonstrated that esaxerenone add‐on therapy was associated with a significantly greater reduction in blood pressure and albuminuria than ARB dose‐escalation therapy in hypertensive patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and albuminuria receiving a standard dose of ARB therapy. Specifically, esaxerenone add‐on therapy resulted in significantly greater reductions in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure, as well as a significantly greater decline in log‐transformed UACR. In contrast, no significant between‐group difference was observed in oxidative stress, as assessed by d‐ROMs. Multivariable stepwise linear regression analysis further showed that baseline log‐transformed UACR, treatment group, changes in systolic blood pressure, and in HDL‐C were independently associated with log‐transformed UACR at Week 24. These findings suggest that esaxerenone add‐on therapy may be more effective than ARB dose escalation for reducing blood pressure and residual albuminuria in this patient population.

Although albuminuria is a strong predictor of both renal and cardiovascular outcomes, and reductions in albuminuria have been associated with a lower subsequent risk of ESKD 20 , 21 , the management of residual albuminuria remains a major clinical challenge in patients with DKD. Current guidelines recommend titrating angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or ARBs to the maximum tolerated or approved dose in patients with diabetes, hypertension, and albuminuria 9 , 10 , 11 . Accordingly, ARB dose escalation represents a guideline‐recommended therapeutic strategy for this patient population. Previous studies have demonstrated that esaxerenone reduces albuminuria when added to ongoing RAS inhibitor therapy; however, no study has directly compared esaxerenone add‐on therapy with ARB dose‐escalation therapy 15 , 22 . To address this gap, the present study directly compared these two treatment strategies. Because several baseline characteristics showed numerical differences between the two groups, between‐group comparisons of clinical and biochemical outcomes at Week 24 were performed using ancova, with the corresponding baseline value included as a covariate, rather than by simple comparison of changes from baseline. Adding esaxerenone to standard‐dose ARB therapy was associated with greater reductions in both blood pressure and UACR than escalating the ARB dose to the maximum approved dose in the present study. The greater reduction in systolic blood pressure observed with esaxerenone add‐on therapy may have contributed, at least in part, to the reduction in albuminuria. However, the treatment group remained independently associated with log‐transformed UACR at Week 24, even after adjustment for baseline log‐transformed UACR and changes in systolic blood pressure, suggesting that the albuminuria‐lowering effect of esaxerenone cannot be fully explained by blood pressure reduction alone. MR activation has been implicated in podocyte injury, inflammation, fibrosis, oxidative stress, and glomerular hemodynamic abnormalities in the diabetic kidney, all of which may contribute to increased albuminuria and the progression of DKD 13 . Recent experimental evidence suggests that esaxerenone may reduce albuminuria by attenuating glomerular hyperfiltration through restoration of tubuloglomerular feedback 23 . Therefore, MR blockade may reduce albuminuria not only through blood pressure lowering but also through direct renoprotective and glomerular hemodynamic effects, potentially mediated by inhibition of these pathogenic pathways. In addition, previous clinical studies have shown that esaxerenone reduces albuminuria in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and albuminuria receiving RAS inhibitor therapy, further supporting the albuminuria‐lowering effects of MR blockade in this population 15 , 22 . Although the present exploratory study was not designed to investigate the underlying mechanisms, our findings are consistent with previous experimental and clinical observations and suggest that adding esaxerenone to standard‐dose ARB therapy may provide additional reductions in albuminuria.

In contrast to the significant reductions in blood pressure and UACR, esaxerenone add‐on therapy did not significantly reduce oxidative stress as assessed by d‐ROMs, nor did it show significant between‐group differences in exploratory biomarkers related to the AGE–RAGE axis or PEDF at Week 24. These findings should be interpreted cautiously for several reasons. First, baseline d‐ROM levels in the present cohort were relatively low and close to the upper limit of the normal reference range reported previously, leaving limited scope for further reduction 24 . Second, all patients had been receiving ARB therapy before enrollment, which may have partially attenuated oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways at baseline 25 . Third, the small sample size and short 24‐week follow‐up period of this exploratory study may not be enough to detect modest changes in oxidative stress and related biomarkers. The AGE–RAGE axis and PEDF have been implicated in oxidative stress, inflammation, and DKD 7 , 12 , 18 , 26 , 27 ; however, no significant between‐group differences were observed in these exploratory biomarkers in the present study. Further larger studies are warranted to clarify whether esaxerenone influences these pathways over a longer term. In addition, although multivariable analysis identified change in HDL‐C as an independent factor associated with log‐transformed UACR at Week 24, this finding should also be interpreted with caution given the small sample size and exploratory nature of the analysis.

In the present study, eGFR decreased significantly in the esaxerenone add‐on group, whereas serum potassium increased significantly from baseline. The decline in eGFR may represent an initial dip, a hemodynamic phenomenon commonly observed after the initiation or intensification of renoprotective therapies, including RAS inhibitors and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors 11 . This initial decline is thought to reflect a reduction in intraglomerular pressure rather than progressive kidney injury, particularly in the setting of the concomitant reduction in albuminuria 23 , 28 , 29 . Hyperkalemia is a well‐recognized adverse effect of MR antagonists, including esaxerenone, particularly in patients with DKD receiving concomitant RAS inhibitor therapy 15 , 22 . In the present study, although the adjusted between‐group difference in serum potassium at Week 24 was not statistically significant, hyperkalemia led to exclusion from the final analysis population in three patients in the esaxerenone add‐on group: two patients did not initiate esaxerenone because hyperkalemia was detected at baseline, and one patient discontinued treatment because of elevated serum potassium after treatment initiation. These findings underscore the need for careful monitoring of renal function and serum potassium during esaxerenone therapy in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and albuminuria receiving ARB treatment.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, and no formal sample size calculation was performed because this was an exploratory trial. Second, the open‐label design may have introduced treatment or assessment bias. Third, the follow‐up period was limited to 24 weeks, precluding evaluation of long‐term kidney outcomes. Fourth, oxidative stress was assessed solely using d‐ROMs, and other oxidative stress biomarkers were not evaluated. Fifth, the study population was limited to Japanese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and albuminuria receiving ARB therapy, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Sixth, the efficacy analyses were based on the final analysis population rather than the full analysis set, and no imputation was performed for missing data. Therefore, the possibility of attrition bias cannot be excluded. Finally, residual confounding due to baseline imbalances cannot be excluded. Although ancova was used to adjust for the corresponding baseline value of each outcome, adjustment for multiple baseline factors was not feasible because of the small sample size and the risk of overfitting.

In conclusion, in hypertensive patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and albuminuria, esaxerenone add‐on therapy reduced albuminuria and blood pressure more effectively than ARB dose escalation but did not significantly reduce oxidative stress. These findings suggest that esaxerenone may be a useful adjunctive treatment option for reducing residual albuminuria, although further larger studies with longer follow‐up are warranted to clarify long‐term kidney outcomes.
