Trigeminal and facial branchiomotor neurons have shared muscle targets in zebrafish larvae
Authors: Ritika Ghosal, Kimberly L McArthur, Johann K Eberhart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe trigeminal and facial branchiomotor neurons are believed to have unique sets of peripheral muscle targets in the first and second branchial arches, respectively. However, their innervation patterns in zebrafish have only been precisely mapped up to 48 h postfertilization (hpf). In this study, we dissected the final peripheral destinations of the trigeminal and facial motor nerves in the ventral branchial musculature of first‐feeding zebrafish larvae. Using a combination of transgenic lines, we found that around 56 hpf, the facial nerve elongates anteriorly to reach the intermandibularis group of muscles, which are classically defined to be innervated by the trigeminal nerve. Intriguingly, the facial nerve could project onto these muscles ipsilaterally and contralaterally. Furthermore, the trigeminal nerve also elongates posteriorly into the hyoid arch territory around 53 hpf to reach the interhyoideus muscles. Targeted ablation of facial neurons revealed that the trigeminal nerve extends further posteriorly to reach the hyohyoideus inferior muscles between 3 and 4 days post fertilization. Strikingly, both these hyoid arch muscles formed neuromuscular junctions even in the absence of facial motor neurons, signifying functional relevance of their trigeminal innervation. Overall, we show that all ventral branchial muscles, except the hyohyoideus superior, receive dual innervation, while the hyohyoideus superior muscle is solely innervated by the facial nerve. We propose that this conjoint innervation of the anteroventral branchial muscles might be essential for their coordinated functional role in respiration or feeding.Keywords: branchial muscles, cranial nerves, facial nerve, metronidazole, nitroreductase, trigeminal nerve

INTRODUCTION

In vertebrates, muscles of the face and neck are innervated by cranial nerves (Guthrie, 2007). The cranial motor neurons originate in the brainstem, from where they project their axons to their target muscles in the periphery. During early development, the hindbrain, which houses cranial motor neurons V–XII, is transiently segmented into a series of rhombomeres (Lumsden & Keynes, 1989). Each rhombomere expresses a unique set of regulatory genes, such as the Hox genes. This differential gene expression gives the cranial ganglia, motor neurons, and neural crest cells (NCCs) born within each rhombomere a unique segmental identity (Chambers et al., 2009; Hunt & Krumlauf, 1991; Moens & Prince, 2002; Trevarrow et al., 1990).

Hindbrain patterning is closely linked to branchial arch patterning through the rhombomere‐specific NCCs that migrate into specific branchial arches. This establishes a functional registration between the NCCs, motor neurons, and their target branchial arches (Lumsden et al., 1991; Trainor & Krumlauf, 2000). Motor neurons project into the branchial compartment that expresses the same Hox repertoire as their progenitor rhombomere. For instance, in zebrafish, NCCs originating from rhombomere 1 to 3 populate the first branchial arch or the mandibular arch. Congruently, the mandibular arch is innervated by the trigeminal branchiomotor nerve (Vth cranial nerve) that also originates from r2 and r3. Similarly, the NCCs originating from r4 populate the second branchial arch or the hyoid arch, which is innervated by the facial branchiomotor nerve (VIIth cranial nerve) that originates from r4 (Guthrie, 2007; Moens & Prince, 2002; Schilling & Kimmel, 1994; Warrilow & Guthrie, 1999).

Hox genes are required for establishing motor axon connectivity between hindbrain and branchial arches. Altering a single Hox gene expression or a mismatch between the Hox code present in the motor neurons and in the branchial arches can disrupt axonal pathfinding. For instance, misexpression of Hoxb1 can respecify a small number of motor neuron axons to project from r2 to the hyoid arch instead of the mandibular arch (Bell et al., 1999). In mouse, the requirement of Hoxb1 in facial motor neurons and premigratory hyoid neural crest was shown to be necessary to establish and maintain facial nerve peripheral connectivity (Arenkiel et al., 2004). Also, rostrocaudal reversal of a single rhombomeric segment can respecify axonal identity. For instance, trigeminal motor neurons from r3 with reversed rostrocaudal polarity aberrantly projected via r4 exit into the hyoid arch instead of the mandibular arch (Warrilow & Guthrie, 1999). Thus, the specificity of the cranial motor neurons is intrinsically linked to the rhombomeric segment of origin and the hox genes expressed in that rhombomere.

The trigeminal and facial branchiomotor neuron axons, after reaching the branchial basket, are thought to be largely restricted to the branchial arch compartments that they initially innervate. Thus, each is thought to have a unique set of target branchial muscles. That is, the trigeminal motor nerve innervates the mandibular arch muscles, while the facial motor nerve innervates the hyoid arch muscles. For instance, in zebrafish, the trigeminal motor nerve has been reported to innervate the adductor mandibulae, intermandibularis anterior, and intermandibularis posterior muscles, while the facial nerve innervates the interhyoideus, hyohyoideus inferior, and hyohyoideus superior muscles (Higashijima et al., 2000; Schilling & Kimmel, 1997). However, subsequent analyses reported trigeminal innervation of the interhyoideus and suggested that only the hyohyoideus muscles were innervated by the facial nerve (Tanaka et al., 2007, Table S1). The separation between BA (branchial arch)1 and BA2 territories is marked by MHJ (Mandibulohyoid junction) in the ventral musculature.

These studies used either anti‐acetylated tubulin (Schilling & Kimmel, 1997) or the isl1:GFP transgenic (Higashijima et al., 2000; Tanaka et al., 2007), neither of which specifically labels either cranial nerve. Also, the location of the facial nerve in the hyoid arch and the trigeminal nerve in the mandibular arch has only been precisely traced up to 48 hpf (Chandrasekhar et al., 1997). There is increasing interest in the functional consequences of inappropriate innervation of these muscles (Luderman et al., 2023). Zebrafish larvae acquire a fully functional feeding apparatus at 4 dpf, with all necessary muscle and nerve connectivity for exogenous feeding. Most larvae begin exogenous feeding after their yolk reserve is exhausted, which is between 4 and 5 dpf (Sanderson & Kupferberg, 1999; Yaniv et al., 2014). Thus, a systematic study of the innervation patterns of the trigeminal and facial motor nerves in the first‐feeding larva at 4/5 dpf is essential.

Here, we used a combination of transgenic lines and single‐cell labeling to detail the time‐course of the trigeminal and facial nerve elongation into the ventral branchial basket of zebrafish. We found that, during their initial pathfinding, the trigeminal and facial nerves project into the mandibular and hyoid arches, respectively. However, as the branchial muscles emerge and mature, the facial motor nerve extends beyond the hyoid arch territory and elongates anteriorly to reach the mandibular arch muscles. Imaging using isl1 and hoxb1 reporter lines also indicated the presence of the trigeminal nerve in some of the hyoid arch muscles. We further investigated the trigeminal nerve innervation pattern by using a nitroreductase/metronidazole‐mediated ablation system to selectively eliminate the facial neurons and reveal the trigeminal nerve innervation pattern. We found that, similar to the facial nerve, the trigeminal nerve at a later developmental stage crosses the mandibular–hyoid arch boundary and extends posteriorly to some of the hyoid arch muscles. This study uncovers that all ventral branchial muscles except the hyohyoideus superior muscle contain both trigeminal and facial motor axons. The hyohyoideus superior muscle is the only ventral branchial muscle in zebrafish that is exclusively innervated by the facial nerve.

METHODS

Zebrafish lines

Fish lines for most experiments were housed in the University of Texas at Austin. Single‐cell labeling experiments were carried out using fish housed at Cornell University (McArthur & Fetcho, 2017). The fish were raised using established protocols (Westerfield, 2000) approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The cranial nerves were visualized using Tg(isl1:GFP)rw0 line (Higashijima et al., 2000) and Tg(isl1‐hsp70l:mRFP)fh1 line (Grant & Moens, 2010). These lines are referred to as isl1:GFP and isl1:mRFP in the text. The branchial muscles were visualized using the Tg(−0.5unc45b:mCherry)au101 line (McGurk et al., 2017), referred to as unc:mCherry. Rhombomere 4‐derived facial neurons were labeled using Tg(hoxb1a‐Mmu.Hbb:Gal4‐VP16)um60 (Choe et al., 2012) along with Tg(UAS‐E1b:Kaede)s1999t (Davison et al., 2007), these lines are referred to as hoxb1a:Gal4 and UAS:Kaede, respectively. Tg(UAS‐E1b:NfsB‐mCherry)c264 line was used for tissue‐specific cell ablation (Davison et al., 2007) and is referred to as UAS:NTR‐mCherry. The hoxb1a:Gal4‐driven expression of UAS:Kaede and UAS:NTR‐mCherry was observably consistent throughout this study; we did not observe any silencing of the transgene. Previously, this hoxb1a:Gal4 line was crossed to UAS:hoxa3aum61 line; the Gal4‐regulated UAS elements were reported to drive robust hoxa3a expression in r4 with no variegation or silencing (Choe et al., 2012).

Confocal microscopy

Fish were anesthetized using Tricaine and mounted in 0.2% agarose on 3% methyl cellulose, or in 2% low melting point agarose. Imaging was performed using Zeiss LSM 710 and LSM 980 laser scanning microscopes. Confocal Z‐stacks were acquired and processed using FIJI/ImageJ.

Kaede photoconversion

24 hpf, hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede embryos were anesthetized, dechorionated, and mounted obliquely for confocal live imaging using a previously described mounting procedure (McGurk et al., 2014). Green Kaede protein in the ventrolateral rhombomere 4 cells was photoconverted to red Kaede protein upon UV exposure using the 405 μm laser on Zeiss 980 microscope. The embryos were then transferred to embryo medium and allowed to develop at 28°C. At 48 hpf, the hindbrains of these embryos were imaged dorsally to ensure the labeled cells were confined to one side of the brain. The fish were raised and imaged at 3 days postfertilization (dpf) and 4 dpf to investigate the ventral innervation pattern of the labeled facial motor neurons.

Time‐lapse imaging

Time‐lapse imaging was performed using a previously described protocol (McGurk et al., 2014). To study cranial nerve routing and elongation, hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede;unc:mCherry and hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede;isl1:mRFP fish were anaesthetized using Tricaine and mounted ventrally in 0.2% agarose on 3% methyl cellulose. Imaging was performed using Zeiss LSM 980 on a temperature‐regulated stage set at 28.5°C. Confocal Z‐stacks were collected with a time interval of 12 min and processed using ImageJ/FIJI.

Whole‐mount immunohistochemistry

Fish harvested at 48 hpf, 72 hpf, and 4 dpf were fixed overnight in 4% PFA. Whole‐mount immunohistochemistry for analysis of neuromuscular junctions and Mauthner cells was carried out according to a previously described protocol (Ghosal & Eberhart, 2025). The pre‐ and postsynaptic terminals were stained using: synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (DSHB SV2, Antibody Registry ID: AB_2315387) at 5 μg/mL and α‐Bungarotoxin (Alexa Fluor 647 conjugated‐B35450) at a 1:250 dilution. To label Mauthner neurons, 3A10 (DHSB, Antibody Registry ID: AB_531874) primary antibody was used at 10 μg/mL. The fish were incubated in primary antibodies at 4°C in 5% normal goat serum, 1% BSA, 1% DMSO, and 0.5% Triton X‐100. Secondary antibody was also incubated overnight at 4°C in 5% normal goat serum, 1% BSA, 1% DMSO, and 0.5% Triton X‐100 at a 1:500 dilution. Secondary antibody incubation was followed by PBS washes and imaging.

Image calculations using FIJI/ImageJ

High‐resolution confocal images of hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede;isl1:mRFP fish were collected at 5 dpf. To eliminate possible artifacts due to channel intensity gain, several background and channel intensity controls were included. As a first check, high‐resolution images with sequential imaging and avoiding any bleed‐through and saturation (using range‐indicator) were used for image calculations. Secondly, for selecting RFP channel intensity, the facial sensory neurons (which should only be labeled in green) were used as control. This ensured that the selected RFP channel intensity did not cross‐detect facial sensory neurons. Similarly, to select the Kaede channel intensity, the trigeminal nerve branch outside the Meckel's cartilage, prior to its entry into the intermandibularis muscles, was used as a negative control. Confocal Z‐stacks of ventral branchial muscles were collected and Z‐projected using Image > Stacks > Z project in ImageJ. Using Image > Color > Split Channels in ImageJ, the Hoxb1 channel (green) was separated from the Islet1 channel (red). Image calculations were performed using the Process > Image Calculator tool in ImageJ. The facial motor component was extracted using the “AND” function between the Hoxb1 channel (green) and the Islet1 channel (red). The facial motor component was then subtracted from the total motor component (Islet1 channel) to derive the trigeminal motor component.

Facial motor neuron ablation using Nitroreductase/Metronidazole‐mediated system

Facial motor neuron ablation was carried out based on a previously described technique on other tissues (Curado et al., 2008; Leach et al., 2022). Nitroreductase (NTR) is an enzyme that reduces the prodrug metronidazole into a cytotoxic compound causing cell death. Expression of NTR under a tissue‐specific promoter combined with metronidazole exposure can selectively ablate target cells with a temporal control. The NTR enzyme was expressed in the rhombomere 4 cells using hoxb1:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry fish line. A 10 mM of metronidazole (Millipore Sigma M3761‐25G) stock solution in embryo medium (EM) was prepared by dissolving metronidazole in EM at 37°C with continuous shaking. The EM containing metronidazole was allowed to cool to 28°C. The hoxb1:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry fish at 24 hpf, 48 hpf, 72 hpf, and 4 dpf were transferred to EM containing metronidazole (with a final concentration of 2.5 mM metronidazole in EM) and were allowed to develop at 28°C. After 24 h, the EM containing metronidazole was replaced by regular EM. Fish were then allowed to develop at 28°C until 4 dpf for characterization of nerves and muscles.

Single‐cell labeling

Individual facial branchiomotor neurons were labeled via single‐cell electroporation of fluorescent dye in the isl1:GFP transgenic line, using methods described previously (McArthur & Fetcho, 2017). In brief, isl1:GFP larvae (4dpf) were anesthetized and immobilized to permit penetration of the hindbrain with a fine‐tipped glass electroporation pipette filled with dye (Alexa Fluor 546 or 647, dextran conjugated, MW 10000, anionic, Molecular Probes; 20% in extracellular solution, in mM: 134 NaCl, 2.9 KCl, 1.2 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 10 glucose, 2.1 CaCl2, adjusted to pH 7.8 with NaOH). Once the tip of the pipette was positioned against a facial branchiomotor neuron cell body, voltage pulses were delivered to the cell surface (3–5 V, 50 Hz, 1‐ms pulses, 3–5 s per train, one train per cell) using an Axoporator device (Molecular Devices). Following electroporation, fish were allowed to recover overnight, then immobilized in low‐melting point agarose (2%; Fisher Scientific) and imaged using a confocal microscope at 5dpf.

RESULTS

Facial branchiomotor neurons innervate mandibular arch muscles

To investigate the branchial muscle targets of the facial motor nerve, we used the hoxb1a:Gal4‐driven expression of UAS:Kaede. Hoxb1a is expressed in rhombomere (r) 4, where the facial motor neurons are born (Figure 1a). In zebrafish, facial motor neurons migrate tangentially (posteriorly) from r4 to reside in r6 and r7. However, they project their axons out of the hindbrain exclusively through the r4 exit point to reach the branchial muscles (Chandrasekhar, 2004; McArthur & Fetcho, 2017). The arrow in Figure 1a shows posteriorly migrating facial motor neurons at 24 hpf. Postmitotic facial motor neurons maintain hoxb1a expression, enabling their visualization in their peripheral targets. We studied the facial nerve innervation in the ventral branchial musculature of zebrafish at 4 dpf using the hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede;unc:mCherry transgenic line. Unexpectedly, we found that the facial nerve was located much more anteriorly than previously characterized in all analyzed fish (n = 12). The facial axons had reached the ventromedial mandibular arch muscle groups, the intermandibularis anterior and posterior muscles (M.IMA and M.IMP, respectively, arrows in Figure 1b). To further characterize facial nerve projections onto mandibular arch muscles, we used the hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede;isl1:mRFP line. In this transgenic, all motor neurons are labeled red, and the facial nerve is labeled green. Co‐labeled fibers were observed in both intermandibularis anterior and posterior muscles, confirming the presence of facial motor axons in these mandibular arch muscles (Figure 1c; n = 6). These co‐labeled facial motor axons were clearly distinguishable from the facial sensory axons, which were singly labeled with Hoxb1a in green and were visible more laterally along the inner side of the Meckel's cartilage (Figure 1c,d; n.VII Sensory). We also investigated the facial motor nerve in cross‐sectional view using orthogonal sections on FIJI/ImageJ. This further confirmed the presence of facial motor axons co‐labeled in red and green in these muscles (Figure S2). Single confocal Z‐slices revealed that the facial motor axons are closely associated with the intermandibularis anterior muscle fibers, likely contributing to its motor innervation (Figure S2B).

We used time‐lapse imaging to characterize the dynamics of the facial nerve elongation. It elongates anteromedially into the mandibular arch territory at around 56 hpf after crossing the mandibulohyoid junction. The facial nerve extends across the intermandibularis posterior then turns medially to enter the intermandibularis anterior muscle fibers at around 68 hpf. We observed that the facial nerve innervates the intermandibularis posterior around 56 hpf, which is around the time when these muscles first start to emerge during development. However, the facial nerve elongation into the intermandibularis anterior muscle at 68 hpf occurs much later after the emergence of the intermandibularis anterior muscle at around 58 hpf (Movie 1).

Facial motor neurons can reach the mandibular arch muscles ipsilaterally or contralaterally

There is a commissure at the mandibulohyoid junction, where facial axons pass from hyoid to mandibular arch territory. To investigate if facial axons innervating the intermandibularis muscles project beyond MHJ, ipsilaterally or contralaterally, we unilaterally labeled the facial motor neurons in the hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede transgenic line. Motor neurons on one side of the hindbrain were photoconverted at 24 hpf (Figure 2a,a',e,e'). Unilateral labeling was confirmed by confocal imaging of the hindbrain at 48 hpf (Figure 2b,b',f, f'). Axons from labeled facial motor neurons were traced in the ventral branchial muscles at 3 dpf (Figure 2c,c',g,g') and 4 dpf (Figure 2d,d',h,h') to examine their innervation patterns. We found that the facial axons innervate the interhyoideus ipsilaterally in all fish. However, after crossing the mandibulohyoid junction, the facial axons elongated to the contralateral side or to both ipsilateral and contralateral sides in 57.6% (n = 15/26) fish. While in 42.3% (n = 11/26) of fish, the facial axons projected ipsilaterally. Interestingly, in humans, the contralateral innervation of the facial nerve in the orbicularis oris muscles has been found both in healthy individuals and in patients with facial palsy (Güllüoğlu et al., 2024). Another study showed contralateral reinnervation of the facial nerve in orbicularis oris and nasalis muscles in a patient with facial palsy (Gilhuis et al., 2001). Whether or not these contralaterally elongating facial axons are pruned at a later stage or if there is a functional relevance to it requires future investigation.

The projection pattern of individual facial motor neurons into the mandibular arch territory was characterized using single‐cell electroporation of fluorescent dye in the isl1:GFP transgenic line performed at 4 dpf and imaged at 5 dpf (Figure 2i–t). At 5 dpf, we observed individual facial branchiomotor neurons projecting axons into mandibular arch territory (Figure 2l–n,r–t, n = 5/7). We found that single facial motor neurons can send large collateral projections to both intermandibularis posterior muscles (Figure 2r–t). Projections were predominantly ipsilateral in nature (Figure 2l–n), though facial motor neurons could also extend to the contralateral muscles (Figure 2r–t), consistent with results from unilateral photoconversion. These single‐cell labeling experiments confirm that individual facial motor neurons can extend to multiple muscles, including muscles in both hyoidal and mandibular territory.

The trigeminal nerve elongates posteriorly onto the hyoid arch muscles

We investigated the trigeminal nerve innervation pattern using the hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede;isl1:mRFP transgenic line that labels all branchial motor axons in red and the facial axons in green. Thus, axons singly labeled with red are trigeminal motor axons, while axons co‐labeled with red and green are facial motor axons. Consistent with previous analyses in isl1:GFP single transgenics (Tanaka et al., 2007), we found that the trigeminal nerve continues to grow posteriorly beyond the intermandibularis posterior onto the interhyoideus muscle around 53 hpf before the facial nerve enters this territory (Figure 3a–f, Movie 2, n = 6). Previous analyses did not characterize the full extent of innervation of the trigeminal nerve after the facial nerve entered the ventral musculature.

To study the trigeminal motor contribution beyond this time, we turned to image calculations using FIJI/ImageJ with high‐resolution confocal images of hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede;isl1:mRFP fish at 5 dpf (image reused from Figure 1c,d). The motor component (labeled with Islet1 in red) is present in both trigeminal and facial motor axons (Figure 3g,k). The Hoxb1 transgenic labels the sensory and motor facial axons in green (Figure 3h,l). We applied the “AND” function (i.e., green and red) in FIJI to extract the facial motor component (Figure 3i,m). We then subtracted the derived facial motor component from the total motor component (isl1 channel) to obtain the trigeminal motor component (Figure 3j,n). Results based on these image calculations indicated that the trigeminal motor component is not only present in the interhyoideus muscles (white arrowhead in Figure 3j) but also in the hyohyoideus inferior muscles (white arrowhead in Figure 3n) (n = 5). The hyohyoideus superior muscles were, however, devoid of the trigeminal motor component suggesting that they are exclusively facially innervated (yellow arrowhead in Figure 3n). Overall, these results indicated a much posterior spread of the trigeminal nerve than previously realized.

Nitroreductase/metronidazole‐mediated ablation of Hoxb1a expressing cells between 72 hpf‐4 dpf does not affect the branchial muscle patterning

We complemented our computational approach with selective ablation of the facial nerve using the nitroreductase (NTR)/metronidazole system (Davison et al., 2007). Trigeminal and facial motor nerves are the only cranial nerves innervating the ventral branchial musculature. Unlike facial neurons, the trigeminal neurons do not carry a unique gene marker that can allow their visualization using a reporter line. We, therefore, decided to ablate the facial nerves in order to reveal the trigeminal innervation pattern in the branchial muscles. To ablate facial neurons, we expressed NTR in rhombomere 4 using a hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede;UAS:NTR‐mCherry line. The mCherry‐tagged NTR protein allowed us to trace the r4‐specific expression of NTR in red along with the Kaede protein in green at 24 hpf (Figure 4c–e). Fish exposed to 2.5 mM metronidazole from 24 to 48 hpf had a near complete loss of r4‐specific cells at 48 hpf (n = 8/8, Figure 4i,j). We also carried out immunohistochemistry using the 3A10 antibody to label the Mauthner cells which originate in r4. Metronidazole‐exposed hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede;UAS:NTR‐mCherry fish lost the Mauthner cells, demonstrating that this method can efficiently ablate r4‐specific neuronal cells (n = 3/3, Figure 4m,o). Visualizing the facial nerves in the branchial muscles of the metronidazole‐exposed hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:Kaede;UAS:NTR‐mCherry fish showed a complete absence of the facial axons in the ventral muscles at 3 dpf (n = 5/5, Figure 4k).

In mouse, migratory neural crest cells that populate the hyoid arch also express Hoxb1 (Arenkiel et al., 2004). Neural crest cells are responsible for patterning cranial muscles (Schneider, 2018; Trainor, 2010). Thus, loss of neural crest cells could indirectly (or potentially directly) disrupt axons projection patterns in cranial muscles. Post‐migratory neural crest in the hyoid arch stop expressing hoxb1a, while facial motor neurons retain hoxb1a expression (Arenkiel et al., 2004; Trainor & Krumlauf, 2000). In zebrafish, the precise timing of hoxb1a downregulation in post‐migratory hyoid crest is not well characterized. However, we observed that exposing hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry embryos to metronidazole from 24 to 48 hpf could disrupt muscle patterning (n = 6/6, Figure 5b), presumably due to remnant NTR protein in these cells. To determine a metronidazole exposure window that does not disrupt branchial muscle development, we exposed the hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry;unc:mCherry fish to 2.5 mM metronidazole from 48 to 72 hpf and 72 hpf to 4 dpf and analyzed their branchial muscle patterns at 4 dpf. We found that a 48–72 hpf treatment window would sometimes result in edema and subtle muscle defects in a few fish (n = 3/5, Figure 5c). However, all fish treated with 2.5 mM metronidazole from 72 hpf to 4 dpf had normal muscle morphology at 4 dpf (n = 4/4, Figure 5d). We, therefore, decided to use the 72 hpf–4 dpf metronidazole treatment window to analyze motor axon projections of the trigeminal nerve.

We exposed the hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry;isl1:mRFP fish to 2.5 mM metronidazole between 72 hpf and 4 dpf to ablate facial motor neurons and track trigeminal projections. A 2.5 mM metronidazole exposure between 72 hpf and 4 dpf effectively eliminated most Hoxb1a‐expressing cells with no observable facial axons in the branchial musculature (Figure S3, n = 3/3). We found that in the metronidazole‐exposed hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry;isl1:mRFP fish (n = 4/4), the hyohyoideus superior muscles were completely devoid of any motor innervation in the absence of the facial neurons (yellow arrow in Figure 5f). However, the hyohyoideus inferior and interhyoideus muscles had received some motor innervation even in the absence of the facial neurons (yellow arrowheads in Figure 5f). The only other cranial motor nerve reaching the ventral brachial muscles is the trigeminal nerve. Thus, presence of motor innervation in hyohyoideus inferior and interhyoideus muscles in the absence of facial neurons implies that these muscles also receive the trigeminal nerve as previously predicted in Figure 3 based on ImageJ calculations. Ablating facial neurons revealed the trigeminal nerve contribution to hyohyoideus inferior and interhyoideus muscles. This also revealed significant loss of innervation in the intermandibularis anterior muscles confirming contribution of the facial nerve to it (white arrowhead in Figure 5f). Since the facial nerve already enters the branchial basket by 72 hpf, cell debris from the existing facial axons after ablation can be observed in Figure 5f. Together, these results show that all ventral brachial muscles except hyohyoideus superior muscles receive both trigeminal and facial motor neurons in zebrafish.

We found that the trigeminal nerve enters the interhyoideus muscle as early as around 53 hpf (Figure 3a–f, Movie 2). We also wanted to determine the timing of the trigeminal nerve elongation into the hyohyoideus inferior muscles. As previously observed in Figure 5, 2.5 mM metronidazole treatment from 24 to 48 hpf in hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry;unc:mCherry fish totally disrupted hyoid muscle patterning at 3 dpf (Figure S4B), while the 48–72 hpf treatment window had mostly normal muscle pattern with occasional edema at 3 dpf (Figure S4C). We, therefore, used the 48–72 hpf treatment window in hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry;isl1:GFP fish and analyzed their axonal patterns at 3 dpf. We only analyzed fish which were devoid of edema and any visible muscle defects. In the control fish, the facial nerve had started to innervate the hyohyoideus inferior muscles at 3 dpf (white arrowhead in Figure S4G,I). In metronidazole‐exposed fish though there were remnant Hoxb1a‐positive cells in the hindbrain, we observed the complete absence of facial motor axons in the ventral branchial muscles (Figure S4M,O) in all fish analyzed (n = 3). In these fish, the hyohyoideus inferior muscles showed complete absence of motor innervation (Figure S4M–O). The motor axons were, however, present in the interhyoideus muscle (yellow arrowhead in Figure S4M). Collectively, these results show that the trigeminal nerve after innervating the mandibular arch muscles extends posteriorly to reach the interhyoideus muscle by 53 hpf and elongates into the hyohyoideus inferior muscles between 3 and 4 dpf.

The trigeminal nerve can form neuromuscular junctions in the interhyoideus and hyohyoideus inferior muscles even in the absence of the facial nerve

We next investigated if the trigeminal axons reaching the interhyoideus and hyohyoideus inferior muscles can form neuromuscular junctions. We detected pre‐ and postsynaptic terminals using Synaptic vesicle 2A antibody and α‐Bungarotoxin, respectively. Neuromuscular junctions are found throughout the musculature of control fish at 4 dpf (Figure 6a–c,g–i). In metronidazole‐treated hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry fish that lose facial neurons, neuromuscular junctions remain in interhyoideous and hyohyoideous inferior muscles (yellow arrows in Figure 6d,f,j,l), demonstrating functional innervation in these muscles (n = 4/4). However, the presynaptic terminals were completely missing in hyohyoideus superior muscles of these fish (white arrows in Figure 6d,f,j,l), confirming a lack of motor innervation when facial neurons are lost. Expectedly, normal neuromuscular junctions were observed in the sternohyoideus muscle even in the absence of the facial nerve (white arrowhead in Figure 6j,l), as it is known to be innervated by branches of the occipito‐spinal nerves (Schilling & Kimmel, 1997).

Next, we investigated if the neuromuscular junctions of interhyoideus and hyohyoideus inferior muscles in metronidazole‐treated fish are residual neuromuscular junctions from the ablated facial nerves. To test this, we treated the hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry fish with metronidazole between 24 and 48 hpf to ablate facial neurons before they can innervate the ventral branchial basket and before the hyoid muscles differentiate (Schilling & Kimmel, 1997, Figure S5A (generated with BioRender)). As previously described, this treatment window disrupts hyoid muscle patterning (Figure 5b, Figure S5E–G). Interestingly, even mispatterned, interhyoideus and hyohyoideus inferior muscles formed neuromuscular junctions at 4 dpf (arrows in Figure S5G). However, the mispatterned hyohyoideus superior muscle fibers were completely devoid of presynaptic terminals (arrowhead in Figure S5E,G, n = 3/3). This indicates that even when facial nerves are completely absent, interhyoideus and hyohyoideus inferior muscles can form neuromuscular junctions. Lack of presynaptic terminals in hyohyoideus superior muscles indicates absence of motor innervation in these muscles when facial neurons are absent.

By 4 dpf, the neuromuscular junctions in all ventral branchial muscles are well formed (Figure 6c,i). To test if facial axons are required for the maintenance of the neuromuscular junctions in the hyoid muscles, we treated the hoxb1a:Gal4;UAS:NTR‐mCherry fish with metronidazole between 4 and 5 dpf, that is, after the neuromuscular junctions are already established in these muscles. We found that the presynaptic terminals in hyohyoideus superior muscles were completely lost in the treated fish, while interhyoideus and hyohyoideus inferior muscles maintained both pre‐ and postsynaptic junctions (Figures S5H–J and S6, n = 4/4). This shows that the hyohyoideus superior is solely innervated by the facial nerve, but the remainder of the ventral branchial muscles are dually innervated. These experiments also demonstrate that facial innervation is necessary for establishing and maintaining neuromuscular junctions in hyohyoideus superior muscles but not interhyoideus and hyohyoideus inferior muscles.

DISCUSSION

This study uncovers two major events during zebrafish branchial muscle innervation: the anterior elongation of the facial nerve in the ventromedial mandibular arch muscles, and the innervation of the interhyoideus and hyohyoideus inferior muscles by the trigeminal nerve. These findings alter the currently understood cranial nerve innervation maps in the ventral branchial musculature (Figure 7). This also implies that the trigeminal and facial nerves do not remain confined within the mandibular and hyoid arch territories as previously thought. Rather, they might be collectively participating in supplying motor innervation to some of the brachial muscles. The significance of dual innervation in coordinating functions of these muscles needs to be further explored in future studies.

Evidence from comparative anatomy

The rostral location of the facial nerve in the mandibular arch territory has been previously described in several comparative anatomy studies. In 1903, Allis described the anterior location of the facial nerve and its physical connection with the trigeminal nerve in Scomber. He described that a branch of the facial nerve, the ramus mandibularis internus facialis, elongates anteroventrally and anastomoses with the maxillaris inferior trigemini branch of the trigeminal nerve, around the symphysis of the mandibles in Scomber (p. 270, Allis, 1903). In Polypterus, Allis described the presence of the ramus palatinus branch of the facial nerve in the anterior edge of the pterygoideus muscle and in the inner surface of the palatoquadrate cartilage. This facial branch also communicates with the ramus maxillaris trigemini branch of the trigeminal nerve (p. 280, Allis, 1922). The anterior branch of the ramus palatinus of the facial nerve also reaches the anterior entopterygoid and beneath the nasal capsule in Polypterus (p. 281, Allis, 1922). Also, in humans, the greater petrosal visceromotor branch of the facial nerve supplies the lacrimal glands and mucosal glands of nasalia (Nturibi et al., 2025). These anatomical studies document a much anterior location of the facial nerve, deep inside the dorsal mandibular arch territory.

Furthermore, Allis's anatomical descriptions of several actinopterygians provide direct evidence for dual innervation in ventral branchial muscles. In Scomber, he found that the ramus hyoideus facialis branch of the facial nerve innervates the hyohyoideus muscles and the geniohyoideus superior (=interhyoideus muscle). He further described that this facial nerve branch continues to run anteriorly, anastomosing completely with a trigeminal nerve branch that innervates the geniohyoideus inferior (=intermandibularis posterior). Allis further detailed that these two nerve branches merged so completely that he was unable to determine their respective limits and that the united nerve branches innervated both divisions of the geniohyoideus (=intermandibularis posterior and interhyoideus muscles) (p. 271–272, Allis, 1903). It should be noted that in osteichthyes, the intermandibularis posterior and interhyoideus muscles have also been called the geniohyoideus inferior and geniohyoideus superior, respectively. This geniohyoideus system of muscles in osteichthyes is not homologous to the geniohyoideus muscle of the tetrapods, which is innervated by the hypoglossal nerve (Diogo et al., 2008; Winterbottom, 1974).

Also, in the ventral branchial muscles of Polypterus, Allis described the ramus mandibularis branch of the trigeminal nerve, which innervates the geniohyoideus inferior muscle and then continues posteriorly to innervate the geniohyoideus superior muscle, similar to our finding with posterior elongation of the trigeminal nerve onto interhyoideus muscles in Figures 3 and 5. Allis further described that this terminal branch of the trigeminal nerve then meets the facial nerve branch and forms a continuous circuit in this muscle (p. 278, Allis, 1922). These anatomical descriptions not only describe the dual innervation of these muscles but also suggest a more anteriorly located facial nerve in the mandibular arch territory and a posteriorly located trigeminal nerve in the hyoid arch territory.

Functional relevance of dual innervation in branchial muscles

Previous anatomical observations by Holmqvist in 1910 and Luther in 1913 described the anterior section of the geniohyoideus (geniohyoideus inferior or intermandibular posterior) as trigeminally innervated and the posterior section of the geniohyoideus (geniohyoideus superior or interhyoideus) as facially innervated. Based on their description, each section of the geniohyoideus was thought to be uniquely innervated by either nerve. Allis, however, claimed that both facial and trigeminal nerves innervate both sections of the geniohyoideus muscles (p. 272, Allis, 1903; p. 278, Allis, 1922). Allis further argued that contraction of either of these muscles will have a similar effect on mandibular retraction or hyoid adduction. He contended that these muscles may be regarded as part of the same muscle separated by an aponeurosis (p. 255, Allis, 1922). Therefore, a continuous circuit of the trigeminal and facial nerve innervation in these muscles might be necessary to coordinate their synchronous contraction during mouth opening in teleost larvae.

Similar to Allis's findings, our study reveals that several ventral branchial muscles receive both trigeminal and facial motor branches. We found that in addition to the intermandibularis posterior and interhyoideus muscles, the intermandibularis anterior muscle and the hyohyoideus inferior muscles also contain both trigeminal and facial nerve branches. We also found that the trigeminal nerve can form neuromuscular junctions in interhyoideus and hyohyoideus inferior muscles, indicating a possible functional role. However, we were not able to test the presence of the neuromuscular junctions between the facial motor axons and the intermandibularis anterior and posterior muscle fibers, primarily due to the prior presence of neuromuscular junctions established by the trigeminal nerve, before the facial nerve enters these muscles. However, high‐resolution confocal images in Figures 1 and Figure S1 demonstrated a close association of the facial motor axons with the intermandibularis muscle fibers, suggesting that the facial motor axons might be innervating these muscles. The conjoint innervation of these four muscles might be linked to their common functional role during the process of mouth opening. For instance, the intermandibularis posterior and the interhyoideus muscles are known to jointly execute the task of lowering the jaws while elevating the hyoid bars in the larval teleosts (Diogo et al., 2008). Thus, from a functional perspective, anastomosis of the trigeminal and facial branches to jointly innervate these muscles seems reasonable, as previously discussed by Allis in other actinopterygians.

Likewise, the intermandibularis anterior muscle that runs between the rami of the mandibles may have a role to play during mouth opening. This muscle might be providing additional strength and stability during lower jaw depression. The hyohyoideus inferior that is inserted onto the ceratohyals functions as hyoid adductors and might additionally function to keep the hyoid skeleton fixed during lowering of the jaws. It is possible that the coordinated contraction of these muscles during lower jaw depression may necessitate their shared innervation pattern.

Hyohyoideus superior, on the other hand, coordinates movement of the branchiostegal membrane and is not linked to hyoid adduction or jaw retraction (Diogo et al., 2008). Correspondingly, we show that this muscle is singly innervated by the facial nerve, contrary to these other four ventromedial muscles, which receive dual innervation. The functional relevance of this collective participation of the trigeminal and the facial nerve in coordinating movements during respiration and feeding requires further detailed investigation.

Inter‐arch contribution in compound musculoskeletal units

Motor neurons and NCCs originating from the same progenitor rhombomere(s) invade the same branchial arch (Guthrie & Lumsden, 1992; Moens & Prince, 2002). This spatial registration between motor neurons, NCCs, and branchial arches upheld the notion of strict branchial arch boundaries and segmental restriction during branchial development. In zebrafish, this was supported by the works of Schilling and Kimmel, where they labeled individual branchial arch precursor cells and showed that their clonal progeny was restricted within the arch boundary (Schilling & Kimmel, 1994).

Confinement of the facial nerve in the hyoid arch and the trigeminal nerve in the mandibular arch was only ever traced up to 48 hpf by retrograde labeling using DiI injections (Chandrasekhar et al., 1997). However, we demonstrate that after 53 hpf, the trigeminal and the facial motor nerves cross the mandibulohyoid junction to innervate the muscles of the adjacent arches. This suggests that the segmental restriction and branchial arch boundaries may not be maintained beyond a certain developmental stage in vertebrates.

This is consistent with several compound musculoskeletal units which require participation of more than one arch. For instance, the levator arcus palatini muscle that functions in hyomandibular abduction is trigeminally innervated but is inserted on the hyomandibula, which is a hyoid arch skeleton. Dilator operculi is another trigeminally innervated muscle inserted on the opercle, which is considered a hyoid arch skeleton. Similarly, facially innervated adductor arcus palatini muscles are attached to the mandibular arch‐derived metapterygoid and entopterygoid. Also, several teleost muscles extend beyond their arch boundaries during late larval stages. One such group of muscles is the adductor mandibulae, which are trigeminally innervated and are initially attached only to the mandibular arch skeleton at 4 dpf. However, at 21 dpf, they extend posteriorly to insert onto hyoid arch derivatives, hyomandibula and preopercle (Diogo et al., 2008).

Collectively, our study shows that initially cranial nerves have a specific branchial arch target. However, at a later stage, cranial nerves breach the branchial arch boundaries and join hands to build compound musculoskeletal units derived from more than one branchial arch to fulfill a common functional role.

Developmental regulation of overlapping projections

Overlapping cranial nerve projections of trigeminal and facial motor neurons described in this study pose two developmental questions. First, does the overlap represent a stable feature of the mature projection in adult zebrafish, or is it a transient larval condition that is later refined or eliminated? Second, if these overlapping projections are a stable feature, then how is this convergence of motor neurons developmentally and functionally regulated?

Motor neuron pool identity and their peripheral muscle targets are specified by Hox genes and their transcriptional regulatory network (Bell et al., 1999; Dasen et al., 2005). Because Hox expression is nested rather than mutually exclusive (Chambers et al., 2009; Itasaki et al., 1996), neurons in adjacent segments share part of the combinatorial Hox code that might yield common targeting cues facilitating some convergent projections. Also, though this code is instructive, homeotic respecification and alteration of axonal identity in response to misexpression of a single Hox gene (Bell et al., 1999) show that this code remains plastic and can be modified at a later developmental stage. In this study, we have observed that trigeminal and facial nerves initially have distinct peripheral targets, projecting into mandibular and hyoid arch territory, respectively. However, after 53 hpf, they cross the mandibulohyoid boundary to anastomose together. It might be possible that the initial pathfinding of trigeminal and facial nerves as they enter their respective arch territories is guided strictly by the early hindbrain hox code. However, this initial code is later refined by secondary gene regulatory networks, allowing for the conjoint innervation necessary for the combined functionality of specific muscle groups. This possibility needs to be explored in future studies.

We note that in the absence of facial nerves, other cranial nerves can aberrantly misproject into the denervated hyoid muscles. This has been previously described in lateral rectus muscles which were shown to be aberrantly innervated by oculomotor nerve fibers in the absence of the abducens nerve (Whitman & Engle, 2017). This raises a possibility of whether the observed trigeminal innervation in interhyoideus and hyohyoideus inferior muscles in the absence of facial nerve was a result of trigeminal misprojection into the denervated hyoid muscles. While we have not directly tested this possibility, we observe that ablating facial neurons at an earlier developmental stage (24–48 hpf) and at a later developmental stage (4–5 dpf) show no difference in the trigeminal innervation pattern (Figure S5). An earlier ablation of facial neurons would allow a longer developmental window (from 48 hpf to 4 dpf) for the trigeminal branch to aberrantly elongate more posteriorly. While in case of late facial ablation, the trigeminal branch can possibly elongate posteriorly only from 4 to 5 dpf. However, in both cases, the posterior extent of the trigeminal elongation was found to be restricted to hyohyoideus inferior muscles (Figure S5), suggesting this might be the normal trigeminal innervation pattern. However, future investigation is required to test this rigorously.

It is known that misrouted axons are often pruned during later developmental stages. For example, retinal axons that exhibit pathfinding errors are typically corrected or eliminated later (Hutson & Chien, 2002). Similarly, aberrant projections of trigeminal axons into the hyoid arch, caused by the rostrocaudal reversal of rhombomere 3, were shown to be eliminated later (Warrilow & Guthrie, 1999). In our study, we have traced the innervation patterns of trigeminal and facial neurons up to 4–5 dpf. By this stage, zebrafish larvae possess fully functional branchial musculoskeletal elements capable of exogenous feeding (Sanderson & Kupferberg, 1999). Given that the trigeminal axons in the hyoid muscles and the facial axons in the mandibular arch muscles persist through 5 dpf, these projections may represent a functional, normal developmental state rather than a misprojection. However, by 30 dpf, juvenile zebrafish undergo significant morphological changes, including an elaboration of the adductor mandibulae system attached to the preopercle and hyomandibula. These structural developments accompany a shift in feeding behavior to “lateral abduction” (Diogo et al., 2008; Sanderson & Kupferberg, 1999; Yaniv et al., 2014). Future research is required to determine whether the conjoint trigeminal and facial nerve projections are maintained in the juvenile zebrafish following this transition in feeding mechanics.
