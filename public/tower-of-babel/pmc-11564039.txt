Opportunities for tuberculosis elimination in the Canadian Arctic: cost-effectiveness of community-wide screening in a remote Arctic community
Authors: Alice Zwerling, Edwina Veerasingam, Ellen Snyder, Andrea Schertzer, Keith Travers, Carolyn Pim, Chris Pease, Sandy Finn, Linette McElroy, Jean Allen, Mike Patterson, Gonzalo G. Alvarez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundIn response to a tuberculosis (TB) outbreak in the remote community of Qikiqtarjuaq Nunavut, Canada, community leaders and the territorial government initiated community-wide screening (CWS) for tuberculosis, an expensive undertaking given the high cost of providing medical services in the Canadian arctic. Our study aim was to assess the cost-effectiveness of the Qikiqtarjuaq CWS.MethodsWe developed a hybrid decision analysis and Markov model to replicate the experience and extrapolate CWS outcomes over a 20-year time horizon. Following a hypothetical cohort with patient characteristics reflecting the demographic and testing data available from the CWS, the model compared a one-time CWS intervention with the reference case of ‘no community-wide screening’.FindingsCWS resulted in improved health gains through prevention of active tuberculosis cases compared with no CWS. It also resulted in increased costs (measured in Canadian dollars), with a very low estimated incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of $25.10 (95% URs: cost savings-$15,874) per additional quality adjusted life year (QALY) gained compared with current standard of care approach (no CWS). Community-wide screening in this context would be considered highly cost-effective in this setting. In probabilistic sensitivity analysis, we found >99% of iterations were cost-effective at a willingness to pay threshold of $50,000/QALY gained.InterpretationWhile costly, coordinated and intensive community-wide tuberculosis screening activities are highly cost-effective in remote arctic communities when utilized in an outbreak context.FundingGovernment of Nunavut.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

There is limited economic evidence concerning the potential cost-effectiveness of community wide screening to achieve TB elimination targets in the Canadian arctic. We searched PubMed, Google scholar and MedRxiv for studies assessing cost-effectiveness of screening approaches for tuberculosis across populations living in the circumpolar region. The following search terms were used (“tb” OR “tuberculosis∗” AND (“cost∗”OR “cost effectiveness∗”OR “Economic”) AND (“screen∗” OR “community wide screening∗” OR “community based screening∗” OR “testing∗”OR “diagnos∗” AND (“Nunavut” OR “Arctic” OR “Inuit” OR “circumpolar region” OR “Alaska” OR “Greenland”. Our search was limited to economic studies in the circumpolar region of the arctic, without date or language restrictions. We identified 5 studies, 3 of which were authored by our team, while all were economic evaluations in the arctic, most focused on assessing specific diagnostics for active TB, while only one assessed a screening scenario among people without signs and symptoms for TB. This study in Nunavut will help inform public health policy, planning and practice across community wide screening approaches for tuberculosis.

Added value of this study

Our study is the first to use programmatic data to assess cost-effectiveness of tuberculosis screening programs in Nunavut. This study employed modelling parameters informed from empirical regional data collected as part of the CWS experience such as participation rates, and treatment acceptance and completion rates. Community-wide screening in this context was found to be highly cost-effective. In probabilistic sensitivity analysis >99% of iterations were cost-effective at a willingness to pay threshold of $50,000 CAD/QALY gained.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings suggest that, when used judiciously, and implemented under certain key conditions community-wide screening may hold great potential to be a highly cost-effective tool for achieving and sustaining TB elimination in remote Arctic communities across Canada.

Introduction

While tuberculosis (TB) incidence rates in the general Canadian population have seen a steady decline in recent decades, tuberculosis incidence among Inuit across Inuit Nunangat (Inuit homeland) have been steadily increasing since 2003.1, 2, 3 In 2021, the tuberculosis incidence rate among Inuit was 450 times higher than the rate in the Canadian born non-Indigenous population (135.1/100,000 versus 0.3/100,000).1, 2, 3

In 2018, the Federal government and Inuit Tapiriit Kanatami (National Inuit representative organization) committed to a 50% reduction in tuberculosis incidence by 2025 and to eliminate tuberculosis in Inuit Nunangat by 2030.4 An initial federal investment of $27.5 million over 5 years was put forward, a national Inuit tuberculosis elimination framework was developed and region-specific action plans were developed by each of the four Inuit regions—the largest of which is Nunavut.5

Community-wide screening (CWS) for tuberculosis is referenced as a potential strategy in the National elimination framework.4,6 It involves screening all members of a community for tuberculosis to interrupt tuberculosis transmission and disease by identifying and treating previously undetected active and latent tuberculosis.7, 8, 9, 10

The Nunavut government faces many challenges in delivering health care to remote Arctic communities: language and cultural barriers, communities accessible only by air for most of the year, inclement weather, unreliable, inadequate and expensive internet access, and chronic human resource challenges that include high turnover and limited tuberculosis care experience.5, 6, 7, 8, 9,11 These challenges exacerbate already extremely high costs of delivering the consistent and sustainable tuberculosis care necessary to eliminate tuberculosis.

In the context of a highly publicized tuberculosis outbreak in the small remote community of Qikiqtarjuaq, Nunavut, community leaders and the government of Nunavut Department of Health began initiated the first CWS in Qikiqtarjuaq in 2018.

Emerging studies suggest CWS may be cost-effective and in some instances cost-saving in the Canadian Arctic.10 Impact, effectiveness, and cost-effectiveness all depend on local epidemiology and screening practices. At present, it is not known whether CWS using new diagnostics and treatment approaches will be cost-effective in Nunavut. As further CWS activities are considered for other remote communities across the Canadian Arctic, it is critical to understand under what conditions and settings will these screening approaches be most cost-effective.

To address this knowledge gap, our study aim was to assess the cost-effectiveness of the CWS activity implemented in Qikiqtarjuaq in Nunavut from February to March 2018.

Methods

Target population, setting and location

The CWS took place in Qikiqtarjuaq, Nunavut (Supplemental Figure S1), which is home to 625 residents, 94% of whom are Inuit; 50% of which is under 24 years of age.12

In 2016 and 2017 the number of residents diagnosed with active tuberculosis was notably higher than in previous years. By late November 2017, more than 10% of the population was on treatment for either active or latent tuberculosis, leading to challenges in contact tracing and managing tuberculosis care with existing human resources in the community. In December 2017, the Government of Nunavut and community leaders decided to initiate a community-wide screening and treatment clinic with the goal of detecting, diagnosing, and treating all residents found to have active or latent tuberculosis. Due to the small size of the community and the desire not to stigmatize individuals already on treatment, all community members were invited to participate in screening.

A portable mini-clinic provided by Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC) was set up in the local community hall. A GeneXpert machine, chest X-ray equipment, and sputum induction equipment were established in the temporary clinic. Most healthcare staff, informational technology (IT) personnel, clinic supplies and equipment for the CWS were flown in. Operational challenges included establishing IT infrastructure (access to telehealth and internet), providing accommodation and food for the CWS staff and staff burnout.

Several community engagement activities also took place including community feasts, radio interviews, and question and answer sessions where the community and its leaders could ask questions about tuberculosis and the CWS activities.

The CWS clinic employed several diagnostic tools that had not yet been implemented outside of Iqaluit, Nunavut's capital city, including sputum induction, GeneXpert, and treatment of latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) with 12 week regimen of once-weekly dose of rifapentine and isoniazid (3HP).

Model overview

We developed a hybrid decision analysis and Markov model to replicate the experience of community members in a remote Arctic community and extrapolate screening and treatment outcomes over a 20-year time horizon to allow for the development of active tuberculosis over an individual's lifetime. We followed a hypothetical cohort with patient characteristics reflecting the demographic and testing data from the CWS in Qikiqtarjuaq. The model compared a one-time CWS intervention with the reference case of ‘no community-wide screening’. The ‘no CWS’ scenario replicated the current standard of care which included contact investigation, screening of high-risk groups, and passive case detection, and estimated the number of active tuberculosis cases that would develop in the absence of the CWS. In the CWS intervention scenario, we modelled community members through the screening activity (accept or refuse screening), testing, diagnosis, and accept/initiate or decline treatment, and estimated the number of individuals that would develop active tuberculosis. A simplified schematic of the decision tree is included in Fig. 1 and the Markov structure is depicted in Fig. 2. Model assumptions & analysis plan

The number of active tuberculosis cases, and persons with LTBI found during the CWS was informed from the CWS activity itself. In the No CWS arm, we assumed passive case finding or the standard of care applied where we assumed cases of active tuberculosis and LTBI could also be identified and initiated on appropriate treatment. This assumption was also applied to the Markov model structure where we assumed subsequent cases of active tuberculosis in either the CWS or no screening arms could be diagnosed and initiated on active tuberculosis treatment. We assumed the probability of having undiagnosed active tuberculosis and LTBI was the same among those who accepted screening and those refusing screening. Due to the high level of exposure in this small community, we assumed anyone diagnosed with LTBI had been recently exposed and therefore had a higher risk of developing active tuberculosis within the subsequent years. It was assumed that all persons diagnosed with LTBI or active tuberculosis were true positive cases. Assumptions were evaluated in sensitivity analyses whenever possible. We assumed adverse events experienced by those on LTBI treatment were mild (Grades 1 & 2), reflecting data from the CWS activity and additional research conducted in Iqaluit, and that completion rates for LTBI treatment were not affected by adverse events.13, 14, 15

In the hybrid model, the main effectiveness outcome was quality adjusted life years (QALYs) and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated between CWS and no CWS over a 20-year time horizon as incremental cost in Canadian dollars (2018)/QALY gained. Epidemiological parameters were sourced from the community CWS data and from the literature when necessary (Table 1). Utility data to parametrize the model was sourced from the literature using data reflecting the Canadian population.33 The CHEERS checklist was employed to ensure all reporting standards for economic Costing data

All costs were derived from the health provider perspective. We derived program expenditure costs for the CWS using financial records for consumables, equipment, salary, travel, and accommodation costs billed to the Government of Nunavut (GN) Department of Health for the CWS in Qikiqtarjuaq. The data included costs for setting up and transporting the mobile clinic, equipping the clinic with sufficient resources to test, treat, monitor, and record patient information, as well as staff salary, travel, and accommodation costs throughout the duration of the CWS. All costs are expressed in 2018 Canadian dollars (unless otherwise specified). These expenses were categorized under consumables, overhead, equipment, staff travel, staff accommodation, and staff wages.

Equipment expenditure was sourced from financial records and invoices, and these costs were annualized over the equipment's expected lifetime. This included items such as the digital X-ray machine and tele-health unit. Items categorized under consumables included gift cards purchased for screening participants (incentives) and medical supplies. This data was sourced from the government of Nunavut department's daily usage and inventory reports. Staff travel costs were derived from financial reports and included all CWS staff travel expenses (i.e., flights, and ground travel). For staff accommodation, data was sourced from CWS financial summaries and verified with hotel invoices. Staff accommodation included per diem costs, hotel expenses, and overnight accommodations enroute to and from the CWS. Staffing unit costs were ascertained using the Nunavut Employees Union collective agreement. A discount rate of 1.5% was employed annually for all costs and outcomes occurring in the future as per Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health (CADTH) guidelines.34 In addition to direct costs associated with screening, downstream costs associated with active tuberculosis and LTBI preventive treatment, treatment monitoring, and adverse events resulting from treatment initiated post-CWS activity are included in the cost-effectiveness modeling (Table 1) and were sourced from government of Nunavut financial records.

Sensitivity and scenario analyses

To assess the impact of key epidemiological and costing parameters, we conducted one-way sensitivity analyses across key parameters by varying parameters across plausible ranges (Table 1). A probabilistic sensitivity analysis was conducted across the 20-year time horizon using Monte Carlo sampling to generate 10,000 repetitions where parameter values are drawn from probability distributions to generate 95% uncertainty ranges (URs) around key model results.

Key planned scenario analyses included assessing differential completion rates for LTBI treatment experienced by those with adverse events versus those without adverse events, assuming a certain proportion of those with LTBI were remotely infected, and therefore conveying lower risk of developing active tuberculosis, modifying the underlying prevalence of LTBI to assess the threshold at which CWS may become cost-effective, similarly differential participation rates in screening, and differential rates of acceptance and completion of LTBI treatment. Model development and analysis was conducted using TreeAge software (TreeAge Pro, Health Care Edition, Williamstown, MA).

This work was funded by the Government of Nunavut Department of Health. The funder itself had no role in the design, conduct, analysis or in the decision to submit for publication. However, members of the Nunavut Department of Health were authors and reviewed and approved the final manuscript accordingly. Study results were presented to community members prior to final submission of the manuscript. This study was deemed to be exempt from full ethical board review as it was considered part of program evaluation activities by the government of Nunavut.

Results

Testing and diagnosis

All residents were invited to participate therefore some who were already on active tuberculosis or LTBI treatment underwent screening. Details of CWS are included in the supplemental. Demographics and co-morbidities for CWS participants and non-participants are listed in Supplemental Table S1 in the supplemental.

Among 514 residents screened, 11 individuals were newly diagnosed with active tuberculosis, and 157 CWS participants were diagnosed with LTBI. Additional outcomes are presented in Supplemental Table S2. All participants diagnosed with active tuberculosis were started on active tuberculosis treatment immediately. Among those diagnosed with LTBI during the CWS, 105/157 (67%) started LTBI treatment, and 78/105 (74%) completed treatment.

Costs of CWS activity

The total estimated cost was $1,336,357 in 2018. Staff salaries constituted the largest component of the total program costs at 29%, with staff travel and accommodation accounting for an additional 32% (Supplemental Table S3). Overhead was the second largest cost accounting for 24% of total CWS costs. While equipment was purchased expressly for this CWS activity, it has continued to be used either in CWS programs or for other programs and communities therefore we annualized the cost of the equipment in the calculation of per person screening costs. When annualization was accounted for equipment, these costs went from 13% of total costs to only 1% given the short duration of time equipment was used for the CWS activity.

Using the total costs for CWS we estimated a cost of $2600 per person screened. We also looked at several different scenarios that may more accurately reflect cost per person screened for future CWS activities in other communities, including cost per person screened using annualized equipment costs and excluding costs associated with Medevac. Medevac costs were paid to reserve the Medevac plane for the duration of the CWS and totalled just under $180,000 (and is included in above overhead estimates) however as this resource was not used, this expense was deemed unnecessary in future CWS. After excluding Medevac costs and annualizing equipment cost, per person screened dropped to $1816 per person screened (Supplemental Table S3).

Cost-effectiveness summary

In the hybrid Markov model we extrapolated CWS results using a 20-year time horizon. In this analysis we found CWS resulted in improved effectiveness (QALYs gained) compared with no CWS but also resulted in increased costs, with an estimated ICER of $25.10 per additional QALY gained (95%URs: cost savings-$15,874) compared with current standard of care approach (no CWS) (Table 2 & Supplemental Table S5 & Supplemental Figure S5). Using a willingness to pay threshold of $50,000 per QALY gained, as is typically used in the Canadian setting, this intervention would be considered highly cost-effective. Probability of undiagnosed LTBI was also an influential variable, with decreased LTBI prevalence leading to higher ICERs or worsening cost-effectiveness (Supplemental Figure S2). The model was highly robust, and in all sensitivity analyses ICERs were consistently under $45,000/QALY gained, below the $50,000 per QALY gained willingness to pay threshold to be considered cost-effective.

In one way sensitivity analysis when the LTBI prevalence was varied, it was clear cost-effectiveness of CWS programs is dependent on higher levels of LTBI prevalence, with a steep improvement in cost-effectiveness from 0 to 15%, with prevalence above 40% leading to cost-savings (Supplemental Figure S4). This finding further suggests CWS should be targeted towards communities with recent tuberculosis outbreaks or ongoing tuberculosis transmission. Other key influential variables included the annual rate of progression from LTBI to active disease (Supplemental Figure S3), when the probability of progression was decreased (p = 0.05 to p = 0.02), ICERs increased dramatically to $6217/QALY. We also investigated the influence of probability of participation in CWS (Supplemental Figure S3), with improved participation leading to improved effectiveness in the CWS arm.

Scenario analyses

Several key scenario analyses were investigated, including differential tuberculosis preventive treatment completion rates according to adverse event occurrence. When we decreased completion from 0.72 to 0.61 among those with adverse events while increasing completion to 0.90 among those without adverse events, CWS dominated over the no CWS option with improved effectiveness and reduced costs (Supplemental Table S4). Given the high rate of community exposure and the small highly connected community, we assumed all residents had been recently exposed. We investigated this assumption by varying the proportion with remote LTBI and associated lower risks of progression to active tuberculosis disease. When the proportion of remote LTBI was increased to 10%, with a commiserate drop in recent LTBI proportion, total costs increased in both the CWS and no CWS arms with an increase in the estimated ICER from $25 to $698/QALY. This is driven largely by decreased rates of progression to disease among those with remote infection compared to those with recent infection, therefore less cost savings due to averted tuberculosis cases over time (Supplemental Table S4).

Discussion

Using parameters derived from the community-wide screening in Qikiqtarjuaq, we used a health economic modelling approach to project potential benefits following a one-time CWS event. This model found community-wide screening to be a highly cost-effective activity with an incremental cost-effectiveness threshold of $25 per QALY gained compared with no community-wide screening over a 20-year time horizon.

Cost-effectiveness was influenced by several key factors. Given relatively high health system costs associated with tuberculosis care, costs associated with treating active tuberculosis were influential on model results. While costs of treating active tuberculosis in the absence of adverse events can be relatively low in remote Arctic communities, people with advanced pulmonary tuberculosis and/or disseminated tuberculosis disease may require costly medical evacuation, lengthy hospitalizations, longer treatment, and may result in higher incidence of post-recovery morbidity—or tuberculosis related death -all of which can exponentially increase costs. By averting active tuberculosis disease through early and efficient tuberculosis and LTBI detection and treatment programs, long term cost savings can be significant.

Underlying prevalence was a key factor, and in communities where LTBI prevalence is low (<15%), community-wide screening would be considerably less cost-effective particularly in the short-term, supporting the approach that community-wide screening should be targeted to communities where LTBI prevalence is known or expected to be higher, notably above 30%. In the scenario with increased LTBI prevalence coupled with improved participation, LTBI acceptance and completion, cost-effectiveness improved further, but it is important to note that 100% acceptance and tuberculosis preventive treatment completion rates are likely not realistic, nor can they be achieved without additional investment in programs such as education and treatment adherence support. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated cost-effectiveness improves as the proportion of recent infection increases, highlighting the utility of community-wide screening for intensive and rapid screening among communities with historically higher tuberculosis incidence or changes in tuberculosis activity suggestive of extensive or ongoing community spread.

It is important to note, that in addition diagnosis of novel LTBI, there were 11 cases of active tuberculosis identified through the community-wide screening in this small community. It has been hypothesized that indeed progression rates from LTBI to active disease are higher amongst certain Inuit populations, possibly due to more acute exposure or the presence of other risk factors, we could expect community-wide screening to be even more cost-effective as more active tuberculosis cases are averted. This also stresses the importance of having access to Inuit specific data for future analyses.

A key advantage of community screening activities is the ability to diagnose and initiate treatment amongst those with sub-clinical tuberculosis disease. While the benefits of early detection and treatment of active tuberculosis is not explicitly captured in this model, we would anticipate that early detection (compared to passive case-finding) would contribute to reducing if not preventing community transmission. Due to the small community size and limited data, a transmission model approach was not employed, and we are unable to assess impacts on transmission over time. However, we would expect earlier detection of both active and latent tuberculosis to lead to reduced transmission and increased cost-effectiveness estimates of community-wide screening.

One limitation of our study was the assumption participants and non-participants would have comparable rates of infection and likelihood of developing active tuberculosis, where in reality non-participants may actually have higher rates of infection. Previous diagnosis of tuberculosis or LTBI, fear of having to leave community for additional testing or isolation, or prior experience of tuberculosis -related stigma may contribute to reluctance to participate in community-wide screening. Cost-effectiveness estimates were not presented by sex/gender as rates of progression were assumed to be relatively similar across sexes. We assumed all cases of active tuberculosis would be identified and did not allow any spontaneous cure among untreated active tuberculosis disease. In our analyses these groups represent a relatively small proportion of the population and sensitivity analysis shows this group unlikely to influence cost-effectiveness findings. This underlines the importance of community engagement and community-wide screening should only be undertaken when communities are open and welcoming as high participation rates are important in ensuring successful and cost-effective programs.

Our study is consistent with a recent publication from Nunavik highlighting the potential cost-effectiveness of tuberculosis screening and prevention programs, highlighting consistent drivers of cost-effectiveness including cost of active tuberculosis disease and builds on earlier work by our team in Nunavut.10,13, 14, 15, 19, 27, 30 This study is the first to assess the cost-effectiveness of community-wide screening in Nunavut and modelling estimates benefited from regional empirical data collected as part of the screening experience, thus we were able to incorporate more accurate estimates of screening participation rate, LTBI treatment acceptance and completion rates.

Finally, it should be recognized, that as communities, regions and nations move closer to tuberculosis elimination goals, and tuberculosis case numbers and incidence rates are reduced, the cost-effectiveness of interventions, particularly those with large, fixed costs such as community-wide screening will become less cost-effective. Well targeted screening activities even in lower prevalence communities could still be quite cost-effective, yet careful consideration must be given to understand these changes over time and avoid withdrawing or reducing successful screening and tuberculosis surveillance activities too soon. Community-wide screening will have the most impact within robust, consistent and well-resourced tuberculosis care programs. Without the maintenance of well-functioning tuberculosis programs, recognition of indicators for initiating a community-wide screening may be delayed, and efforts to treat tuberculosis disease and tuberculosis infection identified during a community-wide screening will struggle. Failure to fully treat those found to have tuberculosis disease or tuberculosis infection during a community-wide screening reduces cost-effectiveness and the potential impact this intervention can and should have on reducing tuberculosis in Inuit communities.

Conclusion

The tuberculosis burden among Inuit in Canada continues to be disproportionately high compared to that of the non-indigenous Canadian-born population. While significant investments are required to reach tuberculosis elimination targets, community-wide screening can contribute to effective tuberculosis detection and prevention efforts without requiring Inuit to leave their communities. Our findings suggest that, when used judiciously, community-wide screening may hold great potential to be a highly cost-effective tool for achieving and sustaining tuberculosis elimination in remote Arctic communities across Canada.
