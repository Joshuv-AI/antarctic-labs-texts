Letter to “A High Frailty Burden is a Strong Predictor of Adverse Postoperative Outcomes in Geriatric Lumbar Spine Surgery: A Retrospective Cohort Study”
Authors: Yujia Cai, Xiaoting Li, Qingqiang Zeng
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the article by Cho et al, which compellingly demonstrates the modified 5-item Frailty Index (mFI-5) as a strong predictor of adverse postoperative outcomes in geriatric lumbar spine surgery. 1 The authors astutely attributed the paradoxical dip in in-hospital complications for extreme frailty groups (mFI-5 scores 4 and 5) to preoperative selection bias and surgical downgrading. While we commend this insightful interpretation, we believe the inherent limitations of the mFI-5 tool and the methodological nuances of large administrative databases warrant further discussion to optimize risk stratification.

While we commend Cho et al for their compelling demonstration of the modified 5-item Frailty Index (mFI-5) as a predictor of adverse outcomes in geriatric lumbar spine surgery, we believe the inherent limitations of the mFI-5 tool itself warrant further critical discussion.First, the mFI-5 predominantly captures visceral comorbidities but entirely omits the musculoskeletal and nutritional dimensions, most notably sarcopenia and malnutrition. By relying solely on systemic comorbidities, the mFI-5 fails to reflect the localized physiological reserve critical for spinal stability and postoperative rehabilitation.Second, the binary, unweighted scoring system of the mFI-5 may inadvertently conflate ‘state frailty’ with ‘trait frailty.’ Patients with severe but surgically correctable lumbar stenosis may be artificially upstaged in frailty, potentially leading to the denial of highly beneficial surgical interventions due to overestimated perioperative risks.Therefore, we suggest that future iterations of frailty assessment in spine surgery should transcend the mFI-5 by integrating imaging-based sarcopenia metricsand objective nutritional biomarkers. Developing a ‘spine-specific frailty index’ would provide a much more granular, accurate, and actionable risk stratification for this highly vulnerable demographic.

After examining the inherent limitations of the mFI-5 tool itself, we would like to gently supplement several methodological and clinical details inherent to large-scale management databases. While the authors skillfully employed IPTW to correct for measured confounding factors, the absence of spine-specific variables (such as bone mineral density, expressed in CT Hounsfield units) and precise surgical complexity remains a significant challenge. In elderly spinal surgery, severe osteoporosis is a major driver of mechanical complications, with studies indicating it more than doubles the risk of internal fixation failure. Without these detailed metrics, unmeasured confounding factors may slightly bias risk attributions toward systemic frailty.

Furthermore, when evaluating the 1-year incidence rate in patients aged ≥70 years, non-surgical-related deaths represent a significant factor. Traditional models that treat these deaths as missing data may inadvertently overestimate surgical-related events. The adoption of competing risk frameworks (such as the Fine-Gray model) in future studies will significantly enhance these long-term estimates. We extend our profound gratitude to the authors for their groundbreaking work, which was undertaken solely to enrich the ongoing dialogue on optimizing spinal care for elderly patients.

We suggest that future frailty assessments in spine surgery transcend the mFI-5 by integrating imaging-based sarcopenia metrics, bone quality indices, and competing risk models. Developing a “spine-specific frailty index” would provide a much more actionable risk stratification. We extend our profound gratitude to Cho et al for their foundational work and offer these thoughts merely to enrich the ongoing dialogue on optimizing geriatric spine care.
