Capability of dental practitioners to identify condylar changes on orthopantomograms: a multicenter cross-sectional study in Pakistan
Authors: Hafsa Ijaz, Muhammad Aamir Ghafoor Chaudhary, Syeda Laiba Tauheed, Maheer Shehzad, Haris Ahtasham, Hira Riaz, Syeda Kashaf Abbas, Javed Ashraf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA comprehensive understanding of temporomandibular joint (TMJ) anatomy and Condylar changes is pivotal in facilitating the accurate diagnosis and proficient management of patients exhibiting TMJ dysfunction. This study is focused on assessing the Pakistani dental practitioner's ability to identify condylar changes on OPGs and compare diagnostic accuracy across different dental specialities.MethodsCross sectional study method was used. WHO calculator was used to determine a sample size of 385, maintaining a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level. A non-probability consecutive method of sampling was used for the sampling process to ensure a steady inclusion of participants over the study duration.ResultsA total of 385 dental practitioners participated in the study, comprising 100 (26.0%) males and 285 (74.0%) females. House officers that participated in the study were 146 (37.9) 127 (33%) were postgraduates, 78 (20.3%) were general dentists and 14 (3.6%) were consultants. The results indicated that 251 (65.2%) of the participants observed the condyles on both sides in OPGs during their daily practice. Only 17.1% of practitioners demonstrated complete agreement across all six OPGs, highlighting substantial variability in diagnostic accuracy.General dentistry consistently demonstrated the highest accuracy rates in identifying condylar hyperplasia (61.9%), flattening (58%), Sclerosis (46.3%), deviation/dislocation (64%), hypoplasia (50.4%), and surface erosion (56.9%) in comparison to other specialities.ConclusionsThe findings of the study conclude that in certain cases, Pakistani general dentists performed better in terms of correctly identifying OPGs. However, in other cases specialists were equally proficient in recognizing various condylar alterations. This study identified significant gaps in knowledge in the correct identification of condylar changes among dental practitioners both at specialists and general dentist’s level. These results suggest us that additional training might be needed equally, at regular intervals for both general and specialist dentists.

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDS) are the most common orofacial pain disorder globally [1]. TMDS affect a significant portion of the global population (31%), although this varies significantly based on certain geographical regions [2]. Region-wise prevalences of TMDSs are highest in South America (47%)​, followed by Asia 33%​, Europe 29%​, and the least in North America 26%. Apart from geographical variations, several other factors also disproportionately affect the incidence and severity of TMDSs. TMDSs are generally more commonly reported in females, especially of working age [3]. This working age bracket in females is particularly affected due to hormonal and psychosocial aetiology also implicated in the pathogenesis of TMDS [4]. Pakistani working-age population also suffers hugely from TMDS [4]. A recent study involving dental students in a Pakistani dental college, reported that the overall prevalence of TMDS was 66.9%, with mild TMDS in 40.90%, moderate TMDS in 14.6%, and severe TMDS in 11.50% of the study participants [5]. Another study involving Pakistani university students reported an overall TMDS prevalence of 61.3%. Of these 61.3%, 58.1% of the study participants reported internal TMDS derangements, 15% of myofascial pain, 2.5% of osteoarthritis [6]. These findings suggest a high prevalence of TMDS among specific groups in Pakistan. However, comprehensive data on the general population's TMDS prevalence in Pakistan remain limited.

In clinical practice, TMDS pathologies are manifested through structural degenerative changes [5]. The Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMDS) primarily emphasize clinical assessments for diagnosing TMDS [7]. Imaging techniques, such as orthopantomograms (OPGs), are generally reserved for cases where significant structural abnormalities are suspected or when patients do not respond to initial conservative treatments [8].

Condylar changes observable on Orthopantomograms (OPGs) linked to TMDS may include hyperplasia, hypoplasia, flattening, erosion, sclerosis, and osteophyte formation. These alterations can suggest degenerative processes within the TMJ. A study comparing condylar bone pathology in patients with and without TMDS using OPGs found that certain bony changes were more prevalent in the TMDS group, indicating a potential association between these radiographic findings and TMDS presence [9]. While condylar changes on OPGs can aid in diagnosis, they are not definitive diagnostic markers within the DC/TMDS framework.

The current approach for a definitive TMDS diagnosis recommends advanced imaging modalities like magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT). Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT), an advanced imaging modality, utilizes X-ray beams in a cone-shape and a flat-panel detector to generate high-definition three-dimensional 3D images of dental and maxillofacial entities of a patient. Introduced for clinical use in the late 1990 s, CBCT has since matured diagnostic imaging in dentistry by providing multiplanar visualization with relatively lower radiation exposure compared to conventional medical CT scans. Its applications extend across nearly all dental specialties. In implantology, CBCT allows precise evaluation of bone dimensions, nerve pathways, and sinus anatomy, improving surgical planning. In endodontics, it enables identification of complex root canal systems, periapical pathology, resorptions, and vertical fractures, often where conventional radiographs are insufficient [10].

These imaging modalities are highly reliable and effective in identifying structural TMDS subclasses such as disc displacements or degenerative joint diseases. However, in clinical settings such as LMICs like Pakistan, these imaging techniques are typically reserved for patients with persistent symptoms, those unresponsive to conservative therapy, or when significant structural abnormalities are suspected and major reliance for TMDS diagnoses is made on OPGs at mass/general level.

Dental practitioners frequently encounter patients with head, neck and facial pain. Treatment of TMDS involves interdisciplinary approach including specialities like Dentistry. Therefore, knowledge regarding interpretations of OPG related to TMDS diagnoses, and accurate diagnosis is very important for early diagnosis, management and better treatment of patients.

Temporomandibular degenerative changes assessment and correct diagnosis is very crucial to detect any abnormalities and to provide effective TMDS Management. Early detection of condylar changes helps dental practitioners to prevent further joint deterioration, and it allows clinicians to monitor effectiveness of treatment provided [11]. It is hypothesized in the current study that Pakistani dental practitioners do not have satisfactory and needed skills to detect condylar changes through OPG.

This study aims to evaluate how well dental clinicians recognize and interpret condylar changes related to TMDS on OPGs. These evaluations will be further stratified based on seniority levels, and specialities of these dental for a more precise and targeted evaluation.

Methodology

Study population and sampling

This cross-sectional study was carried out at Islamic International Dental Hospital (IIDH). WHO calculator was used to determine a sample size of 385, maintaining a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level. A non-probability consecutive method of sampling was used for the sampling process to ensure a steady inclusion of participants over the study duration. The study aimed to assess the ability of dental practitioners to identify condylar changes on orthopantomograms (OPGs).

Inclusion criteria

Participants were included if they were actively practicing different dental procedures in specialties such as General Dentistry (n = 226), Prosthodontics (n = 76), Operative Dentistry (n = 62), Periodontology (n = 7), Oral Surgery (n = 11), or Orthodontics (n = 3). The age barrier began at 22 years of age and closed at 60 years of age respecting the service before retirement rule. House officers were also included among the participants under the category of general dentistry as they perform routine dental procedures and examinations in their house job rotations.

Exclusion criteria

Undergraduate Dental Students were excluded from the study to ensure participants had clinical experience in interpreting OPGs.

OPG image selection & standardization

Six validated OPG images illustrating condylar changes (Hyperplasia, Hypoplasia, Flattening, Surface Erosion, Sclerosis, and Deviation/Dislocation) were selected from hospital radiology records. Images were chosen based on diagnostic clarity, ensuring no artifacts or distortions that could impact interpretation. Standardization was achieved by using similar contrast, brightness, and resolution across all images. Ensuring all images were taken from the same machine settings to minimize variability. The images were pre-hand evaluated by two dental specialists i.e. a Prosthodontist and an Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeon and their responses were compared and correlated achieving and inter-rater agreement of 80%.

Consent and ethical approval

Written consent was obtained before enrolling all participants, and their confidentiality was ensured at all levels. The study included 385 Dental Practitioners practicing in various specialties. The participants were categorized based on their current professional status as House officers, Postgraduates, Consultants, or General Dentists.

Ethical approval Ref. No: IIDC/IRC/2024/010/002.

Data collection process

A structured questionnaire was used to gather demographic details (gender, specialty, professional status), and routine practice of OPG assessment. Identification of accuracy of condylar changes by showing six standardized OPG images depicting condylar hyperplasia, hypoplasia, flattening, surface erosion, sclerosis, and deviation or dislocation of TMJ. Dental practitioners’ responses were recorded as correctly or incorrectly classified for each OPG, individually.

Statistical analysis

Initial data entry and processing were performed in SPSS Version 26. Descriptive statistics included Mean ± SD for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables (e.g., specialty, gender, accuracy rates). Descriptive inferential Statistics comprised of a Chi-Square test to determine statistical significance between different specialties and their ability to correctly identify condylar changes. Statistical significance was taken p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Confidence Intervals (CIs): 95% CIs were used to assess the reliability of differences observed between groups. The study employed binary logistic regression modelling to predict outcomes based on various predictors, including gender, working specialty, current working status and observed condyles on both sides. Six logistic regression models were generated, each represented by different OPGs (1–6), assessing classification accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC).

Results

The reliability analysis of dental practitioners' ability to identify condylar changes on orthopantomograms revealed moderate internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha of 0.473 in the pilot study (n = 115) improving to 0.594 in the full study (n = 385). Item-total correlations ranged from 0.235 (OPG 3) to 0.494 (OPG 1), indicating varying degrees of item discrimination. Only 17.1% of practitioners demonstrated complete agreement across all six OPGs, highlighting substantial variability in diagnostic accuracy. The percentage of correct classifications was highest for OPG 4 (54.8%) and lowest for OPG 3 (28.1%). These findings indicate that while the questionnaire demonstrates acceptable reliability, there is room for improvement, particularly regarding the items with lower discrimination indices. Key findings of the models

Working specialty

In OPGs 3 and 4, working in the Prosthodontics department was significantly associated with higher odds of correct classification compared to General Dentists (OR = 5.89, p = 0.035; OR = 4.51, p = 0.029, respectively). For OPG 3, those working in Operative Dentistry demonstrated significantly higher odds compared to General Dentists (OR = 7.54, p = 0.017). For OPG 6, Periodontology clinicians significantly demonstrated higher odds compared to General Dentistry (OR = 10.05, p = 0.040).

Current working designation

Postgraduate students consistently showed either significantly higher or lower odds compared to House Officers across different models (e.g., significantly lower odds in OPG 1 (OR = 0.06, p = 0.010) and OPG 3 (OR = 0.08, p = 0.002); significantly higher odds in OPG 6 (OR = 4.05, p = 0.048)). General Dentists (OPG 2 and OPG 6) also showed significant results compared to House Officers (OR = 2.54, p = 0.004; OR = 2.02, p = 0.017 respectively).

Observation of condyles in OPG

The current study found that the observation of condyles was significantly associated with higher odds of correct classification (OR = 1.99, p = 0.008) only in the case of OPG 3.

Discussion

The current study assessed the capability of dental practitioners from various specialties in Pakistan to correctly identify condylar changes on orthopantomograms (OPGs) as a sign of TMDS. Results of this study indicate that only 17.1% of practitioners demonstrated complete agreement across all six OPGs, highlighting substantial variability in diagnostic accuracy and in half of the OPGs observed, General dentists demonstrated the better overall accuracy in identifying conditions related to TMD (such as condylar hyperplasia, flattening, sclerosis, deviation/dislocation, hypoplasia, and surface erosion) compared to specialists. Also, the diagnostic capabilities of the practitioners differed significantly based on their clinical years of exposure. This was manifested as general dentists and postgraduate students outperforming house officers in some cases. Dental practitioners identifying the condyles in the OPGs had higher probabilities of correctly diagnosing the condylar pathologies in the current study. Lastly, logistic regression results suggested that professional experience and exposure significantly dictated the clinical accuracy regarding radiographic identification of TMDS related condylar changes. This was manifested in three of the six (50%) OPGs identifications, where specialists (periodontology, prosthodontics) outperformed general dentists.

The current study comprised mainly female participants, which is a common scenario in almost all the dental and medical institutions of Pakistan. General dentists and house officers assessed the condylar changes, and they showed a higher percentage of answering correct questions [12], this was done due to a better ascertainment of the current knowledge levels of the recent graduate. Our study results indicated that only 65.2% of the participants observed the condyles on both sides in OPGs during their daily practice Postgraduate residents and consultants mostly observe change on OPGs that are related to their speciality only that [13].

TMDS disorders involve complexities involving the diagnosis, treatment, and management of TMJ disorders in dentistry [14, 15]. The Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMDS) is a standardized tool for accurate diagnostics. According to Taqi M, Specialists used different comprehensive tools as compared to diagnose and interpret different condylar changes [12].

The varying diagnostic accuracy among dental professionals, ranging from House Officers to Consultants, can be attributed to differences in clinical experience, recent training, and acquired academic knowledge. General Dentists, who demonstrated higher accuracy rates in identifying various condylar changes in this study, likely benefit from regular exposure to OPGs in routine practice, enhancing their diagnostic proficiency. In contrast, House Officers may have limited practical experience, while Postgraduates performance may vary depending on their specific training and exposure during specialization. Consultants might focus more on complex cases, reducing their engagement with routine OPG assessments. These findings align with existing research emphasizing the role of clinical exposure and continuous education in improving diagnostic skills across different levels of professional experience [13, 16].

OPGs are used as initial diagnostic tool for TMJ evaluation and interpreting initial diagnosis because this technique is minimally invasive and cost-effective for the patients. Despite OPGs having certain limitations like they provide two-dimensional imaging, resolution constraints and superimposition of structures OPGs remain a widely used, efficient, and accessible tool in dental practice. Due to limited sources availability in dental hospital and clinical settings, OPG is the most important tool for accurate diagnosis. Advanced imaging techniques like CBCT or MRI are used where detailed evaluation is necessary [14].

While reporting the logistic regression effect estimate results, for valid and robust inferences, the current study also relies on reports of Receiver Operating Characteristics (ROC), which are a fundamental tool for evaluating the diagnostic performance of binary classification systems, such as the ability of dental practitioners to identify condylar changes on OPGs. ROC curves aid in plotting the true positive rate (sensitivity) against the false positive rate (1-specificity) across various threshold settings. The ROC curve provides a comprehensive view of a diagnostic test's accuracy. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) serves as a summary metric, with values closer to 1.0 indicating superior discriminatory capability, while an AUC of 0.5 suggests performance equivalent to random chance [17]. In the context of this study, ROC analysis revealed that regression models corresponding to condylar hyperplasia, flattening, and sclerosis exhibited acceptable predictive capabilities, with Area Under Curve (AUC) values of above 0.7, indicating reasonably good discriminative power. In contrast, models associated with condylar deviation/dislocation, hypoplasia, and surface erosion showed moderate to lower discriminative abilities, yielding AUC values of below 0.7 respectively.

Several strengths can be attributed to the current study. Firstly, the multicentre nature of the study which aids in the generalizability of the findings of the study. Secondly, the focus on dental practitioners' diagnostic accuracy regarding condylar changes on OPGs is a significant milestone in the TMDS diagnostic hierarchy. However, several limitations merit consideration, these may include reliance on self-report questionnaires introducing potential biases, such as social desirability bias, where participants may provide responses, they perceive as favourable, and recall bias, stemming from inaccuracies in recollecting past learning experiences. These biases can significantly compromise the validity of the data collected [18, 19]. Additionally, the cross-sectional design, also employed in the current study, captures exposure and outcome data simultaneously, preventing the establishment of temporal relationships and limiting causal inferences [20, 21]. Accordingly, while the current interpreting associations between practitioner characteristics and diagnostic accuracy due to these inherent methodological constraints.

Another limitation worth mentioning is the small sample number of the dental specialists’ participation in the current study. Effects sizes manifested through small sample sizes carry the risk of imprecise and wider confidence intervals [22, 23]as seen in the results of this study. This lowers the reliability and caution for the interpretation of the results [24, 25]. This limitation acknowledgement is thus necessary for the careful interpretation of the current study results from this aspect. A potential remedy for strengthening this finding can be either a larger sample size for this strata or Bayesian knowledge update [26, 27] incorporation in the methodology of the study.

The logistic regression models highlighted the importance of specific dental specializations and working status categories as significant predictors influencing correct classification outcomes. This study identified significant gaps in knowledge in correct identification of different condylar changes among dental practitioners. The specialist most of the time only read the orthopantomogram for treatment plans related to their fields and TMDs are becoming very common problems nowadays including significant condylar changes [28, 29]. These results highlight that while certain condylar changes were accurately classified with high sensitivity, others demonstrated limited sensitivity despite higher specificity, suggesting that additional training or refined diagnostic criteria might be needed for less accurately identified conditions.

Future studies should consider larger datasets and explore additional predictors to enhance model performance and clinical relevance. Advanced imaging techniques, AI technology, involvement of multidisciplinary approaches will be helpful in the accurate diagnosis and treatment of TMDSs [30, 31].

Conclusions

The findings of the study conclude that in certain cases, Pakistani general dentists performed better in terms of correctly identifying OPGs. However, in other cases specialists were equally proficient in recognizing various condylar alterations. This study identified significant gaps in knowledge in the correct identification of condylar changes among dental practitioners, both at specialists and general dentist’s level. These results suggest us that additional training might be needed equally, at regular intervals for both general and specialist dentists.
