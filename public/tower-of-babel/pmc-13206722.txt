Transcriptomic data and editing rate quantification of apple knock-out transgenic lines for a family of 17 amaranthin-like lectins
Authors: Axelle Frantz, Antoine Bodelot, Nicolas Dousset, Elisa Ravon, Christelle Heintz, Emilie Vergne, Alexandre Degrave, Matthieu Gaucher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Apple amaranthin-like lectins (Malus domestica agglutinins; MdAGGs) are soluble proteins whose encoding genes are strongly upregulated by plant resistance inducers, such as acibenzolar-S-methyl (ASM), which provides broad-spectrum protection against apple pathogens, suggesting a potential role in plant defense. Here, three transgenic ‘Gala’ lines generated via CRISPR-Cas9-mediated editing of the 17 MdAGG genes are characterized. We report editing rates and RNA-sequencing–based quantification of transcript levels for each edited MdAGG following ASM treatment. Furthermore, the whole transcriptome of each transgenic line was compared to the wild type genotype to identify differentially expressed genes after elicitation. These datasets provide (1) insights into the variation in editing outcomes among MdAGG genes across transgenic lines and (2) a basis for assessing the transcriptomic consequences of editing an entire gene family within a plant genome.

Value of the data

Background

Amaranthin-like lectins are widely conserved across plant genomes and several studies suggest their role in resistance to pathogens [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. They have undergone a greater expansion in the apple genome than in any other plant species, with 17 gene-copies identified [8,9]. These genes and their corresponding proteins have been the focus of different studies, particularly due to their strong reactivity to the application of ASM, an effective plant resistance inducer against Erwinia amylovora (Ea) [9,10]. The 17 genes encoding amaranthin-like lectins (MdAGG) were edited in the Malus domestica cultivar ‘Gala’ via CRISPR-Cas9. Five transgenic lines were generated (mdagg-1 to 5) and this data set focuses on three of them. MdAGGs editing rates and related transcript accumulation were quantified after ASM treatment. The impact of these editions on the global leaf transcriptome was assessed after ASM treatment, allowing to compare the transcriptome of the transgenic lines to that of the wild-type ‘Gala’ upon chemical elicitation. Both approaches will allow to determine whether MdAGG editing only affects their own transcript accumulation after ASM treatment, or if it has other impacts on the transcriptome. In parallel to this study, phenotypic assays characterized their susceptibility to fire blight [3]. This dataset will provide a comprehensive support for the correspondence between susceptibility phenotype to a bacterial pathogen and gene expression in response to ASM.

RNA sequencing was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 PE100 platform. Output reads ranged from 33.3 to 42.5 million bases per sample and mapping rates from 85.73 to 87.49% (Table 1). Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Plant material, growth conditions and treatment

The CRIPSR/Cas9 editing technique was employed in apple plants (cultivar ‘Gala’) to generate targeted knockouts against the 17 MdAGGs. These transgenic lines were obtained as previously described [3]. Briefly, two gRNAs targeting conserved regions within the 17 MdAGGs were used to edit the whole family. These gRNA sequences were cloned by Gateway in the pDE-CAS9Kr plasmid [11] to create a binary vector targeting the 17 MdAGGs. This plasmid was incorporated into Agrobacterium tumefaciens, which was further used to transform apple.

In vitro apple shoots were cultivated and propagated as previously described [12]. They were micropropagated on MS salts [13] supplemented with 30 g/L saccharose, 0.5 mg/L 6-benzyladenin, 0.1 mg/L 3-indolebutyric acid, Agar (Condalab, Madrid, Spain). Timentin, kanamycin and cefotaxime were added at 150 mg/L, 100 mg/L and 300 mg/mL respectively for transgenic line culture. All in vitro plants were cultivated at 22–24 °C with a 16:8 h light:dark photoperiod (cool white fluorescent tubes, 40–60 mmol m−2 s−1). Every four weeks, they were transplanted on fresh medium to allow propagation and avoid nutriment lack. To allow rooting, they were transferred on an induction medium (MS salts supplemented with 20 g/L saccharose, IBA 3 mg/L, 7.5 g/L Agar (Condalab, Madrid, Spain) at 22–24 °C for one week in the dark. Then, they were transplanted on MS salts, saccharose 20 g/L, Agar 7.5 g/L to induce root expression for 3 weeks at 22–24 °C with a 16:8 h light:dark photoperiod. The shoots were acclimatized into greenhouse and grown under 22 °C, humidity rate 80% and shading of 500 W.m−² during 8 weeks after acclimation.

They were treated with the plant resistance inducer ASM (Bion® 50 WG, Syngenta, Basel, Switzerland) at 0.2 g/L-1 with a 750 mL hand sprayer for greenhouse grown plants.

Leaf sampling, DNA and RNA extraction/sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

For RNA-seq analysis, two biological replicates per condition were sampled 3 days after treatment, with each replicate consisting of two leaves collected from two individual plants. RNA extractions were performed as previously described [14]. Briefly, frozen leaf samples were ground in powder and total RNA was extracted using a NucleoSpin RNA Plant Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were generated using the Illumina mRNA Stranded protocol and sequenced with the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 PE100 reads technology (Génome Québec, Canada).

For MdAGG editing rate quantification, 5 independent biological repeats composed of at least two leaves of two plants were assessed. Genomic DNA was extracted as described in Fulton et al., 1995 [15], with the modification that the elution step was performed in water. Briefly, frozen leaves were ground in a microprep buffer composed of 2.5 parts of extraction buffer (sorbitol 0.35 M, Tris 0.1 M, EDTA 5 mM, pH 7.5), 2.5 parts of nuclei lysis buffer (0.2 M Tris, EDTA 0.05 M, NaCl 2 M, CTAB 2%), 1 part of sarkosyl 5% and supplemented with sodium bisulfite 0.5% and incubated in 65 °C for 30 min. DNA was then isolated by sequential chloroform, isopropanol and ethanol extractions, as previously described [15].

RNA and DNA concentrations were measured with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

qPCR

Editing rate of MdAGGs in each transgenic line was quantified by qPCR as previously described [14]. Primers designed to anneal immediately downstream of the Cas9 site of one of the gRNA targets are detailed in the same study. This design ensures that amplification occurs only from wild-type alleles, as all reported mutations were located within this sequence [3]. qPCR reactions contained gDNA, MESA Blue 2X PCR MasterMix for SYBR Green Assays with fluorescein (Eurogentec, Liege, Belgium) and primers at the concentrations previously specified [3]. Amplifications were performed using a CFX Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Data analysis

Reads were mapped onto the reference transcriptional units from GDDH13 v1.1 (available at https://iris.angers.inra.fr/gddh13/) using Salmon software [1]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined with DESeq2, including a Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate through the AnaDiff v4.3 pipeline [16]. The criteria for identifying DEGs were as followed: log2 fold change |logFC| ≥ 1.5 and p-value/BH ≤ 0.05.

Bowtie2 aligner was used to reach nucleotide-level specificity of the mapping [17]. Mapped reads were sorted, indexed and merged with samtools [18] before conversion into bigwig files with bamCoverage from deeptools [19] using RPKM normalization and a bin size of 10. Representation of the normalized read coverage gained from the RNA-seq analysis on the selected genomic portions containing MdAGG genes was performed with the “CoverageView” package (version 1.34.0).

Unmodified sequence rates were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Global editing rates (e) were estimated as follows: e = 1/x where x corresponds to the quantity of unedited DNA sequences of MdAGGs relative to Gala WT. Differences of editing rates between each transgenic line and Gala were tested with a Wilcoxon test.

All data were pre-processed using R. Venn diagrams were generated with the packages “ggvenn” (version 0.1.10) and “ggVennDiagram” (version 1.5.6). Other data were visualized using the package “ggplot2” (version 3.5.2) in combination with “ggpubr” (version 0.6.0).
