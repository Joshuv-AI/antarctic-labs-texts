Radiation therapy in a ductal carcinoma in situ patient with ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency: a case report
Authors: Iris Kang, Justin Park, Christine Yang, Aster Kim, Mary Tang, Kaye Lu, Azadeh Carr, Stephen F Sener, Rachel McGowan, Shoji Yano, Stella K Yoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrnithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency is a rare urea cycle disorder that impairs ammonia detoxification, increasing the risk of hyperammonemic crises under metabolic stress. While chemotherapy-related risks have been reported, data on the safety of ionizing radiation therapy (RT) in this population are limited. This case describes a 30-year-old woman with late-onset OTC deficiency diagnosed with right-sided ductal carcinoma in situ. She underwent segmental mastectomy followed by adjuvant 3D conformal RT (40 Gy in 15 fractions with a concomitant boost to 48 Gy). Her multidisciplinary care involved preoperative hydration, close nutritional monitoring, and serial ammonia measurements. She completed RT without treatment interruptions or signs of hyperammonemia. This case illustrates that breast RT can be delivered safely in patients with OTC deficiency with a multidisciplinary, preventative approach. To our knowledge, this is the first report describing RT planning and metabolic surveillance in this population.Keywords: Ornithine carbamoyltransferase deficiency disease, Radiotherapy, Breast neoplasms, Hyperammonemia, Urea cycle disorders, Case reports

Introduction

Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency is an X-linked genetic disorder of the urea cycle caused by mutations in the OTC gene. The OTC enzyme is responsible for converting ornithine and carbamoyl phosphate into citrulline and phosphate, a crucial step in ammonia detoxification in the liver. The clinical severity of mutations in the OTC gene is determined by the type and position of the mutation, which can result in either complete or partial loss of enzyme function [1].

Hyperammonemic crises caused by the complete loss of enzyme function in neonatal-onset OTC deficiency can result in neurologic damage, coma, or death without timely treatment [1-3]. With partial loss late-onset OTC deficiency, the clinical presentation is often milder, with common symptoms including vomiting, lethargy, and confusion. Due to the often nonspecific and intermittent nature of such symptoms, diagnosis can be delayed or missed [4].

Metabolic disorders such as OTC deficiency can pose increased risk to side effects in cancer treatment, as chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiation therapy (RT) can induce significant changes in the body’s metabolic pathways. The metabolic stress caused by cancer treatment with disruptions in amino acid metabolism and nitrogen handling can potentially result in metabolic complications such as hyperammonemia, and underlying metabolic disorders can increase that risk [5].

Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women worldwide, accounting for 11.7% of all new cancer cases in 2020, and RT plays an integral role in treatment [6]. Adjuvant RT reduces local recurrence rate in both invasive and non-invasive breast cancer patients and is associated with improved overall survival in advanced cases [7]. Modern RT techniques enable personalized plans that optimize tumor control while minimizing exposure to adjacent healthy normal tissue.

While we found one case report on chemotherapy’s impact on patients with OTC deficiency, there is a paucity of data regarding the effects of ionizing RT in this population [8]. As RT remains integral in breast cancer treatment, it is essential for physicians to recognize potential risks of RT in patients with urea cycle metabolic disorders such as OTC deficiency.

Case Report

We present the case of a 30-year-old pre-menopausal female with a known history of late-onset OTC deficiency who was diagnosed with stage 0 (pTisN0) estrogen receptor–positive progesterone receptor–positive ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) of the right breast. The tumor was found following a 1-week history of sharp breast pain. Diagnostic mammogram and ultrasound revealed 5 mm grouped calcifications in the right upper outer quadrant of the breast (Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System 4A). Core needle biopsy confirmed DCIS. She subsequently underwent Savi Scout-guided segmental mastectomy with final pathologic diagnosis demonstrating grade 2, cribriform-pattern DCIS with focal necrosis. No lymphovascular invasion was identified.

This patient had a family history of OTC deficiency. Two of her elder brothers died at 5 days and 14 days of age. She was initially diagnosed with OTC deficiency at age 9 after a two-year history of nausea that was exacerbated by infections. Gene sequencing identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the OTC gene (c.274G>A, p.Arg92Gln). During childhood, the patient experienced one to two hyperammonemia events per year, with symptoms of hand tremors, dizziness, and nausea. The patient’s current symptom management includes a modified-protein diet, glycerol phenylbutyrate, and oral supplementation with arginine, carnitine, and multivitamins. During acute hyperammonemia events, she self-manages them with oral electrolyte drinks as needed.

To minimize the patient’s risk of hyperammonemia during breast surgery, multiple perioperative precautions were undertaken. Her fasting time was minimized by scheduling her to be the first operative case that morning at 8:00 AM and having her consume a clear Ensure at 4:00 AM. She was also administered Dextrose 5% in Lactated Ringer IV hydration, before and during her surgery, to suppress catabolism.

The patient’s ammonia level prior to surgery was 25 μmol/L, and her ammonia level was monitored every 4 hours after surgery (Table 1, Fig. 1). At 8:00 PM on the day of her surgery, her ammonia levels increased to 63 μmol/L. However, the patient was asymptomatic, and the subsequent level decreased to 45 μmol/L. She was discharged the following day without any complications or signs of hyperammonemia.

At the radiation oncology consultation, approximately three weeks after surgery, the patient was recovering well with no reported concerns. Final pathology confirmed grade 2 DCIS, 9 mm in greatest dimension, without necrosis or extensive intraductal component. Surgical margins were negative with closest margin being 5 mm at the anterior margin.

The patient underwent computed tomography (CT) simulation scan without contrast with arms overhead on a breast board in the supine position. Prophylactic IV hydration with 10% dextrose with electrolytes was considered; however, ultimately deferred given her prior tolerance of diagnostic CT imaging and relatively low dose of RT exposure during simulation. The patient tolerated the CT simulation well without issue.

A three-dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3D-CRT) plan was generated to a dose of 40 Gy in 15 fractions to the right whole breast, with a concomitant boost to 48 Gy to the tumor bed in the right upper outer quadrant, in accordance with the protocol from clinical trial Radiation Therapy Oncology Group 1005. The beam arrangement included three fields in oblique angles: left and right medial oblique tangents with an additional left anterior oblique beam for the concomitant boost. Field-in-field technique was employed to minimize dose inhomogeneity. Table 2 contains dosimetric parameters and Fig. 2 shows representative images from the plan. In this patient’s 3D-CRT plan, excellent target coverage was achieved, while doses to organs at risk were kept low (heart mean dose 24.2 cGy, left lung V4Gy = 0%, right lung V16Gy = 4.2%). The contralateral breast dose was also minimal (maximum 74.4 cGy). These low heart and lung doses reduced systemic stress and may have contributed to the prevention of hyperammonemic crisis in the setting of OTC deficiency.

The patient received weekly image-guided RT with weekly MV imaging. The patient completed the prescribed treatment without any treatment breaks or interruptions. During the duration of her RT course, the patient was closely followed by the medical genetics clinic. The patient increased her oral nutrition supplementation from two to three times per week to at least daily. The increase in calories and protein intake likely helped prevent catabolism.

Throughout RT, the patient’s ammonia level was closely monitored due to the risk of possible metabolic complications (Table 1, Fig. 1). The patient’s baseline ammonia level prior to RT initiation was 27 μmol/L. Serial lab levels remained stable throughout RT treatment including 15 μmol/L after the first fraction, 14 μmol/L after the fifth fraction, and 14 μmol/L after the tenth fraction. During RT, she reported mild discomfort and firmness of the right lateral aspect of her breast, and on the last week of treatment experienced burning and redness of the skin of the right breast, consistent with grade 1 dermatitis. Her grade 1 dermatitis was well controlled with over-the-counter topical lotion use administered twice a day. Overall, she tolerated the radiation treatment well with minimal radiation side effects and no hyperammonemic events. She started adjuvant endocrine therapy with 20 mg tamoxifen daily.

Discussion

In this case, the patient had an established diagnosis of OTC deficiency and had an effective treatment regimen for symptom control prior to her diagnosis of stage 0 breast cancer. Through close multidisciplinary collaboration with nutrition and medical genetics, a combination of perioperative precautions, dietary interventions, and close monitoring reduced her risk of hyperammonemic crisis during her cancer treatment.

Cancer treatments such as chemotherapy and immunotherapy are associated with direct toxicity to metabolic pathways, endocrine function disruption, and systemic inflammation. These disruptions can cause metabolic toxicities such as endocrine disorders, dyslipidemia, and electrolyte abnormalities [8]. There are documented cases of cancer treatment unmasking underlying OTC deficiency or inducing metabolic toxicities that emulate hyperammonemic crises of OTC deficiency [9-12]. A single case report described a case of a heterozygous carrier of OTC deficiency undergoing treatment for breast cancer who tolerated chemotherapy well with nausea, no vomiting, and no hyperammonemic events. Her protocol included increased caloric intake, protein restriction, infusion of dextrose 10% solution to deliver 3 mg/kg/min of glucose until oral intake resumed, and filgrastim administration after each doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide dose [8]. This case report did not discuss the effects or safety of RT. To our knowledge, there remains a gap in current literature on RT and its potential metabolic implications in patients with metabolic disorders such as OTC deficiency.

RT can cause metabolic stress by inducing DNA damage and disrupting cellular processes. Ionizing radiation creates reactive oxygen species, causing cellular destruction and inducing systemic inflammation [13]. Metabolic pathways such as the pentose phosphate pathway and glutamine metabolism have been shown to have a direct relationship with RT in cancer treatment, as cells increase nucleotide synthesis to repair radiation-induced DNA damage [14].

Although a direct association with hyperammonemia and RT in cancer treatment has not been established, the theoretical risk of RT induced hyperammonemia has been studied in an in vitro study. Arginase is an enzyme that completes the final step of the urea cycle, and its sensitivity to RT was found to be related to induced hyperammonemia. The cells in this study were subject to RT ranging from 0 Gy to 8 Gy, and the results indicated that RT led to a dose-dependent increase in levels of ornithine, citrulline, and ammonia [15]. In addition, a randomized control study on mice revealed that RT increases polyamine synthesis in the brain, liver, and kidneys, consistent with radiation-induced activation of ornithine decarboxylase and increased release of free ammonia into the bloodstream [16]. These findings raise concern that RT may increase systemic ammonia levels, more so in patients with underlying OTC deficiency.

In contrast to the experimental data, our patient maintained stable ammonia levels throughout RT. Several factors may have contributed to this outcome. Given the theoretical clinical risk for a hyperammonemic event in our patient with OTC deficiency, proactive nutritional support with avoidance of prolonged fasting, protein restriction, and glucose supplementation helped minimized catabolic stress. Careful RT planning also helped limit radiation exposure to organs at risk, reducing systemic stress. The use of a 15 fraction regimen also contributed to avoiding larger single fraction doses that may have caused more profound metabolic stress. Serial ammonia monitoring also allowed for early detection of abnormalities, though none developed. The patient’s pre-existing diagnosis of OTC deficiency along with a multidisciplinary approach involving her surgeons, dietitian, geneticist, and radiation oncologist, allowed for successful and safe completion of her treatment course without a hyperammonemic event or treatment interruptions. The details and positive outcome in this case fill a gap in the current existing literature on the interaction between RT and OTC deficiency.

Given the metabolic stress that RT creates and the theoretical risk of hyperammonemia, heightened vigilance is crucial when providing RT to patients with metabolic disorders such as OTC deficiency. Despite theoretical concerns and limited data on the interaction between OTC deficiency and radiation exposure, this patient demonstrated that RT can be delivered safely with multidisciplinary proactive measures. This case report underscores the importance of close monitoring and multidisciplinary care and adds to the current literature on providing cancer treatment, specifically RT, safely to the population of patients with OTC deficiency.
