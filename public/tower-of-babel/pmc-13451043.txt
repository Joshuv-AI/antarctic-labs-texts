Implementing a professional youth mentoring program during times of crisis: lessons from the global pandemic
Authors: Koren G. Hanson, A. Karryn Satchell, Martie L. Skinner, J. Mark Eddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted traditional youth mentoring practices as social distancing limited in-person interactions. This study analyzes how Friends of the Children (FOTC), a professional mentoring program, was able to maintain engagement with mentees during the pandemic. Using a sample of 112 youth (58% Black/African American) with an average age of 12.5, this study investigated how FOTC continued to support mentees during this period. The amount of time and types of activities mentors engaged in with youth before, during, and after the pandemic were assessed to gauge how service delivery and interactions evolved during the crisis. Key factors supporting organizational resilience included professional infrastructure, specialized training, supervision, and peer networks, enabling mentors to sustain relationships and progress towards positive youth outcomes. These findings highlight the resilience of professional youth mentoring programs during a crisis and suggest that this model may help overcome disruptions while maintaining vital support during challenging times.

Introduction

Ecological systems (Bronfenbrenner, 1979) and positive youth development (PYD) (Lerner et al., 2014) theories have had a major influence on the fields of developmental psychology, developmental psychopathology, and prevention science over the past several decades in the United States. Core to these theories is that human development is impacted not only by the individual characteristics of a child, but also by their relationships—with parents, teachers, and other adults, and with family and nonfamily peers—and by the social contexts that surround those relationships throughout their development from birth to adulthood. Consistent with these theories, but long before they were articulated, mentoring programmes have been created throughout the last century to provide supportive positive relationships for children as they develop.

In the United States, youth mentoring programmes have often been employed as a means to provide support to children facing multiple challenges as they strive to achieve developmental outcomes (Raposa et al., 2019). Each year, an estimated 2.5 million children and adolescents are paired with caring adults through mentoring programmes (Raposa et al., 2017), and these programmes have shown promising PYD outcomes for at-risk youth in the areas of competence (academic/school-based outcomes, social skills, career development), confidence (self-esteem, self-cognition), connection (prosocial attitudes, connectedness to peers, family, and school), character (emotional/psychological well-being), caring/compassion (empathy, cooperation), and contribution (community/school service engagement) (Melton et al., 2026).

While mentoring programmes can take different forms, most match an adult with a youth to spend regular one-on-one time together in community settings over a predetermined length of time (often 1 year or less) (Goldner & Ben-Eliyahu, 2021). Central to all mentor–mentee relationships is the frequency, duration, and consistency of meetings, which have been shown to influence both the quality of the relationship and perceived levels of trust and closeness (Grossman & Rhodes, 2002). Review of mentoring programmes meeting high evaluation metrics show positive outcomes on a variety of measures (e.g. Blinn-Pike, 2007; Herrera et al., 2011; Austin et al., 2020), yet these same studies report that the gains made during mentoring have largely dissipated at follow-up assessments. The short duration of most youth mentoring programmes is not consistent with the focus of ecological systems theory on development over the entirety of childhood yet, maintaining a positive, supportive relationship across developmental stages is challenging. Most adult mentors in the United States are part-time volunteers; other mentors receive small stipends, and a very few are hired to work full time as professional mentors. These systemic challenges of mentoring programmes were highlighted with the onset of the global COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, beginning an unprecedented test for the endurance of mentoring relationships founded on frequent, consistent interactions with youth. This study seeks to assess how a professional mentoring programme was adapted during the pandemic to ensure continued provision of the important tenets of mentor–mentee relationships shown to improve youth development.

Mentoring during the COVID-19 pandemic

For many children and teens in the United States, the COVID-19 pandemic brought unparalleled disruptions and challenges, the impacts of which are still being felt today. Schools were closed, stay-at-home orders were enforced, social distancing became the norm, and social life, for many, went fully online. These situations impacted families in a wide variety of ways and, on top of this, parents potentially faced additional insecurities of job loss, furloughs, and reduced work hours. Social media and other digital communications filled some of the isolation created by lockdown orders, but this, in turn, created other types of voids. Research suggests that social isolation and the aftermath of such during the pandemic were associated with increased anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation in youth (Loades et al., 2020). Additionally, lifestyles were drastically altered during the pandemic, with decreases in access to quality foods, physical activity, and extracurricular activities, and increases in consumption of fast food and screen time, all of which combined to impact adolescents in ways that were keenly different from adults (de Figueiredo et al., 2021; Munasinghe et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2021; Scapaticci et al., 2022; Stavrinos et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020). Constants previously present in the lives of mentored youth, such as in-person regular meetings and activities (including recreational sports), were similarly impacted.

The few qualitative studies published about the impact of COVID-19 on youth mentoring programmes report mixed results as both youth and mentors adjusted to regulatory and other contextual changes. Wright et al. (2024) reported that pairs who engaged in online mentoring reported stronger mentor‒mentee bonds during the pandemic, along with the ability to focus on programmatic goals that had previously been set aside in favour of in-person activities. However, these benefits appeared to be offset by a lack of support staff and decreased youth participation (Wright et al., 2024). Preliminary research findings on participation in a cross-age peer mentoring programme during the pandemic suggest increased social support, prosocial pursuits, and purposeful engagement, but were limited to mentor–mentee pairs that were both of high school age (Patel et al., 2023). Studies of youth mentors using online focus groups report frequencies of connections with mentees at levels similar to prior to the pandemic, but altered communication patterns (Kaufman et al., 2022). In that study, mentors voiced the need for increased personal support through connections with other mentors to promote new ideas, keep interest going, and provide encouragement (Kaufman et al., 2022). None of these quantitative studies examines the amount of time mentors spent in contact with their mentees during the pandemic, the activities undertaken during that time, and how youth mentoring programmes adjusted over the course of the pandemic. This study seeks to fill that void and provide deeper insight into how mentor‒mentee relationships were impacted by global changes during COVID-19.

Professional mentoring

Unlike volunteer-based mentoring, professional youth mentoring programmes have the potential to provide services to youth facing obstacles such as poverty that may raise barriers to access for more traditional mentoring programmes (DuBois & Karcher, 2014; Eddy & Haggerty, 2026; Lakind et al., 2014). In some studies, experienced, more highly trained mentors (e.g. paid professional helpers, counsellors, social workers, therapists, paid peer supports) have been found to be more effective than other mentors, particularly those from non-helping field backgrounds (DuBois & Karcher, 2014; Grossman & Rhodes, 2002). Further, in some programmes, professional mentors serve in a long-term capacity, providing them the opportunity to support youth across multiple developmental phases (Eddy et al., 2017; Elledge & Cavell, 2026). Given the more extensive training and support infrastructures of professional mentoring programmes, they may be better able to adapt and adjust during challenging times (Haggerty & Skinner, 2026). Professional mentoring programmes can benefit from organizational structures that allow them to survive an event such as the COVID-19 pandemic through a combination of proactive resilience (absorbing the impact) and adaptive resilience (adopting new practices) (Giustiniano et al., 2018; Meyer, 1982). For example, paid and benefited staff may be more willing to continue as mentors during times of job insecurity; a pay-based organization may be able to provide professional development to staff to help with adjustment during times of crisis; and long-term, paid mentors may be better equipped to adjust to large shifts in the contextual fabric while still providing needed support and guidance to youth.

Friends of the Children (FOTC) is a professional youth mentoring programme designed to provide youth meaningful experiences with both adult mentors and a community of mentors and peers while empowering them to build life skills and make healthy choices (Eddy & Haggerty, 2026). FOTC is delivered through independent programme delivery ‘chapters’ in 22 states as well as several sovereign tribal nations and currently reaches thousands of children in over 1,600 schools nationwide. The programme selects youth aged 4 to 6 years who are showing clear signs of distress (e.g. acting out, withdrawn, anxious, depressed) and who face systemic obstacles to their future success, including having experienced multiple adverse child events prior to kindergarten. Youth are matched with a same-sex mentor and provided mentorship from prekindergarten through high school graduation and beyond (i.e. ‘12 plus years’).

Mentors (who typically have earned a college degree or equivalent, have prior experience working with children/teens, and have knowledge in the fields of education, social work, youth development, and/or school-based programmes) are hired by local FOTC chapters as full-time paid employees with benefits, and provided extensive training, support, and supervision. Each mentor serves approximately 8 to 10 youth at a time, and focuses on developing nine research-based ‘core assets’ with each youth through a combination of ‘direct’ one-on-one time spent with the youth as well as ‘indirect’ time spent consulting with caregivers, teachers, school staff, and seeking out other community resources—efforts all meant to help ensure the positive social, cognitive, and emotional development of each child (Friends of the Children, 2024). Programme guidelines state that mentors should devote 12–16 hours per month to each youth.

Over the long run, the FOTC programme is designed to emphasize the development of values and skills that increase the likelihood that youth complete secondary education, delay family formation, and avoid contact with the justice system. To help ensure success, FOTC has a quality assurance programme administered by their national organization (‘National’) with which all local chapters are affiliated. National mandates that a variety of activities be carried out within each chapter, including training, support, and supervision. The existence of this overarching umbrella organization provides individual FOTC chapters with built-in resiliency, such as the anticipation of crisis (knowing what challenges chapters can face), on-the-ground coping strategies (accepting problems and implementing solutions), and successful adaptation capabilities (using knowledge to implement change) that allows chapters and their mentors to cope effectively with adverse events and adapt to changing conditions (Duchek, 2020). National also mandates that mentors enter comprehensive information into a centralized data system about their interactions with youth, including the frequency and duration of contact with the youth, their caregivers, teachers, or school staff, and notes on the types of activities occurring at each meeting. The existence of these in-depth records provides a unique opportunity to investigate how a professional mentoring programme adapted and evolved when faced with the challenges of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Current study

In this study, we examine the amount of time and types of activities in which professional mentors engaged with FOTC youth before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic to gauge how service delivery and interactions were impacted. The amount of time spent by mentors is defined as the total number of hours devoted to each youth each month, and this is divided into direct and indirect time. Specifically, ‘direct time’ refers to time spent in contact with the youth either in person or remotely, including activities such as engaging in outings, emailing/texting, visiting the youth at home or school, and providing transportation to various activities. In contrast, ‘indirect time’ is time spent in contact with someone connected to the youth (e.g. caregivers, teachers, counsellors) and any planning or training related to the youth (e.g. planning outings, searching for referrals, researching opportunities to pursue an area of youth interest such as sports or music). Within each type of time (i.e. direct and indirect), the frequency of specific activities was also examined to determine how the pandemic impacted the types of activities engaged in by mentor–mentee pairs. Finally, the division of total time between direct and indirect, as well as the frequency of activities, were compared before, during, and after the pandemic to identify how COVID-19 social distancing mandates impacted FOTC mentor–mentee interactions.

Methods

Procedure

Data for this study were originally collected as part of a longitudinal randomized controlled trial (Eddy et al., 2017). Potential study participants were randomly assigned to receive professional mentoring (i.e. the intervention condition—mentoring delivered through FOTC) or not (i.e. the control condition—no services were provided by FOTC). Their caregivers were then contacted, the trial was described, and, if interested, they were invited to participate in the informed consent process. If a caregiver consented, their child was invited to participate in an assent process that paralleled the informed consent process. If a child assented, they were enroled in the trial (see Eddy et al., 2017 for full consent procedures). Enrolment took place across several years—between 2008 and 2010—and within four cities across the United States (i.e. Portland [Oregon], Seattle, New York City, Boston) and their respective FOTC chapters. Thus, there were multiple cohorts in the study, each starting in a different year, and all children do not reach the endpoint of the study in the same year. The original sample size was 278, including 156 intervention condition participants and 122 control condition participants (see Eddy et al., 2017 for a CONSORT diagram). All procedures for the current study were reviewed and approved by the University of Washington Human Subjects Division (CR00009762), and participants consented as adults (18 years old or older) to all phases of data collection, including the use of administrative data and programme mentoring records. Data provided to researchers for this paper from National did not contain any identifying information for either youth or mentors. Unique identification numbers in the National dataset allowed information on service delivery and interactions to be merged across years, and ethical oversight for data collection was managed by National as part of their organizational mandate. Additionally, all FOTC mentors and staff underwent human subject training.

Enroled children assigned to the intervention condition were matched with a mentor. Once a child began to meet with their mentor, data on the amount of time spent with each child was recorded by their mentor, the mentor’s supervisor, a family engagement specialist, and/or a programme director. This was typically done on a daily basis, but at a minimum on a weekly basis. Logging this type of information continued until a child either dropped out of the programme or graduated from the programme (i.e. typically after high school graduation). These records contained information about the type of time (direct versus indirect) and the number of hours spent with each youth. Mentors also reported how that time was spent by selecting various activities from a menu of options and providing detailed descriptions of the activities in open-ended fields. The data included here were collected through National’s single data-entry system (i.e. the ‘Efforts to Outcomes’ database, or ETO) from January 2016 through December 2023 when the youth in the study were in early to late adolescence. For the purposes of these analyses, the pre-COVID-19 time period is defined as lasting from January 2016 until February 2020 (just before stay-at-home orders were issued); the during-COVID-19 time period is defined as lasting from March 2020 until September 2022 (when U.S. President Biden officially declared the pandemic over in the United States); and the post-COVID-19 time period is defined as lasting from October 2022 through December 2023.

At the start of the period of observation (January 2016), 112 youth from the intervention condition of the original randomized trial were still enroled in FOTC. Over half of participants (58%) identified as Black or African American and 54% identified as female at birth (Table 1). The average age was 13, almost 90% received free or reduced-price lunch, and participants had been involved in the FOTC programme for an average of 6 years (Table 1). Twelve percent of the participants were from the Boston chapter, 22% from the New York chapter, 24% from the Seattle chapter, and 42% from the Portland chapter. The FOTC programme is designed to serve youth until they graduate from the programme, defined as occurring at a point soon after graduating from high school. Hence, each year the number of participants decreased due to programme graduation. Table 2 outlines attrition within each time period, with the majority of youth leaving the study after graduating from high school and, thus, from the programme. By the end of this study (December 2023), only 4 youth were still in high school and participating in FOTC.

Data management and analyses

Data were drawn from child-focused activity journals entered into the ETO system by mentors and other FOTC staff throughout the study period. The total number of entries into the activity journals for all youth combined per year ranged between 600 and 12,000, with an average of 6,969 per year. Although mentors could choose from a predetermined list of activity types for each entry, some categories with low frequencies were merged with others for analysis. These included combining interactions classified separately as electronic or in-person between mentors and caregivers/other supportive adults and those with school staff/teachers into single categories, classifying case workers or other FOTC staff as other supportive adults, and merging attending extracurricular activities with youth into the ‘Outing/activity (in person)’ category. Table 3 includes a breakdown of the final activity categories included in the analysis, along with examples of open-ended descriptions to provide insight into the types of activities within each direct and indirect category.

The weekly reported hours of direct and indirect time for each participant were summed by month, then averaged across all participants to create monthly time totals. The frequency of activities was calculated as a percentage of the number of times engaging in each type of activity across all activities. Activity frequencies were then computed at bimonthly levels over the pre-, during-, and post-COVID time periods. To assess changes between time periods, one-way ANOVAs were conducted with post-hoc pairwise comparisons, and p-values were highlighted for differences that reached statistical significance. Given the exploratory nature of this analysis and the limited sample size that decreases over time, covariates were not assessed and results are reported for the sample as a whole.

Results

Distribution of time

Prior to the pandemic (between 2016 and early 2020), the distribution of time between direct and indirect activities remained fairly stable, with the average number of direct hours exceeding indirect hours by about 6.4 hours per month and the average total time per month ranging between 10.6 and 17.1 hours (see Table 4). When stay-at-home orders were executed in March 2020, FOTC mentoring relationships were almost immediately impacted, with mentor hours in direct-time contact with youth dropping below that of indirect hours by May 2020 (see Figure 1). This inverse relationship between direct and indirect hours was short lived, however, and by July 2020 average direct-contact hours once again exceeded indirect hours, although the margins had narrowed. Just over a year into the pandemic, by June 2021, things shifted again as the gap between direct and indirect hours once again widened, although levels did not return to pre-pandemic levels. As the world began to open after the pandemic, the distribution of time between direct and indirect hours followed a pattern established as the pandemic was waning and remained relatively stable throughout the duration of this study (Figure 1). Comparing average monthly direct and indirect hours across the three time periods, direct hours decreased during the pandemic from an average of 10.1 hours to 7.3 hours per month (p < .001, d = 2.09), then increased slightly after restrictions had ended to 8.4 hours but remained significantly lower than pre-COVID levels (p < .001, d = 1.43) (see Table 4). Indirect hours followed an inverse pattern, increasing during the pandemic from a monthly average of 3.7 to 5.6 hours (p < .001, d = −1.77) and remaining higher than pre-pandemic levels once the crisis was over (5.7 hours, p < .001, d = −2.73). Despite changes in how direct and indirect hours were allotted before, during, and after the pandemic, the overall average number of total hours dedicated to youth during all phases remained stable across all three time periods (Table 4).

Frequency of direct activities

As the average number of direct and indirect hours spent with youth shifted during the pandemic, the frequency with which activities were undertaken during those hours also changed. Outings/activities in person accounted for 61% of direct time spent with youth in January 2016 and decreased to 39% by February 2020; over this same period, electronic/virtual/written contact steadily increased from 23% to 47% of direct time, surpassing outings/activities as the most frequent direct interactions in late 2018 (see Figure 2). Almost immediately after lockdowns began in March 2020, the frequency of electronic/virtual/written communications surged from an average frequency of 41% to 67% (p < .001, d = 2.93) of direct time before dropping down to pre-pandemic levels once the crisis was over. Inversely, outings/activities in person dropped from an average frequency of 45% of direct time prior to the pandemic to down to 24% (p < .001, d = 2.63) during the pandemic, then rebounded to 54% (p < .001, d = −3.58) of direct time by the end of the study period (see Table 5). As the pandemic waned, outings/activities in person steadily increased, eventually exceeding pre-pandemic average frequencies and surpassing electronic communications for the first time in 5 years (see Figure 2 and Table 5). With access to meeting places limited, home visits doubled during the pandemic from an average frequency of 3.3% to 6.2% (p = .019, d = −.77), at times outpacing outings/activities during late 2021 through 2022 (see Figure 2). Review of qualitative data during this period described many of these home visits as food, supplies, or school-related drops (e.g. ‘Snack delivery and quick social distance chat,’ ‘Distributed food bags for families,’ ‘Picked up and dropped off cap and gown’) as mentors substituted formal outings with informal home visits. Other interactions dropped immediately after lockdown mandates were issued, including school visits (average frequency of 5.6% pre-pandemic to 1.0% during the pandemic (p < .001, d = 1.69)) and transportation other than outings/activities (from 3.2% to 0.7%, p < .001, d = 3.55), and, while they rebounded slightly once restrictions were lifted, both types of interactions remained significantly lower than pre-pandemic levels (see Table 5). Overall, the period when stay-at-home and social distancing mandates were in place shows a substantially different distribution of direct contact between mentors and mentees compared to both prior to and after the pandemic.

Frequency of indirect activities

The frequency of indirect activities was also impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic, although not as markedly as direct activities. The most common indirect activity across all three time periods was interactions with youth caregivers or other supportive adults and this remained relatively stable throughout the study (see Figure 3 and Table 6). Research and activity planning solidified itself as the second-most-frequent indirect activity during the pandemic, increasing significantly from an average frequency of 19% prior to the pandemic to 27% during COVID-19 (p < .001, d = −1.53); however, once the crisis ended, it reverted back to pre-pandemic levels (Table 6). Communication between mentors and school staff/teachers remained consistent across all time points, with lulls in the summer months, even when schools closed or shifted to online learning for much of the pandemic (see Figure 3 and Table 6). Prior to the pandemic, time spent on planning outings that did not occur accounted for an average of about 5% of indirect time; this decreased as the pandemic started to an average frequency of 1.8% (p < .001, d = 3.70) and remained low after the pandemic (average of 0.9%, p < .001. d = 4.93), freeing up significant time for other activities. In March and April 2020, National implemented revised professional development training, with a new emphasis on remote learning and interactions to ensure that mentors in all chapters had opportunities to learn skills necessary to work successfully in a remote environment (see Figure 3). The relative stability of indirect activities during and after the COVID-19 pandemic highlights how the FOTC programme adapted to comply with local mandates during the pandemic while still maintaining consistent contact with caregivers, other supportive adults, teachers, and schools. Additionally, FOTC was able to reduce inefficiencies such as time spent planning activities that didn’t happen, and implement relevant professional development programmes, effectively learning from the changes brought on by the pandemic.

Discussion

This study sought to investigate how a professional mentoring programme adapted to the restrictions put in place during the COVID-19 pandemic in terms of time spent with youth as well as activities undertaken during that time in order to investigate the resiliency of such a programme in adverse global conditions. These analyses utilized reports of the amount of time and types of activities spent with adolescents participating in the FOTC professional mentoring programme to examine how programme delivery and activities changed during and after the official period of the COVID-19 pandemic. Results showed that while the balance between direct and indirect time spent with youth changed significantly immediately after the start of the pandemic, the total amount of time dedicated to youth by mentors during these three time periods remained stable. Additionally, the types of activities shifted during the pandemic to allow for continued contact within the constraints of stay-at-home mandates while offering mentors professional development on how to provide support remotely. As the pandemic wore on, both youth and mentors were able to adjust and shift back towards a relatively pre-pandemic distribution of time and activities. However, some pandemic-enforced changes in FOTC mentors’ interactions with youth still lingered 2.5 years after the emergency started.

That the FOTC programme was able to quickly adapt to the challenges of the pandemic highlights the resilience of a professional mentoring programme. Studies show that volunteer mentor attrition has been linked to scheduling conflicts, disconnection from the mentoring organization, a lack of training or tools to deal with challenging situations, and the absence of interactions with other mentors (Lancaster et al., 2016; Murphy, 2020). The structure of the FOTC organization allowed chapters to overcome many hurdles facing volunteer-based programmes while adopting new practices and implementing changes needed to ensure continued delivery of services. For volunteer mentors, the reality of their children attending school remotely from home, losing their own jobs, caring for family members during illness, or providing support to those more isolated than themselves could easily take precedence over a volunteer commitment. A recent RCT of Big Brothers Big Sisters supports this, finding that 32% of treatment youth did not get matched during the pandemic, which was higher than the 25% of unmatched cases found in an earlier RCT of the programme (DuBois et al., 2025; Tierney et al., 1995). By providing FOTC mentors with stable employment during the pandemic, these mentors had one less responsibility to balance relative to volunteer mentors, allowing them to focus on serving youth. The professional nature of FOTC allowed the programme to proactively absorb the initial impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, aiding in the long-term resilience of the organization.

FOTC also excelled in adaptive resilience through adopting new practices and using knowledge to implement change. To address the issue of training, FOTC employed professional development opportunities for mentors almost immediately after the start of the pandemic. As part of their employment, mentors were able to dedicate time to learn how to operate effectively in a remote environment by developing new, specific skills. Trainings included remote learning skills, youth engagement strategies, and social and emotional well-being, as well as training on newly emerging topics such as cultural responsiveness in mentoring. As the pandemic lingered, isolation and loneliness became common complaints. FOTC recognized these barriers to remote mentoring and implemented strategies to overcome organizational disconnect and lack of support through continued group and supervision meetings. These activities allowed mentors and staff to maintain regular contact, while also encouraging mentors from across the nation to share experiences, generate new ideas, and provide emotional support to one another. The resilience of the FOTC programme to overcome potential barriers to continued service by offering employment stability, professional development, and mentor collaboration helped FOTC quickly adapt and maintain crucial relationships with youth during the pandemic.

The importance of internal organizational shifts within FOTC cannot be considered without assessing the impact of those changes on the youth supported by the programme. As noted earlier, ecological theory posits that social support, particularly from a parent or other supportive adult, is known to be a significant protective factor among adolescents, and this need was heightened during COVID-19 (Setyawati, 2021). Additionally, research during this time period shows that boredom was inversely related to emotional well-being, especially among young adults (Choi et al., 2021). The ability of FOTC to continue providing contact and services to youth during COVID-19 may have potentially mitigated some of the social isolation and boredom experienced by these adolescents. At a time when many youth’s microsystems were limited to immediate family, FOTC helped maintain vital links to individual support systems. Without the ability to come together through outings and activities, mentoring activities shifted to include interactions such as virtual movie nights, face-time check-ins, online gaming sessions, socially distant porch meetings, and remote homework help. This ongoing social network during a stressful time gave FOTC adolescents the opportunity to seek needed support from a familiar, mature, and trustworthy friend outside their immediate family, providing potential buffers against negative socio-emotional outcomes (Camara et al., 2017). The influence of FOTC during the pandemic may not have been limited to youth alone. Studies have shown that social support at the family level is predicative of engaging in positive coping behaviours, potentially alleviating some of the stress of isolation during quarantine (Moore & Lucas, 2021). The increase in home visits by FOTC mentors during COVID-19 provided such support as mentors were able to talk with youth during contact-free visits and drop off needed food and supplies for families. Delivering weekly groceries, dropping off care packages, shuttling school supplies, and providing general support not only aided the youth but also reduced burdens on caregivers during the pandemic. Families already stressed by the added obligations of homeschooling, potential job loss, and fear of illness had an extra person they could rely on for support, and youth had a stable friend able to reduce some of the isolation and anxiety brought on by the pandemic.

In addition to providing social support through direct and indirect contact with their youth, FOTC mentors also provided a vital link to school staff and teachers that overwhelmed caregivers may not have been able to sustain. Multiple studies have linked the COVID-19 pandemic to increased school drop-out rates, especially for disadvantaged students, often citing an inability to transition to online education, family or financial pressures, or disengagement from the school environment (Antoni, 2021; Farcnik et al., 2021; Tsolou et al., 2021). As schools closed and switched to online learning, FOTC allowed important secondary connections (Bronfenbrenner’s mesosystem interactions) between mentors and school staff and teachers to continue unabated. Mentors described meetings with teachers and staff to discuss how to support youth and keep them engaged during online learning, emailing counsellors to get updates on upcoming test/college application deadlines, collecting supplementary materials and supplies, and monitoring grades. While further research is needed to investigate whether this support resulted in higher graduation rates, all but one of the 97 participants in this study when the pandemic began were either still in high school or had graduated by the end of 2023. As many adolescents and families experienced shrinking environmental systems during the pandemic, the ability of FOTC mentors to retain connections with their mentees, families, and schools provided another layer of support for students and their families.

Given that FOTC is a long-term programme designed to provide services throughout the school years, the impact on youth development and outcomes is still being evaluated and is beyond the scope of this paper. Yet, evidence from prior studies show that even a short break in service can reverse positive development achieved during mentoring (e.g. Blinn-Pike, 2007; Herrera et al., 2011, Austin et al., 2020). The COVID-19 pandemic wore on for over 2 years, with the first months in isolation followed by slow re-openings that varied significantly by state. During that entire time, youth involved in the FOTC programme had access to their mentors and continued to receive services. Additionally, their families benefited from the outside social support built into the FOTC programme. Although the look and feel of that service changed during the pandemic and the ultimate outcomes on youth development still need to be evaluated, the resilience of the FOTC programme to absorb and adapt to external factors demonstrates that professional mentoring programmes may hold promise to positively impact youth development in times of crisis.

Limitations

The results of this study have several limitations. Given the longitudinal nature of this programme, it is important to acknowledge developmental changes facing study participants during the period of this particular report. As noted earlier, the average age of participants increased from 12 to 18.5 years over this observation period, likely bringing with it increased independence, less reliance on caregivers, and new interests that would potentially alter mentor‒mentee interactions in the absence of a pandemic. While it is impossible to tease out developmental changes from COVID-19 impacts, the data suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic had direct and important impacts on how FOTC operated during this critical period. Additionally, the sample size during the post-COVID period was small given that most of the youth had already graduated from the programme by that time. There is also limited information available on how other mentoring programmes operated during the pandemic, so we are unable to determine if FOTC was unique in its provision of services during the pandemic. Similarly, this study is also limited by its focus on a single professional mentoring programme in the United States and may not be replicable with other professional or volunteer mentoring programmes. Finally, we do not have comparison data from youth participating in FOTC during these ages in the absence of a pandemic to allow us to hypothesize that the results were most likely in reaction to the pandemic. Despite these limitations, the data do show that the pattern of direct and indirect time and the frequency of activities between mentors and mentees in FOTC changed during the pandemic before reverting back to pre-pandemic patterns, suggesting that the programme was able to adapt in a way that ensured that youth still received much-needed support during unprecedently challenging times.

Conclusions

Implementing a mentoring programme for youth facing multiple environmental stressors presents many challenges, and doing so during an adverse global event is even more difficult. During the COVID-19 pandemic, both youth and mentors were thrust into a world where traditional mentoring activities were restricted by local, national, and international regulations. At a time when youth were facing increased isolation and mentors feared financial losses, mentoring programmes were forced to reinvent themselves to continue providing much-needed services and support. The FOTC programme demonstrated its adaptive resilience by modifying activities while still maintaining the total amount of time dedicated to their mentees, showing the ability to respond and evolve during times of crisis. Methods of contact with youth shifted to adhere to social-distancing mandates, and activities adjusted as traditional meeting places were shuttered, all while vital contact with teachers and school staff remained unchanged. Important environmental systems between youth, family, friends, and schools were maintained at a time when many struggled with the detrimental effects of loneliness and isolation. Although not immune to the impact of the pandemic, FOTC displayed an ability to overcome many challenges, demonstrating that professional mentoring programmes can continue their planned provision of services even in times of crisis. The resilient infrastructure of a professional youth mentoring programme helps it respond to and overcome challenges, including providing specialized training, supervision, and support to mentors and offering mentors a network of colleagues to rely on as they strive to ensure youth continue on a path towards positive social, emotional, and physical development.
