Availability and Utilization of Radiological Guidance Tools in Interventional Radiology Units in Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of the Congo
Authors: Frederick Tshibasu Tshienda, Tasnime Hamdeni, Jean Mukaya Tshibola, Antoine Molua Aundu, Jean-Marie Kayembe Ntumba, Jean Marie Mbuyi Muamba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and RationaleVarious guidance techniques can be performed under ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT), fluoroscopy, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) guidance; They pave the way for simple, intermediate, and complex minimally invasive procedures by improving visualization of the target, the access route, and device progression.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the availability and utilization of radiological guidance tools in interventional radiology units in Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of the Congo.Materials and MethodsThis descriptive study was conducted in selected medical facilities in Kinshasa. Data were collected from January 2018 to June 2024 using a structured questionnaire and medical record review. Eligible cases included patients who underwent semi-invasive diagnostic or therapeutic interventional radiology procedures performed under direct imaging guidance, with acceptable hemostatic parameters and documented informed consent. The evaluated outcomes included the type of radiological guidance used, anatomical or disease target, procedure complexity, and procedure-related incidents. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations when applicable.ResultsA total of 471 image-guided interventional radiology procedures were analyzed. The evaluated cases involved a range of anatomical and disease-related targets, including liver, breast, thyroid, prostate, spinal, cervical soft-tissue, splenic, pancreatic, renal, lymph-node, and other abdominal or soft-tissue lesions. Female patients represented 55.3% of the study population. Among the 471 interventional radiology procedures analyzed, age was available for 469 cases. The mean age was 48.2 ± 17.2 years, with a range of 1 to 84 years. Ultrasound was the most frequently used guidance modality, accounting for 384 procedures (81.5%), followed by CT guidance in 63 procedures (13.4%) and fluoroscopic guidance in 24 procedures (5.1%). Cone-beam CT guidance was not used. Simple interventional radiology procedures were the most common, accounting for 327 procedures (69.4%), followed by intermediate procedures in 126 cases (26.8%) and complex procedures in 18 cases (3.8%).ConclusionThis study shows that ultrasound guidance remains the most commonly used radiological guidance method in interventional radiology settings in Kinshasa. However, MRI, fluoroscopy-CT, electromagnetic, and cone-beam CT guidance were not used in this series. These findings provide baseline local evidence on current technical capacity and unmet equipment needs, and may help guide future planning and development of interventional radiology services in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.Keywords: radiological guidance, puncture-drainage, interventional radiology – Kinshasa, DRC

Introduction

Guidance techniques in interventional radiology (IR) have enabled the emergence of a new and innovative field at the heart of the diagnostic and therapeutic management of a wide range of conditions, both neoplastic and non-neoplastic.1 These various techniques can be performed under guidance: ultrasound (US); computed tomography (CT); fluoroscopy; and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).2 MRI guidance has also become an important option for selected biopsies, ablation procedures and precision interventions.3–11 They pave the way for simple, intermediate, and complex minimally invasive procedures while ensuring maximum safety through precise real-time visualization of the target, the path to follow, and the progression of the device to the target.12 The indications for image guidance are continually expanding across multiple medical and surgical disciplines. The need for histological characterization, immunohistochemical typing, and the increasingly frequent use of molecular biology to guide therapeutic strategies have made percutaneous needle biopsies virtually routine in the diagnosis of cancer.1,12 The minimally invasive nature of procedures for draining deep collections has made it possible, in these indications, to replace a significant portion of surgical procedures.12 The development of thermotherapy, cryotherapy, and reversible or irreversible electroporation has given percutaneous guidance procedures a prominent role in interventional oncology.13,14 The choice of guidance modality is often multifactorial, depending on the availability of equipment, the technical complexity of the procedure, the operator’s preferences, and the specific advantages and limitations of each technique.15 It is essential to have a good understanding of the resources currently available in order to choose the most appropriate procedure. Although radiological guidance is widely used in interventional radiology, its practical availability and use remain insufficiently documented in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. In Kinshasa, interventional radiology activity is developing in a context where access to modern guidance modalities may vary considerably between institutions. To our knowledge, no previous study has specifically assessed the availability and use of radiological guidance modalities in interventional radiology units in Kinshasa. This lack of local data limits the ability to evaluate existing technical capacity, identify unmet equipment needs, and guide future planning for interventional radiology services. In resource-constrained settings, the development of image-guided interventional radiology depends not only on the theoretical advantages of each guidance modality, but also on the real availability of imaging equipment, dedicated interventional rooms, compatible devices, maintenance capacity, trained personnel, and institutional organization including advanced MRI-guided platforms and associated safety requirements.10,11 In Kinshasa and, more broadly, in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, local data on the practical use of radiological guidance tools remain limited. Documenting which guidance modalities are currently used, which modern tools are unavailable or underused, and how procedures are distributed is therefore essential for identifying technical gaps, planning equipment acquisition, defining training priorities, and supporting the future development of interventional radiology services. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the availability and utilization of radiological guidance tools in interventional radiology units and departments in hospitals in Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of the Congo.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

This study was conducted in seven selected medical facilities in Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of the Congo: the Kinshasa University Clinics (CUK), the Diamant Medical Center of Kinshasa (CMD/Kinshasa), the Medical Center of Kinshasa (CMK), the Presidential Clinic of the African Union (PUA), the Kingasani Hospital Center (CHK), the Pistis Medical Center in Limete (PMC), and the DRC Specialized Diagnostic, Expertise, and Interventional Imaging Center (CDSII/RDC). These facilities were selected because they had personnel, imaging equipment, and technical capacity allowing selected interventional radiology procedures to be performed on site.

Methods

The guidance techniques evaluated were: Ultrasound (US): Real-time guidance tools, ideal for superficial or abdominal lesions. Computed tomography (CT): Used for deep, bony, or pulmonary targets, offering superior anatomical accuracy. Image intensifier (fluoroscopy): Essential for monitoring drain placement and verifying patency via contrast injection. In addition to the tools available in radiology departments in Kinshasa, we also reviewed MRI guidance, Co-beam CT guidance, electromagnetic guidance, and Fluoro-CT guidance.3–11,16 Puncture and drainage devices; aspiration needles; micro-biopsy guns (core biopsy) for histological analysis. Percutaneous drainage kits (Seldinger-type systems or trocars), including guides, dilators, and drainage catheters, were also analyzed.

Study Design

We conducted a multicenter descriptive study assessing the availability and utilization of radiological guidance tools in interventional radiology practice in Kinshasa. The study combined an inventory of available radiological guidance equipment with a review of semi-invasive image-guided interventional radiology procedures performed during the study period. No qualitative analysis was performed.

Study Population

The study population consisted of all eligible semi-invasive diagnostic or therapeutic interventional radiology procedures performed under direct radiological guidance in the participating facilities between January 2018 and June 2024. The unit of analysis was the image-guided interventional radiology procedure. The radiological guidance modalities evaluated were ultrasound guidance, computed tomography guidance, fluoroscopic guidance, MRI guidance, cone-beam CT guidance, fluoroscopy-CT guidance, and electromagnetic guidance.

Sampling and Sample Size Calculation

The participating facilities were selected purposively because they had personnel, imaging equipment, and technical capacity allowing interventional radiology procedures to be performed on site. Within these facilities, all eligible semi-invasive image-guided interventional radiology procedures documented during the study period were included consecutively. A total of 471 procedures met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed. No formal sample size calculation was performed because the study was designed as a descriptive assessment of availability and utilization rather than an analytical comparison between groups.

Data Collection Procedure

Data were collected between January 2018 and June 2024 using two complementary sources. First, a structured questionnaire was used to document the availability of radiological guidance equipment and related interventional radiology resources in the participating facilities. Second, medical records were reviewed to collect procedure-level data for eligible image-guided interventional radiology procedures. The extracted variables included patient sex, age when available, clinical indication, radiological guidance modality used, anatomical or disease target, type of procedure, procedure complexity, nature of the procedure, equipment used when documented, and procedure-related incidents. The structured data-collection questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Material 1. Procedure complexity was classified as simple, intermediate, or complex according to anatomical accessibility, depth of the target, proximity to critical structures, guidance modality required, and technical difficulty of device placement. Simple procedures were defined as image-guided procedures involving superficial or easily accessible targets, usually requiring a single guidance modality and limited device manipulation. Intermediate procedures were defined as procedures involving deeper targets, a more constrained access route, drainage or injection procedures, or greater need for procedural planning. Complex procedures were defined as procedures involving deep or difficult targets, close proximity to critical anatomical structures, technically demanding access routes, multimodal guidance, or higher procedural risk. Procedure-related incidents were classified as minor or major. Minor incidents were defined as events requiring no treatment or only minimal conservative management, without prolonged hospitalization or permanent sequelae. Major incidents were defined as events requiring additional medical or invasive intervention, prolonged hospitalization, transfusion, permanent sequelae, or death. When no incident was documented in the medical record, the procedure was classified as having no documented incident.

Inclusion Criteria

Any radiology and medical imaging device (ultrasound, C-arm, co-beam CT scanner, fluoroscopy-CT, and MRI) used to guide diagnostic or therapeutic interventional radiology procedures; any semi-invasive image-guided interventional radiology procedures performed under direct medical imaging guidance; In this study, an invasive interventional radiology procedure was defined as any image-guided procedure involving percutaneous access to a deep organ, body cavity, bone structure, vascular structure, or pathological lesion for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes. Examples included deep organ biopsy, bone biopsy, abscess drainage, and image-guided epidural injection. A semi-invasive interventional radiology procedure was defined as any image-guided procedure involving limited percutaneous access or superficial tissue penetration, with lower anatomical depth or procedural complexity than invasive procedures. Examples included superficial soft-tissue biopsy, ultrasound-guided aspiration, and image-guided puncture of accessible superficial lesions. Any patient with hemostatic parameters within acceptable limits (Hb ≥ 10 g/dL and Hct ≥ 30%); Any patient who has freely consented to the interventional radiology procedure.

Exclusion Criteria

Any invasive or semi-invasive interventional radiology procedure performed without radiological guidance; Any patient presenting with hemostatic imbalance (TS, TC; Hb; Hct); Any interventional radiology procedure not consented to by the patient.

Bias Management

To minimize selection bias, only invasive or semi-invasive procedures meeting strict eligibility criteria were included (specifically, procedures performed under medical imaging guidance). Each procedure was carefully reviewed by a senior interventional radiologist to determine technical feasibility and the chosen radiological guidance method. A standard pre-procedure reassessment protocol for interventional radiology was applied to all patients, including the guidance method, antibiotic prophylaxis, rigorous skin antisepsis, and type of anesthesia. Data were recorded in a coded spreadsheet and statistically analyzed to minimize measurement and interpretation biases.

Data Analysis

Data were entered into an Excel spreadsheet and analyzed using Python software. The analysis was descriptive. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations when applicable. No inferential statistical comparison was performed because the study aimed to describe the availability and utilization of radiological guidance tools rather than to test differences between groups or establish superiority between guidance modalities. No formal trend analysis was performed; the study period was used to provide a cumulative overview of local availability and utilization.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health at the University of Kinshasa (CE ESP/UNIKIN) and the Ministry of Health (ref: ESP/CE/055/2023). In addition, written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to the procedure, thereby ensuring data confidentiality. Furthermore, all data were anonymized, securely stored, and restricted to the research team to preserve confidentiality and security.

Results

A total of 471 patients were recruited, corresponding to 471 interventional radiology procedures. All 471 procedures were classified as semi-invasive image-guided interventional radiology procedures, corresponding to 100% of the analyzed procedures. No separate invasive category was used in the analysis because the procedures included in this study were percutaneous image-guided interventions performed without open surgical exposure.

An Analysis of Radiological Guidance Tools Specifically

The analysis of the various guidance methods used in the radiology departments and units of Kinshasa in this series revealed a clear predominance of ultrasound guidance, which accounted for 81.5% of procedures, followed by CT guidance and fluoroscopic guidance. Discussion

An analysis of the various radiological guidance methods used in this series reveals that ultrasound guidance was used more frequently than other guidance methods. This was followed by CT guidance and fluoroscopic guidance. It should be noted that electromagnetic guidance, cone-beam CT guidance, and MRI guidance were not reported. It is worth noting that ultrasound guidance emerged in the 1970, when the first ultrasound-guided biopsies were performed.17,18 This study has several strengths. First, it provides original local data on the availability and use of radiological guidance tools in interventional radiology practice in Kinshasa, a setting where such information remains insufficiently documented. Second, the study included several medical facilities with interventional radiology activity, allowing a practical overview of the guidance modalities currently used in different healthcare settings. Third, the study covered a relatively long period, from January 2018 to June 2024, which strengthens the description of local practice over time. Fourth, the analysis documented both the guidance modalities that were used, mainly ultrasound, CT, and fluoroscopy, and the modern modalities that were not used, including MRI guidance, cone-beam CT guidance, fluoroscopy-CT guidance, and electromagnetic guidance. These elements provide useful baseline information for identifying current capacity, technical gaps, and future priorities for the development of interventional radiology services in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The predominance of ultrasound guidance in this series should be interpreted primarily in relation to availability, accessibility, portability, lower cost, and suitability for many superficial or abdominal targets. In the present setting, ultrasound appears to represent the most accessible guidance modality for routine interventional radiology procedures.17,19–24 CT guidance was the second most frequently used modality, although CT fluoroscopy has been reported to improve needle placement accuracy in selected interventions,25,26 but it represented a much smaller proportion of procedures than ultrasound. This distribution may reflect the more selective role of CT guidance for deep, bony, pulmonary, or anatomically difficult targets, as well as the greater logistical requirements of CT-guided procedures. Fluoroscopic guidance was used less frequently and was mainly represented in spinal or osteoarticular procedures. However, the high frequency of ultrasound use in this study should not be interpreted as evidence of superior effectiveness or safety, because diagnostic accuracy, technical success, therapeutic success, and patient outcomes were not formally assessed. Fluoroscopic guidance (C-arm) was used in this study. It allowed us to perform some osteoarticular procedures in the lower extremities. Endovascular procedures were not performed due to the limited performance of the fluoroscopy machines available at our centers.27 The absence of MRI-guided, cone-beam CT-guided, fluoroscopy-CT-guided, and electromagnetic-guided procedures is one of the main findings of this study.16,28–30 These modalities are described in the interventional radiology literature as advanced guidance options for selected complex procedures, fusion imaging, or procedures requiring high anatomical precision. Their absence in this Kinshasa series most likely reflects infrastructure constraints, including equipment availability, dedicated procedural environments, compatible devices, maintenance capacity, and specialized training. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted as evidence of a local technical gap rather than as evidence that these modalities are clinically unnecessary. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was limited to selected hospital facilities in Kinshasa, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other provinces of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Second, the retrospective nature of part of the data collection may have introduced information bias, particularly if some clinical, procedural, or outcome data were incompletely recorded in the medical files. Third, the study included heterogeneous interventional radiology procedures, anatomical targets, and clinical indications. This outcome heterogeneity limits direct comparison between guidance modalities, because ultrasound, CT, and fluoroscopy were not necessarily used for the same types of lesions, procedures, or levels of procedural complexity. Fourth, because the study was descriptive, it did not assess comparative clinical effectiveness, diagnostic yield, therapeutic success, patient outcomes, or superiority between radiological guidance modalities. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as describing availability, use, and procedural distribution rather than establishing the superiority of one guidance modality over another. Finally, the absence of MRI-guided, cone-beam CT-guided, fluoroscopy-CT-guided, and electromagnetic-guided procedures limits the ability to evaluate the role of these modern guidance modalities in the local setting. Another limitation is that, although the study covered the period from January 2018 to June 2024, no formal temporal trend analysis was performed. The study was designed to provide a cumulative descriptive overview of the availability and utilization of radiological guidance tools rather than to evaluate annual changes over time. Future studies should examine trends in the use of guidance modalities and assess whether equipment availability, training, and institutional organization evolve over time.

Strengths

Aside from the limitations listed above, this study has the merit of being the first study in the DRC to provide local evidence on the availability and utilization of radiological guidance tools in interventional radiology settings in Kinshasa.

Conclusion

This multicenter descriptive study provides baseline local evidence on the availability and utilization of radiological guidance tools in interventional radiology practice in Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of the Congo. Among 471 semi-invasive image-guided interventional radiology procedures, ultrasound was the predominant guidance modality, followed by CT guidance and fluoroscopic guidance. MRI-guided, cone-beam CT-guided, fluoroscopy-CT-guided, and electromagnetic-guided procedures were not identified in this series. These findings indicate that current interventional radiology practice in Kinshasa relies mainly on accessible conventional guidance modalities, particularly ultrasound, while several modern guidance technologies remain unavailable or unused. Because the study was descriptive, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical effectiveness, diagnostic accuracy, procedural success, patient outcomes, or comparative superiority of any guidance modality. The results may help identify technical gaps, guide equipment planning, support training priorities, and inform the gradual expansion of interventional radiology services in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.
