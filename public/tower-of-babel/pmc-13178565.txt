mTOR-NAA10-C7orf50 axis senses nutritional status to coordinate ribosome biogenesis and autophagy
Authors: Jingyu Sun, Mei Yang, Qian Li, Jiawei Liang, Haiping Feng, Lijie Gao, Yun Wang, Hongmei Liu, Caixia Guo, Tie-Shan Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellular metabolism is precisely regulated in response to nutrient availability. As an extremely energy-consuming anabolic process, ribosome biogenesis should be tightly controlled in response to nutrient supply. However, how the nucleolus responds to different nutrient statuses remains poorly understood. Here, we show that C7orf50 is a nucleolus-localized protein and functions as a coordinator between ribosome biogenesis and autophagy, acting as what we term a “nutrient-responding nucleolar factor.” C7orf50 undergoes reversible nucleolus-nucleoplasm translocation in response to nutrient deprivation and supply, with its nucleolus and nucleoplasm location dictating ribosome biogenesis and autophagic augmentation, respectively. The location-dependent function of C7orf50 is determined by acetylation at the lysine-71/lysine-72/lysine-76 residues by N-alpha-acetyltransferase 10, a substrate of mammalian target of rapamycin and a nutritional status–responsive acetyltransferase. In vivo and in vitro assays show that C7orf50 acts as an oncoprotein that promotes tumor growth. Our findings reveal a nucleolus-localized coordinating mechanism for the regulation of anabolism and catabolism transition by nutrient status.

INTRODUCTION

Cellular metabolic homeostasis is dynamically maintained through the coordinated regulation of anabolic and catabolic pathways, enabling adaptive responses to intracellular and extracellular nutrient status (1, 2). It is generally appreciated that there are crossroads between anabolism and catabolism in response to changes of nutrient status. When nutrients are deprived, intracellular anabolic processes are inhibited, while catabolic pathways are promoted (3). As an evolutionally conserved anabolic process, ribosome biogenesis is well orchestrated and tightly regulated by mitogenic signal inputs and cellular nutrient supplies (4–7). When cells encounter various stress conditions, such as starvation, ribosome biogenesis is reduced to conserve energy, which is vital for cell survival (8, 9). Meanwhile, autophagy, another evolutionarily conserved catabolic process that degrades cytoplasmic constituents and maintains cellular energy homeostasis, is induced to recycle nutrients for energy production and cell viability upon nutrient deprivation (10, 11). This mechanism helps to maintain energy homeostasis for metabolic fitness during cellular stress.

As the primary site of ribosome biogenesis, the nucleolus is the largest membraneless nuclear organelle and mainly consists of three distinct compartments: the fibrillar center (FC), the dense fibrillar component (DFC), and the granular component (GC). These compartments are primarily formed by low-affinity interactions between ribosomal DNA (rDNA) and/or ribosomal RNA (rRNA) with large numbers of nucleolar factors (12, 13). For many years, the best-known function of the nucleolus is thought to be the factory for rRNA synthesis and ribosome assembly. However, over the past two decades, the nucleolus has gained attention as a central hub for protein trafficking control of critical cellular processes, including DNA repair, cell cycle progression, tumorigenesis, and the cellular response to different types of insults (14–17). Nucleolar stress (NS), which refers to abnormalities in the shape and function of nucleoli caused by stressful events, impairs the ribosome biogenesis and, consequently, affects cellular homeostasis (18, 19). These findings support an important role of the nucleolus in sensing and managing cellular stress including nutrient deprivation. However, the understanding of how the nucleolus responds to changes in cellular nutrition and what molecular changes occur in the nucleolus to coordinate anabolism and catabolism remains limited.

In this study, through quantitative proteomic analysis, we have identified a nucleolar protein encoded by the human gene chromosome 7 open reading frame 50 (C7orf50) as a coordinator between ribosome biogenesis and autophagy. We have demonstrated that its nucleolar localization is essential for nucleolar homeostasis and ribosome biogenesis. Upon nutrient deprivation, C7orf50 is acetylated at lysines 71, 72, and 76 by the N-alpha-acetyltransferase 10 (NAA10), a mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) substrate whose phosphorylation is regulated by nutritional status. Acetylation of C7orf50 promotes its nucleoplasmic translocation, leading to decreased ribosome biogenesis and enhanced autophagy. C7orf50 undergoes reversible nucleolus-nucleoplasm translocation in response to nutrient deprivation and nutrient supply, with its functions in ribosome biogenesis and autophagic augmentation being determined solely by its nucleolar or nucleoplasmic localization. Moreover, C7orf50 is up-regulated in a variety of tumors, including breast cancer (BRCA), and acetylation of C7orf50 abolishes its role in promoting tumor growth. Therefore, C7orf50 serves as a molecular link coordinating ribosome biogenesis (anabolism) and autophagy (catabolism) to maintain energy and metabolic fitness during cellular nutrient stress. Given that tumors heavily rely on anabolic processes and the crucial role of C7orf50 in tumor growth, targeting it could be a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment.

RESULTS

C7orf50 is a GC-localized nucleolar protein

To explore potential nucleolar factors responding to nutrient deprivation, we purified nucleoli from HeLa cells with or without starvation treatment and analyzed them by nano–liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (nanoLC-MS/MS) using label-free quantitative proteome profiling (fig. S1, A and B). Differentially expressed protein analysis revealed that a number of proteins involved in the transcription and processing of rRNA, biogenesis, and assembly of ribosomal subunits were significantly down-regulated in the starvation-treated group (Fig. 1A). Notably, C7orf50, an uncharacterized human protein, was among the top 10 down-regulated proteins upon starvation (Fig. 1A and fig. S1C).

C7orf50 consists of 194 amino acids, with a predicted nuclear localization sequence (NLS) in the N-terminal region (fig. S1D). Using a commercially available antibody against C7orf50, we first examined its subcellular localization through cellular fractionation and immunofluorescence staining. We found that C7orf50 was exclusively detected in the nucleus of HeLa, MCF-7, and MDA-MB-231 cells, with a higher fluorescence intensity in the nucleolus (the nuclear region with no Hoechst signal) (Fig. 1B and fig. S1E). Immunofluorescence and cellular fractionation analysis of ectopically expressed C7orf50 tagged with FLAG revealed a localization pattern highly similar to that of the endogenous C7orf50 (fig. S1, F and G). This distinct localization pattern is clearly determined by its predicted N-terminal NLS sequence (fig. S1D), as deletion of which resulted in the failure of its nuclear localization (fig. S1G).

Further, by using nucleolar localization sequence detector (20), we identified two nucleolar localization signals, i.e., NoLS-I and NoLS (fig. S1H). Given that NoLS-I overlaps with NLS, NLS is used for simplicity thereafter. Through cell fractionation, we confirmed that both endogenous (Fig. 1C) and exogenous (Fig. 1D) C7orf50 was enriched in nucleoli. Notably, deletion of NoLS disrupted C7orf50 nucleolar localization, causing a diffusely nucleoplasmic distribution (Fig. 1E). To precisely delineate the localization of C7orf50 in nucleolus, we examined its colocalization with three marker proteins of the distinct nucleolar compartments, i.e., upstream-binding nucleolar transcription factor 1 (UBTF, marker of FC), fibrillarin (FBL, marker of DFC), and nucleophosmin (NPM1, marker of GC), separately (Fig. 1F). We found that both endogenous (Fig. 1G) and ectopically expressed (Fig. 1H) C7orf50 colocalized with NPM1 but not with UBTF (fig. S1I) or FBL (fig. S1J). Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) showed that C7orf50 interacted with NPM1 and nucleolin (NCL) (Fig. 1I), both of which are members of GC. However, UBTF and POLR1A (RNA polymerase I subunit A), proteins that are located in FC of nucleolus showed no interaction with C7orf50 (Fig. 1I). Together, these results demonstrated that C7orf50 is a GC-localized nucleolar protein.

C7orf50 depletion induces NS and impairs ribosome biogenesis

To elucidate the possible function of C7orf50, we performed immunoprecipitation (IP)–MS analysis to identify proteins that interact with C7orf50. Gene ontology analysis revealed that most of C7orf50-associated proteins were enriched in the pathways related to nucleolar functions (fig. S2A). Using BioPlex 3.0 (21) and STRING database to predict C7orf50 protein interacting network, we found that most of the interacting proteins localize in nucleoli (fig. S2, B and C). Further, Co-IP experiments verified that C7orf50 associates with several key components of the ribosome biogenesis pathway, especially ribosome biogenesis, ribosomal small subunit biogenesis, and ribosomal large subunit biogenesis (fig. S2D). A low concentration of actinomycin D is known to specifically inhibit rDNA transcription by POLR1 to induce NS (22–24). Notably, we found that nucleolar localization of C7orf50, either endogenously or ectopically expressed, was abolished from nucleoli upon actinomycin D treatment (fig. S2E). These data support a potential function of C7orf50 in nucleolus and a connection between NS and loss of C7orf50 nucleolar distribution.

To further elucidate the relationship between C7orf50 and NS, we performed immunofluorescence analysis using redistribution of NPM1, FBL, and UBTF as NS markers (17, 25). In most short hairpin RNA negative control (shNC)-treated HeLa cells, nucleoli exhibited irregular borders delimited by a thick ring of NPM1, with FBL and UBTF distributed homogeneously inside. In contrast, C7orf50 depletion resulted in more than 60% of cells displaying nucleoli with a round and regular shape delimited by a faint ring of NPM1 (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, approximately half of the C7orf50-depleted cells showed FBL and UBTF localized to the edge of the nucleoli (fig. S2F). Nevertheless, depletion of C7orf50 did not affect the number of nucleoli per cell (Fig. 2A), which was confirmed by the immunoblotting, as the expression of NPM1, FBL, and UBTF remained unchanged in C7orf50-depleted HeLa and MCF-7 cells compared to the control (fig. S2G). In accord with these observations, the expression of a ribosome-free r-protein ribosomal protein S6 (RPS6), another hallmark of NS (26), was increased in C7orf50-depleted HeLa and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 2B). Therefore, C7orf50 depletion induces an obvious NS, which leads to impairments in nucleolar morphology and homeostasis.

To explore the possible role of C7orf50 in ribosome biogenesis, we first examined the influence of C7orf50 depletion on rRNA transcription. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) results showed that depletion of C7orf50 reduced the level of 47S pre-rRNA transcript, which could be reversed by the expression of C7orf50 (Fig. 2C). Using Click-iT RNA imaging assay, we found that C7orf50-depleted cells exhibited decreased nascent rRNA transcription and reduced number of nucleoli with 5-ethynyl uridine (5-EU) (Fig. 2D). To determine whether the observed nucleolar abnormalities induce defective rRNA processing, we performed Northern blot analysis. Our results showed that C7orf50 knockdown significantly reduced the levels of 47S pre-rRNA and multiple pre-rRNA intermediates, including the 30S, 21S, and 32S pre-rRNA (fig. S2H). Specifically, C7orf50 deficiency impaired the cleavage of 47S pre-rRNA into 30S and 32S pre-rRNA, while causing a notable accumulation of the 18S-E pre-rRNA, suggesting a distinct blockage at the 18S rRNA maturation stage (fig. S2H). Together, these transcription and processing defects collectively contributed to impaired ribosome assembly (fig. S2I).

Notably, based on the Pfam database (27), we found that C7orf50 harbors the WKF (DUF2373) domain capable of binding RNA and two critical RNA binding residues, Lys120 (K120) and Arg156 (R156), precisely locate within the WKF domain (Fig. 2E) (28). Given its nucleolus localization and role in ribosome biogenesis, we hypothesized that C7orf50 may interact with rRNA. An RNA glutathione S-transferase (GST)–pull-down assay confirmed its specific binding to rRNAs (Fig. 2, F and G, and fig. S2J). In contrast, the Lys120/Arg156→ Gly (K120/R156G) mutant, which lost RNA binding capability, failed to reverse the NS phenotype (Fig. 2H), demonstrating that the rRNA binding ability of C7orf50 is essential for nucleolar homeostasis and ribosome biogenesis. In addition, NS induced by C7orf50 deficiency occurred without altering p53 level (fig. S2K), suggesting the activation of a p53-independent pathway in C7orf50-knockdown cells.

Furthermore, the effect of C7orf50 depletion on the rate of protein synthesis was evaluated using surface sensing of translation (SUnSET) assay (29, 30), which detects the amount of puromycin incorporation into nascent proteins (Fig. 2I). C7orf50 knockdown led to a decreased nascent proteins synthesis in multiple cell types (fig. S2L). Expression of C7orf50, but not the K120/R156G mutant, completely rescued the defects in protein synthesis caused by C7orf50 loss (Fig. 2J), supporting an rRNA binding–dependent function of C7orf50 in ribosome biogenesis.

Collectively, these data show that C7orf50, as an rRNA binding protein, plays an indispensable role in maintaining nucleolar homeostasis. Depletion of C7orf50 causes NS and impairments of ribosome biogenesis.

C7orf50 depletion induces NOP53 destability and promotes autophagy

Recent studies have shown that the NS response can activate autophagy (31, 32). Because starvation treatment significantly reduced nucleolar C7orf50, whose deficiency leads to NS, we hypothesized that C7orf50 might also play a role in the regulation of autophagy. To determine it, we first assessed whether C7orf50 depletion induces autophagy. Under short-term Earle’s balanced salt solution (EBSS) treatment, cells did not undergo apoptosis (fig. S3A) but effectively triggered autophagy (fig. S3B) and decreased nascent protein synthesis (fig. S3C). Immunoblotting results showed that C7orf50 depletion decreased the p62 level but enhanced LC3-II generation in HeLa and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 3A), with the former being further enhanced by EBSS-induced starvation (Fig. 3B) and the latter being boosted by both EBSS-induced starvation and chloroquine (CQ) treatment (Fig. 3C). Similar trends were also observed in other types of cells (fig. S3D). Moreover, these alterations in the p62 and LC3 abundance caused by C7orf50 depletion could be rescued by complementing with C7orf50 (Fig. 3D). These results indicate that C7orf50 depletion promotes autophagy, which is a widespread phenomenon.

We also assessed the effect of C7orf50 depletion on the autophagosome formation by immunofluorescence analysis of green fluorescent protein (GFP)–LC3B puncta. We found that C7orf50 knockdown substantially increased GFP-LC3B puncta formation, which was further enhanced by EBSS (Fig. 3E) or Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS) (fig. S3E) starvation treatment. Moreover, this increase in GFP-LC3-labeled autophagosomes could be rescued by ectopic expression of C7orf50 (Fig. 3F). In addition, by using the mCherry-GFP-LC3B tandem reporter (33–35), which enables observations of the differences between autophagosomes (GFP-positive/mCherry-positive, yellow puncta) and autolysosomes (GFP-negative/mCherry-positive, red puncta), we examined the effect of C7orf50 knockdown on the autophagic flux. We found that C7orf50 depletion enhanced the formation of autophagosomes and autolysosomes under both normal conditions and HBSS starvation conditions (Fig. 3, G and H).

To explore the mechanism by which C7orf50 regulates autophagy, we investigated its potential interactors. Our IP-MS analysis identified NOP53, a nucleolar protein known to regulate autophagy (36), as a binding partner of C7orf50 (fig. S3, F to H). We verified that C7orf50 interacts with NOP53, and this interaction was obviously reduced upon EBSS treatment (Fig. 3I). C7orf50 depletion decreased the NOP53 level but enhanced ULK1 (unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1), ATG7 (autophagy related 7), and ATG12 expression in HeLa and MCF-7 cells (Fig. 3J and fig. S3, I and J), consistent with the reported function of NOP53 in down-regulating these autophagy genes. Next, we determined how C7orf50 depletion down-regulates NOP53 expression. Our results showed that C7orf50 knockdown had no effect on the NOP53 mRNA levels (fig. S3K) but significantly accelerated NOP53 protein degradation (Fig. 3K and fig. S3L). MG132, but not CQ, blocked this degradation (Fig. 3L), indicating a proteasome-dependent degradation of NOP53 when C7orf50 is depleted. Immunoblotting results showed that the up-regulated autophagy caused by C7orf50 depletion could be rescued by complementing with NOP53 (Fig. 3M) but not vice versa (Fig. 3N), suggesting that NOP53 acts downstream of C7orf50 in the regulation of autophagy. Together, these data demonstrate that C7orf50 depletion augments autophagy by destabilizing NOP53, which is subsequently degraded via the proteasome pathway.

Nutrient deprivation results in the release of C7orf50 from nucleoli through ε-lysine acetylation

Our findings that C7orf50 depletion suppresses ribosome biogenesis and promotes autophagy suggest that C7orf50 may play a critical role in the cross-talk between anabolism and catabolism, which prompted us to investigate the underlying mechanism of C7orf50 in cellular energy stress response. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that the nucleolar localization of C7orf50, either endogenous (Fig. 4A, HeLa cells) or exogenously expressed (Fig. 4B, HeLa cells, and fig. S4A, MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells), was significantly reduced upon EBSS treatment (a nutrient deprivation condition). Similar reductions were observed under other conditions of nutrient deprivation, such as amino acid or serum deprivation (fig. S4B). After EBSS withdrawal and replenished with fresh complete Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), the translocation of C7orf50 was reversed (Fig. 4B, bottom row). Live-cell imaging further revealed that C7orf50 translocated from the nucleolus to the nucleoplasm upon nutrient deprivation (fig. S4C and movies S1 to S4) and underwent reversible relocation back to the nucleolus following nutrient replenishment (fig. S4D and movie S5). Furthermore, nutrient deprivation leads to substantial decrease of both endogenous (Fig. 4C) and exogenous (Fig. 4D) C7orf50 from the nucleolus, consistent with the MS data (Fig. 1A). These data demonstrated that C7orf50 has been shuttling back and forth between the nucleolar and the nucleoplasmic pools in response to nutrient availability.

Considering that EBSS treatment did not affect the expression of C7orf50 protein (Fig. 4, C and D), and posttranslational modifications (PTMs) represent a fundamental regulatory mechanism governing protein functions (37–40), we speculated that this nutrient deprivation–induced C7orf50 translocation might be related with its specific PTM(s). We then performed denatured IP assay and examined whether EBSS treatments affect C7orf50 phosphorylation, lactylation, or acetylation. Among the three PTMs, only the acetylation level of C7orf50 showed an obvious change after EBSS treatment (Fig. 4E and fig. S5, A and B).

Then, we examined which lysine residues of C7orf50 are critical for its acetylation and translocation upon nutrient deprivation. We noticed that the two C7orf50 nucleolar localization sequences, namely, NLS and NoLS, especially NoLS, exhibit high sequence homology in multiple species (fig. S5C). Combined with the predicted acetylation sites (fig. S5D) by GPS-PAIL, we narrowed down the potential acetylation sites to two groups, i.e., six Lys residues (K3, K5, K7, K14, K17, and K20) in the NLS and five (K69, K71, K72, K75, and K76) in the NoLS. These Lys residues were then mutated to glutamine (Q) to mimic acetylation or arginine (R) to prevent acetylation. We found that mutation of all six Lys residues of NLS to Q (NLS-6KQ) (Fig. 4F, top row) or R (NLS-6KR) (fig. S5E) caused C7orf50 to be localized predominantly in the cytoplasm in the presence or absence of EBSS treatment and NLS-6KR barely prevented the EBSS-induced translocation (fig. S5E), indicating that the six Lys residues in NLS region are unlikely responsible for C7orf50 translocation upon nutrient deprivation.

In contrast, mutation of the Lys residues in NoLS to Gln, including NoLS-5KQ, NoLS-4KQ (K69Q/K71Q/K72Q/K76Q) and NoLS-3KQ (K71Q/K72Q/K76Q), induced C7orf50 nucleoplasmic translocation, analogous to the effect caused by EBSS treatment (Fig. 4F). To further narrow down the possible acetylated sites responsible for the process, we also constructed multiple double (K71Q/76Q, K71Q/72Q, and K72Q/76Q) (fig. S5F) or single (K71Q, K72Q, and K76Q) (fig. S5G) C7orf50 mutants and found that none of them affected its nucleolar localization. These results show that acetylation of the minimally three Lys residues in NoLS (K71, K72, and K76) is necessary for releasing C7orf50 from the nucleolus to the nucleoplasm. Notably, NoLS-3KQ barely localized at the nucleolus, even under nutritionally adequate condition (Fig. 4, F and G), indicating that simultaneous acetylations of these three Lys residues are enough to induce C7orf50 nucleoplasmic translocation. Consistently, the NoLS-5KR and NoLS-3KR (K71R/K72R/K76R) mutants that disable the required acetylation blocked the EBSS-induced translocation (Fig. 4, H and I, and fig. S5H). Moreover, we showed that the NoLS-3KR mutation almost completely abolished the EBSS-induced up-regulation of C7orf50 acetylation (Fig. 4J), supporting that the three Lys residues of NoLS (K71, K72, and K76) are the main acetylation sites responsible for the translocation of C7orf50 from the nucleolus to the nucleoplasm upon nutrient deprivation.

NAA10 acetylates the NoLS in C7orf50 under energy deprivation

Given that acetylation is critical for the nutrient deprivation–induced translocation of C7orf50 from nucleolus to nucleoplasm, we then want to screen the potential acetyltransferases that show enhanced association with C7orf50 after nutrient deprivation through IP-MS analysis (fig. S6A). The candidates and two other cell metabolism–related acetyltransferases, TIP60 (60 KDa Tat-Interactive Protein)and NAA40, were evaluated by Co-IP and/or immunofluorescence analysis (fig. S6, B to D). The results showed that the interaction between C7orf50 and NAA10 (Fig. 5A) or KAT7 (Lysine Acetyltransferase 7) (fig. S6C) was enhanced upon nutrient deprivation. However, knockdown of NAA10, but not KAT7, prevented the EBSS-induced translocation of C7orf50 from the nucleolus to the nucleoplasm (fig. S6, D and E, and Fig. 5, B and C), suggesting that NAA10 is the acetyltransferase that modifies C7orf50 upon nutrient deprivation. Supportively, purified His-NAA10 could directly bind to GST-C7orf50 (Fig. 5D). We then focused on NAA10 for subsequent studies.

Through denatured IP assay, we found that knockdown of NAA10 in HeLa cells blocked the EBSS-induced notable increase in C7orf50 acetylation (Fig. 5E), which could be retrieved by the exogenously expressed RNA interference–resistant wild-type (WT)–NAA10 but not the catalytically inactive NAA10-R82/G85A mutant (Fig. 5F), demonstrating an essential role of NAA10 for C7orf50 acetylation. Considering that the interaction between NAA10 and its substrates is inhibited by mTOR through phosphorylation of NAA10 at S228 (41), we constructed the NAA10 S228A and S228D mutants to test whether the mTOR-NAA10 regulates C7orf50 acetylation. Our Co-IP data showed that the phosphomimetic mutation (S228D) abolished the enhanced association between NAA10 and C7orf50 after EBSS treatment, while the phosphonull mutation (S228A) promoted the interaction between NAA10 and C7orf50 even under nutrient-sufficient condition (Fig. 5G). To determine whether mTOR inhibition affects the nucleolar release of C7orf50, we treated HeLa cells with the mTOR inhibitors Torin1 or rapamycin. Both inhibitors effectively promoted the release of C7orf50 from the nucleolus to the nucleoplasm (fig. S6, F to H) without causing cytotoxicity (fig. S6I). Moreover, our immunoblotting and immunofluorescence results showed that, compared with empty vector, overexpression of either WT-NAA10 or S228A mutant led to an obvious C7orf50 nucleoplasmic translocation (Fig. 5, H and I, and fig. S6J). Correspondingly, complementing NAA10-depleted cells with NAA10-S228A, but not NAA10 WT or S228D, could inhibit 47S rRNA synthesis (fig. S6K).

Given that the three Lys residues of NoLS (K71, K72, and K76) are the main acetylation sites responsible for the translocation of C7orf50 from the nucleolus to the nucleoplasm, we then tested whether NAA10 can acetylate C7orf50 at these sites. Our in vivo (Fig. 5J) and in vitro (Fig. 5K) experiments demonstrated that the NoLS-3KR mutation blocked the NAA10-induced up-regulation of C7orf50 acetylation, confirming that NAA10 acetylates C7orf50 at K71, K72, and K76 sites under nutrient deprivation. Moreover, expression of NAA10-S228A could no longer impair the nucleolar localization of NoLS-3KR mutant (Fig. 5L). Together, these results demonstrate that upon nutrient deprivation, the mTOR inhibition promotes the association of C7orf50 with NAA10, and thereafter the acetylation of C7orf50 at the NoLS region, leading to the translocation of C7orf50 from the nucleolus to the nucleoplasm.

NAA10-mediated C7orf50 acetylation impairs nucleolar homeostasis while promoting autophagy

To determine whether NAA10-mediated C7orf50 acetylation is involved in NS response, we expressed C7orf50 WT or NoLS-3KQ mutant back to C7orf50-knockdown cells. Unlike WT C7orf50, NoLS-3KQ mutant, an acetylation-mimetic and non-nucleolus–localized C7orf50, failed to revert the round-shaped nucleoli (Fig. 6A), the low 47S pre-rRNA levels (Fig. 6B), the impaired global protein synthesis (Fig. 6C), and the up-regulated autophagy (Fig. 6, D and E) seen in depleted cells. These results further support that mTOR-NAA10-C7orf50 axis senses nutritional status to regulate ribosome biogenesis and autophagy.

We also determined the epistatic relationship between NAA10 and C7orf50 in nascent protein synthesis. Under adequate nutrition conditions, nascent protein synthesis was impaired by depletion of C7orf50 but not NAA10. Simultaneous depletion of C7orf50 and NAA10 had a similar effect as C7orf50 depletion alone. Moreover, complementing with WT C7orf50 or its acetylation-deficient NoLS-3KR but not the acetylation-mimetic NoLS-3KQ mutant could rescue the defective nascent protein synthesis. Under EBSS conditions, the rate of nascent protein synthesis was remarkably decreased, which could be increased by NAA10 depletion or further decreased by C7orf50 depletion. Notably, concurrent depletion of C7orf50 and NAA10 had a similar effect as C7orf50 depletion alone. Moreover, reconstitution with either WT C7orf50 or NoLS-3KR, but not NoLS-3KQ mutant, restored the defective nascent protein synthesis in C7orf50-NAA10–depleted cells (Fig. 6F). These genetic interactions indicate that C7orf50 and NAA10 function in the same pathway to regulate protein synthesis and C7orf50 works downstream of NAA10. Together, these data support that NAA10-mediated C7orf50 acetylation at K71, K72, and K76 sites disrupts its nucleolar localization, thus impairing nascent protein synthesis and promoting autophagy.

C7orf50 promotes tumor cell growth and tumorigenesis

To maintain rapid growth and unrestrained proliferation, cancer cells require enhanced ribosome biogenesis (17, 42, 43). Given the critical role of C7orf50 in promoting ribosome biogenesis, we next investigated its role in tumorigenesis. Pan-cancer gene expression analysis using UALCAN showed that C7orf50 is highly expressed in various tumors compared to corresponding normal tissues (Fig. 7A). We noted that, elevated C7orf50 is correlated with poor recurrence-free survival (RFS) rate and poor overall survival (OS) rate for patients with BRCA (Fig. 7B). In addition, compared to the nontumorigenic human mammary epithelial MCF-10A cell line, a panel of BRCA cells manifested a much higher expression of C7orf50, with the majority of C7orf50 located in the nucleolus (Fig. 7C and fig. S7A), correlating with the highly anabolic state of cancer cells (44).

Since C7orf50 depletion triggers NS and restrains ribosome biogenesis, we then determined whether it is required for tumor growth. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) (Fig. 7D) and colony formation assay (Fig. 7E) showed that the cell growth in MDA-MB-231 cells was inhibited by C7orf50 knockdown, which could be rescued by reintroduction of WT C7orf50 but not by that of the acetylation-mimetic non-nucleolar localized mutant NoLS-3KQ. Similar results were observed in HeLa, HCC1937, and MCF-7 cells (fig. S7, B to D). Notably, although depletion of C7orf50 also impaired the growth of MCF-10A cells (fig. S7B), the inhibition extent was less compared to those in tumor cell lines (fig. S7E). To explore how C7orf50 affects cell growth, we then examined its effects on cell cycle distribution and apoptosis. C7orf50 knockdown resulted in G1 cell cycle arrest (Fig. 7F and fig. S7F) and a higher rate of apoptosis (Fig. 7G and fig. S7G) in MDA-MB-231 cells, which could be rescued by WT but not by NoLS-3KQ C7orf50. These data demonstrate that C7orf50 is required for efficient cell growth, and its acetylation on NoLS impairs cell proliferation and enhances apoptosis.

To further support that C7orf50 promotes tumor growth, we established MDA-MB-231 orthotopic xenografts in female BALB/c nude mice (Fig. 7H). We found that C7orf50 depletion prominently reduced the size and weight of BRCA xenografts (Fig. 7I and fig. S7H), with no obvious influence on the body weight of mice (fig. S7I). Reconstitution of WT C7orf50, but not its NoLS-3KQ mutant, markedly rescued the impaired xenograft tumor growth (Fig. 7J and fig. S7, J and K), low 47S pre-rRNA levels (Fig. 7K), defective nascent protein synthesis (Fig. 7L), and aberrant Ki67/RPS6 expression (Fig. 7M) in C7orf50-depleted tumor tissues. In contrast, the expression levels of p53 remained unchanged regardless of C7orf50 expression in vivo (Fig. 7L). These in vivo results consistently recapitulate our in vitro observations and demonstrate that C7orf50 exerts its oncogenic function during tumor development primarily by promoting ribosome biogenesis.

DISCUSSION

Beyond the canonical functions in maintaining ribosomal homeostasis, nucleolus has been recognized as a stress sensor and a central hub for stress response (16, 45). However, how the nucleolus coordinates ribosome biogenesis and autophagy in response to changing nutrient availability remains to be defined. By analyzing the proteomes of nucleoli with or without nutrient deprivation, we have identified C7orf50, an uncharacterized protein, to be a nucleolar molecular link coordinating ribosome biogenesis and autophagy based on nutrient status. Under adequate nutrition, C7orf50, combined with NOP53, resides in GC region of the nucleolus and is essential for the maintenance of ribosome biogenesis (fig. S8, top). Upon nutrient deprivation, C7orf50 gets acetylated at lysines 71, 72, and 76 by acetyltransferase NAA10 and released from nucleolus to nucleoplasm, which further inhibits nucleolar function and ribosome biogenesis, but boosts autophagy by destabilizing NOP53 (fig. S8, bottom). Notably, NAA10 is a substrate of mTOR, thus conferring mTOR-NAA10-C7orf50 axis a nutritional status–responsive mechanism occurring at the nucleolus. Consistent with this notion, C7orf50 undergoes reversible nucleolus-nucleoplasm translocation in response to nutrient deprivation and nutrient supply, acting as a coordinator between ribosome biogenesis (anabolism) and autophagy (catabolism) (fig. S8). Therefore, we named it “nutrient-responding nucleolar factor.”

As the central controller of cell growth (46–48), mTOR is activated under favorable conditions to promote cell growth by stimulating anabolic processes, such as ribosome biogenesis and protein translation. When nutrients are depleted, mTOR is inhibited to suppress cell growth, whereas catabolic processes, such as autophagy, are induced to support cell survival. Here, we revealed that C7orf50 is a nucleolar factor of mTOR downstream signaling. NAA10, which can interact and acetylate the Lys residues in the NoLS region of C7orf50 (Fig. 5), has been previously reported to be phosphorylated by mTOR (41). We found that the mTOR-phosphorylated form of NAA10 at S228 did not interact with C7orf50 (Fig. 5G), thus leaving C7orf50 unacetylated when mTOR is active. Under nutrients deprivation, mTOR activity is inhibited and fails to phosphorylate NAA10, which, in turn, promotes NAA10-C7orf50 association, C7orf50 acetylation, and its release from nucleoli (Fig. 5, H to L). In support of this, inhibition of mTOR activity can also cause the translocation of C7orf50 even under adequate nutrition conditions (fig. S6, F to H). Thus, mTOR-NAA10-C7orf50 axis represents an unexpected nutritional status–responsive mechanism, which provides insights in the mTOR-regulated nucleolar function for metabolic fitness during cellular nutritional stress.

Ribosomal subunit assembly occurs in the GC region where many structural ribosomal proteins and nucleolar organizers are situated, such as NPM1 and NCL (12, 49). Our findings that C7orf50 is a GC-localized nucleolar protein, and C7orf50 interacts with NPM1, NCL, and other key candidates (Fig. 1, G to I, and fig. S2D) support an essential role of C7orf50 in the assembly and maturation of ribosomal subunits, which deserves further study. As the most energy-demanding organelle in cells, nutrient or energy deprivation can result in NS response (15, 18, 45, 50). C7orf50 deficiency also triggers NS response, as evidenced by typical NS nucleolar morphology, reduced rRNA production, impaired rRNA processing, and protein synthesis (Fig. 2). Considering that C7orf50 depletion–induced NS could be rescued by WT C7orf50 but not non-nucleolus–localized NoLS-3KQ mutant (Fig. 6), and C7orf50 undergoes reversible nucleolus-nucleoplasm translocation in response to nutrient deprivation and nutrient supply (Fig. 4B and fig. S4D), it is possible that C7orf50 nucleoplasmic translocation is a prerequisite step for nutrient deprivation–caused NS.

Both ribosome biogenesis and autophagy are evolutionarily conserved biological processes, representing the main feature of anabolism and catabolism, respectively. Upon nutrient deprivation, anabolic processes are inhibited, and catabolic processes are promoted to maintain cellular homeostasis for metabolic fitness (3). We found that the translocation of C7orf50 from nucleolus to nucleoplasm behaves similar to a switcher for the anabolism-catabolism transition in response to nutrient depletion, and vice versa. Recently, autophagy has been reported to be triggered by NS, and several nucleolar factors have been found to be involved in the transcriptional control of autophagy or to function upstream of mTOR to regulate autophagy (51, 52). Our findings that C7orf50 depletion increases not only autophagosomes but also autolysosomes formation indicate that C7orf50 is a nucleolar factor that regulates autophagy downstream of mTOR signaling. Further mechanistic studies revealed that C7orf50 depletion or its acetylation leads to the destabilization of NOP53, a nucleolar protein involved in autophagy regulation (36), which is subsequently degraded and promotes autophagy. However, the details that how C7orf50 stabilizes NOP53 still require future investigation. Human protein C7orf50 was known as an uncharacterized protein for a long time. Our intensive findings strongly support that C7orf50 is primarily a GC-localized nucleolar protein necessary for efficient ribosome biogenesis. Moreover, C7orf50 has been detected in several other nucleolar proteomic datasets (23, 53–56). Different from these results, a recent study reported that C7orf50 was a cytoplasm-localized protein, acting as a secretory hormone and a suppressor of cholesterol synthesis (57). The discrepancy on C7orf50 subcellular localization remains unknown. Given that nutrient deprivation can cause C7orf50 acetylation and its release from nucleoli, we speculate that a difference in cellular nutritional status may contribute to that discrepancy, which deserves further investigation.

Cancer cells usually sustain hyperactive ribosome biogenesis to fulfill their high demands for protein synthesis. An increased number and size of nucleoli, which reflect the increased rate of ribosome biogenesis, have been recognized as hallmark features of many tumor types (58). Compelling evidences have shown that impairing ribosome synthesis could be a potential strategy for cancer therapy (43, 59, 60). Here, we found that C7orf50 is highly expressed in various tumors. Its high expression is correlated with poor RFS and OS in patients with BRCA. Moreover, C7orf50 is required for efficient tumor cell growth, and its acetylation-mimetic form on NoLS impairs cell proliferation and enhances apoptosis. The oncogenic role of C7orf50 detected in our study suggests that inhibiting C7orf50 expression or its nucleolar localization are able to retard tumor growth.

Cell transfection and lentivirus production

Cell transfections with plasmids were performed using PEI (YEASEN), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Twenty-four hours before transfection, cells were plated at a density of 3 × 106 per 100-mm dish or 1 × 105 per well of a 6-well plate. Six to 8 hours after transfection, replace culture medium with fresh complete medium and continue to culture for 24 to 48 hours, until samples were harvested.

For lentivirus production, HEK293T cells were cotransfected with lentivirus vector and packaging plasmids using PEI. Forty-eight to 72 hours after transfection, lentivirus particles were concentrated and purified by ultrahigh-speed centrifugation (20,000g for 2 hours at 4°C). For transient experiments, 24 to 48 hours after lentiviral particles addition, cells were harvested for analysis. To generate stable cell lines, 48 hours after lentiviral particles addition, medium was replaced, and puromycin (1 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) or blasticidin S HCl (5 μg/ml; Gibco) was added for selection of pLKO.1-vector or Lenti-vector, respectively. Knockdown or overexpression of the specific genes was confirmed by WB.

Nucleocytoplasmic separation

HeLa cells or HeLa cells expressing C7orf50-FLAG were seeded into a six-well plate at a density of 1 × 106 per well 1 day before harvest. The next day, cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then collected by centrifuging at 200g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was removed, and the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were prepared using the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Nucleolar fractionation

Nucleoli from cells were purified as described (61). Whole-cell extract, nucleoplasmic and cytosolic fractions, and nucleolar fractions were subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting. The isolated nucleoli from HeLa cells with or without EBSS starvation treatment were used for quantitative proteomic analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

The isolated nucleoli from HeLa cells with or without EBSS starvation treatment, and the recovered proteins from anti-FLAG IP in C7orf50-knockdown HEK293T cells expressing C7orf50-FLAG were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) (12% gel) and stained with M5 HiPer Supermicroscale Protein Stain (Mei5bio, MF768). The gel bands were manually excised and processed including dehydration, reduction, alkylation, and trypsin digestion. Then, sample solution was transferred to a sample vial for LC-MS/MS analysis.

All nanoLC-MS/MS experiments were performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an Easy n-LC 1200 high-performance LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The MS analysis was performed with Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer with the FAIMS Pro interface (Thermo Fisher Scientific). FAIMS separations were performed with two compensation voltages (−45 and −65). With the data-dependent acquisition mode, the MS data were acquired at a high resolution of 60,000 [mass/charge ratio (m/z), 200] across the mass range of 350 to 1500 m/z.

The raw data from Orbitrap Exploris 480 were analyzed with proteome discovery version 2.4.1.15 using Sequest HT search engine for protein identification. The UniProt human protein database (updated on February 2022) was used for searching the data from samples. False discovery rate analysis was performed with percolator, and false discovery rate of <1% was set for protein identification. Protein label-free quantification was also performed on Proteome Discovery using the areas of identified peptides. Only unique and razor peptides of proteins were selected for protein relative quantification. The normalization mode was selected as total peptide amount to corrected experimental bias.

Total RNA from cells was extracted by TRIzol (Invitrogen, 15596026). Reverse transcription was performed using 2 μg of RNA with the GoStript Reverse Transcription System (Promega, A5001). qRT-PCR reactions for target genes were performed using Hief UNICON Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASON, 11184ES03). For qRT-PCR analysis, the 2−ΔΔCt method was used, and GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalizing the relative gene expression. The primers for qRT-PCR are listed in table S4.

Protein IP and denatured IP

Cells were lysed in NETN buffer [100 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EDTA, 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and 0.5% NP-40] supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04693132001). After incubation for 1 hour at 4°C, the total cell lysates were centrifuged at 4°C to remove cellular debris. Then, the supernatants were incubated with Anti-FLAG M2 beads (Sigma-Aldrich, M8823) at 4°C overnight using a rotating mixer to pull down FLAG-tagged proteins. The next day, the beads were washed three times with NETN buffer. Samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by WB, which were performed following the standard procedures.

For denatured IP, cells were lysed with 1× SDS buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 2% SDS, and 10% glycerol] in a water bath at 100°C for 10 min and centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatants were diluted with NETN buffer at a ratio of 1:10 and subsequently subjected to IP with anti-FLAG M2 beads as mentioned above.

Protein purification and pull-down assay

Transformed BL21 cells were cultured in LB (300 ml per sample) to the log phase (optical density at 600 nm = 0.6 to 0.8) and induced overnight with 0.4 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (GPCSCI, AC367) at 16°C. The harvested cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer [50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, ribonuclease A (RNase A; 10 μg/ml), and deoxyribonuclease I (5 μg/ml)] and sonicated on ice. The supernatants, after being centrifuged (12,000g for 30 min at 4°C), were incubated with the Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (Cytiva, 17075601) and Ni–nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) beads (LABLEAD, N30210) for GST, GST-C7orf50, and His-NAA10 samples, respectively. After the overnight incubation at 4°C with rotation, the beads were pelleted and washed three times with the corresponding buffer. GST-tagged proteins (GST and GST-C7orf50) were eluted by elution buffer [50 mM tris-HCl and 10 mM reduced glutathione (pH 8.0)] and incubated with prewashed Ni-NTA beads. After three sequential washes, the Ni-NTA beads were harvested and analyzed by WB and coomassie blue staining (CBS).

WB assay

Harvested samples were washed with PBS and lysed in SDS loading buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 100 mM DTT, and 0.02% bromophenol blue]. Lysates were heated at 100°C for 10 min in a boiling water bath and clarified by centrifugation at 12,000g for 10 min. Total proteins were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and electrophoretically transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). The PVDF membranes were blocked with 10% skimmed milk in tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature and then probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After three washes with TBST, PVDF membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Easybio, BE0101/BE0102) for 2 hours at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using the Immobilon Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore, WBKLS0500) and quantified by densitometry with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

For the detection of cell samples, cells cultured on glass-bottom dish (Cellvis, D35-14-1-N) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Aladdin, C104188) in PBS for 10 min at room temperature and then permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 solution in PBS for 15 min. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, V900933) in PBS for 30 min at 37°C, the cells were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After that, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 561– or Alexa Fluor 488–labeled secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) at 37°C for 1 hour. Cells were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (10 μg /ml; Enzo Life Science, ENZ-52401) for 5 min to visualize the nucleus. The samples were visualized under the Nikon A1R MP confocal microscope.

For the detection of xenograft tumor samples, the xenografts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, sequentially dehydrated by gradient ethanol, cleared with xylene, and paraffin embedded. Then, 5-μm sections on slides were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated successively in 100, 95, 85, and 75% ethanol and PBS. Antigen retrieval was performed in boiled 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9064) for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, sections were washed with PBS, blocked with 5% FBS and 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 hour, and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (Ki-67, 1:500; RPS6, 1:200). After washing with PBS, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488– and Alexa Fluor 561–labeled secondary antibodies. Subsequently, sections were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. The percentage of Ki-67–positive cells and relative fluorescence intensity of RPS6 were determined from three independent tumors. Images were processed and analyzed by ImageJ and GraphPad Prism.

Live-cell imaging

HeLa cells were plated in 35-mm glass-bottomed cell culture dish (Cellvis) and transfected with C7orf50-Halo vectors. Twenty-four hours later, cells were labeled with the Janelia Fluor 646 HaloTag Ligand (200 nM; Promega) prepared in FluoroBrite DMEM (Gibco) for 60 min in the cell culture incubator. Then, cells were washed three times with prewarmed PBS and cultured in prewarmed EBSS. Imaging and recording were performed using the Andor Dragonfly 505 live-cell system. About 3 to 4 hours, the EBSS could be replaced with complete medium, and the imaging and recording were continued for an additional 3 to 4 hours.

Nascent RNA imaging assay

Nascent RNA was labeled using 5-EU incorporation assays performed with Click-iT RNA Imaging Kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C10330) following the manufacturer’s optimized protocol. Fluorescent signals were acquired using a Nikon A1R MP confocal microscope equipped with a 60× oil-immersion objective. Image stacks were processed through deconvolution using NIS-Elements AR software and subsequently quantified using ImageJ.

Polysome profiling

Polysome profiling was carried out as described (62) with the following modifications. Briefly, 1.5 × 107 negative control or C7orf50-knockdown HeLa cells were seeded 24 hours before collection. Cells were incubated with CHX (100 μg/ml) in growth medium for 5 min and then washed twice with precooled PBS containing CHX (100 μg/ml). Cells were scraped in precooled PBS containing CHX (100 μg/ml) and collected by centrifugation. Then, cells were lysed in hypotonic buffer [5 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, 1.5 mM KCl, 1 × EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail, CHX (100 μg/ml), 1 mM DTT, RNase inhibitor (40 U/ml; Promega, N2515), and 1% Triton X-100]. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min at 4°C. Then, equal amounts of supernatants from each lysate were layered on linear sucrose gradient [10 to 50% in 20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 15 mM MgCl2, 150 mM NaCl, CHX (100 μg/ml), and RNase inhibitor (40 U/ml)], generated by a Gradient Maker (BioComp). Gradients were ultracentrifuged at 33,500 rpm for 3 hours in a SW-41Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter) at 4°C. Then, the gradients were fractionated on the Piston Gradient Fractionator (BioComp) and analyzed by 254-nm ultraviolet (UV) light with an Econo UV Monitor (Bio-Rad) to evaluate polysome profiles.

In vitro RNA and GST-protein pull-down assay

Purified GST, GST-C7orf50, or GST-C7orf50 (K120/R156G) proteins were incubated with total RNA and Glutathione Sepharose 4B (GE Healthcare) for in vitro RNA and GST-protein pull-down assay. Subsequent procedures were performed as described in a previous study (63). The primers for qRT-PCR analysis are listed in table S4.

Northern blotting assay

Total RNA from an equal number of negative control or C7orf50-knockdown HeLa cells were extracted with TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted RNA was subjected to Northern blotting to examine the intermediates of pre-rRNAs. Northern blotting was performed as described (64) with the following modifications. Briefly, total RNA was separated on 1.2% denatured agarose gel in Mops buffer and then transferred to positively charged Immobilon-Ny + Membrane (Millipore, INYC00010) in 10× saline-sodium citrate (SSC) buffer for about 18 hours. The membranes were cross-linked under 254-nm UV with an energy of 120 mJ/cm2 after air dried at room temperature. After 2 hours of prehybridization, the membranes were hybridized with different biotin-labeled probes at 42°C overnight. After blocking and washing, the biotin signals were detected with an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0018FS) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The biotin-labeled probes are listed in table S4.

SUnSET assay

For labeling of nascent proteins, HeLa and MCF-7 cells cultured in six-well plates were incubated with complete DMEM containing puromycin (10 μg/ml) for 15 min under standard culture conditions (37°C in 5% CO2). After washing with PBS, cell lysates were harvested for immunoblotting analysis. Puromycin-incorporated nascent proteins were detected with the antipuromycin antibody (Millipore, MABE343).

For in vivo SUnSET assay, 4 weeks after MDA-MB-231 cells inoculation, BALB/c nude mice received an intraperitoneal injection of puromycin (50 mg/kg in PBS) under anesthesia. The tumor tissues were harvested exactly 30 min postinjection for immunoblotting analysis of puromycin incorporation.

CHX chase assay

The negative control and C7orf50-knockdown (#1 and #2) HeLa cells were treated with CHX (100 μg/ml) and harvested at the indicated time points. The lysates were prepared, and immunoblotting was performed.

Autophagy assay

C7orf50-knockdown and control HeLa cell lines grown on glass-bottom dish (Cellvis, D35-14-1-N) were transfected with GFP-LC3B or mCherry-GFP-LC3B vectors. Forty-eight hours later, the medium was changed to complete medium, EBSS, or HBSS medium for 2 hours. The fluorescence of GFP-LC3B or mCherry-GFP-LC3B puncta was observed under the Nikon A1R MP confocal microscope. The number of puncta was counted from independent visual fields using ImageJ, and statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism.

In vitro acetylation assay

Purified GST-C7orf50, GST-C7orf50 (NoLS-3KR), His-NAA10, or His-NAA10 (R82/G85A) were incubated in an acetylation reaction buffer [50 mM tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 10% glycerol, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 10 μM trichostatin A, 10 mM nicotinamide, and 100 μM acetyl–coenzyme A] at 30°C for 1 hour. The reaction was terminated by adding SDS-PAGE loading buffer, and the reaction products were analyzed by immunoblotting.

Survival analysis

Clinical data of C7orf50 were analyzed in Kaplan-Meier plotter BRCA gene-chip project (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) containing a total of 2032 BRCA patient samples for RFS (split patients by upper quartile) and a total of 943 BRCA patient samples for OS (split patients by best cutoff, follow-up threshold 120 months).

CCK8 assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2000 to 3000 cells per well and allowed to adhere for about 12 hours. Cells were further incubated with CCK8 reagent (Vazyme, A311) at 10% concentration in complete medium for about 2 hours under standard culture conditions (37°C in 5% CO2). Following the manufacturer’s instruction described, cell viability was measured at 0 to 5 days, and the cell growth curves were drawn and analyzed with GraphPad Prism.

Colony formation assay

Colony formation assay was performed as described previously (65). Briefly, HeLa, MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, or HCC1937 cells were seeded into six-well plates (200 to 300 cells per well) and incubated in complete culture medium under standard culture conditions (37°C in 5% CO2) for about 10 days. Then colonies were fixed, stained, and counted. Data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism.

Xenograft experiment

A total of 2.5 × 106 MDA-MB-231 cells were mixed with Matrigel (Corning) and injected into the mammary fat pads of 5-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (n = 6 per group). During the process, the size of xenografts was measured every 2 days using a vernier caliper, and the body weight was also recorded. Tumor volumes were calculated using the equation (length × width2)/2 (in millimeters). Tumor growth was normalized to volumes on day 7 (after injection). Mice were euthanized on the indicated dates. Tumors were removed, weighed, and photographed.

Cell cycle analysis

For cell cycle analysis, stably transfected MDA-MB-231 cell lines were harvested and fixed in 70% ethanol at −20°C overnight. Then, the cells were washed once with PBS and incubated in PI (LABLEAD, P4170) buffer [PI (50 μg/ml), RNase A (200 μg/ml), and 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS) in the dark at room temperature for 30 min before flow cytometry analysis (BD LSRFortessa). Data analysis was performed with FlowJo (v10.6.2).

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis assay was performed using an annexin V–APC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Vazyme, A214) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, stably transfected MDA-MB-231 cell lines or HeLa cells, treated with EBSS or Torin1 (400 nM) for indicated time, were harvested and incubated with annexin V–APC and PI in the dark at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa) to determine the percentages of annexin-staining positive cells. Data were processed and analyzed with FlowJo (v10.6.2).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantitative data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0 and Excel. For multiple comparisons, statistics were evaluated by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey test, and all other statistics were analyzed by two-sided Student’s t test, unless otherwise indicated in figure legend. Quantitative data were presented as means ± SD or SEM of at least three independent experiments. Sample number (n) indicates the number of independent biological samples in each experiment. Sample numbers and experimental repeats are indicated in figure legends. Differences with *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001 were considered statistically significant.
