Tracking progress towards Sustainable Development Goal 3.2 in Kenya using time series models
Authors: Welcome Jabulani Dlamini, Sileshi Fanta Melesse, Henry Godwell Mwambi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2, which aims to reduce under-five mortality rate (UFMR) below 25 deaths per 1000 live births by 2030, is still a crucial target for improved child survival in sub-Saharan Africa because UFMRs are still high and progress has stalled in recent years.ObjectiveThis study aimed to model the possibility of reaching the SDG 3.2 target by 2030 and evaluate trends in under-five mortality in Kenya.MethodThree models: autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), autoregressive fractionally integrated moving average (ARFIMA) and hybrid were fitted to annual national under-five mortality data from 1995 to 2022. Automated model selection showed ARIMA (0,2,1) as the best fitting model from information criteria, predictive accuracy and residual diagnostics. The model was tested with mean absolute error, root mean square error, mean absolute percentage error and tested against the 80/20 train–test split.ResultsKenya’s UFMR has been slightly declining over the course of the study, but the ARIMA projection indicates that the rate of fall is slowing. By 2030, the UFMR is expected to be 27.8 deaths per 1000 live births (95% prediction interval (PI) 25.2 to 30.3), over the SDG 3.2 goal level (signifying an increase in predicted uncertainty). The upper bound of humanity’s real 95% PI still far exceeds the aim, even as the lower bound has started to move closer. Kenya would require an accelerated annual decline in roughly 2.43 fatalities per 1000 starting in 2023 much higher than trends seen in the recent past to meet SDG 3.2.ConclusionKenya’s UFMR has significantly decreased; however, the SDG 3.2 target might not be met by 2030 without more initiatives. To accelerate progress, it will be essential to improve mother and child health services, increase community-level interventions, address social injustices and employ more focused county-specific strategies. Using additional high-quality data and improved modelling tools could enhance child mortality monitoring and prediction in the future.

Introduction

This lofty goal of ensuring long and healthy lives and promoting well-being for all people of all ages is embodied in Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3, specifically 3.2, which aims to reduce under-five mortality to less than 25 deaths per 1000 live births by 2030. Great progress has been made around the world, but the gains in Kenya are uneven and closely related to healthcare access and socioeconomic status. For example, Joseph et al employed geographical and scenario predictions to observe varying ‘post-devolution’ gains in under-five mortality in Kenyan counties, indicating issues with intervention equality.1 Similarly, Keats et al2 identified key factors influencing under-five mortality in Kenya between 1990 and 2015, including maternal education, immunisation coverage, antenatal care use and nutrition. These findings highlight the importance of conducting country-specific assessments to assess previous successes and prospects for achieving SDG 3.2. However, especially in low- and middle-income countries, we are still a long way from where we ought to be. Das et al3 highlight that strong health systems and policies at scale can bring about real improvements in health outcomes across South-East Asia and demonstrate the agnostic value of systematised approaches for achieving SDG 3. A study by Yovo4 emphasises ongoing challenges to achieve target 3.2, that is, reducing under-five mortality rate (UFMR) in resource-limited settings, mentioning such issues as lack of resources, lack of healthcare workers and lack of infrastructure. While some advancement has been made, numerous nations are not on track towards achieving the health-related SDG targets, with significant regional variations, according to research by Sachs et al,5 which offers an international assessment. In this regard, Moyer and Hedden6 state that while there has been progress towards SDG goals, a significant acceleration of progress and targeted interventions will be needed to be in line with the 2030 Agenda. These studies together highlight both the progress achieved and the chronic deficiencies that continue to thwart attainment of SDG 3, and especially SDG 3.2, to reduce child mortality. This ambition is especially pertinent in East Africa, where progress has been made in child mortality reduction in Kenya, but we are still not yet there. Policymakers require an understanding of mortality trends and future progress to tailor interventions.

A hybrid modelling approach is becoming more popular in recent studies in order to enhance the mortality forecast accuracy. Despite autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) being widely acknowledged as a prominent method for time series analysis, as noted by Shumway and Stoffer,7 researchers have identified its inadequacy in capturing non-linear structures. To overcome this limitation, Mwijalilege et al8 conducted an extension of this analysis, comparing forecasting abilities of ARIMA and autoregressive fractionally integrated moving average (ARFIMA) models to forecast under-five mortality in Tanzania and showing ARFIMA was superior for data sets showing long memory. Saleh et al9 applied neural networks (NNs) methods for modelling health spending and emphasised the usefulness of machine learning for complex and non-linear health data patterns. These findings support the increasing use of hybrid models, such as combining ARIMA-ARFIMA and ARIMA with NN (ARIMA-NN), with the complementarity between statistical and machine learning methods.10 Recent studies using these models have shown promise in improving prediction accuracy, particularly in public health prediction, because they line up with both linear and non-linear elements of mortality and health time series data. This study focuses solely on Kenya, with the goal of evaluating national UFMRs from 1995 to 2022 using ARIMA, ARFIMA and hybrid time series models. It also forecasts death rates to 2030 to assess the chances of meeting SDG 3.2, building on the empirical work of Joseph et al1 and Keats et al2 by using statistical forecasting methods using national data. This study is unique in that it expressly evaluates the performance in relation to SDG 3.2 and uses a hybrid ARIMA-ARFIMA forecasting model solely on Kenyan national-level mortality panel data. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first forecast based in Kenya that evaluates the SDGs using both short and long-memory modelling.

Methods and materials

Box-Jenkins methodology

Box-Jenkins modelling pathway and analytical framework. Figure 1 illustrates the study’s analytical framework and shows the finished Box-Jenkins modelling procedure using the under-five mortality panel data set. The methodical, iterative procedure that begins with data extraction and visualisation to investigate the general characteristics of a series is depicted in figure 1. After that, stationarity is checked both visually and using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test. Transformations like differencing are applied until processes stabilise if the series is not stationary. A model is found using Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) based on automatic parameter selection (auto.arima function in R) once stationary has been reached. Least squares or maximum likelihood methods are used to estimate the model parameters, and the Ljung-Box test is used for diagnostic testing of the residuals. Only models that pass these diagnostic tests advance to the forecasting stage, where predicted accuracy is evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE), root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) using an 80% train and 20% test split. Data source

This analysis made use of annual time series from 1995 to 2022 that were sourced from the World Bank’s World Development Indicators (data set).11 The 28 data points (1 per year) in the study data set represented Kenya’s national UFMR (per 1000 live births). To ascertain the extent of potential determinants, additional variables were gathered, including gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, health expenditure (as a percentage of GDP) and access to improved sanitation facilities. However, these variables were not used to make predictions using the univariate forecast framework, which mirrored the study’s methodology. Key packages including forecast, fracdiff and tseries were used for model fitting, diagnostics and residual testing in the analysis, which was carried out using R V.4.4.0.

Time series models

A time series is a sequence of data items recorded at successive, regular time periods. Trends, seasonality and cycles over time play a significant role in its analysis, a critical consideration for forecasting future values based on past data.12 Time series analysis provides insight into the underlying dynamics of a system, identifies deviations from expected behaviour (anomaly detection) and assesses the effects of interventions or events over time.12 13 Statistics is one of the commonly used fields of study for making predictions about the future and making better branches of decisions in economics and finance, environmental studies and health sciences.14 Two time series models with a hybrid model which are considered are: ARIMA, ARFIMA, ARIMA-ARFIMA models.

Stationarity

Stationarity in time series is a series in which the statistical properties mean, variance and autocovariance are constant over time and it is one of the basic assumptions in time series models, for instance, ARIMA.15 The ADF test is frequently employed by analysts to assess non-stationarity, with the null hypothesis indicating the presence of a unit root, thereby suggesting that the series is non-stationary. Conversely, the Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin test operates under the null hypothesis that assumes stationarity.15 16 The Phillips-Perron test is also another alternative that has the advantage of adjusting for autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity differently to the ADF. Results

The UFMR temporal trend for Kenya from 1995 to 2022 is depicted in figure 2. This pattern shows a notable and steady fall over this period. The UFMR was extremely high in the mid-1990s, at roughly 110 per 1000 live births. The largest drops occurred between the mid and late 1990s and about 2010. These declines could have been caused by notable advancements in child health measures, such as increased vaccination programmes, decreased malaria prevalence and overall improvements in living standards. After 2010, there is a noticeable slowdown in the pace of fall, and between 2015 and 2022, the slope flattens, hovering between 42 and 44 fatalities per 1000 live births. Although the long-term trajectory shows substantial national gains, the slowing trend in recent years indicates that Kenya is getting close to structural barriers that prevent further rapid declines, such as disparities in the health system, unequal access to service coverage and ongoing problems with maternal, neonatal and community care.

The automatic model selection using the auto.arima algorithm, which methodically examined a wide range of ARIMA, ARFIMA and hybrid type (ARIMA-ARFIMA) structures, was the only basis for the model identification. The model that generated the best statistical performance profit for a given reduction of overspill was finally selected as optimal by the algorithm, which compared candidate models based on likelihood-based optimisation and information criteria. With the lowest AIC and BIC values (42.77 and 45.29) and superior residual behaviour when compared with other cases examined, the ARIMA (0,2,1) model was determined by automated search to be the best model for the differenced series of mortality. This automated procedure made it easier to choose the final forecasting model in an objective, data-driven manner.

The whole data set and the 80/20 train–test split were used to test the model’s performance. Despite a high MAPE value of 58.72% due to the proportionate baseline mortality rates, the model yielded an MAE of 0.37 and an RMSE of 0.46 in the training set. With a test RMSE of 0.45 and a test MAE of 0.52, the model produced consistent testing results. Small denominators and the sensitivity to percentage-based metrics in low-mortality scenarios are indicated by this high test MAPE (121.85%). The model ARIMA (0,2,1) selected was suitable for prediction, as demonstrated by the model evaluation using the complete data set once more, which revealed an acceptable excellent match (a variance of 0.27, log-likelihood = –19.39). The model’s residuals seemed to be white noise, indicating that the ARIMA (0,2,1) model correctly represented the characteristics of the data. At standard levels of significance, the Ljung-Box Q statistic of 8.02 and p value of 0.627 indicate that there was no substantial autocorrelation remaining in the residuals. The diagnostic test, which uses a sample size of 27 observations, offers compelling proof that the model is well-specified and that, given the limitations of the available data, its predictions can be regarded as trustworthy. It is interesting to note, however, that the combined ARIMA model outperformed the hybrid and ARFIMA models in Kenya, whereas the opposite was observed when comparing the ARFIMA with ARIMA models in a similar study in Tanzania, as reported by Mwijalilege et al,8 where hybrid models exhibit additional complexity. Future research should investigate whether county-level data or the presence of structural breaks could benefit one type of forecasting technique, hybrid framework.

The historical record, which spans 1995–2022, demonstrates a long-term, consistent decrease in child mortality in Kenya. The ARIMA (0,2,1) anticipated trend for 2023 and beyond is represented by the green forecasting line. Additionally, the red horizontal line represents the SDG target (SDG 3.2) for mortality in children under 5 years old, which is to achieve a death rate of less than 25 per 1000 live births by 2030. It is predicted that the number of deaths per 1000 live births would continue to decline, although much more slowly, until 2030. As the forecast horizon lengthens, the appropriate 95% prediction interval (PI) (shown in shaded green) grows. This broadening shows that the longer the model is projected into the future, the greater the uncertainty. Importantly, it can be observed that the 2.5th percentile of this interval is somewhat below the SDG target, while the 97.5th percentile is considerably above it before the age of 30 and beyond it for almost all ages after that. This trend indicates that, even though it might be statistically possible for Kenya to achieve the SDG 3.2 target under the most optimistic conditions, all scenarios within its forecast range show a lower likelihood of doing so by 2030, assuming 63 central forecasts. Therefore, the projection interval reflects both the uncertainty in our model and the insufficient yearly rate of decline to reach the SDG meeting criteria with confidence. When considered collectively, these results suggest that Kenya would require more concentrated and intense efforts to move closer to SDG 3.2.

Discussion

To gauge progress towards SDG 3.2, this article examines past and projected trends in under-five mortality in Kenya. According to automated model selection, the best-fitting ARIMA (0,2,1) model predicted a sharp decline in under-five mortality from 1995 to 2022. However, it predicted that the decline would begin to level off around 2030 and reach an expected rate of 27·8 UFMR in 2030: 27.8 (95% PI 25.2 to 30.3) deaths per 1000 livebirths, which is slightly above the SDG target (25). The top limit of the 95% prediction range is significantly above the target, indicating growing uncertainty and implying that Kenya is on course to fail to meet SDG 3.2 without more aggressive actions, even while the lower bound of the interval barely falls below the target. This is due, as it was indicated previously, to the fact that this research was performed on the single temporal patterns and not in causal aspects of mortality decrease. Nonetheless, prior research has found that reductions in child mortality are driven by improvements in maternal health, vaccination rates and nutritional status.2 The ARIMA model outperformed ARFIMA and the hybrid ARIMA-ARFIMA structure in Kenya, indicating the dominance of short-term dynamics over long-memory effects, despite the hybrid model’s outstanding performance in Tanzania.8 National level modelling may disguise large discrepancies at county level, also in line with Joseph et al1 demonstrating heterogeneity in child survival across Kenya’s decentralised system.

These results are in line with broader evidence that time-series models may be used to accurately forecast future health trends, but these forecasts tend to become less definite the farther into the future they are made.13 14 Also, these results are consistent with the general narrative of health issues for Africa, which prompted Golding et al29 as highlighted in their mapping of under-five mortality across the continent. Kenya is making strides but not on track to meet the SDG target, according to baseline analysis of under-five and newborn mortality by Golding et al.29 According to Sachs et al5 and Moyer and Hedden,6 attaining SDG 3.2 in Kenya will necessitate long-term, multisectoral investments, with special focus on maternal and newborn care, rural health infrastructure and general socioeconomic development.30 The trends’ stagnation indicates that focused interventions like Baby-Friendly Community Initiative (BFCI) and reinforced community-based interventions like Community Health Volunteers (CHV) programmes are crucial.31 32

The forecast also highlights the fact that models that rely solely on historical data are unable to predict future shocks like pandemics, economic crises or disruptions brought on by climate change. Resilience to such new risks is essential to the advancement of global health, as noted by Fischer and Carow.32 Furthermore, the very limited number of annual observations (n=27) broadens PIs, raises forecast uncertainty and might make it more difficult for the model to identify minute structural changes in mortality trends. To increase predictive accuracy, future studies should incorporate richer data sets, such as panel data, county-level variables, higher-frequency time series and external socioeconomic indicators.30 33 Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are two machine-learning techniques that have significant potential to capture non-linearities and include extra factors that conventional time-series models could overlook.34 35

Conclusion and recommendations

Examining Kenya’s progress towards achieving SDG 3.2, which aims to reduce the UFMR to as low as 25 deaths per 1000 live births by 2030, was the main objective of this study. Under-five mortality steadily tended to fall between 1995 and 2022, according to the ARIMA (0,2,1) model chosen using automated selection. However, the anticipated trend also shows a significant slowing of this decline, with Kenya expected to reach about 27.8 UFMR in 2030: 27.8 (95% PI 25.2 to 30.3) fatalities per 1000 live births in 2030, just above the SDG target. This implies that Kenya is likely to miss the target by 2.8 deaths per 1000 live births. The top bound of the 95% PI is still significantly higher than that, indicating significant forecast uncertainty, even as the bottom boundary of the interval approaches the SDG objective.

Kenya would need to achieve a substantially steeper annual decline of about 2.43 deaths per 1000 live births starting in 2023 to meet the SDG 3.2 objective by 2030. This emphasises the need for more concentrated, equity-orientated and targeted measures to prevent the current plateau from becoming a dead end. Increased mother and child health services, improved access to high-quality treatment and programmes addressing significant social determinants of health are all necessary to achieve this faster decline. Focus interventions on populations that are more vulnerable, such as low-income households, rural residents and counties with consistently high mortality rates. Increasing physician qualification rates and improving maternity care quality are both excellent strategies for maintaining improvement, according to lessons learnt from past studies.

This study’s limited sample size of just 27 annual observations causes a wide range of forecasts. By including longer or higher-frequency time series data at the county level to more accurately reflect local variation, future research could increase prediction. By capturing the non-linear aspects of transmission dynamics and incorporating additional covariates like health spending, nutrition status, clean environment/sanitation and economic factors, advanced machine-learning techniques like LSTM networks and XGBoost could potentially increase forecasting accuracy for COVID-19 cases. The results indicate to policymakers that Kenya must act quickly to stop this decreasing trend. Focussing on neglected counties, bolstering community health systems and expanding CHV and BFCI programmes will all be crucial to accelerating the decline in mortality. The nation would be able to effectively prepare for final gap closure and advance towards achieving SDG 3.2 by 2030 with the help of strategic policy action and strong forecasting tools.
