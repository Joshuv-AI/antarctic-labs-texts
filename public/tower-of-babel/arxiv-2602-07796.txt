Thinking Makes LLM Agents Introverted: How Mandatory Thinking Can Backfire in User-Engaged Agents
Jiatong Li, Changdae Oh, Hyeong Kyu Choi, Jindong Wang, Sharon Li

Abstract
Eliciting reasoning has emerged as a powerful technique for improving the performance of large language models (LLMs) on complex tasks by inducing thinking. However, their effectiveness in realistic user-engaged agent scenarios remains unclear. In this paper, we conduct a comprehensive study on the effect of explicit thinking in user-engaged LLM agents. Our experiments span across seven models, three benchmarks, and two thinking instantiations, and we evaluate them through both a quantitative response taxonomy analysis and qualitative failure propagation case studies. Contrary to expectations, we find that mandatory thinking often backfires on agents in user-engaged settings, causing anomalous performance degradation across various LLMs. Our key finding reveals that thinking makes agents more “introverted” by shortening responses and reducing information disclosure to users, which weakens agent-user information exchange and leads to downstream task failures. Furthermore, we demonstrate that explicitly prompting for information disclosure reliably improves performance across diverse model families, suggesting that proactive transparency is a vital lever for agent optimization. Overall, our study suggests that information transparency awareness is a crucial yet underexplored perspective for the future design of reasoning agents in real-world scenarios. Our code is available at https://github.com/deeplearning-wisc/Thinking-Agent .

Introduction

Figure 1:
An overview of the research framework
.

Large language models (LLMs) have recently demonstrated remarkable gains on complex tasks by leveraging test-time reasoning
(
14
)
, often implemented through explicit thinking such as chain-of-thought prompting or dedicated reasoning modules. By explicitly encouraging models to think before acting, prior work has shown promising improvements on a wide range of closed-world benchmarks, including mathematical reasoning
(
30
)
and code generation
(
38
)
. As a result, “thinking before acting” has increasingly become a popular design in modern LLM-based agents
(
8
)
.

However, most real-world agents do not operate in closed, isolated manners. Instead, they are
user-engaged

(
31
;
36
)
: they interact with humans over multiple turns, operate under partial observability, and must actively communicate to elicit missing information, clarify intent, and guide users toward successful outcomes. In such settings, success depends not only on internal deliberation, but also on how agents communicate, disclose information, and guide users throughout the interaction. This raises a fundamental yet unexplored question:

Is mandatory thinking still beneficial for LLM agents in realistic, user-engaged scenarios?

In this paper, we present a systematic empirical study on the effect of thinking in user-engaged LLM agents. Across seven representative LLMs and established user-engaged benchmarks, we find a consistent and counterintuitive pattern: enabling thinking frequently degrades agent performance in multi-turn, user-facing environments. This effect holds across different model families, tasks, and two common instantiations of thinking, including Thinking-as-a-Function (TaaF), where reasoning is enforced as a mandatory pre-action function call in tool-using agents, and Thinking-as-a-Prefix (TaaP), where reasoning is elicited through ReAct-style prompt prefixes in text-based agents
(
37
)
. The observed degradation reflects a systematic interaction-level phenomenon rather than isolated failures or prompt artifacts.
Consequently, the use of thinking in user-engaged environments requires a rigorous understanding.

To understand
why
thinking can be detrimental in these settings, we move beyond aggregate metrics and carefully analyze how thinking changes agent behavior during interaction. Our study unveils that enabling thinking consistently reduces information disclosure (i.e., proactively presenting environment observation to the user) across nearly all models. Instead of proactively disclosing pivotal information, thinking agents become more “introverted”, offering narrower and more selective information. This reduction in outward communication weakens active information exchange and, in interactive environments where progress depends on clarification through dialogue, can significantly hinder task completion. Through qualitative case studies, we further show how such omissions propagate through interaction: by failing to surface critical constraints or alternatives at key decision points, thinking agents inadvertently steer the trajectory toward failure.

Our findings have direct implications for the design, deployment, and evaluation of LLM-based agents in user-engaged settings.
Importantly, our study suggests that agent design should explicitly prioritize transparency, proactive information disclosure, and clarification over purely internal deliberation. To validate the practical benefits of our analysis, we further develop information-disclosure prompting and demonstrate empirically how it can improve performance across diverse model families, indicating that the observed introverted behavior is a correctable design choice rather than an inherent limitation of reasoning-capable models. More broadly, our results highlight the importance of evaluating reasoning mechanisms in realistic, user-engaged settings, as improvements observed on closed-world benchmarks may not reliably translate to interactive agent performance. We summarize our contributions below.

1.

We conduct a
comprehensive empirical study
on the effect of mandatory thinking in user-engaged LLM agents across multiple models, benchmarks, and thinking instantiations. We observe an interesting phenomenon that thinking is not always beneficial, and can even be harmful, in interactive scenarios.

2.

We provide a
fine-grained behavioral analysis
revealing how thinking makes LLM agents “introverted”, by discovering that thinking reduces response length and information disclosure to users, helping explain the observed performance degradation.

3.

We propose an
effective yet simple add-on prompting strategy
that enhances information disclosure to boost agent performance, demonstrating the potential of information transparancy awareness in agent optimization.

Preliminaries

3.1
User-Engaged Agent Tasks

Figure 2:
Overall performance of agents with or without thinking on Retail and Airline.

We study user-engaged agents operating in partially observable environments
(
5
)
, where the agent must interact with users and tools to uncover intent and task-relevant information to achieve a complex final goal, such as travel booking or item retail. At each turn
t
, the agent policy
\pi_{\theta}(a_{t}|o_{t})
maps the current context (interaction history and tool outputs) to an action: either a natural language response or a structured tool call. Crucially, language responses act as environmental dynamics; they directly shape the user’s subsequent actions and the resulting trajectory. Task success thus depends on the agent’s ability to ask for clarification and disclose information to the user by aligning the interaction with the final goal.

3.2
Formulation of Agent’s Thinking

Agent thinking can be viewed as an internal, user-invisible generation process that precedes action selection. Specifically, thinking does not alter the environment; rather, it serves as an intervention on the action sampling distribution. Given an observation
o_{t}
, a thinking agent first generates internal tokens
\tau_{t}
, then samples an action
a_{t}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|o_{t},\tau_{t})
. We consider two common instantiations of this:

Thinking-as-a-Function (TaaF)

is designed for tool-calling agents, where thinking is implemented as a mandatory function call executed before any response or tool invocation. The prompt instruction for TaaF is shown in Appendix
A.1
.

Thinking-as-a-Prefix (TaaP)

is designed for textual agents
(
15
)
, where thinking is elicited via a prompt prefix (e.g., “Thought:…”) and precedes the action generation. This setting follows standard ReAct-style prompting
(
37
)
, shown in Appendix
A.1
.

5
Empirical Analysis of Thought Effect

In this section, we delve deep into understanding why thinking degrades the agent’s performance in user-engaged settings. Our analysis proceeds in three steps.
First, we quantify how thinking affects output (Sec.
5.1
). Second, we examine how reduced output manifests as diminished information disclosure using a fine-grained response taxonomy (Sec.
5.2
).
Finally, we present a qualitative case study illustrating how insufficient disclosure propagates into task failure (Sec.
5.3
).

5.1
Thinking Reduces Output Scaling

We begin by investigating the impact of thinking on output scaling by analyzing changes in response token length before and after enabling the thinking mechanism in agents.
We perform a trajectory-level analysis on the Retail and Airline benchmarks. Specifically, we consider two metrics: (1) the average response length per trajectory and (2) the frequency of long responses within a trajectory, with at least 150 tokens. Details are available at Appendix
A.4
.

As illustrated in Figure
3
, there is a pronounced decline in response length across most LLMs when thinking is enabled. This pattern is further confirmed by paired statistical tests reported in Table
3
, which demonstrate a significant decrease in the number of long responses for the majority of models.
For instance, DeepSeek-V3.1 produces an average of 1.24 long responses per trajectory without thinking, but drops sharply to 0.40 with thinking. These results suggest that thinking forces agents to generate more concise outputs. While brevity can be beneficial in some settings, in interactive scenarios, it may also reduce clarification and information exchange. To further investigate the implications of this reduced output, we conduct a semantic analysis of the responses.

5.2
Thinking Reduces Information Disclosure in Agent Responses

To examine how reduced output length affects interaction quality, we introduce an atomic response taxonomy that decomposes each agent response into semantically indivisible units, referred to as atomic statements. Each atomic statement is categorized into one of two functional types: (1)
information disclosure
, which communicates the current world state or available options to users, and (2)
user engagement request
, which prompts the user for input or action.
For example, as illustrated in Figure
5
, an airline agent’s response can be explicitly partitioned into six atomic statements: four instances of information disclosure and two user engagement requests. Maintaining sufficient information disclosure and engagement is indispensable for effective agent-user interaction. For the scalable analysis, we propose an autonomous taxonomy annotation pipeline that efficiently parses atomic responses and classifies them into categories (see Appendix
A.5
). We validate the reliability of our pipeline via human-in-the-loop annotation (see Appendix
A.6
). Using this taxonomy, we compare the frequency of information disclosure in the scenarios. We focus primarily on information disclosure as it provides the essential context and affordances required for meaningful user engagement, effectively serving as a proxy for the agent’s interactive capacity.

As shown in Figure
6
,
enabling thinking leads to a substantial and consistent reduction in information disclosure to users across nearly all models
, spanning both proprietary and open-source architectures. These results suggest that thinking renders agents more “introverted”: rather than proactively summarizing observations or enumerating options, thinking agents tend to provide narrower, more selective information. As a result, this diminishes active information exchange with the user. In user-engaged scenarios, where the user’s intent is partially observable and must be clarified through interaction, this reduction in disclosure can significantly hinder task progress.

5.3
Case Study: Insufficient Information Disclosure Misleads the User

To concretely illustrate how reduced information disclosure can lead to task failure, we conduct a qualitative case study across three scenarios. We first manually sample the cases where the thinking agent fails while the non-thinking agent succeeds. Subsequently, we perform a backtracking analysis of the failure and success propagation within the agent’s actions. Finally, we provide a trajectory comparison to demonstrate the specific impact of thinking on agent-user interaction.

We consider a retail task scenario, e.g., a user wanting to return a skateboard, with a detailed task description provided in Figure
4
.
The trajectories for the thinking and non-thinking agents are shown in Figure
7
, respectively (additional cases are presented in Appendix
A.7
).
A critical decision point in this scenario hinges on whether the agent informs the user that an item they bought cannot be canceled individually due to order constraints. This requires a sufficient level of information disclosure, as conceptualized in Section
5.2
.

For the agent with thinking (see the left of Figure
7
), we observe that the internal monologue overlooks the specific constraints regarding item cancellation. This localized action planning curtails the information disclosure provided in the subsequent response, causing the agent to omit the necessary warning. Consequently, this lack of transparency misleads the user into making an incorrect decision, resulting in task failure. In contrast, the agent without the explicit thinking proactively discloses the cancellation constraint. This active communication alerts the user to the fact that cancelling the hose alone is infeasible, which in turn empowers the user to make a correct decision based on sufficient information. This case study demonstrates that the thinking may inadvertently lead to insufficient information disclosure, thereby leading to downstream user errors.

6
Discussion and Practical Implications

Our findings have several important implications for the design, deployment, and evaluation of LLM-based agents in interactive settings.

Information transparency can drive agents’ goal achievement.

Our findings suggest that the next generation of user-engaged agents must move beyond raw reasoning power toward transparency-aware design. While current paradigms prioritize internal thinking, future agents must treat external interaction as a top priority of the task process. We demonstrate the potential of this direction by our preliminary experiments with Information Disclosure (
InfoDis
), an add-on prompt that simply
instructing agents to prioritize informative transparency
(see Appendix
A.8
for technical details). As shown in Table
4
, we observed immediate and consistent performance gains across various model families GPT-4o (OpenAI), Gemini-1.5-Pro (Google), and DeepSeek-V3.1 (DeepSeek), such as a +4.00% increase in the
\tau
-Airline for DeepSeek-V3.1. The fact that such a lightweight prompting intervention yields such significant improvements across diverse model families signals a major optimization frontier. It suggests that “introverted” behavior by reasoning is not an inherent limitation, but a design gap that can be bridged. We argue that instilling transparency awareness into LLMs, whether through architectural shifts, supervised fine-tuning, or reinforcement learning, represents a potential lever for better agentic system design. This path promises a new class of agents that not only solve tasks in isolation, but actively manage user uncertainty through strategic disclosure and clarification.

User-centric reasoning LLMs development.

Our empirical analysis demonstrates a counterintuitive trade-off in user-engaged scenarios: thinking can hinder goal achievements by rendering agents more “introverted”. Our results suggest that the common thinking pattern of LLMs should be reconsidered in user-engaged scenarios. In existing settings, thinking is designed for complex but fully observed, single-turn tasks. Such a thinking pattern can suppress information disclosure and reduce exploratory communication, which is harmful to more general user-engaged scenarios. Therefore, developers should pursue new data and training strategies to guide LLMs to acquire more user-centric thinking patterns for agentic utility.

Broader lessons for agent evaluation.

Finally, our results caution against transferring intuitions from closed-world reasoning benchmarks directly to interactive agent deployments. While explicit thinking can improve performance in static tasks, it may introduce unintended side effects in dynamic, user-facing systems. For practitioners, this underscores the importance of validating reasoning techniques under realistic interaction conditions before deploying them in production agents.

Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the impact of mandatory thinking in user-engaged LLM agents, challenging the prevailing assumption about the effectiveness of reasoning. Through a comprehensive evaluation across seven representative models and multiple benchmarks, we uncovered a consistent serendipity: enforcing thinking before actions can degrade performance in user-engaged settings. Our analysis shows that this degradation arises from a systematic behavioral shift, where thinking shortens responses and reduces information disclosure, making agents more “introverted” and less effective at guiding users through underspecified tasks. Qualitative case studies further illustrate how such omissions propagate into task failures. To translate these insights into practical utility, we presented a simple information-disclosure prompting method, and showed that it can reliably recover and improve performance across model families. Overall, our results highlight the importance of evaluating reasoning mechanisms in realistic interactive environments and motivate interaction-aware agent design that balances internal deliberation with proactive communication.

Limitations

While our study provides a systematic analysis of how explicit reasoning affects agent behavior in user-engaged scenarios, we acknowledge several limitations that offer avenues for future research. First, our evaluation is centered on three representative user-engaged benchmarks that primarily represent task-oriented dialogues. Future work should explore whether the thinking effect persists in more open-ended or creative collaboration scenarios where the user’s intent is less structured. Moreover, our behavioral analyses rely on automated response taxonomy and thought categorization pipelines supported by large language models, with human validation. While we observe strong agreement between model-based and human annotations, subtle categorization errors may remain, particularly for borderline cases involving implicit disclosure or nuanced user engagement.

Ethical Considerations

This study presents an empirical analysis of the “thinking effect” within user-engaged agents. The methodology and findings detailed herein are intended to advance the research and practical application of LLM agents, providing an empirical foundation and reference for future development in the field. Furthermore, we remain committed to transparency and reproducibility; all data resources and models utilized in this research are derived from publicly available sources and are used in accordance with their respective licensing agreements. We anticipate no significant foreseeable negative social impacts resulting from this work.

A.3
Analysis of Agent Thought Contents

Quantitative analysis on agent thoughts is inherently challenging due to their linguistic complexity and their implicit influence on task trajectories. To address this, we propose a hierarchical thought taxonomy informed by three key observations: (1) complex agent thoughts can be decomposed into a sequence of simpler, atomic units; (2) each atomic thought serves either as action planning or state perception; and (3) the subject of an atomic thought is either the user or the environment. Based on these principles, we established a two-level hierarchical classification system. An example showing all thougt categories is shown in Figure
8
. For our empirical analysis, we randomly sampled 20% of the trajectories from our dataset and employed GPT-4.1 to decompose and categorize the agent thoughts accordingly.

The distribution of this taxonomy is illustrated in Figure
9
, where yellow segments represent action planning (including user-interactive actions and tool-calling actions) and blue segments represent state perception (including user intention confirmation and database observation summarization). Our visualization reveals two primary findings. First, action planning dominates the thought processes of many agents, particularly within the GPT series. In these models, over half of the generated thoughts focus on planning future actions based on existing observations. While such proactive planning is essential for complex reasoning, it may be suboptimal in user-engaged scenarios where current observations are often insufficient for task completion without further user clarification. Second, the specific instantiation of the "thinking" process significantly shifts thought content. For example, the proportion of tool-calling thoughts in GPT-5 increases from 44.4% to 59.5% when transitioning from Thinking-as-a-Function (TaaF) to Thinking-as-a-Prefix (TaaP) framework. This suggests that thinking patterns are highly sensitive to framework design and prompt instructions. Ultimately, while our taxonomy effectively characterizes agent thinking patterns, it does not directly measure their causal impact on subsequent actions; these effects are further explored through our analysis of response lengths, action taxonomies, and qualitative case studies.

A.6
Human-in-the-loop Validation of Response Taxonomy

To validate the effectiveness of the automatic response taxonomy pipeline, we conduct a human-in-the-loop experiment of response taxonomy annotation. Ideally, if the automatic pipeline is valid, then we could observe a similar number of response categories for human annotation and model annotation. Specifically, we select approximately 1,060 atomic responses from 20 trajectories (10 trajectories from
\tau
-Retail and 10 trajectories from
\tau
-Airline, with DeepSeek-V3.1 as a target agent of investigation). For each scenario, half of the trajectories are from agents without thinking, while the other half are from agents with thinking. Next, we do the manual validation based on the random assignment criteria.

As shown in Table
7
, the human-in-the-loop validation result demonstrates an excellent consistency of annotation between the human expert and GPT-4.1. For both categories, we observe a consistent drop in frequency per trajectory, as the frequency drops from 47.1 to 37.8 with the human expert’s annotation. This result demonstrates the effectiveness of use the automatic pipeline for efficient and reliable annotation in this study. Also, we observe that the human-in-the-loop annotation is time-consuming, taking 2h 10min to complete for 20 trajectories. On average, each trajectory takes 6 min 30 s for a human expert to annotate.

Case study of
\tau
-Airline.

Figure
16
,
17
and
18
show a summary of the task case, trajectory snapshots of the agent with thinking and the agent without thinking, respectively. The key challenge of this task is that the user’s initial returning flight from IAH to LAS has already been nonstop, but the user doesn’t remember. Therefore, the agent needs to disclose this information to the user to prevent unnecessary flight bookings. As shown in Figure
17
, although the thinking agent found available non-stop flights for both outbound and return, it fails to disclose existing flight booking information to the user. This misleads the user to unnecessarily update the return flight to HAT131, causing task failure.

Case study of TS-Phone.

Figure
19
presents the case of TS-Phone. The key challenge of this task lies in the semantic gap between the ground truth (the time length until Christmas day) and the pre-given user initial request (what is the date of Christmas day). The agent must actively interact with the user to propel the user to express the accurate intention. As shown in Figure
19
, the agent with thinking indeed accurately answers the exact date of Christmas Day. However, it does not disclose any other information or any requests to the user. In practice, this introverted response pattern can reduce the interaction intention of the user, leading to task failure. Differently, the non-thinking agent not only accurately answers the date of Christmas Day, but also discloses its current situation and requests. e.g., it cannot look up the exact date at the moment because the Wi-Fi is disabled. Semantically, this more active response pattern encourages the user to actively respond to the agent, too, as shown in the difference between the tone of the user in the two trajectories.

The vanilla prompt is the initial system message to tool-calling agents. For each scenario, the vanilla prompt consists of a demonstration of the sceanrio, and the task policy. The task policy regulates the rules and constraints of agent actions in different situations.
