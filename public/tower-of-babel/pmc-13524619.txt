Optimising superficial parasternal intercostal plane blockade after cardiac surgery: single injection first?
Authors: Sachin Mehta, Ron M Ree, Christopher Prabhakar, Terri Sun, Tim Ting Han Jen
Published: 2026

We congratulate Alli et al. on their multicentre trial, demonstrating that superficial parasternal intercostal plane (SPIP) blockade, compared with sham blockade, significantly reduced 72-h opioid consumption after median sternotomy.1 These findings reinforce prior literature supporting SPIP blockade in cardiac surgery,2 and we agree SPIP blockade should be incorporated into enhanced recovery after cardiac surgery pathways. However, the optimal choice between single-injection and continuous blockade remains uncertain.

Continuous SPIP blockade may not confer meaningful benefits over a single injection. Previous studies have demonstrated significant opioid-sparing and analgesic effects with single-injection SPIP blockade alone.2 Given the current study’s design and the use of 72-h cumulative opioid consumption as the primary outcome, readers cannot discern the extent to which analgesia resulted from the continuous infusion versus the initial block. Therefore, the authors may consider reporting outcomes by time intervals (e.g., day 0/1/2) to better characterise the temporal pattern of analgesic benefit. Multimodal analgesia use, such as acetaminophen or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, should be reported. Notably, despite reduced opioid consumption, no improvements in patient-centred outcomes were observed.

To date, data directly comparing continuous versus single-injection SPIP blockade have yielded mixed results (Table 1).3, 4, 5 This is likely attributable to differences in technique: catheter selection (single- versus multi-orifice), local anaesthetic volume and concentration, initial bolus dosing, infusion regimen (continuous infusion versus programmed intermittent bolus), and multimodal analgesia. These factors are important given that SPIP blockade has been found to have limited cephalocaudal spread from a single injection level. Programmed intermittent boluses or high infusion volumes may be necessary to achieve adequate spread (Table 1). Trials comparing active blockade with sham, such as the present study, can provide indirect evidence on the relative benefits of continuous and single-injection blockade in a future network meta-analysis, complementing the scarce head-to-head data.

Finally, adequate spread must also be balanced against the risk of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST). Two trials involving SPIP catheters reported rising plasma ropivacaine concentrations or suspected LAST within 48 h of infusion.3,4 Given the potentially serious consequences in this at-risk population, further safety studies characterising local anaesthetic systemic absorption from the SPIP must be conducted before continuous blockade can be routinely implemented.

Given the available evidence and remaining uncertainties, we believe single-injection SPIP blockade should be the preferred technique for most patients undergoing median sternotomy, until continuous blockade demonstrates clinically meaningful incremental improvements in patient-centred outcomes that outweigh their additional cost, logistical complexity, and risks.
