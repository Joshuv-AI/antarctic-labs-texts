Re-evaluating the role of magnifying endoscopy in postendoscopic submucosal dissection vessel detection
Authors: Yusuf Bünyamin Ketenci, Müge Ustaoğlu Dede
Published: 2026

To the editor:

We read with great interest the case report by Yang et al1 regarding the use of magnifying endoscopy (ME) to detect hidden vessels after gastric endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD). Although the authors propose a “single-endoscope management strategy,” we believe that the clinical necessity and safety of this approach warrant a more critical evaluation based on procedural efficiency and established guidelines.

First, the necessity of ME in this context is questionable. Penetrating vessels of this nature are typically identifiable under standard white-light endoscopy (WLE) with the aid of a transparent hood and meticulous water-jet cleaning. Utilizing high-magnification modes for routine postprocedural screening is an inefficient use of both time and hospital resources. As noted in the literature, magnifying imaging technologies can significantly prolong procedure times compared with optimized WLE.2,3

Second, the timing of the intervention is a crucial concern. Ideally, penetrating vessels should be identified and managed proactivity during the dissection process—either immediately before or after the initial incision. These vessels are usually visible during the active phase; managing them as they appear is the standard of care. Identifying and intervening upon such vessels during a secondary postprocedural screening phase—especially in an area where the muscularis propria is already compromised—paradoxically increases the risk of iatrogenic perforation.4,5

Finally, the detection in this case appears to be a serendipitous finding linked to a localized injury. This focus introduces a significant selection bias, potentially overlooking high-risk vessels in other regions of the ulcer base where no visible muscle damage is present.

In conclusion, although the high resolution of ME is invaluable for diagnosis, its routine use for post-ESD vessel detection appears redundant. Meticulous intraprocedural management and standard WLE inspection remain the most practical cornerstones for preventing delayed bleeding. In our institution, we strictly adhere to these established clinical guidelines and intraprocedural protocols in our daily practice to ensure optimal patient safety and procedural efficiency.
