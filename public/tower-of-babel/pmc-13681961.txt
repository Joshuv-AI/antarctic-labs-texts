Quantitative proteomic landscape reveals the role of CSPG5 in the recurrence of glioma
Authors: Shijian Wang, Baorui Tian, Huimin Liu, Chen Zhang, Jianan Wang, Xin Song, Ruibing Li, Haoran Guo, Nan Wang, Wanjun Zhang, Yongjun Piao, Yi Shi, Rong Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe high recurrence rate of glioma accounts for the majority of glioma-related deaths. Although numerous studies have investigated genomic and transcriptomic alterations, high-resolution quantitative proteomic profiling studies remain limited.MethodsA total of 63 pairs of primary and recurrent formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded glioma tissues (WHO grade II–IV) were collected. Proteomic profiles were generated by data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (DIA-MS). Differentially expressed proteins were validated using publicly available databases, immunohistochemistry, and Western blotting. The effects of CSPG5 on glioma cell proliferation and invasion were assessed in vitro and in an orthotopic mouse model. Protein–protein interactions were examined by co-immunoprecipitation, and the influence of CSPG5 on ErbB2 degradation was evaluated by immunofluorescence and quantitative PCR.ResultsTwo distinct proteomic clusters (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2) associated with glioma recurrence were identified. Chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 5 (CSPG5) was markedly down-regulated in recurrent tumors. Overexpression of CSPG5 significantly inhibited glioma cell proliferation and invasion in vitro and reduced tumor burden in vivo. Mechanistically, CSPG5 interacted with ErbB2 and promoted its lysosomal degradation, leading to attenuated PI3K-AKT and ERK signaling.ConclusionsCSPG5 acts as a novel suppressor of ErbB2 signaling; its loss facilitates ErbB2 hyperactivation and thereby promotes glioma recurrence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12935-026-04430-z.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and aggressive primary intracranial neoplasm [1] Since the introduction of maximal safe resection plus concomitant chemoradiotherapy, median survival has increased yet remains dismal. However, the median progression-free survival and median overall survival from diagnosis based on the standard treatments remain 6.2–7.5 months and 14.6–16.7 months, respectively [1–3]. This poor outcome is driven by near-universal recurrence after surgery, radiotherapy and temozolomide. Recurrent gliomas—especially high-grade lesions—are highly invasive, allowing cells to evade resection margins and adopt a more proliferative, migratory phenotype [4]. In addition, the post-operative microenvironment—marked by blood–brain barrier disruption, cerebral edema and sustained microglial activation—can reactivate dormant residual tumor cells [5, 6]. Furthermore, therapy-induced hypermutation, clonal selection of pre-existing resistant cells, and acquisition of stem-like properties collectively generate heterogeneous, therapy-refractory lesions that underlie virtually all relapses [7–10].

Previous multi-omics studies have mapped genomic, epigenomic and transcriptomic alterations that accompany glioma recurrence. These analyses revealed that GBM cells exhibit high phenotypic plasticity and can switch between distinct transcriptional states [11, 12]. However, mRNA abundance alone often fails to predict actual protein levels [13]. Therefore, proteomic profiling is essential to decipher the biological processes driving glioma progression and recurrence. Unlike transcriptomic data, proteomic profiles directly reflect the functional state of cells, including post-translational modifications and protein-protein interactions—both of which play a critical role in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance [14].

Chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 5 (CSPG5, also termed CALEB/NGC) is a transmembrane protein predominantly expressed in the nervous system. It harbors an EGF-like domain that binds extracellular matrix proteins such as tenascin-C and growth factors such as bFGF [15–17], thereby modulating pericellular signaling niches. Nevertheless, the functional impact of CSPG5 on glioma onset or recurrence remains unexplored. In contrast, its paralogue CSPG4 has been shown to drive glioma progression [18]. Outside the brain, CSPG5 correlates with prognosis in ovarian and hepatocellular carcinoma, although the underlying mechanisms differ [19, 20].

This study employed data-independent acquisition (DIA)-based quantitative proteomic technology to systematically compare the protein expression profiles of 63 paired primary and recurrent glioma samples across different World Health Organization (WHO) grades (II–IV). For the first time, we identified CSPG5 as a key functional protein in glioma recurrence. Functional assays showed that CSPG5 binds ErbB2, blocks ErbB2/ErbB3 dimerization, triggers ErbB2 internalization and lysosomal degradation. Consequently, total and phospho-ErbB2 levels decrease, suppressing glioma cell proliferation and invasion. More importantly, the expression level of CSPG5 was significantly positively correlated with the progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) of patients, suggesting its potential to serve as a predictive biomarker for recurrence risk.

Materials and methods

Data availability

Paired primary and recurrent formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) glioma tissues and matched clinicopathological data were collected from 63 glioma patients (WHO grade II–IV) who received initial and recurrent surgical resection between 2005 and 2018. All sample collection procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (approval No. S2021-129-01). De-identified clinical data (age, gender, ethnicity, initial diagnosis, recurrence diagnosis, recurrence interval, MGMT methylation, IDH1/2 status, post-resection treatment) are provided in Supplementary Table S1. All patients were adults.

Cell lines and culture

HEK 293T cell line was obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Washington, USA). Mouse glioma cell line GL261 was generously provided by Prof. Kang in Tianjin Medical University General Hospital and grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA),1% penicillin/Streptomycin at 37 ℃, 5% CO2.

Stable cell strain establishment

The coding sequence of CSPG5 was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and cloned into plasmid pLV-EF1α-MCS-IRES-Bsd (Biosettia, San Diego, CA, USA). Stable CSPG5 overexpression was conducted in GL261 cell line. Lentivirus-based RNAi vector pLV-H1-EF1α-puro (Biosettia) with the insertion of shRNA templates was used to conduct ErbB2 and ErbB3 knockdown in GL261 cell line. shErbB2#1: 85% knockdown efficacy; shErbB2#2: 80%; shErbB3#1: 82%; shErbB3#2: 78%. Lentiviral infection followed by 7-day puromycin selection. Cells infected with lentivirus for knocking down or reconstituted expression were selected with 4 µg/mL of blasticidin or 10 µg/mL of puromycin (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for one-week.

Cell counting assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well with 2 mL of culture medium containing 10% FBS. The cells were digested and counted every 24 h until they reached nearly 100% confluence.

CCK-8 assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well with 100 µL of culture medium containing 10% FBS for 12 h. CCK-8 kit (Dojindo Molecular Technologies) was used according to the manufacturer’s manual. Next, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 1 h at 37 ℃. The absorbance at 450 nm was detected by a microplate reader (Promega).

EdU(5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine) staining proliferation assay

The logarithmic growth phase CSPG5 overexpression or control GL261 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 6,000 cells/well. The completely attached cells were incubated with 100 µl EdU solution (50 µM, RiboBio, China) for 2 h at 37℃. Then cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 30 min at room temperature. Next, EdU was labeled with Apollo 567 and cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 333,342. Finally, cells were observed via an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) under ×20 objective magnification.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA from cells and tissues was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and then reverse transcribed into cDNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions of M-MLV reverse transcriptase (M1708, Promega). Real-time PCR analyses were performed with LightCycler®96 system (Roche) using SYBR Green SuperMix (Invitrogen) at the recommended thermal cycling settings: 95℃ 300 s, (95℃ 30 s, 60℃ 45 s) × 45 cycles. The relative expression levels of genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, and gene expression was normalized by β-actin.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well with 2 mL of culture medium containing 10% FBS. After culture for 14 days, the colonies on the plate were fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature, and then stained with crystal violet (Co21,Beyotime) for 15 min; the numbers of colonies were counted by ImageJ software to evaluate cell proliferation ability.

RNA sequencing

The mRNA was isolated using oligo-dT purification from three independent biological replicates from the following cell conditions: empty vector, CSPG5 overexpression. O1A/O2A/O3A = CSPG5-overexpression replicates; V1A/V2A/V3A = vector control replicates (n = 3). The RNA sequencing was performed and analyzed on BGIseq500 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted and analyzed on the basis of GSEA software(http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/downloads.jsp). The log2 transformation was used before pathway enrichment analysis.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissues or cells using 1× RIPA buffer supplemented with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail and 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein samples were heated at 95℃ for 10 min, and 30 µg of clarified lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE under denaturing conditions, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 2 h and then incubated overnight at 4℃ with the following primary antibodies: anti-CSPG5 (Abcam, ab218137, 1:300), anti-ErbB2 (Proteintech, 18299-1-AP, 1:1000), anti-ErbB3 (CST, 12708, 1:1000), anti-phospho-ErbB2 (CST, 2247, 1:1000), anti-phospho-AKT (CST, 4060, 1:1000), anti-phospho-ERK (CST, 4370, 1:1000), and anti-β-actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig, 1:5000). After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (RGAR011 for rabbit primary antibodies, RGAM011 for mouse primary antibodies; Proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized via chemiluminescence detection (LI-COR Biosciences, USA).

Co-immunoprecipitations (Co-IP)

2 µg of anti-V5 antibody (Invitrogen, R960-25) was precipitated with protein A/G magnetic beads (Millipore) for 4 h and washed with PBS. Cells were lysed using Co-IP lysis buffer (20 mmol/L Tris (pH 7.5), 150 mmol/L NaCl, 2 mmol/L EDTA and 1% Triton X-100) with protease inhibitor cocktail. The supernatants of the lysates were collected after centrifugation and then incubated with anti-V5 antibody and protein A/G magnetic beads at 4 °C overnight, then washed with PBS. The proteins were eluted and detected by western blotting. In these assays, anti-V5 antibody was used for immunoprecipitation, and input represented 5% of the total cell lysate.

Protein half-life assay

Cells were treated with cycloheximide (MedChemExpress, HY-12320) for the indicated times, then lysed, finally subjected to western blotting.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue samples were cut into 5 μm sections, and then deparaffinized in xylene. For IHC staining, antigen retrieval was performed in Epitope Retrieval Buffer for 20 min at 100℃ and peroxidase blocking. 5% goat serum was used for blocking tissue sections before being incubated with primary antibodies, biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies (BA-1000, Vector Laboratories), and streptavidin-HRP (SA-5704, Vector Laboratories). After washes, DAB solution (DAKO K3468) was added for 50 s followed by washing and counterstaining with hematoxylin. The images were taken by using microscope scanner (BX53, Olympus, Japan) at 20× and 40×. IHC staining results were evaluated by two independent pathologists in a blinded manner using the H-score method: H-score = (1 × % weak staining) + (2 × % moderate staining) + (3 × % strong staining), with a range of 0-300.

Immunofluorescence staining

CSPG5 OE and control GL261 cells (1 × 104 cells) were seeded on slides in 24-well plates and incubated overnight. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 20 min, and then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min and blocked with 5% goat serum for 60 min. Staining was performed using primary antibodies: anti-ErbB2 (Proteintech,18299-1-AP,1:200dilution), anti-ErbB3 (CellSignalingTechnology,12708,1:100dilution), LAMP1 (Proteintech; CL647-65050, 1:500 dilution ), V5 (Elabscience; E-AB-20010;1:1000 dilution). After three washes with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST), cells were incubated with secondary antibodies(goat anti-rabbit IgG or goat anti-mouse IgG antibodies conjugated with either Alexa488 or Alexa594) for 1 hour, then cells were washed and incubated with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 3 min. Images were acquired using an Olympus FV1000 laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, Japan) with a ×100 oil immersion objective lens. All images were randomly assigned coded names to allow blinded scoring for colocalization and fluorescence intensity quantification by ImageJ. Notably, colocalization observed by immunofluorescence indicates molecular proximity, while direct physical interaction was confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays.

Animal Experiments

1.5 × 10⁵ GL261 luciferase cells stably infected with CSPG5 overexpression or vector were stereotactically injected into the right striatum area of 6–8 weeks female C57BL/6 mice (n = 6 per group). Mice were randomly grouped, and bioluminescence imaging and survival assessment were performed in a blinded manner. The bioluminescence images were acquired at 0,10,20,30 and 40 days after injection using a small animal live imager.

Bioinformatics analysis

All differential analyses, functional analyses, and signaling pathway analyses of the proteome in this study were performed using open-source bioinformatics tools. Specifically, the differential proteomic analysis, unsupervised clustering, and heatmap generation for all detected samples were conducted using R software Version 4.1.3. The R Foundation for Statistical Computing. Volcano plots were generated via the OE Cloud Platform. To analyze alterations between primary and recurrent groups, Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was performed using R software Version 4.1.3. PLS-DA is a supervised analysis that incorporates group labels to maximize statistical separation between cohorts; its coordinate score reflects the multi-dimensional integrated projection value of each specimen, where closer spatial proximity denotes higher proteomic pattern similarity. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was carried out with GSEA-4.3.2 software. Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA), Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were implemented using R software Version 4.1.3.

To externally validate our findings, three independent public datasets were analyzed as distinct validation cohorts: The GEPIA2 platform was utilized based on datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, 668 primary glioma samples across grades II-IV with full age, gender, and survival profiles). Transcriptomic datasets obtained from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA, 1,018 primary and recurrent glioma samples across grades II-IV with comprehensive information on IDH status, MGMT methylation, and adjuvant therapy history) were analyzed for differential expression, survival, GSEA, and GSVA using R software Version 4.1.3. Proteomic datasets retrieved from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC, comprising 88 primary gliomas distributed as 22 grade II, 30 grade III, 36 grade IV specimens, and 10 adjacent normal brain controls) were subjected to protein differential and correlation analyses using R software Version 4.2.1. Crucially, our local 63-paired clinical specimen cohort was independently processed and entirely separate from these three public databases.

Statistical analysis

All data in this study were statistically analyzed using R software (Version 4.1.3) and GraphPad Prism 8. Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median. For continuous variables, comparisons of means between two groups were performed using the independent-samples Student’s t-test. Paired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for paired primary–recurrent samples. For multi-group analyses, one-way analysis of variance was applied after testing for homogeneity of variance. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significant correlations in Pearson and Spearman analyses were defined by two-tailed p values (p < 0.05). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank test. FDR correction was applied for multiple testing in proteomic and RNA-seq analyses.

Result

The proteomic landscape of primary and recurrent gliomas

We selected paired surgically resected primary and recurrent tumors from 63 grade II, III, IV glioma patients’ formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples (n = 126), all of these patients relapsed or died after achieving the second resection. In addition, we collected clinicopathological information on these patients and samples. The research workflow is presented in Fig. 1A. Disease-free survival was significantly shorter after the second resection than after the initial surgery (Fig. 1B). Proteomic analysis was carried out by DIA-MS on a single mass spectrometer under strict quality-control metrics. In total, 7924 proteins were identified from all primary and recurrent samples. No substantial differences in protein identification numbers were observed between matched primary and recurrent samples (Fig. 1C, D).

Proteomic features in primary gliomas compared with recurrent gliomas

Partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) of the primary and recurrent proteomes revealed partial overlap between the two cohorts (Fig. 2A). Comparative analysis identified 780 differentially abundant proteins (FDR < 0.05): 540 up-regulated and 240 down-regulated in recurrent tumors, with 9 and 12 of these showing ≥ 2-fold changes, respectively (Fig. 2B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) mapped these alterations to 20 KEGG pathways (Fig. 2C). Down-regulated pathways were enriched for synapse- and adhesion-related functions, including cell-adhesion molecules, axon guidance, and neurotrophin signalling, which govern synapse and axon formation, neuronal and glial differentiation, neurite extension, synaptogenesis, and synaptic transmission [13]. Several metabolic pathways were also suppressed. Conversely, up-regulated pathways included nucleotide excision repair and mismatch repair dysfunction, which generate a hypermutator phenotype associated with temozolomide resistance and shortened post-recurrence survival [8, 21]. Collectively, these pathway perturbations contribute to the aggressive clinical course observed after glioma relapse.

Proteomic subtype transformation of gliomas from primary to recurrence and their association with prognosis

We analyzed all paired primary and recurrent tumor samples and performed an unsupervised clustering analysis based on their protein expression profiles. All cases were divided into two subclusters named as cluster 1 and cluster 2 (Fig. 3A). Forty-one pairs remained in the same cluster (41/63), whereas 22 pairs switched clusters. Of these, 18 switched from cluster 1 to cluster 2 at recurrence, and 4 switched from cluster 2 to cluster (1) By comparing the prognosis of two subclusters, we observed that the disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) of cluster 2 were significantly shorter than cluster 1 (Fig. 3B). This result indicates that cases with the cluster 2 phenotype have a poor prognosis. The evaluation of the clinical characteristics of two subtypes reveals that the number of grade-II glioma samples in cluster 1 was significantly more than those in cluster 2, while grade-IV glioma samples in cluster 1 were significantly lower than that in cluster (2) Cluster 2 also contained more IDH-wild-type tumors, which are associated with poor prognosis. On the other hand, there was no significant difference between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 in samples with MGMT-methylation; the difference in prognosis between these two clusters may not be related to temozolomide (TMZ) resistance (Fig. 3C). Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) did not differ significantly between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2, indicating that the observed survival disparity is more likely attributable to intrinsic molecular characteristics of the tumors rather than baseline patient performance.

Next, we focused on cases that switched clusters at recurrence. The Ki-67 proliferation index was significantly increased with the subcluster of tumors changed from cluster 1 to cluster 2 at recurrence stage, suggesting an increased proliferation capacity of tumors.The rate of p53 positivity decreased concurrently with the cluster 1→2 transition (Fig. 3D). This phenomenon may be explained by the fact that overexpressed mutant p53 loses its core functions including promoting apoptosis, arresting cell cycle and repairing DNA [22].Differential expression analysis revealed 989 proteins that differed significantly between cluster 1 and cluster 2. Among these, 466 proteins were up-regulated and 523 proteins were down-regulated in Cluster 2. The differential proteins were analyzed by KEGG enrichment analysis. The pathways significantly activated in cluster 2 include complement and coagulation cascades, DNA replication, ECM-receptor interaction, cell cycle, and other pathways closely related to cancer development (Fig. 3E). Down-regulated pathways in cluster 2 included GABAergic synapse, synaptic vesicle cycle, glutamatergic synapse, retrograde endocannabinoid signaling, and cholinergic synapse, all of which mediate synaptic vesicle exocytosis, neurotransmitter release, and interneuronal communication (Fig. 3F). These findings suggest that disruption of neuronal synaptic transmission may influence glioma progression.

We next sought proteins robustly associated with recurrence, as they may represent therapeutic targets and yield mechanistic insight into glioma progression. Among 24 proteins showing significant differences between paired primary and recurrent tumors, five were strongly associated with patient prognosis (P < 0.001). In these proteins, the hazard ratio indicates that the increase in CSPG5 maximally improved clinical outcomes for glioma patients (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, CSPG5 exhibited the largest fold-change between cluster 1 and cluster 2, underscoring its central role in glioma progression.

The expression of CSPG5 decreased at glioma recurrence

Our proteomic data showed that the abundance of CSPG5 in recurrent gliomas was reduced relative to in matched primary gliomas (Fig. 4A). Consistent with our proteomic analysis, the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) database also revealed that the mRNA expression of CSPG5 was downregulated in recurrent gliomas [23]. In addition, the expression of CSPG5 protein was higher in low-grade gliomas and IDH1-mutated gliomas than in high-grade gliomas (Fig. 4A), suggesting that high expression of CSPG5 protein might lead to longer survival in patients. Next, we validated the expression of CSPG5 in 88 brain tumor samples and 10 adjacent normal tissue samples from the CPTAC database. The results showed that the expression of CSPG5 in gliomas was significantly lower than that in adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 4B). Moreover, in our proteomic data, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and CGGA datasets, the expression level of CSPG5 was correlated with overall survival. All these datasets indicated that the high expression level of CSPG5 was positively correlated with the survival of glioma patients (Fig. 4C). In addition to database validation, we also detected the expression of CSPG5 in primary-recurrent glioma patients with different malignant degrees and their paired adjacent normal tissues. The results showed that the expression of CSPG5 in adjacent normal tissues was higher than that in tumor tissues (Figs. 4D, E). Similarly, the expression of CSPG5 in primary tumors was significantly higher than that in recurrent tumors (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, all primary and recurrent pathological sections were divided into two groups according to immunohistochemical scores: the CSPG5 high-expression group and the CSPG5 low-expression group. The disease-free survival (DFS) of the high - expression group was significantly longer than that of the low - expression group (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these data indicate that low CSPG5 expression predicts worse prognosis.

Overexpression of CSPG5 reduced the proliferation and invasion of glioblastoma

CSPG5 is a transmembrane protein composed of an N-terminal segment that contains chondroitin sulfate chains followed by an acidic stretch, an epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like domain, a transmembrane domain, and a cytoplasmic segment. CSPG5 expression is restricted to the central nervous system; the precursor is processed into a truncated transmembrane form with an exposed EGF domain and a secreted isoform [24]. To further investigate the role of CSPG5 in glioma progression, we first used the mouse glioma cell line GL261 to overexpress CSPG5 via lentivirus infection (Fig. 5A). CSPG5 overexpression significantly reduced GL261 cell proliferation. The cell counting experiment showed that the number of GL261 cells significantly decreased in CSPG5 overexpression group(Fig. 5B). The results of colony formation experiments confirmed that overexpression of CSPG5 significantly reduced the colony-forming capacity of GL261 cells(Fig. 5C). CSPG5 overexpression markedly reduced the proportion of EdU⁺ S-phase cells, confirming its potent inhibition of glioma proliferation (Fig. 5D). In addition, CSPG5 overexpression significantly reduced the invasive capacity of GL261 cells, while the migration was not significantly changed (Fig. 5E). Our results demonstrate that CSPG5 reduces proliferation and invasion of GL261 cells. Consistent suppressive effects on cell proliferation and migration were further validated in two human glioma cell lines (U87 and U251), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

CSPG5 suppresses the ErbB signaling pathway through the members of ERBB family of receptor tyrosine kinases

Next, we investigated the potential molecular mechanism by which CSPG5 regulates the growth of GL261 cells. Transcriptomic analysis was performed on GL261 cells with CSPG5 overexpression, followed by differential gene expression analysis. Compared with the control group, 1828 genes were upregulated and 1287 genes were downregulated in the CSPG5 overexpression group, with statistical significance. These differential genes were analyzed using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) (Figs. 7A, B). Meanwhile, CGGA glioma samples were stratified into CSPG5-low and CSPG5-high cohorts based on RNA levels, and GSVA was performed on differential genes (Fig. 7C). We verified the regulatory effect of CSPG5 on the ErbB signaling pathway. Through GSVA analysis, the differentially expressed RNAs in the transcriptome of GL261 cells with CSPG5 overexpression were enriched in the Reactome pathway dataset, and multiple signaling pathways involved in the regulation of the ErbB family were identified. The study found that after CSPG5 overexpression, multiple pathways were upregulated or downregulated, among which the most important ones were ErbB-PI3K-AKT and ErbB-ERK.

Western blot assay was used to verify the expression of signature proteins of the ErbB receptor family and related proteins in these two pathways. CSPG5 overexpression decreased Grb2 expression and reduced phospho-AKT and phospho-ERK levels (Fig. 7D). Co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) assay was performed to verify the interaction between CSPG5 and members of the ErbB receptor family. The results demonstrated that CSPG5 could bind to ErbB2 and ErbB3, but not to EGFR or ErbB4. In addition, overexpression of CSPG5 in GL261 cells reduced the phosphorylation levels of ErbB2 and ErbB3 (Figs. 7E, F).

CSPG5 is a transmembrane protein bearing chondroitin sulfate and EGF domains and is highly expressed on neuronal and glial surfaces. It is mainly involved in the formation of the central nervous system and is associated with synaptic maturation [25, 26]. Immunofluorescence staining was further used to verify the colocalization of CSPG5 with either ErbB2 or ErbB3 in GL261 glioma cells overexpressing CSPG5-V5. Consistent with the results of the CO-IP assay, the immunofluorescence assay also indicated that CSPG5 interacts with ErbB2 and ErbB3 (Fig. 7G). CSPG5 overexpression significantly decreased total and membrane-localized ErbB2 (Fig. 7H). However, there was no significant difference in ErbB3 expression between the vector group and GL261 cells with CSPG5 overexpression (Fig. 7I). Thus, CSPG5 preferentially binds ErbB2 at the membrane and subsequently escorts it to the cytoplasm for lysosomal degradation.

CSPG5 inhibits the growth of GL261 cells in an ErbB2-dependent manner

The members of ErbB family are known to form homo- or heterodimers when stimulated by their ligands [27, 28]. Among them, the dimerization of ErbB3 with ErbB2, which has the strongest kinase activity of all ErbB proteins, represents the most efficient signaling receptor pair in the ErbB family [24]. We further evaluated the role of ErbB2 and ErbB3 in the cancer phenotype of CSPG5 overexpression and control. Stable GL261 lines with lentivirus-mediated ErbB2 or ErbB3 knockdown and corresponding empty-vector controls were generated. ErbB2 knockdown markedly reduced proliferation in vector-control GL261 cells and abolished the difference between CSPG5-overexpressing and control cells (Fig. 8A). By contrast, ErbB3 knockdown reduced proliferation to a limited extent in both groups, but the difference between CSPG5-overexpressing and control cells remained evident (Fig. 8B). Co-IP results revealed that ErbB3 binding to CSPG5 was markedly decreased upon ErbB2 knockdown (Fig. 8C). In contrast, the interaction of CSPG5 and ErbB2 was not significantly reduced when knocking down ErbB3 (Fig. 8D). We further verified that CSPG5 directly binds to ErbB2. We performed co-immunoprecipitation in HEK 293T cells transiently transfected with CSPG5 together with ErbB2 or ErbB3. After transfection with CSPG5 and ErbB2, the binding of CSPG5 and ErbB2 was significantly enhanced compared with the control (Fig. 8E), whereas CSPG5–ErbB3 binding was not significantly enhanced (Fig. 8F).

CSPG5 regulates ErbB2 stability via lysosomal degradation pathway

We explored the correlation between CSPG5 and ErbB receptor family members in glioma using CPTAC database. The results showed that there is a clear correlation between the expression of CSPG5 and ErbB2 but not significant between CSPG5 and other ErbB family members (Fig. 9A, B), which indicates that increased CSPG5 will lead to a decrease in the expression of ERBB2 without significantly affecting other ErbB receptors. Next, we investigated the mechanistic details of CSPG5 reduced ErbB2 expression. First, the mRNA expression levels of ErbB2 and ErbB3 in CSPG5 OE and control GL261 cells were detected. The expression of ErbB2 and ErbB3 remained unchanged upon CSPG5 overexpression, indicating that CSPG5 regulates ErbB2 at a post-transcriptional level (Fig. 9C). Then, we conducted Cycloheximide (CHX) chase experiments to evaluate whether CSPG5 altered ErbB2 protein expression by promoting protein degradation (Fig. 9D). The half-life of ErbB2 was significantly shorter in the CSPG5 OE group than in the control group after treatment with CHX (Fig. 9E). In CSPG5-overexpressing cells, MG132-mediated proteasome inhibition slightly increased ErbB2 levels, whereas chloroquine-mediated lysosome inhibition markedly elevated ErbB2 protein levels (Fig. 9F).

Next, we further verified the degradation pathway for ErbB2. We performed immunofluorescence experiments in CSPG5 overexpression and control GL261 cells treated with chloroquine. The amount of ErbB2 colocalized with lysosomes was significantly higher in CSPG5-overexpressing GL261 cells than in vector controls (Fig. 9G). At the same time, there was no statistical difference between the two groups in the amount of ErbB3 colocalized with lysosomes (Fig. 9H).

Discussion

To date, most studies focusing on primary and recurrent gliomas are related to genomics and transcriptomics. However, there are few studies of primary and recurrent gliomas, especially in low-grade gliomas, using proteomics approaches. Proteins are responsible for the regulation of various physiological and pathological processes. Therefore, compared with genomics and transcriptomics, proteomics reflects the occurrence and development of gliomas more accurately and directly. Recent high-quality proteomic studies have systematically characterized molecular alterations in paired primary-recurrent glioma cohorts to screen recurrence-related biomarkers and dissect therapeutic resistance mechanisms [29–32]. Consistent with these published works, our proteomic data also revealed remarkable molecular continuity between matched primary and recurrent lesions, implying that recurrent gliomas mostly originate from residual subclones of primary tumors after standard treatment.

Here, we quantified the proteomes of 63 paired primary and recurrent gliomas by DIA-MS. Proteomic profiles exhibited substantial similarity, consistent with recurrent tumors arising from residual primary disease. It appears that recurrent tumors derive from residual primary cells in which specific subclones expanded after therapy. Up-regulated pathways from primary to recurrent stage were involved in tumor progression and drug resistance. Down-regulated pathways were associated with the formation of synapses and axons, regulation of synapses, neuronal and glial differentiation, neurite extension, synaptogenesis and synaptic transmission. Unsupervised clustering separated samples into two proteomic subtypes; most paired primary and recurrent tumors occupied the same cluster. However, there were some patients whose primary and recurrent tumors were classified in different clusters. Then, we found that the patients in cluster 1 had longer disease-free survival and overall survival than patients in cluster 2. Tumors that underwent subtype transition were partially accompanied by shifts in tumor grade and MGMT methylation status. Thus, transition from cluster 1 to cluster 2 constitutes proteomic evidence of disease progression. Benefiting from our paired experimental design, we further performed paired comparative statistical analysis on the expression changes of differential proteins between each matched primary and recurrent glioma lesion from the same patient, which effectively eliminated confounding effects from individual genetic background and heterogeneous treatment history, and more reliably screened recurrence-related candidate molecules such as CSPG5.

By analyzing protein expression levels across different stages and clusters, we identified CSPG5 as a protein closely related to glioma prognosis, whose down-regulation may drive tumor progression. CSPG5 is a transmembrane protein composed of an N-terminal segment containing chondroitin sulfate chains and an acidic stretch, an EGF-like domain, a transmembrane domain, and a cytoplasmic segment. CSPG5 expression is restricted to the central nervous system and appears to be generated as a precursor protein that becomes converted to a truncated transmembrane form with an exposed EGF domain or a secreted form [24].Existing studies have demonstrated that CSPG4, a homologous family member of CSPG5, exerts a pro-tumorigenic role in glioma. It can promote the proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis of tumor cells by activating the integrin signaling pathway [18]. In contrast, although preliminary investigations into the function of CSPG5 in other types of cancers have been conducted, the conclusions remain inconsistent. In epithelial ovarian carcinoma, CSPG5 has been reported to be a potential novel immune-related prognostic signature [19]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, its upregulated expression is associated with poor tumor prognosis, and it may regulate tumor growth by modulating metabolism-related pathways [20]. However, there has been no systematic study on the function of CSPG5 in glioma, particularly during the recurrence process, prior to the present research. Distinct from previous cohort-based proteomic studies that only described the correlation between RTK overactivation and glioma recurrence [29–32], our work further identified CSPG5 as a novel upstream post-translational regulator of ErbB2. We first clarified that CSPG5 directly binds to the ErbB2 /ErbB3 heterodimer and accelerates lysosome-dependent degradation of ErbB2, which provides a new molecular explanation for sustained oncogenic ErbB signaling in recurrent glioma. It is worth noting that while our in vitro mechanistic models rely on exogenous CSPG5 overexpression, which may yield protein abundance higher than the suppressed baseline in recurrent lesions, this approach was specifically designed to model the elevated expression levels naturally observed in primary gliomas or adjacent non-tumor brain tissues.

We verified CSPG5 expression in non-tumor, primary, and recurrent tumor tissues. CSPG5 levels were highest in normal brain, lower in primary gliomas, and lowest in recurrent tumors. Additionally, CSPG5 overexpression in GL261 cells reduced proliferation and invasion in vitro. Moreover, CSPG5 overexpression decreased glioma cell infiltration in murine brain and prolonged survival of tumor-bearing mice.

To verify the cross-species conservation of the tumor-suppressive function of CSPG5, we further supplemented functional validation experiments in two human glioma cell lines (U87 and U251). Consistent with the phenotypic results observed in GL261 cells, ectopic CSPG5 overexpression significantly suppressed colony formation and migratory capacity in both human glioma cell lines, accompanied by downregulated ErbB2 protein expression (Supplementary Fig. 1).

We performed transcriptome analysis of CSPG5-overexpressing GL261 cells and leveraged CGGA data. Differential gene sets were analyzed using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). Integrative analysis revealed enrichment of ErbB signaling pathways in both datasets. Additionally, PI3K/Akt and ERK signaling were down-regulated in CSPG5-overexpressing GL261 cells, and total ErbB2 protein was decreased. Also, CSPG5 was able to be co-immunoprecipitated with ErbB2 and ErbB3 but not with EGFR and ErbB4. The members of ErbB family are known to form homo- or heterodimers when stimulated by their ligands [27].Thus, CSPG5 inhibited the ErbB signaling pathway by binding to the ErbB2/ ErbB3 heterodimers, and interacted with the ErbB2 which heterodimerizes with ErbB3. Based on these results, we conclude that CSPG5 inhibits GL261 proliferation by reducing ErbB2 expression through lysosomal degradation rather than proteasomal degradation.

The early diagnosis of glioma recurrence is critical for improving patient prognosis. However, current imaging diagnostic modalities (e.g., magnetic resonance imaging, MRI) struggle to distinguish tumor recurrence from post-treatment responses (such as pseudoprogression) [33], creating an urgent need for specific molecular biomarkers. The present study demonstrates that CSPG5 holds potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for recurrent glioma: First, the downregulation of CSPG5 in recurrent glioma is universal, consistently observed across World Health Organization (WHO) grade II–IV gliomas and subtypes with different IDH mutation statuses. Moreover, its expression change is synchronized with the tumor recurrence process, showing a continuous decrease from primary to recurrent stages. Second, CSPG5 expression exhibits a gradient reduction across adjacent normal tissues, primary tumors, and recurrent tumors (normal tissues > primary tumors > recurrent tumors). Additionally, its expression level can effectively discriminate between Cluster 1 (favorable prognosis) and Cluster 2 (poor prognosis) subtypes. Finally, validation using public databases including the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), and Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) confirms that the positive correlation between CSPG5 expression level and patient prognosis is stably maintained across multiple cohorts. We have also standardized and supplemented complete de-identified clinical baseline data of all 63 paired patients, including age, gender, pathological grade, IDH mutation status, MGMT promoter methylation status, postoperative adjuvant treatment and detailed inclusion/exclusion criteria, which are summarized in Supplementary Table S1, further enhancing the reliability of our clinical prognostic conclusions.

The present study still has certain limitations. First, in vitro functional verification was performed in murine GL261 cells and two human glioma cell lines (U87, U251, Supplementary Fig. 1) in this study, while the regulatory axis of CSPG5-ErbB2 has not been validated in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, which weakens the clinical translational value of our findings. Second, although the clinical samples included 63 pairs of matched specimens, the follow-up duration and clinical information remain to be improved. The predictive value of CSPG5 for long-term prognosis could be verified by extending the follow-up period. Finally, the causes of CSPG5 downregulation (such as promoter methylation, gene mutation, and abnormal transcriptional regulation) have not been clarified. Further exploration of its upstream regulatory mechanisms is needed to provide a more complete molecular network for a comprehensive understanding of the role of CSPG5 in glioma recurrence. In addition, all proteomic data in this study were obtained from FFPE specimens; fresh paired primary and recurrent glioma tissues should be collected in future independent cohorts to externally validate our findings.

Conclusion

This study identified CSPG5 as a key functional protein that suppresses glioma recurrence through the modulation of ErbB2 signaling. Specifically, CSPG5 inhibits the PI3K-AKT and ERK signaling pathways by promoting the lysosomal degradation of ErbB2, thereby suppressing the proliferation and invasion of glioma cells. Downregulated expression of CSPG5 is closely associated with glioma recurrence and exhibits favorable prognostic predictive value. This finding provides a novel molecular basis for the early diagnosis and targeted intervention of glioma recurrence.
