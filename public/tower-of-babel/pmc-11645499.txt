A retrospective cohort study on predicting infants at a risk of defaulting routine immunization in Uganda using machine learning models
Authors: Bartha Alexandra Nantongo, Josephine Nabukenya, Peter Nabende, John Kamulegeya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesUsing machine learning models to predict infants at risk of defaulting routine immunization (RI) and identify significant features for Uganda.Materials and MethodsPrincipal component analysis reduced dimensionality. Datasets were balanced using synthetic minority over-sampling technique. k-Nearest Neighbors, Decision Trees, Random Forests (RFs), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Naïve-Bayes, Logistic Regression (LR), XGBoost, Adoptive-Boosting, and Gradient-Boosting were used on Uganda’s 2016 Demographic and health survey data with social-economic and demographic factors as predictors. Experiments with and without K-fold cross-validation were performed. Models were evaluated for accuracy, recall, precision, and area under a curve (AUC).Results and DiscussionExperimental results revealed that the rate of defaulting increases as an infant’s age increases at 5.3% Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG), 7.3% pentavalentI, 22.9% pentavalentIII, and 22.1% for measles. Significant predictors for BCG were immunization card, polio0, cluster altitude. Similarly, RF had the same precision, recall, AUC at 1.0. RF was the best classifier among the 9 models to predict defaulting RI. The study recommends regular outreaches, daily vaccination, provision of immunization cards, and accessible water sources to reduce defaulting.

Background

Despite the availability of free routine immunization (RI) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), many infants are zero-dose, not completely vaccinated, vaccinated late for age, or drop out from the course of the immunization schedule.1 RI coverage for all recommended Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) vaccines has remained poor in some African countries, like Ethiopia 43%, Ghana 57%, Nigeria 31%, and Uganda 55%2; hence sporadic outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs).3 VPDs account for 29% of deaths among children aged 1-59 months and they are the most common cause of childhood mortality, with an estimated 3 million deaths every year, mainly in Africa and Asia.2

Diseases have a big impact on human life quality, which is why the quest to find strategies to avoid diseases is a priority for researchers using available resources.4 Due to limited resources, governments, development partners, and middle managers are interested in optimally utilizing what is available to effectively control VPDs. This requires timely identification of infants at risk of defaulting and dropping out of schedule. Artificial Intelligence (AI)—based solutions can be used to make predictions, that can in turn be used to guide effective interventions towards improved immunization coverage (IC).

AI-based solutions have enabled early detection of cancer, heart failure, pandemic resource distribution, among others.4 Without the technology to model/visualize risks in large datasets; vaccinators and policymakers are unable to identify target groups/individuals likely to drop out. For this reason, default rates remain high. Predictive analytics algorithms use AI, statistical modelling, machine learning, and multidimensional data mining techniques to accurately identify areas where children are most likely to delay using certain factors as predictors.1 This study experimented the use machine learning models to predict infants at a risk of defaulting RI in Uganda.

Routine immunization

RI is among the most successful interventions, substantially contributing to progress in child survival.5 It ensures that infants receive recommended vaccination on time, at the right age, following the recommended schedule by age, gender, and with ease of access to the point of vaccination.6 If IC is improved worldwide, an additional 1.5 million deaths could be avoided.7

The vaccination achievements so far have been described as “fragile” given the outbreaks of some of these infectious diseases in LMICs8 including Uganda. These outbreaks reflect the existence of communities with partially vaccinated or zero-dose infants whose herd immunity is not high enough to stall the transmission of diseases. Uganda experienced sporadic measles and rubella outbreaks in 2018 in 76 and 33 districts, respectively3; leading to mass immunization intervention for all regions (Figure 1), which may not be financially sustainable. VPDs remains a major cause of child mortality and morbidity in LMICs.5 Infant mortality is the probability of a child dying between birth and exactly 1 year of age expressed per 1000 live births, which is currently 43 deaths per 1000 live births in Uganda.9 Frequent reasons for low vaccination include poor planning, lack of required resources, parental rejection (hesitance), or vaccination taking place when parents were not present such as during a market day or working hours, among others.10

Driven largely by the introduction of new vaccines and the global polio eradication initiative, development assistance for vaccination reached US$ 3.6 billion in 2014. Nonetheless, gaps in coverage persist for both new and established vaccines.5 To maximize the effect of these investments, a locally focused understanding of vaccine coverage factors and patterns is crucial.5

IC as a basis for remedial interventions

IC is a key immunization programme performance indicator that shows how far a country is from VPD outbreaks.11,12 It is a health output with the outcome of a reduction in disease incidence.11 IC percentage is calculated as the percentage of persons in the target age group who received a particular vaccine dose by a specified age and is globally annually tracked.12Figure 1 represents percentage of children 12-23 months that received all basic vaccines. The sample was for living children age 12-13 months weighted to enable easy comparability across regions.13

National indicators like the third dose of diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis3 (DTP3) coverage are used to measure the strength and reach; BCG and DPT1 measure accessibility while the difference between DTP3 and DTP1 indicates the dropout rate of RI.14 DPT-containing vaccine protects against diphtheria, pertussis/whooping cough, Hepatitis B (HepB), and Haemophilus influenza Type b (Hib); it is known as DPT-HepBHib or the pentavalent vaccine.13 BCG protects against tuberculosis.13

Factors that influence/predict RI defaulting

A number of factors have been stated to influence immunization uptake. According to13,15,17 they are categorized into demographic, child, mother/household, community, and social economic. Demographic factors include; Gender, Location/place of residence, Race, Age, Marital status, Family size, Nationality, Ethnicity, Linguistic background, Religion, Ownership/belongings. Child factors comprise of: Age, Place of birth, Gender, Rank of child/birth order, Immunization card. Mother/household entail: Antenatal care, Education, Knowledge about RI, Exposure to child health information through the media/community, VPD awareness, Partner support, Assistance with taking the child for immunization, Socio-economic status, Father’s approval, Paternal education, Number of children. Community factors consist of Individuals/groups educate the community about immunization, Condition of access, Regularity of vaccine supply to health facilities, Place of residence. Lastly, Social-economic factors include: Income, Level of education, Occupation maternal/paternal, Wealth, Social support, Safety, Birth rates, Commitment.15

Machine learning-based vaccine prediction models

The field of machine learning (ML) is concerned with the construction of computer programs that automatically improve with experience. A computer program is said to learn from experience E with respect to some class of tasks T and performance measure P, if its performance at tasks in T, as measured by P, improves with experience E.16 Predictive analytics uses statistical and machine learning techniques to analyse data and make predictions about future events/behaviours.16 The most common form of ML is supervised learning. The task T is to learn a mapping f from predictors x∈X to outputs y∈Y. In classification problems, the output space is a set of C mutually exclusive classes.16 In the context of immunization, predictive analytics can be applied to predict infants likely to default RI schedules. The predictions can then be used to target interventions to improve IC and prevent VPD outbreaks. Predictive analytics is a valuable tool for healthcare providers, public health officials, and policymakers to make data-driven decisions and improve health outcomes. Methods and materials

Study methodology.

Dataset source and characteristics (original dataset)

The study used demographic and health survey (DHS) dataset 2016, 256-country code and phase UG7 for 14 regions (Figure 1) that was collected nationally from June to December 2016. The DHS dataset, was a retrospective cohort study of infants born between 2013 and 2016. The primary outcome was reception of BCG, pentavalentI, pentavalentIII, and measles vaccines. The occurrence of the outcome of interest for this study was determined by transcribing details from immunization cards, other immunization documents, or recording parents/guardian’s response for recall of vaccination event that was done in a 2016 survey. Only infants eligible for reception of a particular vaccine were included as explained in the “Data Preparation” section.

Data preparation

Data cleaning

The month of the interview was used to determine if a child is ready to receive a particular vaccine given the age by month. This enabled the identification of infants eligible for reception of a given vaccine and the elimination of others that were not of age.

Measles

The total records in the dataset after cleaning was 6793.

PentavalentI

Pentavalent vaccine includes Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus, Hepatitis B, and Hib. All vaccines given at birth like BCG, polio0, DPT1, and Heb were included as predictors. The earliest time of the interviews was June 2016. Original records were 8929 born 2013-2016. 436 records for children who had not made 5 months by the time of the interviews were excluded.

BCG

The dataset for BCG had 8932 infants born between 2013 and 2016. No record was dropped since BCG is a dose at birth, all infants are eligible.

PentavalentIII

Original dataset had 8929 records. 7976 infants were eligible.

Feature selection

Dataset features used to train a machine learning model have a huge influence on the performance anticipated to be achieved. Partially relevant or irrelevant features can negatively impact model performance since they increase noise.24 The researchers selected 133 features (Appendix S1) based on the factors that influence RI uptake according to literature and experts in immunization field. The researchers used principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce dimensionality of the original dataset from 133 (Appendix S1) to 20 features in order to reduce computational cost and reduce multicollinearity.

Features

The dataset used has 17 features that were selected to significantly influence the choice of receiving vaccine. Features with high correlation were eliminated (Appendix S2). This study explored the use of machine learning models to predict the likelihood of defaulting BCG, pentavalent1, pentavalentIII, measles, and later identified significant features. Data discretization

Data discretization technique was used to transform numerical data into categories for infants who had received the vaccine and those who had not. This technique reduced complexity by grouping data points into intervals based on predefined constraints making it easier to predict. The code 0 and 8 which represented “no” and “I do not know” in the dataset were assigned zero (0) to mean defaulted. While code 1, 2, 3 which meant “vaccination date on card,” “reported by mother,” and “vaccination marked on card,” respectively were assigned one (1) to indicate immunized.

Data distribution

Data distribution checking before prediction was done to avoid imbalanced classes where some classes may have significantly fewer instances.25 This imbalance may introduce bias or affect model performance. The distribution for the vaccines in the cleaned datasets is illustrated in Figure 3. The rate of defaulting increased with increase in age at 5.3%, 7.3%, 22.9%, and 22.1% for BCG, pentavalentI, pentavalentIII, and measles, respectively.

Dataset shape and distribution.

Data balancing

Given the fact that the original dataset had severe to moderate class imbalances, synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) was used to balance the dataset to 50:50 of defaulters: non-defaulters to improve performance of the prediction model.

Model training

Prediction experiments were done before, and after dataset balancing, with and without cross-validation. We used k-fold cross-validation (CV) that is, k = 10-fold, since it offers better performance and less biased estimates.26 A fold is used in each iteration once as testing data, while the remaining folds are used as training data. The process is repetitive until all datasets are evaluated. K-fold CV results are re-iterated with the mean score of the values of the model.26

Classification methods

We used both parametric and non-parametric classification algorithms to lead to better overall performance and achieve different perspectives on the data. Nonparametric classification methods included k-NN, Decision Trees, RT, Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Naïve Bayes. Parametric methods included Logistic Regression (LR) and ensemble methods had XGBoost, AdaBoost, and Gradient Boosting. The ensemble algorithms combine multiple weak learners (decision trees) sequentially to produce a subsequent model trained to correct mistakes of the previous model. Nine classification algorithms were used and evaluated.

Model evaluation

Evaluation enables ascertaining the level of excellence achieved by a proposed method.27 It involved assessing model performance and effectiveness in making accurate predictions for infants’ defaulting. We used several metrics including; (1). Accuracy: measured the proportion of correctly classified instances over total number. Accuracy alone may not be sufficient since the dataset is imbalanced. (2). Recall: measured the proportion of true positive (TP) predictions over the total number of actual positive instances. Recall is useful when the cost of false negatives is high. (3). Precision: measures the proportion of true positive predictions over the total number of positive predictions. It focuses on the quality of positive predictions and is useful when the cost of false positives is high. (4). Area Under Curve-Receiver Operating Curve (AUC-ROC): ROC curve visualizes the trade-off between the true positive rate (TPR) and the false positive rate (FPR) at various classification thresholds. AUC-ROC illustrates the overall performance of the classifier across all possible thresholds and is particularly useful when the data is imbalanced. (1)Accuracy=(TP+TN)/(TP+FP+FN+TN),(2)Recall=TP/(TP+FN),(3)Precision=TP/(TP+FP),where TP is true positive, FP is false positive, TN is true negative, FN is false negative.

Results

Model performance

Random Forest (RF) displayed the best performance in the second experiment with k = 10 for all vaccines with 96%, 95%, 0.94%, and 84% accuracy, perfect (1.0) precision, recall, ROC AUC for BCG, PentavalentI, pentavalentIII, and measles, respectively. On the other hand, XGBoost, SVM, LR displayed the worst discriminatory power between infants who received from defaulters with ROC AUC ≤0.57.

Discussion

The study revealed RF as the best classifier for vaccine defaulters among the 9 models for both experiments of k = 10 and 80:20. The experimented results revealed a slight reduction in accuracy for some algorithms like DT, gradient boost at an average of ≤0.02. On the other hand, it was noted that cross-validation significantly improved model performance for almost all metrics for example for pentavalentI LR improved accuracy from 0.36 to 0.93; XGBoost 0.11 to 0.83; measles XGBoost 0.48 to 0.64. Similarly, RF improved precision, recall, and AUC to 1.0 for all vaccines. For this reason, the study adopted the second experiment results with k = 10.

The second experiment indicated that RF best-predicted vaccine defaulting compared to previous models in literature at 96%, 95%, 94%, and 84% accuracy, perfect precision, recall, ROC AUC for BCG, PentavalentI, pentavalentIII, and measles, respectively. This research is the first in Uganda to use demographics, socioeconomic, community, health facility, child, paternal, and maternal dimensions to predict vaccine defaulting at national level. In addition, the study revealed RF (Table 4) as the best model closely followed by Gradient boost.

Feature ranking

The 5 most significant predictors for defaulting BCG were immunization card, polio0, cluster attitude, date of birth-and date of first birth. Reception of pneumococcalI, BCG, district, current age of child, and health card for pentavalentI. Polio3, pentavalent3, pneumococcal3, polio2, and altitude for pentavalentIII. Measles predictors were polio active, pentavalentIII, pneumococcal3, polio3, and vitamin A.

Just like20,24 indicated, reception of preceding vaccines, wealth index, district, were among the highest-ranking features for the 4 vaccines. It should be noted that infants from low-income families may have difficulties in affording transport for scheduled immunization appointments while wealthier ones may have greater abilities to prioritize health services. In addition, geographical location of district (urban/rural) may strongly influence uptake where people living in rural or remote areas often face challenges like long distances to health facilities, stock outs or few facilities. In a similar way, reception of preceding vaccines may mean acceptance for all vaccines on schedule.

The study also revealed that, unlike BCG, pentavalentI, pentavalentIII, immunization card did not influence defaulting of measles as stated by earlier scholars.20,24 This interesting phenomenon could be explained by the long duration since the infant last received vaccination (pentavalentIII at 14 weeks as opposed to measles at 9 months).

It also revealed, age of respondent at first birth was influential for pentavalent1 and measles; Husband/partner’s age was significant for measles and BCG while Husband/partner’s occupation was for BCG and pentavalent1. There were some unique features for each vaccine like years lived in place-of-residence, number of antenatal visits during pregnancy for BCG; Place of delivery and time to get to water source pentavalentI; Ethnicity, Native language of respondent; Number of children under 5 in household for pentavalentIII and Education in single years for measles.

Conclusion

This study aimed at using machine learning models to predict infants at risk of defaulting BCG, pentavalentI, pentavalentIII, measles, and identify significant features for Uganda. From the study, the experimental results revealed that the rate of defaulting increases as age increases at 5.3%, 7.3%, 22.9%, 22.1% for BCG, pentavalentI, pentavalentIII, and measles. The most significant predictors for BCG were Immunization card, polio0, cluster altitude, date of birth-CMC, and date of first birth-CMC. Reception of pneumococcal1 and BCG for pentavalent1; polio3, pentavalentII for pentavalentIII; polio active and pentavalentIII for measles. It was noted that previous vaccines were significant for pentavalentI, pentavalentIII, and measles. Cluster altitude is influential for BCG and PentavalentIII.

It was observed that reception of preceding vaccines was a significant predictor for immunization defaulting. This would mean timely addressing of factors that are significant for reception of day zero vaccines like BCG. In this regard, the study recommends that the government of Uganda, Ministry of Health (MoH), development partners and middle level managers find ways of providing immunization cards and easily accessible water sources. In the same way, health facilities should conduct regular outreaches in their catchment area especially in villages that may be far from a health facility or hard to reach. Similarly, given that date of birth is a significant predictor, middle level managers with the help on MoH should ensure daily vaccination at health facilities to avoid missed opportunities.

In regard to comparison of classification models; RF with k-10 had the best performance at predicting RI vaccine defaulting when compared to the rest of the models with an accuracy of 96%, 95%, 94%, and 84% for BCG, PentavalentI, pentavalentIII, and measles, respectively. The study also noted that RF had the same precision, recall, AUC of 1.0. Gradient boosting was the second-best performing model. However, XGBoost, SVM, LR displayed the worst discriminatory power among infants who received the vaccine from defaulters with an average AUC≤0.57.

Notwithstanding the positivity and unique outcome observed in the results, the study had some limitations; datasets were unbalanced for all vaccines, although they were handled by SMOTE. Similarly, the dataset used was for 2016 because it is the most recent DHS dataset available for Uganda, hence the results were customized to RI in Uganda.

None the less, this research may help Uganda’s Ministry of Health, middle level managers, and development partners in tailoring interventions to deter zero-dose, defaulting and dropout.

Developing an ensemble model to improve the accuracy of prediction for measles defaulting will be the next phase of this research.
