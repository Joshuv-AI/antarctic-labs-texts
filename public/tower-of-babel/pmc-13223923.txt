U7snRNA-mediated skipping of intron-derived pseudoexons restores full-length DMD expression in patient-derived cell lines
Authors: Joseph C. Beljan, Stefan Nicolau, Hui Lin, Jacob W. Lay, Emma C. Frair, Liubov V. Gushchina, Robert B. Weiss, Kevin M. Flanigan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
X-linked Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is caused by pathogenic variants of the DMD gene that result in absent or significantly altered dystrophin expression. Deep intronic mutations within the DMD locus account for 1%–7% of all DMD patients. These are typically point mutations that create splice acceptor (SA) or splice donor (SD) sites, resulting in the inclusion of an intronic fragment as a pseudoexon within the DMD mRNA. Because such patients possess all of the canonical DMD coding exons, efficient exclusion of the pseudoexon from the mature mRNA restores a full-length transcript. We induced pseudoexon skipping in patient-derived cells by employing a modified U7 small nuclear RNA (U7snRNA) that targets an SA, SD, or exonic splice enhancer (ESE) sequence of the frame-shifting pseudoexons. The U7snRNA vector repurposes an endogenous U7 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein to induce exon skipping at the pre-mRNA processing step of mRNA maturation. U7snRNA treatment resulted in dose-dependent pseudoexon skipping. In all four cell lines treated, we achieved skipping efficiencies over 90%, at times resulting in over 100% of wild-type dystrophin protein levels. This approach addresses an orphan class of DMD mutations and demonstrates the potential of bespoke gene therapies to restore full-length dystrophin in individual patients.

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a devastating genetic disease affecting 1 in 5,000 male births. DMD, and the milder neuromuscular disease Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD), are both caused by pathogenic variants in the DMD gene, located across a 2.4-Mb locus on the X chromosome.1 A full-length 14-kb DMD mRNA transcript encodes for one of three 427-kDa dystrophin protein isoforms. The muscle-specific isoform of dystrophin, Dp427m, is essential for striated muscle cell sarcolemma integrity.2 Without dystrophin, skeletal and cardiac muscle tissues undergo chronic myofiber or myocyte degeneration, eventually leading to fibrotic replacement of contractile tissue and muscle wasting. During childhood, DMD patients slowly lose ambulation and generally do not live past their third decade of life.3

Current treatments for DMD are directed toward creating internally deleted but functional dystrophin isoforms by restoring the reading frame. In the context of DMD, restoring an in-frame dystrophin transcript can convert a severe DMD phenotype into the milder BMD phenotype. Exon skipping using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) is currently in the clinic to restore an open reading frame in patients with specific exon deletions or duplications.4,5 The FDA-approved ASOs—eteplirsen (exon 51), golodirsen (exon 53), viltolarsen (exon 53), and casimersen (exon 45)—all work by targeting an existing exon at the pre-mRNA level for exclusion during mRNA splicing. While these treatments have demonstrated mild therapeutic benefits, the internally deleted dystrophins produced by exon skipping are sub-optimal in their efficacy and the ASOs require repeated dosing every week.6 The adeno-associated virus (AAV)-delivered microdystrophin gene therapy delandistrogene moxeparvovec (Elevidys) delivers an internally deleted dystrophin isoform. It has received FDA approval for treatment in ambulatory DMD patients,7 despite initially failing to meet pre-defined functional outcomes in a phase 3 trial, highlighting the need for continued pursuit of additional therapeutic options for DMD patients.8,9

As an alternative, vectorized exon skipping (VES) with U7snRNA is an approach that uses a modified small nuclear RNA (U7snRNA) delivered by AAV. The endogenous U7 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein (U7snRNP) is involved in processing the 3′ end of histone pre-mRNA.10,11,12 Delivery of exogenous U7snRNA, engineered to induce exon skipping at the pre-mRNA processing step of mRNA maturation, has been studied for over 2 decades to restore the DMD reading frame and produce internally deleted dystrophin isoforms in DMD model systems.13,14 Our group has developed a U7snRNA backbone modified by the addition of a target-specific 20- to 30-nucleotide (nt) antisense sequence targeting exons of interest. The binding of U7snRNP to its target pre-mRNA sterically hinders the spliceosome from recognizing the splice site, thereby excluding the exon during splicing.15

Here, we apply VES to DMD and BMD patient cell lines that contain intronic point mutations that result in the creation of pseudoexons in DMD mRNA transcripts. Across its 2.4-Mb locus, DMD has 79 exons, ranging in size from 31 to 242 bp, and 78 introns, which range in size from 107 to 248,401 bp, 11 of which are greater than 50 kb. Due to their extraordinarily large size, DMD introns are particularly susceptible targets for genomic mutations. Deleterious intronic point mutations can generate new splice acceptor (SA) or splice donor (SD) motifs or activate exonic splice enhancer (ESE) motifs.16 These splice sites are recognized by the spliceosome as SA and SD sequences during pre-mRNA processing and result in the creation of a pseudoexon, where an intronic sequence is spliced into the mature mRNA transcript. Therefore, identifying the underlying genetic mutation in these DMD or BMD patients generally requires analysis of their mRNA.17 Typically, the analysis shows inclusion of an intronic fragment as a pseudoexon, with phenotypic severity (DMD vs. BMD) correlating with both whether the pseudoexon maintains an open reading frame and the efficiency of pseudoexon inclusion in the mature transcript. Because characterization of mRNA is not typically performed in all patients whose mutation cannot be identified by common clinical testing of exon copy number and sequence, these mutations are likely underascertained, and this mutational mechanism may account for as many as 7% of cases.18 Since such patients still possess all the canonical DMD coding exons, efficient exclusion of the pseudoexon from the mature mRNA restores a full-length wild-type (WT) transcript.

In this study, we focus on four patient-derived pseudoexons that represent three mechanistic classes of intronic mutations (classes 1a, 1b, and 1c).18 To induce skipping of pseudoexons in DMD and BMD patient-derived cells, we have developed modified U7snRNAs that target SA, ESE, or SD sequences within these pseudoexons. We then tested whether U7snRNA-mediated pseudoexon skipping can restore correctly spliced DMD transcripts and full-length dystrophin protein in patient-derived MyoD-transformed cells. Treatment resulted in dose-dependent pseudoexon skipping and expression of full-length dystrophin across all four cell lines tested.

Cell lines were converted to FibroMyoD (FM) cells as previously described.19 Each cell line (non-clonal) batch was then transdifferentiated from fibroblasts to myoblasts in myoblast medium for 3 days, followed by treatment with self-complimentary AAV1-encapsidated U7snRNA constructs and switched to muscle cell differentiation medium. After 14 days of differentiation into myotubes the cells were collected, and RNA and protein were extracted for analysis (experimental timeline Figure S1). Both RNA and protein restoration levels were compared to healthy control cell lines (n = 3). Across all three classes of mutation, dose-dependent increases in correctly spliced DMD transcripts were observed for each cell line, although the magnitude of rescue varied between pseudoexons and U7snRNA targets (Figures 1E–1I).

Effect of AAV1-U7snRNA on mRNA splicing correction

In PE18 cells, the c.2292 + 1024 G>T mutation creates a canonical GT SD site at the 3′ end of the pseudoexon, resulting in the inclusion of a frame-shifting 161-nt fragment of intron 18 into the mRNA (Figure 1A). In this line, all three U7snRNA designs reduced inclusion of pseudoexon 18, with the ESE- and SD-targeting constructs producing the greatest restoration of WT transcript (Figures 1E and 1F). The cell line PE38 had a unique response to U7snRNA-induced pseudoexon skipping, likely due to the location of the pseudoexon. The c.5448 + 67 A>G mutation creates a canonical GT SD site within intron 38 that appends a 66-nt intronic fragment containing an in-frame stop codon to exon 38. Therefore, we did not test a U7snRNA targeting the SA of pseudoexon 38, as that would also ablate the native exon 38 SD site (Figure 1B). When targeting the predicted ESE1 or ESE2 of PE38, “overskipping,” or skipping of the adjacent upstream exon 38, was observed via reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) (Figure 1G). Remarkably, even at the lower 1E4 vector genomes (vg)/cell dose of U7snRNA targeting ESE2, the only transcripts detected were either full-length WT DMD or an internally deleted transcript containing an in-frame deletion of exon 38 (del38).

Native pseudoexon skipping seen in the untreated PE22 cell line was ∼50%, concordant with the BMD phenotype of the patient carrying this mutation. The c.2949 + 909 C>T mutation in PE22 cells activates an ESE located at the +21 position relative to the 259-nt frame-shifting pseudoexon (Figure 1C). In PE22 cells, the U7snRNA construct targeting an ESE resulted in appreciable pseudoexon skipping with increasing doses, while the SA-targeting U7snRNA reached 100% WT transcript restoration at the 1E5 vg/cell concentration (Figure 1H). Similar to the PE22 cell line, PE61 cells also originated from a BMD patient, although the ∼70% native pseudoexon skipping detected via RT-PCR was higher than WT transcripts detected in the patient. The c.9163 + 2510 G>A mutation at the −2 position relative to the pseudoexon SA causes activation of an SA and 3′ UTR, truncating the final transcript (Figure 1D). Of the three U7snRNA constructs tested in the PE61 cell line, only the SA target resulted in a dose-dependent skipping of PE61 (Figure 1I). While not as absolutely quantitative as quantitative RT-PCR or droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), overall, the RT-PCR data clearly demonstrate that U7snRNA constructs can efficiently alter pre-mRNA splicing to exclude pseudoexons, but that the degree of WT transcript restoration is influenced by both the specific pseudoexon and splicing element targeted.

Increased dystrophin protein expression after AAV1-U7snRNA treatment

Dystrophin protein levels were examined via JESS western capillary immunoassay (Figure 2), in some instances producing results that diverged from the mRNA splicing correction data. In U7snRNA-treated PE18 cells, despite robust pseudoexon 18 exclusion at the RNA level, WT dystrophin protein levels only reached 10% of that produced by the WT cell lines, even at the highest vector doses (Figure 2A). Alternatively, PE38 cells treated with U7snRNA revealed a particularly striking outcome. Both ESE1- and ESE2-targeting constructs resulted in >100% WT dystrophin restoration. Treating PE22 cells with U7snRNA resulted in a proportional relationship between WT mRNA transcript and protein restoration. The robust RNA correction observed at higher AAV dosages in PE22 cells corresponded to a substantial rise in protein production, with SA-targeting U7snRNA constructs restoring up to ∼70% of WT dystrophin. Increased WT protein was observed using all three U7snRNA constructs targeting PE22; however, protein restoration was not always in a dose-dependent manner, as the highest WT protein level was observed at the 1E4 vg/cell of the SA target (Figure 2C). Last, the PE61 cells exhibited partial RNA correction across multiple U7snRNA constructs but only minimal protein recovery (Figure 2D), similar to the modest WT protein produced by the PE18 cell line upon U7snRNA treatment.

Discussion

The approval of exon-skipping ASOs for the treatment of DMD represented an important milestone as they were the first clinically available drugs directed toward dystrophin protein restoration therapy. However, a major drawback to ASO treatment is the need for repeated dosing (weekly, with currently approved drugs).6,20,21 Additionally, all four ASOs currently approved for DMD have shown, at best, moderate efficacy after several years of treatment.6,20,21 In contrast, VES may reasonably be expected to provide long-term exon skipping efficacy, as AAV delivery of a therapeutic transgene has shown durability 10 years post treatment in hemophilia B patients.22,23 Successful long-term expression of U7snRNA delivered via AAV in a single treatment dose may be expected to provide a patient with exon or pseudoexon skipping for years, as we demonstrated long-term durability of robust exon skipping in 18-months-old mice treated with U7snRNA at 3 months of age.24 Toxicity and biodistribution studies in nonhuman primates have shown an acceptable safety profile for AAV-encapsidated U7snRNA constructs,25,26 which supported translation toward first-in-human testing for DMD exon 2 duplications to restore the full-length WT transcript and dystrophin protein expression (ClinicalTrials.gov: NTC04240314).

Pseudoexon mutations are typically private mutations, affecting a single patient or family. Developing a therapy for any one of these patients would, therefore, be a bespoke gene therapy and not likely to be commercially viable. Nevertheless, successful restoration of full-length WT dystrophin protein would confer a great advantage compared to expression of the internally deleted dystrophins currently in the clinic, as such internally deleted ASO proteins or microdystrophin gene therapies will at best convert DMD patients to a less-severe BMD phenotype.

Across all four patient-derived pseudoexon cell lines, the primary pharmacologic readout was the extent of pseudoexon exclusion at the mRNA level following U7snRNA treatment. RT-PCR analyses demonstrated dose-dependent pseudoexon skipping in each cell line, with total skipping efficiencies differing by U7snRNA target and cell line (Table 1). Notably, in the PE38 cell line, complete exclusion of pseudoexon 38 was achieved at the relatively low treatment dose of 1E4 vg/cell, indicating high splicing sensitivity to U7snRNA-mediated transcript correction. In others, near-complete correction of aberrant splicing was observed only at higher doses. For example, the PE18 cell line showed greater than 90% pseudoexon exclusion at the highest U7snRNA dose, 1E6 vg/cell. The percent pseudoexon exclusion is the most direct measure of U7snRNA-mediated efficacy in this in vitro model; in this assay, it is derived from an endpoint RT-PCR that amplifies across the pseudoexon-flanking exons and reflects the relative ratio of pseudoexon-included and pseudoexon-excluded amplicons, rather than the absolute abundance of corrected full-length DMD transcript per cell. Dystrophin protein restoration served as a secondary, confirmatory readout of functional transcript correction. Although WT dystrophin was detected in all four cell lines following treatment with at least one U7snRNA target, the magnitude of protein restoration did not scale linearly with RNA correction, consistent with limitations of the FM cell model and the different denominators of the RNA and protein assays. Since protein measurements are normalized to total protein in crude lysates, the apparent magnitude of dystrophin restoration is also influenced by variability in transdifferentiation efficiency, myotube maturity, and induction of muscle-specific proteins across FM lines and across conditions. Because the hTERT and MyoD expression cassettes integrate randomly into the genome during lentiviral transduction, it is possible that cellular processes important for robust muscle-specific protein expression are irreversibly affected. This is illustrated by PE18, where substantial RNA transcript restoration failed to produce proportional WT dystrophin expression, and by PE38, where the U7snRNA constructs targeting either ESE1 or ESE2 of PE38 induced overskipping of exon 38 such that upward of 50% of the protein corresponds to an internally deleted del38 dystrophin isoform rather than full-length WT protein (Figure 1G).

Although protein restoration levels do not always match the corresponding RNA correction, all four cell lines had dose-dependent increases in RNA and WT dystrophin protein resulting from treatment with at least one U7snRNA target. The variability in protein expression we observed may reflect a variety of causes, but we note that lines from the BMD patients (PE22 and PE61) already showed a significant amount of native pseudoexon skipping. In these patients, treatment might not translate into appreciable increases in functional outcomes. Furthermore, we cannot directly correlate the in vitro dose levels with AAV1 to what might be clinically achievable using AAV9 or another myotropic capsid in skeletal muscle.

While we anticipate that AAV9 or another myotropic capsid would be used for systemic delivery in the clinic, we used AAV1 in our cell model system as this serotype has previously been shown to have a greater transduction efficiency treating immortalized in vitro cells.27 Translational development will require further studies, but this proof-of-concept study confirms that the pseudoexon class of DMD mutations is amenable to U7snRNA treatment and demonstrates the potential of such individualized gene therapies to restore full-length dystrophin expression, which may be significant and durable. We note that pseudoexon skipping is, in principle, also feasible using second-generation exon skipping approaches that are more efficient than current FDA-approved ASO therapies.28 In addition, recent FDA draft guidance suggests a pathway toward accelerating the development of such individualized therapies, with use of patient-derived cells as pre-clinical models, providing a potential pathway to develop bespoke therapies for the translational development of these and similar unique mutations.
