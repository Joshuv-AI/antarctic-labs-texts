On manifolds with almost non-negative Ricci curvature and integrally-positive k t ​ h k^{th} -scalar curvature
Alessandro Cucinotta, Andrea Mondino

Abstract
We consider manifolds with almost non-negative Ricci curvature and strictly positive integral lower bounds on the sum of the lowest k k eigenvalues of the Ricci tensor. If ( M n , g ) (M^{n},g) is a Riemannian manifold satisfying such curvature bounds for k = 2 k=2 , then we show that M M is contained in a neighbourhood of controlled width of an isometrically embedded 1 1 -dimensional sub-manifold. From this, we deduce several metric and topological consequences: M M has at most linear volume growth and at most two ends, it has bounded 1-Urysohn width, the first Betti number of M M is bounded above by 1 1 , and there is precise information on elements of infinite order in π 1 ​ ( M ) \pi_{1}(M) . If ( M n , g ) (M^{n},g) is a Riemannian manifold satisfying such bounds for k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , then we show that M M has at most ( k − 1 ) (k-1) -dimensional behavior at large scales. If k = n = dim ⁡ ( M ) k=n={\rm dim}(M) , so that the integral lower bound is on the scalar curvature, assuming in addition that the ( n − 2 ) (n-2) -Ricci curvature is non-negative, we prove that the dimension drop at large scales improves to n − 2 n-2 . From the above results we deduce topological restrictions, such as upper bounds on the first Betti number.

Introduction

A renowned conjecture by Yau asserts that
if
(M^{n},g,p)
is a pointed Riemannian manifold of dimension
n
with
\mathsf{Ric}_{M}\geq-\lambda
, then

\int_{B_{1}(p)}\mathsf{R}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq C(\lambda,n),

where
\mathsf{R}
denotes the scalar curvature. Under the assumption of non-negative sectional curvature, the aforementioned bound on the scalar curvature was obtained by Petrunin in
[
48
]
. A non-collapsed version of Yau’s problem was then proposed by Naber in
[
45
]
, where he conjectured that if
(M^{n},g,p)
is a pointed Riemannian manifold with
\mathsf{Ric}_{M}\geq-\lambda
and
\mathsf{Vol}(B_{1}(p))\geq v
, then
\fint_{B_{1}(p)}\mathsf{R}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq C(\lambda,n,v)
.
If true, Naber’s version of Yau conjecture would allow to define a scalar curvature measure on non-collapsed Ricci limit spaces (see again
[
45
]
).

These conjectures are still open and, more generally, the geometric interplay between Ricci and scalar curvature bounds is still not fully understood.
A step forward in this direction was carried out in a series of recent papers
showing that
3
-manifolds with non-negative Ricci curvature and scalar curvature bounded from below by a strictly positive constant have at most linear volume growth at infinity (see
[
44
,
17
]
).
This is a particular case of a more general conjecture by Gromov
[
31
]
stating that a non-compact pointed Riemannian manifold
(M^{n},g,p)
of dimension
n
with non-negative Ricci curvature and scalar curvature bounded below by a strictly positive constant should satisfy

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\mathsf{Vol}(B_{t}(p))}{t^{n-2}}<+\infty.

(1)

Without the condition on the Ricci curvature, manifolds with uniformly positive scalar curvature can have arbitrarily large volume growth (by the surgery results in
[
29
,
28
]

Nevertheless such
3
-manifolds have been shown to have bounded Urysohn

,

and, assuming also non-negative Ricci curvature,

[

(n-2)
-width.

Motivated and inspired by the above conjectures and results, we next discuss the framework and the main findings of the present work.

We start by recalling some basic notation and terminology about metric spaces. Throughout the section,
(X,\mathsf{d})
denotes a proper, separable, length metric space.
A metric measure spaces
(X,\mathsf{d},\mathfrak{m})
is a triple, where

is a metric space and

is a non-negative Borel measure on

which is finite on bounded sets and whose support is the whole

. We denote by

on

.
Given a smooth manifold

we denote by

and

respectively the Riemannian distance and volume measure induced by

Given an open set

and

We say that two pointed metric measure spaces

3
Integral estimates on scalar curvature and first geometric applications

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Throughtout the section,
(M^{n},g)
will be a smooth, complete (in some cases compact),
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold, with
n\geq 2
. We denote by
\mathsf{R}_{k}:M\to\mathbb{R}
the sum of the lowest
k
eigenvalues, counted with their multiplicity, of the Ricci tensor. We first prove a few lemmas that will be used to establish Theorem
1.1
from the Introduction.

Given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
, we denote by
A^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
its transpose.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\leq n
, and let
c,\epsilon>0
be fixed. There exist
c_{1}(n,\epsilon),\epsilon^{\prime}(n,\epsilon)>0
arbitrarily small satisfying the following. Let
A=(a_{j,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
be a matrix with
|A^{T}A-I_{k}|\leq c_{1}
and
|A|^{2}\leq c
. Consider real numbers
-\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
with
\lambda_{k}\geq\epsilon/2k
.
Then

0\vee\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}a_{j,i}^{2}\geq\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}(\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}).

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that the statement fails.
Hence, we find sequences
c_{h},\epsilon^{\prime}_{h}\downarrow 0
and

A_{h}=(a^{h}_{j,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
with
|A^{T}_{h}A_{h}-I_{k}|\leq c_{h}
,
|A_{h}|^{2}\leq c
and real numbers
-\epsilon^{\prime}_{h}\leq\lambda^{h}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda^{h}_{n}
with
\lambda_{k}\geq\epsilon/2k
, with

0\vee\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda^{h}_{j}(a^{h}_{j,i})^{2}<\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}_{h}}(\lambda^{h}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda^{h}_{k}),\quad\text{for all }h\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular, dividing both sides by
\lambda_{k}^{h}
, it holds

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{\lambda^{h}_{j}}{\lambda^{h}_{k}}(a^{h}_{j,i})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=k}^{n}(a^{h}_{j,i})^{2}\leq k\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}_{h}},

(2)

where the first summand in the l.h.s. is to be considered zero if
k=1
.

Moreover, since
|A_{h}|^{2}\leq c
,
-\epsilon^{\prime}_{h}\leq\lambda^{h}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda^{h}_{n}
and
\lambda^{h}_{k}\geq\epsilon/2k
, it holds

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{\lambda^{h}_{j}}{\lambda^{h}_{k}}(a^{h}_{j,i})^{2}\geq-2k^{2}c\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}_{h}}{\epsilon}.

Combining with (
2
), we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=k}^{n}(a^{h}_{j,i})^{2}\leq k\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}_{h}}+2k^{2}c\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}_{h}}{\epsilon}.

Taking the limit as
h\to\infty
, we obtain a matrix
A=(a_{j,i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
such that

A^{T}A=I_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=k}^{n}(a_{j,i})^{2}=0.

The last two identities are clearly in contradiction: indeed, the former states that the columns of
A
are
k
orthonormal vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, while the latter implies that their span is at most
k-1
dimensional.
∎

The next lemma will be key to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, with
k\leq n
, and let
s\in(0,1)
be fixed.
For every
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta(n,\epsilon,s)>0
such that if
(M^{n},g)
has
\mathsf{Ric}_{M}\geq-\delta
on
B_{20}(x)
, and
B_{10}(x)\subset M
is

(k,\delta)
-symmetric, then:

•

There exists
E\subset B_{1}(x)
such that

\int_{E}\mathsf{R}_{k}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq\epsilon\mathsf{Vol}(B_{1}(x));

(3)

•

There exist
x_{j}\in B_{1}(x)
and
r_{j}\in[0,1/2]
, with
j\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

B_{1}(x)\setminus E\subset\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}B_{r_{j}}(x_{j})\text{ with }\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathsf{Vol}(B_{r_{j}}(x_{j}))r_{j}^{-2s}\leq\epsilon\mathsf{Vol}(B_{1}(x)).

(4)

In particular,
\mathsf{Vol}(B_{1}(x)\setminus E)\leq\epsilon\mathsf{Vol}(B_{1}(x))
and

\fint_{E}\mathsf{R}_{k}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
be fixed. Let
0<\epsilon^{\prime}(n,\epsilon)<\epsilon^{2}
be given by Lemma
3.1
and let
0<\delta<\epsilon^{\prime}
be small enough so that if
B_{10}(x)\subset M
is
(k,\delta)
-symmetric, there exists a harmonic
(k,\epsilon^{\prime})
-splitting map
u:B_{5}(x)\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Plugging the
j
-th entry
u_{j}
of the map
u
into the Bochner Formula yields

\Delta\frac{|\nabla u_{j}|^{2}-1}{2}=|\mathsf{Hess}(u_{j})|^{2}+\mathsf{Ric}(\nabla u_{j},\nabla u_{j}),\quad\text{on $B_{5}(x)$}.

(5)

Theorem
2.7
ensures the existence of a non-negative function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{2}(x))
, with
\phi\equiv 1
on
B_{1}(x)

Multiplying (

Since
\mathsf{Ric}_{M}\geq-\delta\geq-\epsilon^{\prime}
and
\sup_{B_{5}(x)}|\nabla u|\leq 2

\fint_{B_{1}(x)}\mathsf{Ric}(\nabla u_{j},\nabla u_{j})\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq c(n)\fint_{B_{2}(x)}\phi\mathsf{Ric}(\nabla u_{j},\nabla u_{j})\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}+c(n)\epsilon^{\prime}\leq c(n)\epsilon^{\prime}.

Summing over

, we get

\fint_{B_{1}(x)}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathsf{Ric}(\nabla u_{j},\nabla u_{j})\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq c(n)\epsilon^{\prime}.

(6)

We now define
E\subset B_{1}(x)
to be the set of points where
\{\nabla u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
are almost orthonormal. For each point
p\in M
, we fix an orthonormal basis so that

and we use the convention that
\nabla u\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
(i.e., each
\nabla u_{j}
is a column of
\nabla u

c_{1}(n,\epsilon)>0
be the constant given by Lemma

, and set

E:=\{y\in B_{1}(x):|\nabla u^{T}\nabla u-I_{k}|\leq c_{1}\}.

Observe that the condition in the previous definition is independent of the chosen orthonormal basis for
T_{p}M
. Fix now an orthonormal basis in
T_{p}M
such that
\mathsf{Ric}_{M}
in
T_{p}M
is represented by the diagonal matrix
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, whose eigenvalues are
-\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
.

Consider now the subsets of
E

E_{+}:=E\cap\{\mathsf{R}_{k}\geq\epsilon/2\}\quad\text{and}\quad E_{-}:=E\cap\{\mathsf{R}_{k}<\epsilon/2\}.

At every point of
E_{+}

3.1
, it holds

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\vee\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathsf{Ric}(\nabla u_{j},\nabla u_{j})=0\vee\sum_{j=1}^{k}\nabla u_{j}^{T}\Lambda\nabla u_{j}

\displaystyle=0\vee\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\nabla u_{j,i})^{2}\geq\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}(\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{k})=\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}\mathsf{R}_{k}.

Combining with (
6

\displaystyle\int_{E}

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{k}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}=\int_{E_{-}}\mathsf{R}_{k}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}+\int_{E_{+}}\mathsf{R}_{k}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}

\displaystyle\leq(\epsilon/2)\mathsf{Vol}(B_{1}(x))+\int_{E_{+}}\mathsf{R}_{k}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq(c(n)\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}+\epsilon/2)\mathsf{Vol}(B_{1}(x)).

(7)

Choosing
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
small enough, we obtain (
3

To conclude the proof, we need to show that
B_{1}(x)\setminus E
is contained in a union of sufficiently small balls. Let
\eta(\epsilon,n,s)>0
to be chosen later and consider the set
E^{\prime}\subset B_{1}(x)
given by

E^{\prime}:=\Big\{y\in B_{1}(x):r^{2s}\fint_{B_{r}(y)}|\mathsf{Hess}(u)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq\eta\,,\;\text{ for every }r\in(0,1/10)\Big\}.

We claim that

\begin{split}E^{\prime}&\subset E\quad\text{ and}\\

(8)

The thesis (
4
) will follow by combining the claim (
8
) with (
7
).

We first prove that
E^{\prime}\subset E
. It is sufficient to show that at every point of
E^{\prime}
the quantity
|\nabla u^{T}\nabla u-I_{k}|
is smaller than the constant
c_{1}(\epsilon,n)
appearing in the definition of
E

[
5
]
).
Set
\mathcal{E}:=\sum_{i,j}|\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla u_{j}-\delta_{ij}|

For every
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
y\in B_{1}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{2^{-j}}(y)}\mathcal{E}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}

\displaystyle=\Big|\fint_{B_{1}(y)}\mathcal{E}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}-\fint_{B_{1}(y)}\mathcal{E}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}+\fint_{B_{1/2}(y)}\mathcal{E}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}-...+\fint_{B_{2^{-j}}(y)}\mathcal{E}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq c(n)\epsilon^{\prime}+\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\Big|\fint_{B_{2^{-j+1}}(y)}\mathcal{E}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}-\fint_{B_{2^{-j}}(y)}\mathcal{E}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq c(n)\epsilon^{\prime}+c(n)\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-j}\fint_{B_{2^{-j}}(y)}|\mathsf{Hess}(u)|\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol},

where in the last inequality we used the Poincaré inequality.
Using Hölder inequality and the definition of

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{2^{-j}}(y)}\mathcal{E}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}

\displaystyle\leq c(n)\epsilon^{\prime}+c(n)\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-j(1-s)}\Big(2^{-2sj}\fint_{B_{2^{-j}}(y)}|\mathsf{Hess}(u)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\Big)^{1/2}

Choosing
\eta

From now until the end of the proof,
\eta>0

we set

to be the maximal radius such that

r_{y}^{2s}\fint_{B_{r_{y}}(y)}|\mathsf{Hess}(u)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\geq\eta.

Clearly,

\int_{B_{r_{y}}(y)}|\mathsf{Hess}(u)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\geq\mathsf{Vol}(B_{r_{y}}(y))\eta r_{y}^{-2s}.

By Vitali covering theorem, we can cover

with a countable collection of balls

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c(n)}{\eta}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{B_{r_{y_{j}}}(y_{j})}|\mathsf{Hess}(u)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c(n)}{\eta}\int_{B_{2}(x)}|\mathsf{Hess}(u)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq\frac{c(n)}{\eta}\mathsf{Vol}(B_{1}(x))\epsilon^{\prime}.

Observe that, by construction,
5r_{j}\in[0,1/2]
for every

. Since

is fixed, we can choose

to conclude the proof.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Inspecting the proof of Lemma

, one realizes that a priori

estimates on harmonic splitting maps for

would imply that

3.2
First geometric applications in almost non-negative Ricci and integrally-positive scalar curvature

We now obtain the first geometric applications of the results of the previous section. We introduce some notation first (cf.
[
50
]
).

Definition 3.6

(
k^{th}
Ricci curvature bounds)
.

Let
(M^{n},g)
be a manifold, let
1\leq k\leq n-1
, let
x\in M
, and let
c\in\mathbb{R}
. We denote by
R_{M}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
the Riemann curvature tensor of
M
. We say that
\mathsf{Ric}_{M,k}\geq c
(resp.
\leq c
) in
T_{x}M
if, for all
(k+1)
-dimensional subspaces
V\subset T_{x}M
, it holds

\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}R_{M}(e_{i},v,v,e_{i})\geq c\,\,(\text{resp.}\leq c)

for every
v\in V
and every orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k+1}\}
of
V
.

One can check that:

1.

\mathsf{Ric}_{M,k}\geq c
implies
\mathsf{R}_{k}\geq c
.

2.

\mathsf{Ric}_{M,k}\geq c
implies
\mathsf{Ric}_{M,k+1}\geq\frac{k}{k-1}c
.

3.

\mathsf{Ric}_{M,n-1}\geq c
if and only if
\mathsf{Ric}_{M}\geq c
.

4.

\mathsf{Ric}_{1,M}\geq c
if and only if
\mathsf{Sec}_{M}\geq c
.

Definition 3.7

(Integral
k^{th}
Ricci curvature bounds)
.

Let
(M^{n},g)
be a manifold, let
1\leq k\leq n-1
, let
A\subset M
be measurable, let
c\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function.
We say that

\int_{A}F(\mathsf{Ric}_{M,k})\,d\mathsf{Vol}\geq c

if there exists a measurable function
f:A\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

1.

\int_{A}f\,d\mathsf{Vol}\geq c.

2.

For all
x\in A
and
(k+1)
-dimensional subspaces
V\subset T_{x}M
, it holds

F\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}R_{M}(e_{i},v,v,e_{i})\Big)\geq f(x)

for every
v\in V
and every orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k+1}\}
of
V
.

We say that
\int_{A}F(\mathsf{Sec}_{M})\,d\mathsf{Vol}\geq c
if
\int_{A}F(\mathsf{Ric}_{1,M})\,d\mathsf{Vol}\geq c
.

The next theorem should be compared with
[
59
, Theorem 1.1]
, where similar conclusions were obtained under stronger assumptions: non-negative Ricci curvature (below, almost non-negative Ricci curvature) and scalar curvature greater than 2 (below, a local integral lower bound on the scalar curvature).
Whenever we consider a sequence of manifolds
(M_{j},g_{j})
, we write

We now establish some first results of “dimension drop” in general dimension. Building on top of Section

(M,g/r_{j},p)

from the Introduction.

(X,\mathsf{d},x_{\infty})

(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathsf{d}_{eu})

.

Assume that
\mathsf{Ric}_{n-2}\geq 0

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\fint_{B_{r}(p)}0\vee(r^{2-\alpha}\mathsf{R})\wedge L\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}=L.

(X,\mathsf{d},x_{\infty})

\lim_{i\to+\infty}\fint_{B^{i}_{1}(p_{i})}0\vee(r_{i}^{-\alpha}\mathsf{R}_{M_{i},k})\wedge L\,d\mathsf{Vol}_{i}=L.

(17)

Let
(X,\mathsf{d},x_{\infty})
be a pGH limit of a (non-relabeled) subsequence of
(M_{i},g_{i},p_{i})

Let

be a point where

We claim that, for

\fint_{B^{i}_{s}(p^{\prime}_{i})}(s^{2}\mathsf{R}_{M_{i},k})\wedge L\,d\mathsf{Vol}_{i}\geq L/3.

To prove (
18
), it is enough to show that

\frac{\mathsf{Vol}_{i}(B^{i}_{s}(p^{\prime}_{i})\cap\{\mathsf{R}_{M_{i},k}\geq s^{-2}L/2\})}{\mathsf{Vol}_{i}(B^{i}_{s}(p^{\prime}_{i}))}\rightarrow 1,\quad\text{as }i\to+\infty.

(19)

Assume by contradiction that (
19
) fails. Then there exists
\delta>0
such that, up to a non-relabeled subsequence:

\mathsf{Vol}_{i}(B^{i}_{s}(p^{\prime}_{i})\cap\{\mathsf{R}_{M_{i},k}\geq s^{-2}L/2\})<(1-\delta)\mathsf{Vol}_{i}(B^{i}_{s}(p^{\prime}_{i})).

Hence, for
i
large enough:

\displaystyle\int_{B^{i}_{1}(p_{i})}

\displaystyle 0\vee(r_{i}^{-\alpha}\mathsf{R}_{M_{i},k})\wedge L\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq L\mathsf{Vol}_{i}(B^{i}_{1}(p_{i})\setminus B^{i}_{s}(p^{\prime}_{i}))+L(1-\delta)\mathsf{Vol}_{i}(B^{i}_{s}(p^{\prime}_{i}))+r_{i}^{-\alpha}s^{-2}L/2\mathsf{Vol}_{i}(B^{i}_{s}(p^{\prime}_{i})).

Using that
B^{i}_{s}(p^{\prime}_{i})\subset B^{i}_{1}(p_{i})
for
i
large enough, we infer that

\displaystyle\int_{B^{i}_{1}(p_{i})}

\displaystyle 0\vee(r_{i}^{-\alpha}\mathsf{R}_{M_{i},k})\wedge L\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq L\mathsf{Vol}_{i}(B^{i}_{1}(p_{i}))+(r_{i}^{-\alpha}s^{-2}L/2-\delta L)\mathsf{Vol}_{i}(B_{s}(p^{\prime}_{i})).

By Bishop-Gromov’s inequality, we conclude that for a positive constant
c(s,n)>0
it holds

\displaystyle\fint_{B^{i}_{1}(p_{i})}

\displaystyle 0\vee(r_{i}^{-\alpha}\mathsf{R}_{M_{i},k})\wedge L\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}_{i}\leq L+(r_{i}^{-\alpha}s^{-2}L/2-\delta L)c(s,n).

Since
r_{i}\uparrow\infty
, this contradicts (
17
), proving item
1
.

Proof of
2
. Bounds as in (
18
) follow repeating the previous argument for the integral appearing in item
2
. To conclude, one then uses Theorem
3.8
instead of Theorem
1.1
.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

In Theorem

, if we replace the limit symbols with lim-sup symbols, we obtain that

a tangent cone satisfying the corresponding dimensional condition.

Moreover, one can replace

in the statement with an arbitrary positive measurable function

sucht that

as

The proofs of this facts are the same as the one of Theorem

.

Remark 3.11
.

Theorem
3.9
fails for
\alpha=0
. We give a counterexample for
k=1
, and all the other cases follow by taking products with real factors. Indeed, consider the paraboloid
\Sigma:=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:z=x^{2}+y^{2}\}
. The sectional curvature of
\Sigma
is
K(z)\sim 1/4z^{2}
as

. For every

, the subset

is a ball of radius
f(r)\geq r
. Hence, on the paraboloid, for a positive constant

, it holds
f(r)^{2}K(z)\geq c>0
for all points in

. Hence,

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\fint_{B^{\Sigma}_{f(r)}(0)}(f(r)^{2}K)\wedge c\,\mathsf{Vol}=c.

At the same time, the tangent cone at infinity of the paraboloid is a half line.

Remark 3.12
.

Let
L>0
and
\alpha\in(0,2)
be fixed. Another immediate consequence of Theorem

from the introduction.

4
Thin metric spaces

The goal of this section is to show that
thin
metric spaces (see Definition
4.2
, after
[
34
]
) are contained in a neighbourhood of controlled width of an isometrically embedded
1
-dimensional manifold (see Theorem
4.16
). This will be a key step to prove Theorem
1.2
.

In
[
34
, Section 5]
, it is shown that a
non-compact
thin metric space is contained in a finite neighbourhood of a ray or a line. Comparing with
[
34
, Section 5]
, Theorem
4.16
treats the compact case as well, and provides explicit uniform bounds on the width of the aforementioned neighbourhood. These bounds are needed for the applications in the subsequent sections and, in particular, to prove Theorem
1.2
.
On the other hand, unlike
[
34
, Section 5]
, we only consider metric spaces
(X,\mathsf{d})
that are proper, separable, and geodesic.

If not otherwise specified, all curves are assumed to be of finite length and parametrized by arc-length. The length functional is denoted by
\mathrm{L}(\cdot)
.

Definition 4.1

(Ray, segment, line)
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be a metric space. A function
r:[0,+\infty)\to X
is called
a ray
if

\mathsf{d}(r(t),r(s))=|t-s|

for every
t,s\in[0,+\infty)
.
Similarly
r:[a,b]\to X
is called
a segment
if

\mathsf{d}(r(t),r(s))=|t-s|

for every
t,s\in[a,b]
and
r:\mathbb{R}\to X
is called
a line
if

\mathsf{d}(r(t),r(s))=|t-s|

for every
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
.

When considering a ray (resp. a segment or a line)
r
in
X
, by a slight abuse in order to keep notation short, we denote by
r
both the function
r:[0,+\infty)\to X
and its image. Moreover,
[r(a),r(b)]
denotes the set
r([a,b])\subset X
, for every
0<a<b
.

Given a closed set
K\subset X
and a point
x\in X
, we call
the projection
of
x
into
K
the subset
\pi_{K}(x)\subset K
defined by

\pi_{K}(x):=\{y\in K\colon\mathsf{d}(x,K)=\mathsf{d}(x,y)\}.

Given a subset
A\subset X
, we call
the projection
of
A
into
K
the subset
\pi_{K}(A)\subset K
defined by

\pi_{K}(A):=\bigcup_{x\in A}\pi_{K}(x).

Moreover, for every
y\in K
, we denote by
\pi^{-1}_{K}(y)\subset X
the subset

\pi^{-1}_{K}(y):=\{x\in X\colon\mathsf{d}(x,K)=\mathsf{d}(x,y)\}.

(22)

Definition 4.2

(Thin metric spaces)
.

Let
R,D>0
with
R\geq 20D
. Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be a proper, geodesic and separable metric space. We say that
(X,\mathsf{d})
is
(R,D)
-thin
if for every segment
r
of length greater than
2R
in
X
, and for every
t\in(R,\mathrm{L}(r)-R)
and
x\in\pi_{r}^{-1}(r(t))
, it holds
\mathsf{d}(x,r)<D
.

In
[
34
]
, a metric space is defined to be
R
-thin if it is
(R,R)
-thin according to Definition
4.2
. We consider the extra parameter
D>0
and we restrict ourselves to the case
R\geq 20D
as this will be sufficient for the subsequent applications.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be
(R,D)
-thin (see Definition
4.2
) and
let
r
be a ray in
X
.
If a point
x\in X
satisfies
\pi_{r}(x)\cap(r(R),r(\infty))\neq\emptyset
, then
\mathsf{d}(x,r)\leq D
.
This fact follows by applying Definition
4.2
to the segments
[r(0),r(T)]
for
T>2R
.

The next lemma is needed to prove Lemma
4.5
, which is the key technical tool to study
(R,D)
-thin metric spaces.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be a geodesic metric space and let
C_{1}\subset C_{2}\subset X
be closed sets. The set

K:=\{x\in X:\pi_{C_{2}}(x)\cap C_{1}\neq\emptyset\}

is closed.

In particular, if
\alpha:[0,b]\to X
is a curve, then
the set

I:=\{s\in[0,b]:\pi_{C_{2}}(\alpha(s))\cap C_{1}\neq\emptyset\}

is closed as well.

Proof.

Let
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in K
be a sequence converging to
x_{\infty}\in X
. We claim that
x_{\infty}\in K
.
For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\gamma_{i}
be a segment from
x_{i}
to a point
\gamma_{i}(\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{i}))\in C_{1}
such that
\mathsf{d}(x_{i},C_{2})=\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{i})
.
If
\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{i})\to 0
as
i\to+\infty
, then
x_{\infty}\in C_{1}
, so that
x_{\infty}\in K
.

If instead
\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{i})\not\to 0
, modulo passing to a subsequence, the segments
\gamma_{i}
converge to a finite segment
\gamma
from
x_{\infty}
to a limit point of the sequence
\{\gamma_{i}(\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{i}))\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. Since
C_{1}
is closed, all limit points of
\{\gamma_{i}(\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{i}))\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
lie in
C_{1}
, so that also
\gamma(\mathrm{L}(\gamma))\in C_{1}
. By lower semicontinuity of the length:

\mathsf{d}(x_{\infty},C_{2})\leq\mathrm{L}(\gamma)\leq\liminf_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{i})=\liminf_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathsf{d}(x_{i},C_{2})=\mathsf{d}(x_{\infty},C_{2}).

In particular,

\mathsf{d}(x_{\infty},C_{2})=\mathrm{L}(\gamma)
, so that
x_{\infty}\in K
. Hence,
K
is closed.
Being the preimage of
K
via the continuous function
\alpha
, also
I
is closed.
∎

The next lemma contains the key technical properties of
(R,D)
-thin metric spaces (see Definition
4.2
).

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be an
(R,D)
-thin metric space (see Definition
4.2
). Let
\alpha:[0,\mathrm{L}(\alpha)]\to X
be a unit speed curve.

1.

Let
l
be a segment with
\mathrm{L}(l)>2R
. Let
t\in(R,\mathrm{L}(l)-R)
, and let
u\in[0,R]\cup[\mathrm{L}(l)-R,\mathrm{L}(l)]
. If
l(t)\in\pi_{l}(\alpha(0))
and
l(u)\in\pi_{l}(\alpha(\mathrm{L}(\alpha)))
, then the set
\pi_{l}(\alpha)
contains a
3D
-net of either
[l(R),l(t)]
or
[l(t),l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R)]
.

2.

Let
l
be a segment with
\mathrm{L}(l)>2R
. Let
t,u\in(R,\mathrm{L}(l)-R)
with
t<u
. If
l(t)\in\pi_{l}(\alpha(0))
and
l(u)\in\pi_{l}(\alpha(\mathrm{L}(\alpha)))
, then
\pi_{l}(\alpha)
contains a
3D
-net of either
[l(t),l(u)]
or
[l(R),l(t)]\cup[l(u),l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R)]
.

3.

Let
r
be a ray, let
t>R
and let
u\in[0,R]
. If
r(t)\in\pi_{r}(\alpha(0))
and
r(u)\in\pi_{r}(\alpha(\mathrm{L}(\alpha)))
, then
\pi_{r}(\alpha)
contains a
3D
-net of
[r(R),r(t)]
.

Proof.

We consider item
1
first.
Assume by contradiction that the claim fails. Hence, there are
x_{1}\in[R,t]
and
x_{2}\in[t,\mathrm{L}(l)-R]
such that

\pi_{l}(\alpha)\cap B_{3D}(l(x_{1}))=\pi_{l}(\alpha)\cap B_{3D}(l(x_{2}))=\emptyset.

(23)

Consider

\displaystyle\tilde{s}:=\max\Big\{s\in[0,

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}(\alpha)]:\pi_{l}(\alpha(s))\cap[l(x_{1}),l(x_{2})]\neq\emptyset\Big\}.

In order to show that
\tilde{s}
is well-defined, it is sufficient to observe that the set appearing in its definition is:

•

closed
, thanks to Lemma
4.4
;

•

non-empty
, since by assumption
l(t)\in\pi_{l}(\alpha)
.

We claim that
\tilde{s}<\mathrm{L}(\alpha)
. If this is not the case, then, combining with (
23
),

\pi_{l}\big(\alpha(\mathrm{L}(\alpha))\big)\cap[l(x_{1}+3D),l(x_{2}-3D)]\neq\emptyset.

Since
X
is
(R,D)
-thin,
\alpha(\mathrm{L}(\alpha))
is
D
-close to
[l(x_{1}+3D),l(x_{2}-3D)]
, so that

\pi_{l}\big(\alpha(\mathrm{L}(\alpha))\big)\cap l\big([0,R]\cup[\mathrm{L}(l)-R,\mathrm{L}(l)]\big)=\emptyset,

a contradiction. This shows that
\tilde{s}<\mathrm{L}(\alpha)
.

Hence, for every
\epsilon>0
, there is
p_{\epsilon}\in\pi_{l}\big(\alpha(\tilde{s}+\epsilon)\big)
. Let
p\in\pi_{l}\big(\alpha(\tilde{s})\big)\cap[l(x_{1}),l(x_{2})]
. Since
X
is
(R,D)
-thin, it holds
\mathsf{d}(p,\alpha(\tilde{s}))<D
. It follows that
\mathsf{d}(p_{\epsilon},\alpha(\tilde{s}+\epsilon))<D+\epsilon
. Combining these facts, it holds

\mathsf{d}(p,p_{\epsilon})\leq\mathsf{d}(p,\alpha(\tilde{s}))+\mathsf{d}(\alpha(\tilde{s}),\alpha(\tilde{s}+\epsilon))+\mathsf{d}(\alpha(\tilde{s}),p_{\epsilon})\leq 2D+2\epsilon.

This contradicts the fact that
p_{\epsilon}\in[l(0),l(x_{1}-3D)]\cup[l(x_{2}+3D),l(\mathrm{L}(l))]
by (
23
).

The proof of item
2
is very similar to the one of item
1
, and for this reason it is only sketched.
Assume by contradiction that the claim fails. Hence, there are
x_{1}\in[t,u]
and
x_{2}\in[R,t]\cup[u,\mathrm{L}(l)-R]
such that, as in (
23
),

\pi_{l}(\alpha)\cap B_{3D}(l(x_{1}))=\pi_{l}(\alpha)\cap B_{3D}(l(x_{2}))=\emptyset.

One can now assume without loss of generality that
x_{2}\geq x_{1}
(the other case being analogous), and the proof can be carried out in the same way as for item
1
.

We now consider item
3
. Since the image of

is a bounded subset of

\pi_{l}(\alpha)\subset[r(0),r(T)]

. We consider now the segment

. It is easy to check that
\pi_{l}(\alpha)=\pi_{r}(\alpha)

,
\pi_{l}(\alpha)
contains a
3D

or
[l(t),l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R)]

.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})

t_{j}\to+\infty
. Then,
r
is contained in the
2R
-neighbourhood of
r_{y}
.

Proof.

It follows immediately from Lemma
4.6
.
∎

The next proposition shows that an
(R,D)
-thin non-compact metric space is contained in a neighbourhood of controlled width of a ray or a line. We recall that a similar result is proved in
[
34
, Section 5]
. The main difference is that Proposition
4.10
below also gives an explicit bound on the width of the aforementioned neighbourhood.

Proposition 4.10
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be an
(R,D)
-thin metric space (see Definition
4.2
).
Assume that
X
is non-compact and it does not contain a line.
There exists a ray
r
in
X
whose
10R
-neighbourhood contains
X
.

Proof.

We first show that, given any ray
r
in
X
, there exists
D^{\prime}(X,r)>0
such that
X
is contained in the
D^{\prime}
-neighbourhood of
r
.

If the claim were false, we would find points
p_{j}
at arbitrarily large distance from
r
.
For each point
p_{j}
, let
r_{j}
be a ray obtained as a limit of the segments from
p_{j}
to
r(t_{i})
for some sequence
t_{i}\to+\infty
.
By Lemma
4.6
, each ray
r_{j}
has a point
q_{j}
whose distance from
r(0)
is less than
2R
.
Since the distance between
p_{j}=r_{j}(0)
and
q_{j}
goes to infinity as
j
increases, and the sequence
(q_{j})
is contained in a compact subset of
X
, then, up to a subsequence, the rays
r_{j}
converge to a line
l
contained in
X
, a contradiction.

We now prove the statement of the lemma.
If
X
is contained in the
10R
-neighbourhood of
r
, there is nothing to prove.
Otherwise, we claim that there exists an element
y\in X
at maximal distance from
r
.
Let
y_{i}\in X
be a sequence maximizing the distance from
r
. We can assume that
9R\leq\mathsf{d}(y_{i},r)\leq D^{\prime}(X)
. Since
X
is
(R,D)
-thin,
\pi_{r}(y_{i})\subset[r(0),r(R)]
. It follows that the sequence
\{y_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is precompact, so that there exists a limit point
y\in X
at maximal distance from
r
.

Let
r_{y}
be the ray which is the limit of the segments joining
y
and
r(t_{i})
for a sequence
t_{i}\uparrow+\infty
. By Lemma
4.9
, for every
t\geq 0
, it holds
\mathsf{d}(r(t),r_{y})\leq 2R
. Since
\mathsf{d}(y,r)\geq 10R
, it follows that
\pi_{r_{y}}(r)\subset[r_{y}(5R),r_{y}(+\infty))
.

Assume now by contradiction that there exists
y^{\prime}\in X
such that
\mathsf{d}(y^{\prime},r_{y})\geq 10R
. Let
\gamma
be a segment realizing the distance between
y^{\prime}
and
r
. Let
r(t_{\gamma})
be the endpoint of
\gamma
. Let
r_{y}(t_{\gamma}^{y})\in\pi_{r_{y}}\big(r(t_{\gamma})\big)
. By our previous remarks,
t_{\gamma}^{y}\geq 5R
. By Lemma
4.5
,
\pi_{r_{y}}(\gamma)
contains a
3D
-neighbourhood of
[r_{y}(R),r_{y}(5R)]
. Hence,
\gamma
contains a point
p_{1}
which is
7D
-close to
r_{y}(R)
. Recall that we also have
\mathsf{d}(y^{\prime},r_{y})\geq 10R
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(y^{\prime},r)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{L}(\gamma)=\mathsf{d}(y^{\prime},p_{1})+\mathsf{d}(p_{1},r)\geq\mathsf{d}(y^{\prime},r_{y})-7D+\mathsf{d}(y,r)-R-7D

\displaystyle\geq\mathsf{d}(y,r)+9R-14D>\mathsf{d}(y,r)

Hence,
y
was not an element maximizing the distance from
r
, a contradiction.
∎

We now turn our attention to the study of compact
(R,D)
-thin metric spaces. The key result in this sense is Proposition
4.15
. We first need a few lemmas.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be an
(R,D)
-thin metric space.
Let
l
be a segment in
X
and assume that
\mathrm{length}(l)\geq 10R
. Let
\bar{l}
be a segment starting from
l(0)
with
\pi_{l}(\bar{l}(\mathrm{L}(\bar{l})))\subset[l(0),l(R)]\cup[l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R),l(\mathrm{L}(l)]
.
If
\pi_{l}\big(\bar{l}\big)\cap[l(3R),l(\mathrm{L}(l)-3R)]\neq\emptyset
,
then
\pi_{l}(\bar{l})
contains a
3D
-net of
[l(R),l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R)]
.

Proof.

Let
t_{0}\in[0,\mathrm{L}(\bar{l})]
be such that
\pi_{l}\big(\bar{l}(t_{0})\big)\ni l(t)
for
t\in[3R,\mathrm{L}(l)-3R]
.
Let
\bar{l}_{1}:=\bar{l}_{|[0,t_{0}]}
and
\bar{l}_{2}:=\bar{l}_{|[t_{0},\mathrm{L}(\bar{l})]}
.
Hence, both
\bar{l}_{1}
and
\bar{l}_{2}
have an endpoint whose projection on
l
lies in
[l(0),l(R)]\cup[l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R),l(\mathrm{L}(l)]
.

By item
1
of Lemma
4.5
,
\pi_{l}(\bar{l}_{1})
contains a
3D
-net of either
[l(R),l(t)]
or
[l(t),l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R)]
, and the same holds for
\pi_{l}(\bar{l}_{2})
.

Assume by contradiction that both
\pi_{l}(\bar{l}_{1})
and
\pi_{l}(\bar{l}_{2})
contain a
3D
-net of
[l(R),l(t)]
. In this case, there exist
t_{1}\leq t_{0}\leq t_{2}
such that
\bar{l}(t_{1})
and
\bar{l}(t_{2})
are
7D
-close to
l(R)
. Hence,

\mathsf{d}(\bar{l}(t_{1}),\bar{l}(t_{2}))\leq 14D.

(25)

Since
\bar{l}(t_{0})
is
D
-close to a point
l(t)
in
[l(3R),l(\mathrm{L}(l)-3R)]
, it follows

t_{0}-t_{1}=\mathsf{d}(\bar{l}(t_{0}),\bar{l}(t_{1}))\geq\mathsf{d}(l(R),l(t))-8D\geq 2R-8D,

so that

\mathsf{d}(\bar{l}(t_{1}),\bar{l}(t_{2}))\geq 2R-5D.
This violates (
25
).

By the same argument, it is not possible that both
\pi_{l}(\bar{l}_{1})
and
\pi_{l}(\bar{l}_{2})
contain a
3D
-net of
[l(t),l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R)]
, concluding the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be an
(R,D)
-thin metric space.
Let
l
be a segment in
X
and assume that
\mathrm{length}(l)\geq 10R
. Let
p\in X
be a point with
\mathsf{d}(p,l)\geq 10R
and let
\bar{l}
be a segment connecting
p
to
l(0)
.
If
\pi_{l}\big(\bar{l}\big)\cap[l(3R),l(\mathrm{L}(l)-3R)]\neq\emptyset
, then
\mathrm{L}(\bar{l})>\mathrm{L}(l)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathsf{d}(p,l)\geq 10R
and
X
is
(R,D)
-thin, it follows that
\pi_{l}(p)\subset[l(0),l(R)]\cup[l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R),l(\mathrm{L}(l)]
.
By Lemma
4.11
, there is a point
\bar{l}(s_{0})
which is
7D
-close to
l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R)
. Using triangle inequality, this implies

\mathrm{L}(\bar{l})=\mathsf{d}(p,\bar{l}(s_{0}))+\mathsf{d}(\bar{l}(s_{0}),l(0))\geq\mathsf{d}(p,l)-7D+\mathsf{d}(l(0),l(\mathrm{L}(l)-R))-7D.

Using that
\mathsf{d}(p,l)\geq 10R
and that
l
is a segment, it follows

\mathrm{L}(\bar{l})\geq 9R-14D+\mathrm{L}(l)>\mathrm{L}(l).

∎

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be a compact
(R,D)
-thin metric space.
Let
l
be a segment of maximal length in
X
and assume that
\mathrm{length}(l)\geq 10R
. Let
p\in X
be a point with
\mathsf{d}(p,l)\geq 10R
and let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
be segments connecting
p
respectively to
l(\mathrm{L}(l))
and
l(0)
.
Let
b_{1},b_{2}\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},l\}
with
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
. Then
\pi_{b_{1}}(b_{2})\subset[b_{1}(0),b_{1}(3R)]\cup[b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1})-3R),b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1})]
.

Proof.

If
b_{1}=l
, the statement follows by Lemma
4.12
.

We now consider the case
b_{1}\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\}
and
b_{2}=l
. If the statement fails,
\pi_{b_{1}}\big(l\big)\cap(b_{1}(3R),b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1})-3R))\neq\emptyset
. Consider the orientation of
l
such that
l(0)\in b_{1}
. Then,
\pi_{b_{1}}(l(\mathrm{L}(l)))\subset[b_{1}(0),b_{1}(R)]\cup[b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1})-R),b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1})]
since otherwise, using that
X
is
(R,D)
-thin, it follows that
l
is shorter than
b_{1}
.
Hence, by Lemma
4.11
, there exists
s>0
such that
l(s)
is
R+7D
-close to
p
. This contradicts that
\mathsf{d}(p,l)\geq 10R
.

Finally, we consider the case
b_{1}=\gamma_{1}
and
b_{2}=\gamma_{2}
(the case
b_{2}=\gamma_{1}
and
b_{1}=\gamma_{2}
is identical).

We consider orientations such that
\gamma_{1}(\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{1}))=\gamma_{2}(0)
,
\gamma_{2}(\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{2}))=l(0)
and
l(\mathrm{L}(l))=\gamma_{1}(0)
.

Assume by contradiction that
\gamma_{1}(t)\in\pi_{\gamma_{1}}(\gamma_{2})
, with
t\in(3R,\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{1})-3R)
. By Lemma
4.11
, there exists
s>0
such that
\gamma_{2}(s)
is
R+7D
-close to
\gamma_{1}(0)=l(\mathrm{L}(l))
. It follows that

\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{2})=\mathsf{d}(p,\gamma_{2}(s))+\mathsf{d}(\gamma_{2}(s),l(0))\geq\mathsf{d}(p,l)-R-7D+\mathrm{L}(l)-R-7D>\mathrm{L}(l).

This is a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 4.14
.

Let
(X,d)
and
\gamma_{1},\,\gamma_{2},\,l
be as in the previous lemma. Let
\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},l\}
. If
b_{1}(0)\notin b_{2}
, the follows happen.

1.

\mathsf{d}(b_{1}(0),b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})/2))\geq\min\{\mathrm{L}(b_{1}),\mathrm{L}(b_{3})\}+\mathrm{L}(b_{2})/2-10R.

2.

\mathsf{d}(b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1})/2)),b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})/2))\geq\mathrm{L}(b_{1})/2+\mathrm{L}(b_{2})/2-10R.

Proof.

We only prove the first point, the proof of the second one being analogous.
Let
\gamma
be a segment connecting
b_{1}(0)
and
b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})/2)
. If
\gamma
has a point which is
5R
-close to either
b_{2}(3R)
or
b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})-3R)
, the statement follows by triangle inequality.

By the previous lemma,
\pi_{b_{2}}\big(b_{1}(0)\big)\cap[b_{2}(3R),b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})-3R]\neq\emptyset
. If
\pi_{b_{2}}\big(b_{1}(0)\big)
intersects
[b_{2}(R),b_{2}(3R)]
or
[b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})-3R),b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})-R)]
, then
\gamma
has a point which is
5R
-close to either
b_{2}(3R)
or
b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})-3R)
(because
X
is
(R,D)
-thin), otherwise the same conclusion follows by Lemma
4.5
.
∎

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be a compact
(R,D)
-thin metric space (see Definition
4.2
).
Let
l
be a segment of maximal length in
X
and assume that
\mathrm{L}(l)\geq 200R
. If
X
is not contained in a
200R
-neighbourhood of
l
, then there exists
L>0
and a distance preserving map
\phi:S^{1}_{L}\to X
, such that
X
is contained in the
D
-neighbourhood of
\phi(S^{1}_{L})
. In particular,
L\geq 50R
.

Proof.

Let
p\in X
with
\mathsf{d}(p,X)>200R
. Let
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
be segments from
p
to
l(\mathrm{L}(l))
and
l(0)
respectively. Note that both these segments have length greater than
200R
.

On
\gamma_{1}
, we consider the subsegment

I^{\gamma_{1}}:=[\gamma_{1}(\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{1})/2-5R)),\gamma_{1}(\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{1})/2+5R))].

We also consider subsegments
I^{\gamma_{2}}
and
I^{l}
defined in the same way. Observe that these subsegments are independent of the chosen parametrization by arc-length of
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
and
l
.

To construct the map
\phi
of the statement, we will consider the shortest loop passing near each one of the previously defined segments. To this aim, let

\displaystyle A:=\Big\{\phi:

\displaystyle[0,\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+l)]\to X\mid\phi\text{ is }1\text{-Lipschitz, }\phi(0)=\phi(\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+l)),

\displaystyle\quad\exists\,t^{\gamma_{1}},\,t^{\gamma_{2}},\,t^{l}\in[0,\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+l)]\text{ such that }

\displaystyle\quad\pi_{b}\big(\phi(t^{b})\big)\cap I^{b}\neq\emptyset\text{ for every }b\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},l\}\Big\}.

Notice that
A\neq\emptyset
since
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+l\in A
. We claim that if
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset A
and
\phi_{i}\to\phi_{\infty}
uniformly, then
\phi_{\infty}\in A
. First of all
\phi_{\infty}
is
1
-Lipschitz and satisfies
\phi_{\infty}(0)=\phi_{\infty}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+l)
trivially. Let
b\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},l\}
. Consider for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
the corresponding
t_{i}^{b}
. Modulo passing to a subsequence, there exists
t_{\infty}^{b}\in[0,\mathrm{L}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+l)]
such that
t_{i}^{b}\to t_{\infty}^{b}
. By Lemma
4.4
,
\pi_{b}\big(\phi_{\infty}(t_{\infty}^{b})\big)\cap I^{b}\neq\emptyset
.
It follows that
\phi_{\infty}\in A
as claimed.

Consider now a sequence
\phi_{i}\in A
minimizing the length functional. Each image of
\phi_{i}
satisfies
\pi_{\gamma_{1}}\big(\mathrm{Im}(\phi_{i})\big)\cap I_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\neq\emptyset
. Since
X
is
(R,D)
-thin, the image of each
\phi_{i}
has a point which is
D
-close to
I_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}
. Hence, all the images of the maps
\phi_{i}
are contained in a bounded set of
X
. Since the
\phi_{i}
are all
1
-Lipschitz, by Ascoli-Arzelà theorem, there exists (modulo passing to a subsequence)
\phi_{\infty}
such that
\phi_{i}\to\phi_{\infty}
uniformly. By the previous part of the proof,
\phi_{\infty}\in A
. Since the length functional is lower semicontinuous w.r.t. uniform convergence,
\phi_{\infty}
is an element of minimal length in
A
.

Let
T:=\mathrm{L}(\phi_{\infty})
and consider the reparametrizaion
\phi_{\infty}:[0,T]\to X
by arc-length, and the induced map (denoted again by
\phi_{\infty}
)
\phi_{\infty}:S^{1}_{L}\to X
, where
L:=T/2\pi
. By Corollary
4.14
, it follows that
T\geq 60R
, and that the distance in
S^{1}_{L}
between any two points of
\{t^{\gamma_{1}},t^{\gamma_{2}},t^{l}\}
is at least
20R
(in particular they are all distinct).

We claim that
\phi_{\infty}:S^{1}_{L}\to X
is distance preserving and that
X
is contained in the
D
-neighbourhood of
\phi_{\infty}(S^{1}_{L})
. We divide the proof of the claim in steps.

Step 1.
We claim that, for
b\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},l\}
, the projection
\pi_{b}(\phi_{\infty}(S^{1}_{L}))
contains a
3D
-net of
[b(3R),b(\mathrm{L}(b)-3R)]
.

Let
b_{1},b_{2}\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},l\}
with
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
.
Let
t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}\in[0,T]
be the numbers given in the definition of
A
relative to
\phi_{\infty}
. Modulo reparametrizing
\phi_{\infty}
and
\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}+l
, we can assume that
b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1}))=b_{2}(0)
and
t^{b_{1}}\leq t^{b_{2}}
with
t^{b_{3}}\notin[t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}]
, i.e.
[t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}]
corresponds to the arc of
S^{1}_{L}
that connects
t^{b_{1}}
and
t^{b_{2}}
without crossing
t^{b_{3}}
.

Let
b_{1}(\bar{t}^{b_{1}})\in I^{b_{1}}\cap\pi_{b_{1}}\big(\phi_{\infty}(t^{b_{1}})\big)
.
Similarly, let
b_{2}(\bar{t}^{b_{2}})\in I^{b_{2}}\cap\pi_{b_{2}}\big(\phi_{\infty}(t^{b_{2}})\big)
.
We show that
\pi_{b_{1}}(\phi_{\infty}(S^{1}_{L}))
contains a
3D
-net of
[b_{1}(\bar{t}^{b_{1}}),b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1})-3R)]
, while
\pi_{b_{2}}(\phi_{\infty}(S^{1}_{L}))
contains a
3D
-net of
[b_{2}(3R),b_{2}(\bar{t}^{b_{2}})]
. The claim then follows by repeating the same argument for all pairs of segments in
\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},l\}
.

By definition of
\phi_{\infty}
, the restriction
{\phi_{\infty}}_{|[t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}]}
is a segment. By Lemmas
4.13
and
4.5
,
\pi_{b_{1}}({\phi_{\infty}}([t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}]))
contains a
3D
-net of either
[b_{1}(3R),b_{1}(\bar{t}^{b_{1}})]
or
[b_{1}(\bar{t}^{b_{1}}),b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1})-3R)]
.
If the former case happens,
{\phi_{\infty}}([t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}])
has a point which is
7D
-close to
b_{1}(3R)
, so that it also contains a point
q
which is
5R
-close to
b_{1}(0)
.
By Corollary
4.14
,
b_{1}(0)
is at least
\min\{\mathrm{L}(b_{1}),\mathrm{L}(b_{3})\}+\mathrm{L}(b_{2})/2-20R
distant from
\phi_{\infty}(t^{b_{2}})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}({\phi_{\infty}}_{|[t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}]})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{d}(\phi_{\infty}(t^{b_{1}}),q)+\mathsf{d}(q,b_{2}(t^{b_{2}}))

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{L}(b_{1})/2+\min\{\mathrm{L}(b_{1}),\mathrm{L}(b_{3})\}+\mathrm{L}(b_{2})/2-50R

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{L}(b_{1})/2+\mathrm{L}(b_{2})/2+100R.

This is a contradiction, because we would obtain a shorter curve between
\phi_{\infty}(t^{b_{1}})
and
\phi_{\infty}(t^{b_{2}})
by first joining them to their projections
b_{1}(\bar{t}^{b_{1}})
and
b_{1}(\bar{t}^{b_{2}})
, and then joining the projections along
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
themselves.

Hence,
\pi_{b_{1}}({\phi_{\infty}}([t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}]))
contains a
3D
-net of
[b_{1}(\bar{t}^{b_{1}}),b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1})-3R)]
. The same argument shows that
\pi_{b_{2}}({\phi_{\infty}}([t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}]))
contains a
3D
-net of
[b_{2}(3R),b_{2}(\bar{t}^{b_{2}})]
. This concludes the proof of Step 1.

Step 2.
For every
b_{1},b_{2}\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},l\}
, with
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
, assuming as before that
t^{b_{1}}\leq t^{b_{2}}
in
[0,T]
with
t^{b_{3}}\notin[t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}]
, it holds
\pi_{\phi_{\infty}([t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}])}(\phi_{\infty}(t^{b_{2}}+3R))=\{\phi_{\infty}(t^{b_{2}})\}
.

Modulo reparametrizing, we assume
b_{1}(\mathrm{L}(b_{1}))=b_{2}(0)
and
t^{b_{1}}\leq t^{b_{2}}\leq t^{b_{3}}
in
[0,T]
.
By Step
1
, there exist
t_{1,2}\in[t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}]
and
t_{2,3}\in[t^{b_{2}},t^{b_{3}}]
, such that
\phi_{\infty}(t_{1,2})
is
7D
-close to
b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})-20R)
and
\phi_{\infty}(t_{2,3})
is
7D
-close to
b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})+20R)
. Let
\gamma
be a segment connecting
\phi_{\infty}(t_{1,2})
and
\phi_{\infty}(t_{2,3})
. By item
2
of Lemma
4.5
and the fact that
X
is
(R,D)
-thin, either
\pi_{b_{2}}(\gamma)
intersects
I^{b_{2}}
, or
\gamma
contains points that are
7D
close to
b_{2}(R)
and
b_{2}(\mathrm{L}(b_{2})-R)
. In the latter case, since
\mathrm{L}(b_{2})\geq 200R
,
\gamma
would not be a segment. Hence,
\pi_{b_{2}}(\gamma)
intersects
I^{b_{2}}
. It follows that
\gamma+{\phi_{\infty}}_{|S^{1}_{L}\setminus[t_{1,2},t_{2,3}]}
belongs to
A
. In particular,
{\phi_{\infty}}_{|[t_{1,2},t_{2,3}]}
is shorter than
\gamma
, so that it is itself a segment.

At the same time, since
{\phi_{\infty}}_{|[t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}]}
is a segment, it follows

\pi_{\phi_{\infty}([t^{b_{1}},t^{b_{2}}])}(\phi_{\infty}(t^{b_{2}}+3R))=\pi_{\phi_{\infty}([t_{1,2},t^{b_{2}}])}(\phi_{\infty}(t^{b_{2}}+3R)).

Combining with the fact that
{\phi_{\infty}}_{|[t_{1,2},t_{2,3}]}
is a segment, we obtain the statement.

Step 3
: Let
b_{1},b_{2}\in\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},l\}
with
b_{1}\neq b_{2}
. We claim that

defined by

in different path connected components of

As before, w.l.o.g. we can assume that

. Consider the segment

Step 4.
We claim that

is distance preserving and that
X
lies in a
D
-neighbourhood of
\phi_{\infty}(S^{1}_{L})
.

We show first that

preserves distances. Assume that this is not the case. Then, there exist

, and a segment

, connecting

and

and

be the two arcs connecting

and

We would like to show that one between the loops

and

belongs to

. If this is the case, we obtain a contradiction since

was length minimizing.

By step
1
, for every

, we can assume that

Hence, it suffices to show that one between

We consider all the possible cases. If

It follows that

∎

The next theorem summarizes the results of this section.

Theorem 4.16
.

Let

be an

-thin metric space. There exists a

-dimensional manifold

If

is contained in the

-neighbourhood of a loop which is mapped isometrically into

∎

-thin metric spaces have

Let

be the map given by Theorem

Case 1
:

, with

Theorem 5.1
.

and segments

Let us recall some properties of covering spaces.

41
) and Bishop-Gromov’s volume monotonicity, we infer that

\displaystyle\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k}}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k+1}}}\int_{B_{r_{j}/10}(x_{j})}|\mathsf{R}|^{s}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq 10^{2s}\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k}}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k+1}}}\mathsf{Vol}(B_{r_{j}/10}(x_{j}))r_{j}^{-2s}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k}}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k+1}}}c(n)\mathsf{Vol}(B_{r_{j}/50}(x_{j}))\alpha^{-2s(k+1)}.

(43)

We note that the balls
\{B_{r_{j}/50}(x_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are disjoint and

\bigcup_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k}}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k+1}}}B_{r_{j}}(x_{j})\subset T_{\alpha^{k}}(\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k}}).

Hence, choosing
\eta:=1-s
in Theorem
5.13
, and combining with Proposition
5.12
and (
43
), we deduce

\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k}}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k+1}}}\int_{B_{r_{j}/10}(x_{j})}|\mathsf{R}|^{s}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq c(n,v,s)\alpha^{(1-s)k}.

Hence,

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\,\sum_{x_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k}}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{\delta,\alpha^{k+1}}}\int_{B_{r_{j}/10}(x_{j})}|\mathsf{R}|^{s}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq c(n,v,s),

giving the statement.
∎

One can now deduce Jiang-Naber’s result
[
45
, Theorem 2.17]
.

Theorem 5.15

(Jiang-Naber)
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
v>0
,
s\in(0,1)
be fixed and let
(M^{n},g,x)
be a manifold with
\mathsf{Ric}_{M}\geq K
and
\mathsf{Vol}(B_{1}(x))\geq v
. There exists a constant
C(K,n,v,s)>0
such that

\fint_{B_{1}(x)}|\mathsf{Ric}|^{s}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq C(K,n,v,s).

(44)

Proof.

By Theorem
5.14
and a standard rescaling, there exists
r_{0}(K,n,v,s)>0
such that

\fint_{B_{r_{0}}(x)}|\mathsf{R}|^{s}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq c(K,n,v,s),\quad\text{for all }y\in B_{1}(x).

(45)

Consider a covering
\{B_{r_{0}}(x_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of
B_{1}(x)
such that the balls
\{B_{r_{0}/3}(x_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are disjoint.
Then, the combination of (
45
) with Bishop-Gromov’s volume monotonicity yields

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}(x)}|\mathsf{R}|^{s}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{B_{r_{0}}(x_{j})}|\mathsf{R}|^{s}\,{\rm d}\mathsf{Vol}\leq c(n,K,v,s)\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathsf{Vol}(B_{r_{0}/3}(x_{j}))

\displaystyle\leq c(n,K,v,s)\mathsf{Vol}(B_{1}(x)).

(46)

The assumption that
\mathsf{Ric}\geq K
allows to promote the integral bound (
46
) on the scalar cuvature into the claimed integral bound (
44
) on the Ricci curvature.
∎
