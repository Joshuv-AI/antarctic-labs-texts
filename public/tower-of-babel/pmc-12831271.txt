The Cardiometabolic Index (CMI) is associated with an increased risk of obstructive sleep apnea: a population-based study
Authors: Xinyi Chen, Yueyu Zhang, Yu Wang, Yi Tang, Kangrui Zhang, Juncang Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is closely associated with cardiometabolic abnormalities. However, the role of a comprehensive index integrating multiple cardiometabolic parameters in OSA risk assessment remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the potential association between the Cardiometabolic Index (CMI) and OSA risk in a large-scale population cohort.MethodsData were derived from the 2005–2008 and 2015–2018 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), with a total of 39,722 participants included. OSA was defined using self-reported indicators (e.g., daytime sleepiness, nocturnal choking/snoring) rather than objective criteria such as polysomnography (PSG). Logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between CMI and OSA. Segmented regression and non-linear models were constructed to verify the non-linear relationship between the two variables. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to validate the stability of the results, and finally, subgroup analysis was conducted to investigate heterogeneity across different populations.ResultsThe study found a potential association between elevated CMI and OSA. Multivariate adjusted regression analysis showed that in Model 3, the continuous CMI variable was significantly associated with OSA risk (odds ratio [OR] = 1.014, P = 0.024), and the OSA risk in the Q4 group (highest CMI quartile) was significantly higher than that in the Q1 group (lowest CMI quartile) (OR = 1.272, P < 0.001), showing a linear upward trend. The threshold for the association between CMI and OSA was 2.03: when CMI < 2.03, the OR was 1.50 (P < 0.001), while no statistically significant association was observed when CMI exceeded this threshold, indicating a significant “inverted L-shaped” non-linear relationship between the two. After PSM, the OSA risk in the Q4 group was 1.174 times that in the Q1 group (P = 0.018). Subgroup analysis revealed that the association between CMI and OSA might be stronger in populations without hypertension or stroke, especially among females.ConclusionsThis study found a non-linear positive association between CMI and OSA risk. Specifically, the OSA risk in the Q4 group was significantly higher than that in the Q1 group (OR = 1.272, P < 0.001). Additionally, the association was more prominent in young people, females, and populations without hypertension or stroke. These findings suggest that CMI may serve as a convenient indicator for early screening, particularly applicable to high-risk OSA populations with metabolic disorders. The results of this study provide a new perspective for public health interventions and clinical risk assessment of OSA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03687-w.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a highly prevalent and harmful sleep disorder. It is potentially associated with a range of conditions, including cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders, cognitive decline, and depressive symptoms [1–5].

Previous assessments of OSA risk have mainly relied on two approaches: single clinical indicators or polysomnography (PSG) technology. Single indicators cannot fully reflect the multifaceted nature of the disease’s risk. While PSG is widely regarded as the “gold standard” for OSA diagnosis, its high equipment cost, complex operation, and requirement for professional interpretation make it difficult to apply on a large scale for population screening. This limits its feasibility in broad-based risk assessment [6, 7].

In epidemiological studies, questionnaire-based self-reporting of OSA symptoms has become a common method. It balances assessment efficiency and data accessibility, enabling the rapid collection of large-sample data. However, the self-report method has inherent limitations. It depends on the subjective perception and recall accuracy of study participants, which may lead to classification bias. For example, some individuals lack the ability to recognize symptoms of “nocturnal apnea”. Others may underestimate symptom frequency due to cognitive bias. Such bias could potentially impact the correlation analysis between OSA risk and other indicators.

As an emerging composite indicator, the Cardiometabolic Index (CMI) offers unique advantages. It can assess lipid homeostasis through the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL-C) [8]. Conventional indicators like body mass index (BMI) only reflect overall obesity and cannot distinguish fat distribution. Yet visceral obesity is a key inducer of OSA—for instance, abdominal fat compresses the upper airway, triggers inflammation, and exacerbates airway abnormalities [9, 10]. CMI addresses this gap by accurately quantifying visceral fat via waist-to-hip ratio, with its assessment logic more consistent with the airway compression mechanism of OSA. Waist circumference, another commonly used indicator, can preliminarily signal abdominal fat accumulation but fails to reflect metabolic status. Notably, dyslipidaemias such as high triglycerides increase the risk of airway obstruction. Moreover, visceral fat accumulation and dyslipidaemia may be linked to the pathogenesis of OSA [11, 12]. Meanwhile, OSA is recognized as the result of the combined effect of “upper airway structural abnormalities + systemic metabolic disorders” [13]. Multiple cross-sectional studies have suggested a potential association between CMI and OSA risk [14, 15]. By integrating multiple indicators, CMI forms a comprehensive assessment that aligns better with the multi-mechanism pathogenic characteristics of OSA. Its clinical application value is thus significantly superior to single parameters like BMI and waist circumference [16].

This study leverages nationally representative data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to conduct an in-depth analysis of the relationship between CMI and OSA susceptibility. It achieves two key methodological innovations: It comprehensively evaluates the predictive value of CMI for OSA risk in a large-scale population cohort—a direction that has not been fully explored in sleep medicine research. It adopts an advanced statistical framework to simultaneously assess linear associations and non-linear dynamic relationships, including threshold-dependent associations.

Additionally, the study conducts stratified analyses based on demographic characteristics, sociocultural factors, and comorbidities. This allows for a systematic evaluation of effect modification across different strata. The expected results of this study will provide a new perspective and candidate biomarkers for OSA risk stratification, early screening, and prevention. In turn, this will offer references for evidence-based clinical decision-making and population-level prevention strategies.

Methods

Study data

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a nationally representative cross-sectional study conducted by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). It employs a stratified probability sampling framework, aiming to assess the health and nutrition trends of non-institutionalized civilian residents in the United States. Its design is intended to generate nationally representative estimates of population health indicators.

Ethical oversight of NHANES is ensured through review and approval by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). All participants included in the study signed a written informed consent form prior to participation. For this retrospective analysis, data from participants in the 2005–2008 and 2015–2018 NHANES cycles were extracted to ensure consistency in research methods and temporal relevance.

Study population

The study population was derived from participants of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) across two cycles, specifically 2005–2008 and 2015–2018, with an initial total of 39,722 individuals. To ensure the validity and completeness of the analysis, a series of exclusion criteria were applied sequentially: first, 14,561 participants were excluded due to missing data on obstructive sleep apnea (OSA); second, 2969 participants aged less than 20 years were excluded to focus on the target adult population; third, 3344 participants lacking essential laboratory indicators were excluded, which reduced the sample size to 18,848 individuals; finally, an additional 3862 participants were excluded because of missing survey data—including critical information such as baseline characteristics, sleep duration, body mass index (BMI), disease history, and life history. Calculation of the CMI

The CMI was calculated using the following formula: CMI = (WC [cm]/height [cm]) × (TG [mg/dL]/HDL-C [mg/dL]). CMI was treated as a continuous variable and analyzed by grouping into equal quartiles.

The method for measuring triglycerides (TG) is based on the work by Wahlefeld: a lipoprotein lipase derived from microorganisms is used for the rapid and complete hydrolysis of triglycerides into glycerol, which is then oxidized to dihydroxyacetone phosphate and hydrogen peroxide. The generated hydrogen peroxide then reacts with 4-aminophenazone and 4-chlorophenol under the catalytic action of peroxidase to form a red dyestuff (Trinder endpoint reaction). The color intensity of the formed red dyestuff is directly proportional to the triglyceride concentration and can be measured photometrically.

Definition of OSA

Numerous studies have defined OSA (Obstructive Sleep apnea) as follows:

The identification of Obstructive Sleep apnea (OSA) in the NHANES protocol requires affirmative responses to one or more of the three screening criteria: (1) persistent daytime hypersomnolence despite a nightly sleep duration of ≥ 7 h (documented in 16–30 instances); (2) recurrent episodes of respiratory disturbances (including breath cessation, choking sounds, or gasping) occurring ≥ 3 times per week; or (3) habitual snoring ≥ 3 times per week [17–20].

However, using self-reported symptoms to diagnose OSA has significant methodological limitations. First, it exhibits insufficient diagnostic sensitivity due to biases in symptom perception. Second, the false-positive interference caused by overlapping comorbid symptoms cannot be ignored. When participants have comorbid conditions such as Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (CFS) or Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), their Excessive Daytime Sleepiness (EDS) symptoms may be driven by both sleep fragmentation caused by OSA and central hypersomnolence mechanisms induced by comorbidities, resulting in a symptom overlap effect. Additionally, self-reports rely on participants’ retrospective memory, while sleep-related symptoms occur unconsciously and transiently—this factor may directly affect the diagnostic specificity of OSA.

Data extraction

Study data were obtained from publicly available NHANES datasets. Eligibility criteria included participants aged ≥ 20 years with complete data on key variables. The final study cohort comprised 14,986 subjects (including 10,476 individuals without OSA and 4510 individuals with OSA). The extracted variables are as follows:

Demographic and socioeconomic factors: Age (years), sex (male/female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, and multiracial/other), educational attainment (< high school, high school graduate, > high school), marital status (living with partner, living without partner), and poverty-income ratio (PIR).

Metabolic and obesity-related indices: Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), waist circumference (WC, cm), triglyceride (TG, mg/dL), total cholesterol (TC, mg/dL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL, mg/dL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L) levels.

Lifestyle and behavioural factors: Smoking status (never, former, or current smoker), alcohol consumption (never, mild, moderate, heavy, or former drinker), and physical activity (walking/bicycling habits: yes/no). Sleep duration (from questionnaire), total physical activity metabolic equivalents (PA_total_MET). Physical activity refers to various exercises or body movements performed by the human body that consume energy and affect health. In this study, physical activity levels were measured by calculating the total weekly metabolic equivalents (METs) of three types of activities: work/task-related activities, transportation-related activities, and muscle-strengthening/recreational activities [21].

Chronic diseases and comorbidities: Hypertension, diabetes mellitus (including impaired fasting glucose [IFG] and impaired glucose tolerance [IGT]), coronary heart disease (CHD), and history of stroke.

Additional health parameters: Height (cm), weight (kg), systolic/diastolic blood pressure (mmHg), fasting glucose (mg/dL), uric acid (mg/dL), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN, mg/dL).

Statistical analysis

This study adopted a stratified probability sampling framework to generate nationally representative estimates. In accordance with published analytical guidelines, analytical weights for the NHANES fasting subsample were included to address the complexity of the survey design.Categorical variables were presented as proportions, and continuous variables were presented as “mean ± standard deviation”. Collinearity checks were performed to screen for collinearity among covariates (Table S3).Adjusted logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the independent association between the Cardiometabolic Index (CMI) and OSA risk, after controlling for demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical confounders.To control for potential confounders that might affect the results, three models were used in this study:

Model 1: No covariates included for adjustment;

Model 2: Adjusted for age, sex, and race based on Model 1;

Model 3: Further adjusted for marital status (MARITAL), poverty-income ratio (POVERTY), educational attainment (EDU), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), smoking status (SMOKE), alcohol consumption (ALCOHOL), hypertension (HYPERTENSION), diabetes (DIABETES), coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke (STROKE), walking/bicycling habits (WALK_BICYCLE), sleep duration, and physical activity index (physical activity_total_MET) based on Model 2.

In the study, the Cardiometabolic Index (CMI) was grouped into equal quartiles (P25 = 0.78, P50 = 1.42, P75 = 2.66), with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group. Multivariate logistic regression models across 3 different models were used to analyze the association between CMI and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Meanwhile, curve fitting models and restricted cubic spline analysis (with 4 knots) were applied to verify the non-linear relationship between the two, and to fit the dose–response curve of CMI and OSA risk. Additionally, propensity score matching was adopted to verify the stability of results: demographic characteristics, underlying diseases, and other factors were used as covariates to calculate propensity scores, and subjects in the exposed group were matched with those in the non-exposed group. For the threshold effect test, segmented regression was employed—with the optimal breakpoint determined by the criterion of “highest model goodness-of-fit (lowest AIC value)”, and the statistical significance of effect differences before and after the breakpoint verified via likelihood ratio test. The log-likelihood ratio test was also used to evaluate model performance, quantify the threshold effect, and confirm its statistical significance. Finally, stratified analysis was conducted to explore the consistency of the association between CMI and OSA across different population subgroups and potential interaction effects. Missing values in the study were handled using multiple imputation, which involved constructing multiple complete datasets to fully utilize available information and reduce bias caused by missing values. All analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.4.3) with a two-tailed test, and the significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents information on 14,986 participants, including 4510 in the obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) group. The OSA group had a higher average age and a larger proportion of males. Compared with the non-OSA group, the OSA group showed significantly higher values in body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (105.27 ± 16.70 cm vs. 97.22 ± 15.60 cm, P < 0.001), and triglycerides (TG: 172.39 ± 149.31 vs. 149.13 ± 123.12, P < 0.001). In contrast, the level of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) in the OSA group was significantly lower than that in the non-OSA group, while there was no significant difference in total cholesterol (TC) between the two groups. Additionally, the prevalences of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, and stroke in the OSA group were all significantly higher than those in the non-OSA group. Data from Table S4 showed that as the cardiometabolic index (CMI) level increased significantly from Q1 to Q4, the prevalence of OSA gradually rose from 20.42 to 38.72% (P < 0.001). This study analyzed the correlation between CMI and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) using multiple logistic regression models (Table 2). In Model 1, CMI (treated as a continuous variable) was significantly associated with the risk of OSA (OR = 1.076, P < 0.001). In Model 3, CMI (still as a continuous variable) also showed a significant correlation with OSA risk (OR = 1.014, P = 0.024). Specifically, the OSA risk in the Q4 group (OR = 1.272, P < 0.001) was significantly higher than that in the Q1 group. A trend test indicated that as the CMI level increased, the OSA risk showed a significant linear upward trend (P < 0.001). Piecewise regression model analysis and nonlinear association analysis

Table 3 shows that the breakpoint (K) for the association between the Cardiometabolic Index (CMI) and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) risk was 2.03. In the range of CMI < 2.03, the CMI was significantly associated with an increased risk of OSA (OR = 1.50, 95% CI 1.40–1.60, P < 0.001). However, in the range of CMI > 2.03, the association between CMI and OSA risk was not statistically significant. Further results of the log-likelihood ratio test indicated that the threshold effect of this piecewise regression model was statistically significant (P < 0.001), suggesting that the association between CMI and OSA risk has a distinct threshold characteristic. From a clinical perspective, for individuals with CMI < 2.03 (accounting for approximately 50–75% of the population), each 1-unit increase in CMI was associated with a 50% higher risk of OSA (P < 0.001). For individuals with CMI ≥ 2.03 (those with CMI levels in the top 25% or so), there was no statistically significant association with OSA risk. This may reflect that, in this high-CMI subgroup, the saturated state of fat accumulation-related airway effects or survivor bias in the population with extremely high CMI might play a role in leading to OSA risk that exceeds that of CMI, thereby weakening the predictive ability of CMI.

Analyses were conducted on the association between the CMI index and OSA risk, as well as the balance of baseline characteristics before and after matching. Notably, in the subgroup without hypertension, each 1-unit increase in CMI was associated with a 10% higher OSA risk (OR = 1.10, P < 0.001), which was significantly higher than the risk increase in the subgroup with hypertension (OR = 1.06, P < 0.001). In the subgroup without stroke, there was a strong association between CMI and OSA risk (OR = 1.10, P < 0.001). In conclusion, the potential association between CMI and OSA risk may be stronger in populations without hypertension, without stroke, and particularly in females.

Discussion

Based on large-scale population data, this study systematically verified a potential association. The association is between the Cardiometabolic Index (CMI) and the risk of Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA). Segmented regression and nonlinear models were used for further analysis. They confirmed the nonlinear relationship between CMI and OSA risk. After Propensity Score Matching (PSM), logistic regression was applied again. It still showed that elevated CMI is an independent risk factor for OSA. Subgroup analysis was also conducted. It revealed consistent trends across all stratified groups. Notably, the association between CMI and OSA risk was potentially stronger in specific populations. These populations include those without hypertension or stroke, particularly females.

There is a significant nonlinear positive correlation in this research context. It exists between elevated CMI levels and increased susceptibility to OSA. Previous studies have provided support for this conclusion. For instance, research by Wang et al. demonstrated a specific association. It showed that elevated CMI is linked to the prevalence and clinical severity of OSA [22]. A cross-sectional analysis further found relevant connections. It indicated that elevated CMI is associated with two outcomes: an increased risk of sleep-related disorders and all-cause mortality. Specifically, individuals in the highest CMI quartile had a higher mortality risk. The risk was 34% higher compared to other groups (OR = 1.34, P = 0.021) [23]. Additionally, a study by Jia et al. presented consistent results. It showed that elevated CMI is associated with sleep disorders across different population cohorts [8]. While these findings align with the conclusions of this study, our research has unique contributions. It is the first to validate three key points in a large-scale population using rigorous methodology. The three points are: the threshold effect of CMI, subgroup-specific risk differences, and the significant association between CMI and OSA (which persists even after PSM). This work thereby deepens the understanding of OSA pathogenesis.

In clinical practice, traditional OSA screening methods have their characteristics. One such method is Polysomnography (PSG). It is accurate but has notable drawbacks: complex to operate, time-consuming, and costly. These drawbacks limit its widespread use. Specifically, it is not easily applied in primary healthcare settings or large-scale population screenings. In contrast, CMI has clear advantages in accessibility. CMI can be either a combination of indicators (like neck circumference and body mass index) or a single simple indicator. It requires no complex equipment or professional operation. Healthcare providers can quickly calculate it through simple measurements or questionnaires. CMI consists of two core components. The first is waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and the second is lipid metabolism. Lipid metabolism is represented by the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio [TG/HDL-C]. These components address a key limitation. They overcome the shortcomings of traditional single-parameter indicators in cardiometabolic assessment [24, 25]. A study by Tan W et al. provided relevant evidence. It indicated that serum HDL-C levels have a negative correlation with OSA risk (OR = 0.904, 95% CI 0.845–0.967) [26]. A Mendelian randomization analysis went further. It confirmed a causal relationship between HDL-C subtypes and OSA susceptibility [27]. Furthermore, Tang H et al. conducted a study with important findings. They found a direct causal association. It exists between genetically induced elevated triglyceride levels and increased OSA risk. This association persisted even after adjusting for multiple confounding factors [28]. Existing evidence points to a possible mechanism. Reduced triglyceride levels may lower OSA risk. This happens by influencing lipid-related pathophysiological mechanisms [29]. A study by Gasa M et al. highlighted a key point. It emphasized the epidemiological significance of a critical threshold for WHtR. This threshold is closely associated with increased OSA susceptibility [30]. Importantly, CMI has a unique function. It captures the interaction between two factors: visceral adiposity and dyslipidemia. These two factors are synergistic and contribute to cardiometabolic dysfunction. Our study found specific differences in OSA patients. They had higher waist-to-height ratios, higher triglyceride levels (149.13 ± 123.12 vs. 172.39 ± 149.31), and lower HDL-C levels (54.43 ± 16.50 vs. 50.45 ± 15.39). These differential characteristics highlight two key aspects. They underscore the diagnostic validity of CMI and its advantage in integrating multi-factor cardiometabolic risks. This thereby confirms CMI’s translational value in clinical and public health settings.

The association between CMI and OSA is mediated through multiple mechanisms. On one hand, CMI reflects visceral adiposity, which triggers two main effects: upper airway structural abnormalities and inflammatory responses. Specifically, visceral adiposity causes fat infiltration in peripharyngeal soft tissues. It also leads to backward displacement of the tongue base (which weakens tongue muscle contractility). These changes increase the risk of airway obstruction during sleep. Meanwhile, visceral fat releases pro-inflammatory factors. These factors activate systemic inflammatory responses. They also enhance oxidative stress in airway smooth muscle (disrupting anti-inflammatory homeostasis). Additionally, they induce airway mucosal edema and increased microvascular permeability. All these effects further exacerbate OSA [31–35]. On the other hand, OSA-related intermittent hypoxia brings about several consequences. It induces endoplasmic reticulum stress. It inhibits the conversion of white fat to brown fat (which aggravates metabolic disorders and elevates CMI). It also activates apoptosis-related pathways (impairing adipocyte function). Additionally, OSA-induced increased sympathetic nerve activity has an impact. It reduces upper airway muscle tone. When combined with respiratory control center dysfunction, this further triggers nocturnal respiratory events. These processes together form a bidirectional feedback loop [36–39].

Subgroup analysis yielded clear results. It showed consistent trends across all strata. A stronger association was observed between CMI and OSA risk in certain populations. These populations are those without hypertension or stroke, especially females. This phenomenon may be attributed to specific factors. These include potential biological or sociocultural mechanisms underlying gender and ethnic differences (e.g., hormonal regulation, visceral fat distribution, access to healthcare). Younger populations have distinct characteristics. Their declining metabolic compensation capacity and lifestyle factors may differ from other groups. Similarly, hormones (such as estrogen) may play a regulatory role. They may influence fat distribution and inflammatory responses [40–43]. Notably, a key observation was made in the stroke patient subgroup. No significant association between CMI and OSA was observed here. This may be explained by a specific mechanism. Cranial nerve dysfunction caused by stroke directly impairs the regulatory function of the respiratory center [44]. This impairment thereby masks the association between CMI and OSA.

It is important to note that this study has significant methodological advantages. It is the first to systematically validate a specific relationship in a large, representative U.S. cohort (NHANES database). The relationship is the nonlinear threshold between CMI and OSA. It identified a “high-impact range” of CMI. This range refers to the range of CMI values that have a strong impact on OSA risk. This provides actionable quantitative criteria for early clinical intervention. At the same time, it enriches the theoretical framework of the association between metabolic indicators and OSA. Stratified analysis also made a valuable contribution. It further clarified subgroup-specific risk differences. This facilitates more precise identification of high-risk populations. The main limitations of this study include the following: (1) The observational design of the study has a inherent constraint. It prevents causal inference between CMI and OSA. (2) Subgroup analysis has certain limitations that need to be noted. First, subgroup stratification involved multiple dimensions. These dimensions include gender, hypertension, stroke, age, and marital status. Second, multiple independent statistical tests were conducted. Importantly, no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons. This may increase the probability of Type I errors (false positives). As a result, it may lead to potential misjudgment of subgroup association differences (e.g., risk intensity differences between populations with and without comorbidities). Therefore, the reliability of some subgroup conclusions should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, subgroup analysis has a fundamental nature. It is inherently exploratory rather than pre-specified confirmatory. The results of subgroup analysis only serve one purpose: to indicate potential association characteristics. They cannot be directly used as definitive evidence for two key areas: clinical decision-making or causal inference. To address these limitations, future studies should take specific measures. They should expand sample sizes, adopt multiple comparison adjustment methods, or conduct independent external validation cohort studies. These steps will further verify the stability of subgroup conclusions. They will also reduce bias resulting from overinterpretation or chance findings. (3) The definition of OSA in this study has a potential issue. OSA was defined based on self-reported symptoms rather than objective PSG diagnosis. This may compromise the reliability of the results. The reason is the potential for misclassification bias. There are two types of misclassification bias to consider. First, non-differential misclassification (e.g., misjudgment caused by symptom recall bias) may have an effect. It may weaken the true association between CMI and OSA. This would shift the effect toward the null value. Second, differential misclassification (e.g., comorbidities affecting the accuracy of symptom reporting) may also impact results. It may distort the direction of the association. Both types of bias have a common consequence. They limit the ability to quantify causal associations between CMI and OSA. Moreover, self-reporting has inherent limitations in OSA diagnosis. It has limited sensitivity and specificity. Insufficient sensitivity leads to a specific problem. It causes misclassification of some true OSA cases (especially asymptomatic or mild cases) as non-OSA. Insufficient specificity also leads to an issue. It may misclassify non-OSA individuals as OSA. This can happen due to comorbid symptoms (e.g., chronic fatigue). If these scenarios are unrelated to CMI levels (i.e., non-differential misclassification), there may be further consequences. The true strength of the association between CMI and OSA may be further diluted or exaggerated. Additionally, missed diagnosis of asymptomatic OSA cases has a negative impact. It weakens the clarity of the threshold effect in the CMI-OSA association. This leads to underestimation of the threshold’s value. Specifically, it underestimates the value of the threshold in distinguishing true OSA populations. Therefore, future studies should design more rigorous research protocols. They should establish prospective cohorts that incorporate two key elements: objective PSG diagnosis and CMI trajectory analysis. These cohorts will further validate the relevant conclusions of this study.

Conclusion

This study is the first to verify a significant nonlinear positive correlation between the Cardiometabolic Index (CMI) and the risk of Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA). The OSA risk in the Q4 group (OR = 1.272, P < 0.001) was significantly higher than that in the Q1 group. Segmented regression showed that for CMI < 2.03, each 1-unit increase in CMI was associated with a 50% increase in OSA risk. After PSM matching, logistic regression still confirmed that elevated CMI is an independent risk factor for OSA. Subgroup analysis revealed consistent trends across all strata: the association between CMI and OSA risk was stronger in populations without hypertension or stroke (especially females), and CMI exhibited greater predictive value for OSA in younger populations and females. These findings suggest that CMI can serve as a convenient indicator for early OSA screening; however, additional prospective validations using polysomnography are required in the future.
