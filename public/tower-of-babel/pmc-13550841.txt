Perinatal Anxiety and Perceived Labor Control Among Parturients in the United Arab Emirates: A Cross-Sectional Study Guided by Roy’s Adaptation Model
Authors: Priyalatha Muthu, Amena Abdullah Abdulla Al Marzouqi, Manju Avinash Nair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerinatal anxiety is a significant public health concern that affects maternal well-being and childbirth outcomes. Women’s perceived control during labor is a key determinant of childbirth satisfaction and postnatal psychological health. Although maternal mental health has gained increasing global attention, limited research has explored the relationship between perinatal anxiety and labor control within the United Arab Emirates (UAE).PurposeThis study assessed perinatal anxiety levels and perceived labor control among antenatal mothers, examined associations with selected sociodemographic and clinical variables, and determined the correlation between these constructs using Roy’s Adaptation Model.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional design was used to recruit 169 antenatal mothers through convenience sampling at a UAE hospital. Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire, the Perinatal Anxiety Screening Scale (PASS), and the Labor Agentry Scale (LAS) administered 24 hours postpartum. Analyses included descriptive statistics, ANOVA, chi-square tests, and Pearson correlation.ResultsAmong participants, 40.8% exhibited mild-to-moderate anxiety, 39.1% severe anxiety, and 20.1% minimal anxiety. Regarding labor control, 45.6% reported moderate efficacy, 28.4% high efficacy, and 26% low efficacy. Age at marriage was significantly associated with anxiety (p < 0.05). Age, ethnicity, income, and parity were significantly associated with labor control (p < 0.05). Perinatal anxiety and labor control demonstrated a significant negative correlation (r = –0.579, p < 0.001).ConclusionPerinatal anxiety was common among Parturients and showed a strong inverse relationship with perceived labor control. These findings highlight the need for routine postpartum anxiety screening and early identification of women at risk for reduced labor control. Integrating structured psychological support and coping-enhancement strategies into maternity care may improve women’s childbirth experiences and contribute to better short- and long-term maternal outcomes.

Introduction

Childbirth represents one of the most transformative experiences in a woman’s life, encompassing profound physical, psychological, and social changes. The perinatal period—spanning pregnancy through the first year postpartum—constitutes a critical window during which women are particularly vulnerable to anxiety disorders. Contemporary research estimates that perinatal anxiety affects approximately 15–25% of women globally, although prevalence varies considerably across cultural and healthcare contexts.

The importance of maternal mental health during pregnancy has gained substantial recognition worldwide. Nielsen-Scott et al reported that self-reported anxiety symptoms were present in 29.2% of women during the antenatal period and 24.4% during the postnatal period.1 These findings align with evidence indicating that 37.6% of antenatal women exhibit symptoms suggestive of depression, while 28.7% demonstrate clinical levels of anxiety.2 The co-occurrence of anxiety and depression during the perinatal period compounds risks to maternal well-being and infant developmental outcomes.

Women’s perception of control during childbirth is a central determinant of birth satisfaction and postnatal psychological adjustment. Hodnett and Simmons-Tropea conceptualized feelings of control as a sense of mastery over internal and external forces through active, responsible, and self-directed decision-making.3 Green and Baston later expanded this framework, identifying three dimensions of labor control: control over healthcare professionals’ actions, control over one’s own behavior, and control during contractions. This multidimensional perspective provides a comprehensive understanding of women’s agency during labor.

The relationship between perinatal anxiety and labor control has important implications for clinical practice. Townsend et al demonstrated that childbirth satisfaction is associated with perceived control and physiological birth, while higher perceived control correlates with fewer postpartum anxiety symptoms.4 Similarly, Brandão et al found that elevated anxiety and depression levels were associated with diminished perceived labor control, suggesting a bidirectional relationship between these constructs.5

In the Gulf region, including the United Arab Emirates (UAE), cultural and religious values play an important role in shaping women’s expectations and preferences during childbirth. Islamic tradition frames childbirth as a blessing, and many women draw on faith-based coping strategies and family support during labor. However, empirical research examining how these cultural factors relate to psychological experiences—such as anxiety and perceived control—remains limited in the UAE context.

Understanding the interplay between perinatal anxiety and labor control within this sociocultural context is essential for developing culturally appropriate interventions.

Roy’s Adaptation Model served as the theoretical framework for this study, conceptualizing antenatal mothers as adaptive systems responding to childbirth-related stimuli. According to Roy, individuals are bio-psycho-social beings who interact continuously with changing environments through innate and acquired coping mechanisms. The model identifies four adaptive modes—physiological, self-concept, role function, and interdependence—through which adaptive or maladaptive responses manifest. Within this framework, heightened anxiety and diminished labor control may reflect maladaptive responses to focal, contextual, and residual stimuli.

Theoretical Framework

Roy’s Adaptation Model (1976)6 served as the conceptual framework guiding this investigation. The model views individuals as adaptive systems who respond to internal and external stimuli through coping processes that influence overall health and functioning. Its core concepts include the person as an adaptive system, the environment as the source of stimuli, health as the level of integrated adaptation, and nursing as the facilitation of adaptive responses. In this study, antenatal mothers are conceptualized as adaptive systems responding to the complex physiological, psychological, and social demands of childbirth. These demands arise from focal stimuli, such as the immediate physical and emotional challenges of labor; contextual stimuli, including sociodemographic and clinical characteristics; and residual stimuli, such as underlying cultural beliefs, past experiences, and personal expectations. Together, these stimuli activate both regulator (physiological) and cognator (cognitive–emotional) coping processes. Adaptive responses manifest across Roy’s four adaptive modes. The physiological mode reflects how women respond to the physical demands of labor, including pain, fatigue, and bodily changes. The self-concept mode encompasses emotional and psychological reactions, such as fear, confidence, or anxiety, as labor progresses. The role function mode relates to expectations surrounding the maternal role and the woman’s perceived ability to fulfill that role during childbirth. Finally, the interdependence mode captures the influence of support systems and relational dynamics, including the presence and involvement of partners, family members, and healthcare professionals.

In this study, adaptive outcomes are reflected in levels of perinatal anxiety, measured using the Perinatal Anxiety Screening Scale (PASS), and perceived labor control, measured using the Labor Agentry Scale (LAS). Feedback mechanisms within the model suggest that adaptive or maladaptive responses during childbirth may influence subsequent coping and adjustment throughout the perinatal period. Materials and Methods

Study Design

An analytical cross-sectional survey design was employed to assess perinatal anxiety levels and perceived labor control among parturients. Although data were collected at a single point in time, the design allowed examination of associations between psychological constructs (anxiety and labor control) and selected sociodemographic and clinical variables. This approach is appropriate for identifying patterns and relationships that can inform future hypothesis-driven or longitudinal research.

Study Setting

The study was conducted at Kanad Hospital, a 200-bed healthcare facility in Al Ain, United Arab Emirates. Established in 1960 and accredited by the Joint Commission International (JCI), the hospital has played a pivotal role in reducing infant and maternal mortality rates in Al Ain to below 1%. To date, it has facilitated more than 100,000 births. The labor and delivery unit consists of 16 beds and manages an average of 300 deliveries per month.

Participants

The study population comprised antenatal mothers admitted to the labor and delivery unit for childbirth. Using Rao software with a 5% margin of error, 95% confidence level, and a population size of 300, the required sample size was calculated as 169 participants. Convenience sampling was used to recruit eligible mothers.

Data Collection Procedure

Eligible parturients were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. The data collection tools included a demographic and clinical information sheet, the Perinatal Anxiety Screening Scale (PASS), and the Labor Agentry Scale (LAS). These self-administered questionnaires were completed within 24 hours postpartum in the labor and delivery unit to ensure consistency in recall of the childbirth experience.

Instruments

Demographic and Clinical Data Form

This form collected demographic information (age, age at marriage, ethnic group, nationality, income, and education) and clinical data (gestational age and parity).

Labor Agentry Scale (LAS) The Labor Agentry Scale, originally developed by Hodnett and Simmons Tropea, is a validated instrument for measuring women’s perceived control during labor. In this study, the shortened 10-item version was used to minimize respondent burden and ensure feasibility of administration within 24 hours postpartum, when women may experience fatigue and reduced attention span. The abbreviated LAS retains the core items that capture the construct of perceived labor control and has demonstrated psychometric properties comparable to the original 29-item scale. Its brevity therefore supported higher completion rates and reliable measurement without compromising validity.

Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale, yielding total scores ranging from 10 to 70, with higher scores indicating greater perceived control. The Arabic version of the LAS, validated by experts for cultural and linguistic appropriateness, demonstrated acceptable reliability in this study (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.86). Perinatal Anxiety Screening Scale (PASS)

Perinatal anxiety was measured using the Perinatal Anxiety Screening Scale (PASS) developed by Somerville et al (2014). The PASS is a validated 31-item instrument designed to identify clinically significant anxiety symptoms during the perinatal period. It covers domains of acute anxiety, general worry, perfectionism, and social anxiety. The Arabic version of the PASS, previously validated in regional populations, was used in this study to ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety. Standard cut-offs were applied: minimal anxiety (0–20), mild to moderate anxiety (21–41), and severe anxiety (≥42). Internal consistency for the Arabic PASS in this sample was high (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89), supporting its reliability.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of RAK Medical and Health Sciences University (approval no: RAKMHSU-REC-154-2024/25-PG-N) and the Kanad Hospital Research and Ethics Committee (Research authorization #2012.15). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Participants were informed of the voluntary nature of the study and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Unique identification codes ensured anonymity, and all data were stored securely with password protection. Data were used exclusively for research purposes.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. PASS and LAS scores met the assumptions of normality; therefore, parametric tests (ANOVA, independent t-tests, and Pearson correlation) were used to examine group differences and associations. To reduce the risk of Type I error arising from multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust the significance threshold for post-hoc analyses. The dataset was checked for missing values; no missing data were identified, and complete-case analysis was performed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 169 antenatal mothers participated in the study. Nearly half (49.1%) were aged 30–40 years, while 47.9% were between 20–30 years. Almost half of the participants (49.1%) reported marrying before the age of 20. The sample was predominantly of Arabic ethnicity (85.2%), and 61.5% were UAE nationals. Monthly family income ranged between 20,000 and 40,000 AED for 50.3% of participants, and the majority (90.5%) practiced Islam. Most women held a graduate-level education (59.8%), and 62.1% identified as housewives. Regarding clinical characteristics, 72.8% of participants were multiparous, and 76.9% were between 35 and 39 weeks of gestation at the time of admission. Discussion

This study examined perinatal anxiety levels and perceived labor control among antenatal mothers in the United Arab Emirates, applying Roy’s Adaptation Model as the guiding theoretical framework.7 The findings contribute important insights into maternal mental health during the perinatal period and have meaningful implications for clinical practice within this sociocultural context.

The demographic profile of participants reflects the multicultural composition of the UAE population. The predominance of multiparous mothers (72.8%) aligns with cultural values in Arab societies, where family and childbearing are highly emphasized.1,2 The high proportion of women with graduate-level education (59.8%) reflects the UAE’s national investment in women’s education, while the majority being housewives (62.1%) corresponds with traditional gender roles in Gulf countries.1

Perinatal Anxiety Prevalence and Associated Factors

Nearly 80% of participants exhibited mild to moderate or severe perinatal anxiety, a finding that warrants careful attention. Although this prevalence appears high, it is consistent with international evidence indicating that anxiety and related disorders are common during the perinatal period across diverse populations.8 The elevated rates observed in this study may also reflect the sensitivity of the PASS in capturing a broad spectrum of anxiety symptoms. The significant association between age at marriage and perinatal anxiety aligns with previous research. Sezgin and Punamäki found that early marriage and adolescent pregnancy adversely affected mental and somatic health.9 In the present study, 44.6% of women married before age 20 exhibited severe anxiety, suggesting that younger maternal age at marriage may predispose women to heightened anxiety during subsequent pregnancies. The markedly higher rate of severe anxiety among primiparous mothers (87%) compared to multiparous mothers (21.1%) corresponds with findings by Brunton et al, who identified parity as a significant predictor of pregnancy-related anxiety.10 Similarly, Huang et al reported that nulliparity was associated with heightened fear of childbirth among Chinese women.11 Within Roy’s Adaptation Model, these findings may reflect limited adaptive resources among first-time mothers when responding to focal stimuli during childbirth.7

Labor Control Efficacy and Associated Factors

Most participants (45.6%) demonstrated moderate labor control efficacy, suggesting that many women experienced a reasonable sense of control during childbirth. This aligns with findings by Siyoum and Mekonnen, who reported that 25.7% of their study population had low labor control.12 The significant association between ethnicity and labor control warrants consideration within the UAE’s diverse sociocultural landscape. Colley et al similarly found that women’s perceptions of support and control during childbirth were influenced by cultural factors.13 Income also demonstrated a significant association with labor control, supporting broader evidence linking socioeconomic status to maternal health outcomes. This finding aligns with Brandão et al, who reported that higher anxiety levels were associated with diminished perceived labor control,5 and with Townsend et al, who found that perceived control during childbirth was associated with fewer postpartum anxiety symptoms.4 Additional studies have shown that financial circumstances are strongly correlated with maternal prenatal anxiety14 and that a sense of control is linked to improved birth outcomes.15 The substantial disparity in labor control between primiparous and multiparous mothers—80.4% versus 5.7% reporting low efficacy—echoes recent evidence indicating that first-time mothers generally exhibit lower childbirth self-efficacy and reduced sense of control during labor.16

Relationship Between Perinatal Anxiety and Labor Control

The significant negative correlation between perinatal anxiety and labor control (r = −0.579, p < 0.001) represents a central finding of this study. This relationship is consistent with prior research demonstrating that higher anxiety levels are associated with diminished perceived labor control.5 Perceived control during childbirth has been shown to reduce postpartum anxiety symptoms,4 improve birth outcomes,15 and is influenced by psychosocial factors such as optimism and prior experience.17,18

Clinical Implications

These findings have important implications for clinical practice. The high prevalence of perinatal anxiety underscores the need for systematic screening during antenatal care.1,2,19,20 Evidence-based interventions, such as mindfulness programs, have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing labor-related anxiety.21 Bakhteh et al further identified several interventions effective in alleviating fear of childbirth.22 Childbirth preparation classes that emphasize coping strategies may be particularly beneficial for primiparous mothers, who demonstrated both higher anxiety and lower labor control.

Perinatal mental health programs should also consider the impact of pandemic-related stressors on women’s sense of control during childbirth,23,24 as well as the importance of culturally relevant social support systems.25 Integrating these approaches into routine maternity care may enhance maternal coping, strengthen perceived control during labor, and ultimately improve childbirth satisfaction and maternal outcomes.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of validated instruments with established reliability in Arabic and the application of Roy’s Adaptation Model, which provided a strong theoretical foundation for interpreting maternal responses to childbirth-related stimuli. It also contributes valuable evidence from the United Arab Emirates, a region where research on perinatal mental health and childbirth experiences remains limited.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to infer causal relationships between anxiety and labor control. Data were collected from a single hospital, which may limit generalizability to other settings. Convenience sampling may also introduce selection bias. The sample was restricted to full-term parturient undergoing normal vaginal delivery, excluding women with high-risk pregnancies or caesarean births, whose experiences may differ. Baseline anxiety levels prior to childbirth were not assessed, making it difficult to determine whether postpartum scores reflected pre-existing conditions or were influenced by the labor experience itself. Finally, reliance on self-reported measures may have introduced recall or response bias. Future studies employing longitudinal designs, multi-center recruitment, and more diverse samples would enhance understanding and external validity.

Conclusion

This study provides important evidence regarding perinatal anxiety and perceived labor control among antenatal mothers in the United Arab Emirates. The significant negative correlation between perinatal anxiety and labor control underscores the interconnected nature of maternal psychological well-being and childbirth experiences. The findings highlight the vulnerability of primiparous mothers, who demonstrated both higher anxiety and lower labor control, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions.

Healthcare providers should implement systematic perinatal anxiety screening and develop culturally appropriate, evidence-based strategies to strengthen maternal coping mechanisms. Integrating childbirth education, psychosocial support, and faith-sensitive counseling into routine maternity care may enhance women’s sense of control during labor. At the policy level, embedding perinatal mental health services into standard obstetric practice would contribute to improved childbirth satisfaction, better maternal outcomes, and the advancement of patient-centered care in the UAE.
