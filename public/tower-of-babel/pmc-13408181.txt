Sub-second extracellular impedance measurement of epithelial cell monolayers using step excitations and time-domain analysis
Authors: Rongming Guo, Athena J Chien, Jake Hawks, Benjamin Magondu, Bo Yang, Xavier Acevedo, Adrienne L Watson, Bob Lewis, Chris Hatcher, Craig R Forest
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is a versatile technique in epithelial biology, offering quantitative insights into barrier integrity, morphology, and apical-basolateral polarity non-invasively through measurements of transepithelial resistance (TER/TEER), capacitance (TEC), and membrane ratio (). However, due to broad-spectrum frequency sweeps, EIS typically demands tens of seconds per measurement, limiting its applicability to faster biological phenomena. We present Time-domain Epithelial Impedance Measurement (TEIM), a method enabling sub-second extracellular impedance measurements of epithelial monolayers by combining step current excitations and time-domain voltage transient analysis while bypassing Fourier transforms. We experimentally demonstrate TEIM’s ability to measure TER/TEER, TEC, , and model-derived impedance spectrum every 0.3 s, which represents 100-fold time resolution improvement over EIS. TEIM’s accuracy and precision were benchmarked against EIS using electrical circuits and human bronchial (16HBE) and colorectal (Caco-2) epithelial cell lines, yielding average errors for TER, TEC, and ranging from 0.17-3.55%, 1.13-8.96%, and 0.59-26.35%, respectively. Applying TEIM to monitor Caco-2 responses to saponin revealed, for the first time, smoothly gated double-exponential transient TER and TEC dynamics too rapid to be adequately captured by EIS. Overall, TEIM offers a capable framework for probing rapid cellular electrophysiology, and offers a high-resolution alternative for dynamic assays in biological and pharmacological research.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Biophysics, Biotechnology, Engineering

Introduction

Epithelial tissues play a crucial role in the human body by regulating the transport of nutrients and waste products while defending against pathogens. The ability to monitor their barrier function and ion transport dynamics, particularly in real time, is therefore essential for applications such as disease modeling, early infection diagnosis, and therapeutic development.

Epithelial electrophysiology provides a reliable and label-free framework to quantitatively investigate these biological processes. Specifically, barrier functions and the active transport of ions (Fig. 1 (A)) can be represented by equivalent electrical circuits of capacitors and resistors (Fig. 1 (B-C)). While high-fidelity models explicitly distinguish between apical/basolateral and paracellular/transcellular ion transport pathways (Fig. 1 (B)), their inherent mathematical redundancy necessitates both extracellular and intracellular measurements for a unique parameter solution – a process that is not only invasive but also technically delicate and time-consuming1,2. The five-parameter model in Fig. 1 (C), referred to as the RCRC model, provides a practical alternative by absorbing the paracellular resistance into membrane resistances, which allows its parameters to be uniquely determined using non-invasive extracellular measurements alone. Therefore, the RCRC model is used throughout this study. In this model, the transepithelial resistance (TER/TEER), capacitance (TEC), and membrane ratio values are defined as follows:

Various techniques have been established to characterize these epithelial circuits, including measurements of short-circuited current (Isc)3,4 and TEER4–6, as well as electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)2,6–9. Compared to Isc and TEER, which are foundational direct-current (DC)-based metrics that only reflect ion transport dynamics and cell layer confluency related solely to the cell resistance, EIS provides a more comprehensive characterization by applying alternating-current (AC) stimuli across a broad range of frequencies (e.g., 1 Hz - 100 kHz) to obtain the complex impedance spectrum, . Combined with equivalent circuit modeling, EIS extracts not only TEER, but also TEC, a quantity with a promising link to membrane morphology, adhesion and dielectric composition10,11, and membrane ratio (), which reflects the permeability and structural asymmetries between the apical and basolateral surfaces of many epithelial tissues9 and is particularly useful in pharmacological screenings by distinguishing the activity of ion channels located on specific surfaces of the membrane.

Schematic physiological model (A) and electrical circuit models (B-C) of epithelia, modified from Chien et al.9. The physiological model describes behavior of ions and other charged particles, including conduction through transcellular and paracellular pathways and spatial separation by the epithelial barrier (A). The seven-parameter circuit model provides very high physiological fidelity but requires an intracellular measurement at point a to be uniquely resolved (B). The five-parameter (RCRC) model offers relatively high physiological fidelity while requiring only extracellular measurements at point a and e and has been applied to model RPE, 16HBE, and Caco-2 cells6,7,9 (C).

A primary drawback of EIS, however, lies in its limited temporal resolution. While physiological mechanisms that modulate epithelial barrier functions and ion transport dynamics occur over a wide temporal spectrum (Fig. 2 (A)), ranging from days (e.g., cell division, maturation12,13, and structural degradation from chronic inflammation13,14) to hours (e.g., neutrophil transepithelial migration15,16 and the functional remodeling of the tight junctions (TJ)17,18) and down to sub-seconds (e.g., cooperative ion channel gating19,20), the speed of EIS remains significantly constrained. Specifically, depending on the frequency range and the number of cycle repetitions, the acquisition time of a single EIS impedance spectrum can take tens of seconds to over a minute on both custom platforms2,7,21 and commercial instruments such as the cellZscope® (nanoAnalytics, Münster, Germany) and Autolab® (Metrohm, Herisau, Switzerland). This is nearly an order of magnitude slower than TEER measurements, which can be completed within seconds using instruments like the EVOM® Manual (World Precision Instruments (WPI), Sarasota, FL, USA) or the ECIS® (Applied Biophysics, Troy, NY, USA)22. And indeed, the current literature is replete with examples in which the limited sampling rate of EIS fails to capture rapid biological responses with sufficient temporal details. For instance, in a 2023 study by Lewallen et al.2, the authors explicitly noted that a key shortcoming of their intracellular “3P-EIS” method is its limited measurement speed (2 min per measurement), which was too slow to resolve the impedance dynamics of human stem cell-derived retinal pigment epithelia (iPSC-RPE) in response to 100 M ATP; similarly, Bakhiari et al.23, using impedance spectroscopy with custom microfluidic chips at a 10 s sampling rate, clearly undersampled the initial rapid rise in membrane capacitance of human prostate carcinoma (PC-3) cells in response to amiodarone; furthermore, Linz et al.,7 while using EIS with STX-2 (WPI) chopstick electrodes to study human colorectal (Caco-2) cell response to Saponin, Forskolin, and EGTA, failed to capture any of the traces with sufficient temporal resolution. As illustrated in Fig. 2 (B) and (C), a critical tradeoff persists between the information richness of EIS and the speed of TEER, and an entire domain of fast biology is still currently inaccessible with EIS.

Duration (i.e., time constant) of biological events in epithelial cells (A) and time resolution of electrophysiology sensing technologies (B-D). Biological events in epithelia unfold across a wide range of timescales, varying from milliseconds to hours (A). TEER can be measured approximately every second22, and is suitable for events unfolding on the same order (B). EIS-based impedance measurements, while providing richer biological information through both TEER/TER and TEC, can take over one minute7,9, limiting their applicability to slower events unfolding over minutes to hours (C). TEIM extends impedance measurement time resolution to the near-second regime, allowing more rapid biological phenomena to be studied while preserving the information richness of EIS (D).

Since the 1870 s, time-domain transient analysis has been a cornerstone across engineering and life sciences for characterizing unknown dynamical systems, ranging from the classical mass-spring-damper (MSD) models in mechanical engineering24 to the Windkessel model in cardiovascular biology25. While similar concepts have also been explored for epithelial impedance analysis as early as 1946 by Teorell et al26 and 1982 by Suzuki et al27, these early efforts either remained largely at the conceptual level or were hindered by the computational constraints of that era which prevented real-time implementations. Building on these principles, we demonstrate that transitioning from frequency-domain (EIS) to time-domain analysis significantly accelerates epithelial impedance measurements. Specifically, by applying a step-current excitation and analyzing the transient transepithelial voltage, impedance can be calculated without Fourier transforms. Crucially, due to their intrinsic biological properties, epithelial cell layers exhibit time constants () on the order of milliseconds5,7,9, allowing transient responses to settle rapidly, thereby enabling sub-second, real-time measurements.

In this article, we introduce the theoretical framework and prototype implementation of a time-domain, step-excitation-based rapid epithelial impedance sensing technique, which we term Time-domain Epithelial Impedance Measurement (TEIM). Integrated with the commercial extracellular electrophysiology chamber EndOhm (WPI), our prototype TEIM-sensor achieves an average measurement interval of approximately 0.3 s, bridging the speed gap between EIS and TEER for the first time and effectively extending the temporal range of biological phenomena accessible to impedance-based studies by nearly two orders of magnitude (Fig. 2 (D)). We benchmarked the accuracy and precision of the TEIM-sensor with a gold-standard EIS implementation using both electrical circuit mimics and biological samples of human colorectal adenocarcinoma (Caco-2) and immortalized human bronchial epithelial cells (16HBE), yielding average percent errors for TER, TEC, and ranging from 0.17–3.55.17.55%, 1.13–8.96.13.96%, and 0.59–26.35.59.35%, respectively. To demonstrate its scientific utility, the TEIM-sensor was applied to monitor the rapid impedance dynamics of Caco-2 cells during apical exposure to 120g/mL saponin, revealing smoothly gated double-exponential transient TER and TEC dynamics for the first time.

Implementation of the TEIM-sensor

Hardware architecture

We present a prototype implementation of TEIM, referred to as the TEIM-sensor. As illustrated in the functional schematic in Fig. 4 (A), the system consists of a National Instruments (NI) USB-6451 Data Acquisition (DAQ) device (National Instruments, Austin, Texas, USA), an HP (Palo Alto, CA, USA) development computer with an Intel (Santa Clara, CA, USA) Core Ultra 9 185H CPU, a USB 3.1 Gen 2 SuperSpeed+ Cable, and a custom galvanostatic driver (WPI). Software and configurations

A Python® (Version 3.13.2, https://www.python.org/) program controls the DAQ, processes raw data, and executes the fitting algorithm. The step current magnitude is set to 10A, a standard amplitude for EIS or TEER implementations that is safe for most epithelial cells7,9,28. The DAQ’s AO channel is configured to operate at its maximum update rate of 250 kS/s, while the AI channels are configured to operate at 980kS/s (98% capacity). An oversampling (block-averaging) ratio of 49:1 is applied to AI channels to reduce Gaussian white noise, resulting in a final effective sampling rate of 20 KS/s.

Prior to continuous impedance measurements, the system needs to be calibrated using a “blank” transwell insert without cells in the EndOhm chamber. To calibrate, VCCS first commands 0A, and the voltages at OSCI (AI0) and OSCII (AI1) are captured once the waveforms stabilize. These non-zero voltages, typically ranging from several mV to the low tens of mV, reflect the combined hardware offsets arising from the EndOhm chamber, galvanostatic driver, and the DAQ, and are compensated in subsequent measurements. As an optional step to refine the accuracy of Rsol estimation during curve fitting, a 10A test pulse may then be used to independently calculate of Rsol using Ohm’s law.

Following calibration, the measurement loop begins. As shown in Fig. 4 (C), each cycle can be divided into a 0.2 s data acquisition phase and a 0.1 s analysis phase. During the first 0.1 s (e.g., from s to s), the VCCS outputs 0A to ensure all capacitive elements within the system are fully discharged (zero initial condition); during the subsequent 0.1 s (e.g., from s to s), the VCCS current is stepped to 10A, inducing a transient transepithelial voltage response.

The analysis phase begins afterwards. The captured transepithelial voltage response is input to a numerical fitter that solves the optimization problem defined in in Equation 13 to estimate the underlying circuit parameters. From these results, TER, TEC, and values are calculated, and a Nyquist impedance spectrum is reconstructed according to Equation 14 at frequency locations of interest, as shown in Fig. 4 (D). To minimize polarization of both the cell sample and the electrodes, the direction of the current is reversed between successive cycles.

Materials and reagents

Electrical circuit mimics

To isolate inherent biological variability of live cells and facilitate controlled hardware testing, electrical circuit mimics of epithelia cell monolayers were constructed on a breadboard using axial-lead metal film resistors and aluminum electrolytic capacitors adhering to the RCRC topology shown in Fig. 1 (C). The component values are selected within the reported5,7,9 and observed ranges of the specific epithelial cell type being modeled.

Epithelial cell culture

We selected Caco-2 and 16HBE as representative epithelial cell lines for this study as both have been well studied with existing electrophysiology techniques such as TEER and EIS7,13. Caco-2 cells (ATCC HTB-37TM) were obtained at passage 15 and cultured in T-25 flasks according to protocols provided by SynVivo Inc. (Huntsvile, AL, USA). The complete Caco-2 growth medium consisted of 79% RPMI 1640 medium (ATCC Modification, ThermoFisher Scientific, catalog no. A1049101), 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (ThermoFisher, catalog no. A3840002) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Pen/Strep, Gibco 15070-063). 16HBE cells (MilliporeSigma, SCC150) were cultured according to protocols published by the CFFT lab29, except that flasks were not coated with collagen. The 16HBE growth medium consisted of 90% MEM (Gibco, catalog no. 11095-080), 10% FBS (Corning, catalog no. 35-015-CV), and the same 1% Pen/Strep.

Cells were cultured for at least 1 week prior to plating for experiments and used up to 12 weeks after thawing from frozen aliquots. Cells were plated on 1.12 , 0.4 m pore size transwells® (3460, Corning) at a density of roughly 250k cells (16HBE) and 180k cells (Caco2) per well and maintained at liquid-liquid interface with 0.5 mL medium in the apical chamber and 1 mL in the basolateral chamber. Media changes were performed three times a week, and cells were passaged upon reaching >80% confluency.

Saponin

Saponin is a naturally occurring amphipathic glycoside that can rapidly increase the permeability and alter cytoskeletal structure of epithelial cell layers by binding to their cholesterol-rich lipid bilayers and forming pores, leading to abrupt changes in both TER and TEC7,30. Therefore, it is selected as the ideal chemical reagent to demonstrate the advantages of increased sampling rate brought forth by TEIM. To prepare the stock saponin solution, 0.05 g of saponin powder (ThermoFisher Scientific, catalog no. A18820.14) was dissolved in pre-warmed (37.5°C) complete Caco-2 growth medium to a final volume of 10 mL, yielding a concentration of 0.5% w/v, or 5 mg/mL. During experiments, the stock solution was further diluted upon addition to the apical chamber of the transwell.

Results

Accuracy and precision of the TEIM-sensor

We validated the accuracy and precision of the TEIM-sensor by benchmarking its performance against gold-standard references on both electrical circuit mimics and biological samples of Caco-2 and 16HBE cells. These two cell lines were chosen because they capture two distinct fundamental impedance profiles, exhibiting single-arch (Caco-2) and double-arch (16HBE) Nyquist plots, respectively.

For electrical circuit mimics ( for each cell type), the ground-truth references were obtained through a FLUKE 289 True-RMS Multimeter (Fluke Corporation, Everett, WA, USA). Following the multimeter measurements, triplicate TEIM-sensor measurements were performed once every minute. For biological samples ( for each cell type), ground-truth references were established according to the galvanostatic EIS protocol by Chien et al.9, which uses a Metrohm Multi Autolab (Metrohm AG, Herisau, Switzerland) with the same EndOhm chamber and a 66-point frequency sweep spanning 2 Hz to 50 kHz. Prior to data collection, cells were stabilized outside the incubator for 15 minutes to mitigate transient impedance drift caused by temperature and CO2 level fluctuations. Triplicate ground truth (EIS) and TEIM-sensor measurements were performed in an alternating sequence in the same chamber for each sample (EIS 1 st TEIM-sensor 1 st EIS 2nd ...). To match the 1-minute EIS acquisition window, corresponding TEIM-sensor data were acquired continuously for 1 minute as well, yielding 180 data points.

Table 1 summarizes the accuracy and precision of the TEIM-sensor, where accuracy is reported as the percentage error relative to the ground truth and precision is represented by the coefficient of variation among the repeated measurements. Overall, the TEIM-sensor demonstrated higher accuracy and precision on electrical circuit mimics than on biological samples, with the best performance achieved on 16HBE circuit mimics, yielding percentage errors below 2% and coefficients of variation below 1% across TER, TEC, and . Meanwhile, although the TER percentage errors remained below 5% across the subjects, the TEC errors were generally larger, peaking at 8.96% for biological samples of 16HBE. Furthermore, both accuracy and precision for were significantly higher for 16HBE than for Caco-2 on both electrical and biological subjects.

Consistent with the observations in Table 1, TER values are generally statistically indistinguishable (p > 0.05) between EIS and TEIM, with one exception of Caco-2 replicate 3 (Fig. 5 (A)). Conversely, measurements for Caco-2 frequently yield p < 0.05. This can largely be attributed to the degradation in both the accuracy and precision of the TEIM sensor for this particular cell line. Notably, a similar decrease in precision is also observed in EIS measurements of for Caco-2, and a direct comparison between the EIS and TEIM impedance spectra reveals a relatively close agreement at 10% MAPE (Fig. 5 (B)). A formal sensitivity analysis (detailed in the Supplemental Information) indicates that these phenomena – the degradation of both TEIM and EIS’s ability to measure in Caco-2 – stem primarily from the numerical fitting process and the optimization landscape itself, where the error surface of the objective function becomes increasingly “flat” near the Caco-2 region, making accurate and precise convergence more challenging. Across all subjects and replicates, TEC values are consistently overestimated, with the magnitude reaching statistical significance (p < 0.05) in four out of six cases (Figs. 5 (A) and 6 (A)). This systematic bias can be attributed to a hardware limitation of the galvanostatic driver, which cannot generate an instantaneous step current due to a finite slew rate; as a result, it introduces an artificial slow-down effect in the transepithelial voltage response, causing the numerical fitter to assign a greater capacitance value. Furthermore, the increased discrepancies between EIS- and TEIM-measured impedance spectra for 16HBE (Fig. 6 (B)) suggest that the RCRC model slightly loses its fidelity in representing the more complicated electrophysiological characteristics of this specific cell line.

Finally, it is worth noting that a low p-value in this case often arises because both TEIM and EIS are remarkably precise rather than a lack of accuracy in the TEIM sensor. In several instances, such as replicate 1 of 16HBE (Fig. 6 (A)), a percentage error of 4.40% in still yielded sub-0.05 p-values due to exceedingly narrow whiskers. In biological assays, such marginal errors are typically considered negligible.

Real-time monitoring of saponin-induced transient dynamics on Caco-2

To demonstrate the advantages of the high sampling rate enabled by the TEIM-sensor, we continuously monitored the real-time TER and TEC dynamics of Caco-2 monolayers during apical exposure to saponin.

Six replicate samples were cultured to maturation, with three (sample 4–6) assigned to the experimental group and three (sample 1–3) serving as controls. Two hours prior to the experiment, baseline TER and TEC values of all samples were verified using the TEIM-sensor to be around 1200 and 4.5 . To facilitate reagent addition later on, 12 L of media was then removed from the apical chambers (resulting in a final apical volume of 488L) before samples were returned to the incubator for stabilization. Prior to each trial, cells placed into the EndOhm and allowed to stabilize for 5 minutes. 12 L of 0.5% w/v stock saponin solution (experimental) or growth medium (control) was then quickly added to the apical chamber. This resulted in an effective final saponin concentration of 120g/mL in the experimental group, a value within the range established in previous studies, which varies from 10–40 g/mL at the lower end30 and 500 g/mL at the higher end7. Following addition, the TEIM-sensor continuously acquired impedance data for approximately 15 minutes at an average sampling interval of 0.31 s.

The measured relative TER and TEC dynamics (normalized to the first value recorded) are plotted in Fig. 7 (A). To mitigate system noise, data were processed with a 10-sample moving-average filter with a window of 3 seconds. As shown, relative TER in the experimental group declined sharply following a brief initial onset, before reaching an inflection point after approximately 1–2 minutes and slowly approaching a final value close to 0.05. On the other hand, relative TEC exhibited a longer onset period, before increasing asymptotically to a final value between 2 and 3. The resulting fitted parameters are summarized in Table 2, and a representative trace fitted using single- and double-exponential functions are shown in Fig. 7 (B) and (C), respectively. Several tentative biological interpretations may be be drawn from these results. First, a vertical comparison between subplot (B) and (C) indicates that double-exponential captures the saponin-induced dynamics with significantly higher accuracy, and the single-exponential model fails to accurately capture the dynamics particular during the period of inflection (between t = 50 s and t = 400 s). This has been verified to be true across all experimental subjects, and is reflected by the values in Table 2 that are consistently higher for the double-exponential fits. Focusing on the double-exponential fitting results, the value differences across (e.g., 29.44 s vs. 43.01 s in replicate 1), (e.g., 0.02 s s vs. 25.13 s in replicate 1), and (e.g., 321.68 s vs. 542.62 s in replicate 1) between TER and TEC dynamics suggest that saponin likely influenced the permeability and morphology of the cell layer through two distinct pathways. Furthermore, the presence of two exponential modes with significantly different time constants (Table 2) within both TER and TEC dynamics themselves suggests the possibility of multiple sub-processes within each pathway. While speculative, these interpretations demonstrate the possibilities for scientific discovery brought forth by TEIM.

As a result, the multi-stage multi-mode curve-fitting analysis presented above might not have been possible, and the associated biological interpretations might never have been drawn.

Notably, our 120 g/mL concentration was optimized to balance biological response speed and signal-to-noise ratio; a higher concentration at 500g/mL caused an excessive drop of TER to single-digit ohms, rendering the transepithelial voltage signal indistinguishable from the electrical noise in the system, subsequently causing the fitter to fail.

Discussion

This study presents an alternative method for extracellular impedance measurements of epithelial cell monolayers based on step current excitations and time-domain analysis of the transient voltage responses. Due to epithelial membranes’ inherently low resistances and capacitances, such transient responses settle extremely fast, allowing measurements to be performed at sub-second rates – nearly two orders of magnitude faster than the frequency-domain-based EIS. The accuracy and precision results are promising, and a representative application with saponin shows its potential to facilitate scientific discoveries.

Admittedly, several limitations exist for both theoretical and practical aspects of TEIM. From a theoretical perspective, the sub-second measurement speed of TEIM fundamentally relies on a rapid convergence of the voltage transient. Certain cell types, such as the blood-brain barrier (BBB), can exhibit TER values exceeding 5000 cm25,31, which slows down their electrochemical dynamics, demanding longer settling times of the transepithelial voltage step response, and subsequently reducing measurement speed. In addition, certain cell lines, such as 16HBE, cannot be perfectly described by the RCRC circuit model, as evidenced by the nearly 15% MAPE values in Fig. 6 (B). In such cases, EIS (gray traces) still reliably measures the impedance spectrum without imposing specific model assumptions, whereas TEIM (blue traces) can distort the spectrum ever so slightly by force-fitting the time-domain data to a predefined circuit model. However, the extracted TER, TEC, and values are not inherently inferior to those obtained by EIS, since they too rely on model fitting. Furthermore, the dynamics modeling in the Method section can always be extended to incorporate alternative circuit models tailored to different cell types. Indeed, we have derived the time-domain expression of the step voltage response for the more complicated circuit model presented in Fig. 1 (B), and the details are provided in the supplementary materials.

We believe that expanding this time-domain framework to the intracellular domain represents an exciting future direction, and we encourage fellow researchers to further explore that route based on these provided expressions.

From an implementation standpoint, one primary limitation of the TEIM-sensor is the practical impossibility to generate a true current step due to the finite slew rates of real-world galvanostatic drivers. In fact, many inaccuracies in the estimation of Rsol and TEC can be precisely attributed to this issue. As shown in Fig. 3 (A), the value of Rsol is associated with the initial instantaneous rise of the transepithelial voltage. This is already difficult to capture with discrete-sampling hardware, and can be further distorted by the finite current slew rate. One potential solution to these hardware limitations, and a promising direction for future work, is to use a ramp or parabolic excitation current, which provides smoother transitions while still allowing enough transient characteristics to be revealed.

Nevertheless, within most operating conditions, TEIM provides impedance measurements that are quantitatively comparable to EIS. In summary, this method enables real-time impedance monitoring of rapid cellular changes that are difficult to resolve using other systems, such as EIS, due to limited sampling rates, and establishes a foundation for future works in the space of epithelial electrophysiology.
