Abstract
We investigate implications of the (extended) low-degree conjecture (recently formalized in [ MW23 ] ) in the context of the symmetric stochastic block model. Assuming the conjecture holds, we establish that no polynomial-time algorithm can weakly recover community labels below the Kesten-Stigum (KS) threshold . In particular, we rule out polynomial-time estimators that, with constant probability, achieve correlation with the true communities that is significantly better than random. Whereas, above the KS threshold, polynomial-time algorithms are known to achieve constant correlation with the true communities with high probability [ Mas14 , AS15 ] . To our knowledge, we provide the first rigorous evidence for the sharp transition in recovery rate for polynomial-time algorithms at the KS threshold. Notably, under a stronger version of the low-degree conjecture, our lower bound remains valid even when the number of blocks diverges. Furthermore, our results provide evidence of a computational-to-statistical gap in learning the parameters of stochastic block models. In contrast to prior work, which either (i) rules out polynomial-time algorithms for hypothesis testing with 1 − o ⁡ ( 1 ) 1-o(1) success probability [ Hop18 , BBK+21a ] under the low-degree conjecture, or (ii) rules out low-degree polynomials for learning the edge connection probability matrix [ LG24 ] , our approach provides stronger lower bounds on the recovery and learning problem. Our proof combines low-degree lower bounds from [ Hop18 , BBK+21a ] with graph splitting and cross-validation techniques. In order to rule out general recovery algorithms, we employ the correlation preserving projection method developed in [ HS17 ] .

Concurrent work

A concurrent work
[
SW25
]
also provides rigorous evidence for computational lower bound below KS threshold based on low-degree polynomials.
They give the first unconditional low-degree recovery lower bound below the KS threshold in the stochastic block model.
In our setting, for the task of weak recovery (which is to say, achieving constant recovery rate), their lower bound directly rules out estimators based on degree
n^{\delta}
polynomials, which captures many natural candidates of algorithms.
Such lower bound is beyond reach with techniques from
[
Hop18
,
BBK+21a
]
.
For this, they introduce significantly new techniques in analyzing the low-degree polynomials.

In comparison, our main contribution lies in revealing a potential sharp transition of recovery rate for polynomial time (and sub-exponential time) algorithms,
below and above the KS threshold. We give evidence that sub-exponential time algorithms cannot achieve recovery rate
n^{-0.49}
with constant probability below the KS threshold,
while polynomial time algorithms are known to achieve weak recovery above the threshold.
Our techniques is based on relating the rate of recovery to the low-degree conjecture formulated in
[
MW23
]
.
Notably, we did not prove new low-degree lower bounds for our main results, but exploited the existing results from
[

,
BBK+21a
]

In a concurrent work,
[
Li25
]
established computational lower bounds for random graph matching below sharp thresholds, also based on a strengthened version of the low-degree conjecture.
However, their polynomial-time reduction between hypothesis testing with lopsided success probability and recovery differs significantly from ours.
Additionally, they formalize a standard framework for such reductions, which has the potential to be applied to a broader class of problems.

As we discuss next, our result also has implications for other learning tasks in the stochastic block model.

R_{P,Q}(f)\lesssim\max_{\text{deg}(f)\leqslant n^{\delta}}R_{P,Q}(f)\,.

\max_{\text{deg}(f)\leqslant n^{\delta}}R_{P,Q}(f)\leqslant O(1)
, the conjecture is reduced to
1.3
.
The extended low-degree hypothesis is closely related to the low-degree lower bound for random optimization problems (see
[
GJW24
]
).

4
Techniques

4.1
Lower bounds for weak recovery

In this section, we give an overview of the techniques we use to prove lower bounds for weak recovery in the stochastic block model with constant number of blocks
k
and constant average degree
d
. That is, an overview of our proof of (a special case of)
Theorem

2.1
.

Suppose for a contradiction that we have a polynomial-time recovery algorithm for
\text{SSBM}(n,\frac{d}{n},\varepsilon,k)
,
\varepsilon^{2}d\leqslant 0.99k^{2}
, that achieves recovery rate
\delta\geqslant\Omega(n^{-0.49})
, in the sense of
Definition

1.2
.
Using this algorithm, we will construct a function
f(\cdot)
, which can be evaluated in polynomial time, such that
R_{P,Q}(f)\geqslant n^{\Omega(1)}
. Here,
R_{P,Q}(\cdot)
is the parameter (
1.1
) for the distributions
P=\text{SSBM}(n,\frac{d}{n},\varepsilon,k)
and
Q=\mathbb{G}(d,n)
. Assuming the low-degree conjecture (Conjecture
1.3
) for this
P
and
Q
, this implies that there exists a low-degree polynomial
f^{\prime}
with
R_{P,Q}(f^{\prime})\geqslant\omega(1)
. But, since
\varepsilon^{2}d\leqslant 0.99k^{2}
is below the KS threshold, this leads to a contradiction
with the low-degree lower bound of
[
Hop18
]
(
Theorem

3.1
).

In the remainder of this section, we show how to construct the function
f(\cdot)
, and give a sketch of the proof that
R_{{P,Q}}(f)\geqslant n^{\Omega(1)}
.

Regularization via correlation-preserving projection.

We begin with the following tool, which allows us to regularize the estimators of the community membership matrix
M^{\circ}
provided by the recovery algorithm.
Suppose that
\hat{M}_{0}
is a matrix achieving correlation
\delta
with
M^{\circ}
, i.e., satisfying
\langle\hat{M}_{0},M^{\circ}\rangle\geqslant\delta\lVert\hat{M}_{0}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
.
We show that
\hat{M}_{0}
can be projected into a (small) convex set
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
containing
M^{\circ}
, while preserving the correlation (up to a constant). Concretely, the convex set
\mathcal{K}
here is given by

\mathcal{K}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{M\in[-1/\delta,1/\delta]^{n\times n}:M+\frac{1}{k\delta}\Ind\Ind^{\top}\succeq 0\,,\Tr(M+\frac{1}{k\delta}\Ind\Ind^{\top})\leqslant n/\delta}}\right\}.

(4.1)

In particular, elements of
\mathcal{K}
have bounded entries and bounded nuclear norm, which will be crucial in later steps of the proof where we apply a Bernstein inequality.
To achieve this, we make use of the
correlation preserving projection
from
[
HS17
]
(see
Theorem

E.1
), which projects
\hat{M}_{0}
onto a matrix
\hat{M}\in\mathcal{K}
satisfying

\langle\hat{M},M^{\circ}\rangle\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\delta\lVert\hat{M}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}},

In addition,
Theorem

E.1
promises that
\lVert\hat{M}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=\Theta(\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}})
. Thus, we find that

\langle\hat{M},M^{\circ}\rangle\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\delta\lVert\hat{M}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\delta\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\geqslant\delta\cdot\Omega(n^{2}).

(4.2)

Importantly, the correlation preserving projection can be implemented in polynomial time via semidefinite programming. See
Lemma

A.5
for details.

Testing statistics via cross validation.

The basic idea is to construct
f(\cdot)
via cross validation.
Given a random graph
G
, we construct a subgraph
G_{1}
with the same vertex set by subsampling each edge in
G
independently with probability
1-\eta
, where
\eta>0
is a small constant. Note that if
G
is drawn from
\text{SSBM}(n,\frac{d}{n},\varepsilon,k)
, then
G_{1}
is distributed according to
\text{SSBM}(n,(1-\eta)\frac{d}{n},\varepsilon,k)
. If
G
is drawn from
\mathbb{G}(n,\frac{d}{n})
, then
G_{1}
is distributed according to
\mathbb{G}(n,(1-\eta)\frac{d}{n})
. We run the polynomial-time recovery algorithm on
G_{1}
to obtain an estimate
\hat{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
of the community membership matrix
M^{\circ}
, which we regularize using the correlation preserving projection discussed above. Let
Y_{2}
denote the adjacency matrix of
G_{2}:=G\setminus E(G_{1})
.
Our function
f(\cdot)
is then defined as

f(Y):=\begin{cases}1,\quad&\text{if }\langle\hat{M},Y_{2}-\frac{\eta d}{n}\mathbb{1}\mathbb{1}^{\top}\rangle\geqslant n^{0.51},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(4.3)

See
Algorithm

4.1
for an overview of the construction of
f(\cdot)
.
It remains to show that
{R_{P,Q}(f)\geqslant n^{\Omega(1)}}
. For this, we establish a
lower bound
on the expectation
\E_{Y\sim P}f(Y)
of
f
under graphs drawn from the SBM and an
upper bound
on the expectation
\E_{Y\sim Q}f(Y)
of
f
under Erdős-Rényi random graphs.

Algorithm 4.1

(Test function
f(\cdot)
used in the proof of
Theorem

2.1
)
.

Input:
A graph
G
with
n
vertices, given by its adjacency matrix
Y
.

Output:
Test function
f(Y)\in\{0,1\}
.

Algorithm:

1.

Obtain a subgraph
G_{1}
of
G
by subsampling each edge with probability
1-\eta
.

2.

Obtain an estimator
\hat{M}_{0}
by running a recovery algorithm on the graph
G_{1}
.

3.

Obtain
\hat{M}
by projecting
\hat{M}_{0}
onto the set
\mathcal{K}
defined in (
4.1
) using the correlation preserving projection.

4.

Return
f(Y)=\mathbb{1}\{\langle\hat{M},Y_{2}-\frac{\eta d}{n}\mathbb{1}\mathbb{1}^{\top}\rangle\geqslant n^{0.51}\}
, where
Y_{2}
is the adjacency matrix of
G\setminus E(G_{1})
.

Lower bound on the expectation under the SBM.

First, we give a lower bound on the expectation of
f
under the distribution
\text{SSBM}(n,\frac{d}{n},\varepsilon,k)
, i.e, on

\E_{Y\sim P}f(Y)=\ProbOp_{Y\sim P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\langle\hat{M},Y_{2}-\frac{\eta d}{n}\mathbb{1}\mathbb{1}^{\top}\rangle\geqslant n^{0.51}}}\right].

To do so, note that we may decompose
Y_{2}-\frac{\eta d}{n}\mathbb{1}\mathbb{1}^{\top}=\frac{\varepsilon\eta d}{n}M^{\circ}+W_{2}
, where
W_{2}
is a random matrix whose entries are independent with mean zero. Then, we have

\langle Y_{2}-\frac{\eta d}{n}\mathbb{1}\mathbb{1}^{\top},\hat{M}\rangle=\frac{\varepsilon\eta d}{n}\langle M^{\circ},\hat{M}\rangle+\langle W_{2},\hat{M}\rangle\,.

The first term on the RHS above is large with constant probability by (
4.2
). We would like to apply a Bernstein inequality to the second term, but the matrices
W_{2}
and
\hat{M}
are not independent. However, as we show in
Lemma

D.3
, they are
approximately independent
in the sense that there exists a
zero-mean symmetric matrix
\tilde{W}_{2}
with independent entries, independent of
\hat{M}
, so that each entry in
\tilde{W}_{2}-W_{2}
has variance bounded by
O(d^{2}/n^{2})
.

For ease of presentation, we ignore the difference between
\tilde{W}_{2}
and
W_{2}
for now, and assume that
W_{2}
and
\hat{M}
are independent.
In this case, we note that
\langle{W}_{2},\hat{M}\rangle
can be written as the summation of independent zero-mean random variables, namely

\langle{W}_{2},\hat{M}\rangle=\sum_{i,j}{W}_{2}(i,j)\hat{M}(i,j)\,,

where
{W}_{2}(i,j)\hat{M}(i,j)\leqslant O(1/\delta)
for each
i,j\in[n]
. (Here, we have used that
\hat{M}\in\mathcal{K}
).
Moreover, since
\lVert\hat{M}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\Theta(\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})=\Theta(n^{2})
, we have

\sum_{i,j}\hat{M}(i,j)^{2}\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[{W}_{2}(i,j)^{2}}}\right]\lesssim\frac{d}{n}\sum_{i,j}\hat{M}(i,j)^{2}\leqslant O(n^{2}\cdot\frac{d}{n})=O(nd)\,.

By the Bernstein inequality, and using the fact that
\delta\geqslant\Omega(n^{-0.49})
, we then have

\ProbOp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{i,j}{W}_{2}(i,j)\hat{M}(i,j)\geqslant n^{0.501}}}\right]\leqslant\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-n^{0.001}}}\right)\,.

As result, when
d\leqslant O(1),k\leqslant O(1),\eta=\Theta(1)
, with constant probability, we have

\langle Y_{2}-\frac{\eta d}{n},\hat{M}\rangle\geqslant\frac{\eta\varepsilon d}{n}\langle M^{\circ},\hat{M}\rangle-n^{0.501}\gtrsim\delta n-n^{0.501}\gtrsim n^{0.51}-n^{0.501}\geqslant\Omega(n^{0.51})\,.

Therefore, we have
\E_{Y\sim P}f(Y)\geqslant\Omega(1)
.

Upper bound under the null distribution.

Next, we give an upper bound on the expectation of
f
under the Erdős-Rényi distribution
\mathbb{G}(n,\frac{d}{n})
. Our proof shares many ingredients with the proof of the lower bound in the previous section. We show that with high probability under the distribution
\mathbb{G}(d,n)
, we have

g(Y):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\langle Y_{2}-\frac{\eta d}{n}\mathbb{1}\mathbb{1}^{\top},\hat{M}\rangle}}\right\rvert=o(n^{0.51})\,.

To do so, we again apply the argument that
Y_{2}-\frac{\eta d}{n}\mathbb{1}\mathbb{1}^{\top}
and
\hat{M}
are approximately independent.
In particular, let
W_{2}=Y_{2}-\frac{\eta d}{n}\mathbb{1}\mathbb{1}^{\top}
, for some i.i.d. zero-mean symmetric matrix
\tilde{W}_{2}
, independent of
\hat{M}
, so that each entry in
\tilde{W}_{2}-W_{2}
has variance bounded by
d^{2}/n^{2}
.
By the triangle inequality, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\langle Y_{2}-\frac{\eta d}{n}\mathbb{1}\mathbb{1}^{\top},\hat{M}\rangle}}\right\rvert\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\langle W_{2}-\tilde{W}_{2},\hat{M}\rangle}}\right\rvert+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\langle\tilde{W}_{2},\hat{M}\rangle}}\right\rvert\,.

For simplicity, we again ignore the difference between
\tilde{W}_{2}
and
W_{2}
here.
By the same reasoning as above, and again relying on the properties of
\hat{M}
guaranteed by the correlation preserving projection, we have the Bernstein inequality

\ProbOp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\langle\tilde{W}_{2},\hat{M}\rangle}}\right\rvert\geqslant n^{0.501}}}\right]\leqslant\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-n^{0.01}}}\right)\,.

As result, when
d\leqslant O(1)
and
k\leqslant O(1)
, we have
g(Y)\leqslant o(n^{0.501})
with probability at least
1-\exp(-n^{0.01})
and thus
\E_{Y\sim Q}f(Y)\leqslant\exp(-n^{0.01})
.

Finishing the proof

Using the lower and upper bound established above, and the fact that
f(\cdot)\in\{0,1\}
, we get that

\frac{\E_{Y\sim P}f(Y)-\E_{Y\sim Q}f(Y)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}_{Y\sim Q}(f(Y))}}\geqslant\frac{\Omega(1)}{\exp(-n^{0.01})}\geqslant\exp(n^{0.01})\geqslant\omega(1).

4.2
Lower bound for learning the stochastic block model

In this section, we give an overview of the techniques used to prove our results on learning the stochastic block model, stated in
Section

2.2
.

Lower bound for learning edge connection probability matrix

We sketch the proof of
Theorem

2.4
.
We show that if an
O(\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{0.99}}}\right))
-time algorithm can learn the edge connection probability matrix
\theta^{\circ}
such that with constant probability, the error rate
\lVert\hat{\theta}-\theta^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant 0.99kd
, then an algorithm with running time
\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{0.99}}}\right)
can achieve weak recovery when
\varepsilon^{2}d\geqslant 0.99k^{2}
.
The key observation is that, for the symmetric stochastic block model, the edge connection probability matrix is given by
\theta^{\circ}=\frac{(1-\eta)\varepsilon d}{n}M^{\circ}+\frac{(1-\eta)d}{n}
, where
M^{\circ}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1-1/k,-1/k}}\right\}^{n\times n}
is the community membership matrix.
Therefore, when the estimation error is smaller than
0.99\sqrt{kd}
, the estimator
\hat{\theta}-\frac{d}{n}
achieves weak recovery under the distribution
\text{SSBM}(n,\frac{d}{n},\varepsilon,k)
, which contradicts the extended low-degree conjecture (
3.2
).

Lower bound for learning graphon function

We sketch the proof of
Theorem

2.7
.
Let
W_{0}
be the graphon function underlying the distribution
\mathbb{G}(n,\frac{d}{n})
and
W_{1}
be the graphon function underlying the distribution
\text{SSBM}(n,\frac{d}{n},\varepsilon,k)
. We then have
\text{GW}(W_{0},W_{1})\geqslant\frac{d}{n}\sqrt{\frac{0.99k}{d}}
when
\varepsilon^{2}d\geqslant 0.99k^{2}
.

Now suppose there is a polynomial-time algorithm which, given a random graph
G
sampled from an arbitrary symmetric
k
-stochastic block model, outputs an
n
-block graphon function
\hat{W}:[0,1]\times[0,1]\to[0,1]
achieving error
\frac{d}{3n}\sqrt{\frac{k}{d}}

with probability
1-o(1)
.
Then one can construct a testing statistic by taking

f(Y)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\text{GW}(\hat{W},W_{0})\leqslant\frac{3d}{n}\sqrt{\frac{k}{d}},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We have
f(Y)=1
with probability
1-o(1)
under the distribution
\text{SSBM}(n,\frac{d}{n},\varepsilon,k)
and
f(Y)=0
with probability
1-o(1)
under the distribution
\mathbb{G}(n,\frac{d}{n})
.
Therefore, we have
R_{P,Q}(f)\geqslant\omega(1)
.
Since the function
f(\cdot)
can be evaluated in polynomial time, this contradicts the low-degree lower bound (
Theorem

3.1
), assuming
1.3
.

5
Conclusion and future directions

Based on low-degree heuristics, our paper gives rigorous evidence for a computational phase transition of recovery at the Kesten-Stigum threshold.
We view our work as a first step in studying this phenomenon, leaving open many interesting questions:

•

Below the Kesten-Stigum threshold, suppose we are given an initalization achieving recovery rate
n^{-0.49}
, could we boost the accuracy in polynomial time to get a weak recovery algorithm?

•

Can we show a computational-statistical gap for learning the graphon function when the number of blocks satisfies
k\leqslant\sqrt{n}
(as in
[
LG24
]
)?

A.1
Correlation preserving projection

In this part, we prove that we can project the estimator into the set of matrices with bounded entries and bounded nuclear norm, while preserving correlation.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
M^{\circ}\in\{-1/k,1-1/k\}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric matrix with rank-
(k+1)
.
For any
\delta\leqslant O(1)
, given matrix
\hat{M}_{0}
such that
\langle\hat{M}_{0},M^{\circ}\rangle\geqslant\delta\lVert\hat{M}_{0}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
, there is a polynomial time algorithm which outputs
\hat{M}\in\mathcal{K}
such that

\langle\hat{M},M^{\circ}\rangle\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\delta\lVert\hat{M}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\lVert\hat{M}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\geqslant\Omega(\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}})
, where

\mathcal{K}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{M\in[-1/\delta,1/\delta]^{n\times n}:M+\frac{1}{k\delta}\Ind\Ind^{\top}\succeq 0\,,\Tr(M+\frac{1}{k\delta}\Ind\Ind^{\top})\leqslant n/\delta}}\right\}\,.

Proof.

We apply the correlation preserving projection from
[
HS17
]
(restated in
Theorem

E.1
).
By definition,
M^{\circ}=X^{\circ}(X^{\circ})^{\top}-\frac{1}{k}\Ind\Ind^{\top}
is in
\mathcal{K}
.
Let
N
be the matrix that minimizes
\lVert N\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
subject to
N\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}
and
\langle N,\hat{M}_{0}\rangle\geqslant\delta\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\lVert\hat{M}_{0}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
, where

\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{M\in[-1,1]^{n\times n}:M+\frac{1}{k}\Ind\Ind^{\top}\succeq 0\,,\Tr(M+\frac{1}{k}\Ind\Ind^{\top})\leqslant n}}\right\}\,.

Using ellipsoid method, this semidefinite program can be solved in polynomial time.
By
Theorem

E.1
, we have
\langle N,M^{\circ}\rangle\geqslant\Omega(1)\cdot\delta\lVert N\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\lVert N\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\geqslant\delta\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
.
We let
\hat{M}=\frac{\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}}{\lVert N\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}}\cdot N
.
Then it follows that
\hat{M}\in\mathcal{K}
,
\lVert\hat{M}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}
and

By the relation between edge connection probability matrix

For the second term, since with overwhelming high probability,

\langle\frac{\varepsilon d}{n}M^{\circ},M^{\circ}\rangle=\frac{\varepsilon d}{n}\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon d}{2\sqrt{k}}\lVert M^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\,.

On the other hand, by triangle inequality

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\hat{\theta}-\frac{d}{n}\Ind\Ind^{\top}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\leqslant\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\hat{\theta}-\theta^{\circ}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert\theta^{\circ}-\frac{d}{n}\Ind\Ind^{\top}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\leqslant O(\sqrt{kd}+\frac{\varepsilon d}{\sqrt{k}})\leqslant O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon d/\sqrt{k}}}\right)\,,

Then we have

\lVert X-X^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}=\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}d^{2}}\lVert f(Y)-\theta^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}d^{2}}kn}}\right)\leqslant o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{k}{\varepsilon^{2}d}\cdot\frac{n}{d}\cdot n^{2}}}\right)\,.

When
\varepsilon^{2}d\geqslant 0.001k^{2}
and

,
we have
\E\lVert X-X^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant o(n^{2})
.
combining
Lemma

C.3
and
Lemma

C.4
, we have

\frac{\E g(Y)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(g(Y))}}\geqslant n^{0.001}\,.

Since
g(Y)
is a degree-
\ell
polynomial with
\ell\leqslant n^{0.01}
, by
[
Hop18
]
, we have

\frac{\E g(Y)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(g(Y))}}\leqslant\exp(k^{2})\,.

When
\exp(k^{2})\leqslant n^{0.001}
, this leads to a contradiction.
As result, we conclude that no degree-
n^{0.001}
polynomial can achieve error rate

.
∎

Let

\displaystyle=\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle(Y_{12}-\frac{d}{n})X(Y_{12}-\frac{d}{n}),Y_{2}-\frac{d}{n}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon d}{n}}}\right)^{3}\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle X^{\circ}_{12}XX^{\circ}_{12},X^{\circ}_{2}}}\right\rangle+\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle W_{12}XW_{12},W_{2}}}\right\rangle+\frac{2\varepsilon d}{n}\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle W_{12}XX^{\circ}_{12},W_{2}}}\right\rangle

In this section, we analyze the property of the polynomial defined in

C.1
, under the Erdős-Rényi graph distribution, and give a proof for

.

Proof of
Lemma

C.4
.

Under the Erdős-Rényi graph distribution, the entries in
Y_{2}-\frac{d}{n}
are i.i.d zero mean random variables, independent with
Y_{12}
and
Y_{2}
(i.e the rest of the graph).
As result, we have
\E g(Y)=0
.

It remains to bound the variance of the polynomial under the Erdős-Rényi graph distribution, which is to say, we bound

\E g(Y)^{2}=\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y_{12}-\frac{d}{n}}}\right)X\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Y_{12}-\frac{d}{n}}}\right),Y_{2}-\frac{d}{n}}}\right\rangle^{2}\,.

Let
W_{12}=Y_{12}-\frac{d}{n}
and
W_{2}=Y_{2}-\frac{d}{n}
.
The main observation is that
X,W_{12},W_{2}
are all independent.
As result, let
Z=W_{12}XW_{12}
, we have

\displaystyle\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle W_{12}XW_{12},W_{2}}}\right\rangle^{2}=\sum_{ij}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\E W_{2}(i,j)Z(i,j)}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{ij}\E W_{2}^{2}(i,j)\E Z(i,j)^{2}=\frac{d}{n}\E\lVert Z\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,.

\lVert X\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\leqslant O(n)

\lVert W_{12}\rVert\leqslant\sqrt{d}\log(n)

Therefore we have

By taking the square root, we conclude the proof.

Appendix D
Probability theory facts

In this section, we provide probability tools that we will need in the paper.

D.1
Concentration of spectral radii of random matrices

The following concentration inequality for the spectral norm of the centered adjacency matrix of stochastic block model will be useful for our proofs.

Theorem D.1

(Spectral norm bound for random matrices, theorem 2.7 in
[
BGBK20
]
)
.

Let
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric matrix whose upper triangular entries are independent zero mean random variables.
Moreover, suppose that there exist
q>0
and
\kappa\geqslant 1
such that

\displaystyle\max_{i}\sum_{j}\E\lvert H_{ij}\rvert^{2}\leqslant 1\,,

\displaystyle\max_{i,j}\E\lvert H_{ij}\rvert^{2}\leqslant\kappa/n\,,

\displaystyle\max_{i,j}\lvert H_{ij}\rvert\leqslant 1/q\,.

Then we have

\E\lVert H\rVert\leqslant 2+C\frac{\sqrt{\log(n)}}{q}\,.

Moreover, we have

\ProbOp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\lVert H\rVert-\E\lVert H\rVert}}\right\rvert\geqslant t}}\right]\leqslant 2\exp(-cq^{2}t^{2})\,.

As corollary, for stochastic block model, we have the following concentration inequality:

Lemma D.2
.

Let
A
be the adjacency matrix of a random graph with vertex
i,j
independently connected with probability
\theta(i,j)\geqslant\Omega(1/n)
.
Let
d=\frac{n-1}{n}\sum_{i,j}\theta(i,j)
and suppose
\theta(i,j)\leqslant 2d/n
.
Let
H=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2d}}(A-\theta)
.
Then for every
t\geqslant 10000\log(n)
, for some small universal constant
c>0
, we have

\ProbOp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\lVert H\rVert\geqslant t}}\right]\leqslant 2\exp(-ct^{2})\,.

Proof.

We vertify that the matrix
H
here satisfies the conditions in
Theorem

D.1
.
Crucially, since
\theta(i,j)\leqslant 2d/n
, we have
\E H_{i,j}^{2}\leqslant 1/n

First for each
i\in[n]
, we have

\sum_{j\in n}\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert H_{ij}}}\right\rvert^{2}\leqslant 1
.
Finally, we have

\max_{i,j}|H_{i,j}|\leqslant 1
.
Therefore by taking
\kappa=1
and
q=1
in
Theorem

D.1
, we have
\E\lVert H\rVert\leqslant C\sqrt{\log(n)}
, and the concentration bound

\ProbOp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert H}}\right\rVert\geqslant\E\lVert H\rVert+t}}\right]\leqslant 2\exp(-c^{\prime}t^{2})\,.

where
c^{\prime}
is a universal constant.
Taking

, we have the claim.
∎

D.2
Decoupling edge partition

In this section, we give a lemma that describes the approximate independence between edge sets of the subsampling process.

Lemma D.3
.

Let
X\sim\text{Ber}(p)
, and let
X_{1}
be obtained from
X
by subsampling with probability
1-\eta
, i.e
X_{1}=X\xi
, where
\xi\sim\text{Ber}(1-\eta)
is independent of
X
.
Let
X_{2}=X-X_{1}
, and
\tilde{X}_{2}=X_{2}-\E[X_{2}|X_{1}]+\eta p
.
Then we have
\E[\tilde{X}_{2}|X_{1}]=\E[X_{2}]=\eta p
and
\E[(\tilde{X}_{2}-X_{2})^{2}|X_{1}]\leqslant O(p^{3})
.
Moreover, we have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\tilde{X_{2}}-X_{2}}}\right\rvert\leqslant\eta p
.

Proof.

We first note that

\E[\tilde{X}_{2}|X_{1}]=\eta p=\E[X_{2}]\,.

Next we note that

\E[X_{2}|X_{1}]=\frac{\eta p}{1-(1-\eta)p}(1-X_{1})\,,

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\E[(\tilde{X}_{2}-X_{2})^{2}|X_{1}]=\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta p-\frac{\eta p(1-X_{1})}{(1-(1-\eta)p)}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]\leqslant O(\eta^{2}p^{3})

∎

Corollary D.4
.

Let
Y
be the adjacency matrix of a random graph with each edge
(i,j)
sampled with probability
p(i,j)
.
Suppose that
p(i,j)\leqslant p
for each
i,j\in[n]
.
Let
Y_{1}
be the adjacency matrix of the graph obtained by subsampling each edge in
Y
with probability
1-\eta
.
Let
Y_{2}=Y-Y_{1}
.
Then there is a matrix
\tilde{Y}_{2}
such that

•

for every
t\geqslant\log(n)
,
\lVert\tilde{Y}_{2}-Y_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant t\eta p^{3}n^{2}
with probability at least
1-\exp(-t)
,

•

for every
i,j\in[n]
,
\E\tilde{Y}_{2}(i,j)=\E Y_{2}(i,j)

•

moreover
\tilde{Y}_{2}
and
Y_{1}
are independent,

•

and finally the entries in
\tilde{Y}_{2}
are independent.

Proof.

We construct the matrix
\tilde{Y}_{2}
in the following way.
For each
i,j
, we let
\tilde{Y}_{2}(i,j)=Y_{2}(i,j)-\E[Y_{2}(i,j)|Y_{1}]+\eta p(i,j)
.
Then by
Lemma

D.3
, we have
\E\tilde{Y}_{2}(i,j)=\E Y_{2}(i,j)
and
\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{Y}_{2}(i,j)-Y_{2}(i,j)}}\right)^{2}\leqslant O(\eta^{2}p^{3})
.
In addition, we have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\tilde{Y}_{2}(i,j)-Y_{2}(i,j)}}\right\rvert\leqslant O(\eta p)
.

Furthermore
\tilde{Y}_{2}(i,j)
and
Y_{1}
are independent.

Finally since the upper triangular entries in
Y
are independent, we have the upper triangular entries in
\tilde{Y}_{2}
are independent.
By Hoeffding bound, with probability at least
1-\exp(-t)
, we have
\lVert\tilde{Y_{2}}-Y_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant t\eta p^{3}n^{2}\log(n)
.
∎

Appendix E
Useful algorithmic results

In this section, we provide two algorithmic results from previous work that will be useful in our paper.

E.1
Correlation preserving projection

Given a vector
P
that has constant correlation with an unknown vector
Y
,
[
HS17
]
shows that one can project the vector
P
into a convex set containing
Y
, and preserve the constant correlation with
Y
.

Theorem E.1

(Correlation preserving projection, theorem 2.3 in
[
HS17
]
)
.

Let
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a convex set and
Y\in\mathcal{C}
.
Let
P
be a vector with
\langle P,Y\rangle\geqslant\delta\cdot\lVert P\rVert\cdot\lVert Y\rVert
.
Then, if we let
Q
be the vector that minimizes
\lVert Q\rVert
subject to
Q\in\mathcal{C}
and
\langle P,Q\rangle\geqslant\delta\cdot\lVert P\rVert\cdot\lVert Y\rVert
, we have

\langle Q,Y\rangle\geqslant\delta/2\cdot\lVert Q\rVert\cdot\lVert Y\rVert\,.

(E.1)

Furthermore,
Q
satisfies
\lVert Q\rVert\geqslant\delta\lVert Y\rVert
.

We include their proof here for completeness.

Proof.

By construction,
Q
is the Euclidean project of
0
into the set
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{Q\in\mathcal{C}|\langle P,Q\rangle\geqslant\delta\lVert P\rVert\lVert Y\rVert}}\right\}
.
By Pythagorean inequality, the Euclidean projection into a set decreases distances to points into the set.
Therefore,
\lVert Y-Q\rVert^{2}\leqslant\lVert Y-0\rVert^{2}
, which implies that
\langle Y,Q\rangle\geqslant\lVert Q\rVert^{2}/2
.
Moreover,
\langle P,Q\rangle\geqslant\delta\lVert P\rVert\lVert Y\rVert
, which implies
\lVert Q\rVert\geqslant\delta\lVert Y\rVert
by Cauchy-Schwartz.
Thus, we can conclude that
\langle Y,Q\rangle\geqslant\delta/2\cdot\lVert Y\rVert\cdot\lVert Q\rVert
.
∎

E.2
Learning edge connection probability matrix via SVD

Theorem E.2
.

When
d\geqslant\log(n)
, there is a polynomial time algorithm which given the adjacency matrix of a graph sampled from symmetric
k
-stochastic block model
\text{SSBM}(n,\frac{d}{n},\varepsilon,k)
, returns an estimator
\hat{\theta}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
such that
\lVert\theta^{\circ}-\theta\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant kd
with high probability.

Proof.

We take
\hat{\theta}
as the best rank-
k
approximation for the adjacency matrix.
Then since
\lVert A-\theta^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leqslant\sqrt{kd}
with high probability, we have
\lVert\hat{\theta}-A\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leqslant\sqrt{kd}
with high probability.
By triangle inequality, we have
\lVert\hat{\theta}-\theta^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leqslant 2\sqrt{d}
.
As result, we have

\lVert\hat{\theta}-\theta^{\circ}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}\leqslant 2\sqrt{kd}
.
∎

The proof of the extended result follows trivially from the proof of Theorem 2.20 of

]

Theorem F.1

(Restatement of
Theorem

3.1
)
.

Let
d=o(n),k=n^{o(1)}
and
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.
Let
