Representations of Noncommutative Cubes and Prisms
Douglas Farenick, Roghayeh Maleki, Sofia Medina Varela, and Sushil Singla

Abstract
. Representations of the operator system determined by the canonical generators of the free product of two cyclic groups of order 2 2 and k k , or d d cyclic groups of order 2 2 , are studied for the purpose of shedding light on the noncommutative geometry of noncommutative d d -cubes and k k -prisms. By way of the duality of the categories 𝖭𝖢𝖢𝗈𝗇𝗏 {\sf NCConv} and 𝖮𝗉𝖲𝗒𝗌 {\sf OpSys} of noncommutative convex sets and operator systems, respectively, an analysis of noncommutative extreme points, exactness, the lifting property, automatic complete positivity, controlled completely positive extensions, tensor products, and operator system duality is undertaken. Of note is the pairing of two classical dilation theorems of Halmos [ 18 ] and Mirman [ 30 ] to give a complete description of the noncommutative triangular prism in terms of joint unitary dilations.

1.
Introduction

In this article, separate, but categorically related objects, will be considered in tandem. In one category, these are noncommutative convex sets, while in the other category they are operator systems. The two relevant categories,
{\sf NCConv}
and
{\sf OpSys}
, described below, are dual to each other; hence, we study objects that are sometimes viewed geometrically and sometimes algebraically.

The underlying classical geometric objects considered in this paper are cubes
\mbox{\rm C}(d)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\geq 2
, which are formed by Cartesian products of
d
copies of the interval
[-1,1]
, and prisms
\mbox{\rm P}(k)
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which arise from the Cartesian product of the convex hull of the
k
-th roots of unity in
\mathbb{C}
and the interval
[-1,1]
. Two special cases, enjoying additional properties, are the square
\mbox{\rm C}(2)
and the triangular prism
\mbox{\rm P}(3)
. In moving to noncommutative geometry, we shall be considering the following counterparts to the classical geometric objects: noncommutative cubes and prisms,
which are (i) graded sets in the category
{\sf NCConv}
such that their first levels in the grading
are given by
\mbox{\rm C}(d)
and
\mbox{\rm P}(k)
, respectively, or (ii) operator subsystems of (full) group C
∗
-algebras
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\cdots*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
and
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, respectively, determined by the canonical unitary generators, where
G_{1}*G_{2}
denotes the free product of discrete groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.

Two classical dilation theorems in operator theory suggest particular attention should be paid to the triangular prism
\mbox{\rm P}(3)
as its properties in the noncommutative setting are richer than those of the noncommutative prisms arising from
\mbox{\rm P}(k)
for
k\geq 4
.
The first of these results is the Halmos dilation theorem
[
18
]
, which asserts that any Hilbert space contraction
x
has a unitary dilation
u
. If, in addition,
x^{*}=x
, then Halmos’s construction of the dilation
u
shows that
u^{*}=u
; hence, every selfadjoint contraction dilates to a symmetry (that is, to a selfadjoint unitary).
The second motivating result is Mirman’s dilation theorem
[
30
]
:
if
\mbox{\rm T}\subset\mathbb{C}
is a triangle with nonempty interior, and
if
x
is a Hilbert space operator such that
\langle x\xi,\xi\rangle\in\mbox{\rm T}
, for every unit vector
\xi
, then
x
has a dilation to a normal operator
y
whose spectrum consists of the three vertices of the triangle
T
.
Mirman’s result does not extend to convex polygonal sets such as
\mbox{\rm P}(k)\subset\mathbb{C}
, if
k\geq 4
, but it does extend to simplicial sets
Q
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for all
d\geq 2

[
6
]
.

The connection between the Halmos and Mirman theorems rests with the fact that an operator
x
is a selfadjoint contraction if and only if
\langle x\xi,\xi\rangle\in[-1,1]
, for every unit vector
\xi
. In other words, both these results may be viewed as theorems concerning the classical numerical range of an operator. So, it is natural to consider a pairing of these theorems, which in this paper involves the triangular prism in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
arising from the Cartesian product of the triangle determined by the roots of unity and the line segment
[-1,1]
.

In considering noncommutative cubes and prisms in the category
{\sf NCConv}
, we shall be interested in their noncommutative extreme points, which arise from irreducible representations of the associated group C
∗
-algebra. We determine at which levels of the grading one can find these noncommutative extreme points, and uncover some of their properties. We also consider a weaker form of extremality by considering elements of noncommutative cubes or triangular prisms that generate a factor, and examine what factorial types may arise.

As objects in the category
{\sf OpSys}
, we study the structure of noncommutative cubes and noncommutative
prisms by way of operator-theoretic properties, including exactness, the lifting property,
automatic complete positivity, controlled completely positive extensions, tensor products, injectivity, and operator system duality. Some operator system tensor product identities were known previously to be equivalent to the Connes Embedding Problem (CEP), and so the resolution of the CEP in the negative distinguishes some operator system tensor products considered here involving noncommutative cubes and triangular prisms.
Finally, in relation to recent work by Harris
[
20
]
of independent interest, we exhibit as an outcome of our study a four-dimensional operator system of diagonal matrices that does not have the lifting property.

2.1.
Categorical structures

We denote by
{\sf OpSys}
the category whose objects are operators systems, defined in
[
8
]

as matrix-ordered
*
-vector spaces over
\mathbb{C}
possessing a distinguished Archimedean order unit, and whose morphisms are unital completely positive (ucp) linear maps. The category whose objects are noncommutative convex sets and whose morphisms are noncommutative affine functions, as defined in
[
9
]
, is denoted by
{\sf NCConv}
.

For a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the space of bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.
The
noncommutative state space
of a finite-dimensional operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is the graded set
S_{\rm nc}({\mathcal{R}})
given by

S_{\rm nc}({\mathcal{R}})=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}S_{n}({\mathcal{R}}),

where
S_{n}({\mathcal{R}})
is the set of all ucp maps
\phi:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))
. Such ucp maps
\phi

are called
noncommutative states
of
{\mathcal{R}}
.
If we disregard
S_{\aleph_{0}}({\mathcal{R}})
, then the graded set

S_{\rm mtrx}({\mathcal{R}})=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}S_{n}({\mathcal{R}})

is called the
matrix state space
of
{\mathcal{R}}
. (The matrix state spaces of infinite-dimensional
operator systems
{\mathcal{R}}
are defined in exactly the
same way, but the noncommutative state space of an infinite-dimensional operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
could involve cardinalities beyond
\aleph_{0}

[
9
]
.)

If
\mathfrak{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
is a
d
-tuple of elements from an arbitrary operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
,
and if
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
is the operator subsystem of
{\mathcal{R}}
spanned by
\{1,x_{1},x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{d},x_{d}^{*}\}
, where
1=1_{\mathcal{R}}
,
the Archimedean order unit of
{\mathcal{R}}
,
then the
noncommutative numerical range
of
\mathfrak{x}
is the graded set

W_{\rm nc}(\mathfrak{x})=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}W_{n}(\mathfrak{x}),

where, for
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
,

W_{n}(\mathfrak{x})=\left\{\left(\phi(x_{1}),\phi(x_{2}),\dots,\phi(x_{d})\right)\,|\,\phi\in S_{n}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\right\}.

If we disregard the set
W_{\aleph_{0}}(\mathfrak{x})
, then the graded set

W_{\rm mtrx}(\mathfrak{x})=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}W_{n}(\mathfrak{x}),

is called the
matrix range
of
\mathfrak{x}
.

We define the
noncommutative complex plane
to be the graded set

\mathbb{C}_{\rm nc}=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n)).

Noncommutative state spaces, noncommutative numerical ranges, and the noncommutative complex plane
are examples of
noncommutative convex sets.
Recall from
[
9
]

that a
noncommutative convex set
in a separable operator space
\mathcal{V}

is a graded set

K_{\rm nc}=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}K_{n},

where
K_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n}(V)
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
K_{\aleph_{0}}
is a
set of infinite matrices over
\mathcal{V}
with uniformly bounded
finite submatrices. Furthermore,
if
\mathcal{I}
is a finite or countably infinite set, and if
n
and
\{n_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
are cardinalities no larger than
\aleph_{0}
,
then for every family
\{a_{n,n_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
of isometries
a_{n,n_{i}}:\ell^{2}(n_{i})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(n)

such that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{n,n_{i}}a_{n,n_{i}}^{*}=1_{n}
, and every bounded collection
\{\nu_{n_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
of

\nu_{n_{i}}\in K_{n_{i}}
, we have
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}a_{n,n_{i}}\nu_{n_{i}}a_{n,n_{i}}^{*}\in K_{n}
, where the convergence of the sums,
when
\mathcal{I}
is infinite, is with respect to the strong operator topology.

If
K_{\rm nc}
is a noncommutative convex set, then the
set
A_{\rm nc}(K_{\rm nc})
of all continuous noncommutative
affine functions
K_{\rm nc}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{\rm nc}

carries the structure of an operator system
[
9
, Sections 2.5 & 3.2]
.
In particular, with the noncommutative convex set
S_{\rm nc}({\mathcal{R}})
, where
{\mathcal{R}}
is an operator system of finite dimension,
say
{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}
for some
d
-tuple
\mathfrak{x}
, then
the categorical duality results of Webster-Winker
[
38
]
and Davidson-Kennedy
[
9
]
assert

(2.1)

A_{\rm nc}\left(S_{\rm nc}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\right)\cong_{{\sf OpSys}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}}\,\mbox{ and }\,S_{\rm nc}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{x}})\cong_{{\sf NCConv}}W_{\rm nc}(\mathfrak{x}).

2.2.
Minimal and maximal noncommutative convex sets

If
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compact convex set and
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
, then

(1)

K_{n}^{\rm max}
consists of all
d
-tuples
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})

of selfadjoint
a_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))
for which
W_{1}(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq K
, and

(2)

K_{n}^{\rm min}
consists of all
d
-tuples
\mathfrak{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})

of selfadjoint
a_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))
for which there exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
containing
\ell^{2}(n)
as a subspace and
commuting selfadoint operators
y_{1},\dots,y_{d}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})

with joint spectrum equal to the closure of the
set
\partial_{\rm e}K
of extreme points
of
K
such that
a_{j}=py_{j}{}_{|\ell^{2}(n)}
, for each
j
, where
p\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
is the projection with range
\ell^{2}(n)
.

The graded sets
K^{\rm min}=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}K_{n}^{\rm min}
and

K^{\rm max}=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}K_{n}^{\rm max}
are called the
minimal
and
maximal

noncommutative convex sets generated by
K
, and they have the following properties:

K^{\rm min}\subseteq K_{\rm nc}\subseteq K^{\rm max}\,\mbox{ and }\,K_{1}^{\rm min}=K_{1}^{\rm max}=K,

for every noncommutative convex set
K_{\rm nc}
whose first graded level
K_{1}
is
K
.

In general, the inclusion
K^{\rm min}\subseteq K^{\rm max}
is strict; however, it is known that

K^{\rm min}=K^{\rm max}
if and only if
K
is a simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
32
]
.

2.3.
Noncommutative cubes

The
noncommutative
d
-cube
,
as defined in
[
12
]
,
is the operator subsystem
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)

of the group C
∗
-algebra

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\cdots*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
of the free product of
d
copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
that is given by

\mbox{\rm NC}(d)=\mbox{Span}\{1,u_{1},\dots,u_{d}\}\subseteq{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\cdots*\mathbb{Z}_{2}),

where
u_{1},\dots,u_{d}
are the canonical generators of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\cdots*\mathbb{Z}_{2})

determined by the
j
-th copy
u_{j}
of the generator of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\cdots*\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Note that these
elements
u_{1},\dots,u_{d}
are symmetries and each
u_{j}
is of the form
u_{j}=2p_{j}-1
, for some
projection
p_{j}\in{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\cdots*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.
Moreover, the
elements
u_{1},\dots,u_{d}\in\mbox{\rm NC}(d)
are universal for selfadjoint contractions
in the sense that if
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
are any selfadjoint operators acting on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, then there is a unital completely positive
linear map
\phi:\mbox{\rm NC}(d)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

such that
\phi(u_{j})=a_{j}
, for each
j

[
13
, Proposition 4.1]
.

A second definition of the noncommutative
d
-cube is given by
the graded set

[-1,1]^{d}_{\rm nc}=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}[-1,1]^{d}_{n},

where, for
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
,

[-1,1]^{d}_{n}=\left\{(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))\,|\,a_{j}^{*}=a_{j}\mbox{ and }\|a_{j}\|\leq 1,\,1\leq j\leq d\right\}.

How these definitions are related is explained by the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

For every
d\geq 2
,

\mbox{\rm C}(d)^{\rm max}=[-1,1]_{\rm nc}^{d}=W_{\rm nc}(\mathfrak{u})\cong_{{\sf NCConv}}S_{\rm nc}(\mbox{\rm NC}(d))

and

\mbox{\rm NC}(d)\cong_{{\sf OpSys}}A_{\rm nc}\left([-1,1]_{\rm nc}^{d}\right),

where
\mathfrak{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
is the
d
-tuple of canonical symmetries generating

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\cdots*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

In Theorem
2.1
above, the assertion
\mbox{\rm C}(d)^{\rm max}=[-1,1]_{\rm nc}^{d}=W_{\rm nc}(\mathfrak{u})
is
a consequence of the universality of the C
∗
-algebra

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\cdots*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
,
while the isomorphism
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)\cong_{{\sf OpSys}}A_{\rm nc}\left([-1,1]_{\rm nc}^{d}\right)

is a restatement of the Webster-Winkler and Davidson-Kennedy duality.

The operator theoretic properties of noncommutative
d
-cubes were
studied in
[
12
,
13
]
, while the
analysis of noncommutative
d
-cubes was considered, for example, in
[
11
]
.

2.4.
Noncommutative prisms

If
k\in\mathbb{N}
is such that
k\geq 3
, then the
classical
k
-prism

\mbox{\rm P}(k)
is given by

\mbox{\rm P}(k)=\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{k}\,\times\,\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},

where
C_{k}
denotes the representation of the additive cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{k}
as the multiplicative subgroup of the unit circle
S^{1}

consisting of the
k
-th roots of unity. As with noncommutative cubes, the noncommutative convex set
\mbox{\rm P}(k)^{\rm max}
is called
a
noncommutative
k
-prism
in the category
{\sf NCConv}
. Thus, a triple
(a,b,c)
of selfadjoint elements belongs to
the graded set
\mbox{\rm P}(k)^{\rm max}
if and only if the joint numerical range of
(a,b,c)
is contained in the classical
k
-prism

\mbox{\rm P}(k)
.

The analogue of noncommutative
k
-prism in the operator system category
is slightly different from
\mbox{\rm P}(k)^{\rm max}
in which the underlying linear structure is not
3
-dimensional over
\mathbb{R}
.
In the group C
∗
-algebra
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, let
w
and
v
denote the canonical unitaries that arise from the generators
of the cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}_{k}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, respectively. Thus,
w^{k}=v^{2}=1
and
w^{*}=w^{k-1}
. Define

\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)=\mbox{\rm Span}\{1,w,w^{2},\dots,w^{k-1},v\}\subseteq{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2}),

which we call a
noncommutative
k
-prism
. In the case
k=3
, the operator system

\mbox{\rm NCP}(3)=\mbox{\rm Span}\{1,w,w^{2},v\}\subseteq{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})

is called a
noncommutative triangular prism
.

The relationship between these two definitions is as follows.

Theorem 2.2
.

For every
k\geq 3
,

\mbox{\rm P}(k)^{\rm min}\subseteq W_{\rm nc}(w,v)\subseteq\mbox{\rm P}(k)^{\rm max},

where
w
and
v
are the canonical unitaries in
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
that arise from the generators
of the cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}_{k}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, respectively.

The proof of Theorem
2.2
is deferred to Section §
4.1
. However, a much stronger conclusion holds for
k=3
,
as indicated by the following pairing of the theorems of Halmos and Mirman, which is proved in Section §
4.1
.

Theorem 2.3

(Halmos-Mirman)
.

\mbox{\rm P}(3)^{\rm max}=W_{\rm nc}(w,v)\cong_{{\sf NCConv}}S_{\rm nc}(\mbox{\rm NCP}(3))
.

Theorem
2.3
shows that any pair of operators
x,y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, for which the numerical range of
x
is contained in the triangle
\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{3}
and
y
is selfadjoint contraction, is the ucp image of the canonical unitary generators
w
and
v
of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

2.5.
C
∗
- and injective covers

By the Choi-Effros Theorem
[
8
]
, every abstract operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
can be realised as an operator subsystem of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. However, the C
∗
-algebras generated by different spatial realisations of a single operator system can be vastly different;
furthermore, among all such realisations, there are two that are extremal, and they are called the minimal and maximal C
∗
-covers of
{\mathcal{R}}
.

Given an (abstract) operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
, a
C
∗
-cover
of
{\mathcal{R}}
is a pair
(\kappa,{\mathcal{A}})
consisting of a unital C
∗
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}

and a unital complete order embedding
\kappa:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}={\rm C}^{*}\left(\kappa({\mathcal{R}})\right)
.

A C
∗
-cover
(\iota,\mathcal{B})
is
minimal
if, for every C
∗
-cover
(\kappa,{\mathcal{A}})
of
{\mathcal{R}}
, there is a unital *-homomorphism

\pi:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
such that
\iota=\pi\circ\kappa
. The minimal C
∗
-cover exists for every operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
, and is unique
up to an isomorphism that fixes
{\mathcal{R}}

[
19
]
. The minimal pair is denoted by
\left(\iota_{\rm e},{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})\right)
,
and is known in the literature as the C
∗
-envelope of
{\mathcal{R}}
.

A C
∗
-cover
(\nu,\mathcal{C})
is
maximal
if, for every C
∗
-cover
(\kappa,{\mathcal{A}})
of
{\mathcal{R}}
, there is a unital *-homomorphism

\pi:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}
such that
\kappa=\pi\circ\kappa
. The maximal C
∗
-cover exists for every operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
, and is unique
up to an isomorphism that fixes
{\mathcal{R}}

[
27
]
. The maximal pair is denoted by

\left(\iota_{\rm u},{\rm C}^{*}_{\rm max}({\mathcal{R}})\right)
,
and is known in the literature as the universal C
∗
-algebra generated by
{\mathcal{R}}
.

The operator systems considered in this paper arise from the generators of discrete groups, and the following result describes their C
∗
-envelopes.

Theorem 2.4
.

(
[
12
]
)

If
G
is a discrete group generated by a finite set
\mathfrak{u}
of elements of
G
, and if
\mathcal{O}_{G}
denotes the operator subsystem of
{\rm C}^{*}(G)

defined by

(2.2)

\mathcal{O}_{G}=\mbox{\rm Span}\{1,u,u^{*}\,|\,u\in\mathfrak{u}\}\subseteq{\rm C}^{*}(G),

then
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{G})={\rm C}^{*}(G)
.

An
injective cover
of an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is a pair
(\kappa,\mathcal{D})
consisting of an injective C
∗
-algebra
\mathcal{D}
and a unital
complete order embedding
\kappa:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
. Unlike C
∗
-covers, injective covers are not required to be generated by
\kappa({\mathcal{R}})
.
An injective cover
(\kappa,\mathcal{D})
of
{\mathcal{R}}
is
enveloping
if the only ucp map
\phi:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
for which
\kappa=\phi\circ\kappa

is the identity map
\phi(a)=a
, for all
a\in\mathcal{D}
. The existence of an enveloping injective cover was established by Hamana
[
19
]
, and it is
denoted by
(\iota_{\rm ie},\mathcal{I}({\mathcal{R}}))
and called the injective envelope of
{\mathcal{R}}
. This enveloping injective cover also yields a minimal C
∗
-cover for
{\mathcal{R}}
via
the C
∗
-cover
\left(\iota_{\rm ie}{}_{|{\mathcal{R}}},{\rm C}^{*}\left(\iota_{\rm ie}({\mathcal{R}})\right)\right)
; that is,
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})\cong{\rm C}^{*}\left(\iota_{\rm ie}({\mathcal{R}})\right)
.

Theorem 2.5
.

If
G
is a discrete group generated by a finite set
\mathfrak{u}
of elements of
G
such that
{\rm C}^{*}(G)
is primitive and antiliminal,
then the injective envelope of the operator system
defined by

\mathcal{O}_{G}=\mbox{\rm Span}\{1,u,u^{*}\,|\,u\in\mathfrak{u}\}\subseteq{\rm C}^{*}(G)

is an AW
∗
-factor of type III.

Proof.

By hypothesis,
{\rm C}^{*}(G)
is a separable C
∗
-algebra that has a faithful irreducible representation on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that
{\rm C}^{*}(G)
contains no nonzero
compact operators. Because the injective envelope of a separable prime C
∗
-algebra is an AW
∗
-factor of type I or type III
[
1
, Corollary 2.4]
, and
since type I factors are von Neumann algebras, if the injective envelope of
{\rm C}^{*}(G)
were a type I factor, then
{\rm C}^{*}(G)
would contain an essential ideal consisting of nonzero
compact operators
[
1
]
, contrary to the hypothesis that
{\rm C}^{*}(G)
is antiliminal. Hence,
\mathcal{I}\left({\rm C}^{*}(G)\right)
is
a type III AW
∗
-factor. Because the injective envelopes of
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
coincide, for every operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
, we deduce

\mathcal{I}\left(\mathcal{O}_{G}\right)
is
a type III AW
∗
-factor.

\hfill\square

2.6.
Noncommutative extreme points, representations, and purity

Suppose
K_{\rm nc}
is a noncommutative convex set in a separable operator space
\mathcal{V}
; that is,

K_{\rm nc}=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}K_{n},

where
K_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathcal{V})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
K_{\aleph_{0}}
is a
set of infinite matrices over
\mathcal{V}
with uniformly bounded
finite submatrices.

An element
x
is
pure
in
K_{\rm nc}
if
x\in K_{n}
, for some
n
, and the only expressions
of
x
of the form

x=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\gamma_{j}^{*}x_{j}\gamma_{j},

where each
x_{j}\in K_{n_{j}}
,
\gamma_{j}:\ell^{2}(n)\rightarrow\ell^{2}(n_{j})
is linear and bounded, and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}\gamma_{j}^{*}\gamma_{j}=1
(the identity operator on
\ell^{2}(n)
), are those expressions in which

(1)

each
\gamma_{j}
is a scalar multiple of an isometry
\nu_{j}
,

(2)

\nu_{j}^{*}x_{j}\nu_{j}=x
.

An element
x
is
maximal
in
K_{\rm nc}
if
x\in K_{n}
, for some
n
, and the only matrices of the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}x&z_{12}\\
z_{21}&z_{22}\end{array}\right]

in
K_{\rm nc}
are those in which
z_{12}
and
z_{21}
are zero, and
z_{22}\in K_{\rm nc}
.

A simple way to define noncommutative extreme points is to make use of the characterisation given in
[
9
, Proposition 6.1.4]
,
which is stated below as a definition.

Definition 2.6
.

If
K_{\rm nc}
is a noncommutative convex set in a separable operator space
\mathcal{V}
, then an element
x\in K_{\rm nc}

is a
noncommutative extreme point
if
x
is both pure and maximal.

The graded set of noncommutative extreme points of
K_{\rm nc}
is denoted by
\partial_{\rm ext}\,K_{\rm nc}
, and it satisfies the Krein-Milman theorem
in cases where
K_{\rm nc}
is compact (that is, each
K_{n}
is compact):
namely,
K_{\rm nc}
is the smallest compact noncommutative convex set containing
\partial_{\rm ext}\,K_{\rm nc}
.

Endowed with the bounded-weak topology, the
noncommutative state space of an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is an example of a compact noncommutative convex set.
In this case, the noncommutative extreme points of
S_{\rm nc}({\mathcal{R}})
are the pure, maximal ucp maps. However, the situation
is much simpler for operator systems of the form
\mathcal{O}_{G}
, as defined in (
2.2
), as demonstrated by the following
result.

Proposition 2.7
.

If
G
is a discrete group generated by a finite set
\mathfrak{u}
of elements of
G
,
and if
\mathcal{O}_{G}
denotes the operator subsystem of
{\rm C}^{*}(G)

defined by

\mathcal{O}_{G}=\mbox{\rm Span}\{1,u,u^{*}\,|\,u\in\mathfrak{u}\}\subseteq{\rm C}^{*}(G),

then a noncommutative state
\phi:\mathcal{O}_{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))
is a noncommutative extreme point of

S_{\rm nc}(\mathcal{O}_{G})
if and only if there exists an irreducible representation
\pi:{\rm C}^{*}(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))

such that
\pi_{|\mathcal{O}_{G}}=\phi
.

Proof.

Recall that a boundary representation of an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is an irreducible representation
\pi
of
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})

such that the ucp map
\pi_{|{\mathcal{R}}}
has a unique ucp extension, namely
\pi
, to
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
.
By
[
9
, Corollary 6.2.1]
, a ucp map

\phi:\mathcal{O}_{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))
is a noncommutative extreme point of

S_{\rm nc}(\mathcal{O}_{G})
if and only if there exists a boundary representation

\pi:{\rm C}^{*}(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))
such that
\pi_{|\mathcal{O}_{G}}=\phi
.
Since boundary representations are irreducible, it suffices to show that every
irreducible representation
\pi:{\rm C}^{*}(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))
is a boundary representation of
\mathcal{O}_{G}
.

To this end, assume
\pi:{\rm C}^{*}(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))
is an irreducible representation, and let
\phi=\pi_{|\mathcal{O}_{G}}
.
Suppose
\Phi:{\rm C}^{*}(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(n))
is a ucp extension of
\phi
. Then
\Phi(u)=\pi(u)
is unitary, for every
u\in\mathfrak{u}
.
Thus,
u
is in the multiplicative domain for
\Phi
, which implies
\Phi
agrees with
\pi
on the C
∗
-algebra generated by
\mathfrak{u}
.
In other words,
\Phi=\pi
, which implies
\phi
has a unique ucp extension to the irreducible representation
\pi
of
{\rm C}^{*}(G)

\pi
is a boundary representation of
\mathcal{O}_{G}
.

\hfill\square

By Proposition

Suppose now that

3.1.
The noncommutative square

The analysis of noncommutative square involves the group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and the group
C
∗
-algebra
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, both of which have been well studied.
While the main assertion concerning
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, Corollary
3.5
below, can be derived from the results of
[
34
]
,
the linear-algebraic proof given here has the value of simplicity, and it facilitates the determination of the enveloping injective cover of
\mbox{\rm NC}(2)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
are linearly independent symmetries.
If
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
is irreducible, then
\mbox{\rm dim}\mathcal{H}=2
.

Proof.

Decompose
\mathcal{H}
as
\mathcal{K}_{-1}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{1}
,
where
\mathcal{K}_{j}
is the eigenspace of
v_{j}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
j\in\{-1,1\}
. Thus,
v_{1}
and

v_{2}
have the following representations as
2\times 2
operator matrices acting on
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}_{-1}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{1}
:

v_{1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-1_{\mathcal{K}_{-1}}&0\\
0&1_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}\end{array}\right]\,\mbox{ and }\,v_{2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{-1}&y\\
y^{*}&a_{1}\end{array}\right],

for some selfadjoint
a_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}_{j})
and bounded linear operator
y:\mathcal{K}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{-1}
.
We now determine the elements
a_{j}
and
y
, using the hypothesis that
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
is an irreducible set.

Every matrix that commutes with
v_{1}
has the form
x_{-1}\oplus x_{1}
, for
x_{j}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}_{j})
.
Because
v_{2}
is a symmetry,
ya_{1}=-a_{-1}y
. Hence, the diagonal matrix

-a_{-1}\oplus a_{1}

commutes with both
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, and so there exists

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
a_{-1}=\lambda 1_{\mathcal{K}_{-1}}
and

a_{1}=-\lambda 1_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}
. The diagonal entries of the matrix

v_{2}^{2}=1_{\mathcal{H}}
lead to the equations
\lambda^{2}1_{\mathcal{K}_{-1}}+yy^{*}=1_{\mathcal{K}_{-1}}
and

\lambda^{2}1_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}+y^{*}y=1_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}
. Thus,
-1\leq\lambda\leq 1
. However,
|\lambda|\not=1
,
for if it were true that
|\lambda|=1
, then we would necessarily have
y=0
, implying
v_{2}=\lambda v_{1}
,
which is in contradiction to the linear independence of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
. Hence,
\lambda\in(-1,1)
.

Since
yy^{*}=(1-\lambda^{2})\,1_{\mathcal{K}_{-1}}
and
y^{*}y=(1-\lambda^{2})\,1_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}
, the element
z=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}}y

is a linear operator
z:\mathcal{K}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{{-1}}
such that
zz^{*}=1_{\mathcal{K}_{-1}}
and
z^{*}z=1_{\mathcal{K}_{1}}
; that is,
z
is both an isometry
and a co-isometry. Hence,
z:\mathcal{K}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{-1}
is a surjective isometry, and we can assume, henceforth
and without loss of generality, that
\mathcal{K}_{-1}=\mathcal{K}_{1}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{K}
. Thus,
v_{2}
is given by

v_{2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda 1_{\mathcal{K}}&y\\
y^{*}&-\lambda 1_{\mathcal{K}}\end{array}\right],

where
yy^{*}=y^{*}y=(1-\lambda)^{2}1_{\mathcal{K}}
.

The element
g=y\oplus y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
commutes with both
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
,
and so
y=\mu 1_{\mathcal{K}}
, for some
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
such that
|\mu|^{2}=1-\lambda^{2}
.
Thus,

v_{2}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda 1_{\mathcal{K}}&e^{i\theta}\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}1_{\mathcal{K}}\\
e^{-i\theta}\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}1_{\mathcal{K}}&-\lambda 1_{\mathcal{K}}\end{array}\right],

for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
As
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are jointly unitarily equivalent to

\bigoplus_{1}^{\mbox{\rm dim}\,\mathcal{K}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right]\,\mbox{ and }\,\bigoplus_{1}^{\mbox{\rm dim}\,\mathcal{K}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda&e^{i\theta}\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\\
e^{-i\theta}\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}&-\lambda\end{array}\right],

the only way for
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
to be an irreducible set is for
\mbox{\rm dim}\,\mathcal{K}=1
.
Hence,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{K}
has dimension
2
.

\hfill\square

Corollary 3.2
.

The irreducible representations of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
are, up to unitary equivalence,
of the form
\pi_{\lambda}:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, where
\lambda\in(-1,1)
, and

\pi_{\lambda}(u_{1})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right]\,\mbox{ and }\,\pi_{\lambda}(u_{2})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda&\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}&-\lambda\end{array}\right],

or of the form
\varrho_{k}:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, for
k\in\{-1,1\}
, where

\varrho_{-1}(u_{j})=j
and
\varrho_{1}(u_{j})=-j
, for
j\in\{-1,1\}
.

Proof.

Note that, if
\lambda\in(-1,1)
and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, then

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right]\,\mbox{ and }\,\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda&\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}&-\lambda\end{array}\right].

are jointly unitarily equivalent to

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right]\,\mbox{ and }\,\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda&e^{i\theta}\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\\
e^{-i\theta}\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}&-\lambda\end{array}\right].

On the other hand, any unitary that fixes

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right]
must be diagonal, and so such a unitary cannot send

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda&\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}&-\lambda\end{array}\right]
to

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\tilde{\lambda}&\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}&-\tilde{\lambda}\end{array}\right]

unless
\tilde{\lambda}=\lambda
.

\hfill\square

Corollary 3.3
.

The noncommutative extreme points of the noncommutative square

occur at levels
1
and
2
of the grading.

Corollary 3.4
.

If
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

, then the block-diagonal operators

\tilde{u}_{1}=\bigoplus_{1}^{\infty}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-1&0\\

Corollary 3.5
.

If
{\mathcal{A}}

{\mathcal{A}}=\left\{f:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\,|\,f\mbox{ is continuous and }f(-1)\mbox{ and }f(1)\mbox{ are diagonal}\right\},

such that

\pi(u_{1})[\lambda]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-1&0\\

Using the notations established above,

While the results above are essentially present in the literature, in one form or another, the following theorem concerning the injective envelope
of the noncommutative square has not yet been noted. The proof of the result
also gives the determination of the local multiplier algebra of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

If
{\mathcal{A}}
is any C
∗
-algebra, and if
{\mathcal{J}}
and
\mathcal{K}
are essential ideals of
{\mathcal{A}}
for which
{\mathcal{J}}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}
, then there is a canonical embedding
of multiplier algebras
M({\mathcal{K}})\rightarrow M({\mathcal{J}})
. By considering the set
\mathcal{E}({\mathcal{A}})
of essential ideals of
{\mathcal{A}}
, ordered by inclusion, one obtains a
direct limit C
∗
-algebra denoted by
M_{{\rm loc}}({\mathcal{A}})
, given by

M_{{\rm loc}}({\mathcal{A}})=\varinjlim_{{\mathcal{J}}\in\mathcal{E}({\mathcal{A}})}M({\mathcal{J}}).

The unital C
∗
-algebra
M_{{\rm loc}}({\mathcal{A}})
is called the local multiplier algebra of
{\mathcal{A}}

[
33
]
,
and it has a realisation as a C
∗
-subalgebra of the enveloping injective
cover of
{\mathcal{A}}
(indeed, sometimes
M_{{\rm loc}}({\mathcal{A}})
, as we shall see below, is the enveloping injective cover of
{\mathcal{A}}
).

In the case of commutative C
∗

C_{0}(X)
, for a locally compact Hausdorff space
X
, the essential ideals are determined by dense open subsets
U
of
X
,
and the multiplier algebras have the form
C(\beta U)
, where
\beta U
is the Stone-Čech compactification of

.

In light of Corollary
3.5
, our interest is in the case

, but we shall actually use the dense open subset

instead,

M_{{\rm loc}}({\mathcal{A}})=M_{{\rm loc}}({\mathcal{J}})
, for any essential ideal
{\mathcal{J}}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Define a topological space
\Delta_{Y}
by way of inverse topological limits:

\Delta_{Y}=\varprojlim_{U\in\mathcal{E}(Y)}\beta U,

where
\mathcal{E}(Y)
is the set of all dense open subsets of

. The space
\Delta_{Y}
is compact, Hausdorff, and
extremely disconnected, which implies the abelian C
∗
-algebra

is injective.

Theorem 3.6
.

\mathcal{I}\left(\mbox{\rm NC}(2)\right)=C(\Delta_{Y})\otimes\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})=M_{{\rm loc}}\left({\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\right)
.

Proof.

Using Corollary
3.5
, the C
∗
-algebra

{\mathcal{J}}=\left\{f\in{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\,|\,f(-1)=f(1)=0\right\}\cong C_{0}\left((-1,1)\right)\otimes\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})

resulting in

Thus,
M_{{\rm loc}}\left({\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\right)
is an injective cover of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})

Since

\mathcal{I}\left(\mbox{\rm NC}(2)\right)=C(\Delta_{Y})\otimes\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})=M_{{\rm loc}}\left({\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\right)

3.2.
Noncommutative cubes, with
d\geq 3

As the C
∗
-algebra
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\cdots*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
of the free product of
d
copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

is residually finite dimensional
[

, one will find noncommutative extreme points of

at infinitely many
finite levels of the grading of

. In fact, these extreme elements occur at all levels of the grading, as explained by the next theorem.

Theorem 3.7
.

(
[
10
,
11
,
28
]
)

If
d\geq 3
, then noncommutative extreme points of
[-1,1]^{d}_{\rm nc}
occur at every level of the
grading.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a separable Hilbert space of dimension at least three, then
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is the double commutant of three projections in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

[
10
]
.
Since
2p-1
is a symmetry, for every projection
p
, we deduce the existence of irreducible triples
(\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{v}_{2},\tilde{v}_{3})
of symmetries acting on separable
Hilbert spaces of every dimension greater than three. Every such triple
(\tilde{v}_{1},\tilde{v}_{2},\tilde{v}_{3})
induces an irreducible representation
\pi
on

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
via
\pi(u_{j})=\tilde{v}_{j}
, where

are the canonical
symmetries generating
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. At level
2
, one may use an irreducible pair associated with the noncommutative square,
and then add an arbitrary symmetry to create an irreducible triple; similarly, the vertices of the
3
-cube
\mbox{\rm C}(3)

give rise to
1
-dimensional representations of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

For
d>3
, one creates
d
-tuples of irreducible symmetries at any given level of the grading
by adding arbitrary symmetries to the irreducible triple of symmetries from the previous paragraph.

\hfill\square

The following result is a construction of irreducible symmetries that is very different from the symmetries considered by Davis
[
10
]
in the proof of Theorem
3.7

above.

Theorem 3.8
.

If
n=2^{m}
, then there exists an irreducible set
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
of
m+1
symmetries in
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
A_{0},\dots,A_{m-1}
are pairwise
commuting.

Proof.

Let
k
be a positive integer and express
k
in its binary representation

k=\sum_{i=0}^{q}k_{i}2^{i}\hskip 42.67912ptk_{i}\in\{0,1\},

for a certain positive integer
q
. Define
m
diagonal matrices
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{m-1}
by setting

A_{i}(k,k)=(-1)^{k_{i}},

where
A_{i}(k,k)
represents the
k
th diagonal entry of
A_{i}
.
It follows directly that every
A_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq{m-1}
is a symmetry. We claim that only diagonal matrices commute with all
A_{i}
. Suppose that
MA_{i}=A_{i}M
for all
i
, where
M\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Take distinct
h
and
k
with
0\leq h,k\leq{n-1}
. Then, there exists some
i
,
0\leq i\leq{m-1}
, for which
h_{i}\neq k_{i}
. Thus,
A_{i}(h,h)\neq A_{i}(k,k)
. Since
M
commute with
A_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle(MA_{i})(h,k)=(A_{i}M)(h,k)\,\mbox{and }\,M(h,k)A_{i}(k,k)=A_{i}(h,h)M(h,k).

As
A_{i}(h,h)\neq A_{i}(k,k)
, we must have that
M(h,k)=0
. Hence,
M(h,k)=0
whenever
h\neq k
which shows that
M
is diagonal.

Next, define

H=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\otimes\cdots\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}}_{m\text{-times}}

and set
A_{m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}H
, which is a symmetry.

Now, let
M
be a diagonal matrix that commutes with
A_{m}
, hence commutes with
H
. Choose distinct integers
h
and
k
where
0\leq h,k\leq n-1
. Then,

\displaystyle(MH)(h,k)=(HM)(h,k)\,\mbox{and}\,M(h,h)H(h,k)=H(h,k)M(k,k).

Note that all entries of
H
are nonzero, so this implies that
M(h,h)=M(k,k)
. As this holds for all distinct
h
and
k
, we conclude that
M
is a scalar multiple of the identity matrix.

Hence, the common commutant of the symmetries
A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}

are scalar multiples of the identity matrix.

\hfill\square

Corollary 3.9
.

For every
d\geq 3
, there exists a noncommutative extreme point
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})

of
[-1,1]^{d}_{\rm nc}
at level
2^{d-1}
of the grading such that

a_{1},\dots,a_{d-1}
are pairwise commuting.

In a different direction, but in a similar spirit, Sunder
[
36
]

has constructed extremal representations of
d
-cubes in which no proper subset
of
d^{\prime}<d
coordinates yields an extremal representation of the
d^{\prime}
-cube.

4.
Representations of Noncommutative Prisms

Our first goal in this section is to show that the operator system
\mbox{\rm NCP}(3)
and noncommutative convex set
\mbox{\rm P}(3)^{\rm max}

are dual objects. This is what we earlier called the Halmos-Mirman Theorem:

Theorem 4.1

(Halmos-Mirman)
.

\mbox{\rm P}(3)^{\rm max}=W_{\rm nc}(w,v)\cong_{{\sf NCConv}}S_{\rm nc}(\mbox{\rm NCP}(3))
,
where
w
and
v
are the canonical unitaries in
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
that arise from the generators
of the cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, respectively.

4.1.
Proof of the Halmos-Mirman Theorem

To prove the Halmos-Mirman Theorem above, some preliminary results are required, beginning with the
following, somewhat surprising, dilation theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

If operators
a
and
b
have dilations to unitary operators of order
k
and
2
, respectively, then

a
and
b
have a common dilation to unitary operators of order
k
and
2
, respectively.

Proof.

By hypothesis, there are a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
, a unitary operator
y
, and an isometry
z:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}

such that
y^{k}=1_{\mathcal{K}}
and
a=z^{*}yz
. Let
\tilde{b}=zbz^{*}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
, which is a selfadjoint contraction, and
consider the operators
\tilde{y}
and
\tilde{v}
on
\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{K}
given by

\tilde{y}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}y&0\\
0&1_{\mathcal{H}^{\oplus 3}}\end{array}\right]\,\mbox{ and }\,\tilde{v}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\tilde{b}&(1-\tilde{b}^{2})^{1/2}\\
(1-\tilde{b}^{2})^{1/2}&-\tilde{b}\end{array}\right].

Thus,
\tilde{y}
and
\tilde{v}
are unitary operators of order
k
and
2
, respectively. By the universal property of the free product,
there is a unital *-homomorphism
\pi:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{K})

such that
\pi(w)=\tilde{y}
and
\pi(v)=\tilde{v}
, where
w
and
v
are the canonical unitary generators of

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.
Let
g:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{K}
be the linear isometry that embeds
\mathcal{K}
into the first direct summand of
\mathcal{K}\oplus\mathcal{K}
,
and define a ucp map

\phi:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
by
\phi(x)=z^{*}g^{*}\pi(x)gz
, for all
x\in{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Hence,

\phi(w)=z^{*}yz=a\,\mbox{ and }\,\phi(v)=z^{*}\tilde{b}z=z^{*}(zbz^{*})z=b,

which gives a common dilation of
a
and
b
to
w
and
v
.

\hfill\square

The next preliminary result was mentioned already as Theorem
2.2
; it is restated and proved below.

Theorem 4.3
.

For every
k\geq 3
,

\mbox{\rm P}(k)^{\rm min}\subseteq W_{\rm nc}(w,v)\subseteq\mbox{\rm P}(k)^{\rm max},

where
w
and
v
are the canonical unitaries in
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
that arise from the generators
of the cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}_{k}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, respectively.

Proof.

By the definition of minimal and maximal noncommutative convex sets,
we are required to show that the joint numerical range of the pair
(w,v)
is the classical
k
-prism

\mbox{\rm P}(k)
.

The inclusion
W_{1}(w,v)\subseteq\mbox{\rm P}(k)
is apparent from the fact that
w
and
v
are unitaries of order
k
and
2
, respectively. Thus,

\phi(w)\in\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{k}
and
\phi(v)\in\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{2}
, for every state
\phi
on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

To show the reverse inclusion,
it is enough, by the convexity of the
W_{1}(w,v)
and
\mbox{\rm P}(k)
, to show that each extreme point of
\mbox{\rm P}(k)

is an element of
W_{1}(w,v)
. The extreme points of
\mbox{\rm P}(k)
are

(\omega^{j},\pm 1)
for
1\leq j\leq k
, where
\omega
is a primitive
k
-th root of unity.

Let
u\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(\mathbb{C})
be the cyclic shift matrix that permutes the canonical basis vectors of
\mathbb{C}^{k}
as follows:

e_{1}\mapsto e_{k},\,e_{2}\mapsto e_{1},\,\dots,e_{k}\mapsto e_{k-1}.

For each
j=1,\dots,k
, let
\tilde{w}_{j}=\omega^{j}u
, which is a unitary of order
k
, and let
\tilde{v}_{+}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}(\mathbb{C})
be the symmetry

\tilde{v}_{+}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\bigoplus 1_{k-2}.

The unit vector
\xi=k^{-1/2}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}e_{j}
is a common eigenvector for
\tilde{v}_{+}
and all
\tilde{w}_{j}
. An application of the
vector state
\phi(x)=\langle x\xi,\xi\rangle
on
\mathcal{M}_{k}(\mathbb{C})
to all pairs
(\tilde{w}_{j},\tilde{v}_{+})
produces all extreme points of
\mbox{\rm P}(k)

that are of the form
(\omega^{j},1)
. To obtain extreme points of the form
(\omega^{j},-1)
, one applies
\phi
to all pairs
(\tilde{w}_{j},\tilde{v}_{-})
,
where
\tilde{v}_{-}=-\tilde{v}_{+}
. Because these matrix pairs are composed of unitaries of order
k
and
2
, each pair

(\tilde{w}_{j},\tilde{v}_{\pm})
is determined by
some representation
\pi:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{k}(\mathbb{C})
sending
w
to
\tilde{w}_{j}
and
v
to

\tilde{v}_{\pm}
; thus, the composition
\phi\circ\pi
yields a state
\psi
on
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
such that

\left(\psi(w),\psi(v)\right)=(\omega^{j},\pm 1)
. Hence,
\mbox{\rm P}(k)\subseteq W_{1}(w,v)
.

\hfill\square

We can now prove Halmos-Mirman Theorem (Theorem
2.3
).

Proof.

Theorem
4.3
shows that
W_{1}(w,v)=\mbox{\rm P}(3)
and
W_{\rm nc}(w,v)\subseteq\mbox{\rm P}(3)^{\rm max}
.
To prove
\mbox{\rm P}(3)^{\rm max}\subseteq W_{\rm nc}(w,v)
, fix
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\aleph_{0}\}
and
select
(a,b)\in\mbox{\rm P}(3)_{n}
. By definition,
W_{1}(a)\subseteq\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{3}
and
W_{1}(b)\subseteq\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{2}
.
The theorems of Mirman and Halmos show that
a
and
b
have independent dilations to unitaries of order 3 and 2, respectively; therefore,
by Theorem
4.2
,
a
and
b
have a common dilation to such a pair of unitaries, which proves
(a,b)\in W_{n}(w,v)
.

\hfill\square

4.2.
Infinite-dimensional representations

Bédos and Omland
[
4
]
have shown that
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
and
{\rm C}^{*}(*_{1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, where
d\geq 3
,
are primitive, and have
uncountably many non-unitarily equivalent irreducible representations on spaces of infinite dimension. Moreover, these C
∗
-algebras
admit faithful irreducible representations that contain no compact operators.
Hence, by invoking Theorem
2.5
and
[
4
, Corollary 3.4]
, we have the following result.

Theorem 4.4
.

For
k,d\geq 3
,
there are uncountably many non-unitarily equivalent noncommutative extreme points of
\mbox{\rm P}(k)^{\rm max}
and
[-1,1]^{d}_{\rm nc}

at level
\aleph_{0}
of the grading, and the injective envelopes of
\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
and
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)

are AW
∗
-factors of type III.

4.3.
Finite-dimensional representations

Recall that if a finite group
G
has a representation on a vector space
V
,
then
V
can be equipped with an inner product so that the images of elements of
G
under the representation are unitary operators acting on
V

[
35
, Theorem II.1.1]
. With this fact in mind, we can now prove:

Theorem 4.5
.

The noncommutative extreme points of the noncommutative triangular prism
\mbox{\rm P}(3)^{\rm max}
occur at
every finite level of the grading.

Proof.

At the first level of the grading, the extremal elements of
\mbox{\rm P}(3)

are of the form
(\omega^{k},\pm 1)
, where
k\in\{0,1,2\}
and
\omega
is a primitive cube root of unity. Thus, each of these extremal points are homomorphic
images of the canonical generators of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}*\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

For higher finite levels
n
of the grading of
\mbox{\rm P}(3)^{\rm max}
, we shall require the consideration of a number of individual cases.

The first case assumes
n>3
, where
n=q=p^{k}
for some prime
p
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Consider the projective special linear group
\mbox{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. By
[
39
, § 3.3.2]
,
the group
\mbox{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is simple if and only if
q>3
. Consider the elements of
\mbox{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
given by

(4.1)

\displaystyle U=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&-1\end{bmatrix}\mbox{ and }V=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

We note that
U
is the element of
\mbox{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
corresponding to the companion matrix of
t^{2}+t+1
, so
U^{3}=1
and
V^{2}=1
.
It is known that
\mbox{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is generated by the matrices
U
and
V
if and only if
q\neq 9
(see
[
29
]
).

First, we suppose that
q>3
and
q\neq 9
. The representations of the group
\mbox{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
are well understood and there exists an irreducible representation of
\mbox{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
onto a vector space
V
of dimension
q
(see
[
16
]
). Since
\mbox{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is a finite group, the vector space
V
can be equipped with an inner product such that the images of elements of
\mbox{\rm PSL}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
under a representations are unitary operators acting on
V

[
35
, Theorem II.1.1]
.
Consequently, there exists an irreducible unitary representation
\rho
of
\mbox{\rm PSL}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
into
\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
. Since
q>3
, the group
\mbox{\rm PSL}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is simple, hence these representations are faithful. Consequently, we get
\{\rho(U),\rho(V)\}
is an irreducible set of unitary matrices in
\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\rho(U)^{3}=1=\rho(V)^{2}
. By Schur’s lemma, the commutant of
\{\rho(U),\rho(V)\}
must be the set of all scalar matrices. Using the universal property of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, we get an irreducible representation of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
into
\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
, where
q>3
is a prime power such that
q\neq 9
.

Now, let
q=9
. Then, we consider the group
\mbox{\rm PSL}(\mathbb{F}_{17})
. As we have noted before, this group is generated by the elements
U
and
V
given in (
4.1
). Moreover, it admits an irreducible representation (which is faithful by simplicity) of dimension equal to
\tfrac{17+1}{2}=9
. Using a similar argument as above, we obtain an irreducible representation of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
into
\mathcal{M}_{9}(\mathbb{C})
.

The second case assumes
n=2
. The symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{3}
has presentation
\langle U,V|U^{3}=V^{2}=1,VUV=U^{-1}\rangle
. It admits a faithful representation of dimension
2
, so again we can use Schur’s lemma and the argument
above to obtain an irreducible representation of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
into
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

The third case assumes
n=3
. The alternating group
\operatorname{A}_{4}
has presentation
\langle U,V|U^{3}=V^{2}=1,UVU=VU^{2}V\rangle
. The group
\operatorname{A}_{4}
admits a faithful irreducible representation of dimension
3
, so again we obtain an irreducible representation of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
into
\mathcal{M}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
.

The final case assumes
n\geq 2
is an arbitrary integer.
In this case, we consider the prime factorization of
n=2^{\ell}p_{1}^{n_{1}}\dots p_{k}^{n_{k}}
, where
\ell\in\mathbb{\{}0\}\cup\mathbb{N}
and
p_{i}
are distinct odd primes with
n_{i}\geq 1
.

Suppose that
\ell\geq 1
. Using the previous cases, there exist irreducible representations
\rho_{0}:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
into
\mathcal{M}_{2^{\ell}}(\mathbb{C})
when
\ell\geq 1
, and
\rho_{i}:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
into
\mathcal{M}_{p_{i}^{n_{i}}}(\mathbb{C})
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Since the tensor product of irreducible representations of a C
∗
-algebra is an irreducible representation, we get an irreducible representation of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
into
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, where
n=2^{\ell}p_{1}^{n_{1}}\dots p_{k}^{n_{k}}
.

Next, suppose that
\ell=0
or equivalently,
n
is odd. Then, with a similar argument as above, there exists an irreducible representation
\rho_{i}:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
into
\mathcal{M}_{p_{i}^{n_{i}}}(\mathbb{C})
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. The tensor product of these yields an irreducible representation of
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{3}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
into
\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, where
n=p_{1}^{n_{1}}\dots p_{k}^{n_{k}}
.

\hfill\square

4.4.
Factorial representations

If
G
is a finitely generated discrete group with a prescribed set of generators
\mathfrak{u}
, and if
\mathcal{O}_{G}

is the operator system spanned by the identity and the elements of
\mathfrak{u}
and their adjoints, then a noncommutative
state
\phi:\mathcal{O}_{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is
factorial
if the von Neumann algebra
\phi(\mathcal{O}_{G})^{\prime\prime}
is a factor.
If
\phi
extends to a representation
\pi:\mathcal{O}_{G}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, then we say that
\pi
is a
factorial representation
.

Theorems
3.7
,
4.4
, and
4.5
indicate
\mathcal{O}_{G}
admits factorial representations
of type I
n
and I
∞
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
\mathcal{O}_{G}
is one of
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)
or
\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
, with
d,k\geq 3
, .
However, these operator systems also admit factorial representations of type II
1
and
\mbox{\rm II}_{\infty}
.

Theorem 4.6
.

The operator systems
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)
and
\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
, for
d,k\geq 3
, admit factorial representations of type
\mbox{\rm II}_{1}

and
\mbox{\rm II}_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Because
\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, where
k\geq 3
, and the free products of
d\geq 3
copies of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

are infinite conjugacy class groups, their group von Neumann algebras are

\mbox{\rm II}_{1}
factors. Hence, the unitary generators of these
group von Neumann algebras induce factorial representations from the corresponding group C
∗
-algebra, thereby yielding type-
\mbox{\rm II}_{1}

factorial representations of
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)
and
\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
, for
d,k\geq 3
. Factorial representations of type
\mbox{\rm II}_{\infty}

are obtained through tensor products of type
\mbox{\rm II}_{1}
and
\mbox{\rm I}_{\infty}
factorial representations.

\hfill\square

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
in their
reduced group C
∗
-algebras were considered in

]
.

5.
Quotients and Duality

5.1.
Representations as operator system quotients

If
{\mathcal{S}}
is an operator system and
\psi:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a ucp map, then the quotient vector space

{\mathcal{S}}/\ker\psi
has the structure of an operator system. We denote the elements of the quotient by
\dot{x}
, for
x\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
The canonical Archimedean order unit for
{\mathcal{S}}/\ker\psi
is given by
\dot{1}
, where
1\in{\mathcal{S}}
is the Archimedean order unit for
{\mathcal{S}}
.

An operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is an
operator system quotient
if there exist an operator system
{\mathcal{S}}

and a unital completely positive linear map
\psi:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}
such that the induced ucp map

\dot{\psi}:{\mathcal{S}}/\ker\psi\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}
, defined by
\dot{\psi}(\dot{x})=\psi(x)
, is a complete order isomorphism.
In this situation,
\psi
is said to be a complete quotient map.

The noncommutative cubes
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)
were shown, for all
d\geq 2
,
to be operator system quotients of finite-dimensional operator systems of diagonal matrices
in
[
13
, §5]
. The methods used in that paper apply directly to the case of
noncommutative prisms.

Theorem 5.1
.

(
[
13
, §5]
)

For each
k\geq 3
, there is a ucp map
\psi_{k}:\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
such that

\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\cong_{\sf OpSys}(\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2})/\ker\psi_{k}.

A typical use of such a quotient representation is to pull back questions about positivity from the quotient to the source operator system; in particular,
a matrix
Y\in\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mbox{\rm NCP}(k))
is strictly positive if and only if there is a strictly positive matrix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2})

such that
Y=\dot{X}
.

A second typical use is to employ complete quotient maps to analyse the operator system dual of a finite-dimensional operator system, which is our next goal.

5.2.
Representations of the operator system dual

If
{\mathcal{R}}
is a finite-dimensional operator system, then its dual space
{\mathcal{R}}^{\delta}
carries the structure of an operator system by declaring
a matrix
X=[f_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{q}
to be positive if the associated linear map
\hat{X}:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
, where

\hat{X}(x)=[f_{ij}(x)]_{i,j=1}^{q}
,
is completely positive. An Archimedean order unit for
{\mathcal{R}}^{\delta}
with this matrix ordering is given by any faithful state on
{\mathcal{R}}
; hence,
in this regard, there is no canonical choice of Archimedean order unit for
{\mathcal{R}}^{\delta}
in terms of the Archimedean order unit for
{\mathcal{R}}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\delta}\cong_{\sf OpSys}\mathbb{C}^{d}
with respect to
the Archimedean order unit of the operator system dual
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\delta}

given by the faithful positive linear functional
f_{\gamma}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by

f_{\gamma}(\xi)=\langle\xi,\gamma\rangle,\mbox{ for }\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d},

where
\gamma=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}e_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Proof.

Fix
q\in\mathbb{N}
. A matrix
X=[f_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{q}\in\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is positive if and only if the linear map
\hat{X}:\mathbb{C}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
given by
\hat{X}(\xi)=[f_{ij}(\xi)]_{i,j=1}^{q}
is completely positive. Since
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is an
abelian C
∗
-algebra,
X
is
positive if and only if
\hat{X}
is a positive map. Because the canonical basis
\{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
determines the positive cone
(\mathbb{C}^{d})_{+}
,
the positivity of
X
is equivalent to the positivity of
\hat{X}(e_{j})
for all
1\leq j\leq d
.
Hence, there is a linear order isomorphism

\mathcal{M}_{q}((\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\delta})\cong_{\rm ord}\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C})\times\dots\times\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C})=\left(\displaystyle\bigoplus_{1}^{d}\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C})\right).

The canonical isomorphisms that identify
\displaystyle\bigoplus_{1}^{d}\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
,

\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
are positivity preserving, and
so the linear order isomorphism above identifies the positive cones
(\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\delta}))_{+}
and
(\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathbb{C}^{d}))_{+}
.

Hence,
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\delta}
is unital complete order isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Let
\xi=\sum_{j=1}^{d}e_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and consider the faithful positive linear functional
f_{\xi}
given by
f_{\xi}(\eta)=\langle\eta,\xi\rangle
, for all
\eta\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, which is
an the Archimedean order unit for
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\delta}
. The unital complete order isomorphism above sends
f_{\xi}
to
\xi
, the Archimedean order unit of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and so we deduce
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\delta}\cong_{\sf OpSys}\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

\hfill\square

Every
n
-positive linear map
\phi:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}
of finite-dimensional operator systems induces an
n
-positive linear adjoint map

\phi^{\delta}:{\mathcal{T}}^{\delta}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}^{\delta}
. In particular, if
\phi
is a complete quotient map, then
\phi^{\delta}
is a complete order embedding
of
{\mathcal{T}}^{\delta}
into
{\mathcal{R}}^{\delta}
.

Theorem 5.3
.

For every
k\geq 3
,

\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)^{\delta}\cong_{\sf OpSys}{\mathcal{S}}_{k,2},

where
{\mathcal{S}}_{k,2}
is the operator system of all tuples

\zeta=(z_{1},\dots,z_{k},z_{k+1},z_{k+2})
for which

z_{1}+\cdots+z_{k}=z_{k+1}+z_{k+2}.

Proof.

By

[
13
, Theorem 5.1]
,
there exists a unital complete quotient map

\psi_{k}:\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
such that

\ker\psi_{k}=\left\{\lambda\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}e_{j}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{2}e_{k+\ell}\right)\,|\,\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

Therefore, the linear map
\psi_{k}^{\delta}:\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)^{\delta}\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\delta}
is a complete
order embedding.

Let
f_{\gamma}
denote the Archimedean order unit of
(\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\delta}

designated in Lemma
5.2
, and let
\mu\in\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)^{\delta}
be the unique preimage of

f_{\gamma}
under
\psi_{k}^{\delta}
. Then
\mu
is a faithful state on
\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
and, by the isomorphism of
Lemma
5.2
, we may view
\psi_{k}^{\delta}
as a unital complete order embedding of

\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)^{\delta}
into the abelian C
∗
-algebra
\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}
with range
{\mathcal{S}}_{k,2}
.

\hfill\square

5.3.
Automatic complete positivity of positive linear maps

Theorem 5.4
.

If
{\mathcal{T}}
is an operator system and if
{\mathcal{R}}
is any one of
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)
or
\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
, for
d,k\geq 2
,
then every positive linear map

\phi:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}
is completely positive.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.1
,
there exists a unital complete quotient map

\psi_{k}:\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
;
therefore, the linear map
\psi_{k}^{\delta}:\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)^{\delta}\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\delta}
is a complete
order embedding.

Now if
\phi:\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}
is positive, then by considering the codomain of
\phi
to be
the finite-dimensional operator subsystem generated by the order unit of
{\mathcal{T}}

and the range of
\phi
, we may suppose without loss of generality that
{\mathcal{T}}
has finite dimension.
Thus,
\psi_{k}^{\delta}\circ\phi^{\delta}:{\mathcal{T}}^{\delta}\rightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\delta}
is a positive linear map. Since

(\mathbb{C}^{k}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\delta}
is unitally completely order isomorphic to an abelian C
∗
-algebra, the positive linear map

\psi_{k}^{\delta}\circ\phi^{\delta}
is necessarily completely positive. Thus, as
\psi_{k}^{\delta}
is a complete order embedding,
\phi^{\delta}

must also be completely positive, which implies
\phi
is completely positive.

A similar argument applies in the case of
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)
.

\hfill\square

Recall that an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is said to be an
OMAX operator system

if every positive linear map of
{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}
, for every operator system
{\mathcal{T}}
, is completely
positive. Similarly, an operator system
{\mathcal{S}}
is an
OMIN operator system

if every positive linear map
\mathcal{U}\rightarrow{\mathcal{S}}
, for every operator system
\mathcal{U}
, is completely
positive. Thus, the results of this section indicate that if
{\mathcal{R}}
is one of
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)
or
\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
, for
d,k\geq 2
,
then
{\mathcal{R}}
is an OMAX system and
{\mathcal{R}}^{\delta}
is an OMIN system.

6.
Tensor Products

The theory of operator system tensor products
[
24
]

is inspired by the corresponding theory for C
∗
-algebras, although there are some striking differences between the theories.

Henceforth, if

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
are operator subsystems of
{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
, respectively, and if
\otimes_{\sigma}
is an
operator system tensor product, then the notation

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\,\subseteq\,{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{2}

indicates that the canonical algebraic embedding
\iota:{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
of algebraic tensor product spaces is
a completely positive linear map
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
.
The stronger notation,

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\,\subseteq_{\rm coi}\,{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{2},

indicates that the canonical algebraic embedding
\iota:{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
of algebraic tensor product spaces is
a complete order embedding
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
. In this later setting, if a
matrix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{q}({\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{1})
is positive in
\mathcal{M}_{q}({\mathcal{R}}_{2}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{2})
, then it is also positive in
\mathcal{M}_{q}({\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}_{1})
.
Thus, for two operator system tensor product structures
\otimes_{\sigma}
and
\otimes_{\tau}
, we write

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\tau}{\mathcal{T}}
if

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}\,\subseteq_{\rm coi}\,{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\tau}{\mathcal{T}}\,\mbox{ and }\,{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\tau}{\mathcal{T}}\,\subseteq_{\rm coi}\,{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}.

Recall from
[
24
]
that
there exist minimal and maximal operator system tensor product structures
\otimes_{\rm min}
and
\otimes_{\rm max}

such that, for any operator system tensor product structure

\otimes_{\sigma}
, and any operator systems
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
, we have

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}\,\subseteq\,{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}\,\subseteq\,{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{T}}.

The main result of this section shows that, if
{\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{T}}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 2\}
, then

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{T}}\not={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}.

The distinction between
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{T}}
and
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}
above
is known if both
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
are noncommutative cubes
[
12
]
, and so the
novelty here occurs when one or both of the tensor factors is
\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
. To show this, it is useful to use C
∗
-algebra tensor products.

If
{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathcal{B}
are unital C
∗
-algebras, then the operator system tensor products
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{B}
and
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\rm max}\mathcal{B}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathcal{B}

are closely related to the minimal and maximal C
∗
-algebra tensor products, which have the same notation, in which the norm completions of
the operator systems
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{B}
and
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\rm max}\mathcal{B}
result in the C
∗
-algebras
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{B}
and
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\rm max}\mathcal{B}
. Therefore, in what follows,
the notation
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{B}
and
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes_{\rm max}\mathcal{B}
always refers to the C
∗
-algebra tensor products.

As in most works involving operator system tensor products,
the commuting operator system tensor product structure, denoted by
\otimes_{\rm c}
,
has a substantial role here. Recall that a matrix
X\in M_{q}({\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{T}})
is positive, if for all ucp maps

\phi:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
\psi:{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with commuting ranges, the matrix
(\phi\cdot\psi)^{[q]}(X)
is positive in

\mathcal{M}_{q}(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
, where
\phi\cdot\psi
is the linear map on
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
satisfying
(\phi\cdot\psi)(x\otimes y)=\phi(x)\psi(y)
, for all

x\in{\mathcal{R}}
and
y\in{\mathcal{T}}
. An important feature of this tensor product is that
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{A}}
, whenever
{\mathcal{A}}
is a unital C
∗
-algebra.
The determination of
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{T}}
is often difficult; as shown in
[
13
,
23
]
, basic questions
about
\otimes_{\rm c}
can sometimes be logically equivalent to questions regarding the Connes Embedding Problem.

6.1.
Some general propositions

As defined earlier in equation (
2.2
),

\mathcal{O}_{G_{j}}
shall denote the operator subsystem of
{\rm C}^{*}(G_{j})
determined by the unitary generators, and their adjoints, of a fixed finite
set of generators of a discrete group
G_{j}
.

The commuting tensor product
\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}
is induced by the complete
order embedding

\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}\,\subseteq_{\rm coi}\,{\rm C}^{*}_{\rm max}(\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}_{\rm max}(\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}).

Therefore, the
case in which

\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}\,\subseteq_{\rm coi}\,{\rm C}^{*}_{\rm min}(\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}_{\rm min}(\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}})

is quite special and has strong consequences, as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 6.1
.

If
\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
,
then
u_{1}\otimes u_{2}
is unitary in
{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
for each of the finitely-many
unitary generators
u_{j}\in\mathcal{O}_{G_{j}}
of
G_{j}
, and

{\rm C}^{*}_{\rm min}(\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}})={\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2}).

Proof.

As defined in
[
12
]
,
the operator system tensor product
\otimes_{\rm ess}
is given by the inclusion of
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}
into
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{T}})
.
Since
{\rm C}^{*}(G_{j})={\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{G_{j}})
, the hypothesis
\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})

implies
\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm ess}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}=\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}
, which in turn implies

{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})={\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}})
and
that

u_{1}\otimes 1_{G_{2}}
and
1_{G_{1}}\otimes u_{2}
are unitaries in
{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
,
for each of the finitely-many
unitary generators
u_{j}\in\mathcal{O}_{G_{j}}
of
G_{j}

[
12
, Theorem 3.5]
.
Furthermore, by
[
12
, Lemma 3.4]
,
u_{1}\otimes u_{2}=(u_{1}\otimes 1_{G_{2}})(1_{G_{1}}\otimes u_{2})
,
and so
u_{1}\otimes u_{2}
is unitary in
{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
.

\hfill\square

Proposition 6.2
.

If
\mathcal{O}_{G_{j}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{R}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{j})\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{R}
for all operator systems
\mathcal{R}
and
j=1,2
, then

\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}=\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}\implies{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm min}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})={\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2}).

Proof.

Assume
\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}=\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}
. By hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}=\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}

\displaystyle\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}

\displaystyle\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm c}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})

\displaystyle={\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2}).

Let
\mathcal{W}=\{u_{1}\otimes u_{2}\,|\,u_{j}\in\mathcal{O}_{G_{j}}\text{ is a unitary generator of }G_{j}\}\subseteq{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm min}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2}),

where the identity elements of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are considered as unitary generators.
If
\iota:\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}\rightarrow{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
is the unital complete order embedding
that exists by the coi-inclusions above, then,
by Proposition
6.1
,

\iota(u_{1}\otimes u_{2})
is unitary in
{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
, for every
u_{1}\otimes u_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
.
Hence,
\iota
maps the spanning set of unitaries in
\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}

to unitary elements of
{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
; thus,
\mathcal{W}
is in the multiplicative domain of
\iota
.
Hence,
\iota
is a unital
*
-homomorphism on

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{W})={\rm C}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}})={\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}})\otimes_{\rm min}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}),

where the final equality is a consequence of
[
24
, Theorem 4.4]
. Therefore,
we obtain a unital
\ast
-monomorphism

{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm min}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})\rightarrow{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
, which proves

{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm min}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})={\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2}),

as required.

\hfill\square

With the resolution of the Connes Embedding Problem in the negative
[
17
,
21
]
, the C
∗
-algebras

{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm min}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
and
{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
are now known to be non-isomorphic for
many groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
. Hence, as
an immediate corollary of Proposition
6.2
, we deduce the following result.

Corollary 6.3
.

If each of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
contains the free group
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
as a subgroup, and if
\mathcal{O}_{G_{j}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{R}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{j})\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{R}
for every operator system
\mathcal{R}
, then

\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}\neq\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}.

Proof.

If, on the contrary,
\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}=\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}
, then Proposition
6.2

would imply
{\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm min}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})={\rm C}^{*}(G_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}(G_{2})
, which in turn would imply

{\rm C}^{*}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\otimes_{\rm min}{\rm C}^{*}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})={\rm C}^{*}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}^{*}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
by the hypotheis
\mathbb{F}_{2}\subseteq G_{j}

[
13
, Proposition 3.3]
. However, the equality of the C
∗
-algebra tensor products involving
{\rm C}^{*}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})

is Kirchberg’s equivalency to an affirmative outcome of the CEP
[
26
]
; hence, it cannot be that

\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}=\mathcal{O}_{G_{1}}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{O}_{G_{2}}
.

\hfill\square

6.2.
The main result on tensor products

Our aim is to prove the following theorem (Theorem
6.5
):
if
{\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{T}}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 3\}
, then

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{T}}\not={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{T}}\not={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}.

Lemma 6.4
.

For every operator system
\mathcal{R}
, and
d,k\geq 2
,

\mbox{\rm NC}(d)\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{R}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}^{*}(*_{1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}_{2})\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{R}\,\mbox{ and }\,\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{R}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{R}.

Proof.

The assertion
\mbox{\rm NC}(d)\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{R}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}^{*}(*_{1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}_{2})\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{R}
is proved in

[
12
, Lemma 7.2]
.

Consider now the case of
\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
, with
k\geq 3
.
By definition of
\otimes_{\rm c}
, it is sufficient to show that, for every pair of ucp maps

\psi:\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
\phi:\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with commuting ranges, there exists a ucp extension

\Psi:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
of
\psi
such that
\Psi
and
\phi
also have commuting ranges.

As done earlier, denote the canonical unitary generators of
\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)
by
w
and
v
, where
w^{k}=v^{2}=1
. Let

\psi:\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
\phi:\mathcal{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be ucp maps with commuting ranges.
Express
\psi
in its Stinespring decomposition:
\psi=s^{*}\rho s
, for some unital
*
-representation

\rho:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\rho})
and isometry
s:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{\rho}
. As
\rho(w)
is a unitary of order

k
, it admits a spectral decomposition
\rho(w)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\omega^{j}p_{j}
, where
\omega
is a primitive
k
-th root of
unity and each
p_{j}
is a spectral projection. Thus, if
h_{j}=s^{*}p_{j}s
, for each
j
, then

\displaystyle\psi(w)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\omega^{j}h_{j}
.

Consider the isometry
z:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}
, where
\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}
is the direct sum of
k
copies of
\mathcal{H}
, given by

z\xi=h_{0}^{1/2}\xi\oplus\cdots\oplus h_{k-1}^{1/2}\xi
, for
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
. Let
b:\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}
be given by
b=z\psi(v)z^{*}
.
Then,
b
is a selfadjoint contraction and, therefore, admits a Halmos dilation to a symmetry
\tilde{v}
on
\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}
:

\tilde{v}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}b&(1-b^{2})^{1/2}\\
(1-b^{2})^{1/2}&-b\end{array}\right].

If
y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k})
and
\tilde{w}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k})
are given by

y=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{k-1}\omega^{j}1_{\mathcal{H}}\,\mbox{ and }\,\tilde{w}=y\oplus 1_{\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}},

then,
\tilde{w}
is a unitary of order
k
and, by the universal property, there exists
a unital
*
-homomorpshim

\pi:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k})

such that
\pi(w)=\tilde{w}
and
\pi(v)=\tilde{v}
.
With respect to the decomposition
\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k}
, express
\pi
as a

2\times 2
matrix of maps:

\pi=\begin{bmatrix}\pi_{11}&\pi_{12}\\
\pi_{21}&\pi_{22}\end{bmatrix}.

Define a linear map
\Psi:{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
by
\Psi(x)=z^{*}\pi_{11}(x)z
, for
x\in{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.
Because
\pi_{11}
is a compression of a
*
-homomorphism,
\pi_{11}
and, hence,
\Psi
are completely positive. Since
\Psi(w)=\psi(w)

and
\Psi(v)=\psi(v)
, the map
\Psi
is a completely positive extension of
\psi
to
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.

It remains to show that
\Psi
and
\phi
have commuting ranges. To this end,
first note that, because
\rho(w)
is a normal operator with finite spectrum, each spectral projection
p_{j}
of
\rho(w)
is a polynomial in
\rho(w)
,
say
p_{j}=f_{j}(\rho(w))
, for some
f_{j}\in\mathbb{C}[t]
. Thus,
h_{j}=f_{j}(\psi(w))
, for every
j
, and any operator
g
that commutes
with
\psi(w)
also commutes with
h_{j}
; further, by functional calculus,
g
also commutes with
h_{j}^{1/2}
.

For each
r\in\mathcal{R}
, let
\Phi(r)=\phi(r)\oplus\cdots\oplus\phi(r)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\oplus k})
. As
\phi(r)
commutes with
\psi(w)
,
\phi(r)
commutes
with each
h_{j}
and, therefore,
\Phi(r)z=z\phi(r)
. Passing to adjoints in
{\mathcal{R}}
, we also have
z^{*}\phi(r)=z^{*}\Phi(r)
, for every
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
.

Recall
b=z\psi(v)z^{*}
and, for every
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
,
\phi(r)
commutes with
\psi(v)
. Thus,

\Phi(r)b=\Phi(r)z\psi(v)z^{*}=z\phi(r)\psi(v)z^{*}=z\psi(v)\phi(r)z^{*}=z\psi(v)z^{*}\Phi(r)=b\Phi(r),

which implies
\Phi(r)
commutes with
(1-b^{2})^{1/2}
and
\Phi(r)\oplus\Phi(r)
commutes with
\tilde{v}
.

Note that every
\Phi(r)\oplus\Phi(r)
also commutes with
\tilde{v}
. Hence, as
\pi
is a homomorphism, each

\Phi(r)\oplus\Phi(r)
commutes with
\pi(a)
, for every
a\in{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. In passing to the
(1,1)
-entry of the

2\times 2
operator matrix
\pi(a)
,
\Phi(r)\pi_{11}(a)=\pi_{11}(z)\Phi(r)
, for all
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
and
a\in{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
.
Thus,

\phi(r)z^{*}\pi_{11}(a)z=z^{*}\Phi(r)\pi_{11}(a)z=z^{*}\pi_{11}(a)\Phi(r)z=z^{*}\pi_{11}(a)z\phi(r)

yields
\phi(r)\Psi(a)=\Psi(a)\phi(r)
, for all
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
and
a\in{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Hence,

\Psi
and
\phi
have commuting ranges.

\hfill\square

We now are ready to prove the main result of this section. (If
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
are noncommutative cubes,
then
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{T}}\not={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}
was
established previously in
[
12
, Theorem 7.13]
.)

Theorem 6.5
.

If
{\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{T}}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 3\}
, then

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{T}}\not={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{T}}\not={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.4
,
every operator system
{\mathcal{R}}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 3\}

satisfies
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{S}}
for every operator system
{\mathcal{S}}
.
Further, the groups
\ast_{1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2}
each contain
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
as a subgroup, when
k,d\geq 3
.
Therefore, any pair of operator systems
{\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{T}}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 3\}
satisfy the hypothesis of
Corollary
6.3
; hence, by Corollary
6.3
,

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{T}}\not={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{T}}
.

An unpublished result of Kavruk
[
22
, Lemma 6.7]
shows there exist unital complete order embeddings

\iota_{\mathcal{R}}:\mbox{\rm NC}(2)\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}
and
\iota_{\mathcal{T}}:\mbox{\rm NC}(2)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}
and ucp maps

q_{\mathcal{R}}:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mbox{\rm NC}(2)
and
q_{\mathcal{T}}:{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow\mbox{\rm NC}(2)
such that
q_{\mathcal{R}}\circ\iota_{\mathcal{R}}

and
q_{\mathcal{T}}\circ\iota_{\mathcal{T}}
are identify maps on
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
. If there were to exist a unital complete order embedding

\gamma:{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}
, then
(q_{\mathcal{R}}\otimes q_{\mathcal{T}})\circ\gamma\circ(\iota_{\mathcal{R}}\otimes\iota_{\mathcal{T}})

would be a unital complete order embedding

\mbox{\rm NC}(2)\otimes_{\rm c}\mbox{\rm NC}(2)\rightarrow\mbox{\rm NC}(2)\otimes_{\rm max}\mbox{\rm NC}(2)
, which is in contradiction to

[
12
, Corollary 7.12]
. Hence, it must be that

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{T}}\not={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}
.

\hfill\square

7.
Applications and Additional Properties

7.1.
Scaling constants for minimal and maximal noncommutative cubes and prisms

If, for a compact convex subset
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
, there exists a
real number
C>0
such that

K^{\rm max}\,\subseteq\,C\cdot K^{\rm min},

then
C
is called a
scaling constant
for
K
.

While not all compact convex sets
K
admit a scaling constant, the classical prisms and cubes do.
To see this, we draw
on the work of Passer, Shalit, and Solel in
[
32
]
; in particular, they
show that any compact convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
containing
0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

in the interior of
K
admits a minimal scaling constant.

For every
d\geq 2
, the minimal scaling constant for
[-1,1]^{d}
is exactly
\sqrt{d}

[
32
, p. 3233]
.
Let
\theta_{k}
denote the minimal scaling constant for
\mbox{\rm P}(k)
. We aim to find a lower bound for
\theta_{k}
.

By
[
32
, Proposition 3.4]
, if
K,L\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are compact convex bodies, then

\theta(K)\leq\rho(K,L)\theta(L),

where
\theta(K)
and
\theta(L)
are the minimal scaling constants for
K
and
L
, and

\rho(K,L)=\inf\{D>0\,|\,\exists\,R\in\mbox{\rm GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\mbox{ such that }K\subseteq R(L)\subseteq D\cdot K\}.

We shall use these quantities above for the convex bodies

K=\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
, the closed Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
L=\mbox{\rm P}(k)
.
The minimal
scaling constant for
\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
is exactly
d

[
32
]
, and so we seek an upper bound on

\rho(\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}},\mbox{\rm P}(k))
in the inequality

d\leq\rho(\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}},\mbox{\rm P}(k))\,\theta_{k}.

To this end, note that if
\xi\in\mbox{\rm P}(k)
, then
\xi\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}\times[-1,1]
and, therefore,

\|\xi\|\leq\sqrt{2}
. Thus,

\mbox{\rm P}(k)\,\subseteq\,\sqrt{2}\cdot\mathbb{B}_{1}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}}.

Because the origin
0\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is interior to both
\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
and
\mbox{\rm P}(k)
,
there is a maximal
r_{k}>0
for which

r_{k}\cdot\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\subseteq\mbox{\rm P}(k).

By projecting onto the plane determined by the first two coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
we see that the projection of
r_{k}\cdot\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

onto this plane must lie within the circle of radius
s_{k}
inscribed by the convex hull

\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{k}
of the
k
-th roots of unity and centred at the origin. Thus,
r_{k}\leq s_{k}
. Since the incircle of
\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{k}
is tangent to the sides of the regular polygon
\mbox{\rm P}(k)

and touches the sides exactly at the midpoint of sides,
the radius
s_{k}
of the incircle equals the length of the midpoint of line segment
joining
1
and
\omega
.
The midpoint of line segment
joining
1
and
\omega
is
\frac{1}{2}(1+\cos(2\frac{\pi}{k}),\sin(2\frac{\pi}{k}))
, with length equal to
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2+2\cos(2\frac{\pi}{k})}=\cos(\frac{\pi}{k})
.
Hence,

r_{k}\leq s_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right).

Furthermore, if
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\|\xi\|\leq\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)
, then
|\xi_{3}|\leq\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\leq 1
, and
\|(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\|\leq\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)
. Since the radius of incircle in
\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{k}
exactly equals
\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)
, hence
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{k}
, that implies
\xi\in\mbox{\rm P}(k)
. Thus, we actually get

r_{k}=s_{k}=\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right).

From

\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\subseteq\frac{1}{r_{k}}\cdot\mbox{\rm P}(k)\subseteq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r_{k}}\cdot\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}},

we deduce
\rho(\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}},\mbox{\rm P}(k))\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r_{k}}
. Thus,

\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{k}\right)\leq\frac{3}{\sqrt{2}}r_{k}\leq\frac{3}{\rho(\mathbb{B}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}},\mbox{\rm P}(k))}\leq\theta_{k}.

Thus, for the classical triangular prism
\mbox{\rm P}(3)
, we have

\theta_{3}\geq\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}},

resulting in the following variant of the Halmos-Mirman dilation theorem (Theorem
2.3
).

Theorem 7.1

(Scaled Halmos-Mirman Theorem)
.

If
x,y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
are such that
x
has numerical range contained in the triangle
\mbox{\rm Conv}\,C_{3}

and
y
is a selfadjoint contraction, then there are commuting unitaries
u
and
v
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
containing
\mathcal{H}
as a susbspace, and a constant

C\geq\frac{3}{2\sqrt{2}}
, such that
u^{3}=v^{2}=1
and

Cu
and
Cv
are joint dilations of
x
and
y
to
\mathcal{K}
.

7.2.
Exactness and the lifting property

A finite-dimensional operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
is
exact
if

({\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathbb{B})/({\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathbb{K})\cong_{\rm OpSys}{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}(\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}),

where
\mathbb{B}=\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}))
and
\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{B}
is the ideal of compact operators.
A finite-dimensional operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
has the
lifting property
if every ucp map
{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mathbb{B}/\mathbb{K}

has a ucp lift to
\mathbb{B}
.

For finite-dimensional operator systems, the
relation between exactness and the lifting property is given by a theorem of Kavruk
[
23
, Theorem 6.6]
: a finite-dimensional operator system

{\mathcal{R}}
is exact if and only if its operator system dual
{\mathcal{R}}^{\delta}
has the lifting property.

Theorem 7.2
.

If
{\mathcal{R}}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 3\}
, then
{\mathcal{R}}
has the lifting property, but is not an exact operator
system.

Proof.

The operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
has the lifting property because

{\mathcal{R}}
is an OMAX operator system
[
23
, Lemma 9.10]
. To show
{\mathcal{R}}
is not exact, recall that

{\rm C}^{*}({\mathbb{F}}_{2})
admits an embedding into both
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
and
{\rm C}^{*}(\ast_{1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}_{2})

because

{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
is a subgroup of both
*_{1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{k}*\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. As
{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
is not an exact C
∗
-algebra
[
37
]
, neither are

{\rm C}^{*}(\mathbb{Z}_{k}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2})
or
{\rm C}^{*}(\ast_{1}^{d}\mathbb{Z}_{2})
, since exactness passes to subalgebras.
In other words,
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
is not exact. Because
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})
is generated by the unitaries in
{\mathcal{R}}
,
the operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
cannot be exact
[
25
, Corollary 9.6]
.

\hfill\square

In relation to the problem of finding low-dimensional operator systems lacking the lifting property
[
20
]
, we note the following
result.

Corollary 7.3
.

The
4
-dimensional operator system

\mathcal{S}_{3,2}=\{(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4},z_{5})\in\mathbb{C}^{5}\,|\,z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3}=z_{4}+z_{5}\},

with matrix-ordering induced by the C
∗
-algebra
\mathbb{C}^{5}
is exact, but
does not have the lifting property.

Proof.

The operator system
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}
has the lifting property if and only if its dual,
\mbox{\rm NC}(3)
, is exact. Because
\mathcal{S}_{3,2}
generates an abelian C
∗
-algebra, it is an exact operator system.

\hfill\square

Similarly, using noncommutative cubes and

k
-prisms, and
[
13
, Theorem 5.9]
, we have:

Corollary 7.4
.

For every
k,d\geq 3
, the operator systems

\mathcal{R}_{d}=\{(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1},\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}+\beta_{d},\alpha_{d}-\beta_{d})\,|\,\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{C},j=1,\dots,d\}

and

\mathcal{S}_{k,2}=\{(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k+2}\,|\,z_{1}+\cdots+z_{k-2}=z_{k-1}+z_{k}\},

with matrix-orderings induced by the C
∗
-algebras
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
\mathbb{C}^{k+2}

are exact, but
do not have the lifting property.

7.3.
Controlling the codomain of ucp extensions

If one has an injective von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{N}
and an inclusion
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
of operator systems,
then every ucp map
\phi:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
extends to a ucp map
\Phi:{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
. However, usually there
is little one can say about the range of an extension
\Phi
relative to the range of
\phi
. Interestingly, noncommutative cubes and prisms
admit ucp extensions to the C
∗
-envelopes in which the codomain of the extension is the von Neumann algebra generated by the range of
the original map.

Theorem 7.5
.

If
{\mathcal{R}}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\in\mathbb{N}\}
, and if
\phi:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

is a ucp map, then there exists a ucp map
\Phi:{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})\rightarrow\phi({\mathcal{R}})^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
, the von Neumann algebra generated by

\phi({\mathcal{R}})
, such that
\Phi_{|{\mathcal{R}}}=\phi
.

Proof.

Under the stated hypothesis,

{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}})\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}
, for every operator system
{\mathcal{T}}
,
by Lemma
6.4
. Therefore, the assertion about ucp extensions follows from

[
5
, Theorem 4.1(3)]
.

\hfill\square

A general property an operator system
{\mathcal{R}}
may have, related to the property exhibited in Theorem
7.5
, is
the
double commutant expectation property

[
25
]
, which is the
property that, for every unital complete order embedding

\kappa:{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, there exists a ucp map
\phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\kappa({\mathcal{R}})^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
such that
\phi\circ\kappa=\kappa
.

Theorem 7.6
.

If
{\mathcal{R}}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 3\}
, then
{\mathcal{R}}
does not
have the double commutant expectation property.

Proof.

By Theorem
7.2
,
{\mathcal{R}}
has the lifting property. Therefore, for such an operator system, the double
commutant expectation property is equivalent, by
[
23
, Section 4.4]
, to the property that

\mathcal{R}\otimes_{\rm el}\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{R}\otimes_{\rm c}\mathcal{T},

for every operator system
{\mathcal{T}}
, where
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm el}{\mathcal{T}}
denotes the left injective tensor product
of
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
induced by the canonical inclusion of
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}
into
\mathcal{I}({\mathcal{R}})\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}}
.

With the right injective tensor product
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm er}{\mathcal{T}}

of
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
induced by the canonical inclusion of
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}
into
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}\mathcal{I}({\mathcal{T}})
,
the fact that
{\mathcal{R}}
has the lifting property implies, by
[
23
, Theorem 4.3]
, that

\mathcal{R}\otimes_{\rm er}\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}\otimes_{\rm min}\mathcal{R}.

Thus, by exchanging tensor factors,
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm el}{\mathcal{R}}={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{R}}
.
Therefore, if
{\mathcal{R}}
had the double commutant expectation property,
then we would have both
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm el}{\mathcal{R}}={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{R}}

and
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm el}{\mathcal{R}}={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{R}}
, in contradiction to
{\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{R}}\not={\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{R}}
.

\hfill\square

7.4.
Minimal C
∗
-covers and automatic complete positivity with maximal tensor products

Theorem 7.7
.

If
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 3\}
, for
j=,1,2
,
then

{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{R}}_{2})\not={\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{2}).

Proof.

As defined in
[
12
]
, the operator system tensor product structure

\otimes_{\rm ess}
on the algebraic tensor product
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
is the one induced by the canonical inclusion of

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
in
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{2})
.
By Lemma
6.4
, we have that

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1})\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\subseteq_{\rm coi}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{2}),

and so
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm ess}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}={\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
. By
[
12
, Theorem 3.5]
, for any operator system
tensor product structure
\otimes_{\sigma}
such that
{\mathcal{S}}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}\otimes_{\rm ess}{\mathcal{T}}
, for all
{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
,

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm ess}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}={\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}

if and only if
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathcal{R}}_{2})={\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{2})
. Because

{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm c}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}\not={\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
, we deduce
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{R}}_{2})\not={\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{2})
, as asserted.

\hfill\square

It is worth noting another outcome of Theorem
6.5
, as it illustrates the loss of algebraic information when passing to tensor products.

Corollary 7.8
.

{\mathcal{R}}_{j}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 3\}
, for
j=,1,2
, and if
u_{j}\in{\mathcal{R}}_{j}
is one of the
canonical unitary generators of
{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{j})
, then neither
u_{1}\otimes 1
or
1\otimes u_{2}
is a unitary element of

{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{R}}_{2})
.

Proof.

By
[
12
, Theorem 3.5]
,

{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{R}}_{2})\not={\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{1})\otimes_{\rm max}{\rm C}_{\rm min}^{*}({\mathcal{R}}_{2})
implies the asserted claims regarding

u_{1}\otimes 1
and
1\otimes u_{2}
.

\hfill\square

Recall from
[
14
]
that, if
{\mathcal{R}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
are finite-dimensional operator systems, then

({\mathcal{R}}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{T}})^{\delta}\cong_{\sf OpSys}{\mathcal{R}}^{\delta}\otimes_{\rm min}{\mathcal{T}}^{\delta},

for an appropriate choice of order units. Thus, if
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 3\}
, then the operator system dual
of
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
is an operator subsystem of abelian C
∗
-algebras, by Theorem
5.3
. Hence, using the arguments of
Theorem
5.4
, we arrive at the following conclusion.

Theorem 7.9
.

If
{\mathcal{R}}_{j}\in\{\mbox{\rm NC}(d),\mbox{\rm NCP}(k)\,|\,d,k\geq 3\}
, then every positive linear map of
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\otimes_{\rm max}{\mathcal{R}}_{2}

is completely positive.
