Benefit/risk balance of prone positioning as first line treatment for upper airway obstruction in Robin sequence
Authors: Pauline Adnot, Nancy Vegas, Laurianne Coutier, Christèle Gras Leguen, Agnès Giuseppi, Yann Aubineau, Sonia Khirani, Véronique Soupre, Romain Luscan, Brigitte Fauroux, Véronique Abadie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Robin sequence (RS) is a rare condition associating retrognathia and glossoptosis causing upper airway obstruction. For half of the teams, prone positioning (PP) is the first-line treatment considering the benefit experienced on breathing, sleep, and neurodevelopmental outcome of infants with RS. However, this strategy is debated because of the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) associated with PP. This study analyzed the occurrence of SIDS in children < 1 year old with RS during the last 30 years in France to clarify the benefit/risk balance of PP. We gathered data from the French national registry for SIDS. We also collected responses to a survey on SIDS sent to all French national RS reference centers for rare diseases. Finally, we collected data from the national epidemiologic database for causes of deaths (CépiDc). During the last 10 years, the French registry for SIDS documented 1 case of SIDS in a child with RS who died while awake in the back position. Among about 3000 children followed during the last 30 years, the survey to RS centers identified 2 cases of SIDS. Regarding CépiDc data, the proportion of SIDS cases among all causes of death in the RS population was lower than in the general population. Conclusion: In this preliminary work, SIDS was not more common in the RS population than in the general population in France, even if most patients are treated with PP. Taking into account the benefits of this therapy, PP may be proposed in selected patients, with strict cautions. Introduction

Robin sequence (RS) is a rare condition characterized by micro- and/or retrognathia and glossoptosis causing upper airway obstruction (UAO) and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), frequently associated with posterior cleft palate [1]. In about half of centers worldwide, prone positioning (PP) is used as first-line treatment to release UAO in RS [2] and even in up to 95% of centers in a recent British survey [3]. This efficacy is mainly explained by the reduction of the backfall of the tongue during sleep but also by increased tonicity of the pharynx, associated with better thoracic mechanics in PP than the supine position, as observed in non-RS infants with respiratory distress [4]. However, given the well-established increased risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) associated with prone sleeping, this strategy is being questioned and even banned by some teams [5].

Therefore, clarification of the benefit/risk balance of PP in RS seems essential. For that purpose, we first performed a literature analysis of the risks and benefits of PP and second, used a three-way approach to analyze the occurrence of SIDS in children with RS during the last 30 years in France.

Benefits of PP for infants with RS

Few robust studies have provided efficacy data of PP for RS based on sleep studies [6, 7]. Indeed, only four studies analyzed the effects of PP on sleep in infants with RS [8–11]. Hong et al. showed that PP improved UAO and sleep quality evaluated by polysomnography (PSG) in 11 infants with RS, without correcting UAO, defined by an obstructive apnea and hypopnea index (OAHI) < 10 events/h [8]. Kukkola et al. confirmed these results by comparing the PSG data in the prone versus supine position in 59 infants. The OAHI was significantly lower in the PP than supine position (19 vs 31 events/h, p < 0.001), as was the oxygen desaturation index (p = 0.004) [9]. In 18 infants with RS, Coutier et al. found PP associated with significantly better sleep efficiency and a trend toward lower OAHI. However, only 3/18 infants had an OAHI < 10 events/h in the PP [10]. Coutier et al. recently completed their results by adding 29 infants. Of these 47 infants, 34 showed improvement during PP (72%), with 15 achieving an OAHI < 10 events/h with PP only (32%) (personal communication). Finally, recently Wiechers et al. observed a two-fold reduction in the OAHI in 21 infants in the PP versus supine position but without a statistical significant difference [11].

In summary, there are clinical and objective arguments that PP is partially effective in reducing UAO in infants with RS, especially in those with mild or moderate UAO (i.e., with an OAHI of 1.5 to 10 events/h).

In infants with RS and mild UAO, no other non-invasive interventions are usually proposed, and PP remains the only alternative. Therefore, the decision of PP depends on evaluating the risk of SIDS versus the deleterious consequences of mild or moderate UAO in infants during the first months of life, the period of important brain vulnerability. Most studies on this topic were performed in older children or children with Down syndrome. In children without RS, periods of UAO during sleep have been found associated with both immediate and chronic changes in blood pressure, heart rate and cardiac function [12]. Chronic OSA in young infants can cause failure to thrive [13, 14]. Chronic OSA is also known to have long-term neurologic effects. Recently, a meta-analysis including 63 studies found sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) associated with deficits across all cognitive domains [15]. This was also reported in a Canadian birth cohort study finding parent-reported SDB symptoms during the first 2 years of life associated with overall increase in behavior problems at age 2 years [16]. Similarly, Fauroux et al. compared the neurocognitive outcome of two groups of infants with Down syndrome: a screened group that had systematic early detection of OSA (via PSG at age 6 months and every 6 months until 3 years and upper airway surgery if needed) and a group that had standard follow-up and only one systematic PSG at age 3 years. At age 3, children from the screened group had a significantly better neurocognitive development and behavior than the standard care group [17].

There is no study comparing the neurocognitive outcome of children with RS and UAO regarding sleep positioning. We only have indirect data showing that the developmental outcome of children with RS improved greatly after the systematic screening and treatment of UAO, from about 23% of patients with psychomotor delay [18] to normal cognitive scores as shown by Thouvenin et al. in France [19] and Wiechers et al. in Germany [20].

Risks of PP for infants with RS

The risk of SIDS is higher for infants sleeping in PP, as shown in the Carpenter et al. case–control study with a multivariate odds ratio for prone versus supine sleeping of 13.1 [21]. Even worse, being placed to sleep in the side position but found prone at death was associated with a much higher risk (multivariate odds ratio 45.3). The “back to sleep” campaign in the 1990s resulted in a fourfold reduction in the incidence of SIDS [22], which is raising concern about the safety of children with RS sleeping in PP.

No study has specifically focused on this issue in infants with RS. Recently, de Blacam et al. published a review of PP in RS, with no case of SIDS among 875 children treated with PP [23]. Therefore, clarification on the benefit/risk balance of PP in RS seems essential.

This study aimed to determine how many infants with RS died in France from SIDS, and if they did, what was their sleep position.

Methods

We used a three-way approach. First, we collected data from the French national registry on SIDS created in 2015 [24] (Observatoire national des Morts Inattendues du Nourrisson—Registre [OMIN]), an exhaustive database that gathers all cases of SIDS in children < 2 years old since 2015, among 37 centers across all the country. Second, we sent a survey in January 2025 to the coordinators of each RS reference center for rare diseases in our national network (CRMR “SPRATON”) and the SIDS region specialists, asking about their practices of sleep positioning and the occurrence of SIDS in their patients with RS. Finally, we collected data from CépiDc (epidemiology center of medical causes of death, Centre d’épidémiologie sur les causes médicales de décès, INSERM), the national center that has gathered all mandatory death certificates in France since 1968. In every certificate, the disease or injury that initiated the train of morbid event leading directly to death is called the “underlying cause of death” and the co-morbidities are called “multiple cause.” All these diagnoses or causes are coded according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 9th and 10th Revision. We collected data from 1979 to 2022. ICD9 was used from 1979 to 1999 and ICD10 from 2000 to 2022. Because of no specific code for “Robin sequence” or “Pierre Robin sequence”, we chose the codes that are the closest and included RS. We selected the cases of death of children < 1 year old for whom the underlying cause of death was “SIDS” (code 7980 then code R95), and the multiple causes of death were “cleft palate” (code 749 in ICD-9 only) or “anomaly of skull/face bones including RS” (code 7560 in ICD9) or “congenital malformation syndromes predominantly affecting facial appearance” (code Q870 in ICD10).

Ethics

Regarding CépiDc data, analyses were conducted in accordance with the regulatory requirements and data-access procedures established by the CépiDc (INSERM, institut national de la santé et de la recherche médicale), ensuring the protection of individual information and compliance with applicable ethical standards for research. As well, the French OMIN registry was approved in 2016 by the French Data Protection Authority (CNIL, no. 915273) and by an independent ethics committee (Nantes Group for Eth­ics in the Health Sector, no. 2015–01–17). Regarding the data obtained from our rare disease network, the ethics committee of our institution considered that approval from the national ethics committee was not required, as this was a non-interventional study using non-identifiable data, in accordance with French law. All respondents gave their informed consent to participate and for their data to be used in this study.

Results

French national observatory for SIDS

Since 2015, about 2300 cases of SIDS were registered, including only 1 case of SIDS in a child with RS. This 4-month-old child was followed for syndromic RS at the Paris center and died in his father’s arms while awake in the back position after an oral vaccine for rotavirus. The autopsy was inconclusive. We found no SIDS case among infants with RS in PP in this robust database over the last 10 years.

Survey of the French rare disease reference centers for RS

We obtained responses from all 22 RS centers. Regarding sleeping position, 18 use PP and one lateral positioning; 3 teams use other techniques (naso-pharyngeal tube, tongue lip adhesion and non-invasive ventilation and more recently, Tubingen palatal plate). Most of the teams give oral (9/18) or written (10/18) safety instructions to parents. Most of the teams (12/18) provide home cardiorespiratory monitoring.

Two cases of SIDS were reported in this survey. The first was a certain case of SIDS that occurred in a 4-month-old child with a priori isolated RS who died during sleep in PP with oximetry monitoring. The autopsy was inconclusive. The second case was more doubtful because we could not find more information about a child whose form of RS is unknown and who supposedly died in PP in a playpen after breastfeeding.

The population in France is about 68 million people and the birth rate about 780,000 children per year. Given the incidence of RS of about 1/8000 births and the number of births in France in the last 30 years, we can estimate the total number of children born with RS in France at about 3000. For the last 30 years, we found 1 (or 2) case of SIDS in approximately 3000 children, corresponding to a rate of 0.35 (to 0.7)/1000 children, which is close to the rate of 0.3/1000 in France in the general population after the “back to sleep” campaign [25].

Data from the epidemiology center of medical causes of death (CépiDc, INSERM)

From 1979 to 1999, 20,738 cases of SIDS were reported among 115,273 deaths in children < 1 year old (18%). For these 21 years, we found 477 cases of death with “cleft palate” or “anomaly of skull/face bones including RS” as a multiple cause, including 9 cases of SIDS (1.9%) (Fig. 1). From 2000 to 2022, 4683 cases of SIDS were reported among 62,939 deaths in children < 1 year old (7.4%), evidencing the clear decrease in SIDS cases after the “back to sleep” campaign. For these 23 years, we found 224 cases of death with “cleft palate” or “congenital malformation syndromes predominantly affecting facial appearance” as a multiple cause, including only 7 cases of SIDS (3%) (Fig. 2). Taking into account all the limits of these data extractions, the proportion of children who died of SIDS was lower among children with a facial anomaly than in the general population.

Discussion

The review of the literature suggested that PP is partially effective in children with RS to release UAO and improve sleep and neurodevelopmental outcome. Accordingly, and taking into account the simplicity of this strategy and the burden of the other techniques, many teams are using PP as first-line treatment for UAO in RS. The analysis of two national registries and a survey of all national RS centers showed that SIDS is not more common in infants with RS than in the general population, even if most infants are sleeping in PP.

The data from the French national observatory for SIDS are robust because it is an exhaustive database for all cases of sudden unexplained infant deaths including SIDS [24]. Because this database was created in 2015, one may argue that a period of 10 years is not sufficient, taking into account the current low ratio of SIDS in the general population (0.3/1000) and the low incidence of RS (0.1/1000). However, this preliminary work is based on exhaustive and reliable data and represents the current situation.

The data from our French rare disease reference centers for RS is strong because they focus on the specific population of children with RS. France has had an effective national healthcare network for all patients with rare diseases since 2007. The national reference network SPRATON (séquence de Pierre Robin et troubles de succion-deglutition congénitaux) specifically deals with children with RS whose coordinating team is located in Necker Hospital. A total of 22 centers, with extensive experience in RS, are distributed across all the country. Even though the reporting is not exhaustive, all centers have to report to the national database for rare diseases, called BaMaRa, based on Orphanet coding that includes RS and has existed since 2017 [26]. Moreover, we believe that a death within a series is a striking event for medical teams and is not easily forgotten. Despite this major memory bias, the results of the survey are consistent with data from the two databases.

The national epidemiologic database for causes of deaths, CépiDc, is the main reference for statistics on the medical cause of death, but SIDS has a vague code of death and coding depends on death certificate quality and completeness. The second limitation regarding these data is that there is no specific code for RS, but two groups of diseases include RS (Appendixes 1 and 2). In this way, the occurrence of SIDS obtained with the CépiDc database is probably overestimated. This classification bias tends to reduce the incidence of SIDS in children with RS in that it gives data about SIDS in a group of children with RS and other severe facial malformations likely to cause more severe UAO and with higher mortality.

Moreover, these findings, although reassuring, should be interpreted with caution due to the lack of statistical power resulting from the rarity of RS and the currently low incidence of SIDS.

Conclusion

Our three-way approach to analyze the occurrence of SIDS in infants with RS in France, combined with the absence of SIDS reported in the literature [23], allows us to state that the occurrence of SIDS in this specific population of children, of whom about half sleep in PP, is not higher than in the general population. This observation may be partially explained by the parents’ carefulness with their fragile infant and/or the oral and/or written safety instructions for bedtime conditions given to parents (Appendix 3), such as avoiding the presence of a dummy or blanket, avoiding bed-sharing, and respecting the room temperature, which are recognized risk factors for SIDS [27, 28].

Despite the contradiction between SIDS prevention and positioning therapy, given the negative effects of OSA especially on children’s neurocognitive development [15, 17] and the benefits of PP on breathing and sleep quality for selected infants with RS, we consider that PP can be offered to them if the effectiveness of this method is proven by rigorous sleep studies, strict bedtime conditions are explained to the parents, and oximetry home monitoring is provided. To address this, we should propose the standardization of safety practices and protocols by developing a national guideline specifying the criteria for PP, the necessary precautions (temperature, bedding, monitoring), and the procedures for discontinuing PP.

Three perspectives to move forward in this polemic topic could be proposed. The first is of course to continue monitoring the causes of death in infants and specifically in the RS population. The second is to objectively compare risks, burdens, and side effects of the other ways of releasing mild UAO in infants with RS. The third should be performing a prospective randomized controlled multicentric study of RS children with mild/moderate OSA (with OAHI of 1.5 to 10 events/h). The study should compare a “tolerated” prone position with systematic oximetry monitoring versus supine sleeping. It should focus on early and late outcomes such as psychomotor development, but also quality of sleep and parents’ quality of life, and of course adverse events, including apparent life-threatening events and SIDS.
