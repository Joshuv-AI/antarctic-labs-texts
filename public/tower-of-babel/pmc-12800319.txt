Dengue virus infection amongst malaria and typhoid fever suspected acute febrile patients in the Niger river basin of Nigeria
Authors: Raymond Ojodeju Okpanachi, Cornelius Arome Omatola, Joseph Oyiguh Abraham, Martin-Luther Oseni Okolo, Thobela Conco-Biyela, Anyebe Bernard Onoja, Andrew Musa Adamu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent decades, the burden of dengue fever has increased substantially across the globe, making it a major public health concern, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. Unfortunately, dengue is often misdiagnosed and mismanaged as malaria and typhoid fever in endemic areas due to shared clinical symptoms. Although dengue has been reported in other parts of Nigeria, there is currently no documented evidence of the disease in the Niger River basin area, which is a site of attraction for most tourist visitors. In a cross-sectional study, blood samples were obtained from 200 consenting febrile patients from three health facilities in the Niger River Basin area. Dengue virus (DENV) antigen and antibodies were simultaneously detected using the NS1 and DENV IgG/IgM immunoassay kit. Further, the samples were screened for malaria parasite and typhoid bacteria using malaria parasite examination and the Widal test, respectively. Thirty-eight (19.0%) of the 200 patients tested had detectable anti-DENV antibodies, out of which 27(13.5%) were anti-DENV IgG, 8(4.0%) anti-DENV IgM, while 1(0.5%) each was for DENV NS1, both IgG and IgM, and IgG, IgM, and NS1 combined. The overall malaria and typhoid positivity were 104(52%) and 125 (62.5%), respectively. Dengue, malaria, and typhoid monoinfections were 27(13.5%), 37(18.5%), and 58(29%), respectively. Dual malaria and typhoid, dengue and malaria, and dengue and typhoid were 58 (29%), 2 (1%), and 2 (1%), respectively, while 7 (4%) had triple infection. Risk factors for dengue infection included being aged 41–50 years, farming occupation, being married, and environmental factors such as uncovered water containers and stagnant water. Symptoms significantly associated with active dengue included back pain and nausea. This study identified both active and previous dengue in the Niger River Basin area. The high burden of dengue and evidence of co-infections with endemic malaria and typhoid among febrile patients highlight the importance of differential diagnosis to avoid inappropriate treatment.Keywords: Dengue, Febrile illnesses, Co-infection, NigeriaSubject terms: Microbiology, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Dengue remains the most common viral infection spread by mosquitoes worldwide1. According to a global health report, approximately half of the people in the world are currently at risk of being infected with the dengue virus (DENV), with annual morbidity and mortality estimated at 100–400 million and 40,000, respectively. In recent years, there have been a dramatic increase in the global incidence of dengue, from 505,430 cases that were reported to world health organization (WHO) in 2000 to 5.2 million in 2019 and 6.5 million cases in 20232. Currently, dengue is endemic with ongoing transmission in > 100 countries including several countries in Africa, South-East Asia, Americas, Western Pacific and Eastern Mediterranean. Significantly, dengue is spreading to other countries in Europe and South America, where it had never been reported2. The current changes in the arboviral epidemiology are influenced by several factors, including the widespread increase in the vector abundance driven by environmental and sociodemographic factors. International travel and trade, global warming, poor DENV surveillance, unplanned urbanization, silent spread of asymptomatic infection, and rapid adaptation of the arboviral vectors have continued to drive transmission dynamics of dengue worldwide3,4.

Dengue, a key neglected tropical disease, is caused by one of the four genetically closely related but serologically distinct DENV strains (DENV 1–4). DENV infection is frequently transmitted to humans through bites of infected Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus3. The Aedes mosquito is spreading to different continents because of warmer climates, thus facilitating dengue transmission and distribution to countries with no evidence of previous occurrence4. Though rare, transovarial transmission of DENV in the mosquito vector to a newborn5 and viral transmission via blood transfusion during dengue outbreaks6 have also been reported recently, suggesting alternative transmission routes. More than 80% of dengue fever cases in humans are asymptomatic or only present with mild, flu-like illnesses, and only 5 to 10% progress to severe infection, which is typified by hemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome7. Self-management of most cases and misdiagnoses, as other febrile illnesses often lead to under-reporting of the actual number of cases8.

Malaria, with worldwide morbidity of 249 million and 608,000 deaths in 2022, is a major public health concern in tropical and subtropical regions9. Nigeria, with 27% of the cases and 31% of deaths, remains the country with the highest burden of malaria because of the high transmission risks throughout the country9,10. Malaria is caused by Plasmodium species (P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. malariae, P. ovale, and P. knowlesi) transmitted by the female anopheles mosquitoes. P. falciparum, the most virulent of all malaria parasites, accounts for the majority of malaria-associated deaths, with the brunt of the fatality cases occurring in sub-Saharan Africa11. In Nigeria, P. falciparum is responsible for 94–98% of malaria cases, while < 2% of cases are caused by P. malariae and P. ovale11. Typhoid fever (also known as enteric fever) is a life-threatening infection caused by Salmonella typhi and Salmonella paratyphi.13,14 The routes of transmission included mainly through ingestion of contaminated food or water. The risk of Salmonella infection in humans is compounded by poor sanitation facilities, lack of good hygiene, and inadequate access to potable water13,14. Annually, about 11–20 million cases of typhoid and between 128,000 and 161,000 deaths from the disease are reported globally15.

Dengue, enteric fever, and malaria remain infectious diseases of major public health concern in both tropical and subtropical regions, and the etiologic agents of the three diseases share overlapping regions of high endemicity across the globe16,17. In endemic settings where dengue, malaria, and enteric fever overlap, co-infection of DENV with Plasmodium, and/or Salmonella typhi is common, where the specific environmental, ecological, and climatic factors favor both infections in an individual16,18. Notably, findings from a recent meta-analytic study indicated that patients who were co-infected with dengue and malaria had a higher risk of developing a severe form of both diseases than patients with DENV and Plasmodium mono-infections17. Unfortunately, resource limitations and the lack of equipping most laboratories for diagnosing DENV diseases in tropical regions continue to challenge the appropriate public health responses towards the disease8. In Nigeria, patients manifesting febrile conditions are diagnosed as malaria and/or typhoid fever without considering the possibility of arboviral diseases, which also share similar clinical symptoms4,19. Consequently, case mismanagement and underreporting within health care systems are high. Although DENV infection has been documented in the rainforest region of Nigeria20and in parts of the guinea savanna4, there are no data related to DENV mono-infection and its co-infection with malaria and typhoid fever in the study area, where daily outdoor activities associated with farming, fishing, urbanization, and heavy human traffic, including tourist visitors may be creating the opportunities for the spread of DENV. Given the absence of confirmed dengue case data from the Niger River basin, this study is crucial for understanding the sociodemographic and environmental drivers of transmission in this rural setting. Thus, the objective of this study was to determine the seroprevalence of dengue, its predisposing risk factors, and coinfection rates with malaria and typhoid fever in febrile patients residing along the Niger River Basin in the Guinea Savannah region of Nigeria.

Materials and methodology

Study area

The study was carried out at three major hospitals in Ibaji Local Government Area, situated in the southern part of Kogi State, Nigeria. Ibaji, with an estimated human population of 128,129, is located between latitudes 06°52’00” N and 06°87’00” N and longitudes 06°48’00” E and 06°80’00” E. The map of the area is shown in Fig. 1. Ibaji has a tropical climate characterized by dry months (November-March) and wet (April to October) seasons. The inhabitants live along the river basin21 and depend on the river for farming, fishing, transportation, and their livelihoods. The area is often visited by tourists who come to see the beautiful landscapes along the riverbanks, conventional fishing methods, enjoy the unique river culture, and sometimes take boat tours to see the vibrant local markets and diverse wildlife.

(a-b): Map of Kogi State showing Ibaji local government area.

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2024 and January 2025 among febrile patients attending three major clinics in Ibaji, which is in the middle-belt region of Nigeria. Characteristically, dengue can be both endemic and epidemic, thus the use of a cross-sectional study design provides a practical and cost-effective way to gather crucial baseline data relating to the current or previous dengue infection through serological testing for antibodies in the unexplored Niger River Ibaji area. In addition, by simultaneously collecting data on both dengue infection and subject characteristics such as socioeconomic factors, household conditions, preventive behaviors, this study design can reliably identify correlations that can then be explored in more rigorous studies, such as a detailed cohort study to investigate the causal link. The study population included individuals of all age groups who presented with fever, defined with the assistance of a physician, as a high body temperature (> 38⸰C) for 2–10 days, and accompanied by other symptoms such as joint pain, headache, chills, nausea, and abdominal pain. Eligible subjects were assigned identification numbers by hospital personnel, which were documented on their respective cards. Subsequently, a random number generator was used to select the study participants. Approximately 12 samples were obtained per visit from subjects who enrolled for typhoid and malaria examination. The randomly selected febrile subjects were presented at Ayah (n = 78), Onyedega (n = 77), and Unale (n = 45) health care centers. The total number of subjects selected in each clinic was based on the capacity of the healthcare infrastructure. Overall, 200 patients who provided informed consent were randomly recruited for the study. For patients < 18 years, informed consent was obtained from their parent and/or legal guardian for study participation. Clinical and sociodemographic information relevant to the study was collected through the assistance of the hospital personnel using a standardized questionnaire (in both English and local translation) at the time of sample collection. Patients who were illiterate in English completed the Igala version of the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria included patients with underlying medical conditions, such as AIDS, and those who did not provide consent for participation in the study. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the ethical review committee of Kogi State University Teaching Hospital (reference number: KSUTH/ETHICS/005/VOL.1/45).

Sample collection

Five milliliters of whole blood were obtained from each patient by venipuncture into a well-labeled EDTA-filled bottle. Sera was separated from 3 mL of the whole blood by centrifugation (3000 rpm for 8 to 10 s) for dengue serology and Widal assays. The remaining 2 mL of each sample was used to make thin and thick blood films for malaria parasite screening.

Laboratory test procedure for dengue NS1 antigen and IgM/IgG antibody

The assay for DENV was done using a combined careUS™ rapid diagnostic test kit (careUS™ Dengue Combo NS1 & IgM/IgG, WellsBio, Korea) that qualitatively detects dengue NS1 antigen and IgG/IgM antibodies in the patient’s serum. The kit comprises two separate test devices: an NS1 antigen test (left) and an IgG/IgM antibody test (right). Both tests utilize immunoassay principles. For NS1 antigen detection, a double-antibody sandwich method is used. If NS1 antigen levels exceed the detection limit, a visible band appears in the test region (T). For IgG/IgM detection, a capture immunoassay is employed; positive results are indicated by visible bands in the T1 (IgG) and/or T2 (IgM) regions. Testing was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with 25 µL of serum plus 50 µL of diluent in the same well for the NS1 test and 5 µL of serum plus 50 µL dilution buffer added to the well for the IgG/IgM test. Results were read after 15–20 min of room temperature incubation. A pink-colored line appeared in the control (C) region and in the IgM or IgG region, which was interpreted as positive for IgM or IgG antibodies, while a colored band in the NS1 was interpreted as positive for the NS1 antigen. A color line that was present in the C line but absent in the G and M lines was interpreted as negative for the IgG/IgM antibodies, and the same if a color band appeared in the C region and was absent in the NS1 region, indicating a negative result for the NS1 antigen. The kit has a known diagnostic sensitivity value of 79.8%, 89.1%, and 82.6% for NS1, IgM, and IgG, respectively, with the specificity values being 100% for all tests22. Due to some logistical challenges, molecular detection methods such as real-time polymerase chain reaction were not used for DENV characterization.

Microscopy for malaria assay

Plasmodiumparasite microscopic examination of blood films, a gold standard laboratory procedure recommended by the CDC23, was employed for malaria confirmation. Briefly, a thick blood film was made by spreading two drops of blood in a circular pattern across a 2 cm diameter region on a sanitized glass slide. The films were left at room temperature to air dry. After applying 3% Giemsa dye to the thick films for 15 to 30 min, they were cleaned with distilled water, air dried, and examined with a microscope under a 100x oil immersion objective. For a malaria-positive thick smear, thin blood films based on morphological criteria were used for species identification as per the guidelines described by Cheesbrough24. Inter-observer variability during malaria diagnosis was managed through robust quality control measures, including re-reading slides by the senior hospital microscopist and using control samples with known positive and negative results to verify staining and reading accuracy.

Widal assay

The preliminary screening for typhoid bacteria was carried out using the Widal Test Kit (HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Limited, India), which is a widely available serological-based tool for typhoid fever diagnosis in the study area. The screening procedure and interpretation were as specified by the kit manufacturers. Briefly, approximately 8 drops of each patient’s serum were transferred into each of the 8 circles on the test card, and a drop of the polyvalent Salmonella O (somatic antigen) and H (flagellar antigen) antiserum was added. The content of each circle was stirred to mix and then spread over the entire ring of the circle in the test card. After about 4 min of rocking the test card, the level of agglutination was read and recorded at different ratios of 1:20, 1:40, 1:80, 1:160, and 1:320. Based on the manufacturer’s instruction, a serum antibody titer of ≥ 1:80 for either the somatic (O) or H antigen was taken as cutoff value to indicate recent typhoid infection; a titer below this threshold was considered negative. The Widal slide agglutination test kit has a diagnostic specificity and sensitivity of 82.9% and 100%, respectively25. Considering the known limitations of this diagnostic test, such as poor specificity due to cross-reactivity with prevalent non-typhoidal Salmonellaserotypes25, all febrile cases with a positive Widal test result were those further confirmed using stool culture. This test involved using a wire loop to transfer a small portion of the stool sample out of the 1–2 g of stool specimens from the patients onto Salmonella-Shigella agar (SS-Agar). Specimens were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and Salmonella was identified based on growth on the selective SS-agar, characterized by smooth and opaque colonies with a black-center due to H2S production.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 16 was used for statistical analysis of data generated in the study. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize categorical variables. Levels of statistical significance between outcome pathogens and participant variables were assessed using the Chi-square test statistic or logistic regression analysis set at a 95% confidence interval. A probability value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

In this study, 67(33.5%) males and 133(66.5%) females aged between 0 and 65 years were enrolled from three health facilities. Subjects aged 21–30 years dominated the study, while those aged < 60 years least participated. A high number of English illiteracies was observed among the participants in comparison with the 9(4.5%) and 3(1.5%) proportions of patients with diploma and university qualifications, respectively. Participants were mostly farmers (55%), while only 4% were government workers (Table 1). Of the 200 samples tested, 191(95.5%) were positive for dengue, malaria, typhoid, or a combination of one or more infections. Only 9(4.5%) of the subjects had negative assays for the three infections. Dengue, malaria, and typhoid mono-infections accounted for 27(13.5%), 37(18.5%), and 58(29%), respectively. Fifty-eight (29%), 2(1%), and 2(1%) were the dual infection rates for malaria and typhoid, dengue and malaria, and dengue and typhoid, respectively. Seven (4%) of the patients with dengue were simultaneously infected with malaria and the typhoid organism (Fig. 2). The pooled malaria parasite positivity was 104(52%), while 125(62.5%) were for typhoid. Of the 200 febrile patients in the study, 27(13.5%) had anti-DENV IgG, 8(4.0%) had anti-DENV IgM, while 1(0.5%) had DENV NSI, IgG + IgM, and IgG + IgM + NSI each. Overall, anti-DENV antibodies were detected in 38(19.0%) of the patients (Fig. 3). Although more females than males (female: male ratio = 2:1) participated in the study, both were equally comparable with regard to the occurrence of active (p = 0.70) and previous (p = 0.90) dengue (Tables 2 and 3). With the exception of the participants aged 1–10 years, seropositivity to anti-DENV IgM increased up to ages 41–50 years [0(0%) to 3(11%)] but decreased rapidly afterwards in older age categories [3(11%) to 0(0%)]. Analysis by level of education revealed a higher rate of IgM 7(9.6%) in patients with no formal education than in those with primary and secondary qualifications (3/115, 2.4%). Although the observed difference between the educational status of participants and IgM seropositivity was not significant (p = 0.27). On the contrary, the occupation of participant was marginally associated with IgM positivity (p = 0.05). The farmers in the study had nearly twice as high the combined IgM seropositivity rates of patients with business and studentship as their occupation [(8(7.3%) vs. 2(4.9%)]. More married than non-married subjects [(144 (72%) vs. 56(28%)] participated in the current study. The former had a significantly higher rate of DENV IgM positivity compared to the latter groups (p = 0.04) (Table 2). Similar to IgM, a significantly high IgG positivity was observed in patients who were engaged in farming activities (p = 0.001) and were married (p = 0.04). In addition, patients who were aged 41–50 years had more IgG 6(22.2%) than the older age groups, and the evidence of DENV exposure in the different age groups was only in those with at most secondary education [29(45.5%) vs. 0(0%)] (Table 3). There was increased predisposition to dengue among patients with the presence of uncovered containers and stagnant water around their homes, non-users of insecticides, as well as patients who shared beds and owned livestock (Fig. 4). The results of the univariate analysis showed that febrile patients with uncovered water storage containers around their residence had 1.4 times the odds (OR = 1.4; 95% CI = 0.21–3.75) of active DENV infection and 1.1 times the odds (OR = 1.1; 95% CI = 0.37–2.23) of previous DENV infection compared to the non-responder group (Table 4). Furthermore, the results of multivariate analysis indicated a similar odds of exposure to active DENV infection in relation to the presence of open water storage containers (OR = 1.3; 95% CI = 0.31–5.10), though with a 40% lower odds of being diagnosed with a previous infection (OR = 0.6; 95% CI = 0.48–2.77) (Table 5). Although the difference between this factor and the occurrence of dengue was not significant (p > 0.05). Non-users of insecticide had 11(6%) and 25(14%) rates of active and previous dengue against 0(0.0%) and 2(11%) of corresponding infections in consistent insecticide users, respectively. Overall, non-insecticide users had approximately 2-fold chances of being exposed to DENV compared to insecticide users (OR = 1/0.6, 95% CI = 0.36–8.37). Though there was no significant difference between insecticide use among subjects and DENV seropositivity (p = 0.37). Bed sharers had 1.3 times the odds (OR = 1/0.8, 95% CI = 0.29–4.10) of having dengue than non-bed sharers. Accordingly, active and previous dengue accounted for 10(5.5%) and 25(13.8%) in the former group against the corresponding positivity rates of 1(5.3%) and 2(10.5%) in the latter category, respectively. Patients who owned livestock had twice the odds (OR = 2.1; 95% CI: 0.19–0.86) of having dengue compared to those who responded otherwise. Comparatively, the difference between livestock ownership and DENV seropositivity was significant in both univariate (Table 4) and multivariate analysis (Table 5) (p = 0.02). In this category, active and previous DENV infection accounted for 8(8.8%) and 16(17.6%) in livestock owners against the corresponding 2(2.8%) and 11(10.1%) DENV seropositivity rates in those who did not own livestock (Tables 4 and 5). Fever 10(5.8%), headache 11(5.9%), joint pain 11(6.5%), and abdominal pain 10(6.2%) were found to occur more frequently among patients with active dengue (Fig. 5). Although we did observe > 2-fold chances of the patients being diagnosed with active dengue when fever was combined with headache, joint pain, or abdominal pain, the difference between dengue immunoglobulinemia and occurrence of these symptoms was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 5). Furthermore, patients with back pain were 6 times more likely to have active dengue (OR: 6.8; 95% CI = 0.88 − 0.99; p = 0.04) and those with nausea had approximately 5 times the odds of active infection (OR: 4.6, 95% CI = 1.12 − 31.63; p = 0.01) when compared to individuals without the respective symptom (Table 6). Also, in multivariate analysis results, the presence of both nausea (OR: 5.2, 95% CI = 1.33–20.84, p = 0.01) and back pain (OR: 7.2, 95% CI = 7.89–58.30, p = 0.02) significantly predicted active dengue (Table 6). Table 7 shows the risk factors information from participants likely associated with the occurrence of typhoid fever. The table details various behaviors and conditions that may influence the prevalence of typhoid fever among the febrile population. Key variables examined include hand washing after toilet, source of water for domestic and drinking, boiling of water before drinking, consumption of raw food, meat, and fish. Even though none of the factors considered substantially predicted the occurrence of typhoid fever (p > 0.05), improper hand hygiene after toilet [yes vs. no: 83% (19/23) vs. 75%(133/177)], use of river water as drinking water source [77%(97/126) vs. 74%(55/74)], and not boiling of drinking water [76%(149/196) vs. 75%(3/4)] were linked to higher exposure to typhoid bacteria compared to the other responder groups (Table 7).

Discussion

Nigeria faces a significant risk of arboviral infection due to persistent viral activity in regions previously affected, and in areas where vector insects and animal hosts are currently present19. Unfortunately, the public health impact of Dengue in Nigeria remains largely underestimated due to underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis of febrile illnesses as malaria or typhoid, without querying arboviral etiology8. Considering the overlapping risk of arboviral, malaria, and typhoid co-infections in the riverine Niger basin, this study assessed, for the first time in the Ibaji area, dengue mono-infection and its comorbidity with malaria and typhoid.

In this study, 13.5% of patients infected with DENV had neither malaria nor typhoid. This finding underscores the need for differential diagnosis of all febrile conditions, as their treatment as malaria and typhoid patients with antimicrobial drugs (antimalarials and antibiotics, respectively) might be ineffective against dengue. Delaying or forgoing appropriate supportive care for dengue (e.g., fluid management) could lead to complications such as fatal dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS)2. Compared to a recent pooled estimate of 10.9% dengue monoinfection rate among malaria-suspected febrile patients in Africa26, the higher rate in the current study suggests a significant dengue burden in the area. In part of Nigeria, a slightly higher dengue mono-infection rate of 17% (53/310) had been reported27among malaria and typhoid fever suspected febrile patients. In India, a higher dengue monoinfection rate of 41.5% and a comparable rate of 15% had been reported among febrile populations using both serology and real-time reverse transcription PCR28, and dengue serology (NS1/IgM)29, respectively. The variations in dengue prevalence rates may be explained, in part, by the differences in diagnostic assays, sampling seasons, and prevalent environmental factors, which positively drive DENV transmission. The indistinguishability of dengue cases from other febrile illnesses without etiology confirmation in the study and its comparability with previous epidemiological investigations raise a call for improved dengue surveillance and diagnostic investigations, as such efforts could reduce disease underreporting, potential case misclassification, and further improve clinician awareness and patient management protocol.

Overall, 19% of the febrile patients had evidence of exposure to DENV both as a single infection and in the presence of malaria and typhoid. The current rate is higher than the overall DENV seropositivity of 13.02%, 3.0%, and 6.7% reported by Osarumwense et al.30, Suchi et al.31, and Ajayi et al.32 in a similar study in Southeastern, Northcentral, and Southwestern Nigeria, respectively. The unexpectedly high DENV positivity rate found in this study, despite being conducted during the dry months when vector activity is typically reduced, fuels the discussion regarding year-round transmission dynamics in the area. This finding is similar to a high pooled DENV positivity rate observed by Animasaun et al.33in a previous study among the febrile population in parts of the country, which was conducted during the rainy season. The result from our study suggest that other factors besides seasonal parameters may drive DENV transmission. The proximity to water among residents of Ibaji and the yearly presence of waterlogged environments, may be sustaining the arboviral population during the dry sampling months. Furthermore, common outdoor activities in the riverine setting, such as fishing, may increase the opportunities for human exposure to mosquito bites, further contributing to the unusually high prevalence of dengue observed during the dry season in the area. Comparatively, a large-scale surveillance study across all seasons that involved three countries (Burkina Faso, Madagascar, and Sudan) in sub-Saharan Africa documented dengue prevalence of 7.1% to 11% among patients with acute febrile illness34, which is lower than the overall dengue rate observed in the current study. In several West African countries that shared ecological identities with our study setting, varying dengue prevalence rates have been documented. For instance, cross-sectional surveillance studies by Bonney et al.35, Ndeme et al.36, Ferede et al.37, and Gallon et al.38 among febrile patients in Ghana, Cameroon, Ethiopia, and Senegal observed dengue prevalence rates of 0.31%, 14%, 33%, and 57%, respectively. Compared to the dengue rate in the current study, the lower reported rates (0.31% and 14%) in the first two countries and the notably higher rates (33% and 57%) in the latter two countries suggests that despite ecological factors (temperature, humidity, and rainfall) could set the stage for dengue transmission, the actual prevalence in an area may be significantly shaped by a complex interplay of socioeconomic conditions, demographic patterns, and the effectiveness of public health interventions. Therefore, understanding these multifaceted influences is essential for designing targeted and effective dengue prevention and control strategies in different population settings.

Although more females than males participated in the study, the probability of active and previous dengue was the same for both sexes. In contrast to our findings, previous studies by Ndeme et al.36 and Omatola et al.4reported significantly higher DENV infection rates in either females or males, respectively. The gender infection rate observed in the study suggests that both males and females are at potential risk of having dengue in an endemic setting. The significant IgG levels observed in the study when compared to 2.7–4.2% IgG seropositivity rates in similar epidemiological investigations32,39, suggest that dengue has been spreading silently within the population for a considerable time. The increasing trend in both anti-DENV IgM and IgG seropositivity from 21 years up to 50 years is consistent with the findings of Nas et al.40 and Oluwaniyi et al.41 The high prevalence observed in the 21–40 age group may be attributed to their frequent engagement in outdoor activities, which often coincide with the peak biting period of the arboviral vector. The level of education of subjects was positively correlated with active and previous dengue in the study. This observation is comparable with the findings of Hamed42 in Southern Malaysia, Galani et al.18 in Cameroon, and Omatola et al.4 in Nigeria, which posited that lower educational attainment is associated with increased dengue. The significant dengue prevalence among individuals without formal education likely stems from poor awareness of prevention strategies, emphasizing the necessity of robust community sensitization. The significant DENV immunoglobulinemia rate among married subjects in the study has been documented4. The reason for this observation is not clear, as the mutual interaction among couples has not been shown to create a new direct route of horizontal dengue transmission. However, the chances of the typical mosquito-borne transmission may increase due to shared environments and travel. On the contrary, a study elsewhere has reported a lower rate of dengue among married individuals compared to singles42, suggesting that the observed higher rate in married couples might be due to chance. Analysis by occupation indicated participants involved in farming activities were likely more exposed to DENV, an observation consistent with the findings of Omatola et al.4 in part of Nigeria and Ferede et al.37 in Northwest Ethiopia, who posited that the prolonged outdoor nature of farming activities may predispose farmers to DENV infection, especially in areas with dense mosquito populations and insufficient vector control measures. Although occupational exposure was associated with increased DENV seropositivity in the current study, a cohort study in the future may help ascertain the causal relationship. In general, DENV co-occurrence with malaria and/or typhoid agent in patients introduced a layer of complexity in the interpretation of the epidemiological data across different demographic groups. For instance, dengue co-occurrence with endemic malaria and typhoid fever could lead to synergistic or antagonistic interactions between the disease etiologies, which may manifest differently in individuals of different age groups. Thus, the observed age-related disease patterns might be a result of the interplay of infections and age-specific vulnerabilities rather than just age itself. Similarly, the potential influence of educational disparities of patients on health literacy and access to care could mask or exaggerate the true impact of coinfection when underlying socioeconomic factors are present. Moreover, gender-based differential in coinfection rates could be a result of the effect of sex hormones on immune responses or the impact of socio-behavioral factors on pathogen exposure and healthcare-seeking behaviors. Overall, the understanding of these interactions may inform better public health interventions, as possible interplay between different individual characteristics and multiple infections might be influencing the demographic trend.

Certain environmental and behavioral factors appeared to have played a critical role in DENV transmission in the study, as the lack of insecticide use, presence of uncovered water containers, and stagnant water near residential areas were related to increased prevalence of dengue. The last two factors may provide favorable breeding grounds for Aedes spp., which could promote vector-human interactions and the spread of dengue in the area. Therefore, appropriate public health interventions aimed at reducing the disease transmission should include raising awareness about mosquito breeding sites and promoting practices such as regular emptying or covering of water containers. The reason for a significant positive correlation between livestock ownership and DENV infection in the current study is not clear, as there is no known direct link between livestock and DENV. Thus, we hypothesize that this finding may be a result of an indirect link with environmental factors. For example, livestock care often requires the use of uncovered water containers, which could promote DENV vector abundance if not cleaned and maintained regularly. Although our study showed a higher predisposition to dengue in areas with open water containers compared to those without, the result of multivariate analysis indicated a lack of a significant association between these two factors. Thus, the understanding of any biological rationale behind this finding will require further study, which could explore possible specific micro-environmental factors associated with livestock care that may influence human-vector contact. The relationship between climate change and the expansion of Aedes mosquitoes, is directly contributing to the global increase of dengue fever due to the physiological dependence of the vector on ambient temperature. Thus, climate-related extreme weather events such as frequent flooding in the study area21 may complicate disease control efforts by creating stagnant water that inadvertently supports vector proliferation. The plausible role of climate change to the current rate of dengue highlights the need for continuous DENV surveillance nationally.

The clinical symptoms of dengue infection in individuals who participated in this study mainly were complaints about Fever, headache, Back pain, nausea, abdominal pain, and Diarrhea. Elsewhere in Nigeria, similar symptoms were reported31. Although patients with active dengue significantly manifested back pain and nausea, the fact that these symptoms are also common in malaria cases within the study makes disease misclassification by clinicians more likely without a laboratory test. The pooled malaria rate of 52% in the study is comparable to 53% reported from other parts of the country43. However, a lower rate of 26.2% has been documented elsewhere in the southern region of Ethiopia44, suggesting that the infection rate, which is dictated by environmental factors, changes according to the geographical region at a given period. The overall typhoid fever rate of 62.5% observed in the study is comparable with other epidemiological findings of 65.9% and 64.3% by Katnap et al.39 and Sohanang-Nodem et al.45 among febrile patients in Nigeria and Cameroon, respectively. Although none of the information on various behaviors and conditions that could influence the prevalence of typhoid fever significantly predicted the infection, inadequate hand hygiene after toilet use, reliance on river water as a drinking source, and failure to boil drinking water were associated with a higher exposure to typhoid bacteria compared to other respondent groups. Additionally, inadequate cooking and the consumption of raw or improperly prepared food, such as fish, were linked with increased predisposition to typhoid bacteria, underscoring the importance of improving food safety standards, hygiene practices, and water treatment measures to reduce the incidence of typhoid fever and other foodborne diseases. The dual malaria and typhoid fever rate of 29% is relatively higher than the 2.9% reported by Kudi et al.38 from Gombe, Nigeria. Although Kudi et al.46 and our study both employed the gold standard slide microscopy for malarial parasite determination, the use of blood culture with higher specificity for typhoid fever diagnosis in the former study compared to the Widal assay used in our study, probably reduced the occurrence of false positive typhoid results and ultimately, the estimated malaria and typhoid fever co-infection rate in their study compared to the dual positivity rate in the study. Although the confirmatory test with stool culture further boosts confidence in the findings of the Widal assay. In Nigeria, febrile illnesses are typically diagnosed as malaria and, to a lesser extent, typhoid fever, while viral infections often go unaddressed due to lack of diagnostic capacity. Case reports and epidemiological studies on dual and triple co-infections (Dengue/Malaria/Typhoid) have consistently suggested that the combination of these diseases is often associated with increased risk of severe illness and life-threatening complications such as DHF and DSS from dengue, cerebral malaria or severe anemia from malaria, and intestinal perforation or sepsis from typhoid fever47–49. Thus, the simultaneous infection of some patients in the study with dengue, malaria, and typhoid fever, suggest the need for clinical suspicion and differential diagnosis in endemic areas, as early intervention may prevent potential risks associated with their co-occurrence in affected individuals. Although assessing the severity of co-infection is beyond the scope of the current study, further study is warranted in the future to understand the severity and challenges associated with dual and triple co-infection in the area.

One limitation of the current study is that it only focused on symptomatic patients. By excluding subclinical or asymptomatic dengue infections, which constitute a substantial proportion of all cases of dengue, the actual burden of the disease within the broader population is likely underestimated. Secondly, the use of serological assays to detect dengue infection without PCR confirmation may have limited the identification of patients with very early DENV infection, especially those with no detectable viral antibodies. The reliability of PCR in detecting early-stage DENV infections has been proven over the years, and its absence in the current study could underestimate the true number of current infections in the population. Also, serological tests for dengue is prone to false positive results due to cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses such as West Nile, Zika, and Yellow Fever viruses. This cross-reactivity with genetically and antigenically similar viruses makes it difficult to definitively distinguish between a previous DENV infection and another previous infection with related flavivirus. Molecular techniques involving PCR provides a much more specific diagnosis as it directly detect DENV’s unique genetic code, thereby overcoming this limitation. Therefore, the lack of PCR use in our study could lead to case misclassification and overestimation of the true burden of dengue in the presence of co-circulating flaviviruses. In addition, the lack of PCR amplification may limit the ability to detect dengue patients with asymptomatic or subclinical conditions, thereby underestimating the true burden of the disease in the area. Furthermore, the absence of PCR makes it difficult to characterize the circulating strains of dengue in the study area. The serological-based approach employed in the current study cannot reliably distinguish between the different serotypes of DENV, and thus, our ability to describe the specific circulating strains driving the disease burden or that could potentially cause severe disease in the area is limited. A future research effort that could incorporate PCR amplification will ensure early detection of dengue cases, characterization of circulating viral strains, and also provide a more reliable estimate of the disease burden in the Niger river basin area. Thirdly, the Widal kit employed for typhoid assay can potentially cross-react with antibodies against other prevalent febrile pathogens and diagnostic accuracy could be impacted by the baseline antibody titers in the population, making it difficult to rule out the possibility of over-diagnosis which can lead to unnecessary use of antibiotics and under-diagnosis, delaying appropriate therapeutic intervention and potentially leading to complications. Fourthly, despite the cross-sectional study design employed in the current study can reliably provide prevalence estimates in dengue surveillance, it is largely limited due to the episodic nature of dengue. One most important aspect of its limitations is its inability to establish causality. This is because the data relating to both the disease outcome and exposure parameters are collected at a single point in time, making it difficult to determine the temporal sequence of events. Recall bias may also be an issue when participants in the study failed to provide accurate information about past events or behaviors. Furthermore, the episodic nature of dengue implies that a snapshot from the current study might miss a significant portion of the affected population. This is especially true since the study was conducted during the dry period, which is often associated with low viral transmission rates. Thus, by missing out cases during the high dengue transmission periods, the true burden of dengue in the area is likely underestimated. Also, the inability to determine the number of new cases of dengue over a period of time using a cross-sectional study design makes it difficult to understand the rate at which dengue is spreading in the Niger River basin area. Furthermore, the collection of data at a single point in time makes it difficult to ascertain if the two or three infections occurred at the exact same moment or if one infection (e.g., dengue) was followed by the other (e.g., malaria or typhoid). Fifthly, the small sample size and potential selection bias inherent in the study have made it difficult to generalize our findings to the broader population. For instance, the fact that only hospital-based cases were considered could have led to the exclusion of cases in the community, potentially underestimating the burden of dengue and other febrile illnesses considered in the study. Notwithstanding, findings from the current study are important as it has provided baseline epidemiological data for future studies to build on in the area.

In conclusion, we found that some febrile cases with suspected malaria and typhoid conditions in the Niger river basin area were only infected with DENV. The hidden burden of the disease in the area suggests that dengue fever should be considered in differential diagnoses of febrile conditions for proper treatment of cases. The high prevalence among specific age group and those with lower educational qualifications underscores the need for targeted public health interventions, including increased community awareness, improved vector control strategies, routine testing for dengue, and improved sanitation practices. The current study has also provided evidence of dengue fever co-occurrence with endemic malaria and typhoid diseases in the area. However, a more detailed study is needed to understand the epidemiology of both diseases and the potential influence of demographic, occupational, and environmental factors on DENV transmission in the region. The findings from this study in comparison with other epidemiological studies, highlight the need for national integration of dengue surveillance for a more accurate assessment of the disease burden in broader population and effective health planning, including budgeting for diagnosis, treatment, and control of the growing public health threat.

Future research directions

The fact that cross-sectional study design only provides a snapshot in time, the consideration of longitudinal studies such as a cohort study in future research endeavors will build upon this baseline surveillance data by following the same population over certain period to understand the dynamics of dengue and co-morbidity rates with other endemic febrile illnesses, identify dengue virus transmission drivers and the long term effect associated with dengue. Also, future studies aimed at investigating the local mosquito vectors, such as species distribution and their susceptibility to insecticides, could inform policies for effective vector control and other targeted intervention strategies. In addition, exploring the socio-ecological determinants of dengue transmission in the study area, including water storage practices, sanitation facilities, and human behavioral patterns, may help design culturally appropriate and more effective dengue prevention strategies. Strains of the dengue virus circulating in the Niger river basin have not been isolated from human sera for characterization. Thus, an in-depth future research effort will involve the use of highly sensitive PCR amplification and sequencing for full characterization and understanding of the molecular epidemiology of dengue in the area. This information is needed to inform the development of a low-cost and accurate diagnostic tool that can concurrently detect dengue virus and other endemic co-circulating febrile pathogens at local health care facilities. The current epidemiological data have provided the basis for establishing collaborations with national and international research institutions to leverage expertise and resources. Furthermore, a future study that will detail the collection of clinical parameters and immunological investigations into possible genetic factors that influence susceptibility to the dengue virus and disease severity in co-infected individuals in the local population may shed light on the potential synergistic effects on disease progression.
