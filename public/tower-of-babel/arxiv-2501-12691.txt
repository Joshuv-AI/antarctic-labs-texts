When confronted with an undesired cell population, such as bacterial infections or tumors, we seek the most effective treatment, designed to eliminate the population as rapidly as possible. A common practice is to monitor the cells’ short-term response to the treatment, and from that, extrapolate the eventual treatment outcome,
i.e
. will it eradicate the cells, and if yes - at what timescales. Underlying this approach is the assumption that the cells exhibit a homogeneous response to the treatment, and hence the early response patterns can be naturally extended to later times. Recent experiments on cancer cell populations, however, indicate a significant level of cellular heterogeneity, undermining this classic assessment protocol of treatment efficacy. We, therefore, develop here a stochastic framework, to analytically predict the temporal dynamics of a heterogeneous cell population. Quite often, we find, the average cellular parameters, governing the short-term response, fail to predict the actual treatment outcome. In contrast, our analysis, which also incorporates the population’s variability, helps identify the relevant statistical parameters, which in turn, enable us to predict the full trajectory of the cell population, and specifically - the likelihood and typical timescales for remission.

Consider a population of

Classes of population dynamics

As our example above indicates, the cell-population
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
tends, with time, to become dominated by the persistent cell lineages, namely the ones with the smallest
\lambda

N_{0}
, the longest lasting lineage is, typically, the one characterized by
\lambda_{\rm min}

P^{A}_{\lambda}(z;t=0)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}(z-\lambda_{\rm min})^{\Psi_{n}},\vskip-5.69054pt

(8)

\Psi_{n+1}>\Psi_{n}

\Psi_{n}

\lambda_{\rm min}
is guaranteed as long as

is finite. Note that, in principle, the parameters

represents the continuous extension to the factorial operation,

\lim_{t\to\infty}K^{A}_{\lambda}(-t)=-\lambda_{\rm min}t+\ln[C_{0}\Gamma(\Psi_{0}+1)t^{-\Psi_{0}-1}]

\mathcal{N}_{A}(t\rightarrow\infty)=N_{0}C_{0}\Gamma(\alpha)t^{-\alpha}e^{-\lambda_{\rm min}t},

N_{0}

Population extinction - the role of
\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)

Our predictions for
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
in the different classes, are all rooted in the cumulant-based equation (
5
), whose exponential form can never reach the extinction state
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)=0
. This, however, is because
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
represents the
expectation
of
N_{t}
, and hence, overlooks the discrete and potentially stochastic nature of the actual observed population dynamics. Indeed,
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
, as defined in our formulation, captures an average over
M\to\infty
realizations, and can, therefore, assume continuous values, and reach arbitrarily close to zero, without ever going extinct. This is in contrast to the specific stochastic realizations, where extinction is possible, and in some cases even represents the most probable outcome.

As an example, let us revisit Eq. (
7
) and its two recurrent trajectories
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
for
A=1,2
of Fig.
3
h-j. There, while both
1
and
2
eventually enter the proliferation phase, the turnover in
2
occurs much later, at a point where the original population is reduced by a factor of
\sim 10^{-5}
. This is as opposed to
1
, whose turning point occurs at
\sim 5\times 10^{-3}N_{0}
. Consequently, if we begin with a finite population of, say
N_{0}\sim 10^{4}
or
10^{5}
cells, treatment
2
would reach its minimum point when
N_{t}
is of order unity, a point from which it may likely transition to
N_{t}=0
, thus avoiding the recurrent branch. Under similar conditions treatment
1
’s tipping point occurs far from
N_{t}=0
, and hence, while
1
seems more effective at first, it is more likely than
2
to result in eventual recurrence.

The crucial point is that such distinction between treatments
1
and
2
, whose clinical outcomes are, clearly, consequential, cannot be observed through the
expected trajectories

\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
, which, by design, overlook the stochastic and discrete nature of the random
N_{t}
. In fact, even an extremely small fraction, say
\sim 1\%
, of realizations of treatment
1

expected
trajectory
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
to diverge. Yet, still, from a clinical perspective, such outcome represents a
99\%
success rate, which is highly desirable. Hence,
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
’s recurrence does not, on its own, render the treatment unsuccessful. To evaluate
A
’s success, systematically, we must capture the potential deviations from
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
- precisely the role of the variance
\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)
.

The variance, we show in Supplementary Section 2, depends not just on the random variable
\lambda=r_{-}-r_{+}
, but also on
\varphi=r_{-}+r_{+}
. Therefore it incorporates the joint density function
P^{A}_{\lambda,\varphi}(z,u;t=0)
and its ensuing bi-variate cumulant generating function
K_{\lambda,\varphi}(t,s)=\ln[{\rm E}(e^{\lambda t+\varphi s})]
.
44
Our analysis helps predict the variance, which we find, follows

\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)=N_{0}\int\left.\left[2\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}K_{\lambda,\varphi}\left(-2t,s\right)}{\dif{s}}}e^{K_{\lambda,\varphi}\left(-2t,s\right)}-\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}K_{\lambda,\varphi}\left(-t,s\right)}{\dif{s}}}e^{K_{\lambda,\varphi}\left(-t,s\right)}\right]\right|_{s=0}\dif t,

(12)

a closed form solution based on
K_{\lambda,\varphi}(t,s)
. Equation (
12
) takes a heterogeneous cell population, with any arbitrary
P^{A}_{r}(x,y;t=0)
, and hence
P^{A}_{\lambda,\varphi}(z,u;t=0)
, as input, and provides the variance at time
t
as output. This variance quantifies the level of uncertainty around
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
as time progresses. Therefore, in each stochastic realization of our heterogeneous population dynamics, we take
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
of Eq. (
5
) to capture the expected population at time
t
, and
\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)
in (
12
) to describe the potential deviations from this expectation (Fig.
1
b).

To understand how
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
and
\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)
can predict instances of extinction, consider the
observed

N_{t}
at any given time. Using Eq. (
3

\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)[1\pm 1/q_{A}(t)]

q_{A}(t)=\dfrac{\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)}}.

(13)

As long as

,

is guaranteed, with high probability, to remain at a safe distance from

. If, however, at a certain instance we have

we observe a significant probability of

hitting zero, as it fluctuates around the analytically predicted
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
; Fig.
1
d.

For example, consider the recurrent population of Fig.
2
i. If,
e.g
., at the minimum point,
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
is of the same order as
\sqrt{\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)}
, there is a likelihood that a significant fraction of the realizations will hit the absorbing
N_{t}=0

q_{A}(t)\leq 1
. As
q_{A}(t)
decreases, this likelihood grows, until under

\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
may continue to diverge, as predicted, for example, in (
7
), but this divergence will be driven by the increasingly
rare
instances of recurrence, not by the
majority
of extinction events.

Hence, we arrive at our final key prediction, extracting the statistics of
P^{A}_{\lambda,\varphi}(z,u;t=0)
that help characterize treatment
A
’s efficacy:

\bullet
Successful treatment
. Treatment

’s success is predicted by the condition

\begin{array}[]{ccc}Q_{A}\leq 1&&Q_{A}\equiv\displaystyle\min_{t}\Big\{q_{A}(t)\Big\}\end{array},

(14)

namely, that at some point in time

. remission. The smaller is

\bullet
Efficient treatment
. In case (
14

(15)

namely the earliest point in time by which the system satisfies condition (
14
). The smaller is

A
.

N_{0}
adversarial cells (bacteria, tumor etc.), which we subject to therapeutic
A
. The population’s response to

is characterized by
P^{A}_{\lambda,\varphi}(z,u;t=0)

\bullet

in (

\bullet

Discussion and outlook

Matching treatment to malignancy, a clinical challenge, maps quite naturally into a population dynamics problem. In a deterministic setting, it all condenses into a single parameter
\lambda
, characterizing how rapidly the treatment eliminates the malignant cells. Here, however, we have shown that stochastic effects play a crucial role in the actual performance of a selected treatment. Specifically, we discussed two sources of randomness: the ingrained heterogeneity in the cells’ response to the treatment, and the random nature of the sequence of events following treatment. Together, we found, that these two, practically inevitable features of real-world population dynamics, lead to a sharp, qualitative, departure from the classic deterministic solution.

Fortunately, we show that even under this complex, seemingly unpredictable, setting the treatment outcome
can
be predicted. It requires not just a single observable
\lambda
, but rather a set of probabilistic parameters:
\mathcal{N}_{A}(t)
and
\mathcal{V}_{A}(t)
of Eqs. (
5
) and (
12
), and their subsequent
Q_{A}
and
T_{A}
in (
14
) and (
15
). These analytically tractable parameters, rooted in the population’s heterogeneous response to each treatment, provide the desired predictors of the treatment’s potential success and efficiency.

The reduction we offer here, into a limited set of quantifiable parameters, enables making systematic clinical decisions, helping match treatments to malignancies. To apply our methodology, however, we must embark on systematic experiments to measure not just the
average
performance of different therapeutics, but rather their statistical response patterns
P^{A}_{\lambda}(z;t=0)
, and from that extract their characteristic
Q_{A}
and
T_{A}
. A first step was already undertaken in the recent experiments of Fig.
3
e-g, where researchers mapped the distributed response of cancerous cultures to chemotherapeutic agents
25
(see Supplementary Section 6.3). Expanding this line of experimentation to other malignancies/treatments, will help clinicians make informed, mathematically driven, treatment prioritization.
