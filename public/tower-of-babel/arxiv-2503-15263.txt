Gibbs Properties of Equilibrium States
Mirmukhsin Makhmudov 1 and Evgeny Verbitskiy 1,2

Abstract
. We consider the problem of equivalence of Gibbs states and equilibrium states for continuous potentials on full shift spaces E ℤ \displaystyle E^{\mathbb{Z}} . Sinai, Bowen, Ruelle and others established equivalence under various assumptions on the potential ϕ \displaystyle\phi . At the same time, it is known that every ergodic measure is an equilibrium state for some continuous potential. This means that the equivalence can occur only under some appropriate conditions on the potential function. In this paper, we identify the necessary and sufficient conditions for the equivalence.



1.
Introduction

DLR Gibbs measures were introduced by Dobrushin (1968) and Lanford and Ruelle (1969) to describe the collective behaviour of a system composed of a large number of components, each governed by a local law.
Soon after, the Gibbs measures found applications in other fields of science and various areas of mathematics.
In particular, in the early 1970’s,
Sinai
showed that natural invariant measures for hyperbolic dynamical systems are Gibbs measures.
The original definition of Gibbs measures in statistical mechanics is somewhat cumbersome in the context of dynamical systems.
For this reason, Bowen
[
3
]
provided a more suitable definition of Gibbs states from the dynamical systems perspective: a translation-invariant measure
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle\Omega=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
,
\displaystyle E
is finite, is called
Gibbs in Bowen’s sense
, or
Bowen-Gibbs
, for a continuous potential
\displaystyle\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, if for some constants
\displaystyle C>1
and
\displaystyle P
,
for all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega

(1)

\frac{1}{C}\leq\frac{\mu\big(\{\tilde{\omega}\in\Omega:\tilde{\omega}_{0}^{n-1}=\omega_{0}^{n-1}\}\big)}{\exp\big(S_{n}\phi(\omega)-nP\big)}\leq C,

where
\displaystyle S_{n}\phi(\omega)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi(S^{k}\omega)
and
\displaystyle S:\Omega\to\Omega
is the left shift on
\displaystyle\Omega
.
Subsequently, weaker versions of this notion were introduced.
Namely, a translation-invariant measure
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle\Omega
is
weak Bowen-Gibbs
if
\displaystyle\mu
satisfies

(2)

\frac{1}{C_{n}}\leq\frac{\mu(\{\tilde{\omega}\in\Omega:\tilde{\omega}_{0}^{n-1}=\omega_{0}^{n-1}\})}{e^{S_{n}\phi(\omega)-nP}}\leq C_{n},

for some subexponential sequence
\displaystyle\{C_{n}\}
of positive real numbers, i.e.,
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log C_{n}=0
.

Let us stress that Bowen’s definition
of Gibbs measures is actually a theorem in Statistical Mechanics.
More specifically, if
\displaystyle\mu
is a translation-invariant DLR-Gibbs measure
(see Section
2
for the notion), then there exists a continuous function
\displaystyle\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and positive numbers
\displaystyle C_{n}=C_{n}(\phi)
such that (
2
) holds.
Therefore, we prefer to use the name of Gibbs measures for measures which are Gibbs in the DLR sense, and we refer to the measures satisfying (
1
) and (
2
) as Bowen-Gibbs and weak Bowen-Gibbs measures.

The notion of weak Gibbs states in Bowen’s sense is also somewhat misleading, as it suggests some form of non-Gibbsianity and competes with a notion under the same name in Statistical Mechanics.
As we will see below, weak Bowen-Gibbs measures can be bona-fide Gibbs measures in the DLR sense.
We also note that there are examples of weak Gibbs measures in the DLR sense, which are weak Bowen-Gibbs as well
[
14
]
.

Bowen’s definition (
1
) and its weak form (
2
) are extremely convenient from the Dynamical Systems point of view.
At the same time, using such definitions, one can, in principle, say very little about the conditional probabilities of the underlying measure, which is the classical approach to Gibbs measures in Statistical Mechanics.
In fact, there exist Bowen-Gibbs measures that are not DLR-Gibbs measures
[
2
]
*Subsection 5.2.

Another important notion is that of equilibrium states.
A translation-invariant measure
\displaystyle\mu
on
\displaystyle\Omega=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
is called an
equilibrium state
for a (continuous) potential
\displaystyle\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
if

(3)

h(\mu)+\int_{\Omega}\phi d\mu=P(\phi),

where
\displaystyle P(\phi)
is the topological pressure of
\displaystyle\phi
and
\displaystyle h(\mu)
is the measure-theoretic entropy of
\displaystyle\mu
.
For expansive systems like those we consider in this paper, equilibrium states always exist.
It is easy to see that a weak Bowen-Gibbs state is also an equilibrium state for the same potential
[
16
]
.

A fundamental result highlighting the breadth of the class of equilibrium states is the following: if
\displaystyle\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k}
are some ergodic measures on
\displaystyle\Omega
,
then one can find a continuous potential
\displaystyle\phi\in C(\Omega)
such that all these measures are equilibrium states for
\displaystyle\phi

[
19
,
9
,
23
]
.
This remarkable generality suggests that equilibrium states can exhibit a wide range of behaviors, and in particular, one cannot expect them to possess any form of Gibbsianity in general.
This leads to a natural question: under what conditions on
\displaystyle\phi
are the equilibrium states Gibbs, either in the DLR or the Bowen sense?
This question has a long history of research:

\displaystyle\bullet

For
Hölder continuous
potentials, Sinai proved that equilibrium states are Gibbs in the DLR sense
[
20
]
*Theorem 1, and the Bowen-Gibbs property was established by Bowen
[
3
]
.
Haydn extended Sinai’s results to the non-symbolic setup
[
7
,
8
]
.

\displaystyle\bullet

Ruelle
[
18
]
(see also
[
11
]
*Theorem 5.3.1)
studied the DLR Gibbsianity of the unique equilibrium states for potential
\displaystyle\phi
with
summable variations
:

\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathtt{var}_{n}\phi<\infty,\quad\mathtt{var}_{n}\phi:=\sup\big\{\phi(\omega)-\phi(\bar{\omega}):\omega_{j}=\bar{\omega}_{j},\;\;0\leq j\leq n-1\big\}.

The Bowen-Gibbs property was treated by Keller
[
11
]
*Theorem 5.2.4, (c)

\displaystyle\bullet

Walters
[
24
]
considered potentials satisfying even a weaker condition:

(4)

\lim\limits_{p\to\infty}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathtt{var}_{[-p,n+p]}S_{n+1}\phi=0,\;\;\text{ here }\;\;S_{n+1}\phi=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\phi\circ S^{i}.

Walters showed that there exists a unique equilibrium state for potentials satisfying (
4
), and they have the so-called,
\displaystyle g-
measure property, which amounts to saying that the equilibrium state has continuous one-sided conditional probabilities.
Combining this with the result of
[
2
]
, one concludes the DLR Gibbsianity of the unique equilibrium state of a potential in the Walters class.
In

[
6
]
, Haydn and Ruelle extended this to a more general setup than the setup of shift spaces.
In the same paper, Haydn and Ruelle also established the Bowen-Gibbs property of the unique equilibrium state for a potential satisfying the
Bowen condition
:

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\texttt{var}_{[-n,n]}S_{n+1}\phi<+\infty,

which is slightly weaker than Walters’ original condition.

\displaystyle\bullet

More recently, Pfister and Sullivan
[
17
]
established the weak Bowen-Gibbs property of equilibrium states for potentials
\displaystyle\phi
with
summable oscillations
:

\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta_{i}\phi<+\infty,\quad\delta_{i}\phi:=\sup\{\phi(\omega)-\phi(\bar{\omega}):\omega_{j}=\bar{\omega}_{j},\;\;j\neq i\}.

Unlike the preceding conditions, the summable oscillations condition does not imply the uniqueness of the corresponding equilibrium states.
However, the DLR Gibbs property of equilibrium states under summable oscillations has not been addressed.

This paper continues the long line of research on the Gibbsianity of equilibrium states, as discussed above, and also extends the main result of
[
2
]
, where a similar question has been answered in the case of
\displaystyle g-
measures.
In this paper, we show that under a similar assumption on the potential
\displaystyle\phi
, one can establish the Gibbs properties of equilibrium states and vice versa.
This assumption on the regularity of the potential
\displaystyle\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the
extensibility condition
,
which requires that for all
\displaystyle a_{0},b_{0}\in E
the sequence
of functions

\rho_{n}^{a_{0},b_{0}}(\omega):=\sum_{i=-n}^{n}\bigl(\phi\circ S^{i}(\omega_{-\infty}^{-1}b_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})-\phi\circ S^{i}(\omega_{-\infty}^{-1}a_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})\bigr)

converges uniformly in
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
.
The extensibility condition is not very restrictive.
For example, it does not imply the uniqueness of the equilibrium states, unlike the results by Sinai, Bowen, Ruelle and Walters.
Furthermore, the extensibility condition covers the previously treated classes, including
the class of Hölder continuous potentials, potentials with summable variations, Walter’s class, as well as the class of potentials with summable oscillations.
However, the potentials in Bowen’s class do not necessarily have the extensibility property
[
2
]
*Section 5.5.
An important example of extensible potentials is the Dyson potential,
\displaystyle\phi^{D}(\omega):=h\omega_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\beta\omega_{0}\omega_{n}}{n^{\alpha}},\;\omega\in\{\pm 1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, which has been extensively
studied recently
[
10
,
22
,
15
]
, where
\displaystyle h,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\displaystyle\alpha>1
.

The following theorem, the first of our main results in this paper, establishes the Gibbs properties of the equilibrium states of an extensible potential.

Theorem A
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\phi\in C(\Omega)
has the extensibility property.
Then any equilibrium state
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathcal{ES}(\phi)
is

(1)

Gibbs in the Dobrushin-Lanford-Ruelle sense;

(2)

weak Bowen-Gibbs relative to the potential
\displaystyle\phi
.

The proof of Theorem
A
is given in Section
5
, and uses the following idea:
for a given potential
\displaystyle\phi\in C(\Omega)
satisfying the extensibility condition, we construct a natural two-sided Gibbsian specification (a consistent family of regular probability kernels)
\displaystyle\gamma^{\phi}
on
\displaystyle\Omega=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Then we show that any translation-invariant DLR-Gibbs state
\displaystyle\nu
for the specification
\displaystyle\gamma^{\phi}
will be an equilibrium state for
\displaystyle\phi
.
Hence, the set of Gibbs states associated with the specification
\displaystyle\gamma^{\phi}
is a subset of the set of equilibrium states for
\displaystyle\phi
.
Finally, if we take any equilibrium state
\displaystyle\tau\in\mathcal{ES}(\phi)
and a DLR-Gibbs state
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{G}_{S}(\gamma^{\phi})
, we will show that the relative entropy density rate
\displaystyle h(\tau|\mu)
is zero.
This allows us to use the classical variational principle.

[
12
]
*Theorem 4.1 and conclude that
\displaystyle\tau\in\mathscr{G}_{S}(\gamma^{\phi})
as well.

Our second main result is about the translation-invariant Gibbs measures, which in some sense is a converse of Theorem A.

Theorem B
.

Assume
\displaystyle\mu
is a translation-invariant DLR Gibbs measure on
\displaystyle\Omega=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Then
\displaystyle\mu
is an equilibrium state for a
potential
with the extensibility property.

We should note that if
\displaystyle\mu
is a Gibbs measure for a translation-invariant
uniformly absolutely convergent
(UAC) interaction, then the claim is rather standard
[
13
]
*Theorem 3.2.
However, as demonstrated in
[
1
]
, not all translation-invariant Gibbs measures are compatible with a translation-invariant UAC interaction.
Thus, Theorem B generalizes the result in
[
13
]
*Theorem 3.2 to a broader setting, encompassing all translation-invariant Gibbs measures, including those that are not Gibbs for any translation-invariant UAC interaction.

The proof of Theorem
B
is constructive and is also given in Section
5
.
In fact, we construct a natural one-sided potential
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma}
out of the Gibbsian specification
\displaystyle\gamma
for
\displaystyle\mu
.
Then we show that
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma}
is extensible, and this allows us to apply Theorem
A
to
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma}
.

One might observe an analogy between our results and Sullivan’s theorem
[
21
]
*Theorem 1 in Statistical Mechanics.
In Sullivan’s theorem, the role of extensible potentials is played by the so-called
\displaystyle\mathscr{L}
-convergent interactions, a notion that is also syntactically similar to the notion of extensibility.

We also note that the statements of Theorems A and B, along with their proofs presented in this paper, naturally extend to higher-dimensional lattices
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}^{d}
ordered lexicographically.
Since there is no substantial difference in the proofs, we only focus on the one-dimensional lattice
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
.

2.
DLR Gibbs Formalism

The theory of Gibbs states, which is put forward by Dobrushin, Lanford, and Ruelle, is very flexible and allows one to define Gibbs states on very general lattice spaces
\displaystyle E^{\mathbb{L}}
, where
\displaystyle E
is a Polish space and
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}
is a countable set.
In the present paper, we are primarily interested in probability measures
on
\displaystyle\Omega=E^{\mathbb{Z}}
,
\displaystyle E
is finite, which are invariant under the left shift
\displaystyle S:\Omega\to\Omega
.

2.1.
Specifications, Interactions, and Gibbs states in Statistical Mechanics

The standard Statistical Mechanics description of Gibbs states is rather different from the
definitions of Bowen-Gibbs and weak Bowen-Gibbs measures.
The principal point is the explicit description of the family of
conditional expectations

indexed by finite subsets
\displaystyle\Lambda
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
.
More precisely, in Statistical Mechanics, one starts with a family of regular conditional expectations, which for
\displaystyle f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

\gamma_{\Lambda}(f|\omega):=\sum_{\xi_{\Lambda}\in E^{\Lambda}}\gamma_{\Lambda}(\xi_{\Lambda}|\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})f(\xi_{\Lambda}\omega_{\Lambda^{c}}),

where

(5)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\Lambda}(\xi_{\Lambda}|\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\Bigl(-H_{\Lambda}(\xi_{\Lambda}\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})\Bigr)}{Z_{\Lambda}(\omega)},\quad{Z_{\Lambda}(\omega)}=\sum_{\zeta_{\Lambda}\in E^{\Lambda}}\exp\Bigl(-H_{\Lambda}(\xi_{\Lambda}\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})\Bigr).

In order to guarantee the tower property of the conditional expectations, one needs to assume

consistency
of
\displaystyle\gamma_{\Lambda}
’s:
\displaystyle\gamma_{\Lambda}=\gamma_{\Lambda}\circ\gamma_{V}
for
\displaystyle V\subset\Lambda
, where for
\displaystyle f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
measurable and
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
,
\displaystyle(\gamma_{\Lambda}\circ\gamma_{V})(f|\omega):=\int_{\Omega}\gamma_{V}(f|\eta)\gamma_{\Lambda}(d\eta|\omega)
.
The latter is ensured if the functions
\displaystyle H_{\Lambda}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
– called a
Hamiltonian
in
\displaystyle\Lambda
– are of a rather special form:

(6)

H_{\Lambda}(\omega)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\Subset\mathbb{Z}\\
V\cap\Lambda\neq\varnothing\end{subarray}}\Phi_{V}(\omega_{V}),

here the summation is taken over all finite subsets of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
(denoted by
\displaystyle\Subset\mathbb{Z}
) which have
non-empty intersection with
\displaystyle\Lambda
.
Here
\displaystyle\Phi=\{\Phi_{V},V\Subset\mathbb{Z}\}
is called an
interaction
and each function
\displaystyle\Phi_{V}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is
local
, meaning that the value of
\displaystyle\Phi_{V}(\omega)
depends only on the values of
\displaystyle\omega
within
\displaystyle V
, hence, we write
\displaystyle\Phi_{V}(\omega_{V})
.
In order for these expressions to make sense, one needs to assume a suitable
form of summability in (
6
).
The standard and sufficient assumption is
uniform absolute convergence
(UAC): for all
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\sum_{i\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}}\|\Phi_{V}\|_{\infty}=\sum_{i\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}}\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}|\Phi_{V}(\omega_{V})|<\infty.

If
\displaystyle\Phi
is an UAC interaction, the corresponding
specification

\displaystyle\gamma=(\gamma_{\Lambda})_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}}
defined by (
5
), is

•

non-null
: for all
\displaystyle\Lambda
,
\displaystyle\inf_{\omega}\gamma_{\Lambda}(\omega_{\Lambda}|\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})>0
,

•

continuous
: for every
\displaystyle\Lambda
,
\displaystyle\omega\mapsto\gamma_{\Lambda}(\omega_{\Lambda}|\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})
is continuous.

In statistical mechanics, the second property is often referred to as
quasi-locality
.
An important property of positive specifications, which will be used in the proofs, is the so-called
bar moving property
:

(7)

\frac{\gamma_{\Delta}(\xi_{\Delta}|\omega_{\Delta^{c}})}{\gamma_{\Delta}(\zeta_{\Delta}|\omega_{\Delta^{c}})}=\frac{\gamma_{\Lambda}(\xi_{\Delta}\omega_{\Lambda\setminus\Delta}|\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})}{\gamma_{\Lambda}(\zeta_{\Delta}\omega_{\Lambda\setminus\Delta}|\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})},

for all
\displaystyle\xi,\zeta,\omega\in\Omega
and every
\displaystyle\Delta\subset\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
.
The bar moving property is equivalent to the consistency condition of specifications.

A non-null continuous specification
\displaystyle\gamma=\{\gamma_{\Lambda}\}
is called
Gibbsian
.

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\gamma=\{\gamma_{\Lambda}\}
is a Gibbsian specification on
\displaystyle\Omega
.
The measure
\displaystyle\mu
is called Gibbs for
\displaystyle\gamma
,
denoted by
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathcal{G}(\gamma)
,
if for every
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
,

\mu(\omega_{\Lambda}|\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})=\gamma_{\Lambda}(\omega_{\Lambda}|\omega_{\Lambda^{c}}),\;\text{ for }\mu-\text{a.e. }\omega\in\Omega,

equivalently, if the DLR equations hold: for every
\displaystyle f\in C(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
,

\int_{\Omega}\gamma_{\Lambda}(f|\omega)\mu(d\omega)=\int_{\Omega}f(\omega)\mu(d\omega).

For any Gibbsian specification
\displaystyle\gamma
, the set of corresponding Gibbs measures

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\gamma)
is a non-empty convex set. In case,
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\gamma)

consists of multiple measures, one says that
\displaystyle\gamma
exhibits phase transitions.

2.2.
Translation-invariance and the Variational Principle

The modern approach to Gibbsian formalism is to think about Gibbsian specifications
\displaystyle\gamma

in an
interaction independent
fashion.
The reason for this is that a measure

\displaystyle\mu
can be consistent with at most one Gibbsian specification, while there are infinitely many interactions
\displaystyle\Phi
giving
rise to the same Gibbsian specification.
In fact, it was proven by Kozlov that for any Gibbsian specification
\displaystyle\gamma
there exists a (in fact many) UAC interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
such that
\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma^{\Phi}
.
However, such a representation is not always possible in a way that respects translation invariance.
It is shown in
[
1
]
that there exists a Gibbsian specification
\displaystyle\gamma
on
\displaystyle\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
that is
translation-invariant
– meaning
\displaystyle\gamma_{\Lambda+1}=\gamma_{\Lambda}\circ S
for every
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
– but can not be associated with any
translation-invariant UAC interaction

\displaystyle\Phi
, where
\displaystyle\Phi_{\Lambda}=\Phi_{\Lambda}\circ S
for all
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
, via (
5
).
Nevertheless, many important results in Statistical Mechanics, including the
variational principle
, can be formulated independently of interactions.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
12
]

Let
\displaystyle\gamma
be a translation-invariant Gibbsian specification and
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{G}_{S}(\gamma)
.
Then for all
\displaystyle\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{1,S}(\Omega)
, the specific relative entropy
\displaystyle h(\tau|\mu)
exists and

h(\tau|\mu)=0\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\tau\in\mathscr{G}_{S}(\gamma).

In
[
12
]
, the authors proved some technical lemmas about translation-invariant specifications, which would be useful for us.
For
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}:=[-n,n]\cap\mathbb{Z}
.
We fix a letter
\displaystyle\mathtt{a}
in the finite alphabet
\displaystyle E
and we will use the notation
\displaystyle\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}
, to denote the constant configuration consisting of
\displaystyle\mathtt{a}
’s.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\gamma
is a translation-invariant Gibbsian specification on
\displaystyle\Omega
. Then

(i)

(8)

\sup_{\sigma,\omega,\eta\in\Omega}\Big\lvert\log\Big(\frac{\gamma_{[0,n]}(\sigma_{[0,n]}|\omega_{[0,n]^{c}})}{\gamma_{[0,n]}({\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}_{[0,n]}|\omega_{[0,n]^{c}})}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{[0,n]}({\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}_{[0,n]}|\eta_{[0,n]^{c}})}{\gamma_{[0,n]}(\sigma_{[0,n]}|\eta_{[0,n]^{c}})}\Big)\Big\rvert=o(n).

(ii)

the sequence
\displaystyle\Big\{-\frac{1}{|\Lambda_{n}|}\log\gamma_{\Lambda_{n}}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{\Lambda_{n}}|\omega_{\Lambda_{n}^{c}})\Big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges uniformly in
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
to a constant which we denote by
\displaystyle P^{\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}(\gamma)
.

3.
Bowen’s property of Gibbs measures

By comparing the definitions of Gibbs measures in Statistical Mechanics and that of Bowen, it is immediately clear why Bowen’s definition is so attractive
and popular for dynamicists: it captures the most important, from the Dynamical Systems point of view, properties of Gibbsian states – uniform estimates on measures of cylindric sets in terms of ergodic averages of the potential function.
In fact, whether the
measure has the Bowen property or the weak Bowen property, rarely makes any difference in Dynamical Systems: the subexpontential bound is as good as the uniform bound in practically any computations.

Nevertheless, Bowen was fully aware that his definition of Gibbs states is not the same as in Statistical Mechanics:
"In statistical mechanics, Gibbs states are not defined by the above
theorem. We have ignored many subtleties that come up in more complicated systems"
,
[
3
]
*page 6.
To introduce the definition, Bowen was motivated by an example (
[
3
]
*page 5) of a translation-invariant pair interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
,
\displaystyle\Phi_{V}\not\equiv 0
only if
\displaystyle V=\{k\}
or
\displaystyle V=\{k,n\}
,
\displaystyle k,n\in\mathbb{Z}
, satisfying a strong summability condition

(9)

\|\Phi_{\{0\}}\|_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}|n|\cdot\|\Phi_{\{0,n\}}\|_{\infty}<\infty.

The above condition is a special case of a well-known uniqueness condition in thermodynamic formalism
[
19
,
5
]
:

(10)

\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\text{{\text{diam}}}(V)}{|V|}\cdot\|\Phi_{V}\|_{\infty}<\infty.

Let us now discuss the Bowen-Gibbs and the weak Bowen-Gibbs properties of DLR Gibbs states.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\Phi=\{\Phi_{V}\}_{V\Subset\mathbb{Z}}

is a translation-invariant UAC interaction and let

\displaystyle\phi=-\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\Phi_{V}
.
Then there exists a sequence
\displaystyle\{C_{n}\}
with
\displaystyle n^{-1}\log C_{n}\to 0
, such that for every translation-invariant Gibbs measure
\displaystyle\mu
for
\displaystyle\Phi
, for all
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
, one has

\frac{1}{C_{n}}\leq\frac{\mu\bigl(\{\tilde{\omega}\in\Omega:\ \tilde{\omega}_{0}^{n-1}=\omega_{0}^{n-1}\}\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(S_{n}\phi(\omega)-nP(\phi)\bigr)}\leq C_{n}.

If, furthermore, the interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
satisfies a stronger summability condition (
10
),
then there exists a unique Gibbs measure
\displaystyle\mu
for
\displaystyle\Phi
, and
for some
\displaystyle C>1
, every
\displaystyle n\geq 1
and all

,

(11)

\frac{1}{C}\leq\frac{\mu(\{\tilde{\omega}\in\Omega:\ \tilde{\omega}_{0}^{n-1}=\omega_{0}^{n-1}\})}{\exp\bigl(S_{n}\phi(\omega)-nP(\phi)\bigr)}\leq C.

Let us sketch the proof of this theorem using known results in Statistical Mechanics.
The first claim is standard

5

The sequence of functions
\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Lambda_{n}|}\log Z_{\Lambda_{n}}(\eta)

\displaystyle P(\Phi)=P(\phi)
uniformly in
\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle n\to\infty

[
5

*Theorem 15.30, part (a).
Thus
\displaystyle\log Z_{\Lambda_{n}}(\eta)=\exp(|\Lambda_{n}|P+o(n))
.

To study the numerator, we use the estimate (15.25) in
[
5
]

(12)

\sup_{\sigma,\eta\in\Omega}\Big\lvert\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{n}}\phi\circ S^{i}(\sigma)+H_{\Lambda_{n}}(\sigma_{\Lambda_{n}}\eta_{\Lambda_{n}^{c}})\Big\rvert\leq\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\ni i\\
V\not\subset\Lambda_{n}\end{subarray}}\rVert\Phi_{V}\lVert_{\infty},

and the fact that the uniformly absolute convergence of the interaction
\displaystyle\Phi
ensures that

\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\ni i\\
V\not\subset\Lambda_{n}\end{subarray}}\rVert\Phi_{V}\lVert_{\infty}\;=o(|\Lambda_{n}|).

The uniqueness of the Gibbs measures under (
10
) follows from Theorem 8.39 in
[
5
]
(see also Comment 8.41 and equation (8.42) in
[
5
]
).
Note that (
10
) implies that
\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\ni i\\
V\not\subset\Lambda_{n}\end{subarray}}\rVert\Phi_{V}\lVert_{\infty}

remains bounded as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
, in fact, for all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
, one has that

\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\ni i\\
V\not\subset\Lambda_{n}\end{subarray}}\rVert\Phi_{V}\lVert_{\infty}\leq\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}}{\text{{\text{diam}}}(V)}\cdot\|\Phi_{V}\|_{\infty}=\sum_{0\in V\Subset\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\text{{\text{diam}}}(V)}{|V|}\cdot\|\Phi_{V}\|_{\infty}=:D.

This together with (
12

(13)

\sup_{\sigma,\eta\in\Omega}\Big\lvert\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{n}}\phi\circ S^{i}(\sigma)-\sum_{i\in\Lambda_{n}}\phi\circ S^{i}(\sigma_{\Lambda_{n}}\eta_{\Lambda_{n}^{c}})\Big\rvert\leq 2D,

i.e.,
\displaystyle\phi
satisfies Bowen’s condition
[
25
]
.
Then (
11
) follows from Theorem 4.6 in
[
25
]
and from the fact that the translation-invariant Gibbs measures for
\displaystyle\Phi
are equilibrium states for
\displaystyle\phi

[
5
]
*Theorem 15.39.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that it is not a coincidence that
the condition (
10
) implying the Bowen property, also implies uniqueness.
Indeed, suppose
\displaystyle\mu
and
\displaystyle\nu
are two ergodic measures on
\displaystyle\Omega
with the Bowen-Gibbs property for some continuous potential
\displaystyle\phi
. Then the Bowen-Gibbs property implies
that

\frac{1}{C}\leq\frac{\mu([\omega_{0}^{n}])}{\nu([\omega_{0}^{n}])}\leq C

for all
\displaystyle n
and every
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
, and hence the measures
\displaystyle\mu
and
\displaystyle\nu
are equivalent,
and thus are equal.

4.
Potentials, Cocycles, and Specifications

An alternative, more dynamical approach to DLR-Gibbs measures, also known as the Ruelle-Capocaccia approach, was introduced in
[
4
]
.
However, as demonstrated in the original work
[
4
]
, for lattice systems—which is the setting in this paper—the Ruelle-Capocaccia definition of Gibbs measures coincides with the specification-based definition given in
this paper.
Keller’s book
[
11
]
*Chapter 5 provides an excellent summary of Gibbs measures following the Ruelle-Capocaccia approach under the assumption that the underlying potential has summable variations.
Below we explore how this approach extends to cases where the potential lacks summable variations but still has the extensibility property.

Recall that the extensibility condition requires that for all
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
and every
\displaystyle a,\tilde{a}\in E
, the sequence
of functions

(14)

\rho_{n}^{a,\tilde{a}}(\omega)=\sum_{i=-n}^{n}\big[\phi(S^{i}\omega^{a})-\phi(S^{i}\omega^{\tilde{a}})\big],\quad n\geq 0,

converges uniformly as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
.
Here, we use the notation
\displaystyle\omega^{a}=(\omega^{a}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
is given by

\omega^{a}_{k}=\begin{cases}a,&\ k=0,\\
\omega_{k},&\ k\neq 0.\end{cases}

Therefore, we can define a continuous function
\displaystyle\rho_{n}^{a,\tilde{a}}(\omega):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,

\rho(\omega^{a},\omega^{\tilde{a}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\rho_{n}^{a,\tilde{a}}(\omega).

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
\displaystyle\phi
satisfies the extensibility condition, then for any pair
\displaystyle\xi,\eta\in\Omega
,
such that the set
\displaystyle\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\xi_{k}\neq\eta_{k}\}
is finite, the sequence of continuous functions

(15)

\rho_{n}(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{i=-n}^{n}\big[\phi(S^{i}\xi)-\phi(S^{i}\eta)\big],\quad n\geq 0,

converges.
Furthermore,

(1)

the limiting function
\displaystyle\rho(\xi,\eta)=\lim_{n}\rho_{n}(\xi,\eta)
is a
cocycle
, i.e.,
for every
\displaystyle\xi,\eta,\zeta\in\Omega
with
\displaystyle\xi_{i}=\eta_{i}=\zeta_{i}
for all
\displaystyle i
with
\displaystyle|i|\gg 1
, one has

(16)

\rho(\xi,\zeta)=\rho(\xi,\eta)+\rho(\eta,\zeta);

(2)

\displaystyle\rho
is translation-invariant in the sense that for every pair
\displaystyle(\xi,\eta)
with
\displaystyle\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\xi_{k}\neq\eta_{k}\}
finite,

(17)

\rho(\xi,\eta)=\rho(S\xi,S\eta).

(3)

for every
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
and
\displaystyle\eta_{\Lambda},\zeta_{\Lambda}\in E^{\Lambda}
,
\displaystyle\rho(\eta_{\Lambda}\xi_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\Lambda},\zeta_{\Lambda}\xi_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\Lambda})
is a continuous function of
\displaystyle\xi
.

Proof.

We carry out the proof in two steps.

First Step:
Let
\displaystyle\xi,\eta\in\Omega
such that for some
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\displaystyle\xi_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{k\}}=\eta_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{k\}}
.
Without loss of generality, let
\displaystyle k>0
.
Then

(18)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=-n}^{n}[\phi\circ S^{i-k}(S^{k}\xi)-\phi\circ S^{i-k}(S^{k}\eta)]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=-n-k}^{n-k}[\phi\circ S^{i}(S^{k}\xi)-\phi\circ S^{i}(S^{k}\eta)]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=-n}^{n}[\phi\circ S^{i}(S^{k}\xi)-\phi\circ S^{i}(S^{k}\eta)]

(19)

\displaystyle\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}[\phi\circ S^{i}(S^{k}\xi)-\phi\circ S^{i}(S^{k}\eta)]

(20)

\displaystyle\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=-n-k}^{-n-1}[\phi\circ S^{i}(S^{k}\xi)-\phi\circ S^{i}(S^{k}\eta)].

Since
\displaystyle(S^{k}\xi)_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}=(S^{k}\eta)_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}
, the extensibility property of
\displaystyle\phi
yields that the sum in (
18
) converges uniformly to
\displaystyle\rho^{\phi}(S^{k}\xi,S^{k}\eta)
as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
.
Note that for any
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{Z}
,

|\phi\circ S^{i}(S^{k}\xi)-\phi\circ S^{i}(S^{k}\eta)|\leq\delta_{i}\phi\leq\mathtt{var}_{(-|i|,\;|i|)}\phi\xrightarrow[|i|\to\infty]{}0.

Thus for (
19
) and (
20
), one has that

\Big|\sum_{i=n-k+1}^{n}[\phi\circ S^{-i}(S^{k}\xi)-\phi\circ S^{-i}(S^{k}\eta)]\Big|\leq k\cdot\mathtt{var}_{(k-n,\;n-k)}\phi

and

\Big|\sum_{i=-n-k}^{-n-1}[\phi\circ S^{-i}(S^{k}\xi)-\phi\circ S^{-i}(S^{k}\eta)]\Big|\leq k\cdot\mathtt{var}_{(k-n,\;n-k)}\phi.

Thus since
\displaystyle k
is fixed and
\displaystyle\mathtt{var}_{(k-n,\;n-k)}\phi\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0
, both sums in (
19
) and (
20
) converge uniformly to
\displaystyle 0
as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
.
Therefore,
\displaystyle\rho
is defined at
\displaystyle(\xi,\eta)
and
\displaystyle\rho(\xi,\eta)=\rho(S^{k}\xi,S^{k}\eta)
.

Second Step:

Let
\displaystyle\xi,\eta\in\Omega
such that for some
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
,
\displaystyle\xi_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\Lambda}=\eta_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\Lambda}
.
Then there exists
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\displaystyle\Lambda\subset[-m,m]\cap\mathbb{Z}
.
Then for
\displaystyle n>m
, one has

\sum_{i=-n}^{n}[\phi\circ S^{i}(\xi)-\phi\circ S^{i}(\eta)]=\sum_{j=-m}^{m}\sum_{i=-n}^{n}[\phi\circ S^{i}(\xi_{-\infty}^{-m-1}\eta_{-m}^{j-1}\xi_{j}^{\infty})-\phi\circ S^{i}(\xi_{-\infty}^{-m-1}\eta_{-m}^{j}\xi_{j+1}^{\infty})].

By the first step, the sum over
\displaystyle i
on the RHS of the last equation above converges uniformly for each
\displaystyle j
as
\displaystyle n\to\infty
.

We now address statements (1)-(3).
Claim (1) follows directly from the definition of
\displaystyle\rho
.
Equations (
16
) and (
17
) easily follow from the first and second steps discussed above.
The continuity of the map
\displaystyle\xi\mapsto\rho(\eta_{\Lambda}\xi_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\Lambda},\zeta_{\Lambda}\xi_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\Lambda})
for all
\displaystyle\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}
and
\displaystyle\eta,\zeta\in\Omega
is a consequence of the uniform convergence of (
15
).

∎

Now, we shall discuss how to associate a Gibbsian specification with an extensible potential and vice versa.
We have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

(i) Suppose
\displaystyle\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function with extensibility property, then

\displaystyle\gamma^{\phi}=(\gamma^{\phi}_{\Lambda})_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}}
given by

(21)

\gamma_{\Lambda}^{\phi}(\omega_{\Lambda}|\omega_{\Lambda^{c}})=\Big(\sum_{\xi_{\Lambda}\in E^{\Lambda}}e^{\rho^{\phi}(\xi_{\Lambda}\omega_{\mathbb{Z}\setminus\Lambda},\;\omega)}\Big)^{-1},\;\;\omega\in\Omega

is a translation invariant Gibbsian specification.

(ii) Suppose
\displaystyle\gamma=(\gamma_{\Lambda})_{\Lambda\Subset\mathbb{Z}}
is a translation invariant Gibbsian specification, then

(22)

\phi_{\gamma}(\omega)=\log\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}(\omega_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{0}|\mathtt{a}_{-\infty}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})},\;\;\omega\in\Omega

is a continuous function with extensibility property such that
\displaystyle\gamma^{\phi_{\gamma}}=\gamma
.

Proof.

(i):

The association between the Gibbsian specifications and the continuous cocycles given by (
21
) is known and holds in much greater generality than the setup of this paper
[
1
]
*Proposition 2.12 and Proposition 2.13.
Furthermore,
\displaystyle\gamma^{\phi}
is translation-invariant if and only if
\displaystyle\rho^{\phi}
is translation-invariant, and the latter has been shown in the above argument (see also Subsection 2.2 and 2.3 in
[
1
]

(ii):

Pick any
\displaystyle a_{0},b_{0}\in E
and
\displaystyle\omega\in\Omega
.
Since
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma}
is a one-sided function, one can check that for any
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}

(23)

\rho_{n}^{a_{0},b_{0}}(\omega)-\rho_{0}^{a_{0},b_{0}}(\omega)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\phi_{\gamma}\circ S^{-i}(b_{0}\omega_{\{0\}^{c}})-\phi_{\gamma}\circ S^{-i}(a_{0}\omega_{\{0\}^{c}})\Big]

and for the right-hand side of the above equation, one has

(24)

RHS=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}((\omega_{-i})_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\mathbb{N}}(\omega_{-i+1}^{-1}b_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\mathbb{N}}(\omega_{-i+1}^{-1}b_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{1}^{\infty})}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\mathbb{N}}(\omega_{-i+1}^{-1}a_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}((\omega_{-i})_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\mathbb{N}}(\omega_{-i+1}^{-1}a_{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{1}^{\infty})}.

Then, by applying the bar moving property, one gets

\rho_{n}^{a_{0},b_{0}}(\omega)-\rho_{0}^{a_{0},b_{0}}(\omega)=\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{\gamma_{\{0,i\}}((\omega_{-i})_{0}(b_{0})_{i}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\mathbb{N}}(\omega_{-i+1}^{-1})_{1}^{i-1}(\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{i+1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0,i\}}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{0}(b_{0})_{i}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\mathbb{N}}(\omega_{-i+1}^{-1})_{1}^{i-1}(\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{i+1}^{\infty})}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{\{0,i\}}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{0}(a_{0})_{i}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\mathbb{N}}(\omega_{-i+1}^{-1})_{1}^{i-1}(\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{i+1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0,i\}}((\omega_{-i})_{0}(a_{0})_{i}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\mathbb{N}}(\omega_{-i+1}^{-1})_{1}^{i-1}(\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{i+1}^{\infty})}

and thus, by applying the bar moving property once more,

\rho_{n}^{a_{0},b_{0}}-\rho_{0}^{a_{0},b_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{\gamma_{\{i\}}((b_{0})_{i}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\mathbb{N}}(\omega_{-i}^{-1})_{0}^{i-1}(\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{i+1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{i\}}((a_{0})_{i}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\mathbb{N}}(\omega_{-i}^{-1})_{0}^{i-1}(\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{i+1}^{\infty})}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{\{i\}}((a_{0})_{i}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbb{Z}_{-}}(\omega_{-i+1}^{-1})_{1}^{i-1}(\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{i+1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{i\}}((b_{0})_{i}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbb{Z}_{-}}(\omega_{-i+1}^{-1})_{1}^{i-1}(\omega_{1}^{\infty})_{i+1}^{\infty})},

here
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{-}
denotes
\displaystyle-\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
Hence, by the translation-invariance of
\displaystyle\gamma
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho_{n}^{a_{0},b_{0}}(\omega)-\rho_{0}^{a_{0},b_{0}}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}(b_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-i-1}\omega_{-i}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}(a_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-i-1}\omega_{-i}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}(a_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-i}\omega_{-i+1}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}(b_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-i}\omega_{-i+1}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}(b_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-i-1}\omega_{-i}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}(a_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-i-1}\omega_{-i}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\log\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}(b_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-i-1}\omega_{-i}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}(a_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-i-1}\omega_{-i}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}(b_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-n-1}\omega_{-n}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}(a_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-n-1}\omega_{-n}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}-\log\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}(b_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}(a_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}.

Thus

(25)

\rho_{n}^{a_{0},b_{0}}(\omega)=\log\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}(b_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-n-1}\omega_{-n}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}(a_{0}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-n-1}\omega_{-n}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}\;\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 10.27782pt\hskip-10.27782pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\hbox{\hskip 10.2778pt\hskip-10.2778pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\hbox{\hskip 10.2778pt\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{$\displaystyle$}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip-10.2778pt\raisebox{2.35173pt}{\hbox{$\displaystyle\scalebox{1}[.3]{$\displaystyle\rule[1.93747pt]{12.91663pt}{1.5pt}\kern-0.86108pt{\blacktriangleright}$}$}}\hskip-10.2778pt\hskip 10.2778pt}$}\hskip-10.2778pt\hskip-10.2778pt\raisebox{-8.79617pt}{\hbox{$\displaystyle\scalebox{1}[.3]{$\displaystyle\rule[1.93747pt]{12.91663pt}{1.5pt}\kern-0.86108pt{\blacktriangleright}$}$}}\hskip-10.2778pt\hskip 10.2778pt}$}\hskip-10.27782pt\hskip-8.5064pt\raisebox{-9.27515pt}{\hbox{$\displaystyle\scriptstyle\begin{subarray}{c}n\to\infty\\
\omega\in\Omega\end{subarray}$}}\hskip-8.5064pt\hskip 10.27782pt}}\;\log\frac{\gamma_{\{0\}}(b_{0}|\omega_{-\infty}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}{\gamma_{\{0\}}(a_{0}|\omega_{-\infty}^{-1}\omega_{1}^{\infty})}.

The last limit also shows that
\displaystyle\gamma^{\phi_{\gamma}}=\gamma
.

Proofs

The proofs of Theorem A and Theorem B will be based on two lemmas.
Our first lemma states the following.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\phi\in C(\Omega)
be an extensible function and
\displaystyle\gamma^{\phi}
be the associated Gibbsian specification.
Then the one-sided extensible function
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}\in C(\Omega_{+})

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle C\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{1,S}(\Omega)

\int_{\Omega}\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}d\tau=\int_{\Omega}\phi d\tau+C.

Remark 5.2
.

The second part of Theorem
4.2
implies that the chain
\displaystyle\gamma\rightarrow\phi_{\gamma}\rightarrow\gamma^{\phi_{\gamma}}
is closed, i.e.,
\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma^{\phi_{\gamma}}
.

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\phi\rightarrow\gamma^{\phi}\rightarrow\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}

\displaystyle\phi=\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}

Note that
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}
is a half-line function.
For any
\displaystyle\omega_{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\in\Omega_{+}

(27)

\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}(\omega_{\mathbb{Z}_{+}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=-n}^{n}\Big[\phi\circ S^{i}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-1}\omega_{0}^{\infty})-\phi\circ S^{i}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})\Big]

and the above limit is uniform on
\displaystyle\omega_{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\in\Omega_{+}

For any
\displaystyle\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{1,S}(\Omega_{+})
and
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i=-n}^{n}\Big[\phi\circ S^{i}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{-1}\omega_{0}^{\infty})-\phi\circ S^{i}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{-\infty}^{0}\omega_{1}^{\infty})\Big]\tau(d\omega).

\displaystyle\Theta_{[\ell_{1}\ell_{2}]}(\omega)=\omega

Note that the families

), we obtain that

(38)

\mu([\sigma_{0}^{n}])=e^{-nP^{\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}(\gamma)}\cdot\frac{\gamma_{[0,n]}(\sigma_{[0,n]}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,n]^{c}})}{\gamma_{[0,n]}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,n]}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,n]^{c}})}\cdot e^{o(n)}.

Using the bar moving property (
7
), we have that

(39)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{[0,n]}(\sigma_{[0,n]}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,n]^{c}})}{\gamma_{[0,n]}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,n]}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,n]^{c}})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{[0,n]}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,i)}\sigma_{[i,n]}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,n]^{c}})}{\gamma_{[0,n]}(\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,i]}\sigma_{(i,n]}|\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,n]^{c}})}\overset{(\ref{eq: bar moving prop})}{=}\prod_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\gamma_{\{i\}}(\sigma_{i}|\sigma_{(i,n]}{\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}_{[i,n]^{c}})}{\gamma_{\{i\}}({\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}_{i}|\sigma_{(i,n]}{\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}_{[i,n]^{c}})}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{n}e^{\phi_{\gamma}\circ S^{i}(\sigma_{[0,n]}\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,n]^{c}})}.

Note that in the last equation of (
39
), we used the fact that
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma}
is independent of the components in the negative half-line
\displaystyle-\mathbb{N}
.
Combining (
39
) with (
38
), we get

(40)

\mu([\sigma_{0}^{n}])=\frac{e^{S_{n+1}\phi_{\gamma}(\sigma_{[0,n]}\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}_{[0,n]^{c}})}}{e^{nP^{\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}(\gamma)}}\cdot e^{o(n)}.

Note that

(41)

\mathtt{var}_{n}(S_{n+1}\phi_{\gamma})\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathtt{var}_{k}(\phi_{\gamma})

and since
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma}
is continuous,
\displaystyle var_{k}(\phi_{\gamma})\to 0
as
\displaystyle k\to\infty
.
Thus
\displaystyle\mathtt{var}_{n}(S_{n+1}\phi_{\gamma})=o(n)
, and hence we obtain from
(
40
) that

(42)

\mu([\sigma_{0}^{n}])=\frac{e^{S_{n+1}\phi_{\gamma}(\sigma)}}{e^{nP^{\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}(\gamma)}}e^{o(n)}.

We note that the weak Bowen-Gibbs property (
42
) implies that
\displaystyle P^{\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}(\gamma)=P(\phi_{\gamma})
, where
\displaystyle P(\phi_{\gamma})
is the topological pressure of
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma}
.

(ii):

Now take any
\displaystyle\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{1,S}(\Omega)
and
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathscr{G}_{S}(\gamma)
.
The relative entropy
\displaystyle H_{n}(\tau|\mu)
is given by

(43)

H_{n}(\tau|\mu)=\sum_{a_{0}^{n}\in E^{n+1}}\tau([a_{0}^{n}])\log\frac{\tau([a_{0}^{n}])}{\mu([a_{0}^{n}])}=\sum_{a_{0}^{n}\in E^{n+1}}\tau([a_{0}^{n}])\log\tau([a_{0}^{n}]).-\sum_{a_{0}^{n}\in E^{n+1}}\tau([a_{0}^{n}])\log\mu([a_{0}^{n}])

For the first sum in (
43
), one has that

(44)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{a_{0}^{n}\in E^{n+1}}\tau([a_{0}^{n}])\log\tau([a_{0}^{n}])\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}-h(\tau).

For the second sum, by inserting (
41
) and using (
42
), one has

(45)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{a_{0}^{n}\in E^{n+1}}\tau([a_{0}^{n}])\log\mu([a_{0}^{n}])

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{a_{0}^{n}\in E^{n+1}}\tau([a_{0}^{n}])(S_{n+1}\phi_{\gamma}-(n+1)P^{\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}(\gamma)+o(n)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(n+1)\int_{\Omega}\phi_{\gamma}d\tau-(n+1)P^{\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}(\gamma)+o(n).

By combining (
44
) and (
45
), and since
\displaystyle P^{\mathtt{\mathbf{a}}}(\gamma)=P(\phi_{\gamma})
, one concludes that the relative entropy density rate
\displaystyle h(\tau|\mu)
indeed exists and

(46)

h(\tau|\mu)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}H_{n}(\tau|\mu)=-h(\tau)-\int_{\Omega}\phi_{\gamma}d\tau+P(\phi_{\gamma}).

Thus the Variational Principle (Theorem
2.2
) yields that
\displaystyle\tau
is a Gibbs state for
\displaystyle\gamma
if and only if
\displaystyle\tau
is an equilibrium state for
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathscr{G}_{S}(\gamma)=\mathcal{ES}(\phi_{\gamma})
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
A
.

One can easily see that weakly cohomologous potentials have the same equilibrium states because the functionals

\displaystyle\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{1,S}(\Omega)\mapsto h(\tau)+\int_{\Omega}\phi d\tau
and
\displaystyle\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{1,S}(\Omega)\mapsto h(\tau)+\int_{\Omega}\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}d\tau

differ by only a constant
\displaystyle P(\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}})-P(\phi)
.
Furthermore, the weak cohomology between
\displaystyle\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}
and
\displaystyle\phi
also yields the following

[
23
]
*Proposition 2.34:

(47)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\Big\rVert\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\Big[\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}-\phi-P(\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}})+P(\phi)\Big]\circ S^{i}\Big\lVert_{\infty}=0.

Then the first part of Theorem A follows from Lemma
5.1
and the second part of Lemma
5.3
since the weak cohomologous potentials have the same set of equilibrium states, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathcal{ES}(\phi)=\mathcal{ES}(\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}})
.
The second part of Theorem A also follows from Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.3
.
In fact,
by applying the first and second parts of Lemma
5.3
to
\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma^{\phi}
, one obtains from Lemma
5.1
that for any equilibrium state
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathcal{ES}(\phi)
,

(48)

\mu([\sigma_{0}^{n-1}])=\frac{e^{(S_{n}\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}})(\sigma)}}{e^{nP(\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}})}}e^{o(n)},\;\;\sigma\in\Omega,

and by (
47
), one has that
\displaystyle S_{n}(\phi-P(\phi))=S_{n}(\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}-P(\phi_{\gamma^{\phi}}))+o(n)
.
Hence one immediately concludes the weak Bowen-Gibbs property of
\displaystyle\mu
with respect to the potential
\displaystyle\phi
.

∎
