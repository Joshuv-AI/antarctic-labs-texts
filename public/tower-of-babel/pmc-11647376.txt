Not too short, not too long, but just right: does pre-arrival time impact team performance?
Authors: Jacob Schriner, Ryan Dumas
Published: 2024

‘Effective performance is preceded by painstaking preparation.’ Brian Tracy

Although trauma teams often do not have the luxury of ‘painstaking preparation’, trauma resuscitation is a race against time. Increasingly, team performance has been shown to improve patient outcomes1 2 and team readiness and handoff is key to peak performance.3 Rastegar et al should be commended for their efforts to define the ‘sweet spot’ of prearrival preparation time. But how realistic is this ‘sweet spot’?

Rastegar et al demonstrate using trauma video review that a team’s completion of specific resuscitation milestones is impacted by an optimal prearrival ‘prep time’.4 Importantly, however, the data also demonstrate that over 80% of trauma patients arrived outside of this optimal time frame. In many urban settings, preactivation and subsequent notification are impacted by a multitude of different elements, such as mode of transport, (police vs emergency medical service, ground vs air) as well as effective communication between the prehospital environment and hospital receiving team. Ultimately, the ‘sweet spot’ may be hard or impossible to achieve. Although a consistently reproducible preactivation time will likely prove to be unobtainable, steps can be taken to reduce prep time necessary for resuscitation milestone completion. For example, if, as results of this study suggest, 7 minutes of preparation time allows for blood to be transfused 6 minutes faster on average, efforts should be made to identify and eliminate inefficiency in the transfusion pathway. Sullivan et al optimized the location of uncrossmatched blood in the emergency department, and simultaneously standardized intravenous access pathways and developed mentorship for new staff members.5 Their efforts resulted in an 11-minute reduction in time to transfusion. Additionally, early establishment and prioritization of vascular access has been demonstrated to shorten the time to resuscitation.6 7

Although the logical plausibility of these data suggest they would be generalizable, each trauma system faces its own unique challenges and will require individualized evaluation to address their own limitations. Although we agree that attention should be paid to optimizing preactivation time, it is possible that pragmatically waiting to activate the small percentage of traumas with a long preactivation time is the only means the trauma team will have to optimize preactivation time. The focus should be on continuing to improve efficiency of critical action pathways through mechanisms such as team member introductions, standardized roles, and therapeutic pathways that are reinforced outside of the time of the acute trauma resuscitation.
