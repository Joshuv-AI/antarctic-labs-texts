Segmentation of the general public according to differences in knowledge and beliefs about radiation-cluster analysis by attitude, knowledge, belief and anxiety
Authors: Kei Hirai, Asayo Yamamura, Yuko Matsumura, Asako Miura, Ekou Yagi, Ryohei Fujino, Masaharu Tsubokura, Fumio Ohtaka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing the 2011 Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant accident, public behaviors have been marked by excessive avoidance and stigma, driven by fear and uncertainty regarding radiation exposure and its health implications. Despite extensive media dissemination of information on radiation, the precise nature of the public’s knowledge, beliefs and the resultant behavioral responses remain unclear. This study aimed to segment the population based on their attitudes, knowledge, beliefs and anxiety levels about radiation, correlating these factors with their cognitive and behavioral responses to radiation exposure. Surveying 2400 individuals, we identified seven distinct segments that illustrated a spectrum of health concerns, even among those well-informed about radiation. Notably, individuals with higher health anxiety were found to reject discriminatory prejudices linked to radiation, yet they tended to distrust official information, potentially as a psychological mechanism to justify their avoidance behaviors. These findings underscore the need for tailored communication strategies that address the complex landscape of radiation-related perceptions and misinformation.Keywords: Fukushima, radiation risk perception, nuclear power plant accident, segment, action, Japanese

INTRODUCTION

The Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant disaster on 11 March 2011 led to significant radioactive material release and dispersion. This event has sparked ongoing concerns in Japan regarding health effects of radiation and associated fears, extending beyond Fukushima Prefecture.

Radiation levels in most Fukushima areas, excluding the vicinity of the power plant, have declined to levels comparable to global major cities [1]. The radiation from this accident is significantly lower than that of the Chernobyl accident, with no noticeable rise in radiation-related health issues, including genetic effects and cancer [2]. However, the mental health impacts from this accident, including social stigma and self-stigma among evacuees, are profound [3, 4]. Animal studies have shown that radiation induces genetic effects on the next generation, such as an increase in germ cell mutation frequency Nakamura et al. [5], but despite no observed genetic effects of radiation on subsequent generations [6], anxiety exceeding scientific risk assessments persists [7].

The accident also influenced consumer behavior, leading to avoidance of Fukushima-produced foods and travel to the region [8, 9]. Despite testing showing Fukushima rice since 2015 has not exceeded standard limits [10], and minimal radioactive materials in household food [11], public concerns remain [12].

This study examines how the dissemination of diverse health information post-disaster impacts mental health and behaviors [13]. Information sources play a critical role in shaping risk perception and decision-making [14]. Surveys reveal that reliance on informal information sources, like word of mouth, correlates with heightened radiation health concerns [15–17]. This trend underscores the challenges posed by rapidly spreading, unverified information online.

There is an inconsistency in how the Fukushima accident, subjective knowledge and risk perception interplay [18]. Nakayama et al. [16] found that higher health literacy correlates with reduced anxiety, highlighting the need for clear, accessible health communication. Therefore, this study aims to segment the general public based on their attitudes, experiences, knowledge, beliefs and behaviors regarding radiation. By employing the ‘fast and slow’ theory of cognitive processing, this research intends to elucidate the mechanisms behind heightened health anxiety and contribute to more effective health literacy and information dissemination strategies. This approach emphasizes the originality and social significance of this study in addressing the post-disaster challenges of misinformation and mental health impacts.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study participants and procedures

The study was conducted in February 2022. The study participants comprised 2400 individuals (375 men and 375 women in Fukushima Prefecture and 825 men and 825 women outside Fukushima Prefecture; M = 48.98 years, SD = 13.09) aged 20 to 80 years living in Japan. They were recruited by an Internet research firm.

Survey contents

Respondents completed a survey that took ~20 minutes. It encompassed demographic questions such as age, gender, residential area, marital status, child-rearing experience, evacuation history related to the Great East Japan Earthquake and exposure to radiation-related education.

Question items

The survey included items developed based on field surveys and literature ([19–23]):

(a) Attitude toward radiation: Participants responded to 9 questions designed to gauge their attitudes towards radiation. These included items such as:

1. ‘I know the average radiation level in the area where I currently live.’

2. ‘I think I am always exposed to radiation when I am in an unroofed place on earth (outdoors) because radiation is a natural occurrence.’

3. ‘If there are no major health hazards to people living in Fukushima Prefecture now, I would consider that there is little or no radiation effect.’

Responses were captured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘Not at all true’ (1) to ‘Very true’ (5), allowing for nuanced expression of agreement or disagreement with these statements.

(b) Knowledge about radiation: This section comprised 12 questions assessing the respondents’ understanding of radiation. Key questions included:

1. ‘Radiation, radioactivity and radioactive materials are all different things.’

2. ‘Radiation levels in major cities in Fukushima Prefecture are the same or lower than those in Tokyo in some areas.’

3. ‘Exposure to certain levels of radiation can lead to health issues such as cancer.’

Each item was rated on a five-point scale from ‘I had no idea about this’ (1) to ‘I knew about this’ (5), providing insights into the depth of the participants’ knowledge regarding radiation.

(c) Concerns and beliefs about radiation: In this section, participants answered 16 items exploring their beliefs and concerns regarding the health effects of radiation, such as:

1. ‘I think there is a high probability that radiation will have health effects on the next generation of residents in the area where the nuclear accident occurred.’

2. ‘I believe that the risk of getting cancer from radiation exposure is higher than from lifestyle habits (diet, smoking, etc.).’

3. ‘I am concerned about the long-term environmental impacts of radiation exposure.’

Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale from ‘Not at all true’ (1) to ‘Very true’ (5), aiming to capture the breadth and intensity of participants’ concerns and beliefs about radiation’s impact on health and the environment.

Statistical analysis

The study first conducted factor analysis to confirm the structure of the collected items. Subsequently, cluster analysis was employed to segment the subjects and explore variable differences among these segments. These analyses aimed to elucidate distinct attitudes and perceptions toward radiation. IBM SPSS Statistics 26 was utilized for all statistical analyses.

Ethical considerations

The survey was conducted on a voluntary basis, and the right to interrupt or refuse the survey was clearly stated on the web screen and communicated to the collaborators. This study was reviewed by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Education, Graduate School of Human Sciences, Osaka University (No. 21098).

RESULTS

Structural analysis of the scale

Exploratory factor analysis was performed on the 12-item ‘knowledge about radiation’ and 16-item ‘anxiety and belief about radiation’ scales. The eigenvalues for ‘knowledge about radiation’ were 6.21, 1.25, 0.71, 0.52 and 0.50, respectively. For ‘anxiety and belief about radiation,’ the values were 4.61, 2.14, 1.14, 0.97 and 0.88.

Upon conducting factor analysis using the maximum likelihood method and Promax rotation, three factors emerged for ‘Knowledge of radiation,’ cumulatively explaining 70.60% of the variance. These were categorized as ‘General Knowledge of Radiation,’ ‘Knowledge about Radiation Mechanism’ and ‘Knowledge of Current Situation in Fukushima,’ with alpha coefficients of 0.88, 0.76 and 0.79, respectively (Table 1).

Clustering of subjects

K-means cluster analysis identified seven distinct segments: Segment A (251 respondents), Segment B (330), Segment C (385), Segment D (369), Segment E (585), Segment F (275) and Segment G (205).

One-way ANOVA and χ2 tests were conducted with Segment as the independent variable and Age, Gender, Place of residence, Marital Status, Childcare, and childcare experience, Evacuation experience and Opportunity to learn about radiation as dependent variables. The results showed significant differences in all variables except marital status; multiple comparisons using the Tukey method and residual analysis revealed that in terms of age, segments A, C and D were older than segments E, F and G, and segment B was also older than segments F and G. Segment B is also older than segments F and G. It was also shown that segment A was older than segment B and segment E was older than segment G. In terms of gender, it was found that there were more males in segments A and B and more females in segments E and F. In terms of place of residence, it was found that more respondents in segments A and C lived in Fukushima Prefecture, while in the other segments, more respondents lived outside Fukushima Prefecture. In terms of childcare and childcare experience, few respondents in segment B had such experience, while many respondents in segment C had such experience. In Evacuation experience, more respondents had experience in segments A and C, while fewer had experience in segments B, D, E and F. In Opportunity to learn about radiation, more respondents had experience in segments A and C, and fewer in segments D, E and F.

This segmentation and quantification of views are critical for formulating targeted communication and educational strategies to address diverse levels of knowledge and anxiety about radiation in different population segments.

DISCUSSION

Structural analysis of the scales

The factor analysis of ‘knowledge about radiation’ identified three dimensions: ‘General Knowledge,’ ‘Mechanism Knowledge’ and ‘Current Situation in Fukushima.’ Similarly, ‘Fear and Belief about Radiation’ were categorized into ‘Negative Image,’ ‘Utility Value’ and ‘Distrust.’ These findings align with Feng et al. [24], highlighting the public’s negative perception of radiation, influenced by the nuclear accident and carcinogenic classifications. However, as Cross & Price [25] noted, the complexity of issues like radiation often leads to avoidance and misunderstandings. Although Evans et al. [26] suggested a discrepancy in perceived versus actual health risks, our scales’ significance lies in their ability to measure knowledge and beliefs about radiation. However, the lower reliability scores for ‘Utility Value’ and ‘Distrust’ (0.63 and 0.70, respectively) suggest a need for revising these scales to enhance their validity and reliability.

Segmentation and characteristics of attitudes toward radiation

Our cluster analysis, rephrased as segmentation, classified participants into seven distinct groups. Segments A and B exhibited high radiation knowledge, understanding of nuclear energy, and low health concerns. In contrast, Segments C and D, while knowledgeable, showed heightened health anxiety and distrust of nuclear energy. Segments E and F displayed moderate knowledge and health concerns. Segment G, with low knowledge and anxiety, showed significant avoidance behavior. On the other hand, regarding the differences between Segments A and B and Segments C and D, Segments A and C tended to have more residents in Fukushima Prefecture and were more aware of radiation levels in their residential areas. In addition, many of the respondents in Segments A and C had experienced evacuation due to the Great East Japan Earthquake and had many opportunities to learn about radiation. This suggests that Segments A and C had lived in and around Fukushima Prefecture at the time of the Great East Japan Earthquake, had experienced evacuation, had the habit of monitoring radiation levels, and had many opportunities to learn about radiation (Table 4).

Overview of characteristics of each segment

Interestingly, among the well-informed, there was a division between high and low health anxiety groups. Segments C and D, likely influenced by informal sources [15, 16], and displayed heightened anxiety. This finding resonates with Kahneman’s [27] two-system theory, where established stereotypes resist change despite new information, as observed by Kashiwazaki et al. [28]. People with high anxiety about radiation also showed strong opposition to discrimination related to radiation, suggesting cognitive dissonance [29]. This may lead to distrust in authorities and evasive actions due to perceived misinformation. Supporting general health concerns, as Kawasaki et al. [14] proposed, could alleviate this anxiety.

Summary and future directions

This study’s segmentation approach revealed distinct perceptions of radiation, highlighting the need for tailored information delivery. Future work should refine the ‘Knowledge about Radiation’ and ‘Anxiety and Beliefs about Radiation’ scales for improved validity and reliability. Additionally, exploring personalities and behaviors within each segment could enable more targeted communication strategies. Investigating the impact of tailored messages on each segment’s health behaviors, consumption patterns and critical thinking is a crucial future direction. It would be desirable to consider how messages such as updates on radiation, health concern recommendations, recommendations for appropriate preventive action, and more specifically the latest findings on radiation effects (e.g. United Nations Scientific Committee on the Effects of Atomic Radiation (UNSCEAR) findings) and the current status of food safety in Fukushima Prefecture, would be received by each segment.

In summary, this research underscores the importance of understanding diverse public perceptions of radiation and the need for segment-specific communication strategies to address radiation-related knowledge and anxiety effectively.
