Space-Time Statistical Solutions of the Incompressible Euler Equations and Landau-Lifschitz Fluctuating Hydrodynamics
Gregory L. Eyink and Lowen Peng

Abstract
. We study the infinite Reynolds limit of the solutions of the Landau-Lifschitz-Navier-Stokes equations for an incompressible fluid on a d d -dimensional torus for d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . These equations, which model thermal fluctuations in fluids, are given a standard physical interpretation as a low-wavenumber “effective field theory”, rather than as stochastic partial differential equations. We study solutions which enjoy some Besov regularity in space, as expected for initial data chosen from a driven, turbulent steady-state ensemble. The empirical basis for this regularity hypothesis, uniform in Reynolds number, is carefully discussed. Considering the initial-value problem for the Landau-Lifschitz equations, our main result is that the infinite Reynolds number limit is a space-time statistical solution of the incompressible Euler equations in the sense of Vishik & Fursikov. Such solutions are described by a probability measure on space-time velocity fields whose realizations are weak solutions of the incompressible Euler equations, each with the same prescribed initial data. Keywords: inviscid limit, fluctuating hydrodynamics, turbulence, non-uniqueness, spontaneous stochasticity Mathematics Subject Classification numbers: 82M60, 60F17, 35Q31, 35D30, 76F02

Introduction

The main motivation for the present work arises in the theory of
spontaneous stochasticity

for turbulent flows
[
1
]
. Although the pre-history of this notion lies in the
classic study of Richardson on 2-particle turbulent dispersion
[
2
]
,
the concept was first clearly formulated in the work of Bernard, Gawȩdzki, and Kupiainen

[
3
]
. Their fundamental observation was that the Hölder exponent
of the infinite-Reynolds velocity field must be
\leq 1/3,
according to a theorem of Onsager

[
4
]
, and this regularity is too low to guarantee uniqueness of the
solutions to the ODE for Lagrangian particle trajectories. The authors of
[
3
]
showed
that this non-uniqueness manifests in remnant stochasticity of Brownian particles (e.g. dye molecules)
in the limit of large Reynolds and Péclet numbers, at least for the soluble model of Kraichnan

[
5
]
, even though the limiting evolution equations for a fixed velocity realization are deterministic.
Furthermore, they showed that such “Lagrangian spontaneous stochasticity” is necessary in general for a
scalar advected by any turbulent flow, either passively or actively, to exhibit anomalous dissipation
in the infinite Reynolds & Péclet limit
[
6
]
. More recently, it was pointed out by
Mailybaev
[
7
,
8
]
that there is a closely
allied phenomenon of “Eulerian spontaneous stochasticity” in which the entire velocity field
obtained by solving the deterministic Navier-Stokes equations remains random in the infinite-Reynolds
limit, even for initial data with vanishing randomness. This result was anticipated in the profound work
of Lorenz
[
9
]
on turbulence predictability, who argued that “certain formally
deterministic fluid systems which possess many scales of motion are observationally indistinguishable
from indeterministic systems.” Spontaneous stochasticity provides a precise mathematical formulation
of Lorenz’ ideas, in which the “formally deterministic fluid system” exhibiting random solutions
is governed by the ideal Euler equations with fixed initial data, obtained in the simultaneous limit of infinite
Reynolds number and vanishing noise. The mathematical fact which underlies this persistent randomness
is the non-uniqueness of solutions to the Cauchy initial-value problem for the limiting Euler equations,
even with additional admissibility constraints imposed such as Hölder regularity and local dissipativity

[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. The prototypical example of a Kelvin-Helmholtz
unstable vortex sheet provides strong empirical evidence for this phenomenon

[
13
,
14
]
.

The vanishing noise considered in the work of Lorenz was small-scale external perturbations, such as the proverbial flap of a butterfly’s wing

[
15
]
. Intrinsic thermal noise has been more recently investigated, following
the pioneering work of Betchov
[
16
,
17
]
and Ruelle

[
18
]
. The distinct feature of thermal noise is that it is unavoidable, since
it is a necessary consequence of the local thermodynamic equilibrium required for validity of a
hydrodynamic description. The simplest fluid dynamical model which incorporates
thermal fluctuation effects is Landau-Lifschitz fluctuating hydrodynamics
[
19
]
,
a stochastic modification of the Navier-Stokes equations. For incompressible fluids in the limit
of low Mach numbers, this model takes the form in any space dimension
d\geq 2

(1)

\partial_{t}{\mathbf{u}}+\pi_{\Lambda}({\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}){\mathbf{u}}=-{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}p+\nu_{\Lambda}\triangle{\mathbf{u}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\nu_{\Lambda}k_{B}T}{\rho}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}},\qquad{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}}{\mathbf{u}}=0,

where
\pi_{\Lambda}
is the projection to Fourier modes with wavenumbers
|{\mathbf{k}}|<\Lambda,

\nu_{\Lambda}
is (scale-dependent) kinematic viscosity,
T
is absolute temperature,
k_{B}
is
Boltzmann’s constant,
\rho
is mass density, and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}=(2\nu_{\Lambda}k_{B}T/\rho)^{1/2}{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}
is a fluctuating stress modeled as a Gaussian
random matrix field, both symmetric and traceless, with mean zero and covariance

(2)

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{ij}({\mathbf{x}},t)\eta_{kl}({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\frac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\right)\delta^{d}_{\Lambda}({\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

The kinematic pressure
p
is obtained from the incompressibility constraint
{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}}{\mathbf{u}}=0.

For example, see
[
20
,
21
]
. Although the model in (
1
),(
2
)
appears superficially to be given
by a stochastic partial differential equation, such a continuum interpretation is not the customary one in
the physics literature. Instead, the model is understood to contain an explicit high-wavenumber cutoff

\Lambda,
which may be freely chosen in the range
\ell_{\nabla}^{-1}\ll\Lambda\ll\lambda_{mfp}^{-1}

where
\ell_{\nabla}
is the gradient length of the flow and
\lambda_{mfp}
is the molecular mean free path
length of the fluid. In the
language of theoretical physics, the model is an “effective field theory” which describes molecular fluids only at
large length-scales
>|\Lambda|^{-1}

[
22
,
23
]
.
In particular, the subscript on
\delta^{3}_{\Lambda}
in (
2
)
indicates that this is not a true delta-function, but only an effective delta-function for band-limited
functions restricted to wavenumbers
|{\mathbf{k}}|<\Lambda.
For more discussion, see Section
2
.

Simulations of turbulence in the presence of thermal noise have detected sensitivity
of large-scale flow features in individual flow realizations to such noise, even at high Reynolds
numbers
[
24
]
. The prior work of one of us
[
25
]

studied systematically the limit of
high Reynolds number
Re=UL/\nu_{\Lambda}
for the dynamics (
1
), which, after non-dimensionalization
based on large-scale flow parameters, takes the form

(3)

\partial_{t}{\mathbf{u}}+\pi_{\hat{\Lambda}}({\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}){\mathbf{u}}=-{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}p+\frac{1}{Re}\triangle{\mathbf{u}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\theta_{\eta}}{Re^{3(d+2)/4}}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}+\digamma{\mathbf{f}}.

See section
2
for detailed discussion. Here we note only that
\theta_{\eta}

is the ratio of thermal energy to the energy of a Kolmogorov-scale eddy, which is a
generally tiny quantity of order
10^{-8}
or smaller and fixed as
Re\to\infty.

We have added as well a large-scale body force
{\bf f}
to drive turbulence, with a dimensionless
amplitude
\digamma
which is also fixed as
Re\to\infty.
It is important to note, however,
that the dimensionless wavenumber cutoff
\hat{\Lambda}=L\Lambda
diverges together with
Re,

e.g.
\hat{\Lambda}\,\propto\,Re^{3/4}
if the cutoff is fixed relative to the Kolmgorov dissipation
scale
\eta
or
\hat{\Lambda}\propto Re
if instead the cutoff is fixed relative to the
molecular mean free path
\lambda_{mfp}.
Therefore, formally, the limiting dynamics
as
Re\to\infty
is described by the forced continuum Euler equations

(4)

\partial_{t}{\mathbf{u}}+{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}}({\mathbf{u}}\otimes{\mathbf{u}})=-{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}p+\digamma{\mathbf{f}},

with both the viscous diffusion and the thermal fluctuations vanishing in the limit. To investigate
numerically the limiting behavior for
Re\gg 1,
the authors of
[
25
]

studied a simplified “shell model” version of eq.(
1
) which allowed them to simulate
a broad range of Reynolds numbers
10^{6}
–
10^{8}.
For quasi-singular initial data
{\bf u}_{0}
with
K41 spectra over a broad range of wave-numbers, or with even more singular initial data
{\bf u}_{0}

selected from a steady-state turbulent simulation, the transition probability
P_{Re}({\bf u},t|{\bf u}_{0},t_{0})

was found to become
Re
-independent for sufficiently large Reynolds number. Most remarkably, the limiting
statistics were found to be very universal, independent of the selected sequence
Re_{k}\to\infty,

the precise details of the thermal noise model, etc. This universality is one of the hallmarks
of spontaneous stochasticity
[
14
,
26
,
27
]
.

The empirical observation by
[
25
]
of non-trivial infinite-
Re
limits
of transition probabilities for fixed initial data
{\bf u}_{0},
strongly suggests the existence
of non-unique solutions of the Cauchy problem for the limiting Euler equation (
4
).
Such a non-uniqueness is consistent with the recent results from convex integration constructions,

[
10
,
11
,
12
]
, that provide a mathematical underpinning
for spontaneous stochasticity. However, the Euler solutions provided by convex integration
do not correspond to infinite-Reynolds limits. Furthermore, no mathematical justification
was offered in the numerical study of high-
Re
turbulence

[
25
]
that the limiting
velocity realizations should indeed be (weak) solutions of the ideal Euler equation (
4
).
The purpose of the present paper is to fill this gap and to prove that infinite-
Re

limiting probability measures exist for the Landau-Lifschitz dynamics (
3
) that are
supported indeed on such weak solutions of the Euler equations. We shall model our proofs closely
after the work of Vishik & Fursikov
[
28
]
, who generalized Leray’s
construction of weak solutions of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equation to space-time
statistical solutions of incompressible Navier-Stokes equation with random initial data.
In fact, the monograph
[
28
]
developed a very general approach, which can be applied to a
great many models described by PDE’s or stochastic PDE’s.
Unlike the work on Navier-Stokes, however, we cannot derive the necessary regularity for
the limit from
a priori
energy estimates. As is well-known, the compactness that
follows from kinetic energy bounds alone does not suffice to yield standard weak Euler
solutions in the infinite-
Re
limit, but instead only still more generalized notions
of Euler solution such as the “dissipative solutions” in the sense of Lions

[
29
]
or measure-valued weak solutions
[
30
]
.
While it cannot be ruled out that such generalized weak solutions are physically relevant

[
31
,
32
]
, it was pointed out by Chen & Glimm

[
33
]
that standard weak solutions can be obtained in the infinite-
Re
limit
if additional conditions are satisfied that may be empirically verified. See also following work

[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
.
We follow a version of this strategy here, invoking a condition of spatial Besov regularity uniform
in the Reynolds number that was first employed to study inviscid limits in
[
35
]

but which in fact goes back to a remark of Isett
[
37
]
.
Recently, Fjordholm et al.
[
38
]
have proved an inviscid limit result with a similar assumption, but in the framework of statistical solutions in the Foiaş-Prodi sense, which involves single-time measures on spatial velocity fields.
See Remark
1
below.

Theorem Statement
. We now formulate in precise mathematical terms the main result of this work.
We take as flow domain the
d
-dimensional torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}=[0,2\pi)^{d},

d\geq 2,
in order to avoid issues
with boundaries. Indeed, the proper formulation of fluctuating hydrodynamics in the presence of solid walls
is still a subject under active investigation
[
39
]
. We consider the incompressible
fluid velocity field
{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}
governed by the
Landau-Lifshitz-Navier-Stokes
(LLNS) equations written
in the dimensionless form (
3
), or

(5)

\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}=\mathscr{P}\left[-{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot\pi_{\Lambda}({\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu})+\nu\triangle{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}+\pi_{\Lambda}{\mathbf{f}}\right]\mathrm{d}t+\nu^{\kappa}\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot\pi_{\Lambda}\mathrm{d}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}},\quad\theta>0

where we follow standard practice in the mathematics literature of denoting
\nu=1/Re
and considering
the infinite Reynolds number limit as an “inviscid limit.” We have, for simplicity, set
\theta_{\eta}=\digamma=1,

since these finite constants are fixed in the limit
\nu\to 0
and play no essential role in the argument. The
projection
\pi_{\Lambda}
is defined in terms of the Fourier transform
(\mathcal{F}f)({\mathbf{k}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}e^{-i{\mathbf{k}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}}{\mathbf{x}}}f({\mathbf{x}})\,\mathrm{d}^{d}x
by

(6)

\pi_{\Lambda}f({\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},\,|{\mathbf{k}}|_{\infty}<\Lambda}e^{i{\mathbf{k}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}}{\mathbf{x}}}(\mathcal{F}f)({\mathbf{k}}),

where for technical reasons of the proof related to Lizorkin representation of function spaces, we
use the max-norm
|{\mathbf{k}}|_{\infty}=\max_{i=1}^{d}|k_{i}|
and
\Lambda=2^{m}
with
m(\nu)\in{\mathbb{N}}

such that
\Lambda\sim\nu^{-\alpha}
as
\nu\to 0,

3/4\leq\alpha\leq 1.
The proof would work for a wider range
of
\alpha
but, as discussed in the Introduction and in Section
2
, only the above range is of
physical relevance. We have eliminated the pressure which enforces incompressibility in (
3
) by the Leray–Hodge projection

\mathscr{P}\equiv\operatorname{I}-{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\triangle^{-1}\operatorname{div},
given in Fourier representation by

(\mathcal{F}\mathscr{P}{\mathbf{u}})_{i}(\mathbf{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{k_{i}k_{j}}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}\right)(\mathcal{F}{\mathbf{u}})_{j}(\mathbf{k}),\quad i=1,\ldots,d.

To represent the random forcing by thermal noise, we take a probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},{\mathbb{P}}),
an increasing
filtration
(\mathscr{F}_{t}:\,t\in[0,T])
and an
\mathscr{F}_{t}
-adapted cylindrical Brownian motion
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
on the Hilbert space
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})

[
40
]
,
with
\langle{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t),h\rangle
for
t\in[0,T],

h\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
taking values in the space of symmetric, traceless
d\times d

with mean
{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t),h\rangle]={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}
and covariance

{\mathbb{E}}[\langle\xi_{ij}(t),h\rangle\langle\xi_{kl}(t^{\prime}),h^{\prime}\rangle]=\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\frac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\right)\langle h,h^{\prime}\rangle(t\wedge t^{\prime}).

The cylindrical Brownian motion can be represented

40
]
as
\langle{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t),h\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}({\mathbf{x}},t)h({\mathbf{x}})\,d^{d}x

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}({\mathbf{x}},t)=\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\xi_{ij}^{\mathbf{k}}(t)e^{i{\mathbf{k}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}}{\mathbf{x}}}

with covariance

{\mathbb{E}}[\xi_{ij}^{\mathbf{k}}(t)\xi_{kl}^{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})]=\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\frac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\right)\delta_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime},{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}}(t\wedge t^{\prime}),

and thus independent except for the constraints
\overline{\xi_{ij}^{\mathbf{k}}(t)}=\xi_{ij}^{-{\mathbf{k}}}(t),

\xi_{ji}^{\mathbf{k}}(t)=\xi_{ij}^{\mathbf{k}}(t)

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\xi_{ii}^{\mathbf{k}}(t)=0.
The representation (
7

\pi_{\Lambda}

which simply restricts the summation over
{\mathbf{k}}
to

The prefactor
\nu^{\kappa}

would work with any factor vanishing as
\nu\to 0
. We include also in (
5
) a deterministic stirring force

{\mathbf{f}}
to agitate turbulence. In addition to some mild regularity which allows for existence of solutions to (
5
), we require also

({\mathcal{F}}{\mathbf{f}})({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\,\mathrm{d}^{d}x={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},

so that there is no bulk acceleration of the fluid.

The physical necessity of the high-wavenumber cutoff

is crucial for the solution theory of the Landau-Lifschitz

SDE’s with an energy-conserving

\nu>0.
In fact, under very modest regularity assumptions, the same is true with a force
{\mathbf{f}}\neq{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}.
Thus,
for fixed initial data
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}^{\nu}=\pi_{\Lambda}{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
, there is a well-defined distribution
P^{\nu}_{{\mathbf{u}}_{0}}
on solutions of (
5

by the measure
{\mathbb{P}}.
If the initial data satisfies the zero-momentum condition

({\mathcal{F}}{\mathbf{u}}_{0})({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}{\mathbf{u}}_{0}({\mathbf{x}})\,d^{d}x={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},

then it is furthermore easy to show
[
41
]
that

(9)

({\mathcal{F}}{\mathbf{u}})({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}},t)\,d^{d}x={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},\quad\forall t\in[0,T],\quad P^{\nu}_{{\mathbf{u}}_{0}}-a.e.\ {\mathbf{u}}

For the purposes of studying spontaneous stochasticity, we are interested in non-smooth initial velocity data
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\notin C^{1,\alpha}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}),

0<\alpha<1,
for which local existence of a smooth Euler solution is not guaranteed. This restriction would not be necessary, if the smooth Euler
solution should develop a singularity in finite-time for initial data
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).
However, singular initial data are natural,
for example, if the velocity field
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
is chosen from a steady-state turbulent ensemble at
Re=\infty.
More modest regularity such as

{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}),

p>2
is nevertheless expected for such initial data, which can in fact be shown
[
41
]

to be preserved by the dynamics (
5
). We are thus motivated more generally to consider initial data
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
distributed
according to a Borel measure
\mu
on
L^{p}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
and satisfying (
8
)
\mu

the distribution over the solutions of (

The initial data of primary interest for the study

\mu=\delta_{{\mathbf{u}}_{0}},
but we may always restrict attention subsequently
to such a special case.

A principal ingredient of our theorem is the following
hypothesis

:

(10)

\sup_{\nu>0}\int\lVert{\mathbf{u}}\rVert_{L^{r}([0,T],B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}P^{\nu}_{\mu}({\mathbf{u}})<\infty,

with
2<r,p<\infty,

0<\sigma<1.
This condition is a probabilistic version of the assumption of uniform boundedness of Besov norms

invoked in several works

[

36

37
]
to derive sufficient compactness of the sequence
{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}

to guarantee strong convergence of suitable subsequences to weak Euler solutions. We are far from deriving such regularity

[
42
]
) and, as in those previous works, the primary justification is experimental and numerical observations
on turbulent velocity fields. We shall discuss in the following section
2

) holds even in the presence of thermal noise.

With this background, we can now state our main result:

Theorem 1
.

Let
P^{\nu}_{\mu},

\nu>0
be the distribution on zero-space-mean It
\bar{o}
strong solutions to (
5
) for
d\geq 2
, supposing
that the deterministic, zero-mean forcing has regularity
{\mathbf{f}}\in C^{0}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
and that the initial distribution
\mu

is supported on
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
p>2
. We assume that the initial data satisfy
{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\,{\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}}\,{\mathbf{u}}_{0}=0
in the distributional sense, for

\mu-
a.e.
{\mathbf{u}}_{0}.
We furthermore assume the uniform regularity condition (
10
) for
the same
p
and
2<r<\infty,

0<\sigma<1,
with
\Lambda=2^{m(\nu)}\sim\nu^{-\alpha}
for
m:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{N}
and
3/4\leq\alpha\leq 1
.
Then the following statements hold:

(i) The family of measures

is tight on the Borel

{\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{Z}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p})

(11)

\mathcal{Z}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p}:=L^{r}([0,T],L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap C^{0}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad\beta\geq 1+\sigma+2d.

Thus, there exists a subsequence

such that

converges weakly to some measure

i.e.

\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}P^{\nu_{k}}_{\mu}\to\int\varphi\,\mathrm{d}P_{\mu}\ \mbox{ as }\ \nu_{k}\to 0,\quad\forall\varphi\in C^{0}_{b}(\mathcal{Z}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p}),

(ii)

\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p}:=L^{r}([0,T],B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap C^{0}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{x}},t)\,\mathrm{d}^{d}x={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},\quad\forall t\in[0,T]\qquad P_{\mu}-a.e.\ {\mathbf{u}}.

(iii)

\partial_{t}{\mathbf{u}}=\mathscr{P}[-{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{u}}\otimes{\mathbf{u}})+{\mathbf{f}}],\quad{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\cdot$}}{\mathbf{u}}=0,\qquad P_{\mu}-a.e.\ {\mathbf{u}}

\gamma_{0}:{\mathbf{u}}\mapsto{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,0)

\gamma_{0}:\mathcal{Z}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p}\to B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

.
In fact,

and when restricted to
\mathcal{B}(L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

\mu.

We make a sequence of remarks concerning this result:

Remark 1
.

Important physical consequences of the theorem are obtained for the special case of deterministic (Dirac delta)
initial data,
\mu=\delta_{{\mathbf{u}}_{0}}.
In that case, the implication is that any measure
P_{{\mathbf{u}}_{0}}
obtained
as a weak limit for
\nu\to 0
is supported on weak Euler solutions
{\mathbf{u}},
all of which have the same initial
datum
{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,0)={\mathbf{u}}_{0}.
If the measure
P_{{\mathbf{u}}_{0}}
is non-trivial for suitable non-smooth initial data
{\mathbf{u}}_{0},

as suggested by numerical evidence
[
25
]
, then such non-triviality requires non-unique
solutions of the corresponding Cauchy problem for Euler. This is the essence of the concept of “spontaneous stochasticity”. Our proof can be easily modified for the stochastic shell models employed in the numerical studies

[
25
]
and, in fact, the additional regularity assumption (
10
) should be unnecessary in that case
[
43
]
. We remark that the same implications
cannot be drawn using the method of statistical solutions of Foiaş-Prodi, as in the recent
work
[
38
]
. The statistical Euler solutions constructed in that work
are probability measures
P_{\mu}^{(t)}
on a function space such as
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
which are parameterized by time
t\in[0,T]
and which satisfy, in a weak suitable sense, the forward Kolmogorov
or Liouville functional equation for an ensemble of incompressible Euler equations. Such statistical
solutions should be obtained from our space-time statistical solutions as pushforward measures

Remark 2
.

An essential feature of spontaneous stochasticity is the universality of the limiting measure
P_{{\mathbf{u}}_{0}}
which should be independent of the particular subsequence
\nu\to 0
and in fact independent
of the particular type of vanishing noise and regularization (which need not arise from thermal
fluctuations or molecular viscosity).
We obtain existence of a limit measure from tightness alone, so that we can guarantee convergence only along suitable subsequences, whose limits could in principle differ between subsequences. Previous rigorous examples of universal spontaneous statistics in model problems
[
44
,
45
]
have relied on very strong dynamical mixing properties at all scales, which imply in particular a broad spectrum of positive Lyapunov exponents tending to infinite values as
\nu\to 0.
Since current mathematical methods for realistic systems like the Landau-Lifschitz equations (
5
) yield only a positive lower bound for the leading Lyapunov exponent
[

,
47
]
,
it does not seem feasible presently to prove the uniqueness or “universality” of the limit measures whose existence we establish. See
[
48
]
for a numerical RG approach.

Remark 5
.

A comment should be made about the regularity assumptions on our initial data

As discussed earlier,

Remark 6
.

A similar comment is required about the assumed regularity of the force
{\mathbf{f}}
in our theorem.
In fact, for parts (i),(ii) the proofs go through easily assuming only

It seems possible that, with some further

To be clear, a physical forcing should represent stirring only at large scales, so that the force should be extremely

Finally, we make a comment about the dependence of our results on the space dimensionality

2.
Theoretical and Empirical Background

In this section, prior to proving our main theorem, we give some necessary background
on Landau-Lifschitz fluctuating hydrodynamics, its physical justification, empirical
verification and rigorous status. We shall discuss also the non-dimensionalization
of the Landau-Lifshitz equation with large-scale flow parameters that underlies our theorem
and the empirical basis of the uniform Besov regularity which is its main hypothesis.
Readers who are interested mainly in the mathematical results can skip this section on
a first reading and go directly to section
3
where the proofs are presented.

Fluctuating hydrodynamics was originally proposed on a phenomenological basis by Landau & Lifschitz
[
19
]
, who added a fluctuating stress with statistics governed by the fluctuation-dissipation
relation (
2
) to the fluid equations, as the simplest hydrodynamic model to reproduce
the known Boltzmann-Einstein single-time statistics in local thermodynamic equilibrium.
It was later pointed out in
[
71
]
that the original Landau-Lifschitz
theory reproduced the correct local equilibrium fluctuations only if transport coefficients are
state-independent. This observation triggered efforts to derive fluctuating hydrodynamics
microscopically by formal methods of statistical physics
[
72
]
,
with the conclusion that the correct noise given by the fluctuation-dissipation relation must be multiplicative
[
73
]
. See also

[
74
,
75
]
. We are aware of no mathematically
rigorous derivation of the Landau & Lifschitz equations for nonlinear fluctuating hydrodynamics,
as necessary to describe turbulent flow. The closest result of which we are aware is
a rigorous derivation of a large deviations rate function for a microscopic lattice gas in
a “hydrodynamic scaling limit” at small Knudsen and Mach numbers but for arbitrary
(fixed) Reynolds number
[
54
]
. The large-deviations rate function
obtained in this work corresponds formally to that obtained from the incompressible
Landau-Lifschitz theory in a weak-noise limit
[
76
,
51
]
. Unfortunately,
subsequent work
[
55
,
56
]
has shown
that the assumed “hydrodynamic scaling limit” is physically unrealistic for turbulent
flows and probably never applies anywhere in Nature.
On the other hand, the Landau-Lifschitz theory
predicts non-trivial effects of thermal noise, especially in flow states out of global
thermodynamic equilibrium, which are extensively confirmed by laboratory experiments
for many laminar flows
[
77
]
. Since the main physical
assumption of the Landau-Lifschitz theory is a local equilibrium property, it is reasonable
to expect that fluctuating hydrodynamics applies also to turbulent flows as long as this
fundamental property continues to hold there.

A basic conclusion of physicists’ microscopic derivations
[
72
,
75
]

of nonlinear Landau-Lifschitz equations is that, despite superficial appearances, they are
not

stochastic partial differential equations. Instead, these equations contain a specific high-wavenumber
cutoff
\Lambda
and are designed to describe only the length scales
>\Lambda^{-1}.
In the language
of physics, the Landau-Lifschitz hydrodynamics equations are low-wavenumber “effective field theories”

[
22
,
23
]
. A standard feature of such theories is that the
cutoff
\Lambda
is arbitrary
to a large degree but that the parameters of the model depend upon
\Lambda
or “run” with
\Lambda

according to renormalization-group flow equations, so that the objective predictions of the model
are
\Lambda

(
1

simple Newtonian fluid in the limit of small Mach numbers, as discussed carefully in
[
21
]
.
A renormalization group analysis of Forster, Nelson & Stephen
[

studied
the
\Lambda

in the model (

global thermal equilibrium (no mean motion) with the well-known energy spectrum

They found for

\displaystyle\sup_{\Lambda^{-1}<r<L}\left(\frac{Fk_{B}T}{\rho}\Lambda^{3}\right)^{p/2}\frac{1}{U^{p}}\left(\frac{L}{r}\right)^{\zeta_{p}}

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{Fk_{B}T}{\rho U^{2}}\Lambda^{3}\right)^{p/2}(L\Lambda)^{\zeta_{p}}\,=\,\Theta_{\eta}^{p/2}\left(\frac{\eta}{L}\right)^{\frac{p}{3}-\zeta_{p}}(\eta\Lambda)^{\frac{3p}{2}+\zeta_{p}}.

Notice, because of the rapid decay of
S_{p}(r)
for
\Lambda r<1,
that the supremum
over all
r<L
is in fact achieved for
\Lambda r>1
and this leads to the restriction
of the range in (
25
). We have also defined
\Theta_{\eta}:=F\theta_{\eta}.

In terms of the two constants
C_{p}^{NS}
and
C_{p}^{th}

we immediately obtain from (

)

(26)

C_{2}=\sup_{\Lambda^{-1}<r<L}\frac{S_{2}(r)}{U^{2}\left(r/L\right)^{\zeta_{2}}}\leq C_{2}^{NS}+2C_{2}^{th},

from (

) using the Hölder inequality
S_{2}^{NS}(r)\leq[S_{4}^{NS}(r)]^{1/2}

(27)

C_{4}=\sup_{\Lambda^{-1}<r<L}\frac{S_{4}(r)}{U^{4}\left(r/L\right)^{\zeta_{4}}}\leq C_{4}^{NS}+12(C_{4}^{th})^{1/2}(C_{4}^{NS})^{1/2}+12C_{4}^{th}

from (
22

S_{4}^{NS}(r)\leq[S_{6}^{NS}(r)]^{2/3},

S_{2}^{NS}(r)\leq[S_{6}^{NS}(r)]^{1/3},

(28)

\sup_{\Lambda^{-1}<r<L}\frac{S_{6}(r)}{U^{6}\left(r/L\right)^{\zeta_{6}}}\leq C_{6}^{NS}+30(C_{6}^{th})^{1/3}(C_{6}^{NS})^{2/3}+180(C_{6}^{th})^{2/3}(C_{6}^{NS})^{1/3}+120C_{6}^{th},

and so forth. Clearly, the behavior of

for

is crucial.

To complete the argument we must appeal to some properties of the turbulent
scaling exponents
\zeta_{p},
which are known to be concave in
p
and which coincide with the
K41 line
p/3
at exactly two
p
-values,
p=0
and
p=p_{*}\doteq 3

[
91
]
. Note that, because we deal here with structure
functions of absolute velocity increments, the Kolmogorov 4/5th law does not apply,
so that it is not true that
p_{*}=3
exactly. The important consequence is that

\zeta_{p}<p/3
for
p>p_{*}
and we can thus infer from the bound (
25
) that

\lim_{Re\to\infty}C_{p}^{th}(Re)=0,\qquad p>p_{*},

if one assumes that
\eta\Lambda
is held fixed
. In conjunction with the estimates (
27
) for
C_{4},
(
28
) for
C_{6},
etc. we see that all of these constants remain bounded in the presence of thermal noise and, indeed, coincide for
Re\gg 1
with the Navier-Stokes values
C_{4}^{NS},
C_{6}^{NS},
etc. The Besov regularity of the velocity realizations is thus unchanged from the deterministic case
when the cutoff
\Lambda
is taken to be some fixed multiple of
\eta^{-1}.
In fact, one
can also take
\eta\Lambda
increasing with
Re
at a sufficiently slow rate, since the
only requirement is that

remain bounded as
Re\to\infty.
In that case,
(
25
) implies that the choice

\eta\Lambda\,\propto\left(\frac{L}{\eta}\right)^{\frac{\frac{p}{3}-\zeta_{p}}{\frac{3p}{2}+\zeta_{p}}}\sim Re^{\frac{p-3\zeta_{p}}{6p+4\zeta_{p}}}

would suffice to guarantee that

remain bounded as
Re\to\infty,
although with a
different limiting value of the constant than for the deterministic case. With either choice
of
\Lambda
one can conclude that

\sup_{\nu>o}{\mathbb{E}}^{\nu}_{*}\left[\|{\bf u}\|^{p}_{L^{p}([0,T],B^{\sigma_{p}-\epsilon}_{p,\infty})}\right]<\infty,\qquad p=4,6,8,...

at least for even
p
that are not too large.

On the other hand, for
p<p_{*}
instead
\zeta_{p}>p/3,

and thus
\lim_{Re\to\infty}C_{p}^{th}(Re)=\infty!
In that case, one can instead
replace
\zeta_{p}
with

to obtain

\widetilde{C}_{p}^{th}(Re):=\sup_{\Lambda^{-1}<r<L}\left(\frac{Fk_{B}T}{\rho}\Lambda^{3}\right)^{p/2}\frac{1}{U^{p}}\left(\frac{L}{r}\right)^{p/3}=\Theta_{\eta}^{p/2}(\eta\Lambda)^{\frac{11p}{6}}<\infty,

whereas also
\widetilde{C}_{p}^{NS}=\sup_{r<L}\frac{S^{NS}_{p}(r)}{U^{p}\left(r/L\right)^{p/3}}<\infty.

Thus, choosing the cutoff
\Lambda
once again to be some fixed multiple of
\eta^{-1},
one can obtain
at least K41 regularity of the velocity field for
p<p_{*}.
In particular, using (

) for
p=2

it is justified by the empirical observations to assume that

\sup_{\nu>o}{\mathbb{E}}^{\nu}_{*}\left[\|{\bf u}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T],B^{\frac{1}{3}-\epsilon}_{2,\infty})}\right]<\infty.

3.
Proof of the Main Theorem

We follow the general strategy of
[
28
]
, Chapter IV, who proved the existence of space-time statistical
solutions of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, thereby obtaining a probabilistic version of Leray’s construction.
We give the proofs of parts (i)-(iv) of our main theorem in successive subsections. We use frequently
the convenient notation

with the meaning that

for some constant

depending possibly upon parameters that are fixed.

3.1.
Proof of (i):

Our proof of tightness is modeled after Theorem IV.4.3 of
[
28
]
and is likewise based upon their following general result:

Lemma II.3.1 of
[
28
]
:
Let
\Omega_{1},

\Omega_{2}
be Banach spaces, where
\Omega_{2}
is
separable and
\Omega_{1}
is compactly embedded in
\Omega_{2}.
The family
\mathscr{M}
of probability measures defined
on
{\mathcal{B}}(\Omega_{2})
and with support on
\Omega_{1}
is tight on
{\mathcal{B}}(\Omega_{2})
if for any

\mu\in\mathscr{M},
the functional

is
\mu

(30)

\sup_{\mu\in\mathscr{M}}\int\|u\|_{\Omega_{1}}d\mu(u)<\infty

We apply this proposition with
\Omega_{2}=\mathcal{Z}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p}
and with Banach space
\Omega_{1}=\mathcal{E}^{\sigma,\beta,\gamma}_{r,p}
defined for

0<\gamma<1
by

\mathcal{E}^{\sigma,\beta,\gamma}_{r,p}:=L^{r}([0,T],B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap C^{0,\gamma}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

where

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{E}^{\sigma,\beta,\gamma}_{r,p}}:=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{r}([0,T],B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}+\lVert\cdot\rVert_{C^{0,\gamma}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}.

First, we note that
\mathcal{Z}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p}\subseteq L^{r}([0,T],L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),
and the latter is a separable Banach space. By Proposition 3.25 of
[
92
]
, we conclude
\mathcal{Z}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p}=L^{r}([0,T],L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap C^{0}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

is separable.

Next we argue that

\mathcal{E}^{\sigma,\beta,\gamma}_{r,p}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Z}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p}

is a compact embedding for
0<\gamma<\min((r-2)/r,1/2).
We use the following version of the Aubin-Lions-Simons compactness result:

Theorem IV.4.1 of
[
28
]
:
Let
E_{0},

E,

E_{1}
be Banach spaces, such that
E
is
continuously embedded in
E_{1}
and
E_{0}
is compactly embedded in
E.
Let
1<q<\infty
and
M
be a bounded set
in
L^{q}([0,T],E_{0})
consisting of functions equicontinuous in
C^{0}([0,T],E_{1}).
Then
M
is relatively compact
in
L^{q}([0,T],E)\cap C^{0}([0,T],E_{1}).

We note that the original statement of this result assumed that all of the Banach spaces
E_{0},

E,

E_{1}
are reflexive. However, we have checked
that the proof of Theorem IV.4.1in
[
28
]
used reflexivity only in their Lemma IV.4.1 and this result is now well-known
not to require reflexivity. For example, see Lemma II.5.15 in section 5.3 of
[
93
]
. We thus can apply the above proposition with
q=r
and

E_{0}=B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad E=L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\quad E_{1}=B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

even though
B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
are not reflexive. The hypotheses
of Theorem IV.4.1 in
[
28
]
are verified in the following lemmas.

First the compact embedding of
E_{0}
into
E
is given by

Lemma 1
.

For any
1\leq p<\infty
,
0<\sigma<1
, and
d\in\mathbb{N}
the embedding
B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is compact.

Proof.
Following the Kolmogorov–Riesz–Frechét theorem (Theorem 4.26 of
[
92
]
) it suffices to argue that for any
bounded subset
\Omega\subseteq B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

\lim_{|\mathbf{h}|\to 0}\sup_{{\mathbf{v}}\in\Omega}\left\lVert{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot+\mathbf{h})-{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=0.

However, by definition of
B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

\sup_{{\mathbf{v}}\in\Omega}\left\lVert{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot+\mathbf{h})-{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq|\mathbf{h}|^{\sigma}\sup_{{\mathbf{v}}\in\Omega}\lVert{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\to 0\ \mbox{ as }\ h\to 0.\qed

Next, the continuous embedding of
E
into
E_{1}
follows from

Lemma 2
.

For any
1\leq p^{\prime}\leq p<\infty
,
0<\sigma<1
,
\beta\geq 2
and
d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
the embedding
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow B_{p^{\prime},\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is continuous.

Proof.
From the famous Littlewood–Paley theorem (Remark 2 of 3.5.4 in
[
94
]
), we have equivalence of
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

with
F_{p,2}^{0}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),
the Triebel–Lizorkin space. Then we have a chain of continuous embeddings (Remark 4 of 3.5.1 in
[
94
]
)

F_{p,2}^{0}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow B_{p^{\prime},2}^{0}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow B_{p^{\prime},2}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow B_{p^{\prime},\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).\hfill\qed

Finally, boundedness in
C^{0,\gamma}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
immediately implies equicontinuity from
[0,T]
into
B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Thus, any bounded subset of
\mathcal{E}^{\sigma,\beta,\gamma}_{r,p}
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{Z}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p}

by Theorem IV.4.1 of
[
28
]
, so that the embedding of the former into the latter is compact.

Finally, to obtain tightness from Lemma II.3.1 of
[
28
]
, we need the basic bound (
30
), or

(31)

\sup_{\nu>0}\int\left\lVert{\mathbf{u}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{E}^{\sigma,\beta,\gamma}_{r,p}}\,\mathrm{d}P^{\nu}_{\mu}({\mathbf{u}})<\infty.

Such a uniform estimate with the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{r}([0,T],B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
follows immediately from our main physical
hypothesis (
10
). We spend the remainder of this subsection deriving the similar bound for the norm

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{C^{0,\gamma}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}
of the other component space in the definition of
\mathcal{E}^{\sigma,\beta,\gamma}_{r,p}.
Toward this end, we define the quantity
{\mathbf{z}}^{\nu}:={\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}-\nu^{\kappa}\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot\pi_{\Lambda}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, in terms of which we can decompose the
solutions of (

) as

{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}={\mathbf{z}}^{\nu}+\nu^{\kappa}\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot\pi_{\Lambda}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

We obtain the required uniform estimate first for the term
{\mathbf{z}}^{\nu}
and then for the noise term
\nu^{\kappa}\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot\pi_{\Lambda}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

Estimate for
{\mathbf{z}}^{\nu}
:
It follows directly from the definition of
{\mathbf{z}}^{\nu}
that it satisfies the equation

(32)

\partial_{t}{\mathbf{z}}^{\nu}=\mathscr{P}\pi_{\Lambda}\left[-{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}\otimes{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu})+{\mathbf{f}}\right]+\nu\triangle\pi_{\Lambda}{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}.

We shall use the integral form of this equation

(33)

{\mathbf{z}}^{\nu}(\cdot,t)={\mathbf{z}}^{\nu}(\cdot,0)+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t}{\mathbf{z}}^{\nu}(\cdot,s)\,\mathrm{d}s={\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}(\cdot,0)+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t}{\mathbf{z}}^{\nu}(\cdot,s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

to obtain our estimates. We proceed by a series of lemmas to control each term in (
32
).

Lemma 3
.

Take
2\leq s\leq\infty
,
2\leq p<\infty
,
0<\sigma<1
,
\beta\geq 1
, and
{\mathbf{v}}\in L^{s}([0,T],B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. Let

\forall t\in[0,T]:\hskip 10.84006pt\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}{\mathbf{v}}({\mathbf{x}},t)\,\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}=0.

Then
\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}})\in L^{r}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for any
1\leq r\leq s/2
when
s<\infty
, and
\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}})\in L^{r}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for any
1\leq r\leq\infty
when
s=\infty
.

Proof.
To control the Leray projection
\mathscr{P},
we use the fact that it is given by a matrix-valued Fourier multiplier
\mathscr{P}_{ij}({\mathbf{k}})=\delta_{ij}-k_{i}k_{j}/|{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}
which is homogeneous of degree 0. We employ the following specific estimate from Theorem 3.6.3 of
[
94
]
: for
\mathscr{K}>d\left(\frac{1}{\min(p,1)}-\frac{1}{2}\right)

(34)

\left\|\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}m({\mathbf{k}})({\mathcal{F}}f)({\mathbf{k}})\right\|_{B_{p,q}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq c\lVert m\rVert_{\mathfrak{h}^{\mathscr{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|f\|_{B_{p,q}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},

where
c
is a constant and Fourier multipliers
m
, considered as functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d},
are estimated by the norm

\lVert m\rVert_{\mathfrak{h}^{\mathscr{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\lVert\psi^{\ast}m\rVert_{H^{\mathscr{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\lVert\psi(\cdot)m(2^{n}\cdot)\rVert_{H^{\mathscr{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

and where the smooth test functions
\psi^{\ast},\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the properties

\forall{\mathbf{k}}:0\leq\psi^{\ast}({\mathbf{k}})\leq 1,\hskip 10.84006pt\operatorname{supp}\psi^{\ast}\subseteq\{{\mathbf{k}}:|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq 4\},\hskip 10.84006pt\psi^{\ast}({\mathbf{k}})=1\,\textrm{ when }\,|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq 2,

\forall{\mathbf{k}}:0\leq\psi({\mathbf{k}})\leq 1,\hskip 10.84006pt\operatorname{supp}\psi\subseteq\{{\mathbf{k}}:1/4\leq|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq 4\},\hskip 10.84006pt\psi({\mathbf{k}})=1\,\textrm{ when }\,1/2\leq|{\mathbf{k}}|\leq 2.

provide a spectral decomposition.

In order to apply the above bound to the Leray projection, we must modify its multiplier to eliminate the singularity at
|{\mathbf{k}}|=0,
by taking

(35)

(\mathcal{F}\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}{\mathbf{v}})_{i}(\mathbf{k}):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{ij}({\mathbf{k}})(\mathcal{F}{\mathbf{v}})_{j}(\mathbf{k}),\quad{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

with modified matrix multiplier given by

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{ij}({\mathbf{k}}):=\begin{cases}\delta_{ij}-k_{i}k_{j}/|{\mathbf{k}}|^{2},\hskip 10.84006pt|\mathbf{k}|\geq 1/4\\
(\delta_{ij}-k_{i}k_{j}/|\mathbf{k}|^{2})[1-\exp(-|{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}/(1/16-|\mathbf{k}|^{2}))],\hskip 10.84006pt0<|\mathbf{k}|<1/4\\
0,\hskip 21.68121pt\mathbf{k}=0\end{cases}.

which is now
C^{\infty}
and coincident with the original multiplier
\mathscr{P}_{ij}({\mathbf{k}})
for
|{\mathbf{k}}|\geq 1/4.
The only argument

{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
in the expression (
35
) affected by the modification is thus
{\mathbf{k}}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},
so that
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}

and
\mathscr{P}
are identical in their action on fields
{\mathbf{v}}
with zero space-mean. Hence, we can identify
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}=\mathscr{P}
.
Now take
\mathscr{K}>d/2.
Using that
\psi^{\ast},\psi
are smooth and vanish outside a compact set, and that
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{ij}(2^{n}{\mathbf{k}})=\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{ij}({\mathbf{k}})
for
|{\mathbf{k}}|\geq 1/4
, we conclude
\psi^{\ast}\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{ij},\psi(\cdot)\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{ij}(2^{n}\cdot)

and their derivatives are uniformly bounded in
n
and compactly supported. Thus
\lVert\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}\rVert_{\mathfrak{h}^{\mathscr{K}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty.

By the estimate (
34
) we thus obtain with
{\mathbf{v}}
denoting
{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)
for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
that

\lVert\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}})\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\lVert{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}})\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Per Theorem 2.2 and Proposition 2.3 of
[
95
]
, we have the estimate for
\beta\geq 1

\lVert{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}})\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\lVert{\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Since all finite-dimensional norms are equivalent, we use the natural inner product structure of tensored Hilbert space to write:

\left|{\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}}\right|\lesssim\left|\langle{\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{3}}\right|^{1/2}=\left|\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}}\rangle\right|=|{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}\hskip 10.84006pt\implies\hskip 10.84006pt\lVert{\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{L^{p/2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\lVert{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.

For any

we have

\displaystyle\lVert{\mathbf{v}}_{i}(\cdot+\mathbf{h}){\mathbf{v}}_{j}(\cdot+\mathbf{h})-{\mathbf{v}}_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\rVert_{L^{p/2}(\mathbb{T})^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert{\mathbf{v}}_{i}(\cdot+\mathbf{h})-{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})^{d}}\lVert{\mathbf{v}}_{j}(\cdot+\mathbf{h})\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})^{d}}

\displaystyle\hskip 21.68121pt+\lVert{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})^{d}}\lVert{\mathbf{v}}_{j}(\cdot+\mathbf{h})-{\mathbf{v}}_{j}\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T})^{d}}.

All of the RHS terms are bounded by constants times
|\mathbf{h}|^{\sigma},
so that

\lVert{\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\lVert{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.

For
s<\infty
, we use linearity and monotonicity of the Lebesgue integration over time, and then
apply Hölder’s inequality

\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}})(\cdot,t)\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{r}\,\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\lVert{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\rVert_{B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2r}\,\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\rVert_{B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{s}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2r/s},\quad 1\leq r\leq s/2.

Thus

\left\lVert\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}})\right\rVert_{L^{r}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}\lesssim\left\lVert{\mathbf{v}}\right\rVert_{L^{s}([0,T],B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}^{2}.

For
s=\infty
we have

\underset{t\in[0,T]}{\operatorname{ess\,sup}}\,\left\lVert\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot\left[{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\otimes{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\right]\right\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\underset{t\in[0,T]}{\operatorname{ess\,sup}}\,\lVert{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\rVert^{2}_{B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},

which gives for
1\leq r\leq\infty

\left\lVert\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}})\right\rVert_{L^{r}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}\lesssim\left\lVert\mathscr{P}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{v}}\otimes{\mathbf{v}})\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}\lesssim\left\lVert{\mathbf{v}}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{\infty}([0,T],B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}.\qed

Lemma 4
.

Let
1\leq r\leq s\leq\infty
,
2\leq p<\infty
,
0<\sigma<1
,
\beta\geq 2
, and
{\mathbf{v}}\in L^{s}([0,T],B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. Then
\triangle{\mathbf{v}}\in L^{r}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
.

Proof.
Again from Theorem 2.2 and Proposition 2.3 of
[
95
]
, we have

\lVert\triangle{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\lVert{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lesssim\lVert{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\rVert_{B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},

using for the last step the continuous embedding
B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. For
s<\infty
, we again apply the properties of the Lebesgue integral and Hölder inequality

\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\triangle{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{r}\,\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\int_{0}^{T}\lVert{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\rVert_{B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{r}\,\mathrm{d}t\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert{\mathbf{v}}(\cdot,t)\rVert_{B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{s}\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{r/s}.

Thus

\left\lVert\triangle{\mathbf{v}}\right\rVert_{L^{r}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}\lesssim\left\lVert{\mathbf{v}}\right\rVert_{L^{s}([0,T],B_{p,\infty}^{\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}.

Similarly for
s=\infty
we have

K_{t}^{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{u}},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}):=\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\mathscr{P}[{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{u}}\otimes{\mathbf{u}})-{\mathbf{f}}](\cdot,s),{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s

is in fact absolutely continuous in time
t.
Thus, integration by parts is justified and one obtains

(46)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}K_{t}^{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{u}},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})\psi^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}K_{t}^{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{u}},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\mathscr{P}[{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{u}}\otimes{\mathbf{u}})-{\mathbf{f}}](\cdot,t),{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\rangle\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

We thus obtain from the equation (
45
) the condition

(48)

\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,t),{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\rangle\psi^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\mathscr{P}[{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{u}}\otimes{\mathbf{u}})-{\mathbf{f}}](\cdot,t),{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\rangle\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=0.

which is essentially the standard weak formulation of the Euler equations. In view of part (iv) of the theorem concerning
initial data, which we prove in the next subsection, it is useful to remark that this same argument may be applied even if

\psi(0)\neq 0.
In that case, one obtains instead

(49)

\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,t),{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\rangle\psi^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\left\langle{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,0),{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\rangle\psi(0)-\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle\mathscr{P}[{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\cdot({\mathbf{u}}\otimes{\mathbf{u}})-{\mathbf{f}}](\cdot,t),{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\right\rangle\psi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=0.

This is a standard approach to incorporate initial data in the weak formulation of a PDE.

Finally, we note that a weak formulation of the incompressibility condition follows directly from (
39
).
If we take
{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}={\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\chi
for a scalar function
\chi\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
, then substituting into (
39
)
it follows that all of the time-integral terms vanish in the equation (
38
) defining
E_{t}^{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{u}},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})
, because of the
Leray projection
\mathscr{P}.
The resulting equation is

\left\langle{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,t),{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\chi\right\rangle=\left\langle{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,0),{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\chi\right\rangle,\quad\forall t\in[0,T],\forall\chi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

Recall that initial data for the Landau-Lifschitz equation (
5
) are chosen from the measure
\mu
such that

\left\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{0},{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\chi\right\rangle=0,\quad\forall\chi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\qquad\mu-{a.e.}\ {\mathbf{u}}_{0}

Thus, we infer the weak incompressibility condition

\left\langle{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,t),{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}\chi\right\rangle=0,\quad\forall t\in[0,T],\forall\chi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\qquad P_{\mu}-{a.e.}\ {\mathbf{u}}

if it can be shown that the distribution of
{\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,0)
induced by the measure
P_{\mu}
on
{\mathbf{u}}
coincides
with measure
\mu.
This is precisely the result established in the next section.

3.4.
Proof of (iv)

: Our proof is modeled on Theorem IV.5.3 of
[
28
]
. We have already
observed that the trace
\gamma_{0}{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{u}}(\cdot,0)
is well-defined as a map

\gamma_{0}:C^{0}([0,T],B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\to B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

and is strongly continuous on
\mathcal{Z}^{\sigma,\beta}_{r,p}.
Thus, the pushforward measure
P^{(0)}_{\mu}:=\gamma_{0\ast}P_{\mu}
is well-defined on
\mathcal{B}(B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta})
. Our strategy to show
the equivalence of
P^{(0)}_{\mu}
and
\mu
is based on the study of their characteristic functions:

S_{\mu}^{(0)}[{\mathbf{v}}]=\int\exp\left(i\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{0},\mathbf{v}\rangle\right)\,\mathrm{d}P_{\nu}^{(0)}({\mathbf{u}}_{0}),\quad S_{\mu}[{\mathbf{v}}]=\int\exp\left(i\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{0},\mathbf{v}\rangle\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu({\mathbf{u}}_{0})

defined here for
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).
We note first that one can define similarly

P^{\nu,(0)}_{\mu}:=\gamma_{0\ast}P_{\mu}^{\nu}
for
\nu>0
and its
characteristic function satisfies

S_{\mu}^{\nu,(0)}[{\mathbf{v}}]:=\int\exp\left(i\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{0},\mathbf{v}\rangle\right)\,\mathrm{d}P_{\mu}^{\nu,(0)}({\mathbf{u}}_{0})=\int\exp\left(i\langle\gamma_{0}{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{v}\rangle\right)\,\mathrm{d}P_{\mu}^{\nu}({\mathbf{u}})

Because
\exp\left(i\langle\gamma_{0}{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{v}\rangle\right)
is a bounded, continuous function, it follows
from weak convergence that

\lim_{\nu\to 0}S_{\mu}^{\nu,(0)}[{\mathbf{v}}]=\int\exp\left(i\langle\gamma_{0}{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{v}\rangle\right)\,\mathrm{d}P_{\mu}({\mathbf{u}})=\int\exp\left(i\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{0},\mathbf{v}\rangle\right)\,\mathrm{d}P^{(0)}_{\mu}({\mathbf{u}}_{0})=S_{\mu}^{(0)}[{\mathbf{v}}].

On the other hand, we have also by the definition
{\mathbf{u}}^{\nu}_{0}:=\pi_{\Lambda}{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
that

S_{\mu}^{\nu,(0)}[{\mathbf{v}}]=\int\exp\left(i\langle\pi_{\Lambda}{\mathbf{u}}_{0},\mathbf{v}\rangle\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu({\mathbf{u}}_{0}).

Since
\pi_{\Lambda}{\mathbf{u}}_{0}\to{\mathbf{u}}_{0}
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and thus almost sure in
\mu
,
dominated convergence gives also that

\lim_{\nu\to 0}S_{\mu}^{\nu,(0)}[{\mathbf{v}}]=\int\exp\left(i\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{0},{\mathbf{v}}\rangle\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu({\mathbf{u}}_{0})=S_{\mu}[{\mathbf{v}}].

Comparing the two limit expressions, we conclude that

(50)

S_{\mu}^{(0)}[{\mathbf{v}}]=S_{\mu}[{\mathbf{v}}].

We would like to infer from this equality that
P^{(0)}_{\mu}=\mu
by applying the Bochner–Minlos theorem
[
99
]
.
However, this theorem applies to measures on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),
whereas
P^{(0)}_{\mu}
is defined
on
\mathcal{B}(B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta})
and
\mu
on
\mathcal{B}(L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})).
To resolve this difficulty we must
extend both of these measures to
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),
which is the content of the following:

Lemma 9
.

(i) The set
B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
belongs to
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

and the measure
P^{(0)}_{\mu}
has a unique extension
\overline{P}^{(0)}_{\mu}
to
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

that is supported on
B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),
defined by

(51)

\overline{P}^{(0)}_{\mu}(E)=P^{(0)}_{\mu}(E\cap B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad\forall E\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

(ii) The set
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
belongs to
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and the measure
\mu
has a unique
extension
\overline{\mu}
to
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
that is supported on
L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),
defined by

(52)

\overline{\mu}(E)=\mu(E\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad\forall E\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d})).

Proof.
The argument is an elaboration of Remark II.2.1 in
[
28
]
. (i) We prove Borel measurability of
B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

Note first that the extended real-valued function defined on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
by

{\mathbf{u}}\mapsto\lVert{\mathbf{u}}\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

is lower semicontinuous in the Fréchet topology of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. To see this,
take
{\mathbf{u}}_{n}\to{\mathbf{u}}
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and note

\forall{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):|\langle{\mathbf{u}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rangle|\leq\lVert{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rVert_{B_{2/(2-p),1}^{\beta-\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{n}\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Taking the limit infimum as
n\to\infty
of both sides of this inequality gives

\forall{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):|\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rangle|\leq\lVert{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rVert_{B_{2/(2-p),1}^{\beta-\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{n}\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

Then, using the dense embedding
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow B_{2/(2-p),1}^{\beta-\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),

\lVert{\mathbf{u}}\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\equiv\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\,\lVert{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rVert_{B_{2/(2-p),1}^{\beta-\sigma}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=1\}}|\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\rangle|\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\lVert{\mathbf{u}}_{n}\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})},

which proves lower semicontinuity. As a consequence, the sets

M^{\sigma,\beta}_{p}(n):=\left\{{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):\lVert{\mathbf{u}}\rVert_{B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq n\right\}

are closed in the Fréchet topology of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and then

B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\equiv\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}M^{\sigma,\beta}_{p}(n)

is measurable in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

F^{\sigma}
-set in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

A measure
\overline{P}^{(0)}_{\mu}

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

\overline{P}^{(0)}_{\mu}(E)=P^{(0)}_{\mu}(E\cap B_{p/2,\infty}^{\sigma-\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),\quad\forall E\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}^{d})),
