Coronary artery disease polygenic risk score and recurrent cardiovascular events after coronary artery revascularization: A UK Biobank analysis
Authors: Aman Goyal, Zhuqing Shi, Manan Pareek, Humza Saeed, Sumeet A. Khetarpal, Mark J. Ricciardi, Grant W. Reed, Iftikhar J. Kullo, Samir Kapadia, Krishna G. Aragam, Martha Gulati, Jianfeng Xu, Arman Qamar
Published: 2027

Brief communication

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. It is highly heritable, and genome-wide association studies have identified numerous risk loci that underpin polygenic risk scores (PRS), which aggregate the effects of multiple alleles to quantify genetic susceptibility. When combined with conventional clinical factors, PRS may improve CAD risk prediction beyond traditional risk factors [1]. Advances in coronary revascularization, including percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), have improved outcomes. However, patients with severe CAD remain vulnerable to recurrent events, including graft failure, restenosis, stent thrombosis, recurrent myocardial infarction, and repeat revascularization. Although previous studies have examined CAD PRS in secondary prevention [2,3], its association with recurrent events after index coronary revascularization remains less well defined. We therefore evaluated residual genetic risk among patients with established severe CAD undergoing index coronary revascularization, focusing on repeat procedures and cardiovascular mortality rather than broader measures of CAD recurrence.

We conducted a retrospective analysis of UK Biobank participants, with health outcomes ascertained through linkage to National Health Service records [4]. From March 2006 through June 2023, we identified participants who underwent index coronary revascularization, defined as the earliest recorded PCI and/or CABG using self-reported procedures and OPCS-4 codes. CAD genetic risk was estimated using the published multi-ancestry polygenic score PGS003725 [5] and categorized as high (≥80th percentile), intermediate (21st to 80th percentile), or low (≤20th percentile). Outcomes were cardiovascular mortality, repeat coronary artery revascularization, non-fatal ischemic stroke, and all-cause mortality. Fine-Gray competing risk models, with death treated as a competing event, were used for all outcomes except all-cause mortality, which was analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression. Models were adjusted for age at index revascularization, sex, and genetic background (top 10 principal components). Subgroup analyses by procedure type (PCI or CABG) and sensitivity analyses adjusting for baseline comorbidities and stratifying by sex were also performed. The incremental predictive value of CAD PRS was assessed by comparing a reference model including age, sex, the top 10 genetic principal components, triglycerides, lipoprotein(a), hypertension, diabetes, directly measured low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, hyperlipidemia, and obesity with the same model plus CAD PRS. Discrimination was assessed using the C statistic and reclassification using continuous net reclassification improvement (cNRI). Analyses used R version 4.2.2, with two-sided p < 0.05 considered significant.

Of 501,092 UK Biobank participants, 12,288 (2.45%) underwent index coronary artery revascularization after recruitment (73.5% PCI, 18.5% CABG, and 8.0% both), of whom 11,932 had CAD PRS data, and were included. A total of 2387 participants (20.0%) were classified as low risk, 7158 (60.0%) as intermediate risk, and 2387 (20.0%) as high risk. The mean age at revascularization was 66.9 years, and high-risk participants presented at a younger age (p < 0.001) with a higher proportion of females (p < 0.001). High-risk participants exhibited more adverse metabolic profiles, including higher triglyceride and lipoprotein(a) levels and a greater prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia (all p < 0.001). Compared with the low-risk group, the high-risk group had a significantly greater risk of cardiovascular mortality (aHR 1.33; 95% CI: 1.09–1.63; p = 0.01), with no significant difference between the intermediate- and low-risk groups (aHR 0.89; 95% CI: 0.75–1.06; p = 0.19). The risk of repeat coronary artery revascularization increased in a stepwise manner, being higher in the intermediate-risk group (aHR 1.31; 95% CI: 1.09–1.59; p < 0.001) and highest in the high-risk group (aHR 1.72; 95% CI: 1.39–2.14; p < 0.001). Differences in non-fatal ischemic stroke (aHR 1.33; 95% CI: 0.95–1.87; p = 0.10) and all-cause mortality (aHR 1.09; 95% CI: 0.93–1.28; p = 0.28) between the high- and low-risk groups did not reach statistical significance. These associations were consistent among participants undergoing PCI, in whom high-risk PRS was associated with cardiovascular mortality (aHR 1.34; 95% CI: 1.03–1.75; p = 0.03) and repeat revascularization (aHR 1.77; 95% CI: 1.24–2.53; p < 0.001), with similar trends in the CABG subgroup (Fig. 1). After additional adjustment for baseline comorbidities, only repeat revascularization remained significantly elevated in the high-risk group (aHR 1.75; 95% CI: 1.36–2.26; p < 0.001). In sex-stratified analyses, males demonstrated a higher risk of cardiovascular mortality (aHR 1.38; 95% CI: 1.10–1.73; p = 0.01), whereas females demonstrated a higher risk of repeat revascularization (aHR 1.92; 95% CI: 1.19–3.10; p = 0.01). Adding CAD PRS to the reference model modestly improved discrimination for repeat coronary artery revascularization, increasing the C statistic from 0.611 (95% CI: 0.586–0.635) to 0.623 (95% CI: 0.599–0.647; p = 0.048), and improved reclassification (cNRI 0.17; 95% CI: 0.092–0.247; p < 0.001). To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate cardiovascular outcomes after coronary artery revascularization using a CAD PRS. Prior studies examined CAD PRS among patients undergoing coronary angiography or with more broadly defined established CAD [2,3]. Supriami et al. found that high CAD PRS was associated with subsequent revascularization and in-stent restenosis after coronary angiography [2]. Our findings extend this evidence to a larger, clinically homogeneous cohort with CAD severe enough to require revascularization.

The association between high CAD PRS and repeat revascularization persisted after adjustment for cardiometabolic risk factors. Adding CAD PRS to the clinical model also modestly improved discrimination and significantly improved reclassification for repeat revascularization. These findings suggest that CAD PRS may capture residual atherosclerotic risk related to disease progression, restenosis, or disease in untreated coronary segments. The underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation. The attenuation of the association with cardiovascular mortality after cardiometabolic adjustment suggests that genetic risk may influence mortality partly through conventional risk factors rather than providing substantial independent prognostic information.

Although cNRI improved for repeat revascularization, the absolute increase in the C statistic was modest. Thus, CAD PRS may identify residual atherosclerotic risk but does not yet have sufficient predictive utility for routine clinical implementation. It may eventually complement clinical factors in secondary prevention trial enrichment or in identifying patients for intensive risk-factor management or closer surveillance. However, this study does not establish that PRS-guided surveillance, testing, or treatment improves outcomes. External validation, calibration assessment, and prospective evaluation are required before clinical adoption.

This study has several limitations. Its observational design precludes causal inference and permits residual confounding. Restriction to patients undergoing revascularization may introduce selection and index event biases because the cohort included only patients who survived, were referred for, and underwent a procedure. Detailed data on coronary anatomy, lesion complexity, completeness of revascularization, stent or graft characteristics, medical therapy, medication adherence, cardiac rehabilitation, and achievement of lipid, blood pressure, and glycemic targets were unavailable or incompletely captured. Unmeasured and time-varying confounding may therefore remain despite adjustment for measured cardiometabolic factors. Finally, the smaller CABG subgroup limited statistical power, and the predominance of European ancestry limits generalizability.

Ethical considerations

No ethical approval was required for this study design, as all data were obtained from publicly available, de-identified, and anonymized sources.
