Multi-Property Synthesis
Christoph Weinhuber, Yannik Schnitzer, Alessandro Abate, David Parker, Giuseppe De Giacomo, Moshe Y. Vardi

Abstract
We study LTL f \text{LTL}_{f} synthesis with multiple properties, where satisfying all properties may be impossible. Instead of enumerating subsets of properties, we compute in one fixed-point computation the relation between product-game states and the goal sets that are realizable from them, and we synthesize strategies achieving maximal realizable sets. We develop a fully symbolic algorithm that introduces Boolean goal variables and exploits monotonicity to represent exponentially many goal combinations compactly. Our approach substantially outperforms enumeration-based baselines, with speedups of up to two orders of magnitude.

Introduction

The problem of synthesis from temporal specifications lies at the core of many decision-making problems in artificial intelligence, most prominently automated planning and controller synthesis. In this setting, we automatically construct an agent strategy that guarantees the satisfaction of a given temporal specification against all admissible behaviours of the environment. Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) has long served as the standard formalism for expressing temporally extended goals and assumptions in infinite-horizon settings, particularly in reactive synthesis
18
.

In contrast, many real-world AI tasks are inherently
finite-horizon
: plans are meant to terminate, tasks have a clear notion of completion, and success is achieved once a desired condition is reached. The finite-trace variant of LTL, denoted
\text{LTL}_{f}
, has emerged as a natural and expressive formalism for specifying goals in such domains. Over the past years,
\text{LTL}_{f}
synthesis has matured into a standard tool for generating correct-by-construction behaviours for finite-horizon tasks
9
;
10
. Typical application areas include classical and contingent planning
8
;
6
, service composition
16
, and business process modelling
17
;
7
, where the objective is not to react indefinitely, but to reach a satisfactory outcome and stop.

While existing
\text{LTL}_{f}
synthesis techniques are well-suited for reasoning about single, monolithic specifications, they are largely constrained to an
all-or-nothing
paradigm: the procedure either successfully synthesises a strategy that satisfies the entire specification, or yields no strategy at all, declaring the problem unsolvable
13
.

In many realistic scenarios, however, such as robotics, multi-service orchestration, or planning under resource constraints
25
;
3
, agents face
over-subscription

20
. That is, the specification naturally decomposes into multiple properties (or goals), but satisfying all of them simultaneously may be impossible due to conflicting requirements, limited resources, or adversarial environmental choices.

In such settings, the all-or-nothing paradigm is overly restrictive, as failure is rarely binary. Even when the conjunction of all goals is unrealisable, an agent may still be able to guarantee the satisfaction of some meaningful subset of them.
We study the fundamental question:
If not all goals can be achieved simultaneously, which subsets can, and which of them are most desirable?
Answering this question is crucial for enabling informed trade-offs, and principled decision-making in over-subscribed domains.

A straightforward way to address this question is to enumerate all subsets of goals and, for each subset, solve the synthesis problem for the conjunction of its elements
1
. This, however, can become infeasible even for a moderate number of properties, as it requires solving an exponential number of synthesis instances.

We propose a multi-property synthesis technique that reasons about all goal combinations simultaneously. Our approach computes, via a
single
fixed-point computation, the full relation between states and the subsets of goals that can be simultaneously achieved from them. Rather than checking realisability one subset at a time, we compute, in one pass, which combinations of goals can be guaranteed against all admissible environment behaviours.

Crucially, our method is not limited to deciding which subsets of goals
can
be simultaneously achieved; it also synthesises a strategy that achieves them.
The agent interacts with the environment until it reaches a state where the most desirable set of realisable goals has been achieved, at which point it can stop.
Strategies are constructed not to react indefinitely, but to ensure progress toward satisfying the desired goals and to halt when this objective has been met.

Beyond the fixed-point characterisation, we develop a fully symbolic technique to the multi-property computation.
We leverage Boolean synthesis
15
to perform the fixed-point computation in a compact symbolic representation, which is often dramatically more efficient than explicit constructions
28
.
Notice that, although the problem domain spans all subsets of goals, it is structured by monotonicity – achieving a set of goals implies achieving any subset of them.
Our symbolic encoding is designed to exploit this structure directly, allowing a single Boolean representation to compactly characterize exponentially many goal combinations without explicitly enumerating them.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:

1.

We introduce a technique for multi-property
\text{LTL}_{f}
synthesis, capturing the realizability of arbitrary subsets of goals in over-subscribed domains.

2.

We present a fixed-point characterization that computes, in a single construction, all maximal realizable goal sets and the strategies that achieve them.

3.

We develop a symbolic synthesis algorithm that captures the exponentially-many goal combinations in a single compact Boolean form.

4.

We empirically demonstrate that our approach significantly outperforms current state-of-the-art enumeration-based approaches by up to two orders of magnitude.

Related Work

Multi-property Verification.

Analysing multiple properties over a fixed model has also been studied in the verification literature.

5
(
5
) consider checking multiple invariants on the same circuit and show how to reduce repeated work by exploiting shared structure across properties.
Since verification queries are
independent
, one property being true does not come at the expense of another, and the main challenge is amortizing repeated proof effort.
In our synthesis setting, by contrast, properties can
compete
: enforcing one objective may restrict the available behaviors and thereby preclude satisfying another.

In probabilistic verification, multi-objective model checking for Markov decision processes (MDPs) asks whether there exists a strategy that simultaneously meets bounds on the individual probabilities of satisfying multiple properties, and supports trade-off analysis by computing Pareto fronts
14
.
Our multi-property synthesis can be seen as an analogous trade-off analysis in a qualitative, adversarial setting: instead of a continuous Pareto front over probability thresholds, we obtain a
discrete
frontier given by the maximal realizable sets of properties.

Maximum-realizability Synthesis

11
(
11
) study
infinite-trace
LTL synthesis with hard specifications and soft safety requirements, of which potentially not all can be satisfied on a single execution.
They employ a multi-valued quantitative semantics grading how well each soft property is satisfied along infinite executions that meet the hard constraint
12
.
Their technique is optimisation-driven: building on bounded synthesis, they search for a controller that maximises this quantitative score within a bound on controller size
11
.

In our setting, we are given many
\text{LTL}_{f}
goals and compute which subsets are simultaneously realizable, together with strategies fulfill them.
We exploit that
\text{LTL}_{f}
synthesis reduces to DFA reachability games
10
, where satisfaction amounts to reaching accepting states.
This yields a direct fixed-point computation with a local acceptance base case, computing in one construction the realizability relation for all states and goal combinations.

Over-subscription and Planning with Preferences.

Over-subscription and partial-satisfaction planning address settings where limitations prevent fulfilling all goals by utilizing heuristics or utility functions to select valuable subsets
20
;
25
, or by optimizing user-specified preferences
3
;
4
;
21
.
However, these approaches produce a single plan for
non-adversarial
models.
In contrast, we synthesise strategies that fulfill goal sets against all admissible environment behaviours.

Preliminaries

In this section, we review the basics of Linear Temporal Logic over finite traces and the standard reduction of synthesis to DFA reachability games.

3.1
Linear Temporal Logic over finite traces

Linear Temporal Logic over finite traces (
\text{LTL}_{f}
) shares the same syntax as LTL
19
. Given a set of atomic propositions
AP
, the syntax of
\text{LTL}_{f}
formulas is defined as:

\varphi::=\text{true}\mid a\in\textsf{AP}\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi\land\varphi\mid\varphi\mid\textsf{X}\,\varphi\mid\varphi\,\textsf{U}\,\varphi.

A trace is a
finite
sequence
\rho=\rho[0]\rho[1]\cdots\rho[m]
, where each
\rho[i]\in 2^{\textsf{AP}}
specifies which atomic propositions hold at time
i
. We write
|\rho|:=m+1
for the length of
\rho
.
We use the satisfaction relation
\rho,i\models\varphi
to denote that
\varphi
holds at position
i
of
\rho
. The satisfaction relation is defined by:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho,i\models a&\iff a\in\rho[i],\\
\rho,i\models\neg\varphi&\iff\rho,i\not\models\varphi,\\
\rho,i\models\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}&\iff\rho,i\models\varphi_{1}\text{~and~}\rho,i\models\varphi_{2},\\
\rho,i\models\textsf{X}\,\varphi&\iff i+1<|\rho|\text{~and~}\rho,i+1\models\varphi,\\
\rho,i\models\varphi_{1}\,\textsf{U}\,\varphi_{2}&\iff\exists j.~i\leq j<|\rho|\text{~s.t.~}\rho,j\models\varphi_{2}\\
&\qquad~~\text{and~}\forall k.~i\leq k<j,~\rho,k\models\varphi_{1}.\end{array}

A trace
\rho
satisfies
\varphi
, denoted
\rho\models\varphi
, iff
\rho,0\models\varphi
.

3.2
\text{LTL}_{f}
Synthesis and DFA Games

Synthesis for
\text{LTL}_{f}
properties is the process of computing an agent strategy that enforces a temporal specification against all admissible behaviours of its environment.
We partition the atomic propositions
AP
into two disjoint sets:
inputs

\mathcal{X}
controlled by the environment and
outputs

\mathcal{Y}
controlled by the agent.
An execution is a finite trace
\rho
over
2^{\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}}
that is generated as follows: at each time
i
, the agent first chooses a valuation
Y_{i}\in 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
, after which the environment responds with a valuation
X_{i}\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}
.
The resulting combined valuation
(X_{i},Y_{i})
forms the
i
-th position
\rho[i]
of the trace.

An
agent strategy
is a function

g:(2^{\mathcal{X}})^{*}\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}

that maps the (potentially empty) history of observed inputs
X_{0}X_{1}\cdots X_{i-1}
to the next output choice
Y_{i}=g(X_{0}\cdots X_{i-1})
.
The
realizability
problem asks whether there exists such a strategy
g
such that every finite trace consistent with
g
satisfies
\varphi
.
The
synthesis
problem additionally requires constructing a witnessing
winning
strategy. Since
\text{LTL}_{f}
is interpreted over finite traces, a winning strategy must ensure that the play reaches a position
i
for which the trace
\rho=(X_{0}\cup Y_{0})\dots(X_{i}\cup Y_{i})
satisfies
\varphi
and can then terminate.

Definition 1

(
\text{LTL}_{f}
Synthesis)
.

Let
\varphi
be an
\text{LTL}_{f}
formula over atomic propositions
\textsf{AP}=\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}
.
Given an infinite input sequence
\xi=X_{0}X_{1}X_{2}\cdots\in(2^{\mathcal{X}})^{\omega}
and a strategy
g
, the induced finite trace
\rho_{k}(g,\xi)
up to horizon
k
is given as:

(g(\epsilon)\cup X_{0})(g(X_{0})\cup X_{1})\cdots(g(X_{0}\cdots X_{k-1})\cup X_{k}).

We say that
\varphi
is
realizable
if there exists a strategy
g
such that for any
\xi\in(2^{\mathcal{X}})^{\omega}
there is some
k\geq 0
with

\rho_{k}(g,\xi)\models\varphi.

The
synthesis
problem is to construct such a winning strategy.

Notice that, unlike in LTL reactive synthesis over infinite traces, agent and environment do not interact indefinitely: a winning strategy ensures that the interaction generates a finite trace that satisfies
\varphi
and then chooses to stop at that point.

The standard approach to
\text{LTL}_{f}
synthesis is to solve the corresponding
DFA game

9
.
Given an
\text{LTL}_{f}
formula
\varphi
over
\textsf{AP}=\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}
, we can construct a DFA
\mathcal{G}_{\varphi}
over alphabet
2^{\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}}
that accepts exactly the finite traces satisfying
\varphi
.
Synthesis then reduces to solving a reachability game played on
\mathcal{G}_{\varphi}
, also called the
game arena
.

Definition 2

(DFA Game Arena)
.

A DFA game arena is a tuple
\mathcal{G}=(2^{\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}},S,s_{0},\delta,F)
, where
2^{\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}}
is the alphabet of the game,
S
is a finite set of states,
s_{0}\in S
is the initial state,

\delta:S\times 2^{\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}}\to S
is the transition function, and
F\subseteq S
is a set of final states.

A play of the DFA game proceeds in rounds: at round
i
the game is in state
s_{i}\in S
and the agent chooses an output valuation
Y_{i}\in 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
, then the environment chooses an input valuation
X_{i}\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}
, and the game moves to

s_{i+1}:=\delta(s_{i},Y_{i}\cup X_{i})
.
The agent wins a play if it reaches final state belonging to
F
, at which point the play can be considered terminated.

Since
\mathcal{G}_{\varphi}
recognizes exactly the finite traces satisfying
\varphi
, reaching a final state
F
in the automaton is equivalent to having generated a finite prefix that satisfies
\varphi
.
Thus, an agent strategy
g:(2^{\mathcal{X}})^{*}\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
is winning in the DFA game iff, for every infinite input sequence
\xi\in(2^{\mathcal{X}})^{\omega}
, the induced play visits some state in
F
after finitely many rounds.

To characterize from which states the agent can force a win, we use the standard
controllable predecessor
operator
10
:
for a set
\mathcal{E}\subseteq S
, we define

\mathsf{PreC}(\mathcal{E}):=\{s\in S\mid\exists Y\in 2^{\mathcal{Y}}.\forall X\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}.\delta(s,Y\cup X)\in\mathcal{E}\}.

Intuitively,
\mathsf{PreC}(\mathcal{E})
contains the states from which the agent has a one-step move that guarantees reaching
\mathcal{E}
regardless of the environment.
The set of winning states is the least fixed point of repeatedly adding such predecessors, starting from the accepting states:

\mathsf{Win}_{0}:=F\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{Win}_{i+1}:=\mathsf{Win}_{i}\cup\mathsf{PreC}(\mathsf{Win}_{i}).

As
S
is finite, the sequence stabilizes after at most
|S|
iterations in a fixed point
\mathsf{Win}:=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\mathsf{Win}_{i}
, yielding a linear-time computation in the number of game states.
The agent has a winning strategy iff
s_{0}\in\mathsf{Win}
.

Once the winning region
\mathsf{Win}
has been computed, we can extract a concrete winning strategy by choosing, for each winning state
s\in\mathsf{Win}
, an appropriate agent output
Y\in 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Formally, we choose a function
\omega:\mathsf{Win}\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
such that for every
s\in\mathsf{Win}_{i}
with
i>0
,

\forall X\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}.\;\delta(s,\omega(s)\cup X)\in\mathsf{Win}_{i-1}.

Such a choice exists by construction of
\mathsf{Win}

9
. For states
s\in\mathsf{Win}_{0}=F
, the agent chooses a fresh
done
action, indicating that it terminates the play.
Winning strategies can be formulated as deterministic finite-state controllers in the standard form of a transducer:

T=(2^{\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}},Q,q_{0},\eta,\omega),

where
Q:=\mathsf{Win}
,
q_{0}:=s_{0}
, the output function is the chosen
\omega
, and the transition function is

\eta(q,X):=\delta(q,\omega(q)\cup X).

For an input sequence
X_{0}X_{1}\cdots
, the transducer outputs
Y_{i}:=\omega(q_{i})
and updates
q_{i+1}:=\eta(q_{i},X_{i})
, generating a winning play in the game
G
. The transducer therefore implements a concrete winning strategy.

4
Multi-property
\text{LTL}_{f}
Synthesis

We lift the standard notion of synthesis from single properties to sets of properties. We aim to construct a single strategy that forces the play to generate a trace satisfying multiple properties against all input behaviors.

Definition 3

(Multi-property
\text{LTL}_{f}
Synthesis)
.

Let
\Phi=\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\}
be set of
n

\text{LTL}_{f}
properties (or
goals
) over the same atomic propositions
\textsf{AP}=\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}
.
A goal set
C\subseteq\Phi
is
realizable
if there exists a strategy

g:(2^{\mathcal{X}})^{*}\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
such that for every input sequence
\xi=X_{0}X_{1}\cdots\in(2^{\mathcal{X}})^{\omega}
, there is a
k\geq 0
such that the induced finite trace
\rho_{k}(g,\xi)\vDash\varphi_{i}
, forall
\varphi_{i}\in C
.
The
multi-property synthesis
problem is to construct such a winning strategy.

We extend the DFA-game approach to
\text{LTL}_{f}
synthesis from a single property to a set of properties, by constructing a single arena that tracks progress towards all goals simultaneously.
We consider a fixed set of
n

\text{LTL}_{f}
properties (or
goals
)
\Phi=\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\}
over the same atomic propositions
\textsf{AP}=\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}
.
For each goal
\varphi_{i}\in\Phi
, let

\mathcal{G}_{i}=(2^{\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}},\,S_{i},\,q_{i}^{0},\,\delta_{i},\,F_{i})

be a DFA recognizing exactly the finite traces satisfying
\varphi_{i}
.

Definition 4

(Multi-property Game Arena)
.

The
multi-property arena
of
\Phi
is the product:

\mathcal{G}^{\times}=(2^{\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}},\,S^{\times},\,s^{\times}_{0},\,\delta^{\times}),

where
S^{\times}:=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}
is the set of product states,
s_{0}^{\times}:=(q_{1}^{0},\dots,q_{n}^{0})
is the initial product state, and the transition function is defined componentwise by

\delta^{\times}\bigl((q_{1},\dots,q_{n}),a\bigr)\;:=\;\bigl(\delta_{1}(q_{1},a),\dots,\delta_{n}(q_{n},a)\bigr).

Notice that
\mathcal{G}^{\times}
does not come with a designated set of final states: in the multi-property setting, there is no single notion of completion, since a product state may witness the satisfaction of some goals but not others.

The enumeration-based approach fixes a target conjunction for some subset
\Phi^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi
, which induces a corresponding acceptance condition, and then solves a separate synthesis game for each choice of
\Phi^{\prime}
, rebuilding product arenas an exponential number of times.
In contrast, since we aim to compute
all
fulfillable subsets simultaneously, we do not restrict
\mathcal{G}^{\times}
to a single acceptance condition.
Instead, in the following we develop a multi-property synthesis procedure that works on the
single
product arena
\mathcal{G}^{\times}
and simultaneously determines, for all
\Phi^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi
, which goal combinations are fulfillable from each product state and synthesises strategies that fulfill them.

A state
s=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})\in S^{\times}
consists of the component states of the individual automata, one for each property
\varphi_{i}
.
Accordingly, a set of goals
C\subseteq\Phi
is
simultaneously satisfied
at
s
if all corresponding components are accepting.

Definition 5

(Multi-Property Satisfaction)
.

Let
s=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})\in S^{\times}
and let
C\subseteq\Phi
.
We write
s\models C
if for every property
\varphi_{i}\in C
we have
q_{i}\in F_{i}
.

A goal set
C\subseteq\Phi
is realizable from a state
s\in S^{\times}
if there exists a strategy

g:(2^{\mathcal{X}})^{*}\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}

such that for every input sequence
\xi=X_{0}X_{1}\cdots\in(2^{\mathcal{X}})^{\omega}
, the play

s_{0}:=s
and
s_{t+1}:=\delta^{\times}(s_{t},g(X_{0}\cdots X_{t-1})\cup X_{t})

reaches some
k
with
s_{k}\models C
.
At that point the strategy can stop the play, and since each
\mathcal{G}_{i}
recognizes the traces satisfying
\varphi_{i}
, reaching a product state
s_{k}\in S^{\times}
with
s_{k}\models C
means that the induced finite trace is accepted by each automaton
\mathcal{G}_{i}
for
\varphi_{i}\in C
, hence satisfies all goals in
C
simultaneously.

4.1
Solving Multi-property Games

We introduce our approach for computing a
multi-property winning relation
over pairs
(s,C)
, where
s\in S^{\times}
and
C\subseteq\Phi
.
This relation characterizes, for each product state
s
, exactly which goal sets
C
are realizable, and allows us to extract a winning strategy for any such
C
.
As for single properties, it is obtained by a fixed-point computation that lifts winning regions from states to
state–property-set pairs
: the base consists of those
(s,C)
where all goals in
C
are already satisfied at
s
, and otherwise the agent must choose an output
Y\in 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
such that for all
X\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}
the agent can enforce realizing all properties in
C
simultaneously from any successor state. This propagates realizability for all goal sets across all states.

For a relation
\mathcal{E}\subseteq S^{\times}\times 2^{\Phi}
we define the
controllable multi-property predecessor
by:

\displaystyle\mathsf{PreMC}(\mathcal{E}):=\{(s,C)\mid{}

\displaystyle\exists Y\in 2^{\mathcal{Y}}\ \forall X\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}.

\displaystyle(\delta^{\times}(s,Y\cup X),C)\in\mathcal{E}\}.

Intuitively,
\mathsf{PreMC}(\mathcal{E})
contains the pairs
(s,C)
from which the agent has a one-step move that guarantees preserving realizability of the same goal set
C
, regardless of the environment’s input choice.
The multi-property winning relation is then obtained as the least fixed point of repeatedly adding such predecessors, starting from the base pairs where
C
already holds in the current product state:

\displaystyle\mathsf{WinM}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\{(s,C)\mid s\models C\},

\displaystyle\mathsf{WinM}_{i+1}

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{WinM}_{i}\cup\mathsf{PreMC}(\mathsf{WinM}_{i}).

Notice that
\mathsf{WinM}_{0}
can be computed in linear time: since checking
s\models C
only amounts to inspecting which components of a state
s=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
are accepting
q_{i}\in F_{i}
, and thus which goal sets
C
are already satisfied at
s
.
As
S^{\times}
and
\Phi
are finite, the sequence stabilizes after at most
|S^{\times}|\cdot 2^{|\Phi|}
iterations in a fixed point

\mathsf{WinM}:=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\mathsf{WinM}_{i}
.

obtained by the construction above exists and implements a winning strategy for realizing
C
: for every input sequence
\xi\in(2^{\mathcal{X}})^{\omega}
, the play induced by
T_{C}
reaches a state
s\in S^{\times}
with

\varphi_{i}\in C

4.2
Maximal Multi-Property Synthesis

The construction above avoids explicitly solving a separate synthesis instance for each
C\subseteq\Phi
, but the relation
\mathsf{WinM}\subseteq S^{\times}\times 2^{\Phi}
may still be large: in the worst case, a single state can be related to exponentially many sets of properties. Nevertheless, multi-property realizability has an inherent
monotonicity
: if
(s,C)\in\mathsf{WinM}
, then
(s,D)\in\mathsf{WinM}
for every
D\subseteq C
.
Hence, many elements of
\mathsf{WinM}
are redundant and subsumed by larger realizable sets.
Therefore, it is natural to maintain, for each state
s
, only the
maximal
realizable sets.

We capture this compression with an operator that removes all pairs subsumed by a strictly larger set of properties at the same state.
For a relation
\mathcal{E}\subseteq S^{\times}\times 2^{\Phi}
, we define

\mathsf{Max}(\mathcal{E})\,:=\,\{(s,C)\in\mathcal{E}\mid\neg\exists(s,D)\in\mathcal{E}\ \text{with}\ C\subset D\}.

Thus,
\mathsf{Max}(\mathcal{E})
retains exactly those pairs whose set of properties
C
set is maximal with respect to
\subseteq
among all sets associated with the same state.
In the following, we apply
\mathsf{Max}
within the fixed-point computation so that only maximal realizable sets are maintained throughout, while all smaller realizable sets remain implicit.
Once, however, we keep only maximal sets, the predecessor step must not require
the same
set
C
to be stored explicitly for every successor. Instead, it is sufficient that each successor state admits
some
realizable set
D
that
covers

C
, i.e.,
C\subseteq D
, because realizability of
D
implies realizability of all its subsets. Accordingly, we define the
maximal multi-property predecessor
for
\mathcal{E}\subseteq S^{\times}\times 2^{\Phi}
by

\displaystyle\mathsf{PreMMC}(\mathcal{E}):=\{(s,C)\mid{}

\displaystyle\exists Y\in 2^{\mathcal{Y}}\ \forall X\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}\ \exists D\supseteq C.

\displaystyle(\delta^{\times}(s,Y\cup X),D)\in\mathcal{E}\ \}.

Intuitively,
\mathsf{PreMMC}(\mathcal{E})
contains exactly those pairs
(s,C)
from which the agent can, for any environment input, force a step to a successor state where it can enforce some
D\supseteq C
, and therefore can also enforce
C
.

Instead of constructing
\mathsf{WinM}
and pruning redundant goal sets afterwards, we maintain throughout only those pairs whose property set is maximal for the corresponding state.
This yields the following
maximal
fixed-point iteration:

\displaystyle\mathsf{WinM}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{Max}(\{(s,C)\mid s\models C\}),

\displaystyle\mathsf{WinM}_{i+1}

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{Max}\bigl(\mathsf{WinM}_{i}\cup\mathsf{PreMMC}(\mathsf{WinM}_{i})\bigr),

and let
\mathsf{WinM}:=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\mathsf{WinM}_{i}
denote the resulting fixed point.
In contrast to the construction in Section
4.1
, which may accumulate many dominated pairs, the iteration above ensures that dominated pairs are eliminated on the fly while all smaller realizable sets remain implicit by monotonicity.

Theorem 3
.

For all
s\in S^{\times}
and
C\subseteq\Phi
,

\displaystyle(s,C)\in\mathsf{WinM}\;\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle C\text{ is realizable from }s\;\land

\displaystyle\neg\exists D\supset C.\;D\text{ is realizable from }s.

The iteration
\mathsf{WinM}_{i}
stabilizes after at most
|S^{\times}|\cdot 2^{|\Phi|}
steps.

\mathsf{WinM}
relates each state
s
to the
\subseteq
-maximal realizable sets of properties.
For any realizable set of properties
C\subseteq\Phi
with
(s_{0}^{\times},C)\in\mathsf{WinM}
, we can construct a winning strategy for fulfilling
C
as a finite transducer by projecting
\mathsf{WinM}
onto
C
, exactly as in the non-maximal construction in Section
4.1
.

5
Symbolic Synthesis

We now transition from an explicit representation of the game arena to a
symbolic
representation in which states, transitions, and accepting conditions are encoded using Boolean formulas.
This symbolic approach is grounded in Boolean synthesis
15
and yields substantial practical advantages, as fixed-point iterations can be carried out via efficient boolean operations
28
.
We begin by reviewing symbolic DFA arenas, single-property symbolic synthesis and present our symbolic multi-property synthesis procedure.

Definition 6

(Symbolic DFA Game Arena)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(2^{\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{Y}},S,s_{0},\delta,F)
be a DFA game arena.
The corresponding
symbolic DFA game arena
is a tuple

\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z},Z_{0},\eta,f),

where:
\mathcal{Z}
is a set of
\lceil\log_{2}|S|\rceil
Boolean

such that each state
s\in S
is encoded by an assignment
Z\in 2^{\mathcal{Z}}
,
Z_{0}\in 2^{\mathcal{Z}}
encodes the initial state
s_{0}
,
\eta:\;2^{\mathcal{X}}\times 2^{\mathcal{Y}}\times 2^{\mathcal{Z}}\to 2^{\mathcal{Z}}
is a Boolean transition function such that, whenever
Z
encodes
s
, the assignment
\eta(X,Y,Z)
encodes the successor
\delta(s,X\cup Y)
, and
f
is a Boolean formula over
\mathcal{Z}
such that
f(Z)
holds iff
Z
encodes an accepting state, i.e.,
s\in F
.

Intuitively,
\mathcal{F}
represents the game symbolically by viewing
X\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}
,
Y\in 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
, and
Z\in 2^{\mathcal{Z}}
as Boolean assignments to the variables in
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{Y}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
, with a variable set to
true
iff it is contained in the corresponding valuation, so that
\eta
updates the state variables according to the transition relation, and

characterizes the accepting states

5.1
Symbolic Single-Property Synthesis

Following
28
, given a symbolic game arena
\mathcal{F}
, we maintain two kinds of formulas over the state variables:
winning-state
formulas
w_{i}(Z)
and
winning-move
formulas
t_{i}(Z,Y)
.
An assignment
Z\in 2^{\mathcal{Z}}
encodes a winning state iff
w_{i}(Z)
is
true
, and a pair
(Z,Y)\in 2^{\mathcal{Z}}\times 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
encodes a winning choice of output at that state iff
t_{i}(Z,Y)
is
true
.

We initialize with the accepting condition,

t_{0}(Z,Y)\equiv f(Z)
and
w_{0}(Z)\equiv f(Z)
,
since the agent can terminate immediately upon reaching an accepting state.
We iteratively refine these formulas in a symbolic fixed-point computation:

\displaystyle t_{i+1}(Z,Y)

\displaystyle\equiv\;t_{i}(Z,Y)\;\lor

\displaystyle\bigl(\neg w_{i}(Z)\land\forall X.\;w_{i}\bigl(\eta(X,Y,Z)\bigr)\bigr),

\displaystyle w_{i+1}(Z)

\displaystyle\equiv\;\exists Y.\;t_{i+1}(Z,Y).

Intuitively,
t_{i+1}
adds exactly those output choices that force the next state into the current winning region, regardless of the environment input. The conjunct
\neg w_{i}
ensures that each step moves strictly closer to accepting states.
The iteration stabilizes when
w_{i+1}\equiv w_{i}=:w
, at which point
w
characterizes all winning states.
The Boolean operations, can be carried out very efficiently using symbolic representations such as
Binary Decision Diagrams
(BDDs)
15
.

A winning strategy can be extracted
symbolically
if
w(z_{0})
is
true
.
From the final move formula

we can construct a function

\tau:2^{\mathcal{Z}}\to 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
that selects, for each winning state encoding

, i.e., where
t(Z,Y)
is
true

, inducing a symbolic transducer implementing the winning strategy

5.2
Symbolic Multi-Property Synthesis

We now present our symbolic approach for multi-property
\text{LTL}_{f}
synthesis.
Beyond the general benefits of symbolic methods, our approach exploits a key structural advantage: the Boolean formulas we construct naturally capture the monotonicity of multi-property realizability. Our Boolean representation of the maximal realizable sets compactly captures
all
realizable subsets simultaneously without explicitly enumerating them in the formulas.
Remarkably, our symbolic procedure is algorithmically almost identical to the single-property version.
We add a linear number of Boolean variables representing the properties and then apply the same symbolic fixed-point computation as in the single-property approach. Unlike the explicit-state procedure in Section
4
, which required new predecessor operators to handle sets of properties, the symbolic version does not require new operations. The result is a single formula over states and property variables that compactly captures for each state
all
sets of properties that are simultaneously realizable from it.

Definition 7

(Symbolic Multi-Property Game Arena)
.

For each goal
\varphi_{i}\in\Phi
, let
\mathcal{F}_{i}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z}_{i},Z_{i}^{0},\eta_{i},f_{i})
be its symbolic DFA game arena. The
symbolic multi-property arena
is

\mathcal{F}^{\times}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z},Z_{0},\eta),

where
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{Z}_{n}
are the state variables,
Z_{0}=(Z_{1}^{0},\ldots,Z_{n}^{0})
is the initial state, and
\eta=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})
is the componentwise transition function.

To represent sets of properties, we introduce
n
Boolean variables
\mathcal{K}=\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}\}
, where an assignment
K\in 2^{\mathcal{K}}
encodes a subset
C\subseteq\Phi
with
k_{i}=\text{true}
iff
\varphi_{i}\in C
.
We then define the initial winning relation formula by

w_{0}(Z,K):=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(k_{i}\Rightarrow f_{i}(Z_{i})\bigr),

which holds when the state represented by
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})\in 2^{\mathcal{Z}}
satisfies all properties represented by
K\in 2^{\mathcal{K}}
.
A key element of our approach is the use of implications in this formula, which directly captures the monotonicity of multi-property realizability: if
w_{0}(Z,K)
holds for some set of properties, then it also holds for every subset. This allows us to represent exponentially many goal combinations compactly, without explicit enumeration.

We now apply the same symbolic fixed-point computation as in the single-property approach, with formulas ranging over both state variables
Z
and goal variables
K
:

\displaystyle t_{i+1}(Z,Y,K)

\displaystyle\equiv t_{i}(Z,Y,K)

\displaystyle\quad\vee\bigl(\neg w_{i}(Z,K)\wedge\forall X.\,w_{i}(\eta(X,Y,Z),K)\bigr),

\displaystyle w_{i+1}(Z,K)

\displaystyle\equiv\exists Y.\,t_{i+1}(Z,Y,K),

with
w_{0}
defined as above and
t_{0}(Z,Y,K)=w_{0}(Z,K)
.
Crucially, this computation preserves the monotonicity property at each iteration. This allows us to apply the same symbolic operations as in the single-property case, without requiring modifications to handle the exponentially many combinations of properties. Upon stabilization, the resulting formula
w(Z,K)
captures the full multi-property winning relation
\mathsf{WinM}
.
As in the single-property case, a winning strategy for a realizable
C
can be extracted as a transducer by choosing appropriate output assignments from
t(Z,Y,K)
.

7
Conclusion

We presented a symbolic approach to multi-property
\text{LTL}_{f}
synthesis that computes the full realizability relation between states and goal sets in a single fixed-point, avoiding explicit subset enumeration. Leveraging monotonicity, we encode goals with Boolean variables and leverage the standard symbolic fixed-point computations. Our experiments demonstrate speedups of up to two orders of magnitude over enumeration baselines, making our method an effective way to reason about goal trade-offs in over-subscribed domains.

We intend to expand our contribution in three key directions: (i)
online strategy adaptation
, leveraging our precomputed winning relation to dynamically adapt to larger goal sets becoming realizable during execution if the environment acts less adversarial; (ii)
quantitative preferences
, introducing weighted properties and (iii)
probabilistic environments
, extending to Markov Decision Processes by replacing the controllable predecessor with probabilistic Bellman updates to maximize the likelihood of multi-goal satisfaction.

with
s\models C
after finitely many steps, and the generated finite trace satisfies every
\varphi_{i}\in C
.

Proof.

Fix a goal set
C\subseteq\Phi
. The multi-property winning relation restricted to
C
is defined by
\mathsf{WinM}|_{C}=\{s\in S^{\times}\mid(s,C)\in\mathsf{WinM}\}
. Unfolding the definition of the multi-property fixed-point iteration shows that
\mathsf{WinM}|_{C}
satisfies:

1.

\mathsf{WinM}_{0}|_{C}=\{s\in S^{\times}\mid s\models C\}
,

2.

\mathsf{WinM}_{i+1}|_{C}=\mathsf{WinM}_{i}|_{C}\cup\{s\in S^{\times}\mid\exists Y\in 2^{\mathcal{Y}}.\forall X\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}.\delta^{\times}(s,X\cup Y)\in\mathsf{WinM}_{i}|_{C}\}
.

This recovers precisely the traditional fixed-point characterization of single-property realizability played on the DFA arena
\mathcal{G}^{\times}
with final states
F_{C}=\{s\in S^{\times}\mid s\models C\}
.

As shown in
10
, such a game solves the synthesis problem for the
\text{LTL}_{f}
specification
\bigwedge_{\varphi_{i}\in C}\varphi_{i}
.
Our construction of the transducer
T_{C}
coincides with the single-property case established in
[
10
, Theorem 4]
. Consequently, the play reaches
F_{C}
in a finite number of steps, ensuring that the generated finite trace satisfies all properties in
C
simultaneously.
∎

A.3
Correctness of Maximal Multi-Property Synthesis

In this section, we establish that the maximal fixed-point iteration correctly computes the maximal realizable goal sets. In the following, we denote by
\mathsf{WinM}
the multi-property realizability relation from Section
4.1
and by
\mathsf{WinMM}

the maximal variant from Section
4.2
.

For any relation
\mathcal{E}\subseteq S^{\times}\times 2^{\Phi}
, we define its
downward closure
as
\downarrow\!(\mathcal{E}):=\{(s,D)\mid\exists C:(s,C)\in\mathcal{E}\text{ and }D\subseteq C\}
. This operator reconstructs all realizable subsets implied by the maximal sets.

Lemma 3

(Downward Closure Preservation)
.

For any relation
\mathcal{E}\subseteq S^{\times}\times 2^{\Phi}
, the
\mathsf{Max}
operator preserves the downward closure, i.e.,
\downarrow\!(\mathsf{Max}(\mathcal{E}))=\downarrow\!(\mathcal{E})
. Furthermore, the maximal predecessor and standard predecessor satisfy
\downarrow\!(\mathsf{PreMMC}(\mathcal{E}))=\mathsf{PreMC}(\downarrow\!(\mathcal{E}))
.

Proof.

The first identity follows immediately from the definition of
\mathsf{Max}
: since
\mathsf{Max}(\mathcal{E})
only removes pairs
(s,C)
that are subsets of some existing
(s,D)\in\mathcal{E}
, these removed subsets remain present in
\downarrow\!(\mathsf{Max}(\mathcal{E}))
.

For the second identity, fix a pair
(s,C)
. By definition,
(s,C)\in\mathsf{PreMC}(\downarrow\!(\mathcal{E}))
iff there exists
Y\in 2^{\mathcal{Y}}
such that for all
X\in 2^{\mathcal{X}}
, the successor state
s^{\prime}
satisfies
(s^{\prime},C)\in\downarrow\!(\mathcal{E})
. This is equivalent to
\exists D\supseteq C
such that
(s^{\prime},D)\in\mathcal{E}
. This is precisely the defining predicate for
(s,C)\in\mathsf{PreMMC}(\mathcal{E})
, which, when downward closed, yields the result.
∎

Theorem 7

(Restatement of Theorem 3)
.

The maximal fixed point
\mathsf{WinMM}=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}\mathsf{WinMM}_{i}
characterizes the maximal realizable goal sets:

\displaystyle(s,C)\in\mathsf{WinMM}\;\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle C\text{ is realizable from }s\;\land

\displaystyle\neg\exists D\supset C.\;D\text{ is realizable from }s.

The sequence stabilizes in at most
|S^{\times}|\cdot 2^{|\Phi|}
iterations.

Proof.

Let
(\mathsf{WinMM}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be the maximal iteration and consider the sequence of downward-closed relations
E_{i}=\downarrow\!(\mathsf{WinMM}_{i})
. Using the identities from Lemma
3
, we have:

\displaystyle E_{i+1}

\displaystyle=\downarrow\!\bigl(\mathsf{Max}(\mathsf{WinMM}_{i}\cup\mathsf{PreMMC}(\mathsf{WinMM}_{i}))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\downarrow\!(\mathsf{WinMM}_{i})\cup\downarrow\!(\mathsf{PreMMC}(\mathsf{WinMM}_{i}))

\displaystyle=E_{i}\cup\mathsf{PreMC}(E_{i}).

Since
E_{0}=\downarrow\!(\mathsf{Max}(\{(s,C)\mid s\models C\}))=\mathsf{WinM}_{0}
, the sequence
(E_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is identical to the standard multi-property iteration
(\mathsf{WinM}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
. As established in Theorem 1, the limit of this sequence is the full realizability relation
\mathsf{WinM}
.
It follows that
\downarrow\!(\mathsf{WinMM})=\mathsf{WinM}
. This implies:

1.

Soundness:
Every
(s,C)\in\mathsf{WinMM}
is contained in
\mathsf{WinM}
, therefore,
C
is realizable from
s
.

2.

Maximality:
By the application of
\mathsf{Max}
at each iteration, no pair
(s,C)
is maintained if a strictly larger
D\supset C
is already shown to be realizable. Thus,
\mathsf{WinMM}
contains only
\subseteq
-maximal elements.

Conversely, if
C
is a maximal realizable set at
s
, then
(s,C)\in\mathsf{Max}(\mathsf{WinM})
.Since
\mathsf{WinM}=\downarrow\!(\mathsf{WinMM})
, there must exist
(s,D)\in\mathsf{WinMM}
with
C\subseteq D
. Since
D
is realizable and
C
is maximal, it forces
C=D
, proving
(s,C)\in\mathsf{WinMM}
. Stabilization follows from the finiteness of the domain
S^{\times}\times 2^{\Phi}
.
∎

A.4
Complexity of Multi-Property Synthesis

In this section, we establish the computational complexity of multi-property
\text{LTL}_{f}
realizability and synthesis.

Theorem 8

(Restatement of Theorem 4)
.

Multi-property
\text{LTL}_{f}
realizability and synthesis are
2EXPTIME
-complete.

Proof.

The proof follows by establishing both membership in and hardness for the
2EXPTIME
complexity class. For a set of properties
\Phi=\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\}
, we write
m:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\varphi_{i}|
and
|\mathcal{G}_{i}|
for the size of an automaton
\mathcal{G}_{i}
. We write
\text{poly}(m)
for an unspecified polynomial function in
m
.

Membership:

The multi-property synthesis procedure consists of three primary computational phases:

1.

Automata Construction:
We construct a DFA arena
\mathcal{G}_{i}
for each property
\varphi_{i}
. The construction of a DFA from an
\text{LTL}_{f}
formula is at most doubly exponential in the size of the formula
10
. Therefore,
|\mathcal{G}_{i}|\leq 2^{2^{\text{poly}(m)}}
.

2.

Arena Construction:
The multi-property game arena is the product of the individual arenas
\mathcal{G}_{i}
. Its size satisfies

|S^{\times}|\leq(2^{2^{\text{poly}(m)}})^{n}=2^{n\cdot 2^{\text{poly}(m)}}\leq 2^{2^{\text{poly}(m)}}
,
so the arena is at most doubly exponential in the input size.

3.

Fixed-Point Computation:
Solving the reachability game on this arena, whether via the standard pair-based iteration (
\mathsf{WinM}
) or the maximal variant (
\mathsf{WinMM}
), takes time linear in the number of product states
|S^{\times}|
and exponential in the number of properties
n
. Since
2^{|\Phi|}\leq 2^{m}
is only singly exponential while
|S^{\times}|\leq 2^{2^{\text{poly}(m)}}
is
already doubly exponential, the fixed-point phase is bounded by
2^{2^{\text{poly}(m)}}
overall.

As the overall complexity is dominated by the DFA construction step, the problem resides in
2EXPTIME
.

Hardness:

The hardness is immediate from the established
2EXPTIME
-completeness of single-property
\text{LTL}_{f}
synthesis
10
. Any single-property synthesis instance for property
\varphi
can be trivially reduced to a multi-property instance by setting
\Phi=\{\varphi\}
.
∎

Extended Experiments

We provide additional details on the used benchmarks and the full experimental resutls.
All code, benchmark instances, and tool parameters are included in the supplementary material.
All experiments were carried out on an AMD EPYC CPU with 8 cores and 32GiB of RAM.

B.1
Benchmark Families

We evaluate on five benchmark families that stress different aspects of multi-property
\text{LTL}_{f}
synthesis.

Counter.

The
counter
family is based on the counter-game benchmark introduced in the literature.
2
2

2

See the benchmark description in
2
.

Intuitively, the controller maintains a
k
-bit counter and must react to environment-controlled inputs that request (potentially conflicting) counter updates over time, yielding goal sets that cannot always be jointly enforced. This creates a large symbolic arena, already for moderate
k
, and a non-trivial trade-off structure among goals.

Robot navigation.

The
robotnav
family models navigation with multiple region-reaching goals under adversarial constraints
2
.
The environment can disable parts of the world, e.g., by closing doors, and reaching certain regions can irrevocably restrict access to others. As a result, satisfying one navigation objective may rule out another, making this family well-suited for evaluating maximal realizable goal sets and the strategies that achieve them.

Chain, Until, Next.

The
chain
,
until
, and
next
families are parametric temporal-pattern benchmarks that generate large product arenas while keeping the individual formulas syntactically simple.
They are designed to stress specific temporal constructs, e.g., long dependency chains, nested and unfolded
U
structure, and deep
X
-progression, and are adapted from recent benchmark collections used to evaluate temporal reasoning and synthesis at scale
26
.
Across these families, we scale the number of properties
|\Phi|
and the pattern parameters, i.e., formula lengths and nesting depths to obtain instances ranging from small to very large arenas.
