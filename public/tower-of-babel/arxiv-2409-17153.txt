A generalized Apollonius problem
AZIZKHON AZIZOV, SEMYON LITVINOV

Abstract
. We present a self-contained solution of a generalized Apollonius problem of constructing a circle that intersects each of the three pair-wise non-intersecting circles at one of the three given angles.



1.
Introduction

In the
3^{\text{rd}}
century BC, Apollonius of Perga posted and solved the famous problem of constructing – with the help of a straightadge and a compass only – a circle tangent to three given circles - see, for example,
[
4
,
2
,
3
]
for a general statement.

The significance of this problem can hardly be overstated - no wonder the title of the Presidential address delivered in 1967 by the great geometer of the
20^{\text{th}}
century H.S.M. Coxeter to the Canadian Mathematical Congress at the joint meeting with the Mathematical Association of America was ”The Problem of Apollonius.” At the beginning of his presentation Coxeter stated, ”As Sir Thomas Heath remarks
[
6
, p. 182]
, this problem ’has exercised the ingenuity of many distinguished geometers, including Vieta and Newton.’” (The results presented in this address were later published in
[
2
]
.)

As a result, there are many ingenious approaches to the Apollonius problem. Unfortunately, Apollonius’ solution of his problem has not survived. However, François Viète found a solution by considering limiting cases, where any of the three given circles can be reduced to a point or blown up into a line, which one may believe to be the Apollonius’ approach.

The method of inversion yields an efficient way of solving Apollonius problem, where two of the three given circles are transformed either into two parallel lines or into two concentric circles. This approach, in part, utilizes the fact that inversion preserves angles between two intersecting circles. We use this fact, together with the availability of an inversion that transforms two non-intersecting circles into concentric ones, to solve the problem of constructing – using a straightedge and a compass only – a cirle that intersects each of the three given circles at one of the three given angles from the interval
[0,90^{0}]
. The Apollonius problem is a particular case of this problem where the three given angles are zeros.

To solve this problem, we first assume that such a circle exists and perform an inversion of the four circles that transforms two of the three given circles into concentric ones. Then we find a formula for a possible radius of the image of the circle in question and formulas for possible distances from its center to the centers of the images of the given circles. Finally, using these three formulas, we construct a circle
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
that
does
intersect each of the images of the three given ones at one of the three given angles. As the square of an inversion is the identity map, the inversion of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
delivers a circle that intersects each of the three given circles at one of the three given angles.

2.2.
Constructibility

Let us define what it means for an interval, or an angle, to be
constructible
.

Definition 2.1
.

Fix a unit interval, and let
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}
be intervals. Denote their lengths by the same letters. An interval of length

(1)

a=f(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})

is called
constructible
if it can be drawn with the help of straightedge and a compass. In this case, the number
a
is also called constructible. An angle
\alpha
is called constructible if
\cos\alpha
(equivalently,
\sin\alpha
) is a constructible number. If an interval (angle) can be constructed avoiding the use of the unit interval, we will call it
properly constructible
or
p-consructible
.

We presume that the reader is familiar with the following straightedge and compass p-constructions:

(a)

Finding the mid-point of a given interval.

(b)

Drawing the line through a given point and perpendicular to a given line.

(c)

Drawing the line through a given point and parallel to a given line.

Example 2.1
.

P-construct the circle that passes through three non-collinear points
A
,
B
, and
C
.

Solution:
Utilizing constructions (a) and (b), draw perpendicular bisectors of the intervals
[AB]
and
[BC]
.
The point of intersection of these bisectors is the center of the circle though
A
,
B
, and
C
.

To outline the scope of constructible intervals it is sufficient to consider a few examples where we use the constructions (a) - (c) without explicit references.

Example 2.2

Given intervals
a
and

Solution:
Draw a segment
[AB]

[AB]

\frac{|CX|}{|AC|}=\frac{|CB|}{|CX|}\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ \frac{x}{a}=\frac{b}{x}\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ x=\sqrt{ab},

thus, the interval
[CX]
has the desired length.

In particular, when

Example 2.4

Given intervals

4.
Solution of Problem 3.2

An elegant solution of the Apollonius problem - in a particular case given by Figure 4 - is given via an application of inversion as follows. First, the radii of the three given circles are enlarged by the same interval - so that two of the resulting circles become tangent at, say, a point
O
- and then performing an inversion with the center
O
resulting in these two circles turn into two parallel lines.

Our solution is based on the following facts:

(A)

An inversion preserves the angles between two circles - see
[
3
, Theorem 5.51]
.

(B)

Given two non-intersecting circles
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, there exists a circle
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
turn into concentric circles under the inversion with respect to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
- see
[
3
, Theorem 5.71]
. Note that this property can be used to find more solutions to the Apollonius problem - see
[
5
]
.

4.1.
Inversion and its Properties

Referring to Figure 6, consider a circle with center
O
and radius
R
, which we will call the
inversion circle
and denote
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
.

Figure 6

Points
P\neq O
and
P^{\prime}\neq O
are called the
inversion
images of each other with respect to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
if
P
and
P^{\prime}
lie on the same ray aminating from
O
and

|OP|\cdot|OP^{\prime}|=R^{2}.

This transformation of the plane - minus the point
O
- is called the
inversion
with respect to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
. If we denote this inversion by
\mathcal{I}
, then
\mathcal{I}(P)=P^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{I}(P^{\prime})=P
, hence
\mathcal{I}^{2}(P)=P
for every
P\neq O
, and
\mathcal{I}(P)=P
whenever
P\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
.

Figure 6 gives a way of p-constructing the point
P^{\prime}
if
P
is given and vise versa: If
P
is given, let
A
be the intersection of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
with the circle for which
[OP]
is a diameter. Then
PA
is tangent to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
, and if
AP^{\prime}\perp OP
, the triangles
AOP
and
P^{\prime}OA
are similar, so we have

\frac{|OP|}{R}=\frac{R}{|OP^{\prime}|}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ |OP|\cdot|OP^{\prime}|=R^{2}.

If
P^{\prime}
is given, let
A\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
be such that
AP^{\prime}\perp OP^{\prime}
. Then, if
P\in OP^{\prime}
is such that
AP\perp OA
, it follows as above that
|OP|\cdot|OP^{\prime}|=R^{2}
.

Next, it can be proven - see
[
3
, Theorem 5.45]
- that

(i)

If a circle
\mathcal{C}
does not pass through the center
O
of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{I}}
, then
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C})
is a circle.

(ii)

If a circle
\mathcal{C}
passes through
O
, then
\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{C})
is a straigh line.

Let us now describe a construction that will need to be performed repeatedly in the sequel without explicit reference:

\mathcal{C}
