Stable central limit theorems for discrete-time lag martingale difference arrays
Walter Dempsey, Easton Huch

Abstract
Recent work in dynamic causal inference introduced a class of discrete-time stochastic processes that generalize martingale difference sequences and arrays as follows: the random variates in each sequence have expectation zero given certain lagged filtrations but not given the natural filtration. We formalize this class of stochastic processes and prove a stable central limit theorem (CLT) via a Bernstein blocking scheme and an application of the classical martingale CLT. We generalize our limit theorem to vector-valued processes via the Cramér–Wold device and develop a simple form for the limiting variance. We demonstrate the application of these results to a problem in dynamic causal inference and present a simulation study supporting their validity.



Keywords:
Causal inference; Cramér–Wold device; Lindeberg method; stochastic process.

1
Introduction

Recent work by Bojinov, Rambachan, and Shephard (hereafter, BRS) proposes randomization-based methods for estimating time-averaged effects of treatments on future outcomes
(
Bojinov et al., 2021
;
Bojinov and Shephard, 2019
)
.
As a simple example, consider a setting in which an experimenter sequentially randomizes a single individual to a binary treatment
A_{t}\in\{0,1\}
for
T\in\mathbb{N}
time points.
Following each treatment, the experimenter observes an outcome
Y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
.
The proposed methods enable the experimenter to estimate the time-averaged effect of
A_{t}
on
Y_{t+p}
for
p=0,1,2,\ldots
.
With
p=0
, the result is an estimate of the ‘contemporaneous’ or immediate effect of
A_{t}
on
Y_{t}
.
For
p\geq 1
, the resulting estimate provides a measure of effect persistence over time, with significant effects at larger values of
p
indicating greater persistence.
Assessing treatment persistence is a well-recognized goal in dynamic causal inference, and other authors have proposed similar definitions of persistence, usually in a superpopulation setting

(
Blackwell and Glynn, 2018
;
Boruvka et al., 2018
;
Robins et al., 1999
)
.

BRS propose asymptotically Gaussian inference procedures justified by the martingale central limit theorem (CLT)
(
Hall and Heyde, 1980
;
Häusler and Luschgy, 2015
)
;
however, the martingale CLT does not apply for
p\geq 1
.
We show in Section
7
that the theory fails for
p\geq 1
because the supposed-martingale sequence,
(U_{t})_{1\leq t\leq T-p}
, generally does not satisfy the
martingale property
,
\mathbb{E}(U_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1})=0
, where
(\mathcal{H}_{t})_{-p\leq t\leq T-p}
is a filtration with each
U_{t}
being
\mathcal{H}_{t}
-measurable.
Rather, it satisfies a condition that we refer to as the
lag martingale property
:
\mathbb{E}(U_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{t-p-1})=0
.
For
p\geq 1
, we show in (
8
) that the covariance between neighboring time points is nonnegligible; hence, the martingale CLT does not apply.

In spite of this theoretical issue, the proposed framework of BRS poses considerable merit because it produces unbiased estimates of dynamic causal effects under weak assumptions.
In particular, the framework imposes only weak regularity conditions on the data-generating process for the outcomes.
This stands in contrast to many competing methods in dynamic causal inference that impose restrictive conditions, such as ‘no carryover effects’ across time periods
(
Arkhangelsky et al., 2021
;
Athey and Imbens, 2022
;
de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille, 2020
;
Imai and Kim, 2019
)
.
In addition, the BRS framework does not require a superpopulation assumption or independent units, enabling its application to a finite set of experimental units.
Consequently, addressing this theoretical issue would provide significant benefit to practitioners, enabling valid asymptotic inference for the case
p\geq 1
under weak assumptions.

The primary contribution of this work is stable CLTs for lag martingale difference processes (defined in Section
2
), such as those arising from the BRS framework.
Similar to the classical martingale CLT, our results guarantee stable convergence to a scale mixture of Gaussian random variables.
An intuitive characterization of stable convergence is
(
Jacod and Shiryaev, 2003
, Proposition VIII.5.33)

X_{n}\to X\ \mathcal{G}\text{-stably}\quad\iff\quad(X_{n},Y)\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}(X,Y)\quad\text{for all }\mathcal{G}\text{-measurable }Y,

where
\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}
denotes (weak) convergence in distribution.
Stability enables us to obtain a limiting Gaussian distribution (as opposed to a scale-mixture of Gaussian distributions) by applying the stable Cramér–Slutsky rule
(
Häusler and Luschgy, 2015
, Theorem 3.18 (b))
:

X_{n}\to X\ \mathcal{G}\text{-stably},\quad Y_{n}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}Y,\quad Y\text{ is }\mathcal{G}\text{-measurable}\quad\implies\quad X_{n}Y_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}XY

where
\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}
denotes convergence in probability.
In the dynamic causal inference setting discussed above,
1/Y^{2}
is the variance of the estimator and may be random.

From a technical standpoint, our results rely on a Bernstein blocking strategy, partitioning the rows of a triangular array into minor and major blocks
(
Bernstein, 1927
;
Dehling and Philipp, 2002
)
.
The minor blocks are constructed to be asymptotically negligible so that the limiting behavior is determined by partial sums arising from the major blocks, which obey the classical martingale CLT.
Researchers have applied related blocking strategies to obtain CLTs for a wide variety of stochastic processes exhibiting forms of weak dependence across time, including mixingales, near-epoch dependent sequences, and mixing sequences
(
Bardet et al., 2008
;
de Jong, 1997
;
Jiang and Hahn, 2008
;
Merlevede and Peligrad, 2000
;
Merlevède, 2003
)
.

Of the results in the literature, mixingale CLTs bear the greatest similarity to our CLTs because they apply to nonstationary data and guarantee stable convergence relative to a
\sigma
-field large enough to contain the asymptotic variance
(
Ikeda, 2017
;
McLeish, 1977
;
Wu, 2007
)
.
For a fixed lag length
p
, mixingale arrays are easily seen to nest lag martingale difference arrays as a special case; however, our specialized theory offers several benefits.
First, our Theorem
3
allows the lag length to increase sublinearly with the sequence length, enabling application to non-mixingale arrays.
Second, our results provide a convenient form for the asymptotic variance involving only variances and covariances within the lag window, facilitating asymptotic inference.
Third, our approach permits a straightforward extension to multivariate processes.
In fact, these benefits motivated this work and enable various improvements to the BRS framework; though, we leave the methodological details to future work.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
provides the basic setup and definitions of scalar-valued lag-
p
martingale difference sequences and arrays.
Section
3
gives a basic CLT for scalar-valued processes with a fixed, finite lag length.
Section
4
extends the CLT to multivariate processes.
Section
5
provides the extension to diverging, sublinear lag lengths.
Section
6
derives the simplified form of the asymptotic variance.
Section
7
illustrates the application of our theoretical results to the BRS framework and includes a simulation study supporting their validity.
Section
8
concludes with a brief discussion of the results.

2
Lag martingale difference sequences and arrays

Let
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a nondecreasing sequence with
k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{n}=\infty
.
Let
(X_{nk})_{1\leq k\leq k_{n}}
be a sequence of real-valued random variables defined on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{X},P)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
(\mathcal{X}_{nk})_{0\leq k\leq k_{n}}
be a filtration in
\mathcal{X}
so that
\mathcal{X}_{n0}\subset\mathcal{X}_{n1}\subset\ldots\subset\mathcal{X}_{nk_{n}}\subset\mathcal{X}
.
For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we assume that
(X_{nk})_{1\leq k\leq k_{n}}
is adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{X}_{nk})_{0\leq k\leq k_{n}}
, meaning that
X_{nk}
is
\mathcal{X}_{nk}
-measurable for all
1\leq k\leq k_{n}
.
In other words, the triangular array
(X_{nk})_{1\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables is adapted to the triangular array
(\mathcal{X}_{nk})_{0\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\sigma
-fields.
We say that
A\in\mathcal{L}^{q}(P)
if
\mathbb{E}(|A|^{q})<\infty
in
P
-probability.
With this notation in place, scalar-valued lag-
p
martingale difference sequences are defined as follows.

Definition 1

(Lag-
p
martingale difference sequence)
.

Let
p\in\{0,1,\ldots\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k_{n}\geq p+1
.
The sequence
(X_{nk})_{p+1\leq k\leq k_{n}}
is a (scalar-valued)
lag-
p
martingale difference sequence
if
\mathbb{E}(X_{nk}|\mathcal{X}_{n,k-p-1})=0
and
X_{nk}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(P)
for all
p+1\leq k\leq k_{n}
.

Lag-
p
martingale difference arrays are defined similarly, allowing
n
to range over
\mathbb{N}
.
Definition
1
nests martingale difference sequences (and, by extension, arrays) as the special case
p=0
.
We can relate different lag lengths as follows.

Proposition 1

(Lag relationships)
.

Let
p\in\{0,1,\ldots\}
,
q\in\{p+1,p+2,\ldots\}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k_{n}\geq q+1
.
If
(X_{nk})_{p+1\leq k\leq k_{n}}
is a lag-
p
martingale, then
(X_{nk})_{q+1\leq k\leq k_{n}}
is a lag-
q
martingale.

Proof.

Apply the tower property of expectations.
∎

In particular, Proposition
1
implies that a martingale difference sequence is a lag-
p
martingale difference sequence for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
; however, the converse is false in general.

3
Basic CLT

We now provide a stable CLT for scalar-valued lag-
p
martingale difference arrays.

3.1
Setup

Let
p\in\{0,1,\ldots\}
.
We partition the elements of
(X_{nk})_{p+1\leq k\leq k_{n}}
into major and minor blocks.
The starting indices (in
k
) of these major blocks are given by a sequence
(b_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
with
b_{j+1}-b_{j}\geq p+1
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
;
by construction, we set
b_{1}=p+1
.
The minor blocks fall between the major blocks and are of length
p
.
Their starting indices are given by the sequence
(a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
with
a_{j}\coloneqq b_{j+1}-p
denoting the starting index of the minor block immediately following the
j
th major block.
The major block sizes are then given by
d_{j}\coloneqq a_{j}-b_{j}
.
We define the minor and major block sums, respectively, as

A_{nj}\coloneqq\sum_{k=a_{j}}^{\min(b_{j+1}-1,k_{n})}X_{nk},\qquad B_{nj}\coloneqq\sum_{k=b_{j}}^{\min(a_{j}-1,k_{n})}X_{nk}.

We then define corresponding minor and major partial sums as
S_{n}^{(A)}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}^{(A)}}A_{nj}
and
S_{n}^{(B)}\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}^{(B)}}B_{nj}
, respectively, where
j_{n}^{(A)}\coloneqq\max\{j\in\mathbb{N}\colon a_{j}\leq k_{n}\}
is the number of minor blocks through index
k_{n}
and
j_{n}^{(B)}\coloneqq\max\{j\in\mathbb{N}\colon a_{j}-1\leq k_{n}\}
is the number of
complete
major blocks.
We can then decompose the partial sum as
S_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k=p+1}^{k_{n}}X_{nk}=S_{n}^{(A)}+S_{n}^{(B)}+S_{n}^{(C)}
, where

S_{n}^{(C)}\coloneqq\begin{cases}B_{n,j_{n}^{(B)}+1}&\text{if }k_{n}\in[b_{j_{n}^{(B)}+1},a_{j_{n}^{(B)}+1}-2],\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

comprises the final block if it is an
incomplete
major block (i.e., if one or more terms are excluded because
k_{n}<a_{j_{n}^{(B)}+1}-1
).
We define the filtration array
(\mathcal{B}_{nj})_{0\leq j\leq j_{n}^{(B)},n\in\mathbb{N}}
with each
\mathcal{B}_{nj}=\mathcal{X}_{n,a_{j}-1}
.
Lastly, a triangular array is
square integrable
if
X_{nk}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(P)
for all
1\leq k\leq k_{n}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.2
Theorem statement

Our basic CLT generalizes the classical martingale CLT
(
Häusler and Luschgy, 2015
, Theorem 6.1)
.

Theorem 1

(CLT)
.

For some
p\in\{0,1,\ldots\}
, let
(X_{nk})_{p+1\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a square-integrable lag-
p
martingale difference array adapted to
(\mathcal{X}_{nk})_{0\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Assume that there exists a blocking sequence
(b_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
as defined in Section
3.1
such that the following conditions hold:

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{D}
\mathrm{)}

d_{j}\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
,

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{SP}
\mathrm{)}

S_{n}^{(A)}+S_{n}^{(C)}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0
as
n\to\infty
,

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{MP}
\mathrm{)}

\max_{p+1\leq k\leq k_{n}}|X_{nk}|\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0
as
n\to\infty
,

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{N}
\mathrm{)}

\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}^{(B)}}\mathbb{E}\big(B_{nj}^{2}|\mathcal{B}_{n,j-1}\big)\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}\psi
as
n\to\infty
for some
\mathcal{G}
-measurable real random variable
\psi\geq 0
, where
\mathcal{G}_{nj}\coloneqq\cap_{m\geq n}\mathcal{B}_{mj}
and
\mathcal{G}\coloneqq\sigma\big(\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{n,j_{n}^{(B)}}\big)
, and

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{CLB}
\mathrm{)}

\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}^{(B)}}\mathbb{E}\big(B_{nj}^{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{|B_{nj}|\geq\epsilon\}}|\mathcal{B}_{n,j-1}\big)\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0
as
n\to\infty
for every
\epsilon>0
.

Then
S_{n}\to\sqrt{\psi}Z

\mathcal{G}
-stably as
n\to\infty
, where
Z
is a standard Gaussian random variable that is independent of
\mathcal{G}
.

In typical applications, we have a martingale difference sequence
(Y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and corresponding filtration
(\mathcal{Y}_{k})_{k\in\{0\}\cup\mathbb{N}}
.
In this case, we set
X_{nk}=n^{-1/2}Y_{k}
and
\mathcal{X}_{nk}=\mathcal{Y}_{k}
.
As a result, the filtration array
(\mathcal{X}_{nk})_{0\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
nested
because
\mathcal{X}_{nk}=\mathcal{Y}_{k}=\mathcal{X}_{n+1,k}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq k\leq k_{n}
.
Consequently, we can set
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{Y}_{\infty}\coloneqq\sigma\left(\cup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Y}_{k}\right)
, and Theorem
1
yields full stable convergence: the random variable
\psi
can depend on any information in
\mathcal{Y}_{\infty}
, including all elements of
(Y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.

3.3
Basic CLT proof

This section provides a proof of Theorem
1
.

3.3.1
Convergence in distribution.

We first prove the following Lemma.

Lemma 1

(CLT)
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
1
.
Then
S_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}\sqrt{\psi}Z
as
n\to\infty
, where
Z
is a standard Gaussian random variable.

Proof.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded function that is three times continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives, and define
C\coloneqq\max\left\{\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f(x),\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}f^{\prime}(x)\right\}
.
The conclusion of Lemma
1
will follow if we can show that
\mathbb{E}\left\{f(S_{n})-f(\sqrt{\psi}Z)\right\}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
We decompose
\mathbb{E}\left\{f(S_{n})-f(\sqrt{\psi}Z)\right\}
as

\mathbb{E}\left\{f(S_{n})-f\left(S_{n}^{(B)}\right)\right\}+\mathbb{E}\left\{f\left(S_{n}^{(B)}\right)-f(\sqrt{\psi}Z)\right\}=\mathbb{E}(I_{n})+\mathbb{E}(II_{n}).

(1)

By Taylor’s formula and the decomposition of
S_{n}
, we have

f(S_{n})=f\left(S_{n}^{(B)}\right)+\left(S_{n}^{(A)}+S_{n}^{(C)}\right)f^{\prime}(s)\quad\text{for some }s\text{ between }S_{n}^{(B)}\text{ and }S_{n}.

This formula gives
|I_{n}|=\left|S_{n}^{(A)}+S_{n}^{(C)}\right|\cdot\left|f^{\prime}(s)\right|\leq 2C
because
C
bounds
f
.
But we also have
\left|f^{\prime}(s)\right|\leq C
, implying that
|I_{n}|\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0
because
S_{n}^{(A)}+S_{n}^{(C)}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0
by
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{SP}
\mathrm{)}
;
thus, dominated convergence gives
\mathbb{E}|I_{n}|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
As for
\mathbb{E}(II_{n})
, the array
(B_{nj})_{1\leq j\leq j_{n}^{(B)},n\in\mathbb{N}}
is easily shown to be a square-integrable martingale difference array adapted to
(\mathcal{B}_{nj})_{0\leq j\leq j_{n}^{(B)},n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Consequently, we can apply the martingale CLT
(
Häusler and Luschgy, 2015
, Theorem 6.1)
to show that
\mathbb{E}(II_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

3.3.2
Proof of Theorem
1
.

Using Lemma
1
, we now adapt step 4 in the proof of Theorem 6.1 in
(
Häusler and Luschgy, 2015
)
to prove Theorem
1
.
Let
\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{n,j_{n}^{(B)}}
, where
\mathcal{G}_{nj}
is defined in
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{N}
\mathrm{)}
.
The sequence
(\mathcal{G}_{nj})_{1\leq j\leq j_{n}^{(B)},n\in\mathbb{N}}
is nondecreasing in both indices, so
\sigma(\mathcal{E})=\mathcal{G}
,
\Omega\in\mathcal{E}
, and
\mathcal{E}
is closed under finite intersections.
By
(
Häusler and Luschgy, 2015
, Theorem 3.17, (i)
\iff
(iv))
,

S_{n}\to\sqrt{\psi}Z\quad\mathcal{G}\text{-stably}\quad\iff\quad P_{F}^{S_{n}}\to P_{F}^{\sqrt{\psi}Z}\quad\text{weakly for every }F\in\mathcal{E},\,P(F)>0,

where
P_{F}^{Y}
denotes the conditional distribution of
Y
given
F
.
We now let
F\in\mathcal{E}
be arbitrary with
P(F)>0
.
By monotonicity of
(\mathcal{G}_{n,j_{n}^{(B)}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
F\in\mathcal{G}_{m,j_{m}^{(B)}}
.
Define
J\coloneqq j_{m}^{(B)}+1
and
N\coloneqq\min\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:k_{n}\geq b_{J}\right\}
.
Under
P_{F}
, the array
(X_{nk})_{b_{J}\leq k\leq k_{n},n\geq N}
is a lag-
p
martingale difference array adapted to
(\mathcal{X}_{nk})_{a_{J-1}-1\leq k\leq k_{n},n\geq N}
.
We then verify the conditions of Theorem
1
.
First,
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{D}
\mathrm{)}
holds because we have removed only a finite number of terms.
Second,
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{SP}
\mathrm{)}
yields

\sum_{j=J}^{j_{n}^{(B)}}A_{nj}+S_{n}^{(C)}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty\text{ in $P$-probability}

because the omitted terms
(A_{nj})_{1\leq j<J}
are asymptotically negligible by
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{MP}
\mathrm{)}
.
Because
F\in\mathcal{E}
is fixed with
P(F)>0
, the above statement also holds in
P_{F}
-probability, so that
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{SP}
\mathrm{)}
continues to hold for the arrays
(X_{nk})_{b_{J}\leq k\leq k_{n},n\geq N}
and
(\mathcal{X}_{nk})_{a_{J-1}-1\leq k\leq k_{n},n\geq N}
in
P_{F}
-probability.
Third,
\max_{p+1\leq k\leq k_{n}}|X_{nk}|\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0
as
n\to\infty
in
P
-probability by
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{MP}
\mathrm{)}
, so this convergence also holds in
P_{F}
-probability.
We then have
\max_{b_{J}\leq k\leq k_{n}}|X_{nk}|\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0
as
n\to\infty
in
P_{F}
-probability because

\max_{b_{J}\leq k\leq k_{n}}|X_{nk}|\leq\max_{p+1\leq k\leq k_{n}}|X_{nk}|\quad P_{F}\text{-almost surely},

so
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{MP}
\mathrm{)}
continues to hold.
Fourth, by conditions
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{MP}
\mathrm{)}
and
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{N}
\mathrm{)}
, we have

\sum_{j=J}^{j_{n}^{(B)}}\mathbb{E}_{P}\big(B_{nj}^{2}|\mathcal{B}_{n,j-1}\big)\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}\psi\quad\text{in $P$-probability as }n\to\infty

because, again, the omitted terms are asymptotically negligible.
We also have
\mathbb{E}_{P}\big(B_{nj}^{2}|\mathcal{B}_{n,j-1}\big)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{F}}\big(B_{nj}^{2}|\mathcal{B}_{n,j-1}\big)

P_{F}
-almost surely for all
J\leq j\leq j_{n}^{(B)}
and
n\geq N
, which gives

\sum_{j=J}^{j_{n}^{(B)}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{F}}\big(B_{nj}^{2}|\mathcal{B}_{n,j-1}\big)\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}\psi\quad\text{in $P_{F}$-probability as }n\to\infty,

so that
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{N}
\mathrm{)}
holds.
Finally,
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{CLB}
\mathrm{)}
immediately implies that

\sum_{j=J}^{j_{n}^{(B)}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{F}}\big(B_{nj}^{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{|B_{nj}|\geq\epsilon\}}|\mathcal{B}_{n,j-1}\big)\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0\quad\text{in $P_{F}$-probability as }n\to\infty

for all
\epsilon>0
.
Because these conditions are satisfied, we may apply Lemma
1
to obtain that
\sum_{k=b_{J}}^{k_{n}}X_{nk}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}\sqrt{\psi}Z\quad\text{as }n\to\infty
under
P_{F}
.
As for the omitted terms, we have
\sum_{k=p+1}^{b_{J}-1}X_{nk}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0
by
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{MP}
\mathrm{)}
;
thus, we also have
S_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}\sqrt{\psi}Z
as
n\to\infty
under
P_{F}
.

4
Multivariate extension

We now show that Theorem
1
permits a multivariate extension.

4.1
Multivariate definitions

In this section, we consider vector-valued arrays
(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk})_{1\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
adapted to
(\mathcal{X}_{nk})_{0\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Each element
\boldsymbol{X}_{nk}
takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
for some
q\geq 2
.
Letting
\boldsymbol{0}_{q}
denote the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, Definition
1
can then be generalized as follows.

Definition 2

(Multivariate lag-
p
martingale difference sequence)
.

Let
p\in\{0,1,\ldots\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k_{n}\geq p+1
.
The sequence
(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk})_{p+1\leq k\leq k_{n}}
is a
multivariate lag-
p
martingale difference sequence
if
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk}|\mathcal{X}_{n,k-p-1})=\boldsymbol{0}_{q}
and
X_{nkj}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(P)
for all
p+1\leq k\leq k_{n}
and
1\leq j\leq q
.

As above, the array version is similar, allowing
n
to range over
\mathbb{N}
.

4.2
Multivariate CLT

For
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, let
\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}
and
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|=\sqrt{\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}
.
For a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix
\mathbf{Q}
, we let
\mathbf{Q}^{1/2}

Diverging lags

We now consider a generalization of Definition

5.1
Diverging lag definition

We generalize Definition
2
as follows.

Definition 3

(Lag martingale difference sequence)
.

Let
n,q,s\in\mathbb{N}
with
k_{n}\geq s
, and let
(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk})_{s\leq k\leq k_{n}}
be a sequence of random variables in
\mathbb{R}^{q}

Then
(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk})_{s\leq k\leq k_{n}}
is a
lag martingale difference sequence
if there exists a sequence
(p_{k})_{s\leq k\leq k_{n}},\,p_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk}|\mathcal{X}_{n,k-p_{k}-1})=\boldsymbol{0}_{q}
,
X_{nkj}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(P)
, and
k-p_{k}-1\geq 0
for all
s\leq k\leq k_{n}
and
1\leq j\leq q
.

As above, martingale difference arrays are defined similarly, allowing
n
to range over
\mathbb{N}
.
We refer to the lag as
diverging
if
p_{k}\to\infty
a
k\to\infty
.
We say that
(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk})_{s\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a lag martingale difference sequence of
order

\gamma\geq 0
if there exists
C>0
such that
p_{k}\leq Ck^{\gamma}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
The lag is

if
(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk})_{s\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
is of order

.

5.2
Blocking sequences

One challenge in generalizing Theorem
2
to diverging lags is developing a suitable blocking scheme.
Proposition
2
demonstrates the existence of such a scheme for sublinear lags.
In Proposition
2
, condition
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{CN}
\mathrm{)}
ensures that the minor blocks comprise a vanishing proportion of the total number of terms,
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{DN}
\mathrm{)}
ensures that each major block is of negligible size compared to the total number of major-block terms, and
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{L}
\mathrm{)}
guarantees that the martingale property eventually holds for the major-block sums.

Proposition 2

(Diverging blocks)
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk})_{s\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a lag martingale difference array with sublinear lag.
Then there exist sequences
(a_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
defining the starting indices of minor and major blocks, respectively, such that
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{D}
\mathrm{)}
and the following conditions hold:

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{CN}
\mathrm{)}

\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}/\sum_{j=1}^{J}(c_{j}+d_{j})\to 0
as
J\to\infty
, where
c_{j}\coloneqq b_{j+1}-a_{j}
and
d_{j}\coloneqq a_{j}-b_{j}
,

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{DN}
\mathrm{)}

\max_{1\leq j\leq J}(d_{j})/\sum_{j=1}^{J}d_{j}\to 0
as
J\to\infty
, and

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{L}
\mathrm{)}

There exists
J\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk}|\mathcal{X}_{n,a_{j-1}-1})=\boldsymbol{0}_{q}
for all
b_{j}\leq k<a_{j}
,
j\geq J
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma\in[0,1)
be the order of
(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk})_{s\leq k\leq k_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
A,B>0
be arbitrary and let

such that
\gamma<\alpha/(1+\beta)
and
\alpha<\beta
.
The following sequences are easily shown to satisfy the above conditions:

a_{j}\coloneqq\left\lceil\sum_{l=1}^{j}\big(lB\big)^{\beta}+\sum_{l=1}^{j-1}\big(lA\big)^{\alpha}\right\rceil+s,\qquad b_{j}\coloneqq\left\lfloor\sum_{l=1}^{j-1}\left\{\big(lB\big)^{\beta}+\big(lA\big)^{\alpha}\right\}\right\rfloor+s.

∎

6
Limiting variance

This section discusses the limiting variance appearing in Theorems
1
,
2
, and
3
.
For generality, we consider the setting of Theorem
3
with limiting variance
\mathbf{\Psi}
.
Condition
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{N}_{q}
\mathrm{)}
gives a summation that converges in probability to
\mathbf{\Psi}
.
Each term in this summation can be expressed as
\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{\Psi}_{nj}|\mathcal{B}_{n,j-1}\right)
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{nj}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=b_{j}}^{a_{j}-1}\Bigg[\Var\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk}|\mathcal{X}_{n,k-p_{k}-1}\right)+\sum_{l=1}^{\min(p_{k},k-b_{j})}\Big\{\Cov\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk},\boldsymbol{X}_{n,k-l}|\mathcal{X}_{n,k-p_{k}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\ +\Cov\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{n,k-l},\boldsymbol{X}_{nk}|\mathcal{X}_{n,k-p_{k}-1}\right)\Big\}\Bigg].

(3)

In applications, it would be convenient to have an expression for the asymptotic variance that involves only expectations of a finite lag length.
Accordingly, we define

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}^{(B)}}\mathbf{\Psi}_{nj},

(4)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=s}^{k_{n}}\Bigg[\Var\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk}|\mathcal{X}_{n,k-p_{k}-1}\right)+\sum_{l=1}^{\min(p_{k},k-s)}\Big\{\Cov\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{nk},\boldsymbol{X}_{n,k-l}|\mathcal{X}_{n,k-p_{k}-1}\right)

\displaystyle\ +\Cov\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{n,k-l},\boldsymbol{X}_{nk}|\mathcal{X}_{n,k-p_{k}-1}\right)\Big\}\Bigg],

(5)

where the second expression,
\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}
, includes the minor-block terms omitted from
\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}
.
Theorem
4
provides conditions under which
\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}\mathbf{\Psi}
as
n\to\infty
.
Below, we let
\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}\coloneqq\|\textrm{vec}(\mathbf{Q})\|
denote the Frobenius norm of a matrix,
\mathbf{Q}
.

Theorem 4

(Asymptotic variance)
.

Assume that the following conditions hold for the array of matrices given as
(\mathbf{\Psi}_{nj})_{1\leq j\leq j_{n}^{(B)},n\in\mathbb{N}}
:

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{CLB}_{F}^{1}
\mathrm{)}

\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}^{(B)}}\mathbb{E}(\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{nj}\|_{F}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{nj}\|_{F}\geq\epsilon\}}|\mathcal{B}_{n,j-1})\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0
as
n\to\infty
for every
\epsilon>0
and

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{SB}_{F}
\mathrm{)}

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{j_{n}^{(B)}}\mathbb{E}\left(\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{nj}\|_{F}\big|\mathcal{B}_{n,j-1}\right)\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in probability.

Then
\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}\mathbf{\Psi}
as
n\to\infty
.

We provide a proof of Theorem
4
in Appendix

.
Corollary
1
provides a condition under which we also have

as

.

Corollary 1

(Simple asymptotic variance)
.

Assume that the omitted terms
\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}
are asymptotically negligible in the sense of the following assumption:

\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{VN}
\mathrm{)}

\|\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}\|_{F}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}0
as

.

\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{p}}{\to}\mathbf{\Psi}

Proof.

Decompose the normed difference as

\left\|\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}-\mathbf{\Psi}\right\|_{F}\leq\left\|\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}-\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}\right\|_{F}+\left\|\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}-\mathbf{\Psi}\right\|_{F}=I_{n}+II_{n}.

, we have

by
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{VN}
\mathrm{)}
and

.
∎

In applications, provided the variance and covariances within the minor blocks are similar in size to those of the major blocks,
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{VN}
\mathrm{)}
will typically hold because the minor blocks comprise a vanishing proportion of the total number of terms.
We generally expect

to outperform
\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}
as an estimator of
\mathbf{\Psi}
because
\mathbf{\Psi}_{n}
omits the variance contribution of the minor blocks, but
\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}
does not.

7.1
Setup

We consider a simplified setting with a single experimental unit representing, for example, a single individual.
The individual is assigned a binary treatment
A_{t}\in\{0,1\}
at each time point
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\}
for

.
From

(

Associated with each time point, we also have real-valued potential outcomes

.
We denote the outcomes up to time point

as
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}(\boldsymbol{A}_{t})
.
We denote the full set of potential outcomes across all possible treatment paths as
\mathbf{Y}\coloneqq(Y_{t}(\boldsymbol{a}_{t}))_{\boldsymbol{a}_{t}\in\{0,1\}^{t},t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T\}}
.
As in
Bojinov et al., 2021
and
Bojinov and Shephard, 2019
, we assume that the treatments are sequentially randomized.

Assumption 1

(Sequential randomization)
.

For all
t
,
a_{t}
, and
\boldsymbol{a}_{t-1}
, we have

\Pr(A_{t}=a_{t}|\boldsymbol{A}_{t-1}=\boldsymbol{a}_{t-1},\mathbf{Y})=\Pr\left\{A_{t}=a_{t}|\boldsymbol{A}_{t-1}=\boldsymbol{a}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{a}_{t-1})\right\}.

Below, we use colon notation to denote the value of a variable between certain time points; for example,
\boldsymbol{A}_{4:6}\coloneqq(A_{4},A_{5},A_{6})
.
With this notation, we denote the assignment probabilities as follows.

Definition 4

(Assignment probabilities)
.

Let
p\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}
and
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T-p\}
.
For all
\boldsymbol{a}_{t:(t+p)}
, define

p_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{t:(t+p)};\boldsymbol{A}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{a}_{t-1})\right)\coloneqq\Pr\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{t:(t+p)}=\boldsymbol{a}_{t:(t+p)}|\boldsymbol{A}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{a}_{t-1})\right).

For brevity, we write
P_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{t:(t+p)}\right)\coloneqq p_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{t:(t+p)};\boldsymbol{A}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{Y}_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{a}_{t-1})\right)
.
We further assume that the assignment probabilities for the observed treatment path are known.

Assumption 2

(Known assignment probabilities)
.

For all
p\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}
and
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,T-p\}
, the quantity
P_{t}(\boldsymbol{A}_{t:(t+p)})
is known.

We define a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(\mathbf{Y},\boldsymbol{A}_{t})
is the
\sigma
-field generated by
\mathbf{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{t}
.
Because
\mathbf{Y}
is used to generate every
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, the setup and corresponding statistical procedures effectively fix
\mathbf{Y}
at its realized value.

7.2
Causal estimand

Except where noted, the remainder of this section focuses on the special case of
p=1
and the following lag-
1
causal estimand.

Definition 5

(Lag-
1
causal effect)
.

For
t\in\{2,3,\ldots,T\}
, the lag-
1
causal effect is

\tau_{t}\coloneqq\frac{\left\{Y_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},1,1\right)-Y_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},0,1\right)\right\}}{2}+\frac{\left\{Y_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},1,0\right)-Y_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},0,0\right)\right\}}{2}.

The lag-
1
causal effect,
\tau_{t}
, quantifies the effect of treatment
A_{t-1}
on the outcome at the next time point,
Y_{t}
, averaging over
A_{t}=0
and
A_{t}=1
with equal probability.
Our statistical goal is to estimate an average of these lag-
1
causal effects over time.

Definition 6

(Average lag-
1
causal effect)
.

The average lag-
1
causal effect is

\tau\coloneqq\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\tau_{t}.

Definitions
5
and
6
correspond with Definition 2 in
Bojinov and Shephard, 2019
with
p=1
and uniform weights.
The estimand
\tau
describes the degree to which treatment effects persist across two consecutive time periods, averaged over time.
BRS show that
\tau_{t}
and
\tau
can be unbiasedly estimated as

\hat{\tau}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{(-1)^{1-A_{t-1}}Y_{t}}{2P_{t-1}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{(t-1):t}\right)},\quad\hat{\tau}\coloneqq\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{\tau}_{t}.

(6)

To study the properties of their general estimators for
p\geq 0
, BRS define a quantity
U_{t}\coloneqq\hat{\tau}_{t+p}-\tau_{t+p}
and show that
\mathbb{E}(U_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=0
;
however, this definition has the disadvantage that
U_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t+p}
-measurable but not
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable unless
p=0
(in Section
1
,
\mathcal{H}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{t+p}
).
Hence, the classical martingale CLT applies only if
p=0
because
\mathbb{E}(U_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t+p-1})\neq 0
in general.
For clarity, we instead define
W_{t}\coloneqq\hat{\tau}_{t}-\tau_{t}
, which is easily seen to be
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable with
\mathbb{E}(W_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-p-1})=0
.
BRS’s assumption further imply that
W_{t}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(P)
; thus,
(W_{t})_{p+1\leq t\leq T}
is a lag-
p
martingale difference sequence adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
.

To apply Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
with
p=1
, we define the arrays
(X_{nt})_{2\leq t\leq n,n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\mathcal{X}_{nt})_{0\leq t\leq n,n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
X_{nt}\coloneqq n^{-1/2}W_{t}
and
\mathcal{X}_{nt}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{t}
, and we assume that
\mathbf{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{T}
are embedded in an asymptotic regime under which the assumptions of Theorem
1
and Corollary
1
are satisfied.
In particular, this will generally require that
\min\left\{P_{t}(A_{t}),1-P_{t}(A_{t})\right\}
is bounded away from zero (or approaches zero sufficiently slowly); otherwise, the conditional variances may increase without bound.
Combining the
\mathcal{G}
-stable convergence of Theorem
1
with Corollary
1
, we have

\frac{S_{n}}{\sqrt{\bar{\psi}_{n}}\,}\to Z\quad\mathcal{G}\text{-mixing as }n\to\infty,

(7)

where
Z
is standard Gaussian and
\bar{\psi}_{n}
is a scalar version of
\bar{\mathbf{\Psi}}_{n}
defined in (
5
).
This result enables asymptotic Gaussian inference for
\tau_{n}
even though
\bar{\psi}_{n}
converges in probability to a random variable,
\psi
.

In this application,
\bar{\psi}_{n}
is not computable from the observed data because it depends on unobserved potential outcomes.
In their work, BRS address this challenge by proposing an estimable variance bound.
A similar strategy could be applied in our setting; though, we would need to bound the sum of variance and covariance terms defining
\bar{\psi}_{n}
.
Because our primary goal is to validate the theoretical results of Sections
3
–
6
, our simulation study will use the ‘oracle’ variance estimator,
\bar{\psi}
(we omit the subscript
n
in the simulation results).
Future methodological work in this area could address the additional statistical challenge of deriving an estimable bound for
\bar{\psi}
.

We proceed by deriving the terms needed to compute
\bar{\psi}
in the simulation study.
In the expressions that follow,
p_{t}(a_{t};\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},a,\mathbf{Y})\coloneqq\Pr(A_{t}=a_{t}|\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},A_{t-1}=a,\mathbf{Y})
as in Definition
4
.
The variance
\Var\left(W_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-2}\right)
is

\displaystyle\frac{P_{t-1}(0)P_{t-1}(1)}{4}\left\{\frac{\sum_{a=0}^{1}Y_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},1,a\right)}{P_{t-1}(1)}+\frac{\sum_{a=0}^{1}Y_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},0,a\right)}{P_{t-1}(0)}\right\}^{2}+

\displaystyle\sum_{a=0}^{1}\left\{\frac{Y_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},a,1\right)}{p_{t}\left(1;\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},a,\mathbf{Y}\right)}-\frac{Y_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},a,0\right)}{p_{t}\left(0;\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},a,\mathbf{Y}\right)}\right\}^{2}\frac{p_{t}\left(1;\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},a,\mathbf{Y}\right)p_{t}\left(0;\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},a,\mathbf{Y}\right)}{4P_{t-1}(a)}.

The required covariance term,
\Cov\left(W_{t-1},W_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-2}\right)
, is

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{1-A_{t-2}}}{4P_{t-2}\left(A_{t-2}\right)}\sum_{a_{t-1}=0}^{1}\frac{(-1)^{1-a_{t-1}}Y_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},a_{t-1})}{P_{t-1}\left(a_{t-1}\right)}\sum_{a_{t}=0}^{1}Y_{t}(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},a_{t-1},a_{t})

(8)

\displaystyle-\tau_{t}\frac{(-1)^{1-A_{t-2}}}{2P_{t-2}(A_{t-2})}\sum_{a=0}^{1}Y_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{A}_{t-2},a).

7.3.1

8
Conclusion

This paper introduces lag martingales and proves several CLTs for their partial sums.
Our CLTs guarantee stable convergence to a scale mixture of Gaussian random variables.
Our most general CLT (Theorem
3
) applies to vector-valued processes with sublinear lag lengths.
In addition to the CLTs, we also derive a convenient form for the asymptotic variance in Corollary
1
.

This work was motivated by the dynamic causal inference method of BRS
(
Bojinov et al., 2021
;
Bojinov and Shephard, 2019
)
.
Our theoretical results enable application of their framework with lag length
p\geq 1
.
The stability of our CLTs explicitly allows for a random limiting variance—a possibility that is difficult to rule out in practice.
We illustrate the application of our theoretical results to the BRS framework in Section
7
and provide a simulation study to empirically verify their implications.
Future methodological work in this area could leverage our theoretical results to generalize the BRS framework in various ways, including to estimate effect moderation and allow for dependence across units in the treatment assignment process.

The literature on martingale CLTs includes a wide range of sufficient conditions
(
Häusler and Luschgy, 2015
;
Häusler and Luschgy, 2024
)
.
In the present work, we have employed various versions of the nesting
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{N}
\mathrm{)}
and conditional Lindeberg
\mathrm{(}
\mathrm{CLB}
\mathrm{)}
assumptions because other authors have argued that these assumptions produce the “most common and useful” CLT
(
Häusler and Luschgy, 2024
, p. 4)
and, indeed, in our application setting, these assumptions lead to a convenient expression for the asymptotic variance.
Future work could develop additional sufficient conditions for our CLTs and, perhaps, demonstrate their application to other stochastic processes satisfying the lag martingale property, such as moving-average processes.
