Long-read target enrichment sequencing for rAAV integration site analysis in engineered clones with targeted viral insertion
Authors: Erica Coratella, Myriam Lemmens, Mevion Oertli, Yoann Gilbart, Kristian Davidsen, Lara Bontadelli, Rebecca Bohnert, Joyce Rigal, Jennifer Bian, Tara Alpert, Martin Beibel, Vera Ruda, Megumi Onishi-Seebacher, Ulrike Naumann, Azeddine Elhajouji, Alex Odermatt, Eloise Hudry, Silvana Libertini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntegration site analysis (ISA) plays a critical role in the genotoxicity assessment of recombinant adeno-associated viruses (rAAVs); however, ISA is challenged by the low integration rate, frequent vector genome rearrangements and the absence of well-characterized controls. Here, we generated 34 MCF10A-derived clones harboring recombinant adeno-associated viruses (rAAV) insertions at CRISPR-Cas9-induced double-strand breaks as cellular reference material and used these cell lines to evaluate long-read target enrichment sequencing (LR-TES) for rAAV ISA. LR-TES findings were complemented by droplet digital PCR quantification of vector copy number, long-range PCR analysis of on-target integration, and whole-genome sequencing on selected clones. Dilution experiments established a limit of detection of 1% under the tested conditions. LR-TES enabled genome-wide identification of vector-host junctions and structural characterization of integrated vector genomes, detecting targeted and non-targeted insertions, genomic deletions, and extensive vector restructuring across the clone panel. Only ∼29% of the integrated vector payload remained intact, while the observed truncations, rearrangements, or concatemers highlighted the structural complexity that ISA must resolve in rAAV settings. Together, the engineered clonal controls and optimized LR-TES provide reference material and a technical framework supporting rAAV ISA in safety evaluation, particularly when high structural resolution and specificity are needed to interpret predominant integration events.Keywords: MT: Clinical Applications, rAAV, integration site analysis, insertional mutagenesis, target enrichment sequencing, limit of detection, stable clones

Introduction

Over the past decades, gene therapy has achieved remarkable clinical advancements and offered new opportunities for the treatment of numerous pathological conditions, with viral vectors emerging as highly effective tools for the delivery of therapeutic genes.1,2 Recombinant adeno-associated viruses (rAAV) have demonstrated a favorable safety profile for gene transfer, arising from their low pathogenicity,2 minimal immunogenicity,2 and genotoxicity, the latter attributed to their episomal nature,3 which enables sustained transgene expression in non-proliferative tissues.4 Despite being regarded as non-integrative vectors, rAAVs can integrate into the host genome at low frequencies, ranging from approximately 10-3 to 10-5 integration sites (ISs) per cell. This integration rate has been reported in liver and muscle of non-human primates (NHPs) treated with a dose of 5 × 1012 viral genomes (vg)/kg resulting in a vector copy number (VCN) of 0.3–36 viral genomes/diploid genomes (vg/dg),5 as well as in muscle biopsies from human patients receiving a dose of 1 × 1012 vg/kg, with VCN values ranging between 0.7 and 84 vg/dg.5,6 Integration events might theoretically lead to insertional oncogenesis, defined as the activation of oncogenic pathways caused by viral integration into tumor-related genes.7 To date, the only documented instance of AAV-linked tumorigenesis is the onset of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in rAAV-treated neonatal mice8 and in adult mice with predisposing factors,9 following viral insertion in the Rian (RNA imprinted and accumulated in nucleus) locus.10 No tumor development has been related to rAAV administration in other preclinical models, including cats,11 dogs,12 and NHPs.13 Secondary malignancies have been reported in eight rAAV-treated patients across more than 140 clinical trials (clinicaltrials.gov); molecular analysis found no evidence of vector integration contributing to tumorigenesis in seven cases,3 while the remaining case is currently under investigation (https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/regenxbio-announces-regulatory-update-on-ultra-rare-mps-programs-302672061.html).

The potential genotoxic risk associated with unintended viral insertion into the human genome remains a critical concern; therefore, an appropriate method to accurately characterize the frequency and the genomic location of rAAV ISs is crucial to properly assess such risk, not only to support safe gene therapy development, but also to retrospectively investigate potential insertional oncogenesis cases when tumors arise in treated animals or patients.3

Several ISA methods have been developed to enrich vector-host junctions and subsequently sequence them to determine precise IS genomic locations. Common approaches include linear amplification-mediated PCR (LAM-PCR), ligation-mediated PCR (LM-PCR), and sheared-extension primer tag selection (S-EPTS) LM-PCR.14,15,16,17 These methods enrich ISs by priming off the viral sequence and have been successfully applied to lentiviruses and retroviruses, which typically integrate their genome in a structurally intact form. In contrast, rAAV integrations are frequently characterized by intermolecular recombination, structural rearrangement, and inverted terminal repeat (ITR) loss.18,19,20,21 Since primer-based enrichment methods are inherently biased toward insertion events containing primer binding sites, target enrichment sequencing (TES) using hybridization probes spanning the entire rAAV genome has emerged as a more appropriate alternative.22 This approach enables unbiased capture of vector-host junctions regardless of structural alterations of the viral genome. In a direct comparison, TES outperformed S-EPTS/LM-PCR by providing broader vector coverage and identifying a higher ISs number in tissues collected from rAAV-treated cynomolgus monkeys and mice.23

TES can be coupled with either short-read or long-read sequencing platforms, each offering distinct advantages and limitations. While short-read sequencing achieves high coverage with low input, it does not allow for the resolution of complex vector rearrangements or the accurate mapping of repetitive regions.24,25 In contrast, long-read sequencing enables higher-confidence detection of AAV-host genome junctions and provides more insights into the integrated vector structure, albeit with lower coverage.24,25 A comparison and cross-validation of these sequencing approaches utilizing liver samples from AAV-treated NHPs confirmed these complementary strengths: short-read TES identified more ISs, whereas long-read TES offered an informative description of the viral recombination pattern.24

To further advance the application of TES for rAAV ISA, well-characterized controls are essential for evaluating method performance. While artificial spike-in DNA has been used as a technical positive ref.24, cellular models with defined ISs have been lacking. In this study, we generated clonal cell populations with precisely mapped rAAV ISs to assess the performance of TES combined with long-read sequencing for comprehensive characterization of viral integration profiles and integrated vector structures. These clones represent a valuable tool to optimize the experimental LR-TES workflow and to develop a dedicated bioinformatic pipeline. Furthermore, the established cell lines were used to determine the limit of detection (LOD) of the method, by using decreasing amounts of genomic DNA from individually diluted or mixed clones, to mimic a clinical analysis challenged by rare rAAV integration events and the concurrent presence of distinct clonal populations. Finally, the sensitivity of LR-TES was benchmarked against a TES workflow coupled with short-read sequencing for a more comprehensive evaluation.

Results

Generation of stable cellular clones with targeted rAAV integration

To evaluate the performance of LR-TES in detecting rAAV ISs, appropriate controls with viral insertions at defined genomic locations and abundance are essential. Given that rAAV insertion events preferentially occur at pre-existing double-stranded breaks (DSBs),16 site-specific viral integration was promoted by inducing targeted dsDNA breaks via CRISPR-Cas9 in the human MCF10A cell line simultaneously transduced with an eGFP-expressing rAAV (Figure 1A, plasmid map is shown in Figure S1). Gene editing was directed to either the maternally expressed 8 (MEG8) locus or to the protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 12 (PTPN12) gene. Both the MEG8- and PTPN12-targeted clones were initially generated as controls for in vitro transformation assays,26 and subsequently leveraged as well-characterized clonal reference material for ISA. While gene editing increases the likelihood of targeted rAAV integration,18,27 viral insertion does not necessarily occur at every induced dsDNA break. To isolate clonal cell populations stably carrying the integrated vector, single-cell cloning was performed on CRISPR-Cas9-edited rAAV-transduced MCF10A cells (Figures 1A and 1B). To differentiate between the integrated and episomal forms of the virus, eGFP expression in the host cells was monitored over time. Clones retaining the fluorescence signal after two months of passaging likely harbored a stably integrated rAAV (Figure 1C), as episomal DNA would be progressively diluted during cell divisions. In total, 34 cell lines, 17 PTPN12-edited clones and 17 MEG8-edited clones (hereafter, respectively, designated “PC” and “MC”), were selected based on persistent transgene expression as observed by fluorescence microscopy. After two further months in culture, eGFP levels were re-assessed by western blot analysis (Figures S2A and S2B).

Characterization of clones by ddPCR and qualitative PCR

Viral vector copy number (VCN) was determined by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), employing assays designed for either bGHpA or eGFP. The bGHpA element, common to most rAAV vectors, is widely used for VCN measurement. The eGFP gene was also measured as the expression of the reporter gene served for clone selection and, consequently, a positive signal was expected in all selected cell lines (Figures 2A and 2B). In the presence of a full-length viral vector, equivalent levels of bGHpA and eGFP should be detected. Assuming exclusive on-target integration, the ddPCR analysis on the stable clones should enable the approximate distinction between monoallelic and biallelic insertions, with a VCN of 1 and 2 viral genomes/diploid genome (vg/dg), respectively.

In case of exclusive on-target rAAV insertion, heterozygous clones (VCN of 1 vg/dg) should display two bands, one corresponding to the unedited wt allele, and a longer band reflecting the insertion of the viral vector. In contrast, homozygous clones (VCN of 2 vg/dg) would exhibit one band of increased size.

For each target, some clones presented VCNs deviating from the theoretical integer values of 0, 1, or 2 vg/dg (Figures 2A and 2B). This discrepancy might indicate a non-monoclonal population, potentially caused by the unintended expansion of two distinct cells during the single-cell cloning. Alternatively, intra-clonal heterogeneity could have arisen due to partial clearance of the inserted AAV vector through the cell expansion process. Out of 34 clones, 14 showed a VCN of 1 vg/dg for both bGHpA and eGFP while inconsistent VCN values between bGHpA and eGFP were observed in 3 samples (MC7, PC16, and PC17) (Figures 2A and 2B). Surprisingly, although transgene expression was used as a selection criterion, no copies of the transgene nor bGHpA were detected in two PTPN12-edited cell lines (Figure 2B). These discrepancies are likely due to the inherent instability of the rAAV genome, which caused genetic alterations hindering primer or probe binding during ddPCR analysis, leading to false-negative outcomes despite the presence of the viral genome.

While VCN quantification is valuable for measuring viral copies, it does not confirm rAAV integration into PTPN12 and MEG8, as it provides no information about the genomic location of the viral ISs. Targeted rAAV integration was initially assessed by performing long-range PCR (LR-PCR) with primers flanking the expected cleavage site of the Cas9 nuclease within the PTPN12 and MEG8 loci. An increase in amplicon length indicates successful targeted insertion. The viral vector itself is ∼2500 bp in length; thus, if fully integrated, the resulting amplicon is expected to measure approximately 3000 bp (Figure 2C). The amplification of the edited locus confirmed successful rAAV insertion into PTPN12 and MEG8. However, some clones (MC1, MC6, MC9, MC13, MC15, PC1, PC2, PC5, PC6, PC10, PC11, PC15, and PC17) exhibited amplicons smaller than the anticipated size, suggesting the insertion of a truncated vector genome (Figures 2D and 2E). A subset of samples (MC1, PC3, and PC15) displayed a lower molecular weight band than the parental genomic locus, which is likely due to a deletion within the target region upon Cas9-mediated editing (Figures 2D and 2E). Interestingly, some clones (MC13, PC2, PC3, PC5, PC9, PC10, and PC15) showed multiple bands, which may indicate partial clearance of the viral vector over time, or the presence of a mixed clonal population consisting of cells with distinct integration patterns (Figures 2D and 2E).

In cases of viral integration in the targeted loci, the VCN and LR-PCR results are expected to correlate. Nonetheless, discrepancies emerged for most clones. Moreover, while ddPCR confirmed the presence of viral genome copies in nearly all clones, 9 samples only showed amplicons matching the length of the parental locus of either MEG8 or PTPN12 by LR-PCR. This outcome could be attributed to either viral integration or DNA repair processes, both of which may affect the integrity of primer binding sites around the desired IS. Alternatively, rAAV may have integrated into loci distant from PTPN12 and MEG8, rendering them undetectable to our targeted LR-PCR approach.

Integration site analysis by long-read targeted enrichment sequencing

Next, the generated cell lines were comprehensively characterized by performing ISA through TES followed by PacBio SMRT (single molecule, real-time) long-read sequencing. For TES, probes targeting the entire rAAV genome are employed to compensate for potential primer binding failures in truncated or rearranged vector regions, a known limitation of primer-based ISA methods such as LM-PCR.23 Thus, TES enables the capture of ISs despite viral deletions or structural alterations. Subsequently, long-read sequencing is performed to generate reads containing larger portions of the integrated vector or, ideally, reads covering the whole integration event to resolve the detailed architecture of the inserted rAAV genome.

The TES workflow (Figure 3) begins with shearing genomic DNA into fragments longer than the vector, increasing the likelihood that each fragment contains genomic sequences flanking both sides of an rAAV IS. After pre-amplification, fragments containing viral sequences are enriched by probe hybridization, further amplified, and sequenced using PacBio. Bioinformatic analysis is then used to identify vector-host junctions, map the integration sites, and characterize structural variations in the integrated vector genome.

Biotinylated probes tiled 4-fold across the entire 2.5 kbp rAAV genome are used to capture virus-containing fragments, which are subsequently amplified by post-capture PCR (25 cycles) and sequenced through PacBio SMRT. To investigate whether unintended viral insertion may have resulted from CRISPR-Cas9 off-target editing, the computational tool Cas-OFFinder (http://www.rgenome.net/cas-offinder/) was employed to predict potential off-target cut sites based on the sgRNAs targeting PTPN12 and MEG8. Remarkably, none of the unintended ISs were predicted as off-target cut sites (Tables S2 and S3), therefore suggesting that random integrations of the vector payload also occurred in the host genome. The observed integrations are consistent with prior reports showing that rAAV vector genomes can be captured at pre-existing double-strand breaks and subsequently resolved by host end-joining pathways.27,28,29

Following editing of PTPN12 or MEG8, rAAV was predominantly integrated into a single allele; however, clones carrying the viral vector in both alleles were also identified. rAAV integrations at the targeted loci largely clustered around the Cas9-induced breakpoints. Specifically, all PTPN12 insertions occurred within 16 bp of the predicted cleavage site, whereas in MEG8, despite proximal clustering of most insertions, some were detected up to 1′196 bp from the genomic location of the Cas9 cut (Figures 4C and 4D). Translocations, inversions and deletions in the chromosomal DNA were observed, mostly a few base pairs long within the targeted loci (Figure 4E). However, larger deletions up to hundreds of kilobase pairs were detected at non-targeted ISs (Figure 4F).

To assess spontaneous rAAV integration patterns, MCF10A cells were also transduced in the absence of CRISPR-Cas9-mediated editing with the same AAV vector and multiplicity of infection (MOI) used for clone generation. Genomic DNA was extracted 4 days post-transduction, and the VCN was determined at ∼20 copies/cell by ddPCR (data not shown). The sample was subsequently processed in duplicate for LR-TES. In this condition, integration events were randomly distributed across the genome, and a total of 62 unique ISs were detected (Figures S3A–S3C; Table S4).

Assessment of vector integrity by TES

In addition to locations of ISs, TES analysis provided detailed information on the structure of the inserted rAAV and on the integrity of the human genome flanking the integrated vector.

Analysis of sequencing data revealed that the inserted viral genome ranged from 71 to 9′355 bp, whereas the full-length vector measured 2′521 bp (Figure 5A). Most integrations were truncated, with small deletions in the ITR sequence also observed in vectors presenting all viral features; in addition, inserted concatemers and vectors with duplicated regions were detected (Figure 5B). Interestingly, all truncated integration events retained one ITR, apart from a single insertion in one PTPN12 clone (Figure 5C). The most common configuration was the integration of one ITR alongside the CMV promoter (Figure 5C). Of note, in some instances, sequences from the plasmid backbone used for virus production were also found incorporated into the host chromosomes (Figures 5B–5D). Moreover, even though clones were selected based on eGFP expression, thus introducing a bias in the evaluation of integrated features, the transgene was not the most represented genomic region within the integration sites (Figure 5D).

Overall, there was a good correlation between the copies of bGHpA and eGFP detected by TES and measured by ddPCR. However, discrepancies in the quantification of these viral genomic regions were observed for eleven clones (∼30%) and could be only partially explained by examining the integrated vector; in three of these samples, copies of the transgene or bGHpA could not be quantified due to truncations or mismatches interfering with the binding of primers and probes during the ddPCR analysis. For the remaining clones, the observed inconsistencies may be due to the presence of mixed populations or subclonal variation. Estimation of LR-TES limit of detection

The LOD of LR-TES was assessed by diluting genomic DNA extracted from three representative clones (MC11, PC10, and PC14, Figure 6A) with DNA from non-transduced MCF10A cells to obtain clone-derived DNA fractions of 10%, 1%, 0.1%, 0.05%, and 0.01%. This approach simulates limiting clone dilutions to determine the lowest abundance at which integration sites can still be reliably detected. Genomic DNA from non-transduced cells was included as a negative control, and each clone was diluted once, followed by DNA shearing and subsequent splitting in aliquots to generate technical replicates.

(B–D) Read count of the ISs detected by LR-TES and confirmed by WGS across several dilutions for three different clones: (B) MC11, (C) PC14, (D) PC10. For each dilution, all values were transformed to a pseudo-log scale for display. No ISs were detected in the pure genomic DNA from wt MCF10A.

Clone PC14 was chosen for the LOD experiments since it exhibits an almost full-length, monoallelic vector integration in PTPN12 with no detected non-targeted integrations. Its clean insertion profile was concordant with the VCN measured by ddPCR (1 for both eGFP and bGHpA) and the amplification of the edited locus by LR-PCR, showing one unedited amplicon and a larger product corresponding to the rAAV integration site. Additionally, two clones harboring targeted and non-targeted integrations with varying sizes were selected to evaluate the impact of different integrated vector lengths on the LOD. Clone PC10 carries an almost complete viral genome sequence in PTPN12 and the WPRE sequence in chromosome 16 with a lower frequency in comparison to the targeted integration site. Clone MC11 harbors the smallest integration (71 bp) among all the tested clones, corresponding to a partial ITR inserted in the MEG8 locus. Furthermore, this clone presents an almost full-length vector integration in chromosome 5. Similarly to PC14, the VCN quantification of bGHpA and eGFP in both PC10 and MC11 clones aligned with the rAAV integration profile, and the amplification of PTPN12 and MEG8, respectively, confirmed viral insertion in the targeted loci (Figure 6A).

Viral integration at all expected loci was consistently detected in all replicates of every clone down to the 1% dilution, with the exception of the non-targeted IS of PC10 in chr 16 that failed to be detected in one of the replicates (Figures 6B–6D). This discrepancy is likely due to its lower abundance, as indicated by substantially fewer reads compared to the targeted integration site obtained for the same clone by LR-TES.

To calculate the LOD and confidence intervals (CI), a probabilistic model was applied to describe how the detection probability changes with decreasing clone concentration. A logistic model was used because the outcome is binary (detected or not), and the relationship between detection probability and concentration is appropriately captured by a logistic curve. A mixed-effects structure was included by treating each integration site as a random effect, to account for differences in the likelihood of being detected. For example, smaller integrations consistently yielded fewer reads, likely resulting from reduced hybrid capture efficiency in LR-TES. The LOD was then defined as the concentration at which the predicted detection probability reached 95%, representing the average performance across sites (Figure S4A). Of note, the non-clonal IS of PC10 in chr 16 was excluded for this analysis. A 95% CI was calculated using the delta method, which propagates uncertainty from the model’s fixed effect estimates through the LOD calculation. By applying this model, the LOD of LR-TES was determined to be 1.16% with a CI of 0.3%–4.5%. After estimating the sensitivity of the method by using individually diluted clones, the same three samples (MC11, PC10, and PC14) were mixed to simulate a realistic scenario in which different clonal populations may coexist. Each clone was present at either 10% or 1% in two separate mixtures, with the remaining genomic DNA derived from untransduced MCF10A cells. This experiment enabled us to assess whether increased sample complexity compromises the efficiency of IS detection. Importantly, the calculated LOD of 1% was preserved in the presence of multiple insertion events derived from distinct clones (Figure S4B).

Given the higher sensitivity reported for ISA methods employing short-read sequencing,24,25 a direct comparison between sequencing approaches was also performed. The LOD of TES coupled with short-read sequencing was assessed with the PC14 clone, using the same dilution series as described above (100%, 1%, 0.1%, 0.05%, 0.01%, and 0%). The single IS in PTPN12 was consistently detected in both technical duplicates down to 0.1% PC14, corresponding to a 10-fold improved LOD compared to LR-TES (Figure S4C).

Discussion

Various techniques have been developed to perform ISA, initially for integrative vectors such as lentiviruses and retroviruses, and later adapted for rAAVs,14,15,16,17,30 for which the analysis is far more complex due to the high degree of intermolecular recombination events and the much lower integration rate.5

Multiple ISA workflows, including primer-based approaches such as LAM-PCR or LM-PCR and probe-based TES, have been applied across different sample types and both nonclinical and clinical settings, thus precluding a definitive conclusion about the optimal technique for characterizing rAAV insertion profiles. Moreover, while engineered spike-in DNA strings have been used for method evaluation,15,31 the lack of cellular samples with well-characterized ISs at defined frequencies has hindered robust benchmarking. Against this background, the primary aim of the present study was methodological, namely to generate clonal cell populations harboring targeted viral insertions and to assess LR-TES for the analysis of rAAV integrations under controlled conditions.

VCN assessment using ddPCR revealed discrepancies in the bGHpA and GFP quantification in three generated cell lines, while no viral genome copies were detected in two clones, likely due to the loss of primer/probe binding sites within the integrated vector. These findings have important implications for clinical investigations of malignancies arising in rAAV-treated patients. In these instances, ISA is usually conducted only upon measurement of viral genome copies in tumor samples3; however, these values may be underestimated if assay targets are disrupted. Our findings underscore the necessity of employing multiple assays targeting different rAAV genomic regions to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the presence of the virus in the tumor cells.

Long-range PCR amplification of the genomic region flanking the intended IS confirmed targeted rAAV insertion in most samples. By contrast, LR-PCR did not yield the expected on-target amplicon in some clones in which viral DNA was detected by ddPCR, suggesting the presence of non-targeted integration events. Finally, long-read TES identified both intended and unintended integrations, providing detailed visualization of concatemers and enhanced resolution of vector structural organization across all the generated cell lines.

Whereas most of the cell lines exhibited AAV integration in the genes edited via CRISPR-Cas9, ∼32% of the samples displayed viral insertions at alternative genomic loci. This observation holds clinical relevance for gene therapies combining rAAV vectors with nucleases, as it demonstrates that promoting AAV integration at defined genomic loci (a strategy also referred to as “safe-harbor”) through gene-editing tools does not prevent random viral chromosomal insertions.32

Both targeted and non-targeted integration often exhibited viral genome truncations or rearrangements. However, the observed frequency of structural changes in the integrated vector may be underestimated, as the selection of clones based on eGFP expression introduced a bias toward insertion events that preserved promoter and transgene integrity, while excluding clones with transgene loss or transcriptional inactivation. Notably, plasmid backbone insertion was also observed; nonetheless, since no hybridization probes targeted this region, potential integration events involving only the plasmid backbone could have been missed.

Across all the ISs (n = 53), approximately 6% lacked ITRs entirely; only ∼43% of the inserted vectors retained both ITRs, and all of them exhibited truncations or mutations. These findings are consistent with a previous study employing TES for rAAV ISA, where 92.9% of reads containing host-virus junctions included at least one ITR, 85.2% contained two or more and the majority of ITRs were not intact.31 These data support probe-based enrichment of viral integrations for vectors subjected to extensive genomic rearrangements, since primer-based workflows would likely miss events where primer-binding regions are lost or mutated.

While TES improves recovery of ISs with rearranged vectors, the workflow includes multiple PCR steps that can introduce artifacts such as chimeric molecules due to template switching during amplification.33 These chimeras are more prone to form when longer fragments are amplified,34 which might lead to the artificial generation of host-vector junctions in the context of ISA. However, PCR artifacts are unlikely to affect our results, as for each clone multiple original molecules and sequencing reads are generated, allowing the reliable exclusion of false-positive ISs.

The LOD of LR-TES, estimated to be as low as 1% in both individual and mixed clone settings, appears suboptimal compared to that of S-EPTS/LM-PCR for ISA on lentiviruses, which was reported at 0.05%.35 Besides the differences in the ISA technique, this discrepancy might also be attributable to the nature of lentiviral vectors, which integrate as fully intact proviruses, making them inherently easier to detect. Additionally, in rAAV settings, episomal viral genomes may persist and could affect the identification of rare integration events by competing for probe binding during hybridization, even though the LR-TES workflow includes a size selection step intended to deplete episomal AAVs. Nonetheless, the sensitivity achieved by LR-TES is adequate for investigating the potential contribution of rAAVs to secondary malignancies, as a causative insertion would be expected to become enriched through tumor-associated clonal expansion and therefore exceed the detection threshold. In this context, long-read sequencing provides additional value by resolving the vector-host junction and the structure of the integrated vector, which can provide mechanistic insight into insertional oncogenesis. Therefore, LR-TES may be well suited for clinical investigations aimed at elucidating molecular mechanisms when rAAV-driven clonality is suspected.

It should be noted that the sensitivity assessment is dependent on parameters such as the amount of input DNA, sequencing depth, and read counts; therefore, the LOD determined here reflects the specific experimental conditions used in this study. Furthermore, hybridization efficiency might vary depending on the probe sequences, leading to the preferential capture of viral regions with higher binding affinity. Notably, the IS in PC14 and the non-targeted IS in MC11 were recovered in one out of three replicates down to the 0.05% sample, suggesting that employing technical replicates might improve the sensitivity of the method. Despite being shorter than the 120 bp hybridization probes, a 71-bp-long IS was recovered at 1% clone abundance, albeit with reduced efficiency. This finding indicates that probe length is not a limiting factor, although shorter insertions are more challenging to detect.

Short-read sequencing further increased the sensitivity of TES, enabling consistent detection down to 0.1%, while also offering lower costs and greater sequencing depth, in line with previous studies.24,25 Importantly, optimization of TES coupled with short-read sequencing would represent a significant advancement for analysis of clinical samples preserved as formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens, where partially degraded DNA would make long-read sequencing unfeasible.22

In conclusion, this study provided engineered clonal controls for rAAV ISA, which were employed to evaluate the performance of an in-house-developed LR-TES workflow, including wet-lab and bioinformatic pipeline components. Using these controls, we estimated an LOD of ∼1% for LR-TES and showed that long reads enable structural characterization of integrated vector genomes. Collectively, these results support LR-TES as a valuable approach for rAAV safety assessments when structural resolution and interpretation of predominant events are priorities.

Materials and methods

MCF10A cell culture

MCF10A cells (#CRL2254, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in DMEM/F12 (#11330, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Zurich, Switzerland) supplemented with 5% horse serum (#16050122, Gibco, Zurich, Switzerland), 20 ng/mL EGF (#PHG0311, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Zurich, Switzerland), 250 ng/mL cortisol (#H-0888, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 100 ng/mL cholera toxin (#C8052, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), 10 μg/mL insulin (#1882, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) at 37°C under 5% CO2.

Generation of stable clones

An RNP complex containing the Cas9 nuclease (IDT, S. pyogenes Cas9 Nuclease V3; #1081058) with a cr:trRNA targeting MEG8 (5′-TGTGCCAACTCACGGTGCGGTGG-3′) or PTPN12 (5′- GATATGGGAGTATAATGTTG-3′) was electroporated into MCF10A cells. To prepare the cr:trRNA complex, 100 μM stock solutions of crRNA (from IDT, Leuven, Belgium) and tracrRNA (#1072534, IDT, Leuven, Belgium) were mixed in nuclease-free duplex buffer (#11010301, IDT, Leuven, Belgium) to achieve a final concentration of 44 μM, then heated at 95°C for 5 min. A 22 μM solution of the cr:trRNA complex was combined with 18 μM Cas9 nuclease (S. pyogenes Cas9 Nuclease V3, #1081058, IDT, Leuven, Belgium). ∼150,000 MCF10A cells were electroporated using the NEON transfection system (Invitrogen, Zurich, Switzerland) in Buffer R at 1700 V, with a single 20 ms pulse and 10-μL tips. Immediately after electroporation, cells were seeded, and a recombinant AAV6 virus expressing eGFP (pAAV[Exp]-CMV> EGFP:WPRE, VB010000-9394npt, Vector Builder, Heidelberg, Germany), was added to the well at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 105 viral particles/cell. After one week, single-cell cloning by limiting dilution was performed. Both MEG8- and PTPN12-edited cells were diluted to final concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 2 cells per 100 μL. Each of those cell suspensions was seeded into two 96-well plates at 100 μL per well for a total of eight 96-well plates per target. eGFP-positive clones were initially selected through fluorescence microscopy and expanded for two months, to ensure complete dilution of episomal rAAV, thereby retaining only clones whose transgene expression resulted from the integrated viral genome. A total of 22 PTPN12-edited clones and 23 MEG8-edited clones were initially expanded. Afterward, 17 clones per group were selected for comprehensive characterization based on persistent transgene expression. The experimental design is summarized in Figure 1A.

VCN quantification by ddPCR

DNA was isolated from MCF10A-derived clones using the Blood and Cell Culture DNA maxi kit (#13362, Qiagen, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland). For each generated cell line, VCN was determined by ddPCR measurement of bGHpA and eGFP normalized to RPP30. Primers and probe sequences are described in Table 1 ddPCR was performed as described by Morath et al.36 with the modification of using a final volume of 20 μL and 10 ng of DNA for each reaction. The PCR was run with the following steps: 10 min at 95°C, 40 cycles of 30 s at 94°C, 1 min at 60°C, and a final extension step for 10 min at 98°C. Droplet reading was performed by using the QX200 Droplet Reader (#186–4003, Bio-Rad, Cressier, Switzerland).

On-target rAAV integration assessment by LR-PCR

Long-range PCRs were performed using primer pairs (from Microsynth, Balgach, Switzerland) designed on a genomic region surrounding the Cas9-induced cut in either the PTPN12 gene (f: 5′- GCATGCTCTGTATTTCTGCCA-3′, r: 5′-TGAGACATCCTAAAAATGGGTCC-3′) or the MEG8 locus (f: 5′- AGAGTTCCTTCGCTTCCTCC-3′, r: 5′-TAAAACTGTGTGCCAAGCCG-3′). For each clone, the primer pair corresponding to the targeted genomic locus was used. PCR reactions were prepared in a final volume of 50 μL, using 100 ng of genomic DNA and 10 μM stock solutions of each primer with the KOD Xtreme Hot Start DNA Polymerase kit (P/N 71975-3, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The PCR was run with the following steps: 2 min at 94°C, 30 cycles of 10 s at 98°C, 1 min at 59°C, and 4 min at 68°C, and a final extension step for 4 min at 68°C. Amplicon size was assessed using the 2200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany) with the Genomic DNA ScreenTape assay (#5067–5365, Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany) and Genomic DNA reagents (#5067–5366, Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Long-read TES

All samples were adjusted to a volume of 200 μL and a concentration of 20 ng/μL prior to downstream processing, and libraries were prepared using 1 μg of DNA. DNA was sheared using Megaruptor 3 (#B06010003, Diagenode, Seraing, Belgium) and its corresponding kit (#E07010003, Diagenode, Seraing, Belgium), aiming for a fragment length of 7–8 kb (kilobase pairs), and subjected to Twist Long-Read Library Preparation and Standard Hyb v2 Enrichment according to the manufacturers’ instructions (DOC-001320 REV 3.0, Twist Bioscience, Frankfurt, Germany). Pre- and post-capture PCR (8 and 25 cycles, respectively) were performed using the KOD Xtreme Hot Start DNA polymerase (#PN 71975-3, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). For the hybridization capture, a Twist custom panel was designed against the viral genomic sequence (Figure S1) excluding the plasmid backbone with 4× Tiling (overlap of two consecutive probes 90 nt) and subsequently used to capture DNA fragments containing the ISs; the panel consists of 82 overlapping probes, each 120 bp in length, tiled 4× across the whole vector sequence. After the preparation of the libraries enriched for the rAAV-containing sequences, PacBio library preparation for long-read sequencing was done using the SMRTbell prep kit 3.0 (#102-182-700, London, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 0.5 million reads were sequenced per sample.

Clone dilution for LOD estimation of LR-TES

After DNA shearing, samples were either split into two aliquots to generate technical duplicates or processed without splitting in an independent experiment, resulting in three replicates per condition, which were subsequently processed as previously described. DNA from each of the three selected clones (MC11, PC10, and PC14) was individually diluted using genomic DNA from wild-type MCF10A as background material to maintain the total DNA input and concentration. A serial 1:10 dilution scheme was applied to obtain 10%, 1%, and 0.1% conditions. From the 0.1% intermediate, a 1:2 dilution was performed to generate the 0.05% condition, which was further diluted 1:5 to obtain the 0.01% condition.

For LOD assessment using mixtures of MC11, PC10, and PC14, genomic DNA from each clone was independently diluted to either 10% or 1% in MCF10A background DNA and subsequently pooled within each condition. This resulted in two final mixtures in which all clones contributed equally at either 10% or 1% of the total DNA input. Libraries were prepared from 1 μg of DNA per replicate, yielding approximately 300,000 reads per sample.

Long-read TES analysis

Demultiplexed high-fidelity (HiFi) PacBio reads were processed by removing Twist Universal Adapter sequences and removing short reads (<1000 bp) using Cutadapt v5.0. The reads were then aligned to the AAV plasmid sequence using Minimap2 v2.29-r1283 with the preset “map-hifi” optimized for PacBio HiFi reads. Additional alignment settings included a chaining score threshold of 25 and a peak alignment score threshold of 25. Reads containing vector sequences were subsequently aligned to the Ensembl Homo sapiens GRCh38 reference genome using Minimap2 with the same “map-hifi” preset. PCR chimera artifacts were excluded based on the SA-tag information in the BAM files generated during alignment using a custom Python script. Integration site information was extracted using a custom Bash script, which consolidated overlapping ISs, lists the read IDs associated with each integration site, and calculates counts, start and end coordinates, mean quality scores as well as strand information.

Integration site breakpoints and the structural composition of integrated vectors were further characterized through structural variant analysis, performed separately for the reads associated with each individual integration site. To do so, the reads of each integration site were aligned to a custom concatenated reference genome (human GRCh38 reference genome and AAV plasmid sequence) using Minimap2 with the “map-hifi” settings. Structural variant analysis was performed on the resulting BAM files using Sniffles v2.6.2. A custom R script was developed to summarize integration data per sample. The nearest gene to each integration site was annotated using the GT23 R package (v0.1.0). Lastly, each identified integration site was inspected manually to confirm the obtained results.

The accuracy of the described pipeline was tested on a simulated dataset. Integration events were simulated by inserting AAV sequences of varying lengths into reference genomes using custom Python scripts. An expansion parameter allowed modeling of clonal amplification of ISs. Synthetic reads were then resized using gamma-distributed fragment lengths to reflect realistic variability in sequencing fragment sizes. Enrichment was modeled using a probabilistic function defined by a sequence complementarity threshold, parameters for pre-enrichment amplification, and background leakage. Synthetic PacBio reads were generated using Badread (v1.3.0), with defined error profiles, sequencing artifacts and quality score models.

The code used for analysis and simulation is available on GitHub (https://github.com/Novartis/Pipeline_for_TES_AAV_LongReads). The version used in this study is kept as a static branch: https://github.com/Novartis/Pipeline_for_TES_AAV_LongReads/tree/main/Manuscript_coratella_et_al_version_static, where it continues to be refined and updated beyond the version described in this publication.

Statistical analysis for LOD calculation

The LOD was estimated using a generalized linear mixed-effects model with a logit link function using the lme4 package in R (v4.5.2). For each observation, a binary response variable was defined as detected (read count >0) or not detected (read count = 0). Clone concentrations were log10-transformed prior to modeling; zero concentrations were assigned a negligible placeholder value to enable transformation (lowest concentration divided by 1e6). The model was specified as detect ∼ log10(conc) + (1 | Site), where log10-transformed concentration served as the fixed effect and integration site was included as a random intercept to account for site-to-site variability in detection efficiency. The non-clonal integration site (PC10, chr16) was excluded from this analysis. The LOD was defined as the concentration at which the model-predicted detection probability, marginalized over random effects, reached 95%. A 95% confidence interval was derived using the delta method. Briefly, this approach estimates the standard error of the LOD by accounting for the uncertainty in the model’s intercept and slope coefficients via first-order Taylor expansion. The confidence bounds were then obtained by adding and subtracting 1.96 standard errors from the log10-transformed LOD estimate, where 1.96 is the critical value for a two-sided 95% confidence interval under the normal distribution, and converting back to the original concentration scale.

LOD estimation of short-read TES

Genomic DNA from PC14 was serially diluted in commercial genomic DNA (#G3041, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) to final proportions ranging from 0.01% to 100% (corresponding to undiluted PC14 DNA, for a total amount of 1 μg in 40 μL). The serial dilution was performed in duplicate to generate technical replicates. Samples were subjected to Twist Library Preparation EF 2.0 with Enzymatic Fragmentation and Twist UMI Adapter System according to the manufacturers’ instructions (DOC-001239 REV 6.0, Twist Bioscience, Frankfurt, Germany). The enzymatic fragmentation was performed at 30°C for 6 min to obtain an average fragment size of approximately 350 bp. The Twist custom panel was the same as that used for LR-TES. Pre- and post-capture PCR amplification was performed using 5 and 20 cycles, respectively, with a shortened elongation time of 30 s in both PCRs. Approximately 100,000,000 reads were sequenced per sample.

Sequencing reads were analyzed using the short-read pipeline shared by Sheehan et al. (https://github.com/sheehanmarkj/insertional_mutagenesis_short-read_public).24

Whole genome sequencing

DNA was sheared using Megaruptor 3 (#B06010003, Diagenode, Seraing, Belgium) and its corresponding kit (#E07010003, Diagenode, Seraing, Belgium), aiming for a fragment length of 15–18 kb. Compared to LR-TES, a larger fragment size was used to improve alignment confidence, especially within repetitive regions, and to facilitate the resolution of complex structural variants. The library preparation for the subsequent sequencing via PacBio Sequel IIe or the PacBio Revio was done by using the SMRTbell prep kit 3.0 (#102-182-700, PacBio, London, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. BAM files with HiFi reads were generated by SMRTlink (version 13.0.0) and converted into FASTA file format using samtools fasta (version 1.16). The FASTA files were then converted into nucleotide blast databases using makeblastdb (version 2.15.0+). The plasmid sequence (Figure S1) was searched against these databases with blastn (version 2.15.0+). The identifiers of all reads with a partial match to the plasmid sequence were retrieved, and the corresponding sequences were extracted from the FASTA files using the Unix tool grep and mapped against the human genome hg38 using pbmm2 (version 1.4.0). Structural variants were called using pbsv (version 2.4.0) and insertions as potential integration sites were identified. Putative integration sites were further refined as follows: the hg38 reference sequences in 1-kb windows around the putative integration sites were retrieved using bedtools getfasta (version 2.27.1) and converted into a nucleotide blast database makeblastdb (version 2.15.0+). All reads with a partial match to one of the putative integration sites were found by a blastn (version 2.15.0+) search against the database of the 1-kb windows and then mapped against hg38 using pbmm2 (version 1.4.0). Structural variants were again called using pbsv (version 2.4.0) and all insertions with partial matches to the plasmid sequence were identified.
