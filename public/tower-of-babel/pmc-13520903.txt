Introduction

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fistulas are pathological communication between subarachnoid space and outer space. Based on the classification of etiology of cerebrospinal fluid fistulas (CSFF), secondary CSFF are those with clear etiology (traumatic, or iatrogenic), compared to primary CSF fistulas - those with unknown or no etiology at all (Ahmad et al., 2008a; Primary spontaneous cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhoea, 1964). Primary CSFF of frontal and middle fossa are between 3 and 36% of all CSFF (Codina et al., 2017; Elshanawany and Mohammad, 2024; Mousavinejad et al., 2021). Clinical presentation of cranial (primary and secondary) CSFF's are similar: CSF rhinorrhea, CSF hypotension, and often meningitis. Based on the localization most frequent localization of CSF are those in frontal and middle cranial fossa (Keshri et al., 2019). Primary spontaneous CSF rhinorrhea, defined as CSF leak in the absence of trauma, prior surgery, tumor, or other obvious structural cause, accounts for approximately 5–10% of all CSF leaks. The most frequent sites are the cribriform plate, the lateral recess of the sphenoid sinus, and the sella (Lopatin et al., 2003; Hooper, 1971). In contrast, spontaneous CSF leaks originating from a defect in the clivus are exceptionally rare (Mousavinejad et al., 2021;Psaltis et al., 2012).

A review of the literature up to 2025 identified 46 cases of non-traumatic clival defects causing CSF rhinorrhea, confirming that clival CSF leaks remain a rare but increasingly recognised entity. Proposed mechanisms include congenital thinning or dehiscence of the clival bone, persistent notochordal remnants, and chronic CSF pulsations, sometimes in the setting of idiopathic intracranial hypertension.

We present a case of spontaneous clival defect causing CSF rhinorrhea in a patient without any traumatic, inflammation or iatrogenic history, successfully treated by a microscopic endonasal approach, and we briefly review the current literature on this rare condition.

Material and methods

A comprehensive literature search was performed using the PubMed/MEDLINE database to identify relevant publications concerning spontaneous cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) rhinorrhea originating from clival defects. The search strategy included a combination of controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords relevant to the topic. 46 cases are published until today with three big case reports (two with six casess, one with four) and several small with one or two cases. Demographic data, existence of meningeal infection, duration of CSF leak, surgical approach and technique for closure the defect, recurrence, possible etiology or high-risk factors are all included. A region of clival defect is also used in our data summary. Exclusion criteria are clival defects based on trauma or other known etiologies. Only one patient was included with previous surgery, but the surgery is not in this area and likelihood of reason for the CSF leakage is negligible.

Case presentation

A 50-year-old man was admitted with a two-week history of spontaneous clear unilateral nasal discharge, clinically consistent with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) rhinorrhea. The leakage was positional, more pronounced in the upright position and particularly noticeable in the early morning. The patient also reported mild headache, episodes of bradycardia, low arterial blood pressure, and generalized fatigue. There was no history of trauma, recent surgery, or upper respiratory infection. Shortly before admission, the patient developed high-grade fever (39.0°C to 40.8°C) for several consecutive days, accompanied by toxic-infectious symptoms and meningeal irritation. He was initially hospitalized in the Infectious Diseases Department, but due to clinical deterioration he was transferred to the Intensive Care Unit. Empirical triple antimicrobial therapy with ceftriaxone, meropenem, and vancomycin was initiated. Microbiological cultures subsequently isolated Streptococcus pneumoniae, sensitive to vancomycin; antimicrobial therapy was adjusted accordingly, resulting in progressive improvement and normalization of inflammatory markers. After 10 days of targeted treatment and three consecutive sterile CSF samples with normal laboratory parameters, neurosurgical evaluation was requested because the CSF rhinorrhea persisted.

High-resolution thin-slice CT of the skull base (0.625 mm) followed by contrast-enhanced MRI identified a spontaneous CSF fistula arising from the clivus. Given the significant risk of recurrent meningitis, surgical repair was indicated. Preoperative planning included 3D CT reconstruction of the sphenoid sinus to evaluate its dimensions and to design the optimal access window for a microscopic trans-nasal approach. Lumbar drainage was fixed on L4- L5 with clear CSF under moderate pressure. Under general anestesia endonasal transspenoidal approach was utilized via miscroscopic magnification. The nasal septum was mobilized and the right sphenoid ostium was enlarged to improve exposure. Removal of the vomer and anterior-inferior sphenoid wall revealed clear CSF in posteroinferior section of sphenoidal sinus , a bony defect in the clivus with visible CSF leakage and pulsating small arachnoid pouch, protudrding from dura and clival defect.

A multilayer reconstruction was performed using intradural autologous fascia lata and an extradural fat graft secured with Tisseel Lyo® fibrin sealant. Based on our previous experience with traumatic skull-base CSF leak repair, additional reinforcement was achieved with a titanium mesh fashioned intraoperatively from a commercially available cranioplasty implant. The mesh was tailored according to preoperative CT measurements of sphenoidal sinus , while remaining sufficiently sized and shaped to allow insertion through the anterior wall of sphenodial sinus defect and positioning against the reconstructed clival wall. It was placed without rigid fixation, functioning in a valve-like manner, with the fascia lata and fat graft maintaining the implant apposed to the surrounding bony margins. Additional stabilization was achieved with fibrin sealant, without suturing the surrounding mucosa. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 7 in good general condition with oral amoxicillin/clavulanate and topical mupirocin ointment. Discussion

According to the available literature, 80–90% of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fistulas are traumatic in origin, typically following direct craniofacial injury. An additional 16% result from various surgical procedures and interventions, while only approximately 4% (3-36%) of all CSF leaks are considered non-traumatic or spontaneous in nature. (Codina et al., 2017; Hooper, 1971), The vast majority of these non-traumatic leaks occur within the anterior and middle cranial fossae, predominantly involving the planum sphenoidale, cribriform plate, and the region of the sella turcica, that is, the superior aspect of the sphenoid sinus (Lopatin et al., 2003; Morley and Wortzman, 1965). lateral recess of the sphenoid sinus are frequently associated with idiopathic intracranial hypertension, obesity, and signs of long-standing raised intracranial pressure (Illing et al., 2014).

In contrast, spontaneous CSF fistulas originating from the clivus are exceedingly rare, with only 44 cases reported to date in the international literature, identifying the clivus as a distinct and exceptionally uncommon anatomical site responsible for spontaneous naso-liquorrhea (Codina et al., 2017; Mousavinejad et al., 2021; Hooper, 1971; Aljawi and Shkoukani, 2023).

Etiopathogenesis of spontaneous clival cerebrospinal fluid fistulas

The underlying cause and primary pathological mechanism responsible for the development of spontaneous clival osseous defects remain insufficiently elucidated. Nevertheless, current understanding suggests that three fundamental conditions are required for the manifestation of this rare entity: (1) the presence of a bony defect, (2) disruption of the dura mater, and (3) elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) (Aljawi and Shkoukani, 2023).

Embryological and developmental considerations

Several theories propose that the defect may originate during embryological development. Following birth, the occipital and sphenoid bones undergo progressive ossification through the fusion of the basisphenoid and basiocciput components (Codina et al., 2017; Beckhardt et al., 1991). The spheno-occipital synchondrosis typically fuses before 18 years of age, and incomplete ossification may result in persistent structural weakness of the clivus. However, its caudal position relative to the sphenoid sinus in adults limits this hypothesis as a sole explanation. Continued enchondral ossification of the clivus without a defined ossification center has therefore also been proposed as a potential mechanism of structural vulnerability (Shetty et al., 2000). In previous study ecchondrosis physiaphora – a benign nothtordal remnant in the intradural space found in 0,5 to 2% 0.5–2% of cases with spontaneous CSF leak—has also been proposed as a possible contributing factor (Aljawi and Shkoukani, 2023).

Role of sphenoid sinus pneumatization

Extensive sphenoid sinus pneumatization may lead to thinning of the sinus walls and predispose to bony erosion and subsequent CSF leakage. Reported wall thickness ranges from 0.2 to 10 mm, supporting a potential association between increased pneumatization and defect formation, particularly in the sellar type of sphenoid sinus pneumatization (Aljawi and Shkoukani, 2023; Haetinger et al., 2006; Abuzayed et al., 2010).

Influence of intracranial pressure

Elevated intracranial pressure represents a major contributing factor in the pathophysiology of spontaneous clival CSF fistulas (Hooper, 1971). Their almost exclusive occurrence in adults challenges embryological ossification abnormalities as a sole explanation. Berdahl et al. reported ICP values in adults approximately three times higher than in children and, in a separate study, demonstrated a significant association between BMI and ICP, with more than 80% of patients with elevated BMI also showing increased ICP within a range of 9–50 cm H2O (Berdahl et al., 2012). In addition, Van Zele et al. additionally described empty sella and arachnoid pits in two patients, supporting the presence of chronically elevated or pulsatile ICP peaks (Van et al., 2013). hese findings suggest that sustained or intermittent ICP elevation may promote dural attenuation and clival bone remodeling, predisposing to CSF fistula formation.

Connective tissue disorders and other factors

Case has also been described among patient with Marfan syndrome, where systemic connective tissue weakness contributes to dural fragility (Ramos et al., 2007). In contrast, are reported two cases that pulsatile forces exerted by the basilar artery upon the clivus, in combination with increased ICP, may also result in dural tearing and osseous remodeling(Coiteiro et al., 1995; Asad et al., 2017). Additionally, the presence of arachnoid cysts has been proposed as another possible contributing factor.

Demographic characteristics

The male-to-female distribution within the reported cohort demonstrates a clear gender predominance, with 14 male and 33 female patients, corresponding to an approximate ratio of 1:2.46 (≈1:2.5 in favor of females). This trend reinforces previous observations suggesting that spontaneous clival CSF fistulas occur disproportionately more often in women, particularly in those of middle age and beyond with increased BMI. Our patient, a 50-year-old man with a BMI of approximately 30 kg/m2, differs from the reported demographic profile mainly with respect to the observed female predominance, while his age and increased BMI closely correspond to the characteristics most frequently described in this patient population. There were no clinical or laboratory findings suggestive of amyloidosis, and rheumatological evaluation did not identify an underlying connective tissue disorder. Although no marked hypertensive peaks were documented, mild arterial hypertension (approximately 140/80 mmHg) and the associated metabolic profile may have contributed, together with age and increased BMI, to altered intracranial and vascular pulsatility. We therefore consider these factors, particularly increased BMI, as potentially relevant contributors to chronic pulsatile stress and progressive clival remodeling leading to defect formation.

Clinical implications and diagnosis

The median duration of CSF rhinorrhea before diagnosis and definitive treatment was approximately 25 weeks (∼5,7 months) (Table 1), although symptom duration was not reported in many cases, limiting comparative analysis. Diagnostic delay may increase the risk of recurrent meningitis. Chronic or intermittent watery rhinorrhea, particularly from the sphenoethmoidal recess, should raise suspicion of a skull-base CSF leak and prompt β-2 transferrin testing (Illing et al., 2014) High-resolution CT plus MRI (and, if needed, CT cisternography) is essential to map the defect, differentiate it from more common cribriform or lateral sphenoid leaks, and identify associated meningoencephalocele (Wax et al., 1997).

Treatment

A comprehensive review of the published literature identified 47 documented cases of spontaneous clival cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fistulas, including our present case. All patients were managed through a transsphenoidal surgical approach, either endoscopic or microscopic, although several reports did not specify the exact subtype of surgical access. Defects were located in the mid-clivus in 32 cases, the upper clivus in 14, and the lower clivus in only one case, demonstrating a clear regional predilection (Codina et al., 2017; Berdahl et al., 2012; Wax et al., 1997; Maranha et al., 2012; Tandon et al., 2017). Only two patients underwent previous unsuccessful surgical intervention before definitive treatment (Karli and Yildirim, 2018; Tandon et al., 2017).

Regarding reconstruction, five single-layer closure, butwith fascia lata alone was reportedin one only onecase (Karli and Yildirim, 2018). In four casess, a nasoseptal flap was used alone (Tandon et al., 2017;Vyas et al., 2022). Additional four cases used fat combined with fibrin tissue adhesive, though these were not classified as single-layer repairs (Ramos et al., 2007; Tandon et al., 2017; Zanabria et al., 2015; Al- et al., 2005). One case used freeze-dried dura with bone plug, and twocase s incorporated bone augmentation as part of a complex repair (Coiteiro et al., 1995; Lockwood et al., 1980;Takabayashi et al., 2023). The remaining 35 cases underwent multilayer reconstruction using various combinations of fat, fascia, turbinate mucosa, vascularized nasoseptal flaps, tissue adhesives, and perioperative lumbar CSF drainage, particularly in patients with radiological signs of elevated intracranial pressure such as empty sella or arachnoid pits (Lopatin et al., 2003).

Three recurrences were reported, involving two female patients aged 64 and 59 years and one 58-year-old male; all had a BMI over 30 (Sara et al., 2024; Tandon et al., 2017; Asad et al., 2017). Nineteen patients developed meningitis, including our patient, highlighting the clinically significant infectious risk associated with clival CSF fistulas. One case of postoperative meningitis was also reported (Van et al., 2013).

In our case, closure was successfully achieved using a multilayer reconstruction with autologous fat, fascia lata, fibrin glue, and titanium mesh, representing the first documented use of titanium mesh in spontaneous clival CSF fistula repair. This technique provided substantial mechanical reinforcement and demonstrated favorable early postoperative stability without recurrence, suggesting its potential value in selected anatomically complex cases. In most of reviewed literature, endoscopic technique was used and all transsphenoidal approach with few modifications. Based on our experience with microscopic technique and the great result we believe it is not inferior than endoscopic one and magnification can be useful in small defects and creating a multilayer fashion closure of the sphenoidal sinus.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate a clear shift from limited single-layer repairs toward reinforced multilayer reconstruction and vascularized flap (Hadad-Bassagasteguy flap) techniques, guided by advancing understanding of skull-base biomechanics and intracranial pressure regulation (Aljawi and Shkoukani, 2023). The universal application of the transsphenoidal approach supports its reliability and safety in managing spontaneous clival CSF fistulas.

Conclusion

Spontaneous clival CSF fistulas are rare but should be considered in adults with unexplained unilateral rhinorrhea and sphenoid sinus opacification. High-resolution imaging is essential for accurate localization, while endonasal transsphenoidal repair using multilayer reconstruction provides effective and durable treatment. The universal application of the transsphenoidal approach supports its reliability and safety in managing spontaneous clival CSF fistulas. Furthermore, our multilayer reconstruction strategy incorporating titanium mesh provided additional structural reinforcement and may represent a useful adjunct in selected defects, although further evidence is required to define its role in standardized reconstruction.
