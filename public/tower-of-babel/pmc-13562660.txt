The genomic landscape of HER2 negative metastatic breast cancer with loss of estrogen and progesterone receptors
Authors: Ilenia Migliaccio, Marta Paoli, Chiara Biagioni, Cristina Guarducci, Francesca Galardi, Giulia Anichini, Laura Biganzoli, Matteo Benelli, Luca Malorni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLoss of estrogen receptor (ER) and/or progesterone receptor (PR) might occur during the metastatic progression of ER positive and HER2 negative (ER+/HER2−) breast cancer (BC), but the underpinning molecular alterations remain elusive. We explored the genomic context of HER2− tumors with ER and/or PR loss to investigate potential drivers and actionable alterations that might help personalize treatment of ER+/HER2− BC.MethodsWe accessed data from metastatic HER2− BC included in the MSK-2018 dataset to compare outcome, tumor characteristics and genomic alterations of BC with loss of ER (ER+/−, n = 66) to those maintaining ER positivity (ER+/+, n = 364) or ER negativity (ER−/−, n = 50). We also compared metastatic ER+/+ BC with loss of PR (PR+/−, n = 111) to those maintaining PR positivity (PR+/+, n = 192) or PR negativity (PR−/−, n = 41).ResultsIn line with previous reports, ER+/− BC was associated with aggressive clinico-pathological characteristics and poor outcome. ER+/− BC showed significantly higher frequency of TP53 and RB1 mutations and lower frequency of PIK3CA and GATA3 mutations compared to ER+/+. ER+/− or PR+/− status was mutually exclusive with ESR1 mutations and was associated with a significantly higher tumor mutational burden. Moreover, ER+/− BC were enriched in driver alterations in the genes of the Notch and Retinoblastoma pathways and showed a significantly lower frequency of level 1 actionable alterations according to OncoKB.ConclusionsLoss of ER and/or PR may identify a distinct evolutionary trajectory of ER+/HER2− metastatic progression, largely non-overlapping with ESR1-mutant endocrine resistance. Further studies on matched primary and metastatic samples are warranted.

Introduction

The hormone receptors (HR), estrogen (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR), and the epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) are critical biomarkers in patients with breast cancer (BC), informing on prognosis and therapy response. Loss of HR has been well documented during cancer progression, with reported frequencies ranging from 20% to 40% of all BC [1,2]. It is well known that loss of HR is associated with poor outcome [2], but whether a change of therapy might be beneficial for patients remains to be determined. Moreover, as the molecular features of HR loss are largely undefined, it is uncertain if these tumors might benefit from alternative targeted treatments.

Patients with HR-positive and HER2-negative (HR+/HER2−) tumors respond well to endocrine-based treatments including aromatase inhibitors (AI), selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERM) like tamoxifen, and selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERD) like fulvestrant, but endocrine resistance, sustained by multiple mechanisms, represents a major clinical hurdle. Mutations in the ligand-binding domain of ESR1, the gene encoding for ER alpha, arise in around 20-30% of HR+/HER2− recurrent BC and are associated with AI resistance [3]. Additional alterations involving the mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway and ER transcriptional regulators, mutually exclusive with ESR1 mutations, have also been reported [4]. However, genomic alterations that might be associated with loss of HR have not been thoroughly investigated.

Here we accessed the MSK-2018 dataset [4] to assess the frequencies, clinico-pathological features, outcome and genomic alterations of the loss of ER and/or PR in metastatic ER+/HER2− BC. We characterized the genomic landscape of these tumors with the primary aim of identifying driver genes and pathways as well as potentially actionable alterations associated with HR loss.

Methods

Datasets, data collection and sample classification

Detailed, patient-level clinical and treatment data for selected samples were retrieved from cBioPortal [5,6] and from the supplementary data of the MSK-2018 original manuscript [4].

Based on the available information regarding the ER and PR receptor status of the primary and metastatic tumors, as assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC), 483 patients were stratified as:

ER+/+: ER-positive primary tumor and ER-positive metastasis (n = 364),

ER+/−: ER-positive primary tumor and ER-negative metastasis, i.e. ER loss (n = 66),

ER−/−: ER-negative primary tumor and ER-negative metastasis (n = 50),

ER−/+: ER-negative primary tumor and ER-positive metastasis (n = 3).

ER+/+ samples were further categorized as:

PR+/+: PR-positive primary tumor and PR-positive metastasis (n = 192).

PR+/−: PR-positive primary tumor and PR-negative metastasis, i.e. PR loss (n = 111).

PR−/−: PR-negative primary tumor and PR-negative metastasis (n = 41).

PR−/+: PR-negative primary tumor and PR-positive metastasis (n = 20).

Given the small sample size of ER−/+ and PR−/+ tumors, these were not included in the analyses.

In case of multiple samples for one patient, the one taken earlier was considered.

Comparison for clinico-pathological characteristics and associations with type of adjuvant therapy received

Associations between clinico-pathological variables and ER and PR status were assessed using the arsenal R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=arsenal), using Fisher's exact test to evaluate differences in categorical variables across classes, and Kruskal-Wallis Rank Test for continuous variables. To explore associations between ER and PR status and type of treatments, analyses were conducted using pre-treatment samples corresponding to specific treatment lines. For evaluating the relationship between ER and PR status and the type of adjuvant therapy received, only samples classified as pre-treatment relative to first-line therapy were considered, and Fisher's exact test was used. Similarly, to assess the association between ER and PR status and first-line therapy received, samples labelled as pre-treatment relative to second-line therapy were analyzed.

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier analyses were performed using the survival R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survival). Multivariate Cox regression models, adjusted for relevant clinical covariates, were carried out using the finalfit R package (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=finalfit).

To evaluate progression-free survival (PFS), accounting for the type of treatment received, only patients with pre-treatment biopsies, corresponding to the therapy of interest, were included. When multiple lines of the same therapy had been administered, the earliest treatment line was considered.

Genomic analysis

Mutation and copy number alteration data for selected genes and tumor mutational burden (TMB) were downloaded from cBioPortal [5,6]. The oncogenicity classification of each alteration was determined using OncoKB [7,8]. For analysis of alteration frequencies across patient subgroups, only alterations classified as “oncogenic,” “likely oncogenic,” or “resistance” by OncoKB were included. Frequencies of mutations, amplifications, and deletions were assessed separately for each gene. Statistical significance was evaluated using Fisher's exact test. Oncoprints displaying gene alteration patterns across patient groups were generated using the ComplexHeatmap R package [9]. TMB was assessed as continuous variable or categorized according to the 80th quantile [10].

Pathways definition and analysis

Genes included in the Pathway analysis were selected as follow: mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and ER transcriptional regulator (ER-TR) genes were derived from the original publication [4]; other transcriptional regulators (TRANSC_REG) were ZFHX3, RUNX1, CBFB; Notch pathway (NOTCH) was derived from the TCGA oncogenic signaling pathways [11]; genes included as Homologous Recombination Deficiency (HRD) were derived from the publication from Heeke and colleagues [12]; RB1 and CDKN2A, converging on the Retinoblastoma pathway regulation, were assessed together; for Microsatellite Instability (MSI) the genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM were included; selected genes with chromatin remodeler activity were assessed together (CHR). The list of the genes assessed for each pathway is provided as Supplementary Table 1.

For pathway-level analysis, a tumor was considered altered in a given pathway if at least one mutation or copy number alteration was detected in any gene within that pathway. Pathway alteration frequencies were compared across patient subgroups using Fisher's exact test.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (R Core Team, 2022; https://www.R-project.org/). To assess associations between continuous and categorical variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for comparisons between two groups, and Kruskal-Wallis tests for comparisons involving three or more groups. In clinicopathological tables generated using the arsenal::tableby function, Kruskal-Wallis tests were used in all cases, regardless of the number of groups, as per the function's default behavior. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using Fisher's exact test. To account for multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, and adjusted p-values (q-values) are indicated where applicable.

Results

Frequencies of HR loss and associations with clinico-pathological characteristics, therapy and outcome

We first assessed the conversion in BC subtypes from primary to metastases of metastatic samples included in the MSK-2018 dataset. Conversion from ER+/HER2− to ER−/HER2− BC represented the most frequent subtype switch, with approximately 50% of the metastatic ER−/HER2− tumors originating from primary ER+/HER2− tumors, representing ER loss cases (ER+/−) (Supplementary Fig. 1). ER loss occurred in 14% of HER2− primary BC and co-occurred with a loss of PR in over 90% of the cases (Supplementary Fig. 2A and B). PR loss alone (PR+/−) was observed in 30% of all ER+/+ and HER2− BC (Supplementary Fig. 2A).

Associations with clinico-pathological characteristics according to ER and PR classes are shown in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, respectively. Compared to ER+/+, ER+/− BC showed significantly lower ER expression levels (p < 0.001), higher frequency of PR negativity (p = 0.016) and higher tumor grade (p = 0.007) of the primary tumors. Additionally, ER+/− tumors demonstrated a significantly increased prevalence of HER2 0 status in both primary and metastatic tumors (p < 0.001). We found a borderline significant association (p = 0.05) with ER low (<10%) status of primary tumors (Supplementary Fig. 3A), but the majority of ER+/− BC showed ≥10% of ER levels on the primary tumors (Supplementary Fig. 3B). No significant differences were observed in the histological subtype, stage, menopausal status and the site or timing (based on the line of metastatic treatment) of the metastatic biopsy (Supplementary Table 2). Compared to PR+/+, PR+/− tumors were significantly associated with tumor stage (p = 0.025), histology (p = 0.026) and menopausal status (p = 0.006) (Supplementary Table 3).

As shown in Fig. 1, compared to patients with ER+/+ tumors, those with ER+/− showed significantly worse DFS and OS (Fig. 1A and B). Notably, the association between ER+/− status and worse OS remained significant (HR = 2.40; 95% CI 1.43-4.05, p = 0.001) in the corresponding multivariate analysis accounting for grade, histology, stage of the primary tumor as well as the site and timing of the metastatic tumors (Supplementary Table 5). When considering only metastatic biopsies taken before the first line treatment, neither ER+/− nor PR+/− status showed a significant association with the type of adjuvant treatment received (endocrine-, chemo-therapy or both, Supplementary Fig. 4A–D). While ER+/− status was not linked to the specific type of adjuvant endocrine therapy, PR+/− status was significantly more frequent among patients who had received adjuvant AI alone (51%) compared to those treated with SERM alone (29%, p = 0.015) or with both AI and SERM (28%, p = 0.032) (Supplementary Fig. 4B–E). When taking into account biopsy received before the second line treatment, no association was found, for both ER and PR, with the type of endocrine therapy received on first line (Supplementary Fig. 4C–F).

To assess the risk of progression during metastatic disease, accounting for the type of metastatic treatment received, we analyzed patients treated with hormone- or chemotherapy-based regimens, separately. After adjusting for the timing of metastatic biopsy, patients with ER+/− tumors had a significantly higher risk of progression on both chemotherapy- (HR = 1.6; 95% CI 1-2.4, p = 0.043) and hormone-containing (HR = 2.3; 95% CI 1.3-4.1, p = 0.006) regimens (Supplementary Fig. 5A–B) compared to those with ER+/+ tumors. No significant results were obtained for patients with PR+/− compared to PR+/+ status (Supplementary Fig. 5C–D), suggesting a weaker prognostic effect of the loss of PR.

The genomic landscape of metastatic BC undergoing HR loss

We compared the genomic alterations of tumors with HR loss (i.e. ER+/− or PR+/−) to those maintaining receptor positivity (ER+/+ or PR+/+) or negativity (ER−/− or PR−/−) considering only driver or potentially-driver alterations by OncoKB [7,8]. The genomic landscape according to the ER and PR status for the alterations significantly (q < 0.05) enriched in the ER classes are depicted in Fig. 2A and complete data are provided as supplementary tables (Tables 8–13, reporting both p-values and q-values adjusted for multiple testing). Q-values are reported for comparisons across all classes (q all) and for the comparison ER+/+ versus ER+/− (q +/+ vs +/−). For some alterations, ER+/− showed a genomic context intermediate between ER+/+ and ER−/−. TP53 mutations were significantly more frequent in ER+/− tumors compared to ER+/+, albeit less frequent than in ER−/− (ER+/−: 63.6%, ER+/+: 26.4%, and ER−/−: 88%, q all <0.001, q +/+ vs +/− <0.001). Higher frequency of RB1 and PIK3R1 mutations were also observed in ER+/− compared to ER+/+ (RB1: ER+/−: 12.1%, ER+/+: 1.4%, and ER−/−: 8%, q all = 0.0017, q +/+ vs +/− = 0.0068; PIK3R1: ER+/−: 6%, ER+/+: 0.5%, and ER−/−: 12%, q all <0.001, q +/+ vs +/− = 0.21, respectively). On the other hand, mutations in PIK3CA, GATA3 and CDH1 were observed with lower, but not significant, frequency in ER+/− compared to ER+/+, and higher than ER−/− (PIK3CA:f ER+/−: 24.2%, ER+/+: 38.1%, and ER−/−: 12%, q all = 0.004, q +/+ vs +/− = 0.74; GATA3: ER+/−: 7.5%, ER+/+: 16.2% and ER−/−: 0%, q all = 0.01, q +/+ vs +/− = 0.78; CDH1: ER+/−: 10.6%, ER+/+: 15.7% and ER−/−: 0%, q all = 0.027, q +/+ vs +/− = 1). Intriguingly, ESR1 mutations were never observed in ER+/− tumors (ER+/−: 0%, ER+/+: 15.9% and ER−/−: 0%, q all < 0.001, q +/+ vs +/− <0.001). Regarding copy number alterations, amplification of CCND1 were significantly less frequent in ER+/− compared to ER+/+, but more frequent than in ER−/− (ER+/−: 9.1%, ER+/+: 29.7% and ER−/−: 0%, respectively, q all < 0.001, q +/+ vs +/− = 0.0084) while deletion of RB1 were more frequently, albeit not significantly, observed in ER+/− tumors compared to ER+/+, but with lower frequency than ER−/− (ER+/−: 3%, ER+/+: 0.8%, and ER−/−: 6%, q all = 0.33, q +/+ vs +/− = 0.81). Actionable alterations in metastatic BC undergoing HR loss

We finally aimed to investigate the frequencies and type of actionable alterations, according to OncoKB [7,8], in tumors with ER and PR loss (Supplementary Tables 16–18). As shown in Fig. 4, ER+/− tumors had a significantly lower frequency of level 1 alterations (31.8% vs 56.7%, p < 0.001) and a higher frequency of unactionable or level 3B (p = 0.016) alterations compared to ER+/+ tumors (Supplementary Table 16). The proportion of level 1 and unactionable alterations was similar in PR+/+ and PR+/− (p = 0.3, Supplementary Table 17). Level 1 and 2 alterations in ER+/− tumors were mostly found in PIK3CA, PTEN or AKT1 genes, while level 3 included either mutations in ERBB2 or alterations in genes like BRCA1/2, ATM and ATR that are potential targets for PARP inhibitors; level 4 included NF1, ARID1A or FGFR2 alterations (Supplementary Table 18). Discussion

In this study, by taking advantage of a large and well characterized, publicly available BC dataset, the MSK-2018 [4], we aimed to investigate the clinical and genomic peculiarities of tumors with ER and/or PR loss in order to gain new insights on the biology of these tumors and ultimately help improving the personalization of treatment for patients with HER2− metastatic BC.

HR status is critical to inform on prognosis and response to endocrine therapies. Patients with HR+/HER2− tumors respond well to endocrine-based treatment, but resistance driving recurrence is a major clinical problem. It has been largely demonstrated that HR can be lost during cancer progression and that loss of HR is associated with poor outcome [1,2]. Our data confirm the frequencies of ER and PR loss in HR+/HER2− BC, their significant associations with clinico-pathological characteristics [1,2,[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]] as well as the association with survival [2,15,17]. The confirmatory results obtained in the present study highlight the reliability of the MSK-2018 clinical data and strengthen the hypothesis that tumors with ER and PR loss might have peculiar characteristics that impact on outcome and need to be further explored. We found a significant association of ER+/- BC with lower ER levels of primary tumors, and a borderline significant association (p = 0.05) with ER low (<10%) status, suggesting that at least some of the ER+/− BC might derive from ER low primary tumors, which are known to have molecular characteristics and clinical behavior similar to ER− BC [18]. However, the majority of ER+/− tumors derives from ER high primary tumors. In tumors with intermediate, more heterogeneous expression of ER, a clonal selection of pre-existing ER-negative subpopulation might be hypothesized. Nevertheless, ER loss might also occur in tumors that have very high expression of ER in the primary tumor (>90%, Supplementary Fig. 3B), suggesting that arising molecular alterations may possibly cause ER loss in these tumors.

It is unclear whether the loss of HR occurs naturally or it is induced by the selective pressure of specific treatments. In addition, there is lack of evidence that changing therapy based on the ER status of the metastatic biopsy might be clinically useful. As a consequence, while guidelines agree in underlining the importance to obtain a biopsy of the metastasis to re-assess HR status, recommendations in case of discordance might differ [19,20]. Earlier reports on ER or PR loss and the association with adjuvant treatment have been controversial [17,[21], [22], [23]]. In the present study, ER loss and PR loss seemed not to be dependent on the type of adjuvant treatment received (endocrine, chemotherapy or both), but tumors with PR loss were more frequently observed in patients who received adjuvant AI, suggesting a potential drug-induced reduction of PR, but more data are needed. CDK4/6 inhibitors have been shown to improve outcomes of patients with high-risk HR+/HER2− early BC [24,25]. Reduced expression of ER has been described as one of the possible mechanisms of CDK4/6 inhibitor resistance [26,27] and a recent study showed that ER loss occurred in 6 of 12 tumors (50%) [28] recurring after the adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitor abemaciclib. Frequencies of ER and PR loss might rise in the near future paralleling the increasing use of adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitors in addition to ET. Furthermore, new SERD are now utilized for the treatment of metastatic BC and are being tested in the adjuvant setting [29]. How these drugs will affect the expression of ER and PR during the metastatic progression of HR+/HER2− BC remains unknown.

The majority of patients with ER+/− tumors received chemotherapy after the metastatic biopsy. Only 13 patients received hormone-containing regimens. Regardless of type of treatment, PFS tended to be worse for ER+/− compared to ER+/+. On the other hand, no significant association was found between PR loss and PFS, suggesting a stronger effect of ER loss over PR loss alone. We evaluated PFS separately in patients receiving hormone-therapy or chemotherapy to assess prognosis within more homogenous subgroups, rather than to evaluate the treatment effects, which are vulnerable to selection biases in retrospective, non-randomized, datasets. Administration of therapy depends on several factors, including disease burden, visceral involvement, symptoms, time to disease progression, prior therapies, performance status, physician preference, and receptor status of metastatic biopsy. These variables are difficult to control for in retrospective analyses, like the presents. More data are therefore needed to clarify the link between therapy efficacy and HR loss.

The molecular and genomic characteristics of HR loss are largely unknown; therefore, the optimal treatment of patients with these tumors remains undefined. Here we showed that tumors with ER loss display genomic characteristics intermediate between ER+/+ and ER−/−, with higher frequency of TP53 mutations and RB1 alterations and lower frequency of PIK3CA and GATA3 mutation and CCND1 amplification compared to ER+/+. One peculiar characteristic was the complete absence of ESR1 mutations, in line with a previous report of mutual exclusivity between loss of ER and ESR1 mutations [30]. Very sparse reports on the genomic characteristics of tumors with ER loss are available. Loss of ER has been associated, similar to our study, with driver mutations in TP53 (90% of cases) and, differently from our study, ARID1A mutations (30% of cases) [31]. In the study from Yates and colleagues [31] the three tumors that gained ESR1 mutations maintained an ER + status. Intriguingly, recurrences after adjuvant abemaciclib, including those showing ER loss, showed alterations of TP53 itself and the pathway [28]. The high frequency of TP53 mutations in ER+/− tumors and the mutual exclusivity with ESR1 mutations are consistent with the recent data from Li et al. [32] who demonstrated mutual exclusivity between TP53 and ESR1 mutations in ER + BC and the suppression of ER expression and signaling in TP53 mutant tumors. In ER+/− tumors, we also found significantly higher frequency of alterations of the Notch and Rb pathways. Notch signaling has been implicated in ER expression and ER activity regulation, epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT) and endocrine resistance [33], while Rb and Rb family proteins were shown to modulate ER expression and activity through the proteasome pathway or chromatin remodeling [34,35].

For PR+/− tumors we found no genomic peculiarities other than the mutual exclusivity with ESR1 mutations. Previous studies on ER+/PR−/HER2− tumors found higher TP53 and lower PIK3CA mutation rates [36,37], but these did not specifically assess the genomic alterations of tumors with PR loss [36,37]. PR is an indicator of an active ER signaling and mutations in the ligand binding domain of ESR1 are activating, with ESR1-mutant tumors showing an increased expression of ER and ER target genes [38,39]. Therefore, it is not surprising the significant association between PR+/+ status and ESR1 mutations. In PR+/− tumors, we did not detect significantly enriched alterations of the investigated pathways. Although not significant, an increase in the frequency of alterations in the NOTCH, ER-TR, chromatin remodelers and MAPK pathway was observed in PR+/− tumors, suggesting that multiple alterations might contribute to the development of a PR+/− phenotype. On the other hand, we found higher frequencies of ERBB2 mutations and EGFR amplification in PR−/− tumors, in line with previous data demonstrating that low PR status may serve as an indicator of activated growth factor signaling [40].

We investigated actionable alterations in tumors with HR loss, observing a lower frequency of level 1 alterations in ER+/− compared to ER+/+ tumors, mostly affecting PIK3CA, PTEN and AKT1. It must be underlined though, that targeted therapies for these alterations are approved for ER + tumors in combinations with endocrine therapies and no data are available on tumors with ER loss. Our study might suggest alternative treatments to be tested in ER+/− tumors, including HER- or PARP-inhibitors or immune-therapy, given the presence of ERBB2, BRCA, ATM mutations and the high TMB. However confirmatory data on the frequencies of these alterations in tumors with ER loss are needed. Moreover, we assessed TMB according to its continuous value or according to the 80th percentile, based on previous data in a pan-cancer dataset, showing that the highest 20% TMB value in each histology might be useful for prognostication [10]. We however acknowledge that this dataset-relative definition does not necessarily correspond to the clinically validated tumor-agnostic threshold of ≥10 mut/Mb. Further studies on biomarkers of response to immunotherapy in patients with HR loss tumors are warranted.

This study has some limitations. It was performed on a retrospective dataset with inherent bias selections. While genomic alterations were assessed for the metastatic samples, the lack of matched primary tumors left unanswered the question of which molecular changes were already present in primary tumors and which were developed during progression. Moreover, transcriptional and methylation profiles were not available and molecular features, subtypes or methylation events that might impact on ER and PR levels and activity [[41], [42], [43]] could not be investigated. Nevertheless, strengths of the study include the availability of comprehensive genomic data for a large number of sample and the accessibility and the validity of the clinical information. The dataset gave us the possibility to generate new hypotheses and answer an open question regarding the genomic landscape of a group of tumors for which very few data are available. Of note, in MSK-2018 dataset, tumors with ER loss represented half of all the metastatic ER−/HER2− BC, highlighting how research advancements on this topic might be relevant for a large number of patients with metastatic BC.

Conclusions

In this study we found that tumors that experience a loss of ER display a genomic context intermediate between ER+ and ER− tumors and that both ER and PR loss might show unique genomic developmental pathways, largely independent of ESR1 mutations. The finding of some peculiar characteristics in the genomic milieu of tumors with ER loss suggests that these should be further explored in order to find new targets for the treatment personalization of patients with these tumors. Additional validation of our findings in studies assessing genomic changes between matched primary and metastatic samples are warranted.
