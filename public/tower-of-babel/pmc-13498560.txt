Diversity, richness, and species composition of fruit flies with new and first records from Meghalaya, India
Authors: Kennedy Ningthoujam, K J David, Sunita Chetry, Hissay Cheday Bhutia, Bihari Mohapatra Niladri, Kisan Rymbai, Jyotim Gogoi, Deepika Sorahia, Chada Anu Reddy, Pradeep Kumar Chandra, Mareena Sorokhaibam, Khaba Moirangthem, Mahesh Pathak, Uma Ngangom, Lourembam Sanajaoba, K M Ajaykumara, Bimal Kumar Sahoo, Yanglem Herojit Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFruit flies are cosmopolitan insects affecting almost all the fruit growing regions of the world. This insect is of significant economic importance as they can cause serious damage to fruit, sometimes resulting in almost total crop failure. This study investigates the diversity and species composition of fruit flies in relation to host availability with the aim of identifying and recording new species of fruit fly in Meghalaya and across the Indian sub-continent. For this, a three-year survey (October 2020–September 2023) was conducted across three ecosystems, forest, fruit tree and vegetable ecosystems in the Ri-bhoi district of Meghalaya. Influence of host plants, seasons, trap types and attractants on the fruit fly abundance was assessed. Diversity indices, including species richness (S), Shannon–Wiener index (H′), Simpson’s D (dominance), and Pielou’s evenness (J′), were calculated monthly for the three ecosystems. The study recorded 37 fruit fly species across several genera and tribes. These included Bactrocera, Dacus, and Zeugodacus (Dacini, Dacinae); Acroceratitis, Acrotaeniostola, and Paraxarnuta (Gastrozonini, Dacinae); and Rioxoptilona, Themara, Erectovena, and Phorelliosoma (Acanthonevrini, Phytalminae). A total of 16 subgenera were identified, with Bactrocera being the most dominant in Meghalaya. Two new species, Zeugodacus umiam and Z. nasivittatus, were recorded from Khasi mandarin and sweet orange, respectively; the former was attracted to cue-lure, while the latter responded to protein and yeast baits. Additionally, several rare species - Bactrocera abbreviata, Erectovena desperata, and Phorelliosoma hilaratum—were recorded for the first time in the Indian subcontinent. Acroceratitis bimacula and Acrotaeniostola spiralis were exclusively collected from bamboo using yeast-based baits, while P. hilaratum was found on forest trees and attracted to yeast- and protein-based baits. Sample-size and coverage-based rarefaction/extrapolation curves revealed that net traps and Zingerone lures captured the highest fruit fly, indicating effective sampling across sites. Zeta diversity analysis demonstrated higher initial species sharing in forest ecosystems compared with vegetables and fruit tree ecosystems. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed clear differentiation of fruit fly assemblages across ecosystems and lure types, highlighting the influence of habitat and bait preference on community structure. Together, these findings establish a baseline for fruit fly diversity, seasonal patterns, and bait preferences in the region, while also highlighting its rich fauna through the discovery of several new species and first reports across the Indian subcontinent.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56503-8.Keywords: Bactrocera sp., Diversity, Food bait, Fruit fly, Zeugodacus sp.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Plant sciences, Zoology

Introduction

Fruit flies (Diptera: Tephritidae) are economically important insects in the global fruit trade1. They are cosmopolitan, occurring in nearly all fruit-growing regions worldwide, particularly in tropical and subtropical areas. Of approximately 4,500 known species globally, about 200 are recognized as pests, and nearly 70 species are considered of major agricultural importance2. In the Indian subcontinent, 325 species have been reported, including 243 species under 79 genera belonging to four subfamilies Dacinae, Phytalmiinae, Tephritinae, and Trypetinae from India alone3. Chatterjee et al.4 recognized Meghalaya as a priority region for biodiversity conservation due to its high species richness and endemism. The Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan for the Northeast Eco-region documented 3,624 insect species from the region.

Meghalaya, a landlocked state in the northeastern Himalayas covers 22,429 km² (25°02′–26°07′ N; 89°49′–92°50′ E), is recognized as a biodiversity hotspot. Ri-Bhoi shows strong climatic variation—from tropical conditions near Assam to more temperate zones toward East Khasi Hills—supporting diverse broad-leaved vegetation. Its varied altitude, soils, and climate enable cultivation of many crops, including fruits like banana, orange, pineapple, papaya, strawberry, passion fruit, jackfruit, litchi, guava, plum, peach, and pear, grown commercially or in homestead gardens5. Khasi Mandarin, with a Geographical Indication (GI) tag, is particularly important economically, alongside vegetables such as pumpkin, cucumber, gourds, brinjal, tomato, and cabbage.

Fruit flies cause substantial economic losses to fruit crops worldwide. In Asia, fruit fly alone caused 5–100% fruit damage6, at times leading to near-total crop failure. Their economic significance extends beyond direct yield losses and management costs to serious constraints in export markets due to quarantine restrictions. Thakur et al.7 reported that the climatic conditions of the Northeastern Himalayas favor the proliferation of insect pests and their natural enemies. The region’s international borders with five countries further facilitate cross-border insect migration. Consequently, insect pests cause significant yield losses in most crops, with reports indicating increasing damage despite greater reliance on chemical pesticides.

Salazar-Mendoza et al.8 used McPhail traps with different lures to collect male and female fruit flies in guava orchards across elevations. They reported higher captures of Ceratitis capitata and sixteen Anastrepha species in McPhail traps. Fruit fly abundance was greater at lower elevations and during the dry season, whereas species evenness was higher at higher elevations and in the rainy season. Only three species Anastrepha fraterculus, A. ornata, and A. striata emerged from guava fruits. Similarly, Pramanik et al.9 recorded Bactrocera dorsalis, Zeugodacus cucurbitae, and Z. tau as dominant fruit fly species during the fruiting season in snake gourd ecosystem. Methyl eugenol traps showed the highest diversity and evenness, while species richness was comparable between cue lure and methyl eugenol traps due to their attraction to different species groups. Z. cucurbitae was the most abundant and consistently dominated the snake gourd ecosystem. Coelho et al.10 employed McPhail traps baited with hydrolyzed corn protein diluted in water and food attractants to capture fruit flies in diverse fruit crops, including pear, medlar, fig, and quince, in Minas Gerais.

Although conventional alpha and beta diversity metrics effectively quantify local species richness and compositional variation, they provide limited resolution in distinguishing the relative contributions of rare and widespread fruit fly species to overall community structure and organization11,12. In the present study, our goal was to assess the spatial relationship between the host, lures, seasonality and place as a factor to fruit fly richness8. This study aims to study the diversity and richness of fruit flies through host types and different lures for capture efficiency to measure the responses of fruit fly with capture efficiency. The rarefaction and extrapolation (R/E) curve were constructed with observed fruit fly samples of abundance and incidence data along with different traps and attractant types. Extrapolation (E) predicts how many additional species would likely be detected if sampling efforts were increased beyond the observed trap and lure captures. 2 types of rarefactions were estimated as (a) Sample-size-based (or size-based) R/E sampling curves and (b) Coverage-based R/E sampling curves10,13. Coverage-based rarefaction and extrapolation estimate fruit fly species richness by standardizing samples according to their level of completeness, allowing diversity comparisons across traps and host types with unequal sampling effort. Complementing this, fruit fly zeta diversity quantifies incidence-based (presence–absence) biodiversity patterns by measuring how many fruit fly species are shared across multiple locations, hosts and habitats14–16. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used for standardized fruit fly abundance matrix to identify major gradients in species composition and to visualize differences among ecosystems, with biplots illustrating the contribution of individual species to community variation17–19.

Diversity of fruit flies have been studied and documented in different parts of India by many researchers. Kumar et al.20 reported four species viz., B. dorsalis, B. zonata, B. correcta and B. cucurbitae in Gujarat; Majeed et al.21 reported eight species of Bactrocera from guava orchards of Uttar Pradesh. Math et al.22 studied the diversity of fruit flies in guava ecosystem in Bagalkot region and reported B. correcta, B. dorsalis and B. zonata as the predominant species and B. duplicata as rare in guava ecosystem. Fruit fly diversity of Nagpur mandarin orchard from different agro-ecological zones of Vidarbha region of Maharashtra were investigated in which B. dorsalis was the most prominent species and Z. duplicatus and Z. gavisus were very less abundant in all locations23.

A preliminary study on fruit fly diversity conducted by Chetry and Ningthoujam24 in Meghalaya recorded eleven species under the tribe Dacini and genus Bactrocera, Dacus, and Zeugodacus; two species viz., B. abbreviata and D. longicornis for the first time in Meghalaya, of which one species B. abbreviata is a new record in India which was identified and described with the help of Dr. David K Jacob24,25. These findings underscore the ecological significance of Meghalaya as a potential hotspot for fruit fly diversity. Owing to the ecotonal interface between these distinct vegetation types, the region experiences a strong edge effect, which contributes to high levels of floristic and faunal diversity26.

Despite this evidence, comprehensive documentation of fruit fly diversity in the state remains limited, with existing studies largely restricted to specific host plants and fragmented local surveys. Therefore, a systematic and region-wide investigation is necessary to bridge existing knowledge gaps and to provide a clearer understanding of fruit fly species richness and distribution patterns in Meghalaya. Accurately assessing community assembly processes remains a central challenge in contemporary biodiversity research, particularly in taxa such as fruit flies (Diptera: Tephritidae). The present study was conducted to understand the biodiversity of the fruit fly in Meghalaya and also to document new records, rare or new species of fruit fly in Meghalaya and the Indian sub-continent.

Materials and methods

The maximum and minimum temperature, morning and evening relative humidity and rainfall during the study period recorded was 25.80 °C, 13.26 °C, 84.86%, 63.86% and 2000–4000 mm respectively. Sampling method

Fruit flies were collected using three methods: (a) collection of infested fruits (b) specific traps using para pheromonic lures (methyl eugenol, zingerone and cuelure) and (c) setting up food bait traps.

In forest ecosystem, traps were placed randomly at minimum distance of approximately 30 m apart to reduce attractant interference and ensure independent sampling27. Traps were inspected after every 3 days throughout the year for silvicolous fruit fly species. For fruit trees, traps were placed at 20 traps/ha to obtain optimum trap density generally used for mass trapping. Single point sampling was used for vegetables maintaining 1 trap per lifted platform of size approx. 10m2 and observation was done during flowering and fruiting stages for collection of fruit flies. The baits were changed after every 7–10 days.

Different types of traps were deployed for mass trapping of fruit files such as McPhail trap, Eco trap which are standard traps suitable for high rainfall areas. Bottle trap, Net trap and Sweep net (occasionally used) were used as local alternative for collection. Traps baited with attractants were suspended from the branch of trees at 1–3 m above the ground level at the northern side to avoid long exposure to the sunrays. Each trap was marked with the name of the lure in it.

Sampling design

A trap density of 20 traps per hectare (20 traps per 10,000 m²) was maintained following standard mass-trapping recommendations. The number and placement of traps were adjusted according to the area of each ecosystem, including forest, fruit orchards, and vegetable fields, while ensuring comparable sampling effort across ecosystems.

In both forest and fruit tree ecosystems, five trap types were used with five replicates each (25 traps per ecosystem). Traps were proportionally distributed across the study area, maintaining recommended density and ~ 30 m spacing between traps to minimize interference and ensure independent sampling. Trap locations remained fixed, with only lures replaced as needed.

In seasonal vegetable ecosystems, five independent fields were selected. Each field represented one trap type, within which five traps of the same design were installed and treated as replicates. Traps were placed approximately 30 m apart to minimize overlap in attraction radius and to maintain a comparable sampling area (500–1000 m² per trap), consistent with the trap density used in forest and orchard ecosystems.

Across all ecosystems, five replicates were maintained for each trap and bait type. This design minimized spatial bias and ensured standardized sampling across both continuous (forest, orchards) and fragmented (vegetable fields) habitats.

Attractants

Adult flies were collected using different attractants such as parapheromone lures (Cuelure, Methyl eugenol and Zingerone) and food baits (Yeast bait and ProteineX bait). Parapheromone lures are species specific and attract mostly male adult whereas food baits are non-specific/unbiased attracting both the sexes. Three types of parapheromone lures were prepared by mixing 60 ml ethyl alcohol and 40 ml methyl eugenol/zingerone/cue-lure. Different food baits were constituted of (a) ProteineX (10%) + jaggery (10%) + fruit pulp mixed with attraction enhancers viz., ammonium acetate (5%) and boric acid (2%); (b) Soybean (10%) + jaggery (10%) + fruit pulp mixed with attraction enhancers viz. ammonium acetate (5%) and boric acid (2%); and (c) Yeast (10%) + jaggery (10%) + fruit pulp mixed with attraction enhancers viz., ammonium acetate (5%) and boric acid (2%). All the parapheromone lures were analytical grade (99% pure) (a) Methyl Eugenol 4-allyl-1, 2-dimethoxybenzene-carboxylate, CAS 93-15-12, Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd.), (b) Zingerone [4-(4-hydroxy-3-methoxyphenyl)−2-butanone, CAS W312401, Sigma-Aldrich] and (c) Cuelure [4-(4-acetoxyphenyl)−2-butanone, 3572-06-3].

Collection of immature stages from infested fruits

Fruit flies infested fruits having puncture marks made by the entry of the female’s ovipositor were collected from the fields and brought to the laboratory in paper bags. Collected samples were kept in separate wire netting cages (20 × 20 × 26 cm) having a layer of sand at the bottom for emerging larvae to pupate. Each cage was numbered with the help of a marker pen. Samples were regularly checked for puparia. Collected pupae were transferred to a Petridish (10 cm diameter) which was covered with a thin layer of sand and placed in a rearing cage. After the emergence, adult flies were fed with sugar solution and development of colour patterns were used for identification.

Identification of fruit flies

Identification was done based on taxonomic keys and literature3. Some fruit fly species were identified with the help of Dr. David K. Jacob, Senior Scientist at National Bureau of Agriculturally Important Insects (NBAII), Bangalore.

3)Zeta diversity.

Zeta diversity.

ζ1 is the average number of species per site (i.e. alpha diversity). ζ2 is the average number of species shared by any two sites (i.e. beta diversity). ζ3 is the average number of species shared by any three sites i.e., Bhoirymbong, Kyrdemkulai & Umiam and so on until the maximum number of sites is reached (the number of sites as subscript is referred to as the “order” of zeta). The advantage of zeta diversity therefore lies in the fact that low orders of zeta capture the contribution of all species in the community to turnover, whereas high orders of zeta only capture the more widespread (common) species (by definition, rare species cannot be shared by many sites) (Fig. 2).

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a multivariate statistical technique that uses orthogonal transformation to convert a set of correlated variables into a set of orthogonal, uncorrelated axes called principal components30. Prior to analysis, species abundance values were standardized (Hellinger-transformed) to minimize the influence of highly dominant species and ensure comparability among sampling units.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected at 3-day intervals during flowering and fruiting stages. Captured fruit flies were sorted based on host, location, species, and sex.

Differences in species abundance among trap types and ecosystems were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. Species abundance patterns were visualized using bar plots with statistical annotations.

Rarefaction analysis was performed to standardize species richness across samples with unequal numbers of individuals. Both sample-size–based and coverage-based rarefaction approaches were applied. Sample-size rarefaction used a hypergeometric model, while extrapolation of species richness was performed using the Chao1 estimator. These analyses were implemented using the vegan package to allow unbiased comparison across traps and lures with varying capture efficiencies.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted using covariance (or correlation) matrices to examine patterns of species composition across ecosystems. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors were used to determine the variance explained by each principal component. Biplots were generated to visualize clustering of sampling locations and the contribution of individual species.

For zeta diversity analysis, a standardized incidence-based dataset was constructed from fruit fly occurrences across trap and lure types. The mean of five replicates per trap and lure type was used. Zeta diversity of order n (ζₙ) was calculated to quantify the number of species shared among multiple sampling units. Sampling units were defined as combinations of ecosystems, locations, trap types, and lures. All possible combinations were used to assess species retention and turnover patterns.

Result and discussion

Fruit fly assemblages in different ecosystems

Throughout the study period, a total of 35,431 fruit flies were collected, of which Bactrocera dorsalis (Hendel) (15783) was the most abundant followed by B. lombokensis (7457), B. aethriobasis (3579), B. tuberculata, Bezzi (2136) etc. (supplementary file, Table 1).

The study yielded thirty-seven fruit fly species belonging to genera Bactrocera, Dacus and Zeugodacus under the tribe Dacini of subfamily Dacinae; Acroceratitis, Acrotaeniostola and Paraxarnuta under the tribe Gastrozonini of subfamily Dacinae; Rioxoptilona, Themara, Erectovena and Phorelliosoma under the tribe Acanthonevrini of subfamily phytalminae. A total of sixteen subgenera of species were recorded of which Bactrocera subgenus was the most dominant in this region of Meghalaya (supplementary file, Table 1).

The most species rich genus was Bactrocera represented by fifteen species viz., B. dorsalis, B. tuberculata, B. aethriobasis, B. lombokensis, B. zonata, B. wuzhishana, B. latifrons, B. gombokensis, B. divenderi, B. minax, B. thailandica, B. profunda, B. abbreviata and two unidentified species. In the case of Dacus, three species viz., D. longicornis, D. maculipterus and an unidentified species of Dacus sp. were collected (supplementary file, Table 1).

Under Zeugodacus, eleven species were recorded viz., Z. cucurbitae, Z. menglanus, Z. diaphorus, Z. atrifacies, Z. maculatus, Z. diversus, Z. tau, Z. scutellaris, Zeugodacus sp.nr bezziana, Z. umiam and Z. nasivittatus. Under the tribe Gastrozonini, three species were collected viz., Acroceratitis bimacula, Acrotaeniostola spiralis and Paraxurnata anephelobasis and tribe Acanthonevrini was represented by four species viz., Rioxoptilona dunlopi, Themara yunnana, Erectovena desperata and Phorelliosoma hilaratum.

Comparative evaluation of attractants and trap types

Sex-wise responses to attractants showed strong and statistically significant differences (Fig. 7). For male fruit flies (Fig. 7), attractant type had a highly significant effect (S, p < 0.0001), with methyl eugenol capturing the highest number (17,444), followed by cuelure (7,516) and zingerone (6,226), while yeast bait (341) and protein bait (65) were far less effective; several pairwise comparisons were significant (S), though a few remained non-significant (NS). A similar pattern was observed for female fruit flies (Fig. 5 iii B), where the overall effect of attractants was also significant (S, p < 0.0001). Females were most abundant in yeast bait (2,207) and protein bait (1,827), followed by zingerone (878), methyl eugenol (624), and cuelure (488), with multiple significant pairwise differences (S). These results clearly demonstrate strong sex-specific attractant preferences, with male fruit flies responding predominantly to parapheromones and females showing greater attraction to food-based baits. (Fig. 8).

Sex-wise response of fruit flies to different attractants.

The traps density showed no effect on collection because of similar number of traps deployed on forest and other seasonal fruits and vegetable according to collected data. McPhail traps (19,017) were the most efficient, followed by bottle traps (7,062) and net traps (5,705), whereas eco traps and sweep nets showed lower captures (Fig. 5). The overall comparison among trap types was highly significant (S, p < 0.001), confirming strong differences in trapping efficiency.

Seasonal patterns of fruit fly abundance

Seasonal patterns of fruit fly abundance varied across years (Fig. 5). In 2021, total captures were higher during the monsoon (10,857) compared to summer (5,341) and winter (2,638), but the seasonal effect was non-significant. In contrast, 2022 showed a significant seasonal difference (S, p = 0.0109), with the monsoon again recording the highest abundance (5,630), followed by summer (2,759) and winter (1,325). A similar pattern was observed in 2023, where fruit fly abundance peaked in the monsoon (3,795) relative to summer (1,594) and winter (1,492), with the overall test being significant (S, p = 0.0063). However, when data were pooled across all years, seasonal variation was non-significant, despite consistently higher fruit fly abundance during the monsoon season. These findings indicate that attractant type and trap design exert stronger and more consistent effects on fruit fly abundance than season alone, although monsoon conditions generally favour higher populations. The results are similar with Pramanik et al.9 in which the dominant fruit fly species recorded in snake gourd ecosystems using Cue Lure and Methyl Eugenol traps were Bactrocera dorsalis, Zeugodacus cucurbitae, and Zeugodacus tau. The highest species diversity and evenness were observed in methyl eugenol–baited traps. Overall species richness was comparable between cue lure and methyl eugenol traps, as each attracted different groups of fruit fly species.

Host-wise assessment of fruit fly abundance

The host-wise assessment of fruit fly abundance across the sampling period showed pronounced variation among the different hosts, with banana (9,551), forest trees (7,813), guava (6,481), pumpkin (5,477), sweet orange (4,591) and ash gourd (4,027) recording higher total captures (Fig. 6). Moderate captures were recorded on cucumber (2,332), Khasi mandarin (2,594), peach (2,075) and orange (1,816), whereas comparatively lower captures were recorded on plum (1,173), bamboo (1,108), mango (945) and squash (708). Pairwise Wilcoxon comparisons (BH-adjusted) further confirmed significant differences among the hosts. Overall, the results highlight strong host-associated patterns in fruit fly abundance, reflecting differences in host suitability, resource availability, and ecological conditions across the ecosystems.

The diversity indices indicated comparable fruit fly assemblages across trap types, with no significant differences observed (p = 0.8541). Species richness ranged from 17 species in eco-traps to 35 species in McPhail traps, while bottle traps and sweep nets recorded intermediate richness values (28–31 species). Simpson’s diversity (D) varied between 0.509 (eco-trap) and 0.857 (net trap), indicating relatively high dominance evenness in most traps. Similarly, Shannon diversity values ranged from 1.329 to 2.429, and Pielou’s evenness from 0.387 to 0.683, suggesting that although traps differed slightly in their ability to sample species, overall diversity patterns remained statistically non-significant across trap types (supplementary file, Fig. 1).

Across host types, fruit fly diversity also showed non-significant variation (p = 0.9957). Species richness ranged from 12 species in mango to 29 species in banana and guava, indicating host-related differences in species occurrence. Simpson’s diversity values were generally high (0.646–0.840), while Shannon diversity ranged from 1.344 (mango) to 2.083 (sweet orange). Evenness values (Pielou’s index) varied from 0.475 to 0.708, reflecting relatively balanced species distributions across most host plants, despite differences in richness (supplementary file, Fig. 1).

At the ecosystem and location levels, diversity indices showed no significant differences (ecosystem: p = 0.7788; locations: p = 0.8126). Species richness across ecosystems ranged narrowly from 31 to 34 species, with Shannon diversity values of 1.66–2.39 and Simpson’s diversity between 0.694 and 0.858. Location-wise, richness varied from 23 species at Kyrdemkulai to 32 species at Bhoirymbong and Umiam, while Shannon diversity ranged from 1.648 to 2.054 and evenness from 0.526 to 0.593 (supplementary file, Fig. 2). Overall, these indices collectively indicate relatively stable and comparable fruit fly diversity across sampling methods, hosts, ecosystems, and locations, with no strong structuring effect detected by the measured diversity metrics.

Across the three ecosystems viz., forest, fruit tree, and vegetable, month-wise biodiversity indices pooled over three consecutive years (October 2020 till September 2023) showed marked temporal variability, yet no statistically significant monthly differences were detected for Shannon diversity (H′), Simpson’s dominance (D), species richness, or Pielou’s evenness (Kruskal–Wallis tests; p > 0.95 in all cases; (supplementary file, Tables 3, 4 and 5). For instance, Shannon diversity ranged broadly from 0.74 to 2.39, Simpson’s index from 0.32 to 0.88, and richness from 6 to 30 species across months and ecosystems, but without consistent directional trends (supplementary file, Fig. 3). This statistical uniformity indicates that, despite seasonal fluctuations, overall community structure remained stable, suggesting resilience of fruit fly assemblages across habitats. The results aligned with Magurran37; such patterns are characteristic of systems where species turnover occurs without erosion of total diversity, reflecting ecological buffering under fluctuating environmental conditions.

In the forest ecosystem (supplementary file, Table 3), diversity indices generally increased from winter toward the late monsoon period, particularly in 2022–23. Shannon diversity peaked at H′ = 2.24 in July, accompanied by the highest recorded species richness (30 species) and elevated Simpson’s index (D = 0.839), indicating reduced dominance and more equitable species contributions. Pielou’s evenness also increased during monsoon months (up to 0.799–0.925), reflecting balanced assemblages under favourable humidity and host availability. These indicated that structurally complex and less-disturbed habitats, such as forests, promote niche partitioning and sustained insect diversity, especially during periods of high resource input35.

The fruit tree ecosystem (supplementary file, Table 4) exhibited moderate to high diversity with relatively consistent values across seasons, particularly during winter and summer. Shannon diversity ranged from 1.12 to 2.04, with no peaks during March–May (e.g., H′ = 2.04 in March 2021–22), coinciding with flowering and fruiting stages. Species richness remained high during winter months of 2020–21 (up to 24 species), while Pielou’s evenness improved in subsequent years (reaching 0.799), suggesting a transition from dominance-driven assemblages to more evenly structured communities. In contrast, the vegetable ecosystem (supplementary file, Table 5) showed the greatest temporal fluctuation among the three ecosystems. Shannon diversity declined sharply during early monsoon months (e.g., H′ = 0.74–0.82 in July), accompanied by low evenness (0.298–0.374), indicating transient dominance by a few opportunistic species. However, diversity increased during late monsoon, with H′ = 2.39 and D = 0.882 in June 2022–23, reflecting episodic diversification linked to crop heterogeneity and staggered planting. According to Magurran38, agro-ecosystems commonly display such seasonal fluctuations while maintaining long-term functional stability. Comparatively, forest ecosystems supported the most stable and diverse assemblages, fruit tree ecosystem showed intermediate stability, and vegetable ecosystem exhibited high responsiveness to seasonal and management-driven changes. Salazar-Mendoza et al.8 employed McPhail traps baited with various lures to sample male and female fruit flies in guava orchards along an elevational gradient. They documented higher captures of Ceratitis capitata and sixteen Anastrepha species in McPhail traps. Fruit fly abundance was higher at lower elevations and during the dry season, whereas species evenness increased at higher elevations and in the rainy season. Only three species Anastrepha fraterculus, A. ornata, and A. striata were recovered from guava fruits.

Rarefaction

The sample-size–based rarefaction and extrapolation curves (Fig. 9) revealed pronounced differences in fruit fly species richness among trap types and lure treatments, reflecting variation in sampling efficiency and attractant specificity. The net trap recorded the highest estimated richness, reaching ~ 33–35 species at ~ 2,000–3,000 individuals, followed by the McPhail trap (~ 30–31 species; SC = 1; n ≈ 19,017 individuals), Bottle trap (~ 27–28 species; SC ≈ 0.9997; n ≈ 7,062), Sweep net (~ 25–26 species), and Eco trap (~ 23–24 species). The steep initial slopes indicate rapid species accumulation at low sample sizes, while asymptotic trends for net and McPhail traps suggest near-complete sampling, a pattern commonly reported for efficient fruit fly trapping systems39,40. Among lure treatments, Zingerone showed the highest richness (~ 36–38 species), followed by Methyl eugenol (~ 32–33 species), Protein bait (~ 29–30 species), Yeast bait (~ 27–28 species), and Cue-lure (~ 22–24 species). These differences reflect known lure-specific responses of tephritid fruit flies, with plant-derived semiochemicals and male lures attracting a broader range of species than food-based baits41,42. However, such male-biased attraction may inflate apparent richness, potentially underrepresenting females and non-responsive taxa43. In contrast, protein- and yeast-based baits, although yielding lower richness, are ecologically significant due to their effectiveness in attracting nutritionally driven individuals, particularly females, as reported by Prokopy and Roitberg44. Wider confidence intervals at higher extrapolated sample sizes indicate increased uncertainty beyond observed data, a typical feature of extrapolation-based diversity estimates and consistent with the influence of rare species on richness estimation45.

The coverage-based rarefaction/extrapolation curves (Fig. 9) further validated these patterns when species richness was standardized by sampling completeness. At near-complete coverage (~ 0.95–1.00), the net trap achieved the highest richness (~ 32–34 species), followed by McPhail (~ 28–30 species), Bottle (~ 26–27 species), Sweep net (~ 24–25 species), and Eco trap (~ 18–20 species), confirming the superior performance of net and McPhail traps for comprehensive fruit fly surveys. Similarly, at comparable coverage levels, Zingerone supported the greatest richness (~ 34–36 species), with Methyl eugenol (~ 30–32 species) and Protein bait (~ 28–30 species) showing intermediate values, while Yeast bait (~ 26–27 species) and Cue-lure (~ 20–22 species) were less effective, reinforcing earlier reports that male lures generally outperform food-based attractants in species detection but benefit from complementary trapping approaches for a more balanced assessment of fruit fly diversity. The concordance between sample-size– and coverage-based approaches strengthens the inference that net traps and Zingerone lures are most effective for capturing higher fruit fly diversity, as also demonstrated in earlier biodiversity assessments and standardized trapping studies18,40,44.

Zeta diversity

Zeta diversity (Zeta diversity) declined consistently with increasing order (n) across trap types, attractants, and ecosystems, indicating progressive species turnover. Among traps, the highest initial diversity (ζ₁) was observed in Net traps (0.921), followed by McPhail (0.816), Bottle (0.737), Sweep net (0.684), and Eco traps (0.447). Zeta values declined to ζ₁₀ ≈ 0.388, 0.094, 0.028, 0.011, and 0.00004, respectively. Retention ratios were highest in Net traps (0.919–0.897), followed by McPhail (0.811–0.759), Bottle (0.730–0.655), Sweep net (0.676–0.586), and lowest in Eco traps (0.432–0.276), indicating varying levels of species persistence (Figs. 10 and 11).

Zeta diversity decline (ζₙ) of fruit fly communities across dirrerent traps fitted with exponential and power-law decay models.

Zeta diversity decline (ζₙ) of fruit fly communities across different (i) attractants and (ii) ecosystems fitted with exponential and power-law decay models. (F_Ecosystem = forest ecosystem; FT_Ecosystem = fruit trees ecosystem; V_Ecosystem = vegetable ecosystem).

Across attractants, zingerone showed the highest stability (ζ₁: 0.658 to ζ₁₀: 0.0108; ratio: 0.653–0.612), followed by yeast bait (0.579 to 0.0026; 0.573–0.522) and methyl eugenol (0.566 to 0.0020; 0.560–0.507), while soybean bait (0.500 to 0.00049; 0.493–0.433) and cuelure (0.289 to 6.77 × 10⁻⁷; 0.280–0.194) showed higher turnover.

At the ecosystem level, forests showed the highest retention (ζ₁ ≈ 0.895 to ζ₁₀ ≈ 0.277; ratio: 0.892–0.862), followed by fruit crops (0.868 to 0.196; 0.865–0.828) and cucurbit systems (0.816 to 0.094; 0.811–0.759). Across all cases, zeta decline was steep at lower orders (n ≤ 4–5) and stabilized at higher orders, indicating a core assemblage of widespread species. Both exponential and power-law models adequately described these patterns, confirming that fruit fly communities are structured by a few dominant, widely distributed species alongside many rare, habitat- or lure-specific taxa.

Additionally, metacommunity theory suggests that stable habitats such as forests maintain a larger core species pool due to reduced disturbance and greater resource heterogeneity48. Host plant diversity and trophic specialization further regulate fruit fly assemblages through bottom-up interactions, affecting both dominant and rare species persistence49. Together, these mechanisms explain the increasing zeta retention ratio and support the interpretation that forest ecosystems promote conservation of widespread core species while also sustaining rare, habitat-specific taxa. Zeta diversity patterns and seasonal dynamics have important implications for fruit fly conservation and management. Population density peaked during the rainy season (June–July), with higher abundance in hot and wet periods. Species diversity was greatest in natural fallows compared with mango orchards and agroforestry parks, while orchards served as refuge habitats during dry and cold periods, supporting persistence of widespread species reflected in higher-order ζₙ stability19. In Minas Gerais, Coelho et al.10 recorded ten fruit fly species in traps and five species emerging from fruits, along with seven parasitoid species, of which Doryctobracon areolatus was the most frequent. Pitanga and purple guava were identified as key reservoirs of parasitoids, highlighting their ecological importance. These results demonstrate that diverse and semi-natural habitats enhance trophic interactions and support natural biological control. Thus, habitat stability and conservation of native host plants are crucial for maintaining fruit fly parasitoid dynamics and promoting sustainable pest management. The observed zeta diversity patterns can be interpreted in light of fragmentation theory. Consistent with fragmentation theory, fragmentation per se can increase overall gamma diversity through the influx of generalist species46; however, habitat-dependent species often show declines in alpha diversity that are not compensated by beta diversity, resulting in reduced gamma diversity. These patterns underscore that fragmentation effects depend on species’ ecological specialization, emphasizing the conservation value of stable and less-fragmented habitats.

Principal component analysis

The PCA biplot shows the multivariate structure of fruit fly assemblages across ecosystems. The PCA biplot shows the multivariate structure of fruit fly assemblages across ecosystems. Dim1 (11.2%) and Dim2 (10.6%) together explained 21.8% of total variance, revealing partial ecosystem separation typical of diverse insect communities (Fig. 12). Forest samples were distributed toward the positive side of Dim1, indicating more heterogeneous and distinct assemblages, whereas vegetable and fruit tree ecosystems clustered closer to the origin, reflecting greater similarity and dominance of widespread species. Such patterns are consistent with earlier studies showing that structurally complex habitats support higher niche differentiation and species turnover in tephritid communities2,40. Similarly, De Meyer M. et al.47 reported that forest ecosystems harbor more specialized and less overlapping fruit fly assemblages compared to agroecosystems, supporting the observed dispersion of forest samples in ordination space.

Species loadings showed that Dim2 was strongly influenced by B. zonata (0.85), B. wuzhishana (0.80), and B. latifrons (0.67), while Dim1 was influenced by B. gombokensis (0.30), Bactrocera sp. 7 (0.24), and negatively by B. aethriobasis (− 0.30). The strong contributions of these species suggest habitat-linked distributions and their key role in driving community differentiation across ecosystems. This aligns with findings by Vargas R. I. et al.48, who demonstrated that dominant Bactrocera species often structure assemblages due to differences in host range and environmental adaptability. Furthermore, Ekesi S. et al.49 emphasized that species such as B. zonata exhibit strong ecological plasticity, allowing them to dominate across multiple habitats, while others remain habitat-restricted. The observed species-specific loadings are also consistent with niche partitioning and host specialization patterns described by Aluja M. et al.50, reinforcing the role of key taxa in shaping multivariate community structure across heterogeneous landscapes.

Several Dacus and Zeugodacus species are positioned near the center, indicating broad occurrence across ecosystems. Overall, the PCA demonstrates that fruit fly community structure is shaped by ecosystem type, with forest habitats supporting more distinct assemblages. The broader spread of forest–crop interface samples in ordination space mirrors findings that ecotonal habitats harbor mixed fruit fly assemblages and higher beta diversity compared with homogeneous cropping systems50,51. Overall, the PCA patterns observed in this study align well with published research, supporting the interpretation that ecosystem type is a key driver of fruit fly community structure.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of fruit fly (Diptera: Tephritidae) species composition and diversity in Meghalaya, addressing the limited documentation available for the region. The three-year dataset establishes a robust baseline for understanding species richness, seasonal dynamics, host associations, and community structure of Tephritidae in northeastern India. Zeta diversity analysis revealed patterns of species retention and turnover across habitats, highlighting the persistence of widespread core species and the contribution of rare, habitat-specific taxa. PCA further demonstrated distinct community structuring among ecosystems, reflecting habitat-driven differentiation and the influence of dominant species on assemblage composition. Importantly, fragmentation-related patterns suggest that while overall richness may increase due to the influx of generalist species, habitat-dependent taxa are vulnerable to habitat loss and reduced local diversity, emphasizing the need for conserving stable and less-fragmented ecosystems. The recording of Zeugodacus umiam and Z. nasivittatus as new species, along with new national records of B. abbreviata, E. desperata, and P. hilaratum, significantly enhances the taxonomic and quarantine relevance of this work. Overall, the findings provide critical ecological and applied insights for biodiversity conservation, sustainable pest management, and future monitoring of fruit fly assemblages in the region.
