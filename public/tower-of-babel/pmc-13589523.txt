Interpreting Pain-Mood Associations in a Secondary Analysis of a Bariatric Surgery Trial [Letter]
Authors: Haifeng Wang, Ang Liu
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with interest the secondary analysis by Zhang et al, which examined whether transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB) or quadratus lumborum block (QLB), when added to general anesthesia, was associated with improved anxiety and depressive symptoms after laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy.1 The authors reported greater reductions in Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD) and Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) scores among patients receiving peripheral nerve blocks and suggested that relief of visceral pain might contribute to these psychological benefits. The analysis addresses an important aspect of recovery after bariatric surgery. However, three issues warrant clarification.

First, the relation between the registered trial and the present analysis requires more complete reporting. The Chinese Clinical Trial Registry entry (ChiCTR1800019236) lists pain, emotion, cognition, and memory as primary outcomes but does not specify the instruments, assessment times, outcome hierarchy, or statistical methods. The registry planned to enroll 150 participants, whereas the parent randomized trial reported by Xue et al enrolled 225 participants—73 in the general anesthesia group and 76 each in the TAPB and QLB groups—and the present secondary analysis used the same cohort.2 The article states that the study ended on August 21, 2020, whereas the registry, last updated in September 2021, lists recruitment through October 31, 2022 and continues to describe the study as recruiting. These differences may reflect legitimate amendments, but their timing and rationale are not reported. CONSORT 2025 recommends identifying the trial protocol and statistical analysis plan and describing important changes made after trial commencement.3 We therefore invite the authors to provide the dated protocol, statistical analysis plan, and documentation supporting the revised sample size and study timeline.

The retrospective observed-power analysis does not resolve the sample-size issue. The authors calculated power from the observed HAMD and HAMA effect sizes and stated that values exceeding 0.80 verified the adequacy of the sample. However, observed power is largely determined by the observed effect and P value and provides little information beyond that conveyed by the confidence interval.4 It cannot establish whether increasing enrollment from 150 to 225 participants was planned before examination of the data. Precision should instead be evaluated using effect estimates and confidence intervals, together with a clinically meaningful target difference established before analysis.

Second, the longitudinal model and multiplicity strategy require clarification. The article reports significant time-by-group interactions for HAMD and HAMA scores (P < 0.001), implying that psychological trajectories differed among the three groups. However, Table 2 presents coefficients for follow-up time and treatment group without reporting the interaction coefficients, confidence intervals, or time-specific between-group contrasts. Group main effects alone do not establish different trajectories over time. The definitions of the parallel outcomes labeled “HAMD score” and “changes in HAMD score”, and their corresponding HAMA outcomes, also require explanation. Reporting the model equation, random-effects structure, covariance assumptions, interaction estimates, and prespecified contrasts would permit a more direct interpretation.

Multiplicity is also relevant. The analysis includes two psychological scales, continuous and categorical outcomes, original and change scores, three treatment groups, several time points, pairwise comparisons, correlations, and restricted cubic spline models. Bonferroni adjustment is described for selected post hoc comparisons, but no overall inferential hierarchy or multiplicity strategy is provided. Multiplicity should be considered at the design, analysis, and interpretation stages, particularly when several related hypotheses are evaluated.5 This concern is important because some findings central to the interpretation were near the conventional significance threshold, including the QLB coefficient for HAMD (P = 0.045 or P = 0.049, depending on the model) and the correlation between visceral pain and depressive symptoms (r = 0.13; P = 0.047). These findings should be regarded as exploratory unless they remain robust under a prespecified multiplicity strategy. The clinical importance of between-group differences of approximately 1 to 2 HAMD or HAMA points should also be considered separately from statistical significance.

Third, the data do not establish that pain relief explains the psychological improvement or that weight loss does not. The reported correlations between peak visceral pain during the first 48 postoperative hours and subsequent psychological scores were modest (r = 0.13–0.17). Moreover, the adjusted restricted cubic spline analyses showed a significant association only for HAMA at 1 month (P = 0.02); the corresponding associations with HAMD at 1 and 3 months (P = 0.59 and P = 0.60) and HAMA at 3 months (P = 0.11) were not significant. Thus, the adjusted analyses do not demonstrate a consistent association between acute pain and longer-term psychological outcomes.

In addition, Figure 2 correlates psychological scores with absolute weight at each assessment rather than with within-participant weight loss. Weight data were unavailable for 23 participants, and the handling of these missing observations was not described. An absence of correlation with absolute weight cannot demonstrate that weight reduction is unrelated to psychological improvement. More importantly, postoperative pain is a postrandomization variable affected by the intervention. A correlation between this potential mediator and the outcome does not quantify mediation. A formal analysis would require a defined causal estimand, adjustment for baseline psychological status and mediator-outcome confounding, estimates of direct and indirect effects, and sensitivity analyses for the required assumptions. The AGReMA statement provides relevant guidance for conducting and reporting such analyses.6

In summary, this secondary analysis raises an important hypothesis regarding regional analgesia and psychological recovery after bariatric surgery. Nevertheless, clarification of the protocol and sample-size amendments, complete reporting of the longitudinal and multiplicity analyses, and a formal evaluation of the proposed pain-mediated pathway are needed. Until such analyses are available, the conclusion that mood improvement was related to visceral pain relief “instead of weight loss” should be regarded as exploratory.
