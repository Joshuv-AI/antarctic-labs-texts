High-flow Nasal Cannula Oxygen vs Non-invasive Ventilation with Passive Humidification: Are We Comparing Like with Like?
Authors: Sanjay Singhal, Mohan Gurjar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow to cite this article: Singhal S, Gurjar M. High-flow Nasal Cannula Oxygen vs Non-invasive Ventilation with Passive Humidification: Are We Comparing Like with Like? Indian J Crit Care Med 2026;30(5):359–360.Keywords: Endotracheal extubation, Noninvasive ventilation, Respiratory therapy, Ventilator weaning

Endotracheal intubation (ETI) and invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), although essential for critically ill patients, are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality due to underlying illness-related severity and an increased risk of nosocomial infections.1 Early weaning and timely extubation are therefore critical strategies to mitigate these risks. However, despite meeting all weaning criteria and successfully completing a spontaneous breathing trial, approximately 10–20% of patients experience extubation failure.2 Given the grave prognosis associated with extubation failure, considerable emphasis has been placed on its prevention.2

Traditionally, standard oxygen therapy has been used to correct post-extubation hypoxemia, the most common cause of weaning failure.3 While it improves oxygenation, it does not address the underlying pathophysiology of post-extubation acute respiratory failure (ARF).4 In contrast, non-invasive ventilation (NIV) delivers inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP), which unloads the diaphragm and reduces the work of breathing (WOB), and expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP), which prevents alveolar collapse at end-expiration. These effects increase aerated lung volume, improve lung compliance, reduce ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, and enhance oxygenation. Consequently, NIV has emerged as an effective preventive strategy, particularly in patients at high risk of extubation failure.1 Nevertheless, NIV is associated with notable complications, including gastric distension, claustrophobia, mask discomfort, eye irritation, and nasal bridge ulceration, which contribute to intolerance and failure in approximately 15–25% of patients.5

Oxygen therapy with a high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), in contrast, delivers optimally heated and humidified oxygen at high flow rates (≥30 L/min) via a heated circuit and a large-bore nasal cannula, allowing delivery of up to 100% inspired oxygen (FiO2).6 High-flow nasal cannula provides a stable FiO2, reduces anatomical dead space, and generates a low level of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP).6 Additionally, it facilitates secretion clearance, reduces airway resistance, preserves mucociliary function, and enhances patient comfort through active humidification.6 Owing to these advantages, HFNC has emerged as a promising alternative to NIV for preventing extubation failure.

Several randomized controlled trials and recent meta-analyses comparing HFNC with NIV have shown that HFNC is non-inferior to NIV for preventing reintubation in high-risk patients.7–10 High-flow nasal cannula has also been associated with fewer adverse events and improved patient comfort. However, a critical limitation of these studies is the lack of clarity about whether humidification—active or passive—is used with NIV.

In this context, a randomized controlled trial by Hernandez et al. enrolled 142 patients who received either NIV with active humidification via a face mask or HFNC.11 The study reported a lower reintubation rate within 7 days in the NIV group (23.6%) than in the HFNC group (33.3%), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.27). Despite the clinically meaningful absolute reduction of approximately 10%, the study may have been underpowered. Additionally, the inclusion of patients at low-to-intermediate risk of extubation failure and baseline imbalances—such as a longer IMV duration in the NIV group—may have attenuated the observed effect.

In the current issue of the Indian Journal of Critical Care Medicine, Patil et al. explored the utility of NIV vs HFNC in the reduction of extubation failure among high-risk patient populations.12 This study included 150 patients at high risk of extubation failure, defined by criteria such as age >65 years, chronic respiratory disease, congestive heart failure, prolonged IMV (≥7 days), pre-extubation hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45 mm Hg), difficult weaning, obesity (BMI >30), and APACHE-II score >12 on the day of extubation. Patients were randomized to receive either NIV with passive humidification using a heat-and-moisture exchanger (HME) or HFNC for 48 hours. The primary outcome—reintubation within 48 hours—was significantly lower in the HFNC group (18.7%) compared to the NIV group (33.3%) (p = 0.041). Secondary outcomes, including post-extubation respiratory failure at 7 days, nosocomial pneumonia, sepsis, therapy intolerance, hospital length of stay, and mortality, also favored HFNC, alongside better patient comfort.

The observed differences between the studies by Hernandez et al. and Patil et al. may, at least in part, be explained by differences in humidification strategies.11,12 While the former employed active humidification with NIV, the latter used passive humidification (HME). Evidence from both clinical and bench studies suggests that NIV with HME may fail to reduce WOB, likely because of increased dead space and airway resistance.6 In contrast, active humidification using heated humidifiers (HH) improves these parameters. Although a randomized trial by Lellouche et al. did not demonstrate a significant difference in intubation rates between HME and HH in patients receiving NIV for ARF, the study was underpowered, and environmental factors, such as the lack of air conditioning, may have influenced the results.6,13 Ambient air conditioning reduces absolute humidity (AH), which directly impacts the efficiency of HME.6 This may partly explain the findings of Patil et al., who conducted their study in an ICU where air conditioning could have reduced ambient humidity, thereby compromising HME performance.12 Other factors, such as flow rates, unintentional air leaks, NIV settings, and high secretion burdens, can compromise HME performance and ultimately NIV success.6

Inadequate humidification of inspired gases can have clinically significant consequences. Reduced humidification may lead to inspissated secretions, increased sputum viscosity, airway obstruction, and difficult reintubation.6 Supporting this, Xu and Liu. demonstrated that HFNC was associated with lower sputum viscosity at 48 hours post-extubation compared to NIV in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.9 Similarly, studies in COVID-19 patients receiving high FiO2 and high minute ventilation have shown clinical improvement when switching from HME to heated humidification.6

While the current study by Patil et al. supports the use of HFNC to prevent extubation failure in high-risk patients, the role of humidification in modulating NIV efficacy remains insufficiently explored.12 Given the heterogeneity in humidification strategies and the inconsistent findings across studies, there is a compelling need for large, multicenter randomized trials. Such studies should specifically compare NIV with optimized humidification strategies—particularly HME with minimal dead space vs heated humidification—against HFNC in a well-defined high-risk population, including those with severe hypercapnia, high secretion burden, and poor tolerance to NIV.

Ultimately, the apparent superiority of HFNC over NIV in preventing extubation failure may not reflect differences in modality alone, but rather in how these therapies are delivered—particularly with respect to humidification. The current evidence suggests that suboptimal humidification may compromise NIV performance, potentially biasing comparisons in favor of HFNC. Until adequately powered trials using optimized NIV strategies—especially with active humidification—are available, clinicians should interpret existing data with caution. In high-risk patients, the choice between HFNC and NIV should be individualized, with careful attention to physiological needs, secretion burden, and patient tolerance, recognizing that humidification may be the critical, yet underappreciated, determinant of success.
