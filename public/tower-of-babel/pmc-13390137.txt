Stereopsis deficits in schizophrenia: a systematic review with exploratory meta-analysis
Authors: Manju Varshini Bhuvaneswaran, Sananthya Karthikeyan, Dharani Ramamurthy, Madhumitha Haridoss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly sensory processing abnormalities are increasingly recognized as core features of schizophrenia. While impairments in contrast sensitivity, color vision and visual integration are well established, stereopsis (depth perception) has received limited systematic evaluation. This study systematically reviews and synthesizes available evidence on stereopsis deficits in schizophrenia, combining narrative synthesis of all eligible studies with an exploratory meta-analysis of studies reporting sufficiently comparable quantitative data.MethodsThis review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251065135). PubMed, Scopus, and Embase databases were searched, alongside Google Scholar and ClinicalTrials.gov.in, from inception to 16 April 2026. Studies were imported to Rayyan for screening. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the risk of bias. Random-effects meta-analysis pooled standardized mean differences in stereopsis thresholds. Studies unsuitable for pooling were narratively synthesized.ResultsThis systematic review included six studies that assessed stereopsis in individuals with schizophrenia (n = 200) and compared with healthy controls (n = 167). Reduced stereopsis was reported across the included studies. Four studies were eligible for exploratory quantitative synthesis. The pooled standardized mean difference (SMD = 0.824, 95% CI: 0.605 to 1.043) suggested poorer stereopsis performance in schizophrenia. The heterogeneity was low to moderate (I2 = 25.9%, τ² = 0.0013, p = 0.2563). Subgroup analysis suggested a deficit with Titmus Stereotest (SMD = 0.890, 95% CI 0.637 to 1.143; I2 = 0%) and Graded Circles tests (SMD = 0.608, 95% CI -0.083 to 1.300; I2 = 63.3%). Leave-one-out analysis indicated directionally stable effects (SMD range 0.703 to 0.898). Overall methodological quality was good (NOS > 6) with low risk of bias. However, given the small number of studies, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously as preliminary and hypothesis-generating.ConclusionsThis systematic review suggests that currently available studies generally report reduced stereopsis in individuals with schizophrenia. The exploratory meta-analysis, based on only four studies, provides preliminary quantitative support but is insufficient for definitive inference. Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) is a chronic mental health condition with a significant burden on patients, families and healthcare systems [1, 2]. Traditionally characterized by positive and negative symptoms and cognitive impairments, SZ has also been increasingly understood as a disorder of early sensory processing [3, 4]. Deficits in the visual domain, including reduced contrast sensitivity, impaired motion processing, abnormal perceptual organization and diminished susceptibility to visual illusions, have been consistently documented [5–9]. These abnormalities are thought to arise from dysfunction in magnocellular pathways, parvocellular pathways and cortical integration processes, providing a sensory foundation for higher-order cognitive and social impairments [8, 10, 11].

Despite these advances, the stereopsis (depth perception) in individuals with SZ has received only limited systematic investigation. Stereopsis arises from the disparity between the two eyes and is an essential binocular function for perceiving depth, visuomotor coordination, and helps navigate and interact with the environment. Impairments in stereopsis have been associated with functional difficulties in tasks such as object manipulation, mobility, and eye-hand coordination across both clinical and non-clinical populations [4, 12, 13].

Early studies on depth perception in SZ suggested abnormalities, often influenced by affective and contextual factors [3, 10]. However, these studies used non-standardized tasks and provided qualitative rather than quantitative evidence. More recently, standardized clinical stereopsis tests (e.g., Titmus, Graded Circles) have demonstrated measurable deficits in individuals with SZ compared to controls [3, 13]. Such deficits have been observed even in first-episode drug-naïve patients, suggesting they are not secondary to chronic illness or antipsychotic treatment [13].

Given the limited but growing body of literature, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to comprehensively summarize all available studies on stereopsis in SZ, and summarize available evidence and, where feasible, provide an exploratory quantitative estimate of the magnitude of impairment using meta-analysis. By elucidating the nature and extent of stereopsis deficits in SZ, this review seeks to advance knowledge of sensory processing abnormalities and their implications for clinical functional outcomes in SZ.

Methods

Search strategy, inclusion criteria, and exclusion criteria

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The protocol for this study was registered in PROSPERO on 21st June 2025 (CRD420251065135). Databases like PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Google Scholar were used to perform a comprehensive literature search from inception to 16th April 2026. The PECO framework was used: P (Population), children or adults of any age group, E (Exposure) individuals with SZ, C (Comparator) individuals without SZ, and O (Outcome) stereopsis deficits. The search strategies and keywords were designed so as to capture studies examining association between stereopsis and SZ. Additionally, backward snowballing was performed by screening the reference lists of all included articles to identify further relevant studies. The detailed search strategy is presented in Appendix 2: Tables 1, 2 and 3. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) participants of any age diagnosed with SZ; (2) stereopsis assessed using standardized clinical tests such as the Randot stereotest, Titmus graded circles stereotest, Titmus fly stereotest and Frisby stereotest and (3) Studies with or without a control group were included, where present, healthy individuals without SZ served as the comparators. Studies published in languages other than English were also included if an English translation was available. Studies were excluded if they involved participants with psychiatric disorders other than SZ (e.g., bipolar disorder or major depressive disorder), ocular pathologies, or neurological disorders. All included studies reported participants with normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity and excluded individuals with more than one line difference between the two eyes. The primary outcome measured was reduction in depth perception among individuals with SZ, assessed using standardized stereotests at a single time-point evaluation, typically conducted at baseline. Data for this outcome is expressed in log units and synthesized using the standardized mean difference as the summary measure.

Study screening

Studies identified through the database search were imported into Rayyan, a web-based tool for systematic screening [14]. Two reviewers (BMV and SK) independently conducted the screening after duplicate removal. Discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and when necessary, consultation with a third adjudicator (MH). References listed in the included studies were checked manually to identify any additional relevant articles.

Data extraction

Microsoft Excel 365 was used to design the data extraction form and standardized by pilot data extraction for three studies. Two reviewers (BMV and SK) independently extracted the relevant data from the included studies and discrepancies were discussed and resolved with consensus between both reviewers and a third adjudicator (MH). Comprehensive information from each eligible study was compiled in a dataset for the meta-analysis to ensure consistency and accuracy in data extraction. Study identifiers (author, year, country and DOI), study design, characteristics and participant demographics such as sample size, age, duration of illness and education level were the key variables recorded. Clinical parameters such as standardized psychiatric assessments, Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS), Scale for Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS) and Scale for Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS) to evaluate symptom severity in participants with SZ were also included, where available.

Stereopsis related variable included type of stereotest used, mean stereoacuity scores and corresponding standard deviations or standard errors for both cases and controls. Where multiple stereopsis outcomes were reported within a single study, a single outcome was selected based on comparability across studies or averaged where appropriate. Visual acuity of the study participants was also documented to confirm normal vision. Additionally, medication type, illness duration, medication status and diagnostic criteria were also extracted to enable comparability across studies. Plotdigitizer was used to extract data from the graphs when the statistics were not available in the text [15].

All included studies were considered in the narrative synthesis. Studies were eligible for meta-analysis only if they reported stereopsis outcomes using comparable standardized measures and provided sufficient statistical data (mean and standard deviation or convertible metrics). Studies with incompatible outcome measures, insufficient data or substantially different methodologies were excluded from quantitative synthesis but were retained in the narrative review.

Risk of bias

Risk of bias (RoB) of the included six studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [16]. Quality assessment of the included studies was independently performed by the reviewers (BMV and SK) and the discrepancies were resolved through consensus. The Newcastle Ottawa Scale [NOS] assesses quality of study across three domains: Selection (up to four stars), which examines the representativeness and appropriateness of cases and controls; Comparability (up to two stars), which evaluates control for key confounders; and Exposure (up to three stars), which assesses the ascertainment and consistency of exposure measurement. Studies were categorized as poor (0–3), fair (4–6), or good (7–9) quality. All included studies compared participants with SZ (cases) and healthy or non-psychiatric controls (controls). Though one study employed an experimental comparative design, it involved a case-control framework, hence was evaluated using the NOS as it provides a structured and transparent framework for evaluating the validity of observational studies, thereby promoting rigor and consistency in systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Statistical analysis

R Studio version 4.5.1 was used to conduct all analyses. Studies lacking sufficient quantitative data for pooling were summarized narratively. For all two-sided tests, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [17]. Stereopsis thresholds were log-transformed (base 10) where necessary to achieve data normalization. For studies reporting comparable quantitative data (means and standard deviations or their equivalents), a random-effects meta-analysis was performed to pool standardized mean differences (SMD) in stereopsis thresholds between individuals with SZ and healthy controls (HC).

Given the limited number of eligible studies, the meta-analysis was conducted as exploratory and intended to summarize the current direction and approximate magnitude of association rather than provide confirmatory pooled estimates.

Cohen’s d was used to calculate the standardized mean difference, with positive values indicating higher thresholds of stereopsis among individuals with SZ. Heterogeneity between the studies were assessed using the I² Statistic, Cochran’s Q test (reported as chi-square), and τ² (tau-squared). Heterogeneity was considered statistically significant if I² > 25% or p < 0.10 for the Q test, indicating variability among studies. A random-effects model based on the Der Simonian and Laird method [18] was applied to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimate and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to determine if any single study disproportionately influenced the overall effect size.

Publication bias was not assessed using the funnel plots or Egger’s test due to limited number of included studies. A subgroup analysis was conducted based on the type of stereopsis test used (Graded Circles Stereotest vs. Titmus Stereopsis Test) to explore the potential source of heterogeneity.

Protocol registration and deviations

This systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251065135) and was conducted in close accordance with the registered protocol with only minor deviations. The use of mean difference for continuous outcomes was specified in the original protocol, but the analysis was performed using the standardized mean difference (SMD) to ensure comparability of results due to variation in the stereopsis tests and measurement scales across the included studies. The protocol also planned for the pooling of risk ratios for dichotomous outcomes, but as none of the included studies reported stereopsis as binary data, this analysis could not be undertaken. Additionally, the planned subgroup analysis comparing First-Episode Drug-Naive (FEDN) patients and those under Long-Duration Medical Treatment (LDMT) was not statistically conducted because of the limited number of eligible studies. Nevertheless, relevant findings are summarized narratively to provide contextual insights.

Results

Studies for review and meta-analysis based on PRISMA guidelines

The systematic search initially identified 1,780 studies. After removing duplicates, 1,157 articles were screened for eligibility by title and abstract. Following this, 83 full-text articles were evaluated for inclusion. Ultimately, six studies met the inclusion criteria and were considered for review, while 77 studies, including three non-retrievable records in languages other than English, were excluded. All the six included studies were synthesized narratively, while only four studies provided quantitative data suitable for meta-analysis; therefore, the meta-analysis was conducted on an exploratory basis. Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias in the included studies were assessed across standard domains (selection, comparability and exposure), with overall good methodological quality. Clear case definitions and good case representativeness strengthened the methodology of the studies, however a few studies had moderate risk of bias driven by the concerns in the definition and selection of controls that could affect comparability. All studies adjusted for key confounders such as age and education status. Exposure was measured consistently across groups, with low and similar non-response rates. Overall, the included studies had good methodological quality and low risk of bias as depicted with Table 1.

The exploratory meta-analysis comprised of 200 participants in the SZ group and 167 in the control group. The sample size of individual studies ranged from 17 to 180. The Stereoacuity values were log-transformed for normality prior to analysis. Given the small number of eligible studies, the meta-analysis was considered exploratory in nature and standardized mean difference were pooled to estimate group difference in stereopsis between individuals with SZ and HC.

Schechter et al. [10] reported a mean stereoacuity of 142 arcseconds in 17 SZ vs. 55 arcseconds in 19 controls, corresponding to a large effect size (t = 3.2, Cohen’s d ≈ 1.07, p = 0.006). Kantrowitz et al. [19] found consistent deficits across multiple stereopsis paradigms, including the Graded Circles (d ≈ 0.89), Random Dot (d ≈ 0.74), Frisby (d ≈ 0.65), with significant overall effect F = 10.2 (p = 0.003). Hui et al. [3] observed mean log arcseconds = 1.94 in SZ vs. 1.64 in controls, corresponding to a large effect size (d ≈ 0.95). Wang et al. [13] found similar impairments in both FEDN and LDMT subgroups with a moderate effect size (η2 = 0.15, p = 0.001).

Earlier experimental works by Nelson and Caldwell et al. [20] used the Howard-Dolman apparatus with affective vs. neural stimuli (faces vs. geometric shapes) in 22 SZ and 22 healthy controls. Group means did not differ significantly (F = 0.15), though SZ exhibited higher response variability (SDs ranging from 81 to 118 vs. 56–70 in controls) and failed to differentiate among the affective stimuli (F = 0.82), whereas controls demonstrated significant differentiation (F = 2.56). Johannsen et al. [21] examined 120 SZ across chronicity groups and 20 controls, reporting a linear decline in depth accuracy with chronicity (F = 2.46, p < 0.05). Across studies, stereopsis deficits appeared independently of symptom severity, medication, or illness duration and were already evident in FEDN patients [13]. Detection rates declined at disparities of less than 63 arcseconds.

Overall, these findings suggest that stereopsis deficits in SZ are consistent across methodologies, although variability in assessment techniques and susceptibility to monocular cues may influence the magnitude of observed effects.

Estimated effect of stereopsis for SZ and HC across selected studies

The exploratory pooled standardized mean difference for stereopsis is 0.824 [95% CI: 0.605 to 1.043], suggesting a tendency towards poorer stereopsis performance among individuals with SZ compared to HC. A low degree of heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 25.9%, τ² = 0.0013, p = 0.2563), indicating low variability between the studies. (Fig. 2). Since only four studies contributed data, this estimate should be interpreted as a preliminary summary rather than a stable population effect.

Forest plot from exploratory random-effects meta-analysis of standardized mean differences (SMD) comparing stereopsis performance between individuals with SZ and HC

Subgroup analysis based on study methodology

Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine whether the type of stereopsis test accounted for heterogeneity (Fig. 3). Two studies [10, 19] used the Graded Circles Stereo test. The pooled effect size indicated a moderate difference favoring controls (SMD = 0.608, 95% CI: -0.083 to 1.300). However, the confidence interval crossed zero, indicating that this effect did not reach statistical significance. Substantial heterogeneity was observed within this subgroup (I² = 63.35, τ² = 0.1587, p = 0.0987). In contrast, two other studies [3, 13] using the Titmus Stereopsis test yielded a pooled SMD of 0.890 [95% CI: 0.637 to 1,143]. No heterogeneity was detected within this subgroup (I² = 0%, τ² = 0, p = 0.5117), indicating high consistency between studies. However, the test for subgroup differences (χ² = 0.56, df = 1, p = 0.45360) provided no evidence that the type of stereopsis test significantly moderated the overall effect. There is no established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for stereoacuity. Based on the pooled estimates, the findings suggest reduced stereopsis in SZ. However clinical significance cannot be established due to the absence of defined thresholds of stereopsis in individuals with SZ.

Subgroup analysis stratified by stereopsis test used.

Sensitivity (leave-one-out) analysis for the pooled estimate

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the stability of the pooled estimate (Fig. 4). The pooled standardized mean difference remained stable across all iterations, ranging from 0.703 to 0.898, with a consistent overall standardized mean difference estimate of 0.824 [95% CI: 0.605, 1.043, p < 0.0001]. Notably omission of Schechter et al. yielded the largest effect size (SMD = 0.898), whereas the omission of Hui et al. resulted in the smallest estimate (SMD = 0.703). Heterogeneity estimates varied modestly depending on the study removed. The exclusion of Schechter et al. reduced heterogeneity to zero (I² = 0%), suggesting that this study may contribute to between-study variability. In contrast, omission of Kantrowitz et al. or Wang et al. increased heterogeneity (I² = 48.1% and I² = 49.6%, respectively) while omission of Hui et al. resulted in moderate heterogeneity (I² = 30.2%).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrating variability of exploratory pooled estimates

The direction and magnitude of the effect remained consistent across all analyses. However, variation in effect size and heterogeneity indicates sensitivity to study composition, reinforcing the exploratory nature of the pooled estimate.

Discussion

This systematic review and exploratory meta-analysis synthesized evidence from six studies on the stereopsis performance in individuals with SZ compared to HC. Across paradigms, most of the studies reported moderate to large deficits that were evident early in the illness, including first episode drug naive patients, and appeared independent of symptom severity, medication status or illness duration. And thereby likely to be characteristic of the aberrant visual developmental trajectory. The exploratory meta-analysis of four studies suggested a directionally consistent signal towards poorer stereopsis in SZ (pooled SMD = 0.824) corresponding to stereoacuity levels lesser than normative values, however, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of studies and its exploratory nature. Subgroup analysis indicated more consistent deficits with Titmus test, while sensitivity analysis indicated the stability of the effect direction. Overall, the findings suggest a reduction in stereopsis in SZ, though the magnitude and consistency of the deficit remain uncertain. Given that stereopsis is an integral component of visual function, these findings contribute to growing evidence of widespread early sensory processing abnormalities in SZ, alongside reported deficits in contrast sensitivity and color perception [22–30].

A pattern observed in this review is that the type of stereopsis test used appeared to influence the magnitude and consistency of the observed deficits. Studies using local, contour-based stereotests, such as the Titmus Fly or Wirth Circles, showed relatively more consistent differences between patients and controls. These tasks measure fine, local disparity processing and have attentional demands, making them more sensitive to primary sensory deficits. In contrast, studies using global random-dot stereograms produced less consistent findings.

Subgroup analysis supported this pattern with Titmus-based studies showing a significant and homogenous deficit (SMD = 0.890, I2 = 0%) in contrast to Graded Circles which showed a non-significant effect with greater heterogeneity. Although the subgroup difference was not statistically significant, the observed trend suggests that test characteristics may influence sensitivity to stereopsis deficits, rather than confirming differential impairment. A plausible explanation lies in the nature of the visual stimuli. The Titmus test contains monocular cues, allowing partial compensation through non-binocular mechanisms, whereas graded or random-dot stereograms rely more strictly on true binocular disparity processing. Individuals with SZ may rely more heavily on monocular cues due to impairments in binocular integration, resulting in relatively better performance on tests such as Titmus compared to global stereopsis tasks. Additionally, global stereopsis requires higher-level visual integration, sustained attention, and top-down control functions that are often impaired in SZ [31, 32]. Therefore, the variability in global stereopsis findings likely reflects the combined impact of both sensory and cognitive difficulties, rather than an absence of depth-perception impairment [33].

Although the included studies showed substantial heterogeneity most of this variability could be explained by differences in methodology. Important factors included the stereopsis paradigm used, the disparity range tested, viewing conditions, how strictly monocular cues were removed, and differences in stimulus duration. Clinical factors such as illness duration, chronicity and medication exposure also varied widely across samples. Antipsychotic and anticholinergic medications can influence visual processing and eye movements [34–38]. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the direction of effect remained consistent. However, variation in effect size and heterogeneity suggests sensitivity to study composition. The quantitative synthesis should be interpreted in the context of sparse data. Meta-analysis based on a very small number of studies can be informative for evidence mapping, but pooled estimates may be disproportionately influenced by study-level characteristics and should not be considered definitive. Accordingly, the present exploratory meta-analysis is best viewed as hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted with caution.

Evidence from existing literature indicates that deficits in stereopsis may not be specific to SZ. Research on schizotypal personality and major depressive disorder generally reports deficits in stereopsis reflecting a broader transdiagnostic visual processing abnormality [39–41]. Such deficits may relate to illness severity, genetic vulnerability or underlying neurodevelopmental alterations associated with psychosis-spectrum conditions.

The observed effect sizes suggest reduced stereopsis performance in SZ compared to HC, indicating the presence of a visual processing abnormality. This pattern aligns with evidence pointing to dorsal visual stream, magnocellular pathway and parvocellular pathway involvement in SZ [8–11, 42]. However, these interpretations should be considered cautiously given the limited and heterogeneous evidence base.

However, the magnocellular pathway dysfunction in SZ remains an area of active investigation. Several studies provide evidence supporting magnocellular involvement, including findings of reduced magnocellular-driven electrophysiological responses and impaired processing of low spatial frequency stimuli [43, 44]. Javitt et al. (2006, 2008) have demonstrated associations between motion processing deficits and reduced magnocellular activation as well as diminished neural responses to low spatial frequency visual input, suggesting early sensory pathway involvement [43, 44]. In contrast, Skottun & Skoyles et al. (2007) demonstrated that contrast sensitivity reductions in SZ were uniform across spatial frequencies rather than selective for low frequencies, as it relies more on the parvocellular mechanism [11]. Additionally, emerging evidence highlights the role of precortical abnormalities, including visual pathway dysfunction as well as higher-order visual integration deficits that occur trans-diagnostically in association with positive psychotic symptoms rather than being specific to SZ [4, 45]. These findings suggest that stereopsis deficits in schizophrenia are likely multifactorial and not attributable to a single visual pathway. Thus, stereopsis deficits in SZ likely reflect a combination of early sensory, attentional and integrative abnormalities rather than a single dorsal stream pathway dysfunction [4, 45].

Recent psychophysical evidence in individuals with psychotic disorder further supports hierarchical visual processing abnormalities. Impairments in both contrast detection and binocular depth perception have been demonstrated with greater deficits observed in depth processing, suggesting cumulative dysfunction across multiple stages of visual processing. Although this study included individuals with schizophrenia, it majorly explored the contrast sensitivity and depth perception in individuals with psychotic disorders and did not report subgroup-specific outcomes. They also utilized experimental paradigms rather than standardized clinical stereopsis tests. Therefore, it was not included in the systematic synthesis but offer useful context for interpreting the present findings [46].

There also exist certain gaps in the current literature that needs to be considered when interpreting these findings. The overall number of stereopsis studies in SZ remains small, which limits the strength of moderator analysis and makes it difficult to evaluate the presence of publication bias. Additionally, the exclusion of studies using experimental stereopsis paradigms with differing stimulus characteristics and measurement approaches, not directly comparable with standardized clinical stereotests, may limit the comprehensiveness of the synthesis and should be considered as a potential source of bias. There is also a notable lack of research involving medication-naïve or first-episode patients. Because of this, it remains uncertain whether stereopsis deficits represent an early, primary feature of the disorder or whether they develop or worsen with illness progression, treatment exposure, or other secondary factors. Although age was generally controlled across studies through matching and inclusion criteria, other potentially relevant factors, such as chronic stress and trauma, were not consistently assessed or reported and may contribute to variability in sensory processing outcomes. Evidence from contrast sensitivity studies shows that unmedicated first-episode patients performed better than controls, with deficits emerging only after antipsychotic treatment or later in the illness course [23]. A similar trajectory could potentially exist for stereopsis, but the current evidence base is too limited to make a definitive conclusion.

These gaps highlight the need for more methodologically rigorous studies using standardized protocols for stereopsis assessment with good control over the other visual parameters that can act as confounders and particularly in early-stage and unmedicated populations. A comprehensive assessment including other visual measures such as contrast sensitivity, motion perception and neuroimaging of the dorsal-stream would help provide insight on depth abnormalities are part of a broader visual-processing phenotype or a distinct feature.

This study provides a synthesis of available evidence suggesting stereopsis deficits in SZ with consistently reduced performance, particularly on local stereopsis tasks-compared to controls. However, given the small number of studies included in this exploratory meta-analysis, these findings should be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution. The heterogeneity across the included studies were driven by differences in stereotest paradigms, methodological quality, clinical characteristics and control of visual confounders, limits the precision with which these effects can be interpreted. The small number of available studies, the lack of standardized testing protocols, and scarcity of data from first-episode drug-naïve patients further constrain the strength of the evidence. Additionally, stereopsis deficits reported in individuals with SZ may not be specific and unique to SZ alone as similar deficits have been reported in other psychiatric disorders as well. These limitations need to be addressed in future studies through well-designed, adequately powered and methodologically consistent studies that can elucidate the nature, origin and clinical relevance of stereopsis deficits in SZ.

Conclusion

This systematic review indicates that currently available studies generally report poorer stereopsis performance in individuals with SZ. The accompanying exploratory meta-analysis, limited to four studies with comparable data, offers preliminary quantitative support but should not be interpreted as confirmatory evidence. In future adequately powered, methodologically standardized studies are required before reliable pooled estimates and clinical implications can be established. Future studies should incorporate larger, well-characterized cohorts across the SZ spectrum, leverage multimodal approaches including Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), cognitive profiling and multivariate modelling to clarify pathways linking SZ to stereopsis deficits. It might be essential for determining whether specific stereopsis measures, alone or in combination, hold value as early biomarkers or clinically meaningful indicators in SZ.
