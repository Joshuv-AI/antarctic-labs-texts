On bursty star formation during cosmological reionization — influence on the metal and dust content of low-mass galaxies
Anand Menon, Sreedhar Balu, Chris Power

Abstract
Observations indicate that high-redshift galaxies undergo episodic star formation bursts, driving strong outflows that expel gas and suppress accretion. We investigate the consequences for metal and dust content of galaxies at z ≥ 5 z\geq\!5 using our semi-analytical model, Ashvini . We track gas-phase and stellar metallicities ( Z g , Z ⋆ Z_{\text{g}},Z_{\star} ) and dust mass ( M d M_{\rm d} ) in dark matter haloes spanning M h = 10 6 ​ - ​ 10 11 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm h}=10^{6}\text{-}10^{11}\text{M}_{\odot} , comparing continuous and bursty star formation scenarios, which reflect underlying assumptions of instantaneous and delayed feedback, and we allow for metallicity-dependent feedback efficiency. Delayed feedback induces oscillations in Z g Z_{\rm g} and Z ⋆ Z_{\star} , with Z g Z_{\rm g} declining sharply at low stellar and halo masses. This decline shifts to higher stellar and halo masses as the redshift decreases. Reionization introduces significant scatter, producing an upturn followed by rapid Z g Z_{\text{g}} decline. Metallicity-dependent feedback moderates this decline at z = 7 ​ - ​ 10 z=7\text{-}10 , flattening the Z g Z_{\text{g}} –mass relation to ≃ 0.03 \simeq 0.03 – 0.04 ​ Z ⊙ 0.04\,\text{Z}_{\odot} . Dust production tracks Z g Z_{\text{g}} but is sensitive to burst history, causing delayed enrichment. Our results show that burst-driven feedback decouples Z g Z_{\rm g} and Z ⋆ Z_{\star} , imprints intrinsic scatter in mass–metallicity relations, and delays dust growth. These effects are strongest in low-mass halos ( M h ∼ 10 7 ​ M ⊙ M_{\rm h}\sim 10^{7}\,\text{M}_{\odot} ), where shallow potentials amplify the impact of feedback. Our results are consistent with recent hydrodynamical and semi-analytical simulations and provides context for interpreting JWST metallicity and dust measurements, highlighting the importance of episodic star formation in early galaxy chemical evolution.

Introduction

Recent estimates of the star formation histories of high-redshift galaxies using the JWST indicate that star formation proceeds in bursts (i.e. is rapidly varying) in the early Universe
(
Faisst et al. 2019
;
Looser et al. 2023
;
Strait et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. Bursty star formation occurs when the effective equilibrium between the self-gravity of gas in a galaxy and stellar feedback following star formation cannot be maintained
(
Faucher-Giguère 2018
, cf.)
. It is expected to be commonplace in lower-mass galaxies
(
Oñorbe et al. 2015
;
Muratov et al. 2015
;
Sparre et al. 2017
, e.g.)
and, importantly, in galaxies at high redshifts
(
Pallottini & Ferrara 2023
;
Shen et al. 2023
;
Sun et al. 2023
, e.g.)
.

Previous work has shown that bursty star formation will produce naturally strong stellar feedback driven outflows in high-
z
galaxies, especially at lower masses
(
Furlanetto & Mirocha 2022
;
Menon & Power 2024
, e.g.)
. This results in periods of oscillating gas mass in the galaxy as gas is expelled from the inter-stellar medium (ISM) and cosmological gas accretion from the inter-galactic medium (IGM) is suppressed. The massive stars that are the sources of feedback, via e.g. stellar winds and supernovae
(
Lamers & Cassinelli 1999
, e.g.)
, are also the sources of metals that enrich the ISM with metals
(
Mannucci et al. 2010
;
Maiolino & Mannucci 2019
, e.g.)
as well as the primary dust production mechanism in the early Universe
(
Todini & Ferrara 2001
;
Gall & Hjorth 2018
, e.g.)
.

This prompts the important question of
how does bursty star formation influence the metal and dust content of high-redshift galaxies?

From a theoretical perspective, we expect that metals and dust will play a crucial role in galaxy formation as coolants
(
Cox & Tucker 1969
;
Sutherland & Dopita 1993
;
Ploeckinger & Schaye 2020
, e.g.)
, which plays a critical role in regulating a galaxy’s star formation and associated feedback efficiencies. From an observational perspective, a galaxy’s metallicity will influence what we can infer from its spectral energy distribution
(
Robotham et al. 2020
;
Vijayan et al. 2025
, e.g.)
, including diagnostics such as star formation rate. The presence of dust will obscure star forming regions in individual galaxies and star forming galaxies
(
Adelberger & Steidel 2000
, e.g.)
; there are indications from ALMA and JWST data that galaxies at
z\geq\,6
harbour a diverse range of dust content
(
Matthee et al. 2019
;
Rodighiero et al. 2023
;
Barrufet et al. 2023
, e.g.)
.

Astrophysically, the heavy elements that drive the growth of metallicity are the product of nucleosynthesis in the cores of stars, supernovae, and stellar mergers
(
Nomoto et al. 2013
;
Kobayashi et al. 2020
;
Arcones & Thielemann 2022
, cf.)
, which subsequently enrich the ISM, circum-galactic medium (CGM), and IGM via stellar-driven winds and supernovae events. The physics of dust formation and evolution is complex
(
McKee 1989
;
Dwek 1998
;
Calura 2025
, e.g.)
, but its production is believed to be driven primarily by supernovae
(
Todini & Ferrara 2001
;
Gall et al. 2011
;
Gall & Hjorth 2018
;
Leśniewska & Michałowski 2019
;
Ferrara et al. 2022
;
Langeroodi et al. 2024
, e.g.)
at redshifts
z\geq 5
. Feedback that drives gas out of a galaxy will lower the gas-phase metallicity of the ISM, while fresh accretion of nearly pristine gas from the IGM will lower the ISM gas phase metallicity. Similarly, supernovae-driven shocks in the ISM destroy dust, while it will also be entrained in the metal-enriched gas that is expelled from galaxies via winds. As a result, we might expect to see differences with respect to star formation modes in which burstiness is suppressed. This is because galaxies can retain more gas and more metal-enriched gas and dust in their ISM, which should allow for the gas-metallicity and dust content to increase steadily with time.

The first generation of stars, the zero-metallicity Population III, enriched the ISM of their host galaxies
(
Sluder et al. 2015
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
as supernovae
(
Heger & Woosley 2002
;
Heger & Woosley 2010
)
, which led to subsequent generations of Population II stars being metal enriched. We expect increased stellar metallicity to influence stellar mass loss rates via winds, and consequently the efficiency of stellar feedback
(
Krtička & Kubát 2014
;
Gormaz-Matamala et al. 2022
;
Rickard et al. 2022
;
Dekel et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. It also regulates the maximum progenitor mass above which stars collapse directly into black holes without exploding as supernova
(
Zhang et al. 2008
;
O’Connor & Ott 2011
, e.g.)
, which reduces the stellar feedback efficiency for a given stellar population.

In this paper, we study how bursty star formation influences the metal and dust content of high-
z
galaxies using our lightweight semi-analytical model
Ashvini
, which builds on our previous work
(see
Menon & Power 2024
, hereafter
50
)
.

We extend
Ashvini
to track the growth of gas-phase and stellar metallicities, and dust, and we also include a model to account for the metallicity-dependence of the efficiency of feedback. These additions allow us to explore how bursty star formation during reionization imprints scatter and asymmetry in mass–metallicity relations, and how feedback cycles delay chemical and dust enrichment in halos of different mass ranges. We do this by investigating how our results are sensitive to key model parameters, and comparing to a model in which feedback is instantaneous and star formation proceeds in a smooth (i.e. non-bursty) manner.

2.2
The
Ashvini
model

In this subsection, we expand on the new updates to the
Ashvini
model. First, we give a brief summary of the model; the reader is urged to consult

50

for a more detailed discussion.

2.2.1
Core Model

Ashvini
employs a set of coupled differential equations to describe the evolution of gas and stellar mass within a dark matter halo across cosmic time, and enforces mass conservation by balancing inflows, outflows, and internal processes. At a given instant in time,
t
, we compute the growth rate of the gas mass
\dot{M}_{\text{g}}
, the star formation rate
\dot{M}_{\star}
, and the wind-driven mass loss rate
\dot{M}_{\rm w}
from the following:

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{\text{g}}

\displaystyle=\dot{M}_{\text{c,g}}-\dot{M}_{\star}-\dot{M}_{\text{w}}(t^{\prime}),

(1)

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{\star}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\text{sf}}\frac{M_{g}}{\tau_{\text{sf}}},

(2)

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{\text{w}}

\displaystyle=\eta_{\text{fb}}\dot{M}_{\star}(t^{\prime}),

(3)

where we assume the
\dot{M}
values correspond to the instantaneous rates at
t
.
t^{\prime}=t-t^{\rm d}
allows for a time delay (
t^{\rm d}>0
) between when stars form and when they produce feedback;
t^{\rm d}=0
corresponds to the case of instantaneous feedback. In Equation
1
,\dot{M}_{\text{c,g}}
is the cosmological gas accretion from the IGM (see below); in Equation
2
,
{M_{\rm g}}
is the instantaneous mass of the gas reservoir, and
\epsilon_{\text{sf}}
and
\tau_{\text{sf}}
are the star formation efficiency and the star formation timescale (see below) respectively; and in Equation
3
,
\eta_{\text{fb}}
is the feedback efficiency.

As in
50
,
we estimate the cosmological gas accretion rate using,

\dot{M}_{\text{c,g}}=\varepsilon_{\rm UV}\bigg(\frac{\Omega_{\text{b}}}{\Omega_{\text{M}}}\bigg)\dot{M}_{\text{h}},

where
\dot{M}_{\text{h}}
is the halo mass growth rate;
(\Omega_{\text{b}}/\Omega_{\text{M}})
is the cosmic baryon fraction; and
0\leq\varepsilon_{\text{UV}}\leq 1
quantifies the degree to which the accretion rate is suppressed by the UVB, which we assume is effective from a reionization redshift,
z_{\text{rei}}(=7)
. We also assume that
\tau_{\text{sf}}
is a fraction of the Hubble time,

\tau_{\text{sf}}=0.15\frac{f_{\text{sf}}}{H(z)};

we expect
f_{\text{sf}}\gtrsim 1
, but for simplicity we set
f_{\text{sf}}=1
.

Modelling Gas and Star Metallicity

In this work, we update the
Ashvini
model to track the growth of the mass of metals in gas and stars,
{M_{\rm Z,g}}
and
M_{\rm Z,\star}
. We extend Equations

-
3
as:

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{\text{Z,g}}

\displaystyle=Z_{\text{IGM}}\dot{M}_{\text{c,g}}+Y_{\text{Z}}\dot{M}_{\star}(t^{\prime})-\dot{M}_{\text{Z},\star}-\dot{M}_{\text{Z,w}}(t^{\prime}),

(4)

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{\text{Z},\star}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\text{sf}}\dfrac{M_{\text{Z,g}}}{\tau_{\text{sf}}},

(5)

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{\text{Z,w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{\text{Z,g}}}{M_{\text{g}}}\dot{M}_{\text{w}},

(6)

where
Z_{\text{IGM}}
is the metallicity of the freshly accreted IGM gas and
Y_{\rm Z}
is the heavy element yield per unit star formation rate (SFR).

\dot{M}_{\text{c,g}}

Y_{\text{Z}}\dot{M}_{\star}(t-t_{\text{d}})
; the metals in the ISM that are locked in newly formed stars,
\dot{M}_{\text{Z},\star}

\dot{M}_{\text{Z,w}}
.

•

Equation

assumes that the mass of metals in stars tracks the star formation rate
\dot{M}_{\ast}
as given by Equation

({M_{\rm Z,g}}/{M_{\rm g}})
, at that instant of cosmic time.

•

Equation
6
assumes that the gas mass of metals lost via winds is simply
({M_{\rm Z,g}}/{M_{\rm g}})
times
\dot{M}_{\text{w}}
at that instant, which is driven by the star formation rate
\dot{M}_{\ast}
at the earlier time,
t^{\prime}
.

We use as our fiducial values
Z_{\text{IGM}}=10^{-3}\text{Z}_{\odot}

Kravtsov & Manwadkar 2022

2.2.3
Modelling Metal-Dependent Feedback

We employ a momentum-regulated feedback recipe that balances the momentum injected into the ISM gas, by supernovae as well as other feedback processes, to that which is required to eject the gas at the halo’s escape velocity
(
Furlanetto et al. 2017
;
Furlanetto & Mirocha 2022
, also see)
. This yields a prescription that depends on the redshift,
z
, and the halo mass,
M_{\text{h}}
as,

\eta_{\textbf{fb}}=\epsilon_{\text{fb}}\pi_{\rm p}f(Z_{\star})\bigg(\frac{10^{11.5}\text{M}_{\odot}}{M_{\text{h}}}\bigg)^{1/3}\bigg(\frac{9}{1+z}\bigg)^{1/2}.

(7)

Here
\pi_{\rm p}
(which we set to unity) is the total momentum per SNe
(
Furlanetto et al. 2017
;
Furlanetto & Mirocha 2022
;
Menon & Power 2024
, in units of
2\times 10^{33}
g cm s
-2
, see also)
and
\epsilon_{\rm fb}
is the fraction of this momentum that is coupled to the galaxy’s ISM as wind.

The factor
f(Z_{\ast})\leq 1
governs how the feedback efficiency depends on the stellar metallicity,
Z_{\ast}
. If we assume that there is no metal dependence, then
f(Z_{\ast})=1
. However, we expect that the feedback should be metal-dependent at low stellar metallicities
(
Zhang et al. 2008
;
O’Connor & Ott 2011
;
Sukhbold et al. 2016
;
Jecmen & Oey 2023
, eg.)
. In our model, we adopt an empirical parameterisation for
f(Z_{\star})
motivated by the work of
Jecmen & Oey 2023
, who estimated that the total injected momentum decreases by 75% for stellar metallicities
Z_{\star}\lesssim 0.4\text{Z}_{\odot}
. We model this using a sigmoid function, which produces step-like behaviour with a smooth transition, given by,

f(Z_{\star})=\left(b-a\right)\exp\bigg[-\ln\bigg(1+\exp\Big(-\frac{Z_{\star}-m}{s}\Big)\bigg)\bigg]+a.

(8)

Here,
m=0.1,s=0.01,a=0.25
and
b=1
are parameters that control the amplitude and sharpness of the transition of the sigmoid.

2.2.4
Modelling Dust

While the physics of dust formation and evolution is complex
(
McKee 1989
;
Dwek 1998
;
Calura 2025
, e.g.)
, we incorporate an approximate model to obtain estimates of dust mass associated with the gas phase,
M_{\rm d}
. At redshifts
z\geq 5
, dust production is believed to be driven primarily by supernovae
(
Todini & Ferrara 2001
;
Gall et al. 2011
;
Gall & Hjorth 2018
;
Leśniewska & Michałowski 2019
;
Ferrara et al. 2022
;
Langeroodi et al. 2024
, e.g.)
because channels linked to evolved lower mass stars (e.g. the asymptotic giant branch, AGB) are unimportant until later cosmic times
(
Marassi et al. 2019
;
Triani et al. 2020
)
. The standard assumption is that dust is produced by core-collapse supernovae of stars in the mass range
8-40\,\text{M}_{\odot}

(
Heger et al. 2003
;
Gall & Hjorth 2018
, cf.)
and the dust yield per supernova is a function of the metallicity and mass of the progenitor mass.
Gall & Hjorth 2018
estimate this dust yield per supernova to
0.31\text{M}_{\odot}
with an uncertainty of 0.15 dex.

In
Ashvini
, we assume that the rate of dust mass growth tracks the rate of core-collapse supernovae, which is proportional to
\dot{M}_{\star}
, and is lost via destruction in supernovae shocks
(\dot{M}_{\text{d,dest}})
and expulsion via winds
(\dot{M}_{\text{w}})
. We describe this using the equations,

\dot{M}_{\text{d}}=Y_{\text{d}}\dot{M}_{\star}(t^{\prime})-\dot{M}_{\text{d,dest}}(t^{\prime})-\dot{M}_{\text{d,w}}(t^{\prime}),

(9)

with

\dot{M}_{\text{d,w}}=\left(\frac{M_{\rm d}}{{M_{\rm g}}}\right)\dot{M}_{\text{w}}.

(10)

Here, the total dust yield per unit star formation rate is
Y_{\text{d}}=0.004
for our assumed
Chabrier 2003
IMF, following
Gall & Hjorth 2018
and
Langeroodi et al. 2024
. The prefactor
(M_{\rm d}/{M_{\rm g}})
in equation
10
assumes that dust mass loss via winds is proportional to the gas mass loss via winds.
\dot{M}_{\text{d,dest}}
determines the rate at which dust is destroyed by supernovae shocks. The conventional assumption is,

\dot{M}_{\text{d,dest}}=\frac{M_{\rm d}}{\tau_{\text{dest}}},

(11)

where
\tau_{\text{dest}}
is the dust destruction timescale.
\tau_{\text{dest}}
depends inversely on the rate of supernovae (
R_{\text{SN}}
) and the amount of dust in the local ISM that these supernovae can interact with. We estimate
R_{\text{SN}}
as

R_{\text{SN}}=\gamma\frac{\dot{M}_{\star}(t^{\prime})}{\Delta\,M_{\star}(t^{\prime})};

(12)

here
\gamma=1.3\times 10^{-4}
is the fraction of stars that will become core-collapse supernovae for a
Chabrier 2003
IMF and
\Delta\,M_{\star}(t^{\prime})=\dot{M}_{\star}(t^{\prime})\Delta\,t^{\prime}
estimates the instantaneous mass of stars formed at
t^{\prime}
in an interval
\Delta\,t^{\prime}
. Previous work has estimated that
M_{\text{ISM}}
is of order the amount of swept-up mass during the initial phase of a supernova, when the ejecta velocity is
\sim 10^{4}
km s
-1
, until it enters the Sedov phase. This has been estimated to
\epsilon_{\text{d}}\times\,M_{\text{ISM}}\simeq 10^{3}\,\text{M}_{\odot}

(
Gall & Hjorth 2018
;
Calura 2025
, e.g.)
, where
M_{\text{ISM}}\simeq 10^{4}\text{M}_{\odot}
and
\epsilon_{\text{d}}=0.1
.

We compute
\dot{M}_{\text{d,dest}}
as

\dot{M}_{\text{d,dest}}=\epsilon_{\text{d}}\,M_{\text{ISM}}R_{\text{SN}}\,f(M_{\text{ISM}}/M_{\text{crit}}),

(13)

where
f(M_{\text{ISM}}/M_{\text{crit}})
accounts for the efficiency with which the supernovae can sweep out and/or destroy the dust. Dust destruction via shocks is efficient when the ISM mass exceeds a critical threshold,
M_{\text{crit}}
, typically
\sim 10^{5}\,\text{M}_{\odot}
. This allows the supernova ejecta to enter the Sedov phase and sweep up sufficient ambient material.

In low-mass systems
(M_{\text{ISM}}<M_{\text{crit}})
, we assume
f_{\text{dest}}=0
and that dust is entrained in outflows and lost from the system. In high-mass systems (
M_{\text{ISM}}\geq M_{\text{crit}}
), we adopt the standard formulation:

\dot{M}_{\text{d, dest}}={\epsilon_{\text{d}}R_{\text{SN}}M_{\text{ISM}}},

(14)

We assume a smooth transition:

f(M_{\text{ISM}}/M_{\text{crit}})=1-e^{-\,\left(M_{\text{ISM}}/{M_{\text{crit}}}\right)^{\alpha}},

(15)

which suppresses dust destruction in low-mass systems and asymptotes to the standard model at high masses. We set
\alpha=8
and that
M_{\rm ISM}\equiv M_{\rm g}
of the galaxy
2
2

2

This identification of
M_{\rm ISM}
with the total gas mass is a simplifying assumption; in reality, only a fraction of the gas is in the dust-bearing ISM phase, so our model may overestimate dust destruction in gas-rich halos.
.

does dust production begin to balance destruction. (
Choban et al. 2025

and could reconcile observations of high-

;
Barrufet et al. 2023

4
Discussion

We have demonstrated that bursty star formation fundamentally alters the chemical evolution and components of early galaxies compared to smooth star formation scenarios. The delayed feedback inherent to bursty star formation creates oscillatory behaviour in both gas-phase and stellar metallicities, while reionization introduces additional complexity through suppressed gas accretion. The combined effect generates large scatter in gas mass (
{M_{\rm g}}
) and metallicity (
Z_{\mathrm{g}}
), with distinct signatures in the mass–metallicity relation that depend on the feedback prescription.

Our results align closely with a range of recent JWST observations, particularly for galaxies in the
10^{7}
–
10^{8}\text{M}_{\odot}
stellar mass range. Gas-phase metallicities reported by
Arellano-Córdova et al. 2022
and
Sarkar et al. 2025
are consistent with the metallicity plateau and subsequent drop predicted by our delayed feedback models at
z\simeq 6
–10 (Figure
6
), a feature driven by the interplay of reionization suppression and bursty feedback. Similarly,
Chemerynska et al. 2024
find
-2\lesssim\log(Z_{\mathrm{g}}/\text{Z}_{\odot})\lesssim-1
for
M_{\star}\sim 10^{7}\text{M}_{\odot}
, matching our delayed and metal-dependent feedback predictions for low-mass halos (Figures
4
and
6
).

Trump et al. 2023
measure
12+\log(\text{O}/\text{H})\simeq 7.5
–8.0 (
\sim 0.06
–
0.18\text{Z}_{\odot}
) and
Li et al. 2025
find
7.2
–
7.4
(
\sim 0.03
–
0.05\text{Z}_{\odot}
) for
z\simeq 5
–7 galaxies in the same mass range, values that more closely resemble our instantaneous feedback + UV background results. At the same time,
Curti et al. 2024
and
Nakajima et al. 2023
report metallicities in the range
\sim 0.02
–
0.2\text{Z}_{\odot}
for
M_{\star}\sim 10^{6}
–
10^{8}\text{M}_{\odot}
galaxies at
z=3
–10, in good agreement with our delayed + UV predictions. The lower normalization of the mass–metallicity relation at
z\simeq 7
–10 noted by
Heintz et al. 2022
and
Ucci et al. 2022
is also reproduced, reflecting the steep metallicity decline at low masses caused by pristine gas inflows and strong outflows. The decoupling of gas-phase and stellar metallicities seen in our models mirrors the episodic enrichment histories inferred by
Rey et al. 2025
and the elevated N/O ratios reported by
Ji et al. 2025
.

Theoretical and simulation studies further reinforce these trends. The
Delphi23
model
(
Mauerhofer et al. 2025
)
predicts a sharp metallicity drop at low stellar masses, similar to our delayed + UV results at
z=5
. Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations such as
FIRE

(
Ma et al. 2015
)
and
FIRE-2

(
Marszewski et al. 2024
)
exhibit metallicity–mass relations whose shapes and scatter closely match our predictions for the
10^{6}
–
10^{8}\text{M}_{\odot}
range, including the steep declines in
Z_{\mathrm{g}}
at low masses in burst-dominated regimes. In
Ma et al. 2015
, the stellar metallicity–stellar mass relation also resembles our delayed + UV case, with offsets of only
\sim 0.5
–2 dex at comparable redshifts.

Our dust evolution predictions also capture key features seen in recent high-
z
surveys. The delayed growth and large scatter in dust mass (
M_{\mathrm{dust}}
) in our models parallel the diversity observed by
Rodighiero et al. 2023
and
Barrufet et al. 2023
, and match the quenching or decline in dust content seen in
FIRE
simulations
(
Choban et al. 2025
)
. The
CROC
simulations
(
Esmerian & Gnedin 2022
)
predict steady dust growth in massive halos, whereas our delayed feedback scenario instead produces a pronounced quench over similar cosmic timescales. This difference likely reflects our assumption that
M_{\mathrm{ISM}}={M_{\rm g}}
, which may overestimate dust destruction in gas-rich halos.

Taken together, these comparisons show that burst-driven, delayed feedback—particularly when combined with reionization suppression—naturally explains several observed high-
z
trends: oscillatory enrichment, large scatter in
Z_{\mathrm{g}}
, decoupled stellar and gas-phase metallicities, and stochastic dust evolution. At the same time, the split between observational cases favouring delayed versus instantaneous feedback underscores the need for joint constraints using metallicity, dust content, and star formation variability to fully pin down feedback timescales in early galaxies.

5
Conclusions

We have investigated how bursty star formation during cosmic reionization affects the metallicity and dust content of low-mass galaxies using our
Ashvini
model. Bursty star formation can drive strong outflows, which suppress accretion of pristine gas from the intergalactic medium. This can offset metallicity in these galaxies, and the episodic nature of enrichment can decouple gas-phase and stellar metallicities, which will influence how we interpret the observable properties of the high-redshift galaxy population. Our results confirm these expectations:

•

Bursty star formation
, driven by delayed feedback, induces oscillations in gas and stellar metallicities (
Z_{g}
,
Z_{\star}
), particularly in lower-mass halos, as illustrated by our
M_{\rm h}\sim 10^{7}\,\text{M}_{\odot}
example (Figure
1
). This leads to a decoupling of
Z_{g}
and
Z_{\star}
, consistent with the episodic accretion and enrichment histories inferred empirically by
Chemerynska et al. 2024
using JWST data and theoretically by
Rey et al. 2025
using the EDGE simulations of dwarf galaxies.

•

Reionization
introduces significant scatter in
Z_{g}
, producing a sharp upturn followed by a decline at
z\lesssim 7
(Figure
1
, lower panel). This feature aligns with the metallicity plateau and subsequent drop observed in JWST studies
(
Arellano-Córdova et al. 2022
;
Sarkar et al. 2025
, e.g.)
.

•

Metal-dependent feedback
flattens the
Z_{g}
–
M_{\rm\star}
relation to
Z_{g}\approx 0.03
–
0.04\,\text{Z}_{\odot}
at
z=7
–10 (Figure
6
), in agreement with the lower normalization of the mass–metallicity relation reported by
Heintz et al. 2022
and
Ucci et al. 2022
.

•

Dust evolution
broadly tracks
Z_{g}
but is delayed by feedback cycles. The resulting diversity in dust content, especially in high-mass halos, is consistent with ALMA and JWST observations of dust-rich and dust-poor galaxies at
z\gtrsim 6

Rodighiero et al. 2023
and

The results confirm that inferences of dust content or stellar age based on galaxy colour may be misleading in bursty, metal-poor systems. Because dust production follows gas-phase metallicity and is delayed by feedback, both the timing and amount of dust are sensitive to burst histories. Bursty star formation introduces time lags and variability in enrichment. The metallicity of gas inflow properties and the metallicity-dependence of supernovae can critically shape metallicity and dust evolution. These effects are especially strong in low-mass galaxies, where shallow potentials amplify burst effects. As such, observations of galaxies in the early Universe must account for these burst-driven dynamics when interpreting metallicity, dust, and star formation rates.
