Selection bias and molecular blind spots: re-evaluating flumonertinib rechallenge in third-generation TKI-resistant non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Mengnan Li, Yani Xu, Kaili Dai, Lianqin Tao, Junfei Zhu
Published: 2026

We read with interest the real-world study by Wang et al., published in Journal of Thoracic Disease, which suggests that flumonertinib monotherapy is a viable rechallenge option for patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) who have progressed on prior third-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) (1). While the search for effective salvage therapies in this setting is urgent, we identified critical methodological limitations—specifically regarding selection bias, statistical power, and the lack of molecular characterization—that fundamentally undermine the clinical applicability of the authors’ conclusions.

First, the conclusion that initiating flumonertinib in earlier lines (≤3 lines) confers a survival benefit [hazard ratio (HR) =0.43, P=0.02] is likely driven by selection bias and immortal time bias rather than pharmacological efficacy. Patients who survive to receive fourth-line therapy or beyond inherently represent a distinct biological cohort, often characterized by more indolent disease or a history of multiple partial responses. Conversely, patients receiving flumonertinib as a second- or third-line agent (immediately post-osimertinib) comprise a heterogeneous mix of rapid progressors and those with oligoprogression. Comparing these dynamically defined groups without adjusting for the time-dependent nature of treatment lines creates a statistical artifact. Furthermore, the absence of a control group receiving standard-of-care chemotherapy (e.g., platinum-pemetrexed) renders the term “survival benefit” misleading; the benefit is relative only to a worse-prognosis internal subgroup, not to established clinical standards.

Second, the statistical robustness of the subgroup analyses is highly questionable given the small sample size (n=40). The authors performed extensive Cox subgroup analyses [Fig. 4 in the original article (1)] and highlight a significant interaction for monotherapy efficacy (Pinteraction =0.038). However, stratifying 40 patients into multiple subgroups resulted in cell counts as low as 2 to 5 patients per arm. In such underpowered settings, P values are volatile and prone to Type I errors. The finding that flumonertinib monotherapy outperformed combination strategies (with anti-angiogenics or chemotherapy) is biologically counterintuitive in the resistance setting, where tumor heterogeneity typically necessitates combination approaches. This observation likely reflects confounding by indication: patients selected for monotherapy likely had lower tumor burden or better performance status than those requiring aggressive combinations, skewing the results in favor of the less intensive regimen.

Third, the study lacks critical molecular context. The authors report that 55% of patients did not undergo genetic re-testing prior to flumonertinib initiation. Rechallenge with a third-generation TKI after failure of another third-generation TKI (e.g., osimertinib to flumonertinib) is mechanistically plausible only in specific scenarios, such as the emergence of specific tertiary mutations or off-target resistance where the new drug has a different kinase inhibitory profile. Without data on mechanisms like C797S mutations (cis vs. trans), mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET) amplification, or small cell transformation, the observed efficacy remains a “black box”. Blind rechallenge risks exposing patients to ineffective toxicity without targeting the dominant resistance clone.

Finally, the complete omission of safety data—attributed to the retrospective nature of the study—is a significant oversight. Rechallenge strategies, especially those utilizing high-dose regimens (160 mg, used in 35% of the cohort), carry cumulative risks of cardiotoxicity and interstitial lung disease (ILD). Without a safety profile, clinicians cannot assess the risk-benefit ratio of this off-label strategy.

We invite the authors to clarify the baseline characteristics differences between the monotherapy and combination groups to rule out confounding. Additionally, providing any available data on resistance mechanisms (e.g., from the 45% of patients who were re-tested) would greatly enhance the scientific value of this report.
