Abstract
. We use the theory of θ \theta -groups developed by Vinberg, along with computations in the computer algebra system GAP 4, to classify the orbits of Spin ⁡ ( 10 , ℂ ) × SL ⁡ ( 4 , ℂ ) \mathrm{Spin}(10,\mathbb{C})\times\mathrm{SL}(4,\mathbb{C}) acting on its module Δ + ⊗ ℂ 4 \Delta_{+}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{4} , where Δ + \Delta_{+} is a half spin module of Spin ⁡ ( 10 , ℂ ) \mathrm{Spin}(10,\mathbb{C}) .



1.
Introduction

The theory of
\theta
-groups has been developed by Vinberg in the 70’s
(
[
36
,
37
]
). They form a class of representations of linear
algebraic groups that arise from a
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-grading, or a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading, of a
semisimple complex Lie algebra. These representations share many properties
with the adjoint representation of a semisimple algebraic group on its
Lie algebra. In particular it is possible to use Vinberg’s theory to classify
the orbits of a
\theta
-group. The first endeavour in this direction
was the classification by Vinberg and Elashvili of the threevectors of a
9-dimensional complex space (
[
38
]
). Subsequently a number of such
classifications have been undertaken (cf.,
[
1
]
(which has been
translated in
[
26
]
),
[
2
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
29
]
,

[
25
]
).

In this paper we use the same methods to classify the orbits of the
group
\mathrm{Spin}(10,\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathrm{SL}(4,\mathbb{C})
acting on the space
\Delta_{+}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{4}
, where
\Delta_{+}
is a half spin module of
\mathrm{Spin}(10,\mathbb{C})
. However,
in contrast to the above references we also heavily use explicit computation
in the computer algebra system
GAP
4 (
[
11
]
) and especially its
package
SLA
(
[
12
]
). The latter package has a number of
implementations of algorithms for dealing with
\theta
-groups and
with other aspects of simple Lie algebras and their modules.

This orbit classification has applications in geometry to the study of the
spinor tenfold, which is the orbit of the highest weight vector in

\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{+})
. The linear sections of small codimension of the
spinor tenfold are especially interesting, and those of codimension 4
are closely related to the orbits of
\mathrm{Spin}(10,\mathbb{C})\times\mathrm{SL}(4,\mathbb{C})
on

\Delta_{+}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{4}
. We refer to
[
23
]
for an in depth
investigation that, among many other things, uses the results of the present
paper.

We start with a section with preliminaries
on Vinberg’s
\theta
-groups, the particular
\theta
-group that we are
interested in and the construction of the module
\Delta_{+}
. A particular
property of the natural module of a
\theta
-group is that its elements
have a Jordan decomposition, dividing them into semisimple, nilpotent and
mixed elements (the latter are neither nilpotent nor semisimple). The semisimple
orbits are infinite in number, but be can divided into groups having the same
stabilizer. Section
3
is devoted to the classification of these
orbits.
This is followed by Section
5
in which the methods are explained
that have been used to determine the stabilizers of the semisimple elements.
Subsequently, in Sections
6
,
7
the classifications
of respectively the mixed and nilpotent orbits is given. In the last section
we also give the Hasse diagram of the closure relation between the nilpotent
orbits. It shows that the null cone has two irreducible components.

2.
Preliminaries on Vinberg’s
\theta
-groups

In
[
36
]
Vinberg introduced and studied a class of representations of
linear algebraic groups that since have become known as
\theta
-groups.
They are constructed from a semisimple complex Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
together with
a
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-grading

\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{g}_{i}\text{ where }[\mathfrak{g}_{i},\mathfrak{g}_{j}]\subset\mathfrak{g}_{i+j}\text{ for all }i,j.

If
m=\infty
then
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}
. However, in this paper we restrict to the
case
m<\infty
. Such a grading corresponds to an automorphism
\theta
of

\mathfrak{g}
of order
m
which is constructed as follows. Let
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
be a fixed
primitive
m
-th root of unity and set
\theta(x)=\omega^{i}x
for all

x\in\mathfrak{g}_{i}
and extend
\theta
to
\mathfrak{g}
by linearity. Then
\theta:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}

is an automorphism of order
m
. Conversely, any automorphism
\theta
of order

m
yields a
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-grading by letting
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
be the eigenspace of
\theta

with eigenvalue
\omega^{i}
.

We let
G
be the identity component of the inner automorphism group of
\mathfrak{g}
.
We have that the Lie algebra of
G
is equal to
\mathrm{ad}\mathfrak{g}=\{\mathrm{ad}x\mid x\in\mathfrak{g}\}
where
\mathrm{ad}x:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is the adjoint map,
\mathrm{ad}x(y)=[x,y]
.
The subalgebra
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
is reductive and hence there is a unique connected
subgroup
G_{0}\subset G
whose Lie algebra is
\mathrm{ad}\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\{\mathrm{ad}x\mid x\in\mathfrak{g}_{0}\}
. Since
[\mathfrak{g}_{0},\mathfrak{g}_{1}]\subset\mathfrak{g}_{1}
we have that
G_{0}
acts on

\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. The representation
G_{0}\to\mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g}_{1})
is called a

\theta
-representation and the group
G_{0}
together with its module
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is
called a
\theta
-group.

Results of Vinberg (
[
36
,
37
]
) and Vinberg and Elashvili
(
[
38
]
) make it possible to classify the orbits of a
\theta
-group.
The first observation in this direction is that the space
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
inherits the
Jordan decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
, that is, if
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
and
s,n\in\mathfrak{g}
are
such that
x=s+n
is its Jordan decomposition (with
s
semisimple and

n
nilpotent, see
[
18
, §5.4]
) then
s,n\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. This divides the
orbits into three groups: semisimple (whose elements have
n=0
), nilpotent
(whose elements have
s=0
) and mixed (whose elements have
s,n
both nonzero).

Let

Then the Clifford algebra

Define the map

For

\displaystyle\lambda(u)\cdot u_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{i_{k}}=u\wedge u_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{i_{k}}

3.
The semisimple orbits

Consider a
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-grading
\mathfrak{g}=\oplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{g}_{i}
of the semisimple
Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. As seen in Section
2
this yields the reductive
group
G_{0}
which acts on
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Here we first describe some general facts
concerning the semisimple orbits in
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Then we specialize to the particular
example that we are interested in.

A
Cartan subspace
in
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
is a maximal subspace consisting of commuting
semisimple elements. By
[
36
, Theorem 1]
two Cartan subspaces of

\mathfrak{g}_{1}
are
G_{0}
-conjugate. It follows that every semisimple orbit in
\mathfrak{g}_{1}

has a point in a fixed Cartan subspace.

Let
\mathfrak{c}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{1}
be a Cartan subspace and define

\displaystyle Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{c})

\displaystyle=\{g\in G_{0}\mid g(x)=x\text{ for all }x\in\mathfrak{c}\}

\displaystyle N_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{c})

\displaystyle=\{g\in G_{0}\mid g(x)\in\mathfrak{c}\text{ for all }x\in\mathfrak{c}\}

and set
W_{0}=N_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{c})/Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{c})
. The group
W_{0}
is called the

little Weyl group
of the grading. A linear transformation
T
of a
complex vector space is said to be a complex reflection if
T-1
has rank 1.
Then the kernel of
T-1
is a hyperplane, which we call the
reflection
hyperplane
of
T
.
Vinberg showed that
W_{0}
is generated by complex reflections,

[
36
, Theorem 8]
.

By
[
36
, Theorem 2]

any two elements of
\mathfrak{c}
are
G_{0}
-conjugate if and only if they are

W_{0}
-conjugate. It follows that classifying the semisimple orbits in

\mathfrak{g}_{1}
reduces to classifying the
W_{0}
-orbits in
\mathfrak{c}
.
However, this statement can still be refined in the following way.
For
p\in\mathfrak{c}
let

W_{p}=\{w\in W_{0}\mid w(p)=p\}
be its stabilizer in
W_{0}
. By

[
22
, Theorem 9.44]

W_{p}
is a reflection subgroup of
W_{0}
, that is,
it is generated by complex reflections. Now let
M\subset W_{0}
be a
reflection subgroup, and define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{M}

\displaystyle=\{q\in\mathfrak{c}\mid M\subset W_{q}\}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{M}^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\{q\in\mathfrak{c}\mid M=W_{q}\}.

Then
\mathfrak{c}_{M}
is the intersection of all reflection hyperplanes of all complex
reflections in
M
. So it is a linear subspace of
\mathfrak{c}
. Furthermore,

\mathfrak{c}_{M}^{\circ}
is the set of
q\in\mathfrak{c}_{M}
that do not lie in the reflection
hyperplane of any complex reflection in
W_{0}
that does not lie in
M
.
So
\mathfrak{c}_{M}^{\circ}
is Zariski-open in
\mathfrak{c}_{M}
. Now for
w\in W_{0}
and reflection
subgroups
M,M^{\prime}\subset W_{0}
we have

w(\mathfrak{c}_{M}^{\circ})=\mathfrak{c}_{M^{\prime}}^{\circ}\text{ if and only if }wMw^{-1}=M^{\prime}.

Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{r}\subset W_{0}
be the reflection subgroups of
W_{0}
such
that for each
p\in\mathfrak{c}
the stabilizer
W_{p}
is conjugate to exactly one

M_{i}
. Then it follows that every semisimple orbit has a point in a unique

\mathfrak{c}_{M_{i}}^{\circ}
. Moreover, two elements

p,p^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{c}_{M_{i}}^{\circ}
are
W_{0}
-conjugate if and only if they are conjugate
under the group
\Gamma_{i}=N_{W_{0}}(M_{i})/M_{i}
. We conclude that
the classification of the semisimple
G_{0}
-orbits also reduces to the
classification of the
\Gamma_{i}
-orbits in
\mathfrak{c}_{M_{i}}^{\circ}
for

1\leq i\leq r
.

want to find one explicit element (i.e., automorphism of

) in each

is as
in Table

First we need to recall a number of facts. Let
\mathfrak{a}
be a semisimple complex
Lie algebra. Consider the root system of
\mathfrak{a}
with respect to a fixed Cartan
subalgebra. There are root vectors
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\ell}
(corresponding to the
simple positive roots),
f_{1},\ldots,f_{\ell}
(corresponding to the negative
simple roots) and
h_{1},\ldots,h_{\ell}
in the Cartan subalgebra such that

[h_{i},h_{j}]=0,\,[e_{i},f_{j}]=\delta_{ij}h_{i},\,[h_{j},e_{i}]=C(i,j)e_{i},\,[h_{j},f_{i}]=-C(i,j)f_{i}\text{ for }1\leq i,j\leq\ell,

where
C
is the Cartan matrix of the root system. These elements generate

\mathfrak{a}
and are called a canonical set of generators of
\mathfrak{a}
.
Let
\pi
be a permutation of
\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
such that
C(i,j)=C(\pi(i),\pi(j))
for all
i,j
. Then mapping
e_{i}\mapsto e_{\pi(i)}
,

f_{i}\mapsto f_{\pi(i)}
,
h_{i}\mapsto h_{\pi(i)}
extends to a unique automorphism

\sigma_{\pi}
of
\mathfrak{a}
(cf.
[
19
, Theorem IV.3]
). Here we call

\sigma_{\pi}
a
pure diagram automorphism
of
\mathfrak{a}
(with respect to the
fixed choice of a canonical generating set). Let
\Gamma
be
the group of all pure diagram automorphisms. Let
\mathrm{Int}(\mathfrak{a})
be the inner
automorphism group of
\mathfrak{a}
(this is the algebraic subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{a})

generated by
\exp(\mathrm{ad}x)
for all nilpotent
x\in\mathfrak{a}
). Then
\mathrm{Int}(\mathfrak{a})

is the identity component of the automorphism group of
\mathfrak{a}
. Moreover, we
have
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{a})=\Gamma\ltimes\mathrm{Int}(\mathfrak{a})
(cf.
[
5
, VIII.5 no 3, Cor 1]
,

[
19
, §IX.4]
).

Now let
A\subset\mathrm{GL}(V)
be an algebraic group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{gl}(V)
. Suppose that
\mathfrak{a}
is semisimple. Let
g\in A
; then
\mathrm{Ad}(g):\mathfrak{a}\to\mathfrak{a}
with
\mathrm{Ad}(g)(x)=gxg^{-1}
is an automorphism of
\mathfrak{a}
. So we get a
homomorphism
\mathrm{Ad}:A\to\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{a})
. We have that
\mathrm{Ad}:A^{\circ}\to\mathrm{Int}(\mathfrak{a})
is
surjective. Let
g\in A
then we can write
\mathrm{Ad}(g)=\sigma_{\pi}\tau

with
\sigma_{\pi}\in\Gamma
and
\tau\in\mathrm{Int}(\mathfrak{a})
. Let
g_{\tau}
be a preimage
of
\tau
in
A^{\circ}
.
Then
\mathrm{Ad}(gg_{\tau}^{-1})=\sigma_{\pi}
. It follows that every component of
A

contains an element whose image under
\mathrm{Ad}
is a pure diagram automorphism.
In our situation we always have that
A
is a subgroup of
G=\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{g})
.
The Lie algebra of
G
is
\mathrm{ad}\mathfrak{g}=\{\mathrm{ad}x\mid x\in\mathfrak{g}\}
. So

\mathfrak{a}=\{\mathrm{ad}x\mid x\in\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}\}
where
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
is a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
.
For
g\in G
,
x\in\mathfrak{g}
we have
\mathrm{Ad}(g)(\mathrm{ad}x)=\mathrm{ad}g(x)
. It follows that
instead of the adjoint action of
A
on
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathrm{ad}\mathfrak{g}
we can also work with
directly with the action of
A
on
\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}
.

Let
A
,
\mathfrak{a}
be as above. We fix a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{a}
and corresponding
root system
\Psi
with a fixed set of simple roots
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{\ell}
.
We fix a Chevalley basis of
\mathfrak{a}
consisting of
root vectors
x_{\beta}
for
\beta\in\Psi
and

in the Cartan
subalgebra (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 25.2]
). For
\beta\in\Psi
define the
elements

\displaystyle x_{\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp(tx_{\beta}),\,(t\in\mathbb{C}),

\displaystyle w_{\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=x_{\beta}(t)x_{-\beta}(-t^{-1})x_{\beta}(t),\,(t\in\mathbb{C}^{*}),

\displaystyle h_{\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=w_{\beta}(t)w_{\beta}(1)^{-1},\,(t\in\mathbb{C}^{*}).

Let
w
be an element of the Weyl group of
\Psi
and let
w=s_{\beta_{i_{1}}}\cdots s_{\beta_{i_{r}}}
be a reduced expression (so the
\beta_{i_{k}}
are simple roots).
Then we define
\dot{w}=w_{\beta_{i_{1}}}(1)\cdots w_{\beta_{i_{r}}}(1)
. Also let

\Psi_{w}
be the set of positive roots
\beta\in\Psi
such that
w(\beta)
is
a negative root. Let
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}
be the positive roots of

\Psi
and write
\Psi_{w}=\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{q}\}
. Then every element of

A^{\circ}
can be written as

(5.1)

x_{\beta_{1}}(u_{1})\cdots x_{\beta_{m}}(u_{m})h_{\beta_{1}}(t_{1})\cdots h_{\beta_{\ell}}(t_{\ell})\dot{w}x_{\gamma_{1}}(s_{1})\cdots x_{\gamma_{q}}(s_{q})

where
u_{i},s_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
,

and
w
runs over the Weyl group of
\Psi

(cf.
[
15
, Theorem 5.2.23]
,
[
32
, Corollary 8.3.9]
).
For
w\in W
let
C_{w}
denote the set of all elements of the form
(

). Then

is the disjoint union of the sets

.
This is called the Bruhat decomposition of

and the

are called
the Bruhat cells of

. For our purposes this way of writing elements of

is useful because it gives a parametrization of

. Using it we
can use Gröbner basis techniques for finding elements with certain
properties of

(for example those that stabilize a given

).

We also remark here that there exists an algorithm that given a
connected

algebraic
group

with Lie algebra

decides whether a

lies in

, taking as input the element

and a basis of

(see
[

). When computing component groups this
algorithm is very useful, as it allows to decide whether a given element lies
in the identity component of an algebraic group, or whether two elements lie

4. If this is the case then we add a (

On some occasions we have also used the computer algebra system

]

GAP
). We set
\mathfrak{z}=\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime}\oplus\mathfrak{z}_{2}
. We are
interested in the
component group of
Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime})
. Every component of the latter group has
an element that restricts to a pure diagram automorphism of
\mathfrak{z}_{2}
and such
that its restriction to
\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime}
is the identity. As
\mathfrak{z}
-module
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
has
highest weights
(0;31)
,
(2;11)
,
(4;11)
,
(2;31)
(
GAP
).
So again, an element of
Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime})
cannot act
on
\mathfrak{z}_{2}
as a non-trivial pure diagram automorphism. Again we denote the
highest weight vectors by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{4}
.
The elements of
G
that are the identity
on
\mathfrak{z}
and map
v_{i}\mapsto z_{i}v_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 4
form a cyclic
group of order 4 (
GAP
). However, the square of a generator lies in

Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime})^{\circ}
(
GAP
). So the component group of

Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime})
is of order 2.
Let
h_{0}
denote a nontrivial element.
Using the Bruhat decomposition of
Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime})^{\circ}

we find the set
U
of elements
g
of that group with
g(p)=p
(
GAP
).
In this case
every solution set has dimension 1 because of the following reason.
Write
A=Z_{G_{0}}(p)\cap Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime})^{\circ}
. Then
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}(p)\cap\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}(\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime})
is its Lie algebra. We have that
\mathfrak{a}
is the 1-dimensional
centre of
\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}(p)
. Hence the solution set of our equations is
1-dimensional. Actually, the Bruhat decomposition of
Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime})^{\circ}
has
four cells, two of which have empty intersection with
U
and the intersection
of the other two with
U
is a variety of dimension 1. We can compute
an isomorphism
\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{*}\to A^{\circ}
(
GAP
).
We use this to partition
U
into

A^{\circ}
-cosets, of which there are two. One of the two representatives of
these cosets lies in
Z_{G_{0}}(p)^{\circ}
, whereas the other does not.
Secondly, by Bruhat decomposition
again we parametrize the set of
g
in
Z_{G_{0}}(\mathfrak{z}_{1})^{\circ}
with
g(p)=h_{0}(p)
.
For this there turns out to be no solution (
GAP
).
So the component group of
Z_{G_{0}}(p)
is of order 2.

Let
p\in\mathfrak{c}_{M_{7}}^{\circ}
.
Then
Z_{G_{0}}(p)^{\circ}
is of type
2A_{1}

(
GAP
), and the
component group of
Z_{G_{0}}(p)
is of order 2. In order to see this
write
\mathfrak{z}_{1}=\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}(p)
then
\mathfrak{z}_{1}
is semisimple of type
2A_{1}
(
GAP
).
As
\mathfrak{z}_{1}
-module
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
splits as a direct sum of 14 irreducible submodules.
Among these there is a unique module with highest weight
(3,1)
and a
unique module with highest weight
(1,3)
(
GAP
). Let
v_{1},v_{2}
be
corresponding
highest weight vectors. A computation shows that
\mathfrak{g}
is generated by

\mathfrak{z}_{1}
,
p
,
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
(
GAP
). Every component of
Z_{G_{0}}(p)

contains an
element that restricts to either the identity or a pure diagram automorphism
of
\mathfrak{z}_{1}
. Furthermore if
g\in Z_{G_{0}}(p)
restricts to the identity on

\mathfrak{z}_{1}
then
g(v_{1})=z_{1}v_{1}
,
g(v_{2})=z_{2}v_{2}
. All automorphisms of

\mathfrak{g}
satisfying these conditions correspond to the solution set of a
set of polynomial equations in
z_{1},z_{2}
.
It turns out that there are two solutions and both yield elements
that lie in
Z_{G_{0}}(p)^{\circ}
(
GAP
). Next we consider the elements that
restrict to a
pure diagram automorphism of
\mathfrak{z}_{1}
. In this case we have
g(v_{1})=z_{1}v_{2}
,

g(v_{2})=z_{2}v_{1}
. We do the same thing, and again get two solutions yielding
two automorphisms of order 2 (
GAP
). They do not lie in the identity
component, but
are equal modulo the identity component (
GAP
).
Hence only one element of order 2 remains.

Let
p\in\mathfrak{c}_{M_{8}}^{\circ}
.
Then
Z_{G_{0}}(p)^{\circ}
is of type
2A_{1}+A_{2}+T_{1}

(
GAP
).
The stabilizer
Z_{G_{0}}(p)
has two components. This is very
similar to the previous case. Here we work with the derived algebra
\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime}

of
\mathfrak{z}_{1}=\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}(p)
. It is semisimple, we enumerate its Dynkin diagram
as follows: first the two
A_{1}^{\prime}s
, then the
A_{2}
. Now in
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
there are
unique
\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime}
-submodules of highest weights
(2;0;01)
,
(0;2;10)
,

(1;1;10)
,
(1;1;01)
(
GAP
). We see that the only possible diagram
automorphism is the one that
simultaneously interchanges the two
A_{1}^{\prime}s
and reverses the Dynkin diagram of

A_{2}
. We denote the four highest weight vectors by
v_{1},\ldots,v_{4}
.
Then
\mathfrak{g}
is generated by
\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime}
,
p
,
v_{1},\ldots,v_{4}

(
GAP
).
Now we proceed exactly as in the previous case. However, due to the
presence of the 1-dimensional centre, the solution set of our polynomial
equations is also 1-dimensional. Let
T
denote the connected algebraic subgroup
of
G
whose Lie algebra is the centre of
\mathfrak{z}_{1}
(or, more precisely,

ad\mathfrak{z}_{1}
). We can compute an explicit isomorphism
\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{*}\to T

(
GAP
).
Explicit computation shows that
\lambda(t)(v_{1})=t^{-2}v_{1}
(
GAP
).
Let
g\in Z_{G_{0}}(p)
restrict to the identity on
\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime}
and map
v_{i}
to

z_{i}v_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq 4
, or restrict to the only possible nontrivial diagram automorphism
of
\mathfrak{z}_{1}^{\prime}
and such that
g(v_{1})=z_{1}v_{2}
,
g(v_{2})=z_{2}v_{1}
,
g(v_{3})=z_{3}v_{4}
,

g(v_{4})=z_{4}v_{3}
. Then by multiplying
g
by a suitable element of the form

\lambda(t)
we find an element of the same component of
Z_{G_{0}}(p)
and
such that
z_{1}=1
. This extra condition makes the solution set
finite. Similarly to the previous case, we find two elements that restrict
to the identity, and two elements that restrict to a pure diagram automorphism
(
GAP
).
The former elements lie in the identity component, whereas the latter are
equal modulo the identity component. So also here we have a component
group of order 2.

Let
p\in\mathfrak{c}_{M_{9}}^{\circ}
.
We have
\mathfrak{c}_{M_{9}}^{\circ}=0

so that
Z_{G_{0}}(p)=G_{0}
which is connected.

6.
The mixed orbits

In this section we determine the orbits of mixed type. Such an orbit has a
representative of the form
p+e
where
p
is semisimple,
e
is nilpotent

[p,e]=0
and
p,e
are both nonzero. A first remark is that
e
lies in the
graded subalgebra
\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}}(p)

p
lies in one
of the classes
\mathfrak{c}_{M_{i}}^{\circ}
listed in Table
1

p+e
and
p+e^{\prime}
are
G_{0}
-conjugate if and only if

are

Z_{G_{0}}(p)
-conjugate. By Theorem
5.1
the stabilizer
Z_{G_{0}}(p)

does not depend on the choice of the point
p
in
\mathfrak{c}_{M_{i}}^{\circ}
.
Furthermore, in the previous section the component groups of these stabilizers
have been determined. Using the algorithms of

14
]
we can determine
the nilpotent

-orbits in
\mathfrak{z}_{\mathfrak{g}}(p)_{1}

component group of
Z_{G_{0}}(p)

explictly, we can decide this and obtain an irredundant list of nilpotent parts
of the mixed elements with semisimple part from

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple

[h,e]=2e,\,[h,f]=-2f,\,[e,f]=h.

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triples

(h,e,f)

G_{0}

-conjugate (cf.

\mathfrak{g}_{0}
