Caesarean section may be associated with suspected motor developmental delay at 3 years of age in the Japan Environment and Children’s Study
Authors: Ikuyo Sanzen, Kenta Matsumura, Hitomi Inano, Akiko Tsuchida, Junko Sakai, Kanako Shimada, Hidekuni Inadera, the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS) Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe number of births by caesarean section (CS) is increasing worldwide. In line with this trend, there is growing interest in the neurodevelopmental outcome of children born by CS. This study examined the relationship between the mode of delivery and suspected neurodevelopmental delay in young children. A total of 53,716 mother–child pairs participating in the Japan Environment and Children’s Study, a representative Japanese cohort study of children, were included in the analysis of the association between CS and suspected developmental delay, as assessed by the Ages and Stages Questionnaires, Third Edition, in 3-year-old children. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). No significant overall relationship was found between mode of delivery and suspected developmental delay. In an analysis by sex, CS was associated with increased risk of suspected gross motor developmental delay (aOR 1.27, 95% CI 1.07–1.49) in boys and increased risk of suspected fine motor developmental delay (aOR 1.34, 95% CI 1.11–1.63) in girls. These results suggest that there may be a relationship between CS and suspected motor developmental delay at the age of 3 years.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46955-3.Keywords: Caesarean section; Mode of delivery; Neurodevelopmental delay; The Ages and Stages Questionnaires, Third Edition; Birth cohort studySubject terms: Health care, Medical research, Neuroscience

Introduction

Caesarean section (CS) is often performed to protect both the maternal and foetal health and is increasingly performed worldwide1,2–3. The global CS rate has risen significantly from approximately 7% in 1990 to 21% at present, exceeding the ideal acceptable CS rate of approximately 10–15%, according to the World Health Organization (WHO)4,5. CS is indicated when there are foetal health issues, such as abnormal foetal heart rate or poor development, or maternal health issues, such as pregnancy toxicosis and placenta praevia6. Other indications include slow or stalled labour progress and multiple pregnancies6. However, not all CS deliveries are performed for medical reasons, and the rate of CS deliveries performed for non-medical reasons is increasing rapidly7. These include “mother’s requests”, such as alleviating fear and anxiety about vaginal delivery and the pain associated with it, and wanting to give birth on a specific day7. In addition, social, cultural, and religious reasons as well as protection against legal consequences and lawsuits due to adverse outcomes of vaginal delivery have also been linked to CS2,7.

In line with this increasing trend in CS, there have been reports on the potential effect of the mode of delivery (CS vs. vaginal) on the development of diseases in childhood and beyond. CS has been suggested to be associated with a variety of diseases, including asthma, type 1 diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, and obesity in later life8–11–12. There is also an increasing importance of studies evaluating the impact of CS on the long-term development of children13. Developmental delays can be caused by various factors, including congenital factors such as chromosomal abnormalities; social factors such as the child-rearing environment and parental mental disorders (e.g., depression); and prenatal and perinatal factors such as infection and alcohol consumption during pregnancy14,15–16. Apart from these factors, some studies have reported an increased risk of motor delay and intellectual disability associated with CS17,18. We have also reported an increased prevalence of neurodevelopmental delay in 3-year-old children born by CS19. One of the characteristic features of developmental disorders is delayed language development, which is a diagnostic criterion for autism spectrum disorder13,19. Delayed language development often becomes noticeable from around the age of 2 or 3 years and is reported to become more pronounced by 3 years of age20,21. For children with suspected developmental delays, early identification of the cause enables the initiation of appropriate treatment and education tailored to the child’s needs16. With this in mind, mental development tests have been introduced into the early childhood screening programme in Japan22, with evidence suggesting the importance of early detection of developmental delays through limited screening and subsequent follow-up21. The period between the ages of 3 and 4 years is a critical stage of development when pre-school social activities increase, such as attending kindergarten. The objective of this study was to examine whether there is an association between CS and developmental delay as assessed by the ASQ-3 at the age of 3 years.

Methods

Study population

Participants were the members of the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS), an ongoing, nationwide, government-funded birth cohort study focusing on the relationship between various environmental factors and children’s health and development. The JECS’s study design has been reported in detail elsewhere23,24. Briefly, recruitment for the JECS was conducted between January 2011 and March 2014 at Co-operating Health Care Providers, and 15 Regional Centres across Japan, including both rural and urban areas. To be eligible, participants (pregnant women) were required to (1) be living in any of the Study Areas at the time of recruitment and expected to continue living in Japan for the foreseeable future; (2) have a due date between 1 August 2011 and mid-2014; and (3) be able to understand Japanese and respond to self-administered questionnaires. The follow-up programme was designed as follows. Children who participated in the study were followed up until they reached the age of 18 years. Questionnaires, blood and urine samples, and other assessments were conducted on two occasions during pregnancy (during the first and second/third trimesters), at delivery, and during the 1-month postpartum check-up at the hospital. After 6 months, questionnaires were sent out by mail twice a year. We also contacted the local government office of the city where the participants lived to confirm their resident registration status and to determine whether they had moved out of the Study Areas. For participants who had moved out of the Study Areas but still had access to them, the follow‑up programme, including the questionnaire and survey, was continued. This study analysed the jecs-qa-20210401 dataset, which was released in April 2021. This dataset contains data on 103,057 cases of pregnancy. Of these, 5647 cases of multiple participation, 948 cases of multiple pregnancies, and 3521 cases of miscarriages/premature births were excluded to obtain unique mother–child pairs. Of the remaining 92,941 mothers, 532 were excluded due to missing information on CS history. In addition, 20,303 cases with no response to the ASQ-3 and 53 cases with missing data on body mass index (BMI) and birth weight were excluded. Furthermore, 10,722 cases with pregnancy complications, 1281 cases with congenital anomalies25, 117 cases without normal umbilical artery blood gas values, 1,156 cases with non-reassuring foetal status (NRFS), 2239 cases with preterm birth, and 2822 cases with low birth weight were excluded. Ultimately, data from a total of 53,716 mother–child pairs were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). The JECS protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ministry of the Environment’s Institutional Review Board on Epidemiological Studies (100,910,001) and the Ethics Committees of all participating institutions. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The protocol for the present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of all collaborating institutions including the 15 Regional Centers, the JECS Core Center, and the Medical Support Center. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Measures

Data on confounding factors were collected from the self-administered JECS questionnaires completed by the participants during the pregnancy and the 1-month postpartum follow-up and from the medical records transcripts. The outcome (dependent variable) was the presence or absence of suspected developmental delay in five domains of neuropsychological development assessed at the age of 3 years (C3y_ASQ): aComm, bGM, cFM, dProble, and ePersonal (scores below the cutoff value were defined as suspected developmental delay). The Ages and Stages Questionnaires, Third Edition (ASQ-3) is designed to cover children aged 1 month to 5.5 years and has been validated in epidemiological studies26. The Japanese version of the ASQ-3 has also been validated27 and has been used previously in the JECS28,29. The translation of the ASQ-3 was performed under contract with Brookes Publishing Company, which has granted permission for use of the translated version to the JECS27. The quantitative use of the ASQ-3 is a method of screening children at risk of developmental delay, in which parents answer questions in the following five domains: (a) communication—questions related to language skills such as babbling, vocalisation, listening, and understanding, and also including the ability to answer one’s own name and give simple instructions using words; (b) gross motor—questions related to arm, body, and leg movements during exercise and play, such as being able to kick a ball or jump; (c) fine motor—questions measuring hand and finger movement, such as being able to cut paper with scissors or use a pencil or crayon properly; (d) problem solving—questions related to problem-solving ability, such as reciting numbers or arranging blocks as demonstrated as well as learning and playing with toys; and (e) personal–social—questions related to personal care skills, such as being able to dress oneself or follow rules as well as solitary social play, playing with toys, and playing with others. The ASQ-3 consists of a total of 30 questions (six questions for each domain), each of which is answered with “yes” (score 10), “sometimes” (score 5), or “not yet” (score 0), yielding a score of 0–60 for each domain. In each domain, a score below the cutoff value was considered to indicate developmental delay27. The independent variable (major variable) was “whether or not a CS was performed”. Medical data were transcribed from medical records containing information on the mode of delivery (CS vs. vaginal), gestational age, and birth weight, which were recorded by doctors, midwives/nurses, and/or Research Co-ordinators.

Statistical analysis

To examine the relationship between the mode of delivery and developmental delay, we used a logistic regression model to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with crude odds ratios (cORs) and 95% CIs, with vaginal delivery as the reference category. Because multiple comparisons were conducted in the logistic regression analyses, we applied a Bonferroni adjustment and considered corrected P values < 0.01 to indicate statistical significance. Potential confounding factors were selected as variables that were likely to affect or be associated with the mode of delivery and suspected neurodevelopmental delay. For example, autism spectrum disorder is known to be highly heritable and to be associated with a number of comorbid conditions, including anxiety, depression, and hyperactivity30, and maternal autistic traits or a history of mental illness may influence both delivery mode and child development31. Moreover, maternal prenatal and perinatal conditions are known to affect infant motor development32. Therefore, the following potential confounding variables were included: maternal age; pre-pregnancy BMI; number of deliveries; history of mental illness, including depression, anxiety disorder, autonomic imbalance, or schizophrenia; history of physical illness; marital status; employment status; highest educational attainment; annual household income; alcohol consumption33; smoking history; negative attitude towards pregnancy; sex of the child; gestational age; birth weight; maternal autistic traits; and breech presentation. The covariates were classified according to our previous study, following normal medical practice or common practice in Japan, except for the four continuous variables of maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, age at conception, and birth weight34. Among the 53,716 pregnancies, the missing data rate was < 1% for most possible confounders and exposure factors, except for parity, maternal employment status, household income, alcohol intake, and smoking history, for which the missing data rates were 2.56% (n = 1377), 1.19% (n = 639), 6.78% (n = 3642), 1.11% (n = 595), and 1.13% (n = 607), respectively. Missing values for the 17 covariates were coded as a dummy value (99) and included in the logistic regression analysis as a separate category (data not shown). No imputation was performed. An additional analysis stratified by the child’s sex was performed. We calculated variance inflation factors to assess multicollinearity. Standardized mean differences were used to compare baseline characteristics between groups, with values less than 0.1 indicating a negligible imbalance. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS ver. 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 53,716 single births, 7799 were by CS and 45,917 were by vaginal delivery. Table 1 shows the characteristics of the mothers and children, stratified by the mode of delivery. Compared with mothers who had a vaginal delivery, those who underwent CS were older and had higher obesity rates, and their neonates were smaller and more likely to present in the breech position. Regarding the prevalence of suspected developmental delays at 3 years of age, communication delay was reported in 1872 (3.5%), gross motor developmental delay in 2,112 (3.9%), fine motor developmental delay in 3771 (7.0%), problem solving delay in 3642 (6.8%), and personal-social delay in 1580 (2.9%). Compared with mothers who were included in the analysis (n = 53,716), those who were excluded (n = 39,225) tended to be older and obese, to have mental illness, to have a lower education level, and to be a current smoker (Supplementary Table 1). Multicollinearity was not detected among any covariates, as all variance inflation factors were below 1.99.

CS linked to no risk of developmental delay

As shown in Table 2, an unadjusted analysis showed that children born by CS had a higher risk of suspected developmental delay at 3 years of age than those born by vaginal delivery (communication: cOR 1.19, 95% CI 1.05–1.35; gross motor: cOR 1.26, 95% CI 1.13–1,42; fine motor: cOR 1.24, 95% CI 1.14–1.36; problem solving: cOR 1.13, 95% CI 1.03–1.24; and personal–social: cOR 1.22, 95% CI 1.06–1.39). After adjusting for all of the confounding factors listed in Table 1, CS was not associated with any risk of developmental delay.

Discussion

This large-scale cohort study of 53,716 mother–child pairs found that children born by CS were not at higher risk of suspected neurodevelopmental delay at the age of 3 years compared with those born by vaginal delivery. The sex-stratified analysis showed that boys born by CS were at higher risk of suspected gross motor developmental delay, whereas girls born by CS were at higher risk of fine motor developmental delay.

After adjustment for potential confounding factors, this study found that the risk of suspected gross motor developmental delay was 27% higher in boys born by CS than in those born by vaginal delivery. The risk difference was 1.5% for boys. This result differed from the findings of our team’s previous analysis, in which the outcome was a diagnosis of motor developmental delay19. For girls, on the other hand, the risk of suspected fine motor developmental delay was 34% higher in those born by CS than in those born by vaginal delivery. The risk difference was 1.1% for girls. This finding was consistent with previous research19.

For the foetus, the most significant difference between vaginal delivery and CS is mechanical stimulation during vaginal birth. During vaginal delivery, the head of the foetus rotates during passage through the birth canal. This rotary vestibular stimulation may activate the vestibular nuclei in the brainstem. Crucially, early developmental milestones in infancy (head control, rolling over, sitting up) are closely linked to maturation of the vestibular system35–37. Furthermore, vestibular stimulation has been linked to a significant improvement in gross motor skills in infants37. Measuring vestibular stimulation experienced by the foetus during labour is challenging, making it difficult to compare the extent of vestibular stimulation during vaginal delivery with that during caesarean section. However, it is possible that vestibular stimulation during delivery significantly influences the development of the newborn’s motor system, with effects potentially persisting even at the age of 3 years.

Although various factors affect development14,38, another possible reason that CS can be a cause of developmental delay is that the baby is not exposed to the intestinal flora because it does not pass through the vagina. There is growing interest in the involvement of the gut–brain axis in the pathogenesis of various diseases, and accumulating evidence suggests that changes in the composition of the intestinal flora play an important role in the gut–brain axis39. The gut–brain axis is a dynamic, interactive neuroendocrine system, and is a concept used to explain the connection between the digestive tract and the nervous system. There are many regulatory factors shared by the intestinal tract and the central nervous system (CNS). Both the hormones secreted by enterochromaffin cells in the gut and the metabolic products produced by the intestinal flora provide a direct means of communication between the external environment and the brain, thereby interacting with biochemical pathways that affect CNS processes40. Immediately after birth, the neonate is rapidly colonised by microorganisms from the mother and the surrounding environment41. This process of postnatal intestinal flora formation suggests that the mode of delivery (vaginal vs. CS) can be a key factor.

Several previous studies have reported that CS is associated with delayed motor development compared to vaginal delivery42–45. The difference becomes apparent from 48 h after birth44, and CS may continue to affect motor development up to the age of 10 years45. CS deliveries are frequently performed to prevent neurological sequelae in the foetus, and many cases have been avoided due to the procedure, though it may also be linked to long-term motor developmental delays. Future research should investigate whether motor developmental delays associated with CS disappear or persist after the age of 3 years.

Early intervention programmes are particularly valuable when children are first identified as being at high risk of developmental delay38,46,47. The present study indicates that children born by CS are at an increased risk of suspected gross motor developmental delay in boys and fine motor developmental delay in girls. In addition, consistent with various reports on sex differences in post-CS health problems, the present study also found that there were sex differences in the risk of developmental delay in children born by CS compared with those born by vaginal delivery. Meanwhile, there is also a report that temporary motor retardation was observed in children born by CS, but it subsequently disappeared17. Children who exhibit suspected developmental delays may later be diagnosed with developmental disorders, or, with appropriate support, their development may catch up and the delays may resolve48.

The strength of this study is that it used a very large dataset of mother–child pairs from all over Japan, including both rural and urban areas, and the results are likely to be representative of the general Japanese population. Moreover, the analysis was conducted after adjusting for many potential confounding factors. However, there were several limitations to our study, including the following. First, because this study used a self-administered questionnaire, it is unclear whether the responses given by the mothers accurately reflect the children’s developmental status. Second, some children exhibit developmental delays gradually after the age range of this study, and there are also many children who exhibit neurodevelopmental delays in early infancy, but whose development subsequently catches up. It is thus necessary to conduct a similar study in a later stage of development. Third, due to the limitations of this study, we could not include the father’s age as a covariate in the analysis of the child’s delayed neurodevelopment. Fourth, this observational study could not demonstrate a causal relationship between CS and motor developmental delay. Fifth, this study could not distinguish between elective and emergency CS. However, data were collected on the reasons for caesarean delivery. By analysing only cases considered to have a low risk of neurological sequelae for the child, the limitation that we could not determine whether the caesarean was elective or emergency was largely mitigated. Cases with a low risk of neurological sequelae met the following criteria: no pregnancy complications, no congenital anomalies, no foetal distress, umbilical artery blood gas pH ≥ 7.0, gestational age ≥ 37 weeks, and birth weight ≥ 2500 g.

Conclusion

This study revealed that boys born by CS are at higher risk of suspected gross motor developmental delay and girls born by CS are at higher risk of suspected fine motor developmental delay at 3 years of age compared with those born by vaginal delivery. In the combined analysis of boys and girls, there was no significant difference in suspected developmental delay between children born by CS and those born vaginally.

Data Availability Statement

Data are unsuitable for public deposition due to ethical restrictions and the legal framework of Japan. It is prohibited by the Act on the Protection of Personal Information (Act No. 57 of 30 May 2003, amended 9 September 2015) to publicly deposit data containing personal information. Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects enforced by the Japan Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare also restrict the open sharing of epidemiologic data. All inquiries about access to data should be sent to jecs-en@nies.go.jp. The person responsible for handling enquiries sent to this e-mail address is Dr Shoji F. Nakayama, JECS Programme Office, National Institute for Environmental Studies.
