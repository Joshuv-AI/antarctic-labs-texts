On explosion time in stochastic differential equations driven by fractional Brownian motion
Johanna Garzón, Jorge A. León, Soledad Torres, Ciprian A. Tudor, Lauri Viitasaari

Abstract
. In this article, we study the explosion time of the solution to autonomous stochastic differential equations driven by the fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter H > 1 / 2 H>1/2 . With the help of the Lamperti transformation, we are able to tackle the case of non-constant diffusion coefficients not covered in the literature. In addition, we provide an adaptive Euler-type numerical scheme for approximating the explosion time.



2020 AMS Classification Numbers: 65C30, 65L20, 60H10, 60G22

Key Words and Phrases
: Stochastic differential equations, explosion time, Fractional Brownian motion, Lamperti transformation, Euler scheme.

1.
Introduction

Fractional Brownian motion (fBm), with Hurst parameter
H
, denoted by
B^{H}
, is a centered Gaussian process with covariance
R_{H}(t,s)=\mathbf{E}(B^{H}({t})B^{H}({s}))=\frac{1}{2}\left(t^{2H}+s^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\right)
for
t,s\in[0,T]
and
H\in(0,1)
. In the case
H=\frac{1}{2}
the fBm reduces to the standard Wiener process. Analysis of the fBm, stochastic integration concerning it, and stochastic differential equations (SDEs) driven by it; fBm has constituted an important research direction in probability theory during the last few decades. This is due to the fact that fBm is perhaps the simplest generalization of the standard Brownian motion having many wanted features such as Gaussianity, self-similarity, and stationarity of the increments. Contrary to the standard Brownian motion, however, fBm does not have independent increments, allowing its usage in modeling memory effects.
Let us consider the following scalar fractional SDE

(1)

X({t})=x({0})+\int_{0}^{t}b(X(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X(s))dB^{H}({s}),\hskip 14.22636ptt\in[0,T],

where
X(0)=x(0)>0
, the coefficients
b
and
\sigma
satisfy some regularity conditions (detailed later on in Section
2
), and
(B^{H}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a fractional Brownian motion. In the case of standard Brownian motion (
H=\frac{1}{2}
) it is well-known that the solution of the stochastic equation (
1
) may explode in a finite time when the coefficient
b
does not satisfy the linear growth assumption. That is, the trajectories of the solution to (
1
) may approach infinity as
t
approaches a certain finite time
T_{e}
. As the SDE is random, the explosion time
T_{e}
is also random and depends on the particular path. Whether such explosion happens for a given coefficient function
b
is completely understood and characterized, see
[
8
]
or
[
20
]
for a detailed presentation.

The motivation for the study of exploding SDEs comes from applications. Indeed, SDEs with explosions are applied, for example, in stochastic models with fatigue cracking of materials, where the explosion time corresponds to the time of ultimate damage or fatigue failure in the material (see
[
3
,
24
]
and the references therein). On the other hand, in
[
25
]
, the authors claim that modeling this process as a Markov diffusion process requires a short correlation time of the noise term, which may not be realistic in some situations.
Therefore, it is natural to take a Gaussian long memory process as the noise with Hurst parameter
H>1/2
, suggesting that a fractional SDE will be a good model for this type of problem.

There are only a few results concerning fractional SDEs with explosion times. In
[
12
]
, the authors provided necessary and sufficient conditions for the solution to (
1
) explode in finite time in the case of constant diffusion
\sigma
. More precisely, in
[
12
]
, it was proven that if
b
is a positive, locally Lipschitz, and non-decreasing function and
\sigma=1
, then the solution to (
1
) explodes in finite time with probability
1
if and only if

\int_{x(0)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b(s)}ds<\infty.
In a related work,
[
22
]
the authors studied the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the fractional SDE with non-Lipschitz coefficients. They provided criteria for explosion under the assumption that the diffusion is close to zero (in a certain sense). Finally, we mention
[
15
]
where sufficient conditions for the nonoccurrence of explosion for the solution to (
1
) are given in the case that
H>\frac{1}{2}
and the stochastic integral with respect to fBm is understood in the Wick (non-pathwise) sense. In
[
15
]
, the author also gives examples of explosive solutions. For concrete examples of explosion for pathwise fractional SDEs, see also
[
7
]
or
[
5
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, there does not exist a general criterion for the explosion in the case of fractional pathwise SDEs under a more general diffusion coefficient
\sigma
.

Analysis of SDEs (
1
) also requires numerical methods as, in practice, one cannot simulate/solve (
1
) in continuous time. Numerical solutions based on the discretization of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) driven by the classical Brownian motion or, more generally, by semimartingales have been widely analyzed and are already well-understood. For details on various situations and techniques, we refer to the monograph
[
9
]
(see also
[
10
]
). For results on various Euler, Milstein, or Crank-Nicholson schemes in the case of fractional SDEs, see e.g.
[
2
,
4
,
14
,
17
,
19
]
, among others. However, it is worth to emphasizing that the development of numerical schemes for SDEs with an explosion is much more complicated. Indeed, one cannot use simple deterministic discretizations because when one approaches the explosion time, the time steps must be adapted to the sudden rapid growth of the solution.

The main contribution of this article is a method to approximate the explosion time of solution to (
1
), where
H>\frac{1}{2}
and the stochastic integral is understood in the pathwise sense. We begin by providing criteria for the explosion in the case of a more general diffusion coefficient
\sigma
than constant (studied in
[
12
]
) or small (studied in
[
22
]
). To do this, we apply the Lamperti transform, allowing us to treat
\sigma
as constant, after which the Osgood criterion for explosion provided in
[
12
]
is at our disposal. For the approximation scheme, we follow the ideas of
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
to construct an adaptive Euler scheme that reproduces the explosion of the solution. For this purpose, however, we do not have a martingale structure at our disposal, so we need to prove certain key technical estimates via other means. We also present a simple algorithm for approximating of the explosion time based on a prediction formula for the fBm derived recently in
[
21
]
.

The rest of the article is organized as follows: Section
2
recalls some preliminaries on fractional SDEs (
1
) and provides criteria for explosion. In Section
3
, we introduce an adaptive Euler scheme and present our main theorem on its convergence, cf. Theorem
4
. In Section
4
, we illustrate our method with numerical examples. We end the paper with an appendix containing the proof of Theorem
4
.

2.
Fractional SDEs with explosion

In Sections
2.1
-
2.2
, we recall some preliminaries on fractional calculus and corresponding integration with respect to fBm that allow us to study fractional SDEs. We continue with Section
2.3
, where we recall the explosion criteria of
[
12
]
that, when combined with the Lamperti transform, gives us our generalized explosion criteria, Theorem
3
. Interesting examples are gathered in section
2.4
. For details on our preliminaries, see e.g.
[
8
,
18
,
23
]
.

2.1.
Elements of the fractional calculus

The fractional integration theory below is valid for Hölder continuous functions and even beyond, see, e.g.
[
6
]
, but for our purposes, we will restrict ourselves to the case of the fBm. Let now
(B^{H}({t}))_{t\geq 0}
be an fBm with Hurst parameter
H\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
. For notational simplicity, here and in the sequel, we omit the superscript
H
and simply write
B
instead of
B^{H}
whenever confusion cannot arise. We begin by recalling the Hölder continuity of
B
.

Lemma 1
.

For each
T>0
and
0<\rho<H
there is a non-negative random variable
F_{B}=F_{B}({\omega},\rho,T)
such that
\mathbf{E}(F_{B}^{p})<\infty
for all
p\geq 1
and

|B(t)-B(s)|<F_{B}|t-s|^{H-\rho}\ \ \text{a.s.}\ \ \ t,s\in[0,T].

The pathwise integral w.r.t. fractional Brownian motion
B
can be defined by (see
[
23
]
)

(2)

\int_{a}^{b}f_{s}dB(s)=\int_{a}^{b}(D_{a+}^{\alpha}f)(s)(D_{b-}^{1-\alpha}B_{b-})(s)ds,

if last integral is well-defined, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and for
s\in[0,T]
,

(3)

(D_{a+}^{\alpha}f)(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left[\frac{f(s)}{(s-a)^{\alpha}}+\alpha\int_{a}^{s}\frac{f(s)-f(u)}{(s-u)^{1+\alpha}}du\right]I_{(a,b)}(s),

(4)

(D_{b-}^{1-\alpha}B_{b-})(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\left[\frac{B_{b-}(s)}{(b-s)^{1-\alpha}}+(1-\alpha)\int_{s}^{b}\frac{B_{b-}(s)-B_{b-}(u)}{(u-s)^{2-\alpha}}du\right]I_{(a,b)}(s),

are the so-called Riemann-Liouville fractional derivatives, where

B_{b-}(s)=(B(b)-B(s))I_{(a,b)}(s).

If
f\in C^{\nu}(a,b)
(
\nu
-Hölder continuous function) with
\nu+H>1
, the pathwise fractional integral exists for any
\alpha\in(1-H,\nu)
and it admits the estimate

(5)

\left|\int_{a}^{b}f_{s}dB(s)\right|\leq F_{0}\left[\int_{a}^{b}\frac{|f(s)|}{(s-a)^{\alpha}}ds+\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{s}\frac{|f(s)-f(u)|}{(s-u)^{\alpha+1}}duds\right],

where

(6)

F_{0}=F_{0}(b,a)=F_{0}(\omega,b,a)=C\cdot\sup_{a<s<b}|D_{b-}^{1-\alpha}B_{b-}(s)|.

If
a=0
, we denote
F_{0}(b)=F_{0}(b,0)=F_{0}(\omega,b,0)
.

We also recall the following result, see
[
18
, Lemma 7.5]
.

Lemma 2
.

For all
T>0
,
H>1/2
and
p\in[1,\infty)
we have

\mathbf{E}(F_{0}^{p}(T))=\mathbf{E}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t\leq T}D_{t-}^{1-\alpha}B_{t-}(s)\right)^{p}<\infty.

2.2.
The pathwise stochastic differential equation driven by fBm

We consider a fractional stochastic differential equation of the form (
1
) where
B^{H}:=B
is a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
H>1/2
,
x(0)>0
and
\sigma,b:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
are measurable functions. That is, we consider the integral equation

X(t)=x({0})+\int_{0}^{t}b(X(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(X(s))dB({s}),\hskip 14.22636ptt\geq 0,

where the integral with respect to
B
is defined as in (
2
), Section
2.1
.
To guarantee the existence of a solution to (
1
), we suppose that the coefficients
\sigma
and
b
satisfy the following assumptions:

(7)

Assumptions A, B, and C:

A:

\sigma
is differentiable and there exist constants
C_{\sigma},C_{N},K_{N},K>0
and
\kappa\in(\frac{1}{H}-1,1]
, such that
\sigma
satisfies:

(1):

Lipschitz continuity:

|\sigma(x)-\sigma(y)|\leq C_{\sigma}|x-y|,\ \ x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

(2):

Local Hölder continuity for the derivative of
\sigma
:

|\sigma^{\prime}(x)-\sigma^{\prime}(y)|\leq C_{N}|x-y|^{\kappa},\ \ |x|,|y|\leq N.

B:

Local Lipschitz continuity: for any
N>0
there exists
K_{N}>0
such that

|b(x)-b(y)|\leq K_{N}|x-y|,\ \ |x|,|y|\leq N.

C:

Linear growth: for every
x\in\mathbb{R}

|b(x)|\leq K(|x|+1),\ \ x\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us recall
[
18
, Theorem 2.1]
concerning the solution’s existence and to (
1
).

Theorem 1
.

Under conditions
A
,
B
, and
C
, the equation (
1
) has a unique solution
X
in
C^{H-\rho}(0,T)
a.s. for any
0<\rho<H
. Moreover, there exists a positive (random) constant
F_{X}=F_{X}(\omega)
satisfying
\mathbf{E}(F_{X}^{p})<\infty
for all
p\geq 1
such that

(8)

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|X(t)|<F_{X},\ \ \ \sup_{0\leq s\leq t\leq T}|X(t)-X(s)|<F_{X}|t-s|^{H-\rho}.

If the function
b
in the fractional SDE (
1
) does not satisfy Assumption
C
on linear growth, the solution of this equation may explode in finite time. That is, there exists a random time
T^{X}_{e}<\infty
such that
X(t)
is defined in

[0,T^{X}_{e})
and
\displaystyle\lim_{t\uparrow T^{X}_{e}}|X(t)|=\infty
(see McKean
[
13
]
, Section 3.3).

To study the explosion of the solution to (
1
), let
M>x(0)
be fixed and let the functions
b_{M}
and
\sigma_{M}
be such that

(1)

b_{M}
and
\sigma_{M}
are bounded and satisfy the conditions
A
and
B
.

(2)

b_{M}(x)=b(x)
and
\sigma_{M}(x)=\sigma(x)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
with
|x|\leq M
.

By Theorem
1
, there exists a unique solution
X_{M}
, called the local solution, to

(9)

X_{M}(t)=x({0})+\int_{0}^{t}b_{M}(X_{M}(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{M}(X_{M}(s))dB(s),\quad t\in[0,T].

For
M>x(0)
, we define the random time

(10)

T^{X}_{M}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:|X_{M}(t)|\geq M\},

where, as usual,
\inf\{\emptyset\}=\infty
. Clearly, the uniqueness of the solution to (
9
) implies that, for
M_{1}<M_{2}
we have
X_{M_{1}}(t)=X_{M_{2}}(t)
on
[0;T^{X}_{M_{1}})
, and
T^{X}_{M_{1}}\leq T^{X}_{M_{2}}
(actually, due to continuity of the solutions, the equality is possible only when both are infinite).
Let

(11)

T_{e}^{X}=\lim_{M\to\infty}T^{X}_{M}\ \ \text{a.s.}

Note that
T_{e}^{X}\in(0,\infty]
is well defined because the sequence
\{T^{X}_{M}:M>0\}
is non-decreasing. Moreover, there exists a process
X=\{X(t):t\in[0,T_{e}^{X})\}
such that

X(t)=X_{M}(t)
on
[0,T^{X}_{M})
for each
M>x(0)
.
We call this process
X
the solution to (
1
) in the case where
b
and
\sigma
satisfy Assumptions
A
and
B
but not
C
. This gives us the following definition.

Definition 1
.

The random time
T_{e}^{X}
given by (
11
) is called the explosion time of (
1
). If
T_{e}^{X}<\infty
, with positive probability we say that the solution to the SDE (
1
) explodes in finite time.

2.3.
Osgood criteria for the explosion

In what follows, we will give hypotheses on the
coefficients
b
and
\sigma
, which ensure on the one hand, the use of Lamperti’s transformation and, on the other hand, that they satisfy the hypotheses necessary to have a finite-time explosion a.s. as presented in references
[
12
,
11
]
.

First, let us recall the hypotheses and results obtained in
[
11
]
. The authors consider the following equation

(12)

X(t)=x(0)+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)g(X(s))ds+h(t),\quad t\geq 0,

under the following conditions:

H1:

a:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a continuous function such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{t}^{t+\eta}a(s)ds>0
, for some
\eta>0
.

H2:

g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is a continuous function and there exist
-\infty\leq l<\infty
and
-\infty<r<\infty
such that
g>0
and locally Lipschitz on
(l,\infty)
, and
g:[r,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is non-decreasing.

H3:

h:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function such that
\displaystyle\mathop{\overline{\rm lim}}_{t\to\infty}\displaystyle\inf_{0\leq u\leq{\tilde{\eta}}}h(t+u)=\infty
, for some
{\tilde{\eta}}>0
, where
\mathop{\overline{\rm lim}}
denotes the upper limit.

Remark 1
.

In what follows we suppose that
l=-\infty
. Otherwise, as it is point out in
[
11
]
(Remark (3.4)), we only need to assume that the initial condition of (
1
) is large enough.

The next theorem,
[
11
]
, provides criteria for establishing whether the solution to (
12
) explodes in finite time.

Theorem 2
.

Let
x(0)\in\mathbb{R}
and assume
H1-H3
. Then the explosion time
T^{X}_{e}
of the
solution of (
12
) is finite if and only if

\int^{\infty}_{r}\frac{ds}{g(s)}<\infty.

Remark 2
.

Here,
r
is given in
H2
. Also, by plugging in
h(t)=\sigma B(t)
with constant
\sigma
one obtains an explosion criteria for the SDE (
1
) in the case of constant diffusion
\sigma
.

Let us return to our equation (
1
) with a more general function
\sigma
. We pose the following additional hypothesis.

D:

For the coefficient functions
b
and
\sigma
, we assume:

(1):

For any
M>0
, there exist bounded functions
\sigma_{M}
and
b_{M}
satisfying
A
and
B
such that
b_{M}(x)=b(x)
and
\sigma_{M}(x)=\sigma(x)
for any
|x|\leq M
.

(2):

\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
is a strictly positive and continuous function.

(3):

\dfrac{b}{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
satisfies condition

H2
.

Note that the first statement guarantees that the local solutions
X_{M}
exists. Under
D
and with the initial condition
x(0)>0
, we define

(13)

\Theta(x)=\int_{x(0)}^{x}\frac{ds}{\sigma(s)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that
\Theta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is well-defined because
\sigma
is strictly positive and continuous.
Since
\sigma
is positive, it follows that
\Theta
is also strictly increasing, and hence
\Theta^{-1}
exists. Moreover,
\Theta^{-1}
is strictly increasing as well, and locally Lipschitz. Now following
[
16
]
, the process

(14)

Y(t)=\Theta(X(t)),\quad t\geq 0,

is the unique solution to the stochastic differential equation

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dY(t)&=g(Y(t))dt+dB(t),\quad t>0\\
Y(0)&=\Theta(x(0))\end{cases}

with

(16)

g(x)=\frac{b(\Theta^{-1}(x))}{\sigma(\Theta^{-1}(x))},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Now, equation (
15
) has the form of equation (
12
) and the fractional Brownian motion satisfies hypothesis
H3
, as pointed out in
[
12
]
. Moreover, Assumption
D
implies that
g
satisfies
H2
, and
H1
is trivially true for
a\equiv 1
. This leads to the following criteria.

Theorem 3
.

Let Hypothesis
D
hold and assume
\Theta(\infty)=\infty;\ \ \Theta(-\infty)=-\infty
. Then the solution
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
to (
1
) explodes in finite time, with probability one, if and only if

(17)

\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{b(s)}<\infty,

where
a=\Theta^{-1}(r)
and
r
is given in Statement (3) of Hypothesis
D
.

Proof:
Firstly, it follows from Hypothesis
D
that the local solutions
Y_{M}
(as defined in (
9
)) associated with (
15
) exist, and that the explosion time
T_{e}^{Y}
is well-defined. Due to Lamperti’s transform
Y(t)=\Theta(X(t))
, it follows that the local solutions
X_{M}
defined in (
9
) to equation (
1
) are well-defined as well. Moreover, since
\Theta(\infty)=\infty
,
\Theta(-\infty)=-\infty
and
\Theta
and
\Theta^{-1}
are continuous functions, it follows that
Y=\Theta(X)
explodes in finite time if and only if
X=\Theta^{-1}(Y)
explodes in finite time. Now, since
g
is a strictly positive, locally Lipschitz, and non-decreasing function, from Theorem
2
(or
[
12
, Theorem 4.3]
), the solution of equation (
15
) explodes in finite time with probability 1 if and only if

\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{g(x)}<\infty.

The claim now follows since, by the change of variable
u=\Theta^{-1}(x)
, we have

(18)

\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{g(x)}=\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma(\Theta^{-1}(x))}{b(\Theta^{-1}(x))}dx=\int_{a}^{\infty}\frac{du}{b(u)}

where
a=\Theta^{-1}(r)
, which is finite if and only if (
17
) holds. This completes the proof.

Remark 3
.

It is pointed out in
[
12
]
that if
Y
explodes in finite time, then
\lim_{t\uparrow T_{e}^{Y}}Y_{t}=\infty
. Therefore, if the solution
X
explodes in finite time, we have that
\displaystyle\lim_{t\uparrow T_{e}^{X}}X_{t}=\infty
.

2.4.
Examples

In this section, we present the following two interesting examples with non constant
\sigma
, in which cases the
result of
[
12
]
(that assumes constant
\sigma
) cannot be applied.

Example 1.

In
[
15
]
, it is proven that the solution to the stochastic differential equation

(19)

dX(t)=v(t)X^{N}(t)dt+a(t)X(t)dB(t);\quad X(0)=x(0)>0,\quad t\in[0,T],

where
v:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
and
a:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
are continuous functions and
N\geq 2
, explodes in finite time. In addition, it was shown that the solution remains positive.
Now, to fix ideas taking
v(t)=a(t)\equiv 1
and
x(0)=1
leads to

(20)

dX(t)=X^{N}(t)dt+X(t)dB(t);\quad x(0)=1,\quad t\in[0,T].

Note that, in this case, we only need to assume that
\sigma:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is strictly positive and continuous instead of Statement (2) in Hypothesis
D
.

Now from (
13
) we get, for
x\in(0,\infty)
,

\Theta(x)=\ln(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\Theta^{-1}(x)=e^{x}.

Thus (
16
) gives

g(x)=e^{(N-1)x}
that is a positive function, locally Lipschitz, and non-decreasing, and we see that Assumption
D
is satisfied.
Since now
\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{N}}<\infty
for
N\geq 2
, Theorem
3
implies that the solution to (
20
) explodes in finite time. In fact, the process
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
with
Y(t)=\Theta(X(t))
, is the unique solution to the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dY(t)&=e^{(N-1)Y(t)}dt+dB(t),\quad t>0\\
Y(0)&=0.\end{cases}

Thus, Theorem
2
implies that the process
Y
explodes in finite time, and hence the solution
X
also explodes in finite time.

Example 2.
In this example, we choose
b
and
\sigma
as in
[
3
]
. That is, we set
b(x)=(|x|+0.1)^{p}
and
\sigma(x)=(|x|+0.1)^{q}
with
0<q<1
and
p>1
.
Also, we consider the initial condition
x(0)>0
. It follows that
\Theta
, defined in (
13
), is given by

\Theta(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1-q}\left\{(x+0.1)^{1-q}-(x(0)+0.1)^{1-q}\right\},&x\geq 0\\
\frac{1}{1-q}\left\{2\times 0.1^{1-q}-(0.1-x)^{1-q}-(x(0)+0.1)^{1-q}\right\},&x<0,\end{cases}

with an inverse

\Theta^{-1}(x)=\begin{cases}\left\{(1-q)x+(x(0)+0.1)^{1-q}\right\}^{1/(1-q)}-0.1,&x\geq\frac{1}{1-q}[0.1^{1-q}-(x(0)+0.1)^{1-q}]\\
0.1-\left\{2\times 0.1^{1-q}-(x(0)+0.1)^{1-q}-x(1-q)\right\}^{1/(1-q)},&x\leq\frac{1}{1-q}[0.1^{1-q}-(x(0)+0.1)^{1-q}].\end{cases}

Now
Y=(Y(t))_{t>0}
defined as
Y(t)=\Theta(X(t))
is the unique solution to the stochastic differential equation

(21)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dY(t)&=g(Y(t))dt+dB(t),\quad t>0\\
y(0)&=\Theta(x(0))\end{cases}

with

g(x)=\frac{b(\Theta^{-1}(x))}{\sigma(\Theta^{-1}(x))}=(\lvert\Theta^{-1}(x)\rvert+0.1)^{p-q}.

Condition (1) of Hypothesis
D
is not satisfied. However, we can apply Theorem (
3
) since equation (
21
) has a unique solution
Y
that may explode in finite time. In consequence equation (
1
) has a unique solution
X
given by

X(t)=\Theta^{-1}(Y(t)),\quad t\geq 0.

Also, (
17
) is satisfied.

3.
Approximation of fractional SDEs with explosion

In this section, we introduce an adaptive Euler scheme approximating of the explosion time by following the ideas of

[
1
]
and
[
3
]
. To do so, we first consider the case that
\sigma
is a constant diffusion, then use the Lamperti transformation to deal with the general case.

3.1.
Adaptive Euler scheme for constant
\sigma

In this section, we approximate the explosion time of the solution to SDE

(22)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dY(t)&=g(Y(t))dt+\sigma dB(t),\quad t>0\\
Y(0)&=y(0)>0,\end{cases}

where
g
is a positive, locally Lipschitz and non-decreasing function, such that
\int_{y(0)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{g(s)}ds<\infty
(and hence, by Theorem
2
,
Y
explodes in finite time), and
\sigma>0
is a constant. Recall that
T^{Y}_{e}
denotes the explosion time of the solution
Y
. In order to introduce our numerical scheme, we pose an additional assumption that
g
is
bounded away from zero, i.e. for some constant
l_{g}
we have

(23)

0<l_{g}\leq g(y),\ \ \ \forall y\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
0<h<1
be the fixed parameter of the numerical method. The scheme for approximating the solution
Y
to equation (
22
) is defined as

(24)

\begin{cases}Y^{h}(t_{0})&=y(0),\\
Y^{h}(t_{k+1})&=Y^{h}{(t_{k})}+g(Y^{h}(t_{k}))\tau_{k}+\sigma[B(t_{k+1})-B(t_{k})],\end{cases}

where we take the random sequence
\{t_{k}\}
defined by
t_{0}=0
,
t_{k+1}=t_{k}+\tau_{k}
, where

(25)

\tau_{k}=\frac{h}{g(Y^{h}(t_{k}))}.

We interpolate continuously for
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
and we write

(26)

\displaystyle Y^{h}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y^{h}{(t_{k})}+g(Y^{h}(t_{k}))[t-t_{k}]+\sigma[B(t)-B(t_{k})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y(0)+\int_{0}^{t}g(Y^{h}(t_{u}))du+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}dB(u)

with
t_{u}:=t_{n_{u}}
,
n_{u}=\max\{k:t_{k}\leq u\}
. With
\tau_{k}
given by (
25
), we define

(27)

T^{h}_{e}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tau_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{h}{g(Y^{h}(t_{k}))}.

Note that if
T^{h}_{e}<\infty
almost surely, then
\tau_{k}
converges to zero which further implies that
g(Y^{h}(t_{k}))
goes to infinity, and hence
Y^{h}
explodes, because
g
is increasing. Thus
T^{h}_{e}
corresponds to the explosion time of the numerical scheme
Y^{h}
.

We will prove the convergence of the numerical approximations in compact (random) intervals where the solution and the numerical approximation are bounded.
For this result, note that the time steps
\tau_{k}
are
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}
-measurable and verify
\tau_{k}\leq Ch
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}
denotes the sigma-algebra generated by the driving fractional Brownian motion
B
.
Moreover, the numerical approximation
Y^{h}
is a well-defined process
up to time
T^{h}_{e}\wedge K
, for any
K>0
.

Now, let
M>y(0)
be fixed. We define the following random times

(28)

T_{M}^{Y}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:|Y(t)|\geq M\}

and

(29)

T_{M}^{Y^{h}}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:|Y^{h}(t)|\geq M\}.

By definition (
10
), the quantities
T_{M}^{Y}
an
T_{M}^{Y^{h}}
approximate explosion times
T^{Y}_{e}
and
T^{h}_{e}
as
M\to\infty
. The next result gives us the convergence of
Y^{h}
to
Y
and the convergence of their respective explosion times. For the proof, see Appendix
A
. Let us note that the results obtained are analogous to those presented in
[
3
]
for the Brownian motion case.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that
g
is a strictly positive, locally Lipschitz and non-decreasing function such that
\int_{y(0)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{g(s)}ds<\infty
and (
23
) holds. Let
Y
be the solution of the fractional SDE, (
22
) and
Y^{h}
is its approximation, as defined by (
24
) and (
26
). Then the following hold:

(i)

(Convergence of the approximation on intervals where
Y
and
Y^{h}
are finite)
Let
S>0
be a deterministic fixed time,
M>y(0)
be a constant, and let
T_{S}^{h}=T_{M}^{Y}\wedge T_{2M}^{Y^{h}}\wedge S
, where
T_{M}^{Y}
and
T_{M}^{Y^{h}}
are defined in (
28
) and (
29
) respectively. Then, for any
p\geq 1
,

\lim_{h\to 0}\mathbf{E}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{S}^{h}}|Y(t)-Y^{h}(t)|^{p}\right)=0.

(ii)

(a)

For every initial value
z>0
,
Y^{h}(\cdot)
explodes in finite time
T^{h}_{e}<\infty
with probability one.

(b)

We have,

(30)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{Y^{h}(t_{k})}{hk}=1\quad a.s.

(c)

Moreover, for any
\alpha>1
there exists
k_{0}=k_{0}(\omega)
such that, for every
k\geq k_{0}
, we have

\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{h}{g(\alpha hj)}\leq T^{h}_{e}-t_{k}\leq\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\frac{h}{g(\alpha^{-1}hj)}.

(iii)

(Relation between
T_{M}^{Y}
and
T_{M}^{Y^{h}}
) It holds that

P\left(T_{M}^{Y}>T^{Y^{h}}_{2M}\right)\to 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad P\left(T_{M}^{Y^{h}}>T_{2M}^{Y}\right)\to 0\quad\mbox{as}\quad{h\to 0}.

(iv)

(Convergence of the approximated explosion time) For any
\varepsilon>0
we have

\lim_{M\to\infty}\lim_{h\to 0}P\left(|T^{Y^{h}}_{M}-T_{e}^{Y}|>\varepsilon\right)=0.

Remark 4
.

(1)

By Theorem
4
item
(i)
, we have that our adaptive method converges to the solution
Y
of the original SDE (
22
) on compact intervals where both
Y
and
Y^{h}
remain bounded. Actually, the statement
(i)
is a standard convergence result, see, e.g.
[
14
]
, whenever the sample paths
Y
of the solution to (
22
) are uniformly bounded. In the case of an explosion in finite time, which is more interesting,
(i)
simply means convergence on regions where the solution
Y
and its approximation
Y^{h}
remain bounded. In general, we do not expect convergence in larger regions. Indeed, even the statement in
(i)
does not make sense if either
Y
or
Y^{h}
are allowed to explode.

(2)

Item
(iii)
states that we can approximate the explosion time
T^{Y}_{e}
with the sequence
T_{M}^{Y^{h}}
as expected and as illustrated in our numerical simulations see Section
4
. As
T^{Y^{h}}_{M}
is close to
T^{h}_{e}
for large
M
, this also means that explosion times
T^{h}_{e}
for the approximation
Y^{h}
and
T^{Y}_{e}
for the true solution
Y
are close.

3.2.
Adaptive Euler scheme for non-constant
\sigma

In this section, we extend our adaptive Euler approximation scheme to cover the case of non-constant
\sigma
. That is, we approximate the solution
X
to the SDE (
1
). For this purpose, we require the following additional assumption on the coefficients
\sigma
and
b
in (
1
), ensuring that the steps in the approximating scheme are well defined.

Assumption E:
Coefficient functions
b
and
\sigma
satisfy:

(1)

There is a positive constant
L_{2}
such that

0<L_{2}\leq\sigma(x)\leq b(x),\ \ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R},

(2)

b
satisfies (
17
),

(3)

\sigma
satisfies
\int_{x(0)}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{\sigma(s)}=\infty
, that is
\Theta(\infty)=\infty
and
\Theta(-\infty)=-\infty
.

Let
X
now be defined by (
1
). Under condition
D
, by Theorem
3
, the solution of (
1
) explodes in finite time
T_{e}^{X}
if and only if (
17
) holds.
In particular,
X
explodes in finite time under conditions
D
and
E
as Assumption
E
(3) implies
\Theta(\infty)=\infty
. Note also that by Assumption
E
(1), the function
g
defined by (
16
) satisfies

\ 0<l_{g}\leq 1\leq g(x),\ \ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence the process
Y
defined by (
14
) satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
4
. Based on the approximation
Y^{h}
for solution
Y=\Theta(X)
with
\Theta
given by (
13
), we obtain an approximation to the solution of process
X=\Theta^{-1}(Y)
as follows:

Let
Y^{h}(t_{k+1})
be defined by (
24
) with
\tau_{k}
and
t_{k}
defined as in Section
3.1
. We introduce the approximation scheme for process
X
by

(31)

\begin{cases}X^{h}(t_{0})&=x(0),\\
X^{h}(t_{k+1})&=\Theta^{-1}(Y^{h}(t_{k+1})),\end{cases}

where
\Theta
is given by (
13
). The following result gives the convergence of the approximation on regions where solution
X
and its approximation
X^{h}
are bounded, in the spirit of Theorem
4
item
(i)
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\sigma
and
b
be functions that satisfy conditions
D
and
E
, and
S>0
and
M>\Theta(x(0))
be fixed. Then, for any
p\geq 1
,

\lim_{h\to 0}\mathbf{E}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{S}^{h}}|X(t)-X^{h}(t)|^{p}\right)=0,

where
T_{S}^{h}:=T_{M}^{Y}\wedge T_{2M}^{Y^{h}}\wedge S
with
T_{M}
and
T_{M}^{h}
given by (
28
) and (
29
) respectively.

Proof:
For
t\in[0,T_{S}^{h}]
, we have that
|Y(t)|\leq M
and
|Y^{h}(t)|\leq 2M
. Since
\Theta^{-1}
is a locally Lipschitz function,

(32)

\displaystyle|X(t)-X^{h}(t)|=|\Theta^{-1}(Y(t))-\Theta^{-1}(Y^{h}(t))|\leq C_{M}|Y(t)-Y^{h}(t)|.

Hence

\mathbf{E}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{S}^{h}}|X(t)-X^{h}(t)|^{p}\right)\leq C_{M}^{p}\mathbf{E}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{S}^{h}}|Y(t)-Y^{h}(t)|^{p}\right)

and the result is obtained by Theorem
4

(i)
.

Remark 5
.

It follows directly from the transformations
X=\Theta^{-1}(Y)
and
X^{h}=\Theta^{-1}(Y^{h})
that the explosion times for true solutions
X
and
Y
and the approximated solutions
X^{h}
and
Y^{h}
are equal. As such, one obtains Theorem
4

(iii)
for
X
and
X^{h}
directly, allowing us to approximate the explosion time
T_{e}^{X}
with
T_{M}^{X^{h}}
, cf. Remark
4
.

We illustrate our adaptive numerical scheme with two simulation studies related to the examples in Section

. For effective simulations, we use of the prediction formula of the fractional Brownian motion presented in
[
21
]
. That is, the future law of
B(t)
given information up to
u<t
is Gaussian with (random) mean

B(u)-\int_{0}^{u}\Psi(t,s|u)dB(s),

where

\Psi(t,s|u)=-\frac{\sin(\pi(H-1/2))}{\pi}s^{1/2-H}(u-s)^{1/2-H}\int_{u}^{t}\frac{z^{H-1/2}(z-u)^{H-1/2}}{z-s}dz,

and a deterministic covariance

r(t,s|u)=R(t,s)-\int_{0}^{u}K(t,v)K(s,v)dv

with
R
being the covariance of
B

and
K
the Volterra Kernel of the fractional Brownian motion given by

K(t,s)=d_{H}\left[\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{H-1/2}(t-s)^{H-1/2}-(H-1/2)s^{1/2-H}\int_{s}^{t}z^{H-3/2}(z-s)^{H-1/2}dz\right].

t_{k}
(in which case we know
t_{k+1}
as well), we have, with random times
t_{k}
and
t_{k+1}

(33)

B(t_{k+1})-B(t_{k})\sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})

with

\mu=\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\Psi(t_{k+1},s|t_{k})dB(s),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma^{2}=r(t_{k+1},t_{k+1}|t_{k}).

This allows us to generate increments
B(t_{k+1})-B(t_{k})
as in (
33
) by using MATLAB.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
4

To prove Theorem
4
(i), we will first give a priori bounds on our adaptive numerical scheme that controls the increments of the approximation
Y^{h}
defined in (
24
) and the convergence of the adaptive scheme
Y^{h}
to
Y
when we are working with a bounded function
g
. The arguments are similar to the arguments presented in
[
14
]
. However, due to the presence of random times instead of deterministic times in our approximation method, we prefer to provide the key steps for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
g
is a positive, locally Lipschitz and non-decreasing function such that for some
L_{g},l_{g}>0
,

0<l_{g}\leq g(x)\leq L_{g},\forall x.

Then for any
0<\rho<H
and any
S>0
there exists a non-negative random variable
F_{{Y^{h}}}
such that
\mathbf{E}(F_{{Y^{h}}}^{p})<\infty
for all
p>1
and

(34)

|Y^{h}(s)-Y^{h}(u)|\leq F_{Y^{h}}(s-u)^{H-\rho},\ \ 0\leq u<s\leq S.

Moreover, it holds that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq S}|Y(t)-Y^{h}(t)|\leq Fh^{2H-1-\rho}

for a random variable
F
independent of
h
and satisfying
\mathbf{E}(F^{p})<\infty
for all
p\geq 1
.

Proof:
From Lemma
1
and (
26
) we deduce

\displaystyle|Y^{h}(s)-Y^{h}(u)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{u}^{s}|g(Y^{h}(t_{v}))|dv+\sigma\left|\int_{u}^{s}dB(v)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L_{g}(s-u)+\sigma|B(s)-B(u)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L_{g}S^{1-H+\rho}(s-u)^{H-\rho}+\sigma F_{B}(s-u)^{H-\rho}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle F_{Y^{h}}(s-u)^{H-\rho},

where
F_{Y^{h}}=L_{g}S^{1-H+\rho}+\sigma F_{B}
and hence
\mathbf{E}(F_{Y^{h}}^{p})<\infty
for all
p\geq 1
.
For the other claim, we follow the proof of Theorem 3.1 in
[
14
]
. Indeed, by noting that in
[
14
]
the arguments hold almost surely, and hence can be adapted for the random times
t_{k}
.
Hence a priori bound (
34
) leads to

\sup_{0\leq t\leq S}|Y(t)-Y^{h}(t)|\leq Fh^{2H-1-\rho},

where
F
is a random variable such that
\mathbf{E}(F^{p})<\infty
for all
p\geq 1
and
F
does not depend on
h
. This completes the proof.

Proof of Theorem
4
(i):
We define the globally Lipschitz and bounded function
\bar{g}
:

(35)

\bar{g}(x)=\begin{cases}g(x)&\quad\text{if}\quad|x|\leq 2M,\\
g(2M)&\quad\text{if}\quad x\geq 2M,\\
g(-2M)&\quad\text{if}\quad x\leq-2M.\end{cases}

From Theorem
1
, and
S>0
, there exists a unique solution
Z
, of the equation

(36)

dZ(t)=\bar{g}(Z(t))dt+\sigma dB(t),\quad\quad Z(0)=y(0),\ t\in[0,S],

and this solution
Z
satisfies, for any
0<\rho<H
and almost surely,

(37)

\sup_{0\leq s\leq t\leq S}|Z(t)-Z(s)|<F_{Z}|t-s|^{H-\rho},

where
F_{Z}=F_{Z}(\omega)
satisfies
\mathbf{E}(F_{Z}^{p})<\infty
for all
p\geq 1.

Let
Z^{h}
now be the approximation given by

(38)

{Z}^{h}(t_{n+1})={Z}^{h}{(t_{n})}+\bar{g}({Z}^{h}(t_{n}))\tau_{n}+[B(t_{n+1})-B(t_{n})],\quad\quad{Z}(0)=y(0).

As
g(x)=\bar{g}(x)
for
|x|\leq 2M
and
T^{h}_{S}=T^{Z}_{M}\wedge T_{2M}^{Z^{h}}\wedge S
, we have
Y(t)=Z(t)
and
{Y}^{h}(t)={Z}^{h}(t)
for
t\in[0,T_{S}^{h}]
, and thus

\mathbf{E}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{S}^{h}}|Y(t)-Y^{h}(t)|^{p}\right)=\mathbf{E}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T_{S}^{h}}|Z(t)-Z^{h}(t))|^{p}\right)\leq\mathbf{E}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq S}|Z(t)-Z^{h}(t)|^{p}\right)

from which the claim followed by Lemma
3
with
\rho<2H-1
applied to the process
Z
and its approximation
Z^{h}
.

The rest of the proof of Theorem
4
is based on the following lemma: In contrast to the Brownian motion, we do not have martingale property at our disposal;
instead, we rely on the law of the iterated logarithm for fBm.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\{t_{k}\}
be defined by
t_{0}=0
,
t_{k+1}=t_{k}+\tau_{k}
, where
\tau_{k}
is given by

\tau_{k}=\frac{h}{g(Y^{h}(t_{k}))}
(see (
25
)). Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{B({t_{k}})}{k}=0\quad\text{a.s.}

Proof:
Let us consider

\Omega=\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\quad[\{\sup_{l\in\mathbb{N}}t_{l}\}\leq k]\quad\bigcup\quad[\{\sup_{l\in\mathbb{N}}t_{l}\}=\infty].

Let us first consider the case
\omega\in\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\quad[\{\sup_{l\in\mathbb{N}}t_{l}^{n}(\cdot)\}\leq k]
(corresponding to the case of the explosion), which is easy to handle. Indeed, in this case, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{|B({t_{l}(\omega)},\omega)|}{l}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C(\omega,k)k^{H-\delta}}{l}

where
C(\omega,k)
depends only on
k
,
\omega
and
\delta<H
.
Consequently,

\lim_{l\to\infty}\frac{|B({t_{l}(\omega)},\omega)|}{l}=0.

Suppose now the case that
\omega\in\left[\{\displaystyle\sup_{l\in\mathbb{N}}t_{l}\}=\infty\right]
. We recall that for
H\in(0,1)
the fBm satisfies the following law of the iterated logarithm (see Lemma 4.1 in
[
12
]
).

(40)

\mathop{\overline{\rm lim}}_{t\to\infty}\frac{B(t)}{\psi_{H}(t)}=1\quad\text{a.s.},

where we use the notation

(41)

\psi_{H}(t):=t^{H}\sqrt{2\log\log t},\quad t>e^{1}.

Since the sequence
\{t_{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
is increasing to infinity, then from (
40
), there exists
\tilde{l}>0
such that for all
l>\tilde{l}

(42)

\frac{B({t_{l}(\omega)},\omega)}{t_{l}^{H}(\omega)\sqrt{2\log\log t_{l}(\omega)}}\leq 1+\epsilon<2

almost surely. Moreover, for large enough
l
(depending on
\omega
), we also have
\log(t_{l}(\omega))>1
. This, together with the fact that
-B
is also a fBm, gives

(43)

\displaystyle\frac{|B({t_{l}(\omega)},\omega)|}{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|B({t_{l}(\omega)},\omega)|}{t_{l}^{H}(\omega)\sqrt{2\log\log t_{l}(\omega)}}\times\frac{t_{l}^{H}(\omega)\sqrt{2\log\log t_{l}(\omega)}}{l}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{t_{l}^{H}(\omega)\sqrt{2\log\log t_{l}(\omega)}}{l}.

From the definition (
25
) and hypothesis (
23
),
t_{l}(\omega)\leq C_{1}l
for some constant
C_{1}>h/l_{g}
independent of
l
