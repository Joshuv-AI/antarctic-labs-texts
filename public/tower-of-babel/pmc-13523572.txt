Feasibility, safety, and efficacy of epicardial CO2 insufflation to facilitate epicardial access for VT ablation: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Salah H. Alahwany, Mohamed Sharief, Amir Abdelghany, Ahmed Elmorsy Mohamed, Mohab Elnashar, Ahmed Ammar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPercutaneous epicardial access (EA) is essential for catheter ablation of ventricular tachycardia (VT), yet conventional “dry” subxiphoid puncture remains technically challenging and carries substantial risks, particularly in patients with previous cardiac interventions or pericardial adhesions. Epicardial carbon dioxide insufflation (EpiCO2) has recently emerged as a novel strategy to enhance pericardial visualization and facilitate safer, more efficient EA.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, safety signals, and procedural performance of EpiCO2-assisted EA in adults undergoing VT ablation.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251162014) to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and procedural performance of EpiCO2-assisted EA in adults undergoing VT ablation. A comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane, and Embase identified 7 observational studies comprising 336 patients.ResultsEpiCO2-assisted EA demonstrated high feasibility, with a pooled procedural success rate of 94% (95% confidence interval 90–96). Mean carbon dioxide volume insufflated was 155.9 mL, EA time averaged 23.5 minutes, and fluoroscopy time averaged 47.0 minutes. Major complications were infrequent, including pericarditis (4%), bleeding of ≥80 mL (5%), and death (1%). Pericardial adhesions were observed in 7% of patients. Limited comparative meta-analysis revealed no significant differences in procedural success or total procedure time between EpiCO2-assisted and conventional EA technique.ConclusionEpiCO2-assisted EA is feasible with acceptable safety profile in observational VT cohorts. Well-designed prospective multicenter comparative studies are needed to define its potential advantages over conventional “dry” EA.

Introduction

Percutaneous epicardial access (EA) is a cornerstone in the management of ventricular tachycardia (VT), particularly when endocardial approaches fail and/or when the substrate is predominantly epicardial. The standard approach for EA has relied on subxiphoid puncture techniques using Tuohy or micropuncture needles, methods that have remained unchanged since their introduction.1

Traditionally, conventional “dry” subxiphoid EA into the virtual pericardial space has been technically challenging and has been associated with overall complication rates ranging from 1% to 17%.2,3 Inadvertent right ventricular (RV) puncture represents the most common complication of traditional EA approaches. Other complications include cardiac tamponade, hepatic injury, and coronary artery damage, especially in patients with pericardial adhesions or previous cardiac interventions.4, 5, 6

Epicardial carbon dioxide insufflation (EpiCO2) is a novel technique to facilitate EA. Preliminary studies suggest that carbon dioxide (CO2) insufflation may enhance procedural feasibility, reduce complications, and improve overall ablation outcomes. EpiCO2 has emerged as a promising adjunctive technique in this domain, offering improved visualization of the pericardial space and the potential to reduce procedural complications.7, 8, 9, 10, 11

This technique, initially explored through intentional right atrial exit into the pericardial space, demonstrated that CO2 insufflation can safely separate the visceral and parietal pericardial layers, thereby facilitating subxiphoid needle entry and reducing the risk of myocardial injury.12 Building on this foundation, the EpiCO2 registry provided multicenter evidence that intentional coronary vein exit followed by CO2 insufflation is both reproducible and safe, with a 99% success rate and minimal complications.13

More recently, Chaumont et al14 conducted the first systematic single-center analysis of EpiCO2 specifically for the identification of pericardial adhesions during EA. Their findings revealed that CO2 not only enhances visualization of cardiac borders but also allows for the strategic targeting of safe zones for epicardial puncture, sheath manipulation, and mapping. Notably, the technique enabled avoidance of high-risk adhesion areas, with potential implications for procedural safety and efficiency.

Although early data are promising, the safety, efficacy, and procedural benefits of EpiCO2-assisted EA have not been fully established. To address gaps in the evidence, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate whether EpiCO2 improves EA success rates, reduces major complications, and enhances procedural efficiency compared with conventional techniques. This study aimed to provide a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence to inform clinical practice and guide future research.

Methods

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses statement. The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251162014) on October 12, 2025, and no major protocol deviations occurred after International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews registration. The research question was as follows: in adult patients undergoing catheter ablation of VT requiring EA (P), does CO2 insufflation to facilitate pericardial access (I) compared with conventional EA techniques (C) improve feasibility, safety, and procedural efficacy (O)?

Data source and search strategy

We searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane, and Embase using “(epicardial OR pericardial OR subxiphoid) AND (“carbon dioxide” OR “CO2” OR “intentional coronary vein exit” OR “EpiCO2”) AND (“ventricular” OR “VT”)” as keywords. We removed duplicates via EndNote x8® before extracting results into a Microsoft Excel® sheets for manual screening of eligibility. This systematic review and meta-analysis used data from previously published studies; therefore, institutional review board approval and patient consent were waived.

2 investigators checked eligible studies and performed quality assessment using the study quality assessment tool from the National Institutes of Health.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) enrolled patients who were undergoing VT ablation and CO2 insufflation for facilitation of EA, (2) reported on at least 1 of the primary outcomes, (3) included at least 5 patients, and (4) patients were older than 18 years. We excluded studies that (1) did not report clinical outcomes, (2) were non-English studies, and (3) were conference abstracts.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Authors independently extracted summary data of the included studies and baseline demographics of the patients into a prespecified Microsoft Excel sheets. Disagreements were resolved in a panel discussion with the senior author.

Outcomes

The outcomes of interest were successful EA, total EA time (in minutes), total procedure time (in minutes), fluoroscopy time (in minutes), and complications including major complications, significant bleeding, and pericardial adhesions. Definitions of study outcomes, including procedural success, major complications, bleeding thresholds, and the timing of complication assessment, were recorded as reported by the original studies and are presented in Supplemental Table 1.

Statistical analysis

For single-arm binary outcomes, pooled proportions with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. For single-arm continuous outcomes, pooled mean raw values with 95% CIs were calculated. For comparative binary outcomes, effect sizes were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs, whereas comparative continuous outcomes were expressed as mean differences with 95% CIs. Random-effects models were used throughout. The Mantel-Haenszel method was used for comparative binary outcomes, whereas the generic inverse-variance method was used for continuous outcomes. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and categorized as low, moderate, or high at thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75%, respectively; τ2 was also reported where appropriate. For single-arm analyses, pooled estimates were presented with 95% CIs and heterogeneity measures, and overall-effect P values were not emphasized because testing against a null value of zero is not clinically informative for outcomes such as procedure time or event incidence. By contrast, overall-effect P values were retained for comparative meta-analyses, where they reflect between-group differences. Prediction intervals were additionally reported for key single-arm continuous outcomes with substantial heterogeneity. Prespecified subgroup analysis according to CO2 delivery/EA technique was not performed because of the small number of studies within each procedural variant and the limited availability of technique-specific outcome data. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2.

Procedural data

Single-arm meta-analysis showed that the mean volume of CO2 insufflated was 155.9 mL (95% CI 129.22–182.56; I2 = 97.7%) (Figure 2A). The pooled mean total procedure time was 318.74 minutes (95% CI 273.31–364.17; I2 = 93.4%) (Figure 2B). The pooled mean fluoroscopy time was 47.04 minutes (95% CI 43.29–50.78; I2 = 11.1%) (Figure 2C). The pooled mean EA time was 23.49 minutes (95% CI 13.77–33.21; I2 = 98.5%) (Figure 2D). The pooled incidence of pericardial adhesions was 7% (95% CI 3–14; I2 = 30.1%) (Figure 2E). Efficacy and safety outcomes

Single-arm meta-analysis showed a pooled procedural success rate of 94% (95% CI 90–96; I2 = 0.0%) (Figure 3A). With respect to complications, the pooled incidence of pericarditis was 4% (95% CI 2–11; I2 = 0.0%) (Figure 3B), whereas the pooled incidence of bleeding of ≥80 mL was 5% (95% CI 3–8; I2 = 0.0%) (Figure 3C). There was 1 case each of pericardial tamponade, vascular complication, and pulseless electrical activity. The pooled incidence of death was 1% (95% CI 0–5; I2 = 13.3%) (Figure 3D). Comparative meta-analysis

Comparative quantitative synthesis was limited by sparse arm-level reporting across comparative studies. Pooled comparative meta-analysis was feasible only for procedural success and total procedure time. No statistically significant difference was observed between EpiCO2-assisted and conventional EA for procedural success (pooled OR 0.55; 95% CI 0.16–1.85; I2 = 0.0%; P = .3384) or total procedure time (pooled mean difference −15.83 minutes; 95% CI −52.56 to 20.90; I2 = 68.5%; P = .3983) (Figure 4A and 4B). Given that comparative pooling was possible for only 2 outcomes and that most of the available evidence is derived from single-arm studies, these findings should be interpreted as observational and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive evidence of comparative effectiveness.

Publication bias

Publication bias was not formally assessed using funnel plots or Egger’s test because fewer than 10 studies were included, making such analyses unreliable.

Discussion

Percutaneous EA is essential for managing VT but conventional “dry” subxiphoid techniques carry significant risks, including RV puncture, tamponade, and extracardiac injury, particularly in patients with adhesions or previous interventions. EpiCO2 has emerged as a promising adjunctive technique that enhances pericardial visualization and may improve feasibility while potentially reducing complications. Because the included studies were observational and predominantly single-arm, our meta-analysis primarily synthesizes feasibility and safety signals rather than providing definitive evidence of comparative effectiveness.

Recently, a large systematic review and meta-analysis comparing EpiCO2-assisted EA with conventional EA across multiple indications demonstrated significantly lower rates of inadvertent ventricular puncture and complications requiring surgical intervention with the CO2 technique. That analysis included 119 studies and more than 8700 procedures, encompassing a heterogeneous population with different arrhythmia substrates and EA indications.17 A focused synthesis restricted to VT ablation remains clinically valuable to provide VT-specific feasibility/safety signals and procedural performance metrics that are inconsistently reported in broader, mixed-indication analyses.

In contrast, the present systematic review and meta-analysis focuses specifically on contemporary cohorts of patients undergoing VT ablation, a population characterized by higher structural heart disease burden, previous cardiac interventions, and increased risk of pericardial adhesions. By restricting the analysis to VT-specific studies, our meta-analysis provides a more homogeneous and clinically targeted assessment of the feasibility, safety, and procedural performance of EpiCO2-assisted EA in the setting where epicardial ablation is most commonly required. In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 7 observational studies encompassing 336 patients, EpiCO2-assisted EA demonstrated high feasibility and a favorable safety profile.13,16,18,19 Across observational cohorts, EpiCO2-assisted EA consistently demonstrated high procedural feasibility, high success rates, and low rates of major complications, supporting its potential utility as an adjunct technique, with feasibility and safety signals that warrant confirmation in robust comparative studies. Pooled single-arm estimates showed a procedural success rate of 94%, a mean EA time of approximately 23.5 minutes, and a fluoroscopy time near 47 minutes. Major complications were rare; significant pericardial bleeding (≥80 mL) occurred in 5%, pericarditis in 4%, and death in 1%. Pericardial adhesions were observed in approximately 7%.

Feasibility and procedural efficiency

The consistently high success rates observed across diverse centers indicate that EpiCO2-assisted EA is reproducible in real-world practice. By creating a controlled pneumopericardium, CO2 separates the visceral and parietal pericardium, converting a virtual space into a visible, navigable cavity. This visualization facilitates safer subxiphoid puncture, sheath manipulation, and mapping, particularly in patients with challenging anatomy or previous cardiac interventions. In pooled data, EA times were acceptable (approximately 23.5 minutes), and comparative analyses showed no significant prolongation of total procedure duration with CO2, suggesting that the additional steps for venous exit and insufflation do not adversely affect overall efficiency.

To contextualize these findings, conventional (“dry”) EA remains technically variable and is influenced by operator experience, anatomic difficulty, and previous pericardial interventions or adhesions. In recent comparative cohorts, conventional dry EA times have generally been reported in the range of approximately 15–20 minutes, whereas total procedure duration for complex VT ablation has often been in the 300–400 minute range, particularly in patients with structural heart disease undergoing extensive substrate mapping.15,16 When benchmarked against these established ranges, the pooled EpiCO2-assisted EA time (23.5 minutes) and total procedure time (318.7 minutes) observed in our meta-analysis fall within expected values for complex epicardial VT ablation. In addition, in the 2 comparative cohorts included in our review, total procedure duration did not differ significantly between EpiCO2-assisted and conventional EA despite the additional procedural steps associated with venous exit and insufflation. These findings suggest that CO2 implementation does not seem to introduce substantial procedural inefficiency in complex VT populations.

Safety profile and complications

EA procedures can be associated with serious complications. The reported complication rate with traditional EA varies from 1% to 17.5% including RV puncture, tamponade, and extracardiac injury.2,3 In contrast, pooled contemporary experience with EpiCO2-assisted EA shows low rates of major complications; significant bleeding and pericarditis were infrequent, and procedural mortality was low. The physical properties of CO2 (rapid absorption and clear fluoroscopic delineation) likely contribute to safer targeting. Notably, there were no cases of surgical intervention or hematoma causing extrinsic RV compression in the included series. The multicenter EpiCO2 registry (n = 102) reported 99% success with intentional coronary venous exit, no inadvertent RV punctures or coronary injuries, and a 4.9% rate of significant bleeding, none requiring surgery. These findings mirror our pooled safety estimates and reinforce reproducibility across varied centers.13

Although the pooled mortality rate in our analysis was 1%, none of the included studies attributed these deaths to the EpiCO2-assisted EA technique. Instead, the limited mortality reported occurred in the context of complex VT ablation among patients with advanced structural heart disease, a population in whom periprocedural mortality is known to reflect overall clinical risk rather than EA-related complications. No deaths were linked to CO2 insufflation, venous exit, or pericardial instrumentation in any cohort. Accordingly, the pooled mortality signal should be interpreted as reflective of underlying disease severity and procedural complexity, rather than as an indicator of EA-related harm.

Comparative quantitative synthesis for additional safety outcomes in this meta-analysis was not feasible because comparator-arm event data were sparsely and inconsistently reported across the available studies. Although procedural success and total procedure time could be pooled, other clinically important endpoints, including major complications, bleeding, tamponade, and related EA site adverse events, were not provided in sufficiently detailed arm-level format across at least 2 comparative cohorts. As a result, robust pooled comparative estimates for these outcomes could not be generated, and the available evidence is best interpreted descriptively. The pooled mortality estimate should be interpreted cautiously given that the included studies enrolled patients undergoing VT ablation with substantial underlying structural heart disease burden, and the limited reported deaths were not clearly attributable to EpiCO2-assisted EA itself. Accordingly, this signal likely reflects overall postablation risk in a complex population rather than EA-related mortality

Role in patients with pericardial adhesions

Pericardial adhesions were observed in approximately 7% of patients in the pooled analysis, consistent with rates expected in contemporary VT ablation cohorts. Evidence from included studies shows that EpiCO2-assisted EA improves visualization of adhesion planes and reliably identifies regions unsafe for puncture or manipulation. Importantly, in cohorts where adhesions were delineated before subxiphoid entry, no EA-related complications occurred, suggesting that prepuncture adhesion assessment may mitigate risk.15,20

Comparison with conventional EA

Comparative pooling was feasible only for procedural success and total procedure time, and no significant differences were observed between EpiCO2-assisted and conventional EA. Although the available data are limited and observational, the similar success rates and procedure durations—together with a favorable safety profile—are consistent with the limited comparative evidence to date. However, the current evidence base remains insufficient to establish noninferiority or superiority. A high-volume, comparative cohort from the University of Pennsylvania (n = 153) showed similar EA success between EpiCO2-assisted and conventional techniques, but major complications occurred only in the conventional group (7.5% vs 0%), and bleeding of ≥80 mL was significantly reduced with EpiCO2-assisted EA (adjusted OR after accounting for age, repeat access, and antithrombotic agents). Total procedure duration did not differ, despite a longer EA interval with EpiCO2.18

Midvolume center experience from Pisa (n = 22) confirmed feasibility, reported no major complications, and showed that EpiCO2-assisted EA adoption more than doubled epicardial approach utilization without prolonging overall procedure time.16 Single-center learning-curve series (Australia) showed consistent success with decreasing access times over cases and identified preventable pitfalls such as microcatheter retraction and inadvertent CO2 delivery into the RV, events that did not translate into clinical instability.19,21

Periprocedural antithrombotic strategy and bleeding risk

Bleeding mechanisms differ between EpiCO2-assisted and conventional dry EA. With EpiCO2, bleeding relates to venous microperforation rather than myocardial injury; observational data suggest that distal venous exit and timing insufflation before heparinization minimize bleeding,13 and comparative cohorts show lower bleeding rates with EpiCO2-assisted than conventional EA.18 Heparin administered periprocedurally did not abrogate the safety profile of distal venous perforation, and most bleeding resolved with percutaneous drainage without transfusion or surgery.13,18 Antiplatelet therapy was associated with pericardial bleeding, whereas higher BMI was associated with reduced bleeding potentially reflecting hemostatic effects of epicardial fat.14,15

Taken together, these findings support holding antiplatelet therapy when feasible, favoring distal rather than proximal venous perforation, and using cautious periprocedural heparinization with protamine reversal when appropriate, particularly in scenarios requiring transseptal or retrograde aortic access. With right atrial appendage microperforation approaches, attention to hemostasis may be even more important given higher flow dynamics.22

Mechanistic and procedural considerations

The procedural benefits of EpiCO2-assisted EA likely relate to its ability to create a controlled pneumopericardium, separating the visceral and parietal layers and improving fluoroscopic definition of the pericardial space. These mechanistic insights complement, but do not replace, the limited comparative data available. Emerging refinements such as distal coronary venous exit and radiofrequency-assisted right atrial appendage microperforation may further streamline access; however, these techniques remain investigational and require broader validation before definitive conclusions can be drawn.21,22

Limitations

This meta-analysis is limited by the exclusively observational nature and small sample sizes of the included studies, introducing risks of selection bias and residual confounding. Comparative data were sparse, allowing head-to-head pooling only for procedural success and total procedure time, whereas other clinically relevant outcomes were inconsistently reported. Importantly, confounding is likely substantial across the included cohorts. EpiCO2-assisted EA was often introduced in specialized or higher-volume centers, and outcomes may have been influenced by operator expertise, procedural learning curve, case selection, EA history, suspected adhesions, antithrombotic strategy, and center-specific workflow. In addition, several studies were single-arm series without concurrent controls or multivariable adjustment, limiting causal inference. Heterogeneity in procedural techniques, CO2 dosing, and outcome definitions, including variable bleeding thresholds, further contributed to clinical and statistical variability.

Another important limitation is procedural heterogeneity within the pooled EpiCO2-assisted EA literature, given that included studies used different CO2 delivery routes and access refinements that may carry different complexity and risk profiles. Because of the small number of studies and sparse reporting within each technique, stratified analysis by procedural variant was not feasible. Finally, the small number of studies prevented robust assessment of publication bias. Therefore, single-arm pooled estimates cannot establish comparative effectiveness.

Future directions

Prospective multicenter comparative studies, ideally randomized where feasible, are needed to better define differences between EpiCO2-assisted and conventional EA with respect to major complications, EA time, radiation exposure, and long-term arrhythmic outcomes. Standardized protocols for CO2 volume and delivery rate, procedural monitoring, and periprocedural antithrombotic management would improve consistency across studies and may help optimize safety. Future registries should also capture learning-curve metrics, quality-of-life outcomes, and resource utilization to better inform implementation in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes evidence on EpiCO2 for VT ablation requiring EA while highlighting important limitations of the current evidence base. EpiCO2-assisted EA seems feasible and has shown acceptable safety signals in published observational VT cohorts. However, the absence of robust comparative and randomized data limits definitive conclusions regarding its benefit relative to conventional techniques. Prospective multicenter comparative studies with standardized outcome definitions and comprehensive safety reporting are needed to clarify the incremental role of EpiCO2-assisted EA, identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit, and define its place in clinical practice.
