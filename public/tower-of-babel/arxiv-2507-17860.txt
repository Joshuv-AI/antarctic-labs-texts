Towards Facilitated Fairness Assessment of AI-based Skin Lesion Classifiers Through GenAI-based Image Synthesis
Ko Watanabe, Stanislav Frolov, Adriano Lucieri, Andreas Dengel

Abstract
Recent advancements in Deep Learning and its application on the edge hold great potential for the revolution of routine screenings for skin cancers like Melanoma. Along with the anticipated benefits of this technology, potential dangers arise from unforseen and inherent biases. Thus, assessing and improving the fairness of such systems is of utmost importance. A key challenge in fairness assessment is to ensure that the evaluation dataset is sufficiently representative of different Personal Identifiable Information (PII) (sex, age, and race) and other minority groups. Against the backdrop of this challenge, this study leverages the state-of-the-art Generative AI (GenAI) LightningDiT model to assess the fairness of publicly available melanoma classifiers. The results suggest that fairness assessment using highly realistic synthetic data is a promising direction. Yet, our findings indicate that verifying fairness becomes difficult when the melanoma-detection model used for evaluation is trained on data that differ from the dataset underpinning the synthetic images. Nonetheless, we propose that our approach offers a valuable new avenue for employing synthetic data to gauge and enhance fairness in medical-imaging Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems.

Fairness in Skin Lesion Classification

Fairness evaluation in skin lesion classification has been conducted in various ways.
Ansari et al.
[
2
]
proposed a novel data augmentation method to mitigate the class imbalance and skin tone bias. The approach utilized a method to generate synthetic images by combining two different images. One with the selected skin tone image and the other with the diagnosis image. Whereas BiaslessNAS
[
37

focuses on improving fairness by incorporating fairness considerations at every stage of the NAS process. The approach leads to identifying neural networks that are more accurate and fairer. Compared to traditional methods, a 2.55% increase in accuracy and a 65.50% improvement in fairness are achieved.

Compared to existing data augmentation methods, our method can generate synthetic images with various

3.1
Synthetic Test Data Generation

To generate synthetic test data, we first need a generative model.
To that end, we start by training a
SOTA
latent diffusion transformer using the recent LightningDiT
[
42
]
backbone
2
2

2

https://github.com/hustvl/LightningDiT
.
LightningDiT improves both image reconstruction and generation performance by leveraging pre-trained vision foundation models to align the underlying variational autoencoder (VAE).
Specifically, the vision foundation-model-aligned VAE (VA-VAE) ensures that the latent representation of images aligns with pre-trained vision foundation models, such as DINOv2
[
27
]
and MAE
[
16
]
.

We begin by extracting the latent representation of real ISIC images using the VA-VAE on all splits (2016, 2017, and 2018-2024), which collectively comprise approximately 500,000 images.
We use an image resolution of 256 and pair each image with a corresponding text description using the available annotations.
Specifically, we concatenate the labels corresponding to sex, age, size, skin type, and diagnosis into a single text description.
We use CLIP
[
29
]
to produce the text embeddings.
Next, we train the latent diffusion transformer with 675M parameters using standard LightningDiT settings for 80,000 steps, with a global batch size of 1024 and mixed precision on 8 A100 GPUs, employing AdamW
[
23
]
and a learning rate of 0.0002.
This process takes approximately 8 hours.

Finally, we can generate synthetic test data using the trained model by sampling random noise and conditioning it on text prompts, utilizing the targeted labels for fairness evaluation.
We sample images using the standard settings with the Euler solver for 250 steps and a classifier-free guidance scale of 10.

3.2
Fairness Evaluation Against Melanoma Detection Model

As shown in
Figure 1
, we generate synthetic images through prompting.
For the sake of fairness testing, we generate 100 images per
PII
attributes.
Patterns were broken down by sex (2 types), age (8 types), skin type (7 types), and disease case (1 type).
In total, we generate 11,200 (
2\times 8\times 7\times 1\times 100
) images for testing.

To evaluate the fairness of the pre-trained model, we select Demographic Disparity (DP).

DP
also called as
Accuracy Parity (AP)
is the difference in accuracy between different subgroups.

DP
specifically focuses on
AP
between different demographic (
PII
) groups.
Using balanced
PII
considered synthetic test images, we evaluate the
DP
against pretrained models.

In this study, we select three pre-trained models, DeepGuide
[
26
]
, MelaNet
[
43
]
, and SkinLesionDensenet
[
28
]
.
As mentioned earlier, we select these models due to the availability of the pretrained melanoma detection models.
The most significant difference among these three models is that MelaNet and SkinLesionDensenet are both trained on
ISIC
, but DeepGuide was trained using the
HAM
dataset.
Additionally, the model architecture is based on DenseNet for DeepGuide and SkinLesionDenseNet, but MelaNet is trained based on VGG-GAP.

5
Limitation and Future Work

In this paper, we verify two research questions (R1–R2) introduced in
section 1
. We verify several points from this study. For R1, the synthetic image test data generation performed well using LightningDiT. We use the ISIC as a training dataset and also apply it to other images, such as
HAM
and Derm7pt. Hence, our next step for improving the synthetic image generation model is to consider different datasets or different hardware for capturing images. This approach enables fairness and robustness testing through the generation of synthetic images in dermatology. For R2, we verified that synthetic image test data generation can serve as a tool for evaluating the fairness of pre-trained models.
For instance, examining the performance of pre-trained models, such as SkinLesionDensenet, has confirmed the prediction of Melanoma. Next step is to apply concept-based fairness evaluation to understand the actual cause of the fairness issue.

6
Conclusion

In this study, we use LightningDiT for synthetic melanoma test image generation.
We verified that synthetic image can serve as test data for evaluating the fairness of pre-trained models.
We also found that performance declines modestly when the classifier and the generator are trained on disjoint datasets, underscoring the impact of dataset shift.
This means that a strictly privacy-preserving,
PII
-free fairness audit is most reliable when both the generator and the detector originate from the same data distribution.
Beyond fairness, the synthetic test set proved useful for stress-testing model robustness, suggesting that data-driven generation can serve as a general diagnostic tool for
AI
in medical imaging.
