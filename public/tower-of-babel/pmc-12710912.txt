Bacteriophages as vaccine platforms: Opportunities and challenges in translation
Authors: Wangxue Chen, Danielle L Peters, Serena Lam, Maryam Kettal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBacteriophages (phages) have recently received increased interest as versatile candidates for vaccine development. Their inherent characteristics, such as ease of genetic manipulation, high-density antigen display, intrinsic immunostimulatory properties, demonstrated human safety, and scalability in bacterial hosts, make them attractive as next-generation vaccine platforms. Additionally, their cost-effective production, stability, and existing regulatory approval for food and compassionate phage therapy provide a strong foundation for further development of phage-based vaccines. This commentary summarizes the types of phages, the strategies used, and current advances in phage-based vaccine development for viral and bacterial targets, and discusses the promises and challenges of this platform for novel vaccine development. Phage-based vaccines represent an innovative and promising platform for vaccine development to address significant medical and public health challenges, particularly in antimicrobial resistance, pandemic preparedness, and One Health. Accumulative experimental data have demonstrated that phage-based vaccines induce specific cellular, humoral, and mucosal immune responses at magnitudes comparable to those induced by other vaccine platforms. However, a better understanding of phage biology (interactions with the human immune system and microbiome), more carefully designed preclinical studies, Good Manufacturing Practice production development, the regulatory framework, and ultimately clinical trials are needed before the full potential of this platform is realized.KEYWORDS: Vaccine development, bacteriophage, phage vaccines, pandemic responses, One Health

Introduction

Vaccination is among the most effective public health tools for preventing infectious diseases and reducing related morbidity and mortality. It has delivered substantial health, economic, and social benefits worldwide. However, ongoing research and development of innovative vaccine platforms are urgently needed to address the rising threat of emerging and reemerging infections, as well as the rapid escalation of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Multiple vaccine platforms are currently available for the development of vaccines against infectious diseases, including live attenuated, inactivated, recombinant protein, virus-like particle, virus-vectored (replicating and non-replicating), and nucleic acid-based (mRNA and DNA) vaccines. The unprecedented success of the mRNA platform in enabling the rapid development of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines demonstrates the transformative potential of novel vaccine technologies in rapid responses to pandemics and other public health emergencies.

Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses that specifically infect bacteria and display diverse morphologies, genomic compositions, and life cycles. They are broadly classified into lytic phages, which rapidly replicate and lyse their bacterial hosts, and temperate phages, which can integrate into bacterial genomes and persist as prophages.1 Structurally, many phages possess an icosahedral capsid enclosing a DNA or RNA genome, with some featuring contractile or flexible tails that mediate host recognition. Beyond their ecological role in controlling bacterial populations, phages have been utilized in various biomedical applications: as antibacterial agents in phage therapy and food safety, as tools in phage display for epitope mapping and antibody discovery, and as vectors for gene delivery.2 Their unique properties, such as high host specificity, genetic plasticity, and ease of production in bacterial systems, position phages as potent and versatile tools in biotechnology and biomedicine.

This commentary summarizes the major bacteriophage types used in vaccines, highlights current strategies and advances in phage-based vaccine design, reviews their applications against viral and bacterial pathogens, and discusses both the promises and challenges of this technology in developing novel vaccines for emerging infections, pandemics, and One Health initiatives.

Bacteriophages and phage-based vaccine platforms

Pioneered in the late 1980 s by the work of de la Cruz and colleagues,3 phage-based vaccine platforms engineer phages to display antigens of interest on their surface or within their structure. Current strategies in developing phage-based vaccines generally fall into three approaches: phage display vaccines, phage DNA vaccines, and hybrid phage-DNA vaccines (Figure 1).4 Phage display vaccines use phage surface (capsid) proteins to display peptides or proteins of interest to elicit specific immune responses.5 This is achieved by inserting the gene encoding the target peptide into the phage capsid gene. Filamentous phages, such as M13, are most commonly used in phage display vaccines, though T4, lambda and Qβ are also frequently used.

Phage DNA vaccines encode an antigen of interest under the control of a eukaryotic promoter within the phage genome, utilizing the phages as vectors to deliver the DNA to the host’s immune system.6 The phage is then taken up by antigen-presenting cells, and the encoded antigen is displayed to elicit an adaptive immune response. Phages can target specific tissues or cells and enhance the delivery and presentation of DNA vaccines. Phages can also act as natural adjuvants, boosting the overall effectiveness of the DNA vaccine.7

Hybrid phage-DNA vaccines combine the advantages of both phage display technology and DNA vaccines to create more effective immunization strategies.6 In this scenario, the phage display and DNA vaccine can express and encode the same antigen epitopes. Alternatively, the hybrid approach can target multiple epitopes of the antigen (multivalent) to induce broader immune responses. In this regard, a hybrid phage-DNA vaccine against SARS-CoV-2 was developed recently using a T4 platform, which was shown to be highly effective in both a mouse and rabbit model of infection.8 The vaccine is self-adjuvanted and induced both T helper 1 (Th1) and Th2 IgG subclass responses, blocked virus-receptor interactions, neutralized viral infection, and conferred complete protection against viral challenge.

Among these approaches, phage display vaccines are the most commonly investigated, partially due to the relative maturation of phage display technology for studying protein interactions and antibody discovery. In addition to phage engineering, phage display can also be accomplished by chemically conjugating the antigen to the surface of phage particles or phage virus-like particles (PLPs), enabling the presentation of target molecules via diverse chemical methods.4

Phage-based vaccines are being explored for a variety of infectious diseases, including bacterial (AMR pathogens), viral (influenza and SARS-CoV-2), and parasitic infections (malaria). This technology has also been investigated for developing personalized cancer vaccines targeting unique mutations in a patient’s tumor.4,9 Phage-based vaccines can modulate the host immune system and induce immune tolerance for preventing and treating autoimmune diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis.10 The development of phage-based vaccines has received renewed interest and attention in recent years, particularly during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

Types of phages used in vaccine development

Several classes of phages have been exploited for vaccine development, each offering distinct biological properties and practical advantages that make them suitable for specific applications.4,6 These include lytic phages (such as T4, and T7), filamentous phages (M13, fd, and f1), and ssRNA (+) phages (MS2, Qβ, AP205). Lytic phages infect and rapidly lyse bacterial cells, releasing new phage particles. T4 and T7 phages, characterized by their large icosahedral capsids, provide a robust scaffold that can be engineered to display larger, or multiple, antigens of interest. Both phages have been engineered to present complex proteins on their capsids while maintaining structural stability, making them valuable platforms for vaccine development and antigen presentation. They are also suitable for delivering DNA vaccines while simultaneously acting as adjuvants, enhancing the subsequent immune response.11,12 Filamentous phages such as M13 have a long, thin, and flexible morphology. They are commonly used in phage display technology because they can present a diverse range of short antigen peptides and small proteins on their surface at high density.13,14 This makes them particularly well-suited for producing large and diverse libraries of phage particles. Lambda is a temperate phage that can integrate its DNA into the bacterial genome during the lysogenic cycle. This integrated phage DNA is replicated along with the host genome and can be stably inherited by daughter cells, and upon induction of the lytic cycle, produce phage progeny.12,13,15 The lambda phage can be engineered for phage display, or serve as a flexible vector to carry DNA constructs, enabling delivery of antigen-encoding sequences for in situ expression. This makes lambda a useful nucleic acid vaccine carrier where prolonged antigen expression is beneficial.

Among the different types of phages, T4, M13, lambda, and Qβ are the most commonly investigated for vaccine development. Whereas all the phage types listed above have been used in the development of viral vaccines, T4, MS2, Qβ, and M13 are the main phages used in the development of bacterial vaccines. Other phages (such as the f88-4 and fUSE55 phage vectors) have also been explored during the COVID-19 pandemic to dual-display spike protein epitopes on the major capsid protein pVIII of f88-4 and a pulmonary epithelial receptor ligand on the minor capsid protein pIII of fUSE55.16 The resulting chimeric phages are transported from the lung epithelium into the systemic circulation following aerosolization and induce a systemic antigen-specific antibody response.16 AP205 is an icosahedral ssRNA phage encoding a unique coat protein that is distinct from all other known ssRNA phages.17 It has been used to develop vaccines against several viral and bacterial infections, including influenza, SARS-CoV-2, West Nile Virus, and Salmonella. Using the SpyTag/SpyCatcher “superglue” system, a mutant peptide derived from human enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli heat-stable toxin (STh-A14T) was conjugated to AP205 coat proteins to induce cross-reactivity to the low immunogenicity porcine heat-stable toxin.18 Table 1 summarizes the key features, advantages, and limitations in vaccine development of different types of phages. Collectively, these phages represent a diverse toolkit and underpin phages’ adaptability across multiple vaccine strategies, from display-based subunit vaccines to genetic delivery systems.

Most phage-based vaccines studied to date have demonstrated the ability to induce robust and long-lasting antigen-specific T-helper (Th) 1 (IgG2a/2c) and Th2 (IgG1) antibody responses, including broad neutralizing antibodies.15,19,28,32,33,39,40 In many cases, they have also been shown to increase interferon (IFN)-γ and/or interleukin (IL)-4 production,41 as well as granzyme B production by cytotoxic T lymphocytes.37 Importantly, multiple studies have demonstrated that immunization with phage-based vaccines protects multiple animal species, including mice,38 rabbits,40 rats,40 and pigs,25,42 against various routes of pathogen challenge, reduces tissue pathogen burdens, and minimizes associated tissue damage.15,25,28,38,40 Additionally, when used for DNA vaccine delivery, phages have generally induced greater lymphocyte proliferation and cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocyte responses compared to DNA vaccines alone.25,26 However, the protection was not evaluated in all the studies, and many studies failed to include direct head-to-head comparisons with appropriate control vaccines, leaving the potential advantages of phage-based vaccines over other platforms unclear.12,13,34

The potential of T4 phages as a mucosal vaccine platform has recently been demonstrated for SARS-CoV-2 and other respiratory infections of epidemic and pandemic potential.12 Mucosal immunity is critical for vaccine-induced long-term protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection and for stopping viral transmission, both of which are significant challenges for currently licensed COVID-19 vaccines. T4 phage has been engineered using CRISPR-Cas genome editing to display influenza (M2e peptides and hemagglutinin stem regions) or SARS-CoV-2 (spike and nucleocapsid proteins) antigens on its outer capsid proteins (Hoc/Soc), enabling high-density multivalent antigen display (~1,000–1500 copies per phage). These phage particles also carry CpG-rich DNA internally, serving as a self-adjuvant through TLR9 activation. When administered intranasally without additional adjuvant, recombinant T4 phage particles induced robust systemic and mucosal (secretory IgA in nasal washes, saliva, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid) immune responses in mice and ferrets. The serum IgG titers in animals immunized with phage vaccines were comparable to or exceeding those from intramuscular alum-adjuvanted vaccines. The vaccines also elicited strong antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell activation in lungs and spleen, accompanied by a Th1-biased cytokine response (IFN-γ and IL-2). Upon challenge with live influenza or SARS-CoV-2 viruses (including the ancestral WA-1/2020 strain, a mouse-adapted SARS-CoV-2 strain, and the Delta variant), vaccinated animals exhibited complete protection, with no detectable viral replication in lungs or nasal passages and minimal lung inflammation or pathology.20 These findings indicate that the T4 phage can function as both scaffold and adjuvant, inducing sterilizing mucosal immunity via needle-free intranasal administration. However, the safety and durability of immunity after repeated mucosal vaccinations require further investigation. Similar strategies could be adapted for other pathogens, including RSV, MERS, and bacterial toxins.8 These studies also demonstrate that phages retain their infectivity and antigenicity after nebulization or lyophilization, supporting the potential for a broad application of this platform.

More recently, recombinant MS2 phage-derived virus-like particles (VLPs) were engineered to display the L9 epitope of Plasmodium falciparum circumsporozoite protein (CSP) in a constrained loop to mimic its native conformation.29 Mice immunized with these VLPs elicited high-titer, durable anti-CSP antibodies (up to 40–50 weeks with little decline). The vaccine, especially when adjuvanted or in combination with Qβ L9 vaccine, induced sterilizing immunity (no blood-stage infection) in a substantial fraction of vaccinated mice following mosquito-bite challenges. In head-to-head comparisons, the VLP constructs showed better protection than the licensed RTS,S vaccine in the mouse model. These results show that phage-derived VLPs can present constrained epitopes and induce long-duration functionally protective antibodies. A combination of distinct VLP scaffolds (different structural contexts) or inclusion of additional adjuvants can improve sterilizing protection.

The promise of phage-based platforms in vaccine development

While traditional vaccines have been our main armor against various infectious diseases for decades, novel vaccine platforms and technologies are urgently needed to meet the ever-evolving demands of our public health system, particularly in the aftermath of COVID-19 pandemic and the escalating threat of AMR. Phage-based vaccines offer a promising and complementary platform to address these public health challenges. Research efforts to develop phage-based vaccines have increased substantially since the COVID-19 pandemic.

Phage-based vaccine platforms have several unique advantages over other vaccine platforms. The high versatility of phages (natural nanostructures, genetically engineerable, high antigen density) allows easy and rapid engineering to display and deliver a wide range of antigens (protein, peptide, and DNA) individually or in a multiplex form (such as multiple antigen display, and protein-DNA hybrid delivery). The distinct structures of different phage types enable multivalent display of antigen peptides and proteins. Phages can be engineered to carry a wide variety of antigens, making them adaptable to different pathogens. The use of engineered phage PLPs and phage nanoparticles has recently emerged as particularly promising, representing an exciting and evolving area to explore.4 The genetic plasticity and molecular biology tools available for phage engineering enable control over the density of antigens displayed on phages, thereby tailoring the type of immune response that induces.13 In addition to individually tailored phage vaccines, phage-based vaccine platforms allow the rapid “plug and play” vaccine construction for rapid pandemic responses and stockpile of AMR vaccines.12,43

As with any vaccine, the optimal route of administration for phage-based vaccines is determined by several factors, including the pathogen and population targets, vaccine formulation, and safety. Phages have been administered to animals and humans via various systemic and mucosal routes; thus, the possibility of administering phages by multiple routes, including orally or via inhalation, is another advantage of phage-based vaccines. Mucosal immunization induces mucosal immunity, which is critical for preventing the transmission and clearance of mucosal pathogens and maintaining a long-term memory immune response.12 Needle-free mucosal, particularly oral immunization, significantly simplifies the vaccination process and increases vaccine acceptance.

Certain phages, such as T4, lambda, and M13, can stimulate innate host immune pathways by mimicking pathogen-associated molecular patterns and activating Toll-like receptors.44 The intrinsic adjuvanticity of phages enhances the immune response in vaccinated hosts without the need for additional adjuvants. The use of filamentous phages to deliver DNA is a novel and effective approach compared to the traditional DNA vaccine as it often takes advantage of their self-adjuvanticity and the ability for prolonged antigen expression. However, the extent of the pathogen-associated molecular patterns mimicry and its impact on immunogenicity vary across different phage types and are poorly understood. Further research is needed to fully understand and optimize their use as effective immunomodulators in vaccine development.

The robust stability of phages under various harsh environments and temperatures makes the storage and distribution of phage-based vaccines easier without the need for a cold chain. Their scalability and production in bacterial hosts are relatively cost-effective, which makes the access to phage-based vaccines affordable, especially in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).

Phages are generally considered safe as they specifically target bacteria and do not infect or replicate in human cells. They have been used extensively in animals and humans for various applications, including phage therapy to combat AMR and in the food industry for bacterial control.45,46 Recent controlled clinical trials of phage therapy further support their safety and efficacy, demonstrating minimal adverse effects in treated patients.47

Scientific and technical challenges

Compared to other vaccine platforms, phage-based vaccines remain in the early stages of development but are rapidly advancing. Despite the promising preclinical results and overall safety profiles in humans and animals, no licensed phage-based vaccines have been approved for human use. Several scientific, technical, regulatory and manufacturing challenges must be addressed before phage-based human vaccines become a reality. The construction of phage-based vaccines is likely to encounter some technical hurdles. These include the accurate display of antigens on phage capsids, with limitations related to the antigen size and folding constraints for complex proteins. For instance, T4 phages are well-suited for presenting large proteins on their non-essential Hoc and Soc capsid proteins without disrupting capsid assembly. In contrast, the M13 filamentous phage proves less advantageous for displaying large fusion proteins due to its requirement for bacterial secretion, which constrains the display size. Therefore, careful selection of the appropriate phage type is critical to optimize vaccine design. Recent advancements in phage display technology have significantly impacted vaccine development.48 The integration of synthetic biology has enabled the creation of highly diverse phage libraries displaying a vast array of potential vaccine candidates,49 but the limited ability to mimic conformational epitopes for antigen presentation remains a potential hurdle.

Our understanding of the interactions between phages and the human (mammalian) immune system remains very limited. Although preexisting native or acquired immune responses to phages, including neutralizing antibodies, have been documented, their effects on phage replication, persistence, and therapeutic efficacies remain unclear. Recent studies investigating the impact of these immune responses on phage therapy efficacy have produced mixed results.50,51 Therefore, further characterization of human immune responses to phages, especially with repeated dosing, is needed to clarify their influence on the immunogenicity and efficacy of phage-based vaccines. In addition, the risk of inducing anti-phage immunity due to different immunization variables (such as immunization route, dose, frequency, and phage types) should be systematically assessed and compared for developing optimal phage vaccines and vaccination strategies. Similarly, the potential benefit of using additional adjuvants in the phage-based vaccines should be further evaluated. Oral and other mucosal routes of vaccination have many advantages over the commonly used parenteral routes of immunization, but the potential impact of phages on the normal microbiome at the mucosal site will need to be studied and managed.

In vivo stability, biodistribution, and tissue targeting are other considerations in the delivery and formulation of phage-based vaccines. The use of phage nanoparticles in phage-based vaccine formulation offers significant advantages in enhancing vaccine stability, delivery, and targeting.8,40,42 Coupling phages with nanoparticles can improve vaccine efficacy through multiple mechanisms. Nanoparticles act as protective carriers, shielding phages from degradation and prolonging their circulation time in the body. They also enable targeted delivery to specific cells or tissues, increasing the efficiency of vaccine uptake.52,53 Additionally, nanoparticles can promote enhanced antigen presentation, resulting in a more robust immune response, and can serve as vehicles for adjuvants or other immune-stimulating molecules to further amplify vaccine effectiveness.54 The demonstrated success of lipid nanoparticles in mRNA vaccine delivery has stimulated substantial research, deepening our understanding of critical factors in nanoparticle-mediated delivery for various therapeutic cargos. These insights are expected to further optimize the efficacy of vaccines using nanoparticle platforms. Progress in human DNA vaccines has been hindered largely by delivery obstacles and insufficient induction of potent cell-mediated immunity. Integrating phage-based DNA or hybrid vaccines with nanoparticle delivery systems may help overcome these barriers and unlock new potential for this promising vaccine technology.

The development of phage-based vaccines presents unique GMP manufacturing hurdles and regulatory challenges. Key issues include attaining GMP-compliant production at scale with consistent quality, mitigating endotoxin contamination in the final product, and establishing effective purification processes. Therefore, robust GMP protocols and comprehensive product quality control measures at every stage of production are crucial to ensure that phage-based vaccines are consistently safe, potent, and of high quality. As an innovative platform, phage-based vaccines are expected to undergo significant regulatory scrutiny. Regulatory pathways and guidelines must be established to harmonize safety requirements, such as limiting residual bacterial components and ensuring phage genetic stability. In this regard, experience and best practices from GMP-compliant phage production for therapeutic applications, as well as regulatory approval processes for viral and viral vector vaccines, may serve as valuable references. Moreover, the safety record demonstrated by phage therapy and phage probiotics, including in immunocompromised individuals, may help support the safety profile of this platform. Close collaboration with relevant regulatory agencies will be vital to facilitate the licensing and approval of the first phage-based human vaccine.

Future directions

The future of phage-based vaccines is promising, with significant potential to improve how we prevent and treat a wide range of infectious diseases, driven by technological advancements and a growing need for innovative vaccine platforms. Ongoing preclinical research and clinical trials to refine the platforms and overcome current challenges will be crucial to harnessing their full potential as a novel, safe, and effective vaccine platform for global health, particularly in the contexts of AMR, pandemic preparedness, One Health, and LMICs. The rapid scientific and technical advancements in synthetic biology, phage engineering, CRISPR, and other precision genetic editing will enable engineering safer, more flexible phage platforms to display and deliver a broader range of antigens with enhanced versatility, and optimizing phage genomes for better antigen presentation. Artificial intelligence and machine learning should greatly assist and accelerate the design and optimization of the phage display and vaccine constructs. Further research on phage biology and its interactions with the human immune system and normal microbiome is needed to successfully develop phage-based vaccines. To achieve these aims, multidisciplinary collaboration between microbiology, immunology, synthetic biology, regulatory sciences, and public health will be critical.

The establishment of an optimized “plug and play” phage vaccine platform will make a significant impact for easy and rapid vaccine development and production. Such a platform is likely to be increasingly applied to develop vaccines against AMR pathogens and pandemic preparedness. Compared to pediatric vaccines, the target population for many AMR vaccines is relatively small, with minimal to no financial incentives to vaccine developers and investors. Phage-based vaccines could provide a cost-effective platform for establishing AMR vaccine libraries for rapid production and vaccine stockpile when needed.

More studies on the potential of phages as a mucosal vaccine delivery system will be needed. Currently, there are very few safe and effective mucosal adjuvants and vaccine delivery systems. Thus, the immunogenicity and efficacy of phage-based mucosal vaccines should be assessed and compared with other mucosal vaccine platforms. The potential impact of phage vaccines on normal mucosal microbiomes will need to be determined, although the T4-COVID vaccine was reported to have no undesired effects on the gut microbiome.20

Using phages and PLPs as carriers to conjugate carbohydrate antigens to induce T-dependent immune responses may open a new venue for applying phages in the development of glycoconjugate vaccines. If successful, this will make phage-based vaccines an important platform for developing AMR vaccines and other pediatric bacterial vaccines for LMICs. Chemical conjugation has also been used to display protein/peptide antigens on phages when genetic display is difficult. However, the effectiveness of this approach, compared to traditional carrier proteins (such as tetanus toxoid and CRM197), remains to be assessed to determine the potential of this conjugation strategy.

The development of phage-based vaccines will significantly benefit from lessons learned from human phage therapy of bacterial infections, and from the development of viral vectored vaccines. Novel strategies will need to be developed to navigate through the regulatory pathway. With the increasing availability of clinical data from phage therapy trials, it will be more productive to start phage-based vaccines from the skin (such as intradermal, subcutaneous, or transdermal) immunization, since the phage formulations for skin administration are better established, and the tissue and blood distribution of the phages administered are easier to monitor. In the case of developing phage vaccines for pathogens with zoonotic potential, a strategy of developing animal vaccines first, and then human vaccines, can be considered. We anticipate that phage-based vaccines for One Health (animal health and food safety) will lead the advancement of this platform and eventual licensure in the next five years. We will also see the initiation of human Phase 1 and 2 clinical trials of phage-based vaccines during this period. In addition to the traditional phage production using host bacterial cells, new and innovative manufacturing processes, such as cell-free expression systems and using low-endotoxin production strains, may be explored to produce phage vaccines. This will be especially useful for producing small batches of different phages for AMR vaccines. With the recent renaissance of phage therapy and the recognition of phage engineering as one of 2023‘s major technology innovations by the World Economic Forum,55 we anticipate that significant progress in phage-based vaccines will be made in the next five years with increased funding and investments from both public and private sectors to support phage research and development.

Conclusion

Phage-based vaccine platforms have been explored for antigen display, nucleic acid delivery, and hybrid phage-DNA multivalent vaccine formulations with promising results, particularly in infectious disease applications (pandemic responses and AMR). Current research and development of phage-based vaccines remain at the experimental and preclinical stages. The majority of studies have focused on the vaccine construction, formulation, and immunogenicity assessment. The protective efficacies of these vaccine candidates remain to be systematically tested with appropriate benchmark controls. However, significant challenges remain. These include concerns about safety and immunogenicity, limitations in antigen presentation, risks of anti-phage immunity, manufacturing complexities, and the lack of established regulatory pathways. Addressing these barriers will require advances in synthetic biology, innovative delivery strategies, and clear translational frameworks. With sustained interdisciplinary efforts, phages have the potential to expand the vaccine toolkit, particularly as rapid response and cost-effective platforms for global health, and transform from current proof-of-concept to clinical reality.

Biography

Wangxue Chen, PhD, is a principal research officer and the Team Leader of Infectious Diseases in the Human Health Therapeutics Research Center (HHT), National Research Council Canada (NRC) in Ottawa, Canada. His research interests and expertise are the use of clinically relevant animal models to study the immunobiology of emerging infections and antimicrobial resistance (AMR), with the ultimate goal of developing novel mucosal vaccines and alternative therapeutics. Since 2023, his research has focused on the use of bacteriophages as alternatives to antibiotics and for other biotechnological applications. Currently, he leads an NRC Ideation Small Team project on bacteriophage therapy for AMR. He has ~200 peer-reviewed scientific publications and is a co-inventor in several patents on vaccines and novel therapeutic technologies. He serves as a reviewer for many national and international grant agencies and scientific journals and is an Editorial Board member of several scientific journals.
