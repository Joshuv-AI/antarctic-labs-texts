Characterising Thermotolerance Traits and Their Acclimation Potential in Two Important Forestry Species of Eucalyptus
Authors: Ignatious Matimati, Mark J. Hovenden, Chris J. Blackman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs climate warming and more frequent heatwave events cause increasing damage to native and managed forest trees, there is an urgent need to characterise species thermotolerance thresholds and define their capacity to acclimate to warmer growth conditions. This study characterised a range of thermotolerance traits related to thresholds of photosystem II (PSII) function under increasing temperature (T crit) and heat load in saplings and adults of two commercially and ecologically important eucalypt species, Eucalyptus globulus and E. nitens . We also measured photosynthetic and leaf economic traits and examined the acclimation response of leaf thermotolerance in saplings grown under different temperatures and exposed to heatwave conditions. Saplings and adults of each species recorded similar optimum temperatures (T opt) for maximum photosynthesis (E. globulus = 26.8°C and E. nitens = 25.9°C) and similar leaf thermotolerance thresholds (T crit~42°C). When grown under different day/night temperatures (cool, 20/10°C; mild, 25/15°C; and warm, 30/20°C), we found evidence of plasticity in T crit in E. globulus saplings grown under cool (T crit = 42°C) versus warm (T crit = 45°C) conditions, while T crit in E. nitens saplings did not significantly vary. During heatwave conditions, PSII dysfunction increased in saplings of both species across all growth conditions with increasing heatwave intensity, with substantial loss of function at 45°C. We establish for the first time heat intensity‐duration relationships and define thermal acclimation potential for E. globulus and E. nitens saplings. These results provide forest managers with insights into canopy vulnerability when optimising species selection for heat‐prone sites and developing proactive climate adaptation strategies for plantations in a warming environment.

Introduction

Globally, recent patterns of tree mortality across forests have been linked to hot drought events (Hammond et al. 2022). Over the past five decades, rising temperatures and an increasing frequency of extreme heatwaves (Masson‐Delmotte et al. 2021) are exposing trees to an increasing risk of dieback and mortality (Allen et al. 2015). In Australia, periods of extreme heat and drought have triggered widespread mortality in forest trees, including species of the ecologically and commercially important genus Eucalyptus (Matusick et al. 2013; Losso et al. 2022). Heat‐induced damage to trees has become a global challenge affecting native and commercial forest systems and is projected to increase as the climate continues to warm and heatwaves intensify (Allen et al. 2010, 2015; Breshears et al. 2013; Cai and Cowan 2013; Cai et al. 2014; Choat et al. 2018).

While plants are generally well adapted to the thermal conditions of their native environment (Berry and Bjorkman 1980), different species vary in their ability to alter key physiological and allocation traits and acclimate to novel conditions associated with warmer temperatures (Aspinwall et al. 2019; Kumarathunge et al. 2019). Limitations to the ability of plants to acclimate to warmer growth temperatures and increase their photosynthetic thermal tolerance may be an important factor that shapes the distributional range of tree species (Crous et al. 2013; Kumarathunge et al. 2019) and determines future climate envelopes for commercial species of Eucalyptus (Aspinwall et al. 2019). With the predicted acceleration of climate warming across south‐eastern Australia (Richardson 2022; Grose et al. 2023), understanding this acclimation potential and defining the thermotolerance limits of key Eucalyptus species will be essential for protecting the future of the genus that holds significant commercial and ecological value (Bi et al. 2014; Drake et al. 2015; Zhu et al. 2018).

It is well known that warming temperatures influence a range of physiological processes and plant traits across species and environments. These can include increases in the optimum temperature of photosynthesis (Kumarathunge et al. 2019; Crous et al. 2022) and changes in leaf economic traits that help define species ecological strategies (Cui et al. 2020). However, to assess and compare plant thermotolerance limits, researchers commonly measure chlorophyll fluorescence (F V/F M), representing the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (PSII), in response to increasing temperature and define temperature thresholds at which F V/F M begins to decline (T crit) or at which F V/F M declines by 50% from maximum (T 50; Cook et al. 2021; Marchin et al. 2022; Cook et al. 2024). T 50 signifies irreversible damage to the photosystem apparatus (Downton and Berry 1982). Thermotolerance metrics, such as the T crit and T 50, are used to assess the vulnerability of plant species to heat stress under climatic warming (Curtis et al. 2016; Cook et al. 2021; Marchin et al. 2022). However, as single temperature values, these metrics overlook the cumulative nature of physiological stress during heatwave conditions. Heat damage to plants depends on both the temperature intensity and duration of the exposure (Cook et al. 2024; Arnold et al. 2026; Figure S1). An increased duration of elevated temperatures increases the dose effect of heat stress and typically reduces temperature thresholds for heat damage and PSII dysfunction (Ørsted et al. 2022; Neuner and Buchner 2023). Therefore, thermotolerance in thermally dynamic environments is better modelled using temperature intensity ~ duration relationships (Cook et al. 2024; Arnold et al. 2026), rather than single temperature metrics like T 50 and T crit. This measure of plant sensitivity to cumulative heat stress is characterised by measuring T crit over incrementally longer periods of time and defining the slope (z) of the heat intensity~duration relationship (Cook et al. 2024). However, to date it has only been quantified in a limited number of species.

The two eucalyptus species, Eucalyptus globulus and E. nitens , dominate commercial hardwood plantations across south‐eastern Australia, yet their thermotolerance metrics and other physiological indicators of heat sensitivity have not been comprehensively studied. Eucalyptus globulus has a discontinuous natural distribution within south‐eastern Australia, mostly along Tasmania's east coast, but with some populations extending up to 70 km inland. It is found in Bass Strait islands such as Flinders Islands and King Islands, as well as in the south of Victoria at Cape Otway, the Strzelecki Ranges, and Wilsons Promontory (Boland et al. 1984). Eucalyptus nitens naturally occurs at mid to high altitudes (600–1600 m a.s.l.) in disjunct populations distributed from the high tablelands of southern New South Wales (NSW) to southern Victoria's mountain forests, with two outlier populations in northeastern NSW (Shepherd et al. 1976; Eldridge et al. 1994). Reports exist on temperature optimums of photosynthesis in E. globulus and E. nitens under field conditions, with both species increasing their temperature optimum under warming condition (Battaglia et al. 1996). However, differences between these species in leaf thermotolerance traits such as T crit remain unknown. Within the south‐eastern Australian plantation estate, E. nitens is planted in cooler sites, while E. globulus is preferred in warmer ones (Battaglia et al. 1996), reflecting differences in the climate niche of each species' native distribution. Given these differences in climate preference, we might expect E. globulus to exhibit higher leaf thermal tolerance thresholds than E. nitens . However, the link between species climate niche and their thermotolerance threshold is equivocal, with evidence of high thermal tolerance recorded in both warm and cold adapted species (Knight and Ackerly 2002; Zhu et al. 2018; Aspinwall et al. 2019; Briceño et al. 2025).

Elevated growth temperatures have been shown to alter a range of photosynthetic and plant allocation traits and improve resilience by thermopriming plants for heat stress events (Chopra et al. 2025). This acclimation response to growth temperature is driven by a myriad of changes in enzyme activity, hormone signalling, and gene expression (Aswathi et al. 2025). Studies on the acclimation of leaf thermal tolerance traits have also reported changes in T crit in response to changing seasonal and controlled temperature conditions (Zhu et al. 2018), including positive associations suggesting plants can adjust T crit in response to warming conditions (Ghouil et al. 2003; O'Sullivan et al. 2017). In eucalyptus, some studies have shown that growth under warmer temperatures does not alter leaf thermotolerance limits and does not increase resilience during heatwave conditions compared to plants grown under cooler conditions (Drake et al. 2018; Aspinwall et al. 2019), while others have reported an increase in T crit in response to repeated heatwaves (Ahrens et al. 2021). Thus, some uncertainty remains as to whether T crit increases in response to increasing growth temperature within eucalyptus and whether this response varies among species of differing climatic origin.

In this study, we measured the temperature response of photosynthesis and characterised a range of leaf thermotolerance traits, including T crit and T 50, in saplings and adults of two ecologically and commercially important species of Eucalyptus: E. globulus and E. nitens . We also measured the temperature intensity‐duration relationship of leaf thermotolerance in saplings of both species to test whether they differed in their temperature sensitivity to cumulative heat stress. In addition, we grew saplings of each species under different temperature regimes and measured their response in leaf thermotolerance and key photosynthesis, leaf economic, and metabolic traits. We hypothesised that growth under warmer conditions would increase T crit in both species and that this increase in leaf thermotolerance would increase plant resilience during imposed heatwave conditions.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Plant Material

We grew seedlings of E. globulus and E. nitens in 4 L square pots (Garden City Plastics, Hobart, Tasmania) each filled with a thoroughly mixed soil medium comprised of a 1:1 ratio of seedling mix (Horticultural and Landscaping Supplies, Brighton, Tasmania) and screened loam soil (Horticultural and Landscaping Supplies, Brighton, Tasmania). Prior to potting, 3 g L−1 of Scotts Osmocote Controlled Release Fertiliser Native (Scotts Australia Pty Ltd., Baulkham Hills, NSW) was added to the soil mixture and thoroughly mixed for approximately 15 min in a concrete mixer. The filled pots were watered to field capacity and four seeds of E. globulus (Forico Seedlot No. GLOB7420) and E. nitens (Forico Seedlot No. NIT101145) were placed equidistant in each pot, respectively, and lightly covered with about 2 mm of vermiculite. Each species had 80 pots for use across the different experiments. The pots were kept in a glasshouse with natural lighting and controlled day/night temperatures of approximately 25°C/15°C at the University of Tasmania, Hobart, Australia (42°54′18.7″ S 147°19′26.8″ E). To keep the soils moist, all pots were gently irrigated each day. Fourteen days after germination, the seedlings were thinned out to leave a single seedling per pot.

Twelve seedlings of each species were placed inside three growth chambers, each set to a different day/night temperature regime simulating cool (20/10°C), mild (25/15°C), and warm (30/15°C) conditions, and allowed to grow for a further 120 days. The remaining plants of each species were grown inside the glasshouse.

To determine whether the findings from our study were transferable to the field, we measured and compared temperature response curves of photosynthesis and thermotolerance (T crit) values in glasshouse grown saplings and adult trees of each species. For adult trees, sun‐exposed branches approximately 80 cm in length were excised from the middle of the canopy in six healthy individuals approximately 10–15 m tall growing at two sun‐exposed sites within the Hobart area. Branches for E. globulus were sampled from naturally occurring trees growing within a reserve at the University of Tasmania (42°54′19.8″ S 147°19′22.3″ E) and branches for E. nitens were sampled from planted trees at a semi‐rural property in South Hobart (42°54′43.9″ S 147°16′25.3″ E). The climate conditions at these sites were assumed to be the same given their proximity (within 2 km) and similar aspect and elevation (~100 m a.s.l.). Branches were excised using a pole pruner and immediately placed in buckets of fresh tap water, and 10 cm of the cut ends removed under water to minimise artefacts of excision on branch water supply (Brinkhoff et al. 2024). Leaf gas exchange and thermotolerance measurements commenced within 2 h from branch excision and measurements were completed within 6 h.

Photosynthetic Temperature Response

To determine optimal temperatures for A max, leaf‐level gas exchange was measured on recently expanded leaves of six adult tree branches and six glasshouse‐grown saplings of each species placed inside a climate‐controlled growth chamber. For each branch and potted sapling, 10 gas exchange measurements were taken at each of six temperatures (15°C, 20°C, 25°C, 30°C, 35°C, and 40°C) using a LI‐6400XT Portable Photosynthesis System (LiCOR). During gas exchange measurements, the leaves were exposed to a photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) of 1200 μmol m−2 s−1 until assimilation (A max) and stomatal conductance (g s) reached a maximum and stabilised. The flow rate was set at 500 mL min−1, and the CO2 concentration was set to 420 μmol mol−1. At each temperature step, the leaf chamber temperature was adjusted to match the set ambient temperature inside the growth chamber.

To determine whether E. globulus and E. nitens adjust leaf physiological and structural traits in response to different growth temperatures, A max, g s, chlorophyll concentration index (SPAD), and leaf dry mass per unit leaf area (LMA) were measured on saplings grown under the cool, mild, and warm conditions. Gas exchange was measured on the third recently expanded leaf of each plant in each chamber. Gas exchange measurements were performed as described above with the leaf chamber temperature adjusted to match the set growth chamber temperature. Relative chlorophyll concentrations on the third recently developed leaf of each sapling were measured with a portable SPAD 502 Plus (Konica Minolta). Five leaves of each sapling in each growth chamber were sampled and scanned at 300 dpi for leaf area analysis using IMAGE‐J (Schindelin et al. 2012). The scanned leaves were oven dried and weighed to calculate LMA.

Determining Thermotolerance Thresholds (T crit)

To determine the thermotolerances of PSII function in E. globulus and E. nitens , we measured the response of dark‐adapted F V/F M to increasing temperature and defined critical thermotolerance thresholds following the methods of Slot et al. (2021) and Cook et al. (2024). We determined thermotolerance thresholds (T crit) in adult and glasshouse‐grown saplings as well as in saplings grown under the different temperature conditions. For adult trees, a single sun‐exposed branch approximately 80 cm in length was excised from the canopy of six individuals and immediately placed in buckets of fresh tap water. Thermotolerance measurements commenced within 2 h from excision. Six recently expanded leaves of each branch had 10 discs per leaf (20 mm diameter) punched out and immediately placed in petri dishes lined with moist filter paper. Five leaf discs were submerged and incubated for 15 min in each of 12 temperature‐controlled water baths set to 12 different temperatures (21°C, 29°C, 34°C, 36°C, 38°C, 41°C, 42°C, 43°C, 46°C, 48°C, 51°C and 53°C).

To avoid hypoxic conditions during the assay, the leaf discs were wrapped in a piece of Miracloth (Merck Life Science) and placed in small zip‐lock bags with glass marbles as weights. Following heat‐treatment, the leaf discs were taken out of the zip‐lock bags and immediately placed in petri dishes lined with damp filter paper and kept under low light (< 10 μmol m−2 s−1) for 16 h. The treated leaf discs were dark adapted for 30 min and F V/F M measured using a Mini‐PAM II pulse amplitude fluorometer (Heinz Walz). We used the same method described above to determine whether T crit changes in saplings of E. globulus and E. nitens in response to growth under the different temperature conditions.

Thermotolerance Determined as a Function of Heat Intensity and Duration

We determined how F V/F M varies as a function of heat intensity (T) and duration (t; min) using saplings of each species grown under glasshouse conditions. Third and fourth recently expanded leaves were sampled and 20‐mm leaf discs punched out from each replicate and kept in humid petri dishes lined with damp filter paper. Leaf discs were wrapped in Miracloth (Merck Life Science Pty Ltd) in batches of five and placed in water‐tight zip‐lock bags with glass marbles as weights. Unlike the previous assays that incubated for 15 min, different batches of leaf discs were incubated at one of four durations (t = 5, 10, 15 or 30 min) in the temperature‐controlled water baths, set at the same 12 temperatures described above. As before, the heat‐treated leaf discs were kept under low light conditions for 16 h, then dark‐adapted for 30 min, before measuring F V/F M with the Mini‐PAM II. As a control, F V/F M was also measured on dark adapted leaf discs, which were not heat treated.

Temperature‐response parameters were T 50 (the temperature at which F V/F M is reduced by 50%) and z, the steepness (sensitivity) of the decrease in F V/F M across T. We fitted Weibull functions to the F V/F M versus leaf T to extract T 50 values. We fitted linear models between t (log10‐transformed) and the T 50 values. We then used the predict function to fit the obtained linear models to a sequence of 100 t values from 1 to 240 min. The maximum temperature tolerable for 1 min (T max) is the temperature at which the linear regression crossed the abscissa with log10 t. Sensitivity for each species was computed from the slope of regressions of T 50 versus log10 t as z=1/Slope. The z‐values were determined after bootstrapping the T 50 versus Log10 t relationships using the tidyverse packages in R (R Core Team 2023) to calculate z and the intercepts at t = 1 min. We used t = 1 min as log10 t (1 min) = 0.

Variations in Leaf Temperature With Air Temperatures

To understand whether leaf temperature directly corresponds with changes in air temperature, we took simultaneous measurements of leaf and air temperature in the greenhouse grown saplings. Leaf temperatures were measured with a 6400‐04 leaf temperature thermocouple (LI‐COR), interfaced with a LI‐6400XT photosynthesis system (LI‐COR) for the air temperatures from 12°C to 44°C. We observed very strong (R 2 = 0.99) and almost identical linear relationships (Figure S2) that predicted leaf temperatures of E. globulus and E. nitens from ambient air temperatures.

Statistical Analysis

The R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team 2023) was used in all the reported statistical analyses (see Supporting Information). A max was plotted as a function of leaf temperatures for each species. The package drc (Ritz et al. 2015) was then used to fit polynomial regressions with age and a linear temperature function, and a quadratic temperature function (A max~1 + Age + Temperature + I (Temperature2)), which were estimated using ML. The identity function I in the model formula enabled the quadratic terms to be calculated (See Table S1). The dredge function within the package MuMIn (Barton 2026) selected the most parsimonious models based on AIC values. For visualisation of the model with confidence intervals, the predict function was used. The beta functions of the models were used to calculate optimum temperatures (T opt) by age.

An analysis of variance was done to reveal differences in A max, g s, chlorophyll concentration (SPAD) and LMA across E. globulus and E. nitens grown under cool, mild, and warm temperature regimes. Tukey HSD multiple comparisons of means at 95% confidence level were done for mean separations. To test whether cool, mild and warm growth temperatures influenced T crit in E. globulus and E. nitens , the five F V/F M measurements of each batch were averaged for each replicate (n = 6 replicates) before plotting the variations in F V/F M by species as a function of increasing leaf temperature (Figure S2) for each growing temperature regimes. Segmented regression, using the segmented function of R statistical package (R Core Team 2023) were used to determine the breakpoint temperatures (T crit) and confidence intervals, where F V/F M, a proxy of PSII function, started to drop. To test the responses of F V/F M to increasing temperature and duration of heat exposure in E. globulus and E. nitens , we fitted a four‐parameter Weibull function (Ritz et al. 2015) FVFM=−d+a*e−Tcb, where a is the amplitude, b is the shape parameter, c a temperature scale parameter, and d the lower asymptote. The temperature at which F V/F M declined to 50% (T 50) was calculated as T50=clog21b, with standard errors estimated via the delta method using the package drc (Ritz et al. 2015) in R (R Core Team 2023).

Results

Temperature Response Curves and Thermotolerance in Saplings Versus Adults

The temperature response of maximum photosynthesis (A max), measured on saplings and adults of E. globulus and E. nitens is shown in Figure 1. The accompanying temperature response curves indicate that saplings and adults of each species displayed similar temperature optima (T opt; Figure 1). However, A max values in E. nitens at T opt were significantly higher in adults (18 μmol m−2 s−1) than in saplings (16 μmol m−2 s−1). When Amax data were analysed with plant age as a factor (saplings vs. adults), highly significant (p < 0.001) differences emerged between the two species (Table S1). Overall, the optimum temperature for A max (T opt) in E. globulus was 26.8°C for both saplings and adults, while for E. nitens , the T opt values for young and old trees were 25.8°C and 26°C, respectively (Table S2).

We found no significant differences in leaf thermotolerance (T crit) between saplings and adults of each species (Figure S1). Adult trees of E. globulus and E. nitens had approximately the same T crit values (42.1°C and 42.2°C, respectively), while saplings of each species had values within the same range, averaging 40.9°C and 42.4°C, respectively.

Gas Exchange and Thermotolerance in Response to Different Growth Temperatures

For E. globulus saplings, A max, g s, and chlorophyll concentration (SPAD) did not differ between cool and mild growth temperatures; however, the warm growth temperatures resulted in significantly lower A max, g s, and higher SPAD values. For E. nitens , growth temperature did not result in significantly different A max and g s. It was only under the warm growing conditions that SPAD values, which indicate chlorophyll concentrations, became significantly higher in both E. globulus and E. nitens . There were no significant differences in LMA between growth temperatures or between species (Figure 2).

Response to Heatwave Conditions

In response to heatwave conditions, F V/F M declined significantly in all plants of both species from all growth temperatures. Compared to control plants within each temperature treatment, the magnitude of the decline in F V/F M was substantially higher under extreme heatwave conditions set at 45°C (65% decline in E. globulus and 57% decline in E. nitens ) compared to the milder heatwave conditions set at 40°C (11% decline in E. globulus and 13% decline in E. nitens ; Figure 4). For both species, F V/F M did not differ between plants grown under the different temperature conditions when measured under either mild or extreme heatwave conditions.

When we increased the duration (t) of exposure with increasing temperature (T), we observed a decrease in leaf thermotolerance (T 50) in both species, but to varying degrees. In E. globulus, T 50 decreased from 54.2°C at t = 5 min to 48.9°C at t = 30 min, while in E. nitens T 50 decreased from 53.6°C at t = 5 min to 49.1°C at t = 30 min (Figure 6a). When four parameter Weibull functions were fitted to the data, the effects of increasing intensity and duration of heat stress revealed different thermal tolerance metrics for E. globulus and E. nitens (Figure 6a). Log‐linear T 50 versus t (log10 transformed) relationships and z‐values for both species (Figure 6b). At 1 min of heat stress, T 50 was higher in E. globulus (58.9°C) than E. nitens (56.9°C), indicating greater short‐term tolerance. However, a comparison of slopes of the temperature‐duration relationship as a measure of their thermal sensitivity (z, °C) observed for each species indicates that E. globulus (z = 7.3) is more sensitive to increasing temperature with increasing exposure time than E. nitens (z = 5.1) (Figure 6c).

Discussion

In this study we examined the thermotolerance properties of photosynthesis in E. globulus and E. nitens saplings grown under different temperatures representing cool, mild and warm growth conditions, before testing their capacity to tolerate heatwaves of increasing intensity (Cook et al. 2024). The capacity for species to adjust thermotolerance thresholds under novel thermal conditions is expected to play a major role in defining species vulnerability to heat‐damage under a warming climate. This study revealed that leaf thermotolerance (T crit) increased under warmer temperatures in E. globulus but not in E. nitens . Nevertheless, this acclimation response did not make warm‐grown E. globulus saplings more tolerant during extreme heatwave conditions compared to saplings grown under cooler temperatures. We also measured the sensitivity of F V/F M in saplings of each species to the combined effect of heat intensity and duration, with the derived relationships providing metrics for modelling thermal tolerances in E. globulus and E. nitens in a dynamic warming climate (Cook et al. 2024).

The divergent physiological responses observed in this study are strongly reflective of the contrasting ecological and climatic niches occupied by these two species. Eucalyptus globulus shows a discontinuous natural distribution primarily in lowland, coastal regions (< 300 m) of south‐eastern Australia and the Bass Strait islands. This exposure to relatively warmer, maritime‐influenced environments aligns with its status as a foundation tree and its observed capacity for physiological plasticity, allowing it to acclimate its T crit threshold from 42°C to 45°C under warmer growth regimes. In contrast, E. nitens is a high‐altitude specialist, naturally restricted to mountain forests between 600 and 1600 m above sea level. Evolution of these cooler, frost‐prone tablelands has likely prioritised cold hardiness over heat acclimation, explaining why this species maintains a stable, but fixed T crit threshold regardless of growth temperature. These distinct ecological backgrounds dictate their current usage in plantations—with E. nitens preferred for cooler sites—and suggest that their differing physiological mechanisms are deep‐seated adaptations to their respective climatic envelopes.

In our study, key metrics of photosynthetic function under increasing temperature, including T opt and T crit, were found to be similar between glasshouse‐grown saplings and field‐grown adult trees of each species. This suggests that our results obtained on saplings are transferable to adult plants in the field. While optimum temperatures for A max in adults and saplings of E. nitens were similar (ca. 26.0°C and 25.8°C, respectively), the adult trees attained significantly higher A max (by ~2 μmol m−2 s−1) and had an asymmetrical temperature response curve compared to saplings. The T opt values we observed for adults and saplings are comparable to those reported for fully expanded leaves measured by others (Battaglia et al. 1996) on trees grown under field conditions.

In response to the different growth temperatures, E. globulus saplings were observed to reduce A max and g s under the warm growing conditions. Such a decline in A max at increased temperatures in C3 plants is commonly attributed to Rubisco deactivation and reduced chloroplast electron transport rates (Wertin et al. 2011; Scafaro et al. 2023). In contrast, A max and g s were relatively similar across growth temperatures in E. nitens , suggesting that photosynthetic traits were insensitive to different growth temperature conditions. Interspecific differences in photosynthetic traits are often linked to the climate envelope of species distributional range associated with temperature (Berry and Bjorkman 1980). Recent studies from the global dataset FLUXNET2015 have detected widespread thermal acclimation, demonstrated as enhanced canopy‐scale A max under higher air temperatures (Liu et al. 2024). While we did not observe evidence of enhanced A max in saplings of E. globulus and E. nitens grown under warm temperatures, both species increased chlorophyll concentration (estimated as SPAD) with increasing growth temperature. The chlorophyll concentration results are corroborated by evidence that heat stress disrupts physiological thermostability and triggers biochemical reactions vital for plant survival, including fast accumulation of metabolites and heat shock proteins (HSPs; Zhu et al. 2024).

In southeastern Australia, E. nitens is the preferred plantation species in cool environments and E. globulus is planted in warmer environments (Battaglia et al. 1996), yet our results suggest that both species are equally vulnerable to heat stress, with a baseline T crit of 42°C. This supports the idea that species may differ in cold/freezing tolerance and that their distributions are shaped by minimum temperatures (Bennett et al. 2021). While a species' baseline leaf critical temperature can be influenced by its climate of origin, its ability to adjust that limit isn't always linked to its evolutionary history (Cox et al. 2025). The 3°C adjustment of T crit in E. globulus (from 42°C–45°C) aligns with reported acclimation ranges of +1 to 4°C during heat stress (Cox et al. 2025). This E. globulus adjustment represents 0.30°C per 1°C increase in growth temperature, which is slightly higher than the average reported cross‐species adjustment of 0.20°C–0.25°C (Zhu et al. 2018; Cox et al. 2025). Despite evidence of an increase in thermotolerance shown in saplings of E. globulus grown under warm conditions, all E. globulus saplings from each of the three growth temperatures were equally susceptible to the imposed heatwave conditions. Even for the acclimated E. globulus (T crit = 45°C), a 45°C heatwave left a zero thermal safety margin. Because heat stress is cumulative, damage is influenced by both the heat intensity (how far above T crit) and the duration of exposure (Neuner and Buchner 2023). In E. nitens , growth temperatures did not change the T crit and did not alter the effect of increasing heat stress on PSII function. Thus, E. nitens falls into the 58% of species identified in large‐scale studies that either fails to significantly increase their T crit or are impaired by heat stress (Cox et al. 2025). Indeed, the observed 11% and 63% losses in E. globulus and 13% and 57% losses in F V/F M in E. nitens following 3‐h heatwave of 40°C and 45°C, respectively, measured across the three growth temperature treatments, suggests both species are similarly vulnerable to extreme heat. Ramp‐up temperatures inside the growth chamber simulated the highest recorded temperatures in Tasmania (Table S3), recorded at Scamander on January 30, 2009, with a maximum of 42.2°C. Under the extreme heatwave treatment, these conditions were exceeded with growth chamber temperature reaching 45°C for a period of 3 h. Importantly, these extreme temperatures are predicted for many parts of south‐eastern Australia (Grose et al. 2023; CSIRO and BoM 2020), suggesting that both species are vulnerable to heat damage where they are planted in already warm sites.

While acclimation to warmer temperatures implies increased resistance to heat damage, evidence from a recent study on Eucalyptus camaldulensis, E. tereticornis, E. botryoides and E. smithii from cool and warm environments over an 8‐day heatwave experiment somewhat unexpectedly found that warm‐grown seedlings were more susceptible to heat damage (Aspinwall et al. 2019). The study linked this increased vulnerability to lower enzyme concentrations in leaves of warm‐grown plants. Their study indicates that the physiological adjustment to warmer growth temperatures may involve trade‐offs, including a decrease in key enzyme concentrations, likely affecting those central to photosynthesis, such as Rubisco. This could make the plants less resilient during extreme heatwaves. All plants in our 45°C heatwave for 3 h died as temperatures reached or exceeded their T crit. Thermal safety margins explain why some species are more vulnerable to heatwaves, even if they adapt to warmer conditions. Thermal safety margins are the difference between a plant's critical threshold (T crit or T 50) and its highest leaf temperature (T leaf). Cool‐grown saplings operated at lower baseline temperatures, providing a larger buffer of about 22°C between their 20/10°C growth regime and the 42°C T crit. In contrast, warm‐grown saplings, that acclimated by raising T crit to 45°C, also faced higher daytime temperatures of 30°C, this reducing their safety margin to just 15°C.

Species with restricted natural geographic range sizes, such as high altitude or cool origin specialists like E. nitens , are typically more vulnerable to heat damage (Aspinwall et al. 2019). While the geographic origin of a species determines the baseline T crit, it does not necessarily influence the capacity for adjustment during a heatwave (Cox et al. 2025). Although E. globulus and E. nitens recorded the same baseline T crit of 42°C, typical for species from temperate or maritime origins (Zhu et al. 2018), there was a divergence in their survival strategies under warmer conditions. Eucalyptus globulus with a wider geographic range appeared to rely on induced plasticity, whilst E. nitens , a high‐altitude specialist, relied on a stable but fixed threshold (T crit), making it heavily dependent on transpirational cooling to keep its leaves below its critical limit (Drake et al. 2015).

Our findings have potentially dire implications for commercial eucalypt plantations under the predicted Australian climate scenarios (Lawrence et al. 2022). Thermal safety margins, which are the buffer between peak leaf temperature (T leaf) and T crit, may be vanishing. The CMIP5 models predict that heatwaves in Australia will increase by up to 4.8°C by 2100, with regional heatwaves becoming 3°C to 5°C hotter than current averages (Drake et al. 2018). Our E. globulus and E. nitens with T crit values of 42°C–45°C are frequently operating with negative safety margins, as air temperatures during heatwaves often reach these temperatures in parts of Australia (CSIRO and Australian Bureau of Meteorology 2020). The PSII dysfunction at 45°C mirrors the conditions associated with the sudden forest canopy collapse observed in eucalypt forests around south‐western Australia during extreme heat events (Matusick et al. 2013). Trees grown under warmer baseline conditions can sometimes be more susceptible to heatwave damage due to reduced enzyme concentrations in their leaves, indicating that prior acclimation to gradual warming does not always guarantee protection against extreme heatwaves (Aspinwall et al. 2019). Literature sources indicate that species with limited geographical ranges, such as E. nitens from cooler, high altitude areas, are generally more susceptible to heatwave damage and are less effective at cooling through transpiration compared to species with wider distributions. Eucalyptus globulus can adapt to a warming environment, but E. nitens has a fixed threshold, making it more vulnerable to dieback as heatwaves increase in frequency and intensity.

The temperature intensity‐duration curves revealed that compared to E. nitens , E. globulus had a greater thermal tolerance over short periods of time but also a greater sensitivity (z) to increasing duration of heat stress. Although E. globulus possesses a higher acclimated T crit, its higher z‐value of 7.3 suggests a physiological trade‐off. While E. globulus can withstand higher absolute temperatures for short bursts, its photosynthetic function collapses more rapidly as the duration of exposure increases. In contrast, the lower z of 5.1 for E. nitens suggests that it can likely endure moderately stressful temperatures for longer periods before experiencing a 50% drop in photosynthetic function (T 50). A recent study of ecologically diverse species (desert and alpine) showed a relatively wide range of z‐values between 4 and 8 (Cook et al. 2024). Thus, E. globulus sits at the high end of this range, approaching the sensitivity of the Australian desert tree Eucalyptus socialis (z = 8.3), while E. nitens occupies the intermediate range, being less sensitive than the desert shrub Myoporum montanum (z = 6.5), and closer to values observed in some alpine species. While E. globulus appears more fragile to the duration of heat exposure, this may be compensated by its ability to acclimate T crit by 3°C, whereas E. nitens relies on a more stable, less sensitive, but fixed physiological profile.

The obtained metrics of leaf thermotolerance, combined with the robust relationships observed between T air and T leaf (Figure S2), allow for estimates of the likelihood that natural variability in temperatures will reach or exceed critical thresholds of leaf function. These data allow for modelling thermotolerance in E. globulus and E. nitens growing in a thermally changing environment and identifying periods of physiological stress in response to climate change. Importantly, sun‐exposed canopy leaves often experience temperatures 7°C–12°C hotter than ambient air under real world conditions (Perez et al. 2020; Cox et al. 2025). This means that even under current conditions, the baseline T crit of 42°C observed in saplings and adults of both of our study species is frequently approached or exceeded during extreme Australian summer events (CSIRO and Australian Bureau of Meteorology 2020). Although we show that E. globulus can acclimate its thermotolerance threshold to 45°C, the concurrent rise in daytime temperatures significantly shrinks its thermal safety margin, increasing its vulnerability to prolonged heatwaves. Conversely, the limited plasticity in leaf thermotolerance shown by the high‐altitude specialist E. nitens suggests a reliance on transpirational cooling that may fail if heatwaves co‐occur with drought. Integrating these species‐specific biochemical limits into landscape scale vulnerability models will allow for more proactive species selection and plantation management in a rapidly warming world.

Conclusions

This is the first study to comprehensively characterise photosynthetic traits and acclimation potential of leaf thermotolerance traits under warmer conditions in E. globulus and E. nitens, two commercially and ecologically valuable forestry species. As climate warming and intense heatwaves continue to threaten forest ecosystems globally, these species‐specific insights into thermotolerance thresholds, acclimation capacities, and cumulative heat stress responses will inform both adaptive management strategies and predictive models for plantation forestry in south‐eastern Australia and other regions. Future research should link PSII thermotolerance metrics with whole‐plant mortality, investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying differential acclimation responses, validate intensity‐duration relationships under field conditions (including combined heat and drought stress), and integrate the resulting thermal safety margins into landscape‐scale vulnerability models for current and future plantation distributions across south‐eastern Australia.
