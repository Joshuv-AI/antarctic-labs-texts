Peroxidase-mimicking chitosan/dextran-coated cobalt ferrite-graphene oxide nanozyme enhances hydrogen peroxide–mediated killing of cariogenic pathogens in dental biofilm models
Authors: Mohadeseh Ghaffari, Martin Nilsson, Tim Tolker-Nielsen, Asghar Taheri-Kafrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDental caries is a chronic, multifactorial and biofilm-mediated disease driven by complex microbial interactions within the oral cavity. The causative biofilm is particularly challenging to eliminate due to the protective extracellular matrix that shelters the embedded microorganisms and promotes retention of acidic metabolites, leading to enamel demineralization. Conventional antimicrobial agents are often ineffective in disrupting this matrix and fail to eradicate microbes within biofilms. In this study, we report the synthesis of a novel dextran and chitosan- decorated cobalt ferrite-grafted graphene oxide (CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS) nanozyme with high peroxidase-like activity as an innovative strategy to control dental biofilms. The nanozyme was synthesized via a facile sono-chemical co-precipitation and coating method. Comprehensive characterization via XRD, FTIR, DLS, TEM, SEM and EDX confirms the nanozyme’s structural integrity and successful functionalization with biocompatible dextran and chitosan, enhancing targeted biofilm penetration and stability. This nanozyme catalyzes hydrogen peroxide to generate reactive oxygen species in situ, resulting in significant reduction in the number of viable bacteria in oral model biofilms. In a monospecies Streptococcus mutans biofilm, the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme in combination with H₂O₂ exhibited a strong antibiofilm effect, resulting in a reduction of viable S. mutans cells by more than 3-log compared to the untreated control. In a supragingival plaque model biofilm comprising six species a > 2-log reduction in viable S. mutans was observed. Notably, in the same model, the treatment also resulted in a > 3-log reduction of the number of viable Fusebacterium nucleatum and Veillonella dispar as well as in a reduction in the number of viable Actinomyces naeslundii. The biocompatibility of the nanozyme components and its potent catalytic activity highlight its potential for incorporation into mouthwashes or toothpastes to effectively manage dental plaque and prevent caries development. Our findings demonstrate that nanozyme-based catalytic therapeutics offer a promising alternative strategy for the treatment and prevention of biofilm-associated oral diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43315-z.Keywords: Nanozyme, Peroxidase-like activity, Oral biofilm, Anti caries, AntimicrobialSubject terms: Biotechnology, Materials science, Microbiology, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The human oral microbiota comprises hundreds of bacterial species that form complex multispecies biofilm communities on various surfaces in the oral cavity. Under healthy conditions, there is a beneficial balance between the host and microbiota. However, under some conditions, this balance can be disrupted leading to dysbiosis and development of pathogenic biofilms. These biofilms create localized anaerobic microenvironments that facilitate growth and accumulation or oral pathogens in the biofilms. With the addition of low-molecular-weight carbohydrates from the diet, saccharolytic/acidogenic bacteria (such as streptococci, Actinomyces, and lactobacilli) are promoted in the supragingival biofilm, leading to a drop in pH, especially in densely compact areas of the biofilm where acid diffusion is limited. Subsequently, aciduric bacteria (e.g., S. mutans, Lactobacillus) can dominate. The microbes embedded in the biofilm matrix are resistant to antibiotics and contribute to the formation of dental plaque. The glycolytic-metabolic activity of these bacteria in combination with intake of fermentable sugars and poor oral hygiene can result in a highly acidic microenvironment (pH < 5.5), which causes persistent enamel demineralization1–3.

Dental caries is one of the most common and costly biofilm associated disease that causes the progressive demineralization of tooth tissue4,5. In addition to dental caries, polymicrobial biofilms play a central role in the pathogenesis of several oral diseases, including gingivitis, periodontitis, peri-implantitis (PI), implant-related infections (IRIs) and even oral cancer6,7. Multispecies biofilms are formed by diverse microbial species, each contributing unique functional traits, which enable the biofilm to exhibit complex activities that are not observed in mono-species biofilms8. Current and traditional approaches for controlling dental caries and other oral diseases rely on compounds such as chlorhexidine (CHX), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), fluoride, and various chemical biocides.

CHX, widely considered the standard for oral antimicrobial therapy, exhibits strong antimicrobial activity against planktonic bacteria but displays limited efficacy against plaque biofilms. It neither prevents dental caries nor disrupts the extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix critical to biofilm structure. Moreover, CHX is not suitable for routine use due to adverse effects including tooth staining, changed taste, and tartar buildup9–12. Similarly, while H₂O₂ causes reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage bacterial lipids, proteins, and DNA, its clinical use is limited due to slow reaction kinetics and the need for high concentrations to achieve effective antibacterial outcomes. Fluoride is widely recognized as the gold standard for the prevention of dental caries due to its proven efficacy in enhancing enamel remineralization and inhibiting the demineralization process. Although fluoride shows some anti-biofilm activity, it is often insufficient for high-risk populations with compromised oral hygiene or low salivary flow. Overexposure of fluoride can further lead to unpleasant side effects such as dental fluorosis13.

Antimicrobial nanomaterials have been proposed as a novel strategy to combat biofilms, reduce bacterial viability, and prevent their adhesion to surfaces. Extensive studies in the field of nanotechnology have shown that some nanoparticles have enzyme-like properties. These nanoparticles are known as nanozymes and have been introduced as a new generation of synthetic enzymes4. Nanozymes, a new type of functional nanomaterial with enzyme mimic activities are cheaper, more stable, and more resistant to harsh conditions such as high temperature or acidic pH than natural enzymes. Unlike natural enzymes, which are mostly proteins and are subject to denaturation, nano-enzymes have a stable structure and the ability to regulate their enzymatic activity. Depending on their structure and composition, these nanomaterials exhibit a variety of enzymatic activities, including oxidoreductase (e.g., peroxidase, catalase, superoxide dismutase, glucose oxidase, glutathione peroxidase, sulfite oxidase) and hydrolase activities14,15.

Among nanomaterials, graphene oxide (GO) has emerged as a versatile platform in biomedical applications due to its unique physical, chemical, and biological properties. GO may decrease microbial activity through physical damage and chemical oxidation, including membrane stress and ROS-independent oxidative stress. Beyond these conventional antibacterial effects, GO can also serve as a platform for peptide-based nanozymes, where peptide–graphene hybrids exhibit enzyme-mimetic catalytic activity, further expanding its functional potential in biocatalysis and antimicrobial therapy. Moreover, functionalization of GO with polydopamine and chelation of bioactive ions such as strontium has been shown to enhance the mechanical properties and osteogenic induction of composite scaffolds, including PLLA/PGA bone scaffolds, by promoting cell adhesion, proliferation, and bone regeneration. These multifaceted properties highlight the promise of modified graphene oxide for applications ranging from antimicrobial therapy to tissue engineering16–18. Also, another nanoparticle of interest is copper carbonate, which represents a promising biomineralized system capable of modulating enamel demineralization and supporting remineralization processes in dental applications19. The glucose-responsive Zn–Fe MOF/GOx nanozyme integrates glucose oxidase with bimetallic Zn–Fe MOF nanoparticles in a ROS-responsive hydrogel. In hyperglycemic environments, it catalyzes glucose oxidation to generate H₂O₂ and hydroxyl radicals, providing potent antibacterial effects against pathogens20. Iron oxide nanoparticles have received particular attention due to their high biocompatibility, ability to penetrate biological matrices, stability at low pH, and resistance to harsh environmental conditions, and have been approved by the FDA as safe materials21. The addition of cobalt (Co) to the structure of these nanoparticles enhances the fenton reactions, which form the basis of the peroxidative function of magnetic nanoparticles and also increases their chemical stability. However, uncoated cobalt ferrite nanoparticles may undergo oxidation and cause cytotoxicity. In order to overcome this problem, various organic and inorganic coatings such as surfactants, polysaccharides, polymers, organic acids and carbon materials are used to cover their surface4,22. Dental restorative materials are prone to mechanical degradation caused by chewing and brushing, leading to increased friction, wear, and premature failure. To overcome this issue, surface modification and coating strategies have been widely explored to enhance the tribological properties of resin composites. For example, friction-induced PTFE coatings on composite resins have been shown to provide ultralow friction and significantly reduced wear under simulated oral conditions. These findings highlight the potential of tailored surface coatings to improve the durability and clinical performance of dental restorative materials23.

Nanozyme materials require surface coatings for clinical application, since uncoated nanoparticles are unstable in physiological environments. Additionally, they may bind nonspecifically to biological tissues, causing adverse effects to cells. Ideally, surface coatings should boost biofilm targeting and improve biocompatibility, while also conserve catalytic activity. Dextran and chitosan as natural polysaccharides are great nanozyme coatings due to their biocompatibility, biodegradability, and non-toxic nature. Dextran can be incorporated into biofilm matrices by bacterial exoenzymes (e.g., S. mutans glucosyltransferases), assist to targeted nanozyme delivery and enhance anti-biofilm efficacy. Chitosan possesses antibacterial effects by disrupting microbial membranes through electrostatic interactions and intervening with DNA/RNA synthesis after penetrating cells. These properties make both polysaccharides ideal for effective, targeted, and eco-friendly anti-biofilm therapies24,25.

Despite the increasing interest in nanozymes for oral antibiofilm applications, most reported nanozymes have been limited to mono-species biofilms or exhibit lower catalytic efficiency under oral conditions. In this study, we synthesized a novel CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme via a new method, with enhanced peroxidase-like activity. In this study, this nanozyme was evaluated against multi-species dental biofilms, comprising five bacterial species and Candida albicans associated with supragingival plaque, demonstrating potent antibiofilm efficacy. These findings underscore its potential as a nanozyme-based therapeutic platform for the prevention and treatment of oral biofilm-associated diseases.

Materials and methods

Materials

Graphite powder, sodium nitrate (NaNO₃), sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), potassium permanganate (KMnO₄), ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3.6H2O), cobalt chloride hexahydrate (CoCl2.6H2O), sodium acetate (CH3COONa, NaAc), dextran, chitosan, acetic acid (CH3COOH, 99%), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%), and 3,3′,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. Analytical-grade chemical reagents were used throughout this study.

Preparation of graphene oxide nanosheets

Graphene oxide (GO) was synthesized from graphite powder using a modified Hummers’ method. First, 2 g of graphite powder and 2 g of sodium nitrate were added to 50 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), while stirring strongly in an ice bath for 15 min. Later, 4.0 g of potassium permanganate was slowly added to the mixture, while maintaining the temperature in the ice bath, causing a purple-green mixture. The resulting suspension was transferred to a water bath at 40 °C and magnetically stirred for further 90 min. Then, 50 mL of deionized (DI) water was slowly added, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 10 min. Consequently, 6 mL of hydrogen peroxide (30 wt%) was introduced until a golden-brown colloidal solution formed. The mixture was further diluted with 50 mL of DI water, centrifuged (18000 rpm), and washed with DI water until the supernatant reached pH 7. Finally, the purified GO product was dried in an oven at 50 °C for 24 h26,27.

In situ preparation of dextran and chitosan-functionalized GO/cobalt-ferrite nanozymes

The cobalt-ferrite nanoparticles grafted onto graphene oxide (CoFe2O4/GO) were synthesized using a sono-chemical co-precipitation method. In a typical process, 50 mg of graphene oxide was dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water using ultrasonic vibration for 30 min. Separately, 1.01 g of FeCl3.6H2O, 0.296 g of FeCl2.6H2O and 0.496 g of CoCl2.6H2O were dissolved in DI water under stirring. These two solutions were degassed, mixed, added into a 250mL three-necked flask, and consequently ammonia solution was added dropwise until the pH reached 11. The mixture was sonicated while stirring (200 rpm) for 30 min. The power and frequency of the ultrasonic bath were 100 W and 20 kHz, respectively. During the reaction, the pH was maintained at 11, and the temperature was kept at 60 °C. Then, Chitosan was dissolved in 0.6% acetic acid, and dextran was dissolved in deionized water. Both solutions were gradually added to the flask while stirring and sonicating for an additional hour. Finally, the reaction mixture was cooled at room temperature, the precipitate (dextran/chitosan functionalized CoFe2O4/GO nanozymes (CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS)) was magnetically separated, washed three times with deionized water, and dried at 50 °C in an oven28,]21.

Characterization

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of the samples, including graphene oxide, CoFe₂O₄, CoFe₂O₄/GO, and CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS were assessed in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹ to confirm the formation of Fe–O bonds in the nanozymes and dextran/chitosan functionalization. The crystalline structures of the synthesized GO, CoFe₂O₄/GO, and CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozymes were evaluated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. The size distribution of the nanozymes was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) technique (NanoQ, Cordouan Technologies SAS, France) at room temperature. The size distribution and morphology of the synthesized nanozymes were evaluated by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, FEI Nova NanoSEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi 7500).

Peroxidase like activity of nanozymes and kinetic assay

The peroxidase (POD)-like activity of CoFe2O4/GO and CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozymes was assessed using a colorimetric 3,3′,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) assay in the presence of H₂O₂, based on a modified protocol from Jiang et al. (2018). Briefly, 2 mg of each nanozymes were dispersed in 1 mL of 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer, pH 4.5, under ultrasonication for 30 min. The reaction mixture, including 20 µg/mL nanozymes and 2 mM TMB were prepared in a 1 mL quartz cuvette. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 5 min, after which H₂O₂ was added to achieve a final concentration of 1 M. The oxidation of TMB was evaluated by measuring its absorbance at 652 nm for 10 min using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Cary 100). A blank sample was used for all the experimental samples. The linear region of the absorbance curve was assessed to determine the catalytic activity. The nanozymes activity (in units) was calculated based on the linear portion of the absorbance-time curve using Eq. 1.

where, V is the total reaction volume (µL), ε is the molar absorption coefficient of oxidized TMB, 39,000 M− 1.cm− 1 at 652 nm, l is the optical path length of the cuvette (cm), A is the absorbance after blank correction, and ΔA/Δt is the initial linear rate of absorbance change at 652 nm (min⁻¹). One unit of nanozyme activity (U) is defined as the amount of nanozyme required to catalyzed the production of 1 µmol of the oxidized product per minute at 37 °C. The specific activity (SA) of the nanozyme was also determined by dividing the calculated activity (U) by the mass of the catalyst (mg), expressed as U/mg of the nanozyme22,29. The specific activity of nanozymes was determined by evaluating their reaction at various concentrations of nanozymes (10, 20, 40, 80 and 100 µg/mL), with fixed amounts of TMB and H2O2. Each concentration’s activity was calculated individually.

To evaluate the kinetics of the POD-like activity of the synthesized nanozymes, experiments were conducted using TMB and hydrogen peroxide as substrates. Initially, different concentrations of TMB (0.25–2 mM) were mixed with a fixed concentration of nanozyme (20 µg/mL), followed by the addition of H₂O₂ (final concentration of 1 M). The absorbance change at 652 nm was recorded over time to evaluate the catalytic reaction. In a second set of experiments, different concentrations of H₂O₂ (0–1 M) were added to the constant concentrations of TMB (2 mM) and nanozyme (20 µg/mL). The absorbance change at 652 nm was recorded over time to determine the effect of substrate concentration on the reaction rate. Based on the initial reaction velocities (ΔA/Δt), the Michaelis–Menten constant (Km.app) and the maximum reaction velocity (vmax.app) were calculated by fitting the data to the Michaelis–Menten equation plots22,30.

Impact of environmental conditions on the POD-like activity of nanozymes

To evaluate the effect of temperature and pH on the POD-like activity of nanozymes, a series of experiments were conducted under various conditions. To evaluate the effect of temperature on the catalytic performance of the nanozymes, they were synthesized at different temperatures of 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 °C. The POD-like activity of the resulting nanozymes (20 µg/mL) was assessed using TMB substrate in the presence of H₂O₂. The pH dependence of nanozymes was further assessed by dispersing them in acetate buffer solutions at various pH values of 3.5, 4.5, 5.5 and 6.5, and measuring their catalytic activity using TMB substrate in the presence of H₂O₂. To compare catalytic efficiency, reactions were monitored continuously for 10 min at different synthesis temperatures and pH values.

Preparation of strains and inoculum

The following strains were used in the study: Streptococcus mutans (UA159), Streptococcus oralis (CCUG 13229), Fusobacterium nucleatum subsp. nucleatum (ATCC 25586), Veillonella dispar CCUG 54948 T, Actinomyces naeslundii (CCUG 35337) and Candida albicans (SC5314). For routine cultivation S. mutans (UA159) were plated on either Tryptone soy agar (TSA) or Columbia agar base (Oxoid, Ltd) containing 5% defibrinated horse blood (SSI, Diagnostica). The other strains were plated on Columbia agar base (Oxoid, Ltd) containing 5% horse blood. S. mutans were grown either in a 5% CO2 incubator or anaerobically (10% H2, 10% CO2 and 80% N2) at 37 °C. The other strains were only grown anaerobically.

Viability activity assay of S. mutans mono-species biofilm

To assess biofilm viability and metabolism, S. mutans overnight cultures grown in BHI were diluted at 1:10 ratio in BHIS (BHI supplemented with 1% sucrose). 100 µL/well were added to microtiter black plate 96 wells and grown for18 hours in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. After removing the culture medium, the samples were treated with various concentrations of H₂O₂ (0–3%), nanozyme (0–1000 µg/mL), and nanozyme with H₂O₂ (at 43 h the samples were exposed to 1% H2O2 for 10 min). The whole experiment time was 43 h, the groups were treated for 10 min at 0, 5, 19 and 29 h after the formation biofilm. At room temperature (RT), the solutions were removed, and the samples were washed twice with 150 µL of water. Subsequently, 100 µL of PrestoBlue® reagent, prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, was added to each well for the cell viability assay. The kit is based on resazurin, a blue, non-toxic, non-fluorescent substance that is converted to resorufin, a pink, fluorescent compound, by oxidoreductase enzymes in living, metabolically active cells. After the addition of the reagent, the plates were incubated for 10 min at 37 °C in the dark condition. Fluorescence intensity was then measured using a TECAN reader (Infnite F200 PRO) at an excitation/emission wavelength of 570/590 nm, and the values were reported as relative fluorescence units (RFU)31.

Catalytic activity assay within biofilm

To measure the catalytic activity of CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS within biofilms of S. mutans. The S. mutans UA159 inoculum was prepared in BHI containing 1% (w/v) sucrose to an OD600 of 0.02. Biofilms were formed on 96-well microtiter plates and treated at 19 h and 29 h by nanozyme (1 mg/mL) in 0.1 M NaAc, pH 4.5, or control (buffer only) for 10 min at room temperature. At the end of the experimental period (43 h), all biofilms were washed three times with 0.1 M NaAc buffer, pH 4.5, and replaced for 10 min by the reaction buffer (200 µL 0.1 M NaAc buffer, pH 4.5, 1% H2O2 and 2mM TMB). The reaction was allowed to proceed for further 10 min at room temperature without shaking. Finally, the absorbance of the solution was recorded at 652 nm. Three independent experiments were performed.

Oral biofilm models

Colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration assays were applied for assessing the antibiofilm properties of CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS. To evaluate the elimination of mature biofilm under the influence of nanozymes a modified protocol based on Gao et al., 2016 was employed. The assay consisted of the following treatment groups for S.mutans biofilms: (i) buffer control i.e. NaAc (0.1 M, pH 4.5), (ii) nanozyme (1 mg/mL) in buffer, (iii) 1% H2O2 and (iv) nanozyme (1 mg/mL) combined with H2O2 1%. Biofilm were formed on saliva-coated hydroxyapatite (HA) discs (9.5 mm dia x 2 mm thick, Clarkson Chromatography Products Inc.). After each experiment the discs were grounded with 500-grit paper and reused. To allowed for formation of pellicle, each disc was incubated with 800 µL unstimulated, centrifuged (20000xg and sterile filtered (0.22 µM)) saliva for 1 h at room temperature in 24-wells polystyrene cell plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Denmark). Human saliva was collected from a healthy volunteer solely for in vitro experimental purposes to simulate oral environmental conditions on hydroxyapatite discs. No clinical procedures or interventions were performed, and no personal or identifiable data were collected. Written informed consent was obtained from the volunteer prior to sample collection. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations for research involving human biological materials. The experimental protocol for human saliva collection was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee at University of Isfahan. The S. mutans UA159 inoculum was prepared in BHI containing 1% (w/v) sucrose to an OD600 of 0.02. A total of 2.8 mL of a 50:50 mixture of inoculated medium and sterile saliva was added to each well. Each experiment was done with triplicates The samples were subsequently incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 19 h. The whole experiment time was 43 h. After formation of biofilm during this period, the groups were treated for 10 min at 0, 5, 19 and 29 h after the formation biofilm, and then biofilm-covered discs were gently rinsed by double dipping each disc in saline sterile 0.9% (3 × 2 mL) to remove excess and unbound agents (Fig. 1). The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium i.e.50:50 of BHI and saliva. At 43 h the samples were exposed to 2 mL of 1% H2O2 for 10 min, followed by dip-washing (3 × 2 mL) and transfer to falcon tubes containing 1 mL of 0.9% NaCl. The biofilms were harvested by vortexing for 2 min, followed by probe sonication (4 × 30 s, 10% amplitude, 60 W). The sonication procedure disperses the biofilm by providing optimum dispersal and maximum recoverable counts of viable cells. Serial dilutions of the sample were performed from 10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁸ and 20 µl of each dilution was spotted on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA). The plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 72 h and CFUs were determined32,33.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the synthesized nanozymes

We have developed a CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanocarrier for treatment of oral plaque-associated diseases, specifically dental caries. The microbiological studies presented in this study primarily focus on the anti-caries potential of CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS, using single species S. mutans biofilm cultures and a 6-species supragingival model. The individual components of the nanozyme - iron oxide, dextran and chitosan are FDA approved, suggesting that the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme is biocompatible. The presence of the polymer-core shell on nanozyme surface could enhance biofilm targeting specificity. The synthesis process of the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozymes is schematically illustrated in Fig. S1. The GO nanosheets and the abundant oxygen containing functional groups such as hydroxyl, epoxy, carbonyl, and carboxyl groups on their surface, became negatively charged. The presence of these groups promotes bacterial death in the absence of intracellular processes35. During a sono-chemical reaction, the introduction of cobalt and iron salt solution into the graphene oxide (GO) suspension facilitated the successful grafting of magnetite nanoparticles onto the surface of the GO nanosheets, leading to the formation of CoFe₂O₄/GO nanozymes.

The FTIR spectroscopy was employed to verify the formation of Fe–O bonds and the successful surface coating with dextran and chitosan. The FTIR analysis of nanozymes is illustrated in Fig. S2. In the FTIR spectrum of the cobalt ferrite, the absorption band at 581.4 cm⁻¹ is related to the Co–O bonds in the spinel lattice of CoFe₂O₄ structure. In the FTIR spectrum of GO, the presence of the absorption band at 1716.3 cm⁻¹ is a good indication of the oxidation of graphene (strong C = O bond). The absorption bands at 1223.6–1270.8 cm⁻¹ also clearly indicate epoxy groups, which are usually found in large amounts in GO. The FTIR analysis of the CoFe₂O₄/GO nanozyme revealed characteristic absorption bands confirming the formation of cobalt ferrite grafted graphene oxide nanosheets. The broad absorption bands at 3404.7 cm⁻¹, 1351.8 cm⁻¹ and 1068.3 cm⁻¹ are associated to the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups on graphene oxide, the absorption band at 1624.7 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the C = O stretching vibration of carboxyl groups, and the absorption bands at 840.81 cm⁻¹ and particularly 605.5 cm⁻¹ are assigned to the Fe–O and Co–O bonds, confirming the formation of the spinel structure of cobalt ferrite in the GO nanozyme. The coating of chitosan/dextran on the CoFe₂O₄/GO nanozyme was confirmed by broad absorption bands at 3386.3 cm⁻¹, 1618.9 cm⁻¹, 1546.6 cm⁻¹, and 1410.6 cm⁻¹ which are attributed to the O–H, N–H, C–N bending and C = O stretching, respectively. The absorption bands at 1235.1 cm⁻¹, 1149.3 cm⁻¹, and 1020.1 cm⁻¹ are attributed to C–O–C and C–O stretching vibrations, characteristic of glycosidic linkages in dextran and chitosan. The analysis of the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme confirms the successful surface modification with dextran and chitosan, along with the presence of cobalt ferrite and graphene oxide.

XRD analysis was employed to investigate the crystalline structure of GO, CoFe₂O₄/GO, and CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozymes (Fig. S3a). The GO sample exhibits a broad diffraction peak centered at approximately 2θ ≈ 10–11°, corresponding to the (001) plane of graphene oxide, indicative of its oxidized and partially exfoliated structure. For CoFe₂O₄/GO and CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS, the diffraction patterns display characteristic reflections at 2θ ≈ 30.2°, 35.5°, 43.3°, 53.6°, 57.2°, and 62.8°, which are indexed to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) planes of inverse spinel cobalt ferrite (CoFe₂O₄, ICDD PDF No. 00–022-1086), respectively. These results confirm the successful formation of the spinel CoFe₂O₄ crystalline phase36. The relatively broad diffraction peaks suggest nanoscale crystallinity and possible lattice strain, which are typical for cobalt ferrite nanoparticles synthesized via sono-chemical co-precipitation. The characteristic GO (001) reflection becomes significantly weakened or partially obscured in the composite samples due to the low GO content and overlap with the ferrite background. Moreover, no additional crystalline peaks associated with chitosan or dextran are observed, which is expected given their amorphous nature and low loading. The functionalization with chitosan/dextran does not alter the core crystalline structure of CoFe₂O₄, indicating that polymer coating occurs primarily at the surface without inducing bulk phase transformation. The DLS analysis illustrated that the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozymes had an average hydrodynamic diameter of 33.7 ± 5.96 nm with a tinny size distribution with standard deviation (Fig. S3b). Further, the EDX analysis verifies the presence of C, N, O, Fe, and Co in the CoFe₂O₄/GO nanozyme. High weight percentages of Fe (44.02%) and Co (10.55%) confirm the effective fabrication of CoFe₂O₄ nanoparticles onto graphene oxide nanosheets, while the significant C (23.09%) and O (20.79%) contents illustrate the graphene oxide component (Fig. S3c). These findings are consistent with FTIR, EDX, and electron microscopy results, confirming the structural integrity of the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozymes.

The morphology and microstructure of the synthesized nanozyme were examined by FE-SEM and TEM analysis. FE-SEM images at low magnification (1 μm) revealed the nanosheet-like architecture of graphene oxide (Fig. 2a), providing a suitable platform for nanoparticle grafting. At higher magnification (300 nm), CoFe₂O₄ nanoparticles were observed to be uniformly distributed across the GO nanosheets (Fig. 2b), indicating successful decoration. Complementary TEM images at 100 and 50 nm further confirmed the presence of well-dispersed nanoparticles anchored onto the GO nanosheets without noticeable aggregation, demonstrating strong interfacial interactions between the nanoparticles and the GO nanosheets (Fig. 2c,d).

Optimization of the peroxidase-like activity of the as-prepared nanozymes

To evaluate and optimize the peroxidase-like activity of the synthesized nanozymes, the effect of several physicochemical factors including temperature, pH, nanozyme concentration, and TMB substrate concentration were systematically investigated. As illustrated in (Fig. 3a), the relative catalytic activity exhibited a temperature dependent profile. The activity increased progressively with temperature, peaking at 60 °C, beyond which a gradual decline was observed. This thermal behavior suggests that the nanozyme retains structural stability and exhibits maximal catalytic efficiency at moderate temperatures, while elevated temperatures may induce partial deactivation due to thermal denaturation or structural disruption. (Fig. 3b) demonstrates a sharp dependence of catalytic performance on pH. The nanozyme showed optimal peroxidase-like activity in acidic conditions, particularly at pH 3.5 and 4.5. The peroxidase activity of the nanozyme is dependent on acidic conditions. The catalytic activity of the nanozyme was enhanced under acidic conditions (pH 4.5), which mimic the microenvironment of cariogenic biofilms, compared to near-neutral pH (6.5) typically found in non-cariogenic sites. This pH-dependent increase in activity suggests that under acidic conditions, the reduction of hydrogen peroxide and subsequent generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) would be more pronounced, thereby potentiating the antibiofilm efficacy of these nanozymes in cariogenic environments37,38. As shown in (Fig. 3c), increasing the concentration of the nanozyme from 10 to 100 µg/mL led to a corresponding improvement in catalytic activity, evidenced by a linear rise in absorbance at 652 nm. This concentration-dependent behavior is indicative of a greater availability of active catalytic centers, promoting more efficient substrate conversion. Higher nanozyme loadings may further enhance catalytic performance. (Fig. 3d) depicts the impact of varying TMB concentrations on catalytic output. A concentration-dependent increase in absorbance at 652 nm was observed up to approximately 2.0 mM, beyond which the reaction rate began to plateau, indicating substrate saturation at the nanozyme´s active sites. This kinetic behavior is characteristic of enzyme-like catalysis and supports a Michaelis–Menten-type mechanism underlying the nanozyme’s activity.

Kinetic parameters of the bare and modified nanozymes

The POD-like activity of the cobalt ferrite-grafted graphene oxide nanosheet nanozyme, with and without dextran and chitosan coatings, was compared. The impact of dextran and chitosan coating on the catalytic behavior of the nanozyme was assessed through enzyme-inspired kinetic analysis, including apparent maximum velocity (vmax.app) and the Michaelis–Menten constant (Km.app). The reaction rate was measured for both bare and coated nanozymes over a defined concentration of TMB, showing that the reaction followed phenomenological Michaelis–Menten model (Fig. S4a,b). The extracted parameters, including estimates of Km.app which indicated substrate affinity and vmax.app were provided. The Km.app values for the bare and coated nanozymes were 0.21 mM. The vmax.app increased from 15.37 to 15.81 µM/min, indicating that catalytic activity is preserved after surface modification. The apparent kinetic behavior was similarly analyzed for different concentrations of hydrogen peroxide as the substrate, The Km.app values for the bare and coated nanozymes were 0.55 and 0.57 mM, respectively. Also, the vmax.app increased from 13.89 to 18.05 µM/min as shown in (Fig. S4c,d). The specific activity, calculated based on the oxidation rate of TMB and the reduction of H₂O₂ per unit mass of nanozyme, also confirmed that catalytic performance was maintained after coating (Fig. S4e). Comparative data are provided in Table 1 for descriptive contextual reference only, acknowledging differences in experimental conditions across studies. These findings indicate that dextran and chitosan coating does not adversely affect the apparent kinetic behavior of the nanozyme and maintains catalytic performance across both substrates (TMB and H2O2). The specific activity of the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme increased with mass up to 0.02 mg (0.526 U/mg), but declined at higher concentrations (e.g., 0.090 U/mg at 0.08 mg). This trend may indicate a non-linear relationship, possibly related to substrate saturation or nanoparticle aggregation38.

Assessment of peroxidase-like activity of CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme within S. mutans biofilm

To evaluate the peroxidase-like catalytic activity of the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme within S. mutans biofilm (grown for 18 h in microtiter wells), the H₂O₂ decomposition and subsequent ROS generation and oxidation of the TMB substrate was measured at 652 nm after 10 min of reaction. The CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme is designed to catalyze decomposition of H₂O₂, generating ROS under acidic conditions, leading to effective disruption of bacterial and fungal biofilms on the tooth surface. As shown in Fig. 4a, a significant increase in the absorbance of oxidized TMB was observed in the treatment group (nanozyme (1 mg/mL) + 1% H₂O₂) compared to the untreated biofilm control, indicating strong peroxidase-mimetic activity at the biofilm surface (p < 0.01). The control exhibited negligible absorbance, confirming that the catalytic reaction can occur within the biofilm in the presence of both nanozyme and H₂O₂.

Mono-species biofilm viability assay

Following 18 h of biofilm growth, the viability of S. mutans mono-species biofilms under different treatments was evaluated using the PrestoBlue metabolic assay. Treatment with increasing concentrations of H₂O₂ (0.1–3%) resulted in a clear dose-dependent reduction in biofilm viability, with statistically significant effects starting at 0.5% (p < 0.05) and reaching maximum efficacy at 3% (p < 0.0001). Based on prior studies, 1% H₂O₂ was selected as a standard concentration for further combination experiments (Fig. 4b). Treatment with CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme alone at concentrations ranging from 5 µg/mL to 1 mg/mL did not produce any statistically significant effect on biofilm viability compared to the buffer control, indicating that the nanozyme lacks direct cytotoxic or biofilm-disrupting activity (Fig. 4c). However, with co-treatment with CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme and 1% H₂O₂, a significant reduction in biofilm viability was observed with the highest concentration nanozyme i.e. 1 mg/m, p < 0.01 (Fig. 4d). Lower concentrations of CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme in combination with H₂O₂ showed a trend toward reduced viability but did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that the catalytic effect of the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme and H₂O₂ on biofilm disruption is dose-dependent and becomes effective only at higher nanozyme concentrations.

Antimicrobial activity of treatments against mono- and multi-species oral biofilms on hydroxyapatite disc

Mono-species biofilms, typically formed by S. mutans, serve as simplified models to dental caries and can readily be established on saliva-coated HA discs in 24-well microtiter plates for high-throughput model systems31. The antibiofilm effect of the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme was evaluated under cariogenic conditions by assessing the viability of S. mutans biofilm formed on saliva-coated HA discs. The presence of 1% sucrose in BHI medium, enhance the biofilm biomass by promoting assembly of the EPS-rich matrix and development of the three dimensional biofilm architecture39. The antibiofilm activity was investigated using CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme with a short-time exposure of H2O2 (10 min). As shown in Fig. 5, the combined treatment with nanozyme and H₂O₂ resulted in a statistically significant reduction in viable bacterial count (p < 0.001) compared to control and nanozyme or H2O2 alone. This potent antibiofilm effect of CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme with H₂O₂ against S. mutans was as high as > 3-log reduction of viable cells. The CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme catalyzed the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which induced oxidative stress and disrupted the biofilm structure. The CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS surface which was coated with dextran and chitosan, likely create conditions that could provide specific glucan binding proteins40,41. S. mutans has the ability to produce four glucan-binding proteins, including surface bound glucosyltransferase B that can be expressed in high levels. These proteins play a key role in directly mediating adhesion to dextran8,42.

The effect of the nanozyme catalytic antibiofilm activity was further assessed using a supragingival consortium comprising six microbial species, including S. mutans, S. oralis, Actinomyces naeslundii, Fusobacterium nucleatum Veillonella dispar and the opportunistic fungal pathogen Candida albicans, based on the Zurich model43. Quantification of viable cells (CFUs) revealed that treatment with CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme (1 mg/mL) and 1% H₂O₂ for 10 min resulted in a substantial reduction in microbial load across nearly all species tested (Figs. 6 and S5). This effect was statistically significant for S. mutans, F. nucleatum, V. dispar, and C. albicans (p < 0.01 to p < 0.0001), indicating a potent synergistic effect between CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme and H₂O₂. This synergy is consistent with the antimicrobial mechanism previously described for the S. mutans model.

The reduction of S. mutans and S. oralis viable cells of > 2-log were observed compared to control (H2O2). The results suggest that S. mutans in mono-biofilm is less tolerant to ROS than S. mutans in multi-biofilm. This outcome is not unexpected and may be attributed to several factors, either individually or in combination, such as enhanced stress tolerance in the multispecies consortium, more complex biofilm architecture, and metabolic cooperation among microbial species.

S. mutans is a key cariogenic pathogen that produces lactic acid from sucrose fermentation, thereby lowering the pH of the dental biofilm and promoting enamel demineralization. In addition, its glucosyltransferase enzymes (Gtfs) produce EPS, which facilitate adhesion and biofilm formation. Recent studies have shown that S. mutans forms a more potent cariogenic biofilm in the presence of sugars (such as sucrose) and its synergistic interactions with Candida albicans are particularly prominent in severe childhood caries (ECC)43,44. In comparison to two remarkable studies reporting iron oxide nanozymes (Ferumoxytol) and the iron oxide nanozyme stabilize stannous fluoride system, which achieved approximately a > 3-log reduction in mono-species S. mutans biofilms, the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme developed in this study was evaluated against a more complex multi-species biofilm, providing a model that better reflects the ecological complexity of the oral environment. The dextran/chitosan coating of the nanozyme further enhances its affinity for the EPS matrix, promoting efficient ROS generation and disrupting the structural integrity of the biofilm. Consequently, even under these challenging multi-species conditions, where biofilms typically exhibit greater stress tolerance, our nanozyme achieved a > 2-log reduction, highlighting its strong catalytic antibiofilm performance4,13. The strain S. oralis, on the other hand, is recognized as one of the primary colonizers in dental biofilm formation. It produces H₂O₂ which suppresses opportunistic pathogens like S. mutans, but a high-sugar diet can disrupt this balance, promoting the formation of acidic biofilms that accelerate tooth decay. It is demonstrated that the absence of S. oralis in the biofilm resulted in a decrease in the growth of Actinomyces oris and increased abundance of S. mutans, which leads to a cariogenic state40,45,46.

For F. nucleatum, V. dispar and A. naeslundii the treatment with nanozyme and H₂O₂ led to a reduction in the number of viable cells of > 2-log compared to control. Treatment with H₂O₂ or nanozyme alone showed no significant difference from the buffer control, suggesting that neither agent alone is sufficient to overcome the inherent resistance of mature anaerobic biofilms. Importantly, these results emphasize the potential utility of nanozyme-based catalytic systems in targeting anaerobic communities associated with periodontitis and supragingival, especially to enhance local oxidative stress within the biofilm microenvironment.

F. nucleatum subsp. nucleatum, an obligate anaerobic bacterium, functions as a bridging organism between different species including Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria in the biofilm, strengthens subgingival biofilms, and contributes to the strength of the biofilm structure. Although its role in dental caries is relatively limited, F. nucleatum plays a prominent role in periodontal diseases, where it contributes to gingivitis and the progression of periodontitis by producing pro-inflammatory metabolites. However, it can also be detected in advanced caries lesions, such as root caries. Previous studies showed that F. nucleatum strongly exacerbates periodontitis by increasing inflammation and compromising the epithelial barrier, and it has been identified in cariogenic biofilms of children with severe ECC, contributing to complex biofilms formation47–49.

The strain A. naeslundii is present in the initiation of periodontitis as well as root caries and primary biofilm formation. It is involved in secondary and root caries due to its ability to tolerate acidic environments and produce acid. Earlier studies reported that A. naeslundii coexists with S. mutans in secondary caries lesions, where it aids in the development of resistant biofilms. Furthermore, it is associated with gingivitis and mild periodontitis due to its present in subgingival plaque and its interactions with other periodontal pathogens50–52.

The strain V. dispar is an anaerobic bacterium that metabolizes lactic acid produced by streptococci, helping to modulate biofilm pH. It has been found that Veillonella species, including V. dispar, are prevalent in biofilms associated with childhood caries, although their primary role appears to be ecological support within these microbial communities44,51.

The strain C. albicans synergizes with S. mutans within dental biofilms and has been implicated in the development of severe caries, particularly in children. C. albicans contributes to caries progression by increasing EPS production and promoting biofilm adhesion. It is proved that dual-specific biofilms of S. mutans and C. albicans caused subsurface carious lesions in tooth enamel, suggesting a synergistic role for this fungus in caries. Furthermore, C. albicans is present in biofilms associated with periodontal disease, particularly under immunocompromised conditions, where it interacts with bacteria such as F. nucleatum45,52,53.

Although our study did not employ a subgingival biofilm model directly associated with periodontitis, the findings from the supragingival model suggest that the CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS nanozyme may also exert antibiofilm effects against periodontal pathogens such as F. nucleatum. However, further investigations using subgingival models are necessary to validate its therapeutic potential in the context of periodontal diseases.

Conclusion

In brief, we developed the carbon based nanozyme using CoFe2O4 grafted graphene oxide nanosheets (GO) and decorated with dextran and chitosan (CoFe₂O₄/GO@Dex-CS) with peroxidase-like activity. The nanozyme was synthesized through a sono-chemical co-precipitation method and optimized for peroxidase-like activity. The resulting, nanozymes were an average hydrodynamic diameter of 33.7 nm. Quantitative kinetic evaluation demonstrated that dextran and chitosan coating preserved the catalytic behavior of the nanozyme, as Km.app values for TMB remained constant at 0.21 mM and vmax.app slightly increased from 15.37 to 15.81 µM/min after modification. For H₂O₂, comparable Km.app values of 0.55 and 0.57 mM were obtained for bare and coated nanozymes, while vmax.app increased from 13.89 to 18.05 µM/min. In addition, the coated nanozyme exhibited a maximum specific activity of 0.526 U/mg at 0.02 mg loading, confirming that surface functionalization maintained and even improved catalytic efficiency. The antibacterial efficacy of this nanozyme was evaluated against oral biofilm models, including S. mutans and a complex microbial consortium comprising five bacterial species and the fungus C. albicans. The nanozyme together with H₂O₂ was found to exhibit remarkable anti-biofilm activity, achieving up to a 3-log reduction in viable bacterial counts, particularly against anaerobic and Gram-negative organisms, which creates supragingival plaques and causes periodontitis and gingivitis. These findings suggest that this biocompatible nanozyme holds strong potential as an effective antimicrobial agent for incorporation into oral care formulations such as mouthwashes and toothpastes to combat persistent dental biofilms. Furthermore, its superior catalytic performance and biocompatibility underscore its promising applicability in broader biomedical and nanozyme-based therapeutic approaches. The antimicrobial effects of the nanozyme observed in this study were promising; however, the effects of the nanozyme on tissue, enamel, and host cells were not assessed in this study and will be addressed in future investigations, together with evaluations of biocompatibility, safety, and clinically relevant H₂O₂ concentrations. Also, the mechanisms of biofilm disruption by the nanozyme, including Dextran-mediated targeting, Chitosan-mediated electrostatic interactions, and ROS generation, are presented here as plausible hypotheses based on prior studies rather than definitive conclusions. Direct evidence for nanozyme penetration, EPS degradation, or spatial ROS distribution within the biofilm was not obtained in this work, and future studies using CLSM, ROS-sensitive probes, and quantitative EPS assays will be necessary to validate these proposed mechanisms. Together, these future investigations will be crucial for translating this promising nanozyme from a proof-of-concept study into a clinically viable therapeutic agent.
