Surgical resection versus stereotactic body radiotherapy for oligometastatic breast cancer to the liver
Authors: Lianjie Niu, Lixu Zhu, Qiang Zhang, Yongtao Bai, Qigen Fang, Xianfu Sun, Wenzhi Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Comparative evidence for hepatic resection and stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) in breast cancer liver oligometastases remains limited. We compared survival, progression, and recovery after these treatments. This three-center retrospective study included adults with breast cancer and ≤ 3 liver metastases undergoing hepatic resection or SBRT between 2010 and 2023. Patients with uncontrolled extrahepatic disease were excluded. Propensity score matching generated 1:1 treatment pairs. Overall survival (OS) was the primary endpoint. Adjusted Cox models evaluated OS and progression-free survival (PFS); progression outcomes were analyzed with death as a competing event. Among 1,055 eligible patients, 708 were matched (354 per group). During a median follow-up of 54.8 months, 432 deaths occurred. Median OS was 48.3 months after surgery and 38.8 months after SBRT (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] for SBRT versus surgery, 1.24; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.02–1.50). Median PFS was 21.8 and 19.7 months, respectively (adjusted HR, 1.13; 95% CI, 0.96–1.33). The 24 month cumulative incidence of treated-site local failure was 7.1% versus 6.5% (adjusted subdistribution HR, 1.12; 95% CI, 0.75–1.68). Median hospital stay was 8 versus 1 day; among patients restarting systemic therapy, median restart intervals were 45 versus 18 days. The OS association became less precise in analyses restricted to R0 resection or liver-only disease. Resection was associated with longer OS, while SBRT was associated with shorter hospitalization and treatment interruption. Uncertain differences in PFS and local failure do not establish equivalence. Residual confounding and treatment heterogeneity limit causal interpretation and warrant prospective comparative evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10585-026-10436-8.

Introduction

Breast cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide [1]. Although early detection and systemic treatment have improved outcomes, distant recurrence remains an important clinical problem [2]. The liver is a frequent site of metastatic involvement, and prognosis varies substantially with tumor biology, disease burden and available systemic treatments [3, 4].

Oligometastatic disease (OMD) describes a limited metastatic burden, commonly defined as no more than three to five lesions, that may represent an intermediate state between localized and widely disseminated cancer [5]. Selected patients with breast cancer and isolated liver metastases have experienced prolonged survival after local treatment [6]. However, randomized breast-cancer studies have not demonstrated a consistent benefit from adding metastasis-directed therapy to systemic treatment, emphasizing the importance of disease-specific evidence and careful patient selection [7, 8].

Hepatic resection can remove macroscopic disease and has been associated with favorable long-term outcomes in selected patients [9]. Its potential benefits must be weighed against perioperative morbidity, recovery time and the risk of impaired liver function. Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) offers a non-invasive alternative that delivers conformal radiation while limiting exposure of surrounding tissues. Reports across oligometastatic settings, including breast cancer, support its feasibility and local activity [10, 11]. Nevertheless, comparisons with historical surgical series are vulnerable to differences in patient fitness, tumor location, disease burden and treatment selection. Prospective SBRT experience also highlights the need to evaluate toxicity alongside oncologic outcomes [12].

Direct comparative evidence for surgery and SBRT in breast cancer liver oligometastases remains limited. This study framework compares survival, patterns of progression, treatment-related toxicity and recovery after the two modalities, with particular attention to disease history, extrahepatic burden and treatment heterogeneity.

Patients and methods

Study setting and data provenance

The study was a retrospective review of patients treated at Henan Cancer Hospital (Center A), the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Center B), and The Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Center C), and approved by the Institutional Research Committee of each center, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to initial treatment. All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population and clinical variables

The clinical eligibility criteria included adults aged at least 18 years with histologically confirmed breast cancer, radiological or pathological confirmation of no more than three liver metastases, and curative-intent hepatic resection or SBRT between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2023. Patients were required to have received systemic therapy as indicated by molecular subtype and to have sufficient pretreatment information to establish eligibility. Exclusions were uncontrolled extrahepatic disease, another liver-directed treatment instead of or concurrently with the index modality, or insufficient pretreatment records. The index date was the date of surgery or the first SBRT fraction.

The data covered demographics, body mass index, performance status, primary tumor stage, histology, grade, hormone receptor and HER2 status, Ki-67, and molecular subtype. Metastatic characteristics included liver lesion number, maximum diameter, total volume, anatomical distribution and extrahepatic involvement. Systemic treatment variables included regimen, line, duration and response before local therapy. Treatment records described surgical approach, margins, operative time, blood loss and complications, or SBRT dose, fractionation, planning volumes, delivery technique and motion management. Follow-up variables included assessment dates, progression, death, last known vital status and adverse events.

Disease history followed the ESTRO–EORTC framework [13]. Prior polymetastatic disease defined induced OMD; without such a history, the first OMD episode was classified as de novo synchronous or metachronous disease, and a previous OMD episode defined repeat OMD. Total metastatic burden included hepatic and extrahepatic lesions. Extrahepatic disease was characterized by organ, lesion count, prior local treatment and pretreatment activity. Control required no new extrahepatic lesion and no unequivocal progression of an existing lesion relative to the preceding assessment, with the comparison interval recorded. Liver-confined progression was distinguished from extrahepatic progression.

Local treatment and imaging

Surgical characterization included operative approach and extent, blood loss, operative time and pathological margin status. R0, R1 and R2 denoted no residual tumor, microscopic residual tumor and macroscopic residual tumor, respectively.

For SBRT, the gross tumor volume (GTV) was defined as the visible metastatic lesion on planning CT. Contrast-enhanced planning CT and contrast-enhanced MRI were distinguished from diagnostic imaging, and their use was classified by treatment era. With free breathing and abdominal compression, an internal target volume (ITV) encompassed the GTV across four-dimensional CT phases. With breath hold, the GTV contoured on the breath-hold scan served as the ITV. An isotropic margin of 3–5 mm generated the planning target volume (PTV).

The biologically effective dose for an assumed α/β of 10 Gy (BED10) was calculated as D × [1 + D/(10n)], where D denotes total dose in Gy and n the number of fractions. Nominal prescription BED was distinguished from delivered target coverage and organ-at-risk exposure. The dose allocation assigned one schedule to all lesions within each patient.

Follow-up and outcomes

The clinical imaging schedule comprised contrast-enhanced liver CT or MRI at baseline, at 3 and 6 months after local therapy, every 6 months through year 3, and annually thereafter or when clinically indicated. Median follow-up was estimated using reverse Kaplan–Meier. The records used an assumed cutoff of 31 December 2025 and a common censoring clock.

The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), measured from the index date to death from any cause. Progression-free survival (PFS) was measured to the first radiological or clinical progression at any site or death. Patient-level treated-site local failure was the first recurrence in any initially irradiated hepatic lesion or surgical resection bed. The assessment protocol specified serial enlargement with new or increasing enhancement after SBRT, confirmed on subsequent imaging or pathology, and a new or enlarging enhancing lesion at the resection bed after surgery. Two radiologists independently assessed imaging, with disagreement resolved by consensus or a third senior reader under the clinical protocol.

Intrahepatic progression included treated-site failure and new liver metastases; extrahepatic progression included new lesions and progression of pre-existing extrahepatic disease. Follow-up for a compartment continued after progression elsewhere, and death before the relevant compartment event was treated as a competing event.

The toxicity protocol specified abstraction from clinical notes, laboratory results, admissions and interventions retrospectively applying the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0 [14]. Acute SBRT toxicity was evaluated through 90 days, with late toxicity and six-month liver assessments reported using their own evaluable denominators. Surgical complications were classified using the Clavien–Dindo system [15]. All-cause mortality at 30 and 90 days used the full treated denominator. Hospital stay and time to restarting systemic treatment were descriptive recovery measures; the latter was calculated among patients with a recorded restart.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Changes in paired albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) scores were evaluated with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 used as a descriptive significance threshold. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were exploratory, and no non-inferiority or equivalence margin was specified. The data analyses were implemented in Python.

The clinical matching plan specified logistic regression for treatment propensity using age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, molecular subtype, liver lesion number, maximum diameter, extrahepatic disease, disease-free interval (DFI) and systemic regimen. One-to-one nearest-neighbor matching used a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit propensity score. Absolute standardized mean differences (SMDs) assessed balance, with 0.10 as a diagnostic threshold.

OS and PFS were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves with Greenwood log-minus-log 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Univariable analyses described individual associations, expressed as hazard ratios (HRs). Adjusted Cox models retained treatment, age, ECOG status, molecular subtype, DFI, liver lesion number, maximum diameter, extrahepatic disease, pretreatment liver progression, prior systemic therapy lines, central location, treatment era and center, without significance-based variable selection. Standard errors were clustered by pair.

Treated-site, intrahepatic and extrahepatic progression were estimated using Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence functions with competing death. Pointwise CIs were obtained from 300 whole-pair bootstrap samples. Adjusted Fine–Gray models included the same baseline covariates, treatment-specific censoring weights and coefficient uncertainty estimated from 150 whole-pair bootstrap refits using a normal approximation. Cause-specific Cox models provided complementary estimates.

Exploratory OS subgroups were defined by molecular subtype, liver lesion number (1 vs. 2–3) and maximum diameter (≤ 2 vs. > 2 cm). Subgroup HRs were unadjusted Cox estimates with pair-clustered uncertainty; treatment-by-subgroup interaction tests were adjusted. Sensitivity analyses retained complete pairs with R0 surgery, advanced SBRT planning imaging or liver-only disease in both members, and separately added OMD history to the primary model. Residual balance was assessed after restriction. Within SBRT, a cause-specific local-failure model compared nominal BED10 ≥ 100 with < 100 Gy, adjusting for diameter, central location, triple-negative subtype and treatment era.

Results

Study population and disease characteristics

A sum of 1055 patients was eligible among 1547 patients screened, including 588 treated surgically and 467 with SBRT (Supplementary Fig. 1). Before matching, surgical patients were younger (median 57 versus 64 years), more often had ECOG 0 status (74.0% vs. 52.5%), had a longer DFI (45 vs. 34 months), and more often had received no more than one prior systemic therapy line (79.9% vs. 66.4%); all source-reported P values were < 0.001. Their liver metastases were smaller and fewer, and extrahepatic disease was less frequent (16.0% vs. 30.0%; P < 0.001). The paired cohort contained 354 patients per group. Its largest absolute SMD was 0.17 for treatment era, which was included in adjusted models (Table 1).

Baseline patient and tumor characteristics

In the surgery and SBRT groups, respectively, OMD history was de novo synchronous in 46 and 44 patients, de novo metachronous in 202 and 198, repeat in 48 and 47, induced in 50 and 57, and unclassifiable in 8 patients in each group. Extrahepatic disease affected 88 and 85 patients, predominantly in bone only (54 and 50), followed by lung only (14 and 15), distant nodes only (12 and 11), or multiple organs (8 and 9). Among patients with extrahepatic disease, 70 and 64 had one lesion and 18 and 21 had two lesions (Supplementary Table 1).

Treatment characteristics

Open resection was performed in 278 patients (78.5%) and laparoscopic resection in 76 (21.5%). Median operative time was 185 min (IQR 140–245), and median blood loss was 300 mL (IQR 150–500). The SBRT dose allocation comprised 142 patients receiving 26–30 Gy in one fraction, 124 receiving 27–30 Gy in two fractions and 88 receiving 36–40 Gy in three fractions. Median nominal BED10 was 89.7 Gy (IQR 75.0–99.9), with 82 of 354 patients receiving at least 100 Gy. The source median PTV was 45.2 cm3 (IQR 19.8–98.5), and the dose covering 95% of the PTV (D95%) was 96.0% of the prescription dose (IQR 94.0–98.0%) (Supplementary Table 2).

Planning-imaging categories included contrast-enhanced CT with registered MRI in 188 patients, contrast-enhanced CT alone in 98, MRI registration without contrast-enhanced planning CT in 38, and neither advanced planning option in 30. Thus, 324 of 354 patients (91.5%) had at least one advanced planning modality (Supplementary Table 3). Surgical margin categories comprised 334 R0 resections (94.4%) and 20 R1 resections. The first subsequent management assigned to the 20 R1 cases was re-resection in 2, focal postoperative radiotherapy in 3, a systemic regimen change without local salvage in 9, and continuation of systemic treatment without local salvage in 6 (Supplementary Table 4).

Follow-up and survival

At the cutoff of 31 December 2025, reverse Kaplan–Meier median follow-up was 54.8 months (95% CI 50.1–59.7) overall, 58.8 months (95% CI 51.5–64.5) after surgery and 50.8 months (95% CI 45.5–59.1) after SBRT (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 5). There were 432 deaths. Median OS was 48.3 months (95% CI 40.3–54.8) after surgery and 38.8 months (95% CI 34.1–43.4) after SBRT; corresponding 24 month estimates were 75.2% (95% CI 70.3–79.4) and 66.9% (95% CI 61.7–71.6) (Fig. 1). The adjusted HR for SBRT versus surgery was 1.24 (95% CI 1.02–1.50; P = 0.033). Older age, poorer ECOG status, greater maximum diameter and extrahepatic disease were associated with higher mortality, whereas HER2-positive subtype had lower estimated hazards. Estimates for triple-negative subtype, DFI, lesion number and pretreatment progression were imprecise (Table 3).

Safety and recovery

In the SBRT records, 342 of 354 patients were classified as assessable for CTCAE v5.0 regrading, including 94 of 102 treated in 2010–2017 and 248 of 252 treated in 2018–2023; 12 remained unassessable. Grade 3 or 4 events were assigned to 14 of 342 assessable patients (4.1%): nausea in 5, fatigue in 4, vomiting in 2, and liver enzyme elevation in 3, including two grade 3 events and one grade 4 event. Non-classic radiation-induced liver disease was assigned to 10 of 342 patients. Among 314 patients with paired 6 month liver assessments, median ALBI changed from − 2.88 (IQR − 3.14 to − 2.61) to − 2.82 (IQR − 3.17 to − 2.47; paired P = 0.028), and 9 patients shifted from Child–Pugh A to B. Among 343 patients classified as evaluable for late toxicity, biliary stenosis, vascular injury and focal fibrosis were assigned in 4, 3 and 15, respectively (Supplementary Table 6).

In the surgical group, 64 of 354 patients (18.1%) had a major complication category: bile leak requiring drainage in 18, hemorrhage requiring reoperation in 12, liver failure in 8, other grade III–IV complications in 22, and death within 90 days in 4. Two surgical deaths occurred within 30 days; the SBRT records contained one death within both the 30- and 90-day windows. Median hospital stay was 8 days (IQR 6–11) after surgery and 1 day (IQR 1–2) after SBRT. Systemic treatment restart was recorded in 338 patients in each group (95.5%); among those restarting, the median interval was 45 days (IQR 35–57) after surgery and 18 days (IQR 14–23) after SBRT (Supplementary Table 6).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Adjusted treatment-interaction P values for OS were 0.060 for molecular subtype, 0.630 for liver lesion number and 0.502 for maximum diameter (Fig. 4). The OS HR for SBRT versus surgery was 1.18 (95% CI 0.97–1.45; P = 0.105) in 334 R0 pairs, 1.33 (95% CI 1.08–1.63; P = 0.007) in 324 pairs with advanced SBRT planning imaging, and 1.18 (95% CI 0.94–1.49; P = 0.152) in 254 pairs with liver-only disease in both members. Adding OMD history yielded an HR of 1.24 (95% CI 1.02–1.51; P = 0.034). Within SBRT, the cause-specific local-failure HR for nominal BED10 ≥ 100 versus < 100 Gy was 0.60 (95% CI 0.26–1.41; P = 0.243) (Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

In this comparison of surgery and SBRT for breast cancer liver oligometastases, the adjusted OS association favored surgery, whereas PFS, treated-site failure and compartment-specific progression estimates were imprecise. SBRT records showed shorter hospital stays and intervals to restarting systemic treatment, although modality-specific toxicity scales and evaluable denominators differed.

The OMD concept describes a limited metastatic state whose behavior may differ from that of widespread disease [16]. Surgical series have reported prolonged survival after liver metastasectomy in selected patients, particularly those with favorable tumor biology, limited burden and longer DFI [17–20]. These observations support studying local treatment but do not establish its causal benefit. Treatment choice is influenced by operability, performance status, anatomical distribution and prior systemic response, which can also influence survival. The disease-history and extrahepatic-burden framework helps describe this heterogeneity without treating all patients with a small lesion count as biologically equivalent.

Local control is an important objective, but comparisons across studies require compatible endpoint definitions and technical context. The DEGRO experience included 474 patients with 623 liver metastases, of whom 57 had breast cancer [21]. Its heterogeneous doses and motion-management approaches illustrate why outcomes from mixed primary cancers cannot be transferred directly to a breast-specific surgery–SBRT comparison. Other SBRT series differ further in population and treatment setting [22, 23]. A patient-level time-to-event endpoint with competing death accounts for follow-up and mortality more appropriately than an unqualified fixed-time binary control rate. However, wide confidence intervals still permit clinically relevant differences, and a nonsignificant retrospective comparison cannot establish equivalence.

Other liver-directed approaches, including CT-guided high-dose-rate brachytherapy, broaden the local treatment options. Dosimetric comparisons and breast-specific clinical experience suggest that anatomy and adjacent normal structures are important considerations when evaluating these approaches [24, 25]. The same issue applies to SBRT: nominal prescription BED summarizes fractionation but does not fully describe GTV or PTV coverage, dose heterogeneity or organ-at-risk constraints. Lower prescription doses may reflect lesion location and treatment selection. The imprecise BED-stratified estimate here therefore cannot define an optimal threshold. Planning-image quality also matters because inadequate lesion conspicuity or image registration can affect target delineation; interpretation of dose and imaging should remain connected.

Durable local control does not necessarily prevent progression elsewhere. Occult systemic disease and progression of pre-existing metastases can limit PFS despite successful treatment of the targeted liver lesions [16]. The present compartment-specific definitions retain observation after progression at another site and therefore permit overlapping cumulative incidences. This approach answers questions about subsequent hepatic and extrahepatic disease burden, but should not be interpreted as a distribution of mutually exclusive first recurrences.

Systemic therapy further complicates the interpretation of local treatment. PALOMA-3 demonstrated improved PFS with palbociclib plus fulvestrant in hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer [26]. A retrospective multicenter study by Cha et al. also reported longer PFS with local treatment in selected patients receiving endocrine therapy, without a demonstrated OS benefit [27]. Such findings cannot resolve the comparative effect of surgery and SBRT, and a study containing only those two modalities cannot determine the benefit of adding local treatment to systemic therapy. In the randomized breast-cancer EXTEND population and the breast-cancer subgroup of CURB, metastasis-directed treatment did not demonstrate a general PFS benefit [7, 8]. These trials reinforce the need to distinguish treatment setting, cancer type and disease trajectory.

Recovery measures offer complementary information but require precise interpretation. Time to next treatment has been proposed as an outcome reflecting the interval before a subsequent systemic treatment change [28]. In contrast, the interval from a local procedure to restarting an existing systemic regimen principally describes treatment interruption and recovery. A shorter restart interval cannot by itself establish delayed escalation, improved disease control or a survival benefit. Future comparative studies should define these outcomes separately and consider patients who never restart treatment.

Patient selection remains central to the interpretation and applicability of retrospective findings [29, 30]. Clinical characteristics, OMD history and molecular profiling may help characterize disease biology, but candidate biomarkers require independent validation in the relevant population. For example, a serum microRNA signature developed in prostate cancer failed to discriminate oligo- from polymetastatic disease in an independent validation cohort [31]; this finding cautions against transferring exploratory signatures into breast-cancer treatment decisions. Circulating tumor DNA may complement imaging by reflecting systemic burden and treatment-related changes. Exploratory CURB observations support disease-specific investigation, but do not establish a validated rule for choosing hepatic SBRT versus resection [8]. Negative assays may also miss low-shedding disease. Standardized longitudinal sampling should therefore be evaluated prospectively alongside clinical disease history.

Several limitations constrain interpretation. Heterogeneous planning imaging and post-treatment appearances may affect local-failure assessment in the retrospective multicenter study, while incomplete historical toxicity documentation and different grading systems restrict safety comparisons. Late toxicity and liver-function summaries apply only to evaluable patients. Restriction to R0 resection or other selected subgroups introduces additional selection. Finally, the modest bootstrap counts and unstable sparse-event local-failure interval limit inferential precision. Clinical conclusions require verified eligibility, inclusion of early deaths, appropriate endpoint-specific follow-up, and reproducible analyses of the original cohort.

In this multicenter retrospective cohort, hepatic resection was associated with longer OS than SBRT after propensity score matching and covariate adjustment, whereas SBRT was associated with shorter hospitalization and earlier resumption of systemic therapy among patients who restarted treatment. Differences in PFS, treated-site local failure, and intrahepatic or extrahepatic progression remained uncertain and do not establish equivalence. Less precise survival estimates in the R0 and liver-only sensitivity analyses further warrant caution. Residual confounding and treatment heterogeneity limit causal interpretation. Prospective comparative studies incorporating contemporary systemic therapy, standardized local treatment protocols, and consistent outcome assessment are needed to clarify the relative benefits of these approaches and guide patient selection.
