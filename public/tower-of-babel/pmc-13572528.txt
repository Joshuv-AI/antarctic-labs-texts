The investigation of molecular mechanisms influencing variations of sternum calcification in ducks by integrating transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses
Authors: Yinjuan Lu, Zhengyang Shen, Xu Han, Yuan Bai, Lili Bai, Yuanchun Zhu, Jiwei Hu, Hehe Liu, Liang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In ducks, the sternum is the largest flat bone. Its calcification affects breast muscle development, health, and economic value. Delayed calcification can lead to sternum fractures during feeding, slaughter. Thus, we need to understand the molecular basis of sternum calcification. We compared two duck breeds with different body sizes: the fast-growing, large-bodied Cherry Valley (CV) and the smaller-bodied Huaifu (HF) ducks. Results showed a positive correlation between sternum calcification rate and both calcification length and breaking strength (r > 0.4). HF had a higher calcification rate (99%) than CV (95%), and females (HFM: 100%; CVM: 97%) had higher rates than males (HFG: 97%; CVG: 94%). Breaking strength was also higher in HF (43.29 N) than in CV (36.1 N), with females outperforming males. We performed RNA-seq and LC-MS metabolomics on sternal tissues. A clear contrast in gene expression appeared between nearly fully calcified Huaifu males (HFG) and females (HFM): genes including FGF6, BDNF, HTR4, MYLK2, MAP3K6, and CER1 were barely expressed. But these genes were significantly upregulated in CV compared to HF, in CVM compared to CVG, and in high-breaking-strength (HB) versus low-breaking-strength (DB) groups. Those genes showed low expression in fully calcified or non-calcified sterna but high expression during active calcification, suggesting they help drive calcification. Our study identifies multiple biomarkers linked to duck sternum development and offers new insights into avian sternum ossification, which may help improve poultry production and welfare.

Introduction

Bones are the hardest organs in vertebrates and play a critical role in the growth and development of poultry(Zhang et al., 2022). The sternum, a quadrilateral curved plate bone in poultry, extends vertically through the middle of the anterior thorax(Kittelsen, et al., 2021; Nakamura, et al., 2019). The sternum attaches the pectoral muscles and protects the heart and lungs(Jayachitra, et al., 2015; Li, et al., 2021b). It also contributes to body weight and breast muscle yield. Previous work showed that the sternum and pectoral muscles develop together in ducks(Li, et al., 2021a). However, artificial selection for fast growth and large pectoral muscles is often associated with incomplete sternum ossification at slaughter age. The imbalance between rapid growth and insufficient bone mineralization may lead to lung diseases and ascites(Li, et al., 2021c; Zhang, et al., 2017a). The growth rate of the sternum area is much slower than that of pectoral muscle volume(Wang, et al., 2021b), affecting the overall health and welfare of ducks.

Insufficient sternum calcification is due to the slow process of endochondral ossification during the development(Miyajima, et al., 2012). Accelerating sternum calcification is beneficial for alleviating the imbalance between rapid growth and insufficient bone calcification in ducks. Mineralizes in early development and rapidly between 42 and 49 days of age(Zhang, et al., 2017b). Research from Wang et al. showed that the earliest ossification area of the duck sternum appears at 6 weeks of age, and the ossification of the duck sternum is rapidly completed during the 6-9 weeks of age period(Wang et al., 2021b). Our previous research has shown that the sternum of ducks (Nonghua ducks) ossifies between 5 and 9 weeks(Wang et al., 2021b; Wu, et al., 2022b). These findings indicate that sternum mineralization varies over time and is influenced by factors such as breed and gender. For instance, Nakamura et al. found that calcification of the sternum body occurred in newly hatched blue-breasted quail but not in newly hatched Japanese quail(Nakamura et al., 2019). The White Leghorn hens raised by Kittelsen et al. had a higher sternum fracture rate (24%) than the Red Junglefowl hens (10%) under the same conditions(Kittelsen et al., 2021). Studies have indicated that the calcification rate of the female sternum appears to be faster(Kittelsen et al., 2021; Zhang, et al., 2019b).

In this study we examined sternum calcification in fast-growing Cherry Valley (CV) meat ducks and smaller-bodied Huaifu (HF) meat ducks. Because rapid calcification occurs between 7 and 9 weeks, we collected sternal tissues at 8 weeks and performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) metabolomics. We aimed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) linked to breed and sex, and thus reveal potential molecular drivers of sternum development.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Approval No. DKY20170913) and followed the “Guide for the Care and Use of Agricultural Animals in Research and Teaching”.

Animal samples and feeding management

This experiment was carried out at a breeding facility affiliated with Henan Xurui Food Co., Ltd. We used 240 eight-week-old ducks, equally divided between Cherry Valley (CV) and Huaifu (HF) breeds, each with a 1:1 male:female ratio. Ducks were kept indoors on litter floors with a density of 30 birds per treatment. The housing had natural ventilation and supplemental lighting for 16 hours per day. Feed and water were provided ad libitum via troughs and nipple drinkers. Temperature and humidity were kept steady following standard protocols. Feed composition for each growth stage is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Phenotype collection and sampling

At the age of 8 weeks, 4 ducks were randomly selected from each replicate group of CV and HF, totaling 32 ducks (half male and half female) for slaughter. Whole sterna were taken out for phenotype measurement (Fig. 1A), and three tissue samples were cut from the lower keel region (Fig. 1B) for histology, RNA-seq, and metabolomics. Histological samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde; RNA-seq and metabolomics samples were put in RNase-free tubes and stored at −80°C. Observation of sternum calcification at the tissue level

We randomly selected 12 samples (six males, six females, evenly split between CV and HF) for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining. Keel tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for more than 24 hours, then decalcified in 10% EDTA at room temperature for 9 weeks and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections (3 µm) were cut and stained with H&E (nuclei blue, cytoplasm red)(Rigueur and Lyons, 2014). Panoramic images were taken at 20 × magnification with a digital slide scanner (VS120-S6-W, Olympus, Japan), and we calculated the areas of different bone tissues(Zhao, et al., 2018).

Transcriptome Analysis of Sternum

Total RNA was extracted from the same 12 sternum samples. Poly(A) mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads and fragmented to ∼300 bp. First-strand cDNA was made with random hexamers and reverse transcriptase, then second-strand cDNA was synthesized. Libraries were built, size-selected (450 bp), and checked on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Sequencing was done on a BGI platform (paired-end). Raw reads were filtered with Fastp to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads (Q < 20). The clean reads were mapped to the duck reference genome ZJU.1.0 (GCF_015476345.1) using hisat2. Gene expression was normalized to counts per million (CPM). Differential expression was analyzed with DESeq2, using |log2(fold change)| > 1 and P < 0.05 as cutoffs.

Sternum LC-MS determination and analysis

Metabolites were extracted from the same 12 sternum samples. About 100 mg of tissue was ground in 1000 µL of extraction solvent (methanol:chloroform, 9:1, mixed with 25% H₂O, final 75% organic phase) with steel balls, using a tissue grinder (50 Hz, 60 s, twice). After sonication (30 min, room temperature) and ice-standing (30 min), the mix was centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4°C). The supernatant was dried under vacuum. The residue was dissolved in 200 µL of 50% acetonitrile containing 4 ppm 2‑chloro-l-phenylalanine, filtered, and transferred to LC-MS vials.

LC-MS data were collected in positive and negative ion modes. Raw data were converted to mzXML using ProteoWizard (v3.0.8789). Peak detection, filtering, and alignment were performed with the XCMS package in R (v3.3.2) (Supplementary Fig. S1). After within-sample normalization and Pareto scaling, metabolites were identified by matching MS/MS fragments against public databases (HMDB, Metlin, MassBank, LipidMaps, and mzCloud). Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were defined by P ≤ 0.05 and VIP (Variable Importance in Projection) ≥ 1 from the OPLS-DA model(Passos, et al., 2010; Trygg and Wold, 2010). A hierarchical clustering map of relative quantitative values of metabolites was obtained using the pheatmap package in R (v3.3.2). Pathway enrichment analysis of DAMs was performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca).

Combined analysis of transcriptome and metabolome

Pearson correlations between DEG expression levels and DAM intensities were calculated, using thresholds of |r| > 0.6 and P < 0.05. Graphs were created using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2) and R (version 4.2.3).

Statistical Analysis

Data are shown as mean ± SEM. Between-group differences were tested by t-test using SPSS (v20.0, IBM Corp.). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) for both transcriptomic and metabolomic data was done with SIMCA-P (v14.1, Umetrics)(Thévenot, et al., 2015). P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results and analysis

Sternum phenotype descriptions

Based on the phenotypic data (Table), we observed that at 8 weeks of age, the sternum calcification rate in the HF group (99%) was higher than that in the CV group (95%). Additionally, the sternum calcification rates in the HFM (100%) and CVM (97%) groups were higher than those in the HFG (97%) and CVG (94%) groups (Supplementary Table S2). Overall, the calcification rate of the sternum was faster in the HF group compared to the CV group, and faster in female ducks compared to male ducks. There was a moderate correlation (r > 0.4) (Fig. 2B and Fig. 2C) between the breaking strength of the sternum and its calcification rate. This suggests that, to a certain extent, the breaking strength of the sternum can reflect the degree of calcification. The data indicate that the breaking strength in the HF group (43.29 N) was higher than that in the CV group (36.1 N), and that the breaking strengths in the HFM (53.58 N) and CVM (46.47 N) groups were higher than those in the HFG (33 N) and CVG (25.72 N) groups. This further supports the notion that higher breaking strength corresponds to a higher degree of calcification. The tissue morphology of the keel near the sternum is shown in Fig. 2D. The bone marrows of HF and CV contain cavities surrounded by enlarged chondrocytes, with numerous osteoblasts and blood cells present in these bone marrow cavities. The bone matrix appears in the bone marrow cavity of HF, and bone cells are embedded in the bone trabeculae. In CV, more chondrocytes are present. The panoramic detection image of keel slices can be divided into four regions: cartilage matrix area, hypertrophic chondrocyte area, bone marrow cavity area, and trabecular bone area. The area proportion of each part was calculated separately, and the results showed no significant difference in bone composition between the two groups. HF has the highest percentage of bone matrix and the highest percentage of bone marrow cavity area, and the lowest percentage of hypertrophic chondrocytes (Supplementary Table S3).

Overview of metabolome and transcriptome datasets

The typical serum metabolite baseline chromatogram reveals differences in retention time and peak areas between the two groups, indicating significant variations in the types and concentrations of metabolites among different groups. A total of 12 cDNA libraries were synthesized, generating transcriptome RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data. The RNA-seq analysis yielded 56.97 Gb of clean reads, with 96.69% of bases having a quality score of Q20 or higher (Supplementary Table S4). Additionally, 54.32% of clean reads were mapped to the duck reference genome.

The negative mode of metabolites is listed in the attached table (Supplementary Fig. S1). The R2 (Interpretability of the model) and Q2 (Model predictability) values of the OPLS-DA model are greater than 0.5 and 0.4, respectively, indicating that the OPLS-DA model has been successfully established. Sternum transcriptome

Transcriptome sequencing was performed on the sternum tissues of 8-week CV and HF. The mapping rates of all samples were above 90%, indicating that the data are suitable for subsequent analysis. Through comparison, we found that CV had 258 genes upregulated and 842 genes downregulated compared to HF, while HFM had 155 genes upregulated and 356 genes downregulated compared to HFG. CVM had 879 genes upregulated and 2572 genes downregulated compared to CVG. Compared to HB, DB has 1054 genes upregulated and 180 downregulated (Fig. 4A). KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes in the four groups using KOBAS to identify functional categories and pathway enrichment (Fig. 4B). Among them, four pathways enriched in calcium signaling pathway (gga04020), MAPK signaling pathway (gga04010), ECM receptor interaction (gga04512), and Wnt signaling pathway (gga04310) are related to bone development, signal transduction, and interaction with bone cells. However, there was no enrichment of the Wnt and calcium signaling pathways in HFG (Fig. 4C). We conducted a correlation analysis between the differentially expressed genes enriched in these four key pathways and the phenotype (Fig. 4D). For genes with high correlation coefficients with the phenotype, we identified overlapping genes in the HF vs. CV, HFM vs. HFG, CVM vs. CVG, and HB vs. DB comparisons and examined their expression levels in each group (Fig. 4E). We found that FGF6, LAMA3, BDNF, MAP3K6, and CER1 were negatively correlated with sternum calcification rate (r < −0.7) and positively correlated with breaking strength (r > 0.7). Additionally, gene expression analysis revealed that FGF6, LAMA3, BDNF, MYLK2, MAP3K6, CER1, and HTR4 had higher expression levels in the CV group, indicating that FGF6, LAMA3, BDNF, MAP3K6, and CER1 are more highly expressed in non-calcified sternum tissue. Similarly, FGF6, LAMA3, BDNF, MYLK2, CER1, and RASGRF2 show higher expression in CVM compared to CVG, while FGF6, LAMA3, RASGRF2, and CER1 exhibit higher expression in HB compared to DB. Given that sternum calcification results from the proliferation of chondrocytes and their subsequent differentiation into mineralizing osteoblasts, we hypothesize that these genes are highly expressed in the later stages of osteoblast differentiation during sternum calcification, thereby promoting osteoblast maturation.

Sternum metabolome

The base peak chromatogram (Supplementary Fig. S2) detected 15,000 metabolites in positive and 13,061 in negative ion modes. Based on molecular weight and database matching, 418 metabolites were annotated. These metabolites were classified into 47 categories: carboxylic acids and derivatives, fatty acyls, organic oxides, benzene, substituted derivatives, steroids, and steroid derivatives (Supplementary Fig. S3).

In the metabolome, we found that HF had 13 upregulated DAMs and 11 downregulated DAMs compared to CV, HFG had 2 upregulated DAMs and 6 downregulated DAMs compared to HFM, CVG had 9 upregulated DAMs and 8 downregulated DAMs compared to CVM, and HB had 5 upregulated DAMs and 10 downregulated DAMs compared to DB (Fig. 5A). KEGG enrichment analysis of differential metabolites showed enrichment in ABC transporters, arginine and proline metabolism, and the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 5B). We performed a correlation analysis between all DAMs (Fig. 5C) and phenotypes and conducted an expression analysis of the DAMs (Fig. 5D). 12-Keto-tetrahydro-leukotriene B4, Aflatoxin B1, 5-Hydroxyindoleacetic acid, and Mannitol were negatively correlated with the rate of sternum calcification (r < −0.6). Conversely, Catechol, Guanidoacetic acid, 8,9-DiHETrE, Epiandrosterone, and Anandamide were positively correlated with the rate of sternum calcification (r > 0.6). The correlation coefficients indicated that catechol metabolites were positively correlated with MYLK2, HTR4, FGF6, MAP3K6, BDNF, and CER1 (r > 0.5). Conversely, 12-Keto-tetrahydro-leukotriene B4, Aflatoxin B1, and Guanidoacetic acid showed negative correlations with FGF6, CER1, LAMA3, HTR4, MAP3K6, BDNF, MYLK2 and RASGRF2 (r < −0.5). In contrast, Aflatoxin B1 shows higher expression in CVG and DB, suggesting that it inhibits sternum calcification.

Discussion

The sternum plays an important role in the growth of poultry; however, the growth and calcification of the sternum have been overlooked(Wang, et al., 2021a; Wu, et al., 2022a). Ducks at 7-8 weeks of age are at a stage of rapid sternal calcification. This study measured the sternum size and calcification indicators of two breeds of meat ducks at 8 weeks of age and revealed the factors and molecular mechanisms affecting sternum calcification through histological analysis, transcriptomics, and metabolomics. Understanding these mechanisms is vital for enhancing sternal calcification rates in ducks.

In this study, we found that the HF breed exhibits a faster rate of sternum calcification (0.99 ± 0.03) compared to the CV breed (0.95 ± 0.05). Additionally, the sternum calcification rate in HFM and CVM was faster than in HFG and CVG, indicating differences in sternum calcification between breed and gender. Furthermore, when we categorized breaking strength into high and low groups, we found that five female ducks were classified into the high-breaking strength group. Since breaking strength can partially reflect the extent of sternum calcification, Zhang et al.(Zhang, et al., 2019a) reported that the higher the calcium content in the sternum, the greater the fracture strength of the sternum. This suggests faster sternal calcification in female ducks. Correlation analysis further revealed a positive correlation between calcification rate and both calcified length and breaking strength of the sternum, implying that increased sternal calcification leads to more calcium salt deposition. Studies in mammals have also shown that the proliferation and differentiation of chondrocytes, vascular invasion, formation of bone marrow cavities, and mineralization of cartilage and bone matrices are key processes in ossification (Chen, et al., 2023; Sanverdi et al., 2014). Histologically, there are fewer hypertrophic chondrocytes in HF, indicating that the degree of calcification in HF is more complete.

At the transcriptomic level, we identified FGF6, LAMA3, BDNF, HTR4, MYLK2, MAP3K6, CER1, and RASGRF2 as genes associated with bone development across different breeds and genders. At the later stages of bone calcification, the differentiation of osteoblasts is a prerequisite for bone formation(Yanfang, et al., 2017). The role of FGF6 in bone is not well understood(Bosetti, et al., 2010). Takei et al. (Takei, et al., 2015) detected the expression of FGF7 in a cranial cell model but did not detect Fgf6 expression. Similarly, we did not detect FGF6 expression in the nearly fully calcified sternum (HFG vs. HFM) but did observe its expression in the sternum that was not fully calcified. We hypothesize that FGF6 is expressed during the phase when the sternum is not fully calcified. LAMA3 is a gene encoding the alpha 3 subunit of laminin(Caley, et al., 2021). Laminin 332, similar to other laminins found in basement membranes, aids in cell migration and invasion(Nonnast, et al., 2025). The expression level of LAMA3 is upregulated during osteogenesis(Shaik, et al., 2019). BDNF is required for the differentiation and survival of specific neuronal subpopulations. Several studies have shown that BDNF plays a role in chondrocyte differentiation, cartilage development, and osteogenesis (Hutchison, et al., 2010; Zhang, et al., 2025). Our research has found that high expression of BDNF is associated with slower calcification of the sternum, and Guo et al. (Guo, et al., 2016) identified BDNF as a significant candidate gene influencing osteoporosis through GWAS. Knocking out BDNF inhibits osteoblast differentiation. HTR4 is a serotonin receptor, and most reports have focused on cardiac research(Wang, et al., 2016). MYLK2 is enriched in the Calcium signaling pathway. Given the critical role of Ca2+, Calcium signaling plays an essential role in the development, regulation, and regeneration of bones and skeletal muscle(Tu, et al., 2016). Wei et al. (Wei, et al., 2023) found that MYLK2 expression was downregulated when comparing keel fractures in laying hens to normal keel bones. Our research shows that MYLK2 expression is upregulated during the calcification of the sternum keel, suggesting that MYLK2 promotes the calcification of the sternum keel. MAP3K6 is enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway, which consists of a series of kinases that play important roles in bone development. Previous reports have suggested that the MAPK signaling pathway may promote chondrocyte proliferation, development, cell survival, and cell movement(Tian, et al., 2013). In our comparisons of CVG vs. CVM and DB vs. HB, we found that the calcification rate of the sternum is faster in female ducks, with relatively higher expression levels of CER1. Previous studies have reported that variations in CER1 seem to play a significant role in the determination of osteoporosis and, when combined with bone markers, have potential predictive value for osteoporosis in women(Koromila, et al., 2013). Meanwhile, relevant studies have found that the sternum of male ducks matures later than that of female ducks(Wang et al., 2021b). This suggests that CER1 may play an important role in gender differences. RASGRF2 is primarily associated with multiple myeloma(Bong, et al., 2017).

At the metabolomic level and in the integrative analysis, we found strong correlations between Catechol, 12-keto-tetrahydro-leukotriene B4, 5-Hydroxyindoleacetic acid, Aflatoxin B1, Guanidoacetic acid, and sternum phenotype as well as FGF6 genes. Among them, Catechol is particularly useful to promote osteogenesis and angiogenesis(Kim, et al., 2020). Gouty arthritis (GA) is generally caused by the deposition of monosodium urate crystals in cartilage, bone, or other tissues surrounding the joints, 12-Keto-tetrahydro-leukotriene B4 was identified as a biomarker for GA(Lyu, et al., 2019). 5-Hydroxyindoleacetic acid is a metabolite of 5-HT, which has a dual effect on bone formation: low concentrations of 5-HT impair bone formation, while high concentrations of 5-HT tend to promote bone formation(Pawlak, et al., 2016), consistent with our research findings. Aflatoxin B1, a ubiquitous mycotoxin in corn-based animal feed(Medina, et al., 2014), negatively affects bone health at levels >230 ppb by impairing mineralization via disruption of the vitamin D receptor and calcium homeostasis(Paneru, et al., 2024). Our research shows that Aflatoxin B1 is lowly expressed in HB and CVM, and is negatively correlated with genes that promote sternum calcification, which is consistent with our results. Guanidoacetic acid is the direct precursor of creatine and its phosphorylated derivative phosphocreatine in the body, which can promote muscle growth and development(Yan, et al., 2021).

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results indicate that at 8 weeks of age, the sternum calcification rate of HF is faster than that of CV, and the sternum calcification rate of female ducks is faster than that of male ducks. Based on the transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses findings, we observed that multiple pathways influence the development and calcification of the duck's sternum. This study not only identified candidate genes associated with duck sternum development (such as FGF6, BDNF, HTR4, MYLK2, MAP3K6, and CER1) but also indicated that the process of ossification may be closely related to the expression of metabolites (such as Catechol, 12-Keto-tetrahydro-leukotriene B4, 5-Hydroxyindoleacetic acid, and Aflatoxin B1). These results provide valuable insights for further research into the ossification of avian breastbones and their mechanisms and offer new perspectives for improving poultry production performance and welfare.
