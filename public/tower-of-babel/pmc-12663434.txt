IL-27Ra promotes the progression of neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis
Authors: Yuling Yang, Yingyan Liu, Limei Zhong, Chun Yan, Xin Zhong, Chaoting Lan, Sitao Li, Yufeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a life-threatening gastrointestinal disease of premature infants, characterized by immune dysregulation and compromised intestinal barrier integrity. Interleukin-27 receptor α (IL-27Ra), a critical component of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, exhibits dual pro- and anti-inflammatory roles in various inflammatory conditions. However, its role in NEC pathogenesis remains unclear. To elucidate the functional role of IL-27Ra in NEC development and assess its potential as a therapeutic target. A multi-tiered approach was employed, including integrative analysis of clinical NEC specimens by single-cell and bulk RNA sequencing, and a neonatal mouse NEC model. NEC was induced in mice via hyperosmolar formula feeding combined with LPS gavage, intermittent hypoxia, and cold stress. Additional experiments included immunofluorescence staining for IL-27Ra, cytokine profiling (ELISA, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)), use of IL-27Ra knockout (IL-27Ra-/-) mice, and histopathological scoring of intestinal injury. In NEC patient intestinal tissues, IL-27Ra expression was significantly upregulated in immune cells, with expression levels positively correlating with pro-inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-6) and inversely correlating with barrier-associated proteins (e.g., TJP1). In the neonatal mouse NEC model, genetic ablation of IL-27Ra (IL-27Ra-/-) led to reduced histopathology scores, decreased IL-6 production (ELISA and qPCR), and restored tight junction protein expression (TJP1, OCLN). IL-27Ra promotes NEC by amplifying intestinal inflammation and damaging the mucosal barrier. Thus, IL-27Ra is identified as a promising therapeutic target. Pharmacological blockade of IL-27Ra signaling may provide a dual benefit in NEC—mitigating excessive inflammation while restoring barrier integrity. Introduction

Neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a severe inflammatory gastrointestinal disease primarily affecting preterm infants1. Among very low birth weight neonates, NEC affects approximately 7% of cases2, with a mortality rate of nearing 30%3, making it a leading cause of gastrointestinal morbidity and mortality in this population4. Despite advancements in neonatal intensive care, therapeutic strategies remain largely supportive due to an incomplete understanding of its pathogenesis5. NEC is frequently associated with serious complications such as neurodevelopmental delay, intestinal strictures, adhesions, cholestasis, and short-bowel syndrome, all of which are difficult to manage6. Key risk factors for NEC include prematurity, abnormal gut bacterial colonization, and formula feeding7. Recent studies implicate gut dysbiosis-driven immune dysregulation8 and intestinal barrier disruption9 as critical drivers of NEC progression; however, the molecular mediators linking these pathological processes remain poorly defined.

The interleukin-27 receptor α (IL-27Ra) forms a heterodimeric receptor complex with glycoprotein 130 to mediate signaling by the cytokine IL-27 (a heterodimer of p28 and EBI3 subunits)10. IL-27Ra signaling demonstrates functional pleiotropy in inflammatory diseases. For instance, in benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), it suppresses LPS/TLR4-induced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-8, thereby attenuating disease progression11. In contrast, in psoriasis, IL-27Ra exacerbates interferon-γ (IFN-γ)-mediated skin inflammation via the phosphorylated JAK2/STAT1-bet pathway12. This context-dependent duality suggests that IL-27Ra plays tissue and disease specific roles. Notably, single-cell and transcriptomic analyses reveal significant overexpression of IL-27Ra in intestinal immune cells of NEC patients, suggesting a potential role in NEC pathogenesis, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

Prior studies have highlighted the multifaceted roles of IL-27 in intestinal inflammation13. In inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), elevated IL-27/IL-27Ra levels in lesional tissues upregulate major histocompatibility complex class I (HLA-A/B) and class II (HLA-DR) molecules through IRF1-CIITA activation, thereby enhancing antigen-presenting capacity14. Additionally, IL-27 suppresses Th17-mediated mucosal inflammation by inhibiting the transcription factor RORγt transcriptional activity and reducing IL-17A/IL-17F production in naïve T cells15. In a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis model, gut microbiota metabolites activate IL-27Ra signaling in B cells, promoting IL-10⁺ regulatory T cell (Treg) 1 differentiation and mitigating intestinal immunopathology16. Moreover, IL-27 strengthens epithelial barrier integrity through STAT3/STAT6-dependent upregulation of tight-junction proteins occludin and claudin-317.

Despite these insights, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding IL-27Ra in NEC: (1) Does the immature intestinal immune microenvironment of preterm infants—characterized by hyperactive TLR4 signaling18 and a deficiency of Tregs19—skew IL-27Ra signaling toward a pro-inflammatory state? (2) Could targeting IL-27Ra simultaneously confer anti-inflammatory and barrier-protective effects? Addressing these questions is essential to the development of targeted therapies for NEC.

Based on this rationale, we hypothesize that IL-27Ra promotes NEC progression by amplifying the inflammatory cascade and compromising intestinal barrier function. To test this, we integrated analyses of clinical NEC specimens with experiments in genetically engineered mouse models. This approach aimed to determine whether IL-27Ra deletion can alleviate intestinal inflammation and preserve barrier integrity, thereby evaluating IL-27Ra as a potential therapeutic target for NEC.

Methods

Clinical sample collection and ethical approval

This study enrolled 7 neonates with surgically confirmed NEC (Bell’s stage ≥ II) and 7 gestational age-matched control (CTRL) neonates undergoing surgery for non-inflammatory conditions (e.g., intestinal atresia). Intestinal tissue specimens were obtained from Guangzhou First People’s Hospital and subjected to bulk transcriptomic sequencing analyses. The study protocol for investigating NEC pathogenesis in human samples was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangzhou First People’s Hospital (Approval No. K-2023-154-01). We confirm that all experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. Animal model establishment and tissue collection

C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) neonatal mice were obtained from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center, and IL-27Ra knockout mice (IL-27Ra−/−; B6N. 129P2-IL27ratm1Mak/J, 018078) were generously provided by Dr. Zhinan Yin’s research team at Jinan University. All animal procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Guangdong Huawei Testing Co., Ltd. under protocol number 202509001. All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Furthermore, the reporting of all methods and protocols in this study complies with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Pregnant dams were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 5% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to sterilized food and water. To establish an experimental model of NEC, 7-day-old neonatal mice were randomly assigned to either a dam-reared CTRL or an NEC induction group. Subjects in the NEC group were subjected to a daily regimen over four days consisting of the following interventions: intragastric administration of LPS (30 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich) at 8:00 AM after fasting, followed at 9:00 AM by artificial formula feeding (15 µL/g body weight). Hypoxic-ischemic and cold stress challenges were conducted at 11:00 AM and 4:30 PM, each involving 10 min of exposure to a hypoxic atmosphere (5% O₂/95% N₂), 10 min of recovery, and 10 min of cold stress at 4 °C. Additional feedings were provided at 3:00 PM and 9:00 PM (15 µL/g), with a second intragastric dose of LPS administered at 8:00 PM.

At the end of the induction period, pups were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia. Mice were anesthetized in an induction chamber pre-filled with 4% isoflurane (oxygen flow rate: 1.0 L/min) until loss of righting reflex (approximately 2 min), then transferred to a nose cone maintaining 2% isoflurane. Following confirmation of deep anesthesia (absence of pedal withdrawal reflex and corneal reflex), cervical dislocation was performed after sustained anesthesia for ≥ 5 min. Cardiac blood was collected, and the terminal 3 cm segment of the small intestine was harvested. Blood samples were preserved in EDTA-coated tubes. The proximal 1 cm segment was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The middle 1 cm segment was cryopreserved using 30% sucrose followed by OCT embedding for frozen sectioning. The distal 0.5 cm segment was longitudinally opened, rinsed to remove luminal contents, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored for RNA extraction and qPCR analysis.

Intestinal pathology was evaluated based on a validated NEC histological scoring system20, with five defined grades: Grade 0-no abnormality; Grade 1-mild separation of the lamina propria; Grade 2-moderate submucosal separation; Grade 3-severe submucosal separation and/or edema; Grade 4-transmural necrosis.

Immunofluorescence staining for il-27ra in mouse intestinal cryosections

Frozen intestinal sections were used to assess IL-27Ra protein expression. Terminal ileal tissues fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were cryoprotected in a sucrose—PBS gradient at 4 °C for 24 h. Following dehydration, tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, snap-frozen at − 80 °C, and stored for sectioning. Serial longitudinal cryosections were prepared using a cryostat and mounted onto poly-L-lysine-coated slides, which were stored at − 20 °C until further use.

For immunofluorescence, sections were equilibrated to room temperature and rinsed three times with PBS (5 min per wash). The staining area was delineated using a hydrophobic barrier pen. Sections were blocked with 5% goat serum at room temperature for 2 h and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified, dark chamber with rabbit anti-IL-27Ra (1:500 dilution in PBS, Bioss, Cot No. bs-2711R) primary antibody. After three PBS washes, sections were incubated with antibodies Goat anti-rabbit IgG1 H&L (Alexa Fluor® 594) (1:500 dilution in PBS, Abcam, Cat No. ab150080) at room temperature for 2 h in the dark. Slides were then washed, counterstained with DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium, and coverslipped.

Images were acquired using a a laser scanning confocal fluorescence microscope ZEN 2 (blue edition) with a 20 × objective. The excitation/emission wavelengths used were 405/450 nm for DAPI and 594/617 nm for Alexa Fluor 594. Quantification of IL-27Ra⁺ cells was performed using Image J software, expressed as a percentage of total DAPI⁺ cells.

ELISA for plasma IL-6 quantification

Cardiac blood was collected into EDTA-coated tubes and allowed to stand at 4 °C for 2 h. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 20 min, aliquoted into RNase/DNase-free microcentrifuge tubes, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Plasma IL-6 concentrations were quantified using a mouse interleukin-6 (IL-6) ELISA kit (Ruixin Biotech,Cat No. RX203049M) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a fully automated microplate reader, and IL-6 levels were determined via a standard curve fitted using logistic regression.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) for mouse intestinal tissue

Approximately 5 mg of intestinal tissue was used for total RNA extraction using a commercial RNA extraction kit (GOONIE, Cat No. 400–100-1kit). RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically (acceptable A260/A280 ratio: 1.8–2.1; A260/A230 > 2.0). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (TransGen Biotech, Cat No. AE341-02) from high-quality RNA.

Gene-specific primers were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST, targeting exon-exon junctions to avoid genomic DNA amplification. qPCR amplification was performed using SYBR Green chemistry (TransGen Biotech, Cat No. AQ631-02), with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences are listed in Table 2.

Processing and analysis of scRNA-seq data

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were obtained from resected intestinal tissues of NEC patients and control subjects4. The data were processed using the Cell Ranger pipeline (v4.0.0) to align reads and generate gene-cell count matrices. Cells with fewer than 200 expressed genes or genes detected in fewer than three cells were removed. After merging all samples in Seurat (v4.3.0, R v4.3.1), low-quality cells were filtered out based on the following criteria: < 1900 UMIs, < 1000 genes per cell, > 30% mitochondrial content, or > 10% erythrocyte marker expression. The Seurat object was normalized and scaled using the LogNormalize and ScaleData functions, respectively. Highly variable genes (n = 2000) were identified using FindVariableGenes, followed by principal component analysis (PCA). Clustering was performed using the top 20 principal components via the graph-based FindClusters function with a resolution of 0.3. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for dimensionality reduction using the RunUMAP function.

Cluster-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test via FindAllMarkers (parameters: min.pct = 0.25, only.pos = TRUE, logfc.threshold = 0.25, and p.adjust.method = “BH”). Clusters were annotated based on canonical marker gene expression.

Transcriptomic sequencing and analysis

Intestinal tissue samples were collected from 7 neonates with surgically confirmed NEC and 7 gestational age-matched controls diagnosed with congenital intestinal atresia. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat No. 12183555) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Ribosomal RNA was removed with the Ribo-Zero rRNA Removal Kit (Illumina, Cat No. 20040526), and libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, Cat No. RS-122-2001) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control and adapter trimming using fastp (v0.23.2; parameters: -q 20 -u 30 -l 50). Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using the STAR aligner (v2.7.10b) in two-pass mode. Gene expression was quantified using StringTie (v2.0), and batch effects were adjusted using ComBat_seq from the sva package (v3.50.0). Normalization was performed using the TMM method implemented in edgeR (v4.0.16).

Differential expression analysis was conducted using the quasi-likelihood F-test, with significance thresholds set at |log2 (fold change) |> 1 and FDR < 0.05. To assess the relationship between IL-27Ra expression and inflammatory markers (e.g., IL6, TNFα) as well as barrier-related genes (e.g., TJP1, OCLN), Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed. Correlation coefficients (ρ) and FDR-adjusted P-values were reported.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Two-group comparisons were made using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test, a non-parametric alternative to ANOVA, was used followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons (GraphPad Prism 10 software). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

IL-27Ra expression profile in NEC patients

We first examined the cellular distribution of IL-27Ra in the intestine using publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing datasets. Unsupervised clustering and UMAP visualization revealed that IL-27Ra expression was primarily enriched in T cells and natural killer (NK) cells within the intestinal immune compartment (Fig. 1A). Moreover, IL-27Ra mRNA levels were significantly upregulated in immune cell populations from NEC patient tissues compared to CTRL tissues (Fig. 1B).

Red arrows indicate T/NK cell subsets with high IL-27Ra expression. (B) IL-27Ra mRNA expression is significantly upregulated in immune cells from NEC patients compared to CTRL (violin plot).

Correlation of IL-27Ra with inflammatory and barrier genes

Bulk RNA-seq analysis of NEC tissue revealed that IL-27Ra expression was strongly associated with an inflammatory gene signature. IL-27Ra levels positively correlated with multiple pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-6, IL-1B, TNF, CXCL1, CXCL10, SAA1, IFNG, IL17RA, OSM, and S100A9 (Fig. 2A). Conversely, IL-27Ra was inversely correlated with expression of epithelial barrier — related genes such as CDH1, TJP1, and OCLN (Fig. 2B). These trends are summarized in the bubble plot (Fig. 2C), underscoring that high IL-27Ra expression is linked to a pro-inflammatory, barrier-disruptive molecular profile in NEC. Taken together, these findings suggest that IL-27Ra may contribute to NEC pathogenesis by modulating inflammatory signaling and impairing intestinal barrier integrity.

Cross-species validation of IL-27Ra upregulation

We next evaluated IL-27Ra expression in a neonatal mouse model of NEC to validate the clinical findings. Immunofluorescence staining of cryosections revealed markedly increased IL-27Ra protein expression in the intestinal tissues of NEC-WT mice induced by LPS, compared with the CTRL-WT group (Fig. 3A,B). Consistently, intestinal IL-27ra mRNA levels were significantly higher in NEC-WT mice than in CTRL-WT mice, as determined by qPCR (Fig. 3C). This cross-species consistency indicates that NEC triggers upregulation of IL-27Ra in the gut immune compartment both in humans and in experimental animals.

IL-27Ra knockout ameliorates NEC injury

To directly assess the role of IL-27Ra in NEC, we generated IL-27Ra-/- mice and subjected them to NEC induction (Fig. 4A). IL-27Ra deficiency conferred significant protection against NEC-associated intestinal injury. NEC-IL-27Ra−/− mice displayed markedly less mucosal damage on histology compared to NEC-WT mice, with preservation of villus and crypt architecture (Fig. 4B) and significantly lower injury scores (Fig. 4C). The loss of IL-27Ra also blunted the NEC-induced inflammatory response: NEC-IL-27Ra-/- mice showed a trend toward reduced plasma IL-6 levels (Fig. 4D), accompanied by significantly decreased IL-6 mRNA expression in intestinal tissue (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, intestinal tissues from NEC-IL-27Ra-/- mice exhibited a trend toward higher expression of the tight-junction genes TJP1 and OCLN compared with the NEC-WT group (Fig. 4F), suggesting improved barrier integrity. Collectively, these findings indicate that IL-27Ra may drive NEC pathogenesis by promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine production and compromising the intestinal epithelial barrier.

Discussion

Our findings reveal a novel pro-inflammatory role for IL-27Ra in NEC, in contrast to the protective effects that IL-27 signaling has shown in some adult intestinal disorders. This functional heterogeneity may be closely linked to the distinctive features of the intestinal immune microenvironment in premature infants—specifically, excessive TLR4 activation and a marked deficiency in Treg cells—which collectively may shift IL-27Ra signaling from an anti-inflammatory to a proinflammatory orientation. Supporting this concept, IL-27 has been shown to enhance TLR4 expression in human lung fibroblasts and bronchial epithelial cells, leading to increased IL-6 and IL-8 production during infection21. Additionally, Similarly, IL-27 upregulates TLR4 expression and signaling in human monocytes, thereby amplifying LPS-induced NF-κB–dependent inflammatory responses22. It is conceivable that in NEC, elevated IL-27Ra further upregulates TLR4 pathways in intestinal immune cells, creating a feed-forward loop that amplifies LPS-driven inflammation and tissue injury.

IL-27 signaling also has complex, context-dependent effects on Tregs. In a DSS-induced colitis model, IL-27/IL-27Ra engagement promotes the generation of IL-10—producing Treg 1 cells, which mitigate mucosal damage.

In contrast, IL-27 can exacerbate T cell—mediated colitis by inhibiting the conversion of naïve T cells into Foxp3+ Tregs23. NEC appears to parallel the latter scenario. Infants with NEC have significantly fewer Tregs (relative to effector T cells) compared to non-NEC infants19, and a skewed Th17/Treg balance is a hallmark of NEC24–26. Moreover, further investigations have identified a higher proportion of CCR9+CD4+ T cells in both NEC patients and murine models, which predominantly give rise to CCR9+ IL-17+ Treg cells. Although these cells exhibit phenotypic features similar to those of conventional Treg cells, their suppressive function is significantly compromised27,28. Collectively, these findings suggest that IL-27Ra signaling in NEC may restrict the conversion of naïve T cells into Treg cells, thereby diminishing Treg cell numbers or their functionality and ultimately promoting NEC progression.

Disruption of the intestinal barrier is a pivotal step in the pathogenesis of NEC. Under normal conditions, tight junction proteins (e.g., TJP1, OCLN) maintain epithelial integrity, thereby preventing the entry of harmful substances. Our findings demonstrate that elevated IL-27Ra expression is inversely correlated with the abundance of these barrier-related proteins, suggesting that IL-27Ra may compromise the intestinal barrier by downregulating tight junction proteins, thereby increasing gut permeability and advancing the onset and progression of NEC. Notably, our non-NEC IL-27Ra−/− group (CTRL-IL-27Ra−/−) exhibited a completely normal intestinal morphology at baseline (Fig. 4B), which was indistinguishable from that of their wild-type counterparts. This finding indicates that the absence of IL-27Ra does not alter intestinal development or homeostasis. This reinforces the conclusion that the protective effect observed in the NEC-IL-27Ra−/− group is specifically attributable to the amelioration of NEC-associated pathological processes, rather than to any pre-existing differences in their basal state.

From a therapeutic standpoint, we propose that modulating IL-27Ra, which can simultaneously curb inflammation and preserve the intestinal barrier, holds substantial clinical significance. Traditional NEC therapies, including antibiotics or surgical intervention, merely alleviate symptoms, whereas targeting IL-27Ra may offer a more fundamental correction of the underlying pathology. For example, in the absence of IL-27Ra, IL-27Ra-mediated phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT3 in T cells is eliminated, which may account for the ultimately favorable outcomes in Epstein-Barr virus-induced viral disease observed among patients lacking IL-27Ra29. This finding underscores the feasibility of this therapeutic approach. The development of neutralizing monoclonal antibodies or small-molecule inhibitors targeting IL-27/IL-27Ra for the prevention or treatment of NEC in high-risk preterm infants remains unrealized. Furthermore, the pronounced disparity in IL-27Ra expressiPNDon between control and NEC samples underscores its potential as a diagnostic biomarker.

This study has several limitations. First, the human sample size was limited, and all tissue samples were obtained from surgical interventions. While this provides unambiguous evidence of end-stage disease, it also introduces a selection bias toward severe cases. Consequently, our findings may not be generalizable across the full NEC spectrum—particularly to early-stage or medically managed infants. Future studies should prioritize the establishment of a longitudinal cohort that includes non-surgical NEC patients (Bell stage I/II), incorporating non-invasive serial monitoring (e.g., via stool or blood biomarkers) to capture the dynamic expression of IL-27Ra throughout disease progression. Such efforts will be crucial to validate IL-27Ra not only as a therapeutic target for advanced NEC but also as a potential non-invasive predictive biomarker enabling early intervention. Second, while our neonatal mouse model replicates many features of human NEC, it does not fully capture the complexity of the preterm infant environment (e.g., maternal factors, hospital-derived microbiota, and developmental immune differences). Third, our investigation focused on expression correlations and a knockout model; we did not delineate the detailed downstream signaling mechanisms by which IL-27Ra influences specific cell types or pathways in NEC. In particular, the epigenetic regulation of IL-27Ra and its interactions with the gut microbiome in NEC remain to be explored. Finally, although IL-27Ra deletion demonstrated therapeutic potential in mice, we did not test any IL-27/IL-27Ra-targeted treatment in humans. Future studies should involve larger clinical cohorts and preclinical trials (e.g., using IL-27Ra-neutralizing antibodies in animal models) to confirm the therapeutic value of targeting IL-27Ra in NEC and to further unravel the molecular mechanisms at play.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identifies IL-27Ra as a key pathological mediator in the progression of NEC, primarily through its dual roles in amplifying intestinal inflammation and compromising epithelial barrier integrity. These findings highlight IL-27Ra not only as a mechanistic contributor to disease pathogenesis but also as a promising therapeutic target. Strategic modulation of IL-27Ra signaling may offer a novel and dual-pronged approach to NEC treatment—attenuating excessive immune activation while concurrently restoring mucosal barrier function. This work lays a conceptual foundation for the development of IL-27Ra—targeted interventions, which merit further investigation in both preclinical and clinical settings.
