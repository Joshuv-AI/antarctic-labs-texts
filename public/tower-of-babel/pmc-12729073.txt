The association between smoking and diabetic neuropathy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sana Bader, Sanah Hasan, Dianna J. Magliano, Rakibul M. Islam, Shanika Palawaththa, Lorena Romero, Md. Nazmul Karim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe study aimed to systematically review the evidence on the association between smoking and the risk of diabetic neuropathy, with a particular emphasis on the risk of peripheral neuropathy among individuals with diabetes mellitus.Design and methodsMEDLINE, EMBASE, SCOPUS, and CENTRAL databases were searched for studies published before August 2024. Cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort studies that assessed the association between smoking and diabetic neuropathy in individuals diagnosed with diabetes mellitus (type 1 or type 2), reported smoking as an exposure, and any type of diabetic neuropathy as an outcome were included in the study. The risk of bias of the included studies was evaluated using the modified risk of bias in non-randomized studies—of exposures (ROBINS-E).ResultsSeventy-five articles, including 228,699 individuals with diabetes met the inclusion criteria. Fifty-four cross-sectional, 8 case–control, and 13 cohort studies provided data for the meta-analyses. Cohort studies demonstrated a significantly increased risk of diabetic peripheral neuropathy (RR 1.28; 95% CI 1.05, 1.51), the most prevalent subtype of diabetic neuropathy. Meta-analyses of cross-sectional and case–control revealed an increased risk of diabetic neuropathy (all types combined) among smokers with diabetes ([OR 1.29; 95% CI 1.17, 1.41], [OR 1.48; 95% CI 1.23, 1.72]). Meta-regression showed no statistically significant impact of total sample size and type of effect estimates on pooled effect estimates for cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort studies.ConclusionSmoking is associated with an increased risk of diabetic neuropathy, peripheral neuropathy in particular. Future research should evaluate the precise dynamics of neuropathy pathogenesis among individuals with diabetes to prevent such complications.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD42022363380.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-025-02995-w.

Introduction

The global prevalence of diabetes mellitus is expected to grow from 537 million in 2021 to 783 million in 2045 [1]. Diabetes-related deaths continue to increase around the globe [2–4], accounting for 12.2% of the global mortality from all causes in 2021 [1]. Diabetes complications have a significant impact on morbidity and mortality, with complications being classified as microvascular (e.g., nephropathy, neuropathy, and retinopathy) and macrovascular (e.g., coronary artery disease, peripheral artery disease, and cerebrovascular disease) [5]. Healthcare spending on diabetes and its related complications accounts for 11.5% of global health expenditures in 2021 [1].

Diabetic neuropathy is the most prevalent microvascular complication, affecting nearly 50% of individuals with long-term diabetes mellitus [6]. Diabetic neuropathy can be classified into two major types: peripheral neuropathy (also called distal sensory polyneuropathy) and autonomic neuropathy, which includes cardiac autonomic neuropathy [6, 7]. The variation in diagnostic methodologies and testing makes it challenging to accurately predict the prevalence of cardiac autonomic neuropathy, which ranges from 22.1 to 61.6% among individuals with type 2 diabetes [8, 9]. Other less common neuropathies include mononeuropathy (also called focal neuropathy) and proximal neuropathy [6, 7].

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy is the most common form of neuropathy, representing 75% to 90% of all neuropathy cases occurring among individuals with diabetes around the globe [10]. Individuals with diabetes may experience various signs and symptoms, including burning sensations, electrical pain, tingling, ankle reflex, and numbness [5]. Sensory loss to light touch, vibration, and temperature is the most common physical finding in diabetic peripheral neuropathy diagnosis [5]. Inadequate management of diabetic peripheral neuropathy could result in ulceration, infections, and potentially amputation of an affected foot [5]. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy contributes to lower quality of life, increases disability [11], and exacerbates the economic burden of diabetes [12–14].

While its debilitating impacts are relatively well documented, far less is known about the pathogenesis of diabetic neuropathy and its risk factors among individuals with diabetes. The link between smoking and the risk of diabetes [15], as well as its progression to complications, has been well studied.

Among individuals with diabetes, smoking has already been linked to poor metabolic control [16–19] and microvascular complications, such as retinopathy and nephropathy [20]. However, evidence regarding its association with neuropathy is scarce and inconclusive [21, 22]. Several studies have shown an increased risk of diabetic complications (macrovascular and microvascular) among smokers with diabetes. A previous systematic review published in 2015 reported the link between smoking and specifically diabetic peripheral neuropathy [23]. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to generate updated evidence and to expand the available evidence by investigating the association between smoking and different diabetic neuropathy types, with a particular focus on the most prevalent type, diabetic peripheral neuropathy.

Methods

Literature search

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [24] and was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the registration number CRD42022363380. The PRISMA checklist is available in additional file 1.

The literature was searched using MEDLINE, EMBASE, SCOPUS, and CENTRAL for articles published before August 2024. The results of the literature search were exported to Endnote Reference Manager Software 20, and duplicates were removed. The reference lists of relevant published literature were screened, and eligible articles were further examined (snowballing). Titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened against predetermined eligibility criteria. The search strategy consisted of four key concepts: one for diabetic smokers, one for diabetic peripheral neuropathy, one for the study design, and one for diabetic neuropathy diagnosis in individuals with diabetes. Boolean and proximity operators were used as appropriate to refine the search strategy. Medical subject headings (MeSH) terms related to smoking included the following: “smoking and smoking related phenomena,” “bidi smoking,” “pipe smoking,” “smoking,” “vaping,” “adolescent smoking,” “cigar smoking,” “cigarette smoking,” “smoking device,” “cigar,” “cigarette,” “electronic cigarette,” “hookah,” “water pipe smoking,” “tobacco smoking.” MeSH terms related to various types of neuropathy included the following: “neuropathy,” “autonomic neuropathy,” “mononeuropathy,” “mononeuropathy multiplex,” “neuritis,” “peripheral neuropathy,” “polyneuropathy,” “mononeuropathies,” “polyneuropathies,” “peripheral nervous system diseases,” “neuralgia,” “neuritis,” and those related to diabetic neuropathy included terms such as “diabetic neuropathies,” “diabetic neuropathy,” and “diabetic amyotrophy.” The search was restricted to peer-reviewed articles which were published in the English language. The full electronic search strategy is provided in additional file 2.

Study selection criteria

Three types of observational studies were included in this review including cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort studies. Specifically, those considered for inclusion were those that (1) investigated the association between smoking and diabetic neuropathy or any of its types among individuals with diabetes mellitus (type 1 and type 2), (2) reported diabetic neuropathy or any of its types as an outcome, and (3) reported smoking as one of the exposures being assessed.

Observational studies that involved a comparison/control group of individuals with diabetes with no diabetic neuropathy diagnosis were considered for inclusion. In addition, for cohort studies, we only included studies that involved follow-up of participants for a minimum duration of 1 year, as it has been hypothesized that 1 year is the minimum time required to develop neuropathy [23].

Interventional studies, animal studies, commentaries, brief communications and editorials, abstracts, and articles not written in English were excluded.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (SB, SP) and discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (MNK). A standardized data extraction checklist was developed, for collecting information relating to (1) authors name, publication year, and country; (2) study participants’ characteristics (diabetes type, and smoking exposure status); (3) total sample size; (4) effective sample size; and (5) presence or absence of diabetic neuropathy and type of diabetic neuropathy. For studies that reported multiple exposures and outcomes, only smoking was considered. The effect estimates were primarily generated manually in most studies based on the data provided, where studies did not provide estimates or numbers to allow manual calculations; adjusted estimates were extracted. In conducting a meta-analysis, sensitivity analyses were performed to determine whether the adjusted estimate had any impact on the study findings, particularly by determining the influence of potential confounders adjustments on the study findings.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias of the included studies was evaluated using a modified risk of bias in non-randomized studies—of exposures (ROBINS-E tool), designed and validated for nonrandomized studies [25]. The tool assessed the potential for risk of bias in seven different domains: (1) confounding variables, (2) measurement of exposure, (3) participant selection, (4) post-exposure intervention, (5) disclosure and treatment of missing data, (6) measurement of outcome, and (7) reporting of results. For each domain, a series of signaling questions built within an algorithm allows for a judgment to be made regarding the likely level of bias, ranging from low risk, some concerns, high risk, or very high risk of bias. The overall risk of bias judgment was based on the highest level of risk achieved across the seven domains. However, if more than two domains were at risk of bias, the study was considered to have a poorer rating (e.g., articles with more than two domains receiving some concerns were judged to be at a high risk of bias). The risk of bias of included studies was assessed independently by two reviewers (SB, SP), and discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer as required (RMI).

Modifications were made to the second and third domains (measurement of exposure * indicates limited assessment and participant selection) to better distinguish the levels of quality across the studies. Regarding the second domain, as most studies did not report smoking exposure in pack-years, but classified smokers as “current,” “current former,” “former,” and “never,” almost all included studies would receive a “some concerns” classification under the unmodified algorithm, and therefore, almost all studies would receive “some concerns” as an overall rating. Therefore, this criterion was modified such that if smoking status was reported as “current,” “former,” and “never,” the study would receive a low risk of bias score for this domain.

Data synthesis

Summary estimates were obtained through three meta-analyses, with studies stratified by their design: cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort studies. Restricted maximum likelihood random effects models were fitted to pool the effect estimates. For each separate meta-analysis, percentages of variability across studies attributable to heterogeneity beyond chance were estimated using the I2 statistic [26]. Sensitivity analyses, removal of studies with high and very high risks of bias, and adjusted estimates were conducted to assess the influence of study quality and characteristics on the pooled estimate. Subgroup analyses were performed based on diabetes mellitus type (type 1, type 2, or both types combined), diabetic neuropathy type (peripheral neuropathy, cardiac autonomic neuropathy, or any neuropathy), smoking status (current smoking, ever smoking, or never smoking), and risk of bias (low, some, high, or very high).

To examine the influence of study-level covariates on the risk of diabetic neuropathy, univariable and multivariable meta-regression models were fitted. For the multivariable model, all study-level covariates were entered simultaneously to assess their combined influence. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test to assess symmetry in the funnel plot [27]. Trim-and-fill analyses were used to adjust the odd ratio (ORs) for potential publication bias [28]. To assess heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed based on type of diabetes and diabetic neuropathy. All statistical analyses were performed using the Stata version SE/16 (StataCrop, College Station, TX, USA), and all tests were two-sided with a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

The PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process is outlined in Fig. 1. After removing duplicates, 2414 unique citations were retrieved from the database search. Screening titles and abstracts, 214 full texts were examined, resulting in the inclusion of 71 citations (Fig. 1). Four eligible articles were included through snowballing the reference list of the relevant published literature. A total of 75 studies were included in the meta-analysis, consisting of 228,699 participants, with diabetes mellitus (type 1 or type 2) constituting 54 cross-sectional studies [9, 22, 29–80], 8 case–control studies [81–88], and 13 cohort studies [21, 89–100]. Based on the ROBINS-E rating, 46 (61.3%) of the 75 included studies showed a low or some risk of bias, 25 (33.3%) showed a high risk, and 4 (5.3%) showed a very high risk of bias (Fig. 2). Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the relationship between smoking and diabetic neuropathy and its types.

The results demonstrated an increased risk of all types of diabetic neuropathy among individuals with diabetes who smoke compared to those who do not smoke. There were significant associations between smoking and the risk of all types of diabetic neuropathy in both cross-sectional and case–control studies. Although, in cohort studies, there was no significant increase in the overall risk of diabetic neuropathy among smokers in comparison to non-smoker individuals, a significant increase in the risk of diabetic peripheral neuropathy, the most prevalent type, was found among smokers with diabetes compared to their non-smoker counterparts.

The findings of this study suggest a link between smoking and diabetic neuropathy. Although tobacco smoking has been shown to increase the risk of developing type 2 diabetes and its related complications, the prevalence of smoking among individuals with diabetes is increasing [101–104]. Circumstantial evidence of a causal link between smoking and diabetic neuropathy can be inferred from studies that have used neuropathy as an outcome to assess the impact of smoking cessation. Voulgari et al. showed a reduced risk of polyneuropathy among individuals with diabetes who participated successfully in the smoking cessation program [105] and Chaturvedi et al. linked smoking absentia duration with a decreased risk of neuropathy [106].

A previous study revealed that long smoking duration is associated with more severe insulin resistance among individuals with type 2 diabetes [107]. A similar finding was reported in a 6-year follow-up cohort study that reported smoking duration as an independent risk factor for diabetic peripheral neuropathy among smokers with type 2 diabetes [108]. In our analysis, studies with moderate follow-up duration (5–10 years) of individuals with diabetes showed an increased risk; however, studies with the longest duration (> 10 years) of individuals with diabetes did not show this association. Lack of power might have resulted in such a paradox and is worth investigating further.

The progression of diabetic neuropathy due to tobacco smoking has become evident in the recent literature, with oxidative stress being the ultimate mechanism of cellular damage [109, 110]. Excessive free radicals released from tobacco smoke induce demethylation and axonal damage, leading to diabetic neuropathy [109, 111]. Glycotoxins (a major by-product formed when tobacco products are consumed) can similarly induce oxidative stress, leading to diabetic neuropathy [112–114]. A previous systematic review of global evidence revealed that tobacco smoke may be related to the development of diabetes mellitus and worsening of existing diabetic neuropathy by inducing insulin resistance [23], which is caused by nicotine, a major component of cigarette smoke [115]. Nicotine may lead to persistent hyperglycaemia by reducing sensitivity to insulin [116]. Among heavy smokers with diabetes who consume high levels of nicotine, a higher insulin dose may be required to regulate blood sugar levels [117].

Additionally, tobacco smoking may independently induce diabetic neuropathy through hypoxaemia and microvascular insufficiency [23] and has been shown to be associated with peripheral and autonomic neuropathy in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus [23, 118].

Smokers are more likely to report lower adherence to healthcare recommendations than their counterparts [119]. Their high tendency to exhibit unhealthy behaviors such as a poor diet high in fats and insufficient fruit and vegetable intake, lack of physical activity, and alcohol consumption contribute to poor glycaemic control which at present remains the only evidence-based means of preventing or easing diabetic neuropathy [120]. Contrasting evidence suggests that tight glycaemic control is insufficient for diabetic peripheral neuropathy prevention in individuals with type 2 diabetes [121]. This may explain the significant independent association of smoking observed in a few studies that accounted for diabetes control in their analysis. Therefore, it is crucial to consider all the contributing risk factors to effectively prevent diabetic neuropathy in this population.

This systematic review builds on existing evidence [23] while providing a more updated and robust assessment of the association between smoking and diabetic neuropathy. Unlike previous systematic review, this review utilized a different tool for assessing the risk bias. While the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) used in the previous review is an effective tool, it provides a more general quality assessment of the observational studies [122]. However, our use of the ROBINS-E tool adds to the robustness of our methodology by providing a comprehensive assessment of potential biases that may relate to specific exposure (smoking) measurement and confounding factors. This review was also restricted to published literature to ensure a standardized level of methodological quality, rigor, and to minimize biases that may arise from including unpublished literature. Although the exclusion of unpublished literature may result in an overestimation of the examined association, we investigated the presence of publication bias by performing funnel plots and Egger’s tests, and results from case–control and cohort studies did not indicate significant asymmetry or bias in the included studies.

Major strengths of this study included the strict inclusion and exclusion criteria and the comprehensive database search by an expert librarian and the research team. One limitation of this study was the variation in the exposure assessment among the included studies. There was variation in reporting the type of tobacco consumption among studies as well as among those who reported the methodology of smoking. Of these, 12 reported a clear definition of what constituted smoking, and 10 reported no clear definition of what constituted smoking. The definition of current smoking also varied across the studies and was reported as “smoked at least one cigarette daily,” “at least three cigarettes daily,” “one or more cigarettes daily,” etc. One study particularly categorized current smoking into daily consumption of less than 20 cigarettes or more than 20 cigarettes. There was variation in the “ever smoking” definition as well; studies have reported “smoked more than 100 cigarettes in a lifetime,” “smoked two or more cigarettes daily for a minimum of 12 months duration,” and “pack-year smoking.”

Furthermore, there were variations in the categorisation of smoking status and smoking assessment (e.g., self-reported questionnaires, medical testing, and medical records) across the included studies. Such variation in the exposure assessment could potentially introduce heterogeneity by affecting the consistency of the smoking-neuropathy relationship across the included studies. We conducted a subgroup analysis to explore the possible reasons for heterogeneity and conducted a meta-regression to adjust for these factors. However, the association between diabetic neuropathy and smoking status persisted, irrespective of the type of exposure assessment.

The studies included in our meta-analyses utilized different levels of control over confounding factors, duration of follow-up, and participant selection criteria. We extracted and pooled both adjusted and non-adjusted measures of associations, although the impact of pooling adjusted estimates did not reach statistical significance in meta-regression and a slight impact on heterogeneity is not unlikely. Another possible reason for the observed heterogeneity is the variation in the sample sizes of the included studies which ranged from small cohort studies to large population-based studies, although meta-regression revealed no significant impact of the sample size on the pooled estimate.

Moreover, data related to the confounder adjustment of specific clinical variables, such as diabetes duration, glycaemic control, and smoking duration, in the assessment of smoking and diabetic neuropathy association could not be established. The limited provision and inconsistencies in the reporting of these data across all the included studies made it challenging to include them in our meta-regression. This was considered when assessing the risk of bias in the first domain: bias due to confounders, in which adjustments for age, sex, and diabetes duration influenced the overall rating of this domain. Despite this limitation, the extracted adjusted estimates consistently showed a significant association, and the meta-regression confirmed that the adjusted estimates had no impact on the study outcome which contributes to the robustness of our findings.

Additionally, this review included all relevant published studies, regardless of the publication year. Despite constant advancements in healthcare, it is still unlikely that the biological mechanism between smoking and diabetic neuropathy will change over the decades. To address temporal changes, we investigated subgroups based on the study period using meta-regression (see Additional File 4) to explore the potential impact of publication year. We also conducted sensitivity analyses to evaluate the influence of individual studies, and the findings remained robust, regardless of the study publication year.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated a possible association between smoking and diabetic neuropathy, as well as diabetic peripheral neuropathy among individuals with diabetes. Further prospective studies with adequate sample sizes and robust epidemiological designs, large cohort studies in particular, are required. Understanding the relationship between smoking and diabetic neuropathy is crucial to guide effective public health interventions aimed at decreasing the risk of diabetic neuropathy as well as other complications of diabetes.

Implications for future research

Future research could also focus on implementing and assessing a personalized smoking cessation program specifically targeted to this group of patients. The studies reviewed in this study advocated the design of personalized smoking cessation programs for individuals with diabetes and championed for their efficacy and effectiveness compared to standard programs designed for the general population. Future studies could target smokers with diabetes mellitus group of patients, conduct in-depth interviews with them, and assess facilitators and barriers to smoking cessation from their perceptions.
