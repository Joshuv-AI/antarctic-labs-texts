Association between smile arc curvature and skeletal pattern in orthodontic patients: a retrospective cross-sectional analytical study
Authors: Atheer Ganem, Meelaf Alshahrani, Noura Abumelha, Nahar Alqahtani, Rima Albishri, Lina Hafiz, Wajd AlDhafer, Norah Alamri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the association between smile arc curvature, skeletal pattern, and maxillary incisor display among orthodontic patients attending King Khalid University College of Dentistry. This retrospective cross-sectional analytical study included 150 eligible orthodontic patients. Standardized pretreatment posed smile photographs and lateral cephalometric radiographs were analyzed. Smile arc curvature was classified as consonant, flat, or reverse. Associations with sagittal skeletal pattern (ANB), vertical skeletal pattern (SN–MP), and maxillary incisor display were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Multinomial and binary logistic regression analyses were performed to explore adjusted associations. Consonant smile arc was the most prevalent pattern (63.3%). No significant associations were observed with sagittal skeletal pattern or maxillary incisor display. A significant association with vertical skeletal pattern was identified in the unadjusted analysis; however, this association was not maintained after multivariable adjustment. Smile arc morphology appears to be multifactorial. Although an unadjusted association with vertical skeletal patterns was observed, no independent statistically significant associations were identified after adjustment. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the factors influencing smile arc morphology.

Introduction

Smile esthetics are an important component of contemporary orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. The smile arc, defined as the relationship between the curvature of the maxillary incisal edges and the contour of the lower lip during smiling, is an important component of smile attractiveness (Sarver 2001). Consonant or parallel smile arcs are generally perceived as more esthetically favorable than flat or reverse configurations (Parekh et al. 2007, 3 et al. 2021, Horn et al. 2021). Smile arc morphology is multifactorial and may be influenced by dental position, facial proportions, lip dynamics, and treatment-related factors (Sarver 2001, 3 et al. 2021, Ackerman and Ackerman 2002, Mackley 1993).

The relationship between smile arc curvature and skeletal morphology remains unclear. Recent studies have identified differences in selected smile characteristics among skeletal patterns, but these differences are not consistent across all smile components. Pandey et al. found significant differences in lip-line measurements among skeletal groups but no significant association with smile arc (Pandey et al. 2024), whereas Nouh et al. reported differences in smile characteristics between skeletal Class I and Class III females (Nouh et al. 2021). Three-dimensional perceptual research has also shown that skeletal pattern interacts with dentoalveolar and treatment-related factors in determining smile attractiveness (Kreichati et al. 2024). Collectively, these findings suggest that skeletal morphology alone may not adequately explain variation in smile arc curvature.

Evidence specifically addressing the relationship between smile arc curvature and skeletal pattern remains limited, particularly in Middle Eastern populations. To the best of our knowledge, this association has not been specifically investigated among Saudi orthodontic patients. Population-specific evaluation may therefore improve understanding of smile esthetics and support individualized orthodontic diagnosis and treatment planning. Therefore, this retrospective cross-sectional analytical study aimed to evaluate the association between smile arc curvature and skeletal pattern, assessed using the ANB angle for sagittal classification and the SN–MP angle for vertical classification, among orthodontic patients attending the Orthodontic Clinics at King Khalid University College of Dentistry (KKUCOD). The null hypothesis was that no significant association exists between smile arc curvature and sagittal or vertical skeletal pattern.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cross-sectional analytical study was conducted using pretreatment orthodontic records of patients attending the Orthodontic Clinics at King Khalid University College of Dentistry (KKUCOD), Abha, Saudi Arabia. Records of patients treated between September 2022 and March 2026 were retrospectively retrieved from the archived electronic database. All eligible patient records that met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria during the study period were consecutively included in the study. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Pretreatment frontal smile photographs and lateral cephalometric radiographs were retrospectively retrieved from the archived patient records to evaluate the association between smile arc curvature, incisor display and skeletal pattern.

All frontal smile photographs had been obtained according to the standardized photographic protocol routinely followed at the Orthodontic Clinics of King Khalid University College of Dentistry. Patients were photographed in a natural head position while looking directly at the camera, and a posed social smile was requested. Standardization of head posture, lip position, and photographic conditions was maintained to minimize variations that could influence smile arc assessment. For each patient, the standardized pretreatment frontal smile photograph routinely obtained during the initial orthodontic records appointment was used for analysis. Only photographs with adequate visualization of the maxillary dentition and smile arc were included, whereas photographs of insufficient quality or inadequate visualization were excluded according to the predefined eligibility criteria.

Because this was a retrospective study based on archived clinical photographs, a calibration scale was not available. Therefore, maxillary incisor display was categorized according to predefined clinical ranges rather than relying on precise linear measurements, and all assessments were performed under the same standardized photographic conditions.

Study population and sample size

The study population consisted of orthodontic patients with complete pretreatment diagnostic records that met the predefined eligibility criteria. Patient records were retrospectively screened from the electronic database of the Orthodontic Clinics at King Khalid University College of Dentistry during the study period. All available records were assessed for eligibility according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible patients with complete pretreatment records, including standardized frontal smile photographs and diagnostic-quality lateral cephalometric radiographs, were included in the final analysis. The participant selection process, including the numbers of records screened, excluded, and the reasons for exclusion, is presented in Fig. 1. Individual age and sex data were not available in the archived retrospective dataset and therefore could not be included in the statistical analyses.

Sample size was estimated using G*Power software (version 3.1; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) based on a chi-square test of association between smile arc curvature (three categories) and skeletal pattern (three categories). A moderate effect size (Cramer’s V = 0.30) was assumed because no previous studies evaluating this specific association were available to provide an empirical estimate. Assuming a significance level of α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 108 participants. To allow for potential exclusions and maximize the number of eligible participants, a target sample size of 150 participants was selected. Because some contingency table cells contained small, expected frequencies, Fisher’s exact test was applied where appropriate to ensure the validity of the statistical analyses.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients aged 12–30 years with available pre-treatment frontal smile photographs and diagnostic-quality lateral cephalometric radiographs were included. This age range reflects the typical age of patients seeking comprehensive orthodontic treatment at our institution. Because this was a retrospective study, skeletal maturity was not assessed. Therefore, the potential influence of residual craniofacial growth in younger participants could not be evaluated and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Only patients with permanent dentition (excluding third molars) were considered. Patients with craniofacial anomalies, syndromic conditions, history of orthodontic or orthognathic treatment, missing or restored maxillary anterior teeth affecting smile esthetics, or poor-quality records were excluded.

Data collection procedures

Smile arc assessment

Smile arc curvature was evaluated from standardized pretreatment posed frontal smile photographs and classified into three categories: consonant, flat, or reverse, based on the relationship between the curvature of the maxillary incisal edges and the contour of the lower lip, as previously described (Sarver 2001, Ackerman and Ackerman 2002). A consonant smile arc was recorded when the curvature of the maxillary incisal edges closely paralleled the contour of the lower lip during the posed smile. A flat smile arc was recorded when little or no curvature was observed, whereas a reverse smile arc was recorded when the curvature of the maxillary incisal edges was opposite to the contour of the lower lip. All classifications were performed by visual assessment under standardized photographic conditions using the predefined classification criteria.

Incisor display assessment

Maxillary incisor display was visually assessed at the midline from standardized pretreatment smile photographs and clinically categorized as minimal, average, or excessive incisor display according to previously reported clinical criteria (Dong et al. 1999). These categories correspond to commonly used clinical ranges (minimal: approximately 0–1 mm, average: approximately 2–4 mm, and excessive: >4 mm). Because the archived photographs were not calibrated for linear measurements, these categories represented standardized clinical classifications based on visual assessment rather than precise millimeter measurements. All assessments were performed under standardized photographic conditions using predefined clinical classification criteria.

Cephalometric analysis

Lateral cephalometric radiographs were digitally traced to determine skeletal relationships. The ANB angle was used to classify sagittal skeletal pattern into Class I (1–4°), Class II (> 4°), and Class III (< 1°), according to established cephalometric standards (Dong et al. 1999). The SN–MP angle was defined as the angle between the Sella–Nasion plane and the Go-Me mandibular plane, it used to evaluate vertical skeletal pattern and categorized as low (< 30°), normal (30–37°), or high (> 37°), based on established norms (Steiner 1953).

Examiner calibration and reliability assessment

Prior to data collection, four orthodontic interns underwent standardized calibration and training under the supervision of the corresponding author for smile arc classification, incisor display assessment, and cephalometric classification according to predefined diagnostic criteria. To assess intra-examiner reliability, each examiner independently reassessed five randomly selected patient records after a two-week interval, resulting in a total of 20 repeated assessments. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated for each study variable. Inter-examiner reliability was assessed by having each examiner independently reassess five randomly selected records that had previously been evaluated by a different examiner. Thus, 20 paired assessments were included in the inter-examiner reliability analysis. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated based on these paired assessments before any discussion or consensus. Because the statistical analyses were based on categorized ANB and SN–MP variables rather than continuous angular measurements, agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, which is appropriate for categorical data. Any discrepancies identified during the main study were resolved by consensus only after completion of the reliability analysis.

Data management and confidentiality

To ensure patient confidentiality, all data were collected from archived electronic orthodontic records and anonymized prior to analysis. Each participant was assigned a unique study identification code, and all personal identifiers were removed before data extraction. Frontal smile photographs were cropped to the perioral region to prevent patient identification, and no identifiable patient information was retained during data collection or analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (Version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Associations between smile arc curvature and skeletal pattern (ANB and SN-MP classifications) and incisor display were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square test. Fisher’s exact test was applied when the expected cell counts were insufficient to satisfy the assumptions of the chi-square test. Multinomial logistic regression and binary logistic regression analyses were performed to explore adjusted associations between smile arc curvature and the study variables. Multinomial logistic regression was used because smile arc curvature comprised three outcome categories (consonant, flat, and reverse). Binary logistic regression was subsequently performed as a secondary exploratory analysis by combining the flat and reverse smile arc categories into a single non-consonant category as a secondary exploratory analysis to further explore the observed associations. Because the analyses were exploratory and based on prespecified hypotheses, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Therefore, the findings, particularly those from the regression analyses, should be interpreted cautiously. For chi-square analyses, Cramer’s V was calculated to estimate the strength of the association. Measurement reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 150 orthodontic patients were included in the analysis. The distribution of study variables is summarized in Table 1. Consonant smile arc was the most prevalent pattern (63.3%), followed by flat (20.0%) and reverse smile arc (16.7%). The majority of participants exhibited average incisor display (60.0%), while minimal and excessive display were observed in 23.3% and 16.7% of cases, respectively.

Regarding sagittal skeletal classification, Class II pattern was the most common (47.3%), followed by Class I (36.7%) and Class III (16.0%). For vertical skeletal pattern, 46.0% of patients exhibited a normal mandibular plane angle, 43.3% a high angle, and 10.7% a low angle (Table 1). The reliability analysis demonstrated substantial to almost perfect agreement for all study variables (Table 2). Intra-examiner Cohen’s kappa coefficients ranged from 0.897 to 1.000, whereas inter-examiner kappa coefficients ranged from 0.675 to 1.000. Almost perfect agreement was observed for smile arc classification (κ = 0.897 intra-examiner; κ = 0.718 inter-examiner), while perfect agreement was achieved for incisor display and vertical skeletal pattern classifications in both analyses.

Multinomial logistic regression analysis

Multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed using consonant smile arc as the reference category to evaluate the influence of incisor display, sagittal skeletal pattern (ANB), and vertical skeletal pattern (SN–MP) on smile arc type (Table 6). The overall model was not statistically significant (χ² (Alhadlaq 2016) = 18.96, p = 0.089; Nagelkerke pseudo-R² = 0.142), indicating limited model fit. As Nagelkerke pseudo-R² is a measure of model fit rather than the proportion of variance explained, it should be interpreted cautiously and is not directly comparable to the R² statistic used in linear regression. Although the SN–MP angle showed a statistically significant association in one comparison within the model, this isolated finding should be interpreted cautiously because the overall model fit was not significant. In addition, sparse data across several outcome categories may have reduced the stability and precision of the regression estimates. Therefore, no independent predictor of smile arc type was identified after adjustment for the study variables, and the regression findings should be considered exploratory rather than confirmatory.

Binary logistic regression analysis

Due to the relatively low frequencies of flat (20.0%) and reverse (16.7%) smile arcs, smile arc was dichotomized into consonant and non-consonant (flat and reverse) categories to improve statistical stability in the binary logistic regression analysis. This categorization was adopted solely for statistical modeling purposes and should not be interpreted as implying that flat and reverse smile arcs are clinically or biologically equivalent. Binary logistic regression was then performed (Table 7).

The overall model was not statistically significant (χ² (Mackley 1993) = 5.8, p = 0.446), indicating that the included predictors did not collectively explain variation in smile arc type.

Incisor display showed no significant association with non-consonant smile arc, with both minimal (OR = 0.83, p = 0.739) and average (OR = 0.78, p = 0.618) categories demonstrating no significant effect.

Similarly, sagittal skeletal pattern (ANB) was not a significant predictor; however, Class II pattern showed a borderline trend toward reduced odds of non-consonant smile arc (OR = 0.41, p = 0.076).

Vertical skeletal pattern (SN–MP angle) also did not demonstrate a statistically significant association, with both low (OR = 1.61, p = 0.427) and normal (OR = 1.40, p = 0.380) categories showing non-significant effects.

None of the evaluated predictors reached statistical significance in the binary logistic regression model (Table 7).

Discussion

The present study evaluated the association between smile arc curvature and skeletal pattern among orthodontic patients. Consonant smile arc was the most prevalent pattern, while no significant associations were identified with sagittal skeletal pattern or incisor display. Although vertical skeletal pattern showed a significant association in the unadjusted analysis, this relationship was not maintained after multivariable adjustment. These findings reinforce the multifactorial nature of smile arc morphology and suggest that conventional skeletal parameters alone may have limited ability to predict smile arc configuration and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

The predominance of consonant smile arcs is consistent with previous literature and contemporary evidence indicating that a consonant or parallel relationship between the maxillary incisal curvature and lower lip is generally perceived as esthetically favorable [1. 2, 3, 4]. Current evidence also emphasizes that smile esthetics reflects the interaction of multiple dental, skeletal, and soft-tissue characteristics rather than a single anatomical factor (3 et al. 2021, Ackerman and Ackerman 2002). However, this finding should be interpreted as a descriptive observation of the present sample rather than evidence that natural dentofacial relationships inherently favor harmonious smile curvature.

In the present study, no statistically significant association was found between smile arc curvature and incisor display. The relatively similar distribution of smile arc categories across all levels of maxillary incisor display further supports this finding, suggesting that variations in incisor display alone were not associated with differences in smile arc morphology within the present sample. Although incisor display is an important component of smile esthetics, its influence depends on its interaction with incisor position, lip dynamics, gingival display, and facial proportions (3 et al. 2021, Dong et al. 1999). The present findings therefore suggest that the amount of visible maxillary incisor alone may not determine smile arc configuration. However, maxillary incisor display was visually classified into predefined clinical categories under standardized photographic conditions rather than measured from calibrated images. Consequently, these categories should be interpreted as clinical classifications rather than precise linear measurements, which may have reduced the sensitivity for detecting subtle differences between groups. In addition, smile arc is a dynamic characteristic influenced by lip curvature, muscular activity, and other soft-tissue factors that may not be fully captured in static photographic assessments (Pandey et al. 2024).

Similarly, no significant association was observed between smile arc curvature and sagittal skeletal pattern (ANB Classification). This finding is consistent with recent evidence showing that differences in skeletal morphology do not necessarily correspond to differences in smile arc configuration. Pandey et al. reported differences in selected smile components among skeletal patterns but found no significant association with smile arc,⁷ whereas Nouh et al. identified differences in several smile characteristics between skeletal Class I and Class III females (Nouh et al. 2021). Together, these findings suggest that sagittal skeletal classification alone provides limited information regarding smile arc morphology.

In contrast, a statistically significant association was identified between smile arc curvature and vertical skeletal pattern in the unadjusted analysis. However, this association was not maintained after adjustment in the multivariable regression model. Within the present sample, flat smile arcs were most frequently observed among patients with a normal vertical skeletal pattern, whereas reverse smile arcs were more common among those with high mandibular plane angles. Consonant smile arcs showed a relatively balanced distribution across the normal and high vertical pattern groups. These findings suggest that vertical morphology may contribute to smile characteristics but is unlikely to act independently of other dentoalveolar and soft-tissue factors. Contemporary evidence similarly indicates that smile appearance varies with the interaction of skeletal morphology, incisor position, and other treatment-related variables (Pandey et al. 2024, Kreichati et al. 2024). Therefore, the observed relationship should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect the influence of additional unmeasured factors rather than an independent effect of vertical skeletal morphology. This finding highlights the multifactorial nature of smile esthetics, in which skeletal morphology interacts with dental positioning, lip dynamics, and soft-tissue characteristics (Sarver and Ackerman 2003). In addition, the unequal distribution of participants across some study categories may have reduced statistical power and limited the stability of the regression analyses. Consequently, no independent statistically significant associations with smile arc morphology were identified after adjustment.

Clinically, these findings highlight an important distinction between skeletal diagnosis and smile diagnosis. ANB and SN–MP remain useful cephalometric parameters, but they should not be used to infer smile arc morphology. Patients with similar skeletal classifications or levels of incisor display may present with different smile arcs; therefore, direct smile-arc assessment should complement cephalometric evaluation during diagnosis, treatment planning, and finishing, particularly when planning vertical incisor positioning. This study also provides population-specific evidence from Saudi orthodontic patients, contributing to the limited contemporary evidence on the relationship between skeletal morphology and smile arc. Although orthodontic mechanics can influence smile-arc characteristics (Sarver 2001, 3 et al. 2021, Sarver and Ackerman 2003), treatment-related changes were not evaluated in the present study.

Despite these limitations, the present study has several strengths. It was based on standardized pretreatment orthodontic records obtained using a consistent institutional photographic protocol, thereby reducing variability in image acquisition. Examiner calibration, together with both intra- and inter-examiner reliability assessments, enhanced the consistency of the categorical evaluations. Furthermore, the use of multivariable regression analyses allowed the association between smile arc curvature and skeletal pattern to be explored while accounting for potential confounding variables. Finally, this study provides population-specific data from Saudi orthodontic patients, contributing to the limited evidence regarding the relationship between smile arc curvature and skeletal morphology in this population.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

First, the retrospective cross-sectional analytical design precludes causal inference and limited the availability of certain demographic and clinical variables. The inclusion of patients aged 12–30 years may have introduced variability related to residual craniofacial growth, particularly among younger participants. Because skeletal maturity was not assessed and individual age and sex data were unavailable for adjusted analyses, the potential influence of growth on skeletal pattern, lip posture, maxillary incisor display, and smile dynamics could not be evaluated. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Second, smile arc assessment was based on archived standardized frontal smile photographs, which may not fully capture the dynamic nature of smiling. Because the study relied on retrospective clinical records, smile intensity and reproducibility could not be objectively standardized or quantified, and variations in individual smile effort cannot be completely excluded. In addition, a calibration scale was not available in the archived photographs. To minimize this limitation, all photographs had been obtained using a standardized institutional photographic protocol, and maxillary incisor display was visually classified into predefined clinical categories rather than measured from calibrated images. Consequently, some degree of misclassification cannot be completely excluded despite the use of standardized photographic conditions.

Third, several variables known to influence smile arc morphology, including maxillary incisor inclination and vertical position, incisal edge curvature, lower lip curvature, upper lip length, lip thickness, lip mobility, smile width, occlusal plane characteristics, dental compensation, and facial vertical proportions, were not available in the retrospective dataset and therefore could not be included in the analyses. Consequently, residual confounding or mediation by these unmeasured factors cannot be excluded.

In addition, cephalometric reliability was assessed only after categorization of ANB and SN–MP into clinical groups. Because the original continuous angular measurements were not available for repeated quantitative analysis, measurement error using intraclass correlation coefficients or Dahlberg’s error could not be evaluated. Therefore, measurement variability in cases close to the classification cutoff values cannot be excluded.

Finally, the unequal distribution of participants across some smile arc and skeletal pattern subgroups resulted in sparse data, which may have reduced statistical power and limited the stability of the regression analyses. In addition, because the study was conducted at a single university orthodontic clinic in Abha, the findings should not be considered representative of the Saudi population as a whole, and caution is warranted when generalizing the results to other populations or clinical settings. Future prospective multicenter studies including larger and more diverse populations, standardized dynamic smile analysis, three-dimensional imaging, comprehensive demographic data, and assessment of additional dental and soft-tissue variables are recommended to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing smile arc morphology.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this retrospective cross-sectional analytical study, no independent statistically significant association was identified between smile arc curvature and skeletal pattern after adjustment for the study variables. Although vertical skeletal pattern showed a significant association in the unadjusted analysis, this finding was not maintained in the multivariable regression model. These findings suggest that smile arc morphology is likely influenced by multiple interacting skeletal, dental, and soft-tissue factors rather than skeletal pattern alone. Further prospective multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations are warranted to better understand the determinants of smile arc morphology.
