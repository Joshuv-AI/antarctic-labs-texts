CAR-NK cell therapy in multiple myeloma: from preclinical and clinical landscape to joining the force for treatment strategies optimization
Authors: Somayeh Yazdanparast, Mehdi Bakhtiyaridovvombaygi, Zeinab Davoodi-Moghaddam, Gelayol Asadi, Fatemeh Monjezi, Pegah Kiyamehr, Ahmad Gharehbaghian, Saeid Abroun, Nahid Moradi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Natural killer (NK) cells are specialized components of the innate immune system with an essential role in immune surveillance against multiple myeloma (MM). However, patients with MM often exhibit significant impairments in both the quantity and functionality of NK cells, particularly those who are refractory or have relapsed disease. Increasing evidence suggests that harnessing NK cells may provide a promising “off-the-shelf” therapeutic strategy for treating MM. Recent advancements in chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) engineering have enhanced the capabilities of NK cells, allowing them to target MM-associated antigens effectively. This innovative approach may help mitigate the off-target toxicities often associated with NK cells and address challenges such as the ability of MM cells to evade immune detection. CAR-NK therapy shows promise for B-cell malignancies, but its efficacy in MM has been less examined. This comprehensive review revealed that CAR-NK cells elicit a robust immune response against myeloma cells in preclinical studies conducted both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, the development of dual-CAR NK cell therapies indicates promise in overcoming myeloma escape mechanisms. Numerous clinical trials are currently underway to evaluate the efficacy and safety of CAR-NK cell therapy for individuals diagnosed with MM.

First, NK cells as the frontline in the fight against cancer and exert a rapid and potent response; Second, NK cells display inner or immunocompromised tumor microenvironment (TME)-derived impairment in the MM milieu; Third, conventional NK cell therapy emerged as the off-the-shelf, is safe, effective, and relatively cost-efficient cell-based immunotherapy for patients with MM; Fourth, harnessing CAR-NK cell reduced NK cell off-target cytotoxicity; Finally, CAR-NK cell platform is a groundbreaking approach tailored to treat MM.

Background

A clonal plasma cell (PC) proliferation and accumulation within the bone marrow (BM) microenvironment is defined as Multiple Myeloma (MM) [1]. MM is the second most common hematological cancer, a genetically multifaceted condition, which is considered an incurable disease [2]. MM-affected patients require multiple therapeutic regimens to achieve prolonged survival [3].

The chemotherapy drugs used to manage MM involve immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs), proteasome inhibitors (PIs), and histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDIs) [3, 4]. Pharmacological approaches followed by autologous hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation (ASCT) render optimal overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) rates [5]. However, long-term disease control has failed since the majority of MM patients eventually develop drug resistance, and patients with positive minimal residual disease (MRD) frequently experience disease relapse [6]. The second ASCT and combination therapy, such as combining IMiDs with PIs or monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), including daratumumab (targeting CD38) and elotuzumab (targeting SLAMF7 glycoprotein), and PIs with mAbs or HDIs, may be helpful in relapsed/refractory MM (R/RMM) patients [4]. Recently, bispecific antibodies, antibody–drug conjugates, and cell-based therapy, such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells and Natural killer (NK) cells, have also been introduced [3, 7].

Consequently, current MM treatments have yet to achieve complete disease control and have been accompanied by undesirable side effects. As a result, a new therapeutic approach must be employed, combining them with standard therapy according to the stage of the disease to achieve prolonged survival. In this regard, focusing on the tumor microenvironment (TME) is highly suggested as a major contributor to disease development. MM cells reside in immunocompromised cellular and non-cellular TME, which contribute to their proliferation and resistance to anti-tumor therapies. NK cells are pivotal innate immune cells within the MM niche, exhibiting quantitative and functional dysregulation among the various immune components. Thus, restoring the number and function of circulating NK cells may hold promise for the treatment of MM [8]. Furthermore, utilizing CAR-engineered NK cells enables them to mediate cytotoxicity toward MM-specific antigens and reduce off-target cytotoxicity [9].

This comprehensive review offers valuable insights into the biology and function of NK cells, the process of CAR manufacturing, and the challenges associated with NK cell impairment in MM. Furthermore, both preclinical and clinical research regarding MM have been thoroughly examined, encompassing retrospective to prospective studies.

Multiple Myeloma (MM)

The origin of MM cells is traced back to a B-lymphocyte that acquires abnormal genomic alterations in the germinal centers. Abnormal genomic alterations are considered off-target events during somatic hypermutation and class-switch recombination processes, which are mediated by activation-induced deaminases [1]. The disease exhibits a particularly high prevalence among older adults, notably affecting those aged 70 to 74 years [10], and may also occur in younger individuals (< 50 years), although with better outcomes [11]. There are approximately 86,000 new cases of this disease diagnosed annually around the globe, which accounts for 0.8% of all newly diagnosed cancer cases. Additionally, this disease is responsible for 63,000 fatalities each year, representing 0.9% of all cancer-related deaths [12].

Patients diagnosed with MM often exhibit a range of clinical manifestations, such as hypercalcemia, anemia, renal failure, increased susceptibility to infections, and pathological fractures secondary to osteolytic bone destruction [13, 14]. The loss of bone mass in MM is complex and multifactorial, primarily caused by an imbalance among osteoclasts, osteoblasts, osteocytes, and BM stromal cells [15, 16].

There are three phases of MM: monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), a smoldering phase (asymptomatic), and an active phase (symptomatic) [17]. A key criterion for MGUS diagnosis includes a PCs population of less than 10% within the BM. When the PC content exceeds 10%, the condition may progress to either smoldering MM (SMM) or MM, depending on the presence of clinical manifestations [18]. In addition, SMM, as an intermediate clinical stage between MGUS and MM, is defined by elevated serum or urinary monoclonal protein and PCs within a range of 10% to 60%. Moreover, SMM occurs in the absence of additional myeloma-defining events, including hypercalcemia, renal insufficiency, anemia, or the presence of bone lesions [19, 20].

Understanding disease progression and therapeutic responses to MM depends on understanding the immune cell microenvironment. Recent research has revealed significant TME alterations as the disease progresses from precursor stages to more advanced MM, highlighting the intricate relationships between tumor cells and immune components. Understanding these dynamics is essential for developing effective immunotherapies [21–25]. The TME in MM is characterized by a reduction in granulocytes, diminished expression of HLA-DR on CD16+ monocytes and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), as well as a shift in CD8+ T cells and NK cells toward a more inhibitory phenotype [26]. The early stages of disease progression are characterized by an immunosuppressive environment that tends to worsen with time, eventually contributing to resistance to immunotherapy [25]. Furthermore, understanding the complex interactions between the different components of the TME, such as stromal cells, extracellular matrix, and immune cells, can lead to important insights. By applying this understanding, strategies can be developed to eliminate resistance and enhance patient response to treatment, ultimately inspiring hope and progress in the fight against disease.

An overview of cell-based immunotherapy in MM

Cell-based therapies are a revolutionary approach in modern medicine, employing living cells to treat various diseases and conditions [27]. MM is one of the diseases for which new cell-based therapeutic strategies have been developed in the past decade. These novel approaches include utilizing allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) [28, 29], T cells, NK cells, DCs, marrow-infiltrating lymphocytes (MILs) [30, 31], gamma/delta-(γδ) T lymphocytes [32], and genetically engineered CAR-T, CAR-NK, and engineered T cell receptors (TCRs) [30, 31]. Furthermore, products derived from native or engineered immune cells, such as exosomes and microvesicles, are promising and have garnered attention in medical research for MM treatment [33].

Although allo-HSCT may have a favorable graft-versus-myeloma (GVM) effect, resulting in long-term survival or possibly remission, its treatment-related toxicities (TRTs), such as graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) and treatment-related mortality (TRM), reduce its effectiveness [34]. Furthermore, in high-risk and R/RMM patients, allo-HSCT can be therapeutically effective in approximately one-third of patients. Therefore, the utilization of allo-HSCT is determined by an extensive evaluation of individual patient factors and responses to prior treatments [28, 29].

Among other cell-based therapies, the infusion of NK cells exhibits enhanced cytotoxic activity against myeloma cells. Allogeneic and autologous myeloma cells have been successfully eradicated by NK cells, underscoring their ability to circumvent tumor cells' immune evasion mechanisms [35, 36]. In addition, infusion of NK cells in transplant settings aims to enhance the anti-myeloma response while minimizing the risk of GVHD associated with allo-HSCT [37]. Additionally, the potential of NK cell therapy to improve the overall efficacy of other treatments, such as mAbs, is a promising avenue for future research [35].

The subsequent approach in cell-based therapies for MM patients, in research or clinical settings, involves the use of genetically engineered immune cells, particularly CAR therapy. CAR-T cell and CAR-NK cell therapies in the context of MM employ the capacity of genetically engineered immune cells to target and eradicate myeloma cells [38]. Despite the numerous ongoing cases of CAR-T therapy for MM in the preclinical and clinical phases so far, only two autologous CAR-T cell therapies, idecabtagene vicleucel (Abecma) and ciltacabtagene autoleucel (Carvykti), have been able to receive FDA approval and demonstrated significant clinical efficacy in treating adult patients with R/RMM [39]. While both Abecma and Carvykti utilize a lentiviral vector to insert a CAR structure to selectively identify and eradicate B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-expressing cancer cells, the primary distinctions between these products lie in Abecma's ectodomain, which binds to a single epitope of BCMA. In contrast, Carvykti's antigen-binding domain binds to two epitopes of BCMA. Considering the recommended dose, Abecma is indicated at 300–460 × 106 CAR-T cells/kg, whereas Carvykti is shown at 0.5–1.0 × 106 CAR-T cells/kg [40]. CAR-NK cell therapy for MM patients is discussed in detail in the following sections and is still under investigation, but holds promise due to its unique advantages [41].

NK cell: biology, main features, functions, and superiority

NK cells are a classic cytotoxic type of innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), which is involved in anti-tumor and anti-viral immune responses. NK cells act independently of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecule and exert their function through the secretion of cytokines, chemokines, and cytotoxic mediators [42, 43]. NK cell development occurs in the BM and secondary lymphoid organs, including lymph nodes and liver [44, 45]. These cells account for 10–15% of total peripheral blood (PB) lymphocytes in humans and can be identified as large lymphocytes with abundant cytoplasmic granules [46, 47].

NK cells are derived from hematopoietic stem cells (HSC) and mature from a specific subset of cells in the BM known as common lymphoid progenitor (CLP) cells, characterized by the markers CD34+, CD133+, CD45RA+, and CD10+ [48, 49]. The CD122 (interleukin [IL]8-2Rβ) expression signifies that CLPs have irreversibly committed to becoming NK cells [42]. Furthermore, NK cells are distinguished by the lack of CD3/TCR complex and the presence of specific markers CD56 (encoded by neural cell adhesion molecule [NCAM]) and CD16 (encoded by Fc gamma receptor IIIa [FcγRIIIa]) [46, 50]. CD56dim NK cells are the most common population in the PB with high cytolytic potential, comprising 90–95% of circulating NK cells [51], and their function resembles CD8+ cytotoxic T cells in eliminating infected or cancerous cells [52]. They exhibit lower levels of CD94/NKG2A but high expression of killer immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs), CD94/NKG2C, and CD16, along with high perforin/granzymes secretion [53]. They possess significantly more CD16 than the CD56 bright subset, enabling them to mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). CD56dim CD16+ NK cells can be categorized into two subgroups, CD57− NKG2C+ and CD57+ NKG2C+ NK cells. CD57− NKG2C+ NK cells that maintain their ability to proliferate are probably potential precursors to CD57+ NKG2C+. The latter is called adaptive NK cells due to memory characteristics [54, 55].II.CD56bright CD16dim/−. NK cells possess a variety of germline-encoded activating receptors and inhibitory receptors that enable them to recognize cancerous cells (Fig. 1B) [50]. The cytotoxic responses of NK cells are determined by a balance of signals from inhibitory and activating receptors, which enables them to distinguish between abnormal and healthy cells [57]. Both activating and inhibitory receptors can be HLA-specific or HLA-nonspecific. HLA-specific receptors exert their function by binding to HLA molecules, and non-specific HLA receptors bind to other molecules. Moreover, NK cells express transmembrane receptors known as KIRs that recognize MHC-I haplotypes [58]. KIRs consist of two or three immunoglobulin-like domains referred to as KIR2D and KIR3D, respectively. KIRs, as HLA-specific receptors, can be categorized into activating or inhibitory receptors [59].I.Activation receptors. Activating receptors on NK cells identify ligands that are typically expressed under stress, such as in cancerous or infected cells, while being absent in healthy cells. Activating receptors can trigger the NK cell-killing signal transduction by recognizing these specific ligands [60]. Activating KIRs, like KIR2DSs (2DS1-2DS5) and KIR3DS1, contain short cytoplasmic tails associated with the transmembrane adaptor DAP12 to convey activation signals within NK cells [59], which are linked with MHC molecules. KIR2DS1, KIR2DS2, and KIR2DS4 bind to HLA-A, and KIR3DS1 recognizes HLA-B. NKG2C, another HLA-specific receptor, interacts with nonclassical HLA-E [61, 62]. NKG2D homodimer is a C-type lectin-like receptor, which is expressed on the surface of NK cells and interacts with various ligands, including the UL16-binding proteins (ULBP) and MHC-I chain-related molecules A and B (MICA and MICB) [63–65].

NK cell HLA-nonspecific activation receptor, DNAX accessory molecule 1 (DNAM1/CD226) competes with T cell immunoglobulin and ITIM [immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM)] domain (TIGIT) and CD96 to bind to its ligands, PVR (CD155) as well as Nectin-2 (CD112) [66]. Natural cytotoxicity receptors (NCRs) such as NKp30 (CD337), NKp44 (CD336), and NKp46 (CD335) are also categorized as NK cell-activating receptors. NKp46 and NKp30 are potentially expressed, whereas NKp44 appears only following activation. The ligands include Plasmodium falciparum erythrocyte membrane protein (PfEMP1), vimentin, influenza virus hemagglutinin for NKp46, B7-H6, and BAG6/BAT3, galectin-3 for NKp30, and a tumor-associated ligand proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), platelet-derived growth factor DD (PDGF-DD), nidogen-1 for NKp44 [50, 67, 68]. Signaling lymphocyte activation molecule (SLAM) family members, such as SLAMF7 (CD319), CD48, and 2B4 (CD244), act as coreceptors to enhance NK cell activity. In addition, 2B4 is part of the SLAM family of CD2-related receptors that bind to CD48 as its specific ligand [69, 70].

Finally, CD16 (FcγRIIIa) is a unique receptor that stimulates ADCC upon binding the Fc region of antibodies attached to target cells, activating NK cells [71]. Moreover, NK cell activation typically requires the synergy of multiple receptors; however, CD16 is powerful enough to activate NK cells independently, without the need for further activation from other receptors [72].II.Inhibitory receptors. Inhibitory receptors on NK cells recognize the MHC Class I and non-MHC molecules. This inhibitory signal dominates in the absence of activation ligands, thereby preventing the killing of healthy cells [52]. The CD94/NKG2A, as a killer cell lectin-like receptor (KLR), is expressed on both NK cells and T cells, and its ligand allows tumor cells to evade cytotoxicity as the non-classical MHC molecule HLA-E [73, 74]. A KIR with a long tail serves as an inhibitory receptor. Inhibitory KIRs, like KIR2DLs (2DL1-2DL3, 2DL5) and KIR3DLs (3DL1-3DL3), contain an ITIM that regulates NK cell function [59]. Most of the KIRs combine with classic MHC-I molecules [75]. The KIR2DL1-2DL3 bind to HLA-C specifically, while KIR3DL1 and 3DL2 interact with HLA-A or HLA-B. KIR2DL4 recognizes soluble and membrane-bound HLA-G, but signaling occurs only when it binds to soluble HLA-G in endosomes [76]. In addition, NK cells express a variety of other surface receptors, including sialic-acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin (Siglec) family (Siglec-7 and Siglec-9), killer cell lectin-like receptor G1(KLRG1), LAIR-1 (CD305), NKR-P1 (CD161), TACTILE (CD96), TIGIT, T cell immunoglobulin– and mucin domain-containing (TIM)–3, and programmed cell death protein 1(PD-1), and lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3). These receptors recruit protein tyrosine phosphatases, such as SHP1 and SHP2, to inhibit phosphorylation [77–79]. LAG-3, TIM-3, and TIGIT are co-inhibitory receptors with specialized functions in immune regulation [80].

The NK cells induce apoptosis in the target cells when activated, and they directly cause cytotoxicity via various mechanisms, including antigen recognition, cytokine release, degranulation, death receptors, and ADCC, which induce apoptosis indirectly by triggering the adaptive immune response (Fig. 1C).I.Direct cytotoxicity. (1) NK cells detect the lack of self-molecules in missing-self recognition. Tumor cells can evade recognition via CTLs by reducing their MHC class I expression on the surface of tumor cells [83]. Loss of MHC class I reduced inhibition of NK cells and facilitated recognition of stress-induced ligands, leading to NK cell activation and the initiation of killing signals to eliminate tumor cells [84]. (2) NK cells produce chemokines and cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ, which modify immunity to intracellular pathogens [44]. IFN-γ plays a crucial role in stimulating macrophages, shaping T cell-mediated cellular immune response, upregulating MHC class I expression on antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and inhibiting the proliferation of infected or cancerous cells [85]. (3) The degranulation pathway leads to the lysis of target cells through the secretion of granules containing granzyme and perforin to the immunological synapse [50]. Perforin forms pores in the target cell membrane that allow granzymes to enter the cytosol and induce apoptosis [86]. (4) NK cells express FasL and tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), which bind to Fas (CD95) and TRAIL receptors as their ligands on tumor cells [87]. The interaction of these death receptors with ligands activates the enzymatic cascade and leads to the apoptosis of the target cell [45]. (5) NK cells recognize infected cells coated with antibodies via the CD16, which binds to the Fc region of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies and causes the killing of target cells through a process known as ADCC [71].II.Indirect immunomodulatory. NK cells activate the adaptive immune response by secreting cytokines, such as IFN-γ, which can potentially affect various immune cells. For example, these cytokines augment macrophages and DCs' functions and trigger DCs' maturation. Then, cytokines produced by DCs, such as IL-12 and IL-15, potentially induce Th1 CD4+ T cells and CTLs antitumor responses [88]. In addition, NK cells recruit DCs to the TME through chemokine (C–C motif) ligand (CCL)−5, XCL1, and XCL2 production. DCs present tumor antigens to T cells and then amplify the antitumor immune response [89].

Perforin forms pores in the target cell membrane that allow granzymes to enter the cytosol and induce apoptosis [86]. (4) NK cells express FasL and tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), which bind to Fas (CD95) and TRAIL receptors as their ligands on tumor cells [87]. The interaction of these death receptors with ligands activates the enzymatic cascade and leads to the apoptosis of the target cell [45]. (5) NK cells recognize infected cells coated with antibodies via the CD16, which binds to the Fc region of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies and causes the killing of target cells through a process known as ADCC [71].

Direct cytotoxicity. (1) NK cells detect the lack of self-molecules in missing-self recognition. Tumor cells can evade recognition via CTLs by reducing their MHC class I expression on the surface of tumor cells [83]. Loss of MHC class I reduced inhibition of NK cells and facilitated recognition of stress-induced ligands, leading to NK cell activation and the initiation of killing signals to eliminate tumor cells [84]. (2) NK cells produce chemokines and cytokines such as TNF-α and IFN-γ, which modify immunity to intracellular pathogens [44]. IFN-γ plays a crucial role in stimulating macrophages, shaping T cell-mediated cellular immune response, upregulating MHC class I expression on antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and inhibiting the proliferation of infected or cancerous cells [85]. (3) The degranulation pathway leads to the lysis of target cells through the secretion of granules containing granzyme and perforin to the immunological synapse [50]. Perforin forms pores in the target cell membrane that allow granzymes to enter the cytosol and induce apoptosis [86]. (4) NK cells express FasL and tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), which bind to Fas (CD95) and TRAIL receptors as their ligands on tumor cells [87]. The interaction of these death receptors with ligands activates the enzymatic cascade and leads to the apoptosis of the target cell [45]. (5) NK cells recognize infected cells coated with antibodies via the CD16, which binds to the Fc region of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies and causes the killing of target cells through a process known as ADCC [71].

II.Indirect immunomodulatory. NK cells activate the adaptive immune response by secreting cytokines, such as IFN-γ, which can potentially affect various immune cells. For example, these cytokines augment macrophages and DCs' functions and trigger DCs' maturation. Then, cytokines produced by DCs, such as IL-12 and IL-15, potentially induce Th1 CD4+ T cells and CTLs antitumor responses [88]. In addition, NK cells recruit DCs to the TME through chemokine (C–C motif) ligand (CCL)−5, XCL1, and XCL2 production. DCs present tumor antigens to T cells and then amplify the antitumor immune response [89].

Indirect immunomodulatory. NK cells activate the adaptive immune response by secreting cytokines, such as IFN-γ, which can potentially affect various immune cells. For example, these cytokines augment macrophages and DCs' functions and trigger DCs' maturation. Then, cytokines produced by DCs, such as IL-12 and IL-15, potentially induce Th1 CD4+ T cells and CTLs antitumor responses [88]. In addition, NK cells recruit DCs to the TME through chemokine (C–C motif) ligand (CCL)−5, XCL1, and XCL2 production. DCs present tumor antigens to T cells and then amplify the antitumor immune response [89].

As compared to other cellular therapies, especially T cell-based therapy, NK cells have unique characteristics, including (1) they possess cytotoxic activity without prior antigen exposure or HLA restriction, (2) minimal side effects, such as GVHD, cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), and (3) a time-consuming and cost-effective procedure compared to T cell therapy [90]. Furthermore, NK cells within the MM milieu face deficiencies in their quantity and functionality (See Sect. " NK cell impairments in MM"). Consequently, harnessing NK cells to treat MM is highly encouraged. In this regard, empowered NK cells with CAR enable them to target tumor-associated antigens (TAA), thereby building a more effective defense [91].

Quantity/distribution abnormalities

A high number and activity of NK cells are associated with a reduced tumor burden, since PB-derived NK cells (PB-NK) in MM patients are unchanged or increased early on, but decrease as the disease progresses [92, 93]. The levels of IL-10, a protein responsible for inhibiting NK cells, are elevated in MM, whereas the levels of IL-1, a substance that stimulates the proliferation of NK cells, are reduced [7]. In addition, the abundance of NK cells is higher in MRD-negative patients than in MRD-positive patients after ASCT [94].

Besides NK cell counts, NK cell distribution in the TME of MM may be impaired. In this context, a study by Ponzetta et al. demonstrated that NK cell numbers rapidly decrease in the BM of MM tumor-bearing mice, indicating defective maintenance of NK cells in the BM during MM progression due to alterations in chemokine/chemokine receptor axes within the myeloma microenvironment [95]. The increased efflux of these cells from the BM is driven by an increase in the levels of CXCR3 ligands (CXCL9 and CXCL10) and a decrease in the levels of the CXCR4 ligand (CXCL12), thus impairing the antitumor immune response by NK cells at the initial site of tumor growth. Therefore, inhibiting CXCR3 on NK cells can prevent NK cell efflux from the BM [95]. In this regard, Bonanni et al. indicated that inhibition of the CXCR3 axis enhances the therapeutic efficacy of IL-15-activated NK cells against malignant PCs [96].

Quality abnormalities

Dysregulated receptor expression/signaling

Reduce activating receptor expression/signaling

MM patients with active disease show reduced expression of the DNAM-1 (CD226) receptor on CD56dim NK cells compared with patients in remission and healthy individuals [97]. According to Pazina et al., NK cells derived from PB and BM from patients with MM were analyzed using flow cytometry, and the most remarkable differences were observed between R/R patients and post-stem cell transplant (pSCT) patients with less mature NK cells and lower activating receptors in the BM than in the PB [98]. Additionally, single-cell sequencing analysis revealed that the expression levels of CD16 and DNAM decreased. The alteration in these receptors is accompanied by reduced actin polymerization and LFA-1 (an adhesion molecule) activation, leading to NK cell impairment [99].

There was no difference in NKp46 expression between CD56dim and CD56bright NK cells, but this difference was greater in BM-derived NK cells than in PB-derived NK cells, indicating exhaustion of NK cells due to chronic exposure to MM cells [98].

Increase inhibitory receptor expression/signaling

The expression of CD94/NKG2A is increased in cancer-associated NK cells, contributing to their exhaustion [100]. High levels of HLA-E, a ligand for CD94/NKG2A, have been suggested as a potential therapeutic candidate in high-risk MM. Inhibition of NKG2A by antibodies has led to the restoration of NK cell antitumor activity [8]. KIRs could inhibit NK cell responses through interaction with MHC-I molecules, and increased MHC-I levels have also been identified as a characteristic of PCs in MM. In addition to increased KIR2DL1 and KIR2DL2 receptor levels in PB-NK cells, increased levels of KIR2DL1 and KIR2DL2 receptors have also been reported in patients with MM [101, 102]. Increased levels of the inhibitory KIR2DL1 receptor and decreased levels of the activatory CD161 (KLRB1) receptor, which plays a role in enhancing NK cell responses through CD161, impair the antitumor activity of NK cells in MM patients [103].

In addition, increased expression of TIGIT, TIM-3, and GITR on NK cells in PB and BM of MM patients has been demonstrated [98]. The DNAM1 and TIGIT bind to the poliovirus receptor (PVR/CD155) and nectin-2, significantly regulating NK cell activity. The TIGIT binds CD155 with higher affinity than DNAM-1 and may outcompete DNAM-1 for interaction with CD155. The TIGIT can also deliver the inhibitory signal directly to NK cells via its cytoplasmic tail [104]. Similarly, NK cells expressed higher levels of inhibitory receptors, including TIM-3, TIGIT, and GITR, compared to healthy subjects in patients with R/RMM and after SCT [98].

Inhibitory TME

Immune microenvironment

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) have bidirectional communication with MM cells. MDSCs provide immunosuppressive TME for MM development, and myeloma cells support MDSCs' function [105]. MDSCs suppressed cytotoxicity, NKG2D expression, and IFN-γ production of NK cells via membrane-bound transforming growth factor (TGF)-β1 on MDSC [106]. In addition, Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) is involved in MDSC-mediated NK cell anergy through the induction of TGF-β production, resulting in decreased expression of NKp46 and NKp44 [107]. Besides immunosuppressive cytokines, nitric oxide (NO) produced by MDSC suppresses FcRs on NK cells to inhibit NK cell-mediated ADCC [108]. MDSCs deplete amino acids, such as tryptophan and L-arginine, from the TME through the action of indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and Arginase 1 (ARG1), respectively [109]. IDO catabolizes tryptophan to kynurenine, decreasing NKp46 and NKG2D expression and reducing IFN-γ and TNF-α production [110, 111]. MDSCs depletion of L-arginine via ARG1, thereby repressing INF-γ secretion by NK cells [112]. MDSCs also express an elevated amount of CD39 and CD73, leading to the conversion of ATP to AMP and AMP to adenosine, respectively. Adenosine suppressed NK cell killing activity mediated by perforin/granzyme, Fas ligand (FasL), and IFN-γ/TNF-α [113]. MDSCs trigger Treg development via a TGF-β-dependent and independent manner as another immunosuppressive mechanism [114]. In a TGF-β independent manner, IDO expressed by MDSCs promotes Treg expansion and infiltration into the tumor site [115].

Myeloma microenvironment remarkably elevated Tregs along with high levels of TGF-β and IL-10 [116]. Interestingly, NK cell function was inversely correlated with the number of Tregs. Treg depletion leads to the elevation of NK cell anti-tumor activity [117]. Treg cells reduced NKG2D receptors on the NK cell surface, resulting in blocked NK cell function in a TGF-β-dependent manner [117, 118]. Furthermore, Treg-derived TGF-β1 corresponded to PD-1 ligand (PD-L1) expression on MM cells and reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors (IL-6, TNF-α, and CXCL10) [116]. Like TGF-β, IL-10 also inhibits NKG2A-mediated NK cell tumor lysis by decreasing MICA and ULBP2 expression on the target cell's surface. Therefore, NK cell degranulation toward the target cell is reduced in the presence of IL-10 [119].

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) within the MM niche are frequently polarized into a pro-tumorigenic M2 phenotype, which correlates with poor clinical outcomes. M2-TAM releases anti-inflammatory mediators, including IL-10, TGF-β, CCL-17, CCL-18, and CCL-22 into the MM microenvironment [120]. M2-TAM producing TGF-β induces NK cell exhaustion, inhibits cytotoxicity, and reduces the degranulation capacity of NK cells. Based on the studies, TGF-β inhibition restores NK cell cytotoxicity [121]. The hypothesis is that TGF-β-derived M2-TAM induces the CD85j (ILT-2) inhibitory receptor on NK cells upon recognition of its ligands, HLA-G, which are significantly expressed by M2-TAM. Therefore, TGF-β or CD85j inhibition recovered NK cell cytotoxicity and INF-γ production [122].

Matrix microenvironment

Similar to previous immunosuppressive cells, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) produce TGF-β, IL-10, IDO, and PGE2 to inhibit the function of NK cells. Furthermore, MSCs express CD155/CD226 and HLA-G binding to the NK cell inhibitory receptors, TIGIT, and HLA-specific inhibitory receptors. CD155-TIGIT or CD226-TIGIT axis reduced INF-γ production, perforin/granzyme degranulation, and NKG2A expression [123, 124]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), as another crucial component of the MM niche, promote cancer progression and drug resistance [125]. It is hypothesized that CAFs support MM development by suppressing the killing activity of NK cells toward cancer cells through the secretion of immunomodulatory factors (TGF-β, IDO, and PGE2), downregulating NK cell-activating receptors or their ligands, and recruiting other immunosuppressive cells, such as M2-TAM [126].

Osteoblasts (OSBs), as another immunosuppressive cell, directly interact with MM cells, thereby potentiating their growth through the secretion of growth factors [127]. OSBs also decoy NK cell cytotoxicity by secreting IL-10 and IL-6, thereby impairing perforin/granzyme functionality [128].

Myeloma cells escape from NK cells

Expression exhaustion marker

As MM progresses and NK cells continuously activate, PD-L1 is overexpressed in myeloma cells, which interacts with PD-1 on NK cells, reducing NK cell toxicity and decreasing IFN-γ production [129]. A study demonstrated that inhibiting PD1/PD-L1 signaling in NK cells can enhance their degranulation and cytokine-producing capabilities, as well as prevent NK cell apoptosis [130]. Myeloma cells express TIM-3 ligands (galectin-9, HMGB1, and CEACAM-1), potentially activating TIM-3 inhibitory signaling pathways in NK cells [131].

Increase the expression of the NK cell inhibitory receptor ligand

NK cells can activate and kill myeloma cells in the early stages of the disease by recognizing HLA-I ligands. As the disease progresses, the expression of HLA-I ligands on MM cells increases, leading to their resistance to cell death by NK cells [84]. Malignant myeloma cells express HLA-I, which escapes lysis by NK cells by binding to inhibitory receptors, including KIR and CD94/NKG2A [132, 133]. A strong expression of PSGL-1 and sialic acid-derived ligands for Siglec-7 has been reported in MM. Targeted desialylation and/or deletion of the gene encoding Siglec-7 enhanced NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity [134].

Reduce the expression of the NK cell activating receptor ligand

Reduce the expression of the NK cell activating receptor ligand

Myeloma cells have increased levels of ERp5, which inhibits MICA's binding to NKG2D, and the degradation of MICA is associated with the progression of MGUS to MM [135], and the degradation of MICA is associated with the progression of MGUS to MM [136]. In some patients, levels of activating ligands such as MICA and MICB are reduced in myeloma cells, and this reduction in MICA and MICB levels hinders NK cell activation, allowing MM cells to escape NK cell surveillance [135].

Secretion of soluble factors

Like immunosuppressive cells, MM cells produce TGF-β, modulating the myeloma tumor site [137]. In addition, TGF-β is produce by MDSCs, Treg, TAMs, and MM cells, which decreases the expression of NK cell activating receptors (NKG2D, NKp30, and DNAM-1), reduces myeloma cells' recognition ligands, impairs perforin/granzyme and INF-γ production, and ADCC [138]. Accordingly, neutralizing TGF-β augmented the anti-tumor activity of NK cells [139]. Moreover, IDO is produced by myeloma cells and correlates with Treg frequency in TME [140]. Myeloma cells release PGE2, which is associated with IL-6 production and acts as an inhibitor of NK cells [141, 142]. As mentioned before, IDO and PGE2 modify NK cell functions.

MM cells express soluble ligands to block the transduction of NK cell-activating receptors. For instance, soluble MICA is highly elevated in MM patients and corresponds to NKG2D inhibition. Hence, MIC-clearing mAb (huB10G5) restores NKG2D transduction [143]. The high level of total serum immunoglobulins present in the serum of MM patients can inhibit the ADCC function mediated by CD16 NK cells [144].

Depending on the context, the SLAMF7 receptor in cancer can have both stimulatory and inhibitory effects [145]. Malignant myeloma cells have been shown to shed SLAMF7, resulting in increased levels of soluble SLAMF7 (sSLAMF7), and detectable in MM patients. Therefore, SLAMF7 levels may be associated with disease progression. Soluble SLAMF7 also binds to SLAMF7 on MM cells, promoting myeloma cell growth by activating ERK and SHP-2 signaling [146].

Resistance to apoptosis

FasL is a potent inducer of apoptosis and is an effective T/NK cell-killing mechanism mediated by the Fas/FasL pathway [147]. Reduced Fas function and loss-of-function mutations in the Fas-encoding gene have been reported in MM, which may contribute to the pathogenesis and progression of myeloma and render myeloma cells resistant to lysis by this pathway [148].

Hypoxia and metabolic pathways

MM cells residing in a hypoxic microenvironment are demonstrated to trigger myeloma progression [149]. Hypoxia leads to a decrease in the cytotoxicity of NK cells through a reduction of the activating receptors [150]. HIF-1α, produced in a hypoxic environment to support the survival and growth of cancer cells, is directly involved in the increase in PD-1L expression. Together with its inhibitory role on NK cells, it plays a role in the progression of MM [151, 152]. Additionally, hypoxia-induced autophagy results in the degradation of granzyme B, leading to a decrease in the cytotoxic function of NK cells against malignant cells [153].

Additionally, HIFs play a crucial role in transitioning from aerobic to anaerobic metabolism [154]. Thus, MM cells exhibit anaerobic glycolysis and produce high levels of lactate in this hypoxic condition [155]. Lactate not only directly restrains NK cell killing activity but also indirectly recruits MDSCs [156].

Drug-induced NK cell dysfunction

It is suggested that genotoxic agents, such as doxorubicin and melphalan, used in the MM therapeutic regimen, enhance MICA and MICB shedding from myeloma cells, thereby impairing NKG2D signal transduction. This phenomenon results from the ADAM10 metalloproteinase, induced by genotoxic drugs, which cleaves the NKG2D ligands, MICA and MICB. Therefore, conjugating a metalloproteinase inhibitor with a typical therapeutic regimen may be useful [157].

CAR-NK cell sources

CAR-NK development requires effector NK cells with a high potential for ex vivo expansion to yield a large number of activated NK cells with high cytotoxicity capacity for cancer immunotherapy. Our comprehensive review revealed that, from the inception of CAR-NK development to the present, a diverse range of sources, including primary NK cells and NK cell lines, have been utilized to generate CAR-NK cells (Fig. 3A).

Cell line

The primary NK cell sources share a common disadvantage; obtaining a large scale of NK cells is relatively difficult and time-consuming. However, cell lines facilitate easier maintenance and expansion of these cells [158]. Therefore, researchers have begun to explore the use of stable NK cell lines to overcome some of the challenges associated with primary NK cells, such as purification, differentiation, and proliferation, as well as resistance to genetic modification [159]. Different NK cell lines have been developed, including NK-92, HANK-1, NK3.3, NK101, KHYG-1, NKG, NK-YS, YTS, NKL, SNK-6, and IMC-1. Currently, NK-92 cells, derived from a 50-year-old man with malignant non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), have received FDA approval for clinical study and are being investigated as engineered therapies alone or in combination with other agents [159]. NK-92 cells can also be expanded to large numbers [160].

NK-92 cells express some NK activating receptors and secrete perforin/granzyme B, but they are incapable of ADCC function due to the lack of CD16 expression [159]. These cells can be modified to express the high-affinity CD16 receptor, thereby improving their antitumor function [161]. NK-92 cells can be easily genetically modified using non-viral methods, such as electroporation, which enables the expression of specific receptors or ligands that enhance their capacity to target cancerous cells [162]. The activation and proliferation of NK-92 cell lines depend on IL-2 or IL-15 [163]. As a potential limitation, the NK-92 cell line must be irradiated before infusion due to its cytogenetic abnormalities, which decrease its antitumor potency and longevity [161].

Peripheral blood-derived NK (PB-NK)

NK cells isolated from PB are a suitable source for generating CAR-NK cells due to their easy availability. In addition, the mature phenotype of PB-derived NK cells eliminates the need for a differentiation induction period. However, the difference in abundance and function of NK cells between individuals influenced by factors such as age, patient's status, and previous therapeutic regimen, especially in patients with cancer, poses challenges for large-scale in vitro culture [164, 165].

PB-derived NK cells can be formed through an allogeneic or autologous source. Allogeneic NK cells are often preferred for cancer immunotherapy since autologous NK cells are not functionally activated when exposed to self-MHC antigens, and the function of patients' PB-derived NK cells is usually compromised by underlying disease or prior therapy [166]. Besides, the low number of PB NK cells limits their applicability for immunotherapy purposes [167].

Cord blood-derived NK cells (CB-NK)

NK cells constitute approximately ~ 10% of all PB lymphocytes while comprising ~ 30% of cord blood (CB) lymphocytes, which can be obtained from public cord blood banks with a high proliferative capacity. The CB-NK has lower cytotoxic activity, is more immature, and exhibits reduced expression of CD16, perforin/granzyme B, and adhesion molecules CD2, CD11a, CD18, and CD62L, while expressing higher levels of the inhibitory receptor [168].

CB-NK cells are often characterized as poorly differentiated and weakly activated compared to PB-NK, exhibiting high levels of inhibitory receptors, such as NKG2A, and low levels of activating receptors, including CD16, KIRs, DNAM-1, and NKG2C [169]. The immaturity in CB-NK cells renders a higher proliferative capacity than PB-NK after activation, making them an appropriate candidate for cell therapies. On the other hand, CB-NK immaturity results in less immunogenicity and reduces the risk of GVHD after allo-HSCT [164]. In contrast to PB-NK cells, CB-NK cells exhibit reduced cytokine production upon the engagement of CD16, which consequently results in their lower efficacy in ADCC [170].

Stem cell-derived NK cell

One of the drawbacks of PB-NK and CB-NK cells is the inherent variability among donors, resulting in functional heterogeneity in the NK cell product. This issue has prompted the exploration of stem cell-derived NK cells as an alternative for developing a standardized therapeutic product. NK cells derived from hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) exhibit potent cytotoxic activity to provide a uniform population of NK cells [171, 172].

CB-derived HSPCs differentiated into NK cells in the conditioned medium. Subsequently, HSPC-NK cells expanded and were transduced using the CAR structure [172, 173]. HSPCs offer advantages such as fresh isolation from donors, availability via cryopreservation and cord blood banks, and differentiation into homogenous NK cell populations. However, the expansion of these cells is time-consuming and requires a unique medium for each condition, and they lack CD16 expression, which deprives them of ADCC [172–174]. The iPSCs can be differentiated into NK cells through stromal cell-mediated cell sorting and CD34+CD45+ cell differentiation, utilizing IL-3, IL-5, IL-7, FLT3L, and SCF [160]. iPSCs can be engineered to differentiate into a homogeneous population of CAR-NK cells and expanded on a large scale [175].

Advances in genetic engineering and gene-editing techniques create iPSC-derived NK cells with enhanced NK cell expansion, function, and persistence [176]. Generating iPSC-derived CAR-NK (CAR-iNK) can be time-consuming and inefficient, and there is a risk of genetic abnormalities during the process. In addition, iPSC-derived cells are potentially immunogenic and may lead to the destruction of effector cells or even adverse immune responses, such as CRS [177]. However, recent advances in stem cell technology have led to more efficient and reliable methods for differentiating iPSCs into CAR-NK. The survival of CAR-iNK cells can be improved by armoring them with membrane-bound IL-15 (mbIL-15) and differentiating them into memory-like NK (ML NK) cells with the combination of IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18, or by knocking down HLA molecules [178].

CAR-NK expansion

NK cell-based therapy requires large numbers of cells, ranging from 1 × 106 to 1 × 109 cells/kg for effective treatments. In some trials, the desirable dose reaches 1 × 1010 cells/kg. However, generating enough NK cells for clinical trials has faced several challenges, as the donor PB-NK cells are often insufficient. Therefore, there should be a large-scale expansion of Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) NK cells in ex vivo conditions. NK cell expansion is generally divided into feeder-cell or feeder-free systems [179, 180].

Feeder-cell-based expansion

Generally, feeder cells support the expansion of NK cells on a large scale by expressing various ligands that interact with NK cell receptors. The EBV-transformed lymphoblastoid cell lines (EBV-LCLs) and genetically modified HEK293 and K562 cell lines were harnessed as feeder cells [181]. Feeder cells are exposed to γ-irradiation before use for NK cell expansion to reduce the risk of contamination from actively dividing cells [182].

EBV-LCLs express 4-1BBL, CD155, CD48, and CD5 conjugate with their respective stimulatory 4-1BB, DNAM-1, 2B4, and CD2 receptors upon NK cells [182]. Using EBV-LCLs as feeder cells in a fully automated procedure leads to a mean 850-fold expansion of NK cells within day 14 [183].

K562 cells are more efficient feeder cells, correlating with a 20-fold expansion of NK cells and reliable cytotoxicity [184] because K562 reduces NK cell inhibitory signal transduction due to its negative expression of HLA class I, a ligand for inhibitory KIRs, and NKG2A [182, 185]. Furthermore, the modified K562 acts as an artificial antigen-presenting cell (aAPC) with CD64 (FcγRI), CD86 (B7-2), CD137L (4-1BBL), and truncated CD19 expression [186].

Recently, K562 underwent modification to express a membrane-bound cytokine (mb-cytokine). K562 cells are engineered to express mbIL-21, and the 4-1BB ligand reduces NK cell senescence in long-term culture, which is required for expansion. The MBIL-21 K562 cells (NKF feeder cells) induce more than 10,000-fold NK cell expansion at 5 weeks, with high in vitro and in vivo cytotoxicity [187]. Adding mbIL-15 to K562-mb21-41BBL also benefits optimal NK cell expansion [188]. However, K562-mbIL-21 feeder cells display higher NK cell expansion (mean 47,967-fold expansion) compared with K562-mb15 feeder cells (median 325-fold expansion) within 21 days [186].

Particularly, this protocol has been utilized in other studies to expand NK cells before CAR engineering. The K562-mb21-41BBL results in a massive (1,609-fold) expansion of PB-NK cells over 12 days before CAR transduction [189]. Interestingly, CAR-NK expanded with K562-mb21-41BBL shows promising clinical outcomes in a Phase 1/2 clinical trial against CD19+ B-cell tumors (NCT03056339) [190]. Feeder cells engineered with mb-cytokines are utilized in CAR-NK generation for MM; for instance, mbIL-21 expressing feeder cells are applied in NKG2D-CAR-NK production [191], and K562-mbIL-21 feeder cells are used in BCMA CAR-NK cell development [192, 193].

Although the feeder cells are suitable candidates for large-scale NK cells, they present several challenges. First, employing K562 variants or EBV-LCLs raises a potential risk of delivering tumor-derived cellular elements or their genetic material into the patient. Second, it is a costly technique, as it requires irradiation, which is not available in every laboratory, additional materials for culture, and extra laboratory tests to confirm its safety. Therefore, exploring new approaches, such as feeder-free and plasma membrane particle (PM-particle)-based methods, is necessary [194]. Accordingly, PB-NK cells stimulated with PM21 particles showed massive ex vivo (825-fold in 14 days) and dose-dependent in vivo expansion. In this experiment, the PM of K562-mb15-41BBL cells is used instead of K562-mb15-41BBL feeder cells [195].

Feeder-free expansion

Feeder-free NK cell expansion leans on a combination of IL-2, IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, and IL-21 cytokines without feeder cells, which support NK cells' survival, proliferation, activation, cytolytic activity, degranulation, and cytokine production (Cytokine-based expansion) [196]. PBMCs expanded in medium supplemented with IL-2 reached a 193-fold expansion after 21 days, and 55% of the cells were characterized as CD3−CD56+NK cells [197]. In NK cell-based therapy for MM, PB-NK cells were expanded in medium containing IL-2 and IL-15, on average, 486-fold by the fifth week. In addition to cytokines, NK cell pretreatment with anti-CD38 mAbs (daratumumab) is effective [198].

Additionally, cytokine-based expansion was employed for NK cell expansion in the development of CAR-NK cells for MM. NK-92 pretreated with IL-2 and IL-15 were significantly transduced with BCMA-CAR and CD138-CAR [199, 200]. IL-2 and IL-15 are also used for primary NK cell expansion in the design of BCMA-CAR-NK [201]. As previously mentioned, interleukins can also be used in a membrane-bound form on feeder cells such as K562-mb21 or K562-mb15 feeder cells, which represent a relatively effective approach for NK cell expansion. The NK cell expansion with cytokine combination is a straightforward process that does not involve irradiation and complex gene modification. Most importantly, the risks of tumor contamination with infused NK cells are minimized [181], but it is prolonged for several weeks (in some cases, up to 12 weeks) to achieve optimal fold expansion [180].

Readers interested in more comprehensive details of the NK cell expansion methods please refer to an excellent recent review [196].

CAR-NK construction and generation

CAR-NK expresses engineered receptors consisting of three main parts: the ectodomain, the transmembrane (TM) domain, and the endodomain. The ectodomain included an antigen recognition domain that identified the target antigen and an extracellular hinge region, serving as a linker between the antigen recognition domain and the NK cell surface membrane. The TM domain docks the CAR to the cell membrane, and an endodomain or intracellular signaling domain is involved in activating the effector cell (Fig. 3B).

Ectodomain

The antigen recognition domain can be either a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) or a non-scFv design. The scFv domain, composed of the variable regions of the light and heavy chains (VL and VH) of the antibody, binds to the tumor antigen (TA) and determines the specificity of the CAR [202]. Non-scFv extracellular domains are applicable for the extracellular domain of CARs, including Nanobody (Nb)-CAR, Natural receptor-based (NRB)-CAR, and Natural ligand-based (NLB)-CAR. A Nb has a similar antigen-binding affinity to a mAb but is smaller, more soluble, and has more stable physicochemical properties [203]. Nbs are advantageous for producing CAR-NK cells due to their high stability and multiple targeting capabilities. Another strategy is to use natural receptors and ligands for antigen targeting. The ectodomain of the receptor or ligand of interest, along with its TM domain and signaling endodomain, is incorporated into the CAR structure to create an NRB/NLB-CAR. Natural receptors/ligands used as targets in CAR-NK include the NKG2D receptor-ligands (MICA, MICB, ULBP), DNAM-1 receptor-ligands (PVR/CD155 and Nectin-2/CD112), PD-1 and PD-L1, and CD27 and its ligand CD70 [203]. Interestingly, an NRB/NLB-CAR is designed in MM with the NKG2D extracellular domain, which shows cytotoxicity toward target cells expressing high levels of NKG2D ligands (MICA, MICB, ULBP-1, ULBP-2/5/6, and ULBP-3) [191].

The hinge region, known as the extracellular spacer, plays a significant role in anchoring the scFv to the TM domain and acts as a connector and a key motif of the quality of CAR cell products [204]. The length and amino acid composition of the hinge region can significantly impact how CARs interact with target antigens. The optimal hinge lengths may vary depending on the specific location of the epitope on the target cell [205]. In CAR-NK cell engineering, the spacers incorporated into CAR constructs are predominantly derived from modified IgG, CD8α, and CD28 proteins [206–208]. CARs employing a CD8 hinge structure have been systematically refined to enhance the flexibility and accessibility of the CAR in NK cells [209]. Furthermore, the integration of costimulatory signals alongside the hinge region has shown considerable promise in boosting NK cell activation, thereby augmenting their therapeutic effectiveness against various malignancies [210].

TM domain

The TM domain is critical for maintaining the structural integrity of CARs, facilitating optimal orientation, and robust signal transduction. The fundamental link between the extracellular antigen-binding and intracellular signaling domains enables effective communication and functionality [211]. Current research endeavors aim to optimize these domains to enhance the persistence and cytotoxicity of CAR-NK cells, particularly in the context of cancer immunotherapy. The initial designs of CAR-NK cells incorporated TM domains derived from CD8α or CD28, mirroring the approach employed in CAR-T cells. However, recent research has unveiled TM domains from NK-specific receptors, including NKG2D, CD28, CD16, NKp44, NKp46, DNAM-1, and 2B4 [177].

Intracellular signaling domain

CAR-NK binding to a target antigen triggers the intracellular signaling domain, activating signal transduction and leading to cytokine secretion and the lysis of target cells [9]. Several endodomain components can be incorporated into CAR designs to enhance the efficacy of NK cells. The CD3ζ is a prevalent element in CAR, which entails three immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) responsible for transmitting a primary signal [212]. Additionally, various co-stimulatory components, such as 4-1BB, CD28, NKG2D, DNAM-1, and 2B4, serve as key endo-domain elements that mediate secondary or costimulatory signals [212]. Notably, newly identified tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor (TRAF)-binding domains have demonstrated considerable potential in enhancing the persistence and cytotoxicity of NK cells, particularly in environments characterized by low cytokine availability [213].

CAR-NK generation

Since the discovery of CARs in the late 1980 s, there have been considerable advancements in CAR-related therapies to improve key parameters, including activation, expansion, persistence, safety, and efficacy. CAR-NK/T therapies have evolved through first-, second-, third-, and next-generation therapies, incorporating modifications to the end-domain structures and varying the number of co-stimulatory molecules (Fig. 3B) [214].

During the first generation of CAR-NK, only the ITAM signaling domain, mainly CD3ζ, was used for primary signaling without co-stimulatory molecules. The second generation contains an additional intracellular co-stimulatory domain, while the third generation of CAR-NK combines two or more co-stimulatory domains. Generally, CD28, 4-1BB (CD137), 2B4 (CD244), CD27, NKG2D, and DAP-10/12 are costimulatory domains that increase cytotoxic potential and promote cytokine production. The fourth and fifth-generation CARs are both based on the second-generation CAR. Fourth-generation CAR-NK functions as transgenic cytokine carriers, releasing cytokines such as IL-7, IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, and IL-23, or their combinations, thereby enhancing the immune response against tumors through cytokine secretion and supporting the survival of CAR-NK cells [203, 215].

A fifth-generation CAR-NK, which binds STAT sequences and carries an intracellular cytokine receptor such as the IL-2 receptor (IL-2R), enhances cell activation and proliferation [216]. In addition to cytokine genes, the incorporation of a suicide gene into the CAR structure enhances its anti-tumor effects and regulates its activity [217, 218].

Delivery system to engineered NK cells

The central systems for transferring genetic material into cells are generally divided into viral and non-viral vectors. Viral vectors include retroviral vector (RV) methods, lentiviral vector (LV) methods, and adeno-associated viral (AAV) methods. Non-viral methods are based on mRNA/DNA electroporation and DNA-mediated integration as discussed below (Fig. 3C and D).

Viral vectors

Viral vectors offer a slow and sustained method for gene delivery [219]. RVs are the first transduction methods that do not alter the function, phenotype, or proliferative properties of NK cells [220]. Until recently, CAR-NK cells generated based on RVs reached promising preclinical results in MM [201, 221, 222]. Additionally, the Phase 1 clinical trial utilized these vectors to introduce anti-CD19 CAR into NK cells, resulting in favorable clinical outcomes [223]. However, RVs specifically transduced the CAR structure into actively dividing cells [220].

LVs are introduced with high potential for transduction into both dividing and non-dividing cells to overcome this limitation [224]. LVs also indicate optimal efficacy in CAR structure transduction to primary NK cells [225]. Despite this, LVs display higher genotoxicity since they lead to alternative splicing and produce abnormal transcripts after integration into the human genome [226]. The LVs' transduction methods provided effective CAR-NK directed to BCMA, CD138, CS1, and NKG2D in MM [191, 192, 200, 227].

AAV is another vector for CAR structure delivery to NK cells [228]. The AAVs also deliver clustered, regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR/Cas9) to suppress NK cell inhibitory receptors [229]. Although AAVs present a simple and effective delivery platform in CAR-NK cell therapies, their limited packaging capacity restricts the incorporation of long gene sequences [160].

Non-viral vector

NK cells' innate immune properties limit the application of viral vectors, as NK cells react to them [220]. Electroporation is one of the earliest and most useful non-viral methods. In this approach, a quick electrical pulse is delivered to the cells, which creates temporary permeability, enabling the uptake of DNA or RNA that encodes the target genes [220]. Recently, IL-2-expanded NK cells have been reported to transfer plasmid DNA with a 40% efficiency via electroporation [230]. Despite DNA electroporation delivering complex cargo into target cells, it hampers the viability of NK cells [231]. The development of high-purity CAR mRNA electroporation has significantly increased CAR transfection [232]. The disadvantage of this method is the temporary expression of transferred genes, which limits the application of the electroporation method [219]. Additionally, high-voltage pulses induce cell death, necessitating the optimization of parameters to limit damage [233].

The emergence of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) in coronavirus vaccine production has revolutionized non-viral gene delivery to transport CAR-mRNA into NK cells [234]. Golubovskaya et al. employed LNP-mRNA technology to transduce CD19-CAR and BCMA-CAR into PB-NK cells. The CAR expression assay revealed 78.8% and 95.3% CAR-positive cell populations in CD19-CAR-transfected and BCMA-CAR-transfected cells, respectively [193].

New gene editing technologies, such as CRISPR/Cas9 and advancements involving trogocytosis and transposons, have emerged recently. The CRISPR/Cas9 system contains a single guide RNA (sgRNA) directing Cas9 to create double-strand breaks (DSBs) at the specified location. Then, the desired genetic sequence is introduced to the host genome, then the DSBs are repaired by the host cellular machinery [235]. In MM, CRISPR/Cas9 is used to knock out CD38 in CAR-NK cells to prevent fratricide (NK cells destroying their siblings) [221]. The CRISPR/Cas9 has garnered attention due to its high specificity, effectiveness, and manageability. Despite these advantages, CRISPR/Cas9 off-target effects, applicability, safety, and biocompatibility limit its therapeutic potential [236]. Trogocytosis originates from the Greek word trogo, meaning 'gnaw', and refers to immunological synapses where lymphocytes and NK cells interact with APCs and acquire membrane patches [237]. NK cells cocultured with a K562 cell line expressing CCR7 receptors result in 80% of NK cells expressing CCR7 after 1 h, a requirement for NK cells to home in the lymph nodes [238]. Therefore, trogocytosis has the potential for engineering NK cells. In addition, trogocytosis-mediated CAR engineering increased CAR-NK fratricide and patient relapse [239].

Transposons are mobile elements acting through a “cut and paste” mechanism, where the transposase enzyme recognizes specific sequences at the ends of the transposon (foreign DNA) and excises the transposon from one location before inserting it into a new site (host genome) in the genome to stabilize the integration of genes and long-term expression in host cells [240] and introduce the CAR transgene into NK cells. Transposon-modified primary NK cells showed stable and high CAR expression and increased cytotoxicity and vigorous anti-leukemic activity compared to non-transfected NK cells [241].

CAR-NK cell therapy in MM: from bench to bedside

Preclinical studies

Studies based on viral transfection methods

In Ren et al., variable domains of heavy-chain antibodies (VHHs)-directed BCMA-CAR constructs were produced in four distinct structures bearing different intracellular domains (2B4 versus CD28) or different hinge domains (CD28 versus IgG1), which ectopically produced IL-15 [192]. VHHs, also known as single-domain antibodies or Nanobodies, are derived from camelid-derived heavy-chain-only immunoglobulins, which are small in size, thereby enhancing their penetrability, stability, and solubility. Furthermore, the short circulatory half-life of VHHs presents them as an invaluable tool for clinical approaches, such as CAR manufacturing [242]. Ren et al. designed PB-NK cells expressing CAR with the VHH extracellular domain. The results BCMA-CD28-IL15 CAR-NK showed greater cytotoxicity against BCMA-positive tumor cells (MM.1S cells and NCI-H929 cells) compared to BCMA-CD28 CAR-NK (lacking IL-15) and BCMA-hIgG1-IL15 CAR-NK (IgG1 as hinge domain), as elucidated by higher INF-γ, granzyme B, and IL-15 production. On the other hand, BCMA-CD28-IL15 CAR-NK exhibited low cytolytic activity against BCMA-negative RS4;11 cells. Besides in vitro investigation, BCMA-CD28-IL15 CAR-NK abrogated MM growth in an NSG mouse model. In mice treated with BCMA-CD28-IL15 CAR-NK cells, no weight loss was observed. In contrast, mice in the control group (administered with cryoprotectant) and mock-NK group (administered with NK cells without CAR expression) exhibited weight reduction [192].

Despite CAR expression improving NK cell anti-tumor activity, the effective migration of NK cells to the MM niche faced challenges. One reason may be the downregulation of chemotactic receptors, such as CXCR4, on the surface of NK cells. CXCR4 interacts with CXCL12 in the tumor milieu to direct the cells to the tumor niche. Now, the question arises whether equipping CAR-NK cells with CXCR4 could enhance NK cell functionalities. For this purpose, Moles et al. evaluated the CAR-CXCR4-NK-92 cell line and the CAR-CXCR4-primary NK cell, which express a higher amount of CXCR4 compared to the CAR-NK-92 cell line and the CAR-primary NK cell, respectively. Furthermore, NK cells modified with mutant CXCR4 (CAR-CXCR4R334X) show greater CXCR4 expression compared to their wild-type CXCR4 (CAR-CXCR4) counterpart. Additional investigation highlighted that BCMA redirected the CAR-CXCR4-NK-92 cell line not only to further NK cell migration into the tumor site, and triggered NK cell cytotoxicity/degranulation capacity against the MM cell line, but also prolonged CAR surface expression upon NK cell and mediated ZAP-70 recruitment toward CAR-NK cell synapse follow-on CAR activation in the presence of lower amount of antigen. Consequently, CXCR4 expression upon CAR-NK enhanced NK cells' migration toward the tumor site, intensified its cytotoxicity, and strengthened its signal transduction [201].

Although BCMA is the most promising antigen for CAR-NK cell engineering, CD38 is another MM-related antigen that is also being investigated. In Karvouni et al. study, the αCD38-CAR-NK-92 cell line was found to have optimal IFN-γ and degranulation capacity toward the MM cell lines (38high RPMI-8226 and CD38dim MM.1S). In addition, the αCD38-CAR-NK-92 cell line was established, which enhances NK cell killing activity in a CD38-dependent manner, as its cytotoxicity in CD38high BM-derived cells from MM patients is higher compared to that in their CD38dim counterparts. The next step of the experiment demonstrated that patients' PB-derived autologous αCD38-CAR-NK cells potentially recognize autologous myeloma cells [222]. Congruently, Hambach et al. transduced the NK-92 cell line with CD38-specific Nb-CAR, followed by luminescence and flow-cytometry assessment of CAR-dependent cellular cytotoxicity assays (CARDCC) against MM cell lines. Both luminescence and flow-cytometry results outline that CD38-Nb-CAR-NK-92 performed CARDCC on the CD38-positive cell lines dose-dependently but not against the CD38KO cell lines (CD38-deficient tumor cells generated by CRISPR/Cas9). Lastly, CD38-Nb-CAR-NK-92 robustly eliminates primary BM-derived CD38-positive cells from patients with MM compared to control Nb-CAR-NK-92 [221].

CD38 expression on the NK cell surface presents a barrier to this approach because CD38-CAR depletes CD38-expressing NK cells, a phenomenon known as fratricide [222, 243]. IL-2-stimulated NK cells may be helpful to overcome this obstacle. In PB-derived NK cells from healthy donors, ex vivo expansion in the presence of IL-2 resulted in reduced CD38 expression. Similarly, this effect was reported in PB-NK cells derived from patients with MM. Subsequently, αCD38-CAR-CD38dim PB-NK cell effectively activates against CD38-positive MM cell lines [222]. The CD38 knockout, achieved using the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing method, may also be beneficial. The fratricide of CD38KO-CAR NK (NK cells underwent CD38 knockout) was reduced in the presence of daratumumab (anti-CD38 mAb), while control cells (NK cells do not bear CD38KO) displayed a higher level of fratricide [243]. Similarly, Troy et al. designed CD38KO-CAR NK cells that potentially eradicated the MM cell line (H929) and primary MM cells derived from patients [244].

CD138 is another target antigen for designing CAR-NK cells against CD138-presenting MM cells. CD138-CAR-NK-92 significantly induced higher cytotoxicity (in a dose- and time-dependent manner) and apoptosis in CD138-positive H929 and RPMI-8226 cells as opposed to wild-type (WT) counterpart (WT-NK-92 cells). Notably, CD138-CAR-NK-92 selectively lyses CD138-expressing cells, as evident by the lysis of dual CD138/CD19-positive ALL-derived REH cells, but exhibits minimal cytolytic effect toward single CD19-positive BL-derived Raji cells and double CD138/CD19-negative CML-derived cells. Consequently, CD138-CAR-NK-92 corresponds to CD138-positive cells killing without cross-reactivity with other antigens [200].

Additionally, CS1 may be a preferred target for MM treatment in CAR-mediated immunotherapy. Accordingly, Chu et al. designed CS1-CAR-NK-92 specifically to eliminate CS1-positive MM cells (IM9 and L363) but not CS1-negative counterparts (U266) compared with mock-transduced NK-92 cells. Similar results were obtained from the NKL cell line transduced with the CS1-CAR structure. CS1-CAR-NK-92/NKL cells exhibit enhanced cytotoxicity against CS1-expressing myeloma cells in an antigen-dependent manner, with increased IFN-γ secretion observed upon recognition of CS1-expressing MM cell lines in compared to mock-transduced NK-92. Notably, CS1-CAR-NK-92 cells show significantly enhanced cytotoxicity and IFN-γ production against U266 cells with enforced CS1 expression compared to control U266 cells, thereby confirming the specificity and efficacy of CS1-directed cytotoxicity. The CS1-CAR-NK-92 eradicates ex vivo primary MM cells, which are resilient to NK-92-mediated cytotoxicity. An investigation of NSG mice indicated that CS1-CAR-NK-92 prolonged the survival of mice injected with MM cells. Therefore, in vivo experiments on the NSG mouse model support in vitro and ex vivo findings [227].

NK cell receptors can also be used for CAR engineering. In Leivas et al., autologous NK cells and CD45RA-memory T cells were isolated from MM patients (n = 9), followed by NK cell activation and expansion. Then, activated and expanded NK cells (NKAE) and CD45RA− T cells were engineered to express the NKG2D-CAR structure without chromosomal and phenotypic aberrations. NKG2D-CAR-NKAE cells exhibited potent cytotoxic activity against MM cell lines, especially against those expressing the highest levels of NKG2D ligands (MICA, MICB, ULBP-1, ULBP-2/5/6, and ULBP-3). NKG2D-CAR-NKAE cells deliver robust cytotoxicity compared to non-transduced NKAE cells, memory CAR-T cells, CAR-T cells, and memory T cells. In addition, NKG2D-CAR-NKAE cells demonstrated a higher efficacy in reducing the clonogenicity of MM cells compared to NKAE cells. Next, the in vivo experiment revealed that CAR-NKAE/NKAE cells inhibited MM growth, leading to prolonged mouse survival compared to untreated mice. Notably, CAR-T/memory T cell infusion was ineffective in increasing mouse lifespan, and mice exhibited weight loss during the experiment. Finally, CAR-NKAE cells were safe against normal PBMC and lung cell lines [191].

Studies based on non-viral transfection methods

Besides viral transduction methods, non-viral strategies, such as LNP-mRNA technology, are also applied to CAR-NK engineering, as demonstrated by Golubovskaya et al. The mRNA of CD19-CAR and BCMA-CAR was incorporated into LNPs to deliver the CAR structure to NK cells isolated from PBMC. Next, in vitro, cytolytic activity assay determined that both CAR-bearing NK cells target cancerous cells via IFN-γ and granzyme B secretion in a dose-dependent manner. CD19-CAR-NK cells remarkably kill CD19-positive leukemia cell lines (Nalm-6) compared to normal NK cells. A congruent, similar effect was obtained from BCMA-CAR NK cells against BCMA-positive MM cell lines (RPMI-8226 and MM1S) [193].

Similarly, in another experiment by Edri et al., PB-NK cells were transduced with mRNA encoding CD38-CAR via electroporation. Compared to mock-electroporated and untreated NK cells, CD38-CAR-PB-NK cells cultured with nicotinamide (NAM) showed higher mortality rates due to fratricide. Similar results were obtained when CD38-CAR-PB-NK cells were cocultured with CD38-positive RPMI8266 cells. Additionally, fratricide levels increased in three groups in the presence of anti-CD38 mAb (Daratumumab). The CRISPR/Cas9-based CD38KO in PB-NK cells, followed by CD38-redirected CAR engineering, nearly eliminates fratricide with a 24-fold reduction in NK cell self-lysis. Furthermore, CD38KO-CAR NK cells were associated with elevated CD38-directed in vitro killing activity, degranulation, and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, resulting in the killing of MM cells and retaining native NK cell receptor function [243].

Another available non-viral method is known as the piggyBac DNA transposon system. Interestingly, this approach exhibits superior efficacy for NK-92 modified CAR-NK cells, as the NK-92 cell line possesses antiviral properties and cannot be successfully transduced using viral methods. In this line, Motais et al. used electroporated NK-92 cells to transfer plasmids carrying BCMA-CAR, a soluble form of the TRAIL (sTRAIL), and a transposase [199]. The BCMA CAR-NK-92 cell line, expressing sTRAIL, cocultured with MM cell lines (RPMI 8226, MM1.S, U266, and KMS-12-PE), resulted in decreased tumor cell viability, as well as elevated levels of IFN-γ, CD107A (a degranulation marker of differentiation), and activation receptors involved in killing MM cells. They also discovered that the BCMA-CAR-NK-92-TRAIL combination therapy with bortezomib (BZ) and γ-secretase inhibitors (GSIs), promoted transgenic NK cell cytotoxicity, as discussed in Sect. "Combination CAR-NK cell therapies in MM;Combinations with drugs" [199]. Last update of the ASH congress

The abstract presented at the ASH congress by Reiser et al. investigated the combination of FT555 with the anti-CD38 mAb (daratumumab). CAR-iNK FT555 targets GRPC5D, expressing high-affinity, non-cleavable CD16 (hnCD16) to enhance ADCC, IL-15/IL-15 receptor fusion protein (IL15RF) to increase cytokine-independent toxicity, and knockout of CD38 to eliminate fratricide. As a result of FT555 combined with daratumumab targeting GPRC5D and CD38, CAR-NKs are resistant to fratricide, exhibit dose-dependent cytotoxicity, and persist for an extended period (ASH 2022) [245]. Fu et al. also indicated that GPRC5D is a suitable therapeutic target in MM. They engineered anti-GPRC5D iPSC-derived CAR-NK (CAR-iNK), which is remarkably cytotoxic against MM cells expressing both BCMA and GPRC5D. Interestingly, CAR-iNK preserves its cytotoxicity even after cryopreservation and long-distance transport (ASH 2023) [246].

In another study, Yang et al. reported the results of CIB315, a CAR-iNK against MM cells. CIB315 contains multiple genetic modifications, including a CAR specific to GPRC5D, knockout of CD38 to prevent fratricide, and metabolic reprogramming for longer persistence. Their results indicated that CIB315 exhibits potent in vitro/in vivo killing of myeloma cells, particularly in combination with daratumumab. In addition, CIB315 persists for at least two weeks in animal models. Furthermore, CIB315 preserves its cytotoxicity potential after cryopreservation (ASH 2024) [247].

The generation of dual-CAR-expressing NK cells potentially augmented the CAR-NK activity, as indicated by Cao et al. In this study, a dual BCMA/GPRC5D CAR-NK was designed, which effectively lysed low- and high-expressing BCMA/GPRC5D and BCMA-negative MM cells (ASH 2022) [248].

Castellano reported that next-generation anti-BCMA CB-derived CAR-NK overcomes immunosuppressive TME. In this study, CRISPR/Cas9 was utilized to knock out KLRC1 (which encodes NKG2A) and TGFBR2 (which encodes TGF-β receptor) to overcome the inhibitory effect of the NKG2A-HLA-E axis and TGF-β, respectively. This genetic modification enhanced the in vitro antitumoral efficacy of double knockout (DKO) CAR CB-NK cells against MM cell lines, even in the presence of TGF-β. However, DKO CAR CB-NK cells show reduced proliferative capacity. For this, triple knockout (TKO) CAR CB-NK cells are generated through knocking out KLRC1, TGFBR2, and PRDM1. The TKO CAR-NK is similar to the DKO counterpart in the in vitro cytotoxicity against MM cells, and has a higher proliferation capacity than DKO CAR-NK (ASH 2024) [249].

Clinical trials

Following the emergence of CAR-NK immunotherapy in hematological malignancies based on promising results from preclinical studies, various clinical trials in different regions were registered in clinicalTrials.gov. The reported results indicated hopeful outcomes for patients enrolled in these trials. Liu et al. conducted the first human clinical trial (NCT03056339) of CAR-NK therapy on CD19-positive NHL or CD19-positive chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL). In this phase of a 1/2 trial, the safety and efficacy of CD19-CAR-NK were evaluated after infusion to 11 patients with R/R CD19-positive malignancies (at one of these doses: 1 × 105, 1 × 106, or 1 × 107/Kg). The results indicated that CD19-CAR-NK, which persists in circulation for at least 12 months, is not associated with CRS, neurotoxicity, GVHD, and increased inflammatory cytokines levels, and also results in 73% (8/11) overall response (OR) rate and 64% (7/11) complete response (CR) rate [190]. Additionally, in the recent Phase 1 clinical trial (NCT05472558), CD19-CAR-NK cells with a 4-1BB costimulatory domain were infused into nine patients with R/R large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL). The results outlined 62.5% OS at day 30, median 9.5 months PFS, CR in four patients (50%), and no GVHD, CRS, and ICANS cases [250].

These promising reports encourage other researchers and clinicians worldwide to prioritize CAR-NK-based clinical trials, paving the way for improved outcomes in patients with hematological neoplasms. Therefore, many trials have been registered with published or ongoing complete/initial results. Yang et.al [251] provided a comprehensive overview of CAR-NK cell-based therapy for hematological malignancies, while Bahramloo et al. [252], Bakhtiyaridovvombaygi et al. [174], and Jing et al. [253], respectively, reviewed acute myeloid leukemia (AML), NHL, and T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) in more detail.

Numerous preclinical studies have demonstrated the feasibility and effectiveness of CAR-NK cells against MM, both in vivo and in vitro, emphasizing the potential of CAR-NK clinical studies for MM.

Currently, clinical trials are registered to evaluate CAR-NK cell therapy in MM. A search on ClinicalTrials.gov identified 11 underway clinical trials evaluating CAR-NK cell therapy for MM until June 2025. Dhakal et al. reported early results from the interim Phase I clinical trial of FT576 as monotherapy and in combination with daratumumab in patients with R/RMM (n = 9). FT576 is a novel multiplex anti-BCMA CAR-iNK engineered with three modalities, including (1) high-affinity 158 V, non-cleavable CD16 (hnCD16) as an ADCC enhancer; (2) IL-15/IL-15 receptor fusion as a persistency promoter, and (3) CD38 knockout to mitigate NK cell fratricide mediated by anti-CD38 mAbs, providing improved metabolic fitness and resistance to oxidative stress within the TME [254]. This trial was conducted based on promising results reported by Goodridge et al. Based on this study, FT576 overcomes antigen escape, prolongs CAR-NK persistence, increases CAR-NK expansion, and promotes deeper anti-tumor clearance. Furthermore, FT576 was synergistically enhanced when combined with anti-CD38 (daratumumab), anti-SLAMF7 (elotuzumab), and anti-CD19 (ASH 2020) [255].

The trial performed in four arms includes I) Regimen A (Single-dose FT576 monotherapy on Day 1, II) Regimen A1 (Multi-dose FT576 monotherapy on Days 1 and 15), III) Regimen B (Single-dose FT576 with daratumumab on Day 1), and IV) Regimen B1 (Multi-dose FT576 with daratumumab on Days 1 and 15). The trial results reported on July 18, 2022, indicated that in all patients in the first two dose levels of Regimen A (n = 6) and patients in the first dose level of Regimen B (n = 3), no dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) or any incidents of CRS, ICANS, or GVHD were recorded. Therefore, FT576 is safe and well-tolerated (ASH 2022) [254].

Combination CAR-NK cell therapies in MM

Combinations with drugs

Combinations of therapeutic drugs are available to enhance the CAR-NK response in MM. For this purpose, Motais et al. indicated that BCMA-CAR-NK-92-TRAIL combined with BZ and GSIs synergistically increased CAR-NK anti-tumor activity. The BZ as PI intercedes against TRAIL receptor-CR5 overexpression on MM cells, potentiating the sTRAIL-expressing BCMA-CAR NK-92 apoptotic effect. At the same time, GSIs prevent BCMA shedding from MM cells (an immune escape mechanism), enhancing CAR NK-92 anti-myeloma activity through BCMA arms [199].

Combinations with mAbs

Anti-GRPC5D CAR-iNK cells, combined with daratumumab targeting GPRC5D and CD38, resulted in the elimination of CAR-resistant GPRC5DKO target cells, enhanced durability of CAR-NK in vivo, intensified tumor growth suppression, and elevated mouse survival [245]. Similar results were obtained from the investigation by Goodridge et al. Their results indicated that CD38KO-CAR-NK killing activity against myeloma cells is enhanced when combined with daratumumab, elotuzumab, and anti-CD19 [255]. Another study by Yang et al. also investigated anti-GPRC5D CAR-iNK cytotoxicity enhanced in the presence of daratumumab [247].

Combinations with cytokines

NK cells' maturation, activation, and survival can be regulated by a panel of cytokines, including IL-2, IL-12, IL-15, IL-18, and IL-21, as well as type I interferons [256]. Among these, IL-15 is well-studied. IL-15 influences various aspects of NK cell functionality, including enhanced cytotoxicity, promotion of expansion, and activation [257]. In the field of MM, Ren et al. demonstrated that BCMA-CAR-NK expressing an ectopic IL-15 domain robustly eliminates MM target cells compared with BCMA-CAR-NK lacking IL-15 expression [192]. Besides, IL-15/IL-15 receptor fusion protein potentially increased CAR-NK persistency and expansion, triggering cytokine-independent function [245, 255].

Combinations with chemotactic molecule

NK cells migrate to the MM niche and retain their dependence on the interaction between specific molecules, such as CXCR4 and CXCL12. According to Moles et al., CAR-NK-92 and primary NK cells equipped with ectodomain-expressing CXCR4 were potentially redirected toward CXCL12 in the in vitro experiment. In addition, CXCR4 improved BCMA-CAR-NK-92 chemotaxis to myeloma TME and increased its cytolytic activity [201].

Bottlenecks and future direction

There are several advantages associated with CAR-NK cell therapy for MM, but identifying potential target antigens, low persistence, exhaustion, self-destruction, expression of inhibitory receptors impairing NK cell function, poor infiltration to TME, susceptibility to TME's immunosuppressive effects, ignorance by myeloma cells, and scale-up for clinical use are some of the challenges that lie ahead. Therefore, future CAR-NK cell-based therapy should benefit from strategies to mitigate these immunosuppressive mechanisms (Fig. 4). Optimization of CAR-NK structureCAR-NK cells produced in various structures exhibit varying levels of cytotoxicity. Accordingly, it is hypothesized that if the CAR-NK signaling domain is derived from NK-specific signaling elements, there may be increased CAR-NK functionality. For this purpose, Li et al. designed two distinct CAR-iNK, one expressing NKG2D-2B4-CD3ζ (NK-based CAR-iNK cells) and another containing CD28-41BB-CD3ζ (T-based CAR-iNK cells). The results verified that NKG2D-2B4-CD3ζ improved anti-tumor activity compared to its counterpart [175]. In agreement, Christodoulou et al. demonstrated CAR-NK compromising the 2B4 co-stimulatory domain instead of 4-1BB, resulting in higher CAR expression and augmented anti-tumor activity [258].

Optimization of CAR-NK structure

Optimization of CAR-NK structure

CAR-NK function can be enhanced by triggering NK cell activating receptors. In this line, integrating NKG2D in the CAR-NK structure yielded favorable results [175, 191]. CD16 is another activating receptor that is investigated. Zhu et al. produced an hnCD16 by a mutation resistant to cleavage within TME [259]. Castellano et al. demonstrated that hampering NK cell inhibitory receptors also enhanced CAR-NK function by knocking out the NKG2A-encoding gene, KLRC1 [249].

Additionally, incorporating cytokines into CAR-NK enhances the anti-tumor response. In this context, utilizing the secretory interleukin (sIL)−15 domain on CAR-expressing 2B4 enhances the effect in chronic antigen exposure [258]. As mentioned earlier, BCMA-CAR-NK expressing an ectopic IL-15 domain elicited a stronger immune response [192]. IL-15/IL-15 receptor fusion integrated into CAR-iNK (FT55 and FT576) also significantly optimizes CAR-NK activity [245, 255].2)Optimization of CAR-NK expansion

2)Optimization of CAR-NK expansion

Optimization of CAR-NK expansion

Effective NK/CAR-NK immunotherapy requires large-scale expansion methods to meet clinical needs. Both feeder-cell and feeder-free approaches offer benefits and obstacles. New practices were established to augment the advantages and address the challenges that arose.

A recent approach focuses on a novel multifunctional fusion protein produced by CHO-S cells. The protein binds to patients' B cells and induces the presentation of the IL-15 cytokine and the co-stimulatory 4-1BB molecule to patients' NK cells. As a result, NK cells are stimulated, activated, and expanded. This method yielded a 100- to 6,000-fold expansion in PB-NK isolated from MM patients. Additionally, expanded NK cells exhibit 95% efficacy after CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing. In conclusion, this technology is a feeder-cell-free approach with high capability for CAR-NK engineering [260].

Another strategy is to utilize the G-Rex cell culture platform. The G-Rex is a closed system with a gas-permeable membrane that mimics the physiological environment for substantial cell expansion [261]. For example, each G-Rex100 flask can be seeded with 5 × 106 NK cells and 5 × 107 irradiated K562-41BBL-mbIL-15 feeder cells in a specialized medium. This condition yields 5 × 108 NK cells that can be transferred to a WAVE bioreactor bag for a higher expansion rate and purity of CD56+CD3− NK cells, along with an insignificant number of CD3+ T cells, making it suitable for MM therapy [180].

Combining the G-Rex® platform with the feeder-cell-free method holds significant value. The NK Cloudz ™ is an appropriate candidate for this aim, which is based on polymer microspheres that induce NK cell activation and expansion via humanized anti-CD2 and anti-NKp46. Finally, the Cloudz ™ dissolved after exposure to its unique buffer. When cultured and supplemented with human platelet lysate instead of FBS, it yielded a pure NK cell population, resulting in an average 387-fold expansion of NK cells. Noteworthy is the viability and cytotoxicity of feeder-cell-free and FBS-free expanded NK cells, which are similar to those of NK cells expanded in K562-mbIL21-41BBL feeder cells [262].3)Enhancing CAR-NK lifespan

3)Enhancing CAR-NK lifespan

Enhancing CAR-NK lifespan

NK cells are preactivated with a combination of IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18, acquiring adaptive immune features, and are referred to as cytokine-induced memory-like NK cells (CIML-NK cells). CIML-NK cells with a long lifespan are increasingly recognized for their potential in immunotherapy for MM, AML/MDS/MPN, ALL, and lymphoma [96, 263–267] (See also NCT04634435, NCT01898793, NCT02782546, NCT04893915, NCT04354025, NCT04024761, NCT03068819). CIML-NK cells can be armed with a CAR structure to enhance their anti-tumor response. Recently, CIML-NK cells armed with a CAR structure were investigated in vitro against CD19-positive lymphoma cells and NPM1-mutated AML. The results indicated that CIML-NK exhibits higher killing activity than conventional NK and CAR-NK, and shows improved persistence in a xenograft mouse model [268–270].

4)Generation CAR-NK expressing the dual receptor

4)Generation CAR-NK expressing the dual receptor

Generation CAR-NK expressing the dual receptor

Dual CAR-NK

In CAR-T cell therapy for MM, dual targeting involves CAR-T binding to two distinct MM antigens (BCMA and TACI), thereby enhancing tumor-targeting sites and minimizing the likelihood of antigen-negative tumors escaping [271]. In another study, CAR-T targeting BCMA and CS1 simultaneously displayed a superior response compared to a single-expressing CAR-T [272]. Incongruent, dual CAR-NK-engineered targeting of CD19 and BCMA was associated with significantly higher IFN-γ and degranulation capacity than single-CAR NK [273]. Besides, compound CAR-NK targeting CD19/CD33 and CD19/22 were investigated by Fei et al. and Colamartino et al., respectively [274, 275].

In this regard, Cao et al. reported dual CAR-NK efficacy in MM and highlighted that BCMA/GRPC5D dual CAR-NK potentially lysed myeloma cells in vitro and prolonged mouse survival compared to single-targeting CAR-NK [248]. Similarly, Yang et al. designed a BCMA/GRPC5D dual CAR-NK cell potentially targeting both BCMA-positive and GRPC5D-positive myeloma cells, inducing a durable NK cell immune response [276].

TCR-expressing CAR-NK

CAR recognizes limited cell surfaces that express antigen, while the TCR is linked to MHC and presents intracellular antigens [277]. CAR-NK utilizes intracellular CD3ζ and signaling domains to primarily activate effector cells, resulting in excellent efficacy, although it is imperfect. Generation CAR-NK expressing active TCR/CD3 complex may be more efficient than the current CAR-NK [278]. Simon et al. co-transfected T cells with a TCR specific for gp100 and a CAR specific for CSPG4, potentially showing cytotoxicity and cytokine secretion [279]. Taking the lessons from TCR+ CAR-T, Walseng et al. successfully designed a TCR-CAR-NK that combines TCR-based reactivity to the diversity of antigens with CAR-mediated killing activity [280].

NK cell engagers (NKCEs)

NK cell engagers (NKCEs) constitute an immunologic synapse between NK cells and tumor cells, facilitated by the recognition of molecules expressed simultaneously on the surface of both NK cells and tumor cells. The NKCEs could be bivalent or trivalent molecules that target one antigen on target cells and two target antigens on the surface of tumor cells, respectively. The first is referred to as bispecific killer cell engager (BiKE), and the latter is known as tri-specific killer cell engager (TriKE) [281]. A wide range of inhibitory and activating receptors of NK cells are engaged by NKCEs such as CD16a, NKG2D, Nkp30, Nkp46, Nkp80, NKG2C, KIR2DL/KIR2DS, DNAM-1, 2B4, PD-1, IL-2/IL-15, TIGIT, and TIM-3 [170].

Currently, NK cells armed with BiKE are concurrently linked to CD16 on NK cells, and CD38 or BCMA on MM cells were investigated [282, 283]. Furthermore, CD38/CD3xCD28 tri-specific T-cell engager (TriTE) potentially benefits MM patients who have relapsed and/or are refractory to anti-CD38 mAb [284]. To date, there remains a deficiency of studies utilizing NKCEs and CAR-NK cells. Employing this idea could optimally recruit cytotoxic NK cells to myeloma cells.

5)Synergistic combination therapy

5)Synergistic combination therapy

Synergistic combination therapy

Combination with mAbs

NK cells combined with mAbs enhance NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity, whereas mAb resistance is overcome by NK cells. In the field of MM, different mAbs, such as daratumumab (Anti-CD38) and elotuzumab (Anti-CS1), synergistically enhanced NK cell activation, killing activity, and ADCC [285]. As discussed in Sect. " Combination CAR-NK cell therapies in MM;Combinations with mAbs", combining CAR-NK with daratumumab yielded favorable results. Therefore, the combination of CAR-NK cells with other mAbs is highly recommended in the MM context.

Besides, mAbs are utilized to reduce NK cell inhibitory receptors. Inhibition of NK cell inhibitory receptors boosted NK cell function. The IPH2101 and IPH2102 (lirilumab) are known anti-KIR mAbs that block inhibitory KIR signaling [286, 287]. Likewise, Monalizumab is an mAb that downregulates NKG2A, another NK cell inhibitory receptor, resulting in enhanced NK cell activity [288].

Combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)

Several immune checkpoints (ICIs), such as PD-1, LAG-3, TIM-3, TIGIT, and killer cell lectin-like receptor subfamily G member 1 (KLRG1), inhibit NK cell functions and induce NK cell exhaustion. Therefore, targeting the immune checkpoint with ICIs reverts NK cell exhaustion [289]. According to Benson et al., blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 axis with lenalidomide abrogated PD-1 expression on MM cells, thereby enhancing NK cell cytotoxicity [129]. Combining ICIs with CAR-NK is likely to yield favorable results, as reviewed by Yang et al. [289]. In the context of MM, it is also necessary to investigate whether a dual BCMA/GPRC5D CAR-NK combination, combined with anti-PDL1, enhances CAR-NK cytotoxicity and in vivo persistence [276].

Combination with immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs)

The IMiDs enhanced NK cell-induced cytotoxicity through various pathways, including increased degranulation/cytokine production, elevated expression of NK cell-activating receptors, decreased expression of NK cell-inhibitory receptors, and reduced surface expression of NK cell-inhibitory receptor ligands on tumor cells [290]. In MM, IMiDs also promote NK cell activity. These drugs induce MICA and PVR/CD155 upregulation in myeloma cells, thereby activating the NKG2D and DNAM-1 receptors on NK cells, respectively [291]. Consequently, IMiDs in combination with CAR-NK may also be beneficial.

Combination with Proteasome inhibitors (PIs)

PIs such as FAD-approved bortezomib are prescribed for MM patients and optimally elevate patients' survival [292]. A recent study by Sedloev et al. demonstrated that bortezomib sensitizes AML cells (in vivo and in vitro) to CAR-NK anti-leukemia activity [293]. The PI lactacystin increases NK cell cytolytic activity toward myeloma cells by reducing the ligand of the NK cell inhibitory receptor, HLA class I [294]. Thus, PIs might enhance the effectiveness of CAR-NK in MM.

Combination with oncolytic virotherapy (OVT)

Oncolytic Herpes Simplex Virus (oHSV) displays a synergic effect with NK cells in neuroblastoma. On the one hand, NK cells overcome immune cell deficiency within TME, and on the other hand, oHSV positively affects NK cell migration and degranulation [295]. Another study by Li et al. reported that oncolytic vaccinia virus-expressing chemokine CCL5 triggers the infiltration of NK cells armed with CCR5 (a CCL5 receptor) toward the tumor site [296]. According to Susek et al.'s investigation, the herpes simplex-based oncolytic viruses improved NK cell killing activity in MM [297]. Interestingly, OVs, such as reovirus, overcome the protective effects of stromal cells within the MM environment, thereby increasing IMiD efficacy [298]. Thus, when OVT is incorporated, CAR-NK therapy effectiveness could be augmented.

6)Improving CAR-NK cell infiltration into the MM niche

Improving CAR-NK cell infiltration into the MM niche

As discussed, CAR-NK combined with CXCR is effectively directed to the TME. Among them, CXCR4 and CXCR1 were incorporated into BCMA-CAR-NK and CD19-CAR-NK, respectively [201, 299]. Furthermore, CXCR2 was found to indicate increased NK cell homing in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) tumor sites [300]. According to Schomer et al., CCRs, such as CCR7, can also be implicated in CAR-NK migration to tumor niches [301]. Intriguingly, NK cell infiltration into TME can be triggered by OVs, as discussed above.

7)Targeting immunosuppressive TME

7)Targeting immunosuppressive TME

Targeting immunosuppressive TME

Targeting the inhibitory microenvironment could restore NK cell function in MM. As mentioned earlier, hypoxia is a key factor in suppressing the activity of NK cells. Then, HIF-1α suppression may be a helpful action [302]. As another strategy, the inhibition of immunosuppressive cells such as Treg, MDSC, and TAM, along with immunosuppressive metabolites (IDO, TGF-β, IL-10, TNF-α, and IL-6), is strongly recommended [303]. In this context, a recent study determined that CRISPR/Cas9-based knockout of the TGF-β receptor gene (TGFBR2) enhances CAR-NK cytotoxicity toward MM cells [249]. Regarding TGF-β signal transduction mediated by SMAD4 translocation to the nucleus, CRISPR/Cas9-based knockout of SMAD4 would also be beneficial. The SMAD4KO NK cells effectively proliferate, are cytotoxic, and produce cytokines. In addition, SMAD4KO NK cells are potential candidates for CAR-NK manufacturing, as they prevent NKG2D and CD16 downregulation while maintaining CD103 expression and inducing high cytotoxicity. The SMAD4KO NK cells are more functional than those treated with TGF-β inhibitors. Additionally, SMAD4KO NK cells are resistant to activin A-mediated suppression of NK cells. Particularly, activin A is a member of the TGF-β superfamily that signals through the SMAD molecule [304].

8)Reduce NK cell ignorance by myeloma cells

8)Reduce NK cell ignorance by myeloma cells

Reduce NK cell ignorance by myeloma cells

Myeloma cells escape from NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity (See Sect. " Myeloma cells escape from NK cells"). A dual CAR-NK design is suggested to overcome this challenge, as demonstrated by Cichocki et al. in lymphoma and leukemia [305]. The bispecific CAR-T (CS1/BCMA), which prevents myeloma cells from escaping CAR-T treatment [306], supports dual CAR-NK therapy. In the American Association for Cancer Research (AACR) annual meeting in 2023, Yang et al. reported promising results from dual BCMA/GPRC5D CAR-NK, which prevents the immune escape of BCMA-negative MM cells from CAR-NK [276]. Similar results were reported by Cao et al. at the ASH 2022 conference [248].

Hampering the shedding of NK cell-activating receptor ligands on myeloma cells can help restore NK cell activity toward cancerous cells. For this purpose, the anti-MICA α3 domain and the proteolytic shedding site were examined. This approach strongly activates NKG2D, an activating receptor, by inhibiting the shedding of MICA and MICB from the surface of the cancer cell [307].

9)Hampering NK cell fratricide

9)Hampering NK cell fratricide

Hampering NK cell fratricide

The CD38-expressing NK cell fratricide mediated by CD38-CAR or anti-CD38 mAbs restricted NK cell toxicity against myeloma cells. Therefore, developing a remedy to eliminate NK cell self-destruction would be helpful. Two known strategies can be employed for this aim, including 1) downregulating CD38 expression via NK cell expansion in the presence of IL-2 [222] and 2) knocking out CD38 from NK cells [243–245, 254, 255]. Thus, delving into new strategies for removing the fratricide obstruction to CAR-NK therapy in MM is required. Accordingly, a dual receptor CAR-NK was proposed, which should be examined.

10)Ethical considerations in CAR-NK cell therapy

10)Ethical considerations in CAR-NK cell therapy

Ethical considerations in CAR-NK cell therapy

The development of CAR-NK therapy necessitated addressing essential ethical issues, based on a commitment to patient care. Minimizing harm to researchers and patients is considered a cutting-edge ethical issue in CAR-NK therapy. Besides, the ethical considerations of a novel immunotherapy approach should cover both the pre-marketing and marketing phases. The experimental design and conducting clinical trials are incorporated into the pre-marketing phase. In the experimental design stage, researchers' expertise, research departments, experimental design methods, genetic modification, and cell sources should be adapted to the standards required for patients' treatment. During the trial phase, the requirements for eligible patients, including obtaining informed consent, maintaining confidentiality and trust, ensuring patient privacy, ensuring patient safety, and validating CAR-NK efficacy, are essential. Finally, in the post-trial or marketing stage, providing long-term patient care, easy access to the product, monitoring product cost-effectiveness, and assessing its long-term efficacy are essential [308–311].

11)Concluding remark: CAR-NK paving the road for the immunotherapy of MM

11)Concluding remark: CAR-NK paving the road for the immunotherapy of MM

Concluding remark: CAR-NK paving the road for the immunotherapy of MM

In this review, several key questions about the topic were focused on: What insights were established about the topic? What are significant statements? What are the latest achievements? What key idea should guide future investigation on this topic? In response to these questions, our review highlighted that:

MM is an incurable hematological cancer that occurs within an immunocompromised tumor microenvironment (TME). As the disease progresses, myeloma cells develop ways to avoid being killed by NK cells. Therefore, using the anti-tumor effects of NK cells combined with CAR technology to target TAAs shows potential for better outcomes in MM patients. This review thoroughly examines preclinical and clinical studies involving CAR-NK cell use in MM.

The preclinical in vitro investigations show that CAR-NK remarkably eradicates myeloma cells through ADCC, cytokine production, and degranulation. Additionally, preclinical in vivo research results showed that CAR-NK significantly reduced tumor growth in a mouse model and prolonged the mice's survival. The advanced generation of CD38KO CAR-NK inhibits the destruction of its siblings, designing a dual CAR-NK, utilizing additional elements, such as cytokines, chemokines, and hnCD16, upon the CAR-NK. These are intriguing inventions to potentiate CAR-NK functionality. This information provides the basis for registering CAR-NK clinical trials in MM. The early results of the Phase I trial demonstrated that CAR-NK is safe and well-tolerated in patients with R/RMM.

In conclusion, the future of CAR-NK cell therapy in MM presents significant potential to address existing limitations, as detailed in Sect. " Bottlenecks and Future Direction", which suggests an opportunity to further explore its efficacy and safety, establish standardized treatment protocols, and maintain a vigilant approach toward long-term effects. Ultimately, CAR-NK cell therapy represents a state-of-the-art advancement in the MM treatment, distinguished by its unique characteristics.
