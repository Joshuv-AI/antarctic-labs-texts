Well-Posedness for Dean-Kawasaki Models of Vlasov-Fokker-Planck Type
Fenna Müller, Max von Renesse, Johannes Zimmer

Abstract
We consider systems of interacting particles which are described by a second order Langevin equation. The class of equations considered includes the situation where the particle evolution is governed by Hamiltonian dynamics with additional damping and noise satisfying a fluctuation-dissipation relation. Also covered are systems of two equations describing an evolution of interacting agents, as arising in several descriptions of active matter, including models for flocking and swarming. We first show that such particle systems can be represented exactly by so-called equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics, which in this case are stochastic versions of a Vlasov-Fokker-Planck type equation. While the derivation given here is simple, it is a blueprint for the rigorous derivation of equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics. We then show a dichotomy previously known for purely diffusive (first order) systems carries over to the second order setting considered here: Solutions exist for suitable atomic initial data, in which case the solution is, properly scaled, the empirical density describing the particle system. For smooth initial data, however, we prove that no solution exists.



1
Introduction

Equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics are stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) describing the
collective evolution of particles. They are widely used in physics, among others to describe active matter
(e.g.,
[
22
]
). The mathematical analysis of such equations is in its infancy, despite significant
recent progress. Equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics can be thought of as the the mean-field limit of many
particle systems (such as the Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equation) “plus noise”, where the noise is of a
particular structure, reflecting a fluctuation-dissipation relation. Specifically, fluctuating hydrodynamics
is a mesoscopic description of interacting particle systems (as opposed to the mean-field limit describing
infinitely many particles). We show below that this description of many-particle dynamics is exact and can be
derived mathematically using a martingale formulation. The argument given below is a precise mathematical
derivation, complementing the noise replacement methods used in the physics literature
[
14
]
. This
method is applied here to derive new equations describing a large variety of models.

We consider fluctuating hydrodynamics for Vlasov-Fokker-Planck type equations, including hypoelliptic
equations, which describe the evolution of second order Langevin equations (that is, evolution involving
inertia). We show that for purely atomic initial data, such as empirical measures, solution exist in a
martingale sense, while provably no solutions exist for smooth initial data. This dichotomy between existence
and nonexistence was previously only known for purely diffusive
equations
[
32
,
31
]
, i.e., Fokker-Planck equations describing the evolution of
particles undergoing first order Langevin dynamics. One interpretation is that the equations of fluctuating
hydrodynamics considered here describe exactly the dynamics of an arbitrary number of particles experiencing
second order Langevin dynamics.

For instance, the result of this papers covers the collective evolution of
n
particles given by second order
Langevin equations of the type

\displaystyle\text{d}x_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=v_{t}^{i}\text{d}t,

\displaystyle\text{d}v_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=-\gamma v_{t}^{i}\text{d}t-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla G(x_{t}^{i}-x_{t}^{j})\text{d}t+\sqrt{\gamma}\text{d}W_{t}^{i},

where
G
is an interaction potential and
W_{t}^{i}
are independent Brownian motions. More general settings,
including interaction terms depending on the velocities and nonlinear dependence in the first equation, are
considered in Section
4.2
. The class of models covered in this setting includes various
models of active matter, see Section
4
.

As described in Section
2
, the system of particles evolving with this system of
equations can, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be equivalently be described by a stochastic Vlasov-Fokker-Planck
equation for the empirical measure density
\mu_{t}(x,v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i}}
, where

denotes the Dirac delta. For the example above, this stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-\nabla_{x}\cdot(v\mu_{t})+\gamma\nabla_{v}\cdot(v\mu_{t})+\nabla_{v}\cdot(\mu_{t}\nabla G\ast\nu_{t})+\frac{\gamma}{2}\Delta_{v}\mu_{t}+\nabla_{v}\cdot(\sqrt{\gamma\mu_{t}}\dot{W}_{x,v,t}^{v}),

(1)

where

The aforementioned dichotomy, established in Theorem

, shows that equations of fluctuating
hydrodynamics are not well posed in the sense of Cauchy, in fact are very unstable in the sense that even
small smoothing of atomic data with a kernel function immediately renders the equation insoluble. One
interpretation of this result is that equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics are an

the underlying particle system, and not meaningful for other initial data.

While particle systems and associated equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics thus encode the same setting and
are in this sense equivalent, they can offer different advantages. For example, the numerical simulation of
equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics can be much more efficient than the simulation of the underlying
particle model
[
25
,
8
,
7
]
. Also, equations of fluctuating
hydrodynamics are related to large deviation principles in a way which reveals the geometric structure of the
associated hydrodynamic limit
[
28
, Section 3.5]
. In this way, fluctuating hydrodynamics can be
useful to identify a path integral representation of the dynamics. Equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics are
also central to learn the “thermodynamic” evolution operator (such as the Wasserstein operator for
diffusion) from particle data

34
]
.

1.1
Context and related work

We first review the situation for purely diffusive (Fokker-Planck) type of equations, as it is much better
studied. The most prominent purely diffusive example is the Dean-Kawasaki equation, which is a field-theoretic
mesoscopic description of interacting particle systems obeying overdamped (first order) Langevin
dynamics
[
14
,
29
]
, cf.
[
27
]
for a recent survey. The resulting stochastic
partial differential equation (SPDE) is a singular supercritical SPDE with non-Lipschitz multiplicative
noise. It is known
[
32
,
31
]
that the equation is well-posed only for initial
data given by suitable atomic measures, such as empirical measures.

After the results of
[
32
,
31
]
, it was initially not clear whether the
dichotomy (existence for atomic initial data, nonexistence for smooth initial data) is a peculiarity of purely
diffusive Dean-Kawasaki equations. Here we show that this phenomenon holds for a very wide class of equations
of fluctuating hydrodynamics (in fact, we are not aware of an equation of fluctuating hydrodynamics in the
sense considered here where it does not hold).

As mentioned above, the dichotomy has immediate practical implications. It shows that the equations of
fluctuating hydrodynamics, including (
1
), covered by this result are very unstable: Every
attempt to smoothen the initial data with a (possibly small) mollifier immediately destroys the existence of a
solution. One possibility is thus to consider smoothened versions of the Dean-Kawasaki equation, as analyzed
in
[
10
,
17
,
21
,
36
]
. Then it can be shown that more regular,
spatially diffuse solutions exist. For equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics without regularisation in the
noise, however, particle methods need to be employed in the simulation, as in
[
19
,
26
]
;
methods assuming the existence of a smooth solution profile such as finite difference or finite volume methods
are not applicable. A rigorous justification of diffusive fluctuating hydrodynamics for
N
particles by
numerical discretizations to arbitrary order in
N^{-1}
is given in
[
8
]
. Furthermore,
see
[
2
]
for the approximation of McKean-Vlasov equations with additive noise by weighted
particle systems.

We close this section with a brief, and certainly incomplete, discussion of equations of fluctuating
hydrodynamics in different application areas. Variants of the Dean-Kawasaki equation
[
14
]
as the
prototypical equation of fluctuating hydrodynamics are widely used in very different application areas. In
fluid mechanics, they appear as models of hydrodynamic fluctuation theory, for example for thin film
models
[
24
]
and colloidal nucleation theory
[
35
]
. They are also used to study
pattern formation in bacterial colonies
[
5
]
and more generally in active matter

[
6
]
, for example to investigate motility-induced phase transitions. Dean-Kawasaki type
models exist in dynamical density functional theory
[
43
]
. There are also Dean-Kawasaki models for
systems, such as the Keller-Segel model
[
37
]
. For Dean-Kawasaki type equations in dynamic
density functional theory see
[
18
]
. Dean-Kawasaki equations also appear as models for crowd
behaviour, as alternative to agent-based simulations
[
25
]
. The stochastic gradient descent
used in Machine Learning can be viewed as a Dean–Kawasaki type process, as shown and analysed
in
[
41
,
23
]
.

Numerical evidence for the effectiveness of the Dean-Kawasaki field-theoretic descriptions of many particle
systems is presented in
[
7
]
and
[
44
]
, a rigorous error analysis is carried
out in
[
8
,
9
,

]
. Rigidity breaks down in case of singular
interactions
[
1
,
40
]
. In
[
15
]
, Dello Schiavo considers
Dean—Kawasaki-type equations induced from symmetric reversible diffusions with singular drift and a large
class of interactions, extending
[
16
]
by means of Dirichlet forms.

2
Derivation of equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics

We now sketch the derivation of an equation of type (
1
) from a particle system undergoing
Langevin dynamics. The approach we present here is straightforward, but has in our view two benefits: Firstly,
it puts the sometimes indirect derivation of related equations in the purely diffusive setting in the physics
literature on firm mathematical ground. Secondly, it shows that the resulting system of fluctuating
hydrodynamics is an
exact
representation of the underlying particle system, without any approximations
involved.

To work with a specific situation, we consider the case of particles obeying

\displaystyle\text{d}x_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=v_{t}^{i}\text{d}t,

(2)

\displaystyle\text{d}v_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla H(v_{t}^{i}-v_{t}^{j})\text{d}t+\text{d}W_{t}^{i},

(3)

for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
x_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
v_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here
W_{t}^{i}
are independent Brownian
motions. We describe the evolution with a probability measure

\mu_{t}(x,v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(x_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i})}.

(4)

This seems very natural, but marks the crucial departure from previous
work
[
12
,
10
,
11
]
on such systems, where reduced models, where position
and time are the only independent variables. We chose a description of the system by a density which depends
on the position and the velocity of the particles,
(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In the physics literature, the derivation given by Dean
[
14
]
for purely diffusive particles now
applies the Itô formula for the measure
\tilde{\mu}_{t}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{t}^{i}}
. This results in an
expression which is not closed, as the noise depends on the position of the individual particles

\delta_{x_{t}^{i}}
, rather than
\tilde{\mu}_{t}
. In a clever but non-rigorous step, this noise is replaced with a
statistically identical one, which has the same correlations as the noise obtained in the Itô formula. This
resulting noise is
\nabla_{x}\cdot(\sqrt{\mu_{t}}\dot{W}_{x,t})
, where
W_{x,t}
is vector-valued space-time
white noise. This noise term is the “Dean-Kawasaki noise” of pure diffusion, i.e., the analogue
of (
1
) for pure diffusion, with
\gamma=1
.

We provide here a rigorous derivation, for the second order Langevin
equations (
2
)–(
3
). To this behalf, we test with smooth, compactly supported functions

\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We write
\langle\mu_{t},\cdot\rangle
for the space-velocity
integral against
\mu_{t}
. In the following formal calculation, the second equality uses (
2
),
(
3
) and the stochastic chain rule from Itô calculus. In the third equality,
\nu_{t}
is the
second marginal of
\mu_{t}
, i.e.,
\nu_{t}(V)=\mu_{t}(\mathbb{R}^{d},V)
:

\displaystyle\text{d}\langle\mu_{t},\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{d}\varphi(x_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{x}\varphi(x_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i})\cdot v_{i}\text{d}t-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{v}\varphi(x_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i})\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla H(v_{t}^{i}-v_{t}^{j})\text{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{v}\varphi(x_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i})\text{d}W_{t}^{i}+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{v}\varphi(x_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i})\text{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,v)\cdot v\mu_{t}(\text{d}x\text{d}v)\text{d}t-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{v}\varphi(x,v)\cdot(\nabla H\ast\nu_{t})(v)\mu_{t}(\text{d}x\text{d}v)\text{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Delta_{v}\varphi(x,v)\mu_{t}(\text{d}x\text{d}v)\text{d}t+M_{t}(\varphi),

where
M_{\cdot}(\varphi)
is a local martingale corresponding to the sum of Itô integrals above, with
quadratic variation

\text{d}\left[M_{\cdot}(\varphi)\right]_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\nabla_{v}\varphi(x,v)\right|^{2}\mu_{t}(\text{d}x\text{d}v)\text{d}t.

Integration in
time of the previous equality yields that for
t>0

\displaystyle\left\langle\mu_{t},\varphi\right\rangle

\displaystyle-\left\langle\mu_{0},\varphi\right\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\mu_{s},\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,v)\cdot v\right\rangle\text{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle{\mu_{s}},\nabla_{v}\varphi(x,v)\cdot(\nabla H\ast\nu_{t})(v)\right\rangle\text{d}s-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\langle{\mu_{s}},\Delta_{v}\varphi\rangle\text{d}s

is a local martingale with quadratic variation

\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\mu_{s},\left|\nabla_{v}\varphi\right|^{2}\right\rangle\text{d}s.

(5)

From this martingale problem, one can read off with integration by parts the following stochastic
Vlasov-Fokker-Planck (Dean-Kawasaki) equation:

\text{d}\mu_{t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\begin{pmatrix}-v\\
\nabla H\ast\nu\end{pmatrix}\mu_{t}\right)\text{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}\mu_{t}\text{d}t+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\mu_{t}}\text{d}W_{x,v,t}^{v}\right),

where

is again noise which is white in space

5

Rewritten in terms of divergence in space and velocity, the equation reads for
\mu_{t}=\mu_{t}(x,v)
To see
that the noise term in this SPDE induces in fact in the weak formulation the quadratic
variation (
5
), we test the solution of (
6

\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and integrate by parts with respect to

the real-valued semi-martingale
\xi_{t}=\langle\mu_{t},\varphi\rangle

\left[\xi,\xi\right]_{t}=\\
\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla_{v}\varphi(x,v)\sqrt{\mu_{s}(x,v)}\cdot\nabla_{v^{\prime}}\varphi(x^{\prime},v^{\prime})\sqrt{\mu_{s}(x^{\prime},v^{\prime})}\delta_{(x,v)}(\text{d}(x^{\prime},v^{\prime}))\text{d}(x,y)\text{d}s.

This leads after integration to (

\mathrm{d}\left[W^{k}(x,v),W^{j}(x^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right]_{t}=\delta_{k,j}\,\delta_{(x,v)}(\text{d}(x^{\prime},v^{\prime}))\text{d}t.

In this article, we consider a scaled version of (
6

of the scaling is to change the prefactor of the noise from
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
to
1
. This is achieved by
considering
\tilde{\mu}_{t}=\mu_{\alpha t}
with
\alpha=n
. This results in the equation

\text{d}\tilde{\mu}_{t}=\alpha\nabla\cdot\left(\begin{pmatrix}-v\\
\nabla H\ast\tilde{\nu}\end{pmatrix}\tilde{\mu}_{t}\right)\text{d}t+\frac{\alpha}{2}\Delta_{v}\tilde{\mu}_{t}\text{d}t+\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{t}}\text{d}W_{x,v,t}^{v}\right),

(7)

where
\tilde{\nu}
is the space marginal of
\tilde{\mu}
. The main result of this work asserts that in the
space of probability measures, solutions to the SPDE (
7

\alpha=n

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and the initial condition is of the form

\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(x_{0}^{i},v_{0}^{i})}
. In this case, the solution is given in the form

\tilde{\mu}_{t}(x,v)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(x_{nt}^{i},v_{nt}^{i})},

where
(x_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i})
solve the underlying Langevin equations (
2
)–(
3
). (Thus, we
consider empirical measures, hence the scaling is different from the one in
[
14
]
.) This result
will be shown in much greater generality holding for an entire class of SPDEs defined in the next section,
which comprises the model considered in this section.

2.1
Additional comments and relation to other models

Some remarks about this derivation are in order. Firstly, the derivation above can be readily extended to
other models which are structurally similar. For instance, one could add additional forces on the right-hand
side of (
2
) and (
3
) to obtain

\displaystyle\text{d}x_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=v_{t}^{i}\text{d}t+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F^{x}(x^{i}_{t},v^{i}_{t},x^{j}_{t})\text{d}t,

\displaystyle\text{d}v_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla H(v_{t}^{i}-v_{t}^{j})\text{d}t+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}F^{v}(x^{i}_{t},v^{i}_{t},x^{j}_{t})\text{d}t+\text{d}W_{t}^{i},

leading to the phase field SPDE

\text{d}\mu_{t}=-\nabla_{x}\cdot(v\mu_{t})\text{d}t+\nabla_{v}\cdot(\nabla H\ast\nu_{t}(x)\mu_{t})\text{d}t-\nabla\cdot((F*\nu_{t})\mu_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}\mu_{t}\text{d}t+\alpha^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\mu_{t}}\text{d}W_{x,v,t}^{v}\right),

where
\alpha=n,F=(F^{x},F^{y})
,
\nabla\cdot=(\nabla_{x},\nabla_{v})\cdot
is the full divergence with
respect to both coordinates
x
and
v
,
\nu
is the
x
-marginal of
\mu
and
F*\nu
is the convolution of

F
with
\nu
with respect to the
x
coordinate. This equation can be rescaled as above, resulting in the
prefactor of the stochastic perturbation becoming
1
and the prefactor of the deterministic part becoming

\alpha
.

Secondly, we note again that the derivation does not involve any heuristic steps or assumes the system to be
in local equilibrium, in contrast to previous
work
[
12
,
10
,
11
,
35
]
.

Thirdly, as already emphasized, the derivation shows that equations of fluctuating hydrodynamics are an exact
representation of the underlying particle system. This seems to have been unclear, for two reasons: The
physical derivation of the classical Dean-Kawasaki equation
[
14
]
considers a density
\rho_{t}

associated to the underlying particle system and uses Itô calculus to derive an evolution equation for this
density. The equation obtained is initially not closed, as the noise depends on the individual particles and
not on
\rho
. The noise is then replaced by a statistically equivalent one, which depends only on

\rho_{t}
. It is
a priori
not clear if this replacement is exact. The derivation above shows that it
indeed exact in the sense of Itô/martingale calculus.

Also from a mathematical perspective there was possible confusion whether equations of fluctuating
hydrodynamics are correct or only an approximation. The reason is that the limit of particle systems, for
example Brownian particles, converge under suitable assumptions in suitable scaling to the hydrodynamic limit
as the particle number
n
tends to infinity. For Brownian particles, the hydrodynamic limit, describing the
evolution of the limit measure
\bar{\mu}_{t}
, is
\partial_{t}\bar{\mu}_{t}=\Delta\bar{\mu}_{t}
. Then one can study
fluctuations around this limit, and the fluctuations are then described by

\nabla\cdot\left(\sqrt{\bar{\mu}_{t}}W_{x,t}\right)

[
30
, Chapter 11]
. Sometimes fluctuating
hydrodynamics is interpreted in form of an analogy, having the same form except with the coefficient

\bar{\mu}_{t}
, i.e., the limit density, being described by
\mu_{t}
, i.e., the measure describing finitely many
particles. The calculation above shows this is not just a mere analogy, but indeed the correct description.

3
Main result: Well-posedness and rigidity of Vlasov-Fokker-Planck Dean-Kawasaki type equations

We consider equations of the kind

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\alpha L^{*}\mu_{t}+\alpha\nabla\cdot(\mu_{t}F_{\mu_{t}})+\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{\mu_{t}}\sigma\dot{W}_{z,t}),

(8)

where

with

\dot{W}_{z,t}
on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
(If domain and target are of the same
dimension, we omit the superscript in the notation). Here
(\mu,z)\mapsto F(\mu,z)=:F_{\mu}(z)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

describes the interaction between the particles, where
z\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. The divergence
\nabla\cdot
is the full
divergence in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

For the existence result of Theorem

Sometimes we write

\displaystyle=\left<\mu_{t},\varphi\right>-\int_{0}^{t}\left<\mu_{s},\alpha L\varphi+\alpha F_{\mu_{s}}\cdot\nabla\varphi\right>\text{d}s

[M_{\cdot}(\varphi)]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\left<\mu_{s},|\sigma^{T}\nabla\varphi|^{2}\right>\text{d}s.

Above,
\langle\mu,g\rangle
denotes the dual pairing of measures and functions, and the expression

([M]_{t})_{t\geq 0}
stands for the quadratic variation of a general continuous (semi-)martingale

mathematical theory of Markov processes and their semigroups is arguably the most suitable framework for a
concise treatment. However, we chose to avoid the latter for the sake of a more self-contained
presentation. Instead we use the flexible notion of
martingale solutions
to an SDE.

\alpha=n

\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{z_{t}^{i}},

where the system

\text{d}z_{t}^{i}=\alpha\,b(z^{i}_{t})\text{d}t+\alpha F\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{z_{t}^{j}},z^{i}_{t}\right)\text{d}t+\sqrt{\alpha}\,\sigma(z^{i}_{t})\text{d}W^{i}_{t},\quad i=1,\cdots,n,

.

4
Examples

4.1
Inertial Langevin dynamics without interaction

The motivation of this work is inertial Langevin dynamics, i.e.,
n
particles which have at time
t\geq 0
a
position
x_{t}
and a velocity
v_{t}
, evolving by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{d}x_{t}&=v_{t}\text{d}t,\\
\text{d}v_{t}&=-\gamma v_{t}\text{d}t-\nabla U(x_{t})\text{d}t+\text{d}W_{t},\end{split}

(11)

where
W_{t}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion and
(x_{t},v_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume that

\gamma\geq 0
, that
U\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is uniformly bounded from below, and that
\nabla^{2}U
and

\nabla^{3}U
are bounded. To fit Equation (
11
) into the framework of
Equation (
8
), we set
k=2d
and write
z\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
as a combined vector of position and
velocity,
z=(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the drift vector
b
and the multiplicative noise
\sigma

in (
8
) are given by

\displaystyle b(x,v)=\begin{pmatrix}v\\
-\gamma v-\nabla U(x)\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0_{d\times d}\\
1_{d\times d}\end{pmatrix},

where
0_{d\times d}
is a
d\times d
matrix of zeros and
1_{d\times d}
is the identity matrix in
d

dimensions. This defines the operator
L
of (
9
).

In this case the corresponding degenerate Dean-Kawasaki Equation (Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equation) reads, with

F=0
, after rescaling in time as described in Section
2
, with
\alpha=n

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\alpha\left(\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}\mu_{t}-\nabla_{x}\cdot(v\mu_{t})+\nabla_{v}\cdot((\gamma v+\nabla U(x))\mu_{t})\right)+\nabla_{v}\cdot(\sqrt{\mu_{t}}\dot{W}_{x,v,t}^{v}).

Theorem
3.5
asserts that the unique solution is of the form

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(x_{nt}^{i},v_{nt}^{i})}
, composed of independent solutions

\{(x_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i})_{t\geq 0}^{i=1,\cdots,n}\}
of (
11
). To apply the theorem, we have show
that Assumption
3.2
on
L
is fulfilled.

Assumption
(L.1)
:
The conditions imposed on
U
imply that
b
is in

\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
with bounded first derivatives. Thus
b
is globally Lipschitz
continuous. This guarantees the existence and uniqueness of a strong solution to the
SDE (
11
). Furthermore, for any
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, by Itô’s formula, the process

\displaystyle M_{t}(\varphi)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{v}\varphi(x_{s},v_{s})\cdot\text{d}W_{s}=\varphi(x_{t},v_{t})-\varphi(x_{0},v_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}L\varphi(x_{s},v_{s})\text{d}s

is a local martingale, for
t\geq 0
. Further,
M_{\cdot}(\varphi)
is a square integrable martingale, since

[M_{\cdot}(\varphi)]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla_{v}\varphi(x_{s},v_{s})|^{2}\text{d}s\leq||\nabla_{v}\varphi||_{\infty}^{2}t.

Thereby,
(x_{t},v_{t})_{t\geq 0}
uniquely solves the
(L,\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{k}))
-martingale problem and
Assumption
(L.1)
is satisfied.

Assumptions
(L.2)
and
(L.3)
:
We choose
\mathcal{D}
to be the space of
functions
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
which are uniformly continuous and bounded together with their
derivatives up to second order. Due to the bounds on
\nabla^{2}U
and
\nabla^{3}U
, the vector field
b
is
Lipschitz continuous and the second derivatives of
b
are bounded. The system thus satisfies the assumptions
of
[
13
, Proposition 2.5]
. According to this result,
P_{t}\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{D}
and
there exists some
K>0
such that
||P_{t}\varphi||_{2,\infty}\leq K||\varphi||_{2,\infty}
for all

t\geq 0
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
. Here (and later),

||\varphi||_{2,\infty}=||\varphi||_{\infty}+||\nabla\varphi||_{\infty}+||\nabla^{2}\varphi||_{\infty}
for
functions in
\mathcal{D}
. This already implies Assumption
(L.3)
, since
\sigma
is bounded.
Furthermore, Propositions 3.3 and 3.6 of
[
13
]
together state that

LP_{t}\varphi=P_{t}L\varphi
and that
L\varphi
is a uniformly continuous bounded function, thus in
particular
P_{t}L\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{b}
. Hence Assumption
(L.2)
is satisfied as well.

Assumption
(L.4)
:
To establish this property, we first note that our assumptions on
U

imply that the semigroup induced by the solutions to (
11
) is Feller and, moreover,
by
[
45
, Proposition 1.2]
strong Feller. Hence
P_{t}1_{A}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2d}\right)
, if

A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is bounded and measurable.

In particular, for given
r>0
, there exists a
R>0
such that
P_{t}1_{B(0,r)}(z)\leq\frac{1}{2}
for all

|z|\geq R
. It thus remains to estimate
\max_{z\in\overline{B(0,R)}}P_{t}1_{B(0,r)}(z)
. Let

z^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
a point at which the maximum is attained. Now, by Girsanov’s theorem

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{t}^{z^{*}}|>r\right)=\int M_{t}1_{\{|Z_{t}^{z^{*},0}|>r\}}\text{d}P_{z^{*}}^{0}>0,

where

\displaystyle M_{t}=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{t}(\gamma v_{s}+\nabla U(x_{s}))\text{d}W_{s}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\gamma v_{s}+\nabla U(x_{s})\right|^{2}\text{d}s\right),

and
P^{0}_{z^{*}}
is the law of
(Z_{t}^{{z^{*}},0}=(x^{*}+\int_{0}^{t}W_{s}ds,v^{*}+W_{t}))_{t\geq 0}

solving (
11
) with
\gamma=0
and
U=0
starting from
{z^{*}}=(x^{*},v^{*})
,
see
[
45
]
. Since there is no explosions (see
[
45
, Lemma 1.1]
), it holds that
M_{t}>0

almost surely. Moreover
\{|Z_{t}^{z^{*},0}|>r\}\supset\{|v^{*}+\sqrt{2}|W_{t}|>r\}
is not a nullset. Hence,

\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{t}^{z^{*}}|>r\right)>0
, so that indeed

{\sup}_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\displaystyle P_{t}1_{B(0,r)}(z)\leq\max\left(\max_{z\in\overline{B}(0,R)}P_{t}1_{B(0,r)}(z),\frac{1}{2}\right)<1,

which yields Assumption
(L.4)
by choosing, for example,
A_{n}=B(0,n)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d},n\in\mathbb{N}
.

4.2
Active matter

We now consider a class of active matter models, where
n
individual agents at position
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are
driven by a propulsion mechanism
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{l}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for some
l\leq d
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{d}x_{t}&=g(\theta_{t})\text{d}t,\\
\text{d}\theta_{t}&=\text{d}W_{t}.\end{split}

(12)

In this case, the corresponding degenerate Dean-Kawasaki equation (Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equation) reads after
rescaling in time,
t\mapsto\alpha t
with
\alpha=n
,

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\alpha\left(\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\theta}\mu_{t}-\nabla_{x}\cdot(g(\theta)\mu_{t})\right)+\nabla_{\theta}\cdot(\sqrt{\mu_{t}}\dot{W}_{x,\theta,t}^{\theta}).

The classical example is
g(\theta)=v\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)\end{pmatrix}
for some
v\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the angle
\theta
can be interpreted as the direction the active
particle is propelled into. See, for example,
[
39
,
3
]
and references therein. We
assume that
g\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}\left(\mathbb{R}^{l},\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
. Then, a strong solution to the SDE (
12
)
exist; since the coefficients are Lipschitz continuous, the solution is unique. Hence

(x_{t},\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solves the
\left(L,\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d+l}\right)\right)
martingale problem for
the generator

\displaystyle L\varphi(x,\theta)=g(\theta)\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi(x,\theta)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\theta}\varphi(x,\theta).

With the same techniques as in Section
4.1
, one can show that
Assumptions
(L.2)
and
(L.3)
hold. Again
\mathcal{D}
consists of the space of
functions
\mathbb{R}^{d+l}\to\mathbb{R}
which are uniformly continuous and bounded together with their first and second
derivatives.
Finally, to see that
(L.4)
is satisfied, we set
A_{n}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times B(0,n)
. Then,

P_{t}1_{A_{n}}(x,\theta)\leq\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\mathbb{P}(W_{t}+\theta\in B(0,n))<1
for any
t>0
.

4.3
Fluctuating hydrodynamics for Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equation with interaction

We now extend the interaction-free example of Section
4.1
to the case of second order
Langevin particles with interaction, under the same conditions on the on-site potential
U
as in
Section
4.1
. In addition, we assume that particles experience an interaction

f_{\text{int}}\in C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is a function of the positions. That is,
F_{\mu}

depends on the spatial marginal
\rho(\cdot)=\mu(\cdot\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
via

\displaystyle F_{\mu}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x,x^{\prime})\text{d}\nu(x^{\prime})=f\ast\nu(x)

(with a slight abuse of notation of the convolution
\ast
in case the pair interaction does not only depend
on the difference of particle positions).
In this case, the corresponding Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equation (degenerate Dean-Kawasaki equation) is obtained
as described in Section
2
, with rescaling in time
t\mapsto\alpha t
, where

\alpha=n
is the number of particles,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\alpha\left(\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{v}\mu_{t}-\nabla_{x}\cdot(v\mu_{t})+\nabla_{v}\cdot((\gamma v+\nabla U(x))\mu_{t}+\mu_{t}(f*\rho_{t}))\right)+\nabla_{v}\cdot(\sqrt{\mu_{t}}\dot{W}_{x,v,t}^{v}),

and the corresponding particle system
\left(x_{t}^{i},v_{t}^{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
solves for
i=1,\dots,n

\displaystyle\text{d}x_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=v_{t}^{i}\text{d}t,

\displaystyle\text{d}v_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=-\gamma v_{t}^{i}\text{d}t-\nabla U(x_{t}^{i})\text{d}t-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(x_{t}^{i},x_{t}^{j})\text{d}t+\text{d}W_{t}^{i}.

One checks that condition
(F.1)
of Assumption
3.4
is verified by choosing

G(\mu)=\left<\mu,v\cdot F_{\mu}\right>,

so that indeed

\nabla_{v}\frac{\delta G}{\delta\mu}(x,v)=\nabla_{v}\left(v\cdot F_{\mu}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}v^{\prime}\cdot f(x,x^{\prime})\text{d}\mu(x^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right)=F_{\mu}(x).

Moreover,
G
satisfies the regularity and growth condition
(F.2)
.

4.4
Active swimmmers and flocking

We expand on the example of active matter in Section
4.2
to include flocking behaviour. In
the situation where the individual agents occupy a position
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and are driven by a propulsion
mechanism
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{l}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for some
d\geq k
, depending on a noise
\theta_{t}
as specified in
Equation (
12
), flocking behavior is induced by
interactions of the type

\displaystyle F_{\mu}(x,\theta)=\int\chi_{R}(|x-x^{\prime}|)\nabla H(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\text{d}\mu(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}),

(13)

where for some interaction radius
R>0
,
1_{B_{R}}\leq\chi_{R}\leq 1_{B_{R+\epsilon}}
is some smooth cut-off
function and
H\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
. This means that active swimmers interact with those in their
neighbourhood. A classical example is
H(\theta)=-\cos(\theta)
, that is particles tend to align.

In this case the corresponding equation of fluctuating hydrodynamics reads

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\alpha\left(\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\theta}\mu_{t}-\nabla_{x}\cdot(g(\theta)\mu_{t})+\nabla_{\theta}\cdot(\mu_{t}F_{\mu_{t}})\right)+\nabla_{\theta}\cdot(\sqrt{\mu_{t}}\dot{W}_{x,\theta,t}^{\theta}),

and the corresponding particle system solves

\displaystyle\text{d}x_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=g(\theta_{t}^{i})\text{d}t,

\displaystyle\text{d}\theta_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j\sim i}\chi_{R}(|x-x^{\prime}|)\nabla H(\theta_{t}^{i}-\theta_{t}^{j})\text{d}t+\text{d}W_{t}^{i},

where indices
i,j
satisfy
i\sim j
iff
|x_{t}^{i}-x_{t}^{j}|<R+1
and
W_{\cdot}^{1},\dots,W_{\cdot}^{n}
are
independent Brownian motions.

In view of Section
4.2
, to apply our main result, Theorem
3.5
, it remains to
check condition
(F.2)
of Assumption
3.4
is satisfied. Indeed, this is the case
if we assume that
H
belongs to
\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}
and is even, and choose

G(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\int\int\chi_{R}(|x-x^{\prime}|)H(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\text{d}\mu(x,\theta)\text{d}\mu(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}).

5
Proof of the main theorem

The proof proceeds in two steps, extending the the basic strategy of of
[
32
,
31
]
for symmetric Markov operators to the present situation of possibly degenerate diffusions
without symmetry. The central tool for the case
F=0
is a duality (Proposition
5.2
) involving
the Laplace transform of the measure valued random variable
\mu_{t}
. As a result, we obtain a representation
of its moment generating function in terms of a deterministic Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation. This allows to
establish regularity of the moment generating function near the origin, which yields the rigidity of the model
when
F=0
(Proposition
5.4
). In a second step the case
F\neq 0
is reduced to the
situation when
F=0
via an appropriate Girsanov transform (Proposition
5.9
). As main
difference to the previous works
[
32
,
31
]
, some additional care is needed due
to the reduced regularity of the associated (possibly degenerate) nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman semigroup
in the proof of the duality formula. In order to treat physically interesting models with with unbounded
drift, we establish the stability of the second moment of
\mu_{t}
as presented in
Lemma
5.5
. In particular, the rigidity statements of the previous works op. cit. are
extended to a wider class of interactions.

5.1
Interaction-free case

We now prove Theorem
3.5
for the noninteracting case,
F=0
. We restate the result for this case.

Proposition 5.1
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, a solution of the SPDE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}

\displaystyle=\alpha L^{*}\mu_{t}+\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\mu_{t}}\sigma\dot{W}_{z,t}\right)

(14)

with initial data

\displaystyle\mu_{0}

\displaystyle=\mu

(15)

and
\alpha>0
exists if and only if
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mu_{0}
is of the form

\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha}\delta_{z^{i}}
for some
z^{1},\dots,z^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. In
this case the solution is given by

\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha}\delta_{z^{i}_{t}},

where
\{z^{i}_{\cdot}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independent
\alpha L
-diffusions.

We recall that solutions are understood in the martingale sense, as stated in Definition
3.1
.
We first show that solutions in the martingale sense exists for initial data of the form

\mu=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{z^{i}}
, and defer the proof of the uniqueness and the rigidity to
Propositions
5.3
and
5.4
respectively. The existence of such
solutions with atomic initial data essentially follows from Assumption
3.2
,
part
(L.1)
and can be seen as rigorous extension of the derivation of the equations of fluctuating
hydrodynamics in Section
2
. Indeed, let us choose
N
independent solutions

(z_{\cdot}^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
to the
\left(\alpha L,\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}\left(\mathbb{R}^{k}\right)\right)
-martingale problem and set

\mu_{t}^{N}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{z_{t}^{i}}
. Then the process

\displaystyle M_{t}(\varphi):

\displaystyle=\left<\mu_{t}^{N},\varphi\right>-\left<\mu_{0},\varphi\right>-\int_{0}^{t}\left<\mu_{s}^{N},\alpha L\varphi\right>\text{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\varphi(z_{t}^{i})-\varphi(z_{0}^{i})-\int_{0}^{t}\alpha L\varphi(z_{s}^{i})\text{d}s\right)

is a sum of martingales and hence a martingale. Note that the rescaling by
\alpha
corresponds to a
multiplication of the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
by
\sqrt{\alpha}
. (In fact, from the definition of a
martingale solution, it follows immediately that if
\mu_{t}
is a solution of (
15
), then

\tilde{\mu}_{t}=\mu_{t/\alpha}
solves

\partial_{t}\tilde{\mu}_{t}=L^{*}\tilde{\mu}_{t}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\tilde{\mu}_{t}}\sigma\dot{W}_{z,t}\right)

with initial the same initial condition.) Due to the independence of the processes, the
quadratic variation of the aforementioned martingale is given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\sqrt{\alpha}\sigma^{T}\nabla\varphi(z_{s}^{i})|^{2}\text{d}s=\int_{0}^{t}\left<\mu_{s}^{N},|\sigma^{T}\nabla\varphi|^{2}\right>\text{d}s.

This shows existence of a martingale solution in the sense Definition
3.1
, for initial
conditions given by
\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{z^{i}_{t}}
.

Uniqueness in law follows from a Laplace duality, as in
[
32
]
, and the proof is is similar
with small modifications. Namely, we show in Proposition
5.2
that

\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-\left<\mu_{t},\varphi\right>}\right)=e^{-\left<\mu_{0},V_{t}\varphi\right>}

holds, where
V_{t}\varphi
is a solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=\alpha L\psi_{t}-\Gamma(\psi_{t})

(16)

with initial condition

\displaystyle\psi_{0}

\displaystyle=\varphi\in\mathcal{D},

(17)

where
\mathcal{D}
is chosen as in
(L.2)
. This equation admits the Cole-Hopf solution

\psi_{t}=V_{t}\varphi:=-\alpha\ln\left(P_{\alpha t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}\right)
. Indeed, since

f(y)=e^{-y}-1
is in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
f(0)=0
, we have that

\zeta:=e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}-1\in\mathcal{D}
and so

P_{t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}=P_{t}\zeta+1\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
and
\partial_{t}P_{t}\zeta=LP_{t}\zeta
,
both by Assumption
(L.2)
.
Hence, we can use the standard chain rule of calculus to compute

\displaystyle LV_{t}\varphi=-\alpha L\left(\ln(P_{\alpha t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}})\right)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\frac{1}{P_{\alpha t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}}LP_{\alpha t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}+\alpha\left(\frac{1}{P_{\alpha t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{2}\left|\sigma^{T}\nabla P_{\alpha t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\alpha\frac{1}{P_{\alpha t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}}LP_{\alpha t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Gamma(V_{t}\varphi)

with the carré du champs operator from Remark
3.3
, and also

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V_{t}\varphi

\displaystyle=\frac{-\alpha}{P_{\alpha t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}}\alpha LP_{\alpha t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}},

showing that
V_{t}\varphi
is indeed as solution.

Moreover, for the Cole-Hopf solution it holds that

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\varphi(x)}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}\leq P_{t}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}=e^{-\frac{\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\varphi}{\alpha}},

which leads to

||V_{t}\varphi||_{\infty}\leq||\varphi||_{\infty}\quad\forall t\geq 0.

Proposition 5.2

(Laplace duality)
.

Assume
\alpha>0
and
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
. Then for all non-negative functions

\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{k})

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-\left<\mu_{t},\varphi\right>}\right)=e^{-\left<\mu_{0},V_{t}\varphi\right>}.

(18)

Here, the expectation is taken with respect to the probability measure of the initial value
problem (
14
)–(
15
).

Proof.

First we assume that
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{D}
given as in Assumption
(L.2)
,
and
\varphi\geq 0
. Using approximations of time-dependent functions

\psi_{\cdot}\colon[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
of sufficient regularity with

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}||\psi_{t}||_{\infty}+||L\psi_{t}||_{\infty}+||\Gamma(\psi_{t})||_{\infty}<\infty
as
in
[
33
]
, a time-dependent version of the notion of martingale solutions given in
Definition
3.1
may be established. Namely,

M_{t}(\psi_{\cdot}):=\left<\mu_{t},\psi_{t}\right>-\left<\mu_{0},\psi_{0}\right>-\int_{0}^{t}\left<\mu_{s},L\psi_{s}+\partial_{s}\psi_{s}\right>\text{d}s

is a martingale with quadratic variation

[M_{\cdot}]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\left<\mu_{s},|\sigma^{T}\nabla\psi_{s}|^{2}\right>\text{d}s.

Now fix
t>0
and define
\psi_{s}=V_{t-s}\varphi
. Note that by the gradient bound
(L.3)

of Assumption
3.2
, the diffusion property and the fact that

e^{-\frac{\varphi}{\alpha}}-1\in\mathcal{D}
,
(\psi_{s})_{s\in[0,t]}
indeed satisfies the required
boundedness of
LV_{t-s}\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{b}
and
\Gamma(V_{t-s}\varphi)
. Thus, we can apply the
time-dependent Itô formula and obtain that
\left(e^{-\left<\mu_{s},V_{t-s}\varphi\right>}\right)_{s\in[0,t]}
is
a local martingale, since

\displaystyle\text{d}e^{-\left<\mu_{s},V_{t-s}\varphi\right>}

\displaystyle=-e^{-\left<\mu_{s},V_{t-s}\varphi\right>}\text{d}\left<\mu_{s},V_{t-s}\varphi\right>+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\left<\mu_{s},V_{t-s}\varphi\right>}\text{d}[M_{\cdot}(V_{t-\cdot}\varphi)]_{s}

\displaystyle=e^{-\left<\mu_{s},V_{t-s}\varphi\right>}\biggl(-\left<\mu_{s},\alpha LV_{t-s}\varphi+\partial_{s}V_{t-s}\varphi\right>\text{d}s\biggr.

\displaystyle{}\qquad\biggl.-\text{d}M_{s}(V_{t-\cdot}\varphi)+\frac{1}{2}\left<\mu_{s},|\sigma^{T}\nabla_{v}V_{t-s}\varphi|^{2}\right>\text{d}s\biggr)

\displaystyle=-e^{-\left<\mu_{s},V_{t-s}\varphi\right>}\text{d}M_{s}(V_{t-s}\varphi),

where the last line uses that
V_{t-s}\varphi
is a solution to the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman
equation (
17
). This local martingale is uniformly bounded, since for any non-negative compactly
supported function
\varphi
, the Cole-Hopf transform satisfies
0\leq V_{t}\varphi\leq||\varphi||_{\infty}

and hence
0\leq e^{\left<\mu_{s},V_{t-s}\varphi\right>}\leq 1
. At this point, we have established the desired
Equation (
18
) for all non-negative
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}
; by approximating functions
pointwise from below and monotone convergence, we obtain the equality for all nonnegative

\varphi\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
.
∎

Proof.

As in

Proposition 5.4

(Rigidity)
.

Any solution
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
to Equation (
14
) satisfies
\alpha\mu_{t}(A)\in\mathbb{N}
for all sets

A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
and times
t>0
. Furthermore, there exist
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

(x^{i})_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x^{i}}
.

Proof.

As shown in
[
32
, Lemma 2.4]
, if the moment generating function
g_{X}(s)
of a nonnegative
random variable
X
is of the form
g(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{k}s^{k}
, then
X\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
almost surely.
We want to apply this statement to the non-negative random variable
\alpha\mu_{t}(A)
for sets

A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
. Let us consider the moment generating function

\displaystyle g(s)=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-|\ln(s)|\alpha\mu_{t}(A)}\right)=\exp\left(\left<\mu_{0},\alpha\ln\left(P_{t}e^{-|\ln(s)|1_{A}}\right)\right>\right).

Again we follow argument of
[
32
]
. Note that we need
P_{t}1_{A}
to be bounded away from 1
for any
t>0
, in order to obtain that
g
can be extended to a small interval around
0
such that
g
is
analytic in 0. Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
be a set such that there exists a set
A_{n}
of the exhaustion
(Condition
(L.4)
of Assumption
3.2
) such that
A\subset A_{n}
. Hence,

P_{t}1_{A}\leq P_{t}1_{A_{n}}\leq c_{n}<1
is bounded away from
1
. Analogously to the proof
in
[
32
]
, we now can extend

g(s)=\exp\left(\left<\mu_{0},\alpha\ln\left(1+(s-1)P_{t}1_{A}\right)\right>\right)
to

s\in\left(1-\frac{1}{c_{n}},1\right)
, where by assumption
1-\frac{1}{c_{n}}<0
. This bound implies that
the extended function is analytic in
0
, so that we obtain the required expansion

g(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}p_{k}s^{k}+\mathcal{O}(s^{n+1})
as
s\downarrow 0
. Finally, this yields

\alpha\mu_{t}(A)\in\mathbb{N}
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
satisfying
A\subset A_{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an exhaustion, the statement extends to arbitrary
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
.
By the Portmanteau theorem, it then follows that
\alpha\mu_{0}(A)\in\mathbb{N}
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{k})

bounded with
\mu_{0}(\partial A)=0
, which implies that there exist
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

(x^{i})_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that
\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x^{i}}
.
∎

We treat the case

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
b
be of at most linear growth and
F\colon\mathcal{M}_{1}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
be continuous with

\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{1},z\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}|H(\mu,z)|<\infty

(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a

8
) with initial condition
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{1}
. Then
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}_{1}

almost surely for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

The choice of the test function
\varphi=1
in the weak martingale formulation of
(
8
) reveals that the total mass
\mu_{t}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
is conserved, with value
1
. In particular, by
the assumptions on
F
, there is some constant
c
such that
\sup_{t\geq 0,z\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}|F(\mu_{t},z)|<c

almost surely. Here and in below we use the symbol
c
for a generic constant whose value may change from
line to line. Next, for
R>0
, choose
\psi_{R}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
a smooth approximation of the function

r\mapsto(r+1)\wedge R
on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\psi_{R}(r)=R
for
r\geq R
,
r\leq\psi_{R}(r)

for all
r\leq R
, and
\psi_{R}^{\prime}\leq 1
and
|\psi_{R}^{\prime\prime}|\leq c
for uniformly in
R
. Then

\varphi_{R}=\psi_{R}\circ e_{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
and the chain rule for
L
(cf. Condition
(L.2)
) together with
(L.5)
of Assumption
3.4

yields

|L\varphi_{R}(z)|\leq c\left(1+\varphi_{R}(z)\right),

with a constant
c
independent of
R
.
Now, since
\varphi_{R}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, by definition of
\mu_{t}
the process
M_{t}(\varphi_{R})
is a
continuous martingale and hence
\tau_{N,R}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\bigm|\left<\mu_{t},\varphi_{R}\right>\geq N\}
is a
well-defined stopping time, with
\lim_{N\to\infty}\tau_{N,R}=\infty
almost surely, for all
R>0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left<\mu_{t\wedge\tau_{N}},\varphi_{R}\right>\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\left<\mu_{0},\varphi_{R}\right>+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau_{N}}\left<\mu_{s},\alpha L\varphi_{R}+F_{\mu_{s}}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{R}\right>\text{d}s\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\left<\mu_{0},\varphi_{R}\right>\right)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left(\left<\mu_{s\wedge\tau_{N,R}},c\left(1+\varphi_{R}\right)\right>\right)\text{d}s,

, the process is bounded,

\mathbb{E}\left(\left<\mu_{s\wedge\tau_{N,R}},\varphi_{R}\right>\right)
is a continuous function. Hence, by Grönwall’s
Lemma

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left<\mu_{t\wedge\tau_{N,R}},\varphi_{R}\right>\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left<\mu_{0},\varphi_{R}\right>+\left<\mu_{0}\right>ct\right)e^{ct},

where we use again that the mass is preserved almost surely. Using Fatou’s lemma again, we finally
obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left<\mu_{t},\varphi_{R}\right>\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\liminf_{R}\left<\mu_{t\wedge\tau_{N,R}},\varphi_{R}\right>\right)\leq\left(\left<\mu_{0},\varphi_{R}\right>+ct\right)e^{ct}.

Due to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left<\mu_{t},e_{2}\right>\right)

(19)

and so in particular

∎

Next we need an Itô formula along solutions to (

\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathcal{M}):=\bigg\{E\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{M})\bigm|\frac{\delta E}{\delta\mu}\text{ and }\frac{\delta^{2}E}{\delta\mu^{2}}\penalty\ \text{exist and }\penalty\ \frac{\delta E}{\delta\mu}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}),\\
\penalty\ \frac{\delta^{2}E}{\delta\mu^{2}}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}),\frac{\delta E}{\delta\mu}(\mu,\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\text{ and}\penalty\ \frac{\delta^{2}E}{\delta\mu^{2}}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k})\penalty\ \forall\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{F}\bigg\},

Proposition 5.6
.

For a solution
(\mu_{t})_{t\geq 0}

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left<\mu_{s},\sigma\sigma^{T}:D\frac{\delta^{2}E}{\delta\mu^{2}}(\mu_{s},\cdot,\cdot)\right>\text{d}s,

Next, according to
