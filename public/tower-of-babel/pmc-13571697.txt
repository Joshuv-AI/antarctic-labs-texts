Biohybrid nanovesicle-mediated targeted delivery of HSP90 inhibitor for enhanced sorafenib therapy of hepatocellular carcinoma after radiofrequency ablation
Authors: Angfeng Jiang, Xianfei Zhou, Jinchao Zhao, Shaoming Zou, Junkang Ding, Junyi Zhang, Lei Lei, Qiao Jin, Zhe Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) presents significant therapeutic challenges due to enhanced liver cancer stem cell (LCSC) stemness and sorafenib resistance after radiofrequency ablation (RFA). To address these limitations, biohybrid nanovesicles (hNVs) are prepared by fusing milk-derived exosomes with sorafenib-resistant LM3 cell membranes as nanovectors for targeted delivery of the HSP90 inhibitor alvespimycin (17-DMAG). The hNVs exhibit enhanced tumor-homing capability through homologous targeting, achieving 3.2-fold higher tumor accumulation compared to unmodified exosomes. RFA of HCC significantly upregulates the expression of HSP90, leading to enhanced LCSC stemness and sorafenib resistance. In vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that 17-DMAG loaded hNVs (17-DMAG@hNVs) effectively suppress LCSC stemness by downregulating HSP90 and its downstream signal pathways (TGF-β/Smad3 and JAK/STAT3), thereby restoring sorafenib sensitivity. Therefore, 17-DMAG@hNVs remarkably enhance the therapeutic efficacy of sorafenib after RFA of HCC. The reversal of LCSC stemness by inhibiting HSP90 expression using 17-DMAG@hNVs provides a promising approach to overcome sorafenib resistance for enhanced therapy of HCC after RFA.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most common primary liver malignancy, remains a global health challenge due to its high recurrence rate and therapeutic resistance [1] [2]. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, approximately 865,269 new liver cancer cases and 757,948 deaths were reported worldwide [3] [4]. The therapeutic strategies for HCC patients demonstrate a multimodal nature, encompassing stratified interventions ranging from surgical resection, liver transplantation to chemotherapy and immunotherapy [6] [7]. Radiofrequency ablation (RFA), serving as a curative non-surgical approach, plays a crucial role in the therapeutic strategy for HCC patients. RFA achieves local tumor eradication through thermal coagulation but fails to eliminate microvascular invasion and satellite lesions, resulting in 30%-40% postoperative recurrence rates [8] [9]. Therefore, to prolong patient survival and reduce recurrence rates after radiofrequency ablation (RFA), targeted therapy is routinely adopted postoperatively for months to even years. Sorafenib (SOR) is a first-line targeted agent for advanced HCC, which inhibits VEGFR, PDGFR, and RAF kinases to block tumor angiogenesis and proliferation signaling. However, prolonged postoperative use fails to demonstrate significant improvement in median overall survival, and it is widely acknowledged that the development of sorafenib resistance is the primary culprit [10] [11]. Multiple resistance mechanisms have been identified, including but not limited to tumor cell activation of bypass signaling pathways (e.g., MET/AXL-PI3K/AKT axis), hypoxia-induced metabolic reprogramming, and dysregulated epigenetic modifications [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Such resistance likely fuels accelerated recurrence and metastasis in residual tumors [16].

Notably, therapeutic resistance and recurrence are closely related with liver cancer stem cells (LCSCs) stemness [17]. LCSCs maintain self-renewal capacity via activation of Wnt/β-catenin and Hippo/YAP pathways [18] [19], while establishing multilayered resistance barriers. For example, ABCG2 overexpression for drug efflux [20] and OXPHOS dependency to counteract drug-induced oxidative stress [21] are mechanisms observed in such cases. Crucially, LCSCs preserve stemness through epigenetic regulation (e.g., lncRNA H19-mediated DNA methylation) [22] and Notch signaling-driven expression of resistance-associated genes (e.g., ALDH1A1) [23], constructing a hierarchical defense network. The stemness-associated molecular architecture enables LCSCs to evade therapeutic elimination and perpetually drive tumor regeneration, positioning them as central hubs of drug resistance [24]. It is well-known that heat shock proteins (HSPs) such as HSP90 and HSP70 are highly upregulated after RFA of HCC. HSP90 is a pivotal molecular chaperone that can regulate cellular signaling and stress adaptation by facilitating client protein folding and stabilization [25]. The aberrant overexpression of HSP90 in tumors sustains oncoprotein stability (e.g., EGFR, HIF-1α) [26], thereby promoting tumor growth, metastasis, and therapy resistance. Recently, HSP90 was reported to be highly related with LCSC stemness [27] [28]. Owing to highly upregulated HSP90 expression after RFA of HCC, the enhanced LCSC stemness might be an important obstacle for targeted therapy of HCC after RFA. The development of innovative strategies to increase drug sensitivity by reversing LCSC stemness is urgently needed for enhanced targeted therapy after RFA of HCC.

In this contribution, we put forward a new strategy to targeted deliver HSP90 inhibitor to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of SOR after RFA of HCC. Alvespimycin (17-DMAG) is a classic HSP90 inhibitor. However, the systemic toxicity, lack of tumor targeting, and low intracellular delivery efficiency severely hinder the clinical application of 17-DMAG [29] [30]. We prepared a novel hybrid nanovesicle (hNV) by fusing the cell membrane of SOR-resistant LM3 cells with milk-derived exosomes as a nanovector for targeted delivery of 17-DMAG. 17-DMAG loaded hybrid nanovesicles (17-DMAG@hNVs) were prepared to reverse LCSC stemness by inhibiting HSP90 after incomplete RFA (iRFA) of HCC. 17-DMAG@hNVs were injected during the administration of SOR after RFA of HCC, which were expected to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of SOR by reversing SOR resistance. Therefore, 17-DMAG@hNVs were expected to target and accumulate in residual HCC regions after RFA, reversing LCSC stemness by inhibiting HSP90 expression, restoring cancer cell sensitivity to sorafenib, and achieving enhanced therapeutic efficacy of SOR.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of hNVs

In order to improve the bioavailability of 17-DMAG, milk-derived exosomes (mEXOs) were adopted to encapsulate 17-DMAG. We fused the cell membrane of LM3-R into mEXO to generate a brand-new hybrid nanovesicles (hNVs). As shown in Fig. 1a and b, the hydrodynamic diameter of hNVs was 144.55 ± 8.62 nm with the zeta potential of −8.58 ± 1.43 mV measure by dynamic light scattering (DLS). During the extrusion process, the curvature of lipid membranes can be regulated by mechanical forces. The fused membranes tended to form an energetically more stable intermediate size due to surface tension equilibrium, rather than a simple summation of their original sizes. hNVs exhibited excellent stability, without obvious size change after 10 days’ storage (Fig. 1c). To confirm the fusion of exosomes with cell membranes, membranes of LM3-R and mEXOs were stained with FITC and DIR, respectively. The fusion of mEXOs and LM3-M to obtain hNVs was observed by a confocal microscope. As shown in Fig. 1d, both green fluorescence from FITC and red fluorescence from DIR were observed in hNVs, proving the successful preparation of hNVs. To confirm the successful preparation of hNVs, fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis was employed to monitor the membrane fusion process. Specifically, dual-labeled (Cy3/Cy5) exosomes were fused with unlabeled cell membranes at various protein weight ratios (1:0, 1:1, and 1:4) via the extrusion method. As shown in Fig. S3b, the FRET signal exhibited a clear ratio-dependent trend. In the 1:0 group (pure dual-labeled exosomes), a strong FRET effect was observed, characterized by a suppressed Cy3 peak and a prominent Cy5 emission. However, as the proportion of unlabeled cell membranes increased, the FRET efficiency gradually declined, resulting in the progressive recovery of the Cy3 donor fluorescence and a corresponding decrease in the Cy5 acceptor emission. This phenomenon is attributed to the increased average distance between the Cy3 and Cy5 molecules as they redistribute across the expanded surface area of the hybrid membranes. These quantitative data provide direct evidence of the successful integration of the two membrane components.Subsequent transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of hNVs revealed that mEXOs, LM3-M, and hNVs exhibited a spherical bilayer membrane structure, with sizes consistent with the DLS results (Fig. 1e). To prove the homologous targeting ability of hNVs to LM3-R cells, the internalization of DIR-labeled hNVs by LM3-R cells was investigated using DIR labeled mEXO as a control. As shown in Fig. 1f and S3c, much stronger intracellular red fluorescence was observed in hNVs-treated LM3-R cells than that in mEXO-treated LM3-R cells, which demonstrated that hNVs enhanced uptake by sorafenib-resistant LM3-R cells. To further validate the role of homologous targeting, we evaluated the cellular uptake of different hNV formulations—derived from either homologous (LM3-R) or non-homologous (Pan-02) membranes—specifically within the same LM3-R cell line. As shown in the confocal microscopy images (Fig. S1f and g), hNVs constituted of homologous LM3-R membranes exhibited significantly higher Cy3 fluorescence intensity compared to those with heterologous membranes, which confirmed that the specific cell membrane-derived components provided a “homing” signal that facilitated precise, homologous recognition and enhanced uptake. Due to the poor water solubility of 17-DMAG, 17-DMAG encapsulated hNVs (17-DMAG@hNVs) was obtained using the saponin method. The drug loading efficiency of 17-DMAG was calculated by a UV-vis spectrophotometer. As shown in Fig. 1g and h, with the increase of 17-DMAG, the encapsulation efficiency of 17-DMAG in 17-DMAG@hNVs was reduced, while the loading content of 17-DMAG in 17-DMAG@hNVs was increased. In the following experiments, the mass ratio of hNVs and 17-DMAG in feed was set as 1:2 ratio to synthesize 17-DMAG@hNVs. Next, we focused on the biosafety of 17-DMAG@hNVs. The results of the in vitro hemolysis test showed that 17-DMAG@hNVs maintained a hemolysis rate below 5% even at a high concentration of 600 μg/mL, indicating minimal hemolysis of 17-DMAG@hNVs (Fig. 1i and j). We also evaluated the cytotoxicity of hNVs to normal cells. As shown in Fig. 1k, hNVs did not exhibit significant cytotoxicity up to 10 mg/mL. Finally, we tested the drug release of 17-DMAG from 17-DMAG@hNVs at different pH. The inhibition of the stemness of LM3-R by 17-DMAG@hNVs

In this research, sorafenib-resistant LM3 (LM3-R) cells were used as model cells to investigate LCSC stemness after iRFA. In order to confirm the drug resistance of LM3-R cells to sorafenib, we compared SOR sensitivity between LM3-R and parental LM3 cells using CCK-8 assay. Fig. 2showed that LM3-R cells exhibited higher cell viability at various concentrations compared to parental LM3 cells, which confirmed the successful construction of the sorafenib-resistant cell line. Subsequently, RFA in vitro was simulated by incubating cells at 43°C, 45°C or 47°C for 10 min. An even more compelling finding was that LM3-R cells exhibited significantly enhanced resistance to thermal treatment compared to the parental LM3 cells (Fig. 2b). Previous studies indicated that RFA led to an increase in HSP90 expression, which in turn further reduced the thermal sensitivity of tumor cells and resulted in a significant elevation of LCSC stemness. Therefore, it could be speculated that the increase of HSP90 and the enhancement of LCSC stemness after RFA may contribute to sorafenib resistance. Hence, we treated LM3-R with 17-DMAG@hNVs combined with sorafenib. As depicted in Figs. 2c,17-DMAG@hNVs monotherapy exhibited weak cytotoxicity against LM3-R cells, this effect was significantly enhanced when combined with sorafenib, which implied that HSP90 inhibition by 17-DMAG could enhance the therapeutic efficacy of SOR in treating sorafenib-resistant tumors. We compared the therapeutic response of 17-DMAG@hNVs in parental LM3 cells and sorafenib-resistant LM3-R cells. As demonstrated in Fig. S3d, LM3-R cells exhibited a significantly more pronounced response to 17-DMAG@hNVs compared to LM3 cells, particularly at higher concentrations (40 μM), where the viability of LM3-R cells dropped more sharply. To delineate HSP90-mediated regulation of LCSC stemness and associated sorafenib resistance, three experimental cohorts were established: parental LM3 cells (designated as LM3), sorafenib-resistant LM3 cells (LM3-R), and LM3-R cells receiving 17-DMAG@hNVs nanotherapy (LM3-R+17-DMAG@hNVs). It was well known that cancer stem cells (CSCs) exhibit stronger invasive and migratory abilities than differentiated counterparts. Wound-healing assays were used to evaluate the migratory capacity of LM3-R [31]. As shown in Fig. 2d and e, the migration distance of LM3-R cells was significantly longer than LM3 cells, which indicated stronger invasive and migratory abilities of LM3-R cells. However, the migration distance of LM3-R cells was significantly reduced after LM3-R cells were incubated with 17-DMAG@hNVs. The wound healing rates for the LM3, LM3-R, and LM3-R+17-DMAG@hNVs groups after 48 h were 26.8 ± 4.9%,42.2 ± 4.8% and 6.5 ± 1.9%, respectively. CSCs demonstrated inherent self-organization capacity through tumor sphere formation, a hallmark of their stem-like attributes. To comparatively assess stemness maintenance across experimental groups, we conducted functional validation via serum-free suspension culture assays. As shown in Fig. 2g, the number of spheroids formed at LM3-R group was significantly higher than LM3 group. However, this phenomenon was significantly reversed after treatment with 17-DMAG@hNVs. These results further demonstrated progressive enrichment of stem-like populations in LM3-R cultures, and the treatment with 17-DMAG@hNVs achieved phenotypic reversal, reducing tumor sphere counts through disruption of HSP90-mediated stemness maintenance. Then, the transwell migration assay was used to evaluate the metastatic potential and to further prove the LCSC stemness properties. As shown in Fig. 2f and h, LM3-R cells exhibited a 3.2 ± 0.4-fold increase in migratory capacity compared to LM3 cells. Notably, 17-DMAG@hNVs treatment significantly reversed the drug-resistant phenotype, leading to the decrease of migrated cells. These findings demonstrated that acquired chemoresistance in LM3-R cells was closely related to HSP90-dependent pathways and suggested HSP90 was a master regulator of resistance-associated migratory and invasion capacity. These results further demonstrated progressive enrichment of stem-like populations in LM3-R cultures, and the treatment with 17-DMAG@hNVs achieved phenotypic reversal, reducing tumor sphere counts through disruption of HSP90-mediated stemness maintenance.

To assess the differential expression of HSP90 and stem cell markers (CD133 and ALDH1A1) across experimental groups, flow cytometric analysis was performed. As shown in Fig. 2i, the LM3-R cells exhibited much higher expressions of HSP90 and CD133 than LM3 cells, while the treatment of LM3-R+17-DMAG@hNVs could effectively inhibit the expression of HSP90 and CD133. These findings collectively demonstrated that LM3-R cells showed greater LCSC stemness which might be related to the increased expression of HSP90. Notably, 17-DMAG@hNVs significantly attenuated the LCSC stemness by inhibiting HSP90. The reduction of CD133 in LM3-R cells after treated with 17-DMAG@hNVs suggested that HSP90 played a pivotal role in maintaining the chemoresistance-associated stemness signature. Furthermore, western blotting (WB) was used to further assess the expression of HSP90, CD133, and ALDH1A1 after different treatments. It was observed that the expression of HSP90, CD133 and ALDH1A1 was significantly higher in LM3-R cells compared to that in LM3 cells. However, after LM3-R cells were treated with 7-DMAG@hNVs, the expressions of HSP90, CD133, and ALDH1A1 were significantly inhibited (Fig. 2j). Specifically, the expressions of HSP90, CD133 and ALDH1A1 in LM3-R were 1.96 ± 0.08-fold, 1.77 ± 0.04-fold, 1.86 ± 0.06-fold higher than LM3 group, respectively. However, the expression of HSP90, CD133 and ALDH1A1 in the LM3-R+17-DMAG@hNVs group reduced by 58.46 ± 7.37%, 72.63 ± 4.09% and 71.40 ± 3.99% compared to LM3-R group, respectively. These results indicated that HSP90 inhibition by 17-DMAG@hNVs significantly reversed LCSC stemness in LM3-R cells.

The mechanism of hNVs to reverse stemness of LM3-R

To further explore the potential mechanism of HSP90-induced enhancement of LCSC stemness, we systematically investigated two key signaling axes about stemness regulation: the TGF-β/SMAD3 and JAK1/STAT3 pathways. The WB results in Fig. 3a showed that the expression of TGF-β1 and p-SMAD3 in LM3-R was 1.58 ± 0.05-fold, 2.23 ± 0.08-fold higher than that in LM3 group, respectively. However, the expression of TGF-β1 and p-SMAD3 in the LM3-R+17-DMAG@hNVs group reduced by 67.20 ± 3.95% and 82.11 ± 5.98% compared to LM3-R group, respectively. Meanwhile, the expression of p-JAK1 and p-STAT3 in LM3-R was 1.26 ± 0.17-fold,1.57 ± 0.04-fold higher than LM3 group, respectively (Fig. 3b). However, the expression of p-JAK1 and p-STAT3 in the LM3-R+17-DMAG@hNVs group reduced by 59.10 ± 3.90% and 31.89 ± 11.00% compared to LM3-R group, respectively. These results implied that the TGF-β/Smad3 and JAK1/STAT3 pathways may be involved in the process how HSP90 enhanced LCSC stemness. To further substantiate the reliability of the proposed mechanism, we performed rescue experiments using TGF-β (as an activator of the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway) and IL-6 (as an activator of the JAK/STAT3 pathway). As shown in Fig. S3a, the HSP90 inhibitor (17-DMAG) significantly downregulated the expression of stemness markers (CD133, ALDH1A1) and EMT-related proteins (Vimentin). The addition of exogenous TGF-β or IL-6 effectively reactivated the p-Smad3 and p-JAK1/p-STAT3 signaling axes, respectively. More importantly, these agonists partially or significantly restored the expression of stemness and mesenchymal markers, even in the presence of the HSP90 inhibitor. Next, we would like to clarify how tumour stemness resulted in the emergence of sorafenib resistance. Previous studies showed that cancer stem cells exhibited resistance to apoptosis [32]. Therefore, LM3-R resistance to sorafenib might be ascribed to enhanced stem cell stemness, leading to insensitivity to sorafenib-induced apoptosis. To prove if apoptosis resistance played a significant role in sorafenib resistance, four groups were defined as follows, NC group (LM3-R without any treatments), LM3+SOR group, LM3-R + SOR group, and LM3-R + SOR+17-DMAG@hNVs group. The apoptosis-related proteins, including Bcl-2, BAX, and cleaved caspase 3 were detected by WB after different treatments. Bcl-2 is an anti-apoptotic protein, while BAX is a pro-apoptotic protein. Cleaved caspase 3, as an effector protein in the apoptosis pathway, also plays an important role in cell apoptosis. The results in Fig. 3c showed that the pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and cleaved caspase 3 were significantly increased in the LM3+SOR group compared to the NC group, while the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was significantly decreased. In the LM3-R + SOR group, BAX and cleaved caspase 3 were significantly decreased compared to the LM3+SOR group, while Bcl-2 was significantly increased, indicating that LM3-R cells exhibited significant resistance to sorafenib-induced apoptosis. In the LM3-R + SOR+17-DMAG@hNVs group, after treatment with 17-DMAG@hNVs, BAX and cleaved caspase 3 were significantly increased compared to the LM3-R + SOR group, while Bcl-2 was decreased. The results indicated that 17-DMAG@hNVs effectively reversed the resistance of LM3-R to sorafenib-induced apoptosis. JC-1, a cationic lipophilic fluorescent dye, was then used as an indicator to measure mitochondrial transmembrane potential after different treatments. JC-1 accumulates within mitochondria in a membrane potential-dependent manner, forming red-fluorescent J-aggregates in the mitochondrial matrix when the mitochondrial membrane potential is high, and emits green fluorescence when the membrane potential is low [33]. As shown in Fig. 3d and Fig. S1, LM3 cells treated with SOR exhibited strong green fluorescence, indicating a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial damage. In contrast, LM3-R cells treated with SOR maintained strong red fluorescence, indicating resistance to sorafenib. The LM3-R + SOR+17-DMAG@hNVs group exhibited strong green fluorescence, indicating efficient mitochondrial damage. The apoptosis ratios of each group were measured using flow cytometry. The FITC + represents early apoptosis, and the FITC + PI + represents late apoptosis. Our study considered the sum of FITC+ and FITC + PI + as the total apoptosis rate. The apoptosis rates for the NC, LM3+SOR, LM3-R + SOR, and LM3-R + SOR+17-DMAG@hNVs groups were 7.25%, 80.0%, 19.09%, and 63.5%, respectively (Fig. 3e). All these results demonstrated that HSP90 inhibition by 17-DMAG effectively reduced LCSC stemness and reversed resistance to sorafenib-induced apoptosis.

In vivo evaluation of HSP90-Induced inhibition of cancer cell stemness and sorafenib resistance

Based on the potential of 17-DMAG@hNVs to reverse cancer cell stemness and sorafenib resistance by inhibiting HSP90 in vitro, we investigated if 17-DMAG@hNVs could reverse iRFA-induced cancer cell stemness and sorafenib resistance in vivo. LM3-R cells were utilized to establish the murine subcutaneous HCC model. The radiofrequency needle was inserted into the non-central site of the tumor to simulate clinical iRFA. Oral sorafenib (at a dose of 40 mg/kg sorafenib) and tail vein injection of 17-DMAG@hNVs (at a dose of 4 mg/kg 17-DMAG) were administered every two days, for a total of 4 times.The intratumoral expression levels of proteins related to HSP90-driven LCSC stemness and sorafenib chemoresistance were measured by WB. As shown in Fig. 4a, the expression of HSP90 and cancer stem cell markers (CD133 and ALDH1A1) was significantly upregulated after iRFA, which indicated that iRFA could upregulate HSP90 expression and enhance cancer cell stemness. However, 17-DMAG@hNVs could effectively downregulate the expression of HSP90, CD133, and ALDH1A1 after iRFA, which implied that 17-DMAG was beneficial to reverse cancer cell stemness by inhibiting HSP90 in vivo. Similarly, the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was significantly reduced in the iRFA + SOR+17-DMAG@hNVs group, while the pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and cleaved caspase-3 were significantly upregulated, which indicated that HSP90 inhibition by 17-DMAG@hNVs enhanced the SOR-induced apoptosis. Furthermore, the related signaling pathways about tumor stemness regulation were investigated by WB. As shown in Fig. 4b, key proteins in the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway (TGF-β1 and phosphorylated Smad3) and the JAK/STAT3 pathway (phosphorylated JAK1 and phosphorylated STAT3) were significantly downregulated in the iRFA+17-DMAG@hNVs group compared to that in iRFA group. These results suggested that HSP90 may induce LCSC stemness and sorafenib resistance in vivo through the activation of the TGF-β/Smad3 and JAK/STAT3. In vivo targeting and biodistribution of 17-DMAG@hNVs

In order to achieve satisfactory therapeutic efficacy, it is the prerequisite that the nanodrugs should be targeted delivered to pathological sites [34]. To prove the tumor-homing capability of hNVs, CY3 encapsulated hNVs (CY3@hNVs) were prepared due to the inherent fluorescence of CY3. CY3 encapsulated mEXOs (CY3@ mEXOs) were used as control. The in vivo biodistribution of CY3@hNVs was investigated. As shown in Fig. 4c and e, more CY3@hNVs could be accumulated in tumor tissues than CY3@ mEXOs, probably owing to the homologous targeting ability of hNVs. To further analyze the biodistribution of CY3@hNVs in various organs, the major organs and tumors were excised at 24 h post injection and the fluorescent signal of CY3 was detected (Fig. 4d and f). These results indicated that hNVs exhibited superior tumor-targeting ability.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of 17-DMAG@hNVs

The effect of 17-DMAG@hNVs in overcoming sorafenib resistance after iRFA was evaluated on a murine LM3-R tumor-bearing subcutaneous HCC model. When the tumor reached 200 mm3, the mice were divided into 7 groups: untreated NC group, oral sorafenib alone (SOR group), iRFA alone (iRFA group), oral sorafenib combined with iRFA (SOR + iRFA group), SOR+17-DMAG@hNVs group, iRFA+17-DMAG@hNVs group, and iRFA + SOR+17-DMAG@hNVs group (n = 5). Oral sorafenib (at a dose of 40 mg/kg sorafenib) and tail vein injection of 17-DMAG@hNVs (at a dose of 4 mg/kg 17-DMAG) were administered every two days, for a total of 4 times. During the treatment, the body weights of mice in each group remained relatively stable throughout the treatment period, indicating excellent biosafety (Fig. 5f). In vivo Biosafety Evaluation of 17-DMAG@hNVs

Finally, we assessed the in vivo biosafety of 17-DMAG@hNVs. The heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys of the mice in each treatment group were isolated and pathological sections were examined. As shown in Fig. 6, all the histological sections of the aforementioned organs demonstrated normal histological structures, with no pathological changes such as inflammation, necrosis, fibrosis, degeneration, or hyperplasia. These findings indicated that 17-DMAG@hNVs didn't cause significant toxic damage to these vital organs, thereby meeting basic biosafety requirements. The blood samples were collected from the mice to measure biochemical indicators related to heart, liver, and kidney function. As shown in Fig. S2, no significant differences were observed in AST, ALT, LDH, and TBIL levels compared to the control group (NC group), indicating that the treatments did not significantly affect heart or liver function. Similarly, BUN and CR levels showed no significant differences, suggesting no renal toxicity. These results collectively demonstrated the favorable biosafety of 17-DMAG@hNVs in major organs. Additionally, blood routine tests were performed to evaluate the impact on red and white blood cells. Conclusions

In conclusion, to overcome sorafenib resistance after RFA of HCC, we engineered biohybrid nanovesicles (hNVs) by fusing milk-derived exosomes with sorafenib-resistant LM3 cell membranes, enabling tumor-targeted delivery of the HSP90 inhibitor 17-DMAG. The hNVs leveraged homologous targeting to achieve 3.2-fold higher tumor accumulation than unmodified exosomes, effectively disrupting HSP90-mediated cancer stemness via TGF-β/Smad3 and JAK/STAT3 pathways inhibition. This strategy restored mitochondrial apoptosis sensitivity, reducing anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 by 42% and increasing sorafenib-induced apoptosis from 19.09% to 63.5% in LM3-R cells. The incorporation of 17-DMAG@hNVs significantly potentiated the therapeutic efficacy of sorafenib following RFA in vivo. The iRFA + SOR+17-DMAG@hNVs group suppressed tumor growth by 65.87% and induced 78.26% necrotic area, while maintaining biosafety with no organ toxicity. By integrating biomimetic drug delivery with molecular targeting, this work established an innovative nanoplatform for boosted SOR therapy after RFA of HCC. Crucially, this research addressed the root cause of drug resistance through cancer stem cell (CSC) ablation, thereby advancing a new paradigm for clinical precision oncology.

Methods

Isolation and Characterization of Milk-Derived Exosomes and LM3-R Cell Membranes: Milk purchased from a local supermarket was mixed with acetic acid (100:1, v/v) and stirred at room temperature for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane to obtain whey. The whey was then ultracentrifuged at 210,000 ×g for 70 min at 4°C using a Beckman Coulter ultracentrifuge with an SW45Ti rotor. The exosome pellet was resuspended in PBS and subjected to another round of ultracentrifugation. After washing, the pellet was resuspended in PBS and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 5 min at 4°C to remove residual debris. To obtain the membrane material from the LM3-R cells, a previously reported extrusion method was employed. The tumor cells were lysed under hypotonic conditions, followed by mechanical membrane disruption and differential centrifugation (Beckman Optima MAX-XP ultracentrifuge, USA) to empty the intracellular contents, resulting in the isolation of the LM3-R cell membranes.The total protein concentration was measured using a BCA assay kit. The milk-derived exosomes (mEXOs) and LM3-R cell membranes were then stored at −80°C until use. The size of the nanoparticles was measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS), and the morphology of the NPs was observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) [35] [36].

Construction and Characterization of hNVs: Briefly, mEXOs and LM3-R cell membranes resuspended in PBS were repeatedly extruded through a 200-nm polycarbonate membrane filter to obtain uniformly sized hybrid nanovesicles [37]. To confirm the successful hybridization of mEXOs and LM3-R cell membranes, mEXOs were labeled with rhodamine, and the LM3-R cell membranes were labeled with FITC. The fusion of the two fluorescent dyes in the hybrid vesicles was observed using confocal microscopy. The size of hNVs was measured using DLS, and the morphology was observed using TEM.

In Vitro Stability Study of 17-DMAG@hNVs: To evaluate the stability of hNVs, the particle size was measured using a Malvern ZS90 Zetasizer at different time points over 10 days. The 17-DMAG@hNVs samples were suspended in PBS containing 10% FBS to simulate physiological conditions and shaken at 60 rpm at 37°C.

Loading of 17-DMAG into hNVs: hNVs and 17-DMAG were mixed, and 2% saponin was added to the mixture. The solution was shaken at 37°C for 2 h, followed by incubation at 37°C for 60 min to restore the hNVs membrane. Unloaded water-soluble drugs were removed by ultracentrifugation. The drug content was quantified using a UV spectrophotometer, and the loading efficiency and encapsulation efficiency were calculated.

Characterization of Nanoparticles: The size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of the nanoparticles were measured using a Malvern ZS90 Zetasizer (Malvern, Worcestershire, UK). The stability of the nanoparticles was also assessed. The morphology of the membrane-camouflaged nanoparticles was observed using a JEM-1011 transmission electron microscope (TEM) (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

In Vitro Drug Release: The drug release profile of 17-DMAG@hNVs was evaluated using a dialysis method. Two milliliters of 17-DMAG@hNVs suspension (containing 200 μg of 17-DMAG) was placed in a dialysis bag and immersed in 100 mL of release buffer (PBS containing 0.1% w/v Tween 80, pH 5.5 or pH 7.4) at 37°C with shaking (100 rpm). At predetermined time points, 1 mL of the external medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh pre-warmed medium. The concentration of 17-DMAG in the release medium was analyzed using a UV spectrophotometer.

Cell Lines and Animals: The LM3-R hepatocellular carcinoma cell line was purchased from Beijing Lanbo Kangsi. All cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Male nude mice aged 4-6 weeks were purchased from Hangzhou Qizhen Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the animal ethics policy approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University. The assigned approval number was ZJCLA‐IACUC‐20010765.

Cell Uptake: The LM3-R cells were cultured in medium for 24 h. For the cell uptake experiment, cells were incubated with rhodamine-labeled hNVs, mEXOs, or free rhodamine B for 4 h. After removing the medium, the cells were washed three times with PBS. The cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min, stained with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 5 min, and imaged using a Zeiss LSM900 (Zeiss, Germany).

CCK-8 Assay: Cell proliferation and cytotoxicity were assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Beyotime, China). Cells (5 × 103 cells per well) were seeded in 96-well plates, and 100 μL of medium was added and cultured for the specified time. After removing the supernatant, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA) [38].

Wound Healing Assay: A scratch was created on the surface of a 24-well plate using a 200-μL pipette tip. After 24 and 48 h of culture in serum-free DMEM, the changes in scratch width were measured under an optical microscope, and the percentage of wound closure was calculated.

Transwell Assay: Both normal LM3 cells and resistant LM3 cells (5 × 104 cells per well) were suspended in serum-free medium and seeded in the upper chamber of a Transwell plate pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning, USA), while the lower chamber was filled with medium containing 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, the invasive cells in the lower chamber were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and counted using ImageJ software.

Western Blot Analysis: Samples were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. The protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10%-15% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Beyotime, China). After blocking with QuickBlock™ blocking buffer (Beyotime, China), the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using a Bio-Rad imaging system (Bio-Rad) and quantified using ImageJ software [39].

Tumor Sphere Formation Assay: Low-passage cells were collected and washed with PBS. Cells were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 103 cells/mL in 100 μL of medium in 96-well plates with low adhesion. The medium contained DMEM/F12, 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution, 20 ng/mL EGF, 10 ng/mL b-FGF, 1× B27 supplement, 1× insulin-transferrin-selenium supplement, 5 μg/mL heparin sodium, and 1% L-glutamine. Tumor sphere size and number were recorded under a microscope every 3 days.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Detection: After washing with PBS, 1 mL of medium was added to the cells. Subsequently, 1 mL of JC-1 staining working solution (Beyotime, China) was added and mixed thoroughly. After incubation at 37°C for 20 min, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed twice with JC-1 staining buffer. Finally, 2 mL of cell culture medium was added, and the cells were observed and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Flow Cytometry: LM3 and LM3-R cells were collected, washed with PBS, and adjusted to a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Cells were stained with antibodies according to the target of interest, incubated in the dark for 30 min, washed, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software [40].

Animal Model: For the subcutaneous (s.c.) tumor model, 5 × 106 sorafenib-resistant LM3 cells were implanted subcutaneously into the right flank of nude mice. Approximately 14 days later, when the tumor diameter reached 6-8 mm, the mice were randomly divided into 7 groups to receive different treatments.

iRFA model: Under anesthesia maintained with 1.5% isoflurane, mice were placed on the electrode plate. The RFA instrument (MedSphere, Shanghai, China) was connected, a 17-gauge monopolar electrode was inserted into the non-central site of tumor tissue, and the ablation was started with parameters of 5 W and 12 s. Thus, some tumor tissues would residue and the iRFA model was established in vivo.

In Vivo Imaging and Biodistribution Analysis: When the tumor volume reached 100-200 mm3, nude mice were randomly divided into three groups. Free CY3, CY3-labeled mEXO, and CY3-labeled hNVs (100 μL, 200 μg/mL) were injected via the tail vein. In vivo imaging was performed at 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h using an IVIS system. After 24 h, the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and tumors were excised, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using the IVIS system.

Biosafety Evaluation: Whole blood from mice was centrifuged and washed with PBS (10 mM, pH 7.4) to obtain pure red blood cells. Then, 0.75 mL of different concentrations (25, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL) of hNV was added to 0.25 mL of 0.2% red blood cells (v/v) and incubated at 37°C for 4 h. Red blood cells mixed with deionized water were considered 100% hemolysis. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation, and the absorbance at 540 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Biotek, USA). After 14 days of iRFA treatment, whole blood and plasma from mice were processed using an automatic blood analyzer (Jiangxi Tecan, China) and an automatic biochemical analyzer (Shenzhen Lanyun, China).

Statistical analyses: Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6.0. All quantitative results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on at least three independent experiments. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired Student's t-test was employed. For experiments involving multiple groups, such as the in vivo therapeutic efficacy and tumor inhibition studies, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni or Tukey post-hoc tests was utilized to evaluate statistical significance among cohorts. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant (p < 0.05), with higher levels of significance denoted as p < 0.01, p < 0.001, or p < 0.0005 in the respective figures.
