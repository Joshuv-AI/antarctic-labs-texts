Prevalence and characteristics of off-label use of antidepressants in paediatric patients in China
Authors: Xinyan Zhang, Jie Li, Ning Zhang, Tiebang Liu, Tianmei Si, Xiaoyan Nie, Luwen Shi
Published: 2025

To the editor:

Using drugs off-label in paediatric patients (age: 0–18 years) has drawn increasing attention worldwide. Off-label use of drugs implies using drugs beyond the scope of their approved market authorisation (eg, patient age, indication, dosage and route of administration). Previous literature reported that the prevalence of off-label drug use ranged from 36.3% to 97.0% among paediatric patients worldwide.1 Off-label medicinal use is a potential risk factor for adverse drug reactions2 3 and may increase the risk of ineffective treatment.4 The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has enacted numerous laws and policies to incentivise or mandate that pharmaceutical companies consider and conduct paediatric clinical trials. Nonetheless, approximately three‐fourths of prescribed drugs in the USA have not been assessed in the paediatric population.5

There is still a large knowledge gap regarding the current prevalence of off-label use of antidepressants among children in China. A previous Chinese study showed that prescribing antidepressants for children has been on the rise since 2013–2016.6 In addition, the prevalence of off-label use of antidepressants among children and adolescent psychiatric outpatients has been as high as 15.8%.7 Therefore, a need exists to monitor the efficacy and safety of antidepressant off-label drugs in the paediatric population and identify suitable dosages based on valid evidence from drug utilisation studies in China and elsewhere. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of off-label use in China of antidepressants that are approved by the FDA and recommended by the Micromedex database8—a US-based, comprehensive, evidence-based clinical decision support system—for use in both adult and pediatric populations in the USA, but are authorised in China solely for adult use.

This study extracted electronic health records of seven major mental health institutions from China’s Psychiatric-Specialized Medical Center database. Included hospitals are The Sixth Peking University-Affiliated Hospital, Tianjin An Ding Hospital, Hunan Provincial Brain Hospital, Nanjing Brain Hospital, Harbin First Specialist Hospital, Wuhan Mental Health Center and Shenzhen Kangning Hospital, all of which are tier-III teaching and research hospitals providing high-level medical services to patients with psychiatric disorders across China. This study was approved by each hospital’s institutional review board, and written informed consent from patients was waived, given that the data were deidentified in the databases.

In this retrospective study, data were collected from the outpatient visits in 2019 of all patients who met the following criteria1: were of any age and2 were prescribed at least one of five selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) or serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors antidepressants (duloxetine, escitalopram, fluoxetine, venlafaxine or citalopram). All of these drugs have been approved in China for adult use only. All of the above prescribed drugs were coded and categorised according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System as recommended by the World Health Organization Collaborating Center for Drug Statistics Methodology.9 Study drugs were identified from the National Drug Code and the Drugs@FDA databases via the official website of China’s National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) and the FDA, respectively. In addition, we summarised the evidence from the IBM Micromedex-Thomson grading system, which includes effectiveness level IIa, recommendation level IIb and evidence level B and above, as the basis for off-label use. For each antidepressant, we systematically reviewed and retrieved its latest product labelling and appendices as well as the Micromedex database gradings to identify drug indications. A product was considered approved for a paediatric indication if the label included any of the following: (a) specific reference to paediatric patients in the ‘Indications and Usage’ section, (b) specific information regarding paediatric dosing in the ‘Dosage and Administration’ section or (c) specific reference to paediatric patients in the ‘Use in specific populations, Pediatric Use’ section.

The five antidepressants had multiple indications for adults in China, but all were indicated for the treatment of depression, while duloxetine also had a non-psychiatric indication (ie, chronic musculoskeletal pain). According to the Micromedex database, venlafaxine is primarily approved for use in paediatric patients for non-depressive disorders such as anxiety disorders or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder indications.

Among adult patients in China and paediatric patients in the USA, the daily recommended antidepressant dosages for the treatment of depression fell within a comparable range, except for duloxetine and citalopram. However, the daily dosage varied considerably among adult patients using the same antidepressant for different indications in China.

Our primary outcomes of interest were the proportions of paediatric patients (aged 0–18 years) and of patients whose age fell outside the age range approved by the FDA and recommended in the Micromedex-Thomson grading system among all identified patients using the study drugs. We also calculated the number of prescriptions per paediatric patient for those under 18 years old or beyond the age range endorsed by the FDA and Micromedex (online supplemental e-table 1). The secondary outcomes were the gap between the prescribed doses and ages in China, and the age range or dosage shown on the FDA-approved labels as recommended by the Micromedex grading system.

All data were independently collected and extracted by two investigators. We extracted the following variables: (a) whether the drug was approved by the NMPA in China and/or the FDA to have any indication in adult and/or paediatric populations and (b) the approved age and daily dose range. In the Micromedex database, we extracted any indication for which the drug was included in the paediatric population, as well as information on the level of evidence, the drug’s effectiveness rating and the level of recommendation. We conducted a descriptive analysis of the number of patients, prescriptions and prescriptions per patient and provided the median (interquartile range (IQR)) for continuous variables. Additionally, we calculated the number of prescriptions per patient for each of the investigated medications. The maximum daily doses of prescribed antidepressants for each patient during the study period were scatter-plotted by age, with the dotted line indicating the approved ranges in age and dosage. We excluded prescriptions that lacked or provided incomplete information regarding dosage, indication or patient characteristics (online supplemental e-table 1).

A total of 76 120 eligible patients with 303 252 prescriptions were included in this study. The median (IQR) age was 46 (31 to 59) years. For all antidepressants, the number of female patients (n = 55 617) who were prescribed the study drugs was higher than that of males (n = 20 503).

Among the included antidepressants, venlafaxine had the highest number of patients (n =19 239) and escitalopram had the highest number of prescriptions (n =127 907), respectively. Citalopram had the highest annual number of prescriptions per patient (11 458/2376; 4.82 prescriptions per patient) and was closely followed by venlafaxine (80 454/19 239; 4.18 prescriptions per patient). Meanwhile, escitalopram had the lowest annual number of prescriptions per patient (132 036/38 356; 3.44 prescriptions per patient; online supplemental e-table 2).

The prevalence of off-label use in paediatric patients in China endorsed by the FDA and Micromedex is shown in table 1. Overall, the proportion of paediatric patients prescribed each drug ranged from 1.09% to 6.85%. The largest proportion of paediatric patients was observed with fluoxetine (n=437, 6.85%), whereas the smallest was observed with citalopram (n=26, 1.09%). As for the proportion of patients whose age was beyond the age range endorsed by the FDA and Micromedex, fluoxetine (n=5, 0.08%) and escitalopram (n=28, 0.07%) showed slightly larger proportions, while venlafaxine (n=3, 0.02%) and citalopram were rarely prescribed to this group. When examining the number of prescriptions per paediatric patient among those aged<18 years, we found that fluoxetine (1014/437, 2.32) had the highest number among the antidepressants. However, duloxetine had the highest number of patients outside the age range endorsed by the FDA and Micromedex (8/5, 1.6).

Our study is the first to investigate the off-label use of five antidepressants among Chinese paediatric patients. The FDA and Micromedex endorse these drugs for use in both adult and paediatric US populations, yet in China, they are approved only for adult use. This study demonstrated that off-label use of these antidepressants was more prevalent in Chinese psychiatric hospitals’ paediatric population in 2019 (2.61%) than in the hospitalised paediatric populations of European and American countries and regions (0.3%–1.8%).10 11 This may be due to potential confounding factors such as differences in prescribing practices among doctors in various countries and differences in diagnostic criteria for mental disorders. Chinese psychiatrists may prescribe or adjust prescriptions for children in the absence of evidence by referring to international treatment guidelines or medications that have been approved for use in adults in China. But most significantly, according to the evidence contained in the FDA and Micromedex databases, the maximum daily doses of study drugs prescribed to Chinese paediatric patients were lower than those recommended.

We observed that a higher proportion of patients under 18 years were prescribed fluoxetine and escitalopram among the antidepressants studied. In the clinical management of anxiety or mild to moderate depressive symptoms in children and adolescents, non-pharmacological approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy should be the first-line treatment; however, severe symptomatology or ineffective psychotherapy may demand a combination treatment approach including antidepressants.12 One possible explanation for the increased use of fluoxetine is that it is the drug recommended for first-line treatment of paediatric depression by the World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry. Moreover, fluoxetine and escitalopram are the only agents with FDA approval for the treatment of depression in children aged 8 years and above.13 An additional consideration is the FDA’s requirement that manufacturers of all antidepressants must print a ‘black-box’ warning label alerting physicians to the increased risk of worsening depression and suicidality in children and adolescents treated with these agents. As there are no antidepressants approved for the treatment of depressive disorders in children and adolescents in China, physicians need to carefully inform their guardians of the benefits and risks prior to the initiation of the drug and conduct patient monitoring and follow-up during drug use.

Our study’s secondary focus was to compare the prescriptions of these five antidepressants dispensed to Chinese paediatric patients with the dosage recommendations and age ranges shown on FDA-approved labels and the Micromedex-Thomson grading system. We found that the maximum daily doses of the antidepressants prescribed for many paediatric patients in China were lower than the maximum paediatric doses endorsed by FDA and Micromedex. This may be due to several factors: lower liver enzyme metabolic activity in children, SSRIs’ potential heightened risk of triggering activation syndrome in adolescents, clinical guideline recommendations proposing psychotherapy as the first-line treatment for childhood depression and initial low-dose prescriptions when medication is used as a second-line treatment. Therefore, Chinese physicians were also likely to prescribe rather conservatively when prescribing antidepressants to children and adolescents in clinical practice. Additionally, this restrained approach may also stem from cultural or systemic societal factors, such as biases and concerns about the safety of medication for children’s brain development; these can lead to parents’ hesitation in accepting antidepressant prescriptions for their children. Geographical barriers and limited insurance coverage further restrict the implementation of optimal medication strategies. However, the prescription of antidepressants with lower than recommended doses for children may be problematic, as the effectiveness and safety of these drugs at low doses have not been thoroughly evaluated.

The NMPA has introduced several drug regulatory reform measures in recent years to expedite the approval of novel drugs in China.14 4 Yet, more relevant laws and policies are needed to incentivise and mandate clinical trials on the efficacy and safety of antidepressants in the Chinese paediatric population; these could significantly address the dilemma of off-label paediatric drug use.

This study has several limitations. First, the specific indications of these antidepressant prescriptions for paediatric patients were not considered in interpreting the results, as some diagnostic information for patients in this database was missing. Second, the analysis was based on prescription-level data and might not accurately reflect the off-label use of drugs by weight or by indication at the individual level.

In clinical practice, the off-label use of antidepressants was very prevalent among Chinese children and adolescents. This may be driven by various factors, including physician preference based on individual clinical experience or familiarity with specific drugs, international clinical guidelines and the increased treatment demand from parents seeking to safeguard their children, as well as their varying receptivity to the available medications.

This study showed that Chinese physicians are likely to refer to international guidelines when prescribing in the absence of available national evidence on the efficacy or safety of psychotropic drugs in the Chinese paediatric population. Conducting clinical paediatric studies in China is urgently needed to optimise clear instructions for the appropriate prescription of antidepressants in children. This could better ensure the safe, rational use of these drugs, especially in cases where treatment falls outside of the approved usage parameters.
