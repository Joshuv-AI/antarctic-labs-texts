Integrated metabolome and transcriptome reveal the molecular basis of flavonoid pathway-mediated fruit firmness variation in sweet cherry (Prunus avium L.)
Authors: Yanlin Li, Zhen Yan, Jing Li, Huimin Chen, Xin Hua, Shengnan Tan, Yanqiang Gao, Yazhou Zhao, Baojiang Zheng, Xuwei Duan, Xiaoming Zhang, Guohua Yan, Kaichun Zhang, Zheyong Xue, Jing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sweet cherry is rich in diverse bioactive compounds and functional nutrients. Fruit firmness is a key determinant of postharvest storability, shelf life, and commercial value. Secondary metabolism is one of the important factors regulating sweet cherry fruit firmness, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of firmness by secondary metabolism remain unclear. We performed integrated transcriptomic and untargeted metabolomic analyses at four key developmental stages using two cultivars with contrasting firmness: soft-fleshed ‘Shangnan2808’ and hard-fleshed ‘Wanhongzhu’. We also conducted bulked metabolomic analysis on extreme firmness phenotypes from an F1 segregating population derived from these two parents. Fruit firmness differed significantly from color break to full ripeness stage, with epidermal cell thickness and mesocarp cell packing density identified as major structural contributors. KEGG enrichment revealed that flavonoid biosynthesis was the most significantly enriched pathway among six pathways containing 41 stable differential metabolites. Multi-omics integration identified 186 differentially expressed genes involved in these pathways. Among them, the flavanone 3-hydroxylase-like gene FUN_006793 (F3HL) exhibited an expression pattern highly consistent with kaempferol accumulation, suggesting it may be a core candidate regulating fruit firmness. Bulked metabolomics further validated seven key metabolites, including kaempferol and chlorogenic acid, both involved in flavonoid biosynthesis, confirming the central role of this pathway. WGCNA identified three co-expression modules significantly correlated with firmness and allowed the construction of a flavonoid-mediated regulatory network. This study systematically reveals the potential mechanism by which flavonoid biosynthesis may modulate sweet cherry fruit firmness, and provides a theoretical basis for investigating firmness regulatory networks and offer valuable candidate genes, metabolic biomarkers, and functional markers for molecular breeding of high-quality, long-storability sweet cherry cultivars.

Introduction

Sweet cherry (Prunus avium L.) is a deciduous fruit tree of the genus Prunus in the Rosaceae family. Its fruits are prized for their exquisite flavor, rich nutritional profile, abundant bioactive and functional phytochemicals (Kelley et al., 2018; Budak, 2017; Blando and Oomah, 2019). Fruit firmness is one of the important economic characteristics in sweet cherry, serving as an indispensable factor not only of fruit flavor quality and textural properties, but also of postharvest storability, shelf life and commercial value. Sweet cherries with insufficient firmness are highly prone to mechanical damage during harvesting, sorting, transportation and storage, which drives a sharp rise in decay incidence, rapid loss of commercial value, and severe restriction of the market penetration and core industrial competitiveness of this crop (Jiu et al., 2024; Qi et al., 2024). Thus, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of sweet cherry fruit firmness regulation, identifying key functional genes and markers, and breeding high-firmness, storable elite cultivars have become urgent key scientific issues and industrial demands for further development.

Fruit firmness, shaped by a highly complex regulatory network involving cellular structure, cell wall metabolism, phytohormone signaling and genetics, is structurally determined by cuticle properties, epidermal cell characteristics, and mesocarp cell size and packing density (Ng et al., 2015). The cuticle thickness of sweet cherry fruits shows a biphasic trend of rising first and falling later during development, and mesocarp cells expand progressively as fruits ripen. Compact mesocarp cells and a thick epidermal structure maintain high firmness in hard-fleshed sweet cherry cultivars, a conserved pattern across apple, grape and other fruit crops (Knoche et al., 2004; Jia et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2025). Cell wall metabolism is well established as the core mechanism governing fruit softening (Fuentealba et al., 2021). The disassembly of cell wall architecture and degradation of its core components such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin during fruit ripening are the primary driver of firmness loss (Fuentealba et al., 2021; Gutiérrez et al., 2021; Frempong et al., 2022), with pectin degradation being the most prominent event during softening. Key cell wall-degrading enzymes, including polygalacturonase (PG), pectin methylesterase (PME), pectate lyase (PL), and β-galactosidase (β-Gal), play central roles in fruit softening across peach, apple, tomato, strawberry and other crops (Ghiani et al., 2011; Atkinson et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2018, 2025a; Lika et al., 2026). Repressing the expression of genes encoding these enzymes significantly delays softening and enhances fruit firmness: for example, suppressing PG1 or TBG4 (β-Gal 4) in tomato, or silencing PL in strawberry, consistently increases fruit firmness or inhibits softening (Brummell and Labavitch, 1997; Smith et al., 2002; Uluisik et al., 2016).

In recent years, with the advancement of high-throughput metabolomics technology, the regulatory role of secondary metabolism, especially phenylpropanoid, flavonoid, and lignin metabolism in fruit firmness has been gradually revealed, which has expanded the understanding of the molecular regulatory network of fruit firmness beyond the classic cell wall metabolism framework (Liu et al., 2026; Sanchez-Ballesta et al., 2022; Pal et al., 2024). Recent studies have further identified cuticle structure and composition, lignin biosynthesis and accumulation, and cuticular phenolic compounds as key regulators of fruit texture and softening progression (Hao et al., 2016; Peng et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2026). Notably, lignin and flavonoid biosynthesis share p-coumaric acid as a common precursor in the phenylpropanoid pathway (Dong and Lin, 2021), providing a critical new perspective for dissecting secondary metabolism-mediated regulation of fruit firmness.

Phytohormones are critical upstream regulators of sweet cherry fruit development, ripening, and firmness, with abscisic acid (ABA), gibberellins (GAs), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and salicylic acid (SA) acting synergistically in this process (Kuhn et al., 2020; Clayton-Cuch et al., 2021; Nacouzi and El Kayal, 2024; Liu et al., 2025). ABA is the most well-characterized phytohormone in firmness regulation, with its biosynthetic and catabolic genes directly modulating pectin metabolism and fruit firmness in sweet cherry and other fruit crops (Li et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2012). Multiple transcription factors (TFs) and functional genes have also been validated to govern fruit firmness via cell wall metabolism and ripening regulation (Souza et al., 2016; Jones et al., 2002; Chung et al., 2010; Ma et al., 2014; Powell et al., 2003; Cao et al., 2012; Sagar et al., 2013). Genetic mapping studies have consistently identified a major firmness-associated locus on sweet cherry LG4, with the TF NAC56 within this locus confirmed to drive fruit ripening and softening (Cai et al., 2019; Calle and Wünsch, 2020; Crump et al., 2022; Holušová et al., 2023; Qi et al., 2022). With the rapid development of multi-omics technologies, integrated transcriptome and metabolome analysis has emerged as a core strategy for dissecting the molecular mechanisms of fruit quality trait formation in fruit crops. The metabolome directly reflects an organism's phenotypic state and precisely captures dynamic metabolite changes throughout fruit development. Integrated multi-omics analysis effectively links the "gene-metabolite-phenotype regulatory axis, enabling systematic dissection of the regulatory networks underlying trait formation. Combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis has identified PSY, NCED1 and CCD4 as key genes driving differential carotenoid accumulation in apricot fruits (Zhou et al., 2021), and metabolome integration has greatly accelerated candidate gene identification in fruit tree research. Treating metabolite levels as quantitative traits enables metabolic quantitative trait locus (mQTL) mapping (Carreno-Quintero et al., 2012), while integrating metabolomics with genome-wide association study (GWAS) allows metabolome-wide association study (mGWAS) (Luo, 2015). While mQTL analysis has been widely applied in model plants such as Arabidopsis, tomato (Toubiana et al., 2015; Knoch et al., 2017) and staple crops as for rice, maize (Gong et al., 2013), it has also been used to dissect metabolite regulatory relationships and identify candidate genes in cultivated strawberry (Vallarino et al., 2019).

To date, studies on sweet cherry fruit firmness have mainly focused on functional characterization of single genes related to cell wall metabolism and traditional genetic mapping. The regulatory mechanisms of fruit firmness mediated by secondary metabolic pathways have not yet been systematically elucidated. In particular, the roles and regulatory networks of phenylpropanoid-derived secondary metabolites, especially flavonoids, in sweet cherry fruit firmness formation remain largely unclear. Integrated multi-omics analysis, represented by the combination of transcriptomics and metabolomics, has established a bridge between the "gene-metabolite-phenotype" regulatory axis, and has become the core technical approach to dissect the molecular mechanism of fruit firmness formation (Li et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2025). However, with the deepening of research, the limitations of metabolic regulation-based studies have gradually emerged. Most current studies only stay at the level of correlation analysis between metabolites and firmness phenotypes, and lack sufficient causal validation of the regulatory relationship; the spatiotemporal specificity of metabolic regulation and the complex crosstalk between metabolic pathways are far from being fully resolved; the translation of metabolic markers into practical breeding applications still faces huge challenges (Zhu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025).

Given the critical scientific gaps and industrial demands outlined above, this study systematically investigates the molecular regulatory mechanism of sweet cherry fruit firmness using integrated multi-omics approaches. Specifically, this study performed combined transcriptomic and non-targeted metabolomic profiling of sweet cherry fruits with contrasting firmness phenotypes across critical developmental stages, and further validated the potential key regulatory patterns using bulked metabolomic analysis of extreme firmness individuals from an F1 segregating population. We aim to clarify the core metabolic pathways mediating sweet cherry fruit firmness variation, and identify key candidate genes and functional metabolites associated with fruit firmness regulation. The results of this study will deepen our understanding of secondary metabolism-mediated fruit firmness regulatory mechanisms, and may provide a solid theoretical foundation and practical breeding tools for the development of new sweet cherry cultivars with excellent storability and comprehensive fruit quality.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, reagents and instrumentation

The early-maturing, soft-fleshed sweet cherry cultivar 'Shangnan2808' was used as the male parent, and the late-maturing, hard-fleshed cultivar 'Wanhongzhu' was used as the female parent. Both varieties of trees grow well in the cherry germplasm orchard of the Institute of Forestry and Pomology, Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences under unified field management.

Untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed on fruit samples collected from 'Shangnan2808' and 'Wanhongzhu' at four key developmental stages, namely pit hardening stage, color break stage, ripening stage, and full ripeness stage, which corresponded to 20, 35, 42 and 49 days after full bloom (DAFB), respectively. For bulked untargeted metabolomic validation, fruit samples at the ripening stage were collected from 322 individuals of the F1 segregating population derived from the cross 'Shangnan2808' × 'Wanhongzhu'. Fruits were randomly harvested from uniformly growing trees of 'Shangnan2808', 'Wanhongzhu' and their F1 progeny, with at least three biological replicates per sample set. Fruits were pitted in a 0 °C metal bath, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until subsequent analysis. To avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles, samples were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen with a mortar and pestle prior to metabolite extraction. For each sample, 100 mg of the fine powder was accurately weighed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, and metabolites were extracted with 80% HPLC-grade methanol. The extracts were then lyophilized in a vacuum freeze dryer and stored at −80 °C until use. Detailed information on all reagents and instruments used in this study is provided in Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Measurement of fruit firmness and anatomical structure observation

Fruits of the soft-fleshed 'Shangnan2808' and hard-fleshed 'Wanhongzhu' collected at the four aforementioned developmental stages were used for firmness measurement and anatomical structure observation. The longitudinal firmness of intact peel-on fruits was measured using an FT-7 desktop non-destructive compression firmness tester (Firmtech, Germany) (Shirvani et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2017). A flat compression probe and a cherry-specific sample tray were used for all measurements. A standard compression force of 200 g was applied at the equatorial position of each intact fruit. Firmness values were expressed in newtons per millimeter (N/mm). Thirty mature fruits and three technical replicates per fruit were measured for each sample, and the average value was used for subsequent statistical analysis. Immediately after harvest, fruit samples for anatomical analysis were fixed in Formalin-Aceto-Alcohol (FAA) fixative solution (Table S1) and stored at 4 °C. The fixed fruit samples were processed for paraffin sectioning, followed by double staining with safranin O and fast green (Table S1). Then using CaseViewer 2.4 software (3DHISTECH, Hungary) to observe the morphological characteristics of the epidermal and mesodermal cells in the stained paraffin sections of fruit samples. Digital images were acquired at objective magnifications of ten and forty times to examine tissue architecture and cellular morphology respectively. Five representative visual fields were randomly selected from each section for observation and measurement. Cell size arrangement density and structural features of the epidermal and mesocarp tissues were recorded uniformly across all samples to ensure consistent and comparable morphological evaluation.

Untargeted metabolomic profiling via LC-MS/MS

Non-volatile, ambient-separable metabolites were profiled in fruits of two parental cultivars ('Shangnan2808' and 'Wanhongzhu') at four key developmental stages via Triple TOF 6600 LC-MS/MS (Table S2). To maximize the coverage of detectable metabolites, we systematically optimized critical parameters including extraction solvent composition and ratio, sample input amount, injection volume, and mass spectrometry acquisition protocols according to Chen et al. (2023) (Chen et al., 2023). The final optimized extraction and detection workflow was as follows: 100 mg of each fruit powder sample was accurately weighed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, and 1000 μL of 80% HPLC-grade methanol was added as the extraction solvent. Samples were thoroughly vortexed, then shaken at 1400 rpm for 4 h at room temperature on a vortex oscillator. After an additional vortex step, samples were centrifuged at 20000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. 750 μL of the supernatant was collected and completely evaporated using a vacuum concentrator. The dried residue was reconstituted in 200 μL of 5% HPLC-grade methanol, then filtered through a 0.22 μm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed using a mobile phase consisting of solvent A (ultrapure water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid), both of HPLC grade. The flow rate was set to 200 μL/min, the injection volume was 1 μL, and the column temperature was maintained at 30 °C. Mass spectrometry detection was performed in positive electrospray ionization (ESI) mode, with the following parameters: gas temperature 350 °C, mass scan range 50-1000 m/z, with untargeted MS/MS acquisition.

Metabolomic profiling via GC-MS/MS

Volatile, semi-volatile, and thermally stable metabolites were profiled in fruit samples from 'Shangnan2808' and 'Wanhongzhu' at the four aforementioned developmental stages using a Thermo Fisher ISQ-LT GC-MS/MS system (Table S2). To maximize metabolite detection, we optimized the GC-MS/MS protocol (Lisec et al., 2006) by evaluating sample input amount and oven temperature ramping program, with the number of detectable peaks and chromatographic resolution as the key evaluation criteria. The optimal extraction and derivatization workflow was established as follows: 10 mg of fruit powder (the optimal input amount, which yielded the best peak shape and highest resolution among 10-100 mg tested) was transferred to a 2 mL centrifuge tube, and 1400 μL of pre-chilled pure methanol (−20 °C) was added, followed by vortexing for 10 s. 60 μL of adonitol internal standard (0.2 mg/mL dissolved in ultrapure water) was added, and samples were vortexed for 10 s prior to incubation at 70 °C and 950 rpm for 10 min. Samples were then centrifuged at 11000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube. 750 μL of pre-chilled chloroform (−20 °C) and 1500 μL of pre-chilled ultrapure water (4 °C) were added to the supernatant, followed by vortexing for 10 s. After centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, 150 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 1.5 mL glass vial with an injection liner, and dried completely in a vacuum concentrator. The dried residue was dissolved in 40 μL of methoxyamine hydrochloride (20 mg/mL in pyridine) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h for oximation. Subsequently, 70 μL of N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA, 20 μL/mL) was added, and samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min for derivatization. The final derivatized samples were subjected to GC-MS/MS analysis with an injection volume of 1 μL.

The GC-MS/MS detection parameters were set as follows: inlet temperature 220 °C, ion source temperature 230 °C, interface temperature 250 °C, carrier gas flow rate 1 mL/min. The oven temperature program was initiated at 70 °C with a 5 min hold, then ramped to 150 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min, followed by a further ramp to 230 °C at 8 °C/min, at which point the run was terminated.

Metabolomic data processing and statistical analysis

Raw LC-TOF-MS data files were first converted to mzML format using ProteoWizard (Kessner et al., 2008), then converted to ABF format via ABF Converter for downstream processing in MS-DIAL (Tsugawa et al., 2015). Data preprocessing included peak extraction, peak grouping, retention time correction, peak alignment, and missing value imputation. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching mass spectral features against the MetLin database (https://metlin.scripps.edu), yielding retention time, m/z ratio, peak area, and putative metabolite identities for high-confidence matches. Further statistical analysis was performed using the MetaboAnalyst online platform (https://www.MetaboAnalyst.ca). Six technical replicates were performed for each sample, and the mean value was used for analysis. Volcano plot construction, principal component analysis (PCA), and other routine analyses were performed using the default parameters on the BMKCloud platform (www.biocloud.net). PCA was performed to identify the main sources of variation across the large sample dataset; partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was used for sample classification to identify similarities and differences between groups; orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA) was applied to separate inter-group variation from intra-group variation, enabling focused analysis of positively correlated variables associated with phenotypic differences.

Differential metabolites were screened using a combination of Student's t-test and variable importance in projection (VIP) values from the OPLS-DA model. Metabolites meeting the criteria of p < 0.05 and VIP>1.0 were defined as significantly differential metabolites. Metabolic pathway annotation and enrichment analysis were performed using the HMDB, KEGG, Reactome, BioCyc, and MetaCyc databases (Labena et al., 2018), with KEGG compound identifiers retrieved from the KEGG COMPOUND database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/compound/).

Transcriptome sequencing and identification of key genes regulating fruit firmness

Total RNA was extracted from fruit samples of 'Shangnan2808' and 'Wanhongzhu' using the FastPure® Universal Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Table S1). The purity, concentration, and integrity of all RNA samples were assessed, and high-quality RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+gDNA wiper) (Table S1) for cDNA library construction. Briefly, eukaryotic mRNA was enriched using oligo (dT)-coated magnetic beads, then fragmented randomly using fragmentation buffer. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers with fragmented mRNA as the template, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis using buffer, dNTPs, RNase H, and DNA polymerase I. Double-stranded cDNA was purified using AMPure XP beads, followed by end repair, A-tailing, and sequencing adapter ligation. Fragment size selection was performed using AMPure XP beads, and the final cDNA library was enriched via PCR amplification. To ensure library quality, the effective concentration of each library was accurately quantified via qRT-PCR, with a threshold of effective concentration>2 nM for sequencing. Qualified libraries were pooled according to the target sequencing depth and subjected to high-throughput sequencing on the Illumina sequencing platform.

Bioinformatic analysis, data integration, and visualization of transcriptome data were performed using the analytical tools provided by the BMKCloud platform (www.biocloud.net). Raw sequencing reads were filtered to generate high-quality clean data, which were then aligned to the sweet cherry reference genome (Prunus avium Tieton Genome v2.0, https://www.rosaceae.org/Analysis/9262820) to generate mapped reads. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2. Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology and KEGG pathways was conducted using topGO and clusterProfiler. DEGs were screened using the thresholds of |Fold Change|≥2 and FDR<0.01. Aligned reads were assembled and quantified using StringTie (Pertea et al., 2015), with gene expression levels normalized to Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million fragments mapped (FPKM) values. Library quality assessments, including insert fragment length test and randomness test, were performed using the mapped data. Differential expression analysis, functional annotation, and functional enrichment analysis of DEGs were performed based on gene expression levels across different samples and groups. Functional analysis and bioinformatic interpretation of the screened DEGs were performed via functional enrichment and pathway analysis. Weighted gene coexpression network analysis was performed using the WGCNA package with a soft threshold power determined according to scale free topology criteria.

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis for regulatory network construction

For integrated multi-omics analysis, transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling were performed using paired samples from the same fruit of 'Shangnan2808' and 'Wanhongzhu'. For each sample from the same individual tree, same tissue, same developmental stage, each sample was split into two equal aliquots: one for RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing, and the other for metabolite extraction and metabolomic profiling. All assays were performed with three independent biological replicates. Each biological replicate was a pooled sample of uniform fruits from multiple independent trees, with strict one-to-one pairing between the corresponding transcriptomic and metabolomic samples. Based on the paired sample data, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between gene expression and metabolite abundance. Gene-metabolite pairs with a correlation coefficient r ≥ 0.8 and p < 0.05 were defined as significantly correlated pairs, to screen for genes and metabolites with highly consistent expression/accumulation trends.

Screened DEGs and differential metabolites (DEMs) were mapped to the KEGG metabolic pathway database, and the number of enriched DEGs and DEMs in each pathway was counted to calculate enrichment significance. The regulatory network of differential metabolites was constructed based on the KEGG pathway annotation of DEGs and DEMs. Pathway significance enrichment analysis was performed using hypergeometric tests to identify pathways significantly enriched in DEGs compared with the whole genome background. WGCNA (DiLeo et al., 2011) was performed to cluster genes with similar expression patterns into distinct modules. The regulatory network of DEGs was visualized using Cytoscape software (Shannon et al., 2003).

Bulked metabolomic analysis of extreme fruit firmness phenotypes in the F1 segregating population

Phenotypic data for fruit firmness were collected from the 322 individuals of the F1 segregating population derived from the cross 'Shangnan2808' × 'Wanhongzhu'. Based on the firmness distribution, 10 individuals with extreme hard-fleshed phenotype and 10 individuals with extreme soft-fleshed phenotype were selected to construct two bulked pools. Detailed phenotypic information and firmness values of the bulked pools are provided in Tables S3 and S4. Metabolite profiling of the bulked pools was performed using the LC-MS/MS and GC-MS/MS protocols described above. Multivariate statistical analyses, including PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA, were performed on the untargeted metabolomic data from the soft- and hard-fleshed bulked pools (Worley and Powers, 2013). The significance of each variable was evaluated using Student's t-test and VIP values, with metabolites meeting the criteria of p < 0.05 and VIP>1.0 defined as significantly differential metabolites. Metabolic pathway annotation and enrichment analysis were performed using the HMDB, KEGG, Reactome, BioCyc, and MetaCyc databases.

Raw LC-TOF-MS data processing was performed as described above: raw data were converted to mzML format using ProteoWizard (Kessner et al., 2008), then to ABF format via ABF Converter for preprocessing in MS-DIAL (Tsugawa et al., 2015), including peak extraction, retention time correction, peak alignment, and metabolite annotation against the MetLin database (https://metlin.scripps.edu). Further statistical analysis was performed using the MetaboAnalyst online platform (https://www.MetaboAnalyst.ca). Ten technical replicates were performed for each bulked sample, and the mean value was used for analysis. Volcano plot construction and PCA were performed using the default parameters on the BMKCloud platform (www.biocloud.net).

Results

Epidermal thickness and mesocarp cell packing density are the key determinants of firmness variation in sweet cherry fruits

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the firmness difference between hard- and soft-fleshed sweet cherry fruits, we first systematically characterized the fruit morphological traits and mesocarp microstructure. However, 'Shangnan2808' showed extremely significantly lower firmness than 'Wanhongzhu' from the color break stage onward. Fruit firmness dropped sharply in 'Shangnan2808' after color break, reaching only 0.702 N/mm at full ripeness. Across the entire developmental process, epidermal cell size of 'Shangnan2808' was significantly smaller than that of 'Wanhongzhu' (Fig. 2D–I, Table 1). Epidermal thickness measurements revealed that the average epidermal cell thickness of 'Shangnan2808' was only 17.8 μm at the ripening stage and 17.2 μm at the full ripeness stage, markedly lower than the 23.3 μm and 22.5 μm detected in 'Wanhongzhu' at the corresponding stages (Table 1). Mesocarp cells were densely arranged in both cultivars at the pit hardening stage. Fully ripe 'Shangnan2808' fruits are juicier, whereas 'Wanhongzhu' fruits show a crisp and firm texture. These phenotypic differences may be driven by differential gene expression or cell differentiation during fruit development, and further research is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and guide cultivation and postharvest practices. Notably, fruit firmness differed most drastically between the two cultivars at the color break stage (Fig. 2C). Firmness declined sharply in 'Shangnan2808' at this stage, transiently increased in 'Wanhongzhu', and decreased markedly only at the ripening stage. Thus, molecular and metabolic variations at the color break stage are the key focus of our subsequent analyses. Collectively, these results confirm that the soft-fleshed 'Shangnan2808' and hard-fleshed 'Wanhongzhu' are suitable for subsequent untargeted metabolomic, transcriptomic and other omics analyses.

Identification of differential metabolites in fruits via LC-MS and GC-MS

To investigate the metabolic differences between hard- and soft-fleshed sweet cherry fruits, we performed comprehensive metabolomic profiling using LC-MS and GC-MS. Statistical analysis was conducted on the preprocessed peak data, with details listed in Table S5. The number of metabolite peaks generally declined gradually during fruit ripening. In 'Wanhongzhu', metabolite peaks peaked at the pit hardening stage and reached the minimum at the ripening stage, then rebounded at full ripeness, presumably due to the degradation of macromolecules and the release of abundant small-molecule metabolites in the late ripening stage. A total of 123, 152, 163 and 170 annotated metabolites were detected in 'Shangnan2808' at the pit hardening, color break, ripening and full ripeness stages, with 199, 163, 155 and 157 metabolites identified in 'Wanhongzhu' at the corresponding stages (Table 2). The flavonoid biosynthesis pathway drives metabolic and phenotypic divergence

Untargeted metabolomics enables the detection of a vast array of compounds, but this large-scale dataset poses challenges for focusing on core metabolic pathways linked to the target trait. To identify core metabolites associated with fruit firmness, we focused on metabolites with consistently differential accumulation (VIP>1) across all four developmental stages. Venn analysis identified 41 such stable differential metabolites (Fig. 5B), predominantly classified as phenylpropanoids and polyketides. A total of 181 differential metabolites (VIP>1) were identified at this stage, mainly flavonoids, organic nitrogen compounds, prenol lipids, and carboxylic acids and derivatives (Fig. 5A). Transcriptomic sequencing was conducted for fruits of the two cultivars at four developmental stages, generating 150.25 Gb of clean data with a Q30 ratio≥93.83%. Clean reads were mapped to the sweet cherry reference genome from the GDR database, with mapping rates of 94.22%–95.83%. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using thresholds of |Fold Change|≥2 and FDR<0.01 (Table S8). KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted the top 20 pathways, dominated by phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, and plant–pathogen interaction (Fig. 6B). By co-mapping DEGs and differential metabolites to KEGG pathways, we reconstructed the potential biosynthetic pathway of kaempferol based on the identified key DEGs and metabolites. In sweet cherry, p-coumaroyl-CoA is directed into two distinct metabolic branches via the catalysis of different enzymes. First, p-coumaroyl-CoA is catalyzed by shikimate O-hydroxycinnamoyltransferase (KO: K13065) to form chlorogenic acid (caffeoyl quinic acid), which is further converted to caffeoyl-CoA via the catalysis of the same enzyme. The second branch is initiated by chalcone synthase (KO: K00660), which catalyzes the formation of naringenin chalcone from p-coumaroyl-CoA. This is followed by the synthesis of naringenin via the catalysis of chalcone isomerase (KO: K01859), which is then converted to dihydrokaempferol (DHK) by naringenin 3-dioxygenase (KO: K00475). Finally, DHK is catalyzed by the flavanone 3-hydroxylase encoded by FUN_006793 to generate kaempferol (Fig. 6D).

Furthermore, the flavonol synthase gene FUN_006793 may positively regulate kaempferol biosynthesis in sweet cherry. In soft-fleshed 'Shangnan2808', the FUN_006793 expression and kaempferol content remained low at all four stages (Fig. 6E–G). In contrast, both of them were significantly higher in the hard-fleshed 'Wanhongzhu' across all four developmental stages, peaking at the pit hardening and color break stages and declining thereafter (Fig. 6F–H). These results confirm that FUN_006793 may positively modulate kaempferol biosynthesis in sweet cherry.

Metabolomic divergence analysis of extreme bulked pools

To further improve the stability and reliability of metabolomic analysis results between soft- and hard-fleshed fruits, mitigate interference from individual variation and stochastic events, eliminate random fluctuations among biological replicates, and highlight the overall population-level trend, we selected 10 individuals with extremely soft-fleshed phenotype and 10 with extremely hard-fleshed phenotype from the 322 plants of the F1 segregating population derived from the cross between 'Shangnan2808' and 'Wanhongzhu' to construct two extreme bulked pools for untargeted metabolomic profiling via bulked segregant analysis (BSA).

In total, 133 and 166 annotated metabolites were detected in the hard- and soft-fleshed bulked pools, respectively, and their chemical classifications were consistent with those of the parental lines, including benzenoids, lignans, neolignans and related compounds, phenylpropanoids, polyketides and so forth. PCA (Fig. 8A) and PLS-DA (Fig. 8B) of metabolites from the two extreme bulked pools revealed clear divergence in metabolite abundance between the two pools. Volcano plot analysis of the two bulked pools (Fig. 8C) identified only 18 metabolites with significantly differential accumulation between the two groups. A total of 62 metabolites with VIP score>1 were screened via PLS-DA, and the top 15 metabolites with the greatest differential abundance, including C66, C41 and C170 (Table S6), ranked by VIP score are shown in Fig. 8D. KEGG pathway analysis was conducted on the 62 screened metabolites, 23 of which were successfully annotated to multiple metabolic pathways, including purine metabolism, zeatin biosynthesis, thiamine metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, stilbenoid, etc. Fruit firmness differed significantly between the two parental lines at ripening. The extreme phenotype bulked pools were established from extreme firmness individuals of the derived segregating population. Thus, analyzing the common differential metabolites between parents and bulked pools holds greater biological significance for fruit firmness trait association. Venn diagram analysis of VIP>1 differential metabolites between parents and extreme bulked pools (Fig. 8E) revealed 24 consistently differential metabolites. After removing exogenous contaminants, 7 key metabolites were identified, including chlorogenic acid, (−)-epicatechin and AMP. All seven metabolites showed completely consistent differential patterns at the ripening stage (Fig. 8F–L), with high abundance in the soft-fleshed parent 'Shangnan2808' and soft-fleshed pool, but significantly low levels in the hard-fleshed parent 'Wanhongzhu' and hard-fleshed pool. Caffeic acid is both a precursor in the lignin biosynthesis pathway and a degradation product of lignin. At the ripening stage of sweet cherry, lignin degradation accelerates fruit softening and releases a large amount of caffeic acid, which can further combine with quinic acid to synthesize chlorogenic acid (Boerjan et al., 2003). This may account for the high accumulation of chlorogenic acid in the soft-fleshed bulked pool and 'Shangnan2808'.

Discussion

Fruit firmness formation has a complex structural basis. Previous studies have confirmed that cuticle thickness, mesocarp cell size, and packing density are key anatomical features determining firmness in stone fruits, and the developmental status of mesocarp fibre cells also has a potential impact on fruit mechanical strength (Espeleta-Maya et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2023; Al-Dairi et al., 2023). In this study, paraffin sectioning and microscopic observation of fruits across the developmental process of the two contrasting parental lines revealed that from the color break stage to the full ripeness stage, the hard-fleshed 'Wanhongzhu' had significantly greater epidermal cell thickness and more densely packed mesocarp cells than the soft-fleshed 'Shangnan2808'. Meanwhile, there was no significant difference in the number of mesocarp fibre bundles between the two cultivars during the developmental stages with significant firmness divergence. These results indicate that epidermal thickness and mesocarp cell packing density are the core structural determinants of firmness variation in sweet cherry fruits, while the number of mesocarp fibre bundles is not the main driver of inter-cultivar firmness differences. These findings align with previous reports on the anatomical basis of fruit firmness in pear, grape, apple and other fruit crops (Konarska, 2013; Su et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020). The firmness difference between the two cultivars peaked at the color break stage, a key period for secondary metabolite biosynthesis and accumulation (Rienth et al., 2021), which may support our subsequent focus on this stage to identify firmness-related pathways and genes.

Metabolomic analysis revealed significant metabolic divergence between hard- and soft-fleshed sweet cherry cultivars during fruit development. A total of 41 stably differentially accumulated metabolites were identified, which were mainly enriched in six pathways, including flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavone and flavonol biosynthesis. Among them, flavonoid biosynthesis was the most prominently enriched pathway and constituted the potential core differential metabolic pathway, which is consistent with the metabolomic findings in peach cultivars differing in fruit firmness (Drincovich, 2021). In addition, studies on colorless fruit mutants of tomato have shown that flavonoids not only affect fruit coloration, but also integrate into the fruit cuticular network to modulate fruit mechanical firmness, which is consistent with the results of this study (Adato et al., 2009). Furthermore, similar findings have been reported in studies on hard- and soft-seeded pomegranates (Shi et al., 2022a). Flavonoids are ubiquitous secondary metabolites in plants, which not only form an important material basis for the formation of fruit color and flavor quality, but also participate in the regulation of multiple biological processes including plant cell wall development, hormone signal transduction, and stress response (Davies et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026). Flavonoids and lignin, which determines cell wall strength, share the upstream phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway. Recent studies have confirmed that flavonoids indirectly affect cell wall cross-linking, stiffening, and fruit softening by regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in plants (Huang et al., 2025); flavonoid metabolites can also influence fruit texture by regulating lignin biosynthesis and maintaining cell wall structural integrity (Zheng et al., 2025). Our results suggest that the sustained variation in the flavonoid metabolic pathway may serve as a key metabolic basis underlying the phenotypic differences between soft- and hard-fleshed sweet cherry cultivars, which potentially extends the understanding of flavonoid metabolism in modulating fruit firmness in stone fruits.

Flavanone 3-hydroxylase is a key rate-limiting enzyme in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, which catalyzes the C-3 hydroxylation of flavanones to convert the core precursor naringenin into dihydrokaempferol (DHK) (Shen et al., 2025). DHK is the direct precursor for flavonol biosynthesis, and F3H directly determines the metabolic flux toward the flavonol branch by controlling precursor supply (Han et al., 2017). It is therefore a key rate-limiting node for carbon flux partitioning in the flavonoid pathway, and directly regulates the accumulation of downstream flavonols, a finding consistent with similar reports in citrus (Wang et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2023). Through integrated KEGG pathway analysis of the transcriptome and metabolome, we mapped a total of 186 significantly DEGs to the six core differential metabolic pathways. Among these, the expression pattern of FUN_006793 (F3HL) in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway was highly synchronized with the accumulation trend of its pathway metabolite kaempferol: both showed significantly higher expression and accumulation in the hard-fleshed 'Wanhongzhu', but persistently low levels in the soft-fleshed 'Shangnan2808', which is consistent with the study of F3H in mulberries (Yang et al., 2021; Mo et al., 2022). WGCNA results further showed that FUN_006793 was enriched in the core module significantly positively correlated with fruit firmness, indicating this gene may be a potential key candidate regulator involved in sweet cherry fruit firmness (Ruan et al., 2022). Meanwhile, by integrating the phenotypic measurements of hard- and soft-fleshed fruits in Fig. 2C–I with metabolomic and transcriptomic data, we hypothesize that FUN_006793 may modulate the partitioning of precursor metabolic flux between flavonoid and lignin biosynthesis, thereby affecting cell wall structural integrity and consequently regulating fruit firmness. A positive correlation between flavonoid accumulation and fruit firmness in goji berries study further supports our findings (Huang et al., 2021). Previous studies in tomato, litchi, peach and other fruits have found that differential expression of flavonol synthase genes can affect fruit ripening and softening processes by regulating the accumulation of flavonols (Saladié et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2022b; Wang et al., 2025b). This study may provide novel evidence for the potential core role of FUN_006793 and the kaempferol (flavonoids) module in modulating sweet cherry fruit firmness.

To further mitigate the confounding effects of individual variation on metabolomic analysis and improve the stability and reliability of our findings, we constructed two extreme bulked pools (soft-fleshed and hard-fleshed) using individuals with extreme fruit firmness phenotypes selected from 322 plants of the F1 segregating population derived from the parental cross. Untargeted metabolomic analysis identified seven core metabolites with completely consistent differential trends between the parental lines and the extreme bulked pools, namely chlorogenic acid, (−)-epicatechin, trans-caffeic acid, jaceidin, L-glutamine, β-aminolevulinic acid hydrochloride, and AMP. Among these, chlorogenic acid, trans-caffeic acid, and (−)-epicatechin are all core intermediates of the phenylpropanoid-flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, and all showed significantly higher accumulation in the soft-fleshed parent and soft-fleshed bulked pool. The phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway is the core pathway of plant secondary metabolism, and the phenolic compounds it produces are not only direct precursors for lignin biosynthesis, but also participate in the regulation of cell wall cross-linking and structural stability (Ralph et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2021; Ninkuu et al., 2025). We found that the high accumulation of caffeic acid and chlorogenic acid in soft-fleshed fruits may originate from the degradation of lignin during fruit ripening. Both metabolites may participate in the lignin degradation process through the action of peroxidases (Naveed et al., 2018). Meanwhile, the synthetic competition between caffeic acid and quinic acid may shunt the precursor p-coumaroyl-CoA from the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, probably leading to reduced synthesis of kaempferol, which may be positively correlated with firmness in hard-fleshed fruits. These results further suggest a potential important role of the flavonoid-phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway in modulating sweet cherry fruit firmness. In addition, the seven core metabolites identified in this study may serve as potential metabolic biomarkers for sweet cherry fruit firmness traits, which may provide a promising reference for early molecular-assisted breeding of sweet cherry.

Above results hypothesize a potential regulatory model of sweet cherry fruit firmness variation mediated by the flavonoid pathway (Fig. 9) involving three core dimensions. First, kaempferol may enhance fruit mechanical strength and firmness by promoting ABA accumulation to improve peel thickness and epidermal integrity at the early stage of fruit development (Du et al., 2025), which is consistent with previous reports that ABA regulates fruit epidermal cuticle development (Martin et al., 2017; Romero and Lafuente, 2021). ABA may activate downstream MYB TFs through phosphorylation via the core PYR/PYL-PP2C-SnRK2 signaling pathway, directly upregulating the expression of key genes for cuticular wax and cutin biosynthesis, and driving cuticle biosynthesis and structural formation (Fujii et al., 2009; Cutler et al., 2010). Studies in tomato, apple, citrus and various other fruits have confirmed that ABA can directly regulate the deposition, structural remodeling and functional maintenance of the epidermal cuticle during fruit development and ripening (Gao et al., 2025). Second, kaempferol can attenuate auxin-mediated fruit softening effects by competitively inhibiting polar auxin transport, thereby maintaining the structural stability of mesocarp cells (Jacobs and Rubery, 1988; Peer and Murphy, 2007). Third, the accumulation of caffeic acid produced by lignin degradation in soft-fleshed fruits flows into the chlorogenic acid synthesis pathway, creating precursor competition with kaempferol biosynthesis. Meanwhile, lignin degradation directly leads to loose cell wall structure, ultimately accelerating fruit softening (Xu et al., 2026; Naveed et al., 2018). Exogenous GA treatment is often used to delay the ripening and softening process of fruits (Wu et al., 2024). GA is commonly applied to enhance cell wall elasticity and fruit firmness in fruits such as grape, litchi and pomegranate, so as to prevent fruit cracking or improve postharvest transportability (Tang et al., 2025). Notably, the association between DEGs in the GA related pathways and fruit firmness identified in this study remains controversial. Conflicting results have been reported regarding the effects of exogenous GA treatment on sweet cherry fruit firmness across different studies. Although integrated multi-omics analyses have been used to preliminarily explore the molecular basis associated with flavonoid pathway-related differences in sweet cherry fruit firmness in this study, several limitations still exist and require further investigation. First, We speculate that FUN_006793, which may function as a critical rate-limiting gene in the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway of sweet cherry, modulates fruit firmness by positively driving the biosynthesis of kaempferol. The accumulation of kaempferol may not only promotes ABA-mediated cuticle formation and epidermal integrity but also remodels phenylpropanoid and lignin metabolism to reinforce cell wall stability, which in turn determines epidermal thickness and mesocarp cell packing density. Consequently, the high expression of FUN_006793 probably elevates kaempferol content and preserves fruit firmness in hard-fleshed genotypes, whereas its suppressed expression probably reduces kaempferol accumulation and accelerates fruit softening in soft-fleshed cultivars. However, the regulatory relationship between the key candidate gene FUN_006793 and the core metabolite kaempferol identified in this study still requires functional validation through molecular biological approaches, including in vitro enzyme activity assays, dual-luciferase reporter assays, and gene overexpression and silencing. Second, the crosstalk regulatory network between flavonoid metabolism and hormone signaling pathways such as ABA and auxin has not yet been systematically dissected and validated. In addition, the association between the seven identified metabolic biomarkers and fruit firmness traits needs further stability validation in a larger natural population. Future studies will focus on the above aspects to further refine the molecular regulatory network governing sweet cherry fruit firmness formation, and provide a more solid theoretical basis for precision molecular breeding of sweet cherry targeting firmness traits.

Conclusion

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses across four developmental stages, combined with bulked metabolomic validation in an F1 segregating population, suggest that flavonoid biosynthesis may serve as the central metabolic pathway potentially determining fruit firmness divergence between soft- and hard-fleshed sweet cherry cultivars. The FUN_006793 (F3HL) shows a highly consistent expression pattern with kaempferol accumulation and is proposed as a potential key candidate regulator of fruit firmness, while epidermal thickness and mesocarp cell packing density may represent the major structural basis underlying firmness variation. In addition, seven stable metabolites are proposed as potential robust metabolic biomarkers associated with fruit firmness traits.

This study expands the regulatory framework of fruit firmness beyond the classic cell wall metabolism pathway and provides initial insights into the potential regulatory role of phenylpropanoid–flavonoid secondary metabolism in sweet cherry, thus offering novel theoretical references for understanding the molecular mechanism of texture formation in stone fruits. Practically, the identified candidate genes and metabolic biomarkers could serve as valuable potential molecular tools for marker-assisted selection, which may potentially accelerate the breeding of high-firmness, long-storability sweet cherry cultivars with superior commercial performance. Further investigations are required to conduct functional validation of the F3HL gene, systematically elucidate the crosstalk between flavonoid metabolism, fruit firmness and phytohormone signaling transduction, and perform large-scale population validation of metabolic biomarkers to further refine the regulatory mechanism underlying sweet cherry fruit firmness.
