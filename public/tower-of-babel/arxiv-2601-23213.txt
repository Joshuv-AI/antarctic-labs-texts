A complete characterisation of conditional entropies
Roberto Rubboli ∗,†,‡, Erkka Haapasalo ∗, Marco Tomamichel ∗,§

Abstract
Entropies are fundamental measures of uncertainty with central importance in information theory and statistics and applications across all the quantitative sciences. Under a natural set of operational axioms, the most general form of entropy is captured by the family of Rényi entropies, parameterized by a real number α \alpha . Conditional entropy extends the notion of entropy by quantifying uncertainty from the viewpoint of an observer with access to potentially correlated side information. However, despite their significance and the emergence of various useful definitions, a complete characterization of measures of conditional entropy that satisfy a natural set of operational axioms has remained elusive. In this work, we provide a complete characterization of conditional entropy, defined through a set of axioms that are essential for any operationally meaningful definition: additivity for independent random variables, invariance under relabeling, and monotonicity under conditional mixing channels. We prove that the most general form of conditional entropy is captured by a family of measures that are exponential averages of Rényi entropies of the conditioned distribution and parameterized by a real parameter and a probability measure on the positive reals. Finally, we show that these quantities determine the rate of transformation under conditional mixing and provide a set of second laws of quantum thermodynamics with side information for states diagonal in the energy eigenbasis.

Introduction

Entropies are measures of uncertainty about the outcome of a random experiment. Given a discrete random variable
X
on
\mathcal{X}
governed by a probability mass function (pmf)
P(x)
, its surprisal is the random variable
S(X)=\log\frac{1}{P(X)}
. The Shannon entropy
43
is its expectation

1

,

H(X):=\mathbb{E}[S]=\sum_{x}P(x)\log\frac{1}{P(x)}\,.

(1)

While Shannon entropy plays a central role in information processing by characterizing the ultimate performance limits for a range of tasks, most notably data compression
43
, it is evident that a more fine-grained specification of the surprisal is necessary to understand tail events. Notable examples are the min-entropy,
H_{\min}(X)=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}S(x)
, which plays a pivotal role in cryptography, and the Hartley entropy
22
,
H_{\max}(X)=\log\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{1}\{P(x)>0\}
. To fully characterize the distribution we consider the family of exponential expectations,

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]:=\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(tX)\big],

(2)

and observe that
\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X]
recovers the usual expectation and the limits
t\to\pm\infty
recover the maximal and minimal value in
X
can take, respectively. This function is closely related to the cumulant generation function (cgf) given by
K_{t}[X]=t\,\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]
.

Given this, the distribution of the surprisal
S
is conveniently characterised in terms of the family of Rényi entropies
39
with parameter
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
as

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(X):=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\sum_{x}P(x)^{\alpha}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha}[S]\,,

(3)

The Hartley, Shannon, and min-entropy emerge as point-wise limits when
\alpha\to\{0,1,\infty\}
, respectively.
The cgf and Rényi entropies arise naturally whenever tails of the distribution of the surprisal are relevant, e.g., in the large deviation regime of source coding, when investigating error or strong converse exponents, and in one-shot information theory.

Their ubiquity in information theory leads to the question of whether Rényi entropies are complete under some set of well-motivated information-theoretic axioms. Axiomatic approaches to entropy have a rich history, surveyed in the books by Aczél-Daróczy
1
and Ebanks-Sahoo-Sander
12
, as well as the review by Csiszár
10
. All these approaches have in common that at least one of the axioms is chosen for mathematical convenience and does not have an operational justification. As such, they fail to fully explain the role Rényi entropies play in information theory.

In contrast, recent work
19
proposes a set of axioms that
any operationally meaningful measure of entropy should satisfy: monotonicity under doubly stochastic channels, invariance under embedding, additivity for independent variables, and normalization. We shall soon explain the meaning of these concepts.
Any quantity
\mathbb{H}(X)
satisfying these axioms can be expressed as a convex combination of Rényi entropies
35
;
19
.

2

More precisely, a one-to-one relationship between entropy and relative entropy is established in
19
and
35
gives a complete characterization of relative entropy. The statement is also a special case of our main theorem.
Namely,

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(X)=\int_{[0,\infty]}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\,H_{\alpha}(X)

(4)

for some probability measure
\mu
on the extended positive reals. Our goal here is to derive a similar complete characterization for conditional entropy.

Conditional entropies extend the notion of entropy to settings where additional information, known as side information, is available, quantifying uncertainty from the perspective of an observer with access to it.
Consider thus two discrete random variables
X
on
\mathcal{X}
and
Y
on
\mathcal{Y}
governed by a joint pmf
P(x,y)=P(x|y)P(y)
that we decomposed in terms of the conditional pmf
P(x|y)
and marginal
P(y)
. We define the conditional surprisal given an event
Y=y
was observed as
S_{y}(X)=\log\frac{1}{P(X|y)}
for each
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Noting that
H(X|Y\!=\!y):=\mathbb{E}[S_{y}]
is a random variable once we vary
y
, the conditional Shannon entropy of
X
given
Y
is then expressed as a total expectation,

\displaystyle H(X|Y):=\sum_{x,y}P(x,y)\log\frac{1}{P(x|y)}=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[S_{Y}]\big]\,,

(5)

Several definitions of conditional Rényi entropies have been proposed and found operational significance (see, e.g.,
47
and
14
for reviews that cover some aspects of conditional entropy). We introduce some of the most widely used definitions here, and will later show that they are all special cases of a general class. Two prominent examples are
H_{\alpha}^{H}
, due to Hayashi and Skorić
et al.

25
;
44
, and
H_{\alpha}^{A}
, due to Arimoto
2
. They can be expressed as exponential expectations of the entropies
H_{\alpha}(X|Y\!=\!y):=\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha}[S_{y}]
, i.e., as a total exponential expectation

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{H}(X|Y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\bigg(\sum_{x,y}P(y)P(x|y)^{\alpha}\bigg)=\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha}\big[\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha}[S_{Y}]\big]\,,

(6)

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}^{A}(X|Y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log{\bigg(\sum_{y}P(y)\bigg(\sum_{x}P(x|y)^{\alpha}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\bigg)}=\mathbb{E}_{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\big[\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha}[S_{Y}]\big]\,.

(7)

Convex combinations of these definitions have been considered in
24
.
A two-parameter family interpolating between them has been introduced by Hayashi and Tan
23
:

H_{\alpha,\beta}(X|Y):=\mathbb{E}_{\beta(1-\alpha)/\alpha}\big[\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha}[S_{Y}]\big]
.
As part of a study of quantum conditional entropy, its properties were studied in detail by some of the present authors
41
.
This quantity possesses desirable properties if
(\alpha,\beta)
are either in
(0,1)^{\times 2}
or
(1,\infty)^{\times 2}
and reduce to
H_{\alpha}^{H}
and
H_{\alpha}^{A}
for
\beta=\alpha
and
\beta=1
, respectively. The fact that these quantities have desirable properties for a large range of parameters shows that
\alpha
and
\beta
can be chosen largely independently.

In another line of work, Cachin
9
and Renner and Wolf
38
introduced the conditional entropies, which can see as limiting cases of the above family:

\displaystyle H^{C}_{\alpha}(X|Y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}P(y)H_{\alpha}(X|Y=y)=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha}[S_{Y}]]\,\quad\textrm{and}

(8)

\displaystyle H^{R}_{\alpha}(X|Y)

\displaystyle=\max_{y}H_{\alpha}(X|Y=y)=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha}]]\,.

(9)

for
\alpha<1
and with
\max
replaced by
\min
for
\alpha>1
.

Another two-parameter conditional entropy was proposed by Tan and Hayashi
45
. It can be written as

H_{\alpha,\beta}^{T}(X|Y)=\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha,(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)/\beta}[\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha}[S_{Y}],\,\mathbb{E}_{1-\beta}[S_{Y}]]
, where we introduced a two-variable exponential expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t_{1},t_{2}}[X_{1},X_{2}]:=\frac{1}{t_{1}+t_{2}}\log\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(t_{1}X_{1}+t_{2}X_{2})\big]\,.

(10)

This indicates an even larger parameter space of operationally significant conditional entropies.

Indeed, in this work, we consider a very general form of conditional entropy (that we later prove to be the most general form) that is defined in terms of an exponential average of a continuous set of random variables. For any probability measure
\tau
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we define

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,\tau}[\alpha\mapsto X_{\alpha}]=\frac{1}{t}\log\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\exp\Bigg(t\int_{[0,\infty]}X_{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\Bigg)\Bigg]\,,

(11)

which allows us to finally introduce our quantity of interest,

\displaystyle H_{t,\tau}(X|Y)=\mathbb{E}_{t,\tau}\big[\alpha\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{1-\alpha}[S_{Y}]\big]=\frac{1}{t}\log\sum_{y}P(y)\exp\bigg(t\int_{[0,\infty]}H_{\alpha}(X|Y\!=\!y)\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\bigg).

(12)

All the entropies listed above arise as special cases of this general conditional entropy for suitable choices of the parameters (see Section
7.3
for more details).

An axiomatic approach.

We consider the following four axioms (see also
18
;
20
), which are motivated by our desire to interpret conditional entropy as a measure of uncertainty from the perspective of an observer with side information. Below, we view any joint probabilities
P
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
as matrices with rows and columns labeled by
x
and
y
, respectively, i.e.,

(P)_{x,y}=P(x,y)
.

1.

Invariance under relabeling.
We require that relabeling outcomes or embedding them into a larger alphabet will not change the conditional entropy.

Formally, consider two injective functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
and
g:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
and
Q
with
Q\big(f(x),g(y)\big)=P(x,y)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}
and zero elsewhere. We require that

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(X|Y)_{P}=\mathbb{H}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}\,.

(13)

2.

Monotonicity under conditional mixing.
We expect that the entropy is non-decreasing when we apply a mixing (doubly stochastic) channel acting on
X
. For example, if
X
is the value of the top card of a deck, shuffling (i.e., applying a more or less random permutation) should never decrease the uncertainty of
X
from the perspective of an observer.
Moreover, we also want to account for conditional operations where the mixing channel depends on the side information. This accounts for the possibility that an observer is correlated with the randomness used in the mixing channel, e.g., the observer might know which shuffling technique is used, reducing the randomness introduced in the process compared to an observer without such information. Such conditional mixing channels can signal from
Y
to
X
but do not leak information about
X
to the observer, and thus ought not reduce uncertainty.
Finally, we require monotonicity under arbitrary channels applied to the side information
Y
since local processing of the side information can never reduce our uncertainty of
X
.
The closure under composition of such operations gives us conditionally mixing channels.

When representing a joint probability mass function as a matrix, doubly stochastic channels acting on the system
X
can be described by right multiplication with a doubly stochastic matrix
S
. In addition, channels acting on the side information correspond to right multiplication by a row-stochastic matrix
D
. Finally, conditional operations can be represented by a family of row sub-stochastic matrices
D_{i}
acting on the right, whose sum
\sum_{i}D_{i}
is row-stochastic, together with a corresponding family of doubly stochastic matrices
S_{i}
acting on the left (see
18
for more details).
Explicitly, conditionally mixing channels from
m\times n
matrices
P
to
m\times n^{\prime}
matrices
Q
act as

\displaystyle P\mapsto Q=\sum_{i=1}^{k}S_{i}PD_{i}\,,

(14)

where
S_{i}
are doubly stochastic
m\times m
matrices for all
i
and
D_{i}
are sub-stochastic
n\times n^{\prime}
matrices such that their sum
\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}
is row-stochastic. We require that for any conditionally mixing channel, any
P
, and
Q
as defined in (
14
), it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(X|Y)_{P}\leq\mathbb{H}(X|Y^{\prime})_{Q}\,.

(15)

3.

Additivity.
We require that entropies for independent random variables are additive. This axiom expresses the principle that the uncertainty of two independent random variables is the sum of their individual uncertainties, and it admits a natural extension to scenarios with side information.

Formally, for any joint pmf
P
and
Q
, we require that their Kronecker product,
P\otimes Q
, satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(XX^{\prime}|YY^{\prime})_{P\otimes Q}=\mathbb{H}(X|Y)_{P}+\mathbb{H}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}\,.

(16)

4.

Normalization
: We fix the entropy of a fair coin toss to be
1
bit, i.e., we set
\mathbb{H}(X)_{P}=1
for
P=(1/2,1/2)^{T}
.

In this work, we prove that any conditional entropy that satisfies these four properties is given by a convex combination of conditional entropies
H_{t,\tau}
defined in (
12
) and their pointwise limits (see Theorem
7.1
for more details).
While this result determines the most general form of a conditional entropy, some choices of the parameters
t
and
\tau
should be excluded if the resulting
H_{t,\tau}
does not satisfy all required axioms.
Specifically, we derive a set of sufficient conditions on the parameters
t
and
\tau
under which
H_{t,\tau}
satisfies all axioms.
These conditions are as follows:

1.

t>0
,
\;\text{supp}(\tau)\subseteq[0,1]
, and

\int_{[0,+\infty]}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\leq\frac{1}{t}

(17)

2.

t\leq 0
and

(a)

\;\text{supp}(\tau)\subseteq[0,1]
or

(b)

\;\operatorname{supp}(\tau)\subseteq[0,1]\cup\{\alpha_{*}\}
for some
\;\alpha_{*}\in(1,+\infty]
so that also (
17
) is satisfied.

In addition, we show that these conditions are also necessary whenever the measure
\tau
is discrete and supported on a finite number of points, or more generally when it satisfies a regularity assumption (see Section
7
for details).
Our analysis further leads us to conjecture that the same conditions remain necessary for a general measure
\tau
.

Applications.

Majorization is a well-studied concept with applications across many fields, including probability theory and statistics
33
, mathematics
4
, and quantum information
37
;
26
. Conditional majorization provides a natural extension of classical majorization to scenarios involving side information. Recalling the definitions introduced above, given two joint probability mass functions
P
and
Q
, we say that
P
conditionally majorizes
Q
if there exists a conditionally mixing channel that maps
P
to
Q
. When the underlying dimensions of the distributions do not coincide, suitable embeddings are implicitly understood (see Definition
2.9
).
Conditional majorization has found applications in areas such as conditional uncertainty relations in quantum information
18
and the study of games of chance
7
.
In this work, we provide an operational interpretation of the newly defined conditional entropies
H_{t,\tau}
as the quantities that determine the optimal rate
R
for the multi-copy transformation
P_{XY}^{\otimes n}\;\longrightarrow\;Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}^{\otimes Rn}

under conditional majorization, for a sufficiently large number of copies
n
. Specifically, we show that

R(P_{XY}\rightarrow Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}})=\inf\left\{\frac{H_{t,\tau}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}}{H_{t,\tau}(X|Y)_{P}}\Bigg|(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm{bulk}}\right\}\cup\left\{\frac{H_{-\infty,\tau}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}}{H_{-\infty,\tau}(X|Y)_{P}}\Bigg|\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}\right\}.

(18)

Here,
\mathcal{D}_{\rm bulk}
denotes the set of parameters for which
H_{t,\tau}
is monotone under conditionally mixing channels, while
\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}
denotes the corresponding set of parameters for which the limiting entropy
H_{-\infty,\tau}
, obtained as
t\to-\infty
, satisfies the same monotonicity property.
This establishes a direct link between conditional entropies and asymptotic transformation rates.

Our final result addresses transformations with a conditionally preserved probability distribution. We derive sufficient conditions for the existence of such a conditionally preserving map that, given an input distribution together with another probability distribution acting as a catalyst, produces an output distribution arbitrarily close to the desired target, while leaving the catalyst unchanged at the end of the transformation (see Theorem
8.3
).
We further apply this result to derive a set of second laws of thermodynamics in the presence of side information, a setting that has been investigated in several works
42
;
34
;
40
;
3
;
30
;
36
.
In the absence of side information, a complete set of necessary and sufficient conditions for transforming states that are diagonal in the energy eigenbasis under thermal operations—assisted by a catalyst that is returned unchanged at the end of the process—was established in
6
.
These conditions are commonly referred to as the second laws of quantum thermodynamics. In the absence of side information, these laws can be expressed in terms of certain free energies, which are connected to the Rényi relative entropies between a state and its Gibbs state.
In our work, we find that for states that are diagonal in the energy eigenbasis, certain quantities, denoted by
F_{t,\tau}
, can be used to provide a set of sufficient conditions for transformations under conditioned thermal operations assisted by a catalyst. In particular,

F_{t,\tau}
can be constructed from
H_{t,\tau}
in (
12
) by replacing the entropies in the exponential expectation with the Rényi relative entropies between the state and the Gibbs state. These quantities provide a set of sufficient conditions for state transformations under conditioned thermal operations assisted by a catalyst (see Corollary
8.4
for more details).
This partially solves the open problem posed in
(
30
, Section V.B)
.
This result shows that even in the classical case, the presence of side information significantly complicates the second laws. Nevertheless, for the specific case we consider, the problem admits a partial solution, whereas the fully quantum case remains an open challenge, even in the absence of side information.

Methods.

To derive the general form of conditional entropy, we establish sufficient conditions under which, in the limit of a large number of copies, one joint distribution can be transformed into another joint distribution using conditionally mixing channels. This is because, by known arguments
35
(see also
21
for a general statement), this problem can be shown to be equivalent to the axiomatic characterization.
To derive these conditions, we apply the general results on preordered semirings developed in
17
;
16
.
In particular, we construct a specific semiring where the conditional entropies
H_{t,\tau}
arise as the relevant functions of the theory.

The remainder of this manuscript is structured as follows: In Section
2
, we introduce the formal definitions adopted in the manuscript. In Subsection
2.3
, we derive the general form of entropies as convex combinations of Rényi entropies an important result often subsequently needed. In Section
3
, we define the semiring of conditional majorization and determine the general form of the extremal conditional entropies
H_{t,\tau}
. In Sections
4
and
5
, we establish sufficient and necessary conditions on the parameters
t
and
\tau
for which the conditional entropies
H_{t,\tau}
satisfy the required axioms. In Section
6
, we derive large-sample and catalytic results that form the basis for our applications. In Section
7
, we prove the general form of conditional entropies, showing that they are convex combinations of the (extremal) entropies
H_{t,\tau}
. In Section
8
, we discuss applications to rates and the second laws of quantum thermodynamics with side information.

2
Definitions and preliminary results

This section provides the formal definitions that serve as the foundation for the remainder of the manuscript. We also present and prove an exhaustive characterization of unconditional entropies serving as a stepping stone towards the characterization of conditional entropies.

2.1
Definitions relevant for entropies

We denote the (always finite) set of possible values of a random variable
X
by
\mathcal{X}
. The probability mass function of
X
is denoted by
P_{X}
and often if there is no risk of confusion, we may drop the subscript. To avoid confusion, we also use other names for the probability mass functions like
Q_{X}
,
R_{X}
, and the like. We use similar notational conventions for other random variables like
Y
,
Z
, and so forth. We also usually call probability mass functions as probability distributions for brevity.

Definition 2.1

(Embedding and relabeling of probability distributions)
.

Given two probability distributions
P_{X}
and
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}}
, we say that
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}}
is obtained from
P_{X}
through embedding into a larger space and relabeling of outcomes if there exists an injective function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
such that
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}}(f(x))=P_{X}(x)

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}

and zero elsewhere.

Definition 2.2

(Doubly stochastic channel)
.

A channel is called
doubly stochastic
(or
mixing
) if, when acting on a probability vector, it is represented by a matrix with nonnegative entries whose rows and columns each sum to one.

Definition 2.3

(Majorization)
.

Given two probability distributions
P_{X}
and
Q_{X^{\prime}}
, we say that
P_{X}
majorizes
Q_{X^{\prime}}
, written as

P_{X}\succeq Q_{X^{\prime}}

(19)

if there exist probability distributions
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}}
and
\widetilde{Q}_{\widetilde{X}^{\prime}}
, obtained from
P_{X}
and
Q_{X^{\prime}}
via embedding and relabeling, and a doubly stochastic map
D
such that
D\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}}=\widetilde{Q}_{\widetilde{X}^{\prime}}
.

Remark 2.4
.

It is known that it suffices to consider embeddings into a space whose dimension equals the maximum of the supports of the input and output probability distributions. Accordingly, in the following, we assume that the probability distributions
P_{X}
and
Q_{X}
are defined on a common underlying space.

It is well known that the existence of a doubly stochastic map is equivalent to the following condition on the entries of the probability distributions, often referred to as the initial sum condition
5
(see also
33
).

Lemma 2.5

(Initial sum condition)
.

Let
P_{X}
and
Q_{X}
be two
d
-dimensional vectors with nonnegative entries. Moreover, let
P_{X}^{\downarrow}
and
Q_{X}^{\downarrow}
denote the vectors obtained by rearranging the components of
P_{X}
and
Q_{X}
, respectively, in non-increasing order. Then
P_{X}\succeq Q_{X}
if and only if

\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{X}^{\downarrow}(i)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}Q_{X}^{\downarrow}(i)\qquad\text{for all }k=1,\ldots,d-1,

(20)

and equality holds for
k=d
, that is,
\sum_{i=1}^{d}P_{X}^{\downarrow}(i)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}Q_{X}^{\downarrow}(i)
.

Definition 2.6

(Entropy)
.

Let us denote, for any finite random variable
X
, the set of probability distributions on
X
by
\mathcal{P}_{X}
. A function

\mathbb{H}:\bigcup_{X}\mathcal{P}_{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}

(21)

is called entropy if it satisfies the following postulates for all systems
X
,
X^{\prime}
, and probability vectors
P_{X}
and
Q_{X^{\prime}}
:

1.

Invariance under relabeling and embedding: If
P_{X}
can be relabeled and embedded into
Q_{X^{\prime}}
, then
\mathbb{H}(X)_{P}=\mathbb{H}(X^{\prime})_{Q}
.

2.

Monotonicity under mixing channels: if
Q_{X^{\prime}}
is obtained from
P_{X}
using a mixing channel, then
\mathbb{H}(X)_{P}\leq\mathbb{H}(X^{\prime})_{Q}
.

3.

Additivity:
\mathbb{H}(XX^{\prime})_{P\otimes Q}=\mathbb{H}(X)_{P}+\mathbb{H}(X^{\prime})_{Q}

4.

Normalization:
\mathbb{H}(X)_{(1/2,1/2)}=1

Note that the first two properties imply that if
P_{X}\succeq Q_{X^{\prime}}
(i.e.,
P_{X}
majorizes
Q_{X^{\prime}}
), then
\mathbb{H}(X)_{P}\leq\mathbb{H}(X^{\prime})_{Q}
. In other words, any entropy is monotone under majorization. In the following, we will
also often use the notation
\mathbb{H}(X)_{P}=\mathbb{H}(P_{X})
. Taking into account condition
1
above, we may effectively consider an entropy
\mathbb{H}
as a map on the union of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
over
n\in\mathbb{N}
where
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the set of probability distributions over
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

2.2
Definitions relevant for conditional entropies

In the sequel, we shall discuss joint probability distributions
P_{XY}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
. We may often again drop the subscripts.
The marginals of
P_{XY}
are denoted by
P_{X}
and
P_{Y}
, and are defined as

P_{X}(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{XY}(x,y),\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\qquad P_{Y}(y)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P_{XY}(x,y),\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}.

(22)

We will always identify a joint probability distribution
P_{XY}
with the matrix

P_{XY}=\big(P_{XY}(x,y)\big)_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}.

(23)

This makes many of our future notation and definitions, including the next one, simpler.

Definition 2.7

(Conditionally mixing channel)
.

A
conditionally mixing channel
is a channel of the form

P_{XY}\longmapsto Q_{XY^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}S^{(i)}P_{XY}D^{(i)},

(24)

where the following conditions hold:

1.

The matrices
S^{(i)}
are doubly stochastic for all
i=1,\dots,k
.

2.

The matrices
D^{(i)}
are sub-stochastic for all
i=1,\ldots,k
.

3.

The sum
\sum_{i=1}^{k}D^{(i)}
is row-stochastic.

For clarity of notation and to facilitate the understanding of matrix dimensions in the arguments that follow, we note that if
Q_{XY^{\prime}}
is a
d\times n^{\prime}
matrix and
P_{XY}
is a
d\times n
matrix, then the matrices
S^{(i)}
have dimensions
d\times d
, while the selection matrices
D^{(i)}
have dimensions
n\times n^{\prime}
.

Definition 2.8

(Embedding and relabeling of joint probability distributions)
.

Given two joint probability distributions
P_{XY}
and
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}\widetilde{Y}}
, we say that
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}\widetilde{Y}}
is obtained from
P_{XY}
through embedding into a larger space and relabeling of outcomes if there exist two injective functions

f:\mathcal{X}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{X}}
and
g:\mathcal{Y}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{Y}}
such that
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}\widetilde{Y}}(f(x),g(y))=P_{XY}\big(x,y\big)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and zero elsewhere.

Definition 2.9

(Conditional majorization)
.

Given two probability distributions
P=P_{XY}
and
Q=Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
, we say that
P_{XY}
conditionally majorizes
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
, and write

P_{XY}\succeq Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}

(25)

if there exist probability distributions
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}\widetilde{Y}}
and
\widetilde{Q}_{\widetilde{X}^{\prime}\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}}
, obtained from
P_{XY}
and
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
via embedding and relabeling, and a conditionally mixing channel that maps
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}\widetilde{Y}}
into
\widetilde{Q}_{\widetilde{X}^{\prime}\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}}
.
This means that, up to relabeling and embedding,
P_{XY}
can be transformed into
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
using a conditional mixing channel.

Remark 2.10
.

We observe that when considering transformations between two probability distributions
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY^{\prime}}
that have the same number of rows, no relabeling or embedding beyond the conditionally mixing channel is required. In other words, if
P_{XY}\succeq Q_{XY^{\prime}}
according to Definition
2.9
, then there exists a conditionally mixing channel that maps
P_{XY}
to
Q_{XY^{\prime}}
.

To see this, consider embeddings
P_{\widetilde{X}Y}
and
Q_{\widetilde{X}Y^{\prime}}
obtained by padding
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY^{\prime}}
with additional rows of zeros. Since the sum of positive elements can vanish only if all elements are zero, it follows that the rows with index
j>|\mathcal{X}|
of
S^{(i)}P_{\widetilde{X}Y}R^{(i)}

must be zero. Moreover,
P_{\widetilde{X}Y}R^{(i)}
acts solely on the columns, leaving the additional rows
j>|\mathcal{X}|
unchanged at zero. By Birkhoff’s theorem, each
S^{(i)}
can be expressed as a convex combination of permutations. Any permutation that acts only on the zero elements can be replaced by the identity, while permutations transferring mass from
j\leq|\mathcal{X}|
to
j>|\mathcal{X}|
are forbidden since the latter rows must remain zero. Therefore, it suffices to consider only permutations acting within the subspace spanned by rows
j\leq|\mathcal{X}|
.

Definition 2.11

(Conditional entropy)
.

For any finite random variables
X
and
Y
, we denote the set of probability distributions
P_{XY}
by
\mathcal{P}_{XY}
. A function

\mathbb{H}:\bigcup_{X,Y}\mathcal{P}_{XY}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}

(26)

is called a conditional entropy if it satisfies the following postulates for all systems
X
,
Y
,
X^{\prime}
,
Y^{\prime}
, and joint probability distributions
P_{XY}
and
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
:

1.

Invariance under relabeling and embedding: if
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
is obtained from
P_{XY}
through relabeling and embedding, then
\mathbb{H}(X|Y)_{P}=\mathbb{H}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}
.

2.

Monotonicity under conditionally mixing channels: if
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
is obtained from
P_{XY}
using a conditionally mixing channel, then
\mathbb{H}(X|Y)_{P}\leq\mathbb{H}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}.

3.

Additivity:
\mathbb{H}(XX^{\prime}|YY^{\prime})_{P\otimes Q}=\mathbb{H}(X|Y)_{P}+\mathbb{H}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}
.

4.

Normalization:
\mathbb{H}(X|Y)_{(1/2,1/2)\otimes Q_{Y}}=1

Note that the first two properties imply that if
P_{XY}\succeq Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
(i.e.,
P_{XY}
conditionally majorizes
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
), then
\mathbb{H}(X|Y)_{P}\leq\mathbb{H}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}
. In other words, any conditional entropy is monotone under conditional majorization. In the following, we will
also often use the notation
\mathbb{H}(X|Y)_{P}=\mathbb{H}(P_{XY})
.

2.3
The set of entropies

In this section, we derive the general form of an entropy, showing that it can be written as a convex combination of Rényi entropies. We also refer to this representation as the barycentric decomposition of entropy.
This result builds on
(
29
, Proposition 3.7)
, which establishes sufficient conditions for transforming multiple copies of one probability distribution into another under doubly stochastic maps. We refer to this as a large-sample result for entropy. We then
follow the proof strategy of
(
35
, Theorem 2)
, which uses a compact set of conditions characterizing large-sample transformations involving a set of monotones to establish barycentric decompositions based on those same monotones. This approach is quite general, as emphasized in
21
. In the present setting, the monotones coincide with the Rényi entropies.

Lemma 2.12

(
(
29
, Proposition 3.7)
)
.

Let
P_{X},Q_{X}
be two probability distributions and assume that

H_{\alpha}(P_{X})<H_{\alpha}(Q_{X})\quad\forall\alpha\in[0,+\infty]\,.

(27)

Then, for sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a doubly stochastic matrix
S_{n}
such that

S_{n}P_{X}^{\otimes n}=Q_{X}^{\otimes n}
.

The latter result implies a barycentric decomposition for entropies as we state next.
In particular, this yields a barycentric decomposition with respect to a positive measure, which must be normalized if the additional normalization axiom is imposed (see Proposition
2.13
below).
Notably, this result will subsequently serve as a key ingredient in deriving the general form of a conditional entropy. Note that, as we discuss in Remark
2.14
, only the Rényi entropies with

\alpha\geq 0
appear in the decomposition. Indeed, the entropies with
\alpha<0
fail to satisfy the invariance under relabeling and embedding axiom.

Proposition 2.13

(General form of entropies)
.

Let
\mathbb{H}
be an entropy according to Definition
2.6
. Then, there exists a probability measure
\mu:\mathcal{B}\big([0,+\infty]\big)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for all probability distributions
P_{X}

\mathbb{H}(P_{X})=\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X})\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\,.

(28)

Furthermore, if the normalization axiom
4
is not assumed, the conclusion remains valid upon replacing the probability measure with a finite positive measure.

Proof.

The result follows from Lemma
2.12
; the proof is essentially the same as that of
(
35
, Theorem 2)
and this result can be seen as a special case of Theorem 7 of
21
. We give an outline of this proof for completeness. This same proof also works for Theorem
7.1
later in this work with slight adaptations.

The first step is to show that
H_{\alpha}(P_{X})\leq H_{\alpha}(Q_{X})
for all
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
implies
\mathbb{H}(P_{X})\leq\mathbb{H}(Q_{X})
. First, let us assume that
H_{\alpha}(P_{X})<H_{\alpha}(Q_{X})
for all
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
. According to Lemma
2.12
, this means that, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large, there is a doubly stochastic matrix
S_{n}
such that
S_{n}P_{X}^{\otimes n}=Q_{X}^{\otimes n}
. Thus, since
\mathbb{H}
is monotone and additive,

n\mathbb{H}(P_{X})=\mathbb{H}(P_{X}^{\otimes n})\leq\mathbb{H}(Q_{X}^{\otimes n})=n\mathbb{H}(Q_{X}),

(29)

i.e.,
\mathbb{H}(P_{X})\leq\mathbb{H}(Q_{X})
. Let us then assume that
H_{\alpha}(P_{X})\leq H_{\alpha}(Q_{X})
for all
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
. Denoting by
R
the two-outcome uniform distribution
(1/2,1/2)
, it follows that

H_{\alpha}(P_{X}^{\otimes m})=mH_{\alpha}(P_{X})\leq mH_{\alpha}(Q_{X})<mH_{\alpha}(Q_{X})+1=H_{\alpha}(Q_{X}^{\otimes m}\otimes R\big)

(30)

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
. Thus, according our initial step, we also have

m\mathbb{H}(P_{X})=\mathbb{H}(P_{X}^{\otimes m})\leq\mathbb{H}(Q_{X}^{\otimes m}\otimes R)=m\mathbb{H}(Q_{X})+1

(31)

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. This means
\mathbb{H}(P_{X})\leq\mathbb{H}(Q_{X})+1/m
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, so that
\mathbb{H}(P_{X})\leq\mathbb{H}(Q_{X})
, and the initial result is proven.

For the details of the rest of the proof, we refer to the proof of Theorem 7 of
21
and that of Theorem 2 of
35
. The strategy is to define a positive linear functional on the set
C\big([0,\infty]\big)
of continuous real functions on the compact set
[0,\infty]
using the initial step proven above and then use the representation result for such functionals.

Consider the evaluation functions
{\rm ev}_{P}\in C\big([0,\infty]\big)
,
{\rm ev}_{P}(\alpha)=H_{\alpha}(P)
at any finite probability distribution
P
and all
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
. By ‘evaluation’ we essentially mean that we actually identify
[0,\infty]
with the set of Rényi entropies
H_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
. This kind of identification has to be done in the general case of Theorem 7 of
21
where the situation is such that large-sample ordering conditions are given by a compact set of functions which is Hausdorff. This is also the case of Theorem
7.1
later on. It can be shown very easily, using our initial step, that we may set up a well-defined positive functional
F
on the positive cone generated by the evaluation functions in
C\big([0,\infty]\big)
through
F({\rm ev}_{P})=\mathbb{H}(P)
. For the well-definedness, note that if
P
and
Q
are such that
{\rm ev}_{P}={\rm ev}_{Q}
, i.e.,
H_{\alpha}(P)=H_{\alpha}(Q)
for all
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
, then, according to our initial result, also
\mathbb{H}(P)=\mathbb{H}(Q)
. The positivity of
F
follows similarly. With some extra work, we may show that
F
extends into a positive functional
G
on the vector space
V
spanned by the evaluation functions.

Suppose that
f\in C\big([0,\infty]\big)
and fix any
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
n\geq\|f\|_{\infty}
. Denoting by
1
the constant function 1 on
[0,\infty]
and by
R
the two-outcome uniform distribution, we have

f\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\,1\leq n\,1=n\,{\rm ev}_{R}={\rm ev}_{R^{\otimes n}},

(32)

so that all functions in
C\big([0,\infty]\big)
are upper bounded by functions from
V
. Now a theorem due to Kantorovich
31
related to the Hahn-Banach theorem implies that
G
extends into a positive linear functional
I:C\big([0,\infty]\big)\to\mathbb{R}
. Due to the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani theorem, there is a finite positive measure
\mu:\mathcal{B}\big([0,\infty]\big)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
(the Borel
\sigma
-algebra associated with the one-point compactification of the natural topology of
[0,\infty)
) such that

I(f)=\int_{[0,\infty]}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu,\qquad\forall f\in C\big([0,\infty]\big).

(33)

This means

\mathbb{H}(P)=F({\rm ev}_{P})=I({\rm ev}_{P})=\int_{[0,\infty]}{\rm ev}_{P}(\alpha)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)=\int_{[0,\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha).

(34)

Denoting again by
R
the two-outcome uniform distribution, we have

1=\mathbb{H}(R)=\int_{[0,\infty]}\underbrace{H_{\alpha}(R)}_{=1}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)=\mu\big([0,\infty]\big),

(35)

i.e.,
\mu
is a probability measure.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

One might wonder why Rényi entropies for
\alpha<0
are excluded from Proposition
2.13
. The reason lies in the requirement of invariance under isometries stated in Definition
2.6
. Intuitively, when a full-rank probability distribution is embedded into a larger space—effectively appending zeros to the distribution—the Rényi entropy for
\alpha<0
diverges, since raising zero to a negative power yields infinity. As a result, the entropy changes from a finite to an infinite value under such an embedding, violating the isometric invariance axiom
1
.

To formalize this argument, suppose there exists an entropy function that coincides with the Rényi entropy of order
\alpha<0
on full-rank probability distributions, is invariant under embeddings, and satisfies monotonicity under doubly stochastic maps—namely,
H_{\alpha}(X^{\prime})_{P}\geq H_{\alpha}(X^{\prime})_{Q}
whenever
Q_{X^{\prime}}=SP_{X^{\prime}}
for some doubly stochastic matrix
S
. For
\alpha<0
, such monotonicity is expected due to the Schur concavity of the function
\left(\sum_{i}x_{i}^{\alpha}\right)^{1/\alpha}
. In particular, for negative orders
\alpha<0
, the entropies
H_{\alpha}
are expected to be monotone non-increasing, rather than non-decreasing, under doubly stochastic maps.
Let us define

\displaystyle P_{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad P_{X^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad S=\begin{pmatrix}1-\varepsilon&0&\varepsilon\\
0&1&0\\
\varepsilon&0&1-\varepsilon\\
\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad Q_{X^{\prime}}=SP_{X^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1-\varepsilon\\
1\\
\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\,.

(36)

Here,
P_{X^{\prime}}
represents
P_{X}
embedded into a larger space, and
Q_{X^{\prime}}
is the output of the doubly stochastic map
S
applied to
P_{X}^{\prime}
. Then, invariance under isometries and monotonicity under a doubly stochastic map would imply the chain of inequalities

\displaystyle 1=H_{\alpha}(X)_{P}=H_{\alpha}(X^{\prime})_{P}\geq H_{\alpha}(X^{\prime})_{Q}=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log{\left(\frac{1}{2^{\alpha}}\left((1-\varepsilon)^{\alpha}+1+\varepsilon^{\alpha}\right)\right)}\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}+\infty.

(37)

However, since
1\neq+\infty
, this leads to a contradiction. Therefore, no entropy function can simultaneously coincide with the Rényi entropy of order
\alpha<0
on full-rank probability distributions, be invariant under embeddings, and satisfy monotonicity under doubly stochastic maps.

3
The semiring of conditional majorization

In this section, we introduce the main technical tool used to establish sufficient conditions for multiple-copy transformation (large sample result) that lead to the general form of conditional entropies (barycentric decomposition). Our approach relies on the real-algebraic theory of preordered semirings developed in
17
;
16
. This framework is particularly powerful because it provides a large sample theorem (Theorem
3.1
) whenever one can endow the underlying problem with a suitable semiring structure.
The large sample comparison theorem provides a characterization of the sufficient conditions under which multiple copies of one element of the semiring can be transformed into multiple copies of another.
In our setting, the semiring is essentially the set of joint probability distributions
P_{XY}
, and the large-sample theorem yields necessary and sufficient conditions under which multiple copies of a joint distribution
P_{XY}
can be transformed into multiple copies of another joint distribution
Q_{XY}
under conditionally mixing operations in terms of the so-called monotone homomorphisms (see later for the definition) of the theory. In the case of conditional entropies, the monotone homomorphisms and derivations are exactly the extremal conditional entropies (up to a constant and a logarithm). These are the conditional entropies in terms of which any general conditional entropy can be expressed as a convex combination. From here, a standard argument
35
;
21
allows us to deduce from the large sample theorem that any conditional entropy can be expressed as a convex combination of these extremal conditional entropies.

We begin by introducing the fundamental concepts underlying the theory of semirings and the general results that will be employed in our manuscript in Section
3.1
. We then construct the semiring associated with conditional entropies and derive the general form of monotone homomorphisms and derivations for this particular setting in Sections
3.5
, and
3.6
.

3.1
Background on preordered semirings and a
Vergleichsstellensatz

We define a preordered semiring as a tuple
S=(\mathcal{S},+,\cdot,0,1,\trianglelefteq)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a set equipped with binary operations of addition
+
and multiplication
\cdot
, a zero element
0\in\mathcal{S}
, a multiplicative unit
1\in\mathcal{S}
, and a preorder relation
\trianglelefteq
(a reflexive and transitive binary relation) defined on
\mathcal{S}
satisfying

x\trianglelefteq y\ \Rightarrow\ \begin{cases}x+a\ \trianglelefteq\ y+a,\\[3.00003pt]
xa\ \trianglelefteq\ ya,\end{cases}

(38)

for all
a\in\mathcal{S}
.
Moreover,

(\mathcal{S},+,0)
and
(\mathcal{S},\cdot,1)
are commutative semigroups, and the multiplication distributes over the addition.
For compactness, we omit the multiplication dot when writing products in the semiring.
We write
x\sim y
for the equivalence relation induced by
\trianglelefteq
, meaning that
x\sim y
if and only if there exist
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
such that

x\trianglelefteq z_{1}\trianglerighteq z_{2}\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglerighteq z_{n}\trianglelefteq y.

(39)

A preordered semiring
S
has polynomial growth if it admits a power universal element
u\in\mathcal{S}
, that is,

x\trianglelefteq y\quad\Rightarrow\quad\exists\,k\in\mathbb{N}:\ y\trianglelefteq xu^{k}.

(40)

We say that a preordered semiring
S
is a preordered semidomain if

\displaystyle xy=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0\ \text{or}\ y=0,

(41)

\displaystyle 0\trianglelefteq x\trianglelefteq 0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0.

Finally,
S
is zerosumfree if
x+y=0
implies
x=0=y
.

Given preordered semirings
S
and
T
, we say that a map
\Phi:\mathcal{S}\to T
is a
monotone homomorphism
if it satisfies the properties

1.

Additivity:
\Phi(x+y)=\Phi(x)+\Phi(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{S}
,

2.

Multiplicativity:
\Phi(xy)=\Phi(x)\Phi(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{S}
,

3.

Monotonicity:
x\trianglelefteq y

\Rightarrow

\Phi(x)\trianglelefteq\Phi(y)
, and

4.

\Phi(0)=0
and
\Phi(1)=1
.

We call a monotone homomorphism
degenerate
if
x\trianglelefteq y\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Phi(x)=\Phi(y)
. Otherwise, it is called
nondegenerate
.
We need to consider monotone homomorphisms with values in certain special semirings, namely:

1.

\mathbb{R}_{+}
: the half-line
[0,+\infty)
with the usual addition, multiplication, and total order.

2.

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}
: the same set with reversed order.

3.

\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
: the half-line
[0,+\infty)
with the usual multiplication, order, and the tropical sum
x+y:=\max\{x,y\}
.

4.

\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}
: the same as for
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
, but with reversed order.

The term
temperate reals
refers to the pair
(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}})
, while
tropical reals
denotes the pair
(\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}})
.
Given a monotone homomorphism
\Phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, an additive map
\Delta:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is called a derivation at
\Phi
(or a
\Phi
-derivation) if it satisfies the Leibniz rule

\Delta(xy)=\Delta(x)\,\Phi(y)+\Phi(x)\,\Delta(y)

(42)

for all
x,y\in S
.
In this work, we are interested in
\Phi
-derivations at degenerate homomorphisms that are also monotone, i.e.,

x\trianglelefteq y\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Delta(x)\leq\Delta(y).

(43)

The following theorem provides both a large-sample and a catalytic result. It belongs to a series of results collectively known as the “Vergleichsstellensätze”
16
.

Theorem 3.1

(Based on Theorem 8.6 in
16
)
.

Let
S
be a zerosumfree preordered semidomain with a power universal element
u
. Assume that for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
there is a surjective homomorphism
\|\cdot\|:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\cup\{(0,\ldots,0)\}
with trivial kernel and such that

a\trianglerighteq b\ \Rightarrow\ \|a\|=\|b\|\quad{\rm and}\quad\|a\|=\|b\|\ \Rightarrow\ a\sim b.

(44)

Denote the component homomorphisms of
\|\cdot\|
by
\|\cdot\|_{(j)}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
. Let
x,y\in S\setminus\{0\}
with
\|x\|=\|y\|
. If

(i)

for every
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
and every nondegenerate monotone homomorphism
\Phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{K}
with trivial kernel, we have
\Phi(x)>\Phi(y)
, and

(ii)

\Delta(x)>\Delta(y)
for every monotone
\|\cdot\|_{(j)}
-derivation
\Delta:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\Delta(u)=1
for all component indices
j=1,\ldots,d
,

then

(a)

there exists a nonzero
c\in\mathcal{S}
such that
cx\trianglerighteq cy
, and

(b)

if additionally
x
is power universal, then
x^{n}\trianglerighteq y^{n}
for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Conversely, if either of these properties holds for at least an integer
n
or a catalyst
c
, then the inequalities in items (i) and (ii) above hold non-strictly.

Large-sample ordering as described in item
2(b)
of Theorem
3.1
implies catalytic ordering as in item
2(a)
of Theorem
3.1
, where the catalyst can be chosen as

c=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}x^{\ell}y^{n-1-\ell}

(45)

for sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This implication was originally established in
11
for the case where
x,y
are probability vectors, but the argument extends naturally to the more general framework considered here.
The converse, however, generally fails to hold. In particular,
(
15
, Theorem 3)
presents a method for constructing explicit counterexamples within a specific context.

3.2
Definition of the conditional majorization semiring

In this section, we define the semiring of conditional majorization. In particular, we need to specify the elements of the tuple
S=(\mathcal{S},+,\cdot,0,1,\trianglerighteq)
.

1.

The set of elements “
\mathcal{S}
”
. The elements of the semiring are, essentially, all (not normalized) joint probability distributions
P_{XY}
.
However, since distributions that differ only by embedding into a larger outcome space and/or permutation of outcomes are considered equivalent, the set
\mathcal{S}
is formally defined as the set of equivalence classes of joint probability distributions under this similarity relation.
We make this precise in the following.

We can represent (unnormalized) joint distributions as matrices, with rows corresponding to the outcomes of the variable
X
and columns to those of
Y
. For example, if
X
has
d
outcomes and
Y
has
n
outcomes, then
P_{XY}
can be represented as an
d\times n
matrix

P_{XY}=\begin{pmatrix}P(x_{1},y_{1})&\ldots&P(x_{1},y_{n})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
P(x_{d},y_{1})&\ldots&P(x_{d},y_{n})\end{pmatrix}\,.

(46)

We also denote the set of outcomes
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},...,x_{d}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},...,y_{n}\}
.
We denote with
\mathcal{V}_{d\times n}
the set of all
d\times n
matrices with non-negative entries. Furthermore, define

\mathcal{V}:=\bigcup_{d,n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{V}_{d\times n}

(47)

as the set of all matrices with non-negative entries of arbitrary finite dimension.

We say that two joint probability distributions
P_{XY}
and
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
are similar, denoted

P_{XY}\approx Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}\,,

(48)

if one can be obtained from the other by permuting rows or columns, and/or inserting or deleting rows or columns consisting entirely of zeros.
In other words,
P_{XY}
and
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
represent the same distribution up to reordering of outcomes and inclusion or exclusion of impossible events. In yet other words, there is
R_{X^{\prime\prime}Y^{\prime\prime}}
such that both
P_{XY}
and
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
can be relabeled and embedded into
R_{X^{\prime\prime}Y^{\prime\prime}}
when we lift the notion of relabeling and embedding to non-normalized distributions.
For
P\in\mathcal{V}
, we define

[P]:=\{P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}\mid P\approx P^{\prime}\}

as the equivalence class of
P
under the relation
\approx
. The elements of the semiring are then given by the quotient set

\mathcal{S}:=\mathcal{V}/\approx,

(49)

which consists of all equivalence classes of
\mathcal{V}

(54)

4.

if

3.3
Invariance of the homomorphisms under embedding and shifting

In this section, we prove that monotone homomorphisms are invariant under embeddings of the
X
register. This invariance is consistent with the requirement that homomorphisms assign a unique value to each element of an equivalent class. Moreover, we show that they are also invariant under column-wise shifts.
This property will be crucial in characterizing the general form of such homomorphisms, which in turn determines the general form of conditional entropies.

We begin by introducing some notation. Let
0_{n\times 1}
denote the column vector of zeros of dimension
n
,
0_{1\times n}
the corresponding row vector, and
0_{n\times n}
the
n\times n
zero matrix. We denote by
E^{j\times j}_{n\times n}
the matrix of size
n\times n
with a 1 in the
(j,j)
-th entry and zeros elsewhere. Finally,
I_{n\times n}
stands for the
n\times n
identity matrix.

The next lemma states that there exists a transformation from
P_{XY}\oplus 0_{1\times n}
and

P_{X^{\prime}Y}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{XY}\\
0_{1\times n}\end{pmatrix}

(56)

and vice versa. Hence, since the homomorphisms are additive under direct sums and assign zero to the null element, it follows that they assign the same value to both
P_{XY}
and
P_{X^{\prime}Y}
.

Lemma 3.2

(Embedding)
.

Let
P_{XY}\in\mathcal{V}_{d\times n}
. Denote the disjoint union of
X
and
\{1\}
by
X^{\prime}
. We have

P_{XY}\oplus 0_{1\times n}\succeq\begin{pmatrix}P_{XY}\\
0_{1\times n}\end{pmatrix}\succeq P_{XY}\oplus 0_{1\times n}.

(57)

Proof.

The proof follows directly by considering a channel acting on the conditioning system that appends and removes outcomes associated with zero entries. Let us construct these channels explicitly.
Let us denote the matrix in the middle of (
57
) by
P_{X^{\prime}Y}
, where
X^{\prime}
is the extension of the variable
X
with one additional outcome. Similarly, let
Y^{\prime}
denote the extension of
Y
by
n
additional outcomes. Then,
P_{XY}\oplus 0_{1\times n}\in\mathcal{V}_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
.
We prove the two directions by explicitly writing down the channel that transforms one into the other.

Let us first prove that
P_{XY}\oplus 0_{1\times n}\succeq P_{X^{\prime}Y}
. Let us define the matrix that discards
n
outcomes from
Y^{\prime}
, namely

D=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n\times n}\\
I_{n\times n}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(58)

Then, we have that

(P_{XY}\oplus 0_{1\times n})D=\begin{pmatrix}P_{XY}&0_{d\times n}\\
0_{1\times n}&0_{1\times n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{n\times n}\\
I_{n\times n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{XY}\\
0_{1\times n}\end{pmatrix}=P_{X^{\prime}Y}\,.

(59)

To prove that
P_{X^{\prime}Y}\succeq P_{XY}\oplus 0_{1\times n}
, one can add outcomes that never occur by defining the matrix

D=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(60)

Then, we have that

P_{X^{\prime}Y}D=\begin{pmatrix}P_{XY}\\
0_{1\times n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{n\times n}&0_{n\times n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{XY}&0_{d\times n}\\
0_{1\times n}&0_{1\times n}\end{pmatrix}=P_{XY}\oplus 0_{1\times n}\,.

(61)

∎

The next lemma states that the columns can be shifted without affecting the value of any homomorphism, as there exists a channel implementing the transformation in both directions.

Lemma 3.3

(Shifting)
.

Let
P_{XY}\in\mathcal{V}_{d\times n}
. Moreover, for each
i=1,...,n
, let
P_{X,Y=y_{i}}
be the
i
-th column of
P_{XY}
, that is,
P_{XY}=(P_{X,Y=y_{1}},...,P_{X,Y=y_{n}})
. Define

\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}Y}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}P_{X,Y=y_{i}}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{X,Y=y_{1}}&0_{d\times 1}&\ldots&0_{d\times 1}\\
0_{d\times 1}&P_{X,Y=y_{2}}&\ldots&0_{d\times 1}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
0_{d\times 1}&\ldots&0_{d\times 1}&P_{X,Y=y_{n}}\,\end{pmatrix}\,,

(62)

where
\widetilde{X}=X\sqcup\cdots\sqcup X
is the
n
-fold separate union of
X
. We have

\begin{pmatrix}P_{XY}\\
0_{dn\times n}\end{pmatrix}\succeq\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}Y}\succeq\begin{pmatrix}P_{XY}\\
0_{dn\times n}\end{pmatrix}.

(63)

Proof.

Let us first prove that

\begin{pmatrix}P_{XY}\\
0_{dn\times n}\end{pmatrix}\succeq\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}Y}\,.

(64)

For this case, the proof follows by first measuring the
Y
register and, depending on the outcome, performing an appropriate relabeling of the outcomes in the
X
register.
Explicitly, let us define
D^{(j)}=E^{j\times j}_{n\times n}
which selects the outcome
y_{j}
of the
Y
register. For example, for
j=2
, we have

D^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0_{1\times(n-2)}\\
0&1&0_{1\times(n-2)}\\
0_{(n-2)\times 1}&0_{(n-2)\times 1}&0_{(n-2)\times(n-2)}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(65)

The matrix
S^{(j)}
relabels the outcomes on the
X
register. For example, for
j=2
, we have

S^{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{d\times d}&I_{d\times d}&0_{d\times d(n-2)}\\
I_{d\times d}&0_{d\times d}&0_{d\times d(n-2)}\\
0_{d(n-2)\times d}&0_{d(n-2)\times d}&I_{d(n-2)\times d(n-2)}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(66)

To prove the other direction, we use the same matrices as before to select the appropriate outcome of
Y
and then apply the same permutation to restore the original ordering of the entries.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

into

.

3.4
Some important properties of the semiring
S

To use the large-sample results in Theorem
3.1
, we must first show that the semiring is of polynomial growth, i.e., it contains a power-universal element. Since the large sample results apply only when the input element is power-universal, we also need to determine exactly which elements have this property. Lastly, we must construct a surjective degenerate homomorphism
\|\cdot\|:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\cup\{0\}
with the properties required in Theorem
3.1
. We begin with the last point. In the following lemma, we show that such a homomorphism can be chosen as the total weight, given by the sum of the matrix entries associated with elements of the semiring.
Finally, note that the total weight is a scalar quantity rather than a vector, and therefore there exists only a single component homomorphism of
\|\cdot\|
in Theorem
3.1
, namely
j=1
.

Lemma 3.5
.

In the preordered semiring of conditional majorization
S
, the map

\|[P_{XY}]\|=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{XY}(x,y)\,,

(68)

where
P_{XY}
can be chosen to be any representative of the equivalence class
[P_{XY}]
,
defines a surjective homomorphism
\|\cdot\|:\mathcal{S}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
with trivial kernel. Moreover, it satisfies

[P]\trianglerighteq[Q]\quad\Rightarrow\quad\|[P]\|=\|[Q]\|\quad\Rightarrow\quad[P]\sim[Q]\,.

(69)

Proof.

Clearly, the total weight is a homomorphism since
\|[P]\cdot[Q]\|=\|[P\otimes Q]\|=\|[P]\|\|[Q]\|
and
\|[P]+[Q]\|=\|[P\oplus Q]\|=\|[P]\|+\|[Q]\|
for all
[P],[Q]\in\mathcal{S}
.

The first direction, namely that
[P]\trianglerighteq[Q]\Rightarrow\|[P]\|=\|[Q]\|
, follows immediately, since conditionally mixing operations preserve the total weight
\|P\|
of any element
P
. This also shows that the total weight is a degenerate homomorphism since it is invariant under the preorder
\trianglerighteq
.

Let us not turn to the implication
\|[P]\|=\|[Q]\|\Rightarrow[P]\sim[Q]
. We show that if we define
\|P\|=\|Q\|=c
, then
P\preceq c\succeq Q

and hence
[P]\sim[Q]
. In particular, we show that from the element
[c]
we can generate both
P
and
Q
. This is straightforward by considering the matrix with a single non-zero entry
c
in the top-left corner. Indeed, starting from this matrix, we can construct both
P
and
Q
. For example, let us focus on
P_{XY}\in\mathcal{V}_{d\times n}
.
Then the steps are the following. Let us denote with
P_{Y}
the row vectors with components
P_{Y}(y_{i})=\sum_{j}P_{XY}(x_{j},y_{i})
.
First, we create for each outcome
Y
a column whose first entry is
P_{Y}(y_{i})
and all other entries are zero. This is done by multiplying on the r.h.s by a row stochastic matrix.

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}P_{Y}\\
0_{(d-1)\times n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\|P_{XY}\|&0_{1\times(n-1)}\\
0_{(d-1)\times 1}&0_{(d-1)\times(n-1)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\|P_{XY}\|^{-1}P_{Y}\\
\vdots\\
\|P_{XY}\|^{-1}P_{Y}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(70)

Next, we measure the register
Y
and, conditioned on the outcome
y_{i}
, we transform the deterministic column (with a single non-zero entry) into a more distributed column
P_{X,Y=y_{i}}
. Let us denote with
P_{X,Y=y_{1}}
the column with components
P_{X,Y=y_{1}}(x_{j})=P_{X,Y}(x_{j},y_{1})
and with
P_{X|Y=y_{1}}
the column with components
P_{X|Y=y_{1}}(x_{j})=P_{X,Y}(x_{j},y_{1})/P_{Y}(y_{1})
,
For the first outcome
y_{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}P_{X,Y=y_{1}}&0_{d\times(n-1)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{X|Y=y_{1}}&\star_{1\times(d-1)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}P_{Y}\\
0_{(d-1)\times n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0_{1\times(n-1)}\\
0_{(n-1)\times 1}&0_{1\times(n-1)}\end{pmatrix}

(71)

Here,
\star_{1\times(d-1)}
is any entry that completes the matrix to a doubly stochastic one. By summing over all the outcomes
y_{i}
for all
i=1,..,n
, we obtain the desired probability
P_{XY}
. This shows that overall, we have

P_{XY}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}S^{(i)}PDD^{(i)}\,,

(72)

where

\displaystyle P=\begin{pmatrix}\|P_{XY}\|&0_{1\times(n-1)}\\
0_{(d-1)\times 1}&0_{(d-1)\times(n-1)}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad D=\begin{pmatrix}\|P_{XY}\|^{-1}P_{Y}\\
\vdots\\
\|P_{XY}\|^{-1}P_{Y}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(73)

and for example, for
i=1
, we have

\displaystyle D^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0_{1\times(n-1)}\\
0_{(n-1)\times 1}&0_{1\times(n-1)}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad S^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{X|Y=y_{1}}&\star_{1\times(d-1)}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(74)

∎

As we mentioned above, to apply the large sample and catalytic results of Theorem
3.1
, we must also identify a power universal element. By definition, this implies that the semiring is of polynomial growth. As defined in Section
3.1
, a power universal element is one that, when provided in sufficient copies, enables the realization of any transformation between elements of the semiring. Within the semiring of conditional majorization, such an element corresponds to any probability distribution exhibiting non-zero randomness in the
X
register—that is, a probability distribution that is not fully deterministic in
X
. Furthermore, this condition must be satisfied for every possible outcome of the
Y
register.

Proposition 3.6
.

The following statements are equivalent for
[R_{XY}]\in\mathcal{S}
:

1.

[R_{XY}]\in\mathcal{S}
is power universal.

2.

\|[R_{XY}]\|=1
, and every non-zero column
R_{X,Y=y}
of any representative
R_{XY}
contains at least two non-zero entries.

3.

There exists
r\in(0,1)
such that

\begin{pmatrix}r\\
1-r\end{pmatrix}\succeq R_{XY}.

(75)

Consequently, the semiring
S
is of polynomial growth.

Proof.

We prove separately the implications
\ref{item: power universal}\implies\ref{item: two non-zero entries}
,
\ref{item: two non-zero entries}\implies\ref{item: majorization R_r}
and
\ref{item: majorization R_r}\implies\ref{item: power universal}
. This shows that the statements are all equivalent.
In the following, for brevity, we simply write
P_{XY}
to refer to a representative of the equivalence class
[P_{XY}]
, without always stating it explicitly. Throughout this proof, we assume that
R_{XY}\in\mathcal{V}_{d\times n}
(i.e.
|\mathcal{X}|=d
and
|\mathcal{Y}|=n
) and we number
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
explicitly to aid our matrix calculations.

Let us start with the implication
\ref{item: power universal}\implies\ref{item: two non-zero entries}
. The fact that
\|[R_{XY}]\|=1
is immediate, since conditional majorization preserves the total weight of elements. If instead
\|[R_{XY}]\|\neq 1
, the transformation would not be possible. Next, we show that if
R_{XY}
contains a column with only one non-zero entry, then it cannot be a power universal. The key idea is that if
R_{XY}
contains a column with only one non-zero entry, then any tensor power
R_{XY}^{\otimes m}
will also contain a column of the same form. However, starting from a mixed probability distribution, it is impossible to generate a deterministic distribution conditioned on the event that
Y
corresponds to such a column. This follows from the fact that doubly stochastic maps cannot produce a deterministic distribution from a non-deterministic one; instead, they only increase the level of mixing.
Explicitly, let us define
P_{XY}=(1/2,1/2)
and take
Q_{XY}=1
to be the single unit element. Note that we use the subscript
XY
for both, as the smaller matrix can always be embedded into the space of the larger one by padding with zeros.
Observe that
Q_{XY}\succeq P_{XY}
. Assume now that
R_{XY}
is power universal. Then, there exists a sufficiently large
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

P_{XY}\succeq Q_{XY}\otimes R_{XY}^{\otimes m}=R_{XY}^{\otimes m}\,.

(76)

This implies that, for a matrix
R_{XY}\in\mathcal{V}_{d\times n}
, there exist an integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
, doubly stochastic matrices
S^{(1)},\ldots,S^{(k)}
, and (without loss of generality—since the zero entries in the embedding will eliminate the effect of the remaining components) single-row matrices
D^{(i)}=(d^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,d^{(i)}_{n^{m}})
,
such that an embedding of the vector
P_{XY}
can be transformed into
R_{XY}^{\otimes m}

\displaystyle R_{XY}^{\otimes m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}S^{(i)}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}\\
0_{(d^{m}-2)\times 1}\end{pmatrix}\big(d^{(i)}_{1}\ \cdots\ d^{(i)}_{n^{m}}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}S^{(i)}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}d^{(i)}_{1}\ \cdots\ \frac{1}{2}d^{(i)}_{n^{m}}\\
\frac{1}{2}d^{(i)}_{1}\ \cdots\ \frac{1}{2}d^{(i)}_{n^{m}}\\
0_{(d^{m}-2)\times 1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(77)

We now distinguish two cases: either
d_{j}^{(i)}=0
or
d_{j}^{(i)}>0
. In both situations, the column of
R_{XY}^{\otimes m}
with a single non-zero entry cannot be generated by any column of the matrix
P_{XY}D^{(i)}
. Indeed, if
d_{j}^{(i)}=0
, the corresponding column is identically zero; if
d_{j}^{(i)}>0
, then the resulting column contains at least two non-zero entries of the form
\frac{1}{2}d_{j}^{(i)}
, and a doubly stochastic matrix cannot reduce this uncertainty to produce a deterministic outcome. This follows from the fact that both the rows and columns of a doubly stochastic matrix must sum to one, thus preserving entropy rather than eliminating it.

Let us now turn to the implication
\ref{item: two non-zero entries}\implies\ref{item: majorization R_r}

Let us first assume that
\|R_{XY}\|=1
and
R_{XY}
is such that its columns have supports with at least two points. We need to find
r\in(0,1)
such that the condition in item (3) holds.
The key idea is that each column of
R_{XY}
containing at least two non-zero entries can be generated from a vector of the form
(r,1-r)
, for some sufficiently large
r\in(0,1)
. Such a vector represents an almost deterministic distribution, which—after suitable embedding—can be transformed, via mixing, into any non-deterministic distribution with the same support. Furthermore, the same vector
(r,1-r)
can be used to generate all columns of
R_{XY}
, since we are free to enlarge the system
Y
to accommodate additional outcomes if needed. Explicitly,
because of the restriction on
R_{XY}
, we have
R_{XY}(x_{j},y_{i})<R_{Y}(y_{i})
for all
x_{j}\in X
and
y_{i}\in Y
such that the column
R_{X,Y=y_{i}}
is non-zero. Therefore, there exists a
r\in[1/2,1)
such that

R_{Y}(y_{i})r\geq R_{XY}(x_{j},y_{i})\qquad\forall(x_{j},y_{i})\in X\times Y:\ R_{X,Y=y_{i}}\neq 0.

(78)

By Lemma
2.5
, it follows that for each
y_{i}\in Y
with
R_{X,Y=y_{i}}\neq 0
, the two-dimensional embedded vector
\widetilde{R}_{Y}(y_{i}):=\big(rR_{Y}(y_{i}),(1-r)R_{Y}(y_{i})\big)

majorizes the column
R_{X,Y=y_{i}}
.
Hence, for each
y_{i}\in Y
with
R_{X,Y=y_{i}}\neq 0
, there exists a doubly stochastic matrix
S^{(i)}
and an embedding of the vector
\widetilde{R}_{Y}(y_{i})
, denoted by
\widehat{R}_{Y}(y_{i})
, such that
S^{(i)}\widehat{R}_{Y}(y_{i})=R_{X,Y=y_{i}}
.
To isolate the outcome
y_{i}\in Y
, we define the matrices
D^{(i)}
with a
1
in the
(y_{i},y_{i})
-th position and zeros elsewhere. Treating
R_{Y}
as a row-stochastic vector and indexing the outcomes of
Y
as
i=1,..,n
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
1-r\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\succeq\left(\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
1-r\end{array}\right)R_{Y}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}rR_{Y}(y_{1})&\cdots&rR_{Y}(y_{n})\\
(1-r)R_{Y}(y_{1})&\cdots&(1-r)R_{Y}(y_{n})\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\succeq\sum_{i=1}^{n}S^{(i)}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\widehat{R}_{Y}(y_{1})\cdots\widehat{R}_{Y}(y_{n})\end{array}\right)R^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\big(S^{(y_{1})}\widehat{R}_{Y}(y_{1})\cdots S^{(y_{n})}\widehat{R}_{Y}(y_{n})\big)

(86)

\displaystyle=\big(R_{X,Y=y_{1}}\cdots R_{X,Y=y_{n}}\big)

(87)

\displaystyle=R_{XY}.

(88)

Hence, we obtain what we wanted to prove.

Finally, let us prove the implication
\ref{item: majorization R_r}\implies\ref{item: power universal}
. We need to show that if there exists
r\in(0,1)
such that
R_{r}\succeq R_{XY}
, where
R_{r}
is the matrix (column) appearing on the left-hand side of (
75
),
then
[R_{XY}]
is power universal. To establish this, we first show that
[R_{r}]
is a power universal element; that is, for any elements
[P_{XY}]
and
[Q_{XY}]
such that
\|[P_{XY}]\|=\|[Q_{XY}]\|
, there exists a sufficiently large integer
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that

P_{XY}\succeq Q_{XY}\otimes R_{r}^{\otimes m}
. This, in turn, implies that [
R_{XY}]
is also power universal, since
R_{r}\succeq R_{XY}
implies the chain of inequalities

P_{XY}\succeq Q_{XY}\otimes R_{r}^{\otimes m}\succeq Q_{XY}\otimes R_{XY}^{\otimes m}\,.

(89)

Note that we use the subscript
XY
for both
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY}
, since the smaller matrix can always be embedded into the larger one by zero-padding.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
r\in[1/2,1)
, as the case
r\in(0,1/2)
lies in the same equivalence class due to the ability to permute elements.
The fact that
[R_{r}]
is power universal follows from the observation that, given a sufficiently large number of copies of
R_{r}^{\otimes m}
, the non-zero entries of the resulting vector become arbitrarily small. In particular, the largest entry is
r^{m}
, which approaches zero as
m\to\infty
.
By the initial sum condition for majorization (see Lemma
2.5
), this implies that any column of a suitably large embedded distribution
P_{X,Y=y_{i}}
can be transformed into
Q_{X,Y=y_{i}}\otimes R_{r}^{\otimes m}
, provided the corresponding weights match; that is, when
P_{Y}(y_{i})=Q_{Y}(y_{i})
.
If the column weights differ, we can apply an additional stochastic map to
P_{XY}
to adjust the marginals accordingly. Explicitly, let
D
be a row-stochastic matrix such that

P_{Y}D=Q_{Y}

(90)

and denote
\widetilde{Q}_{XY}:=P_{XY}D
. It is easy to notice that the columns
\widetilde{Q}_{X,Y=y}
have the same weight as the columns
Q_{X,Y=y}
for each
y
.
Moreover, let
m\in\mathbb{N}
be large enough so that

r^{m}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}Q_{XY}(x,y)\leq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{X}\\
\widetilde{Q}(x,y)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\widetilde{Q}(x,y)\qquad\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}.

(91)

It then follows, by Lemma
2.5
, that for every
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, the column
\widetilde{Q}_{X,Y=y}
majorizes the column
Q_{X,Y=y}\otimes R^{\otimes m}
.
Let
S^{(i)}
be a doubly stochastic matrix such that

S^{(i)}\widehat{Q}_{X,Y=y_{i}}=Q_{X,Y=y_{i}}\otimes R^{\otimes m}\,,

(92)

where we denoted with
\widehat{Q}_{X,Y}
a suitably embedding of
\widetilde{Q}_{XY}
.
Furthermore, let
D^{(i)}
denote the matrix that selects the outcome
y_{i}
, as defined in Lemma
3.2
; that is, the matrix with a 1 at entry
(y_{i},y_{i})
and zeros elsewhere.
We now have

\displaystyle P_{XY}

\displaystyle\succeq P_{XY}D=\widetilde{Q}_{XY}\succeq\sum_{i=1}^{k}S^{(i)}\widehat{Q}_{XY}D^{(i)}=Q_{XY}\otimes R^{\otimes m}\,.

(93)

Finally, the semiring has polynomial growth, as it evidently contains at least one power-universal element.

∎

Let us note that, whenever
Q_{Y}
is a probability distribution, the joint distribution
(1/2,1/2)\times Q_{Y}
corresponds to a power universal of the semiring
S
according to the above result. Note that this is exactly the kind of joint distribution that we use to normalize our general conditional entropies in the normalization axiom 4 of Definition
2.11
.

3.5
The homomorphisms

In this section, we derive the general form of conditional entropy satisfying the axioms stated in Definition
2.11
. Within the theory of preordered semirings, monotone homomorphisms and derivations are functions designed to satisfy analogous properties. In particular, for the semiring of conditional majorization, these functions correspond—up to an additive constant and a logarithmic factor—to the extremal conditional entropies, which we later denote by
H_{t,\tau}
. As we prove later, these entropies generate the entire family of conditional entropies via convex combinations (see Theorem
7.1
). Hence, for our purposes, it is crucial to determine the form of the homomorphisms and derivations, which is achieved by characterizing them through their mathematical properties.

As we reviewed in Section
3.1
, we need to consider monotone homomorphisms
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{K}
in the fields
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{TR}_{+},\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}\,.

The cases
\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}
correspond to the temperate case, whereas
\mathbb{TR}_{+},\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}
correspond to the tropical cases.
As we show later, the temperate cases correspond to the conditional entropy
H_{t,\tau}
(up to a constant and a logarithmic factor), with the probability measure
\tau
and
t>0
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
t<0
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}
(see Definition
6.1
). The tropical cases yield the limits
t\to+\infty,-\infty
. In particular,
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\rm op}
gives rise to the entropies
H_{-\infty,\tau}
(see Definition
6.1
), corresponding to the limiting form of
H_{t,\tau}
as
t\to-\infty
.
Finally, the case
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
gives rise to
H_{+\infty,0}
, which corresponds to the limiting form of
H_{t,\tau}
as
t\to+\infty
and to the choice of
\tau
as a point measure at
\alpha=0
(see Definition
6.1
). As discussed later, in this last limiting regime, the convexity properties of the function enforce that only point measures supported at
\alpha=0
are admissible. In the following result, we express the statement in terms of a measure
\mu
, which we later decompose as
\mu=t\,\tau
, where
t
denotes the total mass of
\mu
and
\tau
is the associated probability measure.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
S
be the semiring of conditional majorization. The monotone homomorphisms
\Phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
are of the form

\Phi(P_{XY})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\exp\Bigg(\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y_{i}})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\Bigg),&\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}\},\\
\displaystyle\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{Y}\\
P_{Y}(y)>0\end{subarray}}\exp\Bigg(\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\Bigg),&\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}},\\
\displaystyle\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{Y}\\
P_{Y}(y)>0\end{subarray}}\exp\left(kH_{0}(P_{X|Y=y_{i}})\,\right),&\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{TR}_{+}\,,\end{cases}

(94)

where
\mu
is a finite positive measure in case
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and it is a negative measure in cases
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
. Moreover, in case
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{TR}_{+}
,
k
is a positive constant.

Proof.

The proof is organized into several key steps that lead to the general form of the monotone homomorphisms. Specifically, we proceed as follows:

1.

Step 1: Additivity. We use the shifting lemma
3.3
to reduce the problem to determining the monotone homomorphism for a fixed column vector
P_{X,Y=y}
, corresponding to a specific value
Y=y
.

2.

Step 2: Multiplicativity. We then use the property that monotone homomorphisms must be multiplicative under tensor products to further simplify the problem to normalized conditional distributions
P_{X|Y=y}
.

3.

Step 3: Inner barycenter. We invoke Proposition
2.13
, which states that the most general form of an entropy can be expressed as a linear combination of Rényi entropies.

4.

Step 4: Simplifying
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
. We use the fact that, in this case, the monotone homomorphisms are strongly quasi-concave, which allows us to further simplify their form and to single out point measures supported at
\alpha=0
.

Step 1: Additivity
. The first step is to use the additivity of the homomorphism under direct sums to reduce the problem from the matrix
P_{XY}
to a single column vector
P_{X,Y=y}
for a fixed value
Y=y
.
Lemma
3.3
, together with the fact that the homomorphism is additive in both the temperate and tropical sense under summation (see also Remark
3.4
), leads to the following additive form

\Phi(P_{XY})=\begin{cases}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Phi(P_{X,Y=y}),&\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}},\\
\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Phi(P_{X,Y=y}),&\mathbb{TR}_{+},\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}\,.\end{cases}

(95)

Hence, the problem reduces to finding the homomorphisms for the column vector
P_{X,Y=y}
with fixed
Y=y
.

Step 2: Multiplicativity
. The second step applies the multiplicativity of the homomorphism under tensor products, specifically scalar multiplication, to reduce the problem from the unnormalized vector
P_{X,Y=y}
to the normalized vector probability distribution
P_{X|Y=y}=P_{X,Y=y}/P_{Y}(y)
, where
P_{Y}(y)=\sum_{x}P_{XY}(x,y)=P_{Y}(y)
is the total weight of the colum
P_{X,Y=y}
. We then obtain

\Phi(P_{X,Y=y})=\Phi\left(P_{Y}(y)\otimes\frac{P_{X,Y=y}}{P_{Y}(y)}\right)=\Phi(P_{Y}(y))\Phi\left(\frac{P_{X,Y=y}}{P_{Y}(y)}\right)=\Phi(P_{Y}(y))\Phi\big(P_{X|Y=y}\big)\,.

(96)

Next, we analyze the form of
\Phi(P_{Y}(y))
, which corresponds to the homomorphism applied to the single matrix entry
P_{Y}(y)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
By definition, the homomorphism satisfies
\Phi(0)=0
for the zero element and
\Phi(1)=1
for the unit element.
Moreover, from the multiplicativity of
\Phi
, it follows that
\Phi(xy)=\Phi(x)\Phi(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Thus, if the restriction of
\Phi
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfies a suitable regularity condition—such as being bounded on an interval with non-empty interior—it follows that
\Phi(x)=x^{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
We shall next show that
\Phi(x)\leq 1
for all
x\in[0,1]
. Let
x\in[0,1]
. We easily see that

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x&0\\
0&1-x\end{array}\right)\succeq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\succeq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x&0\\
0&1-x\end{array}\right)\,.

(97)

The first implication follows from the fact that we can apply a doubly stochastic map to the second column, conditioned on the second outcome of the register
Y
, and then merge both outcomes into a single
Y
outcome. The second implication follows by applying a stochastic map on
Y
that discards the second outcome and creates two new outcomes with probabilities
x
amd
1-x
. Finally, we permute the outcomes of the
X
register conditioned on the second outcome of the
Y
register. Since the homomorphism must be monotone under both actions, equality must hold for both elements, and thus, for the temperate case, the sum property implies that

1=\Phi(1)=\Phi\left[\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x&0\\
0&1-x\end{array}\right)\right]=\Phi(x)+\Phi(1-x)\geq\Phi(x)\,,

(98)

since
\Phi(1-x)\geq 0
. The tropical case is similar since
\max\{\Phi(x),\Phi(1-x)\}\geq\Phi(x)
. Hence, the homomorphisms are bounded and, for what was discussed earlier,
\Phi(x)=x^{\gamma}
for all
x>0
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Next, we show that
\gamma=1
in the temperate cases
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{op}}_{+}
, while
\gamma=0
in the tropical cases
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
and
\mathbb{TR}^{\mathrm{op}}_{+}
.
By substituting
x=\frac{1}{2}
, and using the equivalence established in (
97
) along with the (temperate or tropical) additivity, we obtain

1=\Phi(1)=\begin{cases}2\,\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)=2^{1-\gamma},&\quad\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}},\\
\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)=2^{-\gamma},&\quad\mathbb{TR}_{+},\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}\,.\end{cases}

(99)

This establishes that
\gamma=1
in the temperate cases
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{op}}_{+}
, and
\gamma=0
in the tropical cases
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
and
\mathbb{TR}^{\mathrm{op}}_{+}
. In the above arguments, we implicitly assumed that
P_{Y}(y)>0
. In the situation where
P_{Y}(y)=0
we have that
\Phi(P_{X,Y=y})=0
since
\Phi(0)=0
. This implies that in the cases
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
and
\mathbb{TR}^{\mathrm{op}}_{+}
, only the
Y
outcomes such that
P_{Y}(y)>0
must be included in the maximization. Substituting the values of
\gamma
into (
95
), we obtain

\Phi(P_{XY})=\begin{cases}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\,\Phi\!\left(P_{X|Y=y}\right),&\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}},\\
\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{Y}\\
P_{Y}(y)>0\end{subarray}}\Phi\!\left(P_{X|Y=y}\right),&\mathbb{TR}_{+},\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}\,.\end{cases}

(100)

Step 3: Inner barycenter
. The final step is to determine the form of the homomorphism for a normalized column vector
P_{X|Y=y}
. Using multiplicativity, for two column vectors
P_{X}
and
Q_{X^{\prime}}
, we have that

\Phi(P_{X}\otimes Q_{X^{\prime}})=\Phi(P_{X})\Phi(Q_{X^{\prime}})\,.

(101)

Furthermore, by monotonicity under doubly stochastic maps, we have that for any vector
P_{X}
and any doubly stochastic matrix
S
,

\begin{cases}\Phi(P_{X})\leq\Phi(SP_{X}),&\quad\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{TR}_{+},\\
\Phi(P_{X})\geq\Phi(SP_{X}),&\quad\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}\,.\end{cases}

(102)

Therefore, the function
P_{X}\mapsto\log{\Phi(P_{X})}
is additive under tensor product and monotone under doubly stochastic maps. Using Proposition
2.13
, we obtain that the function
\log{\Phi(P_{X})}
is a linear combination of Rényi entropies. This leads to the form

\Phi(P_{XY})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\exp\Bigg(\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\Bigg),&\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}},\\
\displaystyle\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{Y}\\
P_{Y}(y)>0\end{subarray}}\exp\Bigg(\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\Bigg),&\mathbb{TR}_{+},\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}.\end{cases}

(103)

where
\mu
is a positive measure in cases
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
, and it is a negative measure in the opposite cases
\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{op}}_{+}
and
\mathbb{TR}^{\mathrm{op}}_{+}
.

Step 4: Simplifying
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
In the case of
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
, the form of the homomorphisms can be further restricted. Specifically, only the case
\alpha=0
for the inner barycenter is admissible. To show this, let us first prove that for elements of the semiring that are column vectors, the tropical homomorphisms in
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
depend only on the support of the vector. Given
P\neq Q
with the same support, we have that there exists
s,t\in(0,1)
and
P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle tQ+(1-t)Q^{\prime}=P\,,\quad sP+(1-s)P^{\prime}=Q

(104)

We only need to consider monotone homomorphisms. In
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
, Proposition
A.1
shows that this is equivalent to the homomorphisms being strongly quasi-concave, with the latter property defined in equation (
348
).
Then, we use the strong quasi-concavity to get

\displaystyle\Phi(P)\geq\max\{\Phi(Q),\Phi(Q^{\prime})\}\geq\Phi(Q)\geq\max\{\Phi(P),\Phi(P^{\prime})\}\geq\Phi(P)\,.

(105)

Hence,
\Phi(P)=\Phi(Q)
, which implies that
\Phi
depends only on the support of the distribution. Since the only Rényi entropy that depends solely on the support, and not on the specific probabilities, is
H_{0}
, we can further restrict the monotone homomorphisms in
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
to

\Phi(P_{XY})=\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{Y}\\
P_{Y}(y)>0\end{subarray}}\exp(kH_{0}(P_{X|Y=y}))

(106)

for some positive constant
k>0
.
All the results together prove the proposition.
∎

3.6
The derivations

In the previous sections, we examined the monotone homomorphisms, which (up to a constant and a log factor) form a subset of the extremal conditional entropies—those that, through convex combinations, generate the entire set of conditional entropies. However, to obtain a complete characterization of this set, it is also necessary to consider derivations at
\|\cdot\|
, which we prove to arise as limiting cases of homomorphisms. In particular, we show that these derivations coincide with generalized convex combinations of the conditional entropies
H_{0,\alpha}
(see Definition
6.1
), corresponding to the limiting form of
H_{t,\tau}
as
t\to 0
.

Let us point out that, according to Theorem
3.1
, we only need to consider derivations
\Delta
with
\Delta\big(P_{X}\otimes(1/2,1/2)\big)=1
for some (and, as we shall see, consequently for all) probability distributions
P_{X}
since
P_{X}\otimes(1/2,1/2)
is a power universal according to Proposition
3.6
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
S
be the semiring of conditional majorization. Then, the extremal monotone derivations at
\|\cdot\|
, as defined in Lemma
3.5
, are given by

H_{0,\alpha}(P)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\,H_{\alpha}\!\big(P_{X|Y=y}\big)\,,

(107)

for
\alpha\in[0,+\infty]
.

Proof.

We need to study of monotone derivations at
\|\cdot\|
. These are additive maps
\Delta\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
that preserve the order, in the sense that
[P]\trianglerighteq[Q]
—and hence
P\preceq Q
for representatives of the corresponding equivalence classes—implies
\Delta(P)\geq\Delta(Q)
. Moreover, they satisfy the Leibniz rule with respect to the semiring product,

\displaystyle\Delta(P_{XY}\otimes Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}})=\Delta(P_{XY})\,\|Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}\|+\|P_{XY}\|\,\Delta(Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}).

(108)

for all
P_{XY},Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{V}
.

The proof is organized into several key steps that lead to the general form of the monotone derivations. Specifically, we proceed as follows:

1.

Step 1: Additive constant. We begin by showing that the derivations can be taken to satisfy
\Delta(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

2.

Step 2: General form. We then exploit the properties of monotone derivations to determine their general form.

3.

Step 3: Extremal derivations. We identify the extremal derivations, which generate the entire set of derivations through convex combinations.

Step 1: Additive constant.
We first show that, without loss of generality, the derivation can be assumed to satisfy
\Delta(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. To establish this, we prove that from any given derivation
\Delta
, one can construct another derivation
\Delta^{\prime}
which satisfies
\Delta^{\prime}(x)=0
and hence it is interchangeable with
\Delta
as defined in Definition 8.2 of
16
. Let
\Delta
be a derivation. We notice that, for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\Delta(xy)=\Delta(x)y+x\Delta(y),

(109)

implying that
\delta:=-\Delta|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is a derivation.
Define a new map
\Delta^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\Delta^{\prime}(P):=\Delta(P)-\Delta(\|P\|).

(110)

By construction,
\Delta^{\prime}(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. It therefore suffices to show that
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
are interchangeable. This will follow if
\Delta^{\prime}
is itself a monotone derivation at
\|\cdot\|
. While this is a general fact, we provide a direct proof here for completeness. We have, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x&0\\
0&y\end{array}\right)\succeq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x+y&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\succeq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x&0\\
0&y\end{array}\right),

(111)

by the same argument that we used in the multiplicativity part of Section
3.5
. Therefore, we obtain that

\Delta(x+y)=\Delta\left[\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}x&0\\
0&y\end{array}\right)\right]=\Delta(x)+\Delta(y)\,.

(112)

In the following, for compactness, we omit the subscripts
X,Y
.
We now have, for all
P,Q\in\mathcal{V}
,

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(P\oplus Q)

\displaystyle=\Delta(P\oplus Q)-\Delta(\|P\oplus Q\|)

(113)

\displaystyle=\Delta(P)+\Delta(Q)-\Delta(\|P\|+\|Q\|)

(114)

\displaystyle=\Delta(P)+\Delta(Q)-\Delta(\|P\|)-\Delta(\|Q\|)

(115)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{\prime}(P)+\Delta^{\prime}(Q)

(116)

and

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}(P\otimes Q)

\displaystyle=\Delta(P\otimes Q)-\Delta(\|P\otimes Q\|)

(117)

\displaystyle=\Delta(P)\|Q\|+\|P\|\Delta(Q)-\Delta(\|P\|\|Q\|)

(118)

\displaystyle=\Delta(P)\|Q\|+\|P\|\Delta(Q)-\Delta(\|P\|)\|Q\|-\|P\|\Delta(\|Q\|)

(119)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{\prime}(P)\|Q\|+\|P\|\Delta^{\prime}(Q),

(120)

showing that
\Delta^{\prime}
is a derivation at
\|\cdot\|
. To prove monotonicity, we consider
[P]
and
[Q]
such that
[P]\trianglerighteq[Q]
. Then, we have

\Delta^{\prime}(P)=\underbrace{\Delta(P)}_{\geq\Delta(Q)}-\Delta(\underbrace{\|P\|}_{=\|Q\|})\geq\Delta(Q)-\Delta(\|Q\|)=\Delta^{\prime}(Q).

(121)

Thus,
\Delta^{\prime}
is also a monotone homomorphism, and we may replace
\Delta
with
\Delta^{\prime}
. This means that we are free to assume that
\Delta(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Step 2: General form.

Next, we derive the general form of the monotone derivations. First, we derive the action of
\Delta
on individual normalized columns. Since
\Delta
satisfies the axioms of an entropy functional, Proposition
2.13
implies the existence of a finite positive measure
\tau\colon\mathcal{B}([0,+\infty])\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for a probability vector
P_{X}

\Delta(P_{X})=\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X})\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\,.

(122)

Combining Lemma
3.3
with the additivity and monotonicity of
\Delta
, along with the observations made above, we now obtain the following expression for any joint probability distribution
P_{XY}

\displaystyle\Delta(P_{XY})

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Delta(P_{X,Y=y})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Delta\big(P_{Y}(y)P_{X|Y=y}\big)

(123)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\underbrace{\Delta\big(P_{Y}(y)\big)}_{=0}\|P_{X|Y=y}\|_{1}+P_{Y}(y)\Delta(P_{X|Y=y})

(124)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\Delta(P_{X|Y=y})

(125)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha).

(126)

Hence, we obtain the final form for the derivations

\Delta(P_{XY})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha).

(127)

Note that the additivity of
\Delta
is immediate given the form in (
127
). It is also straightforward to verify that the form above ensures
\Delta
satisfies the Leibniz rule. Explicitly, if we denote
R_{XX^{\prime}YY^{\prime}}:=P_{XY}\otimes Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
, the additivity
of the Rényi entropies implies that

\displaystyle\Delta(P_{XY}\otimes Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\Delta(R_{XX^{\prime}YY^{\prime}})

(128)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}R_{YY^{\prime}}(y,y^{\prime})\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}\big(R_{XX^{\prime}|YY^{\prime}=(y,y^{\prime})}\big)\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)

(129)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}P_{Y}(y)Q_{Y^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}\big(P_{X|Y=y}\otimes Q_{X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\big)\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)

(130)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in Y}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}P_{Y}(y)Q_{Y^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}\big(P_{X|Y=y}\big)\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)

(131)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}P_{Y}(y)Q_{Y^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}\big(Q_{X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\big)\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)

(132)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\|Q_{Y^{\prime}}\|_{1}}_{=\|Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}\|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}\big(P_{X|Y=y}\big)\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)

(133)

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\|P_{Y}\|_{1}}_{=\|P_{XY}\|}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}Q_{Y^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}\big(Q_{X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\big)\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)

(134)

\displaystyle=\Delta(P_{XY})\|Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}\|+\|P_{XY}\|\Delta(Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}).

(135)

Finally, note that the normalization condition
\Delta\big(P_{X}\otimes(1/2,1/2)\big)=1
implies that the positive measure
\tau
must be a probability measure.

Step 3: Extremal derivations.
Finally, we identify the extremal derivations that generate the entire set of derivations via convex combinations. For
\alpha\in[0,+\infty]
, let us consider the derivations (or conditional entropies)

H_{0,\alpha}(P)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\,.

(136)

From equation (
127
), we observe that any derivation
\Delta
can be written as

\Delta(P)=\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{0,\alpha}(P)\,d\tau(\alpha)

(137)

for some probability measure
\tau
. Hence, each derivation is a convex combination (or, rather, a barycentre) of the extremal derivations
H_{0,\alpha}(P)
.
Consequently, since our goal is to characterize the extremal elements of the set—in order to express the general form of conditional entropies through barycentric (i.e., convex) decompositions—it suffices to consider the derivations

H_{0,\alpha}
with
\alpha\in[0,+\infty]
.
∎

4
Sufficient conditions for the parameters

In this section, we establish sufficient conditions for the parameters under which the homomorphisms and derivations (and, consequently, the associated conditional entropies) are monotone under conditional majorization. In the following section, we show that these conditions are also necessary under additional assumptions. These assumptions include, as special cases, discrete measures with finite support and measures that vanish sufficiently fast as
\alpha\to 1
.

In the previous sections, we derived the general form of monotone homomorphisms and derivations, both of which depend on a choice of positive measure. However, not all such choices are valid, as some lead to violations of monotonicity under conditionally mixing channels. In particular, as we show later, while monotonicity under doubly stochastic maps always holds, monotonicity under channels acting on the conditioning system fails for some choices of the parameters. To characterize the admissible cases, we examine the convexity properties of the homomorphism as a function of the joint probability matrix—a standard and often more tractable approach. Indeed, as discussed in Appendix
A
, convexity and monotonicity under conditionally mixing channels are essentially equivalent. Moreover, it suffices to establish the convexity properties required to ensure monotonicity under channels acting on the conditioning system, which form a subclass of conditionally mixing channels as characterized in Lemma
A.3
. Indeed, monotonicity under doubly stochastic maps on the first system is readily verified for all values of the parameters.

Let us first identify the function for which we need to establish convexity or concavity properties. We observe that, in the temperate case, the homomorphisms take the form (
94
)

\displaystyle\Phi(P_{XY})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{t,\tau}(P_{XY})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\exp{\left(t\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\right)}\,,

(138)

where
\tau
is a probability measure, the normalized version of the measure
\mu
of (
94
), and
t=\mu\big([0,+\infty]\big)\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. As detailed in Appendix
A
, we need to analyze the convexity properties of the function
P_{XY}\mapsto\Phi(P_{XY})
.
Therefore, in the following analysis, it suffices to study the convexity properties of the function defined for
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
by

\vec{x}\longmapsto\|\vec{x}\|_{1}\exp\left(t\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\right)\,,

(139)

where
\|\vec{x}\|_{1}=\sum_{i}x_{i}
and
\hat{x}=\vec{x}/\|\vec{x}\|_{1}
. We begin with the discrete case, where the measure is supported on a finite set of points. We then extend these results to a general measure by constructing a suitable discrete approximation and taking the limit in which the approximation converges to the original measure. Indeed, convexity and monotonicity properties are preserved under taking limits.

Finally, at the end of the section, we establish sufficient conditions for both the tropical case and the derivations by taking limits of the conditions obtained earlier for the temperate case. Indeed, these two cases can be viewed as limiting regimes of the temperate case, where the total weight of the measure
t
diverges to infinity or vanishes to zero, respectively.

4.2
Sufficient conditions for the temperate homomorphisms - general measure

In this section, we extend the convexity result to the case where
\tau
is a general probability measure. The idea is to approximate
\tau
by a sequence of finitely supported probability measures
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, obtained by discretizing the intervals with increasingly fine partitions. Convexity holds for each
\tau_{n}
by the result from the previous section. Since
(\tau_{n})
converges to
\tau
(in a suitable sense), and convexity is preserved under pointwise limits, the result carries over to the continuous case. It turns out that the condition

\int_{[0,+\infty]}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\leq\frac{1}{t}

(161)

is crucial. Here, we adopt the convention that the integral equals
+\infty
if the measure
\tau
has support at
\alpha=1
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\tau:\mathcal{B}\big([0,+\infty]\big)\to\mathbb{R}
be a probability measure,
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. The function defined on
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}

\vec{x}\longmapsto\|\vec{x}\|_{1}\exp\left(t\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\right)

(162)

where
\hat{x}=\vec{x}/\|\vec{x}\|_{1}
is

1.

concave if
t>0
,
\;\textup{supp}(\tau)\subseteq[0,1]
, and (
161
) holds and

2.

convex if

and either

(a)

\textup{supp}(\tau)\subseteq[0,1]
or

(b)

there is
\alpha_{*}\in(1,+\infty]
such that

and (

) holds.

Note that in the first case of

may have support at
\alpha=1

t<0
and for
t>0
the measure cannot have support at
\alpha=1
. Indeed, our convention implies that the integral diverges, and consequently, the bound in (

Proof.

We begin by considering the first case. The idea is to construct a discrete approximation that enables the application of the result from the previous section for each finite level of the approximation. We then take the limit as the approximation becomes increasingly fine, observing that convexity is preserved in the limit.

To show that the approximation converges to the desired function, we apply the dominated or monotone convergence theorem, although, as the functions that we study are monotone, this convergence is rather a simple fact. For this, we rely on the monotonicity of both the Rényi entropies
\alpha\mapsto H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})
and the function
\alpha\mapsto\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
. Our goal is to apply the monotone convergence theorem to approximate the integral using a sequence of discrete functions
f^{(N)}

\int_{[0,1]}f(\alpha)\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{[0,1]}f^{(N)}(\alpha)\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha).

(163)

To invoke this theorem, we must construct a sequence of functions
(f^{(N)})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
that satisfy the monotonicity assumption: each
f^{(N)}
takes only finitely many values (i.e.,
f^{(N)}
is a simple function), there is a
\tau
-integrable function
g
such that
f^{(N)}(\alpha)\leq g(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and converges pointwise, that is,
f^{(N)}(\alpha)\to f(\alpha)
as
N\to\infty
.

We want to construct monotone (or at least dominated) pointwise approximations for both the integral
\int_{[0,1]}H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)
and the constraint
\int_{[0,1]}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\leq 1/t
. For the latter one, because of the inequality involved, we want to use the monotone convergence theorem and approach to the integral from below. As we show below, the monotonicity of the approximations of the latter constraint is needed to ensure that the condition on the parameters for a discrete measure in Lemma
4.3
is always satisfied, so that we can apply the result to each discrete approximation and eventually take the limit.
Note that the assumption on the measure implies
\tau(\{1\})=0
, which is why we include
\alpha=1
in the integration interval.

The discrete approximation
f^{(N)}
is done using a staircase construction.
For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\{b_{n}^{(N)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a strictly increasing sequence with
b_{1}^{(N)}=0
.
The staircase approximation is then given by

f^{(N)}(\alpha):=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}b_{n+1}^{(N)}\chi_{f^{-1}\big([b_{n}^{(N)},b_{n+1}^{(N)})\big)}(\alpha),

(164)

Note that we are converging to
f
from the above; for the integral involving
\alpha\mapsto\alpha/(1-\alpha)
we will approach from below. Here
\chi_{A}(\alpha)
is the characteristic function of a measurable set
A\subseteq[0,1]
, defined by

\chi_{A}(\alpha)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\alpha\in A,\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(165)

and
f^{-1}[b_{n},b_{n+1})
is the preimage of the interval
[b_{n},b_{n+1})
, that is,

f^{-1}\big([b_{n},b_{n+1})\big):=\{\alpha\in[0,1]\mid f(\alpha)\in[b_{n},b_{n+1})\}.

(166)

Hence, by the dominated convergence theorem, we obtain the approximation

\int_{[0,1]}f(\alpha)\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}b_{n}^{(N)}\tau\left(f^{-1}\left([b_{n+1}^{(N)},b_{n+1}^{(N)})\right)\right).

(167)

In our case, we set
f=\mathrm{ev}_{\hat{x}}
, defined by
\mathrm{ev}_{\hat{x}}(\alpha):=H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})
. In this case,
f\leq H_{0}(\hat{x})
and, as
\tau
is a probability measure, the constant upper bound is
\tau
-integrable, and we may use the dominated convergence theorem.
Hence, the staircase approximation yields a rigorous way to evaluate the integral
\int_{[0,1]}H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)
using only simple functions.

We now use the result of the previous section for finite approximation to establish the result for the general case.
Possibly excluding a few initial indices

\displaystyle I^{(N)}_{n}={\rm ev}_{\hat{p}}^{-1}\big(\big[b^{(N)}_{n},b^{(N)}_{n+1}\big)\big)=\big[\alpha^{(N)}_{n},\alpha^{(N)}_{n+1}\big)\quad\text{for}\;\;n=1,\ldots,N-2

(168)

\displaystyle I^{(N)}_{N-1}={\rm ev}_{\hat{p}}^{-1}\big([b^{(N)}_{N-1},+\infty\big)\big)=\big[\alpha^{(N)}_{N-1},1\big)

(169)

With these definitions, the above convergence arguments imply that
\Phi_{N}(\vec{x})\to\Phi(\vec{x})
as

where

\Phi_{N}(\vec{x}):=\|\vec{x}\|_{1}\exp{\left(t\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}b_{n}^{(N)}\tau\big(I^{(N)}_{n}\big)\right)}.

(170)

Denoting
\tau^{(N)}_{n}:=\tau\big(I^{(N)}_{n}\big)
, and
\beta^{(N)}_{n}:=\tau^{(N)}_{n}t\alpha^{(N)}_{n}/(1-\alpha^{(N)}_{n})

\displaystyle\Phi_{N}(\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\|\vec{x}\|_{1}\exp{\left(t\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau^{(N)}_{n}b^{(N)}_{n}\right)}

(171)

\displaystyle=\|\vec{x}\|_{1}\exp{\left(t\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau^{(N)}_{n}H_{\alpha^{(N)}_{n}}(\hat{x})\right)}

(172)

\displaystyle=\|\vec{x}\|_{1}\exp{\left(t\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{\tau^{(N)}_{n}}{1-\alpha^{(N)}_{n}}\log{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\hat{x}_{i}^{\alpha^{(N)}_{n}}}\right)}

(173)

\displaystyle=\|\vec{x}\|_{1}\prod_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\hat{x}_{i}^{\alpha^{(N)}_{n}}\right)^{\beta^{(N)}_{n}/\alpha^{(N)}_{n}}

(174)

\displaystyle=\|\vec{x}\|_{1}^{\beta^{(N)}_{0}}\prod_{n=1}^{N-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{\alpha^{(N)}_{n}}\right)^{\beta^{(N)}_{n}/\alpha^{(N)}_{n}}

(175)

where
\beta_{0}^{(N)}:=1-\beta^{(N)}_{1}-\cdots-\beta^{(N)}_{N}
and in the last line, we used the relation between the normalized and unnormalized vectors, namely
\hat{x}_{i}=x_{i}/\|\vec{x}\|_{1}

To prove concavity, we need to show that for two vectors
\vec{w}
and
\vec{z}
such that

\vec{x}=t\vec{w}+(1-t)\vec{z}

\Phi(\vec{x})\geq t\Phi(\vec{w})+(1-t)\Phi(\vec{z}).

(176)

As done for
\Phi(\vec{x})
above, we define similar approximations
\Phi_{N}(\vec{w})
and
\Phi_{N}(\vec{z})
for
\Phi(\vec{w})
and
\Phi_{N}(\vec{z})
. Using the form (
175
) and Lemma
4.3
for discrete measures, we obtain that

\Phi_{N}(\vec{x})\geq t\Phi_{N}(\vec{w})+(1-t)\Phi_{N}(\vec{z})

(177)

for

\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{\alpha^{(N)}_{n}}{1-\alpha^{(N)}_{n}}\tau_{n}^{(N)}\leq\frac{1}{t}\,.

(178)

We then take the limit on both sides of the above inequality to establish the desired statement. Moreover, the condition on the measure becomes

\int_{[0,1]}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\leq\frac{1}{t}\,.

(179)

Indeed, we may once again apply the monotone convergence theorem by observing that the function
f:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, given by
f(\alpha)=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
, is non-decreasing. Consider the approximation
f^{(N)}:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined as

f^{(N)}=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{\alpha_{n}^{(N)}}{1-\alpha_{n}^{(N)}}\chi_{I_{n}^{(N)}}

(180)

which satisfies
f^{(N)}\leq f
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{\tau_{n}^{(N)}\alpha_{n}^{(N)}}{1-\alpha_{n}^{(N)}}=\int_{[0,1)}f^{(N)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\int_{[0,1]}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\leq 1/t\,.

(181)

The second case follows from a similar argument.

The final case is likewise analogous, where we isolate the point mass at
\alpha_{*}>1
and apply the same approximation to the part of the measure supported on
[0,1)
. It is worthwhile to note that case
22(a)
can be seen as a limiting argument from
22(b)
when
\alpha_{*}\to 1^{+}
: We let
\alpha_{*}>1
with
\tau(\{\alpha_{k}\})>0
converge to 1. At some point, when
\alpha_{*}
is close enough to 1, the integral condition in
22(b)
becomes trivial. We also get the case
\alpha_{*}=\infty
in
22(b)
through a limit argument.
∎

4.3
Monotonicity under doubly stochastic maps

As mentioned at the beginning of this section, monotonicity under doubly stochastic maps acting on the first register

always holds, and its proof is straightforward.
Indeed, since the exponential function is monotone and the map

P_{X}\mapsto\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)

(182)

is monotone under doubly stochastic maps (as each
H_{\alpha}(P_{X})
individually is), it follows directly from the explicit form of
\Phi
in (

) that
\Phi
is monotone as well.

Hence, under the above condition on the measure, Lemma
A.3
ensures that the monotone homomorphisms are monotone under conditionally mixing channels as required for a conditional entropy in Definition
2.11
.

4.4
Sufficient conditions for tropical homorphisms

In this section, we establish sufficient conditions on the measure for the tropical homomorphisms. The key observation is that monotonicity under conditionally mixing channels is preserved under limits; that is, the limit of a monotone quantity remains monotone. This is because taking limits on both sides of an inequality does not alter its direction. Consequently, the convexity and concavity conditions established above, which are equivalent to monotonicity for temperate homomorphisms (see Appendix
A
), provide the basis for extending monotonicity properties to tropical homomorphisms.

In the following, we establish a monotonicity result for the conditional entropies that can be constructed from the tropical homomorphisms. We start with the case
\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\rm op}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\tau:\mathcal{B}\big([0,+\infty]\big)\to\mathbb{R}
be a probability measure. Then, the function

\displaystyle P_{XY}\longmapsto

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{Y}\\
P_{Y}(y)>0\end{subarray}}\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)

(183)

is monotone under conditionally mixing channels acting on
P_{XY}
if one of the following holds:

(a)

\;\textup{supp}(\tau)\subseteq[0,1]
.

(b)

\;\operatorname{supp}(\tau)\subseteq[0,1]\cup\{\alpha_{*}\}
,
\;\alpha_{*}\in(1,+\infty]
,
\;\displaystyle\int_{[0,+\infty]}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\leq 0
.

Note that the result below, according to Proposition
A.1
, implies the quasi-convexity of the tropical homomorphisms in
\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\rm op}
.

Proof.

The conditional entropies constructed from the tropical homomorphisms can be obtained by letting
t\to-\infty
. Thus, to obtain sufficient conditions, it is enough to take these limits and verify which of the conditions we derived in the previous sections on the measure continue to hold.

Explicitly, we have the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to-\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\exp\left(t\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}\big(P_{X|Y=y}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\right)\right)

(184)

\displaystyle\hskip 150.00023pt=\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{Y}\\
P_{Y}(y)>0\end{subarray}}\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}\big(P_{X|Y=y}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\,.

(185)

Let us start with the first case, i.e., we want to show that monotonicity holds whenever the probability measure
\mu
satisfies
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\subseteq[0,1]
. This we get directly when we combine the limit in (
184
) to case
22(a)
of Proposition
4.4
.

Let us now turn to the second case.
In this case, we assume that the conditions of item
2(b)
of the claim hold for a probability measure
\mu
.
It is straightforward to see that for sufficiently large
|t|
as
t\rightarrow-\infty
, the following condition
22(b)
on convexity in Proposition
4.4
holds:

t<0,\;\operatorname{supp}(\tau)\subseteq[0,1]\cup\{\alpha_{*}\},\;\alpha_{*}\in(1,+\infty],\;\int_{[0,+\infty]}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\leq\frac{1}{t}\,.

(186)

Hence, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Next, we turn to
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

The function
P_{XY}\longmapsto\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}H_{0}(P_{X|Y=y})

is monotone under conditionally mixing channels acting on
P_{XY}
.

Proof.

Also in this case, the proof follows by a limit argument. We have the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\exp\left(tH_{0}\big(P_{X|Y=y}\big)\,\right)\right)=\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{Y}\\
P_{Y}(y)>0\end{subarray}}H_{0}\big(P_{X|Y=y}\big)\,.

(187)

The result then follows by combining the limit in (
187
) with case (
1
) for
\alpha=0
in Proposition
4.4
, together with the fact that the limit of a monotone quantity remains monotone.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

We note that unless
\alpha=0
, the condition (
1
) in Proposition
4.4

cannot be used to obtain an alternative set of sufficient conditions for
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
, since the limit
t\to+\infty
eventually violates the integral constraint. Since such limits give rise to monotone homomorphisms in
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
, this observation is consistent with the fact that only the monotone homomorphism with
\alpha=0
is admissible in
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
as established in Section
3.5
.

4.5
Sufficient conditions for derivations

We now turn to the derivations.

Proposition 4.8
.

The function

\displaystyle P_{XY}\longmapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\,,

(188)

is monotone under conditionally mixing channels acting on
P_{XY}
if
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

According to Lemma
A.2
, which shows that convexity and monotonicity are equivalent, the above monotonicity result also establishes the convexity of the function.

Proof.

The derivations can be obtained by choosing a single point measure and letting the total weight of the measure tend to
0
. Thus, to obtain sufficient conditions, it is enough to take these limits and verify which of the conditions we derived in the previous sections on the measure continue to hold.

Explicitly, we have the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\log\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\exp\left(tH_{\alpha}\big(P_{X|Y=y}\big)\right)\right)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)H_{\alpha}\big(P_{X|Y=y}\big)\,.

(189)

We want to show that monotonicity holds whenever
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
The monotonicity in this case can be recovered from the conditions on the temperate case in Proposition
4.4
, item
22(a)
by considering the limit
t\to 0
. Explicitly, we need to choose a single point measure concentrated at
\alpha=1
.
We next apply the convexity conditions from Proposition
4.4
, item
22(a)
, which are equivalent to monotonicity, to the measure
\tau
.
Combining these conditions with the limit in (
189
), and using that monotonicity is preserved under limits, we obtain monotonicity whenever
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
∎

The results of this section thus single out the admissible homomorphisms and derivations which, up to logarithmic and constant factors, coincide with the admissible conditional entropies. These extremal conditional entropies will later appear in Theorem
7.1
, which states the general form of conditional entropies.

5
Necessary conditions for the parameters

In this section, we show that the sufficient conditions on the parameters derived in the previous section that ensure monotonicity under conditionally mixing channels are also necessary, provided some additional assumptions hold. In particular, we assume that the measure vanishes as
\alpha\to 1
faster than
1/(1-\alpha)
; this class of measures includes, as a special case, discrete measures with finite support. Although these regularity assumptions are required to make our analysis rigorous, we expect—as discussed in the later sections—that the sufficient conditions are in fact necessary in full generality.

Our approach consists in identifying an explicit perturbation direction and computing the second derivative of the function along this direction. By analyzing the sign of the second derivative, we show that outside the range specified in Proposition
A.1
, the function fails to exhibit the expected convexity or concavity (which is equivalent to monotonicity under conditionally mixing channels).
Finally, we also note that the problem is inherently challenging: obtaining a sharp characterization of the admissible range requires constructing counterexamples in increasingly high dimensions. Indeed, our numerical investigations indicate that no such violations occur in low-dimensional settings. While the proofs are rather intricate, the underlying idea is simple. We therefore provide a sketch of the proof strategy used to establish the necessary conditions.

Proof idea
. The main idea is to leverage the counterexamples for the convexity properties of the function

\vec{v}\longmapsto v_{1}^{\beta_{0}}v_{2}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots v_{d}^{\beta_{d}}

(190)

with
\beta_{0}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}
. Such counterexamples can be found in
(
35
, Lemma 8)
(see also
(
13
, Proposition 13)
). In the previous section, we argued that it is sufficient to study the convexity properties of the function

\vec{x}\longmapsto\|\vec{x}\|_{1}\exp\left(t\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\right)\,.

(191)

This function is connected to the one defined in equation
190
whenever the measure
\tau
is discrete, under the identification

\displaystyle\beta_{i}\rightarrow\tau(\{\alpha_{i}\})t\frac{\alpha_{i}}{1-\alpha_{i}}\,,\qquad v_{i}\rightarrow\left(\sum_{j}x_{j}^{\alpha_{i}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}}\,.

(192)

The key difference is that, while the variables
v_{i}
can be chosen independently in the first case, the terms
\bigl(\sum_{j}x_{j}^{\alpha_{i}}\bigr)^{1/\alpha_{i}}
are coupled: varying
\vec{x}
simultaneously affects all of them. Nevertheless, one can control the variations of
\vec{x}
so that they influence only a subset of these terms. This is achieved by constructing instances in very large dimensions. Concretely, consider appending a
d
-dimensional tail with weight
1/d
to the vector
\vec{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
, i.e., consider the vector

\vec{y}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\underbrace{1/d,\ldots,1/d}_{d\ \text{times}})\,.

(193)

It is easy to verify that the additional tail affects only the terms with
\alpha_{i}<1
(which diverge), while leaving those with
\alpha_{i}>1
little affected. Indeed, we have that

\left(\sum_{j}y_{j}^{\alpha_{i}}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}^{\alpha_{i}}+d^{\,1-\alpha_{i}}\right)^{\tfrac{1}{\alpha_{i}}}\;\;\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{}\;\;\begin{cases}+\infty,&\alpha_{i}<1,\\[6.0pt]
\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}^{\alpha_{i}},&\alpha_{i}>1.\end{cases}

(194)

Below, we establish a set of necessary conditions under the assumption that the measure satisfies the following property.

Assumption 1
.

The positive/negative measure
\nu:\mathcal{B}\big([0,+\infty]\big)\to\mathbb{R}
(typically
\nu=t\tau
) satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{[0,1)}\nu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right|<+\infty\,,\quad\left|\int_{(1,+\infty]}\nu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right|<+\infty\,.

(195)

As mentioned earlier, this assumption requires the measure to vanish faster than
1/(1-\alpha)
as
\alpha\to 1
, since otherwise the relevant integrals would diverge. In particular, this class of measures includes, as a special case, discrete measures with finite support, for which the corresponding integrals are finite.

5.1
Necessary conditions; temperate case

The temperate case corresponds to the bulk entropies
H_{t,\tau}
.
As discussed at the beginning of Section
4
, it is sufficient to study the convexity properties of the function

\vec{x}\longmapsto\|\vec{x}\|_{1}\exp\left(t\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\right)

(196)

with a probability measure
\tau
and
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. For notational convenience, we will often combine the weight
t
with the probability measure
\tau
and treat their product as a positive/negative measure (i.e., a generally unnormalized finite measure that may take either positive or negative values depending on the sign of
t
). We denote this measure by
\mu
, defined as

\displaystyle\mu:=t\,\tau\,.

(197)

We also define the coefficient

\beta_{0}=1-\int_{[0,+\infty]\setminus\{1\}}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\,.

(198)

Since the proof is rather involved, we divide the argument into several propositions.
We first start with the case where the measure
\mu
is positive, or equivalently, the
\mathbb{R}_{+}
case.

1.

In Proposition
5.1
, we show that the measure cannot have support on values
\alpha>1
, and the total weight on points with
\alpha<1
must be bounded.

We then consider the case where the measure is negative, or equivalently, the
\mathbb{R}^{\rm opp}_{+}
case.

1.

In Proposition
5.2
, we show that if the measure is supported at
\alpha=1
, then no support for
\alpha>1
is allowed. This solves the case when the measure has support at
\alpha=1
.

2.

In Proposition
5.3
, we show that if the measure vanishes at
\alpha=1
and
\beta_{0}>0
, then it cannot have support on points with
\alpha>1
.

3.

In Proposition
5.6
, we show that if the measure is zero at
\alpha=1
, and
\beta_{0}\leq 0
, then the measure cannot have support on more than one point
\alpha>1
.

Together, these results show that the sufficient conditions derived in the previous section are tight under Assumption
1
. A more detailed discussion is given in Section
7.2
.

We begin with the case where
t>0
, i.e.,
\mu>0
. In this case, we demonstrate that the measure cannot be supported on values
\alpha\geq 1
, and that the total weight on points with
\alpha\leq 1
must be bounded.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\mu:\mathcal{B}\big([0,+\infty]\big)\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive measure that satisfies Assumption
1
. Then, the function

\vec{x}\longmapsto\|\vec{x}\|_{1}\exp\left(\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\right)\,,

(199)

where
\hat{x}=\vec{x}/\|\vec{x}\|_{1}
,
is concave only if
\;\textup{supp}(\mu)\subseteq[0,1]
, and
\;\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\leq 1
.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to construct a point and a perturbation direction along which the second derivative of the function becomes strictly positive whenever the condition on the measure from the above proposition is violated. This contradicts concavity, since for a concave function, the second derivative must be non-positive in all directions. To achieve this, we explicitly construct a counterexample in increasingly high dimensions and show that such violations can indeed be found in the high-dimensional regime. Specifically, we first show that the measure cannot have support for
\alpha\geq 1
. Then, we establish that the measure’s weight on
\alpha<1
must be bounded. Both results are proved using the same perturbation. The assumption ensures that the resulting integrals in the analysis are all finite.

First, we introduce the vector
\vec{x}=\hat{x}\|\vec{x}\|_{1}
and split the integral over the range
[0,+\infty]
into several parts

\displaystyle f(\vec{x})=\left(\sum_{i}x_{i}\right)^{\beta_{0}}\exp\Bigg(\int_{[0,1)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)H_{\alpha}(\vec{x})\,+\int_{(1,+\infty)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)H_{\alpha}(\vec{x})\,

\displaystyle\quad\;\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\hskip 50.00008pt+\mu(1)\sum_{i}H_{1}(\hat{x})+\mu(+\infty)H_{\infty}(\hat{x})\Bigg)\,,

(200)

where
\beta_{0}=1-\int_{[0,1)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}-\int_{(1,+\infty)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
. Note that these integrals are well-defined because of Assumption
1
. Here,

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}(\vec{x})=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log{\sum_{i}x_{i}^{\alpha}}\,,\quad H_{1}(\hat{x})=-\sum_{i}\frac{x_{i}}{\sum_{j}x_{j}}\log\left(\frac{x_{i}}{\sum_{j}x_{j}}\right)\,\quad H_{\infty}(\hat{x})=-\log\max_{i}\frac{x_{i}}{\sum_{j}x_{j}}\,.

(201)

To lighten our notations, we denoted
\mu(\alpha):=\mu(\{\alpha\})
for
\alpha\in[0,+\infty]
.
For constant
p\in(0,1)
, we define the point
\vec{x}
and the direction
\vec{v}

\displaystyle\vec{x}=\bigg[\underbrace{\frac{p}{d},\ldots,\frac{p}{d}}_{d\,\textrm{times}},\underbrace{\frac{1-p}{d^{2}},\ldots,\frac{1-p}{d^{2}}}_{d^{2}\,\textrm{times}}\bigg]\,,\quad\vec{v}=c\bigg[\underbrace{-\frac{1}{d},\ldots,-\frac{1}{d}}_{d\,\textrm{times}},\underbrace{\frac{1}{d^{2}},\ldots,\frac{1}{d^{2}}}_{d^{2}\,\textrm{times}}\bigg]

(202)

Here, the constant
c>0
ensures that
p-c>0
as we send
p\rightarrow 0
. Indeed, we can rewrite
\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v}=(1-\lambda)\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v}^{\prime}
, where
\vec{v}^{\prime}=\vec{x}+\vec{v}
. To ensure that this is a valid convex combination in the semiring, we require that
\vec{v}^{\prime}\geq 0
, i.e.,
\vec{v}^{\prime}
must be an admissible element of the semiring. Nevertheless, the factor

c
only contributes an overall constant and does not affect the second derivative. Moreover, since we consider the limit of an infinitesimal perturbation, this constant is not needed and will therefore be omitted in the analysis.

By substituting the chosen point and direction, we have that

\displaystyle f(\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v})=\exp\Bigg(

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log{\left(d^{1-\alpha}(p-\lambda)^{\alpha}+d^{2(1-\alpha)}(1-p+\lambda)^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle+\int_{(1,+\infty)}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log{\left(d^{1-\alpha}(p-\lambda)^{\alpha}+d^{2(1-\alpha)}(1-p+\lambda)^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\qquad\qquad\;\;\qquad\qquad+\mu(1)h_{3}(\lambda)-\mu(+\infty)\log g_{3}(\lambda)\Bigg)\,.

(203)

Here, we defined the functions

\displaystyle h_{3}(\lambda)=\left(-(p-\lambda)\log{\frac{p-\lambda}{d}}-(1-p+\lambda)\log{\frac{1-p+\lambda}{d^{2}}}\right)\,,

(204)

\displaystyle g_{3}(\lambda)=\max\left\{\frac{p-\lambda}{d},\frac{1-p+\lambda}{d^{2}}\right\}=\frac{p-\lambda}{d}\,,

(205)

where the second holds for large enough
d
.
The derivatives of the latter functions are

\displaystyle h_{3}^{\prime}(\lambda)=\log{\frac{(p-\lambda)d}{1-p+\lambda}}\,,\quad h_{3}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=-\left(\frac{1}{p-\lambda}+\frac{1}{1-p+\lambda}\right)

(206)

\displaystyle g_{3}^{\prime}(\lambda)=-\frac{1}{d}\,,\quad g_{3}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=0

(207)

Moreover, we have the asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle h_{3}(0)\rightarrow(2-p)\log{d}\,,\;h_{3}^{\prime}(0)\rightarrow\;\log{d}\,,\;h_{3}^{\prime\prime}(0)\rightarrow-\left(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{1-p}\right)\,.

(208)

Furthermore, if we define the functions

\displaystyle g_{1}(\lambda)=\left(\varepsilon^{1-\alpha}(p-\lambda)^{\alpha}+(1-p+\lambda)^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

(209)

\displaystyle g_{2}(\lambda)=\left((p-\lambda)^{\alpha}+\varepsilon^{\alpha-1}(1-p+\lambda)^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

(210)

and we set
\varepsilon=1/d
. Note that, up to constant factors that do not affect the sign of the derivative, the function becomes

\displaystyle f(\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v})=\exp\Bigg(

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log{g_{1}(\lambda)}+\int_{(1,+\infty)}\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log{g_{2}(\lambda)}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mu(1)h_{3}(\lambda)-\mu(+\infty)\log g_{3}(\lambda)\Bigg)\,.

(211)

Next, we compute the second derivative with respect to
\lambda
and show that if the condition on the measure in the above proposition is not satisfied, the second derivative is positive. This implies the existence of a convex direction, contradicting concavity. We use the formulas for the second derivatives of the exponential and the logarithm of a function

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{2}}\exp(h(\lambda))=\exp(h(\lambda))\left((h^{\prime}(\lambda))^{2}+h^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\right)\,,

(212)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{2}}\log(g(\lambda))=\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{g(\lambda)}-\left(\frac{g^{\prime}(\lambda)}{g(\lambda)}\right)^{2}\,.

(213)

A straightforward computation shows that the second derivative is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{2}}f(\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v})

(214)

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg(\int_{[0,1)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log{g_{1}(\lambda)}+\int_{(1,+\infty)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log{g_{2}(\lambda)}

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt-\mu(+\infty)\log g_{3}(\lambda)+\mu(1)h_{3}(\lambda)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigg[\left(\int_{[0,1)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{g^{\prime}_{1}(\lambda)}{g_{1}(\lambda)}+\int_{(1,+\infty)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{g^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)}{g_{2}(\lambda)}-\mu(+\infty)\frac{g_{3}^{\prime}(\lambda)}{g_{3}(\lambda)}+\mu(1)h_{3}^{\prime}(\lambda)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\;\;\qquad+\int_{[0,1)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\left(-\frac{g^{\prime}_{1}(\lambda)^{2}}{g_{1}(\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}_{1}(\lambda)}{g_{1}(\lambda)}\right)+\int_{(1,+\infty)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\left(-\frac{g^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda)^{2}}{g_{2}(\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}_{2}(\lambda)}{g_{2}(\lambda)}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 192.00026pt-\mu(+\infty)\left(-\frac{g^{\prime}_{3}(\lambda)^{2}}{g_{3}(\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}_{3}(\lambda)}{g_{3}(\lambda)}\right)+\mu(1)h_{3}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)\Bigg]\,.

(215)

Next, we evaluate the second derivative in
\lambda=0
and consider the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. We define the constants

\beta_{2}=\int_{[0.1)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\,,\quad\beta_{1}=\int_{(1,+\infty)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}-\mu(+\infty)

(216)

We obtain up to positive constants the asymptotic (small
\varepsilon
) behavior

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{2}}f(\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v})\right|_{\lambda=0}\rightarrow\left[\left(\mu(1)\log{d}-\frac{\beta_{1}}{p}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{1-p}\right)^{2}-\frac{\beta_{1}}{p^{2}}-\frac{\beta_{2}}{(1-p)^{2}}-\mu(1)\left(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{1-p}\right)\right]\,.

(217)

Note that we also invoked uniform convergence on the respective intervals, together with Assumption
1
, which is readily verified from the simple form of the functions inside the integrals.
We distinguish several cases. First, consider the case where
\mu(1)>0
. In this setting, taking the limit
d\to+\infty
yields an infinite and hence positive value, which provides the desired counterexample. Next, we consider the case
\mu(1)=0
. To show that no points
\alpha>1
are allowed in this case, we expand the expression above to obtain

\frac{\beta_{1}(\beta_{1}-1)}{p}+\frac{\beta_{2}(\beta_{2}-1)}{1-p}-2\frac{\beta_{1}\beta_{2}}{p(1-p)}\,.

(218)

We then take the limit
p\rightarrow 0
and obtain

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{2}}f(\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v})\right|_{\lambda=0}\rightarrow\frac{\beta_{1}(\beta_{1}-1)}{p}\,.

(219)

Since
\beta_{1}\leq 0
, the presence of any points
\alpha>1

Remark 5.4
.

In the proof of the proposition above, we considered a point
\vec{x}
that is not normalized, meaning
\sum_{i}x_{i}\neq 1
. This is justified because the function is defined on the entire domain
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d^{\prime}}
for some dimension
d^{\prime}
. Indeed, the semiring includes vectors that are not normalized, and within this broader space, counterexamples can still arise. By semiring theory, the existence of such counterexamples in this extended setting is sufficient to exclude the corresponding measure values in the barycentric decomposition of conditional entropies stated in Theorem
7.1
, even though the theorem itself concerns normalized probability distributions.
This conclusion relies on the fact that, without loss of generality, we may assume
\|\vec{x}\|_{1}=1
, since any vector
\vec{x}
can be rescaled by a positive constant, and such scaling does not affect convexity properties.

Remark 5.5
.

In the proof of the above proposition, we used a nontraceless direction, i.e. a direction
\vec{v}
such that
\sum_{i}v_{i}\neq 0
.
This is admissible because the function is defined on the whole domain
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d^{\prime}}
for some dimension
d^{\prime}
. In particular, the semiring is defined over the set of unnormalized joint matrices, within which counterexamples can be found. By semiring theory, the existence of such counterexamples in this broader space is sufficient to rule out the corresponding measure values in the barycentric decomposition of conditional entropies in Theorem
7.1
, even though the theorem itself is stated for normalized probability distributions.

This fact follows from the additivity property of homomorphisms, as we now explain. First, without loss of generality, we may assume that
\|\vec{x}\|_{1}<1
; indeed,
\vec{x}
can always be rescaled by a positive constant, and such a multiplicative factor does not affect convexity properties.
Next, consider a perturbation of
\vec{x}
in a direction
\vec{v}
with
\sum_{i}v_{i}\neq 0
, i.e., a direction that is not traceless. To handle this, we embed the problem into a larger space by introducing an additional outcome to the conditioning register
Y
. Instead of a single probability vector
\vec{x}
, we now consider a matrix with two columns,
\vec{x}
and
\vec{x}^{\prime}
, corresponding to a joint distribution over
X\times Y
.
In this extended space, by the additivity of the homomorphism
f
, the total function becomes
f(\vec{x})+f(\vec{x}^{\prime})
. Now, we perturb
\vec{x}
as
\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v}
, and simultaneously perturb
\vec{x}^{\prime}
as
\vec{x}^{\prime}+\lambda\hat{x}^{\prime}\sum_{i}v_{i}
. This ensures that the total weight of the joint system remains constant. Since
f
depends linearly on the normalization of
\vec{x}^{\prime}
, this adjustment does not affect any convexity analysis. Therefore, this construction allows us to effectively study perturbations of
\vec{x}
in directions
\vec{v}
with
\sum_{i}v_{i}\neq 0
, by embedding them into a trace-preserving framework.

5.2
Necessary conditions for derivations

Next, we turn to the necessary conditions for the (extremal) derivations.
These are defined as (see equation (
107
))

H_{0,\alpha}(P_{XY})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)H_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\,.

(264)

Hence, it is sufficient to consider the convexity properties of the function

\vec{x}\longmapsto\|\vec{x}\|_{1}H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})\,.

(265)

Next, we show that in order for the derivations to be monotone, it must be that

\alpha>1
. we note that, by Lemma
A.2
, monotonicity of derivations requires concavity.

Proposition 5.7
.

The function

\vec{x}\longmapsto\|\vec{x}\|_{1}H_{\alpha}(\hat{x})

(266)

is concave only if
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

We must show that the measure cannot have support on
\alpha>1
. By Lemma
A.2
, the derivations must be concave. Thus, we need to show that if the measure has support on
\alpha>1
, we can construct a convex direction, yielding a counterexample.

The point
\vec{x}
and direction
\vec{v}
are the same as in Proposition
5.1
. We define for

\displaystyle\vec{x}=\bigg[\underbrace{\frac{p}{d},\ldots,\frac{p}{d}}_{d\,\textrm{times}},\underbrace{\frac{1-p}{d^{2}},\ldots,\frac{1-p}{d^{2}}}_{d^{2}\,\textrm{times}}\bigg]\,,\quad\vec{v}=\bigg[\underbrace{-\frac{1}{d},\ldots,-\frac{1}{d}}_{d\,\textrm{times}},\underbrace{\frac{1}{d^{2}},\ldots,\frac{1}{d^{2}}}_{d^{2}\,\textrm{times}}\bigg]

(267)

We have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(x_{i}+\lambda v_{i})^{\alpha}=d^{1-\alpha}(p-\lambda)^{\alpha}+d^{2(1-\alpha)}(1-p+\lambda)^{\alpha}

(268)

Therefore, the function becomes

\displaystyle f(\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v})=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log{\left(d^{1-\alpha}(p-\lambda)^{\alpha}+d^{2(1-\alpha)}(1-p+\lambda)^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\,,

(269)

\displaystyle g(\lambda)=\left((p-\lambda)^{\alpha}+\varepsilon^{\alpha-1}(1-p+\lambda)^{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

(270)

and we set
\varepsilon=1/d
, we obtain up to the additive constant factors (that do not affect the derivatives since they are constant)
that the function becomes

\displaystyle f(\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v})=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\log{g(\lambda)}\,.

(271)

Next, we compute the second derivative with respect to
\lambda
and show that if the condition on the measure in the above proposition is violated, this derivative becomes positive. This yields a convex direction, contradicting concavity. We use the identity for the derivative of the logarithm of a function, namely

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{2}}\log(g(\lambda))=\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{g(\lambda)}-\left(\frac{g^{\prime}(\lambda)}{g(\lambda)}\right)^{2}\,.

(272)

The second derivative is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{2}}f(\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v})=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\left(-\frac{g^{\prime}(\lambda)^{2}}{g(\lambda)^{2}}+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{g(\lambda)}\right)\,.

(273)

\lambda=0

(274)

we obtain the large

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{2}}f(\vec{x}+\lambda\vec{v})\right|_{\lambda=0}\rightarrow-\frac{\beta}{p^{2}}\,.

(275)

which, since
\beta\leq 0
, is positive whenever

. This condition is equivalent to the measure having support on
\alpha>1
, thus yielding the desired convex direction. The case
\alpha=+\infty
is done in the same way.
∎

Large sample and catalytic conditional majorization

In this section, we present the large-sample and catalytic results, which serve as the main tools for proving the general form of a conditional entropy in Theorem
7.1
and will also underpin the applications discussed in Section
8
.
In particular, these results follow directly from Theorem
3.1
on large-sample and catalytic transformations for general semirings, applied to the semiring of conditional majorization, together with the characterization of monotone homomorphisms and derivations established in Section

As shown in the following section (see Theorem
7.1
), these homomorphisms and derivations coincide, up to a constant and a logarithmic factor, with the extremal conditional entropies that generate the entire family of conditional entropies through convex combinations.
Since conditional entropies, according to Definition
2.11
, require proper normalization and additivity under tensor products, we reformulate the monotone homomorphisms from Proposition

and the derivations from Proposition

.

Let

7
The set of conditional entropies

In this section, we present the main result of our work, namely the general characterization of conditional entropies in Theorem
7.1
. Specifically, we show that any conditional entropy defined as a function satisfying the axioms in Definition
2.11
can be expressed as a (generalized) convex combination of the entropies
H_{t,\tau}
and its limiting forms
H_{0,\alpha}
,
H_{-\infty,\tau}
and
H_{+\infty,0}
defined in
6.1
. In this sense, the conditional entropies
H_{t,\tau},H_{0,\alpha},H_{-\infty,\tau}
and
H_{+\infty,0}
constitute the extremal points of the set of the conditional entropies; for the extremality, see Theorem 14 of
21
. As discussed in Section
5
, these quantities fail to be monotone under conditionally mixing channels for some parameter values. Consequently, they do not qualify as conditional entropies—according to Definition
2.11
for arbitrary choices of the parameters. Our description of all conditional entropies has to be stated in terms of the abstract parameter sets
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bulk}}
,
\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{0}
whose exact identity is still unknown. However, we discuss substantial progress towards their full characterization in Section
7.2
. In Section
7.3
, we review more in depth how the conditional entropies discussed in the literature and also mentioned in the Introduction relate to our general framework.

7.1
The general form of conditional entropies

Let us note that the set of conditional entropies characterized in Definition
2.11
is convex. This means that, whenever
\mathbb{H}_{1}
and
\mathbb{H}_{2}
are conditional entropies (i.e., satisfy the postulates of Definition
2.11
) and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, then also
\mathbb{H}=\lambda\mathbb{H}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathbb{H}_{2}
,

\mathbb{H}(X|Y)_{P}=\lambda\mathbb{H}_{1}(X|Y)_{P}+(1-\lambda)\mathbb{H}_{2}(X|Y)_{P}\qquad\forall\,P=P_{XY}\,,

(286)

is a conditional entropy. Especially all convex combinations of the conditional entropies
H_{t,\tau}
,
(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm bulk}
, and
H_{-\infty,\tau}
,
\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}
, and their pointwise limits are also conditional entropies. This fact also extends to the generalized convex combinations of these quantities, i.e., barycentres over the set of these special conditional entropies with respect to any (inner and outer regular) probability measure. For the latter statement, we need the fact that the set of these special conditional entropies is a compact Hausdorff space with respect to the topology of pointwise convergence
(
16
, Proposition 8.5)
. The following theorem characterizing the total set of conditional entropies as described in Definition
2.11
tells us that any conditional entropy can be expressed as a generalized convex combination of the special conditional entropies characterized in this work.

Theorem 7.1

(General form of conditional entropies)
.

Let
\mathbb{H}
be a conditional entropy in the sense of Definition
2.11
.
Then

can be represented as a generalized convex combination of the conditional entropies

H_{t,\tau},H_{-\infty,\tau},H_{0,\alpha}
and
H_{+\infty,0}
,
introduced in Definition
6.1
with parameters
(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{bulk}}
,
\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}
, and
\alpha\in[0,1]
, respectively, for the first three families.

We do not give a full proof here, as this result is proven analogously to Proposition
2.13
and it is a special case of Theorem 7 of
21
. Let us however give a brief overview: Let us denote by
\mathcal{H}
the set of conditional entropies
H_{t,\tau}
,
H_{-\infty,\tau}
,
H_{0,\alpha}
, and
H_{+\infty,0}
as defined in (
276
), (
277
), and (
278
) of Definition
6.1
, where the parameters and measures satisfy the required monotonicity conditions. According to Proposition 8.5 of

, this set is a compact Hausdorff space when equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence. Just as in the proof of Proposition

Using this as a stepping stone, we may define a positive linear functional

8.1
Rate of transformation under conditional majorization

As a first application of our result, we show that the conditional entropies
H_{t,\tau}
determine the optimal transformation rate under conditionally mixing operations and isometries, thereby providing them with an operational interpretation as characterizing the number of output copies obtainable per input copy within this class of operations.

Specifically, given two joint probability distributions
P_{XY}
and
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
, we seek the largest
r\in\mathbb{R}
for which
P_{XY}^{\otimes n}\succeq Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}^{\otimes\lfloor rn\rfloor}
holds for all sufficiently large
n
, i.e., we want to determine the value

R(P_{XY}\rightarrow Q_{XY})=\;\sup\left\{r\geq 0\;\middle|\;P_{XY}^{\otimes n}\succeq Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}^{\otimes\lfloor rn\rfloor}\;\;\text{for large }n\right\}.

(301)

It is known that this problem is equivalent to the large sample problem (see e.g.
(
29
, Theorem 3.11)
). In particular, we use the necessary and sufficient conditions for large sample of Theorem
6.3
to obtain

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY}
be two joint probability distributions. Then,

R(P_{XY}\rightarrow Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}})=\inf\left\{\frac{H_{t,\tau}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}}{H_{t,\tau}(X|Y)_{P}}\Bigg|(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm{bulk}}\right\}\cup\left\{\frac{H_{-\infty,\tau}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}}{H_{-\infty,\tau}(X|Y)_{P}}\Bigg|\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}\right\},

(302)

Proof.

The proof follows a similar argument to that of
(
29
, Theorem 3.11)
, with the additional observation that the derivations
H_{0,\alpha}
and the tropical homomorphism
H_{+\infty,0}
can be excluded by taking the infimum in equation (
302
), since they are limits of
H_{t,\tau}
. Indeed, these derivations are monotone only for
\alpha\in[0,1]
, a region that can itself be viewed as the limit of
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\rm bulk}
(see discussion in Section
7.2
). The same argument also holds for
H_{+\infty,0}
. We also refer to the very similar proof of Theorem 5 of
48
.
∎

Let us remark that if Conjecture
7.5
holds, we may simplify the statement of Theorem
8.1
. Indeed, if the conjecture is true, the conditional entropies
H_{-\infty,\tau}
with
\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}
are all pointwise limits of
(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm bulk}
as
t\to-\infty
. This would imply the conjectural form

R(P_{XY}\rightarrow Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}})=\inf_{(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm bulk}}\frac{H_{t,\tau}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}}{H_{t,\tau}(X|Y)_{P}}

(303)

for the optimal rate.

8.2
Conditional channels under conservation conditions and an application to second laws of thermodynamics with side information

A doubly stochastic or mixing channels
S
are exactly those (column) stochastic matrices that fix a suitably large uniform distribution
U
, i.e.,
SU=U
. Conditionally mixing channels are thus conditionally uniformity-preserving channels. It is thus quite natural to consider a generalization of our results where the conserved primary distribution is not uniform
U
but some other fixed distribution
R
. For this purpose, let us introduce the following set of operations that generalize conditionally mixing channels.

Definition 8.2
.

Let us fix a finite random variable
X
together with the probability distribution
R_{X}
. A
conditioned
R_{X}
-preserving channel
has the form

P_{XY}\mapsto Q_{XY^{\prime}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}G^{(i)}P_{XY}D^{(i)}

(304)

where

1.

the matrices
G^{(i)}
are column-stochastic and
G^{(i)}R_{X}=R_{X}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
,

2.

the matrices
D^{(i)}
are substochastic for
i=1,\ldots,k
, and

3.

the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{k}D^{(i)}
is row-stochastic.

We are interested in conditions under which
P_{XY}
can be transformed into
Q_{XY^{\prime}}
catalytically by a conditionally
R_{X}
-preserving channel, with arbitrarily small error
\varepsilon
.
The error here is quantified with the total variation distance defined between joint probabilities
S_{XY}
and
T_{XY}
through

d(S_{XY},T_{XY})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{y\in Y}\big|S_{XY}(x,y)-T_{XY}(x,y)\big|.

(305)

We introduce the quantities
I_{t,\tau}
,

I_{t,\tau}(P_{XY}\|R_{X})=-\frac{1}{t}\log{\left(\sum_{y\in Y}P_{Y}(y)\exp{\left(-t\int_{[0,+\infty]}D_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y}\|R_{X})\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\right)}\right)}\,.

(306)

Here,
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
\tau
is a Borel probability measure on
[0,\infty]
. Moreover, the Rényi relative entropy for
\alpha=[0,+\infty]
is defined as

D_{\alpha}(P_{X}\|R_{X})=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log{\sum_{x\in X}P_{X}(x)^{\alpha}R_{X}(x)^{1-\alpha}}\,,

(307)

with the cases
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=+\infty
defined by the corresponding limits.
We also define
I_{-\infty,\tau}
as the pointwise limit of
I_{t,\tau}
for
t\to-\infty
. In the following, we provide a set of sufficient conditions under which a joint distribution can be transformed into another using conditioned preserving channels and the assistance of a catalyst.

Theorem 8.3
.

Let us denote the uniform distribution of any system
Z
by
U_{Z}
. Fix a finite probability distribution
R_{X}
with rational entries and assume that
P_{XY}
and
Q_{XY^{\prime}}
are joint probability distributions such that

\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{Y}\\
P_{Y}(y)>0\end{subarray}}|{\rm supp}(P_{X|Y=y})|<|{\rm supp}(R_{X})|,\quad Q_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\neq R_{X}\ \forall\,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}.

(308)

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exist joint probability distributions
Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}}
and
S^{\varepsilon}_{X_{\varepsilon}Y_{\varepsilon}}
, such that
d(Q_{XY^{\prime}},Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}})<\varepsilon
and there exists a conditionally
R_{X}\otimes U_{X_{\varepsilon}}
-preserving channel that maps
P_{XY}\otimes S^{\varepsilon}_{X\varepsilon Y\varepsilon}
into
Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}}\otimes S^{\varepsilon}_{X\varepsilon Y\varepsilon}
if

\displaystyle I_{t,\tau}(P_{XY}\|R_{X})

\displaystyle\geq I_{t,\tau}(Q_{XY^{\prime}}\|R_{X})\qquad\forall(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm bulk},

(309)

\displaystyle I_{-\infty,\tau}(P_{XY}\|R_{X})

\displaystyle\geq I_{-\infty,\tau}(Q_{XY^{\prime}}\|R_{X})\qquad\forall\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}.

(310)

Proof.

The proof follows a standard argument based on the so-called embedding map. Specifically, the conditions
I_{t,\tau}(P_{XY},R_{X})\geq I_{t,\tau}(Q_{XY^{\prime}},R_{X})
and
I_{-\infty,\tau}(P_{XY},R_{X})\geq I_{-\infty,\tau}(Q_{XY^{\prime}},R_{X})
are equivalent to the inequalities
H_{t,\tau}(\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}Y})\leq H_{t,\tau}(\widetilde{Q}_{\widetilde{X}Y^{\prime}})
and
H_{-\infty,\tau}(\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}Y})\leq H_{-\infty,\tau}(\widetilde{Q}_{\widetilde{X}Y^{\prime}})
, where
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{X}Y}
and
\widetilde{Q}_{\widetilde{X}Y^{\prime}}
are the embedded versions of
R_{XY}
and
Q_{XY^{\prime}}
, respectively, obtained via the application of the embedding map (see below for the formal definition).
This entropy inequality guarantees the existence of a catalyst and a conditionally mixing channel
that transforms
\widetilde{R}_{XY}
into
\widetilde{Q}_{XY^{\prime}}
according to Theorem
6.3
. We then construct the desired overall channel by composing the embedding map, the conditionally mixing channel, and the (left) inverse of the embedding map. By construction, as we argue later, this overall transformation is
R_{X}
preserving.

Let us denote the support size of
R_{X}
by
d
. By assumption,
R_{X}
has rational entries. Hence, there exists
D\in\mathbb{N}
and
g_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
with
D=\sum_{i=1}^{d}g_{i}
such that

R_{X}=\left(\frac{g_{1}}{D},\ldots,\frac{g_{d}}{D}\right)

(311)

up to eliminating possible zero entries from
R_{X}
. The embedding map
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\Gamma(p):=\Big(\underbrace{\frac{p_{1}}{g_{1}},\ldots,\frac{p_{1}}{g_{1}}}_{g_{1}\ \text{times}},\ldots,\underbrace{\frac{p_{d}}{g_{d}},\ldots,\frac{p_{k}}{g_{d}}}_{g_{d}\ \text{times}}\Big)\,.

(312)

This map has the crucial property of sending
R_{X}
to the uniform distribution
U_{D}:=\big(\tfrac{1}{D},\ldots,\tfrac{1}{D}\big)
. This allows us to apply the results from Section
6
on conditional majorization.
The left inverse
\Gamma^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

\Gamma^{-1}(q):=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{g_{1}}q_{i},\ \sum_{i=g_{1}+1}^{g_{1}+g_{2}}q_{i},\ \ldots,\ \sum_{i=g_{1}+\cdots+g_{k-1}+1}^{D}q_{i}\right),

(313)

It is straightforward to verify that (see e.g.,
(
6
, Lemma 13)
)

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(P_{X}\|R_{X})=D_{\alpha}(\Gamma(P_{X})\|U_{D})=\log{D}-H_{\alpha}(\Gamma(P_{X}))\,.

(314)

The properties of the embedding map applied to the system
X
imply that

\displaystyle I_{t,\tau}(P_{XY}\|R_{X})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{t}\log\Bigg(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\exp\Big(-t\int_{[0,+\infty]}D_{\alpha}(P_{X|Y=y})\|R_{X})\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\Big)\Bigg)

(315)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{t}\log\Bigg(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\exp\Big(-t\int_{[0,+\infty]}D_{\alpha}(\Gamma_{X}(P_{X|Y=y})\|U_{D})\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\Big)\Bigg)

(316)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{t}\log\Bigg(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\exp\Big(-t\log{D}+t\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(\Gamma_{X}(P_{X|Y=y}))\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\Big)\Bigg)

(317)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{t}\log\Bigg(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P_{Y}(y)\exp\Big(t\int_{[0,+\infty]}H_{\alpha}(\Gamma_{X}(P_{X|Y=y}))\,\mathrm{d}\tau(\alpha)\Big)\Bigg)+\log{D}

(318)

Hence, we obtain that

\displaystyle I_{t,\tau}(P_{XY}\|R_{X})=-H_{t,\tau}(X|Y)_{\Gamma_{X}(P_{XY})}+\log{D}

(319)

We note that the inequalities in the assumption of the theorem for
I_{t,\tau}
extend naturally to the quantity
I_{0,\alpha}
, which can be defined as the limit
t\to 0
of
F_{t,\tau}
, in direct analogy with the definition of
H_{0,\alpha}
introduced in Section
6
. Indeed, by a limiting argument, the condition
I_{0,\alpha}(P_{XY}\|R_{X})\geq I_{0,\alpha}(Q_{XY^{\prime}}\|R_{X})
in the region
\alpha\in[0,1]
can be obtained as limits of the inequalities for
I_{t,\tau}
in the bulk region
\mathcal{D}_{\rm bulk}
; see the discussion in Section
7.2
.

To apply Theorem
6.3
, we need to make the inequalities strict. Since these functions are defined in terms of convex combinations of Rényi relative entropies, it suffices to establish the strict inequality for each Rényi relative entropy individually, for all
\alpha\geq 0
. Indeed, taking convex combinations preserves strict order, and the subsequent operations—namely summation, addition of constants, exponentiation, and application of the logarithm—are all strictly monotone. Consequently, the overall functions inherit the strict ordering. To make the inequalities strict, we construct the approximation

Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}}=\left(1-\varepsilon\right)Q_{XY^{\prime}}+\varepsilon(R_{X}\otimes Q_{Y^{\prime}})\,.

(320)

This distribution is an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
Q_{XY^{\prime}}
:

\displaystyle d(Q_{XY^{\prime}},Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}\left|Q_{XY^{\prime}}(x,y^{\prime})-(1-\varepsilon)Q_{XY^{\prime}}(x,y^{\prime})-\varepsilon R_{X}(x)Q_{Y^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})\right|

(321)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}Q_{XY^{\prime}}(x,y^{\prime})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}R_{X}(x)\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}Q_{Y^{\prime}}(y^{\prime})=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.

(322)

The condition
Q_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\neq R_{X}
in the assumption of the claim guarantees that the inequalities between Rényi relative entropies are strict for all
\alpha>0
. Indeed, for any
\alpha>0
and
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
we obtain

D_{\alpha}\!\left(Q_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\middle\|R_{X}\right)>D_{\alpha}\!\left(Q^{\varepsilon}_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\middle\|R_{X}\right).

(323)

This follows from the joint concavity of the map
Q_{\alpha}(P_{X}\|R_{X})=\exp\!\bigl((\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(P_{X}\|R_{X})\bigr)
for
\alpha<1
and its joint convexity for
\alpha>1
, together with the assumption that

Q_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\neq R_{X}
.
To illustrate this explicitly, consider the case
\alpha>1
. Then

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha}\!\left(Q^{\varepsilon}_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\middle\|R_{X}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)Q_{\alpha}\!\left(Q_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\middle\|R_{X}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<Q_{\alpha}\!\left(Q_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\middle\|R_{X}\right),

(324)

where we used that the assumption
Q_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\neq R_{X}
implies
Q_{\alpha}\!\left(Q_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\middle\|R_{X}\right)>Q_{\alpha}\!\left(R_{X}\middle\|R_{X}\right)=1
.
By taking the logarithm, we obtain the desired strict inequality. Since the approximation decreases the value of
D_{0}
, and in particular satisfies
D_{0}(Q^{\varepsilon}_{X\mid Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\|R_{X})=0
, it follows that all
I_{t,\tau}
are strictly ordered if the intersection of the support of
\tau
and
(0,\infty]
is non-empty. It therefore remains to consider the case in which the measure is supported entirely at
\alpha=0
.
In this case, the assumption that the support sizes of
P_{X|Y=y}
are all strictly smaller than that of
R_{X}
guarantees that the inequalities are also strict for
\alpha=0
. Indeed, for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
it holds that

\displaystyle D_{0}(P_{X|Y=y}\|R_{X})\geq D_{0}(Q_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\|R_{X})\geq D_{0}(Q^{\varepsilon}_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}\|R_{X})=0\,.

(325)

Here, the last inequality follows from the fact that
Q^{\varepsilon}_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}=(1-\varepsilon)Q_{X|Y^{\prime}=y^{\prime}}+\varepsilon R_{X}
and hence has the same support of
R_{X}
. The assumption of the theorem on the support of
P_{XY}
implies that
D_{0}(P_{X|Y=y}\|R_{X})>0
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
The preceding observations imply that

\displaystyle H_{t,\tau}(X|Y)_{\Gamma_{X}(P_{XY})}<H_{t,\tau}(X|Y^{\prime})_{\Gamma_{X}(Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}})},\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm{bulk}},

(326)

\displaystyle H_{-\infty,\tau}(X|Y)_{\Gamma_{X}(P_{XY})}<H_{-\infty,\tau}(X|Y^{\prime})_{\Gamma_{X}(Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}})},\quad\quad\quad\;\;\;\forall\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{-\infty},

(327)

\displaystyle H_{0,\alpha}(X|Y)_{\Gamma_{X}(P_{XY})}<H_{0,\alpha}(X|Y^{\prime})_{\Gamma_{X}(Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}})},\quad\,\qquad\quad\quad\;\;\forall\alpha\in[0,1]\,,

(328)

\displaystyle H_{+\infty,0}(X|Y)_{\Gamma_{X}(P_{XY})}<H_{+\infty,0}(X|Y^{\prime})_{\Gamma_{X}(Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}})}.

(329)

Theorem
6.3
implies the existence of a catalyst
S^{\varepsilon}_{X\varepsilon Y\varepsilon}
and a conditionally mixing channel
\Upsilon
such that

\Upsilon(\Gamma_{X}(P_{XY})\otimes S^{\varepsilon}_{X\varepsilon Y\varepsilon})=\Gamma_{X}(Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}})\otimes S^{\varepsilon}_{X\varepsilon Y\varepsilon}\,.

(330)

Note that no additional embedding is required, since
P_{XY}
and
Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}}
have the same number of rows, as discussed in Remark
2.10
.
Therefore, we construct the channel

\Lambda:=(\Gamma^{-1}_{X}\otimes I_{Y^{\prime}}\otimes I_{X\varepsilon Y\varepsilon})\circ\Upsilon_{XY^{\prime}X\varepsilon Y\varepsilon}\circ(\Gamma_{X}\otimes I_{Y^{\prime}}\otimes I_{X\varepsilon Y\varepsilon})

(331)

so that

\displaystyle\Lambda(P_{XY}\otimes S^{\varepsilon}_{X\varepsilon Y\varepsilon})=Q^{\varepsilon}_{XY^{\prime}}\otimes S^{\varepsilon}_{X\varepsilon Y\varepsilon}

(332)

Finally, to show that this map is a conditionally
R_{X}\otimes U_{X_{\varepsilon}}
-preserving operation, one can verify its structure directly. Another way is to observe that, for any distribution
T_{YY_{\varepsilon}}
, there is a distribution
T^{\prime}_{Y^{\prime}Y_{\varepsilon}}
such that

\Upsilon\big(U_{D}\otimes U_{X_{\varepsilon}}\otimes T_{YY_{\varepsilon}}\big)=U_{D}\otimes U_{X_{\varepsilon}}\otimes T^{\prime}_{Y^{\prime}Y_{\varepsilon}}

(333)

which immediately follows from the fact that
\Upsilon
is conditionally mixing and implies that

\Lambda(R_{X}\otimes U_{X_{\varepsilon}}\otimes T_{YY_{\varepsilon}})=R_{X}\otimes U_{X_{\varepsilon}}\otimes T^{\prime}_{Y^{\prime}Y_{\varepsilon}}.

(334)

Moreover,
\Lambda
does not signal from
XX_{\varepsilon}
to
Y^{\prime}Y_{\varepsilon}
meaning that ignoring the
XX_{\varepsilon}
information after performing
\Lambda
results into a legitimate stochastic map
YY_{\varepsilon}\to Y^{\prime}Y_{\varepsilon}
as one easily verifies using the fact that
\Upsilon
is conditionally mixing. These two properties together are equivalent to

being a conditionally

-preserving channel, by an argument analogous to that in
(

.

Let us present a direct physical application of the above result. Let us fix a finite-dimensional quantum system
A
with the Hamiltonian

H_{A}=\sum_{a}E_{a}\left|a\middle\rangle\middle\langle a\right|

(335)

where
\{|a\rangle\}_{a}
is the energy eigenbasis of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{A}
of the system. If all
E_{a}
coincide, we say that the Hamiltonian is trivial. In the inverse temperature
\beta
the Hamiltonian defines the partition function
Z_{A}
and the Gibbs state
\gamma_{A}
through

Z_{A}=\sum_{a}e^{a\beta E_{a}},\qquad\gamma_{A}=\frac{1}{Z_{A}}e^{-\beta H_{A}}.

(336)

A quantum channel
G
, i.e., a completely positive trace-preserving linear map on the operators on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
, is Gibbs-preserving if
G(\gamma_{A})=\gamma_{A}
. Thermal operations are defined as those that allow one to borrow an ancillary system in a Gibbs state, apply a unitary that commutes with the total Hamiltonian, and then discard a subsystem via the partial trace. For classical states, i.e., those that commute with
\gamma_{A}
, however, thermal operations are equivalent to Gibbs-preserving maps
28
;
27
. Accordingly, in the following, we will use the term Gibbs-preserving maps interchangeably with thermal operations.

Conditioned thermal operations generalize this concept in the setting where side information is available and were introduced in
36
. We say that a channel
G
from a bipartite quantum system
AB
to another bipartite system
AB^{\prime}
is conditionally Gibbs-preserving if there exists a channel
G_{B\to B^{\prime}}
such that

{\rm Tr}_{A}\left[G(\rho_{AB})\right]=G_{B\to B^{\prime}}(\rho_{B}),\quad G(\gamma_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})=\gamma_{A}\otimes G_{B\to B^{\prime}}(\rho_{B})

(337)

for all quantum states
\rho_{AB}
on
AB
with the reduced state
\rho_{B}
on
B
.

We say that a state
\rho_{AB}
is classical if there is a joint probability distribution
P_{AB}
and an orthonormal basis
\{|b\rangle\}_{b}
for the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{B}
of
B
such that

\rho_{AB}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}}P_{AB}(a,b)\left|a\middle\rangle\middle\langle a\right|\otimes\left|b\middle\rangle\middle\langle b\right|.

(338)

It follows immediately that, if
\rho_{AB}
and
\sigma_{AB^{\prime}}
are classical states, where
\rho_{AB}
is associated with the joint probability
P_{AB}
and
\sigma_{AB^{\prime}}
with the joint probability
Q_{AB^{\prime}}
, and there is a conditionally Gibbs-preserving channel
G
such that
G(\rho_{AB})=\sigma_{AB^{\prime}}
, then there is a classical conditionally
R_{A}
-preserving channel
\Lambda
, where
R_{A}(a)=e^{-\beta E_{a}}/Z_{A}
, such that
\Lambda(P_{AB})=Q_{AB^{\prime}}
. Naturally, with this observation, we obtain a sufficient condition for asymptotic conditionally Gibbs-preserving transformability between classical states in the catalytic regime as a direct corollary of Theorem
8.3
. However, before stating this result, let us introduce the free energies

F_{t,\tau}(\rho_{AB},\gamma_{A})=\frac{1}{\beta}\big(I_{t,\tau}(P_{AB}\|R_{A})-\log{Z_{A}}\big)

(339)

for any classical state
\rho_{AB}
associated with the joint probability
P_{AB}
where
R_{A}(a)=e^{-\beta E_{a}}/Z_{A}
. We also define the free energies
F_{-\infty,\tau}
as pointwise limits of the above as
t\to-\infty
.

Corollary 8.4

(Second laws of thermodynamics with side information)
.

Let us assume that the Gibbs-spectrum is rational, i.e.,
e^{-\beta E_{a}}\in\mathbb{Q}
for all
a
. Suppose that

\rho_{AB}=\sum_{a,b}P_{AB}(a,b)\left|a\middle\rangle\middle\langle a\right|\otimes\left|b\middle\rangle\middle\langle b\right|,\quad\sigma_{AB^{\prime}}=\sum_{a,b^{\prime}}Q_{AB^{\prime}}(a,b^{\prime})\left|a\middle\rangle\middle\langle a\right|\otimes\left|b^{\prime}\middle\rangle\middle\langle b^{\prime}\right|

(340)

are classical states such that

\max_{b,\ P_{B}(b)>0}\big|{\rm supp}(P_{A|B=b})\big|<d_{A},\qquad Q_{A|B^{\prime}=b^{\prime}}\neq\frac{e^{-\beta E_{a}}}{Z_{A}}\ \forall(a,b^{\prime}),

(341)

where
d_{A}
is the dimension of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exist finite quantum systems
A_{\varepsilon}
and
B_{\varepsilon}
, a classical state
\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{AB^{\prime}}
, a state
\omega^{\varepsilon}_{A_{\varepsilon}B_{\varepsilon}}
and a conditionally Gibbs-preserving channel
G_{\varepsilon}:AA_{\varepsilon}BB_{\varepsilon}\to AA_{\varepsilon}B^{\prime}B_{\varepsilon}
(where
A_{\varepsilon}
carries the maximally mixed state, the Gibbs state of a trivial Hamiltonian of
A_{\varepsilon}
) such that
\|\sigma_{AB^{\prime}}-\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{AB^{\prime}}\|_{1}<\varepsilon
(approximation in trace norm) and
G_{\varepsilon}(\rho_{AB}\otimes\omega^{\varepsilon}_{A_{\varepsilon}B_{\varepsilon}})=\sigma^{\varepsilon}_{AB^{\prime}}\otimes\omega^{\varepsilon}_{A_{\varepsilon}B_{\varepsilon}}
if

\displaystyle F_{t,\tau}(\rho_{AB},\gamma_{A})

\displaystyle\geq F_{t,\tau}(\sigma_{AB^{\prime}},\gamma_{A})\qquad\forall(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm bulk},

(342)

\displaystyle F_{-\infty,\tau}(\rho_{AB},\gamma_{A})

\displaystyle\geq F_{-\infty,\tau}(\sigma_{AB^{\prime}},\gamma_{A})\qquad\forall\,\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}.

(343)

9
Conclusion

We derive the general form of conditional entropy and show that any quantity satisfying operationally relevant axioms admits a representation as an exponential average of Rényi entropies of the conditioned distribution, parameterized by a real parameter and a probability measure.
These results reveal an underlying structure common to the various conditional entropies introduced in the literature and clarify the relationships among them.

We derived conditions for the transformation of joint probability distributions under conditionally mixing channels, both in the many-copy regime and in the presence of a catalyst that may be borrowed during the process and returned unchanged. We showed that these conditions can be expressed in terms of conditional entropies
and that they can be used to characterize the optimal conversion rate under conditionally mixing channels and to derive a set of second laws of quantum thermodynamics with side information.

While the general form of conditional entropies can be expressed in terms of a real parameter and a probability measure, not all choices of these parameters give rise to quantities that satisfy the full set of axioms.
We therefore provide a set of sufficient conditions on the parameters ensuring that the resulting conditional entropy is well defined and satisfies all required axioms.
Moreover, we derive necessary conditions which, for suitably well-behaved distributions—and in particular for discrete probability measures with finite support—yield a complete characterization of the admissible parameter range.
While we conjecture that the sufficient conditions we derive are also necessary in full generality, a complete characterization of the necessary conditions for arbitrary parameter choices remains an open problem and an interesting problem for future research.

In Theorem
6.3
, we provided a set of sufficient conditions for many-copy and catalytic transformations, which include the condition

\displaystyle H_{+\infty,0}(P_{XY})<H_{+\infty,0}(Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}).

(344)

This condition requires the maximal support of the columns of
P_{XY}
to be strictly smaller than that of
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}
and therefore excludes the special case in which the two distributions have equal support.
As a consequence, the applicability of this result is restricted to certain classes of distributions.
This restriction likely stems from the fact that we do not consider conditional Rényi entropies with negative
\alpha
. This is a well-known fact, which also holds in the absence of side information (see
29
;
13
).
Informally, this can be understood by considering the case of a single
Y
outcome and observing that, under the support assumption (
344
), the Rényi entropies of
P_{XY}
with negative
\alpha
take the value
-\infty
, whereas they remain finite for
Q_{XY}
, since
P_{XY}
must be embedded into the space of
Q_{XY}
.
Consequently, the support condition already enforces a strict ordering for all negative values of
\alpha
, and these entropies therefore do not need to be included among the sufficient conditions.
This fact is reflected later when deriving sufficient conditions for catalytic conditionally (gibbs-)preserving operations in Section
8.2
, where we imposed the support-size separation in Theorem
8.3
and Corollary
8.4
.
Hence, an open question is to extend these results to a more general setting without relying on the support-size assumption.
Another open question is whether the assumption that the Gibbs state has rational entries can be dropped. We believe this to be possible, similarly to what is known in the unconditional case
13
. However, since the methods of this manuscript rely on the embedding map, our current construction does not cover the case of irrational entries.

In Theorem
8.1
we derived the optimal rate
R
for the transformation of multiple copies of a joint probability
P_{XY}
into
Q_{XY}
. If our conjecture regarding the characterization of
\mathcal{D}_{\rm bulk}
and
\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}
were correct, by a limit argument, the optimal rate could be written in the simpler form

R(P_{XY}\rightarrow Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}})=\inf_{(t,\tau)\in\mathcal{D}_{\rm bulk}}\frac{H_{t,\tau}(X^{\prime}|Y^{\prime})_{Q}}{H_{t,\tau}(X|Y)_{P}}\,.

(345)

Indeed, we expect that the monotonicity region
\mathcal{D}_{-\infty}
could be obtained as the limit
t\to-\infty
of the monotonicity region
\mathcal{D}_{\rm bulk}
.

Finally, we expect that the conditions in Theorem
8.3
and Corollary
8.4
concerning
I_{-\infty,\tau}
and
F_{-\infty,\tau}
could also be removed by a limiting argument.

Appendix A
Convexity and monotonicity

In this appendix, we present several known equivalences between convexity and monotonicity under channels. These will be useful in the main text to characterize the parameter regimes for which the conditional entropy is monotone under conditionally mixing channels, and thus qualifies as a valid conditional entropy according to Definition
2.11
. In particular, these equivalences allow us to reduce the question of monotonicity to that of convexity, which is often easier to analyze.

In Definition
2.11
, we introduced conditional entropies as quantities satisfying a collection of operational axioms, motivated by the requirement that they quantify conditional uncertainty. A central axiom is monotonicity under conditionally mixing channels.
Within the mathematical framework of preordered semirings, monotone homomorphisms and derivations are functions designed to satisfy closely analogous structural properties (see Section
3.1
). In particular, in the semiring associated with conditional majorization, these functions correspond—up to an additive constant and a logarithmic factor—to (extremal) conditional entropies.
Consequently, understanding their monotonicity properties is essential. In the following, we show that determining whether these functions are monotone under conditionally mixing channels is equivalent to studying their convexity properties.

We begin by recalling several notions of convexity for functions defined on
(unnormalized) joint distributions
\mathcal{V}
, which—up to embedding in a larger space (padding with zeros)—
can be identified with the elements of the semiring of conditional majorization
introduced in Section
3
.
We say that a map
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex if, for any
P,Q\in\mathcal{V}
of the same size and
t\in[0,1]
, we have

F\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)\leq tF(P)+(1-t)F(Q).

(346)

We say that
F
is concave if the above inequality is reversed. We say that
F
is quasi-convex if, for
P,Q\in\mathcal{V}
of the same size and
t\in(0,1)
,

F\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)\leq\max\{F(P),F(Q)\}.

(347)

We say that
F
is strongly quasi-concave if the inequality above is reversed, i.e.,

F\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)\geq\max\{F(P),F(Q)\}.

(348)

In the following, we show that the required convexity properties depend on the output space of the homomorphism. As discussed in Section
4
, the key monotonicity is that under maps acting on the conditioning subsystem of a joint distribution, which form the key subclass of conditionally mixing channels. In matrix form, for a positive joint distribution
P_{XY}
, the action on the conditioning subsystem can be represented as
P_{XY}\rightarrow P_{XY}D
, where
D
is a row stochastic matrix.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
S
be the semiring of conditional majorization of Section
3
. Let

\Phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{K}
be a monotone homomorphism, and hence of the form in Proposition (
3.7
). Here,
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}},\mathbb{TR}_{+},\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}\}
. Then, monotonicity with respect to maps acting on the conditioning system is equivalent to the following convexity properties, depending on the set
\mathbb{K}
:

•

If
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\Phi
is monotone if and only if it is concave.

•

If
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}
,
\Phi
is monotone if and only if it is convex.

•

If
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{TR}_{+}
,
\Phi
is monotone if and only if it is strongly quasi-concave.

•

If
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{TR}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}
,
\Phi
is monotone if and only if it is quasi-convex.

Proof.

Let us first prove the ‘only if’ part of the claim. The proof relies on associating the distributions
tP
and
(1-t)Q
with distinct outcomes of the conditioning variable
Y
. Monotonicity under discarding outcomes of
Y
then yields the desired inequality. Since monotone homomorphisms are invariant under embedding (see the discussion in
Section
3.3
), we may, in the following,
identify their evaluation on equivalence classes with their evaluation on
unnormalized joint distributions.

Explicitly, suppose that
P,Q\in\mathcal{V}
and
t\in(0,1)
. We now have

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}tP&0\\
0&(1-t)Q\end{array}\right)\succeq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}tP&(1-t)Q\\
0&0\end{array}\right)\succeq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}tP+(1-t)Q&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right).

(349)

The first implication follows by applying a permutation on the
X
register conditioned on the second
Y
outcome, while the second follows by discarding both outcomes and introducing a dummy outcome with zero probability.

In case
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, it now follows that

\displaystyle\Phi\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)

\displaystyle\geq\Phi\left[\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}tP&0\\
0&(1-t)Q\end{array}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\Phi(tP)+\Phi\big((1-t)Q\big)

(352)

\displaystyle=\Phi(t)\Phi(P)+\Phi(1-t)\Phi(Q)

(353)

\displaystyle=t\Phi(P)+(1-t)\Phi(Q).

(354)

Similarly, in case
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathrm{op}}
, we get

\displaystyle\Phi\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)

\displaystyle\leq t\Phi(P)+(1-t)\Phi(Q)\,.

(355)

In case
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
, we get

\displaystyle\Phi\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)

\displaystyle\geq\max\{\Phi(P),\Phi(Q)\}\,.

(356)

In case
\mathbb{TR}^{\rm{op}}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi\left[\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}tP&0\\
0&(1-t)Q\end{array}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\max\{\Phi(tP),\Phi\big((1-t)Q\big)\}

(359)

\displaystyle=\max\{\Phi(t)\Phi(P),\Phi(1-t)\Phi(Q)\}

(360)

\displaystyle=\max\{\Phi(P),\Phi(Q)\}.

(361)

Next, we prove the “if” direction and assume that
\Phi
is concave. Let
P\in\mathcal{V}
and let
D
be a row-stochastic matrix. We aim to show that
\Phi(PD)\leq\Phi(P)
. Let us first assume that
P=R=R_{X:YZ}
(colon separating the primary
X
from the conditioning
YZ
) and that
D
is the map ignoring
Z
. We have

\displaystyle\Phi(R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}R_{Z}(z)\Phi(R_{XY|Z=z})\leq\Phi\left(\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}R_{Z}(z)R_{XY|Z=z}\right)=\Phi(R_{XY}),

(362)

where we have used the form of (
94
) in the first equality and the assumed concavity of
\Phi
in the inequality. This proves the first special case. Assume then the case
P_{XY}\succeq P_{XY}D=:Q_{XY^{\prime}}
where
D
is a row-stochastic matrix which has only entries 0 or 1. This means that the matrix entries of
D
are given by
D(y^{\prime}|y)=\delta_{f(y),y^{\prime}}
for some function
f:Y\to Y^{\prime}
. Let us define
R=R_{X:YY^{\prime}}
where
R(x,y,y^{\prime})=P(x,y)
whenever
f(y)=y^{\prime}
and
R(x,y,y^{\prime})=0
otherwise. It immediately follows that
P\approx R
and
R_{XY^{\prime}}=Q=PD
. Thus, using the above result, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(PD)=\Phi(R_{XY^{\prime}})\geq\Phi(R)=\Phi(P).

(363)

Finally, we prove the case of a general row-stochastic matrix
D
. Since
D
can be expressed as a convex combination of row-stochastic matrices
D_{k}
whose entries are either 0 or 1, there exist weights
t_{k}>0
with
\sum_{k}t_{k}=1

such that

D=\sum_{k}t_{k}D_{k}.

(364)

Our observations thus far, together with the concavity of
\Phi
imply

\displaystyle\Phi(PD)

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\sum_{k}t_{k}PD_{k}\right)\geq\sum_{k}t_{k}\Phi(PD_{k})=\sum_{k}t_{k}\Phi(P)=\Phi(P).

(365)

The other cases are similar.
∎

A similar argument applies to derivations, which must be concave.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
S
be the semiring of conditional majorization of Section
3
. Let
\Delta:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
be an extremal monotone derivation at
\|\cdot\|
, and hence of the form in Proposition
3.8
.
Then,
\Delta
is a monotone derivation if and only if it is concave.

Proof.

By assumption,
\Delta:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is a derivation at
\|\cdot\|
of the form in Proposition
3.8
. In particular, it satisfies
\Delta(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Let now
P,Q\in\mathcal{V}
be of the same size and
t\in[0,1]
. Using the same argument in the proof of Lemma
A.1
, and in particular the relation in equation (
349
), we have

\displaystyle\Delta\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)

\displaystyle\geq\Delta(tP)+\Delta\big((1-t)Q\big)

(366)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Delta(t)}_{=0}\|P\|+t\Delta(P)+\underbrace{\Delta(1-t)}_{=0}\|Q\|+(1-t)\Delta(Q)

(367)

\displaystyle=t\Delta(P)+(1-t)\Delta(Q).

(368)

For the reverse implication, the monotonicity of
\Delta
can be shown in exactly the same way as in the second part of the proof of Lemma
A.1
.
∎

The next lemma shows that, for homomorphisms, monotonicity under arbitrary maps acting on the conditioning system already implies monotonicity under the full class of conditionally mixing channels. This follows from the additional facts that homomorphisms are monotone under doubly stochastic maps and additive under direct sums. Together, these properties suffice to establish monotonicity under general conditionally mixing channels.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
S
be the semiring of conditional majorization of Section
3
.
Let
\Phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{K}
be a monotone homomorphism, and hence of the form in Proposition
3.7
. If
\Phi
is monotone under row stochastic maps acting on the conditioning system, then it is monotone under conditionally mixing operations.

Proof.

We present the proof for the case
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
, as the other cases follow analogously.

We first note that, as discussed in Section
4.3
,
monotonicity under doubly stochastic maps is guaranteed by the explicit form of
the homomorphisms derived in Proposition
3.7
, since
these are given by exponential expectations of Rényi entropies, which are
monotone under such maps.

To establish monotonicity under conditionally mixing channels, we introduce an auxiliary register that separates the individual terms appearing in the sum defining the channel, and then exploit the direct-sum property of the homomorphisms. The proof then follows by applying monotonicity under doubly stochastic maps and row-stochastic maps to each term separately.
Explicitly, let
P_{XY}\in\mathcal{V}
,
S^{(i)}
be doubly stochastic, and
D^{(i)}
have non-negative entries (
i=1,\ldots,k
) with
D^{(1)}+\cdots+D^{(k)}
row-stochastic. Let us denote the output of the conditionally mixing channel
Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}=\sum_{i}S^{(i)}P_{XY}D^{(i)}
. Let us define new conditioning systems
\widetilde{Y}:=Y\times\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}:=Y^{\prime}\times\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Let
\pi_{Y^{\prime}|\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}}
be the row-stochastic matrix which ignores the part
\{1,\ldots,n\}
in
\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}
. Let us also define the matrices

\displaystyle P_{X\widetilde{Y}}

\displaystyle:=\big(P_{XY}D^{(1)}\,\cdots\,P_{XY}D^{(n)}\big)

(369)

\displaystyle Q_{X^{\prime}\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=\big(S^{(1)}P_{XY}D^{(1)}\,\cdots\,S^{(n)}P_{XY}D^{(n)}\big).

(370)

Because of the form of (
94
), we have

\Phi(P_{X\widetilde{Y}})=\Phi\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}P_{XY}D^{(i)}\right),\quad\Phi(Q_{X^{\prime}\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}})=\Phi\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}S^{(i)}P_{XY}D^{(i)}\right).

(371)

Putting all this together and noting that
(D^{(1)}\,\cdots D^{(n)})
is row-stochastic and that
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}S^{(i)}
is doubly stochastic, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(P_{XY})

\displaystyle\leq\Phi\big[P_{XY}\big(D^{(1)}\,\cdots\,D^{(n)}\big)\big]

(372)

\displaystyle=\Phi(P_{X\widetilde{Y}})=\Phi\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}P_{XY}D^{(i)}\right)

(373)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi\left[\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}S^{(i)}\right)\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}P_{XY}D^{(i)}\right)\right]

(374)

\displaystyle=\Phi\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}S^{(i)}P_{XY}D^{(i)}\right)

(375)

\displaystyle=\Phi(Q_{X^{\prime}\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}})

(376)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(Q_{X^{\prime}\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}}\pi_{Y^{\prime}|\widetilde{Y}^{\prime}})

(377)

\displaystyle=\Phi(Q_{X^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}),

(378)

showing the monotonicity in case
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The other cases are essentially the same; the sums in (
365
) turn into maxima in the tropical cases
\mathbb{TR}_{+}
and
\mathbb{TR}^{\mathrm{op}}_{+}
, and the inequalities are reversed in cases
\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{op}}_{+}
and
\mathbb{TR}^{\mathrm{op}}_{+}
.
∎

Lemma A.4

3
. Let
\Delta:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
be an extremal monotone derivation at
\|\cdot\|
, and hence of the form in Proposition
3.8
.
If
\Delta
is monotone under row stochastic maps acting on the conditioning system, then it is monotone under conditionally mixing operations.
