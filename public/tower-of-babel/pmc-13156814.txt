Infectious Diseases and Antimicrobial Resistance in the Gaza Strip: Epidemiological Drivers, Health System Disruptions, and Global Implications: A Narrative Review
Authors: Nima Farhadi, Aref Yarahmadi, Hamed Afkhami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe Gaza Strip has faced prolonged blockade, recurrent military conflicts, and systemic infrastructure collapse since October 2023, resulting in an unprecedented public health crisis characterized by surging infectious diseases and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). This narrative review aims to synthesize the available evidence to elucidate the epidemiological drivers, health system disruptions, and environmental determinants underlying this crisis.MethodsA comprehensive narrative review was conducted using peer‐reviewed literature, WHO and UNRWA reports, Gaza Ministry of Health field data (2023–2025), and surveillance summaries. Relevant studies and institutional reports were thematically analyzed to identify key trends in infectious diseases, AMR dynamics, and health system vulnerabilities in conflict‐affected settings.ResultsThe review highlights the re‐emergence of hepatitis A, acute respiratory infections, and poliovirus previously eliminated locally, as well as the proliferation of multidrug‐resistant pathogens such as carbapenem‐resistant Acinetobacter baumannii. Over 200,000 diarrheal cases, half among children under five, reflect the collapse of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) systems, with 97% of groundwater deemed unfit for consumption. Structural damage to health infrastructure, shortages of sterile supplies, and disrupted supply chains have severely impaired infection control and antibiotic stewardship. Environmental degradation, including 23 million tons of debris and untreated sewage, further amplifies the disease transmission risks.ConclusionGaza's infectious disease and AMR crisis stems from the convergence of environmental degradation, infrastructural collapse, and health system disruption. Immediate evidence‐based interventions, including WASH rehabilitation, decentralized AMR diagnostics, and emergency immunization, are urgently needed. These findings underscore the necessity of sustained, coordinated global health responses to prevent wider regional transmission and mitigate mortality in conflict settings.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, Gaza strip, healthcare collapse, infectious diseases

Introduction

War has devastating consequences for public health beyond direct casualties, creating conditions conducive to infectious disease outbreaks and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) through disruptions to water and sanitation infrastructure, population displacement, and health system collapse [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Systematic reviews demonstrate that conflict environments amplify inappropriate antibiotic use, impair stewardship, and create reservoirs of resistant pathogens [5, 6, 7, 8].

Gaza's health system, already severely impaired by a 16‐year blockade since 2007, faced catastrophic collapse following Israel's full siege declared on October 7, 2023. Systematic targeting of healthcare infrastructure has resulted in widespread destruction of hospitals, killing of medical personnel, and severe service impairment [9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. With 95% of the population lacking access to clean water, approximately 1.8 million people (80% of Gaza's population) internally displaced, and extreme population density ( ~ 6300 individuals/km²), Gaza has become a perfect storm for epidemic outbreaks and accelerated AMR [14, 15, 16].

The Gaza Strip, a narrow coastal enclave situated along the Mediterranean Sea in southern Palestine, covers an area of approximately 360 square kilometers. Despite its limited geographical size, it is considered one of the most densely populated areas in the world, with an estimated population of 2.23 million as reported by the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics in 2023. This equates to a population density of nearly 6300 individuals per square kilometer, positioning Gaza among the most overcrowded territories in the world (Figure 1) [17, 19, 20, 21, 22].

Geographical map of the Gaza Strip (adapted from Wikimedia Commons) [17, 18].

The objective of this review is to provide an integrated and evidence‑based assessment of the current epidemiology of infectious diseases and AMR in Gaza, to elucidate the environmental and health system factors driving this crisis, and to formulate actionable recommendations for global health agencies, humanitarian organizations, and research institutions aimed at restoring essential services, strengthening antimicrobial stewardship, and preventing further deterioration of public health in conflict‑affected settings.

Methodology

This narrative review synthesizes evidence from peer‐reviewed literature, official reports from organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO), United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees, and Gaza's Ministry of Health, as well as field mission data collected between October 2023 and April 2025. Field mission data refer to on‐ground observations and assessments conducted by humanitarian organizations (e.g., WHO, UNRWA, Doctors Without Borders) and independent medical teams, including qualitative reports on environmental conditions (e.g., waste accumulation, water contamination), epidemiological surveillance (e.g., case counts for infectious diseases and AMR patterns), and photographic documentation of infrastructure damage. These missions typically involved short‐term deployments (1–4 weeks) by multidisciplinary teams comprising epidemiologists, environmental health experts, and clinicians, focusing on displacement camps, hospitals, and WASH sites in areas such as Al‐Mawasi and Khan Younis. Data were aggregated from publicly available reports and peer‐reviewed studies to ensure reliability and transparency. No primary data were collected by the author; inclusion criteria prioritized sources with verifiable methodologies and relevance to infectious diseases, AMR, and environmental factors in Gaza. This approach allows for a comprehensive, evidence‐based analysis while highlighting gaps for future research.

Search Strategy

To identify relevant peer‐reviewed literature, systematic searches were conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science using combinations of keywords including “Gaza”, “infectious diseases”, “antimicrobial resistance”, “conflict”, “war”, “water, sanitation hygiene (WASH)”, and “public health.” Searches were limited to publications from 2000 to April 2025 to capture both pre‐ and post‐escalation data. Inclusion criteria comprised studies and reports directly addressing infectious diseases, AMR trends, environmental health, or healthcare infrastructure in Gaza or comparable conflict settings. Exclusion criteria eliminated commentaries without empirical evidence, duplicate reports, and sources lacking methodological transparency. Non–peer‐reviewed sources (e.g., humanitarian situation reports, UN agency briefings, and health ministry datasets) were included where peer‐reviewed data were unavailable, given the exceptional constraints of conflict settings. Their reliability was assessed based on the issuing organization's credibility, consistency across multiple independent reports, and methodological rigor where available. Given the narrative nature of this review, a systematic search protocol was not followed. Instead, evidence was drawn from a wide range of peer‐reviewed articles, institutional reports, and field assessments. Counting the Dead in Gaza

Between October 2023 and April 2025, Gaza's Health Ministry—with figures corroborated by the UN and WHO—reported over 52,000 direct conflict‐related deaths. Approximately 80% of casualties are classified as non‐combatants [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Historical analyses suggest indirect deaths (from disease, malnutrition, health system collapse) may reach 3–15 times direct mortality. Applying a conservative multiplier of 4 to confirmed deaths yields an estimated 150,000–186,000 total war‐related deaths (~7.9% of Gaza's 2022 population) [30, 31] (Table 2).

Epidemiological Risks in Post‐Conflict Gaza

According to a WHO report published on December 21, 2023, Gaza has witnessed a sharp increase in the incidence of infectious diseases amid the ongoing conflict. Since mid‐October, over 100,000 cases of diarrhea have been recorded—half of which have affected children under the age of five. This figure represents a 25‐fold rise compared to pre‐war levels. A separate report by UNICEF corroborates these findings, projecting a 2% increase in diarrhea cases among children under five by the end of 2023, highlighting the severe public health emergency fueled by a lack of access to clean and safe drinking water [38].

In addition, more than 150,000 cases of upper respiratory infections have been documented, alongside other illnesses such as scabies, lice, chickenpox, skin rashes, hepatitis A, and various dermatological and gastrointestinal conditions afflicting displaced populations. The deteriorating health situation is primarily driven by acute shortages of clean water and nutritious food, contamination of water sources, and the inability to implement adequate hygiene and safety measures [38, 39].

These conditions have led to widespread outbreaks of foodborne and waterborne illnesses, particularly among children, with symptoms including persistent diarrhea and vomiting. Recent WHO statistics report that the cumulative number of diarrhea cases has surged to 213,085, with 104,213 involving children under five [35, 36].

Compounding the health crisis is the widespread destruction of Gaza's healthcare infrastructure, particularly hospitals, by Israeli military operations. The resulting data gaps make it difficult to track the full scope of the hepatitis A outbreak. Nevertheless, it is widely acknowledged that a significant portion of the displaced population is infected with hepatitis A due to insufficient sanitation and medical care. A rare study conducted during the conflict revealed that more than 32% of the 384 displaced individuals surveyed had contracted the virus.

Further exacerbating the crisis are complications related to wound infections caused by antibiotic‐resistant bacteria. These infections have proliferated in overcrowded medical facilities lacking effective infection control, clean water, and adequate supplies of essential antibiotics. The inability to manage these infections has led to fatal outcomes and limb amputations among affected patients. The disruption of healthcare services, including vaccination programs and disease surveillance, has accelerated the unchecked spread of communicable diseases [35, 37].

Most recently, the detection of poliovirus in sewage samples has raised fresh concerns about the potential resurgence of this previously controlled disease. This discovery underscores the urgency of restoring public health infrastructure, implementing preventive health measures, and securing access to essential medical services to contain further outbreaks among Gaza's displaced population [37, 42].

Historical Context

Gaza has a long history of conflict and political instability, which has significantly impacted its healthcare system. The region has been subject to repeated cycles of violence, with the most recent escalation beginning on October 7, 2023, further exacerbating the challenges faced by its healthcare infrastructure. This ongoing conflict has had devastating effects on the capacity of healthcare facilities, contributing to the breakdown of essential medical services and increasing the population's vulnerability to infectious diseases [33, 51, 69].

Prior to the recent hostilities, Gaza's healthcare system consisted of a complex network of approximately 32 public hospitals and 70 primary healthcare centers, providing services to over 2 million residents. However, years of conflict have severely degraded this infrastructure, leading to the destruction of hospitals and clinics and a significant lack of maintenance, electricity, and clean water. One healthcare worker noted, “The infrastructure is falling apart; there are no sterile spaces left to treat patients, and basic equipment is either broken or unavailable,” highlighting the critical barriers to effective healthcare delivery [33].

The combined impact of military assaults, a protracted siege, and damage to healthcare facilities has not only intensified the healthcare crisis but also contributed to an escalating AMR situation, underscoring the urgent need for global action to address public health in the region. The collapse of the healthcare system in Gaza serves as a stark reminder of the broader implications of prolonged conflict on health outcomes, particularly in fragile and conflict‐affected areas [51].

Current State of Infectious Diseases in Gaza

The state of infectious diseases in Gaza has reached alarming levels, significantly exacerbated by the ongoing humanitarian crisis. According to the Gaza Health Ministry, there is approximately one infectious disease for every two people in the region, a statistic that highlights the severity of the situation. Health officials recently reported detecting around 1 million cases of infectious diseases, describing the circumstances as “extremely catastrophic”. The prevalence of diseases such as diarrhea, chickenpox, respiratory infections, and skin infections has surged, largely due to the collapse of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) systems, alongside a lack of adequate healthcare infrastructure [17, 70, 71].

Contributing Factors

Several critical factors have contributed to the widespread transmission of infectious diseases in Gaza. The prolonged blockade by Israel has severely restricted access to essential resources, including food, water, electricity, and medical supplies, leading to deteriorating sanitary conditions and increased vulnerability to infections. The lack of effective disease surveillance and delayed international aid has further hampered efforts to control outbreaks. Overcrowding in hospitals and shelters for internally displaced persons (IDPs) has accelerated the spread of airborne infections such as TB and respiratory viruses, while the limited access to clean water has facilitated the transmission of waterborne diseases like cholera and typhoid fever. Furthermore, severe shortages of medical supplies, compounded by psychological stress and burnout among healthcare workers, have severely hindered the healthcare system's ability to respond effectively to these outbreaks [50, 52].

Specific Disease Outbreaks

Recent studies and reports indicate that various infectious diseases are notably prevalent among the displaced populations. Conditions such as scabies, lice, and acute respiratory infections are widespread, with significant numbers of cases documented in temporary shelters. In December alone, reports indicated over 360,000 cases of infectious diseases in shelters, a figure that is believed to be an undercount, as the number has reportedly tripled within 3 months due to the ongoing crisis. The situation in Gaza reflects a dire public health emergency that underscores the urgent need for international intervention and support to address both the immediate health crises and the underlying infrastructural deficiencies contributing to the rampant spread of infectious diseases [52, 72].

Conflict‐driven population displacement and environmental disruption also elevate the risk of other neglected and chronic infections. Cutaneous leishmaniasis has been clearly associated with conflict and forced migration in the region (Syria provides a documented example of substantial increases in cutaneous leishmaniasis associated with protracted conflict and population displacement), and similar ecological and social drivers could facilitate autochthonous transmission or imported cases in Gaza and neighboring areas [73, 74]. In parallel, TB dynamics are likely to worsen: displaced populations, interrupted TB services, and diagnostic delays can raise incidence and the risk of ongoing transmission even where formal case notifications may initially decline. Strengthening TB case finding, maintaining treatment continuity, and integrating TB screening into rapid health‐service responses are therefore essential components of infectious‐disease control in this context [75].

Field Observations

The critical shortage of sterile medical supplies has significantly exacerbated the ongoing infection crisis. Fundamental items such as sterile gloves, gowns, and surgical drapes are frequently unavailable, thereby impeding the ability to uphold essential hygiene protocols during clinical procedures. This is further compounded by the limited access to hand hygiene essentials—including clean water, soap, and alcohol‐based sanitizers—which have played a substantial role in the emergence and transmission of antibiotic‐resistant infections, leading to heightened morbidity and mortality rates [76, 77].

During our deployment at EGH, we documented a concerning outbreak of Carbapenem‐Resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) within one of the intensive care units. A pivotal factor contributing to the outbreak was the unavailability of appropriate sterilization equipment for ventilator systems. Furthermore, the scarcity of designated medical waste containers resulted in the use of open barrels to dispose of contaminated instruments and materials, thereby fostering unhygienic conditions conducive to pathogen spread, particularly during bedside interventions in critical care settings [76, 78].

At one juncture, fluoroscopy machines intended for emergency imaging were rendered inoperative following damage from a burst drainage pipe. Complicating clinical management further was the erratic supply of antibiotics, coupled with the absence of microbiological diagnostic services to determine pathogen identity and resistance patterns. Consequently, clinicians were compelled to administer empirical, broad‐spectrum antimicrobial therapies. The reliance on incomplete treatment courses and the utilization of expired or substandard antibiotics has likely contributed to the proliferation of resistant strains, thereby undermining treatment efficacy and compromising patient outcomes [77, 79, 80].

At Al Ahli Arab Hospital, a leading tertiary center for orthopedic surgery in Gaza, one of the primary impediments to delivering effective surgical care is the inability to adequately sterilize medical equipment. Autoclave machines—critical for sterilizing surgical tools via high‐pressure steam—require ultra‐purified water with a Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) level approaching zero to function properly. However, the hospital's water supply has a TDS level of approximately 200, which, although deemed safe for consumption, is unsuitable for sterilization purposes. The presence of dissolved impurities hampers autoclave efficiency, resulting in frequent breakdowns and incomplete sterilization cycles. These malfunctions have disrupted operating room workflows and compromised the sterility of surgical instruments, particularly in open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) procedures, many of which have subsequently been complicated by postoperative surgical site infections related to implanted hardware [50, 81].

In response, the hospital has established a specialized infection control committee tasked with investigating the root causes of these infections. Concurrently, a new initiative has been launched to acquire a dedicated water purification system for the autoclaves to ensure consistent and effective sterilization. Nevertheless, the implementation of this critical infrastructure project has been severely delayed due to restrictions imposed by the Israeli military, which have obstructed the importation and installation of necessary equipment [50, 81].

Furthermore, patients with abdominal trauma from blast or shrapnel injuries frequently develop surgical site infections that are refractory to treatment in the absence of targeted antimicrobial therapy. The situation is especially dire for women, many of whom suffer from untreated urinary tract infections (UTIs) that progress to serious complications such as pyelonephritis and urosepsis, largely due to inadequate hygiene conditions and the lack of timely medical intervention [50].

In one reported case, a young woman presented with renal abscesses, likely attributable to the use of unhygienic cloths as substitutes for menstrual pads. Such instances underscore the urgent need for essential humanitarian aid and the strengthening of community‐based healthcare services capable of delivering timely medical intervention and promoting fundamental hygiene practices. Moreover, a critical shortage of antibiotic susceptibility testing materials was observed across all laboratory facilities, resulting in the manual processing of culture and sensitivity data. This situation was further exacerbated by a lack of basic communication infrastructure, including an insufficient supply of paper, printers, computers, and reliable internet connectivity [81].

Efforts to obtain telehealth consultations with external infectious disease specialists—particularly those advising the use of advanced antibiotics such as Linezolid or Daptomycin—are frequently hindered by the stark reality that these medications are not available locally. Consequently, healthcare providers in Gaza are left with few effective treatment options, significantly compromising patient outcomes in the management of complex infections [50].

Antibiotic prescribing patterns differ across hospitals, primarily guided by the clinical judgment and experience of consulting physicians, with limited input from microbiology laboratories. The availability of specialized care has been severely impacted by the abuse, torture, and targeted killing of numerous Palestinian healthcare professionals, further exacerbating the region's critical shortage of medical expertise. At Shuhada Al‐Aqsa Hospital, third‐generation cephalosporins are currently the antibiotics of choice, while agents such as colistin and amikacin are used less frequently. Vancomycin remains widely prescribed across facilities; however, its inconsistent supply often results in incomplete treatment regimens. Moreover, therapeutic drug monitoring for vancomycin is unfeasible due to the unavailability of essential diagnostic infrastructure in Gaza [50, 82, 83].

Endotracheal intubation in the clinical setting faced considerable obstacles due to inconsistent access to functional, sterile equipment. While medications such as ketamine and succinylcholine (scoline) were generally in stock, direct availability at the point of care was often restricted. Notably, opioids—critical for attenuating the cardiovascular stress response during intubation—were entirely absent. The sterility of endotracheal tubes and laryngoscope blades was unreliable; blades were frequently only surface‐wiped rather than properly sterilized. Moreover, the laryngoscope's light source was commonly dim, defective, or entirely nonfunctional, further complicating airway management. The availability of essential intubation aids, such as bougies and stylets, was similarly problematic, with many being either damaged or inadequately sterilized. Donated airway supplies were frequently rerouted to operating rooms, where stocks, though comparatively sufficient, lacked organized distribution systems. This reallocation exacerbated critical shortages in emergency departments. Post‐intubation sedation options were typically limited to additional ketamine or, less frequently, midazolam. Although propofol was present within the hospital, it was predominantly reserved for operating theaters and intensive care units, thereby limiting its availability for emergency and sedation use outside those settings [50].

Water Problems in Gaza

The Gaza Strip is experiencing an acute crisis in both water quantity and quality, driven by its arid climate, excessive withdrawal from its sole aquifer, and widespread contamination. Unlike the West Bank—which benefits from generous natural recharge—the Coastal Aquifer underlying Gaza receives only about 45 million m³ of rainfall recharge annually, supplemented marginally by treated effluents and return flows, yet suffers withdrawals exceeding 150 million m³ per year. This chronic overdraft creates an annual deficit of 30–40 million m³ (20%–27% of recharge), lowering groundwater levels below sea level and triggering seawater intrusion along the coast. Simultaneously, steepened hydraulic gradients draw in highly saline subsurface waters from the southeast. These processes have converted areas once characterized by fresh to slightly brackish water (200–350 mg/L Cl⁻) into zones with chloride concentrations above 600 mg/L—well beyond the WHO 250 mg/L guideline. To compound natural salinization, human activities such as the discharge of inadequately treated sewage through perforated wastewater lagoons, leaking distribution networks, unregulated agricultural runoff, and unmanaged solid waste have accelerated aquifer degradation, introducing excessive nitrates and pathogens. As a result, virtually no municipal well in Gaza now provides water that meets basic potable standards, and conditions continue to worsen [84, 85, 86].

Decades of conflict, blockade, and administrative neglect have further eroded Gaza's water infrastructure. Aging wells and pipelines—already in need of maintenance—were repeatedly damaged during military operations, with over a thousand wells and tens of kilometers of water mains rendered inoperative during the Second Intifada and subsequent campaigns. After Hamas assumed control in 2007, the Israeli blockade blocked essential imports—pipes, cement, steel, and pumping equipment—causing repair efforts to collapse within weeks. At the blockade's height, up to 40% of Gazan households had no running water at any given time due to diesel shortages that halted pumping stations and sent sewage systems toward collapse. Operation “Cast Lead” (2008–09) inflicted further damage by deliberately targeting water infrastructure, depriving one‐third of the population of safe water for ten days or longer, and inflicting an estimated US $44 million in environmental and agricultural losses. Sporadic pledges of international aid have failed to materialize into large‐scale rehabilitation, prolonging a vicious cycle of infrastructure breakdown and resource depletion [87, 88].

In daily life, Gazans confront highly intermittent water delivery—though 98% of homes are officially connected to the network, only 48% receive supply four or more days weekly, and in some localities water flows just once each week. The majority are forced to rely on private boreholes and informal desalination units—some 70 independently run facilities—paying up to 56 NIS/m³ compared to 0.5–1.5 NIS/m³ for municipal water. Even this costly water is often unsafe: mishandling during transport leads to bacterial contamination in 70% of tanker samples, and only 7.5% of municipal wells meet chloride standards. The convergence of extreme salinity, microbial pathogens, and chemical pollutants has elevated water‐borne illnesses—diarrhea, hepatitis A, typhoid, and parasitic infections—particularly among young children, 20% of whom suffered severe diarrheal disease in recent surveys. Absent an end to excessive groundwater abstraction, lifting of the blockade to permit infrastructure restoration, and comprehensive wastewater treatment, Gaza's water crisis will only intensify, imperiling the Strip's habitability [89, 90].

Recommendations and Future Directions

To improve clarity and facilitate evidence‐informed decision making, recommendations are presented in two distinct categories: A. Evidence‐based recommendations (actions supported by peer‐reviewed studies, systematic surveillance reports, or authoritative agency guidance) and B. Author's expert perspectives and research priorities (interpretive suggestions and proposed research questions where empirical evidence is currently limited). For each evidence‐based recommendation, we provide a short rationale and classify the level of evidence as High, Moderate, or Low (see key below). Although categorized separately for clarity, both evidence‐based recommendations and expert perspectives in this review are derived exclusively from published literature, WHO/UN guidance, and credible field reports; no primary opinions of the author are included.

Limitations and Challenges

Translating the urgent recommendations outlined above into effective public‐health outcomes in Gaza requires confronting a set of interconnected operational, political, and technical constraints. Key implementation challenges include: (1) limited and insecure access for humanitarian actors due to ongoing hostilities and movement restrictions; (2) fragmentation of supply chains and logistical bottlenecks exacerbated by fuel and transport shortages; (3) constrained local capacity and severe shortages in trained health personnel; (4) weak laboratory networks and surveillance systems that impede timely detection and response to outbreaks and AMR; (5) inconsistent financing and donor fatigue that limit scale‐up and sustainability; and (6) governance and coordination gaps across multiple international, national, and local actors that can lead to duplication or unacceptable delays. To address these barriers, international and regional stakeholders should pursue coordinated, practical pathways that combine immediate lifesaving interventions with medium‐ and long‐term system strengthening [51, 104, 105, 106].

While this review draws on diverse peer‐reviewed studies and institutional reports, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, much of the available data from Gaza are derived from aggregate figures reported by humanitarian agencies, government ministries, or non‐governmental organizations. These data are indispensable in conflict settings but may be affected by restricted access, disrupted surveillance systems, and damaged infrastructure, all of which can compromise completeness and accuracy. Second, mortality and morbidity statistics are often compiled under emergency conditions, creating potential risks of undercounting due to inaccessible areas or overestimation when secondary data sources are merged without full verification. Third, non‐peer‐reviewed reports may reflect organizational priorities or methodological variability, introducing possible reporting bias. To mitigate these risks, we prioritized sources from internationally recognized institutions (e.g., WHO, UNRWA, OCHA) and cross‐validated figures against multiple independent assessments wherever possible. Finally, the rapidly evolving conflict context means that some trends reported here may change substantially over time. Acknowledging these constraints is essential to ensure a balanced appraisal of the evidence, and highlights the urgent need for sustained, independent epidemiological surveillance and transparent data‐sharing mechanisms in conflict‐affected settings.

Conclusion

The protracted conflict and blockade in Gaza have precipitated a catastrophic convergence of healthcare collapse, environmental degradation, and rampant infectious disease transmission. This narrative review underscores the alarming surge in communicable diseases such as hepatitis A, acute respiratory infections, and polio—the latter re‐emerging after 25 years—alongside escalating AMR linked to inadequate infection control, antibiotic misuse, and infrastructural failures. Overcrowded shelters, contaminated water sources, and the deliberate targeting of healthcare facilities have created ideal conditions for pathogen spread, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations, including children, pregnant women, and immunocompromised individuals. The collapse of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) systems has further exacerbated waterborne disease outbreaks, with over 200,000 diarrheal cases reported, half involving children under five. Concurrently, multidrug‐resistant pathogens, such as carbapenem‐resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, highlight the dire consequences of disrupted antimicrobial stewardship and diagnostic shortages.

Addressing this crisis demands immediate, coordinated international intervention. Prioritizing WASH infrastructure restoration, rapid deployment of AMR diagnostics, and equitable access to essential medicines are critical to curbing morbidity and mortality. Strengthening disease surveillance, rebuilding healthcare capacity, and enforcing ceasefire agreements to facilitate humanitarian aid are equally urgent. The international community must also advocate for accountability to prevent further erosion of Gaza's health system and environment. Failure to act risks irreversible public health consequences, regional disease spread, and a prolonged humanitarian disaster. This review serves as both a scholarly analysis and a moral imperative, urging global health stakeholders to transcend political barriers and prioritize lifesaving interventions for Gaza's besieged population.

The health crisis in Gaza represents a multifactorial emergency in which the interplay of warfare, siege‐induced resource depletion, and environmental breakdown has fostered rampant infection and the rapid emergence of drug‐resistant pathogens.
