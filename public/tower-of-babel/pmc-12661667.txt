Diagnostic competence of senior dental students in detecting caries on panoramic radiographs with and without artificial intelligence assistance: a cross-sectional studycaries detection on panoramic radiographs
Authors: Oyun-Erdene Batgerel, Melda Pelin Akkitap, Rafat Sasany
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAccurate detection of proximal dental caries on panoramic radiographs is essential for effective treatment planning and preventive care. While senior dental students gradually develop interpretative competence during their training, artificial intelligence (AI) systems have emerged as promising adjuncts to enhance diagnostic performance. This study aimed to compare the diagnostic competence of fourth- and fifth-year dental students in detecting proximal carious lesions on panoramic radiographs, with and without AI assistance. It was hypothesized that AI-assisted evaluation would significantly improve diagnostic accuracy compared to unaided interpretation.MethodsA total of 132 dental students (66 fourth-year and 66 fifth-year) from Biruni University participated in this cross-sectional study. Sixty anonymized panoramic radiographs, each representing a single diagnostic question, and depicting various depths of carious lesions (enamel, dentin, or pulpal) were evaluated by a specialist in oral and maxillofacial radiology and a restorative dentistry expert. Disagreements were resolved by consensus, and their evaluation served as the reference standard. Each student assessed the presence and depth of caries in two separate sessions: first unaided and then assisted by AI-based diagnostic software (DeepDent AI, DeepInsight Technologies), trained on 10,000 annotated panoramic images and designed to highlight carious regions using bounding boxes and probability scores. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27, employing the Chi-square test, Mann–Whitney U test, and Cohen’s Kappa coefficient to compare diagnostic performance and inter-rater agreement. Intra- and inter-observer reliability were high (κ = 0.82 and κ = 0.79, respectively). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsFifth-year students demonstrated higher overall diagnostic performance than fourth-year students (p < 0.05). The highest rate of accurate diagnoses was observed for pulpal caries (39.4%), followed by dentin (6.1%) and enamel (5.3%) lesions. No statistically significant difference was found between the two student groups across caries depths (p > 0.05). The AI system achieved a sensitivity of 88.3%, specificity of 91.7%, and an overall accuracy of 90.1%, significantly outperforming both student groups (p < 0.001). Cohen’s Kappa coefficients (κ = 0.41–0.60) indicated moderate inter-rater agreement between students and the reference standard.ConclusionPanoramic radiographs alone provide limited accuracy for detecting early or shallow proximal caries, but diagnostic performance improves with educational level. The incorporation of AI-assisted diagnostic tools into undergraduate dental curricula may enhance students’ interpretative accuracy, strengthen clinical decision-making, and support early caries detection. Future studies should develop and evaluate standardized AI-integrated educational modules to optimize diagnostic proficiency in dental training.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08269-2.Keywords: Panoramic radiography, Dental caries, Diagnostic accuracy, Artificial intelligence, Student competence

Introduction

Panoramic radiography has become an indispensable diagnostic tool in contemporary dental education and clinical practice, offering a broad, two-dimensional overview of the maxillofacial region with minimal patient discomfort and radiation exposure [1–3]. Its broad anatomical coverage enables the assessment of dental caries, periodontal bone loss, developmental anomalies, and maxillofacial pathologies, making it one of the most frequently used imaging modalities in undergraduate dental curricula [1, 3, 4].

the diagnostic efficacy of panoramic radiographs for detecting proximal caries remains controversial. Limitations such as geometric distortion, overlapping anatomical structures, and relatively low spatial resolution hinder the visualization of early enamel and incipient dentin lesions [4, 5]. Consequently, panoramic radiography is less sensitive than bitewing imaging for detecting small proximal lesions, although it continues to be favored in teaching settings because it reflects real-world diagnostic experience and exposure. [5]

The ability to accurately detect and classify carious lesions radiographically is a core clinical competency that must be cultivated during undergraduate education. Early caries lesions are often asymptomatic and clinically invisible; hence, accurate radiographic interpretation plays a pivotal role in timely intervention and minimally invasive treatment[2, 5].From a pedagogical perspective, radiographic interpretation not only integrates didactic knowledge with clinical application but also reflects the effectiveness of dental curricula in fostering critical diagnostic skills[6].

Although numerous studies have examined diagnostic accuracy across various imaging modalities and clinician experience levels, a notable gap exists regarding how radiographic competence progresses throughout dental training. Comparative data between fourth- and fifth-year dental students who differ in theoretical knowledge retention, clinical exposure, and interpretive confidence remain limited.[2, 4, 6]. Expanding research in this area is crucial for identifying learning milestones and refining teaching strategies in radiologic education. [3, 6]

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have introduced new opportunities to augment radiographic education. AI-based diagnostic tools, particularly convolutional neural network (CNN) systems, can automatically detect carious lesions and provide feedback on lesion depth and location. These systems not only demonstrate superior accuracy compared to human observers but also have potential as interactive educational aids that reinforce diagnostic training and pattern recognition.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate and compare the diagnostic accuracy of fourth- and fifth-year dental students at Biruni University in detecting proximal caries on panoramic radiographs, both with and without AI assistance. It was hypothesized that AI-assisted evaluation would significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy compared with unaided interpretation. The findings of this study are expected to provide insight into the progression of radiographic competence among undergraduate students and support the integration of AI-assisted tools in dental education to optimize diagnostic proficiency.

Materials and methods

Research design

This cross-sectional study was conducted to assess the diagnostic proficiency of fourth- and fifth-year dental students at the Faculty of Dentistry, Biruni University, in identifying proximal dental caries on panoramic radiographs during the 2025 academic year.

A total of 132 students were recruited, comprising equal numbers from each academic year (n = 66 per group). All participants had previously completed foundational radiology coursework and received training in panoramic radiographic interpretation as part of their undergraduate curriculum. Students who had received additional training in dental radiology or had prior professional experience with radiographic diagnostics were excluded to maintain sample homogeneity.

The study protocol was approved by the Biruni University Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2024-BİAEK/07–56), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Radiograph Selection and Reference Standard

A total of 60 anonymized panoramic radiographs, each exhibiting carious lesions of varying depth, were selected from the university’s patient archive. All radiographs were acquired using the Orthophos Plus digital panoramic system (Sirona, Bensheim, Germany) under standardized exposure settings. Patient identifiers were removed, and all images were exported in JPEG format with uniform brightness and contrast adjustments to minimize bias.

Carious lesions were categorized according to a modified version of Lian et al. (2021) classification, based on lesion depth as follows:

Enamel caries: Radiolucency limited to the enamel or outer third of dentin.

Enamel caries: Radiolucency limited to the enamel or outer third of dentin.

Dentin caries: Radiolucency extending to the middle third of dentin.

Dentin caries: Radiolucency extending to the middle third of dentin.

Pulpal caries: Radiolucency penetrating the dentin into the pulp chamber.

Pulpal caries: Radiolucency penetrating the dentin into the pulp chamber.

The reference standard for diagnostic comparison was established by independent evaluations conducted by an oral and maxillofacial radiologist (M.P.A.) and a restorative dentistry specialist (O.-E.B.). Any disagreements between the two examiners were resolved by consensus after separate evaluations.

To ensure reliability, intra-observer agreement was verified by re-assessing 20 randomly selected images after a four-week interval, yielding κ = 0.82 for intra-observer and κ = 0.79 for inter-observer agreement, indicating strong consistency.

Diagnostic Task

Each student evaluated the 60 panoramic radiographs in two separate sessions:

Session 1 – Unaided interpretation: Students assessed each image without assistance.

Session 1 – Unaided interpretation: Students assessed each image without assistance.

Session 2 – AI-assisted interpretation: The same set of radiographs was re-evaluated one week later, this time with assistance from DeepDent AI (DeepInsight Technologies, Seoul, South Korea).

Session 2 – AI-assisted interpretation: The same set of radiographs was re-evaluated one week later, this time with assistance from DeepDent AI (DeepInsight Technologies, Seoul, South Korea).

DeepDent AI is a convolutional neural-network–based software trained on 10,000 annotated panoramic images. It automatically detects carious lesions and displays bounding boxes with probability scores indicating lesion depth (enamel, dentin, pulpal).

Radiographs were presented in randomized order generated by IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27, ensuring that the sequence differed between sessions to minimize recall bias.

All evaluations were performed on calibrated 27-inch LED monitors (1920 × 1080 resolution) under standardized ambient lighting conditions.

Students recorded lesion presence (yes/no) and depth (enamel/dentin/pulpal) using a secure Google Forms interface specifically designed for the study. Each session was limited to 30 min, allowing approximately 30 s per image, consistent with prior diagnostic performance studies.

Each panoramic radiograph represented one diagnostic question. Students were asked to identify the presence and depth of proximal caries on specific tooth surfaces. Examples of diagnostic questions included:

Is there any carious lesion on the mesial surface of tooth 26?

Is there any carious lesion on the mesial surface of tooth 26?

Does the radiolucency on the distal surface of tooth 37 extend into the dentin?

Does the radiolucency on the distal surface of tooth 37 extend into the dentin?

Does the radiolucency involving tooth 46 reach the pulp chamber?

Does the radiolucency involving tooth 46 reach the pulp chamber?

In total, 60 diagnostic questions were presented, covering enamel, dentin, and pulpal lesions across different tooth locations to comprehensively assess diagnostic accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Diagnostic performance was evaluated using Chi-square and Mann–Whitney U tests to compare student groups. Cohen’s Kappa (κ) coefficients were calculated to assess agreement between student assessments and the reference standard. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Diagnostic accuracy

The distribution of correct and incorrect responses for each question across both academic levels is illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2, which depict overall detection trends for enamel, dentin, and pulpal caries. These figures clearly demonstrate that diagnostic accuracy increases with lesion depth, consistent with the reduced radiographic contrast of early enamel lesions. Notably, questions 3 and 35 demonstrated statistically significant deviations in response accuracy (p < 0.05). All participants correctly diagnosed caries in question 35 (pulpal), whereas question 3 (dentin) showed a 25.76% reduction in correct responses across both groups.

Inter-Group agreement

The consistency of diagnostic responses between fourth- and fifth-year students is illustrated in Fig. 4. This figure summarizes Cohen’s Kappa values for selected questions, showing moderate agreement (κ = 0.41–0.60) for most items and fair agreement (κ = 0.21–0.40) for a limited number of dentin-level lesions. These findings indicate variability in diagnostic judgment between academic levels despite similar overall accuracy rates.

AI performance and diagnostic accuracy

The AI system significantly outperformed both student groups, (Table 4) achieving 88.3% sensitivity, 91.7% specificity, and 90.1% overall accuracy (κ = 0.78). Diagnostic performance improved markedly with AI assistance compared to unaided evaluation (p < 0.001), particularly for subtle enamel and dentin lesions that are frequently missed by human observers. AI integration enhanced agreement with the reference standard from moderate (κ = 0.54) to substantial (κ = 0.78), confirming its effectiveness in improving proximal caries detection on panoramic radiographs (Table 5).

Discussion

Accurate detection of dental caries is a critical component of preventive and restorative dental care. Developing diagnostic competence in undergraduate dental students, particularly in identifying early-stage lesions, is essential for ensuring timely interventions. This study aimed to evaluate the ability of fourth- and fifth-year dental students to detect proximal caries on panoramic radiographs a skill with significant clinical relevance in both educational and professional settings.

Radiographic imaging plays an essential role in caries detection, supplementing clinical examination. While occlusal, buccal, and lingual lesions are often detectable during clinical inspection, proximal caries, especially those limited to enamel or located at the enamel–dentin junction, typically require radiographic assessment for accurate diagnosis [6–9]. In this study, only proximal caries were assessed to target a diagnostically challenging yet educationally important lesion type.

Consistent with previous studies, diagnostic accuracy increased with lesion depth. Pulpal caries showed the highest detection rates, while enamel-level lesions were most frequently missed [8–13]. The notably low detection rates for enamel and dentin caries (approximately 5–6%) reflect the inherent limitations of panoramic radiographs in visualizing early demineralization, where anatomical superimposition and low image contrast hinder accurate interpretation.

These findings are consistent with prior reports demonstrating reduced sensitivity of panoramic imaging for incipient caries. [12, 14].

Although bitewing radiographs are considered the gold standard for proximal caries detection, particularly in posterior teeth, panoramic radiographs are widely used due to their broader anatomical coverage, ease of acquisition, and better patient compliance [15]. However, reliance on panoramic imaging alone for early caries detection has been discouraged, given its lower sensitivity for incipient lesions.[6, 11]. This findings reinforce this limitation while also highlighting the importance of structured radiographic training to improve interpretive accuracy.

Fifth-year students, with greater clinical exposure and diagnostic experience, demonstrated higher overall accuracy than fourth-year students, supporting the progressive improvement of interpretative skills through continuous education and hands-on practice [16]. Interestingly, fourth-year students occasionally outperformed fifth-year students in identifying certain enamel and dentin lesions. This may be attributed to a stronger emphasis on theoretical retention in earlier training stages, whereas senior students may prioritize complex clinical decision-making. This pattern underscores the need for periodic reinforcement of foundational diagnostic exercises in advanced curricula.

The inclusion of artificial intelligence (AI) assistance in this study demonstrated substantial improvements in diagnostic performance, particularly for enamel and dentin lesions that were frequently overlooked by students. Beyond enhancing accuracy, AI systems provide valuable educational support by offering immediate visual feedback, highlighting undetected areas, and facilitating repeated exposure to diagnostic challenges. Incorporating AI-driven modules into undergraduate curricula may therefore enhance students’ pattern-recognition abilities, reduce observer variability, and promote data-driven learning in dental education. [2, 5, 7].

A simplified version of Lian et al. [6] lesion classification was used in this study, categorizing lesions as enamel, dentin, or pulp involvement. Such classification systems are useful for teaching purposes, as they allow instructors to better track students’ interpretive progress and error patterns across different lesion severities.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study relied exclusively on panoramic radiographs, which provide lower spatial resolution than bitewing or periapical images. Although this choice standardized imaging conditions and reflected typical screening scenarios, it may limit the generalizability of findings to multimodal diagnostic contexts. Second, the research was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample size, potentially introducing institutional bias. Future multicenter studies including larger and more diverse student populations are recommended to validate these findings and to evaluate standardized AI-integrated training models.

From a clinical perspective, the results emphasize the potential of AI-assisted radiographic interpretation to improve early caries detection, minimize diagnostic inconsistency, and enhance patient outcomes through more accurate and preventive interventions. Integrating AI-based diagnostic systems into dental education could bridge the gap between theoretical learning and clinical application, preparing future dentists for technology-driven practice environments.

Conclusion

Panoramic radiographs remain valuable diagnostic tools for general caries assessment but have inherent limitations in detecting early or shallow proximal lesions due to reduced image resolution and anatomical superimposition. The diagnostic accuracy of dental students improves progressively with clinical education and repeated exposure to radiographic interpretation tasks. Integrating artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted diagnostic systems into undergraduate curricula may further enhance students’ interpretive accuracy, promote active learning through visual feedback, and reduce diagnostic variability.

The combination of structured radiographic training and AI-based educational support can help bridge the gap between theoretical instruction and clinical application, ultimately preparing students for technology-driven diagnostic practice. Future multicenter and longitudinal studies are warranted to evaluate the long-term benefits of AI-assisted training modules in improving both educational and clinical outcomes.
