L-carnosine alleviates wooden breast myopathy in broilers fed oxidized soybean oil: Focus on antioxidant defense, calcium homeostasis, and satellite cell function
Authors: Baohua Dong, Doudou Wang, Xiao'e Xiang, Xiaoli Shi, Liang Zhao, Tong Xing, Feng Gao, Lin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFeeding oxidized soybean oil (OSO) can disrupt the redox homeostasis in broilers and increase the incidence and severity of wooden breast (WB) myopathy. The antioxidant activity of L-carnosine (LC) has been demonstrated to help alleviate oxidative stress generated during metabolic processes. Therefore, the effects of dietary LC supplementation on WB myopathy were investigated in this study. One hundred and twenty-six 21-d-old white-feathered broilers (1.017 ± 0.039 kg) were randomly allocated into three groups (7 replicates per group with 6 broilers per replicate): fresh soybean oil group (CON), OSO group (OSO), and OSO + 800 mg/kg LC (OSO + LC), for a 21-d trial. Compared with the CON group, broilers in the OSO group showed an increased feed-to-gain ratio, elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) contents, and decreased LC content in the pectoralis major (PM) muscle (P < 0.05). Concurrently, disrupted muscle calcium homeostasis was observed in the OSO group, characterized by significant upregulation of calcium-regulating proteins such as MCU, RYR, and NCX, together with downregulation of SERCA in the PM muscle (P < 0.05). Upregulation of MYOD and PAX7 along with downregulation of MYF6 was observed in the OSO group compared to the CON group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the OSO group showed a 33.34% higher incidence of WB and increased muscle collagen deposition than the CON group (P < 0.05). Compared with the OSO group, broilers in the OSO + LC group exhibited reduced levels of oxidative stress markers (ROS, MDA, and protein carbonyls) and increased levels of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), superoxide dismutase (SOD), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) and LC in the PM muscle (P < 0.05). Muscle calcium homeostasis was rebalanced in the OSO + LC group through downregulation of IP3R, MCU, NCX, RYR, and PMCA, combined with upregulation of SERCA (P < 0.05). Moreover, the numbers of MYOD+ and PAX7+ cells in PM muscle of the OSO + LC group were lower than those of the OSO group (P < 0.05). Collagen deposition in the OSO + LC group was significantly reduced (P < 0.001) along with a decreasing trend in WB incidence compared to the OSO group (P = 0.043). The results indicate that OSO induces WB myopathy through oxidative stress, while LC effectively mitigates its adverse effects.Keywords: Broiler, Oxidized soybean oil, L-carnosine, Redox status, Calcium homeostasis, Wooden breast

Introduction

Energy plays a vital role in animal growth and development. In livestock and poultry production, lipids are the most extensively used metabolic substrates to meet their energy requirements. Beyond providing essential fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins, lipids function as solvents and carriers that facilitate nutrient absorption, modify fatty acid profiles in animal products, enhance feather and skin appearance in livestock and poultry, and even reduce dust generation during feed processing (Okrouhlá et al., 2018). However, the unsaturated fatty acids in vegetable oils, especially those in widely used oils like soybean oil in feed, are highly prone to oxidation during storage, transportation, and feed processing. This oxidative rancidity is not incidental but constitutes a prevalent and practical issue within the feed industry. In feed production, factors such as oxygen, light exposure, and metal ions can accelerate the oxidation of oils (Shahidi and Hossain, 2022), a process that generates toxic compounds including aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, and hydrogen peroxide (Valgimigli, 2023). The intake of oxidized oils disrupts normal metabolic processes, induces structural alterations and functional impairment in cells, and ultimately leads to lipid metabolism disorders and oxidative stress (Zhang et al., 2011). More notably, oxidative stress can disrupt calcium homeostasis in muscle cells, inhibit the proliferative capacity of satellite cells (SCs), and thereby impair normal muscle development and repair (Wang et al., 2025c). In commercial broiler production, high-energy diets are commonly used in the finishing phase for fast-growing broilers, with exogenous oil inclusion levels typically ranging from 4% to 8% of the feed formulation. A recent study has reported that high-energy diets and oxidized soybean oil (OSO) can synergistically induce wooden breast (WB) myopathy (Wang et al., 2025a). This result highlights that dietary inclusion of OSO can elevate the risk of WB myopathy in broilers. Characterized by pale, firm, and untextured muscle with reduced nutritional value (Sihvo et al., 2017), WB has become a critical issue in modern poultry production, resulting in substantial economic loss and reduced consumer satisfaction. Therefore, to ensure better broiler health and meat quality, there is an urgent need to explore effective nutritional strategies for reducing the incidence of WB myopathy.

Dietary supplementation with antioxidants is considered an effective strategy to mitigate damage induced by oxidized oils. L-Carnosine (β-alanyl-L-histidine; LC) is an endogenous dipeptide that is most abundant in the muscle and nervous tissues of animals (Kumar et al., 2025). The antioxidant activity of LC is achieved through several distinct mechanisms, including the chelation of metal ions, scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and neutralization of peroxyl radicals (Zhao et al., 2019). Its ability to scavenge peroxyl radicals is primarily attributed to the structural presence of the L-histidine residue. Additionally, carnosine can inhibit the Fenton reaction by chelating metal ions, thereby blocking free radical generation and ultimately reducing oxidative stress levels (Creighton et al., 2022). Recently, Li et al. (2025) reported that carnosine can suppress excessive ROS production and enhance the function of key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), suggesting its potential in ameliorating oxidative stress-related pathologies. Earlier studies have focused on the protective roles of antioxidants such as vitamin E and selenium (the cofactor of GSH-Px) in reducing the incidence of WB myopathy (Kuttappan et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). Research has also indicated substantially lower levels of carnosine and its metabolite anserine in WB muscle compared with normal breast tissue (Abasht et al., 2016). However, research on the impact of exogenous LC supplementation on WB occurrence remains limited. Therefore, this study was designed to explore the effects of LC on muscle redox homeostasis, SCs function, calcium homeostasis, and WB incidence in broilers fed OSO. The findings provide new insights into nutritional interventions to counteract the detrimental effects of oxidized oils and offer practical strategies for improving poultry meat quality and sustainability in intensive farming systems.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (NJAU, No. 20241104226).

Preparation of OSO

Fresh soybean oil was purchased from Shanghai Golden Dragonfly Supply Chain Management Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and used as the raw material. Oxidized oil was prepared by accelerated oxidation through continuous exposure to natural light combined with an airflow rate of 3 L/min (Wang et al., 2025a). The oxidation process was monitored by measuring the peroxide value (PV). When the PV reached 125.56 mEq/kg (initial PV of fresh oil: 10.62 mEq/kg), the soybean oil was considered to have met the oxidation criterion and was used to formulate the experimental diet. The PV of soybean oil was determined according to the method 965.33 of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 2023).

Experimental design, animals, and diets

A 21-d feeding trial was conducted using 126 healthy male Arbor Acres (AA) broilers (21 d of age). The birds were randomly divided into three dietary treatment groups, with each treatment consisting of seven replicates containing six birds per replicate: (1) CON group, a control diet containing fresh soybean oil; (2) OSO group, a diet containing OSO; and (3) OSO + LC group, a diet containing OSO supplemented with 800 mg/kg LC. The LC (98% purity) was obtained from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Throughout the experiments, the chicks had unrestricted access to nutritionally balanced pelleted feed and water. Housing consisted of three-tier stainless steel cages (90 cm × 60 cm × 35 cm) in a controlled environment maintained at 24 ± 1 °C. During the experiment, a photoperiod regimen of 22 h light and 2 h dark (22L:2D) was implemented, with a light intensity of 20 lux. The ingredients and nutrient levels of the diets are presented in Table 1. The experimental diets were formulated to meet the nutrient requirements of broilers according to the NY/T 33 - 2004 (Ministry of Agriculture of the People's Republic of China, 2004). The contents of crude protein (method 954.01), ether extract (method 920.39), calcium (method 968.08) and total phosphorus (method 964.06) in feed ingredients were analyzed according to the official method of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC, 2023). The levels of other nutrients in the diet were calculated based on the corresponding values of each ingredient in the China Feed Database (2019).

Growth performance evaluation

In this study, cages functioned as experimental units, with each treatment having seven replicates. On d 42, the body weight (BW) and feed intake (FI) of the animals were measured, and the body weight gain (BWG) and the feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) were calculated accordingly.

Sample collection

On d 42, one broiler with a BW close to the cage average was selected for sampling from each replicate cage. Thereafter, the bird was humanely euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation and subsequent exsanguination via transection of the carotid artery. Samples of the right PM muscle were immediately immersed in liquid nitrogen after collection and stored at −80 °C. Additionally, muscle tissue blocks (0.5 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm) were excised from the PM muscle and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent paraffin embedding and section preparation.

Redox homeostasis in PM muscle

The levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS; Cat. No. E004-1-1), malondialdehyde (MDA; Cat. No. A003-1-2), and protein carbonyl (PC; Cat. No. A087-1-2), as well as total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; Cat. No. A015-1-2), the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD; Cat. No. A001-1-2), catalase (CAT; Cat. No. A007-2-1), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px; Cat. No. A005-1-2) in PM muscle tissue were determined using commercial assay kits manufactured by the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Determination of carnosine content in feed and PM muscle

Feed samples (300 mg) or PM muscle samples (100 mg) were homogenized in 1 mL of ultrapure water by sonication at 4 °C for 30 min and centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Finally, 300 μL of the obtained supernatant was mixed with 900 μL of methanol to precipitate proteins, followed by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 3 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane and analyzed using a high-performance liquid chromatography system (Ultimate 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) with an injection volume of 10 μL at 30 °C. Separation was performed on a Zorbax ODS column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). The mobile phase was a mixture of 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid (A) and methanol (B) in a ratio of 90:10, with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Detection was carried out at a wavelength of 210 nm. The retention time for LC was established with a standard (Cat. No. SC5570, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Determination of fatty acid content in PM muscle

Muscle samples (1.0 g) were mixed with 1 mL of methanolic sodium hydroxide solution (1.5 mol/L) and homogenized (Tiss-24, Shanghai Jingxin Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). After homogenization, the mixture was centrifuged. The centrifuge tube was rinsed with 3 mL of the same methanolic sodium hydroxide solution, and the rinse liquid was combined with the previous mixture and vortexed thoroughly. After vortex mixing, the mixture was sonicated for 15 min. Subsequently, 4 mL of 1.5 mol/L sulfuric acid in methanol solution was added, followed by vortexing and repeated sonication for 12 min. For extraction, 2 mL of n-hexane was added to each sample and vortexed for 30 s. Phase separation was achieved by adding 5 mL of ultrapure water. The organic phase was collected, filtered through 0.22-μm membranes and analyzed by gas chromatography using an Agilent 8890 system (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a J&W high-performance capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.20 μm; Cat. No. GAAB-24317, Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). A mixture of 37 fatty acid standards (Cat. No. BYG8010, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was used to determine fatty acid retention times. Samples (1.0 μL) were introduced via split injection mode into an Agilent 8890 gas chromatograph system equipped with an autosampler and operated under the following conditions: injector, 270 °C; detector, 280 °C; split mode, 100:1 ratio. The temperature program was 100 °C (13 min hold), 10 °C/min to 180 °C (6 min hold), 1 °C/min to 200 °C (20 min hold), 4 °C/min to 230 °C (10.5 min hold).

Total protein extraction from PM muscle and Western blot analysis

Muscle samples were homogenized in a tenfold volume of radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer, then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatants. The protein concentration of the supernatant was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat. No. C503061, Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and the samples were normalized to a uniform concentration of 5 μg/μL with RIPA buffer. Finally, equal volumes of 5 × loading buffer were added to the samples. After thorough mixing, the mixture was denatured by heating at 100 °C for 10 min. Aliquots containing 20 μg of protein per sample were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to a 0.45 μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane using a wet transfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 90 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies used were as follows: mouse monoclonal anti-PAX7 (Cat. No. AB528428, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, Iowa City, IA, USA), rabbit polyclonal anti-MYOG (Cat. No. AF02847), rabbit polyclonal anti-MYOD (Cat. No. AF7733), rabbit monoclonal anti-NCX (Cat. No. AFRM81734) from Aifang Biological Co., Ltd. (Changsha, Hunan, China); rabbit polyclonal anti-IP3R (Cat. No. A21471), anti-MYF6 (Cat. No. A15291), anti-MEF2C (Cat. No. A12385), anti-MCU (Cat. No. A16281), anti-PMCA (Cat. No. A24346), and anti-SERCA (Cat. No. A11692) from Abclonal Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China); and rabbit polyclonal anti-RYR (Cat. No. K007816P) from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The full names of the abbreviations of all proteins can be found in Table S1. The following day, membranes were washed with Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) buffer for 30 min, incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Danvers, MA, USA) at room temperature (RT) for 1 h, and washed again with TBST. The blots were developed with an ECL chemiluminescence reagent (Cat. No. E422-C1, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), and image acquisition was performed using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). Band intensity was quantified by ImageJ (version 1.44p, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), with β-ACTIN serving as an internal control for normalization of target protein expression.

Immunofluorescence staining

Tissue samples fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned were used for staining. To improve antibody penetration, tissue sections were first treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% Triton X-100 (R21411, Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 10 min at RT. Following a quick PBS wash, the sections were treated with a blocking buffer consisting of 5% goat serum diluted in PBS and kept at RT for 30 min. Following removal of the blocking solution, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with diluted primary antibodies, including mouse monoclonal anti-PAX7 (Cat. No. AB528428, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, Iowa City, IA, USA) and rabbit polyclonal anti-MYOD (Cat. No. AF7733, Aifang Biological Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China). On the following day, the primary antibody solution was removed, and the sections underwent three rinses with PBS. They were then incubated for 1 h in the dark at RT employing the appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies: Cy3-conjugated goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) (Cat. No. A0521, Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Cat. No. A0423, Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). After three further PBS washes, nuclear staining was performed with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min protected from light. The sections were washed with PBS, embedded in anti-fade mounting medium, and coverslipped. Sections were observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Eclipse Ti–U, Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY, USA), and manual quantification was carried out for DAPI+, PAX7+, MYOD+, and PAX7+/MYOD+ double-positive cells.

Organelle isolation and measurement of calcium ion concentration

To extract the sarcoplasm, 0.5 g of muscle sample was homogenized in pre-cooled 0.15 mol/L KCl at a mass-to-volume ratio of 1:9 on ice. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 27,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C (Cat. No. Optima XPN-100, Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA), and the supernatants were collected as the sarcoplasmic fraction. For isolation of the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), 0.5 g of muscle samples were homogenized in pre-cooled extraction buffer A (0.1 mol/L KCl, 0.1 mmol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA], 20 mmol/L 3-(N-morpholino)-propanesulfonic acid (MOPS), pH 7.4) at a mass-to-volume ratio of 1:9 at 4 °C. The homogenate was centrifuged at 5000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then adjusted to a final concentration of 0.6 mol/L KCl by adding 3 mol/L KCl, followed by incubation at 4 °C for 20 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 23,500 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in buffer B (0.3 mol/L sucrose, 20 mmol/L MOPS, pH 7.0) and incubated for 1 h. The suspension was centrifuged again at 40,000 × g for 60 min at 4 °C using an Optima XPN-100 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA). The resulting pellet, representing the SR fraction, was resuspended in buffer B. Mitochondria were extracted from 100 mg of muscle tissue using a mitochondria isolation kit (Cat. No. C3606, Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). After resuspending the mitochondrial pellet in 50 μL of storage buffer, aliquots of the sarcoplasmic supernatant, SR suspension, and mitochondrial suspension were collected. To each sample, 10% trichloroacetic acid was added to achieve a final concentration of 0.8%, followed by a 10-min incubation. Strontium chloride was then introduced to a final concentration of 0.5%, and the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at RT. The supernatants were collected, and the calcium concentration was determined using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (iCAP 7000 Series, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Wooden breast occurrence and grading

Following CO2 stunning and slaughter, all remaining birds were dissected for WB assessment, with occurrence scoring and hardness grading by manual palpation following diagnostic criteria (Tijare et al., 2016; Papah et al., 2017). The WB grading levels are: normal (0 points), the entire PM muscle exhibits normal soft texture and elastic consistency throughout; mild (1 point), <25% of PM muscle belly palpably firmer than normal, with localized firmness in the cranial region with preserved flexibility; moderate (2 points), 25%-75% of PM muscle palpably firmer than normal, but the middle and posterior regions retain some elasticity; severe (3 points), >75% of PM muscle is abnormally firm and rigid throughout. To minimize variability in grading, all PM muscle evaluations were conducted by trained professionals.

Masson's trichrome staining and collagen area measurement

Following embedding in paraffin, transverse sections of muscle specimens were prepared at a thickness of 6 μm and subsequently processed using Masson's trichrome staining technique to differentiate muscle fibers (red) and collagen deposits (blue). Micrographs were acquired at consistent magnification levels employing an Axio Scope A1 optical microscope system (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Baden-Württemberg, Germany). For quantitative analysis of collagen area, three non-overlapping areas were randomly selected from each sample and analyzed using the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics Inc., Rockville, MD, USA). The collagen area was represented as a percentage of the total captured image area.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SAS version 9.1 software (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and all data were normally distributed except for those related to the different scoring degrees of WB myopathy. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed among treatment groups, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons of significant differences between treatments. Redox indicators and carnosine content in PM muscle

As shown in Table 3, compared with the CON group, the OSO group exhibited a 34% higher ROS concentration, a 66.67% increase in MDA content, and a 50% elevation in T-AOC in PM muscle (P < 0.05). The OSO + LC group exhibited a substantial improvement in oxidative status relative to the OSO group, marked by notable declines in ROS, MDA, and PC levels (P < 0.05). Concurrently, antioxidant capacity was enhanced, reflected by a 22.22% increase in T-AOC, and activities of SOD and GSH-Px were increased by 21.46% and 34.40%, respectively (P < 0.05). Compared to the CON group, the OSO + LC group exhibited a 28.60% decrease in PC content, an 83.33% increase in T-AOC, and a 38.40% increase in GSH-Px activity (P < 0.05). Relative to the CON group, LC levels in PM muscle declined by 16.15% in the OSO group; however, the OSO + LC group intervention markedly restored the content, showing a 70.08% elevation over the OSO group (P < 0.001).

Discussion

Excessive oxidation products in OSO decrease dietary lipid retention and reduce energy availability, ultimately impairing animal growth performance (Wang et al., 2025a). Iron-footed, hemp-feathered broilers fed diets supplemented with OSO (PV = 84 mEq/kg) exhibited a significant increase in F/G between 21 and 42 d of age (Chen et al., 2023). Similarly, the present study showed an elevated F/G in the OSO group relative to the CON group. It has been postulated that the depletion of vitamin E and accumulation of lipid peroxidation products in OSO could induce oxidative stress in broilers, resulting in diminished growth performance (Lindblom et al., 2019). Data from Askri et al. (2025) indicated that supplementation with 500 mg/kg carnosine in the diet of Ross 308 broilers did not significantly alter their growth performance. Similarly, in this study, no significant change in growth performance was observed when 800 mg/kg LC was added to feed containing OSO.

Oxidative stress results from an excess of ROS, primarily because the neutralization capacity of the body's antioxidant defense system is exceeded (Olufunmilayo et al., 2023). This study showed that the contents of ROS and MDA in the PM muscle of broilers in the OSO group were elevated, which is consistent with previous studies reporting increased MDA content in animals fed oxidized oil (Tang et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2025). Malondialdehyde is a key marker for evaluating lipid peroxidation and cellular membrane damage (Shu et al., 2022), and its increased concentration reflects lipid peroxidation and even the degree of oxidative stress. L-carnosine supplementation in this study lowered the levels of muscle oxidative products (ROS, MDA, and PC) and elevated antioxidant enzyme activities (T-AOC, SOD, and GSH-Px), an effect consistent with results from carnosine-supplemented diets in finishing pigs (Ma et al., 2010). These results support the hypothesis that feeding OSO creates a redox imbalance and oxidative stress, which can be alleviated by carnosine supplementation. The underlying mechanism involves carnosine serving as an efficient electron donor that directly neutralizes free radicals (Ghodsi and Kheirouri, 2018). Furthermore, carnosine not only detoxifies reactive carbonyl species via its imidazole ring, thereby limiting the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and advanced lipoxidation end products (ALEs), but also directly reacts with protein carbonyl groups to generate “carnosinylated” protein adducts, which effectively prevents cross-linking with other unmodified proteins (Boldyrev et al., 2013). Through these multi-level synergistic actions, carnosine clears oxidative products and helps restore physiological homeostasis. Consistent with previous findings (Kopec et al., 2020), the present study also observed that LC content in the PM muscle decreased in the OSO group, but was restored upon LC supplementation, suggesting that dietary LC supplementation can effectively enhance muscle tissue LC reserves of broilers. The reduction in LC in the OSO group may be due to compensatory consumption by the body to neutralize accumulated ROS (Abasht et al., 2016), which may further exacerbate oxidative stress.

A high intake of SFAs has been linked to an increased risk of obesity, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular diseases (Yi et al., 2023). In contrast, chicken meat is considered a healthy diet choice due to its higher proportion of PUFAs compared to many other meats (Berzaghi et al., 2005). Research shows that the fatty acid profile in broiler muscle closely reflects that of the diet, which directly influences the deposition patterns of fatty acids in tissues (Rymer and Givens, 2005). In the present experiments, broilers in the OSO group exhibited a significant increase in the proportion of total SFAs in their PM muscle compared to those fed the CON diet, while the levels of total MUFAs and PUFAs were decreased. These findings are consistent with previous studies, wherein oxidized dietary oils result in elevated saturated FA levels and diminished PUFAs (Mazur-Kuśnirek et al., 2019). Here, supplementing the OSO diet with LC led to a significant reduction in C16:0, total SFA, and the SFA/UFA ratio in PM muscle, while significantly increasing the levels of C20:5n3, C18:1n9c, C20:3n6, and total MUFAs. This may be due to inhibition of lipid oxidation via LC's antioxidant activity (Zhang et al., 2021).

Calcium ions play crucial roles in various cellular functions, acting as key regulators of enzymatic reactions and mediators in signal transduction pathways (Gong et al., 2021). In this study, analysis of PM muscle tissue from broilers fed OSO revealed significantly elevated Ca2+ concentrations in the sarcoplasm and mitochondria, accompanied by a reduction in the SR. This altered distribution pattern indicates that the oxidized oil disrupted cellular calcium homeostasis (Zhang et al., 2023), consequently affecting SR, which serves as the primary intracellular Ca2+ reservoir and is crucial for maintaining calcium balance and regulating cytosolic calcium release. In this study, the impaired Ca2+ storage function directly led to abnormal accumulation of Ca2+ in both the sarcoplasm and mitochondria. The resulting mitochondrial Ca2+ overload thereby disrupts normal metabolism and indicates a severe impairment of calcium buffering capacity (Giorgi et al., 2018). Following LC supplementation, Ca2+ levels in the sarcoplasm and mitochondria decreased significantly, accompanied by a restored calcium concentration in the SR, thereby confirming that LC supplementation helps re-establish calcium homeostasis. The disruption of Ca2+ homeostasis may be attributed to the excessive ROS production induced by soybean oil-triggered oxidative stress (Bertero and Maack, 2018). Under calcium overload conditions, elevated Ca2+ influx into mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum can stimulate mitochondrial ROS production, creating a vicious cycle (Görlach et al., 2015). By directly scavenging ROS, LC likely exerts its ameliorative effect, thereby breaking this detrimental feedback loop.

In the current study, the OSO group exhibited upregulated MCU, NCX, and RYR protein expression with concurrent downregulation of SERCA in PM muscle of birds, reflecting a molecular mechanism underlying the disrupted Ca2+ distribution reported earlier. Sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium-ATPase is primarily responsible for pumping cytoplasmic calcium back into the SR for storage, and its downregulation directly explains the reduction in SR calcium storage and the elevation of cytoplasmic calcium levels. Consequently, upregulation of calcium-release channels (IP3R and RYR) further promotes SR calcium leakage into the cytoplasm, aggravating cytoplasmic calcium overload (Steiner and Zierler, 2025). In addition, MCU is the core channel for mitochondrial calcium uptake, and its upregulation leads to abnormal accumulation of Ca2+ within mitochondria, which may disrupt mitochondrial function (Fan et al., 2020). On the cell membrane, NCX and PMCA collaboratively regulate intracellular Ca2+ levels. The upregulation of NCX may serve as a compensatory response to counteract cytoplasmic Ca2+ overload (Gu et al., 2025). Ultimately, this functional dysregulation of Ca2+ transporters is primarily driven by oxidative damage to their specific sulfhydryl groups, which thereby disrupts calcium homeostasis (Matuz-Mares et al., 2022). Following LC supplementation, the protein expression levels of IP3R, MCU, NCX, PMCA, and RYR were downregulated, while SERCA expression was restored. This suggests carnosine facilitates calcium homeostasis restoration at the molecular level by mitigating oxidative stress and protecting Ca2+ transporters from oxidative modification.

The maintenance of skeletal muscle relies on its stem cell reservoir, particularly SCs. These cells are located between the basal lamina and sarcolemma of muscle fibers, constituting a unique population of muscle precursor cells (Motohashi and Asakura, 2014; Seale et al., 2000), and serving as the primary cellular source for tissue repair and regeneration following muscle injury (Ahmad et al., 2023). The differentiation of myoblasts into muscle fibers is tightly regulated by myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs), including MYOD, MYOG, and MYF6 (Huang et al., 2024). During the initial activation of SCs, MYOD expression increases rapidly. Its expression is downregulated in mature muscle fibers, while MYF6 remains highly expressed, acting as the predominant MRF in differentiated muscle (Hernández-Hernández et al., 2017). Myocyte enhancer factor 2C is a crucial skeletal muscle-specific transcription factor that functions synergistically with myogenic factors like MYOD (Massenet et al., 2021). Paired box7 maintains SCs in an undifferentiated state by regulating MYOD. Prior to the initiation of terminal differentiation, PAX7 must be downregulated, a critical step for muscle fiber maturation (Hernández-Hernández et al., 2017). This investigation revealed an upregulation in the expression levels of both PAX7 and MYOD proteins, while MYF6 expression was downregulated in the PM muscle of the OSO group, indicating that OSO may activate SCs by initiating the repair of muscle damage. However, the sustained high expression of PAX7 and MYOD, coupled with low MYF6 expression, suggests that SCs failed to complete terminal differentiation and muscle fiber maturation. Compared with the OSO group, the OSO + LC group exhibited significantly decreased protein levels of PAX7, whereas MYF6 expression displayed an increasing tendency. These findings align with earlier research showing carnosine preserves myoblast function and promotes its activity under oxidative stress conditions (Palin et al., 2020), indicating that carnosine likely supports normal muscle repair by mitigating oxidative damage, thereby enabling SCs to progress smoothly from aberrant activation to differentiation and maturation. In muscle development, PAX7 and MYOD are key transcription factors regulating myogenesis. Quiescent SCs are PAX7+/MYOD−, whereas PAX7+/MYOD+ cells represent activated muscle SCs capable of proliferation, expression of myogenic factors, and differentiation (Zammit et al., 2004). Immunofluorescence co-localization analysis of PAX7/MYOD in the PM muscle revealed that the OSO group had a significant increase in the density of total nuclei, SCs, and myoblasts within a defined area, along with an elevated proportion of PAX7+/MYOD+ double-positive cells. These findings indicate that oxidative stress induced by OSO activated the SC repair mechanism, but the differentiation process was likely impaired, as evidenced by the lack of PAX7 downregulation, potentially associated with oxidative stress and the disruption of calcium homeostasis. After LC supplementation, the proportion of PAX7+/MYOD+ cells decreased significantly, likely because LC improved the SC microenvironment through its antioxidant and calcium-buffering effects, facilitating the successful completion of activation, proliferation, differentiation, and fusion processes.

Wooden breast myopathy is characterized by hardened musculature exhibiting hypertonicity, with distinct pathological features including parallel white striations along muscle fibers, gelatinous exudates, hemorrhagic foci in cranial regions, and caudal thickening with spiny protrusions (Sihvo et al., 2014). Histopathological analysis revealed multifocal degenerative changes, necrotic areas, macrophage and heterophil infiltration, and expanded interstitial connective tissue (Pejšková et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025b). While the precise etiology remains unclear, current hypotheses implicate interrelated mechanisms involving localized hypoxia, oxidative stress, inflammatory cascades, and metabolic dysregulation (Petracci et al., 2019). In this study, a higher prevalence of WB was observed in the OSO group (66.67%) compared with the CON group (50.00%). Histological measurements demonstrated an 85.47% elevation in perimysial collagen deposition within PM muscle tissue from OSO compared to CON, mirroring previously documented WB muscle patterns (Zhu et al., 2023). This fibrotic transformation, evidenced by elevated total collagen content, reflects dysregulated tissue repair mechanisms under chronic inflammatory conditions (Wynn, 2008). Fibrosis has also been observed in the PM muscle of broilers, characterized by the accumulation of loose connective tissue, granulation tissue, and collagen-rich connective tissue (Kuttappan et al., 2013). Previous studies have shown alterations in the fatty acid composition of muscles affected by WB, characterized by increased muscle SFAs and decreased PUFAs (Liu et al., 2022). These findings are consistent with the changes in fatty acid composition of PM muscle measured in the present study. The elevated collagen accumulation in the OSO group may originate from oxidative stress-mediated degeneration of muscle fibers and disruption of membrane structures, consequently triggering SR calcium influx leading to necrosis (Wang et al., 2025c). In the absence of adequate capillary support, SC-mediated repair and regeneration processes following injury would be compromised, thereby promoting the fibrotic process. This leads to the progressive deposition of fibrillar collagen within the perimysial and endomysial connective tissues (Velleman, 2019) and ultimately results in the formation of WB meat.

In contrast, dietary LC supplementation resulted in a decreasing trend in the overall incidence of WB, along with a significant decrease in collagen levels within the pectoral muscle. Carnosine, with its strong antioxidant properties, is capable of scavenging hydroxyl radicals and inhibiting protein cross-linking (Fu et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2003). Moreover, studies have indicated that dietary supplementation with LC lowers the incidence of WB in broilers (Askri et al., 2025). Interestingly, a study by Sundekilde et al. (2017) revealed that the LC concentration was markedly reduced in WB muscle compared with normal breast tissue. Collectively, experimental evidence suggests that dietary supplementation with exogenous carnosine can elevate carnosine concentrations in PM muscle of broilers, reduce oxidative damage, and consequently attenuate muscle fiber fibrosis, potentially preventing the development of oxidative myopathy.

Conclusion

In summary, dietary OSO impaired growth performance, disrupted redox balance, and induced oxidative stress in broilers, thereby leading to dysregulation of calcium homeostasis and SC dysfunction, which ultimately promoted WB myopathy. Dietary supplementation with 800 mg/kg of LC effectively alleviated these adverse effects, indicating that LC, as a potent antioxidant, may serve as a nutritional strategy to mitigate the physiological damage caused by OSO in broilers. To establish a solid theoretical basis for the scientific application of carnosine in the poultry industry, future studies should address two key questions, whether exogenous LC supplementation affects endogenous synthesis and through which specific signaling pathways carnosine regulates calcium ion homeostasis and SCs function.
