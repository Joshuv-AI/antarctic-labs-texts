Impact of Tumor Genomic Profile on Adjuvant Chemotherapy Efficacy in Resected Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma: Results From the PRODIGE-24/CCTG PA6 Study
Authors: Andréa Witz, Thierry Conroy, Aurélien Lambert, Julia Salleron, Aboubacar Diallo, Marie Husson, Rémy Nicolle, Pascal Hammel, James Biagi, Daniel J Renouf, Anthony Turpin, Corentin Richard, Jean-Baptiste Bachet, Juan Iovanna, Nelson Dusetti, Laure Monard, Marjorie Mauduit, Jérôme Cros, Alexandre Harlé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEModified fluorouracil, leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin (mFOLFIRINOX/mFFX) is the standard adjuvant chemotherapy for resected pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), offering survival benefits over gemcitabine (GEM). However, the contribution of molecular biomarkers to treatment selection remains unclear. Here, we characterize the molecular landscape of tumors from the PRODIGE-24/CCTG PA6 trial and assess the clinical impact of genomic alterations and molecular subtypes.PATIENTS AND METHODSTumor DNA sequencing was successfully performed in 317/350 tumors (168 mFFX; 149 GEM), complemented by transcriptomic subtyping using the PurIST classifier. Mutational status of four key PDAC driver genes and 24 homologous recombination repair (HRR)–associated genes was analyzed, alongside single-base substitution (SBS) mutational signatures. Primary and secondary end points were disease-free survival (DFS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS), respectively.RESULTSIn the mFFX group, the PurIST subtype was prognostic, with classical tumors showing superior DFS compared with basal-like tumors (stratified hazard ratio [sHR], 0.48 [95% CI, 0.31 to 0.77]). Among KRAS-mutated patients, mFFX significantly improved DFS compared with GEM (sHR, 0.60 [95% CI, 0.45 to 0.79]; P < .001), while no benefit was observed in KRAS wild-type tumors (interaction test, Pint. = 0.010). HRR and BRCA status were not predictive (Pint. = .568 and Pint. = .785, respectively). The benefit of mFFX was consistent across SBS-positive and SBS-negative subgroups.CONCLUSIONOverall, these results do not support a change in current adjuvant treatment strategies. mFFX remains the standard adjuvant regimen in PDAC, and the observed lack of benefit in KRAS wild-type tumors should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrants further investigation.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) accounts for the majority of pancreatic cancer cases.1 The 5-year relative survival rate for pancreatic cancer is 13% across all stages,2 rising to over 20% in patients undergoing upfront surgical resection followed by adjuvant therapy.3 With modified fluorouracil, leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin (mFOLFIRINOX/mFFX) as adjuvant treatment, the 5-year survival rate is 43.2%.4 As a result, mFFX has become the standard of care in the adjuvant setting.5 However, like all adjuvant polychemotherapy regimens, its use is restricted to patients with full recovery of a good performance status within 12 weeks from surgery.6

Recent advances in precision medicine enabled treatment decisions and prognosis to be guided by molecular characteristics, including in early-stage PDAC.7 Notably, transcriptomic analyses from the PRODIGE-24/Canadian Cancer Trial Group (CCTG) PA6 trial validated predictive signatures for response to gemcitabine (GEM) and mFFX.8,9 In parallel, genomic profiling studies in metastatic PDAC expanded, enabling identification of subgroups with potentially actionable alterations,10 although such data remain scarce in resected disease.

Patients with advanced or metastatic PDAC exhibiting homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) have shown improved outcomes when treated with platinum-based chemotherapy.11,12 HRD is primarily defined by mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, but other genes involved in the homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathway—such as PALB2, RAD51, and its paralogs—also garnered increasing interest.13 However, findings in resected PDAC remain inconclusive. Notably, no difference in survival was found between platinum-naïve and platinum-treated patients with HRD-positive resected PDAC, although a trend toward improved outcomes was reported in the latter group.12,14

Here, we characterized selected genomic alterations in 317 patients from PRODIGE-24/CCTG PA6 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01526135), assessed the prognostic impact of basal-like and classical subtypes, and evaluated HR gene alterations in relation to outcomes with mFFX or GEM in the adjuvant setting.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study Design and Population

PRODIGE-24/CCTG PA6 was a previously published multicenter, open-label phase III trial.15 The study protocol was approved by independent ethics committees and by the PRODIGE scientific review board (EudraCT: 2011-002026-52). Written informed consent was obtained for all participants. This study was conducted within the MOSAPAC project and supported by a national grant (PRT-K19-131).

Nucleic Acid Extraction

Tumor biopsies were centralized by UNICANCER within the biobank of the Beaujon Hospital Pathology Department. Tumor areas for macrodissection were selected by a senior pathologist based on tumor-cell content. DNA and RNA were extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor block using the AllPrep DNA/RNA mini kit (Qiagen, Hilde, Germany).

Genomic Analyses

Extracted DNA was qualified using TapeStation System (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and quantified using Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Hilden, Germany). Library was prepared with the Cell3 Target Pan-Cancer panel (Nonacus Limited, Birmingham, United Kingdom; 524 genes, 1.86 Mb) and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA).

FastQ DNA sequencing data were aligned with the hg19 reference human genome using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner.16 Samtools, bcftools,17 and Picard18 tools were used to perform intermediate processing of Sam/Bam files and deduplication. Calling of single-nucleotide variant (SNV) and insertion/deletions (indels) was carried out with Mutect19 and Scalpel,20 respectively, and VarScan221 for both with a minimum variant allele frequency of 2%. Variant effect predictor was used to annotate variants.22 Details are provided in the Appendix (online only).

Transcriptomic Analyses

RNA sequencing was performed by Nicolle et al8: 150 ng RNA was processed with the Lexogen QuantSeq 3’ FWD kit and sequenced by the Institut du Cerveau iGenseq platform on a NovaSeq 6000 (≥10M reads).

Raw RNAseq reads were mapped to the human genome (Ensembl GRCH38) and Ensembl's reference transcriptome using STAR.23 Gene counts were obtained using featureCounts,24 normalized by using the upper quartile procedure, and logged on a base 2.

The Purity Independent Subtyping of Tumors (PurIST) score and classification were determined as previously described.25

Statistical Analyses

Disease-free survival (DFS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were analyzed using stratified Cox proportional hazards models, with results reported as hazard ratios (sHR) and 95% CIs. Median DFS (mDFS) and median CSS (mCSS) were estimated with 95% CIs. The predictive value of mutational status was assessed using interaction terms in Cox models. Sensitivity analyses included multivariable and competing-risks approaches. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < .05. All statistical methods are detailed in the Appendix Supplementary Methods.

Survival Analyses by PurIST Molecular Subtypes

Only tumor grade differed between basal-like and classical PurIST subtypes, with more well-differentiated tumors in the classical group (P = .028; Appendix Table A3).

The interaction between adjuvant treatment and molecular subtype was not statistically significant for either DFS (Pint. = .298; Fig 2A) or CSS (Pint. = .262; Appendix Fig A3). Among patients with a classical subtype (n = 253/317, 79.8%), those treated with mFFX (n = 136/253, 53.7%) had significantly longer DFS compared with those treated with GEM (n = 117/253, 46.3%; sHR, 0.62 [95% CI, 0.46 to 0.84]; Fig 2A). A similar benefit was observed for CSS (sHR, 0.65 [95% CI, 0.46 to 0.91]; Appendix Fig A3). Within the mFFX group, the PurIST subtype was significantly associated with DFS (sHR, 0.48 [95% CI, 0.31 to 0.77]; Fig 2B), but not with CSS (sHR, 0.60 [95% CI, 0.35 to 1.01]; Appendix Fig A3).

Genetic Landscape in Resected PDAC

Oncogenic KRAS mutations were detected in 87.4% of tumors (n = 277/317 patients; Fig 3A), with 12.6% of KRAS-mutated cases (n = 35/277) harboring variant read counts <20. Only Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status differed between KRAS wild-type (KRASwt) and KRAS-mutated (KRASm) tumors, with ECOG 0 more frequent in KRASwt patients (n = 27/40, 68%, v n = 136/277, 49%; P = .033; Appendix Table A4). The most prevalent hotspot mutation was KRASG12D, found in 43.7% of cases (n = 121/277; Fig 3B).

A significant interaction between adjuvant treatment and KRAS mutational status was observed for DFS (Pint. = .010), as well as for individual KRAS variants (Pint. = .034; Fig 3C), suggesting a differential benefit of mFFX versus GEM according to KRAS status. In KRASm patients—particularly those with KRASG12D and KRASG12V—mFFX significantly improved DFS compared with GEM, while no benefit was observed in KRASwt patients or those with non-KRASG12D/G12V variants (Fig 3C).

Within the mFFX group, DFS was significantly longer in KRASm patients compared with KRASwt patients (sHR, 0.44 [95% CI, 0.25 to 0.78]; P = .005; Fig 3D), whereas no significant differences were observed within the GEM group (sHR, 1.21 [95% CI, 0.71 to 2.08]; P = .486). In particular, patients harboring KRASG12D, KRASG12V, and KRASG12R mutations showed significantly better DFS, while no survival difference was observed among KRAS subgroups in the GEM group (Appendix Table A5).

Similar trends were observed for mCSS (Appendix Fig A4 and Table A5).

Among other commonly altered PDAC driver genes, mutations were detected in TP53 (53.6%, n = 170/317), SMAD4 (9.1%, n = 29/317), and CDKN2A (6.6%, n = 21/317; Fig 3A). SMAD4m tumors more often received GEM than SMAD4wt (66% v 45%; P = .036; Appendix Table A4).

No interaction was observed between treatment and TP53, SMAD4, or CDKN2A status for DFS (Fig 3E) or CSS (Appendix Fig A4), and none of these mutations were prognostic (Appendix Table A5).

Alterations in genes involved in the HRR pathway (HRR-associated genes) were identified in 29.7% of patients (n = 94/317; HRm). Among these HRm patients, 45.7% (n = 43/94) harbored mutations in core HRR-associated genes (cHRm; BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, and RAD51C/D), while 54.3% (n = 51/94) had noncore HRR mutations (ncHRm). Pathogenic or likely pathogenic BRCA mutations were identified in 11.6% (n = 37/317) of patients, of which 16.2% (n = 6/37) were estimated to be germline (gBRCAm) and 83.8% (n = 31/37) somatic (sBRCAm). Among BRCAm patients, 35.1% (n = 13/37) were BRCA1-mutated (BRCA1m) and 64.9% (n = 24/37) were BRCA2-mutated (BRCA2m). PALB2 mutations were identified in 2.5% (n = 8/317), while no pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations were found in RAD51C or RAD51D. Notably, two patients within the cHRm group (4.7%) harbored pathogenic mutations in both BRCA and PALB2 genes. HRm was not associated with clinicopathologic characteristics or treatment group (Appendix Table A6).

No interaction was observed between HRm and treatment for DFS or CSS; BRCA and other HRR-associated genes status were also nonpredictive (Figs 4A, 4C; Appendix Fig A5).

We evaluated the prognostic impact of HRR-associated gene mutations within each treatment group (Figs 4B, 4D; Appendix Table A7). In the GEM group, patients with sBRCAm had a sHR for CSS of 2.18 (95% CI, 1.15 to 4.14) compared with BRCAwt patients (Appendix Fig A5 and Table A7). DISCUSSION

In this study, we retrospectively explored the predictive and prognostic value of PurIST subtypes and genomic alterations in 317 patients enrolled in the PRODIGE-24/CCTG PA6 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01526135). Although limited by its retrospective nature and the availability of high-quality tumor samples, the molecular cohort exhibited survival outcomes consistent with those observed in the overall trial population, supporting the representativeness of our findings.

Although BRCA mutations and HRD status are well-established predictors of response to platinum-based therapy,14,26 such alterations are found in only a minority of patients with PDAC.11 In our study, the prevalence of HRm in the 24 genes studied was 30%, consistent with previously published data.12,13 Furthermore, the distribution of alterations in HRR-associated genes was similar between treatment groups, suggesting no allocation bias. To note, alterations detected here in genes involved in the HRR pathway should not be equated with HRD status, which is defined by specific genomic hallmarks and has established clinical relevance. In this study, the DNA sequencing approach did not allow assessment of genomic scarring patterns required to formally define HRD. Multiple definitions and methods exist for assessing HRD status, leading to variability in both reported prevalence and clinical relevance.27 The genomic instability score (GIS) obtained with the US Food and Drug Administration–approved Myriad MyChoice CDx test is the gold standard in ovarian cancer28 and other clinically validated solutions have been proposed.29-31 However, these tests typically require high-quality FFPE samples with >20% tumor-cell content,32 which is often difficult to obtain with PDAC due to its dense stroma and marked heterogeneity—a limiting factor in our study, rendering the shallowHRD method33 noninformative (data not shown). Future analyses incorporating these approaches are essential to draw more definitive conclusions.

Among patients with alterations in core HR genes (ie, BRCA1, BRCA2, and PALB2), neither DFS nor CSS differed significantly compared with HRwt tumors, aligning with previous studies in similar adjuvant settings.14,34 This lack of association also extended to noncore HR-genes, such as ATM and CHEK2. Although some studies reported a trend toward improved survival in PDAC HRm patients receiving platinum-based therapy after resection, these findings did not reach statistical significance when compared with HRwt patients.35,36 Conversely, in the neoadjuvant setting, BRCA mutations have been associated with enhanced response to mFFX, suggesting a tumor stage–dependent predictive value of HRm.37,38

Interestingly, our findings suggest that BRCA mutations may be prognostic with adjuvant GEM, with poorer CSS in sBRCAm patients (23.8 v 36.7 months; sHR, 2.18 [95% CI, 1.15 to 4.14])—a trend consistent with that reported by Pishvaian et al, albeit without significant statistical difference in their study.36 Conversely, we did not observe a prognostic value of BRCA alterations for platinum-treated patients, although patients with presumed gBRCAm in the mFFX group showed improved survival, in line with prior observations.14 However, the statistical power of this comparison was limited due to the small number of gBRCAm cases (n = 5). Importantly, our study could not definitively distinguish between somatic and germline variants due to the absence of matched tumor-normal sequencing. In addition, allelic status (monoallelic v biallelic) could not be assessed, implicating that some identified HRR-associated gene alterations consisted of single-allele events, which may simply be a passenger mutation.39 However, these two factors are known to influence the clinical incidence of HRD.40 Accordingly, HRR-associated gene alterations are reported for descriptive purposes and should not be interpreted as surrogate markers of HRD status. To better clarify the predictive and prognostic relevance of mutations in HRR-associated genes in PDAC, comprehensive germline and somatic profiling is warranted. The ongoing Apollo study (NCT04858334/EA2192) will evaluate adjuvant olaparib in 152 resected PDAC patients with BRCA1/2 or PALB2 mutations and may clarify the impact of germline versus somatic alterations.41

Recent studies have underscored the potential of transcriptomic signatures, such as GemPred, GemCore, and Pancreas-View, to personalize chemotherapy in PDAC. GemPred was developed and validated for guiding adjuvant GEM treatment in resected patients,8 and GemCore for both adjuvant and metastatic diseases.42 The most recent and advanced of these signatures is Pancreas-View, applicable to both resected and metastatic patients, predicting the benefit of GEM and mFFX chemotherapy regimens.8,9,43 In this context, the PurIST classifier has emerged as a promising tool to stratify tumors into classical and basal-like subtypes with distinct biological and clinical behaviors.25 Across studies, the classical subtype is consistently associated with better outcomes, particularly among patients treated with neoadjuvant mFFX.25,44,45 In the PRODIGE-24/CCTG PA6 trial, we confirmed the prognostic value of the classical subtype among patients receiving adjuvant mFFX. Despite hypotheses favoring GEM-based regimens in basal-like tumors, our data do not support enhanced GEM sensitivity in this subgroup, consistent with Singh et al.46 The interaction test between the treatment group and the PurIST molecular subtype was not statistically significant. However, given the imbalance in subgroup size (basal-like, n = 64 v classical, n = 153), limited statistical power precludes definitive conclusions regarding the predictive value of the PurIST classification. These findings highlight the need for novel therapeutic strategies in basal-like tumors rather than preferential reliance on existing GEM-based approaches. Importantly, the PurIST classifier could be implemented in clinical practice using standardized RNA-based assays. The classical subtype could be integrated to refine prognosis stratification and predict mFFX sensitivity in the adjuvant setting.

The prevalence of TP53 alterations in our cohort was consistent with previously published data.47 By contrast, SMAD4 and CDKN2A mutations were less frequent than expected.48 This may partly reflect characteristics of our cohort, which consisted exclusively of patients with early-stage PDAC, but technical factors may also have contributed. Low tumor cellularity can reduce the mutant allele fraction, thereby limiting the sensitivity of variation detection.19,49 Variability in sequencing quality metrics, including coverage depth and uniformity, may further affect the reliable detection of somatic alterations at low allelic fractions.50,51 Moreover, variant calling was restricted to alterations predicted to have functional impact, potentially reducing the apparent prevalence of these alterations. Finally, the present analysis focused on mutational status, and copy-number variations were not assessed. As CDKN2A deletions are frequent and biologically relevant in PDAC,52 this omission may have contributed to underestimation of alterations in this gene. Collectively, these considerations should inform interpretation of the genomic results.

Survival analyses revealed no prognostic or predictive values for SMAD4 and CDKN2A, in agreement with the study by McIntyre et al.48 Although prior studies in the neoadjuvant and metastatic settings have suggested a survival advantage in TP53wt tumors treated with mFFX,53,54 we observed no association between TP53 status and clinical outcomes. Taken together, these findings suggest that, although TP53, SMAD4, and CDKN2A mutations contribute to the molecular landscape of PDAC, they may not serve as robust prognostic or predictive biomarkers in patients undergoing resection followed by either GEM or mFFX adjuvant treatment.

In this cohort, KRASwt tumors comprised 12.6%, at the upper end reported in resected PDAC cohorts.34,55,56 Alternative mechanisms of RAS-pathway activation, such as alterations in NF1,57 may occur, but only one KRASwt tumor harbored a pathogenic NF1 variant in our cohort (data not shown). In addition, false-negative KRAS mutations related to analytical constraints cannot be entirely excluded. These observations underscore the biological and technical heterogeneity of KRASwt tumors, highlight the need for integrative additional genomic analyses beyond mutational status, and warrant cautious interpretation of the results in this subgroup.

Multiple studies reported that KRASm tumors are associated with worse prognosis compared with KRASwt, regardless of disease stage.48,54 This trend was reflected in our GEM group where KRASwt exhibited longer survival than KRASm, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. These findings raise the possibility that KRASwt tumors may represent a distinct subgroup with potential differential sensitivity to GEM-based regimen. In this context, integrating genomic features such as KRAS status with transcriptomic predictors of GEM benefit, including GemPred status,8 warrants further investigation. Conversely, given the poor prognosis and chemoresistance associated with KRASm tumors, especially to GEM, novel therapeutic approaches such as the KRASG12D inhibitor MRTX113358 and the KRASmulti inhibitor RMC-6236 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06625320)59 are currently being explored and may surpass GEM efficacy in this population. In the mFFX group, we observed an unexpected trend toward improved survival in KRASm patients compared with KRASwt, irrespective of the KRAS variant. This observation contrasts with previous findings in resected PDAC patients treated with adjuvant mFFX, in which KRASwt status was typically associated with more favorable outcomes.34 Notably, Keane et al reported a median recurrence-free survival of 22 months for KRASm patients treated with adjuvant mFFX,34 closely mirroring the mDFS of 21.7 months observed in our KRASm subgroup.

Importantly, we identified a predictive value for KRAS mutational status, suggesting that KRAS status may serve as a biomarker to guide adjuvant treatment selection—an association not previously reported in resected PDAC. Supporting this hypothesis, Norton et al observed improved outcomes for certain KRAS variants with FFX over GEM in the metastatic setting.60 The impact of KRAS mutations on response to adjuvant chemotherapy remains understudied, largely due to the small proportion of KRASwt patients in PDAC cohorts, and warrants further validation in prospective data sets with variant-specific analyses.

Beyond individual gene alterations, we investigated broader mutational processes by analyzing SBS signatures, which may offer complementary insights into tumor biology and therapeutic response. Across SBS-positive and SBS-negative subgroups, the overall benefit of mFFX remained consistent. Within the GEM group, SBS2 and SBS13—both associated with APOBEC enzyme activity61—showed a trend toward poorer survival, consistent with previous reports linking APOBEC-related mutagenesis to GEM resistance.62 Further validation in larger cohorts is required.

The SBS3 signature was detected in 9% of patients, approximately half the frequency reported in comparable cohorts using whole-exome sequencing (WES) or whole-genome sequencing (WGS).11,14 This lower prevalence likely reflects methodological limits of the targeted panel used in our study (524-gene panel), which restricts the number and spectrum of somatic variants available for reliable signature extraction. In particular, mutational signatures with relatively flat mutational profiles, such as SBS3, are more challenging to assign reliably and prone to variability across analytical methods, especially when mutation counts are limited.11,63,64 Moreover, SBS3 assignment can vary depending on the analytical pipeline used, with a reported detection rate differing by up to twofold across the tools used.65 These constraints likely reduced the SBS3 detection sensitivity and may also explain the lack of correlation with BRCA mutational status in our cohort, despite the well-established association of this signature with the biallelic BRCA inactivation.66 In this context, SBS3 identified using targeted sequencing panels should be interpreted with caution and does not appear as a reliable HRD biomarker. Additionally, no significant prognostic or predictive value was observed for SBS3, aligning with prior findings by Golan et al.14 Overall, these results underscore the limitations of relying on individual mutational signatures obtained from targeted panels to define HRD, and support the use of integrative models like HRDetect, which incorporate multiple genomic features from WGS data to more accurately identify HRD tumors.67 Indeed, HRDetect has shown promise in identifying HRD tumors and predicting sensitivity to platinum-based therapy or PARP inhibitor.67,68

Collectively, our findings reinforce the role of the adjuvant mFFX regimen as the standard of care in resected PDAC patients with a good performance status, particularly in those harboring KRAS mutations or classical subtype. The use of genomic features, such as HR gene alteration, seems suboptimal in the adjuvant setting, while transcriptomic signatures developed by Fraunhoffer et al9 can accurately predict sensitivity to mFFX in this population. In addition, transcriptomic classifiers such as PurIST offer a clinically feasible approach to define molecular subtype with prognostic value, particularly among patients treated with mFFX. Nonetheless, molecular testing remains a critical component of patient management, especially given the predictive value of HRD status for response to platinum and PARP inhibitors in advanced PDAC.11,12,14 Current guidelines from the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) endorse the implementation of such molecular testing in clinical practice.5,69 In this context, integrating genomic and transcriptomic information may provide a more comprehensive biological framework for patient stratification and treatment response prediction.

Key limitations of our study were identified, particularly due to its retrospective design. Because the study population was defined by sample availability, the balance achieved by random assignment may have been partially compromised, with residual imbalances between GEM and mFFX groups, particularly for sex and tumor location. These imbalances may introduce residual confounding and affect the predictive analyses, although results remained consistent after adjustment for clinically relevant covariates. CSS was analyzed with Cox models for consistency with prior reports; although this may overestimate the probability of death, competing-risks analyses (Fine and Gray) yielded consistent results. Moreover, small subgroups sizes limited statistical power, underscoring the need for larger prospective cohorts. Finally, comprehensive molecular characterization using WGS or WES was not performed, which may have limited the scope of molecular characterization.

APPENDIX

Supplementary Methods

Study Design and Population

Eligible patients were those included in the previously published PRODIGE-24/CCTG PA6 protocol15; briefly, patients were age between 18 and 79 years, had histologically confirmed pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), underwent complete macroscopic resection (R0 or R1), and provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included non-PDAC pancreatic tumors, metastatic disease, serum carbohydrate antigen (CA) 19-9 levels >180 U/mL within 21 days before random assignment, and any previous anticancer treatment (chemotherapy or radiotherapy).

Variants Interpretation and Visualization

Variants present at variant allele frequency between 35% and 65% were considered as germline.70 Variants were interpreted and classified using the ClinVar,71 Varsome,72 and OncoKB databases,73 according to recommendations of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology.74,75 Mutation Annotation Format files were generated using vcf2maf.76 Oncoplots were generated using the Maftools R package77 and the driver genes were selected as previously described by Dietlein et al.78

Identification of Tumors HR Status

Homologous recombination (HR) status was assessed based on the mutational status of 24 genes involved in the HRR pathway: ARID1A, ATM, ATR, ATRX, BAP1, BARD1, BLM, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CHEK1, CHEK2, FANCA, FANCC, FANCD2, FANCG, MRE11, NBN, PALB2, POLE, RAD50, RAD51B, RAD51C, and RAD51D. Tumors were classified as HRm or HRwt based on the presence or absence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations in any of these genes, according to the criteria defined by Koeppel et al.75 For further analysis, HRR-associated genes were subdivided into two categories: core HR genes (cHR), comprising BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, and RAD51C/D, and noncore HR genes (ncHR), including the remaining genes. Among the ncHR group, only ATM and CHEK2 were analyzed individually with respect to survival outcomes, due to the low frequency of alterations in other genes. Alterations in these genes are reported as variants in HRR-associated genes and do not imply the presence of HR deficiency.

Identification of Single-Base Substitution Signatures

Mutational signatures were obtained using SigProfiler79 and defined as described in the COSMIC database61 for each sample. The identification of the single-base substitution (SBS) signature (relative contribution >0) led to the confirmation of the presence of the specific mutational signature. The SBS signatures were considered as positive (SBS+) or negative (SBS–) when their relative contribution exceeded or was less than 0.2 in the sample, respectively. Impact of the SBS1, SBS2-13, SBS3, SBS6-15-20-21-26, SBS10, and SBS30 signatures on survival was analyzed due to their association with aging, activity of APOBEC family of cytidine deaminases, homologous repair deficiency, defective DNA mismatch repair, POLE somatic mutations, and defective base excision repair, respectively.61,80

Statistical Analyses

Qualitative variables were compared using Fisher's exact test or Pearson's chi square test. Absolute standardized mean differences were also calculated to assess the magnitude of imbalance between treatment groups, with values <0.10 considered indicative of negligible differences.81 Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from random assignment to the first cancer-related event, diagnosis of second cancer, or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Cancer-specific survival (CSS) was defined as the time from random assignment to death due to the index cancer or treatment-related complications. Patients without events at the time of analysis were censored at the date of last informative follow-up. Survival analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards model, stratified by resection margin, CA 19-9 level, and lymph node status (N0 <12 v N0 ≥12 v N1), as per in the original PRODIGE-24/CCTG PA6 study15 and its ancillary analyses,8,9 to ensure methodological consistency across studies conducted in this patient cohort. Results were reported as stratified hazard ratio with 95% CI. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. The predictive value of mutational status was assessed using a Cox proportional hazards model (stratified by resection margin, lymph node status, and CA 19-9 level), including treatment group, mutational status, and their interaction term as covariates. P values for interaction (Pint.) were derived from the interaction term in the model. As a sensitivity analysis, to account for potential confounding related to the selection of patients with available genomic data, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models and without stratification were fitted and adjusted for a predefined set of clinically relevant baseline covariates, including age, sex, ECOG performance status, tumor location, primary tumor status, lymph node status (N0 <12 v N0 ≥12 v N1), resection margin status, and CA19-9 level. In addition, for CSS, a competing-risks analysis was performed as a sensitivity analysis using the Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard model, considering deaths from causes other than cancer as competing events. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < .05. For associations between the 11 SBS signatures and survival outcomes stratified by treatment group, the significance was set at P value < .0045 after Bonferroni correction. All statistical analyses were performed using R software v. 4.3.3 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Supplementary Results

Characteristics of Patients

Clinicopathologic characteristics and survival outcomes of the analyzed patients (n = 317) did not differ significantly from those of the 176 excluded patients, except for tumor location (P value = .025; Appendix Table A1 and Fig A1). No significant interaction was observed between tumor location and inclusion status (P value for interaction [Pint.] = .906) and between treatment group and inclusion status for DFS (Pint. = .963), although a higher proportion of patients were treated with mFFX among included patients.

Among the 317 analyzed patients, the median age at diagnosis was 63 years (IQR, 56-67 years) and 175 (55.2%) were male. A total of 168 patients (53%) received adjuvant mFFX, and 149 (47%) received GEM. The mDFS was 15.6 months (95% CI, 13.8 to 20.2) and was significantly longer in the mFFX group compared with the GEM group (20.5 v 12.1 months, sHR, 0.66 [95% CI, 0.51 to 0.86]; P = .002, respectively; Appendix Fig A2). The mCSS was 45.7 months (95% CI, 35.3 to 54.2), with longer survival in the mFFX group than in the GEM group (54.4 v 35.5 months, sHR, 0.71 [95% CI, 0.53 to 0.96]; P = .024, respectively; Appendix Fig A2). The two treatment groups were well balanced for all covariates on statistical testing (P > .05; Appendix Table A2). However, absolute standardized mean differences (ASMD) >0.1 were observed for sex (ASMD = 0.11) and tumor status (ASMD = 0.10; Appendix Table A2).

Sensitivity Analyses

In sensitivity analyses using multivariable Cox models without stratification and adjusted for clinically relevant baseline covariates, results were consistent with the primary analyses for DFS, with no meaningful changes in hazard ratios or interaction estimates (Appendix Table A13). Similarly, adjusted analyses for CSS using multivariable Cox models and competing-risks analyses based on the Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard model yielded consistent results, with no significant differences in effect estimates (Appendix Table A14). In competing risks analyses, a total of 15 non–cancer-related deaths (12 in the mFFX group and 3 in the GEM group) were accounted for.
