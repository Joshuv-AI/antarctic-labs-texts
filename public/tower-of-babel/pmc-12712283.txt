Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles for targeted therapy
Authors: Junjie Ma, Haixin Pei, Fuhang Wang, Deliang Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Traditional drug delivery systems often face problems such as low drug accumulation at the target site, rapid clearance in the body, and potential damage to healthy tissues. Under such circumstances, nanoparticle drug delivery systems have been developed. Although they have certain advantages, they still struggle to achieve efficient and specific targeting and may exhibit cytotoxicity. In contrast, neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles (NM-NPs) inherit the natural cell surface proteins and ligands of neutrophils, possessing numerous advantages such as low immunogenicity, immune evasion, and the ability to achieve precise targeting, thus showing great potential. This review is the first article to comprehensively summarize the application of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles in targeted therapy. It mainly focuses on the pathophysiological role of neutrophils, the advantages of neutrophil membrane coating, the selection and preparation of nanoparticle cores, the targeting mechanism of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles, the types of nano-drugs for targeted delivery, their application in disease treatment, and discussions on related safety and toxicology.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Paul Ehrlich first put forward the concept of targeted therapy in the 1890s. It specifically targets the target and has no additional toxicity [1]. After a long period of development, in the field of contemporary medicine, targeted therapy has become a key approach. Its purpose is to precisely deliver therapeutic drugs to specific lesion sites while minimizing off-target effects [2]. However, traditional drug delivery systems often face issues such as low drug accumulation at the target site, rapid clearance in the body, and potential damage to healthy tissues [3]. These limitations have driven the exploration of advanced drug delivery strategies, among which nanoparticle-based delivery systems have attracted significant attention [4].

Nanoparticles possess numerous advantages, including small size, large specific surface area, and the ability to encapsulate a variety of therapeutic drugs. Despite these merits, unmodified nanoparticles still struggle to achieve efficient and specific targeting in complex biological environments [4, 5], and their cytotoxicity, due to their non-biological origin, cannot be ignored [6]. To address this issue, studies on bionic strategies have been conducted, and the application of membrane-coated nanoparticles stands out as a highly promising solution [7].

Cell membranes can be derived from different types of natural cells, such as red blood cells, platelets, and macrophages, and are used as coatings to cover the surface of nanoparticles [7]. Among them, red blood cell membrane-coated nanoparticles are regarded as "long-acting circulation platforms" and are applied in anti-cancer therapy, phototherapy, and targeted delivery. They aim to extend the circulation time and enhance immune evasion by leveraging the long lifespan of red blood cells [8]. Platelet membrane-coated nanoparticles are considered "damage-targeting platforms" that can target damaged sites. Relying on the natural tendency of platelets to move toward vascular damage sites and inflammatory environments, they can achieve targeting of the injured vascular system and tumors [9]. Macrophage membranes are known as "inflammatory phagocytic platforms" with excellent inflammatory tropism and pathogen-targeting capabilities. They recognize pathogens through Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMPs) and are used as carriers for precise drug delivery [10]. However, the important role of other cells should not be ignored, such as neutrophils. Neutrophils are the most abundant white blood cells in the blood and play a crucial role in immune and inflammatory responses. Their excellent chemotactic ability enables them to be rapidly recruited to sites of inflammation, infection, and injury. This unique characteristic has inspired researchers to use a novel type of cell membrane, neutrophil membranes, to coat nanoparticles, endowing the nanoparticles with the ability to actively target pathological tissues with an inflammatory microenvironment [11, 12].

Studies have found that neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles inherit the natural cell surface proteins and ligands of neutrophils, and these components can mediate specific interactions with target cells or target tissues [13]. For example, adhesion molecules such as CD11b/CD18 on the neutrophil membrane can bind to intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) expressed on activated endothelial cells at inflammatory sites, thereby promoting the targeted delivery of nanoparticles) [14].

Therefore, we believe that neutrophil cell membrane-coated nanoparticles (NM-NPs) have their own unique advantages. Different from other membrane-coated nanoparticles, they are regarded as the "inflammatory targeting and barrier penetration platforms". Based on the expression of specific proteins and receptors on the neutrophil cell membrane, they possess excellent immune evasion ability and biocompatibility, can be rapidly and specifically recruited to inflammatory sites, and have the function of enhancing nanoparticles' penetration of the blood–brain barrier [11, 13, 14].

In recent years, neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles have shown great potential in various therapeutic applications. In cancer treatment, they can be designed to specifically target tumor-related inflammation, improve the delivery efficiency of chemotherapeutic drugs to tumor sites, enhance therapeutic effects, and reduce systemic toxicity [15]. In the treatment of diseases with a local inflammatory microenvironment such as atherosclerosis and arthritis, these nanoparticles can precisely target inflamed tissues and deliver anti-inflammatory drugs [12, 16].

This review comprehensively summarizes the latest research progress in the application of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles in targeted therapy. We will discuss their physiological and pathological basis, targeting mechanisms, applications in different diseases, biocompatibility, as well as the challenges and prospects they face in the biomedical field.

Physiological and pathophysiological basis of neutrophils

Physiological characteristics

Neutrophils originate from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow. After undergoing differentiation, development, and maturation in the bone marrow, they are released into the blood and circulate throughout the body [17]. Mature neutrophils are spherical, with a diameter of approximately 10–12 µm. Their cell nuclei are lobulated (usually 2–5 lobes, connected by thin filaments), so they are also called "polymorphonuclear leukocytes". They are the most abundant type of white blood cells in human blood, accounting for about 50%-70% of the total number of peripheral blood white blood cells [18]. The neutrophil membrane, which surrounds the cytoplasm of neutrophils, is also known as the plasma membrane. It is mainly composed of lipids and proteins, and many important membrane proteins and receptors are highly expressed on it, undertaking complex functions [19–21]. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) can activate downstream signaling pathways (such as NF-κB), promote the release of inflammatory factors (such as TNF-α, IL-6), and enhance the inflammatory response [19]. Chemokine receptors CXCR1 and CXCR2 can bind to the chemokine IL-8 (CXCL8), activate the PI3K/Akt pathway, and guide neutrophils to pass through vascular endothelial cells and migrate directionally to the site of infection [20]. Integrins such as LFA-1 and Mac-1 in the integrin family can bind to ICAM-1 on the surface of vascular endothelial cells, enabling neutrophils to adhere and then pass through the vascular endothelium [21]. These collectively provide a favorable biological basis for neutrophils to sense external signals and participate in immune responses, and neutrophils play a role through mechanisms such as immune recognition, chemotaxis, and adhesion.

Pathophysiological role

The most important role of neutrophils is to defend against pathogens, serving as the first line of defense. They possess significant chemotactic ability and can respond to chemokines such as bacterial peptides, complement fragments (e.g., C5a), and cytokines (e.g., interleukin-8, IL-8), migrating rapidly to the site of infection [22]. After reaching the infection site, neutrophils can recognize and phagocytose pathogens through phagocytosis [23]. Their cytoplasm contains various granules, including azurophilic granules and specific granules, which are released during the phagocytic process and help kill and digest the phagocytosed pathogens [24]. In the process of inflammation resolution, neutrophils are a double-edged sword.On one hand, they are recruited to the inflammatory site, where they release pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor-α, TNF-α; interleukin-1β, IL-1β) and reactive ROS, which can cause tissue damage and pain [25, 26]. On the other hand, anti-inflammatory neutrophils or neutrophil derivatives can regulate the immune microenvironment by releasing substances such as reactive oxygen species and cytokines, control complement activation, and promote inflammation resolution [27, 28]. These pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory neutrophils may regulate the direction of the immune response to infection and inflammation by polarizing M1 or M2 macrophages, respectively [29]. In addition, neutrophils make an indelible contribution to maintaining tissue homeostasis. They can phagocytose and clear apoptotic cells to prevent the release of potentially harmful intracellular substances [30]; they secrete growth factors and cytokines to regulate the behavior of surrounding cells (e.g., fibroblasts and endothelial cells), such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which promotes vascular regeneration and thereby facilitates wound healing and tissue repair [31].

Concept and advantages of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles

Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles are a new type of nano-delivery system constructed based on the principle of bionics [32, 33]. The preparation process usually involves first extracting neutrophil membranes, then fusing them with pre-prepared nanoparticle cores through specific technical means, so that the surface of the nanoparticles is completely wrapped by the neutrophil membranes, forming bionic nanoparticles with a core–shell structure. Among them, the most widely accepted methods for neutrophil isolation include the gelatin method, Percoll gradient method, Histopaque 1.077 gradient method, and Histopaque 1.119/1.077 gradient method [34–37], etc. Subsequently, neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles can be prepared by fusing neutrophil membranes with nanoparticle cores through methods such as ultrasonic treatment and co-extrusion [38, 39]. In addition, methods like nitrogen cavitation [40] can also efficiently generate neutrophil membrane-derived nanovesicles, which provides more options for neutrophil-based drug delivery systems. Since specific methods have been reviewed [41], they will not be elaborated on in this paper.

This unique structure endows the nanoparticles with many advantages. From the perspective of biocompatibility, neutrophil membranes are derived from the organism itself and are highly compatible with the physiological environment of the body, which greatly reduces the risk of immune rejection caused by nanoparticles as foreign substances [42]. In terms of targeting, the biologically active substances such as chemokine receptors and adhesion molecules retained on the membrane surface enable the nanoparticles to respond to chemotactic signals at inflammatory sites like natural neutrophils, achieving active targeting of inflammatory lesions and significantly improving the enrichment of drugs at the lesion sites [43]. At the same time, the presence of neutrophil membranes can also help nanoparticles escape phagocytosis and clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), prolong their half-life in the blood circulation, and increase the chance of drug delivery to the target site, laying a solid foundation for achieving efficient and precise treatment [44].

Selection and preparation of nanoparticle cores

The core of nanoparticles serves as a carrier for therapeutic substances such as drugs and genes. The selection of materials for the core is of crucial importance, and multiple factors including biocompatibility, stability, drug-loading capacity, and degradation characteristics need to be comprehensively considered. Common core materials of nanoparticles include two major categories: inorganic materials and organic materials.

Among inorganic materials, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have become one of the popular choices. Due to their highly ordered porous structure, large specific surface area, and adjustable pore size, they can efficiently load a variety of therapeutic substances and exhibit excellent adsorption performance. Moreover, they have good stability in the body and can stably load small-molecule drugs through coordination [45]. Depending on different components, they can be formed by methods such as the coordination modulation method [46] or reticular synthesis [47]. Magnetite (Fe₃O₄) nanoparticles can not only act as drug carriers but also achieve targeted guidance under the action of an external magnetic field, providing a new approach for precise therapy [48]. The chemical precipitation method is a commonly used preparation technique. During preparation, iron ion solutions can undergo a precipitation reaction under alkaline conditions; by controlling reaction conditions such as temperature, pH value, and reactant concentration, the particle size and crystal form of the nanoparticles can be precisely regulated [49]. It has also been reported that they can be synthesized through a room-temperature reaction between 2-methylimidazolate and zinc nitrate in the presence of Fe [50]. Silica nanoparticles possess characteristics of good biocompatibility and ease of surface modification. They can have their particle size and morphology precisely controlled via the sol–gel method, and are thus widely applied in the field of drug delivery [51]. Such nanoparticles can be synthesized from two common organic sources, for instance, tetraalkoxysilanes (Si(OR)₄) [52] and sodium silicate [53]. By adjusting reaction parameters, nanoparticles with a three-dimensional network structure can be formed, enabling precise control over particle size and morphology.

In terms of organic materials, poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) is a degradable polymeric material approved for clinical use by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Its degradation rate can be precisely regulated by adjusting the ratio of lactic acid to glycolic acid. With good biocompatibility and drug-loading capacity, it is often used to prepare drug carriers such as nanoparticles and microspheres [54]. PLGA nanoparticles are produced by the catalytic ring-opening copolymerization of LA and GA units [55]. During the polymerization process, monomer units are continuously linked together through ester bonds to form the PLGA copolymer [56]. By optimizing factors such as the type and concentration of solvent, and the type and dosage of surfactant, PLGA nanoparticles with uniform particle size and good dispersibility can be prepared [57]. Chitosan, as a natural polysaccharide material, has biodegradability, biocompatibility, and cationic properties. It can combine with negatively charged drugs or genes through electrostatic interaction to achieve efficient loading and delivery [58]. The methods that are mainly used to produce Chitosan nanoparticles are ion gelation, self-assembly, emulsification and cross-linking, complexation with polyelectrolytes, and drying processes [59–62]. In addition, liposomes are also commonly used nanocarriers. They are closed vesicular structures composed of phospholipid bilayers, which can encapsulate both water-soluble and fat-soluble drugs, and have good cell affinity and in vivo distribution characteristics [63]. The self-assembly liposome system [64] is a classic method for preparing liposomes. Targeting mechanism of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles

Targeting mechanism based on chemokine receptors

A variety of chemokine receptors are abundantly distributed on the surface of neutrophil membranes, and these receptors play a central role in the targeting process of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles [67]. When an inflammatory response or lesion occurs in the body, cells at the inflammatory site, such as macrophages and endothelial cells, will release a large amount of chemokines, such as IL-8, forming a specific chemokine concentration gradient [68]. The chemokine receptors on the surface of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles can specifically recognize and bind to these chemokines [69]. For example, the CXCR2 receptor on the neutrophil membrane can tightly bind to the chemokine IL-8; during the binding process, the conformation of the receptor changes, which in turn activates a series of intracellular signal transduction pathways [70]. Specifically, G proteins exist as heterotrimers (α, β, and γ subunits) and bind to the intracellular segments of CXCR1/2 respectively.Once IL-8 binds to the CXCR2 receptor, the β and γ subunits of the G protein are released from Gα (Gαi2 in neutrophils) and activate PI3Kγ, PLCβ2/β3, PLA2, and PLD [71]. Gαi2 inhibits adenylate cyclase to reduce cAMP levels. The small GTPases Ras/Rac/Rho/cdc42/Rap1, PKC, and AKT (PKB) are present downstream of the β and γ subunits, regulating cell adhesion, actin polymerization, membrane protrusion, and ultimately cell migration [72]. In addition, the carboxyl-terminal region of the CXCR2 receptor contains a PDZ ligand-binding site and an LLKIL motif. On the one hand, it can bind to NHERF1, a protein containing a PDZ domain (PSD-95/Dlg/ZO-1 domain), thereby completing coupling with PLC-β2; on the other hand, the relevant binding motif of LLKIL is crucial for recruiting the chemotactic responses triggered by Akt, Rac1, and cdc42 [73]. The activation of PLA2 ultimately leads to an increase in products such as prostaglandins (PG) and leukotrienes (LT), which further act on macrophages and neutrophils to promote the release of inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) [74]. Inflammatory factors can stimulate endothelial cells and other inflammatory cells to release chemokines (e.g., IL-8), which guide neutrophils to migrate from the blood vessels to the inflammatory site through a concentration gradient, forming a positive loop [75]. Interestingly, PLD can also directly activate Akt through membrane recruitment [76], a mechanism that further elucidates the sophisticated targeting process. In addition, the PI3K/AKT pathway positively regulates the NF-κB pathway and promotes inflammation [77], thereby enhancing the chemotaxis and migration of neutrophils and consolidating the foundation for the successful targeting of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles.

In summary, these signaling pathways guide nanoparticles to migrate directionally toward inflammatory sites along the chemokine concentration gradient by regulating the expression of adhesion molecules on the nanoparticle surface and the rearrangement of the cytoskeleton, thereby achieving precise targeting. This targeting mechanism based on chemokine receptors exhibits high specificity and sensitivity, enabling nanoparticles to accurately respond to inflammatory signals [43] and effectively overcoming the problem of insufficient targeting of traditional nanomedicines. It is worth noting that studies have shown that during the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, the expression of TLR4 can be inhibited, and the release of inflammatory factors by lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated microglia can be reduced [78]; moreover, it participates in the polarization process of macrophages and directly exerts anti-inflammatory effects [79]. Targeting and retention mechanism mediated by adhesion molecule

In addition to chemokine receptors, a variety of adhesion molecules, such as those belonging to the integrin family and selectin family, are also present on the surface of neutrophil membranes. These molecules play a crucial role in the adhesion and retention of nanoparticles to vascular endothelial cells at inflammatory sites [80]. During blood circulation, when nanoparticles approach the inflammatory site with the blood flow, they first undergo transient "rolling" contact with the corresponding ligands on the surface of vascular endothelial cells through selectin-mediated weak interactions. This low-affinity interaction allows nanoparticles to move slowly along the vascular wall, increasing the chance of further binding to endothelial cells [81]. Subsequently, under the action of chemokines or other stimulating signals, the integrin molecules on the surface of nanoparticles undergo a conformational change, switching from a low-affinity state to a high-affinity state, and then tightly bind to members of the immunoglobulin superfamily on the surface of endothelial cells (such as intercellular adhesion molecule-1, ICAM-1; vascular cell adhesion molecule-1, VCAM-1), achieving firm adhesion [82]. Specifically, the αLβ2 integrin (LFA-1) can specifically bind to ICAM-1 to form stable adhesive junctions. Meanwhile, the intracellular domain of the β2 subunit of LFA-1 binds to cytoskeletal proteins, activating intracellular signaling molecules such as Src family kinases, and initiating downstream signal transduction including the PI3K-Akt pathway, MAPK pathway, and Rho family GTPases [83–85]. As reported [86], Rho GTPases regulate transendothelial migration, and together with the further recruitment of neutrophils by inflammatory factors, the neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles ultimately reach the target site smoothly.

In summary, once adhesion is successfully achieved, nanoparticles can be retained on the surface of endothelial cells and, through processes such as transendothelial migration, cross the vascular wall to enter the inflamed tissue, enabling precise drug delivery. Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated targeting mechanism

Members of the pattern recognition receptor family, such as TLR4, guide NM-NPs to accumulate at inflammatory or tumor sites by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as LPS [87]. TLR4, which may be present on the neutrophil membrane inherited by NM-NPs, can specifically bind to ligands highly expressed in target tissues (e.g., lipopolysaccharide, heat shock protein). This interaction triggers intracellular signaling pathways, promoting the adhesion of NM-NPs to vascular endothelial cells and their arrival at the lesion through transendothelial migration [88]. Specifically, TLR4 is activated to recognize LPS under the action of CD14 [89], and then recruits TRAF6, triggering a signaling cascade involving TAK1 and TBK1 kinases, activating the NF-κB, MAPK signaling pathways, and Akt kinase, and ultimately participating in the production of inflammatory factors [90]. Since substances such as lipopolysaccharide are generally highly expressed in various pathological microenvironments [91–93], this "natural enrichment of pathological signals" characteristic enables TLR4-mediated targeting to be independent of the specific antigen of a single disease, but directly responds to the common pathological features of the lesion (e.g., tissue damage, immune activation), thus playing a key role in the targeting process of NM-NPs. Active modification for targeting strategy

To make neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles more accurate and specific in targeting, becoming a truly "precision-guided missile," many studies have conducted active modifications on them to enhance their targeting ability. Wei Li and colleagues modified neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles with phosphatidylserine (PtdSer), which can flip outward to the exterior of the cell membrane to release a key "eat me" signal, precisely delivering ferroptosis-specific inhibitors to arterial plaque sites and inflammatory microenvironments, thereby creating a more advanced targeted delivery system [11]. Dehua Lu and others modified neutrophil membranes with cRGD peptides for tumor integrin recognition, further improving tumor targeting efficiency [95]. Ni Yang and team developed a biomimetic ApoA-I mimetic peptide-modified neutrophil membrane-wrapped F127 polymer (R4F-NM@F127), where the R4F peptide modification further endows the nanocarrier with the ability to target the macrophage SR-B1 receptor [16]. Yaohui Jiang and colleagues used neutrophil membranes camouflaged with hemagglutinin (HA) and integrin modifications, providing an effective and feasible targeting strategy for the treatment of myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury (MIRI) [96]. Through appropriate active modifications, neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles can specifically respond to signals from these target sites, further enhancing their targeting ability and therapeutic effects. It can be seen that active modifications serving targeting strategies can achieve satisfactory results.

Response mechanism based on inflammatory microenvironment

It is worth mentioning that there exists a special microenvironment, namely the inflammatory microenvironment, which has unique physicochemical properties, such as a reduced local pH value, elevated levels of ROS, and altered enzyme activity [97]. At inflammatory sites, due to abnormal cellular metabolism (e.g., lactic acid accumulation) and the aggregation of inflammatory cells, the local pH value usually drops to approximately 6.5–7.0, presenting a weakly acidic state compared to the normal physiological environment (pH 7.4) [98]. Some core materials of nanoparticles, such as pH-sensitive polymers, can undergo structural changes in this acidic environment, leading to alterations in the surface properties of the nanoparticles and promoting drug release [99]. For example, certain nanoparticles based on poly(β-amino ester) undergo hydrolysis of their polymer backbones under acidic conditions, which enhances the hydrophilicity of the nanoparticles and allows the drug to be rapidly released from the interior of the nanoparticles, thereby achieving efficient accumulation and release of the drug at the inflammatory site [100]. Meanwhile, the level of ROS in the inflammatory microenvironment is significantly increased. Some nanoparticles containing chemical bonds or groups that can be oxidized by ROS can undergo redox reactions under the action of ROS, triggering drug release or changing the physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles to enhance their interaction with inflammatory cells [101]. In addition, there are some enzymes with high specific expression at inflammatory sites, such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Nanoparticles that can be specifically cleaved by these enzymes can be designed to undergo structural cleavage at the inflammatory site, enabling precise drug release and targeted therapy [102]. This multiple response mechanism to the inflammatory microenvironment allows the designed neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles to flexibly regulate drug release and their own behaviors according to the unique environmental characteristics of the lesion site, significantly improving the precision and effectiveness of treatment. As a special type "among the active modifications serving targeting strategies", we specifically name it the "Response mechanism based on inflammatory microenvironment".

In fact, there are connections between different targeting mechanisms, and they work together in the application of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles. For example, the expression of ICAM-1 can induce the release of chemokines, which links the adhesion molecule-based targeting mechanism with the chemokine receptor-based targeting mechanism [103]. The expression of ICAM-1 can also be induced by NF-κB in response to stimulation by TNF-α or IL-1β, which connects the adhesion molecule-based targeting mechanism with the TLR4-mediated targeting mechanism [104]. ROS can upregulate the expression of adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1 and inflammatory cytokines, thereby linking the adhesion molecule-based targeting mechanism with the response mechanism based on the inflammatory microenvironment [105]. Although active modifications for targeting strategies have been made, such as the use of tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), the upregulation of relevant adhesion molecules like ICAM-1 enhances intercellular interactions [106]. Therefore, although one mechanism may play a dominant role in some cases, in most cases, the various targeting mechanisms do not act in isolation or independently; instead, they work together to achieve the same goal.

Types and attempts of targeted delivery nanomedicines

Non-drug-loaded

In the research on using neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles for targeted delivery of nano-drugs, some studies are quite interesting. They do not coat specific nano-drugs but observe the therapeutic effects in a drug-free form. As far as I know, there are mainly three forms. One is that neutrophil membrane-mimicking nanocompounds are directly used as biomimetic nano-drugs [107]. Other control groups, such as red cell membrane-mimicking nanovesicles (RM), are set up to exclude the potential effect of non-specific binding of inflammatory factors and chemokines on the results, which further demonstrated their therapeutic effect for sepsis-induced liver injury. Similarly, the study by Qing Zhang et al. was also conducted in this way [13]. The results showed that these effects of NM-NPs can collectively reduce inflammatory responses and liver injury in a mouse model of hepatitis by inhibiting neutrophil recruitment and infiltration, reducing systemic and local inflammatory cytokines, and polarizing macrophages to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype.Another approach involves combining neutrophil membrane fragments with phospholipid liposomes to prepare biomimetic neutrophil membrane-derived MBs (MBneu) [108]. Although they are drug-free, they simultaneously possess the typical physicochemical properties of liposome-based MBs. Junhao Pan et al. adopted a similar strategy, employing specific processing steps such as mechanical cutting and ultrasonic treatment to facilitate the combination of cell membranes with nanoscale liposome vesicles, thereby forming neutrophil membrane nanovesicles (NMNV) [109]. Subsequently, they also achieved ideal results through experiments, confirming that NMNV treatment has a positive impact on improving the clinical efficacy in treating sepsis-induced acute kidney injury. The third form is that Peipei Wu et al. constructed engineered hybrid vesicles fused with nanovesicles derived from human neutrophil membranes and hucMSC-sEVs, which were named neutrophil membrane-engineered hucMSC-sEVs (NEX) [110]. Shortly afterwards, Yuting Tang et al. drew on this method and applied it in cancer treatment [15]. Vesicle modification shows great promise in improving targeting precision and therapeutic outcomes. These studies indicate that even without carrying drugs, the characteristics of neutrophil membranes or fused membranes themselves are sufficient to achieve satisfactory clinical effects in the process of targeted therapy.

Chemical drugs

Chemical drugs, especially polymeric nanoparticles, often face numerous obstacles in clinical applications, such as clearance in the organism [111], poor targeting efficacy [112], and harsh modification requirements with high costs [113]. Studies have shown that neutrophil membrane coating can address this challenge. In a study by Hongyi Zhu et al., resveratrol-loaded nanoparticles coated with neutrophil membranes (NMR) were prepared using the emulsion-solvent evaporation method and membrane encapsulation technology, which helps regulate macrophage polarization to alleviate inflammatory responses [114]. Some researchers have fabricated a simvastatin (ST)-loaded and neutrophil membrane-covered nanoplatform (NNPST) to enhance local payload delivery and atherosclerosis management, providing new opportunities for the on-demand delivery of various therapeutic agents in inflammatory diseases involving neutrophils [43]. Some studies suggest that more than one drug can be delivered; after the co-delivery of resolvin D1 (RvD1) and ceftazidime (CEF), vesicles formed from human neutrophil membranes (NMV) enhance inflammation resolution and bacterial killing [115]. Therefore, the delivery of chemical drugs via neutrophil membrane coating has become a new method applicable to clinical targeted therapy. The wide range of drug types, such as anti-inflammatory drugs, anti-tumor drugs, and antibacterial drugs, clearly indicates a broad therapeutic field and application space.

Metal nanoparticles

Metal nanomedicines have attracted much attention recently. With good biocompatibility and excellent protective effects, they have been widely used in various clinical fields [116, 117]. However, the lack of good targeting ability sometimes limits their further transformation and promotion [118]. Herein, wrapping a variety of metal particles in neutrophil membranes to construct neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles is a good solution. As far as I know, there are Mn₃O₄, gold nanoparticles covalent to MSA-2 (MSA-Au), copper-based nanoparticles, immunomagnetic nanoparticles (IMN), etc. Mn₃O₄ NPs can scavenge ROS to alleviate trauma. As a new approach, they have proven their therapeutic potential for ROS-related diseases [39]. MSA-Au are considered ideal X-ray-activated nanoparticles, which can generate more ROS and have high drug-loading efficiency. After being coated with neutrophil membranes, they show significant accumulation in radiation oncology, providing reliable evidence for the clinical transformation of targeted killing of tumor cells in combination with radiotherapy in the future [95]. Neutrophil membrane-coated copper-based nanoparticles (N-Cu5.4O@DFO NPs) exhibit excellent antioxidant and iron ion scavenging abilities. They can target and accumulate in inflamed kidneys, reduce oxidative damage events and inflammatory responses, and ultimately achieve synergistic treatment of renal I/R injury [119]. When immunomagnetic nanoparticles (IMN) enter biological fluids, they will adsorb non-specific proteins, and the formed protein corona covers the surface of targeting ligands, which limits the detection efficiency of IMNs [120]. At the same time, the interaction between surface targeting ligands and white blood cells (WBC) significantly limits the purity of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) separated by IMNs [121]. However, all these problems are solved by neutrophil membrane-coated IMNs (Neu-IMNs), which paves the way for early non-invasive diagnosis of cancer [122]. In addition, in addition to single coating of metal nanoparticles, giving full play to the carrier role of neutrophil membranes and combining with targeted delivery of chemical drugs will achieve better effects. It is common in studies to encapsulate drugs in mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) and deliver doxorubicin or other anti-inflammatory drugs to designated sites, which can achieve a better tumor inhibition effect [42, 123].

Biogenic molecule and biological macromolecules

Molecules derived from the human body that participate in biochemical metabolism can sometimes serve as drugs coated by neutrophil membranes. Since they directly participate in cellular metabolic reactions, the targeted delivery of such molecules in combination with neutrophil membranes can achieve significant therapeutic effects. Octanoic acid (OA) is a lipid oil mainly metabolized in brain astrocytes, with potential functions of combating oxidative stress [124], apoptosis [125], and inflammation [126], as well as the ability to stabilize mitochondria [127]. However, the application of OA is strictly limited by its short half-life and low available concentration in target organs [128, 129]. Yang et al. innovatively developed a relay targeted delivery nanotherapy, which is expected to become a promising therapy for treating brain injury caused by CA/CPR [130]. Studies have shown that acidic fibroblast growth factor (aFGF) has broad application prospects in the treatment of inflammatory diseases [131, 132]. However, as a peptide, aFGF has low stability, a short biological half-life, and degrades rapidly in vivo, which limit its clinical application [133]. Huang et al. constructed a biomimetic nano-platform, namely neutrophil membrane-coated liposomes loaded with acidic fibroblast growth factor (aFGF@NMLs), which can selectively target inflamed lungs and effectively alleviate sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI) [134]. Interleukin-5 (IL-5) is considered a promising cardiac detoxifier during the inflammatory phase after myocardial infarction [135–137]. However, dose control in cytokine delivery and maintenance of biological activity are challenging [138, 139]. Han et al. successfully synthesized biomimetic IL-5 nanoparticles by camouflaging IL-5 nanoparticles in neutrophil membranes [140]. These nanoparticles successfully promoted the accumulation of EOS and angiogenesis in infarcted myocardium, thereby limiting adverse cardiac remodeling after AMI.

Interestingly, the neutrophil membrane coating method may also be applicable to antisense oligonucleotides (ASO) for gene silencing, small interfering RNA, and even engineered small molecules that encode proteins, which adds a new category to nano-drugs for targeted delivery. Metallothionein 1 (MT1) holds promise for application in the treatment of aortic aneurysm lesions. However, due to limitations such as poor in vivo stability, low targeting efficiency, and insufficient specificity, current NP modification strategies have not achieved satisfactory therapeutic results for AAA [141]. Zhang et al. synthesized PLGA-PEG nanoparticles loaded with siMT1 (GlycoRNA-NP-siMT1), which effectively delivered siMT1 to AAA lesions, thereby inhibiting abdominal aortic dilation [142]. Application of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles in disease treatment

Treatment of inflammatory diseases

Acute inflammatory diseases

In the treatment of acute inflammatory diseases such as acute lung injury (ALI) and acute pancreatitis, neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles have shown significant therapeutic potential. ALI is usually caused by various factors, including bacterial infection, endotoxemia, and trauma. Its pathological features include massive infiltration of inflammatory cells in the lungs, damage to alveolar epithelial and vascular endothelial cells, and the formation of pulmonary edema [144]. Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles loaded with anti-inflammatory drugs can utilize their natural inflammatory targeting ability to rapidly chemotax and accumulate at inflammatory sites. For example, in a mouse model of acute lung injury, researchers prepared neutrophil membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles loaded with the anti-inflammatory drug dexamethasone [145]. The experimental results showed that compared with plain PLGA nanoparticles, these biomimetic nanoparticles, by virtue of the chemokine receptors on the neutrophil membrane, could quickly respond to chemotactic signals such as CCL2 and CXCL8 released at the pulmonary inflammatory sites, and accumulate in large quantities in the damaged lung tissues within a short period of time, significantly increasing the drug concentration at the lesion sites. The fluorescence intensity of NM-NPs accumulated at the target site reaches 40 a.u., while that of the control group is less than 20 a.u. This two-fold difference enables NM-NPs to exhibit a significant targeting advantage. At the same time, the dexamethasone carried by the nanoparticles could effectively inhibit the activation of inflammatory cells and the release of inflammatory mediators, reduce pulmonary inflammatory responses and tissue damage, and significantly improve the respiratory function and survival status of the mice.In research on the treatment of acute pancreatitis, neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles were also used to load trypsin inhibitors [146]. During an acute pancreatitis attack, pancreatic tissue releases a variety of chemokines that attract neutrophils to gather. Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles can thus "follow the trail" and accurately reach the inflamed pancreatic site. Studies have shown that NNPs/DiD accumulates selectively in the inflamed pancreas, and its fluorescence intensity is nearly twice that of the control group at 3 h post-injection. Meanwhile, the levels of TNF-α and IL-6—key indicators for the progression of acute pancreatitis—are also effectively reduced, decreasing by more than 25% compared with the control group. Besides, such nanoparticles can significantly reduce the activity of trypsin in pancreatic tissue, decrease the production of inflammatory mediators, effectively alleviate the inflammatory edema and necrosis of the pancreas, and reduce the severity of acute pancreatitis and the incidence of complications.

Chronic inflammatory diseases

Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles also find applications in the treatment of chronic inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis and atherosclerosis. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disease characterized primarily by chronic inflammation of the joint synovium. Long-term inflammatory responses can lead to the destruction of articular cartilage and bone tissue, severely affecting patients' quality of life [147]. Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles exhibit unique advantages in RA treatment [16, 148]. Researchers have loaded anti-inflammatory drugs like salicylic acid into the core of nanoparticles, with the surface coated with neutrophil membranes. In RA animal models, due to the continuous release of various chemokines and cytokines (e.g., CXCL12, TNF-α) at inflamed joint sites, neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles can actively migrate to and adhere to the inflamed joint sites, relying on the chemokine receptors and adhesion molecules on the membrane surface. The fluorescence intensity of the N-PSAs group was approximately 1.3 ± 0.3 times higher than that of the PSAs group at 12 h, and about 2 ± 0.2 times higher at 36 h. After 36 h of injection, it was even around 5 times higher. After a period of treatment, the drugs carried by the nanoparticles can effectively inhibit the proliferation of synovial cells and the infiltration of inflammatory cells, reduce the secretion of inflammatory mediators, alleviate joint swelling, and improve joint function. Compared with traditional drug treatment methods, the systemic toxic and side effects are significantly reduced.Atherosclerosis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disease and a major cause of coronary heart disease, cerebral infarction, and peripheral vascular diseases [149]. In the treatment of atherosclerosis, constructing a nano-drug delivery system that can target inflammatory and plaque sites is crucial. Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles can achieve precise targeting by utilizing chemotactic signals in the plaque inflammatory microenvironment, such as IL-8 and ICAM-1. For example, a study prepared neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles loaded with Fer-1. These nanoparticles showed selective accumulation and a higher fluorescence signal at atherosclerotic plaques and released the drug [11]. The results of animal experiments showed that after intravenous injection, the nanoparticles improved their solubility and drug-loading capacity in vivo, prolonged the sustained release time, and reduced their non-specific distribution. Compared with the control group, the fluorescence intensity of NM-Lipo was 1.3 times that of PM-Lipo, and the fluorescence intensity of PtdSer@NM-Lipo was 1.6 times that of NM-Lipo. This indicates that NM-Lipo and PtdSer@NM-Lipo exhibit selective accumulation and higher fluorescence signals at atherosclerotic plaques, implying stronger targeting ability and enhanced adhesion to aortic plaques. NM-NPs provide a new efficient and low-toxicity strategy for the treatment of AS.

Treatment of tumor

In the field of tumor therapy, chemotherapy is one of the commonly used treatment methods. However, traditional chemotherapeutic drugs lack tumor targeting ability. While killing tumor cells, due to the inability to ensure accurate delivery, they also cause damage to normal tissues and cells, leading to a series of toxic and side effects [150]. Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles provide a new approach to solve this problem. Through the targeted delivery of chemotherapeutic drugs, the tumor immune microenvironment can be regulated, and the inhibition of tumor growth and metastasis can be achieved. For example, to address issues related to hucMSC-sEV such as insufficient circulation time, limited targeting efficiency, and poor therapeutic efficacy, Yuting Tang et al. designed membrane fusion between hucMSC-sEV and human neutrophil membranes to create Neu/MSC-sEV [15]. This modification enhances the targeting ability to tumor cells, reduces clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, prolongs circulation time, inhibits tumor growth, and improves therapeutic efficacy. As proteins associated with proliferation, migration, and apoptosis, the expression levels of E-cadherin, Vimentin, PCNA, Caspase 3, and Bcl-2 in HGC-27 cells changed by more than 20% compared with the control group, which reflects a favorable therapeutic effect. Jing Zhao et al. developed a novel neutrophil membrane-based nanoparticle delivery system that can be used in combination with nsPEF therapy for the treatment of pancreatic cancer [151]. The combined treatment strategy increases the affinity of active ingredients for tumor cells, results in significant accumulation in tumors, and significantly inhibits tumor growth and metastasis. It is worth noting that Xianjia Wu et al. proposed that the surface functionalization of IMNs with neutrophil membranes can simultaneously reduce non-specific protein adsorption, enhance interactions with CTCs, reduce interference from WBCs, and improve the viability of isolated CTCs [122]. In the detection of breast cancer blood samples, Neu-IMNs successfully isolated 19 circulating tumor cells (CTCs) out of a total of 20 samples. Compared with 12 positive reports in the control group, the isolation efficiency and purity of CTCs were significantly higher. Further research related to Neu-IMNs is believed to help pave the way for early non-invasive diagnosis of cancer. This suggests that the neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticle technology can not only be used for treating diseases but also for diagnosing them, which further enhances its value.

Treatment of infectious diseases

Globally, drug-resistant bacterial infections have become a major public health issue that poses a serious threat to human health. Traditional antibiotics often have poor efficacy against drug-resistant bacteria [152]. Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles provide an innovative approach to address the challenge of drug-resistant bacterial infections. By loading antibiotics or substances with antibacterial activity into the core of nanoparticles and coating the surface with neutrophil membranes, the constructed biomimetic nanoparticles can precisely target bacterial infection sites. For example, Jun Liu et al. studied the efficacy of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticle-mediated KLA peptide (KLAKLAKKLAKLAKLAK) and gentamicin in the targeted treatment of drug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumonia) lung infections [153]. The results showed that KLA-NNPs retained the membrane proteins on the neutrophil membrane that exert functional effects, could effectively release KLA, significantly reduce intracellular bacteria and caspase-1 activity, and inhibit the development of pneumonia induced by drug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae.Sepsis is prone to complications such as organ dysfunction, including acute liver injury and lung injury, which often occur in the early stage and are directly related to poor prognosis in clinical studies. However, there is currently no specific therapy for septic liver injury [154, 155]. Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles deserve to be included in the discussion. Yao Xiao et al. explored the therapeutic effect of neutrophil-camouflaged nanovesicles on sepsis, achieving the goals of regulating neutrophil infiltration and neutralizing inflammatory mediators [107]. A single dose of NM treatment significantly increased the 96-h survival rate of sepsis model mice from 16 to 44%, while RM did not significantly improve the 96-h survival rate of sepsis model mice. This reflects the unique advantages of NM. It attenuated LPS-induced ROS production, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) expression, nitric oxide (NO) secretion, and subsequent hepatocyte damage, showing promising therapeutic potential. Some scholars have also alleviated acute lung injury caused by sepsis through a similar method [156].

Treatment of central nervous system

Central nervous system (CNS) diseases have attracted increasing attention, such as cerebral ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury. However, many drugs cannot effectively penetrate the blood–brain barrier (BBB), which brings great troubles to clinical work [157]. Neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles have another important function: they inherit the BBB penetrating ability in the form of membrane camouflage, which makes it possible to cross the BBB for drug delivery to treat brain injury [158]. On one hand, neutrophil membrane-coated nano-drugs can penetrate the BBB and translocate to inflammatory lesions through the affinity between neutrophil surface antigens and upregulated adhesion markers (such as ICAM-1, VCAM-1) and chemokines (such as CXCL2, CXCL12) [159]. On the other hand, these neutrophil decoys will not release harmful factors to aggravate brain injury; instead, they can neutralize inflammation by adsorbing inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), inhibit neutrophil infiltration, and reshape the inflammatory microenvironment [160]. Tang et al. constructed a biomimetic nano-system (Leo@NM-Lipo) using neutrophil membranes, which can effectively penetrate and repair the damaged BBB, target ischemic lesions, and rescue the injured brain, bringing a new strategy for ischemic stroke therapy [161]. Studies have shown that the fluorescence intensity of NM-Lipo penetrating inflammatory BCECs is 2.28 times that of naked Lipo, and 1.37 times that of NM-Lipo passing through the unactivated BCEC layer. These data confirm that NM-Lipo can selectively target and penetrate the inflammatory blood–brain barrier (BBB) to enhance the therapeutic effect. Jingyuan Yang et al. adopted a similar method. Research on the safety and toxicology of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles

In vivo biodistribution and metabolism

Understanding the in vivo biodistribution and metabolic processes of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles is crucial for evaluating their safety and effectiveness. In animal experiments, techniques such as radioisotope labeling and fluorescent labeling are often used to track nanoparticles. Research results have shown that after intravenous injection, neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles are initially mainly distributed in the blood circulatory system, and then gradually accumulate in target sites such as inflammatory sites and tumor tissues under the guidance of signals like chemokines [13]. For example, in tumor model mice, significant fluorescent signals of fluorescently labeled neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles can be detected at the tumor site within a few hours after injection, and the fluorescence intensity at the inflammatory site gradually increases over time, indicating that the nanoparticles can continuously accumulate at the inflammatory site. In contrast, the distribution of nanoparticles in normal tissues is relatively sparse, which fully reflects their excellent targeting ability [95]. Regarding the metabolic pathway of nanoparticles, current studies suggest that they are mainly cleared through the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS). During the in vivo circulation of nanoparticles, some are recognized and phagocytosed by macrophages in organs such as the liver and spleen, and then degraded in organelles like lysosomes [162]. However, because the cell membrane components on the surface of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles can to a certain extent evade phagocytosis by the MPS, their half-life in the blood circulation is longer than that of ordinary nanoparticles, which provides more sufficient time for their targeted delivery [119]. At the same time, studies have also found that the metabolic rate and pathway of nanoparticles may be affected by various factors, such as the particle size of the nanoparticles, the nature of surface charge, membrane coating components, and the type of loaded drugs [163]. Nanoparticles with smaller particle sizes are often more easily taken up and metabolized by the MPS, while the metabolic process of nanoparticles with appropriate surface charges or modified with special groups may change [164]. In-depth research on these influencing factors is conducive to optimizing the design of nanoparticles and further improving their in vivo performance.

Biocompatibility, potential toxicological risks and mechanisms

As a type of biomimetic nanomaterial, although neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles have demonstrated numerous advantages in disease treatment, their biocompatibility and systemic toxicity cannot be ignored, and these are among the key indicators for safety evaluation. Theoretically, since their membrane coating is derived from the body's own neutrophils, they have high compatibility with the body's immune system. Compared with artificially synthesized nanomaterials, their immunogenicity should be lower [165]. Fortunately, current studies on animal models have not reported toxicity of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles to important systemic organs [15]; some studies even mention that they further reduce toxicity compared with nanoparticles without neutrophil membrane modification [166]. The selection and properties of the core material of nanoparticles are among the important factors affecting toxicological risks. For example, some inorganic nanoparticles, such as those containing Cu elements, interact with the plasma membrane, creating a large contact surface between metal ions and cells, thereby causing cellular damage and interfering with the normal physiological functions of cells [167]; they may also accumulate in tissues and organs and release heavy metal ions, which may interact with biological macromolecules and lead to the progression of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease [168]. In addition, surface charge is one of the important physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, and the surface properties of nanoparticles may also have an impact on their toxicological risks. Studies have shown that cationic nanoparticles disrupt plasma membrane integrity more significantly than anionic nanoparticles, leading to more severe mitochondrial and lysosomal damage, as well as the production of more autophagosomes. These reasons may result in higher cytotoxicity [169]. Other common influencing factors include the shape of nanoparticles, the modified coating on the surface of nanoparticles, or the loaded drugs, which may undergo chemical reactions in the body to produce toxic metabolites, all of which may pose potential hazards to the body [170, 171]. In-depth research on these potential toxicological risks and their mechanisms of action is of great guiding significance for comprehensively evaluating the safety of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles and formulating reasonable risk prevention and control measures.

Discussions

Mitochondria are crucial for survival and play a major role in energy metabolism, ion homeostasis, and cell death [172]. Mitochondrial dysfunction, such as mitochondrial fragmentation, abnormal energy metabolism, and ROS accumulation, occurs after IRI and plays a vital role in oxidative stress and apoptosis [173]. Interestingly, in attempts involving neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles, numerous studies have mentioned changes in mitochondrial function, morphology, and membrane potential [114, 130, 156]. Therefore, in addition to participating in inflammatory signals and related pathways, it is reasonable to suspect that the targeting mechanism of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles is also related to mitochondria and their internal biomolecules. For example, relevant studies have shown that under stress conditions such as inflammation and tumors, intracellular mitochondrial DNA can regulate the cGAS-STING axis, thereby activating the NF-κB signaling pathway and amplifying the release effect of inflammatory factors [174]. As discussed earlier, the release of these inflammatory factors is believed to contribute to the precise recruitment of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles. In addition, mitochondria and their related derivatives have also been found in the circulation of human and animal peripheral blood, and they play important physiological roles [175]. Mitochondrial extracellular vesicles containing mitochondrial proteins and RNA can even directly activate signaling molecules such as TLR4, and participate deeply in the signaling pathways mentioned in the targeting mechanism [176]. In-depth research on the mechanisms of neutrophil membrane proteins related to mitochondria may yield unexpected insights.

Circadian rhythms play an important role in organisms [177]. Furthermore, since neutrophils exhibit circadian rhythms, and this regularity is regulated by various cytokines including CXCR4, CXCR2, and TLR4 [178], it is reasonable to infer that neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles, which inherit neutrophil membrane proteins, may also be affected. However, there are currently no relevant research reports. More studies are needed to determine the extent of differences when nanoparticles are intravenously injected at different times, and even to utilize this circadian rhythm characteristic of neutrophils for targeted therapy.

Interestingly, some studies have adopted local injection of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles instead of intravenous injection, mainly focusing on the treatment of motor system disease models, such as spinal cord injury, osteoarthritis, and synovitis [16, 114, 179]. The blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB) and blood-joint barrier may play a hindering role due to their high selectivity for substance permeability [180, 181]. However, since neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles are helpful for crossing the blood–brain barrier, it indicates that they have a good basis for membrane-mediated response. It is conceivable that appropriate modifications may solve this problem and enable them to play a greater role in disease treatment. At the same time, the penetration effect of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles in other biological barriers, such as the blood-intestinal barrier and air-blood barrier, can be studied and further utilized to develop new delivery systems of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles based on intestinal absorption, aerosol administration, and other new methods.

Finally, since almost all current studies are conducted on animal models, which are limited by their inherent limitations, the therapeutic effect and in vivo biocompatibility of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles in clinical applications still need to be further verified. This requires more clinical trials to be carried out in the future.

Conclusions, challenges and future perspectives

As a highly innovative biomimetic nano-delivery system, neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles have shown great potential in the field of disease treatment. By cleverly combining the natural chemotactic and inflammation-targeting properties of neutrophils with nanotechnology, such nanoparticles can precisely deliver therapeutic drugs or diagnostic reagents to lesion sites, significantly improving the therapeutic effect and reducing systemic toxic and side effects.

Although significant progress has been made in the research and application of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles, there are still many challenges and limitations at present. In terms of preparation technology, the extraction process of neutrophil membranes is relatively complex, and the quantity of obtained membranes is limited, which makes it difficult to meet the needs of large-scale production [182]. At the same time, the fusion efficiency and stability between the cell membrane and the nanoparticle core need to be further improved. Nanoparticles prepared in different batches may have quality differences, which pose severe challenges to the standardization and large-scale production of their clinical applications [183, 184]. In terms of targeting performance, although nanoparticles can respond to inflammatory signals and migrate to lesion sites, the targeting efficiency is not completely unaffected. In the complex in vivo environment, they are still interfered by various factors, such as protein adsorption in the blood and macrophage phagocytosis, resulting in some nanoparticles failing to reach the target sites accurately and affecting the therapeutic effect [15, 140]. In addition, the long-term safety and toxicological effects of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles in vivo are still not fully clear. Issues such as whether long-term use will trigger potential immune responses and whether it will have adverse effects on the function of important organs need further in-depth research.

While the translational pathways of clinical-grade neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles (NM-NPs) face numerous challenges, these difficulties are not insurmountable with the synergistic development of materials science, bioengineering technology, and regulatory science. Future research can advance the clinical translation of NM-NPs from three aspects: Firstly, establish a standardized production system. Develop alternative sources of neutrophils based on stem cell-induced differentiation to address raw material shortages; meanwhile, research and develop automated and continuous preparation equipment to achieve large-scale and homogeneous production of NM-NPs. Secondly, build a targeted regulatory framework. Promote synergistic cooperation between regulatory authorities and R&D (Research and Development) institutions, clarify the classification and definition, quality control indicators, safety evaluation standards, and clinical application pathways of NM-NPs, and provide clear regulatory guidance for product translation. Thirdly, strengthen clinical translation research. Conduct multi-center and large-sample preclinical safety and efficacy evaluations, accumulate key clinical data, and verify the clinical value of NM-NPs in the treatment of different diseases. Through the above efforts, neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles will gradually break through translational bottlenecks, become important clinical therapeutic tools in the field of targeted therapy, bringing new therapeutic hope to patients with diseases.

We believe that with continuous technological breakthroughs and deepening mechanistic research, the application prospects of neutrophil membrane-coated nanoparticles in disease treatment will be broader, and they are expected to become an important bridge connecting basic research and clinical treatment.
