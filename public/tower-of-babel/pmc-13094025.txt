Expert collaborative evidence-based consensus for childhood lupus nephritis diagnosis and treatment: unifying clinical approach in Saudi Arabia

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLupus nephritis (LN) affects approximately 60–70% of children with systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE). Clinical practice for childhood lupus nephritis (cLN) management in Saudi Arabia varies significantly between pediatric rheumatologists and nephrologists. Unified, evidence-based national guidelines are needed to standardize care and improve outcomes. We aimed to develop the first evidence-based Saudi consensus recommendations for the diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment of childhood LN through collaboration between pediatric rheumatologists and nephrologists.MethodsA multidisciplinary working group developed evidence-based recommendations for cLN under the Saudi Rheumatology Society and Saudi Society of Nephrology and Transplantation. PICO questions guided a comprehensive literature search in Embase, PubMed, and Cochrane, extending the EULAR SHARE review (up to July 2013) to include studies through December 2022. Two independent reviewers screened, selected, and assessed studies, consulting adult LN guidelines when pediatric evidence was lacking. Recommendations were drafted and refined through a two-round e-Delphi process involving 20 national experts, with ≥70% agreement required for inclusion. Study quality was appraised using a modified Downs and Black checklist, and each statement was assigned a Level of Evidence and Grade of Recommendation per Oxford criteria.ResultsThe consensus panel established comprehensive recommendations for early diagnosis, therapeutic goals, treatment by LN class, and supportive care in cLN. Early evaluation for renal involvement in all cSLE patients and timely biopsy were emphasized, using the 2018 ISN/RPS classification with added histopathologic criteria. Prognostic targets include ≥25% proteinuria reduction at 3 months, ≥50% at 6 months, and <0.7 g/day at 12 months. Class-specific therapy was outlined: corticosteroids (low–moderate dose) for Class I–II, with DMARDs if needed; high-dose corticosteroids + MMF as first-line for proliferative LN, cyclophosphamide as an alternative, and multi-target or biologic regimens for refractory disease. For pure membranous LN, corticosteroids + MMF are preferred, with other agents as second-line. Adjuvant recommendations include hydroxychloroquine, early ACEi/ARB, infection prevention, and ovarian protection during cyclophosphamide.ConclusionThese evidence-based, locally adapted guidelines aim to standardize and optimize the management of cLN in Saudi Arabia, improving early diagnosis, guiding treatment selection by LN class, and promoting supportive strategies to enhance renal outcomes, survival, and quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12969-026-01215-6.

Introduction

Childhood systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE) is a chronic autoimmune disorder with a wide range of clinical features. In Saudi Arabia, lupus nephritis (LN) occurs in approximately 65–73% of children diagnosed with cSLE. LN represents a leading cause of morbidity and long-term organ damage in this group, with 10–19% progressing to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) within 10 years. Early recognition and timely, appropriate treatment of childhood LN (cLN) are therefore crucial to achieving better renal outcomes, prolonging survival, and improving quality of life [1–3].

While several clinical practice guidelines exist for adult LN management [4, 5]. Only one evidence-based guideline for cLN developed by the Single Hub and Access point for Pediatric Rheumatology in Europe (SHARE) initiative was published in 2017 [6].

In Saudi Arabia, notable variability exists in the management of cLN between pediatric rheumatologists and pediatric nephrologists, especially in the choice of induction therapy for proliferative and pure class V disease. Moreover, most centers (77%) lack combined clinics that would enable coordinated, unified care [7]. This underscores the need for a national consensus on cLN management tailored to the region.

This initiative, conducted under the auspices of the Saudi Rheumatology Society and the Saudi Nephrology Society, aims to develop evidence-based national recommendations for the diagnosis, management, monitoring, and prognosis of childhood lupus nephritis (cLN) in Saudi Arabia, guiding pediatric rheumatologists and nephrologists while accounting for regional disease characteristics, variations in phenotype compared to other populations, and available healthcare resources.

Methodology

Formation of the working group

Following approval from the Saudi Rheumatology Society and the Saudi Society of Nephrology and Transplantation, a multidisciplinary working group was established. This team included two pediatric nephrologists, two pediatric rheumatologists, and two methodology experts. Their primary objective was to develop evidence-based recommendations for the diagnosis, management, monitoring, and prognosis of childhood lupus nephritis (cLN). To guide this process, they formulated PICO questions addressing these areas and initiated a comprehensive literature review across major electronic databases, including Embase, PubMed, and Cochrane.

Literature review process

The European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) SHARE guideline on cLN management had conducted a systematic literature review up to July 2013. The working group first assessed the studies previously extracted by the EULAR SHARE team and then extended the search to include publications from July 2013 through December 2022, aiming to capture any new evidence. To maintain consistency and comparability, the same inclusion and exclusion criteria outlined in the EULAR SHARE guideline were applied (see supplementary file 1) [6].

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers (A.M. and M.N.) screened all identified studies and abstracts for relevance. Articles that met the inclusion criteria were shared with all working group members for assessment of validity and for data extraction. The group members (A.M., M.N., I.S., A.B., E.B.) convened online to evaluate the included articles and to draft recommendation statements for both diagnosis and treatment. In cases where pediatric-specific evidence was lacking, adult-based guidelines were consulted to fill the evidence gap [4, 5].

Consensus-building via the e-Delphi method

The draft recommendations were presented to an expert panel composed of 10 pediatric rheumatologists and 10 pediatric nephrologists. Using the e-Delphi software (2024), panellists rated their level of agreement on a 5-point Likert scale: 1 & 2 = Strongly Disagree / Disagree, 3 = Neutral, 4 & 5 = Agree / Strongly Agree.

A recommendation was accepted if it achieved at least 70% agreement (scores of 4 or 5). Statements falling short of this threshold in the first round were revised based on panellist feedback and resubmitted for a second round of voting [4, 5].

Voting rounds and finalization

The first e-Delphi voting round was conducted via email in January 2025, with anonymity preserved for all responses. After reviewing the voting outcomes and expert feedback in an online meeting, the working group revised statements that did not meet the consensus threshold. The second e-Delphi round took place in February 2025, after which the group reconvened to finalize the recommendations. Statements failing to achieve 70% consensus in the second round were excluded from the final guideline.

Assessment framework for validity and quality

The study adopted a rigorous and comprehensive methodology to evaluate the validity and quality of all included articles. The level of evidence was determined under the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) guidelines for diagnostic and therapeutic studies [8]. Additionally, a modified version of the Downs and Black checklist was applied to accommodate various study designs. This checklist includes 27 items assessing reporting quality, external validity, internal validity (bias and confounding), and statistical power [9].

Adaptations of the downs and black checklist

To ensure relevance to different study types, several modifications were made. For observational studies, seven items deemed irrelevant (items 8, 13, 14, 16, 19, 23, and 24) were removed, and six items (items 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, and 12) were revised. For quasi-experimental designs, five items (14, 15, 16, 23, and 24) were excluded. Scoring was binary, with 1 point awarded if a criterion was met and 0 points if it was not. The maximum achievable scores were 19 for longitudinal cohort studies, 17 for cross-sectional or descriptive studies, and 22 for quasi-experimental studies.

Quality classification criteria

In its original form, the Downs and Black checklist categorizes study quality as Excellent (26–27), Good (20–25), Fair (15–19), and Poor (≤ 14) [10]. For this review, these categories were adapted for consistency: “Excellent” was classified as high quality, “Good” as moderate quality, “Fair” as low quality, “Poor” as very low quality.

Review process and discrepancy resolution

Two reviewers independently conducted the validity assessments for all included studies. Any disagreements between them were resolved through discussion. Further details regarding the scoring system and its modifications are available in Supplementary File 2.

Level of evidence and grading of recommendations

For each recommendation statement, the Level of Evidence (LoE: 1–4) and the Grade of Recommendation (GoR: A–D) were assigned following the framework of the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine [11].

Results

The initial literature search revealed 4579 articles from the three electronic databases. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts for the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we obtained 61 articles. Then, we reviewed the articles included in the EULAR SHARE guideline for cLN to arrive at a final total of 120 articles, comprising 64 for diagnosis and 46 for treatment, as shown in Fig. 1.

Recommendation for cLN diagnosis

Renal involvement is common in childhood systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE), with most cases of childhood lupus nephritis (cLN) being diagnosed within the first six months after cSLE onset [12]. Clinical presentations of cLN can include acute or chronic kidney impairment, microscopic or macroscopic glomerular hematuria, proteinuria, nephrotic syndrome, hypoalbuminemia, edema, hypertension, and active urinary sediments such as red blood cell casts and pyuria [13, 14].

As proteinuria in children may have multiple causes, patients with transient low-to-moderate proteinuria should first be evaluated for alternative etiologies before undergoing renal biopsy [15]. While there is no universally agreed proteinuria threshold for diagnosing cLN, most adult lupus nephritis (LN) guidelines recommend 0.5 g/day or a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) ≥ 50 mg/mmol in a first-morning sample [16, 17]. However, LN has been documented in cases with isolated proteinuria < 0.5 g/day, including proliferative forms, in both adults and children—particularly when accompanied by low complement (C3) levels. In such situations, close monitoring and consideration of an early biopsy are advised [18–21].

Quantifying proteinuria is essential for both diagnosis and disease monitoring. The gold standard is a 24-hour urine protein measurement, but this method is impractical for children at every visit. An early morning spot urine sample for UPCR correlates strongly with 24-hour proteinuria, though the correlation is slightly weaker in nephrotic-range cases [22].

In 2018, the International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) updated its LN classification system, clarifying definitions for crescents, adhesions, and fibrinoid necrosis; removing IV-S and IV-G; replacing the active/chronic distinction with modified NIH activity and chronicity indices; and introducing cut-offs for tubulointerstitial lesions [23]. Validation studies in cLN confirmed the value of these activity and chronicity indices in guiding induction therapy and predicting outcomes. They also recommended adding a 10% threshold for cellular and fibrous crescents, as 50% fibrous crescents are rare in cLN [24].

Class switching is common in cLN—often from lower classes (I, II) to proliferative forms, or from pure membranous/proliferative to mixed patterns. Therefore, any significant change in renal presentation (e.g., proteinuria, eGFR) should prompt evaluation for class switching and potential repeat biopsy [25, 26]. Electron microscopy further improves the accuracy of cLN classification [27]. The recommendations are outlined in Table 1.

Therapeutic goals

The 5-year renal survival rate in childhood lupus nephritis (cLN) has markedly improved over the decades, rising from approximately 50% to around 90% after the 2000s [28]. Proteinuria levels and renal function (eGFR) at both diagnosis and during follow-up are the strongest predictors of renal outcomes in cLN [29]. Various renal response criteria have been assessed, with the adult guideline definition for partial and complete remission achieved in 85% of patients at 6 months and 86% at 12 months post-diagnosis [30]. Findings from the MAINTAIN Nephritis Trial identified a proteinuria threshold of < 0.7 g/day at 12 months as the most accurate predictor of favorable renal outcomes [31]. Based on this, a treatment target of at least a 25% proteinuria reduction at 3 months and a minimum of 50% reduction at 6 months from baseline is recommended (Table 2).

Recommendation for cLN treatment

Treatment of class I and class II

No specific studies have been identified for class I cLN, and its management is primarily guided by the extent of extra-renal involvement. While there is no agreed-upon corticosteroid dosing protocol, low- to moderate-dose corticosteroids may be used for less than three months.

Class II LN occurs in 10–25% of cLN cases, with most patients responding well to corticosteroid therapy. A starting dose of prednisolone at 0.5 mg/kg/day for no longer than three months is recommended. In most cases, a three-month corticosteroid course is sufficient to control LN; however, some children with class II LN may require additional DMARDs such as tacrolimus, MMF, cyclophosphamide, or rituximab [32, 33]. For cases unresponsive to corticosteroids alone, reassessment of renal histopathology and consideration of repeat biopsy are important (Table 2).

Treatment of proliferative cLN, pure or mixed with class V

Proliferative lupus nephritis (LN) classes IV and III are the most common, most severe, and carry the worst prognosis in cLN, with class IV occurring more frequently than class III [3, 12, 13, 18]. These forms require early and aggressive management (Fig. 2).

Induction therapy for proliferative cLN

While multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) exist in adult proliferative LN, no RCTs have been conducted in children; most pediatric evidence comes from observational studies [34–37].

The largest observational pediatric study, conducted in the UK, compared induction therapy with mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) versus intravenous cyclophosphamide (IV CYC) in 51 children (34 received MMF; 17 received IV CYC). Both groups had similar remission, flare, and damage accrual rates, with a slightly shorter time to remission in the IV CYC group, though not statistically significant [38]. The Childhood Arthritis and Rheumatology Research Alliance (CARRA) study in North America included 41 newly diagnosed children from 10 centers; 24 received IV CYC, and 17 received MMF, with IV CYC used more often in class IV LN. Both groups showed comparable 6-month response rates [39].

Based on these findings, consensus supports high-dose corticosteroids plus MMF as the preferred induction regimen in children with proliferative LN, whether isolated or combined with class V. IV CYC remains an important alternative, particularly in severe presentations (e.g., crescentic lesions, low eGFR), with evidence from multiple studies in diverse populations showing its efficacy in preserving renal function and preventing relapses [38–41]. In adult LN, low-dose IV CYC (0.5 g every 2 weeks × 6 doses) was comparable to high-dose regimens (0.5–1 g every 4 weeks × 6–7 doses) in European populations, though most pediatric studies used the high-dose approach [40, 42].

Corticosteroids remain a mainstay in cLN and other severe cSLE manifestations, but their side effects and cumulative damage are significant. An early pediatric study found that high-dose IV pulse methylprednisolone (30 mg/kg/day, up to 1 g, for 6 doses) followed by high-dose oral prednisolone resulted in faster renal recovery compared with high-dose oral prednisolone alone [43]. Two adult LN trials reported that initial or intermittent IV methylprednisolone pulses (every 2 weeks × 3 doses) followed by lower-dose oral prednisolone (< 35 mg/day) achieved similar efficacy with fewer adverse effects [44, 45].

In MMF-treated patients with suboptimal response, therapeutic drug monitoring can optimize dosing; an MPA area under the curve (MPA-AUC) > 45 mg·h/L was linked to better outcomes [46]. Combining MMF with a calcineurin inhibitor (CNI; tacrolimus or cyclosporine) as part of multi-target therapy with reduced steroids produced favourable long-term results, especially in Asian children with proliferative LN, and is considered for severe or refractory cases [47].

Rituximab, when combined with MMF and corticosteroids in refractory cLN, improved clinical response and enabled corticosteroid dose reduction [32, 48]. Belimumab, a B-cell–targeted therapy approved for cSLE without nephritis in 2019, improved outcomes in adult LN in the BLISS-LN trial when added to standard therapy (corticosteroid plus MMF or azathioprine) [49]. While no pediatric LN studies were found, combining Belimumab with MMF is suggested as an option in refractory cLN.

For maintenance therapy, MMF is superior to other agents [41] (Fig. 3). If MMF is not tolerated or available, azathioprine (AZA) is a reasonable alternative [50]. In refractory cases with persistent proteinuria, adding tacrolimus to MMF maintenance improves proteinuria and overall renal prognosis [51, 52].

Maintenance therapy for proliferative cLN

In adult LN, continuing non-steroidal immunosuppressants for ≥ 3 years reduced flare risk compared to shorter durations [53]. Pediatric studies vary in maintenance length, but consensus recommends 3–5 years, individualized according to clinical response and prognostic factors (Table 2).

Pure membranous cLN

The available data on childhood pure membranous LN is very limited, with the largest data from the CARRA study, reporting 123 patients with class V, of whom 57 have pure class V LN. The most commonly used therapies are corticosteroids and MMF, and most of them have a favourable renal outcome [54]. Other immune therapies used include IV CYC, Rituximab, AZA, Tacrolimus, and Cyclosporine.

We couldn’t reach a consensus on induction therapy; however, Rituximab was considered an option for cases that didn’t respond to initial therapy, and MMF as 1st option maintenance treatment, and agreed about a minimum of 3 years maintenance therapy. (Table 2)

Adjuvant therapy

Although there is no RCT on hydroxychlorquine in cLN, use of hydroxychlorquine is recommended in all patients with cLN unless contraindicated.

Earlier use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi) or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) in cLN, within 6 months of diagnosis, reduces the corticosteroid duration [55].

The serious infection rate is higher in cLN compared to cSLE without nephritis and to healthy controls, and is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in cLN [56]. Therefore, careful evaluation of immunity status in children with LN and risk for infection, reviewing their immunization status and updating it, annual influenza vaccine, and hydroxychloroquine use are important measures to reduce infection in cLN [57].

Premature ovarian failure is a major concern in females who reach puberty, and the risk differs by age and cumulative cyclophosphamide dose [58]. A small randomized double-blind control trial in adolescent females with SLE showed that GnRH agonist (triptorelin) at a high dose (120 µg/kg body weight) intramuscular injection every 4 weeks was able to induce ovarian suppression, and therefore may provide ovarian protection during the cyclophosphamide course [59] (Table 2).

Discussion

This is the first Saudi consensus regarding childhood lupus nephritis involving both pediatric rheumatologists and pediatric nephrologists from various regions across the country. A total of 36 statements reached a consensus (70% or more). However, some of the important points, like the dose of prednisolone in Class I, did not reach a consensus. Moreover, the induction treatment for pure class V with MMF did not reach a consensus as well. This project found heterogeneity in treating patients with cLN among experts in both fields. This reflects the unmet need for national guidelines.

Our consensus represents an important first step toward establishing standardized practices among physicians in Saudi Arabia. However, further research and additional projects are necessary to develop a comprehensive and practical management pathway for these patients. There is also a clear need for more educational workshops and lectures targeting all healthcare professionals involved in the care of cLN, to facilitate the implementation and dissemination of these guidelines throughout the medical community.

These recommendations have the potential to standardize care for cLN throughout Saudi Arabia by offering a cohesive evidence-based framework that would eliminate unnecessary variation in clinical practice. This could result in better patient outcomes through earlier diagnosis, optimal treatment selection, and structured follow-up. Additionally, the document provides policymakers with a resource on workforce training, medication procurement, and service planning. Crucially, given the rapid advancements in precision medicine and targeted treatments for systemic lupus erythematosus, the consensus is intended to be an established guideline that is updated frequently as new information becomes available.

Limitations

The development of these guidelines was constrained by the limited availability of pediatric-specific evidence on cLN, necessitating reliance on extrapolated data from adult LN studies for some recommendations. Differences in healthcare resources, medication accessibility, and institutional practices across Saudi Arabia may also impact the applicability and uniform adoption of these recommendations. Furthermore, in areas where high-quality evidence was lacking, expert consensus played a significant role, which introduces a potential element of bias. Additionally, there is a scarcity of long-term outcome data specific to Saudi pediatric LN patients, which limits the ability to customize recommendations fully to local epidemiology and prognosis.

Recommendations for future research

Future efforts should focus on conducting multicenter, prospective studies within Saudi Arabia to generate robust pediatric-specific evidence on cLN epidemiology, therapeutic efficacy, and long-term renal and extra-renal outcomes. Evaluating the cost-effectiveness and accessibility of advanced therapies, such as biologics and multi-target regimens, in the local healthcare system will also be important. Establishing national cLN registries can provide real-world data on treatment patterns, outcomes, and adverse events, enabling continual refinement of national guidelines.

Conclusion

This first Saudi evidence-based consensus on cLN offers a unified, class-specific, and locally adapted framework for diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment. By integrating international evidence with national expert opinion, these guidelines aim to standardize care, encourage early intervention, and improve renal outcomes, survival rates, and quality of life for children affected by LN in Saudi Arabia. Continued research and periodic updates will be essential to ensure these recommendations remain current with emerging evidence and evolving clinical practice.
