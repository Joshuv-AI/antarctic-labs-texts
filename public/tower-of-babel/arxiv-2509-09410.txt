Optimal convergence rates in multiscale elliptic homogenization

Introduction

1.1
Motivations

The homogenization theory for elliptic equations with one single microscopic scale has been well understood, for either deterministic or random coefficients. However, many hierarchical composite materials (such as biological tissues and nanocomposites) are not characterized by just one microscopic scale but rather by a hierarchy of scales, i.e., a composite material with inclusions at one scale and further fine structures at smaller scales within those inclusions
[
20
,
11
,
22
]
. The multiscale nature also arises in fluid dynamics with a close relation to the evolution of turbulence; see
[
10
,
12
,
23
,
16
,
3
,
9
]
and references therein.
In this paper, we study the elliptic equations (or systems) with variable coefficients oscillating at multiple microscopic scales. Precisely, we consider

(1.1)

-\nabla\cdot A_{\varepsilon}(x)\nabla u_{\varepsilon}=f,

in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The coefficient matrix
A_{\varepsilon}
takes a form of

A_{\varepsilon}(x)=A\Big(\frac{x}{\varepsilon_{1}},\frac{x}{\varepsilon_{2}},\cdots,\frac{x}{\varepsilon_{n}}\Big),

where
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n})\in(0,1]^{n}
. Importantly, we assume that
A(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})
is 1-periodic in each
y_{i}=(y_{i}^{1},y_{i}^{2},\cdots,y_{i}^{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
1\gg\varepsilon_{1}>\varepsilon_{2}>\cdots>\varepsilon_{n}>0
. The ellipticity and regularity assumptions of
A
will be given later precisely.

The homogenization for equation (
1.1
) was first rigorously studied in the classical monograph
[
6
]
for the case
\varepsilon_{i}=\varepsilon^{i}
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
; also see
[
5
]
for linear elasticity. In this case, since
\varepsilon
is small, the scales
\varepsilon^{i}
’s satisfy
\varepsilon\gg\varepsilon^{2}\gg\varepsilon^{3}\cdots\gg\varepsilon^{n}
, namely, these
n
distinct scales are all well-separated from each other. Thus a natural strategy can be applied: first homogenize with respect to the smallest microscopic scale
\varepsilon_{n}
while keeping the remaining
n-1
larger scales, and then homogenize the second smallest scale
\varepsilon_{n-1}
while keeping the remaining
n-2
scales, and repeat this process until all the microscopic scales are homogenized. As a consequence, the optimal
O(\varepsilon)
convergence rate was obtained in
[
6
]
. This homogenization process is called reiterated homogenization (probably known already on the physical level in the 1930’s
[
8
]
),
whose outcome is a homogenized equation with constant coefficient matrix. This homogenized matrix is uniquely determined by
A(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})
and independent of the microscopic scales
\varepsilon
. Later on, Allaire and Briane
[
1
]
established the qualitative reiterated homogenization under a scale-separation condition, namely,

(1.2)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon_{1}=0,\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\varepsilon_{i+1}}{\varepsilon_{i}}=0,\quad\text{for all }i=1,2,\cdots,n-1.

The condition (
1.2
) is the minimal assumption to guarantee a unique homogenized equation independent of the micro-scales
\varepsilon
; see the example in Section
7.1
. Since then, the method of reiterated homogenization has been extended to various kinds of models, such as nonlinear elliptic equations/functionals
[
7
,
21
,
24
]
, parabolic equations
[
14
,
35
]
, etc. The effective numerical algorithms to solve (
1.1
) have also been developed; see e.g.
[
13
,
17
,
15
]
.

Recently, by the method of reiterated homogenization, the quantitative convergence rate for (
1.1
) was obtained by Niu, Shen and Xu
[
26
]
:

(1.3)

\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\Big(\varepsilon_{1}+\frac{\varepsilon_{2}}{\varepsilon_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{\varepsilon_{n-1}}\Big)\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where
u_{0}
is the solution of the homogenized equation under (
1.2
). It was shown in
[
26
]
that the above convergence rate is optimal at least for Lipschitz coefficients (also see
[
25
]
for parallel results for parabolic equations with several temporal scales). More recently, the reiterated homogenization method has been used to obtain the anomalous dissipation in scalar turbulence
[
3
,
9
]
.

On the other hand, we are also interested in the uniform regularity for the multiscale elliptic equation (
1.1
). With the convergence rate (
1.3
), the uniform Lipschitz estimate was established in
[
26
]
under a quantitative well-separation condition

(1.4)

\varepsilon_{i+1}\lesssim\varepsilon_{i}^{1+\alpha}\quad\text{for all }i=1,2,\cdots,n-1,

for some
\alpha>0
. The condition (
1.4
) is often too strong in applications to multiscale self-similar materials. For example, the case
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon/|\log\varepsilon|
is not included in the condition (
1.4
). More recently, for arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,1]^{n}
without any scale-separation condition, Niu and Zhuge established the uniform
C^{\alpha}
regularity for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
by a compactness method
[
27
]
and the
W^{1,p}
estimates for any
p\in(1,\infty)
by a quantitative method
[
28
]
. However, the uniform Lipschitz estimate for arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,1]^{n}
remains to be an open problem. We mention that in some special cases, such as
\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{1}
are all constants of order
O(1)
satisfying certain Diophantine condition, the quantitative convergence rates and uniform Lipschitz estimate are valid; see
[
19
,
29
,
2
,
33
,
27
]
.

In this paper, we reconsider the optimality of the convergence rates for the equation (
1.1
).
The convergence rate (
1.3
) obtained in
[
26
]
is limited by the ratios
\varepsilon_{i+1}/\varepsilon_{i}
, which could be extremely slow. For example, in the aforementioned case
n=2
,
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon/|\log\varepsilon|
, the convergence rate is
O(|\log\varepsilon|^{-1})
, which is unsatisfactory in application. We emphasize again that it has been seen in
[
26
]
that this convergence rate is in general optimal at least for Lipschitz coefficients by the method of reiterated homogenization.
In view of the above fact, a natural belief in classical homogenization is that, in order to improve the ratio terms of convergence rate, one has to
introduce more correction terms that include the ones of order
O(\varepsilon_{i+1}/\varepsilon_{i})
. Surprisingly, this is not the case for the ratio terms in multiscale homogenization. In the present paper, we will show that the possible slow convergence rates are not due to the missing correction terms, but the regularity of coefficients and the reiterated homogenization method itself, that lead to an inaccurate homogenized equation. The main flaw in the reiterated homogenization method is that it actually only homogenizes one (the smallest) scale at a time and the different coupled scales are not interacting each other. The lack of interaction between distinct scales, particularly for non-well-separated or almost resonant scales, yields inaccuracy in homogenization. To solve this issue, we need to develop a true multiscale method that can handle the interaction between different scales and give the optimal effective equations and convergence rates.

1.2
Main results

We assume that
A=A(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})
satisfies the following assumptions:

•

Ellipticity and boundedness: there exists
\lambda>0
such that for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(1.5)

\xi\cdot A\xi\geq\lambda|\xi|^{2},\qquad|A\xi|\leq\lambda^{-1}\xi.

•

Periodicity: for any
z_{1},\cdots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

(1.6)

A(y_{1}+z_{1},y_{2}+z_{2},\cdots,y_{n}+z_{n})=A(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n}).

•

Real analyticity: there exist
C_{0}
and
\Lambda_{0}>0
such that for any
\ell\geq 0
,

(1.7)

|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}A|\leq C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\ell}\ell!,

where
\mathbf{D}_{n}=(\nabla_{y_{1}},\nabla_{y_{2}},\cdots,\nabla_{y_{n}})
.

The constants
(d,n,\lambda,C_{0},\Lambda_{0})
will be called the characters of
A
.

Now we state our main theorem. For the sake of simplicity and convenience, the theorem is stated without seeking generality (for example, the same result holds for elliptic systems, or different boundary conditions; see
[
31
]
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume
A
satisfies (
1.5
), (
1.6
) and (
1.7
). Let
\Omega
be a bounded
C^{1,1}
domain and
u_{\varepsilon}
be the weak solution of

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot A_{\varepsilon}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}&=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{\varepsilon}&=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Then there exists a constant (effective) matrix
\overline{A}
(depending on
A
and
\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{1}
) such that if
u_{0}
is the weak solution of

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\overline{A}\nabla u_{0}&=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{0}&=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

then

(1.8)

\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(\varepsilon_{1}+\max_{1\leq i\leq n-1}\{e^{-c\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{i+1}}\}\big)\big\{\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\big\},

where
C
and
c
are constants depending only on the characters of
A
and
\Omega
.

The above theorem substantially improves the ratio parts of the convergence rate from linear
\varepsilon_{i+1}/\varepsilon_{i}
to exponential
e^{-c\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{i+1}}
when the coefficients are real analytic. This exponential rate is optimal in the sense that it does not hold generally if we replace
c
by some larger constant
c_{1}
; see the counterexample in Section
7.2
. An inspect of our proof also shows that if
A\in C^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{n\times d})
for some finite
m
, we can also get a rate with a polynomial of
\varepsilon_{i+1}/\varepsilon_{i}
. Recall that in the classical homogenization theory with one scale, the optimal convergence rate of
O(\varepsilon)
actually is independent of the regularity of the coefficients (precisely, bounded measurable coefficients suffice). Thus
O(\varepsilon_{1})
part in (
1.8
) is also optimal and cannot be improved in general (unless the higher-order correctors and boundary layers are introduced).

It is important to point out that the effective matrix
\overline{A}
in Theorem
1.1
is derived through a new multiscale method involving multiscale correctors, and therefore depends not only on
A
, but also on the ratios
\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{1}
, which indicates that the construction of
\overline{A}
takes into account the interaction between different scales. It turns out that
\overline{A}
, as an effective matrix, is more accurate (actually optimal in some sense) than the homogenized matrix
A_{0}
derived merely by reiterated homogenization under (
1.2
), which is independent of
\varepsilon
. Moreover, we can precisely measure the difference between
\overline{A}
and
A_{0}
(see Proposition
4.1
and Remark
4.2
for details), which perfectly explains the difference between the errors in (
1.8
) and (
1.3
).

We now explain briefly why the analyticity/smoothness of the coefficients leads to a better convergence rate. As mentioned earlier, the slow convergence rate is caused by the resonance between close scales and the method of reiterated homogenization itself that ignores the interaction between different scales. The higher order regularity of the coefficients indicates the fast decay of amplitude of high-frequency oscillation that causes resonance between one scale and the next smaller scale. Consequently, the influence of resonance between two close scales can be dramatically reduced for analytic coefficients. This phenomenon seems to be very generic and we give a toy example in Section
7.3
that illustrates how the analyticity/smoothness helps improve the convergence rate in a simple averaging process with two oscillating scales. We recommend that readers look at the example before reading the detailed proof of Theorem
1.1
.
Overall, our main results and their proofs, together with the examples in Section
7
, give a relatively comprehensive picture, including possibilities and limits, on the homogenization of multiscale elliptic equations.

As a general principle in elliptic homogenization, once we obtain an algebraic convergence rate, we can establish the corresponding larger-scale regularity. Particularly, we can show the following (interior) uniform Lipschitz regularity under a mild double-log scale-separation condition.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume
A
satisfies (
1.5
), (
1.6
) and (
1.7
). Let
u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(B_{1})
be a weak solution of
-\nabla\cdot A_{\varepsilon}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}=f
in
B_{1}
with
f\in L^{p}(B_{1})
for some
p>d
. Then there exists some constant
M>0
such that if for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
,

(1.9)

\frac{\varepsilon_{i}}{\varepsilon_{i+1}}\geq M\log\log\varepsilon_{i}^{-1},

then

(1.10)

\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1/2})}\leq C\big(\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}+\|f\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})}\big),

where
M
and
C
depend only on
p
and the characters of
A
.

Compared with the uniform Lipschitz estimate in
[
26
]
,
the scale-separation condition (
1.9
) is much weaker than (
1.4
) due to the double logarithm. As an open problem mentioned earlier, it is not known if (
1.9
) can be completely removed.

Remark 1.3
.

In this paper, we only consider finite number of scales
n
and the constants in the estimates tend to infinity as
n\to\infty
. Though physically not relevant, mathematically one may also consider infinitely many scales
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\cdots,
with
\varepsilon_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
; see e.g.
[
1
,
16
]
. In this case, we need to identify the particular structure in defining coefficients with infinitely many variables. Typical examples include

A_{\varepsilon}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{k}A_{k}(\frac{x}{\varepsilon_{1}},\cdots,\frac{x}{\varepsilon_{k}}),

and

A_{\varepsilon}(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\Big(I+\sigma_{k}A_{k}(\frac{x}{\varepsilon_{1}},\cdots,\frac{x}{\varepsilon_{k}})\Big),

where
I
is the
d\times d
identity matrix, and each
A_{k}
is analytic, periodic and of order
O(1)
. We need
\sum_{k\geq 1}|\sigma_{k}|<\infty
to make the coefficients always well-defined as
\varepsilon_{k}
varies. If
\sigma_{k}
decays fast enough, we can truncate the coefficients at
k\leq N
such that
A_{\varepsilon}=A_{\varepsilon}^{N}+O(\sigma_{N})
and
A_{\varepsilon}^{N}
has exactly
N
microscopic scales. Then we apply our theorem to
A_{\varepsilon}^{N}
and get an error consisting of two parts, one from the convergence rate via Theorem
1.1
(growing as
N
increases) and the other from the difference
A_{\varepsilon}-A_{\varepsilon}^{N}
(decaying as
N
increases). Since our method is quantitative, it is possible to choose the best
N
(and the best effective equation) to minimize the error.

1.3
Strategy of the proof

The key of the proof is to construct the so-called multiscale correctors that take into consideration the interactions between different scales. Here we explain how these multiscale correctors are introduced naturally. First, we notice
A(x/\varepsilon_{1},x/\varepsilon_{2},\cdots,x/\varepsilon_{n})
can be written as
B_{\delta}(x/\varepsilon_{1})
, where

(1.11)

B_{\delta}(x)=A(x,x/\delta_{2},\cdots,x/\delta_{n}),\quad\text{with }\delta_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{1}.

Then
B_{\delta}(x/\varepsilon_{1})
can be viewed as a coefficient matrix with only one oscillating scale
\varepsilon_{1}
. However,
B_{\delta}(x)
depends on
\delta=(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\cdots,\delta_{n})
(we set
\delta_{1}=1
), and it is quasi-periodic and still rapidly oscillating in
x
since
\delta_{i}
are typically very small. Nevertheless, we can still try to construct the correctors associated to the matrix
B_{\delta}
by solving the following equation:

(1.12)

-\nabla\cdot B_{\delta}\nabla\chi^{j}_{\delta}=\nabla\cdot(B_{\delta}e_{j})\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.

If we can find a bounded solution of (
1.12
), then we are able to find the effective equation and homogenize all the scales simultaneously in a single step.
Clearly, the corrector equation (
1.12
) has quasi-periodic, multiscale nature and seems difficult to solve in general. We start by guessing the multiscale structure of the solution
\chi_{\delta}
, if they exist. Actually, since
B_{\delta}
has a structure as in (
1.11
), it is natural to guess the solution
\mathcal{X}_{\delta}=\mathcal{X}_{\delta}^{j}
possessing the same multiscale structure

(1.13)

\mathcal{X}_{\delta}(x)=\mathcal{X}(x,x/\delta_{2},\cdots,x/\delta_{n}),

where
\mathcal{X}(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})
is periodic in each
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Moreover, as
\delta_{i}
varies,
\mathcal{X}_{\delta}
continuously depends on
\delta_{i}
. This means that we may need the complete information of
\mathcal{X}(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})
for
n
independent variables
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For this reason, we need to, as usual, lift the system to a degenerate system in
d\times n
dimensional space. Precisely,

(1.14)

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{X}^{j}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})e_{j}\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{d\times n},

where
\widehat{\nabla}_{n}
is certain “directional gradient” defined by

\widehat{\nabla}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{-1}\nabla_{i},

and
\nabla_{i}=\nabla_{y_{i}}
. Now observe that even though the equation (
1.14
) is periodic, it is extremely degenerate since the ellipticity condition is not satisfied (the dimension of variables is much larger than the dimension of the directional gradient), and the coefficients are singular due to the large factors
\delta_{i}^{-1}
.

To establish the solvability of the equation (
1.14
), we take the following strategy.

Regularization of the corrector equation.
Instead of working on the equation (
1.14
), we regularize the equation by adding a positive zero-order term:

(1.15)

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y+\tau^{2}Y=\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{d\times n},

where
F
is a general smooth periodic function. Even though the leading term of equation (
1.15
) is still degenerate, we can actually find a unique solution by another regularization, whenever
\tau>0
and
F
is smooth enough; see Section
3.2
.

The size of
\tau
will be crucial for us. On one hand, we have to assume that
\tau
is small so that the equation (
1.15
) with
F=Ae_{j}
is indeed a good approximation of (
1.14
). On the other hand, some crucial estimates of the solution turn out to blow up as
\tau\to 0
. Actually, there are two types of estimates needed for the corrector equation (
1.15
), i.e., the energy estimates and uniform estimates. It is the main challenge of this paper to obtain the uniform estimate for suitably small
\tau
(depending on
\delta
). We emphasize that the energy estimates of (
1.15
) alone are not enough for the uniform boundedness independent of
\tau
and
\delta
for the solutions of (
1.15
). The multiscale structure of the equation will play an essential role in overcoming the difficulty. We explain the main idea briefly in the following.

Induction on the number of scales.

In order to get the uniform estimate independent of
\delta=(\delta_{1},\cdots,\delta_{2})
for the solution of the equation (
1.15
), we will take advantage of the multiscale structure of the equation and solve it inductively on the number of scales. If
n=1
, clearly, the equation (
1.15
) is periodic and nondegenerate, and thus it is solvable with energy estimate independent of
\tau
. The case
n=2
will be tricky already but can be solved with uniform estimates by a careful two-scale expansion. Then we assume that we can solve the equation with
n-1
scales and reduce the equation of
n
scales to the equation with
n-1
scales. This idea is possible if we apply the following ansatz

(1.16)

Y=Y(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},y_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{n}^{k}Y_{k}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},y_{n}),

and observation

(1.17)

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}=-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}-\delta_{n}^{-1}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot A\nabla_{n}-\delta_{n}^{-1}\nabla_{n}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}-\delta_{n}^{-2}\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}.

Taking (
1.16
) and (
1.17
) into (
1.15
), we will get a sequence of recursive equations for
Y_{k}
. To obtain the estimates of
Y_{k}
, due to the loss of derivatives, we need higher-order derivatives of the coefficients
A
and
F
. This is exactly where the analyticity of
A
comes into play. We point out that even for analytic (or trigonometric polynomial) coefficients, the infinite series in (
1.16
) does not converge, as the estimate of
Y_{k}
grows as factorial and
\delta_{n}^{k}
decays as exponential in
k
; instead, we have to deal with truncated series and estimate the errors in a proper way. A large part of this paper is devoted to handling these uniform estimates, which lead to considerable complexity in the inductive proofs; see Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.6
for details.

Extra scale-separation condition.

The above argument to solve the multiscale equation (
1.15
) works perfectly, except for an essential flaw due to the degeneracy of equation (
1.15
). To make sure the solution
Y
of
(
1.15
)
has a uniform bound independent of
\delta
, we require a scale-separation condition: for each
2\leq j\leq n
, there exists integer
k_{j}
such that

(1.18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\delta_{j}/\delta_{j-1})(k_{j}+\ell_{0})\lesssim 1,\\
&\tau^{2}\gtrsim\delta_{j-1}^{-1}e^{-k_{j}},\end{aligned}\right.

where we have skipped some constants and a precise formulation can be found in Theorem
3.6
. As we also expect
\tau
to be small, say
\tau^{2}\approx e^{-k_{j}/2}
, then (
1.18
) is reduced to

(1.19)

\varepsilon_{j}\leq\frac{c_{j}\varepsilon_{j-1}}{1+\log(\varepsilon_{1}/\varepsilon_{j-1})},

for some constant
c_{j}>0
. This extra scale-separation condition seems inevitable in the construction of multiscale correctors and will eventually
be removed in the main theorem.

In summary, under the scale-separation condition (
1.19
), we can construct the multiscale correctors from the lifted equation

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{X}^{j}+\tau^{2}\mathcal{X}^{j}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})e_{j}\quad\,\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{d\times n}

as a special case of (
1.15
) and obtain their uniform estimates independent of
\delta_{i}
and
\tau
. Define
\mathcal{X}_{\delta}(x)
as (
1.13
), we see that
\mathcal{X}_{\delta}
satisfies the equation

-\nabla\cdot B_{\delta}\nabla\mathcal{X}^{j}_{\delta}+\tau^{2}\mathcal{X}^{j}_{\delta}=\nabla\cdot(B_{\delta}e_{j})\quad\,\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Since
\tau
is very small,
\mathcal{X}_{\delta}
will serve a role as multiscale correctors. Moreover, the effective coefficient matrix is given by

\overline{A}=\langle A+A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{X}\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
means the average taken over the periodic cell
\mathbb{T}^{d\times n}
. Note that our
\overline{A}
depends essentially on
\delta_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{1}
.

In the construction of flux correctors, we also face degenerate equations and apply a similar argument. However, in this case, we can use essentially the
H^{2}
estimate (as the equation for flux correctors is in nondivergence form) to get rid of the factor
\delta_{j-1}^{-1}
in the second line of (
1.18
), which yields a harmless condition
\varepsilon_{j}\leq c_{j}\varepsilon_{j-1}
for some
c_{j}>0
.

Combination of simultaneous and reiterated homogenization.

With the correctors and flux correctors constructed properly, we can establish the convergence rates using the classical approach in elliptic homogenization under the scale-separation condition (
1.19
). In this case, we actually homogenize all the scales simultaneously in one step, which may be referred as simultaneous homogenization in contrast to reiterated homogenization that homogenizes only one scale in each step. To complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we finally need to remove the extra condition (
1.19
). But since we do not have multiscale correctors without the condition (
1.19
), a different idea is needed as follows. Suppose the condition (
1.19
) is not satisfied. Then we can find the largest integer
m\geq 2
such that the smallest
m
scales
(\varepsilon_{n-m+1},\varepsilon_{n-m+2},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n})
satisfy the condition (
1.19
) (modified with
\varepsilon_{1}
replaced by
\varepsilon_{n-m+1}
), and the smallest
m+1
scales
(\varepsilon_{n-m},\varepsilon_{n-m+1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n})
do not satisfy the condition. This actually implies that the scale ratio between
\varepsilon_{n-m}
and
\varepsilon_{n-m+1}
is exponentially larger than some scale ratio among
(\varepsilon_{n-m+1},\varepsilon_{n-m+2},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n})
, namely,

\frac{\varepsilon_{n-m+1}}{\varepsilon_{n-m}}\lesssim e^{-c_{p}\varepsilon_{p-1}/\varepsilon_{p}},

for some
p\geq n-m+2
.
In other words, the interaction between the scales
(\varepsilon_{n-m+1},\varepsilon_{n-m+2},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n})
and the remaining
n-m
larger scales is extremely small because they are separated so well. Thus, in the first step we can perform a simultaneous homogenization as above to the smallest
m
scales and leave the rest
n-m
scales alone to the next step in the spirit of reiterated homogenization. This first step will give an error as

\frac{\varepsilon_{n-m+1}}{\varepsilon_{n-m}}+\max_{n-m+1\leq i\leq n}\{e^{-c\varepsilon_{i-1}/\varepsilon_{i}}\}\lesssim\max_{n-m+1\leq i\leq n}\{e^{-c\varepsilon_{i-1}/\varepsilon_{i}}\},

where the inequality holds because of the choice of
m
implies
\frac{\varepsilon_{n-m+1}}{\varepsilon_{n-m}}\lesssim e^{-c\varepsilon_{i-1}/\varepsilon_{i}}
for some
i\geq n-m+1
; see Section
5.2
for details. Finally, we repeat this process until all the scales are homogenized. The key difference between our method sketched above and the classical reiterated homogenization is, for the classical reiterated homogenization, only one scale is homogenized in each step; while in our method we simultaneously homogenize several scales in each step by using multiscale correctors.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we define notations and provide basics in tensor calculation. In Section
3
, we construct multiscale correctors and obtain their energy estimates and uniform estimates. In Section
4
, we construct multiscale flux correctors and obtain their energy estimates and uniform estimates. In Section
5
, we establish the optimal convergence rate in Theorem
1.1
. In Section
6
, we prove the uniform Lipschitz estimate in Theorem
1.2
. Finally, in Section
7
, we provide several examples illustrating the optimality of our results.

2
Notations and preliminaries

In this section, we introduce some notations and basics that will be used throughout this paper. The readers are recommended to skip this section first and consult it whenever needed.

Tensors.
For rigorous calculation, we need the notation of tensor. Let
1\leq l\in\mathbb{N}
. By a tensor of order
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we mean a generalized
k
-dimensional matrix indexed by

(j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{k})\in J^{k}:=\{1,2,\cdots,l\}^{k},

written as

T=(T_{j_{1}j_{2}\cdots j_{k}}).

Note that a tensor of order zero is a scalar, a tensor of order 1 is a
l
-dimensional vector and a tensor of order 2 is an
l\times l
matrix. Denote by
\mathcal{T}^{k}
the space of tensors of order
k
. Define the norm of
T\in\mathcal{T}^{k}
by

(2.1)

|T|=\sum_{(j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{k})\in J^{k}}|T_{j_{1}j_{2}\cdots j_{k}}|.

If
T\in\mathcal{T}^{k}
and
S\in\mathcal{T}^{m}
, then
T\otimes S\in\mathcal{T}^{k+m}
is defined by

T\otimes S=(T_{j_{1}j_{2}\cdots j_{k}}S_{j_{k+1}\cdots j_{k+m}}).

Obviously
|T\otimes S|=|T||S|
.

Let
F:\Omega\to\mathcal{T}^{k}
be a tensor-valued function defined in
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
\nabla F:\Omega\to\mathcal{T}^{k+1}
is defined as

\nabla F=(\partial_{x_{j_{k+1}}}F_{j_{1}j_{2}\cdots j_{k}}).

By this we can define generally
\nabla^{m}F:\Omega\to\mathcal{T}^{k+m}
inductively.

Symmetric tensors and symmetrilization.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
be the symmetric group of degree
k
on the symbols
\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
. We view an element
\sigma=\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{k}\}
of
\mathfrak{S}_{k}

as a permutation of
\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
.
For
T\in\mathcal{T}^{k}
, we denote by
\textbf{Sym}(T)
the symmetrilization of
T
, given by

\textbf{Sym}(T)=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}(T_{j_{\sigma_{1}}j_{\sigma_{2}}\cdots j_{\sigma_{k}}}).

By the triangle inequality,
|\textbf{Sym}(T)|\leq|T|
. A tensor
T\in\mathcal{T}^{k}
is symmetric if
\textbf{Sym}(T)=T
. If
f
is a scalar function, then
\nabla^{k}f
is a symmetric tensor of order
k
.

Generalized Leibniz rule.
Given two scalar-valued functions
f
and
g
, it holds

(2.2)

\nabla^{m}(fg)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\textbf{Sym}(\nabla^{k}f\otimes\nabla^{m-k}g),

where the binomial coefficient is given by

\binom{m}{k}=\frac{m!}{k!(m-k)!}.

If
f
and
g
are vectors or matrices, we will also use (
2.2
) by a slight abuse of notation. For example if
A=(a_{ij})
is a matrix and
w=(w_{j})
is a vector, then
Aw=(Aw)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{ij}w_{j}
and (
2.2
) should be interpreted as

\nabla^{m}(Aw)_{i}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\textbf{Sym}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\nabla^{k}a_{ij}\otimes\nabla^{m-k}w_{j}\Big).

Notations of derivatives.

In order to get quantitative estimates for the degenerate corrector equations (
1.15
), we need higher-order regularity of
A
and
F
, which are defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}=\{(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n}):y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
or
\mathbb{T}^{d\times n}\simeq[0,1]^{d\times n}
. Let
\mathbf{D}_{k}=(\nabla_{1},\nabla_{2},\cdots,\nabla_{k})
with
1\leq k\leq n
and
\nabla_{i}=\nabla_{y_{i}}=(\partial_{y_{i}^{1}},\cdots,\partial_{y_{i}^{d}})
. For a scalar function
f=f(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})
,
\mathbf{D}_{k}f
is a
d\times k
dimensional vector-valued function and
\mathbf{D}_{k}^{j}f
is a tensor of order
j
with dimension
d\times k
. This notation has been used in the assumption (
1.7
).

Note that the generalized Leibniz rule also applies to the gradient
\mathbf{D}_{k}
. That is, (
2.2
) holds with functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
or
\mathbb{T}^{d\times n}
and
\nabla
replaced by
\mathbf{D}_{k}
.

Recall that
\widehat{\nabla}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}^{-1}\nabla_{i}
, where
\delta_{i}
’s are in decreasing order.
Given a tensor-valued function
F
, we observe a simple fact:

(2.3)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\nabla}_{n}F|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{k}^{-1}|\nabla_{k}F|\leq\delta_{n}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\nabla_{k}F|=\delta_{n}^{-1}|\mathbf{D}_{n}F|.

Function spaces and mixed norms.

We consider a measurable scalar-valued function
F=F(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})
, 1-periodic in each
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since the function is 1-periodic, we will view it as defined in a periodic cell
\mathbb{T}^{d\times n}
.
For defining function spaces with mixed norms, we will use
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
to indicate certain norms on the first
n-1
variable
(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})
and the last variable
y_{n}
, respectively. Note that the number
n
may vary according to the context.

We say a scalar-valued function
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})=L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})
if
F
is locally
L^{2}
integrable and

\|F\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|F\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})|^{2}dy_{1}\cdots dy_{n}<\infty.

To simplify our notations, we have used
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
as the norm notation instead of
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})}
. This will cause no ambiguity for periodic functions defined on
\mathbb{T}^{d\times n}
.

We say
F\in L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}L^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})
if

\|F\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}L^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}:=\esssup_{(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})\in\mathbb{T}^{d\times(n-1)}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},y_{n})|^{2}dy_{n}<\infty.

We say
F\in L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})
if

\|F\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})|^{2}+|\nabla_{n}F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})|^{2}dy_{1}\cdots dy_{n}<\infty.

We say
F\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})=H^{1}_{\mathbf{xy}}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})
if

\|F\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\|F\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{xy}}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})|^{2}+|\mathbf{D}_{n}F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})|^{2}dy_{1}\cdots dy_{n}<\infty.

Similarly, we can also define the spaces
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})=L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})
,
L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})
,
L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})
,
L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times n})
, etc. If
F
is independent of
y_{n}
, then we will also say

F=F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})\in L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times(n-1)})
if

\|F\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})|^{2}dy_{1}\cdots dy_{n-1}<\infty,

and say

F=F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})\in H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbb{T}^{d\times(n-1)})
if

\|F\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})|^{2}+|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})|^{2}dy_{1}\cdots dy_{n-1}<\infty.

Norms of tensor-valued functions.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{X}
be a norm for the scalar functions defined on
\mathbb{T}^{d\times n}
. In the same spirit of (
2.1
), we extend this norm to tensor-valued functions by

\|F\|_{X}=\sum_{(j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{k})\in J^{k}}\|F_{j_{1}j_{2}\cdots j_{k}}\|_{X}.

Note that
\|\textbf{Sym}(F)\|_{X}\leq\|F\|_{X}
.
Moreover, if for scalar functions, we have
\|fg\|_{X}\leq M\|f\|_{Y}\|g\|_{Z}
(with possibly different function spaces
Y
and
Z
), then for tensor-valued functions
F
and
G
we also have

\|F\otimes G\|_{X}\leq M\|F\|_{Y}\|G\|_{Z}.

For example, by the Hölder’s inequality for scalar functions, we have
\|F\otimes G\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}L^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\|F\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|G\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}L^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

For a multi-index
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
|\beta|=m
, we have

\|\nabla^{m}f\|_{X}=\sum_{|\beta|=m}\frac{m!}{\beta!}\|\partial^{\beta}f\|_{X}.

This is because
\nabla^{m}f
is symmetric and has
m!/\beta!
components equal to
\partial^{\beta}f
. However,
\partial^{\beta}
will not be used in this paper.

3
Multiscale correctors

3.1
Multiscale ansatz

In this subsection, we analyze the structure of the solutions of the corrector equation (
1.15
).
Recall the definition of
\widehat{\nabla}_{n}
and write

(3.1)

\widehat{\nabla}_{n}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}+\delta_{n}^{-1}\nabla_{n}.

Recall that
\delta_{1}=1,\delta_{2},\cdots,\delta_{n-1}
are in decreasing order. We may assume
\delta_{n}\ll\delta_{n-1}
and the solution of (
1.15
) takes a form of

(3.2)

Y=Y(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},y_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{n}^{k}Y_{k}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},y_{n}),

where each
Y_{k}
is 1-periodic in each
y_{i}
. In the above formal expansion, we actually view
\delta_{n}
as the only microscopic scale and the rest scales
(\delta_{1},\cdots,\delta_{n-1})
as the same macroscopic scales implicitly enclosed in
Y_{k}
. Thus both (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) are essentially two-scale expansions.

Using (
3.1
), we have

(3.3)

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}+\delta_{n}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\delta_{n}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}}+\delta_{n}^{-2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}:=-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}:=-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot A\nabla_{n},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}}:=-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}:=-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}.

Here the subscripts
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
only for notation purpose refer to the first
n-1
variables and the last variable, respectively, if there are exactly
n
independent variables. We will only use them in subscripts for operators, spaces or norms (the exact meaning or definition will be given at its first appearance), and will not use them to represent variables of functions.

Applying (
3.3
) to (
3.2
), we have

\displaystyle-\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y+\tau^{2}Y

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\delta_{n}^{-2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}+\delta_{n}^{-1}(L_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}})\delta_{n}^{k}Y_{k}

\displaystyle=\delta_{n}^{-2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{0}+\delta_{n}^{-1}\Big\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{1}+(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{0}\Big\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\delta_{n}^{k-2}\Big\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{k}+(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{k-1}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{k-2}+\tau^{2}Y_{k-2}\Big\}.

Consequently, if
Y
is a solution of the corrector equation (
1.15
), we get a system of recursive equations for
Y_{k}
:

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{0}=0,

(3.4b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{1}+(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{0}=\nabla_{n}\cdot F,

(3.4c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{2}+(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{1}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{0}+\tau^{2}Y_{0}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot F,

(3.4d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{k}+(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{k-1}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{k-2}+\tau^{2}Y_{k-2}=0,\quad k\geq 3.

In the following, we will show how to formally solve these equations recursively. The following notation for averages of periodic functions will be used:

(3.5)

\langle F\rangle_{y_{n}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F(\cdot,y_{n})dy_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle F\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\cdots\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})dy_{1}\cdots dy_{n}.

Find
Y_{0}
:
Note that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}
is a nondegenerate elliptic operator on the last variable
y_{n}
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Hence, from (
3.4a
), we see that
Y_{0}
is independent of
y_{n}
. Thus
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}Y_{0}=0
in (
3.4b
), which can be written as

(3.6)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{1}=\nabla_{n}\cdot F+\nabla_{n}\cdot(A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}).

Starting from
Y_{1}
, we will always decompose
Y_{k}
as

Y_{k}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},y_{n})=Y_{k}^{o}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},y_{n})+Y_{k}^{r}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1}),

where
Y_{k}^{o}
will be called an “oscillating” component (depending on
y_{n}
) and
Y_{k}^{r}
called a “regular” component (independent of
y_{n}
). Thus
Y_{k}^{r}
cannot be seen by the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}
. For example, from (
3.6
), we can only solve for
Y_{1}^{o}
. Note that the right-hand side of (
3.6
) has mean value zero over
y_{n}
. Generally, we let
Y_{k}^{o}
be the unique solution satisfying
\langle Y_{k}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}=0
.

We introduce the one-scale corrector (with respect to
y_{n}
)
\chi(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})=(\chi_{j}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}))
, which solves

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}\chi_{j}=\nabla_{n}\cdot(Ae_{j}).

Then it is not difficult to see that

(3.7)

Y_{1}^{o}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}^{-1}(\nabla_{n}\cdot F)+\chi\cdot\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}.

In general if for some 1-periodic functions
Z=Z(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})
and
F=F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})
satisfying the equation

(3.8)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Z=\nabla_{n}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}F=-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}}F,

then
Z=-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}}F=\chi\cdot\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}F
.

Now with
Y_{1}^{o}
given by (
3.7
), we can use (
3.4c
) to find the equation for
Y_{0}
in
(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})
. First taking average in
y_{n}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
to (
3.4c
), we get

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\nabla_{n}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(\langle A\rangle_{y_{n}}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0})+\tau^{2}Y_{0}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(\langle F\rangle_{y_{n}}).

By (
3.7
), we have

\langle A\nabla_{n}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}=\langle A\nabla_{n}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}^{-1}(\nabla_{n}\cdot F)\rangle_{y_{n}}+\langle A\nabla_{n}\chi\rangle_{y_{n}}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}.

Combining the last two equations, we arrive at the equation for
Y_{0}

(3.9)

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\big\{\langle A+A\nabla_{n}\chi\rangle_{y_{n}}\big\}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}+\tau^{2}Y_{0}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\big\{\langle F+A\nabla_{n}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}^{-1}(\nabla_{n}\cdot F)\rangle_{y_{n}}\big\}.

Define

\widehat{A}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})=\langle A+A\nabla_{n}\chi\rangle_{y_{n}},

and

\widehat{F}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})=\langle F+A\nabla_{n}\mathcal{L}_{yy}^{-1}(\nabla_{n}\cdot F)\rangle_{y_{n}}.

Then (
3.9
) is reduced to

(3.10)

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}+\tau^{2}Y_{0}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{F}.

This equation has exactly the same structure as (
1.15
) with one less scale, which suggests that the original multiscale corrector equation may be solved inductively on the number of scales. We point out that the equation (
3.10
) guarantees that the mean value over
y_{n}
is zero for (
3.4c
) and therefore we can solve for
Y_{2}^{o}
from that equation in the next step.

Find
Y_{1}
:
Recall that we write
Y_{1}=Y_{1}^{o}+Y_{1}^{r}
. In the previous step, we have found
Y_{1}^{o}
in a form of (
3.7
).
It remains to find
Y_{1}^{r}
.

We write (
3.4c
) as

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{2}^{o}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot F-(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{1}^{o}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}}Y_{1}^{r}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{0}-\tau^{2}Y_{0},

where we have used the simple fact
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}Y_{1}^{r}=0
as
Y_{1}^{r}
is independent of
y_{n}
. Using the solution of the general equation (
3.8
), we notice that the solution of the above equation can be formally expressed as

Y_{2}^{o}=\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}+\chi\cdot\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{r},

where
\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}
satisfies the equation

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot F-(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{1}^{o}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{0}-\tau^{2}Y_{0}.

Note that since
Y_{0}
and
Y_{1}^{o}
have been found,
\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}
can be solved from the above equation.

Next, we consider the equation (
3.4d
) with
k=3

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{3}+(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{2}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{1}+\tau^{2}Y_{1}=0.

Integrating in
y_{n}
, we get

\displaystyle-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\nabla_{n}Y_{2}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(\langle A\rangle_{y_{n}}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{r})+\tau^{2}Y_{1}^{r}

\displaystyle=-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(\langle A+A\nabla_{n}\chi\rangle_{y_{n}}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{r})-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}

\displaystyle-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}+\tau^{2}Y_{1}^{r}

\displaystyle=0.

As a result, we have

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{r}+\tau^{2}Y_{1}^{r}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}.

This is again the same type of equation as (
1.15
) with one less scale and therefore can be solved by induction. Note that the solution
Y_{1}^{r}
satisfies
\langle Y_{1}^{r}\rangle=0
. Together with
\langle Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}=0
, we then have
\langle Y_{1}\rangle=0
.

Find
Y_{k}
for
k\geq 2
:
This can be solved inductively. In the previous calculation, we have completely found
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}^{o}
. Now suppose we have completely found
Y_{k-1}
(including
Y_{k-1}^{o}
and
Y_{k-1}^{r}
) and
Y_{k}^{o}
. We then only need to find

and

.

To this end, we consider the equation (

) with

replaced by

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{k+1}+(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{k}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{k-1}+\tau^{2}Y_{k-1}=0.

Using
Y_{k}=Y_{k}^{o}+Y_{k}^{r}
, we get

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{k+1}=-(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{k}^{o}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}}Y_{k}^{r}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{k-1}-\tau^{2}Y_{k-1}.

Using the solution formula of (

), we have

(3.11)

Y_{k+1}^{o}=\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}+\chi\cdot\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k}^{r},

where

(3.12)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}=-(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{k}^{o}-\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{k-1}-\tau^{2}Y_{k-1}.

Inserting (
3.11
) into

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}Y_{k+2}+(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{k+1}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{k}+\tau^{2}Y_{k}=0

and integrating in

(using

), we arrive at

(3.13)

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k}^{r}+\tau^{2}Y_{k}^{r}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}.

Thus provided that we can solve (
1.15
) with
n-1
scales, we can find
Y_{k}^{r}
from (

) and (
3.13
), and then

is given by (

).

We summarize the above procedure in proper order and list the necessary recursive equations as follows:

•

Step 1: Find
\chi
and

:

(3.14)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}\chi_{j}=\nabla_{n}\cdot(Ae_{j}),

(3.15)

\displaystyle\widehat{A}(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n-1})=\langle A+A\nabla_{n}\chi\rangle_{y_{n}}.

•

Step 2: Find
Y_{0}
by the following three equations in order:

(3.16)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{F}=\nabla_{n}\cdot F,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n-1})=\langle F+A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{F}\rangle_{y_{n}},

(3.18)

\displaystyle-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}+\tau^{2}Y_{0}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{F}.

•

Step 3: Find
Y_{1}=Y_{1}^{o}+Y_{1}^{r}
by the following three equations in order:

(3.19)

\displaystyle Y_{1}^{o}=\widetilde{F}+\chi\cdot\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0},

(3.20)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot F+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(A\nabla_{n}Y_{1}^{o})+\nabla_{n}\cdot(A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0})-\tau^{2}Y_{0},

(3.21)

\displaystyle-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{r}+\tau^{2}Y_{1}^{r}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\big(\langle A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}+\langle A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}\big).

•

Step 4: Find

for

recursively by the following three equations in order:

(3.22)

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{o}=\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}+\chi\cdot\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k}^{r},

(3.23)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(A\nabla_{n}Y_{k+1}^{o})+\nabla_{n}\cdot(A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k+1}^{o})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k})-\tau^{2}Y_{k},

(3.24)

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k+1}^{r})+\tau^{2}Y_{k+1}^{r}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}\rangle_{y}+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k+1}^{o}\rangle_{y}.

In the above procedure, except for simple algebraic equations (including (

), (
3.17
), (
3.19
), (
3.22
)), we need to solve two types of elliptic PDEs. One type is associated with the operator

(including (

), (
3.16
), (
3.20
), (
3.23
)), which is easy to solve as it is a nondegenerate elliptic operator with respect to

. The other type of equations is associated with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}=-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}
(including (

), (
3.21
), (

)), which is one scale less than the original operator

. Hence, we expect to solve these equations in an inductive argument on the number of scales.

3.2
Energy estimates

In this section, we will study the energy estimates of corrector equations in the form of

(3.25)

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y+\tau^{2}Y=s_{1}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot F+s_{2}G\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{d\times n},

where
s_{i}=0
or
1
,
\tau>0
is supposed to be small, and
F=F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}),G=G(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})
are
1
-periodic in each
y_{i}.
We add the extra source term
G
(compared to (
1.15
)) for later applications. The equation is very degenerate and its solvability is not obvious. However, we may add another regularization term
-\theta^{2}\Delta Y
, where
\Delta=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\nabla_{k}\cdot\nabla_{k}
, to the equation and then consider

(3.26)

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y-\theta^{2}\Delta Y+\tau^{2}Y=s_{1}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot F+s_{2}G\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{d\times n}.

The equation (
3.26
) is solvable in the energy space
H^{1}_{\mathbf{xy}}
.
If
F
and
G
are smooth enough, due to the term
\tau^{2}Y
, we can estimate
\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y
depending on
\tau
, but independent of
\theta
. Then letting
\theta\to 0
, we can extract a regular periodic solution of (
3.25
). Some details can be found in the following Proposition
3.1
.

We assume for all
\ell\geq 0

(3.27)

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\ell}\ell!,

and for some
q\geq 0
and all
\ell\geq 0
,

(3.28)

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}F\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}G\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
A
satisfies (
1.5
), (
1.6
) and (
3.27
).
Let
Y
be a weak solution of (
3.25
) with
F,G
satisfying (
3.28
). Then there exists a constant
C_{*}
depending only on
C_{0},\lambda
and
d
such that for
\ell\geq 0
and
\Lambda=\max\{\Lambda_{1},C_{*}\Lambda_{0}\}
we have

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq(s_{1}+\tau^{-1}s_{2})C_{*}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda=\max\{\Lambda_{1},C_{*}\Lambda_{0}\}
for some
C_{*}>1
to be chosen later.
The energy estimate (multiply the equation against
Y
and apply the integration by parts) of (
3.25
) yields

\|\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\tau\|Y\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq C_{d}\|s_{1}F\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+C_{d}\tau^{-1}\|s_{2}G\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq C_{d}(s_{1}+\tau^{-1}s_{2})q!.

This is the base case
\ell=0
and we need
C_{*}\geq C_{d}
.
Suppose the desired estimate holds for
\ell=0,1,\cdots,k-1
, we show it holds for
\ell=k
. Applying
\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k}
to the equation (
3.25
), we obtain a tensor-valued equation for
\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k}Y
,

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot(A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k}Y)+\tau^{2}\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k}Y=s_{1}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k}F+s_{2}\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k}G+\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot\bigg\{\sum_{m=1}^{k}\binom{k}{m}\textbf{Sym}(\mathbf{D}_{n}^{m}A\otimes\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k-m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y)\bigg\},

where we have used the generalized Leibniz rule
and
\textbf{Sym}(\cdot)
denotes the symmetrilization of a tensor; see Section
2
for the detailed interpretation.
By the energy estimate and inductive assumption on
\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y
for
\ell\leq k-1
,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k}Y\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}\|s_{1}\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k}F\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+C_{d}\tau^{-1}\|s_{2}\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k}G\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+C_{d}\sum_{m=1}^{k}\binom{k}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{k-m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}(s_{1}+\tau^{-1}s_{2})\Lambda_{1}^{k}(k+q)!+C_{d}(s_{1}+\tau^{-1}s_{2})\sum_{m=1}^{k}\binom{k}{m}C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{m}m!C_{*}\Lambda^{k-m}(k-m+q)!

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}(s_{1}+\tau^{-1}s_{2})\Lambda^{k}(k+q)!+C_{d}C_{0}(s_{1}+\tau^{-1}s_{2})C_{*}\Lambda^{k}(k+q)!\sum_{m=1}^{k}(\Lambda_{0}/\Lambda)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{C_{d}}{C_{*}}+\frac{C_{d}C_{0}}{C_{*}-1})(s_{1}+\tau^{-1}s_{2})C_{*}\Lambda^{k}(k+q)!,

where we have used
\Lambda\geq C_{*}\Lambda_{0}
in the last inequality. In the third inequality of (
3.29
), we have used the simple fact

(3.30)

\binom{k}{m}m!(k-m+q)!\leq(k+q)!.

In the last inequality of (
3.29
), we have used

(3.31)

\sum_{m=1}^{k}C_{*}^{-m}\leq\frac{1}{C_{*}-1},

whenever
C_{*}>1
.

We choose
C_{*}
large depending only on
C_{d}
and
C_{0}
such that

(3.32)

\frac{C_{d}}{C_{*}}+\frac{C_{d}C_{0}}{C_{*}-1}\leq 1.

Thus (
3.29
) implies the desired estimate for
\ell=k
and completes the inductive argument.
∎

Our second energy estimate is for the equation with operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}=-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}
that appears in the recursive equations in the previous subsection.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let

,

and

and

Furthermore, if the assumptions on

are replaced by

\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}F\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}L^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}G\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}L^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

then the norm of

Proof.

The elliptic equation (

are viewed as parameters not involved in the differential operators. Due to

Let

The energy estimate of (

\|Y(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},\cdot)\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C_{d}\big(\|F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}+\|G(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\big).

This is the base case

.

Unlike Proposition

3.3
Uniform estimates for two scales

The estimate of
\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y\|_{L^{2}}
in Proposition
3.1
depends essentially on
\tau
. In order to get estimates independent of
\tau
, we need to take advantage of the structure of the equation. To achieve this, we begin with the case
n=2
, for which
A=A(y_{1},y_{2})
and
\widehat{\nabla}_{2}=\nabla_{1}+\delta_{2}^{-1}\nabla_{2}
.
We will show that the uniform estimate is possible if
\delta_{2}
is sufficiently small and
\tau
is not too small (depending on
\delta_{2}
).

As in (
3.2
), we expect to have a solution of (
1.15
) with
n=2
in a form of

(3.36)

Y(y_{1},y_{2})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta_{2}^{j}Y_{j}(y_{1},y_{2}).

The recursive system for
Y_{j}
is given by (
3.4
) and can be solved through a sequence of equations (
3.14
)-(
3.24
).
However, even though we can solve all these
Y_{j}
’s recursively, the convergence of the above series cannot be guaranteed by the energy estimates. Thus we have to look at a partial sum truncated at
j=k
, denoted by
N_{k}
, and show that
N_{k}
satisfies

-\widehat{\nabla}_{2}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{2}N_{k}+\tau^{2}N_{k}=\widehat{\nabla}_{2}\cdot F+E_{k},

with some error
E_{k}
. With careful quantitative analysis, we can show
E_{k}
is small for
k
suitably larger and thus the error induced by
E_{k}
can be controlled by the energy estimate for suitably small
\tau
. The precise statement is the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
n=2
. Suppose that
A
satisfies (
1.5
), (
1.6
), and (
3.27
), and
F
satisfies (
3.28
). Let
Y
be the solution of

-\widehat{\nabla}_{2}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{2}Y+\tau^{2}Y=\widehat{\nabla}_{2}\cdot F\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{d\times 2}.

Then there exist
C,C_{**}
(depending on

) and
\Lambda=\max\{\Lambda_{1},C_{**}\Lambda_{0}\}
such that whenever
\delta_{2}
and
\tau
satisfy

\delta_{2}C^{3}\Lambda(k+\ell+q)\leq e^{-1}
and
\tau^{2}\geq e^{-k+1}C^{3}\Lambda
for some

and
\ell\geq 0
, then we have

\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq(C+2)\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+1+q)!.

Remark 3.5
.

When applying to the corrector equation in two scales, we take
F=Ae_{j}
with
C_{0}=1,\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{0}
and
q=0
. We use the Sobolev embedding theorem to find bounded correctors with bounded gradients, which only requires
\ell>d+1
. Thus the two constraints in Theorem
3.4
can be written as

(3.37)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\delta_{2}C^{3}\Lambda(k+d+1)\leq e^{-1},\\
&\tau^{2}\geq e^{-k+1}C^{3}\Lambda.\end{aligned}\right.

To guarantee the existence for some
k\geq 1
satisfying both of the above constraints, we need
\delta_{2}\leq c
for sufficient small
c>0
. This scale-separation condition can be assumed without loss of generality for the case
n=2
since otherwise Theorem
1.1
is trivial.
On the other hand, in order to have more accurate correctors, we need
\tau
as small as possible and thus we would like to choose
k
as large as possible due to the second constraint in (
3.37
). This implies that the largest possible

is comparable to
\delta_{2}^{-1}
and
\tau
is comparable to
e^{-c/\delta_{2}}=e^{-c\varepsilon_{1}/\varepsilon_{2}}
for some
c>0
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Before the proof, we emphasize that the constant
C
(depending only on
\lambda,C_{0},
and
d
) will be chosen large enough independent of
\Lambda,\delta_{2},k,\ell
and
\tau
. Other local constants depending on
\lambda,C_{0}
and

will be explained during the proof.

In view of (
3.36
), we need to estimate
Y_{k}
through a system of equations ordered in (
3.14
)-(
3.24
), divided into 4 steps. The estimates of
Y_{k}
for

are only based on the energy estimate in Proposition

.

Step 1:
Estimate of
\chi
and
\widehat{A}
.

The equation of
\chi=(\chi_{j}(y_{1},y_{2}))
is given by (
3.14
), i.e.,

-\nabla_{2}\cdot A\nabla_{2}\chi_{j}=\nabla_{2}\cdot(Ae_{j}).

By Proposition
3.2
with
\tau=0
and
G=0
, for all
\ell\geq 0
, we have

(3.38)

\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{\ell}\ell!,

where
\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}=C_{*}\Lambda_{0}
.

Next, we show the estimate of
\widehat{A}
: for some
\widehat{C}_{0}
,

\|\nabla_{1}^{\ell}\widehat{A}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\widehat{C}_{0}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{\ell}\ell!.

Recall the equation for
\widehat{A}=(\widehat{a}_{ij}(y_{1}))
is given by the algebraic equation (
3.15
), i.e.,

\widehat{A}(y_{1})=\langle A+A\nabla_{2}\chi\rangle_{y_{2}}.

Applying
\nabla^{\ell}_{1}
to the equation and using the generalized Leibniz rule, we have

\nabla_{1}^{\ell}\widehat{A}(y_{1})=\langle\nabla^{\ell}_{1}A\rangle_{y_{2}}+\langle\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\textbf{Sym}(\nabla^{m}_{1}A\otimes\nabla^{\ell-m}_{1}\nabla_{2}\chi_{k})\rangle_{y_{2}}.

It follows from the triangle and Hölder’s inequalities that

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{\ell}_{1}\widehat{A}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla^{\ell}_{1}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\nabla^{m}_{1}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\nabla^{\ell-m}_{1}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\ell}\ell!+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{m}m!C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{\ell-m}(\ell-m)!

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\ell}\ell!+C_{0}C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{\ell}\ell!\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}(\Lambda_{0}/\widehat{\Lambda}_{0})^{m}

\displaystyle\leq(C_{0}+\frac{C_{0}C_{*}^{2}}{C_{*}-1})\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{\ell}\ell!,

where we have used the fact
\Lambda_{0}/\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}\leq C_{*}^{-1}
, the identity

\binom{\ell}{m}m!(\ell-m)!=\ell!,

and

\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}C_{*}^{-m}\leq\frac{C_{*}}{C_{*}-1}.

We rewrite the constant
C_{0}+\frac{C_{0}C_{*}^{2}}{C_{*}-1}
as
\widehat{C}_{0}
and obtain the desired estimate.

Step 2:
Estimate of
Y_{0}
: for some
C
and

, and any
\ell\geq 0
,

(3.39)

\|\nabla^{\ell}_{1}Y_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq C\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!.

Recall that
Y_{0}
is calculated through three equations (
3.16
), (
3.17
) and (
3.18
), also listed below:

(3.40)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{2}\cdot A\nabla_{2}\widetilde{F}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{2}\cdot F,

(3.41)

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(y_{1})

\displaystyle=\langle F+A\nabla_{2}\widetilde{F}\rangle_{y_{2}},

(3.42)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{1}\cdot\widehat{A}\nabla_{1}Y_{0}+\tau^{2}Y_{0}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{1}\cdot\widehat{F}.

We first estimate
\widetilde{F}
.
By Proposition
3.2
applied to (
3.40
), we have

(3.43)

\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\widetilde{F}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

where
\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}=\max\{\Lambda_{1},C_{*}\Lambda_{0}\}
.

Next, we estimate
\widehat{F}
using (
3.41
). It follows from the generalized Leibniz rule that for
\ell\geq 0
,

(3.44)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\widehat{F}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}F\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\widetilde{F}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{m}m!C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}^{\ell-m}(\ell-m+q)!

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!+C_{*}^{2}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}C_{*}^{-m}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+\frac{C_{*}^{3}}{C_{*}-1}\Big)\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}^{3}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

where we need
\Lambda\geq\max\{\widehat{\Lambda}_{1},C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}\}=\max\{\Lambda_{1},C_{*}^{2}\Lambda_{0}\}
, and we have used (
3.30
) and chosen
C_{*}\geq 3
in the last inequality, without loss of generality. Observe that
\widehat{F}
is independent of
y_{2}
and thus
|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\widehat{F}|=|\nabla_{1}^{\ell}\widehat{F}|
.

Now we are ready to estimate
Y_{0}
by applying Proposition
3.2
to the nondegenerate equation (
3.42
) with
n=1
and
A
replaced by
\widehat{A}
. The constant
C_{*}
from Proposition
3.2
should be updated (depending on
d,\lambda
and
\widehat{C}_{0}
), denoted by
\widehat{C}_{*}\geq C_{*}
. Define
\Lambda:=\max\{\widehat{\Lambda}_{1},\widehat{C}_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}\}=\max\{\Lambda_{1},\widehat{C}_{*}C_{*}\Lambda_{0}\}
. Let
C_{**}=\widehat{C}_{*}C_{*}
. Hence we now have

(3.45)

\Lambda/\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}\geq\widehat{C}_{*},\qquad\Lambda/\Lambda_{0}\geq C_{**}.

These two relations will be used frequently through the proof. With the above setting,
it follows from Proposition
3.2
applied to (
3.42
) and (
3.44
) that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{1}^{\ell}Y_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\widehat{C}_{*}C_{*}^{3}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!\leq C\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

where we choose
C\geq\widehat{C}_{*}C_{*}^{3}
.

Step 3:
Estimate of
Y_{1}
.

The procedure to compute
Y_{1}
from
Y_{0}
follows the order
Y_{0}\to Y_{1}^{o}\to\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\to Y_{1}^{r}
, using the equations (
3.19
), (
3.20
) and (
3.21
), also listed below:

(3.46)

\displaystyle Y_{1}^{o}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{F}+\chi\cdot\nabla_{1}Y_{0},

(3.47)

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\nabla_{2}\cdot A\nabla_{2}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}&=\nabla_{1}\cdot F+\nabla_{1}\cdot(A\nabla_{2}Y_{1}^{o})+\nabla_{2}\cdot(A\nabla_{1}Y_{1}^{o})\\
&\qquad\qquad+\nabla_{1}\cdot(A\nabla_{1}Y_{0})-\tau^{2}Y_{0},\end{split}

(3.48)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{1}\cdot\widehat{A}\nabla_{1}Y_{1}^{r}+\tau^{2}Y_{1}^{r}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{1}\cdot\big(\langle A\nabla_{2}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\rangle_{y_{2}}+\langle A\nabla_{1}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{2}}\big).

The first equation (
3.46
) is an algebraic equation, which could be handled directly by the Leibniz rule. Actually, it follows from (
3.43
), (
3.38
) and (
3.39
) that for
\ell\geq 0

(3.49)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\widetilde{F}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\textbf{Sym}(\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}\chi\otimes\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{1}Y_{0})\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\|\nabla^{\ell-m}_{1}Y_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{m}m!C\Lambda^{\ell-m}(\ell-m+q)!

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!+C_{*}C\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}/\Lambda)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{C_{*}}{C}+\frac{C_{*}\widehat{C}_{*}}{\widehat{C}_{*}-1}\Big)C\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

where in the last inequality, we need
C^{*}/C+C_{*}\widehat{C}_{*}/(\widehat{C}_{*}-1)\leq C
.

Now, we use the second equation (
3.47
) to estimate
\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}
by Proposition
3.2
. Let

F_{2}=A\nabla_{1}Y_{1}^{o},\quad\text{and}\quad G_{2}=\nabla_{1}\cdot F+\nabla_{1}\cdot(A\nabla_{2}Y_{1}^{o})+\nabla_{1}\cdot(A\nabla_{1}Y_{0})-\tau^{2}Y_{0}.

Then (
3.47
) can be written as

(3.50)

-\nabla_{2}\cdot A\nabla_{2}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}=\nabla_{2}\cdot F+G_{2}.

Recall that
\langle G_{2}\rangle_{y_{2}}=0
, due to (
3.42
).
It follows from the generalized Leibniz rule and (
3.49
) that

(3.51)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{2}F_{2}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}^{m}_{2}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{1}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\Lambda_{0}^{m}m!C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell-m+1}(\ell-m+1+q)!

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!,

where we have used
|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{1}Y_{1}^{o}|\leq|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m+1}Y_{1}^{o}|
which contributes to the increasing of factorial by 1 in (
3.51
).
Similarly,

(3.52)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}G_{2}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\nabla_{1}F\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\tau^{2}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{1}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}\nabla_{1}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}\nabla_{1}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}Y_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{1}Y_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+\tau^{2}+\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}\Big)C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!.

Then by Proposition
3.2
applied to (
3.50
) and the linearity of the equation, we have

(3.53)

\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C_{*}\Big(1+\tau^{2}+\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}\Big)C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!\leq C^{3}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!.

Here we choose
C\geq C_{*}(1+\tau^{2}+\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1})
.

Next, we consider the equation of
Y_{1}^{r}
. Let

F_{1}^{r}=\langle A\nabla_{2}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\rangle_{y_{2}}+\langle A\nabla_{1}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{2}}.

Then (
3.48
) is reduced to

(3.54)

-\nabla_{1}\cdot\widehat{A}\nabla_{1}Y_{1}^{r}+\tau^{2}Y_{1}^{r}=\nabla_{1}\cdot F_{1}^{r}.

We have by (
3.53
) and (
3.49
),

(3.55)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{1}^{\ell}F_{1}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\nabla_{1}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\nabla_{1}^{\ell-m}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\nabla_{1}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\nabla_{1}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{1}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}C^{3}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!.

By Proposition
3.2
applied to the equation (
3.54
), we have

\|\nabla_{1}^{\ell}Y_{1}^{r}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\widehat{C}_{*}\frac{2C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}C^{3}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!\leq\frac{1}{2}C^{4}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!,

where we need
C\geq 2\widehat{C}_{*}\frac{2C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}
. Here again the constant
\widehat{C}_{*}
might be larger than
C_{*}
as we apply Proposition
3.2
with
A
replaced by
\widehat{A}
.

Step 4:
Estimate of
Y_{k}
for
k\geq 2
.

From the recursive equations in Section
3.1
, we see that the estimates of
(Y_{k}^{o},\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o},Y_{k}^{r})
yield the estimates of
(Y_{k+1}^{o},\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o},Y_{k+1}^{r})
.
We have proved our base case for
k=1
as follows:

(3.56a)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

(3.56b)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C^{3}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!,

(3.56c)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{\ell}_{1}Y_{1}^{r}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}C^{4}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!.

Suppose now that for some
k\geq 1
and
\ell\geq 0
we have

(3.57a)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y_{k}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C^{3k-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k-1}(\ell+k-1+q)!,

(3.57b)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C^{3k}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!,

(3.57c)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{\ell}_{1}Y_{k}^{r}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}C^{3k+1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!.

We would like to show for all
\ell\geq 0
,

(3.58a)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y_{k+1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C^{3(k+1)-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!,

(3.58b)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C^{3(k+1)}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!,

(3.58c)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{\ell}_{1}Y_{k+1}^{r}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}C^{3(k+1)+1}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!.

The proof of (
3.58
) is very similar to the estimates in (
3.56
), based on the recursive equations (
3.22
), (
3.23
) and (
3.24
), restated as follows for
n=2
,

(3.59)

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{o}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}+\chi\cdot\nabla_{1}Y_{k}^{r},

(3.60)

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\nabla_{2}\cdot A\nabla_{2}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}&=\nabla_{1}\cdot(A\nabla_{2}Y_{k+1}^{o})+\nabla_{2}\cdot(A\nabla_{1}Y_{k+1}^{o})\\
&\qquad\qquad+\nabla_{1}\cdot(A\nabla_{1}Y_{k})-\tau^{2}Y_{k},\end{split}

(3.61)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{1}\cdot(\widehat{A}\nabla_{1}Y_{k+1}^{r})+\tau^{2}Y_{k+1}^{r}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{1}\cdot\langle A\nabla_{2}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}\rangle_{y_{2}}+\nabla_{1}\cdot\langle A\nabla_{1}Y_{k+1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{2}}.

We first use the algebraic equation (
3.59
) to estimate
Y_{k+1}^{o}
. In fact, by the inductive assumption (
3.57
), we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y_{k+1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}Y_{k}^{r}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{3k}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{m}m!\frac{1}{2}C^{3k+1}\Lambda^{\ell-m+k}(\ell-m+k+q)!

\displaystyle\leq C^{3k+2}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!

\displaystyle=C^{3(k+1)-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!.

This proves the first inequality of (
3.58a
).

The estimate of
\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}
is similar to
\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}
. Let

F_{k+2}=A\nabla_{1}Y_{k+1}^{o}\qquad\text{and}\qquad G_{k+2}=\nabla_{1}\cdot(A\nabla_{2}Y_{k+1}^{o})+\nabla_{1}\cdot(A\nabla_{1}Y_{k})-\tau^{2}Y_{k}.

Note that by the triangle inequality and the inductive assumption (
3.57
),

(3.62)

\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y_{k}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}+\|\nabla_{1}^{\ell}Y_{k}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq C^{3k+1}\Lambda^{\ell-1+k}(\ell-1+k+q)!,

for
\ell\geq 1
(the estimate for
\ell=0
is the same as
\ell=1
by the Poincaré inequality),
and for all
\ell\geq 0
,

\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\nabla_{1}Y_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell+1}Y_{k}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}+\|\nabla_{1}^{\ell}Y_{k}^{r}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq C^{3k+1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!.

Thus, by these estimates, (
3.58a
), and a familiar calculation as in (
3.51
) and (
3.52
), we have

\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}F_{k+2}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\frac{C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}C^{3(k+1)-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!,

and

\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}G_{k+2}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\big(\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}+\tau^{2}\big)C^{3(k+1)-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!.

Then applying the Proposition
3.2
to the equation

-\nabla_{2}\cdot A\nabla_{2}\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}=\nabla_{2}\cdot F_{k+2}+G_{k+2},

we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}&\leq C_{*}\big(\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}+\tau^{2}\big)C^{3(k+1)-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!\\
&\leq C^{3(k+1)}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!,\end{split}

where we have assumed
C\geq C_{*}\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}+\tau^{2}
previously. This proves (
3.58b
).

Finally, we estimate
Y_{k+1}^{r}
. Let

F_{k+1}^{r}=\langle A\nabla_{2}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}\rangle_{y}+\langle A\nabla_{1}Y_{k+1}^{o}\rangle_{y}.

By a familiar calculation as in (
3.55
),

\|\nabla_{1}^{\ell}F_{k+1}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\frac{2C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}C^{3(k+1)}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!.

Then by Proposition
3.2
applied to (
3.61
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\nabla_{1}^{\ell}Y_{k+1}^{r}\|_{H^{1}_{\mathbf{x}}}&\leq\frac{2C_{0}\widehat{C}_{*}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}C^{3(k+1)}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}C^{3(k+1)+1}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!,\end{split}

where we require
C\geq\frac{4C_{0}\widehat{C}_{*}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}
. Note that this requirement (as well as the previous several requirements on
C
) is independent of
k
and thus
C
can be fixed globally.

Step 5:
Complete the proof.

Consider the partial sum of
Y_{k}
truncated at
j=k
(with a modification):

N_{k}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\delta_{2}^{j}Y_{j}(x,y)+\delta_{2}^{k}Y_{k}^{o}.

Then for
\ell\geq 0
, by the estimate of
Y_{k}
in (
3.62
) and the estimate of
Y_{k}^{o}
in (
3.57a
),

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{2}N_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\delta_{2}^{j}C^{3j+1}\Lambda^{\ell+j}(\ell+j+q)!+\delta_{2}^{k}C^{3k-1}\Lambda^{\ell-1+k}(\ell-1+k+q)!

\displaystyle\leq C\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

provided that
\delta_{2}C^{3}\Lambda(\ell+k+q)\leq 1/e
.

Now by (
3.4
), we see that
N_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle-\widehat{\nabla}_{2}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{2}N_{k}+\tau^{2}N_{k}-\widehat{\nabla}_{2}\cdot F

\displaystyle=E_{k}:=\delta^{k-1}_{2}\big((\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xy}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yx}})Y_{k}^{o}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{k-1}+\tau^{2}Y_{k-1})+\delta_{2}^{k}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{xx}}Y_{k}^{o}+\tau^{2}Y_{k}^{o}).

By a simple observation, we have

E_{k}=-\delta_{2}^{k}\widehat{\nabla}_{2}\cdot A\nabla_{1}Y_{k}^{o}-\delta_{2}^{k-1}(\nabla_{1}\cdot A\nabla_{2}Y_{k}^{o}+\nabla_{1}\cdot A\nabla_{1}Y_{k-1})+\tau^{2}(\delta_{2}^{k}Y_{k}^{o}+\delta_{2}^{k-1}Y_{k-1}).

Let
P_{k}=A\nabla_{1}Y_{k}^{o}
and
Q_{k}=\nabla_{1}\cdot A\nabla_{2}Y_{k}^{o}+\nabla_{1}\cdot A\nabla_{1}Y_{k-1}-\tau^{2}(\delta_{2}Y_{k}^{o}+Y_{k-1})
. Then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}P_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{1}Y_{k}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{m}m!C^{3k-1}\Lambda^{\ell-m+k}(\ell-m+k+q)!

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}C^{3k-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!,

and

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Q_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\Big(\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}\nabla_{1}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{2}Y_{k}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{1}\nabla_{2}Y_{k}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\Big(\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}\nabla_{1}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{1}Y_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell-m}\nabla_{1}^{2}Y_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\Big)

\displaystyle+\tau^{2}\delta_{2}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y_{k}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\tau^{2}\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Y_{k-1}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\Big(C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{m+1}(m+1)!C^{3k-1}\Lambda^{\ell-m-1+k}(\ell-m-1+k+q)!\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\Big(C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{m}m!C^{3k-1}\Lambda^{\ell-m+k}(\ell-m+k+q)!\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\Big(C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{m+1}(m+1)!C^{3k-2}\Lambda^{\ell-m-1+k}(\ell-m-1+k+q)!\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\Big(C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{m}m!C^{3k-2}\Lambda^{\ell-m+k}(\ell-m+k+q)!\Big)

\displaystyle+2\tau^{2}C^{3k-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k-1}(\ell+k-1+q)!

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}+2\tau^{2}\Big)C^{3k-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!.

Now let
Z_{k}
be the solution of

-\widehat{\nabla}_{2}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{2}Z_{k}+\tau^{2}Z_{k}=-E_{k}=\delta_{2}^{k}\widehat{\nabla}_{2}\cdot P_{k}+\delta_{2}^{k-1}Q_{k}.

Using the linearity of the equation and Proposition
3.1
, we obtain

\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Z_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{2}^{k}C^{3k}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{2}^{k-1}\tau^{-1}C^{3k}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!,

where we need to assume
C\geq 2C_{*}(\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}+2\tau^{2})
.
Thus, if
\delta_{2}C^{3}\Lambda(\ell+k+q)\leq 1/e
, we have

\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Z_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\tau^{-2}e^{-k+1}C^{3}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!.

Choose
\tau
such that
\tau^{-2}e^{-k+1}C^{3}\Lambda\leq 1
. Then we have

\|\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\ell}Z_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+1+q)!.

Since
Y=N_{k}+Z_{k}
solves the original equation, from the estimates of
N_{k}
and
Z_{k}
, it follows that

\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{2}Y\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq(C+2)\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+1+q)!.

The assumptions we need are
\delta_{2}C^{3}\Lambda(\ell+k+q)\leq 1/e
and
\tau^{2}\geq e^{-k+1}C^{3}\Lambda.
This completes the proof.
∎

3.4
Uniform estimates for general
n
scales

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Assume that (
1.5
), (
1.6
), (
3.27
) and (
3.28
) hold. Let
Y
be the solution of

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Y+\tau^{2}Y=\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\cdot F\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{d\times n}.

There exist
\widetilde{C}_{j}
,
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j},C_{n},\Lambda_{n}
such that if for some
\ell\geq 0
and any
2\leq j\leq n
, there exists
k_{j}\geq 1
such that

(3.63)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\delta_{j}/\delta_{j-1})\widetilde{C}_{j}^{3}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j}(\ell+k_{j}+q)\leq e^{-1},\\
&\tau^{2}\geq\widetilde{C}_{j}^{3}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j}\delta_{j-1}^{-1}e^{-k_{j}+1},\end{aligned}\right.

then
Y
satisfies

(3.64)

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq C_{n}\Lambda_{n}^{\ell}(\ell+1+q)!.

The constants
\widetilde{C}_{j},C_{n}
depend on
d,\lambda,j,n,C_{0}
, while
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j},\Lambda_{n}
depend additionally on
\Lambda_{0}
and
\Lambda_{1}
.

Remark 3.7
.

Indeed, if (
3.63
) holds for some
\ell=\ell_{0}
, then it holds for all
0\leq\ell<\ell_{0}
.

Proof.

We have proved the case of two scales in the previous subsection. Now we assume that the result holds for
n-1
scales and prove the case of
n
scales by an inductive argument. Precisely, we assume that
V
is the solution of

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}V+\tau^{2}V=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot F,

for
\widehat{A}
and
F
being 1-periodic of
n-1
scales. Moreover,
F
satisfies the condition (
3.28
) and
\widehat{A}
(with the same ellipticity constant
\lambda
) satisfies a similar condition as (
3.27
) with
C_{0}
and
\Lambda_{0}
replaced by different constants
\widehat{C}_{0}
and
\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}
, which will be given shortly.
Then there exist
\Lambda_{n-1},C_{n-1}
and
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j},\widetilde{C}_{j}
with
2\leq j\leq n-1
such that if for each
2\leq j\leq n-1
, there exists
k_{j}\geq 1
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\delta_{j}/\delta_{j-1})\widetilde{C}_{j}^{3}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j}(\ell+k_{j}+q)\leq e^{-1},\\
&\tau^{2}\geq\widetilde{C}_{j}^{3}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j}\delta_{j-1}^{-1}e^{-k_{j}+1},\end{aligned}\right.

then

\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}V\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq C_{n-1}\Lambda_{n-1}^{\ell}(\ell+1+q)!,

where
\widetilde{C}_{j}
and
C_{n-1}
are constants depending only on
d,\lambda,\widehat{C}_{0},j
and
n
, and
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{j}
and
\Lambda_{n-1}
depend additionally on
\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}
and
\Lambda_{1}
. This inductive assumption will not be used until Step 5. Actually we only need the energy estimates in Proposition
3.1
and
3.2
from Step 1 to Step 4.

Recall the formal multiscale ansatz
Y=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta_{n}^{j}Y_{j}
in Section
3.1
and we need to solve
Y_{k}
recursively by (
3.14
) - (
3.24
). The following proof largely follows the same line as Theorem
3.4
in the case of two scales.

Step 1.
Estimate
\chi
and
\widehat{A}
. The equations for
\chi
and
\widehat{A}
are given by

\displaystyle-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}\chi_{j}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{n}\cdot(Ae_{j}),

\displaystyle\widehat{A}(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\langle A+A\nabla_{n}\chi\rangle_{y_{n}}.

Applying the similar argument as in Step 1 of Theorem
3.4
,
we have

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{\ell}\ell!,

and

(3.65)

\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}\widehat{A}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\widehat{C}_{0}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{\ell}\ell!.

Here
\widehat{C}_{0}
is a constant generally larger than
C_{0}
,
\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}=C_{*}\Lambda_{0}
and
C_{*}
is a constant depending only on
C_{0},d
and
\lambda
arising from Proposition
3.2
.
Moreover, by the classical homogenization theory, the ellipticity constant is preserved from
A
to its homogenized matrix
\widehat{A}
, namely,

\lambda|\xi|^{2}\leq\xi\cdot\widehat{A}\xi,\qquad\text{for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Step 2.
Estimate of
Y_{0}
. The involved equations are the following:

(3.66)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{yy}}\widetilde{F}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{n}\cdot F,

(3.67)

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\langle F+A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{F}\rangle_{y_{n}},

(3.68)

\displaystyle-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}+\tau^{2}Y_{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{F}.

By the similar argument as in Step 2 of Theorem
3.4
,
we have for
\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}=\max\{\Lambda_{1},C_{*}\Lambda_{0}\}
,

(3.69)

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widetilde{F}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

and

\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}\widehat{F}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq C_{*}^{3}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

where we need
\Lambda\geq\max\{\widehat{\Lambda}_{1},C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}\}=\max\{\Lambda_{1},C_{*}^{2}\Lambda_{0}\}
.

Now we use the energy estimate in Proposition
3.1
to (
3.68
) to obtain for all
\ell\geq 0
,

(3.70)

\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n-1}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}Y_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\widehat{C}_{*}C_{*}^{3}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!\leq C\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

where we set
\Lambda=\max\{\widehat{\Lambda}_{1},\widehat{C}_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}\}=\max\{\Lambda_{1},\widehat{C}_{*}C_{*}\Lambda_{0}\}
and we assume
C\geq\widehat{C}_{*}C_{*}^{3}
.
Now as before, we define

C_{**}=\widehat{C}_{*}C_{*}
. Recall that
\widehat{C}_{*}
is the constant from the energy estimates in Proposition
3.1
and Proposition
3.2
with
A
replaced by
\widehat{A}
. Moreover, we also have (
3.45
).

Step 3.

Estimate
Y_{1}
.
The involved equations are the following:

(3.71)

\displaystyle Y_{1}^{o}=\widetilde{F}+\chi\cdot\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0},

(3.72)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot F+(\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot A\nabla_{n}+\nabla_{n}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1})Y_{1}^{o}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}-\tau^{2}Y_{0},\end{split}

(3.73)

\displaystyle-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{r}+\tau^{2}Y_{1}^{r}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\big(\langle A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}+\langle A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}\big).

By (
3.69
), (
3.70
), the generalized Leibniz rule and Hölder’s inequality, we obtain from (
3.71
) that

(3.74)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widetilde{F}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{m}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell-m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{m}m!C\Lambda^{\ell-m}(\ell-m+q)!

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!+C_{*}C\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}/\Lambda)^{m}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{C_{*}}{C}+\frac{C_{*}^{2}}{C_{*}-1}\Big)C\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

where we need
C
large enough such that
C_{*}/C+C_{*}^{2}/(C_{*}-1)\leq C
.

Next, we estimate
\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}
from (
3.72
). Let

F_{2}=A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o},\quad\text{and}\quad G_{2}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot F+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(A\nabla_{n}Y_{1}^{o})+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0})-\tau^{2}Y_{0}.

Then
\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}
satisfies

-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}=\nabla_{n}\cdot F_{2}+G_{2}.

It follows from the generalized Leibniz rule and (
3.74
) that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}F_{2}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell-m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\Lambda_{0}^{m}m!\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell-m+1}(\ell-m+1+q)!

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!,

where we have used
\Lambda/\Lambda_{0}\geq C_{**}
and the simple fact (also see (
2.3
))

(3.75)

|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}f|\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-1}|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell+1}f|.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}G_{2}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}F\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\tau^{2}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell-m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell-m}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell-m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell-m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}^{2}Y_{0}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1+\tau+\frac{4C_{0}C_{*}^{2}}{C_{*}^{2}-1}\Big)\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!.

Then by Proposition
3.2
and the linearity of the equation, we have

(3.76)

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C_{*}\Big(1+\tau+\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}\Big)\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{3}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!.

Here we choose
C\geq C_{*}(1+\tau+\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1})
.

Next, we estimate
Y_{1}^{r}
. Let

F_{1}^{r}=\langle A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}+\langle A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}.

Then (
3.73
) is reduced to

(3.77)

-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{r}+\tau^{2}Y_{1}^{r}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot F_{1}^{r}.

By the generalized Leibniz rule, (
3.76
) and (
3.74
), we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}F_{1}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell-m}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{m}A\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell-m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{3}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!.

By Proposition
3.1
applied to (
3.77
), we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}Y^{r}_{1}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{C}_{*}\frac{2C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{3}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{4}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!,

where we need
C\geq 2\widehat{C}_{*}\frac{2C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}
.

Step 4:
Estimate of
Y_{k}
for
k\geq 2
. From the recursive equations, we see that the estimates of
(Y_{k}^{o},\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o},Y_{k}^{r})
yields the estimates of
(Y_{k+1}^{o},\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o},Y_{k+1}^{r})
.
We have proved our base case for
k=1
as follows:

(3.78a)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y_{1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C^{2}\Lambda^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

(3.78b)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{3}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!,

(3.78c)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{1}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}Y^{r}_{1}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{4}\Lambda^{\ell+1}(\ell+1+q)!.

Suppose now that for some
k\geq 1
and all
\ell\geq 0
we have

(3.79a)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y_{k}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k+1}C^{3k-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k-1}(\ell+k-1+q)!,

(3.79b)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{3k}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!,

(3.79c)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}Y^{r}_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{3k+1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!.

We would like to show that for all
\ell\geq 0
,

(3.80)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y_{k+1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{3(k+1)-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!,\\
&\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k-1}C^{3(k+1)}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!,\\
&\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k+1}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}Y^{r}_{k+1}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n-1}^{-k-1}C^{3(k+1)+1}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

The proof of (
3.80
) is very similar to the estimates of (
3.78
), based on the following recursive equations

(3.81)

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{o}=\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}+\chi\cdot\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k}^{r},

(3.82)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(A\nabla_{n}Y_{k+1}^{o})+\nabla_{n}\cdot(A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k+1}^{o})\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k})-\tau^{2}Y_{k},\end{split}

(3.83)

\displaystyle-\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(\widehat{A}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k+1}^{r})+\tau^{2}Y_{k+1}^{r}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}\rangle_{y}+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\langle A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k+1}^{o}\rangle_{y}.

We first use the algebraic equation (
3.81
) to estimate
Y_{k+1}^{o}
. In fact, by the inductive assumption (
3.79
) and the generalized Leibniz rule, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y_{k+1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{k+1}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{m}\chi\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell-m}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{3k}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{m}C_{*}\widehat{\Lambda}_{0}^{m}m!\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{3k+1}\Lambda^{\ell-m+k}(\ell-m+k+q)!

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{-k}_{n-1}C^{3k+2}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!

\displaystyle=\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{3(k+1)-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!.

This proves the first inequality of (
3.80
).

The estimate of
\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}
is similar to
\widetilde{Y}_{2}^{o}
. Let

F_{k+2}=A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k+1}^{o}\qquad\text{and}\qquad G_{k+2}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(A\nabla_{n}Y_{k+1}^{o})+\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot(A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k})-\tau^{2}Y_{k}.

Then
\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}
satisfies

(3.84)

-\nabla_{n}\cdot A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}=\nabla_{n}\cdot F_{k+2}+G_{k+2}.

Note that by the triangle inequality to
Y_{k}=Y_{k}^{o}+Y_{k}^{r}
and (
3.75
), we have

(3.85)

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{3k+1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!.

Moreover, for
0<\tau\leq 1
,

(3.86)

\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y_{k}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}Y_{k}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{xy}}}\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{3k+1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!.

Thus, by a familiar calculation, we have

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}F_{k+2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}\delta_{n-1}^{-k-1}C^{3(k+1)-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k}(\ell+k+q)!,

and

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}G_{k+2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\big(\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}+\tau\big)\delta_{n-1}^{-k-1}C^{3(k+1)-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!.

Then applying Proposition
3.2
to the equation (
3.84
),
we get

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{1}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{*}\big(\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}+\tau\big)\delta_{n-1}^{-k-1}C^{3(k+1)-1}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k-1}C^{3(k+1)}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!,

where we have assumed
C\geq C_{*}(\frac{4C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}+\tau)
previously. This proves the second inequality in (
3.80
).

Finally, we estimate
Y_{k+1}^{r}
. Let

F_{k+1}=\langle A\nabla_{n}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}+\langle A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{k+1}^{o}\rangle_{y_{n}}.

By a familiar calculation, we have

\|\mathbf{D}_{n-1}^{\ell}F_{k+1}^{r}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}}\leq\frac{2C_{0}C_{**}}{C_{**}-1}\delta_{n-1}^{-k-1}C^{3(k+1)}\Lambda^{\ell+k+1}(\ell+k+1+q)!.

Then by Proposition
3.2
applied to

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\delta}(y)=\mathcal{U}(y/\delta_{1},y/\delta_{2},\cdots,y/\delta_{n}),

where
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n})
is 1-periodic in each
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Therefore, the approximate equation (
4.1
) can be lifted to be a degenerate equation

(4.2)

\displaystyle-\widehat{\Delta}_{n}\mathcal{U}+\tau^{2}\mathcal{U}=\overline{A}-A-A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{X}\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d\times n},

where
\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}
. Note that (
4.2
) is degenerate. If we require
\mathcal{U}
to be uniformly bounded in
\tau
, it must hold
\langle\overline{A}-A-A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{X}\rangle=0
, i.e.,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\overline{A}=\langle A+A\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{X}\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
is the average over
\mathbb{T}^{d\times n}
given in (
3.5
).

The constant matrix
\overline{A}
will be called the effective matrix, which is more accurate than the classical homogenized matrix, denoted by
A_{0}
, derived by reiterated homogenization; see e.g.
[
26
]
for the derivation of
A_{0}
. We emphasize that
\overline{A}
depends on
\tau
and
\delta_{i}
’s, while
A_{0}
is independent of
\tau
and
\delta_{i}
. They have the following relation.

Proposition 4.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.8
, we have

(4.4)

\displaystyle|A_{0}-\overline{A}|\leq C(\tau^{2}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{3}\delta_{2}^{-1}+\cdots+\delta_{n}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}).

In particular,

(4.5)

\displaystyle A_{0}=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\delta_{2}\rightarrow 0}\cdots\lim_{\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0}\overline{A}.

Proof.

First of all, due to Theorem
3.8
, the solution
\mathcal{X}
to equation (
3.94
) exists during taking limits in (
4.5
), and thereby the limit in (
4.5
) makes sense.

Recall that in the process of reiterated homogenization, we homogenize first
y_{n}
to get an interim matrix
A_{n-1}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})
, and next homogenize
y_{n-1}
to get
A_{n-2}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-2})
, until every scale is homogenized successively. The last step is to homogenize
y_{1}
from
A_{1}(y_{1})
to obtain the constant matrix
A_{0}
. Now we introduce a new matrix based on
A_{1}
. Set

\displaystyle-\nabla_{1}\cdot A_{1}\nabla_{1}\psi_{\tau}+\tau^{2}\psi_{\tau}=\nabla_{1}\cdot A_{1}\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{d},

and define

\displaystyle A_{\tau}=\langle A_{1}+A_{1}\nabla_{1}\psi_{\tau}\rangle.

We claim that under the assumptions of Theorem
3.8

(4.6)

\displaystyle|A_{\tau}-\overline{A}|\leq C(\delta_{2}+\delta_{3}\delta_{2}^{-1}+\cdots+\delta_{n}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}).

As a byproduct, (
4.6
) implies that

\displaystyle A_{\tau}=\lim_{\delta_{2}\rightarrow 0}\cdots\lim_{\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0}\overline{A}.

We will prove the claim by induction.

In the case
n=2
, according to the expansion for
Y=\mathcal{X}
in Theorem
3.4
, it holds

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}_{2}\mathcal{X}=\nabla_{1}Y_{0}+\nabla_{2}Y_{1}^{o}+Z\quad\text{with }\|Z\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\delta_{2},

where
Y_{0}
and
Y_{1}^{o}
are given in Theorem
3.4
with
F=A
. Especially,
Y_{0}
is identically
\psi_{\tau}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle|\overline{A}-\langle A+A\nabla_{1}Y_{0}+A\nabla_{2}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle|\leq\|Z\|_{L^{2}}=C\delta_{2}.

Noticing that

\displaystyle\langle A+A\nabla_{1}Y_{0}+A\nabla_{2}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle=\langle A_{1}+A_{1}\nabla_{1}Y_{0}\rangle_{y_{1}}=A_{\tau},

we conclude

|A_{\tau}-\overline{A}|\leq C\delta_{2}.

Assume now the claim is true for the case of
n-1
scales. For the general case of
n
scales, by the procedure of reiterated homogenization, we have

\displaystyle A_{n-1}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})=\langle A+A\nabla_{n}\chi\rangle_{y_{n}},

which coincides with
\widehat{A}
in (
3.15
). Note that
Y_{0}
given in Theorem
3.6
is exactly the corresponding multiscale corrector for
\widehat{A}
. By the inductive assumption, this leads to

(4.7)

\displaystyle|A_{\tau}-\langle A_{n-1}+A_{n-1}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}\rangle|\leq C(\delta_{2}+\cdots+\delta_{n-1}\delta_{n-2}^{-1}),

and

\displaystyle A_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta_{2}\rightarrow 0}\cdots\lim_{\delta_{n-1}\rightarrow 0}\langle A_{n-1}+A_{n-1}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta_{2}\rightarrow 0}\cdots\lim_{\delta_{n-1}\rightarrow 0}\langle A+A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}+A\nabla_{n}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle,

under the assumptions of Theorem
3.8
. On the other hand, taking into account the expansion for
Y=\mathcal{X}
given in Theorem
3.6

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\delta_{n}^{j}Y_{j}+\delta_{n}^{k}Y_{k}^{o}+Z_{k},

we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{X}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}+\nabla_{n}Y_{1}^{o}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\delta_{n}^{j}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{j}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}\delta_{n}^{j-1}\nabla_{n}Y_{j}^{o}+\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Z_{k},

where
Z_{k}
is given by (
3.93
). Therefore,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{X}=\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}+\nabla_{n}Y_{1}^{o}+Z,

and
Z
satisfies

\displaystyle\|Z\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\delta_{n}^{j}\delta_{n-1}^{-j}C^{3j+1}\Lambda^{j}j!+\sum_{j=2}^{k}\delta_{n}^{j-1}\delta_{n-1}^{-j+1}C^{3j-1}\Lambda^{j-1}(j-1)!

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n}^{k}\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{3k}\Lambda^{k}k!+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{n}^{k-1}\delta_{n-1}^{-k}\tau^{-1}C^{3k}\Lambda^{k}k!

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{n}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}[2C^{5}\Lambda+\tau^{-1}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}e^{-k+2}C^{6}\Lambda^{2}],

provided that
\delta_{n}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{3}\Lambda k\leq 1/e
. Here the estimates for
Y_{j}
and
\widehat{\nabla}_{n}Z_{k}
have been used. Further, if
\tau^{-1}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}e^{-k+2}C\Lambda\leq 1
, it holds

(4.9)

\displaystyle\|Z\|_{L^{2}}\leq 3C^{5}\Lambda\delta_{n}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}.

Hence, taking (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) into (
4.3
), we have

\displaystyle|\overline{A}-\langle A+A\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}Y_{0}+A\nabla_{n}Y_{1}^{o}\rangle|=C\delta_{n}\delta_{n-1}^{-1},

which, together with (
4.7
), implies (
4.6
). Note that the conditions

\displaystyle\delta_{n}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}C^{3}\Lambda k\leq 1/e\quad\text{and}\quad\tau^{-1}\delta_{n-1}^{-1}e^{-k+2}C\Lambda\leq 1

are fulfilled under the assumptions of Theorem
3.8
.

To end the proof of the proposition, it remains to show

\displaystyle|A_{0}-A_{\tau}|\leq C\tau^{2}.

We introduce the exact corrector
\psi_{0}
for
A_{1}
by

\displaystyle-\nabla_{1}\cdot A_{1}\nabla_{1}\psi_{0}=\nabla_{1}\cdot A_{1}\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{d}

and get

\displaystyle A_{0}=\langle A_{1}+A_{1}\nabla_{1}\psi_{0}\rangle_{y_{1}}.

Comparing the equations of
\psi_{\tau}
and
\psi_{0}
, we deduce from the energy estimate that

\displaystyle|A_{0}-A_{\tau}|\leq\|\nabla_{1}\psi_{\tau}-\nabla_{1}\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\tau^{2},

where
C
depends only on the characters of
A
. This combined with (
4.6
) completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Recall that
\delta_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{1}
and
\tau^{2}\simeq\max_{j}\{e^{-c\varepsilon_{j-1}/\varepsilon_{j}}\}
in our application to
A_{\varepsilon}(x)=A(x/\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,x/\varepsilon_{n})
. Thus (
4.4
) yields

|A_{0}-\overline{A}|\leq C(\max_{j}\{e^{-c\varepsilon_{j-1}/\varepsilon_{j}}\}+\max_{j}\{\varepsilon_{j}/\varepsilon_{j-1}\}).

This perfectly explains the difference between the errors in (
1.8
) and (
1.3
).

4.2
Multiscale ansatz

We consider the following general equation

(4.10)

-\widehat{\Delta}_{n}U+\tau^{2}U=G\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{T}^{d\times n},

where
G=G(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})
is
1
-periodic in each
y_{i}
with
\langle G\rangle=0
, and satisfies (
3.28
). By a regularization argument, this degenerate equation admits a unique periodic solution
U
with
\langle U\rangle=0
.

To use the structure of equation (
4.10
), we apply the ansatz

\displaystyle U(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta_{n}^{j}U_{j}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}).

Then taking the ansatz of
U
into equation (
4.10
), using

\widehat{\Delta}_{n}=\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}+\delta_{n}^{-1}2\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\nabla_{n}+\delta_{n}^{-2}\Delta_{n}

and comparing the order of
\delta_{n}
, we have the following recursive system for
U_{j}
,

(4.11a)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}U_{0}=0,

(4.11b)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}U_{1}-2\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\nabla_{n}U_{0}=0,

(4.11c)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}U_{2}-2\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\nabla_{n}U_{1}-\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{0}+\tau^{2}U_{0}=G,

(4.11d)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}U_{k+2}-2\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\nabla_{n}U_{k+1}-\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{k}+\tau^{2}U_{k}=0,\quad k\geq 1.

Now we solve (
4.11
) one by one. By (
4.11a
),
U_{0}
is independent of
y_{n}
and therefore
U_{0}=U_{0}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})
. Taking average in
y_{n}
to (
4.11c
), we get

\displaystyle-\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{0}+\tau^{2}U_{0}=\langle G\rangle_{y_{n}}.

This equation has the same structure as (
4.10
) with one less scale and suggests that we need to solve it inductively.

Furthermore, since
U_{0}
is independent of
y_{n}
, (
4.11b
) becomes

\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}U_{1}=0,

which means
U_{1}
is also independent of
y_{n}
and
U_{1}=U_{1}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})
. By taking the average in
y_{n}
to the equation (
4.11d
) with
k=1
, we have

\displaystyle-\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{1}+\tau^{2}U_{1}=0,

which implies that
U_{1}=0
by a simple integration by parts (or energy estimate).

Similarly, for general
k\geq 2
, by taking average in
y_{n}
to the last equation in (
4.11
), we get

-\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{k}^{r}+\tau^{2}U_{k}^{r}=0,

where
U_{k}^{r}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1})=\langle U_{k}\rangle_{y_{n}}
. The last equation only has a trivial solution, which means that
U_{k}
has no regular component and
\langle U_{k}\rangle_{y_{n}}=0
for each
k\geq 2
. Then we can solve for
U_{k}
with
k\geq 2
directly from (
4.11c
) and (
4.11d
). In fact, from (
4.11c
), we deduce

(4.12)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}U_{2}=\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{0}-\tau^{2}U_{0}+G=G-\langle G\rangle_{y_{n}}.

Obviously, the right-hand side has mean value
0
with respect to
y_{n}
. Therefore, (
4.12
) admits a unique solution in
y_{n}
with mean value
0
.
Moreover, for
k\geq 1
, if
U_{k+1}
and
U_{k}
have been solved, one obtains from (
4.11d
)

-\Delta_{n}U_{k+2}=2\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\nabla_{n}U_{k+1}+\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{k}-\tau^{2}U_{k}.

Note that this equation admits a unique solution in
y_{n}
with mean value
0
, since
\langle U_{k}\rangle_{y_{n}}=0
. In this way,
U_{k}
can be derived recursively.

We summarize the recursive equations to find all
U_{k}
with
\langle U_{k}\rangle_{y_{n}}=0
:

(4.13)

\displaystyle-\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{0}+\tau^{2}U_{0}

\displaystyle=\langle G\rangle_{y_{n}},

(4.14)

\displaystyle U_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.15)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}U_{2}

\displaystyle=G-\langle G\rangle_{y_{n}},

(4.16)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}U_{k+2}

\displaystyle=2\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\nabla_{n}U_{k+1}+\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{k}-\tau^{2}U_{k},\quad\text{for }k\geq 1.

Note that only the equation (
4.13
) for
U_{0}
needs to be solved by an inductive argument.

4.3
Energy estimates

The solution
U
to the equation (
4.10
) satisfies the following energy estimate.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
G
satisfy (
3.28
) and
\langle G\rangle=0
. Then there exists a constant
C_{d}
, depending only on
d
, such that for
\ell\geq 0
, the solution
U
to equation (
4.10
) satisfies

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}^{2}U\|_{L^{2}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}U\|_{L^{2}}+\tau^{2}\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}U\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{d}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!.

Proof.

The estimate for
\ell=0
follows directly by applying the test functions
-\widehat{\Delta}_{n}U
and
U
to (
4.10
). Alternatively, one can also take
L^{2}
norm of the both sides of (
4.10
) and apply the integration by parts to get

\|\widehat{\nabla}_{n}^{2}U\|_{L^{2}}+\tau\|\widehat{\nabla}_{n}U\|_{L^{2}}+\tau^{2}\|U\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{d}\|G\|_{L^{2}}.

For general
\ell>0
, applying
\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}
to (
4.10
) and the last inequality to the corresponding tensor-valued equation, we have

\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}^{2}U\|_{L^{2}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}U\|_{L^{2}}+\tau^{2}\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}U\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{d}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}G\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{d}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!.

This is the desired estimate.
∎

By applying this proposition to equation (
4.2
) and using (
3.97
) in Theorem
3.8
, we have for all
\ell\geq 0

(4.17)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}^{2}\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}}+\tau^{2}\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{d}\Big(C_{0}+\frac{C_{0}C_{*}^{2}}{C_{*}-1}\Big)\Lambda^{\ell}\ell!,

with
\Lambda=C_{*}\Lambda_{0}
.
However, this estimate is insufficient to achieve the desired convergence rate. To proceed, we need an estimate of
\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}}
uniform in
\tau
.
The following standard
H^{2}
estimate plays an essential role in establishing this uniform bound.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
G
satisfy (
3.28
) and
\langle G\rangle_{y_{n}}=0
. Let
U
be the periodic solution of

\displaystyle-\Delta_{n}U=G\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{T}^{d}

with
\langle U\rangle_{y_{n}}=0
. Then there exists
C_{d}
, depending only on
d
, such that for all
\ell\geq 0
,

(4.18)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}U\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq C_{d}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!.

Furthermore, if instead of (
3.28
),
G
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}G\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}L^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

then the norm of
U
in (
4.18
) can also be replaced by
\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}U\|_{L^{\infty}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}
.

4.4
Uniform estimates

To obtain refined control for
U
, we establish the following estimates of
U_{k}
based on the recursive equations in Section
4.2
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose that
\langle G\rangle=0
and
G
satisfies (
3.28
). Then there exists a constant
C
, depending only on
d
, such that for
\ell\geq 0

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}^{2}U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+\tau^{2}\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

and for
k\geq 2

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}U_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k+2}C^{k-1}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell+k-2}(\ell+k-2+q)!.

Proof.

Firstly, by applying Proposition
4.3
in
n-1
scales to the equation (
4.13
), we obtain directly for
\ell\geq 0

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}^{2}U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+\tau\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}+\tau^{2}\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{d}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!.

Setting
G_{2}=G-\langle G\rangle_{y_{n}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}G_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!.

By Proposition
4.4
applied to equation (
4.15
), this yields

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}U_{2}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq 2C_{d}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!\leq C\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

where we choose
C\geq 2C_{d}
.

Next we establish by induction that for all
k\geq 1,\ell\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}U_{k+2}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{(k+2)-1}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell+(k+2)-2}(\ell+(k+2)-2+q)!.

Suppose now the estimates of
U_{j}
have been known for
1\leq j\leq k+1
. Let
G_{k+2}=2\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\nabla_{n}U_{k+1}+\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{k}-\tau^{2}U_{k}
. Then by the inductive assumption,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}G_{k+2}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta_{n-1}^{-1}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell+1}U_{k+1}\|_{L_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}H_{\mathbf{y}}^{1}}+\delta_{n-1}^{-2}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell+2}U_{k}\|_{L^{2}}+\tau^{2}\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}U\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta_{n-1}^{-1}\delta_{n-1}^{k-1}C^{(k+1)-1}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell+1+(k+1)-2}(\ell+1+(k+1)-2+q)!

\displaystyle+\delta_{n-1}^{-2}\delta_{n-1}^{k-2}C^{k-1}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell+2+k-2}(\ell+2+k-2+q)!

\displaystyle+\tau^{2}\delta_{n-1}^{k-2}C^{k-1}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell+k-2}(\ell+k-2+q)!

\displaystyle\leq(3+\tau^{2})\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{k}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell+(k+2)-2}(\ell+(k+2)-2+q)!.

By applying Proposition
4.4
to the equation (
4.16
) and using (
4.19
), we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}U_{k+2}\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}

\displaystyle\leq(3+\tau^{2})C_{d}\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{k}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell+(k+2)-2}(\ell+(k+2)-2+q)!

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{n-1}^{-k}C^{(k+2)-1}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell+(k+2)-2}(\ell+(k+2)-2+q)!,

where we have assumed that
C\geq(3+\tau^{2})C_{d}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now we combine the structure estimates in Proposition
4.5
and the energy estimates to get a uniform control for the solutions of (
4.10
) under a scale-separation condition. Note that the energy estimate (
4.17
) only provides effective control for
\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}^{2}U\|_{L^{2}}
uniform in
\tau\in(0,1)
, which does not imply the uniform boundedness of
U
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Under the same assumption of Proposition
4.5
, there exist
C,\widetilde{C}
and
C_{n}
, depending only on
d
and
n
, such that if for some
\ell\geq 0
and any
2\leq j\leq n
, there exists
k_{j}\geq 2
such that

(4.20)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\delta_{j}/\delta_{j-1})C\Lambda_{1}(\ell+k_{j}+q)\leq e^{-1},\\
\tau^{2}\geq\widetilde{C}e^{-k_{j}},\end{cases}

then (
4.10
) admits a unique solution
U
satisfying

(4.21)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{D}^{\ell}_{n}U\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{n}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!.

Proof.

Due to the factorial growth in
j
of the estimates of
U_{j}
in Proposition
4.5
, we turn to the truncated partial sum

\displaystyle V_{k}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\delta_{n}^{j}U_{j}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}),

which satisfies

(4.22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&-\widehat{\Delta}_{n}V_{k}+\tau^{2}V_{k}-G\\
&=\mathcal{G}_{k}:=\delta_{n}^{k}(-\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{k}+\tau^{2}U_{k})+\delta_{n}^{k-1}(-2\widehat{\nabla}_{n-1}\cdot\nabla_{n}U_{k}-\widehat{\Delta}_{n-1}U_{k-1}+\tau^{2}U_{k-1}).\end{split}

The choice of
k=k_{n}\geq 2
will be assumed to satisfy the scale-separation condition (
4.20
).
Let
W_{k}
be the solution to

-\widehat{\Delta}_{n}W_{k}+\tau^{2}W_{k}=-\mathcal{G}_{k}.

Then we have

(4.23)

U=V_{k}+W_{k}=U_{0}+\sum_{j=2}^{k}\delta_{n}^{j}U_{j}+W_{k}.

Note that the uniform estimates of
U_{j}
with
j\geq 2
can be derived directly from Proposition
4.5
. In fact,

\Big\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\sum_{j=2}^{k}\delta_{n}^{j}U_{j}\Big\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\sum_{j=2}^{k}\delta_{n}^{j}\delta_{n-1}^{-j+2}C^{j-1}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell+j-2}(\ell+j-2+q)!\leq\frac{Ce\delta_{n}^{2}}{e-1}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!,

provided that
(\delta_{n}/\delta_{n-1})C\Lambda_{1}(\ell+k-2+q)\leq 1/e
. Since
\langle U_{j}\rangle_{y_{n}}=0
, the Poincaré inequality implies

(4.24)

\Big\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\sum_{j=2}^{k}\delta_{n}^{j}U_{j}\Big\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{d}\Big\|\mathbf{D}_{n}^{\ell}\sum_{j=2}^{k}\delta_{n}^{j}U_{j}\Big\|_{L^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}H^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}}\leq\frac{C_{d}Ce\delta_{n}^{2}}{e-1}\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!\leq\Lambda_{1}^{\ell}(\ell+q)!.

5
Optimal convergence rates

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.1
. The proof will be carried out in two steps. In the first step, we consider
n
scales
\varepsilon_{1}>\varepsilon_{2}>\cdots>\varepsilon_{n}
under the scale-separation condition (
3.95
). In this case, we can find the multiscale correctors and flux correctors as in the previous sections, and establish the convergence rate to an effective equation in one step that simultaneously homogenizes all the scales.
Therefore, we will call this process simultaneous homogenization, which is in contrast to the reiterated homogenization that homogenizes one scale at a time and repeat the process until all scales are homogenized.

In the second step, we will consider the general situation that the scales may not be all separated, for which Theorem
3.8
does not apply and we cannot find bounded correctors with full scales.
To handle this tricky situation, we need a new strategy to combine the simultaneous homogenization with reiterated homogenization.

5.1
Under scale-separation conditions

In this subsection, we consider the convergence rate for the equation

(5.1)

\|A_{\varepsilon}+A_{\varepsilon}(\nabla_{y}\mathcal{X}_{\delta})(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})-\overline{A}-(\nabla_{y}\cdot\Phi_{\delta})(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\tau,

where
\Phi_{\delta}=(\Phi_{\delta,\ell ij})
is given by (
4.33
).
Observe that

(\nabla_{y}\cdot\Phi_{\delta})(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})=\varepsilon_{1}\nabla\cdot(\Phi_{\delta}(x,x/\varepsilon_{1}))-\varepsilon_{1}(\nabla_{x}\cdot\Phi_{\delta})(x,x/\varepsilon_{1}).

By the integration by parts, we deduce that

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{1}&\leq\varepsilon_{1}\Big|\int_{\Omega}\Phi_{\delta,\ell ij}(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})\partial_{x^{j}}u_{0}\partial_{x^{\ell}}\partial_{x^{i}}\psi\eta_{\varepsilon}\Big|\\
&\quad+\varepsilon_{1}\Big|\int_{\Omega}\Phi_{\delta,\ell ij}(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})\partial_{x^{\ell}}\partial_{x^{j}}u_{0}\partial_{x^{i}}\psi\eta_{\varepsilon}\Big|\\
&\quad+\varepsilon_{1}\Big|\int_{\Omega}\Phi_{\delta,\ell ij}(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})\partial_{x^{j}}u_{0}\partial_{x^{i}}\psi\partial_{x^{\ell}}\eta_{\varepsilon}\Big|\\
&\quad+\varepsilon_{1}\int_{\Omega}\big|(\partial_{x^{\ell}}\Phi_{\delta,\ell ij})(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})\big||\partial_{x^{j}}u_{0}|\,|\partial_{x^{i}}\psi|\\
&\quad+\int_{\Omega}C\tau\,|\nabla u_{0}|\,|\nabla\psi|\\
&\doteq I_{11}+\cdots+I_{15}.\end{split}

Since
\Phi_{\delta}
is antisymmetric (i.e.,
\Phi_{\delta,\ell ij}=-\Phi_{\delta,i\ell j}
), we have
I_{11}=0
. By the definition of
\Phi_{\delta}
(
4.33
) and Theorem
4.9
,

\|\Phi_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\quad\text{and}\quad\|\nabla_{x}\Phi_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|\nabla_{x}\widehat{\nabla}_{n}\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq CL_{0}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{1}&\leq I_{12}+I_{13}+I_{14}+I_{15}\\
&\leq C\varepsilon_{1}\big\{L_{0}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla^{2}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{3\varepsilon_{1}})}\big\}\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad+C\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{5\varepsilon_{1}})}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{4\varepsilon_{1}})}+C\tau\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

By taking the estimates of
I_{1}
–
I_{5}
into (
5.6
), we derive (
5.5
) immediately.
∎

(5.7)

) we deduce that

(5.8)

The following theorem establishes the optimal convergence rate in
L^{2}(\Omega)
under the scale-separation condition.

Theorem 5.4
.

In addition to the assumption of Lemma
5.1
,
let
\Omega
be a bounded
C^{1,1}
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
u_{\varepsilon}
be a weak solution of (
5.1
),
and
u_{0}
the solution of (
5.3
) with
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
.
Then

(5.11)

\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\leq C\big\{\varepsilon_{1}\big(1+L_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}L_{0}^{2}\big)+\tau\big(1+\varepsilon_{1}L_{0}\big)\big\}\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\big),

where
\tau^{2}\simeq\max_{j}\{e^{-c\varepsilon_{j-1}/\varepsilon_{j}}\}
with
c
depending only on
d,n,\lambda,C_{0}
and
\Lambda_{0}
, and the constant
C
depends additionally on
\Omega
.

Proof.

By the definition of
w_{\varepsilon}
, it suffices to
show that
\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is bounded by the right-hand side of (
5.11
).
We perform a duality argument
as in
[
34
]
(also see
[
32
,
31
]
).
Let
A^{*}(x,y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})
be the adjoint of
{A}(x,y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})
.
Note that
A^{*}
satisfies the same conditions as
A
.
Let
\mathcal{X}^{*}
and
\Phi^{*}
be the corresponding correctors and flux correctors respectively, and

\overline{A^{*}}
be defined by (
4.3
), with
A
and
\mathcal{X}
replaced by
A^{*}
and
\mathcal{X}^{*}
, respectively. Note that
\overline{A^{*}}=\overline{A}^{*}
.

For
G\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, let
v_{\varepsilon}
be the weak solution of the following Dirichlet problem,

-\nabla\cdot{A}^{*}(x,x/\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,x/\varepsilon_{n})\nabla v_{\varepsilon}=G\quad\text{in }\Omega\,\quad\text{ and }\,\quad v_{\varepsilon}=0\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

and
v_{0}
the homogenized solution to the problem (
5.3
) with
\overline{A}
replaced by
\overline{A^{*}}.

Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}_{\varepsilon}(x)=

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}-v_{0}-\varepsilon_{1}\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\delta}(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})\nabla v_{0}\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}},

where
\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is a cut-off function
such that
0\leq\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}\leq 1
,

\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(x)=1\text{ in }\Omega\setminus\Omega_{10\varepsilon_{1}},\quad\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(x)=0\text{ in }\Omega_{8\varepsilon_{1}},

and
|\nabla\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}|\leq C\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}
. By Remark
5.3
, we have

(5.12)

\|v_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(1+L_{0})\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

and

(5.13)

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{w}_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\big\{\varepsilon_{1}^{1/2}\big(1+L_{0}^{1/2}+\varepsilon_{1}^{1/2}L_{0}\big)+\tau\big\}\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

The proof of (
5.13
) is the same as that of (
5.10
).

Now, note that

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\Omega}w_{\varepsilon}\cdot G\,dx\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{\Omega}{A}_{\varepsilon}(x)\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\,dx\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\int_{\Omega}{A}_{\varepsilon}(x)\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{w}_{\varepsilon}\,dx\Big|+\Big|\int_{\Omega}{A}_{\varepsilon}(x)\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v_{0}\,dx\Big|

\displaystyle\quad+\varepsilon_{1}\Big|\int_{\Omega}{A}_{\varepsilon}(x)\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\big[\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\delta}(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})\nabla v_{0}\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}\big]dx\Big|

\displaystyle\doteq J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}.

By using the Cauchy inequality, (
5.10
) and (
5.13
), we obtain

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\widetilde{w}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\big\{\varepsilon_{1}\big(1+L_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}L_{0}^{2}\big)+\tau^{2}\big\}\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\big)\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Next, we use (
5.5
) to obtain

(5.14)

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon_{1}\|\nabla v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big\{L_{0}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla^{2}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{3\varepsilon_{1}})}\big\}

\displaystyle+C\|\nabla v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{5\varepsilon_{1}})}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{4\varepsilon_{1}})}+C\tau\|\nabla v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Note that by (
5.9
),

\|\nabla v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{5\varepsilon_{1}})}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{4\varepsilon_{1}})}\leq C\varepsilon_{1}\|\nabla v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{1/2}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{1/2}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{1/2}\|u_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{1/2}.

This, together with (
5.14
), (
5.12
) and Remark
5.3
,
gives

J_{2}\leq C\big\{\varepsilon_{1}(1+L_{0})+\tau\big\}\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\big)\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

The estimate of
J_{3}
is similar to that of
J_{2}
.
By (
5.5
) we see that

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{3}&\leq C\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\|\nabla\big(\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\delta}\nabla v_{0}\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}\big)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big\{L_{0}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla^{2}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big\}\\
&\quad+C\tau\varepsilon_{1}\|\nabla\big(\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\delta}\nabla v_{0}\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}\big)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

Note that by the estimates of
\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\delta}
as in (
3.112
) and (
3.113
),

\displaystyle\|\nabla\big[\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\delta}\nabla v_{0}\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}\big]\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\nabla\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}})\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\delta}\nabla v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\nabla_{x}\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\delta})(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})\nabla v_{0}\big]\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}\|\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(\nabla_{y}\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\delta})(x,x/\varepsilon_{1})\nabla v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\widetilde{\eta}_{\varepsilon_{1}}\mathcal{X}^{*}_{\delta}\nabla^{2}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}\|\nabla v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+CL_{0}\|\nabla v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\|\nabla^{2}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon_{1}^{-1}+1+L_{0})\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

It follows that

\displaystyle J_{3}

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon_{1}\big\{\big(1+\varepsilon_{1}L_{0}\big)\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big\}\cdot\big\{L_{0}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla^{2}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big\}

\displaystyle+C\tau\big\{\big(1+\varepsilon_{1}L_{0}\big)\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big\}\|\nabla u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\big\{\varepsilon_{1}\big(1+L_{0}\big)+\tau\big\}\big(1+\varepsilon_{1}L_{0}\big)\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\big)\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

By combining the estimates of
J_{1},J_{2}
and
J_{3}
, we obtain for any
G\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\Omega}w_{\varepsilon}\cdot G\,dx\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\big\{\varepsilon_{1}\big(1+L_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}L_{0}^{2}\big)+\tau\big(1+\varepsilon_{1}L_{0}\big)\big\}

\displaystyle\times\big\{\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\big\}\|G\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

from which the desired estimate for
w_{\varepsilon}
follows by duality.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

In particular, if
A=A(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})
is independent of
x
, then (
5.11
) holds with
L_{0}=0
. This is exactly (
1.8
). Thus, we have proved Theorem
1.1
under the extra scale-separation condition (
3.95
).

5.2
Remove scale-separation conditions

In this subsection, we will remove the scale-separation conditions and fully prove Theorem
1.1
. Note that in the case of two scales, the scale-separation condition (
3.95
) reads
\varepsilon_{2}/\varepsilon_{1}\leq c
for some
c>0
. This condition can be assumed automatically since otherwise the convergence rate is trivial. Thus we focus on the cases of more than two scales.

Assume that the scales
(\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n})
do not satisfy the scale-separation condition (
3.95
).
Starting from the smallest scale
\varepsilon_{n}
and with at least three scales, i.e.,
(\varepsilon_{n-2},\varepsilon_{n-1},\varepsilon_{n})
, we examine if the scale-separation condition is satisfied for these scales. If the scale-separation condition is satisfied, then we add one more scale
\varepsilon_{n-3}
and examine if the scales
(\varepsilon_{n-3},\varepsilon_{n-2},\varepsilon_{n-1},\varepsilon_{n})
satisfy the scale-separation condition. Generally, we can keep adding scales until the scale-separation condition is not satisfied. This process helps us locate the scale gap where the scale-separation condition fails and therefore we can apply the reiterated homogenization at this scale gap.
In order to make this idea working for obtaining the optimal convergence rate, we need another subtle modification. In the initial case of three scales, we will examine a slightly stronger separation condition by replacing
c_{j}
by
2^{3-n}c_{j}
in (
3.95
). Then each time we add one more scale in, we increase the constant by multiplying a factor
2
. Since we will add at most
n-3
scales, the constant will increase at most to
c_{j}
. Now suppose the above process stops at
\varepsilon_{n-m}
for some
m
with
2\leq m\leq n
, namely,
(\varepsilon_{n-m+1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n})
satisfies the scale-separation condition with constant
2^{m-n}c_{j}
and
(\varepsilon_{n-m},\varepsilon_{n-m+1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n})
does not satisfy the scale-separation condition with constant
2^{m+1-n}c_{j}
.
Precisely, this means that
m\geq 2
is the largest integer such that for all
n-m+2\leq j\leq n

(5.15)

\varepsilon_{j}\leq\frac{2^{m-n}c_{j}\varepsilon_{j-1}}{1+\log(\varepsilon_{n-m+1}/\varepsilon_{j-1})}.

Moreover, there exists some
n-m+1\leq p\leq n
such that

(5.16)

\varepsilon_{p}>\frac{2^{m+1-n}c_{p}\varepsilon_{p-1}}{1+\log(\varepsilon_{n-m}/\varepsilon_{p-1})}.

Note that if (
5.15
) holds for
m=n
, then
(\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n})
satisfies the scale-separation condition (
3.95
).

The following lemma is crucial in removing the scale-separation condition in our main theorem.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
2\leq m<n
be selected as above. Then we
have either

\varepsilon_{n-m+1}>2^{m+1-n}c_{n-m+1}\varepsilon_{n-m},\quad\text{i.e., }p=n-m+1\text{ in \eqref{est.msep}},

or

(5.17)

p\geq n-m+2\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon_{p}>\frac{2^{m-n}c_{p}\varepsilon_{p-1}}{1+\log(\varepsilon_{n-m}/\varepsilon_{n-m+1})}.

Proof.

It suffices to consider
p\geq n-m+2
. Then by (
5.15
) and (
5.16
), we have

\frac{2^{m+1-n}c_{p}\varepsilon_{p-1}}{1+\log(\varepsilon_{n-m}/\varepsilon_{p-1})}\leq\varepsilon_{p}\leq\frac{2^{m-n}c_{p}\varepsilon_{p-1}}{1+\log(\varepsilon_{n-m+1}/\varepsilon_{p-1})}.

This yields

1+\log(\varepsilon_{n-m}/\varepsilon_{p-1})\leq 2\log(\varepsilon_{n-m}/\varepsilon_{n-m+1}).

Inserting this into (
5.16
), we arrive at (
5.17
).
∎

We are now ready to provide the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The case of
n=1
is well-known
[
6
,
18
,
31
]
.
For the case
n=2
, the desired estimate follows directly from Theorem
5.4
. Indeed, when
n=2
, i.e,
A_{\varepsilon}=A(x/\varepsilon_{1},x/\varepsilon_{2})
, the scale-separation condition (
3.95
) reads
\varepsilon_{2}\leq c\varepsilon_{1}
, which can be assumed automatically for otherwise the convergence rate is trivial. Hence for
n=2
we directly obtain the optimal convergence rate from Theorem
5.4
with
L_{0}=0
and
\tau\simeq e^{-c\varepsilon_{1}/\varepsilon_{2}}
,

\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(\varepsilon_{1}+e^{-c\varepsilon_{1}/\varepsilon_{2}}\big)\big(\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\big).

Let us consider the case
n\geq 3
next.
The proof combines the ideas of reiterated homogenization and simultaneous homogenization mentioned above.
Assume the theorem is true if the number of scales is smaller than
n
. We need to show the theorem holds for
n
scales. Let
m\geq 2
be the largest integer such that (
5.15
) holds for all
n-m+2\leq j\leq n
. If
m=n
, as mentioned earlier, the scale-separation condition (
3.95
) is satisfied. Then the desired convergence rate follows from Theorem
5.4
with
L_{0}=0
.

Suppose now
m<n
.
We use the idea of reiterated homogenization and consider the matrix

\mathcal{A}(x,y_{n-m+1},\cdots,y_{n}):=A(x/\varepsilon_{1},\dots,x/\varepsilon_{n-m},y_{n-m+1},\cdots,y_{n}).

It is obvious that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the ellipticity condition (
1.5
) and is
1-periodic in
y_{j}
for
n-m+1\leq j\leq n
.

With an abuse of notation, let
\mathbf{D}_{m}=\mathbf{D}_{n,m}=(\nabla_{y_{n-m+1}},\cdots,\nabla_{y_{n}})
. Then

\mathbf{D}_{m}^{\ell}\nabla_{x}\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-m}\varepsilon_{i}^{-1}(\nabla_{y_{i}}\mathbf{D}_{m}^{\ell}A)(x/\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,x/\varepsilon_{n-m},\cdot).

Consequently, we have

\sup_{x}\|\mathbf{D}_{m}^{\ell}\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\ell}\ell!,

and

\sup_{x}\|\mathbf{D}_{m}^{\ell}\nabla_{x}\mathcal{A}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\varepsilon_{n-m}}\Lambda_{0}^{\ell+1}(\ell+1)!\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\varepsilon_{n-m}}(2\Lambda_{0})^{\ell}\ell!,

where
C_{1}=C_{0}\Lambda_{0}
and
we have used
\ell+1\leq 2^{\ell}
in the last inequality.
Hence the matrix
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the analyticity conditions (
3.102
) and (
3.103
) with
L_{0}=C_{1}\varepsilon_{n-m}^{-1}
and
\Lambda_{0}
replaced by
2\Lambda_{0}
.
We now homogenize the scales
\varepsilon_{n-m+1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n}
simultaneously. Let

\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x)=\overline{\mathcal{A}}(x/\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,x/\varepsilon_{n-m})

be the matrix of effective coefficients given by Theorem
5.4
, where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=(\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n-m})
, and
v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
be the solution to

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\nabla v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}&=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\\
v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}&=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

As
(\varepsilon_{n-m+1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{n})
satisfies the scale-separation condition (
5.15
), which is a version of (
3.95
) with
m
scales, we apply Theorem
5.4
, with
\varepsilon_{1}
replaced by
\varepsilon_{n-m+1}
, to conclude that

(5.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|u_{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\bigg\{\frac{\varepsilon_{n-m+1}}{\varepsilon_{n-m}}+\max_{n-m+1\leq i\leq n-1}\{e^{-c\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{i+1}}\}\bigg\}\big\{\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\big\}.\end{split}

In view of Lemma
5.6
, by our choice of
m
,
we have either

\varepsilon_{n-m+1}>c_{0}\varepsilon_{n-m},

for some
c_{0}>0
, or there exists
p\geq n-m+2
such that

\varepsilon_{p}>\frac{2^{m-n}c_{p}\varepsilon_{p-1}}{1+\log(\varepsilon_{n-m}/\varepsilon_{n-m+1})}.

In the first case,
\varepsilon_{n-m}
and
\varepsilon_{n-m+1}
are not separated well and therefore the convergence rate in (
1.8
) is trivial as
\varepsilon_{n-m}/\varepsilon_{n-m+1}\simeq 1
. In the second case,
\varepsilon_{n-m+1}
and
\varepsilon_{n-m}
are separated better comparing to the separation between
\varepsilon_{p}
and
\varepsilon_{p-1}
,
and we have

(5.19)

\frac{\varepsilon_{n-m+1}}{\varepsilon_{n-m}}\leq C_{0}e^{-c_{p}\varepsilon_{p-1}/\varepsilon_{p}},

which together with (
5.18
) gives

(5.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|u_{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\max_{n-m+1\leq i\leq n-1}\{e^{-c\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{i+1}}\}\big\{\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\big\}.\end{split}

We note that the application of reiterated homogenization on the break point between
\varepsilon_{n-m+1}
and
\varepsilon_{n-m}
does not cause an essential loss on the convergence rate due to (
5.19
).

On the other hand, since
\overline{\mathcal{A}}(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-m})
satisfies the assumptions (
1.5
)–(
1.7
). By the inductive assumption, there exists a constant (effective) matrix
\overline{A}
, and a unique weak solution
u_{0}
to the problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot\overline{A}\nabla u_{0}&=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\\
u_{0}&=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

such that

\|v_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(\varepsilon_{1}+\max_{1\leq i\leq n-m-1}\{e^{-c\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{i+1}}\}\big)\big\{\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|g\|_{H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\big\},

where
C
and
c
are constants depending only on characters of
A
and
\Omega
.
This together with (
5.20
) gives (
1.8
). The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

The main result of Theorem
1.1
could be extended to the more general case
A_{\varepsilon}=A(x,x/\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,x/\varepsilon_{n})
satisfying conditions (

7
Counterexamples

7.1
Necessity of scale separation

In our main result, we have obtained a fast convergence rate even under a mild (logarithmic) scale-separation condition.
Then one may naturally ask if it is possible to find a suitable constant effective matrix and establish a quantitative convergence rate or qualitative homogenization theorem for the multiscale problem without any separation condition. In the following, we will give a counterexample showing that a multiscale problem with non-separated scales may not converge to an effective problem with constant coefficients.

Let
d=1
and
n=2
. Consider the second-order elliptic equation in one dimension (ODE)

(7.1)

-\frac{d}{dx}a_{\varepsilon}(x)\frac{du_{\varepsilon}}{dx}=f,

where

a_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{1+2\alpha\sin(2\pi x/\varepsilon_{1})\sin(2\pi x/\varepsilon_{2})},

and
0<\alpha<1/2
. We let
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon\ll 1
and
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon)<\varepsilon_{1}
. Thus,
a_{\varepsilon}
has two oscillating 1-periodic scales which are not separated and actually are almost resonant. In the following, we will study the effect of multiplying two almost resonant waves (with high frequency) in homogenization.

By the trigonometric identity

\sin\theta\sin\gamma=\frac{1}{2}(\cos(\theta-\gamma)-\cos(\theta+\gamma)),

we see that

\frac{1}{a_{\varepsilon}(x)}=1+\alpha(\cos(2\pi x)+\cos(2\pi x/\varepsilon_{3})),\quad\text{where }\varepsilon_{3}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}.

Note that
\varepsilon_{3}\leq\varepsilon
. Thus
a_{\varepsilon}
is converted into a function with one rapidly oscillating scale and the other component
\cos(2\pi x)
does not oscillate rapidly.
As
\varepsilon\to 0
, it is well known that the homogenized coefficient for the one-dimensional equation is

\bar{a}=(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}a_{\varepsilon}(x)^{-1})^{-1}=\frac{1}{1+\alpha\cos(2\pi x)},

where the limit is understood as the weak limit in
L^{2}
.
This homogenized coefficient
\bar{a}=\bar{a}(x)
relies on the variable
x
. In fact, by the standard homogenization theory (or a straightforward computation for ODE), one can show that the solution
u_{\varepsilon}
of (
7.1
) converges to the solution of

(7.2)

-\frac{d}{dx}\bar{a}(x)\frac{d\bar{u}}{dx}=f,

with a convergence rate of
O(\varepsilon)
; see e.g.
[
26
]
. Clearly, the equation (
7.2
) generally cannot be further approximated by any equations with constant coefficients.

The above example illustrates the necessity of the scale-separation condition for the existence of an algebraic convergence rate in general multiscale homogenization problems. But recall that in some special scenarios without the scale-separation conditions, it is still possible to obtain algebraic convergence rates , such as under a Diophantine condition
[
19
,
29
]
(also see
[
2
,
33
]
) or with a reperiodization technique
[
27
]
. Nevertheless, the method for these special scenarios cannot be extended to the general cases.

Remark 7.1
.

Note that in the previous example if we consider initially
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon/(1+\beta\varepsilon)<\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon
with a fixed
\beta>1
, eventually we will find
\bar{a}(x)=(1+\alpha\cos(2\pi\beta x))^{-1}
, which is still rapidly oscillating if
\beta
is relatively large. This provides an example showing that a two-scale problem (
7.1
) quantitatively converges to a one-scale problem which is still rapidly oscillating. This phenomenon turns out to be generic. In fact, in
[
28
]
, we have shown that an elliptic equation with
n
oscillating periodic scales can always be quantitatively approximated by another elliptic equation with at most
n-1
oscillating scales. The above example shows that the result in
[
28
]
is the best we can expect in general.

7.2
Optimality of exponential rate

We show that the exponential parts of the convergence rate
\exp(-c\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{i+1})
in our main result are optimal in the sense that it cannot be true if any of them is replaced by
\exp(-C\varepsilon_{i}/\varepsilon_{i+1})
for some large
C>0
.
Indeed, for any
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, we can find
\beta\in(\beta_{0},\beta_{0}+1)
and two scales
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon/\beta<\varepsilon_{1}
, and construct an analytic coefficient such that the solution to some elliptic equation (see (
7.3
) below) satisfies

\|u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}\|_{L^{2}}\geq ce^{-C\beta},

for any solution
\bar{u}
of the equations with constant coefficient, where the constant
c,C>0
are independent of
\varepsilon
and
\beta
.

Let
\beta_{0}
be some large integer and
\beta=\beta_{0}+\varepsilon\in(\beta_{0},\beta_{0}+1)
. This choice of
\beta
is to avoid the possible reperiodization argument introduced in
[
27
]
, which leads to a fast convergence rate. Consider a coefficient in the form of

a(y_{1},y_{2})=\frac{1}{1+b_{1}(y_{1})b_{2}(y_{2})}.

Here, we let

b_{1}(y_{1})=\frac{\beta_{0}!}{\beta_{0}^{\beta_{0}}}\sin(\beta_{0}2\pi y_{1}),\qquad b_{2}(y_{2})=\sin(2\pi y_{2}).

It is easy to verify that
b_{1}
is analytic with
|\frac{d^{k}b_{1}}{dy_{1}^{k}}|\leq(2\pi)^{k}k!
for any
k\geq 0
. The same estimate holds also for
b_{2}
. Therefore,
a(y_{1},y_{2})
is analytic in both
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
with quantitative estimates on all the derivatives. Now consider

a_{\varepsilon}(x)=a(x/\varepsilon,\beta x/\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{1+b_{1}(x/\varepsilon)b_{2}(\beta x/\varepsilon)},

and let
u_{\varepsilon}
be the solution of

(7.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\frac{d}{dx}a_{\varepsilon}(x)\frac{du_{\varepsilon}}{dx}=1\quad\text{in }(0,1),\\
&u_{\varepsilon}(0)=u_{\varepsilon}(1)=0.\end{aligned}\right.

The explicit solution of the above ODE is given by

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}\frac{t+p_{\varepsilon}}{a_{\varepsilon}(t)}dt,

for some constant
p_{\varepsilon}
determined by
u_{\varepsilon}(1)=0
. Using the trigonometric identity

\sin\theta\sin\gamma=\frac{1}{2}(\cos(\theta-\gamma)-\cos(\theta+\gamma)),

and
\beta-\beta_{0}=\varepsilon
, we have

\frac{1}{\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t)}=1+\frac{\beta_{0}!}{\beta_{0}^{\beta_{0}}}\sin(\beta_{0}2\pi t/\varepsilon)\sin(\beta 2\pi t/\varepsilon)=1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta_{0}!}{\beta_{0}^{\beta_{0}}}(\cos(2\pi t)-\cos((\beta+\beta_{0})2\pi t/\varepsilon)).

Thus

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{x}(t+p_{\varepsilon})(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta_{0}!}{\beta_{0}^{\beta_{0}}}(\cos(2\pi t)-\cos((\beta+\beta_{0})2\pi t/\varepsilon)))dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{x}(t+p_{\varepsilon})(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta_{0}!}{\beta_{0}^{\beta_{0}}}\cos(2\pi t))dt+O(\varepsilon/\beta).

Moreover, using
u_{\varepsilon}(1)=0
, one observes that
p_{\varepsilon}=-1/2+O(\varepsilon/\beta)
. Thus, we get

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{2}x(1-x)-\frac{\beta_{0}!}{\beta_{0}^{\beta_{0}}}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}(\pi(2x+1)\sin(2\pi x)+\cos(2\pi x)-1)+O(\varepsilon/\beta).

Now note that any solution
\bar{u}
of

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\frac{d}{dx}\bar{a}\frac{d\bar{u}}{dx}=1\quad\text{in }(0,1),\\
&\bar{u}(0)=\bar{u}(1)=0,\end{aligned}\right.

with constant coefficient
\bar{a}>0
is given by

\bar{u}(x)=\frac{1}{2\bar{a}}x(1-x).

Thus

\displaystyle\inf_{\bar{a}>0}\|u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\beta_{0}!}{\beta_{0}^{\beta_{0}}}\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\inf_{k\in\mathbb{R}}\|\pi(2x+1)\sin(2\pi x)+\cos(2\pi x)-1-kx(1-x)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}-\frac{C\varepsilon}{\beta}

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\frac{\beta_{0}!}{\beta_{0}^{\beta_{0}}}-\frac{C\varepsilon}{\beta_{0}},

where
c_{1}>0
and
C>0
are absolute constants independent of
\varepsilon
and
\beta
. The lower bound
c_{1}>0
is guaranteed by the fact that the trigonometric function
\pi(2x+1)\sin(2\pi x)+\cos(2\pi x)-1
has a positive distance from the linear span of
x(1-x)
in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(0,1)
. This lower bound can even be computed explicitly with a careful calculation.

Now
recall that the Stirling’s formula implies

\frac{\beta_{0}!}{\beta_{0}^{\beta_{0}}}\geq e^{-\beta_{0}}.

We assume
\varepsilon
is small and
\beta_{0}\leq M|\log\varepsilon|
for sufficiently large
M
. This is the typical case that
\exp(-c\varepsilon_{1}/\varepsilon_{2})
dominates the convergence rate and in this case

c_{1}e^{-\beta_{0}}\geq\frac{2C\varepsilon}{\beta_{0}}.

As a result, we obtain

\inf_{\bar{a}>0}\|u_{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}\geq\frac{1}{2}c_{1}e^{-\beta_{0}}\geq ce^{-\beta},

for some
c>0
independent of
\varepsilon
and
\beta
. This proves the optimality of the exponential ratio terms in the convergence rate.

7.3
Analyticity helps in a toy problem

We use a toy example showing how the analyticity of coefficients helps improve the convergence rates. In view of the 1-D example in the previous subsections, it is reasonable to consider the averaging process of the product of two scalar functions oscillating at different scales. Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a 1-periodic analytic function and
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a 1-periodic locally
L^{2}
function.
For
\beta\in(1,\infty)
, define

h_{\beta}(x/\varepsilon)=f(\frac{x}{\beta\varepsilon})g(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}).

Clearly,
h_{\beta}
is quasiperiodic. Let
\bar{h}_{\beta}
be the mean of
h_{\beta}
. If we fix
\beta
, then as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
h_{\beta}
converges to
\bar{h}_{\beta}
in the weak sense. However, the convergence rate can be arbitrarily slow depending on the rationality/irrationality and magnitude of
\beta
. On the other hand, if
\beta
is relatively large (say
\beta=|\log\varepsilon|
), we may use the idea of reiterated averaging/homogenization to show that
h_{\beta}
is close to
\bar{f}\bar{g}
in the weak sense, namely, we can quantify

\rho(\varepsilon,\beta):=\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}h_{\beta}(x/\varepsilon)dx-\bar{f}\bar{g}\bigg|.

The natural question is to estimate this error. A typical idea of reiterated homogenization is to average
g
first as it oscillates much faster than
f
, due to
\beta\gg 1
, and then average
f
. That is, we write

\rho(\varepsilon,\beta)\leq\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}f(\frac{x}{\beta\varepsilon})g(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})dx-\int_{0}^{1}f(\frac{x}{\beta\varepsilon})\bar{g}dx\bigg|+\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}f(\frac{x}{\beta\varepsilon})\bar{g}dx-\bar{f}\bar{g}\bigg|,

and estimate these two errors correspondingly.
This will give an error of
\beta^{-1}+\beta\varepsilon
in general.

In the following, we will use the analyticity of
f
to sharpen this error to
\beta\varepsilon+e^{-c\beta}
, which is optimal in general.
Consider the Fourier series of
f
truncated at
k>0
(to be determined):

f_{k}(y)=\sum_{|j|\leq k}\hat{f}_{j}e^{-2\pi ijy},

where
\hat{f}_{j}=\int_{0}^{1}f(y)e^{-2\pi ijy}dy
. Due to the real analyticity of
f
, we have

\|f-f_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq Ce^{-ck},

for some
C
and
c>0
depending on
f
. Thus,

\displaystyle\rho(\varepsilon,\beta)

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{|j|\leq k}\hat{f}_{j}e^{-2\pi ijx/(\beta\varepsilon)}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{g}_{m}e^{-2\pi imx/\varepsilon}-\bar{f}\bar{g}\bigg|+\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}(f-f_{k})(x/(\beta\varepsilon))g(x/\varepsilon)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\sum_{0\leq|j|\leq k,m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\hat{f}_{j}\hat{g}_{m}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi i(\frac{j}{\beta}+m)x/\varepsilon}dx\bigg|

\displaystyle+\bigg|\sum_{0<|j|\leq k}\hat{f}_{j}\hat{g}_{0}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi i(\frac{j}{\beta})x/\varepsilon}dx\bigg|+Ce^{-ck}\|g\|_{2}.

We notice that the worst term in the summation above is the one when
\frac{j}{\beta}+m
is close to zero, which means those oscillating frequencies of
f
and
g
are almost resonant and canceled, leading to slow convergence. To avoid this situation, we can restrict
j
such that
|j/\beta|\leq 1/2
. Since
m
is always a nonzero integer, we must have
|j/\beta+m|\geq|m|-1/2\approx|m|
. Hence we can handle all the nonresonant estimates of the summation if
k=[\beta/2]
. On the other hand, the real analyticity guarantees the tail error (possibly resonant terms) is exponentially small.

Precisely, we choose
k=[\beta/2]
. Then
|\frac{j}{\beta}+m|\simeq|m|
for
|j|\leq k
and
m\neq 0
. Hence, it is easy to verify that

\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi i(\frac{j}{\beta}+m)x/\varepsilon}dx\bigg|\leq\frac{C\varepsilon}{|m|}\quad\text{for any }m\neq 0.

Similarly, for
0<|j|\leq k
,

\bigg|\int_{0}^{1}e^{-2\pi i(\frac{j}{\beta})x/\varepsilon}dx\bigg|\leq\frac{C\beta\varepsilon}{|j|}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\rho(\varepsilon,\beta)

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon\sum_{|j|\leq[\beta/2]}|\hat{f}_{j}|\sum_{m\neq 0}|m|^{-1}|\hat{g}_{m}|+C\beta\varepsilon\sum_{0<|j|\leq[\beta/2]}|j|^{-1}|\hat{f}_{j}||\hat{g}_{0}|+Ce^{-c\beta/2}\|g\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta\varepsilon+e^{-c\beta/2})\|g\|_{2}.

The above argument explains how the real analyticity helps improve the error bound in a simple averaging process. In homogenization of elliptic equations, the mapping from coefficients to the solutions is much more complicated and how the analyticity plays a role is far from obvious.
