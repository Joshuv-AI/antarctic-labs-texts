Antinuclear Autoantibody‐Negative Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE): A Reality or Shortcomings of Diagnostics and Interpretation?
Authors: Ayman Azhary, Nooh Mohamed Hajhamed, Salahaldeen Ismail Mohammed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis letter highlights the critical need for clinicians to understand antinuclear antibody (ANA) testing methodologies and autoantibody specificities to prevent diagnostic errors and accurately applying classification criteria. Anti‐Ro52 is an antinuclear antibody whose detection fulfills the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification entry criterion.

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest and wish to highlight and discuss some points reported in the case report titled “Full‐house nephropathy in antinuclear antibody‐negative systemic lupus erythematosus: A case report” [1]. The case report describes a 25‐year‐old female admitted to the hospital with diverse clinical features. A plethora of differential diagnoses was suggested, and a workup was performed to exclude infectious causes. Acute kidney injury and malar rash, among other clinical and demographic data, raised suspicion of lupus, leading to sequential investigations, including ELISA tests for antinuclear autoantibody (ANA) and lupus‐specific ANAs such as anti‐dsDNA and anti‐Sm. All of these yielded negative results, seemingly frustrating the expectation. However, based on a strong clinical suspicion, immunosuppressive therapy was initiated, alongside the management of clinical features, with a broader investigation including complement, anti–neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs), and anti‐GBM. Further ANA tests using immunofluorescence, as well as an ANA test of the extractable nuclear antigen, were performed, and a positive ANA finding of anti‐Ro52 was eventually revealed. The management course continued with supportive treatments alongside immunosuppressive and immunomodulatory drugs, and a clinical improvement was observed throughout the course of intervention [1].

The case is interesting, and we appreciate the medical interventions followed. However, we wish to highlight some issues we believe were overlooked in the report—specifically, points where the authors appear to have made a premature conclusion and recommendations based on incomplete data. The main point that we believe was missed in the report is the technical aspects regarding the ANA testing. The authors consistently used the abbreviation ANA without providing a clear technical definition. ANAs are antinuclear autoantibodies directed against different nuclear or cytoplasmic entities within the cell; they are significant diagnostic markers in a range of autoimmune diseases, especially autoimmune rheumatic diseases [2, 3]. There are a number of in vitro diagnostic techniques used for the detection of ANA. The indirect immunofluorescence test (IFT) with Hep2 cells as a substrate is considered the gold standard for ANA detection. The main feature of the Hep2 cell substrate is its comprehensive approach, capable of detecting the broadest range of ANAs currently available. Other techniques are used for different reasons, such as quantitative and/or monospecific detection of ANA. However, all techniques have some limitations that should always be considered.

In the reported case, we observed that the authors demonstrated a lack of clear understanding regarding the ANA tests performed, which consequently appears to underpin their conclusions. The authors, who entitled the case as a case of ANA‐negative systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), directly criticized the 2019 EULAR/ACR diagnostic criteria, claiming the case would have been missed by their application. However, based on the authors’ own data, this claim is inaccurate because the case would not have been missed by the EULAR/ACR criteria [2]. The first point is the claim of an ANA‐negative result following the reported techniques. The first technique that they relied on for ANA detection was the ELISA test, which revealed a negative result, but no further details about this test were provided. These details are very crucial for any ANA ELISA test; the test must specify the antigen targets that are used. Commercial ELISAs vary significantly in their antigen contents—some use 4 antigens, some 10, and some a wider range of antigens. In all cases, the clinician must be informed of the antigenic targets in the ELISA test; thus, the specific details of the ELISA used in this case remain unclear.

In the patient's workup, the authors seemingly performed an IFA Hep2 cell test or rodent tissue IFA test, both of which yielded negative results on all occasions. However, when they performed another monospecific approach test, it revealed positive results for ANA SSA Ro52. We are astonished as to why the authors persist in describing the case as ANA negative when they subsequently found one of the ANAs. The authors also failed to address the discrepancy between the negative ELISA and IFA Hep2 cell test results and the positive monospecific test for ANAs for the SSA Ro52 antibody. This discrepancy could have been easily explained if the authors had provided details of each test's antigenic targets. The ELISA test they used may not contain the Ro52 antigen, or it may not have been adequately detected by the test system. Conversely, the negative IFA test in this case may be attributed to variation in the Hep2 cell preparation they used, as Hep2 cells express Ro52 moderately, and its expression may vary depending on the cell cycle phase or stimulation.

Autoantibodies are important features of autoimmune diseases, playing roles in pathogenesis, pathology, and diagnosis. SLE is the prototype autoimmune disease, with approximately 200 autoantibodies identified. ANAs are the most commonly associated autoantibodies, exhibiting very high prevalence, including some pathognomonic SLE markers such as anti‐Sm and anti‐dsDNA. Given this background, the concept of ANA‐negative lupus is a matter of dispute, especially when considering diagnostic limitations [2, 3]. The concept of seronegative SLE has evolved several times with gradual improvements in the substrates used for ANA detection, that is, in sensitivity, specificity, and inclusiveness. Autoantibodies are not only diagnostic but also an important part in the pathogenesis of almost all autoimmune diseases. However, the possibility of seronegative SLE should not be excluded due to various pathogenic mechanisms of the autoimmunity. The EULAR/ACR 2019 criteria, though mandating ANA detection as an entry criterion, allow for the possibility of seronegative SLE, which may be attributable to physiologically undetectable autoantibody levels, or limitations of detection methods or the detected substrate. The presented case is already anti‐RO52 positive, meaning it is ANA positive, although it does not present the typical SLE ANAs, such as anti‐ds or anti‐Sm antibodies. Anti‐Ro52 is present in 40%–70% of SLE patients [2, 4].

The detection of Ro52 indicates the presence of autoantibodies and a positive ANA test. This aligns with the EULAR/ACR entry criteria, which the authors considered the main obstacle. The patient fulfills this criterion. The authors overlooked this point as the EULAR/ACR system mandates an ANA test as an entry criterion, specifically recommending the Hep2 cell test or equivalent. This lack of ANA test understanding is clear throughout the report. The report often treats the ANA test as a separate test from anti‐dsDNA or anti‐Sm antibodies, even though both are types of ANAs, albeit often performed monospecifically in the SLE workup. Other investigations requested for the patient included anti–neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody and anti‐GBM for exclusion of vasculitis and Goodpasture syndrome, respectively. However, the authors did not comment on these differential diagnoses.

Although the patient exhibited reported SLE features, such as malar rash, photosensitivity, and renal involvement, which were used to support the diagnosis of SLE, this conclusion is still questionable, even with the finding of an autoantibody like SSA (Ro52), whose presence was overlooked by the authors when they considered the case as ANA‐negative lupus. We are seriously considering another possibility for this case, given the absence of pathognomonic SLE autoantibodies. The presence of Ro52 is generally considered nonspecific for SLE and is associated with several other connective tissue diseases such as myositis and systemic sclerosis. The case could even be classified as undifferentiated connective tissue disease. The ambiguity surrounding the ANA tests performed in this case represents a major gap in drawing a clear conclusion. The authors' claim of ANA‐negative lupus is erroneous as demonstrated by their own data showing the presence of anti‐Ro52 ANA. This ambiguity may raise concerns about the reliability of the test.

In conclusion, this case is not an example of ANA‐negative lupus, a concept that is itself debated. The detection of the ANA SSA Ro52 fulfills the 2019 EULAR/ACR entry criteria for lupus. Considering other clinical and immunological parameters, especially the biopsy finding, a diagnosis of SLE can be supported for this case. The ANA tests performed were not adequately interpreted to guide the classification, and other autoantibodies were not individually interpreted. It is crucial to check and evaluate disease diagnosis or management criteria. This is important to enrich the future amendments or lead to entire shifts over time, but this needs to stand on a strong basis. In this case, accurate interpretation and understanding of ANA test were crucial.
