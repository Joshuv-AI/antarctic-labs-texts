What comes next after establishing evidence for video-assisted thoracic surgery lobectomy in early-stage lung cancer?
Authors: Masahiro Yanagiya
Published: 2026

Introduction

A recent meta-analysis by Harris et al. showed a survival benefit of video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) lobectomy for early-stage lung cancer without compromising oncological outcomes (1). This study showed a 21% reduction in mortality risk in VATS lobectomy compared with that in open lobectomy (1).

Although short-term outcomes of VATS have already been reported as prospective studies, and VATS has been widely adopted and recommended in clinical practice, clinical equipoise remains regarding the long-term oncological outcomes of VATS lobectomy (2-7). The meta-analysis mentioned above established solid evidence of superiority of VATS lobectomy over open thoracotomy for early-stage lung cancer regarding long-term outcomes (1).

Strengths of the study

This meta-analysis has several important strengths in its design, particularly in the following four aspects: the integration of prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the use of individual patient data (IPD), the consistent application of intension-to-treat (ITT) analysis, and validation through multiple sensitivity analyses. Regarding the first strength, the study comprised three prospective RCTs comparing VATS with open thoracotomy. Each RCT contained more than 200 participants (1-4). The overall cohort included more than 1,000 patients, which would have had sufficient statistical power (1). Regarding the second strength, this meta-analysis used IPD. The use of IPD is a major source of generating a high-quality meta-analysis. Meta-analyses of IPD have historically established solid evidence on the treatment of primary lung cancer (8,9). The meta-analysis by Harris et al. goes beyond the limitations of conventional aggregate data meta-analyses and resembles a large-scale pooled analysis of prospectively randomized patients (1).

Another major strength of this IPD meta-analysis is the consistent application of ITT principles across the included trials. Although a trial reported by Long et al. excluded more than 11% of patients from their analysis (including conversion to thoracotomy in the VATS group), the use of IPD enabled an ITT analysis and thus remarkably improved analytical certainty (4). Finally, this meta-analysis was verified by multiple sensitivity analyses. In addition to dual statistical approaches, various sensitivity analyses supported the superiority of a long-term survival benefit of VATS.

These strengths of the meta-analysis support the VATS approach for primary lung cancer. The questions arising from this study can be summarized as the three following points: (I) what exactly constitutes VATS; (II) whether the indications of VATS can be extended; and (III) future directions for minimally invasive surgery.

What constitutes VATS lobectomy?

VATS lobectomy is defined as no use of rib spreading, utility incision with a maximum length of 8 cm to deliver the specimen, individual dissection of the vein, arteries, and airway for the lobes, and standard lymph node sampling or dissection using a video screen for guidance (10,11). There are numerous approaches for VATS. VATS is classified into multiport VATS, hybrid VATS, and uniportal VATS.

Multiport VATS has been widely adopted for many years (12). In 1992, Lewis et al. first reported lobectomy via VATS (13). They performed four-port VATS lobectomy. Three RCTs included in the meta-analysis by Harris et al. likely performed multiport VATS for most cases (1) (Table 1). Regarding the VATS procedure in the three trials, their meta-analysis would have established evidence of multiport VATS lobectomy for early-stage lung cancer. Even though this procedure is collectively referred to as multiport VATS, it encompasses a wide variety of approaches. The number and placement of ports, as well as the surgical approaches, such as the look-up view and confronting upside-down monitor styles, vary considerably (14-17).

Hybrid VATS is characterized by direct visualization with the aid of video assistance and mini-thoracotomy (18,19). This meta-analysis would not have included this approach because three RCTs did not allow direct vision.

Uniportal VATS has recently emerged as a novel approach. In 2011, Gonzalez et al. reported single-port VATS lobectomy (20). Uniportal VATS has become one of the newest developments in the minimally invasive approach (21). Among the three RCTs, the VIOLET study reported by Lim et al. included the uniportal approach (3). The Uniportal VATS Interest Group of the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons stated that a maximum size of incision should be ≤4 cm (22).

In contrast to VATS lobectomy, open thoracotomy involves rib-spreading anterolateral thoracotomy or posterolateral thoracotomy with direct visualization (2-4). The study reported by Long et al. (4) mentioned an incision of 9–15 cm, while the others did not mention the length of the skin incision (2,3). Based on the outcomes of the meta-analysis, the avoidance of rib spreading appears be important for minimizing surgical invasiveness as reported previously (1,19).

Should the indications of VATS be extended?

Based on the finding that VATS is superior in the surgical approach for early-stage lung cancer, the next question is whether the indications of VATS should be indicated for advanced lung cancer. Compared with that for early-stage lung cancer, surgery for locally advanced lung cancer is technically more demanding for the following two reasons: (I) it involves complex procedures, such as bronchoplasty and angioplasty; and (II) precise hilar and mediastinal lymph node dissection are performed. Therefore, the central issue is whether VATS can adequately overcome these two challenges.

In 2007, Nakanishi reported initial experiences with VATS bronchoplasty and showed favorable outcomes (23). Since this time, VATS bronchoplasty for advanced lung cancer has been increasingly reported in the literature (24-26). Uniportal VATS bronchoplasty (27,28) and angioplasty (including double-sleeve resection) (29-31) have been reported. Most of the articles mentioned above were retrospective studies with relatively small sample sizes. Recently, Nakanishi et al. reported favorable outcomes of a prospective study of VATS lobectomy requiring complex procedures for locally advanced lung cancer (32). However, their study did not include a comparison with open thoracotomy. Consequently, the superiority of VATS in complex surgery remains to be established.

A meta-analysis reported by Zhang et al. showed that VATS was inferior to open thoracotomy in terms of lymph node dissection (33). However, another systematic review concluded that VATS mediastinal lymph node dissection is becoming easier and more feasible because of the development of endoscopic cameras and surgical instruments (34).

Based on the 21% reduction in mortality observed with VATS lobectomy for early-stage lung cancer (1), the question may no longer be whether minimally invasive surgery has a role in locally advanced disease, but how its technical challenges can be safely addressed. More than three decades have passed since the initial report of VATS lobectomy, and substantial institutional experience has accumulated worldwide (12,13). Based on this long-term experience, many institutions have likely already established expertise in performing VATS for locally advanced lung cancer. Additionally, robotic-assisted thoracic surgery has rapidly gained popularity in recent years (35). By facilitating complex reconstructive procedures and precise dissection through a minimally invasive approach, robotic-assisted thoracic surgery may further expand the feasibility of minimally invasive surgery for locally advanced lung cancer and potentially help reconcile the long-standing trade-off between complex oncologic procedures and minimal surgical invasiveness (35).

Beyond surgical access: the future of minimally invasive thoracic surgery

Although the meta-analysis by Harris et al. showed the minimally invasive nature of VATS lobectomy, this advantage is primarily derived from minimizing chest wall trauma (1). The concept of minimally invasive surgery extends beyond the surgical approach itself and should encompass preservation of functional pulmonary parenchyma. The extent of organ loss does not substantially differ between VATS and thoracotomy lobectomy because both approaches ultimately undergo resection of an entire pulmonary lobe. In this regard, the concept of minimally invasive surgery should not be limited to surgical incisional wounds (19,36).

The landmark JCOG0802 trial showed the superiority of segmentectomy over lobectomy for early-stage lung cancer (37). Overall survival favored segmentectomy, reflecting a 33% reduction in mortality risk (hazard ratio: 0.663; 95% confidence interval: 0.474–0.927) (37). The magnitude of the survival benefit observed in JCOG0802 exceeded that reported for VATS lobectomy in the meta-analysis by Harris et al. (1). The findings of JCOG0802 strongly suggest that preservation of pulmonary parenchyma may have a greater effect on long-term outcomes than minimizing surgical access alone.

Combining pulmonary parenchymal preservation with minimally invasive access could further improve long-term outcomes. Combining both strategies could represent the next major step toward truly minimally invasive thoracic surgery. Uniportal VATS segmentectomy may represent one of the least invasive anatomical lung resections currently feasible, combining minimization of chest wall trauma and preservation of pulmonary parenchyma (38).

Conclusions

VATS lobectomy has been proven to be superior to open thoracotomy for early-stage lung cancer. The survival benefit of VATS in early-stage disease provides a strong rationale for evaluating whether similar benefits can be achieved in locally advanced lung cancer whenever feasible. Moreover, pulmonary parenchyma preservation techniques greatly contribute to minimally invasive thoracic surgery.
