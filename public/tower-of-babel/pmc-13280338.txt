Hybrid membrane nanovesicles reprogram macrophages to resolve oxidative stress in intervertebral disc degeneration
Authors: Yuming Huang, Min Su, Man Li, Qi Ma, Xian Wang, Zhibin Chen, Yiping Chen, Boxuan Xu, Rongsheng Chen, Zhixiang Lu, Zhibin Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is sustained by a self-amplifying inflammatory microenvironment in which oxidative stress and macrophage activation form a pathogenic feedback loop that drives matrix disorganization and progressive tissue failure. However, therapeutic disruption of this immune–redox circuit remains challenging because drugs poorly access the avascular disc niche and rarely modulate both immune and structural compartments simultaneously. Here we develop a hybrid membrane–engineered nanovesicle system (DP@HMV) derived from macrophage and nucleus pulposus cell membranes to enable dual-compartment targeting within degenerative discs. This platform delivers daphnetin and pioglitazone in a co-assembled nanocore and permits intervention at the level of the pathological microenvironment rather than single signaling pathways. Mechanistically, DP@HMV quenches excessive reactive oxygen species and interrupts pro-inflammatory amplification by reprogramming macrophages toward a reparative phenotype. This immune shift is coupled to restoration of matrix homeostasis, characterized by enhanced anabolic matrix synthesis and suppression of fibrotic and catabolic remodeling. Transcriptomic analyses further reveal coordinated downregulation of inflammatory and matrix-degrading programs, indicating systemic rewiring of the degenerative niche. In a rat model of IVDD, DP@HMV preserves disc structure, improves histological integrity, and alleviates pain-associated behaviors. These findings demonstrate that hybrid membrane nanotherapy can reprogram the macrophage-driven redox microenvironment, establishing a microenvironment-centered strategy for treating IVDD.Keywords: Intervertebral disc degeneration, Daphnetin, Pioglitazone, Hybrid membrane vesicles, Macrophage polarization, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Oxidative stress induced by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and dysregulated macrophage polarization, particularly the shift towards pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, has now been recognized as a key driving factor in intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) [1,2]. However, during IVDD and the formation of annular fissures, both oxygen tension and oxidative load significantly increase [3]. Mechanical overload, aging, diabetes, and changes in endplate vasculature further enhance ROS production, while simultaneously impairing the antioxidant system, thus tipping the redox balance towards chronic oxidative stress [4]. Mitochondrial dysfunction in nucleus pulposus (NP) and annulus fibrosus (AF) cells leads to increased generation of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, while reducing the clearance of ROS [5]. Furthermore, the formation of neovascularization and the accompanying inflammatory response trigger the infiltration of immune cells, which release additional ROS and reactive nitrogen species, thereby exacerbating oxidative damage [6]. M1 macrophages secrete high levels of TNFα, IL1β, IL6, and other cytokines, which exacerbate local inflammatory responses and establish a self-sustaining inflammatory microenvironment in degenerated intervertebral discs [7]. These cytokines, along with M1-specific chemokines, promote the recruitment of other immune cells and further drive the shift of macrophages towards the M1 phenotype, thereby intensifying chronic inflammation [8]. M1 macrophage-conditioned medium increases the expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), such as MMP3 and MMP13, in NP and AF cells, while inhibiting the synthesis of type II collagen and proteoglycans, thus accelerating the degradation of the extracellular matrix [9]. Clearing ROS and reducing macrophage M1 polarization are key therapeutic targets in the treatment of IVDD.

Daphnetin (DAP) is a coumarin derivative isolated from various species of the Daphne genus, exhibiting significant antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective activities [10]. Numerous in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that DAP can reduce intracellular and mitochondrial ROS levels, decrease lipid peroxidation (such as malondialdehyde), and restore the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase 2 and heme oxygenase 1 (HO1) [11]. Pioglitazone (PIO), a thiazolidinedione drug, is a synthetic PPARγ agonist that can induce the transition from M1 to M2 metabolic characteristics in various models through both in vitro and in vivo experiments [12]. Activation of PPARγ antagonizes NFκB signaling, thereby reducing M1-mediated inflammatory responses and promoting the formation of the M2 phenotype [13]. However, DAP and PIO exhibit limited water solubility (with PIO being only around 0.015 mg/mL) and suboptimal pharmacokinetic properties, making it difficult to maintain effective drug concentrations in the ischemic areas of the intervertebral disc when administered via conventional formulations [[14], [15], [16]]. Intervertebral disc drug delivery is characterized by limited space and poor mobility, which can easily lead to cascade reactions. Once inflammation or polarized positive feedback amplification occurs, it becomes difficult to suppress, potentially resulting in chronic low back pain.

Due to the limitations of drug concentration, our primary consideration in designing the delivery system was how to enhance drug absorption. This led us to explore a hybrid membrane delivery system composed of macrophage membrane vesicles (MMV) and NP cell membrane vesicles (NMV). During IVDD, the influence of macrophage polarization states cannot be overlooked. MMV inherit characteristics from their parent cells, exhibiting heterogeneity and diversity. Given that macrophage receptor expression may vary under different polarization states, this could affect the delivery efficacy of MMV-coated nanoparticles. Therefore, we utilized CCR2 and CD11b to characterize the macrophages. The intervertebral disc consists of the NP, AF, and endplates, with the primary pathological focus being the NP cells. The HNPC membrane (human NP cell line) retains adhesion proteins and glycoproteins, which enhance its affinity and retention with NPCs and the ECM in the intervertebral disc [17]. We co-extruded and sonicated these two membranes in a 1:1 protein mass ratio to form hybrid membrane vesicles (HMV), which were then coated onto the core surface, carrying homogenized drugs, to construct DP@HMV (Fig. 1A). This delivery system carries dual drugs, which can significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy by targeting the primary pathological cells (NP cells) and the main functional cells (macrophages), providing targeted therapy that enhances immune effects and improves therapeutic outcomes.

Methods

Preparation of DAP/PIO nanoparticles

Briefly, 2 mg of PIO was dissolved in 400 μL DMSO to obtain a clear PIO stock solution. Meanwhile, 2 mg of DAP was ultrasonically dissolved in 2 mL ultrapure water to form a homogeneous aqueous solution. Under magnetic stirring, the PIO solution was slowly added dropwise into the DAP aqueous solution and continuously stirred at room temperature for 1 h to obtain a uniform mixed system. Subsequently, the resulting mixture was slowly introduced into 10 mL ultrapure water, followed by sonication to facilitate the self-assembly of PIO/DAP nanoparticles. The nanosuspension was further stirred at room temperature for 2 h to improve colloidal stability. Thereafter, the suspension was transferred into a dialysis bag and dialyzed against ultrapure water for 24 h to remove DMSO and free small molecules. After dialysis, the DAP/PIO nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 20 min and repeatedly washed with double-distilled water (ddH2O) until the supernatant became clear and colorless.

Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)

To evaluate the assembly efficiency and membrane fusion behavior between NPs and MMV, FRET-based fluorescence analysis was employed. In this assay, the NMV components were labeled with the donor fluorophore DiO, while MMV were labeled with the acceptor fluorophore DiD. Hybrid membrane systems were then reconstructed by mixing at different mass ratios (NMV: MMV = 1:0, 1:1, 1:2, and 2:1).

FRET signals were assessed by exciting the donor fluorophore (DiO) and monitoring the corresponding emission changes of the acceptor fluorophore (DiD). Efficient membrane fusion or reduced intermolecular distance facilitates energy transfer from donor to acceptor, resulting in decreased DiO fluorescence and enhanced DiD emission, thereby serving as an indicator of membrane fusion efficiency.

Comparative emission spectra across different formulation ratios were used to evaluate membrane reconstitution efficiency. The results showed ratio-dependent variations in FRET signals, with the most pronounced effect observed at a 1:1 NMV-to- MMV ratio. This condition exhibited the greatest donor quenching and strongest acceptor enhancement, indicating the highest extent of membrane fusion and the most efficient formation of hybrid vesicles.

Preparation of HMV

Macrophages and NP cells were separately harvested and thoroughly washed 2-3 times with ice-cold PBS to remove residual culture medium and extracellular proteins. The cells were then resuspended in ice-cold hypotonic lysis buffer and incubated on ice to facilitate osmotic disruption of the plasma membrane and release of intracellular contents.

Following lysis, differential centrifugation was performed on each cell lysate to isolate the membrane fraction. The resulting membrane pellets were collected and repeatedly washed 2-3 times with cold PBS to further eliminate cytosolic proteins and impurities, yielding purified macrophage and NP cell membrane components.

Protein concentrations of the isolated membrane fractions were quantified, and the two types of membranes were precisely mixed at a 1:1 total protein mass ratio. The membranes were subjected to repeated freeze-thaw cycles (alternating between −80°C and room temperature) to promote membrane disassembly and reorganization. Subsequently, the mixture was processed through extrusion to enhance membrane fusion and homogeneity, ultimately forming uniform and stable HMV.

Preparation of DP@HMV

DAP/PIO nanoparticles were encapsulated into HMV using a membrane coating strategy combined with sequential extrusion to achieve efficient surface wrapping and structural integration. Briefly, pre-prepared and purified DAP/PIO nanoparticles and HMV vesicles were separately resuspended in sterile PBS and gently sonicated in an ice bath to ensure uniform dispersion and prevent aggregation.

The nanoparticle suspension was then mixed with HMV at a predefined ratio and incubated at 4°C under gentle rotation for 60 min, allowing sufficient interaction between nanoparticles and membrane components to facilitate initial adsorption and partial membrane association.

Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to serial extrusion through polycarbonate membranes with decreasing pore sizes to promote membrane reorganization and uniform coating of the nanoparticles, ultimately forming a stable DP@HMV nanostructure with a well-defined core-shell architecture.

Preparation of RMV

Fresh anticoagulated whole blood was collected and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The upper plasma layer and buffy coat were carefully removed, and the erythrocyte pellet was collected. The red blood cells were resuspended in pre-cooled PBS and washed three times by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. Subsequently, the purified erythrocytes were mixed with 20 vol of pre-cooled 0.25× PBS hypotonic lysis buffer and incubated in an ice bath for 30 min to induce hypotonic hemolysis and release hemoglobin. The lysate was then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, and the red supernatant was discarded. The pale pink membrane pellet was collected and repeatedly washed with hypotonic buffer until the supernatant became nearly colorless, yielding purified erythrocyte membranes. The obtained erythrocyte membranes were resuspended in PBS and subjected to probe sonication under ice-bath conditions (2 s on, 3 s off, total sonication time of 5 min). The resulting membrane suspension was subsequently extruded through a 200 nm polycarbonate membrane to obtain uniformly sized erythrocyte membrane vesicles.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The morphology of nanoparticles was examined using a Tecnai G20 STWIN transmission electron microscope operating at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. For sample preparation, a 5–10 μL aliquot of nanoparticle suspension was dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to adsorb for 2 min. Excess liquid was removed using filter paper, followed by negative staining with 0.2% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid for 30–60 s. After air-drying at room temperature, samples were visualized under TEM.

Particle size and zeta potential analysis

The hydrodynamic size and electrophoretic potential of DP, HMV and DP@HMV were characterized through dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis and zeta potential determination. Prior to measurement, vesicle dispersions were diluted with deionized water and carefully homogenized to achieve consistent distribution. Data acquisition was conducted at room temperature using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, UK). Prior to measurement, samples were diluted 10–20-fold with deionized water and gently vortexed to ensure homogeneity. Measurements were conducted at 25°C with a detection angle of 173°. For each group, a minimum of three independent measurements was performed, and the average particle diameter together with the surface charge was calculated to guarantee data accuracy and reproducibility.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectra of DP@HMV, DP nanoparticles, and raw drugs (DAP and PIO) were recorded using a Bruker Vertex 70V Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Bruker Optics, Germany). Prior to analysis, all samples were fully lyophilized under vacuum (−50°C, <10 Pa, 24 h) to remove residual moisture that may interfere with infrared absorption signals. For pellet preparation, dried samples were thoroughly homogenized with spectroscopic-grade potassium bromide (KBr) at a mass ratio of approximately 1:100 (sample: KBr). The mixture was finely ground in an agate mortar for 5–10 min to ensure uniform dispersion of the analyte within the KBr matrix, which is critical for minimizing light scattering and improving spectral reproducibility. The resulting homogeneous powder was then compressed into transparent pellets under a hydraulic press at a pressure of 8–12 MPa for 60–90 s.

Spectral acquisition was performed in transmission mode over the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 co-added scans per sample to improve signal-to-noise ratio. Background spectra were collected using a pure KBr pellet under identical conditions prior to each measurement. All spectra were baseline-corrected and normalized using OPUS software (Bruker, Germany) before further analysis.

UV-visible spectroscopy

Lyophilized DP@HMV, DP, and raw drugs (DAP and PIO) were redispersed in deionized water and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. The UV-Vis absorption spectra were recorded using quartz cuvettes over the wavelength range of 200-800 nm (Agilent Cary 60), with the corresponding solvent used as a blank for background subtraction.

Synergy determination using SynergyFinder

Drug synergy was evaluated with the online tool SynergyFinder 3.0 (https://synergyfinder.fimm.fi), using the "fibrosis inhibition rate" as the response metric. Response surface modeling and the ZIP (Zero Interaction Potency) method were applied. The fibrosis inhibition rate was defined based on the suppression of the fibrosis marker gene Acta2 mRNA, calculated as follows: Fibrosis inhibition rate = (Acta2 mRNA expression level in control group - Acta2 mRNA expression level in experimental group)/Acta2 mRNA expression level in experimental group. According to the SynergyFinder user guide, the effect of drug combinations is reported as a synergy score: a score >10 indicates synergy, a score < −10 indicates antagonism, and a score between −10 and 10 suggests an additive or non-interactive effect (no true synergy). A heatmap of the drug combination responses was also generated to assess the potential therapeutic relevance of the combined treatment.

Encapsulation and drug load efficiency of DAP and PIO

A 10 mg portion of DP@HMV (dry powder) loaded with DAP and PIO was resuspended in 20 mL of physiological saline and incubated at 37°C under gentle agitation (100 rpm) for 24 h to facilitate partial release of encapsulated drugs. After incubation, 2 mL of the suspension was withdrawn and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min to separate free drug. The absorbance of the obtained supernatant was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Cary 60) at the characteristic wavelengths of DAP and PIO, respectively. As a control, equivalent amounts of free DAP and PIO were dissolved in saline, centrifuged under the same conditions, and analyzed to generate reference values. The concentrations of DAP and PIO were calculated based on pre-established standard calibration curves correlating absorbance with concentration. Molecular Modeling and dynamics simulation methods

Molecular Modeling and Parameterization. The initial structures of DAP, DMSO, and PIO were constructed using GaussView. Geometries were optimized at the DFT level with the B3LYP hybrid functional and the 6-31G(d) basis set using Gaussian 09. RESP (Restrained Electrostatic Potential) atomic charges were then derived for the optimized structures via Multiwfn. The General Amber Force Field (GAFF) parameters for the small molecules were generated using the Sobtop program.

Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulation Setup. All simulations were performed using GROMACS 2023.3. Molecules were parameterized with the GAFF force field, and water molecules were described using the TIP3P model within the Amber99sb force field. The simulation system was constructed by placing DAP and PIO in a cubic box, which was then solvated with DMSO molecules. A minimum distance of 15 Å was maintained between the solutes and the box boundaries. System preparation involved an initial energy minimization (1000 steps of steepest descent) followed by a 0.5 ns NPT equilibration during which the temperature was gradually increased from 0 K to 298 K. Production MD simulations were conducted for 100 ns (including an 8 ns annealing phase) with a 2-fs time step, saving coordinates every 10 ps. The temperature was maintained at 298 K using the v-rescale thermostat, and the pressure was kept at 1 bar using the C-rescale barostat. For non-bonded interactions, a 10 Å cutoff was applied to van der Waals interactions with dispersion correction (EnerPres), and long-range electrostatic interactions were handled using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method.

Trajectory Analysis. Trajectory snapshots were rendered using VMD 1.9.3 and PyMOL 2.5.0. Key structural and interaction properties, including the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), interaction energy, and hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) between molecules, were calculated from the simulation trajectories.

CCK-8 cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. NP cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 8000 cells per well. After adherence, the cells were treated with various concentrations or formulations of HMV for 24 h. Subsequently, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Following this, 100 μL of fresh culture medium containing 10% (v/v) CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 3 h at 37°C. The absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) was measured using a multimode microplate reader (PerkinElmer). Cell viability was calculated relative to the untreated control group, which was normalized to 100%. Each experiment was performed with at least six independent replicates.

Flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy for ROS detection

Intracellular ROS levels were detected using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). For flow cytometric analysis, cells were trypsinized, collected by centrifugation, and washed twice with cold PBS. The cell pellets were then resuspended in serum-free medium containing 10 μM DCFH-DA and incubated at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS to remove excess probe and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. The fluorescence intensity of the oxidized product, dichlorofluorescein (DCF), was detected at an excitation wavelength of 510 nm and an emission wavelength of 610 nm.

For fluorescence imaging, cells cultured in plates were washed with PBS and incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA in serum-free medium under the same conditions. After staining, cells were rinsed three times with serum-free medium to remove residual probe. Intracellular ROS accumulation was visualized by capturing DCF red fluorescence using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Live/dead cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a fluorescent staining assay with propidium iodide (PI). Cells were washed with PBS to remove culture medium before staining. A working solution containing PI at a final concentration of 4.5 μM was added to the cells, followed by incubation in the dark for 20 min at room temperature. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove excess dye. Fluorescent images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope. Dead cells were identified by green fluorescence from PI.

Measurement of inflammatory cytokines by ELISA

To evaluate the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, NP cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with ctrl IVDD, DP or DP@HMV (50 μg/mL) for 12 h. Following this pretreatment, all groups were exposed to 250 μM H2O2 for 4 h to induce an inflammatory response. After the stimulation period, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged to remove any cellular debris. The concentrations of secreted cytokines, were determined using specific commercial rat ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's protocols. The absorbance was measured, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve generated for each assay.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol™ Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, samples were homogenized in TRIzol, followed by phase separation with chloroform. The aqueous phase containing RNA was collected and mixed with isopropanol to precipitate RNA. The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in RNase-free water. RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically. Subsequently, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a commercial 5 × All-In-One RT MasterMix (Applied Biological Materials). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a real-time PCR detection system. Each 20 μL reaction contained 2 μL of diluted cDNA, 10 μL of 2× SYBR Green master mix, and 0.4 μM of each gene-specific forward and reverse primer. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 60 s. A melt curve analysis was performed at the end of each run to confirm amplification specificity. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method and normalized to a housekeeping gene (Gapdh). The sequences of all primers used are listed (Table S1).

Western blot analysis

Protein expression was analyzed by Western blot (WB). Cells or tissue samples were lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit. Equal amounts of protein (20–40 μg) were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, denatured at 95°C for 5 min, and separated by electrophoresis on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels. Subsequently, proteins were electrophoretically transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies (details provided in the table below). After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following another wash step, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate. The chemiluminescent signal was captured by exposing the membrane to autoradiography film in a darkroom. The exposure time was adjusted based on signal intensity. The film was then developed and fixed using standard photographic reagents. Band intensities were quantified by densitometric analysis of the scanned film images using ImageJ software, and protein expression levels were normalized to those of GAPDH or β-actin as an internal loading control. The specifications for the antibodies are presented (Table S2).

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunofluorescence analysis, tissues were processed similarly. After blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against COL1A1 (1:200, CST #72026), α-SMA (1:200, Abcam ab7817), IL-1β (1:50, Abcam ab254360), and TNF-α (1:500, Santa Cruz sc52746), followed by secondary antibodies conjugated with iFluor™ 488 and iFluor™ 594. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich F6057-20 ML) and visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX53). The specifications for the antibodies are presented (Table S2).

For cultured cells, immunofluorescence was performed on cells grown on coverslips or in chamber slides. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Subsequent incubation with primary and secondary antibodies followed the same protocol as for tissue sections. Finally, coverslips were mounted onto slides with an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI. The specifications for the antibodies are presented (Table S2).

Fluorescent images for both tissue sections and cultured cells were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 980).

MRI of rat lumbar spine

In vivo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine was performed on rats to assess IVDD. Prior to imaging, rats were anesthetized using a gaseous anesthesia system with isoflurane (induction: 4-5%, maintenance: 1-2.5% in oxygen) and placed in a prone position on a dedicated animal holder. Respiration rate was monitored throughout the procedure to ensure stable anesthesia.

MRI scans were conducted using a preclinical MRI system (Bruker BioSpec) equipped with a dedicated volume coil for signal reception. High-resolution sagittal T2-weighted images of the lumbar spine were acquired using a fast spin-echo sequence. Typical imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) = 3000-4000 ms, echo time (TE) = 50-60 ms, field of view (FOV) = 50 × 50 mm2, matrix size = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 0.8-1.0 mm, and number of averages (NEX) = 4-6. The total scan time per animal was approximately 15-20 min.

Following acquisition, the degree of disc degeneration was evaluated based on the T2-weighted images. Assessment was performed by [two independent observers blinded to the group allocation] using a validated grading system, such as the Pfirrmann classification, which evaluates NP signal intensity, disc height, and structure. Alternatively, quantitative measurements of the normalized signal intensity of the NP or the disc height index were performed using image analysis software.

Histological staining

For histological evaluation, rat lumbar spine segments were fixed, decalcified, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned sagittal at 5 μm thickness. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with hematoxylin, differentiated, blued, counterstained with eosin, dehydrated, cleared, and finally mounted. For Safranin O/Fast Green (SO) staining, sections were similarly processed, sequentially stained with Fast Green and Safranin O solutions to visualize general tissue structure and glycosaminoglycan content (stained red) specifically, followed by dehydration, clearing, and mounting. Stained sections were imaged under a light microscope. The intensity of SO staining in the NP was quantified using image analysis software for comparative analysis.

Cell isolation and culture

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Fujian Medical University (Approval No.: IACUC FJMU 2024-Y-1089). Under sterile conditions, NP tissues were collected from 6-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats and thoroughly rinsed with PBS containing 1% penicillin. The tissues were transferred to trypsin-EDTA solution and digested at 37°C for 5–10 min. After terminating the digestion, the NP tissues were collected by centrifugation and further digested in 0.2% type II collagenase at 37°C for 15 min. Tissue debris was removed using a 100 μm cell strainer, and the NP cells were collected by centrifugation. The collected cells were resuspended in DMEM complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were cultured in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 and 21% O2, and the culture medium was changed every 2–3 days. When cell confluence reached 70%–80%, cells were passaged for further experiments.

Establishment of the rat mechanical loading and LBP model

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old, 200 ± 20 g) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Fujian Medical University. Rats were housed in a standard specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment with a constant temperature of 21–24°C and a 12 h light/dark cycle. Animals were randomly and blindly assigned to each experimental group. To simulate human low back pain (LBP) caused by IVDD, a lumbar spine instability (LSI) model was established to induce abnormal mechanical loading on the discs. Under anesthesia with 3% sodium pentobarbital, L4–L5 spinous processes and the corresponding supraspinous and interspinous ligaments were surgically removed. In the sham group, only the paraspinal muscles and soft tissues of the L4–L5 segment were dissected without removing the spinous processes or ligaments.

24 h after surgery, rats in the DP@HMV treatment groups received a single intradiscal injection of 2 μL DP@HMV (50 μg/mL). Rats in the untreated puncture groups received an equivalent volume of sterile PBS as a vehicle control.

The progression of IVDD was assessed in vivo using MRI at the 4- and 8-week endpoints prior to sacrifice and tissue collection for subsequent histological and molecular analyses.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance of differences was determined using two-tailed Student's t-test or one-way/two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate possible correlations between two variables. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of DP@HMV

First, DAP and PIO co-assemble into stable DP nanoparticles through van der Waals, hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking particles with intrinsic antioxidant and anti-inflammation activities. Simultaneously, macrophages and NP cells membranes were collected to prepare HMV. Using co-extrusion technology, the HMV were coated onto the core of the DP, resulting in the formation of DP@HMV hybrid nanoparticles (Fig. 1A). To evaluate the synergistic anti-fibrotic effects of PIO and DAP, a BLM-induced fibrosis model was used to screen different concentration combinations. ZIP synergy analysis revealed a positive synergy score of 16.809, indicating a synergistic interaction between PIO and DAP, with the optimal synergistic region mainly observed around the indicated concentration range in the heatmap (Fig. S1A, Supporting Information). To further validate the anti-fibrotic effect of the combined treatment, the expression levels of fibrosis-associated proteins were examined by Western blot. BLM stimulation markedly increased the protein levels of COL1A1 and α-SMA, confirming the successful induction of a fibrotic phenotype. Compared with the BLM group, combined PIO/DAP treatment reduced the expression of both COL1A1 and α-SMA to varying degrees, among which the 0.2PIO +0.4DAP combination showed a pronounced inhibitory effect on fibrotic marker expression (Fig. S1B–D, supporting Information). These results demonstrate that PIO and DAP exert synergistic anti-fibrotic effects in the BLM-induced fibrosis model, supporting their combined use for subsequent formulation and functional studies. The structural evolution during the simulation is shown (Fig. S2A, Supporting Information). To gain mechanistic insights into the self-assembly of DP nanoparticles, MD simulations were performed using the GROMACS package to investigate the aggregation behavior of DAP and PIO molecules in a water/DMSO mixed solvent system. The simulations revealed that DAP and PIO molecules rapidly aggregated from a dispersed state into a single nanoparticle cluster within the first 10 ns, with most molecules forming a stable nanostructure by 100 ns (Fig. 1B). Consistent with these observations, the RMSD increased sharply during the initial 10 ns and plateaued at ∼3.9 nm by ∼80 ns, indicating structural equilibration (Fig. S2B, Supporting Information). In parallel, the radius of gyration (Rg) progressively decreased and stabilized at ∼2.5 nm (Fig. S2C, Supporting Information), while the SASA decreased to ∼215 nm2 (Fig. S2D, Supporting Information), collectively suggesting the formation of a compact and stable nanoparticle core. Energy-decomposition analysis identifies dispersion (van der Waals) interactions as the dominant driving force, with a secondary electrostatic contribution; synergistic π–π stacking and hydrophobic contacts consolidate the final nanostructure (Fig. S2E, F Supporting Information). The DP@HMV is expected to achieve dual targeting of macrophages and NPCs, with capabilities to scavenge ROS, repair mitochondria, and promote M2 polarization. TEM clearly showed that smaller DP (Fig. 1C) were successfully encapsulated within larger HMV (Fig. 1D), forming DP@HMV with an average diameter of 136.8 nm (Fig. 1E), and surface potentials of −5.7 mV, −9.0 mV, and −9.5 mV, respectively (Fig. 1F). To verify the successful co-assembly and membrane fusion between NMV and MMV, FRET analysis and CLSM imaging were performed (Fig. S3, Supporting Information). As shown in the fluorescence spectra, NMV/MMV membrane mixtures exhibited distinct fluorescence emission profiles at different ratios after co-assembly. Compared with the NMV: MMV = 1:0 control, the co-assembled membrane systems showed enhanced acceptor fluorescence signals, indicating efficient energy transfer between the DiO- and DiI-labeled membranes and suggesting close spatial integration of NMV and MMV membranes (Fig. S3A, Supporting Information). Among the tested ratios, the NMV:MMV = 1:1 group displayed a strong fluorescence response, supporting effective membrane interaction and fusion. CLSM imaging further confirmed this co-assembly behavior. In the physical mixture group, the green fluorescence from DiO-labeled NMV and the red fluorescence from DiI-labeled MMV were largely separated, indicating limited spontaneous fusion. In contrast, after co-assembly, extensive yellow fluorescence signals were observed due to the overlap of green and red channels, demonstrating successful fusion and hybridization of NMV and MMV membranes (Fig. S3B, Supporting Information). We assessed the drug encapsulation efficiency and loading capacity of DP@HMV using ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy (Fig. 1G and H), and the results indicated that the nanomaterial efficiently encapsulated both PIO and DAP drugs. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) and UV-Vis spectroscopy further confirmed the successful encapsulation of DAP and PIO in DP@HMV (Fig. 1I and J). Additionally, WB analysis of DP@HMV detected characteristic proteins of macrophages and NPCs. Combined with the previous TEM images, this indicates that HMV derived from macrophages and NPCs was successfully coated on the surface of DP@HMV (Fig. 1K). Furthermore, we verified that DP@HMV maintained good stability under various temperature and pH conditions (Fig. 1L–N).

The cell viability of NP cells treated with increasing concentrations of DP@HMV for 24 h (Fig. S4, Supporting Information). The intracellular ROS levels in NP cells were measured by flow cytometry (Fig. 2A). Representative fluorescence images of ROS staining in NP cells are shown (Fig. 2B), where red fluorescence indicates the presence of ROS, and bright-field images provide contextual information about cell morphology and density. Lipid peroxidation, a marker of oxidative stress, was quantified in NP cells. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in LPO levels in the DP@HMV-treated group, suggesting its protective effect against oxidative damage (Fig. 2D). Additionally, the GSH levels, a major antioxidant in cells, were measured, and the data showed a substantial increase in GSH in cells treated with DP@HMV, indicating enhanced antioxidative capacity (Fig. 2E). The extent of cellular apoptosis was evaluated using PI staining, which showed reduced cell death numbers in the DP@HMV-treated group (Fig. 2R and S). These findings suggest that DP@HMV has a protective effect against apoptosis in NP cells. DP@HMV modulates macrophage polarization

Immunofluorescence staining of CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker) was performed to evaluate macrophage polarization in RAW264.7 macrophages under different treatment conditions (Fig. 3A–C). To investigate whether HMV coating enhanced the cellular internalization and tissue retention of DP, the uptake behavior of DP and DP@HMV was evaluated in vitro and in vivo (Fig. S5, Supporting Information). Time-dependent fluorescence imaging showed that both DP and DP@HMV could be internalized by macrophages, as indicated by the gradually increased red fluorescence signals from 30 min to 12 h (Fig. S5A, Supporting Information). Notably, compared with free DP, DP@HMV exhibited stronger and more widespread intracellular red fluorescence at the same time points, particularly after 4–12 h, suggesting that HMV coating facilitated the cellular uptake of DP. The tissue distribution of DP and DP@HMV was further examined in intervertebral disc tissues. In the DP-treated group, red fluorescence signals were detectable but relatively scattered and weak within the disc region, with limited overlap with α-SMA-positive areas (Fig. S5B, Supporting Information). In contrast, DP@HMV showed markedly enhanced red fluorescence accumulation in the nucleus pulposus region, accompanied by stronger colocalization with α-SMA-positive fibrotic areas, as reflected by the merged yellow/orange signals (Fig. S5C, Supporting Information). Green fluorescence indicates the presence of CD86, a marker for M1 macrophages, while red fluorescence corresponds to CD206, a marker for M2 macrophages. The quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity confirmed that DP@HMV treatment promoted the transition from M1 to M2 polarization, as reflected by increased CD206 expression and reduced CD86 expression. Flow cytometry further confirmed the expression of M1 (CD86) and M2 (CD206) markers on the macrophage surface (Fig. 3D). The dot plots clearly indicated that DP@HMV treatment induced a shift towards the M2 phenotype, evidenced by a reduction in the M1 marker CD86 and an increase in the M2 marker CD206. Quantitative PCR analysis was performed to assess the mRNA expression levels of M1 markers (CD86 and Nos2) and M2 markers (CD206 and Arg1) in macrophages treated with DP@HMV (Fig. 3E–H). The results showed that DP@HMV significantly downregulated M1-associated genes and upregulated M2-associated genes, supporting the shift towards a more anti-inflammatory, tissue-repairing M2 phenotype. Protein levels of M1 (CD86 and iNOS) and M2 (CD206 and ARG1) markers were quantified using WB (Fig. 3I–M). The WB analysis confirmed that DP@HMV treatment reduced M1-related protein expression and enhanced M2-related protein expression, consistent with the gene expression findings. The mRNA expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines, including Il10, Ccl1, and pro-inflammatory Il1b, and Tnfa, was measured in macrophages under different treatment conditions (Fig. 3N–Q). DP@HMV treatment led to a significant reduction in the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β), highlighting its anti-inflammatory effects. These findings were further confirmed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) measurement of cytokine concentrations in the cell culture supernatants (Fig. 3R–U). DP@HMV treatment significantly decreased the secretion of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. WB analysis of key regulatory proteins in the PPARγ and NFκB pathways (Fig. 3V–Y) revealed that DP@HMV treatment activated PPARγ (as indicated by increased levels of p-PPARγ) and inhibited NFκB signaling (evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα). These results suggest that DP@HMV promotes macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype by activating PPARγ and suppressing NFκB-mediated inflammation.

DP@HMV modulates extracellular matrix homeostasis and fibrosis

The in vivo biosafety and toxicity of DP@HMV were assessed and are presented (Figsure S6, Supporting Information). WB analysis was performed to assess the protein levels of key ECM components and fibrotic markers in NP cells treated with DP@HMV. The results showed that DP@HMV significantly increased the expression of the ECM protein COL2A1 and Aggrecan, thereby supporting the homeostasis and health of the NP (Fig. 4A–H). In contrast, the fibrotic markers COL1A1, α-SMA, MMP3, MMP9, and MMP13 were significantly reduced in DP@HMV-treated cells, suggesting a reduction in ECM degradation and fibrosis. Quantitative analysis confirmed that showed a clear shift towards an anti-fibrotic phenotype with DP@HMV treatment. Immunofluorescence staining was used to examine the localization and expression of COL1A1 (green) and α-SMA (red) in NP cells (Fig. 4I). The staining results showed reduced COL1A1 and α-SMA expression in DP@HMV-treated NP cells, further supporting the anti-fibrotic effects of DP@HMV. The quantitative analysis of Mean Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) confirmed the reduction in the expression of these fibrotic markers, indicating a shift towards ECM preservation and reduced fibrosis. To further investigate the signaling pathways involved in ECM regulation and fibrosis, the phosphorylation levels of Smad proteins were assessed (Fig. 4J–L). WB analysis revealed that DP@HMV treatment reduced the phosphorylation of Smad2 and Smad3, indicating inhibition of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway, which is closely associated with fibrotic ECM remodeling. These results suggest that DP@HMV may attenuate fibrosis by suppressing aberrant TGF-β/Smad signaling, thereby contributing to the restoration of ECM homeostasis in NP cells. To evaluate the interaction between macrophages and NP cells in ECM remodeling and fibrosis, a co-culture system was established (Fig. 4M). WB analysis of ECM proteins (COL2A1, Aggrecan) and fibrotic markers (COL1A1, α-SMA, MMPs) in the co-culture system showed that DP@HMV treatment not only promoted ECM synthesis in NP cells but also inhibited the fibrotic response, especially in the presence of macrophages (Fig. 4N–U). These findings further suggest that DP@HMV has a potent effect in modulating ECM turnover and fibrosis through macrophage polarization and the modulation of inflammatory signals. This result suggests that DP@HMV can attenuate the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines caused by macrophage polarization, thereby slowing down IVDD.

DP@HMV alleviates intervertebral disc degeneration in a rat model

An overview of the experimental design for the rat IVDD model and the DP@HMV treatment regimen is provided (Fig. 5A). MRI was utilized to assess the degree of intervertebral disc degeneration in rats subjected to the IVDD model. The results demonstrated that DP@HMV treatment significantly reduced the severity of disc degeneration (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that DP@HMV effectively alleviates IVDD progression and improves disc integrity. Histological evaluations were performed to further examine the effects of DP@HMV on the intervertebral disc tissues. H&E staining revealed that DP@HMV treatment reduced the structural damage to the intervertebral disc, showing improved cell morphology and less degeneration. Additionally, Safranin O/Fast Green (SO&FG) staining was used to assess the glycosaminoglycan content, which is indicative of ECM integrity (Fig. 5C). DP@HMV-treated discs showed increased glycosaminoglycan staining, suggesting a preservation of the ECM. Quantitative histological analysis (Fig. 5D) further confirmed that DP@HMV treatment led to a significant improvement in disc histology, as indicated by higher scores for tissue integrity. Behavioral assessments were conducted to evaluate the effects of DP@HMV on pain and discomfort in rats with IVDD. The Rat Grimace Scale (RGS) was used to assess pain-related behaviors (Fig. 5E), and the von-Frey filament test was employed to measure mechanical allodynia (Fig. 5F). The results indicated that DP@HMV treatment significantly reduced low back pain behaviors and mechanical allodynia, demonstrating its potential in improving pain management in IVDD. WB analysis was performed to assess the expression of M1 and M2 macrophage markers (Fig. 5G–K). The data showed that DP@HMV treatment led to a significant reduction in M1 markers (CD86 and iNOS) and an increase in M2 markers (CD206 and ARG1), confirming the promotion of macrophage polarization towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This shift is indicative of a reduced inflammatory response in the treated discs. Additionally, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) were quantified in the cell culture supernatants using ELISA (Fig. 5L–O). DP@HMV treatment significantly decreased the secretion of these cytokines, further supporting its anti-inflammatory effects. Immunofluorescence staining of TNF-α and IL-1β in IVDD tissue sections (Fig. 5P) demonstrated reduced expression of these pro-inflammatory cytokines in DP@HMV-treated discs, particularly in the NP regions. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity confirmed the significant reduction of TNF-α and IL-1β in the treatment groups, indicating a suppression of the inflammatory cascade associated with IVDD. WB analysis of ECM components and fibrosis markers (COL2A1, Aggrecan, COL1A1, α-SMA, MMPs) was performed to evaluate the impact of DP@HMV on ECM remodeling and fibrosis in IVDD tissue (Fig. 5Q–U). The results showed that DP@HMV treatment significantly increased the expression of essential ECM components (COL2A1 and Aggrecan), and reduced the expression of proteins associated with fibrotic remodeling (COL1A1, α-SMA) and ECM degradation (MMP3, MMP9, MMP13). These findings indicate that DP@HMV mitigates IVDD by shifting the ECM balance toward preservation and repair, while suppressing pathological fibrosis and breakdown. Immunofluorescence staining for COL1A1 (green) and α-SMA (red) in IVDD tissue sections (Fig. 5V) revealed that DP@HMV treatment significantly reduced the expression of these fibrotic markers in the NP regions, which is consistent with the WB results. The quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity confirmed that DP@HMV effectively reduces fibrosis and promotes ECM integrity in IVDD tissues. The in vivo toxicity evaluation of DP@HMV was conducted on major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney (Fig. S7, Supporting Information). Pfirrmann grading based on MRI evaluation was performed to assess disc degeneration across groups (Fig. S8, Supporting Information). G1 mainly showed grade I discs, whereas G2 exhibited a marked shift toward grade IV-V, confirming severe degeneration. Compared with G2, the treatment groups showed improved grading distributions, with reduced high-grade degeneration and increased lower-grade discs. Notably, G5 displayed the most favorable outcome, characterized by a higher proportion of grade I-II discs and no grade IV-V changes, indicating effective attenuation of degenerative progression. Quantitative analysis of pain-related behavioral assessments showed that the model group (G2) exhibited markedly decreased PAT values and shortened latency to fall compared with the normal control group (G1), indicating enhanced mechanical pain sensitivity and impaired motor function after intervertebral disc degeneration induction (Fig. S9, Supporting Information). In contrast, the treatment groups showed progressive functional improvement, as reflected by increased paw withdrawal thresholds and prolonged rotarod latency, with G5 displaying the most pronounced recovery and approaching the levels observed in G1.

DP@HMV modulates IVDD-related pathways revealed by transcriptomics

To investigate the therapeutic mechanisms of DP@HMV in IVDD, we employed bulk RNA-seq transcriptomic analysis. PCA performed on IVD tissues from control, IVDD, and DP@HMV-treated rats showed a clear separation, suggesting that the treatment effectively modulates the disease-altered gene expression landscape (Fig. 6A). Differential gene expression between IVDD and DP@HMV-treated groups was visualized using a volcano plot (Fig. 6B), with thresholds set at p < 0.05 and |fold change| > 1.5. The analysis identified a substantial set of differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Significantly upregulated genes were predominantly enriched in anabolic processes, such as ECM synthesis, while downregulated genes were largely involved in catabolic processes, including inflammatory responses and matrix degradation. These DEGs underscore the molecular changes induced by DP@HMV treatment in IVDD. Heatmap analysis detailed DP@HMV transcriptional effects. The inflammation-related gene heatmap (Fig. 6C) showed a concordant downregulation of key mediators (Il1b, Il6, Ccl2) in treated versus IVDD rats, confirming anti-inflammatory efficacy. Similarly, the ECM-related heatmap (Fig. 6D) demonstrated a reparative shift, with suppression of fibrotic (Col1a1, Col4a1) and catabolic markers (Lamc2). (Fig. 6D). To systematically understand the functional impact of these transcriptional changes, we performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses on the downregulated DEGs. The GO analysis (Fig. 6E) highlighted significant enrichment in terms related to “immune system process,” “leukocyte migration,” and “extracellular matrix organization”, aligning with the observed attenuation of inflammation and fibrosis. Consistently, KEGG analysis (Fig. 6F) identified key downregulated pathways, including “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction,” “NF-kappa B signaling pathway,” and “ECM-receptor interaction”. This collective suppression of central inflammatory and matrix-degrading signaling networks provides a mechanistic basis for the therapeutic effects of DP@HMV. To validate the key transcriptional changes identified by RNA-seq, we performed RT-qPCR on a panel of representative DEGs. Consistent with the sequencing data, DP@HMV treatment significantly downregulated pro-inflammatory genes (Il1b, Il6, Ccl2) and fibrotic markers (Col1a1), while downregulating adhesion ECM components (Col4a1, Lamc2) at the mRNA level (Fig. 6G and H). Furthermore, WB analysis was performed to confirm these therapeutic effects at the protein level. Consistent with the transcriptional data, DP@HMV treatment led to a concerted alteration in the abundance of key proteins: it significantly reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, CCL2) and the fibrotic marker COL1A1, while reducing the levels of the adhesion ECM components COL4A1 and LAMC2 (Fig. 6I–L). This coherent alteration from transcript to protein solidifies the conclusion that DP@HMV effectively modulates these pivotal targets in vivo.

Discussion

IVDD is a complex process driven by oxidative stress, inflammation, and ECM degradation. Numerous studies in the preliminary phase have confirmed that various drugs can achieve effective inhibition in in vitro environments. However, the narrow ecological niche of the intervertebral disc and the limited drug delivery methods restrict the therapeutic efficacy in treating IVDD. In this study, we investigated the therapeutic mechanisms of DP@HMV, a novel drug delivery system, in modulating these pathological features of IVDD. The results demonstrate that DP@HMV significantly mitigates oxidative stress, inflammation, and ECM degradation, offering promising therapeutic potential for treating IVDD.

Our study revealed that DP@HMV treatment, even at very low concentrations, effectively reduces the inflammatory response in IVDD tissues, as evidenced by decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and CCL2. The shift towards an anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage phenotype is a critical mechanism underlying these effects. Immunofluorescence and flow cytometry analyses showed that DP@HMV promotes macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This polarization is likely facilitated by the activation of PPARγ and inhibition of NFκB signaling, as indicated by our WB data. Previous studies have highlighted the crucial role of macrophage polarization in regulating the inflammatory microenvironment in IVDD [18,19], and our findings suggest that DP@HMV can restore the balance towards a more reparative state. This shift could play a key role in reducing the chronic inflammation that drives IVDD.

DP@HMV represents a novel and promising therapeutic strategy for IVDD, demonstrating substantial advantages over conventional treatments. The unique combination of DAP and PIO encapsulated within HMV enhances the therapeutic efficacy of each drug while overcoming their individual limitations, such as poor solubility and bioavailability. The HMV system allows for dual-targeting of macrophages and NP cells, which is critical in modulating the inflammatory and degenerative processes in IVDD. This dual-targeting capability is a major strength, enabling DP@HMV to not only deliver the drugs more effectively to the therapeutic sites but also to directly influence the key cellular players involved in the pathogenesis of IVDD.

Another remarkable aspect of DP@HMV is its ability to modulate macrophage polarization. The transition from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, which is effectively induced by DP@HMV, plays a crucial role in suppressing inflammation and promoting tissue repair in IVDD. Macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype is a key therapeutic strategy in tissue regeneration, as M2 macrophages are known to secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines and promote ECM remodeling, essential for disc tissue repair. This ability to manipulate macrophage behavior gives DP@HMV an edge over conventional treatments, which typically fail to address the immune response in a targeted and therapeutic manner.

Moreover, DP@HMV has shown strong capabilities in preserving ECM integrity, as demonstrated by its ability to increase the synthesis of key ECM components such as COL2A1 and Aggrecan, while simultaneously inhibiting the degradation of ECM through the downregulation of MMPs and fibrotic markers like COL1A1 and α-SMA. This anti-fibrotic effect is particularly important in IVDD, where excessive fibrosis contributes to disc degeneration and pain. By not only preventing ECM breakdown but also promoting the repair and preservation of healthy disc matrix, DP@HMV addresses the core pathology of IVDD in a multifaceted way.

In addition, in vivo studies have further highlighted the therapeutic potential of DP@HMV, showing significant improvements in disc integrity, pain management, and overall disc function. MRI and histological assessments clearly demonstrated that DP@HMV treatment significantly alleviates disc degeneration, reduces pain behaviors, and enhances disc morphology, making it a promising candidate for clinical translation in the treatment of IVDD.

Conclusion

The findings from this study provide compelling evidence that DP@HMV is a promising therapeutic strategy for IVDD. By targeting both macrophages and NP cells, DP@HMV effectively modulates inflammation, oxidative stress, ECM degradation, and fibrosis. The dual-targeting nature of DP@HMV, combined with its ability to shift macrophage polarization and enhance ECM integrity, highlights its potential as a novel therapeutic approach for treating IVDD and possibly other degenerative disc diseases.
