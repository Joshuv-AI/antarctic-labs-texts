In this issue of CMAJ, Van and colleagues present a cost–utility analysis of several different strategies for introduction of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) to patients with heart failure.1 This well-executed study shows the potential of simultaneous initiation of 3–4 drugs used to treat heart failure to improve outcomes at an attractive cost. I discuss why these findings are important and next steps for evidence generation and health-system readiness.

Heart failure is an increasingly common and fatal disease and causes a heavy patient, health care–system, and cost burden.2 Approximately half of all heart failure is due to poor left ventricular systolic function (heart failure with reduced ejection fraction [HFrEF]), which in past years carried a median survival less than 5 years after diagnosis. Fortunately, management of this condition has undergone a revolution. Currently, optimal therapy includes 4 classes of disease-modifying drugs (β adrenergic receptor blockers, angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors [SGLT2Is]), which have dramatically improved prognosis.3 Recently, an analysis of several landmark randomized controlled trials (RCTs) confirmed that initial inclusion of all 4 drug classes, now termed GDMT, reduces 2-year mortality in patients with HFrEF from 35% to 10%, a 73% risk reduction4 compared with a diuretic alone. However, little guidance for clinicians (apart from expert opinion) exists as to the best manner to achieve GDMT.5,6

As a result, clinical practice has mirrored the approach taken in clinical trials — adding medications to background therapy 1 at a time, titrating to target or tolerated doses before repeating the process with the next medication. Data from registries of heart failure have thus indicated prolonged titration periods, lasting up to a year — lengthening patient exposure to a condition that is only partially treated.7 Supported by the proposition that earlier achievement of “quadruple therapy,” or GDMT, will improve clinical outcomes, experts have increasingly advocated more aggressive initiation and titration schedules. However, no RCT has analyzed the optimal manner in which clinicians might deliver multiple medications to patients with heart failure, many of whom will be very ill. Hesitancy to simultaneously administer different drug classes relates to fear of adverse effects, and cost and workloads related to frequent clinician visits for monitoring.8 Although many cost-effectiveness analyses have been published for individual drugs used to treat heart failure, the relative cost benefits of different treatment strategies have not been examined. The 20% lifetime risk of heart failure, its attendant burden, and its requirement for comprehensive GDMT reinforce the need for improved treatment strategies.9

Van and colleagues undertook a thoughtful and comprehensive assessment of costs and outcomes associated with several GDMT sequencing strategies. They reviewed published sequencing regimens and divided them into categories of single-drug sequential titration method (the traditional and reference strategy), initial 2-drug strategy, initial 3-drug strategy, and initial 4-drug strategy (simultaneous initiation). These approaches were further categorized into weekly or biweekly visits. The authors then applied previously published patient trajectories after drug initiation and titration (i.e., adverse effects, need for a pause in titration, hospital admission, and death), to construct a microsimulation model that could be used to calculate expected costs and outcomes based in Canadian dollars. From this they calculated expected quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and used this to determine the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER).

The results are striking. First, all sequencing strategies, which ranged in duration from 4 to 24 weeks, were associated with large clinical benefits including an extra 9.55–9.72 QALYs, at a desirable ICER (as low as Can$11 400). Second, although the traditional strategy was associated with the lowest overall cost, it also offered the lowest QALY gain, yet the somewhat more costly, most aggressive strategies titrated over less time — up to 16 fewer weeks — offered the largest QALY gains. Third, and perhaps somewhat surprisingly, weekly titration schemes were less cost-effective than biweekly schemes, likely owing to the increased costs of clinician visits coupled with the small difference in titration duration between weekly and biweekly strategies. Fourth, the dominant factors driving differences in savings between strategies were the observed hospital admission and mortality rates, the cost of hospital admission, and the discount rate (the adjustment for inflation) rather than the costs associated with medications and their adjustments, or those associated with management of adverse effects, which were more minor.

The current study is not an RCT involving different drug-sequencing strategies; the model is informed by data from prior trials. The authors used a crude estimate of health status attribution as they did not have access to individual patient health status. Moreover, the authors made several reasonable but unproven assumptions, such as universal eligibility of all patients for all 4 drug classes, the linear additive impact of each drug, lack of importance of the order of drug sequence, and maximal drug beneficial effect immediately upon introduction. However, the study’s findings clearly support clinical approaches of simultaneous or near-simultaneous administration of GDMT to patients with HFrEF as highly cost-effective. For a disease as common as heart failure, the potential cumulative health-system effects of these improvements are large. By shortening the GDMT titration period by 16 weeks — using the same medications — one may confer an additional 8.8 weeks of QALYs. Most patients would likely accept this proposition, but putting the approach into operation is not so simple. Many patients will not be candidates for 1 or more drugs for various reasons. Others will not tolerate higher GDMT doses or accept and manage multiple changes in a single visit.8 Close follow-up may not be feasible, and most of all, the introduction of 3 or 4 drugs simultaneously may not be tolerable. As such, real-world outcomes may not mirror Van and colleagues’ model.

The STRONG HF (Safety, tolerability and efficacy of uptitration of guideline-directed medical therapies for acute heart failure) trial randomly assigned 1078 patients admitted with heart failure not receiving GDMT to either usual care or to a high-intensity care arm, in which they were administered all classes of GDMT while in hospital and followed weekly for at least 4 weeks after discharge.10 The trial was terminated for clinical benefit including an absolute 8.1% reduction in 180-day all-cause death, a 20% higher medical therapy intensity score seen within 2 weeks of follow-up, and improved quality-of-life score at all visits in patients who received the intervention. Against these gains, the high-intensity group experienced an excess of 5% adverse effects.11 The findings of the STRONG HF trial make it clear that sequential single-drug titration is likely a thing of the past and that clinicians should now focus on how to most effectively deliver optimal GDMT to patients with HFrEF.

Owing to the large number of patients with heart failure, widespread implementation of rapid sequence therapy would require changes in health care delivery. Randomized controlled trials should now focus on addressing the manner in which to initiate GDMT and the implementation of successful strategies. Factors to consider include simultaneous drug administration, titration aids and technology, and identification of patients who are not suitable for aggressive strategies.12 Valuable human resources such as nurses and pharmacists remain underused and should be included in this research. In today’s reality of limited health care resources, evaluation of the relative cost benefits of newly developed treatment strategies will help shape future practice, leading to better care for patients with heart failure.
