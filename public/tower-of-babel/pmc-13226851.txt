Ciclopirox reprograms effector responses and modulates Notch1 activation in activated human T cells
Authors: Aprajita Tripathi, Debolina Dasgupta, Mehak Ahuja, Gabrielle Fisette, Emily Burt, Nadine Santana-Magal, Robyn Wood, John A. Taylor, Stefan H. Bossmann, Scott J. Weir, Kalyani Pyaram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCiclopirox (CPX) is an FDA approved, broad-spectrum antifungal agent. The prodrug of CPX, fosciclopirox, was evaluated for safety and preliminary efficacy in patients with advanced solid tumors, including bladder cancer, and was found to inhibit cell proliferation, the γ-secretase complex, and Notch signaling. However, the effects of CPX on human T cell function have remained undefined. In this study, we investigated the impact of CPX on primary human T cell activation and their effector responses. CPX enhanced early activation markers and IL-2 production; yet, suppressed activation-driven expansion, altered cell cycle progression, and reduced effector functions, including IFN-γ production and cytotoxic granule expression, in vitro. Mechanistically, CPX modulates Notch1 activation temporally and reprograms T cell metabolism by limiting glycolysis, both of which impact proliferative and effector responses in activated T cells. Together, these findings identify CPX as a modulator of T cell immunity, highlighting the broader immunologic implications for its therapeutic application.

Introduction

Ciclopirox (CPX) is a synthetic hydroxypyridone derivative and broad-spectrum antifungal agent that has been widely used for the treatment of fungal infections since its regulatory approval in 1982.1,2,3 It is found in several topical formulations including creams, lotions, gels, shampoos, and nail lacquers either in free acid or the olamine salt form.4,5 Although CPX exhibits fungistatic and fungicidal activity against a broad spectrum of fungal organisms,6 the antifungal mechanisms of CPX remain poorly understood. One proposed mechanism of action involves the chelation of polyvalent metal cations, such as Fe3+, which suppresses metal-dependent enzymatic systems including cytochromes, catalase, and peroxidase, thereby disrupting mitochondrial electron transport and impairing energy production.2,4,5,7,8

Over the past several years, CPX has gained increasing attention for its potential therapeutic applications that extend beyond its established antifungal activity. Recent research has explored its repurposing for a variety of non-infectious diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disease, polycystic kidney disease, and onychomycosis patients with diabetes etc.9,10,11,12,13,14 Preclinical studies have demonstrated the anti-cancer activity of CPX against various solid tumors13,15,16,17 as well as hematologic cancers.18,19,20 In solid tumors like cervical and pancreatic cancers, CPX promotes apoptosis and inhibits proliferation by targeting PARK7 (Parkinsonism-associated deglycase) or by increasing the reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation.21,22 In lung adenocarcinoma, CPX strongly inhibits tumor cell proliferation, induces ROS production, triggers DNA damage, and activates the ATR-CHK1-p53 signaling pathway.23 Notably, CPX suppresses the growth of glucocorticoid-resistant T cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) cell lines by disrupting the β-catenin-c-Myc signaling pathway.24 Additionally, CPX demonstrates antiviral activity against HIV and orthopoxviruses.25,26

CPX also exhibits immunomodulatory properties, particularly through its anti-inflammatory effects.4,27,28 In macrophages, it inhibits the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, a key driver of inflammation, and upregulates peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) to protect from mitochondrial damage.29 CPX protects against LPS-induced septic shock by suppressing inflammation via the sortilin 1 (SORT1)-mediated Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.30

Oral CPX, evaluated in relapsed and refractory leukemia, demonstrated biological activity but was discontinued due to dose-limiting gastrointestinal toxicity and poor oral bioavailability resulting from extensive first-pass effect.20 To overcome these limitations, researchers at the University of Kansas Cancer Center developed a water-soluble prodrug, fosciclopirox, which upon intravenous administration is rapidly converted to its active metabolite, CPX, with high systemic and urinary exposure.15 Fosciclopirox has been evaluated in a phase 1 dose-escalation trial (NCT03348514) for advanced solid tumors,31 followed by safety and efficacy trials planned in both muscle-invasive (NCT04608045) and non-muscle-invasive (NCT04525131) bladder cancer types. Subsequent studies established the anticancer activity of fosciclopirox and CPX in preclinical models of bladder cancer, where fosciclopirox treatment reduced the tumor size and grade in mouse models in vivo.5 CPX was found to inhibit the γ-secretase complex and suppress Notch signaling in bladder carcinoma cell lines.5

In mammals, there are four evolutionarily conserved Notch receptors: Notch1, Notch2, Notch3, Notch4,32,33,34 which collectively mediate Notch signaling and play a critical role in regulating cell fate decisions, cell development, differentiation, proliferation, tissue homeostasis, cancer, and immune function.32,35,36,37,38,39 Dysregulation of this pathway is often implicated in a wide range of human cancers.36,37,40 Initiation of Notch signaling involves sequential cleavage by ADAM/TACE (ADAM 17) and γ-secretase, leading to the release of the Notch intracellular domain (NICD), which translocates to the nucleus, and regulates the transcription of target genes.32,34,35,41,42

Notch signaling plays a pivotal role in T cell lineage commitment and effector function.43,44 The γ-secretase complex, which mediates the proteolytic activation of Notch receptors, is essential for T cell development in the thymus and for regulating peripheral T cell activation, differentiation, and cytokine responses.38,45,46,47 As central mediators of cell-mediated immunity, T cells play a pivotal role in combating infections and suppressing cancer.48 Naive T cells recognize antigens presented by major histocompatibility complex molecules through their T cell receptors (TCRs), allowing them to mount specific immune responses.49,50 Upon activation by antigen recognition, T cells undergo clonal expansion and initiate effector responses such as cytokine secretion and cytotoxic activity.48,49,51 Despite more than four decades of clinical use of topical CPX, its well-documented roles in inflammation and cancer11,13,28,29,30 and the early-phase cancer trials of its prodrug fosciclopirox described above, the direct effects of CPX on T cell activation and effector function remain poorly characterized.

To address this gap, we investigated the in vitro effects of CPX on primary human T cells. Here, we demonstrate that CPX suppresses activation-driven proliferation, reprograms effector functions and diminishes the cytotoxic potential of activated T cells. Mechanistically, we identified that CPX temporally modulates Notch1 activation, which may partially contribute to the observed effects. Additionally, CPX reduces glycolysis, thereby altering cellular metabolism in activated T cells. Together, these results identify CPX as a modulator of human T cell activation, proliferation, and effector responses, with potential implications for its therapeutic use.

Results

CPX treatment impacts the expansion of activated human T cells

Activated T cells undergo rapid expansion in response to stimulation.52 Given that CPX suppresses the proliferation of various cancer cells,5,13,53,54 we investigated the effect of CPX on activation-driven expansion of T cells. For this, human T cells isolated from the blood of healthy donors (Table S1) were activated in vitro using αCD3 and αCD28 antibodies (to mimic antigen recognition)55 in the presence of increasing concentrations of CPX (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2 μM) (Figure 1A). CPX treatment limited the expansion of activated T cells in vitro in a dose-dependent, with pronounced reduction in cell numbers at 1 and 2 μM CPX, and at days 5–7 post activation (Figure 1B). The observed differences could not be due to increased cell death as no decrease in T cell viability was observed with CPX treatment except at the highest (2 μM) CPX concentration (Figures 1C and S1A). c-Myc (MYC) plays a pivotal role in regulating activation-induced T cell proliferation and metabolic reprogramming.56 We found MYC gene expression to be downregulated by CPX treatment in activated T cells as compared to untreated controls (Figure 1D). To determine whether the reduction in cell numbers with CPX treatment was due to decreased proliferation, we evaluated the expression of proliferation marker, Ki-67 in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure S1B). Consistent with reduced cell counts, Ki-67 levels were significantly lower in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells treated with 1 or 2 μM CPX than in untreated controls (Figures 1E and 1F). A few studies reported that CPX disrupts cell cycle progression by causing an arrest either in the G0/G1 or an increase in S-phase cells5,17 in different cell lines or cancer cells. Thus, we performed cell cycle analysis using BrdU/7-AAD staining and found that CPX exhibits comparable effects in activated human T cells. CPX-treated T cells demonstrated an increased frequency of cells in G1 and G2/M phases, accompanied by a reduced frequency of cells in S-phase (Figure 1G). Consistently, CPX-treated cells demonstrated an increasing trend of CCND3 (cyclin D3), a key regulator of G1/S phase progression in the cell cycle57(Figure S1C). CPX enhances IL-2 expression by activated human T cells

Expanding T cells depend on an autocrine IL-2 feedback loop to sustain proliferation and survival.58,59 We therefore quantified IL-2 levels in culture supernatants to determine whether the reduced proliferation and altered cell cycle progression observed in CPX-treated T cells were due to diminished IL-2 production. On the contrary, IL-2 concentrations were significantly higher in the supernatants of T cells treated with 1 and 2 μM CPX than in untreated T cells (Figure 2A). Consistently, transcript levels of IL2 and the downstream signaling mediator STAT5A60 were also elevated in CPX-treated T cells. (Figures 2B and S1D). To determine whether the increased extracellular IL-2 reflected enhanced production or impaired utilization, we assessed intracellular IL-2 expression on days 3 and 5 post-activation. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a dose-dependent increase in IL-2 expression in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells treated with CPX compared with untreated controls at day 3 (Figure 2C). By day 5, a significant increase was maintained only in the 1 μM CPX condition, whereas other doses no longer showed significant differences relative to controls (Figure 2D). Collectively, these data indicate that CPX treatment enhances IL-2 expression and secretion in activated human T cells. CPX suppresses T cell effector functions

Upon activation, naive T cells differentiate into effector cells characterized by the production of inflammatory cytokines and cytotoxic granules.48 We next investigated whether CPX modulates T cell effector functions. To this end, we examined the production of IFN-γ, a pleiotropic cytokine that plays a critical role in shaping inflammatory responses.63 Secreted IFN-γ levels in the T cell culture supernatants as well as intracellular IFN-γ expression in activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells treated with CPX or left untreated, were measured on days 3 and 5 post-activation. In contrast to the increased IL-2 secretion observed with CPX treatment, IFN-γ levels in the culture supernatants were significantly reduced by CPX (Figure 4A). CPX temporally modulates Notch1 levels in activated T cells

In a previous study, we identified CPX as a potential modulator of Notch signaling in cancer cells.5 Notch1 is critical for T cell lineage commitment, as its absence in thymic progenitors impairs T cell development.67,68 In peripheral T cells, Notch signaling has been implicated in diverse effector functions.38,47 First, we assessed if CPX alters the surface expression of Notch receptors 1 and 2 in activated T cells and found that CPX treatment reduced surface Notch1 expression at day 2, with no significant effect observed at day 1, while Notch2 expression remained unchanged on both days (Figure S2). Based on these observations and given that Notch1 is highly expressed in activated T cells and plays an important role in T cell function,69 we examined if CPX alters Notch1 activation in T cells by measuring intracellular and intranuclear levels of NICD. However, by day 2, both intranuclear and intracellular levels of Notch1 NICD were significantly reduced by CPX in a dose-dependent manner relative to untreated controls (Figure 6B). Collectively, these findings indicate that CPX temporally modulates Notch1 activation in activated human T cells.

To determine whether the effects of CPX on T cell effector functions are mediated through canonical Notch signaling, we next employed the γ-secretase inhibitor dibenzazepine (DBZ), a well-established pharmacologic inhibitor of Notch activation.71 Accordingly, activated T cells were treated with DBZ (0, 0.062, 0.125, and 0.250 μM), and T cell effector functions were evaluated to determine whether inhibition of canonical Notch signaling recapitulates the effects of CPX. While DBZ did not inhibit the intranuclear Notch1 NICD levels on day 1, it induced a significant reduction in both intracellular and intranuclear Notch1 NICD by day 2 (Figure S3A). Notably, DBZ demonstrated a greater potency than CPX in suppressing Notch1 signaling as 0.062 μM DBZ reduced intranuclear NICD levels to an extent comparable to 1 μM CPX under similar conditions (Figure S3A). Furthermore, like CPX, DBZ (0.250 μM) also showed a decreasing trend in Ki-67 expression in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure S3B), suggesting reduced proliferative capacity following Notch inhibition.

We observed increased IL-2 production by T cells in response to CPX (Figure 2). Similarly, DBZ treatment also led to increased IL-2 expression in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells compared with untreated controls (Figure S4A), likely in response to reduced proliferation. We next examined whether DBZ recapitulates the effects of CPX on T cell effector responses. In contrast to CPX, which reduced IFN-γ production by activated T cells, DBZ treatment showed an increasing trend in IFN-γ expression in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure S4B). These findings suggest that CPX-mediated suppression of IFN-γ is not solely attributable to γ-secretase-dependent Notch inhibition. With respect to cytotoxic function, DBZ partially mimicked CPX as it significantly reduced granzyme B and modestly decreased perforin levels in CD8+ T cells (Figure S4C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DBZ does not fully reproduce the effector phenotype induced by CPX suggesting that CPX-mediated modulation of T cell effector function involves both Notch-dependent and Notch-independent mechanisms.

CPX alters cellular metabolism of activated human T cells

Upon antigenic stimulation, naive T cells dramatically upregulate glycolysis to meet the bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands of proliferation, effector function, and differentiation.72,73 To determine whether CPX alters T cell metabolism, we performed glycolysis stress test to measure extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and mitochondrial stress test to determine oxygen consumption rate (OCR) using seahorse assay. Based on our findings, we treated activated T cells with 1 μM CPX, the concentration that showed the most pronounced effects and compared it to untreated (0 μM) cells. CPX-treated T cells exhibited significantly lower ECAR than untreated T cells indicating a reduction in glycolytic activity (Figure 7A). Both glycolysis and glycolytic capacity decreased in activated T cells following CPX treatment (Figure 7B). Consistently, gene expression of LDHA (lactate dehydrogenase A), a central enzyme of aerobic glycolysis,74 was lower in CPX-treated T cells relative to untreated controls (Figure 7C). In contrast, CPX-treated T cells demonstrated higher OCR than untreated controls (Figure 7D), with modest increase in basal respiration and maximal respiration (Figure 7E) indicative of enhanced OXPHOS in the treated cells. Consistently, ATP production also showed an increasing trend in CPX-treated T cells (Figure 7F). Discussion

CPX is an FDA-approved topical antifungal agent and potent chelator of iron and other metals.2,8 Beyond its antifungal use, CPX has shown therapeutic potential in preclinical models of diabetes, cancer, inflammatory disorders, and cardiovascular disease.9,10,13,27,28,75 However, its clinical utility via oral administration is limited by dose-limiting gastrointestinal toxicity, poor bioavailability, and low water solubility, which hinder formulation as an injectable.5,20

In a first-in-human phase I clinical trial (NCT00990587), patients with relapsed or refractory hematologic malignancies received oral CPX olamine (5–80 mg/m2/day for five days in 21-day cycles), with gastrointestinal toxicity emerging as the dose-limiting adverse effect at 80 mg/m2.20 To address these challenges, Weir et al. developed a water-soluble prodrug, fosciclopirox (CPX-POM), a phosphoryloxymethyl ester of CPX, which is rapidly and completely converted to active CPX in mice, rats, dogs, and humans.15 CPX exhibited in vitro anticancer activity in multiple human high-grade urothelial cancer cell lines, while fosciclopirox demonstrated in vivo efficacy in preclinical bladder cancer models.5 Fosciclopirox has been evaluated in a phase 1 dose-escalation trial for advanced solid tumors (NCT03348514), where it was well tolerated and achieved sufficient systemic, tumor, and urinary CPX exposure.31 Based on these encouraging findings, phase 2 safety and efficacy trials are planned to confirm and expand these results in non-muscle-invasive and muscle-invasive bladder cancer patients (NCT04608045; NCT04525131). These clinical advances highlight the importance of understanding the immunomodulatory potential of the active metabolite, CPX, particularly in T cells, which are key regulators of adaptive immune responses.76

In this study, we investigated the effects of CPX on the activation-driven expansion and effector functions of human T cells. CPX has been widely reported to suppress proliferation across multiple cancer types;5,53,77 yet, its effect on primary T cell proliferation is unknown. We observed that CPX-treated activated T cells exhibited reduced cell counts without a significant loss in viability, indicating that CPX likely limits expansion of activated T cells. This phenotype in CPX-treated T cells was accompanied by reduced expression of proliferation marker Ki-67 and decreased transcript levels of c-myc (gene MYC), a key regulator of metabolic reprogramming and clonal expansion in activated lymphocytes.56 Furthermore, cell cycle analysis revealed an increased frequency of CPX-treated T cells in the G1-phase and a corresponding reduction in S-phase, indicative of impaired G1-S transition. These observations align with prior reports demonstrating CPX-induced G1 arrest in various cancer cell types.5,17 Notably, we also detected an increased proportion of cells in G2/M-phase, raising the possibility of an additional checkpoint arrest at this stage by CPX. Together, these findings support a model in which CPX limits cell cycle progression and expansion in activated human T cells. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this phenotype are yet to be elucidated.

We next examined the production of IL-2, a key autocrine cytokine that drives T cell growth, proliferation, and survival.58,59 Based on the reduced T cell expansion observed with CPX treatment, we initially expected diminished IL-2 production. Unexpectedly, CPX significantly increased IL-2 secretion and expression by activated T cells. Furthermore, the expression of IL-2Rα (CD25) was also elevated in CPX-treated T cells. One possible explanation for the discrepancy between increased IL-2 production and reduced proliferation is that elevated IL-2 may represent a compensatory response to support proliferation or survival of CPX-treated cells. Alternatively, CPX likely impairs the proliferative capacity of T cells downstream of IL-2 signaling, potentially by interfering with cell cycle progression or metabolic pathways58,78,79 Together, these findings support a model in which CPX limits T cell expansion independently of IL-2 production, but further studies are required to delineate the precise molecular mechanisms.

We investigated the effect of CPX on the effector functions of T cells including cytokine production and expression of cytotoxic granules. IFN-γ, a proinflammatory cytokine secreted by both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells80,81 was significantly reduced in the culture supernatants of CPX-treated T cells. Intracellular IFN-γ levels also decreased in CD4+ T cells and to a lesser extent in CD8+ T cells, suggesting that CPX potentially suppresses the effector cytokine responses of activated T cells. Further, CPX treatment also dampened the expression of cytotoxic molecules, granzyme B and perforin, produced by effector CD8+ T cells for direct cell lysis.66,82 Furthermore, our findings indicate that CPX suppresses key effector functions of activated human T cells, as evidenced by reduced expression of IFN-γ, granzyme B, and perforin. From a therapeutic perspective, while such immunomodulatory effects could be detrimental in contexts requiring robust T cell responses (e.g., infections, cancer immunotherapy, etc.), they may be beneficial in settings of excessive immune activation, such as autoimmunity or transplant rejection. Additional studies are required to assess whether CPX also impacts inflammatory and cytolytic functions of T cells in vivo.

Previously in Weir et al.,5 we reported that CPX modulates Notch signaling in bladder cancer cell lines. We sought to determine whether CPX similarly influences Notch activation in human T cells. While CPX induced a small increase in activated Notch1 (NICD) early on, it significantly reduced surface Notch1 as well as Notch1 NICD at day 2 post activation, indicating a temporal regulation of canonical Notch signaling in activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. To further investigate the contribution of CPX-modulated canonical Notch signaling in the observed phenotype, we used the well-characterized γ-secretase inhibitor DBZ,83,84 which potently inhibits Notch signaling as confirmed in our study. DBZ partially recapitulated CPX-associated phenotypes while exerting a more potent inhibition of Notch1 activation. Like CPX, DBZ reduced granzyme B expression and modestly increased IL-2 levels. However, in contrast to CPX, DBZ increased IFN-γ expression and modestly altered perforin levels. These findings indicate that while canonical Notch signaling contributes to aspects of the CPX-mediated effects on T cell proliferation and cytotoxic differentiation, the modulation of T cell effector functions by CPX cannot be explained solely by γ-secretase-dependent Notch inhibition. While overlap between CPX and DBZ suggests a plausible shared Notch-associated mechanism, further studies will shed light on the relative contributions of Notch-dependent and independent pathways driving these phenotypes.

Metabolic reprogramming is a critical driver of T cell effector functions, including proliferation, production of cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ) and cytotoxicity.72,73,85 CPX has been shown to alter cellular metabolism in various biological contexts.16,17,86,87 We examined its effects on metabolism of activated human T cells by seahorse analysis and found that CPX markedly suppresses glycolysis, a metabolic pathway essential for sustaining T cell expansion and effector responses.88 The suppression of glycolysis likely underlies the reduced proliferation, decreased cytotoxic granule production, and lowered IFN-γ secretion we observed with CPX treatment of T cells. In contrast, CPX modestly increased OCR and ATP production, suggesting enhanced mitochondrial activity in activated T cells, possibly as a compensatory adaptation to reduced glycolysis, as reported previously in T cells under nutrient or signaling stress.89

In summary, our findings uncover a previously unrecognized effect of the antifungal drug CPX on the expansion and effector responses of activated human T cells. We demonstrate that CPX reduces the proliferation, attenuates effector cytokines, and modulates Notch1 activation, while paradoxically enhancing activation marker expression, IL-2 secretion and altering metabolic program in primary human T cells upon activation. These effects highlight the need to carefully evaluate T cell functional outcomes when considering the repurposing of CPX or related derivatives, such as fosciclopirox, for cancer therapy or other immune-mediated diseases.

Limitations of the study

This study has a few limitations to consider. In this study, we did not investigate the in vivo effects of fosciclopirox, the prodrug of CPX, on T cell responses, which will be critical to establishing the translational relevance of these findings. Although our data demonstrate correlations between reduced proliferation, altered cell cycle, increased IL-2 expression, Notch1 activation, and reduced glycolysis, the precise molecular mechanisms driving these outcomes remain to be fully elucidated. This study focused primarily on Notch1 and did not comprehensively evaluate other Notch receptors or downstream transcriptional programs that may contribute to the temporal modulation of NICD levels observed in activated human T cells.5 Whether CPX broadly alters Notch pathway or selectively targets Notch1-dependent signaling remains yet to be determined. Lastly, the human blood donor cohort in this study was limited in both sex and ethnic diversity, which may not fully represent the broader population, affecting the generalizability and broader applicability of our findings. Addressing these gaps will be important to fully delineate the immunomodulatory potential of CPX and guide its safe and effective therapeutic application.

All procedures adhered to regulatory standards for human subject protection, including CFR 45 Part 46, HIPAA guidelines, and approval by the KUMC Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Method details

In vitro human T cells isolation and stimulation

Human whole blood samples in EDTA tubes from healthy donors were provided by The University of Kansas Cancer Center’s Biospecimen Core repository (BRCF). From the donor samples, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using Ficoll-paque (Cytiva) and Sepmate tubes (STEMCELL TECHNOLOGIES) as demonstrated by Cui et al.96 and Tripathi et al.97 Following PBMC isolation, human T cells were purified using the EasySep human T cell isolation kit (STEMCELL TECHNOLOGIES) as per the manufacturer’s protocol.

Isolated human T cells (1 × 106 cells/ml) were stimulated using plate-bound anti-CD3 antibody (5 μg/mL; BioLegend) and soluble anti-CD28 (1 μg/mL; BioLegend) in RPMI-1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 1X Penicillin/Streptomycin/Glutamine solution (Cytiva), and β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco). 3 × 105 cells/well were seeded in a 96 well plate for each condition (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2 μM CPX) or (0, 0.062, 0.125, 0.250 μM DBZ) and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for an indicated amount of time. Cells were split on day 4 and day 6 for in vitro T cell culture experiments.

Drug treatment

Ciclopirox olamine (molecular weight 268.35 g/mol; CAS 41621-49-2; IUPAC Name: 2-aminoethanol;6-cyclohexyl-1-hydroxy-4-methylpyridin-2-one;

C14H24N2O3) was selected as the test drug for the studies described herein. Addition of CPX-O to cell culture media results in the dissociation of olamine salt from ciclopirox (CPX). Ciclopirox (CPX) (IUPAC Name: 6-cyclohexyl-1-hydroxy-4-methylpyridin-2-one;

C12H17NO2) was selected for the in vitro studies described herein (rather than the prodrug fosciclopirox) for three reasons. First, fosciclopirox lacks pharmacologic activity in vitro. Second, fosciclopirox is rapidly and completely metabolized in vivo to CPX following intravenous administration to mice, rats, dogs, and humans as reported by Weir et al.15 Third, the in vitro models employed for these studies lack phosphatases necessary to metabolize fosciclopirox to CPX.

Additionally, Dibenzazepine (DBZ; molecular weight 463.48 g/mol; MedChem Express; CAS 209984-56-5; IAPUC Name: (2S)-2-[[2-(3,5-difluorophenyl)acetyl]amino]-N-(5-methyl-6-oxo-7H-benzo[d][1]benzazepin-7-yl)propenamide; C26H23F2N3O3), a γ-secretase inhibitor, was used in parallel to determine whether the observed phenotype was associated with γ-secretase inhibition.

Flow cytometry

Approximately 0.3–0.9 x 106 cells per sample were resuspended in 100 μL of FACS buffer (1X PBS, pH 7.2–7.4 + 1% FBS) and stained with human fluorophore-conjugated antibodies at a 1:400 dilution in FACS buffer for 30 min at 4°C in dark. Yellow fixable dye was used at a 1:500 dilution in FACS with the same staining conditions. The antibodies used were as follows but not limited to: anti-human CD3 Alexa Fluor 700 (Clone: OKT3, BioLegend), anti-human CD8 BV650 (RPA-T8, BioLegend), anti-human CD4 APC-Cy7 (OKT4, BioLegend), anti-human CD69 PE-Cy7 (FN50, BioLegend), anti-human CD25 APC (Clone: BC96, BioLegend), anti-human CD44 FITC (Clone: IM7, BioLegend), and Yellow Fixable Dye (Invitrogen). Following this incubation, cells were washed twice in FACS buffer, centrifuged at 500 ×g and resuspended in 200 μL of FACS buffer. Samples were acquired in the Cytek Aurora Flow Cytometer.

For intranuclear staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription buffer staining kit (Invitrogen). After permeabilization, cells were washed with 1X permeabilization buffer and stained for NOTCH1 PE (mN1A Invitrogen, 1:100 dilution in 1X permeabilization buffer) and Ki-67 PerCP (SolA15, Invitrogen, 1:800 dilution in 1X permeabilization buffer) for 45 min at room temperature (22°C–25°C) in the dark. Cells were then washed in the 1X permeabilization buffer followed by a wash in FACS buffer, centrifuged at 500 ×g and resuspended in 200 μL FACS buffer for flow cytometry acquisition. For intracellular Notch1 staining, cells were fixed using IC Fixation Buffer (Invitrogen) and permeabilized according to the manufacturer’s protocol, stained with NOTCH1 PE (mN1A Invitrogen, 1:100 dilution), and all subsequent steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

For intracellular cytokine staining, approximately 0.9–1 × 106 cells were stimulated with/without ionomycin (Sigma Aldrich, 750 ng/mL), phorbol 12-myristate 13 acetate (PMA, Sigma-Aldrich, 50 ng/mL), Brefaldin A (BioLegend, 1:1000), and 2-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, 1:1000) for 4 h at 37°C. Cells were then washed with 1X FACS buffer, centrifuged at 500 ×g and stained for surface marker antibodies (CD4, CD8, CD3, Live-Dead Yellow) for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. After staining, cells were washed with FACS buffer, centrifuged at 500 ×g and fixed using Cytofix (BD Biosciences) for 10 min in dark at room temperature (22°C–25°C). Following permeabilization, cells were washed and centrifuged at 500 ×g using 1X BD Wash/Perm buffer (BD Bioscience) and stained using 1:200 dilution for anti-human IL-2 APC (MQ1-17H12, BioLegend), anti-human IFN-γ PE (4S.B3, BioLegend), anti-human Granzyme B FITC (GB11, BioLegend), anti-human Perforin APC (dG9, BioLegend) in 1X permeabilization wash/buffer for an hour at 4°C in the dark. Cells were then washed in 1X permeabilization wash/buffer, centrifuged at 500 ×g and subsequently 2X with FACS buffer. Cells were resuspended in the 200 μL FACS buffer for flow cytometry acquisition.

An in vitro BrdU incorporation assay was performed to assess cell proliferation. Activated T cells (48 h post-activation) were harvested and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with BrdU at a final concentration of 10 μM (10 μL of 1 mM stock per mL of culture medium) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. On day 3, cells were harvested and stained for BrdU and 7-AAD using the BrdU Flow Kit (BD Biosciences) following the manufacturer’s protocol and samples were acquired in the Cytek Aurora Flow Cytometer.

For all experiments, single-color reference controls were prepared using unstained cells (Unstained, Yellow Fixable Dye), and Cytek FSP CompBeads for spectral unmixing. Data were acquired on Cytek Aurora flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo (software version 10.10.0; Becton, Dickinson and Company). Flow cytometry histograms are shown as “% of max,” where each dataset’s highest peak is normalized to 100%, allowing for comparison of distribution patterns independent of total event count.

Seahorse assays

Human T cells were isolated from healthy donors and 1 × 106 cells/well were seeded in 48 well plate a for each condition (0 and 1 μM CPX) and cultured in vitro for 72 h as described above. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured using the Seahorse XF96 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA). For OCR measurements, cells were washed twice with FACS buffer and resuspended in XF Base Media (Agilent Technologies) and supplemented with 2 mM L-Glutamine (Agilent Technologies) and 1 mM Sodium Pyruvate (Agilent Technologies), 10 mM D-Glucose (Agilent Technologies). For ECAR measurements, cells were washed twice with FACS buffer and resuspended in XF Base Media (Agilent Technologies) and supplemented with 2 mM L-Glutamine (Agilent Technologies). 5 x 105 cells per well were plated equally on XFe96 culture plates (Agilent Technologies) in Agilent DMEM XF Base media, the plate was briefly centrifuged at 500 ×g for 1 min to settle the cells at the bottom of the plate. Cells were incubated for 30 min in a non-CO2 incubator. OCR was measured by sequential addition of 1 μM oligomycin (Sigma), 1 μM carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP; Sigma), 1 μM of antimycin A (Sigma) and rotenone (Sigma) injections. ECAR was measured by the sequential addition of 10 mM D-glucose (Agilent Technologies), 2 μM oligomycin (Sigma) and 100 mM 2-deoxyglucose (Sigma) injections. Data were analyzed using Seahorse Wave/Wave Pro desktop (Agilent Technologies) software by generating mitochondrial stress test and glycolysis stress reports for OCR and ECAR. Graphs were plotted using GraphPad Prism software.

ELISA

On days 3 and 5, culture supernatant from activated human T cells treated with 0, 1, 2 μM CPX were collected. IL-2 (BioLegend) and IFN-γ (BioLegend) levels were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader.

RNA isolation and qPCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from activated human T cells using the Macherey-Nagel NucleoSpin RNA kit (Fisher Scientific, NC9581114) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For qPCR analysis, total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using an M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase kit (Fisher Scientific, 28025013) following the manufacturer’s protocol. qPCR was performed using Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Applied Biosystems, A25742). Gene expression levels were normalized to adjusted ACTB mRNA using the standard ΔΔCt method. A complete list of qPCR primers used in this study is provided in the Key Resources Table under the oligonucleotide section.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical tests and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software) unless otherwise specified. Schematics were generated using bioRender software. Each n number represents an individual healthy blood donor. It is mentioned in the figure legends where technical replicates were used. Two-tailed paired t-tests were used for comparing untreated (0 μM) and CPX (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2 μM) or DBZ (0.062, 0.125, 0.250 μM) treated T cells. For cell counts, two-way ANOVA was performed followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparison. Data represented as mean ± SEM and p-values are represented as follows as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
