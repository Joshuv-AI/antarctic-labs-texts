HGMT: a database of human gut microbiota for tumors and immunotherapy response
Authors: Jinxin Liu, Mingyu Wang, Chentao Xu, Longhao Jia, Senying Lai, Zi-Chao Zhang, Jinglong Zhang, Wei-Hua Chen, Yucheng T Yang, Xing-Ming Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHGMT is a database designed to analyze, explore, and visualize gut microbiomes from diverse tumor types. We process metagenomic datasets from 18,630 stool samples across 37 tumor types, including 2,207 samples from immunotherapy-treated patients across 12 tumor types. HGMT provides an interactive portal for querying taxonomic and functional profiles, visualizing cross-dataset differential abundance taxa in tumors, and identifying their pan-tumor associations. Our analysis reveals the capability of gut microbiota in diagnosing gastrointestinal tumors and predicting immunotherapy response for non-small cell lung carcinoma. HGMT represents a valuable resource for investigating the roles of gut microbiota in tumors and immunotherapy response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-025-03865-3.Keywords: Gut microbiome, Tumor diagnosis, Immunotherapy response

Background

Microbes are able to colonize almost all human body sites [1, 2]. They primarily reside in the gut system on a massive scale, named the gut microbiota, where their quantity and complexity amplify progressively from the stomach to the colon [3]. Gut microbiota dysbiosis manifests as the disturbed balance between the microbial community and the human host [4], including changes in taxonomic composition, metabolic products and secretory vesicles. Such dysbiosis has been associated with multiple human diseases, including intestinal [5–7], autoimmune [8, 9], metabolic [10, 11], neurological and mental [12–14] diseases.

In recent years, the roles of microbiota in the development of tumors and therapeutic response have gained increasing attention [15–20]. The studies in this field were generally performed based on two distinct types of biosamples [21]. Some studies focus on the gut microbiota in stool samples from tumor patients, while others focus on the intratumoral microbiota derived from tumor tissue samples. Previous studies have revealed that the gut microbiota from tumor patients could influence both local and distant tumor progression by modulating the host immune system [22–24]. In contrast, the intratumoral microbiota residing within tumor/immune cells and tumor microenvironment could potentially modulate tumor progression and antitumor immunity [25–30]. Notably, it is difficult to accurately identify and quantify the intratumoral microbiota in tumor tissue samples. In current studies, the unmapped reads against the human genome and transcriptome [31–34] were leveraged for characterizing the intratumoral microbiota given the extremely low microbial biomass in the tumor samples [35–38]. The computational challenges have fueled ongoing debates regarding the validity of intratumoral microbiota signals [35, 39–41]. In this study, we focus on the human gut microbiome from tumor patients by analyzing the metagenomic shotgun sequencing (WGS), 16S ribosomal RNA genes (16S) and Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) sequencing datasets [21].

The putative associations between gut microbiota and host and their roles in tumor diagnosis [42–46], prognosis [47–52] and targeted therapy [53, 54] have been explored in individual tumor types. However, the systematic characterization of the associations between gut microbiota and tumor diagnosis and prognosis across multiple tumor types is still lacking. In recent years, several large-scale initiatives, such as the Human Microbiome Project [55] and MetaHIT [56], have generated valuable data resources for studying gut microbiome implicated in human diseases. In addition, some public databases, such as HMDAD [57], Disbiome [58], gutMDisorder [59], MicroPhenoDB [60], GMrepo [61, 62], mBodyMap [63], HGMA [64] and curatedMetagenomicData [65] R package have been constructed for exploring the associations between microbes and human diseases or interventions. However, these existing data resources generally focus on the bacterial signatures in common diseases and only cover limited types of tumor.

In this study, we develop HGMT (Human Gut Microbiota across Tumor types) to characterize the human gut microbiota across 37 tumor types, and analyze the associations between gut microbiota and tumor diagnosis and prognosis. In total, we process 18,630 stool samples in 111 projects using our uniform pipeline. In particular, we include 2207 samples collected from the individuals treated with immunotherapy. We provide curated metadata for each sample, including country, sex, age, body mass index (BMI) and disease information. We also annotate multi-kingdom taxonomic and functional profiles using state-of-the-art toolsets. The user-friendly interface of HGMT allows users to explore differential abundance taxa across datasets under personalized settings. We further quantitatively evaluate the capability of gut microbiota for tumor diagnosis and immunotherapy response prediction. We hope that HGMT will provide novel insights into the host-microbiome interactions in tumors.

Construction and content

Data collection

We systematically searched NCBI BioProject database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject) for recently published projects using keywords, including ‘human metagenomics and tumor’, ‘tumor and microbiome’, ‘tumor and 16S’, ‘tumor and metagenomics’ (Fig. 1A). This search retrieved a total of 1,053 projects (Additional file 1: Table S1). All projects were further filtered based on the following criteria: (i) availability of clearly defined phenotype information; (ii) provision of raw sequencing data from human stool samples; (iii) inclusion of more than four tumor patients; and (iv) no medical treatments prior to immunotherapy.

B Data processing and taxonomic/functional assignment. Read-based toolsets were used for taxonomic profiling, and assembly-based toolsets were used for functional profiling. The relative abundance of taxa (i.e., genus or species) and KEGG Orthologs (KOs) across samples were calculated. C Quality control for results. We retained runs with more than two taxa (relative abundance ) as ‘processed runs’ for the subsequent analysis. A higher score indicates a stronger association between the corresponding tumor and the alterations of gut microbiota

Raw sequencing reads from 18,630 human stool samples across 111 projects (Additional file 1: Table S2) were downloaded from NCBI SRA database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) using SRA-Tools (https://github.com/ncbi/sra-tools). For each sample, the corresponding metadata were also retrieved from the SRA database. These metadata contained technical details, including the sequencing platform, assay type (i.e., sequencing technology) and library layout, as well as sample characteristics, including country, sex, age, BMI and host disease status. For individuals undergoing immunotherapy, treatment-related information was additionally collected, including immunotherapy response category, progression-free survival, and overall survival. The metadata were then manually curated and supplemented with information extracted from the original publications. Tumor types reported in these publications were uniformly classified according to MeSH terminology [66] (Additional file 1: Table S3).

Taxonomic assignment

Within each project, the samples were divided into batches according to assay type (e.g., 16S, WGS, ITS) and library layout type (e.g., paired-end and single-end). We performed quality assessment of all sequencing reads using FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/).

For the 16S and ITS sequencing datasets (Fig. 1B), we used the QIIME2 pipeline (version 2021.4) [67] to estimate the microbial taxonomic profiles. Raw reads were denoised into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using DADA2 (version 1.18.0) [68]. We then performed taxonomic assignment by classifying the ASVs against Greengenes2 [69, 70] and UNITE (version 10.0) [71, 72] database for the 16S and ITS sequencing data, respectively. Finally, the relative abundance (total sum scaling, TSS) of recognizable genera in each sample was calculated. This approach could minimize biases derived from the variable library size and sequencing depth, thereby enabling fair comparisons across samples.

For the WGS sequencing datasets (Fig. 1B), we first removed host-derived reads by mapping the reads to the human reference genome (hg38) using Bowtie2 [73]. Trimmomatic [74] was utilized to trim adapter sequences and remove low-quality nucleotides, and the reads shorter than 50 or 75 bps after trimming were discarded. The remaining reads could be considered as ‘clean reads’, which were subsequently used for microbial profiling. The bacterial taxonomic profiles were generated with MetaPhlAn4 (version 4.0.6) [75] using mpa_vOct22_CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202212 database and default parameters. The fungal classifications were assigned with Kraken2 (version 2.1.2) [76] using fungal reference genomes from NCBI RefSeq database (accessed in April 2023), and taxonomic relative abundances were further refined with Bracken (version 2.8) [77]. Finally, the relative abundance of recognizable taxa across clades from phylum to species in each sample was estimated. In this study, downstream analyses for the WGS sequencing data were performed at the genus or species level unless stated.

Functional assignment of WGS sequencing data

For the WGS sequencing data, the clean reads were assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT (version 1.2.9) [78] with ‘meta-sensitive’ parameters, and contigs shorter than 500 bp were discarded. Previous studies have revealed that assembling contigs from short reads could mitigate the fragmentation of genetic information and improve the accuracy of gene prediction and functional annotation [79–81]. We then performed gene prediction with Prodigal (version 2.6.3) [82] using the metagenome mode (-p meta). CD-HIT (version 4.8.1) [83] was applied to construct a non-redundant microbial gene reference with a sequence identity threshold of 0.95 and minimum coverage of 0.9. Functional annotation of the non-redundant microbial gene catalog was carried out using EggNOG-mapper (version 2.1.0) [84] against the EggNOG orthology database. The abundance of microbial gene family was estimated using CoverM (version 0.4.0, https://github.com/wwood/CoverM) by mapping clean reads to the non-redundant microbial gene reference. Finally, we estimated the relative abundance of annotated KEGG Orthologs (KOs) in each sample (Fig. 1B).

Tumor ranking based on bacterial taxonomic profiles

The runs containing more than two different genera (relative abundance ) were retained as ‘processed runs’ and assigned (Fig. 1C). The aforementioned processed batches were subsequently divided into different differential abundance (DA) batches for between-group comparisons of microbial signatures. For immunotherapy response prediction, tumor patients were further extracted from the DA batches corresponding to tumor diagnosis. Each DA batch was restricted to one tumor type, and required samples in both the case and control groups. Unless otherwise specified, tumor ranking analyses of WGS and 16S datasets were performed using genus-level bacterial profiles.

Alpha-diversity (within-sample diversity) was computed based on the Shannon Index (Fig. 1D) using the diversity function from the vegan R package. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was applied to evaluate between-group differences in alpha-diversity within each DA batch. Beta-diversity (between-sample diversity) was calculated based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity (Fig. 1D) using the vegdist function. The PERMANOVA test was performed to investigate between-group microbial community differences with 999 permutations using the adonis function.

Subsequently, differential abundance analysis was conducted for each taxon within each DA batch (Fig. 1D). To mitigate noise from low-abundance taxa, only those present in at least 10% of samples (relative abundance ) were retained. Potential confounding factors within each DA batch were identified using Fisher’s exact test for qualitative variables (e.g., country and sex) and Wilcoxon rank sum test for quantitative variables (e.g., age and BMI). Factors with were adjusted as covariates in the subsequent DA test. Candidate differentially abundant taxa were then identified using ANCOM-BC [85], which corrects for the compositional effects-induced bias and adjusts for confounding factors. Finally, taxa with were considered significant candidate differential abundance taxa. Moreover, values between case and control groups were calculated to quantify the magnitude and direction of enrichment.

To determine the classifying ability of gut microbiota in tumor diagnosis and immunotherapy response prediction, we constructed classification models using LASSO in the SIAMCAT [86] R package, as described in our previous study [43]. Model performance was assessed via fivefold cross-validation repeated 10 times, with results summarized by the area under the curve (AUC) values (Fig. 1D).

Finally, we quantitatively evaluated the potential of gut microbiota in tumor diagnosis and immunotherapy response prediction by developing the GMTD (Gut Microbiota for Tumor Diagnosis) score and GMTP (Gut Microbiota for Tumor Prognosis) score, respectively. These two scores could integrate the findings from four analytical scenarios (i.e., alpha-diversity, beta-diversity, differential abundance analysis, and classification model) into a composite value ranging from 0 to 20 (Fig. 1D). Each scenario contributed a sub-score from 0 to 5, reflecting the magnitude of differences between case and control groups. Specifically, (i) alpha-diversity was scored based on the value of the Wilcoxon rank sum test; (ii) beta-diversity was assessed using the value and explained variance of the PERMANOVA test; (iii) differential abundance analysis was scored according to the number of significant taxa; and (iv) the classification model was evaluated based on the AUC value. A higher GMTD score suggests a stronger diagnostic capability of the gut microbiota for tumor detection, while a higher GMTP score reflects a greater predictive power for immunotherapy response.

Utility and discussion

Overview of HGMT

We uniformly processed 18,630 sequencing runs from 111 projects across 37 tumor types (Fig. 1A; Fig. 2A), including 11,356 16S, 7205 WGS, and 69 ITS datasets (Additional file 1: Table S2). For each sample, we manually curated the metadata (Additional file 1: Table S2), including both technical and biological information. Four variables, i.e., country (82.39%), age (66.3%), sex (65.29%) and BMI (50.14%), were considered as potential confounding factors for differential abundance analysis (Fig. 2B). Only 3.9% of the samples contained all four variables, while 42.12% contained none of them, and the rest contained one (14.04%), two (13.57%) or three (26.37%) variables (Fig. 2C). These distributions are comparable to other existing human microbiome-disease datasets [61, 63], underscoring the challenges of reusing metagenomic data and the necessity for standardized guidelines in metadata collection and processing. Notably, we processed 2207 runs with available immunotherapy response information, including 584 16S and 1623 WGS datasets across 12 tumor types (Fig. 2D). These stool samples were collected before immunotherapy treatment, with the majority derived from patients with non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC), followed by melanoma (MEL). We anticipate that HGMT will serve as a valuable data resource for advancing the studies on the gut microbiota and highlighting its biomedical significance in predicting tumor immunotherapy response [47, 50, 52, 87, 88].

After completing taxonomic profiling and functional profiling for each sample, we performed quality control on the results (Fig. 1C). In total, 17,391 passed quality control and were retained as ‘processed runs’, which were then grouped into different DA batches. Specifically, 75 DA batches across 20 tumor types (Additional file 1: Table S4) and 17 DA batches across 4 tumor types (Additional file 1: Table S5) were further applied to assess the potential of gut microbiota in tumor diagnosis and immunotherapy response prediction (Fig. 1D), respectively.

Compared to other similar published databases focusing on gut microbial alterations from tumor patients (Additional file 1: Table S6), HGMT shows several key improvements: (i) covering a broader range of tumor types; (ii) integrating metagenomic data generated by different assays (i.e., 16S, WGS and ITS); (iii) providing multi-kingdom taxonomic profiles (i.e., bacteria and fungi) and functional profiles (i.e., KOs); (iv) evaluating the capability of gut microbiota in tumor diagnosis and immunotherapy response prediction.

Identification of healthy-enriched differential abundance genera in multiple tumor types

We first performed differential abundance analysis between patients and healthy controls using 75 DA batches across 20 tumor types (Additional file 1: Table S4), aiming to identify consistent gut microbial associations with tumors. The majority of DA batches were from gastrointestinal tract (GI) tumors. In total, we identified 319 tumor diagnosis markers (Additional file 1: Table S7). To highlight pan-tumor associations, we focused on genera showing differential abundance in more than four tumor types, and grouped them into five clusters based on W statistics from the differential abundance analysis (Fig. 3A). Notably, 13 genera from cluster 3 and cluster 4 were pervasively enriched in healthy controls across multiple tumor types (Additional file 1: Table S8) and were considered pan-tumor associated genera. Among these, Lachnospira and Anaerostipes showed significantly differential abundance in 13 and 11 tumor types, respectively (Fig. 3A; Additional file 1: Table S8). The two bacteria, together with Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Coprococcus, Anaerostipes, Blautia and Fusicatenibacter have been revealed as favorable genera in multiple tumor types [15]. Of the 13 genera, 12 genera belonged to the Bacillota (Firmicutes) phylum and eight genera belonged to the Lachnospiraceae family, which are consistent with the reported roles of Firmicutes and Lachnospiraceae in promoting host health and favorable immune responses [30, 52, 89]. Moreover, all 13 genera are gram-positive bacteria capable of producing short-chain fatty acids, e.g., butyrate and propionate. These metabolites are known to enhance regulatory T cell differentiation, suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines, and modulate antigen-presenting cell function [4, 19, 29, 90], thereby engaging both pro- and anti-tumorigenic pathways within the context of host-microbiome interactions.

We next identified consistent gut microbial associations with immunotherapy response using the 17 DA bathes across four tumor types (Additional file 1: Table S5). From 94 markers for immunotherapy response prediction, we extracted genera showing differential abundance in more than two tumor types (Additional file 1: Table S9), and further grouped them into two clusters (Fig. 3B). Six genera from cluster 1 were pervasively enriched in responders (Additional file 1: Table S10). Among them, genera Faecalibacterium and Roseburia have been associated with favorable responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) across multiple tumor types [50, 51, 91, 92], and are known to boost ICI efficacy [93, 94]. For example, Roseburia intestinalis could inhibit tumorigenesis through butyrate production, which also enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy by promoting functional CD8⁺ T cell responses [93]. As expected, all 94 markers for immunotherapy response prediction were significantly enriched in all 319 tumor diagnosis markers (, Fisher’s exact test), and the six responder-enriched genera were significantly enriched in the 13 healthy-enriched genera (, Fisher’s exact test) (Fig. 3C). The overlapping genera (i.e., Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Fusicatenibacter) are well-known favorable genera [15], capable of producing beneficial metabolites that support gut health and exert anti-inflammatory effects [95].

In summary, we identified 13 healthy-enriched differential abundance genera contributing to good outcomes across multiple tumor types (Fig. 3A) and geographical regions (Additional file 1: Table S8 and S10). Extending the analysis to the species level, we confirmed similar pan-tumor associations (Additional file 1: Table S11-S14; Additional file 2: Fig. S1), reinforcing our genus-level findings. As expected, all 177 species-level markers for immunotherapy response prediction (Additional file 1: Table S13) were significantly enriched in all 350 tumor diagnosis markers (Additional file 1: Table S11) (, Fisher’s exact test), and 13 responder-enriched species (Additional file 1: Table S14; Additional file 2: Fig. S1B) were significantly enriched in the 10 healthy-enriched species (Additional file 1: Table S12; Additional file 2: Fig. S1A) (, Fisher’s exact test) (Additional file 2: Fig. S1C). The overlapping species (e.g., R. intestinalis [93] and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii [94]) could enhance anti-tumor immunity and improve ICI responses via the production of favorable metabolites and complex crosstalk with the host immune system. Our findings suggest that restoring the abundance of such favorable microbes may represent a promising therapeutic avenue in oncology through dietary interventions, probiotics, or fecal microbiota transplantation.

Identification and prioritization of tumor types associated with gut microbial alterations

The tumor ranking analysis incorporated multiple lines of evidence on between-group differences in microbial compositions. This framework provided a systematic approach to prioritize tumor types associated with gut microbial alterations, both in the context of tumor diagnosis (GMTD score) (Additional file 1: Table S15) and immunotherapy response prediction (GMTP score) (Additional file 1: Table S16) (Fig. 1D). We prioritized tumors using the 75th percentile of GMTD or GMTP scores across all DA batches for a given tumor type (Fig. 3D; Additional file 2: Fig. S2). We found that the diagnostic capability of gut microbiota was pervasively higher in GI cancer groups (e.g., stomach neoplasms and colorectal neoplasms), followed by benign GI tumor groups (e.g., adenomatous polyps) and then non-GI cancers (e.g., brain neoplasms and glioma) (Fig. 3D). These patterns were consistent with our previous study [43]. To further illustrate the utility of this ranking approach, we examined colorectal neoplasms (CRC) across 32 DA batches (Additional file 1: Table S15). For alpha-diversity, 11 out of the 32 DA batches showed significant differences between CRC and healthy controls (Shannon index; , Wilcoxon rank-sum test). For beta-diversity, nearly all batches (30 out of 32) exhibited significant differences (Bray–Curtis dissimilarity; , PERMANOVA test). These findings align with previous studies, further underscoring the strong associations between gut microbial diversity and CRC.

In the case of immunotherapy response prediction, gut microbiota in NSCLC patients had greater prognostic capability than MEL patients (Additional file 2: Fig. S2). However, gut microbiota in patients with renal cell carcinoma and GI tumors had limited prognostic capability.

Web interface and usage

HGMT provides a user-friendly and interactive portal for browsing tumor-related metagenomic data and visualizing cross-dataset differential abundance taxa (Fig. 4) (https://mai.fudan.edu.cn/hgmt). HGMT is organized into four main sections (i.e., Home, Explore, Data, Help), and each involves multiple query modes and result viewers. We briefly introduce each query mode below.

B A summary table of all DA batches in a specific tumor type, i.e., CRC. C The ‘tile view’ provides an interactive visualization of cross-dataset differential abundance taxa. D The ‘Data’ page shows the curated metadata of projects and runs in HGMT. E The available feature table in each batch, including taxonomic profiles (i.e., bacteria and fungi) and functional profiles (i.e., KOs). F The summary and visualization of differential abundance taxa for each DA batch in a specific project. These pages can be cross-searched to provide more detailed information. CRC, colorectal neoplasms

In the ‘Explore’ page, HGMT provides two major functions: (i) tumor ranking, which evaluates the capability of gut microbiota in tumor diagnosis and immunotherapy response prediction using GMTD and GMTP scores; (ii) cross-dataset comparison of differential abundance (DA) taxa within a specific tumor type (Fig. 4A-C). For example, users can access the ‘marker taxa’ page of colorectal neoplasms (CRC) (https://mai.fudan.edu.cn/hgmt/explore/D015179) by clicking the ‘view details’ button on the phenotype table (Fig. 4A). The query output shows a summary table of all CRC-related DA batches, reporting the number of DA genera or species (Fig. 4B). In addition, the ‘tile view’ module provides interactive visualization of cross-dataset DA taxa. Users can adjust parameters, including assay type, kingdom, taxon level, project cutoff and cutoff (Fig. 4C). By enabling the ‘Exclude conflicting markers’ option, users can observe consistent DA taxa with the same enrichment direction across datasets (Fig. 4C). Consistent with previous reports, disease-associated microbial DA markers are generally heterogeneous across studies, except for typical GI diseases, such as CRC [43, 45, 46]. Among 17 CRC-related DA batches, we identified 36 species showing consistently differential abundance between healthy controls and CRC (assay type = WGS, kingdom = Bacteria, , ) (Additional file 2: Fig. S3). These species included well-known CRC-enriched species, e.g., Fusobacterium nucleatum [96, 97], Parvimonas micra [98, 99] and Gemella morbillorum [45, 100], as well as healthy-enriched Faecalibacterium prausnitzii [101–103]. Of these, F. nucleatum and P. micra have been implicated in both tumor development [104–107] and therapy response [108–111].

The ‘Data’ page presents the manually curated metadata of metagenomic projects and runs, which can be directly downloaded (Fig. 4D). Additional links to external resources, including NCBI BioProject, NCBI SRA, PubMed/bioRxiv and NCBI MeSH Browsers, are available for each project, run, original publication and host phenotype, respectively. For example, users can search for PRJEB6070 in the project table via the navigation bar, and then click the corresponding entry for detailed information (Fig. 4D). Each project contains three blocks (Fig. 4E and F), i.e., project summary, associated runs, and marker taxa (https://mai.fudan.edu.cn/hgmt/data/PRJEB6070). The block of associated runs provides both the metadata of PRJEB6070 and annotated taxonomic and functional profiles of its processed runs (Fig. 4E). Users can also visualize the results of differential abundance analysis for each DA batch from PRJEB6070 (Fig. 4F). To ensure consistency and facilitate effective reuse of microbial data across sources, all microbial genera and species were systematically organized using their corresponding NCBI Taxonomy ID (Additional file 1: Table S17) [112].

Future directions

We anticipate that several future directions can be explored based on HGMT. First, many unprocessed metagenomic sequencing datasets from various human body sites except for stool (Additional file 1: Table S1) await processing and integration into HGMT. Second, higher-resolution profiling at the subspecies and strain levels will help clarify the roles of microbes in modulating tumor microenvironments and therapeutic efficacy [113–115]. Integrating taxonomic and functional profiles will further advance mechanistic insights into the gut microbiome-host interactions. In addition, more functions need to be developed in HGMT. For example, users may eventually upload their own data to estimate GMTD or GMTP scores, enabling direct comparison with our pan-tumor baseline results. Finally, multi-omics integration will be crucial for deciphering molecular mechanisms underlying microbial influences on tumorigenesis and treatment outcomes [21, 30, 116], especially interactions with the host immune system. For instance, we can acquire both gene expression profiles and microbial compositions from host bulk RNA-seq data [31, 32, 117], or high-resolution intratumoral microbial signatures from single-cell RNA-seq data [118–123] and spatial transcriptomics data [124]. Notably, the computational approaches used for studying the microbiota from the high-biomass (e.g., gut) samples cannot be directly applied to the low-biomass (e.g., tumor) samples. More efficient computational tools and gold standards for quantifying the low-biomass microbes in the sequencing datasets need to be established in the future.

Conclusions

Here, we develop HGMT, a database for studying human gut microbiota from tumor patients, and revealing the putative associations between gut microbiota and tumorigenesis and immunotherapy response. The current release of the HGMT database integrates 111 metagenomic datasets generated by three sequencing technologies from 18,630 stool samples across 37 tumor types. In particular, 2207 samples from 12 tumor types were collected from the individuals treated with immunotherapy. Besides taxonomic and functional annotations, HGMT evaluates the capability of gut microbiota for tumor diagnosis and immunotherapy response prediction, and provides a user-friendly web interface for easy data access and exploration. We hope that HGMT could be a valuable resource for the exploration of host-microbiome interactions across different tumor types, driving the translational investigations on immunotherapy for tumors based on gut metagenomes.

Data Availability Statement

HGMT is publicly available at https://mai.fudan.edu.cn/hgmt. Users can download data from the ‘6. How do I download the data?’ section of the ‘Help’ page in a faster way. All public gut metagenomic datasets used during the current study are available in Additional file 1. The HGMT source codes are freely available via GitHub (https://github.com/ZhaoXM-Lab/HGMT) [125] and Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15781163) [126] under the MIT License.
