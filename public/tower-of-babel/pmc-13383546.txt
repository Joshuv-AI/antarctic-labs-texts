The effect of stress ball application on anxiety and fetal well-being during non-stress testing in high-risk pregnant women: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Hümeyra Tülek Deni̇z, Nedime Gül Doğan Özdemi̇r, Nazlı Baltacı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effect of stress ball use during the non-stress test (NST) on anxiety and fetal well-being in high-risk pregnant women.MethodA randomized controlled pretest-posttest study was conducted with 86 high-risk pregnant women undergoing NST in a public hospital in northern Türkiye. Participants were randomly assigned to intervention (n = 43) and control (n = 43) groups. The intervention group used a stress ball during the NST procedure, while the control group received routine care. Data were collected using a Personal Information Form, the State Anxiety Inventory (SAI), and a Fetal Well-Being Assessment Form. Statistical analyses included t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, Chi-square tests, Wilcoxon tests, and two-way ANCOVA.ResultsThe intervention group had significantly lower posttest anxiety scores (35.56 ± 6.55) than the control group (40.05 ± 6.82) (p = 0.003), with a significant within-group decrease of 9.49 points (p = 0.000). Moreover, fetal movement count and basal fetal heart rate increased significantly in the intervention group compared with the control group (p = 0.002 and p = 0.005, respectively). These changes were also significant within the intervention group (p = 0.000).ConclusionStress ball use during NST may be associated with short-term improvements in fetal well-being indicators and maternal anxiety. It is recommended as a simple, low-cost, noninvasive, and drug-free intervention in prenatal care.Clinical trial numberNCT06302010 (Registration Date: 02.10.2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09235-6.Keywords: Non-stress test, Stress ball, Anxiety, Basal fetal heart rate, Fetal movement, Nursing

Introduction

High-risk pregnancy describes pregnancies in which the risk of complications in the mother or baby is increased compared to the general population (NICE, 2018). Although there is no single universal definition, the main aim of all international and national protocols is to ensure the early diagnosis of these pregnancies and their management in accordance with the risk level. These complications may include conditions such as premature birth, gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, intrauterine growth retardation, premature rupture of membranes, and placenta previa [1–4] NICE, 2018; WHO, 2017; AAP & ACOG, 2017, Turkish Ministry of (Health, 2014). Globally, approximately 20 million women experience high-risk pregnancies each year. Alarmingly, over 700 women lose their lives daily in 2023 due to perinatal complications—equivalent to one maternal death every two minutes. It is estimated that 6% to 33% of these fatalities are associated with high-risk pregnancies [5]. According to data from the Türkiye Demographic and Health Surveys (TDHS), the incidence of high-risk pregnancies in Türkiye was 44.3% in 1994, declined to 31.1% in 2014, and then rose again to 35.2% in 2018. This trend indicates that approximately one in three pregnant women in Türkiye is classified as high-risk during gestation [6–8].

Hospitalization, while medically necessary, may exacerbate psychological distress in women with high-risk pregnancies, particularly anxiety and emotional discomfort [9]. Such distress has been associated with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes, including hypertensive disorders, preterm birth, and impaired fetal development [10–13]. In this context, frequent non-stress test (NST) monitoring, often required during prolonged hospitalization, may further increase anxiety due to the need for prolonged immobility and the clinical environment.

The NST is a widely used, noninvasive antenatal screening method performed after 32 weeks of gestation to assess fetal well-being through basal fetal heart rate patterns and uterine activity, particularly in high-risk pregnancies requiring close fetal surveillance and guiding clinical decision-making [14]. Although it is painless, the NST typically lasts 20–30 min and requires the pregnant woman to maintain a fixed position throughout the session. This immobility, combined with the clinical environment, can lead to heightened stress and anxiety. Elevated anxiety during the NST may negatively influence the results, potentially increasing the likelihood of false-positive outcomes and misinterpretation of fetal well-being. Such false-positive findings can contribute to a rise in unnecessary operative births, further complicating obstetric care [14–15].

Implementing effective coping strategies for high-risk pregnant women is crucial to minimize anxiety during NST and thus protect both maternal and fetal health [15]. Stress balls are inexpensive, readily available, safe, and easy to use. A review of the current literature indicates that stress ball interventions have been examined in various clinical settings, including colonoscopy, endoscopy, prostate biopsy, radiological imaging, hemodialysis, intravenous catheterization, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing, and peripheral intravenous placement. Across these studies, effectiveness has been assessed using both psychological and physiological outcomes, including anxiety, pain, stress, depression, physiological parameters (e.g., pulse and blood pressure), and patient comfort and satisfaction [16–25]. A study shows that the effect of stress balls on anxiety during NST in healthy pregnant women has been studied [26]. Still, no research has examined their impact on fetal well-being (fetal movement count, heart rate, etc.) in high-risk pregnancies. Thus, the lack of evidence on the combined effects of stress ball use on maternal anxiety and fetal physiological outcomes in high-risk pregnancies represents a significant gap in the literature. Accordingly, this study aimed to determine the effect of stress ball application during NST on anxiety levels and fetal well-being in high-risk pregnant women.

Methods

Study design

The research is a two-arm, parallel, analyst-blinded, pre-test-post-test randomized controlled experimental study.

Participants

The study included high-risk pregnant women with clinically confirmed medical diagnoses requiring active treatment by a specialist physician, who were hospitalized in the perinatology unit of a public hospital located in northern Türkiye between August 2024 and January 2025. Only hospitalized patients were eligible; outpatients attending the outpatient clinic were excluded. Eligible participants were selected based on specific inclusion criteria and had undergone NST during their hospital stay. A priori power analysis, based on findings from a similar study by Karataş and Gezginci (2023), determined that a minimum of 39 participants per group would be required to achieve 95% confidence (1 − α), 99% statistical power (1 − β), a margin of error of 0.05, and an effect size of d = 0.985 [22]. Although the intervention was short-term, a 10% attrition allowance [27] was applied to the calculated sample size to account for potential exclusions due to unexpected clinical events during NST in high-risk pregnancies, such as acute fetal distress, onset of uterine contractions, urgent medical interventions, or technical data loss. This adjustment was made to preserve the study’s statistical power. The final study sample included 86 pregnant women, with at least 43 participants assigned to both the intervention and the control groups.

High-risk pregnancy classification was carried out in accordance with international guidelines established by NICE (2018), WHO (2017), AAP & ACOG (2017), and the clinical protocols of the Turkish Ministry of Health (Turkish Ministry of Health, 2014) [1–4]. In this study, specific high-risk conditions that warranted inclusion were gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), preeclampsia, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), oligohydramnios, threatened preterm labor (TPL), and placenta previa. Participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: being 18 years of age or older; having a clinically confirmed high-risk medical diagnosis during pregnancy warranting hospitalization; being at ≥ 30 weeks of gestation; being literate in Turkish; carrying a singleton live fetus; having eaten within two hours before the NST procedure; and having abstained from tobacco and alcohol use not only during the pre-NST period but also throughout their pregnancy or hospital stay. The requirement of food intake within 2 h before NST was applied as a standard inclusion criterion for both groups to promote fetal activity and to reduce the likelihood of low fetal movement related to recent fasting. Exclusion criteria included the presence of decelerations or uterine contractions during NST; fetal cardiovascular conditions, fetal distress, or congenital anomalies; the need for immediate medical intervention as determined by the attending physician; a diagnosed psychiatric disorder; or visual, auditory, speech, physical, or intellectual impairments that could hinder participation.

Randomization and blinding

Participants who met the inclusion criteria were allocated to either the intervention or the control group using simple randomization. The randomization sequence was generated using the online tool “QuickCalcs GraphPad” (http://www.graphpad.com/quickcalcs/, Accessed: January 20, 2024). This method was selected because the sample size (n = 86) was considered sufficient to achieve baseline homogeneity between groups (e.g., age, education level, gestational week) without the need for block randomization or stratification. The risk of selection bias was minimized because participants were assigned to the intervention and control groups independently of appointment times and based on a pre-generated, computer-generated closed list. The allocation process is illustrated in the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) flow diagram (Fig. 1). Participant blinding was not feasible due to the nature of the intervention, as participants were aware of using the stress ball. Blinding was applied only during data analysis in this study. To minimize statistical bias, the group identities were anonymized during data entry into the statistical program by coding the intervention and control groups as “x” and “y.” Analysts conducting the statistical procedures were unaware of group assignments. Furthermore, to minimize interparticipant influence, NST assessments were conducted individually in private single-occupancy rooms within the perinatology unit, thereby limiting potential communication between participants.

Instruments

Data were collected using the “Personal Information Form,” “State Anxiety Inventory (SAI),” and “Fetal Well-Being Assessment Form.”

Personal information form

The personal information form, created by researchers in line with the literature [28–30], consists of 18 questions. These questions include sociodemographic characteristics of pregnant women, such as age, education, employment status, income level, place of residence, obstetric characteristics, and NST information.

State Anxiety Inventory (SAI)

The Turkish adaptation and validation of the SAI, originally developed to assess individuals’ situational anxiety levels [31], was conducted by Öner and Le Compte in 1983. This self-report instrument evaluates how a person feels at a specific moment and under undefined circumstances. The scale consists of 20 items and is structured as a 4-point Likert-type format (1 = not at all, 2 = somewhat, 3 = quite a bit, 4 = very much), including both positively and negatively worded items. Reverse-scored items include statements 1, 2, 5, 8, 10, 11, 15, 16, 19, and 20. During scoring, the total for the reverse-scored items is subtracted from the total of the directly scored items, and a constant value of 50 is added to the result. The final score ranges from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater state anxiety. The original Turkish version of the inventory demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.94 to 0.96 [32]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was calculated as 0.89, indicating strong reliability.

Fetal well-being evaluation form

This form, created by researchers based on the literature [10, 29], includes parameters such as basal fetal heart rate, fetal movement count, variability, acceleration, and reactivity, which are used to evaluate fetal well-being during NST. Basal fetal heart rate, variability, accelerations, and reactivity) were assessed by the researcher based on the NST device’s outputs. In contrast, fetal movement counts were obtained from maternal self-reports.

Procedures

Pregnant women who met the study’s inclusion criteria were first informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, and ethical considerations. Written informed consent was then obtained from those who agreed to participate. Following consent, participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group. Prior to the intervention, all participants completed a Personal Information Form to collect baseline demographic and clinical data. To ensure consistency among participants, all assessments were conducted in isolated, single rooms. Data were collected face-to-face by the same researcher, based on the pregnant women’s self-reports. Measurements were taken twice, before and after each NST session, in accordance with the standard NST procedure. As a pre-test for the study, immediately before the NST procedure, the pregnant woman’s state anxiety level, the number of fetal movements in the last half hour, and basal fetal heart rate were measured. As a post-test for the study, immediately after completion of the NST procedure, the state anxiety level, basal fetal heart rate, fetal heart rate variability, acceleration, reactivity parameters, and the number of fetal movements during the NST were recorded.

Control Group (CG)

Pregnant women in the control group were given routine care during NST and were accompanied by healthcare staff. Routine care includes preparing the pregnant woman for NST, informing her about NST, positioning her, monitoring the NST (20–30 min), evaluating the results, helping her stand up after NST, protecting her privacy, and ensuring hygiene.

Intervention Group (IG)

Pregnant women in the intervention group used a stress ball, which is a nonpharmacological and noninvasive tool, among the distraction and relaxation methods in addition to routine care. A stress ball is a soft toy, approximately 7 cm in both length and width, that is squeezed and manipulated with the fingers to relieve stress, reduce muscle tension, and work the muscles [16]. The stress ball used in the study also has this feature because it is easy to hold and has a colorful and flexible structure. These stress balls were disinfected with disposable aseptic wipes before being given to the pregnant women to minimize contamination in the hospital environment. The applications made to the intervention group are given in Table 1. Pregnant women in the intervention group were questioned about their nutritional status, smoking, and alcohol use before the NST; the use of the stress ball was explained practically. During the NST (20–30 min), the pregnant women used the stress ball by squeezing and releasing it with their fingers, accompanied by breath awareness. The application was performed with the hand/dominant hand preferred by the pregnant woman according to her comfort. However, participants with insufficient hand strength were allowed to use both hands to ensure comfort and continuity. The researcher monitored the correct use of the stress ball. All participants received the exact standardized verbal instructions from the same researcher. Anxiety level, fetal movement count, and NST parameters (basal fetal heart rate, variability, acceleration, reactivity) were evaluated; uterine contractions and fetal distress were monitored (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0. The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis coefficients (within ± 2 range), and the homogeneity of variances was verified using Levene’s test. Intervention and control groups were compared using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent-samples t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. Within-group changes were analyzed using paired-samples t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as applicable. To evaluate the primary study outcomes and the effectiveness of the stress ball intervention over time, a two-way repeated-measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted, examining the main effects of group (intervention vs. control), time (pre-test vs. post-test), and the group × time interaction. Given the significant baseline differences in the number of living children and parity (p < 0.05), these variables were included as covariates to control for potential confounding. Effect sizes were calculated and interpreted using Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons and partial eta squared (η2) for ANCOVA models to quantify the magnitude of the intervention’s impact. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and, when multiple comparisons were conducted, the Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust p-values.

Results

Sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics

Table 2 presents the distribution of sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics of the pregnant women in the intervention and control groups. No statistically significant differences were found between the groups in terms of age, gestational age, number of pregnancies, number of pregnancy losses, educational level, employment status, income level, family structure, place of residence, history of risk in previous pregnancies, prior experience with NST, or knowledge about NST (p > 0.05). These findings indicate that the intervention and control groups were largely homogeneous in their baseline sociodemographic and obstetric profiles. In this study, conditions defined as high-risk pregnancies included gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), preeclampsia, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), oligohydramnios, threatened preterm labor (TPL), and placenta previa. In the intervention group, the most common diagnosis was TPL risk (25.6%), followed by IUGR (20.9%), GDM (16.3%), placenta previa (16.3%), preeclampsia (11.6%), and oligohydramnios (9.3%). In the control group, TPL risk (27.9%) was also the most frequent diagnosis, followed by GDM (18.6%), oligohydramnios (16.3%), IUGR (14.0%), placenta previa (11.6%), and preeclampsia (11.6%). The distribution of high-risk pregnancy types was similar between the intervention and control groups (p = 0.868). Additionally, all participants reported satisfaction with the care received during hospitalization, and none had prior experience using a stress ball.

State anxiety scores

The results of the pre-test and post-test comparisons of intra- and within-group situational anxiety scores for pregnant women, along with the summary of the two-way repeated-measures covariance analysis, are presented in Table 3. Initially (pre-test), the SAI scores of the intervention and control groups were similar (IG 45.05 ± 10.72, CG 41.16 ± 9.11; t (84) = 1.81, p = 0.074), and the effect size was low to moderate (d = 0.39). After the intervention (post-test), the anxiety scores of the intervention group (35.56 ± 6.55) were found to be significantly lower than those of the control group (40.05 ± 6.82) (t (84) = − 3.11, p = 0.003) and a moderate-high effect size (d = 0.67) was observed. In within-group analyses, a significant decrease of 9.5 points in anxiety scores was observed in the intervention group (t (42) = 7.218, p < 0.001). In contrast, no statistically significant change was observed in the control group (p = 0.366).

Parity and number of living children were entered as covariates in a two-way repeated-measures ANCOVA to adjust for baseline differences. According to the ANCOVA results, the group effect, which represents the main difference between groups, was found to be statistically insignificant (F (1, 82) = 0.048, p = 0.828, η2 = 0.001). The change in anxiety scores over time was statistically significant (F (1, 82) = 15.744, p < 0.001) and had a small effect size (η2 = 0.161). Time and group interaction were significant (F (1, 82) = 20.898, p < 0.001), and the stress ball application was associated with a decrease in anxiety level, with a small-to-moderate effect size (η2 = 0.203). It was determined that the variables number of living children (p = 0.719) and parity (p = 0.677) included in the model did not have a significant confounding effect on the change in anxiety scores.

Fetal well-being

Table 4 presents the comparisons of fetal movement counts between and within groups in pregnant women before and after the intervention. Before the intervention (pre-test), the fetal movement count in the intervention group (3.30 ± 1.21) was statistically significantly lower than in the control group (4.19 ± 1.84) (U = 685.500, p = 0.032) and had a moderate-to-large effect size (d = 0.568). After the intervention (post-test), the fetal movement count in the intervention group was statistically significantly higher than in the control group (IG 4.67 ± 0.94, CG 4.09 ± 1.36; U = 575.000, p = 0.002) and had a small-to-moderate effect size (d = 0.497). Within-group analyses revealed a statistically significant increase of 1.37 units in fetal movement count in the intervention group (Z = -6.687, p < 0.001; d = 1.020, large effect size). In contrast, no statistically significant change was observed in the control group (p = 0.683). A comparison of these differences between groups showed that the increase in the intervention group was statistically significantly higher than in the control group (Table 4, p < 0.001).

According to the results of the two-way repeated-measures ANCOVA applied to control for the confounding effects of parity and the number of living children between groups, the group effect—representing the primary difference between groups—was found to be statistically insignificant (F (1, 82) = 1.495, p = 0.225). The change in basal fetal heart rate over time was statistically significant (F (1, 82) = 30.454, p < 0.001), with a low-to-moderate effect size (η2 = 0.271). The group-by-time interaction was also statistically significant (F (1, 82) = 11.895, p = 0.001), with a small effect size (η2 = 0.127). It was determined that the variables of number of living children (p = 0.280) and parity (p = 0.387) included in the model did not have a statistically significant confounding effect on the basal fetal heart rate results (Table 5).

Discussion

High-risk pregnancies often require hospitalization and frequent NST monitoring, which may increase maternal anxiety due to concerns about maternal and fetal health. Elevated anxiety during NST has been associated with changes in fetal physiological parameters, underscoring the need for supportive interventions. In this context, the present study examined the association between stress ball use during NST and maternal anxiety and fetal well-being indicators in high-risk pregnant women, and the findings are discussed in relation to the existing literature.

Although NST is a non-invasive procedure, the clinical environment and procedural factors—such as prolonged immobility, anticipation of abnormal results, deviations in basal fetal heart rate, reduced perceived fetal movements, or fetal sleep states—may contribute to increased psychological stress, particularly in the third trimester [15, 28, 33, 34]. Elevated maternal anxiety during NST has been associated with adverse maternal–fetal physiological responses, highlighting the clinical relevance of interventions aimed at anxiety reduction in this population [16, 33, 35]. In this study, the use of a stress ball during NST was associated with a significant mean reduction of 9.5 points in situational anxiety scores in the intervention group (t (84) = − 3.111, p = 0.003) and a moderate-to-high effect size (D = 0.671). This finding is consistent with previous literature data reporting that the use of a stress ball during NST and outpatient gynecological procedures reduces anxiety in healthy pregnant women [26, 36]. Furthermore, evidence from diverse clinical settings, including endoscopy [22], angiography [37], cystoscopy [19], and phlebotomy [38], also supports the anxiolytic potential of the stress ball and confirms that its benefits extend beyond the discipline of women’s health. Literature indicates that various non-pharmacological interventions during NST—including the use of colored drapes or belts, music therapy, and virtual reality—are effective in reducing maternal anxiety [26, 28, 29, 33–35, 39–41]. Consistent with these findings, the present study suggests that stress ball use may similarly reduce maternal anxiety through mechanisms related to cognitive distraction and relaxation. However, the observed anxiolytic effect cannot be attributed solely to the stress ball itself, as the intervention protocol included intentional rhythmic breathing (inhalation during squeezing and exhalation during release), a well-established anxiety-reducing technique. Thus, respiratory regulation, focused attention, and potential placebo or Hawthorne effects may have acted as confounding mechanisms contributing to anxiety reduction. Despite these considerations, stress balls can be considered an intervention that may support short-term psychological well-being in high-risk pregnant women during pregnancy monitoring.

High-risk pregnant women are known to experience higher anxiety levels than low-risk pregnancies, which may activate physiological stress pathways, including increased cortisol secretion. As these stress hormones can cross the placental barrier, elevated maternal anxiety may influence uteroplacental perfusion and fetal physiological parameters [33, 35]. High anxiety during NST has also been associated with an increased frequency of nonreactive NST results, potentially leading to misinterpretation and unnecessary interventional births [15–17]. In the present study, post-intervention fetal movement counts and basal fetal heart rate were significantly higher in the intervention group compared to the control group (p < 0.05). Specifically, the intervention group showed a mean increase of 1.37 movements and a mean increase in heart rate of 8.6 beats per minute (p < 0.001), indicating significant short-term improvements in indicators of fetal well-being. It is thought that this effect may be related to the fact that using a stress ball supports the balance of the autonomic nervous system by increasing maternal relaxation and indirectly improving uteroplacental perfusion. Although the effect of stress ball application on fetal parameters during NST has not been previously examined, similar short-term improvements have been reported with other non-pharmacological interventions, such as music therapy [28, 29, 39, 41–45], virtual reality applications [40, 41], and maternal positioning [46]. Collectively, these approaches may operate through shared mechanisms, including relaxation, attention focus, and modulation of the maternal autonomic nervous system.

Importantly, not all studies have demonstrated consistent effects of nonpharmacological interventions on NST parameters [28, 34]. Differences in study populations (high-risk versus healthy pregnancies), intervention characteristics (e.g., music, virtual reality applications), duration, and outcome definitions may explain these discrepancies. Furthermore, in the present study, the stress ball intervention was accompanied by intentional rhythmic breathing, suggesting that respiratory regulation and cognitive distraction may have functioned as confounding mechanisms. Additionally, placebo and Hawthorne effects cannot be excluded, as participants were aware they were receiving an intervention. Therefore, these factors are acknowledged as alternative explanations for the findings, rather than attributing the observed improvements solely to the stress ball. Consequently, tress ball use is thought to be associated with reduced maternal anxiety during NST, which may be related to positive changes in fetal well-being indicators.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. Firstly, the sample size and the study’s single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader high-risk pregnant population. Second, the inability to blind participants due to the nature of the intervention may have led to performance bias; however, this was mitigated by blinding the data analysts. Third, since the intervention integrated rhythmic breathing with stress ball squeezing, it is difficult to separate the independent effect of the stress ball from the well-known anxiolytic effects of respiratory regulation, which could confound the results. Fourth, despite the inclusion of objective NST parameters, the anxiety and fetal movement data are partly based on subjective self-reports. Finally, the lack of long-term follow-up limits conclusions regarding the sustainability of these improvements beyond the clinical setting.

Conclusion

In high-risk pregnancies, the use of stress balls during NST may be associated with reducing maternal anxiety and providing short-term improvements in fetal well-being indicators. These results may increase awareness among healthcare professionals of the potential importance of this non-invasive, non-pharmacological, sustainable, and low-cost technique that helps manage anxiety through distraction. Accordingly, the use of this intervention during NST monitoring of high-risk pregnancies across different levels of healthcare settings may help reduce false-positive results and operative delivery rates. On the other hand, implementing such supportive procedures may improve satisfaction with the quality of care among high-risk pregnant women and caregivers. It is recommended that future studies use larger samples and longitudinal designs, incorporate physiological stress markers, and include comparator groups to isolate respiratory regulation effects, thereby clarifying underlying mechanisms and assessing long-term sustainability.
