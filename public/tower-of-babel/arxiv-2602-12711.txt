Another Way to Lower the Bound for Distinct Squares
Eitatsu Tomita, Tomohiro I

Abstract
A square is a word of the form x ​ x xx for a non-empty word x x . Brlek and Li [Comb. Theory, 2025] proved that the number of distinct squares in a word w w of length n n is at most n − σ n-\sigma , where σ \sigma is the number of letters used in w w . The same authors extended the proof to lower the upper bound to n − Θ ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) n-\Theta(\log n) in [WORDS, 2023]. In this paper, we present another proof to obtain the same bound n − Θ ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) n-\Theta(\log n) .

Introduction

A
square
is a word of the form
xx
for a non-empty word
x
.
Among other formalism of repetitions, squares have been a central matter in combinatorics on words
[
16
,
17
,
18
]

with the pioneering work on square-free words by Thue
[
24
]
.

Fraenkel and Simpson
[
9
]
paid attention to
the size of the set
\mathit{SQ}_{w}
of distinct squares in a finite word
w
of length
n
.
While conjecturing
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|<n
,
they proved
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|<2n
by showing that at most two squares in
\mathit{SQ}_{w}

share the same position for their rightmost occurrences.
Since then, their proof technique has been refined to get tighter upper bounds:

2n-\Theta(\log n)
by Ilie
[
10
]
,

\frac{95}{48}n
by Lam
[
12
]
,

\frac{11}{6}n
by Deza et al.
[
6
]
, and

1.5n
by Thierry
[
23
]
.

Aside from this line of research, some variations of the conjecture have been raised:
Deza et al.
[
5
]
took the number
\sigma
of distinct letters used in
w
as a parameter and conjectured that
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq w-\sigma
,
which was updated in
[
7
]
to a slightly stronger conjecture

|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq n-\sigma-\Theta(\log(n-\sigma))
.
1
1

1

In
[
5
,
7
]
, only primitively rooted squares are considered.

Also, Jonoska et al.
[
11
]
conjectured that

|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq\frac{2k-1}{2k+2}n
for a binary word
w
,
where
k\leq n/2
is the number of occurrences of the less frequent letter in
w
.

Recently, Brlek and Li
[
4
]
proved that
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq n-\sigma
,
breaking a barrier with a new approach based on Rauzy graphs
[
21
]
.
The Rauzy graph
\Gamma_{w}(\ell)
of order
\ell
of
w
is a directed graph such that
each vertex corresponds to a factor of length
\ell
of
w

and each arc corresponding to a factor of length
\ell+1
of
w

connects its prefix and suffix of length
\ell
.
Brlek and Li
[
4
]
showed that
there is an injection from
\mathit{SQ}_{w}
to an independent cycle set
of the graph
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{n}\Gamma_{w}(\ell)
,
which is a collection of Rauzy graphs of orders from
1
to
n
.
The result
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq n-\sigma
is obtained
because the maximum size of an independent cycle set of

\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{n}\Gamma_{w}(\ell)
is
n-\sigma
.
Furthermore, the techniques have been extended to show
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq n-\Theta(\log n)
in
[
3
]
, and
to prove the conjecture of
[
11
]
affirmatively in
[
15
]
.

As for lower bounds of maximum
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|
over all words
w
of length
n
,
Fraenkel and Simpson
[
9
]
showed how to build
binary words that contain
n-\Theta(\sqrt{n})
squares.
Jonoska et al.
[
11
]
showed
that the same asymptotic lower bound can be obtained from structurally simpler binary words.
Since the leading term of both upper and lower bounds of maximum
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|
meets at
n
,
the maximum density
\frac{|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|}{n}
of distinct squares converges to
1
as
n
increases.
The result of Manea and Seki
[
20
]
implies that
the convergence is valid for any
\sigma\geq 2
and the limit is never reached.

Although the leading term
n
in the upper bound is optimal in general,
it is expected that lower terms in the current best upper bound
n-\Theta(\log n)
of
[
3
]
can be lowered further.
Based on computational results of
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|
for binary words (listed as A248958 in OEIS
[
22
]
),
Brlek and Li
[
3
]
posed the following conjecture:

|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq\lceil n+1-\sqrt{n}-\log_{2}\left(\sqrt{n}\right)\rceil.

To pursue this quest, we aim to present another proof for
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq n-\Theta(\log n)
.

We also describe the connection between cycles and squares with a slightly different perspective from the previous work
[
3
,
14
,
13
,
4
]
:
Instead of identifying interesting cycles by comparing their lengths with the order
\ell
of Rauzy graphs, we classify the cycles based on Lyndon words.
We restate and prove the existing lemmas in this perspective to make this paper self-contained.

Preliminaries

For two integers
b
and
e
, let
[b..e]
denote the set
\{b,b+1,\dots,e\}
of integers if
b\leq e
, and otherwise, the empty set.
We also use
[b..]
to denote the set
\{b,b+1,\dots,\}
of integers that are larger than or equal to
b
.

A finite
word

w=w[1]w[2]\cdots w[|w|]
is a sequence of letters, where
|w|\in[0..]
denotes the length of
w
and, for
i\in[1..|w|]
,
w[i]
denotes the
i
-th letter of
w
.
The
empty word

\varepsilon
is the word of length
0
.
For integers
1\leq b\leq e\leq|w|
, the
factor
of
w
beginning at
b
and ending at
e
is denoted by
w[b..e]=w[b]w[b+1]\cdots w[e]
.
For convenience, let
w[b..e]
with
b>e
be the empty word.
A
prefix
(resp.
suffix
) of
w
is a factor that matches
w[1..e]
(resp.
w[b..|w|]
) for some
e\in[0..|w|]
(resp.
b\in[1..|w|+1]
).
The
concatenation

xy
of two words
x
and
y
is an associative binary operation such that
(xy)[1..|x|]=x
and
(xy)[|x|+1..|xy|]=y
.

Let
\preceq
denote an arbitrary defined total order over letters,
which is also extended to represent the lexicographic order over words:
For any two words
x
and
y
,
x\preceq y
if and only if
either
x
is a prefix of
y
or there exists an integer
\ell\geq 0
such that
x[1..\ell]=y[1..\ell]
and
x[\ell+1]\prec y[\ell+1]
.

For any word
x
and integer
m\geq 0
,
let
x^{m/|x|}
denote the word of length
m
such that
x^{m/|x|}[i]=x[j]
for any
i\in[1..m]
and
j\in[1..|x|]
with
j\equiv i\mod m
.
If
w=x^{|w|/|x|}
with
|x|\leq|w|
, the integer
|x|
is called a
period
of
w
.
A word
w
is called
primitive
if it is not written as
w=x^{k}
for some word
x
and
k\in[2..]
.
A non-empty word is a
k
-power
if it is of the form
x^{k}
for some word
x
and
k\in[2..]
(here
x
is not necessarily primitive).
In particular, a 2-power is called a
square
.

For any words
x
and
y
, two words
xy
and
yx
are said to be
conjugate
.
For any word
z
, the
conjugacy class

[z]
is defined as follows:

[z]=\{z[i..|z|]z[1..i-1]\mid i\in[1..|z|]\}

For any integer
m\geq 0
, we also define

[z]_{m}=\{x^{m/|x|}\mid x\in[z]\}.

Remark that we allow
m
to be smaller than
|z|
.
Intuitively,
[z]_{m}
is the set of factors of length
m
in the infinite word
z^{\infty}
that repeats
z
.

Example 1
.

For
z=\mathtt{aab}
,
[z]_{0}=\{\varepsilon\}
,
[z]_{1}=\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\}
,
[z]_{2}=\{\mathtt{aa},\mathtt{ab},\mathtt{ba}\}
,
[z]_{3}=[z]=\{\mathtt{aab},\mathtt{aba},\mathtt{baa}\}
,
[z]_{4}=\{\mathtt{aaba},\mathtt{abaa},\mathtt{baab}\}
, and
[z]_{5}=\{\mathtt{aabaa},\mathtt{abaab},\mathtt{baaba}\}
.

We will mainly consider
[z]
and
[z]_{m}
for primitive words,
choosing
Lyndon words
as a prominent representative of the conjugacy class for a primitive word.

Definition 2

(
[
19
,
16
]
)
.

A primitive word
z
is called a
Lyndon word

if
z
is the lexicographically smallest word in
[z]
, or equivalently,

z\prec z[i..|z|]
holds for any
i\in[2..|z|]
.

Lemma 3
.

The smallest period of a Lyndon word
z
is
|z|
.

Proof.

If
z
has a period
p
smaller than
|z|
, then its suffix
z[p+1..|z|]
of length
|z|-p>0
is lexicographically smaller than
z
because
z[p+1..|z|]=z[1..|z|-p]\prec z
, a contradiction.
∎

Since any
k
-power (including squares for
k=2
) can be written as
x^{kr}
for some primitive word
x
and integer
r\geq 1
,
we associate it uniquely with the Lyndon word
z
such that
x\in[z]
.
We call such

the
Lyndon root
of the
k
-power.

For any word
w
, we define the following sets of words:

•

\mathit{Fac}_{w}=\{x\mid w=uxv\}
: The set of factors of
w
.

•

\mathit{Fac}_{w}(\ell)=\{x\in\mathit{Fac}_{w}\mid|x|=\ell\}
: The set of factors of length
\ell\geq 0
in

.

•

\mathit{Lyn}_{w}=\{z\in\mathit{Fac}_{w}\mid z\text{~is a Lyndon word}\}
: The set of Lyndon factors of
w
.

•

\mathit{SQ}_{w}=\{x\in\mathit{Fac}_{w}\mid x\text{~is a square}\}
: The set of squares in
w
.

•

\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)=\{x^{2r}\in\mathit{SQ}_{w}\mid x\in[z],r\in[1..]\}
:
The set of squares in
w
with Lyndon root

.

Remark 4
.

For any word
w
,
\mathit{SQ}_{w}=\bigcup_{z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}}\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)
and
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|=\sum_{z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}}|\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)|
.

A
directed graph
consists of two distinct sets of vertices and arcs, where each arc connects two vertices with the direction from its
initial vertex
to its
terminal vertex
.
2
2

2

In our work, we need
multigraphs
to deal with the Rauzy graph of order
0
,
thus allow distinct arcs to have the same pair of initial and terminal vertices.
We also allow a
loop
, an arc whose initial vertex coincides with its terminal vertex.

A sequence
(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{m})
of arcs is called a
path
of length
m
if the terminal vertex of
u_{i}
coincides with the initial vertex of
u_{i+1}
for any
i\in[1..m-1]
.
The path is called a
circuit
if the terminal vertex of
u_{m}
coincides with the initial vertex of
u_{1}
.
In paths and circuits, we consider moving from the initial vertex of
u_{1}
to the terminal vertex of
u_{m}
while following the direction of arcs in the sequence.
If we are allowed to traverse arcs also in the opposite direction, the graph is treated as an
undirected
graph.
In the undirected case, the counterparts of paths and circuits are called
chains
and
cycles
, respectively (see
[
2
,
4
]
for a more formal definition).
Note that a path (resp. circuit) is always a chain (resp. cycle) in this terminology.
A graph is called
weakly connected
if, for every pair of distinct vertices, there is a chain going from one to the other.
A maximal weakly connected subgraph is called a
component
.

Definition 5
.

For a graph with the arc set
\{u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{n_{\mathsf{a}}}\}
,
the
cycle-vector
of a cycle
C
is the vector
\mu(C)=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n_{\mathsf{a}}})

in the
n_{\mathsf{a}}
-dimensional space such that
c_{i}=r_{i}-s_{i}
, where

r_{i}
and
s_{i}
are the numbers of times
u_{i}
is traversed in one direction and respectively in the opposite direction in the cycle.

Definition 6
.

A set
\{C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{k}\}
of cycles is called
independent
if their cycle-vectors are linearly independent, i.e.,
\alpha_{1}\mu(C_{1})+\alpha_{2}\mu(C_{2})+\dots+\alpha_{k}\mu(C_{k})=\mathbf{0}
implies that
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\dots=\alpha_{k}=0
.

Theorem 7

(Theorem 2, Chapter 4 in
[
2
]
)
.

For a multigraph with
n_{\mathsf{v}}
vertices,
n_{\mathsf{a}}
arcs and
n_{\mathsf{c}}
components,
the
cyclomatic number
of the graph, defined to be
n_{\mathsf{a}}-n_{\mathsf{v}}+n_{\mathsf{c}}
, is equal to the maximum size of an independent cycle set.

Definition 8

(Rauzy graph
[
21
]
)
.

The
Rauzy graph

\Gamma_{w}(\ell)

\ell\in[0..|w|]
for a word

and the arc set
\mathit{Fac}_{w}(\ell+1)
such that each arc

Let

be the graph that collects Rauzy graphs of all orders

in one graph with the vertex set
\mathit{Fac}_{w}
, the arc set
\mathit{Fac}_{w}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
and
|w|+1

Note that every word

appears as a vertex and also as an arc in

, but they are distinguished from each other.
See

Lemma 9
.

For any word

, the maximum size of an independent cycle set of

Proof.

Since

Another proof for
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq|w|-\Theta(\log|w|)

Brlek and Li
[
4

showed that
there is an injection from
\mathit{SQ}_{w}

More precisely, the squares in

13

Although the proof for

We start with two simple lemmas (

Lemma 10
.

For any word
w
and
z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}
with
[z]_{m}\subseteq\mathit{Fac}_{w}
for some integer
m>0
,
[z]_{m-1}\subseteq\mathit{Fac}_{w}
holds.

Proof.

For any word
u\in[z]_{m-1}
, there is a word
x\in[z]
such that
u=x^{(m-1)/|x|}
.
Since
u
is a prefix of
x^{m/|x|}\in[z]_{m}\subseteq\mathit{Fac}_{w}
,

u
must be a factor of
w
.
∎

Lemma 11
.

For any word
w
and
z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}
with
[z]_{m}\subseteq\mathit{Fac}_{w}
for some integer
m>0
,
\Gamma_{w}(m-1)
contains a circuit whose arc set is
[z]_{m}
.

Proof.

Let
x_{i}=z[i..|z|]z[1..i-1]
for any
i\in[1..|z|]
.
Then,
[z]_{m}=\{x_{1}^{m/|z|},x_{2}^{m/|z|},\dots,x_{|z|}^{m/|z|}\}
.
We show that the sequence
(x_{1}^{m/|z|},x_{2}^{m/|z|},\dots,x_{|z|}^{m/|z|})
of arcs in
\Gamma_{w}(m-1)
is a circuit:
For any
i\in[1..|z|-1]
,
the terminal vertex of the arc
x_{i}^{m/|z|}
is
x_{i}^{m/|z|}[2..m]=x_{i+1}^{(m-1)/|z|}=x_{i+1}^{m/|z|}[1..m-1]
, which coincides with the initial vertex of the next arc
x_{i+1}^{m/|z|}
.
Similarly, the terminal vertex of the arc
x_{|z|}^{m/|z|}
is
x_{|z|}^{m/|z|}[2..m]=x_{1}^{(m-1)/|z|}=x_{1}^{m/|z|}[1..m-1]
, which coincides with the initial vertex of
x_{1}^{m/|z|}
.
∎

Definition 13

For any word

\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)

The next lemma shows that the sets of
\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)
are disjoint for any pair of Lyndon factors
and their union constitutes an independent cycle set of
\Gamma_{w}
:

Lemma 14
.

For any word
w
, the set
\bigcup_{z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}}\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)
contains
\sum_{z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}}|\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|
circuits, and is an independent cycle set of
\Gamma_{w}
.

Proof.

Recall that the arc set of any circuit in
\mathrm{CS}_{w}(z)
is of the form
[z]_{m}
for some integer
m\geq|z|
.
We claim that the circuit can be uniquely assigned to the lexicographically smallest arc
z^{m/|z|}
in
[z]_{m}
.
For two distinct Lyndon factors
z_{1}
and
z_{2}\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}
with
|z_{1}|\leq|z_{2}|
and integer
m\geq|z_{2}|
,
it must hold that
z_{1}^{m/|z_{1}|}\neq z_{2}^{m/|z_{2}|}
because:
If
|z_{1}|=|z_{2}|
, then it is clear that
z_{1}^{m/|z_{1}|}\neq z_{2}^{m/|z_{2}|}
as
z_{1}\neq z_{2}
.
Otherwise,
z_{1}^{m/|z_{1}|}=z_{2}^{m/|z_{2}|}
implies that
z_{2}
has a period
|z_{1}|<|z_{2}|
, which contradicts
lemma

3
.
Therefore,
\mathit{CS}_{w}(z_{1})
and
\mathit{CS}_{w}(z_{2})
are disjoint, which leads to
|\bigcup_{z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}}\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|=\sum_{z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}}|\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|
.

Let
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{k}
with
k=\sum_{z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}}|\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|
denote the circuits in
\bigcup_{z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}}\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)
sorted
in strictly increasing order by their lexicographically smallest arcs,
which is possible because those arcs are pairwise distinct as seen above.
Note that, for any
1\leq i<j\leq k
, the lexicographically smallest arc of
C_{i}
is not contained in
C_{j}
.
We now show that

\alpha_{1}\mu(C_{1})+\alpha_{2}\mu(C_{2})+\dots+\alpha_{k}\mu(C_{k})=\mathbf{0}\Longrightarrow\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\dots=\alpha_{k}=0.

In order to have the equation
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}\mu(C_{i}))=\mathbf{0}
,
we need
\alpha_{1}=0
, since otherwise,
the component of the vector corresponding to the lexicographically smallest arc of
C_{1}
cannot be zero.
Applying the same argument recursively to the remaining equation
\sum_{i=2}^{k}(\alpha_{i}\mu(C_{i}))=\mathbf{0}
,
we can see that all coefficients
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{k}
must be zero.
∎

The following is immediate from
lemmas

9
and
14
:

Lemma 15
.

For any word
w
,
|\bigcup_{z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}}\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|\leq|w|
.

The next lemma evaluates the sizes of
\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)
and
\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)
.

Lemma 16
.

For any word
w
and
z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}
with
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)|\geq 1
,

\displaystyle|\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)|

\displaystyle=|z|(r-1)+s,

\displaystyle|\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|

\displaystyle\geq 2|z|(r-1)+s+1,

where

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\max\{i\in[1..]\mid\exists x^{2i}\in\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)\},

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\left|\{x\in[z]\mid x^{2r}\in\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)\}\right|.

Proof.

Let
x_{i}=z[i..|z|]z[1..i-1]
for any
i\in[1..|z|]
, and
let
\{k_{j}\}_{j=1}^{s}
be the set of integers with
0<k_{1}<k_{2}<\dots<k_{s}<|z|

such that
x_{k_{j}}^{2r}\in\mathit{SQ}_{w}
for any
j\in[1..s]
.

For every pair
i\in[1..|z|]
and
d\in[1..r-1]
,
x_{i}^{2d}
forms a distinct square,
which exists in
\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)
because
x_{i}^{2d}
is a factor of
x_{k_{1}}^{2r}
.
Then, it is clear that
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)|=|z|(r-1)+s
from the definition of
s
.

In order to see
|\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|\geq 2|z|(r-1)+s+1
,
let
g=\max\{k_{j+1}-k_{j}\mid j\in[1..s]\}
, where
k_{s+1}=k_{1}+|z|
.
It is easy to see that
g\leq|z|-s+1

because
g
is maximized when
k_{j+1}-k_{j}=1

for every
j\in[1..s]
except for the one that achieves maximum
g
.
We now set
M=2|z|r-g+1
and claim that
[z]_{M}\subseteq\mathit{Fac}_{w}
.
For any
i\in[1..|z|]
with
i\in[k_{j}..k_{j+1}-1]
for some
j\in[1..s]
,

x_{i}^{M/|z|}
is a factor of
x_{k_{j}}^{2r}
because
i-i_{j}+1\leq g
and
the repeats of
x_{i}
beginning at
i-k_{j}+1
of
x_{k_{j}}^{2r}

continues
2|z|r-(i-k_{j}+1)+1\geq 2|z|r-g+1=M
letters, long enough to cover
x_{i}^{M/|z|}
.
Similarly, for any
i\in[1..k_{1}-1]
,
x_{i}^{M/|z|}
is a factor of
x_{k_{s}}^{2r}
.
Therefore,
[z]_{M}\subseteq\mathit{Fac}_{w}
. See
fig.

2
for an illustration.

It follows from
lemma

10
that
[z]_{m}\subseteq\mathit{Fac}_{w}
for any
m\in[1..M]
,
which produces a circuit with the arc set
[z]_{m}
by
lemma

11
.
Among these circuits,
\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)
contains
M-|z|+1=2|z|r-|z|-g+2\geq 2|z|(r-1)+s+1
circuits,
spreading across the Rauzy graphs of orders from
|z|-1
to
M-1
.
∎

.

,
r=3

,
k_{1}=2

k_{3}=5
.
Since
k_{2}-k_{1}=1,k_{3}-k_{2}=2,k_{4}-k_{3}=k_{1}+|z|-k_{3}=2

g=2
.

contains
s
squares of the form
x_{k_{j}}^{2r}
for

, which are aligned under

.

2|z|r-g+1
of
z^{\infty}

Definition 17
.

For any word
w
and
z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}
with
|\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|\geq 1
, we define

\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)=\frac{|\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)|}{|\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|},

and consider that every circuit in
\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)
is loaded with
\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)
squares.

From the lower bound of
|\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|
obtained in
lemma

16
with variables
r
and
s
,
one would anticipate that
\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)
can reach its maximum
\frac{|z|}{|z|+1}

when
r=1
and
s=|z|
,
which is formally proved in the next lemma.

Lemma 18
.

For any word
w
and
z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}
with
|\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|\geq 1
,
\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)\leq\frac{|z|}{|z|+1}
.

Proof.

If
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)|=0
, it is easy to see that
\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)=0\leq\frac{|z|}{|z|+1}
.
For the remaining case, we show that
\frac{1}{\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)}\geq\frac{|z|+1}{|z|}
.
Using variables
r
and
s
in
lemma

16
, we have

\frac{1}{\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)}=\frac{|\mathit{CS}_{w}(z)|}{|\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)|}\geq\frac{2|z|(r-1)+s+1}{|z|(r-1)+s}=1+\frac{|z|(r-1)+1}{|z|(r-1)+s}.

Since
1\leq s\leq|z|
, the last expression is minimized when
s=|z|
, i.e.,

\frac{1}{\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)}\geq 1+\frac{|z|(r-1)+1}{|z|(r-1)+|z|}=1+\frac{|z|(r-1)+1}{|z|r}.

Since
|z|\geq 1
, we have
|z|(r-1)+1\geq r
, and hence,

\frac{1}{\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)}\geq 1+\frac{r}{|z|r}=1+\frac{1}{|z|}=\frac{|z|+1}{|z|}.

Therefore,
\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)\leq\frac{|z|}{|z|+1}
holds.
∎

We also have the following property:

Lemma 19
.

For any word
w
and
\ell\in[0..|w|]
, a circuit in
\Gamma_{w}(\ell)
is loaded with at most
\frac{\ell+1}{\ell+2}
squares.

Proof.

Since each circuit in
\Gamma_{w}(\ell)
is loaded with squares in
\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)
with
|z|\leq\ell+1
, its loaded value is at most
\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)\leq\frac{|z|}{|z|+1}\leq\frac{\ell+1}{\ell+2}
by
lemma

18
.
∎

To prove
theorem

22
, we divide the problem into two cases depending on whether the longest square in
w
is sufficiently short (
lemma

20
) or not (
lemma

21
).

Lemma 20
.

For any word
w
of length
n
that does not contain a square of length
\geq 2\sqrt{n}
,
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq n-\sqrt{n}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathit{SQ}_{w}=\sum_{z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}}\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)
.
For a fixed Lyndon factor
z\in\mathit{Lyn}_{w}
with
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}(z)|\geq 1
, the assumption on
w
implies that
|z|\leq\sqrt{n}-1
.
Thus, it follows from
lemma

18
that every circuit in
\Gamma_{w}
can be loaded with at most
\mathit{avg}_{w}(z)\leq\frac{|z|}{|z|+1}\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}-1}{\sqrt{n}}
squares.
Since there are at most
n
circuits to be loaded by
lemma

15
, we have

|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq\frac{n(\sqrt{n}-1)}{\sqrt{n}}=n-\sqrt{n}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 21
.

For any word
w
of length
n
that contains a square of length
\geq 2\sqrt{n}
,
|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq n-\Theta(\log n)
.

Proof.

Let
M\geq\sqrt{n}
be an integer such that there is a square of length
2M
of
w
.
Since the square contains all conjugates of length
M
,
we have
[z]_{M}\subseteq\mathit{Fac}_{w}
with its Lyndon root
z
.
It follows from
lemmas

10
and
11
that
\Gamma_{w}(m-1)
contains at least one circuit for any
m\in[1..M]
.
Since
lemma

19
implies that each circuit in
\Gamma_{w}(m-1)

can be loaded with at most
\frac{m}{m+1}
squares,
there are
M
circuits whose loaded squares amount up to

\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{m}{m+1}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(1-\frac{1}{m+1}\right)=M-\Theta(\log M).

By
lemma

15
, there are at most
n-M
remaining independent circuits,
which are loaded with at most
n-M
squares in total.
Putting these together, we have

|\mathit{SQ}_{w}|\leq(n-M)+(M-\Theta(\log M))=n-\Theta(\log M)\leq n-\Theta(\log n).

This concludes the proof.
∎

theorem

22
is obtained from
lemmas

20
and
21
.

Theorem 22
.

For any word
w
of length
n
,
the number of distinct squares in
w
is upper bounded by
n-\Theta(\log n)
.

4
Conclusion and future work

We presented another proof for the upper bound
n-\Theta(\log n)
for the number of distinct squares in a word of length
n
.
We would expect that the new insights obtained in this paper will be extended to lower the bound further.
Also, it would be interesting to see if our techniques can be applied to improve related bounds such as
bounds for distinct
k
-powers in a word
[
13
]
, and
bounds for distinct squares in a circular word
[
1
]
.
