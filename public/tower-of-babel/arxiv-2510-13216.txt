Edgington’s Method for Random-Effects Meta-Analysis Part II: Prediction
David Kronthaler, Leonhard Held

Abstract




Abstract

Statistical inference about the average effect in random-effects meta-analysis has been considered insufficient in the presence of substantial between-study heterogeneity. Predictive distributions are well-suited for quantifying heterogeneity since they are interpretable on the effect scale and provide clinically relevant information about future events. We construct predictive distributions accounting for uncertainty through confidence distributions from Edgington’s
p
-value combination method and the generalized heterogeneity statistic. Simulation results suggest that 95% prediction intervals typically achieve nominal coverage when more than three studies are available and effectively reflect skewness in effect estimates in scenarios with 20 or less studies. Formulations that ignore uncertainty in heterogeneity estimation typically fail to achieve correct coverage, underscoring the need for this adjustment in random-effects meta-analysis.

Keywords
:
Confidence distribution; estimation uncertainty; heterogeneity (variance); meta-analysis; prediction;
p
-value function.

1
Introduction

Meta-analysis describes statistical methodology for synthesizing evidence across independent studies. In
Kronthaler and Held, 2025
, we discussed Edgington’s method for estimation of the average effect in random-effects meta-analysis. Here, we focus on the prediction of future study effects. Predictive distributions enable meaningful inference under the random-effects model
(
Higgins et al., 2008b
)
. In this framework, variation in effect estimates is attributed to sampling noise and variability of true effects across studies, with the mean effect reflecting the average of the effect distribution
(
IntHout et al., 2016
)
. Accordingly, inference about the average effect may be insufficient in the presence of heterogeneity, and understanding the variability of effects across studies becomes essential
(
Riley et al., 2011
;
IntHout et al., 2016
)
.
Viechtbauer, 2006
emphasizes that
“quantifying the amount of heterogeneity and exploring its sources are among the most important aspects of systematic reviews”
.

Predictive distributions of future effects are useful for quantifying heterogeneity as they express variability on the effect measure scale and inform about clinically relevant future events
(
Higgins et al., 2008b
;
IntHout et al., 2016
)
. In contrast, traditional heterogeneity measures such as the estimated between-study variance and Higgins’
I^{2}
have been criticized for their limited interpretability and sensitivity to study sample sizes
(
IntHout et al., 2016
;
Borenstein et al., 2017
)
. For instance, Higgins’
I^{2}
, being a relative measure, does not convey the absolute effect variation, which is typically of interest in practice.

Frequentist predictive distributions are commonly summarized using prediction intervals, and several authors have advocated for reporting these in random-effects meta-analyses
(
Riley et al., 2011
;
Guddat et al., 2012
;
IntHout et al., 2016
)
.
Higgins et al., 2008b
suggest that
“predictive distributions are potentially the most relevant and complete statistical inferences to be drawn from a random-effects meta-analysis”
. Prediction intervals frequently suggest strong variability in responses to interventions or treatments. According to
IntHout et al., 2016
, in approximately 70% of random-effects meta-analyses in the Cochrane database with 95% confidence intervals not covering zero, 95% prediction intervals indicate that future effects may be zero or opposite in direction to the estimated average effect; in 20%, they include effects that are opposite in direction and at least as large in absolute magnitude as the estimated average effect.

Despite growing recognition of the need for meta-analytic methods that account for data skewness
(
Higgins et al., 2008a
;
Noma et al., 2022
;
Held et al., 2025
)
, most existing methods for constructing frequentist predictive distributions constrain them to be symmetric around the estimate of the average effect
(
Higgins et al., 2008b
;
Partlett and Riley, 2016
)
. These methods tend to be poorly calibrated for skewed data, with prediction intervals not achieving nominal coverage
(
Matrai et al., 2024
)
. Further, uncertainty about the heterogeneity estimate is typically addressed by applying a
t
-distribution, but this
post hoc
adjustment is relatively arbitrary, especially concerning the degrees of freedom, with several alternative methods proposed
(
Higgins et al., 2008b
;
Partlett and Riley, 2016
;
Veroniki et al., 2018
)
. Simulation studies indicate that predictive distributions adjusted in that manner may be poorly calibrated in meta-analyses with few studies or considerable heterogeneity
(
Partlett and Riley, 2016
;
Wang and Lee, 2019
)
.

To address these limitations, we propose constructing predictive distributions using Edgington’s method for random-effects meta-analysis. Edgington’s method serves as a viable substitute or complement to classical random-effects meta-analysis, yielding a confidence distribution
(
Schweder and Hjort, 2016
)
of the average effect which accurately reflects data skewness
(
Held et al., 2025
;
Kronthaler and Held, 2025
)
. We use this confidence distribution to adjust for estimation uncertainty and similarly account for uncertainty about the heterogeneity estimate through its confidence distribution implied by the generalized heterogeneity statistic
(
Viechtbauer, 2006
)
. We illustrate the methods with a case study and evaluate their performance in a simulation study.

2
Methods

2.1
The Random-Effects Model

The proposed predictive distributions are constructed assuming that future effects
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}
and true effects of observed studies
{\theta_{i}},i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, are exchangeable and jointly normally distributed around mean
\mu
with variance
{\tau^{2}}
. The effect estimates
{\hat{\theta}_{i}}
are assumed to be normally distributed around
{\theta_{i}}
with variance
{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}
, the squared standard error from study
i
, which is treated as fixed. This hierarchical
k+2
parameter model collapses to a two-parameter model by marginalization:

{\theta_{i}}\sim\Nor(\mu,{\tau^{2}}),\quad{\hat{\theta}_{i}}\mid{\theta_{i}}\sim\Nor({\theta_{i}},{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}})\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\hat{\theta}_{i}}\sim\Nor(\mu,{\tau^{2}}+{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}).

(1)

Under the exchangeability assumption, the target of prediction coincides with the estimation of the distribution of true effects. In contrast, predictive distributions should not be used to quantify heterogeneity under a fixed-effects model
(
Rice et al., 2018
)
, because in this framework
{\theta_{i}}
and
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}
are not connected via a distribution.

2.2
Prior Work on Predictive Distributions

To our knowledge, the earliest predictive distribution was proposed by
Skipka, 2006
, and can be understood as integrating the normal effect distribution over the normal sampling distribution of the inverse-variance weights (IVW) estimator
{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}
of the average effect:

f_{\Nor}({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}\mid{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}},{\hat{\tau}^{2}}+\se({\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}})^{2})=\int f_{\Nor}({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}\mid\mu^{\prime},{\hat{\tau}^{2}})\ f_{\Nor}(\mu^{\prime}\mid{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}},\se({\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}})^{2})\ {\mathrm{d}}\mu^{\prime},

where
f_{\Nor}
denotes a normal density. To account for uncertainty in
{\hat{\tau}^{2}}
, the Higgins–Thompson–Spiegelhalter (HTS) method applies a
t
-distribution with
k-2
degrees of freedom
(
Higgins et al., 2008b
)
:

{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}\sim{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}+t_{k-2}\sqrt{{\hat{\tau}^{2}}+\se({\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}})^{2}}.

The degrees of freedom
k-2
reflect the uncertainty in two parameter estimates, but several alternative distributions using different degrees of freedom or standard errors have been proposed
(
Partlett and Riley, 2016
;
Veroniki et al., 2018
)
. Simulation studies suggest limitations in this heterogeneity uncertainty adjustment
(
Partlett and Riley, 2016
;
Matrai et al., 2024
)
, and
Nagashima et al., 2018
proposed a parametric bootstrap procedure incorporating the standard error
{\se_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{HKSJ}}}}({\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}})
of the IVW estimator
{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}
by
Hartung and Knapp, 2001
and
Sidik and Jonkman, 2002
(
Sidik and Jonkman, 2002
; HKSJ):

\displaystyle{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}

\displaystyle={\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}+Z\cdot\tau-T\cdot{\se_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{HKSJ}}}}({\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}),

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle\sim\Nor(0,1),\quad T\sim t_{k-1}.

A sample
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}
is generated by drawing
{\tau^{2}}
from its confidence distribution implied by the exact distribution of Cochran’s
\Q
statistic
(
Biggerstaff and Jackson, 2008
)
, which is a weighted sum of
\chi^{2}
variables with weights depending on
{\tau^{2}}
, recomputing
{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}
and
{\se_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{HKSJ}}}}({\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}})
, and sampling random variables from a standard normal and a
t_{k-1}
-distribution. Further,
Wang and Lee, 2019
proposed a non-parametric approach based on the empirical distribution of ensemble estimates
{\hat{\theta}_{i}}^{*}
of
{\theta_{i}},i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, by
Louis, 1984
;
Louis, 1991
:

{\hat{\theta}_{i}}^{*}={\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}+\sqrt{\frac{{\hat{\tau}^{2}}}{{\hat{\tau}^{2}}+{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}}}\ ({\hat{\theta}_{i}}-{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}).

The ensembles are centered at
{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}
and calibrated to have asymptotic variance
{\tau^{2}}
, since the variances of
{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}
and
{\hat{\tau}^{2}}
are asymptotically negligible. Their empirical distribution is therefore an estimate of the predictive distribution. However,
Matrai et al., 2024
reported poor coverage of prediction intervals derived from this approach in scenarios with few studies, potentially due to not adjusting for estimation uncertainty and relying too strongly on the asymptotic argument.

2.3
Predictive Distributions using Edgington’s Method

Assuming that future effects
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}
and effects of observed studies
{\theta_{i}}
are exchangeable, the basic form of the proposed predictive distribution under the model in (
1
) is a
\Nor(\mu,{\tau^{2}})
. Difficulty lies in incorporating uncertainty about parameter estimates
{\hat{\mu}}
and
{\hat{\tau}^{2}}
. Within a frequentist framework, parameter uncertainty can be represented through confidence distributions. These are probability distributions over the parameter space that act as frequentist distribution estimators, analogous to Bayesian posteriors, but are constructed without prior distributions
(
Xie and Singh, 2013
;
Marschner, 2024
)
. Edgington’s method for estimation in random-effects meta-analysis yields a confidence distribution of the average effect with density
c(\mu\mid{\tau^{2}})
, which, in contrast to the confidence distribution stemming from classical random-effects meta-analysis, preserves data skewness
(
Held et al., 2025
;
Kronthaler and Held, 2025
)
.

In brief, Edgington’s method for meta-analysis combines study-specific
p
-value functions for the average effect
\mu
using the sum of
p
-values
(
Edgington, 1972
)
. Study-specific
p
-value functions are constructed from the pivotal statistics
Z(\mu)=\Var({{\hat{\theta}_{i}}})^{-1/2}({\hat{\theta}_{i}}-\mu)
, which follow a standard normal distribution. Under the random-effects framework, heterogeneity is accounted for by using
\Var({{\hat{\theta}_{i}}})={\tau^{2}}+{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}
, scaling the
p
-value functions to have lower slope, thereby downweighting large studies as compared to a fixed-effect framework which uses
\Var({\hat{\theta}_{i}})={\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}
, and producing wider confidence intervals.

We construct a simple predictive (confidence) distribution
PCD-fixed
by integrating the normal effect distribution over Edgington’s confidence distribution
c(\mu\mid{\hat{\tau}^{2}})
, thereby accounting for uncertainty about the estimation of
\mu
:

c_{\text{fixed}}({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}\mid{\hat{\tau}^{2}})=\int f_{\Nor}({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}\mid\mu,{\hat{\tau}^{2}})\ c(\mu\mid{\hat{\tau}^{2}})\ {\mathrm{d}}\mu.

(2)

This approach resembles those of standard predictive distributions, but differs in integrating over a confidence distribution of
\mu
rather than a sampling distribution of
{\hat{\mu}}
. We emphasize that integration over the confidence distribution of a parameter is not generally guaranteeing correct frequentist coverage, and performance should be investigated via simulations
(
Schweder and Hjort, 2016
;
Pawitan and Lee, 2021
)
.

The predictive distribution in (
2
) relies on a fixed estimate
{\hat{\tau}^{2}}
and ignores uncertainty in its estimation. As in
Kronthaler and Held, 2025
, we incorporate heterogeneity estimation uncertainty by integrating over a confidence distribution of
{\tau^{2}}
, implied by the generalized heterogeneity statistic

\Q({\tau^{2}})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}+{\tau^{2}}}\ \left({\hat{\theta}_{i}}-{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}({\tau^{2}})\right)^{2},

which varies in
{\tau^{2}}
and follows a
\chi_{k-1}^{2}
distribution under the model in (
1
)
(
Viechtbauer, 2006
)
. The version
PCD-simplified
in (
3
) simplistically uses a plug-in estimate
{\hat{\tau}^{2}}
in Edgington’s confidence distribution, whereas the version
PCD-full
in (
4
) fully integrates over the parameter conditionals:

\displaystyle c_{\text{simplified}}({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}})

\displaystyle=\int\int f_{\Nor}({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}\mid\mu,{\tau^{2}})\ c(\mu\mid{\hat{\tau}^{2}})\ c({\tau^{2}})\ {\mathrm{d}}\mu\ {\mathrm{d}}{\tau^{2}},

(3)

\displaystyle c_{\text{full}}({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}})

\displaystyle=\int\int f_{\Nor}({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}\mid\mu,{\tau^{2}})\ c(\mu\mid{\tau^{2}})\ c({\tau^{2}})\ {\mathrm{d}}\mu\ {\mathrm{d}}{\tau^{2}}.

(4)

We remark that
Nagashima et al., 2018
employ a related idea: they draw samples of
{\tau^{2}}
from the confidence distribution of the standard
\Q
statistic, whose distributional parameters depend on
{\tau^{2}}

(
Biggerstaff and Jackson, 2008
)
, and then incorporate these samples within their bootstrap algorithm.

The integrals in (
3
) and (
4
) are computationally demanding to evaluate via deterministic numerical integration. Therefore, we extend the Monte Carlo sampling algorithm presented in
Kronthaler and Held, 2025
to generate samples of the three unknown quantities
{\tau^{2}}
,
\mu
and
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}
for the predictive distributions in (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
). The algorithm generates independent random variables, denoted by
{{\tau}_{(b)}^{2*}}
,
{{\mu}_{(b)}^{*}}
, and
{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(b)}}
in draw
b\in\{1,\dots,B\}
. A summary of the Monte Carlo sampling procedure is given in Table
1
.

We recommend using at least
B=100{,}000
draws to reduce Monte Carlo noise. For three to 50 studies, this requires approximately four to eight seconds for the PCD-full distribution and yields relatively stable quantiles of the predictive distributions. The empirical distribution of
{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(1)},\ldots,{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(B)}}
estimates the predictive distribution and enables computation of prediction intervals, including equi-tailed and highest confidence density (HCD) intervals. The HCD interval is formulated analogous to a highest posterior density (HPD) interval in Bayesian statistics, minimizing interval width for a given target coverage. Its definition can be formulated analogously to that of a Bayesian HPD interval:

Definition 2.1
.

Highest confidence density prediction interval:
Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
be a fixed prediction level. A
\gamma\cdot 100\%
prediction interval
I=[c_{l},c_{u}]
is called a
highest confidence density prediction interval
(HCDP interval) if

c({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}})\geq c(\tilde{\theta}_{\scriptstyle{\text{new}}})

for all
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}\in I
and all
\tilde{\theta}_{\scriptstyle{\text{new}}}\notin I
.

Equi-tailed and HCDP intervals may differ since predictive distributions are not restricted to symmetry, but inherit skewness from Edgington’s confidence distribution. In intervention-related meta-analyses, it may be valuable to quantify the likelihood that a future effect will be beneficial, neutral, or harmful
(
Riley et al., 2011
)
. We intentionally refer to this as the
confidence
or
confidence probability
of the event
(
Marschner, 2024
)
to emphasize that our predictive distributions incorporate epistemic probability through confidence distributions. Employing a margin
\delta
, these confidences can be estimated directly. For example, the confidence that
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}
is equal to or larger than
\delta
is

\text{Conf}({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}\geq\delta)=\int_{\delta}^{\infty}c({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}})\ {\mathrm{d}}{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}},

with Monte Carlo estimator

\widehat{\text{Conf}}({\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}\geq\delta)=B^{-1}\left(\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{I}\left\{{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(b)}}\geq\delta\right\}\right),

where
\mathbb{I}\left\{{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(b)}}\geq\delta\right\}=1
if
{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(b)}}\geq\delta
and zero otherwise. The proposed methods are available in
R
through the
edgemeta
package (
https://github.com/davidkronthaler-dk/edgemeta
).

2.4
Example: Corticosteroids and Mortality in Hospitalized COVID-19 Patients

In
Held et al., 2025
, a case study was presented based on seven randomized controlled trials reporting log odds ratios quantifying the association between corticosteroids, compared to placebo or usual care treatment, and mortality in hospitalized COVID-19 patients
(
WHO REACT Working Group, 2020
)
. We revisit this example to extend the earlier analysis by examining predictive distributions. A summary of the data is provided in Table
2
.

Figure
1
presents the results of a random-effects meta-analysis. Between-study heterogeneity is estimated using the Paule–Mandel estimator
(
Paule and Mandel, 1982
)
to match the approach of the original analysis in
WHO REACT Working Group, 2020
, yielding
{\hat{\tau}^{2}}
= 0.03 (95% confidence interval from 0.00 to 2.13, Higgins’
I^{2}
= 14.01%). Using the HKSJ method, the estimated average effect is -0.36 (95% confidence interval from -0.72 to -0.004, p = 0.048, skewness of 0.00), while the CD-Edgington estimator yields -0.18 (95% confidence interval from -0.61 to 0.46, p = 0.39, skewness of 0.20;
Kronthaler and Held, 2025
), both indicating a beneficial effect of corticosteroids on average. Interval skewness is computed as

\beta=\frac{\text{upper}+\text{lower}-\text{2 center}}{\text{upper}-\text{lower}}.

Figure
1
also displays the confidence densities of the average effect obtained from the HKSJ and CD-Edgington approach. Unlike the symmetric HKSJ confidence distribution, the CD-Edgington distribution has a Fisher skewness coefficient of 0.90, reflecting the right-skewness of the data, as quantified by Fisher’s weighted skewness coefficient of
3.77

(
Ferschl, 1980
)
. The CD-Edgington distribution estimates a confidence probability of 0.80 that the average effect is negative on the log odds scale (favorable to corticosteroids), compared with 0.99 under the HKSJ approach.

3
Simulation Study

3.1
Design

We present our proof-of-concept simulation study
(
Heinze et al., 2024
)
according to the ADEMP framework
(
Morris et al., 2019
)
.

3.1.1
Aims

Assess calibration and sharpness of the proposed predictive distributions.

3.1.2
Data-Generating Mechanism

The data-generating mechanism is described in detail in
Kronthaler and Held, 2025
. We vary the number of studies
k\in
{3, 5, 10, 20, 50}, the between-study heterogeneity determined by Higgins’
I^{2}

\in
{0%, 30%, 60%, 90%}, the number of large studies
k_{\text{large}}\in
{0, 1, 2} and whether the true effects
{\theta_{i}}
and future effects
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}
are generated from a normal distribution or from a left-skewed skew-normal distribution
(
Azzalini and Capitanio, 2013
)
. Study sizes are set to 50 for normal studies and to 500 for large studies. We perform the simulation study in a full-factorial manner. Deviating from the design presented previously, in each iteration we generate
k
true effects with effect estimates and standard errors, and additionally 10,000 future effects
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{(j)}}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,10{,}000\}
, for the evaluation of predictive performance.

3.1.3
Estimands and Other Targets

Predictive distributions estimate the joint effect distribution, corresponding to a
\Nor(\mu,{\tau^{2}})
with mean
\mu=-0.3
and variance
{\tau^{2}}
determined by Higgins’
I^{2}
, or a skew-normal
\SN(\xi,\omega,\alpha)
with skewness
\alpha=-4
and location
\xi
and scale
\omega
obtained by moment-matching such that the mean is
-0.3
and the variance is
{\tau^{2}}

(
Azzalini and Capitanio, 2013
)
.

3.1.4
Methods

We compare the equi-tailed 95% prediction intervals obtained from Edgington’s predictive distributions with the HTS prediction interval
(
Higgins et al., 2008b
)
and the parametric bootstrap prediction interval
(
Nagashima et al., 2018
)
. The latter two are accessed through the
R

meta
package
(
Balduzzi et al., 2019
)
. Performance measures that investigate the entire predictive distribution are computed for the proposed and for the HTS predictive distributions, which can be easily constructed. The complete predictive distribution by
Nagashima et al., 2018
is currently not accessible. The restricted maximum likelihood estimator is used for estimating heterogeneity across all methods, as it has shown good performance in simulation studies
(
Langan et al., 2018
)
. For methods employing stochastic algorithms we generate
B=100{,}000
samples.

3.1.5
Performance Measures

The primary outcome is the coverage of 95% prediction intervals. Typically, a prediction interval should achieve nominal coverage. However, under no heterogeneity, 100% coverage is desirable, reflecting that the interval covers the common effect
\mu
which coincides with the future effects
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{(j)}}
. In each iteration
s
, the estimated coverage corresponds to the proportion of future effects
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{(j)}}
which lie between the limits of the prediction interval:

{\text{Cov}}_{s}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{10{,}000}\mathbb{I}\{{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{(j)}}\in 95\%\text{ PI}\}}{10{,}000},\quad\Var({\text{Cov}}_{s})=\frac{{\text{Cov}}_{s}(1-{\text{Cov}}_{s})}{10{,}000}.

The variance is maximized for a coverage of
0.5
, inducing a maximum within-iteration standard error of 0.005. To account for between-iteration variability, the variance was estimated from pilot studies and was as large as
0.05
in simulations with 2500 iterations. To achieve sufficient precision we require the Monte Carlo standard error for the mean coverage (
Cov
) to be at maximum
0.005
. We estimate the required number of iterations as:

{n_{sim}}=\frac{\widehat{\Var}({\text{Cov}}_{s})}{{\se_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{MC}}}}({\text{Cov}})^{2}}=\frac{0.05}{(0.005)^{2}}=2000.

To ensure precise estimates even if results differ from pilot scenarios, we increased the iterations to 4000.

Secondary outcomes include skewness and width of 95% prediction intervals. Skewness is evaluated by correlating prediction interval skewness
\beta
with the skewness of effect estimates and true effects using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, and by quantifying sign agreement using Cohen’s kappa. Effect estimate skewness is computed as Fisher’s weighted skewness coefficient
(
Ferschl, 1980
)
:

\gamma=\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}}\left(\hat{\theta}_{i}-{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}^{\text{(fixed)}}\right)^{3}\right)\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}}}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}}\left(\hat{\theta}_{i}-{\hat{\mu}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{IVW}}}}^{\text{(fixed)}}\right)^{2}\right)^{3/2}}.

Setting
{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}
to one yields Fisher’s (unweighted) skewness coefficient, which is used to estimate skewness of true effects. The HTS prediction interval, due to its additive construction always being symmetric, has always zero skewness.

The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS;
Matheson and Winkler, 1976
;
Gneiting and Raftery, 2007
) is used to evaluate the entire predictive distribution. It is a strictly proper scoring rule, applied here to all simulated future outcomes
{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{(j)}}
with the average score still being strictly proper, and simultaneously captures calibration and sharpness
(
Gneiting et al., 2008
)
. Lower values indicate better predictive performance. The CRPS can be written as:

\text{CRPS}({F_{{\theta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}}}},\theta_{0})=\E\left[|{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(1)}}-\theta_{0}|\right]-\frac{1}{2}\E\left[|{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(1)}}-{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(2)}}|\right],

(5)

where
\theta_{0}
denotes an observed future outcome, and
{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(1)}}
and
{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(2)}}
are independent random variables distributed according to the predictive distribution. Our simulation imposes substantial computational demands, since each predictive distribution must be evaluated against 10,000 future effects.
Gneiting et al., 2008
proposed the
energy score
, an extension of the CRPS to multivariate settings, which is computed using a Monte Carlo approximation. We adopt this approximation to the univariate CRPS and approximate it for a single
\theta_{0}
as:

\text{CRPS}({{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(1)},\ldots,{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(B)}},\theta_{0})\approx\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}|{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(b)}}-\theta_{0}|-\frac{1}{2(B-1)}\sum_{b=1}^{B-1}|{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(b)}}-{{\theta}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{new}}}^{*(b+1)}}|.

3.1.6
Computation Details

The simulation study is programmed in the
R
programming language
(
R Core Team, 2023
)
and conducted in
R
version 4.5.0 (2025-04-11) on a remote Debian GNU/Linux server (platform: x86_64-pc-linux-gnu). Random number generator streams are employed to ensure reproducibility in parallel execution. The code, results and detailed information on the computational environment are publicly available on Github (

3.2
Results

In 37 iterations (
0.008
%) the parametric bootstrap prediction interval did not converge. Non-convergences occurred only in scenarios with three studies and more frequently under large heterogeneity (Table S1). All other approaches including the proposed PCD methods always produced valid results. We applied case-wise deletion and evaluated performance only based on convergent repetitions.

3.2.1
Coverage of 95% Prediction Intervals

Figure
shows the mean coverage of 95% prediction intervals for normally distributed effects. Without heterogeneity, all methods typically achieve mean coverage above the nominal level, as expected: under no heterogeneity, future effects coincide with the common effect, and prediction intervals that cover it achieve 100% coverage. The PCD-full and PCD-simplified intervals are typically close to 100% coverage, whereas the PCD-fixed method consistently falls farthest from 100% coverage. The HTS interval typically attains 100% coverage for three to five studies, but coverage declines as the number of studies increases. The bootstrap interval approaches 100% coverage as the number of studies increases, but has consistently lower coverage than prediction intervals from Edgington’s method with heterogeneity uncertainty adjustment.

Under heterogeneity, all methods approach nominal coverage as the number of studies increases to 50, generally performing better when heterogeneity is large. However, the HTS and PCD-fixed prediction intervals demonstrate relatively poor performance. For Higgins’
I^{2}
of 30% and 60%, the HTS prediction interval exhibits a U-shaped pattern in its coverage: it tends to over-cover for three studies and under-cover for five to 50 studies. Notably, it performs well under large heterogeneity and at least five studies. The PCD-fixed prediction interval generally exhibits undercoverage, often falling below 80% under three studies, and approaching nominal coverage only with 50 studies and
I^{2}
of 60% and 90%.

The prediction intervals from Edgington’s method which incorporate heterogeneity estimation uncertainty (PCD-simplified and PCD-full) and from the parametric bootstrap are often close to nominal coverage under heterogeneity and are only minimally affected by the number of large studies. Coverages of these methods are typically similar, but slight differences occur: The bootstrap approach is closest to nominal coverage for Higgins
I^{2}
of 30% and 90% with three studies, whereas the PCD-full method shows slightly better coverage in several scenarios with Higgins
I^{2}
of 30% and five to ten studies, and with Higgins
I^{2}
of 60% and three studies. The PCD-simplified interval often deviates strongest from nominal coverage in scenarios with few studies among these three methods. The PCD-full interval typically achieves higher coverage than the PCD-simplified interval, reflecting the additional uncertainty incorporated.

Highly comparable patterns are observed under a skew-normal effect distribution, with only minimal declines in coverage for the three well-performing methods (Figure S2). As recommended by
Matrai et al., 2024
, we also present the distributions of coverage probabilities in Figures S3 to S9. The three well-performing intervals show highly similar coverage distributions across methods and effect distributions. In contrast, coverage probability distributions for the HTS prediction interval often exhibit a bimodal shape under scenarios with Higgins’
I^{2}
of 30% and 60%, with one mode near the nominal level and another around 0.5. Similar patterns are observed for the PCD-fixed prediction interval, which typically shows an even wider spread in these scenarios.

3.2.2
Skewness of 95% Prediction Intervals

Figure
shows the Pearson correlation between the skewness of prediction intervals and effect estimates under normally distributed effects. The correlation is typically moderate to strong for the PCD-full prediction interval under less than 50 studies, and often for the PCD-fixed interval under less than 20 studies. The PCD-simplified interval typically yields weaker correlations, though it remains substantial under some scenarios with zero to moderate heterogeneity. As the number of studies increases, this correlation typically tends toward zero for all methods, potentially reflecting the increasing symmetry of the effect estimate distribution as it converges toward the underlying normal distribution. The number of large studies strongly affects the parametric bootstrap interval. When none are present, the correlation remains near zero. As the number of large studies increases, the correlation increases and can match that of the most skew-sensitive methods in scenarios with few studies, several large ones, and zero to moderate heterogeneity.

Similar trends are observed under a skew-normal effect distribution (Figure S10) and for Cohen’s kappa (Figures S11 and S12). Pearson correlations and Cohen’s kappa are generally lower when assessing prediction interval skewness and skewness of true effects, with only the PCD-full prediction interval reflecting effect skewness reasonably well under large heterogeneity and less than 50 studies (Figures S13 to S16).

3.2.3
Width of 95% Prediction Intervals

Figure
displays the average width of 95% prediction intervals for normally distributed effects. For three studies, the HTS prediction interval is substantially wider than all other intervals, particularly if heterogeneity is large. This may be attributed to the use of the
t
-distribution, which exhibits heavy tails when degrees of freedom are low. The PCD-fixed prediction interval is consistently the narrowest. The prediction intervals derived from the parametric bootstrap and from Edgington’s approach accounting for heterogeneity estimation uncertainty (PCD-simplified and PCD-full) are similar in average width across all scenarios, with a tendency for the parametric bootstrap intervals to be wider under few studies. The average widths of all intervals converge under 50 studies. The presence of large studies substantially narrows intervals when only few studies are available. Highly comparable trends are observed under a skew-normal effect distribution (Figure S17).

3.3
Continuous Ranked Probability Scores

Figure S18 display the average CRPS for normally distributed effects. CRPS increases with increasing heterogeneity, reflecting decreasing sharpness of distributions. The predictive distributions based on Edgington’s method outperform the HTS predictive distribution when the number of studies is small and/or there is no or low heterogeneity. For ten or more studies, CRPS are practically identical across methods. For two large studies and Higgins’
I^{2}
of 60% and 90%, the CRPS increases in the number of studies for Edgington’s predictive distributions. However, the effect is relatively modest and average CRPS remains stable for ten or more studies. Trends are highly comparable under a skew-normal effect distribution (Figure S19).

3.4
Summary of Simulation Results

Simulation results suggest that the predictive distributions PCD-full and PCD-simplified, which account for heterogeneity estimation uncertainty, are typically well-calibrated in scenarios with more than three studies, with 95% prediction intervals often approaching nominal coverage. They outperform the HTS prediction interval in terms of coverage and perform comparably to the parametric bootstrap interval, while sometimes being narrower under scenarios with few studies. The PCD-full interval captures the skewness of estimates effectively, achieving substantial correlations between interval skewness and data skewness under less than 50 studies. In contrast, the simpler predictive distribution PCD-fixed, which does not account for heterogeneity estimation uncertainty, produces 95% prediction intervals that approach nominal coverage only when the number of studies is very large. Therefore, this method is unlikely to be useful in practical applications.

4
Discussion

We proposed a novel approach for constructing predictive distributions in random-effects meta-analysis that accounts for parameter uncertainty through Edgington’s confidence distribution and through a confidence distribution of the heterogeneity parameter. In practice, predictive distributions are commonly summarized by prediction intervals, partly because many widely used software implementations provide only such summaries. However, we consider this reduction unnecessary for many predictive tasks, as it discards distributional information. By enabling direct access to samples from the predictive distribution, our method facilitates broader inferences beyond interval summaries.

We applied the confidence distribution of the generalized heterogeneity statistic, which varies in
{\tau^{2}}
, to incorporate uncertainty with respect to the heterogeneity parameter, while
Nagashima et al., 2018
employed the confidence distribution of the standard
\Q
statistic, whose exact distribution has parameters dependent on
{\tau^{2}}
. The simulation suggests that both approaches perform relatively similar in terms of calibration and sharpness, and further investigation is needed to assess whether methodological differences lead to performance differences under specific conditions. However, generally we conclude that these methods, though subject to Monte Carlo noise, should be favored over the the HTS approach.

Our simulation study was limited to effect estimates on the standardized mean difference scale. Future work should expand these evaluations by incorporating alternative effect scales and by exploring model misspecification through effect distributions beyond the normal and skew-normal, as in
Matrai et al., 2024
. Further, following
Held et al., 2024
, Edgington’s predictive distributions could be extended by incorporating study-specific weights to downweight studies at risk of bias.

Since the proposed predictive distributions are restricted to be unimodal and are limited in extent of flexibility due to the use of the normal model, extensions could further focus on accommodating a broader range of effect distributions, such as multimodal or skewed distributions, to more accurately reflect complex distributional patterns in effect estimates. For instance, the skew-normal distribution has been applied in meta-analysis by
Lee and Thompson, 2007
within a Bayesian framework, and a multivariate version has been used by
Negeri and Beyene, 2020
for the meta-analysis of diagnostic test accuracy studies under a frequentist framework. Both conclude that accounting for deviations from normality can improve precision when the underlying effects are truly skewed. To our knowledge, a skew-normal model has not yet been explored for frequentist predictive distributions. Our methodology could be extended to incorporate a skew-normal effect distribution by using standardized skew-normal pivots to obtain individual study specific
p
-value functions, conditional on moment-estimated shape and scale parameters
(
Thiuthad and Pal, 2019
)
under a univariate formulation of the normal-skew-normal model by
Negeri and Beyene, 2020
. A confidence distribution of the location parameter could then be constructed by combining
p
-values using Edgington’s method or another
p
-value combination technique. However, it remains unclear how to appropriately incorporate uncertainty about the scale and shape parameter estimates, which we strongly recommend, as we here have demonstrated the value of accounting for estimation uncertainty in meta-analytic models.

In summary, in
Kronthaler and Held, 2025
and in the present work, we have discussed Edgington’s approach for combining
p
-value functions, or equivalently, confidence distributions, for both estimation and prediction in random-effects meta-analysis. Although further investigation is needed, results indicate that Edgington’s method has potential as a viable alternative or complement to classical random-effects meta-analysis.
