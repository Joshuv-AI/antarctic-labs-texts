GraphGhost: Tracing Structures Behind Large Language Models
Xinnan Dai, Kai Guo, Chung-Hsiang Lo, Shenglai Zeng, Jiayuan Ding, Dongsheng Luo, Subhabrata Mukherjee, Jiliang Tang

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate remarkable reasoning capabilities, yet the structural mechanisms underlying these abilities remain under explored. In this work, we introduce GraphGhost, a unified framework that represents neuron activations and their signal propagation as graphs, explaining how LLMs capture structural semantics from sequential inputs and generate outputs through structurally consistent mechanisms. This graph-based perspective enables us to employ graph algorithms such as PageRank to characterize the properties of LLMs, revealing both shared and model-specific reasoning behaviors across diverse datasets. We further identify the activated neurons within GraphGhost and evaluate them through structural interventions, showing that edits to key neuron nodes can trigger reasoning collapse, altering both logical flow and semantic understanding. Together, these contributions position GraphGhost as a powerful tool for analyzing, intervening in, and ultimately understanding the structural foundations of reasoning in LLMs.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated promising capabilities in capturing structural information from text
Sui et al. 2024
;

;

To understand the mechanisms of constructing reasoning structures from text, prior studies have often simplified reasoning tasks into toy examples such as graph-based reasoning to examine how LLMs trace reasoning paths within giving structures
Sanford et al. 2024
. The underlying hypothesis is that uncovering how decoder-only transformers construct such graphs may yield insights that generalize to real-world tasks
Saparov et al. 2025
. While these toy graph settings provide preliminary insights—highlighting mechanisms such as token merging
Dai et al. 2025b
and edge memorization
Wang et al. 2024
, they fall short when applied to complex, real-world structured data. To bridge this gap, we introduce GraphGhost, a framework that provides a structural perspective on how LLMs across different architectures perform reasoning tasks in diverse domains. GraphGhost captures neurons and their signal propagation inside LLMs, revealing how neurons are activated across samples and datasets, enabling shared semantic understanding and supporting the construction of reasoning processes across contexts.

As illustrated in Figure
1
a), GraphGhost represents a structural mechanism modeled within LLMs, where neuron activations capture semantic information and guide token outputs. In the sentence-reading stage, LLMs merge tokens to derive semantic meaning, thereby activating specific neurons associated with those tokens. These activated neurons then propagate signals to higher layers, ultimately influencing the logit neurons in the output layers. Notably, while certain neurons are activated for the given samples, many remain silent. These silent neurons are reserved for unseen inputs from different domains, enabling shared output behaviors across contexts. For example, the symbol ‘X’ may activate the ‘=’ symbol in mathematical equations, even if the current sample does not require it, leaving those neurons in a silent state until needed.

We define neurons as nodes and the signal propagation among neurons as edges. This representation allows us to analyze LLMs from a graph perspective.
For instance, by inspecting high in-degree nodes or applying PageRank, we can identify key nodes that control next-token prediction, as further elaborated in Section
3
.
Furthermore, we evaluate the effectiveness of these nodes through structural interventions, such as node removal (Figure
1
b)). Removing specific nodes can lead to semantic shifts, such as switching to another language, or logical changes. In addition, we conduct statistical analyses of these structural perturbations to quantify their effects, as presented in Section
4
. In conclusion, our key contributions are summarized as:

1.

We introduce GraphGhost, a unified framework represented in graphs, explaining how LLMs capture structural semantics from sequential inputs and generate outputs through a structurally consistent mechanism.

2.

We provide a graph-based perspective for understanding LLMs and apply graph algorithms on GraphGhost to characterize the properties of LLMs, revealing both unique and shared behaviors across different models and datasets.

3.

We identify the activated neurons within LLMs and evaluate them by editing the structure of GraphGhost. Our experiments show that key neuron nodes can trigger reasoning collapse, altering both the logical flow and semantic understanding.

2.3
Path tracing in the decoder-only transformer

Graph reasoning tasks, particularly path reasoning, offer a prototypical setting for understanding how structural information in sequential inputs can be mapped to outputs that describe those structures
Wang et al. 2023
. In this task, the model is provided with a graph description and a query consisting of a start node and an end node, and the objective for LLMs or decoder-only transformers is to predict the path connecting the two nodes
Dai et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
. We adopt this basic setup to investigate how decoder-only transformers perform path reasoning. Following prior work
Wang et al. 2024
, we use simplified graph descriptions, and a query (“from start point S to end point E”) and must predict the connecting path.
as illustrated in Figure
2(a)
, and train a 5-layer GPT-2 architecture decoder-only transformer on this task. The trained model achieves 98% accuracy on the test set. We then apply the circuit-tracing method, specifically using transcoder
Dunefsky et al. 2024
, to analyze how the transformer models the path-finding process, as shown in Figure
2(b)
. Full experimental details are provided in Appendix
C.1
.

Circuit tracing reveals how decoder-only transformers review the previous context to identify related nodes and predict the next token.
From a structural understanding perspective, neighboring nodes are merged in the middle layers. For example, at the third layer, node 4 is merged with node 5, forming the semantic representation of the edge between them. Further, in generating the output tokens, the final token in the sequence receives the message that an edge exists between 4 and 5 at layer 3, leading the model to predict 5 as the next token.

From this toy example, we observe that decoder-only transformers are not only capable of reasoning about paths within given graph structures, but that their underlying mechanism involves activated neurons propagating signals across layers. However, beyond the toy example with decoder-only transformers, does this mechanism extend to LLMs that can solve more complex tasks expressed in natural language? To address this, we introduce GraphGhost, a unified framework that represents these neuron activations and their signal flows as graphs.
GraphGhost enables us to trace and mergeattribution graphs defined in Section
2.1

across diverse reasoning tasks, revealing how LLMs encode and reuse structural reasoning patterns.
In the following sections, we apply GraphGhost to multiple models and domains to analyze how their internal reasoning structures build up, interact, and generalize.

For a target token

A_{n+1}=\text{CircuitTracer}(t_{n+1}),

(t_{i},\ell)

h^{(\ell)}(t_{i})=f^{(\ell)}(h^{(\ell-1)}(t_{i}))
,
and each directed edge
w_{s\to t}
encodes the contribution from neuron
s
to
t

\hat{T}(t_{n+1})=\{\,t_{i}\mid(t_{i},\ell)\in A_{n+1}\,\}
.
where
\ell
denotes the layer index.

Beyond the toy examples, we apply

GraphGhost traces neuron activations that contribute to next-token generation, represents them as local attribution graphs, and merges these across samples into a global weighted graph.
This unified structure reveals stable signal propagation patterns shared across tasks and models.

3.1
Tracing the GraphGhost

To construct a unified GraphGhost that captures reasoning structures shared across multiple samples,
we aggregate the attribution graphs obtained from each sample.
Given a set of samples
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{m}\}
, where each sample
s_{i}
contains an answer token
t_{n}^{(i)}
,
we initialize a global graph
G=(V,E,W)
with empty node, edge, and weight sets.
For each sample
s_{i}
, we start from its answer token
t_{n}^{(i)}
and iteratively trace the contributing tokens and their local attribution graph using the
\text{CircuitTracer}(\cdot)
function.
Each traced graph produces a set of neuron nodes
\hat{V}
and directed edges
\hat{E}
, which are merged into the global GraphGhost.
If an edge
e\in\hat{E}
does not exist in
E
, it is added with an initial weight
W(e)=1
; otherwise, its weight is incremented by one.
This cumulative weighting reflects the frequency of edge occurrences across samples, capturing the stability and recurrence of signal propagation pathways.
After processing all samples, the resulting GraphGhost
G=(V,E,W)
represents a multi-sample structural abstraction of the model’s internal reasoning dynamics, which is a weighted directed graph describing neuron-level signal propagation.

fromAppendix
B

3.2
Experiments setting

We evaluate a range of LLMs and datasets to investigate whether GraphGhost constitutes a general mechanism across models. Specifically, we consider Qwen3-0.6B (28 layers), Qwen2.5-1.5B (28 layers), Llama3.1-8B-Instruct (32 layers), DeepSeek-distilled-Qwen2.5B-1.5B (DS-Qwen-1.5B) (28 layers), and DeepSeek-distilled-Llama-8B (DS-Llama-8B) (32 layers). For datasets, we examine three domains: (i) mathematical reasoning (GSM8K, MAWPS), (ii) logical reasoning (ProntoQA, BoolQA), and (iii) scientific reasoning (ARC-Easy, QASC). To elicit the models’ reasoning processes, we apply Chain-of-Thought prompting. Following
Hanna et al. 2025
, we then train transcoders at every layer for each dataset–model pair to probe neuron activations during reasoning, ensuring that the transcoders effectively capture the most strongly activated neurons. All the experiments are run on a single H200 GPU. Full experimental details are provided in Appendix
C.2
.

3.4
Analysis on GraphGhost

In Figure

._{22}

Semantic understanding

In Section
2.3
, we discussed how edge information is merged within the inner layers. Building on this, we analyze the token-merging behavior of neuron nodes by examining their average in-degree across layers in LLMs’ settings. The statistical results, summarized in the Appendix
D
and illustrated in Figure
5
, show the top tokens across different datasets and models. We observe that the tokens with the highest in-degree are domain-sensitive. For example, in the ProntoQA dataset, the model is more sensitive to the period token than to the comma. This suggests that LLMs activate specific neurons in response to domain-specific data to capture semantics. Across models, however, the activated neurons are generally consistent: tokens such as <space>, period, and “the" consistently appear among the top-ranked, and this alignment persists even after finetuning.

in-degree ratio

(t_{i},\ell)

4
GraphGhost affects the reasoning process

GraphGhost paves a new way in which LLM reasoning processes can be analyzed and controlled.
To evaluate this, we perform perturbation experiments at two complementary levels.
(i) At the input level, we modify the contributing tokens identified in Section
4.1
to examine how altering key linguistic elements affects the model’s reasoning trajectory and output behaviors.
(ii) At the neuron level, we intervene on the influential neuron nodes detected via in-degree analysis and PageRank in Section
4.2
, assessing how structural disruptions within the reasoning pathways influence the model’s internal logic flow.
Together, these two perturbation perspectives reveal how surface-level token changes and internal structural shifts jointly shape the reasoning dynamics captured by GraphGhost.

4.1
Impact of contribution tokens

GraphGhost in LLMs leverages contributing tokens to capture context and perform reasoning. In practice, we assess their importance by masking tokens, replacing each with a blank token. To quantify this effect, we evaluate two metrics: Sparsity and Fidelity. Sparsity measures the proportion of contributing tokens in a given question, while Fidelity evaluates whether the answer generated from only the contributing tokens remains consistent with the original answer. Formally,
\mathrm{Sparsity}=\frac{\#C}{\#T}
and
\mathrm{Fidelity}_{T}=\mathds{1}(LLM(C),LLM(T))
, where
C
denotes the set of contributing tokens,
T
is the full set of tokens, and
\mathds{1}
is an indicator function returning 1 if the two answers are identical and 0 otherwise. In practice, we use gemini-2.5-flash-lite to identify whether the two predictions are the same, with the prompt as: "Comparing two answers for a question: Answer1 {
LLM(\hat{T})
}, Answer2 {
LLM(\hat{T})
}. Do they have the same final results?"
To compare the effectiveness of GraphGhost across models, we evaluate 100 samples that are solvable by Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen2.5-1.5B, and DS-Qwen-1.5B. The results are presented in Figure

4.2
Modification on the graph structure

To evaluate whether GraphGhost successfully identifies the most influential tokens in LLMs, we mute specific neuron nodes and observe the resulting changes in the outputs. Specifically, neuron nodes are muted by setting the corresponding positions of their layer–token pairs to zero during the reasoning process.
We evaluate the impact through both case studies and statistical analysis.

Case study

First, as discussed in Section
3.4
, the impacts include changes in semantic understanding and logic shifting. As shown in Figure
10
, the semantic understanding is reflected in changing the language formulation and word expression. When the neuron node “the” is deleted at layer 20, the English semantic pathway is disrupted, activating Chinese neurons that share the same semantic signal. As a result, the output appears in Chinese, directly translated from English. Furthermore, in the word-expression change case, deleting the neuron “.” at layer 17 causes the token “/” to replace “÷.” This suggests that another neuron is responsible for propagating the division signal, expressing it as “/.” These findings indicate that LLMs store semantics redundantly across multiple neuron nodes: when the most relevant neuron is muted, the model relies on alternative neurons that produce similar expressions. In Figure
11
, we demonstrate how GraphGhost influences reasoning by altering key steps or driving the generation into loops. In the key-step change case, deleting the neuron node “.” at layer 25 introduces a new reasoning token such as “So", ultimately leading the model to produce the correct answer. In the loop case, deleting the neuron node “the” at layer 27 causes the reasoning to degenerate into a repetition, producing “theShaper Information.” These cases suggest that even a single neuron in the middle layer can fundamentally change the outcome of the reasoning process.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced GraphGhost, a framework that models neuron activations and their propagation as graphs, providing a structural view of LLM reasoning. Using graph algorithms and structural interventions, we show how key neurons shape the logical flow and semantic consistency. These findings establish GraphGhost as an interpretation framework for understanding the structural foundations of reasoning in LLMs.

Limitations

The fidelity of GraphGhost relies heavily on the precision of the underlying attribution tracing method.
Because current attribution estimates can be noisy or incomplete, they may produce missing or spurious edges, yielding only partial views of the model’s reasoning process.
Advancing the design of circuit tracer would further enhance GraphGhost’s ability to identify key neuron nodes and capture more precise reasoning structures.

Appendix A
Related work

A.1
Interpretation

Interpreting how LLMs perform various reasoning tasks is an important step toward aligning them with human understanding
Yao et al. 2023b
;
Zhao et al. 2024
. Current work mainly falls into two directions: self-explanation
Gu et al. 2024
;
Koa et al. 2024
and uncovering the underlying causal mechanisms
Bloom et al. 2024
;
Dunefsky et al. 2024
. The self-explanation approach, such as Chain-of-Thought prompting
Wei et al. 2022
;
Kojima et al. 2022
, requires LLMs to verbalize their reasoning steps, which can help guide them to correct answers. However, there is ongoing debate about whether such reasoning chains are truly faithful
Turpin et al. 2023
;
Barez et al. 2025
.

A.2
Reasoning tasks

Reasoning tasks are among the most important benchmarks for evaluating the ability of LLMs to solve complex problems. Specifically, they assess model performance across mathematical, scientific, and logical reasoning. Mathematical reasoning evaluates a model’s capacity to perform structured, multi-step numerical computations expressed in natural language
Cobbe et al. 2021
;
Hendrycks et al. 2021
. Scientific reasoning tasks require LLMs to draw on background knowledge and apply it in reasoning processes
Khot et al. 2020
;
Clark et al. 2018
. Logical reasoning tasks assess whether models can apply formal rules rather than relying on surface-level correlations
Clark et al. 2019
;
Saparov and He 2022
. Recent research further suggests that LLMs are capable of transferring reasoning strategies across diverse tasks, indicating that despite differences in domain, the underlying reasoning logic may be shared.

C.1
Synthetic Experiment

We first generate directed graphs with 10 nodes and an edge density of 0.6, then randomly drop 60% of the edges. From each resulting subgraph, we randomly select two nodes and retain the subgraph if the path length between them is 3. In total, we collect 100,000 such graph cases. For training, we use a 5-layer decoder-only transformer with a hidden size of 96 and 12 attention heads. The model is trained for 20,000 iterations to ensure stable convergence.

For circuit tracing, we employ the ReLU version of the transcoder, with the dead feature window set to 50 and the L1 coefficient set to 0.0005. During visualization, we set the node ratio to 0.48 and the edge ratio to 0.86.
