Learning and Generalization with Mixture Data
Harsh Vardhan, Avishek Ghosh, Arya Mazumdar

Abstract
In many, if not most, machine learning applications the training data is naturally heterogeneous (e.g. federated learning, adversarial attacks and domain adaptation in neural net training). Data heterogeneity is identified as one of the major challenges in modern day large-scale learning. A classical way to represent heterogeneous data is via a mixture model. In this paper, we study generalization performance and statistical rates when data is sampled from a mixture distribution. We first characterize the heterogeneity of the mixture in terms of the pairwise total variation distance of the sub-population distributions. Thereafter, as a central theme of this paper, we characterize the range where the mixture may be treated as a single (homogeneous) distribution for learning. In particular, we study the generalization performance under the classical PAC framework and the statistical error rates for parametric (linear regression, mixture of hyperplanes) as well as non-parametric (Lipschitz, convex and Hölder-smooth) regression problems. In order to do this, we obtain Rademacher complexity and (local) Gaussian complexity bounds with mixture data, and apply them to get the generalization and convergence rates respectively. We observe that as the (regression) function classes get more complex, the requirement on the pairwise total variation distance gets stringent, which matches our intuition. We also do a finer analysis for the case of mixed linear regression and provide a tight bound on the generalization error in terms of heterogeneity.

Introduction

It is a common assumption in elementary machine learning that “data”, which comprise of tuples of
(feature, label)
, are generated IID (identical and independently distributed) from some distribution
\mathcal{D}
. Conditions of learning depend on the properties of
\mathcal{D}
, and rigorous statistical performances (either via so-called uniform convergence bounds or sharp statistical error rates via M-estimation) are extremely well-studied
[
1

,
[
2
]
.

However, often times in practice, data sources are diverse, and the IID assumption fails in many ways. In some cases, data may come from different sources, and therefore follow different distributions. Due to other benefits we may still want to learn a single model using the combined training data.
A natural setup where
non-iid
data naturally arises is in the very popular federated learning framework
[
3
,
4
]
, where data lies in users’ personal devices, and since no two users are identical, one needs to learn with heterogeneous data.
It may be the case that data is in fact coming from a mixture distribution, where components represents sub-populations (such as medical and biological data, recommendation systems etc.
[
5
]
).

In statistics, a standard way to represent the presence of sub-populations within an overall population is the use of mixture models. Mixture of Gaussians have been extremely well-studied from a theoretical as well as an algorithmic lens over the last several decades
[
6
]
.
Apart from the unsupervised setup, the mixture models have been investigated for several supervised machine learning problems as well. Most prominent among these is the problem of
mixed linear regression

[
7
]
. In this setting, the label is generated by randomly picking a hyperplane uniformly from a set of finitely many hyperplanes. The goal here is to recover the underlying regressors from observations. This problem has received considerable attention in the recent past, both in the framework where a generative structure is assumed on the label
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
as well in the non-generative framework, where given (covariate, label) pairs, the objective is to fit a finite number of lines
[
15
]
.

However, although statistical learning with mixture data has been a topic of interest over the last few decades, most advances are made either for the parameter recovery type problems in the unsupervised setup (e.g. Gaussian mixtures) or in a relatively simple supervised setup like mixed linear regression. In this paper, we study a statistical learning perspective under the assumption that the data is sampled from a mixture distribution. Moreover, we work in the supervised learning paradigm.

We start by characterizing the heterogeneity in a mixture distribution in a
generic
way. Suppose the mixture distribution is
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
, which is defined as

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{j\in[m]}a_{j}\mathcal{D}_{j}
, where
\{\mathcal{D}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are the
m
base distributions on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, and
\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are the
m
mixture weights, with
a_{j}\geq 0,\,\forall j\in[m]
and
\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}=1
. Then a natural way to characterize the heterogeneity in
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}

is via an appropriate statistical distance between the component distributions. If this distance is large, then the statistical behavior with
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
deviates a lot from any of the base distributions. On the other hand, if the distance is small, then
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
approximately behaves like a homogeneous distribution altogether.

Definition I.1
.

(
\gamma
-Heterogeneous Mixture)

Suppose, for the
j
th component distribution, we define,
\gamma_{j}\equiv\lvert\lvert\mathcal{D}_{j}-\tilde{\mathcal{D}}\rvert\rvert_{TV},j\in[m],
where
\|P-Q\|_{TV}\equiv\sup_{A\subseteq\Omega}(P(A)-Q(A))
stands for the total variation (TV) distance between the distributions
P
and
Q
with common sample space
\Omega
; and
\gamma\equiv\max_{j}\gamma_{j}.

Note that
\gamma
in the above definition is the TV radius of the set of base distributions. Instead we could have defined
\gamma
as the diameter of the space, and all our results will still hold.
Throughout the paper, we assume that the mixture distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
is unknown and the learner can draw samples from
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
.

Our goal is to learn functions from
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
for
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that best fits a mixture distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
over
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
. We assume that the learner is given access to
n
data samples
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
from
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
, with maximum pairwise TV distance of
\gamma
. As usual, in the PAC setting, there exists a hypothesis class (or mapping class)
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, and our goal is to learn
\mathcal{F}
through samples.

Let
\ell(f(x),y)
be the loss function with datapoint
(x,y)
and mapping
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Also, let
L(f)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}\ell(f(x),y)
be the population loss and
L_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i})
be the empirical loss. The goal in generalization is to minimize the ‘excess risk’ given by
L(\hat{f})-L(f^{*})
, where
\hat{f}=\mathrm{argmin}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}L_{n}(f)
and
f^{*}=\mathrm{argmin}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}L(f)
. It is easy to see that the excess risk is upper-bounded by 2
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|L_{n}(f)-L(f)|
, known as the generalization error.

Since we will be concerned only about generalization error, a good setting for our results is to assume,

\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{j}}\ell(f(x),y)=f^{\star},\forall j.

(1)

This ensures that we only consider the generalization error due to data heterogeneity, and not error resulting from
model mismatch
, though as we will see in
Section
IV
, the latter can also contribute to total risk.

I-A

Summary of Contributions

In this paper, we aim to characterize several learning theoretic objectives when the learner has samples from the mixture distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. We study how learning from mixtures affects the performance in terms of (a) generalization in the PAC framework, and (b) statistical error rates in the least square regression in both parametric and non-parametric setup.

Generalization with mixtures:
In Section
II
, we start with the generalization performance with
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
by controlling the Rademacher complexity in the standard PAC framework. In particular, we assume that the datapoints (covariate, label)
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
is a
\gamma
-heterogeneous mixture distribution. We first control the Rademacher complexity (corresponding to the underlying hypothesis class that maps the covariates to labels) with
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. Then, by appropriately defining the generalization error and connecting it to the Rademacher complexity, we obtain generalization guarantees with mixture distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. In particular, we obtain the range of
\gamma
where the generalization of
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
is (order-wise) same as that of a homogeneous base distribution
\mathcal{D}_{j}
, for any
j\in[m]
.

Statistical rates for mixed non-parametric least squares:
In Section
III
, we obtain the statistical rates of several regression problems with least squares objective. We start with a simple linear regression, and we move to the more generic statistical problem of non-parametric regression. In particular we study Lipschitz (
\mathcal{F}_{Lip}
), Convex-Lipschitz (
\mathcal{F}_{conv}
) and
\alpha
-Hölder smooth (
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
) regression problems
[
2
,
1
]
where the covariates are drawn from a
\gamma
-heterogeneous mixture
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
, and we obtain sharp statistical rates (also called prediction error) for such problems. In order to obtain this, we first characterize the local Gaussian complexity with mixed data corresponding to these non-parametric function classes; and then using the connection of Gaussian complexities with prediction error
[
1
, Chap. 13]
, we obtain the statistical error of these problems. Maintaining the theme of this paper, we also characterize the range of
\gamma
where the statistical rate with
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
matches to that of any (homogeneous) base distribution.

We observe that as the (regression) function classes get more complex, the requirement on
\gamma
to maintain the same statistical rate gets stringent, which matches our intuition. Indeed, the
\gamma
dependence strictly gets stronger, from
\gamma\sim n^{-1/3}
for Lipschitz regression to
\gamma\sim n^{-4/5}
for Convex-Lipschitz to
\gamma\sim n^{-\frac{2\alpha}{1+2\alpha}}
for general
\alpha
-Hölder smooth with
\alpha>2
.

Statistical rates for mixed linear least squares:
We now focus on a special case of heterogeneous data, where we do not assume (
1
) to be true, and do a finer analysis of the well-known mixed linear regression problem (Section
IV
). Unlike past works on mixed linear regression, which focuses on algorithmic (Alternating Minimization(AM) or Expectation Maximization (EM)) solutions, our focus here is to obtain
excess risk
(generalization) result which is agnostic to any learning algorithms. We consider the setup where each base distribution
\mathcal{D}_{j}
for each
j\in[m]
, corresponds to a different linear regressor, and we aim to learn a global model that minimizes a population loss (where the expectation is taken with respect to
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
). Notice that this framework is different that that of the special case of linear regression in Section
III
, where the covariates are drawn from mixture distribution but we are only one regressor in the problem. Our objective is to show here that heterogeneity (which is due to model mismatch here)
affects the excess error rate. We characterize this in terms of
\gamma
, and moreover obtain ranges of
\gamma
, where this sample complexity (order-wise) matches to that of any base distribution.

II
Generalization with Mixture Data

In this section, we provide generalization guarantees where the data is sampled from a mixture distribution. We adhere to the standard PAC setup of learning theory.

Define the composite class
\mathcal{H}=\ell\circ\mathcal{F}=\{(x,y)\rightarrow\ell(f(x),y):f\in\mathcal{F}\}
. It turns out that the generalization error is connected to a complexity measure, Rademacher complexity
[
1
, Chap. 4]
, which we now study.

Rademacher Complexity with Mixtures:

In order to obtain generalization error guarantees on
\mathcal{H}
, Rademacher complexity is a standard tool. In particular, with
n
samples drawn from distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
, the empirical and population Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{H}

[
20
,
21
]
is defined as,

\displaystyle\small\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H})=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{\sigma}}[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}h(x_{i},y_{i})|],\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{H})=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H}).

where
\mathbf{\sigma}_{i}^{\prime}s
are a set of independent Rademacher RV’s. We can similarly define the Rademacher complexities for the base distributions
\{\mathcal{D}_{j}\}
of
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
, where for the
j
-th distribution the expectation is taken (population) or the samples are drawn (empirical) from
\mathcal{D}_{j}
.

II-A

Rademacher complexity bound for mixtures

In this section, we obtain Rademacher complexity bounds for mixture distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. In particular we assume the mixture distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
to be
\gamma
-heterogeneous (Definition
I.1
). With this, we have the following result.

Proposition II.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}
denotes the hypothesis class and let
B(n)
denotes the upper bound on
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H})
. The population Rademacher complexity satisfies

\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{H})\leq\min_{j\in[m]}\big(\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})+2\gamma_{j}B(n)\Big)
, where
\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the Rademacher complexity assuming the data is drawn (in i.i.d fashion) from
\mathcal{D}_{j}
for all
j\in[m]
.

So, it is clear that in order to control the rademacher complexity of the mixture, one requires to upper-bound the empirical rademacher complexity
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H})
(i.e.,
B(n)
). We now show some special cases where this can be done.

Case I: Linear
\ell_{2}
-regularized Hypothesis class:
We consider the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}=\{h(x)=\langle w,x\rangle|\|w\|\leq W_{2},\|x\|\leq R\}
. We have
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H})\leq\frac{W_{2}R}{\sqrt{n}}
and hence
B(n)=\frac{W_{2}R}{\sqrt{n}}
.

Case II: Linear
\ell_{1}
-regularized Hypothesis class:
We consider the hypothesis class as
\mathcal{H}=\{h(x)=\langle w,x\rangle|\|w\|_{1}\leq W_{1},\|x\|_{\infty}\leq X_{\infty}\}
. With this we have
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H})\leq X_{\infty}W_{1}\sqrt{\frac{2\log\,d}{n}}=B(n)
.

Case III: Bounded Hypothesis class:
We now consider
\mathcal{H}=\{h(.)|\|h(.)|\leq b\}
. Here, we obtain
B(n)=b
.

Proof of these cases are deferred to the Appendix.
The above statement connects the Rademacher complexity with mixture samples to the same with samples drawn from a particular base distribution (here
j
-th distribution,
\mathcal{D}_{j}
). This reduction is often useful since it is easy to work with base distributions compared to a mixture.

Corollary II.2
.

Let
B(n)
be an upper bound on empirical Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{H}
. If for any
j\in[m]
,
\gamma_{j}\leq\frac{\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})}{2B(n)}
, the Rachemacher complexity with mixture distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
satisfies
\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{H})\leq 2\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})
.

Discussion:
Note that based on the decay of
\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})
with
n
, we may have different tolerable values of
\gamma
. For example, if
\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})\asymp B(n)
, we may have
\gamma_{j}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

The above corollary allows us to connect the generalization (upper) bounds of a mixture learning to the same where data is drawn from a single distribution.

II-B

Implications for Generalization error

We continue with the framework, where data-points
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are drawn from a
\gamma
-heterogeneous distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
, and let
\mathcal{D}_{j}
denote the
j
-th base distribution. We have the following:

Theorem II.3
.

Suppose the composite class
\mathcal{H}
consists of
B^{\prime}
-bounded functions such that
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}(\mathcal{H})\leq B(n)
. Then, with probability at least
1-\exp(-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{2(B^{\prime})^{2}})
, we have
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|L_{n}(f)-L(f)|\leq 2\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})+4B(n)\,\gamma_{j}+\delta
for any
j\in[m]
. Furthermore, if for any
j\in[m],\gamma_{j}\leq\frac{\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})}{2B(n)}
, the generalization error is
\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H}))
with high probability.

We compare this with the homogeneous setup where datapoints
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are drawn from the
j
-th base distribution
\mathcal{D}_{j}
. In that case, using a lower bound on generalization error
[
1
, Chap.4]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})-\frac{B(n)}{2\sqrt{n}}-\delta\,\,\,\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|L_{n}^{(j)}(f)-L^{(j)}(f)|\leq\,\,\,2\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})+\delta,

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-\frac{n\delta^{2}}{2(B^{\prime})^{2}})
, where
L_{n}
and
L
are empirical and population loss functions with distribution
\mathcal{D}_{j}
. The above implies that the generalization error is concentrated (upper and lower bounds) at
\Theta(\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H}))
. Hence, we have following conclusion.

Corollary II.4
.

If for any
j\in[m]
,
\gamma_{j}\leq\frac{\mathfrak{R}^{(j)}_{n}(\mathcal{H})}{2B(n)}
, then, with high probability, we have

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|L_{n}(f)-L(f)|\lesssim\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}|L_{n}^{(j)}(f)-L^{(j)}(f)|
.

Remark II.5
.

Provided
\gamma_{j}
is small enough, the generalization error, obtained from mixture data
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
is (order-wise) no-worse than the generalization error yielded from learning via a homogeneous base distribution
\mathcal{D}_{j}
.The simple calculations above characterizes a level of heterogeneity below which the mixture distribution may be treated like a single distribution. With this, we now have a sufficient condition about when to treat a mixture distribution as a single base distribution.

III
Statistical Rates of Mixed Data Least Squares

In this section, we characterize the performance of least squares estimation problems on a large class of functions including non-parametric functions when the data points are sampled from a mixture distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. Let us formalize the setup first. The learner is given access to data points
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, where the covariates,
\{x_{i}\}\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. We adhere to the standard model where the labels
y_{i}
comes from the following model:

y_{i}=f^{*}(x_{i})+\xi_{i}

for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, where
f^{*}(.)\in\mathcal{F}
is the unknown regressor function, and
\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\zeta^{2})
. The goal here is to estimate
f^{*}
through
n
samples. A natural estimator (which is also the maximum likelihood estimator in this setting) is the least squares estimator given by
\widehat{f}\in\mathrm{argmin}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-f(x_{i})\right)^{2}\}
. A standard metric to characterize the quality of the estimator is
in-sample
prediction error given by

\displaystyle\small\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{f}(x_{i})-f^{*}(x_{i})\right)^{2}\equiv\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n},

where we use
\|.\|_{n}
for shorthand. A key quantity to obtain bounds on
\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|_{n}
is the empirical and the population
local
Gaussian complexity, given respectively by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{n}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{w}[\sup_{g\in\mathcal{F}^{*},\|g\|_{n}\leq\delta}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}g(x_{i})|],\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*})=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}\hat{\mathcal{G}}_{n}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*}),

where
\mathcal{F}^{*}
denotes the shifted function class given by
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\{f-f^{*}|f\in\mathcal{F}\}
,
u_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and
\delta
is the local radius.

We first characterize the (population) local Gaussian complexity when the data points are drawn from the mixture distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. The tightest bounds on prediction error can be bounded using local Gaussian complexity with the local radius
\delta
satisfying a specific critical equation
[
1
, Eq (13.17)]
. Note that obtaining upper bounds directly on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*})
is quite non-trivial since the data is coming from a mixture distribution. So, we first connect the Gaussian complexity of a mixture to that of an individual base distribution.

Proposition III.1
.

Suppose
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
is
\gamma
-heterogeneous (Definition
I.1
) with
j
-th base distribution
\mathcal{D}_{j}
. We have
\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*})\leq\min_{j\in[m]}\big(\mathcal{G}^{(j)}_{n}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*})+2\zeta\delta\gamma_{j}\big),
where
\mathcal{G}^{(j)}_{n}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*})
denotes the local Gaussian complexity with data drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{j}
for any
j\in[m]
.

The above bound is particularly interesting since it upper bounds the local Gaussian complexity of a mixture with one of the base distributions
\mathcal{D}_{j}
, and we may use standard tools from empirical process theory to upper bound the Gaussian complexity with distribution
\mathcal{D}_{j}
.

Corollary III.2
.

For a
\gamma
-heterogeneous mixture, if for any
j\in[m]
,
\gamma_{j}\leq\frac{\mathcal{G}^{(j)}_{n}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*})}{2\zeta\delta}
, then the local Gaussian complexity satisfy

\mathcal{G}_{n}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*})\leq 2\mathcal{G}^{(j)}_{n}(\delta,\mathcal{F}^{*}).

III-A

Special Cases

(Parametric) Linear Regression:

We consider the function class
\mathcal{F}_{Lin}=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}|\hskip 2.84526ptf(x)=\langle x,\theta\rangle,\hskip 2.84526pt\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
. We compare with the setup where
\{x_{i}\}\sim\mathcal{D}_{j}
for any
j\in[m]
. From standard results on learning theory (see
[
1
]
), provided
n\geq d
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta^{2}d}{n}\lesssim\|\widehat{f}^{(j)}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim\frac{\zeta^{2}d}{n}\quad\text{w.p.}\hskip 5.69054pt1-2\exp(-n/\zeta^{2}),

where
\widehat{f}^{(j)}
is the empirical regressor when
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{j}
. Here, the lower bound is in the minimax sense. Hence, the statistical rate is tight around
\Theta(\frac{\zeta^{2}d}{n})
.

Theorem III.3
.

Suppose
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}_{Lin}
and data
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. Provided,
n\geq d
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-n/\zeta^{2})
, we have
\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim(\delta^{*})^{2}
, where
\delta^{*}
is the solution of the critical equation
\zeta\delta\sqrt{d/n}+2\zeta\delta\gamma=\delta^{2}
. Correspondingly, provided
\gamma\leq\sqrt{d/n}
, we get
\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim\frac{\zeta^{2}d}{n}
, implying that
\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim\|\widehat{f}^{(j)}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}
.

Remark III.4
.

When
\gamma\leq\sqrt{d/n}
, the statistical rate is (order-wise) no-worse than that of a homogeneous linear regression.

Non-parametric Classes:
We now consider several non-parametric function classes. In particular we consider Lipschitz (
\mathcal{F}_{Lip}
), Convex-Lipschitz (
\mathcal{F}_{conv}
) and
\alpha
-Hölder smooth (
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
) which are increasingly complex.
We show that as this complexity increases, the requirement on
\gamma
gets stringent.

Remark III.5
.

As parametric linear regression is not Lipschitz, so the results are not directly comparable with the non-parametric classes.

Lipschitz Regression:

We consider
\mathcal{F}_{Lip}=\{f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}|f(0)=0,\hskip 2.84526ptf\hskip 5.69054pt\text{is}\hskip 5.69054ptL\text{-Lipschitz}\}
. If
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{j}
for any
j\in[m]
, from standard results on (homogeneous) non-parametric inference (see
[
22
]
,
[
1
, Chap. 13]
), we obtain

\displaystyle(\frac{L\zeta^{2}}{n})^{2/3}\lesssim\|\widehat{f}^{(j)}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim(\frac{L\zeta^{2}}{n})^{2/3}

with probability
1-2\exp(-n^{1/3}/2\zeta^{2/3})
,
where the lower bound is in the minimax sense. Hence, the statistical rate is tightly bounded around
\Theta((\frac{L\zeta^{2}}{n})^{2/3})
for homogeneous Lipschitz regression.

Theorem III.6
.

Suppose
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}_{Lip}
and data
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. With probability at least
1-\exp(-n^{1/3}/2\zeta^{2/3})

\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim(\delta^{*})^{2}
, where
\delta^{*}
is the solution of the critical equation
\zeta\sqrt{L\delta/n}+2\zeta\delta\gamma_{j}=\delta^{2}

\gamma_{j}\leq\left(\frac{L}{\zeta n}\right)^{1/3}

\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim(\frac{L\zeta^{2}}{n})^{2/3}
, implying
\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim\|\widehat{f}^{(j)}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}
.

Convex-Lipschitz Regression:

We characterize a stricter class of functions, namely the set of all convex Lipschitz functions
\mathcal{F}_{conv}=\{f:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}|f(0)=0,\hskip 2.84526pt\,f\hskip 5.69054pt\text{is}\hskip 5.69054pt\text{convex and }1-\text{Lipschitz}\}
. If
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{j}
for any
j\in[m]
, and again using some standard results,
[
22
]
,
[
1
, Chap. 13]
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\frac{\zeta^{2}}{n})^{4/5}\lesssim\|\widehat{f}^{(j)}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim(\frac{\zeta^{2}}{n})^{4/5}

with probability
1-2\exp(-\frac{n}{\zeta^{2}}(\frac{\zeta^{2}}{n})^{\frac{4}{5}})
,
where the lower bound is in the minimax sense.

Theorem III.7
.

Suppose
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}_{conv}
and data
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
. With probability at least
1-\exp(-(\frac{n}{\zeta^{2}})^{\frac{1}{5}})
, we have
\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim(\delta^{*})^{2}
, where
\delta^{*}

\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim\|\widehat{f}^{(j)}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}

Hölder Smooth Function Classes:

To generalize the above mentioned function classes, we consider the Hölder smooth classes. Fix
\alpha>0
, and
\beta
be the largest integer strictly smaller than
\alpha
. The function class
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
consists of functions
f
on
[0,1]
satisfying the following:
(a)

is continuous on
[0,1]
and
\beta
times differentiable on
(0,1)
; (b)
|f^{(k)}(.)|\leq 1
for all

; and (c)
|f^{(\beta)}(x)-f^{(\beta)}(y)|\leq|x-y|^{\alpha-\beta}
for all

Note that if
\alpha=1
,
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is just class of all bounded Lipschitz functions. For
\alpha=2
, we get the convex Lipschitz class. In general,

denotes the smoothness of the class.
Using bounds for statistical error (see,
[

]
), if
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{j}
for any

\displaystyle(\frac{\zeta^{2}}{n})^{\frac{2\alpha}{1+2\alpha}}\lesssim\|\widehat{f}^{(j)}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim(\frac{\zeta^{2}}{n})^{\frac{2\alpha}{1+2\alpha}}

with probability
1-2\exp(-\frac{n}{\zeta^{2}}(\frac{\zeta^{2}}{n})^{\frac{2\alpha}{1+2\alpha}})

So, the statistical rate is tightly bounded around
\Theta((\frac{\zeta^{2}}{n})^{\frac{2\alpha}{1+2\alpha}})

Theorem III.8
.

Suppose
f^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}
and data

. With probability at least

, where
\delta^{*}
is the solution of the critical equation

. Accordingly, provided

\|\widehat{f}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}\lesssim\|\widehat{f}^{(j)}-f^{*}\|^{2}_{n}

Remark III.9
.

The conditions on

gets more stringent when we consider more complex regression classes. For Lipschitz to Convex to Hölder smooth (which are increasingly complex for

IV
Mixture of Hyperplanes

In this section, we focus on out-of-sample prediction error of linear regression where the covariate distribution is homogeneous, but the labels are generated from a mixture of hyperplanes. The learner has access to
n
data points
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
Each base distribution
\mathcal{D}_{j}
corresponds to a unique hyperplane
w_{j}^{\star}
. To sample the
i^{th}
datapoint from the mixture
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\mathcal{D}_{j}
, we first sample
x_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\nu^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d})
. Then we sample
w
from a set
\{w^{\star}_{1},w^{\star}_{2},\dots,w^{\star}_{m}\}
, such that
\Pr(w^{\star}_{j})=a_{j},j=1,\dots,m
, the mixing weights. Then, we generate the label
y_{i}
according to
y_{i}=\langle w,x_{i}\rangle+\xi_{i}
, where
\xi_{i}
is a
0
-mean
\zeta^{2}
-subGaussian random variable independent of
w
and
x_{i}
.

\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
now denotes a mixture of
m
different hyperplanes.

Definition IV.1

(Heterogeneity)
.

The heterogeneity in the mixture of hyperplanes is characterized by
\Delta_{w}\triangleq\max_{j,j^{\prime}\in[m]}\lvert\lvert w_{j^{\prime}}^{\star}-w_{j}^{\star}\rvert\rvert
(this is exclusively due to model mismatch).

One possible method to obtain statistical rates for this mixture is to characterize the subGaussian parameter of this mixture in terms of
\Delta_{w}
and plug it in the results for homogeneous distribution (e.g., from
[
23
]
). To do so, if a datapoint
y_{i}
is generated as
y_{i}=\langle w,x_{i}\rangle+\xi_{i}
we can express it as
y_{i}=\langle w_{j}^{\star},x_{i}\rangle+(\langle w_{j}^{\star}-w,x_{i}\rangle+\xi_{i})
where
(\langle w_{j}^{\star}-w,x_{i}\rangle+\xi_{i})
is the noise. The noise has subGaussian parameter
\zeta^{2}+\sigma^{2}\Delta_{w}^{2}
but, we cannot use this as the results for the homogeneous case crucially require the noise to be either independent of
x_{i}
or bounded almost surely both of which are unsatisfied here
[
23
]
. In the following, we resort to an analysis that uses the special properties of this mixture to obtain the statistical error guarantees.

Remark IV.2
.

Note that,
\Delta_{w}
denotes the maximum distance between the hyperplanes of two base distributions. This also turns out to be a bound on several natural measures of distances between base distributions, such as the KL divergence. In particular, we can connect the heterogeneity measure in Definition
I.1
to pairwise TV distance, if
\xi
were Gaussian instead of subGaussian. Indeed, using chain rule and Pinsker’s inequality,

\displaystyle\small\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\max_{j,j^{\prime}\in[m]}\sqrt{KL(\mathcal{D}_{j}||\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime}})/2}

\displaystyle\leq\small\max_{j,j^{\prime}\in[m]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{x}[KL(\mathcal{D}_{j}||\mathcal{D}_{j^{\prime}})|x]/2}\leq\frac{\nu\Delta_{w}}{\sqrt{2}\zeta}.

Using the square loss,
\ell(w,(x,y))=\frac{1}{2}(\langle w,x\rangle-y)^{2}
, we learn an estimator which minimizes the empirical risk given by
\hat{w}=\arg\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{n}\ell(w,(x_{i},y_{i}))
. To compute the out-of-sample prediction error, we define the population risk for mixture
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
.

\displaystyle F(w)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}[\ell(w,(x,y))],

(2)

\displaystyle w^{\star}\equiv\arg\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}w_{j}^{\star}

The out-of-sample prediction error of
\hat{w}
is
F(\hat{w})-F(w^{\star})
.

Theorem IV.3
.

Let
\delta>0
.
For linear regression on mixture of hyperplanes
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
, where heterogeneity is defined by Definition
IV.1
, and sample complexity
n>n_{\delta}=\Theta(d(\log d+\log(1/\delta))
, with probability atleast
1-\delta
, the out-of-sample prediction error is

\displaystyle F(\hat{w})-F(w^{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\frac{\zeta^{2}(d+\sqrt{d\log(1/\delta)}+\log(1/\delta))}{n}+\frac{d\nu^{2}\Delta_{w}^{2}}{n}\log\frac{1}{\delta})

The proof is in the full paper. Note that, the model mismatch led to a second term in generalization error.
We now consider the homogeneous setting. For base distribution
\mathcal{D}_{j}
, we sample
n
datapoints
(x_{i},y_{i})
, where
x_{i}
is sampled from the same distribution,
y_{i}
is generated using a fixed
w_{j}^{\star}
. The distribution of noise is still the same,
0
-mean
\zeta^{2}
-subGaussian. Using the same loss function
\ell
, the empirical risk minimizer in this case is
\hat{w}_{j}
, the population risk is
F_{j}(w)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{j}}[\ell(w,(x,y))]
, its minimizer is
w_{j}^{\star}
and the out-of-sample prediction error is
F_{j}(\hat{w}_{j})-F_{j}(w_{j}^{\star})
. Then, with probability
1-\delta
, for
n\geq n_{\delta}
, we obtain,

\displaystyle\frac{d\zeta^{2}}{n}\lesssim F_{j}(\hat{w}_{j})-F_{j}(w_{j}^{\star})\lesssim\frac{d\zeta^{2}}{n}.

The upper bound is provided in
[
23
]
and the (standard) lower bound can be found in
[
2
,
24
]
.
Comparing this with Theorem
IV.3
, the mixture of hyperplanes obtains strictly larger out-of-sample error than base distribution, with the difference depending on heterogeneity
\Delta_{w}
. However, both the mixture and base distribution require the same number of samples
n_{\delta}
.

Corollary IV.4
.

If
\Delta_{w}\leq\frac{\zeta}{\nu}
, then the out-of-sample prediction error of the mixture
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}
is no worse (order-wise) than a component
\mathcal{D}_{j}
,
F(\hat{w})-F(w^{\star})\lesssim F_{j}(\hat{w}_{j})-F_{j}(w_{j}^{\star})
, for any
j\in[m]
.

\displaystyle F_{j}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\lvert\lvert w-w_{j}^{\star}\rvert\rvert^{2}

and independence of
\xi

x

w_{j}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}[\ell(w,(x,y)]=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}F_{j}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\lvert\lvert w-w^{\star}\rvert\rvert^{2}+\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\lvert\lvert w^{\star}-w_{j}^{\star}\rvert\rvert^{2}+F_{j}(w_{j}^{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}\lvert\lvert w-w^{\star}\rvert\rvert^{2}+F(w^{\star})

Lemma C.3

(Projections on a convex set)
.

If
\mathcal{W}
is a convex and bounded set, then
\forall w_{1}\notin\mathcal{W}
and
w_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
,

\langle w_{1}-proj_{\mathcal{W}}(w_{1}),w_{2}-proj_{\mathcal{W}}(w_{1})\rangle\leq 0

(5)

Assume by contradiction that
\exists w_{1}\notin\mathcal{W}
and
w_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
such that
\langle w_{1}-proj_{\mathcal{W}}(w_{1}),w_{2}-proj_{\mathcal{W}}(w_{1})\rangle>0
. Let
\hat{w_{1}}=proj_{\mathcal{W}}(w_{1})
. Then, we obtain,

\displaystyle\langle w_{1}-\hat{w_{1}},w_{2}-\hat{w_{1}}\rangle>0

\displaystyle\lvert\lvert w_{1}-\hat{w_{1}}\rvert\rvert^{2}+\lvert\lvert w_{2}-\hat{w_{1}}\rvert\rvert^{2}>\lvert\lvert w_{1}-w_{2}\rvert\rvert^{2}

We use
2\langle a,b\rangle=\lvert\lvert a\rvert\rvert^{2}+\lvert\lvert b\rvert\rvert^{2}-\lvert\lvert a-b\rvert\rvert^{2},\,\forall a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This implies that the angle between
w_{1}-\hat{w_{1}}
and
w_{2}-\hat{w_{1}}
is acute. By the definition of projection,
\lvert\lvert w_{1}-\hat{w_{1}}\rvert\rvert^{2}\leq\lvert\lvert w_{2}-w_{1}\rvert\rvert^{2}
, therefore by property of triangles, angle between
w_{1}-w_{2}
and
\hat{w_{1}}-w_{2}
is also acute. Thus, the triangle formed by
w_{1},w_{2}
and
\hat{w_{1}}
is acute. Therefore,
\exists w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
on the line joining
w_{2}
and
\hat{w_{1}}
which such that

is perpendicular to
w_{2}-\hat{w_{1}}
. Since the set is convex, so

. Thus, we obtain,

\displaystyle\langle w_{1}-w^{\prime},\hat{w_{1}}-w^{\prime}\rangle=0

\displaystyle\lvert\lvert w_{1}-w^{\prime}\rvert\rvert^{2}+\lvert\lvert\hat{w_{1}}-w^{\prime}\rvert\rvert^{2}=\lvert\lvert\hat{w_{1}}-w_{1}\rvert\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\lvert\lvert\hat{w_{1}}-w^{\prime}\rvert\rvert^{2}<\lvert\lvert\hat{w_{1}}-w_{1}\rvert\rvert^{2}

We again use
2\langle a,b\rangle=\lvert\lvert a\rvert\rvert^{2}+\lvert\lvert b\rvert\rvert^{2}-\lvert\lvert a-b\rvert\rvert^{2},\,\forall a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and

\lvert\lvert\hat{w_{1}}-w^{\prime}\rvert\rvert^{2}>0
