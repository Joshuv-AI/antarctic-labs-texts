Association between maternal personality traits and fetal atherogenic indices: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Leyla Haghighi Kaffash, Mohammad Miri, Hafez Heydari-Zarnagh, Mojgan Agahheris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersonality traits have been linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases in adults through the development of atherogenic dyslipidemia. However, the potential influence of maternal personality traits on fetal atherogenic indices remains unexplored. This study aimed to determine the association between maternal personality traits and atherogenic indices in cord blood.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 384 mother-newborn pairs were enrolled. Triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels were measured in cord plasma. Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and non-HDL-C were calculated using TG, TC and HDL-C levels. Fetal atherogenic indices including TG/HDL-C, TG/LDL-C, TC/HDL-C, LDL-C/HDL-C and non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratios, were computed. Maternal personality traits were assessed using the NEO Five-Factor Inventory (NEO-FFI) questionnaire. The association between maternal personality traits and fetal atherogenic indices was examined using linear regression models.ResultsNostatistically significant associations were observed between maternal personality traits and fetal levels of TG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C and non-HDL-C. Additionally, no significant associations were found between maternal personality traits and any of the fetal atherogenic indices (all p > 0.05).ConclusionTheresults indicate that maternal personality traits are not associated with atherogenic indices in fetal cord blood.Keywords: Personality, Atherogenic dyslipidemia, Lipids, Cholesterol, Fetal programming

Introduction

Personality traits are essential aspects of an individual’s psychological makeup, encompassing a range of enduring characteristics and behaviours that shape their unique adaptation to life [1].

Several studies have established correlations between personality traits and atherogenic lipid levels [2–7]. Individuals with traits such as aggression, competitiveness, hostility, impatience, time-consciousness, deadline-focus, inability to relax, excessive anger and stress responses, and cynicism typically demonstrate elevated levels of total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels, along with reduced levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [8–12]. These atherogenic lipids consequently enhance susceptibility to cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) among individuals with such personality chracteristics [13, 14].

To date, no investigation has explored the potential relationship between maternal personality traits and fetal atherogenic lipid levels. We hypothesized that maternal personality may influence fetal lipid levels through multiple pathways, including maternal stress responses and lifestyle factors such as dietary patterns and physical activity levels. According to the developmental origins of health and diseases paradigm, fetal exposure to elevated levels of atherogenic lipid concentrations, particularly during the third trimester, can disrupt normal cardiovascular system development and increase susceptibility to CVDs in later life [15–19]. Therefore, maternal personality traits might potentially modulate early-life programming of CVD susceptibility through their influence on atherogenic lipid levels.

This study aimed to investigate the association between maternal personality traits and both lipid profiles and atherogenic indices in umbilical cord blood samples. Atherogenic indices offer a more comprehensive CVDs risk assessment compared to individual lipid parameters, as they simultaneously account for the adverse effects of triglyceride (TG), TC and LDL, along with the protective influence of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) on cardiovascular health [20–23]. Additionally, these indices demonstrate enhanced sensitivity for detecting dyslipidemia and are less susceptible to confounding by dietary intake and physical activity levels, thereby reducing potential misinterpretation of results [24].

Methods

Study location

Sabzevar is situated in northeastern Iran, within Razavi Khorasan province. The city lies at a latitude of 36°12’54.66"N, a longitude of 57°40’4, and an elevation of 977.6 meters above sea level. According to the 2019 census, Sabzevar has an estimated population of approximately 250,000, of whom 60% reside in urban areas. As the citys population is predominatly ethnically Persian, all participants in the cross-sectional study shared a homogenous ethnic background.

Study population

This study enrolled pregnant women admitted to Mobini Hospital for delivery between February and June 2023. The inclusion criteria were residency in Sabzevar, a singleton pregnancy, and normal liver and kidney function. Exclusion criteria comprised a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), gestational diabetes (GDM) -including those who did not complete GDM screening- preeclampsia, hypertension, endocrine disorders, and active smoking. Women under medication that known to influence lipid or carbohydrate metabolism were also excluded. Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained from all subjects, following approval by by the Ethics Committee of Payam Noor University (IR.PNU.REC.1402.115).

Blood collection

Umbilical cord blood samples (4 mL) were collected immediately after delivery.The blood was drawn into tubes containing ethylene diamine tetra-acetic acid (ETDA) as an anticoagulant. Plasma was subsequently separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min.

Biochemical analysis

Plasma glucose levels were measured using the glucose oxidase method. TC and TG levels were measured using via enzymatic methods. HDL-C was assessed by a precipitation technique. and the level of LDL-C level was calculated employing the Friedewald formula: LDL cholesterol (mg/dl) = TC (mg/dl) – HDL cholesterol (mg/dl) - ()[25]. Additionally, non-HDL cholesterol (non-HDL-C) was determined by substracting HDL-C from TC (non-HDL-C = TC – HDL-C).

Calculation of atherogenic indices

The following atherogenic indices were calculated: TG/HDL-C, TG/LDL-C, TC/HDL-C, LDL-C/HDL-C, and non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratios. All calculationswere performed using STATA software (version 21) [21].

Assessment of maternal personality traits

Maternal personality traitswere assessed using the Iranian short from of the Revised NEO Personality Inventory (NEO-PI-R). This 60 item instrument measures the five major domains of the Five-Factor Model (FFM): neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness. Each domain comprises distinct items and is further subdivided into six facets. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. The inventory has a well-stablished factor structure that has been validated across diverse cultures, including Iran, and its Persian version has demonstrated good reliability and validity [26–28]. The personality assessment questionnaires were administered to all participants upon their enrollment in the study, which for all of them occurred during the third trimester of pregnancy (weeks 28 to 32). This procedure was implemented to standardize the stage of pregnancy and minimize the potential influence of mood fluctuations or pregnancy stage-specific stress on the assessment of stable personality traits.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 15 (Stata Crop LP, College Station, Texas). Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. Linear regression models were employed to examine the the associations between cord plasma lipid levels and maternal personality traits. The models were adjusted for potential confounders, including pre-pregnancy BMI (continuous), maternal age at delivery (continuous), gestational age at delivery (continuous), parental education (categorized as university, secondary school, primary school, or no education), and exposure to tobacco smoke at home during pregnancy (yes, No).,Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) per IQR increase in personality traits. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Similiary, after adjustment for confounders, none of the personality traits showed a significant association with any of the calculated atherogenic ratios, including TG/HDL-C, TG/LDL-C, TC/HDL-C and LDL-C/HDL-C ratios (Tables 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between maternal personality trait and fetal cord blood atherogenic indices. The main finding of this study was the absence of any significant associationbetween the five major maternal personality traits and the fetal cord blood lipid profile, which included levels of TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C and non-HDL-C. Furthermore, no significant correlations were observed between these personality traits and any of the calculated atherogenic indices (TG/HDL-C, TG/LDL-C, TC/HDL-C, LDL-C/HDL-C and non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratios).

Interpretation of results

This study is the first to investigate the association between maternal personality traits and the fetal cord blood lipid profile. Consequently, direct comparisions with prior research are not feasible. However, our null findings are consistent with a body of evidence suggesting a weak or absent correlation maternal and fetal lipid levels. Several studies have reported that mild to moderate increases in maternal lipid levels, particularly TC and LDL-C, are not associated with significant alternations in umbilical cord blood lipid levels [29, 30]. Leiva et al. (2015) in a cross-sectional study of 249 pregnant Chilean women found no significant association between maternal lipid profiles across all trimesters and newborn lipid levels [31]. Additionally, in another cross-sectional study by Leiva et al. (2013) on 43 women with normal pregnancy and 28 women with mild increase in TC, they reported that TC, HDL-C and TG levels in newborns of mothers with mild elevated cholesterol were not altered [30]. Another study compared TG, TC and HDL-C in cord blood of normal pregnancy with mothers with mild elevated TC and reported that there is no significant association between two groups [32]. Fuenzalida et al. (2018) reported that HDL-C, very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) and TG levels in cord blood are not altered by supraphysiological hypercholesterolemia [33].

While our study found no significant associations between the broad personality domains measured by the NEO-FFI questionnaire and fetal lipid profiles, certain methodological considerations should be acknowledged. Although the NEO-FFI provides comprehensive assessment of the five major personality domains, it does not capture specific facets such as ‘Anger’ (a component of Neuroticism) or ‘Impulsiveness,’ which have been more directly linked to lipid profiles in previous literature [insert relevant references]. Therefore, it is possible that utilizing more precise instruments targeting these specific traits might reveal more nuanced associations between maternal personality and fetal lipids. Nonetheless, our overall null finding regarding the major personality domains remains noteworthy, as these broad traits encompass the very facets that have shown associations in other contexts.

Furthermore, our finding of no association between maternal personality traits and fetal atherogenic indices aligns with the important physiological reality that fetal lipid levels and atherogenic indices are not solely dependent on lipid transfer from the mother. They are also substantially influenced by the highly active and independent metabolic processes of the fetal liver. Consequently, endogenous mechanisms regulating lipid metabolism within the fetus itself likely counteract the potential effects of any personality-related alterations in the intrauterine environment.

Plausible mechanisms

The primary finding of this study was the absence of any significant association between maternal personality traits and lipid profiles or atherogenic indices in cord blood. This outcome can be explained by considering several physiological mechanisms. First, as previously mentioned, independent fetal metabolism plays a decisive role in determining final cord blood lipid levels. The fetal liver is highly active and may compensate for minor variations in the maternal lipid environment. Second, the placenta acts as a highly efficient regulatory organ. Studies have shown that in response to elevated maternal cholesterol levels, the expression of cholesterol transporters such as ABCA1 and ABCG1, as well as LDL receptors (LDLR) in the placenta, can be modulated to prevent excessive lipid transfer into the fetal circulation [34, 35]. These protective placental mechanisms help maintain relatively stable lipid homeostasis in the fetus. Consequently, any potential effects of maternal personality traits on fetal lipids, if they exist, appear to be neutralized by these robust physiological barriers (fetal metabolism and placental regulation). Therefore, the focus of future studies should not be solely on simple lipid transfer, but rather on the complex interplay between maternal factors (including psychological ones), placental function, and fetal metabolism.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first to explore the relationship between maternal personality traits and atherosclerotic indices in newborns. Additionally, it is the first to utilize atherogenic indices, which provide more accurate information compared to simple lipids, in examining the relationship between personality traits and lipid metabolism. However, our study has several limitations that need to be taken into account when interpreting the results. Firstly, the most significant limitation of this study is the lack of measurement of maternal blood lipid levels and atherogenic indices. Had these data been available, we could have gained a better understanding of the direct relationship between maternal personality traits and her own lipid profile, and subsequently, its indirect association with the fetal lipid profile. Secondly, cross-sectional studies are unable to establish a cause-and-effect relationship or analyze behaviour over a period of time [36]. Thirdly, we did not investigate maternal diet during pregnancy, which could impact lipid levels in newborns. Fourthly, we lacked information about the mother’s physical activity, which can also affect lipid levels [37].

Conclusion

In summary, this study investigated the potential association between maternal personality traits and early-life atherogenic indices in offspring. No significant associations were observed, suggesting that while specific personality traits are known contributors to atherogenic dyslipidemia in adults, their influence may not extend to the fetal lipid profile. A key limitation of this study is it’s cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference and and assessment of long-term trajectoris. Future longitudinal research should aim to explore dynamic changes in these relationships across childhood and later life. Additionally, such studies would benefit from incorporating potential mediating factors, including maternal diet and physical activity during pregnancy, to provide a more comprehensive understaning of the determinants of fetal and offspring lipid metabolism.
