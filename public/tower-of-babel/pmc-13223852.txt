The association between scapular orientation and spinopelvic parameters
Authors: Rami El Abiad, Yorgho Maalouf, Mohammad Daher, Ziad Feghaly, Doumit Daou, Christele Lahoud, Antoine Haddad, Moussa Samaha, Alan H. Daniels, Joseph A. Abboud, Amer Sebaaly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe scapula plays a crucial role in upper limb movement and stability. Studies have reported that scapular orientation including scapular internal rotation (SIR), scapular protraction (PRO), and scapular upward rotation (SUR) should be integrated into the planning of reverse total shoulder arthroplasty. While previous studies assessed scapular orientation in relation to the spinal parameters, they stop at thoracic kyphosis (TK) without examination of spinopelvic parameters such as lumbar lordosis, pelvic incidence, pelvic tilt, or sacral slope. Therefore, this study aims to examine the association between spinopelvic parameters and scapular orientation.MethodsThis is a retrospective single-center study including adult patients undergoing positron emission tomography scans for nonorthopedic indications, with no spinal deformity or upper extremity pathology affecting scapular positioning. Scapular orientation and spinopelvic parameters were measured for each patient. Patients were grouped into 3 posture groups (A, B, and C) based on their SIR as defined by previous studies. Correlations between scapular and spinopelvic parameters were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients, and group differences were evaluated using analysis of variance with post-hoc analysis.ResultsOne-hundred patients were included with a mean age of 49.8 ± 9.8 years, and 70% being females. The mean values of the scapular orientation parameters were 41.6 ± 9.8° for SIR, 87.2 ± 4.9° for PRO, and 12.4 ± 4.1° for SUR. SIR positively correlated with TK (coefficient = 0.20, P = .046) and Barrey ratio (coefficient = 0.21, P = .04); PRO negatively correlated with T1 pelvic angle (coefficient = −0.21, P = .03), T1 slope (coefficient = −0.21, P = .04), and C7 slope (coefficient = −0.20, P = .048); and SUR positively correlated with lumbar lordosis (coefficient = 0.20, P = .04). Group C had a lower pelvic tilt (P = .02) and a lower T1 pelvic angle (P = .04) compared to group B. In addition, group C had a higher Barrey ratio compared to group A (P = .03). As for scapular orientation parameters, group C had a higher PRO compared to group B (P = .001) and a higher SUR compared to group B (P < .001) and group A (P = .001).ConclusionSpinopelvic parameters are associated with scapular orientation, supporting the concept of a global biomechanical relationship between the spine, pelvis, and shoulder girdle. These findings suggest that global sagittal alignment, beyond TK alone, may influence scapular positioning and might need to be considered in future studies and clinical applications.

The scapula plays a crucial role in upper limb movement and stability. Its complex 3-dimensional orientation is essential for optimal shoulder function.2,15 Understanding this orientation requires considering its position relative to the thorax, its various angles, and the dynamic interplay of muscles that influence its movement.15 In fact, the scapula’s position relative to the torso is determined by 3 rotational movements: internal/external rotation, anterior/posterior tilting, and upward/downward rotation as well as combined forms with translational changes like protraction.8,15

Moroder et al reported that scapular orientation and posture should be integrated into the planning of reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA).9,11 The main measurements of scapular orientation include scapular internal rotation (SIR), scapular protraction (PRO), and scapular upward rotation (SUR).9,11 They also identified 3 different posture types (A-C) based on SIR values for enhanced appraisal of scapulothoracic orientation.9,11 Patients with a type A posture have an upright posture with a retracted scapula (SIR < 36.2°). Patients with a type B posture have a SIR between 36.2° and 46.6°, while patients with a type C posture have a kyphotic posture with a protracted scapula (SIR > 46.6°).9,11 In their study, they reported that patients with a type C posture require more humeral component retroversion to balance external and internal rotation after rTSA.9,11 Moroder et al have also reported that the 3 types of posture influence range of motion after rTSA, further highlighting the clinical relevance of SIR.9,11

While previous studies assessed scapular orientation in relation to the spinal parameters, they examined thoracic kyphosis (TK) without examination of spinopelvic parameters such as lumbar lordosis (LL), pelvic incidence (PI), pelvic tilt (PT), or sacral slope.3,9,11 The TK has been shown to be affected by spinopelvic parameters which poses the question of whether or not spinopelvic parameters are associated with scapular orientation parameters.12,14 We therefore aim to answer this question and hypothesize that specific spinopelvic parameters are associated with scapular orientation in a predictable manner, reflecting compensatory mechanisms of global sagittal alignment.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a retrospective single-center study conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and approval was obtained by the local ethics committee. Patients were included if they had a positron emission tomography (PET) scan (same PET scan machine for all patients) for nonorthopedic indications done in a standard fashion (supine, arms on the side, elbows resting on examination table), if the PET scan was of enough quality to permit 3-dimensional reconstruction of the shoulder and scapula, had complete depiction of the trunk from the base of the skull to the proximal femora including both humeri, and were aged between 20 and 60 years. Patients were excluded if they had spinal deformity (defined as clinically or radiographically evident abnormalities in spinal alignment including scoliosis, hyperkyphosis, or prior spinal surgery) or any pathology of the upper extremities or thorax that could affect scapular orientation and bony metastasis involving the spine, shoulders, or scapula.

Measurements

The measurements were performed by one senior orthopedic surgery resident and one orthopedic surgery attending with means between the measurements used for final analysis. For scapular orientation, SIR was measured as the angle between the perpendicular line to the sagittal vertebral axis and the line from the glenoid to the root of the scapular spine in the transverse plane (Fig. 1A). PRO was measured as the angle between the sagittal vertebral axis and a line from the glenoid to the center of vertebral body T1 in the transverse plane (Fig. 1B). SUR was measured as the angle between the longitudinal vertebral axis and the line from the root of the scapular spine to the inferior scapular angle in the frontal plane (Fig. 1C). These measures were done using Radiant DICOM viewer and validated by doing 2 measurements for the first 20 cases. The calculated intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.94 and the interclass correlation coefficient was 0.91. Spinopelvic parameters were measured using sagittal cuts of the PET scan with KEOPS which was previously validated for spinal and spinopelvic measurements.7 These parameters included PI (angle between a line perpendicular to the sacral endplate at its midpoint and a line connecting that point to the center of the femoral heads axis), PT (angle between the line connecting the midpoint of the S1 endplate to the center of the femoral heads axis and a vertical line drawn through the femoral head axis center), sacral slope (angle between the S1 endplate and a horizontal reference line), LL, TK, spinosacral angle (angle subtended by the upper sacral endplate and the line from the center of C7 vertebral body to the center of upper sacral endplate), T1 pelvic angle (angle between the line connecting the center of the femoral heads axis to the center of T1 and the line connecting the center of the femoral heads axis to the center of the S1 endplate), C7 tilt (angle between the upper endplate of C7 and a horizontal reference line), the T1 tilt (angle between the upper endplate of T1 and a horizontal reference line), and the Barrey ratio (the ratio of distance taking in account the vertical line through the femoral head and the C7 plumb line and a vertical line through posterior corner of S1). All measurements were performed using supine PET imaging. Although sagittal alignment is traditionally assessed in the standing position, prior studies have shown that supine imaging provides reliable assessment of structural spinopelvic parameters, with key relationships largely preserved despite positional changes.4

Statistical analysis

The data have been analyzed using the SPSS 26.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Paired student t-test was used to assess the difference in scapular orientation between the left and right side. Analysis of variance test with post-hoc Bonferroni correction was used to compare continuous parameters across the 3 posture groups and adjusted P values were reported. Chi-square was used for categorical variables. Pearson's correlation test was used to examine correlations between spinopelvic parameters and scapular orientation. P = .05 was used as a statistical significance threshold.

Results

Characteristics

One-hundred patients were included with a mean age of 49.8 ± 9.8 years, and 70% being females. There was no difference in mean SIR (P = .53), mean PRO, and mean SUR (P = .20) between the right and left sides. Therefore, the mean between the right and left shoulder was used for the remainder of the analysis. The resulting mean values of the scapular orientation parameters were 41.6 ± 9.8° for SIR, 87.2 ± 4.9° for PRO, and 12.4 ± 4.1° for SUR. Twenty-seven patients were in posture group A, 34 in group B, and 39 in group C. In addition, group C had a higher Barrey ratio compared to group A (1.6 ± 1.3 vs. 1.0 ± 0.6, P = .03). Discussion

Scapular orientation parameters have been shown to affect planning for rTSA and post-operative range of motion,9,11 leading to the development by Moroder et al of 3 posture groups based on the values of SIR. While the relation of SIR with TK has already been established, this is not the case for other remaining spinopelvic parameters.9,11 Therefore, our study examined the association between the scapular orientation and spinopelvic parameters reporting a correlation between SIR with TK and Barrey ratio; PRO with T1 pelvic angle, T1 slope, and C7 slope; and SUR with LL. In addition, group C had a lower PT and lower T1 pelvic angle compared to group B and a higher Barrey ratio compared to group A.

In their study, Moroder et al have reported the mean SIR to be 41.4°, PRO 88.4°, and SUR 13.3°.9,11 Their values were closely similar to our results. In addition, our study reports a correlation between SIR and TK with a correlation coefficient of 0.20. This is in agreement with what was reported by Moroder et al, reporting a correlation coefficient of 0.27.9 There was also a positive correlation between Barrey ratio and SIR. This could be explained by the correlation between SIR and TK. In fact, when TK increases, C7 is shifted anteriorly, which would increase Barrey ratio. However, group C had a higher Barrey ratio compared to group A with no difference in TK possibly meaning that SIR is related to the global spinal alignment more so than to TK exclusively. Conversely, we did not identify any correlation between SIR, LL, or other pelvic parameters. This observation may be explained by the fact that SIR is primarily dependent on TK, particularly on the geometry of its upper arc. Indeed, Sebaaly et al demonstrated that the upper arc of the TK is nearly constant (approximately 25°) and is not influenced by PI. This relative anatomical invariance likely accounts for the absence of correlation between SIR measurements and pelvic parameters.13

As for PRO, it negatively correlated with T1 pelvic angle, T1 slope, and C7 slope. When TK increases, T1 pelvic angle naturally increases because the higher the TK, the more T1 would be inclined anteriorly, thus increasing T1 pelvic angle. Similarly, T1 slope increases with TK.6 When the scapula is protracted, to maintain global alignment, the body can compensate by flattening upper TK which could explain why PRO correlated with T1 pelvic angle (a global alignment parameter) and T1 slope. C7 slope can be used as a surrogate marker for T1 slope which could explain why PRO would correlate with C7 slope if it correlated with T1 slope.1 Nevertheless, the change in upper TK may not be impactful as PRO did not correlate with TK. In addition, group C had a lower T1 pelvic angle compared to group B. This could be explained by the higher PRO in group C with PRO negatively correlating with T1 pelvic angle. However, group C also had a lower PT compared to group B, meaning that pelvic anteversion plays a role in decreasing the T1 pelvic angle and not only TK.

In addition, SUR positively correlated with LL. Patients with a prominent lordosis tend to have a higher PI and often have a type 4 spine shape according to the Roussouly classification.5 These patients most commonly have a larger TK and a trunk that is more extended. This extension produced by the thoracolumbar extensors can influence the scapula. In fact, the trapezius extending from the occipital bone to the lower thoracic spine is partly responsible for SUR. Therefore, an extended trunk likely influences the function of the trapezius, leading ultimately to an upward rotation of the scapula.

Although this study is primarily focused on anatomic and biomechanical relationships, these findings may have important clinical implications. Scapular orientation has been shown to influence implant positioning, range of motion, and functional outcomes in rTSA.9, 10, 11 The observed associations between scapular orientation and spinopelvic parameters suggest that global sagittal alignment may indirectly affect shoulder mechanics. As such, evaluating spinopelvic alignment in addition to local shoulder parameters may improve pre-operative planning and patient-specific optimization of implant positioning. Furthermore, variations in spinopelvic alignment could potentially contribute to differences in post-operative range of motion or complications, although this warrants further investigation in clinical outcome studies.

Of note, the correlations identified in this study were statistically significant but modest in magnitude, with coefficients around 0.2, indicating weak associations. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and not as strong predictors of scapular orientation. In addition, statistical significance does not necessarily imply clinical relevance. The absolute differences observed between posture groups were relatively small (few degrees) and may fall within the range of measurement variability. As such, the clinical impact of these differences remains uncertain. Future studies should aim to determine whether these variations translate into meaningful differences in functional outcomes or surgical planning.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to assess the relationship between scapular orientation and spinopelvic parameters. However, it is not without potential limitations. First, all shoulder and spinopelvic parameters were measured in the supine position which was previously validated for such measurements.4 However, although supine imaging is considered a valid method for morphologic assessment, it may not fully reflect functional alignment under physiologic loading conditions. Furthermore, we did not examine the impact of spinopelvic parameters on rTSA planning or the post-operative outcomes of rTSA; however, this was not the purpose of this study and will be an area of future study based on the results of this investigation. In addition, the sample size in our study was relatively small. Finally, the mean age of this patient cohort was 50 years, making them younger than the typical candidates for rTSA. However, this was because patients without spinal deformity were included, to understand the relationship between the normal spine and the scapula. Future studies should evaluate how spinal deformity can affect this relationship.

Conclusion

This is the first study to assess the association between spinopelvic and scapular orientation parameters, demonstrating that spinopelvic parameters are significantly associated with scapular orientation. Specifically, SIR was correlated with TK and global alignment, while PRO was associated with T1 pelvic angle, T1 slope, and C7 slope, and SUR was associated with LL. These findings suggest that scapular positioning is influenced by global sagittal alignment, reflecting the biomechanical interplay between the spine, pelvis, and shoulder girdle.
