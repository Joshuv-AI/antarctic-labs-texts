A deterministic particle approximation for a fourth-order equation
Charles Elbar, Alejandro Fernández-Jiménez

Abstract
. We provide a deterministic particle approximation to a fourth order equation with applications in cell-cell adhesion. In order to do that, first we show that the equation can be asymptotically obtained as a limit from a class of well-posed nonlocal partial differential equations. These latter have the advantage that the particles’ empirical measure naturally satisfies the equation. Afterwards, we obtain stability of the 2 2 -Wasserstein gradient flow of this family of nonlocal equations that we use in order to recover a deterministic particle approximation of the fourth order equation. Up to our knowledge, in this manuscript we derive the first deterministic particle approximation for a fourth-order partial differential equation. Finally, we give some numerical simulations of the model at the particles level.

Introduction

In this manuscript we are interested in the deterministic particle approximation (DPA) of the fourth-order equation with backward diffusion

(1.1)

\partial_{t}\rho+\mbox{div}(\rho\nabla\Delta\rho)+\Delta\rho^{m}=0

on the
d

{\mathbb{T}}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\mathbb{Z}^{d}

In

Using a JKO scheme the authors show existence of solutions for some values of

. More recently, in
[

the authors study the stability for
m=2
in dimension

. Moreover, there exists further literature focused on the mathematical analysis of some related equations, mostly of Cahn-Hilliard and thin-film type, c.f.
[

In addition, this family of equations also models tissue growth and patterning due to cell-cell adhesion
[

. In particular, in
[
43

, Falcó, Baker and Carrillo show that a
2

1.1
) keeps cell-sorting phenomena, see also
[

for further details. However, there exists further literature where the authors suggest that nonlocal equations can be used to describe the cell-cell adhesion phenomenon
[

,

. Therefore, one of the goals of this manuscript is to rigorously bridge the two approaches by obtaining the local equation as an asymptotic of a nonlocal equation. This shows from a mathematical point of view that we can use both the local and nonlocal model in order to understand this phenomenon. Furthermore, as a consequence of our analysis we are able to go even deeper and provide a microscopic description of the phenomenon. In particular, in this manuscript we present a deterministic particle approximation, i.e. a DPA.

It is not uncommon in nature to observe biological models with some components that might be stochastic. This happens for example for small copy numbers or rare events. On the other hand, we can also observe different types of situations such as cell-cell adhesion, large copy numbers or fast reactions where nature behaves deterministically. In particular, these phenomena can be understood via a DPA, e.g.
[

. Therefore, it is also relevant to understand this type of models.

Before stating our DPA for (
1.1
), let us explain how to obtain it starting from the more general aggregation equation (which is a nonlocal equation)

(1.2)

\partial_{t}\rho-\mbox{div}(\rho\nabla W_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho)=0,

where
W_{\varepsilon}
is an interaction kernel depending on a small parameter
\varepsilon
accounting for the range of interaction between the particles or cells.
More precisely, this formulation hinges on deterministic approaches since
particles are solutions
, i.e. the empirical measure

\rho^{N}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{i}(t)}

is a weak solution of (
1.2
), where
X_{i}(t)
gives the position of the particles. For any
i=1,\ldots,N
, the positions
X_{i}
solve the system of ODEs

\dot{X_{i}}(t)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla W_{\varepsilon}(X_{i}(t)-X_{j}(t))=-\nabla W_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho^{N}(t).

Moreover, if
W_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\delta_{0}
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
we recover the porous medium equation

\partial_{t}\rho-\frac{1}{2}\Delta\rho^{2}=0

as a limit of (
1.2
) when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.
Choosing appropriately the interaction kernel as a
squared kernel
, that is
W_{\varepsilon}=\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}
for some kernel
\omega_{\varepsilon}
, one can make this argument rigorous. This reasoning provides a microscopic description of the porous medium equation: a DPA. The result we explain here is due to Lions and Mas-Gallic in
[
55
]
who studied the problem for the first time. Recently, there has been several further results in this direction for more general non-linear diffusion evolution in time equations

and for certain cases of aggregation-diffusion equations

,

looking at a microscopic description. Furthermore, in
[

Bertini, Landim and Olla derived a constant mobility Cahn-Hilliard model as a hydrodynamic limit from a stochastic Ginzburg-Landau model. Since then, there has also been several results regarding nonlocal to local convergence for various fourth-order models. The case of Cahn-Hilliard type equations with constant mobility is covered in

Therefore, eventhough the nonlocal to local convergence problem for Cahn-Hilliard is well-studied, up to our knowledge, there are no results in the literature providing a deterministic particles approximation for any fourth-order partial differential equation. In order to derive this DPA let us make the following observation. From a Taylor expansion we have that for symmetric
\omega_{\varepsilon}
which has only second moments on the diagonal and is compactly supported on the ball with radius
\varepsilon
:

(1.3)

B_{\varepsilon}[\rho]\coloneqq\frac{\rho-\rho\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}=-\Delta\rho+O(\varepsilon)

for
\omega_{\varepsilon}
symmetric and an aproximation of
\delta_{0}
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. Hence, we can approximate (
1.1
) by

\partial_{t}\rho-\mbox{div}\left(\rho\nabla\left(\frac{\rho-\rho\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\right)+\Delta\rho^{m}=0

as it is suggested in the different nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard models in the litterature. Nevertheless, this approximation contains the terms
\Delta\rho^{2}
and
\Delta\rho^{m}
which diffuses particle initial data to a continuous density, i.e., the empirical measure does not remain as a sum of Dirac deltas due to the smoothing effect of the porous medium equation. Therefore this nonlocal equation is not at the origin of a DPA as the empirical measure cannot be a solution of the equation. In order, to overcome this difficulty we suggest the following equation

(1.4)

\partial_{t}\rho-\mbox{div}\left(\rho\nabla\left(\frac{\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}-\rho\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\right)+\frac{m}{m-1}\mbox{div}(\rho\nabla\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast(\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})^{m-1})=\varepsilon^{*}\mbox{div}(\rho\nabla\rho\ast R_{\alpha})

where
\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}=\omega_{\widetilde{\varepsilon}}
corresponds to another kernel converging to
\delta_{0}
at the same time as
\omega_{\varepsilon}
but slower. We take,
\varepsilon\ll\widetilde{\varepsilon}\ll\varepsilon^{*}
. In particular, in view of our proof, we need

(1.5)

\varepsilon\coloneqq o(\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{\frac{d+6}{2}})\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\varepsilon}\coloneqq o(\varepsilon^{\ast})\quad\text{as $\varepsilon\to 0$}.

Note the presence of a purely artificial term with parameters
\varepsilon^{*},\alpha
and kernel
R_{\alpha}
. Indeed we need to introduce a viscosity term
\varepsilon^{*}\Delta\rho^{2}
to recover
H^{1}
compactness. However, in order to preserve the DPA we take the nonlocal approximation of this viscosity term with a parameter
\alpha
. Therefore our system has 4 parameters:

•

\varepsilon
: the main parameter, controlling the nonlocality of the fourth-order term.

•

\widetilde{\varepsilon}
: a parameter introduced to obtain a DPA; it must converge slower than
\varepsilon
.

•

\varepsilon^{*}
: an artificial parameter added to obtain the necessary
H^{1}
bounds when
\alpha=0
; this term must vanish in the limit.

•

\alpha
: a parameter introduced to obtain a DPA and to guarantee
H^{1}
bounds on the solution, which are recovered as
\alpha\to 0
. Later, for
m=2
we show that we can also take
\alpha=\alpha(\varepsilon)
converging to
0
at the same time as
\varepsilon
, see
Section

5
.

We keep these notations, rather than already choosing the last three parameters in terms of
\varepsilon
as the computations and the role of each parameter will become clearer.
Formulation (
1.4
) has the advantage that an empirical measure is a weak solution where
the equation of motion for the particles is given by

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla W_{\varepsilon}(X_{i}(t)-X_{j}(t))+\frac{m}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}(X_{i}(t)-X_{j}(t))\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}(X_{j}(t)-X_{k}(t))\right)^{m-1}

\displaystyle\quad-\varepsilon^{*}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla R_{\alpha}(X_{i}(t)-X_{j}(t))

where

W_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}-\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

At this point we realise that both (
1.1
) and (
1.4
) have a gradient flow structure with respect to the
2
-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}

[
72
,
68
,
69
]
. In particular, for (
1.1
) we consider
the (extended) energy functional

(1.6)

\mathcal{F}[\rho]\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\rho(x)|^{2}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\mathcal{E}_{m}[\rho],&\rho\in L^{m}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}),\,\nabla\rho\in L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}),\\
+\infty,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For (
1.4
) we take into account the regularised functional

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]\coloneqq\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}[\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]-\mathcal{E}_{m}[\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]+\frac{\varepsilon^{*}}{2}\mathcal{E}_{2}[\rho\ast R^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\alpha}]

where
R_{\alpha}=R^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\alpha}\ast R^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\alpha}
and

(1.7)

\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}[\rho]=\iint_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}|\rho(x)-\rho(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{E}_{m}[\rho]=\frac{1}{m-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{m}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

In particular, the energy decreases in time along the solution of the associated gradient flow. Thanks to the gradient flow structure, we show stability of the gradient flows in
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. There are several stability results of this nature in the literature covering various cases of non-linear diffusion
[
55
,
20
,
17
]
. There are also results in this direction for aggregation-diffusion equations, such as
[
13
,
25
]
where the authors also introduce a deterministic particle approximation through a blob method.
From this approach and taking advantage of the
\lambda
-convexity of the regularised free-energy functional we are able to achieve a rigorous particle approximation that follows from the
\lambda
-stability (or contractivity) of Wasserstein gradient flows
[
3
]
. However, one can only achieve a qualitative result since the initial datum needs to be approximated enough
[

. Thereby, quantitative results are left as an open problem.

Further related to particle methods, we refer the reader to Chertock’s comprehensive review on deterministic particle methods
[
24
]
. We also mention the seminal paper by Oelschäger
[

]
, where a stochastic particle approximation is proven for classical and positive solutions of the quadratic porous medium equation in

and for weak solutions in the one dimensional case. We also mention the much more recent results in
[

]
covering systems. In
[

]
, the author derive strong

-solutions of the quadratic porous medium equation from a stochastic mean field interacting particle system with the addition of a vanishing Brownian motion. Finally, there has been further research for the viscous porous medium in
[

,

]
and for the porous medium equation with fractional diffusion in

Main results

We solve three different research questions. The well-posedness of the nonlocal equation (

Definition 1.1

(Mollifying kernel)
.

We say that
\omega_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

i

\omega_{1}
is smooth, in

ii
.

It is symmetric, i.e.
\omega_{1}(x)=\omega_{1}(-x)
.

iii
.

\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\omega_{1}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=1

\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}y\omega_{1}(y)\,dy=0
and
\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}y_{i}y_{j}\omega_{1}(y)\,dy=\delta_{i,j}\frac{2}{d}
.

Hence, from
\omega_{1}
one can generate a mollifying sequence. This one is defined by
\omega_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-d}\omega_{1}(x/\varepsilon)
for
\varepsilon>0
and
In particular, for

Definition 1.2

(Vanishing viscosity kernel)
.

We say that
R
is an admissible kernel whenever:

i
.

R
is nonnegative, in
W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and has bounded second moments.

ii
.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}R(x)\,dx=1.

iii

The Fourier transform

is positive and satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{a}\leq\widehat{R}(\xi)\leq 1,&&|\xi|\leq 1,\\
&\frac{a}{|\xi|^{2k}}\leq\widehat{R}(\xi)\leq\frac{b}{|\xi|^{k}},&&|\xi|>1,\end{aligned}\right.

for some
k>0
.

iv
.

The decomposition

, defined by
\widehat{R^{\frac{1}{2}}}\coloneqq(\widehat{R})^{1/2}

|\nabla R^{\frac{1}{2}}(x)|\leq(R^{\frac{1}{2}}*h)(x),\quad\text{a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

, i.e. Borel measure with bounded total variation.

v
.

For any

, define

and consider the intermediate scale

(1.8)

R_{\alpha}=R_{\eta}*L_{\alpha,\eta},\qquad\widehat{L_{\alpha,\eta}}\coloneqq\frac{\widehat{R_{\alpha}}}{\widehat{R_{\eta}}}.

The kernel

so defined belongs to

and satisfies for some constant

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x||L_{\alpha,\eta}(x)|\,dx\leq C\alpha.

vi
.

k
is large enough so that both

have bounded second derivatives.

Moreover, let us specify our definition of Fourier transform as

\widehat{f}(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\mbox{div}\left(\rho\mathbb{v}[\rho]\right)=0,\\

2.1.
Optimal transport and Wasserstein metric

A key tool for the analysis is the Wasserstein metric (see also
[
3
,
72
,
68
]
for further background and more details on the definitions and remarks on this subsection). This is a distance function in the space of probability measures.

Definition 2.1

(
2
-Wasserstein distance)
.

For
\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
(the space of probability measures), we define the
2
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
as

(2.1)

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\left\{\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}c_{2}(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y)\right\}^{\frac{1}{2}},

where the transport cost on the torus is

c_{2}(x,y)=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|x-y+k|^{2},

\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
is the set of transport plans between
\mu
and
\nu
,

\Gamma(\mu,\nu)=\left\{\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}}):(\pi_{x})_{\#}\gamma=\mu,(\pi_{y})_{\#}\gamma=\nu\right\},

and
\pi_{x}
,
\pi_{y}
are the projections onto the first and second variables, respectively.

In the expressions above, we obtain
\mu
and
\nu
as the push-forward of
\gamma
through the projections
\pi_{x}
and
\pi_{y}
. For a measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
and a Borel map
T:{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the push-forward of
\mu
through
T
is defined by

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}T_{\#}\mu(y)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(T(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\quad\text{for all Borel functions }f\text{ on }{\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

It is known that when
\mu\ll\mathcal{L}^{d}
where
\mathcal{L}^{d}
is the Lebesgue measure then the optimal transport plan
\gamma_{0}
is induced by a transport map
T:{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\to{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
satisfying
\gamma_{0}=(Id,T)\sharp\mu
. Moreover,
T(x)=x-\nabla\varphi(x)
where
\varphi
is the so-called Kantorovich potential. In addition to this, there exists
\psi
such that
T^{-1}(x)=x-\nabla\psi
and we have
(\text{id}-\nabla\varphi)\sharp\mu=\nu
and
(\text{id}-\nabla\psi)\sharp\nu=\mu
.

Finally, we have the following lemma which can be adapted from
[
3
, Theorem 8.4.7]
and
[
2
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in L^{\infty}([0,T];\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))
, absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure. Assume that they solve the convection equations

\partial_{t}\mu_{i}(t,x)+\mbox{div}(v_{i}(t,x)\mu_{i}(t,x))=0,\quad i=0,1,

in distribution, with

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mu_{i}(t,x)|v_{i}(t,x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty,\quad i=0,1.

Then, the following formula holds for
\mathcal{L}^{1}-a.e

t\in(0,T)
:

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0}(t,\cdot),\mu_{1}(t,\cdot))\right]=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\varphi^{t}(x)\cdot v_{0}(t,x)\mu_{0}(t,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\psi^{t}(x)\cdot v_{1}(t,x)\mu_{1}(t,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

where
(\varphi^{t},\psi^{t})
is any pair of Kantorovich potentials in the optimal transport problem of
\mu_{0}(t)
onto
\mu_{1}(t)
. In particular

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0}(t,\cdot),\mu_{1}(t,\cdot))=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mu_{0}|\nabla\varphi^{t}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Furthermore, there exists some constant
C
such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}(\|\nabla\varphi^{t}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}+\|\nabla\varphi^{t}\|_{BV({\mathbb{T}^{d}})})\leq C.

Remark 2.3
.

The previous bounds do not hold on an unbounded domain. Indeed
\nabla\varphi^{t}(x)=x-T^{t}(x)
where
T^{t}
is the transport map from
\mu_{0}(t)
to
\mu_{1}(t)
, and we can immediately see on the torus that this quantity is bounded in
L^{\infty}
. The
BV
bound can easily be obtain by using the well known fact that
D^{2}\varphi\leq I_{d}
. Such a reasoning does not hold on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for instance
x-T(x)
can be large if mass is transported very far. This is the main reason to work on the torus.

Another notion that is going to be relevant to us is the one of
\lambda
-convexity. In the Eulerian setting we say that a function
F
is
\lambda
-convex if
F(x)+\frac{\lambda}{2}|x|^{2}
is convex. Let us now consider the gradient flow problem
\dot{x}(t)=-\nabla F(x(t))
with initial datum
x(0)=x_{0}
.
Then, for two solutions
x_{1},x_{2}
it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{2}|x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle=(x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t))\cdot(x_{1}^{\prime}(t)-x_{2}^{\prime}(t))=-(x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t))\cdot(\nabla F(x_{1}(t))-\nabla F(x_{2}(t)))

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda|x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t)|^{2}.

Thus, from Grönwall’s inequality we have that

|x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t)|\leq e^{-\lambda t}|x_{1}(0)-x_{2}(0)|

which, for instance, implies uniqueness of solutions. This argument can be extended to
(\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}),\mathcal{W}_{2})
. In order to do that we first need to introduce the notion of
\lambda
-convexity along constant speed geodesic curves,
[
58
]
.
We say that a curve
\gamma:[0,1]\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
is a
constant speed curve
if

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\gamma_{s},\gamma_{t})=(t-s)\mathcal{W}_{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\quad\text{for all }0\leq s\leq t\leq 1.

Thus, we can introduce the definition of
\lambda
-geodesically convex functionals

Definition 2.4
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
(positive or negative),
we say
\mathcal{F}
is
\lambda
-geodesically convex if for any
\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\in\mathrm{Dom}(\mathcal{F})
there exists a constant speed geodesic
\gamma
with
\gamma_{0}=\nu_{0}
,
\gamma_{1}=\nu_{1}
such that

\mathcal{F}[\gamma_{t}]\leq(1-t)\mathcal{F}[\gamma_{0}]+t\mathcal{F}[\gamma_{1}]-\frac{\lambda}{2}t(1-t)\mathcal{W}_{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})^{2}\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,1].

Furthermore, we say
\mathcal{F}
is displacement convex if
\lambda\geq 0
.

Therefore, we can extend the Eulerian problem in order to study the problem
\partial_{t}\rho=-\nabla_{\mathcal{W}_{2}}(\mathcal{F}[\rho])
with initial datum
\rho(0)=\rho_{0}
. Hence, if
\mathcal{F}
is
\lambda
-geodesically convex we have that for two solutions
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
we have that

(2.2)

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{1}(t),\rho_{2}(t))\leq e^{-\lambda t}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{1}(0),\rho_{2}(0)),

see
[
3
, Theorem 11.2.1]
. This result is very fruitful since it helps to understand uniqueness, stability, or the long time behaviour among others.

The following lemma can be found in
[
3
, Chapter 9]
(see also
[
19
, Theorem 2.1]
).

Lemma 2.5

(
\lambda
-geodesic convexity of the interaction energy)
.

Let
\lambda\leq 0
and
W:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
\lambda
-convex function with
W(-x)=W(x)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the functional

\mu\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}W(x-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(y),\quad\text{for every $\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})$}

is
\lambda
geodesically convex.

2.4.
Sketch of proof

Our first result is the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the system (
NL
).
The equation has the structure

\partial_{t}\rho+\mbox{div}(\rho\,\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho])=0,

where
\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]
is smooth, being given by a convolution with smooth kernels.
Such equations can be seen as aggregation equations with smooth interaction kernels,
for which several existence results are available.
Here we adopt a simple approach based on viscosity approximation combined with a fixed point argument.

More precisely, for fixed smooth
v
, the equation

\partial_{t}\rho-\nu\Delta\rho+\mbox{div}(\rho\nabla v)=0

has smooth solutions.
Schauder fixed point theorem then yields solutions of

\partial_{t}\rho-\nu\Delta\rho+\mbox{div}(\rho\nabla\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho])=0.

Letting
\nu\to 0
, we pass to the limit thanks to weak compactness in
L^{2}

and the strong convergence of
\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]
(again due to the convolution structure).
This proves existence of weak solutions,
1.5
.
Moreover, in regimes where
\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]
is Lipschitz (for instance when
m\geq 2
),
we can also prove uniqueness, that is
1.6
.
The argument is based on the
H^{-1}
norm of the difference between two solutions
and applying Gronwall’s lemma.
This approach is standard in the study of aggregation–diffusion equations (see, e.g.
[
9
]
).

Our second main result is the convergence from the nonlocal to the local model.
For clarity, let us temporarily set aside the artificial regularization term involving
\alpha
.
Without this additional term, we are not able to prove convergence.
The main obstacle is that the passage to the limit involves
H^{1}
estimates on the solutions,
which we cannot obtain, even depending badly on
\varepsilon
.

To overcome this, we add a nonlinear diffusion of the form
\frac{\varepsilon^{*}}{2}\Delta\rho^{2}
.
From the entropy dissipation, that is computing
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho\log\rho
, one obtains bounds of the type

\varepsilon^{*}\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{2}}\leq C.

Even if the bound depends badly on
\varepsilon
, this is enough to prove convergence as long as
\varepsilon\ll\varepsilon^{*}
.
However, simply adding
\varepsilon^{*}\Delta\rho^{2}
directly into the nonlocal equation
is not relevant for our purposes,
since our goal is to derive a DPA of a fourth–order equation,
as explained in the introduction.

A natural alternative is to introduce instead the regularisation

\varepsilon^{*}\mbox{div}\big(\rho\nabla\rho\ast R_{\alpha}\big).

Sending
\alpha\to 0
yields the term
\frac{\varepsilon^{*}}{2}\Delta\rho^{2}
.
One could then let
\varepsilon\to 0
with
\varepsilon^{*}
depending on
\varepsilon
,
but this is a double limit procedure as we explain in
1.7
.
In general, we expect to rely only on a single limit depending on
\varepsilon
,
1.8
.

Our strategy is therefore to keep the nonlocal regularization

\varepsilon^{*}\mbox{div}\big(\rho\nabla\rho\ast R_{\alpha}\big)

and to quantify the convergence rate in terms of
\alpha
.
More precisely, generalizing the method of
[
2
]
in high dimensions (see also
[
14
]
in one dimension) to the aggregation diffusion case,
we obtain an estimate in the Wasserstein distance of the form

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha},\rho_{\varepsilon,0})\leq C\frac{\alpha^{r_{1}}}{\varepsilon^{r_{2}}}\exp{\left({\frac{T}{\varepsilon^{r_{2}}}}\right)},

for suitable rates
r_{1},r_{2}
.
Here
\rho_{\varepsilon,0}
denotes the solution of the limiting system with
\alpha=0
,
which includes the term
\frac{\varepsilon^{*}}{2}\Delta\rho^{2}
.

Finally, let
\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
be the sequence of solutions to the original nonlocal problem.
For
\alpha=0
, i.e. in the diffusion case with
\frac{\varepsilon^{*}}{2}\Delta\rho^{2}
,
one can choose
\varepsilon^{*}
depending on
\varepsilon
such that a subsequence
\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},0}

converges to some limit
\rho
in some weak topology.
Combining this with the rate in
\alpha
, we obtain for smooth
\varphi

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\varphi(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}-\rho)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}(t,\cdot),\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},0}(t,\cdot))+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\varphi(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},0}-\rho)

\displaystyle\leq C\,\frac{\alpha_{k}^{r_{1}}}{\varepsilon_{k}^{r_{2}}}\exp{\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon_{k}^{r_{2}}}\right)}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\varphi(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},0}-\rho).

By choosing
\alpha_{k}
appropriately in terms of
\varepsilon_{k}
,
both terms vanish as
k\to\infty
, which concludes the proof.

Finally, taking advantage of this last result we are able to recover a deterministic particle approximation relying on the
blob method
and the
\lambda
-convexity of the regularised free energy, cf
1.10
.

3.
Nonlocal problem: Well-posedness for fixed
\varepsilon,\alpha>0

The aim of this section is to discuss the well-posedness of the nonlocal problem (
NL
) for fixed
\varepsilon,\alpha>0
. We prove existence when
m>1
,
1.5
, and uniqueness when
m\geq 2
,
1.6
. We note that the proof can be easily adapted on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(assuming also that the initial condition has a bounded second moment).

The problem (
NL
) is a gradient flow with respect to the
2
-Wasserstein metric
\mathcal{W}_{2}
. Therefore, a possible proof of existence would be to use the celebrated JKO scheme, introduced in
[
53
]
by Jordan, Kinderlehrer, and Otto to study
the Fokker-Planck equation from a gradient flow perspective. For example, existence for the local problem (
1.1
) is shown via a JKO scheme in
[
16
]
. Here, we decide to approach the problem from a fixed point argument, which we consider very natural since the kernels are smooth. With respect to the uniqueness problem we choose a Grönwall type argument.
Since we only cover the nonlocal problem during this section we will use the notation
\rho\coloneqq\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
when we refer to the solutions of the problem (
NL
).

3.1.
Existence for a regularized system

We add an artificial viscosity term
-\nu\Delta\rho
and first prove existence for the following system

(P
ν
)

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}-\nu\Delta\rho+\mbox{div}\left(\rho\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]\right)=0

on
(0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
and with
\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0}
where
\rho_{0}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
with
\rho_{0}\geq 0
,
\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{1}}=1
(without loss of generality) and
\nu>0
fixed. We state the following result.

Proposition 3.1

(Existence for the viscosity problem)
.

Assume
\nu,\varepsilon,\alpha>0
, and
\rho_{0}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
. Then, the problem (
Pν
) has a classical solution on
(0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.

In fact
\rho_{0}
is only in
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
so on
t\in[0,T]
our solution is weak, but becomes smooth and classical for
t>0
by standard parabolic regularization. Before proving this statement let us recall some properties of the Fokker-Planck equation

(3.1)

\partial_{t}\rho-\nu\Delta\rho+\mbox{div}(\rho v)=0.

The problem (
3.1
) with initial condition
\rho_{0}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
,
v
smooth enough, and
\nu>0
has a unique classical solution. This solution also satisfies the Maximum Principle. The literature concerning this problem is extensive, we refer for instance to
[
54
]
. We also obtain some
a priori
bounds.

Lemma 3.2

(Estimates on the viscous equation)
.

Assume
\rho_{0}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
,
v
given and smooth enough, and
\nu>0
. Then, the unique classical solution
\rho
of the problem (
3.1
) is such that for all
p\in[1,\infty]
(with convention
\frac{p-1}{p}=1
when
p=\infty
) it follows that:

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho(t)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho_{0},\quad\text{for all $t\in[0,T]$},

\displaystyle\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\exp{\left(\frac{(p-1)\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}T}{p}\right)},

\displaystyle\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\leq\frac{\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}}{\sqrt{2\nu}}\left(1+\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}T\exp{\left(\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}T\right)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\rho\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq\nu\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})}+\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\|\rho\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})}.

Proof.

The first estimate just follows by integrating in space the equation, using the periodic boundary conditions. We now focus on the
L^{p}
estimates.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{p}

\displaystyle=p\nu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{p-1}\Delta\rho-p\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{p-1}\mbox{div}(\rho v)=-p(p-1)\nu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{p-2}|\nabla\rho|^{2}-(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{p}\mbox{div}(v)

\displaystyle\leq(p-1)\|v\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho^{p}.

Hence, due to Grönwall inequality

\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\exp{\left(\frac{(p-1)\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}T}{p}\right)}.

Letting
p\rightarrow\infty
we also obtain the
L^{\infty}
estimate.
Furthermore, from the first computation with
p=2
it also follows that

\displaystyle 2\nu\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}|\nabla\rho|^{2}\leq\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho_{0}^{2}+\|v\|_{W^{1,\infty}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho^{2}.

Therefore,

\sqrt{2\nu}\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\leq\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\left(1+\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}T\exp{\left(\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}T\right)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In order to obtain the time regularity, we take a test function
\varphi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))
, then

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\partial_{t}\rho\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right|

\displaystyle=\left|-\nu\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\rho+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\nabla\varphi\cdot v\rho\right|

\displaystyle\leq\nu\|\nabla\rho\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\quad+\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\|\rho\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})}.

Taking the supremum over
\varphi
with
\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))}\leq 1
yields the
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
bound by duality.
∎

With these remarks, we use Schauder’s fixed point theorem in order to prove the existence result.

Proof of
3.1
.

Let us fix a time
T>0
. We split the proof in several steps.

Step 0. Define the fixed point operator.

First we define

\mathcal{T}:W^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})\rightarrow L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})

as the functional that maps a velocity
v
with the solution of (
3.1
) for initial data
\rho_{0}
and velocity
v
. From the previous computations, this map is well-defined. By a slight abuse of notation, as a second step of the construction of our fixed-point operator we consider the map

\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}:L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})\rightarrow W^{1,\infty}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}}),

where we recall

\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]=\nabla\left(-B_{\varepsilon}[\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]+\frac{m}{m-1}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast(\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})^{m-1}-\varepsilon^{*}R_{\alpha}\ast\rho\right).

We realise that
\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]
is smooth since the kernels (i.e.
\omega_{\varepsilon}
,
\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}
, and
R_{\alpha}
) are smooth. Furthermore, we control the derivatives of the velocity,

(3.2)

\|D^{k}\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq C(\|\omega_{1}\|_{W^{k+1,\infty}},\|R\|_{W^{k+1,\infty}})(\|\rho\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}+\|\rho\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{m-1})\leq C,

where
C
depends on
\varepsilon,\alpha
but these quantity are fixed. Finally, we define the operator we use for the fixed point argument as

S=\mathcal{T}\circ\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}:L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})\rightarrow L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}}).

Step 1.
S
maps
X
to
X
.
Taking into account
3.2
and estimate (
3.2
) we choose the constant
A=\exp{((C(\|\omega_{1}\|_{W^{2,\infty}},\|R\|_{W^{2,\infty}})(\|\rho\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}+\|\rho\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{m-1})T)/2)}
. We now show that the set

X\coloneqq\left\{u\in L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}}),\,\|u\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq A\sqrt{T}\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})},\,u\geq 0\text{ a.e. }\,\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}u(t)=1\,\text{ for a.e. $t$}\right\}

is such that
S(X)\subseteq X
. The non-negativity of
\rho
follows from the Maximum Principle considering
0
as a subsolution. Hence, in view of Lemma
3.2
it follows that

\|S[\rho]\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\exp{\left(\frac{\|\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}T}{2}\right)}\leq A\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}.

Therefore,

\|S[\rho]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq A\sqrt{T}\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}

and
S(X)\subseteq X
.

Step 2.
S
is compact.
Using Lemma
3.2
one realises that

\|S[\rho]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq C.

Furthermore, we also have the time regularity

\|\partial_{t}S[\rho]\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq C.

Since
S[\rho]
is bounded in the set

\{f\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})),\,\partial_{t}f\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))\}

we can apply the Aubin-Lions Lemma to conclude compactness in
L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})
. Therefore
S
is compact in
X
.

Step 3. Continuity of
S
. Let us consider a sequence
g_{n}\in X
that converges to some
g\in L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})
. Let us also consider the sequence
\rho_{n}=S[g_{n}]
. In
Step 2
we prove compactness of
S
in
X
. Hence, there exists a subsequence in
X
such that
\rho_{n}
converges to
\rho=S[g]
. Finally, since
\rho
is the unique solution for the problem (
3.1
) with velocity
\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[g]
(uniqueness for such a parabolic equation is classical) the whole sequence converges. Thus
S
is continuous.

Step 4. Schauder fixed point theorem
. Finally, we combine
Step 1-3
with the Schauder fixed point theorem in order to conclude that there exists
\rho
, a weak solution of (
Pν
) on
(0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
with initial condition
\rho(0,\cdot)=\rho_{0}
. Since
\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]
is smooth, from classical theory, the weak solution is also classical.
∎

3.2.
Existence for fixed
\varepsilon,\alpha
. Viscosity limit
\nu\to 0
. Proof of
1.5

For the sake of clarity, in the following subsection, when we refer to the solution of the problem (
Pν
) obtained in
3.1
we will denote it by
\rho_{\nu}
.

Let us take a sequence
\nu_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. From Lemma
3.2
and Banach-Alaoglu Theorem, it follows that, up to a subsequence,

\rho_{\nu_{k}}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\rho\quad\text{weakly star in $L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))$}.

Moreover the convolution by a kernel upgrade the weak convergence to a strong convergence in space, and using also the estimate on
\partial_{t}\rho_{\nu_{k}}
which gives the same estimate on
\partial_{t}\rho_{\nu_{k}}\ast\omega
for smooth kernel
\omega
we can apply Aubin-Lions lemma to recover that

\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho_{\nu_{k}}]\to\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]\quad\text{strongly in $L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})$}.

Lemma
3.2
also shows that
\nu_{k}\nabla\rho_{\nu_{k}}
converges to 0 strongly in
L^{2}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})
. Indeed

\|\nu_{k}\nabla\rho_{\nu_{k}}\|_{L^{2}}=\sqrt{\nu_{k}}\|\sqrt{\nu_{k}}\nabla\rho_{\nu_{k}}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\sqrt{\nu_{k}}.

We know that
\rho_{\nu_{k}}
is a classical solution of the problem (
3.1
), so it is also a weak solution. Then, for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})
,

-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{\nu_{k}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho_{0}\varphi(0,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho_{\nu_{k}}\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho_{\nu_{k}}]\cdot\nabla\varphi-\nu_{k}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\rho_{\nu_{k}}\cdot\nabla\varphi

converges to

-\int_{0}^{T}\rho\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho_{0}\varphi(0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]\cdot\nabla\varphi.

3.3.
Uniqueness for fixed
\varepsilon,\alpha
. Proof of
1.6

Consider
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
, two weak solutions of the problem (
NL
) bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))
with the same initial conditions. We take
\rho\coloneqq\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}
its difference. In particular,
\rho(0,\cdot)=0
. We introduce
\varphi
such that

-\Delta\varphi=\rho

which is well defined as
\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\rho=0
.
Observe that
\lVert\nabla\varphi(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}=\lVert\rho(t,\cdot)\rVert_{H^{-1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
. Also,

-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\rho\partial_{t}\varphi=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{2}

Hence, using
\varphi
as a test function we recover that

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\nabla B_{\varepsilon}[\rho_{2}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]\cdot\nabla\varphi-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}\nabla B_{\varepsilon}[\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]\cdot\nabla\varphi

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{m}{m-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\nabla\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast(\rho_{2}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})^{m-1}\cdot\nabla\varphi

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{m}{m-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}\nabla\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\left[(\rho_{2}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})^{m-1}-(\rho_{1}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})^{m-1}\right]\cdot\nabla\varphi

\displaystyle\quad-\varepsilon^{*}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\nabla(R_{\alpha}\ast\rho_{2})\cdot\nabla\varphi-\varepsilon^{*}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}\nabla(R_{\alpha}\ast\rho)\cdot\nabla\varphi

\displaystyle=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4}+I_{5}+I_{6}.

Concerning

\rho=-\Delta\varphi
and the identity

(3.3)

\nabla u\Delta u=\mbox{div}(\nabla u\otimes\nabla u)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla|\nabla u|^{2}.

Thereby, we obtain that

\displaystyle|I_{1}|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\nabla\varphi\otimes\nabla\varphi):D^{2}B_{\varepsilon}[\rho_{2}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\Delta B_{\varepsilon}[\rho_{2}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{2}\|D^{2}B_{\varepsilon}[\rho_{2}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\|D^{2}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}+\|D^{2}\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\right)\|\rho_{2}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})},

which is uniformly bounded by
C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
. In order to deal with

we use again

. By properties of the convolution we have that

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}\nabla B_{\varepsilon}[\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}\nabla(\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|+\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}\nabla(\rho\ast\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}(\Delta\varphi\ast\nabla(\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}))\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|+\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}(\Delta\varphi\ast\nabla(\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}))\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}(\partial_{j}\varphi\ast\partial_{ij}(\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}))\partial_{i}\varphi\right|+\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}(\partial_{j}\varphi\ast\partial_{ij}(\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}))\partial_{i}\varphi\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}C\|\rho_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\left(\|D^{2}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}+\|D^{2}\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\right)\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{2}.

We continue with

. We use

and (

) again to obtain that

\displaystyle|I_{3}|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{m}{m-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\nabla\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast(\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho_{2})^{m-1}\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{m}{m-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\nabla\varphi\otimes\nabla\varphi):[D^{2}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast(\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho_{2})^{m-1}]-\frac{m}{2(m-1)}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}[\Delta\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast(\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho_{2})^{m-1}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{2}\|D^{2}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\|(\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho_{2})^{m-1}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{2}.

For
I_{4}
, we recall
\rho_{i}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))
. Then,
\rho_{i}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}
are uniformly bounded by a constant

. For

, the function

is Lipschitz in

. Combining everything, for

, we get that

\displaystyle|I_{4}|

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{m-1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{1}\nabla\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\left[(\rho_{2}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})^{m-1}-(\rho_{1}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})^{m-1}\right]\cdot\nabla\varphi\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\rho_{1}||\nabla\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}||\rho_{2}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}-\rho_{1}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}||\nabla\varphi|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\rho_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\|\nabla\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\|\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}

\displaystyle=C\|\rho_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\|\nabla\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\left\|\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\varphi\ast\partial_{i}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{2}.

The terms
I_{5}
and
I_{6}
are analogous to
I_{3}
and
I_{4}
respectively for
m=2
. In the end we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\|\nabla\varphi(t)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{2}\leq C\|\nabla\varphi(t)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}^{2},\quad\|\nabla\varphi(0,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}=0.

Grönwall’s inequality yields
\|\nabla\varphi(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}=\|\rho(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{-1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}=0
for all
0<t<T
. Note that this reasoning also yields continuous dependance with respect to the initial data.

4.
Convergence nonlocal to local

In the following, to make the distinction, we use the notation
\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
for the solutions of the problem (

). We keep the notation
\rho_{\nu}
for the solutions of (

) since whenever we require them, the parameters
\varepsilon
and
\alpha
are fixed. The main goal of this section is to prove convergence of the solutions of the problem (
NL
) to the solutions of (
CH
) when

. Therefore, from now on we assume

. In order to achieve this goal we divide this section into three subsections. First, in Subsection

we introduce some properties and

we compile preliminary results regarding compactness in

and

4.1.
Gradient flow structure of the problem

3.2
provides estimates for the viscous equation with a prescribed velocity. Applied to the viscous approximation of (
NL
) these bounds are not uniform in
\varepsilon,\alpha
because they involve
\|\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]\|_{W^{1,\infty}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}
. Therefore, the aim of this subsection is to study the gradient flow structure of the problem in order to recover estimates uniformly in
\varepsilon,\alpha
. In particular, we take advantage of the following
a priori
estimates.

Proposition 4.1
.

Solutions of the problem (
NL
) constructed in Theorem
1.5
are such that for
t\in(0,T)

(4.1)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(t,\cdot)]+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\left|\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}]\right|^{2}\leq\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(0,\cdot)],\\
&\|\partial_{t}\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(0,t;H^{-s}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq C\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}}\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\|_{L^{2}((0,t)\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})}\end{split}

where
s>\frac{d}{2}+1
. In particular, from
2.10
and since the initial condition is of bounded free energy, there exists
C
independent of
\varepsilon
and
\alpha
such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\|\nabla(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\|(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})}

\displaystyle\leq C

(4.4)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\|S_{\varepsilon}[\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C.

for any
p\in[1,2^{*}]
,
d\neq 2
, and for any
p\in[1,2^{*})
when
d=2
.

Proof.

We divide the proof in several steps.

Step 1. Dissipation of the free energy.

Let
t\in(0,T)
. Let us take the problem (
Pν
) and consider its corresponding free-energy

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha,\nu}[\rho]=\nu\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\log\rho+\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho].

Since the solution
\rho_{\nu}
of (
Pν
) is classical we can take its time derivative to obtain that

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha,\nu}[\rho_{\nu}]=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{\nu}\left|\nu\nabla\log\rho_{\nu}-\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho_{\nu}]\right|^{2}.

From here it follows

(4.5)

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{\nu}\left|\nu\nabla\log\rho_{\nu}-\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho_{\nu}]\right|^{2}\leq\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha,\nu}[\rho_{\nu}(0,\cdot)]-\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha,\nu}[\rho_{\nu}(t,\cdot)].

Let us take a sequence
\nu_{k}\rightarrow 0
and
\rho_{\nu_{k}}
a sequence of solutions of the problem (
Pν
). From (
4.5
), Fatou’s Lemma and the previous convergences, the result follows.

Step 2. The time derivative.

Concerning the estimate on the time derivative we write (in the weak sense)

\partial_{t}\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}=\mbox{div}\left(\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}}\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}}\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\right).

By conservation of mass we obtain
\|\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,t;L^{2}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))}\leq\|\rho_{0}\|_{L^{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Moreover
\sqrt{\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}}\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
is bounded in
L^{2}((0,t)\times{\mathbb{T}^{d}})
from the first estimate of this proposition. Therefore the product is bounded in
L^{2}(0,t;L^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}}))
. Taking a test function and working by duality then yields the result.
∎

4.2.
Uniform estimates in
\varepsilon,\alpha

We discuss other several estimates concerning
\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
uniform in
\varepsilon
and
\alpha
. Until now we only used the free energy of the system and its gradient flow structure. There exists another Lyapunov functional, typical for fourth order equation like the thin-film/ Cahn-Hilliard equations, which is called the entropy. We recall its definition

\Phi[\rho]=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho(\log\rho-1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

In this subsection and until the end we assume that
\widetilde{\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon^{*}
satisfy (
1.5
). In particular,
\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{d+6}}\to 0
and
\frac{\widetilde{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{*}}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
We obtain the following estimates, that can be made rigorous in the approximating scheme and then sending the viscosity to zero.

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume
\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
is a solution of (
NL
) constructed in Theorem
1.5
. Assume furthermore that
\sup_{0<\varepsilon,\alpha<1}\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho_{0}],\Phi[\rho_{0}]<\infty
. Then, there exists
C
independent of
\varepsilon
such that:

(4.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\left|S_{\varepsilon}[\nabla(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\left|S_{\varepsilon}[(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast R^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\alpha})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(t,\cdot)\log\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(t,\cdot)

\displaystyle\leq C.

Proof of
4.2
.

The following computations are formal but can be made rigorous by coming back to the viscosity system where
\nu>0
and then passing to the limit
\nu\to 0
. For simplicity, we omit this technicality here. We compute

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\Phi[\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\frac{\partial\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}}{\partial t}=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\log\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\mbox{div}(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}])

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\cdot\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}]

\displaystyle=-\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|S_{\varepsilon}[\nabla(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})]|^{2}-2\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})\Delta(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\quad-\varepsilon^{*}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast R^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\alpha})|^{2}.

Hence, if we take the integral in time, use Young’s inequality and (
2.4
) letting

be the constant in this inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\Phi[\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}(t,\cdot)]+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\left|S_{\varepsilon}[\nabla(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})]\right|^{2}+\varepsilon^{*}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast R^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\alpha})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi[\rho_{0}]+\frac{1}{2C_{GN}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\Delta(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})|^{2}+C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi[\rho_{0}]+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\iint_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\left|S_{\varepsilon}[\nabla(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})]\right|^{2}+C\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{d+6}}\right).

Moreover, from Proposition
4.1
we can bound

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\rho_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon})^{2}\leq C.

From this inequality and the boundedness of the entropy initially we obtain the result.
∎

From these estimates we can obtain compactness. In particular we find the following result.

(Weak and Strong Convergences)
.

\displaystyle\rho_{\varepsilon,0}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\to\rho\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})).

Proof.

Note that the first part of this prospoition is only here to mention that we can contruct solutions of the system with

satisfy (

can then be identified as a weak solution of (

4.3.

We study the convergence of the nonlocal term corresponding with the aggregation.

(4.12)

\begin{split}J&=-2\int_{0}^{T}\int\mbox{div}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}\nabla\left(\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho_{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\varphi=2\int_{0}^{T}\int\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast(\rho_{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi)\cdot\nabla(\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho_{\varepsilon})\\

is a smooth mollifier depending on the parameter
\varepsilon
. By properties of the Kantorovich potentials we have
\nabla\varphi^{t}(x)=x-T^{t}(x)
,
\nabla\psi^{t}(x)=x-S^{t}(x)
where
T^{t}
,
S^{t}
represent the optimal transport maps from
\rho_{\eta}(t)
to
\rho(t)
and
\rho(t)
to
\rho_{\eta}(t)
respectively. Thus
T_{t}\sharp\rho_{\eta}=\rho
and
\nabla\psi^{t}\circ T=-\nabla\varphi^{t}
since
S\circ T=id
.

We obtain

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\nabla\psi^{t}\cdot(-\rho\nabla W_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho))

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\nabla\varphi^{t}\rho_{\eta}(\nabla W_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho)\circ T^{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\nabla\varphi^{t}(x)\rho_{\eta}(x)\nabla W_{\varepsilon}(T^{t}(x)-y)\rho(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\nabla\varphi^{t}(x)\rho_{\eta}(x)\nabla W_{\varepsilon}(T^{t}(x)-T^{t}(y))\rho_{\eta}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

Therefore

G=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\nabla\varphi^{t}(x)\rho_{\eta}(x)\left[\nabla W_{\varepsilon}(T^{t}(x)-T^{t}(y))-\nabla W_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\right]\rho_{\eta}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

Since
\|D^{2}W_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C
for some
C
depending polynomially on
\varepsilon^{-1}
. We obtain by definitions of the Kantorovich potential and the Wasserstein distance, see Lemma
2.2
, and the Jensen’s inequality:

\displaystyle|G|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}|\nabla\varphi^{t}(x)|\rho_{\eta}(x)\rho_{\eta}(y)\left[|\nabla\varphi^{t}(x)|+|\nabla\varphi^{t}(y)|\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho(t),\rho_{\eta}(t)).

Hence, we summarise all the bounds and we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho_{\eta})(t)-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho_{\eta})(0)\leq\int_{0}^{t}-\varepsilon^{\ast}C_{\rho,\rho_{\eta}}^{(1,2,3)}-(C_{\rho,\rho_{\eta}}^{(4)}+C_{\rho,\rho_{\eta}}^{(5)}+C_{\rho,\rho_{\eta}}^{(6)})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau+C\int_{0}^{t}W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho_{\eta})(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

Let us choose
\eta=\alpha^{1+\gamma}
with
0<\gamma<\frac{1}{2pk}
. Then, we find that there exists
r>0
and
C
depending polynomially on
\varepsilon^{-1}
such that

\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho_{\eta})(t)-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho_{\eta})(0)\leq C\alpha^{r}+C\int_{0}^{t}W_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho_{\eta})(\tau)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau.

By Gronwall’s lemma we obtain that there exists
k^{\prime}>0
and
C
independent of
\varepsilon
and
\alpha
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
we have that

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho_{\eta})(t)-\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho_{\eta})(0)\leq C\frac{\alpha^{r}}{\varepsilon^{k^{\prime}}}\exp{\left(\frac{T}{\varepsilon^{k^{\prime}}}\right)}.

∎

From the result described in
5.4
we can also recover the following convergence result that connects the nonlocal problem with and without viscosity term.

Corollary 5.5
.

There exists constants
r
,
k^{\prime}
,
C>0
, independent of
\alpha
, and
\varepsilon
such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho,\rho_{\alpha})(t)\leq C\frac{\alpha^{\frac{r}{2}}}{\varepsilon^{\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}}}\exp{\left(\frac{T}{2\varepsilon^{k^{\prime}}}\right)}

where
\rho_{\alpha}
is a solution of (
NL
) for
\alpha>0
and
\rho
is a solution of (
NL
) for
\alpha=0
.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality and
5.4

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho,\rho_{\alpha})(t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho,\rho_{\eta})(t)+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\eta},\rho_{\alpha})(t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho,\rho_{\eta})(0)+\frac{\alpha^{\frac{r}{2}}}{\varepsilon^{\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}}}\exp{\left(\frac{T}{2\varepsilon^{k^{\prime}}}\right)}+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\eta},\rho_{\alpha})(t).

Note that in particular
\rho(0)=\rho_{\alpha}(0)
. Therefore, we have that

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho,\rho_{\alpha})(t)\leq\frac{\alpha^{\frac{r}{2}}}{\varepsilon^{\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}}}\exp{\left(\frac{T}{2\varepsilon^{k^{\prime}}}\right)}+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\eta},\rho_{\alpha})(0)+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\eta},\rho_{\alpha})(t).

Let us now recall that
\rho_{\eta}=R_{\eta}\ast\rho_{\alpha}
. We define

\pi(x,y)=R_{\eta}(x-y)\rho_{\alpha}(y).

Therefore, it is easy to verify that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\pi(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\rho_{\alpha}(y),\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\pi(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\rho_{\eta}(x)

and hence
\pi
is a transport plan between
\rho_{\eta}
and
\rho_{\alpha}
. Thus, we have that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|x-y|^{2}\pi(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|x|^{2}R_{\eta}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{\alpha}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq C\eta^{2}.

Therefore, it follows that, up to changing
\eta
,

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\eta},\rho_{\alpha})\leq C\eta\ll\frac{\alpha^{\frac{r}{2}}}{\varepsilon^{\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}}}\exp{\left(\frac{T}{2\varepsilon^{k^{\prime}}}\right)},

from where we recover the desired result.
∎

5.2.
The limit along a subsequence

First from
1.7
we deduce the following lemma:

Lemma 5.6
.

There exists a subsequence
\varepsilon_{k}
such that for all
\varphi\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})
:

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},0}\,\varphi\to\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\rho\,\varphi,

where
\rho
is a weak solution of (
CH
).

Let us now introduce the following auxiliary result which can be easily proved by the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality of the Wasserstein distance.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\text{Lip}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
and
f,g
smooth enough. Then

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\varphi(f-g)\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{W}_{2}(f(t),g(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

where
C
depends only on
\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;Lip({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))}
.

Now we are ready to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of
1.8
.

From the triangular’s inequality and Lemma
5.7
we get that for all
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\text{Lip}({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}-\rho)\,\varphi\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}(t),\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},0}(t))+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},0}-\rho)\varphi.

From
5.5
we have that

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}},\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},0})\leq C\frac{\alpha_{k}^{\frac{r}{2}}}{\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}}}\exp{\left(\frac{T}{2\varepsilon_{k}^{k^{\prime}}}\right)}.

Therefore, it follows that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}-\rho)\,\varphi\leq C\frac{\alpha_{k}^{\frac{r}{2}}}{\varepsilon_{k}^{\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}}}\exp{\left(\frac{T}{2\varepsilon_{k}^{k^{\prime}}}\right)}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},0}-\rho)\varphi.

Hence, if we choose the ratio between
\alpha_{k}
and
\varepsilon_{k}
adequately and for the first term on the RHS the result follows. The convergence can then be upgraded to a narrow convergence by convergence of the masses.
∎

6.
Convexity and particle approximation. Proof of
1.10

In this section we provide a deterministic particle approximation in view of the nonlocal approximation (
NL
) extending the so-called
blob method

[
25
]
. We need to prove that the energy functional
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
is
\lambda
convex. Instead of using the
above the tangent inequality
,
[
27
]
, we use Lemma
2.5
in order to obtain
\lambda
convexity of our functional.

Proposition 6.1
.

The functional
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
is
\lambda_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
-geodesically convex with

\lambda_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\simeq-\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{-d-2}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{-d-2}+\varepsilon^{\ast}\alpha^{-d-2}\right).

for some
C>0
and independenf of
\varepsilon,\alpha
.

Proof.

Let us remark that by symmetry properties of the kernels,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}[\rho]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}[\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]-\mathcal{E}_{2}[\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]+\mathcal{E}_{2}[\varepsilon^{\ast}\rho\ast R^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\rho B_{\varepsilon}[\rho\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}]-\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\rho\,(\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho)+\varepsilon^{*}\int_{{\mathbb{T}^{d}}}\rho\,(R_{\alpha}\ast\rho)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\rho\,(W_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\ast\rho)

where

W_{\varepsilon,\alpha}=\frac{\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}-\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{*}R_{\alpha}.

Since all the kernels have bounded second derivatives, we can compute in order to recover that

\displaystyle\|(D^{2}W_{\varepsilon,\alpha})_{-}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\|D^{2}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\varepsilon^{-2}\|D^{2}\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|D^{2}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\varepsilon^{\ast}\|D^{2}R_{\alpha}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\|D^{2}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}(\|\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}}+\|\omega_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}}\|\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}})+\|D^{2}\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}}+\varepsilon^{\ast}\|D^{2}R_{\alpha}\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon^{-2}\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{-d-2}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{-d-2}+\varepsilon^{\ast}\alpha^{-d-2}).

Thus, we have that
W_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
is
\lambda_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
convex with

\lambda_{\varepsilon,\alpha}\simeq-\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{-d-2}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{-d-2}+\varepsilon^{\ast}\alpha^{-d-2}\right).

Applying Lemma
2.5
yields the result.
∎

Moreover, in view of
1.8
, we know that along a subsequence
(\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k})
we have convergence in the
2
-Wasserstein distance and that
\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{k}(\varepsilon_{k})
.
This information is enough to show existence of a unique gradient flow of
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}
for fixed
\varepsilon_{k}>0
. It can be done following the theory in
[
3
]
and
[
13
, Section 5]
. We do not provide the details in here but we refer to
[
25
]
to the interest reader for similar computations and to
[
20
,
17
]
for further examples.

In our setting, we consider (
NL
) as a continuity equation where the velocity is given by
\mathbb{v}_{\varepsilon,\alpha}
. Therefore, under mild assumptions on
\omega_{1}
, the empirical measure
\rho_{\varepsilon_{k}}^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{\varepsilon_{k}}^{i}(t)}
is a weak solution to (
NL
) provided the particles satisfy the following equation of motion

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla W(X_{i}-X_{j})+2\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon_{k}}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon_{k}}(X_{i}-X_{j})

\displaystyle\quad-\varepsilon_{k}^{*}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla R_{\alpha_{k}}(X_{i}-X_{j})

where

W=\frac{\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon_{k}}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon_{k}}-\omega_{\varepsilon_{k}}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon_{k}}\ast\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon_{k}}}{\varepsilon^{2}_{k}}.

Thus, as a consequence of the usual stability estimate for
\lambda
-gradient flows
[
3

, we know

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}^{N}(t),\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}(t))\leq e^{\lambda_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}t}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}^{N}(0),\rho_{\varepsilon_{k},\alpha_{k}}(0)).

We choose
N=N(k)\rightarrow+\infty
as
k\rightarrow\infty
at the correct speed such that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho_{\varepsilon_{k}}^{N}(0),\rho_{\varepsilon_{k}}(0))=0.

Therefore, by the triangular inequality, Theorem
1.8
and Lemma
5.7
we infer Theorem
1.10
.
