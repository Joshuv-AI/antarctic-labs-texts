Measurable entire functions II
Adi Glücksam, Benjamin Weiss

Abstract
Let ℰ \mathcal{E} denote the space of entire functions with the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets. The action of ℂ \mathbb{C} by translations on ℰ \mathcal{E} is defined by T z ​ f ​ ( w ) = f ⁡ ( w + z ) T_{z}f(w)=f(w+z) . Let 𝒰 \mathcal{U} denote the set of entire functions whose orbit under T T is dense. Birkhoff showed, in [ B ] , that 𝒰 \mathcal{U} is not empty. One of the problems in the collection by T-C Dinh and N. Sibony [ DS ] asks whether there exists an invariant probability measure on ℰ \mathcal{E} whose support is contained in 𝒰 \mathcal{U} . We will show how an old construction of the second author can be modified to provide a positive answer to their question. Furthermore, we modify the construction to produce a wealth of ergodic measures on the space of entire functions of several complex variables.



Introduction

The complex plane
\mathbb{C}
acts on the space of entire functions
\mathcal{E}
by translations, for
f\in\mathcal{E}
,

T_{z}f(w)=f(w+z)
. With the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets,
\mathcal{E}
is a Polish space and the translation action is continuous.
In
[
B
]
Birkhoff constructed an entire function with a dense orbit, showing that this translation action is topologically transitive. This is equivalent
to the fact that there is in fact a dense
G_{\delta}
set of entire functions whose orbits are dense (see for example
[
GH
]
Theorem 9.20).
Answering a question of George Mackey, in
[
W
]
, the second author constructed many invariant ergodic measures on
(\mathcal{E},T_{z})
. In their recent collection of open problems

[
DS
]
Dinh and Sibony asked can there be such a measure which is concentrated on functions whose orbit is dense.

In the following note we will first show how to modify a construction in
[
W
]
to achieve this goal.
The measure
\mu

will be constructed using a simple ergodic action of
\mathbb{C}
by translation on a compact group
G
via a dense subgroup
H
which is a copy of
\mathbb{C}
. This action
preserves the Haar measure
\rho
and since
H
is dense the action is ergodic. We will construct a complex valued function
F
on
G
such that for a.e. point
g_{0}\in G

the restriction of
F
to
Hg_{0}
defines an entire function in
\mathcal{U}
, i.e., its orbit under the translation map is dense. The measure
\mu
is then
simply the image of Haar measure under this mapping. More formally, the mapping
\hat{F}
from
g
to
\mathcal{E}
is given by
\hat{F}(g)(z)=F(g+z)
, where we have
switched to an additive notation and identified
H
with
\mathbb{C}
. As the image of an ergodic measure we are assured that
\mu
will ergodic. The special properties
of the function
F
that we will construct will guarantee the desired result.

In the next section we describe the group
G
and the tools from ergodic theory that we will need while in the second section the construction of
F
will be given.
In the third section we show how to extend this construction to the space of entire functions of several complex variables.

The final sections are devoted to showing how to go from the translation action on the compact group
G
(the solenoid) to any free action of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
as the sample space for the measures on the space of entire functions.

1
Solenoids and their towers

For the sake of clarity we will first briefly review a simple analogue of the compact group
K
and the towers related to
H\triangleleft K
that we will need.
Denote by
[r]
the group
\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}
(we will denote the elements of
[r]
by their representatives
\{0,1,\cdots r-1\}
) , and then
for a sequence of integers
\mathbb{R}=\{r_{1},r_{2},\cdots r_{n},\cdots\}
define
R_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}
. There is a natural homomorphism from
[R_{n+1}]
onto
[R_{n}]
and the inverse
limit of this series of groups,
G(\mathbb{R})
, is called a generalized odometer. It is naturally identified with
X=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}[r_{i}]
and the element

u
defined by
u(1)=1
and
u(i)=0
for all
i>1
, generates a copy of
\mathbb{Z}
where the positive elements are those with only a finite number of coordinates different from
0
.
If all the
r_{i}=2
, this is the group of
2
-adic integers. The action defined by translation by
u
, in this case, is called the dyadic odometer.

The Haar measure
\rho
on the group
G(\mathcal{R})
is the product of the uniform measures on each
[r_{i}]
. As is usual in ergodic theory we will denote translation by
u
by
T
.
Denote by
(X,\rho)
this measure space and as is usual in ergodic theory we will denote translation by
u
by
T
. In the system
(X,\rho,T)

define
B_{n}=\{x:x(i)=0,\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{ for all }1\leq i\leq n\}
. It is clear that the sets
T^{i}B_{n}
are disjoint for all
0\leq i\leq R_{n}-1
and their union is all of
X
. We call
this partition of
X
a tower over
B_{n}
. It
is also clear that
\rho(B_{n})=\frac{1}{R_{n}}
, and that the tower over
B_{n+1}
is divided into
r_{n+1}
translates of subsets of
B_{n}
. There is a simple algebraic description of these sets,
namely
by its definition as an inverse limit there are homomorphisms
\pi_{n}:G(\mathcal{R})\rightarrow[R_{n}]
and the
B_{n}
’s are the kernels of these mappings.

In exactly the same manner one can define a continuous analogue of this construction replacing
\mathbb{Z}
by
\mathbb{R}
. The finite cyclic groups are replaced by circles
\mathbb{R}/R_{n}\mathbb{Z}
and the resulting
inverse limit is called a
solenoid
. It contains a copy
H
of
\mathbb{R}
as a dense subgroup. Once again we will denote by
\pi_{n}
the continuous homomorphisms from the solenoid onto the circles

\mathbb{R}/R_{n}\mathbb{Z}
.

There is a useful way to view the dynamics of addition by
H\cong\mathbb{R}
on the solenoid.
We first mark partitions of the line
\mathbb{R}
into adjacent intervals
[t+ir_{1},t+(i+1)r_{1})
where
t
is any point in
[0,r_{1})
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Next these intervals are formed into consecutive groups of size
r_{2}
,
and these are marked by an additional mark. This produces intervals
[t+iR_{2},t+(i+1)R_{2})
of length
R_{2}
where
t
now ranges over
[0,R_{2})
. This process is continued by induction. The elements of
the solenoid are represented by this hierarchal sequence of partitions and the action by
\mathbb{R}
is simply translation of this sequence of partitions. What correspond to the bases of the towers
B_{n}

are now those partitions which in stage
n
have
t=0
. As before this just the kernel of
\pi_{n}
. This is a compact subgroup with its own
Haar measure which when multiplied by the Lebesgue measure
on
[0,R_{n})
gives the Haar measure on the solenoid.

Exactly the same construction can be carried out for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, using the sequence
R_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
The resulting
2
-dimensional solenoid
G
, containing an
H\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
, will define the ergodic
system that we need. The new projections
\pi_{n}:G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}/R_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
will define the sets
B_{n}=\textrm{ker}(\pi_{n})
that will be the basis for our construction. By
S_{n}
we will denote
the square
[0,R_{n})^{2}
which is a fundamental domain for
\mathbb{R}^{2}/R_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Note that
S_{n+1}
is partitioned into
r_{n+1}^{2}
translates of
S_{n}
and we will denote
these as follows:

S_{n,ij}=[iR_{n},(i+1)R_{n})\times[jR_{n},(j+1)R_{n})\hskip 2.84544pt\textrm{for}\hskip 2.84544pt0\leq i,j<r_{n+1}.

For each
n
the group
G
can be identified with
S_{n}\times B_{n}
and
\rho
, Haar measure of
G
,
with the product of the Haar measure on
B_{n}
with Lebesgue measure on
S_{n}
.
The group
G
depends
of course on the sequence
\{r_{n}\}
, which can be arbitrary, the only requirement that will be needed is that the sum of the series
\sum_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r_{n}}
is finite.

2
The construction

The tool from the theory of complex variables that we will need is Runge’s theorem which we now recall.

Theorem 2.1
.

(C. Runge, 1985)
If
K
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
with a connected complement and
f
is holomorphic in some neighborhood of
K
then given
\varepsilon>0
there is a polynomial
p

such that

\sup_{z\in K}|f(z)-p(z)|<\varepsilon.

To illustrate its strength here is a simple proof of Birkhoff’s result. In order to show that the flow
(\mathcal{E},T_{z})
is topologically transitive given two open sets

U_{0},U_{1}
in
\mathcal{E}
we must find a point
f_{0}\in U_{0}
and some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
T_{z_{0}}f_{0}\in U_{1}
. Both
U_{i}
contain sets
V_{i}
defined by polynomials
p_{i}
, and constants

(M,\varepsilon)
of the form

V_{i}=\{f\in\mathcal{E}:\sup_{|z|\leq R}|f(z)-p_{i}(z)|<\varepsilon\}.

Now define a compact set
K
as the union of the two disks
D_{0}=\{z:|z|\leq R\}
and
D_{1}=\{z:|z-3R|\leq R\}
, and in a neighborhood of
K
define a holomorphic function
f
as

p_{0}(z)
on neighborhood of
D_{0}
and
p_{1}(z-3R)
on a neighborhood of
D_{1}
. Applying Runge’s theorem there is a polynomial
p
within
\varepsilon
of this holomorphic function
f
on
K
. This
polynomial, which is clearly in
\mathcal{E}
is an element of
V_{0}
and when translated by
3R
is in
V_{1}
as required.

Remark 2.2
.

There is no result as general as Runge’s theorem in several complex variables. However there is such a result for the disjoint union of two compact convex sets which suffices to extend
the above proof to the space of entire functions on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Details can be found for example in
[
B-G
]
.

We fix now a sequence
\{r_{n}\}
and let
G
denote the group that was defined in the previous section. We also fix a sequence of polynomials
\{p_{n}\}
that are dense in
\mathcal{E}
. In each stage
we will define a complex valued function
F_{n}
and these will converge to the desired function. While we defined the sets
S_{n}
as subsets of the Euclidean plain we will also
view them as subsets of the complex plane and freely denote their elements using complex variables. To define
F_{1}
on
G
set
G=S_{1}\times B_{1}
and set
F_{1}(z,b)=p_{1}(z)
.

In stage 2 we first define compact subsets

K_{1,ij}\subset S_{1,ij}
for
0\leq i,j<r_{2}
by

K_{1,ij}=\left[iR_{1}+\frac{1}{10},(i+1)R_{1}-\frac{1}{10}\right]\times\left[jR_{1}+\frac{1}{10},(j+1)R_{1}-\frac{1}{10}\right].

In the tiling of
S_{2}
by
r_{2}^{2}
copies of
S_{1}
these are disjoint squares strictly contained in the original tiles.
Now view
G
as
S_{2}\times B_{2}
. Restrict
F_{1}
to the union of the
K_{1,ij}\times B_{2}
. In addition to this on
K_{1,00}\times B_{2}
replace
F_{1}
by
p_{2}
. Now apply Runge’s theorem to this function,
which is clearly holomorphic on a neighborhood
of the union of the
K_{1,ij}\times B_{2}
, with
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{10}
and this defines
F_{2}
, which is a polynomial, on
S_{2}\times\{w\}
for each
w\in B_{2}
.

For the induction we assume that we have defined
F_{n-1}

which is a polynomial when restricted to
S_{n-1}\times\{w\}
for each
w\in B_{n-1}
.
Next define compact subsets
K_{n-1,ij}\subset S_{n-1,ij}
as follows:

K_{n-1,ij}=\left[{iR_{n-1}+\frac{1}{10^{n-1}},(i+1)R_{n-1}-\frac{1}{10^{n-1}}}\right]\times\left[{jR_{n-1}+\frac{1}{10^{n-1}},(j+1)R_{n-1}-\frac{1}{10^{n-1}}}\right].

Now view
G
as
S_{n}\times B_{n}
. Restrict
F_{n-1}
to the union of the
K_{n-1,ij}\times B_{n}
and on
K_{n-1,00}\times B_{n}
replace
F_{n-1}
by
p_{n}
. Apply now Runge’s theorem to approximate
this new function on the union the
K_{n-1,ij}\times B_{n}
with
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{10^{n-1}}
to obtain a polynomial
F_{n}
. This defines
F_{n}
on
G
, it is of course a polynomial on

S_{n}\times\left\{{w}\right\}
on each
w\in B_{n}
.

Let
D_{R}:=\left\{{\left|{z}\right|<R}\right\}
, then for a.e
g
for every
n
large enough
D_{R}g:=\underset{z\in D_{R}}{\bigcup}T_{z}g\subset\bigcup_{ij}K_{n,ij}B_{n}
, where the union extends over all
1\leq i,j\leq r_{n}-1
. This shows that for a.e
g
the functions
F_{n}^{g}:D_{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by
F_{n}^{g}(z)=F_{n}(T_{z}g)
, when restricted to
Hg
, converge uniformly on compact subsets of
Hg
to an entire function.

The translates of this function will contain portions that are
within
\sum_{m\geq n-1}\frac{1}{10^{m}}
of
p_{n}
on large neighborhoods of 0. This shows that for
\rho
-a.e.
g
the limiting function
F
restricted to its
H
orbit is an element of

\mathcal{U}
as required. The required measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{E}
is defined as the push forward of
\rho
by the mapping
F
. This completes the proof of our main theorem:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
G
be a solenoidal group as described in section 1, denote by
\rho
its Haar measure and by
H
the dense subgroup which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
. There is a complex
valued function
F
on
G
which when restricted to
\rho
-a.e. coset of
H
is an entire function with a dense orbit under the translation action of
\mathbb{C}
on
\mathcal{E}
. The push forward of the measure
\rho
by the mapping
F
,
F\circ\rho
, is an ergodic measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{E}
, and
\mu
-a.e
f
belongs to
\mathcal{U}
, the entire functions with dense orbits.

3
Several complex variables

There is no general analogue of Runge’s theorem in several complex variables. Nonetheless, somewhat surprisingly, there is such an approximation theorem
for the generalizations to higher dimensions of the compact sets that were involved in the previous construction. Since this is not so well known we will sketch
a proof of the result that we need. In this and the following sections we will denote elements of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
by
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}
, i.e.
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{d})
. We need the following definition:

Definition 3.1
.

The
polynomially convex hull

\hat{K}
of a compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
is defined by:

\hat{K}=\{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}:|p(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w})|\leq\sup_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in K}|p(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z})|\hskip 2.84544pt\textrm{for all polynomials}\hskip 2.84544ptp\}.

The compact set
K
is called
polynomially convex
if
\hat{K}=K
.

In one dimension the condition for a compact set to be polynomially convex is that its complement is connected. In higher dimensions there is no simple
criterion. There is an approximation theorem for polynomially convex sets in higher dimensions
called the
Oka-Weil Approximation Theorem
. It asserts that if a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
is polynomially convex then any complex valued function
f

that is holomorphic in a neighborhood of
K
can be approximated uniformly
on
K
by polynomials (see
[
G
]
III.5). The simplest polynomially convex sets are the convex sets. Eva Kallin
[
K
]
found a simple condition for
the union of two disjoint polynomially convex sets to be polynomially convex. Here is her result:

Lemma 3.2
.

(E. Kallin 1964) Let
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
be compact subsets in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and assume that there exists
a polynomial
p
satisfying
\widehat{p(K_{1})}\cap\widehat{p(K_{2})}=\emptyset
, then

\widehat{K_{1}\cup K_{2}}=\hat{K_{1}}\cup\hat{K_{2}}
.

In particular if
K_{1},K_{2}
are convex their union is polynomially convex since they can be separated
by a one degree polynomial.
In order to simplify the notation we will now set
d=2
, the general case needs no new ideas. Here is the proposition
that we will need.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose we have two collections of pairwise disjoint compact subsets in
\mathbb{C}
,
\{K_{1},K_{2},\cdots K_{a}\}
and
\{L_{1},L_{2},\cdots L_{b}\}
such
that the complements
\mathbb{C}\backslash\bigcup_{i=1}^{a}K_{i}
and
\mathbb{C}\backslash\bigcup_{j=1}^{b}L_{j}
are connected. Assume in addition that each product
K_{i}\times L_{j}

is polynomially convex. Then the union

\bigcup_{i=1,j=1}^{a,b}K_{i}\times L_{j}

is polynomially convex.

Proof.

The proof is by induction. Let
b=1
and set
\mathcal{K}_{u}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{u}K_{i}
.
Suppose that for
u<a
we have already shown that
\mathcal{K}_{u}\times L_{1}
is polynomially convex. By the hypothesis this holds for
u=1
, the base of the induction. By
Runge’s theorem we can find a polynomial
p
of one variable such that
sup_{z\ {\mathcal{K}}_{u}}|p(z)|\leq 0.1
and
sup_{z\ K_{u+1}}|p(z)-1|\leq 0.1
. We can view
p
as a polynomial
p
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}

by setting
\textbf{p }(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z})=p(z_{1})
and then apply Kallin’s lemma, Lemma
3.2
, to the sets
\mathcal{K}_{u}\times L_{1}
and
K_{u+1}\times L_{1}
to conclude that the set

\mathcal{K}_{u}\times L_{1}\cup K_{u+1}\times L_{1}={\mathcal{K}}_{u+1}\times L_{1}

is also polynomially convex.
By induction we then conclude that
\mathcal{K}_{a}\times L_{1}
is polynomially convex. Now the same argument shows that each of the sets
\mathcal{K}_{a}\times L_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq b
is polynomially
convex. Repeating the inductive argument
using the second coordinate will conclude the proof. ∎∎

With this proposition a construction analogous to the one in the previous section can be carried out in several complex variables. In general, we would identify
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
construct the
2d
-dimensional solenoid as an inverse limit of
\mathbb{R}^{2d}/R_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
with the sequence
R_{n}
as before. It is in fact
G^{d}
where
G
is the 2-dimensional solenoid found above.
With our previous notation
B_{n}^{d}
is now a compact subgroup and
G^{d}
can be identified with
B_{n}^{d}\times S_{n}^{d}
. In the inductive step of the construction the use of Runge’s theorem
is now replaced by Oka-Weil Approximation Theorem. The use of this theorem is possible since the compact sets where we want to approximate
F_{n-1}
are products of compact sets in one complex variable of the form
\bigcup_{j,i}K_{j}\times L_{i}
, and therefore, following Proposition products, the union is polynomially convex. We will leave the details to the reader and simply formulate the result. Denote by
\mathcal{E}(d)
the entire functions on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and by
\mathcal{U}(d)
those functions with a
dense orbit under the translation action of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
on
\mathcal{E}(d)
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
G
be a solenoidal group as described in section 1. The Haar measure on
G^{d}
is
\rho^{d}
and
H^{d}
is the dense subgroup which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. There is a complex
valued function
F
on
G^{d}
which when restricted to
\rho^{d}
-a.e. coset of
H^{d}
is an entire function on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with a dense orbit under the translation action of
\mathbb{C}
on
\mathcal{E}
. The push forward of the measure
\rho^{d}
by the mapping
F
,

F\circ\rho^{d}
, is an ergodic measure on
\mathcal{E}(d)
with support in
\mathcal{U}(d)
.

4
Almost Polynomially Convex Sets

To extend the proof for general free actions of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, we will need to show that a finite union of cubes, which are no longer necessarily of the form
\bigcup_{i,j}K_{j}\times L_{i}
, is also polynomially convex if one is willing to remove small parts of the set, i.e., it is almost polynomially convex. The goal of this section is to show that any collection of unit cubes forms an almost polynomially convex set, see Proposition
4.1
. The ideas of the proposition and its proof are reminiscent of
[
GA
, Lemma 2.1]
. To state the proposition rigorously, and prove it and the supporting lemmas we begin with explaining the notation and definitions we will use in this section.

4.1
Definitions, Notation, and the Main Lemma

From now on
q
will denote some vector in
\mathbb{C}^{d}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
Q_{0}:=\left[{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{2d}
. We will say a cube
Q
is
parallel to the axes
if the facets of
Q
are parallel to the hyperplanes
\left\{{x_{k}=0}\right\}
. In particular, any cube of the form
Q=q+Q_{0}
is a unit cube parallel to the axes, centred at
q
.

Let
Q=q+Q_{0}
be a unit cube parallel to the axes. Partitioning every segment defining this cube into two segments of equal length, we create the collection
\left\{{Q^{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{i}}}\right\}_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{i}\in\left\{{-1,0}\right\}^{2d}}
defined by

Q^{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{i}}=q+\underset{{j}={1}}{\overset{2d}{\prod}}\;\left[{\frac{i_{j}}{2},\frac{i_{j}+1}{2}}\right],

where, for every
j

\left[{\frac{i_{j}}{2},\frac{i_{j}+1}{2}}\right]=\begin{cases}\left[{-\frac{1}{2},0}\right]&,i_{j}=(-1)\\
\left[{0,\frac{1}{2}}\right]&,i_{j}=0\end{cases}.

We will call every sub-cube,
Q^{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{i}}
, a
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube of
Q
.

The collection of all unit cubes centered on
\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
is called
the grid
, and every cube in the grid is of the form,
Q=q+Q_{0},q\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
, and is called a
grid-cube
.

Figure 1:
The figure to the left demonstrates grid cubes, the dots are the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
. The figure to the right shows all the
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cubes of a cube in
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Given
q\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, we denote by
\left[{q}\right]\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
the point from
\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
closest to
q
, i.e.,

\left[{q}\right]_{j}=\begin{cases}\lceil q_{j}\rceil&,q_{j}-\lfloor q\rfloor>\frac{1}{2}\\
\lfloor q_{j}\rfloor&,q_{j}-\lfloor q\rfloor\leq\frac{1}{2}\end{cases}.

Proposition 4.1
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
and any finite collection of pairwise disjoint unit cubes parallel to the axes,
\left\{{Q_{j}}\right\}_{j=1}^{M}
, there exists a polynomially convex set
U\subset\bigcup_{{j}={1}}^{M}Q_{j}
satisfying that for every
j
, there exists a
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube of
Q_{j}
,
B_{j}
, so that
B_{j}^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{M\cdot 2^{7d}}}\subset U
and
m_{2d}\left({\bigcup_{{j}={1}}^{M}Q_{j}\setminus U}\right)<\varepsilon
, where

B_{j}^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{M\cdot 2^{7d}}}:=\left\{{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in B_{j},dist(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z},\partial B_{j})\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{M\cdot 2^{7d}}}\right\}.

If
Q_{j}
was a grid cube, then
Q_{j}^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{M\cdot 2^{7d}}}\subset U
.

4.2
On Polynomial Convexity and Hyperplanes

To prove Proposition
4.1
, we require several geometric lemmas, which we prove in this subsection.

Lemma 4.2
.

Every closed unit cube parallel to the axes intersects
\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be a unit cube parallel to the axes, i.e.,
Q=q+Q_{0}
. Let
\left[{q}\right]
be the point from
\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
closest to
q
. Since for every coordinate, by definition,
\left|{q_{j}-\left[{q}\right]_{j}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
q
is the center of the unit cube,
Q
, then
\left[{q}\right]\in Q\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For every unit cube parallel to the axes,
Q
, there exists a grid-cube,
G
, so that
G\cap Q
contains a
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube of
Q
.

Remark 4.4
.

Note that if
Q
is centered exactly at the intersection of
2^{2d}
grid-cubes, then all of them contain a
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
-sub-cube of
Q
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be a unit cube parallel to the axes, i.e.,
Q=q+Q_{0}
. We define the cube
G=\left[{q}\right]+Q_{0}
.

It is enough to show that the cube
G
contains both the center of
Q
and one of its vertices to conclude it must contain a
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube of
Q
. This is since the intersection of two closed boxes parallel to the axes is always a closed box parallel to the axes.

Assume that
G
does not contain any vertex of
Q
. Following Lemma
4.2
,
\left[{q}\right]\in G\cap Q
and so their intersection cannot be empty, then
G
must be contained in
Q
. However, since they share the same edge-length,
Q
cannot contain
G
, unless
G=Q
and, in particular,
G
must contain a vertex of
Q
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
be two polynomially convex sets, and assume that there exists a hyperplane separating them, then
K_{1}\cup K_{2}
is polynomially convex.

Remark 4.6
.

While the proof uses Runge’s theorem, it only uses it for one variable and therefore it does not restrict the dimension,
d
.

Proof.

Let
H
be the hyperplane separating the sets
K_{1}
from
K_{2}
, and let
L:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a linear map satisfying
H=\left\{{L=0}\right\}
. While
L
is a linear map over
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
it can be viewed as a map (but not linear) over
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, i.e., if
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{d})\cong\left({x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},\cdots,x_{d},y_{d}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d}
, then we abuse the notation of
L
and define
L:\mathbb{C}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
L(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z})=L\left({x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},\cdots,x_{d},y_{d}}\right)
.

Since
H
separates the sets
K_{1}
from
K_{2}
, and both sets are compact, there exists
\varepsilon>0
so that
K_{1}\subset\left\{{L(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z})>\varepsilon}\right\}
and
K_{2}\subset\left\{{L(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z})<-\varepsilon}\right\}
(if the reverse holds the linear map
(-L)
satisfies those requirements).

Note that polynomial convexity is invariant under rotations and translations. This holds since the set of polynomials is invariant under compositions with translations and rotations. We may therefore assume without loss of generality that
L(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z})=\Re(z_{1})=x_{1}
.

Denote by
A_{j}
the projection of the set
K_{j}
onto the complex plane, i.e.,
z\in A_{j}
if there exists
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in K_{j}
so that
z_{1}=z
. Note that
A_{1},A_{2}
are compact sets as projections of compact sets. Moreover if
z\in A_{j}
denotes the projection of
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in K_{j}
, then

dist(A_{1},A_{2})=\underset{z\in A_{1}\atop w\in A_{2}}{\sup}\;\left|{z-w}\right|\geq\underset{z\in A_{1}\atop w\in A_{2}}{\sup}\;\left|{\Re(z-w)}\right|=\underset{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in K_{1}\atop\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}\in K_{2}}{\sup}\;\left|{L(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z})-L(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w})}\right|\geq\varepsilon-(-\varepsilon)=2\varepsilon.

Let
p_{0}:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a polynomial satisfying
\left|{p_{0}(z)}\right|<\frac{1}{3}
for every
z\in A_{1}
and
\left|{p_{0}(z)-1}\right|<\frac{1}{3}
for every
z\in A_{2}
. Such a polynomial can be constructed by applying Runge’s theorem to the model map assigning
0
to every
z\in A_{1}
and
1
to every
z\in A_{2}
with error
\frac{1}{3}
.

Define the polynomial
p(z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{d}):=p_{0}(z_{1})
. Then

\widehat{p(K_{1})}\cap\widehat{p(K_{2})}\subset\widehat{\left({-\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3}}\right)}\cap\widehat{\left({\frac{2}{3},\frac{4}{3}}\right)}=\emptyset,

and following Kallin’s lemma, Lemma
3.2
,
\widehat{K_{1}\cup K_{2}}=\widehat{K_{1}}\cup\widehat{K_{2}}=K_{1}\cup K_{2}
, i.e.,
K_{1}\cup K_{2}
is polynomially convex.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a compact set which does not intersect the boundary of any grid-cube. If for every grid-cube,
G
, the set
K\cap G
is polynomially convex, then
K
is polynomially convex.

Proof.

We will prove this proposition by induction on the dimension. Note that since
K
is compact, then there exists
N
so that
K\subset[-N,N]^{2d}
.

Step 1:
For every
\left({j_{2},j_{3},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d-1}
fixed, and every
-N\leq k\leq N
, the cube

B_{k}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}:=\left({k,j_{2},j_{3},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)+Q_{0},

is a grid-cube. Now,
K\cap B_{-N}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}
is separated from
K\cap B_{-N+1}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}
by the hyperplane
H_{1}^{1}=\left\{{x_{1}=-N+\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
. Applying Lemma
4.5
, we conclude that the set

K\cap\left({B_{-N}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}\cup B_{-N+1}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}}\right)

is a polynomially convex set. Next, the set
K\cap\left({B_{-N}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}\cup B_{-N+1}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}}\right)
is separated from the set
K\cap B_{-N+2}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}
by the hyperplane
H_{2}^{1}=\left\{{x_{1}=-N+1+\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
, and as before, applying Lemma
4.5
, the set

K\cap\left({B_{-N}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}\cup B_{-N+1}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}\cup B_{-N+2}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}}\right)

is polynomially convex. By induction, for every
\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2n}}\right)
, the set

K^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}:=K\cap\bigcup_{{k}={-N}}^{N}B_{k}^{\left({j_{2},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}

is a polynomially convex set.

Step 2:
For every
\left({j_{3},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d-2}
fixed, the set
K^{\left({-N,j_{3},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}
is separated from the set
K^{\left({-N+1,j_{3},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}
by the hyperplane
H_{1}^{2}:=\left\{{y_{1}=-N+\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
. Applying Lemma
4.5
, the set

K^{\left({-N,j_{3},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}\cup K^{\left({-N+1,j_{3},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}

is polynomially convex. As in Step 1, by induction, the set

K^{\left({j_{3},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}:=\bigcup_{{k}={-N}}^{N}K^{\left({k,j_{3},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}

is polynomially convex.

Step n:
Assume without loss of generality that
n
is even, the odd case is identical. For every
\left({j_{n+1},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d-n}
fixed, the sets
K^{\left({k,j_{n+1},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}
are polynomially convex by the induction assumption on step
(n-1)
. In addition, the set
K^{\left({-N,j_{n+1},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}
is separated from the set
K^{\left({-N+1,j_{n+1},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}
by the hyperplane
H_{1}^{n}:=\left\{{y_{\frac{n}{2}}=-N+\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
. If
n
is odd, the hyperplane will be defined by
\left\{{x_{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}=-N+\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
. Applying Lemma
4.5
, the set

K^{\left({-N,j_{n+1},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}\cup K^{\left({-N+1,j_{n+1},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}

is polynomially convex. As in Step 1, by induction, the set

K^{\left({j_{n+1},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}:=\bigcup_{{k}={-N}}^{N}K^{\left({k,j_{n+1},\cdots,j_{2d}}\right)}

is polynomially convex.

After
2d
steps we obtain that the set
K=K\setminus\underset{q\in\mathbb{Z}^{2d}}{\bigcup}\partial\left({q+Q_{0}}\right)
is polynomially convex.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a compact set and assume that
K
does not intersect a finite collection of hyperplanes parallel to the axes. If for every box,
B
, generated by those hyperplanes, the set
K\cap B
is polynomially convex, then
K
is polynomially convex.

The proof is the same as the proof of Proposition
4.7
but the notation is more complicated.

4.3
The proof of Proposition
4.1

Proof.

To simplify the notation in the proof, we write
\mathbb{C}^{d}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2d}=\left\{{\left({x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{2d}}\right),x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}}\right\}
.

Since the collection of unit cubes is finite there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\bigcup_{{j}={1}}^{M}Q_{j}\subset\left[{-N,N}\right]^{2d}
. For every
1\leq k\leq 2d
and
-N\leq j\leq N-1
we define the hyperplane
H_{k}^{j}:=\left\{{x_{k}=j+\frac{1}{2}}\right\}
, and the strip
S_{k}^{j}:=\left\{{\left|{x_{k}-\left({j+\frac{1}{2}}\right)}\right|<\delta}\right\}
, where
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,M)
will be defined at the end of the proof. Let
U_{0}:=\left({\bigcup_{{j}={1}}^{M}Q_{j}}\right)\setminus\left({\bigcup_{{k}={1}}^{2d}\bigcup_{{j}={-N}}^{N-1}S_{k}^{j}}\right)
. Using Proposition
4.7
, it is enough to show that there exists a set
U\subset U_{0}
so that for every grid-cube,
G
, the set
G\cap U
is polynomially convex, and
m_{2d}\left({\bigcup_{{j}={1}}^{M}Q_{j}\setminus U}\right)<\varepsilon
.

We will modify the set
U_{0}
inside each grid-cube. First note that since the grid-cubes and the cubes,
Q_{j}
, are both closed, parallel to the axes, and have the same edge-length, then the intersection of any two of them is either empty of must contain a vertex of the grid-cube. Since the collection
\left\{{Q_{j}}\right\}
is pairwise disjoint, this means that for every grid-cube,
G
,

\#\left\{{j,Q_{j}\cap G\neq\emptyset}\right\}\leq\#\left\{{v,\text{vertex of }G}\right\}=\#\left\{{\left\{{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}\right\}^{2d}}\right\}=2^{2d}.

Next, following Lemma
4.3
, for every cube,
Q_{j}
there exists a grid-cube, which we will denote
G_{j}
, so that
Q_{j}\cap G_{j}
contains a
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube of
Q_{j}
. Since we saw that
Q_{j}
contains the center of
G_{j}
, every grid-cube contains at most
one

\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube of
Q_{j}
for some
Q_{j}
in the collection.

Fix a grid-cube,
G
.

Case 1:

G
does not contain a
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube of any cube in the collection,
\left\{{Q_{j}}\right\}
.

For every
Q_{j_{\nu}}
intersecting
G
, let
\left\{{x_{k}=a_{k}^{\pm j_{\nu}}}\right\}_{k=1}^{2d}
denote the collection of hyperplanes defining the cube
Q_{j_{\nu}}
, i.e.,
Q_{j_{\nu}}=\bigcap_{{k}={1}}^{2d}\left\{{a_{k}^{-j_{\nu}}\leq x_{k}\leq a_{k}^{+j_{\nu}}}\right\}
. We define

U\cap G:=G\cap U_{0}\setminus\left({\underset{j_{u}}{\bigcup}\bigcup_{{k}={1}}^{2d}\underset{\sigma\in\left\{{+,-}\right\}}{\bigcup}\left\{{\left|{x_{k}-a_{k}^{\sigma j_{\nu}}}\right|<\delta}\right\}}\right),

i.e., we remove from all the other cubes which intersect
G
a ’fattening’ by
2\delta
of each hyperplane defining
Q_{j\nu}
. The hyperplanes
\left\{{x_{k}=a_{k}^{\pm j_{\nu}}}\right\}_{k=1}^{2d}
for
Q_{j_{\nu}}\cap G\neq\emptyset
separate the components of
U\cap G
, which are now closed boxes. Each closed box is a polynomially convex set. Following Corollary
4.8
, the set
U\cap G
is polynomially convex as well. See Figure
2
below.

Case 2:

G
contains a
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube, i.e.,
G=G_{j}
for some cube,
Q_{j}
.

Essentially we will repeat the first case, but we first partition
G
according the hyperplanes defining
Q_{j}
. Formally, assume
Q_{j}=\bigcap_{{k}={1}}^{2d}\left\{{a_{k}^{-}\leq x_{k}\leq a_{k}^{+}}\right\}
, and define
S_{k}^{\pm}:=\left\{{\left|{x_{k}-a_{k}^{\pm}}\right|<\delta}\right\}
. Then
G\setminus\bigcup_{{k}={1}}^{2d}S_{k}^{\pm}
is a union of closed boxes, one of them contains a
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube of
Q_{j}
that ’lost’ at most
2d
’fattened’ hyperplanes from its boundary.

Next, we will repeat the same argument described in the first case in each box composing the set
G\setminus\bigcup_{{k}={1}}^{2d}S_{k}^{\pm}
, to construct a set
U
. As in the first case, the set
U
satisfies that in each of those boxes,
B
, the set
B\cap U
is polynomially convex. Then we apply Corollary
4.8
to the grid of hyperplanes giving rise to the cube
Q_{j}
to conclude that
U\cap G
is polynomially convex.

Figure 2:
The top left figure shows four grid cubes (whose boundary is dark gray), and three cubes in the collection,
\left\{{Q_{j}}\right\}
(empty cubes with black boundary).

The first case, where the grid cube is striped, is depicted in the top line. The second case, where the grid cube is dark gray, is depicted in the bottom line. The thick black lines are the strips we remove to create the set
U
. As described above, in the second case we have two steps.

It is left to estimate how much measure we lost. Note that the volume every strip removes for every cube is bounded by
2\delta
since
S\cap Q_{j}=q+[-\delta,\delta]\times\left[{0,1}\right]^{2d-1}
where
q\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is some translation of the strip. We will first bound how many strips we remove from every cube.

By duality between grid-cubes and cubes in the collection, the number of grid-cubes that intersect
Q_{j}
is bounded by
2^{2d}
as well. Each grid cube is responsible to removing up to
2^{2d}
strips itself, and up to
2^{2d}\times 2^{2d}
strips from the cubes in the collection it might intersect (
2^{2d}
cubes each gives rise to at most
2^{2d}
strips). Overall,

\displaystyle m_{2d}(Q_{j}\setminus U)

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta\cdot\#\left\{{S,\text{strip},S\cap Q_{j}\neq\emptyset}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta\cdot\#\left\{{G,\text{grid-cube},G\cap Q_{j}\neq\emptyset}\right\}\cdot\#\left\{{S,\text{Strip in }G}\right\}\leq 2\delta\cdot 2^{2d}\left({2^{4d}+2^{2d}}\right)\leq\delta\cdot 2^{7d}.

Overall, we see that

m_{2d}\left({\bigcup_{{j}={1}}^{M}Q_{j}\setminus U}\right)\leq M\cdot\delta\cdot 2^{7d}=\varepsilon,

if we set
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,M)=\frac{\varepsilon}{M\cdot 2^{7d}}
. Naturally, if
Q_{j}
was a grid cube, then
Q_{j}^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{M\cdot 2^{7d}}}\subset U
concluding the proof.
∎

.

Note that since the

Lebesgue measure of hyperplanes is zero, the lemma holds for half open half closed cubes of the form

as well.

5
Actions of Arbitrary Free Ergodic Groups

This section will extend the construction done for the solenoidal group for general ergodic free actions of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. The first subsection compiles definitions and known results that will be required for the proof. All of the definitions and proofs can be found in
[
GA
]
and
[
W
]
.

5.1
Definitions and Preliminaries

Throughout this section we let
\left({X,\mathcal{B},\mu}\right)
be a standard probability space and let
T:\mathbb{C}^{d}\rightarrow PPT(X)
be an arbitrary free action of the group
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, i.e., for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
,
T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}x=x
implies that
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}=\overset{\rightharpoonup}{0}
. In other words, the set of all periodic points has measure zero.

Definition 5.1
.

A map
F:X\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is called
measurably entire
if it is a non-constant measurable function and for
\mu
-almost every
x\in X
the map
F_{x}:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by
F_{x}(z):=F(T_{z}x)
is entire. We will call it
measurably entire dense
if in addition
F_{x}
has a dense orbit under the action by translations, for almost every
x\in X
.

We would like to define a function
F_{x}
without worrying about the measurability in
x
. We will therefore use the notion of
S
-sets:

Definition 5.2
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
be a compact set. A set
B\in\mathcal{B}
is called an
S
-set
if

(
F_{1}
)

For every
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\neq\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}\in S
,
T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}B\cap T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}}B=\emptyset
.

(
F_{2}
)

For every
B^{\prime}\subset B\subset X
measurable, and every
A\subset S\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
measurable, the set
AB^{\prime}:=\underset{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in A}{\bigcup}T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}B^{\prime}
is a measurable subset of
X
.

In the definition above, the set
S
is the set of ’shifts’ by the action of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, marked
T
, while
B\subset X
is a
\mu
-null set that we ’shift’ by elements of
S
in the space
X
. While
B
is
\mu
-null set, we can still identify the set
SB
with the product space
S\times B
:

Remark 5.3
.

[
GA
, Remark 2.5]

For every square
S\subset\mathbb{C}
and every
B\in\mathcal{B}
, an
S
-set

\mu\left({AB}\right):=\mu\left({\left\{{T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}B,\;\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in A}\right\}}\right)=\frac{m_{2d}(A)}{m_{2d}(S)}\cdot\mu\left({SB}\right),\;\text{ for every }A\subset S\text{ measurable},

where
m_{2d}
denotes the
2d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

We can use this property to define a measure on Borel subsets of the set
B
-

\nu(B^{\prime})=\frac{\mu\left({SB^{\prime}}\right)}{\mu\left({SB}\right)},

and, in turn, define the product measure on the product space as follows; for every
B^{\prime}\subset B
and
A\subset S
we let

\left({\left({\frac{1}{m_{2d}(S)}\cdot m_{2d}(\cdot)}\right)\times\nu\left({\cdot}\right)}\right)(A\times B^{\prime})=\frac{m_{2d}(A)}{m_{2d}(S)}\cdot\nu(B^{\prime})=\frac{\mu\left({AB}\right)}{\mu\left({SB}\right)}\cdot\frac{\mu\left({SB^{\prime}}\right)}{\mu\left({SB}\right)}.

For our purpose,
S
will be a
2d
dimensional cube.

We are interested in
S
-sets, for
S\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
a cube, because these sets allow us to assign for every
x\in B
a function
F_{x}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, which is holomorphic in
S
, creating a measurably entire function
F:SB\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{d}
defined by
F(T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}x)=F(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z},x)=F_{x}(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z})
without worrying about inconsistencies in the definition of
F
. Note that as
B
is an
S
-set, the map
T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}x\mapsto(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z},x)
is well defined, and so is our function
F
. We would therefore like to approximate our space
X
by a sequence of sets
\left\{{S_{n}B_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
where
B_{n}
is an
S_{n}
-set, and
S_{n}=\left[0,a_{n}\right)^{2d}
for
a_{n}\nearrow\infty
.

The first lemma is a natural extension of Rokhlin’s lemma, that approximates the space
X
by a sequence of product spaces
S_{n}\times B_{n}
where
B_{n}\subset X
and
S_{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

[The Nested Towers Lemma]
Let
\left\{{a_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be an increasing sequence of positive numbers such that
\underset{{n}={1}}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\;\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n+1}}<\frac{1}{2}
. Then there exists a sequence of sets
\left\{{B_{n}}\right\}\subset\mathcal{B}
such that

(
N_{1}
)

B_{n}
is an
S_{n}=\left[0,a_{n}\right)^{2d}
-set.

(
N_{2}
)

S_{n}B_{n}\subset S_{n+1}B_{n+1}
.

(
N_{3}
)

\mu\left({S_{n}B_{n}}\right)\nearrow 1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

A detailed proof can be found for example in the appendix of
[
GA
]
. While the proof deals with the case of
d=1
the same proof holds for all
d
.

Let
\left\{{B_{n}}\right\}
be a sequence of sets given by the Nested Towers Lemma, Lemma
5.4
. Assume that
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
is a finite partition of
B_{n-1}
into measurable sets
\left\{{B_{n-1}^{j}}\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{n-1}}
. For every
x\in B_{n}
and
1\leq\ell\leq k_{n-1}
define the set

R_{n}^{\ell}(x):=\left\{{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in S_{n};\;T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}x\in B_{n-1}^{\ell}}\right\}.

Intuitively, the set
R_{n}^{\ell}(x)
is the set of all points in the cube
S_{n}
which will send
x
back to
B_{n-1}^{\ell}
. Since
B_{n-1}
is an
S_{n-1}
-set, for every
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\neq\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}\in R_{n}^{\ell}(x)
we have that
S_{n-1}T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}x\cap S_{n-1}T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}}x=\emptyset
which implies
\left|\left|{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}-\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}}\right|\right|_{\infty}>a_{n-1}
. In particular,
R_{n}^{\ell}(x)
is a finite set and therefore compact.

Following
[
GA
]
, given
\delta>0
, we say a partition
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\left\{{B_{n}^{j}}\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{n}}
is a
\delta
-fine partition consistent with
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
if it is a finite measurable partition of
B_{n}
, and for every
1\leq j\leq k_{n}
for every
x,y\in B_{n}^{j}
,
d_{H}(R_{n}^{\ell}(x),R_{n}^{\ell}(y))<\delta
for every
1\leq\ell\leq k_{n-1}
, where
d_{H}
denotes the Hausdorff distance between sets (see Figure
3
below).

Figure 3:
An example of the sets
R_{n}^{\ell}(x)
and
R_{n}^{\ell}(y)
for some
y,x
in the same partition set.

Lemma 5.5
.

[
GA
, Lemma 2.7]

Let
\left\{{B_{n}}\right\}
be a sequence of sets given by the Nested Towers Lemma. For every sequence of positive numbers
\left\{{\delta_{n}}\right\}
there exists a sequence of partitions
\left\{{\mathcal{P}_{n}}\right\}
such that for every
n
,
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a
\delta_{n}
-fine partition consistent with
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
.

As in the case of the Nested Towers Lemma, this lemma was proved for
d=1
but the same proof extends to higher dimensions.

5.2
On the Existence of Measurably Entire Dense Functions

Theorem 5.6
.

For every standard probability space,
\left({X,\mathcal{B},\mu}\right)
, and every free action of the group
\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
T:\mathbb{C}^{d}\rightarrow PPT(X)
, there exists a measurably entire dense function.

Proof.

Let
\left\{{a_{n}}\right\}\nearrow\infty
be a sequence growing fast enough and denote by
S_{n}:=\left[0,a_{n}\right)^{2d}
. Let
\left\{{B_{n}}\right\}
be a sequence of sets obtained for the sequence
\left\{{a_{n}}\right\}
by Weiss’ Nested Towers Lemma, Lemma
5.4
, such that
\mu\left({X\setminus S_{1}B_{1}}\right)<\frac{1}{200}
. Let
\left\{{p_{n}}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be an enumeration of all polynomials over
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with complex-rational coefficients. Note that not only this set is dense in the set of holomorphic functions over
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, if we remove a finite number of elements, it remains a dense set.

We will set
X_{n}:=S_{n}B_{n}
, and define the sequence of functions
F_{n}
as a linear combination of step functions,

F_{n}\left({T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}x}\right)=F_{n}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z},x}\right)=\underset{{j}={1}}{\overset{k_{n}}{\sum}}\;F_{n}^{j}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}\right)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{B_{n}^{j}}(x),

where
\left\{{F_{n}^{j}}\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{n}}
are holomorphic, and
\left\{{B_{n}^{j}}\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{n-1}}
is a measurable partition of
B_{n}
, denoted
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Note that
F_{n}
is well defined, since
B_{n}
is an
S_{n}
-set and therefore the mapping
T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}x\mapsto\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z},x}\right)
is well defined. Each such function will satisfy:

(Condition (D))

\displaystyle\text{ For every }1\leq k\leq n\text{ there exists a translation }\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}_{k}^{j,n}(S)\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\text{ so that}

\displaystyle\underset{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in Q_{k}^{-\underset{{\ell}={k+1}}{\overset{n}{\sum}}\;2^{-\ell}}}{\sup}\left|{F_{n}^{j}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}_{k}^{j,n}+\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}\right)-p_{k}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}\right)}\right|<\underset{{\ell}={k+1}}{\overset{n}{\sum}}\;2^{-\ell},\text{ where }Q_{k}\text{ is a }\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\text{ sub-cube of }S_{k},

where given a set
U
and
\varepsilon>0
we define

\displaystyle U^{+\varepsilon}:=\left\{{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{d},dist(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z},U)<\varepsilon}\right\}

\displaystyle U^{-\varepsilon}:=\left\{{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in U,dist(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z},\mathbb{C}\setminus U)>\varepsilon}\right\}.

Intuitively
U^{+\varepsilon}
is a ’fattening’ of
U
by
\varepsilon
and
U^{-\varepsilon}
is ’shrinking’
U
by
\varepsilon
.

Formally, we will construct this sequence inductively.

Define
F_{1}:X_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by

F_{1}\left({T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}x}\right)=F_{1}\left({x,\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}\right)=p_{1}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}\right).

F_{1}
is measurable, since it is constant with respect to one variable, and continuous with respect to the other, and it satisfies
Condition (D)
.

Assume that
F_{n-1}:X_{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
was defined as

F_{n-1}\left({T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}x}\right)=F_{n-1}\left({x,\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}\right)=\underset{{j}={1}}{\overset{k_{n-1}}{\sum}}\;F_{n-1}^{j}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{B_{n-1}^{j}}(x),

satisfied
Condition (D)
, and

(1)

\displaystyle\mu\left({x\in X_{n-2},\left|{F_{n-1}(x)-F_{n-2}(x)}\right|>2^{-(n-1)}}\right)<2^{-(n-1)}.

Since
F_{n-1}^{j}
is holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
for every
j
fixed, it is uniformly continuous on
S_{n-1}^{+1}=\left[-1,a_{n-1}+1\right)^{2d}
, and therefore there exists
\delta_{n}\in(0,1)
such that for every
1\leq j\leq k_{n-1}
:

\underset{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z},\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}\in S_{n-1}^{+1}\atop\left|{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}-\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}}\right|<\delta_{n}}{\sup}\;\left|{F_{n-1}^{j}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}\right)-F_{n-1}^{j}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}}\right)}\right|<\frac{10^{-2n}}{2}.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
be a partition of
B_{n}
which is
\delta_{n}
-fine and consistent with
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}=\left\{{B_{n-1}^{\ell}}\right\}_{\ell=1}^{k_{n-1}}
, the partition of
B_{n-1}
used to define
F_{n-1}
. Such a partition exists by Lemma
5.5
.

For every
j
we use the axiom of choice to choose a representative
x_{n}^{j}\in B_{n}^{j}
. We associate every point
x_{n}^{j}
with a collection of cubes. First, we note that the Nested Tower Lemma naturally relates for every
x\in B_{n}
a collection of copies of
S_{n-1}
inside
S_{n}
defined by

\Lambda_{n}^{j}:=\left\{{\lambda\in S_{n},T_{\lambda}x_{n}^{j}\in B_{n-1}}\right\}.

Since
B_{n}
is an
S_{n}
-set, the collection of cubes
\left\{{\lambda+S_{n-1}}\right\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}^{j}}
is pairwise disjoint. We will choose the sequence
\left\{{a_{n}}\right\}
so that
\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-1}}\in\mathbb{N}
. Before applying Proposition
4.1
to this collection, we want to ’clear out’ one cube in the center of each
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube of
S_{n}
to make space for the polynomial
p_{n}
. Assume, for example, that
a_{n}=2^{m_{n}}
for some sequence
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
m_{n}\nearrow\infty
. Then
\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-1}}=2^{m_{n}-m_{n-1}}
and in the center of each of the
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cubes there exists a copy of
S_{n-1}
(indeed the choice is not unique, there are
2^{2d}
options). Denote the centers of the chosen cubes by
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}_{k}^{n},1\leq k\leq 2^{2d}
. Note that this choice does not depend on the point
x_{n}^{j}
only on the ratio
\frac{a_{n}}{a_{n-1}}
. In addition, it is important to choose one sub-cube in each
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
of the sub-cubes of
S_{n}
since Proposition
4.1
only guarantees one of the
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cubes will be almost fully included in the almost polynomially convex set,
U
.

Define the collection of cubes

\mathcal{P}_{n}^{j}:=\left\{{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}_{k}^{n}+S_{n-1}}\right\}_{k=1}^{2^{2d}}\cup\left\{{\lambda+S_{n-1},\left({\lambda+S_{n-1}}\right)\cap\left({\bigcup_{{k}={1}}^{2^{2d}}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}_{k}^{n}+S_{n-1}}\right)}\right)=\emptyset,\lambda\in\Lambda_{n}^{j}}\right\}.

This collection is a finite collection of disjoint cubes all contained in
S_{n}
. We can therefore apply Proposition
4.1
to obtain a polynomially convex set,
U_{n}^{j}\subset S_{n}
, satisfying that

1.

For every
1\leq k\leq 2^{2d}
,
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}_{k}^{n}+S_{n-1}^{-2^{-n}}\subset U_{n}^{j}
, where
S_{n-1}^{-2^{-n}}=\left[2^{-n},a_{n-1}-2^{-n}\right)^{2d}
, as
\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}_{k}^{n}+S_{n-1}
are grid cubes.

2.

For every
Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{j}
there exists a
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cube,
B_{Q}
satisfying
B_{Q}^{-2^{-n}}\subset U_{n}^{j}
.

3.

\frac{m_{2d}\left({\left({\underset{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{j}}{\bigcup}Q}\right)\setminus U_{n}^{j}}\right)}{m_{2d}(S_{n-1})}<2^{-n}
.

Define the map
j_{n}:B_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
by
j_{n}(x)=j
if
x\in B_{n}^{j}
, and the function

G_{n}^{j}(\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z})=\begin{cases}p_{n}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}-\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}_{k}^{n}}\right)&,\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}_{k}^{n}+S_{n-1}\\
F_{n-1}^{j_{n-1}\left({T_{\lambda}x_{n}^{j}}\right)}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}-\lambda}\right)&,\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}\in\lambda+S_{n-1}\end{cases}.

We use
Oka-Weil Approximation Theorem
to approximate
G_{n}^{j}
on the polynomially convex set
U_{n}^{j}
with error
2^{-n}
and denote the approximation
F_{n}^{j}
. Since
U_{n}^{j}
contains
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cubes of
S_{n-1}
, excluding a-
2^{-n}
strip along its boundary, each of them contain
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cubes of
S_{n-2}
excluding a
2^{-(n-1)}
strip along its boundary and so on. By induction,
U_{n}^{j}
contains copies of
\frac{1}{2^{2d}}
sub-cubes of
S_{k}
excluding a
\underset{{m}={k}}{\overset{n}{\sum}}\;2^{-m}
strips on their boundaries. In particular
F_{n}^{j}
satisfies
Condition (D)
.

We define
F_{n}:X_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by

F_{n}\left({T_{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}x}\right)=F_{n}\left({x,\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}\right)=\underset{{j}={1}}{\overset{k_{n}}{\sum}}\;F_{n}^{j}\left({\overset{\rightharpoonup}{z}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{B_{n}^{j}}(x).

Finally, let
E_{n}
be the set where either
F_{n-1}
was not defined or
F_{n}
does not approximate
F_{n-1}
well, namely

E_{n}=X\setminus X_{n-1}\cup\left\{{x\in X_{n-1},\left|{F_{n}(x)-F_{n-1}(x)}\right|>2^{-n}}\right\}.

Then, following Remark
5.3

\displaystyle\mu\left({E_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mu\left({\bigcup_{{j}={1}}^{k_{n}}\left({\left({\underset{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{j}}{\bigcup}Q\setminus U_{n}^{j}}\right)B_{n}^{j}}\right)}\right)+O\left({\frac{a_{n-1}}{a_{n}}}\right)+\mu\left({X\setminus X_{n-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\underset{{j}={1}}{\overset{k_{n}}{\sum}}\;\frac{m_{2d}\left({\left({\underset{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{j}}{\bigcup}Q}\right)\setminus U_{n}^{j}}\right)}{m_{2d}\left({\underset{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{j}}{\bigcup}Q}\right)}\cdot\mu\left({\left({\underset{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{j}}{\bigcup}Q}\right)B_{n}^{j}}\right)+O\left({\frac{a_{n-1}}{a_{n}}}\right)+\mu\left({X\setminus X_{n-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-n}\mu\left({S_{n}B_{n}}\right)+O\left({\frac{a_{n-1}}{a_{n}}}\right)+\mu\left({X\setminus X_{n-1}}\right)<2^{-n}

where
O\left({\frac{a_{n-1}}{a_{n}}}\right)
comes from the fact that we replace up to
\left({2^{2d}}\right)^{2}
elements in the sets
\Lambda_{n}^{j}
to create the collection
\left\{{\overset{\rightharpoonup}{w}_{k}^{n}+S_{n-1}}\right\}
. We conclude that (
1
) holds.

Lastly, let
F=\underset{{n}\rightarrow{\infty}}{\lim}\;F_{n}
, then using Borel-Cantelli’s Lemma,

\mu\left({\left\{{x\in X\text{ s.t. the orbit of }F_{x}\atop\text{does not have a dense orbit}}\right\}}\right)\leq\mu\left({\bigcap_{{n}={1}}^{\infty}\bigcup_{{m}={n}}^{\infty}E_{m}}\right)=0,

since
\underset{{m}={1}}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\;\mu\left({E_{m}}\right)\leq\underset{{m}={1}}{\overset{\infty}{\sum}}\;2^{-m}<\infty,
concluding the proof of Theorem
5.6
.
