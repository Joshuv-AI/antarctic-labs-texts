Probable real injury to the abdomen in the Damoxenos-Creugas encounter: historical narratives interpreted through trauma mechanics
Authors: Kun Hwang
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Historical narratives frequently describe extraordinary acts of violence, challenging modern readers to distinguish literary embellishment from anatomically plausible injury patterns. One such account is the confrontation between Damoxenos and Creugas, as recorded by Pausanias (Fig. 1) [1]. In this encounter, Damoxenos reportedly inflicted severe abdominal trauma through a direct digital assault. Although many scholars regard this account as an exaggeration consistent with heroic narrative conventions, closer analysis through the lens of modern trauma medicine suggests that some degree of injury may have been plausible, even if “digital disembowelment” is biomechanically impossible. Evaluating the anatomical feasibility, biomechanical requirements, and probable clinical sequelae of such an assault provides insights relevant to both historical scholarship and contemporary trauma education.

HISTORICAL AND LITERARY CONTEXT

Pausanias’ account describes a fierce struggle culminating in Damoxenos using his finger or a sharpened fingernail to strike Creugas’ abdomen. The text emphasizes the dramatic intensity of the act, portraying it as both violent and heroic [2]. Scholars have long debated the plausibility of this description. Some argue that it is metaphorical and reflects cultural motifs of martial prowess, whereas others suggest that low-force penetrating injuries may have been underrecognized in historical accounts. The narrative is notable because it depicts an unusual form of abdominal trauma: a focused, sharp-pointed force applied through a natural human digit. Although literary exaggeration is likely, the proposed mechanism can be examined using contemporary anatomical and biomechanical principles.

BIOMECHANICAL ANALYSIS

Even in trained athletes, maximum fingertip force has been estimated at approximately 100 to 150 N, concentrated at the fingertip. Although this magnitude of force may be sufficient to produce superficial or partial-thickness injury, it is substantially lower than the force required for full abdominal penetration leading to evisceration.

Complete disembowelment would require disruption of all abdominal wall layers, as well as the ability to overcome intra-abdominal pressure and the structural integrity of the intestines. Estimates suggest that more than 500 N or even exceeding 1,000 N of localized force would be necessary—well beyond the capability of a human digit. Modern trauma literature indicates that even sharp implements rarely cause evisceration without substantial leverage or momentum (Table 1) [3].

Accordingly, the description of full digital disembowelment is biomechanically impossible. A more realistic injury pattern would involve localized penetration of the skin and abdominal musculature, potentially extending to the peritoneum, which would be consistent with contemporary descriptions of low-force penetrating abdominal trauma [4].

CONCLUSIONS

Although the Damoxenos-Creugas encounter contains elements of literary exaggeration, careful analysis using contemporary trauma principles suggests that a probable abdominal injury could have occurred. This intersection of historical narrative and medical science not only enriches understanding of ancient accounts but also provides contemporary trauma surgeons with a perspective on unusual penetrating injury mechanisms. Evaluating historical descriptions through an anatomical and biomechanical framework underscores the enduring relevance of fundamental trauma principles across contexts.
