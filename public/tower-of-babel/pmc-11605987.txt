The relationship between physical activity and school adjustment in high school students: the chain mediating role of psychological resilience and self-control
Authors: Hanwen Chen, Tianci Lu, Haoran Sui, Chong Liu, Yuan Gao, Baole Tao, Jun Yan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdaptive elements such as learning and interpersonal interactions of high school students need to be developed in the school environment. Thus, school adjustment is an essential developmental task in the academic growth of adolescent students. Based on the developmental task theory, combined with the conservation of resources theory and the strength model of self-control, this study aims to investigate the chain-mediated roles of psychological resilience and self-control in the physical activity and school adaptation of high school students, in terms of the internal development of the individual mentality.MethodsThe study utilized whole population sampling and selected 2054 first- and second-year students from eight high schools in four regions of Jiangsu Province (M = 16.45 years, SD = 0.72 years). The questionnaires included the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-S), Adolescent Psychological Resilience Scale, Self-Control Scale, and School Adjustment Scale for High School Students. Descriptive and correlation analyses were conducted for each variable, and a chain mediation model was constructed after controlling for the gender and region of the participants. Data were analyzed using SPSS and Process 4.0 macros.ResultsThe direct and indirect associations of physical activity on high school students’ school adjustment were significant, and the indirect associations included three pathways: first, the separate mediating association of psychological resilience; second, the separate mediating association of self-control; and third, the chain mediating association of psychological resilience and self-control.ConclusionThe study’s results revealed the relationship and mechanism of action of physical activity on high school students’ school adjustment, which provides essential theoretical and reference value for improving their school adjustment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-024-02227-1.Keywords: Adolescents, Physical activity, School adjustment, Mediation role

Introduction

Adolescence is the second leap in developing adolescents’ self-awareness and an essential period for individuals to learn and master various knowledge and skills. Unlike adults, adolescents’ social adaptation has unique adaptation content and developmental tasks due to the uniqueness of their developmental environment [1]. Specifically, adolescents’ social adaptation focuses on school adjustment and aims at individual socialization. Some studies have pointed out that school is the primary social activity of adolescents; adolescents in school time account for two-thirds of their waking time, and students’ learning and interpersonal interaction and other adaptive elements need to be developed in the school environment [2]. Therefore, school adjustment is an essential developmental task in the learning and growth of adolescent students. Adolescents’ school adjustment has been the focus of much attention. For adolescents, school adjustment is an essential indicator for evaluating their mental health, and reasonable school adjustment is one of the manifestations of their mental health. Individuals with poor school adjustment are more likely to have academic problems such as withdrawal, dropout, poor academic performance, depression, poor teacher-student relationships, and peer relationships [3–5]. Therefore, considering the role of school adjustment in the development of adolescent groups, it is necessary to explore the influencing factors and mechanisms of school adjustment to improve adolescents’ school adjustment.

School adjustment refers to the condition in which a student participates happily in school activities in a school context and shows success in learning, interpersonal interactions, participation in school activities, and emotional adjustment [6]. There are various factors affecting high school students’ school adjustment, including family environment, school environment, interpersonal factors, intra-individual psychological development factors, as well as social and environmental factors caused by social changes [7]. Therefore, it is essential to find ways to promote middle school students’ school adjustment and further promote the overall development of adolescents. According to Havighurst’s Developmental Task Theory, “developmental tasks” are specific things, jobs or tasks that need to be accomplished at various stages of an individual’s growth. At different stages of life, individuals need to develop appropriate skills, knowledge, functioning, and attitudes in order to conform to the expectations or requirements of society [8]. Individuals who are able to successfully accomplish the developmental tasks at each stage are considered healthy or well-adjusted. Conversely, those individuals who fail to achieve developmental tasks may experience negative emotions and may even lead to failure in the socialization process. In adolescence, with the rapid development of the individual’s body and mind, physical maturity and regular physical activity are essential for the successful completion of developmental tasks [8], and participation in sports or other recreational activities will enable the individual to complete some of the “developmental tasks” at the current stage and positively impact the development of the individual, which in turn will have a positive impact on the individual’s development. Whether it is participation in physical activity or other recreational activities, it allows the individual to fulfill some of the “developmental tasks” of the current stage, which positively impacts the individual’s development and thus positively adapts to the surrounding environment. As an active lifestyle, physical activity has been well documented by scholars to promote school adjustment and academic achievement [9]. Appropriate physical activity can help adolescents establish and strengthen communication with peers, reduce negative emotions, and improve school adjustment [10]. Some studies have also found that adolescents’ participation in physical activity leads to better integration into the group, an essential safeguard for adolescents to adapt to school life [11].

Developmental Task Theory emphasizes the importance of individual psychological development and takes a changing, developmental view of the wholeness and continuity of the stages of an individual’s psychological development. Thus, One of the concerns of this study regarding mediating mechanisms is the mediating association of psychological resilience. Psychological resilience emphasizes an individual’s positive internal strengths and qualities when he or she is in an adverse situation, and it is an essential psychological quality for individuals to cope with social pressure and a key indicator of their mental health level [12]. Some studies have found that the level of physical activity is an important variable affecting individual psychological resilience [13]. Physical activity contributes to the positive development of an individual’s psychological resilience and is significantly enhanced with increased physical activity [14]; regular physical activity not only strengthens the body, but spontaneous physical activity can also bring psychological resilience and well-being by improving mental and physical health, and among young people, psychological resilience is an essential mediator of the relationship between physical activity and mental health [15]. In addition, conservation of resources theory states that when an individual is resource-rich, he has more opportunities to acquire new resources through resource inputs and increase the stock of resources; the resources that an individual possesses are closely related to adaptation; the more resources he possesses, the better he adapts [16]. Individuals with high psychological resilience have abundant psychological resources and can mobilize and reasonably allocate psychological resources to cope with setbacks promptly in the face of pressure, showing good adaptation. The level of psychological toughness of individuals is closely related to the existence of adaptability; the higher the level of psychological toughness of individuals, the more effectively they can utilize various resources to cope with difficult situations and the more control they have over the external environment [17]. As a protective factor of individual adaptive development, psychological resilience will minimize physiological and psychological costs to cope with stress and adversity to achieve positive and healthy development of individuals [18]. It has been found that psychological resilience can negatively predict behavioral problems in school-age children [19]. Psychological resilience is positively related to school adjustment, with psychological resilience positively predicting school adjustment in elementary and middle school students [20, 21], suggesting that individuals with high psychological resilience can recover quickly from current adversity and flexibly adapt to various changes. In addition, it has been found that by increasing psychological resilience, students can increase their positive emotions and promote cognitive functioning, improving their school adjustment [22]. In summary, this study proposes hypothesis H2: psychological resilience mediates the relationship between physical activity and school adjustment among high school students.

One of the concerns of this study regarding mediating mechanisms is the mediating association of self-control. Self-control is often thought of as the ability to strive to suppress short-term, gratifying impulses to achieve longer-term, more worthwhile goals [23]. One study found that aerobic exercise may be an effective measure to enhance an individual’s self-control [24]. The strength model of self-control states that self-control resources work similarly to muscles and can be enhanced by appropriate exercise [25], just as muscle energy can be temporarily depleted and then recovered or made stronger after appropriate rest. Cognitive neuroscience research has similarly found that mild exercise induces cortical activation in the dorsolateral left prefrontal and frontal pole regions and that activation of these brain regions is associated with increased self-control [26]. In addition, the developmental perspective of positive psychology suggests that self-control in adolescents is one of the most critical indicators of an individual’s positive socialization [27] and that self-control is a critical element of positive adaptation, as well as a favorable predictor of an individual’s positive life outcomes, and is associated with essential aspects of adolescents’ daily lives and positive development. For example, self-control predicts the grade point average (GPA) of students in school, and individuals with solid self-control are also more likely to overcome difficulties in completing their education [28], have better interpersonal adjustment and life satisfaction, as well as protect their mental health and promote adaptive competence [29, 30].

Conversely, poor self-control increases the prevalence of symptoms of psychological disorders, leading to problems such as poor school performance, task performance failure, and school burnout [31]. Students with high levels of self-control tend to adapt better to the school environment because higher self-control implies a more remarkable ability to alter their responses to achieve long-term goals [25]. Therefore, self-control may be a potential mechanism to explain the relationship between physical activity and school adjustment. The present study proposes Hypothesis H3: Self-control mediates the relationship between physical activity and school adjustment among high school students.

According to the “acquisition spiral” viewpoint in resource conservation theory, when an individual’s resources are abundant, the individual will have more opportunities to obtain new resources through resource inputs, increasing his or her resource stock [16]. However, individuals’ general psychological resources are stored in a resource pool with limited capacity, and each time an individual exercises self-control, the resources in the resource pool will decrease [25]. It has been found that individuals with low psychological resilience are unable to recover well from stressful situations. Then, they will be under stress for an extended period, leading to continuous depletion of self-control resources and maladaptation [32]. Individuals with high psychological resilience can expand their cognitive scope and have richer positive psychological resources, which can be used to reduce the harm caused by adversity and promote individual health [33]. In other words, individuals with high psychological resilience can maintain a positive mindset in the face of problems and, at the same time, have more general resources for self-control, which is likely to be an essential manifestation of psychological resilience in mobilizing internal resources [34]. Therefore, psychological resilience can increase the capacity of an individual’s resource pool, slow down the depletion of an individual’s self-control resources, and increase an individual’s self-control ability, thus promoting an individual’s school adjustment.

Although a growing body of research suggests that physical activity predicts adolescents’ school adjustment, there is still a gap in research on the mechanisms of the two, and no study has examined psychological resilience and self-control in terms of both physical activity and school adjustment, or examined the two in the context of a group of high school students. Therefore, this study examines the relationship between physical activity and high school students’ school adjustment based on developmental task theory. Because developmental task theory emphasizes the importance of individual psychological development, in exploring the internal mechanisms of physical activity and school adjustment, we also combined conservation of resources theory and strength model of self-control to explore the mediating roles of psychological resilience and self-control, aiming to fill the gaps in current research on the predictors of Chinese high school students’ school adjustment and to expand the Developmental Task Theory from the perspective of the internal development of the individual psychology. The present study proposes the following hypotheses: (1) physical activity positively predicts high school students’ school adjustment; (2) psychological resilience and self-control mediate the relationship between physical activity and high school students’ school adjustment, respectively; and (3) psychological resilience and self-control chain-mediate the relationship between physical activity and high school students’ school adjustment.

Method

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from May to June 2022 in Jiangsu Province, located in the eastern region of China. Through the preliminary study and research, we learned that the total population of high school students in Jiangsu Province is about 1.43 million [35], and there are 664 public schools eligible for participation in this study. In order to ensure the representativeness and diversity of the sample, according to the regional division of Jiangsu Province [36], we selected high school students from four regions: northern Jiangsu, central Jiangsu, southern Jiangsu, and Nanjing city, with one city and one rural public high school in each region, for a total of eight high school schools as survey participants. According to the sample size formula: , where =1.96(confidence interval = 95%); d = 1 (variable score error > 1); (variable standard deviation up to 22.11 [6, 37]). Therefore, the required sample size is ==1878. Considering the possibility of 20% invalid questionnaires, a minimum of 2253 questionnaires need to be collected for this study. A total of 2,345 questionnaires were recovered, and to ensure that the data collected were rigorous and valid, after the questionnaires that were answered arbitrarily or incompletely were excluded, a total of 2,054 valid questionnaires were returned, with an effective recovery rate of 87.56%. The questionnaires were all from first and second year high school students (M=16.45, SD=0.72). The reason for the absence of senior students is that the questionnaires were not distributed to senior students because of the proximity of the university entrance exams to ensure that the exams would go smoothly for the senior students. The questionnaire was administered offline through a paper-and-pencil test, and uniform instruction was used to guide the subjects to fill in the scale according to their actual situation and collected on the spot. The inclusion criteria were willingness to cooperate in filling out the questionnaire, no recent major illness, and no mental disorder or cognitive impairment. The Ethics Committee of Yangzhou University School of Medicine approved the study protocol.

Outcome measures

International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-S).

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-S) was used to evaluate the physical activity level of high school students. This questionnaire was introduced by Li Keji and Qu Ningning [38]. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form consists of seven questions on walking, moderate physical activity, and heavy physical activity. Total energy values were calculated based on the metabolic equivalents (MET-min) corresponding to each type of physical activity, walking (3.3 MET-min/min), moderate physical activity such as carrying light loads, bicycling at a regular pace, or doubles tennis (4.0 MET-min/min), and heavy physical activity such as heavy lifting, digging, aerobics, or fast bicycling (8.0 MET-min/min).

The IPAQ-S questionnaire categorized participants into three groups [38]:

low physical activity (individuals who did not meet criteria for moderate or heavy categories);

low physical activity (individuals who did not meet criteria for moderate or heavy categories);

moderate physical activity (Engaging in at least 3 days of vigorous-intensity activity lasting at least 20 min per day, or at least 5 days of moderate-intensity activity and/or walking for at least 30 min per day. Alternatively, engaging in any combination of walking, moderate, or vigorous-intensity activities for 3–5 days or more, achieving a total physical activity (PA) of at least 600 MET-minutes per week.);

moderate physical activity (Engaging in at least 3 days of vigorous-intensity activity lasting at least 20 min per day, or at least 5 days of moderate-intensity activity and/or walking for at least 30 min per day. Alternatively, engaging in any combination of walking, moderate, or vigorous-intensity activities for 3–5 days or more, achieving a total physical activity (PA) of at least 600 MET-minutes per week.);

heavy physical activity (vigorous-intensity activities on at least 3 days, with a total PA of at least 1500 MET-minutes per week, or engaging in a combination of walking and moderate or vigorous-intensity activities on 7 days, with a total PA of at least 3000 MET-minutes per week.).

heavy physical activity (vigorous-intensity activities on at least 3 days, with a total PA of at least 1500 MET-minutes per week, or engaging in a combination of walking and moderate or vigorous-intensity activities on 7 days, with a total PA of at least 3000 MET-minutes per week.).

As part of the Comprehensive Health Assessment Questionnaire, which is used to assess the daily physical activity of the study population, it is a questionnaire currently widely used to measure physical activity in international and national contexts. Studies have shown that its reliability and validity are promising [39]. In the present study, the total MET values of the individuals in a week were calculated by calculating the total MET values of the individuals corresponding to the different types of physical activities in the questionnaire and summing them up for the next step of the study.

Adolescent psychological resilience scale

The Adolescent Psychological Resilience Scale (PRS) developed by Chinese scholars Hu Yueqin and Gan Yiqun was used [37]. The scale consists of 27 questions and is divided into five dimensions, i.e., goal focus, emotional control, positive cognition, family support, and interpersonal assistance. The scale adopts a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores representing a better level of psychological resilience. In this study the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale was 0.86 and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the subdimensions were 0.77, 0.77, 0.80, 0.88, and 0.85. The results of confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were: Chi-Square to Degrees of Freedom Ratio (𝑥2/df) = 5.86, Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 0.93, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) = 0.92, and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) = 0.048, indicating that the scale has good reliability and validity.

Self-control scale for high school students

The Self-Control Scale (SCS) revised by Tan Shuhua et al. in 2008 was used [40], which is divided into five dimensions, namely, healthy habits, abstaining from entertainment, resisting temptation, focusing on work, and impulse control, and consists of 19 questions, and is based on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating better self-control. Higher scores represent better self-control. In this study the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale was 0.76 and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the subdimensions were 0.82, 0.60, 0.76, 0.71, and 0.72. The results of the CFA were: 𝑥2/df = 4.09, CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96, and RMSEA = 0.04, indicating that the scale has good reliability and validity.

School adjustment scale for high school students

The School adjustment Scale for High School Students (SAS), developed by Hou Jing, a Chinese scholar, was used as a test subject for high school students in Beijing [6]. The scale consists of 82 items, including seven dimensions: learning adaptation, peer relationship, school attitude, collective adaptation, loneliness, teacher-student relationship conflict, and teacher-student relationship intimacy. The scale is based on a 5-point Likert scale, and the dimensions are summed to obtain a total school adjustment score, with a higher total score representing a better school adjustment. In this study the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale was 0.97 and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the subdimensions were 0.90, 0.91, 0.88, 0.93, 0.92 and 0.91. The results of CFA were: 𝑥2/df = 4.50, CFI = 0.89, TLI = 0.89, and RMSEA = 0.07, indicating that the scale has good reliability and validity.

Statistical analyses

The raw data came from offline paper and pencil quizzes. SPSS 26.0 software is used for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and percentages, were used to characterize the participants’ social demographics and the study variables. Pearson’s correlation analysis calculates the correlation between physical activity, Psychological Resilience, self-control, and school adjustment. We used model6 in Hayes’ PROCESS plug-in for SPSS (version 4.0) to explore the mediating role of psychological resilience and self-control in physical activity and school adjustment [41]. In addition, based on the analysis of demographic variables, gender and geography were found to be significantly associated with school adjustment, so gender (male = 1, female = 0) and geography (dummy coding) were included as control variables in the model. The bootstrap method (sampling repeated 5000 times) estimates 95% confidence intervals for significance testing of mediating associations. Direct or indirect associations are considered significant when the confidence interval does not include zero.

Since this study only used subjects’ self-reported methods to collect data information, the results may be affected by common method bias. To minimize the impact of common method bias, we implemented standardization procedures. These measures included setting the appropriate length of the questionnaire, ensuring the anonymity of respondents, and implementing reverse scoring for certain scale items. Harman’s single-factor test was employed to assess common method bias. Exploratory factor analysis was conducted in SPSS software on all items in the Adolescent Psychological Resilience Scale, the High School Student School Adjustment Scale, and the High School Student School Adjustment Scale. Based on the unrotated factor analysis results, 19 factors with a characteristic root > 1 were extracted, and the explained variance of the most significant factor was 22.996%, well below the critical criterion of 40%. Therefore, there was no significant common method bias in this study.

Discussion

Overview of findings

This study examined the relationship between physical activity and high school students’ school adjustment through the mediating roles of psychological resilience and self-control. The results of this study found that there is a significant positive correlation between physical activity and high school students’ school adjustment, and physical activity positively predicts high school students’ school adjustment, which validates hypothesis 1 and is consistent with the results of previous studies [43]. Improving and developing adolescents’ school adaptive ability is crucial to their future socialization growth and personality improvement, etc. Better school adaptive ability can help secondary school students establish good partnerships in school, promote adolescents’ personality development and value formation, and, at the same time, lay the foundation for lifelong development [44]. One study found that moderate to high-intensity physical activity can significantly reduce the depressive response related to school adjustment. Hence, adolescents who exercise more are less likely to have school adjustment problems [45]. Students with high levels of physical activity have a comprehensive understanding of their bodies, and their levels of self-esteem, sense of accomplishment, and other psychological indicators are higher, which positively contributes to school adjustment [46]. Physical activity, as an adaptive-related socio-cultural activity, may contribute to school adjustment in the following two ways: First of all, according to the Havighurst’s Developmental Task Theory, whether they participate in sports or other recreational activities, it will enable individuals to fulfill some of the “developmental tasks” at the current stage, and high school students need to interact with others in order to acquire motor skills or interpersonal collaboration when they are engaged in physical activities, in which high school students are more easily accepted by their peers and can communicate with each other, which will enhance the individual’s ability to communicate, and thus develop the individual’s processing and problem solving skills and techniques, which will lead to the enhancement of the level of adjustment to the school; Second, regular physical activity can enhance the cognitive function of the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, which can help to promote the individual’s academic level and learning efficiency [47],. and improve the individual’s learning adjustment at school, increasing the individual’s school adjustment ability.

These findings indicate that schools and families should recognize the rapid physical and mental development of adolescents, and should inspire students to participate in physical activity and encourage adolescents to engage in more physical activities. For example, schools can provide safe, high-quality and diversified sports facilities and venues, and ensure that students have enough time for physical activity every day, so that adolescents can enjoy the fun of physical activity and enhance their physical fitness, In this way, young people can have fun, strengthen their physical fitness, improve their personality and will, and enhance their ability to adapt to school in the process of physical activity.

This study found that psychological resilience mediates the relationship between physical activity and school adjustment in high school students, supporting Hypothesis 2. Its mediating association accounted for 45.45% of the total associations, indicating that mental flexibility is a very important mediating variable in the relationship between physical activity and high school students’ school adjustment. It has been suggested that every type of stress that is not excessive in intensity is viewed as having a potential to enhance adaptive success when it is not excessive [48], and that this occurs below the optimal level of stress; in other words, although repeated exposure to uncontrollable stress eventually creates resistance to stress, exposure to controllable stress will accomplish this more quickly [49]. Related research suggests and supports that physical activity may enhance a person’s level of psychological resilience by enhancing their response to stress [50]. This, in turn, is consistent with Salmon’s view that the particular value of physical activity may lie in the fact that it is a controllable stressor [51]. Combined with the theory of resource preservation model, then when physical activity is regarded as a controllable and moderate stressor, being under this kind of stress frequently will cause changes in adolescents’ physiological as well as psychological responses; at the same time, successful coping with stress is also an important way for individuals to obtain resource augmentation, and psychologically resourceful students are usually able to stick to their goals and focus on them, and then cope with the impacts of adversity, and adapt better to the effects of adversity. and adapt better to school life [52]. Individuals with high levels of psychological resilience have better coping styles, resilience, and other positive psychological resources, and even in unfavorable environments, they effectively cope with the negative impacts from stress and better adapt to the environment [53]. In addition, individuals with high levels of psychological resilience have a good and stable social support system [54], and when necessary, they can actively seek and obtain support and help from others to better cope with stressful events, thus improving the adaptive capacity of individuals.

The study results showed that self-control mediates the relationship between physical activity and school adjustment in high school students, validating Hypothesis2. The strength model of self-control explains how physical activity increases levels of self-control. The strength model of self-control states that self-control resources work similarly to muscles, and just as muscle capacity can be temporarily depleted and become more vital when it recovers after appropriate rest, the resources needed to perform self-control can be enhanced through long-term training [25]. Evidence from brain science suggests that acute aerobic exercise of moderate to low intensity increases cerebral blood flow by raising heart rate, which improves brain metabolism while also recruiting more neurons in the prefrontal lobe to participate in the self-control task [55, 56], which will facilitate the recovery of the excess resources for self-control, resulting in more tasks to continue the self-control task in the subsequent period, which will, in turn, enhance self-control. As a psychological function of changing one’s responses to bring thoughts, emotions, and behaviors in line with social norms and helping to achieve long-term goals, well-developed self-control leads to better interpersonal interactions. It enhances the flexibility of individuals in adapting to their environments [57]. It has been found that the higher an individual’s level of self-control, the higher their life satisfaction [58], and they will be better at managing their goals; through better goal-pursuit strategies, they will be able to effectively organize their lives [59], better resist external temptations, focus on current learning activities [60], abstain from recreational activities, and be able to better cope with stress, with a higher level of tolerance for frustration, and thus adapt better. In other words, individuals with higher self-control can inhibit impulses, shift their attention from negative to positive aspects, focus on learning through self-restraint as well as self-management, and control activities unrelated to their learning, as well as better control their emotions and reduce problematic behaviors, thus adapting to school life. Therefore, self-control is one of the most important psychological mechanisms for adapting to school and the environment.

Finally, the present study found that psychological resilience and self-control were chain mediators in the relationship between physical activity and school adjustment. Psychological resilience positively predicted self-control, which validates previous research [61], which suggests that students with high psychological resilience have greater self-control. This result supports the strength and resource conservation models of self-control. According to the strength model of self-control, self-control depends on limited internal resources, which are rapidly depleted when an individual exercises self-control, resulting in a state of “self-exhaustion” [25]. In the depleted state, it is difficult for individuals to control themselves, leading to self-control failure. However, high school students can increase their psychological resilience through physical activity, and the resource conservation model classifies resources into four categories, among which enduring intrinsic resources include psychological resilience and the acquisition of enduring intrinsic resources will have a long-term and more significant impact on the acquisition of resources in the future [62]. Then, the present study concluded that high school students increased their psychological resilience through physical activity. The increase in psychological resilience not only enables students to better adapt to school life but also enriches the individual’s psychological resources so that the individual will have more chances to acquire new psychological resources through resource inputs and to increase their stock so that the individual will have more psychological resources for self-control, and thus adapt to school life. Therefore, the higher the level of psychological resilience of high school students, the more psychological resources they can utilize internally, and the stronger the individual’s self-control ability.

This study analyzed in depth the relationship between physical activity and school adjustment and its internal mechanism of high school students by constructing the chain mediation model, based on the theory of development task, and combining the strength model of self-control and the conservation of resources theory, so as to provide references for promoting high school students’ physical activity, psychological resilience, self-control, and school adjustment ability. The various influences on school adjustment do not work independently of each other, but are interconnected. This has some implications for the promotion of adolescents’ school adjustment: High school students’ school adjustment can be improved by increasing physical activity, mental flexibility, and self-control; Rather than focusing solely on physical activity, mental resilience, and self-control, we should emphasize the combined effects of these factors, and pay as much attention as possible to all of the different factors that affect an individual’s adjustment to school, in order to promote better adjustment to school life for high school students.

Limitations

Although this study explored the underlying mechanisms of the relationship between physical activity and school adjustment among high school students and achieved the positive findings described above, the current study has limitations. First, the present study was cross-sectional and explored only predictive results based on relevant research strategies, making it difficult to draw strict causal inferences that could be further clarified in the future through follow-up or experimental studies. Second, this study measured physical activity through the use of a self-report questionnaire, IPAQ, which is a subjective measurement of physical activity; therefore, future research needs to improve the research paradigm and research methods by combining observation, measurement, experiment, and tracking with advanced scientific and technological means, and further research should consider the use of more objective measurement devices (e.g., accelerometers, pedometers) to measure physical activity amount, to deeply explore the mechanism of influencing high school students’ mental activity. Finally, this study only analyzed the mediating roles of total physical activity Meto values, mental flexibility, self-control, and total school adjustment scores, and the correlations among the sub-dimensions of each variable need to be further investigated.

Conclusion

Our study is the first to include psychological resilience and self-control in a study of the relationship between physical activity and school adjustment, and the first to examine both in a population of high school students. The results of the study found that physical activity, psychological resilience, and self-control significantly predicted high school students’ school adjustment. Furthermore, psychological resilience and self-control acted as chain mediators between physical activity and high school students’ school adjustment. Despite the limitations, our study further expands the relationship between physical activity and high school students‘ school adjustment and its pathways, and enriches the results of developmental task theory by providing theoretical basis and reference value for promoting high school students’ school adjustment using developmental task theory as the main framework. In addition, we highlighted the crucial role of psychological resilience and self-control in the relationship between physical activity and school adjustment through the conservation of resources theory and strength model of self-control.
