Exploring the roles of Polygonati Rhizoma in delaying skin aging using network pharmacology and molecular docking
Authors: Xilong Wang, Qinzhen Lei, Fenqiang Cai, Xiaoyan Luo, Xinyuan Duo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolygonati Rhizoma has been reported to exhibit the ability to retard skin aging. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying remain largely elusive. In this study, we screened 9 active compounds in Polygonati Rhizoma using the TCMSP and SwissADME databases. Subsequently, 285 potential targets were identified via Swiss Target Prediction Database. Concurrently, 800 genes related to skin aging were retrieved from GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD databases. By intersecting these datasets with the potential targets of Polygonati Rhizoma, we pinpointed 17 overlapping genes. These genes were further subjected to GO function annotation and KEGG pathway analysis using DAVID database. A compound-target-pathway network was then constructed using Cytoscape software, highlighting two compounds (4’,5-dihydroxyflavone and baicalein) and six targets (MAPK1, MAPK10, MMP9, PTGS2, PDGFRB, and CYP1B1). Molecular docking revealed that the binding energy between 4’,5-dihydroxyflavone and baicalein with the six targets was less than − 5 kcal/mol, particularly for MMP9, PTGS2, and CYP1B1, indicating a stable interaction. The molecular dynamic simulations demonstrate that both ligands form stable complexes with MMP9 and PTGS2. Finally, the antioxidant capacity of 4’5-dihydroxyflavone and baicalein was evaluated in vitro, confirming significant antioxidant activity. Collectively, our findings provide a systematic foundation for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-aging effects of Polygonati Rhizoma and offer valuable insights into the development of anti-skin aging cosmetics.Keywords: Polygonati Rhizoma; 4',5-dihydroxyflavone; Baicalein; Anti-skin agingSubject terms: Biochemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

Polygonati Rhizoma, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb recognized for its dual role as both a food and medicine, has been employed for over two thousand years. Its earliest recorded mention appears in the classical text “Ming Yi Bie Lu”. This herb is acclaimed for a wide range of pharmacological effects, including the replenishment of Qi, nourishment of Yin, pulmonary moistening, spleen strengthening, kidney tonification, and enhancement of immune function1,2. Recent pharmacological investigations have further elucidated its efficacy in delaying the aging process, augmenting immune responses, mitigating fatigue, reducing blood glucose levels, exerting antitumor activities, protecting cardiovascular health, improving memory, and potentially offering therapeutic benefits against COVID-193–5. These therapeutic properties are largely attributed to its diverse bioactive constituents, such as alkaloids, flavonoids, steroidal saponins, lignans, amino acids, and polysaccharides6,7. Among these, polysaccharides are identified as particularly significant, exhibiting a broad spectrum of biological activities including antioxidant, anti-aging, anti-fatigue, immunomodulatory, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, lipid-lowering, anti-atherosclerotic, anti-osteoporotic, hepatoprotective, antidiabetic, anticancer effects, as well as potential preventive actions against Alzheimer’s disease6,8,9. Similarly, the flavonoid components of Polygonati Rhizoma demonstrate multiple beneficial effects, encompassing anticancer, antioxidant, anti-atherosclerotic, antibacterial, hypoglycemic, and antihyperlipidemic activities10–14. Consequently, Polygonati Rhizoma presents substantial potential for development in the domains of functional foods and pharmaceutical applications as a natural phytotherapeutic agent.

Human aging constitutes a complex and dynamic physiological phenomenon characterized by a gradual decline in functional capacity and an increased susceptibility to various diseases, impacting all bodily tissues and organs15. The skin, as the largest organ of the human body, functions as a vital barrier that segregates internal structures from the external environment, thereby preventing water loss and microbial infiltration16. Given its position as the outermost organ, the skin is continuously subjected to both intrinsic and extrinsic factors, which manifest as visible signs of aging, including wrinkles, loss of elasticity, pigmentation changes, and surface roughness17. Beyond its protective role, the skin holds significant cosmetic importance, a youthful and aesthetically pleasing appearance positively influences social interactions and reproductive success. This has motivated substantial investment in cosmetic and pharmaceutical products aimed at delaying or reversing cutaneous aging16,18. The global anti-aging market is reported to exceed 60 billion USD19. Traditionally, middle-aged and elderly populations constituted the primary consumers of anti-aging products, however, recent trends indicate an increasing interest among younger demographics in preventive strategies20.

In recent years, advancements in science and technology have significantly enhanced quality of life, leading to increased emphasis on health and wellness. Simultaneously, growing concerns about the toxicity and adverse effects associated with synthetic pharmaceuticals have driven a shift toward natural and safer alternatives. Traditional Chinese medicinal herbs have garnered substantial interest due to their proven efficacy, minimal side effects, and low allergenic potential. There is an increasing consumer demand for cosmetic products composed solely of natural ingredients. Polygonati Rhizoma, with its long history of use, has been demonstrated in previous studies to possess antioxidant, antimicrobial, skin-whitening, and moisturizing properties, highlighting its potential application in natural cosmetic formulations21–23. Prior research has shown that Polygonati Rhizoma enhances antioxidant stress resistance in Caenorhabditis elegans by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and augmenting antioxidant enzyme activity, thereby prolonging the organism’s lifespan24. Moreover, Polygonati Rhizoma exhibits notable protective effects against age-related damage across various organs, including the brain, liver, kidney, intestine, heart, and blood vessels, contributing to longevity and attenuating the aging process25. Nevertheless, the effects and underlying mechanisms of Polygonati Rhizoma concerning skin aging remain insufficiently elucidated. In the present study, we employed network pharmacology and molecular docking methodologies to investigate the potential anti-skin aging properties of Polygonati Rhizoma.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of active ingredients and potential targets of Polygonati Rhizoma

The chemical constituents of Polygonati Rhizoma were retrieved from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) database (https://www.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php), filtered based on oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug likeness (DL) ≥ 0.126,27. The canonical simplified molecular-input line-entry system (SMILES) representations of these compounds were subsequently obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) or the NovoPro webpage tool (https://www.novopro.cn/tools/mol2smiles.html). These SMILES were then analyzed using SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/) for pharmacokinetic evaluation, with active ingredients being selected based on gastrointestinal absorption marked as “yes” and at least two “yes” responses in the drug likeness subcategories28. Finally, the SMILES were input into the Swiss Target Prediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) to identify potential targets with a probability greater than 0.129.

Identification of skin aging-related targets

Three databases, namely GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM (https://www.omim.org/), and TTD (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/), were utilized to search potential targets associated with skin aging. The search term “skin aging” was employed across all three databases to compile a comprehensive list of targets. Subsequently, the resulting datasets were integrated, duplicates were removed, and the final consolidated subset was validated as comprising skin aging-related targets.

Intersection of targets for skin aging and Polygonati Rhizoma

The targets associated with skin aging were integrated with those of Polygonati Rhizoma. Subsequently, the Venny 2.1 platform (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html) was utilized to map and identify overlapping targets. These intersecting targets will serve as the foundation for subsequent analyses.

Enrichment analysis of gene ontology (GO) and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathways

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-skin aging effects of Polygonati Rhizoma, we conducted enrichment analysis on the GO functions and KEGG pathways associated with the common targets identified for delaying skin aging30–32. This analysis was performed using the DAVID bioinformatics tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). GO terms and KEGG pathways with a p value ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For further interpretation, the top 10 GO terms in each of the cellular component (CC), molecular function (MF), and biological process (BP) categories, as well as the top 20 KEGG pathways, were visualized using the Wei Shen Xin mapping platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/)33.

Construction and analysis of network

The compound-target-pathway (C-T-P) network was constructed utilizing Cytoscape software (version 3.10.3https://cytoscape.org/download.html ), with all parameters set to their default values. Within this network, nodes represent compounds, targets, and pathways, while edges denote the interactions between these nodes. Topological properties, including degree centrality (DC), betweenness centrality (BC), and closeness centrality (CC), were comprehensively evaluated using the Centiscape 2.2 plugin to identify key components and critical targets.

Molecular docking

The two-dimensional structures of the ligand molecules in SDF format were obtained from the PubChem database. Subsequently, Chem3D software (version 15.0) was employed to minimize the energy and convert the files into PDB format. Concerning the protein receptor, its accession number (Reviewed, Human) was obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Subsequently, its three-dimensional structure was searched for in the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The structure was then download under the criteria of being derived from Homo sapiens and having a resolution higher than 2.0 Å. Finally, PyMOL software (version 3.1.3.1, https://pymol.org/ ) was utilized to eliminate extraneous ligands and water molecules prior to conducting the docking experiment. Molecular docking simulations were conducted utilizing AutoDockTools software (version 1.5.7, https://ccsb.scripps.edu/mgltools/downloads/ )34. During the docking calculations, Gasteiger charges and hydrogen atoms were appended to the proteins by means of the automated docking tool. The auxiliary program Autogrid was employed to configure the docking boxes, which were delineated according to the crystal structures of protein complexes with known ligands. The Lamarckian genetic algorithm (LGA) was implemented for each docking process. The binding affinities between the active compounds and target proteins were evaluated, and the conformations of the ligand-receptor complexes exhibiting the lowest binding energies were visualized using PyMOL software.

Molecular dynamic simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS 2022. The receptor protein was described with the AMBER14SB force field, while the ligand was parameterized with GAFF2 using sobtop 1.0 (dev3.1), with RESP-derived atomic charges. The system was solvated in a cubic box of TIP3P water with a 1 nm edge length and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl. Energy minimization was carried out in stages, applying positional restraints first to the protein and then to ions, followed by an unrestrained minimization using steepest descent (3000 steps) and conjugate gradient (2000 steps) algorithms. Subsequently, a 100 ns NPT production simulation was conducted with a 2 fs time step. Temperature (310 K) and pressure (1 bar) were maintained using the Nosé–Hoover thermostat and Parrinello–Rahman barostat, respectively. Short-range non-bonded interactions were truncated at 1.0 nm, and long-range electrostatics were treated with Particle Mesh Ewald (PME). All bonds were constrained with the LINCS algorithm. Trajectory analyses included: Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and fluctuation (RMSF); Hydrogen bond occupancy; Radius of gyration (Rg); Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA); The binding free energy was calculated using the MM-PBSA method via the g_mmpbsa package.

Evaluation of the free radical scavenging capacity of 4’,5-dihydroxyflavone and baicalein

In order to evaluate the antioxidant capabilities of the two active components in Polygonati Rhizoma. 4’,5-Dihydroxyflavone and baicalein were purchased from Shanghai Yuan Ye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Their antioxidant activities were determined through DPPH and hydroxyl radical scavenging experiments according to the established methods with minor modifications35. Specifically, sample solutions with diverse concentrations were initially prepared. Regarding 4’,5-dihydroxyflavone, it was diluted with Dimethyl sulfoxide to concentrations of 0, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 µg/L. Similarly, baicalein was diluted with Dimethyl sulfoxide, and the concentrations were set at the same as the 4’,5-dihydroxyflavone solution. In the DPPH radical scavenging assay, each of these sample solutions was mixed with 2.0 mL of a 0.1 mM ethanol DPPH solution. Dimethyl sulfoxide was utilized as the blank control for 4’,5-dihydroxyflavone, while absolute ethanol served as the blank control for baicalein. Moreover, the DPPH solution itself was employed as a background control. After 20 min of incubation in the dark, the absorbance (A value) was measured at a wavelength of 517 nm. In the hydroxyl radical scavenging assay, 1.0 mL of a 6 mM ferrous sulfate solution and 1.0 mL of a 6 mM salicylate alcohol solution were carefully and thoroughly mixed in a 10 mL cuvette. Subsequently, 1.0 mL of a 6 mM hydrogen peroxide solution and 1.0 mL of the sample solutions were added. Meanwhile, dimethyl sulfoxide was used as the blank control. Deionized water was used instead of the hydrogen peroxide solution to serve as the background control. The absorbance (A) was measured at 510 nm in a constant-temperature water bath maintained at 37 °C for 30 min. In both assays, ascorbic acid at the same concentration was used as a positive control, and the clearance rate was calculated as follows.

Clearance (%) = (1 – (A sample – A back))/A empty

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluations were conducted using Graph Pad Prism® version 8.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). The data were expressed as mean values with standard deviation (SD). To compare multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. p < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Thirty-eight chemical constituents of Polygonati Rhizoma were retrieved from the TCMSP database. Following the application of screening criteria (OB ≥ 30% and DL ≥ 0.18), twelve active components of Polygonati Rhizoma were identified. These compounds were further evaluated using pharmacokinetic parameters via the SwissADME web tool. Ultimately, nine compounds were confirmed as potential active ingredients. Common targets of Polygonati Rhizoma and skin aging

A thorough screening of the Swiss Target Prediction database revealed 285 possible targets linked to Polygonati Rhizoma. At the same time, by searching for “skin aging,” we obtained 982 relevant targets from the Genecard database, 131 from the OMIM database, and 2 from the TDD database. These targets were filtered to include only those with a relevance score above 1, then combined and duplicates removed, resulting in a total of 800 targets associated with skin aging. By comparing the targets related to Polygonati Rhizoma with those connected to skin aging, we found 17 common targets (Fig. 2), which were identified as potential candidates for further study.

Venn diagram of Polygonati Rhizoma targets and skin aging targets.

GO enrichment analysis

Through GO enrichment analysis of 17 target genes common to Polygonati Rhizoma and skin aging, a total of 94 GO terms were identified, comprising 56 biological processes (BP), 15 cellular components (CC), and 23 molecular functions (MF). Based on the p values and the number of enriched genes, the top 10 enriched BPs, CCs, and MFs were selected for visualization, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The enriched BPs primarily encompassed signal transduction, positive and negative regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, among others. The CCs were predominantly associated with the nucleus, cytosol, and cytoplasm, while the MFs were largely related to protein binding, zinc ion binding, and metal ion binding.

GO enrichment analysis of Polygonati Rhizoma targets in anti-skin aging.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

KEGG pathway analysis of the intersecting genes identified a total of 37 enriched pathways. Based on p values and the number of enriched genes, the top 20 pathways were categorized into four major groups: Environmental Information Processing, Cellular Processes, Organismal Systems, and Human Diseases (Fig. 4). Key biological pathways included those related to cancer, TNF signaling, IL-17 signaling, lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis, as well as MicroRNAs in cancer.

KEGG pathways enrichment analysis for anti-skin aging of Polygonati Rhizoma.

Construction and analysis of C-T-P network

The C-T-P network was constructed using Cytoscape_v3.10.3 software, consisting of 52 nodes and 133 edges (Fig. 5). The Centiscape2.2 plugin was employed to analyze the network based on degree centrality (DC), betweenness centrality (BC), and closeness centrality (CC). Nodes with values less than the predefined threshold were filtered out, leading to two active components and six target proteins within the network (Table 2). The identified active components were MOL006331 (4’,5-Dihydroxyflavone) and MOL002714 (baicalein). The six target proteins were MAPK1, MAPK10, MMP9, PTGS2, PDGFRB, and CYP1B1.

Molecular docking results

Two key components (4’,5-dihydroxyflavone and baicalein) and six target proteins (MAPK1, MAPK10, MMP9, PTGS2, PDGFRB, and CYP1B1) were selected for molecular docking analysis. The results demonstrated that the binding energy between each receptor protein and the key components was negative (as shown in Table 3), indicating favorable interactions between the receptors and ligands. Among these receptors, MMP9, PTGS2, and CYP1B1 exhibited notably lower binding energy values with the ligands. The interactions between these three receptors and their respective ligands are presented in Fig. 6. The findings reveal that hydrogen bonds were formed between all three proteins and their ligands. Specifically, MMP9 formed four and six hydrogen bonds with its ligand, PTGS2 formed one and nine hydrogen bonds, and CYP1B1 formed five and two hydrogen bonds, respectively.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Guided by molecular docking results, the two target proteins (MMP9 and PTGS2) exhibiting the lowest binding energies with the ligands baicalein and 4’,5-dihydroxyflavone were selected for molecular dynamics simulations to assess the stability and interactions of the resulting complexes. The conformational stability of each complex was assessed by monitoring the RMSD. As shown in Fig. 7a, the MMP9–baicalein and MMP9–4’,5-dihydroxyflavone complexes reached equilibrium within 10 ns, with final RMSD values fluctuating around 1.2 Å and 1.6 Å, respectively. The PTGS2–baicalein complex stabilized after 20 ns (~ 2.2 Å), while the PTGS2–4’,5-dihydroxyflavone complex required 50 ns to equilibrate, maintaining a higher RMSD near 4 Å.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis of the protein-ligand complexes. (a) RMSD. (b) Rg. (c) SASA. (d) HBonds. (e) RMSF. (f) FEL.

The overall structural compactness, evaluated by the Rg, remained stable for all complexes (Fig. 7b), indicating no major structural expansion or contraction. Consistent with this, the SASA showed minimal variation upon ligand binding (Fig. 7c), suggesting the ligands did not induce significant structural perturbation.

Analysis of intermolecular interactions revealed persistent hydrogen bonding between each ligand and its target (Fig. 7d). The average number of hydrogen bonds ranged from approximately 1 to 2 across the complexes. Furthermore, RMSF analysis indicated low residual flexibility for most amino acids in all systems (Fig. 7e), with values predominantly below 3–7 Å, confirming high structural rigidity.

The FEL for each complex, constructed using RMSD and Rg as reaction coordinates, identified deep, well-defined low-energy basins (Fig. 7f), corresponding to stable conformational states.

Finally, the binding affinities were quantified using the MM/PBSA method (Fig. 8a). The calculated binding free energies were all favorable: − 17.1 kcal/mol for MMP9–baicalein, − 11.92 kcal/mol for MMP9–4’,5-dihydroxyflavone, − 10.95 kcal/mol for PTGS2–baicalein, and − 20.87 kcal/mol for PTGS2–4’,5-dihydroxyflavone. Energy decomposition analysis identified key residues contributing significantly to the binding in each complex (Figs. 8b–e), highlighting potential hotspots for interaction.

MMPBSA binding free energy calculation. (a) The binding free energies of the protein-ligand complexes. (b–e) Key residue contribution analysis of protein-ligand complexes.

In summary, the simulations demonstrate that both flavonoids form stable complexes with MMP9 and PTGS2, characterized by sustained structural integrity, specific hydrogen-bond networks, and favorable binding energetics.

Experimental verification

Given the fundamental roles of free radicals in the aging process, we evaluated the antioxidant capacity of the two key active components in Polygonati Rhizoma. As shown in Fig. 9, the findings indicated that both active components exhibited promising abilities to scavenge DPPH and hydroxyl radicals. Specifically, the IC₅₀ values of 4’5-dihydroxyflavone and baicalein for DPPH radical scavenging were 4.90 ± 0.54 µg/L and 10.37 ± 0.70 µg/L, respectively, as compared to vitamin C’s IC₅₀ of 13.64 ± 0.95 µg/L. Likewise, their IC₅₀ values for hydroxyl radical scavenging were 28.68 ± 2.52 µg/L and 40.45 ± 4.83 µg/L, respectively, while vitamin C showed an IC₅₀ of 129.45 ± 6.19 µg/L. This indicates that Polygonati Rhizoma possess potent antioxidant properties and may serve as a promising candidate for skin anti-aging agents.

Discussion

In recent years, phytomedicines have attracted considerable attention in pharmaceuticals and functional foods due to their natural origin and minimal side effects. As a traditional herbal plant, using of Polygonati Rhizoma in cosmetic is an attractive strategy to against skin aging. It has been reported that Polygonati Rhizoma was effective in moisturizing and whitening the skin36. These effects were primarily attributed to its active ingredients, such as polysaccharides and flavonoids. Specifically, Polygonati Rhizoma polysaccharides contain a large number of alcoholic hydroxyl groups that act as hydrogen donors, neutralizing reactive hydroxyl radicals in the body and thereby exerting an antioxidant effect37. Additionally, the polysaccharides possess numerous hydrophilic groups capable of forming hydrogen bonds with water molecules, creating a hydration film on the skin surface and thus enhancing skin moisturization23. Furthermore, Polygonati Rhizoma polysaccharides can inhibit tyrosinase activity, reducing melanin production and contributing to skin whitening38. Besides polysaccharides, flavonoids also exhibit various anti-skin aging properties, including antioxidation, promotion of cell renewal, protection against UV damage, moisturization, and reduction of pigmentation39,40.

To date, over 30 theories have been proposed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying skin aging41. Among these, reactive oxygen species (ROS) were recognized as playing a pivotal role by inducing the expression of metalloproteinases, which can destroy the integrity of the extracellular matrix and thereby contribute to wrinkle formation42. Furthermore, ROS also capable of triggering inflammatory responses43. Notably, our study has identified three key targets (MMP9, PTGS2, and CYP1B1) for anti-skin aging treatment using Polygonati Rhizoma. MMP9 is a metalloproteinase implicated in the degradation of the extracellular matrix, leading to the development of skin wrinkles44. PTGS2, a cyclooxygenase enzyme, plays a crucial role in inflammation-associated pathological processes45. CYP1B1, a member of the cytochrome P450 superfamily with monooxygenase activity, is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production, both of which are critical factors contributing to skin aging46. Collectively, our findings may offer promising therapeutic targets for combating skin aging.

Two small flavonoid compounds (4’,5-dihydroxyflavone and baicalein) from Polygonati Rhizoma were screened for their potential to delay skin aging. Molecular docking analysis revealed that the binding energy of these compounds to skin aging targets were lower than − 5 kcal/mol, indicating strong binding affinities47. Meanwhile, 4’,5-dihydroxyflavone has been characterized as a collagenase inhibitor, thereby exhibiting anti-degradative properties48. Baicalein suppresses the expression of matrix metalloproteinase-1 and displays robust antioxidant effects against H2O2-induced oxidative stress in HaCaT keratinocytes49,50. The validation experiments demonstrated that both 4’,5-dihydroxyflavone and baicalein possess powerful antioxidant effects. In summary, these compounds hold significant promise as essential ingredients in anti-aging skincare formulations.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that 4’,5-dihydroxyflavone and baicalein in Polygonati Rhizoma can target MMP9, PTGS2, and CYP1B1, which are key proteins associated with skin aging, thereby contributing to the delay of skin aging. This research not only provide a foundation for a fully understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-aging effects of Polygonati Rhizoma but also offers valuable insights into the development of novel anti-aging cosmetics.
