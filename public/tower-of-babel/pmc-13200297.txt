Peripheral CD8⁺ T cell subsets and physical frailty in community-dwelling older Thai adults: the role of age and multimorbidity
Authors: Jarupa Soongsathitanon, Ticha Homjan, Prasert Assantachai, Weerasak Muangpaisan, Chanachai Sae-Lee, Kobporn Boonnak, Tararaj Dharakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAge-related immune decline is basically recognized as a key contributor to frailty. However, associations between immune markers, including T cell subsets, and frailty remain inconclusive and data from Southeast Asia are limited. This study investigated CD8⁺ T cell phenotypes and their associations with frailty among community-dwelling older Thai adults, aiming to identify blood-based biomarkers indicative of immune decline related to frailty.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 189 adults aged ≥ 60 years were classified as robust, pre-frail, or frail using Fried’s criteria. CD8⁺ T cell subsets—naïve (TN), central memory, effector memory, and terminally differentiated effector memory RA⁺ (TEMRA)—and expression of CD28 and PD-1 were analyzed by flow cytometry. Multivariable ordinal logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of pre-frailty and frailty, with sex-stratified analyses.ResultsReduced TN and increased TEMRA CD8⁺ T cells were clearly shown in our study population of older Thai adults. Frail participants exhibited significantly lower TN and higher TEMRA proportions than robust individuals. TN depletion predominated in frail females, whereas TEMRA expansion was more pronounced in frail males. In addition, CD28⁻ and PD-1⁺ phenotypes within the TN gate were increased in frail adults. In the adjusted analyses, the percentage of CD28⁻ TN (OR = 1.649, p = 0.003), age (OR = 1.222, p < 0.001), and number of comorbidities (OR = 1.334, p = 0.003) independently predicted frailty severity. Sex-stratified analyses showed that age and comorbidity burden were consistently associated with frailty, while the association with immune markers was attenuated.ConclusionAlterations in CD8⁺ T cell phenotypes, particularly naïve T cell depletion and increased CD28⁻ cells within the TN subset, were associated with frailty among community-dwelling older Thai adults. Female and male participants exhibited different CD8⁺ T cell subset distributions; however, these sex-specific patterns were not independently associated with frailty. These findings suggest that the immune alterations observed in frailty may partly reflect age-related immune changes and underlying health conditions, while chronological age and multimorbidity remain important factors associated with frailty in this population.Trial registration numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07456-0.Keywords: CD8 T lymphocyte, CD28 loss, Frailty, Immunosenescence, Naïve T cell, TEMRA

Introduction

Frailty is a common geriatric syndrome characterized by increased vulnerability to external stressors due to reduced physiological reserves, resulting in greater risk of disability, hospitalization, and mortality. A Systematic review and meta-analysis study in 2018, estimated pooled prevalence of 17.4% frailty and 49.3% prefrailty in community-dwelling older adults in low-income and middle-income countries [1]. In Thailand, data from the Fourth Thai National Health Examination Survey conducted between 2008 and 2009, which assessed frailty using the deficit-accumulation Frailty Index, revealed a frailty prevalence of 22.1% among individuals aged 60 years and older, with a higher prevalence in women (26.8%) than in men (17.2%) [2].

Age-related immune decline, or immunosenescence, is hypothesized to play a central role in the development of frailty [3, 4]. This process is characterized by diminished immune function and chronic low-grade inflammation, known as “inflamm-aging” [5, 6]. Among immune cells, T lymphocytes are particularly important mediators of these changes [7]. Previous studies have reported associations between alterations in T cell subsets and frailty; however, findings are inconsistent across populations and the frailty definitions, such as the frailty index [8] or the Fried frailty phenotype [9, 10].

CD8⁺ T cells, which play a critical role in antiviral and cytotoxic immunity, undergo distinct stages of differentiation throughout life. These subsets include naïve (TN), central memory (TCM), effector memory (TEM), and terminally differentiated effector memory re-expressing CD45RA (TEMRA) T cells. Aging is associated with a decline in TN cells and an expansion of TEM and TEMRA subsets [7, 11]. Loss of CD28 expression, together with the acquisition of CD57, is a hallmark of terminal differentiation [12–15]. CD8⁺CD28⁻ T cells exhibit shortened telomeres, limited proliferative capacity, and increased pro-inflammatory phenotypes [14, 16], while CD57 expression serves as a marker of replicative senescence [17]. Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) is among the inhibitory receptors that define T cell exhaustion, a condition resulting from chronic antigenic stimulation and progressive functional decline [18, 19]. PD-1 upregulation has been reported in aged mice [20], and age-associated increases in PD-1 expression on T cells have also been documented in human studies [21]. Despite these biologically relevant phenotypes, only a single study from Singapore has reported an association between memory CD8⁺CD28⁻CD27⁺ T cells and physical frailty in older adults [22], and no studies to date have examined the association between PD-1 expression and frailty.

Our study goal is to identify blood-based biomarkers that predict the risk of immune decline in Thai older adults with frailty. The present study focuses on physical frailty as defined by the Fried phenotype, emphasizing functional components such as weakness, slowness, and low physical activity. Regarding T cells, we examined CD8⁺ T cells because age-related changes are more pronounced and consistently reported than those of CD4⁺ T cells [23–25]. As an exploratory analysis, we evaluated CD28⁻ and PD-1⁺ expression across CD8⁺ T cell subsets as markers associated with age-related changes in T cell function. Additionally, we used multinomial logistic regression to assess the influence of age, medical conditions and immune markers to clarify the factors with independent association with physical frailty in this older adult population.

Methods

Study participants

Older adults aged 60 years or older were recruited between January and April 2023 from residents of 16 communities in the Bangkok Noi district of Bangkok, Thailand. Eligible individuals were invited to participate through community advertisements coordinated with local community leaders. Participants with any condition causing disability that impair walking ability or handgrip function, as well as those with major psychiatric illness or cognitive difficulties severe enough to preclude participation in the interview or study procedures, were excluded. To ensure adequate representation, recruitment aimed to enroll approximately equal numbers of men and women within each frailty category, with a target of 60 participants per group. All eligible individuals were initially enrolled consecutively. During the later stages of recruitment in some communities, enrollment was restricted to eligible individuals of the underrepresented sex to achieve the predefined sex distribution within each group. This strategy was applied only after most eligible participants had been enrolled and was intended to ensure balanced representation for sex-stratified analyses. The participant flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Flow diagram of participant recruitment

All participants underwent interviews conducted in Thai using Thai-translated versions of standardized questionnaires, including the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression (CES-D) scale [26, 27], the WHO Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [28], and the widely used Thai mental state examination (TMSE) [29]. Physical evaluations and blood sample collection were conducted by trained personnel. Medical histories were self-reported and included physician-diagnosed chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory disease, stroke, cancer, chronic kidney or liver disease, arthritis, and other specified diagnoses. Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of two or more chronic medical conditions. Participant demographics are summarized in Table 1.

Frailty measurements

Frailty status was determined using the Fried frailty phenotype [30], which classifies individuals as robust, pre-frail, or frail based on the presence of five criteria: weakness, slowness, low physical activity, exhaustion, and unintentional weight loss. Weakness and slowness were defined using the cutoffs recommended by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia, which have been validated for Asian populations [31]. The criteria were as follows: (1) weakness (handgrip strength female < 18 kg, male < 28 kg), (2) slowness (walking speed < 1.0 m/s), (3) low physical activity (GPAQ female < 368 METs min/week, male < 600 METs min/week) [32], (4) self-reported exhaustion (CES-D scale score 3–4), (5) unintentional weight loss (> 5% in 1 year). Participants were categorized as robust (n = 54), pre-frail (n = 80), or frail (n = 55), with a distribution of sexes across groups (26 males/28 females in robust group, 35 males/45 females in pre-frail, and 18 males/37 females in frail group). Of note, the cutoff for slowness was not adjusted for sex and height, as described [30]. In addition, the Thai Mental State Examination (TMSE) was used to assess cognitive function, with a score below 23/30 indicating mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Participants who were classified as frail and had MCI were identified as having cognitive frailty.

Sample preparation and flow cytometry staining

Peripheral venous blood was collected into EDTA tubes (Greiner Bio-One, Chonburi, Thailand). Complete blood counts, including absolute and relative lymphocyte counts, were obtained using standard hematology analyzers. For flow cytometry, 100 µL of whole blood was incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) specific for CD45 (APC-Cy7), CD8 (FITC), CD45RA (PE-Cy7), CCR7 (APC), CD28 (PerCP-Cy5.5), and PD-1 (PE). After 30 min of incubation at 4 °C, red blood cells were lysed with BD FACS™ Lysing Solution. Samples were acquired on a BD FACSLyric™ flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.0). Fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls were included to establish gating boundaries for low-intensity or dim populations.

CD8⁺ T cell subsets were defined based on CCR7 and CD45RA expression as follows: naïve (TN, CCR7⁺CD45RA⁺), central memory (TCM, CCR7⁺CD45RA⁻), effector memory (TEM, CCR7⁻CD45RA⁻), and terminally differentiated effector memory (TEMRA, CCR7⁻CD45RA⁺). CD28⁻ and PD-1⁺ expression within each subset was quantified using FMO-guided gates to characterize features associated with late differentiation and activation/exhaustion-related states (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) serology assay

CMV-specific IgG titers were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using the SERION ELISA classic CMV IgG kit (Virion\Serion). Values greater than 40 PEI-U/ml were classified as seropositive, values between 25 and 40 PEI-U/ml as borderline, and values below 25 PEI-U/ml as seronegative.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on distribution. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous data. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical comparisons.

To address potential multicollinearity and reduce model overfitting due to the large number of immune markers relative to the sample size, a penalized regression approach was used as a variable screening step. Elastic net was applied with frailty status as the dependent variable and all measured peripheral immune markers entered simultaneously as candidate predictors. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined using cross-validation. Immune markers with non-zero coefficients in the final elastic net model were considered to have the strongest associations with frailty and were selected for further evaluation. Markers identified through elastic net were subsequently examined in multivariable ordinal logistic regression models, with frailty status (robust, prefrail, frail) treated as an ordered outcome. These models were adjusted for predefined confounders selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature, including age, sex, smoking status, obesity, cognitive impairment, and comorbid conditions (hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia). Other comorbid conditions with a prevalence of less than 5% (Table 1) were excluded due to sparse data. Medications corresponding to underlying disease conditions were not included separately in the analyses to avoid collinearity in the model. Polypharmacy accounted for only 8% of the total sample and was also excluded from the adjusted analyses. The proportional odds assumption was assessed using the test of parallel lines. For the overall cohort and in female-stratified analyses, the proportional odds assumption was satisfied; therefore, ordinal logistic regression was considered appropriate, and the results are presented accordingly. In the male subgroup, the proportional odds assumption was violated; therefore, binary logistic regression was performed, with frailty dichotomized as robust versus prefrail/frail.

Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Participant characteristics

Our study is based on a population of urban communities within the metropolitan city of Bangkok. The study included 189 older adults aged 60–90 years, of whom 58% (n = 110) were female. The participants were comprehensively interviewed and evaluation for physical frailty based on Fried’s frailty phenotype. Participants were classified as frail (n = 55, 29.1%), pre-frail (n = 80, 42.3%), or robust (n = 54, 28.6%) (Table 1). In the frail group, 72% met three criteria, 24% met four, and 4% met all five criteria. The most common criteria were low physical activity and slowness. Among pre-frail individuals, 55% met one criterion and 45% met two. Frail participants had a significantly higher median age compared to robust and pre-frail groups (76.1 vs. 67.1 and 67.5 years, p < 0.001). Of note, the entire study participants were tested seropositive for CMV IgG.

To identify cognitive frailty, cognitive impairment was assessed using the Thai Mental State Examination (TMSE), with a score below 23/30 indicating mild cognitive impairment [33]. We observed that the prevalence of mild cognitive impairment increased progressively across frailty categories, with 29.6% among frail, 17.1% among pre-frail, and 11.9% among robust individuals (p = 0.048). Overall, the prevalence of cognitive frailty among participants was 8%.

As shown in Table 2, the median white blood cell (WBC) count among all participants was 6,070 (IQR: 5,105-7,285) cells/µl, falling within the normal adult range (4,500–11,000 cells/µl). Median lymphocyte count was 2,012 cells/µl, also within the established reference range for Thai adults (1,300–3,500 in males; 1,200–3,400 in females) [34].

CD8⁺ T cells accounted for a median of 30.8% (IQR: 24.7–36.5%) of all lymphocytes, with a median count of 619 cells/µl (IQR: 454–823). Among CD8⁺ T cell subsets, terminal effector memory cells re-expressing CD45RA (TEMRA, 49.5%) represented the largest fraction, followed by effector memory (TEM, 34.6%), naïve (TN, 8.0%) and central memory cells (TCM, 4.8%). This distribution reflects an immune system skewed toward effector memory and terminal differentiation, characteristic of aging and chronic antigen exposure.

Upon stratification by age groups (60–64, 65–74, and ≥ 75 years), no significant differences were observed in systemic inflammatory markers, including WBC count, neutrophil count, neutrophil percentage, or neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), suggesting stable systemic inflammation across age brackets in this cohort. Total CD8⁺ T cell counts and percentages remained stable with advancing age (p > 0.1), indicating maintenance of overall CD8⁺ T cell numbers even in participants aged ≥ 75 years. However, there was a significant age-associated decline in the naïve CD8⁺ T cell subset: the absolute TN count dropped sharply from a median of 73 cells/µL in the 60–64 age group to 32 cells/µL in those ≥ 75 (p < 0.001), paralleled by a similar drop in TN percentage (p < 0.001). This loss of naïve CD8⁺ T cells reinforces the role of aging in reducing adaptive immune potential. TCM and TEM counts and percentages did not significantly differ by age. However, the proportion of TEMRA cells increased with age, from 47.6% in the 60–64 group to 50.2% in those ≥ 75 (p = 0.030), further supporting age-related immune remodeling driven by chronic antigenic stimulation.

Peripheral blood cell counts and CD8⁺ T cell subset distribution across frailty groups

Significant alterations in immune cell composition were observed across frailty groups (Table 3). Frail individuals had higher total white blood cell (WBC) counts and neutrophil counts compared to robust participants (p = 0.013 and p = 0.006, respectively). The percentage of neutrophils also increased significantly with frailty (p = 0.021), while absolute lymphocyte counts remained unchanged. However, the lymphocyte percentage was significantly lower in frail individuals than in the robust group (p = 0.018). Consequently, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a marker of systemic inflammation, was significantly elevated in frail participants (p = 0.015).

Although total CD8⁺ T cell counts and their relative proportion among lymphocytes did not differ significantly across frailty groups, changes in CD8⁺ T cell subset distribution were evident. Frail individuals had significantly lower counts of naïve CD8⁺ T cells compared to robust participants (p = 0.016), consistent with immune aging and diminished T cell repertoire diversity. Additionally, the percentage of CD8⁺ TEMRA cells was significantly higher in the frail group (p = 0.046), suggesting an increased proportion of terminally differentiated CD8⁺ T cells with greater frailty.

Sex-specific immune changes across frailty groups

Stratification by sex revealed distinct immune alterations associated with frailty (Table 4). Among female participants, WBC counts increased significantly with frailty (p = 0.022), although neutrophil and lymphocyte counts did not differ across groups. Notably, frail females exhibited a significant reduction in both the absolute count (p = 0.042) and percentage (p = 0.029) of CD8⁺ TN cells, suggesting depletion of the T cell repertoire. No other CD8⁺ T cell subsets showed significant differences.

To characterize age-related immune alterations, expression of CD28 and PD-1 on CD8⁺ T cells was assessed. No significant differences in either the percentage or absolute number of CD8⁺CD28⁻ cells were found among frailty groups (Fig. 2a). However, stratification by age revealed a significantly higher proportion of CD8⁺CD28⁻ cells in participants aged ≥ 75 years compared to those aged 60–64 years (p = 0.020) (Fig. 2b), consistent with the accumulation of highly differentiated T cells in advanced age (Fig. 2).

Across all participants, CD8⁺CD28⁻ cells represented 62% of CD8+ T cells. Their proportions increased progressively through the differentiation stages, from TN (~ 8–13%) and TCM (~ 2–3%) to TEM (~ 40–50%), reaching their highest levels in TEMRA (~ 94–95%). Nearly all TEMRA cells lacked CD28 expression, consistent with CD28 loss as a marker of advanced differentiation and late-stage T cell senescence. In contrast, the CD8⁺PD-1⁺ phenotype (25% of CD8+ T cells) was most abundant within memory subsets, particularly TCM (~ 37–43%) and TEM (~ 47–54%), and declined in TEMRA (~ 11–15%), TN (~ 8–15%). This pattern suggests that PD-1 expression reflects chronic stimulation or regulatory activation within memory compartments rather than terminal differentiation.

Participants with frailty exhibited significantly higher proportions of CD8⁺CD28⁻ (p = 0.001) and CD8⁺PD-1⁺ cells (p = 0.008) within the TN gate (Table 5). Although CD8⁺CD28⁻ frequencies were also elevated in other subsets among frail individuals, these differences were not statistically significant (Table 5). CD28⁻PD-1⁺ cells accounted for only ~ 10% of total CD8⁺ T cells and showed no meaningful variation across subsets; therefore, these data were not included in the Table.

Age-stratified analyses showed that CD8⁺CD28⁻ proportions increased with advancing age, with significant differences observed in total CD8⁺ T cells, TN, TEM, and TEMRA subsets (Supplementary Table 2). While overall CD8⁺PD-1⁺ levels did not differ significantly across age groups, PD-1 expression within the TN gate increased markedly in the oldest age group.

Taken together, these findings indicate that markers associated with chronic activation and advanced T cell differentiation are detectable across CD8⁺ T cell subsets—including those phenotypically classified as naïve—in individuals with physical frailty. This pattern highlights the potential contribution of early and late immunosenescent changes to impaired adaptive immunity in aging and frailty.

Association between T cell phenotypes, clinical parameters, and frailty

Given the high degree of correlation typically observed among T cell subsets and related inflammatory markers, elastic net regularized regression was selected and applied to all immune markers identified in previous results. The optimal penalty parameter was determined using cross-validation. Immune markers with non-zero coefficients in the final model were retained as independently contributing variables for subsequent analyses. Age- and sex-adjusted analyses were performed for each immune marker selected by the elastic net model (Supplementary Table 3). After adjustment for age and sex, only some variables showed significant associations with frailty. Variables demonstrating statistical significance in the adjusted model (p < 0.05)—including neutrophil count, CD28⁻ TN cell count, percentage of lymphocytes, and percentage of CD28⁻ TN cells—were subsequently entered into the full multivariable regression model, whereas non-significant variables were excluded.

In the overall cohort, ordinal logistic regression analysis demonstrated that immunological and clinical factors were significantly associated with frailty severity (Table 6). In univariate analysis, a lower percentage of CD28⁻ TN cells, older age, a higher number of comorbidities, and the presence of MCI were significantly associated with increased frailty severity. After multivariable adjustment, %CD28⁻ TN (OR = 1.649, 95% CI: 1.188–2.289, p = 0.003), age (OR = 1.122, 95% CI: 1.071–1.174, p < 0.001), and number of comorbidities (OR = 1.334, 95% CI: 1.101–1.617, p = 0.003) remained independently associated with frailty severity. Sex, MCI, and smoking were not independently associated in the adjusted model. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for individual comorbidities yielded similar results (Supplementary Table 4).

In sex-stratified analyses, the female subgroup showed that older age and a greater number of comorbidities were independently associated with higher frailty severity. Although %CD28⁻ TN was significant in univariate analysis (OR = 2.018, 95% CI: 1.215–3.351, p = 0.007), it did not remain significant after adjustment (OR = 1.801, 95% CI: 1.400-2.799, p = 0.063). In contrast, age (OR = 2.408, 95% CI: 2.284–2.548, p < 0.001) and number of comorbidities (OR = 1.964, 95% CI: 1.655–2.469, p = 0.008) remained independent predictors.

Due to the relatively small number of male participants, frailty was dichotomized (robust vs. prefrail/frail), and binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. In this subgroup, older age and a greater number of comorbidities also showed significant associations with frailty. No CD8+ T cell subsets were remained significantly associated with frailty status after adjustment. NLR was significant in univariate analysis (OR = 3.333, 95% CI: 1.323–8.403, p = 0.011), it did not remain significant after adjustment (OR = 1.606, 95% CI: 0.738–3.493, p = 0.232).

Additionally, associations between individual components of physical frailty—gait speed, handgrip strength, and physical activity—and CD8⁺ T cell subsets were evaluated after adjustment for age and multimorbidity. Weight loss and exhaustion were not analyzed due to their low prevalence in the cohort (< 10% of all participants exhibited these components). Most components were not significantly associated with the immune markers assessed. A modest association was observed only between the percentage of TCM cells and slower gait speed (Supplementary Table 5).

Overall, these findings indicate that chronological age and comorbidity burden are the primary factors associated with greater frailty severity in this cohort, with a higher proportion of CD28⁻ naïve T cells also contributing independently in the overall analysis. The association of this phenotype was less consistent in sex-stratified models, remaining non-significant after adjustment in females and absent in males, likely due to limited sample size.

Discussion

Our study cohort represented a community-dwelling older adult population within the metropolitan city of Bangkok, Thailand. We observed that frailty increased with advancing age and was significantly associated with multimorbidity, particularly hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) was also more common among frail participants, resulting in cognitive frailty in approximately 8% of the cohort. Cognitive frailty and cardiovascular disease have been reported to share common pathophysiologic mechanisms, with hypertension and diabetes serving as key risk factors for both physical frailty and cognitive decline [35].

This study demonstrates that frailty in older Thai adults is associated with marked shifts in CD8⁺ T cell composition. While total CD8⁺ T cell counts remained stable across age groups, significant age-related changes in subset distribution were observed, characterized by a marked decline in TN cells and an expansion of TEMRA cells with advancing age. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing age-associated T cell remodeling [7, 13, 14]. In our cohort, TEMRA cells constituted the largest proportion of CD8⁺ T cells, followed by TEM, TN, and TCM subsets, a pattern likely influenced by universal CMV seropositivity among participants and consistent with distributions reported in other older adult populations [6, 36].

Given that sex-related differences influence the incidence of frailty, with a higher prevalence observed in females than in males [37], we performed sex-stratified analyses to evaluate frailty-associated immune alterations in each sex. Among males, frailty was characterized by a reduction in TEM cells and an expansion of TEMRA cells, indicating a shift toward terminal differentiation. In contrast, frail females exhibited a significant decline in TN cells, suggesting depletion of the T cell repertoire. Notably, TN cell counts and percentages were already low in males across the robust and pre-frail groups, which may explain the absence of a statistically significant difference between frail and non-frail males, unlike the pattern observed in females. Additionally, frail males demonstrated a significant increase in the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a pattern not observed in females. These findings underscore sex-based differences in frailty-associated immune aging.

An underlying mechanism for these sex differences in immune cell distribution is likely related to hormonal differences that influence T cell development and function [38]. A study in healthy adults demonstrated that females had higher number of recent thymic emigrants compare to males [39]. Specifically, androgen-driven thymic involution can lead to lower baseline TN counts in males [40]. Supporting this notion, mechanistic studies using androgen-deprivation therapy in murine models have demonstrated restoration of recent thymic output [41]. Another influential factor is chronic CMV infection which was present in all participant of this cohort. CMV has been shown to exert stronger effects in men, leading to greater CD8⁺ TEMRA expansion in association with frailty compared with women [42].

In this study, we explored the loss of CD28 and PD-1 upregulation as markers of age-related changes in T cell function. Although a previous study demonstrated an association between increased CD8⁺CD28⁻ T cells and frailty [22], our findings did not support this link. Instead, we observed that the loss of CD28 was significantly associated with chronological age, but not with frailty status. CD8⁺CD28⁻ cells increased progressively through the differentiation stages: TN, TCM, to TEM, and highest in TEMRA, nearly all of which lacked CD28 expression. In general, CD28- phenotype is associated with reduced proliferative capacity and shorter life span compared to their CD28+ counterparts [43]. Nevertheless, the loss of CD28 primarily reflects an antigen-experienced and highly differentiated stage, rather than specifying the functional properties of the cells. Additional markers such as CD57 or CD25 are required to accurately define the phenotypes of these cells [44].

We also conducted an exploratory analysis of PD-1 expression on T cells in our cohort, given that its expression together with the CD28 loss recognized as an indicative of severe exhaustion and functional decline [45, 46]. The distribution of PD-1 expression across CD8⁺ T-cell subsets in our cohort was consistent with previous reports showing high expression in memory CD8⁺ T cells—up to 60% in healthy individuals—and minimal expression in naïve T cells [47, 48]. PD-1 expression alone likely reflects chronic activation within memory T cell pools rather than irreversible exhaustion. Clinical relevance of this molecule is underscored by the ability of PD-1 blockade to restore T cell function and proliferative capacity in cancer immunotherapy. Moreover, elevated CD8⁺PD-1⁺ T-cell levels have been associated with disease progression and severity in several chronic conditions, including autoimmune diseases and cancers [49, 50]. The presence of CD8⁺PD-1⁺ T cell subsets in our study implied their susceptibility to PD-1 blockade in cancer treatment in those with frailty or advanced ages.

Although naïve T cells are typically CD28⁺ and PD-1⁻, we unexpectedly observed significantly elevated proportions of CD28⁻ and PD-1⁺ cells within the TN population in the frail group, despite their very low absolute frequencies. Several mechanisms may explain the emergence of these phenotypes in older or frail adults. First, chronic systemic inflammation and persistent antigenic stimulation can drive early differentiation or functional impairment within the naïve pool. Second, CCR7⁺CD45RA⁺ gating may inadvertently include T memory stem cells (TSCM), a long-lived subset with naive-like surface markers but memory-like behavior [51]. TSCM cells can upregulate activation or inhibitory receptors, including PD-1, particularly in settings of chronic immune activation [52]. Our findings align with emerging evidence that not all CCR7⁺CD45RA⁺ cells represent true naive T cells. It has been demonstrated that cells that preserve a naïve surface phenotype but exhibit memory-like functional characteristics, or TSCM, were accumulated with aging in human [11, 53]. These cells increase in frequency across the lifespan and reflect chronic antigenic stimulation. Their presence supports the possibility that a portion of the CD28⁻ or PD-1⁺ cells observed within the CCR7⁺CD45RA⁺ (TN) gate in our frail participants may represent antigen-experienced, memory-derived cells rather than truly naïve T cells. Accurate interpretation of this cell population requires more detailed phenotypic characterization, including the assessment of markers such as CD95, CD27, and CD127, to distinguish bona fide naïve T cells from TSCM populations.

When multivariable ordinal logistic regression was applied to the entire cohort, age and multimorbidity were consistently associated with frailty. The associations between most immune cell markers and frailty were attenuated after adjustment for age and comorbidities. These findings suggest that chronological aging contributes substantially to frailty in this cohort, and that part of the observed relationship between immune markers and frailty may reflect age-related immune changes and underlying health conditions rather than frailty per se. Only CD28⁻ naïve CD8⁺ T cells remained significantly associated with frailty after adjustment, suggesting that alterations in this specific T cell phenotype may represent a more robust immunological correlate of frailty, beyond the effects of chronological aging and comorbidity burden. However, the role of CD28⁻ naïve CD8⁺ T cells in frailty requires further evaluation in large-scale prospective studies to establish its reliability and clinical relevance.

In sex-stratified analyses, age and multimorbidity remained a consistent predictor in both sexes. Age emerged as the strongest predictor in females, whereas the effect of CD28⁻ naïve CD8⁺ T cells on frailty was attenuated. In males, although NLR was significantly higher among frail males in the comparative analysis and showed an association with frailty in the univariate model, this relationship was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for potential confounders. This attenuation suggests that the initial association may have been partly explained by other factors correlated with both systemic inflammation and frailty, such as age, comorbid conditions, or other immune parameters. NLR is considered a marker of systemic inflammation, and an increased NLR has been linked to frailty in older adults identified using the frailty index or the FRAIL scale [54, 55]. Nevertheless, its lack of independent association in the adjusted model indicates that NLR alone may not be a robust predictor of frailty in this population.

The associations between individual components of physical frailty and CD8⁺ T cell subsets adjusted for age and multimorbidity showed that most frailty components were not significantly associated with the immune markers assessed. A modest association was observed between a higher proportion of TCM cells and slower gait speed, suggesting that shifts in memory T cell populations may be modestly related to functional decline in mobility. However, the lack of consistent associations across other frailty components suggests that immune alterations captured by CD8⁺ T cell phenotyping may relate more strongly to the overall frailty phenotype rather than to any specific physical deficit.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of sex-stratified analysis of CD8⁺ T cells as immune aging biomarkers of frailty in older Thai adults. While ageing is hypothetically associated with immunosenescence—characterized by reduced naïve T cells and expansion of terminally differentiated subsets—our findings suggest that certain immune alterations observed in frailty may not be fully explained by chronological age alone. Frailty may reflect an accelerated manifestation of age-related immune remodeling rather than a completely distinct biological process. Collectively, our findings provide a preliminary basis for future biomarker development and highlight the potential utility of CD8⁺ T cell phenotypes as indicators of immune vulnerability in frailty. Further studies are required to clarify the independent contribution of immune marker alterations to frailty and their clinical implications.

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; accordingly, longitudinal studies are needed to examine the temporal dynamics of immune changes and their causal relationship with frailty progression. The modest sample size and single-center setting warrant replication in independent cohorts to confirm the robustness of the findings. As the data were derived from a single cohort of community-dwelling Thai older adults, generalizability may be limited, underscoring the need for larger multicenter studies within the region. In addition, frailty was assessed using the physical phenotype approach; future studies incorporating multidimensional frailty measures, including deficit-accumulation–based frailty indices, are needed to enable comparisons across different frailty constructs. Finally, CD4⁺ T cells were not assessed, and therefore the findings may not reflect the full spectrum of T lymphocyte distribution.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that frailty in older Thai adults is closely linked to age-related immune remodeling and the burden of multimorbidity. The observed alterations in CD8⁺ T cell composition are consistent with processes of immunosenescence, indicating that immune changes accompanying chronological aging may contribute to increased biological vulnerability reflected in frailty. These results highlight the complex interplay between aging, immune system alterations, and frailty status. Together, the findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the potential role of immune profiling in improving frailty assessment and future research in aging population.
