Integrated assessment of a leadership course for premedical students: exploring the interprofessional education from selective collaboration with nursing students
Authors: Janghee Park, Eun Go, Yewon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis evaluation study assessed the learning outcomes of a newly developed Leadership and Communication course for premedical students and explored the potential of interprofessional education through collaborative activities with nursing students.MethodsTo evaluate the learning outcomes of a leadership course involving 99 premedical students, we analyzed two distinct data sources. First, we reviewed the results of an anonymous course evaluation conducted by the university, with 98 students responding. Second, we analyzed 376 reflective notes submitted by 99 premedical students regarding four collaborative learning activities with 70 nursing students. After excluding unreliable or non-submitted reflective notes, a total of 376 reflective notes were included in the analysis. Textual analysis was performed using Voyant Tools, a web-based platform that enables rapid visualization and analysis of qualitative data.ResultsCourse evaluation revealed positive response rates averaging 60–70% across all course components. However, the students requested improvements in the operational aspects of this first interdisciplinary course format involving students from different departments. Textual analysis of reflection reports identified ‘communication’ as the most frequently mentioned keyword, often associated with ‘important.’ Analysis revealed core IPE competency for Interprofessional Collaborative Practice (IPCP) in order of frequency: ‘communication,’ ‘teams and teamwork,’ ‘roles and responsibilities,’ and ‘values and ethics.’ Two specific activities—special lectures and hospital development plans—effectively encompassed all core IPE competency keywords.ConclusionsSimple textual analysis of student-submitted reflective journals combined with institutional course evaluation reviews enabled comprehensive and nuanced assessment of course outcomes. The integration of selective collaborative activities with nursing students within existing course structures proved to be a pragmatic alternative that simultaneously enhanced premedical students’ learning outcomes in leadership and communication, mitigated implementation challenges inherent to comprehensive interprofessional education (IPE) programs, and fostered deeper interprofessional understanding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09146-2.

Background

Leadership and communication skills are pivotal in enhancing patient safety, fostering interprofessional collaboration, advancing physicians’ professional development, and addressing ethical and legal considerations in patient care [1–3]. Consequently, medical education increasingly emphasizes these competencies. Effective communication among healthcare professionals, particularly within today’s complex and rapidly evolving medical environment, reduces medical errors and improves patient outcomes. The resulting trust and cooperation among team members facilitate comprehensive and coordinated patient care [4, 5]. Moreover, healthcare professionals must collaborate effectively within interprofessional teams, and strong leadership and communication skills are essential for fostering positive team dynamics and enabling effective team leadership [6]. Leadership competencies also significantly influence career advancement in medicine by equipping physicians with managerial responsibilities and actively contributing to improving healthcare systems [7].

To optimize the development of leadership and communication competencies in medical education, a dual emphasis on theoretical rigor and practical application is essential. Respectful interprofessional engagement, collaborative decision-making, and team-based care constitute foundational competencies for effective healthcare leadership, directly impacting patient safety, care quality, and resource efficiency [8, 9]. Leadership training programs for medical students are predominantly conducted exclusively with medical students and consist primarily of didactic lectures, projects, seminars, and simulations. A systematic review of leadership studies revealed that among 16 programs demonstrating meaningful educational impact, seven targeted premedical students only, three were directed solely at clinical students, one exclusively at MD-PhD students, and another at both clinical medical students and residents. When medical schools develop and implement their own leadership training programs, opportunities for delivery in conjunction with students from other healthcare professions are exceedingly rare [10].

Since healthcare delivery involves team-based patient care, interprofessional education (IPE) grounded in mutual understanding among different healthcare disciplines contributes to the enhancement of leadership competencies and related professional skills. The World Health Organization (WHO) has advocated integrating interprofessional education (IPE) into the educational curricula of all healthcare professionals. In line with WHO recommendations, institutions are increasingly developing and utilizing core competencies for Interprofessional Collaborative Practice (IPCP), emphasizing values and ethics, roles and responsibilities, effective communication, and teamwork [11]. Education programs designed around these competencies emphasize collaborative problem-solving and healthcare management skills [12, 13]. Additionally, educational methods have evolved from traditional lectures and seminars, prevalent in previous decades, to practical, experience-based learning, such as simulations and direct patient care. Modern educational settings now include classrooms, online platforms, simulation centers, and clinical environments [13, 14].

Evaluating course outcomes via multiple data sources is an effective strategy. An integrated assessment with multiple data sources facilitates a comprehensive assessment of learning outcomes by capturing both the range of students’ responses and the depth of their attitudinal changes. Quantitative methods systematically measure general student responses to curricular interventions, providing statistical evidence. However, qualitative analysis of reflective essays reveals nuanced shifts in professional attitudes, self-concept, and leadership identity that quantitative metrics alone cannot disentangle [15].

Reflective writing by students provides a valuable window into their attitudinal changes, fostering greater self-awareness and professional identity development [16]. Therefore, qualitative analyses of reflective essays, in addition to quantitative evaluations, are vital for accurately assessing attitudinal shifts and learning outcomes in courses designed to enhance leadership and communication competencies.

Text mining analysis of reflective essays is an innovative and effective method for assessing attitudinal changes. Text mining systematically analyzes unstructured qualitative data—such as open-ended survey responses or student reflections—using natural language processing (NLP). This method quantifies qualitative data, identifies key themes, visualizes important concepts, and maps relationships within text data, facilitating deeper understanding [17–19]. Recent research has demonstrated the value of text mining techniques in educational assessment, highlighting students’ needs, strengths, weaknesses, and potential curricular improvements [8]. Thus, employing text mining in leadership and communication courses provides an insightful approach, integrating quantitative precision with qualitative depth.

Methods

A new Leadership and Communication course was developed for premedical students with the dual aim of enhancing leadership education outcomes and exploring the potential of interprofessional education (IPE). To evaluate the effectiveness of the newly developed program, institutional course evaluation results were reviewed and students’ reflection notes were subjected to text analysis. To maximize the impact of leadership education and investigate the feasibility of interprofessional education, collaborative group activities focused on leadership development were conducted jointly with nursing students. To verify the potential of interprofessional education, reflection notes were analyzed through text analysis utilizing interprofessional education competency frameworks as the analytical foundation.

Leadership and communication course overview

The ultimate aim of the course was to foster students’ awareness of the critical roles that leadership and communication play in healthcare environments. Furthermore, it sought to encourage meaningful collaboration with students from other health professions—particularly nursing—thereby promoting mutual understanding and respect. Through these collaborative group activities, students were expected to internalize the core competencies of interprofessional education (IPE), such as values and ethics, roles and responsibilities, effective communication, and teamwork. To operationalize these broader aims, the course presented students with specific learning outcomes focused on communication theory, leadership models, and collaborative engagement.

The course spanned eight weeks of lectures, five weeks of practical training, and two weeks of evaluation (Table 1).

Lectures covered topics such as self-understanding, communication theories, leadership models, and team development methods.

Selective collaborative group activities: premedical and nursing students

Practical training sessions involved collaborative group activities designed to strengthen IPE competencies through collaboration between premedical and nursing students. The details of the collaborative group activities were as follows.

Collaborative group activity 1. Special lectures

Students attended an orientation followed by presentations from four instructors emphasizing leadership, communication, and cooperation in the clinical setting. The dean of the medical school discussed the importance of IPE, whereas the hospital director emphasized the necessity of communication within the hospital setting. An emergency medicine professor presented a video on cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) to highlight the critical role of communication in emergency situations. Finally, the head of the quality control team, a nurse, addressed the need for collaboration to ensure quality patient care. After the lecture, the students participated in an ice-breaking activity and selected a group leader. The first assignment for each student involved summarizing the lecture contents and reflections.

Collaborative group activity 2. In-Hospital communication (ISBAR)

This activity introduced the ISBAR framework (identity, situation, background, assessment & action, response & rationale) for clinical handover communication [20]. Students collaboratively created scenarios depicting communication failures, produced videos based on these scenarios, and participated in feedback sessions during a debriefing week.

Collaborative group activity 3. In-Hospital simulation game (FNER)

This simulation game replicated hospital management scenarios over a simulated 24-hour period. During the Friday in the Emergency Room (FNER) game, students were divided among the main hospital departments: emergency rooms, operating rooms, intensive care units, and inpatient rooms. This simulation allowed participants to engage in systems thinking and socialization and to foster improved teamwork [21, 22].

Collaborative group activity 4. Hospital creation plan

Students collaboratively designed a hypothetical hospital’s mission, vision, core values, name, and organizational structure. The outcomes were presented via video submissions alongside individual reflective reports. Owing to limited resources for simulation tools, students alternated between simulation games and hospital creation activities over two weeks.

The learning objectives of each collaborative group activity were mapped to the IPEC core competencies [Table 2]. The distribution of competencies was balanced across activities, with stronger focus on leadership and communication, reflecting the course objectives.

Participants

The Leadership and Communication course was newly developed in 2023 for second-year premedical students. A total of 99 premedical students participated in the course during its first year of implementation. The course structure included both lectures and practical sessions. The practical component was conducted in collaboration with the Communication and Human Relations course, which was offered to 70 second-year nursing students. Unlike the newly introduced course for premedical students, the nursing course had been well established and had been in operation for several years.

A total of six faculty members were involved in delivering the two courses: three professors for the Leadership and Communication course for premedical students and three professors for the Communication and Human Relations course for nursing students. The professors involved in the premedical course came from diverse academic backgrounds—medical education, emergency medicine, and business administration. Moreover, the instructors for the nursing course were experienced registered nurses with clinical backgrounds.

Group allocation

Thirty groups were formed, comprising 70 nursing students and 99 premedical students distributed as evenly as possible. Each group consisted of five to six students with balanced allocation across disciplines, with minor adjustments in group composition according to activity. At baseline, 13 groups included three premedical and three nursing students, 8 groups included three premedical and two nursing students, and 9 groups included four premedical and two nursing students. For the hospital design activity and the FNER exercise, group allocation was modified to align with the game format, resulting in 18 groups with three students from each discipline, 11 groups with three nursing and four premedical students, and 2 groups with four nursing and three premedical students.

Reviewing the leadership & communication course surveys reports

The results of an anonymous course evaluation conducted by the university were reviewed, with 98 out of 99 medical students responding. The university’s Performance Management Center administered a standardized course evaluation questionnaire, independently developed by the center, to all enrolled students on an anonymous basis. Only aggregated results were provided to the course director. The course was conducted over 15 weeks starting March 1, 2023, and the course evaluations were administratively conducted by the university among the enrolled students between May 22 and June 18, 2023. The university analyzed the survey results and provided instructors with frequency analyses for each item and summaries of the open-ended responses, without including individual student information.

The administrative course survey consisted of 14 items measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Strongly Agree” to “Strongly Disagree,” covering the following dimensions: overall course evaluation (1 item), instructor characteristics (2 items), educational content (4 items), teaching methods (4 items), assessment strategies (2 items), and course management (1 item). The open-ended questions gave the students the opportunity to provide additional feedback regarding the strengths of the course and suggestions for improvement. The positive response rate of the course evaluation items was analyzed. Positive responses (“Strongly Agree” or “Agree”) were compared with negative responses (“Disagree” or “Strongly Disagree”) and were excluded neutral responses (“Neutral”).

Reflection notes

The practical component of the Leadership and Communication course for premedical students was conducted collaboratively with an existing nursing course, Communication and Human Relations, which is offered to 70 second-year nursing students. Only outcomes related to the premedical course were evaluated in this study.

Qualitative data were derived from reflections within the individual reports submitted by the students after each group activity. Reflection notes were open narrative. As 99 premedical students participated in four collaborative group activities, a reflection note was required for each activity. Reflection notes that were not submitted (missing) or contained content unrelated to the activity were excluded from the analysis. A total of 376 reflection notes were included in the final analysis (95 for CGA1, 94 for CGA2, 92 for CGA3, and 95 for CGA4).

Text analysis of reflection notes

Text analysis was a method that quantitatively examined qualitative data. In this study, we employed Voyant Tools, an open-source, web-based platform for text analysis and visualization. This tool, developed by Stéfan Sinclair and Geoffrey Rockwell, offered user-friendly features that facilitated research in ways that traditional methods could not. Voyant Tools used the bag-of-words model, a natural language processing technique that tokenized text into corpus units to calculate and visualize the frequency of words and phrases. Additionally, it incorporated machine learning models to analyze the subject matter and emotional tone of the text [18, 19, 23].

To use Voyant Tools, users visited the website (https://voyant-tools.org) and either input text directly or uploaded text files for analysis. This program allowed for the simultaneous analysis and comparison of multiple documents. In this study, we analyzed student reflection documents from four different activities. Although Voyant Tools supported various languages, it did not support Korean. Therefore, all the student reflections were translated into English prior to analysis to ensure accurate results. Prior to conducting text analysis using Voyant Tools, we implemented a multi-step translation process to minimize potential loss of meaning from the original Korean texts. Student reflections were first translated into English by bilingual researchers fluent in both Korean and English. The translated texts were back-translated and reviewed by team consensus to ensure semantic and contextual equivalence. Because the student reflections consisted of a few brief sentences, the translation process was straightforward.

After the text or files were uploaded and the “Reveal” button was clicked, the default interface (or “skin”) provided access to tools such as Cirrus, Reader, Trends, Summary, and Contexts. Among these tools, we utilized Cirrus, summary, and mandala for this study.

The summary tool was used to analyze key metrics for each group activity, including document length, average word count per sentence, most frequent words, and five unique words. Document length referred to the total number of words in each reflection document, whereas the average word count reflected the mean number of words per sentence. The calculation of sentences required careful consideration due to complexities introduced by abbreviations and punctuation marks. Additionally, we identified the top five unique words in each document and compared them with those from reflections on other activities.

The Cirrus tool visualized frequent words for each activity in word cloud format. In these visualizations, frequently occurring words were displayed in larger font sizes and were centrally positioned. Importantly, the color and exact position of a word within the cloud were not considered meaningful; the word cloud displayed up to 50 words whose font size was proportional to word frequency [19, 24, 25].

The word graph (Cirrus) presented high-frequency terms across the corpus, while the Collocate graph was used to examine conceptual relationships among key terms based on co-occurrence rather than statistical correlation [26].

The Mandala tool was used to examine relationships among terms related to IPE core competencies, as reflected in the students’ reflections. A mandala served as a conceptual visualization illustrating connections between specific terms (referred to as “magnets”) and documents within the corpus. Each magnet attracted documents on the basis of the frequency of the term within them. For this study, we analyzed terms related to the four core competencies of interprofessional collaborative practice (IPCP), namely, values and ethics, roles and responsibilities, communication, and teams and teamwork, as defined in Version 3 (2023) [11].

By leveraging these tools, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of student reflections across all activities while identifying patterns related to IPE competencies.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol utilizing secondary data (student reflection texts) was reviewed and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Soonchunhyang (IRB No. 202405-SB-045-03). All secondary data were fully anonymized by de-identifying personal information prior to analysis, and the IRB waived the requirement for informed consent according to relevant law and institutional policies.

Additionally, the course evaluation data used in this study were anonymized, pre-existing institutional reports provided by the university for program evaluation. The author did not participate in data collection or evaluation, and no personal identifiers were accessible.

Text analysis of reflection notes about collaborative group activities

Text analysis was performed on reflections from four collaborative group activities between premedical and nursing students. Two types of analyses were conducted: keyword frequency visualization and interprofessional education (IPE) competency correlation.

The characteristics of the four collaborative group activities

Reflections on the four collaborative group activities (CGA) were compiled into four documents, and the text within these documents was analyzed. The frequency of words was visualized via a word cloud, whereas the relationships among words were depicted via a collocate graph (Fig. 2). The key metrics for each group activity, including the total length of the document, average words per sentence, most frequent words, and distinctive words, were presented in Appendix 2.

Word clouds (frequent words) and Collocate graphs (co-occurring terms)

The development of interprofessional education (IPE) core competencies was clearly reflected in the students’ reflection notes. With respect to communication, the students recognized the necessity and impact of effective communication, particularly in emergency and clinical settings. With respect to team and teamwork, through group learning and gameplay, they also experienced significant collaboration within hospital systems, highlighting the importance of teams and teamwork. In terms of roles and responsibilities, activities involving the creation of a hypothetical hospital encouraged students to reflect on professional role distinctions and to consider leadership responsibilities. Furthermore, students engaged with values and ethics by contemplating shared missions and core values, particularly during the hospital creation process. Collectively, these findings suggested that collaborative group activities within leadership and communication courses can effectively cultivate IPE competencies, especially when they are grounded in realistic clinical contexts and supported by meaningful student engagement.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of a newly developed course designed to enhance leadership competencies among premedical students by reviewing institutional course evaluation reports. Additionally, to further strengthen the impact of leadership education and explore the feasibility of interprofessional education, the study analyzed reflection notes submitted by premedical students following collaborative group activities with nursing students.

Evaluation of a new leadership and communication course for premedical students

This study analyzed the learning outcomes of a newly developed course designed to help premedical students recognize the importance of leadership and communication skills for patient safety and to experience these competencies through practical activities with nursing students To gain a comprehensive understanding of learning outcomes, integrated assessment was used with multiple sources of data [15, 16]. University-administered course evaluations were conducted anonymously without revealing student identities to instructors, thereby encouraging honest feedback on the overall course. However, the results of the course evaluations indicated considerable variation in the proportion of positive responses across survey items, making it difficult to confirm the effectiveness of the course solely through quantitative averages. To assess changes in students’ attitudes more concretely, reflective notes on the practical sessions were analyzed. Positive evaluations of the instructor’s personality, attitude toward students, course content, and teaching methods consistently outweighed negative evaluations. Nevertheless, positive responses regarding course recommendations and overall class experience were only marginally greater than negative responses. Text analysis further identified frequent co-occurrences of terms such as “physician’s role,” “leadership and communication,” and “importance,” suggesting that the course emphasized these elements as critical to patient safety in clinical settings. Reduced satisfaction with certain course components appeared to be linked to students’ preference for practical leadership scenarios pertinent to hospital environments over more general leadership theories.

Although the students had not yet experienced clinical practice, they exhibited greater interest in concise lectures specifically addressing the significance of leadership in medical settings rather than broader general leadership lectures. For example, lectures delivered by a business administration professor, which extensively covered leadership theories and corporate case studies, were perceived as overly simplistic and monotonous. In contrast, brief presentations by an emergency medicine physician that incorporated leadership and communication principles directly relevant to emergency room scenarios, supported by related videos, elicited greater student engagement. Practical activities such as producing communication videos based on clinical scenarios and participating in hospital simulation exercises further enhanced student involvement and contributed to the development of professional identities as future physicians. These findings were consistent with the concept of early clinical exposure (ECE), which was known to increase student interest in coursework, facilitate the development of physician identity [27], and promote a deeper appreciation for basic medical sciences by underscoring their clinical relevance [28].

Exploring IPE through collaborative activities with premedical students and nursing students

Leadership education programs targeting medical students primarily involved various activities conducted by the medical students themselves, and only a small number of studies involved collaboration with other professions [10]. However, in our study, in order to enhance the effect of leadership education, we partially conducted leadership and communication activities together with nursing students, who have the closest relationship with medical students in clinical settings, thereby increasing the effectiveness of learning.

These collaborative experiences heightened student awareness of the importance of these skills and facilitated understanding of different professional roles, fostering interpersonal connections.

Evidence of interprofessional education competencies was identified across the collaborative activities implemented in this course. Given that the course was specifically designed to cultivate leadership and communication competencies, textual analysis of reflection notes revealed frequent mentions of keywords aligned with these pedagogical objectives. However, through two collaborative activities (Special lectures and Hospital Creation Plan), students demonstrated balanced reflection across all core competencies for Interprofessional Collaborative Practice (IPCP): values and ethics, roles and responsibilities, effective communication, and teamwork [11].

Theoretical, practical, methodological significances

From a theoretical perspective, this study explored interprofessional education through a course, not as a simple description, but by examining how collaborative activities between premedical and nursing students align with a core interprofessional competency model [11]. We also identified the strengths of each activity, which can inform the development of future interprofessional competency models.

From a practical perspective, considering leadership and communication in clinical settings when providing leadership education to premedical students is highly appropriate in terms of early clinical exposure and motivating students. Although interprofessional education on such topics is effective, developing and implementing entirely new programs across professions with different curricula and cultures presents various challenges [9, 29]. Therefore, collaborating on selective collaboration activities within subjects that deal with similar topics in each profession is relatively easier to implement, making it a realistic alternative.

From a methodological perspective, the most reasonable approach is to analyze data collected through various methods and draw integrated conclusions. It is valid to comprehensively assess the learning effects by analyzing both objective and subjective survey responses, as well as reflection notes related to activities. Text analysis via the Voyant tool was straightforward and freely accessible, producing results on text frequency, relationships between texts, and document summaries, all of which are presented through various graphical displays [18, 19, 23, 24]. Unlike contemporary artificial intelligence-driven analytical methodologies which necessitate continuous model refinement and iterative prompt optimization to generate researcher-specific analytical outputs [30]. The Voyant tool provides a distinctive advantage through its user-friendly interface. By uploading text data, researchers can readily access multifaceted analytical results displayed in both numerical and graphical formats, thereby reducing technical barriers and enhancing accessibility for researchers at all experience levels.

Challenges and implications for future interprofessional education

As the importance of interprofessional collaboration increases, the demand for interprofessional education is also growing. However, coordinating course content, teaching methods, and schedules across different departments presents numerous challenges, often making such collaborative courses difficult to implement. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the feasibility of a collaborative class conducted partly with the nursing department, which offered similar content during certain parts of the course. Through implementation, several challenges to be addressed were identified. First, scheduling required careful coordination to align availability between departments. The activity-based nature of the course made it difficult for students to transition between classes. Although adjustments were made to align afternoon class times, subsequent nursing classes limited student participation [9, 13, 14]. Second, instructor collaboration involving joint content development, role sharing, and standardized evaluation criteria was essential. Faculty collaboration across departments provided a model of interprofessional cooperation. However, discrepancies in operational practices, such as different timings for disclosing evaluation materials, caused complaints among premedical students [13]. Third, equalizing student learning motivation was crucial. Second-year premedical students, who manage demanding coursework and relatively secure career paths, sometimes prioritized leisure activities, whereas second-year nursing students, who were preparing for clinical rotations and facing competitive job markets, engaged more actively. These differences limited course effectiveness [13, 14].

In future studies, analyzing assignments from nursing students alongside those of premedical students will allow for the examination of attitudinal changes and differences between the two professions from an IPE perspective, as defined by the WHO. In line with this approach, future research should expand collaborative activities to include a broader range of healthcare professions, thereby enhancing learners’ understanding of diverse professional roles [13, 29]. Additionally, addressing scheduling and space challenges by implementing hybrid online-offline learning methods could alleviate logistical issues [31–35]. Finally, conducting simultaneous quantitative and qualitative assessments is recommended for the comprehensive evaluation of IPE outcomes via validated tools such as interprofessional learning scales, attitudes toward healthcare teams, TeamSTEPPS, and the Readiness for Interprofessional Learning Scale [13, 36, 37].

Conclusions

Premedical students’ recognition of the importance of leadership and communication for patient safety was confirmed through both course survey review and reflective analyses. For courses designed to influence students’ attitudes and competencies, evaluating effectiveness through a combined methodology of surveys and textual analysis of reflections provided more comprehensive and insightful assessment outcomes.

Healthcare professionals in team-based patient care settings require effective leadership and communication competencies to optimize clinical outcomes. This study confirmed that selective integration of collaborative activities drawn from parallel courses across professions—even without fully developed interprofessional education (IPE) curricula—effectively promotes interprofessional understanding and facilitates development of core competencies for Interprofessional Collaborative Practice (IPCP). These findings underscore the practical feasibility and methodological efficacy of implementing pragmatic, incremental approaches to interprofessional education within existing institutional constraints.
