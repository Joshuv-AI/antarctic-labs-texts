Utilizing imaging features of preoperative gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI for predicting lymphovascular invasion in colorectal cancer liver metastases and exploring its impact on survival
Authors: Qian Xing, Yong Cui, Xiao-Lei Gu, Xiao-Ting Li, Ying-Shi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo construct an imaging model based on preoperative gadoxetic acid- enhanced MRI for predicting lymphovascular invasion (LVI) in colorectal cancer liver metastases (CRLM) and explore its impact on survival.MethodA total of 91 patients with CRLM were retrospectively enrolled in this study. The liver lesions were categorized into two groups, with LVI and without LVI by pathological examination. The long diameter, vascular penetration sign, peritumoral hepatobiliary phase (HBP) hypointensity and other qualitative signs were evaluated. The mean value and standard deviation (SD) value of signal intensity (SI) of the liver lesion and the region with 5-mm tumor expansion were recorded. The relative enhancement rate (RER) was calculated on each phase. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to construct the imaging model (LVI model) for predicting LVI in CRLM. Each liver lesion in all the patients was assessed using the LVI model to classify patients into predicted LVI-negative or predicted LVI-positive groups.ResultsThe vascular penetration sign (odds ratio [OR] = 30.052, p<0.001) and SD of tumor on HBP (SDTumor−HBP)(OR = 1.004, p = 0.026) were used to construct the imaging model for predicting LVI and the AUC of the model was 0.874 (95%CI:0.747-1.000). The liver recurrence-free survival (LRFS) of 22 predicted LVI-positive patients was significantly lower than that of 69 predicted LVI-negative patients (median 9.0 vs. 28.0 months, p = 0.028). The overall survival (OS) of predicted LVI-positive patients was significantly lower than that of predicted LVI-negative patients (median 21.0 vs. 63.0 months, p = 0.001).ConclusionThe imaging model based on vascular penetration sign and SDTumor−HBP has good efficiency in predicting lymphovascular invasion and survival in patients with CRLM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02395-z.Keywords: Colorectal cancer liver metastases, MRI, Lymphovascular invasion

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks the third most common cancer globally [1]. The liver is the most common metastatic organ of CRC [2]. Liver resection remains the main approach for patients with colorectal cancer liver metastases (CRLM) to achieve long-term survival. Although preoperative chemotherapy together with liver resection has improved the 5-year overall survival (OS) in CRLM patients [3], 40–55% of patients will develop recurrence, even after R0 resection [4]. Thus, the prognostic factors need to be identified and a stratified strategy should be developed to determine whether patients can benefit from the treatment.

In patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), macrovascular or microvascular invasion (MVI) is a well-established risk factor [5–8]. Numerous studies investigated imaging features and radiomics for preoperative MVI prediction in HCC [6, 9], especially with gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI [10–12]. A wide surgical margin improves long-term survival in HCC patients with MVI [13], thus preoperative identification of MVI is of great importance. Lymphovascular invasion (LVI) has also been reported as a prognostic factor in many other malignant tumors, such as CRC, pancreatic tumors and non-small-cell lung cancer [14–16], and can significantly affect patient OS [17–19].

As for the field of CRLM, studies show that the existence of intrahepatic lymphatic and microvascular invasion is also associated with an early recurrence and poor OS [20]. Approximately 60% of hepatic vessels were invaded by or attached to liver lesions after chemotherapy [21]. Even though tumors at the edge of vessels do not affect the prognosis, vascular invasion is associated with survival [22]. However, studies on LVI in CRLM remain limited, and imaging-related research of this field is still insufficient. We have adopted the assessment methods for evaluating MVI in HCC and applied them to the assessment of LVI in CRLM, which is defined as the presence of tumor cells within a definite endothelial-lined space [19, 23]. This attempt is entirely new in the field of CRLM. Preoperative and noninvasive prediction of LVI may provide valuable guidance for individualized clinical decision-making. And it is of great value for the selection of surgical approaches and the stratified treatment of patients with CRLM. For patients with predicted LVI, intensive preoperative chemotherapy and radical resection with a wider surgical margin are recommended [24]. For lesions with predicted LVI that are treated with radiofrequency ablation (RFA) or radiotherapy instead of surgical resection, regular postoperative follow-up should be conducted to facilitate early detection of recurrence.

Materials and methods

Patients

In this retrospective study, we enrolled 91 patients with CRLM who underwent liver resection in our institution from January 2017 to December 2021 (Fig. 1). This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, which waived the need for obtaining informed consent.

Study flowchart shows the selection of patients. CRLM= colorectal cancer liver metastases, ECA= extracellular contrast agent, LVI = lymphovascular invasion

The inclusion criteria were: (a) Patients underwent preoperative gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI at our hospital, and the interval between the last preoperative MRI and surgery was less than one month. (b) There was at least one measurable liver metastasis with a long diameter ≥ 10 mm on MRI. (c) All liver metastases underwent radical surgery with confirmed R0 margin for resected lesions. (d) Postoperative pathology confirmed CRLM and had assessment of lymphovascular invasion.

The exclusion criteria were: (a) Patients underwent local therapy before surgery. (b) MRI had poor image quality. (c) Patients with identified macrovascular invasion on preoperative MRI. (d) Patients had peri-operative death. (e) Patients who lost to follow-up.

MRI

The gadoxetic acid-enhanced (gadolinium-ethoxybenzyl diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid, Gd-EOB-DTPA) MRI scans were performed using 3.0T (GE Discovery 750, GE Healthcare) and 1.5T (GE OPTIMAL 355, GE Healthcare) MR scanners with an 8-channel phased array coil. The MR sequences included: T2-weighted imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging, T1-weighted in- and opposed-phase imaging and dynamic T1-weighted contrast enhanced imaging.

The gadoxetic acid-enhancement was performed before and after injection of 0.1 ml/kg gadoxetic acid (Primovist, Bayer Schering). The gadoxetic acid-enhanced imaging acquisition protocols were as follows: TR 3.2ms, TE 2ms, FOV 380 × 380 mm, slice thickness 6 mm and slice gap 1 mm. The enhanced imaging was acquired during the pre-contrast phase, arterial phase (25–30 s after injection), portal venous phase (60–80 s after injection), transitional phase (180s after injection) and hepatobiliary phase (HBP) (20 min after injection).

Pathological Examination

Resected lesions were subjected to routine pathological examination and liver lesions were classified as positive or negative for LVI based on resection histopathology reports. The resected specimens of tumor and surrounding tissues were subjected to pathological examination. LVI was defined as the presence of cancer cells arranged in clusters within an endothelial-lined vessel-like structure in the peritumoral tissue, without immunohistochemical staining to distinguish between blood vessels or lymphatic vessels. LVI could be single or multiple, and any positive detection of LVI was recorded as with LVI. All evaluated liver lesions underwent a comparison between MR imaging and pathology.

Image analysis and model construction

We analyzed the imaging features of one lesion from each patient on the last preoperative gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI to construct an imaging model for predicting LVI. For patients with LVI we analyzed the corresponding liver lesion with LVI. And for patients without LVI we analyzed the largest liver lesion of each patient. The analysis and measurement were on Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) and the radiologists were blinded to the pathological results.

The long diameter of liver lesions was measured on the largest transverse axis plane on HBP. The region of interest (ROI) of the targeted lesion was drawn manually on the largest slice along the edge of the target lesion. The ROI of peritumoral expansion region (including the tumor) was drawn manually approximately 5 mm outward expansion from tumor margin, avoiding vessels adjacent to the lesion and extrahepatic tissues. Each ROI of the targeted lesion and peritumoral expansion region was delineated on pre-contrast phase, arterial phase, portal venous phase, transitional phase and HBP, respectively (Fig. 1S). The ROI of paraspinal muscle was drawn on the same slice as the target lesion on each phase as a circle with an area of approximately 10mm2. The mean and standard deviation (SD) of signal intensity (SI) of each ROI were recorded.

All quantitative parameters were measured by two radiologists with 8-year experience (Q.X. and X.L.G.). After confirming the inter-measurement consistency, the average value of the two measurements was recorded. We used the parameters with the optimal AUC in each phase for predicting LVI.

Besides quantitative parameters, we also assessed qualitative imaging features, which were determined according to previous literature and clinical experience [25]: (a) The vascular penetration sign was assessed on the portal venous phase, showing the relative positional relationship between target lesion and intrahepatic venous vessels observed on axial image combined with coronal enhanced image. The liver lesion showing clear demarcation from the intrahepatic veins (Fig. 2a) or the intrahepatic veins running adjacent to the liver lesion (Fig. 2b, c) belongs to the vascular penetration sign negative. The intrahepatic veins extending into the lesions with the distal segment partially visible or invisible belongs to the vascular penetration sign positive (Fig. 2d-f). (b) The peritumoral HBP hypointensity sign was defined as a part or a complete annular hypoenhancing area around the liver lesion on hepatobiliary phase (Fig. 3). (c) T2WI signal: slightly hyperintense/ non-slightly hyperintense. (d) Boundary clarity: well defined/ ill-defined. (e) Margin morphology: smooth/ irregular. (f) Ring enhancement: positive/ negative assessed on the portal venous phase. The qualitative parameters were all assessed by two radiologists (Q.X. and X.L.G.) and disagreements were resolved by a senior abdominal radiologist with more than 20 years of experience (Y.C.).

LVI and survival analysis

The established LVI prediction model was applied to all liver lesions of the total enrolled patients. A patient with at least one liver lesion predicted to be with LVI was classified as predicted LVI-positive. Otherwise, the patient was classified as predicted LVI-negative. Survival analysis was performed based on the above patient stratification.

Follow-up

Patients were regularly followed with CT or MR every 2–3 months after liver resection, and follow-up ended in May 2025. Recurrence was defined as the occurrence of new liver metastases, and liver recurrence-free survival (LRFS) was the time from the date of liver resection to the date of the first recorded recurrence or the last follow-up date. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of liver resection to the date of death or the last follow-up date.

Statistical analysis

SPSS (version 25.0) and MedCalc (version 20.100) were used for statistical analyses.

The continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were expressed as means ± SD and compared with the Student t-test. The continuous variables with a non-normal distribution were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared with Mann-Whitney U test. The categorical variables were expressed as number and compared with chi-square test. Inter-observer agreement for categorical variables was analyzed with Kappa coefficient. The Kappa value of 0.81-1.00, 0.61–0.80, 0.41–0.60 and 0-0.40 represent almost perfect agreement, substantial, moderate, fair or slight agreement. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated for quantitative parameters. The ICC value of 0.9-1.0, 0.75–0.9, 0.5–0.75 and 0-0.5 indicate excellent, good, moderate and poor agreement.

The diagnostic values of imaging features for LVI were evaluated with univariate analysis and variables with p < 0.1 were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. The imaging features with statistical significance screened by multivariate logistic regression were used for model construction. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess collinearity.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was drawn and the area under the curve (AUC) was used to evaluate the predictive efficacy of imaging features in each phase and the constructed model. The optimal cut-off value for constructed model in predicting LVI was determined by the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). DeLong test was used for comparing the performances of imaging features and the constructed model. R software (version 4.5.2) was used to establish a nomogram. Internal validation of the LVI prediction model was performed using the bootstrap resampling method with 1000 iterations.

The postoperative LRFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Survival curves were drawn, and the log-rank test was utilized for comparison. Cox regression model was established to explore the effects of multiple factors on OS. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

There were 34 patients with gene mutation, including 29 with KRAS mutations, 4 with NRAS mutations and 1 with BRAF mutation. No macrovascular invasion was identified on MRI in any patient.

After bootstrap internal validation with 1000 iterations, the bias-corrected AUC was 0.874(95%CI:0.711–0.978, Fig. 2S), indicating good model stability.

Diagnostic performance and subgroup analysis based on treatment regimen

Only 9 patients who underwent surgery without preoperative chemotherapy were classified into the without LVI group. No valid assessment of model performance could be conducted in this subgroup. Eighty-two patients underwent surgery after NACT. The LVI model achieved an AUC of 0.872 (95%CI:0.743-1.000, p < 0.001) in the NACT subgroup, as shown in Fig. 5b, which was comparable to that in the overall cohort, indicating that the model maintained consistent predictive performance in NACT subgroup.

Survival in patients

Overall, 63.7% of patients (58/91) experienced local recurrence of the liver during a median follow-up of 37 months. A total of 199 liver lesions in 91 patients were assessed using the LVI model. Among them, 22 patients were classified as predicted LVI-positive, while 69 patients were classified as predicted LVI-negative.

LRFS was significantly shorter in predicted LVI-positive patients than in predicted LVI-negative patients (median 9.0 vs. 28.0 months, p = 0.028, Fig. 6a). OS was also significantly shorter in predicted LVI-positive patients than in predicted LVI-negative patients (median 21.0 vs. 63.0 months, p = 0.001, Fig. 6b).

Discussion

Macrovascular invasion and microvascular invasion (MVI) are well-established prognostic factors in HCC patients. Many imaging features have been established to predict MVI with great diagnostic value, especially in gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI [9]. However, there is little research on lymphovascular invasion in patients with CRLM, and its clinical significance has been underestimated. Liver metastases with LVI in CRLM patients have similar underlying pathological changes to those in HCC with MVI. Based on previous studies, we have screened out the imaging features that may be related to the occurrence of LVI. By establishing an LVI prediction model, preoperative assessment can be achieved, which may further guide individualized clinical decision-making, including wider surgical margins, more intensive NACT regimens, and optimized postoperative follow-up strategies.

Due to the impaired liver function in the peritumoral region, the uptake of gadoxetic acid is reduced and the peritumoral HBP hypointensity is present [8]. Although peritumoral HBP hypointensity was not an independent predictive factor for LVI in multivariate analysis, the AUC of its independent prediction reached 0.691. Because of LVI, the venous blood flow decreases, neovascularization and compensatory arterial blood flow increases [26, 27], which explains that the RERTumor−A was higher in the with LVI group than the without LVI group (median 163.7% vs. 79.5%, p = 0.002). In addition to functional impairment, the peritumoral region exhibits changes such as edema, fibrosis and inflammation [28]. Thus, the peritumoral signal is more complex, showing as higher SDExpansion−T in the with LVI group than the without LVI group (median 229.3 vs. 154.0, p = 0.04), and was especially prominent in transitional phase. SDTumor−HBP reflects the signal heterogeneity within the tumor, which was higher in the with LVI group than the without LVI group (median 195.6 vs. 116.3, p = 0.024). The tumor with stronger invasiveness had higher heterogeneity [29], and higher likelihood of developing LVI. The vascular penetration sign signifies that the tumor encases the blood vessel with various degrees of adventitial invasion, forming the basis for LVI. The proportion of patients with vascular penetration sign was higher among those with LVI than those without LVI (9/14 vs. 5/72, p < 0.001). Hepatic vessels passing through the neoplasm has been discussed in previous articles [30, 31], it was observed in metastatic adenocarcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma as well as melanoma and considered to be related to the microvascular invasion of CRLM [25]. As the lymphatic vessels are mainly distributed around portal tracts or hepatic veins [32], liver lesions with vascular penetration sign also demonstrate a close relationship with the lymphatic vessels, even though the lymphatic vessels are not visible on MR images. As there were many more lesions without LVI than those with LVI, we selected the largest liver lesion from people with multiple lesions none of which were with LVI, to ensure a balanced distribution between the two groups and to better visualize the imaging features. Because the long diameter was comparable between all liver lesions without LVI and liver lesions with LVI, the long diameter of liver lesions was not included in the multivariate analysis, despite its statistical significance (OR = 0.926, p = 0.045) in the univariate analysis.

The imaging model we constructed for predicting LVI used vascular penetration sign and SDTumor−HBP. It has good efficacy in predicting LVI (AUC = 0.874, p < 0.001), better than other imaging features used alone. The LVI model establishes a linear predictive equation based on logistic regression. Vascular penetration sign shows the relative position between liver lesion and blood vessels and acts as a strong independent predictive factor that could increase the risk of LVI. It is the main effect of risk stratification. While SDTumor−HBP shows the heterogeneity of the liver lesion, which presents a weak positive contribution of LVI. In cases with a negative vascular penetration sign, only when the SDTumor−HBP value reaches 236.25, the LVI model predicts the lesion to be with LVI. The constant shows a low risk of LVI when both predictors remain at low level. This model can address the gaps in the study of radiology on lymphovascular invasion in patients with CRLM.

For patients without LVI, the one-year liver recurrence rate was 29.2% higher than that of patients with LVI. And for predicted LVI-negative patients, the one-year liver recurrence rate was 24.9% higher than that of predicted LVI-positive patients. Blood vessel invasion is considered to be the initial step of hematogenous metastasis. Lymph-vessel invasion has also been reported to be a strong independent prognostic factor in patients with CRLM [20, 33]. Identification of LVI would enable us to identify a subgroup of patients who should get more intensive treatment regimens and follow-up. And the treatment regimens for patients with LVI require further exploration and optimization.

This study had some limitations. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. Independent external validation is still required to confirm its generalizability before any clinical application. Accordingly, future studies will aim to expand the sample size and conduct external validation using independent cohorts from multiple centers. Second, due to the retrospective nature of this study, immunohistochemical staining was not performed in pathological evaluation to distinguish between blood vessels invasion and lymphatic vessels invasion. Future subgroup analyses should be conducted to further explore the impact of vascular invasion and lymphatic vessel invasion. Third, the interobserver agreement was moderate in peritumoral HBP hypointensity and vascular penetration sign. As preoperative chemotherapy-induced liver injury manifests as hypointensity on HBP phase, which affects the observation for the peritumoral hypointensity. And compared with extracellular contrast agents, Gd-EOB-DTPA provides lower visualization of vascular enhancement in dynamic phases, which may reduce the interobserver agreement of vascular penetration sign. Therefore, the imaging features of extracellular contrast agent-enhanced MRI remain to be explored. Last, the scanner variability remains in different MR devices, although we have standardized scanning protocols and used relative enhancement rate instead of signal intensity.

Conclusion

Based on gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI, we built an imaging model using vascular penetration sign and SDTumor−HBP for predicting LVI. The imaging model had good predictive value for LVI. Predicted LVI status of the patient and CA19-9 are independent risk factors for OS.
