Introduction

For
q\geq 2
, each integer
n
can be uniquely written as

n=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}n_{k}q^{k},

where

n_{k}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}
for all
k\geq 0
are the digits which are zero starting from some finite rank. The sum-of-digits function
s_{q}
is a well-studied object in number theory, it is defined for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
by

s_{q}(n)=s_{q}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}n_{k}q^{k}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}n_{k}.

Although quite simple to define, the sum-of-digits function has raised many questions. A natural question that could be asked is how this function distributes itself within arithmetic progressions. In an influential paper, Gelfond
[
10
]
provided a new method to study the sum-of-digits function via exponential sums and product representations.
He obtained the following result concerning the distribution of the sum-of-digits function in arithmetic progressions.

Theorem A

(Gelfond, 1967/68)
.

Let
q,b,m\geq 2
be integers such that
(b,q-1)=1
. Then, for all
a\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}
and for all
r\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
, we have

|\{n<N\,:\,s_{q}(n)\equiv a\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b),\ n\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)\}|=\dfrac{N}{bm}+O(N^{\lambda}),

where

\lambda=\dfrac{1}{2\log(q)}\log\dfrac{q\sin(\pi/2b)}{\sin(\pi/2bq)}<1.

Remark 1.1
.

Rebuilding on work of Morgenbesser, Shallit and Stoll
[
18
]
it can be shown that there exist infinitely many
m\geq 1
such that

\min\{n\geq 1\,:\,s_{q}(n)\equiv 1\pmod{b},\ n\equiv 0\pmod{m}\}\geq c_{b}m^{1/(b-1)}
, for some
c_{b}>0
only depending on
b
(one may consider
m=q^{\ell(b-1)}+q^{\ell(b-2)}+\cdots+q^{\ell}+1
, for instance).
Therefore, the implied constant cannot be independent of
m
(at least for the Thue-Morse sequence). This dependence of the constant on the modulus
m
is the object of our Bombieri-Vinogradov-type average result, see Theorem
1.3
.

In addition to his results in
[
10
]
, Gelfond proposed several open questions related to the sum-of-digits function. In particular, can we substitute in Theorem
A
,
s_{q}(n)
by
s_{q}(p)
for
p
primes or by
s_{q}(P(n))
where
P
is a polynomial
P
such that
P(n)\in\mathbb{N}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
?
These questions have been addressed in many recent works.

The most emblematic instance of a sequence related to the sum-of-digits function is the (Prouhet–)Thue–Morse sequence defined (for instance) in its multiplicative way by

t(n)=(-1)^{s_{2}(n)}=\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}s_{2}(n)\right),

where
s_{2}(n)
is the sum of the binary digits of the integer
n
, and
\mathrm{e}(t)=e^{2\pi it}
. The Thue–Morse sequence is a fundamental object in various areas of mathematics, and in particular in number theory and combinatorics on words (see
[
1
]
for an overview). Mauduit and Rivat
[
17
]
solved Gelfond’s problem on the distribution of
s_{q}(p)
,
p
primes, in arithmetic progressions. In the proof of their breakthrough result, the authors introduced several new tools, and most notably an ingenious use of the van der Corput inequality paired with a “carry propogation lemma”, which, simply put, states that in the addition of a large and a small integer expressed in base-
q
, the highly significant digits of the large number are rarely affected by the addition. In respect of Gelfond’s problem, we cite the result of Drmota, Mauduit and Rivat
[
6
]
, who showed that the Thue–Morse sequence is normal along squares.

Spiegelhofer
[
21
]
observed that an iterative use of the van der Corput inequality followed by several digits shiftings leads to Gowers norms. He then was able to use the estimate of Gowers norms for the Thue–Morse provided by Konieczny in
[
13
]
to conclude. Bounds on these norms have far-reaching applications (see, for instance,
[
22
]
for an application for the sum of digits of cubes).

1.1
Results on the level of distribution of the sum of digits

Fouvry and Mauduit
[
9
]
started the investigation of the level of distribution of the Thue–Morse sequence and its generalizations along arithmetic progressions. For

two integers such that
(b,q-1)=1

A_{q}(y,z;a,b,r,m)=\Big|\{y\leq n<z\,:\,s_{q}(n)\equiv a\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b),\;\;n\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)\}\Big|.

The paper
[
9
]
is devoted to the base 2.

Theorem B

(Fouvry/Mauduit, 1996)
.

Let
C_{b}
be a constant such that for all

we have

\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{0\leq n<N}|\cos(\pi(2^{n}t+\xi))|dt=O(C_{b}^{N}),\qquad N\rightarrow\infty.

Let

\gamma(\alpha)=1+\dfrac{\log\left(\beta(\alpha)\right)}{\log 2},

\beta(\alpha):=\sqrt{\max_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\cos(\pi\left(t+\alpha\right))\cos(\pi(2t+\alpha))|},

and set

\gamma_{b}=\max\{\gamma(\alpha)\;:\;\mathrm{e}(\alpha)^{b}=1\;,\;\alpha\not\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

For

, we set

. Then, as

,

\sum_{1\leq m\leq D}\max_{1\leq z\leq x}\;\max_{0\leq r<m}\Big|A_{2}(0,z;a,b,r,m)-\dfrac{z}{bm}\Big|=O\left(x^{1+\log(C_{b})/\log(2)}D+x^{\gamma_{b}}\left(D^{3-2\gamma_{b}+2\log(C_{b})/\log(2)}+\log x\right)\right).

Theorem

allowed them to show that

is a level of distribution of the Thue–Morse sequence

. More precisely, they showed in
[
9
]
the following theorem:

Theorem C

(Fouvry/Mauduit, 1996)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}
and
D=x^{0.5924}
. There exists

such that

\sum_{1\leq m\leq D}\max_{1\leq z\leq x}\;\max_{0\leq r<m}\Big|A_{2}(0,z;0,2,r,m)-\dfrac{z}{2m}\Big|\leq Cx(\log 2x)^{-A},\qquad x\to\infty.

They also investigated the level of distribution of the sequence
(s_{q}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for general base of numeration
q
. In their paper
[
8
, p.340]
they proved the following result.

Theorem D

(Fouvry/Mauduit, 1996)
.

Let
q\geq 2
,

,

be integers such that
(b,q-1)=1
. Then, for all
x\geq 1
, for all

and for all

\sum_{1\leq m\leq x^{\theta_{q}-\varepsilon}}\max_{1\leq z\leq x}\;\max_{0\leq r<m}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n<y\\
s_{q}(n)\equiv a\ (\mathrm{mod}\ b)\\

where

is defined by

\theta_{q}=1-\dfrac{\log(M(q))}{\log(q)},

where

M(q)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\cos\left(\frac{2k+1}{4n}\pi)\right)^{-1},&\text{if }q=2n;\\
\dfrac{1}{2n+1}\left(1+2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\cos\left(\frac{k}{2n+1}\pi\right)^{-1}\right),&\text{if }q=2n+1.\end{cases}

Moreover,

for

Spiegelhofer
[
21
]
improved largely Theorem

, which can be seen as an optimal result. This means that for all

there exists

such that

\sum_{1\leq m\leq x^{1-\varepsilon}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,z\geq 0\\

(1)

Since

for all

[

recalled below

Theorem E

(Spiegelhofer, 2020)
.

Let

. There exists

such that

\sum_{1\leq m\leq x^{1-\varepsilon}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,z\geq 0\\
z-y\leq x\end{subarray}}\max_{0\leq r<m}\Big|A(y,z;r,m)-\dfrac{z-y}{2m}\Big|\ll x^{1-\eta},\qquad x\to\infty,

where

A(y,z;r,m)=\Big|\{y\leq n<z\,:\,t(n)=0,\;\;n\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)\}\Big|.

The very remarkable part in this theorem is that

Explicit results existed before the work of Spiegelhofer. In 2014, Martin, Mauduit, and Rivat

Let

(2)

The number

2.3
The van der Corput inequality

As in previous articles on the sum-of-digits function, the van der Corput inequality and its variations play a major role. The original version can for example be found in the book of Graham/Kolesnik
[
11
, (2.3.4)]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
0\leq a<b
be two integers and set
I=[a,b[
. Let
f\,:\,\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a complex-valued function such that
f(n)=0
for
n\notin I
.
Let
0<H\leq|I|
be an integer. We have

\Big|\sum_{n\in I}\mathrm{e}(f(n))\Big|^{2}\leq 2\dfrac{|I|^{2}}{H}+4\dfrac{|I|}{H}\sum_{1\leq h<H}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n+h\in I\end{subarray}}\mathrm{e}(f(n+h)-f(n))\Big|.

Mauduit and Rivat
[
15
, Lemme 17]
generalized the van der Corput inequality for general shifts, and showed that it can be used, in an elegant manner, to remove lower-placed digits. As we will see later, removing iteratively windows of digits via this inequality, allows to get a small window of digits only to consider in the end. For the purpose of handling better these iterations, we rewrite their inequality with a single sum over all
h\in\{0,\dots,H-1\}
, without isolating the diagonal term (
h=0
).

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
0\leq a<b
be two integers and set
I=[a,b[
. Let
f\,:\,\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a complex valued function such that
f(n)=0
for
n\notin I
. Let
K>0
be an integer.
Let
0<H\leq|I|
be an integer. We have

\Big|\sum_{n\in I}\mathrm{e}(f(n))\Big|^{2}\leq 2\dfrac{|I|+K(H-1)}{H}\sum_{0\leq h<H}\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n+Kh\in I\end{subarray}}\mathrm{e}(f(n+Kh)-f(n))\Big|.

The first lemma will be used when we begin to perform many digits shiftings, in order to remove a large portion of the digits of the integers and to be able to concentrate our attention to a small window of digits only (see

Lemma 3.1

(Cutting out digits)
.

Let
\alpha,u_{0},u_{1}\geq 0
be integers. Let
\beta>\alpha.
We have the equality

s_{q}^{{\beta}}(u_{0}+q^{\alpha}u_{1})-s_{q}^{{\beta}}(u_{0})=s_{q}^{{\alpha},{\beta}}(u_{0}+q^{\alpha}u_{1})-s_{q}^{{\alpha},{\beta}}(u_{0}).

Proof.

By the definition of

, we have

s_{q}^{{\alpha},{\beta}}(u_{0}+q^{\alpha}u_{1})-s_{q}^{{\alpha},{\beta}}(u_{0})=s_{q}^{{\beta}}(u_{0}+q^{\alpha}u_{1})-s_{q}^{{\beta}}(u_{0})-(s_{q}^{{\alpha}}(u_{0}+q^{\alpha}u_{1})-s_{q}^{{\alpha}}(u_{0})),

and we observe that
s_{q}^{{\alpha}}(u_{0}+q^{\alpha}u_{1})-s_{q}^{{\alpha}}(u_{0})=0

.

The next Lemma allows us to justify the stability of the recursion formula when we work with

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

. For all

s_{q}^{{\rho}}(n)=s_{q}^{{\rho-1}}\left(\left\lfloor\dfrac{n}{q}\right\rfloor\right)+u_{q}(n).

The following property is useful to transform the sum-of-digits function

into a periodic function

, at the expense of a manageable error. This transformation will occur during the first iteration of the van der Corput inequality. Mauduit and Rivat
[

have used this method in their resolution of the problem of Gelfond regarding the distribution of

along primes. It has since then be applied in various contexts and shown to be very useful. The following version is a generalization of
[
21
, Lemma 4.5]
. Note that we will only use the case

but we state the more general (Beatty sequence) version since the proof is not more difficult and the analogy to

]
is more apparent in that way.

Lemma 3.3

(Carry propagation lemma)
.

Let
r>0
,
\lambda\geq 1
,
N\geq 1
be integers and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha>0
and
\beta\geq 0
. Let
I\subset[0,+\infty[
be an interval in
\mathbb{R}
containing exactly
N
consecutive integers.
Then

\Big|\Big\{n\in I\ :\ s_{q}(\lfloor\alpha(n+r)+\beta\rfloor)-s_{q}(\lfloor\alpha n+\beta\rfloor)\neq s_{q}^{{\lambda}}(\lfloor\alpha(n+r)+\beta\rfloor)-s_{q}^{{\lambda}}(\lfloor\alpha n+\beta\rfloor)\Big\}\Big|<r\left(\dfrac{N\alpha}{q^{\lambda}}+2\right).

(11)

Proof.

If
r\alpha\geq q^{\lambda}
then
r\left(\dfrac{N\alpha}{q^{\lambda}}+2\right)\geq N
and the result follows immediately. We may therefore suppose that
r\alpha<q^{\lambda}
. Let
n\in I
such that
n\alpha+\beta\in[kq^{\lambda},(k+1)q^{\lambda}[
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
that we will make precise later.

If
n\alpha+\beta+r\alpha\in[kq^{\lambda},(k+1)q^{\lambda}[
, then
n
is not in the set involved on the left hand side of (
11
).
We therefore only need to consider the integers
n
such that

n\alpha+\beta+r\alpha\geq(k+1)q^{\lambda}.

This second condition (combined with the condition
n\alpha+\beta\in[kq^{\lambda},(k+1)q^{\lambda}]
) allows us to estimate
n
. We obtain

\dfrac{(k+1)q^{\lambda}-\beta-r\alpha}{\alpha}\leq n<\dfrac{(k+1)q^{\lambda}-\beta}{\alpha},

and the number of such integers is therefore bounded by

\dfrac{(k+1)q^{\lambda}-\beta}{\alpha}-\dfrac{(k+1)q^{\lambda}-\beta-r\alpha}{\alpha}=r.

Finally, we need to bound the number of possible
k
. Write
I=[a_{1},a_{2}[
. Since
n\leq a_{2}
, we have

a_{2}\alpha+\beta+r\alpha\geq(k+1)q^{\lambda},

which implies

k\leq\dfrac{a_{2}\alpha+\beta+r\alpha}{q^{\lambda}}-1.

Moreover, we have

(k+1)q^{\lambda}>a_{1}\alpha+\beta,

and

k>\dfrac{a_{1}\alpha+\beta}{q^{\lambda}}-1.

Therefore, the number of possible
k
is bounded by

\dfrac{(a_{2}-a_{1})\alpha+r\alpha}{q^{\lambda}}\leq\dfrac{(N+1)\alpha}{q^{\lambda}}+1<\dfrac{N\alpha}{q^{\lambda}}+2,

where in the last step we used
\alpha\leq r\alpha<q^{\lambda}
. This concludes the proof.

∎

The following lemma provides a bound on the number of

such that

is close to an integer. It is the analogue in base-

[

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
N>1
be an integer,
\sigma>1
and

such that
H<q^{\sigma-1}
. Moreover, let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

|\{n\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}\ :\ \|n\alpha+\beta\|<H/q^{\sigma}\}|\leq ND_{N}(\alpha)+\dfrac{2HN}{q^{\sigma}}.

Proof.

This follows by elementary interval translations and the definition of the discrepancy, i.e.

\displaystyle|\{n\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}\ :\ \|n\alpha+\beta\|<H/q^{\sigma}\}|

\displaystyle=|\{n\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}\ :\ n\alpha+\beta\in(\mathbb{Z}+[-H/q^{\sigma},H/q^{\sigma}])\}|,

\displaystyle=|\{n\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}\ :\ n\alpha\in(\mathbb{Z}+[0,2H/q^{\sigma}]-\beta-H/q^{\sigma})\}|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2HN}{q^{\sigma}}+ND_{N}(\alpha).

∎

w_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and
\mathbf{w}=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{k-1})
the vector of the binary digits of the integer
w
(we will often switch from the vector representation to the integer without giving further indication.) We denote

t_{q}^{(w)}(n)=\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{s_{2}(w)}\dfrac{\ell}{b}s_{q}(n)\right).

(18)

We observe that (
18
) is a generalization of (
4
). The factor
(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}
appears naturally in the context of Gowers norms, as we will see later.
Let
\mathbf{r_{0}}=(r_{0,w})_{w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}
. For
\rho\geq 0
, we consider

A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}})=\dfrac{1}{q^{(k+1)\rho}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq n<q^{\rho}\\
\mathbf{h}=(h_{0},\ldots,h_{k-1})\in\{0,\dots,q^{\rho}-1\}^{k}\end{subarray}}\prod_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}t_{q}^{(w)}(n+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h}+r_{0,w}),

(19)

where
\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h}
is the usual dot-product defined by

\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{i}h_{i}.

In the first step we establish a recurrence formula for
A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}})
based on the
q
-multiplicativity of
(t_{q}^{(w)}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. This recurrence relation can be interpreted as a recurrence relation of the type

X_{\rho}=MX_{\rho-1},

where
X_{\rho}
is a vector and
M
a matrix such that
|M|
is stochastic.
This will lead us to examine the underlying “probabilistic graph” related to
M
. For the first step, we follow the argument given by Konieczny in the proof of
[
13
, Lemma 2.1, p.1902]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
k\geq 3
be an integer. For any fixed
\mathbf{r_{0}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}
, the sequence
(A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}}))_{\rho\geq 1}
satisfies the recursion formula

\displaystyle A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}})=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{q^{k+1}}\;\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}\sum\limits_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}r_{0,w}\right)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{e}=(e_{0},\ldots,e_{k})\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}^{k+1}}\end{subarray}}A(\rho-1,\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)})\;\mathrm{e}\left(-\dfrac{q\ell}{b}\sum\limits_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}\delta(\mathbf{r_{0}};\mathbf{e})_{w}\right),

where
\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}=(\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}_{w})_{w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}}

\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}
is the vector defined by

\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}_{w}=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{r_{0,w}+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}}{q}\Big\rfloor,

(20)

for all
w\in\{0,\ldots,2^{k}-1\}
, with the shorthand notation
(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}=e_{0}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}w_{i-1}e_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
0\leq n<q^{\rho}
and
h=(h_{0},\ldots,h_{k-1})
with

0\leq h_{i}<q^{\rho}
for
0\leq i\leq k-1
. Using the Euclidean division, there exists a unique
(2k+2)
-tuple of integers
(n^{\prime},h_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,h_{k-1}^{\prime},e_{0}^{\prime},\ldots,e_{k}^{\prime})
satisfying
0\leq n^{\prime}<q^{\rho-1}
,
0\leq h^{\prime}_{i}<q^{\rho-1}
(
1\leq i\leq k-1)
and
0\leq e^{\prime}_{i}<q
(
0\leq i\leq k
) and such that

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle=qn^{\prime}+e_{0},

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle=qh^{\prime}_{0}+e_{1},

\displaystyle h_{k-1}

\displaystyle=qh^{\prime}_{k-1}+e_{k}.

Summing up, for all
\mathbf{w}=(w_{0},\dots,w_{k-1})
, we have

\displaystyle n+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h}=q(n^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h^{\prime}})+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}.

By the uniqueness of the parameters involved in the Euclidean division, we then observe that

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=\{n+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h}\,|\,w\in\{0,\ldots,2^{k}-1\}

\displaystyle=\{q(n^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h^{\prime}})+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}\,|\,w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}\}

is in bijection with the cube

Q^{\prime}=\{n^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h^{\prime}}\,|\,w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}\}.

By the definition of
A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}})
(see (
19
)),

A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}})=\dfrac{1}{q^{(k+1)\rho}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq n^{\prime}<q^{\rho-1}\\
\mathbf{h^{\prime}}=(h^{\prime}_{0},\dots,h^{\prime}_{k-1})\in\{0,\dots,q^{\rho-1}-1\}^{k}\\
\mathbf{e}=(e_{0},\ldots,e_{k})\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}^{k+1}\end{subarray}}\prod_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}t_{q}^{(w)}(q(n^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h^{\prime}})+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+r_{0,w}).

(21)

For all
0\leq n^{\prime}<q^{\rho-1}
,
\mathbf{h^{\prime}}\in\{0,\dots,q^{\rho-1}-1\}^{k}
and
\mathbf{e}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1}
, we recall that

t_{q}^{(w)}(q(n^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h^{\prime}})+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+r_{0,w})=\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{s_{2}(w)}\dfrac{\ell}{b}s_{q}(q(n^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h^{\prime}})+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+r_{0,w})\right).

Since for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

s_{q}(n)=s_{q}\left(\Big\lfloor\dfrac{n}{q}\Big\rfloor\right)+u_{q}(n),

(22)

where
u_{q}(n)
is the unit place digit of
n
in base-
q
,
we obtain

\displaystyle t_{q}^{(w)}(q(n^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h^{\prime}})+

\displaystyle(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+r_{0,w})=

(23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{s_{2}(w)}\dfrac{\ell}{b}(s_{q}(n^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h^{\prime}}+\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}_{w})+u_{q}((1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+r_{0,w}))\right),

where

\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}_{w}=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{r_{0,w}+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}}{q}\Big\rfloor.

Inserting the identity (
23
) into (
21
), we get

\displaystyle A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}})=\dfrac{1}{q^{(k+1)\rho}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq n^{\prime}<q^{\rho-1}\\
\mathbf{h^{\prime}}=(h^{\prime}_{0},\dots,h^{\prime}_{k-1})\in\{0,\dots,q^{\rho-1}-1\}^{k}\\
\mathbf{e}=(e_{0},\ldots,e_{k})\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}^{k+1}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\prod_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{s_{2}(w)}\dfrac{\ell}{b}u_{q}((1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+r_{0,w})\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}t_{q}^{(w)}(n^{\prime}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{h^{\prime}}+\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}_{w}).

Since the first product is independent of
n^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{h^{\prime}}
, it follows that

A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}})=\dfrac{1}{q^{k+1}}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{e}=(e_{0},\ldots,e_{k})\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}^{k+1}}\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}S(\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{e})\right)A(\rho-1,\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}),

(24)

where

S(\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{e})=\sum_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}u_{q}((1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+r_{0,w}).

We note that Lemma
3.2
provides the same recursion formula (
24
) when we handle
s_{q}^{{\rho}}
instead of
s_{q}
.
Until the end of the proof, we will only consider
s_{q}
knowing that we can replace any occurrence of
s_{q}
by
s_{q}^{{\rho}}
.

We now rewrite the sum
S(\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{e})
in a more convenient way. By (
20
),

u_{q}((1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+r_{0,w})=(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+r_{0,w}-q\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}_{w}

and therefore

\displaystyle S(\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{e})

\displaystyle=\sum_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}((1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+r_{0,w}-q\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}_{w})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}(r_{0,w}-q\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}_{w})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
2\nmid s_{2}(w)\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
2\mid s_{2}(w)\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}.

We claim that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
2\mid s_{2}(w)\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
2\nmid s_{2}(w)\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}.

To see this, we write

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
2\nmid s_{2}(w)\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}

\displaystyle=e_{0}\,|\{0\leq w<2^{k}\,:\,2\nmid s_{2}(w)\}|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{w}=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{k-1})\in\{0,1\}^{k}\\
2\nmid\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{i}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{r=1}^{k}w_{r-1}e_{r}

\displaystyle=e_{0}\,|\{0\leq w<2^{k}\,:\,2\nmid s_{2}(w)\}|+\sum_{r=1}^{k}e_{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{w}=(w_{0},\ldots,w_{k-1})\in\{0,1\}^{k}\\
2\nmid\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k-1}w_{i}\end{subarray}}w_{r-1}

\displaystyle=e_{0}\,|\{0\leq w<2^{k}\,:\,2\nmid s_{2}(w)\}|+\sum_{r=1}^{k}e_{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{0},\ldots,w_{k-1}\in\{0,1\}\\
w_{r-1}=1\\
2\mid\sum\limits_{i\neq r-1}w_{i}\end{subarray}}1.

(25)

Analogously, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
2\mid s_{2}(w)\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}=e_{0}|\;\{0\leq w<2^{k}\,:\,2\mid s_{2}(w)\}|+\sum_{r=1}^{k}e_{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w_{0},\ldots,w_{k-1}\in\{0,1\}\\
w_{r-1}=1\\
2\nmid\sum\limits_{i\neq r-1}w_{i}\end{subarray}}1.

(26)

We notice that the sets
\{0\leq w<2^{k}\,:\,2\mid s_{2}(w)\}
and
\{0\leq w<2^{k}\,:\,2\nmid s_{2}(w)\}
are in bijection. Indeed, when we switch one fixed binary digit of
w
, we pass from one set to the other (or, equivalently, we can use
\sum_{0\leq j<2^{k}}(-1)^{s_{2}(j)}=0
).
A similar digit switching argument (noticing that
k\geq 2
)
works to show that for fixed
r
both inner sums in (
25
) and (
26
) are equal.

We therefore get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
2\mid s_{2}(w)\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
2\nmid s_{2}(w)\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}

and

S(\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{e})=\sum\limits_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}(r_{0,w}-q\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}_{w}).

Plugging this into (
24
) we get
the stated recursion formula.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

For the case
b=q=2
, which corresponds to the Thue–Morse sequence, the recursion formula in Lemma
4.1
becomes

A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}})=\dfrac{(-1)^{|\mathbf{r_{0}}|}}{2^{k+1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{e}=(e_{0},\ldots,e_{k-1})\in\{0,1\}^{k+1}}\end{subarray}}A(\rho-1,\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}),

where
|\mathbf{r_{0}}|:=\sum_{w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k-1}\}}r_{0,w}
.
This is the same formula as in the article of Konieczny
[
13
, (2.1)]
and of Spiegelhofer
[
21
, (5.13)]
. We note that in our more general case, the corresponding formula is more involved since there are additional multiplicative weights (
b
th roots of unity) attached to the sum and the arguments. Somewhat surprisingly, these
b
th roots of unity are connected to the binary digit sum of the summation index
w
.

In order to have a summation ranging over the whole set
\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}
, we
gather the
\mathbf{e}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1}
such that
\mathbf{r_{1}}=\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}
. The formula in Lemma
4.1
becomes a recursion with normalized weights attached to its terms.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
\mathbf{r_{0}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}
, the sequence
(A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}}))_{\rho>0}
satisfies the recursion formula

A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}})=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{r_{1}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}}A(\rho-1,\mathbf{r_{1}})p_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{1}}},

(27)

where

p_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{1}}}=\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}\left(q\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r_{1}})-\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r_{0}})\right)\right)\dfrac{|\{\mathbf{e}\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}^{k+1}\,:\,\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}=\mathbf{r_{1}}\}|}{q^{k+1}},

(28)

with

\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r})=-\sum\limits_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}r_{w}

for

\mathbf{r}=(r_{w})_{w\in\{0,\ldots,2^{k}-1\}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}.

The vector
\mathbf{r}=(r_{w})_{w\in\{0,1\}^{k}}
such that
r_{w}=0
for all
w\in\{0,\ldots,2^{k}-1\}
will be written as

\mathbf{0}:=(0,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}.

The recursion formula (
27
) and the weights that can be seen as transition complex random variables, suggests to consider a directed graph modelled in the set
\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}
consisting of the vectors
\mathbf{r}
as vertices and being connected to each other whenever a specific condition on the associated weights is verified: there is an edge between
\mathbf{r_{0}}
and
\mathbf{r_{1}}
if and only if there exists
\mathbf{e}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1}
such that

\mathbf{\delta(r_{0};e)}=\mathbf{r_{1}}.

A vertex
\mathbf{r_{1}}=(r_{1,w})_{w}
is said to be reachable from a vertex
\mathbf{r_{0}}
if there exists
d>0
and
\mathbf{r^{(1)}},\dots,\mathbf{r^{(d)}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}
such that

p_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r^{(1)}}}\times p_{\mathbf{r^{(1)}},\mathbf{r^{(2)}}}\times\cdots\times p_{\mathbf{r^{(d-1)}},\mathbf{r^{(d)}}}\times p_{\mathbf{r^{(d)}},\mathbf{r_{1}}}\neq 0.

We write

\mathbf{r_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbf{r^{(1)}}\rightarrow\mathbf{r^{(2)}}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\mathbf{r^{(d)}}\rightarrow\mathbf{r_{1}},

whenever
\mathbf{r_{1}}
is reachable from
\mathbf{r_{0}}
via
\mathbf{r^{(1)}},\mathbf{r^{(2)}},\dots,\mathbf{r^{(d)}}
. We now turn our attention to the graph built by the elements
\mathbf{r_{1}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}
reachable from
\mathbf{0}
. The following result shows that this is a finite subgraph in above graph.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{R}
be the set of elements
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}
reachable from
\mathbf{0}
. Then
\mathfrak{R}
is finite and for
\mathbf{r}=(r_{w})_{w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}}
reachable from
\mathbf{0}
and for
0\leq w<2^{k}
we have

0\leq r_{w}\leq s_{2}(w).

Furthermore,

|\mathfrak{R}|\leq\prod_{i=0}^{k}(i+1)^{\binom{k}{i}}.

Proof.

We suppose that

\mathbf{0}\rightarrow\mathbf{r^{(1)}}\rightarrow\mathbf{r^{(2)}}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\mathbf{r^{(d)}}\rightarrow\mathbf{r_{1}}.

Since
p_{\mathbf{r^{(d)}},\mathbf{r_{1}}}\neq 0
, there exists an element
\mathbf{e^{(d)}}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1}
such that for all
w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\},

\delta(\mathbf{r^{(d)}};\mathbf{e^{(d)}})_{w}=\Bigg\lfloor\dfrac{r^{(d)}_{w}+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e^{(d)}}}{q}\Bigg\rfloor=r_{1,w}.

In the same way, when
d\geq 2
and if
p_{\mathbf{r^{(d-1)}},\mathbf{r^{(d)}}}\neq 0
, we deduce the existence of
\mathbf{e^{(d-1)}}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1}
such that

r_{1,w}=\Bigg\lfloor\dfrac{\Big\lfloor\dfrac{r^{(d-1)}_{w}+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e^{(d-1)}}}{q}\Big\rfloor+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e^{(d)}}}{q}\Bigg\rfloor.

For all
\mathbf{e}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1}
and all
0\leq w<2^{k}
we have the simple bound

(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e}\leq(q-1)(1+s_{2}(w)).

Iterating the process above, we will reach the point
\mathbf{0}
, after a finite number of steps. We therefore have the bound

r_{1,w}<(q-1)(1+s_{2}(w))\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{q^{r}}=1+s_{2}(w)

and we get
r_{1,w}\leq s_{2}(w)
. Moreover, we notice that for all
w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}
, we have

r^{(1)}_{w}=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e^{(0)}}}{q}\Big\rfloor\geq 0,

and therefore
r^{(2)}_{w},\ldots,r^{(d)}_{w},r_{1,w}\geq 0
for all
w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}
. This implies that the set
\mathfrak{R}
of the vertices reachable from
\mathbf{0}
is finite and for all
\mathbf{r}=(r_{w})_{w}\in\mathfrak{R}
we have

0\leq r_{w}\leq s_{2}(w).

For the cardinality of
\mathfrak{R}
, we observe that
for any
\mathbf{r}=(r_{w})_{w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}}\in\mathfrak{R}
and any fixed
0\leq i\leq k
, there are
\binom{k}{i}
choices of elements
w
such that
s_{2}(w)=i
. Thus, there are
(i+1)^{\binom{k}{i}}
possible choices for the coordinates of
\mathbf{r}
indexed by a number
w
such that
s_{2}(w)=i
, leading to the stated bound.
∎

In addition to the finiteness, our graph is strongly connected. To prove this, we introduce a new notation.

For
\mathbf{e}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1}
, we define the function
\delta_{\mathbf{e}}\,:\,\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2^{k}}
by

\delta_{\mathbf{e}}(\mathbf{r})=\delta(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{e}).

Lemma 4.5
.

For
K:=\left\lfloor\dfrac{\log(k)}{\log(q)}\right\rfloor+1
we have for all
\mathbf{r}\in\mathfrak{R}
:

\underbrace{(\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\circ\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{\mathbf{0}})}_{K\,\text{times}}(\mathbf{r})=\mathbf{0}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{r}\in\mathfrak{R}
and
w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}
. Then

0\leq\mathbf{\delta_{0}(\mathbf{r})}_{w}=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{r_{w}}{q}\Big\rfloor\leq\dfrac{r_{w}}{q}.

Thus,

0\leq\mathbf{\delta_{0}}(\mathbf{\delta_{0}}(\mathbf{r}))_{w}\leq\dfrac{r_{w}}{q^{2}}.

By induction on the number of iterations of
\mathbf{\delta_{0}}
, and using the fact that
r_{w}\leq s_{2}(w)\leq k
, we have

0\leq\underbrace{(\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\circ\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{\mathbf{0}})}_{K\,\text{times}}(\mathbf{r})_{w}\leq\dfrac{k}{q^{K}}.

Since
k<q^{K}
and
\underbrace{(\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\circ\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{\mathbf{0}})}_{K\,\text{times}}(\mathbf{r})_{w}\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\underbrace{(\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\circ\delta_{\mathbf{0}}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{\mathbf{0}})}_{K\,\text{times}}(\mathbf{r})_{w}=0.

∎

Lemma
4.5
assures us that after applying
K
times the operator
\mathbf{\delta_{0}}
to any vertex of the graph, we inevitably end up at vertex
\mathbf{0}
(this is sometimes also described as the property that the graph is
synchronizing
).

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{1}}\in\mathfrak{R}
. Then,
\mathbf{r_{1}}
is reachable from
\mathbf{r_{0}}
.

Proof.

Lemma
4.5
allows us to reach
\mathbf{0}
in a finite number of steps. Then, the definition of the graph enables us to reach
\mathbf{r_{1}}
in a finite number of steps from
\mathbf{0}
. We can thus concatenate the two previous paths and connect
\mathbf{r_{0}}
to
\mathbf{r_{1}}
.
∎

We can now interpret formula (
27
) in Lemma
4.3
in a recursive way in our graph setting. For
1\leq j\leq\rho
, and for
\mathbf{r_{0}}\in\mathfrak{R}
, we have

A(\rho,\mathbf{r_{0}})=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{r_{j}}\in\mathfrak{R}}A(\rho-j,\mathbf{r_{j}})p^{(j)}_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{j}}},

(29)

where
p^{(j)}_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{j}}}
denotes the sum of all weights of paths connecting
\mathbf{r_{0}}
to
\mathbf{r_{j}}
of length
j
. Recall that the weight
p(\gamma)
of a path
\gamma
connecting
\mathbf{r_{0}}
to
\mathbf{r_{j}}
,

\gamma\ :\ \mathbf{r_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbf{r^{(1)}}\rightarrow\mathbf{r^{(2)}}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\mathbf{r^{(d)}}\rightarrow\mathbf{r_{j}}

is defined by

p(\gamma)=p_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r^{(1)}}}\times p_{\mathbf{r^{(1)}},\mathbf{r^{(2)}}}\times\cdots\times p_{\mathbf{r^{(d-1)}},\mathbf{r^{(d)}}}\times p_{\mathbf{r^{(d)}},\mathbf{r_{j}}}.

Since
\mathfrak{R}
is finite, the quantity
v_{\rho}=\max\limits_{\mathbf{r}\in\mathfrak{R}}(|A(\rho,\mathbf{r})|)<+\infty
is well-defined. We obtain, for all
1\leq j\leq\rho
,

v_{\rho}\leq v_{\rho-j}\max\limits_{\mathbf{r_{0}}\in\mathfrak{R}}\left(\sum\limits_{\mathbf{r_{j}}\in\mathfrak{R}}|p^{(j)}_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{j}}}|\right).

(30)

In order to proceed with the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we will show in the next section that there exists an integer
j\geq 1
such that

\sum\limits_{\mathbf{r_{j}}\in\mathfrak{R}}|p^{(j)}_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{j}}}|<1

(31)

for all
\mathbf{r_{0}}\in\mathfrak{R}
.

4.2
An effective value of the exponent
\eta_{0}
in Theorem
1.3

The aim of this section is to prove (
31
), and, more precisely, to provide an effective value for
\eta_{0}
. This allows us to get the effective version of Theorem
1.3
with an explicit value for
\eta_{0}
in the end.
We will show that there exists
j\geq 1
such that

4.2.1
Trivial path and Konieczny’s path

As for the

(

\mathbf{0}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{0}}\mathbf{0}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{0}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\mathbf{0}}\mathbf{0}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{0}}\mathbf{0}\xrightarrow{\mathbf{0}}\mathbf{0}.

(32)

For the

[

. We set

\forall w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\},\qquad r^{(t)}_{w}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\,w_{0}=\cdots=w_{t-1}=1;\\

Case

. We take
\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{(0)}}=(q-1,1,0,\dots,0)
. We have

\mathbf{\delta(r^{(0)};e_{Ko}^{(0)})}=\mathbf{r^{(1)}}\\

To prove this, we observe that for

we have

\delta(\mathbf{r^{(0)}};\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{(0)}})_{w}=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{0+q-1+w_{0}}{q}\Big\rfloor.

This is equal to
1

, and
0
if
w_{0}=0
. Hence, in this case,

\delta(\mathbf{r^{(0)};e_{Ko}^{(0)}})_{w}=r^{(1)}_{w}.

The transition weight from
\mathbf{r_{0}}
to
\mathbf{r_{1}}
is given by

\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}\left(q\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(1)}})-\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(0)}})\right)\right),

where

\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(1)}})=-\sum_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}r_{w}^{(1)}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
w_{0}=1\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}=0

and

\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(0)}})=-\sum_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}r_{w}^{(0)}=0.

Case
1\leq t<k-1
.
We choose
\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{(t)}}=(q-2,0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)
, where
1
is at the
(t+1)
position.
For
w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}
, we have

\delta(\mathbf{r^{(t)};e_{Ko}^{(t)}})_{w}=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{r^{(t)}_{w}+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{(t)}}}{q}\Big\rfloor=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{r^{(t)}_{w}+q-2+w_{t}}{q}\Big\rfloor.

Thus,
\delta(\mathbf{r^{(t)};e_{Ko}^{(t)}})_{w}=1
if and only if
r_{w}^{(t)}=1
and
w_{t}=1
. By definition of
r_{w}^{(t)}
,

\delta(\mathbf{r^{(t)};e_{Ko}^{(t)}})_{w}=1\iff w_{0}=\dots=w_{t}=1.

We deduce that

\mathbf{\delta(\mathbf{r^{(t)};e_{Ko}^{(t)}})}=\mathbf{r^{(t+1)}}.

According to (
28
), the weight attached to the transition from
\mathbf{r^{(t)}}
to
\mathbf{r^{(t+1)}}
is given by

\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}\left(q\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(t+1)}})-\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(t)}})\right)\right),

where

\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(t+1)}})=-\sum_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}r_{w}^{(t+1)}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
w_{0}=\cdots=w_{t}=1\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}=0

and

\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(t)}})=-\sum_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}r_{w}^{(t)}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
w_{0}=\cdots=w_{t-1}=1\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}=0.

Hence,

\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}\left(q\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(t+1)}})-\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(t)}})\right)\right)=1.

Let
t=k-1
.
We set
\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{(k-1)}}=(q-2,0,\dots,0,0,1)
.

For
w\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}
, we have

\delta(\mathbf{r^{(t)};e_{Ko}^{(t)}})_{w}=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{r^{(k-1)}_{w}+(1,\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{(k-1)}}}{q}\Big\rfloor=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{r^{(k-1)}_{w}+q-2+w_{k-1}}{q}\Big\rfloor.

Thus,
\delta(\mathbf{r^{(k-1)};e_{Ko}^{(k-1)}})_{w}=1
if and only if
r_{w}^{(k-1)}=1
and
w_{k-1}=1
. By definition of
r_{w}^{(k-1)}
we have

\delta(\mathbf{r^{(k-1)};e_{Ko}^{(k-1)}})_{w}=1\quad\iff\quad w_{0}=\dots=w_{k-1}=1.

We deduce that

\mathbf{\delta(\mathbf{r^{(k-1)};e_{Ko}^{(k-1)}})}=\mathbf{r^{(k)}}.

Here, we have a change regarding the transition weights.
While

\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(k-1)}})=-\sum_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}r_{w}^{(t)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
w_{0}=\cdots=w_{k-2}=1\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}=0

we here have

\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(k)}})=-\sum_{w=0}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}r_{w}^{(t)}=-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=0\\
w_{0}=\cdots=w_{k-1}=1\end{subarray}}^{2^{k}-1}(-1)^{s_{2}(w)}=-(-1)^{k}=(-1)^{k+1}.

Thus, the weight attached to the transition from
\mathbf{r^{(k-1)}}
to
\mathbf{r^{(k)}}
equals

\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}\left(q\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(k)}})-\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(k-1)}})\right)\right)=\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{k+1}\dfrac{q\ell}{b}\right).

Let
t=k
.
Here we set
\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{(k)}}=\mathbf{0}
. Thus,

\delta(\mathbf{r^{(k)}};\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{(k)}})=\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{r^{(k+1)}}.

The weight attached to the transition from
\mathbf{r^{(k)}}
to
\mathbf{r^{(k+1)}}
is given by

\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}\left(q\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(k+1)}})-\tilde{S}(\mathbf{r^{(k)}})\right)\right)=\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{k}\dfrac{\ell}{b}\right).

Note that the weights
\tilde{S}
only depend on
\mathbf{r^{(t)}}
(not on
{\bf e}
). We therefore have

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{r^{(t)}},\mathbf{r^{(t+1)}}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{|\{\mathbf{e}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1}\,:\,\mathbf{\delta(r^{(t)};e)}=\mathbf{r^{(t+1)}}\}|}{q^{k+1}},\qquad\qquad 0\leq t<k,

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{r^{(k-1)}},\mathbf{r^{(k)}}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{k+1}\dfrac{q\ell}{b}\right)\dfrac{|\{\mathbf{e}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1}\,:\,\mathbf{\delta(r^{(k-1)};e)}=\mathbf{r^{(k)}}\}|}{q^{k+1}},

(33)

\displaystyle p_{\mathbf{r^{(k)}},\mathbf{r^{(k+1)}}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{k}\dfrac{\ell}{b}\right)\dfrac{|\{\mathbf{e}\in\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1}\,:\,\mathbf{\delta(r^{(k)};e)}=\mathbf{r^{(k+1)}}\}|}{q^{k+1}}.

4.2.3
Partitioning of the set of the words

The set
W^{*}
of words of length

which
do not contain

\omega_{0}^{K+b(k+1)}
satisfies the inequality

|W^{*}|\leq(q^{(k+1)(K+b(k+1))}-1)^{\frac{j}{K+b(k+1)}}.

(34)

Indeed, when subdividing a word of length
j
into contiguous subwords of length
K+b(k+1)
(leaving a possibly short subword at the end), each word not containing the word
\omega_{0}^{K+b(k+1)}
must necessarily not contain
\omega_{0}^{K+b(k+1)}
as these subwords. There are
q^{(k+1)(K+b(k+1))}-1
different choices of words other than
\omega_{0}^{K+b(k+1)}
per subword, and there are fewer than
j/(K+b(k+1))
full subwords.

In what follows, we will focus on words containing
\omega_{0}^{K+b(k+1)}
, those that do not contain this subword will be trivially estimated.

We have

p_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{j}}}^{(j)}=\dfrac{1}{q^{(k+1)j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e^{(1)}},\dots,\mathbf{e^{(j)}})\in(\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1})^{j}\\
\mathbf{r_{j}}=\mathbf{\delta_{e^{(j)}}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{e^{(1)}}(r_{0})}\end{subarray}}W(\mathbf{e}),

where

W(\mathbf{e})=\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}\sum_{t=1}^{j}\left(q\tilde{S}(\mathbf{\delta_{e^{(t)}}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{e^{(1)}}(r_{0})})-\tilde{S}(\mathbf{\delta_{e^{(t-1)}}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{e^{(1)}}(r_{0})})\right)\right),

(35)

with the initialization, for

:

\mathbf{\delta_{e^{(t-1)}}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{e^{(1)}}(r_{0})}=\mathbf{r_{0}}.

Let us define

(
=F(\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{j}})
, to avoid cumbersome notation we will omit the dependency in
\mathbf{r_{0}}
) the set of the elements
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e^{(1)}},\dots,\mathbf{e^{(j)}})
such that there exists

such that
\mathbf{e^{(m)}}=\cdots=\mathbf{e^{(m+K+b(k+1)-1)}}=\mathbf{0}
. For

, we note

the set of the elements
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e^{(1)}},\dots,\mathbf{e^{(j)}})
such that

is the smallest integer satisfying
\mathbf{e^{(m)}}=\cdots=\mathbf{e^{(m+K+b(k+1)-1)}}=\mathbf{0}
. With these definitions, we have the partition

F(\mathbf{r_{j}})=\biguplus_{m=1}^{j-K-b(k+1)}F_{m}(\mathbf{r_{j}}).

In the following subsection, we will associate to each

containing the subword

4.2.4
Switching and coupling with Konieczny’s path

Definition 4.8

(
\lambda
-switching function)
.

For
\lambda\in\{1,\dots,b-1\}
, we define the function
\varphi_{\lambda}\,:\,F(\mathbf{r_{j}})\longrightarrow(\{0,\dots,q-1\}^{k+1})^{j}
as follows:

For
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e^{(1)}},\ldots,\mathbf{e^{(j)}})\in F_{m}(\mathbf{r_{j}})
, we set
\varphi_{\lambda}(\mathbf{e})=(\mathbf{g^{(1)}},\ldots,\mathbf{g^{(j)}})
, with

\begin{cases}\mathbf{g^{(m+K+t)}}=\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{t\bmod{(k+1)}}}\ &\text{if }\,t\in\{0,\dots,\lambda(k+1)-1\};\\
\mathbf{g^{(t)}}=\mathbf{e^{(t)}}\ &\text{if }\,t\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}\cup\{m+K+b(k+1),\ldots,j\},\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{(0)}},\ldots,\mathbf{e_{Ko}^{(k)}}
are the vectors defined in the beginning of Section
4.2
and corresponding to
Konieczny’s path
.

We write

p_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{j}}}^{(j)}=\dfrac{1}{q^{(k+1)j}}\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in F(\mathbf{r_{j}})\cup\varphi_{1}(F(\mathbf{r_{j}}))\cup\dots\cup\varphi_{b-1}(F(\mathbf{r_{j}}))}W(\mathbf{e})+\dfrac{1}{q^{(k+1)j}}\sum_{\mathbf{e}\notin F(\mathbf{r_{j}})\cup\varphi_{1}(F(\mathbf{r_{j}}))\cup\dots\cup\varphi_{b-1}(F(\mathbf{r_{j}}))}W(\mathbf{e}).\\

(36)

For all
1\leq m\leq j-K-b(k+1)+1
, let us denotes

the set of the elements
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e^{(1)}},\ldots,\mathbf{e^{(j)}})\in F(\mathbf{r_{j}})\cup\varphi_{1}(F(\mathbf{r_{j}}))\cup\dots\cup\varphi_{b-1}(F(\mathbf{r_{j}}))

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in F(\mathbf{r_{j}})\cup\varphi_{1}(F(\mathbf{r_{j}}))\cup\cdots\cup\varphi_{b-1}(F(\mathbf{r_{j}}))}W(\mathbf{e})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{j-K-b(k+1)+1}\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in H_{m}(\mathbf{r_{j}})}W(\mathbf{e})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{j-K-b(k+1)+1}\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in F_{m}(\mathbf{r_{j}})}(W(\mathbf{e})+W(\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{e}))+\dots+W(\varphi_{b-1}(\mathbf{e}))).

Moreover, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

For all
\mathbf{e}\in F_{m}(\mathbf{r_{j}})
and for
1\leq m\leq j-K-b(k+1)+1

W(\mathbf{e})+W(\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{e}))+\dots+W(\varphi_{b-1}(\mathbf{e}))=0.

(37)

Proof.

We prove the case
2\leq m\leq j-K-b(k+1)
. The cases
m=1
and

are proven in the same way. The only difference lies in the number of parts unchanged by our function (only one unchanged part).

According to (
35
) and the definition of
\varphi_{\lambda}
, we have, for
2\leq m\leq j-K-b(k+1)
,
\lambda\in\{0,\dots,b-1\}
and for

\displaystyle q\tilde{S}(\mathbf{\delta_{\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{(t)})}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{(1)})}(r_{0})})-\tilde{S}(\mathbf{\delta_{\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{(t-1)})}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{(1)})}(r_{0})})

\displaystyle\qquad=q\tilde{S}(\mathbf{\delta_{e^{(t)}}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{e^{(1)}}(r_{0})})-\tilde{S}(\mathbf{\delta_{e^{(t-1)}}\circ\dots\circ\delta_{e^{(1)}}(r_{0})}).

(38)

These two quantities, which are unchanged by the function

Moreover, the part changed by the function

(which corresponds to

Konieczny’s paths)

We then deduce that

\displaystyle|W(\mathbf{e})+W(\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{e}))+

\displaystyle\dots+W(\varphi_{b-1}(\mathbf{e}))|

\displaystyle=\Big|1+\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{k+1}\dfrac{(q-1)\ell}{b}\right)+\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{k+1}\dfrac{2(q-1)\ell}{b}\right)+\cdots+\mathrm{e}\left((-1)^{k+1}\dfrac{(b-1)(q-1)\ell}{b}\right)\Big|

\displaystyle=0.

∎

Since

\sum_{\mathbf{e}\in F(\mathbf{r_{j}})\cup\varphi_{1}(F(\mathbf{r_{j}}))\cup\cdots\cup\varphi_{b-1}(F(\mathbf{r_{j}}))}W(\mathbf{e})=0.

4.2.5
Final estimates

We write

Using inequality (
30

, the bound

\displaystyle v_{\rho}

\displaystyle\leq v_{\rho-j}\max\limits_{\mathbf{r_{0}}\in\mathfrak{R}}\left(\sum\limits_{\mathbf{r_{j}}\in\mathfrak{R}}|p^{(j)}_{\mathbf{r_{0}},\mathbf{r_{j}}}|\right)

with

\eta_{0}=-\frac{\log(1-\frac{1}{q^{(k+1)(K+b(k+1))}})}{(\log q)(K+b(k+1))}.

Using the inequality

Proof of Theorem
1.3

Let

be two integers

We recall that

It is sufficient to show that there exists

for

satisfying

Indeed, if we suppose that the inequality (

\displaystyle S_{0}(N,D,\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{D\leq m<qD}\max_{a\geq 0}\Big|\sum\limits_{0\leq n<N}\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}s(nm+a)\right)\mathrm{e}(n\xi)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{q^{\nu-1}\leq m<q^{\nu}}\max_{a\geq 0}\Big|\sum\limits_{0\leq n<N}\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}s(nm+a)\right)\mathrm{e}(n\xi)\Big|+\sum_{q^{\nu}\leq m<q^{\nu+1}}\max_{a\geq 0}\Big|\sum\limits_{0\leq n<N}\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}s(nm+a)\right)\mathrm{e}(n\xi)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq S_{0}(N,q^{\nu-1},\xi)+S_{0}(N,q^{\nu},\xi)

\displaystyle\leq CN^{1-\eta_{1}}q^{\nu-1}+CN^{1-\eta_{1}}q^{\nu}

We follow the proof method of

\displaystyle\Big|\sum\limits_{0\leq n<N}\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}s_{q}(nm+a)\right)\mathrm{e}(n\xi)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{2N^{2}}{H_{0}}+\dfrac{4N}{H_{0}}\sum\limits_{1\leq h_{0}<H_{0}}\Big|\sum_{{0\leq n<N}}\mathrm{e}\left(\dfrac{\ell}{b}\left(s_{q}^{{\lambda}}((n+h_{0})m+a)-s_{q}^{{\lambda}}(nm+a)\right)\right)\Big|

∎

\displaystyle=\dfrac{z-y}{bm}+O\left((z-y)^{\lambda}\right).

Since
z-y\leq x
, we have

N_{y,z}(a,b;r,m)-\dfrac{z-y}{bm}=O\left(x^{\lambda}\right),

where the implied constant depends on
b
and
q
. We deduce that

\sum\limits_{1\leq m<x^{\delta_{1}}}E(m)=O(x^{\delta_{1}+\lambda}).

Concerning the second sum, we split the interval into
q
-adic intervals

[x^{\delta_{1}},x^{\delta_{2}}]\subset\biguplus_{\alpha=A_{1}}^{A_{2}}[q^{\alpha},q^{\alpha+1}],

where

A_{1}=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{\delta_{1}\log(x)}{\log(q)}\Big\rfloor,\qquad A_{2}=\Big\lfloor\dfrac{\delta_{2}\log(x)}{\log(q)}\Big\rfloor-1.

Thus,

\sum_{x^{\delta_{1}}\leq m\leq x^{\delta_{2}}}E(m)\leq\sum_{\alpha=A_{1}}^{A_{2}}\;\sum_{q^{\alpha}\leq m\leq q^{\alpha+1}}E(m).

Moreover, for
\alpha\in\{A_{1},\dots,A_{2}\}
, and for large
x
, we have

x^{\delta_{1}/2}\leq q^{\alpha}\leq x^{\delta_{2}}.

Applying Theorem
1.3
for
D=q^{\alpha}
, with
\delta_{1}/2
and
\delta_{2}
in place of
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
,

\sum_{1\leq m\leq x^{1-\varepsilon}}E(m)=\sum_{1\leq m<x^{\delta_{1}}}E(m)+\sum_{x^{\delta_{1}}\leq m\leq x^{\delta_{2}}}E(m)\leq O\left(x^{\delta_{1}+\lambda}\right)+Cx^{1-\frac{\eta}{1+\varepsilon_{3}}},

(82)

for all
0<\varepsilon_{3}<1
and for
\eta=\eta(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
defined in Theorem
1.3
. Recalling
\delta_{2}=1-\varepsilon
and
\delta_{1}=\varepsilon/2
, we get with (
1.4
),

\displaystyle\dfrac{\eta}{1+\varepsilon_{3}}

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{(\delta_{1}/2)(1-\delta_{2})^{2}\min\left(1/4,3\log_{q}\left(P^{-}(q)\right)\right)}{1800\times 8^{(1-\delta_{2})^{-1}}\left(\log(4q/(1-\delta_{2}))+5b\log(q)/(1-\delta_{2})\right)(1+\varepsilon_{3})}

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\left({-\dfrac{5}{1-\delta_{2}}\left(\log(4q(1-\delta_{2})^{-1})+\dfrac{5b\log(q)}{1-\delta_{2}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\varepsilon^{3}\min\left(1/4,3\log_{q}\left(P^{-}(q)\right)\right)}{3600\times 8^{1/\varepsilon}\left(\log(4q/\varepsilon)+5b\log(q)/\varepsilon\right)(1+\varepsilon_{3})}

(83)

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\left({-\dfrac{5}{\varepsilon}\left(\log(4q/\varepsilon)+\dfrac{5b\log(q)}{\varepsilon}\right)}\right).

In particular,
\dfrac{\eta}{1+\varepsilon_{3}}\leq\varepsilon
. Indeed, since
0<\varepsilon<1
, we have
\log(4q/\varepsilon)>0
and
5b\log(q)/\varepsilon>5b\log(q)>1
. Thus,

3600\times 8^{1/\varepsilon}(\log(4q/\varepsilon)+5b\log(q)/\varepsilon)>1

and
\varepsilon^{3}<\varepsilon
. Therefore,

\dfrac{\eta}{1+\varepsilon_{3}}\leq\varepsilon.

Now, we want to compare the two exponents appearing in (
82
). We write

\delta_{1}+\lambda-\left(1-\dfrac{\eta}{1+\varepsilon_{3}}\right)\leq\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}+\lambda-1+\varepsilon.

For
\varepsilon<\dfrac{2}{3}(1-\lambda)
, the last quantity is negative. Thus, we deduce that there exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that

\sum_{1\leq m\leq x^{1-\varepsilon}}E(m)\leq C^{\prime}x^{1-\frac{\eta}{1+\varepsilon_{3}}}.

In order to have the
\eta
announced in Theorem
1.3
, we use the equation (
83
) and we use the fact that
1+\varepsilon_{3}\leq 2
.

Finally,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\eta}{1+\varepsilon_{3}}

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{\varepsilon^{3}\min\left(1/4,3\log_{q}\left(P^{-}(q)\right)\right)}{7200\times 8^{1/\varepsilon}\left(\log(4q/\varepsilon)+5b\log(q)/\varepsilon\right)}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left({-\dfrac{5}{\varepsilon}\left(\log(4q/\varepsilon)+\dfrac{5b\log(q)}{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)

and we can therefore take

\eta\left(\varepsilon\right)=\dfrac{\varepsilon^{3}\min\left(1/4,3\log_{q}\left(P^{-}(q)\right)\right)}{7200\times 8^{1/\varepsilon}\left(\log(4q/\varepsilon)+5b\log(q)/\varepsilon\right)}\times\exp\left({-\dfrac{5}{\varepsilon}\left(\log(4q/\varepsilon)+\dfrac{5b\log(q)}{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)

as stated. This ends the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Appendix B
Farey sequences

The following results are standard and can be found in
[
12
]
.

Definition B.1

(Farey sequence)
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer. We call the Farey sequence of order
n
, denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the sequence formed by irreducible fractions
0\leq\frac{a}{b}\leq 1
with
b\leq n
. Two fractions are said to be neighbours in the sequence
\mathcal{F}_{n}
if they are consecutive in
\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

Remark B.2
.

By translation, we define the Farey sequence of order
n
over
\mathbb{R}
, which we will continue to denote as
\mathcal{F}_{n}
hereafter.

Lemma B.3

(Midpoint lemma)
.

Let
\frac{a}{b}<\frac{c}{d}
be two irreducible fractions. Then

\dfrac{a}{b}<\dfrac{a+c}{b+d}<\dfrac{c}{d}.

The fraction
\frac{a+c}{b+d}
is called the midpoint of the fractions
\frac{a}{b}
and
\frac{c}{d}
.

Proposition B.4

(Farey’s criterion)
.

Let
\frac{a}{b}<\frac{c}{d}
be two neighbouring fractions in
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. Then,

\Delta=bc-ad=1\ \text{ and }\ b+d>n.

Additionally,

\dfrac{a+c}{b+d}-\dfrac{a}{b}<\dfrac{1}{bn}

and

\dfrac{c}{d}-\dfrac{a+c}{b+d}<\dfrac{1}{dn}.

Definition B.5
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\frac{a}{b},\frac{c}{d}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
be two neighbouring fractions such that

\dfrac{a}{b}\leq\alpha<\dfrac{c}{d}.

If
\alpha<\frac{a+c}{b+d}
, then we define

\dfrac{P_{n}(\alpha)}{Q_{n}(\alpha)}=\dfrac{a}{b}.

Otherwise, we define

\dfrac{P_{n}(\alpha)}{Q_{n}(\alpha)}=\dfrac{c}{d}.

Proposition B.6

(Farey approximation)
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

|Q_{n}(\alpha)\alpha-P_{n}(\alpha)|<\dfrac{1}{n}.
