Plasma-activated water as a solvent for foliar fertilizer: effects on the growth and biochemical properties of oregano (Origanum vulgare L.)
Authors: Andrzej Kalisz, Andrzej Kornaś, Joanna Gil, Barbara Domagała, Martin Koudela, Agnieszka Sękara, Karen Khachatryan, Gohar Khachatryan, Anna Wajs-Bonikowska, Paweł Kubica, Agata Krakowska, Andrzej Sałata, Katarzyna Gawrońska, Bożena Muszyńska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlasma-activated water (PAW) is a promising innovation in agriculture, with the potential to promote seed germination and to increase plant growth and development. PAW contains reactive oxygen and nitrogen species being considered as substitute for standard fertilizers. It may enhance also plants’ natural defense mechanisms through activating several physiological and metabolic pathways.ResultsOregano was foliar‑sprayed with either distilled water (control) or PAW (prepared by exposing to low temperature glow plasma for 30 min), with and without the addition of commercial fertilizer to obtain concentrations of 2.5, 5.0 and 7.5 mg/mL. PAW, both alone and combined with fertilizer, induced significant alterations in oregano metabolism, modifying bioactive compounds levels, nutrient contents, and enzyme activities, in comparison to controls (distilled water, with or without fertilizer). PAW without fertilizer induced mild oxidative stress, as evidenced by increased levels of H₂O₂ and carotenoids, although catalase activity and glutathione and sugar contents were reduced. It increased chlorophyll content, though it did not significantly increase shoot biomass. The most pronounced effects occurred with PAW combined with 7.5 mg/mL fertilizer, where heightened levels of H₂O₂, malondialdehyde, ascorbic acid, carotenoids, phenolics, sugars, and SOD activity were observed. Protocatechuic acid concentration dropped significantly in this treatment. Foliar application of PAW caused lower content of potassium, magnesium, sodium, zinc, and iron in the plants’ leaves, but it increased copper and manganese.ConclusionThis is a study in which for the first-time plasma-activated water has been used as a solvent of foliar fertilizer which has been applied on oregano plants. Our findings indicate significant impact of PAW on plant biochemistry and stress responses, providing valuable data that can be used for further research purposes and future agricultural applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07952-2.

Background

Plasma-activated water (PAW) has recently attracted attention in agriculture as an environmentally friendly technology capable of increasing seed germination, affecting plant growth and development or improving stress tolerance, due to its effects on various biochemical and physiological processes in plants [1—3]. Generated by exposing water to non‑thermal plasmas, PAW is enriched with long‑lived reactive oxygen (ROS) and nitrogen species (RNS) such as H₂O₂, NO₂⁻, NO₃⁻, and O₃, which serve as both potent signaling molecules and nutrient sources for plants [4—6]. Numerous studies across a range of crops, including wheat, tomato, maize, pea, and potato, have documented PAW’s capacity to increase chlorophyll level, modify antioxidant enzymes activity, non-enzymatic antioxidants synthesis, improve nutrient uptake, and boost biomass [2, 7—10]. These beneficial effects are mediated through complex physiological and molecular mechanisms: PAW-derived RNS modulate phytohormones, phytoalexins, stress-response genes, metabolic pathways involved in photosynthesis, secondary metabolism, and redox balance [10—12]. Despite the growing number of studies on the effects of PAW on plants, little is known about its use as a fertilizer solvent for foliar nutrition, and the interactive effects of such a solution on herb species remain largely unexplored. It is particularly important to understand whether PAW with foliar fertilizer will support plant growth and desired metabolic processes at least as well as, or perhaps even better than, standard water containing the same fertilizer. The findings presented in this article offer valuable insights that help address this knowledge gap.

Oregano (Origanum vulgare L.) is one of the most prominent aromatic plant species, valued not only for its long-standing use as a culinary spice but also for its importance in the food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical industries [13]. It is a particularly rich source of biologically active compounds, including an essential oil composed of several dozen constituents [14]. This diverse chemical profile provides oregano plants antibacterial, antifungal, antiparasitic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antitumoral properties, as well as beneficial effects in skin disorders, antihyperglycemic and anti-Alzheimer activities as confirmed in numerous studies [13]. Oregano is used in phytotherapy to support the respiratory and digestive systems, enhance overall immunity, and combat bacterial infections; moreover, its strong antioxidant activity makes it important in the prevention of diseases associated with oxidative stress [13, 14]. Owing to these properties, oregano is gaining increasing importance as a natural ingredient in herbal medicines, dietary supplements, and functional foods. For this reason, oregano represents a good model to investigate PAW’s ability to modulate plant metabolism and the content of phytoactive constituents.

In this study, we applied plasma-activated water (PAW) on the leaves, either alone or in combination with a commercial foliar fertilizer at various concentrations, to assess the effects of the treatments on oxidative stress markers, enzyme activity, nutrient uptake, and bioactive compound accumulation in oregano. We hypothesize that PAW, due to its unique physicochemical properties, may act as a plant biostimulant and represent alternative or complementary approach to traditional synthetic fertilizers by promoting plant growth, enhancing physiological activity and tolerance to abiotic stresses. Furthermore, we suggest that PAW may improve the solubility and availability of nutrients present in the fertilizer, thereby improving their absorption through the leaf surface. Enhanced nutrient assimilation could allow plants to maintain optimal growth and metabolic balance even with reduced fertilizer concentrations compared with conventional water-based solutions, potentially enabling a reduction in the fertilizer application rate and lowering environmental load.

Results

Hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde

The concentration of H2O2 was higher by 16.5% in plants sprayed with the PAW without fertilizer in comparison to the pure distilled water (dH2O) (Table 1). When fertilizer was applied in combination with PAW, the response varied depending on the concentration. The use of PAW with fertilizer at a concentration of 5.0 and 7.5 mg/mL caused an increase in H2O2 concentration (by 19.3% and 38.2%, respectively), while at the applied concentration of 2.5 mg/mL, the H2O2 concentration decreased by 9.5% compared to equivalent fertilizer concentrations in distilled water. The changes in H2O2 content in the plants did not always correlate with the concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) (Table 1). There were no significant differences in the MDA content between plants treated with dH2O and PAW. Fertilizer applied with PAW at concentrations 2.5 mg/mL and 5.0 mg/mL reduced the MDA level by 11.7% and 7.8%, respectively, than with dH2O. On the contrary, PAW with 7.5 mg/mL fertilizer increased the MDA content by 5.5%. In the case of application of fertilizer dose 5.0 mg/mL with PAW, the MDA level was lower despite the high H₂O₂ level in the plants.

PAW treatment influenced oxidative stress markers in a concentration-dependent manner. While PAW generally raised H₂O₂ levels, its effect on MDA was inconsistent: sometimes reducing lipid peroxidation (at 2.5–5.0 mg/mL) but increasing it at the highest fertilizer concentration (7.5 mg/mL).

Non-enzymatic antioxidants

Application of PAW with fertilizer at a concentration of 7.5 mg/mL increased the L-ascorbic acid content in oregano plants by 43.8% compared to its dH2O counterpart (Fig. 1A). No significant effects of pure PAW or PAW with lower fertilizer concentrations (2.5 and 5.0 mg/mL) on the L-ascorbic acid contents were found. Compared to dH2O (no fertilizer), PAW without fertilizer decreased glutathione (GSH) content by an average of 9.3% (Fig. 1B). Foliar-applied PAW with low and medium concentrations of fertilizer increased GSH content in plants by 11.0% and 15.6% at 2.5 mg/mL and 5.0 mg/mL, respectively. A high fertilizer concentration of 7.5 mg/mL in PAW reduced GSH level by 15.1%. When PAW was used without fertilizer, the carotenoid content was 52.7% higher compared to the control (pure dH2O) (Fig. 1C). In the case of high fertilizer concentration (7.5 mg/mL) with PAW, the carotenoid content was 57.1% higher, while at medium concentration (5.0 mg/mL) it was 20.7% lower than fertilization treatment of 5.0 mg/mL dH2O, respectively.

PAW treatment showed a concentration-dependent effect on selected oregano antioxidants. At high fertilizer levels, it increased L-ascorbic acid, and a similar trend was observed for carotenoids, which also positively responded to PAW applied without fertilizer. GSH rose at low to medium concentrations, but both GSH and carotenoids declined at certain concentrations.

Changes in the content of phenolic acids in oregano plants under the influence of PAW treatment, with or without fertilizer, are presented in Fig. 2. No statistically significant changes in the amount of these compounds were observed after spraying pure PAW plants without fertilizer compared to the control, which was distilled water (dH2O without fertilizer). However, a varied effect of the applied fertilizer concentrations in PAW on the phenolic acids content in oregano was observed. Compared to dH2O, the gallic acid content was significantly lower by 20.2% at the fertilizer concentration of 2.5 mg/mL (Fig. 2A). Fertilizer concentration at 5.0 mg per 1 mL PAW did not cause any change in the amount of gallic acid, syringic acid and rosmarinic acid (Figs. 2A, C and D), however, the content of protocatechuic acid significantly decreased by 37.3% in comparison to 5.0 mg per 1 mL dH2O (Fig. 2B). Higher contents of gallic acid (on average by 18.8%) and rosmarinic acid (by 35.5%) were noted at the high concentration of 7.5 mg/mL PAW in comparison to its dH2O counterpart, while the content of protocatechuic acid decreased by 73.1% (Fig. 2B).

PAW treatment influenced phenolic acids in a concentration-dependent manner: it enhanced gallic and rosmarinic acids at high fertilizer levels but consistently reduced protocatechuic acid, with the strongest decline at 7.5 mg/mL.

Antioxidant enzymes activity

No significant effect of PAW or low and medium PAW fertilizer concentrations on superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was observed (Table 2). Applying a 7.5 mg/mL PAW solution with fertilizer significantly increased SOD activity by 51.8% compared to a dH2O solution at the same concentration. Catalase (CAT) activity was reduced by 26.3% when PAW was used compared to the control (pure dH2O). The activity of this enzyme was lower by 12.1% when PAW was used with high fertilizer concentrations. Application of fertilizer at concentrations of 2.5 mg/mL and 5.0 mg/mL dissolved in PAW increased CAT activity by 12.6% and 42.4%, respectively.

Total chlorophyll and total sugar

The treatments’ effect on the total chlorophyll and total sugar content is shown in Table 3. PAW was found to significantly increase chlorophyll content (by 30.3%) compared to pure distilled water. An increase in chlorophyll content of over 39.3% was observed in the case of PAW with a 7.5 mg/mL fertilizer concentration. However, no significant effect of PAW and lower concentrations of fertilizer on chlorophyll content was observed. Application of solution with 7.5 mg fertilizer per 1 mL PAW increased total sugar content by 66.3% compared to dH2O solution at the same concentration. When pure PAW was used, total sugar content was 17.5% lower compared to the control (pure dH2O), as well as when PAW with fertilizer at concentrations 2.5 and 5.0 mg/mL was used – a decrease reached 11.2% and 15.3%, respectively, in comparison to dH2O with parallel fertilizer concentrations.

Elemental composition

Plasma-activated water (without fertilizer) significantly reduced the potassium, magnesium, sodium, and iron content in oregano plants, by 13.4%; 11.2%; 17.3%, and 31.0%, respectively, compared to the control (dH2O, without fertilizer) (Fig. 4). A high fertilizer concentration of 7.5 mg/mL PAW significantly reduced the K and Mg content compared to dH2O solution of the same concentration. However, low and medium fertilizer concentrations dissolved in PAW had no effect on the content of these elements (Figs. 4A and B). All fertilizer concentrations dissolved in PAW used in this experiment reduced the content of Na and Fe in oregano. The greatest difference in Na and Fe was observed in the PAW mixture with concentration of 2.5 mg/mL for sodium (a decrease of 13.8%) and 7.5 mg/mL for iron (a decrease of 39.1%) in comparison to respective dH2O treatments (Figs. 4C and D).

Plasma-activated water tended to decrease content of K, Mg, Na, and Fe in oregano, especially in the case of sodium and iron, regardless of fertilizer concentration.

Shoot fresh weight and dry weight content

No significant changes were observed in plant fresh weight (FW) or plant dry weight (DW) when using plasma-activated water without fertilizer (Table 4). However, use of PAW as a fertilizer solvent varied the plant response depending on the concentration applied. Fertilizer concentrations of 2.5 mg/mL and 7.5 mg/mL in the PAW solution significantly reduced plant FW (by 11.2% and 18.0%, respectively) compared to the same concentration of dH2O solution. The difference was insignificant when using 5.0 mg/mL. The opposite trend was observed for oregano DW. Fertilizer concentrations of 2.5 mg/mL and 7.5 mg/mL in the PAW solution resulted in higher dry weight than with the same concentration of dH2O solution, while 5.0 mg/ml resulted in a decrease in DW.

The results concerning plants sprayed with dH2O and plasma-activated water without fertilizer in them require a separate discussion; it turned out that the use of PAW or DW as a fertilizer solvent and spraying oregano leaves with solutions of different fertilizer concentrations often resulted in a different plant response. To sum up the effect of PAW alone (without fertilizer) on plants, a decrease in the content of most elements (Na, Mg, K, Fe, Zn) should be indicated in relation to the control, except for Cu and Mn. In addition, a decrease in the content of glutathione, sugars and also a decrease in the catalase activity was observed; the reduction in protocatechuic acid level was not statistically confirmed. On the other hand, in plants treated with pure plasma water, the content of chlorophyll, carotenoids, gallic acid, L-ascorbic acid, H2O2 and malondialdehyde increased compared to oregano sprayed with distilled water; however, slight increase for gallic acid, L-ascorbic acid, malondialdehyde was not confirmed statistically.

Dissolving the foliar fertilizer in PAW and dH2O to obtain concentrations of 2.5 mg/mL and 5.0 mg/mL and then applying these solutions to the plants resulted in several changes in the assessed parameters. At a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL PAW, the content of gallic acid, H2O2 and fresh weight decreased compared to the control (dH2O with fertilizer of the same concentration), changes in the concentration of elements were similar to those observed when PAW was used without fertilizer (K and Mg levels have changed quite slightly). Some essential changes occurred when the fertilizer concentration was increased to 5 mg/mL. Plants treated with a solution of 5.0 mg/mL PAW had more H2O2, glutathione, slightly more syringic acid, and the activity of SOD and CAT also increased, difference was statistically confirmed only for CAT enzyme. In the case of elements, the trends were similar to those in the previously described treatments (significantly confirmed for Fe, Na, and Zn), but Cu content slightly increased in the control (distilled water with fertilizer, concentration 5 mg/mL).

The most visible changes occurred in the chemical composition of the plants treated with PAW and distilled water with dissolved fertilizer at concentration of 7.5 mg/mL. Oregano plants sprayed with PAW with fertilizer (concentration 7.5 mg/mL) had more DW, gallic acid, rosmarinic acid, syringic acid (in this case differences were not confirmed statistically), apigenin, sugars, total chlorophylls, carotenoids, L-ascorbic acid, H2O2, malondialdehyde, and were also characterized by higher total antioxidant activity and SOD activity compared to the control (dH2O, concentration 7.5 mg/mL). In the former plants there was more Mn, but less of the remaining determined elements (for Cu levels were quite similar for PAW and dH2O, 7.5 mg/mL) as well as glutathione and protocatechuic acid, CAT activity and fresh weight decreased.

Percentage changes in selected parameters of oregano plants from different treatments relative to the absolute control (dH₂O without fertilizer) were graphically summarized in Supplementary Figures S4, S5 and S6.

Discussion

Plasma-activated water (PAW) is produced by treating water with a plasma, with a plasma plume above or below the water surface. The plasma changes the water’s pH, conductivity, and redox potential. Interaction of the plasma with water leads also to the formation of ROS and RNS. Key reactive species include hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), ozone (O₃), nitrite (NO₂⁻), nitrate (NO₃⁻), hydroxyl radicals (OH•), superoxide (¹O₂), superoxide anion radicals (O₂•⁻), nitric oxide (NO•), and peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻) [1, 4, 5]. Long-lived RONS produced in PAW, such as H₂O₂, NO₂⁻, NO₃⁻, can be easily transported into plant cells and act as signaling molecules in plant metabolism or can be a source of nutrients for the plant, but excessive amounts of RONS cause oxidative stress. In our study, we compared the effects of PAW application to oregano leaves versus distilled water (dH2O), both without the addition of commercial fertilizer, as well as using PAW and dH2O as fertilizer solvents at target concentrations of 2.5 mg/mL, 5.0 mg/mL, and 7.5 mg/mL. We found significant effects of PAW on a number of plant biochemical parameters, although these effects also depended on the combination of PAW and fertilizer at a given concentration.

We observed that the concentration of H2O2 was significantly higher in plants sprayed with the PAW without fertilizer in comparison to control (dH2O). This finding aligns with previous studies indicating that H₂O₂ is commonly elevated following PAW treatment due to the presence of reactive oxygen species in PAW [1, 15]. When PAW was used together with fertilizer, the plant response varied depending on the dose, concentration-dependent modulation of ROS by PAW has been also noted by Zhang et al. [16]. Higher concentrations of fertilizer (5.0 mg/mL and 7.5 mg/mL) in PAW caused an increase in H2O2 in comparison to the plants treated with equivalent fertilizer concentrations in dH2O, suggesting that PAW enhances the oxidative effects of fertilization at these mentioned concentrations. However, application of PAW with fertilizer at concentration 2.5 mg/mL and 5.0 mg/mL decreased MDA content. In the first case (2.5 mg/mL), this reduction paralleled the decrease observed in H₂O₂, suggesting a reduced oxidative stress at this dosage. In the second case (5.0 mg/mL), differences may indicate an activation of antioxidant defense in plants that mitigate lipid peroxidation under moderate oxidative pressure [17]. When dose 7.5 mg/mL was used, it increased MDA level in the plants, reflecting the threshold above which PAW-fertilizer combinations produce stronger plant responses.

Only plants sprayed with PAW at the highest fertilizer concentration (7.5 mg/ml) were characterized by a significant increase in the content of L-ascorbic acid compared to dH2O with the fertilizer at the same concentration. This high fertilizer dosage, combined with PAW’s unique properties (presence of reactive species) induced oxidative stress in the plants that stimulated synthesis of L-ascorbic acid, a strong antioxidant that can effectively neutralize ROS like H2O2 [18]. When comparing the effect of dH2O and PAW without fertilizer on the content of GSH, a significant decrease of GSH was observed in the plants sprayed with pure PAW. It can be assumed that pure PAW indeed caused mild oxidative stress on plants (see also high carotenoid content in that treatment), and GSH was consumed to neutralize ROS, reducing its levels [19]. However, a reduction in GSH content was also observed for the treatment 7.5 mg/mL PAW compared to the treatment 7.5 mg/mL dH2O. At 7.5 mg/mL, the high nutrient concentration combined with PAW’s reactive species may amplify oxidative stress beyond the plant’s GSH regeneration capacity. Medium concentrations of fertilizer (2.5 mg/mL and 5.0 mg/mL) in PAW increased markedly glutathione content in the plants. This can be explained by the fact that these fertilizer concentrations likely provide an optimal nutrient supply enhancing GSH synthesis – nutrients like sulfur and nitrogen in the fertilizer support the production of cysteine and glutamate, key GSH precursors; moreover, PAW’s reactive species may upregulate GSH synthesis [20]. As mentioned earlier, pure PAW significantly increased the carotenoid content in oregano, resulting in enhanced carotenoid biosynthesis as a reaction that neutralized ROS and protected photosynthetic membranes [21]. An increase in carotenoids level was observed by Okruhlicová et al. [22] in the maize leaves after PAW application, similarly carotenoids content increased in potato leaves after PAW treatment, which was evidenced by Rashid et al. [10]. High dosage of fertilizer (7.5 mg/mL) in combination with PAW’s ROS/RNS significantly amplify plant response, prompting the plant to produce more carotenoids for photoprotection and antioxidant defense [17]. Only treatment with 5.0 mg fertilizer per mL PAW decreased carotenoids content compared to 5.0 mg/mL dH2O, which is puzzling and may indicate a suboptimal nutrient-stress balance. On the other hand, applied fertilizer dosage (5.0 mg/mL) is recommended for plants by fertilizer producer. In this treatment possibly metabolic resources were diverted to other pathways (e.g., GSH synthesis, which increased at this PAW’ fertilizer concentration).

According to the Marrelli et al. [23], hydroquinone is the most frequently isolated phenol in Origanum spp., while Soltani et al. [24] pointed to rosmarinic acid as the major phenolic acid that was identified in O. vulgare. Both free flavonoids (flavones, flavonols, flavanones and dihydroflavonols) and flavonoid glycosides are present in this plant. The most abundant flavonoids of O. vulgare are flavones, in addition, 6-substituted and 6, 8-disubstituted flavonoids are uncommon elsewhere. A number of O-glycosides and C-glycosides have been found in oregano, luteolin is the most common aglycone, followed by apigenin [24]. In review of Marrelli et al. [23] there is a list of flavonoids that have been identified in different Origanum species, including such compounds like apigenin, genkwanin, chrysin, neglectein, mosloflavone, apigenin 7-O-glucuronide, isovitexin, acacetin, apigenin-7,4-dimethylether, cirsimaritin, cinaroside, luteolin-7-O-glucuronide, luteolin-7-O-rutinoside, luteolin, quercetin, chrysoeriol isoorientinchrysoeriol-7-O-glucuronide, thymonin, thymusin, kaempferol and many others. Among phenolic acids p-hydroxybenzoic, vanillic, syringic and protocatechuic acids were identified in Origanum spp., and also hydroxycinnamic acids, subclasses of these acids include caffeic and cinnamic acids together and their esterified forms: rosmarinic acid and chlorogenic acid [25]. New phenolic compounds in oregano are continually being discovered. Zhang et al. [26] isolated six new phenolic compounds along with known ones. Liu et al. [27] identified three new polyphenolic compounds, origanine A, origanine B and origanine C. Rao et al. [28] described that two protocatechuic acid ester derivatives, origanol A and origanol B had been discovered from the methanolic extract of O. vulgare collected from India. The cultivar, geographical and environmental factors can affect the concentration and distribution of phenolic compounds in O. vulgare. For example, rosmarinic acid exhibited different contents in various chemotypes within the species of O. vulgare ssp. hirtum and European O. vulgare ranging from 13.73 to 63.69 mg/g on a DW basis [29]; these results showed a broader range of rosmarinic acid in comparison with Austrian O. vulgare ssp. vulgare chosen plants of 19 populations (9.4 to 37.2 mg/g DW) [30].

In our study, the application of PAW (without fertilizer) on oregano plants did not result in significant changes in the concentration of the tested phenolic compounds in comparison to control (dH2O, without fertilizer). According to Abouelenein et al. [31], it is different in the case of rocket, where a significant increase in the content of some phenolics was observed under the influence of plasma water (plants were soaked for 20 min), such as 3,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid, ferulic acid, caffeic acid, p-coumaric acid, some flavonol glycosides, and quercetin, compared to the control sample. Plasma water contains reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS and RNS), which can act as stress factors, stimulating plants to produce secondary metabolites such as phenolic compounds, which was noted by Okruhlicová et al. [22] for maize. This hormesis effect can lead to an increase in these compounds, improving the antioxidant properties of plants. However, we did not observe such an effect applying PAW without fertilizer. If such application was quite enough to enhance some primary metabolite biosynthesis (e.g., carotenoids), it did not increase the amount of secondary metabolites as phenolics tested. However, adding fertilizer to plasma water and treating the plants with particular solution caused reactions in several cases: the content of the phenolic compounds was decreasing (e.g., gallic acid at 2.5 mg/mL), which was spectacular especially in the case of protocatechuic acid (at 5.0 and 7.5 mg/mL). The differences reached 37.7% and 73.1% in comparison to its dH2O counterparts. On the other hand, the increase in the level of gallic acid, rosmarinic acid and apigenin occurred when the highest concentration of fertilizer dissolved in PAW (7.5 mg/mL) was used. Generally, protocatechuic acid may be more sensitive to plasma water combination with fertilizer at high dose due to simpler and less stable structure of this acid than other phenolic compounds and higher susceptibility to oxidation by ROS, particularly hydroxyl radicals (OH•), present in PAW [32]. Although protocatechuic acid is not a direct intermediate of the main shikimate pathway, it can be formed from its intermediates (3-dehydroshikimate) and play a role as a secondary metabolite in the biosynthesis of phenolic acids and aromatic compounds [33], which means that its levels may naturally decrease when the other phenolic compounds pathways are activated. It should be emphasized, however, that the observed changes in the levels of individual phenolic compounds may result from a combination of the oxidizing effects of PAW and the influence of the fertilizer on the plant’s metabolic pathways – the strongest effect on phenolic compounds was observed at the highest fertilizer concentration (7.5 mg/mL) that can be treated as overdose.

Among antioxidant enzymes, superoxide dismutase and catalase are very important [17, 34]. The first of these enzymes is responsible for the first line of defense against the toxic effects of elevated levels of ROS. SOD removes O₂•⁻ by catalyzing its dismutation, one O₂•⁻ being reduced to H2O2 and another oxidized to O2. The second of these enzymes, CAT, has the potential to directly dismutate H2O2 into H2O and O2, protecting cells from the harmful effects of hydrogen peroxide, which is a byproduct of many metabolic processes. Here, we present results on the activity of superoxide dismutase as an enzyme that protects plant cells from oxidation. PAW may disturb the oxidation-reduction balance in cells [35]. However, in our experiments, we found no effect of pure PAW compared to pure dH2O. No significant changes in SOD activity indicates the involvement of other components of the plant antioxidant system in overcoming some stress, which could be caused by PAW application. Compared to the effect of pure PAW, the effect of high fertilizer concentration (7.5 mg/ml) on antioxidant enzyme activity was somewhat more complex, significantly increasing SOD activity by around 52%. This indicates increased oxidative stress in plants sprayed with PAW at high nutrient concentration. Rashid et al. [10] found the highest activity in SOD when plasma activated water was used most often (6-times over 2-times). Also, high concentrations of nutrients applied could have caused excessive stress, activating SOD. When PAW and distilled water were applied without fertilizer, CAT activity decreased in PAW-treated plants. This is in contradiction with the results of Rashid et al. [10], who showed an increase in CAT activity after repeated application of PAW to potato plants. On the other hand, Ferreyra et al. [3] did not show any significant effects in CAT activity in bell pepper plants treated with plasma-activated water. In our study, when the fertilizer dissolved in PAW was at a concentration of 2.5 and 5.0 mg/mL, CAT activity increased compared to its dH2O counterparts. When PAW is combined with optimal doses of nutrients, the presence of RONS may play important role in enhancing CAT expression. The decrease in CAT activity at the highest applied concentration of fertilizer dissolved in PAW is more difficult to explain. Probably, fertilizers may act as toxic agents at high doses, overloading the capabilities of some components of the defense system, in this case CAT activity. Presumably, based on our results, we state that in some treatments other antioxidants have taken over the role of CAT in the detoxification of reactive oxygen species.

Assessing the pigment content in leaves is extremely important from a physiological perspective, as they have a direct impact on both photosynthesis and primary production. Chlorophyll plays a key role in photosynthesis, absorbing sunlight and converting it into chemical energy. The level of chlorophyll in plant leaves indicates the state of the plant’s mineral nutrition and has a significant impact on key processes occurring in the plants [36]. In our study, the concentration of photosynthetic pigments, i.e., chlorophylls, was 30% higher in the plants sprayed with PAW without fertilizer compared to the control (pure dH2O). An increase in the amount of chlorophyll in plants treated with PAW was also observed by Rashid et al. [10] for potato, by Punith et al. [9] for tomato, and by Kucerová et al. [37] for wheat. The increase in chlorophyll content could be the result of biological processes occurring in the plant under the influence of PAW spray. PAW contains, among others, nitrates (NO₃⁻), nitrites (NO₂⁻) and molecules such as NO• and peroxynitrite [38]. Previous studies have shown that water acidification during plasma discharge can be linked to the production of HNO2 and HNO3 formed from NOx gases dissolving in water during plasma activation [39]. These RNS are available forms of nitrogen, which are essential for chlorophyll synthesis, and they can act as direct nitrogen sources (albeit in small amounts) or molecules which activates antioxidant pathways protecting chlorophyll from degradation. The higher chlorophyll content observed in plants treated with PAW can be attributed mostly to the increased concentration of nitrate ions [36]. Nitrate is not only essential for plant growth as a primary source but also plays a key role in chlorophyll production [40]. As a result, elevated nitrate levels in PAW-treated plants contribute to increased chlorophyll synthesis [41]. We did not observe any significant changes between PAW and dH2O with 2.5 mg and 5.0 mg fertilizer per mL, but at 7.5 mg/mL plants sprayed with PAW had more chlorophylls in comparison to dH2O. Fertilizer provides bulk N, however, at lower concentrations (2.5 and 5.0 mg/mL) it may not exceed the internal threshold for stimulating extra chlorophyll synthesis, even with PAW. In our opinion, fertilizer at 7.5 mg/mL, combine with PAW’s stimulatory effects, supplies enough N to saturate metabolic demand for chlorophyll biosynthesis and antioxidant mechanism was sufficient to counteract chlorophyll degradation.

Content of sugars in the plants treated with PAW and dH2O without fertilizer and with fertilizer applied at dose 2.5 mg/mL and 5.0 mg/mL decreased. When PAW was used with fertilizer at a concentration of 7.5 mg/mL, the sugar content increased significantly in comparison to its counterpart control. As mentioned earlier, PAW may induce mild oxidative stress triggering defense-related metabolism, where sugars act as osmoprotectants and help in the maintenance of the cellular redox balance, ROS detoxification, protection of photosynthetic apparatus, and up-regulation of stress-related genes [42, 43]. A relatively high dose of fertilizer (7.5 mg/mL) combined with the properties of PAW could have caused an imbalance in the redox balance, which initiated the synthesis of sugars. The decrease in sugar content when fertilizer was applied in PAW at 2.5 mg/mL and 5.0 mg/mL is probably due to a mismatch between stimulated metabolic demand and available nitrogen supply – these treatments may promote enhanced metabolic activity (e.g., respiration, detoxification, growth signaling), where sugars are consumed, but at these N levels, there is not enough nitrogen to support an increase in photosynthetic sugar production.

Plasma-activated water is rich in ROS and RNS that can alter leaf surface pH, redox potential, and membrane permeability, affecting how nutrients are absorbed or redistributed after the application of foliar fertilizer [44]. RONS can oxidize membrane proteins and ion transporters, disrupting the plant’s ability to retain or transport minerals like K, Mg, Na, Fe, and Zn, especially K and Mg are especially vulnerable to lower absorption from leaves’ surface due to oxidative stress caused by PAW, which damages membrane pumps [45, 46]. Decreases in the content of the above-mentioned elements also occurred in the experimental objects in which foliar fertilizer was dissolved in PAW in various concentrations. It seems, therefore, that in the case of foliar application of PAW with fertilizer, the absorption of nutrients is weaker than in the case of dH2O with fertilizer, which probably results from the simultaneous effect of PAW and fertilizer. In the case of Cu and Mn, PAW-induced mild oxidative stress that might stimulate the uptake or redistribution these elements to enhance antioxidant defenses as they lay roles in oxidative stress response enzymes (e.g., superoxide dismutase isoforms, such as Cu/Zn-SOD or Mn-SOD [34]). Oxidative changes induced by PAW can sometimes enhance permeability for certain micronutrients, especially those actively involved in redox reactions like Mn and Cu that are likely prioritized by redox-sensitive transporters or transient pores [47]. Our results highlighted PAW’s potential to selectively modulate nutrient uptake in plants.

The biological parameters measured to determine the amount of biomass produced by a plant are total fresh weight and dry weight. Limited studies focus exclusively on foliar PAW application, with most research combining soil and foliar treatments or focusing on only soil applications. Škarpa et al. [48] did not determined any significant changes in FW or DW of maize whose leaves were sprayed with PAW several times. However, the positive effect of PAW on plant growth, biomass, and leaf size has been found by another research team [49]. Our results indicate that foliar applied PAW alone (without fertilizer) was insufficient to alter oregano’s FW or DW, likely due to lack of nutrients that could penetrate the leaf, and the properties of the plasma-activated water itself were not enough to make changes in this parameter. Perhaps only single PAW application in our experiment was also insufficient in this respect. The fresh weight of above-ground parts of oregano plants sprayed with fertilizer at concentrations of 2.5 mg/mL and 7.5 mg/mL in a solution with PAW was lower, and the dry weight of the above-ground parts was higher compared to the corresponding controls. The 7.5 mg/mL concentration reduced FW, suggesting a threshold for oregano’s tolerance. Such high concentration of fertilizer interacted with PAW and may lead to an increase in osmotic potential on the leaf surface, which disturbs the water balance of leaf cells and the transport of substances across membranes (e.g., impaired uptake of ions or water).

Conclusions

The use of plasma-activated water (PAW) without and with fertilizer (concentrations: 2.5, 5.0 and 7.5 mg/mL) caused modifications of the metabolic pathways of oregano plants, inducing changes in the levels of organic compounds, elements and enzymes activities compared to pure dH2O or dH2O with fertilizer at concentrations corresponding to the given PAW treatments. Holistic graphic shows relative changes in all determined parameters between PAW and dH2O without fertilizer (Fig. 7). Based on statistical analyses we can conclude that plants sprayed with pure PAW showed some symptoms of mild oxidative stress – higher content of H2O2 and carotenoids, and at the same time a decrease in the amount of glutathione and sugars indicating their consumption in ROS detoxification or osmoprotective action and a decrease in CAT activity probably resulting from a shift in the balance in the ROS-detoxification system, where CAT is not the dominant pathway. PAW stimulated also chlorophyll biosynthesis or prevent its degradation; however, single use of PAW was not enough to enhanced fresh weight of the plants. Changes in the content of bioactive compounds or enzyme activity were significant after the application of PAW with 7.5 mg/mL fertilizer. Such treatment induced stronger oxidative stress than in other treatments, and there were evidenced that the content of H2O2, malondialdehyde, L-ascorbic acid, carotenoids, most of the tested phenols, sugars and SOD activity increased. Protocatechuic acid decreased in plants treated with PAW 7.5 mg/mL compared to its dH2O counterpart, probably because this acid may be more sensitive to such treatment due to simpler and less stable structure and higher susceptibility to oxidation. Could PAW reduce fertilizer input in commercial oregano production? The answer is ambiguous: from the point of view of hormesis, the use of PAW (pure or with fertilizer) is indicated to increase plant tolerance to stress and support plant antioxidant system activity. However, plasma-activated water caused a lower level of potassium, magnesium, sodium, zinc, and iron content in oregano plants, while only copper and manganese content increased. This is not very promising, because the application of PAW with foliar fertilizer was supposed to improve the absorption of nutrients by plants. Taking this into account, it should be stated that the use of PAW as a fertilizer solvent does not allow for the reduction of the fertilizer dose, bringing positive effects only in the case of a very few tested elements. Future research should explore repeated PAW applications and its effect on yield and essential oil composition.

Methods

Plant material

The plants used in the experiment were golden oregano Origanum vulgare L. ‘Aureum Crispum’ obtained from a local farmer in Kraków, Poland. The plants were propagated in multiplates with 112 cells (multiplate size: 53.0 × 32.5 cm, volume of a single cell ca. 43 mL) in substrate Ceres Substrate B (coconut fiber, peat with a fraction of 0–7 mm and agroperlite) supplied by Ceres International sp. z o.o. (Pyzdry, Poland). PG Mix 14-16-18 with micronutrients (Yara Poland Sp. z o.o., Szczecin, Poland) was added (dose 1 kg/m3) to the substrate before starting plant production. Next plants were transferred to the white rounded pots 11 VOL from MZ Forma Sp. Jawna, Łódź, Poland (diameter 10.5 cm, height 9 cm, capacity ca. 0.5 L) (Fig. 8). Pots were filled with the Ceres Substrate B previously mixed with PG Mix 14-16-18 (dose 1.5 kg/m3), Osmocote Pro (ICL Polska sp. z o.o., Warsaw, Poland) was also added with dose 1.5 g per pot.

Oregano plants arranged in rows on ebb and flow table in the greenhouse. The photo was taken after spraying the plants, treatments are described in the Experimental design section. Photo by A. Kalisz

Conditions in the greenhouse compartment were as follow: relative humidity (RH) ca. 75%, average temperature 22 °C from 5:00 am to 9:00 pm, 18 °C at other time, conditions of natural light (day length: 16 h), curtains shaded the plants when light intensity increased above 200 W/m2.

Preparation of PAW and its physico-chemical properties

Plasma-activated water was prepared according to the methods described in previous reports [50, 51]. Water (2000 mL) in a glass volumetric flask was placed in reactor chamber [51, 52] and subjected to glow plasma for 30 min. Plasma at a temperature of 38 °C was generated at a pressure of 5 × 10⁻³ mbar, a voltage of 600 V, a current of 50 mA, and a frequency of 280 GHz. Conductivity, pH and oxygen content were, respectively: 2.32 µS, 5.56 and 61.93% (per cent of saturation) for distilled water and 1.46 µS, 5.19 and 63.53% for plasma-activated distilled water, respectively. The recorded FTIR-ATR spectrum (MATTSON 3000 spectrophotometer, Madison, WI, USA) exhibited a high degree of similarity with the spectrum obtained for plasma-treated water after 30 min in the study by Białopiotrowicz et al. [50].

Experimental design

After the plants were delivered to the greenhouse they were placed on the table. After two days they were sprayed with dH20 (control) or plasma-activated water (PAW). Foliar application of these types of waters was performed without fertilizer and with Kristalon Zielony fertilizer (Yara Poland Sp. z o.o., Szczecin, Poland) that contained 18% N (N-NO3 – 4.9%, N-NO4 – 3.3%, N-NO2 – 9.8%) + 18% P2O5 + 18% K2O + microelements soluble in water: 3% MgO, 5% SO3, 0.025% B, 0.01% Cu, 0.07% Fe, 0.04% Mn, 0,004% Mo, and 0.025% Zn). Fertilizer was dissolved in distilled water (dH2O) and plasma-activated water (PAW) to obtain 3 final concentrations: 2.5 mg/mL, 5.0 mg/mL and 7.5 mg/mL (Fig. 9). The manufacturer’s recommended dose of this fertilizer for foliar application on the plants is 2.0–4.0 mg/mL. The solutions were applied to the plants (15 plants per treatment, 5 plants per repetition), with a dose of ca. 10 mL per plant. Spraying was carried out only once, from above on the plant leaves and from the side, perpendicular to the plants to also wet the underside of the blades. Spraying was carried out with a 1 L hand sprayer with a nozzle giving very fine drops. From that moment, the plants were only irrigated by flooding the table up to 1/3 of the pot height.

Experimental design of the study (F – fertilizer)

Plant samples were taken one week after spraying the plants. The shoots of the plants were cut, washed and weighed, and the leaves were used for analysis, without the youngest apical leaves and without the petioles. For the analysis of antioxidant enzyme activity, H2O2 and MDA, leaf blades were prepared (approximately 50 per treatment), which were wrapped in silver foil and immediately flooded with LN2. The plant material for analysis phenolic compounds and elements was also lyophilized.

Hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde

To determine concentrations H2O2, 0.3 g of frozen leaves were ground in a mortar in 1.5 mL of 0.1% TCA. The obtained homogenates were centrifuged for 10 min/14,000 rpm. The supernatant was collected and a reaction mixture was prepared containing 0.5 mL of extract, 0.5 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer and 2 mL of 1 M KJ. The mixtures were incubated for 60 min in the dark and then absorbance was measured at 390 nm (Ultraspec 2100 Pro, GE Healthcare, UK). Data were related to a calibration curve of H2O2 in the range of 0.01 mM−10 mM.

To determine concentration MDA, frozen leaves were ground in a ceramic mortar with the addition of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution in a ratio of 0.1 g FW/1,000 µL of acid. The obtained homogenates were centrifuged for 5 min/10,000 rpm. The supernatant was collected and mixed with 20% TCA solution containing 0.5% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) in a ratio of 1:4 (v/v). The tubes were incubated in a heating block for 30 min at 95 °C. Then the tubes were cooled in an ice container. After cooling, the tubes were centrifuged for 5 min/10,000 rpm. The supernatant was collected in fresh tubes. The measurement was performed using an Ultraspec 2100 Pro spectrophotometer (GE Healthcare, UK) at a wavelength of λ = 532 nm and λ = 600 nm (correction). MDA content was calculated using the molar absorption coefficient for the TBA-MDA complex ε = 155 dm3/mmol/cm and expressed in units of µmol/g FW.

L-ascorbic acid, glutathione, chlorophylls and carotenoids

The content of L-ascorbic acid was assessed using an iodometric titration method based on the approach described by Ikewuchi and Ikewuchi [53], with slight modifications. A 1.25 g portion of plant tissue was homogenized in 10 mL of 1% oxalic acid, acting as an acidity regulator, and the homogenate was subsequently centrifuged at 3,492 × g for 10 min at 18 °C. From the resulting supernatant, a 5 ml was mixed with 1 mL of a 1% aqueous starch solution and titrated with iodine in potassium iodide until a persistent blue coloration appeared, indicating the endpoint. As long as ascorbic acid was present in the extract, iodine was reduced and the starch remained colourless. The concentration of AsA was calculated based on the volume of iodine solution required for complete oxidation.

The reduced form of GSH was quantified according to the protocol adapted from Guri [54], with minor adjustments. Fresh leaf tissue (1.0 g) was finely chopped and ground in a chilled mortar with 10 mL of 0.5 mM EDTA prepared in 3% TCA, maintaining the temperature at 4 °C throughout the process. The extract was then centrifuged at 13,968 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. To 2 ml of the supernatant, 5 mL of potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) was added and the pH was adjusted to neutrality. From this mixture, 2 mL was transferred to a test tube and combined with an additional 1 mL of the same buffer. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.1 mL of Ellman’s reagent (5,5’-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid). Absorbance was measured at 412 nm with Helios Beta UV-VIS spectrophotometer, against a reagent blank containing buffer but no Ellman’s reagent. GSH levels were calculated based on the absorbance readings.

The concentration of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and carotenoids was measured following the method established by Lichtenthaler and Wellburn [55]. Fresh leaf tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized in 25 mL of 80% acetone (v/v), with the addition of 3 mg magnesium carbonate (MgCO₃) to stabilize the pigments. The extraction was carried out in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The resulting suspension was filtered, and the absorbance of the filtrate was recorded at 646, 663, and 470 nm using a UV-VIS spectrophotometer. The content of the pigments was calculated using specific equations developed by Lichtenthaler and Wellburn [55].

HPLC analysis of phenolic compounds

The dried leaves of O. vulgare were extracted using methanol in an ultrasonic bath at room temperature. Each 500 mg sample was extracted three times using 10 mL portions of methanol. The extraction time was 30 min. each time. The three extracts from each sample (30 mL in total) were collected in a crystallizer and evaporated at room temperature. The dry extract was eluted to a volume of 2 mL, obtaining a concentration of 250 mg of biomass in 1 mL. The samples were filtered using a 0.22 μm syringe filter and analysed using the HPLC-DAD method. Then, 200 µL of each sample was diluted with methanol to obtain a concentration of 50 mg/mL and analysed. Each sample was prepared in triplicate.

The amounts of protocatechuic acid, gallic acid, syringic acid and apigenin were determined in the 250 mg/mL samples. The amount of rosmarinic acid was determined in the 50 mg/mL samples. The identification and concentration of compounds in extracts was carried out using a modified HPLC DAD method according to Ellnain Wojtaszek and Zgórka [56] – the method was validated by the team of the Faculty of Pharmacy UJCM in Kraków (Poland) [57].

The analyses were performed using a Merck-Hitachi HPLC System with a Purospher RP-18e analytical column (4 × 250 mm, 5 μm, Merck). A gradient program was used, with the mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.5% water solution of acetic acid and methanol (4:1 v/v) and (B) methanol. The flow rate was set at 1 mL/min, the sample injection volume was 10 µL, the analytical wavelength 254 nm, and the temperature 25 °C. Compounds were identified by comparing their retention times and UV spectra with reference substances.

Activity of antioxidant enzymes

For the analysis of antioxidant enzymes, the plant material was frozen in liquid nitrogen (LN2) and stored at -80 °C. Plant extracts for analysis were prepared by grinding 1 g of frozen leaves in 10 mL of 100 mM phosphate buffer pH 8.0 containing 3 mM EDTA, at 4 °C. Homogenates were centrifuged for 5 min/10,000 rpm. Soluble protein concentration was determined by the Bradford [58] method using a ready-made reagent from Bio-Rad (USA). Bovine serum albumin was used as a standard.

Catalase activity (CAT, E.C. 1.11.1.6) was measured spectrophotometrically (Ultraspec 2100 Pro, GE Healthcare, UK) according to the method of Aebi [59] recording the rate of H2O2 decomposition. The essence of the measurement is to determine the decrease in absorbance within 2 min measured at a wavelength of λ = 240 nm. The measurement was carried out in 50 mM phosphate buffer pH 7.0 containing 13 mM H2O2. The absorption coefficient ε240 = 43 (µmol/L)/cm was used for conversion.

SOD activity was measured using native PAGE with discontinuous gel system (12%, 5%) at 4 °C and 180 V [60]. Each lane was loaded with an amount of extract corresponding to 10 µg of protein. SOD was visualized according to the procedure of Beauchamp and Fridovich [61]. Gels were incubated in 50 mM staining buffer (phosphate buffer, pH 7.8, containing 10 mM EDTA, 28 mM TEMED, 22 mM riboflavin and 0.25 mM NBT) for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. After incubation, gels were exposed to white light until bands were obtained. Then, gels were scanned and analysed using ImageJ (free GPL license).

Total sugar

Sugar concentration (soluble sugars and starch) was measured using the anthrone method [62]. The sugars were extracted by homogenizing leaves (fresh 1 g plant sample) in 5 mL of 95% (v/v) ethanol. The supernatant, after centrifugation, was collected for the analysis of the soluble sugar contents, while the pellet underwent starch hydrolysis through an incubation with 35% HClO4 at 4 °C for 24 h. After centrifugation, the resulting supernatant was used for further analysis.

The sugar content in both the ethanol phase and the supernatant obtained after starch hydrolysis was determined using anthrone reagent prepared by dissolving 0.2 g of anthrone in 1 L of 96% H2SO4. The samples were heated at 100 °C for 10 min, and after cooling, the absorbance was measured at 620 nm with UV‒VIS Helios Beta spectrophotometer and EvolutionTM 201 (Thermo Scientific).

Elemental composition

The oregano biomass samples were wet mineralized using the Magnum II microwave apparatus (ERTEC, Wrocław, Poland). For the analysis of mineralization, 0.2 g of lyophilized and homogenized samples were utilised. The mineralisation process was comprised of three distinct stages (first step – duration 10 min, second step – duration 30 min and third step – duration 15 min). Mineralization was carried out in Teflon vessels at a temperature of 220 °C. Following mineralisation, the solutions obtained were transferred to quartz evaporators and placed on a heating plate, where they were evaporated at 120 °C. The residue obtained after the mineralization process was quantitatively eluted with quadruple-distilled water, bringing the final volume to 10 mL. The quantitative determination of macro- and microelements (Cu, Fe, K, Mg, Mn, Na, Zn) in the samples was performed by the Flame Atomic Absorption Spectrometry method (FAAS) using a Thermo Scientific spectrometer (model - iCE3500, UK). Atomic absorption spectrometry is a quantitative analysis method. The method requires the preparation of a series of standard solutions with known initial concentrations. In order to determine the concentration of individual elements, the absorbance values for the standard solutions are measured. Then, the concentration dependencies (x-axis) are determined as a function of the measured absorbance value of the sample (y-axis). Based on the determined dependency – the equation of the function A = f(concentration), the content of the element in the tested sample is determined [63]. Each sample was analysed in six independent replicates, and the analysis result was presented as an average value.

Shoot fresh and dry weight

To estimate the fresh weight (FW), 15 plants were selected for each treatment, each was weighed separately, with an Ohaus PA214CM/1 balance (OHAUS Europe GmbH, Nänikon, Switzerland), and the average was calculated (5 plants per repetition). The plant samples were placed in an oven at 65 °C until a constant weight was achieved, after which the dry material was reweighed. The difference in weight (after taking into account the weight of the vessels) gave the result in the form of the percentage of dry weight (DW) in FW (% or g per 100 g of fresh weight). FW was expressed in grams per plant.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using Statistica version 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine the significance of the treatments, with a threshold of p < 0.05. After ANOVA analysis, the LSD Fisher test was used to group the means into homogeneous groups (p < 0.05). Standard deviations (SD) were calculated for all the datasets. Heatmap coupled with hierarchical clustering was generated using online MetaboAnalyst software (ver. 6.0). Uploaded files contained table data with the content or activity of particular components, and a table of descriptors of the samples. Data were auto-scaled (mean-centered and divided by the standard deviation of each variable) before generating heatmap, next Euclidean distance measurement and Ward clustering algorithms were applied. The color scale varied from light green (lower values) through black to light red (higher values) was used.
