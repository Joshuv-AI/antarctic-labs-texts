Re: Marc A. Furrer, Benjamin Lyttwin, Michael S. Pärli, et al. Incidental Prostate Cancer Is an Uncommon but Clinically Non-negligible Cause of Death in Selected Long-term Survivors of Urothelial Carcinoma: Oncological Outcomes and a Proposed Follow-up Strategy from a Tertiary Referral Center. Eur Urol Open Sci 2026;86:10–19
Authors: Neeraj Singh, Monika Srivastav
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the report by Furrer and colleagues on incidentally detected prostate cancer in men undergoing radical cystoprostatectomy for urothelial carcinoma, coupled with a pragmatic prostate-specific antigen follow-up strategy [1]. The strengths include systematic whole-prostate histopathology and a long observational window that captures late prostate cancer events, which is the core clinical concern in contemporary long-term bladder cancer survivors.

A major interpretive risk is attribution of metastatic events and cause-specific mortality in a population where urothelial carcinoma is biologically dominant early and remains a plausible driver of bone and nodal disease later. The reported recurrence pattern for incidental prostate cancer, with frequent bone and lymph node involvement, overlaps with typical urothelial dissemination, creating a non-trivial differential diagnosis at the moment surveillance triggers escalation [2]. In practice, misattribution can lead to premature androgen deprivation therapy, delayed urothelial salvage, or both [3]. It may be helpful to anchor the proposed escalation pathway to lesion verification standards, such as prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography plus targeted biopsy when feasible, and to specify how discordant imaging phenotypes should redirect workup toward urothelial recurrence rather than reflex prostate-directed management.

The proposed surveillance logic also underspecifies the downstream clinical decision it intends to improve. A risk-adapted prostate-specific antigen schedule is only clinically justified if earlier detection changes a treatment that is both feasible and beneficial after urinary diversion and altered pelvic anatomy [4]. The authors appropriately note the practical constraints of postcystectomy pelvic radiotherapy, yet the protocol still risks converting biochemical signals into low-yield imaging and systemic therapy in patients who are asymptomatic. A more patient-centered, implementation-facing algorithm would integrate competing mortality from urothelial carcinoma with life expectancy and treatment tolerance, then explicitly define the actionable threshold: detection of low-volume systemic disease where systemic intensification is expected to be tolerated and aligned with patient goals, rather than a biochemical recurrence threshold alone.

A further concern is that the survival message may be misread as a population-level mandate. Prostate cancer deaths were rare in absolute terms, with seven prostate cancer-attributed deaths occurring late (median 72 mo), whereas most mortality clustered earlier and was largely urothelial or noncancer. The clinically useful displacement is that incidental prostate cancer is not a routine surveillance problem [5], but a selective survivorship problem. A clearer framing would present surveillance as a “gated” pathway that activates only after durable urothelial control is established and when a management pivot is plausible, which better matches real-world follow-up capacity and avoids medicalizing low-risk incidental disease.

The study advances an important survivorship question in the immunotherapy era and provides a workable starting framework. Refining event attribution, specifying action thresholds, and aligning surveillance triggers with feasible postcystectomy interventions may improve clinical signal-to-noise and reduce unnecessary downstream testing while still protecting the small subgroup at meaningful late risk.
