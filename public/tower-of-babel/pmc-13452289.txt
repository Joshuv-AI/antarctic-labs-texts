Pediatric scimitar syndrome with an intrahepatic venous-venous shunt: A rare imaging case report
Authors: Marjeta Tanka, Albert Koja, Numila Kuneshka, Ermela Celaj, Fjorda Tuka, Erjona Abazaj, Kostandin Tanka, Vasiana Tanka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScimitar syndrome (pulmonary veno-lobular syndrome) is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by abnormal pulmonary venous drainage. The coexistence of this syndrome with a veno-venous shunt connecting an accessory hepatic vein to the main hepatic vein is exceptionally rare; to our knowledge, this is the first such case reported in an asymptomatic patient. A pediatric patient was referred to cardiology following the incidental discovery of a heart murmur during a routine exam. While the patient was asymptomatic, an echocardiogram revealed mitral valve regurgitation and right-sided heart dilation. A chest X-ray showed the classic “scimitar” sign (a curved paracardiac opacity). Subsequent CT angiography confirmed that the entire right lung drained into the inferior vena cava and identified an incidental veno-venous shunt between the inferior right accessory hepatic vein and the main hepatic vein. This report highlights a unique variant of Scimitar syndrome involving an unusual hepatic vascular shunt. It underscores the critical role of advanced imaging in detecting latent vascular anomalies, even when clinical symptoms are absent.Keywords: Scimitar syndrome, Congenital intrahepatic veno-venous shunt, Computed tomography, Angiography

Introduction

Scimitar syndrome is a rare, complex congenital malformation involving the heart, blood vessels, and lung parenchyma. Cooper and Neill first described the condition in 1836 [[1], [2]]. It features an anomalous partial or complete pulmonary venous return from the right lung to the inferior vena cava (IVC). This anatomy produces a distinctive curved vascular silhouette on an anteroposterior chest radiograph [[1], [2]]. The anomaly resembles a Turkish sword, giving the syndrome its name. The documented incidence is roughly 1-3 cases per 100,000 live births [[3], [4], [5]]. However, its true prevalence remains unknown because many asymptomatic patients go undetected [6].

Clinicians categorize the syndrome into 3 distinct types based on the age at diagnosis [[7], [8]]. The infantile form presents under 1 year of age with cyanosis, tachypnea, and severe heart failure [9]. The child/adult form manifests later with subtle symptoms like dyspnea, murmurs, or no symptoms at all. The third form involves coexistence with complex congenital cardiac anomalies. Frequently, the syndrome is associated with right lung hypoplasia and dextroposition of the heart [[7], [10]].

Associated vascular anomalies involving the hepatic system are exceptionally rare but carry significant clinical implications. While right pulmonary veins occasionally drain directly into the hepatic portion of the IVC, true intrahepatic shunts remain a separate vascular pathology [11]. Intrahepatic veno-venous shunts feature atypical, isolated connections between the hepatic veins or the portal system [12]. Most cases present asymptomatically or with mild hepatomegaly [13]. Pure veno-venous communications between accessory and main hepatic veins are seldom observed [[14], [15]].

The co-occurrence of Scimitar syndrome and intrahepatic venous anomalies is a recent focus in literature. A handful of similar combined anomalies have emerged over the last few years. For instance, a recent case series emphasized that variant presentations frequently present with atypical symptoms like severe hepatomegaly or unique systemic collaterals [12]. Similarly, current pediatric cohorts demonstrate that precise multimodal imaging is essential to isolate these distinct vascular tracts before planning interventions [[13], [16]]. Despite these sparse findings, an isolated veno-venous communication involving hepatic pathways remains virtually undocumented in standard pediatric Scimitar variations [[6], [14], [15], [17]].

This study presents a unique case of pediatric Scimitar syndrome without pulmonary hypoplasia. The condition coexists with an intrahepatic veno-venous shunt between the inferior right accessory hepatic vein and the right hepatic vein.

Case presentation

Clinical history and physical examination

A 3-year-old boy, showing normal growth and development, was referred to pediatric cardiology following the incidental discovery of a cardiac murmur. Upon examination, his vital signs were stable with an oxygen saturation of 98%-100% on room air. There were no indications of respiratory distress. A cardiac examination revealed a systolic murmur originating from the mitral valve. The remainder of the clinical evaluation revealed no abnormalities; the child exhibited normal growth and development. Preliminary laboratory assessments indicated that routine blood tests were within normal parameters.

Initial imaging

Echocardiography demonstrated mitral regurgitation, right-sided heart dilatation (atrium and ventricle), and elevated pulmonary artery pressure. Chest X-rays demonstrated the presence of right paracardiac opacity, which bears a resemblance to a Turkish sword (scimitar) (Fig. 1, red arrow). The examination reveals no indication of lung hypoplasia. These findings prompted further investigation via CT angiography (CTA).

Anteroposterior chest X-ray shows a right paracardiac tubular opacity extending from the right hilum inferiorly to the lower right lobe close to the hemidiaphragm, appearing wider distally. It resembles a Turkish sword (scimitar) (red arrow).

The patient was referred to undergo a CT angiography of the chest and abdomen to facilitate further evaluation. The CT scan was performed, which revealed an anomalous pattern of pulmonary venous return in the right lung, involving a vessel draining into the IVC in the vicinity of its confluence with the right atrium (scimitar vein) (Fig. 2, red arrow).

Axial CTA image (A) and coronal CTA (B, D) clearly depict the scimitar vein (yellow arrow) at the level of drainage into the inferior vena cava. Coronal CT angiography (C) and 3D CT reconstructions (E, F) showing a veno-venous shunt between a right accessory hepatic vein (red arrow) and the main hepatic vein (green arrow).

Furthermore, the CT scan demonstrated the existence of a right inferior accessory hepatic vein (Fig. 2, red arrow) that enters the inferior vena cava at the inferior border of the liver. The right accessory hepatic vein anastomoses via small veins with the main hepatic veins (Fig. 2, green arrow) thus forming an intrahepatic veno-venous shunt (Fig. 2, blue arrow).

Right atrial, right ventricular, and pulmonary artery trunk dilatation were also observed. However, there is an absence of right lung hypoplasia or parenchymal lung abnormalities.

Discussion

The term “Scimitar syndrome” refers to a rare congenital anomaly of pulmonary venous return, comprising 3%-6% of all cases of these partial anomalous pulmonary venous connections (PAPVC) and characterized by the abnormal drainage of part or all of the right pulmonary veins into the IVC [18]. Recent epidemiological data place the baseline incidence rate of this anomaly at approximately 1-3 per 100,000 live births [19]. In the present case, the congenital anomaly of the pulmonary veins is associated with an intrahepatic vascular variant, characterized by a veno-venous shunt between the right accessory hepatic vein and the main hepatic veins. To our knowledge, following a comprehensive multidatabase literature search of PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar using the indexing terms “Scimitar syndrome,” “accessory hepatic vein,” “intrahepatic veno-venous shunt,” and “congenital hepatic vascular malformation,” this specific vascular constellation has not been previously documented.

While the established literature extensively correlates Scimitar syndrome with a diverse array of cardiopulmonary and systemic vascular anomalies—such as right lung hypoplasia, horseshoe lung, or aberrant systemic arterial supply from the descending aorta [20]—the identification of a veno-venous shunt connecting a right accessory hepatic vein to the primary hepatic vein constitutes an entirely novel phenotypic variant within the known spectrum of this syndrome.

It is imperative to comprehend the embryological underpinnings of Scimitar syndrome and intrahepatic veno-venous shunts to elucidate their coexistence in our patient. According to normal embryologic development, the pulmonary and hepatic veins are derived from the cardinal and vitelline venous networks [[21], [22]]. The pulmonary veins undergo independent development from the splanchnic plexus and subsequently connect to the left atrium [22]. In patients diagnosed with Scimitar syndrome, an aberrant deviation in this process occurs; specifically, the primitive pulmonary-splanchnic communication fails to obliterate, and the right pulmonary vein persists in its connection with the vitelline-subcardinal system [22]. Consequently, this results in the right pulmonary vein draining into the IVC rather than the left atrium.

Additionally, the persistence of branches of the inferior embryonic hepatic veins can result in the formation of an accessory hepatic vein, typically tracking as an inferior right hepatic vein [23]. Moreover, the absence of normal physiological atrophy within the embryonic anastomoses connecting these localized branches of the hepatic veins contributes to the development of an intrahepatic veno-venous shunt. While isolated intrahepatic veno-venous shunts involving accessory hepatic pathways are rare incidental findings in the general population, their co-occurrence with an extracardiac pulmonary venous anomaly has broad developmental significance. Therefore, the association of Scimitar syndrome with a veno-venous shunt between the inferior right accessory hepatic vein and the hepatic vein indicates a shared underlying, multiregional defect in the regression, regression failure, and reorganization of the embryonic venous system during early gestation.

The rarity and anatomical complexity of such overlapping vascular anomalies mean that accurate diagnosis relies heavily on advanced cross-sectional imaging techniques rather than clinical presentation alone, which can be highly variable or completely asymptomatic in adult-type variants, contrasting with the severe pulmonary hypertension often seen in infantile forms [[18], [24]]. Detailed cross-sectional imaging facilitates the crisp delineation of anomalous pulmonary venous drainage, a hallmark of Scimitar syndrome, along with a meticulous evaluation of hepatic venous anatomy. In particular, this submillimeter assessment is of critical significance, as it exerts a direct influence on the process of therapeutic decision-making and serves to avert misinterpretation, the consequence of which could be an inappropriate surgical intervention [[23], [25]].

From a functional hemodynamic standpoint, the intrahepatic veno-venous shunt in this patient demonstrated no evidence of systemic volume overload, portal hypertension, or localized hepatic congestion. Consequently, it represents a classic “leave-alone” vascular variant that requires no prophylactic transcatheter embolization or surgical ligation. This differentiates it from atypical, hemodynamically significant dual venous drainages that mandate modern transcatheter interventional techniques to control abnormal flow dynamics [25].

From a definitive management perspective, the surgical correction of Scimitar syndrome in adult or symptomatic patients typically dictates either an intra-atrial baffled tunnel repair or direct reimplantation of the anomalous pulmonary vein into the left atrium [26]. The intra-atrial baffle technique involves utilizing a synthetic or autologous pericardial patch to create a conduit through the IVC to the left atrium, frequently requiring a concomitant atrial septal defect (ASD) closure or creation. Alternatively, direct re-implantation necessitates extensive mobilization of the scimitar vein from its infradiaphragmatic or hepatic insertion, followed by direct anastomosis to the posterior wall of the left atrium [26]. Both techniques carry inherent risks of postoperative pulmonary venous obstruction (PVO), caval stenosis, or localized arrhythmias due to extensive atrial suture lines, influencing long-term survival rates and complication profiles in recent clinical cohorts [27].

The presence of an intrahepatic veno-venous shunt involving a right accessory hepatic vein introduces distinct structural and technical variables into this surgical planning matrix. First, during extensive mobilization of the infradiaphragmatic scimitar vein, surgeons frequently encounter dense perivascular tissue and collateral networks near the cavohepatic junction. Precise preoperative cross-sectional mapping ensures that the surgical dissection plane remains strictly localized to the anomalous pulmonary venous wall, ensuring that the adjacent right accessory hepatic vein is neither inadvertently tractioned nor skeletonized, which could predispose it to localized thrombosis or vasospasm.

Second, because the intrahepatic shunt decompresses a portion of the right hepatic lobe into the primary hepatic veins rather than directly into the intended site of the pulmonary baffle or anastomosis, it does not alter the intra-caval volume dynamics at the repair site. If the accessory vein had drained directly into the scimitar vein itself—a phenomenon occasionally observed in other complex partial anomalous pulmonary venous connections—surgical redirection of the scimitar vein would have forced systemic hepatic blood into the low-pressure left atrium, creating an obligatory right-to-left shunt and subsequent systemic cyanosis or volume overload. Alternatively, ligating such a hypothetical hepatopulmonary connection would precipitate acute localized Budd-Chiari syndrome.

By definitively demonstrating that the veno-venous shunt is entirely confined to the intrahepatic parenchymal architecture and functions independently of the pulmonary venous trunk, advanced imaging allows the surgical team to confidently proceed with a standard, unmodified repair. This bypasses the need for high-risk surgical revisions of the hepatic venous anatomy or intraoperative flow measurements, ultimately minimizing cardiopulmonary bypass time, reducing cross-clamp duration, and preventing postoperative hepatic or pulmonary vascular mismatch.

The association of Scimitar syndrome with rare venous variants, as in this case, underscores the necessity for their accurate recognition and meticulous evaluation to avert potential operative complications. Although the intrahepatic veno-venous shunt in this patient has minimal hemodynamic significance, its identification is crucial for achieving a more accurate preoperative assessment to ensure optimal outcomes in cases of Scimitar syndrome.

Conclusion

While Scimitar syndrome is frequently suspected in children presenting with unexplained right heart dilation, this case underscores the critical necessity of advanced multimodal imaging to uncover rare, asymptomatic associated anomalies such as intrahepatic portosystemic shunts. Precise characterization of these vascular variations is not merely diagnostic; it provides essential anatomical roadmapping for preoperative planning and risk stratification. Incorporating comprehensive imaging protocols early ensures a tailored, multidisciplinary approach to intervention and monitoring, ultimately optimizing surgical strategy and patient outcomes in complex congenital phenotypes.
