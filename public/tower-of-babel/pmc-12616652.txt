The complete mitochondrial genome of the symmetrical brain coral Pseudodiploria strigosa (Dana, 1846) (Scleractinia: Vacatina: Faviidae)
Authors: J Antonio Baeza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe complete mitochondrial genome (mitogenome) of the symmetrical brain coral Pseudodiploria strigosa (Dana, 1846) is sequenced for the first time in this study. The newly assembled mitogenome is 16,906 bp long and encodes 13 protein coding genes (PCGs), 2 tRNA genes (trnM[cat] and trnW[tca]), and 2 ribosomal genes (rrnL and rrnS). A group I intron bisected the PCG nad5. The studied mitogenome also includes two relatively long non-coding regions, one 910 bp long, located between cox3 and cox2 and a second 799 b long, located between cox1 and trnM(cat). Mitochondrial synteny is identical to that previously reported in cofamilial corals. The mitogenome of P. strigosa is AT-rich with nucleotide use as follows: A = 24.84%, C = 13.21%, G = 20.41%, and T = 41.54%. A maximum-likelihood phylomitogenomic analysis that relied on the phylogenetic signal provided by translated PCGs supported the monophyly of the family Faviidae to which P. strigosa belongs. This newly assembled mitochondrial genome will be employed as a reference in studies biomonitoring this and other imperiled corals in the Caribbean basin using environmental DNA.Keywords: Phylomitogenomics, hard corals, eDNA, reference mitochondrial genome, mitogenome

Introduction

Hard corals (Hexacorallia: Scleractinia) which constitute the base of coral reefs, provide essential services to both human societies and marine communities (Moberg and Folke 1999; Kittinger et al. 2012; Ferrario et al. 2014; Fisher et al. 2015). Unfortunately, hard corals are currently exposed to a number of global (i.e. global climate change) and local (i.e. pollution, habitat alteration, and destruction) stressors that are driving their population decline and increasing their risk of extinction (Kleypas et al. 1999; Baker et al. 2008; Pandolfi et al. 2011).

In the greater Caribbean Sea and tropical western Atlantic, nearly 60–70 species of reef-forming corals have been identified (Cramer et al. 2020), and most of their populations are quickly deteriorating (Bayraktarov et al. 2020). Short- and long-term biomonitoring of these corals is most important for the development of conservation efforts and the implementation of restoration strategies (Hughes 1994; Hedley et al. 2016; Obura et al. 2019) and during the last decades eDNA (= environmental DNA) has raised as a dependable strategy for the monitoring of corals and other imperiled marine vertebrate and invertebrate species (Beng and Corlett 2020). Nonetheless, there is a paucity of genetic and genomic resources, including reference mitochondrial genomes, for Caribbean reef-forming corals (Tucker et al. 2023 and references therein) that is currently limiting the development and implementation of non-intrusive biomonitoring protocols in these scleractinian corals.

To support future biomonitoring strategies using eDNA (targeting multiple mitochondrial markers) focused on reef-forming corals inhabiting the greater Caribbean basin, in this study we report on the sequencing of and assembling the mitochondrial genome belonging to the symmetrical brain coral Pseudodiploria strigosa (Dana, 1846). The 2025 International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List of Threatened Species classifies Pseudodiploria strigosa as ‘Critically Endangered’ (IUCN 2025). This study is a part of a more comprehensive project that aims to sequence reference mitochondrial genomes for all imperiled corals inhabiting the greater Caribbean Sea (Tucker et al. 2023; Baeza and Rosales 2025).

Material and methods

The dataset (holobiont metagenome) herein employed to assemble the mitogenome of Pseudodiploria strigosa (Figure 1) was previously generated by Lima et al. (2023). Details about sampling locality (Bernuda), coral metagenomic sampling, DNA extraction, and sequencing (Illumina short reads) are provided in Lima et al. (2023). The metagenome sample was obtained from a specimen inhabiting shallow reefs in Bermuda (32.3078° N, 64.7505° W) collected during May 18th–22nd, 2017 (Lima et al. 2023). A total of 47,683,811 pair-end (PE, 150 bp) reads were downloaded from NCBI’s (National Center for Biotechnology Information) GenBank database (SRA Accession Number: SRR32085965) to assemble the mitochondrial genome of the studied coral using the target-restricted-assembly software GetOrganelle v1.2.3 (Jin et al. 2020). The mitochondrial genome of the cofamilial boulder brain coral Colpophyllia natans (NC_008162.1) was used as a ‘seed’ during the assembling process that was run with k-mer sizes 21, 55, 85, and 115 (Jin et al. 2020).

The symmetrical brain coral Pseudodiploria strigosa (photograph by Louis Wray used with permission).

The assembled mitogenome was annotated with the software MITOS2 (Bernt et al. 2013), as implemented in the platform Galaxy (Abueg et al. 2022) and manually curated.

To determine the phylogenetic position of Pseudodiploria strigosa and the family Faviidae (= Mussidae) to which it belongs, a Maximum Likelihood (ML) analysis was conducted relying on the phylogenetic signal provided by the 13 (translated) PCGs. The analysis included the newly assembled mitochondrial genome of Pseudodiploria strigosa plus those of other two cofamilial corals (Diploria labyrinthiformis [PV487495] and Mussa angulosa [Medina et al. 2006]) available in GenBank. Outgroups included 17 other species belonging to the closely related families Caryophylliidae, Diploastraeidae, Lobophylliidae, Merulinidae, and Pleasiastreidae. To conduct the analysis, nucleotides from each of the 13 PCGs were first translated to amino acids, and the latter were aligned using the program MAFTT (Katoh et al. 2002). Then, poorly aligned sections, if any, were removed with the software trimAl (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009). Next, the best partition and fitting model(s) of sequence evolution for the analyzed dataset were found with the software PartitionFinder (Lanfear et al. 2012). Lastly, the concatenated and partitioned PCG amino acid dataset was used to conduct a ML phylogenetic analysis in the web server IQ-TREE (Nguyen et al. 2015). The robustness of the estimated tree topology was determined by carrying out 1,000 bootstrap replicates of the tree search as in Baeza and Rosales (2025).

Results and discussion

The software Getorganelle assembled and circularized the mitochondrial genome of Pseudodiploria strigosa (GenBank accession number: PV889394) with a coverage of 354.5X per nucleotide (Supplementary Materials Table S1 and Figure S1). The newly assembled mitogenome was 16,906 nucleotides long and encoded 13 protein coding genes, 2 tRNA genes (trnM[cat] and trnW[tca]), and 2 ribosomal genes (rrnL and rrnS) (Figure 2). The PCG nad5 was bisected by a group I intron, as previously shown to occur in the cofamilial corals Colpophyllia natans (Locatelli and Baums 2025), Diploria labyrinthiformis (PV487495), and Mussa angulosa (Medina et al. 2006). Group I introns, either dissecting the nad5 or the cox1 gene are frequently observed in scleractinian corals and assumed to be functional (van Oppen et al. 2002; Medina et al. 2006; Xiao et al. 2021; Tucker et al. 2023; Locatelli and Baums 2025). The assembled mitogenome also contained two relatively long non-coding regions, one 910 bp long, located between cox3 and cox2, and a second 799 b long, located between cox1 and trnM(cat). Mitochondrial gene order is exactly the same as previously reported for the cofamilial Diploria labyrinthiformis (PV487495), Colpophyllia natans (Locatelli and Baums 2025), and Mussa angulosa (Medina et al. 2006).

The mitochondrial genome of Pseudodiploria strigosa is AT-rich with nucleotide use as follows: A = 24.84%, C = 13.21%, G = 20.41%, and T = 41.54%, in line to that reported for scleractinian genomes in which nucleotide composition has been studied (Baeza and Rosales 2025 and references therein).

The ML phylogenetic analysis fully supported the monophyletic status of the family Faviidae (bootstrap support value [bv] = 100) and, in the aforementioned family, Pseudodiploria strigosa was retrieved as the sister taxon to Diploria labyrinthiformis (bv = 100) (Figure 3).

The newly assembled mitochondrial genome of Pseudodiploria strigosa, together with others (e.g. Tucker et al. 2023; Baeza and Rosales 2025), will support future biomonitoring strategies of scleractinian corals using eDNA in the greater Caribbean basin.
