Adsorption performances and electrochemical characteristics of methyl blue onto CuxMn(1−x)Fe2O4 magnetic nanoparticles prepared via the self-propagating combustion process
Authors: Lei Sun, Min Liu, Zhou Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe synthesis of CuxMn(1−x)Fe2O4 magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) was carried out via self-propagating combustion process, their characterizations were performed using VSM, TEM, and XRD. To achieve an increased surface-to-mass ratio and optimal saturation magnetization for separation, the component ratio, calcination temperature and solvent dosage were optimized. For methyl blue (MB) removal, Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs thermally processed at 400 °C for 2 h demonstrated a mean diameter of 16 ± 4 nm and saturation magnetization of 26.8 emu/g. MB adsorption mechanisms on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs were resolved via kinetic, isothermal and thermodynamic analyses. MB adsorption on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs was optimally modeled by pseudo-second-order kinetics and the Langmuir isotherm (R2 > 0.98), capturing both the uptake dynamics and equilibrium state, signifying monolayer adsorption governed by chemisorption; while, thermodynamic statistical analysis confirmed endothermic MB adsorption on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs (ΔH° > 0) in the thermodynamic temperature range of 303–323 K, with elevated temperatures significantly enhancing removal efficiency due to favorable energetics. The positive correlation between pH elevation and adsorption capacity revealed pH-tunable adsorption characteristics. After 4 cycles, with 73.3% retention of initial adsorption capacity, Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs demonstrated moderate cyclic stability and significant potential for dye removal applications.Keywords: Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 magnetic nanoparticles, Preparation, Self-propagating combustion, Methyl blue, Adsorption

Introduction

Following swift industrial expansion, the demand for dyes continues to grow. However, dye production generates a large amount of wastewater [1, 2]. Dye wastewater is one of the main causes of water pollution [3, 4]. Discharge of this nature compromises environmental integrity and jeopardizes human well-being [5, 6]. Therefore, it is urgent to treat dye wastewater [7].

The physical [8], chemical [9], and biological methods [10] are the basic methods to treat dye wastewater. Chemical and biological methods have certain limitations [11] and require special equipment. Compared with biological and chemical methods, the advantages of physical treatment for dye wastewater are the relatively simple operation, strong adaptability to the change of water quality and quantity of wastewater, minimal chemical reaction [12], low risk of secondary pollution, and stable treatment effect. Consequently, the simplicity and affordability of physical methods have established them as one of the most common and effective options [13, 14]. Compared with other physical methods, the adsorption method [15, 16] consumes less energy, is easier to operate and maintain [17], and produces less waste [18]. The adsorbent plays a central role in determining the adsorption effect, and its performance directly affects the adsorption capacity and selectivity of the target substance, and then determines the efficiency and quality of separation, purification, and other operations. Therefore, the choice of adsorbent is critically important [19]. Nanomaterials [20, 21] have been widely used in adsorption [22] because of their advantages, such as the surface hydroxyl effect and the synergistic combination of extended specific surface area and intensified surface energy [23, 24]. However, non-magnetic nanomaterials are not only expensive but also difficult to collect and reuse, which limits their application in sewage treatment. Magnetic nanomaterials [25] make up for those very well, having renewable properties, and because magnetic nanomaterials themselves are magnetic, magnetic nanomaterials enable facile separation from dye-laden wastewater via external magnetic field application [26]. Among magnetic nanomaterials, ferrite-based magnetic nanomaterials revealed super dye removal capacities, and were diffusely applied for the adsorption of dyes [27, 28], therein, CuxMn(1−x)Fe2O4 exhibits excellent adsorption performance. Therefore, it is selected as the adsorbent in this study.

There are many methods for preparing magnetic nanomaterials, such as physicochemical method, carbonyl method, hydrothermal method [29], coprecipitation method [30, 31], sol-gel method [32] and so on. Among them, the self-propagating combustion method with low consumption of raw materials [33], low cost, simple operation [34] and short reaction time seems to be one of the most convenient methods.

In this project, CuxMn(1−x)Fe2O4 magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) were synthesized via self-propagating combustion process [35], and Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs were employed to efficiently remove methyl blue (MB) [36], and their adsorption performances and electrochemical characterization were systematically evaluated.

Experimental

Fabrication and characterization of CuxMn(1−x)Fe2O4 MNPs

According to the stoichiometric ratio of CuxMn(1−x)Fe2O4, and the product for each sample was controlled at 2 g, x was selected in the range of 0.1 to 0.9, and a certain amount of copper nitrate, manganese nitrate, and iron nitrate were precisely weighed on an analytical balance, and dissolved in anhydrous ethanol (20–100 mL gradient volumes), and followed by vigorously magnetic stirring to homogenize the precursor solution. The precursor solution was volumetrically transferred to alumina crucibles and instantaneously ignited by a butane torch, and fired in air, releasing a large amount of heat that caused the metal salt to decompose and oxidize. When the fire went out, a gel formed, and the combustion process was usually completed within a few minutes. After the combustion, stepwise calcination at 400 –700 °C (ΔT = 100 °C) was conducted on precursor powder for 2 h dwell time per temperature with the heating rate of 3 °C/min, so that the precursor powder was further crystallized to form well-crystallized CuxMn(1−x)Fe2O4 MNPs. Subsequent to calcination, the crucible was removed and subjected to natural cooling until room temperature was attained. The material was then finely pulverized in a mortar to obtain CuxMn(1−x)Fe2O4 MNPs.

Crystallographic parameters were determined using Rigaku D/max 2500 XRD, augmented by SEM-based topographical examination and TEM for elemental mapping. Magnetic properties were evaluated by vibrating sample magnetometry (ADE DMS-HF-4 VSM), complemented by Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area measurements using a NOVA 2000e instrument.

Adsorption of MB onto Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs

The adsorption properties of MB on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs were investigated. Gradient MB solutions (150–300 mg/L) were dispensed into 12 centrifuge tubes preloaded with 5 mg Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs per vessel. Adsorption exposure durations were maintained at defined intervals (10–180 min in 12 gradients). After adsorption, the magnet was placed at the bottom of the centrifugal tube, so that the Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs were tightly adsorbed on the wall of the centrifugal tube to get out the supernatant. UV-Vis spectrophotometry (PerkinElmer Lambda 35, double-beam) was employed to record the 600 nm absorbance of the centrifugate. Computational derivation via Eq. (1) quantified the MB adsorption capacity of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs.

Key experimental parameters included: solution volume V (L), absorbent mass m (g), initial MB concentration C0 (mg/L), and the equilibrium MB concentration C (mg/L) measured post-adsorption.

The isothermal adsorption experiments of MB on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs were similar to the researches for adsorption kinetics. The equilibrium adsorption capacity was calculated using Eq. (1) after 24 h of exposure to MB solutions at ten concentrations (400–4000 mg/L). Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models were employed to describe the adsorption equilibrium based on the experimental dataset. All the adsorption experiments had been carried out for thrice.

Adsorption thermodynamics

MB solutions at each concentration (400–4000 mg /L) were added into centrifuge tubes containing 5 mg Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs, respectively. The solutions in the centrifuge tubes were shaken and placed on a tilting mixer for 3 h. After centrifugation, the centrifuge tubes were carefully taken out. Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs were attracted to the wall of the centrifuge tube with a magnet, and the supernatants were removed. Absorbance measurements were conducted at 600 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Thermodynamic parameters could be computed using Eqs. (2–4) [37].

In this context, R referred to the ideal gas constant (8.314 J/(mol·K)), Kd was the dimensionless distribution coefficient, and T stood for the absolute temperature (K). Furthermore, Ce was defined as the aqueous-phase MB concentration at adsorption saturation (mg/L), while qe indicated the mass-normalized adsorption quantity at equilibrium (mg/g).

Electrochemical experiment of the adsorption process

The magnetic glass carbon electrode (MGCE) surface was sequentially polished using 0.3 μm alumina suspension and ultrasonically cleaned (10 s) in ultrapure water. MGCE, unmodified Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs, and MB-adsorbed Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs were investigated. The working electrolyte was 0.1 M KCl supplemented with 5 mM potassium ferri/ferrocyanide. cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) elucidated interfacial processes during MB adsorption on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 surfaces.

Results and discussion

Characteristics of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs

Figure 1 illustrated the structural and morphological characteristics of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs synthesized via 400 °C calcination (2 h) using 20 mL anhydrous ethanol solvent. Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs, whose spherical or quasi-spherical morphologies were shown in Fig. 1A (TEM), had a mean diameter of 16 ± 4 nm (n = 50). Due to their inherent magnetic property, certain agglomeration was particularly significant. The XRD pattern of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs (Fig. 1B) showed that diffraction peaks were observed at 30.063, 35.597, 56.973 and 62.491° corresponding to the plane (220), (311), (511) and (440), respectively. Among them, the crystal planes (220) and (331) indicated the presence of iron oxide. The crystal planes (511) and (400) corresponding to the standard card (JCPDS 20–0358) indicated the presence of copper ferrite. The diffraction peaks of the crystal planes for the Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs corresponded to the standard cards, indicating that the atomic arrangement within the sample was consistent with the standard spinel structure. The appearance of these crystal planes reflected the integrity and regularity of the crystal structure. The hysteresis loops of the Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs were shown in Fig. 1C. Compared to bulk spinel ferrites, the Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs maintained soft ferromagnetic properties (Ms = 26.8 emu/g, Mr = 0.98 emu/g, Hc = 26.24 Oe) as determined by VSM at room temperature. The magnetic property of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs would facilitate their separation from the solutions when a magnetic field was applied. The BET examination for Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs was displayed in Fig. 1(D), the adsorption-desorption curve belonged to the IV Nitrogen sorption isotherm. Obviously, the adsorption curve was not consistent with the desorption curve, and formed a hysteresis loop, which revealed the absorption-desorption performance of mesoporous materials. The calculated specific surface area reached 83.6 m2/g, and the pore size distributed from 2.6 to 3.4 nm. All the results suggested that Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs had a larger adsorption capacity.

Optimizations of calcination temperature and solvent amount

The calcination temperature played a pivotal role in tailoring nanomaterial functionality. Thus, Fig. 3 illustrated how calcination temperature affected the XRD diffractograms (Fig. 3A) and magnetic hysteresis loops (Fig. 3B) of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 nanoparticles. The evolution of XRD patterns characterized by narrowing peak widths and growing intensities with elevated temperatures, confirmed calcination-induced crystallinity improvement. The peak pattern at 400 °C indicated that the crystallinity of the nanoparticles was moderate. Moderate crystallinity ensured that there was a sufficient number of adsorption sites with appropriate properties, and the surface-active sites were not reduced due to excessive crystallinity. Saturation magnetization exhibited a pronounced inverse dependence on calcination temperature, rising from 1.4 emu/g (700 °C) to 26.8 emu/g (400 °C). At 400 °C, the slope of the rising stage of the hysteresis loop was also more suitable, which reflected a high magnetic susceptibility. The rapid magnetic responsiveness of particles facilitated accelerated adsorption-separation kinetics, thereby enhancing overall removal efficiency. Therefore, Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs were prepared at 400 °C.

During synthesis, solvent volume significantly impacted nanomaterial preparation. Figure 4 contrasted XRD spectra and magnetic hysteresis behavior in Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs calcined at 400 °C (2 h), highlighting ethanol volume effects during co-precipitation. As could be seen from Fig. 4A, when the volume of anhydrous ethanol was 50 mL, the characteristic peak intensity first increased and then decreased. This phenomenon stemmed from two competing effects at elevated ethanol volumes spanning 20–50 mL: prolonged combustion due to increased heat capacity, and enhanced precursor dispersion from dielectric modulation, wherein exothermic reaction kinetics prevailed over colloidal stabilization. For anhydrous ethanol volumes above 50 mL, colloidal dispersion became the governing factor, the characteristic peak intensity decreased, and the particle size decreased.

Adsorption kinetics

Kinetics modeling elucidated temporal adsorption evolution, identified rate-limiting mechanisms, revealed fundamental adsorbate-adsorbent interaction, and provided evidence for constructing the adsorption mechanism model. Pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and intraparticle diffusion models were therefore applied to adsorption kinetics data, bridging experimental observations with theoretical principles, their equations were followed as Eqs. (6, 7, 8).

The symbols were defined as follows: Qe signified the equilibrium MB uptake per gram of adsorbent; Qt corresponded to the temporal MB adsorption density; k1 and k2 represented the pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order rate constants, respectively; ki stood for the intraparticle diffusion rate coefficient; and Ci reflected the boundary layer effect constant.

Based on physical adsorption assumptions, the quasi-first-order model interpreted kinetic regulation solely through diffusional processes such as liquid film diffusion, intentionally omitting chemical bonding effects. The quasi-second-order model framed adsorption as a chemical process, asserting that kinetics were controlled by bimolecular surface interactions (ion exchange, complexation) at vacancy sites, involving electron sharing or covalent bond creation. Founded on the principle of internal pore diffusion, the intra-particle model asserted that adsorption rates were dominated by adsorbate diffusion through the particle interior [28].

Adsorption curves illustrating MB uptake by Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs under varying initial MB concentrations (150–300 mg/L) were shown in Fig. 5. The accelerated initial adsorption kinetics stemmed from abundant unoccupied active sites. However, after reaching equilibrium, because adsorption sites were gradually occupied, the contact between adsorbent and adsorbate was reduced, and the adsorption amounts were slightly increased. A progressive increase in adsorption capacity was observed with elevated initial MB concentrations, culminating in 53.38 ± 0.29 mg/g (150 mg/L), 70.04 ± 0.59 mg/g (200 mg/L), 85.24 ± 0.58 mg/g (250 mg/L), and 100.94 ± 0.59 mg/g (300 mg/L). As the adsorption time went by, the adsorption capacity of MB on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs gradually increased, and the adsorption equilibria were reached after 100 min. Figure 6 compared experimental data with kinetic model fittings, while Table 1 quantified key parameters, including R2 and k. The pseudo-second-order model versus alternative kinetic approaches obtained markedly better fits (R2 > 0.98), suggesting that the adsorption processes of MB onto Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs could be described by the pseudo-second-order model [38].

Adsorption isotherms

Through temperature-controlled equilibrium studies, the adsorption isotherm delineated the adsorbent’s removal capacity by establishing the relationship between residual and adsorbed contaminant concentrations. The isotherm model served as a mathematical representation of the adsorption equilibrium, derived from systematic analysis of experimental results. Different isotherm models offered complementary parameterized approaches for modeling adsorption equilibrium phenomena. In this work, Various isotherm models, including Langmuir, Freundlich and Temkin, had been proposed to explain adsorption phenomena with multiple parameters. Fundamental to the Langmuir model was monolayer-limited adsorption, involving site-specific binding at uniform surface locations and the absence of multilayer coverage. The mathematical formulation of the Langmuir model was provided in Eq. (9) [39].

In adsorption studies, Qe signified the solid-phase MB loading at equilibrium (mg/g), whereas Ce denoted the liquid-phase concentration post-adsorption (mg/L). The Langmuir constant KL and Qm respectively represented the adsorption affinity and theoretical monolayer saturation capacity (mg/g).

Unlike the Langmuir model, the Freundlich model addressed multi-layer adsorption on non-uniform (heterogeneous) surfaces. Energetic heterogeneity across the adsorbent surface dictated differential adsorbate-binding affinities at localized active sites. The Freundlich isotherm was represented mathematically as Eq. (10) [40].

The parameter Qe described the mass of adsorbate retained per unit mass of adsorbent at equilibrium. KF served as an empirical constant reflecting adsorption capacity, while n represented a dimensionless constant characterizing adsorption intensity. Ce denoted the dissolved adsorbate concentration in solution at equilibrium.

Central to the Temkin isotherm was the assumption that adsorption energy declines linearly with rising surface coverage. Equation (11) expressed the Temkin isotherm for the joint adsorption of monolayer and multilayer [41, 42].

The Temkin equilibrium constant AT was expressed in units of liters per gram (L/g). Parameter B represented the mean adsorption heat and was related to the system through the expression B was equal to RT divided by KT. The universal gas constant R maintained a value of 8.314 joules per mole kelvin (J/(mol·K)), while T indicated the absolute system temperature in kelvin (K). KT characterized the energy parameter for adsorption heat variation with units of joules per mole (J/mol).

Equilibrium adsorption behavior of MB on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs was illustrated in Fig. 7, and Table 2 summarized the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm constants and the respective regression statistics. MB adsorption capacity on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs progressively increased with the increase of initial concentration. Compared with the simulated statistical variances of 0.9048 and 0.9706 for Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models, Langmuir isotherm model obtained larger variance (0.9947) for the experimental data of MB onto Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs. According to the assumption of Langmuir isotherm model, the adsorption of MB onto Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs belonged to the monolayer absorption mechanism.

Thermodynamics

Temperature was one of the core elements in exploring the adsorption phenomenon, which helped us to deeply understand the adsorption characteristics of various adsorbents [43, 44]. The thermodynamic experiments were carried out in a thermostatic oscillator with a vibration rate of 100 rpm to guarantee the precise temperatures, the thermodynamic analysis elucidated temperature-dependent MB adsorption mechanisms on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs. Elevating the temperature from 303 K to 323 K (Table 3) caused ΔG to decrease from − 1.021 kJ/mol to − 2.496 kJ/mol, while, at 303 K, 313 K, and 323 K, lnK0 values were 1.500, 1.766, and 2.533, respectively. Which indicated enhanced adsorption spontaneity and confirming that higher temperatures favor MB adsorption onto Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs, the plots of the linear Van’t Hoff for lnK0 vs. 1/T were shown in Fig. 8, the regression equation was lnK0 = − 2554.1977/T + 8.8006 with R2 = 0.947, the other thermodynamic parameters were also listed in Table 3. ΔH = 21.236 kJ/mol > 0 was thermodynamically consistent with endothermic adsorption. The endothermic nature (ΔH > 0) necessitates energy input for adsorption. Higher temperatures facilitate this process by amplifying entropic contributions, thereby increasing spontaneity. The entropy change ΔS = 73.163 J/(mol·K) was positive, reflecting enhanced configurational freedom at the solid-solution interface. This entropy rise was attributed to either adsorbate rotational degrees of freedom or compensatory solvent entropy from desorption events. Thermodynamic analyses revealed temperature-dependent adsorption behavior, confirming a spontaneous endothermic process with positive entropy change [45]. From the application point of view, the Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs could be used more effectively to adsorb MB at higher temperatures.

Regeneration efficiency and pH-dependent adsorption behavior

Figure 9 demonstrated the influence of cyclic properties and pH on MB adsorption by MNPs. Cycle-dependent performance decay for the recalcined Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs under the same preparation conditions was evident in Fig. 9A: relative removal decreased consistently with repeated use, implying adsorbent deactivation. But the initial value of 73.3% could still be maintained even after 4 cycles, which had a certain cycle capacity. The decrease in removal rate might have been due to repeated adsorption and desorption operations, which could cause subtle changes in the structure of the nanoparticles, such as increased agglomeration. After the agglomeration of nanoparticles, the specific surface area was reduced, which reduced the chance of contact with the adsorbent and reduced the adsorption performance. The initial pH values of MB solutions were adjusted using 1 M HCl or NaOH solution. Figure 9B revealed suppressed MB adsorption capacity on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs under various pH values. Identical positive charges on MB (molecular) and acidified MNPs surfaces (solid) generated repulsive forces that suppressed adsorption. Adsorption capacity exhibited pH-dependent enhancement, peaking at 119.31 mg/g. The zero-point charges of pH (pHzpc) of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs were determined as Fig. 9C, the surfaces of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs became positively charged at lower pH, while, they became negatively charged surface. The reason for which was that the protons were adsorbed onto the adsorption layer at lower pH values and they were released at higher pH values. The pHzpc of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs was 9.89, indicating that a stable positive charge of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs across a pH range of 3–13 [46].

FT-IR spectra

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy characterized MB adsorption mechanism on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs as Fig. 10. Analysis of curve a confirmed the 835 cm−1 peak as the benzene ring’s out-of-plane bend, whereas the 1041 cm−1 peak indicated potential C-O stretching. MB molecules with C-O structures such as alcohol hydroxyl groups, phenolic hydroxyl groups or ether bonds were absorbed in this region. The 1538 cm−1 peak corresponded to aromatic C = C in-plane stretching vibrations. The conjugated double-bond system between carbon atoms in the benzene ring vibrated, generating absorption near this wavenumber, which was the key characteristic peak of the existence of the benzene ring. The 580 cm−1 band in spectrum b corresponded to Fe-O stretching vibrations in spinel ferrites. It reflected the lattice vibration characteristics of MNPs, which were generated by the expansion and contraction of chemical bonds between metal ions and oxygen ions. Bending vibrations of surface-adsorbed H2O were the probable origin of the 1635 cm−1 peak. MNPs had a large specific surface area and were prone to adsorbing water molecules. Their bending vibrations were absorbed at this wavenumber. Similar to curve a, curve c displayed diagnostic peaks at 817 cm−1 and 1577 cm−1. Due to the adsorption of MB on MNPs, the vibration absorption of its characteristic functional groups was presented in the spectrum. Curve d was subjected to high temperature calcination, and MB was removed from the Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs, and the characteristic peak of MB disappeared.

Adsorption electrochemical behavior of MB onto Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs

CV and EIS studied the electrochemical activity and charge-transfer resistance of MB adsorbed on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs and were displayed in Fig. 12A and B, and the corresponding fitted Nyquist plots was shown in Fig. 12C, their equivalent-circuit fitting data were listed in Table 4. From Fig. 12A, the signal peak value of CV for bare MGCE was maximum compared with the others, and the signal peak value decreased with the drop of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs onto MGCE, and the signal peak value of Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4/MB was minimum. Corresponding to the EIS image (Fig. 12B), Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs modification on the electrode surface enhanced steric hindrance (Rct 475.1 Ω) compared with bare MGCE (Rct 123.2 Ω), impeding [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− mass transfer. This inhibition diminished redox peak currents and amplified charge-transfer resistance. MB adsorption on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs induced interfacial steric crowding (Rct 1106.0 Ω), triggering significant current attenuation and elevated charge-transfer resistance. These findings confirmed effective MB adsorption onto Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs surfaces.

Conclusions

A self-propagating combustion route employing anhydrous ethanol in dual roles (solvent/fuel) effectively produced phase-pure Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs. With the increase of x, the saturation magnetization first increased and then decreased. Consequently, the optimal copper content (x = 0.1) in CuxMn(1−x)Fe2O4 nanoparticles was established, alongside the optimal synthesis conditions of 20 mL solution volume and a temperature of 400 °C.

A self-propagating combustion route employing anhydrous ethanol in dual roles (solvent/fuel) effectively produced phase-pure Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs. With the increase of x, the saturation magnetization first increased and then decreased. Consequently, the optimal copper content (x = 0.1) in CuxMn(1−x)Fe2O4 nanoparticles was established, alongside the optimal synthesis conditions of 20 mL solution volume and a temperature of 400 °C.

Data confirmed pseudo-second-order kinetics governed MB adsorption onto Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs, and monolayer adsorption of MB was confirmed by the Langmuir isotherm analysis for Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs, corroborated by R2 = 0.9947. The efficacy of MB adsorption on Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs was validated via electrochemical detection.

The removal rate of MB from Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs was still 73.3% of the initial value after 4 cycles, indicating that Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs had moderate cyclic performance, which was similar with the adsorption of MB onto Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs [47–49]. Although the adsorption capacity of MB on the Cu0.1Mn0.9Fe2O4 MNPs might remain high, it declined sharply at pH values below 5; however, the MNPs regained their adsorptive performance.
