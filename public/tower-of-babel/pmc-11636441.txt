The actor‐partner interdependence model of fertility stress and marital quality among couples undergoing in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer: The mediating role of dyadic coping
Authors: Xiaorun Song, Jing Zhao, Zhuoni Xiao, Hui Ye, ShiSi Dong, Lili Hu, Zhongxiang Cai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo explore the relationships among fertility stress, dyadic coping and marital quality in couples undergoing in vitro fertilization‐embryo transfer (IVF‐ET). Couples receiving IVF‐ET treatment at the clinic of the reproductive medicine centre of a hospital in China from February 2023 to October 2023 were selected by convenience sampling. A general information questionnaire, the Infertility Fertility Stress Scale (COMP‐FPSS‐SF), the dyadic coping inventory, and the marital adjustment test were used to evaluate the results. AMOS24.0 software was used to construct an actor‐partner interdependence model that extended to mediation to analyze the relationships among couples' fertility stress, dyadic coping, and marital quality. The fertility stress level of IVF‐ET wives was significantly higher than that of their husbands (p < 0.05). Wives' levels of dyadic coping and marital quality were significantly lower than those of husbands (p < 0.05). Fertility stress, dyadic coping, and marital quality were positively correlated between IVF‐ET couples (p < 0.01). In terms of the actor effect, the fertility stress of IVF‐ET couples had a significant impact on their marital quality through their dyadic coping (β = −0.188, p < 0.05; β = −0.109, p < 0.05). In terms of partner effects, wives' fertility stress significantly affected their husbands' marital quality through their own or their husbands' dyadic coping (β = −0.055, p < 0.01; β = 0.157, p < 0.01). Dyadic coping mediates the relationship between fertility stress and marital quality in IVF‐ET couples. Nurses can use husbands and wives as central individuals and dyadic coping as the starting point to formulate intervention measures to reduce fertility stress and improve marital quality.Keywords: dyadic coping, fertility stress, in vitro fertilization‐embryo transfer, marital quality

INTRODUCTION

Infertility is a disorder leading to dysfunction characterized by the absence of a confirmed clinical pregnancy after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse or the inability of a woman to achieve pregnancy as a result of the impaired reproductive capacity of the individual or her partner (Vander Borght & Wyns, 2018). According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 1 in 6 people worldwide experience infertility at some point in their lives, and the lifetime prevalence of infertility is approximately 17.5% (Cox et al., 2022). With economic development and improved education levels, it is expected that the incidence of infertility will continue to rise as more men and women delay childbearing (Mathews & Hamilton, 2016). This is because with age, female fecundity decreases due to a decrease in the number of ovarian follicles, altered reproductive endocrinology, increased reproductive tract defects and a decrease in oocyte quality. Moreover, increasing paternal age is associated with decreasing androgen levels, a deterioration in semen quality and an increase in pregnancy complications and adverse outcomes for offspring (Qiao et al., 2014; Schmidt et al., 2012). Infertility has an impact on the families and communities of affected individuals (Chorosho et al., 2023). Improvements in controlled ovarian stimulation methods and embryo laboratory conditions have led to the development of assisted reproductive therapy (ART) for infertile patients, in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer (IVF‐ET), which has brought hope to many infertile patients (Li et al., 2023). However, IVF‐ET treatment is a process that is both promising and disappointing due to its long cycle, frequent visits, high cost, wide variety of traumatic procedures, and uncertain results, which can cause stress for infertile couples and affect their mental health (Iordachescu et al., 2021; Ni et al., 2021; Teklemicheal et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023).

Infertility can be considered a dyadic stressor because it is rooted in the couple's relationship (Chaves et al., 2019). Many couples described the diagnosis and treatment of infertility as the most stressful period of their lives (Onat & Kizilkaya Beji, 2012). In the psychophysiological context, Koolhaas and colleagues defined stress as the expected or perceived inability to successfully cope with unknown, unpredictable, and/or uncontrollable situations (Weckesser et al., 2019). In 2022, the University of Alabama School of Nursing used Rodgers' concept analysis method to clarify the definition of fertility stress. Antecedents included infertility, wanting to have children, and fear of the unknown. The characteristics included identity crisis, social isolation and stigma, sexual stress, and financial stress. The consequences included the disruption of therapeutic treatment and marital strain. The characteristics and outcomes were anxiety, depression, and decreased quality of life (Woods et al., 2022). According to statistics, the overall prevalence of fertility stress in infertile women is approximately 80%–92.71% (Teklemicheal et al., 2022). In China, patients undergoing IVF‐ET have a high degree of fertility stress (Ouyang et al., 2022), which can have multiple negative effects on IVF‐ET couples by affecting their marital relationship and quality of life, reducing treatment compliance, and even leading to the cessation of treatment (Cooper et al., 2007; Rooney & Domar, 2018; Trolice, 2021).

Marital quality is considered the most important source of support in the treatment of infertility and to influence the effect of assisted reproductive treatment. It is based on the subjective evaluation and feelings of the parties, and its main content is the level of interaction and adjustment between a husband and a wife. It is an important component of individual quality of life and is closely related to individual health (Chen, 2019). Several studies suggest that psychological stress can worsen marital relationships (Iordachescu et al., 2021). For example, Bodenmann's stress‐divorce model suggests that daily stress impacts couples' functioning, leading to mutual distancing and slowly reducing relative relationship quality over time (Randall & Bodenmann, 2009). This may be because infertility can negatively affect couples' satisfaction with their sexual life. Infertile women tend to react to this situation by showing increased suspicion and hostility, which in turn affects their relationship with their partner (Malina et al., 2016). Furthermore, negative attitudes towards childless marriage in society may exacerbate feelings of loss and embarrassment as well as the emotional crisis associated with infertility and hinder marital relationships.

Coping is a key variable in understanding the impact of stress on couples. Dyadic coping is the ability of close partners to cope with stress together (Falconier et al., 2023). When one or both partners in a couple face stress, it may translate into dyadic stress, which activates dyadic coping due to the interdependence of the partners (Bodenmann, 1995). This process includes the personal coping effort, communication between partners about stress, and the coping response of partners as a dyad, which is an important factor in regulating the relationship between husbands and wives (Falconier et al., 2015). Dyadic coping is aimed at restoring or maintaining both partners' individual well‐being by reducing the partners' levels of stress and by promoting couple functioning through reciprocal trust, mutual closeness, and intimacy, as described by the systematic transactional model (Bodenmann & Randall, 2012; Chaves et al., 2019). The level of dyadic coping in infertile Chinese couples is at a medium level, and it is positively correlated with family cohesion and adaptability (Tang et al., 2022, 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). Studies have shown that the strategies adopted to cope with infertility and ART can affect the personal and marital well‐being of patients (Molgora et al., 2019), and dyadic coping is significantly associated with marital quality (Bodenmann et al., 2006; Chaves et al., 2019). Stronger dyadic coping can decrease partnership conflict, improve individuals' ability to cope with stressful events, and improve their relationship satisfaction and marital adjustment (Chachamovich et al., 2009; Tao et al., 2012; Vrankić Pavon et al., 2023; Ying et al., 2016). Thus, given the dyadic nature of infertility, dyadic coping may be a potential medium that links fertility stress and marital quality in IVF‐ET couples.

To date, however, most studies have been conducted from an individual perspective, and there is a lack of research on pairwise relationships among IVF‐ET couples (Ying et al., 2018). Interdependence theory states that there is strong interpersonal interaction between two people in a close relationship. According to Kenny et al., an individual's behaviour in a relationship can be influenced by the behaviour of his or her partner (Marmarosh et al., 2014). The actor‐partner relationships of IVF‐ET couples regarding fertility stress, dyadic coping, and marital quality have not been reported in the Chinese context.

In this context, this study aims to explore the dyadic relationships among the three variables and examine the potential mediating role of dyadic coping on the association between fertility stress and marital quality in IVF‐ET couples. The actor‐partner interdependence model extended to mediation (APIMeM) is an appropriate technique for exploring the dyadic effect (Ledermann et al., 2011). The model can simultaneously assess the effects of within‐individual variables and between‐individual and spousal variables. To date, this method has been used for cancer patients, pregnant women, infertile people, and other patients (Brandão et al., 2020; Suo et al., 2021; J. Y. Wang et al., 2022). Based on the literature and APIMeM, we propose the following hypotheses.

Fertility stress, dyadic coping, and marital quality have pairwise relationships in IVF‐ET couples.

Dyadic coping mediates the actor effects of fertility stress and marital quality.

Dyadic coping mediates the partner effects of fertility stress and marital quality.

METHOD

Participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee. Participants were recruited from the outpatient department of the Reproductive Medicine Centre of a hospital in China. From February 2023 to October 2023, convenience sampling was used to select couples who received IVF‐ET treatment as the research subjects. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1)couples of childbearing age who visited the reproductive medicine centre; ② at least one spouse who was diagnosed with infertility according to the WHO definition; ③ receiving IVF‐ET treatment; ④ having a junior high school education or above; ⑤ being able to use a smartphone; and ⑥ providing informed and voluntary participation in this study. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: ① withdrawing from the study; ② being diagnosed with a serious organic disease or serious mental disorder; and ③ having cognitive impairment and inability to complete the questionnaire. G*power software was used to calculate the sample size, and moderate effects were defined according to the following criteria: multivariate analysis f 2 = 0.15, α = 0.05, and 1−β = 0.90 (Faul et al., 2007). In this study, there were 46 independent variables, and the total sample included 273 pairs. Considering a 10% rate of invalid questionnaires, at least 328 infertile couples needed to be surveyed. A total of 595 infertile couples were included in this study according to the above criteria. The husbands' ages ranged from 20 to 60 (34.08 ± 4.88). The wives' ages ranged from 21 to 47 (32.7 ± 4.41). The remaining information is shown in Table 1.

Measures

The participants completed an investigator‐designed demographic questionnaire that included sociodemographic data and information on the characteristics of infertility diseases. The sociodemographic data included age, gender, religious belief, place of residence, family type, education level, occupation, monthly income, marital history, and reproductive history. The disease‐related data included the cause of infertility, type of infertility, duration of infertility, and treatment history.

COMPI fertility problem stress scales‐short form (COMPI‐FPSS‐SF)

The COMPI‐FPSS‐SF (Table 2) was developed by Sobral et al., in 2017 based on the COMPI‐FPSS Fertility Stress Scale of the Copenhagen Multicenter Psychosocial Infertility Research Project of Schmidt (Schmidt et al., 2005; Sobral et al., 2017). In 2022, Hu Shuang translated the scale into Chinese to screen for fertility‐related stress in infertile patients (Hu et al., 2024). For the nine items of the scale, a five‐point Likert scale was utilized to assess the extent of patients' agreement with items 1 and 2 (ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree), while a four‐point Likert scale was employed to gauge the level of stress experienced by the patient in relation to various aspects of items 3–9 (ranging from not at all to a great deal). The total score ranged from 9 to 38. Higher scores indicate more severe fertility‐related stress in infertile patients. The total Cronbach's alpha of the scale was 0.907, and each dimension ranged from 0.760 to 0.855. In the present study, the Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.922 for men and 0.908 for women.

Dyadic coping inventory (DCI)

The DCI (Table 3) was developed based on Bodenmann's theory (Bodenmann & Perrez, 1992). Bodenmann's system interaction model is the most widely used theory for dyadic coping. Xu et al. translated the DCI into Chinese in 2016 and verified its reliability, with a Cronbach's alpha ranging from 0.51 to 0.80 (Xu et al., 2016). The Chinese version of the DCI can be used to measure the coping behaviours of Chinese couples in the face of common stressors. The scale consisted of 37 items and used a 5‐point scoring method ranging from 1 to 5 points. The DCI includes 6 dimensions: stress communication, supportive coping, delegated coping, common coping, negative coping, and coping quality evaluation. The items on negative dyadic coping were reverse scored. The total score ranged from 35 to 175, with higher total scores indicating more supportive behaviours and better dyadic coping status. In the present study, the Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.944 for men and 0.931 for women.

Marital adjustment test (MAT)

This tool (Table 4) was developed by Locke and Wallace in 1959 (Locke & Wallace, 1959). The scale has 15 items, and the score ranges from 2 to 158 points. A complex scoring system is used in the MAT, including 7‐point (Item 1), 6‐point (Items 2–9), and 4‐point (Items 11, 13, and 15) Likert‐type scales and 3‐ and 2‐choice responses (Items 10, 14, and 12). A total of 100 is defined as a marital disorder, and ≥100 is defined as good marital adjustment. Higher total scores indicate better the marital adjustment (Jiang et al., 2013). In the present study, the Cronbach's alpha of this scale was 0.735 for men and 0.719 for women.

Data collection methods

The researchers recruited participants on site, conducted a pretest before the formal study, consulted the archives of infertile couples in the department after obtaining consent from the department, and selected 50 couples undergoing IVF‐ET who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria for pre‐investigation. We checked whether the design of the questionnaire was reasonable, whether the content was easy to understand, whether the instructions were clear, and whether the time required to complete the questionnaire was acceptable. In the formal investigation, unified instructions were used to explain the purpose and significance of the study. After informed consent was obtained from the patients, the research nurses distributed the electronic questionnaire codes to them on the spot, and the participants scanned the two‐dimensional code with their smartphones to participate in the study. Consistent explanations were given for questions raised by the research subjects, and the use of instructive and personal tendentious words was avoided. The couples were asked to fill out the questionnaire independently and anonymously to avoid being disturbed by the other party. If the data were incomplete, they were supplemented immediately. To increase patient participation, we offered rewards to the participants. Each couple was given medical supplies (masks, alcohol pads, ice packs) and a portable storage bag. In this study, a total of 680 couples were surveyed, and 1360 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding those with an answer time less than 300 s, more than 30% of the responses were homogeneous, and the questionnaires were answered by only one member of each couple. As a result, 595 couples participated in the study and completed the questionnaires. The questionnaire recovery rate was 87.5%.

Statistical methods

Excel2019 software was used to double‐check the data entry, and SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 24.0 were used for data analysis. Differences in fertility stress, dyadic coping, and marital quality between IVF‐ET couples were calculated via paired sample t tests. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlations between the research variables of the IVF‐ET couples. AMOS24.0 software was used to construct the APIMeM for fertility stress, dyadic coping, and marital quality for the IVF‐ET couples and to analyse the actor effects and partner effects between each path.

Among the IVF‐ET couples, the total fertility stress score was significantly greater for wives than for husbands (p < 0.01). The dyadic coping score for wives was significantly lower than that for husbands (p < 0.05). The marital quality score for wives was significantly lower than that for husbands (p < 0.01) (Table 5).

APIMeM analyses

To investigate the mediating effect of dyadic coping on marital quality in the actor‐partner interdependence model, the analysis method and process of the APIMeM proposed by domestic and foreign scholars were used (Coutts et al., 2019; Ledermann et al., 2011; Qiu et al., 2021). To estimate the confidence interval (CI) more directly, the nonstandardized coefficient was used, and the couple was regarded as a separable pair relationship to construct the model. The model consisted of six variables in two parts: two independent variables (fertility stress in IVF‐ET couples), two outcome variables (marital quality in IVF‐ET couples), and two potential mediating variables (dyadic coping in IVF‐ET couples). The bootstrap sampling procedure was repeated 5000 times, and a 95% CI was set. The model was a saturation model, and the mediation model and path are shown in Figure 1.

Solid lines indicate that the path coefficient is significant. e1, e2, e3 and e4 represented the residual variables of wives' dyadic coping, husbands' dyadic coping, wives' marital quality and husbands' marital quality, respectively. R 2 represents the proportion of variance that can be explained. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

The results showed that the effects of fertility stress on dyadic coping were valid for couples undergoing IVF‐ET (β = −0.289, p = 0.033; β = −0.315, p = 0.011). The partner effects of wives' fertility stress on husbands' dyadic coping were significant (β = 0.417, p = 0.002), while the partner effects of husbands' fertility stress on wives' dyadic coping were not significant (β = −0.047, p = 0.708). The dyadic coping of couples undergoing IVF‐ET had effective actor effects on their marital quality (β = 0.594, p < 0.001; β = 0.376, p < 0.001). Wives' dyadic coping had a significant partner effect on husbands' marital quality (β = 0.175, p < 0.001); however, the partner effect of husbands' dyadic coping on wives' marital quality was not significant (β = 0.043, p = 0.339). Finally, the actor effects of fertility stress on the marital quality of IVF‐ET couples was significant (β = −1.625, p < 0.001; β = −1.075, p < 0.001). The partner effect of husbands' fertility stress on wives' marital quality was significant (β = −0.490, p < 0.001), but the partner effect of wives' fertility pressure on husbands' marital quality was not significant (β = −0.80, p = 0.601).

The bootstrap method was used to test the mediating effect, and the results showed that dyadic coping played a mediating role in the relationships between fertility stress and marital quality in IVF‐ET couples (β = −0.188, p = 0.012, 95% CI = −0.332 to −0.04; β = −0.109, p = 0.035, 95% CI = −0.239 to −0.008). The fertility stress of IVF‐ET couples affects their marital quality through dyadic coping. Wives' dyadic coping and husbands' dyadic coping played mediating roles in the relationship between wives' fertility stress and husbands' marital quality (β = −0.055, p = 0.006, 95% CI = −0.120 to −0.013; β = 0.157, p = 0.001, 95% CI = 0.064–0.274). DISCUSSION

It is often difficult for the infertile population to adequately manage fertility stress, as an unplanned stressor, as it can lead to a series of adverse consequences. The marital quality of IVF‐ET couples is worse than that of fertile couples (Ying et al., 2016). Through a literature review, we found that dyadic coping can buffer the negative effects of stress and improve marital quality. Using the APIMeM, this study aimed to explore the relationships among fertility stress, dyadic coping and marital quality in the Chinese IVF‐ET population through a dyadic approach.

Gender difference

The study showed that the fertility stress of couples undergoing IVF‐ET was high and that the fertility stress of wives was greater than that of husbands, consistent with the findings of domestic and foreign studies (Donarelli et al., 2015; Lei & Wei, 2022; Li et al., 2021; Swift et al., 2021; Zhou & Dong, 2016). The dyadic coping of IVF‐ET couples was at a low normal level, similar to the research results of Hu Shunan et al. (Hu et al., 2022). The marital quality of the IVF‐ET couples was at a medium level, and the marital quality of wives was lower than that of husbands, consistent with the findings of Wang Jieyu et al. (Wang et al., 2022). These findings were lower than those of Pakistani infertile women in research by Qadir F et al. and lower than those of infertile couples investigated by Bhamani S S et al. (Bhamani et al., 2022; Qadir et al., 2015). This could be related to the following reasons. First, the causes of infertility in this study were mostly female, and women are more susceptible to infertility and its treatment. Due to the long IVF‐ET treatment cycle, repeated invasive procedures, uncertain treatment results, and excessive attention from family and friends, women are often under multiple pressures. Second, stress may have adverse effects on health‐related outcomes, and most of the participants in this study were undergoing IVF‐ET for the first time. Additionally, many had insufficient knowledge, disease acceptance, and treatment readiness, which reduced their dyadic coping ability. Third, under the influence of traditional concepts in China, childbearing is regarded as a responsibility and obligation. The frustration of failing to achieve childbearing, an inharmonious sexual life, and the pressure of public opinion from others affect both husbands and wives and cause tension in their relationship as well as a decrease in marital quality. Women are more susceptible to this influence.

Hypothesis 1

In this study, fertility stress, dyadic coping, and marital quality were positively correlated among IVF‐ET couples, which supported hypothesis 1. These findings indicate that IVF‐ET couples have a consistent style of coping with infertility and related treatment, and their fertility stress and marital quality affect each other. These findings further indicate that there is interdependence among fertility stress, dyadic coping, and marital quality in IVF‐ET couples. In addition, the fertility stress of IVF‐ET women was negatively correlated with dyadic coping, which means that dyadic coping plays an important role in reducing the psychological burden of infertility patients (Rusu et al., 2020). The fertility stress of IVF‐ET couples was negatively correlated with their marital quality, indicating that the greater the fertility stress of the couple was, the lower their marital quality (Gana & Jakubowska, 2016). Moreover, the dyadic coping of IVF‐ET couples was positively correlated with their marital quality, indicating that the greater the dyadic coping level of these couples is, the better their marital quality, and dyadic coping can significantly predict their relationship satisfaction (Falconier et al., 2015; Staff et al., 2017).

Hypothesis 2

The APIM analysis in this study showed that in terms of the actor effect, the greater the fertility stress of wives and husbands was, the lower their marital quality. Consistent with Bodenmann's model of the role of stress in close relationships, in dyadic relationships, stress leads to negative relationship outcomes such as alienation, low relationship satisfaction, and an even greater risk of divorce (Randall & Bodenmann, 2009).

The mediation analysis showed that the fertility stress of wives and husbands could change their marital quality through dyadic coping. These results confirmed our hypothesis 2. According to related studies, infertile patients with poorer dyadic coping experience had poorer marital quality; however, the greater their dyadic coping level and the more they communicate about their stress, the better their marital quality was (Bodenmann et al., 2006). Dyadic coping includes stress communication, supportive coping, agent coping, and joint coping, which can effectively improve the close relationship between couples. Communication about worries, needs and relationships specifically promotes the sharing of coping experiences between couples or engages in other communication behaviours aimed at maintaining or enhancing the relationships associated with greater marital adjustment (An et al., 2021).

Hypothesis 3

In terms of partner effects, wives' marital quality was negatively affected by their husbands' fertility stress. However, the correlation between husbands' marital quality and wives' fertility stress was not significant. The possible explanations are as follows: first, compared with men, women are more susceptible to influence from the outside world, family and society and are affected by the factors of their husbands due to their sensitive personality. In addition, men participate less in the infertility treatment process than women, resulting in less attention, less fertility stress and less psychological burden. This gender disparity poses challenges for men to empathize with their spouses (Bose et al., 2021).

The mediation analysis also revealed wives' fertility stress can affect husbands' marital quality through wives' or husbands' dyadic coping, indicating that hypothesis 3 is partly valid. Wives' increased communication and ability to cope with infertility stress are conducive to maintaining harmonious relationships, thus improving their husbands' marriage quality. When husbands perceive their wives' high‐stress states and emotional reactions, their socially expected role as family protectors triggers their dyadic coping strategies to help their partners cope better with the stress of infertility. Wives feel the support and help they provide and provide positive feedback, which can help them realize the value of husbands as caregivers and enhance men's self‐esteem, thereby improving husbands' marital quality (Bodenmann et al., 2006; Pasch et al., 2002).

However, husbands' fertility stress did not affect wives' marital quality through husbands' or wives' dyadic coping. This may be because the level of fertility stress in men is not high enough to motivate their spouses to adopt dyadic coping strategies or because the support provided by women does not buffer the negative effects of stress on men (Hilpert et al., 2018). Husbands' dyadic coping did not significantly affect wives' marital quality in this study, which differs from previous research on dyadic coping, which showed that for women, their partner's dyadic coping was a predictor of their marital quality, whereas for husbands, their dyadic coping predicted their marital quality (Bodenmann et al., 2006; Herzberg, 2013; Papp & Witt, 2010). This may be due to sample differences or population differences. Global cultural and gender differences influence how couples' coping behaviour affects relationship outcomes (Hilpert et al., 2016).

Strengths and limitations

This study has certain strengths to acknowledge. First, the sample size was large, with 595 couples (1190 participants). Second, this study used a suitable data analysis strategy, namely, the APIMeM, which shed light on the intrinsic and interpersonal effects of couples' fertility stress, dyadic coping, and marital quality.

Nonetheless, we need to consider the limitations of the methods used in this study. First, this was a single‐centre study, which may lead to selectivity bias. Further multicenter studies should be conducted. Second, cross‐sectional studies cannot determine whether the relationship between variables is causal. Longitudinal studies should be used in the future to further explore the dynamic changes and interaction mechanisms between these variables. Third, the data in this study were self‐reported, and reporting bias may exist. Biological indicators and other assessment methods can be used in the future. Fourth, several factors, such as the number of treatments received (we included only treatment histories in the general data), might have influenced the results; further studies should distinguish between patients who received their first treatment and those who received two or more treatments.

CONCLUSIONS AND PRACTICAL IMPLICATIONS

This study revealed that IVF‐ET couples, especially wives, had severe fertility stress, low marital quality, and low dyadic coping levels. There were correlations among the three variables of fertility stress, dyadic coping, and marital quality for wives and husbands, and the variables were significantly correlated between couples. Through APIMeM analysis, we found that dyadic coping played a mediating role in the actor effect of fertility stress on the marital quality of IVF‐ET couples and in the actor‐partner effect and partner‐actor effect of the wife's fertility stress on the husband's marital quality. Therefore, in the treatment and care of IVF‐ET patients, interventions can be formulated based on couples as the centre and dyadic coping as the entry point to reduce fertility stress and improve marital quality.

This study provides a better understanding the marital quality of Chinese IVF‐ET couples in the face of fertility stress and the buffering role of dyadic coping, which is clinically relevant. First, IVF‐ET treatment is a process in which couples engage in together; it is highly interactive. Couples should view infertility as a common problem and work together to solve problems, share information, and support each other to reduce the impact of stress and improve relationship quality. Second, medical staff need to start from the perspective of couples' dyadic coping, actively conduct targeted interventions such as group psychotherapy and couples' coping enhancement training to improve their level of dyadic coping, reduce fertility stress, and improve marital quality in future clinical research (Merom et al., 2008; Monirian et al., 2022). However, health care providers who take care of infertile couples must consider gender differences and partner effects on emotional factors during physical intervention and counselling (Kim et al., 2018).
