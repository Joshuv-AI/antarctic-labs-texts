Comparative genomic study of blaKPC-2-carrying multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae in the United Arab Emirates
Authors: Akela Ghazawi, Mohammed Elbediwi, Tibor Pal, Agnes Sonnevend, Ashrat Manzoor, Ihab Habib, Farah Al-Marzooq, Abiola Senok, Laurent Poirel, Mushtaq Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe performed whole-genome sequencing of three multidrug-resistant KPC-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates recovered from patients in a tertiary-care hospital in Abu Dhabi, UAE, and conducted a comparative genomic analysis with previously reported blaKPC−2-positive isolates from the region. The study aimed to characterize acquired antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), assess their phylogenetic context, and analyze the plasmids carrying blaKPC−2. Between 2017 and 2018, 162 carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) isolates were collected from four tertiary care hospitals in Abu Dhabi. Antibiotic susceptibility was determined using the Vitek2 system, and PCR was used to detect carbapenemase genes. Whole-genome sequencing was performed for the three blaKPC-2-positive isolates using Illumina NovaSeq, and assemblies were generated with SPAdes 3.9. PlasmidSPAdes and PLACNETw were used to reconstruct blaKPC-2-carrying plasmids, which were then compared with plasmids described in earlier UAE reports. The three K. pneumoniae isolates belonged to sequence types ST11, ST20, and ST231, respectively. All harbored the blaKPC−2 gene on IncFII-type plasmids of varying sizes. Our comparative analysis demonstrated variability in the genetic platforms carrying blaKPC−2, with two transposons (Tn1721a and Tn4401b) identified as the main structures associated with this gene in UAE isolates. Conclusions: Our findings highlight the diversity of genetic platforms carrying blaKPC-2 in the UAE, particularly the frequent association with IncFII plasmids and their potential role in the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance. Although blaKPC-2 is a globally widespread carbapenemase gene, it has been rarely reported in the United Arab Emirates (UAE). To date, only two studies describing blaKPC-2-carrying Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates have been published from the region—in 2015 and 2019. Our study presents a comprehensive genomic analysis of blaKPC-2-positive isolates collected in 2017–2018 from tertiary care hospitals in the UAE. Compared to previous reports, this study employed advanced bioinformatics tools and conducted deep genomic surveillance, including resistome, plasmidome, transposon mapping, and phylogenetic comparisons with public genomes. We identified distinct genetic platforms carrying blaKPC-2, notably the association with IncFII plasmids and the continued presence of the Tn4401b transposon. Although blaKPC-2 is present in the UAE, it remains underreported, likely due to limited regional sequencing efforts. This study provides essential baseline genomic data and a comparative framework to support ongoing and future surveillance of antimicrobial resistance in the Gulf region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-29427-y.Keywords: Klebsiella pneumoniae, blaKPC-2, Transposons, Whole genome sequencing, IncFIISubject terms: Genetics, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Carbapenemase-producing gram-negative bacteria (GNB) are associated with infections, including outbreaks, in hospitals and long-term care facilities around the world1. The rapid transmission of mobile genetic elements, the ineffective application of measures for detection and control, and co-resistance to other antibiotics are all factors that contribute to the growing public health threat of these multidrug-resistant pathogens. Klebsiella pneumoniae causes a broad spectrum of infections and continues to result in increased acquisition of resistance to antibiotics2,3. Carbapenems are considered the most reliable and last resort class of antibiotics that are effective in the treatment of infections caused by ESBL-producing bacteria4. Currently, one of the major threats is increasing resistance to carbapenems, especially in K. pneumoniae5,6.

K. pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC), a class A carbapenemase, is mostly located on self-conjugative plasmids and is strongly associated with K. pneumoniae, with KPC-2 and KPC-3 being the most common variants7. KPC enzymes hydrolyse carbapenems, cephalosporins, and monobactams. Although blaKPC genes have been reported worldwide8, only sporadic cases of K. pneumoniae isolates harboring blaKPC−2 have been documented in the UAE9,10, with a recent outbreak in Oman11. blaKPC−2 is known to disseminate through plasmids of different sizes and replicon types9,12–19. KPC-producing K. pneumoniae are largely polyclonal, wiyth some sequence types (STs) being confined to specific geographical regions, but most being considered as epidemic and/or endemic20. The blaKPC−2 gene is known to be associated with several STs, with the most common being ST11, ST258 and ST231.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) technology is increasingly used for tracking the clonal expansion of multidrug-resistant (MDR) K. pneumoniae recovered either from the community or hospital settings, for identifying acquired antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) but also virulence genes21.

This study provides a comparative genomic analysis of blaKPC-2-producing K. pneumoniae isolates recovered in Abu Dhabi (2017–2018) in relation to previously reported UAE isolates, focusing on antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), plasmid backbones, and phylogenetic characteristics.

Materials and methods

Strains

A total of 162 non-duplicate carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) isolates recovered between October 2017 and December 2018 in four hospitals in the Abu Dhabi Emirate and stored at − 80 °C in Tryptic Soy Broth (MAST, Merseyide, UK) containing 20% glycerol in the Department of Medical Microbiology and Immunology, UAE University, were investigated. PCR for the carriage of the blaKPC gene22 was carried out. Only the isolates positive for blaKPC (n = 3) were further characterized using WGS and included in this study.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

The antibiotic susceptibility of the isolates was tested via the Vitek2 compact system (bioMérieux, France), and the AST-N419 card was used to test for the following antibiotics: ampicillin/sulbactam, cefepime, ceftazidime, cefotaxime, ceftazidime/avibactam, ceftolozane/tazobactam, piperacillin/tazobactam, imipenem, meropenem, gentamicin, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, tigecycline and cotrimoxazole. The minimum inhibitory concentration of colistin was determined via broth microdilution (BMD) in cation-adjusted Muller‒Hinton broth (Oxoid, UK) using of colistin sulfate (Sigma‒Aldrich, USA). ATCC 25,922 was used as a control. Susceptibility testing was interpreted according to the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) recommendations23, with the exception of tigecycline, where FDA breakpoints were used24. Strains were considered multidrug resistant if they were non-susceptible to ≥ 3 different classes of drugs tested.

Plasmid profile analysis and southern blot hybridization

Plasmid extraction and profiling were performed using a modified Kado and Liu method25. Bacterial cells were collected from overnight cultures on TSA at 37 °C, suspended in lysing solution (3% SDS, 50 mM Tris, pH 12.57), and incubated at 60 °C for 45 min with regular agitation. Phenol-chloroform extraction followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 15 min was used to for plasmid extraction, those latter being anamyzed on a 0.8% agarose gel at 120 V for 3 h. Gels were stained with ethidium bromide, destained, and visualized using the Biometra gel documentation system.

For Southern blot hybridization, gels were depurinated (0.25 M HCl), denatured (0.5 M NaOH, 1 M NaCl), and neutralized (1 M Tris, 0.6 M NaCl), with each step performed for 15 min at room temperature and repeated twice. Plasmids were transferred to Hybond N + membranes (Roche) via overnight capillary transfer in 20X SSC and cross-linked using UV light. Hybridization probe for blaKPC−2 was generated by PCR, purified, quantified, and labeled with digoxigenin (DIG). Membranes were pre-hybridized in buffer and hybridized at optimal temperatures calculated based on probe characteristics. Post-hybridization washes were conducted with 2X SSC/0.1% SDS at room temperature and 0.1X SSC/0.1% SDS at 68 °C. Detection was achieved using DIG-labeled antibodies and colorimetric substrates, and membranes were visualized using the Biometra system.

Whole-genome sequencing

The genomic DNA of the three isolates harboring blaKPC was extracted via the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by sequencing via the Illumina NovaSeq platform (150 bp paired-end) via a commercial service by Novogene UK. The reads were assembled via SPAdes 3.9, which is an online tool (http://www.genomicepidemiology.org/). We retrieved raw reads for all published UAE blaKPC− 2–positive K. pneumoniae available in public repositories (ABC220 and PFZ 360, 363, 401, 410, 529 and 758 isolates (identified earlier to carry blaKPC− 2 were downloaded from ENA (ERR6018697, ERR9126960, ERR9126959, ERR9361113, ERR9361122, ERR9357813, and ERR9419472) and reprocessed them with the same pipeline used for our isolates (assembly, ARG/plasmid/transposon annotation). Inclusion of these data provided country-level context for a rare mechanism, reduced analytic bias from a small single-site collection, and aligned with international best practices that encourage re-use of public genomes to strengthen surveillance and comparative analyses). The acquired resistance gene content was assessed via ResFinder 4.1 via default parameters26, and the plasmid replicon types were determined via PlasmidFinder version 2.127 via an identity percentage higher than 95% and a coverage cut-off greater than 90%. The transposons and IS elements were identified via the ISFinder database (https://www-is.biotoul.fr/). The presence of the virulence gene was identified by aligning the sequences of virulence factors from the database (http://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/). The contigs were uploaded to Ridom SeqSphere to create a phylogenetic tree of the different STs for comparison with publicly available genomes. For further analysis of the blaKPC− 2-carrying plasmids, the plasmid sequences were inferred from whole-genome sequences by using Plasmid SPAdes (http://spades.bioinf.spbau.ru/plasmidSPAdes/) with default parameters and PLACNETw28. The reconstructed plasmid contigs were aligned against the NCBI database to select the best plasmid match. The BRIG tool was used to display circular comparisons between the new reconstructed plasmids and the references. For the phylogenetic comparison of blaKPC− 2-producing isolates, Ridom SeqSphere was used to retrieve 1,546 complete genomes of K. pneumoniae from the NCBI database. The software was then utilized to analyze the isolates, and only those harboring the blaKPC− 2 gene across different ST types (n = 362) were selected for phylogenetic tree construction.

All read data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI BioSample database under BioProject ID PRJNA1209551 (SAMN46223266, SAMN46223267, SAMN46223268) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/1209551).

Results

Isolate characteristics

Three blaKPC−2−positive K. pneumoniae isolates, namely CRE51, CRE79 and CRE93, were recovered from urine, perianal swabs and rectal swabs, respectively. By inclusion criteria, all isolates in the screening cohort were carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP), i.e., non-susceptible to at least one carbapenem (162/162, 100%). Among the three blaKPC−2-positive isolates subjected to WGS, imipenem and meropenem non-susceptibility was observed (Supplementary Table 1). All three remained susceptible to ceftazidime–avibactam, consistent with expected activity against KPC producers.

Clonality of the KPC-2-producing isolates

As shown in Table 2, the blaKPC-producing K. pneumoniae isolates were distributed across six different STs: ST11, ST14, ST15, ST20, ST48, and ST231. Among these, CRE51 (ST11) shared its sequence type with PFZ529, whereas CRE93 (ST231) clustered with PFZ360 and PFZ363, despite notable time gaps between their isolation. Other strains included ABC220 (ST14), CRE79 (ST20), PFZ401 (ST15), PFZ410 (ST15), and PFZ758 (ST48). Furthermore, core genome MLST analysis confirmed that all three ST231 isolates (CRE93, PFZ360, and PFZ363) were closely related, forming a distinct cluster (Fig. 1). In contrast, CRE51 and PFZ529, which belong to ST11, did not cluster together. A total of 362 publicly available complete K. pneumoniae genomes harboring the blaKPC−2 gene were analyzed, along with our isolates, highlighted in red in Fig. 2. Our ST231 isolates (CRE93, PFZ360, and PFZ363) formed a distinct cluster, indicating close genetic relatedness, while our ST11 isolates (CRE51 and PFZ529) were dispersed, reflecting the broader diversity within the ST11 lineage—the most prevalent sequence type, with 220 isolates. The phylogenetic tree also highlighted other key sequence types associated with blaKPC−2, including ST258 (49 isolates), ST15 (12 isolates), and ST307 (9 isolates), which formed well-defined clusters. In total, 37 different sequence types were identified, highlighting the genetic diversity of blaKPC−2-producing K. pneumoniae. Notably, several sequence types also harbored additional carbapenemase genes, such as blaNDM−1, blaNDM−5, blaOXA−232, blaOXA−48, and blaIMP−8. Worth mentioning, blaOXA−232 was found in addition to blaKPC−2 only in our isolates (CRE93 and PFZ360). Notably, ST20 was represented by a single isolate (CRE79), uniquely associated with blaKPC−2 in this dataset.

Resistome of the KPC-2-carrying isolates

The antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) harbored by the KPC-producing K. pneumoniae isolates are presented in Fig. 3. All the isolates possessed blaKPC−2 with CRE93 and PFZ360 additionally harboring the blaOXA−232 carbapenemase gene. Notably, all the isolates additionally possessed an ESBL gene, but also broad-spectrum aminoglycoside resistance determinants, with the 16 S rRNA methylase encoding genes rmtB in CRE51 and rmtF in CRE93, PFZ360 and PFZ363. The majority of the isolates contained the following plasmid-mediated fluoroquinolone resistance genes, namely aac(6′)Ib-cr, qnrB1 or qnrS1, and the oqxAB multidrug efflux pump. Other antibiotic resistance genes detected included dihydrofolate reductases, macrolides, fosfomycin, and chloramphenicol resistance determinants (Fig. 3).

Virulence genes of KPC-2-carrying isolates

The virulence profile of the isolates was assessed, the genes of each isolate were categorized into subsystem categories, and further subdivision was performed for genes involved in virulence, pathogenesis and defense (Supplementary Table 3). None of the isolates had colibactin genes, which play a role in inducing DNA damage. The rmpA/A2 genes were also absent. The type 3 fimbriae cluster (mrkABCDFHIJ), which belongs to the chaperone-usher class of fimbriae, was found in all the isolates. Additionally, our results revealed another gene cluster coding for enterobactin synthesis (entA, entB, entC, entE, and entF) in all the strains. All the aerobactin genes were present to some extent. The yersiniabactin siderophore cluster (ybtAEPQSTUX) and the yersiniabactin receptor gene fyuA were present in all the isolates, with the exception of CRE79.

The T6SS, which contains 18 core conserved genes that are encoding the proteins making up the basic secretion apparatus, was identified in all sequenced isolates. The capsular typing analysis revealed that the ST231 isolates were associated with K-type KL51, whereas the ST15 isolates were linked to K-type KL19. Additionally, several other KL types, namely, KL47, KL111, KL64, KL2, and KL62, were identified.

blaKPC−2-carrying plasmids and the genetic environment of blaKPC−2

A total of three to five plasmids were identified in all isolates. Plasmids of various compatibility groups, including IncFII, IncR, IncFIB, IncFIA, ColRNAI, and ColKP3, were detected across the UAE isolates. Based on short-read WGS plasmid reconstruction and Southern blot analysis, the blaKPC−2 genes were inferred to be plasmid-borne in all isolates, with the exception of PFZ758, in which the hybridization pattern suggested a possible chromosomal location (Supplementary Fig. 1). The blaOXA−232 gene was associated with small (~ 6,110-bp) plasmids in isolates CRE93 and PFZ360. The characteristics of the blaKPC−2-carrying plasmids are summarized in Table 1. All isolates analyzed carried blaKPC−2 on IncFII plasmids, except for ABC220 and PFZ758. Notably, pCRE51-KPC also contained an IncR replicon in addition to IncFII, consistent with a multireplicon plasmid.The analysis also revealed that other resistance genes, such as blaTEM−15 and dfrA14 in pCRE79 and blaSHV−12, blaTEM−1, blaCTX−M−65, fosA3, catA2 and rmtB in pCRE51 and blaSHV−12 and blaCTX−M−65 in pPFZ529, were co-harbored with the blaKPC−2 gene on the same plasmid. Notably, pCRE93, pPFZ360, 363, 401 and 410 did not carry resistance genes other than blaKPC−2. Further analysis of pCRE51 revealed that the resistance gene fosA3 is flanked by two IS26 elements. In addition, there is a resistance determinant blaCTX−M−65 flanked by insertion sequences IS26 and IS903D, which is one of the commonly reported core platforms for the blaCTX−M−65 gene.

A BLAST search of the NCBI database identified several bacterial isolates with plasmids 99–100% identical to the sequences of the plasmids characterized in this study (with 85–99% coverage). These bacteria are mainly Enterobacterales. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the plasmids with the highest similarity to each reconstructed plasmid in this study. K. pneumoniae KP1034 (NZ_KP893385.1) and 16HN-263 (NZ_CP045264.1) (recovered in 2010, 2016, China), pKPC2 (CP061353.1), pKPC2_1332 (CP063432.1) and pKPC2_sg1 (MN542377.1) (recovered in 2015, 2014 and 2013, Singapore) were the genomes most similar to those of pCRE51 and pCRE79, respectively, and all of them were obtained from human samples (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B).

K. pneumoniae BA33875 (CP035181.1) (recovered in 2016) from human samples in India presented the highest similarity to the reconstructed plasmids pCRE93-KPC, pPFZ360-KPC, pPFZ363-KPC, pPFZ401-KPC and pPFZ410-KPC (Supplementary Fig. 2C), which also presented high similarity.

In the case of PFZ529, the reconstructed plasmid was highly similar to those of BJ108KPC (NZ_MT108209.1) and CR-hvkP005-KPC-P1 (NZ_CP119014.1), which were isolated from human samples in China (recovered in 2017) (Supplementary Fig. 2D).

The ST14, ST15, and ST231 strains, on the other hand, predominantly harbor the blaKPC−2 gene within the Tn4401b transposon. These strains are associated with IncFII or IncX3 plasmids, with plasmid sizes varying between 46,900 and 88,451 bp. Unlike the ST11 strains, the majority of the ST14, ST15, and ST231 strains do not carry additional resistance genes, which suggests a more focused role of these plasmids in carbapenem resistance. In contrast, the ST20 strain carries not only the blaKPC−2 gene on an IncFII plasmid but also blaTEM−15 and dfrA14, resulting in a broader resistance profile. Discussion

The blaKPC genes are important sources of carbapenem resistance worldwide. In this retrospective study, the blaKPC−2-producing K. pneumoniae isolates analyzed in this study were recovered from diverse specimen types (urine, wound, rectal/perianal swabs, respiratory samples, and blood) across multiple tertiary-care hospitals in UAE (Table 2). This diversity highlights the clinical and epidemiological relevance of these isolates, encompassing both infection and colonization events in different hospital settings. To mitigate the limitations of a small, single-period collection, we incorporated all publicly available UAE blaKPC−2 genomes and reanalyzed them under a unified pipeline, consistent with genomic-surveillance guidance, thereby enabling a national-scale comparative view of STs, plasmid backbones, and transposon platforms. All the strains were highly resistant to the most commonly used antimicrobial agents. All the isolates presented XDR phenotypes, and they were resistant to agents of all the antimicrobial categories tested except for tigecycline and ceftazidime/avibactam. In addition to the blaKPC−2 gene, other β-lactamase genes (such as blaCTX−M, blaSHV and blaTEM) were identified. The blaCTX−M−65 gene identified in CRE51 and PFZ529 encoded a group 9 CTX-M β-lactamase gene, being responsible for broad spectrum cephalosporin resistance29. CTX-M-65 is a variant of CTX-M-14 that differs by 2 amino acid substitutions (Ala78Val and Ser273Arg)30, and whose gene was identified in the variable regions of F33:A-:B- plasmids from food-producing animals in China31. The coexistence of these multiple broad-spectrum β-lactamase genes in KPC-producing K. pneumoniae confers resistance to 1st-, 2nd- and 3rd-generation cephalosporins, leading to difficulties in treating the corresponding bacterial infections. Recently, studies have shown that antibiotic stress, such as exposure to carbapenems, can significantly increase the relative copy number of plasmid-borne resistance genes like blaKPC−2, leading to elevated resistance levels. This highlights the role of selective pressure in enhancing plasmid-mediated resistance and underscores the dynamic nature of resistance phenotypes32.

Recent studies have identified a “transitional state” of resistance phenotypes in KPC-producing K. pneumoniae, where strains co-harbor both blaKPC−2 and blaKPC−33 variants. blaKPC−2 confers resistance to carbapenems but sensitivity to CAZ/AVI, while blaKPC−33 is resistant to CAZ/AVI but sensitive to carbapenems. This coexistence creates mixed populations, complicating treatment approaches, particularly under CAZ/AVI exposure. The rapid transition between these states, often within 9 days of therapy, highlights the need for advanced diagnostic methods to identify these mixed strains and guide effective treatment strategies33.

MLST revealed that the KPC-producing K. pneumoniae strains from the UAE were composed of various STs (ST11, ST20, ST48, ST14, ST15 and ST231). Consistent with previous reports, ST11 was the major ST type attributed to the spread of KPC-2 in CRKP isolates reported by several studies. A disseminated ST11 KPC-KP clone has been identified in China, Japan and Korea34. In previous studies, ST20 has always been correlated with the presence of the blaNDM−1 gene35,36. While ST20 is typically associated with blaNDM−1, its linkage to blaKPC−2 has also been observed in our study, highlighting its role in the dissemination of carbapenem resistance genes. This association contrasts with previous reports and underscores the diversity of antimicrobial resistance gene distribution. ST231 has been reported in multiple regions and is frequently associated with blaOXA−232. Mancini et al.37 reported the emergence of a multidrug-resistant (MDR) K. pneumoniae ST231 strain in Switzerland, which carried blaCTX−M−15 and rmtF genes. A more recent study on Indian isolates confirmed a strong association between blaOXA−232 and ST23138. In our study, all three ST231 isolates carried blaKPC−2. Additionally, two isolates (CRE93 and PFZ360) harbored both blaKPC−2 and blaOXA−232, whereas PFZ363 carried only blaKPC−2.

Plasmids are key vectors for the spread of resistance genes such as blaKPC−2, with IncFII plasmids being the most frequently detected39. In our study, all in silico reconstructed plasmids carrying blaKPC−2 belonged to IncFII, except for ABC220 and PFZ758. The IncFII family replicates across various enterobacterial species, significantly contributing to antimicrobial resistance gene dissemination40. While several plasmid types carrying blaKPC−2 have been identified, previous UAE studies reported IncX3 as the carrier (ABC220), differing from our IncFII findings. Recent reports indicate that IncFII plasmids may harbor both blaKPC−2 and blaKPC−33, illustrating their genetic plasticity and clinical relevance in shaping resistance phenotypes33. A limitation of our study is that plasmid and chromosomal locations of blaKPC−2 were inferred from short-read assemblies and supported by Southern blot hybridization. While these approaches provide useful insights, they cannot definitively distinguish chromosomal integration from plasmid carriage. Methods such as pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) with I-CeuI digestion, which selectively cuts the chromosome at rRNA operons while leaving plasmids intact, or long-read sequencing would be required to confirm these genomic contexts.

In terms of the genetic context surrounding the blaKPC−2 genes, there are three different scenarios: (1) Tn4401-like transposons with ISKpn7-blaKPC−2-ISKpn6 as their core structure; (2) Tn1721-based transposons featuring a core structure of ISKpn27-blaKPC−2-ΔISKpn6; and (3) an “ISKpn27-blaKPC−2-ΔISKpn6” cassette-centering, IS26-like transposable element41. In most countries and regions, such as Europe and the United States, blaKPC−2 is located within Tn440142.

Comparative analysis of blaKPC-adjacent regions in the sequences of our KPC-Kp isolates identified two Tn3-like transposons, Tn4401b and Tn1721a, associated with blaKPC−2, consistent with their known role in mobilizing resistance genes. ​ In the case of CRE51, CRE79 and PFZ529, our results are consistent with those from previous studies in which linear genomic alignment revealed that an almost identical cassette (Tn1172a: ISKpn6-blaKPC−2-ISKpn27) surrounded blaKPC−2. In the case of CRE51, the plasmid belongs to the IncFII-IncR type, which is identical to the previously published plasmid of ST859 (a single locus variant of ST11) isolated from China43. Notably, a search of public databases for the ISKpn6-blaKPC−2-ISKpn27 harboring sequences revealed its presence on several plasmids across numerous bacterial species previously reported from many countries. In a recent study from Saudi Arabia44, the same flanking region was found surrounding blaKPC−2 on an IncX6 plasmid isolated from K. quasipneumoniae. The insertion of the fosA3 gene sandwiched by two IS26 sequences was found in the transferable blaKPC−2-carrying plasmid, resulting in additional fosfomycin resistance. The alarming coexistence of fosA3 and other resistance genes, including blaKPC−2, blaCTX−M−65, blaSHV−12, rmtB and catA2, would allow fosA3 to be co-selected by most β-lactams and aminoglycosides and thus further facilitate its dissemination42. Beyond blaKPC−2, carbapenem resistance in K. pneumoniae reflects multiple mechanisms. In the UAE, OXA-48-like and NDM carbapenemases predominate in CRE surveillance, with KPC detected sporadically (10). In addition, outer-membrane porin alterations (e.g., OmpK35/OmpK36 loss or mutation), particularly when combined with ESBL or AmpC production, can confer or augment carbapenem non-susceptibility. These mechanisms contextualize our finding that two isolates co-harbored blaOXA−232 and underscore the importance of integrating genotypic data with phenotypes in local stewardship programs.

These KPC-producing K. pneumoniae strains illustrate the clinical challenges posed by emerging resistance and pathogenesis, and they are responsible for life-threatening infections that affect hospitalized and immunocompromised individuals. More than 50% of vulnerable patients with hospital-acquired pneumonia die when their infection is caused by a strain that produces KPC-2. On the basis of the literature, the presence of blaKPC−2 in the Gulf Region and in the UAE used to be very rare and sporadic9–11.
