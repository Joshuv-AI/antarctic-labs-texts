Action plan strategies for a new approach to the combination of yeast and ultrasonic waves for the reduction of heavy metal residues in rice
Authors: Bohlooli Shahnaz, Ramezan Yousef, Hosseini Hedayat, Eskandari Soheyl, Esfarjani Fatemeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study was conducted to combine two physical (ultrasonic) and biological (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) methods to reduce heavy metals such as Arsenic (As), Cadmium (Cd), and Lead (Pb) in rice, which is the most widely consumed grain. The concentrations of As, Cd, and Pb were found to be significantly affected after different treatments, were on uncooked rice samples (R1-R9): As R9 (2.05 ± 0.01) Cd R1, R3, R5, R8 (1.20 ± 0.01), Pb R6 (3.04 ± 0.173) (µgkg− 1) and in cooked rice samples were (C1-C10): As C10 (0.35 ± 0.27) Cd C9, C10 (1.00 ± 0.02), Pb C1 (3.52 ± 0.17) (µgkg− 1). The results showed that using ultrasound treatment combined with yeast (C10) had the greatest effect on reducing As among all treatments. In addition, C9, and C10 treatments were effective in reducing Cd concentration in cooked rice samples, and R6 treatment was effective in reducing Pb concentration in uncooked samples. The rice’s thermal qualities and specific textural traits were also affected by some or all of these treatments. The samples with modified particle sizes had the greatest sensory evaluation scores (C9). Moreover, sensory evaluation results showed that a specific treatment combining yeast and ultrasound (C3) maintained good sensory qualities and did not negatively affect the sensory qualities without sacrificing texture. Overall, this approach provides a promising method for the reduction of heavy metals in rice. A combination of biological and physical treatments can be used to reduce the heavy metals content (As, Cd, Pb) in rice. Stakeholders can use the aforementioned actions to optimize heavy metal content in the food chain, which requires the support of policymakers to promote public health.Keywords: Heavy metals (as, Cd, Pb), ICP-Mass, Rice, Yeast, Ultrasound, Functional propertiesSubject terms: Nutrition, Health policy

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sative L.) is a staple food, particularly in developing countries and one of the most widely consumed nutritious cereals, which is high in carbohydrates and fibers, moderate in vitamins and minerals, low in proteins, and contributes to more than half of the needs of the world’s population, especially Asian countries as in Iran1, . The average consumption of rice in the world and Iran is about 58.8 and 42.5 (kg per capita), respectively. Thus, it is estimated that rice is the second most widely consumed product in Iran2. Rice is the staple food or major dietary source for almost 50% of the world’s population. Besides the high nutritional value of rice, it is of considerable importance to pay attention to its pollutants, especially toxic metals such as Arsenic (As), Cadmium (Cd), and Lead (Pb)1. The accumulation of heavy metal compounds formed in plants intended for human consumption may be an important factor in their toxicity to humans3. Soil contamination with heavy metals is considered a global challenge, and the uptake of heavy metals such as cadmium, lead, mercury, and other heavy metals in agricultural fields poses a challenge to human health4. Excessive accumulation of these metals causes cellular components such as lipids, proteins, enzymes, and DNA to change and destroy human life5.

In a study conducted in Iran to explore the existence of As and Pb in rice from various Iranian regions as well as to calculate the heavy metals’ carcinogenic potential in rice consumers; the systematic review part showed that the Golestan province (0.01 ± 0.01 mg/kg d.w) and the Gillan province (3 mg/kg d.w) had the lowest and maximum concentration of As, whereas Shahrekord (0.07 ± 0.02 mg/kg d.w) and Mazandaran province (35 mg/kg d.w) had the highest concentration of Pb6. The anthropogenic activities are directed towards environmental contamination caused due to the accumulation of heavy metals (naturally occurring elements) into water bodies, soil7, food, feed, and water8. Discharged wastewater from commercial industries includes traces of heavy metal pollutants like As, Cd, and Pb. Some of these elements in the form of pesticides, insecticides, and fertilizers are also required to carry out the biological metabolism, but due to their high dosage, they enter into the food chain and so are manifested as highly toxic to the flora and fauna7.

Arsenic (As) is a toxic element whose contamination in water and food has always been one of the major scientific challenges9,10. It can be introduced into surface water by discharging industrial effluents, petroleum refining, use of pesticides, herbicides, and fertilizers containing arsenic in agriculture, etc11,12. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has placed As in Group 1 of carcinogenic agents13. Genotoxicity in humans is promoted by consuming 500 g of cooked rice with an As content ≥ 200 mg14. Rice is more likely to accumulate heavy metals as compared to other cereal grains, particularly Cd and As15,16.

Exposure to more than the safe level of As to humans causes keratosis; melanosis; skin, bladder, and lung cancers; hyperkeratosis; damaged intellectual (brain) function; gangrene; cardiac coronary diseases; bronchiectasis, and diabetes17.

Cadmium (Cd) has long been regarded as a toxic trace element that can primarily cause kidney damage and threaten pulmonary, cardiovascular, and musculoskeletal systems. The major source of Cadmium intake for Asian countries is rice accumulates more Cd than other cereals. Handling activities (such as many agricultural practices) could increase exposure to Cd18.

Lead (Pb) is the third most harmful element after Cd, for its occurrence, toxicity, and exposure potential. Pb accumulation in rice can reduce rice growth and yield, as well as damage the human body’s immune, digestive, and nervous systems19,20.

Biological processes are an alternative to the traditional treatments. It has been reported that yeast can remove heavy metals from solutions21. Among all the methods, using living organisms to absorb pollutants and remove heavy metals from the environment is quite interesting. Microorganisms are the most acceptable because they are easier to work with. The main advantage of using microorganisms to remove toxic elements is their safety in human aspects. One of these microorganisms is the bakery yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), widely used in the food industry8. Some studies have proved the removal of arsenic10, cadmium22, and lead23 by S. cerevisiae. It is generally considered safe for human consumption10,22,23.

The acoustic cavitation property of Ultrasound as an emerging and non-thermal technology, is a promising technique for the enhancement of chemical treatment. Successful applications of ultrasonic on enzyme inactivation phytic acid reduction and heavy metal decontamination have been reported previously24. Ultrasound-assisted digestion protocols have been applied to digest and determine heavy metals in various environmental and biological samples25.

Therefore, to our knowledge, this is the first study combining biological and physical methods for reducing heavy metal residues (As, Cd, Pb) in uncooked and cooked rice using S. cerevisiae yeast and ultrasonic waves, and determining thermal properties, texture, pH and compared morphological features by using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) also, to recommend an action plan for policymakers by planning how to reduce this issue in rice to increase public health.

Materials and methods

Sampling

In this semi-experimental study, verified contaminated raw rice (Oryza sativa L.) samples (1000 g) were randomly selected from a batch with a high concentration of As (4.02 ± 0.01), Cd (2 ± 0.01) and Pb (8.43 ± 0.01) (µg kg− 1) which had been analyzed and reported by ICP-Mass (R0 = Control sample without any treatment).

Sample preparation

Contaminated rice samples were weighed in the laboratory by using Sartorius Cubis precision balance with an accuracy of ± 0.0001 g (Sartorius, Gottingen, Germany)26, and divided into 20 samples (50 g), then put into zipper plastic bags, tagged and stored in laboratory condition until the beginning of the study. Preparation of uncooked samples including washing, soaking, and cooking steps was done according to different treatments (Table 1).

Washing and cooking methods

For each sample, 50 ± 0.001 (g) rice was washed three times with deionized water (DW) and then the water was discarded. DW (200 mL, corresponding to a rice-to-water ratio of 1:4 by weight) was used for the initial washing, pre-soaking, and parboiling. After that, rice samples were cooked in fresh deionized water using the absorption method (rice to water 1:2 by volume or 1:2.5 by weight)1,26.

Reagents and solutions

All chemical reagents used in the experiments were received as analytical purity from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). DW with a conductivity of 220 V (LAN SHAN, LSRO-701 A, Iran) was used for all required solubilizations. All pipettes and containers were acid washed by soaking in 10% (v/v) HNO3 overnight to prevent the transmission of elemental contamination and then rinsed with DW. All the glassware was washed with DW before usage27.

The yeast S. cerevisiae (PTCC 5010) was in the form of freeze-dried (Razi yeast and alcohol company, Iran). Provide biomass seed culture and its colony count.

The master culture solution of S. cerevisiae was daily activated for each series of bio-absorption tests by inoculation of 5% v/v. The daily new culture solution was activated by shaking for 16 h at 27 °C. Then cell count was done by serial dilution procedure (1 ml of it, was diluted at a ratio of 1:10 and this was repeated 10 times serially). From each dilution, 1 mL was poured into a plate of Nutrient Agar medium, in duplicate, and the plates were incubated at 30 °C for 3 days. Counting of yeast colonies was then performed and revealed the mean of yeast count 1.014 × 1010 CFU/mL28.

Physical method

Physical treatment was carried out within an ultrasound bath processor (Parasonic Model 11s, Iran) operating at a frequency of 28 ± 5% kHz with a pressure of 10 W/cm2 and maximum output power of 150 W. This operation was conducted for 35 min intervals at the constant temperature of 25 ± 1 °C (by circulating cold water in the bath). Each treatment was performed with two replicates for each sample providing twenty subsamples26.

Samples preparing for laboratory (ICP-MS)

All treated samples (as shown in Table 1) were cooled at room temperature in Pyrex plates before being put in zipped-lock bags and tagged with a code. Each sample was thoroughly mixed and homogenized before being transported to the laboratory to analyze heavy metal residues (As, Cd, Pb). The samples were stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C immediately before the preparation steps and yeast and ultrasound treatment were applied sequentially.

To prevent chemical contamination, sterile and chemical-resistant flask containers were used. Initial analytical work began immediately after cleaning all equipment with dilute nitric acid (HNO3 10%) and then rinsing with DW. The samples were put in a petri dish to dry in a 70 °C oven for 24 h until they turned into a fine powder28.

10 ml of 65% nitric acid and 37% hydrogen peroxide (v/v) were used for the digestion of 0.05 g of dried rice samples (Merk, Darmstadt, Germany). The solution was left at room temperature overnight until it became clear. The digestion was stopped at 150 °C after 4 h after bringing the solution to room temperature (22–23 °C). The solution was then diluted to 50 ml with DW, filtered with 0.45 l of acid-resistant filter paper, and stored for later examination29. Using the heat-block-assisted acid digestion method and the ICP-MS methodology following the FDA30 Elemental Analysis Manual, the total amount of As, Pb, and Cd were analyzed. The blank solution was made similarly but without the use of rice. The criteria for detection in rice using ICP-MS are listed in Table 2. The method employed in inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, also known as ICP-MS, included ionizing the sample with an inductively coupled plasma. ICP-MS is well-known for its application in detecting exceedingly low quantities of different non-metals and metals in liquid samples. For determining the level of heavy metals in the food industry, this method is thought to be the fastest and most reliable. Estimation was done using an Inductively Coupled Plasma-Mass Spectrometer (ICP-MS, ULTIMA2, 6100 DRC-e Perkin Elmer Elan)29.

Phase II

Thermal properties

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) is a valuable technique used in various fields for analyzing phase transitions and thermal properties of materials32. The thermal behaviors of the samples were determined using DSC (Model Differential Thermal Analyzer (DTA), DSC 214 Polyma Differential Scanning Calorimeter, NETZSCH-Gerätebau GmbH, Germany).

Uncooked rice was weighted directly in a DSC pan, and DW was added to obtain a flour-to-water ratio of 1:2.3 (w: w). The pan was then hermetically sealed for 1 h before thermal analysis. Thermal scanning was undertaken from 4°C to 150°C at a heating rate of 10°C/min. The gelatinization onset (To), peak (Tp), and conclusion (Tc) temperatures and the transition enthalpy (ΔH) were determined from the peak area of the DSC endotherm33.

Textural properties

Texture profile analysis (TPA) of rice grains (with an average length of 0.9–1.0 cm) was performed using a texture analyzer (TA.XT plus, stable-micro system, United Kingdom) with a two-cycle compression. A cylindrical probe P/36 R was used for measuring the texture. To compress a pair of grains with a test speed of 5 cm/min. The instrument was calibrated with a 50 kg load cell. The deformation ratio was set at 50%. Hardness, adhesiveness, brittleness, cohesiveness, gumminess, chewiness, and elasticity were determined from the two-cycle curves. Fifteen pairs of grains (cooked rice) were measured for each sample34. Five replicate aliquots were tested on each sample and the recorded values from the resulting compression cycle for each test35.

pH value

Measuring the pH of rice samples during the treatment process is to monitor and control the acidity or alkalinity of the rice, which can affect its quality, safety, and nutritional properties. Specifically, and also helps optimize the rice treatment process for quality control, safety, and desired product characteristics36.

The pH was measured using a digital pH meter (Model MW151MAX, pH/ORP/Temp Meter, Europe). About 50 g of each uncooked sample was added to 200 mL of DW and soaked for 1 h, and the pH was then measured before and after different treatments. Calibration was performed using standard buffers provided by the manufacturer at pH 4, 7, and 10 at room temperature37. In each measurement, the electrodes were completely immersed in the samples.

Sensory evaluation of cook-treated rice samples

The sensory assessment of cooked rice plays a vital role in consumer approval. Numerous investigations have concentrated on evaluating the sensory attributes of rice, encompassing scent, flavor, consistency, and visual appeal. Scholarly inquiries have delved into techniques like quantitative descriptive analysis and principal component analysis to assess sensory features such as taste, texture, and appearance38,39. Moreover, the inclusion of elements such as sprouted barley in the preparation process has displayed the ability to elevate sensory characteristics, consequently resulting in heightened overall approval40. Analysis of consumer preferences for rice quality requires evaluation of concrete variables and consumption scenarios, supported by instrumental techniques that facilitate the prediction of quality adjustments throughout the cooking process41. Understanding consumer demand for rice quality entails evaluating concrete factors and consumption scenarios, with the assistance of instrumental techniques to forecast quality fluctuations during the cooking process.

In this study sensory evaluation of cook-treated rice was conducted by 10 trained panelists from Technical Quality System Golestan Company as the protocol of (MEILGAARD; CIVILLE; CARR,2024)42,43. Panelists were seated in individual sensory booths under white light and water was provided to clean the palate between evaluations. For the sensory evaluation of samples, Qadir and Wani’s method (2023) was used with some modifications. All samples were coded with random 1-digit numbers for identification and presented separately in a randomized order. Panelists tasted samples. A 9-point hedonic scale (1: dislike extremely, 5: neither like nor dislike, 15: like extremely) was used, and selected parameters were aroma, sweetness, appearance, particle size, elongation, coherence, stickiness, hardness, flavor, roughness, and overall acceptability44. Since, heavy metals were significantly reduced to safe levels, making the rice suitable for sensory evaluation.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Utilizing Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) proves to be an efficacious method for scrutinizing the microstructure of diverse materials at elevated magnifications. This advanced technique enables intricate visualization of surfaces through meticulous scanning with a concentrated electron beam, thereby furnishing insights into the tridimensional topography, composition, and morphology of samples45. TESCAN VEGA’s 4th generation Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) was used to analyze the surface etching of rice grains. The scanning electron microscope (SEM) is a powerful instrument that produces high-resolution surface images of a material by focusing an electron beam. It has a large depth of field for studying intricate three-dimensional structures and is capable of analyzing a wide range of materials, including metals, ceramics, polymers, and biological samples. SEM can be used in conjunction with other methods, such as X-ray computed tomography (CT) and X-ray microtomography (XMT), to provide a thorough understanding of a sample’s characteristics. Its adaptability and compatibility with many analytical methods make it an essential tool in many disciplines, including biology, geology, and materials science35,46.

Therefore, technical methods should be considered for the preparation of these types of samples. Performing the preparation steps according to the type of texture and structure for each sample in general such as fixing, dehydrating and drying are the same47.

Action plan strategies

The action plan is a valuable instrument for policymakers to devise strategies and attain specific objectives. It furnishes a framework for identifying and organizing strategies. The tool is efficacious and expeditious, enabling the observation of prospective alterations through a systematic approach to introspection48. The authors developed the action plan strategies based on their experiences and the relevant literature on the subject. In the present study, the technique was employed to identify the potential and limitations of the region and to propose practical solutions for the development of policy strategies to optimize safe food choices and reduce the heavy metals in food baskets in order to promote public health.

Statistical analysis

All examinations for each sample were performed three times. Data analysis was conducted using IBM’s Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 21. The data was summarized using counts and percentages for categorical variables and mean (Standard Error) for quantitative variables. To test the differences between means, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range post-hoc test was used to determine the significant differences (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

This research was carried out as a new approach to combining biological (Saccharomyces cerevisiae yeast) and physical methods (ultrasonic waves) for reducing heavy metal residues (As, Cd, Pb) in uncooked and cooked rice (Oryza sativa L.).

The results of different phases of research are as follows:

Phase I

The results showed the efficacy of different treatments to reduce the concentration (mean ± SE) of heavy metals (As, Cd, and Pb) in rice samples (Table 3). The average concentration of heavy metals like As, Cd, and Pb in raw and cooked rice grains significantly varied with treatments. Maximum arsenic was noted in R0 (4.02 µg/kg), while minimum in C10 (0.35 µg/kg). Similarly, Cd was also higher in R0 at 2.00 µg/kg and generally low in cooked samples. Minimum values were observed in C10. Among them, the maximum concentration of lead, 8.43 µg/kg, was obtained in R0, while the minimum value was found in C1 with a concentration of 3.52 µg/kg. A significant difference at p < 0.05 among the treatments was observed and expresses that methods of cooking applied have significant effects on the concentration of heavy metals.

The results showed that using ultrasound treatment combined with yeast C10 (0.35 ± 0.275) (µgkg− 1) treatment had the greatest effect on reducing As among other treatments.

Fortunately, several techniques are being investigated to reduce arsenic levels: One method is milling, which removes the bran layer, the concentration points for As. This reduces the amount of As but also removes important nutrients50. Milling is a common method of reducing As in rice. Arsenic concentrates in the bran layer, which is removed during milling51,52. However, milling also removes valuable nutrients, such as calcium and potassium51. A study conducted in Bangladesh revealed that parboiling whole-grain rice instead of rough rice resulted in a reduction in the inorganic As content of the final polished grain. However, this process also led to a statistically significant median enrichment of calcium and a reduction in potassium51. This approach had a minimal impact on milling quality, suggesting that it could be a viable method for reducing As while preserving nutrients. Another approach involves the use of manganese oxide nanoparticles to reduce As content in rice by milling using a ball mill method to give a particle size of 20–5000 nm manganese oxide nanoparticles52. This method can effectively reduce the content of As in paddy soil and rice produced.

The results of a study in 2019 in Iran emphasized the use of S. cerevisiae as safe technology to reduce the trace amounts of As in water resources and food53.

After different treatments, a significant change in Cd concentration was observed. The results showed that using ultrasound treatment and combined with yeast (C9 and C10) (1.00 ± 0.017 µgkg− 1) had the greatest effect on reducing Cd among other treatments. Yang et al. showed that the described method had the maximum Cd removal. Further analysis showed that the maximum sorption capacity of Lactobacillus plantarum for Cd increased and the equilibrium sorption capacity increased after ultrasonic intervention54. Rice must undergo Cd removal before becoming a food raw material. However, in industrial production, soaking is not sufficient to remove Cd from heavily contaminated rice55.

Past studies used various methods to effectively remove Cd from rice flour, without focusing on Cd reduction from whole rice. There are several techniques to remove Cd from rice56. The cleaning method is the most convenient method for Cd removal, but its Cd removal rate is limited57. The rate of Cd removal in the soaking method can be higher, but this method requires hours of soaking time and thus loses the quality of rice58. At the same time, a study showed that the removal rate of Cd was minimal when using short-term (1–8 h) soaking59. Starch extraction from Cd-contaminated rice was done using an alkaline method, the finished starch had a significant reduction in Cd content compared to the raw material60. The highest Cd removal rate, up to 92%, was obtained using hydrochloric acid or disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution for soaking rice proteins61. However, chemical reagents cannot be used in actual food production because their residues remain in the treated material. So, Biological methods are safe and also the rate of Cd removal is high. In this case, the reduction of Cd mainly involves microbial fermentation, absorption of Cd and its metabolites, separation of Cd from proteins, or promoting the dissolution of Cd-binding proteins to reduce Cd in the system62,63. Studies have shown that fermentation of L. plantarum achieved more of Cd64–66.

A significant change in Pb concentration was observed after different treatments. The lowest Pb concentration was in sample R6 (3.04 ± 0.017) where yeast was used. Huang et al., in 2022, systematically reviewed and meta-analyzed the data that was available on the concentrations of Pb and Cd in rice from various parts of China. A total of 24 articles on Pb levels and 29 studies on Cd levels, published between 2011 and 2021, were included in the meta-analysis. For rice, the combined Pb content was 0.10 mg/kg dry weight (95% CI 0.08 − 0.11) and the combined Cd content was 0.16 mg/kg dry weight (95% CI 0.14 − 0.18). These values fall within the range given by the national standards for food safety. Nonetheless, the combined target hazard quotient for both metals for both adults and kids was more than 1.0, indicating a possible health risk associated with rice eating67. Pb in cereals, pulses, and legumes is allowed up to 0.2 mg/kg (200 µg/kg) according to the Codex Standard68. Additionally, the highest amount of Cd allowed in rice is 0.4 mg/kg, according to the Codex Alimentarius Commission69.

Therefore, the combination of ultrasound and yeast was used to reduce heavy metal content in rice due to their complementary mechanisms, which enhance the removal process. Here’s the reasoning behind this approach:

Ultrasound treatment

Ultrasound involves using high-frequency sound waves to create mechanical vibrations in liquids. This treatment helps reduce heavy metal content by:

Cavitation Ultrasound generates tiny bubbles that collapse near the surface of rice grains, creating intense micro-jets and turbulence. This mechanical action helps break up contaminants and loosen metals bound to the surface of rice grains.

Enhanced Desorption The cavitation effect of ultrasound improves the desorption (detachment) of heavy metals from rice surfaces, making it easier to remove the contaminants.

Increased Surface Area Exposure Ultrasound increases the surface area exposed to the cleaning agents (like yeast or water), improving the efficiency of metal removal from rice.

Yeast application

Certain species of yeast, especially biosorption-capable yeasts, can naturally bind to heavy metals through their cell walls. The yeast contributes to heavy metal removal by:

Biosorption: Yeast cells have functional groups such as hydroxyl, carboxyl, and phosphate on their cell walls, which can attract and bind heavy metals like arsenic, cadmium, and lead, effectively removing them from rice grains.

Bioaccumulation: Some yeast strains can accumulate heavy metals inside their cells, which aids in capturing metals from the surrounding environment (such as rice surfaces during treatment).

Non-toxic and Biodegradable: Yeast is a safe, natural, and biodegradable option, making it an eco-friendly method for removing heavy metals compared to chemical treatments.

Combined effect

When ultrasound and yeast are combined:

Ultrasound enhances yeast performance: Ultrasound can disrupt the rice surface and release trapped heavy metals, making them more accessible for biosorption by yeast. Additionally, ultrasound can help disperse the yeast more uniformly across the rice grains, ensuring more efficient metal capture.

Increased Removal Efficiency: The combination results in a synergistic effect where the ultrasound’s ability to detach metals and the yeast’s biosorption capabilities lead to a more effective reduction of heavy metal content.

Overall, the combination of ultrasound and yeast provides a more thorough and efficient method for reducing heavy metal contamination in rice by leveraging the mechanical action of ultrasound and the natural biosorption ability of yeast. This method is also safe, environmentally friendly, and scalable for food safety applications.

It was reported that the soaking of rice in water with a lower pH can increase the leaching of metals from rice starch due to the competition between metals and excess H + ions for binding to the carboxyl group of starch as a metal binding site. The effect of soaking solution pH on the removal of heavy metals was also reported for rice bran24,73.

Also, the relationship between pH changes and the bio-absorption efficacy and sensory qualities of rice is a complex interplay influenced by various factors such as processing methods, genetic traits, and environmental conditions. Understanding these relationships is crucial for improving rice quality and consumer acceptance.

Effects of pH on bio-absorption efficacy

Starch Digestibility: The digestibility of rice starch is significantly affected by pH levels during cooking and processing. Lower pH can enhance the hydrolysis rate of starch, leading to higher digestibility74.

Nutrient Availability: pH influences the solubility and availability of essential nutrients such as iron and zinc in rice grains. The mechanisms of nutrient uptake are regulated by both chelation and reduction processes, which are sensitive to pH changes. For instance, acidic conditions can enhance the release of phytosiderophores that facilitate iron absorption, thereby improving nutritional quality75.

Sensory qualities affected by pH

Texture and Cooking Properties: The pasting properties of rice flour are notably influenced by pH. At lower pH levels, the gel hardness increases, affecting the texture of cooked rice. This change in texture can significantly impact consumer preferences, as a softer and fluffier texture is generally more desirable76.

Aroma Compounds: The production of aromatic compounds, such as 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline (2-AP), is also affected by environmental conditions, including pH during grain formation. Variations in moisture and salinity during growth can lead to differences in aroma intensity, which is a critical sensory attribute for many rice consumers75.

Among 69 SEM micrographs of rice with different treatments, In Fig. 3, 20 images show scanning electron microscope images applied to uncooked rice. Each step of preparation of biological samples for SEM imaging can change the structure of the sample and create new undesirable structures such as wrinkling and artifacts.

The micrographs indicated that the non-sonicated control rice samples exhibited a dense and compact surface. In contrast, significant alterations were noted in the microstructure of uncooked rice kernels following sonication. The morphological integrity of the rice was compromised due to sonication, leading to the exposure of parenchyma cell walls, which became discernible. This process of sonication facilitated the exposure of the cells, uncovering the sub-aleurone layer predominantly composed of starch granules79. The sonication process induces structural alterations on the surface of the endosperm, facilitating the exposure of its cellular contents. A significant observation is the emergence of cracks and fissures as a result of sonication. As ultrasound waves travel through the medium, the bubbles undergo oscillation and collapse at the surface, creating cavitational zones and generating locally varying positive and negative pressures, which leads to the expansion or compression of the material80. The packing of the starch granules becomes fragmented and loosened as a result of the enhanced water penetration facilitated by the mechanical jet impact of power ultrasound81. Furthermore, the disruption of cell walls by ultrasonic waves increases the surface area significantly, facilitating the mass transfer of cellular fragments into the water medium during sonication82, and this results in empty cell walls making the surface porous. Sonication thus promoted the formation of micro-pores on the surface of the rice as well as cracks and fissures in the grain83. Also, numerous cracks appeared inside the rice after ultrasonic-assisted immersion83,84. The cracks could act as channels for water migration and facilitate starch hydration and gelatinization during rice cooking, which could provide softer textural properties85.

Following the ultrasound-assisted parboiling treatment, external fissures or cracks were no longer present. The starch granules exhibited an increase in size, merging together, sealing granular spaces, and forming a smooth surface through tight packing. As the duration and amplitude of the ultrasound treatment increased, a greater number of granules became slightly fused and lost their original shape. This alteration may be attributed to the de-branching of amylopectin, along with the rearrangement and alignment of starch chains, which were influenced by the ultrasound waves. This effect was likely due to acoustic streaming, shear force, and the penetration of water into the starch granules, which facilitated the transformation of random coils into a double helix structure86,87.

Nowadays, the relentless expansion of the global population has precipitated a concern about the need to ensure food security and address the contamination of food and agricultural products with heavy metals. Therefore, responsible organizations must screen and monitor the heavy metal concentrations in foodstuffs and crops, as screening tends to pick up slowly progressive conditions that are less aggressive and more amenable to treatment.

One of the primary sources of consumer exposure to heavy metals is food. Therefore, particular emphasis has been placed on this issue. In addition to controlling the initial quality of rice, it is important to find suitable process solutions to reduce rice contaminants.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effectiveness of diverse physical and biological interventions in alleviating As, Cd, and Pb levels in rice (Oryza sativa L.).A new and efficient combination of biological action of the Saccharomyces cerevisiae yeast with physical treatment of ultrasound was applied to reduce heavy metal residues of As, Cd, and Pb in rice staple grain consumed all over the world. According to the obtained results, combined treatment significantly reduced the content of arsenic, cadmium, and lead in raw and cooked rice without compromising the basic sensory/textural characteristics. The synergistic effect of ultrasound and yeast improved the biosorption and mechanical dislodgment, which resulted in the highest reduction of arsenic, especially in cooked rice, while this method also ameliorated the removal of cadmium and lead. Besides, this treatment maintained the thermal and sensory characteristics of rice; hence, this is a viable proposal for industrial application. These ultrasound-assisted yeast treatments did not affect the sensory attributes of critical importance to consumer acceptance, such as aroma, texture, and particle size. The findings indicate that this combination approach is highly effective in diminishing toxic heavy metals in rice but maintains its desirable qualities. The current study has provided not only a practical means to address one of the major public health concerns but also a ready-to-use blueprint for policymakers, food producers, and agriculture in general for applying the method in large-scale food processing. Such interventions have the potential to increase the safety of the food supply and, by extension, public health across the world, especially in rice-consuming countries. Further research is needed regarding this approach on different varieties of rice and its application on a larger scale, considering also the possibility of integration into food safety regulations and guidelines. Therefore, more research and development needed to create practical tools for implementation at the household scale seems necessary. Stakeholders can use the aforementioned actions to optimize heavy metal content in the food chain, which requires the support of policymakers for immediate actions in this field so that effective steps can be taken to improve public health.
