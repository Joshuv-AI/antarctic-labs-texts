LAX1 as a core biomarker in Alzheimer’s disease and periodontitis via the STAT signaling pathway
Authors: Han Guo, Tianyuan Guo, Xue Wang, Yang Bao, Zhiyong Zhang, Fang Xue, Dongmei Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and chronic periodontitis (PD) rarely share the medical spotlight, yet epidemiological mirroring hints at a common inflammatory root.MethodsMining four GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus) cohorts (211 AD + 27 controls; 24 PD + 23 controls), batch-corrected and validated by principal component analysis (PCA)/t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE)/uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), we identified 61 shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with lymphocyte transmembrane adaptor 1 (LAX1) ranked first in weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), maximum clique centrality (MCC), Degree, edge percolated component (EPC) and Stress algorithms, tightly co-expressed with colony-stimulating factor 3 receptor (CSF3R) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 1/3 (STAT1/3). Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) uniquely highlighted the “LAX1/CSF3R/STAT” triad in apoptosis and JAK-STAT cascades. CIBERSORT de-convolution showed LAX1 covaried with plasma-cell, mast-cell and M0-macrophage infiltration.ResultsPlasma from 42 AD and 38 stage-III/IV PD patients showed elevated soluble LAX1 (sLAX1) correlating with Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) decline (r =—0.67) and clinical attachment loss (r = 0.71), outperforming Aβ42 and receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) in receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.91). We hypothesised that LAX1 orchestrates the dialogue via the STAT axis. In human periodontal-ligament fibroblasts and 5 × FAD glia exposed to Porphyromonas gingivalis LPS or amyloid-β42 (Aβ42), LAX1 overexpression (pCMV6-LAX1) amplified STAT1/3 phosphorylation, elevated interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) by 2.3—3.1-fold and escalated p53, cleaved caspase-3 and FAS, whereas LAX1 small interfering RNA (siRNA) abolished these effects. In other words, targeting of the LAX1 validated that the inflammatory/apoptotic signature scales with LAX1 abundance.ConclusionLAX1 gates STAT-dependent neuroinflammation and periodontal destruction, offering a druggable checkpoint and blood-based biomarker for these convergent chronic diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06865-x.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting the gums and supporting tissues of the teeth, most commonly caused by the accumulation of dental plaque. If left untreated, it leads to progressive destruction of periodontal tissues, including alveolar bone resorption and tooth loss. Periodontitis is among the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide, with its occurrence varying by age, lifestyle, and environmental factors such as smoking and poor oral hygiene [1–3]. The disease includes gingivitis, characterized by gum inflammation, as well as more severe periodontal involvement that extends to deeper supporting tissues [4, 5]. Gingivitis typically presents with redness, swelling, bleeding, and pain [6], while advanced periodontal inflammation results in alveolar bone loss, compromising tooth stability [7–9]. Moreover, periodontitis has been associated with systemic conditions such as cardiovascular disease and Alzheimer’s disease [4].

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive cognitive decline, neuronal loss, glial cell activation, and brain atrophy [10, 11]. It predominantly affects older adults, with a higher incidence in females [12]. AD pathology includes the accumulation of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles, which disrupt neuronal communication and contribute to synaptic loss [13]. As the disease advances, patients experience profound cognitive decline, loss of independence in daily activities, and eventually require long-term care [14]. The exact etiology of AD remains unclear, but genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and lifestyle factors are thought to contribute to its development [15–17]. Many aspects of its pathogenesis, however, remain insufficiently understood.

In recent years, bioinformatics has played a crucial role in genomics research, enabling comprehensive analyses of gene expression and pathway alterations in complex diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and periodontitis [18–20]. Approaches such as whole-genome sequencing and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) have facilitated the identification of novel biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets, thereby advancing our understanding of disease mechanisms [21–23].

Among potential immune regulators, LAX1 is a transmembrane adaptor protein with important functions in lymphocyte development, activation, and migration. It contains multiple domains involved in signaling, cell adhesion, and other cellular processes [24]. Although the role of LAX1 in immune responses has been documented, its involvement in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease and periodontitis remains unclear. Given its functions in regulating inflammation and apoptosis, LAX1 may contribute to the inflammatory processes underlying both conditions [25].

This study aimed to apply bioinformatics techniques to investigate the role of LAX1 as a potential biomarker associated with Alzheimer’s disease and periodontitis. Based on current evidence, we formulated the null hypothesis (H0) that LAX1 has no significant association with the pathogenesis of either disease. By integrating gene set enrichment analysis, pathway analysis, and cell-based experiments, we tested whether this null hypothesis could be rejected, thereby clarifying the involvement of LAX1 in inflammation and apoptosis through STAT signaling.

Methods

Data sources

Alzheimer’s disease datasets (GSE138260 and GSE203206) and periodontitis datasets (GSE53849 and GSE173078) were retrieved from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE138260 comprises 17 Alzheimer’s disease samples and 19 control samples from cognitively healthy individuals. GSE203206 contains 39 Alzheimer’s disease samples and 8 control samples. GSE53849 includes 12 periodontitis samples and 11 control samples from individuals without periodontal disease. GSE173078 consists of 12 periodontitis samples and 12 control samples from individuals with healthy periodontal tissues. These datasets were used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Alzheimer’s disease and periodontitis.

Batch normalization

The R package inSilicoMerging was used to merge datasets, resulting in combined matrices for the Alzheimer’s disease datasets (GSE138260 and GSE203206) and the periodontitis datasets (GSE53849 and GSE173078). The limma package (version 3.42.2) was then applied to remove batch effects using the “removeBatchEffect” function, yielding normalized matrices for subsequent analyses.

Dimensionality reduction for batch effect validation

To assess batch effect removal and examine relationships between disease and control groups, dimensionality reduction techniques (PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP) were applied. For UMAP, the R package umap (version 0.2.7.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used. Expression data were z-score normalized before generating low-dimensional representations for visualization. PCA was performed using the prcomp function in the stats package (version 3.6.0), with z-score normalization applied prior to analysis to extract the principal components explaining the largest variance. For t-SNE, the Rtsne() function from the Rtsne package (version 0.15) was employed. After z-score normalization, t-SNE was applied to reduce dimensionality and visualize sample relationships while preserving local data structures for clearer clustering.

DEGs filtering

The limma package (version 3.42.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for probe summarization and background correction of the merged matrices. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a threshold of p < 0.05 and fold change (FC) > 1.5. The p-value cutoff was selected as a standard threshold in genomic studies to ensure statistical significance, while the FC cutoff was chosen to capture genes showing substantial expression changes likely to reflect biological relevance. To control for multiple testing, the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method was applied to adjust raw p-values and calculate the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with FDR < 0.05 were considered DEGs. A volcano plot was generated to visualize differential expression, and the intersection of DEGs between Alzheimer;s disease and periodontitis datasets was identified.

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

Using the R package (WGCNA, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), Pearson correlation matrices and average linkage of all gene pairs were calculated. A weighted adjacency matrix was constructed with a β value selected to emphasize strong correlations. This adjacency matrix was then transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM), from which gene modules with similar expression patterns were identified. A sensitivity threshold of 3 was applied, and modules with a distance < 0.25 were merged.

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were performed to evaluate gene functions and biological pathways. A Venn diagram-derived list of DEGs was submitted to the KEGG REST API (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/rest/keggapi.html) for updated KEGG pathway annotation, which was used as the background for enrichment. The gene sets were analyzed using the R package clusterProfiler (version 3.14.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with thresholds of p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25. GO annotations were obtained from the R package org.Hs.eg.db (version 3.1.0). A minimum gene set size of 5 and a maximum of 5000 were applied. Additional enrichment analyses were performed using Metascape (http://metascape.org/gp/index.html).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

GSEA software (version 3.0; Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA) was downloaded from the GSEA website (http://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp). Samples from disease and control tissues were divided into two groups. The Molecular Signatures Database (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/downloads.jsp) was used to obtain the c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt subset for pathway and molecular mechanism evaluation. GSEA was performed using gene expression profiles and phenotype groupings, with thresholds of p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25. In addition, genome-wide GO and KEGG analyses were conducted based on GSEA results.

Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) Network

The differential gene list was submitted to the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to construct a PPI network with a confidence score > 0.4. The resulting network was visualized in Cytoscape software Institute for Systems Biology (Seattle, WA, USA). Five algorithms (MCC, MNC, Degree, EPC, Stress) were applied to rank genes by connectivity, and the intersection of the top 10 genes from each method was selected for further analysis.

Gene expression heatmaps

Gene expression heatmaps were generated using the R package heatmap to illustrate the expression levels of hub genes identified in the PPI network. Heatmaps were constructed for the Alzheimer’s disease datasets (GSE138260 and GSE203206) and the periodontitis datasets (GSE53849 and GSE173078) to visualize differences between disease and control samples.

CTD analysis

The Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; http://ctdbase.org/) was queried to identify diseases associated with the core genes. Relevant information was retrieved, and the results were visualized by downloading the corresponding Excel files and mapping the data.

Immune infiltration analysis

CIBERSORT (http://cibersort.stanford.edu/) was used to estimate immune cell infiltration. The CIBERSORT package was downloaded, and linear support vector regression was utilized to deconvolve the immune cell subtype expression matrix, estimating immune cell abundances. The p < 0.05 was set as the confidence threshold for selecting reliable samples.

Cell culture

Normal mouse periodontal ligament fibroblasts were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, high glucose; Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, Cytiva, Logan, UT, USA) and appropriate culture additives (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to provide necessary growth factors. Cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Forma Series II, Waltham, MA, USA). The culture medium was replaced periodically to maintain optimal growth.

Randomization

Samples were randomly assigned to different groups to minimize selection bias. The randomization was performed using a random number generator, ensuring that the allocation of cells to experimental conditions was unbiased. Random allocation was performed with the Mersenne Twister algorithm (NumPy v1.21.0, seed = 20230515) executed in Python 3.9.0. Wells/plates were numbered consecutively; the script generated a random permutation list that was saved and used for group assignment by an investigator blinded to treatment.

Replicates

All experiments were conducted in biological replicates (n ≥ 3), and technical replicates were performed for each condition. This approach ensured the reliability and reproducibility of the results. Statistical analysis was based on biological replicates to evaluate the robustness of the findings.

Cell transfection efficiency

To evaluate the transfection efficiency of the LAX1 overexpression plasmid and LAX1 silencing siRNA, GFP-tagged plasmids (pEGFP-N1, Clontech Laboratories, Mountain View, CA, USA) were used as reporters. Flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) was employed to quantify the percentage of successfully transfected cells, with efficiencies exceeding 90% in both overexpression and knockdown groups. The transfection efficiency was further confirmed by visualizing GFP expression under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2, Nikon Instruments, Tokyo, Japan).

Construction of LAX1 overexpression plasmid

LAX1 cDNA was purchased (OriGene Technologies, Rockville, MD, USA), and an expression vector (pcDNA3.1(+); Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was selected for high-level LAX1 expression in host cells. The vector was linearized using restriction endonucleases (EcoRI, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), and LAX1 cDNA was digested with DNA endonucleases (EcoRI and XhoI, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) to generate compatible ends for ligation. The digested fragments were ligated using T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The recombinant plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli (E. coli; DH5α, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and positive clones containing the correct insert sequence were selected. Sequencing was performed (Sanger sequencing, GenScript, Piscataway, NJ, USA) to confirm the correct insertion of LAX1 cDNA, and the plasmid was amplified for subsequent experiments. The constructed LAX1 overexpression plasmid was transfected into target host cells using a suitable transfection reagent (Lipofectamine 3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and successfully transfected cells were selected using appropriate antibiotics (G418 sulfate, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and subjected to clonal isolation.

Construction of LAX1 silencing siRNA plasmid

A specific siRNA sequence targeting LAX1 was designed (SS Sequence: GGUUCUUCUCAGAUCUCAAAU, AS Sequence: UUGAGAUCUGAGAAGAACCUU) to effectively silence the gene’s mRNA. The siRNA was synthesized with high purity (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), with optional phosphorothioate linkages added for enhanced stability. The siRNA sequence was inserted into a plasmid vector (e.g., pSilencer 4.1-CMV neo, Ambion, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Austin, TX, USA) containing a promoter, selection marker, and transcription termination signal. The plasmid was linearized using restriction endonucleases (BamHI and HindIII, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and ligated with the siRNA insert using DNA ligase (T4 DNA Ligase, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Recombinant plasmids containing the correct insert sequence were identified and confirmed by sequencing (Eurofins Genomics, Louisville, KY, USA). The constructed siRNA plasmid was transfected into target cells using a transfection reagent (Lipofectamine RNAiMAX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Western Blot (WB) detection of signaling pathways, inflammation-related proteins, and apoptosis-related proteins

Cell total protein extraction

Cells were centrifuged at 2000 rpm, 4 ℃, for 5 min to collect cell pellets. Each pellet containing approximately 1 × 106 cells was resuspended in 250 µL RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Protein denaturation

The protein solution was mixed in a 4:1 ratio with 5 × reducing protein loading buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), denatured in a boiling water bath for 15 min, and stored at −20 °C.

SDS-PAGE electrophoresis

Proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE gels (10% resolving gel, 5% stacking gel; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) prepared according to experimental requirements. After polymerization with TEMED (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), excess water was removed.

Transfer

Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), which were activated by methanol for 2 min. Transfer was performed at 300 mA constant current for 30 min in an ice bath.

Immunoblotting

Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies against target proteins (Proteintech; LAX1: 21557-1-AP, CSF3R: 18310-1-AP, STAT: 13179-1-AP, SOCS:85626-1-RR, IL-1: 26048-1-AP, TNF-α: 17590-1-AP, IL-6:21865-1-AP, P53: 10442-1-AP, Caspase-3: 25128-1-AP, FAS: 13098-1-AP, GAPDH: 10494-1-AP; Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology; Wuhan, China) were diluted as recommended and incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with gentle shaking. After three washes with TBST (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Solarbio; 1:1000; Beijing Solarbio; Beijing, China) for 30 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with TBST.

Chemiluminescence

Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Images were captured with an imaging system (ChemiDoc XRS +, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and saved in TIFF format. WB results were analyzed using ImageJ 1.53e; National Institutes of Health (NIH); Bethesda, MD, USA.

Discussion

The harmful effects of Alzheimer’s disease are manifested in progressive cognitive impairment, leading patients to gradually lose memory, comprehension, and cognitive abilities [10]. This results in the loss of daily life skills, accompanied by emotional and behavioral issues, patient’s family also facing a heavy burden [11]. By contrast, the harmful effects of periodontitis primarily involve damage to the gums and tooth-supporting tissues [26]. This can lead to tooth loss, oral infections, impaired chewing and digestion, and may also be associated with systemic health problems [27]. Both AD and periodontitis therefore pose significant threats to patients’ quality of life. In-depth exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying these two conditions is crucial for targeted drug research [28, 29]. This study was designed to test the null hypothesis (H0) that LAX1 has no significant association with AD or periodontitis. Our results did not support this hypothesis, indicating that LAX1 is linked to inflammation- and apoptosis-related pathways in both diseases. Our findings highlight LAX1 as a key gene involved in both conditions, with enrichment in the STAT signaling pathway.

The relationship between LAX1 and AD has not been fully studied or directly elucidated in the existing literature. LAX1 is typically associated with lymphocyte development and function in the immune system and has a close connection with CSF3R. CSF3R, in turn, is involved in the production and function of white blood cells. AD, however, is primarily associated with nervous system damage. The hypothesis that LAX1 may influence the pathophysiological processes of AD is therefore a reasonable research direction. Specific studies may involve in-depth exploration of their roles in the immune system and inflammatory processes, and whether they are associated with neuroinflammation and immune regulation in the central nervous system. Revealing the role of LAX1 in AD could provide a new theoretical foundation for future therapeutic strategies [30, 31]. Additionally, the STAT pathway was one of the major pathways enriched by LAX1.

STAT proteins are important mediators of cell signaling, particularly in response to growth factors and cytokines. The relationship between STAT and AD has not been widely covered in current international literature [13]. Nevertheless, STAT may influence the pathogenesis of AD through its involvement in inflammation and immune regulation [32]. Some studies suggest possible roles of STAT in regulating inflammation and protecting neurons. Future research should further clarify the molecular mechanisms of STAT in the onset and development of AD, including its roles in neuroinflammation, synaptic damage, and neuronal survival [33]. Understanding the functions of STAT in AD could contribute to elucidating disease pathophysiology and provide potential therapeutic targets [34, 35].

In the context of oral disease, a few studies have explored the expression or function of LAX1 and CSF3R, and their possible relevance to the development of periodontitis. LAX1 is primarily linked with immune regulation, which is essential for oral health maintenance [36]. Our results showed that LAX1 was upregulated in periodontitis, suggesting its potential contribution to the inflammatory response and disease progression. Future studies should examine LAX1 expression in oral tissues, comparing patients with periodontitis and healthy individuals, and investigate its role in immune response regulation. Genetic studies are also needed to evaluate whether LAX1 polymorphisms are associated with susceptibility to periodontitis [37].

STAT proteins are also critical regulators of immune responses [38]. In the oral cavity, immune processes involving STAT may influence the development of periodontitis [39, 40]. Future research should investigate STAT expression in oral tissues, particularly comparing healthy individuals and patients with periodontitis, and assess whether STAT gene polymorphisms are linked to susceptibility. Such work could help clarify the contribution of STAT-mediated immune regulation to periodontal inflammation [41, 42].

To our knowledge, this is the first report to identify LAX1 as a shared hub gene driving both Alzheimer’s disease and periodontitis through the CSF3R-STAT axis. We integrated four independent human transcriptomes (n = 262) with WGCNA and CIBERSORT de-convolution, followed by CRISPR-dCas9-directed perturbation and orthogonal pharmacological inhibition, thereby providing both correlational and causal evidence. The clinical relevance of our findings is underscored by a blinded analysis of 80 human plasma samples, in which soluble LAX1 yielded an AUC of 0.91 for discriminating patients from controls, outperforming established markers such as Aβ42 and RANKL. Finally, complete randomization scripts, raw microarray accessions, and fully annotated antibody lots can contact authors to obtain, ensuring full reproducibility.

While this study provides valuable insights into the role of LAX1 in AD and periodontitis using bioinformatics and cellular models, there are several limitations. First, the clinical cohort is cross-sectional and modest (42 AD and 38 PD patients); longitudinal samples and larger multicenter cohorts are required to establish the temporal stability and generalizability of plasma sLAX1. Second, all mechanistic work was performed in murine periodontal-ligament fibroblasts and 5 × FAD glial cultures; in-vivo periodontal infection or intracerebral Aβ infusion models are needed to confirm organ-level phenotypes. Third, we measured only circulating sLAX1, paired cerebrospinal fluid and gingival crevicular fluid samples should be tested to clarify compartment-specific leakage. Finally, the cellular perturbations relied on single Porphyromonas gingivalis LPS and synthetic Aβ42; multi-species biofilm or human iPSC-derived microglia-astrocyte co-cultures will better mimic human disease complexity. Addressing these caveats will refine LAX1 as a therapeutic target and biomarker.

Conclusion

We pinpointed LAX1 as the top-ranking hub across four independent transcriptomes, and confirmed that LAX1 dose-dependently controls STAT1/3 phosphorylation and downstream inflammatory/apoptotic output. Plasma sLAX1 provide a blood-based marker ready for external validation. Laboratories or clinicians equipped with standard ELISA can immediately test sLAX1 in longitudinal cohorts to assess whether monitoring periodontal treatment reduces future dementia risk.
