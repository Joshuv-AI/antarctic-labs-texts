The iceberg of frailty: underlying mechanisms beyond self-reported weight trajectories
Authors: Xuefeng Liu, Yuedong Liu
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Upon careful reading of the insightful study “Association between weight change patterns in adulthood and frailty risk among middle-aged and older adults” (10.1007/s40520-025-03082-9) published in your esteemed journal [1], we were profoundly impressed by its rigorous exploration of weight dynamics across the adult lifespan. The study’s innovative use of NHANES data to characterize weight trajectories—coupled with its comprehensive analysis of frailty risk—represents a significant contribution to geriatric epidemiology. The meticulous categorization of participants into five weight change patterns, from stable normal to persistent obesity, offers a valuable framework for understanding how lifelong weight management influences late-life health. We write to share several considerations that may further enrich the interpretability of these findings, offered in the spirit of constructive dialogue.

Reflections on weight trajectory assessment

The study’s reliance on self-reported weight at age 25 and 10 years prior to recruitment—while pragmatic for large-scale surveys—may present opportunities for methodological refinement. Self-reported data, particularly for distant time points, can be subject to recall variation, as evidenced by studies noting discrepancies between self-reported and measured weights, especially among individuals with a history of obesity [2, 3]. For instance, the 1.69% of participants categorized as “obese to non-obese” reported an average weight loss of 18.46 kg, a substantial change that might benefit from verification through medical records or longitudinal measurements. Future investigations might consider triangulating self-reported data with objective measures (e.g., early adulthood medical charts) to enhance accuracy, particularly when defining critical transitions like obesity remission.

Additionally, defining weight patterns solely by BMI could potentially obscure nuanced relationships between body composition and frailty. While BMI effectively classifies overall adiposity, it cannot distinguish between lean mass and fat accumulation—key determinants of sarcopenia and frailty. For example, the “non-obese to obese” group (OR = 2.57) and “obese to non-obese” group (OR = 2.50) exhibited similar frailty risks, which might be better explained by underlying changes in muscle-fat ratio rather than BMI alone. Incorporating measures like appendicular lean mass index or visceral fat area via imaging could offer deeper mechanistic insights into these associations.

Considerations for frailty measurement and classification

The study’s use of the deficit accumulation frailty index (FI) provides a robust framework for assessing vulnerability, yet the optimal FI cutoff for middle-aged populations (FI ≥ 0.21) may warrant further validation. While sensitivity analyses using FI ≥ 0.25 yielded consistent results, emerging research suggests that frailty phenotypes in midlife may differ from those in older adults, potentially requiring age-specific threshold adjustments [4]. Notably, the stable obese group—despite having a younger mean age (60.87 years)—exhibited the highest frailty prevalence (39.92%), hinting at possible age-related differences in deficit accumulation. Longitudinal studies tracking FI progression across decades could help establish more precise cutoff values for different life stages.

The cross-sectional design, while informative for prevalence estimates, limits our ability to disentangle the temporal relationship between weight trajectories and frailty onset. Future longitudinal analyses—perhaps leveraging the Health and Retirement Study’s multi-wave data—could explore whether weight changes precede frailty progression or vice versa, addressing questions of causality that are challenging to answer with cross-sectional data .

Enhancing covariate adjustment and mechanistic insight

The study’s covariate panel, including demographic and lifestyle factors, is comprehensive, yet incorporating additional biomarkers could deepen mechanistic understanding. For example, while the discussion highlights chronic inflammation as a potential mediator between obesity and frailty, the analysis does not adjust for inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein or interleukin-6. Including such biomarkers would help disentangle whether weight-related frailty risk is driven by adiposity-induced inflammation, muscle wasting, or other pathways. Similarly, markers of metabolic health (e.g., fasting glucose, lipid profiles) could clarify the role of insulin resistance in the observed associations.

Subgroup analyses exploring effect modification by sex, race, or socioeconomic status—while briefly mentioned in sensitivity analyses—might benefit from more detailed exploration. For instance, prior research has shown racial disparities in BMI-frailty relationships, with non-Hispanic Black adults often exhibiting lower frailty rates at higher BMIs [5]. Elucidating such heterogeneity could inform targeted prevention strategies for diverse populations.

Toward integrative future research

To build upon these findings, future investigations might consider integrating genetic, epigenetic, and body composition data to create a more holistic model of weight-frailty associations. For example, variants in genes like PNPLA3—implicated in lipid metabolism and liver health—have been linked to both obesity and frailty, and their inclusion could help identify susceptible subgroups. Combining such genetic markers with longitudinal weight data and frailty assessments would bridge epidemiological observations with biological mechanisms, enhancing the translational value of this research.

We commend the authors for their important contribution to understanding how adult weight trajectories influence frailty risk. These reflections are offered in the hope of fostering further inquiry into this complex relationship, which holds significant promise for advancing geriatric preventive care.
