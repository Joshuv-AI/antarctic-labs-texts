Shadowing property for set-valued map and its inverse limit
Zhengyu Yin

Abstract
. In this article, we investigate the relationship between the shadowing property of set-valued maps and their associated inverse limit systems. We show that if a set-valued map is expansive and open in the context of set-valued dynamics, then certain induced inverse limit systems have the shadowing property. Additionally, we prove that a continuous set-valued map has the shadowing property if and only if some of its induced inverse limit system also has shadowing property. Finally, we establish that the shadowing property of a set-valued map is equivalent to the shadowing property of its induced inverse set-valued system.

1.
Introduction

The shadowing property, also known as pseudo-orbit tracing property, is a key concept in dynamical systems that describes the stability of orbits under small perturbations
[
25
]
. This property plays a crucial role in both stability and ergodic theory
[
3
,
4
,
25
,
26
]
, and has been extensively studied in a variety of contexts (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
9
,
21
,
22
]
). Recently, in
[
10
]
, Good and Meddaugh uncovered a fundamental connection between the shadowing property and inverse limits of inverse systems satisfying the Mittag-Leffler condition, particularly those consisting of shifts of finite type.

For single-valued maps, Sakai
[
24
]
explored various shadowing properties for positively expansive maps, proving that an expansive map possesses the shadowing property if and only if it is open. In the case of homeomorphisms, Lee and Sakai
[
17
]
demonstrated that different shadowing properties are equivalent for expansive systems. Considering the inverse limit, Chen and Li
[
5
]
showed that a continuous map has the shadowing property if and only if its inverse limit also possesses shadowing property.

For set-valued maps, the inverse limit concept is similarly important, particularly in the context of topological and dynamical properties such as continuum theory
[
18
,
12
,
15
]
and chaos
[
13
]
. Some related topics also appear in these two excellent and rich books
[
1
,
11
]
written by Aubin and Frankowska, Shouchuan Hu and Papageorgiou, respectively. For dynamical properties, recently, Kelley and Tennant
[
16
]
introduced the notion of topological entropy for set-valued maps, analogous to the classical topological entropy for single-valued maps. Raines and Tennant
[
23
]
extended the specification property from single-valued maps to set-valued maps, proving that if a set-valued map has the specification property, it is topologically mixing and has positive entropy.
Cordeiro and Pacifico
[
7
]
defined the concept of continuum-wise expansiveness for set-valued maps and proved if a set-valued map on positive dimension space has
cw
-expansive then the set-valued map has positive entropy.
In
[
19
]
, Metzger, Morales, and Thieullen. established that a set-valued map satisfying positive expansiveness (as defined by Williams
[
27
]
) and the pseudo-orbit tracing property (shadowing property, as defined by Pilyugin and Rieger
[
22
]
) ensures that its inverse limit is topologically stable in the sense of set-valued maps.

In this paper, we focus on the shadowing property in set-valued systems and its relationship with inverse limits. Our goal is to investigate the conditions under which a set-valued map with the shadowing property implies that its induced inverse limit also possesses the shadowing property in the sense of single-valued systems.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we review key definitions and fundamental properties. Section 3 investigates the relationship between openness of set-valued maps and their induced inverse limit actions. In Section 4, we explore the shadowing property for set-valued maps and their corresponding inverse limit systems.

2.
Set-valued maps

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. For each
x\in X
and
A\subset X
, we denote
\overline{A}
as the closure of
A
, and define the distance from a point
x
to a set
A
as

d(x,A)=\inf\{d(x,a):a\in A\}.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we define the open ball centered at
x
with radius
\varepsilon
as

B_{d}(x,\varepsilon)=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<\varepsilon\},

and for a set
A\subset X
, we define the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
A
as

B_{d}(A,\varepsilon)=\{y\in X:d(y,A)<\varepsilon\}.

We denote by
2^{X}
the collections of all nonempty compact subsets of
X
. Given a compatible metric
d
on
X
, the
Hausdorff metric

d_{H}
on
2^{X}
is defined by

d_{H}(A,B)=\max\{\sup_{a\in A}d(a,B),\sup_{b\in B}d(A,b)\}.

It is known that the topology induced by the Hausdorff metric on
2^{X}
is independent of the choice of the compatible metric
d
on the space
X
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a metric space with metric
d
. A set-valued map
F:X\to 2^{X}
is defined as follows:

(a)

F
is
upper semicontinuous
if for every
x\in X
and every open set
U\supset F(x)
, there exists an open neighborhood
V
of
x
such that
F(y)\subset U
for all
y\in V
;

(b)

F
is
lower semicontinuous
if for any
x\in X
and an open set
U
intersecting
F(x)
(i.e.,
F(x)\cap U\neq\emptyset
), there exists an open neighborhood
V
of
x
such that
F(y)\cap U\neq\emptyset
for all
y\in V
;

(c)

Moreover,
F
is
continuous
if it is both upper and lower semicontinuous.

If
F:X\to 2^{X}
is a set-valued map on
X
, we call the pair
(X,F)
a
set-valued system
.
In this paper, we use the notation
f
to denote a single-valued map and
F
to denote a set-valued map. Unless otherwise stated, we assume that
X
is a compact metric space.

For a subset
A\subset X
, we denote

F(A):=\{x\in X:x\in F(a)\text{ for some }a\in A\}=\bigcup_{a\in A}F(a),

and the inverse image of
F
by

F^{-1}(A):=\{x\in X:F(x)\cap A\neq\emptyset\}.

Next, we recall some basic properties for set-valued maps, some of which may have appeared in previous works (see e.g.,
[
1
,
11
,
20
]
).

Proposition 2.2
.

(
[
1
,
11
]
)
A set-valued map
F
on
X
is upper semicontinuous if and only if for any sequence
(x_{n},y_{n})
with
y_{n}\in F(x_{n})
that converges to a point
(x,y)\in X\times X
, it follows that
y\in F(x)
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
(X,F)
be a set-valued system and
d
a compatible metric on
X
. Then
F
satisfies the following:

(a)

F
is upper semicontinuous if and only if
F^{-1}(A)
is closed for each
A\in 2^{X}
;

(b)

F
is lower semicontinuous if and only if
F^{-1}(B)
is open for each open subset
B
of
X
;

(c)

is continuous if and only if

converges to

under Hausdorff metric whenever
x_{i}
converges to
x
in
X
.

Let
f
be a single-valued map on
X
. Recall that
f
is
open
(resp.
closed
) if
f
maps open (resp. closed) subset to open (resp. closed) subset As an application of the continuity of a set-valued map, we have the following known lemma. For completeness, we include proof here.

Lemma 2.4
.

(Continuity of fiber map)
Let
f:X\to Y
be a continuous map between compact metric spaces. Then
f
is open if and only if the fiber set-valued map
F:X\to 2^{X}
defined by

F(x)=f^{-1}(f(x))

is continuous.

Proof.

Suppose that
f
is open. Let
U\subset X
be an open subset. Then

x\in F^{-1}(U)\Longleftrightarrow f^{-1}(f(x))\cap U\neq\emptyset\Longleftrightarrow f(x)\in f(U)\Longleftrightarrow x\in f^{-1}(f(U)).

Thus,
F^{-1}(U)=f^{-1}(f(U))
is open, proving that
F
is lower semicontinuous.

Let
A\in 2^{X}
. By a similar argument,
F^{-1}(A)=f^{-1}(f(A))
is closed. Therefore,
F
is upper semicontinuous. Hence, by Proposition
2.3

F
is continuous.

Conversely, suppose
F
is continuous. An equivalent description of a continuous map being open is that for any closed subset
A\subset X
, the union of all fibers contained in
A
is itself closed (see
[
14
, Theorem 3.10]
). Notice that the set
Q
consisting of points such that
f^{-1}(f(x))\subset A
is equal to
(F^{-1}(A^{c}))^{c}
and as
F
is lower semicontinuous,
Q

is closed. Thus,
f
is open.
∎

3.
Inverse limit and openness

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space and
\mathbb{A}=-\mathbb{N}\text{ or }\mathbb{N}
. Let
X^{\mathbb{A}}
be the space endowed with the product topology. We consider the compatible metric
\rho
on

which is defined by

\rho((x_{n}),(y_{n}))=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{A}}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{d(x_{n},y_{n})}{2^{|n|+1}},\text{ for all }(x_{n}),(y_{n})\in X^{\mathbb{A}},

and let
\rho_{H}
be the corresponding Hausdorff metric on

induced by

As
X
is compact, for convenience, we all assume that the diameter of

is
1
under its compatible metric

.

In

,
23
]

\displaystyle{\rm{Orb}}_{l}(X)=\{(\cdots,x_{-1},x_{0})\in X^{-\mathbb{N}}:x_{i}\in F(x_{i+1})\},

\displaystyle{\rm{Orb}}_{r}(X)=\{(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots)\in X^{\mathbb{N}}:x_{i+1}\in F(x_{i})\},

\displaystyle{\rm{Orb}}_{inv}(X)=\{(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots)\in X^{\mathbb{N}}:x_{i}\in F(x_{i+1})\}.

Due to the nice property of upper semicontinuity (see Proposition
2.2
), if
F
is an upper semicontinuous set-valued map, then these orbit spaces are all closed subsets of
X^{\mathbb{A}}
.
Furthermore, if
F
is a single-valued continuous map, then
{\rm{Orb}}_{inv}(X)
coincides with the inverse limit in the classical sense (see
[
5
]
).

For convenience, we denote points in
{\rm{Orb}}_{l}(X)
,
{\rm{Orb}}_{r}(X)
and
{\rm{Orb}}_{inv}(X)
by

and

, respectively. Denote

by the

to

, and by

With the above notations above, we can define the following single-valued and set-valued maps, respectively.

\displaystyle{\sigma}_{lF}:{\rm{Orb}}_{l}(X)\to{\rm{Orb}}_{l}(X)\text{ by }(x_{-n})\mapsto(x_{-n-1}),

\displaystyle{\sigma}_{rF}:{\rm{Orb}}_{r}(X)\to{\rm{Orb}}_{r}(X)\text{ by }(x_{n})\mapsto(x_{n+1}),

\displaystyle{F}_{inv}:{\rm{Orb}}_{inv}(X)\to 2^{{\rm{Orb}}_{inv}(X)}\text{ by }[x_{n}]\mapsto F(x_{0})\times[x_{n}].

It is easy to check that

is upper semicontinuous (continuous) if and only if

is upper semicontinuous (continuous).

Similar to single-valued case, we say that a set-valued map

is

if

for all

and

is open for any open subset

Lemma 3.1
.

(

,

)
If

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,F)
be a continuous set-valued system with metric
d
on
X
. For each
\delta>0
, there is
\delta_{1}>0
such that if

, then
\rho_{H}(\pi_{0}^{-1}(x),\pi_{0}^{-1}(y))<\delta
, where
\rho_{H}
is the Hausdorff metric on

.

Proof.

Choose
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}{1}/{2^{n}}<\delta/2.

Since
F
is continuous, by Proposition
2.3
there exists a sequence
\{\delta_{1},\cdots,\delta_{N-1}\}
, where
\delta_{i}<\delta/4
satisfying:

(N-1)

if
d(x,y)<\delta_{1}
, then
d_{H}(F(x),F(y))<\delta_{2},

(N-2)

if
d(x_{i-1},y_{i-1})<\delta_{i}

d_{H}(F(x_{i-1}),F(y_{i-1}))<\delta_{i+1}
for all

,

(N-3)

if
d(x_{N-2},y_{N-2})<\delta_{N-1}
, then
d_{H}(F(x_{N-2}),F(y_{N-2}))<\delta/4
.

Let
(y_{n})\in\pi_{0}^{-1}(y)
. Since
d(x,y)<\delta_{1}
we can find
x_{1}\in F(x)
such that

By induction, we construct a sequence

such that

(1)

x_{i+1}\in F(x_{i})
and

(y_{n})\in\pi_{0}^{-1}(y)

∎

4.
Inverse limit and Shadowing property

First, we review some fundamental shadowing property concepts related to single-valued maps and set-valued maps.

Let
(X,F)
be a set-valued system and
d
a metric on
X
. Given
\delta>0
, a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
in
X
is called a
\delta
-pseudo-orbit
if

d(x_{n+1},F(x_{n}))<\delta\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

and
\{x_{n}\}
is called an
F
-orbit
if

(x_{n})\in{\rm{Orb}}_{r}(X).
In particular, if
F
is a single-valued mapping, these notions apply equally well.

Definition 4.1
.

(
[
25
]
)
Let
(X,f)
be a single-valued system. We say that
f
has
shadowing property
(or
POTP
), if for each
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that for every

\delta
-pseudo-orbit
\{x_{n}\}
, there is a point

such that
d(f^{n}(x),x_{n})<\varepsilon\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.
In this case, we say that
\{x_{n}\}
is
\varepsilon
-shadowed by
x
.

Definition 4.2
.

(cf.
[
22
]
)
Let
(X,F)
be a set-valued system. The map
F
is said to have
shadowing property
, if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that for each
\delta
-pseudo-orbit
\{x_{n}\}
of
F
, there exists an
F
-orbit
(y_{n})
satisfying

d(x_{n},y_{n})<\varepsilon\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.
In this case, we say that
\{x_{n}\}
is
\varepsilon
-shadowed by
(y_{n})
.

Definition 4.3
.

(
[
19
]
)
A set-valued system
(X,F)
is said to have

if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that for each finite sequence
\{x_{0},\cdots,x_{n}\}
satisfying

(

The following statement shows that multivalued maps with shadowing property can be generated by carrying out symmetry operations on single-valued maps with shadowing property.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

and

a continuous single-valued map on

, and

is the Euclidean metric on

. If

has shadowing property, then the set-valued map

defined by

\{f(x),-f(x)\},&x\in[a,b],\end{cases}

also has shadowing property.

Proof.

Suppose
f
has shadowing property. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
witness
\varepsilon
-shadowing. Let
\{x_{n}\}
be a
\delta
-pseudo-orbit of

.
If all
x_{n}
are non-negative or all
x_{n}
are non-positive, then, since

has the shadowing property (and so does

for

), there exists a point
y\in[a,b]
or

such that either:

\{y,f(y),\ldots,f^{n}(y),\ldots\}\quad\text{or}\quad\{y,-f(-y),\ldots,-f^{n}(-y),\ldots\}

\varepsilon
-shadows
\{x_{n}\}
.

If
\{x_{n}\}
contains both positive and negative elements, we proceed as follows. Assume

then we set

. Since

, we still have

Let
x_{i}
(for

) be the first negative element in the sequence. Then we set

, by a straightforward calculation, we have

By replacing negative terms in the sequence with their positive counterparts iteratively (i.e.,
x_{i}^{\prime}=x_{i}
if

and
x_{i}^{\prime}=-x_{i}
otherwise), we construct a non-negative sequence
\{x_{n}^{\prime}\}
. Then this sequence is a
\delta
-pseudo-orbit of

.
As

has shadowing property, then there exists a point

Finally, we define

and

, otherwise.
From the construction of

, it is straightforward to verify that
(y_{n}^{\prime})
is an

\varepsilon
-shadows

.
Therefore,

for each
j=1,\cdots,N

Thus, for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
there is

Thus,
\delta_{1}
is the number witnessing the condition of (b). Also, since
F
is onto, we extend the sequence
\{x_{n}\}
by adding a finite orbit points
(x_{-N},\cdots,x_{-1})
to the front of
\{x_{0},\cdots,x_{N},\cdots\}
, we obtain
\{x_{-N},\cdots,x_{-1},x_{0},\cdots,x_{N},\cdots\}
. Therefore,
\{x_{0},\cdots,x_{N},\cdots\}
can be
\varepsilon
-shadowed by an
F
-orbit.

(b)\Longrightarrow(c)
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
witness the condition of (b) with respect to
\varepsilon/4
. Given a
\delta
-pseudo-orbit
\{x_{n}\}
, then
\{x_{n+N}\}
can be
\varepsilon/4
-shadowed by some
F
-orbit
(z_{n})
and

d(x_{i}^{N},z_{n})<d(x_{i}^{N},x_{i+N})+d(x_{i+N},z_{i})<\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}.

Thus,
(c)
holds.

(c)\Longrightarrow(b)
. It follows a similar discussion as in
(b)\Longrightarrow(c)
.
∎
