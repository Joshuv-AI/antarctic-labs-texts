Tyrosinase Inhibitory Properties of Compounds Isolated from Artocarpus integer Roots
Authors: Ittipon Siridechakorn, Dina Nur Shinta, Ardiansah Ardiansah, Paratchata Batsomboon, Nattaya Ngamrojanavanich, Siwattra Choodej, Khanitha Pudhom
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA comprehensive phytochemical investigation of Artocarpus integer root extract led to the isolation of two new geranylated xanthones (1 and 2), one new geranylated flavone (3), one new flavanone (4), and one unique benzopyran (5), along with 16 known compounds. Structures of the new compounds were elucidated by a combination of spectroscopic and computational methods. Two different types of compounds, flavone 12 and arylbenzofuran 19, displayed the most potent antityrosinase activity with IC50 values of 1.7 ± 0.2 and 1.2 ± 0.1 μM, respectively. In addition, kinetic measurements and molecular docking simulations of compounds 12 and 19 were performed and revealed that compound 12 is a competitive inhibitor binding with the tyrosinase active site, while compound 19 is a noncompetitive tyrosinase inhibitor binding the enzyme at the allosteric site.

Melanin, a pigment crucial for shielding the skin from ultraviolet (UV) damage and modulating skin pigmentation, serves as a cornerstone in preserving skin integrity.1 Melanogenesis, a sophisticated biosynthetic process, involves the synthesis of melanin by melanocytes situated in the basal layer of the epidermis of human skin. This intricate pathway relies on three key enzymes: tyrosinase (TYR), tyrosinase-related protein-1 (TRP-1), and TRP-2, which play crucial roles in mediating the melanogenesis process. Notably, TYR is indispensable for the execution of melanogenesis.2,3 Tyrosinase, also known as polyphenol oxidase, is a copper-dependent enzyme that is pivotal in the regulation of melanin biosynthesis. This enzyme facilitates the hydroxylation of monophenol l-tyrosine to o-diphenol 3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA) and the subsequent oxidation of DOPA to DOPAquinone. The resultant quinone acts as a substrate for the synthesis of both eumelanin and pheomelanin.4,5 The active site of tyrosinase contains two Cu ions (CuA and CuB) that are coordinated by three conserved histidine residues, which play critical roles in the catalytic activity of the enzyme. The binding of substrates to these copper ions facilitates electron transfer, enabling the enzymatic oxidation processes that are crucial for melanin production. Understanding the structure and function of the tyrosinase active site is essential for designing effective inhibitors that can regulate melanin biosynthesis. Studies have shown that the copper ions at the active site are key targets for inhibitor binding, and several natural compounds have been investigated for their potential to interact with these metal centers, thereby suppressing tyrosinase activity.6,7

The genus Artocarpus (Moraceae), which consists of approximately 60 species and is mainly distributed throughout South and Southeast Asia, is a rich source of diverse flavonoids, particularly prenylated flavonoids, chromones, and aurones.8 Many of such compounds have been demonstrated to possess significant bioactivities, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antimalarial, antityrosinase, and anticancer activities.9−14Artocarpus integer Merr., commonly known as the “chempedak”, is popularly cultivated in Southern Thailand. Traditionally, its seeds are used for treating diarrhea, while its roots are used for malaria fever.15 The phytochemical study of this plant is rare and intensively focused on leaves and stem bark.16,17

In an effort to explore the potential of A. integer as a source of antityrosinase agents, as it was found that its root MeOH extract displayed significant activity (IC50 0.90 ± 0.12 μg/mL). We aimed to investigate the chemical constituents of this extract and applied them to the tyrosinase enzyme to evaluate their inhibitory ability and behavior. Furthermore, the most active compounds were analyzed for their mode of inhibition through kinetic studies, and their interactions with the enzyme were predicted using molecular docking simulations.

Results and Discussion

The MeOH extract of A. integer roots was subjected to various column chromatographs to provide 21 pure compounds. Five of them (1–5) were found to be new compounds. Known compounds were identified by comparing their spectroscopic data with data reported previously: artoxanthocarpuone B (6),18 artoindonesianin S (7),19 artoindonesianin A (8),20 artonin A (9),21 3-prenylluteolin (10),22 integrin (11),23 cyclochampedol (12),24 2,8-dihydroxy-3,10-dimethoxy-6-(2-methyl-1-propen-1-yl)-6H,7H-[1]benzopyrano[4,3-b][1]-benzopyran-7-one (13),25 cyclocommunol (14),26 artoindonesianin A-2 (15),27 artoindonesianin B-1 (16),28 moracin D (17),29 artocarstilene A (18),30 licocoumarone (19),31 artoindonesianin A-1 (20),32 and 4-((E)-3,3-dimethylpenta-1,4-dienyl)-phenol (21).33

Compound 1, obtained as a yellow viscous oil, had a molecular formula of C36H40O8 established from the HR-TOFMS data at m/z 601.2187 ([M + H]+, calcd 601.2801), implying 17 indices of hydrogen deficiency (IHDs). The IR spectrum exhibited absorption bands corresponding to hydroxy (3462 cm–1), carbonyl in cyclopentanone and ester (1745 cm–1), conjugated carbonyl (1655 cm–1), and benzene (1606 and 1472 cm–1) functional groups, and the UV spectrum was typical of a xanthone derivative. The 1H and 13C NMR (Table 1) and HMQC data of 1 revealed the presence of three carbonyl carbons (δC 196.9, 179.8, and 174.5), 16 fully substituted sp2 carbons, one sp2 methylene, three sp2 methines, one oxygenated sp3 tertiary carbon, one methoxy, five methylenes, six methyls, and a chelated hydroxy (δH 12.85) group. The remaining IHDs required four additional rings. Additionally, an aromatic proton at δ 8.27 (H-8) exhibited a carbonyl anisotropic effect, indicative of a xanthone skeleton.18 Analysis of 1H–1H COSY and HMBC spectra (Figure 1) supported the presence of an isopropenyl, a prenyl, and a geranyl group. Observed HMBC correlations of the C-23 methylene proton to the C-3, C-4, and C4a aromatic carbons confirmed the position of the geranyl group at C-4. Similarly, the positions of the isopropenyl at C-7 and the prenyl at C-2 were confirmed by HMBC correlations of Me-12/C-7 and H2-18/C-2, respectively. The existence of a cyclopentanone on a xanthone core was established by comparison with the spectra for compound 6, artoxanthocarpuone B, as well as was confirmed by HMBC cross-peaks from H2-15 to two aromatic carbons at δC 121.6 (C-5) and 156.5 (C-6), a slightly upfield ketone at δC 196.9 (C-14), and an O-bearing tertiary carbon at δC 77.3 (C-16). The configuration of the Δ24,25 double bond in the geranyl moiety was assigned as E due to the lack of a NOESY correlation between Me-32 and H-24 (Figure S7). The C-16 configuration was ascertained through electronic circular dichroism (ECD) spectroscopy, revealing a positive Cotton effect at 235 nm and a negative Cotton effect at 311 nm (Figure 2). To leverage the ECD data for configuration determination, theoretical ECD spectra for two stereoisomers of 1, 1-(R) and 1-(S), were generated through density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Consequently, the absolute configuration of compound 1 was conclusively established as 16S. Thus, the structure of 1 was identified as shown and designated as artointegrin A.

Compound 2 was obtained as a yellow viscous oil. The molecular formula C36H38O8, two mass units less than 1, was deduced from HR-TOFMS at m/z 599.2366 ([M + H]+, calcd 599.2645). The NMR spectroscopic data (Table 1), as well as the UV and IR spectra, of 2 exhibited similarities to those of 1. The most significant difference was the absence of the triplet H-19 and doublet H2-18, which were replaced by a double-bond-coupled doublet resonating at δH 5.63 and 6.73 (J = 10.0 Hz). These supported the ring closure between 3-OH and the prenyl moiety to form a gem-dimethylpyran ring, which was in agreement with the molecular formula and strongly supported by the presence of an additional O-bearing tertiary carbon resonating at δC 78.6 (C-20). Finally, it was confirmed by HMBC cross-peaks from H-18 to C-3 and C-20 and those from Me-21 to C-19, C-20, and C-22 (Figure 1). Likewise, lack of a NOESY correlation of Me-32 and H-24 (Figure S16) indicated an E-configuration for the Δ24,25 double bond. The specific rotation of 1 ( −254) and 2 ( −199) enabled us to assume that both 1 and 2 share the same absolute configuration, 16S. Moreover, the experimental ECD spectrum of 2, displaying a positive Cotton effect at 256 nm and a negative Cotton effect at 310 nm, did match with the calculated one of compound 2-(S) (Figure 2). Thus, the structure of 2 was identified as shown and named artointegrin B.

Compound 4 was obtained as a yellow gum and possessed the molecular formula of C19H20O7 as established from the HR-TOFMS data at m/z 361.1216 ([M + H]+, calcd 361.1287). The flavanone motif was assumed from the UV absorption maxima at 230, 283, and 332 nm and from the presence of typical signals for an ABX system at δH 5.98 (1H, dd, J = 13.5, 3.0 Hz, H-2), 3.92 (1H, dd, J = 17.5, 13.5 Hz, H-3a), and 2.49 (1H, dd, J = 17.5, 3.0 Hz, H-3b) in the 1H NMR spectrum (Table 2).35 Additionally, four methoxy groups resonating at δH 3.83 (3H, s), 3.80 (6H, s), and 3.77 (3H, s) and a chelated hydroxy group resonating at δH 12.4 (1H, s) were observed. The positions of the methoxy groups at C-7, C-2′, C-4′, and C-5′ were confirmed by the HMBC correlations (Figure 1). The absolute configuration of C-2 was assumed as 2S by comparing its specific rotation with that of (−)-5-hydroxy-3′,4′,7-trimethoxyflavanone.36 Finally, the 2S configuration of 4 was unambiguously elucidated by the TDDFT-ECD method (Figure 2). Thus, the structure of 4 was identified as shown and named artointegrin D.

All compounds were evaluated for tyrosinase inhibitory activity using l-DOPA as a substrate and kojic acid, a marketed whitening agent, as a positive control. Three compounds, 10, 12, and 19, displayed the most potent activity with IC50 values of 3.1 ± 0.4, 1.7 ± 0.2, and 1.2 ± 0.1 μM, respectively, which were 8–20-fold more potent than kojic acid (IC50 25.9 ± 1.4 μM). Compound 11 was moderately active (IC50 = 16.2 ± 1.2 μM), whereas compounds 16 and 17 showed weak activity (IC50 = 43.3 ± 1.6 and 52.7 ± 1.7 μM, respectively). The remaining compounds did not show any significant activity at a screening dose of 50 μM. Preliminary SAR for flavones 12–15 showed that the number and position of hydroxy groups on the phenyl B ring critically affected the activity. Two hydroxy groups at meta and para positions as in 12 were specifically required, while replacing one hydroxy group with a methoxy group as in 13 and 15 and the absence of a hydroxy group as in 14 resulted in a loss of activity.

Two different type compounds, flavone 12 and arylbenzofuran 19, exhibiting the most potent activity were considered for performing a kinetic study to reveal their modes of action using Lineweaver–Burk plots. The plots of 1/V vs 1/[S] for 12 gave four straight lines with difference slopes that intersected at the point of the y-axis (Figure 3). Regardless of the concentration of 12, the Vmax was unaffected. Yet, the KM value increased along with increasing concentration of 12, indicating that the compound acted as a competitive inhibitor of tyrosinase with a Ki value of 2.6 μM (Table 4). Conversely, 19 produced four straight lines merging at the x-axis, as shown in Figure 3, where Vmax decreased with increasing concentrations of compound and substrate. The results indicated that compound 19 inhibited tyrosinase through a noncompetitive mechanism by binding to an allosteric site on the enzyme, distinct from the catalytic site, with a Ki′ value of 2.6 μM (Table 4).

Further, a molecular docking study was performed to discover the binding mode and molecular interaction of tyrosinase with the compound. The docking results for 12 demonstrated that it inhibited the enzyme’s active site through π–π interactions with HIS263 (Figure 4). This inhibition was effective on only one part of the active tyrosinase (Cu401). In this scenario, the resorcinol moiety docked at the enzyme’s active site, acting as an alternative substrate for tyrosinase.37 The hydrogen bonding involved the catechol, with both hydroxyl groups strengthening the binding. Both compounds 12 and 19 exhibited similar binding interactions with the same residues. From the estimated binding energy, it is shown that 12 inhibited in the active site, while 19 inhibited in the allosteric site.

2D (A) and 3D (B) Interaction between the tyrosinase enzyme and compound 12 in the active site.

Compound 19 was docked in both the active site and the allosteric site, acting as a noncompetitive inhibitor. According to Table 5, the estimated binding energy in the active site was −6.12 kcal/mol, which is smaller in absolute value than the binding energy in the allosteric site (−7.51 kcal/mol). Specifically, the alkyl part of 19 bound to the active site with His61, His85, and His259 (in both Cu400 and Cu401), while the resorcinol moiety bound to other residues (Figure 5A and B). This interaction led to unstable energy because 19 bound to both copper ions. Additionally, the other active site residue, His263, interacted via π–σ bonding with the enzyme. The hydroxyl groups of 19 interacted with four residues via hydrogen bonding. Previous reports indicated that the interaction of the resorcinol ring with copper in the active site, replacing a histidine residue, significantly increased inhibitory activity.37,38 The results suggested that the hydrogen bonding between 19 and the enzyme at noncatalytic residues did not substantially contribute to the overall inhibitory effect. In contrast, 19 bound more strongly in the allosteric site. The dominant interactions involved conventional hydrogen bonding of hydroxyl in both resorcinol and benzofuran rings with Glu359, Thr308, Asp312, and Glu356 (Figure 5C and D). In addition, there were two π-interactions between the alkyl group of 19 with Lys379 and Lys372. This result was aligned with the previous study that mentioned some compounds prefer the allosteric site of tyrosinase.39

In conclusion, we report the discovery of five new compounds, including two xanthones (1 and 2) and one flavone (3) containing a geranyl moiety and one unique benzopyran (5), from A. integer root extract, along with 16 known compounds. Among them, two known compounds, flavone 12 and arylbenzofuran 19, exhibited the most potent activity against the tyrosinase enzyme. The kinetic study and molecular docking simulations revealed that compound 12 is a competitive inhibitor, while compound 19 acts as a noncompetitive inhibitor against tyrosinase. These results indicated that both compounds could be potential agents for developing antityrosinase leads.

Experimental Section

General Experimental Procedures

Optical rotations were measured on a JASCO P-1010 polarimeter (Easton, MD, USA). UV spectra were recorded on a PerkinElmer Lambda 950 UV–vis-NIR spectrometer (Waltham, MA, USA) in MeOH or MeCN. CD spectra were recorded on a JASCO j-815 CD instrument (Easton, MD, USA). IR spectra were collected from a PerkinElmer FTS FT-IR spectrophotometer (Waltham, MA, USA). NMR spectra were recorded on a JEOL (JNM-ECZR series, 500 MHz) spectrometer (Akishima, Tokyo, Japan). The chemical shifts are expressed in ppm with reference to the residual nondeuterated solvent signal, CDCl3. HR-MS spectra were obtained from a JEOL AccuTOF LC-Plus instrument (JMS-T100LP) (Akishima, Tokyo, Japan). Column chromatography was performed on silica gel G60 (60–200 mm; SiliCycle Inc., CA, USA), Sephadex LH-20 (Pharmacia Biotech AB, Uppsala, Sweden), and ODS (100 μm, Merck, USA). TLC was performed using silica gel GF254 plates (Merck, USA).

Plant Material

The roots of A. integer were collected from Surat Thani province, Thailand, in July 2023. The plant was authenticated by a member of the Royal Forest Department based in Surat Thani province. A voucher specimen (CUCHEM2023-004) has been deposited at the Department of Chemistry, Faculty of Science, Chulalongkorn University.

Extraction and Isolation

The air-dried roots of A. integer (0.5 kg) were mashed and macerated with MeOH (4 L each, 3 times for 7 days) at room temperature. The removal of the solvent under reduced pressure gave 32.2 g of the extract. The MeOH extract was then subjected to column chromatography (CC) with a gradient flow of hexanes/EtOAc (0:1 to 1:0, v/v), followed by a polarity gradient with MeOH, resulting in ten fractions (A-−). Fraction C (2.46 g) was purified by siliga gel CC (CH2Cl2–hexanes, 6:4, followed by MeOH–CH2Cl2, 1:99) to obtain 4-((E)-3,3-dimethylpenta-1,4-dienyl)-phenol (21, 3.8 mg). Fraction E (599.8 mg) was separated by RP-18 CC (MeCN–H2O, 6:4), followed by Sephadex-LH20 CC (MeOH) to give artointegrin B (2, 12.6 mg), artointegrin C (3, 4.4 mg), and artointegrin E (5, 2.3 mg). Fraction F (693.0 mg) was fractionated using Sephadex-LH20 CC (MeOH) to afford four subfractions (F1–F4). Subfraction F3 (383.5 mg) was separated over silica gel (acetone–hexanes, 3:17) to obtain artointegrin A (1, 1.5 mg), while subfraction F4 (80.0 mg) gave artointegrin D (4, 12.1 mg), artoxanthocarpuone B (6, 2.0 mg), and artoindonesianin A (8, 20.0 mg) from a silica gel column (hexanes–CH2Cl2–EtOAc, 19:80:1). Fraction G (1.86 g) was chromatographed over Sephadex-LH20 (MeOH) to give six subfractions (G1–G6). Subfraction G4 (183.3 mg) was purified by a silica gel column (MeOH–CH2Cl2, 5:95) to yield artonin A (9, 12.9 mg) and 2,8-dihydroxy-3,10-dimethoxy-6-(2-methyl-1-propen-1-yl)-6H,7H-[1]benzopyrano[4,3-b][1]-benzopyran-7-one (13, 3.8 mg). Subfraction G5 (48.8 mg) was separated over silica gel (hexane–CH2Cl2–acetone, 2:2:1), then over Sephadex-LH20 (MeOH–H2O, 8:2) to give artoindonesianin B-1 (16, 4.3 mg), moracin D (17, 4.1 mg), and artocarstilene A (18, 1.6 mg). Fraction H (2.42 g) was fractionated using Sephadex-LH20 CC (MeOH), resulting in six subfractions (H1–H6). Subfraction H3 (271.9 mg) was purified by silica gel CC (hexanes–CH2Cl2–acetone, 2:7:1, then 2% MeOH–CH2Cl2, 2:98) to obtain artoindonesianin S (7, 4.6 mg), integrin (11, 3.8 mg), and cyclocommunol (14, 3.1 mg), while subfraction H6 (74.1 mg) gave artoindonesianins A-1 (20, 17.2 mg) from silica gel CC (hexanes–CH2Cl2–acetone, 2:2:1). Fraction I (6.71 g) was subjected to Sephadex-LH20 CC (MeOH) to obtain four subfractions (I1–H4). Subfraction I3 (380.3 mg) was purified by silica gel CC (MeOH–CH2Cl2, 1:9) to yield 3-prenyl luteolin (10, 3.4 mg) and licocoumarone (19, 4.1 mg). Subfraction H4 (685.5 mg) was separated by silica gel CC (acetone–hexanes, 4:6, then MeOH–CH2Cl2, 5:95) yielding cyclochampedol (12, 25.0 mg) and artoindonesianins A-2 (15, 1.4 mg).

ECD Calculation

A conformational search within 5 kcal/mol of the energy window was performed by using Spartan 20 with the MMFF94 molecular mechanics mode. All density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 rev. C.01 program.40,41 The low-energy conformers were further optimized at the ωB97XD/cc-PVDZ level of theory of the gas phase. All optimized conformers were confirmed to be the true minimum of electronic potential energy through calculation of the vibrational frequencies at the same level, and no imaginary frequency was detected. Each conformer with a population over 1% based on a Boltzmann distribution of Gibbs free energies was subject to the following ECD calculations. The ECD spectra of 1, 2, 4, and 5 were simulated using TD-DFT at the M06–2x/def2-SVP level of theory for 30 excited states with IEFPCM of the acetonitrile solvent model.42−44 The simulated ECD curves were generated using SpecDis with the Boltzmann average all conformers and an overlapping Gaussian function with an exponential half-width (σ = 0.35).45,46 Theoretical ECD spectra of the related enantiomers were generated from the direct inversion of the simulated ECD spectra.

DP4+ Analysis

All magnetic shielding tensors (σ) of the low-energy conformers with Boltzmann populations greater than 1% were weight-calculated using the GIAO method at the mPW1PW91/6-31+G(d,p) level in solvent (PCM).47 All structures were virtualized by using CYLview.48

Tyrosinase Inhibitory Assay

The inhibitory effect of the isolated compounds on mushroom tyrosinase was assessed by the spectrophotometry method, as previously reported, with slight modifications.49l-DOPA was used as the substrate in this experiment. Compounds (50 μL) were added to the 96-well plate in various concentrations, followed by tyrosinase solution (50 μL, 50 units, in PBS). After the first incubation for 10 min, 50 μL of substrates (l-DOPA in PBS, pH 6.8, final concentration 0.5 mM) were added, followed by second incubation for 20 min. The absorbance was immediately monitored at 492 nm to represent the enzyme activity. Kojic acid was used as a positive control.

Kinetic Study on Tyrosinase

Lineweaver–Burk plots were made to ascertain the tyrosinase inhibition manner of 12 and 19.44 Tested compounds (50 μL, final concentrations 0, 2, 4, and 8 μM) and mushroom tyrosinase (50 μL, 50 units) were added to an aqueous solution of l-DOPA at concentrations of 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.063, and 0.03125 mM (pH 6.8). The mixture was then incubated for 20 min and the absorbance was measured at 495 nm. Tyrosinase-l-DOPA maximum velocities were obtained by employing Lineweaver–Burk plots (1/V vs 1/[S]). Convergence points of the plots were used to evaluate the different forms of tyrosinase inhibition.

Molecular Docking Study

The molecular docking study was conducted to predict the binding of 12 and 19 with the tyrosinase model from Agaricus bisporus (PDB ID 2Y9X) using AutoDock 4.2 (The Scripps Research Institute, La Jolla, CA, USA). The protein preparation followed the method described by Roselan et al.50 The 3D structures of the compounds were drawn in Hyperchem and optimized using the semiempirical AM1 method. The ligand was then converted to a pdbqt file using AutoDock Tools. Docking simulations were performed within a grid box of 40 Å × 40 Å × 40 Å, positioning the ligand in the enzyme’s active site residues (Cu400 with His61, His85, His94; Cu401 with His259, His263 and His296) at coordinates −10.004, −28.280, and −43.443. The Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm was employed for docking, with 100 runs as the search parameter. Blind docking was further performed to compare the results with those obtained from docking at the active site.39 The conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected and visualized by using Discovery Studio Visualizer 21.1.
