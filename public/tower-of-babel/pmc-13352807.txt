Smoking and depressive symptoms: the mediating role of systemic inflammation in a US nationally representative sample
Authors: Xuyu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmoking and depressive symptoms are strongly associated, but the underlying biological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Systemic inflammation has been proposed as a potential mediator linking smoking behavior to depressive symptoms. However, few studies have simultaneously examined multiple inflammatory biomarkers in large, nationally representative samples.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate whether systemic inflammation, measured by C-reactive protein (CRP) and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), mediates the association between current smoking and depressive symptoms in a US nationally representative sample.MethodsI analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2005–2018, including 28,456 adults aged 20 years and older. Smoking status was categorized as current, former, or never smoker. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Serum CRP levels and NLR were used as inflammatory biomarkers. Mediation analysis was performed using the Baron and Kenny approach with bootstrap methods to estimate indirect effects.ResultsCurrent smokers had significantly higher prevalence of depressive symptoms. compared to never smokers (12.8% vs. 7.2%, p < 0.001). Both CRP levels and NLR were significantly elevated in current smokers compared to never smokers (CRP: 3.2 ± 0.1 vs. 2.1 ± 0.1 mg/L; NLR: 2.4 ± 0.1 vs. 2.0 ± 0.1, both p < 0.001). In mediation analysis, CRP mediated 15.3% (95% CI: 12.1–18.7%) and NLR mediated 8.7% (95% CI: 6.2–11.4%) of the association between current smoking and depressive symptoms. The combined mediation effect of both inflammatory markers was 21.2% (95% CI: 17.8–24.9%).ConclusionsSystemic inflammation, as measured by CRP and NLR, partially mediates the association between smoking and depressive symptoms in US adults. These findings support the inflammatory hypothesis of depression and suggest that anti-inflammatory interventions may be beneficial for smokers with depressive symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-026-00675-0.Keywords: Smoking, Depression, Inflammation, C-reactive protein, Neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio, Mediation analysis, NHANES

Introduction

The relationship between smoking and depressive symptoms represents one of the most robust associations in psychiatric epidemiology, with mounting evidence suggesting a complex bidirectional relationship that extends beyond simple behavioral comorbidity [1]. Current smokers are approximately twice as likely to experience depressive symptoms compared to non-smokers, and individuals with depression are significantly more likely to initiate and maintain smoking behaviors [2, 3]. This association has profound public health implications, as both smoking and depression are leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with their co-occurrence creating a particularly challenging clinical scenario that is associated with poorer treatment outcomes and increased healthcare utilization [4, 5].

Despite extensive epidemiological evidence documenting this association, the underlying biological mechanisms linking smoking to depressive symptoms remain incompletely understood. Traditional explanations have focused on the self-medication hypothesis, suggesting that individuals with depressive symptoms use nicotine to alleviate negative mood states through its acute effects on neurotransmitter systems [6]. However, this model fails to fully account for the observed bidirectional relationship and the persistence of the association even after controlling for potential confounding factors. More recently, researchers have begun to explore shared biological pathways that might explain the smoking-depression link, with particular attention to the role of systemic inflammation as a potential mediating mechanism [7, 8].

The inflammatory hypothesis of depression has gained substantial empirical support over the past two decades, with numerous studies demonstrating elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and acute-phase proteins in individuals with depressive symptoms [9, 10]. Meta-analytic evidence consistently shows that individuals with depression have significantly higher levels of inflammatory markers, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and C-reactive protein (CRP), compared to healthy controls [11]. These findings have led to the conceptualization of depression as a systemic inflammatory condition, with inflammation potentially contributing to the pathophysiology of depressive symptoms through multiple mechanisms, including alterations in neurotransmitter metabolism, activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and disruption of neuroplasticity [12, 13].

Smoking represents a potent stimulus for systemic inflammation, with tobacco smoke containing over 4,000 chemical compounds, many of which are known to activate inflammatory pathways [14]. Chronic exposure to tobacco smoke leads to sustained elevation of inflammatory markers, including CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α, through multiple mechanisms including direct tissue damage, oxidative stress, and immune system activation [15, 16]. The inflammatory response to smoking is dose-dependent and persists even after smoking cessation, although levels gradually decline over time [17]. This inflammatory response may represent a key biological pathway through which smoking contributes to the development and maintenance of depressive symptoms.

C-reactive protein (CRP) has emerged as one of the most widely studied inflammatory biomarkers in the context of depression. As an acute-phase protein synthesized by the liver in response to inflammatory stimuli, CRP serves as a sensitive marker of systemic inflammation [18]. Numerous studies have documented elevated CRP levels in individuals with depression, with meta-analytic evidence suggesting a moderate but consistent association [19, 20]. The relationship between CRP and depression appears to be bidirectional, with some longitudinal studies suggesting that elevated CRP levels predict the subsequent development of depressive symptoms [21]. In the context of smoking, CRP levels are consistently elevated in current smokers compared to non-smokers, with a dose-response relationship observed between smoking intensity and CRP levels [22, 23].

The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) represents a more recently recognized.

inflammatory biomarker that has shown promise in psychiatric research. NLR is calculated as the ratio of absolute neutrophil count to absolute lymphocyte count and reflects the balance between innate and adaptive immune responses [24]. Elevated NLR indicates a predominance of neutrophilic inflammation, which is characteristic of acute inflammatory states. Several studies have reported elevated NLR in individuals with depression, with some evidence suggesting that NLR may be more sensitive to acute inflammatory changes than traditional markers like CRP [25, 26]. The advantage of NLR lies in its simplicity and cost-effectiveness, as it can be calculated from a standard complete blood count without requiring additional laboratory tests.

Based on the existing literature and theoretical considerations, I hypothesized that: (1) current smoking would be associated with elevated levels of both CRP and NLR; (2) elevated CRP and NLR would be associated with higher levels of depressive symptoms; and (3) CRP and NLR would partially mediate the association between smoking and depressive symptoms, with the mediation effect being statistically significant and clinically meaningful. Methods

Study design and data source

This study employed a cross-sectional design using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative survey of the non-institutionalized US population conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). I analyzed data from seven consecutive NHANES cycles spanning 2005–2018, which provided a large sample size and enhanced statistical power for mediation analyses. NHANES uses a complex, multistage probability sampling design to ensure representativeness of the US population, with oversampling of certain demographic groups to improve precision of estimates for these populations [27].

Study population

The initial sample included 70,190 participants from NHANES 2005–2018. I restricted our analysis to adults aged 20 years and older, as the depression screening instrument (PHQ-9) was only administered to this age group. I excluded participants with missing data on key variables, including smoking status (n = 1,247) and depressive symptoms (n = 8,932). I further excluded participants with missing CRP measurements or CRP levels greater than 10 mg/L (n = 3,110), as values above this threshold likely reflect acute inflammatory conditions rather than chronic low-grade inflammation relevant to depression [28]. Among the remaining participants, those with missing complete blood count data required to calculate the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) were excluded (n = 889). After applying these exclusion criteria, the final analytical sample comprised 28,456 participants. The flowchart of participant selection is shown in Fig. 2. Key baseline demographic and mental health characteristics of participants included in the analytical sample and those excluded due to missing data are presented in Table S1.

Variable definitions and measurements

Smoking status was assessed through standardized questionnaires administered during the household interview. Participants were classified into three categories based on their responses to questions about lifetime and current tobacco use: (1) never smokers (smoked < 100 cigarettes in lifetime and not currently smoking), (2) former smokers (smoked ≥ 100 cigarettes in lifetime but not currently smoking), and (3) current smokers (smoked ≥ 100 cigarettes in lifetime and currently smoking) [29]. Among current smokers, smoking intensity was assessed as the self-reported number of cigarettes smoked per day and treated as a continuous variable. Dose-response analyses were restricted to current smokers. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a validated screening instrument for depression that has been widely used in population-based studies [30]. I used the PHQ-9 in two ways: (1) as a continuous measure (total score ranging from 0 to 27), and (2) as a binary measure of clinically significant depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) [31]. Serum CRP levels were measured using latex-enhanced nephelometry. CRP levels were log-transformed for analyses due to their skewed distribution [32]. Complete blood counts were performed using the Coulter HmX Hematology Analyzer. NLR was calculated as the ratio of absolute neutrophil count to absolute lymphocyte count. Values were log-transformed for analyses due to their skewed distribution.

Covariates

We included demographic variables (age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, income), health behavior variables (alcohol consumption, physical activity, BMI), and indicators for major chronic diseases (diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease).

Statistical analysis

All analyses accounted for the complex survey design of NHANES using appropriate survey weights. I conducted mediation analysis using the causal steps approach outlined by Baron and Kenny [33], supplemented by modern statistical methods for estimating and testing indirect effects [34]. I estimated indirect effects using the product of coefficients method and calculated confidence intervals using bias-corrected bootstrap procedures with 5,000 replications [35]. Missing data were present in several covariates, while all primary variables of interest (smoking status, depressive symptoms, CRP, and NLR) had complete data. Cases with missing values on any covariate were excluded using a listwise deletion approach. To assess the robustness of the primary findings, I performed sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions of depressive symptoms, and stratified analyses by sex, age, and race/ethnicity. All sensitivity analyses used the same modeling approach and covariate adjustments as the primary analyses.

Results

Sample characteristics

The final analytical sample comprised 28,456 adults aged 20 years and older, representing approximately 206.3 million US adults after applying survey weights (Table 1). The weighted mean age was 46.8 years (SE = 0.3), and 51.2% of participants were female. The prevalence of smoking was: 55.8% never smokers, 24.2% former smokers, and 20.0% current smokers. The weighted prevalence of clinically significant depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) was 8.4% overall, with significant variation by smoking status: 7.2% among never smokers, 8.9% among former smokers, and 12.8% among current smokers (p < 0.001).

Inflammatory biomarker distributions

The distribution of inflammatory biomarkers varied significantly by smoking status. The geometric mean CRP level was 2.1 mg/L (95% CI: 2.0-2.2) among never smokers, 2.5 mg/L (95% CI: 2.4–2.6) among former smokers, and 3.2 mg/L (95% CI: 3.0-3.4) among current smokers (p < 0.001 for trend). The geometric mean NLR was 2.0 (95% CI: 1.98–2.02) among never smokers, 2.1 (95% CI: 2.08–2.12) among former smokers, and 2.4 (95% CI: 2.36–2.44) among current smokers (p < 0.001 for trend).

Associations between smoking and depressive symptoms

In unadjusted analyses, current smoking was strongly associated with higher PHQ-9 scores compared to never smoking (mean difference: 2.15 points, 95% CI: 1.91–2.39, p < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model controlling for all covariates, current smoking remained significantly associated with depressive symptoms (adjusted mean difference: 1.34 points, 95% CI: 1.13–1.55, p < 0.001).

Associations between smoking and inflammatory biomarkers

Current smoking was strongly associated with elevated inflammatory biomarkers in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses. In the fully adjusted model, current smoking was associated with a 0.42-unit increase in log-transformed CRP (95% CI: 0.36–0.48, p < 0.001), corresponding to a 52% increase in geometric mean CRP levels. Similarly, current smoking was associated with a 0.28-unit increase in log-transformed NLR (95% CI: 0.24–0.32, p < 0.001), corresponding to a 32% increase in geometric mean NLR.

Associations between inflammatory biomarkers and depressive symptoms

Both CRP and NLR were significantly associated with depressive symptoms in adjusted analyses. Each unit increase in log-transformed CRP was associated with a 0.78-point increase in PHQ-9 score (95% CI: 0.62–0.94, p < 0.001). Each unit increase in log-transformed NLR was associated with a 0.65-point increase in PHQ-9 score (95% CI: 0.47–0.83, p < 0.001).

Mediation analysis results

The mediation analysis revealed significant indirect effects of smoking on depressive symptoms through both inflammatory pathways (Fig. 3). For the comparison between current smoking and never smoking, CRP mediated 15.3% of the total association (indirect effect: 0.33 points on PHQ-9 scale, 95% CI: 0.24–0.42; Table 2). NLR mediated 8.7% of the total association (indirect effect: 0.18 points on PHQ-9 scale, 95% CI: 0.13–0.24, p < 0.001).

Dose-response relationships

Among current smokers, I observed significant dose-response relationships between smoking intensity and both inflammatory biomarkers and depressive symptoms. Each additional cigarette smoked per day was associated with a 0.018-unit increase in log-CRP (95% CI: 0.012–0.024, p < 0.001) and a 0.012-unit increase in log-NLR (95% CI: 0.008–0.016, p < 0.001) (Table 3). The mediation effects also showed dose-response patterns, with heavier smokers showing larger indirect effects through inflammatory pathways.

Sensitivity analyses

The results were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses (Fig. 5). When using alternative definitions of depressive symptoms, the mediation effects remained significant and of similar magnitude. When stratifying by demographic characteristics, the mediation effects were generally consistent, although some variation was observed. Among women, the CRP-mediated effect was slightly larger (17.2% vs. 13.4% among men), while the NLR-mediated effect was similar between sexes.

Discussion

This large, nationally representative study provides population-based evidence suggesting that systemic inflammation may represent a plausible biological pathway linking smoking and depressive symptoms. These findings demonstrate that current smoking is associated with significantly elevated levels of both CRP and NLR, two complementary markers of systemic inflammation, and that these inflammatory biomarkers are independently associated with depressive symptoms. Most importantly, the mediation analysis reveals that CRP and NLR together account for approximately 21% of the total association between current smoking and depressive symptoms, with CRP contributing 15.3% and NLR contributing 8.7% of the mediation effect. These findings add mechanistic context to the well-established epidemiological association between smoking and depression. The magnitude of the observed mediation effects appears potentially meaningful at the population level. The combined indirect effect of 0.51 points on the PHQ-9 scale represents approximately 23% of the total effect of current smoking on depressive symptoms. The size of the indirect effect is comparable to that reported for some established correlates of depressive symptoms, such as chronic medical conditions or social isolation [36, 37].

These findings are consistent with and extend previous research on the relationships between smoking, inflammation, and depression. The association between smoking and elevated CRP levels observed in our study (52% increase in geometric mean CRP among current smokers) is similar to that reported in previous NHANES analyses and other population-based studies [38, 39]. Similarly, the finding of elevated NLR among smokers is consistent with recent studies that have identified NLR as a sensitive marker of smoking-related inflammation [40, 41]. The proportion of mediation observed in the study (21.2% for the combined effect of CRP and NLR) is somewhat higher than that reported in some previous studies but is consistent with others. The variation in mediation effects across studies likely reflects differences in study populations, inflammatory markers examined, and analytical approaches used.

Although mediation analysis is ideally conducted with longitudinal data, it has also been applied in cross-sectional studies to explore potential mechanistic pathways and to assess whether observed associations are consistent with hypothesized models [34, 42]. In this study, the analysis was intended to examine whether systemic inflammation may plausibly link smoking and depressive symptoms. Consistency with prior experimental and longitudinal evidence, biological plausibility, and robustness across multiple biomarkers and sensitivity analyses support interpreting the observed indirect effects as pathway-consistent associations rather than definitive causal effects.

The biological mechanisms underlying the inflammatory mediation of the smoking-depression relationship are complex and multifaceted. Smoking represents a potent stimulus for systemic inflammation through multiple pathways. The inhalation of tobacco smoke exposes the respiratory system to over 4,000 chemical compounds, many of which are toxic and pro-inflammatory [43]. These compounds cause direct tissue damage in the lungs and other organs, leading to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns that activate innate immune responses [44]. CRP, as an acute-phase protein synthesized by the liver in response to inflammatory stimuli, serves as a sensitive marker of this systemic inflammatory state. NLR reflects a different aspect of the inflammatory response, specifically the balance between innate and adaptive immune responses. Smoking has been shown to alter immune cell populations, leading to increased neutrophil counts and decreased lymphocyte counts, resulting in an elevated NLR [45]. The mechanisms by which systemic inflammation contributes to depressive symptoms are also well-established. Inflammatory cytokines can cross the blood-brain barrier and directly affect brain function through multiple pathways [46]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines can activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to increased cortisol production and dysregulation of stress response systems [47]. Inflammation also affects neurotransmitter systems that are central to mood regulation and can affect neuroplasticity and neurogenesis [48, 49].

The findings of the study have important implications for both clinical practice and public health policy. From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the association between smoking and depressive symptoms. However, due to the cross-sectional design, causality cannot be established. It is possible that depressive symptoms lead to increased smoking (reverse causality), or that shared factors contribute to both smoking and depression. Nevertheless, healthcare providers may consider monitoring depressive symptoms among smokers as part of routine clinical care. The inflammatory mediation pathway identified in the study also suggests potential therapeutic targets for treating depression in smokers. Anti-inflammatory medications, which have shown promise in treating depression in general populations, may be particularly beneficial for smokers with depressive symptoms [50, 51]. Several randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that anti-inflammatory drugs such as celecoxib and minocycline can improve depressive symptoms, particularly in individuals with elevated inflammatory markers [52, 53]. From a public health perspective, These findings add mechanistic context to existing evidence supporting smoking prevention and cessation efforts. Reducing smoking rates in the population would not only prevent the direct health consequences of tobacco use but would also reduce the burden of depression and other mental health conditions. The dose-response relationships observed in our study provide additional support for harm reduction approaches to smoking cessation. While complete smoking cessation is the ultimate goal, our findings suggest that even reductions in smoking intensity can lead to meaningful reductions in inflammatory burden and potentially depression risk.

The study has several important strengths that enhance the validity and generalizability of our findings. First, the use of NHANES data provides a large, nationally representative sample that allows for robust statistical analyses and generalization to the US adult population. Second, our comprehensive approach to mediation analysis, including the simultaneous examination of multiple inflammatory biomarkers and the use of bootstrap methods to estimate confidence intervals, provides robust statistical evidence for the mediation effects. However, the study also has several important limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of NHANES limits causal inference. Mediation analysis is conceptually rooted in causal inference and is ideally performed using longitudinal data. Although cross-sectional mediation analyses are commonly used to explore potential mechanisms, this approach remains exploratory and controversial. Therefore, the mediation results in the present study should be interpreted as pathway-consistent and hypothesis-supporting rather than as definitive evidence of causality. In addition, although data from multiple NHANES cycles were pooled to increase statistical power, I did not formally examine temporal trends across survey years. Second, missing data were present in several covariates, resulting in the exclusion of approximately one-third of the eligible sample through listwise deletion. Importantly, all primary variables of interest, including smoking status, depressive symptoms, CRP, and NLR, had complete data. Therefore, while listwise deletion may have reduced statistical power, it is unlikely to have materially biased the estimated associations or mediation effects. Nevertheless, future studies could consider alternative approaches such as multiple imputation to further address missing covariate data. Third, our measurement of inflammation was limited to two biomarkers (CRP and NLR), which may not capture the full complexity of the inflammatory response. Fourth, our assessment of depressive symptoms was based on a screening instrument (PHQ-9) rather than clinical diagnostic interviews. Finally, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be completely eliminated, as unmeasured factors could potentially explain some of the observed associations. In addition, major chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease) were included as covariates because of their strong and well-established associations with both systemic inflammation and depressive symptoms, which could act as sources of confounding. These conditions may lie downstream of smoking-related inflammation and could therefore be part of the causal pathway linking smoking to depressive symptoms. As a result, adjustment for these variables may have led to conservative estimates of the associations and mediation effects. Importantly, all interpretations regarding mediation and potential mechanisms should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

These findings suggest several important directions for future research. First, longitudinal studies are needed to establish the temporal relationships between smoking, inflammation, and depression. Second, intervention studies are needed to test whether anti-inflammatory treatments can improve depressive symptoms in smokers. Third, studies examining the genetic and epigenetic factors that influence these relationships would provide insights into individual differences in susceptibility.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence from a large, nationally representative sample suggesting that systemic inflammation may partially mediate the association between smoking and depressive symptoms. These findings demonstrate that current smoking is associated with elevated levels of both C-reactive protein and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and that these inflammatory biomarkers independently contribute to depressive symptom severity. The mediation analysis reveals that inflammatory pathways account for approximately 21% of the total association between current smoking and depressive symptoms, representing a substantial proportion of the observed association.

These findings have important implications for both clinical practice and public health policy. From a clinical perspective, they suggest that healthcare providers should consider screening for depressive symptoms among smokers and that integrated interventions addressing both smoking cessation and mental health may be particularly beneficial. The identification of inflammatory pathways as mediators also suggests that anti-inflammatory treatments may be promising therapeutic approaches for smokers with depressive symptoms.

From a public health perspective, these findings provide additional justification for aggressive tobacco control policies and smoking prevention efforts. The fact that smoking contributes to depression risk through inflammatory mechanisms underscores the broader health consequences of tobacco use beyond the well-known effects on cardiovascular and respiratory health.

While the cross-sectional design limits causal inferences, the consistency of our findings with existing literature, the biological plausibility of the inflammatory mediation pathway, and the robustness of our results across multiple sensitivity analyses provide strong support for our conclusions. Future longitudinal studies and randomized controlled trials of anti-inflammatory interventions are needed to confirm these findings and to translate them into effective clinical and public health strategies.

In summary, this study advances our understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying the smoking-depression relationship and highlights inflammatory pathways as potential targets for future investigation. By elucidating the role of systemic inflammation in mediating the effects of smoking on mental health, our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the inflammatory hypothesis of depression and provide a foundation for developing more effective approaches to preventing and treating both smoking and depression.
