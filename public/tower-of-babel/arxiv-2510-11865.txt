Subvarieties of low degree on general hypersurfaces
Nathan Chen and David Yang

Abstract
. The purpose of this note is to show that the subvarieties of small degree inside a general hypersurface of large degree come from intersecting with linear spaces or other varieties.



Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
be a general hypersurface of degree
d\geq 2n
. There has been considerable interest over the years in understanding what curves or other subvarieties can be found on
X
. It is elementary that
X
contains lines if and only if
d<2n
, and Wu
[
Wu90
]
observed that if
n=3
and
d\geq 6
, then the only curves
C\subseteq X
of degree
\delta\leq 2d-2
are plane sections of
X
. Our first result partially generalizes Wu’s statement to arbitrary dimensions:

Theorem A
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a general hypersurface of degree
d\geq 2n
and let
Y\subset X
be a positive-dimensional subvariety such that
\deg Y\leq d+2
. Then
\deg Y=d
and
Y=X\cap\Lambda
is the intersection of
X
with a linear subspace
\Lambda\cong\mathbb{P}^{\dim Y+1}
.

When
d\gg n
, the statement that
\deg Y\geq d
follows from
[
CCZ24
, Theorem A]
, where the authors establish a more general degree bound for subvarieties of complete intersection varieties. Theorem
A
answers
[
CCZ24
, Question 6.7]
for hypersurfaces.

Under stronger degree hypotheses, we prove an analogous statement for subvarieties of higher degree:

Theorem B
.

Fix any integer
s
. There exists a positive integer
d_{0}=d_{0}(s,n)
with the following property.
1
1

1

See Theorem
4.1
and Remark
4.2
for more precise numerics.
Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
be a general hypersurface of degree
d\geq d_{0}
and let
Y\subset X
be any subvariety of dimension
m\geq 1
and degree
\delta\leq ds
. Then
\delta
is a multiple of
d
and
Y
is equal to the generically transverse intersection
Y=X\cap V
for a unique variety
V\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of dimension
m+1
.

In other words, subvarieties of relatively small degree with respect to
d
all arise as complete intersections of
X
with proper subvarieties of the ambient projective space, up to embedded points. By
generically transverse
intersection, we mean that
X\cap V
is generically reduced and
Y
is equal to
(X\cap V)^{\text{red}}
as schemes. Note that one can get embedded points if
V
is singular. For example, let
V\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
be the projection of the Veronese surface
\sigma\colon\mathbb{P}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{5}
from a general point lying on the secant variety to
\sigma(\mathbb{P}^{2})
; then the degree of
V
is equal to 4 and
V
is not Cohen-Macaulay at a finite number of double points. Therefore, the intersection
V\cap X
with any smooth hypersurface
X
passing through one of these singular points of
V
will be non-reduced.

In a slightly different direction, a theorem of Voisin states that a very general hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
d\geq 2n
does not contain any rational curves. This story goes back to the work of Clemens
[
Cle86
]
and Ein
[
Ein88
]
, and since then there has been a great deal of work by many others on studying the geometric genus of subvarieties in very general hypersurfaces (cf.
[
Xu94
,
Pac04
,
CR04
,
CR19
]
). For instance, if
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
is a very general hypersurface of degree
d
, then under suitable numerical hypotheses it is known that any subvariety of
X
must be of general type. Theorem
A
can be combined with the methods in
[
Voi96
]
to give a bound on the geometric genus of any curve in
X
. There are also some related conjectures of Griffiths–Harris
[
GH85
]
from the mid-80s about curves on a very general hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
of degree
d\geq 6
; the strongest form of their conjecture simply asks whether every curve in
X
is the complete intersection with some surface. This was answered in the negative in a paper of Voisin
[
Voi89
]
, where she constructed counterexamples on any smooth threefold hypersurface.

The starting point for this paper is the observation that the numerics for the geometric genus of curves in very general hypersurfaces closely mirror those coming from Castelnuovo-type bounds (and its variations due to Halphen, Harris, and Gruson–Peskine) for a non-degenerate curve in projective space. In §1, we will reduce the proof of Theorem
A
to a special case (Theorem
1.1
). Using incidence correspondences and regularity results of Gruson–Lazarsfeld–Peskine
[
GLP83
]
for curves, we will prove in §2 that low degree curves in general hypersurfaces satisfy fairly strong arithmetic genus lower bounds. In §3, we will show that these lower bounds violate the Castelnuovo-type bounds unless the dimension of the span of the curve is small to begin with, and use this to ultimately prove Theorem
1.1
. Finally, the proof of Theorem
B
will be given in §4, and involves an induction argument with Theorem
A
as the base case.

Conventions.
Throughout, we work over the complex numbers. By
variety
, we always mean a projective integral scheme of finite type over
\mathbb{C}
. A
curve
is a variety of dimension 1.

We will use
\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}
to denote the projective space parametrizing hypersurfaces
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
d
, where
N_{d}=\binom{d+n+1}{n+1}-1
. Since there are finitely many Hilbert schemes parametrizing curves in
\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of bounded degree, in almost all of our results we may assume without loss of generality that
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
is a
very
general hypersurface, meaning outside of a countable union of subvarieties in
\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}
. For the reader’s convenience, we will use parenthesis to indicate when this assumption can be dropped a posteriori. By
\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})
we mean the parameter space of linear embeddings
\mathbb{P}^{r}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
.

1.
Genus bounds for curves on very general hypersurfaces

In order to prove the results stated in the introduction, in the next three sections we will aim to prove a special case:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a general hypersurface of degree
d\geq 2n
and let
C\subset X
be a curve of degree
\delta
. If
\delta\leq d+2
, then
\delta=d
and
C=X\cap\Lambda
for some 2-plane
\Lambda
.

Theorem
A
follows immediately from this:

Proof of Theorem
A
.

As in the statement of the theorem, let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
be a general hypersurface of degree
d\geq 2n
and let
Y\subset X
be a positive-dimensional subvariety such that
\deg Y\leq d+2
. Consider general hyperplanes
H_{1},\ldots,H_{s}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
, where
s=\dim Y-1
. Slicing down
Y
, we then obtain a curve

V\coloneqq Y\cap H_{1}\cap\cdots\cap H_{s}

of degree
\leq d+2
which is contained in
X
(by Bertini’s theorem
V
is integral, but we will not need this). Theorem
1.1
implies that
\deg V=d
and
V
is a plane curve, i.e.
\dim\Span V=2
. At each step of slicing, the degree stays the same and the span decreases by 1 since the
H_{i}
are general. Thus, working backwards we see that
\deg Y=d
and
\dim\Span(Y)=2+s=\dim Y+1
. Setting
\Lambda\coloneqq\Span(Y)\cong\mathbb{P}^{\dim Y+1}
, it follows that
Y=X\cap\Lambda
.
∎

An invariant that will feature prominently in the proof of Theorem
1.1
is the dimension of the span of
C
inside
\mathbb{P}^{n+1}_{\mathbb{C}}
. As mentioned in the introduction, a general hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
d\geq 2n
does not contain any lines, so
\dim\Span(C)\geq 2
. In fact, we claim that it suffices to show
\dim\Span(C)=2
:

Proposition 1.2
.

On a general hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
d\geq 2n
, the intersection
X\cap\Lambda
with every 2-plane
\Lambda
is an integral curve.

Proof.

By a dimension count,
X
does not contain any 2-planes. The case
n=2
follows from the Noether-Lefschetz theorem (plus a standard Hilbert scheme argument to pass from very general to general). Assuming
n\geq 3
, we will estimate the space of hypersurfaces for which the intersection with a 2-plane
\Lambda
splits off a plane curve of degree
a<d
. Consider the incidence correspondence:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{a}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\{(X_{d},f\colon\mathbb{P}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n+1},C)\in\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}\times\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(a)|\colon f(C)\subset X_{d}\}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\coloneqq}

There is an action of
\PGL_{3}=\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{2})
on
\Phi_{a}
which is defined by
\phi\cdot(X,f,C)=(X,f\circ\phi,\phi^{-1}(C))
. Since we are interested in the orbit space, we will pass to the quotient
\Phi_{a}/\PGL_{3}
, which then admits the following projection maps:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{a}/\PGL_{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(a)|)/\PGL_{3}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}

If
\dim\Phi_{a}/\PGL_{3}<N_{d}
, then
\Phi_{a}/\PGL_{3}
cannot dominate
\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}
and so we are done.

When
n\geq 3
, a very general
X
does not contain rational curves
[
Voi96
]
, so we may assume
a\geq 3
. Fixing a point in the image of
\pi_{2}
amounts to fixing the 2-plane
\Lambda
and a plane curve
C\subset\Lambda
of degree
a
. The fiber over such a point is isomorphic to the projectivization of
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{I}_{C}(d))
. The latter group is the kernel of the restriction map
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n+1}}(d))\twoheadrightarrow H^{0}(\Lambda,\mathcal{O}_{\Lambda}(d))\twoheadrightarrow H^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))
, so
\pi_{2}
has relative dimension equal to
h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n+1}}(d))-h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))-1
. On the other hand, the image of
\pi_{2}
has dimension at most

\displaystyle\dim[(\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(a)|)/\PGL_{3}]=3(n-1)+h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(a))-1.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\dim\Phi_{a}/\PGL_{3}

\displaystyle\leq 3(n-1)+h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{2}}(a))-1+h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n+1}}(d))-h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))-1

\displaystyle=N_{d}+3n-4-a(d-a).

From this we see that
\dim\Phi_{a}/\PGL_{3}<N_{d}
if
a(d-a)>3n-4
, which is true when
\frac{d}{2}\geq a\geq 3
(by symmetry it is enough to check this range) and
d\geq 2n
.
∎

By further slicing with general hyperplanes, it follows that the intersection of a general hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
d\geq 2n
with
any
linear subspace of dimension
\geq 2
is reduced and irreducible.

The argument above can be generalized to the case of quadric surfaces:

Proposition 1.3
.

On a general hypersurface
X\cap\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
d\geq 2n
, the intersection
X\cap Q
with every quadric surface
Q
is an integral curve.

Proof.

By a dimension count,
X
does not contain any quadric surfaces. As mentioned before, the case
n=2
follows from the Noether-Lefschetz theorem. Assuming
n\geq 3
, we will estimate the dimension of the space of hypersurfaces for which the intersection with a quadric surface splits off a component.

Let us first work out the case where
Q\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a smooth quadric surface. For two nonnegative integers
a
and
b
, consider the incidence correspondence:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{a,b}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\{(X_{d},f\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n+1},C)\in\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}\times\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a,b)|\colon f(C)\subset X_{d}\}}.}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\coloneqq}

Here, an element of
\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})
consists of the Segre embedding
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
composed with a linear embedding
\mathbb{P}^{3}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
. There is an action of
\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})
on
\Phi_{a,b}
which is defined by
\phi\cdot(X,f,C)=(X,f\circ\phi,\phi^{-1}(C))
. Since we are interested in the orbit space, we will pass to the quotient
\Phi_{a,b}/\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})
, which then admits the following projection maps
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi_{a,b}/\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a,b)|)/\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}

We wish to show that if
(a,b)\neq(d,d)
, then
\Phi_{a,b}/\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})
cannot dominate
\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}
. When
n\geq 3
, a very general
X
does not contain rational curves
[
Voi96
]
, so we may assume by symmetry that
d-2\geq b\geq a\geq 2
. It suffices to show that in this case,
\dim\Phi_{a,b}/\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})<N_{d}
.

Fixing a point in the image of
\pi_{2}
amounts to fixing a quadric
Q
and a curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
of bi-degree
(a,b)
. The fiber over such a point is isomorphic to the projectivization of
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{I}_{C}(d))
. The latter group is the kernel of the restriction map
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n+1}}(d))\twoheadrightarrow H^{0}(Q,\mathcal{O}_{Q}(d))\twoheadrightarrow H^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))
, so
\pi_{2}
has relative dimension equal to
h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{O}(d))-h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}(d))-1
. On the other hand, the image of
\pi_{2}
has dimension at most

\displaystyle\dim[(\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a,b)|)/\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})]=4(n-2)+9+h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a,b))-1.

It follows that we have

\displaystyle\dim\Phi_{a,b}/\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})

\displaystyle\leq 4(n-2)+9+h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a,b))-1+h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{O}(d))-h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}(d))-1

\displaystyle=N_{d}+4n+h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a,b))-h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))

Since
h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))=h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(d,d))-h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(d-a,d-b))
, we see that
\dim\Phi_{a,b}/\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1})<N_{d}
if

h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(d,d))-h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(d-a,d-b))-h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a,b))-4n>0.

This simplifies to checking that
(a+b)d-2ab-1-4n>0
, which is true when
2\leq a\leq b\leq d-2
,
d\geq 2n
, and
n\geq 3
.

Let us now consider the case where
Q
is a singular quadric cone (spanning
\mathbb{P}^{3}
). The blow-up of
Q
at the singular point is a Hirzebruch surface
\pi\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
which satisfies
\NS(\mathbb{F}_{2})=\mathbb{Z}\langle F,E\rangle
, where
F
is the class of a fiber of
\pi
and
E
is the unique section with negative self-intersection. These satisfy the relations
F^{2}=0
,
F\cdot E=1
, and
E^{2}=-2
. The morphism
\mathbb{F}_{2}\rightarrow Q\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
is then given by the linear series
|E+2F|
. We will now recall some facts:

(
i
)

The nef cone of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is given by
\Nef(\mathbb{F}_{2})=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot F+\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cdot(E+2F)
.

(
ii
)

If
C\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}
is an integral curve, then either
C=E
or
C\in\Nef(\mathbb{F}_{n})
.

(
iii
)

If
aE+bF
is a line bundle on
\mathbb{F}_{2}
with
b\geq 2a\geq 0
, then
E+2F=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(1)
under the identification
\mathbb{F}_{2}\cong\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(2))
so we can compute global sections of
aE+bF
by pushing forward to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
:

\displaystyle h^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{2},aE+bF)

\displaystyle=h^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{2},a(E+2F)+(b-2a)F)

\displaystyle=h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\text{Sym}^{a}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(2))\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(b-2a))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{a}h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(b-2a+2k))

\displaystyle=(a+1)(b-a+1),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{when }b\geq 2a.

On the other hand, if
0\leq b\leq 2a
and
a\geq 0
, then this sum actually only depends on
b
:

\displaystyle h^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{2},aE+bF)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=\lceil\frac{2a-b}{2}\rceil}^{a}h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{1},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(b-2a+2k))

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1+3+\cdots+(b+1)&\text{if $b$ is even,}\\
2+4+\cdots+(b+1)&\text{if $b$ is odd;}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{(b+2)^{2}}{4}&\text{if $b$ is even,}\\
\frac{(b+1)(b+3)}{4}&\text{if $b$ is odd}.\end{cases}

This makes sense in light of the fact that
h^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{2},(a-1)E+bF)=h^{0}(\mathbb{F}_{2},aE+bF)
when
b<2a
.

Now following the same strategy as before, consider the incidence:

\Psi_{a,b}\coloneqq\{(X_{d},f\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n+1},C)\in\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}\times\mathcal{V}\times|\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(aE+bF)|\colon f(C)\subset X_{d}\}.

Here,
\mathcal{V}
is the space of maps that factor through a singular quadric
Q\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
, and note that the space of singular quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
has dimension 8. As before, it suffices to show that
\dim\Psi_{a,b}/\Aut(\mathbb{F}_{2})<N_{d}
. A nearly identical computation as the one above reduces to checking that

(1)

h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(dE+2dF))-h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(aE+bF))-h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}((d-a)E+(2d-b)F))-4n-1>0

when
2(d-2)\geq b\geq 2a
and
a\geq 2
. Our assumptions on
a
and
b
follow from the description of the effective classes in (ii) together with the fact that we are only interested in breaking off integral non-rational curves
C
. Using (iii), we can write

\displaystyle h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(dE+2dF))-h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(aE+bF))-h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}((d-a)E+(2d-b)F))

\displaystyle=(d+1)^{2}-(a+1)(b-a+1)-h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}((d-a)E+(2d-b)F))

\displaystyle\geq(d+1)^{2}-(a+1)(b-a+1)-(2d-b+2)^{2}/4

\displaystyle\geq(d+1)^{2}-(\frac{b+2}{2})^{2}-(\frac{2d-b+2}{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(d+1)^{2}-3^{2}-(d-1)^{2}

\displaystyle=4d-9,

which implies (
1
) since
d\geq 2n\geq 6
.
∎

An ingredient that we will frequently use later is Castelnuovo’s bound for the arithmetic genus of a non-degenerate curve in projective space:

Proposition 1.4

(Castelnuovo)
.

Fix
r\geq 3
and let
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
be a non-degenerate integral curve of degree
\delta
. If we set
M\coloneqq\lfloor\frac{\delta-1}{r-1}\rfloor
, then

p_{a}(C)\leq M\cdot\left(\delta-\frac{M+1}{2}(r-1)-1\right).

Setting the right-hand side equal to
f(M)
, it is straightforward to check that the function
f(m)
is maximized when
m=\frac{\delta-1}{r-1}-\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, for any
r\geq 3
we have

(2)

p_{a}(C)\leq f\left(\frac{\delta-1}{r-1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2(r-1)}\delta^{2}-\frac{(r+1)}{2(r-1)}\delta+\frac{(r+1)^{2}}{8(r-1)}.

When
r
is even, the expression
\frac{\delta-1}{r-1}-\frac{1}{2}
can never be an integer so there is a slight improvement:
f(m)
is maximized when
m=\frac{\delta-1}{r-1}-\frac{r}{2(r-1)}
(or equivalently when
m=\frac{\delta-1}{r-1}-\frac{r-2}{2(r-1)}
). For future reference, we will write out this inequality for
r=4
:

(3)

p_{a}(C)\leq f\left(\frac{\delta-1}{r-1}-\frac{r}{2(r-1)}\right)\Big|_{r=4}=\frac{1}{6}\delta^{2}-\frac{5}{6}\delta+1.

For
r=3
, one can show that

(4)

p_{a}(C)\leq\frac{1}{4}\delta^{2}-\delta+1.

We will also need a number of refinements of Castelnuovo’s theorem. For instance, Halphen
[
Hal21
]
proved that a non-degenerate curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
of degree
\delta
which lies on a cubic surface but not a quadric surface satisfies

(5)

\displaystyle p_{a}(C)\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{6}\delta^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\delta+1&\text{ if }\delta\equiv 0\pmod{3}\\
\frac{1}{6}\delta^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\delta+\frac{1}{3}&\text{ if }\delta\not\equiv 0\pmod{3}\\
\end{cases}

More generally, Gruson–Peskine
[
GP78
]
showed that if
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
is a non-degenerate curve of degree
\delta>s(s-1)
which is not contained in a surface of degree
<s
, then
p_{a}(C)\leq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}\left(s+\frac{\delta}{s}-4\right)
. (In fact, they prove a slightly stronger bound, but the above weaker inequality is simpler to state and suffice for our purposes). Evaluating this with
s=3
and
4
, we see that if
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
is a non-degenerate curve not contained in a surface of degree
\leq 3
, then

(6)

\displaystyle p_{a}(C)\leq\begin{cases}\lfloor\frac{1}{8}\delta^{2}\rfloor+1&\text{ if }\delta>12;\\
\lfloor\frac{1}{6}\delta^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\delta\rfloor+1&\text{ if }12\geq\delta>6.\end{cases}

Finally, the main theorem of Harris
[
Har80
]
says that if
C
is contained in an irreducible surface
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
of degree
k
, then

(7)

p_{a}(C)\leq\begin{cases}\pi(\delta,k)=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2k}+\frac{1}{2}(k-4)\delta+1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(k-\varepsilon-1+\frac{\varepsilon}{k})&\text{if }\delta>k(k-1),\\
\pi(\delta,\left\lfloor\frac{\delta-1}{k}\right\rfloor+1)&\text{if }\delta\leq k(k-1);\end{cases}

where
0\leq\varepsilon\leq k-1
is such that
\delta+\epsilon
is a multiple of
k
. Note that
-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}(k-\varepsilon-1+\frac{\varepsilon}{k})\leq 0
, so

\pi(\delta,k)\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2k}+\frac{1}{2}(k-4)\delta+1\eqqcolon R(\delta,k).

2.
Incidence correspondences

In this section, we will set up a number of incidence correspondences and use these to deduce various lower bounds for the arithmetic genus of a low degree curve on a general hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
. A key ingredient in this estimate is a theorem of Gruson–Lazarsfeld–Peskine
[
GLP83
]
, which says that hypersurfaces of degree
d
trace out a complete linear system on the curves we are interested in:

Lemma 2.1
.

Fix a positive integer
d
and let
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
be an integral curve of degree
\delta\leq d+2
. Suppose that
\Span(C)\cong\mathbb{P}^{r}
for some positive integer
r\geq 3
. Then

h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{I}_{C}(d))=h^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{O}(d))-(d\delta+1-p_{a}(C)).

Proof.

By assumption,
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
is non-degenerate and
\deg C=\delta
. Since
d\geq\delta+1-r
(since
r\geq 3
), by the main theorem of
[
GLP83
]
we get a surjection
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathcal{O}(d))\twoheadrightarrow H^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))
. By combining this with the obvious surjection
H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{O}(d))\twoheadrightarrow H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathcal{O}(d))
, we have an exact sequence

(8)

0\rightarrow H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{I}_{C}(d))\rightarrow H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},\mathcal{O}(d))\rightarrow H^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))\rightarrow 0.

Thus, we reduce our problem to computing the quantity
h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))
. Before doing so, it will be useful to introduce some additional terminology. A coherent sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
\mathbb{P}^{r}
is said to be
n
-regular
if
H^{i}(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathcal{F}(n-i))=0
for
i>0
. By Mumford’s theorem, this is equivalent to
\mathcal{F}
being
(n+k)
-regular for all
k\geq 0
. We will say that a curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
is
n
-regular if its ideal sheaf
\mathcal{I}_{C}
is.

Recall from
[
GLP83
, Theorem 1.1]
that a non-degenerate curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
of degree
\delta
is
(\delta+2-r)
-regular. Since
r\geq 3
, by our assumption we see that
H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathcal{I}_{C}(d))=0=H^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathcal{I}_{C}(d))
for
d\geq\delta-2
. Let us apply this to the long exact sequence on cohomology associated to the ideal sheaf sequence for
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
:

0=H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathcal{I}_{C}(d))\rightarrow H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathcal{O}(d))\rightarrow H^{1}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))\rightarrow H^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathcal{I}_{C}(d))=0.

This implies that
H^{1}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))\cong H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathcal{O}(d))=0
, and Riemann–Roch gives

(9)

h^{0}(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))=\chi(C,\mathcal{O}_{C}(d))=d\delta+1-p_{a}(C).

Putting together (
8
) and (
9
) gives the desired result.
∎

We will use the previous lemma to give a lower bound on the arithmetic genus of a low degree curve on a general hypersurface.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
be a general hypersurface of degree
d
and let
C\subset X
be an integral curve of degree
\delta\leq d+2
. Let
f\colon C^{\prime}\rightarrow C
be the normalization and suppose that
\dim\Span(C)\leq r
. Then

(10)

p_{a}(C)\geq d\delta-(r+1)(n+1-r+h^{0}(f^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{C}(1)))-4p_{g}(C)+5

Proof.

Let
g^{\prime}
be the geometric genus of
C
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{g^{\prime}}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},d)
be the stack of degree
d
maps from a (smooth) curve of genus
g^{\prime}
to
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Fix a substack
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{g^{\prime}}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},d)
whose general member is a generically injective map
i:C^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r}
such that the image curve
C=i(C^{\prime})
has arithmetic genus
g
. We begin with the following:

Claim.
The dimension of
\mathcal{M}
is bounded from above by

4g^{\prime}-4+(r+1)\cdot h^{0}(i^{\ast}\mathcal{O}(1)).

To see this, note that the dimension of
\mathcal{M}
is bounded from above by the dimension of the tangent space to
\mathcal{M}_{g^{\prime}}(\mathbb{P}^{n+1},d)
at
[i]\in\mathcal{H}
, which is bounded from above by
h^{0}(i^{\ast}T_{\mathbb{P}^{r}})+3g^{\prime}-3
. By pulling back the Euler sequence to get
0\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{C^{\prime}}\rightarrow i^{\ast}\mathcal{O}(1)^{\oplus r+1}\rightarrow i^{\ast}T_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}\rightarrow 0
, we see that
h^{0}(i^{\ast}T_{\mathbb{P}^{r}})\leq(r+1)\cdot h^{0}(f^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{C}(1))-h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{C^{\prime}})+h^{1}(\mathcal{O}_{C^{\prime}})=(r+1)\cdot h^{0}(f^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{C}(1))+g^{\prime}-1
. Combining these two inequalities proves the claim.

Next, consider the incidence variety

\Psi\coloneqq\left\{(X,f\colon\mathbb{P}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n+1},i\colon C^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r})\in\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}\times\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times\mathcal{M}\mid f(i(C^{\prime}))\subset X\right\}.

There is a natural action of
\PGL_{r+1}=\Aut(\mathbb{P}^{r})
on
\Psi
via
\phi\cdot(X,f,i)\mapsto(X,f\circ\phi^{-1},\phi\circ i)
, which gives
\Psi
the structure of a
\PGL_{r+1}
-bundle. Since we are interested in the orbit space, let us pass to the quotient, which admits the following maps:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Psi/\PGL_{r+1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times\mathcal{M})/\PGL_{r+1}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}

The fact that
X
is general implies that
\Psi/\PGL_{r+1}
must dominate
\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}
under the first projection. Note that the dimension of the fiber of
\pi_{2}
is given by Lemma
2.1
. On the other hand,
{(\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times\mathcal{H})/\PGL_{r+1}}
admits a map to the Grassmannian
\mathbb{G}(r,n+1)
, whose fibers have dimension bounded from above by the Claim. This implies that

\displaystyle N_{d}\leq\dim\Psi/\PGL_{r+1}

\displaystyle\leq N_{d}-(d\delta+1-g)+\dim\mathbb{G}(r,n+1)+4g^{\prime}-4+(r+1)\cdot h^{0}(i^{\ast}\mathcal{O}(1)).

Rearranging the terms and solving for
g
, we arrive at the desired inequality.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

In the proposition above, we did not assume that
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
is non-degenerate. In fact, choosing different values of
r
may give better lower bounds. Setting
r=n+1
gives

(11)

p_{a}(C)\geq d\delta-(n+2)h^{0}(f^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{C}(1))-4p_{g}(C)+5

amounts to studying a simpler incidence involving just pairs
(X,C)
.

Finally, we conclude this section with some incidence arguments involving curves in general hypersurfaces which are also contained in low degree surfaces. We will need to fix some notation. Let
\mathcal{H}_{P}
be a locally closed subscheme of the Hilbert scheme
\mathcal{H}
such that each point of
\mathcal{H}_{P}
corresponds to an integral surface
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
with Hilbert polynomial
P
, and let
\mathcal{H}_{\delta,g}
be the locally closed subscheme of the Hilbert scheme consisting of points corresponding to integral curves
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
of degree
\delta
and arithmetic genus
g
. We will prove:

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
be a (very) general hypersurface of degree
d
and let
C\subset X
be an integral curve of degree
\delta\leq d+2
. Suppose that
\Span(C)\cong\mathbb{P}^{r}
for some
r\geq 3
, and furthermore assume that
C
is contained in a surface
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
belonging to
\mathcal{H}_{P}
. Then

p_{a}(C)\geq d\delta-\dim\mathbb{G}(r,n+1)-\dim\mathcal{H}_{P}-\frac{\delta^{2}}{\deg S}

Proof.

Consider the incidence variety

\Phi_{P,\delta,g,r}^{d}\coloneqq\left\{(X,f\colon\mathbb{P}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n+1},S,C)\in\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}\times\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times\mathcal{H}_{P}\times\mathcal{H}_{\delta,g}\middle|f(C)\subset f(S)\cap X\right\}.

Note that
\PGL_{r+1}
acts on the last three components in a natural way. Passing to the quotient, we arrive at the following maps:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Phi_{P,\delta,g,r}^{d}/\PGL_{r+1}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times\mathcal{H}_{P}\times\mathcal{H}_{\delta,g})/\PGL_{r+1}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}

We know that
\Phi_{P,\delta,g,r}^{d}/\PGL_{r+1}
must dominate
\mathbb{P}^{N_{d}}
under the first projection. The dimension of any fiber of
\pi_{2}
is given by Lemma
2.1
. On the other hand, the image of
\pi_{2}
lies in the locus
Z
of triples
(f,S,C)
with
C\subseteq S
. Let us bound the dimension of
Z
.

The variety
(\Lin(\mathbb{P}^{r},\mathbb{P}^{n+1})\times\mathcal{H}_{P})/\PGL_{r+1}
has dimension equal to
\dim\mathbb{G}(r,n+1)+\dim\mathcal{H}_{P}
, so it suffices to bound the dimension of the fiber of
Z
above any fixed pair
(f,S).
This fiber can be identified with the closed subscheme
\mathcal{H}_{\delta,g}^{S}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\delta,g}
parametrizing those
C
that lie inside
S
. If we take a minimal resolution
\nu\colon S^{\prime}\rightarrow S
, there will be a dense open subscheme
U\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\delta,g}^{S}
such that the universal family of curves
C_{U}\subseteq S\times U
lifts to a universal family of curves
C^{\prime}_{U}\subseteq S^{\prime}\times U
in a way so that for any point
p\in U
, the fiber
C^{\prime}_{p}
of
C^{\prime}_{U}
over
p
is the strict transform of the fiber
C_{p}
of
C_{U}
over
p
. The dimension of
U
(equivalently, of
\mathcal{H}_{\delta,g}^{S}
) at
p
is bounded from above by the dimension of the tangent space to the corresponding Hilbert scheme of
S^{\prime}
, which is given by
h^{0}(N_{C^{\prime}_{p}/S^{\prime}})=h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{C^{\prime}_{p}}(C^{\prime}_{p}))
. Let us abuse notation and suppress the dependence on
p
, i.e., we will write
C^{\prime}
for
C^{\prime}_{p}.

To bound
h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{C^{\prime}}(C^{\prime}))
, we note the following Lemma: If
C^{\prime}
is an integral Gorenstein curve and
D
is an effective divisor on
C^{\prime}
, then
h^{0}(C^{\prime},D)\leq\deg D+1
. The line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{C^{\prime}}(C^{\prime})
has degree equal to
(C^{\prime})^{2}
which, by the Hodge Index theorem on
S^{\prime}
, satisfies
(C^{\prime})^{2}\leq\frac{(C^{\prime}\cdot\nu^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{S}(1))^{2}}{\mathcal{O}_{S}(1)^{2}}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{\deg S}
. Plugging this into the lemma, we find that

h^{0}(N_{C^{\prime}/S^{\prime}})\leq\max\left\{0,\frac{\delta^{2}}{\deg S}+1\right\}\leq 1+\frac{\delta^{2}}{\deg S}.

Combining all of the above ingredients, we see that

\displaystyle N_{d}

\displaystyle\leq\dim\Phi_{P,\delta,g,r}^{d}/\PGL_{r+1}

\displaystyle\leq N_{d}-(d\delta+1-p_{a}(C))+\dim\mathbb{G}(r,n+1)+\dim\mathcal{H}_{P}+1+\frac{\delta^{2}}{\deg S}

Rearranging the terms and solving for
p_{a}(C)
gives the desired bound.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

For the convenience of the reader, let us recall the statement of the theorem:

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}_{\mathbb{C}}
be a general hypersurface of degree
d\geq 2n
and let
C\subset X
be a curve of degree
\delta
. If
\delta\leq d+2
, then
\delta=d
and
C=X\cap\Lambda
for some 2-plane
\Lambda
.

The case where
n=1
is trivial, the case where
n=2
follows from the Noether–Lefschetz theorem, and the case where
n=3
was proved by Wu
[
Wu90
]
. Thus, we may assume that
n\geq 4
. The overarching goal will be to use the ingredients from §2 to derive a contradiction with the Castelnuovo-type bounds in §1. A fact that we will use at some point is the following: a non-degenerate curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{r}
always satisfies
\deg C\geq r
, and equality holds iff
C
is a rational normal curve. The next case where
\deg C=r+1
can hold only if
p_{g}(C)\leq 1
. Since
X
does not contain any rational curves
[
Voi96
]
, from the condition
r\geq 3
we know that
\delta\geq 4
. Let
f\colon C^{\prime}\rightarrow C
denote the normalization. Assume for contradiction that
\dim\Span(C)\geq 3
and
C
is not contained in such a surface
S
.

Step 1
: Let us show that
p_{g}(C)\geq 4
.

First assume that
(n,d)\neq(4,8)
. Since
\delta\geq 4
, from
[
CR22
, Theorem 6.1]
and the fact that
X
does not contain any lines it follows that
p_{g}(C)\geq 3
. (It is not strictly necessary to use
[
CR22
]
, but doing so simplifies the argument.) We can also rule out
\delta=4,5
because otherwise

implies (by the Castelnuovo theorem for

) that
p_{g}(C)\leq 2
, which contradicts the previous sentence. Thus, we may assume that
\delta\geq 6
, and the same result
[
CR22
, Theorem 6.1]
shows that
p_{g}(C)\geq 4
.

Now let us analyze the case
(n,d)=(4,8)
separately. We claim that a very general such
X
does not contain any curve
C
of degree
\delta\leq d+2\leq 10
and geometric genus

. Suppose for contradiction that

contained such a curve. Applying Proposition

with

and using the fact that

(because

\displaystyle p_{a}(C)

\displaystyle\geq d\delta-(r+1)(n+1-r+h^{0}(f^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{C}(1)))-4p_{g}(C)+5

\displaystyle\geq(7-r)\delta-(r+1)(5-r)-4p_{g}(C)+5.

For

2

.

Thus, from now on we may assume that

.

Step 2
: We claim that

. The cases where

p_{a}(C)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{1}{8}(\delta^{2}-6\delta+9)\right\rfloor.

Since

, we may assume that

.

In this step as well as the next ones, we will need to bound

. Riemann–Roch implies that

, and if

is special then Clifford’s theorem says

.

\displaystyle=(d-n-2)\delta+(n-2)(p_{g}(C)-1)+1

\displaystyle\geq(d-n-2)\delta+7

The second to last inequality uses the fact that

: We claim that

\displaystyle=(d-5)\delta-5n+p_{g}(C)-5h^{1}(f^{*}\mathcal{O}_{C}(1))+15

\displaystyle\geq(d-5)\delta-5n+19

\displaystyle\geq(d-5)(\delta-5/2)-25/2+19

\displaystyle\geq(\delta-7)(\delta-5/2)-25/2+19

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}-\frac{19}{2}\delta+24,

We will first show that
\delta\geq 8
. Note that
\delta\geq 7
, because otherwise
H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(3))=20
so we would be able to find a cubic surface
S
meeting
C
in at least 19 points. By Bézout’s theorem,
C
is contained in
S
, which is a contradiction. Next, we will need a result of Gruson-Peskine
[
GP78
, Théorème de Spécialité]
, which says that if
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
is an integral curve of degree
\delta
which is not contained in a surface of degree
<s
, then for any integer
n\geq s+\frac{\delta}{s}-4
, the line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{C}(n)
is special if and only if
n=s+\frac{\delta}{s}-4
and
C
is a complete intersection curve of type
(s,\frac{\delta}{s})
. Consider the two situations below:

(1)

Suppose
\delta\leq 12
. Apply the theorem above with
n=3
,
s=3
. Since
C
is not contained in surface of degree
\leq 3
, in particular it cannot be a complete intersection curve of type
(s,\frac{\delta}{s})
and so
\mathcal{O}_{C}(3)
is nonspecial.

(2)

Suppose
\delta\leq 15
. Apply the theorem above with
n=4
,
s=3
. Since
C
is not a complete intersection curve of type
(s,\frac{\delta}{s})
, as before we see that
\mathcal{O}_{C}(4)
is nonspecial (note that for
\delta=11,12
this follows from the previous bullet point).

Both of these observations will be used later. For now, we note that Riemann–Roch and (i) imply that if
\delta=7
, then

h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{C}(3))=3\delta+1-p_{a}(C)=22-p_{a}(C)<20=h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(3)).

The middle inequality follows from Step 1, which showed that
p_{a}(C)\geq p_{g}(C)\geq 4
. But this contradicts the fact that
C
is not contained in a cubic surface. So
\delta\geq 8
as claimed.

Case 5(a)
: Suppose
\delta\geq 8
,
p_{g}(C)\geq 4
, and
h^{1}(f^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{C}(1))=0
. Applying Proposition
2.2
with
r=3
and using Riemann–Roch (along the lines of Case 2a) gives

\displaystyle p_{a}(C)

\displaystyle\geq d\delta-4(n-2+\delta+1-p_{g}(C))-4p_{g}(C)+5

\displaystyle=(d-4)\delta-4n+9

\displaystyle\geq(\delta-6)(\delta-2)+1

where the last line uses the fact that
d\geq 2n
. This contradicts (
6
) (assuming
C
is not contained in a surface
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
of degree
\leq 3
) when
\delta\geq 8
.

Case 5(b)
: Suppose
\delta\geq 8
,
p_{g}(C)\geq 4
, and
h^{1}(f^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{C}(1))\not=0
. Then applying Proposition
2.2
with
r=3
and using Clifford’s theorem to bound
h^{0}(f^{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{C}(1))\leq\lfloor\frac{\delta}{2}\rfloor+1
(similar to Case 2b) gives

\displaystyle p_{a}(C)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{5}\left[d\delta-4(n-2+\lfloor\frac{\delta}{2}\rfloor+1)+5\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{5}\left[d(\delta-2)-4\lfloor\frac{\delta}{2}\rfloor+9\right].

Plugging in
d\geq\delta-2
and comparing this with (
6
), we arrive at a contradiction when
\delta\geq 15
. We will finish the cases where
\dim\Span(C)=3
and
8\leq\delta\leq 14
by hand.

When
8\leq\delta\leq 9
and
\delta\leq d+2
, one can apply (i) plus the arithmetic genus bound above to Riemann–Roch, which gives
h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{C}(3))=3\delta+1-p_{a}(C)<20=h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(3))
. This contradicts the fact that
C
is not contained in a cubic surface. When
10\leq\delta\leq 14
, the same strategy using (ii) implies that
h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{C}(4))<35=h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{3}}(4))
. Therefore,
C
is contained in a quartic surface, which contradicts Step 4. This completes the proof. ∎

4.
Subvarieties of higher degree

In this section, we would like to prove a number of results which extend beyond curves of degree
\leq d+2
. We begin with the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

Fix any
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Then there exists a positive integer
d_{0}=d_{0}(s,n)
such that if
d\geq d_{0}
and
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
is a general hypersurface of degree
d
and dimension
n\geq 3
, then any integral curve
C\subset X
of degree
\delta\leq sd
is equal to a generically transverse intersection
X\cap S
for a unique surface
S
of degree
\leq s
. Furthermore, one can choose
d_{0}(1,n)=2n
and

d_{0}(s,n)\coloneqq\max\left\{(s+1)(s+3n-1),\ \frac{2(s+1)}{(n-1)(s-1)}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\sqrt[n-i]{n!(s+1)}\right\}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }s\geq 2.

Proof.

We will prove this by induction on
s
. The base case
s=1
follows from Theorem
1.1
. Let us assume that the theorem holds for all
s\leq k-1
(for some positive integer
k
). We would like to prove the theorem for
s=k
. Let
C\subset X
be a curve of degree
\delta\leq kd
contained in a very general hypersurface of degree
d
. By the inductive hypothesis, we may assume that
(k-1)d+1\leq\delta\leq kd
.

Claim 1.
If
d\geq d_{0}(k,n)
, then
C
is contained in a surface
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
\leq k
.

To see this, suppose for contradiction that
C
is not contained in such a surface. The assumption on
d
and
\delta\geq(k-1)d+1
implies that

\delta>\frac{2(k+1)}{n-1}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\sqrt[n-i]{n!(k+1)}.

Given this lower bound on
\delta
, the main result of
[
CCDG93
]
is the upper bound

(13)

p_{a}(C)\leq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}(w+m-2)-\frac{m+1}{2}(w-\epsilon)+(w+1)\frac{vm}{2}+\rho,

where
m,\epsilon,w,v,
and
\rho
are defined in loc.cit. (Note that our conventions differ from that of
[
CCDG93
]
; since our assumption involves
\leq k
, in their definitions of
m,\epsilon,w
, and
v
we must replace
s
with
k+1
everywhere.)

We will weaken (
13
) to a more convenient bound which does not require the definition of any new variables. Using the naive inequalities

m\leq\frac{\delta-1}{k+1},\hskip 10.00002ptw\leq\frac{k}{n-1}\leq k,\hskip 10.00002ptv\leq n-2,\hskip 10.00002pt\epsilon\leq k,\hskip 10.00002pt\rho\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\leq\frac{k}{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\ w-\epsilon\geq-\epsilon\geq-k,

(
13
) implies that

\displaystyle p_{a}(C)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}(w+m-2)+\frac{m+1}{2}\cdot k+(w+1)\frac{vm}{2}+\rho

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\delta}{2}(k+\frac{\delta-1}{k+1}-2)+\frac{k(\delta+k)}{2(k+1)}+\frac{(n-2)(\delta-1)}{2}+\frac{k}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2(k+1)}+(\frac{k}{2}+\frac{k-1}{2(k+1)}-1+\frac{n-2}{2})\delta+\frac{k^{2}}{2(k+1)}-\frac{(n-2)}{2}+\frac{k}{2}+1

(14)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2(k+1)}+\frac{k+n-3}{2}\cdot\delta+\frac{2k-n+4}{2}.

Strictly speaking, the main result of
[
CCDG93
]
assumes that
C
is a non-degenerate curve in
\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
. However, if
C
spans a lower-dimensional linear subspace
\Lambda
, we claim that the bound still applies. The reader can verify that the assumption on
\delta
is even easier to satisfy (the lower bound on
\delta
decreases as
n
decreases),
C\subset\Lambda
is still not contained in a surface of degree
\leq k
, and the bound on the arithmetic genus in (
4
) becomes stronger as
n
decreases. For this last part, observe that the right hand side is a linear function in
n
with slope
\frac{1}{2}(\delta-1)\geq 0
.

On the other hand, Voisin
[
Voi96
]
(cf.
[
Che24
, Proposition 3.1]
) has shown that on a very general hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
d\geq 2n
, the geometric genus of any integral curve
C\subset X
is bounded from below by

p_{g}(C)\geq 1+\frac{1}{2}(d-2n-1)\delta.

We can now use the inequality
p_{g}(C)\leq p_{a}(C)
to see that:

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{2}(d-2n-1)\delta

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2(k+1)}+\frac{k+n-3}{2}\cdot\delta+\frac{2k-n+4}{2}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{2(k+1)}+\frac{k+3n-2-d}{2}\cdot\delta+\frac{2k-n+2}{2}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{k+1}+k+3n-2-d+\frac{2k-n+2}{\delta}.

We have the simple bound

\frac{2k-n+2}{\delta}<\frac{2k}{\delta}<\frac{2k}{(k-1)d}\leq\frac{4}{d}<1,

so we get

d<\frac{\delta}{k+1}+k+3n-1,

or

(k+1)d-\delta<(k+1)(k+3n-1).

Because
\delta\leq kd
, we have
(k+1)d-\delta\geq d
so
d<(k+1)(k+3n-1)
, which contradicts our assumption
d\geq d_{0}(k,n)
.

From Theorem
A
, we see that
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
does not contain any surfaces of degree
\leq k
since
d\geq 2n
and
d\geq k+1
. Since
C
is contained in a surface of degree
\leq k
, it suffices to show:

Claim 2.
If
d\geq d_{0}(k,n)
, then the intersection of
X
with
any
integral surface
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
\leq k
is integral.

Suppose for contradiction that there exists an integral surface
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
\leq k
such that
S\cap X
is not integral. Then we may write the
1
-cycle
S\cap X
as a nontrivial sum
\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}
of a finite number of irreducible curves. Note that some of the
C_{i}
may be the same if some irreducible component of the intersection is nonreduced. The condition
m\geq 2
and the fact that
d\geq 2n
guarantee that for each
i
, we have
d\leq\deg C_{i}\leq(k-1)d
. The inductive hypothesis then implies that each component
C_{i}
is equal to a generically transverse intersection
T_{i}\cap X
, for some surfaces
T_{i}
of degree
a_{i}
with
\sum a_{i}=\deg S
. By degree reasons,
T_{i}\not=S
for all
i
. But then, for any
i
, we have

C_{i}\subset S\cap T_{i},

which implies that

d\cdot a_{i}\leq\deg S\cap T_{i}\leq\deg S\cdot a_{i}\leq k\cdot a_{i}<d\cdot a_{i},

a contradiction.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The last term in the expression for
d_{0}(s,n)
in Theorem
4.1
grows approximately like
C\cdot s^{1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n-1}}
, where
C=C(n)
is a constant depending only on
n
. For
n\leq 4
, this implies that
d_{0}(s,n)
grows quadratically in
s
. The proof below will show that the same constants that appear above for
d_{0}(s,n)
can be used in the statement of Theorem
B
.

Next, we will give:

Proof of Theorem
B
.

Choose
d_{0}
as in Theorem
4.1
or Remark
4.2
.

First, we note that the uniqueness of
V
is automatic. Indeed, if there were two subvarieties
V
and
V^{\prime}
of degrees
\frac{\delta}{d}
with
Y=X\cap V=X\cap V^{\prime},
then
Y
would be a connected component of
V\cap V^{\prime}.
But then we would have
\deg V\cdot\deg V^{\prime}=\frac{\delta^{2}}{d^{2}}\geq\deg Y=\delta,
so we would have
\delta\geq d^{2}
and
s\geq d
, which is impossible for our choice of
d_{0}
.

Now we will induct on
\dim Y
. The base case follows from Theorem
4.1
. Let
Y\subset X
be a subvariety of dimension
\geq 2
. Consider a general pencil of hyperplanes
\{H_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{P}^{1}}
, each of which meets
Y
transversally. Applying the inductive hypothesis to the general slice
Y\cap H_{t}
(which is integral by Bertini’s theorem), there is a unique subvariety
V_{t}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
of degree
e\leq s
with the property that
Y\cap H_{t}=X\cap V_{t}
. By uniqueness, the
V_{t}
belong to a family parametrized by
t\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. We claim that the
\{V_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{P}^{1}}
sweep out the desired
V
with the property that
Y=X\cap V
.

Fix any
t\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
. It suffices to show that
V_{t}=V\cap H_{t}
. For this, it suffices to show that for any other point
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
, the intersection
V_{t^{\prime}}\cap H_{t}
is independent of
t^{\prime}
. Let
\Delta
be the base locus
\cap H_{t}
. The intersection
V_{t^{\prime}}\cap H_{t}
is a degree
e
subvariety of
\Delta
with the property that
(V_{t^{\prime}}\cap H_{t})\cap X=H_{t}\cap(V_{t}^{\prime}\cap X)=H_{t}\cap(H_{t^{\prime}}\cap Y)=\Delta\cap Y
. As previously discussed, this property uniquely determines
V_{t^{\prime}}\cap H_{t}
, showing that it is independent of
t^{\prime}
.
∎

We conjecture that the following extension of Theorem
1.1
should hold:

Conjecture 4.3
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n+1}
be a general hypersurface of degree
d
and dimension
n\geq 3
such that
\frac{3}{2}n+2\leq d\leq 2n-1
. Then any curve
C\subset X
of degree
\delta\leq d
must be contained in a 2-plane.

By a variation of the argument in Proposition
1.2
together with the main result of
[
RY20
]
, this would show that
C
must be one of the following curves: a line, an irreducible plane curve of degree
d-1
(that is residual to a line), or an irreducible plane curve of degree
d
.
