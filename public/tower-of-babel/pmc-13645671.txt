Gasdermin D-mediated delivery of caspase inhibitors to suppress pyroptosis
Authors: Katarzyna M Groborz, Melissa E Truong, Irma Stowe, Katherine E Wickliffe, Bettina Lee, Stefan Bauernfried, Robert S Jones, Emile Plise, Elizabeth S Levy, Ponien Kou, Wyne P Lee, Juan Zhang, Hanna Budayeva, Christopher M Rose, Julia Nguyen, Malgorzata Kalinka, Marcin Drag, Nobuhiko Kayagaki, Kim Newton, Marcin Poreba, Vishva M Dixit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaspase-1, -4, -5 and -11 activate gasdermin D (GSDMD) pores, causing pyroptotic cell death and the release of the interleukins IL-1β and IL-18 (ref. 1). Blocking this pathway holds therapeutic promise for the treatment of inflammatory disorders, but cell-permeable caspase inhibitors have not proved successful in clinical trials2. Here we describe covalent caspase inhibitors that selectively block pyroptosis and IL-1β secretion despite being excluded from healthy cells. These inhibitors did not prevent caspase-driven apoptosis, implying that GSDMD pores facilitated their uptake. Membrane-impermeable dyes entered the cells rescued from pyroptosis, consistent with transient membrane permeabilization by GSDMD pores. Caspase inhibition prevented rather than delayed cell death, consistent with membrane repair mechanisms neutralizing the initial GSDMD pores. Inhibiting caspase-1 and caspase-11 suppressed IL-1β and IL-18 production in a mouse model of endotoxic shock, underscoring the therapeutic potential of exploiting GSDMD pores for targeted caspase inhibition in inflammatory diseases.Subject terms: Proteases, Innate immunity

Main

Cell death in multicellular organisms has pivotal roles in development, homeostasis and defence against pathogens1. The programmed cell death pathways of apoptosis and pyroptosis are mediated by aspartate-specific cysteine proteases called caspases. The proinflammatory caspases (human caspase-1, -4 and -5, and mouse caspase-1 and -11) are activated within inflammasome complexes and cleave the pore-forming protein GSDMD to unleash pyroptosis, a lytic form of cell death. Distinct caspases, including caspase-8 and caspase-9, initiate apoptosis by cleaving and activating caspase-3 and caspase-7. In excess, both apoptosis and pyroptosis have been shown to cause inflammatory disorders in mice1. The potential therapeutic benefit of caspase inhibition in human diseases that are characterized by uncontrolled cell death remains uncertain. Clinical trials of cell-permeable pan-caspase inhibitors have failed owing to toxicity or lack of efficacy2. One potential liability of pan-caspase inhibition is that inactivation of caspase-8 sensitizes certain cell types to a lytic form of cell death termed necroptosis1. The pursuit of caspase-selective inhibitors has been challenging because of pronounced structural homologies among the caspases3. Extracellular nanobodies targeting GSDMD can, under certain conditions, reduce pyroptotic cell death, possibly traversing early GSDMD pores and then limiting subsequent pore formation4,5. These findings prompted us to test whether typically membrane-impermeable caspase inhibitors with selectivity for caspase-1, -4, -5 and -11 might also enter cells through GSDMD pores and prevent pyroptosis. If these caspase inhibitors only enter cells that have engaged the pyroptosis pathway, they might be tolerated better than cell-permeable pan-caspase inhibitors.

Substrate specificity of caspase-1, -4 and -5

As a first step towards generating libraries of potentially selective caspase inhibitors, we screened the substrate preferences of human caspase-1, -4 and -5 using hybrid combinatorial substrate libraries (HyCoSuLs) incorporating diverse tetrapeptides with Asp in the P1 position (Extended Data Fig. 1). HyCoSuL sublibraries P2, P3 and P4 were composed of tetrapeptides with an equimolar mixture of natural amino acids in two positions (P2 and P3) and a fixed natural or unnatural amino acid in the remaining space5 (P4). Human caspase-1, -4 and -5 resembled mouse caspase-1 and -11 (ref. 6) by favouring His in the P2 position (Extended Data Fig. 1). By contrast, human caspase-3 prefers small, aliphatic P2 residues such as Val and Ile7. All five caspases preferred Glu in the P3 position, but caspase-11 better tolerated bulky residues such as Bip (3-biphenyl-4-yl-l-alanine), Bpa (4-benzoyl-l-phenylalanine) and Tyr(2,6-Cl2-benzyl)5 (Extended Data Fig. 1). Caspase-11 also accommodated small, aliphatic P4 residues (Tle, Val and Ile), whereas caspase-1, -4 and -5 favoured bulky P4 residues (Trp and Tyr). Thus, human caspase-4 and mouse caspase-11 have distinct amino acid preferences in their substrate-binding regions, despite being the core component of the non-canonical inflammasome that responds to cytoplasmic lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in both species8,9.

Given that human caspase-1, -4 and -5 have similar cleavage preferences, we tested their ability to cleave a set of tetrapeptide caspase substrates described previously6. A tetrapeptide bearing the Trp-Val-His-Asp sequence found in human pro-IL-1β was among those most susceptible to cleavage by human caspase-1, whereas hydrolysis of the Phe-Leu-Thr-Asp sequence found in human GSDMD was approximately threefold less efficient (Extended Data Table 1). Interestingly, caspase-1 exhibited catalytic efficiencies (kcat/KM) that were an order of magnitude greater than those of caspase-4, -5 and -11 (ref. 6) (Extended Data Table 1). The incorporation of non-natural amino acids into the tetrapeptide sequences elevated the cleavage efficiencies, while concurrently enhancing the selectivity factor for proinflammatory over proapoptotic caspases.

Screening a caspase inhibitor library

Taking into account the cleavage site preferences of caspase-1, -4, -5 and -11, we synthesized around 100 tetrapeptides bearing an acyloxymethyl ketone (AOMK) reactive group to covalently modify their targets in an irreversible manner10,11. We determined their inhibition parameters on recombinant human caspase-1, -3, -4, -6, -7, -8, -9 and -10 (Supplementary Table 1). Tetrapeptides KGR-3, KGR-23 and KGR-72 (Extended Data Fig. 2a) stood out as potent and somewhat selective inhibitors of human caspase-1 (Supplementary Table 1) and were subsequently found to also inhibit mouse caspase-1 (Extended Data Table 2). The cell permeability of KGR-3, KGR-23 and KGR-72 was deemed very low when assessed in gMDCKI cells lacking the efflux pump MDR1 (ref. 12) (Extended Data Table 3).

To determine whether any of our tetrapeptides might pass through GSDMD pores in the cell membrane to inhibit caspase-1, we tested their ability to block NLRP3- and caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis in LPS-primed human THP-1 monocytic cells treated with nigericin. Cell lysis was monitored by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release. As expected, nigericin-induced cell lysis was inhibited by the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 (refs. 13–15), the pan-caspase inhibitor emricasan (IDN-6556)16, caspase-1 deficiency or GSDMD loss (Extended Data Fig. 2b). Several tetrapeptides, including KGR-3, KGR-23 and KGR-72, greatly reduced or inhibited nigericin-induced cell lysis (Extended Data Fig. 2c).

We also screened a subset of our tetrapeptides for their ability to block caspase-4- or caspase-11-driven pyroptosis, focusing on those with the highest potency against recombinant caspase-1 or that best blocked NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis in THP-1 cells. Rather than electroporate or transfect LPS into cells to activate caspase-4 or caspase-11 (refs. 9,17,18), we engineered THP-1 cells to express either FK506 binding protein 12 (FKBP)–caspase-4 or FKBP–caspase-11 fusions in which FKBP replaced the caspase pro-domain. Cell-permeable B/B ligand was added to dimerize the FKBP fusions, resulting in GSDMD cleavage and pyroptosis that was inhibited by the pan-caspase inhibitor emricasan (Extended Data Fig. 3a–d). Cell death was determined by uptake of the membrane-impermeable dye propidium iodide (PI; Extended Data Fig. 3b), by CellTiter-Glo assay, which measures cellular ATP content (Extended Data Fig. 3c), or by LDH release (Extended Data Fig. 3d). We identified several tetrapeptides that were comparable to emricasan in suppressing cell death induced by caspase-4 and/or caspase-11 (Extended Data Fig. 3d). For example, KGR-3 blocked GSDMD processing and cell death induced by caspase-4 but was less effective against caspase-11 (Extended Data Fig. 3d). Others, including KGR-23, KGR-53 and KGR-72, suppressed cell death induced by caspase-4 or caspase-11. To exclude tetrapeptides that might access cells in a GSDMD-independent manner and be less selective for caspase-1, -4 or -11, we also screened for inhibition of staurosporine-induced apoptosis, which is thought to be dependent on BAX or BAK in the mitochondrial pathway that activates caspase-9 (ref. 19). However, unlike emricasan, none of the selected tetrapeptides suppressed staurosporine-induced cell death (Extended Data Fig. 3e).

Characterization of inhibitor KGR-3

We selected KGR-3, one of the more potent inhibitors of caspase-1 and caspase-4, for further characterization. To determine whether KGR-3 suppresses the release of IL-1β from pyroptotic cells, we primed THP-1 cells expressing FKBP–caspase-4 with the TLR1–TLR2 agonist Pam3CSK4 for 4 h to induce IL1B. The addition of B/B ligand for a further 24 h triggered pyroptosis and IL-1β secretion, whereas B/B ligand plus KGR-3 did not (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 4a). As expected, KGR-3 blocked GSDMD cleavage in the B/B-treated cells (Extended Data Fig. 4b). KGR-3 also suppressed IL-1β release from phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)-differentiated and Pam3CSK4-primed THP-1 cells treated with ATP to engage the NLRP3–ASC–caspase-1 inflammasome20,21 (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 4c), and Ker-CT human keratinocytes treated with Val-boroPro (VBP) to activate the NLRP1–ASC–caspase-1 inflammasome22 (Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 4d,e). Interestingly, VBP-induced IL-1β release from Ker-CT cells was mediated by GSDMD and GSDME (also called DFNA5; Extended Data Fig. 4f). These results indicate that KGR-3 can suppress gasdermin-dependent IL-1β secretion downstream of caspase-1 or caspase-4.

As expected22, VBP was not a potent death stimulus for Ker-CT cells, killing less than 25% of cells after 24 h (Extended Data Fig. 4g). Human endothelial-like EA.hy926 cells were more susceptible to VBP, with cell death being driven by a CARD8–caspase-1 inflammasome (Fig. 1d,e and Extended Data Fig. 4h–j). Although EA.hy926 cells expressed NLRP1, they lacked detectable ASC (Extended Data Fig. 4j). We noted that neither KGR-3 nor GSDMD loss were as effective as caspase-1 deficiency at blocking VBP-induced EA.hy926 cell death (Fig. 1d,e), perhaps reflecting the ability of caspase-1 to also trigger apoptosis23,24. We examined the morphology of VBP-treated EA.hy926 cells and observed caspase-1- and GSDMD-dependent swelling, cell lysis (pyroptosis) and nuclear PI staining (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 4k). However, dying GSDMD-deficient cells exhibited apoptotic blebbing (Fig. 1f) and caspase-3 cleavage (Extended Data Fig. 4l). Blebbing was followed by secondary membrane damage as measured by PI uptake (Extended Data Fig. 4k). Thus, caspase-1 can trigger GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis or GSDMD-independent apoptosis in EA.hy926 cells.

KGR-3 suppressed VBP-induced pyroptosis in EA.hy926 cells (Fig. 1d–f), exhibiting a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of approximately 2.5 μM when monitoring PI uptake (Extended Data Fig. 4m) or LDH release (Extended Data Fig. 4n). Notably, caspase-1-driven apoptotic blebbing and caspase-3 cleavage were resistant to KGR-3 (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 4l). The ability of KGR-3 to prevent caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis, but not caspase-1-dependent apoptosis, strongly suggests that KGR-3, similar to other tetrapeptide caspase inhibitors with poor membrane permeability25, requires gasdermin pores to access caspase-1 in the cytoplasm.

Available evidence suggests that pyroptosis occurs when the assembly of GSDMD pores outpaces their elimination from membranes by the ESCRT (endosomal sorting complexes required for transport) machinery4,26. Inhibition of caspase-1 by KGR-3 must favour membrane repair such that cell death is averted. By contrast, apoptotic cells that do not form GSDMD pores (for reasons that are unclear in wild-type cells) exclude KGR-3 and are not protected. Remarkably, addition of KGR-3 to EA.hy926 and Ker-CT cells several hours after VBP treatment arrested further cell lysis (Fig. 1g) and IL-1β release (Fig. 1h), respectively. Moreover, cell death was prevented rather than just delayed by KGR-3 because EA.hy926 cells continued to grow over the course of 12 days, whereas few cells emerged from the control cultures exposed to VBP alone (Fig. 1i and Extended Data Fig. 5a). Thus, the asynchronous nature of lethal GSDMD pore formation and pyroptosis provides a window for intervention after the initial death stimulus.

We also examined the effect of KGR-3 on the death of EA.hy926 cells expressing FKBP–caspase-4. KGR-3 suppressed GSDMD-dependent cell death, swelling and lysis in the B/B-treated cells (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). Cell death induced by FKBP–caspase-4 was more rapid than that induced by VBP, with most of the cells dying within 6 h (Extended Data Fig. 5c). Consistent with the ESCRT machinery repairing GSDMD-mediated membrane damage in cells treated with KGR-3, dominant-negative versions of ESCRT proteins VPS4A (VPS4A(E228Q))27 and CHMP3 (CHMP3-1–179)28 overcame the protective effects of KGR-3 (Extended Data Fig. 5d,e).

Consistent with KGR-3 having low permeability in apoptotic cells, it did not affect TRAIL-induced caspase-8-dependent extrinsic apoptosis (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b) or intrinsic apoptosis induced by the MCL-1 inhibitor S63845 (Extended Data Fig. 6c–e). Necrosis induced by the pore-forming protein listeriolysin O was also unaffected by KGR-3 (Extended Data Fig. 6f).

Small-molecule uptake via GSDMD pores

To support the notion that inhibitors such as KGR-3 enter living cells through GSDMD pores, we determined whether small amounts of membrane-impermeable, fluorescent small molecules such as PI and YOYO-1 enter the cytoplasm of live cells in a GSDMD-dependent manner. When cultured in 10 μg ml−1 PI (which is 10 times more PI than routinely used to monitor plasma membrane integrity), EA.hy926 cells treated with VBP and KGR-3 had detectable PI in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2a,b). Importantly, VBP-induced PI uptake in the presence of KGR-3 required both caspase-1 and GSDMD. Similar results were obtained with YOYO-1 (Fig. 2c,d). These data suggest that the initial GSDMD pores compromise the integrity of the cell membrane towards small molecules such as PI and YOYO-1, but inhibition of caspase-1 prevents the irrevocable commitment to pyroptosis. Attempts to image KGR-3 uptake directly were unsuccessful. Tagging of KGR-3 with different fluorophores reduced its potency, enhanced its cell permeability, or caused cellular toxicity. Visualization of caspase inhibitors passing through endogenous gasdermin pores is a future goal.

Characterization of inhibitor KGR-53P

Caspase-11-dependent pyroptosis is an important mediator of LPS-induced endotoxic shock in mice8,29; we therefore characterized KGR-53, a more potent inhibitor of caspase-11 than KGR-3 (Extended Data Table 2). KGR-53 suppressed but did not prevent caspase-11-dependent pyroptosis in LPS-transfected mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Given that GSDMD pores may transport negatively charged compounds less efficiently30, we derived KGR-53P (Extended Data Fig. 7b,c), which lacks the negative charge of the P3 Glu. KGR-53P was better than KGR-53 at inhibiting LPS-induced pyroptosis in BMDMs (IC50 approximately 4 μM; Fig. 3a,b), despite having very low cell permeability when assessed in healthy gMDCKI cells (Extended Data Table 3). KGR-53P blocked LPS-induced GSDMD cleavage, as well as caspase-11-dependent secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 (Fig. 3c–e). KGR-53P reduced cell death and IL-18 secretion even when added several hours after LPS transfection (Fig. 3e,f). By contrast, KGR-53P did not protect BMDMs from venetoclax-induced intrinsic apoptosis and thymocytes from Fas ligand (FasL)-induced extrinsic apoptosis (Extended Data Fig. 7d–h).

Next, we determined whether KGR-53P suppressed caspase-1, caspase-11 and GSDMD-dependent IL-18 and IL-1β production in C57BL/6N mice dosed with LPS from Escherichia coli O111:B4 (refs. 8,17,29). Pharmacokinetic analyses of mice dosed intraperitoneally with KGR-53P suggested that near-micromolar concentrations could be achieved in blood, but repeat dosing was warranted owing to rapid compound clearance (Extended Data Fig. 7i). By contrast, the poor pharmacokinetic properties of KGR-3 precluded its use in vivo. As expected, mice receiving 20 mg kg−1 LPS had significantly increased IL-18 and IL-1β in their serum after 12 h compared with mice given vehicle alone (Fig. 4). However, markedly less IL-18 and IL-1β was observed when mice were treated with KGR-53P at 30 min before and 6 h after LPS dosing. CCL5, which exits cells via the classical secretory pathway, was suppressed to a lesser extent in mice receiving KGR-53P (Fig. 4). We noted that KGR-53P increased liver enzyme levels in the serum even in the absence of LPS (Extended Data Fig. 7j), but whether this is an off-target effect of the tool compound is unclear. Further optimization of KGR-3 and KGR-53P is needed to facilitate a broader assessment of caspase-1 and/or caspase-11 inhibition in pre-clinical disease models. Regardless, the data indicate that an inhibitor of caspase-1/11 that is impermeable in healthy cells can nonetheless limit the GSDMD-dependent secretion of proinflammatory IL-1β and IL-18 in vivo, presumably accessing its intracellular target caspases through GSDMD pores. This finding indicates that caspase inhibitors with poor cell permeability profiles may yet have therapeutic potential in the treatment of diseases characterized by excessive inflammasome activation, including sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome and COVID-19 (refs. 31–33).

Methods

Reagents

All chemicals were from commercial sources and used without further purification. Fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl (Fmoc)- and tert-butyloxycarbonyl (Boc)-protected amino acids were from Iris Biotech GmbH, Sigma Aldrich, Bachem, Creosalus and PE Biosciences Limited. Rink amide AM resin (200-300 mesh, loading 0.74 mmol g−1), 2-chlorotrityl chloride resin (100–200 mesh, loading 1.59 mmol g−1), biotin, HBTU (hexafluorophosphate benzotriazole tetramethyl uronium), HATU (hexafluorophosphate azabenzotriazole tetramethyl uronium), piperidine, diisopropylcarbodiimide, 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) were from Iris Biotech GmbH. Anhydrous HOBt (1-hydroxybenzotriazole) was from Creosalus. 2,4,6-Collidine (2,4,6-trimethylpyridine), acetonitrile (ACN, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) gradient grade), triisopropylsilane (TIPS), hydrobromic acid solution (Hbr, 30% wt in acetic acid), N-methylmorpholine, tetrahydrofuran (anhydrous), isobutylchloroformate and 2,6-dimethylbenzoic acid (2,6-DMBA) were from Sigma Aldrich. N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF, pure for analysis), methanol, dichloromethane (DCM), acetic acid, diethyl ether and phosphorus pentoxide (P2O5) were from POCh. Diazomethane used for the synthesis of AOMK inhibitors was generated according to the Aldrich Technical Bulletin (AL-180) protocol. All compounds (peptides, 7-amino-4-carbamoylmethylcoumarin (ACC) fluorescent substrates, aggregation-induced emission fluorescent substrates and inhibitors) were purified by reverse-phase HPLC on a Waters system (Waters M600 solvent delivery module and Waters M2489 detector system) using a semi-preparative Discovery C8 column (particle size 10 μm). Compound purity was confirmed by HPLC using an analytical Discovery C8 column (particle size 10 μm). Phase A was water/0.1% TFA. Phase B was ACN/0.1% TFA. For purification and compound analysis, the assay was run for 30 min in a linear gradient (from 5% phase B to 100% phase B). The molecular weight of each synthesized compound was confirmed on a WATERS LCT Premier XE High Resolution Mass Spectrometer with electrospray ionization (ESI) and a time of flight (TOF) module.

Synthesis of caspase substrates

Substrates were synthesized as described5. In brief, Fmoc-P2-OH (2.5 eq.) was preactivated with HATU (2.5 eq.) and 2,4,6-collidine (2.5 eq.) in DMF, then added to a cartridge containing H2N-Asp(O-tBu)-ACC-resin. The mixture was gently agitated for 3 h, filtered and washed with DMF. The Fmoc-protecting group was subsequently removed using 20% piperidine in DMF, with a ninhydrin test performed after each coupling and deprotection step. A solution of Fmoc-P3-OH (2.5 eq.), HATU (2.5 eq.) and 2,4,6-collidine (2.5 eq.) in DMF was added to the resin, followed by agitation for 3 h. Next, the Fmoc group was deprotected, and Fmoc-P4-OH amino acid was conjugated in a similar manner. After Fmoc deprotection from the P4 position, the N terminus was protected with an acetyl group using acetic acid (5 eq.), HBTU (5 eq.) and DIPEA (5 eq.) in DMF. Following solvent removal, the resin was washed with DMF, DCM and methanol, then dried over P2O5 and cleaved from the resin with a mixture of TFA/TIPS/H2O. The crude product was purified by HPLC and lyophilized. Purity and molecular weight were confirmed with liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)6. Substrates were dissolved in peptide-grade DMSO to a concentration of 20 mM and stored at −80 °C.

Synthesis and characterization of caspase inhibitors

Ac-P4-P3-P2-P1-AOMK inhibitors were synthesized as described34,35. Boc-l-Asp(tBu)-OH was converted to Boc-Asp(tBu)-CH2N2 using a solution of diazomethane in diethyl ether. The resulting diazoketone was subsequently transformed into Boc-Asp(tBu)-CH2Br by treatment with HBr solution (30% wt. in acetic acid) and water. The crude bromomethyl ketone product, obtained as a pale-yellow oil, was then reacted with 2,6-dimethylbenzoic acid (2,6-DMBA) in the presence of KF in DMF to afford Boc-Asp(tBu)-AOMK. After removal of the Boc protecting group using 50% TFA in DCM, the resulting Asp-AOMK intermediate was used in subsequent syntheses without further purification. In parallel, selected Ac-P4-P3-P2-OH peptide fragments bearing appropriate side-chain protecting groups were synthesized on 2-chlorotrityl chloride resin and used without further purification; for example, Ac-Tle-hGlu(tBu)-hCha-OH. The peptide fragments were then coupled with Asp-AOMK in DMF using HATU and DIPEA as coupling reagents. The crude products were purified by HPLC using a Waters system equipped with an M600 solvent delivery module and an M2489 UV/Vis detector, fitted with a semi-preparative Discovery C8 column, 10 µm particle size. Purification was performed using a water-acetonitrile gradient (0 to 100% in 25 min) at a flow rate of 15 ml min−1, followed by lyophilization. KGR-53P, Ac-Tle-hGlu(tBu)-hCha-Asp-AOMK, was used without further deprotection. The remaining inhibitors were deprotected using 50% TFA in DCM, after which TFA and DCM were removed under reduced pressure. The resulting products were subjected to a second round of HPLC purification, lyophilized and dissolved in DMSO to obtain stock solutions at a final concentration of 10 mM. Each inhibitor was analysed by LC–MS using a Waters system equipped with an ACQUITY QDa detector, Arc HPLC module and 2489 UV/Vis detector. Analyses were performed on an analytical C8 column, 10 µm particle size, using a water-acetonitrile gradient (0 to 100% in 20 min) at a flow rate of 1 ml min−1. The purity of each compound was above 95%, as determined by UV absorbance at 220 nm. The molecular mass of each inhibitor was confirmed by mass spectrometry using either positive or negative ionization mode on the QDa detector (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Recombinant caspases and kinetic assays

Caspases were expressed, purified and active site titrated using z-VAD-FMK (Promega) as described36. Kinetic assays were conducted at 37 °C using an fMax fluorimeter (Molecular Devices) operating in kinetic mode. Excitation for ACC-based substrates was at 355 nm, while emission was detected at 460 nm with a cutoff set at 455 nm. The assay buffer was 1 M sodium citrate, 10% w/v sucrose, 20 mM Pipes, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 10 mM DTT, pH 7.2–7.4, except for caspase-3 and caspase-7, when 1 M sodium citrate was omitted. In Extended Data Fig. 1, caspases were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min prior to mixing with 100 μM substrate in 100 μl. Reactions contained 3 nM caspase-1, 100 nM caspase-4 or 120 nM caspase-5, and were monitored for 30 min. Alternatively, E. coli expressing one of the caspases were resuspended in 50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 100 mM NaCl and sonicated for 2 min at full power with 50% duty cycle (on for 0.5 s, then off for 0.5 s, Branson Sonifier 250). Lysates were centrifuged for 30 min at 18,000g. Supernatants were filtered (0.45 μm) and diluted twofold with 2× buffer (described above) prior to measurements. The total protein concentration in the lysates varied between 150–300 mg ml−1, and the final concentration of the lysate was adjusted during caspase activity measurements with an appropriate substrate (for example, Ac-DEVD-ACC for caspase-3, Ac-WEHD-ACC for caspase-1, -4 and -5). HyCoSuLs were screened with 100 μM substrate. Velocity calculations were performed using the linear phase of the progress curve, typically observed between 15–30 min. Experiments were performed in triplicate, and reported results are mean values, with variation between individual measurements consistently <10%. Data analysis involved normalizing total relative fluorescence units (RFUs) for each sub-library, with the highest value set to 100% and adjustments made accordingly.

In Extended Data Table 1, reactions contained 2–15 nM caspase-1, 3–35 nM caspase-3, 35–200 nM caspase-4, 20–250 nM caspase-5, 40–400 nM caspase-7, 6–42 nM caspase-8, or 20–150 nM caspase-9, and were performed using 8 different substrate concentrations between 0.05 and 500 μM. DMSO in the reactions was kept below 2% (v/v). Each reaction was done in triplicate. kcat/Km calculations were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0.2.

In Supplementary Table 1, the kobs/I (observed pseudo-first-order rate constant/inhibitor concentration) parameter was determined using the equation: Y = (V0 − VS) × (1 − exp(−kobs[app]))/kobs[app] + VS × X, where Y denotes enzyme activity, V0 stands for initial enzyme activity, [app] is the apparent inhibitor concentration and VS represents enzyme activity at equilibrium. Initially, we screened all inhibitors in triplicate at concentrations of 10 nM, 100 nM, 1 μM and 10 μM against each caspase. Subsequently, we calculated the kobs[app]/I values from the plots that exhibited the best fitting to the equation curve. The kobs[app]/I values for the most effective inhibitors were derived from the analyses conducted at 10 nM and 100 nM inhibitor, while those for less effective inhibitors were determined using 1 μM and 10 μM inhibitor. We calculated kobs/I using the formula kobs/I = kobs[app]/I × (1 + Km/[S]), where Km represents the Michaelis–Menten constant for the substrate used, and [S] denotes the substrate concentration. Each caspase was incubated with an appropriate ACC-labelled substrate to monitor the reaction: caspase-1, Ac-WEHD-ACC, [S] = 50 μM, Km = 11 μM; caspase-3, Ac-DEVD-ACC, [S] = 50 μM, Km = 20 μM; caspase-4, Ac-WEHD-ACC, [S] = 100 μM, Km = 43 μM; caspase-6, Ac-VEID-ACC, [S] = 100 μM, Km = 42 μM; caspase-7, Ac-DEVD-ACC, [S] = 100 μM, Km = 48 μM; caspase-8, Ac-LEHD-ACC, [S] = 50 μM, Km = 8 μM; caspase-9, Ac-LEHD-ACC, [S] = 200 μM, Km = 102 μM; caspase-10, Ac-LEHD-ACC, [S] = 50 μM, Km = 27 μM.

In Extended Data Table 2, inhibitors were examined with greater rigour against both human and mouse caspases. Mouse caspase-1, Ac-WEHD-ACC, [S] = 50 μM, Km = 12.5 μM; mouse caspase-1, Ac-DEVD-ACC, [S] = 50 μM, Km = 25 μM; mouse caspase-8, Ac-LEHD-ACC, [S] = 50 μM, Km = 6.5 μM; mouse caspase-11, Ac-WEHD-ACC, [S] = 200 μM, Km = 132 μM. Reactions contained 1.9 nM human caspase-1, 2.3 nM human caspase-3, 43 nM human caspase-4, 4.1 nM human caspase-8, 14 nM human caspase-9, 2.2 nM mouse caspase-1, 1.8 nM mouse caspase-3, 3.2 nM mouse caspase-8, or 38 nM mouse caspase-11, ensuring that the lowest inhibitor concentration was at least fourfold higher than the caspase concentration. Inhibitors were diluted in 384-well plates, mixed with substrate and preincubated at 37 °C. Caspases were preincubated separately at 37 °C for 15 min, and then added to the mix of inhibitor and substrate. Fluorescence was monitored for 20 min. kobs values were calculated as before, and kobs/I was determined by plotting kobs values against [I] and applying linear regression. Parameters were derived from at least three independent experiments and presented as average values.

In vitro permeability assays

Permeability assays in gMDCKI cells (mycoplasma tested but not authenticated) were performed as described12. In brief, cells were seeded at 7 × 105 cells per ml in 96-well transwells in 100 μl DMEM containing phenol red, 10% BSA, 1% pen/strep, 1× GlutaMAX and 5 μg ml−1 plasmocin, grown until confluent (around 4 days), and then placed in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution containing 25 mM sterile filtered HEPES buffer, pH 7.4 (Invitrogen). 25 μM caspase inhibitor was added to the donor compartment together with the monolayer integrity marker Lucifer Yellow (Sigma Aldrich). Inhibitor concentrations in the donor and receiver compartments after 3 h at 37 °C were determined by LC–MS/MS. The apparent permeability (Papp) of test compounds was determined using the equation Papp = (dQ/dt) × (1/AC0), where dQ/dt is the rate of compound appearance in the receiver compartment, Q is the quantity of compound, C0 is the concentration in the donor compartment and A is the surface area of the insert. Efflux ratio was calculated as (Papp, B-to-A)/(Papp, A-to-B), where A is apical and B is basolateral.
