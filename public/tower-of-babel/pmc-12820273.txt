MVPHI: a multi-view learning framework for predicting complex microbial interactions
Authors: Yun Xie, Jie Pan, Dan Li, Qi Wang, Yanmei Sun, Shiwei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses that infect bacteria. As the natural regulators of microbial communities, it plays a crucial role in microbiome turnover. Predicting phage–bacteria interactions (PBIs), as well as bacteria–bacteria interactions (BBIs) is essential for advancing microbiome research. Given the high cost and risk of wet-lab techniques, computational biology and bioinformatics methods are reasonable alternatives. However, existing approaches suffer from the low predictive accuracy and poor efficiency. In this study, we proposed a multi learning-based model named MVPHI for predicting complex microbial interactions. More specifically, we first construct a heterogeneous multi-attributed microbial network (MAMN) based on pathogenic bacteria and associated phages. Next, MVPHI introduces three different view microbial characteristics for training the model, including the statistical-view, textual-view and topology-view features. Experimental results on seven benchmark datasets indicated that MVPHI achieves superior performance compared with the six variant models and eight baseline algorithms. Moreover, case study and protein docking experiments further demonstrated the robustness and generalization of our model. In conclusion, the proposed MVPHI model has potential ability to predict novel PBIs and BBIs, and can also provide valuable insights for phages screening and bacterial community research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32359-2.

Introduction

Bacteriophages (Phages) are natural predators that invade bacteria and have co-evolved with bacteria for nearly 4 billion years1–3. As one of the most abundant organisms on earth (about 1031 particles in the biosphere), they can affect the function, structure, and evolution of natural microbial communities4,5. Recently, phages have been found with the potential for food preservation, environmental governance, and nanotechnology, while all of which depended on the bactericidal action of phages6. Additionally, antibiotic-resistant bacteria are rapidly emerging owing to the misuse of antibiotics, and phage therapy is considered to be one of the most promising alternatives for treating bacterial infections7–9. Host ranges are the key to explain the role of phages in complex molecular mechanisms, and to be designed as effective tools for manipulating microbial communities10. Therefore, prediction of phage–bacteria interactions (PBIs) is essential in modifying phages and exploring complex relationships between microorganisms11. In addition, microbial interactions are inherently governed by heterogeneous biological information spanning multiple levels, including genomic structure, sequence composition, protein semantic attributes, and complex network topology12. Each level reflects a distinct aspect of microbial behaviour and interaction mechanisms. Therefore, relying on any single type of information is insufficient to fully characterize the inherent complexity of PBIs and bacteria–bacteriainteractions (BBIs)13.

In the field of microbiology, most techniques for identifying PBIs still rely on traditional wet-lab experiments, including plaque assays, liquid culture, microfluidic PCR, and flow cytometry14,15. However, such biological approaches are laborious, time-consuming and costly. With the rapid development of biotechnology, biomedical data of phages and bacteria have emerged and drawn extensive attention16. Therefore, it is reasonable to develop computational models for predicting BBIs and PBIs17. To date, many computational approaches have been proposed and can be broadly categorized into three groups: sequence similarity−based methods, machine learning–based methods, and network-based methods.

Sequence similarity-based methods require the evolutionary information of microbes to construct the similarity network. After phages infect bacterial, their genome will be integrated into the bacterial genome. Thus, sequence similarity-based methods are preliminary used to predict BBIs and PBIs. For example, Andrzej et al.18 designed an extension for BLAST, named Phirbo. It related the sequence and taxonomic similarity of phages and target bacterial sequences through intermediate public database, that have potential homologous for both phages and bacteria hosts. Wang et al.19 proposed a new model named GERMAN-PHI, which collected the host−host and phage-phage similarity information to construct a heterogeneous network, and combined multi-head attention mechanism with graph embedding representation learning technique to predict PBIs. Pan et al.20 proposed a deep learning-based prediction framework named GSPHI to identify PBIs by integrating DNA and protein sequence data with graph embedding techniques. The model employs a natural language processing approach for initial node representation, followed by structural deep network embedding (SDNE) to extract topological features, and finally uses a deep neural network for accurate prediction. Gonzales et al.21 proposed PHIStruct, a structure-aware phage–host interaction prediction model that leverages embeddings derived from SaProt, a structure-informed protein language model. By inputting receptor-binding protein features into a multilayer perceptron, PHIStruct can effectively predict phage hosts across ESKAPEE genera. Experimental results demonstrate that PHIStruct achieves superior and more stable F1 scores, particularly under low sequence similarity scenarios, outperforming traditional machine learning tools by a notable margin. Despite their interpretability and biological relevance, sequence similarity-based methods suffer from several limitations22,23. First, these approaches rely on the availability and completeness of genomic sequences, which can be challenging due to incomplete or fragmented data in public databases. Second, sequence similarity may not fully capture the functional relationships or phenotypic effects of microbial interactions, especially in cases where sequence conservation is low but functional similarity remains high. Third, such methods may fail to identify novel interactions, as they are typically constrained by known homologies and cannot generalize well to unseen patterns. These drawbacks highlight the need for more advanced computational models that integrate diverse biological information beyond sequence similarity.

Machine learning-based models are always combined with network analysis-based methods. After combining, computational models can analyze the topological structures of microbial network to regrade their complex relationships. For example, Li et al.24 developed a novel deep learning framework named PHIAF, which integrates GAN-based data augmentation to alleviate data scarcity in PHI prediction. It fuses DNA and protein sequence features to enhance representation learning. Furthermore, an attention-enhanced CNN module is employed to improve interpretability and classification performance. Wang et al.25 developed a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based model called DeepHost that leverages a novel encoding strategy. DeepHost transforms phage genomes into three-dimensional matrices and applies CNN to infer host taxonomy. It is alignment-free, eliminating the need for sequence or k-mer comparisons with existing datasets, thereby improving computational efficiency on large-scale data. It also incorporates spaced k-mers, which enhance tolerance to SNPs and InDels. In addition, it employs a learning-based model that directly outputs classification results, avoiding manual selection of distance measures or thresholds. LMFH-VH26 and ILMF-VH27 extracted microbial information from three networks, including phage–bacteria network, bacteria–bacterianetwork, and phage−phage network. Both of them adopted neighborhood regularization logistic matrix factorization to predict PBIs. The difference of LMFH-VH and ILMF-VH is that ILMF-VH adds the similar network fusion to integrate multiple-source features. Wei et al.28 proposed a novel computational model named DeepPBI-KG that predicts PBIs by focusing on key proteins and genes involved in phage–bacteria interactions. A validation module based on essential genes is introduced to highlight biological relevance. Unlike traditional genome-wide approaches, DeepPBI-KG selectively targets interaction-related genes. In addition, K-means clustering is used to generate high-quality negative samples, improving data balance and predictive performance. Benchmark results confirm the model’s robustness and effectiveness. However, machine learning-based approaches still encounter several inherent limitations. Many models rely on predefined features or simplified representations, which may not sufficiently capture the complex biological mechanisms underlying PHI29,30. In addition, the predictive performance of these models can be affected by the imbalance between positive and negative samples, especially in datasets with sparse validated PHIs. Deep learning models, while powerful, often function as black boxes, limiting their interpretability and hindering biological insight extraction. Furthermore, most existing methods fail to incorporate the heterogeneous biological contexts, such as host immunity, horizontal gene transfer, or environmental conditions that are critical for accurately modeling microbial interactions. These limitations highlight the need for more interpretable and biologically informed learning strategies.

Network-based methods model microbial entities (e.g., phages and hosts) as nodes and their interactions as edges, enabling the use of graph theory and network representation learning to capture topological and relational patterns. These approaches are particularly effective for revealing complex interaction structures that are difficult to identify through sequence information alone. For example, Xiao et al.31 developed PHISGAE, a host specificity-aware graph autoencoder framework for phage−host interaction prediction. This method integrates both phage genome and protein sequence data to construct initial phage-phage relationships, which are further refined using a K-nearest neighbor strategy to preserve meaningful local semantics in a heterogeneous network. By explicitly incorporating host specificity into the representation learning process, PHISGAE achieves superior predictive performance at both species and genus levels, and demonstrates robust generalization in identifying potential hosts for novel phages discovered in metagenomic data. Zhou et al.32 proposed PHISDetector, a comprehensive computational framework that predicts phage–host interactions by detecting and integrating diverse genomic signals, such as CRISPR matches, prophage regions, and protein–protein interactions. The model employs machine learning techniques to assess the likelihood of interaction based on these features. PHISDetector is suitable for both individual predictions and large-scale metagenomic analyses, offering a flexible solution for exploring phage-microbe relationships. While network-based methods effectively capture topological patterns in phage−host relationships, they also present several limitations33,34. These approaches often depend on the availability of high-quality interaction data, making them susceptible to noise and sparsity. In addition, they may struggle to incorporate dynamic or heterogeneous biological information, which can reduce their interpretability and generalizability. Compared to previous studies, the proposed MVPHI model has several distinct advantages. MVPHI extracted multi-view features from four different level (structural-level, constituent-level, textual level and topology-level). Additionally, we utilized a graph attention mechanism to combined the structural-level and constituent-level information as the statistical-view features to reduce the noise and redundancy, and explores the robustness and superiority of our model.

Owing to these inherent limitations of single-view models, multi-view learning has recently emerged as an effective paradigm for integrating complementary biological information from different perspectives35. By jointly modeling structural, compositional, semantic, and topological features, multi-view frameworks enable a more comprehensive and robust characterization of complex microbial interactions. In this study, we propose a novel multi-view learning model named MVPHI, which integrated statistical-view features, protein textual-view features and graph topology-view features. More specifically, we first collect the ESKAPE (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp.) based bacteria and their target phages to build a heterogeneous multi-attributed microbial network (MAMN), from which we can capture multi-view information to characterize microbial nodes from different perspectives. We first used the composition/transition/distribution (CTD) and k-mer algorithm to extract the structural-level and constituent-level information of microbial genomes. Next, we introduce a graph attention mechanism to integrate them as statistical-view features. Then, considering the attribute information of protein sequences, we adopted a pre-trained natural language processing method, doc2vec, to aggregate neighborhood protein textual-level information. To further enhance the predictive power, we use a graph transformer network to capture the topology pattern features of MAMN. Following the preceding steps, we can obtain informative attribute representation, which incorporate the statistical-view, textual-view and topology-view information. Finally, a two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) is adopted to training and predicting the potential interactions between phages and target bacterial. Extensive experiments have performed and demonstrated that MVPHI can fully leverage multi-source biological information to improve the prediction accuracy. Additionally, we also conducted the case study of K. pneumoniae to further demonstrated the effectiveness of our model.

Constituent-level information

In the field of bioinformatics, k-mer is a simple but powerful method for analyzing sequence, which is used to express the occurrence frequency of k neighbouring nucleic acids41. This model can capture the pattern information of local sequences. In this work, we applied 3-kmer to encode the protein sequence as a 64-dimensional vector to capture the constituent information from MAMN. Model training and prediction

Performance of MVPHI in PBIs and BBIs

We validate the performance of MVPHI through 5-fold cross-validation (CV) framework. Taking the E. faecium BBIs task as an example, the whole E. faecium datasets were divided into 5 equal subsets. One subset is chosen for testing and the four remaining were selected as training set. All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel i7-0700 CPU, 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 5060 GPU. Under this setting, the average training time of MVPHI for one fold is approximately 5–8 min, depending on the dataset size. Table 2 summarizes the experimental results of MVPHI obtained on seven benchmark datasets. As can be seen that MVPHI achieves the average values of ACC with 0.8243, 0.8332, 0.8109, and 0.8401 on the E. faecium, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and PBIs dataset, respectively. They show a relatively lower level on the S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, and Enterobacter spp. datasets with the ACC value of 0.8088, 0.8033, and 0.7955, respectively. The main reason may be due to the presence of some specific biological features or genetic variation that may not have been fully captured. However, it also achieves high AUC values with 0.8835, 0.8822, and 0.8718, respectively. The corresponding ROC and PR curves are presented in Fig. 2.

Parameter optimization analysis

In MVPHI model, parameter selection plays essential role to model fitting and performance, with learning rates and hidden units is even critical. In order to obtain the optimum model, we conducted optimization experiments for these two hyper parameters. For the parameter of learning rate, we increased it from 0.00001 to 0.01, and the experimental results of PBIs prediction task is shown in Fig. 3a. While these prediction values of ACC, PRE, REC, F1, AUC and AUPR indicators vary, they showed a pattern of first rising and then decline tendency. According to the experimental results, we selected the 0.001 as the optimal learning rate, at which MVPHI can obtain the best results. Similarly, we also conduct the experiments for searching the best hidden unites from the unites 16 to 256, and the comparison results are shown in Fig. 3b.

Impact of iteration number

In order to find the best combination of predictive performance and operating efficiency, we explore the impact of the parameter about iteration number on the PBIs prediction task. In the MVPHI model, the number of iterations is the key parameter of the local smoothing strategy. The optimal iterations number is able to fully learn the pattern in the data and avoid overfitting. Specifically, we set different iteration numbers including 5, 15, 50, 150, and 500 to train the model, and the rest parameters were unchanged. The impact of diverse iteration numbers is shown in Fig. 4. It can be observed that with the increase in the number of iterations, the AUC and AUPR values initially rise rapidly and then levelled off after 50 iterations. It can be concluded that when the iteration number is less than 50, MVPHI cannot achieve the optimal performance, whereas beyond 50 iterations, only minimal improvement is obtained. Additionally, exceeding 50 iterations increases the requirement of computational resources. Therefore, considering these factors, we choose the 50 iterations as the optimal training parameter.

Case study

To further evaluate the effectiveness and biological relevance of MVPHI, we conducted case studies on Klebsiella pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus, two clinically important pathogens belonging to the ESKAPE group and major contributors to multidrug-resistant infections worldwide. These species are representative targets for phage therapy and therefore provide biologically meaningful benchmarks for validating PBIs. In the K. pneumoniae case study, all known phages that associated with K. pneumoniae were excluded from the training data and used solely for testing, while the remaining data were used to train the model. After prediction, all candidate phages were ranked by their predicted interaction scores with K. pneumoniae, and the top 20 phages were selected for further validation. As shown in Table 4, among these top-ranked candidates, 17 phages were verified through publicly available databases, while 3 phages (ranked 3rd, 9th, and 15th) were not previously reported, indicating potential novel phage-host associations.

Similarly, in the S. aureus case study, we applied the same leave-out strategy by removing all phages interacting with S. aureus from the training data. The top 20 high-scoring phages were then selected based on their predicted interaction probabilities and compared with known database records. As presented in Table 5, 15 out of the 20 predicted phages were successfully confirmed, while the remaining 5 lacked existing experimental validation, suggesting their potential as novel candidates for future biological investigation.

Molecular docking

To further verify the biological plausibility of the experimental results in case studies, we conducted molecular docking experiments on phages that have not been confirmed by public databases. For more specific, we first used the AlphaFold 345 sever to generate the three-dimensional structures of phages and bacteria hosts. Subsequently, we performed rigid-body docking simulations through the GRAMM server, and visualized the resulting binding interfaces using PyMOL46. Binding energy is a key indicator which represents the change in free energy when two molecules form a stable complex via non-covalent interactions47. In protein-protein docking, it reflects the energetically favourable nature of the predicted binding pose. The more negative the binding energy, the stronger and more stable the interaction. Based on experience, binding energies below -5 kcal/mol are generally considered to indicate biologically meaningful interactions.

As shown in Fig. 7a-c, the computed binding energies ranged from −6.6 to −25.0 kcal/mol. Among them, A0A1W5LJ61 displayed the most favourable binding profile with a binding energy of −25.0 kcal/mol, which suggested a highly stable and potentially functional interaction with the bacterial host. Similarly, Fig. 7d-h illustrates the docking outcomes for five unconfirmed phages predicted to target Staphylococcus aureus, with binding energies ranging from − 7.0 to −25.1 kcal/mol. The lowest binding energy was observed for AXY82734.1 (−25.1 kcal/mol), indicating a strong interaction with the host receptor. These results offer important structural and energetic support for the computational predictions, suggesting that even phage–host pairs lacking prior experimental evidence may represent true biological interactions worthy of future validation.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed a novel computational model named MVPHI to predict PBIs and BBIs through multi-view features. Specifically, we constructed a heterogeneous multi-attributed microbial network (MAMN) based on ESKAPE pathogenic bacteria and phages, which can help researchers to elucidate the intricate relationship between pathogens and target viruses. Next, MVPHI fully leverages multi-view features to release the best performance of MAMN. To this end, we used CTD and k-mer algorithm to extract the structure and constituent information from the evolutionary information of microbial nodes. Then a graph attention mechanism was adopted to combine structure and constituent information as statistical features. The doc2vec algorithm is performed to extract protein textual features from amino acid sequences, and a graph transformer network is also used to capture the topological features of MAMN. MVPHI designed a two-layer MLP to train these multi-type features and predict potential associated pairs. Extensive experimental results demonstrate that the proposed MVPHI model significantly outperforms the state-of-the-art methods. Additionally, to verify the efficacy of MVPHI in real-life problems, the experiment of case study on K. pneumoniae and molecular docking experiments further indicated that our method is effective and reliable. MVPHI is expected to facilitate in-depth understanding of microbial genomics and advance research of phages discovery.
