Urinary angiotensinogen as a novel mortality predictor in diabetic kidney disease: a propensity-matched analysis from the KNOW-CKD
Authors: Sang Heon Suh, Dohyeon Lee, Hong Sang Choi, Chang Seong Kim, Eun Hui Bae, Seong Kwon Ma, Kook-Hwan Oh, Soo Wan Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatients with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) face a substantially higher risk of premature death compared to nondiabetic counterparts, yet accurate biomarkers for stratifying this mortality risk beyond conventional kidney function measures are currently lacking. We investigated whether urinary angiotensinogen (UAGT), reflecting intrarenal renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system activity, predicts all-cause mortality differently among patients with versus without diabetes in chronic kidney disease (CKD).MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 824 propensity score-matched patients (412 with diabetes and 412 without diabetes) from the KoreaN cohort study for Outcome in patients With Chronic Kidney Disease. Participants were categorized into quintiles by baseline urinary angiotensinogen-to-creatinine ratio (UAGT/Cr). Cox proportional hazards models estimated adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for all-cause mortality.ResultsDuring median follow-up of 8.9 years, 149 deaths occurred. In patients with diabetes, the highest UAGT/Cr quintile was independently associated with increased mortality compared to the reference quintile (adjusted HR 3.61, 95% confidence interval 1.67–7.81, P < .001) after adjustment for kidney function, proteinuria, and comorbidities. Penalized spline analysis demonstrated a monotonic relationship between UAGT/Cr and mortality in patients with diabetes. In contrast, no significant association was observed in patients without diabetes (P for interaction <.001). Findings remained robust in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionUAGT/Cr independently predicts all-cause mortality specifically in CKD patients with diabetes but not in those without diabetes, suggesting its potential utility as an etiology-specific prognostic biomarker for risk stratification in DKD.Keywords: angiotensinogen, biomarkers, chronic renal insufficiency, diabetes mellitus, mortality

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) worldwide, accounting for ∼30%–50% of all cases in developed countries [1]. The global burden of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) continues to escalate in parallel with the rising prevalence of type 2 diabetes, affecting over 40% of individuals with diabetes during their lifetime [2, 3]. Accordingly, DKD is now representing the most common cause of end-stage kidney disease in most developed countries [4, 5].

Compared to kidney diseases in patients without diabetes, DKD is characterized by accelerated kidney function decline, with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decreasing at nearly twice the rate [6]. Moreover, among patients with CKD, those with DM face substantially higher mortality risk despite similar kidney function parameters [7, 8], highlighting the need for diabetes-specific prognostic biomarkers. In addition, compared to their nondiabetic counterparts, higher rates of infectious complications, hospitalization, and healthcare resource utilization have been reported among the patients with DKD [9, 10].

The intrarenal renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of CKD, particularly in diabetic nephropathy [11–15]. Urinary angiotensinogen (UAGT) has emerged as a promising biomarker that reflects intrarenal RAAS activity, as angiotensinogen is excluded from the glomerular filtration and is secreted by proximal tubular epithelial cells [16]. Elevated UAGT levels have been demonstrated to correlate with kidney function decline, proteinuria progression, and histological severity of kidney damage in various CKD populations [17–20]. Indeed, UAGT provides complementary prognostic information beyond conventional markers such as albuminuria and eGFR, potentially identifying patients at higher risk for adverse kidney outcomes.

It is noteworthy that, even among the CKD patients with similar eGFR and proteinuria levels, UAGT level is significantly higher in patients with diabetes than in those without diabetes [21]. The prognostic value of UAGT for predicting the mortality risk specifically in patients with DKD, however, remains elusive. Previous studies have addressed the association between UAGT and CKD progression [19, 22, 23], whereas few have investigated its relationship with all-cause mortality, and none have systematically compared the prognostic impact of UAGT between diabetic and nondiabetic patients with CKD. Given the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical trajectories of DKD versus nondiabetic CKD, it is plausible that UAGT may have differential predictive value across these subgroups.

Therefore, we conducted a propensity score-matched study to investigate whether baseline UAGT levels predict the risk of all-cause mortality in CKD patients with diabetes versus those without diabetes. We hypothesized that elevated UAGT levels would be independently associated with higher mortality risk, and that this association would be more pronounced in diabetic compared to nondiabetic CKD patients, reflecting the augmented intrarenal RAAS activity characteristic of diabetic nephropathy [24, 25].

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

The KNOW-CKD (KoreaN cohort study for Outcome in patients With Chronic Kidney Disease) is a prospective cohort study on the Korean patients with CKD, and enrolled the patients at stages ranging from 1 to predialysis 5. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The participants who voluntarily provided informed consent were recruited from the nine tertiary hospitals in South Korea between 2011 and 2016. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of each participating center: Seoul National University Hospital (1104–089-359, 25 May 2011), Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (B-1106/129–008, 24 August 2011), Yonsei University Severance Hospital (4–2011-0163, 2 June 2011), Kangbuk Samsung Medical Center (2011–01-076, 16 June 2012), Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital (KC11OIMI0441, 30 June 2011), Gil Hospital (GIRBA2553, 8 August 2011), Eulji General Hospital (201105–01, 10 June 2011), Chonnam National University Hospital (CNUH-2011-092, 5 July 2011), and Busan Paik Hospital (11–091, 26 July 2011). After excluding the participants lacking the baseline measurement of urinary angiotensinogen-to-creatinine ratio (UAGT/Cr) in spot urine (n = 547) and those missing the date on the follow-up duration (n = 3), a total of 824 participants including the patients with DM (n = 412) and their age- and sex-matched nondiabetic patients from the KNOW-CKD were finally included for the analyses in the current study (Fig. 1). The median follow-up duration was 8.9 years.

Flow diagram of the study participants.

Data collection from the participants

The baseline data were collected at the time of enrollment, including demographic information, anthropometric measurements, medical histories, and laboratory and echocardiographic measurements according to the study protocol [26]. Blood and urine samples were obtained after an overnight fast, and were subsequently transported to the central laboratory (Lab Genomics, Seongnam, Korea) for the analyses. eGFR was determined using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation using serum creatinine level [27]. Spot urine samples were collected preferably as the second void in the morning.

Determination of UAGT/Cr

UAGT levels were measured using spot urine samples. The urine samples were centrifuged at 1500 r.p.m. for 10 min at 4°C. The urinary supernatants were pooled, and the UAGT concentrations were measured using human angiotensinogen ELISA kits (Catalog No. 27412, IBL, Takasaki, Japan) [28]. The intra-assay and interassay coefficients of variation were <5% and <8%, respectively. The lower limit of quantification was 0.31 ng/ml, with values below this limit assigned a value of 0.31 ng/ml for the calculation of UAGT/Cr. All samples were analyzed in duplicate, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis. UAGT level was normalized to urinary creatinine contents to calculate UAGT/Cr, which offers a standardized, noninvasive measure that accounts for variations in urinary concentration and has shown promise in risk stratification across different CKD etiologies [19, 22, 28].

Exposure and study outcomes

The participants with or without DM were categorized into the quintile by UAGT/Cr levels, respectively (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4, and Q5). The study outcome was all-cause death. The occurrence of the event was closely monitored at each participating center throughout the entire observational period, and was cross-validated by the investigators from the other participating centers to exclude reporting errors.

Statistical analysis

For propensity score matching analysis, the treatment group was defined as patients with DM. Patients whose primary cause of CKD was not DM in the original dataset of the KNOW-CKD were dichotomized as non-DM. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model, with DM status as the dependent variable and age and sex as independent variables. No missing values were present for age or sex. Following propensity score estimation, 1:1 greedy nearest-neighbor matching was performed using the logit of the propensity scores. Balance diagnostics were assessed based on the distributions of age and sex between the matched groups. Only the propensity score-matched cohort was included in the final analysis. Standard statistical methods were used for descriptive and comparative analyses. Continuous and categorical variables were compared using appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests. For nonparametric survival analysis, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed and compared using the log-rank test. Semiparametric survival analysis was performed using Cox proportional hazards regression models to calculate adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all-cause mortality, with statistical inference based on the Wald test. Covariates for the multivariable models were selected based on a combination of clinical importance and statistical significance (P < .10) in univariable analyses. To ensure model stability, multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF); variables with a VIF >5 were considered for exclusion, though all final covariates in our model met the criterion of VIF <5. The third quintile (Q3) was prespecified as the reference category, as the risk of CKD progression was found to be lowest in this population in our previous study [19]. To evaluate effect modification in the predefined subgroups (diabetic vs nondiabetic CKD), interaction terms between UAGT/Cr quintiles and diabetes status were incorporated into the models and tested for statistical significance. Considering that the reporting of death event is substantially interfered after the onset of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), a competing risk analysis using Fing–Gray model, where the onset of ESKD was treated as a competing risk, was additionally conducted. To evaluate the incremental predictive value of UAGT/Cr for all-cause mortality, we calculated Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) for both the base model [comprising age, sex, eGFR, and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR)] and the extended model (base model plus UAGT/Cr). To account for potential nonlinear associations between the exposure and outcome, penalized spline curves were incorporated into Cox proportional hazards models. The log-transformed UAGT/Cr values were used as the spline term, with simultaneous adjustment for predefined confounding variables as covariates. The degrees of freedom for the spline function were prespecified to balance model flexibility with prevention of overfitting. A penalty term was applied to constrain excessive curvature and ensure smooth, biologically plausible dose–response relationships. Sensitivity analyses included the Cox regression analyses with the exclusion of the participants with eGFR ≥ 90 or < 15 ml/min/1.73 m2 as well as multiple imputation. Missing data in this study were handled under the assumption of missing at random using multiple imputation techniques. The variables included in the imputation model were those used in the primary multivariable Cox analysis to ensure consistency. Imputation methods were selected according to variable type: predictive mean matching for continuous variables, logistic regression for binary variables, and discriminant function analysis for nominal categorical variables. The number of imputed datasets was prespecified as 30 to ensure adequate representation of variability in the predictive distribution of missing values. For each imputed dataset, Cox proportional hazards models were fitted, and the resulting parameter estimates were combined using Rubin’s rules to derive pooled HRs, 95% CIs, and P-values for final inference. Lastly, for more rigorous balancing of the baseline characteristics, we designed an additional propensity score-matched cohort between DM and non-DM patients incorporating CKD stage as well as age and sex, to repeat the primary analysis. The balance of covariates in both groups was visualized by Love plots. All tests of statistical significance were two-sided, with a significance level of P < .05. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS OnDemand for Academics (SAS Studio 3.8; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of the participants

After propensity score matching for age and sex, 824 patients were included: 412 with diabetes and 412 without diabetes (Table 1). Overall, missingness was minimal, with most variables showing <1.0% missing data. The highest missing rates were for left ventricular mass index (LVMI; 7.4%) and E/e' ratio (3.3%). The missing rate for all the other parameters, including UAGT/Cr, age, sex, and laboratory values, was no more than 1.1%. Age (59.1 ± 9.8 years) and sex distribution (65% male) were well balanced between groups. The DM group had higher Charlson comorbidity indices (80.8% with scores 4–5 vs 12.9%, P < .001) and were more frequently treated with diuretics, lipid-lowering agents, and antithrombotics. Primary causes of nondiabetic CKD included hypertensive nephrosclerosis (35.3%), glomerulonephritis (33.8%), and polycystic kidney disease (18.3%). The DM group had higher body mass index (BMI) (25.3 ± 3.3 vs 24.6 ± 3.0 kg/m², P = .002), systolic blood pressure (SBP; 134 ± 19 vs 127 ± 15 mmHg, P < .001), lower hemoglobin (11.8 ± 1.2 vs 13.1 ± 1.9 g/dl, P < .001), lower eGFR (36.4 ± 22.8 vs. 46.9 ± 26.7 ml/min/1.73 m², P < .001), and higher albuminuria (median 1124 vs 274 mg/g, P < .001). Notably, median UAGT/Cr levels did not differ between the two groups (5.2 [1.6, 25.1] vs 5.0 [1.7, 19.4] μg/g, P = .421). Echocardiography revealed higher LVMI (102.0 ± 25.7 vs 93.6 ± 22.7 g/m², P < .001), elevated E/e' ratio (12.3 ± 4.5 vs 9.9 ± 3.5, P < .001), and more frequent regional wall motion abnormalities (7.4% vs 2.7%, P < .001) in the DM group (Table S1). Across UAGT/Cr quintiles (Tables S2 and S3), higher quintiles were associated with lower eGFR, higher albuminuria, more advanced CKD stages, increased LVMI, and elevated E/e' ratio.

All-cause mortality by UAGT/Cr levels in CKD patients with versus without diabetes

During follow-up (median 8.0 years), 149 deaths occurred. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a significant association between UAGT/Cr quintiles and mortality in the overall cohort (P < .001, by log-rank test) (Fig. S1). However, when stratified by diabetes status (Fig. 2), this association was confined to the DM group (P < .001, log-rank test), with no association in the non-DM group (P = .394, by log-rank test).

A Kaplan-Meier survival curve showing the cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality over a median follow-up period of 8.9 years for the DM and Non-DM groups. P-value by log-rank test. Q1, first quintile; Q2, second quintile, Q3, third quintile; Q4, fouth quintile; Q5, fifth quintile.

Cox regression analysis was conducted to exclude the bias imposed by potential confounders (Table 2). In the DM subgroup, compared to Q3, the fully adjusted HRs were significantly higher in Q5 (adjusted HR 3.61, 95% CI 1.67–7.81, P < .001). In the non-DM subgroup, no quintile showed significant association with mortality (all P > .05). The interaction between UAGT/Cr and diabetes status was highly significant (P for interaction <.001). In a competing risk analysis of all participants using Fing–Gray model (Table S4), where the onset of ESKD was treated as a competing risk, the elevation of UAGT/Cr (Q5) was still significantly associated with the risk of all-cause death (HR 2.660, 95% CI 1.03–6.89, P = .044).

To evaluate whether UAGT/Cr provides incremental predictive value for all-cause mortality over conventional markers, we compared the C-indices of the models in the entire study population (i.e. all together with DM and non-DM groups). The base model, which included age, sex, eGFR, and urine ACR, showed a C-index of 0.7233. The addition of UAGT/Cr to the base model significantly improved the discriminatory power, with the C-index increasing to 0.7281, with the positive integrated discrimination improvement (0.0011) and the negative net reclassification improvement (−0.0675). This suggests that UAGT/Cr provides independent prognostic information beyond what is captured by traditional kidney function parameters.

Penalized spline analysis (Fig. S2 and Fig. 3) demonstrated a monotonic, near-linear positive relationship between log UAGT/Cr and mortality hazard in the DM group, while the non-DM group showed a flat relationship across the entire range of UAGT/Cr values.

Sensitivity analyses

Results remained consistent after excluding participants with eGFR ≥ 90 ml/min/1.73 m² (Table S5) or eGFR < 15 ml/min/1.73 m² (Table S6). In both analyses, Q5 maintained strong associations with mortality in the DM subgroup (adjusted HR 3.56, P < .001 and adjusted HR 3.29, P = .004, respectively), while the non-DM subgroup showed no significant associations (P for interaction <.001 in both). Multiple imputation analysis with 30 datasets (Table S7) yielded similar results. In the DM subgroup, Q5 remained independently associated with mortality (adjusted HR 3.37, 95% CI 1.61–7.04, P < .001), while the non-DM subgroup showed no associations. The interaction term remained highly significant (P < .001). These sensitivity analyses confirmed that UAGT/Cr independently predicts all-cause mortality specifically in diabetic CKD patients, with no prognostic value in nondiabetic CKD. Even in the propensity score-matched cohort incorporating age, sex, and CKD stage (Fig. S3), the elevation of UAGT/Cr levels was significantly associated with the risk of all-cause death only in DM patient (HR 2.19, 95% CI 1.02–4.70, P = .044), but not in non-DM patients (P for interaction <.001) (Table S8).

DISCUSSION

In this propensity score-matched cohort study of 824 patients with CKD, we identified UAGT/Cr as a novel, etiology-specific biomarker for mortality risk stratification in DKD. Specifically, we demonstrated that elevated baseline UAGT/Cr independently predicted a 3.6-fold increased risk of all-cause mortality in diabetic CKD patients after comprehensive adjustment for conventional risk factors, while showing no prognostic value in nondiabetic CKD. This diabetes-specific predictive value of UAGT/Cr highlights the distinct pathophysiology of DKD and suggests its potential utility for personalized risk assessment and therapeutic decision-making in diabetes care.

Our findings suggest that UAGT/Cr measurement may serve as a novel risk stratification tool to identify CKD patients with diabetes at higher risk of all-cause death who might benefit from more intensive monitoring and therapeutic interventions. Current risk prediction models for DKD primarily rely on eGFR, albuminuria, and clinical risk factors [10, 29], and are not applicable for the prediction of death events. The adoption of UAGT/Cr could potentially facilitate the personalized therapeutic approach to the patients with DKD. For instance, given that elevated UAGT/Cr reflects enhanced intrarenal RAAS activity, patients with higher UAGT/Cr levels may particularly benefit from optimized RAAS inhibition through maximally tolerated doses of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi)/ angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), as recommended by current guidelines [30, 31]. More importantly, our results support the concept that intrarenal RAAS activation represents a distinct pathophysiological axis in DKD that extends beyond its effects on proteinuria and kidney function decline. The independent association with mortality suggests that UAGT/Cr correlates with systemic cardiovascular and metabolic derangements that are not fully reflected by conventional measures. This aligns with emerging evidence that tissue-level RAAS activation contributes to end-organ damage through mechanisms including oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and endothelial dysfunction [32, 33]. Lastly, the striking difference in the prognostic value of UAGT/Cr between diabetic and nondiabetic CKD highlights the importance of etiology-specific biomarkers in nephrology practice. Current guideline presents uniform risk assessment tools for CKD patients regardless of the etiology [29], whereas our data clearly suggest that the use of biomarker should be specified depending on the primary cause of CKD.

Of particular significance is the potential utility of UAGT/Cr in addressing the “residual risk” that remains uncaptured by conventional markers. While eGFR and albuminuria are powerful predictors of kidney failure [10, 29], they may not fully reflect the systemic burden of intrarenal RAAS activation, which is intrinsically linked to cardiovascular damage and mortality in diabetes. In our study, UAGT/Cr predicted mortality independent of these traditional markers, suggesting it provides complementary prognostic information. This observation is particularly relevant to the emerging concept of precision medicine in nephrology. The fact that UAGT/Cr serves as a mortality predictor exclusively in the diabetic population—but not in the nondiabetic matched cohort—supports the use of etiology-specific biomarkers. By identifying a subgroup of diabetic patients with heightened intrarenal RAAS activity who are at the highest risk of death, clinicians may be better positioned to implement targeted interventions, such as the optimization of RAAS inhibitors or the introduction of novel agents that modulate this pathway, thereby moving beyond a “one-size-fits-all” approach to risk management.

Several putative mechanisms may explain why elevated UAGT levels predict the risk of all-cause death specifically in CKD patients with diabetes but not in those without diabetes. Most of all, diabetes induces profound dysregulation of the intrarenal RAAS [21], characterized by increased local angiotensinogen production in proximal tubular cells, which process is driven by hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and advanced glycation end-products [14]. This diabetic milieu directly stimulates the gene transcription of angiotensinogen, resulting in markedly elevated intrarenal angiotensin II generation that exceeds the degree of systemic RAAS activation [14]. Increased intrarenal angiotensin Ⅱ generation contributes to systemic hypertension through sodium retention and volume expansion, promotes cardiac remodeling and fibrosis through paracrine effects and neurohormonal activation, and accelerates atherosclerosis through pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidant mechanisms [34, 35]. As a precursor of angiotensin II, i.e. an effector of RAAS [36], the urinary concentration of angiotensinogen is mainly affected by the kidney-specific local production, because the filtration of systemically circulating angiotensinogen molecules are limited by glomerulus due to high molecular weights [37]. UAGT is, thus, a well-established surrogate of intrarenal RAAS activation, and, apart from the systemically circulating level of angiotensinogen, independently correlates with blood pressure levels and LVMI [38–40]. The strong association between UAGT levels and the risk of all-cause death in diabetic patients likely reflects this augmented intrarenal RAAS activity, which extends beyond kidney-specific effects, such as cardiovascular events. The UAGT levels between the diabetic and nondiabetic CKD patients, however, were not statistically different propensity score matching in the current study, though UAGT level is in general significantly higher in the diabetic than in the nondiabetic subpopulation [19, 21]. Rather, it seems more likely that the systemic consequences of enhanced intrarenal RAAS activation may be particularly detrimental in diabetic patients. The diabetic condition is often complicated with pre-existing endothelial dysfunction, and insulin resistance [41] is an independent cardiovascular risk factor. The additional burden of the heightened intrarenal RAAS activity may represent a critical tipping point that substantially amplifies the mortality associated with cardiovascular events. On the other hand, the lack of association between UAGT/Cr and mortality in nondiabetic CKD deserves consideration. Several factors may also explain this finding. First, nondiabetic CKD encompasses heterogeneous etiologies (e.g. hypertensive nephrosclerosis, glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, and tubulointerstitial disease) with distinct pathophysiological mechanisms, potentially diluting any etiology-specific associations. Second, intrarenal RAAS activation may play a less central role in disease progression and outcomes in nondiabetic CKD compared to DKD. Third, competing risk factors (such as immunological mechanisms in glomerulonephritis or genetic etiology in polycystic kidney disease) may predominate over RAAS-mediated pathways in determining outcomes in nondiabetic populations.

Our findings have several important implications for clinical diabetes care and CKD management. First, UAGT/Cr measurement could enhance current risk prediction models for DKD, which primarily rely on eGFR and albuminuria [29]. The addition of UAGT/Cr may identify high-risk patients with diabetes who would benefit from intensified diabetes management, including optimal glycemic control, blood pressure management, and maximally tolerated RAAS inhibition [30, 31]. Second, UAGT/Cr may serve as a target for therapeutic monitoring, as intrarenal RAAS activity represents a modifiable pathophysiological pathway in DKD. Third, the etiology-specific nature of UAGT/Cr underscores the need for diabetes-tailored biomarkers in nephrology practice, moving beyond uniform risk assessment tools [29].

Our study has several notable strengths. The propensity score-matched design minimizes confounding by balancing baseline characteristics between patients with and without diabetes. The analysis of a hard outcome (i.e. all-cause mortality) reduces concerns about measurement error and outcome misclassification. A comprehensive adjustment for the potential confounders strengthens confidence in the independence of observed associations. The quintile-based analysis combined with penalized spline curves provides robust assessment of dose–response relationships without imposing parametric assumptions.

Some limitations should be also noted. First, as an observational study, we cannot establish causality. Second, the residual confounding by the variables not included in the regression models such as dietary sodium intake, physical activity, adherence to medications, and socioeconomic determinants may persist despite extensive covariate adjustment. Additionally, the current study lacks detailed adjudicated data on specific causes of death (e.g. cardiovascular vs noncardiovascular mortality). While UAGT/Cr strongly predicted all-cause mortality in diabetic patients, we could not definitively confirm whether this association is driven primarily by cardiovascular pathways. Future studies with adjudicated cause-specific outcomes are necessary to confirm the mechanistic link between intrarenal RAAS activity and specific mortality causes. Third, UAGT/Cr was measured only at baseline, precluding assessment of longitudinal changes or intra-individual variability, while serial measurements may provide additional prognostic information and help distinguish patients with persistently elevated vs transiently elevated UAGT levels. Fourth, while we adjusted for ACEi/ARB use as a binary variable, our analysis does not account for detailed dosing intensity (e.g. percentage of maximum tolerated dose) or medication adherence. Since the enrollment period (2011–2016) preceded the widespread use of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, ACEi/ARBs were the primary therapeutic standard, and patients were generally maintained on the highest clinically tolerated doses. However, the lack of granular data on individual dose–response and adherence remains a limitation, as these factors can directly modulate intrarenal RAAS activity and urinary UAGT excretion. In line with this, this study lacks longitudinal data to demonstrate whether UAGT/Cr levels can be modified by therapeutic interventions such as renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors. Further prospective studies are warranted to determine if UAGT/Cr can serve as a dynamic biomarker for monitoring treatment response and guiding optimized RAS inhibition strategies. Fifth, the relatively small sample size in each UAGT/Cr quintile, particularly after stratification by diabetes status, may have limited statistical power to detect weaker associations or evaluate effect modification by other factors. Sixth, while our choice of Q3 as the reference group followed established rationale from previous findings [19], we acknowledge a potential discrepancy between models. Specifically, the nonsignificant but elevated HR in Q1 suggests a nonmonotonic (U-shaped) trend in categorical analysis, contrasting with the near-linear relationship in the spline models. This limitation may arise from the inherent nature of quintile-based categorization and warrants cautious interpretation.

In conclusion, our study identifies UAGT as a novel mortality predictor in diabetic, but not nondiabetic, CKD patients, independent of conventional risk factors. These findings highlight the distinct pathophysiology of DKD and support the clinical utility of UAGT for diabetes-specific risk stratification. Integration of UAGT/Cr measurement into clinical practice may facilitate targeted interventions in high-risk diabetic patients.
