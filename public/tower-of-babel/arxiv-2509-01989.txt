Bethe Ansatz solution for a model of global-range interacting bosons on the square lattice
Jon Links

Abstract
Quantum systems on a one-dimensional lattice are ubiquitous in the study of models exactly-solved by Bethe Ansatz techniques. Here it is shown that including global-range interaction opens scope for Bethe Ansatz solutions that are not constrained to one-dimensional quantum systems. A bosonic model on a square lattice is defined, and the exact Bethe Ansatz solution is provided for open, cylindrical, and toroidal boundary conditions. Generalising the result for an integrable defect leads to a Bethe Ansatz solution that is not exact.

Introduction

For each of the fundamental particle properties known as spin, boson, and fermion, there exists a fundamental, one-dimensional model accommodating a Bethe Ansatz solution. These are, respectively, the Heisenberg spin-1/2 model
[
1
]
, the Bose gas
[
33
]
, and the Hubbard model
[
34
]
. While the Heisenberg and Hubbard models are defined on a lattice, the Bose gas Hamiltonian is formulated through continuous field operators. The natural discretised version of the Bose gas, the Bose-Hubbard model, is generally considered to not be integrable
[

,

[
5
,
26
]

[
15
,
31
,
48
]
. Moreover, while there are well-developed methods for generating and/or testing integrability in models for spin
[

,
22
,

,
47
]
or fermionic
[
11
,
37
]
degrees of freedom using boost (a.k.a. ladder) operator techniques and the Reshetikhin criterion, these do not readily extend to bosonic systems. Consequently, examples of exactly-solved bosonic lattice models are infrequently encountered.

Here, a Hamiltonian is introduced describing bosons on a square lattice with nearest-neighbour hopping, and a global-range (a.k.a. infinite-range) collective interaction term. The form of this interaction was proposed in
[

to model the interaction mediated by an optical cavity in an experiment using

For a

[

There has been a resurgence of interest in exactly-solvable models with long-range interactions in recent times; e.g.
[

12

17
,
27
]
. These works consider the systems to be one-dimensional spin chains. However, as will be discussed below, once long-range interactions are incorporated there is a decreased clarity in the notion of dimensionality. A case in point is provided by the Haldane-Shastry model
[

[
4
]
. The model is generally considered to be one-dimensional, but the geometry of the model can be viewed as being a ring with closed boundary conditions embedded in two-dimensional Euclidean space. This is due to the strength of spin interactions in the Hamiltonian being being dependent on the

In the next section the model with global-range interaction is formulated in terms of canonical boson operators associated with the sites of a square lattice. A key element in the analysis is the introduction of the adjacency matrix to describe the Hamiltonian.
The Bethe Ansatz solution for the Hamiltonian of this study is obtained by undertaking a canonical transformation to a spectrally equivalent system with hopping on a collection of disjointed lattices. This simplifies the form of the Hamiltonian, and allows the solution to be read off from previously published results.
The case of open, toroidal, and periodic boundary conditions are discussed. However it will be seen that this approach, developed for bosons on the square lattice, bears little resemblance to the typical transfer matrix method used for spin systems; e.g.
[
10
]
.

2
Integrable model with global-range interaction

The hopping interactions of the Hamiltonian will be formulated on an
L\times L
square lattice, where
L
is assumed to be even. The approach taken is aided by implementing the notion of an
adjacency matrix
, and by exploiting the
bipartite
nature of the lattice
[
25
]
.
Let
\{a_{j},\,a_{j}^{\dagger}:j=1,\dots,m\}\cup\{b_{j},\,b_{j}^{\dagger}:j=1,\dots,m\}
, where
m=L/2
, denote mutually commuting sets of canonical boson operators satisfying

\displaystyle[a_{j},\,a_{k}^{\dagger}]=[b_{j},\,b_{k}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{jk}I,

\displaystyle[a_{j},\,a_{k}]=[a_{j}^{\dagger},\,a_{k}^{\dagger}]=[b_{j},\,b_{k}]=[b^{\dagger}_{j},\,b_{k}^{\dagger}]=0.

For adjacency matrix of the form

\displaystyle A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&|&B\\
-&&-\\
B&|&0\end{array}\right)

where the submatrix
B
is a symmetric matrix, the Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=U(N_{a}-N_{b})^{2}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}B_{jk}(a_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}+b_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k})

(4)

where

\displaystyle N_{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j},\,N_{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{j}
.

The first term on the right-hand side of (
4
) is precisely the form of interaction used
to model the effect of an optical cavity applied to bosonic cold atoms in an optical square lattice
[
7
,
14
,
23
,
32
]
.

Note that
N=N_{a}+N_{b}
commutes with (
4
), indicating that the Hamiltonian conserves total particle number. In addition, there exists a set of conserved operators that follow from the
Yang-Baxter integrability of (
4
), which have been derived in
[
25
]
.
The conserved operators satisfying
[C(y),\,C(z)]=0
are given by

\displaystyle C(2p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}{B}^{2p}_{jk}(a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k}+b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}),

(5)

\displaystyle C(2p+1)

\displaystyle=2U\sum_{i=0}^{2p}D(2p,i)+\sum_{j,k=1}^{m}{B}^{2p+1}_{jk}(a_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}+b_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k})

(6)

with

\displaystyle D(2p,i)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{j,k,r,q=1}^{m}{B}^{i}_{jk}{B}^{2p-i}_{rq}(a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{q}a^{\dagger}_{r}a_{k}+b^{\dagger}_{j}b_{q}b^{\dagger}_{r}b_{k}),\qquad i\,\,{\rm even},\\
\displaystyle\sum_{j,k,r,q=1}^{m}({B}^{i}_{jk}{B}^{2p-i}_{rq}+{B}^{2p-i}_{jk}{B}^{i}_{rq})a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{q}b^{\dagger}_{r}b_{k},\qquad i\,\,{\rm odd}.\end{cases}

Above,
B^{q}_{jk}
denotes the
(j,k)
entry of the matrix power
B^{q}
.
We have
N=N_{a}+N_{b}=C(0)
and
H=C(1)-U(N^{2}+2(m-1)N)
.

2.1
Bethe Ansatz solution

The Bethe Ansatz solution is obtained by mapping the Hamiltonian (
4
) to an equivalent model via a unitary transformation. Let
X
denote a unitary matrix that diagonalises
B
, viz.

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{m}X^{\dagger}_{jp}X_{pk}=\delta_{jk},\qquad\sum_{p,q=1}^{m}X^{\dagger}_{jp}B_{pq}X_{qk}=\varepsilon_{j}\delta_{jk},

with
\{\varepsilon_{j}:j=1,...,m\}
denoting the spectrum of

B
.
Introducing

\displaystyle a_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{kj}c_{j},\quad b_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X_{kj}d_{j},\quad a^{\dagger}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X^{\dagger}_{jk}c^{\dagger}_{j},\quad b^{\dagger}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}X^{\dagger}_{jk}d^{\dagger}_{j},

leads to
\displaystyle N_{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}=N_{c}
,

\displaystyle N_{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}d_{j}^{\dagger}d_{j}=N_{d}
, and

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=U(N_{c}-N_{d})^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{j}(c_{j}^{\dagger}d_{j}+d_{j}^{\dagger}c_{j}).

(7)

Note that

\displaystyle\hat{N}_{j}=c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{j}+d^{\dagger}_{j}d_{j}

are conserved operators; let
N_{j}
denote their eigenvalues. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{j}=N

gives the total number of particles.

The effect of the transformation is that takes the square lattice model and maps it to the spectrally equivalent model (
7
). The transformed model is defined on a lattice that is the union of disconnected adjacency graphs, each with only two vertices and one edge. These adjacency graphs are weighted by

\epsilon_{j}
, the coupling for the tunneling strength between the two connected vertices. However observe that the form of the interaction term with coupling
U
is invariant under the transformation.
A graphical representation the transformed Hamiltonian is provided in panel (d) of Figs.
1
,
2
, and
3
.

Expressions for the energy eigenvalues of (
7
) are known from
[
35
]
, with the form

\displaystyle E=UN^{2}+4U\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{N_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}}{v_{n}-\varepsilon_{j}^{2}}

(8)

subject to the parameters
\{v_{n}:n=1,\dots,N\}
satisfying the Bethe Ansatz equations

\displaystyle\sum_{m\neq n}^{N}\frac{2v_{n}}{v_{n}-v_{m}}+\frac{\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{m}(v_{n}-\varepsilon_{j}^{2})^{N_{j}}}{\displaystyle 16U^{2}\prod_{m\neq n}^{N}(v_{n}-v_{m})}

\displaystyle=N-1+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{N_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}}{v_{n}-\varepsilon^{2}_{j}}

(9)

for
n=1,\dots,N
. Note that both (
8
) and (
9
) are functions of the
squares
of the

\{\varepsilon_{j}:j=1,...,m\}
. This is due to a symmetry resulting from unitary transformations of the form
c_{j}\mapsto-c_{j}
which induces a sign change for the
\varepsilon_{j}
in (
7
).

The Bethe eigenstates of the transformed operator (
7
) arise as a generalisation of results presented in
[
9
]
. Alternatively, they may be obtained as a specialisation after a transformation (e.g. see
[
45
]
) of a more recent construction developed in
[
44
]
. A distinctive character of the Bethe eigenstate formulation is that it is described through a set of pseudovacua, rather than a single pseudovacuum, analogous to the constructions in
[
46
]
. For a given collection of quantum numbers
\{N_{1},\,\dots,\,N_{m}:N_{j}\in\,{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\}
set

\displaystyle|N_{1},\dots,\,N_{m}\rangle=(d_{1}^{\dagger})^{N_{1}}\dots(d_{m}^{\dagger})^{N_{m}}|0\rangle

where
|0\rangle
denotes the physical vacuum. Then, for a solution of the Bethe Ansatz equations (
9
) the associated Bethe eigenstate reads

\displaystyle|v_{1},\dots,v_{N};N_{1},\dots,\,N_{m}\rangle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}C(v_{n})|N_{1},\dots,\,N_{m}\rangle

where

\displaystyle C(u)=\frac{1}{2U}I+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{2\varepsilon_{j}}{u-\varepsilon_{j}^{2}}c_{j}^{\dagger}d_{j}.

6
Integrable defect

The previous examples, dealing with the three different types of boundary conditions, illustrate how the integrability is a structural feature of the class of Hamiltonians of the form (
4
), and is not reliant on any particular properties of the adjacency submatrix
B
. The formulae (
5
,
6
) for the conserved operators hold completely independent of the choice for
B
. This opens an avenue for investigating integrability preserving defects in the system.

This choice of
B
does, however, strongly influence the character of the Bethe Ansatz solution; the solution depends explicitly on the eigenvalues of
B

For the previous three classes of boundary conditions it was found that the Bethe Ansatz solution can be expressed in closed form, because the eigenvalues are obtained in terms of trigonometric functions. It will be seen that the closed form property is no longer present for the defective integrable model. This will be illustrated through an example, where the defect is associated with the boundary of an open system for
L=8
.

7
Discussion

In this work the Yang-Baxter integrable Hamiltonian (
4
), with conserved operators (
5
,
6
),
was interpreted as a square lattice model with global-range interaction. The square lattice structure was obtained by a suitable choice of adjacency submatrix
B
, which was shown to be amenable to open, cylindrical, and toroidal boundary conditions. A Bethe Ansatz solution was presented that is dependent on the eigenvalues of
B
.
However, in general the matrix
B
can be chosen to have more general forms. As an example, the case of a boundary defect was discussed for an open boundary
8\times 8
lattice.

For arbitrary lattice sizes, the characteristic polynomial of
B
has order
L^{2}/2
. Instinctually, at some value of
L
the numerical evaluation of the eigenvalues of
B
for a system with randomly chosen integrable boundary defects will become intractable. On the other hand, the closed form (
19
) for defect-free open boundaries is valid for all
L
. Similarly for the cylindrical (
29
) and toroidal (
39
) boundary conditions. These observations contend that within the class given by (
4
) there are some Hamiltonians for which an exact,
closed-form expression for the Bethe Ansatz solution (
8
,
9
) is known. There are some Hamiltonians for which an exact, closed-form expression for the Bethe Ansatz solution (
8
,
9
) cannot be known. These intuitive considerations are consistent with the formalist approach taken in
[
36
]
to conclude that Yang-Baxter integrability is not synonymous with exact-solvability.
