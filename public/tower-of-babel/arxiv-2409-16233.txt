Abstract
. Shnirel’man’s inequality and Shnirel’man’s basis theorem are fundamental results about sums of sets of positive integers in additive number theory. It is proved that these results are inherently order-theoretic and extend to partially ordered abelian and nonabelian groups. One abelian application is an addition theorem for sums of sets of n n -dimensional lattice points.

1.
Shnirel’man density and Shnirel’man’s inequality

Let
\mathbf{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
be the set of positive integers and

\mathbf{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}
the set of nonnegative integers.

Let
A
be a set of positive integers.
The
counting function
of the set
A
is

A(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
1\leq a\leq n\end{subarray}}1.

The
Shnirel’man density
of the set
A
is

\sigma(A)=\inf\left\{\frac{A(n)}{n}:n\in\mathbf{N}\right\}.

It follows that
\sigma(A)=1
if and only if
A=\mathbf{N}
.

Shnirel’man density has some expected properties, such as the inequality

0\leq\sigma(A)\leq 1

for every set
A
. Also,
\sigma(A)=1/2
if
A
is the set of odd
integers. Shnirel’man density also has some unusual properties.
For example,
\sigma(A)=0
if
A
is the set of even integers.
More generally,
\sigma(A)=0
if
1\notin A
, and so

\sigma(A)>0\quad\text{ implies }\quad 1\in A.

For sets
A
and
B
of positive integers, we define the
sumset

A+B=\{a+b:a\in A\cup\{0\}\text{ and }b\in B\cup\{0\}\}.

The sum of
h
sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{h}
of positive integers is

A_{1}+\cdots+A_{h}=\left\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}:a_{i}\in A_{i}\cup\{0\}\text{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}\right\}.

In particular, if
A_{i}=A
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
, we have the
h
-fold sumset

hA=\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}:a_{i}\in A\cup\{0\}\text{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}\}.

The set
A
is a
basis of order
h
for the nonnegative integers
if
hA=\mathbf{N}_{0}
.
The set
A
is a
basis
for the nonnegative integers
if
A
is a basis of order
h
for some
h\geq 1
.
Note that
A
is a basis of order
h
if and only if
\sigma(hA)=1
.
This is one reason why Shnirel’man density is a good density for many additive problems.

Theorem 1
.

Let
A
and
B
be sets of positive integers.
If
\sigma(A)+\sigma(B)\geq 1
, then
A+B=\mathbf{N}_{0}
.

Proof.

The proof is a simple pigeonhole principle argument.
Let
\sigma(A)=\alpha
and
\sigma(B)=\beta
.
We have
0+0\in A+B
.
Because
\alpha>0
or
\beta>0
, we have
1\in A\cup B

and
1=1+0\in A+B
.

Let
n\geq 2
. If
n\in A\cup B
, then
n=n+0\in A+B
.
Suppose that
n\notin A\cup B
. Let
A^{\prime}=A\cap\{1,\ldots,n-1\}

and
B^{\prime}=B\cap\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Then

n-B^{\prime}=\{n-b:b\in B^{\prime}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n-1\}.

Because
n\notin A\cup B
, we have

|A^{\prime}|=A(n-1)=A(n)\geq\alpha n

and

|n-B^{\prime}|=|B^{\prime}|=B(n-1)=B(n)\geq\beta n

and so

|A^{\prime}|+|n-B^{\prime}|\geq(\alpha+\beta)n\geq n.

Because
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
are subsets of
\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
,
the sets
A^{\prime}
and
n-B^{\prime}
are not disjoint and so there exist

a\in A^{\prime}\subseteq A
and
b\in B^{\prime}\subseteq B
such that
a=n-b
.
Thus,
n=a+b\in A+B
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 2

(Shnirel’man’s inequality)
.

Let
A
and
B
be sets of positive integers.
If
\sigma(A)=\alpha
and
\sigma(B)=\beta
, then

(1)

\sigma(A+B)\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta

Proof.

This is a special case of Theorem
12
below.
∎

Note that
0\leq\alpha,\beta\leq 1
implies

0\leq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta=\alpha+\beta(1-\alpha)\leq 1.

The inequality

\sigma(A+B)\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta

is equivalent to

1-\sigma(A+B)\leq(1-\alpha)(1-\beta).

It follows by induction on
h
that if
A_{1},\ldots,A_{h}
are sets of positive integers
with
\sigma(A_{i})=\alpha_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
, then

1-\sigma(A_{1}+\cdots+A_{h})\leq\prod_{i=1}^{h}(1-\alpha_{i}).

If
A_{i}=A
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
and
\sigma(A)=\alpha
, then

1-\sigma(hA)\leq(1-\alpha)^{h}

or

\sigma(hA)\geq 1-(1-\alpha)^{h}

If
\alpha>0
, then there exists
h_{0}
such that

\sigma(h_{0}A)\geq 1/2
.
From Theorem
1
we obtain

\sigma(2h_{0}A)=1
. This proves

Theorem 3

(Shnirel’man’s basis theorem)
.

Let
A
be a set of positive integers.
If
\sigma(A)>0
, then the set
A
is a basis for the nonnegative integers.

It is natural to ask if Shnirel’man’s inequality and Shnirel’man’s basis theorem
can be extended to
n
dimensions, that is, to sums of subsets of
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}
,
the semigroup of
n
-dimensional nonnegative lattice points,
for all
n\geq 2
.
In a New York Number Theory Seminar talk “Shnirel’man density and the Dyson transform”
(cf. Nathanson
[
8
]
)
on September 5, 2024, I said that Shirel’man’s inequality and Shnirel’man’s basis
theorem were unsolved problems for lattice points in
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}
.

Mirabile dictu
, the following week I found on my computer some rough notes on
Shnirel’man density that I had written 10 years ago
and that included a proof of Shnirel’man’s inequality for lattice points.
These notes contained no references and I do not know if it was my proof or if
it was a proof that I had found in a published paper or in a document floating
in the internet.
The proof in these old notes was specifically about sums of sets of lattice points, but
a study of the argument showed that it depended only on
order-theoretic properties of the group of lattice points
and that the theorem could be extended to partially ordered abelian and nonabelian groups.
This paper is devoted to proving these group theoretic results and recovering
the addition theorems for
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}
.

The first step is to find analogues of Shnirel’man density that can be
constructed on arbitrary sets and, in particular, on ordered
or partially ordered abelian and nonabelian groups.
We can describe classical Shnirel’man density as follows.
Let
\mathcal{J}
be the set of all intervals of integers of the form
J=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
For every set
A
of positive integers,

\sigma(A)=\inf\left\{\frac{|A\cap J|}{|J|}:J\in\mathcal{J}\right\}.

This suggests the following construction of a generalized density
on the set of subsets of an nonempty set
X
.
Let
\mathcal{J}
be a set of nonempty finite subsets of
X
.
For every subset
A
of
X
, let

(2)

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)=\inf\left\{\frac{|A\cap J|}{|J|}:J\in\mathcal{J}\right\}.

This density has the following properties.

Theorem 4
.

Let
X
be a set and let
\mathcal{J}
be a set of nonempty finite subsets of
X
.
Let
A
and
A^{\prime}
be subsets of
X
.

(i)

If
A\subseteq A^{\prime}
, then

0\leq\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)\leq\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A^{\prime})\leq 1.

(ii)

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)=0
if
A\cap J=\emptyset
for some
J\in\mathcal{J}
.

(iii)

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)=1
if and only if
\bigcup_{J\in\mathcal{J}}J\subseteq A
.

(iv)

If
X=\bigcup_{J\in\mathcal{J}}J
, then
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)=1
if and only if
A=X
.

Proof.

If
A\subseteq A^{\prime}
and
J\in\mathcal{J}
, then
A\cap J\subseteq A^{\prime}\cap J\subseteq J
and so

0\leq\frac{|A\cap J|}{|J|}\leq\frac{|A^{\prime}\cap J|}{|J|}\leq 1.

This proves (i).

The definition of
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)
immediately implies (ii).

To prove (iii), it suffices to observe that
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)=1
if and only if

\frac{|A\cap J|}{|J|}=1

for all
J\in\mathcal{J}
if and only if
J\subseteq A
for all
J\in\mathcal{J}

if and only if
\bigcup_{J\in\mathcal{J}}J\subseteq A
.
This implies (iv) and completes the proof.
∎

2.
Partially ordered groups

A
partial order
on a nonempty set
X
is a relation
\leq
that satisfies
the following properties:

(1)

Reflexivity:
x\leq x
for all
x\in X
,

(2)

Anti-symmetry: For all
x,y\in X
, if
x\leq y
and
y\leq x
, then
x=y
,

(3)

Transitivity: For all
x,y,z\in X
, if
x\leq y
and
y\leq z
, then
x\leq z
.

We write
x<y
if
x\leq y
and
x\neq y
.
A
total order
on
X
is a partial order
\leq

such that, for all
x,y\in X
, either
x\leq y
or
y\leq x
.

The following is a fundamental result in the theory of ordered sets.

Theorem 5

(Szpilrajn
[
9
]
)
.

Every partial order on a set
X
can be extended to a total order on
X
.

For a short and beautiful proof of Szpilrajn’s theorem, see Mandler
[
6
]
.

A
lower bound
for a nonempty subset
Y
of a partially ordered set
X

is an element
y_{0}\in X
such that
y_{0}\leq y
for all
y\in Y
.
A partially ordered set
X
is
well-ordered
if every
nonempty subset of
X
contains a lower bound.

Let
G
be a group with identity element
e

and let
\leq
be a partial order on the set
G
.
The following definitions are standard in the theory of partially ordered groups
(cf. Clay-Rolfson
[
1
]
and Glass
[
3
]
).

The group
G
is
right partially ordered
if
a\leq b
implies
ac\leq bc

for all
a,b,c\in G
.

The group
G
is
left partially ordered
if
a\leq b
implies
ca\leq cb

for all
a,b,c\in G
.

The group
G
is
partially ordered
if it is both left and right partially ordered.

In a right or left partially ordered group, if
a>e
and
b>e
, then
ab>e
.

The group
G
is
ordered
if it is partially ordered and the
partial order is a total order.

Let
G
be an ordered group. If
x\in G
and
e<x
, then

e<x<x^{2}<x^{3}<\cdots
and so
x
has infinite order.
Thus, every ordered group is torsion-free, but
not every torsion-free group is an ordered group.
However, Levi
[
4
,
5
]
proved that torsion-free abelian groups
and free groups of finite rank are orderable .

A subgroup of a right (resp. left) partially ordered group
is a right (resp. left) partially ordered group.
If
(G_{i},\leq_{i})
is a right (resp. left) partially ordered group for all
i\in I
,
then the direct product
\prod_{i\in I}G_{i}
is a right (resp. left) partially ordered group
with the
rectangular partial order

defined by
(a_{i})_{i\in I}\leq(b_{i})_{i\in I}
if
a_{i}\leq_{i}b_{i}
for all
i\in I
.

Let
X
be a well-ordered set and let
G
be a totally ordered additive abelian group.
The set
G^{X}
of functions
f:X\rightarrow G
is an additive abelian group
with addition defined by
(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)
.
The
lexicographical order
on
G^{X}
is a total order
that is defined as follows:

f\preceq_{\text{lex}}g
if
f=g
or if
f\neq g
and
f(y_{0})<g(y_{0})
, where

y_{0}\in Y
is the lower bound of the nonempty set
Y=\{y\in X:f(y)\neq g(y)\}
.
The element
y_{0}\in Y
exists because the set
X
is well-ordered.

For example, in the additive abelian group
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
, let

\mathbf{x}=\vectorxn,\qquad\mathbf{y}=\vectoryn,\qquad\mathbf{z}=\vectorzn.

Denote the zero vector in
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
by
\mathbf{0}
.
The rectangular partial order in
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
is defined by
\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{y}

if
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
For all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{Z}^{n}
, the open interval

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{Z}^{n}:\mathbf{x}<\mathbf{z}<\mathbf{y}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbf{Z}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\}:x_{i}\leq z_{i}\leq y_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\right\}

is an
n

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-x_{i}+1)-2
.
Thus, every open interval in
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
is finite with respect to the rectangular partial order.

Every lattice point in
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
is a function from the well-ordered set

\{1,\ldots,n\}
into
\mathbf{Z}
.
The
lexicographical order
on
\mathbf{Z}^{n}

\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\text{lex}}\mathbf{y}
if
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
or if

x_{i}<y_{i}
, where

in
\{1,\ldots,n\}

The lexicographical order in
\mathbf{Z}^{n}

\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{y}
implies
\mathbf{x}\preceq_{\text{lex}}\mathbf{y}
for all

\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
. But not conversely: For the vectors

\mathbf{e}_{1}=\left(\begin{matrix}1\\

\mathbf{e}_{2}=\left(\begin{matrix}0\\

\mathbf{e}_{2}\not\leq\mathbf{e}_{1}
.
The extension of
\leq

Let

.
For

.

We define the

If

The set

.

.

Theorem 6

Let

Let
\mathcal{J}

.
Let

The open interval

.

From the definition of the density

3.
Addition theorems for groups

In this section we prove an analogue of Shnirel’man’s inequality (Theorem
2
) for partially ordered
abelian and nonabelian groups.

Theorem 8
.

Let
G
be a right or left partially ordered group with positive cone

G^{+}=\{x\in G:x>e\}
.
Let
B
be a nonempty subset of
G^{+}
.
Let
J
be a downward closed subset of
G^{+}
such that

J^{*}=J\setminus B
is nonempty and, for all
x\in J^{*}
, the set

B^{*}(x)=\{b\in B:b<x\}=(e,x)\cap B

is nonempty and finite.
Then there is a set
\{J_{\ell}:\ell\in L\}
of pairwise disjoint nonempty subsets of
J^{*}

such that

J^{*}=\bigcup_{\ell\in L}J_{\ell}

and, for all
\ell\in L
, there exists
b_{\ell}\in B
such that

(i)

if
G
is right partially ordered, then

J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}
is a downward closed subset of the cone
G^{+}
,

(ii)

if
G
is left partially ordered, then

b_{\ell}^{-1}J_{\ell}
is a downward closed subset of the cone
G^{+}
.

Note: A stronger condition on the group
G
is that every interval
(e,x)
is finite and
that
(e,x)\cap B\neq\emptyset
for all
x\in J^{*}
. The condition that open intervals
be finite is a discreteness condition on a partially ordered group.
Here are two examples of groups in which every interval is infinite.
Consider the additive group
\mathbf{R}
with the usual ordering.
If
G
is a subgroup of
\mathbf{R}
that contains
\mathbf{Q}
,
then
(0,x)
is infinite for all
x>0
.
If
G
is an ordered divisible group, then for all
x>e
and for all
k\in\mathbf{N}

there exists
y\in G
such that
x=y^{k}
and so

e<y<y^{2}<\cdots<y^{k}=x

and
|(e,x)|\geq k-1
. Thus, the open interval
(e,x)
in
G
is infinite for all
x>e
.

Proof.

By Szpilrajn’s theorem (Theorem
5
),
there is a total order
\preceq_{tot}
on
G
that extends
\leq
.
Note that we do not assume that
G
is a right or left partially ordered group
with respect to
\preceq_{tot}
.

Let
G
be a right partially ordered group with respect to
\leq

and let
x\in J^{*}=J\setminus B
.
Because the set
B^{*}(x)
is nonempty and finite and
because
\preceq_{tot}
is a total order, there exists

b^{*}(x)\in B^{*}(x)
such that
b\preceq_{tot}b^{*}(x)
for all
b\in B^{*}(x)
.
Let

\{b^{*}(x):x\in J^{*}\}=\{b_{\ell}:\ell\in L\}

where
b_{\ell_{1}}\neq b_{\ell_{2}}
for all
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in L

with
\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}
.
For all
\ell\in L
, let

J_{\ell}=\left\{x\in J^{*}:b^{*}(x)=b_{\ell}\right\}.

For each
x\in J^{*}
there is a unique
\ell\in L
such that

b^{*}(x)=b_{\ell}
and so the sets
\{J_{\ell}:\ell\in L\}
are pairwise disjoint and

J^{*}=\bigcup_{\ell\in L}J_{\ell}.

If
x\in J_{\ell}
, then
b_{\ell}<x
and so
e<xb_{\ell}^{-1}
.
Thus,
J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}
is a subset of
G^{+}
.
To show that
J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}
is a downward closed
subset of
G^{+}
,
we must prove that if
a\in G^{+}
and
a<xb_{\ell}^{-1}

for some
x\in J_{\ell}
, then
a\in J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}
.
Equivalently, we must prove that
w=ab_{\ell}\in J_{\ell}
.

Because
e<a
and
e<b_{\ell}
, we have

e<b_{\ell}<ab_{\ell}=w<x.

The set
J
is a downward closed subset of
G^{+}
and

x\in J_{\ell}\subseteq J^{*}\subseteq J.

It follows that

w\in J.

Because the total order
\preceq_{tot}
extends the partial order
\leq
,
the inequality
b_{\ell}<w
also implies
b_{\ell}\prec_{tot}w
.
If
w\in J\cap B
, then
w<x
implies
w\in B^{*}(x)
and so

b_{\ell}\prec_{tot}w\preceq_{tot}b^{*}(x)=b_{\ell}

which is absurd. Therefore,
w\in J\setminus B=J^{*}
.

Because
x\in J_{\ell}
, the inequality

b_{\ell}<w<x

implies

b_{\ell}\preceq_{tot}b^{*}(w)\preceq_{tot}b^{*}(x)=b_{\ell}

and so

b_{\ell}=b^{*}(w)=b^{*}(x).

Therefore,
ab_{\ell}=w\in J_{\ell}
and

J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}
is a downward closed subset of
G^{+}

for all
\ell\in L
.
This completes the proof when
G
is right partially ordered.
The proof is similar when
G
is left partially ordered.
∎

Theorem 9
.

Let
G
be a right or left partially ordered group with positive cone

G^{+}=\{x\in G:x>e\}
and let
\mathcal{J}
be the set of all downward closed
nonempty finite subsets of
G^{+}
.
Let
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}
be the density defined by
\mathcal{J}
on subsets of
G^{+}
.
Let
A
and
B
be subsets of
G^{+}
with densities

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)=\alpha
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(B)=\beta
.
Suppose that, for all
J\in\mathcal{J}
and
x\in J\setminus B
, the set

B^{*}(x)=\{b\in B:b<x\}=B\cap(e,x)

is nonempty and finite.

(i)

If
G
is right partially ordered, then

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(AB)\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta.

(ii)

If
G
is left partially ordered, then

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(BA)\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta.

Proof.

Let
AB=C
. Recall that
A\subseteq C
and
B\subseteq C
. We must prove that

(3)

\frac{|C\cap J|}{|J|}\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta

for all
J\in\mathcal{J}
.

If
J\in\mathcal{J}
and
J\subseteq B
, then
J=B\cap J=C\cap J

and

\frac{|C\cap J|}{|J|}=\frac{|J|}{|J|}=1\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta.

Thus, we can assume that
J\not\subseteq B
and so

J^{*}=J\setminus B

is a nonempty subset of
G^{+}
.
By Theorem
8
,
if
G
is a right partially ordered group,
then there is a set
of pairwise disjoint nonempty finite sets
\left\{J_{\ell}:\ell\in L\right\}

such that
J^{*}=\bigcup_{\ell\in L}J_{\ell}
and, for all
\ell\in L
,
an element
b_{\ell}\in B
such that the set
J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}
is a downward closed
nonempty finite subset of
G^{+}
, that is,
J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}
.

The set
J
is the disjoint union of the following finite sets:

J=(B\cap J)\cup J^{*}=(B\cap J)\cup\bigcup_{\ell\in L}J_{\ell}.

Since
B\subseteq C
, we have
B\cap J\subseteq C\cap J
,
and
C\cap J
is the disjoint union of the following finite sets:

C\cap J=(B\cap J)\cup\bigcup_{\ell\in L}\left(C\cap J_{\ell}\right).

We shall compute a lower bound for the cardinality of
C\cap J_{\ell}
.
If

a\in A\cap J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1},

then

ab_{\ell}\in J_{\ell}\qqand ab_{\ell}\in C

and so

ab_{\ell}\in C\cap J_{\ell}.

Therefore,

\left(A\cap\left(J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}\right)\right)b_{\ell}\subseteq C\cap J_{\ell}.

Because
J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}\in\mathcal{J}
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)=\alpha
, we have

\displaystyle|C\cap J_{\ell}|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\left(A\cap\left(J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}\right)\right)b_{\ell}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|A\cap\left(J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\left|J_{\ell}b_{\ell}^{-1}\right|

\displaystyle=\alpha|J_{\ell}|.

It follows that

\displaystyle|C\cap J|

\displaystyle=|B\cap J|+\sum_{\ell\in L}|C\cap J_{\ell}|

\displaystyle\geq|B\cap J|+\alpha\sum_{\ell\in L}|J_{\ell}|

\displaystyle=|B\cap J|+\alpha|J^{*}|

\displaystyle=|B\cap J|+\alpha\left(|J|-|B\cap J|\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)|B\cap J|+\alpha|J|

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha)\beta|J|+\alpha|J|

\displaystyle=(\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta)|J|

and so

\frac{|C\cap J|}{|J|}\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta

and

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(C)=\inf_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\frac{|C\cap J|}{|J|}\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta.

This completes the proof when
G
is right partially ordered.
The proof is similar when
G
is left partially ordered.
∎

Theorem 10
.

Let
G
be a partially ordered group with positive cone

G^{+}=\{x\in G:x>e\}
and let
\mathcal{J}
be the set of all downward closed
nonempty finite subsets of
G^{+}
.
Let
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}
be the density defined by
\mathcal{J}
on subsets of
G^{+}
.
For
h\geq 2
, let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{h}
be subsets of
G^{+}
with

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A_{i})=\alpha_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
.
Suppose that, for all
J\in\mathcal{J}
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
,
and for all
x\in J\setminus A_{i}
, the set

A_{i}^{*}(x)=\{a_{i}\in A_{i}:a_{i}<x\}=A_{i}\cap(e,x)

is nonempty and finite.
Then

1-\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}\left(A_{1}\cdots A_{h}\right)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{h}\left(1-\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}\left(A_{i}\right)\right).

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
9

by induction on
h
.
∎

Theorem 11
.

Let
G
be a partially ordered group,
let
G^{+}=\{x\in G:x>e\}
be the positive cone in
G
, and
let
\mathcal{J}
be the set of all downward closed nonempty finite subsets of
G^{+}
.
Let
A
be a nonempty subset of
G^{+}
such that,
for all
J\in\mathcal{J}
and
x\in J\setminus A
, the set

A^{*}(x)=\{a\in A:a<x\}=A\cap(e,x)

is nonempty and finite.
Let
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}
be the density defined by
\mathcal{J}
on subsets of
G^{+}
.
If
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)=\alpha>0
, then
A
is a basis for
G
.

Proof.

Let
x\in G^{+}
. If
x
is an atom in
G
, that is,
if the open interval
(e,x)
is empty, then the set
\{x\}
is
a downward closed nonempty finite subset of
G^{+}
and so
\{x\}\in J
.
We have

0<\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)\leq\frac{|A\cap\{x\}|}{|\{x\}|}=|A\cap\{x\}|

and so
x\in A\subseteq A^{h}
for all
h\geq 2
.

Let
h\geq 2
. Applying Theorem
10

with
A_{i}=A

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,h\}
, we obtain

1-\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A^{h})\leq(1-\alpha)^{h}

and so there exists
h_{0}
such

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A^{h_{0}})>\frac{1}{2}.

If
(e,x)
is not empty, then, by Theorem
7
,
there exist
a,b\in A^{h_{0}}
such that
x=ab\in A^{2h_{0}}

and so
A
is a basis for
G
of order
2h_{0}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

4.
Sums of sets of lattice points

We return to additive number theory for lattice points.
Let
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
be the partially ordered additive abelian group of lattice points
with the rectangular order.
The positive cone of
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
is the semigroup
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}

of nonzero nonnegative lattice points.

The set
A
of nonnegative lattice points is a

basis of order
h
for
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}

if
hA=\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
The set
A
is a
basis
for
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}

if
A
is a basis of order
h
for
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
for some
h
.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
be the partially ordered additive abelian group of lattice points
with the rectangular order and with positive cone
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{J}
be the set of all downward closed
nonempty finite subsets of
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}

and let
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}
be the density on
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
defined by
\mathcal{J}
.
If
A
and
B
are subsets of
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
with densities

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)=\alpha
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(B)=\beta
, then

(4)

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A+B)\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta.

Proof.

We must prove that

\frac{\left|(A+B)\cap J\right|}{\left|J\right|}\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta

for all
J\in\mathcal{J}
.
Because
A\subseteq A+B
, if
\beta=0
, then

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A+B)\geq\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)=\alpha=\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta.

Thus, we can assume that
\beta>0
.

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let
\mathbf{e}_{i}
be the standard unit vector whose
j
th coordinate
is the Kronecker delta
\delta_{i,j}
. If
\mathbf{x}
is a nonzero vector in
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
then
x_{i}\geq 1
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and so
\mathbf{e}_{i}\leq\mathbf{x}
.
It follows that if
J
is a downward closed nonempty subset of
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
then

\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n}\}\cap J\neq\emptyset.

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the unit vector
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is an atom
and the set
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}
is a downward closed finite nonempty
subset of
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Thus,
\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}\in\mathcal{J}
.
We have

0<\beta=\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(B)\leq\frac{\left|B\cap\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}\right|}{\left|\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\}\right|}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\mathbf{e}_{i}\in B$}\\
0&\text{if $\mathbf{e}_{i}\notin B$ }\end{cases}

and so
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in B
and

\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n}\}\subseteq B.

Therefore,

B\cap J\neq\emptyset
for all
J\in\mathcal{J}
.
If
B\cap J=J
, then
B\subseteq A+B
implies

\frac{\left|(A+B)\cap J\right|}{\left|J\right|}\geq\frac{\left|B\cap J\right|}{\left|J\right|}=\frac{\left|J\right|}{\left|J\right|}=1\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta.

Thus, we can assume that
B\cap J\neq J
and so
J\setminus B\neq\emptyset
.
If
\mathbf{x}\in J\setminus B
, then
\mathbf{x}>\mathbf{e}_{i}>\mathbf{0}

for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in B^{*}(\mathbf{x})=B\cap(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x})
.
Every interval in
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
is finite and so
B^{*}(\mathbf{x})
is nonempty and finite
for all
\mathbf{x}\in J\setminus B
.
It follows from Theorem
9
that

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A+B)\geq\alpha+\beta-\alpha\beta.
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 13
.

Let
\mathbf{Z}^{n}
be the partially ordered additive abelian group of lattice points
with the rectangular order.
Let
\mathcal{J}
be the set of all downward closed
nonempty finite subsets of
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}

and let

be the density on

.
If
A
is a subset of

with density

\sigma_{\mathcal{J}}(A)>0
, then

is a basis for
the semigroup
\mathbf{N}_{0}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}

Proof.

This follows from Theorems

and

.

5.
Open problems

It is natural to ask what other results about the Shnirel’man density of
sets of positive integers extend to lattice points and to arbitrary
abelian and nonabelian partially ordered groups.
For example, Mann proved an addition theorem for finite sets of integers
that leads to an important and best possible
strengthening of Shnirel’man’s addition theorem
(Theorem
2
).

Theorem 14

(Mann
[
7
]
)
.

Let
A
and
B
be sets of positive integers with Shnirel’man densities

\sigma(A)=\alpha
and
\sigma(B)=\beta
. Then

\sigma(A+B)\geq\min(1,\alpha+\beta).

Dyson
[
2
]
refined Mann’s combinatorial result
about finite sumsets (see Nathanson
[
8
]
).
Do these results generalize to partially ordered groups?
