Lagrange-like interpolation in unitary rings, Boolean algebras and Boolean posets
Ivan Chajda, Helmut Länger

Abstract
It is known that every function with a finite support over a given field can be interpolated by means of the Lagrangian polynomial. The question is if a similar interpolation is possible if one considers a unitary ring or a Boolean algebra instead of a field. We get a positive answer to this question provided the similarity type of the algebra in question is enriched with one more unary operation, the so-called Baaz delta. We get an explicit construction of this interpolation polynomial in both the cases. When going to Boolean posets, we have a lack of operations but these can be substituted by the operators Min ⁡ U \Min U and Max ⁡ L \Max L . Hence, we generalize also the Baaz delta for posets as an operator and then we can derive an explicit interpolation term constructed by means of these operators also for Boolean posets.

1
Introduction

The aim of this short note is to show that a Lagrange-like interpolation of a given function with finite support by a certain polynomial is possible also in unitary rings and Boolean algebras when their signature is enriched with a certain unary operation, the so-called Baaz delta.

Having a field
\mathbf{K}=(K,+,\cdot)
, every function with a finite support, i.e.
f\colon\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}\to K
for some distinct elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
of
K
, can be interpolated by a polynomial
p(x)
over
\mathbf{K}
where
p(a_{i})=f(a_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. The most profound interpolation is by means of the so-called Lagrangian polynomial, see e.g.
[
7
]
. For the reader’s convenience, we recall it.

In the following we use
Kronecker’s delta

\delta_{ij}
defined by
\delta_{ij}:=1
if
i=j
and
\delta_{ij}:=0
otherwise and the
Baaz delta

2)

\Delta
defined by
\Delta(0):=0
and
\Delta(x):=1
otherwise.

Theorem 1.1
.

(Lagrange’s interpolation formula)
Let
\mathbf{K}=(K,+,\cdot)
be a field,
n>1
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
different elements of
K
and
f\colon K\to K
and define
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n},p\colon K\to K
by

\displaystyle p_{i}(x)

\displaystyle\>=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{x-a_{j}}{a_{i}-a_{j}}\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(a_{i})p_{i}(x)

for all
x\in K
. Then
p(x)
is a polynomial over
\mathbf{K}
satisfying
p(a_{k})=f(a_{k})
for
k=1,\ldots,n
.

Proof.

We have
p_{i}(a_{k})=\delta_{ik}
for
i,k=1,\ldots,n
and hence

p(a_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(a_{i})p_{i}(a_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(a_{i})\delta_{ik}=f(a_{k})

for
k=1,\ldots,n
.
∎

In the next sections we will modify the construction of the polynomials
p_{i}(x)
in such a way that the interpolation polynomial
p(x)
can be derived similarly as in Theorem
1.1
also for unitary rings and Boolean algebras.

2
Interpolation in unitary rings

For unitary rings we can formulate our interpolation formula as follows:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(R,+,\cdot,0,1)
be a unitary ring,
n>1
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
different elements of
R
and
f\colon R\to R
and define
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n},p\colon R\to R
by

\displaystyle p_{i}(x)

\displaystyle:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\Delta(x-a_{j})\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(a_{i})p_{i}(x)

for all
x\in B
. Then
p(x)
is a polynomial over the enriched unitary ring
(R,+,\cdot,\Delta,0,1)
satisfying
p(a_{k})=f(a_{k})
for
k=1,\ldots,n
.

Proof.

It is evident that
p(x)
is a polynomial over
(R,+,\cdot,\Delta,0,1)
. We have
p_{i}(a_{k})=\delta_{ik}
for
i,k=1,\ldots,n
and hence

p(a_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(a_{i})p_{i}(a_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(a_{i})\delta_{ik}=f(a_{k})

for
k=1,\ldots,n
.
∎

Note that the function
p(x)
from Theorem
2.1
is in fact a polynomial over the enriched unitary ring
(R,+,\cdot,\Delta,0,1)
, but it need not be a polynomial over
(R,+,\cdot,0,1)
.

Example 2.2
.

Consider a unitary ring
(R,+,\cdot,1)
,
a,b,c,d,e,g,h,i\in R
such that
a,b,c,d
are different and a mapping
f\colon R\to R
with

\displaystyle f(a)

\displaystyle=e,

\displaystyle f(b)

\displaystyle=g,

\displaystyle f(c)

\displaystyle=h,

\displaystyle f(d)

\displaystyle=i.

In accordance with Theorem
2.1
define
p_{1},\ldots,p_{4},p\colon R\to R
by

\displaystyle p_{1}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x-b)\cdot\Delta(x-c)\cdot\Delta(x-d),

\displaystyle p_{2}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x-a)\cdot\Delta(x-c)\cdot\Delta(x-d),

\displaystyle p_{3}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x-a)\cdot\Delta(x-b)\cdot\Delta(x-d),

\displaystyle p_{4}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x-a)\cdot\Delta(x-b)\cdot\Delta(x-c),

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle:=e\cdot p_{1}(x)+g\cdot p_{2}(x)+h\cdot p_{3}(x)+i\cdot p_{4}(x)

for all
x\in R
. Then really

\displaystyle p(a)

\displaystyle=e,

\displaystyle p(b)

\displaystyle=g,

\displaystyle p(c)

\displaystyle=h,

\displaystyle p(d)

\displaystyle=i.

3
Interpolation in Boolean algebras

We continue with the case of Boolean algebras.

It is well-known that for Boolean algebras
(B,\vee,\wedge,{}^{\prime},0,1)
the corresponding Boolean ring
(B,+,\wedge,0,1)
is an idempotent unitary ring satisfying the identity
x+x\approx 0
and hence also the identity
x-y\approx x+y
, see e.g.
[
2
]
. The binary operation
+
defined by

x+y:=(x^{\prime}\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge y^{\prime})

for all
x,y\in B
is called
symmetric difference
. Now
x+y=0
is equivalent to
x=y
since
(B,+,0)
is an involutory group. Hence we can derive the interpolation formula for Boolean algebras from that valid for unitary rings stated in Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(B,\vee,\wedge,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a Boolean algebra,
n>1
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
different elements of
B
and
f\colon B\to B
and define
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n},p\colon B\to B
by

\displaystyle p_{i}(x)

\displaystyle:=\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\Delta(x+a_{j})\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle:=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(a_{i})\wedge p_{i}(x)\big)

for all
x\in B
. Then
p(x)
is a polynomial over the enriched Boolean algebra
(B,\vee,\wedge,{}^{\prime},\Delta,0,1)
satisfying
p(a_{k})=f(a_{k})
for
k=1,\ldots,n
.

Remark 3.2
.

Instead of

p(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(a_{i})\wedge p_{i}(x)\big)

we can take

p(x):=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(a_{i})\wedge p_{i}(x)\big)

since

\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(a_{i})\wedge\delta_{ik}\big)=f(a_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(a_{i})\wedge\delta_{ik}\big)

where the sum sign stands for the symmetric difference.

Hence the function
p(x)
from Theorem
3.1
is indeed a polynomial over the enriched Boolean algebra
(B,\vee,\wedge,{}^{\prime},\Delta,0,1)
.

Example 3.3
.

The lattice depicted in Fig. 1

is the
16
-element Boolean algebra
(B,\vee,\wedge,{}^{\prime},0,1)
. Now consider a mapping
f\colon B\to B
with

\displaystyle f(a)

\displaystyle=c^{\prime},

\displaystyle f(g)

\displaystyle=a^{\prime},

\displaystyle f(b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=h,

\displaystyle f(1)

\displaystyle=e^{\prime}.

In accordance with Theorem
3.1
define
p_{1},\ldots,p_{4},p\colon B\to B
by

\displaystyle p_{1}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x+g)\wedge\Delta(x+b^{\prime})\wedge\Delta(x+1),

\displaystyle p_{2}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x+a)\wedge\Delta(x+b^{\prime})\wedge\Delta(x+1),

\displaystyle p_{3}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x+a)\wedge\Delta(x+g)\wedge\Delta(x+1),

\displaystyle p_{4}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x+a)\wedge\Delta(x+g)\wedge\Delta(x+b^{\prime}),

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle:=\big(c^{\prime}\wedge p_{1}(x)\big)\vee\big(a^{\prime}\wedge p_{2}(x)\big)\vee\big(h\wedge p_{3}(x)\big)\vee\big(e^{\prime}\wedge p_{4}(x)\big)

for all
x\in B
. Then really

\displaystyle p(a)

\displaystyle=c^{\prime},

\displaystyle p(g)

\displaystyle=a^{\prime},

\displaystyle p(b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=h,

\displaystyle p(1)

\displaystyle=e^{\prime}.

4
Interpolation in Boolean posets

Now we turn our attention to Boolean posets (alias Boolean ordered sets, see e.g.
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
). For this purpose let us recall several concepts introduced already in our previous paper
[
3
]
.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
be a poset,
a,b\in P
and
A,B\subseteq P
. Then
\Max A
and
\Min A
denote the set of all maximal and minimal elements of
A
, respectively. We define

\displaystyle A\leq B

\displaystyle\text{ if }x\leq y\text{ for all }x\in A\text{ and all }y\in B,

\displaystyle L(A)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in P\mid x\leq A\},

\displaystyle U(A)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in P\mid A\leq x\}.

Here and in the following we identify singletons with their unique element. Instead of
L(\{a\})
,
L(\{a,b\})
,
L(A\cup\{a\})
,
L(A\cup B)
and
L\big(U(A)\big)
we simply write
L(a)
,
L(a,b)
,
L(A,a)
,
L(A,B)
and
LU(A)
. Analogously we proceed in similar cases.

Hence, by the binary operator
\Max L(x,y)
and
\Min U(x,y)
we mean the mapping from
P^{2}
to
2^{P}
assigning to each
(x,y)\in P^{2}
the set of all maximal elements in the lower cone
L(x,y)
of
x
and
y
and the set of all minimal elements in the upper cone
U(x,y)
of
x
and
y
, respectively. Of course, if the poset in question is a lattice then
\Max L(x,y)=x\wedge y
and
\Min U(x,y)=x\vee y
. Thus also in the more general case of a poset,
\Max L(x,y)
and
\Min U(x,y)
should play the role of
x\wedge y
and
x\vee y
, respectively.

Moreover, if there exists
\sup(a,b)
then
\Min U(a,b)=a\vee b
and, similarly, if
\inf(a,b)
exists then
\Max L(a,b)=a\wedge b
. Of course, if
(P,\leq,0,1)
is a bounded poset then
a\vee 0=a
,
a\vee 1=1
,
a\wedge 0=0
and
a\wedge 1=a
.

Let
P
be set and
\,{}^{\prime}
a unary operation on
P
. Then
\,{}^{\prime}
is called an
involution
if it satisfies the identity
(x^{\prime})^{\prime}\approx x
. If
(P,\leq)
is a poset then
\,{}^{\prime}
is called
antitone
if
x,y\in P
and
x\leq y
imply
f(y)\leq f(x)
. If
(P,\leq,0,1)
is a bounded poset then
\,{}^{\prime}
is called a
complementation
if it satisfies the identities
L(x,x^{\prime})\approx 0
and
U(x,x^{\prime})\approx 1
. A
poset

(P,\leq)
is called
distributive
(see e.g.
[
6
]
) if it satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions:

\displaystyle L\big(U(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx LU\big(L(x,z),L(y,z)\big),

\displaystyle UL\big(U(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx U\big(L(x,z),L(y,z)\big),

\displaystyle U\big(L(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx UL\big(U(x,z),U(y,z)\big),

\displaystyle LU\big(L(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx L\big(U(x,z),U(y,z)\big).

A
Boolean poset
is a distributive bounded poset with a complementation. It can be shown that the complementation of a Boolean poset is unique and it is an antitone involution (see e.g.
[
3
]
).

For Boolean posets
(B,\vee,\wedge,{}^{\prime},0,1)
we define a binary operator
+\colon B^{2}\to 2^{B}
by

x+y:=\Min U\big(L(x^{\prime},y),L(x,y^{\prime})\big)

for all
x,y\in B
. Observe that
x+y=\Min U\big(\Max L(x^{\prime},y),\Max L(x,y^{\prime})\big)
for all
x,y\in B
.

An elementary but important result for our study of interpolation is the following proposition which shows that the binary operator
+
in Boolean posets satisfies a similar property as the symmetric difference in Boolean algebras.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(B,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a Boolean poset and
a,b\in B
. Then
a+b=0
if and only if
a=b
.

Proof.

First assume
a+b=0
. Then
\Min U\big(L(a^{\prime},b),L(a,b^{\prime})\big)=0
and hence
L(a^{\prime},b)=L(a,b^{\prime})=0
. Now we conclude

\displaystyle a^{\prime}

\displaystyle\in U(a^{\prime})=U(0,a^{\prime})=U\big(L(a,b^{\prime}),a^{\prime}\big)=UL\big(U(a,a^{\prime}),U(b^{\prime},a^{\prime})\big)=UL\big(1,U(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)=

\displaystyle=ULU(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=U(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\subseteq U(b^{\prime})

and hence
b^{\prime}\leq a^{\prime}
, i.e.,
a\leq b
. Interchanging the roles of
a
and
b
we obtain
b\leq a
and therefore
a=b
. If, conversely
a=b
then

a+b=a+a=\Min U\big(L(a^{\prime},a),L(a,a^{\prime})\big)=\Min U(0,0)=0.

∎

It is worth noticing that
a+b
need not be an element of
B
but may be an arbitrary subset of
B
.

Although every finite uniquely complemented lattice is a Boolean algebra, see e.g.
[
4
]
, this is not the case for posets.

The next example shows that the assertion from Proposition
4.1
need not hold if the complemented poset in question is not distributive.

Example 4.2
.

The poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
visualized in Fig. 2

is not distributive since

L\big(U(a,b),c\big)=L(d^{\prime},1,c)=L(c)\neq 0=LU(0,0)=LU\big(L(a,c),L(b,c)\big),

but the unary operation
\,{}^{\prime}
defined by the table

\begin{array}[]{l|llllllllll}x&0&a&b&c&d&a^{\prime}&b^{\prime}&c^{\prime}&d^{\prime}&1\\
\hline\cr x^{\prime}&1&a^{\prime}&b^{\prime}&c^{\prime}&d^{\prime}&a&b&c&d&0\end{array}

is a complementation on it. We have

b+c=\Min U\big(L(b^{\prime},c),L(b,c^{\prime})\big)=\Min U(0,0)=\min U(0)=0

though
b\neq c
.

The concept of Baaz delta can be extended for a poset
(P,\leq,0,1)
as follows: We define
\Delta(0):=0
and
\Delta(A):=1
for each subset
A
of
P
different from
\{0\}
. In this case it is not an operation but a unary operator on the set
P
. A term constructed by the afore mentioned operators
\Max L
,
\Min U
,
′
and
\Delta
will be called an
operator term
over
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},\Delta,0,1)
. In particular, also the operator
+
as defined above is an operator term over
\mathbf{P}
.

For a Boolean poset enriched with the operator
\Delta
we can state and prove the following interpolation formula.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
(B,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a Boolean poset,
n>1
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
different elements of
B
and
f\colon B\to B
and define
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\colon B\to B
and
p\colon B\to 2^{B}
by

\displaystyle p_{i}(x)

\displaystyle:=\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\Delta(x+a_{j})\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle:=\Min U\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(a_{i})\wedge p_{i}(x)\big)\Big)

for all
x\in B
. Then
p(x)
is an operator term over
(B,\leq,{}^{\prime},\Delta,0,1)
satisfying
p(a_{k})=f(a_{k})
for
k=1,\ldots,n
.

Remark 4.4
.

Observe that

\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\Delta(x+a_{j})

can be written in the form

\Max L\big(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\Delta(x+a_{j})\big)

and
f(a_{i})\wedge p_{i}(x)
in the form
\Max L\big(f(a_{i}),p_{i}(x)\big)
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Clearly,

\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\Delta(x+a_{j})

is defined because every member
\Delta(x+a_{j})
of this intersection equals either
0
or
1
. Thus we have
p_{i}(a_{k})=\delta_{ik}
for
i,k=1,\ldots,n
and hence

p(a_{k})=\Min U\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(a_{i})\wedge p_{i}(a_{k})\big)\Big)=\Min U\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\big(f(a_{i})\wedge\delta_{ik}\big)\Big)=\Min U\big(f(a_{k})\big)=f(a_{k})

for
k=1,\ldots,n
.
∎

That the interpolation formula from Theorem
4.3
really works for Boolean posets which need not be Boolean algebras is shown in the following example.

Example 4.5
.

Consider the Boolean poset
(B,\leq,0,1)
depicted in Fig. 3

One can see that e.g.
b+c^{\prime}
is not an element of
B
, namely

b+c^{\prime}=U\big(L(b^{\prime},c^{\prime}),L(b,c)\big)=\Min U(a,d)=\{b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\}.

Now consider a mapping
f\colon B\to B
with

\displaystyle f(0)

\displaystyle=a,

\displaystyle f(a)

\displaystyle=c,

\displaystyle f(b)

\displaystyle=d^{\prime},

\displaystyle f(c^{\prime})

\displaystyle=1.

In accordance with Theorem
4.3
define
p_{1},\ldots,p_{4}\colon B\to B
and
p\colon B\to 2^{B}
by

\displaystyle p_{1}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x+a)\wedge\Delta(x+b)\wedge\Delta(x+c^{\prime}),

\displaystyle p_{2}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x+0)\wedge\Delta(x+b)\wedge\Delta(x+c^{\prime}),

\displaystyle p_{3}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x+0)\wedge\Delta(x+a)\wedge\Delta(x+c^{\prime}),

\displaystyle p_{4}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Delta(x+0)\wedge\Delta(x+a)\wedge\Delta(x+b),

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle:=\Min U(\{a\wedge p_{1}(x),c\wedge p_{2}(x),d^{\prime}\wedge p_{3}(x),1\wedge p_{4}(x)\})

for all
x\in B
. Then really

\displaystyle p(0)

\displaystyle=a,

\displaystyle p(a)

\displaystyle=c,

\displaystyle p(b)

\displaystyle=d^{\prime},

\displaystyle p(c^{\prime})

\displaystyle=1.

Remark 4.6
.

Having in mind the non-distributive complemented poset from Example
4.5
, we see immediately that a similar interpolation formula is not possible in this case since
p_{i}(a_{k})
need not be equal to Kronecker’s delta
\delta_{ik}
, namely, if
n=2
,
a_{1}=b
,
a_{2}=c
and
f(a_{1})\neq 0
then

\displaystyle p_{1}(a_{1})

\displaystyle=\Delta(b+c)=\Delta(0)=0\neq 1=\delta_{11},

\displaystyle p_{2}(a_{1})

\displaystyle=\Delta(b+b)=\Delta(0)=0,

\displaystyle p(a_{1})

\displaystyle=\Min U\big(f(a_{1})\wedge p_{1}(a_{1}),f(a_{2})\wedge p_{2}(a_{1})\big)=\Min U(0,0)=0\neq f(a_{1}).
