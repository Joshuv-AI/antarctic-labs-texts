Spatio-temporal evolution characteristics of cultivated land based on topographic gradient in typical agro-pastoral zones of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau
Authors: Hua Wu, Zelin Zhang, Jianwei Zhou, Jiannan Zhang, Xiangyun Kong, Runchi Wang, Yongqing Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the spatiotemporal evolution characteristics of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley based on terrain gradient analysis, aiming to provide a scientific reference for the rational utilization of cultivated land in this region. By integrating long-term high-resolution land use data with methods including terrain gradient factor analysis, kernel density analysis, PLUS model and standard deviation ellipse theory. we analyzed the spatiotemporal changes, topographic gradient differentiation patterns, and evolution trends of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley from 1980 to 2020. The results showed that: (1) Cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley exhibited a net loss of 119.24 km² from 1980 to 2020, predominantly converted to grassland, water area, and built-up area. (2) Higher cultivated land density in river valleys contrasted with sparse distribution in mountainous zones. The spatial centroid shifted 2.277 km northwestward, accompanied by progressive spatial contraction. (3) Cultivated land was concentrated on sunlit slopes of hills and plains below 2,772 m altitude and 17.42° slope gradient, where human activities induced significant land use transitions. (4) Population density was the dominant factor influencing cultivated land changes, with a contribution of 0.12. The increase in cultivated land was primarily distributed along the Datong River and the Yellow River, while cultivated land decreased rapidly in the Xining section of the Huangshui River. Location-specific management strategies are imperative to optimize resource allocation and ensure sustainable agricultural development under heterogeneous environmental constraints. Given the significant terrain gradient differentiation in the distribution and change of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley, sustainable management practices should adhere to local conditions to promote the sustainable development of cultivated land resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55913-y.Keywords: Cultivated land, Topographic gradient, Driving factors, PLUS model, Hehuang ValleySubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Cultivated land serves as a fundamental resource for human survival and development, fulfilling multiple functions including food production, ecological security, and social stability1. As the primary carrier of agricultural production, the sustainable utilization of cultivated land is intrinsically linked to national food security and ecological civilization construction2,3. Currently, global population growth and accelerated industrialization have posed severe challenges to cultivated land resources4,5. On one hand, urban expansion and infrastructure development have triggered drastic reductions in cultivated land area4,5. On the other hand, climate change and frequent extreme weather events exacerbate the risk of cultivated land degradation6. China, home to over 20% of the global population, possesses less than 40% of the world’s per capita cultivated land7, with high-quality arable land predominantly concentrated in ecologically sensitive regions, creating pronounced human-environment conflicts8. Empirical studies demonstrate that China’s total cultivated land has continuously declined since the 21st century, accompanied by intensified fragmentation and spatial heterogeneity, which threaten regional food security and agricultural sustainability7,9. Against this backdrop, investigating the spatiotemporal dynamics and driving mechanisms of cultivated land change holds critical practical significance for optimizing territorial spatial planning and formulating differentiated conservation policies10.

The study of cultivated land spatiotemporal evolution has evolved into a multidimensional and multiscale research framework over decades of development11,12. Early investigations primarily focused on macro-level analyses of cultivated land quantity and spatial patterns, leveraging remote sensing and GIS technologies to enable dynamic monitoring of cultivated land cover changes13,14. With the integration of landscape ecology theory, landscape metrics such as patch density and aggregation index have been widely applied to characterize cultivated land fragmentation and spatial agglomeration features15. In recent years, machine learning methods—including geodetector, random forest, and PLUS model—have emerged as core tools for identifying cultivated land driving mechanisms, owing to their robust nonlinear fitting capabilities15,16. Studies have demonstrated that socioeconomic factors (e.g., population density, GDP, and transportation accessibility) generally exhibit higher contribution rates to cultivated land changes than natural factors (e.g., topography and climate)17,18. This pattern is particularly prominent in rapidly urbanizing regions, where cultivated land is often preferentially encroached upon by high-economic-value land uses19. Notably, existing research predominantly concentrates on economically developed regions18,20. Additionally, traditional studies typically adopt administrative units as analytical scales12,17, overlooking the constraining effects of topographic gradients on cultivated land distribution19. In reality, topographic factors (e.g., altitude, slope, and aspect) profoundly shape the spatial differentiation patterns of cultivated land by influencing hydrothermal conditions and farming costs19,21,22. Therefore, there is an urgent need to uncover the underlying mechanisms of cultivated land changes from a topographic gradient perspective to provide theoretical support for differentiated land use planning.

As an “ecological corridor” on the northeastern margin of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, the Hehuang Valley serves as one of Qinghai Province’s most critical grain production bases, with its cultivated land resources playing an irreplaceable role in sustaining regional food security and agricultural development23. Since China’s reform and opening-up, the implementation of national strategies such as the Western Development Program and the Grain-for-Green Initiative has driven profound economic transformation and population agglomeration in the Hehuang Valley19,24. This has led to a sustained increase in regional grain demand, while intensive human activities have exposed cultivated land resources to multiple risks25. Currently, the Hehuang Valley faces a critical juncture between ecological conservation and economic development. Achieving sustainable utilization of cultivated land under the premise of ensuring food security has emerged as an urgent scientific challenge. This study, taking topographic gradients as the analytical starting point, systematically investigates the spatiotemporal evolution characteristics and driving mechanisms of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley from 1980 to 2020. It further analyzes the impacts of cultivated land area changes on regional ecosystem services, aiming to provide theoretical foundations and decision-making support for cultivated land conservation and optimized territorial spatial configurations in cold alpine ecologically fragile areas.

Materials and methods

Overview of the study area

The Hehuang Valley, situated in the transitional zone between the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and the Loess Plateau26, lies at the northeastern margin of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau as an agro-pastoral ecotone (Fig. 1). It is an alluvial valley formed by the Yellow River and its tributary, the Huangshui River. Administratively, it encompasses 17 county-level administrative regions across Xining City, Haidong City, Haibei Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Huangnan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, and Hainan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture in Qinghai Province23, serving as a critical component of the Lanzhou-Xining Urban Agglomeration. Covering an area of approximately 35,261.11 km², the Hehuang Valley accounts for 4.50% of Qinghai Province’s total land area. With altitudes ranging from 1,654 to 5,213 m, its landforms are dominated by high mountains (over 3,500 m altitude), which constitute approximately 72.40% of the total area, with the remaining regions comprising plains, hills, and terraces26. Due to the flat terrain and fertile soils of the river valleys along the Yellow River, Huangshui River, and Datong River27, over 80% of Qinghai Province’s cultivated land and more than 70% of its population are concentrated in the Hehuang Valley. The Hehuang Valley features a plateau temperate arid climate28, with a mean annual temperature of 5–9 °C and mean annual precipitation of 250–500 mm. Characterized by a mild climate and concurrent heat and rainfall (aligning with the growing season), it primarily cultivates wheat, highland barley, and rapeseed, making it one of the regions on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau with exceptionally favorable natural conditions. By the end of 2020, the Hehuang Valley had a permanent resident population of 4.6308 million and a GDP of 202.494 billion yuan, accounting for approximately 78.17% and 67.37% of Qinghai Province’s totals, respectively.

Data sources

The data sources used in this study are as follows: (1) The land use data were obtained from the Resources and Environment Science Data Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (http://www.resdc.cn/), with a spatial resolution of 30 m. These data were derived from the visual interpretation of Landsat remote sensing imagery, featuring an overall accuracy exceeding 90% 8, which met the research requirements. (2) The geomorphic data were sourced from the Topographic Atlas of the People’s Republic of China (1:1,000,000); the Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data were obtained from the Geospatial Data Cloud (http://www.gscloud.cn/) with a spatial resolution of 30 m, from which slope and aspect were further processed. (3) Annual mean humidity, annual mean land surface temperature, NDVI, annual mean wind speed, accumulated temperature ≥ 10 °C, GDP per unit area, annual sunshine duration, annual evaporation, annual mean precipitation, annual mean temperature and population density data were all collected from the Resources and Environment Science Data Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (http://www.resdc.cn/), each with a spatial resolution of 1 km. (4) Data on railways, highways, settlements, and rivers were obtained from the 1:1,000,000 basic geographic information data released by the National Basic Geographic Information Center (https://www.webmap.cn/).

Results

Spatio-temporal changes of cultivated land

Cultivated land emerged as a predominant land use type in the Hehuang Valley during 1980–2020, ranking second only to grassland and forest land in areal coverage. Throughout this 40-year period, cultivated land accounted for approximately 14.42% of the valley’s total area (Table 3). The cultivated land area exhibited an initial increase followed by a decline trajectory, with an overall reduction of 2.32% (119.24 km²). Specifically, cultivated land expanded steadily from 5,128.97 km² in 1980 to a peak of 5,138.56 km² in 2000, before contracting sharply to 5,009.74 km² by 2020. Spatial analysis revealed pronounced heterogeneity in land use distribution across the Hehuang Valley during this period, with cultivated land demonstrating concentrated distribution in three distinct zones (Fig. 2), including northern alluvial plains along the Datong River, central Huangshui River valleys, and southeastern flat terraces bordering the Yellow River. These findings suggest that while natural constraintsfundamentally govern cultivated land spatial patterns, its temporal dynamics are predominantly driven by intensifying anthropogenic activities.

Spatial aggregation of cultivated land

To analyze the spatial agglomeration characteristics of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley, this study calculated the kernel density values of cultivated land from 1980 to 2020 using a kernel density analysis tool, and classified these values into five grades (No. /km²) via the natural breaks method: Extremely low [0.00, 8.23], low (8.23, 24.30], medium (24.30, 42.34], high (42.34, 62.72], and extremely high (62.72, 99.96]. Results indicated significant differences in the spatial distribution of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley (Fig. 4), exhibiting a pattern of low density in high-mountain river valleys. Specifically, areas with extremely high and high density accounted for approximately 3.52% and 9.05% of the total area of the Hehuang Valley, respectively, highly concentrated in flat river valley zones along the Datong River, Huangshui River, and Yellow River where altitudes are low and topography is flat. Medium-density areas, accounting for about 12.75%, were primarily distributed in low- altitude undulating mountainous regions at the edges of extremely high- and high-density zones, serving as transitional areas between cultivated land, forest land, and grassland, with a medium proportion of cultivated land. Areas with low and extremely low density, covering approximately 14.94% and 59.72% of the total area, were generally distributed in high- altitude undulating mountainous regions in the northern and southern parts of the Hehuang Valley, where the proportion of cultivated land was extremely low. From 1980 to 2020, the areas of extremely high and medium density cultivated land decreased by 13.08% and 0.76%, respectively, with contraction concentrated in the Xining section of the Huangshui River and densely populated urban zones along the Yellow River in Huangnan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture. In contrast, areas of extremely low density (0.85%), low density (0.44%), and high density (0.13%) expanded, primarily in flat river valleys in the southern and eastern parts of the Hehuang Valley. Notably, the extremely high-density zone mainly transitioned to the high-density zone (232.25 km²), while the medium-density zone primarily shifted to low density (237.88 km²) and high density (125.23 km²) zones. Additionally, significant transitions occurred from low density (121.06 km²) and high density (250.82 km²) zones to medium-density zones. These findings suggest that the spatial changes in the kernel density of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley are not only constrained by natural environmental factors such as altitude and landform but also influenced by human activities like urbanization advancement.

Transition analysis of cultivated land

Analyzing the transitions in cultivated land area in the Hehuang Valley from 1980 to 2020 (Table 5), the total cultivated land increased during 1980–2000, with net inflows of 6.82 km² and 2.78 km² in the sub-periods 1980–1990 and 1990–2000, respectively. However, it decreased from 2000 to 2020, with net outflows of 51.42 km² (2000–2010) and 119.24 km² (2010–2020). Compared to the first two decades, the spatial transitions of cultivated land area were particularly pronounced from 2000 to 2020: during 2000–2010, 120.33 km², 19.03 km², and 60.67 km² of cultivated land were converted to grassland, water area, and construction land, respectively. From 2010 to 2020, conversions to forest land, grassland, and built-up area reached 8.89 km², 58.24 km², and 65.71 km², respectively. Over the entire period (1980–2020) (Table 5; Fig. 6), conversions of cultivated land to grassland and water area totaled 162.40 km² and 34.92 km², primarily distributed along the Yellow River in the southern study area. Conversions to construction land amounted to 151.07 km², highly concentrated in the densely urbanized zones of Xining and Haidong along the Huangshui River. Additionally, significant areas of forest land (15.91 km²), grassland (194.24 km²), water area (16.14 km²), and built-up area (14.25 km²) were converted to cultivated land, spatially clustered in relatively flat areas of Menyuan and Huzhu in northern study area. Notably, spatial transitions of cultivated land area were far more intense from 2000 to 2020 than in the preceding two decades. These findings indicate that the transitions and spatial distribution of cultivated land area in the Hehuang Valley from 1980 to 2020 were profoundly influenced by urbanization and construction activities.

Distribution and change of topographic gradients in cultivated land

Taking 2020 as an example, the topographic gradient distribution of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley was further analyzed. Results indicated significant differences in the topographic gradient distribution of cultivated land (Fig. 7). The area of cultivated land exhibited a distribution pattern of first increasing and then decreasing with rising altitude and aspect, with the highest concentrations observed in altitude zone III (1442.22 km²) and e aspect zone Ⅴ (696.95 km²). Notably, no cultivated land was distributed in altitude zones Ⅷ–Ⅸ. For the landform gradient, the area of cultivated land displayed a distribution pattern of first decreasing, then increasing, and finally decreasing again, where landform zones II and Ⅵ served as transition zones with areas of 197.22 km² and 1359.72 km², respectively. Meanwhile, the maximum cultivated land area was found in landform gradient zone Ⅰ (1630.85 km²). Additionally, the area of cultivated land decreased with increasing slope gradient, peaking at 1956.20 km² in slope gradient zone Ⅰ and dropping to only 19.79 km² in zone Ⅸ. Concurrently, altitude zones Ⅰ–Ⅳ, landform zones Ⅰ–Ⅴ, aspects Ⅰ and Ⅳ–VIII, and slope gradients Ⅰ–III were identified as dominant zones for cultivated land distribution, accounting for 91.32%, 68.82%, 68.16%, and 72.82% of the total cultivated land area in the Hehuang Valley, respectively. These findings reveal that cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley is highly concentrated in small undulating river valley plains and mountainous areas below 3029 m in altitude and 17.42° in slope gradient, with most distributed on sunny aspects.

Spatiotemporal evolution trend of cultivated land

Analyzing the changes in standard deviational ellipse parameters of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley from 1980 to 2020 (Table 6), the azimuth angle of its spatial distribution fluctuated within the range of 147.638°–148.733°, showing a fluctuating decreasing trend overall, which indicates a shift in the expansion direction of cultivated land from the “northwest-southeast” orientation toward the “east-west” direction. The minor axis shortened from 46.291 km in 1980 to 45.998 km in 2020, while the major axis increased from 86.361 km in 1980 to 87.367 km in 2020. These changes in the minor and major axes suggest that the spatial extent of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley contracted between 1980 and 2020, with forest land remaining relatively stable in the east-west direction but generally expanding in the north-south direction. The flattening ratio increased from 1.866 in 1980 to 1.899 in 2020, further indicating a trend toward increasing spatial agglomeration of cultivated land in the study area.

Driving factors of cultivated land changes

Considering the natural and economic conditions of the Hehuang Valley and referencing relevant previous studies19,31, this study selected 18 driving factors (Table 2), to analyze the driving factors of cultivated land area changes in the Hehuang Valley from 1980 to 2020. The results (Fig. 10) indicate that population density was the dominant factor influencing cultivated land area changes, with a contribution value of 0.12. This was followed by distance to railways, mean annual wind speed, altitude and GDP per unit area, each with contributions ranging from 0.07 to 0.10. The remaining factors had relatively low contributions, spanning 0.02 to 0.05. By overlaying the increased cultivated land areas with the population density raster data (Fig. 11), it was observed that the newly added cultivated land was highly concentrated in flat valley plains characterized by high population density.

Contribution of driving factors of land use change in the study area.

Superposition of increasing regions of land use types and their highest driving factors.

To analyze the causes of cultivated land change in the Hehuang Valley, it is necessary to further examine the driving factors behind the reduction in cultivated land area. According to Table 5, from 1980 to 2020, most of the converted cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley was transformed into grassland (44.81%), built-up area (41.69%), and water area (9.64%, primarily due to human activities such as hydropower station construction). Conversions to forest land and unused land were relatively limited, accounting for only 3.43% and 0.43% of the total converted cultivated land area, respectively. This indicates that the increase in grassland, water area, and built-up area areas largely resulted from the transfer of cultivated land. Therefore, the reasons for the reduction in cultivated land area in the Hehuang Valley can be indirectly elucidated by analyzing the driving factors behind the expansion of these three land use types. As shown in Fig. 11, the dominant factor influencing changes in grassland area is the distance to railways. Meanwhile, population density, mean annual humidity, and mean annual temperature also significantly affect grassland area changes. The increase in grassland area is highly concentrated in the southern part of the Hehuang Valley, characterized by sparse population and rugged mountainous terrain. The dominant factor for changes in water area is mean annual precipitation, followed by elevation, mean annual ground temperature, and mean annual humidity. The expansion of water area mainly occurs in flat river valleys with relatively abundant annual precipitation. For built-up area, the dominant factor is population density, followed by mean annual wind speed, distance to settlements, and GDP per unit area. The increase in construction land is concentrated in the Huangshui Valley, which features high population density and developed economic conditions. Overall, changes in cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley from 1980 to 2020 were influenced not only by natural factors such as altitude, mean annual humidity, mean annual wind speed, and mean annual precipitation, but also by socioeconomic factors including population density, distance to railways, GDP per unit area, and distance to settlements.

Discussion

Uniqueness of the spatio-temporal evolution of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley

This study, utilizing topographic gradient factors and spatio-temporal analysis methods, revealed the spatial differentiation pattern of “dense in river valleys, sparse in mountainous areas” and the northwestward contraction trend of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley from 1980 to 2020, which shares certain commonalities with previous research on cultivated land and land-use changes in adjacent regions of the Hehuang Valley17,22. Regarding the spatio-temporal evolution characteristics of cultivated land, prior studies have primarily focused on the cultivated land pressure index and food security38, emphasizing issues such as non-agricultural conversion and non-grain production of cultivated land driven by urbanization19. In contrast, this research further quantified the constraining effects of topographic gradients on cultivated land distribution, identifying hilly plain areas below 2772 m in altitude and with slopes < 17.42° as the core distribution zone of cultivated land—a conclusion consistent with previous findings on cultivated land studies in the Tibetan Plateau region22,39. In terms of driving mechanisms, this study identified population density (with a contribution value of 0.12) as the dominant factor influencing changes in cultivated land area, which is consistent with the situation in neighboring regions where socioeconomic factors such as population density or GDP per unit area dominate attribution analysis in similar arable land studies15,31. Additionally, by applying the standard deviational ellipse theory, this research uncovered the azimuthal characteristics of cultivated land spatial contraction, addressing the deficiency in traditional dynamic analyse s of the spatial distribution centroid of regional cultivated land.

Impacts of human activities on cultivated land change

From 1980 to 2020, the sharp decline in cultivated land area in the Xining section of the Huangshui River highlighted severe encroachment on cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley driven by urban agglomeration development and population growth. Concurrently, during the non-agricultural conversion of cultivated land, the expansion of construction land coexisted with the “non-grain production” of cultivated land19. On one hand, the development of industrial parks (e.g., Ganhe Industrial Park) occupied large areas of high-quality cultivated land40; on the other hand, the increased proportion of cash crop cultivation (e.g., large-scale promotion of large cherry cultivation in Ledu District of Haidong City in the early 21st century) led to a decline in grain sown area41. With the introduction of the Territorial Spatial Planning of Qinghai Province (2021–2035) and the future focus on “three zones and three lines” delimitation in the Hehuang Valley42, the rigid constraints of ecological protection red lines and urban development boundaries are expected to reduce the risk of further cultivated land encroachment19. Additionally, the plan proposes initiatives such as “high-standard farmland construction”43 and the “cultivated land occupation-compensation balance mechanism”44 (e.g., Datong County’s terraced field transformation to enhance cultivated land quality), providing new pathways for sustainable cultivated land utilization. However, population concentration in central cities under the new-type urbanization strategy may exacerbate the marginalization and fragmentation of cultivated land45. Balancing development and protection through territorial spatial use regulation and ecological compensation mechanisms is therefore critical to promoting the sustainable development of cultivated land resources46.

Topographic gradient conservation of cultivated land

This study found that cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley is highly concentrated in flat river valleys and hilly areas at low elevations, which is consistent with findings from related studies conducted in adjacent regions, such as the upper reaches of the Minjiang River47, Yuzhong County in Gansu Province48, and the Lhasa River Basin49. Based on the topographic gradient characteristics and driving mechanisms of cultivated land distribution in the Hehuang Valley, tailored land-use strategies should be strictly implemented in accordance with local conditions3. Specifically, in low-elevation areas (< 2205 m), non-agricultural construction should be strictly limited, while land consolidation should be promoted to enhance plot connectivity, accompanied by water-saving irrigation to prevent salinization. In the mid-elevation transition zone (2205–2503 m), terracing should be widely adopted, and the development of sloping cultivated land should be controlled in conjunction with the “Grain for Green” policy. In high-elevation marginal areas (> 2503 m), fallow rotation and the restoration of vegetation buffer zones should be implemented to effectively maintain the ecological environment. Meanwhile, for sloping cultivated land with gradients of 17.42–27.34°, soil-fixing techniques such as biological hedgerows should be employed to limit mechanical intensity50, and “slope-to-terrace” conversion should be promoted to improve cultivated land quality51. Additionally, in densely populated areas with convenient transportation, ecological engineering for transportation corridors and policies such as “industrial development on slopes and residential development in valleys” should be implemented to release high-quality cultivated land resources in the valleys3. Finally, the policy of “requisition-compensation balance” should be reformed into “compensating superior land for superior requisitioned land.” Under the context of urban construction encroaching on valley cultivated land, ecological reclamation should be carried out on slopes steeper than 27.34°52, aiming to establish a sustainable cultivated land use network that harmonizes topographic gradient characteristics with human activities.

Research limitations and future prospects

This study systematically analyzed the spatio-temporal evolution and topographic gradient differentiation characteristics of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley from 1980 to 2020, though certain limitations persist. First, the 30 m resolution land use data exhibited limited sensitivity to fragmented cultivated land changes, potentially leading to misclassification of field ridges and terraced field structures in sloped terrain. Furthermore, the PLUS model demonstrated relatively weak dynamic simulation capabilities for policy factors, particularly in quantifying policy interventions like ecological restoration projects53. Additionally, the reliance on macro-level factors such as population density neglected micro-scale behavioral data (Such as farmers’ land transfer preferences), which constrained the interpretation of human-nature interactions. Future research could integrate Sentinel-2 hyperspectral imagery to enhance cultivated land classification accuracy and incorporate socio-economic survey data to establish a “policy-market-household” multi-scale driving force model, thereby addressing these methodological gaps and improving predictive capacity for sustainable land management strategies. While this study provides a comprehensive overview of the spatiotemporal evolution of land use in the study area, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations regarding the depth of the driving force analysis. Although we quantified the contributions of the selected driving factors (Table 2), the current analysis primarily focuses on drawing on previous research results rather than exploring the underlying mechanisms in detail. Specifically, the complex interactions between natural factors and socio-economic factors, as well as the spatial non-stationarity of these factors, were not exhaustively examined in this manuscript due to the scope constraints of the current study. The visualization of the mechanistic pathways through which specific factors influence land expansion remains an area for further improvement. Future research should therefore move beyond mere factor identification to incorporate more sophisticated models (Such as Geographically Weighted Regression or Structural Equation Modeling54,55 to disentangle the direct and indirect effects of these drivers, thereby providing a more profound theoretical basis for land management policies.

Conclusion

Using land use data (1980–2020) and topographic factors, this study analyzed the spatiotemporal evolution and driving mechanisms of cultivated land in the Hehuang Valley. The results showed that: (1) Cultivated land comprised 14.45% of the region, decreasing by 119.24 km² (converted to grassland, water bodies, construction land). Highest holdings occurred in Datong, Huangyuan, Ledu, Minhe, Huzhu, Hualong, and Menyuan (426.99–803.31 km²). (2) Kernel density was “dense in river valleys and sparse in mountainous areas,” focusing on the Datong River, Huangshui River and Yellow River corridors (< 2772 m) but scattered across southern and northern mountains. (3) Topographic distribution varied by altitude (Ⅰ–IV), landform type (Ⅰ–Ⅴ), aspect (Ⅰ, IV–VIII), and slope (Ⅰ–III), concentrating in hilly and plain areas with slopes < 17.42° and sunny exposures. (4) Key drivers included population density (0.12), distance to railway, Mean annual wind speed, altitude and GDP per area. Expansion along the Datong River and Yellow River correlated with high population and GDP, low altitude. Reduction in the Huangshui River’s Xining section occurred despite similar conditions. The center of gravity migrated 2.277 km northwestward, indicating contraction.
