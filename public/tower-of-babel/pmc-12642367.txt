Addressing gaps in pre-diabetes care: health professionals’ knowledge, attitudes, and counseling practices
Authors: Kashif Shafique, Nadia Shah, Saba Mughal, Iqra Ansari, Rija Siddiqui, Muhammad Fareed Uddin, Riasat Ali Khan, Asima Khan Niazi, Shabeen Naz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrediabetes is a global concern and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Early detection of prediabetes plays a crucial role since it is a reversible disease. Healthcare providers play a very important role in screening at-risk patients and implementing preventive measures early. This study aimed to assess healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitude and counseling practices regarding prediabetes which will serve as a tool to create targeted training in the future.MethodWe conducted a cross-sectional study on 312 general practitioners and junior doctors. A structured questionnaire designed after detailed literature search was used to collect data on knowledge (23 questions), attitudes (6 questions) and counseling practices (8 questions) regarding prediabetes. The relationship between a range of factors with knowledge, attitude and counseling practices was studied using univariate and multivariate analyses.ResultsOf total, 71.5% (223) of the study participants were males; the average age was 42.7 ± 15.1 years; Only 13.1% had adequate knowledge, 19.9% showed a positive attitude and 77.6% had good counseling practices for prediabetes and diabetes patients and their families. Compared to those with inadequate knowledge, participants with adequate knowledge had been practicing medicine for less than 10 years. Positive attitude and good counseling were however observed more in those with 20 or more years of clinical experience. Females were less likely to have adequate knowledge when compared to males (OR = 0.2, 95% CI 0.1–0.6, p-value = 0.004). A positive attitude toward prediabetes management was significantly higher among participants older than 40 years (OR = 18.6, p-value < 0.001), females (OR = 6.7, p-value < 0.001), and those with over 20 years of clinical experience (OR = 6.3, p-value = 0.014) in managing prediabetes/diabetes patients compared to their counterparts. Additionally, participants with more than 20 years of experience in managing diabetes patients were significantly more likely to exhibit good counseling behavior (OR = 9.1, 95% CI: 1.5–51.7, p = 0.013) compared to those with less than 10 years of experience.ConclusionThis study identified significant gaps of knowledge among general practitioners. While general practitioners demonstrated poor attitudes, they exhibited commendable counseling practices toward patients with prediabetes and their families. The identified gaps and challenges stress the need for tailored educational initiatives and intervention but also targeted efforts to address attitudes alongside enhancing clinical skills. Limitations of the study, including the potential sampling biases and development of a new, non-validated questionnaire, should be considered when interpreting the results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07517-4.Keywords: Prediabetes1, Knowledge2, Attitudes3, Counseling4, General practitioners

Introduction

Prediabetes is a metabolic disorder with high blood sugar levels not reaching the threshold for diabetes [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), prediabetes is an intermediate hyperglycemic state based on two parameters: impaired fasting glucose (IFG), which is fasting plasma glucose (PG) ranging from 6.1 to 6.9 mmol/L, and impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), which occurs when 2 h PG levels range from 7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L after ingesting 75 g of an oral glucose load [2]. On the other hand, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) agrees on the cut-off values for IGT but has a wider range for IFG, with a lower cut-off value at 5.6 mmol/L. The ADA also defines prediabetes as HbA1c levels ranging from 5.7% to 6.4% [3].

Prediabetes is a global concern that is substantial and growing. In a review conducted on 7014 studies by Rooney et al., the age-adjusted prevalence of IGT among adults aged 20–79 years old was 464 million and was projected to rise to 638 million by 2045 [4]. By 2045, the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) predicts a higher prevalence of IGT among adults (730 million) [5]. In the United States alone, the CDC reports a staggering 97.6 million adults with prediabetes, accounting for more than one-third of the population [6]. Akhtar et al., through a meta-analysis of studies in Pakistan, identified a prediabetes prevalence of 11.43%, emphasizing the urgency for both national and international attention to tackle this rapidly growing health challenge [7].

Prediabetes is now understood to have far-reaching implications beyond its status as a precursor to diabetes. Independent risks associated with prediabetes encompass significant damaging effects on various organs and systems, amplifying the risk of microvascular diseases, macrovascular diseases, chronic kidney diseases, neuropathies, and even cancer [8]. It has a cause-and-effect link with cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality, according to several studies. Sen et al. studied 62 patients with acute coronary syndrome who were admitted to a tertiary hospital in India to determine the percentage of patients who had prediabetes. They found that 48.4% of the patients had prediabetes. Fortunately, the hyperglycemic state of prediabetes is reversible [9].

To effectively reverse the state of prediabetes, the key is early detection [10]. Healthcare providers play an important role in screening at-risk patients and implementing preventive measures. Despite groundbreaking evidence showcasing the success of lifestyle changes, including diet changes and physical activity, in reversing an intermediate hyperglycemic state [11], 90% of prediabetic patients remain unaware that they have prediabetes [12]. In 2015, 84.1 million people were estimated to have prediabetes but only 11.6% of these people had been informed of this by a health provider [6]. This can be attributed to inadequate knowledge of health care professionals about prediabetes, poor attitude, and counseling practices concerning the condition.

Pakistan, specifically, faces additional public health challenges. Karachi, the largest city in Pakistan, with an estimated population of over 17 million, is the economic hub of the country and has an extensive network of slums, where a large proportion of the population resides. These factors present a unique set of challenges to addressing prediabetes at a national level.

There is extensive literature on prediabetes but healthcare professionals must stay updated and educated on the latest research and guidelines. By doing so, they can provide accurate information and appropriate interventions to help prevent the progression of prediabetes to diabetes. Additionally, public awareness campaigns and community education programs can play a vital role in increasing knowledge about prediabetes and its potential consequences [13].

The focus of this study, therefore, is to survey healthcare professionals to assess their knowledge, attitudes, and counseling practices regarding prediabetes in Pakistan. Knowledge will assess the healthcare professionals’ awareness and understanding of the diagnostic criteria, risk factors, and evidence-based management strategies for prediabetes. Teoh et al. found that only 2.8%-42% of the healthcare providers identified the diagnostic criteria and risk factors for prediabetes [13]. Attitudes assesses the healthcare professionals’ perspectives on lifestyle counseling, empathy toward obese patients and confidence in assisting with weight management, crucial strategies to prevent and manage prediabetes. The counseling practices include actions taken by healthcare professionals to support patients with prediabetes including providing education on nutrition, physical activity and behavorial change.

By obtaining comprehensive insights, we aimed to lay the foundation for designing targeted interventions that will enhance healthcare professionals’ awareness, knowledge, and counseling practices concerning prediabetes. This, in turn, has the potential to significantly contribute to the early identification and management of prediabetes, ultimately curbing the rising incidence of diabetes and its associated complications among the Pakistani population. The study also aspires to empower healthcare professionals with the tools and insights needed to make a substantial impact on diabetes prevention in Pakistan.

Methods

Study design, duration and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design, targeting general practitioners (GPs) and junior doctors, to assess their baseline knowledge, attitude and counseling practices related to prediabetes before a planned training intervention.

The training sessions, designed for healthcare professionals, aimed to improve their knowledge of evidence-based guidelines for diagnosing, managing and counseling patients with prediabetes. The sessions focused on critical areas such as lifestyle intervention strategies, patient education on nutrition and physical activity, and the use of pharmacological treatments like metformin in high-risk populations. Developed by consultant experts in medicine and endocrinology, these training modules emphasized practical approaches to prediabetes management, including behavior change strategies and motivational interviewing techniques. The sessions were conducted across a timeline spanning from November 2021 to November 2022.

Participants were recruited through invitations circulated across Karachi via relevant organizations and institutions. The pre-training phase involved interviewing participants to establish baseline levels of knowledge, attitudes, and counseling practices. Training sessions were planned to be delivered to an initial group of 20–30 leading GPs in a two-day program at Dow University of Health Sciences. These trained GPs were then expected to cascade the training to broader groups, targeting approximately 300 GPs across Karachi. This paper reports findings from the baseline assessment conducted before the training sessions Relevant details (email IDs, contact details) were collected prospectively to conduct post-test interviews with the same cohort of doctors.

Sample size

GPs and junior doctors from both government and private healthcare settings across Karachi to ensure representation from diverse clinical environments and those who provided consent to participate in this study were included. The sample size was calculated using Openepi. The proportion of correct knowledge about pre-diabetes among healthcare providers was taken as 22.9% [14], with 5% margin of error, and 95% confidence level, the total sample size came out to be 272. 312 individuals were included after the addition of 10% of the calculated sample size, to reduce error chances and minimize the missing information.

Survey questionnaire

A structured questionnaire was designed by conducting a detailed literature search [15–19] related to knowledge, attitude, and behaviors towards prediabetes including the systematic review by Teoh et al. (2023) [13], which highlighted significant gaps in validated knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) instruments related to prediabetes. Although a number of studies assessed KAP toward prediabetes, Teoh et al. reported that only two quantitative studies among healthcare professionals were available, and different, non-standardized survey tools were used across studies. The initial draft of the questionnaire was further reviewed by a panel of multidisciplinary experts including professionals in public health, epidemiology, endocrinology, and clinical medicine to ensure content relevance and face validity. Their feedback was incorporated to refine the questions for clarity and relevance. There were a total of 42 questions. The questionnaire collected data on demographics (5 questions), knowledge (23 questions), attitudes (6 questions) and counseling practices (8 questions). The knowledge section was further broken down into questions about screening and diagnostic tests, risk factors, prevalence, motivational interviewing/steps of behavior change and management. This comprehensive questionnaire aimed to capture insights into the participants’ understanding, attitudes, and practices related to prediabetes. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to assess the internal consistency of the attitude and counseling-related sections measured on 5-point Likert scales, yielding an overall score of 0.68. While slightly below the commonly accepted threshold of 0.7, this value was considered acceptable for a newly developed tool. Since no pre-existing scoring system for prediabetes knowledge was available, knowledge scores were calculated based on correct responses to dichotomous (yes/no) questions. The scoring approach was reviewed and approved by experts, lending credibility to the assessment framework.

Statistical analysis

Knowledge questions were coded as 1 to correct answer and 0 to wrong answer. For attitude strongly agree/ agree was coded as 1 and neutral/ disagree/ strongly disagree was coded as 0 in the question showing positive attitude whereas reverse coding was used in those statements which showed negative attitude. Counseling practices were assessed using binary responses, coded as 0 for no and 1 for yes. A composite score was generated by summing all the responses for each knowledge (range: 0–23), attitude (range: 0–6) and counseling practices (range: 0–8), separately. All scores were categorized into two groups using 60% cut-off of correct/ positive responses: knowledge (score ≤ 13 inadequate, > 13 adequate), attitude (score ≤ 4 negative, > 4 positive) and counseling practices (score ≤ 5 poor, > 5 good).

Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviations for quantitative variables and frequency (percentage) for qualitative variables. Chi-square test was used to evaluate associations between categorical variables. Binary logistics regression was applied to determine the effects of participants’ characteristics on their knowledge, attitude, and counselling practices. Odds ratios were reported with 95% confidence intervals. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant, and all analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.

Discussion

In our study, we found substantial gaps in physician knowledge across key domains, including risk factors, screening and diagnostic criteria, and management of prediabetes. These findings echo similar inadequacies i observed in other regions of Pakistan, such as in a tertiary care hospital in Islamabad [20]. However, this issue is not confined to Pakistan and appears to be a global concern. A systematic review by Teoh et al. highlighted that, across various countries, only 2.8%–42% of healthcare providers correctly identified diagnostic criteria and risk factors for prediabetes. Additionally, less than half were aware of recommended lifestyle changes, clinical targets, and pharmacological therapies for prediabetes patients [13]. In our study, among the 212 participants, only 38 doctors achieved the highest number of correct responses, correctly answering 10 out of 23 questions. These gaps in knowledge can help explain why Pakistan’s diabetes prevention initiatives have not yet caught up with those of other countries.

We identified a significant inverse correlation between knowledge and duration of practice of health care providers. HCPs with less than 10 years of practice were more likely to have adequate knowledge suggesting that younger doctors stay more attuned to current trends and updates in prediabetes. This is consistent with findings shown by Pi et al. [21]. This highlights the need for ongoing education for healthcare professionals to effectively diagnose and manage prediabetes. In our findings, female health professionals demonstrated less adequate knowledge compared to their male counterparts, contrasting with Pi et al.‘s results [21], which indicated higher prediabetes knowledge and better practices among females. Inaku et al., however, found similar knowledge levels between genders [14]. Our study and its findings highlight the urgent need to initiate public health policies that aim to improve physician knowledge, attitude and counseling practices for prediabetes. Specific policies could include mandatory continuing medical education (CME) programs for physicians that will ensure that they stay updated with current guidelines and best practices in prediabetes management. Training with certification and regular assessments can also enhance physician competence and ultimately improve patient outcomes. Understanding these variations can inform targeted training programs for individuals, emphasizing the importance of tailoring educational interventions to specific characteristics within the healthcare professional community.

We believe our study is the first of its kind in the region to assess physicians’ (particularly those involved in the care of prediabetes and diabetes patients) attitudes and beliefs concerning the management of obese patients. Notably, a resounding 96.2% of the participants acknowledged the importance of lifestyle intervention counseling for prediabetes management. However, in a striking contrast, 62.8% of respondents thought that patients who are overweight or obese are less likely to adhere to recommendations for changing their lifestyle. This disparity highlights the importance of focused training and competencies in managing obesity, particularly in prediabetics. Enhanced training can significantly improve physician attitudes, leading to better care for obese people [16]. Additionally, nearly 30% of participants admitted feeling uncomfortable when dealing with overweight or obese patients, while over 37% found it challenging to empathize with this demographic compared to their lean counterparts. These findings underscore the pervasive issue of weight-related stigma within healthcare settings [4], as highlighted in Guglielmi et al.‘s study [22], where 45% expressed a preference for lean individuals. Research consistently reveals that obese patients are often blamed for their lack of self-control or assumed to be “lazy” [23]. This prevailing bias may explain the obvious paradox in our study, where 189 participants (60.6%) believed that obese patients cannot lose weight while 287 participants (92%) had confidence in assisting patients with weight reduction. Obesity is a risk factor for prediabetes and diabetes and its prevalence has reached epidemic levels, globally [24]. Pakistan ranks 10th out of 188 countries, with almost half of the population being overweight or obese [25]. It thus becomes imperative to acknowledge and rectify these biases towards obese patients, avoid stigmatization, and create an environment that promotes empathy and understanding. The role of primary care providers in obesity care was emphasized by a recent study that implemented a practical, primary care-based method to guide patients on a low-carb diet. This approach resulted in a mean weight loss of 10 kg and improved diabetic control in 97% of those interested in the approach, with sustained results for nearly three years [26]. This reinforces the need for comprehensive training and support in handling the complexities of obesity care.

When examining counseling practices among healthcare professionals in the context of prediabetes, a substantial 77.6% showed good counseling practices towards patients and their families. This looks promising but with the gaps in knowledge of evidence-based guidelines, counseling may not be fully effective in preventing or managing prediabetes. The importance of counseling in prediabetes management cannot be overstated, as it serves as a key factor in behavioral change. Motivation emerges as a cornerstone, particularly in asymptomatic conditions like prediabetes, where long-term adherence is crucial [27]. Our study highlighted that healthcare professionals with over 20 years of experience were more likely to exhibit good counseling practices, emphasizing the potential positive impact of extensive practical knowledge.

87.8% of participants extended counseling to the immediate families of diabetes patients, which is promising given that family-centered approaches for the treatment and prevention of physical illnesses are superior to traditional medicine [28]. The willingness of physicians to counsel immediate families can encourage diabetes educators to enhance their training programs. These programs could incorporate strategies for family-based diabetes prevention, encompassing joint exercise sessions, fostering healthier eating habits, and more [29]. Focusing on family systems is recognized as a powerful approach to promoting well-being, as positive relationships within families contribute to healthier attitudes.

Physicians also identified challenges in counseling, particularly due to time constraints and patients’ limited attention to lifestyle interventions. According to studies by Mainous et al. [17] and Tseng et al. [30], around 75.3% to 76.9% of doctors find time constraints a significant barrier to educating patients about diabetes prevention. Tseng et al. also highlight the obstacle of patients’ lack of motivation. Effectively addressing these barriers requires recognizing the pivotal moment when PCPs discuss prediabetes diagnosis with patients, emphasizing the need for dedicated time to motivate individuals toward crucial lifestyle changes.

Limitations

While our study provides valuable insights, it has its limitations. The cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causation, and the reliance on self-reported data introduces the potential for bias. The sample, though representative of Karachi, may not fully capture the diversity of healthcare professionals across Pakistan, particularly those practicing in rural areas. Rural regions face unique challenges like limited access to continuing medical education (CME) programs, inadequate regulatory oversight, and the prevalence of unregulated practices [31]. These factors could significantly impact their awareness and management strategies for prediabetes. Another limitation of our study is the use of a non-validated scoring system due to the lack of existing tools tailored to our research setting. To address this, we calculated Cronbach’s alpha for the adapted questionnaire, which was 0.68, indicating an acceptable level of reliability.

A potential limitation of this study is the selection bias introduced during participant recruitment, as individuals with a prior interest in prediabetes management may have been more likely to participate. This may have influenced the baseline knowledge and attitudes observed in the study cohort. Future studies should aim for random sampling to reduce such biases and ensure broader generalizability of findings. Our study did not specifically capture the reasons why some participants did not provide counseling. Future research should explore the barriers preventing GPs from offering counseling, such as lack of confidence, insufficient training, or patient-related factors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study can serve as a guide for addressing the complexities of prediabetes management in Karachi. The identified gaps and challenges stress the need for tailored educational initiatives and intervention and addressing obesity stigmas. The positive counseling practices and family-centric approach offer avenues for intervention. By acknowledging these findings and addressing our study’s limitations, we can pave the way for more effective and empathetic prediabetes management in Pakistan.
