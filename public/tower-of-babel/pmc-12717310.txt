Efficacy and safety of hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy plus lenvatinib and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors versus systemic chemotherapy plus lenvatinib and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in advanced unresectable cholangiocarcinoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zhenzhen Zhang, Meixia Wang, Guobin Chen, Yanfang Wu, Xiaochun Chen, Xinkun Guo, Boheng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) provides sustained high intrahepatic drug exposure with limited systemic toxicity, potentially enhancing antigen release and immune sensitization. This study compared the efficacy and safety of HAIC combined with lenvatinib and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors versus systemic chemotherapy-based triple therapy in advanced unresectable intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA).MethodsA retrospective analysis of 67 patients with unresectable iCCA treated with HAIC-based triple therapy (n = 32) or systemic triple therapy (n = 35) was conducted. Clinical efficacy, survival, prognostic factors and safety profiles were evaluated. Baseline imbalances were adjusted using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses before and after IPTW assessed treatment effects on overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS).ResultsHAIC-based triple therapy achieved superior tumor control versus systemic therapy, with higher objective response rate (59.4 vs. 20.0%; P < 0.001), disease control rate (90.6 vs. 68.6%; P < 0.001), and tumor shrinkage (+ 6.5 vs. − 1.0 mm; P = 0.002). Local (93.8 vs. 66.7%; P = 0.006) and distant (90.3 vs. 72.7%; P = 0.071) control rates favored HAIC-based triple therapy. After IPTW adjustment, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that HAIC-based triple therapy significantly improved both OS (24.1 vs. 15.5 months; P = 0.010) and PFS (P = 0.020) compared to systemic therapy. In the IPTW-adjusted multivariate Cox regression analysis, HAIC-based triple therapy was associated with a markedly reduced risk of OS (HR [95% CI]: 0.221[0.070–0.693]) and PFS (HR[95% CI]: 0.129[0.028–0.592]). Toxicities were manageable, and immune-related adverse events correlated with reduced progression.ConclusionHAIC-based triple therapy provided superior tumor control, prolonged survival, and preserved hepatic function with acceptable safety in unresectable iCCA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04255-1.Keywords: Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, HAIC, Lenvatinib, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor, Survival, Biomarkers

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) is the second most common primary hepatic malignancy, characterized by a poor prognosis due to delayed diagnosis and limited therapeutic options [1, 2]. More than 60% of patients with iCCA are diagnosed with unresectable disease, leading to a median survival of less than 12 months, even with current gemcitabine-based chemotherapy regimens [3]. Gemcitabine-based chemotherapy remains the standard first-line treatment for iCCA with a median overall survival (OS) of approximately 10 months in patients with CCA [4].However, despite its efficacy, these treatment options often fail to achieve long-term survival benefits, highlighting the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies.

Emerging research evidence suggests that combination therapies that incorporate systemic chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 blockers) may offer improved therapeutic outcomes [5]. For instance, the TOPAZ-1 trial demonstrated that, compared with gemcitabine–cisplatin therapy, durvalumab–gemcitabine–cisplatin combination therapy significantly improved OS to 12.9 months in patients with advanced cancer of the biliary tract [5]. Similarly, the KEYNOTE-966 trial established pembrolizumab plus gemcitabine/cisplatin as a new standard treatment that achieved a median OS of 12.7 months [6]. These results have contributed to the incorporation of chemo-immunotherapy into the latest NCCN and ESMO guidelines (2023–2024) as a first-line therapeutic strategy for advanced biliary tract cancers [7].

Given these advancements, there is growing interest in the combination of hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC), lenvatinib, and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors as a promising alternative approach [8–11]. HAIC is a locoregional therapy in which chemotherapy drugs are administered directly into the hepatic artery, allowing for high local concentrations of chemotherapeutic agents within the liver while minimizing systemic exposure. This method improves the delivery of chemotherapy to the tumor by bypassing the liver’s first-pass metabolism, enhancing the effectiveness of the treatment and reducing side effects. Mechanistically, HAIC not only provides targeted chemotherapy but also induces tumor necrosis and immunogenic cell death (ICD), which can stimulate an immune response by promoting antigen presentation and T cell activation [12, 13]. Lenvatinib [5], a multi-kinase inhibitor targeting VEGFR and FGFR pathways, not only inhibits angiogenesis but also normalizes the tumor vasculature, reduces hypoxia, and mitigates VEGF-mediated immune suppression, thereby improving immune cell infiltration. Additionally, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors restore exhausted T cell function, enhancing the cytotoxic immune response against residual tumor cells [14]. This synergistic combination of locoregional therapy, vascular modulation, and immune checkpoint blockade may be particularly beneficial for patients with bulky, liver-dominant disease.

Several studies have demonstrated that HAIC-based combination therapies improve disease control and survival rates in patients with unresectable iCCA [8–15]. A retrospective analysis found that HAIC combined with lenvatinib and PD-1 inhibitors significantly improved clinical outcomes in advanced intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, with a median OS of 16.8 versus 11.0 months and median PFS of 12.0 versus 6.9 months compared to first-line systemic chemotherapy [8]. Another retrospective cohort study revealed that HAIC-based triple therapy significantly prolonged survival in advanced iCCA, achieving a median OS of 20.8 versus 14.8 months and PFS of 9.1 versus 6.2 months compared with systemic chemotherapy [5]. Moreover, recent studies have shown that first-line HAIC in combination with lenvatinib and PD-1 inhibitors outperforms systemic chemotherapy regimens such as cisplatin and gemcitabine in iCCA [13].

Despite the promising data supporting HAIC-based triple therapy, there is still a lack of direct comparative studies evaluating its efficacy and safety against systemic triple therapy (systemic chemotherapy, lenvatinib, and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors). Moreover, patient stratification and toxicological profiles have not been fully elucidated in the context of iCCA. To provide critical evidence for the broader clinical application of HAIC-based triple therapy, this study aims to compare the efficacy and safety of HAIC-based triple therapy with systemic triple therapy in patients with iCCA.

Materials and methods

Participants

Eligible patients were retrospectively identified and stratified according to first-line treatment regimens. A total of 109 patients with histologically confirmed iCCA treated at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Xiamen, Fujian, People’s Republic of China), between January 2019 and December 2023 were screened. After applying eligibility criteria, 67 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure S1).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) histologically confirmed diagnosis of iCCA; (3) newly diagnosed and treatment-naïve patients receiving first-line therapy during the initial study period; (4) treatment with triple therapy regimens, including HAIC or systemic gemcitabine-based chemotherapy, in combination with lenvatinib and a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) prior systemic treatment for iCCA (n = 8); (2) survival duration < 3 months (n = 12), referring to patients with Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG-PS) > 3, Child–Pugh class C liver function, or severely impaired organ function who were unable to tolerate anti-cancer treatment; (3) presence of other active malignancies or multiple tumors (n = 5);(4) incomplete medical or follow-up data (n = 4); and (5) treatment with non-triple regimens (n = 13).

Ultimately, 32 patients received HAIC-based triple therapy and 35 patients received systemic triple therapy as first-line treatment.

Staging and tumor burden assessment

Baseline staging was performed according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification for iCCA. Full T (T1–T4), N (N0–N1), and M (M0–M1) categories, as well as overall Stage I–IV distributions, were recorded for each cohort. Regional lymph nodes were defined as nodes located along the hepatoduodenal ligament, common hepatic artery, celiac axis, and peripancreatic region; N1 status was assigned when at least one regional node demonstrated radiologic or pathologic involvement.

To ensure comprehensive tumor burden evaluation, disease characteristics were assessed using multiphasic contrast-enhanced liver MRI or CT, supplemented by chest CT and/or FDG-PET/CT when clinically indicated. The largest diameter of the primary lesion, number of measurable intrahepatic lesions (solitary vs. multiple), presence of vascular invasion, extrahepatic metastasis (solitary vs. multiple), and biliary involvement were documented. Baseline functional and biological indicators included ECOG-PS, Child–Pugh class, albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) grade, serum CA19-9, etiological status (HBV/ Cholecystitis and gallstones), and hepatic reserve parameters (presence of cirrhosis).

Study design and follow-up

This was a retrospective, single-center cohort study designed to compare the efficacy and safety of HAIC-based triple therapy versus systemic triple therapy in advanced iCCA. Patients were stratified by first-line regimen as described above and followed from the date of treatment initiation until disease progression, death, or last follow-up (May 30, 2024). Clinical, radiological, and laboratory data were extracted from institutional medical records.

Tumor response was evaluated every 6–8 weeks according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 [16]. Safety was assessed based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Endpoints

The primary endpoints were: objective response rate (ORR): proportion of patients achieving complete response (CR) or partial response (PR); disease control rate (DCR): proportion achieving CR, PR, or stable disease (SD);

The secondary endpoints included: overall survival (OS): defined as the time from initiation of first-line therapy to death from any cause or last follow-up. Progression-free survival (PFS): defined as the time from initiation of therapy to radiologically confirmed disease progression or death. Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs): incidence and severity of adverse events graded by CTCAE v5.0.

Treatment regimens

The participants were assigned to one of two cohorts based on the first-line treatment modalities: HAIC-based triple therapy or systemic triple therapy. Patients in the HAIC-based cohort received hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC) with either gemcitabine–oxaliplatin (GEMOX) or gemcitabine–cisplatin (GP), in combination with lenvatinib and a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor. Patients in the systemic cohort were treated with intravenous GEMOX or GP, together with lenvatinib and a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor, depending on cisplatin eligibility and physician discretion.

After 4–6 cycles of first-line therapy, patients without disease progression were eligible for maintenance therapy, consisting of continued lenvatinib plus PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Second-line treatment options included chemotherapy regimens such as FOLFOX, AG (albumin-bound paclitaxel + gemcitabine), or FOLFIRI, as well as locoregional interventions including transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) or transarterial embolization (TAE) or HAIC. These could be combined with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, CTLA-4 inhibitors (e.g., ipilimumab), or next-generation sequencing (NGS)-guided targeted therapies in patients harboring actionable mutations.

The choice of regimen, number of cycles, and treatment duration were determined by multidisciplinary team (MDT) evaluation and physician discretion. Detailed drug doses, schedules, and administration protocols are provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

Assessments and outcomes

Data were recorded at three timepoints during treatment as follows: at baseline (C1), after completion of first-line treatment (C2), and after the completion of second-line treatment (if applicable, C3); tumor response was evaluated according to the RECIST v1.1. Baseline imaging was performed using contrast-enhanced CT or MRI, centrally reviewed by two senior independent radiologists blinded to treatment allocation. Subsequent imaging assessments were conducted every 6–8 weeks or as clinically indicated. Target lesions were measured by the longest diameter, and response was categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). Laboratory tests and safety were evaluated by routine blood tests, liver function tests, and tumor markers (e.g., CA19-9). Follow-up data encompassed details of the initiation time, adverse events, disease progression, death events, and the end date of follow-up (May 30, 2024). The study was approved (authorization number B2021–002R) by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Xiamen). The requirement of informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.3.2). The normality of quantitative data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, histograms, and Q-Q plots. The homogeneity of variance between the cohorts was tested using Levene’s test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and intergroup comparisons among the treatment cohorts were conducted using independent samples t-tests, categorical variables were expressed as the frequency and proportion [n (%)]. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, data were expressed as the median (P25, P75), and comparisons among cohorts were performed using the rank-sum test.

To address baseline imbalances between the HAIC-based triple therapy and systemic triple therapy groups, we applied inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). The IPTW was based on propensity score to construct the weighted cohort of patients with similar baseline characteristics except for HAIC-based triple therapy group. Propensity score for each patient is estimated by using Logistic regression model upon potential confounding variables with P values less than 0.1 between groups [tumor size, differentiation, cirrhosis, C1-Child–Pugh score, C1-WBC count, C1-N, C1-CRP, C1PCT, and C1-NLR]. The balance of baseline covariates between the two groups after IPTW was assessed according to the absolute standardized mean difference (SMD), and values < 10% were considered well-balanced results. The SMD plot was provided in the supplementary materials. The Kaplan–Meier curve and the log-rank test before and after ITPW were performed for the comparison of OS and PFS. Univariate and weighted cox risk regression models with IPTW were used to assess the effectiveness of baloxavir relative to oseltamivir. The hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CIs were calculated. Based on clinical relevance and pre-specified clinically relevant covariates, the following covariates were evaluated: age, sex, ECOG-PS, baseline liver function parameters and systemic inflammation markers (C1-Child–Pugh class, C1-ALBI grade, C1-NLR), tumor characteristics (tumor size, number of lesions, TNM stage, presence of metastasis, history of resection), and treatment-related factors (first-line treatment regimen, treatment duration, adverse events, maintenance therapy, and receipt of second-line treatment). In addition, post–first-line treatment parameters were also assessed, including tumor size (C2-tumor size), TNM staging (C2-TNM stage), presence of metastasis (C2-metastasis), as well as liver function and systemic inflammation markers (C2-ALBI grade, C2-Child–Pugh class, and C2-NLR). Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis, as well as those with established clinical relevance, were included in multivariate Cox proportional hazards models to assess the impact of baseline and treatment-related variables on OS and PFS before and after IPTW adjustment.

Multivariate analysis of survival status was conducted using a logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of survival outcomes (i.e., survival vs. death). All variables found to be significant in univariate analysis (P < 0.05) were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the strength of association between covariates and survival status. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the 67 participants included in the final analysis, 32(52.2%) received HAIC-based triple therapy and 35(47.8%) systemic triple therapy (Figure S1 and Supplementary Table 1). During the follow-up period, 52 patients (77.6%) continued with maintenance therapy and 45 patients (67.5%) underwent second-line treatment. These treatment trajectories reflect a clinically relevant continuum of care and ensure comparability between the two cohorts in subsequent outcome analyses.

Baseline characteristics were generally comparable between the two treatment groups (Table 1). However, there were some notable differences that required attention. Specifically, the HAIC group presented with larger tumors (79 vs. 45 mm, P = 0.037) and higher systemic inflammatory markers (WBC, neutrophils, NLR, and PCT), indicating a higher inflammatory burden at baseline. On the other hand, the systemic group showed slightly worse liver function as reflected by higher Child–Pugh scores (5(5, 6) versus 5(5, 5), P = 0.046). Other demographic and clinicopathological variables, including age, sex, ECOG-PS, TNM stage, metastasis, vascular invasion, biliary involvement, cirrhosis, ALBI grade, and prior resection, were well-balanced between the two groups. To address these imbalances, we performed IPTW to balance the baseline variables between the two treatment groups (Supplementary Table 2, Figure S2). Standardization by IPTW resulted in a number of confounders that showed good balance between the two groups.

In both cohorts, most patients presented with advanced disease characteristics, including large tumor diameter, multiple lesions, frequent metastatic spread, and a predominance of stage IV disease, reflecting the heavy baseline tumor burden in our study population, underscoring the clinical severity of the included cases.

Efficacy outcomes and post–first-line evaluation

As shown in Table 2, HAIC-based triple therapy achieved superior tumor control compared with systemic therapy. The ORR was significantly higher in the HAIC cohort (59.4 vs. 20.0%; P < 0.001), and the DCR was also significantly better (90.6% vs. 68.6; P < 0.001). Disease progression occurred less frequently in the HAIC group (9.4 vs. 31.4%, P = 0.003). Median tumor shrinkage was significantly greater with HAIC cohort (+ 6.5 mm vs.–1.0 mm, P = 0.002). For local control, the HAIC cohort showed a significantly higher rate (93.8 vs. 66.7%; P = 0.006). Regarding distant control, while the HAIC cohort (90.3%) showed a trend toward better control compared to the systemic therapy cohort (72.7%), the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.071).At the first reassessment (C2, Supplementary Table 2), most patients remained at stage IV with multiple metastases, and intergroup differences in tumor burden or hematologic indices were not significant. However, Child–Pugh scores were worse in the systemic cohort (P = 0.034), suggesting better preservation of hepatic function with HAIC-based triple therapy.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis before and after IPTW weighting

In the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (Fig. 1), a trend toward improved OS was observed in the HAIC-based triple therapy cohort compared to the systemic triple therapy cohort. The median OS was 24.1 months in the HAIC group versus 15.5 months in the systemic therapy group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance in the unadjusted analysis (P = 0.130). Following IPTW to balance baseline confounders between the two groups, the Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a statistically significant OS benefit favoring the HAIC-based regimen (P = 0.010). Furthermore, both before and after IPTW adjustment, the HAIC-based triple therapy cohort showed a significant improvement in PFS compared to the systemic therapy cohort (PFS after IPTW: P = 0.020), indicating that the HAIC-based strategy was associated with superior disease control.

Univariate analysis of prognostic factors

Univariate analysis (Table 3) was performed to identify potential prognostic factors associated with OS. Key variables found to be significantly associated with both OS included prior surgical resection, smaller tumor size, absence of metastasis, earlier TNM stage, better ECOG-PS, longer first-line treatment duration, maintenance therapy, second-line therapy and favorable liver function (C1-ALBI). These variables along with clinically relevant covariates were subsequently incorporated into a multivariate Cox regression model to assess their independent effects on survival outcomes.

Multivariate cox regression analysis before and after IPTW weighting

The multivariate Cox regression analysis (Table 4) showed postoperative recurrence, first-line regimen, treatment duration, maintenance therapy, TNM Stage (C1) and C1-NLR were significantly associated with improved OS. Specifically, HAIC-based therapy demonstrated a protective effect, with a HR of 0.365 (95% CI: 0.166–0.802, P = 0.012) compared to systemic therapy. Following IPTW adjustment, the HR for OS in the HAIC group improved to 0.221 (95% CI: 0.070–0.693, P = 0.010), providing stronger evidence of the effectiveness of HAIC-based therapy. Similarly, HAIC-based therapy remained a significant protective factor for PFS (HR [95%CI]: 0.056 [0.005, 0.634], P = 0.020). After IPTW matching, the HR for PFS was 0.129 (0.028, 0.592), with a P-value of < 0.008, indicating a significant benefit for HAIC-based therapy. The IPTW method effectively mitigated baseline imbalances, offering a more reliable estimate of the treatment’s impact on survival outcomes. These findings underscore the clinical benefit of HAIC-based therapy, not only in terms of survival but also in improving disease control.

Subgroup and logistic regression analyses

When stratified by survival status, the death cohort was characterized by heavier tumor burden (Supplementary Table 4) (multiple metastases: 78.8 vs. 58.8%, P = 0.022; stage IV: 90.9 vs. 67.6%, P = 0.033), poorer liver reserve (ALBI grade 2: 48.5% vs. 23.5%, P = 0.033), and worse ECOG-PS (P = 0.001). At C2 reassessment (Supplementary Table 5), higher NLR (3.44 vs. 2.18, P = 0.009) and more frequent severe AEs (27.3 vs. 11.8%, P = 0.025) were observed in the death group. Moreover, shorter first-line therapy (4.4 vs. 5.9 months, P = 0.030) and more frequent second-line treatment (90.9 vs. 44.1%, P < 0.001) characterized patients with poorer survival (Table 5). Logistic regression (Supplementary Table 6) analyses confirmed second-line treatment (OR = 9.745, P = 0.003) and impaired baseline ALBI (OR = 3.750, P = 0.048) as independent adverse prognostic factors, with ECOG-PS showing a near-significant trend (P = 0.075). Collectively, these findings underscore the prognostic importance of baseline tumor burden, systemic inflammation, functional status, and treatment intensity.

Safety profiles

The incidence and spectrum of treatment-related adverse events were comparable between groups (Table 6). The most frequent targeted-related AEs were hand–foot syndrome, hypertension, and gastrointestinal reactions; immune-related AEs included endocrine–metabolic disorders and immune-mediated inflammation. No treatment-related deaths occurred. Importantly, the incidence of grade ≥ 3 AEs was 22.9% in the systemic group and 15.6% in the HAIC group, consistent with the ~ 25% rate reported in TOPAZ-1[17], supporting the safety of both regimens. Notably, patients who developed immune-related AEs had significantly lower progression rates (14 vs. 45.3%, P = 0.034) (Supplementary Table 7), suggesting a potential link between irAEs and treatment benefit.

Discussion

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma remains a therapeutic challenge, with 5-year survival rates stagnating at 5–10% despite advances in systemic therapy [3]. In this study, HAIC-based triple therapy provided superior tumor control, prolonged survival, and preserved hepatic function with acceptable safety in unresectable iCCA.

Patients in the HAIC-based cohort achieved superior ORR, DCR, and prolonged PFS, with a 12-month PFS rate of 65.1%. Median OS also favored the HAIC cohort (24.1 vs. 15.5 months. These findings are consistent with prior studies showing that HAIC enhances disease control and prolongs survival in hepatobiliary malignancies [9–11]. Xu et al. [18] and Lin et al. [8] both showed a median OS of ~ 16.8 months, while Sun et al. [10] and Zheng et al. [11]. reported OS around 20 months with HAIC + lenvatinib + PD-1. Furthermore, across meta-analyses and retrospective cohorts[15], HAIC combined with a TKI and PD-1/PD-L1 blockade has yielded ORR of 35–50%, DCR of 77–88%, median PFS of 9–12 months, and OS extending beyond 16–21 months, consistently outperforming systemic chemotherapy (ORR 6–21%, PFS 6–8 months, OS 11–15 months). By contrast, our HAIC-based cohort achieved a lower ORR (31.3%) but a notably longer OS (24.1 months) and a 12-month PFS rate of 65.1%. These discrepancies may relate to differences in patient selection, baseline tumor burden, timing of HAIC initiation, and the use of maintenance therapy. These findings reinforce the biological plausibility and clinical potential of HAIC-based triple regimens, while highlighting the need for prospective randomized trials against contemporary chemo-immunotherapy standards.

The biological rationale is strong: HAIC achieves high intratumoral drug concentrations, inducing necrosis and immunogenic cell death, which promote tumor antigen release and dendritic cell (DC) activation, thereby enhancing antigen presentation and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration [12]. Lenvatinib, in addition to its anti-angiogenic effects, normalizes tumor vasculature and alleviates VEGF-mediated immunosuppression, improving immune cell infiltration and function [19]. PD-1/PD-L1 blockade restores the effector function of exhausted T cells and sustains durable antitumor immunity [3, 13–20]. Together, these mechanisms foster an immunogenic tumor microenvironment, enhance lymphocyte recruitment and activation, and maintain long-term immune responses, providing a strong biological rationale for the superior tumor control achieved with HAIC-based triple therapy.

Higher ORR and DCR in the HAIC group underscore its capacity for stronger local disease control. These results align with pivotal trials such as TOPAZ-1 and KEYNOTE-966, where early tumor responses predicted long-term outcomes[5, 6]. In TOPAZ-1[5], gemcitabine/cisplatin + durvalumab achieved an ORR of 26.7%, while the chemotherapy-alone arm had 18.7%. In KEYNOTE-966[5], pembrolizumab + gemcitabine/cisplatin achieved an ORR of 29%, compared to 20.5% in the chemo-alone arm. By comparison, our systemic triple therapy cohort yielded 20.0%, which is slightly lower than TOPAZ-1/KEYNOTE-966 immunotherapy arms but consistent with the chemotherapy-alone arms of these pivotal studies. Importantly, Shi et al. [21] reported much higher ORR (80%) with toripalimab + lenvatinib + GEMOX in a phase II trial of iCCA, but that regimen differs in intensity and patient selection. The generally high baseline tumor burden across both cohorts—characterized by large tumor size, multiple tumors, frequent metastases, and a high proportion of stage IV patients—likely limited overall response rates, underscoring the challenges of achieving tumor shrinkage in such advanced settings.

Patients in the HAIC cohort demonstrated a significantly greater reduction in tumor diameter compared with systemic therapy (Δtumor size –1.0 vs. + 6.5 mm, P = 0.002), supporting the potent local cytotoxic effect of HAIC and its capacity to augment systemic immunotherapy and targeted therapy [21]. The generally high baseline tumor burden across both cohorts—characterized by large tumor size, multiple tumors, frequent metastases, and a high proportion of stage IV patients—likely limited overall response rates, underscoring the challenges of achieving tumor shrinkage in such advanced settings.

Our findings also highlight the translational relevance of systemic inflammatory markers. Elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) have consistently been linked to poor prognosis in hepatobiliary cancers [22]. In our cohort, favorable post-treatment NLR and CRP values correlated with improved OS and PFS, suggesting that systemic inflammation modulates therapeutic responsiveness. These results are consistent with recent studies reporting that dynamic changes in NLR or CRP may serve as predictive biomarkers of immunotherapy efficacy across solid tumors [23, 24]. Mechanistically, persistent systemic inflammation drives immunosuppression through cytokine release and expansion of immunosuppressive cell subsets, thereby attenuating antitumor immunity [24, 25]. Thus, integrating NLR and CRP into clinical prognostication could help identify patients most likely to benefit from HAIC-based regimens.

The safety profiles of HAIC-based and systemic triple therapy were comparable, with overall grade ≥ 3 adverse events (AEs) ranging from 15.6–22.9%, similar to the ~ 25% reported in the TOPAZ-1 trial [17]. The most frequent toxicities—hypertension, hand-foot syndrome, and myelosuppression—were manageable, and no treatment-related deaths occurred. Importantly, immune-related adverse events (irAEs) correlated with improved efficacy, as patients experiencing irAEs had significantly lower progression rates, echoing evidence that irAEs may serve as surrogate markers of effective immune activation [26].

This study has inherent limitations. Although IPTW was applied to adjust for key baseline differences, the study remains retrospective and conducted at a single center, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. The relatively small sample size and heterogeneity of subsequent treatments also represent potential limitations, as these factors may introduce variability in the results. Despite these challenges, the use of IPTW was effective in addressing baseline imbalances, providing a more reliable comparison between the treatment groups. However, unmeasured confounders such as physician preference may still have influenced outcomes despite multivariate adjustment. Additionally, the initially considered non-triple cohort was excluded due to its small size and heterogeneity. While this decision improved the clarity and interpretability of our analyses, it inevitably restricted the generalizability of the findings to patients receiving alternative regimens. Despite these limitations, the consistent superiority of HAIC-based triple therapy over systemic triple therapy in terms of PFS and OS highlights its potential clinical value, particularly in patients with high tumor burden or liver-dominant disease. Prospective, multicenter randomized trials directly comparing HAIC-based strategies with the current chemo-immunotherapy standards are warranted to confirm these findings.

In conclusion, HAIC-based triple therapy demonstrated superior tumor control and prolonged survival, with manageable toxicity, compared with systemic triple therapy in unresectable iCCA. The integration of inflammatory biomarkers such as NLR and CRP provides additional translational relevance and may aid in optimizing patient selection. These results highlight the promise of combining locoregional chemotherapy, anti-angiogenic therapy, and immune checkpoint blockade as a biologically grounded strategy for advanced iCCA, warranting confirmation in larger prospective randomized trials.
