A novel dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis: Proof-of-concept in an ovine model
Authors: Jinmiao Chen, Xiaojie Gu, Lai Wei, Sun Pan, Huan Liu, Krzysztof Bartus, Nianwei Zhou, Shengzhang Wang, Tao Hong, Chunsheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveConventional large mitral bioprostheses may cause left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. A dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis was designed for both surgical and transcatheter valve replacement.MethodsPulsatile flow testing was used to measure the effective orifice area (EOA). Computational fluid dynamics analysis was conducted to assess thrombogenic and hemolysis risk. An ovine model was used to evaluate procedural safety, hemodynamic performance, and leaflet calcification.ResultsPulsatile flow testing showed that the EOAs of surgical dual-orifice valve (17K, 18K, 19K, 20K, 21K) were 2.0 ± 0.2, 2.1 ± 0.3, 2.4 ± 0.4, 2.5 ± 0.1, and 2.7 ± 0.3 cm2 under 5 L/min cardiac output, respectively. The mean hemolysis index and platelet activation state of 19K surgical dual-orifice valve under 7 L/min cardiac output were 1.3 × 10−8 and 2.2 × 10−8 in the computational fluid dynamics analysis, respectively. Six ovine underwent implantation of the 19K surgical dual-orifice valve and were followed for 20 weeks. The mean pressure gradient and EOA were 3.7 ± 0.8 mm Hg and 2.7 ± 0.3 cm2 immediately after implantation and 5.0 ± 2.3 mm Hg and 2.5 ± 0.2 cm2 at 20 weeks, respectively. One ovine was implanted the 19K transcatheter dual-orifice valve and followed up for 125 days. The mean pressure gradient was 5.0 mm Hg immediately after implantation and 3.0 mm Hg at the end point.ConclusionsThe dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis may represent an alternative design for mitral valve replacement, especially for patients with large mitral annuli. This findings also show the potential of this design in transcatheter mitral valve replacement.Key Words: dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, surgical mitral valve replacement, transcatheter mitral valve replacement

The novel surgical and transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis.

Central Message

Dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis may be an alternative design strategy for surgical or transcatheter mitral valve replacement, especially for patients with large mitral annuli.

Perspective

Surgical and transcatheter implantation of dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis was feasible with acceptable hemodynamic performance, low thrombogenic potential, and favorable short-term outcomes in an ovine model. These findings support the dual-orifice design as a potential alternative for surgical or transcatheter mitral valve replacement for patients with large mitral annuli.

Currently, surgical and transcatheter aortic valve replacement have been recognized as the mainstream therapies for aortic valve disease.1 The design of 3 leaflets with 3 commissures, inspired by the native aortic valve, has been fully verified in clinical use by hemodynamic performance and the durability of both surgical and transcatheter bioprostheses in the aortic position.2, 3, 4 Although mitral valve repair remains the first-line recommendation for mitral valve disease, mitral valve replacement (MVR) is needed for some unrepairable conditions.1 However, the mitral valve presents a saddle-shaped bileaflet structure with chordae tendineae, whereas the native aortic valve is a circular trileaflet structure. These morphologic differences contribute to challenges such as left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction and potentially limited durability when aortic valve–derived designs are applied to the mitral position.5,6

To address these limitations, a novel mitral bioprosthesis with dual-orifice configuration (Shanghai Cingular Biotech Corporation) was designed for both surgical and transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR). The dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis is constructed from 2 stented bioprosthetic valves assembled in parallel, creating 2 independent flow orifices—hence the designation “dual-orifice.” The surgical dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis was used to test hemodynamic performance, thrombogenic and hemolytic potential, and the procedural feasibility of dual-orifice design in vitro and in vivo evaluation. Transcatheter implantation of dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis was also tentatively validated in an ovine model.

Methods

Device

The surgical dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis (17K, 18K, 19K, 20K, and 21K) was fabricated by suturing 2 single-orifice bovine pericardial valve components onto an Elgiloy stent of dual-orifice configuration with an integrated suture ring. The single-orifice bovine pericardial valve was validated in our previous clinical trial.7, 8, 9 The diameter of each single-orifice component was 17 mm (17K), 18 mm (18K), and increased accordingly for larger sizes (Figure 1).

The novel dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis. A-C, Surgical dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis. D-F, Transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis. G, The transapical delivery system.

The transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis replacement system comprised dual-orifice mitral bioprostheses (17K, 19K, and 21K) and a transapical delivery system. The main stent of dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis consisted of an elliptical outer stent and 2 parallel straight cylindrical stents with integrated valves. The outer stent was wrapped and sutured with skirt fabric. Barbs were distributed at the outer stent to improve anchoring performance. Each single-orifice valve was assembled by suturing a nitinol frame, a sealing skirt, and the valve leaflets together (Figure 1). The delivery system consisted of 3 components: a delivery sheath, a dilator, and a dedicated system designed for loading the dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis (Figure 1).

Pulsatile Flow Testing

Three samples for each size of surgical dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis were tested using a pulsatile flow tester (Shanghai Heartpartner Testing Equipment Co, Ltd) calibrated with a St Jude Masters Series Mechanical Valve. Effective orifice area (EOA) and mean pressure gradient (MPG) were tested under 70 cycles/min heart rate, 35 ± 5% systolic duration, 120 ± 5 mm Hg peak differential pressure across the closed valve, and cardiac output (CO) varied sequentially (2, 3.5, 5, and 7 L/min).

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) Assessment

For hemodynamic evaluation, the smallest dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis (17K) was designated as the worst-case condition, because smaller orifices elevated local flow velocity and shear stress, thereby increasing risks of platelet activation, hemolysis, and thrombosis. The mid-sized valve (19K) was selected as the typical model. Both valves were tested in a pulsatile flow loop filled with a 60:40 water:glycerol mixture, of which hemodynamic properties was analogous to blood. A particle image velocimetry system (TSI Incorporated) was used to acquire flow-field data at COs of 2 and 7 L/min. Valve motion and flow field parameters were extracted as boundary conditions for fluid-solid coupling modeling. CFD simulations were conducted on the ANSYS Workbench FLUENT platform to compute the flow fields and shear stress distributions of the valves. The hemolysis index (HI) and platelet activation state (PAS) were calculated to evaluate the risk of hemolysis and thrombosis.

Animal Experiments

Implantations of both surgical and transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprostheses in a juvenile ovine model were conducted at Suzhou Golden Wing Medical Inc, and the study protocol and publication of data were approved by its Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (GW23065I01 for surgical implantation, approved at October 20, 2023 and GW23013I01 for transcatheter implantation, approved at May 18, 2023). All animals received humane care in compliance with the 2011 Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Six healthy male ovine underwent surgical implantation of a 19K dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis and were killed humanely at 20 weeks postoperatively. Each animal was placed in the right lateral position, then a left lateral thoracotomy was made between the fourth and fifth ribs and the rib was resected. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established by inserting an arterial perfusion cannula via the ascending aorta and a venous drainage cannula via the right atrial appendage. An incision was made in the left atrial appendage to expose the mitral valve, after which the anterior leaflet was excised. The surgical dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis was then implanted using an interrupted suture technique.

A pilot study was conducted to test the feasibility of transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis in a healthy male ovine. A left lateral thoracotomy was made to expose cardiac apex. A purse-string suture was placed on the apex. A puncture sheath was advanced into the heart via the purse-string suture, and a guidewire was inserted through the sheath to reach the left atrium. Then, the sheath was withdrawn. A delivery sheath with a dilator was advanced along the guidewire to the left atrium. Then, the dilator and guidewire were removed, and the delivery sheath was kept in the position. Finally, the transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis delivery system was advanced and released under the guidance of fluoroscopy and transesophageal echocardiography.

Warfarin was used for anticoagulation after implantation of the surgical and transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis. The international normalized ratio was targeted to be maintained within the range of 1.5 to 2.5 after implantation.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography was performed to measure EOA, mean flow velocity, MPG, and regurgitation of the dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis immediately after implantation and during postoperative follow-up to assess hemodynamic performance. Planimetry was used to measure EOA. The ultrasonic plane was adjusted to be parallel to the maximal opening position of the valve plane. When the valve was fully open, manual tracing was performed along the inner edge of the valve orifice to calculate EOA. Spectral Doppler was applied to measure the peak transvalvular flow velocity and MPG. The ultrasonic plane was adjusted to be parallel to the blood flow direction (with the incident angle less than 20°). The sample volume (approximately 2-4 mm) of pulsed-wave Doppler was accurately positioned at the level of the mitral valve leaflet tips to capture diastolic blood flow signals, followed by the calculation of the peak flow velocity and MPG.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The Insight 4G (TSI Incorporated), Tecplot 360 (Tecplot, Inc), and MATLAB (The MathWorks, Inc) were used for processing the particle image velocimetry and CFD data.

Results

Pulsatile Flow Testing

A total of 15 dual-orifice mitral bioprostheses underwent pulsatile flow testing. During pulsatile flow testing, the leaflet opening and closing movements of each valve showed no clinically significant difference from traditional valve. The leaflets of the 2 orifices opened and closed synchronously with no measurable time difference (Figure E1). The MPG and EOA of all valve sizes exhibited a clinically significant upward trend with the increasing of CO (Table E1). Under low-flow conditions, EOA did not show an increasing trend with the enlargement of valve sizes.

Pulsatile flow testing. A, Valve closed. B, Valve open.

CFD Assessment

CFD flow fields for the surgical dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis were calculated at flow rates of 2 L/min and 7 L/min for the 17K and 19K valve sizes (Figure E2). For a given valve size, shear stress, HI and PAS exhibited greater mean, minimum, median, and maximum values at 7 L/min CO compared with 2 L/min CO. However, at the same CO, only minor variations were observed between the 17K and 19K valve sizes. The greatest mean HI value (1.3 × 10−8) was observed in the CFD results for the 19K surgical dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis at a CO of 7 L/min, whereas the greatest mean PAS value (2.7 × 10−8) occurred in the CFD results for the 17K valve under the same 7 L/min condition (Table E2).

Computational fluid dynamics assessment. A, 17K surgical dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis. B, 19K surgical dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis.

Surgical MVR

Six animals were successfully implanted with the 19K surgical dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis and were killed humanely at 20 weeks for necropsy and examination. MPG, mean flow velocity, and EOA were assessed by echocardiography. Over a 20-week period, the MPG increased gradually from 3.7 mm Hg to 5.0 mm Hg. Mean flow velocity increased gradually from 88.5 cm/s to 97.6 cm/s. The EOA decreased gradually from 2.7 cm2 to 2.5 cm2 (Table 1 and Figure 2). No leaflet calcification and thrombosis were found (Figure 3). No aortic regurgitation was detected. The mean and peak pressure gradient of LVOT were low after implantation (Table E3). All animals exhibited normal cardiac structures without evidence of thrombosis or infarction. Only one case had mild paravalvular leakage. All bioprostheses achieved satisfactory endothelialization, and no leaflet thickening or calcification was detected (Figure E3).

Transcatheter Mitral Valve Replacement

One animal underwent successful transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis replacement and was killed humanely at 125 days for necropsy and examination. The transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis was delivered through the apex and released in a single step at the appropriate position (Figure 4 and Video 1). Mild paravalvular leakage was present after the operation (Figure 5). During follow-up, the mean flow velocity decreased from 108.0 cm/s to 81.9 cm/s, the MPG decreased from 5.0 mm Hg to 3.0 mm Hg, and the EOA remained stable with no clinically significant changes. The mean and peak pressure gradient of LVOT after implantation were 1 and 2 mm Hg, respectively. The inflow and outflow of the leaflet surfaces were smooth without abnormal tissue proliferation or thrombus formation. Both the inner and outer stents exhibited normal morphology without evidence of deformation or structural damage. The dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis had good endothelialization (Figure 5). Radiographic imaging revealed the absence of calcification (Figure E3).

Discussion

This study presented a concept of novel dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis and provided a comprehensive preclinical evaluation integrating in vitro hemodynamic testing, CFD analysis, and short-term in vivo implantation in an ovine model. The principal findings were that the dual-orifice design achieved EOA and MPG within accepted ranges for mitral bioprostheses, demonstrated low predicted thrombogenic and hemolytic potential under high-flow conditions, and can be implanted successfully via both surgical and transcatheter approaches in a large-animal model.

The dual-orifice concept was developed to address several persistent challenges associated with MVR, particularly when adapting prosthetic designs derived from the trileaflet aortic valve to the anatomically and functionally distinct mitral position. Large single-orifice mitral prostheses may increase the risk of LVOT obstruction and can be difficult to anchor securely in dilated or elliptical annuli, especially in the context of TMVR. Although many new devices and techniques aimed to solve this problem, it was still a big challenge for patient selection for TMVR.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 By dividing the inflow area into 2 parallel orifices, the present design reduced the effective stent height and altered the spatial profile of the bioprosthesis, potentially mitigating these challenges (Figure E4).

Schematic diagram of implantation of conventional and dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis. A, Schematic diagram of heart valves. B, Implantation of conventional bioprosthesis in mitral position. C, Implantation of dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis in mitral position.

A fundamental concern of the dual-orifice configuration was the theoretical loss of effective flow area at the junction between the 2 orifices. In the present study, pulsatile flow testing demonstrated that the EOA values of valve sizes ranging from 17K to 21K increased appropriately with CO and fell within ranges reported for commercially available mitral bioprostheses of comparable nominal size. Importantly, the mid-sized 19K valve achieved an EOA that was comparable with literature-reported values for conventional single-orifice mitral valves under similar test conditions.7, 8, 9,16, 17, 18 These findings suggested that the dual-orifice configuration could provide adequate forward flow capacity in vitro despite partial geometric area loss.

The in vivo ovine data further supported the hemodynamic adequacy of the design. After surgical implantation of the 19K dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis, the MPG remained low immediately after implantation and increased only modestly over 20 weeks of follow-up, whereas EOA values remained stable. Similarly, the transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis demonstrated stable gradients and orifice area during midterm follow-up in a single feasibility animal. Although these observations were descriptive and based on a limited sample size, they indicated that the dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis can maintain acceptable hemodynamic performance in the mitral position over the short term. Moreover, the main targeted candidate of this dual-orifice bioprosthesis was the patient with mitral valve disease and a large mitral annulus.

Another theoretical limitation of the dual-orifice design was the potential for increasing local flow velocity and shear stress because of the smaller diameter of each individual orifice, which could elevate the risk of hemolysis and thrombosis. To address this concern, CFD analysis informed by particle image velocimetry data was performed under both low- and high-flow conditions. The calculated HI and PAS values were low across valve sizes and COs, including under worst-case conditions. These results were consistent with reported values for established mitral bioprostheses and suggested that the dual-orifice geometry did not introduce excessive shear-related blood damage in silico.19,20 The in vivo findings corroborated these computational predictions. No macroscopic or histopathologic evidence of leaflet thrombosis, abnormal tissue proliferation, or calcification was observed in the surgical cohort at 20 weeks or in the transcatheter feasibility case at 125 days. Leaflet surfaces were smooth, and endothelialization of the prosthesis was satisfactory. Although longer follow-up and larger cohorts are required to draw definitive conclusions, these early results support the hemocompatibility of the dual-orifice design in a preclinical setting.

Beyond hemodynamics and blood compatibility, the dual-orifice architecture may offer practical advantages for TMVR. The reduced stent height and altered inflow geometry may lessen interaction with the LVOT, a critical concern in the field of TMVR. In the present study, the mean and peak pressure gradient of LVOT after implantation were very low, which initially validated our hypothesis. In addition, the transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis incorporated a hybrid-anchoring strategy to enhance fixation in noncircular and dilated mitral annuli. Although anchoring stability was satisfactory in the present feasibility experiment, systematic evaluation of anchoring forces and migration resistance was beyond the scope of this study. The further improvement and update of the transcatheter dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis is ongoing.

Limitations

The present work had several important limitations. First, the number of animals was small, particularly in the transcatheter cohort, precluding statistical comparisons or robust assessment of variability. Second, the follow-up duration was insufficient to evaluate long-term durability, structural valve degeneration, or late calcification. Although the 6-leaflet configuration may theoretically reduce stress on individual leaflets, this hypothesis was not directly tested and cannot be confirmed by the current data. Third, comparisons with conventional mitral bioprostheses were indirect and determined on the basis of published benchmarks rather than head-to-head testing. Despite these limitations, this study established the feasibility of a dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis and provided a foundation for further investigation. Future studies should focus on longer-term durability testing, larger in vivo cohorts, direct comparison with contemporary mitral prostheses, and detailed assessment of LVOT interaction and anchoring performance in anatomically relevant models.

Conclusions

In this preclinical proof-of-concept study, a novel dual-orifice mitral bioprosthesis demonstrated acceptable hemodynamic performance, low predicted thrombogenic and hemolytic potential, and favorable short-term in vivo outcomes in an ovine model. Both surgical and transcatheter implantation were feasible with stable hemodynamic performance during midterm follow-up. These findings support further development of the dual-orifice concept as a potential alternative design for MVR, especially for patients with large mitral annuli.
