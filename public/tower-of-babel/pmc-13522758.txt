A unified framework and benchmark for generalizable biomedical knowledge extraction and applications with large language models
Authors: Wuyang Lan, Siqi Zhang, Wenzheng Wang, Ke Hu, Tianrun Gao, Lei Shi, Zongbo Han, Yanjun Chen, Hao Zhang, Song Wu, Xiaohong Liu, Guangyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBiomedical information extraction (BIE) is fundamental for transforming unstructured biomedical text into structured, computable knowledge, yet the effectiveness of large language models (LLMs) remains limited by dataset heterogeneity and lack of unified benchmarks. We present InfoFlowEX, a unified framework for generalizable biomedical knowledge extraction with LLMs. InfoFlowEX incorporates an automated data integration pipeline using ontology-guided alignment to construct BIE-Corpus, a large-scale multi-domain benchmark unifying 40 public datasets for named entity recognition and relation extraction. We further introduce a task-conditioned schema instruction tuning strategy encoding 28 biomedical entity and relation types into a schema codebase, enabling LLMs to align heterogeneous annotations and generalize across settings. Finally, we evaluated InfoFlowEX in diverse applications, including evidence retrieval for question-answering, clinical diagnosis from electronic health records, and knowledge graph expansion. Results demonstrate that InfoFlowEX equips LLMs with robust adaptability, achieving consistent gains over baselines with minimal task-specific customization, highlighting InfoFlowEX for real-world biomedical applications.

Introduction

Biomedical knowledge is predominantly embedded in the unstructured text of millions of scientific publications and clinical records. With the rapid growth of biomedical text, there is an increasingly urgent need for scalable and reliable methods to transform this unstructured information into computable and reusable knowledge. Biomedical knowledge extraction addresses this challenge by automatically converting unstructured biomedical text into structured knowledge representations. Biomedical information extraction (BIE) provides the techniques for this process by identifying entities and relations from unstructured corpora (e.g., scientific literature and electronic health records [EHRs]) and transforming them into structured knowledge1 through tasks such as named entity recognition2 (NER) and relation extraction3 (RE). As a fundamental step toward knowledge formalization, biomedical knowledge extraction underpins a wide range of downstream biomedical applications, such as clinical decision support,4 literature mining,5 and drug discovery.6

Traditional machine-learning approaches for BIE rely heavily on large amounts of manually annotated, domain-specific data, making the process costly, labor-intensive, and fundamentally non-scalable.7 Recent advances in large language models (LLMs), particularly decoder-based generative models (e.g., GPT-4o8 and Llama9), have demonstrated potential for information extraction (IE) by enabling a task-unified, instruction-driven paradigm. In this setting, a single model can be prompted to perform diverse IE tasks, such as extracting different types of entities10 and relations,11without task-specific architectures.

Despite these advances, substantial challenges remain before LLM-based biomedical knowledge extraction can be reliably deployed in real-world applications. First, existing BIE datasets are highly heterogeneous and fragmented, resulting in the lack of unified benchmarks for evaluating biomedical knowledge extraction systems. For example, a protein-based drug is labeled as a chemical in DrugProt,12 whereas the same entity type is annotated as a gene product in AIMed.13 As these datasets were developed independently for distinct research objectives and follow inconsistent annotation, integrating them into a large, unified resource is highly challenging. Second, although current LLMs can be instruction-tuned for extraction tasks, they do not inherently adhere to biomedical annotation schemas out of the box. Most existing instruction-tuning strategies14 only provide label names in prompts (e.g., “TASK: the task is to extract disease entities in a sentence.”15), while the underlying label definitions vary substantially across datasets. As a result, current LLM-based methods lack an effective mechanism for generalizing across unseen biomedical domains with heterogeneous annotation guidelines. Therefore, a unified framework and benchmark capable of systematically integrating heterogeneous biomedical resources and enabling schema-consistent and generalizable LLM training remains an open challenge.

To address these issues, we introduce InfoFlowEX, a unified framework for generalizable biomedical knowledge extraction with LLMs. The proposed framework consists of two major components designed to support automated data processing and efficient schema-aware LLM development. First, to mitigate data heterogeneity across existing BIE corpora, we develop an automated data integration pipeline that employs an ontology-guided alignment across heterogeneous biomedical annotation schemas. Leveraging this pipeline, we construct BIE-Corpus, a large-scale, multi-domain biomedical knowledge extraction corpus that integrates 40 publicly available datasets into a standardized resource. Building upon BIE-Corpus, we further establish a comprehensive benchmark to systematically evaluate the performance of current LLMs, including both general-purpose (e.g., Falcon,16 Qwen,17 Codellama,18 Deepseek,19 Mistral,20 and LlaMA39) and domain-specific (e.g., MMed-Llama21) models, on NER and RE tasks across diverse biomedical domains. Furthermore, we introduce a task-conditioned schema instruction tuning method that is model-agnostic and readily applicable to existing LLMs, enabling schema-aware domain adaptation for biomedical text extraction and generalization across multiple downstream tasks. The proposed method adopts a code-based schema representation to unify BIE tasks. Conditioned on the target task, the most relevant schemas are dynamically selected from the schema collection to construct task-adaptive instructions, ensuring efficient and targeted domain adaptation.

Finally, we perform a comprehensive evaluation of InfoFlowEX on three representative biomedical application scenarios, including evidence retrieval for biomedical question-answering, clinical disease diagnosis on real-world EHRs, and dynamic knowledge graph expansion from biomedical literature. The results demonstrate that InfoFlowEX facilitates robust adaptation of LLMs to diverse biomedical applications, achieving consistent performance improvements with minimal task-specific customization, highlighting its generalization capability for biomedical knowledge extraction and real-world biomedical applications.

The framework comprises two major components: an automated data integration pipeline that harmonizes heterogeneous BIE datasets into a unified corpus, and a task-conditioned schema instruction tuning method that enables efficient, schema-aware domain adaptation of LLMs across diverse biomedical domains (Figures 1A and 1B). To address the challenges of fragmented and heterogeneous BIE datasets, we develop an automated data integration pipeline that aligns multi-domain annotations to a unified ontology (Figure 1A). This ontology is first constructed with 28 biomedical semantic entity and relation types, derived from Biomedical Informatics Ontology System (BIOS),22 which is a widely used biomedical knowledge graph (BioKG) (Table S1). These categories span multiple biological scales, from genes, proteins, cells, diseases, and anatomical structures to therapeutic interventions. Built upon this, we train a biomedical term embedding model that automatically projects entity type names from diverse datasets into a shared embedding space and aligns them to the predefined ontology (see more details in STAR Methods). To validate alignment quality, we have performed the evaluation on a manually constructed gold-standard set across three internal datasets (BC5CDR,23 NCBI-Disease,24 and DrugProt12) and one external dataset (ChemProt25). The results demonstrate that the ontology alignment pipeline generalizes across both internal and external data, with an overall macro F1 of 0.947 across 3,673 entities (Table S2). Applying this pipeline to 40 high-quality public datasets yields BIE-Corpus (Table S3), a large-scale, ontology-aligned benchmark, containing 184,000 samples, 12 million tokens, and 626,237 curated entity mentions, which represents a comprehensive multi-domain resource for BIE to date. Based on the BIE-Corpus, we further establish a comprehensive benchmark, including NER-bench and RE-bench (see more details in STAR Methods), to evaluate the state-of-the-art open-source LLMs covering both general-purpose models (e.g., Falcon,16 Qwen,17 Codellama,18 Deepseek,19 Mistral,20 and LlaMA39) and domain-specific MMed-Llama21 (Table S4). This benchmark reveals significant performance variability on NER and RE tasks across diverse biomedical domains, providing actionable insights for selecting and deploying extraction models in real-world biomedical applications.

Furthermore, to support generalizable biomedical knowledge extraction, we propose a task-conditioned schema instruction tuning strategy based on a biomedical schema collection comprising 28 canonical entity and relation types (Figure 1B). Each schema is encoded in a code-based representation, formalized as a Python class that captures the semantic definition of the label, type guideline, and representative examples (see more details in STAR Methods and Table S5). Leveraging this schema representation, instance-specific instructions are constructed by combining the schema definition with the corresponding input text and target annotations. Conditioned on the target task, the most task-relevant schemas are dynamically selected, enabling precise, schema-aware tuning and supporting dynamic adaptation of LLMs across diverse biomedical tasks. To evaluate the contribution of individual schema design components within InfoFlowEX, we conducted ablation experiments to evaluate the removal of candidate examples and negative schemas, using the full BIE-Corpus training set and a stratified 10% sample of the test set for evaluation efficiency (Table S6). The results confirm that each schema component contributes meaningfully to the overall performance of InfoFlowEX. Finally, to comprehensively validate the applicability of InfoFlowEX, we conduct experiments across three representative biomedical scenarios, including (1) entity-enhanced retrieval for biomedical question answering (BioQA), (2) EHR-based entity extraction for disease classification, and (3) biomedical RE for dynamic knowledge graph expansion (Figure 1C).

Overall, the models exhibit substantial limitations in maintaining consistent entity extraction performance across diverse domains. For example, under the fine-tuning setting (Figure 2B; Table S7), models achieve comparatively high performance in the chemical category, with Llama3 emerging as the top-performing model, achieving a macro F1 score of 0.500 (95% CI: 0.493–0.506). These findings reveal substantial domain-specific performance fluctuations across LLMs, indicating that current approaches still lack effective mechanisms to adapt to unseen domains and highlighting the need for a task-adaptive BIE framework. To address this gap, we introduce InfoFlowEX, and evaluate its effectiveness by comparing the strongest baseline, LLaMA3 (with the highest average performance across all domains) in the following experiments.

To further assess the generalizability of InfoFlowEX beyond the BIE-Corpus training distribution, we evaluated it on five external biomedical NER datasets not included in BIE-Corpus: Drugs,26 ChemProt,25 miRNA,27 ProteinStruct,28 and BioRED.29 Across all five datasets, InfoFlowEX consistently outperformed standard fine-tuning (Table S12), confirming its generalizability to unseen corpora.

Entity-enhanced retrieval for biomedical question answering

BioQA systems are designed to provide accurate and reliable answers to natural language questions in the biomedical domain.30 However, the rapid growth of biomedical literature presents a significant challenge for evidence retrieval due to the sheer volume of information. A key limitation of current mainstream retrieval strategies is their tendency to neglect granular, entity-level semantics, resulting in retrieval inaccuracies and the failure to identify crucial information.31

To overcome the aforementioned limitations, we enhance the retrieval process by integrating InfoFlowEX, which incorporates biomedical entities as an auxiliary signal to inject fine-grained entity-level cues into the retrieval mechanism (Figure 3A, see more details in STAR Methods). We evaluate the BioQA accuracy of Llama3 with and without the InfoFlowEX-based entity enhancement under both sparse and dense retrieval settings (Figures 3B and 3C; Figures 3D and 3E). Specifically, sparse retrieval is exemplified by the BM25 algorithm32 and dense retrieval leverages semantic representations, typically from a sentence-transformer model33 (see more details in STAR Methods). Additionally, we conduct validation on the PubmedQA dataset,34 which covers seven major Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), including anatomy & diseases, organisms, drugs & chemicals, techniques & analytical, diagnostic, phenomena & processes, named groups, and health care categories. EHR-based entity extraction and disease classification

EHRs serve as a key source of clinical information fundamental to medical diagnosis. However, since this information is predominantly recorded in unstructured natural language, directly performing diagnosis on raw text makes models highly sensitive to noise and documentation variability, limiting their robustness and hindering clinical practice.35 In contrast, diagnostic classification models built upon structured entities extracted from the text can substantially enhance transparency by grounding predictions in clinically meaningful concepts.

Therefore, we extend InfoFlowEX to establish an automated pipeline for diagnostic analysis from EHRs, which begins by leveraging a BIE model (Llama3) to transform unstructured clinical text into structured entities, and then feeds these structured data into a machine learning model (logistic regression36) for diagnostic analysis (see more details in STAR Methods). To evaluate the effectiveness of biomedical entities extracted via InfoFlowEX in mining EHRs for diagnosis, we curate a real-world EHR dataset, MedDX-MD8, from MedDX,37 composed of approximately 40,000 records from eight medical departments, including pulmonology, gastroenterology, urology, cardiology, immunology, psychiatry, neurology, and endocrinology (see more details in STAR Methods).

Furthermore, to evaluate the dynamic and domain-adaptive capability of InfoFlowEX, we conduct a controlled experiment on the fine-tuned Llama3 enhanced by InfoFlowEX under two prompt settings: one utilizing all 25 schema types and the other using only clinically relevant schemas, referred to as the specialist setting (Figures 4D and 4E; Table S10). Compared with the models using all schemas, the specialist setting achieves the highest performance, with a top-3 accuracy score of 0.885 (95% CI: 0.861–0.909) when averaged across all tasks (Figure 4D). Similar trends are observed across different departments, where selecting the most relevant domain-specific schemas further improves model performance, with gains of up to 5% accuracy (e.g., cardiology). The results indicate the superior domain-adaptation capability of the constructed schemas, highlighting their effectiveness in capturing clinically relevant information.

To further assess the clinical interpretability of extracted entities during disease diagnosis, we employ Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) plots to visualize the impact of entities on the diagnostic process for grade 3 hypertension (Figure 4F). In the visualization, red indicators represent feature values that positively contribute to the prediction of the disease. In the case of grade 3 hypertension, the features flagged by the fine-tuned Llama3 primarily consisted of generic medical entities, such as “tablet,” “capsule,” “sustained-release form,” and “injection”. In contrast, Llama3 enhanced by InfoFlowEX captures more specific, disease-relevant features, such as “serum albumin” and “nifedipine”. According to previous studies, serum albumin levels are crucial for the diagnosis of hypertension,38,39 while nifedipine is a recommended first-line treatment for severe hypertension.40 This alignment with medical consensus demonstrates that InfoFlowEX enables the extraction of clinically meaningful and discriminative features for accurate and interpretable diagnosis.

Notably, the results indicate that InfoFlowEX is effective at uncovering biologically meaningful relations. For example, it successfully extracts a previously unrecorded association between IL-6 and JAK2, a linkage that is supported by existing biological evidence showing that IL-6 can promote hepatocellular carcinoma progression by activating JAK2, which in turn phosphorylates STAT3.44 Building on this finding, and given the analogous oncogenic role of JAK2 activation, we can further hypothesize a potential synergistic interaction between IL-6 and CCL2. We independently searched the existing biomedical literature as post hoc validation and found supporting evidence45 confirming the biological plausibility of this framework-generated hypothesis. This finding has also been supported by the literature,45 reporting that medulloblastoma cells exploit a CCL2/CCR2/IL-6 signaling loop to enhance their invasiveness. These results further demonstrate that InfoFlowEX is capable of extracting entity relations from existing literature, thereby enabling timely updates of BioKGs, which might potentially facilitate the generation of scientific hypotheses.

Discussion

Despite the rapid progress of LLMs, robust biomedical knowledge extraction in real-world applications remains fundamentally constrained by two practical challenges. First, existing benchmarking resources are highly fragmented, with incompatible annotation conventions that reflect divergent implicit ontologies rather than mere labeling noise. Second, current LLM-based extraction paradigms exhibit limited robustness in adhering to domain-specific schemas when transferred across heterogeneous biomedical corpora. The primary challenge in BIE lies in enabling rapid adaptation across diverse biomedical sub-domains, where annotation scope, semantic granularity, and conceptual definitions vary substantially.46 Although LLMs offer powerful semantic understanding and flexible generative capabilities, directly applying them to unseen domains or naively fine-tuning them on mixtures of heterogeneous datasets does not fundamentally resolve these challenges.47 Instead, such strategies often amplify inconsistencies in supervision, leading to brittle generalization behavior under schema shifts.

Our analysis shows that dataset heterogeneity across BIE datasets primarily arises from differences in task objectives and annotation guidelines rather than incidental labeling inconsistencies. For example, datasets curated under different biological assumptions assign distinct semantic roles to identical entities: JNLPBA48 distinguishes DNA, RNA, and cell lines as separate entity types, whereas BC2GM49 collapses these biologically distinct concepts into a single “gene” category. Similarly, a protein-based therapy is labeled as a “chemical” in DrugProt,12 but as a “gene product” in AIMed,13 reflecting task-specific perspectives rather than annotation errors. Even when concepts are semantically aligned, naming conventions remain inconsistent, as adverse reactions are explicitly labeled as “adverse effect” in the ADE corpus50 but often subsumed under broader “disease” categories in BC5CDR.23 These discrepancies represent conflicting implicit ontologies that undermine the effectiveness of cross-dataset learning and limit the upper bound of model generalization.

To address this issue, we present an automated integration pipeline based on ontology-guided alignment, enabling the consolidation of heterogeneous annotation schemas through semantic similarity rather than manual rule design. This approach facilitates the construction of BIE-Corpus, a large-scale, standardized benchmark integrating 40 publicly available datasets across multiple biomedical domains. By aligning labels at the semantic level, BIE-Corpus serves not only as a training resource but also as a unified benchmark for evaluating cross-domain generalization in biomedical knowledge extraction. Importantly, this integration strategy reframes dataset unification as a scalable ontology consolidation problem, rather than a dataset-specific engineering effort.

However, a unified corpus alone is insufficient to ensure robust domain adaptation. Effective transfer across biomedical domains also requires learning mechanisms that can explicitly account for schema variability. Existing BIE approaches, including dataset-specific fine-tuning and universal instruction-based extraction, often struggle to reliably enforce precise schema constraints,51 particularly as the label space grows or task definitions become more complex. This limitation becomes especially pronounced when LLMs are deployed to unseen corpora with annotation conventions that differ from those encountered during training.47 To this end, we propose a task-conditioned schema instruction tuning paradigm within the InfoFlowEX framework, which treats extraction schemas as reusable inductive biases rather than static task definitions. By organizing schemas into a modular library and dynamically selecting task-relevant subsets during training, this approach enables LLMs to adapt to unseen domains while preserving strict schema awareness. Notably, this strategy is model-agnostic and can be applied across different LLM backbones, offering a scalable alternative to dataset-specific fine-tuning. Conceptually, this paradigm shifts instruction tuning from a task-centric formulation to a schema-centric learning process, which is critical for achieving generalizable biomedical knowledge extraction across heterogeneous biomedical settings.

In summary, we present InfoFlowEX, a unified framework for generalizable biomedical knowledge extraction with LLMs. By integrating an automated ontology-guided alignment pipeline with a task-conditioned schema paradigm, we construct BIE-Corpus, a large-scale, standardized benchmark derived from 40 public datasets and demonstrate generalizability across multiple domains and applications. Beyond improving intrinsic extraction metrics, InfoFlowEX provides a reusable infrastructure for transforming unstructured biomedical text into interoperable, computable knowledge, thereby supporting multiple diverse biomedical applications and scalable biomedical discovery across diverse contexts.

Limitations of the study

Despite these advances, several limitations warrant further investigation. First, while the ontology-guided alignment pipeline substantially mitigates label heterogeneity, it relies primarily on semantic similarity and may overlook subtle domain-specific distinctions. Incorporating human-in-the-loop validation or expert-guided refinement could further improve alignment precision, particularly for fine-grained biomedical concepts. Second, class imbalance remains a challenge across biomedical datasets. Although task-conditioned schema instruction tuning helps disambiguate relevant entity types, future work could integrate resampling52 or cost-sensitive learning53 strategies to further address this issue.

Finally, the real-world applicability of InfoFlowEX needs to be further established across broader biomedical and clinical contexts. Although this study examined three representative biomedical application scenarios, these results provide initial evidence of practical utility but may not fully capture the complexity of real-world deployment. Extending InfoFlowEX in open biomedical settings could further strengthen its capacity to support scalable biomedical knowledge discovery and translational research.

Method details

Datasets

In this study, four datasets are employed to develop and evaluate InfoFlowEX. Specifically, we construct BIE-Corpus, a large-scale, multi-domain biomedical knowledge extraction dataset, to support model training and benchmarking evaluation. To assess InfoFlowEX’s performance across downstream tasks, we curate and utilize three specialized datasets: PQA-L for biomedical question answering, MedDX-MD837 for EHR-based clinical disease diagnosis from EHRs, and PrimeKG-PPI43 for the expansion of biomedical knowledge graph.

BIE-corpus

To enhance and evaluate the performance of LLMs in multi-domain biomedical information extraction, we construct a large-scale and comprehensive dataset named BIE-Corpus. We integrate 40 publicly available datasets (see Tables S1 and S2) into a collection termed BIE-Corpus-PA (BIE-Corpus Pre-Align), spanning biological scales from molecular entities to organismal species. However, BIE-Corpus-PA exhibits heterogeneity in its annotation schemes, encompassing diverse labels and varying levels of granularity. To address this challenge, we employ an embedding-based method to align and integrate these raw datasets into a single, unified collection (see Ontology embedding for BIE data integration in Methods). The resulting dataset, named BIE-Corpus, comprises 184,000 samples, 12 million tokens, and 626,237 entities. Based on the BIE-Corpus, we further establish a comprehensive benchmark comprising NER-bench and RE-bench, designed to systematically assess the performance of current LLMs on NER and RE tasks across diverse biomedical domains. Both datasets are composed of a tuning set for LLM fine-tuning and a test set for LLM evaluation. Specifically, NER-bench covers 25 entity types, and RE-bench encompasses three major relation types. Both benchmarks include a tuning set for model fine-tuning and a held-out test set for evaluation, with the splits preserving the original training/test partitioning of the source data. All compared models were fine-tuned on the same training subset, with the same number of training instances for each task, and the training data split was kept identical.

PQA-L

To evaluate the effectiveness of InfoFlowEX in enhancing retrieval-augmented question answering, we adopt the PQA-L, a sub-dataset comprising 1,000 expert-annotated question-answer instances from the PubMedQA dataset. Each instance of PQA-L consisted of a question, supporting passages, a long answer, and a label (yes/no/maybe). For downstream analysis, we streamline the dataset in two stages. First, we focused on the multiple-choice QA format for its computational efficiency. Second, to establish a more rigorous evaluation benchmark, we filter out all ambiguous instances labeled “maybe,” retaining only those with definitive “yes” and “no” labels. Additionally, we merge the context passages from all instances to create a unified knowledge base. This knowledge base serves as the foundation for information retrieval in our subsequent question-answering tasks.

MedDX-MD8

To evaluate the performance of InfoFlowEX on the EHR-based disease diagnosis task in real-world clinical settings, we curate the MedDX-MD8 dataset from EHR data sourced from MedDX.37 The MedDX-MD8 dataset encompass patient records across eight departments: pulmonology, gastroenterology, urology, cardiology, immunology, psychiatry, neurology, and endocrinology. For each department, we sample the 10 most prevalent diseases and extracted 500 medical records per disease, resulting in a balanced dataset of 40,000 records. Each record includes medical history and examination reports. We conduct data pre-processing for the corpus, which involve de-identification, the removal of special tags and characters, and tokenization. Prior to feature extraction, diagnosis strings, assessment sections, and disease-specific templates were removed from all input texts to prevent label leakage.

PrimeKG-PPI

To evaluate the capability of InfoFlowEX to support dynamic knowledge graph expansion, we construct PrimeKG-PPI, a subgraph of the PrimeKG,43 which contains 642,150 protein–protein interaction (PPI) triples. In our experimental setup, PrimeKG-PPI serves as the seed graph for incremental expansion. The BIE models are tasked with processing recent biomedical literature, extracting new PPI relations, and inserting them into the graph. This task reflects real-world scenarios in which knowledge graphs must be continuously expanded with the latest discoveries, thereby supporting applications in precision medicine, drug discovery, and systems biology.

InfoFlowEX framework

To address the challenges of dataset construction and LLM instruction tuning across large-scale heterogeneous datasets in biomedical knowledge extraction, we propose an open-source InfoFlowEX framework, which comprises two major components: (1) An automated data integration pipeline that applies an ontology-guided alignment strategy to harmonize heterogeneous annotation labels across datasets, enabling the construction of BIE-Corpus. (2) A task-conditioned schema instruction tuning method that is model-agnostic and utilizes a code-based schema representation to unify BIE tasks, thereby facilitating efficient domain adaptation. Built upon these components, InfoFlowEX further supports a unified benchmark for systematic LLM comparison and facilitates effective deployment across representative biomedical scenarios.

Ontology-guided alignment for BIE data integration

The ontology-guided alignment for BIE data integration is designed to harmonize heterogeneous entity taxonomies across diverse BIE datasets. Specifically, it maps ontology terms from diverse BIE datasets into a unified set of ontology categories based on their semantic representations, ensuring that models learn from consistent biological concepts. By integrating these unified ontology categories with ontology-guided alignment, we construct a scalable, ontology-consistent BIE dataset, the BIE-Corpus, which serves as a standardized foundation for multi-domain evaluation (Figure 1A).

Firstly, we curate a biomedical ontology based on the 26 entity categories defined in the BIOS knowledge graph.22 To ensure high semantic precision, we retain 25 categories with well-defined semantic types while excluding the ‘Unknown’ category at this stage. These 25 entity categories are strictly consistent with those defined in NER-bench, and we manually defined three molecular interaction-related relations based on RE-bench. The resulting ontology spans multiple biological scales, encompassing entities ranging from genes, proteins, and cells to diseases, anatomical structures, and therapeutic interventions.

Then, to develop a model for aligning dataset-specific entity labels with our predefined entity categories, we construct a dedicated supervision dataset comprising 25,000 entities sampled from BIOS, with 1,000 examples per category. Each entity is annotated with its canonical ontology label. Unlike the ontology construction stage, where the ‘Unknown’ category is excluded to ensure semantic clarity, the alignment model requires explicit handling of out-of-ontology terms. Therefore, we introduce an additional ‘Unknown’ class during training to capture entities that do not belong to any of the 25 predefined categories. In our experiments, we adopt CODER54 as the embedding model to align dataset-specific entity labels. CODER is pretrained with contrastive learning on UMLS synonyms and knowledge graph relations, which can capture fine-grained biomedical semantics and produce structurally coherent embeddings particularly effective for this ontology-guided alignment task. During training, each entity is encoded by CODER, and the resulting embeddings are fed into a multi-layer perceptron (MLP)55 classifier. The classifier is trained to assign each entity to one of the entity categories or to the “unknown” class. To preserve semantic representations, the embedding model is kept frozen, and only the classifier parameters are updated.

Finally, we apply the proposed alignment strategy to the BIE-Corpus-PA dataset, generating an ontology-aligned dataset, BIE-Corpus, which provides a consistent and scalable supervisory signal for aligning heterogeneous entity categories across multiple biomedical datasets.

Task-conditioned schema instruction tuning

We propose a task-conditioned schema instruction tuning strategy to standardize BIE tasks, enabling precise and consistent learning of entity and relation types, thereby enhancing the performance of LLMs on complex biomedical knowledge extraction tasks with numerous entity types.

First, we construct a biomedical schema collection comprised of 28 canonical entity and relation types (see Supplementary Information), corresponding to the 28 predefined categories in the curated ontology (see Ontology-guided alignment for BIE data integration). Inspired by the GoLLIE56 framework, we implement the schema collection via a code-based instruction construction approach. Specifically, we unify tasks of NER and RE via structured presentation in the form of a Python code schema, which consists of three parts: label, guideline, and candidates (Figure 1B). For each entity category, we design a dedicated Python class to encode its schema. The formal template is constructed as follows:

class <EntityCategory>:

“““<Natural Language Definition of the Entity Category>”””

name: <type> #e.g., “<example_i>” …

where <EntityCategory> serves as the class name for the entity category. The docstring, <Natural Language Definition of the Entity Category>, provides a textual description and the guidelines of the category for the model. The name attribute is assigned a type hint, <type>, to constrain its value type. Finally, one or more comments provide illustrative examples, <example_i>, to show the model’s typical instances of the attribute’s value. Here, we take the classes for the Chemical and Disease categories as an example, which are defined as follows:

class Chemical:

“““ Represents a chemical compound or drug.”””

name: str #e.g., “cetuximab”, “aspirin”.

class Disease:

“““Represents a disease or syndrome.”””

name: str #e.g., “colorectal cancer”.

The complete constructed instruction is composed of three parts, including the schemas, the input text, and the output annotations:

# The schemas.

@dataclass

class Chemical(Entity):

“““Represents a chemical compound or drug. ”””

span: str #e.g., “cetuximab”, “aspirin”, “Metformin”.

@dataclass

class Disease(Entity):

“““Represents a disease or syndrome.”””

span: str #e.g., “colorectal cancer”, “leukemia”, “Type 2 diabetes”.

# The input text

text = “Cetuximab is a monoclonal antibody used in the treatment of colorectal cancer.”

# The output annotations

result =

[Chemical(span = “Cetuximab”),

Disease(span = “colorectal cancer”)]

Secondly, we introduce a dynamic, instance-level instruction construction strategy for InfoFlowEX. For each sample, the instruction is composed of: (1) positive schemas corresponding to the entity types present in the text, and (2) a randomly selected subset of negative schemas representing types not present. This design encourages the model to learn to follow relevant schemas while ignoring irrelevant ones, thereby enhancing its ability to discriminate between correct and incorrect entity types. During instruction tuning, a standard autoregressive loss function57 is employed to maximize the conditional likelihood of the target structured output given the input text and its dynamically constructed instruction: L(θ)=−∑t=1TlogP(yt|X,y<t;θ). Formally, the input X combines the original text with the instance-specific instruction, and the target Y represents the annotated entities and relation and θ represents all trainable parameters of the model.

The approach also confers flexibility in inference. Unlike fine-tuning, where schema selection is guided by ground-truth labels, the inference schemas can be defined based comprehensive extraction (all categories) or targeted extraction (specific subsets). For example, given the input text “Ibuprofen can effectively relieve headache symptoms” a prompt including only the Drug and Symptom schemas yields the precise, structured output [Drug(name = “Ibuprofen”), Symptom(name = “headache”)] while ignoring irrelevant categories such as Gene or CellLine. This demonstrates InfoFlowEX’s schema-aware, task-adaptive capability and practical utility for diverse biomedical extraction scenarios.

Downstream applications

For each downstream application, the backbone LLM was selected based on its top performance on the corresponding benchmark. Specifically, the NER-based pipelines employ LLaMA3, the top-performing model on NER-bench, while the RE-based pipeline utilizes Mistral, the top-performing model on RE-bench.

Entity-enhanced retrieval for question answering

To provide accurate and reliable answers to natural language questions in the biomedical domain, we propose to enhance the retrieval process by incorporating biomedical NER extracted by BIE models as an auxiliary signal. This mechanism is implemented by a dual-channel parallel retrieval branch (Figure 3A), which supplements the conventional full-text retrieval channel with the entity-based one, providing auxiliary signals from recognized entities.

Given a medical question q in natural language, the retrieval task is to select one or more evidentiary documents di from a large corpus C = {d1,d2, …,dN}, which provides the basis for generating an accurate answer. Prevailing baseline approaches to this task can be categorized as either sparse or dense retrieval. Sparse methods,58 such as BM25,32 rank documents by computing a lexical relevance score, SBM25(q,di), and return the top-k candidates. In contrast, dense methods59 use a deep encoder model, fenc(·), to map the query and documents into a shared vector space, retrieving the top-k documents with the highest cosine similarity, cos(fenc(q),fenc(di)).

The initial full-text retrieval channel of our pipeline aims to produce a base candidate set Cbase of size m from the corpus C accomplished by ranking documents based on their similarity to the question q. The sparse retrieval utilizes the BM25 algorithm32 and the resulting candidate set can defined as Cbase = argtop-mSBM25(q,di),di∈C. The dense retrieval strategy leverages semantic representations, typically from a Sentence-Transformer model59 and its candidate set is formed by Cbase = argtop-mScos(q,di),di∈C. In the entity-based retrieval channel, the focus shifts from full-text matching to comparing biomedical entities, directly assessing the alignment of core medical concepts. Specifically, we first utilize the BIE model, to identify and extract a set of entities from each document, E(di). Subsequently, the BM25 algorithm calculates the matching score between question q and E(di), forming an entity-based candidate set, CBase = argtop-mScos(q,di),di∈C.

Then, the text-based and entity-based candidate sets for each retrieval paradigm are merged and reranked. For each document diin the merged candidate pool, a final score, Sfinal(di), is calculated using the following formula: Sfinal(di) = α·Sbase(di)+(1-α)Sentity(di) where Sbase(di) is the document’s full-text retrieval score, Sentity(di) is its entity-based retrieval score, and α is a weighting factor. The fusion weight α was determined via a parameter search over the interval [0, 0.5] based on validation set performance, with optimal values of α = 0.4 and α = 0.1 were selected for the sparse and dense settings, respectively, and applied consistently across all experiments. Finally, the top-k documents are selected to form the final evidence set, denoted as Ck∗={d1∗,d2∗,…,dn∗}. In our experiments, we set k = 3. For the answer generation phase, we employ the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct20 model, chosen for its strong instruction-following capabilities and excellent performance on both biomedical and general-domain benchmarks.34 The final answer a is generated by conditioning the model on an augmented input that concatenates the retrieved evidence with the original question: [d1∗,d2∗,d3∗;q].

EHR-based entity extraction and disease classification

To enhance LLM’s diagnostic capability based on EHRs, we propose a multi-stage automated diagnostic pipeline built upon structured entities extracted from the text. Initially, a BIE model, E(·), parses unstructured EHR data (Draw) to extract and structure various clinical entities into a feature matrix X = [x1,x2, …,xn]. Then, we formulate the diagnostic task as a classification problem, using the feature matrix X as input and the diagnostic results as output Y. We aim to learn a mapping f:X→Y. Here, machine learning models such as Random Forest,60 Support Vector Machines,61 and others can be exploited for disease classification, and our default implementation used a highly interpretable logistic regression model.62 Specifically, the classifier is implemented with L2 regularization, regularization strength C = 1.0, the ‘lbfgs’ solver, multinomial multiclass optimization, and a maximum of 1,000 iterations. To ensure diagnostic explainability, we leverage the SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) framework for post-analysis. By calculating the SHAP values for each feature, we can accurately quantify its contribution to the model’s predictions. The pipeline then ranks these features to isolate the most influential clinical indicators for a given diagnosis.

Relation-enhanced biomedical knowledge graph expansion

To dynamically update the knowledge graph (KG) for capturing the ongoing evolution of scientific discoveries, we introduce an automated KG expansion process, comprising knowledge extraction and knowledge fusion.

In the knowledge extraction stage, we employ BIE models to transform unstructured text into a set of structured candidate relation triplets. Given a collection of documents D = {d1,d2, …,dn}, the BIE model processes each document di to automatically identify relation. The output is a set of candidate triplets, denoted as Tc = {t1,t2, …,tn}, where each triplet is of the form tj=(h,r,t), representing the head entity, relation, and tail entity, respectively.

In the knowledge fusion stage, a lightweight, rule-based strategy is responsible for integrating the set of candidate triplets, Tc = t1,t2, …,tn, into a seed knowledge graph, G=(E,R,T), where E,R, and T represent the sets of entities, relations, and triplets, respectively. The process began by iterating through each candidate triplet tj=(h,r,t) in Tc. A filtering step based on entity matching is applied to ensure that entities in new triplets are connected to the existing entity set E, thereby preventing the introduction of isolated nodes. According to this rule, a triplet tj is discarded if it satisfies the condition (h∉E)∧(t∉E). For triplets that pass the filter, an update operation is performed based on their association with the entity set E, resulting in the updated knowledge graph G′=(E′,R′,T′). If the triplet contains exactly one entity already in E, i.e., (h∈E)∧(t∉E) or (h∉E)∧(t∈E), the new entity is added to the entity set. The updated state is: E′ = E∪{h,t},R′ = R∪{r},T′ = T∪{(h,r,t)}. If both the head and tail entities already exist in E, i.e., (h∈E)∧(t∈E), a new relational edge is added between the existing entities. The updated state is: E′ = E,R′ = R∪{r},T′ = T∪{(h,r,t)}.

Quantification and statistical analysis

We utilize the macro F1 score to assess the performance across all NER and RE tasks, enforcing strict matching protocols. Specifically, an extracted instance is deemed correct only when it perfectly aligns with the ground truth annotation in terms of both span and entity/relation category. We also calculate the mean and standard error of the performance. To compute the 95% CIs, we use a non-parametric bootstrap procedure with 1,000 resamples to assess the stability and confidence level of the statistical results. For the disease classification task, the ROC-AUC scores are calculated using the predicted probabilities.
