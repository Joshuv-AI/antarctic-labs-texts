The Hadamard parametrix on half-Minkowski with Robin boundary conditions: Fundamental solutions and Hadamard states
Beatrice Costeri, Claudio Dappiaggi, Benito A. Juárez-Aubry, Raman Deep Singh

Abstract
We address the problem of constructing fundamental solutions and Hadamard states for a Klein-Gordon field in half-Minkowski spacetime with Robin boundary conditions in d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 spacetime dimensions. First, using a generalisation of the Robin-to-Dirichlet map exploited by Bondurant and Fulling [J. Phys. A: Math. Theor. 38 7 (2005)] in dimension 2 2 , we obtain a representation for the advanced and retarded Green operators in terms of a convolution with the kernel of the inverse Robin-to-Dirichlet map. This allows us to prove the uniqueness and support properties of the Green operators. Second, we obtain a local representation for the Hadamard parametrix that provides the correct local definition of Hadamard states in d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 dimensions, capturing ‘reflected’ singularities from the spacetime boundary. We show that our fundamental solutions abide by this local parametrix representation. Finally, we prove the equivalence of our local Hadamard condition and the global Hadamard condition with a wave-front set described in terms of generalized broken bi-characteristics, obtaining a Radzikowski-like theorem in half-Minkowski spacetime. Keywords: Hadamard states, Robin boundary conditions, microlocal analysis 2020 MSC classes: 81T20, 35E05

Introduction

Boundary value problems for hyperbolic operators on spacetimes with timelike boundary have attracted a lot of attention in the past few years due to their relevance in the formulation of several different physical models at a classical but, especially, at a quantum level. Among these, certainly noteworthy are those whose underlying ambient manifold is the
d
-dimensional anti-de Sitter spacetime
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}_{d}
, which is a maximally symmetric solution of Einstein’s equations with a negative cosmological constant. In the Poincaré patch,
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}_{d}
is conformal to a subset of Minkowski spacetime
({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\eta)
, namely,
\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}\doteq\{(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{d-2},z)\;|\;z>0\}
. This entails that
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}_{d}
has a conformal boundary, which is located at
z=0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

The presence of a timelike boundary is the source of several complications especially in the study of quantum field theoretical models, since our current understanding of these theories is mainly based on the assumption that the underlying background is globally hyperbolic; see,
e.g.
,
[
BGP07
, Def. 1.3.8]
. Here, we consider the algebraic formulation of quantum field theory, which is a versatile, computationally efficient framework, which allows to discuss with mathematical rigor both free and perturbatively interacting theories; see,
e.g.
,
[
BDFY15
,
Rej16
]
for recent introductions. Focusing on the example of a real scalar field, which is the case considered in this work, we can summarize the algebraic approach as a two-step procedure. In the first step, one individuates a unital
*
-algebra of observables which encompasses all the structural properties of the underlying theory, such as locality, the dynamics and the canonical commutation or anti-commutation relations. This first element of the algebraic quantization scheme relies on a main ingredient, namely the difference between the advanced and retarded propagators of the operator which encodes the dynamics. On a globally hyperbolic spacetime these propagators exist, they are unique and, most notably, they are causally supported, see
[
BGP07
]
.

The second step consists of the identification of a quantum state, namely a normalized and positive, linear functional on the algebra, out of which one can recover the probabilistic interpretation proper of quantum theories via the Gelfand-Naimark-Segal theorem. Among the plethora of possible quantum states, one usually restricts the attention to the
Gaussian
or
quasi-free
ones, see
[
BDFY15
]
, which are completely determined by the underlying two-point correlation function. This is a bi-distribution which codifies structural properties such as positivity, the equations of motion and the canonical commutation relations, the latter condition necessary if we restrict the attention to scalar fields. Nonetheless, in order to guarantee the existence of a covariant renormalization scheme as well as that the quantum fluctuations of all observables are finite, one needs to further restrict the class of admissible two-point correlation functions by imposing the
Hadamard condition
, see e.g.
[
FV13
]
. This can be stated as a constraint on the singular structure of the underlying two-point correlation function, say
\omega_{2}
, which is best codified using the language of microlocal analysis in terms of the wavefront set of
\omega_{2}
. In addition, as shown by Radzikowski in his seminal works
[
Rad96a
,
Rad96b
]
this is fully equivalent to establishing that, on each geodesically convex neighbourhood of the underlying manifold, the integral kernel of
\omega_{2}
has to acquire a specific expression, known as
local Hadamard form
which is unique up to smooth remainders as first characterized by Kay and Wald
[
KW91
]
(see also
[
Mor21
]
).

The situation is drastically different as soon as one drops the assumption of the underlying manifold being globally hyperbolic in the sense of
[
BGP07
, Def. 1.3.8]
and a timelike boundary is allowed to exist. All the results listed above are no longer automatically valid and, to avoid a lengthy and possibly tedious discussion we content ourselves with listing the two main difficulties:

1.

Advanced and retarded propagators are no longer unique. If one considers a normally hyperbolic operator, such as the Klein-Gordon one, initial data on a Cauchy surface do not suffice to identify a unique solution unless they are supplemented with suitable boundary conditions. These can be selected imposing that we work with a closed system, a feature which translates at a mathematical level in a suitable Green’s identity holding true. Yet, even after choosing an admissible boundary condition, existence and uniqueness of the advanced and retarded propagators are not guaranteed
a priori
nor it is automatic that, even if existent, they abide by the standard causal properties,

2.

The notion of a Hadamard two-point correlation function
\omega_{2}
is strongly tied to the propagation of singularities theorem, which guarantees that the singular structure of
\omega_{2}
is localized along light cones. Yet, the presence of a timelike boundary drastically changes the picture, since one needs to account for the reflection of light rays at the boundary. This classical phenomenon has far-reaching consequences; in particular it lies outside the scope of the results of
[
Rad96a
,
Rad96b
]
, leading to the necessity of establishing a novel notion of Hadamard states tailored to these scenarios.

Although in the past decade these questions have been investigated and several progresses have been made, a fully cohesive and satisfactory picture is still missing. From the PDE perspective of classifying admissible boundary conditions and of establishing the existence of advanced and retarded propagators, if the underlying manifold is static, one can resort to boundary triples and spectral techniques to give a complete answer; see
[
DDF20
]
. Yet, this procedure falls short of the additional goal of characterizing the support properties of the propagators, which needs to be proven separately in a case-by-case setting. In some instances, this can be achieved with energy estimates, see,
e.g.
, Theorem 6 in
[
JW20
]
, though a suitable energy functional needs to be defined for each boundary condition and finding one can be highly non-trivial. At the level of two-point correlation functions, the literature is quite vast, especially if one restricts the attention to anti-de Sitter spacetimes. In order to summarize succinctly the current status with reference to the problem investigated in this paper, also in this case we list the main results and open points:

1.

From the viewpoint of microlocal analysis, using
b
-calculus techniques, it has been proven a generalization of the propagation of singularities theorem, first considering Robin boundary conditions, see
[
GW22
]
, and then more general scenarios in
[
DM20
]
.

2.

A notion of Hadamard states in terms of the wavefront set of the underlying two-point correlation function has been formulated by different research groups and it accounts both for the propagation of singularities along light directions and for their reflection at the timelike boundary, see,
e.g.
,
[
DF17
,
DM21
,
GW22
,
Wro21
]
.

Yet, in the literature, a counterpart of the results due to Radzikowski is still missing, the main difficulty lying in the characterization of the integral kernel of the two-point correlation function in a geodesic neighborhood that intersects the boundary. Herein, one needs to account at the same time both for the standard propagation along light geodesics and for their reflection at the boundary. In addition, finding a characterization of the admissible two-point correlation functions both at a microlocal level and at that of integral kernel is of paramount relevance if one wants to fully exploit the effectiveness of the algebraic, perturbative approach to interacting quantum field theories, see
[
Rej16
]
.

In this work we start a programme aimed at solving these open issues for real scalar fields. We begin by considering the
d
–dimensional half–Minkowski spacetime,
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}\doteq\{(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{d-2},z)\;|\;z\geq 0\}
. On the one hand
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
is the simplest Lorentzian manifold with a timelike boundary and, for this class of spacetimes; it plays the same rôle of the
d
-dimensional Minkowski spacetime for globally hyperbolic backgrounds. On the other hand, since our ultimate goal is to solve the problems discussed above on a generic curved background with a timelike boundary, one should keep in mind that, locally, any of these manifolds is diffeomorphic to an open subset of
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
. Hence, proving a counterpart of Radzikowski theorems on
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
is a natural and necessary step.

On top of
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
we consider, a real, massive Klein–Gordon field
\Phi
whose dynamics is ruled by the operator
P:=\Box_{\eta}+m^{2}
, where
\Box_{\eta}
is the D’Alembert wave operator while
m^{2}\geq 0
. We endow it with Robin boundary conditions at
z=0
,

(\partial_{z}+\kappa)\Phi\big|_{z=0}=0,\qquad\kappa\geq 0.

(1.1)

Robin boundary conditions are of special relevance since, on the one hand, they include as special cases, those of Neumann (
\kappa=0
) and of Dirichlet (
\kappa\to\infty
) type. For these cases the advanced and retarded propagators as well as the two-point correlation functions for the ground state can be constructed explicitly from their counterpart on the full Minkowski spacetime
({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\eta)
employing the method of images, see,
e.g.
,
[
DNP16
]
. On the other hand, Robin boundary conditions are of great physical relevance since they do not lead to total reflection at the boundary, but they allow for the existence of a smooth tail propagating at
z=0
, see,
e.g.
,
[
BF05
]
. We observe that we restrict the attention to
\kappa>0
since, with our sign conventions, if
\kappa<0
, the two-point correlation function exhibits unstable modes which are exponentially suppressed in space though exponentially growing in time, see
[
DDF20
]
.

Our aim is twofold: First of all we construct advanced and retarded propagators of
P
on
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
subordinated to the boundary condition as per Equation (
1.1
), proving uniqueness and, above all, causal support, hence extending the results of
[
DDF20
]
with which we make contact in Section
4
. Secondly we discuss the notion of Hadamard states, both globally using microlocal techniques and locally working at the level of integral kernel. We prove that one can individuate on
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
a two-point correlation function, denoted by
\omega_{2,\kappa}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{H}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{H}}^{d})
which is consistent with both notions. This allows us in Section
5.3
to prove a counterpart of Radzikowski’s theorems in the case under scrutiny.

Most notably, when working at the level of integral kernels, we can show that Hadamard states abiding by Robin boundary conditions as per Equation (
1.1
), can be written as the sum of a smooth function and of a singular kernel, called
Robin-Hadamard parametrix
which is in turn built out of two constituents. The first is the same Hadamard parametrix which appears in the analysis of the Klein-Gordon equation on a globally hyperbolic spacetime, such as Minkowski, while, the second is a novel contribution which is defined out of
\sigma_{-}
, a
reflected
counterpart of the standard Synge world function, associated to mirror points across
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
. We can therefore summarize our main results as follows:

❈

Propagators for Robin boundary conditions.
Extending the application of the Robin-to-Dirichlet transform introduced by Bondurant and Fulling in
[
BF05
]
for the analysis of the two-dimensional scenario, we construct distributions
G_{\kappa}^{\pm}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{H}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{H}}^{d})
which are inverses of the Klein-Gordon operator
P
and which satisfy the boundary condition as per Equation (
1.1
) in both entries. In particular, using spectral theory arguments, we prove uniqueness, whereas convolution techniques allow us to establish that
G^{\pm}_{\kappa}
enjoy the support properties of advanced and retarded propagators.

❈

Robin Hadamard states and local Robin parametrix.

Adapting the global Hadamard condition to
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
, see,
e.g.
,
[
DF17
]
, we characterize admissible two–point functions
\omega_{2,\kappa}
in terms of their wavefront set, here adapted to encompass the existence of a boundary using the language of
b
-calculus. In our framework we can establish the existence of a canonical choice for
\omega_{2,\kappa}
which is obtained out of the Poincaré vacuum two-point correlation function. In addition we can show that the integral kernel of
\omega_{2,\kappa}
has the
local Robin Hadamard form
, that is its singular part is the sum of a standard Hadamard parametrix written in terms of the Synge world function
\sigma
and of a reflected counterpart built out of
\sigma_{-}
. More precisely we establish that

\displaystyle\omega_{2,\kappa}(x,x^{\prime})=\widetilde{H}_{\kappa}(x,x^{\prime})+W(x,x^{\prime})=

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{U(x,x^{\prime})}{\sigma_{\epsilon}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}+\delta_{d}V(x,x^{\prime})\ln\left(\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}}{\lambda^{2}}\right)+\frac{U^{\prime}(x,x^{\prime})}{\sigma_{-,\epsilon}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}+\delta_{d}V^{\prime}(x,x^{\prime})\ln\left(\frac{\sigma_{-,\epsilon}}{\lambda^{2}}\right)+W(x,x^{\prime}),

where
W
is smooth,
\lambda
is a reference length,
\delta_{d}=0
if
d
is odd and
1
if
d
is even, while
\sigma_{\epsilon}(x,x^{\prime})=\sigma(x,x^{\prime})+i\epsilon(t(x)-t(x^{\prime}))+\epsilon^{2}
,
t
being the time coordinate. In addition, the functions
U,V
and
U^{\prime},V^{\prime}
can be written as a formal power series in
\sigma
and
\sigma_{-}
respectively, whose coefficients can determined in terms of a set of recursive transport equations with prescribed initial data in the first case and boundary conditions in the second. The first ones are nothing but the standard Hadamard recursion relations while the second ones account for the boundary conditions and for both reflection and propagation along
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
. A similar idea has been recently investigated in
[
PMRB25
]
on spacetimes with conical singularities.

❈

Equivalence of global and local formulations; Feynman parametrices.

We extend the classic Radzikowski theorem on
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
proving that the local Robin Hadamard form implies and it is implied by the global counterpart expressed in term of a wavefront set condition on the underlying two-point correlation function. Hence we establish an equivalence between local and global Hadamard states in this boundary setting, which entails, as a byproduct, the possibility of defining a Feynman parametrix, the building block of the perturbative approach to interacting quantum field theories.

Synopsis –

In Section 2 we fix the geometric and analytical setting on half-Minkowski spacetime
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
, we introduce the reflection map
\iota_{z}
and the associated reflected Synge world function
\sigma_{-}
.

In Section 3 we review the defining notions associated to fundamental solutions, propagators and parametrices in the case with an empty boundary, the classical Hadamard expansion as well as the global/local equivalence on globally hyperbolic spacetimes, preparing the ground for the generalization to the case of a manifold with a timelike boundary.

In Section 4 we construct the advanced and retarded propagators for the Klein-Gordon operator
P
with Robin boundary conditions. After defining the Robin-to-Dirichlet map
T_{\kappa}
, generalizing the work of Bondurant-Fulling
[
BF05
]
, as well as the kernel
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}
of its inverse, we prove via suitable convolution estimates the causal support properties and we establish uniqueness.

In Section 5 we develop a notion of Hadamard two-point correlation function
\omega_{2,\kappa}
, tailored to Robin boundary conditions. We establish a global Hadamard condition on
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
adapting the one already present in the literature and using the language of
b
-calculus. In particular we show that
\omega_{2,\kappa}
can be written as the sum between the Neumann two-point function,
\kappa=0
, and a correction term which is less singular and it accounts for propagation along the boundary. Furthermore we prove that
\omega_{2,\kappa}
has the desired wavefront set and we construct a
local Robin Hadamard parametrix
whose singular part is a sum of standard and reflected Hadamard terms. These are determined as power series in both
\sigma
and
\sigma_{-}
whose coefficients abide by suitable recursive transport equations. We conclude by proving the equivalence between the local and global formulations of Hadamard states and by constructing a Feynman parametrix
G_{\kappa}^{\mathrm{F}}
.

Geometric setting

The goal of this section is to set the main conventions used in this work and to introduce succinctly the geometric structures that we shall be employing. More precisely we call
d-
dimensional Minkowski half-space

(\mathbb{H}^{d},\eta)
,
d\geq 2
, the subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, which, on standard Cartesian coordinates
(t,x_{1},...,x_{d-2},z)\equiv(\underline{x},z)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, coincides with the upper half-space

\mathbb{H}^{d}:=\{(\underline{x},z)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:z\geq 0\},\hskip 5.69046ptd\geq 2,

(2.1)

endowed with a Lorentzian metric
\eta
of signature
(+,\underbrace{-,\ldots,-}_{d-1})
. The associated line element reads

ds^{2}_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}=dt^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{d-2}dx_{i}^{2}-dz^{2}.

Note that the boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}:=\{(\underline{x},0)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
is isometric to Minkowski spacetime
(\mathbb{R}^{d-1},\eta)
in dimension
d-1
. As mentioned in the introduction this is the prototype of a globally hyperbolic spacetime with a timelike boundary in the sense of
[
HFS20
]
and therefore it is the starting point to understand quantum field theories on this larger class of backgrounds.

Remark 2.1
.

For later convenience, we denote by
(\mathbb{H}^{d-1},\delta)
,
d\geq 2
, the subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
, which, on standard Cartesian coordinates
(x_{1},...,x_{d-2},z)\equiv(\mathsf{x},z)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, coincides with the upper half-space

\mathbb{H}^{d-1}:=\{(\mathsf{x},z)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}:z\geq 0\},\hskip 5.69046ptd\geq 2,

(2.2)

endowed with the Euclidean metric
\delta
of signature
(-,\ldots,-)
. The associated line element reads

ds^{2}_{\mathbb{H}^{d-1}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{d-2}dx_{i}^{2}-dz^{2}.

This can be read as the model space for a constant time hypersurface in half-Minkowski spacetime via the embedding
{\mathbb{H}}^{d-1}\mapsto\{t\}\times{\mathbb{H}}^{d-1}\subset{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
, for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Remark 2.2
.

It is worth mentioning that, in the mathematical physics literature, the idea of a background with a timelike boundary is often implicitly associated to the
d-
dimensional Anti-de Sitter spacetime
\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}_{d}
,
d>2
, which corresponds to maximally symmetric solutions of Einstein’s field equations with a negative cosmological constant
\Lambda<0
. Since this possesses closed, timelike curves which violate any strong causality assumption, it is customary to restrict the attention to the
Poincaré patch

P\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}_{d}
. This is isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{d}\setminus\partial\mathbb{H}^{d}
and the associated line element in standard Cartesian coordinates
(t,x_{1},...,x_{d-2},z)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
reads

ds^{2}_{P\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}_{d}}=\frac{l^{2}}{z^{2}}(dt^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{d-2}dx_{i}^{2}-dz^{2}),\,\,l^{2}=-\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2\Lambda}.

(2.3)

Being the metric in Equation (
2.3
) singular in the limit
z\rightarrow 0^{+}
, the Poincaré patch of Anti-de Sitter is
not
a globally hyperbolic spacetime with a timelike boundary. Yet, if we consider a conformal transformation we recover
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}\setminus\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
, see Equation (
2.1
). In other words
P\mathbb{A}d\mathbb{S}_{d}
can be endowed with a conformal timelike boundary. Our analysis can be used to study models also on this class of backgrounds provided that one reformulate them on
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
by means of a suitable conformal transformation.

In this work we will be using some basic geometric functions, whose definitions are here recollected for the reader’s convenience and to set the notation once and for all. For definiteness, we specialize the attention to the class of backgrounds we are interested in, although this is not strictly necessary in what follows.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
({\mathbb{H}}^{d},\eta)
be half-Minkowski spacetime as per Equation (
2.1
). Given
x\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
and any among its normal convex neighborhoods
\mathcal{O}_{x}\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
, see
[
Lee18
, Ch. 6]
, we call
geodesic distance
between
x
and
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}_{x}
the length of the unique geodesic connecting the two points and we denote it by
s(x,x^{\prime})
. Furthermore we define the
Synge’s world function
as

\sigma(x,x^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{2}s(x,x^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu\,\nu}(x-x^{\prime})^{\mu}(x-x^{\prime})^{\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\big[(t-t^{\prime})^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{d-2}(x_{j}-x^{\prime}_{j})^{2}-(z-z^{\prime})^{2}\big].

(2.4)

We also stress that a very powerful analytic tool on half-Minkowski spacetime is the method of images. This relies on a counterpart of the Synge’s world function which encodes the reflective nature of the boundary and which is here defined for later convenience using a geometric language.

Definition 2.4
.

Consider
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\eta)
and define the reflection map
\iota_{z}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d},(\underline{x},z)\mapsto(\underline{x},-z)
. We call
reflected Synge’s world function

\sigma_{-}(x,x^{\prime}):=({\iota_{z^{\prime}}}^{-1})^{*}\sigma(x,x^{\prime})=(\iota_{-z^{\prime}})^{*}\sigma(x,x^{\prime}),

(2.5)

whose expression in Cartesian coordinates is

\sigma_{-}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\big[(t-t^{\prime})^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{d-2}(x_{j}-x^{\prime}_{j})^{2}-(z+z^{\prime})^{2}\big].

(2.6)

Observe that, by restriction, Equation (
2.6
) is meaningful also on
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
, while its geometric counterpart in Equation (
2.5
) requires the full Minkowski spacetime. The Synge’s world functions plays a prominent rôle in the theory of parametrices for normally hyperbolic operators as well as in that of Hadamard states for quantum fields on a curved background. Therefore we shall be using it extensively and we shall enjoy some of its notable properties. In the following remark we list those which are relevant to us, following
[
PPV11
, Sec. 3.3]
to which we refer for a thorough analysis. We highlight that all identities are given both for
\sigma
and for its reflected counterpart
\sigma_{-}
and the derivation of the latter ones follows slavishly that of the former. Hence we omit it.

Remark 2.5
.

Consider a geodesically convex open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and, bearing in mind Definition
2.3
we denote by
\sigma_{\mu}:=\partial_{\mu}\sigma
and
(\sigma_{-})_{\mu}:=\partial_{\mu}\sigma_{-}
, where
\partial_{\mu}
are the partial derivatives subordinated to a choice of local coordinates in
\mathcal{O}
. It holds that

\begin{cases}\sigma^{\mu}\sigma_{\mu}=2\sigma,\\
\sigma_{-}^{\mu}(\sigma_{-})_{\mu}=2\sigma_{-}.\end{cases}

(2.7)

Furthermore, given any smooth function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
which depends only on
\sigma
and
\sigma_{-}
, namely
f\equiv f(\sigma,\sigma_{-})
, it holds that we can rewrite as follows the action of derivative operators:

\begin{cases}\partial_{\mu}=\sigma_{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}+(\sigma_{-})_{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma_{-}},\\
\Box_{\eta}=d\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}+2\sigma\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial^{2}\sigma}+2\sigma^{\mu}(\sigma_{-})_{\mu}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\sigma\partial\sigma_{-}}+d\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma_{-}}+2\sigma_{-}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial^{2}\sigma_{-}}.\end{cases}

(2.8)

To conclude the section, we introduce a last convention: given
({\mathbb{H}}^{d},\eta)
and a geodesically convex open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}_{x}\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
of a point
x\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
, for any scalar, continuous function
B:\mathcal{O}_{x}\times\mathcal{O}_{x}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, we can define its
coinciding point limit

[B]:\mathcal{O}_{x}\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad x^{\prime}\mapsto[B](x^{\prime})\doteq B(x,x).

(2.9)

In particular, as shown in
[
PPV11
, Sec. 3]
, we can apply this definition to some notable functions constructed out of the geodesic distance:

[\sigma_{\mu\,\nu}]=\eta_{\mu\,\nu}\quad\textrm{and}\quad[\sigma^{\mu}{}_{\mu}]=d,

(2.10)

where
d
is the dimension of the underlying manifold.

3
Fundamental solutions and Parametrices

In this section we recollect some well-established analytic results concerning wave-like operators and their associated solution theory, assuming in the process that the reader is already familiar with the basic notions of microlocal analysis. As main references for the background material at the heart of this part of our work we consider
[
BGP07
]
and
[
Hör90
]
and, only for the sake of this section, we do not consider a single spacetime, but rather a whole class. More precisely by
(\mathcal{M},g)
we denote a globally hyperbolic spacetime, see
[
BGP07
, Def. 1.3.8]
, recalling that, by definition, it has an empty boundary,
\partial{\mathcal{M}}=\emptyset
. On top of
{\mathcal{M}}
we consider a real, massive, scalar field
\Phi:{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and the associated Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}P\Phi:=\left(\Box_{g}+m^{2}+\xi R\right)\Phi=f\\
\Phi|_{\Sigma}=\Phi_{0}\\
\nabla_{\mathfrak{n}}\Phi|_{\Sigma}=\Phi_{1},\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
\Box_{g}:=g^{\mu\,\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}
is the D’Alembert wave operator,
m^{2}\geq 0
is a parameter to be interpreted as squared mass of the field, while
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
is a free coupling to the scalar curvature
R
built out of
g
. In addition
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathcal{M}})\equiv\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{M})
is source term, while
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{M}
is a spacelike Cauchy hypersurface with
\mathfrak{n}
its future-directed timelike unit normal vector field. For definiteness we choose the initial data
(\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1})
both as elements lying in
\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{M}})
although this specific assumption plays no rôle in our analysis.

Advanced and Retarded Propagators –

As a matter of fact, the solution theory for Equation (
3.1
) is fully under control, see
[
BGP07
]
and it is best understood using the theory of fundamental solutions. Most notably the following result holds true
[
BGP07
, Thm. 3.3.1, Cor. 3.4.3 & Prop 3.4.8]
:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
({\mathcal{M}},g)
be a globally hyperbolic spacetime and let
P
be as per Equation (
3.1
). Then there exists unique
advanced
(-)
and retarded (+) Green’s operators

\mathcal{G}^{\pm}:\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{M}})\to C^{\infty}({\mathcal{M}})
such that these maps are sequentially continuous and

1.

P\circ\mathcal{G}^{\pm}=\mathrm{id}|_{\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{M}})}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\pm}\circ P|_{\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{M}})}=\mathrm{id}|_{\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{M}})},

2.

for any
f\in\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{M}})

\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{G}^{\pm}(f))\subseteq J^{\mp}(\mathrm{supp}(f)).

Remark 3.2
.

In view of Proposition
3.1
and of the Schwartz kernel theorem, we can associate to
\mathcal{G}^{\pm}
unique bi-distributions
G^{\pm}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}})
. Henceforth we shall mainly work at this level and we shall still refer to them as
advanced and retarded propagators
. In addition we introduce the
advanced-minus-retarded (causal) propagator

G=G^{-}-G^{+}
which is such that, working at the level of integral kernels,

G^{-}(x,x^{\prime})=\Theta(t-t^{\prime})G(x,x^{\prime})\quad\textrm{and}\quad G^{+}(x,x^{\prime})=-\Theta(t^{\prime}-t)G(x,x^{\prime}),

(3.2)

where
\Theta
denotes the Heaviside step function. In addition
G
can be realized as the solutions of the following distributional initial value problem on
{\mathcal{M}}
:

\begin{cases}(P\otimes\mathbb{I})G=(\mathbb{I}\otimes P)G=0\\
\left.G\right|_{t=t^{\prime}}=0\quad\textrm{and}\quad\left.\partial_{t}G\right|_{t=t^{\prime}}=\left.-\partial_{t^{\prime}}G\right|_{t=t^{\prime}}=\delta_{\Sigma},\end{cases}

(3.3)

where
\delta_{\Sigma}
is the Dirac delta supported on the diagonal of
\Sigma\times\Sigma
, while the symbol
|_{t=t^{\prime}}
is a concise notation to denote the pullback of
G\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}})
to
(\{t\}\times\Sigma)\times(\{t\}\times\Sigma)
. Observe that such operation is well-defined on account of standard microlocal arguments, in particular
[
Hör90
, Thm 8.2.4]
.

Establishing explicit expressions for the advanced and retarded propagators is in most of the cases not possible, notable exception being static spacetimes where one can employ spectral techniques, see
e.g.

[
DDF20
]
, or maximally symmetric backgrounds.

Remark 3.3
.

A concrete example of this last statement is the
d
-dimensional Minkowski spacetime
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\eta)
,
d\geq 2
. In this case, using complex analytic techniques, the advanced and retarded propagators, denoted by
G^{\pm}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
, can be computed also exploiting Remark
3.2
. In particular, working at the level of integral kernels, it turns out that the advanced-minus-retarded fundamental solution reads

G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(x,x^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}d^{d-1}k\frac{e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})}}{2\sqrt{|\bm{k}|^{2}+m^{2}}}\left(e^{i\sqrt{|\bm{k}|^{2}+m^{2}}(t-t^{\prime})}-e^{-i\sqrt{|\bm{k}|^{2}+m^{2}}(t-t^{\prime})}\right),

where we adopt the short-hand notation
(t,\bm{x})\equiv(t,x_{1},...,x_{d-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The above equation admits a closed form expression as

G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(x,x^{\prime})=\begin{cases}(-1)^{k-1}\,\dfrac{\mathrm{sgn}(t-t^{\prime})}{(2\pi)^{k}}\,\Bigg(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\Bigg)^{k-1}\Big[J_{0}\!\big(m\,\sqrt{2\sigma}\,\big)\,\Theta(\sigma)\Big],&d=2k,\\[11.99998pt]
\,\dfrac{\mathrm{sgn}(t-t^{\prime})}{(2\pi)^{\,k+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\Big(\dfrac{m}{\sqrt{2\sigma}}\Big)^{k-\frac{1}{2}}J_{\,k-\frac{1}{2}}\!\big(m\,\sqrt{2\sigma}\,\big)\,\Theta(\sigma),&d=2k+1.\end{cases}

(3.4)

where
\sigma(x,x^{\prime})
is the Synge’s world function which takes the form of Equation (
2.4
). In addition,
\mathrm{sgn}
and
\Theta
denote respectively the sign and the Heaviside function, while
J_{\alpha}
is the Bessel function of first kind of order
\alpha
. The corresponding advanced and retarded propagators can be constructed using Equation (
3.2
).

Parametrices –

In place of looking for the explicit construction of propagators, a more efficient and informative tool to work with is that of a parametrix. Despite being a standard concept, we feel worth recalling succinctly its definition, see
[
Gar64
]
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
({\mathcal{M}},g)
be a globally hyperbolic spacetime and let
P
be as per Equation (
3.1
). We call
parametrix
associated to
P
any
H\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}})
such that

(P\otimes\mathbb{I})H=\delta+R_{1}\quad\textrm{and}\quad(\mathbb{I}\otimes P)H=\delta+R_{2},

where
R_{1},R_{2}\in C^{\infty}({\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}})
.

Among the infinitely many parametrices associated with the Klein-Gordon operator
P
, four of them are know to be
distinguished
due to their specific singular structure codified in their wavefront set. Two of them
H^{\pm}
are nothing but the propagators
G^{\pm}
in Proposition
3.1
up to a smooth remainder, while the other two, denoted by
H^{F},H^{\bar{F}}
, are called the Feynmann and anti-Feynmann parametrices. The underlying theory has been developed in
[
DH72
]
and it can be seen as one of the pillars of the modern formulation of quantum field theory on curved backgrounds. We shall not delve into the details, but we content ourselves with reporting the explicit structure of their wavefront set:

\text{WF}(H^{\pm})=\{(x,k_{x},x^{\prime},k_{x^{\prime}})\in T^{*}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M})\setminus\{0\}\,|\,(x,k_{x})\sim_{\pm}(x^{\prime},-k_{x^{\prime}}),k_{x}\neq 0\}\cup\text{WF}(\delta_{2}),

(3.5a)

WF(H^{F/\overline{F}})=\{(x,k_{x},x^{\prime},k_{x^{\prime}})\in T^{*}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M})\setminus\{0\}\,|\,(x,k_{x})\sim_{F/\overline{F}}(x^{\prime},-k_{x^{\prime}}),k_{x}\neq 0\}\cup\text{WF}(\delta_{2}).

(3.5b)

In the first one
\sim_{\pm}
entails that
x\in J^{\pm}(x^{\prime})
and that there exists a lightlike geodesic
\gamma
connecting
x
to
x^{\prime}
such that
-k_{x^{\prime}}
is the parallel transport of
k_{x}
along
\gamma
, while
\delta_{2}
is the Dirac delta supported on the diagonal of
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}
. In the second one, instead,
\sim_{F}
(
resp.

\sim_{\overline{F}}
) entails that there exists a lightlike geodesic
\gamma
connecting
x
to
x^{\prime}
such that
-k_{x^{\prime}}
is the parallel transport of
k_{x}
along
\gamma
and, if
x^{\prime}\in J^{+}(x)
, then
k_{x}\triangleright 0
(
resp
.
k_{x}\triangleleft 0
), while if
x^{\prime}\in J^{-}(x)
, then
k_{x}\triangleleft 0
(
resp
.
k_{x}\triangleright 0
). The symbol
\triangleright 0
(
resp.

\triangleleft 0
) signifies that a vector is causal and future (
resp.
past) directed.

Hadamard Two-Point Distributions –

The last key ingredient that we introduce does not stem from the theory of partial differential equations of hyperbolic type or from microlocal analysis. It finds its origin in the formulation of free quantum field theories on globally hyperbolic curved backgrounds. Its physical significance is vast and discussing these aspects would bring us far from the main goal of this work. Therefore, we refer an interested reader to
[
BDFY15
]
, Chapter 5 in particular. Here we content ourselves with giving its mathematical definition, following mainly the seminal work of
[
Rad96a
,
Rad96b
]
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
({\mathcal{M}},g)
be a globally hyperbolic spacetime and let
P
be as per Equation (
3.1
). We say that
\omega_{2}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}})
is a
Hadamard two-point function
if, denoting by
G
the advanced-minus-retarded propagator as in Remark
3.2

1.

(P\otimes\mathbb{I})\omega_{2}=(\mathbb{I}\otimes P)\omega_{2}=0
,

and for all
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}({\mathcal{M}})

2.

\omega_{2}(f,f^{\prime})-\omega_{2}(f^{\prime},f)=iG(f,f^{\prime})
,

3.

\Im\left(\omega_{2}(f,f^{\prime})\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(G(f,f^{\prime})\right)
, where
\Im
denotes the imaginary part,

4.

|G(f,f^{\prime})|^{2}\leq 4\omega_{2}(f,f)\omega_{2}(f^{\prime},f^{\prime})
.

In addition the singular structure of
\omega_{2}
is such that

\text{WF}(\omega_{2})=\{(x,k_{x},x^{\prime},-k_{x^{\prime}})\in T^{*}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M})\setminus\{0\}\,|\,(x,k_{x})\sim(x^{\prime},k_{x^{\prime}})\,\text{and}\,k_{x}\triangleright 0\},

(3.6)

where
\sim
entails that there exists a lightlike geodesic
\gamma
connecting
x
to
x^{\prime}
so that
k_{x^{\prime}}
is the parallel transport along it of
k_{x}
. In addition,
k_{x}\triangleright 0
entails that
k_{x}
is causal and future-pointing.

Bi-distributions
\omega_{2}
abiding by Definition
3.5
are also said to be of
global
Hadamard form and their existence is a well established result, see
[
BFK96
, Chap. 5]
and references therein. It is worth mentioning that the very same argument which leads to the proof of this statement highlights that, unless the background is highly symmetric, it is hard to give explicit examples of Hadamard states. As a matter of fact, especially in the theoretical physics literature, it is more customary to work with bi-distributions which are of
local Hadamard form
,
i.e.
, in every geodesically convex open neighbourhood
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
, their integral kernel reads

\omega_{2}(x,x^{\prime}):=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{U(x,x^{\prime})}{4\pi\sigma_{\epsilon}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(x,x^{\prime})}+\delta_{d}V(x,x^{\prime})\ln\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}(x,x^{\prime})}{\lambda^{2}}+W(x,x^{\prime}),\,\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

(3.7)

where
\lambda>0
is a reference scale length while
\sigma_{\epsilon}(x,x^{\prime})=\sigma(x,x^{\prime})+i\epsilon(t(x)-t(x^{\prime}))+\epsilon^{2}
. Here
\sigma
is the Synge’s world function, see
[
PPV11
]
, which is a generalization of Definition
2.3
, while
t:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a temporal function, whereas

\delta_{d}=\begin{cases}1\,\,\text{if $d$ is even},\\
0\,\,\text{if $d$ is odd}.\end{cases}

The functions
U,V,W\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{O})
and, while
W
encodes the local freedom in choosing a Hadamard states, the others are fully determined by the underlying geometry and equations of motion. More precisely, see
[
Fri75
]
, starting from Equation (
3.7
), it is convenient to expand
U
and
V
as formal power series in
\sigma
, namely,

\displaystyle U=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}u_{j}(x,x^{\prime})\left(\frac{\sigma}{\lambda}\right)^{j}\;\textrm{and}\;V=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}v_{j}(x,x^{\prime})\left(\frac{\sigma}{\lambda}\right)^{j},\quad u_{j},v_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{O}).

(3.8)

By imposing the equations of motion, one obtains
the Hadamard recursion relations
for the coefficients
\{u_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
and

\{v_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
, see also
[
DF05
]
. In
even
dimensions, setting
u_{-1}=v_{-1}=0
, they read for
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{-1\}
,

\begin{cases}Pu_{j}+(2j+4-d)\sigma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}u_{j+1}+(j+1)\left(\sigma^{\mu}{}_{\mu}+2j+4-2d\right)u_{j+1}+\frac{(2-d)}{2}(\sigma^{\mu}{}_{\mu}-d)u_{j+1}=0,\\
[u_{0}]=1,\;\textrm{and}\;[u_{j+1}]=-\frac{[Pu_{j}]}{(j+1)(2j+4-d)},\\
\\
Pv_{j}+2(j+1)\sigma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}v_{j+1}+(j+1)(\sigma^{\mu}{}_{\mu}+2j)v_{j+1}=0,\\
[v_{0}]=-\frac{[Pu_{\frac{d}{2}-2}]}{(d-2)}\;\textrm{and}\;[v_{j+1}]=-\frac{[Pv_{j}]}{(j+1)(d+2j)},\end{cases}

(3.9)

whilst in
odd
dimensions they become

\begin{cases}Pu_{j}+(2j+4-d)\sigma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}u_{j+1}+(j+1)\left(\sigma^{\mu}{}_{\mu}+2j+4-2d\right)u_{j+1}+\frac{(2-d)}{2}(\sigma^{\mu}{}_{\mu}-d)u_{j+1}=0,\\
[u_{0}]=1,\;\textrm{and}\;[u_{j+1}]=-\frac{[Pu_{j}]}{(j+1)(2j+4-d)}.\end{cases}

(3.10)

Here the bracket
[\cdot]
is defined as per Equation (
2.9
). Observe that Equations (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) are a set of transport equations with prescribed initial conditions in terms of coinciding point limits.

Remark 3.6
.

Although individually the Hadamard recursion relations admit a solution and in addition all the coefficients are symmetric functions
[
Mor00
]
, the series in Equation (
3.8
) are in general only asymptotic, unless the underlying background is analytic. In this case convergence to an analytic function can be established, see
[
Fri75
, Thm 4.3.1]
.

Definition
3.5
and Equation (
3.7
) are actually two sides of the same coin as established by the celebrated Radzikowski theorem
[
Rad96a
,
Rad96b
]
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
({\mathcal{M}},g)
be a globally hyperbolic spacetime and let
P
be as per Equation (
3.1
). Then a bi-distribution
\omega_{2}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}})
is of global Hadamard form if and only if it is of local Hadamard form.

Remark 3.8
.

One of the most important aspects of Equation (
3.7
) is the realization that, locally, the singular component of any Hadamard state as per Definition
3.5
is fully determined by the underlying geometry and dynamics. For this reason it is customary to call
Hadamard parametrix
the singular component in Equation (
3.7
),
i.e.
,

H_{\lambda}(x,x^{\prime}):=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{U(x,x^{\prime})}{4\pi\sigma_{\epsilon}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(x,x^{\prime})}+\delta_{d}V(x,x^{\prime})\ln\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}(x,x^{\prime})}{\lambda^{2}},\,\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.11)

In comparison to Definition
3.4
, this nomenclature might appear ambiguous since
H_{\lambda}
does not identify a bi-distribution on the whole manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
, but rather only on any but fixed convex geodesic neighborhood
\mathcal{O}_{x}
. In addition
H_{\lambda}
is a bi-solution of the equation of motion rather than an inverse up to a smooth contribution. Yet, since this nomenclature is ubiquitous in the literature, we shall employ it.

Following this analysis we can combine point
2.
in Definition
3.5
with Equation (
3.7
) to infer a local expression for the integral kernel of
G
, the advanced-minus-retarded fundamental solution of the Klein-Gordon operator, which reads

G(x,x^{\prime}):=\begin{cases}\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta t)\frac{V(x,x^{\prime})}{2}\Theta(\sigma(x,x^{\prime})),&d=2,\\[11.99998pt]
\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta t)\left(\frac{(-1)^{\,\frac{d-2}{2}}}{2(2\pi)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}\,U(x,x^{\prime})\delta^{\frac{d-4}{2}}(\sigma)+\pi V(x,x^{\prime})\Theta(\sigma(x,x^{\prime}))\right),&d=2k,\;k>1,\\[11.99998pt]
\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta t)\,\frac{(-1)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}\frac{U(x,x^{\prime})}{(2\sigma)^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(x,x^{\prime})}\Theta(\sigma(x,x^{\prime})),&d=2k+1.\end{cases}

(3.12)

Feynman Parametrix –

Having established the concept of Hadamard two-point function as per Definition
3.5
, we can now establish a different albeit equivalent characterization of the Feynman propagator for the Klein-Gordon operator on a globally hyperbolic spacetime
({\mathcal{M}},g)
, namely
[
Rej16
]

G^{F}:=G^{+}-i\omega_{2}

(3.13)

where
\omega_{2}
is as per Definition
3.5
while
G^{+}
is the retarded propagator as per Proposition
3.1
. Observe that Equation (
3.13
) differs from part of the literature by a multiplicative constant
i
. Bearing in mind
[
DH72
]
, there exists an associated parametrix
H^{F}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M})
, the
Feynman parametrix
, such that, on every but fixed geodesically convex open neighborhood
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{M}
, the associated integral kernel reads

H^{F}_{\lambda}(x,x^{\prime})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{U(x,x^{\prime})}{\sigma_{\epsilon,F}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(x,x^{\prime})}+\delta_{d}V(x,x^{\prime})\ln\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon,F}(x,x^{\prime})}{\lambda^{2}},

(3.14)

where
\sigma_{\epsilon,F}(x,x^{\prime}):=\sigma(x,x^{\prime})+i\epsilon
. Here
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma(x,x^{\prime})
is the Synge’s world function on
({\mathcal{M}},g)
while
U
and
V
abide by the Hadamard recursion relations as per Equations (
3.9
) and (
3.10
).

4
Fundamental Solutions on half-Minkowski Spacetime

In this section we formulate the problem that we investigate in this work, focusing henceforth our attention to half-Minkowski spacetime
({\mathbb{H}}^{d},\eta)
as in Equation (
2.1
). In particular we consider a real scalar field abiding by the Klein-Gordon equation with Robin boundary conditions and we adress the problem of constructing the advanced and retarded propagators proving in particular their causal support properties.

4.1
Formulation of the Problem

On top of
({\mathbb{H}}^{d},\eta)
we consider a real, scalar field
\Phi:{\mathbb{H}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
which abides by the Klein-Gordon equation

P\Phi=(\Box_{\eta}+m^{2})\Phi=0,

(4.1)

where
\Box_{\eta}
is the D’Alembert wave operator, while
m^{2}\geq 0
. Yet, contrary to the scenario considered in Section
3
,
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}\neq\emptyset
entails that the Cauchy problem is not well-defined and, in order to guarantee both existence and uniqueness of the solutions, one needs to supplement initial data with boundary conditions assigned at
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
.

This hurdle has therefore a deep impact on all those structures which we have introduced in Section
3
starting from an initial value problem, namely the advanced and retarded propagators
G^{\pm}
,
cf.
Equations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), and the Hadamard distributions, see Definition
3.5
. Among the infinitely many boundary conditions which can be consistently assigned, in this work we shall consider those of
Robin type
and, to start with, we give a definition of advanced and retarded propagators, tailored to this choice, although generalization to other boundary conditions are straightforward. Similarly the following concept can be extended almost verbatim to any globally hyperbolic spacetime with a timelike boundary, see
[
HFS20
,
DDF20
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

Given half-Minkowski spacetime
({\mathbb{H}}^{d},\eta)
as per Equation (
2.1
) as well as
P
, the Klein-Gordon operator as per Equation (
3.1
), we call
advanced
(-)
and retarded (+) propagators with Robin boundary conditions
any
G^{\pm}_{\kappa}:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{H}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{H}}^{d})
such that, denoting by
\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}={\mathbb{H}}^{d}\setminus\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
,

(P\otimes\mathbb{I})G^{\pm}_{\kappa}|_{\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}\times\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}}=\delta|_{\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}\times\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad(\mathbb{I}\otimes P)G^{\pm}_{\kappa}|_{\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}\times\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}}=\delta|_{\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}\times\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}},

(4.2)

and

(\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\otimes\mathbb{I})G^{\pm}_{\kappa}|_{\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}}=(-\kappa\otimes\mathbb{I})G^{\pm}_{\kappa}|_{\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}},\quad\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}},

(4.3)

where
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\equiv\partial_{z}
denotes the derivative along the direction normal to
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
while
|_{\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}}
stands for the pull back of
\mathcal{G}^{\pm}_{\kappa}
to
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
along the first entry. In addition we require that, for all
f\in\mathcal{D}(\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d})
,

\textrm{supp}(G^{\pm}_{\kappa}(f))\subseteq J^{\mp}(\textrm{supp}(f)),

(4.4)

where the partial evaluation is on the second entry.

Remark 4.2
.

Equation (
4.3
) can be generalized including formally
\kappa\to\pm\infty
as the cases corresponding to Dirichlet boundary conditions, that is
G^{\pm}_{\infty}|_{\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}}=0
.

In the same spirit of Proposition
3.1
it is convenient to introduce the
advanced-minus-retarded propagator

G_{\kappa}\doteq G^{-}_{\kappa}-G^{+}_{\kappa}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{H}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{H}}^{d})
abiding by the following distributional mixed initial and boundary value problem:

\begin{cases}[P\otimes\mathbb{I}]G_{\kappa}=[\mathbb{I}\otimes P]G_{\kappa}=0,\\
G_{\kappa}|_{t=t^{\prime}}=0,\,\partial_{t}G_{\kappa}|_{t=t^{\prime}}=-\partial_{t^{\prime}}G_{\kappa}|_{t=t^{\prime}}=\delta_{\mathring{\Sigma}},\\
(\partial_{\mathbf{n}}G_{\kappa}+\kappa G_{\kappa})|_{\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}}=0,\,\,\kappa\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(4.5)

where the symbol
|_{t=t^{\prime}}
denotes the pull back of
G
on the initial value surface while
\mathring{\Sigma}=\Sigma\setminus\partial\Sigma
denotes the interior of a constant time Cauchy surface of
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
. Observe that, since the first two lines in Equation (
4.5
) correspond to Equation (
4.2
), we should be denoting a restriction of
G_{\kappa}
to
\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}\times\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}
. Yet, we prefer avoiding it since it would make the notation too heavy and we feel that the meaning is clear from the context. As mentioned in the introduction, we reiterate two remarkable facts concerning advanced and retarded propagators on globally hyperbolic manifolds with a timelike boundary and abiding by Robin boundary conditions:

❈

existence of
G_{\kappa}
has already been proven in
[
DDF20
, Thm. 30]
on every static, globally hyperbolic spacetime with a timelike boundary using spectral analytic techniques, but it is still unknown whether the ensuing propagators
G^{\pm}_{\kappa}
obtained using Equation (
3.2
) obey the causal support properties listed in Proposition
3.1
unless
\kappa=0
or
\kappa\to\pm\infty
.

❈

there is no known counterpart for Equation (
3.12
), in particular if one considers convex geodesic neighborhoods centered at a point
x\in\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
.

In the following we devise a procedure which will allows us to give an answer to both these questions.

Remark 4.3
.

In Equation (
4.5
) we have formulated the Robin Boundary conditions for every value of
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
. As far we work at the level of fundamental solutions, this requirement is harmless, but, when considering instead two-point correlation functions as in Section
5
, for
\kappa<0
modes exponentially growing in time are present and they lead to models which are considered to be unstable from the physical viewpoint. For this reason, henceforth we shall restrict our attention to
\kappa\geq 0
.

4.2
Fundamental solutions on
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
with Robin boundary conditions

As explained in the introduction the strategy that we follow is based on several separate steps which we discuss in the following.

Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions –

Our first step consists of considering on
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
Equation (
4.5
) though with two distinguished boundary conditions:
Dirichlet
and
Neumann
, corresponding respectively to
\kappa\to\pm\infty
and
\kappa=0
in Equation (
4.3
). Denoting for simplicity the associated propagators respectively by
G_{D}
and
G_{N}
, we are imposing

G_{D}|_{\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}}=0\quad\textrm{and}\quad\partial_{z}G_{N}|_{\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}},=0

(4.6)

where the pull-back to
\partial{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
has to be referred only to the first entry of the bi-distributions. Adapting to the case in hand the
method of images
the following statement holds true, see also
[
DNP16
]
:

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
({\mathbb{H}}^{d},\eta)
,
d\geq 2
, denote half-Minkowski spacetime as per Equation (
2.1
),
P
the Klein-Gordon operator as per Equation (
3.1
) and
G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
the advanced-minus-retarded fundamental solution of
P
on the whole Minkowski spacetime as per Equation (
3.4
). It holds that, working at the level of integral kernel, for every
(\underline{x},z),(\underline{x^{\prime}},z^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{H}}^{d}
, the bi-distributions

\displaystyle G_{D}(\underline{x},z,\underline{x^{\prime}},z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\underline{x}-\underline{x^{\prime}},z-z^{\prime})-G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\underline{x}-\underline{x^{\prime}},z+z^{\prime}),

(4.7)

\displaystyle G_{N}(\underline{x},z,\underline{x^{\prime}},z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\underline{x}-\underline{x^{\prime}},z-z^{\prime})+G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\underline{x}-\underline{x^{\prime}},z+z^{\prime}),

(4.8)

are the unique solution of Equation (
4.5
) with boundary condition as in Equation (
4.6
).

Proof.

Observe that
G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\underline{x}-\underline{x^{\prime}},z+z^{\prime})
is the integral kernel of
\left(\iota^{*}_{z}\otimes\mathrm{id}|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\right)G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
where

\iota_{z}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\quad(\underline{x},z)\mapsto\iota_{z}(\underline{x},z)=(\underline{x},-z).

Since
\iota_{z}
is a discrete isometry of the Minkowski metric, it holds that

(P\otimes\mathbb{I})\circ\left(\iota^{*}_{z}\otimes\mathrm{id}|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\right)G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}=\left(\iota^{*}_{z}\otimes\mathrm{id}|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\right)\circ(P\otimes\mathbb{I})G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}=0,

from which it descends that
G_{D/N}
are distributional solutions of the Klein-Gordon equation in both entries. In addition, the boundary conditions as in Equation (
4.6
) are satisfied as one can infer per direct inspection. With reference to Equation (
4.5
), only the initial conditions are left to be checked. Yet, still Equation (
3.4
) entails that, denoting by
\underline{x}\equiv(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{d-2})
and, similarly, by
\underline{x}^{\prime}\equiv(t^{\prime},x_{1}^{\prime},...,x_{d-2}^{\prime})

G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\underline{x}-\underline{x^{\prime}},z+z^{\prime})|_{t=t^{\prime}}=0

and

\partial_{t}G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\underline{x}-\underline{x^{\prime}},z+z^{\prime})|_{t=t^{\prime}}=-\partial_{t^{\prime}}G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\underline{x}-\underline{x^{\prime}},z+z^{\prime})|_{t=t^{\prime}}=\delta(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})\dots\delta(x_{d-2}-x^{\prime}_{d-2})\delta(z+z^{\prime})=0,

where we used that
z+z^{\prime}>0
. To conclude, uniqueness descends from the general statement in
[
DDF20
, Thm. 30]
.
∎

4.2.1
The Bondurant-Fulling map

Having discussed how to explicitly construct Dirichlet and Neumann fundamental solutions on
d-
dimensional half-Minkowski spacetime
(\mathbb{H}^{d},\eta)
, we shall now tackle the problem of constructing a counterpart of
G_{D}
and
G_{N}
in Proposition
4.4
with generic Robin boundary condition. In this endeavor we shall make use of a notable map introduced in
[
BF05
]
. In addition to defining it, in the following we study its properties, especially in connection to its interplay with distributions, hence extending the analysis in
[
BF05
]
.

Definition 4.7
.

Given
(\mathbb{H}^{d},\eta)
,
d\geq 2
, as per Equation (
2.1
), denoting by

C^{\infty}_{D}(\mathbb{H}^{d}):=\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{d})\,|\,f|_{z=0}:=f(\underline{x},0)=0\},

(4.9)

and by

C^{\infty}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{H}^{d}):=\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{H}^{d})\,|\,\partial_{z}f|_{z=0}=-\kappa f|_{z=0}\},\,\,\kappa>0,

(4.10)

we call
Robin-to-Dirichlet (or Bondurant-Fulling) map

\displaystyle T_{\kappa}:C^{\infty}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{H}^{d})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow C^{\infty}_{D}(\mathbb{H}^{d})

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto T_{\kappa}(f):=(\mathbb{I}_{\underline{x}}\otimes(\partial_{z}+\kappa\mathbb{I}_{z}))f.

(4.11)

Note that the operator
T_{\kappa}
is linear and its kernel is

\text{ker}(T_{\kappa})=\{f\in C^{\infty}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{H}^{d})\,|\,f(\underline{x},z)=c(\underline{x})e^{-\kappa z}\}.

(4.12)

Hence, the linear map

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}:D(\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}):=\frac{C^{\infty}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{H}^{d})}{\text{ker}(T_{\kappa})}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\text{Ran}(T_{\kappa})\subset C^{\infty}_{D}(\mathbb{H}^{d})

\displaystyle[f]

\displaystyle\mapsto\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}[f]:=T_{\kappa}(f),

(4.13)

is bijective per construction and, as a consequence, it admits an inverse
\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}^{-1}:\text{Ran}(\widetilde{T}_{\kappa})\rightarrow D(\widetilde{T}_{\kappa})
. Since
C^{\infty}_{D}(\mathbb{H}^{d})\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
, we can look for the corresponding distributional inverse. Denoting by
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}:=\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}^{-1}
, we look for
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{H}^{d})
such that

\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}=\delta.

(4.14)

This can be readily solved by

\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}(z)=\Theta(z)e^{-\kappa z},

(4.15)

where, with a slight abuse of notation, we have omitted the dependence on the coordinates tangent to the boundary since they will play no rôle in the following discussion. Observe that, choosing
\kappa>0
,
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, where we have implicitly taken into account the natural inclusion
{\mathbb{H}}^{d}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Our strategy consists of using
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}
to construct tempered distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which automatically abide by Robin boundary conditions, hence codifying an inverse of the statement in Definition
4.7
. The first step consists of identifying distributions which obey Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Definition 4.8
.

Given
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we establish the following subspaces:

❈

the set of distributions which are constructed out of a reflection along the hyperplane
z=0
:

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{-}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,|\,\exists v\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),u=v-\iota_{z}^{*}v\},\;\textrm{and}\;\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{-}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{-}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(4.16)

where the map
\iota_{z}
is as per Definition
2.4
,

❈

Dirichlet (tempered) distributions
,
i.e.
, those admitting a vanishing pull-back at
z=0
:

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,|\,j_{0}^{*}u=0\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(4.17)

where

j_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\underline{x}\mapsto j_{0}(\underline{x}):=(\underline{x},0).

Having established a notion of tempered Dirichlet distributions, the next step consists of showing that one can consider their convolution with
{\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}
as per Equation (
4.15
).

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as per Equation (
4.16
). Then, if
\kappa>0
, the convolution
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\star u
exists and, additionally,
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\star u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Observe that

\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}}(p_{z})=\frac{1}{ip_{z}-\kappa},

which entails that, since, for all
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}}\hat{u}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

since
\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}}
is smooth on the real axis and decaying at infinity. Hence, we can define
\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\star u:=\widecheck{\widehat{\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}}\hat{u}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

Motivated by the above theorem, we can give the following definition.

Definition 4.10
.

We call
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of
Robin tempered distributions
defined as

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\star\mathcal{S}_{0}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(4.18)

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as per Definition
4.10
. Then,
u
satisfies Robin boundary conditions, that is,
j_{0}^{*}\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}u=0
.

Proof.

By definition, if
u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists
v\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
u=\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\star v
. Since
\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}
is a differential operator, we can write

j_{0}^{*}\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}u=j_{0}^{*}(\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}(\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\star v))=j_{0}^{*}((\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}\mathcal{L}_{\kappa})\star v)=j_{0}^{*}v=0,

which completes the proof.
∎

∎

Remark 4.13

Proposition 4.14

For all

Proof.

In order to avoid a heavy notation, we shall omit the dependence on the

and

\left((\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star(G_{D}(\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}f))\right)(z)=\left((\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star(G_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}f))\right)(z)-\left((\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star(G_{r}(\widetilde{T}_{\kappa}f))\right)(z),

It is worth observing that, in the case

Lemma 4.15
.

Given the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}
as per Equation (
4.15

\delta(2\sigma_{-})\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
the bi-distribution whose integral kernel is
\delta((t-t^{\prime})^{2}-(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})^{2}-\ldots-(x_{d-2}-x^{\prime}_{d-2})^{2}+(z+z^{\prime})^{2})
, it holds that

\text{supp}\left(\left(\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta\right)\star\delta(2\sigma_{-})\right)\cap({\mathbb{H}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{H}}^{d})\subset\text{supp}(\Theta(2\sigma_{-}))\cap({\mathbb{H}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{H}}^{d}),

where
\Theta
denotes the Heaviside step function.

Proof.

We work at the level of integral kernels and, not to burden the notation, we omit to indicate explicitly the dependency on the coordinates relative to the directions tangent to the boundary. In addition, with a slight abuse of notation, for a reason which will become clear in a moment, we set
\tau^{2}=(t-t^{\prime})^{2}-(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})^{2}-\ldots-(x_{d-2}-x^{\prime}_{d-2})^{2}
. Equation (
4.21
) entails that we are interested in

\displaystyle((\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star\delta(2\sigma_{-}))(z,z^{\prime})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\widetilde{z}\,\Theta(\widetilde{z}-z)e^{-\kappa(z-\widetilde{z})}\delta(\tau^{2}-(\widetilde{z}+z^{\prime})^{2}),

which entails that
\tau^{2}\geq 0
, else
\delta(\tau^{2}-(\widetilde{z}+z^{\prime})^{2})=0
. Bearing this in mind and considering
\tau^{2}>0
,

\displaystyle((\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star\delta(2\sigma_{-}))(z,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2|\tau|}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\widetilde{z}\,\Theta(\widetilde{z}-z)e^{-\kappa(z-\widetilde{z})}[\delta(\widetilde{z}+z^{\prime}-|\tau|)+\delta(\widetilde{z}+z^{\prime}+|\tau|)]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2|\tau|}\left[\Theta(|\tau|-z-z^{\prime})e^{-\kappa(-|\tau|+z+z^{\prime})}+\Theta(-|\tau|-z-z^{\prime})e^{-\kappa(|\tau|+z+z^{\prime})}\right].

(4.26)

z+z^{\prime}\geq 0
, it holds that on
\mathbb{H}^{d}\times\mathbb{H}^{d}

((\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star\delta(2\sigma_{-})))(z,z^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2|\tau|}\Theta(|\tau|-z-z^{\prime})e^{-\kappa(-|\tau|+z+z^{\prime})}=-\frac{1}{2|\tau|}\Theta(2\sigma_{-})e^{-\kappa(-|\tau|+z+z^{\prime})},

where we exploited that, on

\Theta(2\sigma_{-})=\Theta((|\tau|-z-z^{\prime})(|\tau|+z+z^{\prime}))=\Theta(|\tau|-z-z^{\prime})

In order to complete the analysis we need to investigate what happens as
\tau=0

z+z^{\prime}>0

\mathring{{\mathbb{H}}}^{d}

sufficiently small

(\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star\delta(2\sigma_{-})
vanishes. On the contrary, if
z+z^{\prime}=0
such contribution is non-vanishing and it yields a term proportional up to smooth terms to
\frac{\Theta(\tau^{2})}{|\tau|}

∎

Given the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}
as per Equation (
4.15

\Theta(2\sigma_{-})\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})

\Theta((t-t^{\prime})^{2}-(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})^{2}-\ldots-(x_{d-2}-x^{\prime}_{d-2})^{2}+(z+z^{\prime})^{2})

\text{supp}\left(\left(\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta\right)\star\Theta(2\sigma_{-})\right)\cap({\mathbb{H}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{H}}^{d})\subset\text{supp}(\Theta(2\sigma_{-}))\cap({\mathbb{H}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{H}}^{d}),

where
\Theta
denotes the Heaviside step function.

Proof.

Also in this case we work at the level of integral kernels and, for simplicity of the notation, we omit to indicate explicitly the dependency on the coordinates relative to the directions tangent to the boundary. In addition, with a slight abuse of notation, we set
\tau^{2}=(t-t^{\prime})^{2}-(x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1})^{2}-\ldots-(x_{d-2}-x^{\prime}_{d-2})^{2}

4.21

\displaystyle((\mathcal{L}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star\Theta(2\sigma_{-}))(z,z^{\prime})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\widetilde{z}\,\Theta(\widetilde{z}-z)e^{-\kappa(z-\widetilde{z})}\Theta(\tau^{2}-(\widetilde{z}+z^{\prime})^{2}),

∎

We are interested in evaluating convolutions of the form

({\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star(V\ln\sigma_{-})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\kappa s}V(\underline{x},z+s,x^{\prime})\ln|\tau^{2}-(z+z^{\prime}+s)^{2}|ds,

(5.10)

where the factor
\frac{1}{2}
from the Synge world function has been omitted since it gives rise to a smooth contribution. We divide the analysis of the integral in two parts depending on
\tau^{2}
.

❈

If
\tau^{2}\leq 0
and if
z+z^{\prime}>0
, the integrand is smooth and therefore the integral contributes to
W
in Equation (
5.9
).

❈

If
\tau^{2}>0
, then the only singular contribution comes from the points for which
\tau^{2}=(z+z^{\prime}+s)^{2}
or, equivalently
s=s_{*}=-(z+z^{\prime})+|\tau|
. Observe that we can consider the coordinate change
s\mapsto\alpha(s)=\tau^{2}-(z+z^{\prime}+s)^{2}
. This is well defined since
\partial_{s}\alpha(s)=-2(z+z^{\prime}+s)
which is non vanishing since
s\geq 0
and
z+z^{\prime}>0
. In addition
\alpha\in(-\infty,2\sigma_{-}]
and the integral under scrutiny can be therefore rewritten as

\int\limits_{-\infty}^{2\sigma_{-}}F(\alpha)\ln|\alpha|d\alpha,

where
F(\alpha)=e^{-\kappa s(\alpha)}V(\underline{x},z+s(\alpha),x^{\prime})\frac{\partial s}{\partial\alpha}
. Observing that
F
is smooth and rapidly decaying at
-\infty
, we can introduce the primitive

K(x)=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{x}F(\alpha)d\alpha,

which is also rapidly decreasing as
x\to-\infty
. A direct computation yields that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{2\sigma_{-}}F(\alpha)\ln|\alpha|d\alpha

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{2\sigma_{-}}F(\alpha)(\ln|\alpha|-\ln|2\sigma_{-}|+\ln|2\sigma_{-}|)d\alpha

\displaystyle=K(2\sigma_{-})\ln|2\sigma_{-}|+\int\limits_{-\infty}^{2\sigma_{-}}F(\alpha)\ln\left|\frac{\alpha}{2\sigma_{-}}\right|d\alpha.

The second term in this last identity is locally integrable and it contributes with a logarithmic singularity.

The
\sigma_{-}^{\frac{2-d}{2}}
term:
We are interested in evaluating convolutions which read formally at the level of integral kernel

({\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star(U\sigma_{-}^{\frac{2-d}{2}})=2^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\kappa s}U(\underline{x},z+s,x^{\prime})(\tau^{2}-(z+z^{\prime}+s)^{2})^{\frac{2-d}{2}}ds.

(5.11)

This requires to split the analysis between
d
even and odd.

❈

If
d
is even, setting
n=\frac{d-2}{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider

({\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star(U\sigma_{-}^{-n}),

where
\sigma_{-}^{-n}
should be interpreted as the distribution
\text{PV}(\sigma_{-}^{-n})+c_{n}\delta^{(n-1)}(\sigma_{-})
where
c_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
is an irrelevant numerical factor. As before we observe that the integral yields a smooth contribution if
\tau^{2}<0
or if
\tau^{2}=0
and
z+z^{\prime}>0
. Therefore, we consider
\tau^{2}>0
and we introduce a cut-off
\chi(s)
supported in an arbitrarily small neighbourhood around
s_{*}=|\tau|-z-z^{\prime}
, which we denote by
(s_{*}-\epsilon,s_{*}+\epsilon)
,
\epsilon>0
. Adding in Equation (
5.11
) a factor
1=\chi+(1-\chi)
yields

({\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star(U\sigma_{-}^{\frac{2-d}{2}})=\chi({\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star(U\sigma_{-}^{\frac{2-d}{2}})+(1-\chi)({\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star(U\sigma_{-}^{\frac{2-d}{2}}).

We start focusing on the first term. As in the analysis of the
\log
-term, we can consider the change of variables
s\mapsto\alpha(s)\doteq\tau^{2}-(z+z^{\prime}+s)^{2}\in(-\infty,2\sigma_{-}]
which is well-defined on the whole domain of
s
, namely
(0,\infty)
. Working at the level of integral kernels this amounts to considering up to multiplicative numerical factors

\int\limits_{\alpha_{-}}^{\alpha_{+}}\chi(s(\alpha))e^{-\kappa s(\alpha)}U(\underline{x},z+s(\alpha),x^{\prime})\frac{ds}{d\alpha}\alpha^{-n}d\alpha=\int\limits_{\alpha_{-}}^{\alpha_{+}}G_{\chi}(\alpha)\alpha^{-n},

(5.12)

where
\alpha_{\pm}=\pm 2|\tau|\epsilon-\epsilon^{2}
, while
G_{\chi}(\alpha)=\chi(s(\alpha))e^{-\kappa s(\alpha)}U(\underline{x},z+s(\alpha),x^{\prime})\frac{ds}{d\alpha}\equiv\chi(\alpha)\widetilde{G}(\alpha)
. Without loss of generality we choose
\epsilon
so that
2|\tau|\epsilon-\epsilon^{2}>0
. The contribution due to
\delta^{(n-1)}(\sigma_{-})=\delta^{(n-1)}(\frac{\alpha}{2})
is smooth since the above integral is a formal expression for

\left(\delta^{(n-1)}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right),G_{\chi}(\alpha)\right),

where
G
is a smooth function compactly supported on
(\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+})
. We can focus on the principal value recalling the distributional identity valid for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
:

\left(\text{PV}(\alpha^{-n}),\varphi\right)=\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\left(\frac{d^{n}\ln|\alpha|}{d\alpha^{n}},\varphi\right)=-\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\left(\ln|\alpha|,\frac{d^{n}\varphi}{d\alpha^{n}}\right).

The role of
\varphi
is played by
G(\alpha)
and we end up with

\left(\mathrm{PV}\,\frac{1}{\alpha^{n}},\,G_{\chi}(\alpha)\right)=-\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\left(\ln|\alpha|,\frac{d^{n}G_{\chi}}{d\alpha^{n}}\right),

which gives a smooth contribution. We are left with the analysis of the second term containing
(1-\chi)
. This yields two integrals, the first being

\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\alpha_{-}}(1-\chi(s(\alpha)))e^{-\kappa s(\alpha)}U(\underline{x},z+s(\alpha),x^{\prime})\frac{ds}{d\alpha}\alpha^{-n}d\alpha,

(5.13)

which yields a smooth contribution since the integrand decays exponentially at
\alpha\to-\infty
. Observe that the dependence on
\alpha_{-}
is fictitious since it is reabsorbed by the one due to Equation (
5.12
). the second integral instead reads

\int\limits_{\alpha_{+}}^{2\sigma_{-}}(1-\chi(s(\alpha)))e^{-\kappa s(\alpha)}U(\underline{x},z+s(\alpha),x^{\prime})\frac{ds}{d\alpha}\alpha^{-n}d\alpha=\int\limits_{\alpha_{+}}^{2\sigma_{-}}\frac{G_{1-\chi}(\alpha)}{\alpha^{n}}d\alpha.

(5.14)

Integrating repeatedly by parts with respect to the factor
\alpha^{-n}
entails that this integral can be written as a linear combination of the from
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{c_{k}(x,x^{\prime})}{\sigma_{-}^{k}}+c_{0}(x,x^{\prime})\ln|\sigma_{-}|
where the coefficients
c_{k}
are smooth functions in both
x
and
x^{\prime}
for all
k=0,\dots,n-1
. As in the previous case the contribution form
\alpha_{+}
is reabsorbed by the one due to Equation (
5.12
).

❈

If
d
is odd we are interested in evaluating convolutions which read formally at the level of integral kernel

({\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}\otimes\delta)\star(U\sigma_{-}^{\frac{2-d}{2}})=2^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\kappa s}U(\underline{x},z+s,x^{\prime})(\tau^{2}-(z+z^{\prime}+s)^{2})^{\frac{2-d}{2}}ds.

(5.15)

This is structurally identical to Equation (
5.11
) and it can be dealt with in a similar way, hence we discuss it succinctly. Introducing the same cut-off function and coordinate change as in the case of
d
even we have to deal with two contributions, one due to
\chi
and one due to
(1-\chi)
. Working at the level of distributions, the first one reads

\left(\alpha^{\frac{2-d}{2}},G_{\chi}(\alpha)\right)=\left(\alpha^{\frac{2-d}{2}},G_{\chi}(\alpha)\right)=\left(C_{d}\frac{d^{N}|\alpha|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{d\alpha^{N}},G_{\chi}(\alpha)\right)=(-1)^{N}C_{d}\left(|\alpha|^{\frac{1}{2}},\frac{d^{N}G(\alpha)}{d\alpha^{N}}\right),

where
N=\frac{d-1}{2}
while
C_{d}=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}-N)}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})}
,
\Gamma
being the Euler Gamma function. The second contribution is structurally identical to Equations (
5.13
) and (
5.14
) and, the last one in particular, yields a term of the form
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\frac{c_{k}(x,x^{\prime})}{\sigma_{-}^{k-\frac{1}{2}}}
where
c_{k}(x,x^{\prime})
is smooth for all
k
.

Putting together all these data, the sought conclusion descends.
∎

This theorem shows that, when working with
\omega_{2,\kappa}

can be determined from the Klein-Gordon operator in terms of a power series either in
\sigma
or in
\sigma_{-}
whose coefficients abide by suitable recursion relations, which reflect the behaviour highlighted by Theorem

. In Section

we shall compare Definitions
3.5
and
5.6
investigating to the case under scrutiny the result in
[
Rad96a
]
valid on globally hyperbolic spacetimes.

\sigma_{-}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}v^{\prime}_{0}=\frac{2\kappa}{z+z^{\prime}}=-v^{\prime}_{0},

(5.30)

so that the first equation is satisfied. Focusing on the boundary conditions, we have that

\partial_{z}(u_{0}+u^{\prime}_{0})|_{z=0}+\partial_{z}\sigma_{-}(v_{0}-v^{\prime}_{0})|_{z=0}=2\kappa=\kappa(u_{0}+u^{\prime}_{0})|_{z=0}.

(5.31)

Hence
v^{\prime}_{0}
satisfies the Hadamard recursion relations with Robin boundary conditions. Focusing on the coefficients

v^{\prime}_{j+1}|_{z=0}=\frac{(\partial_{z}+\kappa)(v^{\prime}_{j})|_{z=0}}{(j+1)z^{\prime}},\hskip 5.69046ptj\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.\end{cases}

(5.32)

These are the Hadamard recursion relations as in Equation (

5.2.2

We discuss odd dimensions by focusing only on the main formulae since their derivation is structurally identical to that in

Remark 5.13

Appendix A
Generalized Broken Bicharacteristics

In this appendix we discuss some distinguished geometric features of a class of Lorentzian manifolds which have half-Minkowski spacetime as per Equation (
2.1
) as their prototype. More precisely, we call
spacetime
,
(\mathcal{M},g)
, a
d-

[

Definition A.1
.

Under the previous assumptions, we call

Among the set of oriented and time-oriented Lorentzian manifolds with

[
BGP07
]

HFS20
]

Definition A.2

A spacetime

(i)

(\mathcal{M},g)

(ii)

for any pair of points

, denoting by
