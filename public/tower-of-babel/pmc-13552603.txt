Sepsis and Antibiotics in Extremely Preterm Infants: Early Empirical Treatment Was Common, While Late‐Onset Sepsis Accounted for Most Cases and Antibiotic Use
Authors: Anna Bjerager Arnesen, Nini Møller, Louise Dyrberg Vibede, Kristian Vestergaard, Sara Krøis Holm, Cæcilie Trier, Hristo Stanchev, Gholamreza Dayani, Ulrikka Nygaard, Bo Mølholm Hansen, Emma Malchau Carlsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe aim was to investigate the incidence of sepsis and use of antibiotics among extremely preterm infants, given the limited number of population‐based studies examining this.MethodsThis retrospective study in Eastern Denmark included all liveborn infants from 23 + 0 weeks/days to 27 + 6 weeks/days of gestation from 2019 to 21. Data on dispensed intravenous antibiotics were obtained from the regional joint electronic health platform. Early‐onset sepsis was defined as occurring within the first 72 h of life and late‐onset sepsis from 3 to 90 days. Positive blood culture episodes were classified as proven sepsis.ResultsWithin the first six months of life, 187 of the 232 (81%) infants received intravenous antibiotics. Empirical treatment was initiated for early‐onset sepsis in 164 of the 232 infants (71%) and late‐onset sepsis in 118 (51%). Late‐onset sepsis accounted for 90% of proven sepsis, predominantly caused by Staphylococcus aureus and Coagulase‐negative Staphylococcus. Antibiotic use for late‐onset sepsis was three‐fold higher than for early‐onset sepsis (9902 versus 3413 days per 1000 live births). Sepsis caused two deaths (9 per 1000 live births infants).ConclusionSuspected early‐onset sepsis was the most common indication for antibiotic initiation, but late‐onset accounted for most proven sepsis episodes and antibiotic exposure in extremely preterm infants.Keywords: blood stream infections, early empirical treatment, extremely preterm infants, late‐onset sepsis, neonatal infections

Introduction

Neonatal infections are a major cause of morbidity and mortality, and the incidence escalates as gestational age decreases. Early‐onset sepsis (EOS) occurs within the first 72 h of life and is typically acquired through maternal transmission via the genital tract. In contrast, late‐onset sepsis (LOS), which occurs after 72 h of life, is mainly of environmental origin. LOS may be classified as hospital acquired or community acquired and is particularly problematic in extremely preterm infants born before 28 + 0 weeks/days of gestation. These infants often need intensive therapy, including intravascular catheters and prolonged hospitalization, which increases their susceptibility to hospital‐acquired infections [1].

Studies have found that infections accounted for approximately 10% of mortality in extremely preterm infants [2]. Timely treatment with antibiotics is critical. Antibiotics are often initiated at low thresholds due to the lack of reliable diagnostic tools to rule out infection at onset. This practice increases the use and may also lead to prolonged antibiotic treatment courses [3].

Antibiotics can save lives, but they are also linked to both short‐term and long‐term morbidities. Early exposure to antibiotics has been shown to disrupt the developing gut microbiota and has been associated with an increased risk of several chronic diseases [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. In extremely preterm infants, both the use and duration of antibiotics for EOS have been linked with short‐term morbidities such as necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) [9, 10]. In LOS, an association with NEC was also reported, although it has been suggested that this association may be limited to culture‐positive episodes [11]. Given the burden of disease and potential adverse consequences of treatment, close monitoring of both infections and antibiotic use is essential.

This study aimed to describe the epidemiology of sepsis and intravenous antibiotic use during the first six months of life in extremely preterm infants. In addition, we aimed to explore associations between culture‐positive and culture‐negative LOS with major neonatal morbidities.

Patient and Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a retrospective, population‐based cohort study in Eastern Denmark using data from the regional joint electronic health platform (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona Wisconsin, USA). Eastern Denmark covers nearly half of Denmark's population, approximately 30 000 births annually [12]. The initial care for extremely preterm infants is centralized at one Level 3 unit at Rigshospitalet in Copenhagen. When the infants are clinically stable and have reached post menstrual age above 27 + 6 weeks/days and weight of more than 1000 g, they are transferred to one of seven local Level 2 neonatal units. All infants born extremely preterm at 23 + 0 weeks/days to 27 + 6 weeks/days of gestation between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2021 were included if they received intravenous antibiotics within the first six months of life. Infants born < 23 + 0 weeks/days of gestation were excluded, as they rarely receive active treatment in Denmark.

The data were extracted using personal identification numbers that are issued to all Danish citizens at birth and follow them throughout their lives. The data included all hospital‐administered antibiotics. All treatment episodes in which intravenous antibiotics had been used were included, while those using only oral antibiotics were excluded. The first author (A. B. Arnesen) validated the antibiotic data by medical records and collected detailed information on the characteristics of infants, their clinical course, and clinical risk factors for infection. Data on all live births during the three‐year study period and overall mortality at 4 weeks past term date were obtained from the Danish Newborn Quality Database [12].

Definitions

Sepsis was identified using antibiotic administration as a proxy and classified as suspected, probable, or proven sepsis. Blood culture‐negative episodes were classified by the duration of antibiotic treatment as suspected sepsis, < 5 days, or probable sepsis, ≥ 5 days. Proven sepsis was defined as antibiotic treatment ≥ 5 days with a positive blood culture for a causative pathogen not considered contamination. Prolonged treatment with antibiotics was defined as treatment for more than 14 days. Sepsis episodes were further stratified by the timing of the treatment initiation. EOS occurred ≤ 3 days after birth, and LOS occurred between 4 and 90 days. In addition, the study included infectious episodes treated with intravenous antibiotics after 90 days until 6 months of age. These were classified as community acquired if antibiotics were initiated within 48 h of admission from home.

The incidences, rates, and use of antibiotics were calculated relative to all live births within the defined strata of gestational age.

Blood cultures obtained before initiation of intravenous antibiotic treatment were retrieved for the first treatment episode with antibiotics to estimate the overall frequency of pre‐treatment blood culture sampling. Cultures were considered positive if pathogenic organisms were identified. Blood cultures with Coagulase‐negative Staphylococci (CoNS) were considered positive only if both of two obtained cultures were positive and vancomycin was added to the treatment. This was in accordance with local recommendations.

Data on central venous catheters and peripherally inserted central catheters reflected the presence of either device at the time of antibiotic initiation. Information on catheter status prior to this time point was not collected.

Infection relapses were defined as a new antibiotic treatment episode lasting more than 3 days and occurring within 72 h after completion of the prior course. Sepsis‐related mortality was assessed through medical records and only included cases where the attending physicians determined sepsis as the cause of death. The sepsis fatality rate was also calculated and was defined as cases of death within 5 days from culture‐positive infections.

Severe BPD was defined as the need for respiratory support at a postmenstrual age of 40 + 0 weeks/days. This definition was used to select the severe cases of BPD with predictive value for both respiratory morbidity and neurosensory outcome [13]. Severe NEC was defined as any surgically treated NEC. Due to the retrospective study design, accurate classification of NEC according to Bell's criteria was not feasible. Only cases where antibiotics were administered prior to NEC were included in analyses of the association between LOS and NEC. Severe retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) was defined as any ROP requiring treatment. Severe brain injuries detected by ultrasound were not included in analyses of the association between LOS and major neonatal morbidities, as most such events were expected to occur before LOS.

Statistical Analysis

Incidences were calculated as episodes per 1000 live births within the gestational age group. Antibiotic use was calculated as treatment days per 1000 live births. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Associations between LOS and major neonatal morbidities were assessed by univariate logistic regression. These analyses were stratified in probable or proven LOS based on blood culture‐positivity. The reference group comprised infants who were not treated for LOS or those only for suspected LOS. Deaths were excluded from these analyses, as sepsis‐related mortality was examined separately. Mortality rates were calculated unadjusted.

All data processing and statistical analysis were performed using R and RStudio version 4.2.2 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Epidemiology and Use of Antibiotics

There were 87 120 infants born alive in Eastern Denmark during the three‐year study period. Of these, 232 infants (0.3%) were born extremely preterm between 23 + 0 weeks/days and 27 + 6 weeks/days of gestation. The median gestational age was 26 weeks (IQR 25–27), and the median birth weight was 953 g (IQR 672–984).

Overall, 187 out of 232 liveborn infants (81%, 95% CI 75%–85%) received intravenous antibiotics within the first six months of life (Figure 1). Antibiotics were most frequently initiated for suspected EOS, with 164 out of the 232 infants (71%, 95% CI 60%–82%) receiving treatment within the first 72 h of life. There were 118 infants treated for LOS (51%, 95% CI 45%–57%), while 22 infants were treated with intravenous antibiotics between 90 and 180 days of age (9%, 95% CI 6%–14%) (Table 1 and Figure 2).

A total of 220 antibiotic treatment episodes occurred among the 118 infants treated for LOS. Of these, 55 of the 118 (47%) infants were treated once, while 63 (53%) of the infants accounted for 165 of the 220 (75%) antibiotic treatment episodes. Ninety‐seven (82%) infants treated for LOS had previously received antibiotics for EOS. Antibiotic use for any classified EOS amounted to 3413 treatment days per 1000 live births. Corresponding figures were 9901 treatment days for LOS and 1129 treatment days for infections occurring after 90 days of age (Figure 2).

Proven Sepsis and Microbiology

Among infants with any classified EOS, 4 out of 164 (2.4‰) had culture‐proven infection. Based on the number of live births (232), this corresponded to an incidence of 17 per 1000 live births (95% CI 5–43 per 1000). Two episodes were caused by Group B Streptococci (GBS) (Table S1). There were 40 culture‐negative episodes per culture‐proven case of EOS. Of antibiotic courses for LOS, 41 out of 220 (19%) were culture‐positive, yielding an incidence of 177 per 1000 live births (95% CI 123–231 per 1000). The most common pathogens were Staphylococcus aureus , with 19 out of 41 cases (46%) and CoNS, with 7 out of 41 (17%). There were five culture‐negative episodes per culture‐proven case of LOS. Beyond 90 days of age, there were no blood culture‐positive infections, and five of the 22 episodes were community acquired.

Infection relapse occurred in 14 of all 425 (3%) initiated treatment courses. Of the infants with infection relapse, 11 had received antibiotics only for suspected sepsis (1–4 days). Seven of these 11 (64%) infants had a culture‐positive sepsis.

Clinical Course and Mortality

During the entire admission, 176 of the total cohort of 187 (94%) infants treated with antibiotics required continuous positive airway pressure with a median duration of 29 days (IQR 14–44). In addition, 128 (68%) infants were treated with invasive mechanical ventilation with a median duration of 7 days (IQR 3–18). Of infants treated for LOS, 83 of 118 (70%) infants had either a central venous catheter or a peripherally inserted central catheter in place at the time of antibiotic initiation.

A total of 175 of the 232 (80%) live births survived until 4 weeks beyond term equivalent age. We found that 16 infants died within 48 h from birth without being treated with antibiotics. Of all infants who died, 11 had a blood culture‐positive episode of sepsis during their entire admission time. Comorbidities were common among these 11 infants, including NEC, intraventricular haemorrhage and hydrocephalus. The sepsis fatality rate was 13 per 1000 live births (n = 3). Two of these three deaths were considered as sepsis‐related deaths by the attending physicians, corresponding to a sepsis‐related mortality of 9 infants per 1000 live births. The identified pathogens in these two cases were GBS and Candida albicans , respectively.

Late‐Onset Sepsis and Association With Neonatal Morbidities

Univariate logistic regression analyses were performed with a reference group of infants who were not treated for LOS or those treated only for suspected LOS. The infants with blood culture‐negative LOS had significantly increased odds of NEC (OR 5.38, 95% CI 1.79%–16.23%). The first identified full‐course treatment episodes for LOS was used in the analyses, and all episodes preceded the onset of NEC (Figure 3). Blood culture‐negative LOS was also associated with increased odds of ROP (OR 5.19, 95% CI 1.42%–19.02%). Blood‐culture‐positive LOS was not significantly associated with NEC, ROP or BPD.

Discussion

In this Danish population‐based cohort study, 187 of 232 (81%) infants born extremely preterm between 23 + 0 weeks/days and 27 + 6 weeks/days received intravenous antibiotics within the first six months of life. The high proportion of infants treated with antibiotics was mainly driven by suspected EOS. Sepsis was confirmed by blood cultures in only a minority of these episodes. There were 40 culture‐negative treatment episodes for every blood culture‐proven episode of EOS. For LOS, there were five treatment episodes per culture‐proven case. The observed fatality rate was low. LOS accounted for most treatment episodes, with approximately 50% of infants receiving treatment. Antibiotic treatment was frequently continued without confirmation by blood cultures. Overall, the antibiotic use related to LOS was almost three‐fold higher than that associated with EOS. The use of antibiotics declined substantially beyond 3 months of age.

The use of antibiotics was substantially higher for extremely preterm infants compared with that found for infants born preterm after 28 weeks of gestation and in term infants [14, 15]. The widespread use of antibiotics during the first days of life for extremely preterm infants has previously been documented and was consistent with our findings. Studies reported antibiotic use within the first week of life in 73%–100% of infants born extremely preterm [1, 16, 17]. This contrasted with the reported incidences of proven EOS between 1.6% and 1.8%, which closely aligned with our findings [16, 18]. The low number of proven EOS limited our microbiological data. However, the bacterial pattern in EOS differed from that observed in LOS, where nosocomial pathogens were dominating as in other studies [19, 20, 21]. LOS accounted for nearly nine out of 10 blood culture‐positive infections in this cohort. Reported incidences of proven infections ranged from 20% to 25% for LOS, comparable with the 19% in our study [19, 20, 22]. Staphylococcus aureus was the most frequently identified pathogen in our cohort, whereas CoNS was often reported as the most common pathogen in LOS among extremely preterm infants in other studies [14, 15]. This finding may be related to our local strategy of classifying CoNS as true pathogens only when both of two blood cultures are positive. Using this classification, we aimed to reduce the risk of misclassifying contamination as infection. In addition, we used the initiation of vancomycin by the attending physician as a pragmatic marker of a clinical decision that CoNS represented a true infection. Our microbiological pattern in LOS suggested potential opportunities for preventative strategies to reduce these infections. This was supported by an 11‐year longitudinal study by Bizzarro et al., in which targeted initiatives to reduce healthcare‐associated infections resulted in a significant decrease in rates of LOS [23]. This was further underscored by the high proportion of infants with culture‐positive LOS, who had a central venous catheter in place when antibiotics were initiated. A specific strategy with fungal prophylaxis was not used in the study period and is now implemented.

Although one of five LOS episodes was culture‐positive, most antibiotic treatments were administered for culture‐negative episodes. This pattern was also consistent with other studies, which reported rates between 38%–80% [1, 3, 16]. Descisions regarding continuation or discontinuation of antibiotics may be challenging when blood cultures remain negative. The non‐specific clinical signs in this population, often indistinguishable from infection, complicate early discontinuation. Moreover, the fear of infection relapse after antibiotic discontinuation may lead clinicians to continue treatment [24]. Relapse of infection occurred in 3% of treatment episodes in our cohort. However, event numbers were low and most of these cases had previously received short antibiotic courses. We also found associations between LOS and major neonatal morbidities. The odds of NEC and ROP were increased, but only among infants with blood culture‐negative LOS. We cannot exclude a causal relation, but the finding should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of cases and the possibility of residual confounding. It is also plausible that antibiotic exposure reflects the underlying illness severity as sicker infants are more likely both to receive prolonged antibiotic treatment and to develop these morbidities.

Of the infants who died and were born extremely preterm during the study period, 35 of 57 (61%) received antibiotics. The mortality rate was lower in the subgroup that did not receive antibiotics compared to infants treated with antibiotics. Moreover, a substantial proportion of infants who died without receiving antibiotics did so within the first 48 h of life. This likely reflected early clinical assessments of non‐viability, such as severe congenital malformations, which preluded initiation of blood cultures or antibiotic therapy. The sepsis‐related mortality accounted for approximately 4% of all deaths. In comparison, the sepsis fatality rate was 5%. Both appeared low compared with other studies that reported 4%–11% [19, 22]. This may reflect our definitions and methods of calculation of sepsis related death compared to these.

Continuous monitoring of antibiotic use and sepsis rates is essential in the care of extremely preterm infants, a high‐risk population vulnerable to sepsis. To balance the benefits and potential risks, strategies to guide appropriate treatment rates are needed. More than half of all antibiotic courses in this cohort were due to LOS, mainly episodes classified as probable. Most proven episodes of LOS were nosocomial with typically hospital‐acquired pathogens. These findings underscore the need for enhanced strategies to reduce these potential preventable infections when considering the high morbidity in this population. Initiatives should be focused on improving appropriate hand hygiene and reducing central line‐associated bloodstream infections.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of our study was the population‐based design, which allowed us to quantify incidences and antibiotic use on a population level. A more accurate presentation of sepsis and antibiotic use was achieved by including all infectious episodes from birth until 6 months of age. In comparison, many studies were not population‐based and only considered initial antibiotic treatments. Our data were based on dispensed antibiotics, which we believe offered a more reliable indicator of potential sepsis than diagnoses alone, which may be underreported.

The study was limited by the relatively small number of positive blood culture results, thus limiting the statistical strength of the findings. The lack of infants who received no antibiotics limited our ability to fully assess the impact of antibiotic treatments. Other limitations included a lack of information on all obtained blood cultures and the volume of blood used. This may have affected the accuracy of the results. In addition, the retrospective and observational design may have affected data quality derived from the medical records. Other limitations included the absence of data on other indications for antibiotic initiation such as ventilator‐associated pneumonia, meningitis, or skin infections.

Conclusions

The study found that four out of five extremely preterm infants received intravenous antibiotics within the first six months of life. More than 70% of the infants received empirical antibiotics within the first 72 h of life with only a few proven cases of EOS. Approximately 50% were treated for LOS, which accounted for most cases of proven sepsis and more than three times the antibiotic burden compared to EOS. Staphylococcus aureus and CoNS were the most commonly identified pathogens in LOS. The substantial burden of nosocomial infections in this vulnerable population underscores the need for continued surveillance to monitor infection rates and inform prevention strategies.
