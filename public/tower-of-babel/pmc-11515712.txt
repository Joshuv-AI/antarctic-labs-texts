Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and cardiovascular events among patients with type 2 diabetes and low-to-normal body mass index: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Yuichiro Mori, Toshiaki Komura, Motohiko Adomi, Ryuichiro Yagi, Shingo Fukuma, Naoki Kondo, Motoko Yanagita, O Kenrik Duru, Katherine R Tuttle, Kosuke Inoue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with low-to-normal body mass index (BMI; < 25.0 kg/m2) were underrepresented in major randomized controlled trials on sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors for type 2 diabetes. The present study aims to investigate the effectiveness of SGLT2 inhibitors for cardiovascular outcomes among patients with type 2 diabetes and low-to-normal BMI, using finer stratification than previous trials.MethodsThis cohort study with a target trial emulation framework was conducted using insurance claims and health screening records of more than 30 million working-age citizens in Japan acquired from April 1, 2015 to March 31, 2022. 139,783 new users of SGLT2 inhibitors matched to 139,783 users of dipeptidyl protease (DPP) 4 inhibitors with stratification by BMI category (< 20.0, 20.0–22.4, 22.5–24.9, 25.0–29.9, 30.0–34.9, and 35.0 ≤ kg/m2). The primary outcome was a composite of all-cause death, myocardial infarction, stroke, or heart failure. Secondary outcomes were the components of the primary outcome. Cox proportional hazard models were used to compare SGLT2 inhibitors with DPP4 inhibitors in the whole population and subgroups defined by the BMI category.ResultsAmong participants, 17.3% (n = 48,377) were female and 31.0% (n = 86,536) had low-to-normal BMI (< 20.0 kg/m2, 1.9% [n = 5,350]; 20.0–22.4 kg/m2, 8.5% [n = 23,818]; and 22.5–24.9 kg/m2, 20.5% [n = 57,368]). Over a median follow-up of 24 months, the primary outcome occurred in 2.9% (n = 8,165) of participants. SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with a decreased incidence of the primary outcome in the whole population (HR [95%CI] = 0.92 [0.89 to 0.96]), but not in patients with low-to-normal BMI (< 20.0 kg/m2, HR [95%CI] = 1.08 [0.80 to 1.46]; 20.0–22.4 kg/m2, HR [95%CI] = 1.04 [0.90 to 1.20]; and 22.5–24.9 kg/m2, HR [95%CI] = 0.92 [0.84 to 1.01]).ConclusionsThe protective effect of SGLT2 inhibitors on cardiovascular events among patients with type 2 diabetes appeared to decrease with lower BMI and was not significant among patients with low-to-normal BMI (< 25.0 kg/m2). These findings suggest the importance of considering BMI when initiating SGLT2 inhibitors.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-024-02478-7.

Introduction

Diabetes is a major contributor to cardiovascular disease (CVD), accounting for more than 10% of global mortality [1] with an increasing prevalence in working-age adults [2, 3], a major target of early preventive intervention. In 2017–2018 in the US, 18.5% of adults aged 45–64 had diabetes, increased from 13.1% in 1999–2000 [4]. Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are recommended in the management of diabetes [5] due to their effectiveness in reducing CVD risk in patients with type 2 diabetes [6–8]. Recently, the benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors for heart failure (HF) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) have been extended to individuals without diabetes [9–13]. From evidence syntheses of these randomized controlled trials (RCTs), SGLT2 inhibitors were considered effective in a broad range of patient subgroups [14, 15].

A notable limitation in the existing evidence on SGLT2 inhibitors is the lack of CVD outcomes data in patients with type 2 diabetes and low body mass index (BMI). The major randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on SGLT2 inhibitors for diabetes included cohorts with a mean BMI above 30.0 kg/m2 [6–8, 14, 16]. Consequently, subgroup analyses of those trials combined patients with low-to-normal BMI (< 25.0 kg/m2) or even overweight (< 30.0 kg/m2) into a single category [7, 17–19]. Given that starvation mimicry related to net urinary caloric loss is a proposed mechanism of cardioprotective effects [20–23], and since there are a non-negligible number of patients with type 2 diabetes and low-to-normal BMI [24], there is a need for a study of patients with low-to-normal BMI to advance understanding for the effectiveness of SGLT2 inhibitors in this subpopulation.

The aim of this study was to use one of the largest nationwide databases of the working-age population in Japan to investigate the effectiveness of SGLT2 inhibitors versus an active comparator, dipeptidyl protease (DPP) 4 inhibitors, for CVD outcomes and death across BMI strata. We applied a target trial emulation framework [25] to explicitly emulate a hypothetical RCT.

Methods

Study design: target trial emulation

The present study aimed to emulate a pragmatic randomized controlled trial that evaluates the effectiveness of SGLT2 inhibitors for CVD outcomes. To align the timing of eligibility assessment, intervention, and the start of follow-up [25], we implemented an active-comparator prevalent new-user design [26] as implemented in previous target trial emulations on SGLT2 inhibitors [27–29]. Dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4) inhibitors were used as active comparators, in line with previous target trial emulations on SGLT2 inhibitors with prevalent new-user design [27–30], because of their neutral effect on cardiovascular outcomes [31, 32] and widespread use in Japan [33]. The comparison of the target trial and the present study is listed in Table S1. The study was reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [34] (Table S2).

Setting

We used claims data, death records, and annual health screening records of the Japan Health Insurance Association (JHIA) from the beginning of fiscal year 2015 to the end of fiscal year 2021 in Japan (April 1, 2015–March 31, 2022). The JHIA, the largest health insurer in Japan, provides healthcare coverage to over 30 million working-age individuals, mainly employees of small-to-middle-sized companies, representing approximately 40% of the working-age population in Japan [35]. The details of Japan’s government-led annual health screening program have been explained elsewhere [36, 37]. Every government-certified public health insurer in Japan, including the JHIA, is obliged to provide health screening to their members at ages 40–74 every year. The attendance rate of the JHIA members for the screenings was 52.3% in 2020 [38]. The screenings included self-reported lifestyle information, physical examination, and laboratory testing. Claims data consisted of outpatient, inpatient, and pharmacy claims, linked with the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) disease codes.

Study population

We first identified a base cohort of all individuals who were at least once new users of SGLT2 inhibitors or users of DPP4 inhibitors from April 2015 to March 2022 and have at least once attended health screenings during the same period. Being a new user was defined as having an initial prescription record of the medication with a minimum of six months of preceding insurance coverage to confirm the absence of prior use. Those who became new users of both SGLT2 inhibitors and DPP4 inhibitors during the same month were not included in the base cohort.

From the base cohort, we constructed monthly exposure datasets for every month from October 2015 to March 2022 and analyzed the pooled data of these datasets. For each exposure dataset, the inclusion criterion was defined as those who had not initiated SGLT2 inhibitors up to the respective index month. We then applied exclusion criteria to each exposure dataset. Individuals were excluded if they met at least one of the following conditions: 1) no evidence of diabetes (i.e., no use of glucose-lowering agents, fasting blood glucose [FBG] ≤ 126 mg/dL, and hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c] ≤ 6.4%); 2) annual health screening records were not available from the previous fiscal year up until the index month; 3) had missing data in covariates (see Pre-baseline covariates section); 4) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73m2; 5) had a hospitalization event due to CVD within two months before the month; and 6) had received a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes (ICD-10: E100–109) or cancer (C000-970, D000–099, or D370–489). As the health screening program requires measuring only either FBG or HbA1c, eligibility criteria regarding these values were assessed based on available measurements.

Exposure ascertainment

Within each monthly exposure dataset, participants were classified into two groups: SGLT2 inhibitor new users and DPP4 inhibitor users. The SGLT2 inhibitor new users included incident new users (i.e., those without prior prescription of DPP4 inhibitors) and prevalent new users (i.e., those with prior prescription of DPP4 inhibitors), while DPP4 inhibitor users included incident new users (i.e., those who initiated DPP4 inhibitors in the index month) and prevalent users (i.e., those who initiated DPP4 inhibitors up until the index month).

Outcome ascertainment

The primary outcome was the composite of all-cause death and hospitalization due to stroke (I60–63), HF ( I50, I110, I130, or I132), and myocardial infarction (MI; I21 or I22), as validated in Japanese administrative data [39]. The secondary outcomes were the components of the primary outcome. Outcomes were ascertained monthly. All participants were followed until an occurrence of the outcome, disenrollment from the health insurance plan, or the end of study period (March 2022).

Pre-baseline covariates

For each monthly exposure dataset, the following variables were extracted from the claims data up until the month and the most recent health screening data: age, sex, prescription histories within last six months (insulin, statin, and glucose-lowering agents other than SGLT2 or DPP4 inhibitors), past CVD histories (defined as same as Outcomes), self-reported lifestyle information and objectively measured items in the annual health screening (smoking status [yes if: more than 100 cigarettes lifetime, smoking duration longer than six months, and the last smoking within a month], BMI, HbA1c, FBG, systolic blood pressure, and eGFR). The eGFR was calculated with equations modified for Japanese adults [40]. We categorized BMI into six groups (< 20.0, 20.0–22.4, 22.5–24.9, 25.0–29.9, 30.0–34.9, and ≥ 35.0 kg/m2), systolic blood pressure into three groups (< 110, 110–139, and ≥ 140 mmHg), and eGFR into four groups (30–44, 45–59, 60–89, and ≥ 90 ml/min/1.73m2).

Matching

Within each exposure dataset, SGLT2 inhibitor new users were propensity-score matched to DPP4 inhibitor users in a 1:1 ratio. The matching was stratified by BMI category and used a nearest neighbor approach with a caliper of 0.10. Specifically, incident new users of SGLT2 inhibitors were matched with incident new users of DPP4 inhibitors, whereas prevalent new users of SGLT2 inhibitors were matched with prevalent users of DPP4 inhibitors with the same duration of the prevalent use of DPP4 inhibitors [26]. We conducted the conducted the matching chronologically from the dataset of October 2015 to the dataset of March 2022 essentially without replacement, but prevalent new users of SGLT2 inhibitors were included in the match even if they had been once matched as a DPP4 inhibitor users in prior [27].

Propensity scores for initiating SGLT2 inhibitors were also separately constructed for each exposure dataset with pre-baseline covariates. To comprehensively include eligible participants in the matching, those with available HbA1c data were matched based on models with HbA1c, whereas others were on models with FBG. The following three interaction terms were incorporated into the model: 1) HbA1c (or FBG) and insulin use, 2) HbA1c (or FBG) and the use of other glucose-lowering agents, and 3) insulin use and the use of other glucose-lowering agents.

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the pooled data of all matched datasets. First, we compared the risks of composite outcome events between SGLT2 inhibitor users and DPP4 inhibitor users during the follow-up period after matching by plotting a Kaplan–Meier curve. Second, we fitted multivariable Cox proportional hazard models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for outcome events. We used robust standard error to account for correlations in the data for individuals appeared in both arms. In this model, we adjusted for age, sex, previous CVD diagnosis, BMI, systolic blood pressure, eGFR, prescription histories of insulin, statin, and glucose-lowering agents other than SGLT2 and DPP4 inhibitors within the last six months before the matched month, and the duration between the month of entering the base cohort and the month of being matched. Third, we constructed Cox proportional hazard models separately for each BMI category to assess the effect heterogeneity of SGLT2 inhibitors across BMI categories.

We also conducted subgroup analyses according to age (< 60 years, ≥ 60 years), sex (male, female), and user types (with or without prevalent use of DPP4 inhibitors). In the analysis on secondary outcomes and the subgroup analyses, we compared the effectiveness of SGLT2 inhibitors between those with BMI < 22.5 kg/m2 and BMI ≥ 22.5 kg/m2 to secure a sufficient number of events in every comparison arm.

When summarizing patient characteristics before and after matching, pooled data of randomly matched DPP4 inhibitor users in each exposure dataset were used as representative of DPP4 inhibitor users before matching [27]. This approach was necessary because the characteristics of the same patient may differ depending on the index month in which they were matched. The absolute value of standardized difference < 0.10 was considered as an indicator of sufficient balance between groups. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1. The study was approved by the institutional review board at Kyoto University (IRB#R3452).

Results

Across a total of 616,887 adults in the base cohort, we identified 177,290 eligible new users of SGLT2 inhibitors and 401,423 eligible users of DPP4 inhibitors. Of these, 279,566 individuals (SGLT2 inhibitor users: 139,783; DPP4 inhibitor users: 139,783) were matched (Figure S1). Before matching, SGLT2 inhibitor new users were generally younger, had higher BMI, were more likely to use other glucose-lowering agents, and had higher FBG or HbA1c, and eGFR compared to DPP4 inhibitor users. The patient characteristics were well balanced after matching (Figure S2). Among the matched SGLT2 inhibitor new users, the mean age (standard deviation [SD]) was 55.9 (8.7) years, and 17.3% (24,203/139,783) were female. The proportion of DPP4 inhibitor users who were matched afterward as SGLT2 inhibitor new users was 22.9% (32,049/139,783). The prevalence of BMI categories of < 20.0, 20.0–22.4, 22.5–24.9, 25.0–29.9, 30.0–34.9, and ≥ 35.0 kg/m2 were 1.9%, 8.5%, 20.5%, 46.4%, 17.7%, and 5.0%, respectively. (Table 1). Demographic characteristics in each BMI category were displayed in Tables S3 and S4.

During the median follow-up period of 24 months (interquartile range: 10–42 months), there were 5,350 events of death and hospitalization due to CVD. Overall, SGLT2 inhibitor users experienced a lower cumulative incidence of the primary outcome than DPP4 inhibitor users (Fig. 1). Among the components of the primary outcome, this decreased incidence was led by all-cause deaths and hospitalizations due to stroke (Figure S3). The Cox proportional hazards model showed that SGLT2 inhibitor users had a lower risk of the primary outcome than DPP4 inhibitor users after adjusting for all pre-baseline covariates and the period between the duration of prevalent DPP4 inhibitor use (HR [95% CI], 0.92 [0.89 to 0.96]).

Across BMI categories, SGLT2 inhibitor users with BMI of < 20.0, 20.0–22.4, 22.5–24.9 kg/m2 did not show a significant decrease in the primary outcome compared to DPP4 inhibitor users with the same BMI category (HR [95%CI]: BMI < 20.0 kg/m2, 1.08 [0.80 to 1.46]; 20.0–22.4 kg/m2, 1.04 [0.90 to 1.20]; and 22.5–24.9 kg/m2, 0.92 [0.84 to 1.01]), but those with BMI of 25.0–29.9, 30.0–34.9, and ≥ 35.0 kg/m2 showed lower incidence of the primary outcome (25.0–29.9 kg/m2, 0.93 [0.88 to 0.99]; 30.0–34.9 kg/m2, 0.87 [0.80 to 0.95]; and ≥ 35.0 kg/m2, 0.84 [0.72 to 0.99]) (Fig. 2). When we categorized participants into two groups by BMI (< 22.5 vs. ≥ 22.5 kg/m2), the estimated effects were significantly different between these two groups (HR [95% CI] in BMI < 22.5 vs. ≥ 22.5 kg/m2, 1.05 [0.92 to 1.20] vs. 0.91 [0.87 to 0.95]; p-for-interaction = 0.029). Regarding secondary outcomes, we found similar trends for all-cause death and HF, while the latter was not statistically significant (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this population-based cohort study with a target trial emulation framework and stratification of BMI categories, SGLT2 inhibitors, compared to DPP4 inhibitors, did not significantly reduce the incidence of a composite cardiovascular outcome, particularly all-cause death and HF hospitalization, among adults with type 2 diabetes and low-to-normal BMIs. There was a trend where the effectiveness of SGLT2 inhibitors appeared to decrease with lower BMI. This trend was observed particularly among older adults, male, and prevalent users of DPP4 inhibitors (Graphical Abstract).

Our study including individuals with a wide range of BMI adds further insights into the current state of knowledge about the effectiveness of SGLT2 inhibitors in individuals with type 2 diabetes. Previously, patients with low-to-normal BMI were less likely to be included in RCTs on SGLT2 inhibitors, resulting in insufficient evidence for that population. While there are also several observational studies on SGLT2 inhibitors using large databases, they either did not collect information on BMI [41] or mostly included overweight and obese patients [42, 43]. The lack of a protective effect in this subgroup in our study contrasts with the broader positive outcomes seen in earlier research conducted mainly among patients with high BMI (e.g., mean BMI > 30 kg/m2). Also, our approach of using a target trial emulation framework [25] with a prevalent-new user design [26] aligns with methodological advancements in observational studies. By emulating an RCT, we have provided complementary results to existing RCTs in patients with high BMI, particularly highlighting the different results in patients with low-to-normal BMI.

Given the current incomplete understanding of the cardioprotective mechanisms of SGLT2 inhibitors [21], further molecular and epidemiological researches are needed to understand why their effectiveness was diminished in individuals with low-to-normal BMI. One of the plausible hypotheses may be the vulnerability of this population to starvation mimicry, a mechanism proposed to underlie the cardioprotective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors by promoting ketogenesis and favoring energy utilization in the myocardium [20–23]. Indeed, the treatment benefit for HF hospitalizations was mitigated among those with lower BMI, despite the benefit in those with higher BMI, as demonstrated in previous RCTs. In contrast, no clear treatment effect heterogeneity by BMI was observed for stroke hospitalizations, for which SGLT2 inhibitors might be beneficial through blood pressure lowering among those without established CVD [44].

Our findings support individualized approaches to prescribing SGLT2 inhibitors in patients with type 2 diabetes. For this population, current guidelines recommend metformin as a first-line glucose-lowering drug therapy in addition to lifestyle modifications. Additionally, SGLT2 inhibitors are considered first line agents for CVD risk reduction irrespective of need for glycemic control [5]. For patients with elevated CVD risk and low-to-normal BMI, our results would not necessarily discourage the use of SGLT2 inhibitors, but provide a cautionary note about expectations for their clinical benefit. In this context, clinicians should take into account the uncertainties about benefit of SGLT2 inhibitors for CVD risk reduction versus risks of adverse events, such as ketoacidosis [14, 45], when prescribing these medications.

There are several limitations in our study. First, although we included an extensive set of covariates in our model, there are likely unmeasured confounders, such as socioeconomic status, which could affect the use of SGLT2 inhibitors given their higher out-of-pocket costs than DPP4 inhibitors. Second, we cannot rule out the possibility of information bias due to the misclassification of confounders (especially self-reported lifestyle information). Third, our study design did not assess the dose–response relationship between SGLT2 inhibitors and CVD outcomes, though cardioprotective effects of these agents appear to be largely dose-independent [6]. Fourth, since we restricted the cohort to individuals who had undergone health screenings, our findings might be subject to self-selection bias if adherence rates for health screenings vary across individual characteristics. Fifth, given the relatively low CVD risk and low proportion of those with established CVD (slightly over 2%) in our cohort compared to previous studies [6–8], the results could be different in patients with a higher CVD risk or established CVD. Sixth, our database did not contain information on the cause of death. Last, although the JHIA is the largest public health insurer, covering approximately 40% (30 million) of the working-age population in Japan, our results might not be generalizable to the entire population with type 2 diabetes, such as those who have retired. The low prevalence of diabetes among working-age women in Japan (roughly one-third of men [46]) and the different epidemiology of obesity between Japan and other countries [47] may also restrict the generalizability of our findings to other settings. Further investigations into the effectiveness of SGLT2 inhibitors among a wide range of patient characteristics with low-to-normal BMI are needed.

Conclusion

The protective effect of SGLT2 inhibitors on CVD events appeared to decrease with lower BMI and was not present in patients with type 2 diabetes and low-to-normal BMI (< 25.0 kg/m2). These patients were underrepresented in previous RCTs compared to those with higher BMI. The present findings offer new insights to support personalized treatment strategies for type 2 diabetes, including consideration of BMI when initiating SGLT2 inhibitors.
