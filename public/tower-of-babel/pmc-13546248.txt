Enhanced Macular Imaging Referral Pathway: Bridging Community Optometrists and Hospital Specialists for Smarter Eye Care
Authors: Serena Salvatore, Josh Harvey, Amy Hughes, Lisa Gibson, John Hopcroft, Sarah Swift, James Marsterson, Clare Bailey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHospital eye services in England face sustained demand, with nearly 9 million outpatient appointments in 2023–2024. Teleophthalmology pathways that combine community-acquired imaging with hospital virtual review may reduce unnecessary face-to-face visits. We evaluated an enhanced macular imaging referral pathway linking community optometry optical coherence tomography (OCT) and colour fundus imaging with hospital-led teleophthalmology review.MethodsWe performed a retrospective service evaluation of 376 consecutive adult macular referrals received at University Hospitals of Bristol and Weston, between July 2024 and March 2025. Community optometrists submitted bilateral macula-centred 45° colour fundus photographs, bilateral OCT volume scans in digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) format and clinical data via a secure platform. Referrals were reviewed by a consultant ophthalmologist or hospital optometrist within 1 working day. Under the previous pathway, all accepted referrals required an initial face-to-face hospital appointment. Primary outcomes were referral appropriateness, avoided hospital appointments and redirection to other sub-specialties; secondary outcomes were management decision and diagnostic category. Analyses were descriptive.ResultsMedian age was 74 years (IQR 14); 54.3% were female. Overall, 327/376 referrals (87.0%, 95% CI 83.2–90.0) were appropriate for macular assessment. Of the referrals, 49 (13.0%) were inappropriate because of incomplete imaging (n = 18, 4.7%) or redirection to another sub-specialty (n = 31, 8.2%), most commonly vitreoretinal services (n = 27, 7.2%). Among appropriate referrals, 241/327 (73.7%, 95% CI 68.7–78.2) avoided the mandatory first hospital visit required under the previous pathway. Management outcomes across the cohort were: neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) pathway, 17.1%; redirection, 6.1%; discharge to community, 10.2%; virtual follow-up, 15.3%; and face-to-face appointment, 51.2%. Mean time to treatment for nAMD was 8.6 days (95% CI 6.0–11.2 days).ConclusionsThis teleophthalmology-enabled macular referral pathway achieved high referral appropriateness and substantially reduced mandatory first-visit hospital appointments while supporting timely treatment for sight-threatening macular disease. The model appears scalable and may help National Health Service hospital eye services manage rising demand more efficiently.

Introduction

Ophthalmology is the largest outpatient specialty in England, accounting for nearly 9 million appointments in 2023–2024 [1], and hospital eye services (HES) continue to experience sustained growth in demand. Ageing populations, wider use of multi-modal retinal imaging in community optometry and expanded screening programmes have collectively increased referral volumes to HES. Within the UK, community optometrists refer incidental ocular abnormalities identified during National Health Service (NHS) sight tests at their discretion [2]. To maintain timely access for sight-threatening disease, referral pathways that triage risk and enable timely review of imaging are required.

Teleophthalmology and digital referral models that integrate community-acquired imaging with specialist virtual review have demonstrated reductions in unnecessary face-to-face appointments [3], shorter waiting times and high patient satisfaction across macular and glaucoma pathways. Prior programmes report that 20–48% of patients can avoid an in-person visit when imaging is reviewed remotely [4], and targeted appointments can be pre-booked for those requiring treatment. Building on this evidence, our service implemented an enhanced macular imaging referral pathway at University Hospitals of Bristol and Weston (UHBW), Bristol Eye Hospital, to streamline adult macular referrals from community optometry.

This study reports real-world outcomes from the first 376 consecutive referrals assessed through this pathway, describing referral appropriateness, redirection to alternative sub-specialties, the proportion of avoided hospital appointments and subsequent management decisions. We also describe key service design elements, including the 1-working-day review standard and use of digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) optical coherence tomography (OCT) files, and discuss implications for capacity and sustainability. Most macula referral pathways may include referrals with no imaging, single static OCT line scans and/or OCT summary report only. The principal innovation evaluated here is the transmission of complete, scrollable DICOM OCT volume datasets directly from community optometry into the hospital picture archiving and communication system (PACS), enabling full volumetric specialist review equivalent to that available in clinic, integration into the hospital electronic record and structured same-day feedback to the referring optometrist. Accordingly, the aim of this service evaluation was to assess the feasibility and operational performance of this enhanced-imaging pathway against the preceding standard pathway, in which every accepted macular referral required an initial face-to-face hospital appointment before any onward management decision could be made.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive adult macular referrals assessed via an enhanced macular imaging referral platform between July 2024 and March 2025 at University Hospitals of Bristol and Weston NHS Foundation Trust, Bristol Eye Hospital, a tertiary referral centre. The referral pathway applied to both urgent and routine adult macular referrals, excluding pure vitreomacular pathologies. The reference comparator was the preceding standard referral pathway operating at the same centre, under which every accepted adult macular referral was allocated an initial face-to-face hospital appointment before any management decision, and in which referrals were typically accompanied by, at most, a single static OCT line-scan image or summary report rather than a full volumetric dataset. The enhanced pathway replaced this mandatory first visit with asynchronous specialist review of complete community-acquired DICOM OCT volumes and colour fundus images. As a retrospective service evaluation, the study was designed to characterise the feasibility and operational performance of the enhanced pathway rather than to provide a controlled comparative-effectiveness analysis; the structural requirement for a face-to-face appointment under the previous pathway provides the denominator against which avoided appointments are defined.

Ethical Approval

This study was conducted as a retrospective service evaluation using routinely collected, fully anonymised clinical data. The project was registered with the Clinical Audit Department at University Hospitals of Bristol and Weston NHS Foundation Trust (reference A&EP/CA/202526/01). In line with UK Health Research Authority guidance, formal research ethics committee approval was not required. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments. As all data were fully anonymised prior to analysis, individual informed consent was not required; the requirement for consent was waived by the institutional clinical audit team.

Referral Review and Clinical Workflow

Virtual review of submitted referrals was undertaken during normal working hours by the hospital macular team, comprising consultant ophthalmologists and hospital optometrists, with the review workload divided approximately 40% to ophthalmologists and 60% to specialist hospital optometrists. Where review activity exceeded job-planned virtual clinic capacity, additional reviewing sessions were undertaken as remunerated extra sessions to maintain the 1-working-day standard. For each referral, the reviewing clinician examined the full bilateral DICOM OCT volume and colour fundus images alongside the structured clinical dataset, recorded a management decision and returned structured feedback to the referring optometrist through the secure platform.

Referrals identified as patients suspected to have neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) were contacted by telephone by the reviewing team and booked directly into a face-to-face clinic for repeat imaging, visual acuity assessment, consent and same-day treatment where indicated; these patients in addition received written confirmation by letter. All other patients were notified of the outcome of their referral, and of any onward appointment or discharge decision, by letter. At the hospital visit, repeat imaging, clinical examination and the consent process were undertaken by specialist hospital optometrists. Repeat imaging at the hospital was performed when treatment decisions and consent required contemporaneous, device-matched acquisition and visual acuity; the community imaging served to triage urgency and appropriateness rather than to replace pre-treatment assessment.

Referral Pathway and Data Capture

Adult patients identified as requiring a medical retinal referral for macular findings to HES during an eye examination with a community optometrist were eligible for the pathway.

Participating community optometrists submitted a standardised dataset through the Primary Eyecare Services (PES) IT platform comprising demographics, history of presenting complaint, best-corrected visual acuity (Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study [ETDRS] letters), intraocular pressure, anterior segment findings (including presence of cataract) of both eyes and diabetes status. Each referral required the upload of a bilateral 45° colour fundus photograph centred on the macula and bilateral OCT volume scan in DICOM format, uploaded into a secure picture archiving and communication system (PACS) with a link to review the referral and a link to provide structured feedback to the referring optometrist.

Funded by NHS England via an Eyecare Transformation Accelerator grant, the pathway was co-developed by Avon Local Optical Committee, the Bristol, North Somerset and South Gloucestershire Integrated Care Board (ICB), NHS England South West Outpatient Transformation Team, the Macular Society and UHBW. Bespoke software allows optometrists to transmit full DICOM OCT scans and colour photographs via the PES secure IT platform for remote referral review. PES holds a contract with the ICB to coordinate a small fee payment, for each complete and appropriate referral, to community optometrists.

Referrals were automatically booked by the PES administration team into a dedicated HES clinic list via the Electronic Referral Service (ERS) and data uploaded through a secure web form to the HES review centre for assessment. The patient’s summary care record was attached, and the general practitioner was automatically notified of the referral. OCT images were imported into the HES PACS and electronic medical records for future reference.

Service Standard

All referrals were triaged with a target standard of review within 1 working day of receipt. Where appropriate, structured feedback on referral quality and decision making was provided electronically to the referring optometrist.

Outcomes and Definitions

The primary outcomes were (1) appropriateness of referral to the macular pathway, defined as the proportion deemed suitable for macular assessment on the basis of submitted imaging and clinical data; (2) proportion of avoided hospital appointments, defined as the proportion of appropriate referrals for which the initial face-to-face HES appointment, mandatory for all referrals under the preceding pathway, was not required following virtual review (that is, referrals managed without a separate diagnostic clinic visit); and (3) redirection rate to alternative sub-specialties. Secondary outcomes included final management decision (discharge to community, virtual review, face-to-face clinic appointment or enrolment into the nAMD treatment pathway), imaging completeness, time to review and the proportion of referrals reviewed within the 1-working-day target.

Data Handling and Statistical Analysis

Clinical data were stored on UK regional servers with General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)-compliant safeguards. Statistical analysis was descriptive throughout, as appropriate for a retrospective service evaluation of a complete consecutive cohort rather than a sampled population; no formal hypothesis testing was prespecified and no inferential comparisons were performed. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and summarised as median with interquartile range (IQR) where non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were summarised as counts and proportions. Referral appropriateness, management outcomes, redirection rates and the proportion of avoided hospital appointments were reported as percentages, and denominators were stated explicitly for all sub-group analyses. The primary outcome, referral appropriateness and avoided hospital appointments was in addition reported with a 95% confidence interval calculated using the Wilson score method for a binomial proportion. Agreement between the provisional reason for referral assigned by the community optometrist and the diagnosis recorded after hospital review was assessed using the percentage of exact agreement, reported with a 95% confidence interval and Cohen’s kappa coefficient, interpreted according to the benchmarks of Landis and Koch. Analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Sex data were collected as part of the standardised referral dataset. Given the retrospective service evaluation design and small sample, no sex stratified efficacy analysis was prespecified; however, participant sex is reported in the cohort characteristics.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

We analysed 376 consecutive referrals. Median age was 74 years (IQR 14); 54.3% were female and 45.7% male. Of the participants, 73.9% self-identified as White British, 24.0% did not declare their ethnicity, 1.6% were Caribbean or Black British Caribbean and 0.5% belonged to other ethnic groups.

Referral Appropriateness and Redirection

Overall, 327/376 referrals (87.0%, 95% CI 83.2–90.0%) were deemed appropriate for macular assessment. Of them, 49 referrals (13.0%) were considered as inappropriate for the macular pathway: 18 (4.7%) owing to incomplete imaging and 31 (8.2%) owing to redirection to another sub-specialty. Of those redirected, the majority were routed to vitreoretinal services (n = 27, 7.2%); the remainder were directed to paediatric ophthalmology (n = 1, 0.3%) or other services (n = 3, 0.8%). This redirection occurred at the point of initial triage, on the basis of the referral information and accompanying imaging submitted by the community optometrist, before the referral entered the macular assessment pathway.

Avoided Hospital Appointments and Management Decisions

Of 327 referrals appropriate for macular assessment, 241 (73.7%, 95% CI 68.7–78.2%) did not require the initial face-to-face diagnostic clinic appointment that would have been mandatory under the preceding pathway and in this sense avoided a hospital appointment. Across the full cohort (n = 327), management outcomes were: 17.1% entered the nAMD pathway (booked directly for treatment-pathway assessment without a separate diagnostic visit), 6.1% were redirected to another sub-specialty, 10.2% were discharged to community care, 15.3% were scheduled for virtual follow-up and 51.2% were booked for face-to-face examination. Clinicians contacted patients with suspected nAMD by telephone and scheduled patients directly into clinics for updated imaging, visual acuity assessment, consent and treatment on the same day. Mean time to treatment was 8.6 days (95% CI 6.0–11.2 days). Of the patients, 91% with nAMD received treatment within 14 days of referral, compared with 40.3% reported in the National Ophthalmology Database (NOD) audit [5].

Diagnostic Categorisation and Concordance

Diagnostic categorisation, reflecting the reason for referral as classified by the referring optometrist, was determined for the 358 referrals with imaging available for assessment. Neovascular (‘wet’) age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) was the most common reason for referral, accounting for 49.7% of cases. This was followed by diabetic macular oedema (8.4%), cystoid macular oedema (8.1%) and retinal vein occlusion (8.1%). Epiretinal membrane accounted for 5.0% of referrals, central serous chorioretinopathy 4.7%, macular hole 3.9%, dry AMD 2.0%, post-operative cystoid macular oedema 1.4% and myopic choroidal neovascularisation 0.3%. Agreement between the reason for referral assigned at the point of referral and the diagnosis recorded after hospital assessment was assessed across 358 paired records. Exact agreement was observed in 196 of 358 cases (54.7%, 95% CI 49.6–59.8%), with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.473, indicating moderate agreement (Landis and Koch). Examination of the discordant cases showed that disagreement was driven predominantly by the safe direction of triage. The single largest category comprised referrals submitted as neovascular AMD that were subsequently confirmed as the non-neovascular (dry) form following hospital assessment, accounting for 69 of 162 disagreements (42.6%). The remaining disagreements reflected lateral reclassification between conditions of comparable clinical urgency. Critically, instances in which a referral submitted as a lower-urgency diagnosis was subsequently found to represent treatable neovascular AMD were rare (6 of 358 paired records, 1.7%), and in every case, the patient had already been directed into the pathway for specialist assessment, such that the correct diagnosis was reached without exclusion from care.

Time to Review

The pathway standard required review within 1 working day of referral. Mean time to review was 0.8 days, and 96.3% of referrals were reviewed within the 1-working-day target.

Discussion

In this real-world evaluation of a community-to-hospital enhanced macular referral pathway, almost nine in ten referrals were appropriate for macular assessment and nearly three quarters of appropriate referrals avoided a hospital appointment. These findings are consistent with prior teleophthalmology initiatives in macular disease, in which rapid hospital review of community-acquired imaging enabled timely decision making and reduced unnecessary clinic attendances [3, 4]. Together with targeted booking for those requiring anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy, this approach prioritises capacity for sight-threatening disease while sustaining access for a growing population. The small proportion of referrals subsequently redirected to another sub-specialty following full virtual review (6.1%), as distinct from redirection at initial triage, illustrates a further strength of this model: review of a complete DICOM OCT volume and colour fundus image set can identify sub-specialty pathology that referral information and a single static image would not have flagged.

The small proportion of incomplete imaging referrals (4.7%) points to a pragmatic quality improvement target. Standardising OCT acquisition protocols and ensuring adequate training will further reduce avoidable inappropriate referrals. Prior literature consistently highlights the importance of optometrist education, feedback loops and clear referral criteria in achieving safe and efficient shared care [4, 6].

The proportion of avoided hospital appointments (73.7% of appropriate referrals) suggests meaningful operational efficiency relative to the previous pathway, in which all of these referrals would have required an initial face-to-face appointment. Asynchronous teleophthalmology review may therefore expand effective specialist capacity and direct the patients to the right pathway at the right time. For patients, fewer hospital visits would be expected to reduce travel and carer burden, with possible environmental co-benefits through decreased travel; however, we did not perform a formal economic or carbon analysis, and these potential benefits remain to be quantified in future work.

The time-to-treatment data are encouraging. Of patients, 91% with nAMD received treatment within 14 days of referral, compared with 40.3% in the NOD audit [5]. This apparent difference should, however, be interpreted with caution and not as a like-for-like comparison: the NOD figure is derived from a large multi-centre national dataset with a different denominator (treatment-naive eyes entering the nAMD pathway) and time period, whereas our figure reflects a small single-centre sub-set selected through this pathway. The comparison is intended to be descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than to demonstrate superiority, and a prospective comparison using matched denominators would be required to substantiate any performance claim.

The moderate concordance between the reason for referral by the community optometrist and the hospital diagnosis (54.7%, κ = 0.473) merits careful interpretation, as it speaks directly to the safety and the purpose of the pathway. A central finding of this evaluation is that the pathway directed patients to an appropriate initial management stream irrespective of whether the provisional referral label precisely matched the eventual diagnosis. The predominant direction of disagreement was towards over-inclusion of potentially sight-threatening disease: the largest single category of discordance comprised referrals submitted as nAMD that were subsequently confirmed as the non-neovascular form. This pattern is the signature of a well-calibrated triage system. A definitive macular diagnosis frequently cannot be established from community-acquired imaging alone; the value of this pathway lies not in replacing in-person diagnosis but in ensuring that each patient is directed to the right place at the right time, avoiding unnecessary hospital appointments and adding value to the optometrist assessment in the community.

The principles of this pathway are potentially transferable to other sub-specialties; for example, the evaluation of possible disc swelling referrals within neuro-ophthalmology, where structured asynchronous imaging review may similarly reduce unnecessary attendances.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-centre retrospective service evaluation without a concurrent randomised or contemporaneous control arm; although the preceding pathway, which mandated a face-to-face appointment for every referral, provides a structural reference point for defining avoided appointments. Second, while we assessed diagnostic concordance between the reason for referral and hospital diagnosis, we did not evaluate diagnostic accuracy against an independent reference standard or capture longer-term clinical outcomes. The pattern of discordance was nonetheless reassuring, erring consistently towards the over-inclusion of potentially treatable disease and supports the safety of the triage model; multi-centre evaluation would further establish its generalisability. Third, ethnicity was undeclared for approximately 24% of the cohort, which constrains assessment of equity of access and the representativeness of the population served. Fourth, 45° colour imaging constrains detection of peripheral pathology, and ultrawide-field imaging may identify lesions not visible on macula-centred photographs. Finally, we did not perform a formal health–economic or environmental analysis; the pathway was delivered with grant funding that covered the community imaging fee, and the cost-effectiveness of a funded model, in which both community and hospital imaging would require resourcing, remains to be established.

A related consideration is whether an imaging-dependent pathway favours larger optometry practices and chains over smaller independent practices. Our onboarding experience to date does not support this concern. Of the 74 General Ophthalmic Services practices in our catchment, identified from the most recent data available to us (January 2025), 43 have onboarded, 10 are in progress of onboarding and 7 have not yet engaged; a further 10 hold no national contract permitting participation. Among the eligible practices, only 3 currently lack a compatible imaging device and 1 does not have an OCT device, identifying a specific and addressable equipment gap rather than a structural disadvantage for smaller practices; uptake by practice size will nonetheless continue to be monitored as the pathway scales.

Furthermore, we acknowledge that housebound patients seen through domiciliary optometry, who represent a meaningful proportion of those with nAMD, cannot generate the imaging on which the pathway depends. Importantly, this constraint is not introduced by the pathway: these patients were equally unable to provide imaging under the previous standard of care and continue to be managed through a conventional face-to-face route. By diverting the majority of imaging-complete referrals away from the mandatory first-visit clinic, the pathway releases face-to-face capacity that remains available to patients who cannot be assessed remotely, such that this group is arguably an indirect beneficiary rather than being further disadvantaged.

Prospective, multi-centre evaluation incorporating diagnostic-accuracy end points, matched comparators, health–economic analysis and patient-reported outcomes is warranted before wider adoption.

Conclusions

A teleophthalmology-enabled enhanced macular imaging referral pathway integrating community optometry with hospital virtual review achieved high referral appropriateness and avoided a substantial proportion of face-to-face appointments, supporting rapid access to treatment for nAMD and enhancing sub-specialty triage. The study’s methodological approach may offer a transferable framework for shared-care clinical models, easing hospital demand and improving access without compromising the quality of patient’s care. Wider adoption, with standardised imaging protocols, timely clinician feedback to community optometrists and robust data governance, could help NHS hospital eye services meet rising demand while improving patient experience and increasing sustainability.
