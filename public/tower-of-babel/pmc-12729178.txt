Absorbable versus non-absorbable sutures in Achilles tendon rupture repair: a retrospective cohort study evaluating medium- to long-term outcomes via musculoskeletal ultrasound and functional metrics
Authors: Dong-liang Chen, Yu-jie Liu, Liang-xiao Zheng, Jie Sun, Peng Liu, Jin-wei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSuture material selection probably impacts outcomes after surgical repair of acute Achilles tendon rupture. Absorbable and non-absorbable sutures possess distinct mechanical and biocompatibility properties. This study compared medium-to-long-term functional recovery and ultrasonographic healing characteristics between patients receiving absorbable versus non-absorbable sutures.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study screened 210 patients undergoing primary acute Achilles tendon repair (2013–2024). Propensity score matching generated comparable cohorts of 50 patients each: the absorbable group received Interfascicular Repair, and the non-absorbable group received modified Kessler Repair. Identical postoperative rehabilitation protocols were followed. Primary outcomes assessed at 6 and 12 months included: ultrasonographic parameters (Achilles tendon thickness ATT, mm), margin echotexture (ME) at the repair site, blood flow signals (BFS) at the repair site, active gliding function (AGF), passive gliding function (PGF) and functional assessment (VISA-A score).ResultsAt 6 months, statistically significant differences were observed between the absorbable and non-absorbable suture groups across ATT; AGF; BFS. The absorbable suture group exhibited significantly lower ATT compared to the non-absorbable group (7.07 ± 0.41 mm vs. 7.81 ± 0.52 mm; p < 0.001). The absorbable suture group demonstrated greater AGF (1.86 ± 0.35 vs. 1.06 ± 0.31; p < 0.001). VISA-A score were also significantly higher in the absorbable suture group (81.82 ± 7.48 vs. 74.44 ± 6.71; p < 0.001). The BFS was significantly reduced in the non-absorbable suture group (1.28 ± 0.88 vs. 2.32 ± 0.82; p < 0.001). At the twelve-month follow-up, no significant intergroup differences were detected in ATT (p = 0.126), AGF (p = 0.650), VISA-A score (p = 0.063), and BFS (p = 0.569). At 6 months, ATT correlated inversely with AGF (r = − 0.487, p < 0.001) and VISA-A score (r = − 0.311, p = 0.002). Complication (absorbable: 1 re-rupture; non-absorbable: 2 inflammatory reactions).ConclusionAlthough functional and structural outcomes converge by 12 months, absorbable sutures confer early advantages in tendon morphology and gliding function during the initial healing phase. Suture material selection should be individualized, considering patient-specific factors such as anticipated rehabilitation adherence and activity demands.

Introduction

Surgical repair remains the standard for acute Achilles tendon rupture (ATR), offering superior restoration of anatomical continuity, reduced re-rupture rates, and improved functional outcomes compared to non-operative management [1–5]. Despite advances in surgical technique, the optimal suture material remains remains a subject of debate. Clinical practice varies between non-absorbable sutures, prized for their durable tensile strength, and absorbable sutures, which theoretically facilitate a more biologically integrated healing process through controlled degradation [6–8].

Non-absorbable braided sutures (e.g., Ethibond®) provide immediate and sustained mechanical stability during the critical early healing phase [9]. However, their permanent presence may trigger chronic foreign-body reactions, peritendinous adhesions, granuloma formation, and infection [10–12]. Conversely, Absorbable sutures (e.g., Polysorb®) elicit a transient inflammatory response during the degradation process. This phenomenon, which was historically regarded as potentially adverse, is now increasingly acknowledged as an essential impetus for neovascularization, macrophage—mediated debris clearance, and collagen realignment. [13–15]. Preclinical models suggest this dynamic milieu enhances extracellular matrix organization and reduces fibrotic scarring [16], yet robust clinical validation is lacking. Critically, no prior study has mapped the temporal evolution of tendon microstructure—quantified by musculoskeletal ultrasound (MSK-US)—concurrent with functional recovery across the healing continuum. Existing literature focuses primarily on re-rupture rates or endpoint functional scores, neglecting the intermediate biomechanical and morphological transitions that determine long-term tendon health [17, 18].

We hypothesized that: Absorbable sutures yield non-inferior long-term functional outcomes while promoting more favorable early-stage tendon healing dynamics, as evidenced by reduced tendon thickening, improved gliding function, on MSK-US. To test this, we conducted a retrospective cohort study with propensity score matching to compare structural and functional outcomes between absorbable and non-absorbable suture groups at 6 and 12 months post-repair.

Methods

Study design and participant selection

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (Approval No.: KYLL-KS-2025181). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. From 560 consecutive patients treated for acute ATR between July 2013 and June 2024, 210 met inclusion criteria: Age 18–60 years; Acute closed rupture (injury-to-surgery interval ≤ 14 days); Diagnosis confirmed by positive Thompson test and musculoskeletal ultrasound or MRI; Absence of diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, or metabolic diseases; No history of recent fluoroquinolone antibiotic use, which is a known risk factor for tendon disorders [19]. Complete 6-month and 12-month follow-up including musculoskeletal ultrasound and functional scores. Exclusion criteria comprised open ruptures, prior Achilles injury/surgery, concomitant lower limb injuries, chronic corticosteroid/immunosuppressant use, and > 20% missing follow-up data (Fig. 1).

Surgical technique and suture allocation

All procedures were performed by a single senior orthopedic team using standardized protocols. Patients were allocated to suture type based on institutional registration sequence: Absorbable Group (n = 60): Fascicular repair with 3–0 Polysorb® (polyglactin 910/bioresorbable copolymer) sutures, augmented by a continuous 4–0 absorbable epitendinous running suture. Non-Absorbable Group (n = 150): Modified Kessler core repair with 3–0 Ethibond® (polyester) sutures, reinforced with a 4–0 non-absorbable epitendinous suture.

All repairs were completed via open approach with minimal dissection and secure knot tying (square knots with 5 throws).

Postoperative rehabilitation protocol

All patients underwent identical, protocol-driven rehabilitation: Weeks 0–4: Short-leg cast in 20 plantarflexion, non-weight-bearing. Weeks 5–8: Transition to walking boot with heel lift, progressive weight-bearing, active ankle dorsiflexion within pain limits. Weeks 9–12: Gradual removal of heel lift, initiation of eccentric loading (heel drops), proprioceptive training. Beyond 12 weeks: Return to sport-specific conditioning under physiotherapist supervision. No patient received adjunctive therapies (e.g., PRP, shockwave).

Propensity score matching (PSM)

To minimize selection bias arising from the non-randomized allocation and differing group sizes, PSM was employed to create comparable groups for a more robust comparison, reducing potential confounding factors. We performed 1:1 PSM using the nearest-neighbor method with a caliper of 0.2 Standard Deviation(SD). The propensity score was estimated based on age, sex, and Body Mass Index (BMI). Final analysis included 100 patients (50 per group). Power calculation (G*Power 3.1) indicated that n = 50 per group provided 80% power (α = 0.05, β = 0.2) to detect a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of ≥ 8 points in VISA-A score [18].

Musculoskeletal ultrasound assessment

Imaging protocol

Scans were performed using two high-resolution systems: Philips iU Elite (L12-5 transducer, Germany); Toshiba Aplio 500 (14L5 transducer, Japan).

Two experienced MSK sonographers (≥ 5 years’ experience), blinded to suture type and clinical outcomes, performed all scans. Three measurements per parameter were acquired at the maximal cross-sectional area of the repair site and averaged. All scans followed standardized protocols with probe pressure minimized to avoid tissue compression artifacts.

Complication screening

Defined as: re-rupture: Focal fiber discontinuity with gap widening > 5 mm during dorsiflexion (complete) or > 2 mm with asynchronous motion (partial). Intratendinous fluid: Hypoechoic areas within tendon substance. Infection/inflammation: Clinical signs (e.g., erythema, warmth, purulent discharge, elevated serum inflammatory markers) combined with sonographic findings such as loss of fibrillar pattern, peritendinous edema, hypervascularity, sinus tract formation. Granuloma: Persistent hyperechoic linear structures with surrounding acoustic shadowing. Local irritation and scar formation were also assessed clinically based on patient reports of tenderness, palpable nodules, and adhesion-related stiffness.

Functional outcome assessment

The Victoria Institute of Sport Assessment–Achilles (VISA-A) questionnaire, a validated, disease-specific tool with established reliability and responsiveness [18], was administered at 6 and 12 months. Scores range from 0 (severe symptoms) to 100 (asymptomatic).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 28.0 (IBM Corp.). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD; categorical variables as frequency (%). Normality was assessed via Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons used independent t-tests (parametric) or Mann–Whitney U tests (non-parametric). Within-group changes over time were evaluated using paired t-tests. Correlations were assessed via Pearson’s r. Significance was set at p < 0.05. PSM balance was confirmed via standardized mean difference and variance ratio tests.

Complications

Absorbable group: 1 partial re-rupture (2%) at 9 weeks, managed conservatively with extended immobilization and successful recovery. Non-absorbable group: 2 cases (4%) of chronic granulomatous inflammation with persistent hyperechoic suture tracts and local tenderness, requiring suture excision at 6 months. No infections occurred. No other complications (e.g., wound dehiscence, deep vein thrombosis) were reported.

Discussion

Our longitudinal MSK-US monitoring revealed that the non-absorbable suture group exhibited significantly greater tendon thickness and reduced gliding function alongside lower VISA-A scores compared to the absorbable suture group at the 6-month postoperative mark. By 12 months postoperatively, the disparity in tendon thickness resolved, and no statistically significant difference in VISA-A scores persisted between the groups. These findings indicate that both non-absorbable and absorbable suture materials can achieve effective long-term functional restoration of ruptured Achilles tendons, as reflected in equivalent clinical outcomes at 12 months.

Within the initial 6-month healing phase, tendons repaired with non-absorbable sutures demonstrated pronounced fibrotic scarring and diminished gliding function relative to those repaired with absorbable materials. This observation is supported by studies suggesting that persistent foreign material can alter the tendon’s mechanical environment and healing response [17, 20]. While this scar tissue confers some degree of mechanical stability, it fundamentally alters the tendon’s native mechanical integrity. The impaired gliding function observed concomitantly with intratendinous scar formation likely adversely impacts force transmission during ankle motion [21, 22]. Furthermore, the altered tendon behavior during lengthening and shortening cycles suggests reduced elasticity. Such modulation of tendon biomechanics may predispose to an elevated risk of re-rupture, particularly if rehabilitation is overly aggressive [2, 23]. Fibrotic alterations within the tendon extracellular matrix post-injury are implicated in the subsequent development of chronic, degenerative tendinopathy [24, 25]. Consequently, the persistent presence of non-absorbable suture material may contribute to sustained stress-shielding, potentially inhibiting intrinsic tendon remodeling and favoring the deposition of scar collagen over the regeneration of normal tendon architecture [26, 27].

Non-absorbable sutures are favored by some surgeons due to their superior initial security and long-term maintenance of tensile strength. However, their permanent retention carries the inherent risk of provoking chronic inflammation, adhesion formation, suture granulomas, or other persistent foreign body reactions [28, 29]. Some reports highlight these complications, including cases of granulomatous reaction following Achilles repair using braided non-absorbable suture with infection potentially exacerbating the severity of the foreign body response [30, 31]. Studies emphasize the criticality of suture material choice, noting that both polyester (e.g., Ethibond®) and ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) braided sutures can incite significant granulomatous reactions [32]. This evidence warrants judicious use of braided non-absorbable sutures in Achilles tendon repair. Some experts consequently advocate for the routine use of absorbable, monofilament sutures, which are buried within the tendon and undergo absorption within 3–6 months, thereby mitigating the risk of long-term foreign body reactions, albeit with potential concerns regarding retained strength during the critical healing phase [33, 34].

In our cohort, non-absorbable sutures were identifiable as hyperechoic linear structures on ultrasound at 6 months, exhibiting a low rupture risk profile. Conversely, absorbable sutures, undergoing gradual absorption, were associated with transient local hypoechoic zones (potentially representing degradation byproducts or edema) and relatively increased vascularity during the resorption period. A critical consideration is the potential temporal mismatch between the rate of suture degradation and the progression of tendon healing. If degradation outpaces sufficient tissue maturation, a transient reduction in repair site strength could occur, necessitating vigilance for early rupture risk [35–37]. For instance, the tensile strength of polydioxanone sutures (PDS II) diminishes by approximately 50% between 4 and 6 weeks post-implantation [36], coinciding with rising tendon stresses as rehabilitation progresses. During this period, newly deposited collagen lacks adequate cross-linking and is susceptible to micro-tears. Rehabilitation strategies must therefore meticulously balance stress application—avoiding excessive loads that risk failure while providing sufficient stimulus to promote adaptive remodeling and functional recovery [32, 33]. This underscores the importance of patient compliance with rehabilitation protocols, as highlighted in studies evaluating tendon healing and return to play [38, 39]. Consequently, we suggest absorbable sutures may be preferable for patients with high compliance who can adhere strictly to protected weight-bearing protocols (ideally supervised by specialized physiotherapists to optimize stress modulation) and those with known sensitivity to foreign materials. Non-absorbable sutures may offer advantages for patients requiring early aggressive mobilization or for high-demand scenarios, such as complete ruptures in elite athletes.

Although significant differences in scar formation and suture zone vascularity were observed at 6 months depending on suture material, these differences did not translate into divergent VISA-A scores or gliding function at the 1-year endpoint. This aligns with previous studies reporting similar long-term functional outcomes between absorbable and non-absorbable sutures [1, 6, 29]. It is important to note that the Achilles tendon undergoes continuous remodeling beyond one year. Musculoskeletal ultrasound remains a valuable tool for dynamically monitoring this process and guiding rehabilitation. Nevertheless, extended follow-up periods are warranted to accurately correlate evolving sonographic findings with definitive clinical outcomes over time.

The clinical relevance of this study lies in providing evidence-based guidance for surgeons regarding suture material selection in ATR repair. Our findings suggest that while long-term outcomes are similar, absorbable sutures may offer mid-term advantages in tendon morphology and function, potentially reducing adhesion-related issues. This information can aid in individualizing treatment plans based on patient factors like rehabilitation adherence and activity level.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The non-randomized, retrospective design inherently introduces potential selection bias, and the absence of blinding in outcome assessment raises the possibility of detection bias. Furthermore, the study evaluated only a single type of absorbable (Polysorb®) and non-absorbable (Ethibond®) braided suture material; findings may not generalize to other suture types or configurations (e.g., monofilament absorbable, newer high-strength tapes). Despite this limitation regarding material scope, our results provide clinically relevant insights for orthopedic surgeons. Finally, the reliance solely on musculoskeletal ultrasound for assessing tendon integrity, without supplementary diagnostic modalities (e.g., advanced MRI, or biomechanical testing), may weaken the comprehensiveness of the evidence regarding structural healing.

Conclusion

Both absorbable and non-absorbable sutures deliver comparable long-term functional outcomes in Achilles tendon repair. The choice of suture should be tailored to the patient’s individual condition and surgical requirements.
