1  Introduction

Monomial ideals offer a rich setting for exploring the interplay between algebraic, combinatorial and topological structures. One such foundational interconnection was described in Taylor’s thesis
[
18
]
, which was further extended and developed in
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
12
]
, and
[
15
]
, to name a few. These works collectively highlight how algebraic invariants of monomial ideals, such as Betti numbers and minimal free resolutions, can be studied through combinatorial and topological constructions like simplicial and cellular complexes.

The Taylor resolution of any monomial ideal
I
, minimally generated by
q
monomials, is a free resolution of
I
constructed using a
(q-1)
-simplex. This resolution is obtained by
“homogenizing”
the simplicial chain complex of the
(q-1)
-simplex. This simplex, with vertices labeled by the monomial generators and faces labeled by the least common multiples of those vertices, is called the Taylor complex of
I
, denoted by Taylor
(I)
. Bayer and Sturmfels
[
2
]
extended the idea of constructing a free resolution of a monomial ideal from the
(q-1)
-simplex to more general simplicial complexes on
q
vertices, and provided a criterion for when such a homogenized chain complex would be a resolution of the ideal. If the homogenized simplicial chain complex of a simplicial complex
\Delta
results in a free resolution of
I
, then
\Delta
is said to “support” a free resolution of
I
.

The Taylor complex
\text{Taylor}(I)
on
q
vertices works for any monomial ideal generated by
q
monomials and always supports a free resolution of such ideals. However, this simplex is often excessively large, resulting in a resolution for
I
that is far from minimal. Consequently, the upper bounds on the Betti numbers provided by the Taylor resolution, i.e.,
\beta_{j}(I)\leq\binom{q}{j+1}
, are often far from sharp.
This issue becomes more pronounced when considering the Taylor resolution of powers of
I
. For example, if
q\geq 2
, the Taylor resolution of
I^{2}
is never minimal due to the divisibility relations shown in
Lemma

3.6
. In
[
4
]
, the authors introduced a simplicial complex
\mathbb{L}_{q}^{2}
, a
“prototype”
for the Taylor complex of
I^{2}
in the case where
I
is generated by
q
square-free monomials. This complex is a proper subcomplex of a
\binom{q}{2}
-simplex. They showed that the resolution of
I^{2}
supported on
\mathbb{L}_{q}^{2}
is closer to a minimal resolution than the Taylor resolution. This not only provides sharper bounds on the Betti numbers but also is minimal for many ideals.

However, there is a limitation to the complex
\mathbb{L}_{q}^{2}
as it works only for square-free monomial ideals. Indeed,
Example

3.1
provides an example where
\mathbb{L}_{q}^{2}
does not support a free resolution for the quadratic power of a monomial ideal. In this paper, we address this gap by introducing a new simplicial complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
and a subcomplex
\mathbb{M}^{2}(I)
, and show that both support the free resolution of
I^{2}
for
any
monomial ideal generated by
q
monomials (
Theorem

3.7
). This complex is also a proper subcomplex of
\Taylor(I^{2})
, substantially improving the upper bounds on the Betti numbers of
I^{2}
compared to those provided by
\Taylor(I^{2})
.

We also provide further evidence to the generally acknowledged fact that polarization, a widely used technique in the study of monomial ideals, may be insufficient when studying resolutions of powers of monomial ideals (see
Remark

3.11
). Polarizing powers of ideals can lead to the loss of several homological invariants, such as projective dimension, regularity, and Betti numbers.

To make this paper self-contained, we have included a preliminaries section that recalls all the basic notions and concepts used here. In
Proposition

3.3
, we show that
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
is a simplicial tree, which then leads to the proof that
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
and
\mathbb{M}^{2}(I)
support free resolutions of
I^{2}
when
I
is an ideal generated by
q
monomials.
Corollary

3.8
provides a sharp upper bound on the projective dimension of
I^{2}
.
Corollary

3.12
establishes sharper general bounds on the Betti numbers of
I^{2}
, while
Corollary

3.13
provides even tighter bounds by using information about the generators of
I^{2}
.

In
Section

4
, we introduce the permutation ideal
\mathcal{T}_{q}
, which is generated by
q
monomials. These ideals turn out to be useful in studying the quadratic powers of monomial ideals.
Theorem

4.8
, our second main theorem, shows that the Scarf complex of the square of the permutation ideal coincides with
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
. On the other hand,
Corollary

4.10
shows that the Betti vector of the permutation ideal
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
serves as an upper bound for the Betti vector of the quadratic power of any monomial ideal.

We begin by letting
K

S=K[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]

A simplicial complex is a fundamental structure in algebraic topology and combinatorial mathematics. It also plays a vital role in this paper. We introduce the concept of simplicial complexes and several associated terms.

Definition 2.1
.

Let

(a)

A member of
\Delta

face
.

(b)

The

(c)

The

\dim(F)=|F|-1

(d)

The
dimension
of
\Delta
is the largest dimension of any of its faces.

(e)

\Delta

(f)

The

of a

-dimensional simplicial complex
\Delta
is a
(d+1)-\text{tuple}
of integers, where

represents the number of
i

\Delta
.

A simplicial complex can be uniquely determined by its facets. We denote a simplicial complex
\Delta

as

\Delta=\langle F_{1},\ldots,F_{q}\rangle.

Free resolutions are fundamental tools for studying algebraic structures in commutative and homological algebra. Their history traces back to David Hilbert in the early twentieth century. In recent decades, modern approaches to studying free resolutions have become more combinatorial and geometric, e.g., see
[
6
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
17
]
. In this paper, we aim to explore the free resolution of the second power
I^{2}
for a monomial ideal
I
through a topological approach.
We now define free resolutions and introduce some related concepts.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
I
be a monomial ideal in the polynomial ring
S=K[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
, minimally generated by
q
monomials.
A
free resolution
of
I
is an exact sequence of the form

(2.1)

\cdots\to F_{i}\to F_{i-1}\to\cdots\to F_{1}\to F_{0}\to I\to 0,

where
i\in\mathbb{N}
and each
S
-module
F_{i}
is isomorphic to a finite direct sum of copies of
S
. A free resolution of
I
is called a
minimal free resolution
if the rank of each
F_{i}
, denoted by
\beta_{i}
, is the smallest possible among all free resolutions of
I
at the
i^{\text{th}}
position. That is, it is of the form

(2.2)

0\to S^{\beta_{p}}\to S^{\beta_{p-1}}\to\cdots\to S^{\beta_{1}}\to S^{\beta_{0}}\to I\to 0.

The minimal free resolution of an ideal is unique up to isomorphism. The integer
\beta_{i}
in (
2.2
), for each
i\in[p]=\{1,\ldots,p\}
, is called the
i^{\text{th}}
Betti number
of
I
.
The length
p
of the minimal free resolution of
I
is called the
projective dimension
of
I
over
S
and is denoted by
\pd_{S}(I)
.

In her thesis
[
18
]
, Taylor showed that a free resolution of a monomial ideal
I
in
S
with
q
minimal generators can be constructed using a
(q-1)
-simplex. This simplex is known as the Taylor complex of
I
, denoted
\Taylor(I)
, and it supports a free resolution of
I
. In this complex, the vertices are labeled with the
q
minimal monomial generators of
I
, and each face is labeled with the least common multiple of the monomial labels of its vertices. Further study in this area by Bayer, Peeva, and Sturmfels
[
1
,
2
]
showed that other simplicial complexes with
q
vertices and such labeled faces can also be used to derive a free resolution of
I
by homogenizing their simplicial chain complexes. For a detailed illustration of how homogenization works in the case of a
2
-simplex, see
[
3
, Example 2.1]
. The criteria for a simplicial complex to support a free resolution of a monomial ideal are described in
Theorem

2.4
below. To state this theorem, we first introduce some necessary notation.

For a monomial ideal
I
minimally generated by
m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}
and
A\subseteq[q]=\{1,\ldots,q\}
we define

m_{A}=\lcm(m_{a}\colon a\in A)

and we let
\LCM(I)
be the
lcm lattice
of
I
, which is the set

\LCM(I)=\{m_{A}\colon A\subset[q]\}

partially ordered by division.

If
\Delta
is a simplicial complex on
q
vertices, we label its
0
-dimensional faces with minimal monomial generators of
I
and assign any other face
\tau\in\Delta
the label

m_{\tau}=\lcm\{m_{i}\colon m_{i}\in\tau\}.

For any monomial
\mathbf{m}
, we use the notation
\Delta_{\mathbf{m}}
to denote the induced subcomplex of
\Delta
consisting of those faces whose monomial labels divide
\mathbf{m}
, that is,

\Delta_{\mathbf{m}}=\{\tau\in\Delta\colon m_{\tau}\mid\mathbf{m}\}.

Theorem 2.4

(
Criterion for supporting a free resolution).

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex on
q
vertices, and let
I
be an ideal minimally generated by
q
monomials in a polynomial ring over a field. Label the vertices of
\Delta
with the
q
monomial generators of
I
and label each face
\tau\in\Delta
with the monomial
m_{\tau}
. Then

(1)

\Delta
supports a free resolution of
I
if and only if for every monomial
\mathbf{m}
in
\LCM(I)
,
\Delta_{\mathbf{m}}
is empty or acyclic (see
[
1
]
);

(2)

if
\Delta
is a simplicial tree, then
\Delta
supports a free resolution of
I
if and only if for every monomial
\mathbf{m}
in
\LCM(I)
,
\Delta_{\mathbf{m}}
is empty or connected (see
[
8
]
);

(3)

the free resolution of
I
supported by
\Delta
is minimal if and only if for every pair of faces
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\Delta
with
\tau_{1}\subsetneq\tau_{2}
, we have
m_{\tau_{1}}\neq m_{\tau_{2}}
(see
[
1
]
).

Remark 2.5
.

Theorem

2.4
establishes that the
f
-vector of a simplicial complex
\Delta
, which supports a resolution of a monomial ideal
I
, serves as an upper bound for the Betti vector of
I
. More precisely, for each
i\leq d=\dim(\Delta)
, we have

\beta_{i}(I)\leq f_{i},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\mathbf{f}(\Delta)=(f_{0},\dots,f_{d}).

Furthermore, if
\Delta
provides a minimal free resolution of
I
, then equality holds in the above relation.

Theorem

2.4
says that the minimality of a free resolution of an ideal depends on the condition that no two nested subfaces share the same monomial label. If we extend this to an extreme condition where no two faces share the same monomial label, then this leads to the following definition.

Definition 2.6

(
Scarf Complex).

Let
I
be a monomial ideal minimally generated by the
q
monomials
m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{q}
. The
Scarf complex
of
I
, denoted by Scarf
(I)
, is the simplicial complex

\Scarf(I)=\left\{\tau\in\Taylor(I)\mid m_{\tau}\neq m_{\tau_{1}}\text{ for all }\tau_{1}\in\Taylor(I),\tau_{1}\not=\tau\right\}.

The ideal
I
is called a
Scarf ideal
if its Scarf complex,
\Scarf(I)
, supports a free resolution of
I
. The faces of the Scarf complex of
I
are referred to as the
Scarf faces
of
I
. A free resolution of
I
supported on its Scarf complex is called a
Scarf resolution
, which, in fact, forms a minimal free resolution of
I
(see
[
13
]
for details). The following lemma from
[
7
]
characterizes the Scarf faces of a monomial ideal and we will use it later in this paper.

Lemma 2.7
.

(
[
7
, Lemma 2.2]
)

Let
I
be a monomial ideal and let
\gamma\in\Taylor(I)
. Then
\gamma\in\Scarf(I)
if and only if both of the following statements hold:

(1)

m_{\gamma}\neq m_{\gamma\setminus\{v\}}
for all vertices
v\in\gamma
;

(2)

m_{\gamma\cup\{v\}}\neq m_{\gamma}
for all vertices
v\in\Taylor(I)\setminus\gamma
.

We want to show that the simplicial complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
supports a resolution of
I^{2}
for any monomial ideal generated by

\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}

Proposition 3.3
.

For any
q\geq 1
, the simplicial complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
is a simplicial tree.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{M}^{2}_{q}
is a connected collection of facets all joined in the same face, every facet of
\mathbb{M}^{2}_{q}
is a leaf, and any other facet is its joint. Every subcollection has the same form, so the result follows immediately.
∎

If
I

generates

. However, this set may not be the minimal generating set of
I^{2}
, and

may contain redundant generators as shown in the next example.

Example 3.4
.

We consider the two ideals below of
S=K[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}]

(a)

If

of

divides
{m_{2}}^{2}
. This shows that
{m_{2}}^{2}
is a redundant generator of

.

(b)

Similarly, we see that for the ideal

J=(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=({x_{1}}^{2}{x_{2}}^{4}{x_{3}}^{3}x_{4},{x_{1}}^{3}{x_{2}}^{3}{x_{3}}^{2}{x_{4}}^{2},{x_{1}}^{5}{x_{2}}^{2}{x_{3}}^{3}{x_{4}}^{3}),

In such cases where the square of an ideal has redundant generators, the complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
will provide an unnecessarily large free resolution, coming from the extra vertices corresponding to the redundant generators. In order to avoid this, we now define a labeled induced subcomplex of
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
by deleting some vertices that correspond to these redundant generators.

Definition 3.5
.

For an ideal
I
minimally generated by the monomials
m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}
, we define
\mathbb{M}^{2}(I)
to be a labeled induced subcomplex of
\mathbb{M}^{2}_{q}
formed by the following three rules.

(1)

Label each vertex
\ell_{i,j}
of
\mathbb{M}^{2}_{q}
with the monomial
m_{i}m_{j}
.

(2)

For any indices
i,j,u,v\in[q]
with
\{i,j\}\neq\{u,v\}
, if we have
m_{i}m_{j}\mid m_{u}m_{v}
, then:

•

if
m_{i}m_{j}=m_{u}m_{v}
and
i=\min\{i,j,u,v\}
, then delete the vertex
\ell_{i,j}
;

•

if
m_{i}m_{j}\neq m_{u}m_{v}
, then delete the vertex
\ell_{u,v}
.

(3)

Label each of the remaining faces with the least common multiple of the labels of its vertices.

The remaining labeled subcomplex of
\mathbb{M}^{2}_{q}
is called
\mathbb{M}^{2}(I)
, and is a subcomplex of
\Taylor(I^{2})
.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
I=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{q})
is a monomial ideal minimally generated by
q
monomials
m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}
in
S=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, then for any
i,j\in[q]
we have the relation
m_{i}m_{j}
divides
\mathrm{lcm}({m_{i}}^{2},{m_{j}}^{2})
.

Proof.

For each
i
, let us write

m_{i}={x_{1}}^{a_{i1}}{x_{2}}^{a_{i2}}\cdots{x_{n}}^{a_{in}},

where
a_{ik}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, for all
i\in[q]
and
k\in[n]
. With this notation, we have

m_{i}m_{j}={x_{1}}^{a_{i1}+a_{j1}}{x_{2}}^{a_{i2}+a_{j2}}\cdots{x_{n}}^{a_{in}+a_{jn}},

m_{i}^{2}={x_{1}}^{2a_{i1}}{x_{2}}^{2a_{i2}}\cdots{x_{n}}^{2a_{in}},

m_{j}^{2}={x_{1}}^{2a_{j1}}{x_{2}}^{2a_{j2}}\cdots{x_{n}}^{2a_{jn}},

and

\mathrm{lcm}({m_{i}}^{2},{m_{j}}^{2})={x_{1}}^{2\max\{a_{i1},a_{j1}\}}{x_{2}}^{2\max\{a_{i2},a_{j2}\}}\cdots{x_{n}}^{2\max\{a_{in},a_{jn}\}}.

Now observe that for any
i,j\in[q]
and
k\in[n]
,

a_{ik}+a_{jk}\leq 2\max\{a_{ik},a_{jk}\}.

Thus,
m_{i}m_{j}\mid\mathrm{lcm}({m_{i}}^{2},{m_{j}}^{2})
.

∎

Theorem 3.7

(
Main Theorem 1).

Let
I\subset S
be a monomial ideal minimally generated by
q
monomials
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{q}
. We have the following:

(a)

\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
supports a free resolution of
I^{2}
;

(b)

\mathbb{M}^{2}(I)
supports a free resolution of
I^{2}
.

Proof.

(a)
By
Proposition

3.3
,
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
is a simplicial tree. According to

2.4
, to show that
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
supports a free resolution of

, we must show that
(\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2})_{\mathbf{m}}
is either empty or connected for each monomial
\mathbf{m}\in\LCM(I^{2})
, where
(\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2})_{\mathbf{m}}
is the subcomplex of
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
induced on the set
V_{\mathbf{m}}=\{\ell_{i,j}\in V:m_{i}m_{j}\mid\mathbf{m}\}
.

Now observe that if for some

we have
{m_{i}}^{2},{m_{j}}^{2}\mid\mathbf{m}
, then by
Lemma

3.6

m_{i}m_{j}\mid\mathbf{m}
, and so every time

\ell_{i,j}\in(\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2})_{\mathbf{m}}
. Moreover, if for

and

, implying that

. Therefore, once again, the edge connecting

is in

(b) The simplicial complex

denote the set of vertices in
\mathbb{M}^{2}(I)
. The proof of this part follows along the same lines as that of part (a) with the following modifications:

for any

4  Permutation ideals and quadratic powers

Let
q
be a positive integer, and let
S_{q}
denote the group of all permutations of the set
[q]=\{1,2,\dots,q\}
. For any permutation
\sigma=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{q}\in S_{q}
, define
\sigma(j)=i_{j}
. That is,
\sigma(j)
is the element in the
j^{th}
position of the permutation
\sigma
, reading from left to right. For instance, if
q=5
and
\sigma=35412
, we have

\sigma(1)=3,\quad\sigma(2)=5,\quad\sigma(3)=4,\quad\sigma(4)=1,\quad\sigma(5)=2.

In this section, we introduce a special class of monomial ideals, called permutation ideals. A permutation ideal, denoted as
\mathcal{T}_{q}
, is given by
q
generators. These ideals are defined to play a similar role for general monomial ideals as the extremal ideals, see
[
5
]
,
[
7
]
, play in the study of powers of square-free monomial ideals. Permutation ideals are constructed such that for any monomial ideal
I
minimally generated by
q
monomials in the polynomial ring
S=K[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
, we have

\beta(I^{2})\leq\beta({{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}}).

They are also important because the simplicial complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
supports a minimal resolution of the square of
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
. In fact, our main theorem of this section proves that
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
is the Scarf complex of
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
(
Theorem

4.8
).

Definition 4.1

(
Permutation Ideal).

Let
q
be a positive integer, and let
S_{q}
denote the symmetric group of permutations of the set
[q]
. For each permutation
\sigma\in S_{q}
, associate a variable
x_{\sigma}
, and consider the polynomial ring
S_{\mathcal{T}}=K[x_{\sigma}:\sigma\in S_{q}]
over a field
K
.
For each
i\in[q]
, define a monomial
\tau_{i}
in
S_{\mathcal{T}}
as

\tau_{i}=\prod\limits_{\sigma\in S_{q}}{x_{\sigma}}^{\sigma(i)},

and define the permutation ideal

\mathcal{T}_{q}=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{q}).

Note that
S_{\mathcal{T}}
is a polynomial ring in
q!
variables over
K
. For each
i\in[q]
,

\deg(\tau_{i})=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{q}}\sigma(i)=(q-1)!(1+2+3+\cdots+q)=\frac{(q+1)!}{2}.

.

, we have

. We will use

to achieve this goal. We first need to show that the ideal

Let
\sigma\in S_{q}
and

(4.1)

{x_{\sigma}}^{2q}\mid\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\iff i=j\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma(i)=q.

and

{x_{\sigma}}^{2q}\nmid\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{x_{\sigma}}^{2q-1}\mid\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\iff\{\sigma(i),\sigma(j)\}=\{q,q-1\}.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{q}
be the permutation ideal in
S_{\mathcal{T}}
. Then

(a)

\mathcal{T}_{q}
is minimally generated by
\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{q}\}

(b)

{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
is minimally generated by
\{\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\colon 1\leq i\leq j\leq q\}
. In particular,

|\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2})|=\binom{q}{2}+q=|V(\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2})|.

Proof.

The statement in (a) follows from the fact that for any distinct
i,j\in[q]
, we can choose a permutation
\sigma\in S_{q}
such that
\sigma(i)=q
and
\sigma(j)=q-1
, which implies that
\tau_{i}\nmid\tau_{j}
. Therefore, no generator
\tau_{k}
divides any other, and the set
\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{q}\}
is indeed the minimal generating set of
\mathcal{T}_{q}
.

To see (b), observe that the set

G=\{\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\colon 1\leq i\leq j\leq q\}

is a generating set of
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
. We need to show that there are no redundant generators of
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
in

. Suppose, on the contrary, that there exist two distinct (multi)subsets
\{i_{1},j_{1}\}
and
\{i_{2},j_{2}\}
of

such that

(4.3)

\tau_{i_{1}}\tau_{j_{1}}\mid\tau_{i_{2}}\tau_{j_{2}}.

Case 1
:
If
i_{1}\neq j_{1}
, pick
\sigma\in S_{q}
such that

\sigma(i_{1})=q
and
\sigma(j_{1})=q-1
. Then by (

) and (
4.3
)

{x_{\sigma}}^{2q-1}\mid\tau_{i_{1}}\tau_{j_{1}}\mid\tau_{i_{2}}\tau_{j_{2}}.

By (

), we then have either
\{i_{1},j_{1}\}=\{i_{2},j_{2}\}
, in which case we are done, or otherwise

{x_{\sigma}}^{2q}\mid\tau_{i_{2}}\tau_{j_{2}}
, which by (

) means that

, and
\sigma(i_{2})=q
. As

is bijective, this means that

. Hence we have

\tau_{i_{1}}\tau_{j_{1}}\mid{\tau_{i_{1}}}^{2}\implies\tau_{j_{1}}\mid\tau_{i_{1}}

contradicting (a).

Case 2
:
If
i_{1}=j_{1}
, pick
\sigma\in S_{q}
such that

\sigma(i_{1})=q
. Then by (

) and (

)

which by (

) implies that

. This contradicts the assumption that
\{i_{1},j_{1}\}
and
\{i_{2},j_{2}\}
are distinct (multi)sets.

{\tau_{2}}^{2}=x_{123}^{4}x_{132}^{6}x_{213}^{2}x_{231}^{6}x_{312}^{2}x_{321}^{4}\hfil\hskip 60.00009pt&\tau_{2}\tau_{3}=x_{123}^{5}x_{132}^{5}x_{213}^{4}x_{231}^{4}x_{312}^{3}x_{321}^{3}\\
\hfil\hskip 60.00009pt&\\
{\tau_{3}}^{2}=x_{123}^{6}x_{132}^{4}x_{213}^{6}x_{231}^{2}x_{312}^{4}x_{321}^{2}\hfil\hskip 60.00009pt&\tau_{1}\tau_{3}=x_{123}^{4}x_{132}^{3}x_{213}^{5}x_{231}^{3}x_{312}^{5}x_{321}^{4}.\end{array}

Notation 4.5
.

Lemma

4.3
allows us to assign a new labeling to the faces of
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
, which we will use for the remainder of this section. The labeling is as follows: we assign to each vertex
\ell_{i,j}
of
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
the corresponding monomial generator
\tau_{i}\tau_{j}
of
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
, and to each face
\gamma\in\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
the monomial label

m_{\gamma}=\lcm\{m\mid m\in\gamma\}.

With this labeling, the simplicial complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
now consists of the facets

\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{q}
, where for each
i\in[q]
,

\mathcal{M}_{i}=\mathcal{M}\cup\{{\tau_{i}}^{2}\},

such that

\mathcal{M}=\{\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq q\}.

Moreover, if
A=\{\tau_{i_{1}}\tau_{j_{1}},\tau_{i_{2}}\tau_{j_{2}},\ldots,\tau_{i_{s}}\tau_{j_{s}}\}
is any subset of
V(\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2})
, then

(4.4)

m_{A}=\prod\limits_{\sigma\in S_{q}}x_{\sigma}^{\max\{\sigma(i_{1})+\sigma(j_{1}),\ \sigma(i_{2})+\sigma(j_{2}),\ \dots\ ,\ \sigma(i_{s})+\sigma(j_{s})\}}.

We now establish a few more results that will be useful in proving the main result of this section and, more broadly, in the study of permutation ideals.

Lemma 4.6
.

With the notation set above, we have

(i)

m_{\mathcal{M}}=\prod\limits_{\sigma\in S_{q}}{x_{\sigma}}^{2q-1};

(ii)

for a face
\gamma\in\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
and a permutation
\sigma\in S_{q}
,

(4.5)

{x_{\sigma}}^{2q}\mid m_{\gamma}\iff{\tau_{i}}^{2}\in\gamma\ \text{for some}\ i\in[q]\ \text{with}\ \sigma(i)=q;

(iii)

for a face
\gamma\in\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
and
\sigma\in S_{q}
with
\sigma(i)=q
and
\sigma(j)=q-1
, if
{\tau_{i}}^{2}\notin\gamma
then

(4.6)

{x_{\sigma}}^{2q-1}\mid m_{\gamma}\iff\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\in\gamma;

(iv)

for any
k\in[q]
, we have
m_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}=\prod\limits_{\sigma\in S_{q},\ \sigma(k)=q}{x_{\sigma}}^{{2q}}\prod\limits_{\sigma\in S_{q},\ \sigma(k)\neq q}{x_{\sigma}}^{{2q-1}};

(v)

m_{V(\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2})}=\prod\limits_{\sigma\in S_{q}}{x_{\sigma}}^{2q}
.

Proof.

Using (
4.4
) and (
4.2
), to find
m_{\mathcal{M}}
we observe that

m_{\mathcal{M}}=\prod\limits_{\sigma\in S_{q}}x_{\sigma}^{\max\{\sigma(i)+\sigma(j)\colon 1\ \leq\ i\ <\ j\ \leq\ q\}}=\prod\limits_{\sigma\in S_{q}}{x_{\sigma}}^{2q-1}.

The statement in (
4.5
) follows directly from
(
4.1
) and, similarly, (
4.6
) is a consequence of
(
4.2
).
The remaining statements now follow immediately.

∎

Proposition 4.7
.

For any two distinct faces
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
of
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
, we have
m_{\gamma_{1}}\neq m_{\gamma_{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
be any two distinct faces of
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
and assume
m_{\gamma_{1}}=m_{\gamma_{2}}
. By (
4.5
) if a vertex
{\tau_{i}}^{2}
is in one of
\gamma_{1}
or
\gamma_{2}
and not in the other, we immediately get
m_{\gamma_{1}}\neq m_{\gamma_{2}}
. So we may assume they both contain the same vertices of the form

{\tau_{i}}^{2}
. Since a facet of
\mathbb{M}^{2}_{q}
can have at most one such vertex, we may assume both
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{i}
.

Suppose the vertex labeled
\tau_{a}\tau_{b}

is in
\gamma_{1}
but not in
\gamma_{2}
for some
a\neq b
and let
\sigma\in S_{q}
be a permutation such that
\sigma(a)=q
and
\sigma(b)=q-1
. Then

{x_{\sigma}}^{2q-1}\mid m_{\gamma_{1}}=m_{\gamma_{2}}
. By (
4.6
) we must have
{\tau_{a}}^{2}\in\gamma_{2}
, which implies that
a=i
.

Now let
\sigma^{\prime}\in S_{q}
be a permutation such that
\sigma^{\prime}(b)=q
and
\sigma^{\prime}(a)=q-1
. Then

{x_{\sigma^{\prime}}}^{2q-1}\mid m_{\gamma_{1}}=m_{\gamma_{2}}
. Thus by (
4.6
) we must have
{\tau_{b}}^{2}\in\gamma_{2}
, which implies that
b=i
.

Now we have
a=b=i
, but this is a contradiction to our assumption
a\neq b
. Therefore we have
\tau_{a}\tau_{b}\in\gamma_{1}
if and only if
\tau_{a}\tau_{b}\in\gamma_{2}
.
∎

We now show that the minimal free resolution of the square of the permutation ideal
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
is supported by
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
and that these ideals are also Scarf ideals. To establish these results, we will use
Lemma

2.7
.

Theorem 4.8

(
Main Theorem 2).

If
q
is any positive integer, then the Scarf complex of the square of the permutation ideal,
\mathcal{T}_{q}^{2}
, coincides with the simplicial complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
. That is,

\operatorname{Scarf}({\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2})=\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}.

Proof.

It has already been shown in
Theorem

3.7
that for any monomial ideal
I
minimally generated by
q
monomials, the simplicial complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
supports a free resolution of
I^{2}
. In particular, this implies that
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
also supports a free resolution of
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
. Therefore, we have the following inclusion

(4.7)

\operatorname{Scarf}({\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2})\subseteq\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}.

Now, to prove the other inclusion, we show that every face
\gamma
of
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
is a Scarf face. Using
Lemma

2.7
, we need to show that for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
, we have the following two conditions satisfied:

C1:

m_{\gamma}\neq m_{\gamma\setminus\{v\}}
for all vertices
v\in\gamma
;

C2:

m_{\gamma\cup\{v\}}\neq m_{\gamma}
for all vertices
v\in V(\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2})\setminus\gamma
.

Since
\gamma
is a face of
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
, any subset of
\gamma
, and in particular
\gamma\setminus\{v\}
, will also be a face of
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
. This implies, by
Proposition

4.7
, that condition C1 is satisfied.

Now, considering the second condition, if adding a vertex
v\in V(\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2})\setminus\gamma
to
\gamma
results in
\gamma\cup\{v\}
staying within
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
, then
Proposition

4.7
can once again be applied to conclude that condition C2 is satisfied.

Suppose
\gamma\cup\{v\}
is no longer a face of
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
and
m_{\gamma\cup\{v\}}=m_{\gamma}
. The face
\gamma
is contained within a unique facet, say
\mathcal{M}_{i}
, containing
{\tau_{i}}^{2}
, and so we must have
v={\tau_{j}}^{2}
for some
j\neq i
. We now consider a permutation
\sigma\in S_{q}
such that
\sigma(j)=q
, so that
{x_{\sigma}}^{2q}\mid m_{\gamma\cup\{v\}}=m_{\gamma}
. By (
4.5
) we must have
{\tau_{j}}^{2}\in\gamma
, a contradiction. Therefore,
m_{\gamma\cup\{v\}}\neq m_{\gamma}
.

We conclude that
\operatorname{Scarf}({\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2})=\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
, and thus
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
is a Scarf ideal.

∎

The following two corollaries follow directly from
Theorem

4.8
and highlight its significance.

Corollary 4.9
.

The simplicial complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
supports the minimal free resolution
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
.

Proof.

From
Theorem

3.7
, it follows that the simplicial complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
supports a free resolution of
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
. Furthermore, by
Theorem

4.8
, we imply that this free resolution is, in fact, the Scarf resolution of
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
. Therefore, the simplicial complex
\mathbb{M}_{q}^{2}
supports the minimal free resolution of
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}
.

∎

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
I
be any monomial ideal minimally generated by
q
monomials in the polynomial ring
S=K[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}]
. Then for all
0\leq i\leq{q\choose 2}
, we have

\beta_{i}({I}^{2})\leq\beta_{i}({{\mathcal{T}_{q}}^{2}}).

Proof.

By combining
Remark

2.5
,
Theorem

4.8
, and
Corollary

4.9
, we obtain the desired conclusion.

∎

We conclude this article with the following remark.

Remark 4.11
.

From
Lemma

4.6
, as well as the proofs of
Proposition

4.7
and
Theorem

4.8
, one can observe that the monomial generators of the permutation ideal
\mathcal{T}_{q}
can be replaced with alternative generators involving fewer variables, while still preserving the validity of both
Proposition

4.7
and
Theorem

4.8
.

For instance, for each
i\in[q]
, consider a monomial
\tau_{i}^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{T}}
defined by

\tau_{i}^{\prime}=\prod\limits_{\sigma\in S_{q}\,:\,\sigma(i)\in\{q-1,\,q\}}x_{\sigma}^{\sigma(i)}.

Define the corresponding new ideal by

\mathcal{T}_{q}^{\prime}=(\tau_{1}^{\prime},\tau_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,\tau_{q}^{\prime}).

Then,
Theorem

4.8
continues to hold for the square of this ideal,
{(\mathcal{T}_{q}^{\prime})}^{2}
. However, we prefer to work with the permutation ideal
{\mathcal{T}_{q}}
rather than
{\mathcal{T}_{q}^{\prime}}
. The reason is that, when studying higher powers of monomial ideals, it becomes necessary to introduce more variables to ensure distinct least common multiples in the lcm lattice of the ideal. However, to investigate higher powers, one must use alternative approaches such as techniques from Discrete Morse Theory. Our ongoing work explores this direction in greater detail.
