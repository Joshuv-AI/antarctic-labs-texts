Kinematic Alignment in TKA Using the Contralateral Healthy Knee as Reference: A Combined Finite Element and Clinical Analysis
Authors: Lingce Kong, Wei Lin, Huijun Kang, Muchen Yu, Ming Li, Chenyue Xu, Fei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveMechanical alignment (MA) and kinematic alignment (KA) are common total knee arthroplasty (TKA) strategies. Patients with bilateral knee osteoarthritis lack a precise individualized alignment reference, whereas those with unilateral OA have a potential reference from the contralateral healthy knee. This study proposes a natural KA (NKA) strategy referencing the contralateral healthy knee and compares biomechanical and clinical outcomes of MA, KA, and NKA for TKA.MethodsFinite element models (neutral/mild/moderate varus) were established based on three selected patients with unilateral knee OA using bilateral CT data to simulate MA/KA/NKA implantation. This exploratory biomechanical analysis was designed to identify potential patterns. Stress distribution on the femoral component, polyethylene insert, and tibia, along with contact pressure, contact area, and implant displacement, was analyzed. Additionally, a retrospective clinical study included 101 patients who underwent TKA with a minimum 1‐year follow‐up. Patients were divided into MA, KA, and NKA groups based on postoperative radiographic alignment. Postoperative alignment parameters, range of motion (ROM), Knee Society Score (KSS), and Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) were compared.ResultsFinite element analysis revealed that in the neutral and mild varus models, NKA resulted in a more balanced mediolateral biomechanical distribution, lower implant stress, and minimal component subsidence. In the moderate varus model, MA showed the most balanced mediolateral load distribution and the largest contact area. Clinical follow‐up indicated that the MA group achieved lower limb alignment close to mechanical neutrality postoperatively, while the KA and NKA groups retained some physiological varus. No significant differences were observed in KSS among the three groups. However, FJS showed a statistically favorable trend in the KA and NKA groups compared with the MA group, although the clinical significance of this difference requires further investigation.ConclusionThe NKA strategy shows potential biomechanical advantages by optimizing stress distribution in selected neutral/mild varus models and is associated with better short‐term patient‐reported satisfaction than MA. NKA provides a feasible coronal alignment concept for precision TKA in patients with unilateral knee osteoarthritis without significant contralateral deformity. Long‐term safety and generalizability require further validation.Keywords: clinical outcomes, contralateral reference, finite element analysis, natural kinematic alignment, total knee arthroplasty

Introduction

The success of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) depends not only on implant design but also critically on surgical technique, particularly the alignment strategy applied to the femorotibial joint [1, 2]. Conventional mechanical alignment (MA) aims to establish a standardized neutral mechanical axis. However, this approach may alter the patient's native knee anatomy and kinematics and has been associated with patient dissatisfaction rates as high as 15% [3, 4]. Various personalized alignment strategies have emerged, including classic kinematic alignment (KA), restricted kinematic alignment (rKA), and functional alignment (FA). Their clinical execution logic differs substantially: classic KA relies on the surgeon's assessment of cartilage wear to retrospectively infer the pre‐arthritic anatomy; rKA sets an upper limit on postoperative residual varus to avoid mechanical risks associated with extreme deformities; while FA prioritizes soft‐tissue tension balance over strict anatomical bone resection. Among these, KA is widely applied and aims to restore the pre‐arthritic knee alignment, aiming for more natural function and higher satisfaction [5, 6, 7].

However, a major challenge inherent to the classic KA technique is the difficulty in reliably assessing each patient's native joint morphology [8]. Current KA primarily relies on preoperative imaging assessment and surgeon experience to estimate cartilage wear thickness and thereby determine the joint line level [9, 10]. As osteoarthritis is often accompanied by bone defects, osteophyte formation, and uneven settlement of the proximal tibia [11, 12], this experience‐based reconstruction is inevitably prone to estimation errors and subjectivity and therefore lacks true precision for individualized restoration.

For patients with unilateral knee osteoarthritis due to factors such as trauma, the contralateral healthy knee presents an ideal anatomical reference template [13, 14]. The morphology of this healthy knee represents the patient's natural anatomy in a non‐pathological state and can be used to guide the reconstruction of a “natural” KA. This strategy is defined in our study as natural KA (NKA). Unlike classic KA, our proposed NKA uses the contralateral healthy knee as a patient‐specific anatomical template to reduce subjectivity in estimating native joint geometry. It does not rely on estimated cartilage wear or soft‐tissue adjustments on the affected side, thereby minimizing intraoperative subjectivity and providing a truly individualized coronal alignment reference. Nonetheless, given the considerable inter‐individual variation in knee morphology [15] and the current lack of patient‐specific biomechanical investigations [16], analyses using real patient data are necessary to simulate postoperative stress and strain states of the bone and implant.

This study aimed to use finite element analysis to compare the differences in postoperative alignment, stress distribution, contact pressure, contact location, and implant displacement among MA, KA, and NKA techniques. Models were constructed based on full‐length bilateral lower limb computed tomography (CT) data from patients with unilateral knee osteoarthritis. Concurrently, we conducted a retrospective radiographic and clinical follow‐up study to evaluate differences in postoperative lower limb alignment and knee function.

We hypothesized that NKA would achieve more individualized lower limb alignment than MA and better biomechanical distribution than KA, but in cases of severe deformity, both KA and NKA might lead to medial stress concentration. Patients whose alignment preserves physiological varus may achieve higher patient‐reported satisfaction.

Materials and Methods

Finite Element Study

This finite element analysis was designed as a preliminary, hypothesis‐generating study. Three different finite element models of osteoarthritis were constructed in this retrospective study. Three patients scheduled for TKA in 2025 with unilateral knee osteoarthritis were selected. Their contralateral knees were asymptomatic, uninjured, and radiographically confirmed to be free of osteoarthritis. The healthy‐limb hip–knee–ankle (HKA) angles for these patients measured 0° ± 1°, 5° ± 1°, and 10° ± 1° of varus, respectively, and they were matched for age, height, and weight. The eligibility criteria for defining a healthy contralateral knee were as follows: (1) absence of pain, trauma, or prior knee surgery; (2) normal physical examination with negative varus and valgus stress test results; (3) Kellgren–Lawrence grade < 2 on weight‐bearing anteroposterior and lateral radiographs [17]. For quality assurance of bilateral alignment measurements, all full‐length standing radiographs were obtained with standardized positioning: knees in full extension, patellae facing forward, and feet at shoulder‐width apart. The study protocol was approved by the appropriate Institutional Review Board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants (Hebei Medical University Third Hospital Ke2024‐08‐1).

Data from the following three patients were used to construct bilateral lower limb models, representing neutral, mild varus, and moderate varus alignments, respectively: (1) a 65‐year‐old male with an affected‐side HKA of varus 3° and a healthy‐side HKA of varus 1°; (2) a 65‐year‐old female with an affected‐side HKA of varus 8° and a healthy‐side HKA of varus 5°; and (3) a 65‐year‐old female with an affected‐side HKA of varus 15° and a healthy‐side HKA of varus 9° (Figure 1). CT data were segmented (threshold: 250 HU) into cortical and cancellous bone regions using Mimics 21.0 (Materialize HQ, Leuven, Belgium) for finite element analysis [18]. This differentiation was performed to more reasonably simulate the mechanical characteristics of the bony structures around the knee joint. Modeling the cortical and cancellous bone separately enables closer approximation of the true anatomical morphology and captures the differential load transfer behavior between these two bone types. This approach facilitates the assessment of overall load transmission and proximal tibial stress distribution under different alignment strategies.

In the MA‐TKA simulations, a standard resection technique was employed. In the coronal plane, the femoral and tibial components were aligned perpendicular to the mechanical axes of the femur and tibia, respectively. In the axial plane, the femoral component was implanted parallel to the surgical transepicondylar axis, and the tibial component was aligned according to the anteroposterior axis of the tibia, consistent with prior studies [19]. After implantation, the mechanical axis of the lower limb passed through the center of the knee in all models.

For KA‐TKA, femoral and tibial resections were performed to restore each model's native joint line level and angles, following the methodology described by Nakamura et al. [18]. In the coronal plane, a 2‐mm cartilage wear compensation was applied medially on both the femoral and tibial sides. The femoral and tibial components were then implanted tangentially to the estimated articular surfaces of the distal femur and proximal tibia, respectively. In the axial plane, the femoral component was aligned tangentially to the posterior femoral condyles, disregarding posterior cartilage defects. Tibial component rotation was set by aligning its anteroposterior axis parallel to the major axis of the nearly elliptical boundary of the lateral tibial plateau. The postoperative HKA angles for the three models were 2°, 5°, and 9° of varus, respectively.

The NKA‐TKA procedure was similar to KA‐TKA, but used the contralateral healthy knee's joint geometry as the reference for bone resections. The contralateral knee geometry was mirrored in the coronal plane, and the components were implanted tangentially to the corresponding bony surfaces of the femur and tibia. All other steps remained identical. The resulting postoperative HKA angles for the three NKA models were 1°, 3°, and 5° of varus, respectively.

All virtual bone resections and implant positioning procedures for all models were performed under the supervision of a surgeon with over 10 years of experience in primary and revision TKA. Subsequently, a second surgeon with 5 years of clinical practice in arthroplasty independently reviewed the final component placement to eliminate single‐operator bias.

A fixed‐bearing, cruciate‐retaining total knee prosthesis (Loong Knee, Naton, China) was used for the analysis. A single implant size (Size 4 femoral and tibial components paired with a 6‐mm polyethylene insert) was utilized across all three finite element models to isolate the independent effect of alignment strategy from implant size variability while satisfying the dimensional requirements of all three patients. Two independent loads were applied in this study. First, a vertical axial load of 600 N was applied at the reference point of the femoral head. Second, a 600‐N traction force of the quadriceps was loaded along the anatomical pulling direction of the quadriceps tendon. The proximal quadriceps load was transmitted to the superior margin of the patella via MPC coupling, distributing the force over the corresponding attachment area instead of concentrating it on a single nodal point of the patella [20]. This standardized loading magnitude was adopted to facilitate consistent comparative analysis of mechanical performance among different alignment strategies. The finite element models were constructed using ANSA 21.0 (BETA CAE Systems S.A., Greece) and the analysis was performed with ABAQUS 6.14 (Dassault Systèmes, France). Analyzed parameters included contact stress and location on the femoral component and polyethylene insert, tibial bone stress, tibial component displacement, and patellofemoral congruency.

Cementless fixation was simulated with direct contact between the implant and bone surfaces. A friction coefficient of 0.1 was assigned to all contact pairs [21]. The distal ends of the tibia and fibula were fixed in all translational degrees of freedom to simulate the distal support condition during single‐leg stance, while the proximal femur was loaded axially without additional displacement constraints. Schematic diagrams of lower limb coupling constraints and contact interactions are presented in Figures S1 and S2. Material properties were assigned as follows: the femoral and tibial components were made of cobalt‐chromium‐molybdenum alloy with an elastic modulus of 220 GPa [22, 23]. The polyethylene insert was modeled as a linear elastic material with an elastic modulus of 1000 MPa and a yield strength of 20 MPa. Cortical and cancellous bones were defined as linear elastic materials with elastic moduli of 17 and 0.35 GPa. The posterior cruciate ligament, patellar ligament, patellofemoral ligaments, lateral collateral ligament, and medial collateral ligament were all modeled as tension‐only linear elastic materials with an elastic modulus of 260 MPa [24, 25, 26]. Detailed material parameters are listed in Table S1.

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the finite element results and to exclude the influence of mesh density, a mesh convergence analysis was performed. Using the results from a 0.8 mm mesh as the reference baseline, the relative differences for all key output parameters calculated with a 1.2‐mm mesh were found to be below 3%. Detailed parameters of the mesh convergence analysis are provided in Table S2. Therefore, considering both computational accuracy and efficiency, a mesh size of 1.2 mm was selected for the final models. Meshes for the femoral component, tibial component, and polyethylene insert were generated using four‐node linear tetrahedral elements with local refinement, resulting in an average global element size of 1.2 mm. The mesh size for cortical and cancellous bone was set to 1.2 mm near the implant interfaces and approximately 3 mm elsewhere. A schematic diagram of the mesh and its locally refined regions is shown in Figure S3. The final meshes for the femoral component, polyethylene insert, and tibial component comprised 185,885, 204,559, and 126,198 solid elements, respectively. On average, the bone mesh for each model consisted of 13,768 shell elements and 98,719 solid elements for the cortical and cancellous bone regions. The cortical bone regions were modeled using three‐node shell elements, while solid elements were adopted for cancellous bone. Cortical bone thickness was derived from CT segmentation and preserved with spatial variation across the model.

Radiographic and Clinical Follow‐Up

A retrospective clinical study was conducted on patients who underwent TKA in 2024 to evaluate the outcomes associated with different postoperative alignment strategies. Appropriate Institutional Review Board approval and participant informed consent were obtained (Hebei Medical University Third Hospital Ke2024‐08‐1). Patients admitted for primary TKA due to knee osteoarthritis were included. Exclusion criteria were: valgus knees, severe varus (exceeding 15° or flexion contracture exceeding 20°), rheumatoid arthritis, history of high tibial osteotomy, or other previous knee surgeries. All patients received the same implant system used in the finite element study.

Patients were divided into three groups based on preoperative and postoperative radiographic measurements (Figure 2): (1) MA group: patients with a postoperative mechanical axis passing through the center of the knee, defined as a HKA angle of 180° ± 1° (39 knees); (2) KA group: patients whose postoperative coronal alignment (MPTA and mLDFA) was restored to within 1° of their preoperative constitutional alignment on the affected side (35 knees); and (3) NKA group: patients with unilateral osteoarthritis whose postoperative MPTA and mLDFA were within 1° of the corresponding values of the contralateral healthy knee (27 knees). A 1° threshold was adopted, as coronal alignment within ±1° of the mechanical axis is conventionally considered optimal [27]. Preoperative baseline data, including age, body mass index (BMI), knee range of motion (ROM), HKA, mLDFA, and MPTA, were recorded for all patients.

Comparison of postoperative full‐length standing radiographs from the MA, KA, and NKA groups. The mechanical lateral distal femoral angle (mLDFA) and medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) are illustrated. KA, kinematic alignment; MA, mechanical alignment; NKA, natural kinematic alignment.

In the retrospective clinical study, patients were not randomized but were assigned to MA, KA, or NKA groups postoperatively based on actual radiographic alignment achieved. The specific surgical approach is determined through preoperative consultation between the surgeon and the patient. All surgeries were performed by the same surgical team using a robotic‐assisted system (Naton, China). For preoperative planning, each patient underwent a CT scan. The images were segmented to construct a three‐dimensional knee model. The planning process was individualized to minimize bone resection and restore the native periarticular anatomy as much as possible. Planning for femoral and tibial component placement aimed for either a neutral mechanical axis or preserving some varus, adhering to MA, KA, or NKA strategy. For NKA, contralateral templating was performed by mirroring the healthy knee CT data in the coronal plane using Mimics software. The mirrored bony surfaces (distal femur and proximal tibia) were then rigidly registered to the affected side to determine the target resection levels and coronal angles. The joint line was established based on the three‐dimensional spatial positions of the femoral and tibial articular surfaces derived from the mirrored contralateral geometry. For cases targeting a neutral mechanical axis, femoral external rotation was set relative to the Whiteside line and the surgical transepicondylar axis, and the tibial component was implanted with 5°–7° of posterior slope. For cases preserving varus alignment (KA and NKA), the femoral resection was referenced from the posterior condylar line, and the native tibial slope observed on the preoperative CT was replicated. Alignment tolerances were set at 1° for all planes, including coronal, sagittal, and rotational axes. Regarding soft‐tissue balancing, medial release was performed only when the flexion and extension gaps were asymmetric, following the measured resection principle. In the KA and NKA groups, no extensive medial release was performed unless a fixed deformity could not be corrected passively. Intraoperatively, reflective marker arrays were first affixed to the tibia and femur through small incisions. Then, optical motion capture coupled with a registration probe was used to map the key anatomical landmarks. This step mapped the surgical field and allowed dynamic referencing of the tibia and femur on the 3D model to determine and guide the final positioning and installation of the implants.

Patients were followed up postoperatively at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter. The minimum follow‐up was 1 year. Lower limb alignment was assessed using HKA, mLDFA, and MPTA measurements on full‐length standing radiographs. ROM, Knee Society Score (KSS), and Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) were also recorded and compared. Clinical assessments were performed by a trained research assistant who was blinded to group allocation. All radiographic measurements were performed independently by two experienced orthopedic surgeons who were blinded to group allocation. Measurements were repeated after a 2‐week interval, and the intra‐ and interobserver reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC values for all parameters were > 0.90, indicating excellent reliability.

The finite element analysis and clinical cohort study were conducted independently. The FEA served as a hypothesis‐generating biomechanical exploration to identify potential mechanical patterns among the three alignment strategies, while the retrospective clinical study provided a separate observational evaluation of the corresponding radiographic and patient‐reported outcomes. These two components were not nested; rather, they were designed to complement each other by linking biomechanical plausibility to short‐term clinical performance, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the effects of each alignment strategy.

Statistical analysis of clinical outcomes was performed using SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for each continuous variable. Variables following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non‐normally distributed variables are expressed as median. Differences among the three groups for continuous variables were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA. Fisher's least significant difference post hoc test was applied for pairwise comparisons. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistically significant pairwise differences are indicated by different superscript letters in the tables.

Results

Finite Element Study

Neutral Alignment Models

The NKA model achieved the most favorable load distribution (Table 1, Figures 3, 4, 5, 6). It exhibited the lowest insert stress (28.88 MPa) and tibial bone stress (19.12 MPa) among the three groups, along with the minimal component subsidence (0.40 mm) and the largest contact area (44.52 mm2). The MA group showed the highest stresses on the femoral component (27.16 MPa) and the insert (33.92 MPa). Although the KA group had the highest medial contact pressure (293.02 N), its medial and lateral contact pressures were the most comparable. The patellofemoral congruency was similar across all three groups.

Mild Varus Models

The NKA model showed advantages in reducing stresses on the femoral component (23.28 MPa) and the insert (31.80 MPa), and also exhibited the smallest subsidence (0.39 mm). The MA group still had the highest femoral component and insert stresses (33.83 and 39.99 MPa, respectively) but demonstrated the most balanced distribution of medial and lateral contact pressures and the largest contact area (57.81 mm2). Assessment of the patellofemoral joint revealed that the patella was positioned more medially in the KA and NKA groups compared to the MA group.

Moderate Varus Models

The mechanical differences among the alignment strategies were most pronounced in these models. While the overall magnitude of component subsidence was less than in the neutral and mild varus models, its distribution changed noticeably, showing a trend toward generalized sinking. The KA group displayed pronounced medial stress concentration, with a medial contact pressure as high as 529.18 N compared to a lateral contact pressure of only 151.22 N. The MA group showed the most balanced mediolateral contact pressure distribution and the largest contact area. However, the stresses on the femoral component (30.11 MPa) and the insert (36.25 MPa) in the MA group remained elevated. The NKA group presented an optimal compromise. It had the lowest insert stress (27.88 MPa) among the three groups. Although its medial contact pressure was still high (524.39 N), it was lower than that of the KA group, and its lateral contact pressure (208.23 N) was higher than that of the KA group, resulting in a more balanced mediolateral load distribution compared to KA. The patellofemoral congruency was superior in the KA and NKA groups.

Clinical Follow‐Up

A total of 101 knees completed at least 1 year of follow‐up. No statistically significant differences were found in the preoperative baseline characteristics among the three groups (Table 2). At the final follow‐up, postoperative radiographic measurements (Table 3) revealed significant differences in lower limb alignment among the groups (p < 0.001). The MA group achieved an HKA angle corrected close to mechanical neutrality (179.41° ± 0.83°). The KA group retained some anatomical varus with a postoperative HKA of 176.34° ± 1.08°. The NKA group's HKA was 177.19° ± 1.02°, a value intermediate between the MA and KA groups. A similar trend was observed for the postoperative MPTA: 89.52° ± 0.46° in the MA group, 86.10° ± 1.33° in the KA group, and 87.20° ± 1.11° in the NKA group, with significant intergroup differences (p < 0.001). The mean follow‐up duration was 16.66 ± 3.49 months (range: 12–22 months). During this period, 14 patients were lost to follow‐up (7 in the MA group, 4 in the KA group, and 3 in the NKA group) and were excluded from the final analysis. No complications such as periprosthetic joint infection, periprosthetic fracture, patellar maltracking, joint instability, or implant loosening occurred in any patient during the follow‐up period.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the effects of the three alignment strategies, MA, KA, and the novel NKA, in TKA by combining finite element analysis with a retrospective clinical study. Our preliminary findings suggest that NKA may achieve more balanced compartmental loading than KA in neutral and mild varus models while preserving individualized alignment. In the short‐term clinical follow‐up, although significant differences were observed in postoperative lower limb alignment among the three groups, functional recovery was similar. However, both KA and NKA, which aim to restore physiological alignment, showed a favorable trend toward better patient‐reported joint awareness compared with MA.

Biomechanical Comparison of Alignment Strategies

Accurately reconstructing pre‐arthritic anatomy remains a key challenge for KA [28]. During the progression of osteoarthritis, in addition to cartilage wear, bone defects, and osteophyte formation, uneven settlement of the proximal tibia constitutes a significant factor contributing to varus deformity [29, 30]. Thus, KA techniques that only compensate for cartilage wear may fail to restore constitutional alignment. Several studies suggest anatomical symmetry between bilateral knees [31, 32], supporting the use of the contralateral healthy knee as a reconstruction template for the affected side. However, other studies contend that the contralateral coronal alignment is not a reliable reference for TKA [33, 34]. But these conclusions are primarily based on population‐level statistics and lack patient‐specific biomechanical validation. our finite element results suggest that NKA referenced to the contralateral knee may optimize stress distribution, supporting its anatomical rationale. Nevertheless, this remains an area of active investigation, and larger studies are needed.

The KA strategy seeks to preserve and restore the individual's constitutional varus alignment. It better preserves native soft tissue tension and may facilitate patient adaptation. However, this raises concerns that a varus‐aligned TKA might lead to uneven force distribution and stress concentration, potentially resulting in implant instability and accelerated polyethylene wear [35, 36, 37]. Our finite element analysis suggests that in the absence of an accurate reference from the contralateral healthy knee, KA may lead to overcorrection in varus knees, particularly in moderate varus models, where the postoperative varus surpasses the patient's constitutional range. This could induce significant medial stress concentration. In contrast, while MA emphasizes neutral alignment to avoid mechanical imbalance, this approach compromises the knee's inherent biomechanical environment. This alteration may lead to increased bone–implant interface stress and unnatural kinematics [38, 39]. The NKA strategy, referenced to the contralateral healthy knee, adheres to the personalized reconstruction strategy while utilizing the contralateral coronal alignment as a natural benchmark to restrict severe varus alignment. This approach may achieve a balance between individualization and biomechanical safety, consistent with previous findings that appropriate varus alignment reduces stress concentration [40]. It should be noted that when the reference alignment itself is excessively varus, as in our moderate varus models, both KA and NKA resulted in an excessive mediolateral pressure difference exceeding the 267 N threshold suggested by Meneghini et al., which was associated with poorer clinical outcomes [41]. Consequently, when a patient's contralateral healthy knee exhibits constitutional varus [17], the alignment restored by this method might still be aberrant, necessitating targeted corrective adjustments.

Patellofemoral Outcomes and Clinical Implications

Patellofemoral dysfunction is a significant contributor to patient dissatisfaction following TKA and correlates closely with abnormal patellar tracking [42, 43]. In our mild and moderate varus models, patellofemoral contact locations in the KA and NKA groups were similar and superior to the MA group, where contact was located more laterally. Simon et al. reported that varus‐aligned TKA reduces quadriceps force during flexion and increases patellofemoral contact area [44], while Shatrov et al. found that KA and FA better restore trochlear groove anatomy [45]. These findings suggest that coronal alignment strategies conforming to native anatomy improve patellofemoral congruency. Beyond tracking, patellar clunk syndrome is another concern. Bulum and Degirmenci reported a 7.5% incidence in 330 TKAs, associated with posterior‐stabilized prostheses, smaller femoral components, and thicker inserts [46]. Although patellar clunk syndrome was not assessed in our models, this highlights that implant selection—alongside alignment strategy—merits attention in optimizing patellofemoral outcomes. The comparable patellofemoral contact between NKA and KA further supports using the contralateral healthy knee as a mirroring reference, effectively restoring native patellofemoral kinematics.

Clinical Outcomes and Patient‐Reported Satisfaction

Radiographic and clinical outcomes provide essential corroboration for the biomechanical analysis. Follow‐up in this study revealed significant postoperative differences in HKA, MPTA, and mLDFA among the three groups. However, regardless of the alignment strategy used, ROM demonstrated significant improvement postoperatively in all patients, and no significant intergroup differences were found in ROM and KSS. In contrast, the FJS, which measures a patient's awareness of their prosthesis after TKA, demonstrated a preference for the KA and NKA groups compared to the MA group [47]. However, this statistical advantage warrants cautious interpretation, as the observed 3‐ to 4‐point difference is substantially below the reported minimal clinically important difference for the FJS (13.7–16.6 points)—the threshold for a perceptible improvement in joint awareness [48]. This modest numerical gap suggests that, despite achieving slightly higher FJS scores on a group level, patients in the personalized alignment groups may not experience a perceptible difference in their daily joint awareness compared with those who received MA. The gap may be partially attributable to the moderate sample size; larger cohorts with extended follow‐up are needed to determine whether it could accumulate into a clinically meaningful benefit. Nevertheless, the consistent trend favoring KA and NKA—even in the absence of complete joint “forgetfulness”—suggests that coronal alignment closer to native anatomy may facilitate psychological and functional adaptation to the replaced knee. The traditional MA technique has been associated with dissatisfaction rates as high as 15% [49, 50]. With rising quality‐of‐life expectations, patients increasingly desire a knee that feels natural. KA and NKA, by restoring a more physiological joint line and soft tissue tension, may better address this deeper patient need. It is recognized that adaptation to a replaced knee often requires considerable time. Although the current study involved only a 1‐year follow‐up, it already indicates a subjective benefit associated with personalized alignment strategies. However, whether this advantage persists or diminishes over a longer timeframe remains to be determined. Our findings highlight the necessity of further validation in larger‐scale studies to clarify the most suitable personalized alignment approach.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to combine finite element analysis with clinical follow‐up to evaluate the NKA strategy referencing the contralateral healthy knee, providing both biomechanical and patient‐reported evidence. The use of bilateral CT data enabled patient‐specific modeling and direct anatomical comparison between affected and healthy sides. Robotic‐assisted surgery ensured high precision in implant positioning, and the multimodal assessment—integrating biomechanical simulations, radiographic measurements, and clinical outcomes—offers a comprehensive evaluation of the NKA strategy.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the finite element analysis was exploratory, including only three patient‐specific models and static coronal‐plane loading without simulating dynamic kinematics. The use of a uniform implant size, homogeneous bone material properties, and the absence of experimental validation preclude interpretation of absolute stress values; results should be interpreted as comparative trends. Second, the clinical groups were defined based on postoperative radiographic measurements rather than randomized allocation, which may have introduced selection bias. Third, the follow‐up was limited to 1 year, and the NKA strategy was evaluated only in patients with unilateral osteoarthritis; thus, long‐term durability and generalizability to bilateral OA remain to be established. Fourth, sagittal, axial rotational, and joint‐line parameters were not quantitatively assessed. Future studies with larger samples, dynamic simulations, multiplanar evaluation, and extended follow‐up are warranted to validate these preliminary findings.

Conclusion

In TKA, NKA, referenced to the contralateral healthy knee, presents a potential individualized strategy as an alternative to MA and KA. It may provide more balanced stress distribution and improved implant stability while preserving physiological limb alignment. Short‐term clinical outcomes suggest a trend toward better patient‐reported joint awareness, but larger, prospective studies with longer follow‐up are required to confirm safety and efficacy.
