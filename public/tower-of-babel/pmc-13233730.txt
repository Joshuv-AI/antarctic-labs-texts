Fit cells, fertile ground: Immunometabolic programming and bone marrow niche support in durable CAR T cell response in leukemia
Authors: Xiuli Wang, Lior Goldberg
Published: 2026

Introduction

CD19-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has transformed the treatment landscape for relapsed/refractory (r/r) B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), offering patients with otherwise limited options a meaningful chance at remission. Despite impressive initial response rates, however, between 30% and 80% of patients ultimately experience CD19-positive relapses, underscoring a critical unmet need: understanding why some patients achieve durable remissions while others do not.1 We hypothesized that the answer to this question lies, at least in part, in the metabolic programming of CAR T cells—both at the time of infusion and as they operate within the bone marrow (BM) microenvironment after infusion.

Our study

To address this question, we leveraged samples from our institutional phase 1/2 clinical trial evaluating investigational CD19-CAR T cell therapy in adults with r/r B-ALL (NCT02146924).2 We identified two distinct patient sub-groups from our cohort of 16 responders: long-term responders (LTRs), who maintained remission without additional therapy following CAR T cell infusion, and short-term responders (STRs), who experienced CD19-positive relapses within a mean of 142 days of initial response. These sub-groups were carefully pairwise matched for relevant clinical variables.

In our recent paper “Immunometabolic determinants of long-term response in leukemia patients receiving CD19 CAR T cell therapy,” published in Nature Communications, we applied a comprehensive, multi-layer immunometabolic profiling strategy to pre-infusion CAR T cell products and post-infusion BM samples from both groups. Our approach integrated Seahorse-based functional metabolic assays, transmission electron microscopy for mitochondrial morphological evaluation, mass cytometry (cytometry by time of flight [CyTOF])-based single-cell proteomics, untargeted metabolomics via mass spectrometry, and mitochondrial mass and membrane potential by flow cytometry.3

Key findings

Our analyses revealed striking immunometabolic differences between LTR and STR that were present even before infusion. Pre-infusion CAR T cells from LTR exhibited significantly elevated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), fatty acid oxidation (FAO), and pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) activity. At the organelle level, LTR products displayed greater mitochondrial mass and structurally tighter cristae—morphological features associated with enhanced mitochondrial efficiency and T cell longevity.4,5 In parallel, LTR pre-infusion products showed lower baseline mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) expression, a finding consistent with literature associating mTOR suppression with a less differentiated, more stem-like T cell phenotype capable of long-term persistence and enhanced anti-tumor activity.6

Untargeted metabolomics further corroborated our functional and single-cell proteomic findings: LTR products showed enrichment of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle metabolites, nucleotide biosynthesis intermediates, and sphingolipid metabolism pathways—all consistent with a metabolically fit, oxidative phenotype—compared to STR products, which showed higher nucleotide catabolism activity.

Beyond the pre-infusion product, we also characterized CAR T cells retrieved from the BM at day 28 post-infusion. Strikingly, post-infusion CAR T cells recovered from the BM of LTR demonstrated high immunometabolic plasticity and dynamic mTOR-pS6 signaling, suggesting these cells were able to adapt their metabolic programs to the BM niche in ways that STR CAR T cells could not. To understand whether the BM microenvironment itself might be contributing to this phenotype, we performed untargeted metabolomics on BM serum samples. Notably, LTR had significantly elevated levels of asparagine, alanine, and arginine—three amino acids known to potentiate mTOR activity in T cells—providing a plausible extrinsic, microenvironmentally driven mechanism for the heightened mTOR-pS6 signaling we observed in post-infusion CAR T cells of this cohort.7 Beyond mTOR-activating amino acids, LTR also showed significant enrichment of serine8 and inosine,9 metabolites required for optimal T cell proliferation and known to enhance adoptive immunotherapy efficacy and reverse CAR T cell exhaustion in preclinical models, as well as lactic acid, which has been shown preclinically to improve adoptive immunotherapy outcomes10 and may be related to the increased LDHA expression we observed in this group. Collectively, these findings support a model in which the BM microenvironment plays an active and instructive role in sustaining the immunometabolic flexibility of CAR T cells in LTR, through the provision of a metabolite milieu that fuels both mTOR-dependent signaling and broader T cell fitness.

A therapeutic strategy: Transient mTOR inhibition during manufacturing

Perhaps our most translatable finding was that the metabolic differences we observed between LTR and STR products could be exploited during CAR T cell manufacturing. We found that transient pharmacological inhibition of mTOR during the manufacturing process of patient-derived T cells induced broad metabolic reprogramming of CAR T cells, shifting them toward metabolically rested and highly fitted immunometabolic phenotype. Critically, these metabolically reprogrammed CAR T cells demonstrated enhanced anti-tumor activity in preclinical medullary and extra-medullary leukemia models, providing a compelling proof of concept that modulating metabolism during manufacture can improve CAR T cell function.

Summary and future directions

Taken together, our findings establish immunometabolic fitness as a key determinant of long-term response to CD19-CAR T cell therapy in B-ALL (Figure 1). We further demonstrate that metabolic features are not fixed but can be pharmacologically induced during manufacturing through transient mTOR inhibition, opening a practical and clinically actionable path toward improving the durability of CAR T cell responses.
