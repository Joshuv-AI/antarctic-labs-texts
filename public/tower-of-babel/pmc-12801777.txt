Automated written corrective feedback: fostering writing accuracy, self-regulation, and motivational dynamics in EFL learners
Authors: Yingying Zhai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines the psycho-emotional impact of Automated Written Corrective Feedback (AWCF) delivered via Grammarly on English as a Foreign Language (EFL) learners, specifically focusing on writing accuracy, self-regulation, and Directed Motivational Currents (DMCs). Employing a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design, this research engaged intermediate-level EFL learners in a sophomore English Composition course at a Chinese university. Participants were divided into two groups: the experimental group, which received both teacher feedback and AWCF, and the control group, which received only teacher feedback. Quantitative findings from pre- and post-assessments on writing performance, self-regulation, and DMC scales indicate that AWCF significantly enhanced writing accuracy, particularly in task response, coherence and cohesion, lexical resources, and grammatical range. Furthermore, AWCF fostered stronger self-regulatory behaviors and a positive motivational trajectory in learners, as evidenced by higher self-regulation and DMC scores; however, the DMC increase was not statistically significant after adjusting for baseline pre-test scores. Qualitative insights from semi-structured interviews highlight AWCF’s role in promoting self-monitoring, confidence, and skill improvement, alongside challenges such as cognitive overload and the need for more contextualized feedback. This study adds to the growing research on technology-enhanced language education by underscoring AWCF's potential to foster both linguistic and psycho-emotional development in EFL contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03765-y.Keywords: Automated Written Corrective Feedback (AWCF), English as a Foreign Language (EFL), Writing accuracy, Self-regulation, Directed Motivational Currents (DMCs), Technology-enhanced learning, Psycho-emotional traits

Introduction

The ability to write effectively underpins successful foreign language acquisition. It empowers learners to express themselves clearly and confidently, fostering deeper engagement with the target language [1]. For English as a Foreign Language (EFL) learners, particularly those in large classroom settings, obtaining timely and constructive feedback on their writing can be a significant hurdle [2, 3]. Automated Written Corrective Feedback (AWCF) tools like Grammarly offer a promising solution to bridge this gap. AWCF has garnered considerable attention in recent years, demonstrating its efficacy in enhancing writing accuracy, particularly in mechanics [4–6]. Studies even suggest AWCF can be as effective as instructor feedback in refining writing skills [7, 8]. However, recent mixed-methods studies highlight a critical tension between the technical reliability of such tools (i.e., their accuracy in identifying errors) and their perceived pedagogical value (i.e., their ability to provide meaningful, context-aware guidance that fosters deep learning) [9]. This creates a critical knowledge gap regarding the impact of AWCF on aspects beyond basic mechanics. Can AWCF, even if technically accurate, foster a more profound engagement with the writing process, promoting self-regulation and intrinsic motivation?

Self-regulation, a learner's capacity to manage their learning journey, encompasses setting goals, monitoring progress, and employing effective learning strategies [10, 11]. Research suggests technology can significantly promote self-regulated learning by providing tools for self-monitoring and reflection [12, 13]. While the potential of AWCF to cultivate self-regulation in L2 writing contexts seems intuitive, limited research has directly investigated this connection. Directed motivational currents (DMCs) offer another compelling perspective for understanding motivation in L2 writing [14]. DMCs represent surges of intense motivation fueled by a clear vision or goal. Although technology use has been linked to increased motivation in language learning [15], the possibility of AWCF triggering DMCs in L2 writing remains unexplored.

This study aims to bridge these knowledge gaps by investigating the influence of AWCF on writing accuracy, self-regulation strategies, and reported DMCs among Chinese EFL learners. By examining these constructs concurrently, this research delves deeper into the multifaceted ways AWCF might influence the writing development of L2 learners. By delving deeper than the realm of basic mechanics, this research has the potential to significantly contribute to the field of L2 writing instruction in three key ways. Firstly, it expands our understanding of AWCF's effectiveness by exploring its potential to promote not only writing accuracy but also self-regulation and intrinsic motivation through the lens of DMCs. Secondly, the findings can inform pedagogical practices by guiding educators in strategically integrating AWCF tools to cultivate learner autonomy, self-monitoring skills, and a more positive learning environment within L2 writing classrooms. Finally, this study paves the way for future research by highlighting the need to optimize AWCF design to foster a more holistic and motivational learning experience for L2 writers.

Literature review

Theoretical framework

Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), outlined by Bandura [16, 17], provides a foundation for understanding learning as a continuous interplay between cognitive, behavioral, and environmental factors. Central to SCT is reciprocal determinism, where individual thought processes, observed behaviors, and environmental context mutually influence learning outcomes and motivation. This framework is particularly relevant for studying Automated Written Corrective Feedback (AWCF) in EFL contexts, as it clarifies how learners interact with technology-enhanced feedback systems and develop self-regulation and motivation.

Self-regulation, a core SCT construct, involves setting goals, monitoring progress, and adapting behaviors to achieve desired outcomes [18]. Bandura [19] argued that self-regulation is a self-directed process influenced by social contexts and feedback, making it applicable to environments where learners engage with tools like Grammarly. AWCF provides immediate feedback, reinforcing self-regulatory practices by prompting reflection on errors, strategy adjustment, and progress tracking [20, 21]. SCT describes self-regulation as an iterative process where individuals continuously refine behaviors in response to feedback [22], aligning with how EFL learners use AWCF to progressively enhance writing skills.

SCT also emphasizes self-efficacy, the belief in one’s ability to achieve specific goals, in shaping motivation and persistence [17]. Higher self-efficacy correlates with greater engagement in challenging tasks, effective feedback use, and sustained motivation [23]. AWCF can support self-efficacy by providing measurable evidence of progress, such as reduced grammatical errors or improved coherence, reinforcing learners' belief in their capacity to improve [24]. Furthermore, SCT notes that self-efficacy strengthens through vicarious experiences, like observing peers’ success [ 25], which can motivate active self-regulation when classmates benefit from AWCF.

This interplay of self-regulation and self-efficacy is central to the cognitively demanding process of revision. From an SCT perspective, revision is not just simple editing; it is a complex act of self-evaluation where learners must detect discrepancies between their current text and their intended goal [26]. This is notoriously difficult, as L2 writers may lack the domain knowledge (Content Knowledge) to identify errors or the self-efficacy to correct them, leading them to overestimate their performance [23]. AWCF tools, in this context, function as a critical environmental scaffold. They can help learners "notice" discrepancies that they would otherwise miss, providing the external feedback needed to trigger the self-regulatory cycle of monitoring, judging, and reacting [18, 27]. A key goal of this study, therefore, is to understand how an AWCF tool alters this complex self-evaluation process.

Another key SCT concept, observational learning, highlights how individuals learn by observing others’ behaviors and their outcomes [16]. In educational settings, this occurs when students observe peers’ interactions with AWCF, noting how others respond to specific feedback or adopt revision strategies [28]. Integrating AWCF into classrooms where learners can observe each other encourages the adoption of effective strategies and self-reflection on feedback practices. Research indicates that observational learning, particularly in peer-assisted settings, enhances technology engagement and fosters stronger self-regulation [29, 30].

The SCT concept of reciprocal determinism further clarifies learner-AWCF interaction within educational contexts. According to SCT, behavior, cognitive processes, and environment reciprocally influence each other [16]. With AWCF, the feedback tool becomes part of the environment, interacting with learners’ cognitive processes like self-efficacy and self-regulation. Learners’ engagement with AWCF, influenced by their self-efficacy and prior experiences, shapes how they perceive and use feedback. Concurrently, environmental factors, such as teacher guidance and task structure, influence how effectively learners engage with AWCF to enhance their writing [18, 31].

SCT’s focus on environmental factors provides insights into optimizing AWCF tools within instructional design. Teachers can create supportive settings that encourage meaningful interaction with AWCF tools by setting clear goals, prompting reflection, and facilitating feedback discussions [32, 33]. Strategically incorporating AWCF into environments that promote collaboration, goal-setting, and peer feedback maximizes its motivational and self-regulatory benefits for EFL learners. SCT suggests that aligning environmental factors with learners’ cognitive needs increases the likelihood of positive learning outcomes from technology-based interventions [10, 28].

Technology integration and the TPACK framework

Given that this study integrates a specific technology (AWCF) into a pedagogical context (L2 writing instruction), it is also informed by the Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) model [34]. TPACK argues that effective technology integration requires more than just knowing how to use the tool (Technological Knowledge, or TK). It demands a complex interplay between TK, knowledge of the subject matter (Content Knowledge, or CK), and knowledge of effective teaching strategies (Pedagogical Knowledge, or PK) [35].

In the context of this study, CK is the knowledge of L2 writing (e.g., grammar, coherence, task response), while TK is the knowledge of Grammarly as an AWCF tool. However, the true challenge lies at the intersections: Technological Pedagogical Knowledge (TPK), which involves understanding how to use Grammarly pedagogically to foster learning, and Technological Content Knowledge (TCK), knowing how the tool specifically represents writing rules [36].

Our study is particularly concerned with the PK and TPK required for a successful AWCF implementation. Simply introducing the tool (TK) is insufficient; the instructor must design a pedagogical wrapper (PK)—in this case, a hybrid feedback model that blends AWCF with teacher feedback—that guides student interaction with the tool. By using stimulated recall to examine the process of student engagement, this study contributes to the TPACK framework. It provides empirical insights into the specific pedagogical strategies (PK) and feedback literacy training needed to successfully integrate AWCF tools (TK) to teach writing (CK), particularly for managing challenges like cognitive overload and perceived lack of feedback nuance.

Automated Written Evaluation (AWE)

Automated Written Evaluation (AWE) systems are computer programs designed to assess and score written text, offering analytical capabilities from lexical to discourse-level evaluation [37, 38]. Early systems like the Project Essay Grader (PEG) in the 1960 s focused on grammar and syntax [39]. AWE has since evolved into sophisticated tools providing semantic feedback, such as the Intelligent Essay Assessor (IEA) developed in the 1990 s using latent semantic analysis [40, 41]. Modern AWE systems, including E-rater, IntelliMetric, and Knowledge Analysis Technologies, analyze text across multiple linguistic layers—grammar, mechanics, discourse organization, and style—making them instrumental in large-scale assessments like TOEFL and GMAT [42–44]. E-rater, for example, combines statistical and rule-based natural language processing to evaluate features like grammatical errors, discourse coherence, and vocabulary usage, offering nuanced analysis [45–47].

Scholars highlight several advantages of AWE systems. These tools achieve assessment precision comparable to human feedback with greater speed and cost-efficiency, suitable for high-demand educational settings [7, 8]. They also foster independent learning, scaffolding student learning and promoting self-regulation during drafting, thus cultivating student autonomy and motivation [2, 48]. Cotos [49] argues AWE’s role in consistent, unbiased evaluations is essential where human assessment may vary due to subjectivity, a point reinforced by Zhai and Ma [50] regarding standardized feedback across diverse student populations.

In EFL education, AWE is invaluable for providing timely, constructive feedback, aiding effective revision [32, 51–53]. Automated Written Corrective Feedback (AWCF) specifically offers immediate revision resources, enhancing writing performance by reducing errors [24, 54]. Studies by Ranalli et al. [55], Stevenson & Phakiti [21], and Wang et al. [48] demonstrate that AWCF not only facilitates error correction but also encourages higher levels of self-directed engagement, leading to marked improvements in writing outcomes.

Grammarly, as an AWE tool, has gained attention for its effectiveness with EFL learners. Research shows its capacity to enhance specific language skills, such as grammatical accuracy, with Ebadi, Gholami, and Vakili [56] noting its effectiveness in self-correcting challenging grammatical structures. Dewi [57] found Grammarly positively impacts learners' grammar and vocabulary confidence through immediate feedback, promoting autonomous corrections. However, limitations like the cost of premium features and offline functionality affect its accessibility for some learners.

Further pedagogical exploration of Grammarly by Nova [58] highlighted its strengths in identifying mechanical errors and enhancing engagement, but also its weakness in capturing contextual nuances, which can lead to misguidance. This suggests Grammarly, while effective for lower-order concerns, does not fully substitute for teacher feedback, particularly in academic writing. Dizon and Gold [59] echoed this, showing Grammarly's feedback could reduce Foreign Language Anxiety (FLA) and foster Learner Autonomy (LA) in EFL settings, indicating automated feedback can lower anxiety and bolster confidence for independent skill refinement. This gap between AWCF's utility for lower-level mechanics and its perceived weakness in providing deep, contextual guidance reflects the broader tension between technical reliability and pedagogical value in AI-assisted assessment. For instance, Lo & Chan [9] found that while a generative AI tool demonstrated high reliability in grading, both students and instructors remained skeptical of its pedagogical value, citing a lack of personalized and meaningful guidance.

Grammarly’s integration with traditional feedback has also proven beneficial. This hybrid approach is often positioned as a solution to the reliability-value discrepancy. Thi and Nikolov [60] found that by efficiently addressing surface-level language issues, Grammarly allows teachers to focus on complex writing elements like content development and organization. This complementary approach is advantageous in high-student-ratio contexts, offering a balanced, comprehensive feedback system combining human and automated strengths. A study by Wei, Wang, and Dong [61] with Chinese EFL learners demonstrated Grammarly's significant improvement on writing dimensions such as coherence, cohesion, and grammatical accuracy. Importantly, their findings also suggest Grammarly’s impact is more pronounced among learners with higher initial proficiency and self-efficacy, underscoring individual learner characteristics' influence on AWE tool efficacy.

Technology and self-regulation

Self-regulation refers to an individual's ability to proactively monitor, control, and adapt thoughts, emotions, and behaviors to achieve personal goals [18, 33]. This multifaceted process, crucial for effective learning and growth, involves setting goals, employing strategies, maintaining motivation, and reflecting on outcomes [62, 63].

Technology integration in education has transformed self-regulated learning by offering new opportunities for skill enhancement [10, 64, 65]. Technology-based environments provide learners access to resources, interactive activities, and personalized experiences, fostering self-regulatory skill development [11, 13, 66]. These environments often include interactive components [67–69] and metacognitive support tools like progress trackers and reflection prompts [12, 70–72]. Such tools empower learners to set goals, monitor progress, and evaluate understanding, bolstering metacognitive awareness and self-regulation. Additionally, adaptive learning algorithms offer personalized feedback and recommendations, guiding effective strategy and behavior regulation [73–75].

Collaborative learning technologies, such as online discussion forums, foster social interaction and knowledge construction, promoting self-regulated learning individually and collaboratively [30, 76, 77]. In these settings, learners engage in collaborative problem-solving, peer feedback, and joint regulation, enhancing collective self-regulatory capacities [28, 78]. Mobile technologies have ushered in ubiquitous learning, enabling anytime, anywhere access to educational resources [79]. Mobile applications and augmented reality experiences offer spontaneous learning opportunities and real-world knowledge application, necessitating effective self-regulation for managing distractions and adapting strategies [80–83].

Several studies highlight the interplay between technology and self-regulation in L2 writing. Han et al. [15] demonstrated technology's positive impact on EFL academic writing, enhancing performance and self-regulated learning strategies. Similarly, Yu [84] and Bai et al. [85] showed the efficacy of automated writing evaluation software and e-learning interventions in promoting self-regulation among adult language learners and primary school students, respectively. Research by Qiu and Lee [86] and Umamah and Cahyono [87] underscored peer collaboration and online resources in fostering self-regulated writing practices for university students. Finally, Teng [88] found interactive whiteboards in collaborative writing settings enhance writing achievement, metacognitive activities, and co-regulation. In summary, the literature suggests technology holds immense potential in supporting and enhancing self-regulation in L2 writing, offering diverse resources for learners to become more self-directed and strategic in their writing development.

Directed motivational currents

Dörnyei et al. [14] introduced Directed Motivational Currents (DMCs) as a novel theory explaining strong, long-lasting motivational surges. These surges are triggered "when a variety of time and context‐related factors come together in an individual to prompt a firm decision to pursue a goal/vision which is considered personally significant, highly relevant to one's desired identity and emotionally satisfying" (p. 27). Regardless of uniqueness, all DMC experiences share three primary characteristics: goal/vision-orientedness, a prominent facilitative structure, and positive emotionality [89, 90].

A DMC is inherently goal- or vision-oriented, always progressing toward a specific objective [91, 92]. This objective can range from mastering L2 communication to completing a class assignment. While both goals and visions are directed, visions are distinct in their enhancement by vivid imagery of achieving objectives [93]. According to Dörnyei et al. [94], a clear goal or vision ensures long-term commitment, focuses energy on intended objectives, and unifies efforts. However, this vision-centric component of the DMC framework has been challenged. Al-Hoorie and Al Shlowiy [95], for instance, highlight the substantial conceptual overlap between 'vision' and the long-standing 'goal-setting' theory, questioning whether vision is an essential, distinct component or rather a powerful support mechanism for inducing and sustaining a goal-governed state. This suggests DMCs might be more practically framed as intensely goal-governed, end-oriented experiences. Furthermore, recent research cautions against a purely positive view of this intense goal focus. Sak and Gurbuz [96] identified significant negative side-effects of DMCs, including mental distress and a sense of inadequacy, which can arise from "excessive dedication towards goal achievement." These critiques add important nuance to the construct, suggesting its application requires a balanced understanding of its goal-driven nature and potential affective risks.

The second primary feature is an obvious facilitative structure [97, 98]. This distinct structure maintains motivational momentum and provides opportunities for feedback and achievement checks. Its uniqueness stems from three elements: a) individuals follow recurrent behavioral routines, creating "motivational autopilot",b) a clearly defined beginning and ending; and c) frequent progress inspections where long-term progression is broken into sub-goals for assessing progress and sustaining momentum through positive feedback [99], (Hiver & Al‐Hoorie, 2020) [100, 101].

Positive emotionality is the third DMC trait [14, 102]. Dörnyei et al. [94] state that DMC participants experience a profound pleasure distinct from ordinary enjoyable activities. This delight arises because the goal or vision is meaningful and deeply connected to the learner's identity (i.e., self-concordant). This unique sense of fulfillment contributes to an inspiring, self-propelling wave [97, 103, 104]. However, recent research adds a critical note of caution to this positive-centric view, suggesting a more holistic account is necessary. While the experience is largely positive, the intense, all-consuming nature of a DMC can also have significant negative emotional side-effects. Muir [105] and Sak and Gurbuz [96], for example, documented instances where participants experienced stress, anxiety, and mental distress as a direct result of their DMCs. This affective "dark side" is not merely incidental,recent studies show these distressing emotions can arise directly from the DMC process itself. Sak and Pietluch [106] identified sources such as unsatisfactory progress, negative feedback, and the difficulty of multitasking, which in turn led to self-efficacy doubts and a loss of motivational momentum. Similarly, Pietluch and Sak [107] found that learner well-being during a DMC is highly variable, not static, with declines often linked to the quality of feedback and the perceived adequacy of their own study routines. Thus, a complete understanding of DMCs must acknowledge this complex emotional variability.

DMCs are linked to complex dynamic systems, coordinating elements to steer behavior and overcome environmental intricacies [97, 100]. In language classrooms, a DMC can manifest as a strong motivational surge, encouraging and sustaining long-term behavior toward a cherished personal goal or vision. Scholars [94, 108] now consider DMCs optimal for engaging individuals in projects and tasks due to their motivational power.

Recent investigations offer systematic empirical data on DMCs, providing validity evidence. Muir [109] conducted a multinational study, finding 39% of participants reported motivating experiences with DMC features, and over 90% experienced extended high motivation. Muir [109] also showed that Dörnyei et al.'s [94] seven intervention templates can enable L2 DMCs through intense group projects. Muir [109] emphasizes that language learning projects with relevant purpose, measurable outputs, and explicit success criteria can create group-level DMCs. Dörnyei et al. [94] and Muir [109] offer useful frameworks for instructors to initiate group-level DMCs. This study aims to determine if the DMC construct adequately describes group-level motivating experiences in academic settings and to investigate the structure and energizing elements of such programs.

Henry et al. [89] conducted the first comprehensive empirical investigation of DMCs with three Swedish as a second language (STL2) learners. Their results confirmed learners' motivated behavior aligned with the three primary DMC components [14]. Zarrinabadi and Tavakoli [110] and Zarrinabadi et al. [92] indicated Iranian instructors and EFL students might experience high motivational states from DMC elements. Zarrinabadi et al. [92] found DMC initiation triggered by teacher remarks, family encouragement, tourist conversations, or internal factors like English TV comprehension difficulties. They also noted DMCs positively impacted participants' L2 willingness to communicate (WTC), independence, self-confidence, and self-concept. Dörnyei et al. [94] provided qualitative data supporting the construct's validity in L2 contexts. Muir [105] surveyed individuals from 71 countries, finding nearly 39% reported extended, intense motivational states with essential DMC elements.

The present study and theory of change

Drawing upon the theoretical foundations of Social Cognitive Theory and the existing literature on Automated Written Evaluation, this study aims to provide a comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the impact of AWCF on EFL learners. This investigation is guided by a specific theory of change that outlines the mechanisms by which we expect the AWCF intervention to produce its effects.

Our central hypothesis is that AWCF, when pedagogically scaffolded by a teacher, functions as a critical environmental tool [16] that fosters a cyclical development of self-regulation and self-efficacy. We propose a causal pathway that begins as learners use AWCF (Grammarly) in a hybrid model with teacher feedback. The tool provides immediate, consistent, and granular feedback on lower-order errors, a resource often unavailable in large EFL classes [2]. This immediate feedback is expected to prompt learners to "notice the gap" [111] between their current output and the target form. This awareness, in turn, triggers the self-regulatory cycle [18], scaffolding learners in monitoring (detecting errors), judging (evaluating the AWCF suggestion), and reacting (accepting or rejecting the change). We expect that this repeated, structured engagement with the revision process will improve not only Writing Accuracy but also build more robust Self-Regulation strategies. As learners successfully use the tool to identify and correct these errors, they experience repeated "mastery experiences." According to SCT, these mastery experiences are the most powerful source for building self-efficacy [17]. We expected this rising confidence would then reduce writing anxiety [59] and foster positive emotionality. Finally, this enhanced self-efficacy, combined with clear evidence of progress and a structured process (the AWCF-driven revision routine), is hypothesized to create the conditions necessary for a Directed Motivational Current (DMC) [14]. In this model, the tool helps provide the facilitative structure and positive feedback loop that can sustain long-term motivation.

To test this theory of change, we employed a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design. The quantitative phase (Phase 1) measures the outcomes of this pathway (changes in accuracy, self-regulation, and DMCs). The process-oriented qualitative phase (Phase 2), using screen recordings and stimulated recall, is essential for observing the mechanisms themselves. It provides a direct window into the cognitive and self-regulatory processes (the how and why) that explain the quantitative results. This study, therefore, addresses the following research questions:

Methods and materials

Design

A sequential explanatory mixed-methods design [112] was adopted for this study to provide a holistic and deeply contextualized understanding of AWCF's impact. Phase 1's quantitative findings established the breadth of effects, while Phase 2's qualitative inquiry, critically enhanced by process-oriented data collection, served to elucidate underlying mechanisms and capture lived experiences, thereby offering a more granular interpretation of the observed quantitative shifts in writing accuracy, self-regulation, and DMCs.

The study comprised two distinct phases. The first, a quantitative quasi-experimental phase, aimed to establish baseline data and investigate the immediate effect of AWCF. This phase utilized a pre-test-post-test, non-equivalent control group design [113] involving two intact EFL classes. One class, designated as the experimental group, received both teacher written corrective feedback (WCF) and AWCF, while the control group received only teacher WCF. The independent variable was the feedback condition (teacher WCF vs. teacher WCF + AWCF). Correspondingly, the dependent variables measured included writing accuracy, assessed through a writing task; writing self-regulation, evaluated using a validated questionnaire; and directed motivational currents (DMCs), gauged via a survey instrument designed to capture students' goal orientation, self-efficacy beliefs, and task value perceptions related to writing tasks.

Following this, the second phase involved qualitative exploration, seeking deeper insights into student experiences with AWCF and aiming to contextualize the quantitative findings. This qualitative approach specifically incorporated screen recording sessions, which were then followed by stimulated recall interviews conducted with a purposive sample of students from the experimental group. During these interviews, questions explored participants' experiences using AWCF, their perceptions of its effectiveness in improving their writing, and any potential impacts on their self-regulation and motivation throughout the writing process. This sequential design therefore ensured that a foundational understanding of AWCF's effects was established quantitatively before delving into the nuanced qualitative exploration of student experiences and the cognitive processes underpinning their engagement.

Context and participants

This study investigated the impact of AWCF on the writing performance of intermediate-level EFL learners recruited from a mandatory sophomore-level English Language Composition course at a university in China. All participants, aged 18–22 (M = 20.27, SD = 2.63), had not previously lived or studied abroad in an English-speaking environment. Their primary motivations for improving their English stemmed from a desire for employability in the globalized job market and the need to fulfill graduation requirements, often mandated by the university's English language proficiency standards.

To ensure a homogenous participant pool and comparable groups for the study, a purposive sampling strategy was employed. Students enrolled in the aforementioned English Language Composition course were first identified. Following this, participants were selected based on meeting specific criteria: classification as intermediate-level EFL learners according to a standardized English language proficiency test administered by the university and Mandarin Chinese as their native language.

From this pool, two intact classes were selected for the study, totaling 67 participants. Due to institutional scheduling, individual randomization was not feasible. Therefore, the two classes were randomly assigned as units to either the experimental group (EG, N = 33) or the control group (CG, N = 34). To control for instructor-related variables and ensure consistency in teaching methodology, the first author, an experienced EFL instructor (and thus aware of the study's purposes), taught both groups throughout the semester. The two classes met at different times, and students were not informed about the specific feedback procedures being used in the other class, which minimized the risk of contamination or compensatory rivalry.

To establish baseline comparability, the two groups were first compared on key demographic and prior proficiency variables. There was no significant difference in the gender distribution between the experimental group (15 female, 18 male) and the control group (17 female, 17 male), χ2(1, N = 67) = 0.12, p = 0.73. The groups were also equivalent in age (EG M = 20.1, SD = 2.5; CG M = 20.4, SD = 2.8; t(65) = –0.45, p = 0.65) and in their initial standardized English language proficiency test scores (EG M = 78.2, SD = 5.1; CG M = 77.5, SD = 5.5; t(65) = 0.52, p = 0.60).

Furthermore, independent samples t-tests were conducted on all study-specific pre-test measures. The results confirmed that the experimental and control groups did not differ significantly at the outset. Specifically, there were no significant differences in Overall Writing (EG M = 5.93, SD = 0.67; CG M = 5.78, SD = 0.72; t(65) = 0.88, p = 0.38), Writing Self-Regulation (EG M = 3.48, SD = 0.57; CG M = 3.39, SD = 0.49; t(65) = 0.69, p = 0.49), or DMCs (EG M = 3.87, SD = 0.57; CG M = 3.78, SD = 0.61; t(65) = 0.63, p = 0.53). T-tests on the writing subscales (Task Response, Coherence & Cohesion, Lexical Resources, and Grammatical Range & Accuracy) also yielded non-significant results (all p > 0.05). This baseline equivalence strengthens the internal validity of the study. Importantly, none of the participants had prior experience using automated writing feedback tools like Grammarly.

Prior to the start of the semester and before group assignment, all participants provided written informed consent after receiving a detailed explanation of the study's purpose, procedures, and potential risks and benefits. Participant anonymity was maintained throughout the study.

Instrumentation

This study employed a multifaceted research design to assess the impact of AWCF on student writing outcomes, self-regulation in second language writing, and directed motivational currents (DMCs) experienced by EFL learners. In addition, process-oriented qualitative data, specifically screen recordings and stimulated recall interviews, were utilized to explore the nuanced perceptions and cognitive processes of students regarding their experience of AWCF. The quantitative and qualitative instruments were selected to align directly with the study's four research questions.

Writing accuracy

To address RQ1 (Writing Accuracy), this study employed a pre-test and post-test design to gauge the participants' English academic writing skills. To ensure consistent difficulty across both assessments, topics were carefully selected and adapted from a workbook specifically designed to help learners achieve a target score on a standardized English proficiency exam (adapted from [114]).

The evaluation process leveraged the publicly available IELTS Task 2 band descriptors [115], focusing on the four analytic criteria: Task Response (TR), Coherence and Cohesion (CC), Lexical Resource (LR), and Grammatical Range and Accuracy (GRA). The first author, an experienced IELTS academic writing instructor with a history of attending relevant training workshops, marked the student essays using these descriptors. To assess inter-rater reliability and mitigate potential bias, a second experienced IELTS instructor, also well-versed in delivering academic writing courses, was invited to independently mark both the pre-test and post-test essays. To ensure a shared understanding of the criteria, both raters first participated in a two-hour calibration session. During this session, they reviewed the four analytic descriptors in detail and co-scored a set of 10 sample essays from a separate, non-participant cohort. Any disagreements in scores were discussed and resolved by referencing the official descriptor clarifications before the raters proceeded to mark the study data independently. All essays were coded, anonymized, and presented to the raters in a randomized order, ensuring that both raters were blind to participant group (experimental vs. control) and time point (pre-test vs. post-test).

Following independent marking, the instructors calculated Cohen's Kappa (κ) to assess the level of agreement between their scores. This statistic measures inter-rater reliability for categorical data, in this case, the IELTS band scores assigned to each essay. Gratifyingly, the analysis revealed a high level of agreement between the raters, with a Cohen's Kappa of 0.82, indicating almost perfect agreement according to established benchmarks. Furthermore, as the writing scores were treated as continuous variables in the ANCOVA, we also calculated an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Using a two-way random-effects model based on absolute agreement, the ICC was 0.84 (95% CI [0.78, 0.89]), indicating a high level of reliability for the continuous scores. In cases where the two raters' final overall scores differed by more than one full band, the essay was given to a third, senior rater, whose score was taken as final.

Self-regulation scale

To address RQ2 (Self-Regulation), the Writing Self-Regulation Scale (SLWS) was employed to assess students' self-regulatory strategies in writing [116]. Developed by Han and Hiver [116], the SLWS is a five-item instrument adapted from Tseng et al. [117]. In its validation study, Han and Hiver [116] reported high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.89) and confirmed its unidimensional construct validity through confirmatory factor analysis. This Likert-scale questionnaire (ranging from 1 [strongly disagree] to 5 [strongly agree]) evaluates the strategies EFL learners use to plan, organize, and manage their writing goals and processes. Sample items include, "I think about what I want to achieve before I start writing" (planning) and "I know how to reduce my stress from learning writing in English" (affective regulation). The instrument also demonstrated good internal consistency in the present study, with a Cronbach's Alpha of 0.81.

Directed Motivational Currents (DMCs)

To address RQ3 (DMCs), the DMCs experienced by students were assessed using the Chinese version of the DMC Disposition Questionnaire, designed and validated by Muir [109]. To ensure linguistic and cultural appropriateness for the Chinese EFL context, a standard translation and back-translation procedure was followed. The original English questionnaire was translated into Mandarin Chinese by one bilingual researcher and then independently back-translated into English by another bilingual researcher. The two researchers then met to compare the original and back-translated versions, resolving any minor discrepancies in wording to ensure the conceptual equivalence of the items. Following this, the final Chinese version was pilot-tested with a small group of sophomores. This pilot phase evaluated the questionnaire's clarity, accuracy, and its ability to effectively measure their experiences.

The questionnaire itself comprised four sections. The first section, "Background Information" (7 items), gathered basic demographic data about the participants. The "DMC Experiences" Sect. (6 items) delved deeper, examining the frequency, duration, and intensity of the participants' DMCs related to English language learning. Items 1–3 within this section utilized a five-point Likert scale ranging from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree." Item 4 specifically inquired about the frequency of their DMC experiences, Item 5 addressed the duration, and Item 6 assessed the motivational intensity associated with these experiences.

The core of the instrument resided in the "Main Questionnaire" (15 items). This section, which included items such as "I remember thinking about my goal all the time," prompted participants to rate the intensity and duration of their DMC experiences across various situations using the same five-point Likert scale (Items 7–19, except Item 13). Item 13 served a distinct purpose, asking participants to self-assess their perceived English language proficiency during these DMC experiences. Finally, the "Additional Information" Sect. (1 item) assessed participants' willingness to experience DMCs again and whether they had observed others experiencing them. The questionnaire demonstrated a high degree of reliability within the instrument, with a Cronbach's Alpha of 0.86, indicating good internal consistency.

Semi-structured interviews and stimulated recall

To address RQ4 (Learner Experiences), and to gain richer, process-oriented insights into student experiences using AWCF, screen recordings of their writing and revision sessions were captured, followed by stimulated recall interviews with a purposive sample of nine students from the experimental group. These students volunteered for the interviews and were then randomly chosen from the pool of volunteers. This approach ensured a range of perspectives within the experimental group while providing a direct, observable window into their cognitive engagement and decision-making during the revision process.

The interview protocol consisted of open-ended and probing questions (see Appendix A) designed to explore the participants' perceptions of their experiences using AWCF. During the stimulated recall sessions, the interviewer played back segments of the participant's own screen recording of their writing and revision process with Grammarly. Participants were prompted to verbalize their thoughts, feelings, and decisions at specific points (e.g., when a particular error was flagged, when they accepted/rejected a suggestion, or when they paused to consider feedback). This technique, based on the principles of stimulated recall [118], aimed to elicit more accurate and detailed reflections on their real-time cognitive and emotional states, thereby minimizing retrospective recall bias. Recognizing that participants might be more comfortable expressing themselves in certain situations, the interviewer adopted a flexible approach [119], allowing participants to use either English or their L1 (Mandarin Chinese) to elaborate on their responses, particularly for nuanced or complex ideas. However, the primary language of the interview remained English to maintain focus and consistency. All interviews were audio-recorded and later transcribed verbatim, with relevant screen recording segments timestamped for analysis.

Grammarly

To deliver enhanced AWCF to the experimental group, this study employed Grammarly Premium, a paid subscription tier of the popular online writing platform (https://www.grammarly.com/). Grammarly Premium leverages artificial intelligence (AI) to provide comprehensive feedback that extends beyond the basic mechanics addressed in the free version.

The decision to utilize Grammarly Premium stemmed from its advanced features, which directly align with the research objectives. Unlike the free version, Grammarly Premium offers a more robust error detection system, identifying and correcting a wider range of grammatical mistakes encompassing subject-verb agreement, tense inconsistencies, and stylistic improvements. This in-depth analysis goes beyond basic mechanics to include vocabulary enhancement suggestions. Grammarly Premium helps students refine their writing style by recommending impactful synonyms and phrases to replace overused or imprecise vocabulary. Furthermore, Grammarly Premium delves deeper into readability and engagement through clarity and engagement checks. It identifies areas where sentences can be simplified for improved understanding or suggests rephrasing to maintain reader interest. The tone detection feature in Grammarly Premium is particularly valuable for EFL learners who may struggle with nuanced language styles. It helps students ensure their writing conveys the intended formality and aligns with the target audience. Finally, Grammarly Premium offers a more comprehensive plagiarism checker compared to the free version, providing peace of mind for both students and instructors. In essence, the advanced features of Grammarly Premium facilitate a more in-depth analysis of AWCF's influence on EFL learners' writing abilities, encompassing vocabulary development, sentence clarity, and overall writing style, which aligns perfectly with the research objectives of investigating the impact of AWCF beyond basic mechanics.

Data collection

Experimental group

The experimental group's procedure was designed as a hybrid feedback model. This approach, which combines AWCF with teacher feedback, was chosen based on evidence from the field (e.g., [60, 120]). Our rationale was that this hybrid workflow allows AWCF to act as a "first pass" on lower-order mechanical issues. This, in turn, frees the human instructor to focus on more complex, higher-order concerns like content and organization. This model is intended to preserve the technical gains of AWCF while enhancing the perceived pedagogical quality and motivation that comes from teacher feedback.

To initiate the semester, the teacher dedicated a 40-min computer lab session in Week 1 to introduce Grammarly, the chosen AWCF tool. This session began with a brief overview of AWCF technology and its potential benefits for improving writing. The teacher then transitioned to a hands-on demonstration, navigating the Grammarly interface and highlighting key functionalities. This included showcasing how Grammarly identifies and categorizes mechanical errors related to spelling, grammar, and punctuation. To solidify understanding, students participated in individual exercises where they used Grammarly on pre-loaded computers to identify and categorize errors in anonymized student essays, replicating the teacher's demonstration. The teacher circulated the lab throughout this exercise, offering guidance and answering any questions that arose.

Throughout the semester, three in-class writing tasks were assigned, spaced four weeks apart (Weeks 3, 7, and 11). Each task focused on a specific writing genre or theme relevant to the course curriculum. Students were allocated 40 min to complete these writing assignments in class, ensuring a controlled environment and minimizing external influences on their writing process. Once the writing time concluded, students electronically submitted their essays directly to Grammarly through a secure online portal. This facilitated immediate automated feedback on their work.

The following 20 min were dedicated to a student-led revision cycle. Students reviewed the Grammarly feedback, focusing on suggestions related to spelling, grammar, and punctuation. They then electronically revised their essays, incorporating the AWCF suggestions where applicable. The teacher's role during this phase was as a facilitator, not a corrector. The teacher circulated the lab to provide technical support (e.g., logging in) and answer students' clarification questions about why Grammarly flagged an error (e.g., 'What does "comma splice" mean?'), but did not provide direct feedback on the writing content itself. After this initial revision based on AWCF, students electronically submitted their revised essays to the teacher for further feedback. The teacher 's subsequent written feedback deliberately focused on providing comprehensive written feedback encompassing content, organization, clarity of ideas, and higher-level writing aspects that go beyond mechanics. The teacher aimed to spend approximately 20 min per essay on this comprehensive feedback, ensuring a consistent level of attention for each student's work.

Control group

To ensure methodological equivalence and control for the potential effects of feedback-related training, the control group also received a 40-min training session in Week 1. This session, led by the same instructor, focused on the principles of effective revision and traditional feedback literacy. The instructor provided an overview of the different types of teacher-based feedback they would receive (e.g., direct correction on mechanical errors vs. indirect comments on higher-order concerns like 'content' and 'organization'). Using anonymized sample essays from a previous course marked with handwritten comments, the teacher demonstrated how to interpret and act upon these different types of feedback. Students then participated in a brief exercise identifying and categorizing different types of teacher comments and proposing revisions.

The control group followed a similar writing schedule throughout the semester. They completed three in-class writing tasks spaced four weeks apart (Weeks 3, 7, and 11), mirroring the experimental group's schedule. Each task received the teacher's full attention, with students allocating 40 min to complete their essays in class. Upon finishing, students directly submitted their work to the teacher for comprehensive written feedback. In line with their training, this feedback was provided via written comments that explicitly identified both mechanical aspects (spelling, grammar, and punctuation) and higher-level writing considerations (content, organization, and clarity of ideas). The teacher aimed to dedicate approximately 40 min per essay to ensure thorough review. Students then revised their essays based on the teacher's feedback before the next class session.

To ensure both groups received a well-rounded writing experience beyond the specific interventions, they participated in various in-class activities led by the teacher throughout the semester. These common activities typically lasted 20 min per session and included lectures on specific writing techniques relevant to the course curriculum. Examples included crafting thesis statements, structuring paragraphs, and utilizing effective transitions. Additionally, structured peer review sessions were incorporated, where students exchanged essays in randomly assigned pairs or small groups to provide constructive feedback on content, organization, and clarity. Interactive class discussions on various writing topics were also held, fostering critical thinking about writing strategies, analyzing writing styles, and learning from each other's experiences.

During the final week of the course (Week 12), all participants, including the control group, completed a post-test writing task electronically. This task mirrored the pre-test format, requiring them to write a persuasive essay on a new topic to assess their writing development. These anonymized essays were stored for later analysis by a research assistant. Professor Li then reviewed all documents to ensure accuracy, finalizing the data collection process. This ensured a consistent approach to data collection across both groups.

Process data collection

For the qualitative phase, a subset of the experimental group (N = 9, the same participants selected for semi-structured interviews) had their computer screens recorded during one of the in-class writing and revision sessions (specifically, the Week 7 writing task). These recordings captured their real-time interactions with the writing task, their drafting process, and their engagement with Grammarly's feedback interface. To ensure ethical considerations and participant comfort, students were fully informed about the screen recording and provided explicit consent prior to the session. These screen recordings served as the primary stimulus for the subsequent stimulated recall interviews, allowing for a detailed, moment-by-moment analysis of their writing and revision behaviors in response to AWCF.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the characteristics of the sample and the pre-test and post-test scores on the writing performance, self-regulation, and DMCs measures. These statistics included means (M) and standard deviations (SD) for all variables. To assess within-group changes in writing performance, self-regulation, and DMCs between pre-test and post-test scores, paired-samples t-tests were conducted. Furthermore, to explore the main effect of the intervention (AWCF) on post-test outcomes while controlling for baseline differences, one-way ANCOVAs were conducted. To ensure a precise analysis of group differences, the term "prior writing experience" used in our initial reporting referred specifically to the pre-test score of the dependent variable in question. For each ANCOVA, the respective pre-test score (e.g., pre-test Overall Writing for the post-test Overall Writing analysis; pre-test Self-Regulation for the post-test Self-Regulation analysis) was used as the covariate. This statistical technique allows researchers to isolate the impact of the independent variable (AWCF) on the dependent variable… by statistically accounting for the influence of the baseline covariate [121].

Thematic analysis was employed to analyze the qualitative data collected from the semi-structured interviews and the accompanying stimulated recall verbalizations. This qualitative research method, following the systematic process outlined by Braun & Clarke [122], involved iterative coding of the interview transcripts and verbalizations. To ensure reliability, the first author and a research assistant trained in qualitative methods served as coders. An initial codebook was developed based on the research questions and a subset of the data. Both coders then independently coded 25% of the transcripts, achieving a strong inter-coder agreement (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.87). All discrepancies were resolved through discussion to refine the codebook, which the first author then used to code the remaining data. The analysis focused on identifying patterns (themes) related to AWCF's strengths, challenges, suggestions for improvement, and its perceived impact on learning, with particular attention to observable self-regulatory behaviors, real-time decision-making processes, and emotional responses as evidenced in the synchronized screen recordings and verbal reports. Several steps were taken to enhance credibility [119]: (a) findings were triangulated by systematically comparing participants’ interview statements with their observable actions captured in the screen recordings,(b) the primary researcher maintained a reflexive journal to bracket assumptions and document analytical decisions; and (c) member checking was conducted at the end of each stimulated recall interview, where the researcher summarized the key interpretations and asked the participant to verify their accuracy. These codes were then grouped into broader themes to capture the essence of student experiences with the intervention and to provide deeper explanatory power for the quantitative findings (See Appendix B for a summary table of themes and representative quotations).

Findings

This section presents the study's findings, beginning with the quantitative analysis of writing performance, self-regulation, and Directed Motivational Currents (DMCs). This is followed by a qualitative exploration of student perceptions and experiences with Automated Written Corrective Feedback (AWCF) delivered via Grammarly, critically informed by process-oriented data from stimulated recall interviews.

Qualitative results

A descriptive analysis of the screen recordings from the nine participants provided a quantitative snapshot of their engagement with the tool. On average, learners in this subset received 42.5 (SD = 11.2) suggestions from Grammarly on their first draft. They spent an average of 14.8 min (SD = 4.1) engaged in the AWCF-led revision cycle. The overall acceptance rate for suggestions was high (M = 81.4%), though this was much higher for mechanical errors like spelling and punctuation (95.2%) than for stylistic or clarity suggestions (55.3%). This high engagement with mechanical fixes helps explain the significant quantitative gains in grammatical accuracy, while the lower acceptance of stylistic suggestions aligns with the qualitative themes of cognitive overload and lack of trust in higher-order feedback.

The qualitative analysis of student experiences with the AWCF intervention, delivered through Grammarly, yielded rich data that complements and contextualizes the quantitative findings. The integration of screen recordings with stimulated recall interviews provided a granular, process-oriented view of learners' real-time interactions and cognitive engagement, offering critical explanatory power for the observed quantitative results. This section explores student perceptions regarding AWCF's strengths, encountered challenges, recommendations for improvement, and its reported impact on their learning.

Perceived advantages of AWCF

Students consistently identified several positive aspects of AWCF, often detailing specific moments of insight captured during stimulated recall. The immediacy and specificity of feedback were frequently cited as transformative. Li Wei, a meticulous writer, when reviewing his screen recording, visibly paused on a complex sentence where Grammarly had suggested a concise rephrasing. He remarked: "Grammarly felt like having a personal grammar tutor by my side. It identified even minor errors, helping me refine my writing to a higher level. Seeing the correction right there, and then hearing myself think aloud about why it was better, made the learning stick and improved my self-monitoring." For others, the in-context suggestions were particularly valuable. Wang Hao, who found traditional grammar lessons dry, shared, while watching his cursor hover over a highlighted grammatical error and then click Grammarly's detailed explanation: "Grammarly wasn't just about corrections; it explained the grammar rules behind them. I could see myself hesitating, then clicking the 'more info' button. This helped me understand and apply them more effectively in the future, rather than just memorizing rules." The ease of integration with their workflow also contributed to sustained engagement. As Zhang Ling, a busy student, noted, pointing to her seamless navigation between her word processor and Grammarly's suggestions, with minimal clicks or delays: "Having Grammarly integrated with my writing software meant I could get instant feedback, without uploading documents or waiting for reviews. It felt like a natural, uninterrupted part of my drafting process, fostering a sense of efficiency." These comments, supported by direct observation of their interaction patterns, highlight AWCF's accessibility, instructional value, and seamless integration into the writing process.

Challenges encountered during AWCF Use

While generally well-received, AWCF presented some notable challenges, which were often vividly illustrated in the screen recordings and articulated during stimulated recall. A common concern was information overload, particularly when a large number of errors were flagged. Ma Kai, a visual learner, commented while reviewing a section of his essay with numerous Grammarly suggestions, his screen recording showing rapid, disengaged scrolling through the highlighted errors: "Sometimes Grammarly would highlight so much that it felt overwhelming. You can see here [pointing to the screen] I just scrolled past a lot of them. Prioritizing critical errors or offering them step-by-step would be helpful, so I don't get lost and become demotivated." This observable behavior directly corroborated his verbal report of feeling overwhelmed. A few students desired more nuanced feedback beyond basic grammar correction. For instance, Chen Jie, a creative writer, expressed, after reviewing a passage where Grammarly had suggested a grammatically correct but stylistically bland alternative, her screen recording showing her re-typing the sentence several times without accepting Grammarly's suggestion: "While catching typos and comma splices is important, I would have liked Grammarly to suggest alternative phrasing or comment on the overall flow of my writing. Here, it corrected a comma, but it didn't tell me if my sentence was too long or if there was a more impactful way to phrase my idea for a persuasive essay." These challenges, grounded in specific interaction instances and cognitive processes, point to a need for more adaptive and comprehensive feedback mechanisms.

Recommendations for improvement

Students offered valuable suggestions for refining AWCF in the future, often stemming directly from their observed interactions and frustrations. The development of a customizable feedback profile was a recurring recommendation. As Sun Yi, who felt overwhelmed by technical terms, suggested, after her screen recording showed her consistently dismissing advanced grammar suggestions that she deemed irrelevant to her immediate learning goals: "A feature that allows you to choose the formality level or the types of errors flagged would be great. That way, I could focus on the aspects most relevant to my specific writing task. I only want to see the basics when I'm drafting, not all the complex stylistic notes that distract me." Offering optional resources or tutorials linked to specific grammar or mechanics rules within the feedback itself was seen as another way to enhance learning. According to Li Jing, whose screen recording visibly showed her opening multiple external browser tabs to research a grammar point flagged by Grammarly, interrupting her revision flow: "Grammarly could be even more helpful if it provided explanations or exercises related to the flagged errors right there. This would allow me to learn from my mistakes and improve my understanding without breaking my concentration." Finally, some students suggested the possibility of integrating AWCF with plagiarism detection tools to provide a more comprehensive feedback package.

Self-reported impact on learning and motivational dynamics

Students overwhelmingly reported a positive impact of AWCF on their writing, with stimulated recall providing granular insights into the cognitive and emotional processes involved. Increased awareness of their grammatical strengths and weaknesses was a consistent theme. Li Wei (from point 4.2.1) shared, reflecting on his repeated engagement with specific error types, his screen recording showing him consistently focusing his revision efforts on recurrent errors identified by Grammarly: "Grammarly helped me identify areas where I make repetitive mistakes. I could see in the recording how often I made the same error, and then I focused on that specific rule during my revision, which felt very self-directed." Beyond basic mechanics, AWCF was credited with fostering a sense of confidence in their writing abilities, providing qualitative depth to the quantitative trend observed in self-regulation. As Huang Mei, another student, stated, her voice reflecting satisfaction as she watched herself confidently accept a complex correction, her typing speed visibly increasing thereafter: "I used to be insecure about my writing, but Grammarly's constant feedback made me feel more confident in my ability to communicate clearly and effectively. Each successful correction felt like a small win, building my belief that I could improve, which made me want to keep writing."

While the ANCOVA for DMCs did not yield a statistically significant differential impact, the stimulated recall interviews provided anecdotal evidence of micro-level motivational surges aligning with DMC characteristics. For instance, Chen Jie described a palpable "surge of accomplishment" after successfully resolving a particularly challenging grammatical error flagged by Grammarly, stating, "It felt like a small victory that pushed me to keep going, almost like a mini-mission accomplished." This aligns with the "positive emotionality" component of DMCs [103, 104] and suggests that while AWCF may not trigger large-scale, sustained DMCs for all learners, it can contribute to momentary motivational boosts that reinforce engagement. These perceptions, grounded in observed behaviors and immediate reflections, highlight AWCF's role not just as a corrective tool, but as a confidence-building mechanism that promotes active engagement and a positive learning trajectory, aligning with SCT's emphasis on self-efficacy and the iterative nature of self-regulation.

These qualitative findings, substantially enriched by the process-oriented data from screen recordings and stimulated recall, provide valuable insights into the student experience with AWCF. By understanding both the perceived advantages and the encountered challenges at a micro-level of cognitive and behavioral interaction, educators can explore more targeted strategies to maximize the effectiveness of Grammarly or similar writing assistant tools in future implementations. This can include tailoring instruction to address student needs (e.g., prioritizing errors or offering visual aids), or integrating AWCF with other learning resources to provide a more comprehensive learning experience, all informed by a deeper understanding of learners' dynamic cognitive processes during feedback engagement.

Discussion

This study examined the effects of Automated Written Corrective Feedback (AWCF), delivered via Grammarly, on EFL learners' writing accuracy, self-regulation, and Directed Motivational Currents (DMCs). The findings indicate that AWCF significantly enhances both writing performance and self-regulatory behaviors, with qualitative process data providing critical insights into the mechanisms underlying these effects.

Quantitatively, the results demonstrated that the experimental group receiving AWCF showed a significantly greater improvement in overall writing performance than the control group. This enhancement was evident across multiple dimensions of writing, including task response, coherence and cohesion, lexical resources, and grammatical range and accuracy. These improvements align closely with previous research on AWE tools’ efficacy in improving writing accuracy [7, 24, 51]. The significant gains observed in grammatical range and accuracy can be attributed to Grammarly’s capacity for immediate and detailed feedback on language mechanics. As revealed in stimulated recall interviews, learners frequently verbalized their recognition of error patterns and their active decision-making processes in rectifying these errors in real-time. This immediate feedback, observed directly in screen recordings as students paused, reflected, and applied corrections, fosters iterative learning by allowing students to apply corrections in subsequent tasks, which contributes to long-term retention of language structures [2, 20, 21, 48].

Further supporting these findings, improvements in coherence and cohesion suggest that AWCF also positively influences higher-order writing skills. Although Grammarly’s primary focus is grammar and mechanics, the tool’s suggestions for sentence structure and word choice may have contributed to enhanced textual organization and flow, extending Dong and Shi’s [123] work, which observed that AWCF can support students in integrating sources effectively and thereby aid discourse-level skill development. However, qualitative data, particularly from stimulated recall, revealed that some students actively sought more nuanced feedback on content and stylistic elements, sometimes expressing frustration when Grammarly's suggestions felt too prescriptive or lacked contextual understanding. This highlights a limitation in AWCF’s ability to address complex rhetorical and contextual aspects of writing, aligning with Nova [58] and Li and Li’s [124] findings, which noted that AWCF, while effective for lower-level concerns, cannot fully replace teacher feedback on higher-order writing skills. Integrating AWCF with traditional instructor feedback may, therefore, offer a more comprehensive approach to writing instruction, leveraging automated precision alongside human insight [32, 60].

Beyond writing performance, the study observed a significant increase in self-reported self-regulation strategies among students using AWCF, consistent with Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), which emphasizes self-regulation as critical to learning [18, 19]. Grammarly’s immediate feedback likely facilitated observable self-monitoring and self-evaluation, as evidenced by students' frequent pauses and re-readings of flagged sections in their screen recordings. These behaviors are essential to self-regulated learning [33, 70]. By enabling students to independently identify errors and make corrections, AWCF promotes autonomy and active engagement in the writing process [65, 66]. Stimulated recall further revealed how the ability to receive and respond to immediate feedback encouraged students to set specific, immediate goals (e.g., "fix all comma errors"), monitor their progress through Grammarly's interface, and adjust their strategies in real-time (e.g., re-phrasing a sentence multiple times until Grammarly indicated no errors) [63, 85]. This process of reflection and adjustment, captured in the moment, aligns with SCT’s iterative approach to self-regulation [10, 12]. Qualitatively, students reported feeling more confident and motivated in their writing endeavors, which aligns with Bandura’s [17] assertion that increased self-efficacy promotes persistence and effort. The positive emotional responses—such as feelings of accomplishment and reduced anxiety—verbally articulated during stimulated recall after successful corrections, may have further reinforced their commitment to self-regulation [59, 91].

The study also found that AWCF may influence students’ DMCs, or intense, long-term motivational drives toward significant goals, with both the experimental and control groups reporting increases in DMCs; however, the effect size was larger for the experimental group. DMCs are known for their long-term motivational surges, often fueled by goal/vision-orientedness, structure, and positive emotionality [14, 109]. While the ANCOVA results did not show a statistically significant differential impact on DMCs between groups after controlling for their baseline DMCs scores, the qualitative data from stimulated recall provided nuanced insights into how AWCF might contribute to elements of DMCs at a micro-level. The immediate, personalized feedback from Grammarly may have contributed to the emergence of "micro-DMCs" or momentary motivational boosts by providing clear indicators of progress and achievement [89, 99]. For instance, participants frequently described a "surge of accomplishment" or "mini-mission accomplished" feeling upon successfully resolving a complex error flagged by Grammarly, which then propelled them to tackle the next correction. This alignment of AWCF with students’ personal goals for language proficiency appears to enhance the goal/vision-oriented component of DMCs [94, 98]. As students observed improvements in their writing, their motivation to pursue further advancement likely intensified, establishing a self-sustaining motivational cycle [90, 116]. Additionally, AWCF’s structured nature aligns with DMC’s facilitative structure, which emphasizes regular checkpoints and feedback to maintain momentum [97, 100]. The positive emotionality associated with overcoming challenges through AWCF, directly reported during stimulated recall moments, may further amplify DMCs’ intensity [103, 104].

However, despite these observed benefits and qualitative indicators, the ANCOVA results indicated that the differences in DMCs between groups were not statistically significant after controlling for baseline DMCs. This finding reflects a tension common in AWE research, where tools can be technically accurate yet perceived as 'pedagogically thin' [9]. Our qualitative process data provide a plausible explanation for this gap. As screen recordings revealed, some students experienced cognitive overload from the sheer volume of feedback, causing them to scroll past dense flagging (as noted by Ma Kai). Others, like Chen Jie, resisted stylistically 'flattening' suggestions, perceiving them as unhelpful for the persuasive task. This suggests that while the tool improved technical accuracy, its failure to align with deeper rhetorical goals or its overwhelming presentation may have limited its impact on sustained, personal motivation (DMCs). Thus, AWCF’s influence on motivational currents may be mediated by factors such as perceived pedagogical value and cognitive load, as well as individual differences and prior exposure to technology-based feedback [101, 125]). This indicates the need for further research to explore the interactions among AWCF, self-regulation, and sustained motivation across diverse learner profiles, potentially employing longitudinal qualitative methods to track the evolution of DMCs over extended periods.

Interpreting these findings through SCT highlights how AWCF functions as an environmental tool dynamically interacting with learners’ cognitive processes, such as self-efficacy and self-regulation, which then shape their behaviors in writing tasks. SCT’s concept of reciprocal determinism posits that personal factors, behavior, and environmental influences interact dynamically to shape learning outcomes [16]. The observed increase in self-regulation strategies, corroborated by real-time behavioral shifts and verbalized metacognitive awareness in stimulated recall, strongly supports SCT’s emphasis on self-regulatory skills developed through feedback and interaction [18, 22]. Additionally, the confidence students reported, often linked directly to successful corrections observed in their screen recordings, corresponds with SCT’s assertion that mastery experiences and positive feedback enhance self-efficacy [17, 23]. As students observed improvements in accuracy, their confidence in their writing abilities increased, motivating them to engage more deeply with writing tasks. SCT’s observational learning component may also have played a role, as students likely benefited from sharing AWCF experiences or observing peers use it effectively [28, 30].

This study contributes significantly to the expanding body of literature on AWCF efficacy in language learning. Aligning with Ngo et al. [7] and Zhang [8], the findings support that AWCF can significantly improve EFL learners’ writing quality. Critically, this research extends past studies by demonstrating AWCF’s effects on linguistic accuracy and self-regulatory behaviors, providing a novel, process-oriented perspective on how these effects manifest through real-time learner-tool interaction. Qualitative findings, enriched by direct observations and immediate verbalizations from stimulated recall, align with the observations of Ebadi et al. [56] and Dewi [57], in which students found AWCF beneficial for identifying errors and building confidence. Challenges highlighted by students, such as the need for nuanced feedback and the risk of information overload, substantiated by observable disengagement patterns in screen recordings, echo the concerns of Nova [58] and Li and Li [124] regarding AWCF’s limitations in higher-order feedback. This underscores the crucial importance of integrating AWCF with teacher feedback, as suggested by Thi and Nikolov [60] and Wang et al. [48]. By combining AWCF’s efficiency and teacher feedback’s contextual nuance, educators can offer a more holistic writing instruction approach that enhances both mechanical accuracy and stylistic competence, informed by a deeper understanding of learners' dynamic engagement with automated feedback.

Conclusion

This study provides robust evidence that AWCF, delivered via Grammarly, significantly enhances EFL learners' writing accuracy and self-regulatory strategies. The positive impact on writing performance supports AWCF's integration into language instruction as a valuable tool for immediate, individualized feedback. Aligning with Social Cognitive Theory, these findings underscore the dynamic interplay between cognitive processes, behavior, and environmental factors in language learning. Crucially, by incorporating screen recordings and stimulated recall, this study moved beyond surface-level observations to elucidate the mechanisms through which AWCF influences writing development, offering a granular understanding of learners' real-time engagement and decision-making. This contribution deepens our understanding of technology-enhanced language learning. The enhancement of self-regulation and the potential stimulation of DMCs suggest AWCF can play a multifaceted role in supporting learners' academic growth. Future research should continue to explore AWCF's long-term effects and its integration with pedagogical practices to optimize its impact on EFL learners.

Implications

Building on the debate about AWCF's effectiveness in L2 writing instruction, this study offers fresh evidence for its potential to propel EFL learners' writing beyond basic grammar correction. By delivering immediate, targeted feedback and fostering learner autonomy, AWCF can significantly enhance both the cognitive and metacognitive processes involved in writing. However, our findings of cognitive overload and resistance to stylistic feedback suggest that how AWCF is integrated is paramount, translating into practical applications that can transform the L2 writing classroom.

Based on these data, we propose a hybrid feedback protocol that leverages the complementary strengths of AWCF and human instruction, similar to models found effective in other EFL contexts (e.g., [60, 120]). Educators can leverage AWCF tools like Grammarly to provide a 'triage' for mechanical issues and sentence-level clarity. This targeted approach frees up valuable teacher time, allowing educators to shift their focus to 'feedforward' on higher-order concerns such as argumentation, content development, organization, and audience awareness. This hybrid model directly addresses the cognitive load issues observed in our screen recordings, as students would not be overwhelmed by simultaneous feedback on all aspects of their writing (e.g., [60]).

Furthermore, this protocol must be paired with explicit feedback literacy training. Stimulated recall interviews revealed that students struggled to prioritize suggestions or assess the tool's limitations. To address this, educators should include brief activities that teach students how to interpret and prioritize high-impact AWCF suggestions (e.g., systemic errors) versus lower-impact stylistic flags, and, critically, to recognize when to seek human guidance. This step is essential for transparent communication about the tool's role and empowers students to take ownership of their learning journey by actively and strategically engaging with feedback. By analyzing flagged errors and explanations, students can identify recurring mistakes and target specific grammar rules for focused study. This not only improves writing skills but also instills self-efficacy and a growth mindset towards language learning.

The implications extend beyond grammar and mechanics. Some AWCF tools provide feedback on vocabulary usage, suggesting synonyms or more nuanced word choices, prompting students to explore richer language and elevate writing sophistication. Additionally, with customization, AWCF can be tailored to address specific writing styles or academic conventions, such as flagging informal language for research papers while encouraging creativity in other assignments.

In essence, this study strengthens the case for integrating AWCF into the L2 writing classroom. However, AWCF is a tool, not a panacea; its effectiveness hinges on a thoughtful, structured pedagogical protocol and ongoing teacher guidance to ensure students can effectively utilize the feedback and navigate writing complexities, as highlighted by the cognitive load and contextual needs identified in our process-oriented data.

Limitations and future directions

While this study sheds light on AWCF's significant potential benefits for EFL learners, it also acknowledges limitations that pave the way for future research. One limitation lies in its scope: conducted in a single academic setting with a specific intermediate-level EFL learner population, the findings' generalizability requires further investigation. Future research should explore AWCF's effectiveness across diverse educational contexts, such as public schools, private language institutes, or online platforms. Additionally, examining its impact on learners with varying proficiency levels, learning styles, and cultural backgrounds will provide a more comprehensive picture of AWCF's utility. Another limitation pertains to the intervention's duration: this study employed a relatively short period. While positive short-term effects on writing performance were demonstrated, the sustainability of these improvements remains unclear. Longitudinal studies tracking learner progress over extended periods can offer valuable insights into AWCF's long-term impact on writing development, assessing whether initial gains translate into lasting improvements in writing proficiency.

Another key limitation is that this study did not include a quantitative measure of students' perceived feedback quality. While our qualitative interviews and stimulated recalls provided rich, process-oriented data on this topic (e.g., cognitive overload, trust in feedback), we did not administer a perception questionnaire. Future research would benefit from including a validated instrument to systematically measure perceptions of usefulness, specificity, contextuality, and trust [120]. This would allow for a statistical analysis to examine if and how perceived pedagogical value mediates or moderates the impact of AWCF on self-regulation and motivation.

Relatedly, the study design involved a difference in the time and focus of teacher feedback. The control group received approximately 40 min of comprehensive teacher feedback covering both mechanics and higher-order concerns, while the experimental group received approximately 20 min of teacher feedback after their AWCF revision. We acknowledge this difference in instructor time-on-task as a potential limitation. We sought to minimize this bias by design: the teacher's 20 min for the experimental group were focused exclusively on higher-order skills (e.g., content, organization), as AWCF had already addressed most surface-level errors. This approach reflects a real-world pedagogical advantage where AWCF frees instructors to focus on more complex writing elements [60]. Nonetheless, the different nature and duration of the human feedback is a variable that cannot be fully discounted. Future studies could employ a design that holds teacher feedback time constant to better isolate the specific contribution of AWCF.

Furthermore, the study primarily focused on the cognitive aspects of writing, investigating improvements in grammar, vocabulary, and overall mechanics. However, effective writing encompasses more than technical correctness, including style, content organization, and audience/purpose tailoring. Future research can delve deeper into AWCF's influence on these higher-order writing skills. While our stimulated recall data provided initial insights into learners' engagement with higher-order suggestions, a dedicated focus employing more targeted think-aloud protocols or specific prompts during screen recording sessions could further illuminate how students utilize AWCF feedback to refine not just mechanics, but also the overall flow, persuasiveness, and rhetorical effectiveness of their writing.

Finally, the study acknowledges the limitations of the specific AWCF tool used (Grammarly). While Grammarly offers various feedback features, its primary focus remains on surface-level errors. Future research can explore the effectiveness of AWCF tools with more nuanced feedback capabilities, such as suggestions for sentence structure variation, context-specific vocabulary choice, or rudimentary feedback on content organization and logical flow. Investigating the impact of such enhanced AWCF tools can provide valuable insights into their potential to address a wider range of writing challenges faced by EFL learners. Moreover, future studies could leverage the process-oriented methodology to compare how learners interact with different types of AWCF (e.g., rule-based vs. AI-driven generative feedback) and how these distinct feedback modalities influence their self-regulatory strategies and motivational states in real-time. This would further advance our understanding of optimal AWCF design and implementation.
