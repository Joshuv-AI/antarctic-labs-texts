The impact of tropical cyclones Pam, Harold, Winston and Yasa on tree cover loss in Vanuatu and Fiji
Authors: Nicholas Metherall, Elisabeth Holland, Moleni Tu’uholoaki, Sara Beavis, Salote Nasalo, Ceceilia Carol Louis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Many Pacific Small Island Developing States are vulnerable to Tropical Cyclones (TCs) leading to an estimated average annual loss of USD 1.08 billion. The study quantifies the impacts of tropical cyclones on tree cover and associated ecosystem services, beginning with coastal protection and the loss of carbon, for inclusion in Post Disaster Needs Assessment (PDNAs), Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), catastrophe risk insurance payments and loss and damage accounting. The study focuses on the impacts of tree cover loss resulting from four separate category five tropical cyclones in Fiji and Vanuatu: Pam, Harold, Winston and Yasa. Compared to national average annual tree cover losses between 2000 and 2023, TCs Pam and Harold increased tree cover loss 4.6 and 5.2-fold in Vanuatu and TCs Winston and Yasa increased tree cover loss 3.6 and 3.1-fold in Fiji, respectively. The resulting loss of carbon storage adds an estimated 23.4–25.0% in economic losses based on IPCC Tier II emissions factors and 37.2% for IPCC Tier I emissions factors to the Vanuatu and Fiji PDNA economic loss estimates, respectively. The focus on carbon emissions is a first step towards a quantification of the loss of ecosystem services in countries whose people depend on natural resources for daily subsistence. The study makes a case for inclusion of environmental damage in both PDNA and loss and damage estimates to justify additional financial investments in disaster recovery.

Impacts of tropical cyclones in Pacific small island developing states

Tropical cyclones (TCs) in the Pacific Ocean frequently pass over the non-equatorial Pacific islands, including the 14 Pacific Small Island Developing States (PSIDS) which are also member nations of the Pacific Islands Forum. A total of 5878 TCs have been documented in the Pacific Ocean Basin between January 1897 and April 2024, representing 44.18% of the global total (13,305)1. In the same period, within the South Pacific Ocean Basin specifically, 1220 TCs have occurred representing 9.27% of the global total1. These TC events have contributed to significant economic loss and damage. An estimated USD 1.08 billion is lost annually to TCs in PSIDS, constituting nearly 5% of their combined gross domestic product (GDP)2. According to UNESCAP, TCs account for 49.6% (USD 533.2 million) of the total average annual losses from natural hazards across PSIDS. followed by losses due to droughts, earthquakes, floods and tsunamis21. Furthermore, many PSIDS are economically vulnerable due to undiversified economies3. In PSIDS, per capita average annual economic losses due to natural hazards are more than three times greater than average annual losses for South-East Asia, South and South-West Asia, and North and Central Asia2.

Within PSIDS, approximately 26% of the population and 39% of economic assets are exposed to TCs2. Exposure is often greatest in coastal areas where the highest densities of populations, economic assets and critical infrastructure frequently intersect with the most severe wind forcing, intense rainfall and extreme waves associated with TCs4. TC damage to buildings accounts for 62.4% of the losses, followed by cash crops (31.7%) and infrastructure (5.9%)5. In addition to TC damage, TC impacts also include long-term indirect losses with economic, ecological and cultural implications. As a result, PSIDS have pushed to ensure the Warsaw International Mechanism for loss and damage of the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) is operational6. The Pacific Catastrophe Risk Insurance Company has also been established to provide services under TC event scenarios for the wider 22 Pacific Island Countries and Territories (PICTs).

In 2016, the most powerful TC ever recorded in the Southern Hemisphere (Winston) left 44 dead, 130 injured and wiped out ⅓ of the value of Fiji’s GDP within 36 hours7. Record-breaking TC events such as TC Winston in Fiji and TC Pam in Vanuatu have left populations and economic assets in what has been described as a “constant state of recovery”8. The unique geographic circumstances of Vanuatu and Fiji have exposed them to significant hazard damage. For Vanuatu, TCs Pam (2015) and Harold (2020) led to losses of USD 449.4 million and 408 million, respectively8,9. TC Pam in Vanuatu set new global wind speed records in 2015 only to be broken by TC Winston in 2016. For Fiji, TC Winston (2016), TC Harold (2020) and TC Yasa (2020), the reported recovery costs were USD 900 million, 180 million and 500 million, respectively10. Through the period of 1923 to 2024, 182 and 158 TCs made landfall over Vanuatu and Fiji, respectively (Fig. 1). Because of TCs, both Vanuatu and Fiji have faced losses of more than 10% of their GDP annually in recent years. Post Disaster Needs Assessments (PDNAs) that guide disaster relief investments after TC and other hazard events are focused on quantifying significant economic and infrastructure losses. However, PDNAs currently neglect loss of ecosystem services, despite the important coastal protection provided by coastal habitats including mangroves and wider forests. Alongside protecting coastal communities from TC wind forcing, these ecosystems provide a range of provisioning, maintenance and regulation, sustaining and cultural ecosystem services.

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) Advisory opinion (AO) on Obligations of States in respect of Climate Change has highlighted the fact that the impacts of climate-related hazards including tropical cyclones are disproportionately inflicted upon countries who have neither created the greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions nor benefited economically from fossil fuel-based development. Within the international frameworks identified by the AO. Loss and Damage, the Paris Agreement’s Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) and Article 6 all constitute relevant examples of climate financing mechanisms for developing or non-Annex I UNFCCC member countries. Loss and Damage and Catastrophe Risk Insurance provide mechanisms for reimbursement of economic assets lost due to TCs and other climate climate-related hazards. These mechanisms do not yet account for ecosystem services. At the same time, The Paris Agreement NDC targets and Article 6 provide an avenue for countries to access other climate finance modalities through carbon credit units and carbon markets but do not currently consider the impacts of climate-related hazards.

Expanding climate finance modalities requires baseline and time series data to quantify ecosystem services and carbon stocks into economic units. This research seeks to provide an example. Global Forest Watch (GFW) and the International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (IBTrACS) datasets were used to quantify tree cover loss following four Bureau of Meteorology (BOM) category 5 TCs: Pam, Winston, Harold and Yasa in 2 PSIDS, Vanuatu and Fiji (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4). The study adopts the Global Forest Watch definition of tree cover loss based on the biophysical land cover rather than land use definitions11. As a result, tree cover loss means biophysical presence of tree cover at point-(a) in time and the loss of this biophysical tree cover at point-(b) in time as detected by Landsat Satellite Imagery11. This is equated to ‘stand-replacement disturbance’ including natural and anthropogenic changes observable in Landsat imagery11. The study represents a first step in documenting and quantifying the ongoing loss of ecological resilience including loss of coastal protection, tree cover loss and the impacts on carbon emissions. These are only few of the many ecosystem services impacted by climate intensified TCs. The study analyses the influence of TC events on both the spatial and temporal dynamics of tree cover loss.

TC risks under future climate scenarios

The 22 PICTs listed in the Pacific Data Hub12 make up approximately 84,000 km2 of combined landmass and 34,000 km of coastlines13 within the largest and deepest global ocean basin. The average coastline-to-land area ratio of 405 m km2 across the 22 countries is well above the average global 16.96 m km214. Vanuatu and Fiji have coastline-to-land area ratios of 257 and 254 m km2 respectively. According to census data, in Vanuatu 64%, 94% and 99% of the population live within 1, 5 and 10 km distances from the coastline, respectively15. In Fiji, 27%, 76% and 91% of the population live within 1, 5 and 10 km distances from the coastline, respectively15. On average, 90% of Pacific Islanders live within 5 km of the coast (excluding Papua New Guinea with significant interior land area)15. Overall, significant proportions of population, economic assets, agricultural land, utilities, and critical infrastructure are concentrated along coastlines in Fiji, Vanuatu and other PSIDS, consequently increasing their vulnerability and disaster exposure.

For the Pacific Ocean Basins, TCs are projected to experience increased intensity and rates of precipitation (AR6 Chap. 11) with some studies projecting an increase in TC frequency in the Pacific over the coming decades to centuries16,17. Projected increases in exposure including through population growth are also linked to increased risk and damage under climate scenarios including an estimated doubling in human mortality18,19. Numerous studies have highlighted the Madden-Julian Oscillation, El Nino/Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) as drivers of variability in TC activity at intraseasonal and interannual timescales, respectively19–22. During future El Niño events, TCs are expected to increase in frequency by 20–40% compared with present climate El Niño events23. Vanuatu and Fiji are among the PSIDS suffering from the impact of TCs and are ranked 1 and 6, respectively, out of a total of 65 countries for predicted fatalities per million per year due to future TCs19. For PICTs, the ratio of damage to GDP is above average because of the concentration of economic activity near the coastline and the relatively high coastline to land area ratio24,25.

Results

Between 2000 and 2023, tree cover loss was greatest during years of high-intensity TC events (Fig. 2; Tables 5 and 6). In Vanuatu, tree cover loss peaked in 2015 and 2020, the years of TCs Pam and Harold, respectively (Fig. 2). During 2015, the year of TC Pam, tree cover loss was 35.30 km2, an increase of 326% over the mean annual tree cover loss of 7.52 km2 for the period of 2000 to 2023. In 2020, the year of TC Harold, annual tree cover loss reached 39.23 km2, an increase of 423% over the annual average. Total tree cover loss for Vanuatu in 2015 and 2020 accounted for 43% of total tree cover loss between 2000 and 2023. In Vanuatu, from 2000 to 2023, including tree cover loss for the two category 5 TC years (2015 and 2020) increases the standard deviation of annual tree cover loss from 3.96 to 9.86 km2 and increases the annual average tree cover loss from 4.70 to 7.50 km2. Category 5 tropical cyclones contributed to the interannual variability of tree cover loss in Vanuatu.

In Fiji, between 2000 and 2023, tree cover loss peaked in 2016 and 2021 in the aftermath of TCs Winston and Yasa, respectively (Fig. 2). In 2016, the year of TC Winston, the annual tree cover loss was 83.73 km2, a 263% increase over the mean tree cover loss of 23.04 km2, correlating with a maximum tree cover loss density of 17.79% per grid, the highest density of tree cover loss recorded in the 2000–2023 period based on analysis of the GFW dataset. TC Winston was the strongest cyclone ever recorded to hit Fiji, with estimated sustained winds of 230 km h− 1. Correspondingly, the tree cover loss associated with TC Winston was the highest recorded for this study. In 2021, the year following TC Yasa, the detected tree cover loss increased to 72 km2, an increase of 212% against the annual average. Total tree cover loss for Fiji in 2016 and 2021 accounted for 29% of total tree cover loss between 2000 and 2023. For Fiji from 2000 to 2023, including tree cover loss for the two category 5 TC years (2016 and 2021) increases standard deviations from 7.94 to 18.50 km2 and increases the annual average tree cover loss from 17.82 to 23.04 km2. Category 5 tropical cyclones also contributed to the interannual variability of tree cover loss in Fiji.

The GFW dataset assigns IPCC Tier I land-use classification framework emission factors to convert tree cover loss pixels into estimated CO2e emissions (Table 7)30. For Vanuatu, emissions linked to total tree cover loss for the years of TCs Pam and Harold, were 1.94 million tonnes or megagrams (Mg) CO2e and 2.63 million MgCO2e, respectively. For Fiji, emissions linked to total tree cover loss for the years of TCs Winston, Harold and Yasa were 4.80 million MgCO2e, 1.18 million MgCO2e and 4.27 million MgCO2e, respectively. These emissions represent a significant component of national emissions compromising these countries’ efforts to meet their nationally determined contributions periodically reported to the UNFCCC. Wind speed swath data are used to more closely assess the impacts of TC events on tree cover loss. As summarised in the methods section, localised Tier II emissions factors are also used to provide greater accuracy in terms of resulting CO2e emissions (Table 8).

Tree cover loss within TC wind speed swaths

Across all category 5 TC events, the proportion of tree cover loss was greatest in the 64-knot wind speed swath (Fig. 3). In Fiji, during the year of category 4 TC Harold, 31.76% of tree cover loss occurred outside of the wind speed swath considered. The eye of TC Harold passed over the Ocean making landfall only across the southern Island of Kadavu. The average percentage of total tree cover loss within the 64-knot wind speed swaths across each of the TC events was 71.05%. The additional average percentage of tree cover losses were 10.52, 12.00, and 6.43% for the 50-knot swath, 34-knot swath and other tree cover loss at the national scale, respectively. Overall, across the five TC events, the proportions of tree cover loss correlated to increased TC wind speed. The amount of land exposed to the TC paths and wind speed swaths was also a factor. Across all five TC events, total land coverage within the 64, 50 and 34-knot wind speed swaths was 28.00%, 49.56%, and 85.99%, respectively. The proportions of tree cover loss within different provinces of Vanuatu and Divisions of Fiji were also linked to the overlapping area of wind speed swaths within each of these zones (Tables 2 and 3).

To take a first step towards estimating the ecological and socio-economic losses of natural assets resulting from TCs, tree cover loss was calculated within the narrow 64 knot wide speed swath (Fig. 3; Table 4). Selecting the narrowest TC wind speed swath, provides the most conservative estimate of tree cover loss associated-CO2e emissions. The results showed that within the 64-knot wind speed swaths of TCs Pam and Harold in Vanuatu, tree cover loss amounted to up to 1.44 million and 2.60 million MgCO2e emissions for Tier I IPCC emissions factor estimates, respectively (Table 7). This was equivalent to 6.46 and 11.66 times greater than the total GHG emissions across all economic sectors in Vanuatu reported in the 2021 GHG inventory (approximately 0.22 million MgCO2e)29. In Fiji, within 64-knot wind speed swaths of TCs Winston and Yasa in Fiji, tree cover loss was up to 4.33 million and 3.56 million MgCO2e for Tier I IPCC emissions factor estimates, respectively. This was equivalent to 2.50 and 2.06 times greater than the total GHG emissions across all economic sectors in Fiji reported in the 2021 GHG inventory (approximately 1.73 million MgCO2e)27,29. Using Tier II IPCC estimates results in more conservative ranges of estimated emissions (Table 8). For example, in Vanuatu, estimated emissions are 0.51 and 0.76 million MgCO2e, for TC Pam and Harold respectively. In Fiji, estimated emissions are 2.74, 0.09 and 2.14 million MgCO2e for TCs Winston, Harold and Yasa, respectively.

Conservative estimates of the social-economic losses are estimated from the Environmental Protection Authority estimates of Social Costs of Greenhouse Emissions (SC-GHG) (Table 9)32. Note, these estimated costs were in the lower range compared to costs calculated in other studies31,32. Social costs of the CO2e emissions released from tree cover loss within the 64-knot wind speed swaths were approximated to range from $62–172 million USD, $91–312 million USD, $328–521 million USD, $254–427 million USD for TCs Pam and Harold in Vanuatu and TCs Winston and Yasa in Fiji, respectively. These values are estimated as ranges since there is uncertainty around emissions factors and future costs of CO2e. In Vanuatu, the inclusion of the TC associated carbon emissions would have added 14–38% and 22–77% to PDNA economic losses for TCs Pam and Harold, using Tier II and Tier I emissions factors, respectively. In Fiji, the inclusion of the TC associated carbon emissions would have added 37–58%, 6–7% and 51–85% to PDNA economic losses for TCs Winston, Harold and Yasa, using Tier II and Tier I emissions factors, respectively. Across all TC events covered in this study, the failure to account for the loss of forest carbon storage ecosystem services results in an underestimation of the economic loss compared to losses originally reported in the respective PDNAs. The weighted average underestimations were approximately 23.4–25.0% for Tier II and 37.2% Tier I approaches to estimating CO2e− emissions, respectively. While Tier II estimates are more conservative, both Tier I and II estimates have sources of uncertainty as summarised in the methods section. Overall, each of these approaches highlight significant underestimates against losses reported in PDNAs, making a clear case for the inclusion of these natural asset accounts.

Tree cover provides the essential ecosystem service of coastline protection for islands in the path of a TC. The presence or absence of terrestrial and coastal forest types including mangroves are an indicator of resilience or vulnerability to hazards such as TCs. Tree cover loss within a coastal buffer of 0.1 and 1 km from the coast was also investigated as an indicator of coastal protection from damage during TCs (Table 3). In Vanuatu, coastal buffer tree cover loss ranged from 0.76 to 14.47 km2 within the 64-knot wind speed swath of TC Pam, equivalent to 2.15–40.99% of total tree cover loss for 2015. For TC Harold in Vanuatu, coastal tree cover loss ranged from 0.00 to 0.01 km2, with a smaller 0–0.03% of total tree cover loss for 2020 reflecting the limited land cover under TC Harold’s path. In Fiji, coastal tree cover loss ranged from 1.65 to 18.12 km2 for TC Winston: 1.97–21.64% of total tree cover loss for 2016. For TC Harold in Fiji, coastal tree cover loss ranged from 0.25 to 1.54 km2: representing 0.80–4.98% of total tree cover loss for 2020. It is worth noting that TC Harold in Fiji was the only case of a category 4 TC compared to all other case studies which were category 5 TC events. TC Yasa resulted in coastal tree cover loss ranging from 0.40 to 8.35 km2: representing 0.56–11.60% of total tree cover loss for 2021. TCs Pam, Harold, Winston and Yasa all resulted in substantial losses of tree cover, coastal protection and other associated ecosystem services for Vanuatu and Fiji.

Discussion

For many communities, particularly within PICTs, TCs have caused significant economic losses relative to total GDP. Past estimates of TC risk, loss and damage have commonly focused on population displacement and deaths, settlements, buildings, infrastructure, utilities, agricultural commodities and other man-made economic assets without accounting for loss of natural assets and associated ecosystem services35,36. As demonstrated in this study and others, tree cover loss associated with wind-throw, storm-induced stand replacement disturbance and wider ecological degradation can be combined with several parameters linked to TCs (proximity, windspeed, central wind pressure, and power dissipation index) to establish causal relationships and document forest degradation35,36. Globally, TC damage has highly destructive short-term impacts, which then accumulate over the long term, permanently changing the structure of forests and impacting communities dependent on their resources37. In Cook and Goyens, Category 5 TC Monica snapped and uprooted trees within 50 km wide swaths in Northern Australia38. TCs further affect wider ecosystem processes such as primary productivity and nutrient cycling39. In the United States, Zeng et al. found that TC events affected an average of 97 million trees yearly (1851–2000) equivalent to an average of 53,000 Mg in biomass loss and 9.2 × 107 MgCO2e emissions per year43. Forest damage has been positively correlated with distance from the cyclone eye40.

The relationships between TCs, tree mortality and damage to forest ecosystems within continental contexts is well documented41–44. However, relatively few studies have addressed the unique biogeographic contexts of PICTs. Limited studies in Fiji have observed relationships between TCs on remote sensing indices within agriculture and forests45,46. Such approaches may only show correlations between TC events and fluctuations in vegetation indices as a proxy of unobserved (latent) status of land cover changes47. Additional research is needed to quantify losses of natural assets associated with tree cover loss into units that can be translated into environmental-economic accounting systems for example environmental degradation adjusted measures of gross or net domestic product (GDP/NDP), economic growth rates and government budget deficits48–50. Incorporation of environmental-economic accounting will contribute towards PDNAs, catastrophe risk parametric insurance and loss and damage accounting. Field surveys have been conducted to validate the rates and drivers of tree cover loss in conjunction with GFW datasets at the catchment scale51. We take the first step towards environmental economic accounting by determining the relationship between TC paths, wind speeds and tree cover loss with two island nation case studies of loss and damage to ecosystem services.

Exposure to TCs can compromise carbon storage, hindering progress by PSIDS in setting the standard for meeting and exceeding NDC targets to climate change mitigation. TC-induced tree cover loss in low-lying coastal areas further compromises protection for populations and economic assets. Declines in coastal protection and increases in carbon emissions are likely to lead to ‘compounding and cascading risks’54. Repeated TCs may also have long-term impacts on the structure of forests and the communities dependent on them worldwide36. The magnitude of these impacts is a critical determinant of PSIDS’ vulnerability to climate change, in the context of projected increases in TC intensity with climate change. The loss of tree cover and associated ecosystem services must be considered in terms of losses and damages associated with climate change, PDNAs following disasters, a country’s ability to meet their NDCs, national communications, and the National Action Plans for both Adaptation and Mitigation, as well as economic and ecosystem resilience20. This approach would enable countries applying for loss and damage funding and other climate finance mechanisms to also consider natural assets alongside other economic assets, buildings, critical services and infrastructure, based on their own national disaster recovery needs and priorities. The PDNA mechanism is currently designed for more immediate disaster relief. Including ecosystem services within the scope of loss and damage provides a means of longer-term ecosystem restoration thus supporting the ‘human right to a clean and healthy environment’ passed in the United Nations General Assembly Resolution 76/30055,56. The inclusion of tree cover loss in PDNAs could also strengthen efforts to document climate and disaster-related impacts on the reporting for the UNFCCC, Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction, the Convention on Biological Diversity, and the post-2023 Sustainable Development Goals to inform at local to international levels for the Small Island Developing States and the Global South.

Conclusions

This research highlights the impacts of severe tropical cyclones on tree cover loss and associated ecosystem services. In Vanuatu, TC years were found to increase rates of tree cover loss by 4.62 and 5.23 times the national average for TC Pam (2015) and TC Harold (2020), respectively, accounting for loss of 42.86% of total tree cover in Vanuatu from 2000 to 2023. In Fiji, TC years were found to significantly increase rates of tree cover loss by 3.63 and 3.12 times the national average for TC Winston (2016) and TC Yasa (2021), respectively, accounting for loss of 29.38% of total tree cover in Fiji from 2000 to 2023. Spatial analysis found a clear correlation between higher wind speed swaths and greater proportions of tree cover loss. The study sought to take a first step towards estimating the ecological and socio-economic losses of natural assets resulting from TCs. Conservative estimates of social-economic losses were estimated from the tree cover losses calculated within the narrow 64 knot wide speed swath. Across the four category 5 TC events, the failure to account for the loss of forest carbon storage ecosystem services resulted in significant underestimation of the weighted average economic loss by approximately 23.4–25.0% for Tier II and 37.2% for Tier I compared to economic losses originally reported in the respective PDNAs. Future climate models project increasing intensity of tropical cyclones. The findings have broader implications for loss and damage, community, climate and ecological resilience, carbon stocks, monitoring of climate risk vulnerability, ecosystem services and biodiversity which collectively call for a systematic holistic approach to include ecosystem services.

Methods

Tropical cyclone data

The study collated data for tropical cyclone (TC) parameters from the International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (IBTrACS) version 04 for plotting TC data in Geopandas, Rasterio and Xarray Python libraries, Matlab and QGIS. The data was processed within a Jupyter Environment within Digital Earth Pacific (DE Pacific). The key tropical cyclone IBTrACS parameters were recorded at 6-h timestamp intervals. The wind speeds were plotted using column 11: ‘WMO_WIND’57. The linked parameters included latitude, longitude, maximum sustained wind speed, wind speed radii (the extents of specific wind speed thresholds including 34, 50 and 64-knots). The study investigated the impacts of 4 TCs and 5 landfall case studies across Vanuatu and Fiji.

Tree cover loss data

Global Forest Watch (GFW) datasets provided insights into both temporal and spatial dynamics of tree cover losses across these case studies for the period of 2000 to 202356. These datasets were collated from Hansen et al.59 The datasets were downloaded from the Google Earth Engine Catalogue repository, clipped by GADM boundaries then uploaded to the Jupyter Environment within Digital Earth Pacific. The GFW dataset defines forested land within a threshold of greater than 30% canopy cover56. The definition of GFW tree cover loss includes both wind-throw and stand replacement disturbance. Stand replacement is defined as tree mortality and the loss of the majority of tree cover in each 30-m resolution pixel56. The mortality and stand disturbance resulting from TCs, windthrow and storms are likely to be detected by the tree cover loss data. The extent to which this loss is detected depends upon the intensity of the TC event as well as a range of other biophysical factors. The study has excluded other topographical variables such as slope, aspect, land use and land cover data variables, terrain roughness and shielding. The study has not delved into ecological variables such as forest types and structure. Further studies may investigate the relationship between TC paths and vegetation changes while also considering these factors. Consistent data to address other possible drivers of forest cover loss, including storm surge, inundation and landslide destruction are not yet available across all case studies at the time of writing. Further research will also be needed to assess the proportions of tree throw and other categories of stand replacement disturbances against defoliation.

Spatial analysis

In terms of spatial analysis, TC paths were overlaid over these GFW datasets to visualise the correlations with the spatial distribution of tree cover loss (Fig. 3). Analysis included calculations of lengths of TC paths over land as well as the total tree cover loss within the three wind speed categories (34, 50 and 64-knots) and swath widths of each category. The TC swaths were plotted using the average radii distance derived from columns 27–38 of the IBTrACS dataset since these were the only complete columns for each of the quadrants for each of the wind speed categories1,55. Total tree cover loss pixels falling within each of the three wind speed swathes were calculated and plotted as a proxy of the relationship between wind speed and tree cover loss. The percentage of land area was also calculated using Vanuatu and Fiji’s Global Administrative Area (GADM) national country boundary datasets.

The IBTrACS data was also plotted to show the different TC categories based on column 120: ‘NADI_CAT’. Based on the IBTrACS v.04 column documentation this column indicates the type of system for each cyclone at the time of the observation and is consistent with the Bureau of Meteorology definitions1,58. Tree cover loss based on GFW data was matched to the year of the TC and calculated for 64, 50 and 34 knot wind speed swaths of TCs from the IBTrACS archive. Note, that cyclones can evolve during their lifetimes and so the category was based on the average wind speeds upwind and downwind of land based on the ‘WMO_WIND’ column1.

The estimates of tree cover loss collated in Table 2. show additional tree cover loss for each wind speed swath. The 50-knot wind speed swath already includes the tree cover loss of the 64-knot wind and the 34-knot wind speed swath already includes the tree cover loss of both the 50 and 64-knot wind speed swaths. The estimates of additional tree cover loss only account for the outer swaths by subtracting the tree cover loss from the inner wind speed swaths.

In terms of calculations of tree cover loss on coastal areas, it is important to note that the GFW tree cover extent and loss data only include mangrove forests that meet the definitions of forest (i.e. woody vegetation over 5 m in height). This may lead to underestimating the full extent of mangroves, other coastal tree cover and vegetation impacted by TC losses.

Data visualisation

To visualise the spatial distribution of tree cover across the national scales of Vanuatu and Fiji, the study used zonal histograms of GFW tree cover loss datasets51,59. The zonal histograms were based on tree cover loss at approximately 30 m × 30 m pixel resolution sampled within hexagon grids of 24 km2 in area. The grids were generated across Fiji and Vanuatu for analysis of the tree cover changes following standard forest inventory sampling methods, including those developed by the US Forestry Services57,58. Temporal analysis

Temporal analysis was undertaken to investigate the variability in rates of tree cover loss over time. GFW annual tree cover loss was plotted per province, division and nationwide to identify the years of greater annual tree cover loss within the period of 2000 to 202356. The extent of deviation from the annual average was calculated for each year (Tables 5 and 6). The years identified to have the highest annual tree cover loss were selected as the case study years for the analysis of this study. These years corresponded to tropical cyclone events in 2015 (TC Pam), 2020 (TC Harold) in Vanuatu, as well as 2016 (TC Winston), 2020 (TC Harold) and 2021 (TC Yasa) in Fiji. TC Yasa formed on 11 December 2020 and made landfall in Fiji on 17 December before becoming extratropical on 19 December and dissipating on 24 December. The Landsat 9 satellite that was orbiting earth during this period had a revisit time allowing it to potentially capture multispectral images of Fiji every 16 days. Combined with high percentages of cloud cover and the masking necessary, it is likely that the tree cover loss associated with TC Yasa would only be detected in 2021. As a result, this paper has linked TC Yasa to 2021 tree cover loss.

The spatial and temporal patterns of tree cover loss were consistent with TC induced tree cover loss, yet these datasets do not distinguish between natural or anthropogenic causes. Estimating CO2e emissions

The range of estimates of MgCO2e included both IPCC Tier I and Tier II approaches. The Global Forest Watch annual totals assign Tier I IPCC land-use classification framework emission factors to convert tree cover loss pixels into estimated CO2e emissions28,56,61. These estimates represent gross emissions from tree cover loss estimated as the carbon released from the biomass and soils28. The volumes were of emissions were calculated based on the proportions of tree cover loss within each of the wind speed swaths for the corresponding year57. The GFW detection of tree cover loss accounts for land use changes and different drivers of tree cover loss (e.g. shifting agriculture, wildfire and wind-throw storm disturbance)61–65. Yet, the approach in estimating emissions based on proportions of tree cover loss occurring within wind-speed swaths may lead to additional uncertainty due to challenges in accounting for the different drivers and rates of emissions28,64,65.

Across the five TC events, the IPCC Tier I approach of GFW estimated a mean of 554.04 MgCO2 ha− 1 across Vanuatu and Fiji61–63. The second range of estimates was based on IPCC Tier II approaches using localised Forest Reference Emission Levels (FREL) reports from Vanuatu and Fiji66–71. In Vanuatu, emissions were estimated to range from 196.31 MgCO2e ha− 1 to 276.10 MgCO2e ha− 1 in open and dense forest, respectively66,67. The Department of Forestry in Vanuatu has not yet published the landcover stratification maps of open and dense forest so estimates are provided as a range based on tree cover loss66,67. In Fiji, emissions factors ranged from 262.43 MgCO2e ha− 1 to 322.15 MgCO2e ha− 1 in upland and lowland forests above and below 600 m elevation, respectively68–70. Upland and lowland forest was delineated using Within Digital Earth Pacific, an open-source public technology infrastructure that has been established to support PICTs to access and analyse satellite data. The Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data was Copernicus DEM GLO-30 data was loaded from the Microsoft Planetary Computer SpatioTemporal Asset Catalog (STAC)73. The data was plotted in the Jupyter Environment of the Digital Earth Pacific Analytics Hub to calculate tree cover loss within upland and lowland categories (Fig. 4)74. These Tier II estimates only account for above and below ground biomass using root-to-shoot ratios provided by the FREL reports of Vanuatu and Fiji66–70. Because these FRELs did not include emissions factors for dead wood, litter and soil organic carbon these carbon pools are not included in the Tier II approaches66–70. GFW categorises tropical cyclones as ‘other natural disturbances’ resulting in full loss of live biomass but no change to dead wood, litter and soil carbon pools28. Sources of uncertainty within Tier I and II approaches include potential for marginal overestimation of rates of emissions associated with TC events because the resulting dead wood from windthrow may take time to decay and release CO2 emissions into the atmosphere59,61,69,71. Variability in the rates of dead wood decay in tropical biomes remains a source of uncertainty in global models of the carbon cycle61,71. The IPCC has also highlighted that decay rates vary by forest type, climatic conditions and management practice61. Decay rates in the tropics are much greater than other forest biomes such as boreal forests highlighting that within these case studies dead wood will more rapidly decay and contribute to CO2 emissions61. The separate reductions in gross removals of carbon through photosynthesis over time are outside of the scope of this study. These factors including other carbon pools, localised rates of decay and gross removals of carbon should be considered in further studies.

Unit measurements

All carbon measurements were reported in units of megagrams (Mg) CO2. Where units were converted from the International System of Units (SI units) of C to CO2, the study followed approaches consistent with other studies by multiplying by a factor of 3.6774.

Estimation of social economic costs

The estimated social economic costs are based on the 120 USD per MgCO2e Near-term Ramsey Discount Rates30. The 120 USD option was the most conservative discount rate option. A weighted average percentage underestimation was calculated to range from 23.4 to 37.2% for Tier II lower range and Tier I estimates, respectively (Table 9). Tier II estimates ranged from 23.4 to 25.0% weighted underestimations compared to the total losses reported in he PDNA documents. The lower-range of Tier II estimates were based on assumed open forest coverage emissions factor for Vanuatu.
