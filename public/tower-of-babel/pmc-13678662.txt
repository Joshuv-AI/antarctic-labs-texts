Factors Associated With In‐Hospital Mortality and Length of Stay Among Hospitalised Patients Receiving Carbapenem Therapy: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Abdullah Faiz Zaihan, Dhivyaa K. Ganesan, Chia Siang Kow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients receiving carbapenems are clinically heterogeneous and have severe infections and multiple comorbidities. Evidence on factors associated with mortality and length of stay (LOS) in this treatment‐defined population remains limited.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included adult hospital episodes involving at least one carbapenem dose at Hospital Shah Alam during 2025. Firth penalised logistic regression was used for in‐hospital mortality, and median regression with 2000 bootstrap resamples was used for right‐skewed LOS. Covariates were selected a priori. Carbapenem continuation beyond 72 h was treated as a postbaseline characteristic and was not included in outcome models.ResultsAmong 112 eligible episodes involving 107 patients, 38 deaths occurred (33.9%); median LOS was 19.0 days (IQR: 10.8–28.0). Older age (adjusted OR: 1.41 per 10 years, 95% CI: 1.07–1.92) and definitive indication at carbapenem initiation (adjusted OR: 0.28, 95% CI: 0.10–0.70) were associated with in‐hospital mortality. Hospital‐acquired infection was associated with a longer adjusted median LOS (median difference 8.63 days, 95% bootstrap CI: 0.50–14.44). Definitive indication was not clearly associated with LOS after adjustment (median difference −5.54 days, 95% bootstrap CI: −12.00–4.62).ConclusionsOlder age and definitive indication at carbapenem initiation were associated with in‐hospital mortality, while hospital‐acquired infection was associated with longer median LOS. The treatment‐indication association should not be interpreted causally because residual confounding, particularly by acute illness severity and indication, remains likely.

1. Introduction

Carbapenem antibiotics are among the most potent broad‐spectrum antimicrobial agents and are widely used in hospital settings for the management of severe infections, particularly those caused by multidrug‐resistant Gram‐negative organisms. Their use is typically reserved for patients with complex clinical profiles, including those with multiple comorbidities, healthcare‐associated infections and severe or rapidly progressing disease [1, 2]. As a result, patients receiving carbapenem therapy often represent a high‐risk population with a substantial burden of illness and an increased likelihood of adverse clinical outcomes.

In‐hospital mortality and prolonged length of stay (LOS) are key outcomes in this population, reflecting both disease severity and the effectiveness of clinical management. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that older age and comorbidity burden are associated with increased mortality in patients with severe infections [3–5]. Similarly, healthcare‐associated infections, delayed clinical response, and complications during hospitalisation are recognized contributors to prolonged hospital stay [6–9]. However, these outcomes are multifactorial and may be influenced by a combination of patient characteristics, infection‐related factors and treatment decisions.

In addition to patient and infection‐related factors, treatment‐related variables may play an important role in determining outcomes [10–12]. In clinical practice, carbapenem therapy may be initiated empirically in patients with suspected severe infections or used as definitive therapy based on microbiological confirmation [13, 14]. These treatment strategies reflect different stages of clinical decision‐making and may be associated with varying disease severity, pathogen profiles and responses to therapy. However, the independent contribution of such treatment‐related factors to clinical outcomes remains unclear, particularly in real‐world settings.

Antimicrobial stewardship programmes have emphasised the importance of optimising antibiotic use, not only to reduce antimicrobial resistance but also to improve patient outcomes [15]. Despite this, most stewardship efforts have focused on prescribing appropriateness and antibiotic utilization, with comparatively less emphasis on understanding how clinical and treatment‐related variables interact to influence outcomes such as mortality and LOS among patients receiving broad‐spectrum antibiotics.

Furthermore, evidence from real‐world clinical settings remains limited, especially in Malaysian healthcare systems where patterns of infection, antimicrobial resistance and prescribing practices may differ from those reported in international studies. Evaluating outcomes within a local cohort is, therefore, important to provide context‐specific insights that can inform clinical decision‐making and stewardship strategies.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate baseline demographic, clinical, infection‐related and treatment‐indication factors associated with in‐hospital mortality and LOS among hospitalised adults receiving carbapenem therapy at a secondary hospital in Malaysia.

2. Methods

2.1. Study Design and Setting

A retrospective cohort study was conducted at Hospital Shah Alam, a secondary care public hospital in Selangor, Malaysia. The study considered all hospital episodes involving carbapenem therapy between 1 January 2025 and 31 December 2025.

2.2. Study Population

All consecutive hospital episodes involving patients aged 18 years or older who received at least one dose of meropenem, ertapenem or another documented carbapenem exposure were eligible. Episodes were identified through pharmacy dispensing records and verified against electronic and paper medical records.

Five patients contributed two episodes each. Repeated admissions were analysed as separate episodes because exposures and outcomes were episode specific; a sensitivity analysis retained only each patient’s first eligible episode.

2.5. Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised using medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. The distribution of LOS was assessed using its range, skewness and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Residual normality and homoscedasticity in the originally specified linear model were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Breusch–Pagan tests, respectively.

Adjusted models used covariates selected a priori for clinical relevance and potential confounding rather than univariable p values: age, sex, baseline eGFR, number of recorded comorbidities, acquisition source, respiratory infection and definitive versus empirical indication at carbapenem initiation.

In‐hospital mortality was analysed using Firth penalised logistic regression to reduce small‐sample maximum‐likelihood bias with 38 outcome events [17]. Associations are reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with profile‐likelihood 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). Discrimination and fit were summarised using the c‐statistic with a 2000‐resample bootstrap CI, Brier score and Hosmer–Lemeshow test.

Because LOS and linear‐model residuals were markedly right skewed, LOS was analysed using median (quantile) regression [18]. Adjusted coefficients represent differences in median LOS in days. Percentile 95% CIs and two‐sided p values were estimated using 2000 pairwise bootstrap resamples; bootstrap convergence and median‐regression pseudo‐R 2 were recorded.

The primary models used complete cases. All prespecified model variables and both outcomes were complete in the 112 eligible episodes; no imputation was performed. Smoking status recorded as ‘unknown’ was retained as a descriptive category rather than treated as missing. A sensitivity analysis of mortality retained the first eligible episode for each patient. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p value < 0.05.

4. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 112 adult hospital episodes involving carbapenem therapy, older age and definitive indication at carbapenem initiation were associated with in‐hospital mortality, while hospital‐acquired infection was associated with longer median LOS.

The association between older age and mortality is consistent with evidence from patients with severe infection and sepsis, in whom reduced physiological reserve, frailty, comorbidity and vulnerability to organ dysfunction contribute to poorer outcomes [3, 4, 19–21]. The effect estimate in this study should nevertheless be interpreted within the broad uncertainty produced by the modest number of deaths.

Definitive indication at carbapenem initiation was associated with lower mortality, but this finding does not establish that definitive carbapenem therapy reduced mortality. Classification as definitive required microbiological information to be available when carbapenem was selected and may, therefore, identify a different clinical pathway and patient group. Disease severity, source control, pathogen and susceptibility profiles, appropriateness and timing of preceding antibiotics and early clinical response could influence both treatment classification and outcome [10, 11, 22]. In addition, because empirical classification was fixed at carbapenem initiation, the empirical group could include patients who deteriorated or died before organism identification and susceptibility results became available. Such patients would remain classified as empirical and could enrich that group with rapidly fatal illness, potentially contributing to the observed mortality association. The timing of microbiological results relative to clinical outcome was not a predefined study variable, so this potential mechanism could not be quantified. Residual confounding by indication, therefore, remains a substantial alternative explanation.

Continuation beyond 72 h was deliberately excluded from both adjusted models. Patients classified as continuing therapy must remain alive and under observation long enough to acquire that status, whereas early death or transfer may prevent classification as continued therapy. Treating this postbaseline status as a conventional predictor can, therefore, introduce immortal time bias for mortality [16]. For LOS, prolonged hospitalisation itself creates more opportunity for therapy to continue, producing reverse causality. Mortality within 72 h of carbapenem initiation was not collected as a predefined study variable and could not be reliably ascertained for all patients; it was, therefore, not analysed separately. Accordingly, a 72‐h landmark or time‐dependent outcome analysis was not performed.

Hospital‐acquired infection was associated with a longer adjusted median LOS, consistent with previous reports linking healthcare‐associated infection with additional treatment, monitoring, source‐control requirements and hospital resource use [6, 7, 23, 24]. However, only 19 episodes were classified as community‐acquired, resulting in an imbalanced comparison and a wide CI. The estimate should, therefore, be viewed as an association that may also reflect unmeasured illness complexity. Although the median‐regression pseudo‐R 2 was low (0.056), this statistic reflects limited overall model fit rather than a literal estimate that 5.6% of LOS variance was explained. The adjusted 8.63‐day difference in median LOS may nevertheless be clinically and operationally meaningful, particularly for hospital bed use and resource planning, while substantial unexplained heterogeneity in LOS remains.

Definitive indication was not clearly associated with median LOS in the revised model. Accordingly, the present data should not be used to infer that microbiologically directed carbapenem therapy shortens hospitalisation. Evidence supporting antimicrobial reassessment and de‐escalation comes from studies designed around a clearly timed stewardship decision, including target‐trial and propensity‐based analyses [25–27], rather than from the treatment‐indication comparison used here.

Clinically, the findings support careful risk assessment in older adults and reinforce the substantial resource burden associated with hospital‐acquired infection. They also emphasise the value of obtaining microbiological data promptly. However, this study does not determine whether carbapenem continuation, discontinuation, escalation or de‐escalation changes mortality or LOS, and it should not be used as a treatment‐selection rule.

Several limitations should be considered. The retrospective design permits incomplete documentation and misclassification. Acute illness severity variables—including ICU admission, septic shock, vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, organ dysfunction and validated severity scores—were not systematically captured. Lactate was available in only 9 of 112 episodes. Mortality within 72 h, timing of microbiological results relative to clinical outcomes and time‐resolved antibiotic escalation or de‐escalation were not predefined study variables. Consequently, the adjusted models could not adequately control for acute severity or fully reconstruct antibiotic treatment pathways, and the definitive‐indication association is particularly susceptible to residual confounding by indication.

The modest sample and 38 deaths limited model complexity and precision; Firth regression reduced small‐sample estimation bias but could not eliminate overfitting or unmeasured confounding. LOS ended at either discharge or death, so early death may shorten observed LOS. Five patients contributed two episodes although a first‐episode sensitivity analysis produced a similar mortality estimate for definitive indication. eGFR values recorded as > 90 were top‐coded at 90. Finally, this single‐centre secondary‐hospital cohort may not be generalisable to other patient populations or carbapenem‐use pathways.

Despite these limitations, the revised analysis distinguishes baseline factors from postbaseline stewardship status, uses methods appropriate to the event count and skewed LOS distribution and provides transparent estimates that can inform the design of larger studies with time‐resolved treatment and severity data.

5. Conclusion

Among hospitalised adults receiving carbapenem therapy, older age and definitive indication at carbapenem initiation were associated with in‐hospital mortality, while hospital‐acquired infection was associated with longer median LOS.

These findings describe associations rather than causal treatment effects. Larger studies incorporating acute illness severity, microbiological timing, source control and time‐varying antibiotic exposure are required before treatment‐related conclusions can be drawn.
