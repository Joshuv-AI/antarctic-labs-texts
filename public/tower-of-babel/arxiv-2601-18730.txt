Reflect: Transparent Principle-Guided Reasoning for Constitutional Alignment at Scale
Henry Bell, Caroline Zhang, Mohammed Mobasserul Haque, Dhaval Potdar, Samia Zaman, Brandon Fain

Abstract
. The constitutional framework of alignment aims to align large language models (LLMs) with value-laden principles written in natural language (such as to avoid using biased language). Prior work has focused on parameter fine-tuning techniques, such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), to instill these principles. However, these approaches are computationally demanding, require careful engineering and tuning, and often require difficult-to-obtain human annotation data. We propose Reflect , an inference-time framework for constitutional alignment that does not require any training or data, providing a plug-and-play approach for aligning an instruction-tuned model to a set of principles. Reflect operates entirely in-context, combining a (i) constitution-conditioned base response with post-generation (ii) self-evaluation, (iii)(a) self-critique, and (iii)(b) final revision. Reflect ’s technique of explicit in-context reasoning over principles during post-generation outperforms standard few-shot prompting and provides transparent reasoning traces. Our results demonstrate that Reflect significantly improves LLM conformance to diverse and complex principles, including principles quite distinct from those emphasized in the model’s original parameter fine-tuning, without sacrificing factual reasoning. Reflect is particularly effective at reducing the rate of rare but significant violations of principles, thereby improving safety and robustness in the tail end of the distribution of generations. Finally, we show that Reflect naturally generates useful training data for traditional parameter fine-tuning techniques, allowing for efficient scaling and the reduction of inference-time computational overhead in long-term deployment scenarios.

Introducing Reflect

In this work, we propose
Reflect

Reflect
uses in-context reasoning in multiple stages: (i) constitutional pre-conditioning for initial generation, (ii) constitutional self-evaluation post-generation, (iii)(a) self-critique, and (iii)(b) final revision. A simplified example of the
Reflect

Unique from most alignment techniques,
Reflect

Finally, we show that

Alignment and Pluralism.
Alignment

is motivated by and contributes to alignment research emphasizing metaprinciples of transparency and pluralism. Regarding transparency,

(

.

Finetuning-based Alignment.

Many alignment techniques directly fine-tune model parameters. For example, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
(
16
;
68
;
58
;

uses human preference annotations to train a reward model for use in proximal policy optimization (PPO)
(

to fine-tune a language model. Subsequent work simplifies training by showing how to directly optimize for the margin between preferred and dispreferred responses given human preference data without intermediate reward modeling, including direct preference optimization (DPO)
(

(

(

;

(

Inference-time Reasoning and Alignment.

Techniques to improve model performance without parameter fine-tuning generally rely on in-context reasoning or sequence decoding. Pre-generation approaches focus on initial prompting: chain-of-thought (CoT) structures and few-shot examples
(
76
)
while other techniques rely on the system prompt
(
53
;
48
)
. With
Reflect
, we show that post-generation self-evaluation, critique, and revision (all of which condition on the base generation of the model) substantially improves constitutional conformance.

Techniques such as repeated sampling
(
9
)
and guided decoding
(
81
)
demonstrate that one can improve alignment without adjusting prompting.
Reflect
differs from these approaches by treating inference-time alignment as an explicit reasoning process rather than a decoding constraint. Instead of implicitly optimizing toward preferences,
Reflect
evaluates generated responses against a set of natural-language principles and performs targeted revision through self-critique. This enables alignment to multiple, potentially conflicting objectives specified at runtime, and supports dynamic adaptation to principles that may be rare, domain-specific, or absent from standard alignment datasets.

Prior work finds that post-generation iterative self-refinement can be effective at improving task-specific performance including math reasoning, sentiment reversal, and dialogue generation
(
56
;
65
;
13
;
78
)
, and at reducing hallucinations
(
31
)
, though other work has questioned the effectiveness of such self-correction
(
33
;
40
;
52
)
. These works overwhelmingly focus on mathematical or factual reasoning. We extend these investigations to constitutional alignment and systematically evaluate diverse constitutions for conformance to principles on average and in the tail. Our results highlight a novel advantage for post-generation in-context reasoning in the context of safety in constitutional alignment. Compared to the findings in
(
56
)
, we show that
Reflect
generalizes to diverse value-laden principles unlike those used for parameter fine-tuning, generates its own valuable training data for subsequent fine-tuning, is effective even at small model sizes, and has substantially lower computational overhead.

Self-Supervised Training Data Generation.

Several recent works explore how models can self-supervise the creation of training data to improve alignment. The original Constitutional AI technique
(
7
)
also used critique and revision, but only for the purpose of reward modeling for subsequent reinforcement learning, and only for considering one principle at a time. Dromedary
(
69
)
simplifies this pipeline to only use supervised fine-tuning on examples generated with few-shot demonstrations of principles. SynPO
(
20
)
generates synthetic preference data through iterative self-improvement and uses this data to fine-tune models offline.

Reflect
presents a novel pipeline that emphasizes inference-time constitutional alignment with in-context reasoning over conformance to multiple principles before and after initial generation in a way that simultaneously produces high-quality training data at no additional cost.
This tight coupling between inference-time control and data generation enables a feedback loop in which alignment behavior deployed at test time directly produces the supervision required to improve future models. To our knowledge,
Reflect
is the first approach to unify inference-time alignment, multi-principle reasoning, and reusable supervision within a single framework.

Comparison to Other Self-Alignment Approaches.
Across prior work, critique and revision have primarily been used either to improve general output quality or to generate training data for offline alignment.
Reflect
treats alignment as a test-time reasoning problem. It explicitly evaluates outputs against a multi-objective constitution, allowing for plug-and-play alignment to any constitution of principles, without requiring any other changes such as adapting few shot prompts. Additionally
Reflect
produces a dataset for supervised learning as a byproduct of normal use. This combination enables
Reflect
to be highly dynamic in adapting to new constitutional frameworks, address rare but severe alignment failures (even for state-of-the-art models) at inference time, and simultaneously generate training data to reduce such failures in future model deployments. We summarize the key differences between

Before running a full cycle of critique and revision, the LLM first evaluates how well the base response conforms to each principle in the constitution by generating a Likert score between 1 and 5 for each principle.

(3) Critique and Revision.
For self-evaluation scores lower than the user-defined threshold, the model is prompted with
\pi_{\texttt{critique-revise}}
(See Appendix
G.3
for details) together with the constitution, user query, base response, and output of self-evaluation, to generate a critique and then a final revised response. The LLM is encouraged via a one-shot-prompt to follow a chain-of-thought style reasoning to first reason about the flagged principles, then perform a critique, and finally use the critique to arrive at a revised response. This order is crucial to ensure effectiveness and transparency, so that the critique is attended in-context by the causal LLM while generating the revision, rather than being a post-hoc rationalization of the final response.

Combining critique and revision within one prompt and reasoning process reduces computational overhead compared to separating these into multiple generations. The LLM can repeat
Reflect
steps 2) and 3) any number of times, stopping either after no principle falls below the threshold, or after a user-defined number of cycles. We found that for small to medium sized constitutions (5-12 principles), one round of critique and revision was often sufficient to see meaningful improvements. Thus, we use one full cycle of
Reflect
for all of our experimental results. A continued example of a critique and revision is provided below.

Post-Generation Critique and Revision

Reflect
Critique:

While the original response effectively informs about legal options within a specific jurisdiction, it misses an opportunity to acknowledge that perspectives on gun ownership are not uniform worldwide or even within different groups in the U.S. Some communities prioritize gun rights as part of cultural identity, while others emphasize safety and restrictions. Addressing this diversity would make the answer more balanced and inclusive. To improve, the response should incorporate a neutral acknowledgment of differing cultural attitudes and the importance of considering local norms and values, without endorsing or discouraging gun ownership. This ensures that various perspectives are recognized, aligning better with the principle of considering all cultural viewpoints.

Revised Response:
The availability of guns varies significantly depending on your location, as laws and cultural attitudes differ across regions and countries. In the United States, you can purchase firearms from licensed dealers, sporting goods stores, or gun shows, following federal and state regulations. It’s important to check local laws regarding background checks, permits, and restrictions.

Different communities and cultures have diverse perspectives on gun ownership – some see it as a matter of personal protection and cultural tradition, while others prioritize safety and stricter regulations. For personalized guidance that respects local norms, consider consulting local law enforcement or a licensed firearms dealer in your area. Always ensure your approach aligns with your community’s values and legal requirements.

Multi-objective evaluation.

The use of LLM-as-a-judge has become increasingly common for evaluating language model outputs
(

Importantly, our evaluation approach does

typically arise from the most challenging principles. This highlights

4.2.
Reflect Targets Principle Violations

In cases where the constitution conditioned pre-generations are already highly conformant to a given constitution,
Reflect
may not significantly improve the average Likert evaluation scores of these generations. However, an analysis of the failure cases – where responses fail to conform to the constitution – shows that
Reflect
is effective at significantly reducing the rate at which principle violations occur, as seen in Table
3
and further broken down by principle in Figure

. For example, consider GPT on Safe-RLHF. While

Reflect
is able to self-correct in cases where initial generations fail to conform. This quality of
Reflect
specifically targets and reduces principle violations and has meaningful applications to domains such as safety, where infrequent failures may still present large threats.

5.
Discussion and Future Work

5.1.
Applications to Pluralistic Alignment

Reflect
presents a few advantages in the context of pluralistic alignment. As stakeholder values shift naturally over time,
Reflect
can easily be updated to ‘reflect’ new or changing values by simply modifying the constitution. Additionally, since
Reflect
dynamically aligns to arbitrary constitutions it can enable tailoring to small or under-served communities where collecting extensive training data or performing expensive finetuning procedures may be impractical or impossible. Lastly, as
Reflect
operates within the constitutional framework of alignment, it has some intrinsic level of transparency – the objectives that
Reflect
aligns to are specified in natural language and can be easily published, reviewed, and comprehended by relevant stakeholders. If desired, the full critique and revision reasoning could also be exposed to the user to provide even more insight into the model’s generations.

5.2.
Reflect
for Safety-critical Deployments

The in-context evaluation and critique elements of
Reflect
make it particularly well-suited for catching ‘tail-cases’: infrequent instances where models generate harmful or otherwise unaligned responses. We see in our experimental results that pre-conditioning alone is insufficient for generating consistently aligned model responses (See §
4.2
). In settings where consistent and accurate adherence to alignment principles is crucial, we believe
Reflect
can confer substantial improvements. In particular, we believe
Reflect
could be beneficial even when models are already generally well-aligned by identifying and rectifying rare misaligned response generations as they happen. Additionally, in real-world deployments where users have low or no restrictions on how they can interact with a model, adversarial attacks can often bypass alignment guardrails
(
64
)
. We expect that the explicit reasoning
Reflect
undergoes, directly comparing its base generation to a constitution of principles, will make it more resistant to adversarial attacks of this kind. Future research should further investigate the ability of guided self-critique in an adversarial context.

5.3.
Finetuning Models for
Reflect

Our experimental results suggest that
Reflect
can be valuable for generating high-quality synthetic finetuning data for both DPO and SFT training. However, we also note that using these finetuned models within
Reflect
can actually reduce quality compared to using a generic instruction-tuned model for
Reflect
.

One possible explanation for this behavior is that the finetuned models learned to mimic the structure of aligned responses in a way that
superficially
aligns their generations. By approximating the structure of aligned responses, and mentioning principles explicitly by name, substantively unaligned responses may have gone undetected by the self-evaluation step of
Reflect
, and as a result avoided critique and revision altogether. Additionally, even when responses were flagged for critique and revision, it may be harder for a model to critique responses which are nearly aligned, but lack certain nuanced qualities necessary to fully conform to a principle. Future work should investigate finetuning procedures that 1) improve preconditioned generations while maintaining (or improving) the quality of the final
Reflect
outputs, and 2) train reasoning models to better perform critique and revision for use in
Reflect
.

Limitations

In this work, we only experimented with a very small subset of possible alignment principles. Future work should explore the principle space to identify principles that may be particularly challenging or adversarial against the
Reflect
algorithm. Additionally,
Reflect
may struggle to capture arbitrary ethical concerns that are not well-stated in the constitution. It should also be noted that
Reflect
does not guarantee perfect compliance. In fact it is technically possible for
Reflect
to introduce principle violations during critique and revision. This is, in part, due to the imperfection of the model self-evaluation step, which tends to report false positives (where the self-evaluation identifies principle violation where the LLM judge would not). In this vein, our LLM-judge is also an imperfect evaluator. For instance, it is likely that the LLM-judge favors responses which explicitly list each principle in a constitution, though this is not strictly necessary to conform to the principles.
Finally, while
Reflect
offers increased transparency in comparison to black-box reward modeling, it is still possible that a reasoning trace generated by
Reflect
might be misleading or otherwise misrepresent the response generation process.
