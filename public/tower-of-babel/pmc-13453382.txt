Comparative Effectiveness of Colistin Sodium versus Polymyxin B Sulfate Against CR-GNB: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Wuping Shuai, Yizhen Huang, Xiao Ling, Rongrong Wang, Ping Yang, Yanfang Zhang, Yuanyuan Zhong, Saiping Jiang, Yuzhen Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare the clinical effectiveness of colistin sodium (CS) and polymyxin B sulfate (PMB) in the treatment of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria (CR-GNB) infections.Patients and methodsAdult patients receiving intravenous CS or PMB ≥ 72 hours between October 2021 and November 2022 were retrospectively enrolled. Demographic information and pertinent clinical laboratory data were collected for each patient. The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality, and the secondary outcomes consisted of microbial clearance, clinical success, and normalization of inflammatory marker. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to address potential confounding. Cox regression with IPTW was used to evaluate the association between polymyxin type and mortality.ResultsA total of 93 CS patients and 118 PMB patients were included in this study. After IPTW, a significant difference in 28-day all-cause mortality was observed between the CS and PMB groups (11.0% vs 26.7%, P = 0.016). However, multivariable Cox regression analysis showed that CS therapy was not independently associated with 28-day all-cause mortality (HR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.06–4.64, P = 0.547). No significant differences in microbial clearance rate or clinical success were found between the two groups either before or after IPTW (all P > 0.05). The rate of procalcitonin normalization was significantly higher in the CS group than in the PMB group prior to IPTW (43.0% vs 28.0%, P = 0.033). However, this difference did not persist after weighting (39.6% vs 30.0%, P = 0.177). No between-group differences were observed for other inflammatory markers.ConclusionAlthough IPTW-adjusted analysis revealed a significant difference in mortality between CS and PMB groups, multivariable Cox regression incorporating additional covariates did not confirm an independent protective effect of CS. This suggests that the observed survival benefit may be attributable to confounding, and the finding warrants validation in larger prospective multicenter studies.Keywords: colistin sulfate, polymyxin B sulfate, effectiveness, 28-day mortality

Introduction

In recent years, the detection rates of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria (CR-GNB) remain persistently high, with the associated mortality and disease burden remaining severe.1 The primary CR-GNB include carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB),2 and carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA). However, the development of new antibacterial agents is progressing slowly,3 and clinical treatment options for CR-GNB infections are extremely limited, resulting in a dire scarcity of effective therapies in clinical practice.

In the late 1950s, polymyxins were introduced into medical use, mainly to treat infections caused by gram-negative bacteria. However, owing to their significant neurotoxicity4 and nephrotoxicity,5–7 their clinical use gradually declined. Polymyxins are a group of cyclic antibiotics produced by Paenibacillus polymyxa.8 Polymyxins bind to lipopolysaccharides on the outer membrane via polar/hydrophobic interactions,9,10 thereby disrupting membrane integrity and permeability, inducing reactive oxygen species that cause oxidative damage, and resulting in rapid cell death.11–15 Consequently, owing to their unique and rapid mechanism of action, polymyxins were reinstated in clinical practice and are now considered the final‑line antibiotics for refractory infections caused by CR-GNB.1,16,17

Currently, three injectable polymyxin formulations are available in the market, including polymyxin B sulfate for injection (PMB), colistimethate sodium for injection (CMS), and colistin sulfate for injection (CS). Among these, CS was approved in 2018 by the National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) of China. In clinical practice, CS and PMB exhibit complex pharmacokinetics (PK) in critically ill patients.18 Additionally, PMB has a higher risk of tissue accumulation after repeated dosing and more pronounced interindividual PK variability.19,20 Moreover, several retrospective studies21,22 have reported that CS is associated with a significantly lower incidence of adverse reactions than PMB. Furthermore, multivariable Cox regression analysis suggested that CS therapy is an independent protective factor against acute kidney injury (AKI),22,23 while the duration of PMB therapy is an independent risk factor for AKI during PMB treatment.24

In line with these observations, CS is widely used in clinical practice in China, and its efficacy has been supported by existing studies.25 Nevertheless, real-world, head-to-head comparisons between CS and PMB for the treatment of CR-GNB infections were notably lacking. Our research group previously conducted a separate study23 specifically investigating CS- and PMB-related acute kidney injury using the same patient cohort. Accordingly, this retrospective cohort study was undertaken to assess the comparative clinical effectiveness of CS and PMB against CR-GNB infections, to systematically explore potential determinants of clinical outcomes using real-world data, and to propose evidence-based therapeutic strategies for clinical practice.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single center—The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Records of patients who received intravenous polymyxin B sulfate (Shanghai First Biochemical Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) or colistin sulfate (Shanghai SPH New Asia Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were examined. The primary objective of this study was to compare the clinical effectiveness of CS and PMB for CR‑GNB infections.23

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

In order to compare the effectiveness of CS and PMB, the following inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria were applied.

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥18 years, regardless of sex; (2) first receipt of CS or PMB; (3) treatment with CS or PMB intravenously for at least 72 hours; (4) diagnosis of one of the following infection types: pulmonary infection, bloodstream, intra-abdominal infection, urinary tract infection, gastrointestinal infection, or intracranial infection; and (5) confirmed CR-GNB infection prior to polymyxin administration, as evidenced by culture of sputum, throat swab, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, peritoneal drainage fluid, blood, midstream urine, bile, or stool.

Exclusion criteria: (1) sequential use of both CS and PMB during the same illness episode; (2) non-therapeutic use of polymyxins; (3) receiving polymyxins solely via non‑intravenous routes; (4) polymyxin resistance in the CR-GNB isolate; (5) negative microbial culture at the start of polymyxin treatment; (6) missing critical data; and (7) colonization or contamination isolates.

Administration

For polymyxin susceptibility testing, broth microdilution (BMD) was adopted as the reference method and conducted in compliance with ISO-20776 standards. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) breakpoints were interpreted following the CLSI M100-S3526 guidelines: isolates with an MIC ≤ 2 μg/mL were categorised as intermediate (I), whereas those with an MIC ≥ 4 μg/mL were defined as resistant (R).

International guidelines recommend an initial loading dose of 2.0–2.5 mg/kg of PMB, followed by a maintenance dose of 2.5–3.0 mg/kg/day administered intravenously in two divided doses.27 The package insert for CS recommend a maintenance dose of 44–66 mg/kg/day, administered intravenously in two or three divided doses. However, owing to concerns regarding nephrotoxicity and cost‑effectiveness, real‑world polymyxin utilization might not have strictly adhered to these recommendations. Given the retrospective nature of this study, the attending physicians independently determined the specific type of polymyxin, the administration of a loading dose, the maintenance dosage, and the dosing frequency, without any influence from this study.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality, defined as death from any cause occurring within 28 days after the initiation of CS or PMB treatment during hospitalization.

The secondary outcomes assessed in this study included microbial clearance, clinical success, and the proportion of patients who achieved normalization of inflammatory parameters. Microbial clearance was defined as the eradication of CR‑GNB, as confirmed by two consecutive etiological examinations performed at least 3 days apart following the initiation of polymyxin therapy. Clinical success was defined as the resolution of clinical signs and symptoms, the normalization of at least two inflammatory markers (including white blood cell count [WBC], neutrophil percentage [NEUT%], C‑reactive protein [CRP], and procalcitonin [PCT]), and survival after 7 days of treatment. Normalization of inflammatory parameters was defined as the attainment of values within the following ranges at 7 days post‑treatment: WBC, 4–10 10E9/L; NEUT%, 50%–70%; PCT, 0–0.5 ng/mL; and CRP, 0–6 mg/L.

Covariates

This study collected sociodemographic information, baseline comorbidities and clinical conditions, Charlson scores, ICU admission status, and laboratory values documented within 24 hours before the first intravenous dose of CS or PMB for the current infectious episode. In addition, this study assessed whether patients fulfilled the Sepsis‑3.028 definitions for sepsis or septic shock. The CR‑GNB species, infection source, polymyxin susceptibility (the minimum inhibitory concentration [MIC]), and the dose of polymyxin administered were extracted from the medical records. Additionally, this study documented the use of concomitant antimicrobial agents prescribed in combination with polymyxin for CR‑GNB infection—any of which might have affected 28‑day all‑cause mortality. These collected variables were subsequently incorporated into multivariable models to evaluate their independent associations with clinical outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size determination was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), dichotomous variables as frequencies or percentages, and ordinal variables as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were compared between groups using the chi‑square test or Fisher’s exact test, whereas continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test or independent samples t‑test. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to balance baseline covariates and mitigate potential bias. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were calculated to assess covariate balance between the CS group and the PMB group, with an SMD < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance.

A Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for factors associated with 28‑day all‑cause mortality. We defined baseline characteristics, exposure indicators, and survival endpoints as critical variables and assessed the proportion of missing proportions. Among the 808 initially screened patients, only 11 (1.36%) had incomplete critical data; therefore, we excluded these cases rather than applying multiple imputation.

All statistical tests were two‑tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.2 and IBM SPSS Statistics 25, and graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism 8. A post-hoc power analysis was also performed for the final multivariable Cox regression model. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s f2=0.15) and a two-sided α of 0.05, the achieved power with the current sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software.

Result

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Over the study period, 808 patients were treated with either CS or PMB for the index infectious episode. Of these, 211 met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled in this study. Following the selection process, this study included 118 patients in the PMB group and 93 patients in the CS group in the final analysis (Figure 1). The baseline characteristics of the patients included in this study were presented in Table 1. The mean age of the cohort was 61.2 years, and males constituted 75.4% (n = 159) of the sample. Diabetes mellitus, malignancy, and hypertension were among the most prevalent comorbid conditions, and their frequencies did not differ significantly between the two groups. Similarly, Charlson comorbidity scores showed no significant inter‑group differences. Notably, every patient in the PMB group had a documented surgical history within the prior two months (100%), whereas this characteristic was not uniformly present in the CS group—a disparity that underscored the intrinsic selection biases associated with the retrospective, observational design of this study.

Nevertheless, Kaplan–Meier analysis (Figure 2) revealed that the separation in cumulative survival between the two groups did not achieve statistical significance.

Secondary Outcomes

This study observed that 24 patients (25.8%) in the CS group and 24 patients (20.3%) in the PMB group achieved microbial clearance, a difference that was not statistically significant (P = 0.333) and remained non-significant after IPTW adjustment (27.4% vs 21.3%, P = 0.485) (Table 2). Similarly, clinical success was attained by 31 patients (33.3%) in the CS group and 32 patients (27.1%) in the PMB group, with no significant difference either before IPTW (P = 0.408) or after IPTW adjustment (32.2% vs 28.0%, P = 0.535).

With respect to inflammatory parameter normalization, this study noted that the proportion of patients with normalized PCT was significantly higher in the CS group compared with the PMB group in the unadjusted analysis (43.0% vs 28.0%, P = 0.033). Nevertheless, following IPTW adjustment, this disparity attenuated and was no longer statistically significant (39.6% vs 30.0%, P = 0.177). For WBC count, NEUT%, and CRP, there were no significant differences between the two groups, regardless of IPTW adjustment.

Discussion

During the study period, CR-GNB persisted at alarmingly high prevalence rates, posing an ongoing threat to global public health. According to the 2024 annual report of the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network,29 resistances to imipenem and meropenem among Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates was 22.6% and 23.4%, respectively, while Pseudomonas aeruginosa exhibited resistance rates of 21.3% to imipenem and 17.3% to meropenem. Notably, Acinetobacter baumannii demonstrated substantially higher resistance, approaching 65% for both carbapenems. Polymyxins constituted a cornerstone of treatment for such infections, yet real‑world comparisons between CS and PMB were scarce. To address this evidence gap, this study directly compared the clinical effectiveness of CS and PMB in patients with CR‑GNB infections, with the goal of informing therapeutic decision‑making.

A total of 211 patients infected with CR-GNB were enrolled in this study, and all patients received intravenous administration of CS or PMB. The initial characteristics of the two treatment groups were similar. During the study period, most patients had underlying comorbidities. The most common condition was prior surgery within 2 months, followed by hypertension, malignant tumor, a history of organ transplantation, and diabetes mellitus. The most common pathogen was CRAB, followed by CRKP and CRPA, which were isolated either alone or in combination with other carbapenem-susceptible organisms. These CR-GNB isolates demonstrated high susceptibility to polymyxins (MIC ≤ 2 μg/mL).

The 28-day all-cause mortality rate was markedly lower in the CS group than in the PMB group (8.6% vs 29.7%, P < 0.001), and this survival benefit remained significant after IPTW (11.0% vs 26.7%, P = 0.016). However, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed no significant difference in survival rates between the two groups (P = 0.056). Consistently, multivariable Cox regression analysis indicated that CS therapy was not independently associated with 28-day all-cause mortality (HR = 0.51, P = 0.547). Because the crude 28-day mortality rate differed markedly between the two groups (8 vs 35), a statistically significant difference was readily detected by conventional tests such as the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test (P < 0.05). Based on these findings, we hypothesize that CS treatment may not be an independent predictor of 28-day all-cause mortality.

This study found no significant differences between the CS group and the PMB group in any of the secondary outcomes, including microbial clearance, clinical success, and the proportion of patients who achieved normalization of inflammatory parameters. Of particular note, microbial clearance was achieved in 25.8% of patients in the CS group and 20.3% of patients in the PMB group (P = 0.333). Although one retrospective cohort study21 reported significantly higher microbial efficacy in the CS group than in the PMB group among ICU patients with CR‑GNB infection (57.1% vs 30.8%, P = 0.022), the sample size of that investigation was small (particularly the CS group, n = 36), which might have limited its statistical power. In contrast, several other retrospective cohort studies22,30–32 indicated no significant difference in clinical efficacy between PMB and CS in critically ill populations. However, we noted that CS is currently only approved and marketed in China, which inevitably limits the direct comparability of prior studies in terms of study design and patient populations. Taken together, we speculate that microbial clearance did not differ materially between CS and PMB.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, it was conducted as a retrospective cohort study. Although IPTW adjustment was applied, residual confounding from unmeasured variables still exists due to inherent biases associated with the retrospective study design. Secondly, all participants were recruited from a single center; the sample size was relatively modest (n = 211), which may restrict the generalizability of the results. Thirdly, post-hoc power analysis for a medium effect size (f2=0.15) yielded 69.7% power with 43 events, below the 80% benchmark. Moreover, the resulting wide confidence interval also indicates an increased risk of committing a type II of error. Thus, further multicenter prospective randomized controlled trials or retrospective studies are warranted to validate the robustness of our findings. Fourthly, dosage regimens and dose modifications were determined at the clinicians’ discretion, and not all patients received a loading dose. Future research should focus on developing standardized dosing guidelines for CS and PMB, as well as assessing their effectiveness when used under these standardized conditions.

Conclusion

Taken together, although the IPTW-adjusted analysis showed a difference in mortality between CS and PMB, multivariable Cox regression did not confirm an independent protective effect of CS, suggesting that the observed survival benefit may be confounded. No significant differences were observed in microbial clearance, clinical success, or inflammatory markers. Therefore, without considering safety outcomes, we speculate that the clinical effectiveness of CS may be comparable to that of PMB. However, given the retrospective single-center design and potential residual confounding, this finding warrants further validation through prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes.
