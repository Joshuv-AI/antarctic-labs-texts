Abstract
Forecasting risk (as measured by quantiles) and systemic risk (as measured by Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016 ’s ( Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016 ) CoVaR) is important in economics and finance. However, past research has shown that predictive relationships may be unstable over time. Therefore, this paper develops structural break tests in predictive quantile and CoVaR regressions. These tests can detect changes in the forecasting power of covariates, and are based on the principle of self-normalization. We show that our tests are valid irrespective of whether the predictors are stationary or near-stationary, rendering the tests suitable for a range of practical applications. Simulations illustrate the good finite-sample properties of our tests. Two empirical applications concerning equity premium and systemic risk forecasting models show the usefulness of the tests. Keywords: Change Points, CoVaR, Mild Integration, Predictive Regressions, Quantiles JEL classification: C12 (Hypothesis Testing); C52 (Model Evaluation, Validation, and Selection); C58 (Financial Econometrics)

Motivation

Predictive regressions are a widely-used tool to forecast economic and financial variables.
However, the predictive content of some variables may change over time.
For instance, empirical evidence suggests that parameters in predictive models for macroeconomic time series vary.

Stock and Watson 2003
show this for models predicting output growth via the term spread, and inflation prediction models using goods and commodity prices.
In finance, the predictability of stock returns via valuation ratios (such as dividend–price ratios) appears to vary over time
(
Welch and Goyal 2008
;
Pettenuzzo and Timmermann 2011
;
Goyal et al. 2023
;
Tu and Xie 2023
)
, as does the predictability of dividend growth by dividend yields
(
Chen 2009
)
.
In response, many formal tests for changes in predictive mean regressions have been developed
(
Gonzalo and Pitarakis 2012
;
Georgiev et al. 2018
;
Zhu et al. 2023
)
.

One feature in predictive regressions that has attracted a lot of (past and recent) interest is that covariates may display different degrees of persistence.
In the literature on testing stock return predictability,
Stambaugh 1999
was the first to demonstrate that using persistent variables (such as dividend-price ratios) to forecast stock returns distorts standard inference tools, which are developed for stationary predictors.
This observation has spawned a plethora of subsequent work on persistence-robust inference in predictive regressions
(
Campbell and Yogo 2006
;
Cai et al. 2015
;
Kostakis et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2021
;
Demetrescu et al. 2022
)
.

In this paper, we connect the structural break literature with that focusing on robustifying inference in predictive regressions with respect to different degrees of predictor persistence.
Specifically, our first main contribution is to develop structural break tests for the coefficients in predictive quantile regressions, which are valid irrespective of whether the predictors are stationary or near-stationary.
Extending these tests also to predictive CoVaR regressions is our second main contribution.

Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016
’s
(
Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016
)
CoVaR is essentially a conditional quantile, such that CoVaR regressions naturally extend quantile regressions.
As
Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016
argue, the fact that CoVaR is a
conditional
quantile renders it a suitable systemic risk measure.

Such change point tests for quantile and CoVaR regressions are important for at least two reasons.
First, unstable models should be used with caution (if at all) for forecasting purposes
(
Pesaran et al. 2013
)
.
Second, knowledge of structural breaks may improve modeling attempts and generate further research into the underlying causes of the instabilities.
For instance,
Smith and Timmermann 2021
carry out this program for predictive mean regressions.
Specifically, they show that suitably accounting for breaks improves the forecasting performance of several predictors of stock returns.

We mention that typically change point tests in regression contexts require knowledge of the persistence of the predictors.
For instance, in their change point tests for predictive regressions,
Zhu et al. 2023
distinguish between stationary and non-stationary covariates, and show that asymptotic distributions depend on which of the two are present.

So one key feature of our change point tests is the robustness to the predictor persistence.
Indeed, there are many applications of quantile and CoVaR regressions, where covariates exhibit different degrees of serial dependence.
In quantile regressions, the prime examples are perhaps (very persistent) dividend–price ratios and other valuation measures as predictors for quantiles of the stock return distribution
(
Lee 2016
;
Fan and Lee 2019
;
Cai et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
.
In forecasting systemic risk in the financial sector via CoVaR regressions,
Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016
use the short-term TED spread (i.e., the difference between the 3-month LIBOR rate and the 3-month secondary market Treasury bill rate) and equity volatility as systemic risk predictors, both of which are known to be highly persistent.

Brunnermeier et al. 2020
use strongly serially dependent inflation as a predictor of systemic risk, citing evidence that it is a contributor to financial crises.
They also include 10-year government bond rates (which are a classic example of an integrated, highly persistent time series), since these account for the connection between sovereigns and banks.
In light of this variety of predictors displaying different degrees of serial dependence, persistence-robust change point tests for quantile and CoVaR regressions—such as ours—seem to be called for.

The works most closely related to our first main contribution (i.e., the development of persistence-robust change point tests in predictive quantile regressions) are those of
Lee 2016
and
Fan and Lee 2019
on the one hand, and
Qu 2008
and
Oka and Qu 2011
on the other hand.

Lee 2016
and
Fan and Lee 2019
propose hypothesis tests for the parameters in predictive quantile regressions that are valid for stationary, near-stationary and even integrated predictors.
However, their tests implicitly assume that the predictive relationship is stable throughout the sample.
This assumption also pervades the subsequent quantile prediction literature spawned by their work
(
Cai et al. 2023
;
Fan et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
.

Qu 2008
and
Oka and Qu 2011
develop structural break tests for quantile regressions.
Yet, their tests are only valid for stationary covariates.
In contrast, we derive inferential procedures for structural change in predictive quantile regressions that are robust to (certain degrees of) predictor persistence.
A further strand of related work is that of
Cai et al. 2015
and
Georgiev et al. 2018
.
These authors derive break tests for predictive mean regressions (instead of quantile regressions) that are robust to integrated or near-integrated predictors (instead of stationary and near-stationary) predictors.
Moreover, the predictors are mostly assumed to be univariate, whereas our framework allows for multivariate predictors with more complex dynamics by building on
Magdalinos and Phillips 2020
.

The econometric literature related to our second main contribution (i.e., the development of persistence-robust change point tests in predictive CoVaR regressions) is very limited.
To the best of our knowledge, there are only theoretical results for standard inference in CoVaR regressions with stationary regressors
(
Dimitriadis and Hoga 2024
;
Leng et al. 2024
)
.
In particular, these papers do not consider the problem of structural break testing.

Our persistence-robust change point tests work by comparing estimates of regression coefficients based on suitably chosen subsamples.
As will be shown below, the subsample estimates converge (in a functional sense) to Brownian motion both when predictors are stationary and near-stationary.
Because these Brownian motions only differ in their covariance matrices, self-normalized structural break tests à la
Shao and Zhang 2010
are valid for stationary and near-stationary predictors in the sense that no
a priori
knowledge of their persistence is required.
The technical development relies on recent results for stationary and near-stationary variables in
Magdalinos and Phillips 2020
, and also on an extension of
Knight 1989
’s
(
Knight 1989
)
Convexity lemma, which is due to
Kato 2009
.

Apart from delivering persistence-robustness, self-normalization (SN) also offers further advantages.
First, it is straightforward to implement, as it only requires recursively estimated coefficients as inputs.
Second, our functional results underlying the SN-based change point tests also facilitate “off-the-shelf” inference for the full-sample estimates when no break was detected; see Section
4.2
for an application.
Third, “standard” change point tests that involve consistent estimation of asymptotic (long-run) variances often suffer from the problem of nonmonotonic power, where power may even
decrease
in the distance from the null
(
Vogelsang 1999
)
.
By a clever construction of the normalizer, SN sidesteps this problem and does not suffer from this non-monotonicity
(
Shao and Zhang 2010
)
.
Fourth, compared with approaches that directly estimate asymptotic variances, SN-based tests often offer more adequate size control in finite samples
(
Shao 2015
)
.

Simulations show that our tests possess excellent finite-sample performance.
Under both stationary and near-stationary predictors, size is close to the nominal level for sample sizes typically encountered in practice.
This not only holds for the quantile regression (QR) but even for the CoVaR regression, where the effective sample size is severely reduced by the very definition of CoVaR as a systemic risk measure.
We also show that our tests have high power in detecting deviations from the null of constancy.
Such deviations may occur early, in the middle or late in the sample without unduly affecting the detection frequency.

Our third main contribution is to apply our tests to two real data sets.
First, we use (updated) data from
Welch and Goyal 2008
on the equity premium and several putative predictors, such as the dividend-yield.
While
Welch and Goyal 2008
,
Goyal et al. 2023
and others find that hardly any variable contains true predictive content for the
mean
of the equity premium, Lee and coauthors find some evidence for predictability of
quantiles
of the equity premium
(
Lee 2016
;
Fan and Lee 2019
;
Fan et al. 2023
)
.
Applying our QR stability test, we find that the predictive relation between equity premium quantiles and a wide range of forecasting variables is subject to marked changes.

Our second application considers systemic risk prediction in the US financial sector using the volatility index (VIX), which measures the short-term future volatility in the S&P 500 expected by market participants
(
Whaley 2009
)
.
The VIX and other measures of market volatility are widely used as predictors of systemic risk
(
Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016
;
Han et al. 2016
;
Nedeljkovic 2020
)
.
We measure systemic risk for all global systemically important US banks with respect to the S&P 500 Financials.
Here, we find little evidence for changes in the predictive relationship.
This implies that full-sample estimates of the CoVaR regression are trustworthy.
Computing these reveals (statistically significant) predictive content of the VIX for future levels of systemic risk in the US banking system.

Main Results

2.1
Predictive QR

Let
Y_{t}
denote the dependent variable of interest, such as the equity premium (used in Section
4.1
).
This variable is forecasted by a
(k+1)\times 1
-vector of predictors
\bm{X}_{t-1}
.
(We use bold font to denote vector- or matrix-valued quantities and normal font to denote scalar-valued objects.)
As mentioned in the Motivation, several possibly persistent variables have been used in quantile regressions as predictors.

To introduce the (conditional) quantiles, the time-
t
information set is
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(Y_{t},\bm{X}_{t},Y_{t-1},\bm{X}_{t-1},\ldots)
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
denotes the probability level.
The quantity to be modeled is the conditional
\alpha
-quantile
Q_{\alpha}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Q_{\alpha}(F_{Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}})
of the cumulative distribution function
F_{Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{P}\{Y_{t}\leq\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\}
of
Y_{t}
conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
.
This is typically done in the predictive QR

Y_{t}=\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\alpha}_{0}+\varepsilon_{t},\qquad Q_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=0,

(1)

where
\bm{X}_{t-1}=(1,\bm{x}_{t-1}^{\prime})^{\prime}
contains the
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-valued (non-constant) predictors
\bm{x}_{t-1}
.
The assumption on the QR error
\varepsilon_{t}
ensures that
Q_{\alpha}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\alpha}_{0}
.
Therefore, the coefficient
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
measures the strength of the predictive content of
\bm{X}_{t-1}
for the
\alpha
-quantile of
Y_{t}
.

Given a sample
\big\{(Y_{t},\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime})^{\prime}\big\}_{t=1,\ldots,n}
, the parameters in
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
are typically estimated via

\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}=\argmin_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\rho_{\alpha}(Y_{t}-\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\alpha}),

where
\rho_{\alpha}(u)=u(\alpha-\mathds{1}_{\{u<0\}})
is the standard pinball loss known from quantile regressions
(
Koenker and Bassett 1978
)
.
However, underlying this estimator is the assumption that the predictive relationship in (
1
) is unchanged throughout the sample.
The next subsection develops tests for this tacit assumption.

2.2
Change Point Test for Predictive QR

Since the predictive content of the
\bm{X}_{t-1}
may vary over time, the coefficients
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
in (
1
) may vary with
t
, such that

Y_{t}=\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\alpha}_{0,t}+\varepsilon_{t},\qquad Q_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=0.

Our goal is to develop a test for the constancy of the
\bm{\alpha}_{0,t}
’s:

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{Q}\colon\bm{\alpha}_{0,1}=\ldots=\bm{\alpha}_{0,n}\equiv\bm{\alpha}_{0}.

To do so, our method of break detection relies on estimating the QR parameters based on subsamples
\big\{(Y_{t},\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime})^{\prime}\big\}_{t=\lfloor nr\rfloor+1,\ldots,\lfloor ns\rfloor}
, where
0\leq r<s\leq 1
and
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
denotes the floor function.
Specifically, the subsample estimate is

\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}(r,s)=\argmin_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}}\sum_{t=\lfloor nr\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\rho_{\alpha}(Y_{t}-\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\alpha}).

The derivation of our change point test requires several assumptions on the predictive QR model.
Regarding the QR errors
\varepsilon_{t}
, we impose:

Assumption 1

(Conditional density of QR errors)
.

(i)

There exists some
d>0
, such that the distribution of
\varepsilon_{t}
conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
has a density
f_{\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(\cdot)
on
[-d,d]
.

(ii)

\sup_{x\in[-d,d]}f_{\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(x)\leq\overline{f}<\infty
for all
t\geq 1
.

(iii)

There exists some
L>0
, such that for all
t\geq 1
,
\big|f_{\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(x_{1})-f_{\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(x_{2})\big|\leq L|x_{1}-x_{2}|
for all
-d\leq x_{1},x_{2}\leq d
.

Assumption
1
imposes smoothness conditions on the conditional distribution of
\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, viz. the existence of a uniformly bounded and Lipschitz-continuous density.
The conditions are identical to the assumptions on the QR error densities in
Fitzenberger 1997
.

In line with almost all of the predictive regression literature, we assume the predictors to be generated by the additive components model

\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{x}+\bm{\xi}_{t},

where
\bm{\mu}_{x}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-valued constant and the (zero-mean) stochastic component
\bm{\xi}_{t}
obeys the following autoregression:

Assumption 2

(Predictors)
.

The stochastic
\bm{\xi}_{t}
are generated by

\bm{\xi}_{t}=\bm{R}_{n}\bm{\xi}_{t-1}+\bm{u}_{t},

(2)

where
\bm{u}_{t}
is a linear process defined in Assumption
3
below and the autoregressive matrix
\bm{R}_{n}
satisfies

\bm{C}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=n^{\kappa}(\bm{R}_{n}-\bm{I}_{k})\underset{(n\to\infty)}{\longrightarrow}\bm{C},

(3)

where
\bm{I}_{k}
denotes the
(k\times k)
-identity matrix and
\kappa\geq 0
.
The variables
\bm{\xi}_{t}
belong to one of the following classes:

(I0)

Stationary predictors:
Equation (
3
) holds with
\kappa=0
and
\bm{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bm{R}_{n}=\bm{I}_{k}+\bm{C}
has spectral radius
\rho(\bm{R})<1
.

(NS)

Near-stationary predictors:
Equation (
3
) holds with
\kappa\in(0,1)
and
\bm{C}
is a negative stable matrix, i.e., all its eigenvalues have negative real part.

The process
\bm{\xi}_{t}
in (
2
) is initialized at
\bm{\xi}_{0}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
under (I0) and
\bm{\xi}_{0}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{\kappa/2})
under (NS).

It turns out that the estimator
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}(0,1)
has a different convergence rate depending on whether the predictors are stationary or near-stationary in Assumption
2
.
To unify notation across these two cases, we introduce the normalizing matrix

\bm{D}_{n}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{n}\bm{I}_{k+1}&\text{for (I0)},\\
\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{n},n^{\frac{1+\kappa}{2}}\bm{I}_{k})&\text{for (NS)}.\end{cases}

Our Assumption
2
corresponds to the case of stationary and near-stationary regressors in Assumption N from
Magdalinos and Phillips 2020
with, in their notation,
\kappa_{n}=n^{\kappa}
.
Variables generated according to (NS) in Assumption
2
have also been termed mildly integrated
(
Phillips and Magdalinos 2009
;
Lee 2016
)
or moderately integrated
(
Magdalinos and Phillips 2009
)
.
However, we adopt the terminology of
Magdalinos and Phillips 2020
here, as it is closest to our framework.

We also follow the linear framework of
Magdalinos and Phillips 2020
for the predictor innovations
\bm{u}_{t}
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the spectral norm.

Assumption 3

(Predictor innovations)
.

For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bm{u}_{t}
has linear process representation

\bm{u}_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bm{F}_{j}\bm{\varepsilon}_{t-j},\qquad\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|\bm{F}_{j}\|<\infty,\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j\|\bm{F}_{j}\|^{2}<\infty,

where
\bm{F}_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bm{I}_{k}
,
\bm{F}(1)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\bm{F}_{j}
has full rank, and
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-valued martingale difference sequence with respect to
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}=\sigma(\bm{\varepsilon}_{t},\bm{\varepsilon}_{t-1},\ldots)
, such that
\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t-1}}[\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}^{\prime}]=\bm{\varSigma}_{\varepsilon}>0
and the sequence
\{\|\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\|^{2}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is uniformly integrable.

Assumption
3
ensures that the partial sums of
\bm{u}_{t}
satisfy a functional central limit theorem (FCLT).
Specifically, let
D_{k}[0,1]
denote the space of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-valued functions that are càdlàg componentwise.
We endow this space with the Skorohod topology
(
Billingsley 1999
)
.
Then, we obtain from
Magdalinos and Phillips 2020
that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\bm{u}_{t}\overset{d}{\underset{(n\to\infty)}{\longrightarrow}}\bm{\varOmega}_{\xi\xi}^{1/2}\bm{W}(s)\qquad\text{in }D_{k}[0,1],

where
\bm{\varOmega}_{\xi\xi}=\bm{F}(1)\bm{\varSigma}_{\varepsilon}\bm{F}^{\prime}(1)
and
\bm{W}(\cdot)
is a
k
-variate standard Brownian motion.

In their cointegrated system,
Magdalinos and Phillips 2020
consider
\bm{Y}_{t}=\bm{A}\bm{x}_{t-1}+\bm{u}_{0t}
(instead of
Y_{t}=(1,\bm{x}_{t-1}^{\prime})^{\prime}\bm{\alpha}_{0}+\varepsilon_{t}
as we do in (
1
)).
In particular,
Magdalinos and Phillips 2020
impose a linear process assumption jointly on
(\bm{u}^{\prime}_{0t},\bm{u}_{t}^{\prime})^{\prime}
.
We do not have to impose a similar joint linear process assumption on our
(\varepsilon_{t},\bm{u}_{t}^{\prime})^{\prime}
.
Instead, the following suffices for our purposes:

Assumption 4

(Joint behavior of QR errors and predictors)
.

It holds uniformly in
s\in[0,1]
that, as
n\to\infty
,

\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}f_{\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(0)\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}s\bm{K}

for some positive definite
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}
.

Note that the predictors
\bm{X}_{t-1}
are solely functions of past
\bm{u}_{t}
.
Therefore, Assumption
4
implicitly restricts the serial dependence in
(\varepsilon_{t},\bm{u}_{t}^{\prime})^{\prime}
by imposing a uniform law of large numbers for
\big\{f_{\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(0)\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\big\}
.
For stationary predictors, where
\bm{D}_{n}=\sqrt{n}\bm{I}_{k}
, Assumption
4
reads as
n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}f_{\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(0)\bm{X}_{t-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}s\bm{K}
.
In this form, it is virtually identical to assumptions entertained by
Qu 2008
,
Su and Xiao 2008
and
Oka and Qu 2011
.

Utilizing a version of the Convexity lemma of
Knight 1989

To introduce it, we define

\bm{\varOmega}_{XX}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\bm{\mu}_{x}^{\prime}\\
\bm{\mu}_{x}&\bm{\varOmega}_{xx}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\bm{V}_{XX}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{V}_{\xi\xi}\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{V}_{\xi\xi}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{r\bm{C}}\bm{\varOmega}_{\xi\xi}e^{r\bm{C}}\,\mathrm{d}r
and
\bm{\varOmega}_{xx}=\bm{\mu}_{x}\bm{\mu}_{x}^{\prime}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\bm{R}^{j}\big[\bm{\varGamma}_{u}(0)+\bm{R}\bm{\varGamma}+\bm{\varGamma}^{\prime}\bm{R}^{\prime}\big](\bm{R}^{j})^{\prime}
with
\bm{\varGamma}=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\bm{R}^{l-1}\bm{\varGamma}_{u}^{\prime}(l)
and
\bm{\varGamma}_{u}(l)=\mathbb{E}[\bm{u}_{t}\bm{u}_{t-l}^{\prime}]

Theorem 1
.

Under
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{Q}
and Assumptions

where
\bm{W}(\cdot)
is a
(k+1)

\bm{\varSigma}=\alpha(1-\alpha)\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{\varOmega}\bm{K}^{-1}

\bm{\varOmega}=\bm{\varOmega}_{XX}

\displaystyle=\int_{\epsilon}^{s}\big\{\bm{W}(r)-(r/s)\bm{W}(s)\big\}\big\{\bm{W}(r)-(r/s)\bm{W}(s)\big\}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\int_{s}^{1-\epsilon}\Big\{\big[\bm{W}(1)-\bm{W}(r)\big]-\big((1-r)/(1-s)\big)\big[\bm{W}(1)-\bm{W}(s)\big]\Big\}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\Big\{\big[\bm{W}(1)-\bm{W}(r)\big]-\big((1-r)/(1-s)\big)\big[\bm{W}(1)-\bm{W}(s)\big]\Big\}^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}r.

.

The test statistic
\mathcal{U}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}
has two different components.
First, the terms involving
\big[\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}(0,s)-\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}(s,1)\big]
indicate a structural instability if they are far from zero and, thus, give the test its power.
When
s
is close to 0 or 1, this difference is weighted down by the factor
s^{2}(1-s)^{2}
, because then either
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}(0,s)
or
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}(s,1)
is based on very few observations, such that larger differences between the two estimates are not uncommon.

The second component of
\mathcal{U}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}
is the normalizer
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}(s)
, which serves to give a nuisance parameter-free limiting distribution.
It does so by canceling out the
\bm{\varSigma}
matrix from Theorem
1
—for (I0) and (NS) predictors alike.
To see the other convenient feature of
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}(s)
, suppose that there is a single break in
s^{\ast}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{H}_{1}^{Q}\colon\bm{\alpha}_{0,1}=\ldots=\bm{\alpha}_{0,\lfloor ns^{\ast}\rfloor}\neq\bm{\alpha}_{0,\lfloor ns^{\ast}\rfloor+1}=\ldots=\bm{\alpha}_{0,n}\quad\text{for some}\ s^{\ast}\in(0,1)

holds.
Then,
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}(s^{\ast})
is kept from exploding because the two integrals, being based on break-free estimates, remain small.
Note that
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}(s^{\ast})
diverging to infinity would have the detrimental effect of a “small” value of
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}^{-1}(s^{\ast})
, leading to a loss of power.
We mention that change point tests that estimate
\bm{\varSigma}
directly (instead of exploiting self-normalization) often possess nonmonotonic power, i.e., power that does not increase in the distance from the null, precisely because the estimator of the nuisance parameters (here:
\bm{\varSigma}
) pushes the test statistic down under the alternative with a break in
s^{\ast}
.
We refer to the discussion in
Shao and Zhang 2010
for more detail.

The type of self-normalized structural break test statistic
\mathcal{U}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}
is due to
Shao and Zhang 2010
.
This test statistic is specifically designed to be consistent for the one-break alternative
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{Q}
.
Indeed, it may be shown similarly as in
Shao and Zhang 2010
that under
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{Q}
the power of our test converges to 1, as
n\to\infty
, when
s^{\ast}\in[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]
.
We leave details for brevity.
We mention that test statistics tailored for multiple possible breaks may also be constructed along the lines of
Shao and Zhang 2010
and
Zhang and Lavitas 2018
.

One noteworthy feature of the limiting distribution
\mathcal{W}_{k+1}
is that it is identical under (I0) and (NS) predictors, which delivers the robustness of our test.
The reason self-normalization leads to such a unified limit lies in Theorem
1
, which shows that the functional limit of the subsample estimates is Brownian motion.
Thanks to SN, the limit is also nuisance parameter-free (e.g., it does not depend on
\bm{C}
in the (NS) case), such that critical values can easily be simulated.

2.3
Predictive CoVaR Regressions

To introduce CoVaR regressions, we first define CoVaR as a systemic risk measure.
Denote by
Z_{t}
a variable whose systemic riskiness with respect to
Y_{t}
is to be measured.
In the empirical application in Section
4.2
,
Z_{t}
will denote the log-losses of a large US bank and
Y_{t}
those of the S&P 500 Financials.
Slightly overloading the notation introduced in Section
2.1
, we now define
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(Z_{t},Y_{t},\bm{X}_{t},Z_{t-1},Y_{t-1},\bm{X}_{t-1},\ldots)
to be the information set of interest.
Then, for
\beta\in(0,1)
, we define

\operatorname{CoVaR}_{\beta\mid\alpha}\big((Z_{t},Y_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)=Q_{\beta}\big(Z_{t}\mid Y_{t}\geq Q_{\alpha}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}),\ \mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)=Q_{\beta}(F_{Z_{t}\mid Y_{t}\geq Q_{\alpha}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}),\,\mathcal{F}_{t-1}})

to be the
\beta
-quantile of the distribution of
Z_{t}\mid Y_{t}\geq Q_{\alpha}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}),\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
.
Conceptually, the CoVaR is simply a quantile.
It is only distinct from a standard quantile by the conditioning event
\{Y_{t}\geq Q_{\alpha}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\}
.
For
\alpha=0
, this conditioning event occurs with probability one, and the CoVaR degenerates to the VaR, i.e.,
\operatorname{CoVaR}_{\beta\mid\alpha}\big((Z_{t},Y_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)=Q_{\beta}(Z_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
.
As
\alpha\uparrow 1
, the CoVaR measures the risk in
Z_{t}
(via its
\beta
-quantile) conditional on
Y_{t}
exceeding a large quantile, such that the
systemic
risk interpretation of the CoVaR becomes apparent.
We model this quantity similarly as the predictive quantiles in (
1
) via the (quantile, CoVaR) regression (henceforth simply called a
CoVaR regression
)

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\alpha}_{0}+\varepsilon_{t},\qquad Q_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=0,

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\delta_{t},\qquad\operatorname{CoVaR}_{\beta\mid\alpha}\big((\delta_{t},\varepsilon_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)=0.

(4)

Once again, the assumption on the (quantile, CoVaR) errors
(\varepsilon_{t},\delta_{t})^{\prime}
ensures that
\operatorname{CoVaR}_{\beta\mid\alpha}\big((Z_{t},Y_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)=\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\beta}_{0}
, such that
\bm{\beta}_{0}
quantifies the forecasting power of
\bm{X}_{t-1}
for future levels of systemic risk.

To estimate the predictive CoVaR regression, we adapt the estimator of
Dimitriadis and Hoga 2024
to our context.
Given a sample
\big\{(Z_{t},Y_{t},\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime})^{\prime}\big\}_{t=1,\ldots,n}
, we estimate
\bm{\beta}_{0}
via

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{Y_{t}>\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}\}}\rho_{\beta}(Z_{t}-\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\beta}).

This estimator is almost identical to the QR estimator
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}
, except that only observations
(Z_{t},Y_{t},\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime})^{\prime}
are used for which
Y_{t}
is larger than its forecasted conditional quantile, i.e.,
Y_{t}>\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}
.

2.4
Change Point Test for Predictive CoVaR Regressions

Much like for predictive QR, the predictive content of some variables for systemic risk may vary over time.
When this occurs, the regression parameters
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
and
\bm{\beta}_{0}
may be subject to change over time, leading to

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\alpha}_{0,t}+\epsilon_{t},\qquad Q_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}),

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\beta}_{0,t}+\delta_{t},\qquad\operatorname{CoVaR}_{\beta\mid\alpha}\big((\delta_{t},\varepsilon_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)=0.

Our goal is to construct a test of the no-break hypothesis

\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\operatorname{CoVaR}}\colon\bm{\alpha}_{0,1}=\ldots\bm{\alpha}_{0,n}\equiv\bm{\alpha}_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\beta}_{0,1}=\ldots\bm{\beta}_{0,n}\equiv\bm{\beta}_{0}.

To do so, we again rely on subsample estimates
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}(r,s)
and

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}(r,s)=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}}\sum_{t=\lfloor nr\rfloor+1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\mathds{1}_{\{Y_{t}>\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}(r,s)\}}\rho_{\alpha}(Z_{t}-\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{\beta}).

Deriving functional central limit theory for these subsample estimates requires some further assumptions beyond those introduced in Section
2.2
.
Specifically, we impose the following analog of Assumption
1
.

Assumption 5

(Conditional density of CoVaR errors)
.

(i)

The distribution of
(\varepsilon_{t},\delta_{t})^{\prime}
conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
has a density
f_{(\varepsilon_{t},\delta_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(\cdot)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(ii)

\sup_{x,y\in[-d,d]}f_{(\varepsilon_{t},\delta_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(x,y)\leq\overline{f}<\infty
for all
t\geq 1
, and
d>0
from Assumption
1
.

(iii)

There exists some
L>0
, such that for all
t\geq 1
, all
-d\leq x_{1},x_{2}\leq d
and all
-d\leq y_{1},y_{2}\leq d
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{(\varepsilon_{t},\delta_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(x_{1},y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{(\varepsilon_{t},\delta_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(x_{2},y)\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg|\leq L|x_{1}-x_{2}|,

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{(\varepsilon_{t},\delta_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(x,y_{1})\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{(\varepsilon_{t},\delta_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(x,y_{2})\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg|\leq L|y_{1}-y_{2}|.

Similarly, the following assumption is the counterpart to Assumption
4
.

Assumption 6

(Joint behavior of CoVaR errors and predictors)
.

It holds uniformly in
s\in[0,1]
that, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{(\varepsilon_{t},\delta_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(x,0)\,\mathrm{d}x\bigg)\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}s\bm{K}_{\ast},

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{(\varepsilon_{t},\delta_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(0,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg)\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}s\bm{K}_{\dagger}

for some positive definite matrices
\bm{K}_{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}
and
\bm{K}_{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}
.

For the next theorem, define
\bm{\varOmega}_{\ast}=\alpha^{-1}\beta(1-\beta)\bm{\varOmega}+\big[(1-\beta)\bm{K}-\bm{K}_{\dagger}\big]\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{\varOmega}\bm{K}^{-1}\big[(1-\beta)\bm{K}-\bm{K}_{\dagger}\big]
(with
\bm{\varOmega}
defined in Theorem
1
) and

\overline{\bm{K}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{K}_{\ast}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\overline{\bm{\varOmega}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\varOmega}&\big[(1-\beta)\bm{K}-\bm{K}_{\dagger}\big]\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{\varOmega}\\
\big[(1-\beta)\bm{K}-\bm{K}_{\dagger}\big]\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{\varOmega}&\bm{\varOmega}_{\ast}\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem 2
.

Under
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\operatorname{CoVaR}}
and Assumptions
1
–
6
it holds for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
that, as
n\to\infty
,

s\begin{pmatrix}\bm{D}_{n}\big[\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}(0,s)-\bm{\alpha}_{0}\big]\\
\bm{D}_{n}\big[\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}(0,s)-\bm{\beta}_{0}\big]\end{pmatrix}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\overline{\bm{\varSigma}}^{1/2}\overline{\bm{W}}(s)\qquad\text{in }D_{2k+2}[\epsilon,1],

where
\overline{\bm{W}}(\cdot)
is a
(2k+2)
-variate standard Brownian motion and
\overline{\bm{\varSigma}}=\alpha(1-\alpha)\overline{\bm{K}}^{-1}\cdot\overline{\bm{\varOmega}}\cdot\overline{\bm{K}}^{-1}
.

See Appendix
C
.
∎

Theorem
2
can be used in exactly the same way as Theorem
1
to construct a change point test.
To so so, define
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{n}(r,s)=\big(\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}^{\prime}(r,s),\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}^{\prime}(r,s)\big)^{\prime}
.

Corollary 2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
it holds for any
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
that, as
n\to\infty
,

\mathcal{U}_{n,\bm{\gamma}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{s\in[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]}s^{2}(1-s)^{2}\big[\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{n}(0,s)-\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{n}(s,1)\big]^{\prime}\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\bm{\gamma}}^{-1}(s)\big[\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{n}(0,s)-\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{n}(s,1)\big]\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{W}_{2k+2},

where the normalizer
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\bm{\gamma}}(s)
is defined as
\bm{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}(s)
with
\widehat{\bm{\alpha}}_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
replaced by
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
at every occurrence.

Analogous to the proof of Corollary
1
.
∎

In Corollary
2
we have followed the bulk of the literature on testing for changes in regression models by basing our test on sequentially estimated coefficients
(
Andrews 1993
;
Qu 2008
;
Oka and Qu 2011
;
Georgiev et al. 2018
)
.
This leads to valid tests of the null of stability, yet the test result stays silent on
which
coefficient was responsible for the break.
Such questions of attribution are a worthy goal of future research.

One possible approach to this problem may be to apply a Bonferroni correction in testing
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\operatorname{CoVaR}}
.
Specifically, an overall level-
\iota
test of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\operatorname{CoVaR}}
is obtained by first testing
\bm{\alpha}_{0,1}=\ldots\bm{\alpha}_{0,n}\equiv\bm{\alpha}_{0}
at level
\iota/2
.
A rejection in this first stage indicates an instability in the QR, yet nothing further can be inferred about the CoVaR regressions (which requires a stable quantile regression to be reliably estimated).
If stability of the QR can, however, not be rejected,
\bm{\beta}_{0,1}=\ldots\bm{\beta}_{0,n}\equiv\bm{\beta}_{0}
is tested at level
\iota/2
in a second step (based on a test statistic
\mathcal{U}_{n,\bm{\beta}}
defined in analogy to
\mathcal{U}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}
and with the same distributional limit as given in Corollary
1
).
A rejection at this stage indicates an instability only in the CoVaR regression, not the quantile regression.

Once again, our test based on
\mathcal{U}_{n,\bm{\gamma}}
is, by construction, consistent against the one-break alternative

\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\operatorname{CoVaR}}\colon\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\alpha}_{0,1}\\
\bm{\beta}_{0,1}\end{pmatrix}=\ldots=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\alpha}_{0,\lfloor ns^{\ast}\rfloor}\\
\bm{\beta}_{0,\lfloor ns^{\ast}\rfloor}\end{pmatrix}\neq\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\alpha}_{0,\lfloor ns^{\ast}\rfloor+1}\\
\bm{\beta}_{0,\lfloor ns^{\ast}\rfloor+1}\end{pmatrix}=\ldots=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\alpha}_{0,n}\\
\bm{\beta}_{0,n}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for some}\ s^{\ast}\in(0,1);

see
Shao and Zhang 2010
.
We leave details to save space.

3.1
Data-Generating Process

As the data-generating process (DGP) we use the predictive CoVaR regression with

,
\alpha=\beta=0.9
and

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}Y_{t}\\
Z_{t}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\alpha}_{0,t}^{\prime}\\
\bm{\beta}_{0,t}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\bm{x}_{t-1}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{t}\\
\delta_{t}\end{pmatrix}.

Here, the predictors are generated by

\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{x}+\bm{\xi}_{t},\quad\bm{\xi}_{t}=\bm{R}_{n}\bm{\xi}_{t-1}+\bm{u}_{t}

with
\bm{\mu}_{x}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{\xi}_{0}=\bm{0}
.
In the (I0) setting, we use
\bm{R}_{n}=\bm{R}=\bm{I}_{k}+\bm{C}
for
\bm{C}=-c\bm{I}_{k}
with
c
ranging between
0.5
and
0.01
(in increments of
0.01
).
This choice of
\bm{C}
ensures that the spectral radius of
\bm{R}
is smaller than one, because
\rho(\bm{R})=1-c<1

Therefore,
\bm{R}_{n}=r_{n}\bm{I}_{k}

r_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=r\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1-c

Similarly, to generate (NS) predictors, we put

is negatively stable.

The predictor innovations are generated from the VAR(1) model

\bm{u}_{t}=\bm{\varPhi}\bm{u}_{t-1}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{t},

(5)

and the CoVaR errors are

\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{t}\\
\delta_{t}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{1t}-Q_{\alpha}(v_{1t})\\
v_{2t}-\operatorname{CoVaR}_{\beta\mid\alpha}\big((v_{2t},v_{1t})^{\prime}\big)\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{v}_{t}=(v_{1t},v_{2t})^{\prime}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}
are independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) draws from a joint normal distribution.
More specifically,

\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}_{t}\\
\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}\end{pmatrix}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(\bm{0},\bm{\varPsi}),\qquad\bm{\varPsi}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\varPsi}_{\bm{v}}&\bm{\varPsi}_{\bm{v}\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\prime}\\
\bm{\varPsi}_{\bm{v}\bm{\varepsilon}}&\bm{\varPsi}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{cases}\bm{\varPsi}_{\bm{v}}&=(1-\rho_{\bm{v}})\bm{I}_{2}+\rho_{\bm{v}}\bm{1}_{2}\bm{1}_{2}^{\prime},\\
\bm{\varPsi}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}&=(1-\rho_{\bm{\varepsilon}})\bm{I}_{k}+\rho_{\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{1}_{k}\bm{1}_{k}^{\prime},\\
\bm{\varPsi}_{\bm{v}\bm{\varepsilon}}&=\rho_{\bm{v}\bm{\varepsilon}}\bm{1}_{k}\bm{1}_{2}^{\prime},\end{cases}

such that
\bm{\varPsi}_{\bm{v}}
and
\bm{\varPsi}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}
are equicorrelation matrices
(
Engle and Kelly 2012
)
and
\bm{\varPsi}
is a multiple-block equicorrelation matrix.
For the parameters of the normal distribution of
(\bm{v}_{t}^{\prime},\bm{\varepsilon}_{t}^{\prime})^{\prime}
, we choose
\rho_{\bm{v}}=\rho_{\bm{\varepsilon}}=0
and
\rho_{\bm{v}\bm{\varepsilon}}=-0.95
.
The choice of
\rho_{\bm{v}\bm{\varepsilon}}=-0.95
is taken from
Lee 2016
and
Fan and Lee 2019
.
It is motivated by the fact that in predictive regressions for the equity premium with valuation ratios as predictors, the innovations exhibit strong negative correlation.
Similarly, for the autoregressive matrix of the predictor innovations in (
5
) we put
\bm{\varPhi}=-0.95\bm{I}_{k}
.

4.1
Quantile Predictability of the Equity Premium

The equity premium, defined as the excess return of equities over riskless bonds, is on average around 6–9% per year in developed countries
(
Mehra and Prescott 2008
, Table 3)
.
Much work in recent decades has investigated whether the equity premium can be forecasted by a wide range of predictors
(
Welch and Goyal 2008
)
.
The growing body of evidence, summarized by
Goyal et al. 2023
, seems to suggest that the historical mean as a forecast of the average equity premium remains a hard-to-beat benchmark, implying that few, if any, variables truly contain predictive content for the mean of the equity premium going forward.

However, non-predictability of the mean does not preclude predictability of quantiles of the equity premium to exist.
In fact,
Lee 2016
finds evidence for quantile predictability of the US equity premium for a large set of predictors.
Generally speaking, he finds that predictability of the median is weakest, with stronger degrees of predictability to be found in the tails.
Subsequently, Lee and coauthors extended inference methods for predictive quantile regressions
(
Fan and Lee 2019
;
Fan et al. 2023
)
.
However, the aforementioned authors assume structural stability of the forecasting models.

Therefore, we here investigate the stability of predictive quantile models for the equity premium.
Indeed, there may be a number of reasons why equity premium predictability may be liable to change over time.

Pesaran and Timmermann 2002
mention speculative bubbles, business cycles, rare disasters, time-varying risk aversion and changes in monetary policy as possible causes.
For instance, monetary policy changes could shift the fundamental value of stocks due to its influence on economic growth.
Accordingly,
Paye and Timmermann 2006
and
Timmermann 2008
find that return prediction models may work sometimes, but—more often than not—fail.
More recently,
Farmer et al. 2023
have explained this finding of “pockets of predictability” by a sticky expectations model with slow updating of investor beliefs about the long-lived component of future cash flows.

To formally study the stability of predictive QRs for the equity premium, we use our test based on the statistic
\mathcal{U}_{n,\bm{\alpha}}
from Corollary

We consider the monthly data analyzed by

4.2
The VIX as a Predictor for Systemic Risk

Up until the great financial crisis of 2007–9, financial regulation focused almost exclusively on microprudential objectives; that is, it focused on limiting the risks of each financial institution in isolation.
However, such individual regulations were insufficient to prevent the great financial crisis.
Thus, in the aftermath of the crisis, attention has shifted towards macroprudential objectives, where—next to ensuring the financial viability of each bank in isolation—the goal is to improve the stability of the financial system as a whole.
A key tool to achieving this are systemic risk measures, which gauge the interconnectedness of financial institutions
(
Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016
;
Acharya et al. 2017
)
.

As a consequence, researchers have aimed for a better understanding of systemic risk.
For instance,
Brunnermeier et al. 2020
examine the relationship between systemic risk (as measured by the CoVaR) and asset price bubbles.

Giglio et al. 2016
and
Brownlees and Engle 2017
find increases in systemic risk in the financial system to be connected to declines of real economic activity.

Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016
and
Han et al. 2016
investigate the predictive content of volatility for future levels of systemic risk.

However, whether such predictive relationships remain constant over time has not been investigated before due to a lack of appropriate statistical methodology which this paper provides.
We do so here specifically for the VIX as a predictor.
The VIX, whose value at time
t
we denote by
\operatorname{VIX}_{t}
, measures the risk-neutral expectation of the variance in stock market returns on the S&P 500.
It is computed by the Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE) based on option prices.
Figure
5
plots the VIX over our sample period from 2014–2023.
The most marked spike in expected volatility occurs during the COVID crisis in early 2020.
One can see clearly that the VIX took a long time to return to its pre-COVID level, suggesting a reasonably high persistence.

Figure 5
:
Level of the VIX from 2014–2023.

More formally, consistent with
Han and Zhang 2012
, we find that the VIX is somewhere on the borderline of stationarity.
The AR(1) coefficient estimate is 0.965, and the KPSS test rejects the null of stationarity at any conventional significance level.
However, the ADF–GLS test also rejects the null of a unit root in the VIX, with a
p
-value well below
1\%
.
In light of both test results, the exact degree of persistence in the VIX is difficult to determine.
Therefore, the persistence robustness of our test becomes an empirically desirable feature when testing for changes next.

We now test for constancy of the predictive relationship between the VIX and future levels of systemic risk in the US banking sector by applying our test from Corollary
2
.
Specifically, we use daily log-losses of the S&P 500 Financials (SPF) index as our
Z_{t}
, and the log-losses on a particular US bank as
Y_{t}
.
We focus on those US banks that are ranked as a global systemically important bank (G-SIB) by the
FSB 2023
.
Then,
\operatorname{CoVaR}_{\beta\mid\alpha}\big((Z_{t},Y_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)
measures the impact that an extreme loss of the bank has on the financial sector, i.e., its systemic riskiness.
We examine the structural stability of the following predictive model for the CoVaR:

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\operatorname{VIX}_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t},

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=0,

(6)

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\operatorname{VIX}_{t-1}+\delta_{t},

\displaystyle\operatorname{CoVaR}_{\beta\mid\alpha}\big((\delta_{t},\varepsilon_{t})^{\prime}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\big)=0.

(7)

Therefore, we investigate whether the predictive power of volatility for systemic risk is subject to change over time.
To estimate the model, we use daily data from 2014 to 2023, all obtained from
finance.yahoo.com
, resulting in a sample of size

Our above definition of the CoVaR corresponds to the standard definition of
Adrian and Brunnermeier 2016
, where the system (here: S&P 500 Financials) is considered conditional on some institution (here: US G-SIB) being in distress.
However, as pointed out by

The two definitions give complementary information.
For instance, a small, but highly connected, bank may have a large Exposure CoVaR, due to its many links with other institutions.
However, its “standard” CoVaR would be low because of its small size, that would allow other banks to quickly pick up its positions in case of failure.
Since the Exposure CoVaR is also of interest in its own right, we repeat part of the above analysis in Appendix

We stress that this significance result for the VIX is robust to whether or not the VIX is stationary or near-stationary.

5
Conclusion

Recent work has proposed a variety of hypothesis tests for parameters in predictive quantile regressions that are valid for different degrees of persistence in the regressors
(
Lee 2016
;
Fan and Lee 2019
;
Cai et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
.
In contrast, the issue of devising persistence-robust structural change tests in predictive QR has received no attention—despite the fact that a) predictive relationships are well-known to change over time and b) predictors with varying degrees of persistence are often employed in empirical work.
Our tests fill this gap not only for predictive QR, but also for predictive CoVaR regressions.

In his review of self-normalization,
Shao 2015
writes on the use of SN that “when the dependence in the time series is too strong (say, near-integrated time series), inference of certain parameter becomes difficult because the information aggregated over time does not accumulate quickly due to strong dependence.”
We show in this paper that when the dependence in the predictors is not too strong (at most nearly stationary), then self-normalized change-point tests still work in the sense of providing unified inference on possible breaks in the predictive relationship.

Two empirical applications highlight the importance of persistence-robust tests, where some putative equity premium predictors and the VIX offer examples of predictors somewhere on the boundary between stationarity and non-stationarity.
Our tests can validly be applied (in the sense of holding size) regardless of whether the variables are stationary or nearly-stationary.
While we find instabilities in quantile prediction models for the equity premium, the predictive relationship between volatility and future systemic risk of US banks seems more stable.

Future work could consider robustifying inference on change points also with respect to (I1) predictors.
In this case, we no longer expect SN to work, but the IVX approach of
Magdalinos and Phillips 2009
and
Phillips and Magdalinos 2009
may be a feasible option.
Indeed, IVX has been used successfully in developing unified inference in predictive QR
(
Lee 2016
)
.
One drawback, however, is that IVX leads to slower rates of convergence for the (NS) case and, thus, possibly lower power.
Investigations such as these are left for further research.

(NS) case:

With (
B.2
) and
\bm{x}_{t-1}=\bm{\mu}_{x}+\bm{\xi}_{t-1}

\displaystyle\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})\begin{pmatrix}n^{-1/2}\\
n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}\bm{x}_{t-1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})\begin{pmatrix}0\\
n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}\bm{\mu}_{x}+n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}\bm{R}_{n}^{t-1}\bm{\xi}_{0}\end{pmatrix}+\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})\begin{pmatrix}n^{-1/2}\\
n^{-1/2}\bm{\xi}_{n,t-1}\end{pmatrix}.

(B.3)

We consider each of the above terms separately.

Denote by
R_{n}^{t-1}
some generic element of the matrix
\bm{R}_{n}^{t-1}
.
Observe that
\big\{\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}R_{n}^{t-1}\big\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
is a martingale difference array (MDA), because

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\big[\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}R_{n}^{t-1}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\big[(\alpha-\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon_{t}\leq 0\}})\big]n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}R_{n}^{t-1}

\displaystyle=\big[\alpha-\mathbb{P}_{t-1}\{\varepsilon_{t}\leq 0\}\big]n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}R_{n}^{t-1}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:QR model}}{=}0.

By standard maximal inequalities for martingales
(e.g.,
Davidson 1994
, Theorem 15.14)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg\{\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\bigg|\sum_{t=1}^{i}\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}R_{n}^{t-1}\bigg|>Kn^{-\kappa/2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq K^{-2}n^{\kappa}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big\{\sum_{t=1}^{n}\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}R_{n}^{t-1}\Big\}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=K^{-2}n^{\kappa}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\Big[\psi_{\alpha}^{2}(\varepsilon_{t})n^{-(1+\kappa)}\big(R_{n}^{t-1}\big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq K^{-2}n^{\kappa}n^{-\kappa}\Big\{\max_{t=1,\ldots,n}\big|R_{n}^{t-1}\big|\Big\}^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\psi_{\alpha}^{2}(\varepsilon_{t})\big]

\displaystyle=K^{-2}O(1)\alpha(1-\alpha)

\displaystyle=O(K^{-2}).

(B.4)

Here, we have used in the penultimate step that
\|\bm{R}_{n}^{t-1}\|\leq\max_{j=1,\ldots,n}\|\bm{R}_{n}^{j}\|=O(1)

(
Magdalinos and Phillips 2020
, Lemma 2.1 (ii))
, and, from
Q_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\big[\psi_{\alpha}^{2}(\varepsilon_{t})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\big[(\alpha-\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon_{t}\leq 0\}})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\big[\alpha^{2}-2\alpha\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon_{t}\leq 0\}}+\mathds{1}_{\{\varepsilon_{t}\leq 0\}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}-2\alpha\mathbb{P}_{t-1}\{\varepsilon_{t}\leq 0\}+\mathbb{P}_{t-1}\{\varepsilon_{t}\leq 0\}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}-2\alpha^{2}+\alpha

\displaystyle=\alpha(1-\alpha).

(B.5)

We conclude from (

) that
\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\big\|\sum_{t=1}^{i}\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}\bm{R}_{n}^{t-1}\big\|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-\kappa/2})
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\bigg\|\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}\bm{R}_{n}^{t-1}\bm{\xi}_{0}\bigg\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\xi}_{0}\|\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\bigg\|\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}\bm{R}_{n}^{t-1}\bigg\|

\displaystyle=o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{\kappa/2})O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-\kappa/2})

\displaystyle=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Similar, but simpler, arguments also show that

\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\bigg\|\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\psi_{\alpha}(\varepsilon_{t})n^{-(1+\kappa)/2}\bm{\mu}_{x}\bigg\|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

In light of (
B.3
) and the previous two displays, it remains to show that, as

Therefore, we only have to verify the conditions of the functional central limit theorem (FCLT) for vector MDAs in
Jacod and Shiryaev 1987
.
First, the conditional variances converge as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\big[\bm{\zeta}_{nt}\bm{\zeta}_{nt}^{\prime}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\big[\psi_{\alpha}^{2}(\varepsilon_{t})\big]\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}\bm{X}_{t-1}(\bm{0},\bm{0})\bm{X}_{t-1}^{\prime}(\bm{0},\bm{0})\bm{D}_{n}^{-1}

(B.7)

where we used (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg\{\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\Big[\|\bm{\zeta}_{nt}\|^{2}\mathds{1}_{\{\|\bm{\zeta}_{nt}\|^{2}>\delta^{2}\}}\Big]>\varepsilon\bigg\}

Since

\displaystyle\|\bm{\zeta}_{nt}\|^{2}

\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathbb{Z}\\
j\rho\in[-K,K]\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\bigg\{\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\bigg|\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\big[\nu_{2t}(j\rho)-\overline{\nu}_{2t}(j\rho)\big]\bigg|>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigg\}=o(1).

Therefore, we only have to show that the final right-hand side term in (
D.11
) converges to zero.
Note that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}-K\leq v_{1},v_{2}\leq K\\
|v_{1}-v_{2}|<\rho\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\bigg|\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\big[\nu_{2t}(v_{1})-\overline{\nu}_{2t}(v_{1})\big]-\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\big[\nu_{2t}(v_{2})-\overline{\nu}_{2t}(v_{2})\big]\bigg|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}-K\leq v_{1},v_{2}\leq K\\
|v_{1}-v_{2}|<\rho\end{subarray}}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\bigg|\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\big[\nu_{2t}(v_{1})-\nu_{2t}(v_{2})\big]-\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\big[\overline{\nu}_{2t}(v_{1})-\overline{\nu}_{2t}(v_{2})\big]\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}-K\leq v_{1},v_{2}\leq K\\
|v_{1}-v_{2}|<\rho\end{subarray}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|\nu_{2t}(v_{1})-\nu_{2t}(v_{2})\big|+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}-K\leq v_{1},v_{2}\leq K\\
|v_{1}-v_{2}|<\rho\end{subarray}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|\overline{\nu}_{2t}(v_{1})-\overline{\nu}_{2t}(v_{2})\big|

\displaystyle=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V_{1n}+V_{2n}.

For
V_{2n}
, we get by monotonicity of the indicator function in
\overline{\nu}_{2t}(\cdot)
that

\displaystyle V_{2n}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\ell}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|\overline{\nu}_{2t}((\ell+2)\rho)-\overline{\nu}_{2t}(\ell\rho)\big|

\displaystyle=\max_{\ell}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\Big[\big(\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq(\ell+2)\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq\ell\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}\big)\big|\psi_{\beta}(\delta_{t})\big|\Big]|D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}|,

(D.12)

where the maximum is taken over the integers
\ell
satisfying
[\ell\rho,(\ell+2)\rho]\subset[-K,K]
.
The conditional expectation in the above expression may be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\Big[\big(\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq(\ell+2)\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq\ell\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}\big)\big|\psi_{\beta}(\delta_{t})\big|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|\ell\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}|}^{|(\ell+2)\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}|}f_{\varepsilon_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\overline{f}2\rho|D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}|.

Insert this into (
D.12
) to get that

V_{2n}\leq K\rho\sum_{t=1}^{n}|D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}X_{t-1}^{\prime}D_{n}^{-1}|=\rho O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

(D.13)

by Lemma
2
.
This implies that

\lim_{\rho\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}-K\leq v_{1},v_{2}\leq K\\
|v_{1}-v_{2}|<\rho\end{subarray}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|\overline{\nu}_{2t}(v_{1})-\overline{\nu}_{2t}(v_{2})\big|>\varepsilon\Bigg\}=0.

(D.14)

For
V_{1n}
we obtain a similar bound as in (
D.12
):

V_{1n}\leq\max_{\ell}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big[\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq(\ell+2)\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq\ell\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}\big]\big|\psi_{\beta}(\delta_{t})D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\big|.

By arguments similar to those in the proof of Lemma
4
, we obtain for each fixed
\ell
that

\sum_{t=1}^{n}\bigg\{\Big[\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq(\ell+2)\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}\big|\psi_{\beta}(\delta_{t})D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\big|-\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq\ell\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}\big|\psi_{\beta}(\delta_{t})D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\big|\Big]\\
-\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\Big[\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq(\ell+2)\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}\big|\psi_{\beta}(\delta_{t})D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\big|-\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq\ell\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}\big|\psi_{\beta}(\delta_{t})D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\big|\Big]\bigg\}\\
=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Using this and the fact that

\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{t-1}\Big[\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq(\ell+2)\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}\big|\psi_{\beta}(\delta_{t})D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\big|-\mathds{1}_{\{0<\varepsilon_{t}\leq\ell\rho D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\}}\big|\psi_{\beta}(\delta_{t})D_{n}^{-1}X_{t-1}\big|\Big]\\
\leq\rho O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

from the arguments leading up to (
D.13
), we deduce that

\lim_{\rho\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Bigg\{\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}-K\leq v_{1},v_{2}\leq K\\
|v_{1}-v_{2}|<\rho\end{subarray}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|\nu_{2t}(v_{1})-\nu_{2t}(v_{2})\big|>\varepsilon\Bigg\}=0.

From this and (
D.14
) it then follows that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}-K\leq v_{1},v_{2}\leq K\\
|v_{1}-v_{2}|<\rho\end{subarray}}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\bigg|\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\big[\nu_{2t}(v_{1})-\overline{\nu}_{2t}(v_{1})\big]-\sum_{t=1}^{\lfloor ns\rfloor}\big[\nu_{2t}(v_{2})-\overline{\nu}_{2t}(v_{2})\big]\bigg|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

as
n\to\infty
, followed by
\rho\downarrow 0
.
Therefore, the final right-hand side term in (
D.11
) can be made arbitrarily small, concluding the proof.
∎
