Stress and resilience among first-year medical students: a cross-sectional application of the medical student stress scale
Authors: Shea E Randall, Hana Seastedt, Shayda M Abazari, Luis Aguilar, Marina Feffer, Sarah Thilges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurnout and clinical depression are often experienced by medical students in the United States, which impacts individual wellbeing as well as professionalism, empathy, and patient care. This study aimed to evaluate stress and wellbeing among first-year medical students at one accredited M.D. institution by administering the Medical Student Stress Scale (MSSS), a context-specific measure designed to capture multidimensional sources of medical student stress.MethodsThe MSSS, a 22-item questionnaire, was administered to first-year medical students during the 2024–2025 academic calendar, in the fall and spring, alongside a Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) and demographic questionnaire. According to the MSSS, stress was measured by calculating a summative score, ranging from 0 to 88, with higher scores indicative of greater levels of stress. The BRS measures resilience with a total score determined as a summation of the six item responses, categorized as low (1-2.99), normal (3-4.3), or high (4.31-5).ResultsThe overall response rate was 61% (107/175) in the fall and 47% (87/175) in the spring. Average student stress scores in the fall and spring were 34.3 and 38.8, respectively. The resilience score in the fall and spring was 3.6 and 3.5, respectively. Multivariable linear regression showed that student stress decreased by 10 and 13 points with every 1-point increase on the BRS in the fall and spring, respectively (p < 0.001). This correlates with a 11–15% reduction in stress. Additionally, at both time points, males displayed a significantly lower estimated stress score than females (p = 0.044 and p = 0.016). In the spring, compared to students of Christian faith, Jewish students displayed an estimated 10-point increase in stress (p = 0.024), and Muslim students displayed an estimated 17-point increase in stress (p = 0.005). Additionally, students that reported they were low-income displayed an estimated 8-point increase in stress compared to non-low-income students (p = 0.009).ConclusionTo identify trends in both stress and resilience, the MSSS and BRS are feasible surveys to implement in medical schools. Understanding how stress and resilience are affecting medical students provides an opportunity to create tangible interventions to better support student wellness and create resilient physicians.Keywords: Medical student stress, Resilience, Burnout, Mental health, Wellbeing, Conceptual framework, Measurement validity

Background

Medical training for M.D. and D.O. candidates in the United States is a rigorous four-year process combining didactic and clinical learning. Burnout and stress, common in both medical school and clinical practice, negatively impact attention, academic honesty, sleep, and increase risk for substance use. Burnout, a psychological syndrome characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment, is pervasive in healthcare [1]. In 2016, 27.2% of medical students reported depression or depressive symptoms, and 11.1% reported suicidal ideation [2]. Among medical students screening positive for depression, only 15.7% sought psychiatric treatment [2]. Despite growing attention to student wellness, teaching critical skills such as resilience and stress management is often overshadowed by academic and clinical demands. The field still lacks consensus on how to conceptualize and measure stress and resilience in ways that inform meaningful institutional change. While many studies report the prevalence and correlates of burnout, few clarify how and why stress evolves during training or which student characteristics predict vulnerability or adaptability.

Stress is a state of mental or emotional strain arising from challenging or adverse circumstances. Unmanaged, it can present with symptoms such as anxiety, sleep disturbance, difficulty concentrating, or emotional exhaustion. One in three medical students experience significant psychological or behavioral concerns including sleep problems, stress, depression, anxiety, burnout, substance use, eating disorders, internet addiction, and suicidal ideation [3]. These symptoms and experiences can be shaped by the demanding nature of medical training, the hierarchical structure of medical education, and the developmental vulnerabilities of young adulthood [3]. Traditional conceptualizations often treat stress as a unidimensional construct. However, newer frameworks in health professions education (HPE) emphasize that stress reflects the interaction between individual coping capacity, institutional culture, and identity-based experiences. Understanding these dynamics requires tools that capture not only the amount and cause of stress but also its type and the populations most affected.

Resilience among medical students is the capacity to adapt positively and recover from stressors or challenging circumstances encountered during medical training. It includes individual attributes such as coping strategies and self-control, as well as institutional factors like faculty support, the learning environment, and access to resources [4]. Resilient students can maintain or regain psychological well-being despite academic and clinical pressures [4]. Studying resilience alongside stress may clarify whether it operates as the inverse of distress or as a protective factor with distinct determinants that can be targeted through interventions.

Accurately assessing stress and resilience requires validated tools. Prior studies often rely on instruments originally developed for other populations including the Maslach, Copenhagen, and Oldenburg burnout inventories, or the DASS-21, GHQ-12, and PH1-9 [5–8]. The Medical Student Stress Scale (MSSS), however, was specifically designed and validated for medical students [9]. This exploratory pilot study aimed to assess perceived stress and resilience among first-year medical students using the MSSS and Brief Resilience Scale (BRS), and to examine associations between resilience, sociodemographic factors, and stress at two time points during the academic year.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted among actively enrolled first-year medical students at Loyola University Chicago Stritch School of Medicine enrolled in the MD, MD/MPH, or MD/PhD programs during the 2024–2025 academic year. Students were surveyed with identical surveys at two time points, once near the beginning of the fall semester and once near the beginning of the spring semester of the M1 year. The surveys were anonymous, and individual responses could not be linked across time points.

Data collection procedures

Following informed consent and acknowledgement that participation was voluntary, anonymous, and had no bearing on academic standing, students were administered a 10-minute electronic survey via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure, web-based data collection platform. Students were provided 10 min of protected lecture time to complete the survey upon its immediate release and were additionally given a window of up to one week to complete the survey at their convenience.

Outcome variables

Perceived stress was assessed using the Medical Student Stress Scale (MSSS). The MSSS consists of 22 items rated on a five-point Likert scale (0 = Never, 1 = Rarely, 2 = Sometimes, 3 = Often, 4 = Always). In the MSSS, total scores are calculated as the sum of all item responses, resulting in a possible range of 0–88, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress.9 Scores were treated as continuous summed scores. The MSSS has been previously validated in medical student populations and demonstrates high internal consistency and validity. Stress, rather than burnout, was selected as the primary outcome given the early timing of assessment and the study’s focus on acute stress experiences and the potential protective nature of resilience. The MSSS was chosen because it is brief, relatively newly developed, tailored to medical students, and designed to capture relevant stressors while minimizing response burden.

Independent variables

Resilience was measured using the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS), which consists of six items rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree). Total resilience scores were calculated as the mean of item responses and categorized as low (1.00–2.99.00.99), normal (3.00–4.30.00.30), or high (4.31–5.00.31.00) [10].

Sociodemographic variables collected included age, gender, race, religious beliefs, prior degree history, and first-generation and low-income status.

Statistical analysis

Student demographics were summarized using frequencies and percentages, stratified by survey time point. Differences in summary scores across time points were described but not interpreted as within-individual change given the anonymous nature of survey responses. Stress scores were summarized as means and standard deviations. Resilience was summarized in both continuous and categorical forms and presented as means with standard deviations or frequencies and percentages, respectively.

Multivariable generalized linear regression models were developed to examine the association between resilience and student demographics with stress scores. These models capture associations within each sample at a single time point but should not be interpreted as representing developmental trajectories. Model assumptions were verified before determining appropriate model fit. Results are displayed as regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical tests were two-sided, with a threshold for statistical significance defined as α < 0.05. All analyses were conducted via Stata version 18 [11].

Ethical considerations

Approval for this study was granted by the Loyola University Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB #218574). Informed consent was obtained electronically prior to study participation. All responses were anonymous, and no identifying information was collected.

Results

Among the 175 students surveyed, 107 completed surveys in the fall and 82 completed surveys in the spring were collected. This yielded response rates of 61% and 47%, respectively. Student demographics are displayed by time point and did not vary between sampling; results are presented in Table 1. In the fall, students’ average stress score was 34.3 (sd: 12.5), while in the spring, students’ average stress score was 38.8 (sd:14.0). In the fall, the average resilience score was 3.6 (0.6), while in the spring, resilience averaged a score of 3.5 (sd:0.6). In spring, resilience, gender, religion, and low-income status were found to have a significant effect on stress. Stress is estimated to decrease by approximately 13 points with every 1-point increase on the BRS (β: −12.8, 95% CI (−16.7, −8.9), p < 0.001). Additionally, males display an approximately 6-point lower estimated stress score than females (β: −6.4, 95% CI (−11.6, −1.2), p: 0.016). Compared to students of Christian faith, Jewish students display an estimated 10-point increase in stress, and Muslim students display an estimated 17-point increase in stress (β: 9.6, 95% CI (1.3, 17.9), p: 0.024; β: 17.2, 95% CI (5.3, 29.2), p: 0.005; respectively). Additionally, students that reported they were low-income displayed an estimated 8-point increase in stress compared to non-low-income students (β: 8.4, 95% CI (2.1, 14.7), p: 0.009). The observed 10–13-point reductions correspond to approximately 11–15% decreases in stress. These changes also represent large shifts relative to sample variability: in the fall sample (mean: 34.3, sd: 12.5), a 10-point reduction is roughly 0.8 sd, and in the spring sample (mean = 38.8, sd = 14.0), a 13-point reduction is roughly 0.9 sd.

Average item-level MSSS and BRS student responses in the fall and spring are shown in supplementary Tables 1 and 2, respectively. Students at both time points self-reported higher stress scores for items reflecting feeling “anxious,” “overly self-critical,” and “overwhelmed by everything there is to do” as well as “hardly hav[ing] enough time to get things done.” Likewise, resilience scores were highest in students who reported that they “tend to bounce back quickly after hard times.”

Discussion

This study assessed stress and resilience among first-year medical students using the MSSS and the BRS. In this cohort, higher resilience scores were associated with lower stress levels at both time points. The findings from this study provide insight into how stress and coping patterns evolve across early medical training and how demographic factors and resilience may shape that trajectory. This is the first study to assess the feasibility of evaluating stress and resilience among first-year medical students at multiple time points using the MSSS and BRS. This data provides a baseline which can inform wellness initiatives and interventions.

The strong inverse association between stress and resilience at both time points is consistent with existing literature that resilience is a protective factor against psychological distress among students and in general [10, 12]. Beyond confirming known relationships, this study quantifies how each incremental increase in resilience corresponds to a substantial (10–13 point or 11–15%) reduction in stress scores which offers educators a tangible metric when seeking to evaluate the impact of resilience interventions. Given the observational and cross-sectional nature of the analysis, the directionality of this relationship unfortunately cannot be established. For example, higher stress may influence perceived resilience or both may be influenced by unmeasured contextual or environmental factors. The association between stress and resilience was larger in the spring semester which highlights the importance of considering the relationship between stress and resilience, particularly during periods of higher stress.

The observed increase in stress from fall to spring aligns with previous research that examines psychosocial changes during the first year of medical school [13, 14]. Some of this increase in stress may be due to mounting academic demands over time, closer proximity to board examinations, and a decline in the novelty of medical training. Other studies have suggested that more systemic concerns such as lack of support for career planning, assessment pressures, and administrative failures contribute to the observed shift in stress over time [15, 16]. The persistence of moderate resilience despite higher stress may suggest that while students may have baseline coping abilities, institutional factors such as workload or structured support systems may moderate how effectively existing resilience can be applied.

Between genders, there was a consistent difference observed. Male students reported significantly lower stress scores than female students, a finding consistent with prior research in medical education. A longitudinal survey study which examined stress and behavior patterns among medical students across all four years of training had a similar finding. Female trainees more frequently reported burnout risk symptoms, psychosomatic distress, high-exam related stress, and patient-contact anxiety compared to their male counterparts [12]. Furthermore, a lower proportion of females demonstrated healthy patterns defined as a good balance between study-related ambition, resistance toward stress, and emotional wellbeing. Additionally, females showed relatively higher rates of risk patterns for eventual overexertion [12] Such risk patterns include those who scored high in the dimensions of study-related ambition but also demonstrated poor ability to distance oneself from work/study and impaired emotional wellbeing. Prior studies have attributed gender differences in stress to a difference in coping strategies with women typically choosing emotion-focused or support-seeking strategies while men may use more avoidant tactics [17]. Importantly, some studies have found the association between poor mental health and academic performance significant among female students but absent in males, even when adjusting for academic workload and grades [18]. Qualitative evidence also suggests that gender-specific experiences such as receiving different expectations or facing implicit bias during clinical evaluation may contribute to differential stress burdens [19].

Further subgroup analysis suggests stress levels vary among different social demographics groups. However, definitive relationships cannot be classified due to small sample sizes. This exploratory data suggests varied stress by religious affiliation, with Jewish and Muslim students reporting higher MSSS scores compared to their Christian peers in the spring semester and low-income students reporting higher MSSS scores compared to high income students. While these findings must be interpreted cautiously due to sample size and possible contextual factors, they align with previous literature that reports that students from minority identity groups often face unique pressures related to representation, implicit bias, and accommodation of cultural practices [20]. Similarly, the higher stress scores observed among low-income students may reflect the added burden of financial strain, barriers to resource access, and potential overlap with other underrepresented or disadvantaged identities [21]. Unfortunately, low-income status was not explicitly defined in the study questionnaire and all responses were based on self-identification alone. Because this suggests a potential bias in self-perception of relative financial status, a limitation of our study design is the inability to draw conclusions regarding our low-income students beyond reporting the relationship between stress and self-perception of potential financial strain. This relationship is hypothesis-generating, but warrants further research.

It is important to recognize that student stress may be influenced not only by academic pressures but also by political, cultural and social implications. Of note, this analysis was exploratory and not designed to infer causality. Therefore, while these conclusions are speculatory and require further investigation, they highlight the need for more nuanced approaches to understanding how intersecting identities influence wellness. Future work could examine how institutional inclusivity efforts and the social climate moderate the relationship between identity and stress. In addition, it is important to consider how political and cultural contexts may also influence the reported levels of stress among minority and low-income students. For example, the second survey was administered shortly after the United States Inauguration in January of 2025. Although speculative, we cannot definitely rule out that this shift in political climate was not a confounder that did not influence stress among certain minority groups. Overall, these findings suggest that both religious minority status and socioeconomic disadvantage represent important but underexplored risk factors for elevated stress in medical training. Our current systems may not be adequately supporting these groups and reducing burdens is critical for promoting diversity in medicine.

This study is the first at our institution to evaluate stress and resilience among first-year medical students. For this analysis, the decision to use the MSSS and BRS was guided by their conceptual and psychometric strengths in capturing the unique stress landscape of medical students. The MSSS was specifically developed to assess stressors inherent to medical education, including factors such as academic pressure, workload intensity, and institutional culture. Older or broader stress measures often overlook these dimensions. Its calibration and validation in a prior study at a different institution established its internal consistency and content validity, and it offers other institutions an opportunity to adapt it for their own needs related to assessing stress. The BRS complements the MSSS by providing a brief, validated measure of resilience that quantifies students’ ability to recover from stress rather than their static traits. Together, these instruments characterize wellness not simply as the absence of stress but as the capacity to sustain equilibrium in the face of it.

Strengths of this study include the feasibility of implementation of the MSSS and BRS. Students were able to complete the study in less than 10 min during a dedicated time on campus. The questions are specific to challenges faced by medical students. The BRS and demographic questionnaire also allowed for additional analysis to better understand how stress is influenced by various factors. Additionally, this survey can be easily distributed multiple times per year to students allowing for data to be collected at multiple time points. Weaknesses of this study include limited sample size; response rates were 61% and 47% in the fall and spring, respectively. Due to reduced response rates, our data may be skewed to represent increased or decreased stress levels depending on potential response bias. Additionally, certain demographic groups may not be well represented because of the reduced sample size limiting our ability to draw definitive conclusions, and require further investigation. Moreover, this study was implemented at only one medical school. Although the MSSS was created and validated at a different medical school, findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of the medical school’s curriculum and institutional environment, which may differ from other institutions. To improve the generalizability of the data, this study should be repeated to include students across all four years of medical school at a number of different institutions. Additionally, since data was collected anonymously, it was not possible to determine which students did and did not respond at the second time point. Therefore, differences in scores across time points were described but not interpreted as within individual change, limiting our ability to draw longitudinal conclusions. This also might have created a response bias, however it is important to note that baseline characteristics at each time point did not vary much between each survey.

While a strength of our study is that it was administered at two time points, the particular political and societal context during the study period may have influenced reports of student stress, highlighting the need for repeated testing. Additionally, other common medical student stressors such as perceived competitiveness of specialties of interest, in-state/out-of-state status, relationship status, family status and academic pressures were not collected and were therefore not controlled for in the study design. These factors can also affect perceived levels of stress and thus reflect a limitation in the design of the study. Further research to better understand the multifactorial nature of stress among medical students is needed to implement long term interventions and change.

This work illustrates how small-scale studies at a single institution can serve as the foundation for a broader understanding of student wellness. By operationalizing stress and resilience through validated tools and analyzing them with demographic factors, this study contributes to a growing conceptual framework that integrates psychosocial and contextual determinants of wellbeing in medical education. Future research should explore how institutional interventions such as curricular design, mentorship programs, or faith-inclusive wellness initiatives affect stress longitudinally and/or resilience development. Resilience is not a fixed trait but a dynamic process that can be developed and strengthened through focused interventions, such as psychological skills training, mentorship, and supportive curricular changes. Academic institutions may therefore play a critical role in both reducing stressors and fostering resilience by providing a supportive environment, promoting positive faculty-student relationships, and integrating feedback into its curriculum [4]. This exploratory study can be used as a guide to further explore how resilience and stress are associated among medical students.

Conclusion

Overall, the MSSS and BRS were feasible to implement and identified clear trends in stress based on demographic factors. Understanding how stress and resilience are affecting medical students provides an opportunity to create tangible interventions to better support student wellness and create resilient physicians. Specifically, exploring how demographic and resilience data relates to stress levels helps identify specific groups of students who might face additional unique challenges and benefit from additional interventions. This can help inform institutional support and curriculum development to ensure ample opportunity to teach and develop resilience throughout medical school. Not only will this serve to offset student stress levels in medical school but help build skills that physicians can continue to use throughout their career to reduce stress, burnout, and other mental health challenges.
