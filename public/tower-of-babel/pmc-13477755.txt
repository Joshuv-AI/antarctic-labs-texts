A Case of Fibrous Dysplasia Arising in the Lumbar Pedicle Successfully Diagnosed and Treated by Safe Lesion Biopsy Using O-Arm Navigation and Endoscopic Techniques
Authors: Takashi Dezawa, Katsunori Fukutake, Keiji Hasegawa, Akihito Wada, Daisuke Kamakura, Akinori Tani, Aki Mitsuda, Naobumi Tochigi, Hiroshi Takahashi
Published: 2026

This report demonstrates that a combined navigation-assisted and endoscopic approach allows safe biopsy and curettage of a pedicle lesion.

The patient was a 17-year-old female. She had experienced lower back pain for 2 years. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a lesion extending from the posterior portion of the right L4 vertebral body to the lamina, showing low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and mixed low and high signal intensity on T2-weighted images. (Figure 1A-C). Computed tomography (CT) revealed an osteolytic lesion extending from the right L4 pedicle to the vertebral body (Figure 1D and E). The differential diagnosis includes metastatic tumors, osteoblastoma, giant cell tumor, aneurysmal bone cyst, and infection. In the present case, imaging showed a solitary osteolytic lesion with a relatively well-defined margin, without obvious cortical destruction or a large soft tissue mass. In addition, whole-body CT revealed no evidence of a primary malignancy or other metastatic lesion. These findings suggested a low likelihood of malignancy. However, histological confirmation was required. Therefore, biopsy and curettage of the lesion were planned. As the lesion was localized in the pedicle, O-arm navigation (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and endoscopy (METRx system, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) were used in combination to perform the biopsy and curettage safely and accurately. Neuromonitoring was not used because no neural decompression was required. O-arm navigation was used with automatic registration, and the pedicle trajectory was confirmed using a probe.

An 18-mm tubular retractor was placed directly over the pedicle, and the subsequent procedure was performed under endoscopic visualization (Figure 2A). A bone window approximately 10 mm in width was created directly above the lesion. The lesion cavity was empty, and the internal septa were resected using a curette and submitted for pathological examination (Figure 2B). After curettage, the defect was filled with bone graft substitute (Figure 2C). Histopathological examination revealed irregularly shaped woven bone trabeculae within cellular fibrous connective tissue, leading to a definitive diagnosis of fibrous dysplasia (FD) (Figure 3). Postoperatively, no corset was used, and no activity restrictions were imposed. Postoperatively, the patient required no analgesics, and pain improved, with the Visual Analog Scale score decreasing from 8 to 1. CT imaging at 6 months postoperatively demonstrated bone union at the site of bone grafting with no evidence of recurrence (Figure 4).

FD is a developmental bone disorder characterized by the replacement of normal bone with fibrous tissue1,2). FD accounts for approximately 7% of primary bone tumors and most commonly affects the skull, femur, humerus, tibia, and ribs3,4). Spinal FD accounts for only 2.5% of cases and is divided into two subtypes: the monostotic and the polyostotic forms5). While the polyostotic form is the most common presentation in the spine, the monostotic form is unusual6). In spinal FD, the vertebral body is most frequently involved. Given the close anatomical relationship, pedicle involvement is commonly observed in cases in which the vertebral body is affected7).

Surgical treatment should be considered in cases with severe pain, neurological symptoms, or the development of scoliosis or instability associated with vertebral collapse8). Pedicle-localized lesions are rare, with few reported surgical cases, including osteoma and bone cysts, and are technically challenging because of their proximity to neural and vascular structures9,10). However, in this case, the lesion was successfully treated using a combined endoscopic and navigation-assisted approach. This technique may also be applicable to vertebral body lesions, although deep lesions may be technically demanding. It may also be useful for other benign pedicle tumors, such as osteoblastoma or giant cell tumor, when safe biopsy and limited curettage are required. The follow-up period in this case was relatively short, and long-term observation is necessary to monitor potential recurrence or structural weakening of the pedicle.
