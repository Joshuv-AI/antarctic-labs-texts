MicroRNA and target gene dynamics in potato under nitrogen deficiency
Authors: Zahra Hajibarat, Abbas Saidi, Zohreh Hajibarat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant growth and stress tolerance depend critically on nitrogen (N), an essential macronutrient that regulates key molecular and physiological processes. High potato yields and quality require optimized N fertilization, while improving nitrogen use efficiency minimizes fertilizer use. Developing N-use efficient potato varieties necessitates advanced breeding programs focused on identifying genes that enhance NUE. The regulatory roles of potato microRNAs (miRNAs) and their target genes in N metabolism under nitrogen deficiency (ND) remain poorly understood, despite research on their responses to various stresses. This study will investigate the functions of miRNAs in enabling plants to adapt to N). A total of 303 conserved miRNAs were identified, including 91 novel and 212 known miRNAs. Gene ontology classification yielded 53 functional annotations across 20 molecular functions, 13 cellular components, and 20 biological processes. Anticipated target genes are linked with transcription factors including NAC, ARFs, NF-YA, AP2, antioxidant enzymes, nitrate transporters, and root development processes. Our research demonstrated the crucial roles of miR169, miR172, miR399, miR408, and miR167 as key regulators of ND response in potato. Expression analysis revealed up-regulation of miR408, miR399, and miR172 in the tolerant genotypes Avin and D394, while miR169 and miR167 were down-regulated. These differentially expressed miRNAs, exhibiting high or low levels in the roots of Avin and D394, contribute to enhanced root growth, increased antioxidant enzyme activity, and improved nitrate transport. Furthermore, this study suggests strategies for manipulating these miRNAs and their target genes to develop N-use efficient potato varieties through transgenic or breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32874-2.Keywords: Antioxidant enzymes, Molecular function, Nitrogen use efficiency, Root development, Transcription factorsSubject terms: Biotechnology, Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Nitrogen deficiency (ND) negatively impacts numerous plant growth parameters1. Studies have shown that potato plants exhibit significantly lower biomass accumulation under N-deficient conditions relative to optimal nitrogen supply2,3. ND also leads to decreased leaf area, chlorophyll content, and photosynthetic rates in sorghum, and is a well-known cause of leaf chlorosis4. Low nitrogen levels are associated with altered root growth, producing longer, thinner roots5, and influence lateral root formation6. Under ND conditions, tea plants exhibit elevated expression of nitrate transporter genes, increased auxin accumulation, and enhanced ethylene biosynthesis—collectively promoting lateral root development7. This demonstrates a complex interplay between nitrogen supply, lateral root development, and auxin signaling. Reactive oxygen species and oxidative stress in plants are induced by both insufficient and excessive N. Plants combat this by increasing production of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants, including phenolics8. ND promotes increased biosynthesis of phenolic compounds in plants, suggesting that phenolic content may serve as a robust biochemical marker for assessing N stress responses9.

Acting as key controllers within biological systems, microRNAs (miRNAs) play a vital role in regulating metabolic pathways, with a significant impact on how organisms cope with stress. They achieve this by either boosting or suppressing the activity of specific genes10–12. Their importance in regulating plant nutrition is well-established13–15. Plants detect ND under low N stress, amplifying and relaying this signal to downstream molecules. Specific miRNAs actively control the expression of genes involved in low-nitrogen stress by degrading or inhibiting the translation of their target transcripts, which consequently improves the plant’s tolerance to low-N conditions16. Elucidating the molecular responses to ND and characterizing miRNA function are essential for understanding how plants adapt to such conditions14,17. Studies have profiled miRNAs and examined their regulatory mechanisms under ND conditions in various plants, including Arabidopsis thaliana18, Oryza sativa19, Zea mays15, Coffea arabica20, and Dendranthema morifolium17.

miRNAs are a category of diminutive, intrinsically produced RNA molecules that do not code for proteins. These molecules originate from precursor RNA strands that fold into a hairpin configuration, subsequently undergoing processing by the RNase III enzyme named Dicer21. Upon maturation, miRNAs direct RNA-induced silencing complexes (RISC) toward specific messenger RNAs. This interaction results in either the breakdown of the mRNA or the prevention of its translation, with the specific consequence dictated by the level of sequence matching. Although initially documented in animal systems, the creation and operational mechanisms of miRNAs are extensively preserved in plant life. These crucial molecules are vital for coordinating diverse metabolic functions, developmental progression, and reactions to environmental stressors22. Research shows miRNAs control how plants adapt to nutrient deficiencies (NUD). The discovery of small regulatory RNAs, ranging from 22 to 25 nucleotides in length, led researchers to recognize their significant role in posttranscriptional gene regulation23. These small RNAs primarily include miRNAs and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs). The creation of miRNAs begins with single-stranded RNA molecules that adopt hairpin configurations. This folding event is facilitated by enzymes akin to ribonuclease III, specifically Dicer-like 1 (DCL1) or DCL4. In contrast, siRNAs are produced from long double-stranded RNAs. These can either be synthesized by RNA polymerase 6 (RDR6)/RDR2 or arise from overlapping antisense mRNAs. Plant miRNAs play diverse roles in developmental processes such as flowering, leaf and root formation, embryogenesis, and auxin signaling24. Moreover, recent research has demonstrated that miRNAs also modulate plant responses to NUD, thereby contributing to adaptive mechanisms25. As key instigators of communication pathways, microRNAs exhibit a pronounced capacity for movement. They are able to either pass directly from one cell to another or journey across considerable ranges, ultimately ensuring the exact calibration of N signaling transduction26. The majority of miRNAs, including miR167, miR169, and miR399, control the expression of N transporters by targeting transcription factors (TFs). To illustrate, in Arabidopsis, the promoter regions of AtNRT1.1 and AtNRT2.1 contain four and one “CCAAT” elements, respectively, which serve as binding sites for Nuclear Factor-Y Subunit A (NFYA)27. This suggests that miR169 regulates nitrate uptake and remobilization. In transgenic Arabidopsis overexpressing miR169a, the expression of AtNRT1.1 and AtNRT2.1 is significantly reduced, leading to a decrease in N uptake capacity28. Overexpression of TaNFYA-B1 significantly increases nitrate uptake at various nutrient supply levels by up-regulating the expression of nitrate transporters. Soybean root growth’s nitrate signaling is known to be regulated, in part, by the well-characterized miR16729. Under ND conditions, the expression of miR167a is significantly upregulated, resulting in the repression of its target gene ARF8, a crucial regulator of lateral root initiation30. Conversely, nitrogen starvation significantly down-regulates miR169, while its target gene, NFYA, is up-regulated28. Numerous miRNAs that play a key role in maintaining nitrogen and phosphorus homeostasis have been extensively studied and represent promising candidates for application in plant breeding. The molecular and physiological functions of the auxin-regulated miR167-ARF/IAR3 module, the miR169-NFYA-ENOD/NRT module, and the miR399-PHO2 module are noteworthy. Initially, MiR167 was investigated in Arabidopsis, revealing its targeting of ARF8. Under nitrogen stress, the expression level of miR167 diminishes in the roots of Arabidopsis, leading to increased levels of ARF8 in the pericycle and lateral root caps. This accumulation enhances auxin signaling, facilitating the formation of lateral roots and promoting nitrogen absorption30.

In soybean, the expression of miR167c increases in the vasculatures, pericytes, and cortex of roots following inoculation with Bradyrhizobium. MiR167c enhances the number of nodules by inhibiting GmARF8a and GmARF8b29. Additionally, auxin promotes the expression of miR167 while simultaneously suppressing its target genes. The influence of miR167 on nodule formation is reliant on the Nod factor receptor, which operates upstream of several nodulation-related genes, including nodule inception, nodulation signaling pathway1, early nodulin40-1, NF-YA1 (formerly known as HAEM activator), and NF-YA229. Therefore, the auxin-mediated miR167-ARF/IAR3 module plays a positive role in regulating nitrogen homeostasis. Transgenic rice that overexpresses miR169o exhibits greater biomass accumulation, along with significantly higher levels of nitrate and total amino acids31. This suggests that members of the miRNA family may have distinct functions. Additionally, in maize, extended periods of nitrate depletion may suppress the transcription of miR169i/j/k and stimulate the post-transcriptional expression of NFYA5, thus affecting root development32. Overall, miR169 serves as one of the key regulators of nitrogen homeostasis. The objective of this research was to identify both conserved and novel miRNAs in potato plants exposed to ND conditions, in comparison to plants grown under optimal N supply, within a controlled environmental framework. Additionally, we conducted expression profiling of chosen miRNAs and their target genes to validate the predicted target genes. Our findings will broaden the understanding of the role of miRNAs in adapting to ND condition in potato.

Material

Plant material and growth conditions

The plant material was officially obtained from the Seed and Plant Improvement Institute (SPII), formally identified by the corresponding author Dr. Abbas Saidi, and confirmed by Dr. Ahmad Mousapour Gorji in the Vegetable Department, at the SPII, which deposits the collected material. Furthermore, the use of plants in the present study complies with all necessary international, national, and institutional guidelines and legislation. All plant material is owned by the authors and no permission is required for their use. Information on the voucher specimen was obtained by Dr. Ahmad Mousapour Gorji. In this research, eight cultivars (Avin, Bahar, KG911-91, D394, Santé, Jelly, Agria, and Javid) were utilized as the research materials. Tuber seeds of these genotypes were sourced from the Seed and Plant Improvement Institute (SPII), which is associated with the Ministry of Jehad Agriculture in Karaj, Iran. The experimental setup included two distinct treatments: control (CK) and ND. The tubers were grown in plastic containers filled with 5 L of nutrient solution, following the method described by Chen et al.33 with several modifications. Twenty-one days post-planting, half of the seedlings were treated with a ND Hoagland solution containing 0.2 mM ammonium nitrate and calcium nitrate 0.15 mM, while the remaining seedlings received a control Hoagland solution with 2 mM ammonium nitrate and calcium nitrate 1.5 mM (The solution formulation was detailed in Table S1). After five (ND-5) and 10 days of treatment (ND-10), samples were collected from all seedlings. The seedlings were maintained in a growth chamber under conditions of 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness, with a temperature of 25 ± 3 °C and approximately 45–55% relative humidity. To facilitate subsequent RNA extraction, root samples were obtained at 5 and 10 days after ND stress onset. Immediately after collection, samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 °C.

Data filtering and statistics of miRNAs

To explore the impact of nitrogen deprivation on potato plant gene expression, we built a miRNA-seq. We leveraged pre-existing RNA sequencing data from potato plants subjected to nitrogen-deficient conditions, sourced from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The original transcriptome data, encompassing both nitrogen-stressed and control samples, can be retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) using the identifier PRJNA4891502. Initial integrity checks on the raw sequencing data were conducted with FastQC v0.11.9. This process involved assessing per-base sequence quality, determining the guanine-cytosine content, and identifying any adapter contamination. Following this, adapter fragments and bases with insufficient quality were precisely removed using Cutadapt v3.534 The trimming parameters were set to ensure a minimum read length of 15 nucleotides, a maximum read length of 34 nucleotides, and a Phred quality score cutoff of 30. Additionally, sequences exhibiting polyA or polyT tails were excluded. To eliminate redundancy, identical reads were collapsed into unique entries, and the resulting count distribution for these processed sequences was then calculated for each sample.

Removal of non-miRNA small RNAs

To isolate pure small RNA species, clean reads underwent alignment against established rRNA, tRNA, snRNA, and snoRNA annotations sourced from the Rfam v14.6 database. This alignment was performed using Bowtie v1.3.1, strictly enforcing a zero-mismatch policy within the seed region. Any reads that successfully mapped to these non-microRNA sequences were then excluded from the dataset.

Known and novel miRNA identification

The unmapped reads were aligned to mature miRNA sequences from miRBase v22 via BLASTN, permitting no more than two mismatches or gaps to identify evolutionarily conserved miRNAs. Sequences that did not align to known miRNAs were subsequently mapped to the Solanum tuberosum reference genome (DM v6.1) using Bowtie2 (version 2.4.5), with the constraint of allowing at most a single mismatch within the seed region. Novel miRNA candidates were then predicted using MiRDeep2 (v2.0.1.2) under default settings, evaluating hairpin secondary structures, minimum free energy thresholds, and characteristic Dicer processing patterns. For every predicted novel miRNA, attributes such as read abundance, sequence length, and positional nucleotide bias were systematically documented.

Profiling differentially expressed miRNAs

The number of reads for each miRNA across all samples was statistically analyzed, and the expression levels were normalized to a consistent magnitude using the reads per million (RPM) value.

The DESeq2 R package was utilized to determine differentially expressed miRNAs between the two samples, relying on the key criteria of an expression | fold change (FC) | greater than 2 and a p-value less than 0.05. The statistically tested values were adjusted for false discovery rate (FDR).

Determination of soluble sugars, antioxidant enzymes, and anthocyanin during both days of post-treatment

Following five and 10 days of exposure to ND treatments, three similarly grown potato plants were selected for further analysis. Fresh root tissues weighing 0.5 g were pulverized into a fine powder using liquid N and then combined with 10 mL of chilled phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8) that included 1.0% (w/v) PVP. Following this step, the resulting mixture was centrifuged at 8000 × g. The supernatant obtained from this process was utilized as the crude enzyme extract for the enzyme assays. The activities of catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and peroxidase (POD) were determined based on the methods outlined by Ekinci et al.35, Nakano and Asada36, and Chance and Maehly37, respectively. Following the addition of H2O2, absorbance changes were recorded over 120 s at wavelengths of 240 nm, 290 nm, and 470 nm to quantify CAT, APX, and POD activities, respectively.

The dry plant material (including leaves and roots) was subjected to sugar extraction by adding 10 ml of 70% ethanol to 0.1 g of the dried material, and the mixture was stored in the refrigerator for a week. After this period, 1 ml of the supernatant was taken from the root samples and diluted with 2 ml of distilled water. Subsequently, 1 ml of 5% phenol was added to the diluted solution, followed by the careful addition of 5 ml of concentrated sulfuric acid under controlled conditions, with thorough mixing in between.

The root samples were homogenized in a 1% methanolic hydrochloric acid solution and stored in the dark for 48 h. The resulting alcoholic extract was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The anthocyanin content was quantified using spectrophotometry by measuring the absorbance of the samples at a wavelength of 550 nm. The amount of anthocyanin was determined using the following equation.

A = εbc,

A: Represents the absorption intensity.

b: Denotes the cuvette width in centimeters (cm).

c: Refers to the anthocyanin concentration, which is 130 mol/L.

ε (extinction coefficient): Is equivalent to mol–1 cm–1, with a value of 0.458 × 105.

Prediction of target genes and analysis using Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG

Using the psRNATarget tool with default parameters, potential target genes of the differentially abundant miRNAs were predicted. The potato genome sequence from the NCBI database was used as the target sequence for alignment. Gene Ontology (GO) is a globally recognized system for classifying gene functions, offering a continuously updated set of standardized vocabulary to thoroughly characterize the features of genes and their products across different organisms. The GO term serves as the unit of analysis, and a hypergeometric test is utilized to identify GO terms with a p-value less than 0.05 in comparison to all expression backgrounds, thereby designating them as significantly enriched in the target gene. The ShinyGO database was employed to conduct GO enrichment and KEGG analyses on the identified target genes. The scope of this analysis was specifically limited to GO terms encompassing biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions38–40.

Verification of miRNA and target genes expression through qRT-PCR

Total RNAs were isolated utilizing a plant Anazol RNA kit. A Nanodrop spectrophotometer was used to assess the quality and concentration of the isolated RNA, and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis was used to confirm RNA integrity. The analysis of the transcriptome via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using the 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Mix Kit. A three-step protocol was employed for fluorescence quantitative PCR detection on the ABI PRISM 7500 Real-Time PCR system. The relative expression levels of the selected differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs) and their target genes were normalized against StEF-1α. The StEF-1α gene was employed as the endogenous control for quantitative real-time PCR analysis. Its suitability was rigorously tested and confirmed by evaluating its expression stability across the entire set of experimental conditions with the RefFinder tool41,42, thereby ensuring dependable normalization for target gene expression. Table S2 provides details on the miRNA primers and their target genes used for gene expression analysis. The 2−ΔΔCt method, as described by Livak and Schmittgen43, was used to analyze gene expression data.

Distribution of novel and known miRNAs

The chromosomal locations of the identified miRNAs (known and novel) were plotted and graphically represented in R with the ggplot2 package, using their genomic positions as annotated in the potato reference genome.

Interaction of miRNAs and their target genes and TFs

To decipher the complex TF-miRNA-mRNA regulatory architecture, our initial step involved computationally predicting potential interactions. We utilized the extensive prediction tools and curated data collections provided by the Solgenomics platform. Specific TF-miRNA links were established by scanning promoter sequences for predicted TF binding motifs, a functionality integrated within Solgenomics’ (https://solgenomics.net/) regulatory modules. Similarly, miRNA-target gene associations were retrieved using the platform’s microRNA target prediction capabilities, adhering to predefined criteria like seed region complementarity and the thermodynamic stability of the resulting duplexes.

The predicted TF-miRNA and miRNA-mRNA interactions were then systematically processed: curated, merged, and formatted into relational edge tables for subsequent analysis. Network assembly, integrating all identified interaction layers, was performed in Python using the NetworkX library’s directed graph (DiGraph) implementation, ensuring the preservation of regulatory flow. We subsequently calculated essential network topological metrics, including node degree and betweenness centrality, via NetworkX’s inherent algorithms to pinpoint key regulatory players within the network. Finally, the complete TF-miRNA-mRNA interaction map was graphically represented using Matplotlib. A physics-based spring layout was applied to optimize node distribution, while distinct shapes and sizes were assigned to TFs, miRNAs, and their target genes to enhance the visual interpretation of the network.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3), with exceptions for the physiological parameters of soluble sugar, anthocyanin, and antioxidant enzymes. Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 6.0 software. The results were presented as the arithmetic mean ± standard error (SE), and statistical differences at a 0.05 probability level were determined using LSD’s post hoc tests. To uncover relationships between variables, we utilized R’s corrplot library for correlation analysis. Dimensionality reduction via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was achieved using the Factoextra package, also executed within the R statistical computing framework.

Results

Root physiological responses to ND in eight genotypes

Physiological traits under ND conditions

Anthocyanin content in root during both days of post-treatment Following ND application, root ANT content significantly varied at 5 and 10 days. Specifically, the Avin and D394 genotypes showed significant increases in ANT content during the two time points (Fig. 1a).

Anthocyanin, sugar content, POD, CAT, and peroxidase content in three conditions (Normal; ND-5 nitrogen deficiency at 5 days; ND-10 nitrogen deficiency at 10 days).

Sugar content in root under ND Significant increases in sugar content were observed in the Avin, D394, Agria, Javid, and Santé genotypes at both 5 and 10 days after the ND application. Relative to normal conditions, the KG911-91 genotype demonstrated a significant increase in sugar content 10 day’s post-ND application. Unlike the plants thriving with consistent nitrogen, both the Bahar and Jelly varieties displayed a notable dip in sugar concentration five days following the nitrogen deprivation (ND) protocol. This difference in sugar levels between the ND-treated and standard-nitrogen groups was statistically significant, as visually represented in Fig. 1b.

POD content in root under ND

The POD enzyme exhibited substantial variations in response to ND application across different genotypes at two time points. The Avin and Bahar genotypes displayed significant increases in POD content 5 and 10 days after ND application compared to the normal treatment. After 10 days of nitrogen limitation, a significant increase in POD content was observed in the Agria genotype relative to the control treatment. In the Santé, KG911-91, and Jelly genotypes, a significant decrease was observed at both 5 and 10 days following ND application. Additionally, Javid and D394 genotypes showed significant increases in POD content in the root 5 days after ND application (Fig. 1c).

CAT content in roots

The examination of the CAT enzyme in the roots of the KG911-91, Javid, and Bahar genotypes revealed a notable increase at both 5 and 10 days following the ND application. Meanwhile, the Avin and D394 genotypes exhibited a significant rise in CAT levels 10 days after the ND treatment compared to the normal condition (Fig. 1d).

APX Content in Roots

The APX content in the roots of the Avin, KG911-91, D394, Agria, and Javid genotypes showed significant increases at both 5 and 10 days post-ND application, compared to the normal treatment. Additionally, the Jelly cultivar demonstrated a notable rise in CAT content at 5 days after the ND application. Conversely, there was a significant decline in APX content observed at 5 days after the ND treatment, although the Bahar cultivar showed a substantial increase in APX levels 10 days post-ND application. The Santé genotype did not show any significant changes in the APX content of the roots (Fig. 1e).

Expression of the NF-YA gene

The Avin and Agria genotypes exhibited up-regulation of the NF-YA gene 10 days after ND application. Expression of the NRT2.1 gene

ND condition at 5 and 10 days post-application resulted in elevated StNRT2.1 expression levels in the Avin, D394, Bahar, and Santé genotypes, indicating a transcriptional activation of high-affinity nitrate uptake mechanisms. In contrast, the Javid, Agria, and KG911-91 genotypes exhibited reduced StNRT2.1 transcript accumulation under identical conditions.

stu-miRNA167a-5p gene expression in response to ND treatment

Expression of the stu-miRNA167a-5p gene.

In the Javid, Jelly, and Bahra genotypes, the expression of the stu-miRNA167a-5p gene increased after 10 days post-ND treatment compared to 5 days. Conversely, in the D394 and Agria genotypes, stu-miRNA167a-5p expression was higher at 5 days than at 10 days post-ND application. Meanwhile, the Avin, Santé, and KG911-91 genotypes exhibited significantly low stu-miRNA167a-5p expression at both 5 and 10 days after ND treatment.

Expression of the StARF8 gene

The StARF8 gene was significantly expressed in the Avin, D394, Jelly, Santé, and Agria genotypes at both 5 and 10 days post-ND application. However, in the KG911-91 genotype, StARF8 expression was markedly higher at 5 days compared to 10 days after ND treatment. In contrast, all other genotypes showed significantly low StARF8 expression levels at both time points following ND application. Through in silico analysis employing the bioinformatics tool psRNATarget, stu-miR167a-5p is predicted to bind to the PGSC0003DMT400041126 transcript. The binding site is localized within the coding sequence (CDS) of this messenger RNA, covering positions 2376 to 2396. This molecular interaction is hypothesized to instigate gene suppression through cleavage, where the miRNA facilitates targeted fragmentation of the mRNA structure, ultimately promoting the breakdown and diminishing the activity of the affected transcript.

Expression of the stu-miRNA399o-3p gene

High levels of stu-miR399o-3p gene were notably expressed in the Avin, D394, Santé, and Javid varieties, signifying a dominant presence of this microRNA across these specific samples. Notably, stu-miRNA399o-3p expression in these genotypes exhibited a further decline at 10 days post-ND treatment relative to expression levels at day 5, indicating sustained downregulation over time under nitrogen-limited conditions. Conversely, in the Agria genotype, stu-miRNA399 expression was significantly higher at 10 days than at 5 days following ND application.

Expression of the StPHO2 gene

In the Avin, Bahar, Santé, Agria, and Javid genotypes, StPHO2 gene expression decreased significantly at 5 and 10 days after ND treatment. In contrast, the KG911-91 cultivar showed a significant increase in StPHO2 expression at both time points (Fig. 5). Additionally, in the Jelly genotype, StPHO2 expression was significantly higher at 5 days compared to 10 days following ND treatment. Using the computational resource psRNATarget, it is predicted that the regulatory microRNA stu-miR399o-3p will bind to the transcript PGSC0003DMT400076435. This binding is expected to occur at three specific locations within the messenger RNA’s coding region, specifically between nucleotides 811–831, 1039–1059, and 1095–1115. The anticipated outcome of these interactions is the silencing of the gene through the cleavage and subsequent breakdown of the mRNA molecule.

Expression of the stu-miRNA172a-3p gene

In the Agria cultivar, stu-miRNA172a-3p transcript levels were significantly elevated at five days post-ND treatment compared to ten days, indicating a temporal decline in miRNA-mediated regulation during prolonged nitrogen limitation. The Avin, Bahar, D394, Jelly, and Javid genotypes exhibited significantly elevated stu-miRNA172a-3p levels at both 5 and 10 days post-ND application.

Expression of the StAP2 gene

The StAP2 gene showed significantly increased expression in KG911-91, Santé, and Agria at 5 days compared to 10 days after ND treatment. In the Jelly genotype, StAP2 expression was significantly upregulated at both five and ten days following nitrogen limitation treatment, indicating sustained transcriptional activation. In contrast, the Javid genotype exhibited a significant increase in StAP2 expression only at day 10. In contrast, Avin, D394, and Bahar displayed reduced StAP2 expression at both time points. A highly complementary binding sequence was uncovered for stu-miR172a-3p within the coding region (nucleotides 1818–1838) of the AP2-like TF PGSC0003DMT400010229 in potato. This discovery, made through computational analysis using psRNATarget, suggests that regulation of this factor likely proceeds via transcript cleavage.

Expression of the stu-miRNA408a-3p gene

In the Jelly and Javid cultivar, stu-miRNA408a-3p expression was significantly higher at 10 days than at 5 days after ND application. In both D394 and Avin genotypes, stu-miRNA408a-3p expression levels remained significantly elevated at five and ten days post nitrogen limitation treatment, indicating a sustained miRNA-mediated stress response. Conversely, in Bahar, KG911-91, and Santé. stu-miRNA408a-3p levels decreased significantly at both 5 and 10 days after ND exposure. In Agria cultivar, stu-miRNA408a-3p expression was significantly higher at 5 days than at 10 days after ND application.

Expression of the BCP gene

StBCP gene expression increased significantly in Bahar and Jelly cultivars at 5 days compared to 10 days post-ND treatment. StBCP gene expression increased significantly in Avin, Santé, D394, and KG911-91 at 10 days compared to 5 days post-ND treatment. In Javid and Agria, StAP2 expression was maintained significantly at both 5 and 10 days after ND application. The Basic blue copper protein (PGSC0003DMT400061040) is anticipated to be a direct target of stu-miR408a-3p. A predicted binding site, spanning nucleotides 48–68 of the coding sequence, suggests that the miRNA will inhibit the translation of this gene’s transcript.

Expression of the StNAC and StGPX genes under ND

StNAC and StGPX are both targeted by novel miRNAs (novel_chr06_289426 and novel_chr08_424067). These two target genes were examined in eight genotypes at 5 and 10 days after ND treatment. In the Avin, KG911-91, D394, and Jelly genotypes, StNAC gene expression significantly increased at 5 and 10 days after ND application. Conversely, StNAC gene expression was significantly reduced in Bahar, Santé, Javid, and Agria for the same time points (5 and 10 days post-ND treatment). A predicted interaction exists between the novel miRNA chr06_289426 and the coding sequence of PGSC0003DMT400081229. This interaction, localized to nucleotides 280–300, may result in the translational repression of the target mRNA. The novel identified miRNA, chr08_424067, is predicted to form a complementary bond within the coding sequence of PGSC0003DMT400032154. This interaction specifically occurs at nucleotides 739–761, thereby suggesting a mechanism of translational inhibition.

The expression of the StGPX gene was significantly upregulated in the Avin, KG911-91, and D394 cultivars at both 5 and 10 days following ND treatment. In contrast, the Agria, Javid, and Bahar genotypes exhibited decreased StGPX expression under the same conditions, indicating potential sensitivity to oxidative stress induced by ND. Additionally, StAPX gene expression was enhanced—after 5 days in Santé and after 10 days in Jelly—following ND application (Fig. 2).

Analysis of correlations between physiological traits and gene expression levels at 5 and 10 Days after ND conditions

A positive correlation was observed between stu-miRNA399o-3p and sugar content at 5 days after ND condition. stu-miRNA169a-5p demonstrated positive correlations with StNF-YA and StNAC, suggesting its role in modulating TFs related to nitrogen limitation stress. Conversely, it exhibited a negative correlation with StCAT, implicating a potential inhibitory effect on catalase-mediated reactive oxygen species detoxification. Moreover, the positive correlation observed between StGPX and StARF8 may point to a connection between auxin signaling and antioxidant activity. Correlations between traits were analyzed 10 days after ND conditions (Fig. 3). stu-miRNA399o-3p showed a positive correlation with sugar content, suggesting a potential regulatory role in carbohydrate accumulation or mobilization under stress conditions. The TF ANT exhibited positive correlations with antioxidant enzymes StAPX and StPOD, implying involvement in oxidative stress regulation. However, ANT was negatively correlated with both sugar content and stu-miRNA399o-3p. Additionally, StAPX and StPOD were positively correlated with each other.

Conserved and novel miRNA target genes

Post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression is achieved by miRNAs, small RNA molecules that lack protein-coding capacity. These molecules modulate the fate of target genes, either by impacting the rate at which they are translated or by influencing their lifespan within the cell. The mechanism by which miRNAs regulate target gene expression is a contentious subject, with evidence supporting target gene destabilization, translational repression, and even activation of gene expression. A diverse array of genes is subject to regulation and interaction by a collection of miRNAs, including but not limited to miR172, miR408, miR67, miR169, and miR399. These include genes encoding proteins such as NAC domain-containing proteins, APETALA2 (AP2), auxin-responsive factors (ARFs), nitrate transporter (NRT), Nuclear Factor Y (NF-YA), peroxidase (POD), PHOSPHATE2 (PHO2), and Blue copper protein (BCP). Experimental evidence suggests that the regulation of target genes by their corresponding miRNAs play a significant role in potato root development under ND conditions.

The detection of pathways associated to predicted known and novel miRNA target genes under ND conditions

GO and KEGG pathway analyses were performed on the target genes of all differentially expressed miRNAs to further elucidate their potential roles in modulating the ND response. From these analyses, 53 GO annotation terms were identified, categorized into 20 molecular processes, 13 cellular processes, and 20 biological process groups. A comparison of GO annotations under ND conditions versus normal conditions revealed significant enrichment in key functional categories. Specifically, in biological processes (BP), enriched terms included cellular macromolecule metabolic process, protein metabolic process, biosynthetic process, organic cyclic compound metabolic process, and organic substance biosynthetic process. For cellular components (CC), nucleus, cell periphery, and plasma membrane were highly represented. The most enriched molecular function (MF) categories were protein binding, small molecule binding, anion binding, nucleotide binding, nucleoside phosphate binding, carbohydrate derivative binding, and ribonucleotide binding (Fig. 5, 6).

KEGG analysis of target genes of known and novel miRNAs.

Pathway enrichment analysis, utilizing the KEGG database, was performed to understand the biological functions of the differentially expressed miRNA target genes. The genes were mainly implicated in pathways including lysine degradation, tryptophan metabolism, spliceosomal processes, ABC transporters, glycerophospholipid metabolism, broader metabolic pathways, and the biosynthesis of cofactors and secondary metabolites. These findings collectively suggest that the distinct expression profiles of miRNAs and their respective target genes underpin the wide spectrum of ND tolerance in cultivars. Moreover, the results imply that miRNA expression is dynamically regulated (induced or repressed) in response to ND stress, leading to altered expression levels of their target genes.

Detection of Known and novel miRNA

Within the root tissue samples, a total of 655 evolutionarily conserved miRNA genes demonstrated expression. An analysis of the length distribution for these established miRNA species revealed a notable prevalence of sizes at 22, 23, and 25 nucleotides (nt) (Fig. 8). Sequences that lacked homology with any previously characterized miRNAs were subsequently aligned with the Solanum tuberosum genome sequence, employing the mirdeep2 algorithm, in order to identify prospective novel miRNA candidates. Across the ND and CK conditions, 483 new miRNAs were successfully identified. This discovery implies that the folding free energy of their precursor molecules was sufficiently low to foster a stable secondary structure. The dimensions of these newly characterized miRNAs encompassed a range of 19 to 25 base pairs (bp), with the maximum observed length being 25 nt (Fig. 8a).

MiRNA response to ND

The size distribution patterns of short RNA molecules exhibited similarity under experimental condition. Analysis revealed a clear differentiation in small RNA constituents across the two experimental conditions. Conventional profiles were largely defined by 22-nucleotide known microRNAs, while a greater concentration of 25-nucleotide RNAs distinctly marked the novel patterns. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) constituted the vast majority of the small RNA pool, averaging 87.23%, followed by transfer RNA (tRNA) at 9.51%, small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) at 6.76%, and small nuclear RNA (snRNA) at 0.01% (Figs. 8b and 9).

Interaction of miRNA-TF

An analysis of the regulatory interplay between TFs and miRNAs in potato (Solanum tuberosum) has uncovered a set of central regulatory hubs. Network analysis revealed that ARF (Auxin Response Factor), ERF (Ethylene Response Factor), and NAC (NAM, ATAF, and CUC) TFs are subject to regulation by a diverse array of miRNAs, positioning them as key hubs in the transcriptional reprogramming of potato under ND. ARF TFs emerged as the most connected nodes, indicating their central role in auxin-mediated root development, lateral root initiation, and nutrient foraging plasticity. In parallel with the central role of ARF TFs in auxin signaling, our analysis highlights distinct yet complementary functions for ERF and NAC TFs within the nitrogen stress-responsive regulatory network. The ERF family is primarily associated with ethylene signaling pathways and regulating genes involved in abiotic stress responses, including oxidative stress, hypoxia, and nutrient deprivation. On the other hand, NAC (NAM, ATAF, and CUC) family is largely implicated in senescence, nutrient remobilization, and stress tolerance and in regulating downstream genes involved in programmed cell death, leaf aging, and nitrogen recycling. The analysis identified stu-miR160, stu-miR172, and stu-miR396 as the principal miRNAs governing the expression of key TFs such as ARF, ERF, and NAC under ND in potato. This regulatory pattern aligns with their well-established roles in model plant systems like Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa. The architecture of this regulatory network suggests that miRNAs provide a layer of precise modulation over TF expression, thereby orchestrating the crosstalk between different hormone pathways and the plant’s response to stress. Collectively, this evidence indicates that regulatory pairings between TFs and miRNAs are fundamental to the potato’s ability to manage its growth and adapt to environmental challenges. This relationship is especially crucial under conditions of nitrogen deprivation, a stressor known to significantly impact auxin, ethylene, and senescence-related pathway.

Interaction of miRNA and their target genes

The potato, Solanum tuberosum, exhibits a remarkably complex web of connections between miRNAs and the genes they regulate. This intricate miRNA-target relationship underscores a sophisticated level of control exerted after genes are initially transcribed, modulating gene expression in response to both the plant’s growth and development, as well as its reaction to environmental stressors. Several key miRNAs, including stu-miR169, stu-miR530, stu-miR156, and stu-miR167, serve as central hubs in this system, each controlling a wide variety of genes involved in the plant’s development and stress responses (Fig. 10 and Supplementary data S2, 3). The broad influence of key miRNAs on multiple TFs and downstream target genes underscores their role as master regulators of gene expression under nitrogen stress in potato. A key finding is that many genes are controlled by more than one miRNA at a time. This overlap creates a robust regulatory system with built-in redundancy, while also allowing for the fine-tuning of critical life processes under different environmental pressures. The network is also organized into distinct clusters where miRNAs and their target genes appear to work together in specialized modules, particularly for tasks like regulating gene transcription, hormone signaling, and defense. Ultimately, this dense web of control shows how potato uses miRNAs to orchestrate a delicate balance between growth and adaptation to stress. The discovery of these hub miRNAs and their target genes modules is crucial, as it suggests they play a central role in how the plant responds to nutrient shortages, such as ND, by adjusting key genetic pathways to manage metabolism and growth for survival.

Discussion

Under typical field conditions, plants face persistent exposure to numerous abiotic stresses, which can occur separately or simultaneously and severely reduce their growth and productivity44,45. Unreliable and scarce N availability is a particularly significant stress, driven by factors like soil erosion, rainwater leaching, atmospheric loss, and microbial uptake46. While farmers in developed nations often counteract this scarcity by applying excessive N fertilizer, this practice leads to significant waste and environmental contamination. In contrast, limited access to resources prevents many farmers in developing countries from using sufficient N fertilizer, leading to diminished crop yields. Improving NUE in crops such as potato is therefore critical to lessen dependence on heavy fertilization while still achieving high productivity in ND conditions47. Promisingly, recent breakthroughs in plant molecular biology, such as the discovery of miRNAs as crucial regulators of stress adaptation, provide new tools for developing crops with greater resilience and NUE48. The significance of miRNAs in bolstering crop productivity and resilience is central to this research, identifying them as pivotal elements for achieving food security and sustainable agricultural systems. Advanced biotechnological tools, including transgenic modifications and CRISPR/Cas genome editing, enable the precise manipulation of both upregulated and downregulated miRNAs. These interventions are a powerful means of optimizing key agronomic traits, such as tolerance to abiotic stresses like drought and salinity, resistance to pests, and improved nutrient utilization efficiency49. Creating and choosing potato varieties with higher NUE would not only make fertilizer application more efficient but also lower environmental hazards and production expenses, making a substantial contribution to sustainable agriculture and global food security50. Our work aims to understand how plants respond to nitrogen deficits in order to devise strategies that boost NUE, either by increasing yields with existing N levels or by maintaining yields with less nitrogen input. To identify a ND-tolerant genotype and characterize DEMs, this study performed a screening of miRNAs and their target genes in the roots of eight potato genotypes. Significant variation in miRNA expression was observed among these genotypes in response to ND. Under ND conditions, 303 DEMs (comprising 91 novel and 212 known miRNAs) were identified from the miRNA families expressed in the main roots of potato seedlings. These DEMs encompassed several prominent families, including stu-miRNA167, stu-miRNA169, stu-miRNA172, stu-miRNA399, and stu-miRNA408. The target genes of these miRNA families play crucial roles in diverse biological functions such as protein metabolism, root system architecture (RSA) development, defense against oxidative damage, nitrate transport, and hormone biosynthesis to improve root growth. A notable finding was the unique stress response exhibited by each genotype. Broadly, these responses could be categorized into two primary classes: those directly linked to known miRNAs and those involving the target genes of both novel and established miRNAs.

Root system architecture (RSA) development

To effectively absorb nitrogen from the soil, plants have evolved advanced regulatory systems for root development and N transport. Various recent reviews51–54 have detailed root adaptation in response to changes in N availability and the potential for genetically engineering optimal root traits. Several miRNAs have been characterized in plants for their involvement in N signaling during root development, including miR174, miR172, and miR16755–57. The miR167-ARF8 regulatory circuit plays a crucial role in plant lateral root development and their response to nitrogen29. For instance, high nitrogen treatment applied to Arabidopsis thaliana prior to lateral root germination has been shown to suppress miR167a expression while simultaneously enhancing ARF8 transcription and accumulation. Beyond this direct circuit, miR167 also collaborates with other miRNAs to precisely control the number of adventitious roots in plants. Moreover, an intricate network exists where ARF6 and ARF8 interact with ARF17, functioning as both positive and negative modulators of adventitious root initiation. This reciprocal regulation occurs at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels, largely mediated by miR160 and miR16758. MiR164 and miR167 both respond to ND across plants like wheat, Arabidopsis, maize, and rice. While reduced expression of both miR164s and miR167s is associated with low-N tolerance in rice59, their individual behaviors under N starvation can differ markedly in other species. A consistent trend shows miR167 down-regulation in Arabidopsis60,61, maize roots62, and durum wheat roots with higher NUE (miR167h63). Conversely, miR164 often exhibits up-regulation, as observed in Arabidopsis64, maize leaves62, and high-NUE durum wheat roots (miR164d63) under N stress.

In this study, the Avin, Santé, and KG911-91 genotypes were down-regulated at two time-points. In Arabidopsis, miR167 plays a pivotal role in lateral root development by post-transcriptionally regulating the auxin response factors ARF6 and ARF8, which are essential for auxin-mediated root initiation and elongation. A decrease in nitrogen availability causes a down-regulation of miR167 expression. ARF proteins exert their regulatory function on auxin-responsive gene expression by binding to specific cis-acting promoter elements. Furthermore, miR167 also target genes IAA-Ala Resistant-3 (IAR3), a gene crucial for releasing active auxin through the hydrolysis of its inactive precursors. This secondary pathway also contributes significantly to robust root development, especially under stressful conditions. Consequently, the observed down-regulation of miR167 during ND effectively removes its suppressive effect on ARF TFs, thereby invigorating auxin signaling pathways that are essential for stimulating lateral root development.

Specific miRNAs significantly impact crop NUE, as recent studies show65,66. Wheat (cv. AK58) and cotton (cv. ZS57) exhibit upregulated miR172 in N-deficient roots, influencing AP2 TFs to improve root architecture and nitrogen acquisition60,67. Likewise, potato cultivars Yanshu4 and Atlantic show upregulation of stu-miR408b-3p_R-1 in roots and leaves during early growth under nitrogen stress. This miRNA’s role in mitigating oxidative stress, regulating copper metabolism genes, and promoting root development leads to better root elongation, increased lateral root formation, and reduced ROS damage, all vital for efficient nitrogen uptake and stress tolerance under nitrogen limitation. The coordinated expression of specific miRNAs and their target genes suggests a tightly regulated feedback system that fine-tunes root architecture in response to developmental cues and environmental conditions. Additionally, these findings may assist in identifying potato tolerance to ND based on expression profiles. In this study, Avin and D394 exhibited similarities to another potato research68, which found that increasing stu-mir408 in Yanshu4, an N-efficient potato variety, compared to Atlantic, an N-inefficient potato variety, was consistent.

Functional roles of NAC, AP2, ARF8, and NF-YA in root development

Root development is a crucial process in the plant life cycle. Recent studies by Piya et al.69 revealed that many miRNAs actively respond to root growth and development by regulating TFs. TFs indirectly influence the expression of stress-responsive genes, a role complemented by miRNA gene expression patterns70. TFs from different families, including NAC, NF-Y, and the auxin-modulated ARF and AUX/IAA, display differential expression between N-stressed and control durum wheat tissues71. Identifying NAC TFs as target genes of both novel_chr08_424067 and stu-miR164 suggests a potentially conserved and layered regulatory mechanism in root development and stress response. NAC TFs contribute to several key biological functions, including shoot apical meristem development, embryogenesis, lateral root formation, and the response to abiotic stress32.

In 2014, Sorin et al72, demonstrated that miR169defg controls primary root growth through its targeting of NF-YA2 and NF-YA10. They found that interference with this regulatory pathway results in modified root meristem cell structure and an indirect effect on lateral root initiation. In rice, miR169 regulates ND responses by directly targeting NF-YA gene Mrna73. NAC1 transmits auxin signals essential for lateral root development and is also a known target gene of mir16474. The expression of miR169 is repressed under ND conditions in Arabidopsis; this miRNA target AP2 TFs associated with NUD and drought stress62. Overall, key TFs—NFYA1/3, ARFs, AP2/EREBP, and NAC—are central to RNA-mediated transcriptional regulation (Fig. 9). They are specifically targeted by miR169, miR160, miR172, and miR6445, respectively, and are crucial for plant development and nitrogen response75. We found significant variation in the up- and down-regulation of miRNA172, mir169, and mir167, and their target genes, across the eight genotypes. Specifically, the expression patterns in tolerant genotypes like D394 and Avin are consistent with crop resistance to ND, suggesting that these genotypes possess molecular traits that confer enhanced tolerance. These genotypes could serve as donor lines in breeding programs aimed at improving NUE. Expression markers from D394 and Avin might also be used for marker-assisted selection or gene editing target genes.

Nitrate transport and sugar content led to higher nitrogen tolerance using mir169

Both nitrate and sugar are capable of triggering NRT2.1 expression76. Sugars activate genes for nitrate, phosphate, and sulfate transport77, demonstrating a close link between photosynthesis, carbon metabolism, and nutrient uptake. In the present study, Avin, D394, Agria, Javid, and Santé had high sugar content at two time points. NRT2.1 expression is induced by low nitrate concentrations78 and further stimulated by light and sugars79. These regulatory mechanisms are thought to modulate root nitrate uptake, adjusting it to the plant’s N and carbon status. ND spurred rapid root growth, boosted the transport of sorbitol and sucrose to roots, and accelerated sugar metabolism80. In wild-type plants, N starvation induced NRT2.1 expression. However, in plants overexpressing miR169a, N starvation inhibited NRT2.1 expression, reducing NRT2.1 mRNA levels to 56–74% of wild-type levels. This indicates impaired nitrogen uptake in the transgenic plants. Conversely, N-tolerant Arabidopsis genotypes exhibit suppressed miR169a expression, leading to increased NF-YA levels and consequently enhanced NRT2.1 expression, improving nitrogen acquisition and stress tolerance27.

MiRNA-TF interaction under ND

The emergence of advanced molecular tools has led to the discovery of critical miRNAs that are responsive to nitrogen levels. This has opened a new research frontier dedicated to understanding their regulatory functions and identifying the genes they target in nutrient signaling pathways. It has been observed that these miRNAs often regulate their target genes by acting on TFs, a mechanism essential for the broad, dynamic control needed to maintain nutrient homeostasis throughout a plant’s different tissues. Therefore, altering miRNA expression represents a powerful strategy for engineering crops with improved NUE, an approach strongly supported by recent findings on how miRNA activity changes under nitrogen-deprived conditions. Under nitrogen limitation, miR167 targets ARF6 and ARF8, influencing lateral root development.

In Arabidopsis, ARF8 expression increases in the pericycle and lateral root cap under ND.

These regulatory interactions highlight the key role of miRNA–TF modules in root adaptation to ND28,81. The miR169 targets NFYA TFs, which regulate nitrate transporters NRT1.1 and NRT2.1 in Arabidopsis82. Under ND, miR169 is downregulated, leading to NFYA upregulation and enhanced N uptake. Overexpression of miR169 reduces NFYA expression, causing lower N accumulation, chlorosis, anthocyanin buildup, and early senescence, highlighting its key role in nitrogen uptake and remobilization60. Functional analysis of miRNA-targeted genes reveals that TF families such as AP2/ERF, ARF, and HD-ZIP play pivotal roles in helping potato plants cope with nitrogen limitation. These TFs regulate distinct yet interconnected pathways that modulate development, nutrient uptake, and stress resilience. They achieve this through precise control of root system architecture83.

The impact of antioxidant enzyme in ND

The variation in miR408 expression across eight genotypes is a strong indicator of underlying genetic diversity, especially in traits linked to root development and stress response. The high conservation of miR156, miR167, miR395, miR319, miR408, and miR2111 is attributed to their up-regulation during four different types of nutrient stress61. In another study on potato, miR408 was up-regulated under ND in tolerant cultivars68. This study examined BCP gene expression at two time points. At 10 days post-ND, BCP expression was higher in Avin and D394. Zhou et al.84 similarly found that miR408 levels don’t always reflect the expression of its target genes, providing experimental evidence of miR408 cleaving OsUCL30 mRNA.

While miR408 is typically known to downregulate its copper-binding protein targets (like UCL8 or Plantacyanin), a study in Oryza sativa (rice) revealed a lack of correlation between miR408 expression and its target gene levels85. The activity of antioxidant enzymes including POD, CAT, and APX was significantly affected with ND. Variation in the measured expression levels of miR408 or its target genes across the eight cultivars strongly suggests that their genetic backgrounds influence regulatory dynamics86. Abiotic stressors, exemplified by NUD and drought conditions, induce metabolic perturbations and precipitate an overabundance of reactive oxygen species (ROS). This ROS accumulation leads to oxidative damage of critical biomolecules and compromises cellular functionality87. In response, plants initiate defense pathways involving the upregulation of antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD, CAT, APX, and POD. Augmented antioxidant capacity facilitates ROS detoxification and the maintenance of redox homeostasis. Consequently, genotypes endowed with enhanced antioxidant defense mechanisms exhibit diminished ROS accumulation and consequently, greater tolerance to abiotic stresses88. There were the high amount of POD and APX content in Avin and D394. The amount of antioxidant compounds, how quickly they accumulate, and when this accumulation begins are crucial for reducing the negative effects of ND. This may explain why the Avin and D394 genotypes exhibit greater tolerance.

Conclusion

This study characterized both known and novel microRNAs (miRNAs) in potato and their predicted gene targets. In conditions of ND, the genes targeted by these miRNAs were found to be associated with stress adaptation, the metabolism of lipids, ABA hormone transport, and the metabolic pathways for lysine and tryptophan. Furthermore, specific miRNAs were observed to control essential TFs, including NAC, ARFs, NF-YA, and AP2. Their regulatory scope also extends to the modulation of antioxidant enzymes and the transporters responsible for nitrate uptake. These combined effects foster the development of a more effective root architecture, thereby improving the plant’s capacity for nutrient absorption. Among the miRNAs studied, miR169, miR172, miR399, miR408, and miR167 were detected as central regulators during nutrient-related stress. The stress-tolerant varieties, Avin and D394, were distinguished by an increased expression of miR408, miR399, and miR172, coupled with a corresponding decrease in the expression of miR169 and miR167. The observable variations in root growth among eight different genotypes under nutrient scarcity can be attributed to substantial differences at the molecular level. The Avin and D394 varieties demonstrated superior adaptation through mechanisms like accelerated growth, greater lateral root formation, and enhanced antioxidant activity. These advantages were driven by their distinct miRNA profiles, which contributed to the formation of more efficient root systems and optimized metabolic performance. These discoveries help to clarify the precise functional roles that miRNAs and their target genes play in the plant’s response to a lack of essential nutrients. Consequently, the most influential miRNAs and their corresponding targets could be utilized as biomarkers for breeding new potato lines with superior nutrient efficiency. Future validation of these miRNA-target relationships could be achieved through advanced methods like gene editing or direct manipulation of gene expression. Gaining a deeper knowledge of how miRNAs regulate nutrient transporters can also inform the development of smarter fertilization strategies, which could reduce both agricultural costs and environmental impact. By integrating this miRNA data with findings from transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, scientists can uncover the complex, interconnected regulatory networks within the plant. These comprehensive multi-omics approaches will be invaluable for guiding sophisticated crop improvement programs. Ultimately, this study provides a molecular framework for creating stress-resilient and nutrient-efficient potato varieties, significantly advancing the current understanding of miRNA-mediated regulation in plants.
