Detecting Coordination on Short-Video Platforms: The Challenge of Multimodality and Complex Similarity on TikTok
Inga K. Wohlert, Davide Vega, Matteo Magnani, Alexandra Segerberg

Abstract
Research on online coordinated behaviour has predominantly focused on text-based social media platforms, where coordination manifests clearly through the frequent posting of identical hyperlinks or the frequent re-sharing of the same textual content by the same group of users. However, the rise of short-video platforms like TikTok introduces distinct challenges, by supporting integrated multimodality within posts and complex similarity between them. In this paper, we propose an approach to detecting coordination that addresses these characteristic challenges. Our methodology, based on multilayer network analysis, is tailored to capture coordination across multiple modalities, including video, audio, and text, and explicitly handles complex forms of similarity inherent in video and audio content. We test this approach on political videos posted on TikTok and extracted via the TikTok researcher API. This application demonstrates the capacity of the approach to identify coordination, while also critically highlighting potential pitfalls and limitations.

1
Introduction

Methods for detecting coordinated behaviour are of increasing interest in the study of online communication
(
Mannocci et al 2024
)
. A key motivation is the recognition that
social media platforms, which today reach a significant portion of a population
(
Merten 2020
;
Newman et al 2024
)
, can serve as sites for coordinated manipulation, not least in the context of digital election interference and disinformation campaigns.
Coordination is central to problematic phenomena such as astroturfing, where several coordinated social media accounts create a false appearance of widespread and authentic grassroots support for a particular issue or cause
(
Kovic et al 2018
;
Zerback and Töpfl 2021
)
. At the same time, coordination also plays a key role in earnest political campaigns and media communication strategies that lack malicious intent, yet are still worthy of study.
Existing methods for detecting coordination in social media have focused mainly on text-based platforms such as Twitter (now X) and Facebook
(
Mannocci et al 2024
;
Ng and Carley 2023a
)
, but the rise in popularity of short video-based platforms poses new challenges.
A first challenge concerns multimodality. On short-video platforms, nearly all posts integrate multiple modalities, such as video, audio, and semi-structured text, and no modality is individually sufficient to understand the post. For example, to understand a video it is typically necessary to consider both the visuals and audio.
The second challenge is the complex similarity between posts. Assessing whether two posts enact the same message is ambiguous and technically challenging. For example, a video may be re-posted by multiple users with minor modifications, still providing a sign of potential coordination. This departs from the assumptions underpinning most literature on coordination in text-based platforms, where the posting of identical elements such as Web links is used as a signal of coordination.
This paper presents an approach to detect coordination on short-video-based platforms that addresses these challenges. The approach has two key features. First, we address the prominence of multimedia content by accounting for coordination across multiple modalities, represented as the layers of a multilayer network. In particular, we consider music, speech, video frames, video descriptions, and hashtags, both individually and in combination.
Second, we identify and validate functions relevant to addressing the complex similarity challenge in the short-video platform context.

We apply our approach to a prominent short-video-based platform, TikTok, which provides an archetypical representation of a recommender-based short-video system. Currently the globally most downloaded social media platform
(
Statista 2024
)
, TikTok has evolved from being primarily associated with dancing and lip-syncing, to a highly relevant political environment
(
Newman et al 2024
)
. The relevance of TikTok for the detection of coordinated behaviour was accentuated during the recent Romanian elections, where TikTok user accounts were reportedly paid to promote a pro-Russian candidate who had previously received minimal recognition and subsequently performed well in the elections
(
TikTok 2024
;
Nash 2024
;
BBC News 2024
)
.

We work with the Researcher API to evaluate the feasibility of our approach within the constraints of the API context. Our data contains videos and slide shows posted within the two weeks prior to the 2024 European Parliament elections and is gathered using the hashtags that were used the most by German political party accounts during this period. To identify signals of possible coordination, we build a multilayer network, where in each layer two user accounts are connected based on a different type of co-action, e.g. having posted a very similar video description at a very similar time.
Finally, we investigate the resulting clusters of accounts, using different network filtering approaches, to identify and further examine patterns of potential coordination.

Our approach yields evidence of potential coordination, such as accounts with similar username structure that were only active on election period. We also find different groups of user accounts in the different modalities, confirming the intuition that a multimodal approach is important in this kind of platform context in order not to miss evidence of coordination or important user groups. Some of the evidence of potential coordination is obtained thanks to the use of similarity functions matching related but not identical content, which highlights their importance in the context of video-first environments.

2
Background

This section first highlights the relevance of TikTok as a potential site of manipulated and organic political content and coordination, as evidenced by recent empirical studies. We then provide an overview of the literature on the detection of online coordinated behaviour based on the construction of networks of co-actions.
We focus on the two main aspects that speak to the key challenges we identify with contemporary short-video centred platforms: multimodal and similarity-based coordination detection. We conclude by detailing the challenge of multimodality and complex similarity within the specifics of the features of TikTok posts and platform affordances.

2.1
TikTok as a platform for political content dissemination

TikTok has established itself as a politically relevant social media platform, with its own political landscape. While early influential political content was predominantly far-right
(
Weimann and Masri 2020
)
, the platform’s user base has expanded beyond its initially primarily young demographic, broadening and reshaping the political landscape
(
Brodovskaya et al 2022
)
. Meanwhile, various types of actors are now also well-represented on the platform, including political parties, governments, news organisations, advocacy groups and political influencers.

While TikTok prohibits paid political ads, the platform has become the site for political campaigns of various kinds. Political parties across different countries use it as a platform for their campaigning

(
Cervi and Marín-Lladó 2021
;
Widholm et al 2024
;
Grantham 2024
;
TikTok 2024
;
Albertazzi and Bonansinga 2023
)
. It is also used for protest mobilisation
(
Brodovskaya et al 2022
;
Hohner et al 2024
)
, as a space for playful meme politics
(
Vijay and Gekker 2021
;
Cervi and Divon 2023
;
Hautea et al 2021
)
, and in war propaganda
(
Bösch and Divon 2024
)
.

Observers have highlighted the risk that TikTok could become a site of malicious coordinated behaviour. As noted, a key example is the risk around election campaigns, with concerns surfacing in relation to the 2024 Romanian elections
(
BBC News 2024
)
. One early study examined ‘known’ coordination on Twitter and TikTok in Mexico, analysing 52 TikTok videos using content analysis
(
Espinoza and Piña-García 2023
)
. However, there has been little systematic scholarly attention to coordination and the underlying patterns or potential coordination involved in the dissemination of political messages on this platform (for a recent exception, see
Luceri et al 2025
).

Nevertheless, in principle, this is a prime area to draw on the tradition of scholarly work on the detection of coordinated behaviour that developed for other social media platforms.

2.2
Detecting Coordinated Behaviour using Co-Actions

A rich literature has emerged in the study of Coordinated Behaviour and Influence (CBI) in the context of online behaviour, including its problematic expressions. We follow Manocci et al. in defining coordinated online behaviour as a group of users (actors), who perform synergic actions in pursuit of an intent
(
Mannocci et al 2024
)
. The term “coordinated inauthentic behaviour” has its origin at Facebook
(
Facebook Newsroom 2018
)
, where it was used to refer to groups or accounts working together to mislead others about what they are doing. As different platforms employ different strategies and terminology, on TikTok similar actions are tracked under the term of “covert influence operations”
(
TikTok 2024
)
. As noted, the work on detecting coordinated behaviour has primarily focused on Facebook and Twitter.

A powerful approach to analysing coordinated action on platforms such as Facebook and Twitter hinges on the detection of “co-action” in the context of a network analysis framework. In the diversity of approaches using co-actions, the majority define the term “co-action” as two users performing the same action on the same target or content. Therefore, co-actions can involve reacting to existing content or sharing similar posts. The most studied form is co-sharing of identical elements such as Web links
(
Giglietto et al 2020
;
Gruzd et al 2022
)
and as co-retweets
(
Nizzoli et al 2021
;
Pacheco et al 2021
)
, while other types of co-actions include co-commenting, co-replying, and co-liking
(
Kirdemir et al 2022
;
Jahn and Rendsvig 2023
;
Mannocci et al 2024
;
Mannocci 2025
)
.

In addition to detecting the co-action itself, studies often require the co-action to occur almost simultaneously, which poses a methodological challenge in defining and identifying a cut-off point. Usually, researchers set a fixed time-frame, e.g. always one minute
(
Soares 2023
)
or the time-frame can be calculated based on the data such as done by
Giglietto et al 2020
. Some different approaches to deal with the temporal component in understanding coordination, are the use of a multiplex network, where the temporal component is a feature to be studied
(
Tardelli et al 2023
)
.

It would clearly be fruitful to extend the co-action framework for detecting coordination to the short-form video platforms that have become prominent in the increasingly video-first media ecology. In order to do so, however, the framework needs traction on two characteristic features of short-video platforms: their advanced multimodality and contexts of complex similarity between posts.

2.2.1
Multimodal coordination detection

A handful extant studies consider multiple social media platforms together
(
Barbero et al 2023
)
or multiple co-actions (referred to as synchronised actions)
(
Magelinski et al 2021
)
. Only recent studies started considering multi-modality within one platform in the form of text-similarity and link-sharing
(
De Clerck et al 2024
;
Venâncio et al 2024
)
or different types of user actions, such as co-retweet, co-reply, or co-hashtag on Twitter
(
Mannocci 2025
)
. Combining multiple modalities in one approach is still a largely unexplored topic.

2.2.2
Similarity-based coordination detection

A small number of studies take the route of similarity-based coordination detection. Post similarity-based co-actions include co-posting, co-images, and co-videos
(
Pacheco et al 2021
;
De Clerck et al 2024
)
. Within post similarity, research has mainly focused on detecting similarities in text, such as co-mentions, co-URLs, and co-hashtags
(
Ng and Carley 2023a
;
Magelinski et al 2021
)
. Some approaches convert images into text using machine learning to analyse descriptions rather than visuals
(
Stampe et al 2023
)
, which has been shown to better capture connotative aspects of the images
(
Arminio et al 2024
)
. Studies have also explored text within images
(
Soares 2023
)
and metadata analysis
(
Yu 2021
)
. To identify similar posts, researchers create content networks (e.g. text-text or image-image) that can be transformed into user-user coordination networks
(
Ng et al 2022b
;
Ng et al 2022a
)
.

Similar to the approach we propose in this paper, recent work on TikTok independently performed by
Luceri et al 2025
also turns to similarity functions. The study found that measures from other platforms can translate well to TikTok, such as using the hashtag-sequence, while also revealing major challenges in addressing the problem of coordinated behaviour detection on TikTok in the definition of reasonable similarity function, especially for the audio transcript.

2.3
The challenge of multimodality and complex similarity on TikTok

TikTok as platform creates an environment that encourages co-actions, shapes the multitude of co-action types that can occur, and at the same time poses challenges to detection in the dynamic complexity of communication that results. In addition, the specificities of the TikTok Researcher API further constrains the analytical approach that can be used. Chief among the challenges from the perspective of detecting similarities, thereby co-actions, and by extension possible coordination, are the deep multimodality of posts and the complex similarities that can be involved.

TikTok posts include a multitude of variables across several modalities that may matter for assessing similarity. Typical posts include a video description, a list of hashtags, and the actual video. When uploading a video a user is encouraged to select a music or audio snippet to layer over or instead of the original video audio. The videos themselves also comprise a number of variables, including the frames, the text displayed on the frames and the audio. This multiplicity of variables can be combined in a variety of ways in the course of the memetic communication for which TikTok is known.

A key characteristic of the TikTok environment is to encourage a memetic communication that involves the repetition, imitation and referencing of highly similar material
(
Zulli and Zulli 2020
;
Cervi and Divon 2023
;
Hautea et al 2021
)
. Figure
1
shows common visual alterations of videos. Often videos will have additional texts and if the original video is in a different format than the one accepted by TikTok, some users zoom in, while others autofill the space. Sometimes such visual cues are used to actually showcase disagreement with the underlying video, thus not constituting a sign of coordination.
Yet, even when the video and overall message are to all extents and purposes the same, such complex similarities in the variety of minor visual alterations makes detection much more challenging.

Clearly, the central TikTok practice of altering videos undermines classic approaches to detecting similarity in videos posited on detecting duplicate segments.
The largest focus for detecting video similarity is in near-duplicate video retrieval to detect copyright violation
(
Han et al 2021
;
Yang et al 2014
)
. In general, reliance on duplication will not work in this environment.

As the table makes clear, no function (or modality) is on its own sufficient to identify similar posts that may be to all extents and purposes the same.

A common user action is the traditional video repost. Being a repost, measures such as Audio (audio transcript), Music ID (the unique identifier used by TikTok for music or audio snippets), Video and Video Description remain identical. However, in the TikTok architecture, reposts do not result in a new original post; the video is added as a repost on a user’s profile. As a consequence, reposts need to be searched per user account; filtering the Researcher API for videos does not return them. Another use scenario is to download and reupload a video. In this case, it appears as a new post, and the Audio stays the same, but the Music ID will be different, and additional information such as video descriptions may also be changed.

Two further uses are presented in the possibility to duet or stitch another TikTok video, features that need to be enabled by the original poster. Duetting creates a new post, and maintains the same Audio and Music ID, but creates a split screen with the original video and audio to allow for joint dancing but also visual “commenting”. The video description may also be different. A stitch allows a commentary after the original video, therefore creating a partial audio match, meaning that the audio transcript of the initial video will be a subset of the new audio transcript. The post counts as a new post, and does not necessarily have the same Music ID, video, or description, as these features can or are automatically changed.

The overview in Table 1 highlights that no function is sufficient to identify all possible (and common) ways in which content can be the same or highly similar on TikTok. One conclusion is that a robust approach to detecting coordination on TikTok will need to be able to account for several types of multimodal combinations.

This said, another conclusion from the overview is that audio emerges as a promising key element in an approach to detect co-action in this environment. Audio stays the same across several, albeit not through all use scenarios. However, the audio transcript is not always given, but this could be countered by retrieving transcripts separately, albeit with higher computational effort. An issue to bear in mind with an approach involving any audio element is that trending audio is common on TikTok and these audio tracks can organically appear in very different contexts. This brings a risk of detecting false positives, for example videos with content referring to very different issues and contexts being linked to each other through the use of the same audio. Selecting an available audio snippet, a video will have the same Music ID as others having selected the same audio, even if different parts of the audio are selected.

3
A Methodology to Detect Coordination on TikTok

In this work, we adopt a multilayer network-based methodology, informed by prior literature on coordinated behaviour and influence (CBI) detection (See Section
2.2
). Our approach is based on the concept of
co-actions
, defined as instances in which users perform identical or similar actions in relation to specific content, potentially indicating coordinated behaviour.

To account for TikTok’s
multimodal structure
, we construct a multilayer coordination network where each layer captures a distinct type of co-action (e.g. identical hashtags, similar audio transcripts, matching video descriptions, shared Music IDs, or visual similarity). This multilayered actor-centric model enables us to comprehensively analyse interactions between user accounts, capturing coordination within individual modalities and across multiple modalities simultaneously.

Building this network involves first extracting and filtering relevant TikTok content (Section
3.1
). Each node in the resulting multilayer network represents a distinct user account, and each network layer corresponds to a specific modality-based co-action defined through tailored similarity metrics (Section
3.2.1
). To explicitly address the second major challenge of
complex similarity
, each modality-specific layer undergoes a customized filtering process based on empirically validated thresholds (Section
3.2.2
). This approach ensures that minor yet significant variations — such as slightly altered reposted videos — are effectively captured as meaningful signals of coordination, overcoming the limitations of simpler, exact-matching methods.

Finally, the multilayer network is analysed both quantitatively, through the evaluation of structural properties like degree distributions and overlapping edges, and qualitatively, by examining the actual content within identified network clusters. This dual analytic approach allows us to reliably identify and interpret groups exhibiting potentially coordinated behaviours.

3.1
Dataset

We focus on the 2024 European Parliament election in Germany, held on June 9, 2024. Using the TikTok Research API, we began our data collection by gathering TikTok videos posted by accounts associated with the main political parties at both national and state levels, as well as their lead candidates, during the 30 day period preceding the election (i.e. May 10 to June 9, 2024). The initial list of accounts was based on the study of the 2021 German elections by
Righetti et al 2022
.

We then filtered the collected posts to include only those from parties that secured at least three seats in the European election, matching the minimum number of seats held by the smallest party represented in the Bundestag
1
1

The national parliament of the Federal Republic of Germany
at that time. Consequently, our filtering process excluded posts from parties with minimal media presence but retained content from most represented parties in the Bundestag, as well as some newly established parties. Notable examples include BSW and Volt. BSW is a party established only a few months prior to the EU elections, which has since gained a significant presence in the German political sphere. Volt, a smaller party, achieved notable success among younger voters during the EU campaign by leveraging social media, particularly TikTok
(
Tagesschau 2024
)
.

In the second step of our data collection, we identified and selected the primary hashtag representing each party, including:

The resulting dataset comprises 24,463 videos in various formats (e.g. stand-alone, slideshows), including 959 videos originally posted by the main party and candidate accounts.

For each of the posts, we gathered the original TikTok video as well as the metadata associated with it. However, due to varying availability, certain metadata elements were present only for subsets of videos. Specifically, audio transcripts provided through TikTok’s API were available only for 5,690 videos.
Additionally, data availability varied considerably across modalities. For instance, while TikTok’s API grants access to video comments, it does not allow retrieval of comments linked to specific users. Engagement metrics face similar constraints: although likes and reposts can be obtained via user information, likes are rarely shared publicly, and collecting repost data is slow and inefficient, as the API does not support time-specific queries. These limitations informed our decision to exclude such features from the similarity functions (See Section
3.2.1
) considered in this study.

3.2
A multilayer approach to multimodal analysis

The next step in our methodology involved constructing the multilayer coordination network. For each modality, we compared all relevant user actions to determine whether a given pair of actions should be considered similar — a binary decision based on the modality-specific similarity functions described below. In addition to the similarity, we annotated each pair of actions with their temporal proximity, measuring how closely in time they occurred. Once these pairwise similarities were established, we built user–user networks for each modality, where an edge between two users indicates that they performed similar actions. Edge weights reflect the number of such co-actions between the users, capturing the frequency and intensity of their potential coordination. While network analysis is a common tool to detect coordination, in this paper we use multilayer networks for the joint analysis of multiple modalities.

3.2.1
Layer definition based on co-actions

To identify and model potentially coordinated behaviour, we constructed a set of co-action layers, each corresponding to a specific modality of similar behaviour. Each layer captures a specific type of alignment between user actions, thereby mapping user activity to interpretable patterns of content-based coordination. Some forms of similarity, like identical hashtags or video descriptions, can be captured through straightforward exact matching. Others — particularly in the audio and visual domains — require more nuanced computational methods to account for minor variations, edits, or transcription errors.

To validate the reliability and interpretability of these similarity metrics, we conducted a manual evaluation focused on the more complex audio and video layers. Two native German-speaking coders independently annotated 20 video pairs across 25 unique videos, evaluating similarity along three dimensions: visual similarity, audio similarity, and the underlying message conveyed. Based on their annotations, we tuned algorithmic thresholds to optimize precision and recall for the classification of both exact and partial matches. The coders reached full agreement in all but one case (95% agreement).

These annotations formed the basis for establishing empirically grounded thresholds for the audio and video layers described below.

•

Hashtag-Sequence.
We consider two videos part of the same co-action if they contain the exact same sequence of hashtags in their description. Videos with an empty hashtag sequence (often posted by official party accounts) are excluded. While it is common for several users to use similar hashtags to promote their videos, the use of the exact sequence might be a strong signal of resharing the same content.

•

Video Description.
We connect two videos if they share an identical video description. As with all layers, the resulting video-video network is projected into a user-user network layer. Exact repetition of the same description across different accounts may indicate coordinated messaging or the use of templated content.

•

URLs
We first extract URLs from video descriptions using regular expressions, then connect two users if they have used exactly the same URL; indicating an intent to reference the exact same content rather than the same source.

•

Music ID.
The Music ID is an audio identifier provided via the TikTok Research API (See Section
2.3
). We connect two videos if they use the exact same Music ID. This identifier is assigned automatically either when a user reposts or reuses audio from another existing post — typically by sharing or remixing that content — or when the users manually assign a curated, trending audio during video creation. Thus, while it is possible for two users to independently select the same popular audio, it is equally plausible that shared Music IDs result from internal redistribution or imitation of existing content. This layer helps identify patterns of coordinated reuse or algorithmically encouraged convergence around specific audio trends.

•

Partial and same audio.

Because of minor transcription errors or subtle modifications (e.g. trimming), two videos may have identical or nearly identical audio without sharing the same audio transcript or Music ID. To capture such cases, we use edit distance metrics to measure the textual similarity of audio transcripts, quantifying how many operations are needed to transform one transcript into another. We distinguish between two types of similarity: exact audio (fully matching transcripts) and partial audio (one being a subset of the other).

Using a manually labelled dataset, we identified optimal similarity thresholds based on precision-recall curves: 88 for exact audio and 68 for partial matches. Both thresholds achieved perfect precision and recall in our test set. When similarity scores spanned both categories, we selected the midpoint between the thresholds. This layer enables detection of coordinated messaging that may reuse audio content in ways not captured by TikTok’s internal identifiers, such as re-recordings or minor edits.

•

Video Similarity.
To detect visual coordination, we apply a perceptual image hashing method that enables approximate matching between video frames. We extract one frame every three seconds and compare each frame from the shorter video to frames in the longer one, requiring that all frames have a near-equivalent match. This technique is designed to tolerate minor differences while detecting visually similar content. While the approach does not yield perfect precision and recall under a single threshold, we prioritized high precision to avoid false positives. The method performed well in identifying reused video snippets, including different cuts from the same interview, but was more sensitive to visual modifications such as zooming or autofill effects. This layer captures subtle forms of content coordination that may not be evident through audio or text alone. We apply a similarity threshold of 1, determined based on validation against manually labelled data.

3.2.2
Layer filtering and community detection

To identify clusters of users more likely to exhibit coordinated behaviour, we applied filtering to each co-action layer by removing edges based on either (i) the
frequency
of co-actions between pairs of users, or (ii) the
temporal proximity
of those co-actions. The goal of this filtering process is twofold: to eliminate noisy or spurious connections and to retain structures that meaningfully reflect patterns of potential coordination.

Rather than selecting thresholds heuristically, we adopted a systematic and comparative approach. For each layer, we generated six filtered versions: three based on
co-action frequency
and three based on
co-action timing
.

•

In the frequency-based filters, we retained edges only if the number of shared co-actions between a pair of users (i.e. edge weight) was at least 2, 10, or equal to the average edge weight for that layer. These values were chosen based on established thresholds in the literature
(
Soares 2023
;
Schoch et al 2022
;
Ng and Carley 2023b
)
.

•

In the time-based filters, we retained only those edges for which the annotated time difference between co-actions was equal to or less than 60 seconds, 120 seconds, or 5 minutes. These thresholds also follow precedents in prior work
(
Soares 2023
;
Ng and Carley 2023b
;
Giglietto 2024
)
.

To determine the most informative threshold for each layer, we applied the following evaluation procedure:

1.

Edge and component pruning.
We first discarded any filtered candidate layers where too few or no edges remained, or where no connected component exceeded 8 nodes in size.

2.

Component analysis.
For the remaining candidates, we examined the structure of the largest, second-largest, and third-largest connected components. This included analysing their internal coherence in terms of shared content, behavioural similarity, and naming conventions across user accounts.

3.

Threshold selection.
We selected the optimal threshold as the one that produced the most coherent and interpretable components — i.e. those that clustered users together based on clear, content-driven similarities.

4.

Cross-threshold comparison.
As a supplementary check, we compared the distribution of user accounts across the different filtered networks to assess consistency and sensitivity to the chosen thresholds.

This filtering process yielded a set of refined co-action layers, each capturing stronger and more plausible signs of coordination (See Table
3
). Notice that the temporal proximity of interactions has not been as informative as the frequency of such interactions. We find that often similar content is posted over a longer time frame. For some of the clusters we find, some of the users in the cluster do indeed post within short time spans of each other, however filtering by a shorter time-window leads to missing the larger picture of the cluster. In addition, co-actions which are common in the data, are likely to appear within a short time-frame by chance, while the repetition of a co-action seemingly makes chance less likely. These layers formed the basis for the structural and content-based analysis presented in the following sections.

we find differences in network structure, edges, and user representation across the different modalities. This is partly dependent on the prevalence of the various co-actions.
For example, when posting a video on TikTok the platform encourages the use of an existing audio (Music ID), by suggesting fitting or currently trending audio tracks,
which leads to users to co-act more on the platform. As a result, about 50% of the users present in our dataset, use the same Music ID as another user at least once. As another example, the smaller audio transcript networks are most likely due to the audio transcript not being provided for many posts, e.g. slide shows. Differently from audio tracks, including URLs in the video description is not encouraged by the platform, hence we only find very few cases of users posting the same URL (especially because, in contrast to
Luceri et al 2025

Hashtag-Sequence

The Hashtag-Sequence layer reveals clusters of potential coordinated accounts. Among the users in these clusters, we find user accounts’ names starting with the word “user” followed by a set of numbers, highlighting a potential of automated account creation. The content is usually created with little effort, as video snippets of news or talk shows are often used, while the content is commented on through text within the videos. Often this text contains a bias towards the politicians or content appearing in the videos. Most of these accounts were only active around the election time and have since stopped posting.

We note that this layer also contains potential false positives, due to user accounts using a hashtag referring to a party to criticise rather than agree, making it unlikely to be part of a joint coordinated effort with other users in the cluster.

4.1.2
Video Description

This layer is related to the Hashtag-Sequence layer, because hashtags are part of the video descriptions.
However, although all accounts in this layer also appear in the Hashtag-Sequence layer, the clustering using the video description is more distinct, with sub-clusters of accounts showing the same features. It is interesting to note that the difference in network size becomes less significant at higher thresholds for both the hashtag sequence and the video description. This suggests that for potentially coordinated clusters found through the hashtag sequence, the entire video description is often the same. Investigating the clusters, we find that users were usually only active around the election, either portraying themselves as a news platform or as a personal account.

Music ID

As we discussed in Section

Nevertheless, this layer includes interesting sub-clusters that are not visible in other layers.

As an example, we find one large cluster, where a majority of the connections are based on using the song “L’amour Toujours” as their audio (Figure

This song has been known for its popularity within the political right wing in Germany and caught nation wide attention when a video appeared of people at a bar shouting racist sentences to the song on the 19th of May, in the lead up to the European election, causing a nation wide backlash. The cluster contains videos with the same underlying message in support of the right wing party.
These accounts are still active today, and despite the easy-to-produce content of the post, they do not show any clear signs of automation. Therefore, this case might rather show the power of using sound as a collective, where sound creates a social bond that unites the supporters of the right-wing party. 4.2
Cross-layer analysis

A key feature of our approach is the capacity not only to explore the different clusters and components we find in each layer, but also to combine this with looking across the layers. In principle, this should make it possible to characterise different types of coordination and coordination strategies in two regards. One is the user leveraging different modalities for strategic purposes, which could be revealed through comparing the different clusters that become apparent across the individual layers. The other concerns the multilayer signature, that is, how users engage different layers in combination. In principle, these different types may correspond to different coordination strategies in this TikTok context.

This analysis indeed reveals clusters of users that are distinct in how they leverage different modalities, indicating diverse usage trajectories and possibly different strategies. For example, Figure
7
reveals clusters that can only be found on the Music ID layer (e). Given the high prevalence of Music ID in the results, it could be an attractive coordination strategy to focus on sharing music, in part to benefit from popular spread carried by the music and in part to be masked by a high amount of organic activity in the same vein.

With respect to the expectation that it might be possible to distinguish clusters that exhibit multilayer signatures, however, we were not able to identify obvious clusters of users that characteristically combine elements from multiple layers. Nevertheless, we do find multilayer signature combinations of just a few layers, especially in areas in which this would be highly expected. For example, there is a large overlap between the layers of hashtags and video description, which is where hashtags appear (a, b, d).

5
Discussion and Conclusion

This paper presents an approach for detecting coordinated behaviour on short-video platforms that addresses the novel and challenging combination of integrated multimodality and complex similarity that is becoming prominent in a video-first environment. This approach uses multilayer network analysis as a way to identify signs of coordination within and across modalities. We focused on the case of TikTok, as a pre-eminent short-video platform, and applied our approach to communication around the European Parliament elections in the German context.

Applying our approach demonstrates its capacity to detect potential coordinated behaviour on TikTok, but also potentially different strategies of coordination. Like
Luceri et al 2025
, who work on US election data, we find clusters of user accounts that share similar user names. We also found user accounts that were only active during the election period. Such episodic activity is suggestive of accounts that were created for this purpose. Given the algorithmic architecture of the platform, it is possible to get a large number of views without followers, which in turn means it is possible for custom created accounts to gain reach.

Our proposed methodology based on multilayer network analysis shows a promising path to address coordination on short-video platforms such as TikTok. Nevertheless, there is vastly more work to be done. This includes the development and validation of additional similarity, filtering, and clustering methods for specific modalities, and further exploration of cross-layer patterns. Considering multiple layers together may reinforce the signal of coordinated behaviour, reveal new coordination strategies, or help identifying false positives.

We note that the present application of our approach is circumscribed by the current capabilities of the TikTok Research API, such that not all meaningful similarity measures are possible to examine. For example, measures such as comments, reposts and likes are not fully accessible through the API, either because they are not made available or because it is not possible to set a time frame that would reduce the number of calls to the API to a reasonable level.

Conversely, it is also important to consider that the areas in which organic user engagement is encouraged by the platform clouds the analysis of strategically coordinated behaviour. Our analysis shows that the similarities that the platform encourages stand out as larger networks. For example, the ease of searching content by hashtag and sound seems to find echoes in the prevalence of the same audio identifier in our results. As a consequence our similarity detection algorithms likely detect a significant proportion of organic co-actions not based on coordination. At the same time, the popular areas also suggest styles of content that might become particularly attractive covers for strategic coordination.

We also note that while our data serves our present purposes of applying our approach, it is important to bear in mind that this paper does not report on a full empirical study of coordinated behaviour on TikTok in the German context of the European Parliament elections. This study delivers at best a partial view of communication around the elections, in part because of the character of the data set we are using. There was a time lapse between the election and the collection of the data, meaning posts may have been deleted in the interim. In addition, we focused specifically on hashtags related to the different parties, which excluded non-party actors, topics or hashtags, such as for example the “Reclaim TikTok” hashtag.

A final observation concerns the importance of considered ethical reflection when working with visual content, social media data and a relatively new platform for which research best practices are not well established. To this end, we close with observing that we have, among other things, taken extra care to anonymise user account information and personal identifiers such as faces in visuals of non-public figures.
