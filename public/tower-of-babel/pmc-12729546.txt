A study on the mechanism of relative leader-member exchange on employees' taking charge behavior: from self-perception perspective
Authors: Li Ma, Leqing Liu, Xianshan Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this era of constant change, how to motivate employees to innovate work methods, improve work efficiency, and promote the sustainable development of enterprises has become a hot topic for managers. Drawing on social cognitive theory, this study examines how employees’ perception of relative leader-member exchange (RLMX) drives taking-charge behavior through the perspective of self-perception. We further investigate the mediating role of perceived insider status and the moderating roles of social comparison orientation and role-breadth self-efficacy, thereby clarifying the underlying mechanisms linking RLMX to taking-charge behavior. Using cross-sectional data from 381 employees across diverse industries in China, we test our moderated-mediation model with hierarchical regression analyses. The results show that: RLMX is positively related to taking charge behavior, perceived insider status plays a partial mediating role between RLMX and taking charge behavior, social comparison orientation moderates the impact of RLMX on perceived insider status, and role breadth self-efficacy moderates the relationship between perceived insider status and taking charge behavior. The study provides fresh empirical insights into the mechanisms linking RLMX to taking charge behavior from perspective of self-perception and suggests practical implications for enhancing employees’ taking charge behavior.Keywords: Relative leader-member exchange, Perceived insider status, Social comparative orientation, Role breadth self-efficacy, Taking charge behavior

In the VUCA (Volatile, Uncertain, Complex, and Ambiguous) era, the ability to adapt and evolve is paramount. Organizations seeking to thrive amidst rapid shifts in the economic climate must lean heavily on their workforce to question the status quo, spearhead change, innovate work processes, and propel the company forward. As noted by Homung and Kpinpuo, this requires employees to excel not just within the confines of their job descriptions but also to embrace roles and responsibilities that extend beyond these formal boundaries—what is often termed as proactive extra-role behaviors [1, 2]. Onyishi and Ogbodo emphasize that in such an environment, employees are expected to go above and beyond their prescribed duties [3]. This has led to a surge in academic interest in various forms of proactive extra-role behaviors, including employee voice behavior [4, 5], organizational citizenship behavior [6, 7] and innovative behavior [8, 9]. Among these, taking charge behavior has garnered significant scholarly attention due to its demonstrated positive impact on job satisfaction, affective organizational commitment, and job performance [10, 11].Taking charge behavior is characterized by employees who proactively engage in constructive actions aimed at improving the organizational structure, refining work procedures, boosting work efficiency, and facilitating the achievement of organizational objectives [12]. This behavior is seen as a critical component in navigating the dynamic challenges of the VUCA era.

Previous research on the determinants of taking charge behavior has concentrated on factors at both the individual and organizational levels. For example, studies have examined how proactive personality traits, as per the trait activation theory, influence employees' propensity to take charge [13]. Leadership styles, such as inclusive leadership [14], humble leadership [15], shared leadership [16] and empowering leadership [17], have been identified as direct or indirect antecedents of taking charge behavior. Additionally, ethical and transformational leadership have been posited as antecedent to taking charge behavior [18, 19].

While these studies acknowledge the significant impact of positive supervisor-subordinate interactions on taking charge behaviors, they often neglect the inherent comparisons that arise from such interactions. Employees not only consider their own relationships with their leaders to form leader-member exchange (LMX) perceptions [20], but they also make comparisons with their peers' relationships under the same leader. This leads to the concept of RLMX [21, 22]. China is a society where collectivist culture prevails [23], emphasizing the relative status of individuals within groups. People place more importance on comparisons between each other, and employees in Chinese contexts may be more sensitive to RLMX [24]. At the same time, China's high power distance culture (Hofstede theory) strengthens employees' obedience and dependence on leadership authority, and intensifies their attention to leadership preferences [25]. Therefore, RLMX has more important research significance.Employees with high RLMX, akin to "insiders," tend to receive greater care and support from leaders than others, and thus, are more likely to be allocated organizational resources, enhancing their perceived insider status (PIS). Conversely, those with low RLMX relationships may feel less supported than others and exhibit reduced loyalty to both their leaders and the organization. It's crucial to recognize that individual differences play a role in decoding external information. Employees with a heightened social comparison orientation (SCO) are more attuned to the outcomes of these comparisons [26], and are particularly concerned about their insider status, which in turn affects their work attitudes and initiatives.

Furthermore, proactive work behaviors, such as taking charge behavior, are instrumental in bolstering organizational effectiveness and work efficiency through self-motivation and self-promotion. The enactment of these behaviors is contingent upon three key motivational factors: Can Do, Reason Do and Energized Do, as outlined by Parker [27]. Perceived Insider Status, as previously discussed, mirrors an employee's evaluation of their standing within the organization. This perception significantly impacts their readiness and drive to partake in proactive behaviors. However, for this readiness and drive to materialize into tangible actions, employees must have a precise understanding of their self-efficacy, which communicates the message, "I can do it." So, this study introduces role breadth self-efficacy (RBSE) as a moderating variable. RBSE pertains to an employee's perceived capability to undertake a broader range of proactive behaviors that surpass the technical demands of their role, encompassing the resolution of long-term issues and the enhancement of inefficient work processes, among others [28]. It has been established that there is a correlation between RBSE and proactive behaviors [29]. Moreover, it is posited that based on an employee's conviction that they are an integral part of the organization, the stronger their perception of their own competence in their job roles, the more likely they are to engage in taking charge behavior [30].

According to social cognitive theory, external environmental stimuli prompt individuals to adjust their internal cognition, which subsequently influences their behavioral responses. Simultaneously, individual factors ultimately affect behavior by shaping cognitive processes [31]. Therefore, this study will explore how RLMX as a crucial interpersonal environmental factor in organizations influences employees' perceived insider status, and how these cognitive processes lead to their proactive responsibility-taking charge behaviors. Meanwhile, individual differences in SCO and RBSE play a moderating role in this cognitive process, ultimately impacting taking charge behaviors.

This study contributes to the existing literature in the following ways.First, it explores the relationship between RLMX and employees taking charge behavior under the context of Chinese culture, enriches the research on the antecedents of taking charge behavior. This serves as a valuable supplement to existing studies that primarily focus on the individual level [13, 14, 17] and the organizational level [18, 19] to explore the antecedents of taking charge behavior. At the same time, it effectively illuminates the enhancement mechanisms of social comparison on employee proactive behavior within the Chinese context, addressing a limitation in traditional LMX theory that often neglects the group comparison perspective [19]. Second, based on social cognition theory, we introduced perceived insider status as a mediating variable from a self-perception perspective, which comprehensively captures the psychological progression from external relationship perception to internal motivation for taking charge behavior. It provides a fresh theoretical framework for proactive behavior research. Third, this study introduces SCO and role breadth self-efficacy as boundary conditions that shape the relationship between RLMX, perceived insider status, and taking charge behavior in China's high power distance cultural environment. Incorporating these variables into the above model highlights the role of individual differences in the social comparison process and cognition of self-role competence and their impact on taking charge behavior. This offers valuable empirical support for cross-cultural organizational behavior research.

Theoretical background and hypotheses development

Taking charge behavior

In the face of a fiercely competitive market, companies are setting higher bars for their staff. They expect employees not only to fulfill their designated tasks but also to go beyond their role boundaries, to adapt their work practices, and to boost overall efficiency. Morrison and Phelps term these actions as taking charge behavior, which refers to voluntary and constructive efforts made by employees to ameliorate the ways in which their work is carried out [12]. It is an extra-role behavior that employees freely decide to perform [12]. The most significant difference between taking charge behavior and other proactive extra-role behaviors is that taking charge behavior is inherently change-oriented and aimed at improving work procedures and organizational effectiveness [12]. This behavior is marked by its spontaneity, adaptability, and the benefits it brings to the organization.

Firstly, taking charge behavior is distinct from voice behavior. While voice behavior involves employees offering constructive feedback and ideas about their work [32], it does not ensure that these suggestions will be put into practice. Taking charge behavior, on the other hand, demands that employees take proactive actions that contribute to the organization's future development, showing a strong inclination towards action.Secondly, taking charge behavior includes the removal of inefficient processes within the organization and the advocacy for functional changes [12]. It is primarily focused on tackling ineffective work activities, rather than on the innovation of products, such as new products and processes, which sets it apart from innovative behavior. Moreover, as a change-oriented behavior, taking charge behavior requires employees to break from routine and take risks. Therefore, the quality of the relationship between the employee and their leader [33], the employee's perceived status within the organization, and the employee's own ability [34] to recognize and implement taking charge behavior significantly impact the execution of this behavior [35].

Relative leader-member exchange and taking charge behavior

RLMX was originally proposed by Graen, Liden, and Hoel as an extension of the leader-member exchange theory [36]. RLMX refers to the difference between an employee's individual LMX and the average LMX of their workgroup. This concept is distinguished from LMX in that it reflects the relative position of employees in the workgroup and focuses on the relative value of the quality of exchange relationship [26]. Studying RLMX is important because employees not only value the quality of two-way interactions with their leaders but also consider and compare their relationships with other coworkers and their leaders. This comparison serves as a benchmark for evaluating one's own sense of value, which, in turn, influences positivity and proactivity in future work [35, 37]. RLMX can thus explain variations in employees' work attitudes and behaviors through the lens of social comparison. In the context of Chinese culture, individuals pay more attention to their relative position in the group, as an ancient Chinese saying goes, “ It is not the scarcity that we should worry about, but the inequality in distribution”. So the tendency for employees to engage in social comparisons is particularly pronounced [38], and they place significant importance on the outcomes of these comparisons. Recent studies have confirmed that the RLMX positively influences proactive behaviors, such as constructive actions [39] and organizational citizenship behaviors [40]. However, further research is needed to explore employees' taking charge behavior.

Social cognitive theory proposed by Bandura posits a reciprocal deterministic relationship between the individual, his or her environment, and behavior; all three elements dynamically and reciprocally interact with and upon one another to form the basis for behavior, as well as potential interventions to change behaviors [31]. Based on social cognitive theory, supportive factors in the work environment (such as leadership support, organizational culture, etc.) can affect individual behavior. Employees with a high RLMX are often able to access more organizational resources, opportunities, and support from their leaders than their peers. These supports and resources can provide employees with more favorable environmental support, which promotes proactive behavior. These employees tend to communicate more effectively with their leaders, feel more energized at work, and enjoy certain psychological benefits, which makes them more likely to engage in extra-role behaviors that go beyond their assigned tasks [41]. In contrast, employees who feel they are not receiving preferential treatment or are being marginalized by their leaders are allocated fewer resources and opportunities. They often lack support from their superiors, experience lower job satisfaction [42], and are less willing to engage in taking charge behavior as a result. Secondly, based on the principle of reciprocity, employees who perceive a stronger relationship with their leaders and have access to more resources and opportunities are likely to exhibit more taking charge behavior in return. On the flip side, employees who feel their relative status is lower and do not receive adequate attention and recognition from their leaders may reduce their taking charge behavior. Lastly, engaging in taking charge behavior often involves various risks. In the context of China, where relationalism is prevalent, employees who perceive their supervisory relationship with their leader as superior to that of their coworkers tend to experience a greater sense of psychological security, which makes them more inclined to engage in taking charge behavior.In summary, this paper proposes the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1 RLMX is positively related to employees' taking charge behavior.

Hypothesis 1 RLMX is positively related to employees' taking charge behavior.

The mediating role of perceived insider status

Perceived insider status is the degree to which employees within an organization identify themselves as part of an in-group, or insiders [43]. This concept differs from the actual level of organizational inclusion and provides a novel lens through which to examine the relationship between employees and the organization [44]. Studies have shown that support and assistance from leaders can substantially boost employees' perception of their insider status [45].

In the context of Chinese culture, leaders often distinguish between insiders and outsiders among their subordinates, extending more resources and support to those classified as insiders. Consequently, employees who perceive themselves as insiders enjoy additional preferential treatment and benefits, which leads to a swift increase in their sense of self-worth. This, in turn, reinforces their sense of identity and belonging within the organization, thereby solidifying their insider identity. On the contrary, employees who perceive themselves as outsiders may feel marginalized due to differential treatment from their leaders. In such instances, they are more likely to see themselves as outsiders and maintain only the most basic working relationships with their leaders.Furthermore, supervisors often act as key symbols of the organization, and the quality of the relationship with a supervisor is indicative of an individual's status within the organization. According to social cognitive theory, RLMX as an environmental factor will have an impact on employee’s cognition. Employees with high RLMX feel more support, attention, and resource allocation from their leaders, such as frequent communication, more mentoring, and training opportunities; also have a higher trust relationship with their leaders, which enhances their psychological security; and are usually given higher role expectations and more fair treatment, which causes employees to perceive themselves as core members of the organization and as “insiders”. Therefore, high RLMX is significantly correlated with employees' perceived insider status.

Perceived insider status encapsulates the sentiment, "I am part of the organization; I share in its successes and failures." When an individual's identity is closely intertwined with the organization, they experience a profound sense of belonging that fosters a deeper commitment to the organization's interests [46]. This sense makes them more inclined to engage in proactive behaviors and to assume responsibilities that extend beyond their defined role [44, 47]. Drawing from social cognitive theory, employee’s cognition of insider status will influence behavior. When employees perceive themselves as insiders within the organization, they develop a heightened sense of responsibility and mission. This sense of responsibility drives employees to take the initiative to undertake tasks that are beyond the scope of their regular duties, such as coming up with innovative solutions, solving complex problems, or initiating responsibility in difficult situations. Empirical research has established that the perceived insider status positively influences employees' innovative behavior. The greater the perceived insider status, the more positive the emotions felt toward the organization, which in turn drives a stronger motivation to take initiative and accept additional responsibilities [44, 47].

From the analysis above, it is evident that RLMX is likely to influence employees' taking charge behavior through its positive impact on their perception of insider identity. When employees compare themselves to their teammates and perceive that their leader trusts them more, they gain valuable insights about the organization from the leader ahead of others. This heightened sense of insider identity and organizational belonging can motivate employees to engage in taking charge behavior and enhance ineffective work methods.Social cognitive theory emphasizes the interconnectedness of environment, cognition, and behavior. Perceived insider status, a significant cognitive variable, serves as a crucial mediator between the environment and behavior.Consequently, the RLMX, an essential factor in corporate interpersonal environments, influences taking charge behavior by shaping the perceived insider status. In other words, perceived insider status mediates the relationship between the relative leader member exchange and taking charge behavior.In summary, this paper proposes the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2 Perceived insider status mediates the relationship between RLMX and taking charge behavior.

Hypothesis 2 Perceived insider status mediates the relationship between RLMX and taking charge behavior.

The moderating role of social comparison orientation and role breadth self-efficacy

The moderating role of social comparison orientation

Differences inevitably lead to comparisons. In social life, individuals frequently compare their abilities, situations, and perspectives with similar others. However, the propensity for social comparison varies among individuals [38], resulting in divergent interpretations of comparison outcomes. Social comparison orientation (SCO) reflects a chronic tendency to evaluate one’s opinions and abilities by comparing oneself with others [38]. As a personality trait, SCO determines how sensitive individuals are to comparison situations and how they interpret the outcomes. It represents a specific psychological state characterized by a strong preference for comparing oneself with others [38]. Individuals with this trait, often experiencing high self-concept uncertainty, tend to rely on others' opinions—especially through social comparisons—when evaluating themselves.

From the perspective of differential allocation of cognitive resources, individuals with high SCO, due to their heightened sensitivity to social hierarchy cues, actively monitor the exchange relationships between leaders and colleagues, interpreting differences in RLMX as signals of status [48] This monitoring behavior makes them more likely to internalize comparison results as a basis for self-evaluation, whereas individuals with low SCO tend to ignore such information [49].

Social comparison is generally categorized as upward, downward, or lateral [50]. Individuals with high SCO are more inclined to engage in comparisons and are more susceptible to their outcomes [26]. When high SCO individuals compare themselves with colleagues who have higher RLMX, this upward comparison amplifies feelings of resource scarcity ("I receive less than others"), triggering a sense of relative deprivation, fostering perceptions of organizational indebtedness, and weakening perceived insider status. Conversely, downward comparisons reinforce awareness of privilege ("I receive more than others"), activating a sense of relative advantage, strengthening perceptions of organizational belonging, and enhancing PIS. In contrast, individuals with low SCO, due to their low motivation for social comparison, even when aware of RLMX differences, diminish the self-relevance of comparison results through cognitive restructuring (e.g., "Differences are temporary") or value disengagement (e.g., "Resource allocation is unrelated to identity"), thereby maintaining stable identity cognition [51]. In summary, this study proposes:

Hypothesis 3 SCO positively moderates the relationship between RLMX and perceived insider status, such that the positive relationship of RLMX on perceived insider status is stronger for employees with high SCO than for those with low SCO.

Hypothesis 3 SCO positively moderates the relationship between RLMX and perceived insider status, such that the positive relationship of RLMX on perceived insider status is stronger for employees with high SCO than for those with low SCO.

The moderating role of role breadth self-efficacy

Self-efficacy refers to an individual's judgment of his or her ability to perform some specific behavior and represents the degree to which an individual perceives his or her self-competence, Bandura argues that self-efficacy is a psychological belief that plays a role in people's efforts to achieve a goal. It influences an individual's emotional thought patterns, behavioral choices, and specific behavioral performance [52, 53]. As an important component of self-concept, self-efficacy is a person's belief in his or her overall ability to overcome difficulties and accomplish challenging behaviors [54]. Individuals with a high sense of self-efficacy have higher expectations in terms of self-perception and appraisal, greater subjective initiative, greater confidence in their ability to accomplish tasks beyond the requirements of their role, and greater ability to cope with potential obstacles. As a result, employees with higher self-efficacy are more likely to implement extra-role behaviors to benefit the organization [10, 54]; on the contrary, people with a low sense of self-efficacy will always avoid and retreat when they encounter problems, and do not believe in their own abilities. Social cognitive theory suggests that efficacy can effectively stimulate an individual's subjective motivation, and that specific self-efficacy is more strongly driven than generalized self-efficacy [55].

Based on this, Paker proposed role breadth self-efficacy (RBSE), which refers to the perceived level of competence and self-confidence of employees in performing a broader set of more dynamic work behaviors in addition to their own technical work, including, but not limited to, improving inefficient organizational processes and solving long-standing problems [28]. RBSE differs from general self-efficacy in that it does not specifically refer to a particular competency, but rather emphasizes the broader role competencies possessed by the employees themselves, which allows employees to break free from the shackles of their roles and to mobilize their own initiative to perform out-of-role work tasks. In addition, RBSE, like perceived insider status, is a subjective judgment of the employee and is not necessarily a reflection of true competence [30].

However, under the same level of perceived insider status, employees with different degrees of RBSE also differ when they implement charge-taking behavior. First, RBSE strengthens the subjective agency of employees by fostering a belief in their ability to perform a wide range of dynamic work behaviors. This belief empowers employees to perceive themselves as capable of influencing organizational outcomes beyond their formal role boundaries. When employees experience PIS, they are emotionally motivated to engage in proactive behavior due to their sense of belonging and responsibility. However, this emotional motivation alone may not suffice to drive actual proactive behavior unless employees also believe they possess the necessary capabilities to act. RBSE bridges this gap by providing employees with the confidence to translate their emotional motivation into concrete actions. Second, RBSE enhances employees' ability to accurately assess their own capabilities. Employees with high RBSE are more likely to recognize their potential to perform challenging tasks and to perceive opportunities for proactive behavior as feasible. This capability assessment is critical because it reduces the psychological barrier of self-doubt that might otherwise inhibit employees from taking initiative. In contrast, employees with low RBSE may lack the confidence to act, even when they feel emotionally motivated by PIS. This lack of confidence can lead to inaction or reluctance to take on multiple roles, thereby weakening the positive effect of PIS on proactive behavior.

Proactive behavior requires both the motivation to act and the confidence to execute. While PIS provides the emotional motivation, RBSE supplies the psychological resource (i.e., self-confidence in broader role competencies) necessary to transform motivation into action [56]. This dual mechanism explains why RBSE moderates the relationship between PIS and proactive behavior. Specifically, when employees perceive high RBSE, they are more likely to view their PIS as a call to action, leading to higher levels of proactive behavior. Conversely, when RBSE is low, the emotional motivation from PIS is insufficient to overcome self-doubt, resulting in weaker proactive behavior.

Therefore, employees with high RBSE believe that they have the ability to perform their own work or even other challenging work, and are more likely to transform the emotional motivation brought by PIS into practical action. In this case, PIS more strongly promotes taking charge behavior. On the contrary, employees with low RBSE are discouraged from taking charge behavior due to a lack of self-confidence in their own ability, are unwilling to take on multiple work roles, and are less likely to act proactively. As a result, the positive effect of PIS on taking charge behavior is weaker in this scenario. In summary, the following hypotheses are proposed:

Hypothesis 4 RBSE positively moderates the relationship between perceived insider status and employee taking charge behavior, such that the positive relationship of perceived insider status and taking charge behavior is stronger for employees with high RBSE.

Hypothesis 4 RBSE positively moderates the relationship between perceived insider status and employee taking charge behavior, such that the positive relationship of perceived insider status and taking charge behavior is stronger for employees with high RBSE.

Methods

Participants and procedure

We collected data through a questionnaire survey method administered to employees in Chinese enterprise. The targeted participants were employees from multiple industries, including manufacturing, services, and technology sectors, to ensure a diverse representation of workplace contexts. Respondents were selected based on the following criteria: (1) employee in the team must consist of at least three members, (2) team members must have worked together for at least six months to ensure established interpersonal dynamics. To ensure the quality of the data, a pilot survey was conducted with 20 participants from three teams in similar industries. Feedback from the pilot survey was used to refine the questionnaire content, including clarifying ambiguous questions and adjusting response formats to reduce respondent burden. Additionally, to mitigate common method bias, we implemented several procedural safeguards. First, the taking charge behavior was evaluated by the employee's team leader and the rest of the variables were evaluated by the employees themselves. Second, participants were assured of anonymity and informed that their responses would not affect their performance evaluations, reducing potential social desirability bias.

The final sample was collected using a stratified random sampling approach, which ensured proportional representation of teams across different industries and organizational sizes. This method was chosen for its suitability in capturing diverse workplace contexts while maintaining statistical rigor. Ultimately, 450 questionnaires were distributed, and after discarding invalid responses such as those with regular patterns and incomplete answers, 381 valid questionnaires were retrieved,yielding an 84.67% response rate.

In the survey sample, 36.75% were female and 63.25% were male. In terms of age distribution, 33.86% were under 25 years old, 42.78% were between 26 and 40 years old, and 23.36% were over 40 years old. Regarding educational background, 9.97% had a high school education or less, 30.97% had an associate degree, 43.04% held a bachelor's degree, and 16.01% possessed a master's degree. With regard to the number of years of experience, 25.98% had worked for one year or less, 35.7% for 1–3 years, 17.06% for 3–5 years and 21.26% for more than 5 years. In terms of enterprise type, state-owned or state-controlled enterprises represented 15.22%, collective enterprises 23.88%, private enterprises 19.42%, foreign-funded enterprises 19.95%, institutions 11.81%, and other types of organizations accounted for 9.71%.

Measures

The measurement instruments for all variables in this study were adapted from established scales. Following the standard procedure of translation and back-translation, the questionnaires were professionally translated into Chinese. Discrepancies between the translated versions and the originals were carefully reviewed, leading to modifications in the phrasing of certain questions to better align with the research context. All items were measured on a five point Likert-type scale where 1= strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree.

Relative leader-member exchange

We used the method outlined by Henderson et al. [37], where RLMX is calculated as each employee's individual LMX score minus the average LMX (ALMX) of the employee's team. The LMX-7 questionnaire was utilized for the relevant measurements which has been repeatedly employed since its inception and has demonstrated applicability within the Chinese context [24]. An example item was: "How well does your leader recognize your potential?" The Cronbach's alpha for this scale in the current study was 0.836.

Perceived insider status

PIS was measured using the six-item scale adopted from Stamper and Masterson [43]. An example item was: "I feel I am an ‘insider’ in my work organization." The Cronbach's alpha for this scale in the current study was 0.873.

Social comparison orientation

SCO was measured using eleven items adopted from Gibbons and Buunk [38]. An example item was: "I am not the type of person who compares often with others (reversed)," The Cronbach's alpha for this scale in the current study was 0.908.

Role breadth self-efficacy

RBSE was measured with the 7-item version adopted by Parker [57], a sample item was: "How confident would you feel when visiting people from other departments to suggest doing things differently." The Cronbach's alpha for this scale in the current study was 0.897.

Taking charge behavior

TCB was measured using the ten-item scale developed by Morrison [12]. An example item was: "This person often tries to adopt improved procedures for doing his or her job," The Cronbach's alpha for this scale in the current study was 0.912.

Controls

Sex, age, education, and tenure were included as controls because each has been linked—both theoretically and empirically—to employees’ job behavior [58–61]. Specifically, older employees and those with longer tenure possess greater task-relevant experience and social capital, whereas higher education signals deeper human-capital resources; together, these attributes may enhance self-efficacy and proactive behaviors. Results

Common method bias test

To address potential common method bias stemming from the collection of variables from the same respondents, we conducted the Harman's single-factor test using SPSS 26.0. This statistical procedure aids in assessing the extent of common method bias in the data. The principal component analysis (PCA) revealed the presence of six factors, which collectively accounted for 60.52% of the total variance. Significantly, the first principal component explained 30.673% of the variance, which is less than the recommended minimum standard (40%) [62]. This study employed the common latent factor approach by adding a latent factor to the original five-factor model. We compared the original model with a modified six-factor model incorporating the common latent factor to assess whether the model fit was significantly improved [63]. The results indicated that even after adding the common latent factor, the six-factor model’s fit indices (χ2/df = 2.496, CFI = 0.897, IFI = 0.898, TLI = 0.878, RMSEA = 0.085) remained poorer than those of the original five-factor structural model. Combined with the results of Harman’s single-factor test and the common latent factor analysis, it indicated no significant common method bias issue in this study.

Descriptive statistics and correlation analysis

Table 2 presents means, standard deviations, correlations for all variables. The RLMX was significantly positively related to PIS (r = 0.408,p < 0.01) and TCB (r = 0.433,p < 0.01). Hypothesis tests

Prior to conducting the regression analysis, a multicollinearity test was first carried out, revealing that the VIF values of each model in this study were all below 1.5, and the tolerance levels were above 0.5. Thus, indicating the absence of significant multicollinearity issues. All variables were centered as a standard procedure for moderation analysis. As displayed in Table 3, the RLMX exerted a substantial influence on employees' taking charge behavior even after adjusting for demographic variables (β = 0.449, p < 0.001, Model 6), affirming the RLMX was significantly positively related to taking charge behavior. Therefore, Hypothesis 1 was supported.

Conclusion and discussion

Guided by social cognitive theory, this study examined how RLMX relates to employees’ taking charge behavior from both self-perception and social comparison perspectives. The analyses supported all hypothesized relationships: RLMX was positively related to taking charge; RLMX was also positively related to PIS; PIS mediated the association between RLMX and taking charge; SCO moderated the link between RLMX and PIS; and RBSE moderated the relationship between PIS and taking charge.

Theoretical implications

First, this study tests the positive relationship between RLMX and taking charge behavior in Chinese context, addressing a gap in the existing literature regarding the antecedents of taking charge behavior. Previous studies have focused on the positive effects of both individual factors (e.g.,proactive personality [64], individual status [65], leader-member exchange relationships [10]) and leader factors (inclusive leadership [14], humble leadership [15], shared leadership [16] and empowering leadership [17]), but ignored the possibility that employees would compare their relationship with their leaders to other colleagues. Furthermore, due to the distinctiveness of China's local culture, the phenomenon of “involution” of corporate employees is serious, in which social comparison has become normative in employees' daily work [66]. In such a cultural context, employees' perceptions of their position in the organization and resource allocation are particularly sensitive, making RLMX more explanatory than LMX. The positive association between RLMX and taking charge behavior confirms that relative relational status, rather than absolute LMX quality, more strongly drives proactive behaviors in such settings. This finding expands LMX theory by highlighting that “being better off than others” in leader relationships matters as much as “being well-treated”, especially in high power distance cultures where employees are sensitive to hierarchical distinctions. Thus, our work enriches LMX theory by establishing RLMX as a culturally relevant extension that enhances its predictive power in collective-oriented societies.

Second, this study revealed the mediating effect of perceived insider status between RLMX and taking charge behaviors from a self-perception perspective. Previous scholars' studies on the mediating effect on the formation of taking charge behavior have mostly focused on employee cognition [67], motivation [68], work status [14], and relationship status [69]. Using perceived insider status as a mediating variable, this study continues Dyvik et al.'s (2016) and Yang et al.'s (2019) exploration of the role of “employee cognition” in the formation of taking charge behavior, and further expands the understanding of the mechanism of the formation of taking charge behavior [70, 71]. Moreover, Guided by social cognitive theory, which posits reciprocal interactions between environment, cognition, and behavior, our study advances this framework by identifying PIS as a key cognitive mediator that bridges RLMX (environmental factor) and taking charge behavior (outcome). Social cognitive theory emphasizes that environmental stimuli shape internal cognitions, which in turn drive behavioral responses; our findings specify how this process unfolds in organizational settings. PIS, defined as employees’ sense of being “insiders” in the organization, serves as a critical cognitive link by transforming relational signals (RLMX) into psychological ownership. This study extends social cognitive theory by demonstrating that relational quality (RLMX) should first be internalized as a sense of belonging (PIS) to motivate proactive actions—a mechanism previously underexplored in studies on proactive behaviors.

Finally, this study validates the moderating role of SCO and RBSE providing theoretical evidence that clarifies the boundary conditions for the formation of insider identity perceptions and the emergence of taking charge behavior. In the Chinese cultural context, although employees tend to be more concerned with social comparison, individual differences still exist. Previous studies have found that employees with high SCO are more sensitive to comparison outcomes. This explains why employees who perceive themselves as less close to their leaders than others exhibit different reactions: some are eager to determine their position within the organization and classify themselves as insiders or outsiders, while others remain indifferent and focus solely on their tasks. In Chinese culture, personal relationships and the concept of face are highly valued. Individuals with high SCO perceive good RLMX as an advantageous position within the organizational network. To maintain this advantage and foster a positive relationship with their leaders, they tend to be more proactive in their behavior, aligning with Chinese cultural expectations of harmonious interpersonal relationships and reciprocity. Social cognitive theory suggests that a sense of efficacy can stimulate individuals’ subjective initiative. At the same level of insider identity perception, employees with high RBSE have greater confidence in their ability to perform out-of-role work behaviors and are more likely to transform the emotional motivation derived from insider identity perception into practical actions, thereby taking active responsibility. This indicates that RBSE can enhance the positive effect of PIS on taking charge behavior. Conversely, employees with low RBSE lack confidence in their abilities; although they believe they should engage in taking charge behaviors, they are unable to do so, thus weakening the positive effect of PIS on taking charge behaviors.

In conclusion, this study explored the integration of environmental factor (RLMX), cognitive factor (PIS), individual differences (SCO and RBSE), and taking charge behavior into a model based on social cognitive theory. That is to say, PIS influenced by environmental factor and SCO lays the emotional foundation for employees to be willing and actively engaged in taking charge behavior. RBSE further deepens behavioral motivation from the perspective of "being able to do," thereby promoting the occurrence of taking charge behavior [5, 27]. This provides a new theoretical framework for research in this field.

Implications for practice

The findings of this study also have practical implications. First, the taking charge behavior taken by staff should not be overlooked in contemporary society. It can engender not only the smooth operation of the enterprise but also rejuvenate its vitality and foster long-term development. In order to encourage the initiative of employees, managers need not only to pay attention to the quality of the relationship between superiors and subordinates, but also to pay attention to the relative leader- member exchange. Managers should consciously invest additional attention and communication with employees, enabling them to feel greater trust, support and psychological safety from their superiors, thereby fostering proactive behavior. For example, managers can prioritize building high-quality relationships with employees through regular one-on-one meetings, mentorship programs, and personalized feedback sessions. Additionally, managers can tailor support to employees' individual characteristics and needs, helping them feel more valued and enhancing their RLMX, which in turn promotes their taking charge behavior. Examples include providing mentorship support for new employees and career development planning for high-potential staff.

The second practical implication is that when employees perceive themselves as insiders, they are more likely to invest additional time and energy into the organization by engaging in activities beyond their formal job roles that contribute to the organization's health and development. Managers can foster an open and inclusive organizational culture that makes employees feel like integral members of the organization. For example, regular team-building activities or cultural events can enhance employees' sense of belonging. Additionally, managers can strengthen employees' perception of insider status by promoting information sharing, involving employees in decision-making processes, granting them sufficient autonomy and resources for key tasks, and promptly recognizing and rewarding their contributions. Examples include establishing employee feedback mechanisms such as anonymous surveys, suggestion boxes, or regular employee forums to allow employees to express their thoughts and suggestions; assigning employees to lead specific innovation projects or improvement initiatives; and implementing recognition programs such as “Monthly Stars” or “Innovation Awards” to honor outstanding employees. These practices can enhance employees' feelings of value and belonging, thereby promoting their taking charge behavior.

The third practical implication is that the negative consequences of social comparison often surpass the positive ones, prompting managers to guide such comparisons constructively to minimize adverse effects. Training and learning initiatives can help employees recognize the inevitability of differences as opportunities for growth, encouraging proactive communication with supervisors for guidance. Team building activities and psychological counseling can also be used to help employees establish a fair psychology, clarify their position and build harmonious human relations [72]. Managers should also assist employees in establishing a fair mindset, clarify their roles, build harmonious interpersonal relationships, and cultivate a supportive and cooperative organizational culture.

Finally, managers should value the RBSE of employees in their management practices, as it addresses the issue of "being able to do." RBSE is not only an individual trait but can also be acquired through subsequent training and learning. Therefore, managers can select candidates who are confident, sociable, highly resilient, and proactive during the recruitment process. Additionally, they can enhance employees' perception of RBSE and foster proactive responsibility through targeted training and encouragement. For example, action learning programs enable employees to strengthen their self-efficacy by solving practical problems [73], while job rotation programs allow employees to gain experience in various roles, thereby boosting their self-confidence. Through these measures, managers can more effectively motivate employees to take initiative and assume responsibility for their actions, ultimately improving the organization's overall performance and competitiveness.

Limitations and future research directions

While this study offers actionable insights into fostering employees' taking charge behavior, it is not without its limitations and areas for improvement. First, although Harman's single-factor test and the common latent factor approach indicated no significant common method bias, most variables—except for taking charge behavior, which was evaluated by leaders—were self-reported by employees, potentially introducing subjectivity. Future research could benefit from adopting a multi-source assessment approach (e.g., leader and peer evaluations) to enhance data comprehensiveness and reliability. Second, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Collecting all data at a single time point may amplify the observed relationships between variables and introduce measurement bias. Future studies could employ longitudinal or experimental designs to establish directionality and causality. Finally, this study focuses on individual-level factors, such as personal perceptions and traits, without considering organizational-level influences. Subsequent studies could conduct cross-level analyses to provide a more holistic understanding of the mechanisms underlying RLMX and taking charge behavior.
