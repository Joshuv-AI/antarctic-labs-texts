Gene Expression Dynamics and Network Analysis Reveal Key Molecular Pathways in Spermatogenic Progression from Spermatogonial Stem Cells to Round Spermatids
Authors: Ali Qorbanee, Hossein Azizi, Marwa Fadhil Alsaffar, Thomas Skutella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Spermatogenesis is a tightly regulated process that involves an orchestrated transcriptional and translational event across the seminiferous epithelium. While individual regulators of this process have been studied, the network-level dynamics underlying the transition from spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) to round spermatids (RSs) remain poorly understood. The dynamic molecular transitions between SSCs and RSs have not been fully characterised, particularly at the network level. Therefore, this study aimed to characterise the molecular and network-level dynamics underlying the transition from SSCs to RSs by integrating transcriptomic profiling, protein–protein interaction network analysis, functional enrichment, and immunohistochemical validation.Materials and Methods:In this experimental study, we evaluated the gene expression dynamics from SSCs to RSs. We analysed publicly available microarray datasets (GEO) comprising four biological replicates for RSs and three biological replicates for SSCs. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and mapped onto protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks, followed by prioritisation of central regulators. Functional enrichment analysis was performed to characterise biological pathways. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for SOX9, n-MYC, VASA, SOX2, and DAZL was conducted to examine spatial and developmental protein expressions across the seminiferous tubules.Results:A set of 3598 DEGs was identified between SSCs and RSs. This analysis highlighted central genes, Actb, Kras, Pten, Jun, Cdc42, and Gart, that act as central nodes in spermatogenic networks. These genes were associated with pathways that govern cell cycle regulation, chromatin organisation, and signal transduction, consistent with their established roles in germline development. Protein-level localisation confirmed stage-specific expression patterns within the seminiferous tubules. SOX9 localised to Sertoli cells, n-MYC and VASA were activated in differentiating germ cells, DAZL was restricted to early progenitors, and SOX2 appeared only in late-stage subsets.Conclusion:This study used transcriptomic profiling with network analysis and molecular experiments to reveal novel regulatory connections that underlie the SSC-RS transition. These findings provide a systems-level framework for understanding male germline development and may facilitate future research on infertility and reproductive health.

Introduction

Spermatogenesis is the fundamental process by which spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) differentiate into mature spermatozoa. It unfolds in the seminiferous tubules through a series of coordinated transitions, including SSC self-renewal, mitotic expansion, meiosis, and post-meiotic remodelling of round spermatids (RSs) into elongated spermatozoa (1). Each stage is tightly regulated by transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and epigenetic mechanisms to ensure genomic stability and the successful transmission of genetic material.

High-throughput transcriptomic studies have identified numerous genes required for distinct spermatogenic events. Previous studies have identified key genes and

pathways involved in SSC self-renewal, differentiation, and meiosis, including those related to DNA replication, chromatin remodelling, and RNA processing (2, 3). For instance, genes such as Plzf and Id4 are critical for SSC maintenance (4, 5), while Dmc1 and Spo11 regulate meiotic recombination (6, 7). Although previous studies have examined individual stages or subsets of genes, systematic comparisons between the stem cell state and postmeiotic RSs are also needed. Such endpoint comparisons are critical to reveal large-scale regulatory shifts across spermatogenesis. While transcriptomic data enable insights into mRNA levels to complement transcriptomic findings, we incorporate qualitative protein-level observations via immunohistochemistry (IHC) that provide context for molecular changes across tubule stages.

This study aims to systematically investigate gene expression dynamics between SSCs and RSs to address gaps in understanding the molecular networks that underlie the SSC-RS transition. Unlike many previous studies that focused on individual genes or specific stages, we adopt a systems-level perspective by integrating transcriptomic profiling with network-based and protein-level analyses. This approach allows us to identify key differentially expressed genes (DEGs), explore their interactions, and highlight central regulatory hubs that may coordinate germ cell differentiation. By combining network and enrichment analyses with immunohistochemical experiments, we aim to provide a more comprehensive view of the molecular processes that drive the SSC-RS transition and contribute to a better understanding of spermatogenic regulation.

This study aims to systematically characterize the transcriptional and regulatory changes underlying the transition from SSCs to RSs by integrating transcriptomic profiling, network analysis, pathway enrichment, and immunohistochemical validation. Through this integrative approach, we seek to identify key molecular regulators and regulatory networks involved in spermatogenic progression and provide insights into the mechanisms governing male germline development.

Materials and Methods

Microarray data analysis and normalisation

This was an experimental in silico study that utilised publicly available gene expression data. We used dataset GSE23119, containing nine samples, from the NCBI GEO database for microarray analysis. Three spermatogonia samples (GSM573579, GSM573580, and GSM573581) were selected from this dataset. Additionally, four RS samples (GSM820582, GSM820583, GSM820584, and GSM820585) were obtained from dataset GSE33132 (eight total samples). The analysis was performed on the GPL1261 platform. Expression data were processed using Transcriptome Analysis Console software (version 4.0, United States, Affymetrix). The robust multi-array average (RMA) normalisation method and ANOVA method were applied. Genes with an FDR P<0.05 and an absolute log fold change (|LogFC|) ≥ 2 were classified as significantly DEGs (4).

Construction of protein-protein interaction networks and modularity analysis

The STRING app (v.12.0), integrated as a plugin in Cytoscape (v.3.6.0), was employed to predict protein-protein interactions (PPI) among the identified DEGs. The analysis was conducted for Mus musculus using a medium confidence score of 0.400 (4).

Enrichment analysis

Enrichment analysis was performed using the Shiny -GO enrichment analysis tool (https://bioinformatics.sd-state.edu/go/) to further explore the filtered DEGs in the network. Enrichment data were retrieved from KEGG, WikiPathways, Reactome databases, and Gene Ontology (GO), with a false discovery rate (FDR) p-adj cutoff of <0.05 and the Mus musculus species.

Testis digestion and culture of testicular cells

Testicular cells were obtained from C57BL/6 mice, utilising GFP transgenic mice with an Oct4-promoter reporter. The testes were decapsulated and subjected to a single-step enzymatic digestion. Procedures for digestion, suspension, and plating followed protocols from our prior studies. Cells were cultured in mouse GSC medium at 37°C with 5% CO2, as detailed in our earlier work (8).

Tissue processing and immunostaining steps

To complement transcriptomic analyses with protein-level observations, we performed immunohistochemical staining on mouse testicular tissue and cultured germ cells. The goal was to assess spatial localisation and relative protein expression patterns of selected candidate genes identified in transcriptomic analyses. These qualitative observations provide supportive evidence for transcriptional changes and contextualise molecular dynamics within the seminiferous tubule microenvironment. Testicular tissues were carefully collected from male mice, washed with PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde to maintain cell structure. Tissue processing, embedding, sectioning, and staining adhered to established methods from our previous research. Frozen sections were air-dried for 30 minutes at room temperature to ensure proper adhesion. For immunostaining, cultured cells in 24-well plates were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, washed with PBS, and permeabilised with 0.1% Triton in PBS. Non-specific binding was reduced by blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After removing the blocking solution, cells were incubated overnight with primary antibodies for optimal binding. Primary antibodies used were VASA (DDX4, goat polyclonal, R&D Systems, AF2030, 1:100), n-MYC (mouse monoclonal, Abcam, ab16898, 1:50), SOX2 (rabbit polyclonal, Abcam, ab15830, 1:100), SOX9 (rabbit polyclonal, Linaris, PAK0058, 1:50), and DAZL (mouse monoclonal IgG1, AbD Serotec, MCA2336, 1:100). Omission of each primary antibody in the sample was used as the negative control for all markers. Following additional washes, cells were incubated with fluorochromeconjugated secondary antibody for improved visualisation. Cells were then counterstained with 0.2 μg/ml DAPI for three minutes at room temperature and mounted with Mowiol 4-88. Labelled cells were examined using a Zeiss LSM 700 confocal microscope, with high-resolution images captured via a Zeiss LSM-TPMT camera for detailed structural analysis (8).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The Ethics Committee of Amol University of Special Modern Technologies, Amol, Iran approved the animal study protocol (IR.ASMT.REC.1403.006).

Results

Microarray analysis revealed 3598 genes with significant dynamic changes

We performed microarray analysis to investigate gene -level expression changes between SSCs and RSs within the murine seminiferous tubules. The RMA method was applied for normalisation (Fig .1A). DEGs were identified based on an FDR P<0.05 and an absolute log fold change (|LogFC|) ≥ 2. Following filtration, a total of 3598 (7.99%) DEGs were detected with significant expression differences between the two groups (Fig .1B). Of these, 1995 (55.45%) were downregulated, with Prm1, Galntl6, Hk1, and Uqcr10 among the most highly expressed. Conversely, 1603 (44.55%) genes were upregulated, with Dr1, Vamp3, Vnn1, and Grtp1 showing the lowest LogFC values in SSCs compared to RSs (Fig.1C, D, Supplementary File 1, See Supplementary Online Information at www.ijfs.ir). The observed gene expression profile indicates a distinct regulatory shift between SSCs and RSs, suggestive of the activation and suppression of specific biological pathways during spermatogenesis. This large-scale shift in gene expression reflects the fundamental reprogramming of SSCs, which must exit a stem-like state, repress self-renewal genes, and activate differentiation-specific programs to form mature spermatids.

Protein-protein interaction network analysis underscores the most critical genes

We identified 3598 DEGs based on an FDR-adjusted P<0.05 and a LogFC range from <–2 to >+2. These genes were input into the STRING app within Cytoscape. The initial network generated comprised 2604 nodes and 20 217 edges, representing a broad interaction landscape. However, this network exhibited considerable dispersion, likely incorporating many genes with marginal or indirect roles in the biological processes under investigation.

To refine the network and focus on the most impactful genes, we applied centrality measures -specifically degree, betweenness and closeness centrality, to prioritise nodes by their potential biological importance. Nodes located within the top 10th percentile (90th percentile cutoff) for all three-centrality metrics were retained. This focused filtration resulted in a reduced network of 166 nodes and 2091 edges (Fig .2, Supplementary File 2, See Supplementary Online Information at www.ijfs.ir). Within this refined network, several nodes emerged as highly central. Notably, ACTB, KRAS, PTEN, JUN, CCND1, SIRT1, CALM3, HSPA4, and TARDBP were identified as major hubs (Supplementary File 3, See Supplementary Online Information at www.ijfs.ir). The centrality of these nodes suggests that they have considerable influence over network integrity and function, likely playing critical roles in the underlying biological processes of cell cycle regulation, signal transduction, and cellular stress responses. These hub genes do not function in isolation. Their regulatory influence depends on extensive interactions with additional proteins, including those with lower centrality values in the PPI network. This emphasises that spermatogenesis is driven not only by highly connected hubs but also by cooperative interactions with less central nodes, which may contribute stage-specific or context-dependent functions. Such findings open the possibility that non-hub genes, often overlooked in network studies, could play critical yet more specialised roles in germ cell differentiation.

Enrichment and biological pathway analysis

Enrichment analysis of the filtered DEGs revealed several functional themes across the GO biological process, GO molecular function, KEGG, Reactome, and WikiPathways, which are pertinent to spermatogenic progression.

In the GO Biological Process category, terms such as macromolecule modification and protein modification process indicate that post-translational regulatory events are significant in the transition from undifferentiated SSCs to RSs. The enrichment in processes like positive regulation of nitrogen compound metabolic processes and cell cycle regulation suggests that the DEGs likely play roles in controlling metabolic and proliferative events. The involvement of catabolic processes and specific regulation of the cell cycle reinforces the idea that timely protein turnover and cell division checkpoints are essential during this differentiation phase (Fig .3A). The GO Molecular Function results indicate an overrepresentation of binding activities, including nucleotide binding, RNA binding, and specific interactions such as protein kinase binding and histone deacetylase binding (Fig .3B). These findings suggest that the DEGs are functionally equipped to modulate enzyme activity and chromatin structure-key processes that could affect both transcriptional regulation and signal transduction in differentiating germ cells.

The Reactome database further emphasises the critical nature of cell cycle regulation, with enriched pathways in the M phase, mitotic checkpoints, and overall cell cycle dynamics. Moreover, the presence of RNA metabolism and post-translational modification pathways underscores the integrated control of gene expression at multiple levels, from transcriptional regulation to protein processing. These findings align with the need for tight regulation as cells transition from a stem-like state to more specialised RSs (Fig .3C).

In the KEGG pathway analysis, the enrichment of pathways such as ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis highlights the significance of protein degradation and quality control mechanisms during spermatogenic differentiation. Additionally, the presence of pathways related to cellular senescence and oocyte meiosis may reflect shared molecular machinery or pleiotropic roles of these regulators in cell cycle control and metabolic homeostasis. There are some pathways that might seem unexpected in the context of spermatogenesis (Fig .3D).

Finally, enrichment in WikiPathways such as MAPK signalling and Delta-Notch signalling points to the importance of intercellular signalling mechanisms that integrate external cues with internal developmental programs. The identification of pathways linked to focal adhesion and EGFR1 signalling also suggests potential roles in modulating cell adhesion and migration within the seminiferous tubules, which could be important for the spatial reorganisation of cells during differentiation (Fig .3E).

Overall, the enrichment analysis paints a picture of a highly regulated network where modifications at the protein and RNA levels, coupled with robust cell cycle control and integration of extracellular signals, form the backbone of the transition from undifferentiated SSCs to RSs. These results provide a functional context for the filtered DEGs and support their involvement in key regulatory processes essential for spermatogenesis.

Immunohistochemistry reveals dynamic protein expression patterns of key germline regulators across the seminiferous tubules

To further examine the spatial and developmental dynamics of spermatogenesis, immunohistochemical double staining was performed for SOX9, n-MYC, VASA, SOX2, and DAZL across the seminiferous tubules. This analysis provided insight into the stage-specific and region-specific expressions of these markers at the protein level.

SOX9 was strongly expressed in cells adjacent to the basal membrane of the seminiferous tubules, in cells which were predicted to be Sertoli cells. These SOX9 -positive cells did not co-express n-MYC. As progression through spermatogenesis continued toward the luminal compartment, a clear increase in n-MYC expression was observed in SOX9-negative cells. In the luminal region, only n-MYC-positive cells were detected, which indicated that n-MYC becomes active at later stages of germ cell differentiation, especially in the middle regions. This expression pattern gradually diminishes closer to the luminal region. A similar but distinct pattern was observed for VASA. No VASA expression was detected in basal SOX9-positive cells. As cells progressed toward the middle of the tubule, VASA expression increased markedly, indicating activation during early-to-mid germ cell differentiation. However, VASA expression declined again near the luminal side. Not all VASA-positive cells were n-MYC -positive, but those nearest to the lumen frequently coexpressed both markers, suggesting a subset of more differentiated germ cells. SOX2 showed limited expression across the tubule, with almost no or low signal except in a small population of cells very close to the luminal surface. These SOX2-positive cells also co-expressed n-MYC, hinting at a role for SOX2 in a late differentiation phase or a specialised subset of germ cells. DAZL protein was absent in SOX9-positive cells but detected in a different subset of cells near the basal compartment, possibly representing undifferentiating and differentiating progenitors. Interestingly, DAZL expression diminished entirely toward the lumen, which suggested that its function is restricted to an early-to-intermediate window of germ cell development (Fig .4).

Taken together, these spatial expression patterns highlight the tightly regulated, stage-specific expression of germline regulators throughout spermatogenesis. The non-overlapping and sequential appearance of these markers reinforces their distinct roles during the transition from undifferentiated SSCs to more mature germ cells and suggests a layered regulatory program within the seminiferous epithelium.

Discussion

Our integrative analysis provides insights into the molecular dynamics that underlie the transition from SSCs to RSs. While studies have identified individual regulators of spermatogenesis, our work tries to highlight how these regulators operate within broader transcriptional and PPI networks. This systems-level approach emphasises not only the presence and involvement of prior well-established genes but also shows their involvement in pathways and their connected, interacting genes in the PPI network, which collectively govern germline differentiation.

The dataset used to represent the SSC stage (GSE23119) comprises isolated spermatogonia, which include both true SSCs and committed progenitor cells. SSCs are a small subset of spermatogonia, so the identified DEGs and pathways may reflect broader spermatogonial biology rather than SSC-specific dynamics. Nevertheless, the dataset represents highly enriched spermatogonial fractions (>90% purity), and SSC transcriptomic signals are embedded within this population. Therefore, while some progenitor-specific expressions may provide a contribution, our analysis of DEGs, PPI networks, and pathway enrichment still provides meaningful insights into early germline differentiation and regulatory networks relevant to SSC function. Our network analysis shows the identified hub genes with interconnected roles within a unified framework. The hub genes identified in our refined PPI network, Actb, Kras, Pten, Jun, Cdc42, and Gart, were not unexpected, as many are known regulators of spermatogenesis. Their emergence as network centralities, however, underscores their potential role as integrators of multiple regulatory axes through spermatogenesis. Prm1, encoding protamine 1, stands out as an upregulated gene in RSs, which is critical for chromatin condensation during sperm maturation. This finding aligns with prior studies that demonstrated the activation of sperm-specific genes during the haploid phase of spermatogenesis (9, 10). The PPI network analysis, refined through centrality measures, revealed a highly interconnected subset of 166 genes, with hubs like Actb and Pten playing pivotal roles. The high centrality of β-actin (Actb), for example, points toward a foundational role for cytoskeletal remodelling. It implies that a transcriptional groundwork for intercellular bridge stability, organelle transport, cellular morphology, and motility is actively laid down as cells exit meiosis (11). Pten, a negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT pathway, is implicated in SSC self-renewal and differentiation, consistent with its role in balancing proliferation and apoptosis (12, 13). The interaction between Pten and Cdc42 imply a regulatory axis integrating lipid signalling with cytoskeletal dynamics, which could explain how germ cells navigate the seminiferous epithelium during migration and reorganisation. Additionally, the co-centrality of the proto-oncogene Kras and the tumour suppressor Pten highlights a critical signalling axis. Rather than acting independently, their positions within the network suggest a finely tuned balance between the PI3K/ AKT survival pathway and the RAS/MAPK proliferation pathway. This signalling nexus may act as a key checkpoint, integrating extracellular cues to guide differentiating spermatogonia through meiosis while suppressing reversion to a self-renewing state or induction of apoptosis. The prominence of Kras and Cdc42 in our network suggests their involvement in signalling cascades, such as MAPK and Rho GTPase pathways, which are crucial for cell migration and intercellular communication (14). The identification of Jun, a component of the AP-1 transcription factor complex, may point to its role in transcriptional regulation during meiosis and spermiogenesis (15). Gart, involved in purine biosynthesis (16), emerges as a less-explored node, possibly linking metabolic flux to nucleotide availability for rapid transcriptional bursts during meiosis, warranting further mechanistic dissection. These hub genes collectively highlight the intricate interplay of cytoskeletal, signalling, and transcriptional networks in spermatogenesis.

Functional enrichment analysis using ShinyGO further elucidated the biological significance of the filtered DEGs. The enrichment of pathways such as ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, cell cycle regulation, and histone deacetylase binding underscores the importance of protein turnover, cell division checkpoints, and epigenetic regulation in the SSC-to-RS transition. Ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis is critical for degrading misfolded proteins and regulating protein levels during spermatogenesis, ensuring cellular homeostasis (17). The enrichment of cell cycle-related pathways aligns with the need for precise regulation of mitotic and meiotic divisions during germ cell development (18, 19). Notably, the identification of histone deacetylase binding suggests epigenetic modifications, such as histone acetylation/deacetylation, play a role in modulating gene expression during differentiation, consistent with previous reports on chromatin remodelling in spermatogenesis (20, 21). Additionally, the enrichment of MAPK and Delta-Notch signalling pathways points to the integration of extracellular cues with intracellular developmental programs, facilitating coordinated differentiation within the seminiferous tubules.

While immunohistochemical experiments did not directly validate the protein products of our hub genes, they provided spatial and stage-specific insights into protein expression, complementing our transcriptomic findings. The distinct expression patterns of SOX9, n-MYC, VASA, SOX2, and DAZL across the seminiferous tubules highlight the tightly regulated, stage-specific roles of these proteins. For instance, the dynamic changes in expression of n-MYC across the seminiferous tubules and its upregulation in cells near the luminal compartment suggest a role in later stages of differentiation (22). Similarly, the transient expression of VASA during early-to-mid differentiation and its decline in luminal cells align with its known role in germ cell development. The limited expression of SOX2 in luminal cells co-expressing n-MYC suggests a specialised role in late-stage germ cells, possibly in regulating spermiogenesis. These findings reinforce the layered regulatory program governing spermatogenesis and demonstrate corresponding protein-level dynamics.

The identification of central regulatory hubs, together with their interconnections within the PPI network, provides potential molecular targets for fertility preservation and treatment of male infertility. By highlighting not only the hubs themselves but also their interactions with other genes and pathways, this systems-level framework may inform the development of diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring or enhancing spermatogenesis. Although this study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our reliance on publicly available microarray datasets, although robust and widely used, may not fully capture the complexity of gene expression compared with newer approaches such as single-cell RNA sequencing, which offers higher resolution, isoform-level detection, and cell-type specificity. Bulk microarray analysis also inherently masks cellular heterogeneity within SSC and RS populations, potentially overlooking rare or transiently expressed yet biologically significant transcripts. Second, the PPI network analysis, while useful for identifying central hub genes, may bias against low-abundance or dynamically regulated genes that nevertheless play critical roles in spermatogenesis. Third, the use of GEO-derived datasets introduces potential heterogeneity, as differences in sample isolation protocols, mouse strains, and experimental conditions across studies could confound DEG identification and interpretation. The relatively small sample size (three biological replicates for SSCs and four for RSs) further limits statistical power and reduces confidence in the detection of subtle expression changes, particularly in low-abundance transcripts.

Conclusion

This study reframes the understanding of the SSC-to -RS transition from a list of individual genetic players to an integrated, network-driven model. Our analysis demonstrates that a core set of highly connected hub genes, including Actb, Kras, and Pten, likely orchestrates the complex interplay between cell cycle machinery, signal transduction, and the epigenetic reprogramming essential for male germline development. The key takeaway is that spermatogenesis relies on a coordinated system where central nodes simultaneously regulate multiple downstream pathways, ensuring the robust and orderly progression from a diploid stem cell to a haploid spermatid. While integrating transcriptomics and IHC is an established strategy, our application provides a compelling link between systems-level data and the spatial organisation of the seminiferous tubule, illustrating how global molecular programs are executed in specific cellular microenvironments. Future studies should integrate single-cell and spatial transcriptomics to capture cellular heterogeneity and rare germ cell populations. Integrating multi-omics approaches, including proteomics and epigenomics, would provide a more comprehensive view of regulatory mechanisms that underlie SSC differentiation and spermatid formation. Functional validation of hub genes, using CRISPR/Cas9 or overexpression models, is essential to establish causal roles. Investigating epigenetic regulators and chromatin remodelling could clarify stagespecific transcriptional changes. Finally, in vivo imaging and lineage-tracing experiments would help link molecular programs to cellular behaviors within the seminiferous tubules.
