Successful neonatal outcome after postmortem caesarean section at Sumbawanga Regional Referral Hospital, Rukwa, Tanzania: case report
Authors: Ismail H. Macha, Ng’holo M. Malimi, Francis J. Mashigala, Gibert G. Msoma, Issa Sabi, Kelly P Msafiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiac arrest in pregnancy can occur for a variety of reasons. During maternal cardiac arrest or shortly thereafter, the fetus may remain viable. Postmortem caesarean section can potentially save the fetus with or without long-term neurological sequelae; in the absence of timely intervention, however, fetal death is inevitable. Given the limited documentation in developing countries and the possibility of unfavourable outcomes, this report presents one of the few documented cases from resource-limited Sub-Saharan Africa describing a live birth with a sustained favourable neurological outcome following a postmortem caesarean section performed after maternal death from a dental abscess. This case expands the evidence base for the feasibility and value of such a procedure in settings lacking advanced critical care.Case summaryWe report the case of a 34-year-old multigravida at a gestational age of 36 weeks and 2 days, who was admitted to Sumbawanga Regional Referral Hospital as a referral from a lower facility, presenting with a painful left jaw swelling and a diagnosis of dental abscess. She had a one-month history of this swelling associated with fever, hoarseness of voice, and purulent discharge. Abdominal examination revealed a gravid uterus with fundal height of 36 cm, longitudinal lie, and cephalic presentation. There were no palpable contractions, and the fetal heart rate was 143 bpm. The patient was managed as a case of dental abscess in a near-term pregnancy.The dental team performed incision and drainage, and during the procedure, the patient developed cardiorespiratory arrest attributed to endotoxic septic shock secondary to the dental abscess. The exact cause of death, beyond endotoxic shock, could not be determined in the absence of an autopsy, and other potential causes of death cannot be definitely ruled out. Despite resuscitation attempts, the patient could not be saved and was pronounced dead.In consultation with the Obstetric team, an immediate postmortem caesarean section was performed, delivering a female neonate weighing 2600 g with an APGAR score of 6 and 8 in the 1st and 5th minutes, respectively. The newborn was admitted to the neonatal care unit for further management. At the time of this publication, the child is one year and nine months old and has no apparent neurological impairment, with normal developmental milestones.ConclusionDespite several limitations and the possibility of poor neonatal outcomes, a timely postmortem caesarean section performed at a favourable gestational age, in an appropriate setting with skilled personnel, basic equipment, and access to a neonatal care unit, can result in a live birth with good early neonatal outcomes even in a resource-limited environment.

Introduction

Postmortem caesarean section refers to the surgical delivery of the fetus following maternal death. The primary objective is to rescue the fetus before the inevitable fetal death or permanent neurological damage occurs. The likelihood of neonatal survival and favourable neurological outcome depends on several factors, including the interval between maternal cardiac arrest and delivery, the geographical location where cardiac arrest occurred, the attending personnel’s skills, the underlying maternal cause of cardiac arrest, and the gestational age at the time of maternal death [1, 2]. Reported cases, often with poor results and adverse outcomes, are probably underreported [3].

In Sub-Saharan Africa, where healthcare resources are limited, this intervention presents inherent challenges. Constraints include inadequate infrastructure, shortages of skilled personnel, and limited equipment. These are further compounded by cultural, legal and religious beliefs that may delay or prevent timely intervention and thus contribute to poor neonatal outcomes [3, 4] Gestational age carries additional implications in these settings; while neonatal survival is possible as early as 22 weeks of gestation in high income countries, viability in low and middle income settings is typically considered only at 28 weeks of gestation or slight as early as 26 weeks, attributed to limited infrastructure and access to advanced neonatal intensive care for extremely preterm infants. Consequently, when maternal death occurs before 28 weeks of gestation in such an environment, the probability of neonatal survival is very low [5].

Despite these constraints, documented cases of postmortem caesarean section with sustained favourable neurological outcomes from resource-limited Sub-Saharan Africa hospitals are scarce, which limits the evidence base required to guide practice in these settings. This case report addresses that gap by presenting a live birth with a good neurological outcome following a postmortem caesarean section performed at a regional referral hospital in Tanzania, where maternal death resulted from an unusual infectious aetiology.

Case report

The patient was a 34-year-old multigravida, married, with primary level education, engaged in subsistence farming, and originating from a remote area of the Rukwa Region, who was admitted to Sumbawanga Regional Referral Hospital at 36 weeks and 2 days of gestation. The patient was referred from a lower-level healthcare facility with a presenting complaint of a painful left-sided mandibular swelling of approximately one month’s duration. Associated symptoms included episodic fever, hoarseness of voice (dysphonia), and scant purulent discharge from the swelling.

The patient denied the following: dizziness, vomiting, dysuria, and any difficulty in breathing or coughing. Appetite was reported to be reduced (anorexia). There was no known drug or food allergy, and no family history of diabetes mellitus or hypertension. She was not smoking cigarettes, abusing drugs, or taking alcohol. Fetal movements were reported as normal. There was no history of abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, or rupture of membranes. The patient’s obstetric history is notable for spontaneous vertex delivery in all prior pregnancies.

During the examination, she was fully conscious. Vital signs recorded were body temperature 37.3 °C, blood pressure 115/78 mmHg, pulse rate 93 bpm, and oxygen saturation 94% in room air. Local findings on the left jaw revealed a tender, fluctuant, yellowish-white mass with a focal point of purulent discharge, warm with smooth margins. Additionally, she had palpable and tender cervical lymph nodes. Obstetric examination showed a gravid abdomen with fundal height of 36 cm, longitudinal lie, and cephalic presentation. There were no palpable contractions, and fetal heart rates were 143 bpm.

A bedside investigation, specifically a random blood glucose measurement, yielded normal results of 7.9 mmol/L. Additionally, laboratory investigation included a complete blood count, which demonstrated leukocytosis with predominant neutrophilia. Total white blood cell count was 11.926 × 103/µL, with a laboratory reference range of 3.7 to 10.1 × 103/µL. Neutrophils were 7.612 × 103/µL, with a reference range of 1.63 to 6.96 × 103/µL. Platelet count and haemoglobin concentration were 317.7 × 103/µL and 13.53 g/dL, respectively, and were both within normal reference ranges. At the time of treatment, blood culture could not be performed because the necessary facilities were not within the hospital’s available resources. A periapical radiograph was not obtained, as it was deemed unnecessary at the time; furthermore, even if indicated, the requisite imaging modality was not immediately available. A working diagnosis of dental abscess in a near-term pregnancy was reached, and treatment was initiated with intravenous antibiotics (amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 1.2 g) and intravenous analgesics (paracetamol 1 g).

The dental team was consulted and planned incision and drainage of the abscess as an inpatient under local anesthesia. Initially, the patient declined consent, which delayed the procedure by several hours. After repeated counselling, she consented to surgery. Approximately 30 min after the procedure was initiated, the patient developed gasping respiration. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was instituted as per protocol. Upon recognition of agonal respiration, a head tilt-chin lift maneuver was performed while simultaneously summoning assistance. Chest compressions were then administered at the rate of 100 per minute over the lower half of the sternum, adhering to a compression-to-ventilation ratio of 30:2. This regimen was continued until the senior provider determined that the resuscitation was futile and the mother was pronounced dead.

The obstetric team was immediately consulted, and within five minutes of maternal cardiac arrest, a postmortem caesarean section was performed. A female infant weighing 2,600 g was delivered with Apgar scores of 6 at the first minute and 8 at the fifth minute. The maternal cardiac arrest is believed to have been precipitated by irreversible endotoxic septic shock, which developed as a complication of delayed treatment of a dental abscess.

A postmortem examination was not performed on the deceased as the family declined any further investigative procedures after death. The newborn was admitted to the neonatal care unit for observation and supportive care. She remained admitted for a few days before being discharged to the family. Caregivers received counselling regarding feeding options, and follow-up visits were arranged for assessment of growth and development. At the time of this report, the child is one year and nine months old, demonstrates age-appropriate developmental milestones, and shows no evidence of neurological impairment.

Discussion

Maternal death causes cessation of circulation and immediate interruption of uteroplacental blood flow, leading to fetal hypoxia, hypercapnia, and acidosis, where the severity will depend on the duration of perfusion loss. The fetus possesses adaptive mechanisms that transiently protect against asphyxia, including higher haemoglobin levels than in adults, preferential shunting of oxygenated blood via the ductus venosus and ductus arteriosus, and the “brain-sparring” effect (redistribution of blood flow from peripheral tissues to vital organs such as the brain) [6, 7]. However, prolonged interruption of uteroplacental blood flow overwhelms these compensatory mechanisms and results in hypoxic-ischaemic injuries and eventually may lead to fetal death.

Fetal survival after death critically depends on the timing of delivery. A widely cited optimal window is within 4 min of maternal cardiac arrest, yet cases of good neonatal outcomes after exceeding 30 min have been reported [8, 9]. In the present case, delivery occurred approximately 5 min after cardiac arrest, and the child remains neurologically intact. This aligns with the 4-minute benchmark but also illustrates that brief delays beyond the ideal window do not invariably lead to poor outcomes, consistently with reports of longer survival intervals [8, 9].

Gestational age at the time of maternal death is another key determinant, particularly in low-resource settings. In high-income countries, survival with acceptable morbidity may be achievable from 22 to 24 weeks of gestation, whereas in Sub-Saharan Africa and similar settings, realistic viability begins at 26–28 weeks [1, 5, 10]. Our patient was 36 weeks and 2 days of gestational age at the time of maternal death, with a birth weight of 2600 g, which greatly facilitated postnatal care. Had gestational age been below 28 weeks, the outcome would likely have been significantly poorer; a finding that reinforces the strong interaction between gestational age and resource availability.

The availability of skilled personnel and essential equipment strongly influences outcomes. In remote African settings with poor road infrastructure and limited transport, timely intervention is unlikely, and neonatal survival chances are minimal. Conversely, when maternal cardiac arrest occurs in a hospital with trained staff and available equipment improve the probability of a successful neonatal outcome [1]. In this case, the cardiac arrest occurred in-hospital, enabling rapid intervention and contributing to a live birth with a good neurological outcome.

Strengths of this case may include, but are not limited to, precise documentation of the arrest-delivery interval of 5 min, fetal monitoring data, and routine follow-up showing intact neurological status. A major constraint is that consent for postmortem caesarean section was not prospectively obtained; it was given only for incision and drainage. There was no opportunity to obtain separate consent after maternal cardiac arrest. This highlights a recurrent ethical and practical challenge balancing the urgency of neonatal salvage against local norms and legal issues requiring next of kin consent, a process that can be fatally time-consuming [1, 3].

The ethical framework for performing postmortem caesarean section in resource-limited settings often conflicts with the urgency to salvage the fetus, creating a tension between the duty to rescue and legal requirements for informed consent [11]. In a setting where legal statutes may not explicitly address perimortem intervention, the doctrine of necessity often justifies proceeding without immediate authorization to prioritize fetal survival, provided the action aligns with the physician’s duty to rescue [11, 12]. This approach is supported by international literature indicating that maternal cardiopulmonary arrest in the second half of pregnancy mandates immediate delivery to optimize fetal outcomes, even when maternal survival is not possible [13].

Applying these principles in the presented case, legal statutes, cultural norms, and beliefs, along with the initial difficulty encountered in obtaining consent for incision and drainage of the dental abscess, attempting to secure formal written consent for postmortem caesarean section before delivery would have made saving the baby extremely difficult. Therefore, consent was obtained after delivery, at which point the infant had already been safely rescued.

The underlying cause of maternal death can also influence the likelihood of a favourable neonatal outcome. Commonly reported causes of maternal deaths necessitating perimortem or postmortem caesarean section are trauma, maternal cardiac disease, eclampsia, and amniotic fluid embolism [1, 9]. Trauma is frequently cited as the leading cause, and it can directly compromise both maternal and fetal status depending on the mechanism, severity, and anatomical location of injury. Eclampsia similarly poses risks to both mother and fetus. In our case, delayed management of a dental abscess was followed by maternal deterioration, which led to irreversible endotoxic septic shock and eventually death; timely delivery allowed fetal salvage. The precise cause of death beyond cardiac arrest as a result of endotoxic septic shock secondary to dental abscess could not be ascertained due to the unavailability of an autopsy, and alternative causes of death cannot be definitively excluded.

Limitations

This case report has several limitations. First, a maternal autopsy could not be conducted, precluding definitive confirmation of the cause of death. Second, the available maternal and neonatal diagnostic data are limited, and long-term follow-up beyond the documented age is lacking. Consequently, the absence of standardized long-term follow-up remains underexplored. This may include use of validated tools like the Bayley Scale of Infant Development (BSID) at 12 and 24 months; the Age and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ) for periodic screening, and formal neurological examinations to systematically assess cognitive, motor, and behavioural outcomes. Serial assessment at 6, 12, 18, and 24 months of age would strengthen the evidence for neurodevelopmental integrity after postmortem caesarean section. Finally, the use of retrospective consent and the influence of contextual local factors may reduce the generalizability of the findings to other settings.

Conclusion

This reported case from a resource-limited regional referral hospital in Tanzania illustrates that a timely postmortem caesarean section, performed at a favourable gestational age by skilled personnel with basic equipment and access to a neonatal care unit, can result in a live birth with good early neurological outcome. It highlights the critical importance of maintaining institutional readiness for this rare but time-sensitive emergency procedure, even in resource-constrained settings, and reinforces the value of incorporating postmortem caesarean section considerations into obstetric emergency protocols.
