On the Nonexistence of Global Solutions for Nonlocal Parabolic Equations with Forcing Terms

. 1.
Introduction and main results

The study of nonlinear parabolic equations has been a central topic in mathematical analysis due to their wide-ranging applications in real-word problems. Among these, the nonlinear heat equation with nonlocal source and/or forcing terms has garnered significant attention because of its ability to model complex phenomena. In this paper, we investigate the Cauchy problem for a nonlinear heat equation that incorporates both an integral nonlinearity and a continuous forcing term. The equation under consideration is given by:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=\|u(t)\|_{q}^{\alpha}|u|^{p}+\,t^{\varrho}\,\mathbf{w}(x),&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{N},\end{cases}

where
N\geq 1
,
p>1
,
q\geq 1
,
\alpha\geq 0
,
\varrho>-1
, and
\mathbf{w}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function.
The problem (
1.1
) arises in the theory of quasi-regular and quasi-conformal mappings, which have significant applications in modeling non-Newtonian fluid flow, population dynamics in biological systems, and other physical phenomena (see, e.g.,
[
13
,
38
]
). For a comprehensive overview of physical models derived from various fields and their mathematical analysis, we refer to
[
13
,
20
,
31
,
38
,
41
,
21
,
17
,
40
,
32
,
33
,
40
,
43
,
44
]
and the references therein.

In the case
\mathbf{w}(x)=0
and
\alpha=0
, problem (
1.1
) reduces to the classical Fujita-type equation:

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{matrix}\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=|u|^{p},&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty),\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.\end{matrix}\right.

In their seminal works
[
11
,
12
]
, Fujita studied the initial value problem (
1.2
) for nonnegative initial data
u_{0}(x)\geq 0
. He established the following fundamental results:

•

If
p<1+\frac{2}{N}
, then all nontrivial solutions of (
1.2
) blow up in finite time.

•

If
p>1+\frac{2}{N}
, then (
1.2
) admits both bounded global solutions and solutions that blow up in finite time. Specifically, for initial data
u_{0}(x)
bounded by a sufficiently small Gaussian
\varepsilon e^{-|x|^{2}}
, the solution exists globally. For further details, see
[
40
, Theorem 20.1, p. 129]
.

In the borderline case
p=1+\frac{2}{N}
, it was shown by Hayakawa
[
19
]
for dimensions
N=1,2
and by Kobayashi, Sino, and Tanaka
[
23
]
and Aronson and Weinberger
[
2
]
for all
N\geq 1
that all nontrivial solutions to (
1.2
) blow up in finite time. The critical exponent
p_{F}:=1+\frac{2}{N}
is now widely known as the
Fujita exponent
for problem (
1.2
).
The case of a weighted nonlinearity of the form
|x|^{b}|u|^{p}
was investigated in
[
39
]
(see also
[
4
,
27
]
). The Cauchy problem (
1.2
) is shown to have no global solution if
p<1+\frac{2+b}{N}
, but global solutions exist when
p>1+\frac{2+b}{N}
provided that
b>-1
for
N=1
and
b>-2
for
N\geq 2
. See
[
39
, Theorem 1.1, p. 125]
for a precise statement. However, the critical case
p=1+\frac{2+b}{N}
is still open.

Note that the case
\alpha=\varrho=0
was investigated by Bandle, Levine and Zhang in
[
5
]
. They showed that (
1.1
) does not have global solutions provided that
p<\frac{N}{N-2}
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,{\mathbf{w}}(x)\,dx>0
.
As noted in
[
5
]
, the exponent
\frac{N}{N-2}
is closely tied to the critical exponent of the corresponding elliptic problem. This exponent is particularly significant because it is analogous to determining the smallest value of
p
for which the equation
\Delta v+v^{p}=0
admits singular radial solutions of the form
v(r)=Ar^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}
.

In
[
22
]
, the authors consider (
1.1
) with
\alpha=0
and
\varrho>-1
. They showed that the critical exponent is given by

\begin{split}p^{*}(\varrho)=\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{cllll}\frac{N-2\varrho}{N-2\varrho-2}&\mbox{if}&\quad-1<\varrho<0,\\
\infty&\mbox{if}&\quad\varrho>0.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

In
[
29
]
, the author considers the equation

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=|x|^{\alpha}|u|^{p}+\mathtt{a}(t)\mathbf{w}(x),

where the function
\mathtt{a}(t)
exhibits the following asymptotic behavior:

\mathtt{a}(t)\sim\begin{cases}\mathtt{a}_{0}\,t^{\sigma}&\text{as }t\to 0,\\
\mathtt{a}_{\infty}\,t^{m}&\text{as }t\to\infty,\end{cases}

with
\mathtt{a}_{0},\mathtt{a}_{\infty}>0
,
\sigma>-1
, and
m\in\mathbb{R}
.

It was shown that the critical exponent for this problem is given by

p^{*}(m,\alpha)=\begin{cases}\frac{N-2m+\alpha}{N-2m-2}&\text{if }m\leq 0\text{ and }\alpha>-2,\\
\infty&\text{if }m>0\text{ and }\alpha>-2,\end{cases}

provided that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathbf{w}(x)\,dx>0
.
Later, in
[
1
]
, a broad class of forcing terms of the form
\mathtt{a}(t)\mathbf{w}(x)
is studied.
To the best of our knowledge, the first rigorous investigation of the critical Fujita exponent for diffusion equations with nonlocal nonlinearity was conducted by Galaktionov and Levine in
[
16
]
. They studied a class of equations of the form

(1.3)

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}K(y)u^{q}(y,t)\,dy\right)^{(p-1)/q}u^{r+1},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad t>0,

where
p\geq 1
,
r\geq 0
,
p+r>1
, and
1\leq q<\infty
. The function
K
is assumed to be nonnegative and measurable, with additional technical assumptions. Using a subsolution approach, the authors determined the critical Fujita exponent under suitable conditions. Specifically, for the case
K(y)=1
, any nontrivial nonnegative solution blows up in finite time provided that

0\leq r\leq\frac{2}{N}\quad\text{and}\quad p<1+\frac{2q(1-Nr/2)}{N(q-1)}.

Later, the following nonlocal equation

(1.4)

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=u^{p}\left(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{q}(y,t)dy\right)^{r},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad t>0,

was considered in
[
25
]
. Here,
p>1
and
q,r>0
. Among other results, it is established that if
p+(q-1)r\leqslant 1+\frac{2}{N}
, then all nontrivial nonnegative solutions blow-up in finite time. A similar problem was also studied in
[
24
]
, namely

(1.5)

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=u\left(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}k(y)u^{q}(y,t)\,dy\right)^{p/q},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad t>0,

where
p>0
and
q\geq 1
. The kernel
k(y)
is assumed to be measurable, nonnegative, and satisfying the estimates

(1.6)

(1+|y|)^{\gamma}\lesssim k(y)\lesssim(1+|y|)^{\gamma},\quad\gamma\in\mathbb{R}.

It was shown that every nontrivial nonnegative solution blows up in finite time if one of the following conditions holds:

•

0\leq\gamma<N(q-1)
and
0<p\leq\frac{2q}{N(q-1)-\gamma}
;

•

N(q-1)\leq\gamma
and
p>0
.

It is tempting to conjecture that the
Fujita critical exponent
can be deduced from scaling considerations. Specifically, consider a nonlinear parabolic equation on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with the property that if
u(x,t)
is a solution with initial data
u_{0}(x)
, then the rescaled function
\lambda^{a}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)
, for
\lambda>0
, is also a solution with initial data
\lambda^{a}u_{0}(\lambda x)
. This scaling invariance implies that the only Lebesgue space that preserves the norm of the rescaled initial data
\lambda^{a}u_{0}(\lambda x)
is
L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, where
p_{c}=N/a
. Consequently, the Fujita critical exponent
p_{F}
is expected to be determined by the condition
p_{c}=1
, which corresponds to
a=N
. This scaling argument accurately predicts the value of
p_{F}
for a broad class of nonlinear local diffusion equations
[
3
,
4
,
14
,
15
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
, as well as for certain nonlinear nonlocal parabolic problems. For example, it applies to equations of the form

u_{t}=\Delta u+u(0,t)^{p-q}u(x,t)^{q}\quad\text{with}\quad p>q\geq 1,

as investigated in
[
46
]
. However, there exist nonlinear nonlocal equations for which the Fujita exponent
p_{F}=\infty
cannot be predicted by scaling arguments; see
[
45
,
46
]
. A particularly interesting example is studied in
[
7
]
, where the Fujita exponent is finite, but its value deviates from that expected based on scaling invariance.

When applied to equation

(1.7)

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=\|u(t)\|_{q}^{\alpha}|u|^{p},

the scaling argument yields the Fujita exponent

(1.8)

p+\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\alpha=1+\frac{2}{N}.

The novelty in studying (
1.1
) lies in the interplay between the nonlocal nonlinearity
\|u(t)\|_{q}^{\alpha}|u|^{p}
and the forcing term
t^{\varrho}\mathbf{w}(x)
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time such a problem has been considered.

It is worth noting that several well-established methods exist for studying the blow-up phenomenon, each with its specific domain of applicability to problems in mathematical physics. For a comprehensive survey of blow-up results for solutions of first-order nonlinear evolution inequalities and related Cauchy problems, we refer to
[

,

and the references therein. Further detailed discussions can be found in the books
[

,

We adopt the following notion of global weak solution.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that
u
is a global weak solution of (
1.1
) if it satisfies the conditions

u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\quad u\in L^{\infty}_{loc}((0,\infty),L^{q}),\quad\|u(t)\|_{q}^{\alpha}|u|^{p}\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty))

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty)}u(-\partial_{t}\psi-\Delta\psi)dxdt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u_{0}(x)\psi(x,0)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty)}\|u(t)\|_{q}^{\alpha}|u|^{p}\psi dxdt+

\displaystyle\qquad{}\hskip 85.35826pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty)}t^{\varrho}\mathbf{w}\psi\,dxdt

for all
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,\infty))
.

As is standard practice, (
1.1
) is equivalent, within an appropriate framework, to the Duhamel formulation

(1.9)

u(t)={\mathbf{S}}(t)u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbf{S}}(t-\tau)\|u(\tau)\|_{q}^{\alpha}|u(\tau)|^{p}\,d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\tau^{\varrho}{\mathbf{S}}(t-\tau)\mathbf{w}\,d\tau

where
{\mathbf{S}}(t)=:{\rm e}^{t\Delta}
denotes the linear heat semi-group. A solution of the integral equation (
1.9
) is commonly referred to as a
mild solution
of (
1.1
). In our setting, it can be shown that a mild solution also constitutes a weak solution. For a detailed discussion on the equivalence between the differential and integral formulations for the nonlinear heat equation, we refer to
[
47
,
48
,
42
,
26
]
.

1.1.
Main results

In what follows, we will make use of the following assumptions on
\mathbf{w}
, which play a key role in applying the comparison principle and deriving blow-up results. Specifically, we assume that
\mathbf{w}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
satisfies either

(1.10)

\inf_{\lambda>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,{\rm e}^{-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{\lambda}}\,\mathbf{w}(y)dy\right)\geq 0,

or

(1.11)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,\mathbf{w}(x)dx>0.

The latter assumption is frequently used in the literature; see, for instance,
[
1
,
5
,
22
,
29
,
30
]
.

Our first main result addresses the local well-posedness in Lebesgue spaces.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N\geq 1
,
p>1
,
q\geq 1
,
\alpha\geq 1
, and
\varrho>-1
. Furthermore, suppose that
q>\frac{N(p-1)}{2}
. Then the initial value problem (
1.1
) is locally well-posed in
L^{q}
. More precisely, given
u_{0},\mathbf{w}\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, then there exist
T>0
and a unique mild solution
u\in C([0,T],L^{q})
to (
1.1
).

Remark 1.1
.

a)

The restriction
\alpha\geq 1
is required for the application of a fixed-point argument based on the inequality (
2.13
) below. While the case
\alpha=0
is straightforward, the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
remains unresolved.

b)

The assumption
\varrho>-1
is necessary to ensure that
t^{\varrho}\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(0,\infty)
.

When
q\geq p
, it is meaningful to consider the weak solution
u\in C([0,T],L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
in the integral sense as defined in (
1.9
). Uniqueness of the solution is guaranteed within this class, as established in the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
N\geq 1
,
p>1
,
q\geq 1
,
\alpha\geq 1
, and
\varrho>-1
. Assume the following conditions hold:

(1.12)

q>N(p-1)/2,\;\;q\geq p,\;\;\mathbf{w}\in L^{q}.

Then, for any
T>0
, the initial value problem (
1.1
) admits at most one solution in the space
C([0,T],L^{q})
.

Remark 1.2
.

Note that the case
\alpha=0
was studied in
[
6
, Theorem 4]
for both
q>N(p-1)/2
(resp.
q=N(p-1)/2
) and
q\geq p
(resp.
q>p
). In this work, however, we are only able to address the case where
q>N(p-1)/2
and
q\geq p
.

In what follows we set

(1.13)

\delta=\alpha\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
N\geq 3
,
p>1
,
q\geq 1
,
\alpha\geq 0
, and
\varrho\leq 0
. Suppose
\delta<\frac{2}{N}
. Assume further that
\mathbf{w}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
satisfies the conditions (
1.10
) and (
1.11
). If the inequality

(1.14)

p+\frac{N\delta}{N-2\varrho-2}<\frac{N-2\varrho}{N-2\varrho-2}

holds, then the problem (
1.1
) admits no global weak solution with nonnegative initial data.

Remark 1.3
.

When
\alpha=0
, condition (
1.14
) reduces to
p<\frac{N-2\varrho}{N-2\varrho-2}
, a result that was previously established in
[
29
]
.

We now turn to the analysis of the global theory. The fundamental result in this context is as follows:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
N\geq 2
,
\delta<\frac{2}{N}
and
-1<\varrho<0
. Assume that
p\geq\frac{N-2\varrho-N\delta}{N-2\varrho-2}
, and,
\frac{N(p-1)}{2}\leqslant q\leqslant p
. Let us define
\ell=\frac{N\left((p-1)(q-1)+\delta\right)}{2(q-1)+N\delta+2(\varrho+1)(p-1)(q-1)+2(\varrho+1)\delta}
and
p_{c}=\frac{N\left((p-1)(q-1)+\delta q\right)}{2(q-1)+N\delta}
. Then, for any initial data
u_{0}\in L^{p_{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\mathbf{w}\in L^{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, with the property that
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{p_{c}}}+\|\mathbf{w}\|_{L^{\ell}}
is sufficiently small, the equation (
1.1
) admits a global solution
u
in time.

2.
Useful tools & Auxiliary results

In this section, we introduce key tools and auxiliary results that are essential for establishing our main results. Let
\{{\mathbf{S}}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be the linear heat semigroup defined by
{\mathbf{S}}(t)\,\varphi=G(\cdot,t)\star\varphi,t>0,
where
G(\cdot,t)
is the heat kernel given by

G(x,t)=\left(4\pi t\right)^{-N/2}\,{\rm e}^{-|x|^{2}/(4t)},\;\;\;t>0,\;\;\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

In the subsequent lemma, we gather the smmothing effect regarding the heat semigroup
\mathbf{S}(t)
that will be helpful for our purposes.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
1\leq a\leq b\leq\infty
,
t>0
, and
\varphi\in L^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Then

(2.1)

\|{\mathbf{S}}(t)\,\varphi\|_{L^{b}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq t^{-\frac{N}{2}(\frac{1}{a}-\frac{1}{b})}\|\varphi\|_{L^{a}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}.

We now state a comparison principle for (
1.1
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
N\geq 1
,
p,q\geq 1
,
\alpha\geq 0
, and
\varrho>-1
. Suppose
u
is a global mild solution to problem (
1.1
) with initial data
0\leq u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and assume that condition (
1.10
) holds. Then, there exists a positive constant
\mathtt{C}_{0}
, depending only on
N
and
u_{0}
, such that the following estimate holds:

(2.2)

u(x,t)\geqslant\mathtt{C}_{0}t^{-\frac{N}{2}}\exp\left({-\frac{|x|^{2}}{t}}\right),\quad t\geq 1,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Proof.

Let
v
satisfy the following problem:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v-\Delta v=t^{\varrho}\mathbf{w}(x),&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,\infty),\\
v(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.\end{cases}

The solution
v
can be expressed as

v(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(x-y,t)u_{0}(y)\,dy+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(x-y,t-\tau)\tau^{\varrho}\mathbf{w}(y)\,dy\,d\tau,

where
G
is the heat kernel. Using (
1.9
) and the assumption (
1.10
), we observe that for
t\geq 1
,

\begin{split}u(x,t)&\geq v(x,t)\\
&\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(x-y,t)u_{0}(y)\,dy\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(4\pi t)^{-\frac{N}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{4t}\right)u_{0}(y)\,dy\\
&\geq(4\pi t)^{-N/2}\exp\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2t}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\left(-\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}\right)u_{0}(y)\,dy.\end{split}

Finally, we obtain the desired estimate (
2.2
) with the constant

\mathtt{C}_{0}=(4\pi)^{-N/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\exp\left(-\frac{|y|^{2}}{2}\right)u_{0}(y)\,dy.

∎

Remark 2.1
.

A direct consequence of (
2.2
) is that, for any
1\leq q<\infty
, the following estimate holds:

(2.3)

\|u(t)\|_{q}\geq Ct^{-\frac{N}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)},\quad t\geq 1,

where the constant
C
is given by
C=\pi^{N/2q}q^{-N/2q}\mathtt{C}_{0}.

The comparison of the two conditions (
1.10
) and (
1.11
) is discussed in the following statement.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. The following hold:

(i)

The assertion (
1.10
) implies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\,\mathbf{w}(x)dx\geq 0
.

(ii)

There exists a function
\mathbf{w}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
satisfying (
1.11
) and the additional condition:

(2.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\rm e}^{-|y|^{2}}\mathbf{w}(y)\,dy\leq 0.

This demonstrates that (
1.11
) does not, in general, imply (
1.10
).

Proof.

The proof of part (i) follows by taking the limit in (
1.10
) as
\lambda\to\infty
and applying the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem.

For part (ii), consider the following simple example

(2.5)

\mathbf{w}(y)=a\,{\rm e}^{-|y|^{2}}-{\rm e}^{-2|y|^{2}},\quad 2^{-N/2}<a\leq(\frac{2}{3})^{N/2}.

To analyze this, we use the well-known result for Gaussian integrals:

(2.6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\rm e}^{-\theta|y|^{2}}dy=\left(\frac{\pi}{\theta}\right)^{N/2},\quad\theta>0.

Applying (
2.6
), we compute the following integrals:

(2.7)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathbf{w}(y)\,dy&=\pi^{N/2}\left(a-2^{-N/2}\right),\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{-y^{2}}\mathbf{w}(y)\,dy&=\pi^{N/2}\left(a2^{-N/2}-3^{-N/2}\right).\end{split}

The conclusion follows directly from (
2.7
).
∎

We also recall the well known
\varepsilon-
Young inequality.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
a,b\geq 0
,

and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1.

Then

(2.8)

ab\leq\varepsilon\,a^{p}+(p\varepsilon)^{-q/p}\,\frac{b^{q}}{q}.

When applying the test-function method to analyze the blow-up, we will utilize the following result.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
g\in C^{\infty}([0,\infty))
be a smooth, nonnegative function such that
g(\tau)=0
for all
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
, where
\tau_{0}>0
is a fixed constant. For
T>0
and
\theta>2
, define the function
g_{T,\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(2.9)

g_{T,\theta}(x)=\left(g\left(\frac{|x|^{2}}{T}\right)\right)^{\theta}.

Then, there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
g
,
\theta
, and the dimension

, such that

(2.10)

\Big|\Delta\,g_{T,\theta}(x)\Big|\leq\frac{C}{T}\left(g\left(\frac{|x|^{2}}{T}\right)\right)^{\theta-2}.

where
\Delta
denotes the Laplacian operator.

Proof.

By performing the change of variable
y=\frac{|x|^{2}}{T}
, we express

g_{T,\theta}(x)=g^{\theta}(y)=v(r),\quad\text{where }r=|x|.

To compute the Laplacian, we utilize the well-known formula for radially symmetric functions:

(2.11)

\Delta v=v^{\prime\prime}+\frac{N-1}{r}v^{\prime}.

Applying this formula, we derive

(2.12)

\Delta\,g_{T,\theta}(x)=\Bigg(2\theta Ng^{\prime}(y)g(y)+4\theta yg^{\prime\prime}(y)g(y)+4\theta(\theta-1)yg^{\prime}(y)^{2}\Bigg)\frac{g(y)^{\theta-2}}{T}.

Since
g
has compact support, its derivatives
g(y)
,
g^{\prime}(y)
, and
g^{\prime\prime}(y)
are bounded. Furthermore, since
y\leq\tau_{0}
, it follows from (
2.12
) that

|\Delta g^{\theta}(y)|\leq\frac{C}{T}g(y)^{\theta-2},

where the positive constant
C
depends on
N
,
\theta
,
\tau_{0}
,
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}
,
\|g^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}
, and
\|g^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

To establish well-posedness and apply a fixed-point argument, we will utilize the following estimates, which can be easily derived.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(E,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed vector space,
p>1
,
a,b\geq 0
,
\alpha\geq 0
, and

\Big|a^{p}\|x\|^{\alpha}-b^{p}\|y\|^{\alpha}\Big|\lesssim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\|y\|^{\alpha}|a-b|(a^{p-1}+b^{p-1})+a^{p}\|x-y\|(\|x\|^{\alpha-1}+\|y\|^{\alpha-1})&\text{if}\,\,\alpha\geq 1\\
\|y\|^{\alpha}|a-b|(a^{p-1}+b^{p-1})+a^{p}\|x-y\|^{\alpha}&\text{if}\,\,\alpha<1\end{array}\right.

The following classical result will be employed to establish the desired regularity for the local solution of (

) in the

Lemma 2.7
.

Let

,
T>0
, and

. Then,

(2.14)

\mathbf{v}(t)=:\int_{0}^{t}\,{\rm e}^{(t-s)\Delta}\,F(s)\,ds\in\text{C}([0,T],L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})).

Proof.

For completeness, we provide a proof of the above lemma. Fix

, and write

\mathbf{v}(t)-\mathbf{v}(t_{0})=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}{\rm e}^{(t-s)\Delta}F(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t_{0}}{\rm e}^{(t_{0}-s)\Delta}\left({\rm e}^{(t-t_{0})\Delta}F(s)-F(s)\right)\,ds.

Using (
2.1
), it follows that

(2.15)

\|\mathbf{v}(t)-\mathbf{v}(t_{0})\|_{r}\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\|F(s)\|_{r}ds+\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\|{\rm e}^{(t-t_{0})\Delta}F(s)-F(s)\|_{r}ds.

By the dominated convergence theorem and the fact that
{\rm e}^{t\Delta}\phi\to\phi
as

in

, we deduce from (

) that
\mathbf{v}
is continuous at

∎

We also make use of the following singular Gronwall inequality

.

Let

and

\psi(t)\leq A\,{\mathcal{E}}_{1-\sigma}\left(M\Gamma(1-\sigma)\,t^{1-\sigma}\right),\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

3.
Local well-posedness and unconditional uniqueness

3.1.
Local well-posedness

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we use the contraction mapping principle in a complete metric space.
Fix
M>\|u_{0}\|_{q}
. For
T>0
, define

\mathbf{X}(T)=\left\{u\in L^{\infty}((0,T),L^{q})\cap L^{\infty}_{loc}((0,T),L^{pq});\;\sup_{0<t<T}\|u(t)\|_{L^{q}}\leq M\;\mbox{and}\;\sup_{0<t<T}t^{\sigma}\|u(t)\|_{pq}\leq M\right\},

where

(3.1)

\sigma=\frac{N(p-1)}{2pq}.

We endow
\mathbf{X}(T)
with the distance

d(u,v)=\sup_{0<t<T}\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{q}+\sup_{0<t<T}t^{\sigma}\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{pq}.

It is clear that
\left(\mathbf{X}(T),d\right)
is a nonempty complete metric space.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Given
u\in\mathbf{X}(T)
, we define

(3.2)

\Phi(u)(t)={\mathbf{S}}(t)u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbf{S}}(t-\tau)\|u(\tau)\|_{q}^{\alpha}|u(\tau)|^{p}\,d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\tau^{\varrho}{\mathbf{S}}(t-\tau)\mathbf{w}\,d\tau.

Our goal is to show that, for a suitably chosen
T
, the map
\Phi
is well-defined from
\mathbf{X}(T)
into itself and constitutes a contraction.

For
u\in\mathbf{X}(T)
and
t\in(0,T)
, it follows from (
2.1
) that

(3.3)

\begin{split}\|\Phi(u)(t)\|_{q}&\leq\|u_{0}\|_{q}+M^{\alpha+p}\int_{0}^{t}\tau^{-p\sigma}d\tau+\frac{T^{\varrho+1}}{\varrho+1}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{q}\\
&\leq\|u_{0}\|_{q}+\frac{T^{1-p\sigma}}{1-p\sigma}M^{\alpha+p}+\frac{T^{\varrho+1}}{\varrho+1}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{q}.\end{split}

Similarly, it follows that

(3.4)

\begin{split}\|\Phi(u)(t)\|_{pq}&\leq t^{-\sigma}\|u_{0}\|_{q}+M^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\sigma}\|u(\tau)\|_{pq}^{p}\,d\tau+\left(\int_{0}^{t}\,\tau^{\varrho}(t-\tau)^{-\sigma}\,d\tau\right)\|\mathbf{w}\|_{q}\\
&\leq t^{-\sigma}\|u_{0}\|_{q}+M^{\alpha+p}t^{1-\sigma-p\sigma}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\,s^{-p\sigma}(1-s)^{-\sigma}\,ds\right)+t^{\varrho+1-\sigma}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{q}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\,s^{\varrho}(1-s)^{-\sigma}\,ds\right)\\
&\leq t^{-\sigma}\bigg(\|u_{0}\|_{q}+M^{\alpha+p}T^{1-p\sigma}{{\mathscr{B}}}(1-p\sigma,1-\sigma)+T^{\varrho+1}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{q}{{\mathscr{B}}}(1+\varrho,1-\sigma)\bigg),\end{split}

where
{\mathscr{B}}
denotes the well-known Beta function. Here, we have used the condition
\varrho>-1
and the straightforward observation that
\sigma<\frac{1}{p}<1
where
\sigma
is given by (
3.1
).

From (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), it follows easily that
\Phi(u)\in\mathbf{X}(T)
, provided
T>0
is chosen sufficiently small such that

(3.5)

\begin{split}\frac{T^{1-p\sigma}}{1-p\sigma}M^{\alpha+p}+\frac{T^{\varrho+1}}{\varrho+1}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{q}&<M-\|u_{0}\|_{q}\\
M^{\alpha+p}T^{1-p\sigma}{{\mathscr{B}}}(1-p\sigma,1-\sigma)+T^{\varrho+1}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{q}{{\mathscr{B}}}(1+\varrho,1-\sigma)&<M-\|u_{0}\|_{q}\end{split}

The following step is to show that
\Phi
is a contraction. To verify this, let us consider
u,v\in\mathbf{X}(T)
, and write using (
2.1
) together with (
2.13
)

(3.6)

\begin{split}\left\|\Phi(u)(t)-\Phi(v)(t)\right\|_{{q}}&\leqslant C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|u(\tau)\right\|_{pq}^{p}\left\|u(\tau)-v(\tau)\right\|_{q}\left(\left\|u(\tau)\right\|_{q}^{\alpha-1}+\left\|v(\tau)\right\|_{q}^{\alpha-1}\right)d\tau\\
&\quad+C\int_{0}^{t}\left\|v(\tau)\right\|_{q}^{\alpha}\left\|\left|u(\tau)-v(\tau)\right|\left(\left|u(\tau)\right|^{p-1}+\left|v(\tau)\right|^{p-1}\right)\right\|_{q}d\tau\\
&\leq CM^{\alpha+p-1}T^{1-p\sigma}d(u,v),\end{split}

where we have used Hölder’s inequality with
\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{pq}+\frac{p-1}{pq}
.

On the other hand, by using the smoothing effect inequality (
2.1
), and taking into account (
3.1
) and (
2.13
), we get

(3.7)

\begin{split}\left\|\Phi(u)(t)-\Phi(v)(t)\right\|_{pq}&\leqslant C\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\sigma}\left\|u(\tau)\right\|_{pq}^{p}\left\|u(\tau)-v(\tau)\right\|_{q}\left(\left\|u(\tau)\right\|_{q}^{\alpha-1}+\left\|v(\tau)\right\|_{q}^{\alpha-1}\right)d\tau\\
&\quad+C\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\sigma}\left\|v(\tau)\right\|_{q}^{\alpha}\left\|\left|u(\tau)-v(\tau)\right|\left(\left|u(\tau)\right|^{p-1}+\left|v(\tau)\right|^{p-1}\right)\right\|_{q}d\tau\\
&\leq CM^{\alpha+p-1}t^{1-p\sigma-\sigma}d(u,v).\end{split}

It follows from (
3.6
)
and (
3.7
) that

(3.8)

d\left(\Phi(u),\Phi(v)\right)\leqslant CM^{\alpha+p-1}T^{1-p\sigma}d(u,v).

It follows from (
3.8
) that for
T>0
sufficiently small, the mapping
\Phi:\mathbf{X}(T)\to\mathbf{X}(T)
is a contraction. By the Banach fixed-point theorem,
\Phi
admits a unique fixed point
u\in\mathbf{X}(T)
, which constitutes a solution to (
1.9
).

To complete the proof, it remains to establish that
u
possesses the regularity
u\in C([0,T],L^{q})
. Since
u\in\mathbf{X}(T)
, and under the assumptions
p\sigma<1
,
\mathbf{w}\in L^{q}
, and
\varrho>-1
, it follows that the term
\|u(t)\|_{q}^{\alpha}|u|^{p}+t^{\varrho}\mathbf{w}(x)
belongs to
L^{1}((0,T),L^{q})
. Invoking Lemma
2.7
, we conclude that
u\in C([0,T],L^{q})
, as desired.
∎

3.2.
Unconditional uniqueness

Our goal is to prove the unconditional uniqueness stated in Theorem
1.2
. To this end, let
T>0
, and let
u,v\in C([0,T],L^{q})
be two solutions of (

). Define

From (
1.9
), (
2.13
), and (
2.1
), we derive the inequality

\psi(t)\leq C\mathcal{M}^{\alpha+p-1}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\sigma}\psi(\tau)\,d\tau,

where
\sigma
is given by (
3.1
). Invoking Lemma
2.8
, we conclude that

for all

in

. This establishes the desired uniqueness.

4.
Nonexistence of global solutions

Our goal is to prove the nonexistence of global weak solutions under the assumptions stated in Theorem
1.3
. To achieve this, we employ the test function method, following the approach used in
[
18
,
22
,
29
,
30
,
37
]
for instance.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Suppose that the conditions of Theorem
1.3
hold, and assume that

(4.1)

p+\frac{N\delta}{N-2\varrho-2}<\frac{N-2\varrho}{N-2\varrho-2}.

We proceed by contradiction, assuming that the problem (
1.1
) admits a global weak solution with nonnegative initial data in the sense of Definition
1.1
. To derive a contradiction, we employ the test function method. Specifically, we introduce carefully chosen cut-off functions

\psi_{k}\in C^{\infty}_{0}([0,\infty))
,
k=1,2
with
0\leq\psi_{k}\leq 1
and

\psi_{1}(s)=\begin{cases}1\hskip 3.41418pt\text{if}\hskip 3.41418pt1/2\leq s\leq 3/4\\
0\hskip 3.41418pt\text{if}\hskip 3.41418pts\in[0,1/4]\cup[4/5,\infty)\end{cases},\quad\psi_{2}(s)=\begin{cases}1\hskip 3.41418pt\text{if}\hskip 3.41418pts\in[0,1]\\
0\hskip 3.41418pt\text{if}\hskip 3.41418pts\geq 2.\end{cases}

Next, for
T>1
sufficiently large, we define the function

\psi_{T}(x,t)=\psi_{1}\bigg(\frac{t-1}{T}\bigg)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\psi_{2}\bigg(\frac{|x|^{2}}{T}\bigg)^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}.

By multiplying both sides of the equation (
1.1
) by
\psi_{T}
and integrating over the domain
(1,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we obtain

(4.2)

\begin{split}-\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\,\partial_{t}\psi_{T}\,dx\,dt-\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\,\Delta\psi_{T}\,dx\,dt&=\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|u\|_{q}^{\alpha}|u|^{p}\,\psi_{T}\,dx\,dt\\
&+\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}t^{\varrho}\mathbf{w}(x)\,\psi_{T}\,dx\,dt\\
&+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u_{1}(x)\,\psi_{T}(x,1)\,dx.\end{split}

Since
\psi_{1}(x,1)=0
, it follows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u_{1}(x)\psi_{T}(x,1)dx=0.

Moreover, from (
4.2
), we deduce that

(4.3)

\mathbb{K}(T)+\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}t^{\varrho}\mathbf{w}(x)\psi_{T}dxdt\leq\mathbb{I}(T)+\mathbb{J}(T)

where

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u||\Delta\psi_{T}|dxdt,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|\left|\partial_{t}\psi_{T}\right|dxdt,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}t^{-\frac{N\alpha}{2}(1-\frac{1}{q})}|u|^{p}\psi_{T}dxdt.

Here, we have used (
2.3
) for the last term
\mathbb{K}(T)
.

Applying the
\varepsilon-
Young inequality (
2.8
), we obtain

(4.7)

\begin{split}\mathbb{I}(T)&=\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(t^{-\frac{N}{2p}\alpha\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)}|u|\psi_{T}^{\frac{1}{p}}\right)\left(t^{\frac{N}{2p}\alpha\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)}\psi_{T}^{-\frac{1}{p}}\left|\Delta\psi_{T}\right|\right)dxdt\\
&\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{K}(T)+C\underset{\mathbb{I}_{1}(T)}{\underbrace{\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}{t^{\frac{N}{2}\frac{\alpha}{p-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)}}\psi_{T}^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\left|\Delta\psi_{T}\right|^{\frac{p}{p-1}}dxdt}}.\end{split}

To bound the term
\mathbb{I}_{1}(T)
, we apply Lemma
2.5
after performing the change of variable
\tau=\frac{t-1}{T}
. This leads to

(4.8)

\begin{split}\mathbb{I}_{1}(T)&\leq CT^{-\frac{p}{p-1}}\left(\int_{1}^{T}t^{\frac{N}{2}\frac{\alpha}{p-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)}\psi_{1}\left(\frac{t-1}{T}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}dt\right){\left(\int_{|x|<\sqrt{2T}}dx\right)}\\
&\leq CT^{1+\frac{N}{2}-\frac{p}{p-1}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}(1+T\tau)^{\frac{N}{2}\frac{\alpha}{p-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)}\psi_{1}(\tau)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}d\tau\right)\\
&\leq CT^{1+\frac{N}{2}-\frac{p}{p-1}+\frac{N\delta}{2(p-1)}},\end{split}

where
\delta
is given by (
1.13
). Therefore, from (
4.7
), we obtain

(4.9)

\mathbb{I}(T)\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{K}(T)+CT^{1+\frac{N}{2}-\frac{p}{p-1}+\frac{N\delta}{2(p-1)}}.

Similarly, we find that

(4.10)

\mathbb{J}(T)\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{K}(T)+CT^{1+\frac{N}{2}-\frac{p}{p-1}+\frac{N\delta}{2(p-1)}}.

Returning to (
4.3
) and combining the estimates (
4.9
) and (
4.10
), we obtain

(4.11)

\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}t^{\varrho}\mathbf{w}(x)\psi_{T}(x)\,dx\,dt\leq CT^{1+\frac{N}{2}-\frac{p}{p-1}+\frac{N\delta}{2(p-1)}}.

To complete the proof, we establish a suitable lower bound for the left-hand side of (
4.11
). Writing

(4.12)

\begin{split}\int_{1}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}t^{\varrho}\mathbf{w}(x)\psi_{T}(x)\,dx\,dt&=\left(\int_{1}^{T}t^{\varrho}\psi_{1}\left(\frac{t-1}{T}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\,dt\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\psi_{2}\left(\frac{|x|^{2}}{T}\right)^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}\mathbf{w}(x)\,dx\right)\\
&\geq CT^{\varrho+1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathbf{w}(x)\psi_{2}\left(\frac{|x|^{2}}{T}\right)^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}\,dx,\end{split}

we observe that since
\mathbf{w}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\psi_{2}(0)=1
, the dominated convergence theorem implies

\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathbf{w}(x)\left(\psi_{2}\left(\frac{|x|^{2}}{T}\right)\right)^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathbf{w}(x)\,dx>0.

Thus, for sufficiently large

(4.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathbf{w}(x)\,dx\leq CT^{\frac{N}{2}-\frac{p}{p-1}+\frac{N\delta}{2(p-1)}-\varrho}.

Noting that

(4.14)

\frac{N}{2}-\frac{p}{p-1}+\frac{N\delta}{2(p-1)}-\varrho=\frac{N-2\varrho-2}{2}+\frac{N\delta-2}{2(p-1)},

and recalling that

and (

), we easily see that

(4.15)

\frac{N-2\varrho-2}{2}+\frac{N\delta-2}{2(p-1)}<0.

Letting

which is a contradiction. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.∎

\displaystyle\leqslant\nu^{p+\alpha}t^{-\frac{N}{2r}(p-1)-\beta p-\beta\alpha+1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s)^{-\frac{N}{2r}(p-1)}s^{-\beta(p+\alpha)}ds

(5.11)

\displaystyle=C\nu^{p+\alpha}t^{-\frac{N}{2q}(p-1)-\beta p-\beta\alpha+1}{\mathscr{B}}\left(1-\beta(p+\alpha),1-\frac{N}{2r}(p-1)\right)

(5.12)

\displaystyle=C\nu^{p+\alpha}t^{-\beta},\quad t>0,

We observe that, by (
5.4
) and the condition
\beta(p+\alpha)<1
, the quantity

{\mathscr{B}}\left(1-\beta(p+\alpha),1-\frac{N}{2r}(p-1)\right)

is well-defined. Similarly, we can conclude that

(5.13)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|\tau^{\varrho}e^{(t-\tau)\Delta}\mathbf{w}\|_{r}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{0}^{t}\tau^{\varrho}(t-\tau)^{-\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{1}{l}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}d\tau\right)\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\ell}

(5.14)

\displaystyle=t^{\varrho-\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{1}{l}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}s^{\varrho}(1-s)^{-\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{1}{l}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}ds\right)\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\ell}

(5.15)

\displaystyle=t^{\varrho-\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{1}{l}-\frac{1}{r}\right)+1}{\mathscr{B}}\left(1+\varrho,1-\frac{N}{2}(\frac{1}{l}-\frac{1}{r})\right)\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\ell}

(5.16)

\displaystyle=Ct^{-\beta}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\ell},\quad t>0.

We emphasize that, under the assumption
\varrho>-1
and condition (
5.4
), the quantity

{\mathscr{B}}\left(1+\varrho,1-\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{1}{l}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\right)
is well-defined. Consequently, the above estimates enable us to conclude that

(5.17)

t^{\beta}\|\Phi(u)(t)\|_{r}\leq C\left(\|u_{0}\|_{p_{c}}+\nu^{p+\alpha}+\|\mathbf{w}\|_{\ell}\right),\quad t>0

By selecting
\epsilon>0
and
\nu>0
sufficiently small, we deduce that

(5.18)

\sup_{t>0}t^{\beta}\|\Phi(u)(t)\|_{r}\leq\nu.

This proves that
\Phi
maps
\mathbf{Y}
into itself. To complete the proof, we now show that
\Phi:\mathbf{Y}\rightarrow\mathbf{Y}
is a contraction.

Using arguments similar to those above, we can show that for any
u,v\in\mathbf{Y}
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\|\Phi(u)(t)-\Phi(v)(t)\|_{r}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left\|e^{(t-\tau)\Delta}\left(|u(\tau)|^{p}\|u(\tau)\|_{q}^{\alpha}-|v(\tau)|^{p}\|v(\tau)\|_{q}^{\alpha}\right)\right\|_{{r}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{p}{r}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}\left\||u(\tau)|^{p}\|u-v\|_{q}\left(\|u(\tau)\|_{q}^{\alpha-1}-\|v(\tau)\|_{q}^{\alpha-1}\right)\right\|_{{\frac{r}{p}}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{p}{r}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}\|v(\tau)\|_{q}^{\alpha}\left\||u-v|\left(|u(\tau)|^{p-1}-|v(\tau)|^{p-1}\right)\right\|_{{\frac{r}{p}}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{p}{r}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}\tau^{-\beta p}(\tau^{\beta}\|u\|_{r})^{p}\tau^{-\beta}(\tau^{\beta}\|u-v\|_{r})\tau^{-\beta(\alpha-1)}(\tau^{\beta}\|u\|_{r})^{\alpha-1}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\frac{N}{2}\left(\frac{p}{r}-\frac{1}{r}\right)}\tau^{-\beta\alpha}(\tau^{\beta}\|v(\tau)\|_{q})^{\alpha}\tau^{-\beta}(\tau^{\beta}\|u-v\|_{q})\tau^{-\beta(p-1)}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left((\tau^{\beta}\|u\|_{r})^{p-1}+(\tau^{\beta}\|v\|_{r})^{p-1}\right)\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq t^{-\beta}\nu^{p+\alpha-1}{\mathscr{B}}\left(1-\beta(p+\alpha),1-\frac{N}{2r}(p-1)\right)d(u,v).

Therefore,

d(\Phi(u),\Phi(v))\leq C\nu^{p+\alpha-1}d(u,v).

By choosing
\nu>0
sufficiently small, we conclude that
\Phi:\mathbf{Y}\rightarrow\mathbf{Y}
is a contraction. Consequently, the global existence of a solution follows from the Banach fixed point theorem applied to the complete metric space
\mathbf{Y}
.
