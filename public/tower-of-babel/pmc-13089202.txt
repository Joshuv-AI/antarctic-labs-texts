Acute Environmental Triggers and Intermediate-Term Modulators of Emergency Migraine-Related Health Care Encounters
Authors: Ido Peles, Lena Novack, Michal Gordon, Batia Sarov, Victor Novack, Gal Ifergane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesMigraine activity is shaped not only by the biological vulnerability but also by environmental factors that may influence susceptibility and contribute to fluctuations in migraine occurrence, although the evidence remains inconsistent. We aimed to evaluate the intermediate-term and short-term effects of environmental exposures on migraine activity and to examine a conceptual layered model comprising biological vulnerability, intermediate-term environmental modulators, and acute triggers.MethodsWe conducted a population-based case-crossover study among patients with migraine identified from the Negev Migraine Cohort (2000–2023), using electronic medical records from Clalit Health Services and Soroka University Medical Center. Eligible participants were adult patients (≥18 years) residing in Be'er Sheva, Israel, with migraine diagnoses or triptan prescriptions. Daily exposure to air pollutants and meteorologic conditions were obtained from fixed monitoring stations. Outcomes were emergency migraine-related encounters as a marker of triggered events and quarterly triptan use as a marker for disease activity. Intermediate-term modulators were defined as weekly climatic conditions hypothesized to modify the short-term effects of daily exposures. Associations were evaluated using conditional logistic and Poisson regression.ResultsThe analysis included 7,032 adult patients with migraine residing in Be'er Sheva. Short-term exposure to nitrogen dioxide (NO2) (odds ratio [OR] 1.41; 95% CI 1.13–1.77) and solar radiation (OR 1.23; 95% CI 1.07–1.42) was associated with an increased risk of emergency migraine-related encounters. Cumulative exposure to NO2 was associated with a higher triptan use (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 1.10; 95% CI 1.00–1.21), as was cumulative particulate matter ≤2.5 µm (PM2.5) exposure during the preceding quarter (IRR 1.09; 95% CI 1.00–1.19). Weekly climatic conditions modified short-term effects: high temperatures and low humidity during summer amplified NO2-related risk (OR 2.18; 95% CI 1.06–3.30), whereas cold and humid winter weeks intensified the effect of PM2.5 (OR 3.78; 95% CI 1.74–5.82).DiscussionShort-term and cumulative exposure to ambient air pollution and climatic factors were associated with increased migraine activity. Although these findings support a layered model of environmental modulation of migraine activity, their generalizability is limited by the use of emergency health care encounters as a proxy outcome, underscoring the need for further research using complementary measures.

Introduction

Migraine is a leading cause of neurologic disability.1,2 Its biological basis includes genetic predisposition and CNS sensitization,3-5 yet predicting when episodes occur remain elusive. This uncertainty limits clinicians' ability to provide anticipatory care and patients' ability to prevent escalation. Weather fluctuations and air quality are frequently cited as precipitants,6 but neurology lacks a coherent model linking environmental exposures to intrinsic brain vulnerability.

Environmental factors influence migraine through well-defined mechanisms. Nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) induce oxidative stress, vascular dysfunction, and systemic inflammation, activating the trigeminovascular complex and the release of calcitonin gene–related peptide (CGRP).7-12 Transient receptor potential (TRP) channels act as molecular sensors for irritants, temperature, and oxidative stress, directly linking exposures to neuronal depolarization.13-15 Solar radiation (SR) and temperature variability engage hypothalamic circuits regulating circadian and autonomic balance.16,17 These inputs converge on the brain characterized by hyperexcitability and central sensitization, thereby lowering the threshold for initiating an episode.18,19

Most previous studies have focused on isolated short-term triggers, leaving the interaction between sustained modulators and acute precipitants poorly defined.20-23 Recent work demonstrated interactions between long-term NO2 exposure and temperature, but these associations were examined at monthly and yearly time scales, reflecting chronic modulatory effects rather than acute triggering dynamics.24 We propose a layered model of migraine activity: stable biological vulnerability, intermediate-term environmental modulators, and acute triggers. This framework clarifies how environmental exposures shape migraine dynamics and establishes a basis for integrating environmental risk into precision neurology, enabling risk profiling, anticipatory prevention, and patient-centered care. In this case-crossover study, we assess both intermediate-term and short-term effects of environmental exposures on migraine activity.

Methods

Clinical Setup

This study evaluated associations between ambient air pollution, climatic factors, and migraine activity in Be'er Sheva, Israel (∼220,000 residents), located in the arid northern Negev desert. The area is characterized by hot, dry summers, mild winters, and wide diurnal temperature variation,25,26 affecting both human physiology and pollutant dynamics. Air quality is influenced by desert dust and urban emissions. Frequent dust storms increase particulate matter (PM10 and PM2.5) levels,27 whereas traffic and industries contribute to NO2 and ozone (O3) concentrations.28 These interacting exposures highlight the need for continuous environmental monitoring and adaptive public health strategies.

The study population was insured by Clalit Health Services (CHS), which covers ∼70% of Be'er Sheva residents. Soroka University Medical Center (SUMC), a 1,191-bed hospital affiliated with CHS, is the sole tertiary hospital in the southern district. CHS maintains a centralized electronic medical record system encompassing hospital, community, and pharmacy data, offering detailed longitudinal information. This combination of environmental variability, integrated care, and comprehensive data supports analysis of environmental exposures and migraine outcomes.

Study Design and Population

This retrospective, population-based case-crossover study is part of the “Migraine and the City” project, a collaborative initiative involving the Negev Environmental Health Research Institute, the Clinical Research Center, the Neurology Department at SUMC, and the Be'er Sheva Municipality. The study integrates multiple data sources, including ground-based air pollution, climate monitoring stations, and comprehensive clinical records. All data were linked at the individual level with daily temporal resolution.

The study population consisted of adult patients (≥18 years) residing in Be'er Sheva who were identified from the updated 2023 version of the Negev Migraine Cohort, described in previous publications.29 Patients were included based on ICD-9 codes or triptan claims (2000–2023). Only individuals with a valid Be'er Sheva address were eligible. Those missing demographic or exposure data were excluded. For patients who relocated during the study period, only person-time corresponding to their residence within Be'er Sheva was included.

Exposure Assessment

Ambient air pollution and meteorologic data were obtained from the Ministry of Environmental Protection monitoring network. Exposure data were derived from fixed monitoring stations across the study region, including 28 stations for meteorologic variables, 20 for O3, 35 for NO2, 34 for sulfur dioxide (SO2), 10 for SR, 12 for particulate matter ≤10 µm (PM10), and 17 for particulate matter ≤2.5 µm (PM2.5). Pollutants analyzed included NO2, SO2, carbon monoxide (CO), O3, PM10, and PM2.5. Meteorologic parameters included relative humidity, temperature, and SR.

Measurements were recorded at 5-minute intervals and aggregated into daily averages. For each individual, daily exposure values were assigned based on the nearest monitoring station that measured the specific pollutant or meteorologic variable. The primary short-term exposure window included the day of the migraine episode (lag 0) and the preceding days (lags 1, 2, and 7). Lagged exposure terms were defined relative to the event day, with lag 1 corresponding to exposure 1 day before the migraine-related health care encounter, lag 2 to 2 days ago, and so forth up to lag 7. In addition, for cumulative effect of chronic exposure, we calculated quarterly averages by averaging daily exposures over the current and preceding calendar quarters (i.e., a 6-month window). To assess potential exposure misclassification, we performed a negative control analysis using lead NO2 exposure (after event exposure). Analyses were further stratified by residential distance to major roadways. Distance to major roads was defined according to municipal traffic-engineering classifications based on vehicle volume per unit time and measured in meters from the residential building.

Outcome Assessment

In this long-term, population-based cohort, diary-based or prospectively recorded headache-day data were not available. Therefore, routinely collected health care data and pharmacy data served as proxy measures of migraine activity.

The primary outcome for migraine triggering was the occurrence of emergency migraine-related health care encounters. These included visits to the emergency department or primary care system in which the primary diagnosis was acute migraine, as recorded in electronic health records. Emergency migraine-related encounters represent the severe end of the clinical spectrum and constitute the only outcome consistently recorded across all patients and all years of follow-up.

The primary outcome for disease activity was quarterly triptan use, serving as a proxy for ongoing migraine disease activity. Quarterly triptan dispensation was measured through pharmacy records as the count of dispensed triptan doses per 90-day interval. Follow-up was divided into consecutive 90-day intervals from the first triptan purchase, and doses per quarter were recorded alongside average exposure. Triptan dispensation was analyzed only among individuals who had ever used triptans (46.7% of the cohort), as this proxy is not applicable to nonusers. To evaluate the stability of this measure, we examined repeated purchases across quarters. Among triptan users, 49.4% had dispensation in at least 2 years, and 36.4% across 3 or more years. These repeated patterns support triptan purchase frequency as a longitudinal marker of sustained migraine burden in this subgroup. Data on preventive migraine therapies, including CGRP monoclonal antibodies, were not consistently available in the electronic medical records. Therefore, medication-based analyses focused on triptan dispensation as a standardized and longitudinally available marker of acute migraine treatment.

Socioeconomic status (SES) was estimated using the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics' socioeconomic index (1–20), grouped as low (1–9), intermediate (10–12), and high (13–20).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, extracted from CHS records. Means and SDs were reported for continuous variables, whereas categorical variables were presented as percentages. To estimate the association between environmental exposures and emergency migraine-related health care encounters, we applied conditional logistic regression. For cumulative exposure and triptan use, we used Poisson regression models examining the association between exposure in the current and previous quarters, and medication use in the current quarter. Accordingly, triptan counts in each calendar quarter were modeled in relation to environmental exposures averaged over the same quarter (concurrent exposure) and the immediately preceding quarter (lagged exposure).

The case-crossover design inherently controls for time-invariant confounders by comparing each participant's exposure during migraine episodes with their own exposure during control (nonevent) periods. For each migraine-related emergency visit, control days were selected within the same calendar month and matched on a day of the week, ensuring control for systematic weekly patterns in health care utilization. Associations were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs for case-crossover analyses and as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% CI for Poisson models. No adjustment aside the one by a case-crossover design was included in the models. To standardize exposure values, pollutant levels were scaled from 0 to 1 within each patient's exposure range, where the maximum value received 1 and the minimum 0. This transformation allowed estimation of the maximum individual effect of each pollutant by comparing ORs for emergency migraine-related health care encounters at peak vs at minimal exposure, a calibration approach that has been applied in previous epidemiologic studies to place heterogeneous exposures on a common, unit-free scale.30,31 All point estimates in the study represent a multiplicative effect of an increase in an environmental factor from minimum (scaled at 0) to maximum (scaled at 1) exposure.

In addition, we examined whether intermediate-term meteorologic conditions modified the short-term associations between daily environmental exposures and migraine risk. Weekly averages of selected climatic variables were calculated to characterize the intermediate-term environmental context and categorized using percentile-based thresholds (elevated: >75th percentile; reduced: <25th percentile). Interaction terms were then incorporated to assess whether the effects of daily air pollutant exposures differed during these extreme weekly conditions compared with all remaining study days, which served as the reference category. This analytical approach reflects a layered framework in which acute environmental exposures act as triggers, while intermediate-term conditions modulate susceptibility to these triggers, as illustrated conceptually in Figure 1. Sex and SES effect modifications were also explored using stratified case-crossover analyses.

Sensitivity analyses tested alternative exposure windows and lag periods (0–14 days). We also conducted subgroup analyses comparing episodes requiring hospitalization to those treated solely in the community. To evaluate potential effect modification by day-of-week variation in health care utilization, we conducted additional descriptive and sensitivity analyses comparing emergency migraine-related health care encounters during week days vs weekends. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) in the RStudio 2024.04.2 environment (Posit PBC, Boston, MA).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This study was approved by the institutional review board at SUMC and adhered to ethical guidelines for research using anonymized electronic health records. Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of deidentified patient data.

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Results

In this study, we analyzed migraine episodes of 7,032 patients with migraine residing in Be'er Sheva. Most were female individuals (77.4%) and of Jewish origin (97.9%), with a mean age of 46.9 years (SD = 16.4) (Table 1). SES, ranging from 1 (lowest) to 20 (highest), was between 10 and 12 in 46.0% of the patients, while 32.4% patients had a score below 10. During the study period, 46.7% of the patients purchased triptan medication at least once. Among these users, the average triptan use was 2.1 tablets per month (SD = 1.6), with 9.3% of patients using 5–9 tablets per month, suggesting a possible need for prophylactic treatment, and 2.3% using ≥10 tablets, an indicative of potential medication overuse headache. These usage categories are reported relative to the subgroup of patients who used triptans at least once, not the full study population.

Among 7,032 patients, 2,215 patients (31.5%) had at least 1 emergency migraine-related encounter during follow-up, contributing a total of 24,608 events. Among those with ≥1 encounter, 48.4% had a single visit, 22.3% had 2–3 visits, and 29.3% had ≥4 visits (median 2, interquartile range 1–4). Emergency migraine-related health care encounters were substantially less frequent during weekends than week days.

The environmental factors monitored throughout the study align with a semidry hot climate.

Specifically, the average temperature was 20.16°C (SD = 6.01), average NO2 concentration was 8.67 ppb (SD = 3.74), and PM10 and PM2.5 averaged 57.94 and 22.29 µg/m3, respectively (eTable 1). On the day with the highest number of emergency migraine-related health care encounters in the dataset (October 23, 2011), environmental conditions showed elevated pollutant levels compared with the study-wide averages. Specifically, PM10 was 119.90 µg/m3 (study mean: 57.94 µg/m3), PM2.5 was 27.29 µg/m3 (mean: 22.29 µg/m3), and NO2 was 11.15 ppb (mean: 8.67 ppb). Conversely, the day with the fewest encounters (December 18, 2009) exhibited lower pollutant levels: PM10 at 46.19 µg/m3, PM2.5 at 17.99 µg/m3, and NO2 at 7.15 ppb.

Table 2 summarizes the results of the conditional logistic regression model assessing the association between short-term environmental exposures and emergency migraine-related health care encounters. Exposure on the day of the event showed no effect, therefore only lagged exposure values were considered in the analysis (eTable 2). NO2 exposure 1 day before an episode was associated with increased risk (OR 1.41; 95% CI 1.13–1.77; p = 0.003), as was SR (OR 1.23; 95% CI 1.07–1.42; p = 0.004). PM2.5 was not significantly associated with emergency migraine-related health care encounters across any lag period. Temperature demonstrated a borderline association 1 day before an episode (OR 1.14; 95% CI 0.99–1.33), which did not persist at other lags. Figure 2 illustrates these associations, highlighting the strongest short-term effects for NO2 and SR. For interpretability, additional analyses were conducted using exposure variables expressed in their original units, with effect estimates reported per 10 units increase in pollutants and per 5°C increase in temperature. These analyses are summarized in eTable 3.

In exploratory stratified analyses, associations for NO2, and temperature were more pronounced among men than women, although age-stratified analyses did not reveal substantial heterogeneity. SES-stratified analyses showed heterogeneity in effect estimates across groups; however, statistical significance was limited and no consistent SES-based effect modification was observed (eTable 4). Specifically, for NO2, statistically significant associations were observed only in the medium-SES group, whereas for PM2.5, none of the SES strata demonstrated statistically significant associations at the examined lags.

In negative control analyses, lead NO2 exposure was not associated with emergency migraine-related health care encounters in any proximity stratum. By contrast, NO2 exposure at lag 1 remained associated with increased risk, with stronger associations observed among individuals residing closer to major roadways (OR 1.82; 95% CI 1.60–2.12; p < 0.001).

Table 3 summarizes the results of the Poisson regression model evaluating the association between cumulative environmental exposure and triptan usage. NO2 exposure during the previous quarter was significantly associated with increased triptan use (IRR 1.10; 95% CI 1.00–1.21; p = 0.048). SR did not show a statistically significant association with triptan use (IRR 1.06; 95% CI 0.98–1.15; p = 0.129). PM2.5 exposure was associated with triptan use in both, within the current quarter (IRR 1.08; 95% CI 0.99–1.18; p = 0.079) and 3 months before its purchase (IRR 1.09; 95% CI 1.00–1.19; p = 0.049). By contrast, cumulative CO exposure was associated with decreased triptan use during the current quarter (IRR 0.90; 95% CI 0.84–0.96; p = 0. 002). A statistically significant protective association was also observed for SO2 exposure during the current quarter (IRR 0.92; 95% CI 0.86–0.99).

Sensitivity analyses tested alternative lag structures and outcome definitions. Restricting the analysis for episodes requiring hospitalization or those treated solely in the community did not alter the observed associations. Extending the exposure window to 2 weeks demonstrated that acute effects were concentrated within lags 1–2, with delayed effects stabilizing around approximately 1 week. Intermediate lags3-6 did not contribute additional information because of its high temporal correlation with adjacent days. Sensitivity analyses stratified by week day vs weekend periods yielded similar exposure-outcome associations, with no meaningful differences in effect estimates. Because pollutants and lagged exposures are highly correlated, the set of odds ratios in Table 2 does not represent independent tests. The consistent temporal profile seen across primary and sensitivity analyses reduces the likelihood that the observed associations arise from chance alone.

Discussion

In this population-based study, we found that environmental exposures were associated with migraine activity across distinct time scales. Short-term increases in selected air pollutants and climatic factors were associated with acute migraine-related health care encounters, whereas cumulative exposures were associated to markers of sustained disease activity. In addition, intermediate-term meteorologic conditions modified the magnitude of these short-term associations, indicating that susceptibility to environmental triggers varies according to recent environmental context. Together, these findings support a layered model of migraine activity, in which environmental exposures act both as acute triggers and as modulators of susceptibility over time. This framework extends the concept of migraine thresholds by linking real-world exposures to established neurobiological mechanisms. Using health care encounters and triptan dispensation as pragmatic proxies for migraine diaries, we show that cumulative exposure to NO2 and PM2.5 increases susceptibility to episodes, whereas transient surges in NO2 and SR function as acute precipitants.

Our findings suggest that environmental factors influence migraine through at least 2 physiologic pathways. Cumulative exposure to PM2.5 seems to act through a slower process of systemic inflammation, promoting central sensitization over several days,9,32-34 this effect is consistent with previous reports.35,36 By contrast, NO2 exerts a rapid effect by activating trigeminovascular pathways through TRP channels, leading to the release of vasoactive peptides such as CGRP.10,37-41 SR and ambient temperature also act as fast-acting triggers, likely mediated through effects on autonomic tone, thermoregulation, and cerebrovascular dynamics that engage hypothalamic and brainstem nuclei implicated in migraine initiation.14,42 SR may further contribute via photoreceptive, hormonal, and behavioral pathways, including effects on hydration and sleep, which lowers the threshold for episode onset.12,43-45 To our knowledge, SR has not been evaluated within a broader multivariable environmental framework that simultaneously considers air pollutants and climatic modulators.

Beyond identifying pollutants and climatic conditions as independent triggers, our results demonstrate that temperature and humidity modulate their effect. High temperatures combined with low humidity amplified the effect of NO2, whereas cold and humid conditions intensified the effect of PM2.5. In addition, a hot day occurring within an otherwise cold, humid week may generate a larger thermoregulatory and autonomic shift, producing a stronger trigger, whereas a similarly hot day during an already warm, dry week likely elicits a smaller physiologic response. These findings align with recent evidence showing that associations between temperature and migraine are heterogeneous and sometimes conflict across regions and study designs, suggesting that temperature may exert its influence primarily through contextual or interacting environmental pathways rather than as a standalone trigger.46 These findings are also consistent with previous research demonstrating joint effects of air pollution and temperature on various health outcomes47 and suggest that temperature variability and seasonal transitions, rather than absolute temperature alone, may be more significant in determining migraine risk.14,20 Such dynamics are consistent with evidence that abrupt weather changes disrupt thermoregulatory homeostasis and activate hypothalamic and sympathetic pathways.16,21

Temporal patterns of health care utilization were also considered. Emergency migraine-related encounters were markedly less frequent during weekends than week days, consistent with established health care-seeking behavior.12 It is important to note that, sensitivity analyses stratified by weekday vs weekend periods yielded comparable exposure–outcome associations. This indicates that while absolute encounter frequency varies by day of week, the observed associations between environmental conditions and migraine risk are unlikely to be explained by day-of-week variation in health care utilization.

Humidity, in combination with temperature, contributed to the modulation of short-term environmental effects on migraine activity. Specifically, cold and humid conditions intensified the association between PM2.5 exposure and migraine-related health care encounters, while attenuating the observed effects of SO2. These observations highlight that environmental influences on migraine are synergistic rather than isolated, underscoring the need for models that account for combined exposures.48 Clinically, this supports the use of season-specific anticipatory strategies, such as heightened preventive vigilance during transitional periods or pollution peaks that coincide with adverse meteorologic conditions.

Although SO2 showed an inverse association in some models, we do not interpret this as evidence of biological protection. In Be'er Sheva, SO2 elevations occur predominantly on relatively cold, humid winter days characterized by reduced SR, and lower exposure to heat-related or photic triggers. In this context, SO2 likely serves as an indicator of meteorologic conditions that inherently involve lower triggering potential, rather than as a causal modulator of migraine risk. This interpretation aligns with recent meta-analytic findings showing no association between SO2 and migraine.49

For clinical practice, these findings highlight opportunities for precision and anticipatory care. Forecasted high-risk exposure periods can be mitigated through behavioral measures (limiting outdoor activity and using high-efficiency particulate air filtration), short-term prophylaxis (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or gepants), or early initiation of migraine-specific acute treatment. Environmental risk alerts can be delivered through targeted notifications or integrated into migraine prediction applications, providing patients and clinicians with practical tools to align treatment strategies with exposure dynamics.

The major strength of this study lies in its scale and completeness, leveraging a population-based cohort that has been followed for more than 2 decades, with linked daily exposure, clinical, and pharmacy data. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, particularly those related to exposure assessment and outcome definition. First, exposure was assigned using fixed monitoring stations, and individual-level behaviors that influence personal exposure, including time spent indoors, use of air conditioning or air filtration, occupational setting, and daily mobility were not available. Migraineurs often modify their behavior in response to perceived triggers, and intentional avoidance of outdoor activity on high-pollution or high-temperature days may further reduce actual exposure relative to ambient measurements. These factors likely attenuate exposure-outcome associations and may bias effects toward the null. Although exposure was assigned using fixed-site monitoring and does not capture individual-level microenvironmental variability, particularly near major roadways, negative control analyses based on exposure timing and road proximity support the temporal specificity of the observed associations. Accordingly, findings should be interpreted as reflecting associations with ambient urban environmental conditions rather than personal exposure.

Second, our outcomes rely on routinely collected clinical and pharmacy data rather than diary-based headache information. These constraints reflect data availability and are balanced by the strengths of the cohort. Migraine episodes were identified only through health care encounters, which likely represent the more severe end of the spectrum, but they constitute the only population-wide, longitudinally stable outcome available for more than 2 decades of follow-up. Episodes managed at home were not captured, leading to an underestimation of total migraine activity. Triptan dispensation offers complementary information on sustained disease burden but applies only to the 46.7% of patients who ever used triptans. Within this subgroup, repeated purchases across multiple quarters support its validity as a longitudinal marker of migraine activity, although its 90-day resolution limits temporal precision and likely biases associations toward the null.

Finally, although findings may not be generalizable to all geographic regions, the climatic profile of Be'er Sheva, marked by recurrent heatwaves, dust storms, and anthropogenic pollution, is increasingly shared by many areas worldwide in the context of climate change. Projections indicate that exposure to extreme heat events is expected to rise substantially in the upcoming decades, potentially expanding the populations experiencing weather patterns similar to those examined in this study.50 Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as characterizing environmental modulation of clinically severe migraine presentations rather than overall migraine occurrence.

In conclusion, this study supports a layered model of migraine activity in which environmental exposures act both as acute triggers and as modulators of brain susceptibility. By linking real-world exposures to mechanisms of hyperexcitability, hypothalamic dysregulation, TRP activation, and trigeminovascular sensitization, our findings extend the concept of migraine thresholds beyond biology to the lived environment. Clinically, these results highlight opportunities for anticipatory prevention; aligning behavioral strategies, short-term prophylaxis, and acute treatment with forecasted high-risk exposure periods.

At the same time, because migraine activity was captured through emergency health care encounters and pharmacy data, the findings primarily reflect clinically severe presentations and may not generalize to milder or self-managed migraine episodes. Future studies incorporating diary-based outcomes, wearable data, or individual-level exposure assessment will be essential to validate and extend this layered framework across the full spectrum of migraine activity. As climate change intensifies the frequency of heatwaves, dust storms, and pollution episodes, integrating environmental risk into precision neurology will be essential for patient-centered care. This layered perspective reframes migraine management from a reactive treatment approach to a proactive, risk-based, and time-sensitive intervention.
