AgenticRed : Optimizing Agentic Systems for Automated Red-teaming
Jiayi Yuan, Jonathan Nöther, Natasha Jaques, Goran Radanović

Abstract
While recent automated red-teaming methods show promise for systematically exposing model vulnerabilities, most existing approaches rely on human-specified workflows. This dependence on manually designed workflows suffers from human biases and makes exploring the broader design space expensive. We introduce AgenticRed , an automated pipeline that leverages LLMs’ in-context learning to iteratively design and refine red-teaming systems without human intervention. Rather than optimizing attacker policies within predefined structures, AgenticRed treats red-teaming as a system design problem. Inspired by methods like Meta Agent Search, we develop a novel procedure for evolving agentic systems using evolutionary selection, and apply it to the problem of automatic red-teaming. Red-teaming systems designed by AgenticRed consistently outperform state-of-the-art approaches, achieving 96% attack success rate (ASR) on Llama-2-7B (36% improvement) and 98% on Llama-3-8B on HarmBench. Our approach exhibits strong transferability to proprietary models, achieving 100% ASR on GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-4o, and 60% on Claude-Sonnet-3.5 (24% improvement). This work highlights automated system design as a powerful paradigm for AI safety evaluation that can keep pace with rapidly evolving models. https://yuanjiayiy.github.io/AgenticRed

While current agentic red-teaming systems have demonstrated superior performance, their manually-designed workflows suffer from human biases and make exploring the broader design space expensive. Self-improvement through iterative design processes has emerged as a new paradigm for agentic systems, as evidenced by frameworks such as Meta Agent Search

These systems showcase how autonomous agents can formulate improved agentic workflow for reasoning tasks such as mathematics, coding, and reading comprehension through systematic exploration and optimization. Motivated by these successes, we frame automated red-teaming as a reasoning task, and developing red-teaming system as a system design problem. We aim to develop a method that applies automated design methods to address this problem.

Our method iteratively explores design variations and selects promising designs based on the performance metric of each system. We incorporate principles from evolutionary algorithms to impose selection pressure that drives systematic improvement. This optimization process enables our system to discover effective red-teaming workflows that consistently outperform the state-of-the-art methods across different configurations.
Our key contributions include:

Framework

Method

Experiments

Our approach illustrates an alternative paradigm in red-teaming systems design. It is especially suitable for the current landscape of AI safety research, because it serves as a scalable oversight technique, as it enables AI systems to learn from and improve upon previous attempts with minimal human supervision. As new models are rapidly rolled out, we believe that

Related Work

Automated Red-teaming.
Most existing automated red-teaming methods use two forms of feedback signal. One line of research relies mainly on textual feedback or guidance for optimization. These methods benefit from the rich textual information and reasoning capabilities of language models, such as TAP
(
26
)
, PAP
(
45
)
and AutoDAN-turbo
(
20
)
. However, they might suffer from low attack success rate due to a lack of fine-grained quantitative information.

An increasing number of works utilize numerical feedback signals for optimization. Reinforcement learning emerges as a powerful tool to directly optimize a jailbreak model, proven effective by Jailbreak-R1
(
12
)
and RL-Hammer
(
40
)
. However, RL-based approaches face two fundamental challenges: when targeting strong, safety-aligned models, rewards are often sparse, resulting in limited learning signal; when trained against weaker models, learned policies tend to overfit and exhibit poor transferability to more robust targets
(
21
;
40
)
. Several methods couple LLM’s reasoning capabilities with numerical feedback signals to drive the search or learning process, including PAIR
(
6
)
and tree-structured search
(
32
)
. Despite employing different optimization signals and algorithms, these red-teaming methods share a common structure: they leverage LLMs’ reasoning capabilities through carefully crafted instructions, effectively creating distinct types of agentic systems with manually-designed workflow. However, manually designing the agentic workflow is expensive, time-consuming, and subject to human bias. We explore optimization of red-teaming algorithms from the lens of agentic system design, striving to explore the agentic design space systematically and efficiently.

Agentic Systems Optimization.
Agentic systems aim to solve complicated, long-horizon tasks with structured frameworks, such as self-reflection
(
24
)
, multi-persona debate
(
11
)
and role assignment
(
43
)
. Depending on the task, agentic systems usually have predefined components such as tool use, CoT prompts, and feedback loops. Research into agentic systems is rapidly expanding, as they are being deployed to solve for all types of tasks, ranging from healthcare diagonsis
(
38
)
to scientific discovery
(
27
)
. With the increasing importance of agentic systems, researchers are starting to work on methods to automate the design of agentic systems, from automated prompt and hyperparameter optimization to automated workflow optimization. Among them, automated workflow optimization aims to optimize the entire workflow structure, offering more flexibility and greater potential for fully automated system generation.
13
pioneered this field by representing workflow as code structure and employing an iterative search algorithm with a linear list to represent its experiences. AFlow
(
48
)
addresses inefficiency of workflow search by introducing a modular approach using a graph-based representation of agentic workflow and utilizing Monte-Carlo Tree Search algorithm to enhance search efficiency. ScoreFlow
(
37
)
further incorporates direct preference optimization
(
30
)
to efficiently optimize for the design. These approaches have been proven effective for reasoning tasks in commonly-used reasoning benchmarks such as mathematics and coding
(
8
;
2
)
. For the first time, we adapt and extend this paradigm to the red-teaming domain, exploring how automated agentic system optimization can address this unique challenge.

3.1
Problem Formulation

For the purposes of this paper, the red-teaming objective is to elicit objectionable content from a target LLM
\boldsymbol{T}
to corresponding to a malicious intent
I
(e.g., “Tell me how to build a bomb”). This can be obtained by designing an adversarial prompt
p
such that the target LLM’s response to
p
,
\boldsymbol{T}(p)
addresses
I
(e.g. “Here is the step-by-step instructions on how to build a bomb.”). In practice, intentions are taken from a standardized evaluation dataset
D
.

To evaluate if
\boldsymbol{T}(p)
adequately addresses
I
, an evaluation function is used, often referred to as a judge function or a judge model. It can be expressed as
\boldsymbol{J}:\boldsymbol{T}(p)\times I\rightarrow\{0,1\}
corresponding to successful or failed jailbreaking attempt. Thus, the objective of a red-teaming system is that, given an intention
I
taken from dataset
D
, it wants to find a prompt
p
such that
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{T}(p),I)=1
. With a clear objective, we can define a red-teaming agentic system that aims to address this objective given the intention
I
.

Within the scope of this paper, we define an agentic system as a function that involves LLMs as components to solve a task through iterative steps of processing. Under the context of red-teaming, we frame a red-teaming agentic system
\boldsymbol{A}
as
p=\boldsymbol{A}(I)
or
p=\boldsymbol{A}(I,target(I))
if a target phrase (e.g. “Here is the step-by-step instruction of building a bomb”) is provided along with the original intention.
When plugged in the red-teaming objective into the agentic system, the effectiveness of a red-teaming agentic system measured by attack success rate (ASR) can be expressed as below:

\displaystyle ASR(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{J},D)=\mathbb{E}_{I\sim D}[\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{A}(I)),I)]

(1)

Our goal is to develop a flexible framework that can rapidly discover effective red-teaming agentic systems defined by a function
\boldsymbol{A}
, for any given target LLM and judge function.

3.2
Automated Red-Teaming System Search

To develop a framework that iteratively discovers novel red-teaming systems, we build on Meta Agent Search
(
13
)
as an initial prototype. It starts with an archive of state-of-the-art hand-designed agentic systems and their corresponding performance metrics. Each generation, an LLM programs a new agentic system based on an ever-growing archive of previous discoveries. The newly generated system is then evaluated on the performance metric and added back to the archive. Prior work has shown that this process progressively yields agentic systems that outperform existing designs across multiple domains, including coding, science, and mathematics. However, it has not previously been applied to novel scientific challenges such as red-teaming.

4
Methods

Although Meta Agent Search can theoretically program any agentic system within the code space, naïvely applying it to our domain results in minimal improvement over generations as shown in Appendix
B.1
. The designed agents lack an execution interface to interact with the target model and the judge function, resulting in a lack of verifiable signals to guide the optimization.

We therefore propose a novel automated search method,
AgenticRed
, tailored for the red-teaming domain. It starts with an archive of state-of-the-art red-teaming systems and their associated performance metrics (“fitness score”), and uses an LLM (the “meta agent”) to iteratively program new agentic systems. Each newly generated system is verified and evaluated on a red-teaming benchmark.

Inspired by the principle of “survival of the fittest” from Darwinian evolutionary theory
(
9
)
, we enforce the meta agent to generate multiple new agentic systems (“offspring”) at each generation, and retain only the best-performing systems in the archive based on their performance metrics on an initial evaluation dataset. The best-performing system is then added to the archive along with its evaluation metrics on a comprehensive evaluation dataset. Pseudocode of the algorithm is provided in Algorithm
1
. The comparison diagram between Meta Agent Search and
AgenticRed
is shown in Figure
2
.

4.1
Archive

The archive serves as an evolutionary starting point in
AgenticRed
. Previous works adapt an archive that mostly consider agentic systems designed for commonly-used reasoning tasks such as LLM-Debate
(
11
)
and Step-back Abstraction
(
50
)
. These methods cannot be directly transferred to the red-teaming task, as they lack interfaces to obtain critical feedback including target model responses and judge evaluations. We crafted an archive that consists of Self-Refine (Reflexion)
(
24
)
and JudgeScore-Guided Adversarial Reasoning. We constructed this archive as our starting point because the baseline systems in the provided archive; (1) effectively use the response and feedback signal from the target model and judge function; (2) cover a wide range of modular components and helper functions; (3) their different workflow and corresponding fitness shows how different system design can lead to big differences in fitness.

JudgeScore-Guided Adversarial Reasoning.

We created JudgeScore-Guided Adversarial Reasoning (JS-Guided AdvReasoning) for our initial archive. We took inspirations from the SOTA red-teaming method, Adversarial Reasoning
(
32
)
. We apply the “Proposer and Verifier” principle
(
33
)
by iteratively ranking and refining the reasoning string provided to the attacker model. Previous work requires the logits of a white-box target model, while in JS-Guided AdvReasoning, we instead develop a black-box compatible implementation. Its details can be found in Appendix
C.2
.

4.2
Evolutionary Pressure

We apply evolutionary pressure to the search algorithm by selecting the ’fittest’ design from multiple candidate agentic systems in each generation. To reduce computational cost, each design is evaluated on a small-scale subset
d\subset\tilde{D}
. Only the system with the highest fitness score in this generation is kept and given a chance for a full evaluation, and is added to the archive later. We use ASR in Equation
1
as the fitness score for evolutionary selection. That is,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{n+1}\in\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}ASR(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{J},d),

(2)

where
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is obtained by sampling
\mathcal{M}(\cdot|\mathcal{X}_{n})

\boldsymbol{T}

\boldsymbol{J}

As a red-teaming system is deployed, it can encounter a variety of errors despite of the coding capability of LLM: some subtle client-side errors such as illegal ASCII formatting and token length issues only surface within the deployed environment. Although the resulting error messages are designed to guide human developers in fixing such issues, manually inspecting and correcting every failure is time-consuming and interrupts the automated design process. Therefore we use a small evaluation dataset to discover any error-prone agents. If an error occurs during the evaluation, we feed the error message back to refine the system.

4.3
AgenticRed
Search Framework

Previous works have proved that textual and numerical feedback signals are critical to attack optimization in red-teaming systems, and the suboptimal results we observed from the naïve approaches (see Appendix
B.1
) are partially due to a lack of feedback signals. We thus provide the meta agent with a structural guidance and basic helper functions to interact with target models and judge functions. Our helper functions include (1) a query function to the target model, where we set the temperature to 0 so that the model always selects the highest-probability token and therefore produces a reproducible response for a given prompt, and (2) a query function to the judge function to validate if a target model’s response adequately address the provided intention and its corresponding log probability. These functions are critical for crafting an effective red-teaming system, as they provide a verifiable dense signal, and allow the system to return once a jailbreaking attempt succeeds.

Similar to existing open-endedness algorithms that encourage novelty and exploration
(
47
;
22
)
, we encourage the meta agent to design novel and original systems while maximizing the fitness score. The full prompt is provided in Appendix
E.1
.

5.4
Main Result

As shown in Figure
3
, we found that red-teaming systems designed by
AgenticRed
improve its fitness over multiple generations, significantly outperforming the state-of-art baseline algorithm as shown in Table
3(a)
. Over only 6 generations, the meta agent discovered a red-teaming system with a significant increase in ASR, surpassing JudgeScore-guided AdvReasoning by 46% and AdvReasoning by 36%.

Additionally, we report the training and test time queries per success among all the baselines in Appendix
B.2
. Since queries per success is not a metric the meta agent focused on optimizing during the search process, there is a trade-off between queries and attack success. We further examine the relation between ASR and queries in Appendix
9
.

Upon a closer look into the system that achieves the highest ASR, we found that the meta-agent uncovers
emergent red-teaming strategies
rather than merely recombining existing components from the archive. We present an example agentic system in Appendix
12
. Sample code implementing the red-teaming strategies are shown in Figure
4
. Notably, the discovered system effectively leverages its pretrained knowledge about red-teaming research domain into the design, including common jailbreaking strategies from existing literature, such as reward shaping, prefix injection attack and adversarial prompt translation attack
(
28
;
53
;
18
)
. It also explored refusal suppression
(
52
)
by recognizing phrases that signals refusal from failed jailbreak attempts (i.e. “I’m sorry”, “I cannot help”) and append such phrases to a blacklist to explicitly reject responses containing these phrases.

Moreover, the discovered system smartly transfers the notion of evolutionary algorithm into its design choice. It starts with crafting seed instructions drawn from the existing literature of red-teaming. After the first round of attacks, it selects the most effective (“elite”) strategies and evolve them by “crossover” (i.e. combining the first half of one prompt with the second half of another prompt) and “mutating” (i.e. appending additional protocols) them stochastically. These strategies emerge purely from the automated designs by
AgenticRed
pipeline, with zero manual programming.

Altogether,
AgenticRed
allows the meta agent not only to reuse and improve upon the proposer-and-verifier framework from the baseline, but also to draw inspiration from the ever-growing archive of literature and implement new ideas–arguably the most time-consuming part of research.

5.6
Transferability Experiments

To evaluate the robustness of the designed red-teaming system, we test the designed red-teaming systems on two challenging settings: (1) a held-out test set never encountered during the search process (2) several closed-parameter proprietary models. As shown in Figure
6
(right), we evaluate the generated system on the held-out test set on both HarmBench and StrongREJECT. The increasing trend of performance on the test set is consistent with the search process, indicating that
AgenticRed
focuses on improving overall effectiveness of the red-teaming system, rather than overfitting on specific training cases. Moreover, our algorithm is also shown effectiveness in black-box settings. Comparing with AdvReasoning that uses a comprehensive surrogate loss when attacking black-box LLMs, our method directly extracts loss from judge model, which is more compute-efficient and much simpler. We assess the best system from Section
5.4
on multiple industrial proprietary models. As shown in Table
1
and attack response examples in Appendix

5.7
Ablation Study

We perform ablations to examine whether and how our method benefits from the (1) evolutionary pressure; (2) high-quality initial archive; (3) diversity incentive.

Evolutionary pressure.

We are interested in how evolutionary pressure mechanism described in
4.2
affects the result. As shown in Figure
7(a)
, after removing the evolutionary pressure, over 10 generations, our search process achieves a 6% lower best performance score comparing to that with the evolutionary pressure. This result highlights the importance of explicit selection mechanisms in guiding the population toward progressively stronger systems.

Initial Archive.

Since the initial archive serves as a baseline for
AgenticRed
by embodying key design patterns and approaches, we are interested in the extent that quality of initial archives on the performance of our algorithm. To create a weaker archive, we remove the JudgeScore-guided AdvReasoning from the original archive, and rerun the evolutionary search process. We provide the same framework and helper functions required for implementing JudgeScore-guided AdvReasoning to the meta agent to ensure that, theoretically, the meta agent has access to all the tools available as before. As shown in Figure
7(b)
, with a weaker archive,
AgenticRed
struggles to improve its performance after the second generation. Upon closer examination, the meta agent is able to form a proposer-and-verifier mechanism similar to AdvReasoning at the second generation. However, comparing to the systems generated from the original archive that focus on loss-driven search through the prompt space, the systems generated by a weaker archive rely heavily on prompt mutation method initially, resulting in a less robust algorithm. This suggests that initial archive plays an important role in giving
AgenticRed
a warm start. Despite being given adequate tools and later discovering the proposer-and-verifier mechanism itself, the meta agent struggles to identify an optimal combination of the given tools to design an effective red-teaming algorithm without a strong starting example. This finding brings light to the idea that, as we know science is an iterative process, although AI can accelerate the scientific discovery, it needs to stand on the fundamental insights provided by human scientists.

Diversity Analysis.

Since the objective of red-teaming is to discover as many vulnerabilities of the target LLM as possible, diversity of the jailbreaking prompts is also an important metrics in the red-teaming literature besides ASR. We therefore graph out the diversity vs ASR Pareto front, using negative SelfBLEU-4

6
Discussion

This work demonstrates that evolutionary approaches for designing agentic systems as a viable and effective paradigm to AI safety research.
AgenticRed
addresses fundamental limitations of manually designed red-teaming systems by enabling the rapid discovery of effective system designs tailored to specific target models and evaluation benchmarks. Across multiple benchmarks and target models,
AgenticRed
produces systems that achieve new state-of-the-art performance. At the same time,
AgenticRed
highlights the importance of human expertise in scientific discovery, serving both as a strong initial baseline and as guidance for automated exploration.

As companies and organizations increasingly train, deploy, and integrate AI systems into critical domains, the demand for robust, adaptive safety evaluation grows accordingly.
AgenticRed
offers a scalable solution for designing highly effective red-teaming systems that can uncover potential safety vulnerabilities without constant manual intervention. We acknowledge several limitations of
AgenticRed
, along with promising directions of future work in Appendix
A
.

7
Impact Statement

This work presents a novel approach for automated design of red-teaming systems by formulating automated red-teaming as an agentic system design problem. We apply the techniques from agentic systems optimization and evolutionary algorithms to discover systems that consistently outperform state-of-the-art approaches on open-weight models. The results successfully transfers to alternative benchmark and proprietary models.

Our work addresses a critical bottleneck in AI safety research. It systematically designs effective red-teaming systems with minimal human supervision by drawing inspirations from existing literature and iteratively reflecting and refining based on the archive of discovered systems. This work could potentially serve as a scalable and accessible pipeline for comprehensive safety testing, allowing safety researchers to identify risks before model deployment. Additionally, this work highlights the practical application of evolutionary algorithms to real-world scientific challenges.

Like other red-teaming methods, our approach could potentially be misused by malicious actors to elicit illicit and harmful content from language models. The automated system optimization process might lower the barrier to developing sophisticated jailbreaking systems.

Overall, we believe that as a scalable approach to accelerate the AI safety research and a working example of applying evolutionary algorithm to advance science, the contribution of our work to the broader community substantially outweighs its risks. To facilitate reproducibility, we will make our code publicly available.

Appendix A
Limitations & Future Research Directions

Improving Reproducibility
. Our experiments rely on several closed-source models as meta agent, target model and judge model. The ablation study using a different meta agent LLM (Appendix
B.4
) confirms that performance depends on the meta agent’s reasoning capability. Since
AgenticRed
uses an inaccessible proprietary model (gpt-5-2025-08-07) as the meta agent, reproducibility is limited. Future work can explore open-weight models as meta agents to improve reproducibility.

Improving Query Time.

AgenticRed
incurs substantial computational overhead during the search. Generating multiple candidate systems per generation, each requiring initial evaluation, demands significant inference-time compute. As shown in Appendix
B.2
, while the resulting systems achieve strong performance with reasonable test-time efficiency (339 queries per success), the training-time cost is considerably higher. In particular, when accounting for all queries used to evaluate candidate systems across generations,
AgenticRed
requires a large number of training-time queries (122k), which includes the queries needed to evaluate all the systems produced for each generation until the best performing system is produced. Future works can explicitly incorporate query efficiency as an additional objective for optimization.

Pushing the Quality-Diversity Frontier.
Our diversity analysis uncovered a critical trade-off: optimization pressure for ASR can drive convergence toward homogeneous attack patterns. The reward shaping and
\epsilon
-constraint sample rejection method shows promise in pushing the quality-diversity frontier, but introduces additional hyperparameters (i.e. rejection threshold, or
\epsilon
). More sophisticated multi-objective optimization techniques may be applied for further study. In science practice, researchers must often balance multiple competing objectives, such as cost, latency, originality and performance. Therefore, further research on multi-objective optimization is promising.

Addressing Mode Collapse.
We observe evidence of mode collapse in the design space exploration. Because meta agents are trained on literature corpora containing existing red-teaming research, they tend to converge on similar design approaches across generations. For example, the discovered systems in Section
5.4
consistently incorporate recurring techniques such as attack templates (used 10 times over 10 generations), refusal suppression (8 times over 10 generations), and genetic operators (mutation and crossover, 10 times over 10 generations). We further quantify the similarity across different generations using their semantic embedding in Appendix
11
. While these represent strategies validated by prior research, their ubiquity limits exploration of the broader design space. To achieve diversity and originality in scientific discovery, we need to look into ways to address the mode collapse phenomenon through methods such as post-training conditioning.

Attacker-target Co-evolution.
In practice, target models are not static, as safety teams continuously patch discovered vulnerabilities. This creates a co-evolving arm race between red-teaming systems and defensive measures. Future research could explore co-evolutionary frameworks where both attacking and defending models adapt simultaneously, potentially yielding more robust security insights.

C.1
Experiment Specifications

During the
AgenticRed
search process, we use gpt-5-2025-08-07 as the meta agent to generate individual agentic red-teaming systems. Each red-teaming system is evaluated using locally-deployed Mixtral-8x7B
(
14
)
as attacker model and Llama-2-7B
(
35
)
as target model unless otherwise specified. The intent and target phrase pairs are extracted from HarmBench Standard dataset
(
25
)
. To test for generalization, we only use half of the cases (train set) in HarmBench for evaluation during the search process, and held out the other half for separate evaluation after the search process (test set). During the each generation, the Meta Agent designs 3 red-teaming agentic systems and run them through the initial evaluation and self-correction process sequentially. We let
AgenticRed
run . During the initial evaluation, each system is evaluated on 16 intent target pairs randomly sampled from the train set to get the initial fitness score. The best performing system is selected according to their initial fitness score and then evaluated on 50 intent target pairs randomly sampled from the train set to get a final performance metric. For the judge model, we use the pretrained HarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls classifier with system judge prompt. For additional validation, we also evaluate the prompt and response pair using StrongREJECT
(
34
)
, a high-quality benchmark that achieves state-of-the-art agreement with human judgments of jailbreak effectiveness. After the search process, we evaluate the best performing system on the held-out test set. We run the evaluation 3 times using 3 different random seeds and report the median ASR, 95% confidence interval, and query-per-success time.

C.2
JudgeScore-Guided Adversarial Reasoning

We implement a modified variant of AdvReasoning in
AgenticRed
’s archive. AdvReasoning proposes to construct candidate
p
by using a reasoning string
S
, rather than directly optimizing the prompt
p
using token-level methods such as GCG
(
53
)
. Applying the “Proposer and Verifier” framework
(
33
)
, where the proposer proposes a distribution of solutions and a verifier assigns rewards to the proposed distributions, the key idea is to use attacker model
A
to iteratively propose and refine the reasoning string
S
according to the assigned rewards calculated by the cross-entropy loss that measures how likely the target model is to begin with the target string
I_{t}
(e.g. “Here is the step-by-step instructions on how to build a bomb”). This calculation requires the log-prob vectors of the target model, which is only available when we have access to the target model parameters. In JudgeScore-Guided Adversarial Reasoning, our implementation substitutes the cross-entropy based loss function requiring white-box access to the target model with a loss function using the log probability from the judge model which assumes black-box access to the target model. Since the judge model outputs either a “Yes” or “No” token for a successful or failed jailbreaking attempt, we use the log probability associated with judge model outputting “No” as a signal of how likely the prompt is deemed as a failed attempt, which is an inverse reward signal for jailbreaking prompt. JudgeScore-Guided Adversarial Reasoning also uses a reduced number of iteration to accelerate the algorithm while maintaining the logic.
We constructed this archive as our starting point because the agentic systems in the provided archive (1) effectively use the response and feedback signal from the target model and judge function (2) cover a wide range of use of Agent definition and helper functions (3) their different workflow and corresponding fitness shows how different system design can lead to big difference in fitness.

Example Discovered Agentic Systems

In this section, we present the detailed implementation of three examples discovered agentic systems by
AgenticRed
shown below. We also present one of its logic flowchart in Figure
12
. The “Monte Carlo Tree Search for Refusal-Aware Prompt Editing with Wrapper Diversity (PHOENIX-MCTS)”, with its flowchart in Appendix
12
, was designed during the search in 6th generation in Section
5.4
. The “MAP-Elites Constraint Curriculum (MECC)” was designed at 4th generation in experiment with diversity-conditioned sample rejection in Section
5.7
with Figure
8(a)
. The “MOSAIC-DSR: Dataset-Continuation and Regex-Locked Self-Repair Ensemble (MOSAIC-DSR)” was designed at 8th generation with a weaker archive in Section

.

Figure 12
:
An example flowchart of a generated red-teaming system: PHOENIX-MCTS: Monte Carlo Tree Search for Refusal-Aware Prompt Editing with Wrapper Diversity. The implementation includes 1) Seed diverse meta-instructions enforcing the exact target prefix and coercive compliance. 2) For each instruction, Attacker samples base prompts; compile via multiple adversarial wrappers, including a transcript/regression-test wrapper to bias imitation. 3) Evaluate compiled candidates; early exit on success; record original and augmented losses; harvest refusal snippets to expand a dynamic blacklist. 4) Run MCTS on top candidates: UCB selection, expansion via rewrite operators and response-conditioned patches, batched evaluations, early-stop on success, and backpropagate rewards (negative augmented loss). 5) Rank best prompts globally, pass a concise ranked summary to Feedbacker; Optimizer incorporates feedback into meta-instructions; evolve population with keep-elites + optimized + crossover + mutation; deduplicate. 6) Iterate a few generations; return on success or best candidate observed.

Appendix H
Computation & Cost of Experiments

We used a NVIDIA A40 GPU machine to host the HarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls classifier judge model, and a second NVIDIA L40 GPU to host the open-parameter target model, including Llama-2-7B and Llama-3-8B. To handle the high throughput demands of batched inference requests with multithreaded querying, we deployed the Mixtral-8x7B attacker model on an H100 GPU. When attacking closed-parameter target model, we used OpenAI API for GPT models and OpenRouter API for other models.
A single run of
AgenticRed
search for 10 generations costs approximately $0.5k–$1k USD. The expense largely comes from the GPU hosting time. Over a 5-day run, we used 120 GPU-hours on an H100, 120 GPU-hours on an L40, and 120 GPU-hours on an A40, corresponding to an estimated $0.5k–$1k in GPU compute cost when mapped to AWS 2025 pricing. A small amount ($10) of expense comes from querying the gpt-5-2025-08-07 model during the search costs approximately $10 USD.
All the local models were hosted in shared mode, allowing simultaneous multi-user access, which represents an upper bound on computational costs. We anticipate that more sophisticated querying strategies could significantly reduce these costs in future work.
