Quantum Algorithms for Stochastic Differential Equations: A Schrödingerisation Approach
Shi Jin, Nana Liu, Wei Wei

Abstract
Quantum computers are known for their potential to achieve up-to-exponential speedup compared to classical computers for certain problems. To exploit the advantages of quantum computers, we propose quantum algorithms for linear stochastic differential equations, utilizing the Schrödingerisation method for the corresponding approximate equation by treating the noise term as a (discrete-in-time) forcing term. Our algorithms are applicable to stochastic differential equations with both Gaussian noise and α \alpha -stable Lévy noise. The gate complexity of our algorithms exhibits an 𝒪 ⁡ ( d ​ log ⁡ ( N ​ d ) ) \mathcal{O}(d\log(Nd)) dependence on the dimensions d d and sample sizes N N , where its corresponding classical counterpart requires nearly exponentially larger complexity in scenarios involving large sample sizes. In the Gaussian noise case, we show the strong convergence of first order in the mean square norm for the approximate equations. The algorithms are numerically verified for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes, geometric Brownian motions, and one-dimensional Lévy flights. Introduction

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) finds applications in various fields including physics, climate modeling, finance and machine learning
[
35
,
46
,
50
]
. Their broad applicability stems from their ability to encapsulate the randomness in nature, providing insights into the dynamics of complex systems with uncertainty. For example, the stochastic Langevin equation and its variants are widely applied in physics and machine learning, including generative AI
[
14
,
16
,
24
,
46
]
. Another important application of SDEs is in Monte Carlo simulations, where SDEs are used to model and simulate random processes over time.

For many applications, it becomes interesting to develop quantum algorithms to leverage the potential advantage offered by quantum computers. Quantum computers, which are different from their classical counterparts, use qubits instead of bits.
Some of the quantum algorithms have already proved to have quantum advantages over their classical counterparts in, for example, searching
[
20
]
, cryptography
[
45
]
, sampling
[
1
]
, solving linear algebraic equations
[
12
,
21
]
and etc. Quantum algorithms also have applications in computational finance, which is also closely related to SDEs
[
19
,
36
,
43
]
.

There are quantum algorithms for stochastic processes that have demonstrated quantum advantage. For example,
as an analogue of classical random walk, the quantum walk achieves up-to exponential speedup
[
11
]
and can serve as a universal computational primitive
[
10
]
for simulating arbitrary quantum circuits. It finds wide applications in Hamiltonian simulation, quantum information processing, and search algorithms
[
47
]
.
Quantum algorithms have also been proposed for Markov Chain Monte Carlo simulation, commonly used to sample from the Boltzmann distribution
[
38
]
. They potentially ease the computational bottlenecks posed by this sampling problem and achieve quantum speedup in optimization
[
44
]
. Quantum algorithms can also provide nearly quadratic speedup to Monte Carlo methods for computing expected values of random variables
[
4
,
41
,
8
]
.
The Lindbladian equation, which describes an open quantum system, can be interpreted as the master equation for the reduced density matrix of a quantum stochastic differential equation. Classical numerical schemes for SDEs also enable quantum computing methods for the Lindbladian equation to achieve arbitrarily high-order accuracy
[
15
]
.

Although classical schemes for SDEs, such as the Euler-Maruyama scheme, have been proposed for decades and are widely used in various areas, the quantum algorithms for SDEs are underdeveloped.
The motivation for designing quantum algorithms for SDEs arises from the fact that, while simulating a single sample path incurs only a manageable computational cost, achieving a target precision
\epsilon
with the Monte Carlo method requires at least
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{2})
simulations, leading to substantial computational expense
[
4
]
.
Several quantum algorithms have been proposed for SDEs and Monte Carlo simulations. Reference
[
36
]
develops a quantum-classical hybrid algorithm based on variational quantum simulation. It discretizes the SDE into a trinomial tree model and computes the nodes of the tree by time-evolution of a quantum state. The nodes of the tree of each level represent the distribution of the SDE at each time step. However, the tree model itself suffers from insufficient accuracy. Reference
[
41
]
guarantees the near-quadratic speedup of quantum computers in the sampling process of Monte Carlo methods. Based on this, reference
[
4
]
develops quantum algorithms for multi-level Monte Carlo simulation with simulation of SDEs. However, it is developed based on oracles for the expectation of SDEs, and does not offer a concrete quantum algorithm.
Reference
[
15
]
uses the relationship between open quantum systems, SDEs, and Hamiltonian simulations to develop high order quantum algorithms for the open quantum system.
Thus, quantum algorithms for SDEs that both match the accuracy of classical schemes and offers greater efficiency, are of significant interest.

Recently, a Schrödingerisation approach
[
32
,
30
]
has been proposed to solve general linear ordinary and partial differential equations via Hamiltonian simulation, which can also be implemented in quantum circuits
[
22
,
31
]
and applicable to a wide variety of differential equations
[
23
,
27
,
29
,
28
,
33
]
. The method of Schrödingerisation involves the incorporation of an auxiliary variable to transform a linear differential equation into an equation analogous to the Schrödinger equation, with unitary evolution process, in one higher dimension. Subsequently, the transformed equation lends itself to implementation on quantum computers through Hamiltonian simulation. The desired result is recovered from the Schrödingerised equation on a properly chosen interval of the auxiliary variable. This method maintains the major structure of the original classical differential equations, thus, consequently, one can develop quantum algorithms that retain the convergence rate of classical schemes while leveraging the benefits of quantum computing.

In this paper, we propose a general quantum algorithm to simulate the solutions of linear SDEs, including SDEs with additive noise and multiplicative noise. The main idea is to treat the noise term as a discrete-in-time forcing term and then uses the Schrödingerisation method for inhomogeneous differential equations
[
29
]
. While most quantum algorithms focus on SDEs with Gaussian noise, our approach is applicable to SDEs driven by stable Lévy processes, which is a more general type of noise. We design the quantum algorithms for the approximate equation through the Schrödingerisation method, which approximates the corresponding SDEs under mean square norm in the Gaussian noise case. We also discuss the important issue of the choice of the recovery interval and its influence on the precision of the final result. Theoretically, for both the additive and multiplicative Gaussian noise cases, we prove the strong convergence of first order for the approximate solution.
The quantum advantage of our algorithm is exhibited by its gate complexity of
\mathcal{O}(d\log(Nd))
dependence on the dimensions
d
and the sample size
N
, which is a nearly exponential speedup compared to its classical counterparts.
We illustrate the validation of the algorithms to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes, geometric Brownian motions and one-dimensional Lévy flights by numerical experiments.

This paper is organized as follows: Section
2
introduces the approximate equations for both SDEs with additive noise and multiplicative noise. By the Schödingerisation method, we provide the technical details of the quantum algorithms for SDEs. We give the gate complexity of our quantum algorithm in subsection
2.4
and provide a complexity analysis which demonstrates the quantum advantage for dimensions and sample sizes. In Section
3
, we show the correctness of our method by numerical experiments for a one dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, a one dimensional geometric Brownian motion and a one dimensional Lévy flight. In Section
4
, we show the convergence rate of the approximate equations in the mean square sense. Together with the error of the recovery part, we give the overall error of our algorithms. We summarize our method and discuss further applications in Section
5
.

Quantum algorithms for SDEs

In this section, we will introduce the quantum algorithms for linear SDEs with Gaussian noise. The algorithms are based on the combination of the Euler-Maruyama scheme and the Schrödingerisation technique
[
32
,
30
]
.

2.1
Linear SDEs with additive Gaussian noise

Given a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, we consider the following
d
-dimensional SDE:

dX(t)=AX(t)\,dt+B\,dW_{t},\quad X(0)=x_{0},\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

(1)

Here,
W_{t}
is the
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion.
A
and
B
are
d\times d
matrices.

The Euler-Maruyama scheme for equation (
1
) is described as follows.

Let
t_{k}=kT/N_{T},\Delta t=T/N_{T}
, and
\{\widehat{X}(t_{k})\}_{0\leq k\leq N}
be the numerical solution to (
1
):

\widehat{X}(t_{k+1})=\widehat{X}(t_{k})+A\widehat{X}(t_{k})\Delta t+B\Delta W_{k},\quad\widehat{X}(t_{0})=x_{0}\textrm{ and }k=0,1,\dots,N_{T}-1

(2)

where
\Delta W_{k}=W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_{k}}
is a Gaussian random variable with mean
0
and covariance matrix
\Delta tI
.

We first replace the discrete time in the Euler-Maruyama method with a continuous time variable, so it becomes an ordinary differential equation for each time interval. More precisely, for the temporal mesh
\{t_{k}=k\Delta t:\Delta t=T/N_{T},k=0,1,\cdots,N_{T}\}
, we aim to calculate the solution of the following ordinary differential equation in the
k
-th iteration:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{X}(t)=A\widetilde{X}(t)+\frac{B\Delta W_{k}}{\Delta t},\quad t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1},\quad k\geq 1,\\
&\textrm{with }\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\textrm{ calculated in the }(k-1)\textrm{-th iteration.}\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

Here
\widetilde{X}(t_{0})=x_{0}
and
\Delta W_{k}=W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_{k}}
which can be simulated by a group of independent
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian random variables
\{\bm{\xi}_{k}:\bm{\xi}_{k}\sim N(0,\bm{I})\}
:

\Delta W_{k}\overset{d}{=}\sqrt{\Delta t}\bm{\xi_{k}}.

Now we can implement the Schrödingerisation approach
[
30
]
to (
3
). Take

\widetilde{A}_{k}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\Delta W_{k}/\sqrt{\Delta t}\\
\bm{0}^{\top}&0\end{array}\right),\quad\widetilde{Y}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widetilde{X}(t)\\
1/\sqrt{\Delta t}\end{array}\right),

then equation (
3
) becomes,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{Y}(t)&=\widetilde{A}_{k}\widetilde{Y}(t),\quad t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1},\\
\widetilde{Y}(0)&=(x_{0},1/\sqrt{\Delta t})^{\top}.\end{aligned}\right.

(4)

For every
\widetilde{A}_{k}
, we decompose it into a Hermitian matrix
H_{1,k}
and an anti-Hermite matrix
iH_{2,k}
.

\widetilde{A}_{k}=H_{1,k}+iH_{2,k},

where

H_{1,k}=\frac{\widetilde{A}_{k}+\widetilde{A}_{k}^{\dagger}}{2}=H_{1,k}^{{\dagger}},\quad H_{2,k}=\frac{\widetilde{A}_{k}-\widetilde{A}_{k}^{\dagger}}{2i}=H_{2,k}^{{\dagger}}.

Apply the warped phase transformation to
\widetilde{Y}
, that is taking
\bm{v}(t,p)=e^{-p}\widetilde{Y}(t)
for
p>0
. Then,
\bm{v}(t,p)
satisfies the
(d+1)
-dimensional equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\bm{v}(t,p)&=-H_{1,k}\partial_{p}\bm{v}(t,p)+iH_{2,k}\bm{v}(t,p),\quad t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1},\\
\bm{v}(0,p)&=e^{-|p|}\widetilde{Y}(0),\quad p\in(-\infty,\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

By taking the Fourier transform of
\bm{v}(t,p)
with respect to
p
and denoting it as
w(t,\eta)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}w(t,\eta)&=-i\eta H_{1,k}w(t,\eta)+iH_{2,k}w(t,\eta),\quad t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1},\\
w(0,\eta)&=\frac{1}{\pi(1+\eta^{2})}\widetilde{Y}(0).\end{aligned}\right.

(6)

Here we have derived an equation (
6
) analogous to the Schrödinger equation.

Let
H_{1}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{T}-1}H_{1,k}\mathbbm{1}_{[t_{k},t_{k+1})}(t)
.
If all eigenvalues of
H_{1}(t)
are negative, the solution
\widetilde{Y}(t)
can be restored by the integration:

\widetilde{Y}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{v}(t,p)dp.

If
H_{1}(t)
contains some positive eigenvalues, which will be the case with the noise term, then
\widetilde{Y}(t)
can still be restored through the normalized integration formula:

\widetilde{Y}(t)=\frac{\int_{p^{*}}^{p^{*}+R}\bm{v}(t,p)dp}{\int_{p^{*}}^{p^{*}+R}e^{-p}dp},

(7)

where
p^{*}+R>p^{*}\geq\lambda^{+}_{\text{max}}(H_{1})T
, with
\lambda_{\max}^{+}(H_{1})=\max\{\sup_{0<t<T}\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(H_{1}(t)),\lambda>0\},0\}
[
29
, Theorem 3.1]
.

In Section
4
, we will show in Proposition
4.7
that the mean square norm between
\widetilde{X}
and
X
satisfies

\|\widetilde{X}(T)-X(T)\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\Delta t.

Remark 2.1
.

Equation (
6
) can be seen as a piecewise constant time-dependent Schrödinger equation with Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}(t)
defined by

\widetilde{H}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{T}-1}\big(\eta H_{1,k}-H_{2,k}\big)\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{k},t_{k+1}]}(t),

where
H_{1,k}
and
H_{2,k}
are linear Hermitian random matrix. The random matrix
H_{1,k}
and
H_{2,k}
have the following explicit expression:

H_{1,k}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{\dagger})&\frac{1}{2}B\Delta W_{k}/\sqrt{\Delta t}\\
\frac{1}{2}(\Delta W_{k}^{\top}/\sqrt{\Delta t})B^{\dagger}&0\end{array}\right),\quad H_{2,k}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{2i}(A-A^{\dagger})&-\frac{i}{2}B\Delta W_{k}/\sqrt{\Delta t}\\
\frac{i}{2}(\Delta W_{k}^{\top}/\sqrt{\Delta t})B^{\dagger}&0\end{array}\right).

Remark 2.2
.

The Schrödingerisation approach can also be applied to linear SDEs driven by
\alpha
-stable Lévy processes with
1<\alpha<2
. Specifically, consider a
d
-dimensional linear SDE driven by isotropic
\alpha
-stable Lévy processes:

dX(t)=AX(t)\,dt+BdL^{\alpha}_{t},\quad X(0)=x_{0},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

(8)

where
L_{t}^{\alpha}
is a
d
-dimensional isotropic
\alpha
-stable Lévy process with
1<\alpha<2
and it satisfies
L^{\alpha}_{t}\overset{d}{=}t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}L_{1}
. Then we can proceed with the approximate equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{X}(t)=A\widetilde{X}(t)+\frac{B\Delta L^{\alpha}_{k}}{\Delta t},\quad t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1},\quad k\geq 1,\\
&\textrm{with }\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\textrm{ calculated in the }(k-1)\textrm{-th iteration.}\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

Here
X(t_{0})=x_{0}
and
\Delta L^{\alpha}_{k}=L^{\alpha}_{t_{k+1}}-L^{\alpha}_{t_{k}}\overset{d}{=}\Delta t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}L_{1}
are independent with identical stable distribution. Take random variables
\bm{\xi}_{k}\overset{d}{=}L_{1}
. We can solve (
9
) by the same procedure for SDEs with additive Gaussian noise by replacing the matrix
\widetilde{A}_{k}
with

\widetilde{A}_{k}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\Delta t^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\bm{\xi}_{k}\\
0&0\end{array}\right),\quad\widetilde{Y}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widetilde{X}(t)\\
\frac{1}{\Delta t}\end{array}\right).

Here, the one stage approximation of the approximate equation (
9
) is the same as the Euler-Maruyama scheme for the SDE (
8
). And the Euler-Maruyama scheme itself is proven to admit strong rate of convergence in this case, see
[
37
]
. For
0<\alpha<1
, the moments of the
\alpha
-stable Lévy process do not exist, hence we do not consider this case in this paper.
In Section
3
, we present a numerical example of a one dimensional Lévy flight.

2.2
Linear SDEs with multiplicative Gaussian noise

We consider the following SDE:

dX(t)=AX(t)dt+\sum_{l=1}^{m}B^{(l)}X(t)dW^{(l)}_{t},\quad X_{0}=x_{0}.

(10)

Here,
W_{t}=(W_{t}^{(1)},\cdots,W_{t}^{(m)})
is a
m
-dimensional standard Brownian motion.
\{A,B^{(1)},\cdots,B^{(m)}\}
are
d\times d
matrices.

We introduce the following approximate equation for the Schrödingerisation approach. For
t_{k}=kT/N_{T}
, we aim to compute the following ordinary differential equation,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{d\widetilde{X}(t)}{dt}&=\widetilde{A}_{k}\widetilde{X}(t),\quad\widetilde{X}(0)=x_{0}\textrm{ and }t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1}\\
\widetilde{A}_{k}&=A-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}(B^{(l)})^{2}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}B^{(l)}\frac{\Delta W^{(l)}_{k}}{\Delta t},\end{aligned}\right.

(11)

where
\Delta W^{(l)}_{k}=W^{(l)}_{t_{k+1}}-W^{(l)}_{t_{k}}
is a standard scalar Gaussian random variable. The summation
-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}(B^{(l)})^{2}
is from the correction term of the Ito’s formula.

Similarly as the additive noise case (
4
), we decompose
\widetilde{A}_{k}
into a Hermitian matrix
H_{1,k}
and an anti-Hermite matrix
iH_{2,k}
.

\widetilde{A}_{k}=H_{1,k}+iH_{2,k},

where

H_{1,k}=\frac{\widetilde{A}_{k}+\widetilde{A}_{k}^{\dagger}}{2}=H_{1,k}^{{\dagger}},\quad H_{2,k}=\frac{\widetilde{A}_{k}-\widetilde{A}_{k}^{\dagger}}{2i}=H_{2,k}^{{\dagger}}.

Through the warped phase transformation to
\widetilde{X}
to get
\bm{v}(t,p)
and the Fourier transformation on the auxiliary variable
p
to derive
w(t,\eta)
, we arrive at the equation analogous to the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}w(t,\eta)&=-i\eta H_{1,k}w(t,\eta)+iH_{2,k}w(t,\eta),\quad t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1},\\
w(0,\eta)&=\frac{1}{\pi(1+\eta^{2})}\widetilde{X}(0).\end{aligned}\right.

(12)

Then we can follow exactly the same steps as in the additive noise case to solve equation (
12
) by the Hamiltonian simulation and recover the solution through the normalized integration formula (
7
).

Different from the additive noise case, equation (
11
) is already a homogeneous system so we do not need to expand
\widetilde{X}
into a
(d+1)
-dimensional vector
Y
.

In Section
4
, we will show in Proposition
4.8
the mean square norm between
\widetilde{X}
and
X
satisfies

\|\widetilde{X}(T)-X(T)\|_{2}\lesssim\Delta t.

Remark 2.3
.

If we assume the matrices
\{B^{(1)},\cdots,B^{(m)}\}
commute, that is

B^{(k)}B^{(l)}=B^{(l)}B^{(k)},

by the definition of the matrix exponential, we get the one stage approximation

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{X}(t_{k})+\widetilde{A}_{k}\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\Delta t+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}B^{(l)}B^{(j)}\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\Delta W^{(l)}_{k}\Delta W^{(j)}_{k}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t\Delta W)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{X}(t_{k})+A\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\Delta t+\sum_{l=1}^{m}B^{(l)}\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\Delta W^{(l)}_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}(B^{(l)})^{2}\widetilde{X}(t_{k})((\Delta W^{(j)}_{k})^{2}-\Delta t)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}B^{(l)}B^{(j)}\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\Delta W^{(l)}_{k}\Delta W^{(j)}_{k}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t\Delta W),

which is the Milstein scheme(see, for example,
[
34
, Section 10.3]
) for the SDE (
10
).

If we further assume that matrices
\{A,B^{(1)},\cdots,B^{(m)}\}
commute, the analytical solution to (
10
) is (
[
34
, Section 4.8]
),

X(t)=x_{0}\exp\left[(A-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}(B^{(l)})^{2})t+\sum_{l=1}^{m}B^{(l)}W^{(l)}_{t}\right].

(13)

In this case, it is clear that

\widetilde{X}(t_{k})=x_{0}\exp\left[(A-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}(B^{(l)})^{2})t_{k}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}B^{(l)}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\Delta W^{(l)}_{j}\right],

which is exactly the sample path of (

).

2.3
Discrete numerical form

In this section, we will numerically solve the linear differential equation arose in (
4
) and (
11
), which takes the form

\frac{d\widetilde{Z}(t)}{dt}=\widetilde{A}_{k}\widetilde{Z}(t),\quad\widetilde{Z}(0)=z_{0}\textrm{ and }t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1}.

(14)

Here
\widetilde{Z}=(\widetilde{X},1/\sqrt{\Delta t})
for the additive noise case (
3
) and
\widetilde{Z}=\widetilde{X}
for the multiplicative noise case (
11
).
\tilde{d}=d+1
for the additive noise case and
\tilde{d}=d
for the multiplicative noise case.

By the warped phase transformation, we arrive to solve the linear equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\bm{v}(t,p)&=-H_{1,k}\partial_{p}\bm{v}(t,p)+iH_{2,k}\bm{v}(t,p),\quad t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1},\\
\bm{v}(0,p)&=e^{-|p|}z_{0},\quad p\in(-\infty,\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

(15)

Next, we will numerically solve equation (
15
) by the discrete Fourier transform on a sufficiently large interval
[-L,L]
so that
e^{-L}\approx 0
. We adopt the symbols in
[
30
]
and refer to
[
30
, Sec.II.A.2]
for more details.

Let
\Delta p=L/N
. For
p_{j}=-L+(j-1)\Delta p
, denote

\bm{w}(t):=[\bm{w}_{1}(t);\bm{w}_{2}(t);\cdots;\bm{w}_{2N}(t)]=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}{v}_{h}(t,p_{j})|j\rangle\otimes|h\rangle,\quad\bm{w}_{j}(t)=\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}{v}_{h}(t,p_{j})|h\rangle.

Here
v_{h}|h\rangle
is the
\tilde{d}
-dimensional vector with the
h
-th entry being the
h
-th entry
v_{h}
of
\bm{v}
and the other entries being zero.
|j\rangle
is the
j
-th computational basis corresponding to the
j
-th point in the mesh of the variable
p
. The symbol “;” indicates the straightening of
\{\bm{w}(t,p_{k})\}_{k\geq 0}
into a column vector.
Let
\widetilde{\bm{w}}
be the spectral approximation of
\bm{w}
with respect to
p
, which means

\bm{w}(t)\approx\widetilde{\bm{w}}(t)=(\Phi\otimes\bm{I})\widetilde{\bm{c}}(t).

Here,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{w}}(t):=[\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{1}(t);\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{2}(t);\cdots;\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{2N}(t)]=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}\widetilde{w}_{h}(t,p_{j})|j\rangle\otimes|h\rangle,\quad\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{j}(t)=\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}\widetilde{w}_{h}(t,p_{j})|h\rangle,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{c}}(t):=[\bm{c}_{1}(t);\bm{c}_{2}(t);\cdots;\bm{c}_{2N}(t)]=\sum_{l=1}^{2N}\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}c_{l}^{(h)}(t)|l\rangle\otimes|h\rangle,\quad\bm{c}_{l}(t)=\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}c_{l}^{(h)}(t)|h\rangle.

and

It is clear that
\Phi^{-1}
is the discrete Fourier transform on variable
p
.
Then
\widetilde{\bm{w}}
satisfies

\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bm{w}}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{T}-1}-i(P_{\bm{\mu}}\otimes H_{1,k}-\bm{I}\otimes H_{2,k})\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{k},t_{k+1}]}(t)\widetilde{\bm{w}}(t).

(16)

Here
P_{\bm{\mu}}=-i\partial_{p}
is the momentum operator and on the computational basis

P_{\bm{\mu}}\Phi|l\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\mu_{l}e^{i\mu_{l}(p_{j}+L)}|j\rangle.

A direct computation shows that
D_{\bm{\mu}}=\Phi^{-1}P_{\bm{\mu}}\Phi
with

\mu_{l}=\pi(l-N-1)/L

\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bm{c}}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{T}-1}-i(D_{\bm{\mu}}\otimes H_{1,k}-\bm{I}\otimes H_{2,k})\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{k},t_{k+1}]}(t)\widetilde{\bm{c}}(t),

which is a system of linear ordinary differential equations.
It can be rewritten as

\partial_{t}\widetilde{\bm{c}}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{T}-1}-i(\widetilde{H}_{1,k}-\widetilde{H}_{2,k})\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{k},t_{k+1}]}(t)\widetilde{\bm{c}}(t),

(17)

with

\widetilde{H}_{1,k}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}H_{1,k}&&&\\
&\mu_{2}H_{1,k}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\mu_{2N}H_{1,k}\end{pmatrix},\quad\widetilde{H}_{2,k}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{2,k}&&&\\
&H_{2,k}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&H_{2,k}\end{pmatrix}.

(18)

Denote
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
as the discrete Fourier transform on variable
p
,

\mathcal{F}|l\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{j=1}^{2N}e^{i\pi\frac{(l-1)(j-1)}{N}}|j\rangle,\quad\mathcal{F}^{-1}|j\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\sum_{l=1}^{2N}e^{-i\pi\frac{(l-1)(j-1)}{N}}|l\rangle.

Then
\Phi=\sqrt{2N}S\mathcal{F},S=\textrm{diag}([1,-1,\cdots,1,-1]_{2N\times 1})
.
Let

\widetilde{\bm{c}}(0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}((\mathcal{F}^{-1}S)\otimes\bm{I})\bm{w}(0).

(19)

We obtain
\bm{\widetilde{w}}
by

\bm{\widetilde{w}}(t_{k})=\sqrt{2N}\left((S\mathcal{F})\otimes\bm{I}\right)\widetilde{\bm{c}}(t_{k}).

(20)

Furthermore, we can also derive the discrete Fourier approximation
\bm{\widetilde{w}}^{c}
of
\bm{v}
using
\bm{\widetilde{c}}
:

\bm{\widetilde{w}}^{c}(t_{k},p)=[\widetilde{w}_{1}(t_{k},p),\cdots,\widetilde{w}_{\tilde{d}}(t_{k},p)],\quad\widetilde{w}_{h}(t_{k},p)=\sum_{l=1}^{2N}c_{l}^{(h)}(t)e^{i\mu_{l}(p+L)}.

The numerical approximation of
\widetilde{Z}
, denoted by
\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}
, is recovered by the
normalized integration method

\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}(t_{k})=\frac{\sum_{p_{j}\in U_{p}}\bm{\widetilde{w}}^{c}(t_{k},p_{j})\Delta p}{\sum_{p_{j}\in U_{p}}e^{-p_{j}}\Delta p},

(21)

where
U_{p}=[p^{*},p^{*}+R]
. Denote the recovered solution
\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}=(\widetilde{X}^{\widetilde{w}},\cdot)
for the additive noise case and
\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}=\widetilde{X}^{\widetilde{w}}
for the multiplicative noise case. In Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
, we will show the mean square error between the solution
X
and the recovered solution
\widetilde{X}^{\widetilde{w}}
.

Remark 2.4
.

In quantum computers, we can directly obtain
\{\bm{c}(t_{k})\}
by Hamiltonian simulation. Note that equation (
17
) is the system with piecewise constant Hamiltonian and thus we can simply apply schemes for time-independent Hamiltonian simulation multiple times in this case. General time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation algorithms are also applicable here, for example, the Trotterization based methods
[
2
,
3
,
49
]
and a newly proposed method by
[
9
]
which transfers the non-autonomous system, that is the time-dependent Hamiltonian system, into an autonomous system in one-higher dimension.

For a verification in classical computers, we use the second order i-stable Runge-Kutta method
[
5
]
to solve equation (
17
) on the temporal mesh
\{t_{k}=k\Delta t:\Delta t=T/N_{T},k=0,1,\cdots,N_{T}\}
. It means

\displaystyle\bm{\widetilde{c}}(t_{k+1})=\bm{\widetilde{c}}(t_{k})+\Delta tK_{3},

\displaystyle K_{1}=F_{k}\bm{\widetilde{c}}(t_{k}),\quad K_{2}=F_{k}(\bm{\widetilde{c}}(t_{k})+\Delta tK_{1}/3),\quad K_{3}=F_{k}(\bm{\widetilde{c}}(t_{k})+\Delta tK_{2}/2),

\displaystyle F_{k}=-i\widetilde{H}_{1,k}+i\widetilde{H}_{2,k},\quad k=0,1,\cdots,N_{T}-1.

An i-stable is a scheme whose stability region contains part of the imaginary axis
[
5
]
. Such a property is important since the spectral of the Schrödingerized differential operator are purely imaginary, thus a scheme like forward Euler method will be unstable.

2.4
Complexity

For the
d
-dimensional SDE (
1
), we will need
d\times(2L)/\Delta p
grid points for solving the approximate SDE (
3
). So the number of qubits
n
that is needed for registers is

n\sim\textrm{log}(1/\Delta p)+\log(d).

Given a matrix or vector
H
, let
\|H\|_{\max}
denote the largest entry of
H
in absolute value and
s(H)
denote the sparsity of
H
which is the number of nonzero entries of
H
in every column and row. Denote
\widetilde{H}_{k}=\widetilde{H}_{1,k}-\widetilde{H}_{2,k}
.
The following proposition is from
[
40
, Theorem 3]
and it gives the complexity for implementing Hamiltonian simulation.

Proposition 2.5
.

A
d
-sparse Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
on
n
qubits with matrix elements specified to
\tau_{b}
bits of precision can be simulated for time interval
t
, error
\epsilon
, and success probability at least
1-2\epsilon
with
\mathcal{O}(td\|\hat{H}\|_{\textrm{max}}+\log(1/\epsilon)/\log\log(1/\epsilon))
queries and a factor
\mathcal{O}(n+\tau_{b}\textrm{polylog}(\tau_{b}))
additional quantum gates.

Hereafter, we assume the precision
\tau
and error
\epsilon
are fixed. The following theorem gives the gate complexity of the Schrödingerisation approach.

Theorem 2.6
.

For the
d
-dimensional SDE with additive Gaussian noise (
1
), the Schrödingerisation approach to obtain
\widetilde{X}(T)
of equation (
16
) has gate complexity

N_{\text{Gate},\text{Schr}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{Td}{\Delta p}\Bigl(\log(\frac{1}{\Delta p})+\log(d)\Bigr)\Bigl(\|A\|_{\max}+|\Delta W|_{\max}/\sqrt{\Delta t}\Bigr)\right),

where
|\Delta W|_{\max}:=\max_{1\leq k\leq N_{T}}\|\Delta W_{k}\|_{\max}
.

Proof.

The procedure of the Schrödingerisation approach can be realized as follows:

\widetilde{\bm{w}}(t_{0},\cdot)\xrightarrow{F_{p}^{-1}}\bm{c}(t_{0})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{e}^{-i\widetilde{H}_{1}\Delta t}}\bm{c}(t_{1})\cdots\xrightarrow{\mathrm{e}^{-i\widetilde{H}_{k}\Delta t}}\bm{c}(t_{k})\cdots\xrightarrow{\mathrm{e}^{-i\widetilde{H}_{N_{T}}\Delta t}}\bm{c}(T)\xrightarrow{F_{p}}\widetilde{\bm{w}}(T,\cdot)

(22)

where
\widetilde{H}_{k}=\widetilde{H}_{1,k}-\widetilde{H}_{2,k}
and
F_{p}=\Phi
is the discrete Fourier transform. The Hamiltonian simulation using the Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}_{k}
is employed in the intermediate steps. It is clear from (
19
) that
F_{p}
can be realized based on quantum Fast Fourier transform (see, for example,
[
45
]
). So, the Schrödingerisation approach can be realized by quantum Fast Fourier transform and Hamiltonian simulation. According to Proposition
2.5
, we need to compute the sparsity
s(\widetilde{H}_{k})
and
\|\widetilde{H}_{k}\|_{\max}
.

For
s(\widetilde{H}_{k})
,

\displaystyle s(\widetilde{H}_{k})

\displaystyle=s(\widetilde{H}_{1,k}-\widetilde{H}_{2,k})=s(D_{\bm{\mu}}\otimes H_{1,k}-\bm{I}\otimes H_{2,k})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq l\leq 2N}s(\mu_{l}H_{1,k}-H_{2,k})\leq d+1.

For
\|\widetilde{H}_{k}\|_{\max}
,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{H}_{k}\|_{\max}

\displaystyle=\|D_{\bm{\mu}}\otimes H_{1,k}-\bm{I}\otimes H_{2,k}\|_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq l\leq 2N}\|\mu_{l}H_{1,k}\|_{\max}+\|H_{2,k}\|_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq(\pi/\Delta p+1)(\|A\|_{\max}+|\Delta W|_{\max}/\sqrt{\Delta t}).

Here
|\Delta W|_{\max}:=\max_{1\leq k\leq N_{T}}|\Delta W_{k}|
.

Let
n_{p}
denote the number of qubits that is needed for the
p
-variable. It known that the one-dimensional quantum Fourier transform on
n_{p}
qubits can be implemented using
\mathcal{O}(n_{p}\log n_{p})
gates
[
42
]
. By proposition
2.5
, for fixed
m
bits of precision and error
\epsilon
, the overall gate complexity to obtain
\widetilde{\bm{w}}(T,\cdot)
is

\displaystyle N_{\text{Gate},\text{Schr}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\big(n_{p}\log n_{p}\big)+N_{T}\mathcal{O}\bigl(n(\Delta t\max_{1\leq k\leq 2N}\{s(\widetilde{H}_{k})\|\widetilde{H}_{k}\|_{\max}\})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\big(n_{p}\log n_{p}\big)+\mathcal{O}\bigl(Td(\log(1/\Delta p)+\log(d))(\|A\|_{\max}+|\Delta W|_{\max}/\sqrt{\Delta t})/\Delta p\bigr).

As
n_{p}\sim\log(1/\Delta p)
, we have

N_{\text{Gate},\text{Schr}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{Td}{\Delta p}\Bigl(\log(\frac{1}{\Delta p})+\log(d)\Bigr)\Bigl(\|A\|_{\max}+|\Delta W|_{\max}/\sqrt{\Delta t}\Bigr)\right).

(23)

With the state
\widetilde{\bm{w}}(T,\cdot)
, to recover
\widetilde{X}(T)
, we can directly apply the projection measurement
\sum_{j^{*}}^{j^{**}}|j\rangle\langle j|
where
p=j\Delta p
and the state
\widetilde{X}(T)/\|\widetilde{X}(t)\|
can be recovered with probability
O(\|\widetilde{X}(T)\|^{2}/\|\widetilde{X}(0)\|^{2})

[
26
,
32
]
. So, the overall gate complexity to derive
\widetilde{X}(T)
is the same as (
23
).
∎

With similar arguments, we can derive the gate complexity for the SDE with multiplicative noise.

Corollary 2.7
.

For the
d
-dimensional SDE with multiplicative Gaussian noise (
10
), the Schrödingerisation approach to obtain
\widetilde{X}(T)
has gate complexity

N_{\text{Gate},\text{Schr}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{Td}{\Delta p}\Bigl(\log(\frac{1}{\Delta p})+\log(d)\Bigr)\Bigl(\|A\|_{\max}+m\|B\|^{2}_{\max}+\frac{m\|B\|_{\max}|\Delta W|_{\max}}{\Delta t}\Bigr)\right),

where
\Delta t=T/N_{T}
.

Proof.

Similar as Theorem
2.6
, we only need to compute the sparsity
s(\widetilde{H}_{k})
and
\|\widetilde{H}_{k}\|_{\max}
.

As
\widetilde{A}_{k}
is a
d
-dimensional matrix and

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{A}_{k}\|_{\max}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\|A-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}(B^{(l)})^{2}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}B^{(l)}\frac{\Delta W^{(l)}_{k}}{\Delta t}\Bigr\|_{\max}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|A\|_{\max}+m\|B\|^{2}_{\max}+\frac{m\|B\|_{\max}|\Delta W|_{\max}}{\Delta t}.

For
s(\widetilde{H}_{k})
,

\displaystyle s(\widetilde{H}_{k})

\displaystyle=s(\widetilde{H}_{1,k}-\widetilde{H}_{2,k})=s(D_{\bm{\mu}}\otimes H_{1,k}-\bm{I}\otimes H_{2,k})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq l\leq 2N}s(\mu_{l}H_{1,k}-H_{2,k})\leq d.

For
\|\widetilde{H}_{k}\|_{\max}
,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{H}_{k}\|_{\max}

\displaystyle=\|D_{\bm{\mu}}\otimes H_{1,k}-\bm{I}\otimes H_{2,k}\|_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq l\leq 2N}\|\mu_{l}H_{1,k}\|_{\max}+\|H_{2,k}\|_{\max}

\displaystyle\leq(\pi/\Delta p+1)(\|A\|_{\max}+m\|B\|^{2}_{\max}+\frac{m\|B\|_{\max}|\Delta W|_{\max}}{\Delta t}).

Applying Proposition
2.5
and projection measurement
\sum_{j^{*}}^{j^{**}}|j\rangle\langle j|
, we derive the desired result.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

Let
\tau:=\Delta ts(A)\|A\|_{\max}
. Let
\tau=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon)/\log\log(1/\epsilon))
and Proposition
2.5
is valid as established in
[
40
]
.
By Lévy modulus
[
39
]
, we know

\|\Delta W\|_{\max}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\Delta t\log(1/\Delta t)}),\quad\textrm{almost surely.}

Then, for additive noise case, we have

\tau=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t\sqrt{\log(1/\Delta t)}/\Delta p).

It suffices to set
\Delta t\sqrt{\log(1/\Delta t)}/\Delta p=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon)/\log\log(1/\epsilon))
to meet the requirement of Proposition
2.5
.

Similarly, for the multiplicative noise case, we have

\tau=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\Delta t\log(1/\Delta t)}/\Delta p).

Let
\sqrt{\Delta t\log(1/\Delta t)}/\Delta p=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon)/\log\log(1/\epsilon))
and it will meet the requirement of Proposition
2.5
.

2.1
, we can compute the sample path of (

) using Hamiltonian simulation. We use the classical computer to compute

4
Analysis on convergence rate

In this section, we will give the proof of the convergence rate of the Schrödingerisation approach for both SDEs with additive Gaussian noise and multiplicative Gaussian noise. Specifically, we will show the mean square error between the analytical solution
X(T)
of equation (
1
) and equation (
10
) between their corresponding recovered solution
\widetilde{X}^{\widetilde{w}}(T)
, which collects the first
d
components of
\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}
defined in (
21
). We know

\|X(T)-\widetilde{X}^{\widetilde{w}}(T)\|_{2}\leq\|X(T)-\widetilde{X}(T)\|_{2}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{X}(T)-\widetilde{X}^{\widetilde{w}}(T)\|^{2}},

(29)

where
\widetilde{X}
is the analytical solution of the approximate equation (
3
) and (
11
). Here we use
\|\cdot\|_{2}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\|\cdot\|^{2}}
to denote the mean square norm, where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm for vectors and operator norm for matrices. It is clear that
\|X(T)-\widetilde{X}(T)\|_{2}
is the error between the approximate equation and the SDE, and the error
\|\widetilde{X}(T)-\widetilde{X}^{\widetilde{w}}(T)\|
comes from the recovery part.

Denote
I=[-L,L]
,
H_{1}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{T}-1}H_{1,k}\mathbbm{1}_{[t_{k},t_{k+1})}(t)
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}^{+}(H_{1})

\displaystyle=\max\{\sup_{0<t<T}\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(H_{1}(t)),\lambda>0\},0\},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}^{-}(H_{1})

\displaystyle=\max\{\sup_{0<t<T}\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(H_{1}(t)),\lambda<0\},0\},

where
\sigma(H_{1}(t))
collects all eigenvalues of
H_{1}(t)
.

We use
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(I)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(I)}
to denote the
L^{2}
-norm and
H^{1}
-norm on interval
I
of variable
p
.

Assumption H1
.

Assume the eigenvalues of
H_{1}
has the following order

\lambda_{1}(H_{1})\leq\lambda_{2}(H_{1})\leq\cdots\lambda_{d}(H_{1}),\quad\textrm{for all }t\in[0,T].

Assumption H2
.

Let
\epsilon\ll 1
be the desired accuracy. Assume the interval
I=[-L,L]
satisfies

e^{-L+2\lambda_{\max}^{+}(H_{1})T}\leq\epsilon,\quad e^{-L+\lambda_{\max}^{+}(H_{1})T+\lambda_{\max}^{-}(H_{1})T}\leq\epsilon.

We have the following results.

Theorem 4.1
.

For the
d
-dimensional SDE with
m
-dimensional additive Gaussian noise (
1
), suppose the recovery region

is properly chosen, which means the solution to (
5

(30)

where
C_{2}=\sqrt{{mc_{1}(e^{2\|A\|T}-1)}/(2\|A\|})
with

and

Proof.

Note that

Following
[
29
]
, we introduce the boundary value problem:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{d}{dt}\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t,p)=-H_{1,k}\partial_{p}\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t,p)+iH_{2,k}\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t,p),\quad t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1},\\
&\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t,-L)=\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t,L),\\
&\bm{\mathcal{V}}(0,p)=e^{-|p|}z_{0},\quad p\in[-L,L].\end{aligned}\right.

(32)

Equation (
32
) can be naturally extended to an initial value problem for
p\in(-\infty,\infty)
via periodic extension, by setting
\bm{\mathcal{V}}(0,p)=\mathcal{G}(p)z_{0}
. Here
\mathcal{G}
is a periodic function satisfying

\mathcal{G}(p)=e^{-|p-2jL|}\quad(2j-1)L\leq p<(2j+1)L.

(33)

Lemma 4.3
.

For
\bm{\mathcal{V}}
governed by equation (
32
), the
H^{1}
-estimate of
\bm{\mathcal{V}}
satisfies

\|\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t_{k},\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(I)}=\sqrt{2(1-e^{-2L})}\|z_{0}\|.,\quad 0\leq k\leq N_{T}.

Proof.

The Fourier series for
\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t,\cdot)
is

\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t,p)=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\bm{\mathcal{V}}}_{l}(t)e^{\frac{il\pi}{L}p},\quad\hat{\bm{\mathcal{V}}}_{l}(t)=\frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t,p)e^{-\frac{il\pi}{L}p}dp.

For every
l\in\mathbb{Z}
, by equation (
32
), we have

\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\bm{\mathcal{V}}}_{l}(t)=-\frac{il\pi}{L}H_{1,k}\hat{\bm{\mathcal{V}}}_{l}(t)+iH_{2,k}\hat{\bm{\mathcal{V}}}_{l}(t),\quad t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1}.

As matrices
H_{1,k}
and
H_{2,k}
are Hermite matrices, the solution operator
\exp(-i(\frac{l\pi}{L}H_{1,k}-H_{2,k})\Delta t)
is a unitary matrix. It means

\|\hat{\bm{\mathcal{V}}}_{l}(t_{k+1})\|^{2}=\|\exp(-i(\frac{l\pi}{L}H_{1,k}-H_{2,k})\Delta t)\hat{\bm{\mathcal{V}}}_{l}(t_{k})\|^{2}=\|\hat{\bm{\mathcal{V}}}_{l}(t_{k})\|^{2}.

Then,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t_{k+1},\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(I)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\left[1+(\frac{l\pi}{L})^{2}\right]\left\|\hat{\bm{\mathcal{V}}}_{l}(t_{k+1})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\left[1+(\frac{l\pi}{L})^{2}\right]\left\|\hat{\bm{\mathcal{V}}}_{l}(t_{k})\right\|^{2}=\|\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t_{k},\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(I)}.

For
\|\bm{\mathcal{V}}(0,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(I)}
, we know

\|\bm{\mathcal{V}}(0,\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=\|z_{0}\|\sqrt{\int_{-L}^{L}e^{-2|p|}+\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial p}e^{-|p|}\right|^{2}dp}=\sqrt{2(1-e^{-2L})}\|z_{0}\|.

∎

For
0\leq k\leq N_{T}
, recall that
\bm{\widetilde{w}}^{c}(t_{k},p)=[\widetilde{w}_{1}(t,p),\cdots,\widetilde{w}_{\tilde{d}}(t_{k},p)]
where
\widetilde{w}_{h}(t_{k},p)=\sum_{l=1}^{2N}c_{l}^{(h)}(t)e^{i\mu_{l}(p+L)}
with
c_{l}^{(h)}
solved in equation (
17
). The following lemma follows from
[
29
, Lemma 4.3]
. It shows the
L^{2}
error between
\widetilde{\bm{w}}^{c}
and
\bm{v}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose the Assumptions
H1
and
H2
are satisfied.
For the numerical solution
\widetilde{\bm{w}}^{c}
defined by the solution
\widetilde{\bm{w}}
of equation (

), and the solution

of equation (

\|\bm{v}(t_{k},\cdot)-\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t_{k},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(I)}\lesssim(e^{-L+\lambda_{\max}^{+}(H_{1})t_{k}}+e^{-L+\lambda_{\max}^{-}(H_{1})t_{k}})\|z_{0}\|,

and

\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}^{c}(t_{k},\cdot)-\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t_{k},\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(I)}\lesssim\Delta p\|\bm{\mathcal{V}}(t_{k},\cdot)\|_{H^{1}(I)}.

The following proposition gives the error rate for recovered solution
\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}
of (

).

Proposition 4.5

(Recovery error analysis)
.

Suppose the recovery region
U_{p}=[p^{*},p^{*}+R)
is properly chosen, which means the solution to (
15
) satisfies
\bm{v}(t,p)=e^{-p}\widetilde{Z}(t)
for
p\in U_{p}
. Further assume the Assumption
H1
and
H2
are satisfied.
Then, for the recovered solution
\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}(t_{k})
and the solution
\widetilde{Z}(t_{k})
of the approximate equation (
14
), we have

\|\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}(t_{k})-\widetilde{Z}(t_{k})\|\lesssim\left(\Delta p^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{\lambda_{\max}^{+}(H_{1})T}+\Delta p^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-\widetilde{C}(t_{k})}\right)\|\widetilde{Z}(0)\|,

(34)

where

\widetilde{C}(t_{k})=\max\{L-\lambda_{\max}^{-}(H_{1})t_{k}-\lambda_{\max}^{+}(H_{1})T,L-\lambda_{\max}^{+}(H_{1})t_{k}-\lambda_{\max}^{+}(H_{1})T\}.

(35)

Proof.

We only need to show

\|\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}(t_{k})-\widetilde{Z}(t_{k})\|^{2}\lesssim\frac{e^{2p^{*}}\Delta p}{(1-e^{-R})^{2}}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}^{c}(t_{k},\cdot)-\bm{v}(t_{k},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(I)}.

(36)

According to
[
29
, Theorem 3.1]
, it suffices to take
p^{*}=\lambda_{\max}^{+}(H_{1})T
and
R\gg 1
. By Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
, we get the desired estimate (
34
).

Now we continue to prove estimate (
36
). As
\bm{v}(t,p)=e^{-p}\widetilde{Z}(t)
for
p\in U_{p}
, it is clear that

\widetilde{Z}(t_{k})=\frac{\int_{U_{p}}\bm{v}(t_{k},p)dp}{\int_{U_{p}}e^{-p}dp}=\frac{\sum_{p_{j}\in U_{p}}\bm{v}(t_{k},p_{j})\Delta p}{\sum_{p_{j}\in U_{p}}e^{-p_{j}}\Delta p}.

For the solution
\widetilde{Z}(t)
of the approximate equation (
14
) and the recovered solution
\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}(t)
of equation (
21
), we have

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}(t_{k})-\widetilde{Z}(t_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}|\widetilde{Z}_{h}^{\widetilde{w}}(t_{k})-\widetilde{Z}_{h}(t_{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\big(\sum_{p_{j}\in U_{p}}e^{-p_{j}}\big)^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}\sum_{p_{j}\in U_{p}}\big(\widetilde{w}_{h}(t_{k},p_{j})-v_{h}(t_{k},p_{j})\big)^{2}.

Choose
U_{p}=[p^{*},p^{*}+R)=[j_{1}\Delta p,j_{2}\Delta p
), then

\frac{1}{\sum_{p_{j}\in U_{p}}e^{-p_{j}}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{j=j_{1}}^{j_{2}-1}e^{-p_{j}}}=\frac{1-e^{-\Delta p}}{e^{-j_{1}\Delta p}-e^{-j_{2}\Delta p}}

For
\mu_{l}=\pi(l-N-1)/L
and
p_{j}=-L+(j-1)\Delta p
, a direct computation shows

\int_{-L}^{L}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2N}\hat{u}_{l}e^{i\mu_{l}(p+L)}\right)^{2}dp=2L\sum_{l=1}^{2N}\hat{u}_{l}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{2N}\hat{u}_{l}e^{i\mu_{l}(p_{j}+L)}\right)^{2}\Delta p,

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\Bigl(\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{l}e^{i\mu_{l}(p_{j}+L)}\Bigr)^{2}\Delta p

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\Bigl(\sum_{l=1}^{2N}\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty}(\hat{u}_{l+2\tau N})e^{i\mu_{l}(p_{j}+L)}\Bigr)^{2}\Delta p

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{2N}\Bigl(\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{l+2\tau N}\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}|\hat{u}_{l}|^{2}=2\Bigl\|\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{k}e^{i\mu_{l}(p+L)}\Bigr\|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{p_{j}\in U_{p}}\big(\widetilde{w}_{h}(t_{k},p_{j})-v_{h}(t_{k},p_{j})\big)^{2}\Delta p

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{2N}\big(\widetilde{w}_{h}(t_{k},p_{j})-v_{h}(t_{k},p_{j})\big)^{2}\Delta p

\displaystyle\leq 4L\|\widetilde{w}_{h}(t_{k},\cdot)-v_{h}(t_{k},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(I)}.

Here we used the fact that

v_{h}(t_{k},p_{j})=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{v}_{l}^{(h)}(t_{k})e^{i\mu_{l}(p_{j}+L)},\quad\hat{v}_{l}^{(h)}(t_{k})=\frac{1}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}v_{h}(t_{k},p)e^{-i\mu_{l}(p+L)}dp,

and

\widetilde{w}_{h}(t_{k},p_{j})=\sum_{l=1}^{2N}c_{l}^{(h)}(t)e^{i\mu_{l}(p_{j}+L)}=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{l}^{(h)}(t)e^{i\mu_{l}(p_{j}+L)},\textrm{ with }c_{l}^{(h)}=0\textrm{ for }l\neq 1,\cdots,2N.

We know

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{Z}^{\widetilde{w}}(t_{k})-\widetilde{Z}(t_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}|\widetilde{Z}_{h}^{\widetilde{w}}(t_{k})-\widetilde{Z}_{h}(t_{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\big(\sum_{p_{j}\in U_{p}}e^{-p_{j}}\big)^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}\sum_{p_{j}\in U_{p}}\big(\widetilde{w}_{h}(t_{k},p_{j})-v_{h}(t_{k},p_{j})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8L(1-e^{-\Delta p})^{2}}{\Delta p(e^{-j_{1}\Delta p}-e^{-j_{2}\Delta p})^{2}}\sum_{h=1}^{\tilde{d}}\|\widetilde{w}_{h}(t_{k},\cdot)-v_{h}(t_{k},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(I)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{e^{2p^{*}}\Delta p}{(1-e^{-R})^{2}}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}^{c}(t_{k},\cdot)-\bm{v}(t_{k},\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(I)}.

∎

The following proposition shows the convergence rate for the one-dimension SDE:

dX(t)=aX(t)dt+rdW_{t},\quad X(0)=x_{0}.

(37)

Proposition 4.6
.

In one-dimension, the estimate

\|\widetilde{X}(T)-X(T)\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\Delta t,

holds for the solution
\widetilde{X}
of the approximate equation (
3
), where
X
is the solution of the SDE (
37
) and
C_{1}={|r|\sqrt{2a(e^{2aT}-1)}}/{4}
.

Proof.

Recall that the solution to the one-dimensional SDE (
37
) is

X(t)=e^{at}\left(x_{0}+r\int_{0}^{t}e^{-as}dW_{s}\right),

then

X(t_{k+1})=e^{a\Delta t}X(t_{k})+r\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}e^{a(t_{k+1}-s)}dW_{s}.

The solution to the corresponding approximate equation (
3
) is

\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1})=e^{a\Delta t}\widetilde{X}(t_{k})+(e^{a\Delta t}-1)\frac{r\Delta W_{k}}{a\Delta t}=e^{a\Delta t}\widetilde{X}(t_{k})+r\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\frac{e^{a\Delta t}-1}{a\Delta t}dW_{s}.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1})-X(t_{k+1})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=e^{2a\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\big(\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\big)^{2}+r^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}e^{a(t_{k+1}-s)}-\frac{e^{a\Delta t}-1}{a\Delta t}dW_{s}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle+2re^{a\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\big)\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}e^{a(t_{k+1}-s)}-\frac{e^{a\Delta t}-1}{a\Delta t}dW_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=e^{2a\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\big(\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\big)^{2}+r^{2}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\big(e^{a(t_{k+1}-s)}-\frac{e^{a\Delta t}-1}{a\Delta t}\big)^{2}ds.

In the last equality, we used the Itô Isometry and the independence between the stochastic integral and the difference
\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})
.
For
|a|\Delta t\ll 1
and the fact that

\max_{0\leq s\leq\Delta t}\left|e^{a(\Delta t-s)}-\frac{e^{a\Delta t}-1}{a\Delta t}\right|=\max\left\{\left|e^{a\Delta t}-\frac{e^{a\Delta t}-1}{a\Delta t}\right|,\left|\frac{e^{a\Delta t}-1}{a\Delta t}-1\right|\right\}=\frac{1}{2}|a|\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}),

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1})-X(t_{k+1})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2a\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\big(\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\big)^{2}+r^{2}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\frac{1}{4}a^{2}\Delta t^{2}ds+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})ds

\displaystyle=e^{2a\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\big(\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\big)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(ar)^{2}\Delta t^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{4}).

Let
b_{k}
denote
\mathbb{E}\big(\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\big)^{2}
, and the above inequality gives

b_{k+1}\leq e^{2a\Delta t}b_{k}+\frac{1}{4}(ar)^{2}\Delta t^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{4}).

Then we have

b_{n}\leq e^{2an\Delta t}b_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}e^{2ak\Delta t}\big(\frac{1}{4}(ar)^{2}\Delta t^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{4})\big).

Given
b_{0}=0
, which means
X(0)=\widetilde{X}(0)
, we have

b_{n}\leq\frac{(e^{2an\Delta t}-1)}{4(e^{2a\Delta t}-1)}\big((ar)^{2}\Delta t^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{4})\big).

Take
n=k
,
t_{k}=k\Delta t
and
e^{2a\Delta t}-1=2a\Delta t+\mathcal{O}((a\Delta t)^{2})
,

b_{k}\leq\frac{r^{2}a(e^{2at_{k}}-1)\Delta t^{2}}{8}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3}).

Then

\max_{t_{k}}\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\|_{2}\leq\frac{|r|\sqrt{2a(e^{2aT}-1)}\Delta t}{4}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{\frac{3}{2}}),

and

\|\widetilde{X}(T)-X(T)\|_{2}\leq\frac{|r|\sqrt{2a(e^{2aT}-1)}\Delta t}{4}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{\frac{3}{2}}).

It means that the solution of the approximate equation (
3
) converges to the solution of equation (
24
) with error rate
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)
under mean square norm.
∎

.

For the

Proof.

According to
[
34

, the solution to (

X(t)=e^{At}x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{A(t-s)}B\,dW_{s}

Then

\displaystyle X(t_{k+1})

\displaystyle=e^{A(t_{k}+\Delta t)}x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{k}}e^{A(t_{k}+\Delta t-s)}BdW_{s}+\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}e^{A(t_{k+1}-s)}BdW_{s}

\displaystyle=e^{A\Delta t}X(t_{k})+\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}e^{A(t_{k+1}-s)}BdW_{s}=e^{A\Delta t}X(t_{k})+\sum_{l=1}^{m}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}e^{A(t_{k+1}-s)}B^{(l)}dW_{s}^{(l)}.

For the corresponding approximate equation (

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1})-X(t_{k+1})\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big\|e^{A\Delta t}(\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k}))\big\|^{2}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left\|\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\frac{e^{A(\Delta t-s)}B^{(l)}}{\Delta t}-\frac{e^{A(t_{k+1}-\tau)}B^{(l)}}{\Delta t}dsdW_{\tau}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(e^{A\Delta t}\big(\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\big)\Big)^{\dagger}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\frac{e^{A(\Delta t-s)}B^{(l)}}{\Delta t}-\frac{e^{A(t_{k+1}-\tau)}B^{(l)}}{\Delta t}dsdW_{\tau}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq e^{2\|A\|\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\big\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\big\|^{2}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\left\|\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\frac{e^{A(\Delta t-s)}B^{(l)}}{\Delta t}-\frac{e^{A(\Delta t-\tau)}B^{(l)}}{\Delta t}ds\right\|^{2}d\tau.

In the last equality, we used the Itô Isometry and the independence between the stochastic integral and the difference
\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})
.
By the definition of the matrix exponential, for
\tau\in[0,\Delta t]
and
\|A\|\Delta t\ll 1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\frac{e^{A(\Delta t-s)}B^{(l)}}{\Delta t}-\frac{e^{A(\Delta t-\tau)}B^{(l)}}{\Delta t}ds\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t^{2}}\left\|\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\Big(\textrm{I}_{d}+A(\Delta t-s)+\frac{1}{2}A^{2}(\Delta t-s)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})\Big)B^{(l)}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\Big(\textrm{I}_{d}+A(\Delta t-\tau)+\frac{1}{2}A^{2}(\Delta t-\tau)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})\Big)B^{(l)}ds\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t^{2}}\left\|\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\Big(A(\tau-s)+\frac{1}{2}A^{2}(\tau-s)(2\Delta t-\tau-s)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3})\Big)B^{(l)}ds\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta t^{2}}\left\|AB^{(l)}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}(\tau-s)ds+A^{2}B^{(l)}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}(\tau-s)(2\Delta t-\tau-s)ds+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{4})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|AB^{(l)}\|^{2}\Delta t^{2}+\frac{2}{9}\|A^{2}B^{(l)}\|^{2}\Delta t^{4}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{6}).

Then, take
c_{1}=\max_{1\leq l\leq m}\frac{1}{2}\|AB^{(l)}\|^{2}
and
c_{2}=\max_{1\leq l\leq m}\frac{2}{9}\|A^{2}B^{(l)}\|^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1})-X(t_{k+1})\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2\|A\|\Delta t}\mathbb{E}\big\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\big\|^{2}+m\big(c_{1}\Delta t^{3}+c_{2}\Delta t^{5}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{7})\big).

Similarly as the one-dimensional case, take
b_{k}=\mathbb{E}\big\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\big\|^{2}
,

\displaystyle b_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2\|A\|\Delta t}b_{k}+mc_{1}\Delta t^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{5})

\displaystyle=e^{2({k+1})\|A\|\Delta t}b_{0}+m\sum_{j=0}^{k}e^{2j\|A\|\Delta t}\big(c_{1}\Delta t^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{5})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{mc_{1}(e^{2(k+1)\|A\|\Delta t}-1)}{e^{2\|A\|\Delta t}-1}\big(\Delta t^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{5})\big).

Take
t_{k}=k\Delta t
and
e^{2\|A\|\Delta t}-1=2\|A\|\Delta t+\mathcal{O}((\|A\|\Delta t)^{2})
, we have

b_{k}\leq\frac{mc_{1}(e^{2\|A\|t_{k}}-1)}{2\|A\|}\Delta t^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{4}).

It means

\max_{t_{k}}\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})-X(t_{k})\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{mc_{1}(e^{2\|A\|T}-1)}{2\|A\|}}\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}),

and

\|\widetilde{X}(T)-X(T)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{mc_{1}(e^{2\|A\|T}-1)}{2\|A\|}}\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}).

In
d
-dimensions, the solution of the approximate equation (

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{m}\Delta t)
under mean square norm.
∎

In the following, we will move on to show the convergence rate for SDEs with multiplicative Gaussian noise.

Recall the SDE with multiplicative Gaussian noise:

dX(t)=AX(t)dt+\sum_{l=1}^{m}B^{(l)}X(t)dW^{(l)}_{t},\quad X_{0}=x_{0},

(39)

and the approximate equation,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{d\widetilde{X}(t)}{dt}=\widetilde{A}_{k}\widetilde{X}(t),\quad\widetilde{X}(0)=x_{0}\textrm{ and }t_{k}<t\leq t_{k+1},\\
&\widetilde{A}_{k}=A-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}(B^{(l)})^{2}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}B^{(l)}\frac{\Delta W^{(l)}_{k}}{\Delta t}.\end{aligned}\right.

(40)

Here,
W_{t}=(W_{t}^{(1)},\cdots,W_{t}^{(m)})
is a
m
-dimensional standard Brownian motion and
\Delta W_{k}^{(l)}=W_{t_{k+1}}^{(l)}-W_{t_{k}}^{(l)}
.
\{A,B^{(1)},\cdots,B^{(m)}\}
are
d\times d

\Delta t=1/N_{T}

Proposition 4.8
.

For the
d
-dimensional SDE with multiplicative Gaussian noise(
39
), the estimate

\|\widetilde{X}(T)-X(T)\|_{2}\lesssim\Delta t,

holds for the solution
\widetilde{X}
of the approximate equation(
40
).

Proof.

We show this estimate by comparing the solution
\widetilde{X}
to the Milstein approximation
\widehat{X}
:

\widehat{X}(t_{k+1})=\widehat{X}(t_{k})+A\widehat{X}(t_{k})\Delta t+\sum_{l=1}^{m}B^{(l)}\widehat{X}(t_{k})\Delta W_{k}^{(l)}+\sum_{l,j=1}^{m}B^{(l)}B^{(j)}\widehat{X}(t_{k})I_{k}(j,l),\quad\widehat{X}_{t_{0}}=x_{0}.

(41)

Here
I_{k}(j,l)
represents the Ito’s integral

I_{k}(j,l)=\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}(W_{s}^{(j)}-W_{t_{k}}^{(j)})dW_{s}^{(l)}.

Using Ito’s formula, it is clear that

I_{k}(l,j)+I_{k}(j,l)=\Delta W_{k}^{(l)}\Delta W_{k}^{(j)}\quad I_{k}(l,l)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl((\Delta W_{k}^{(l)})^{2}-\Delta t\bigr).

In the
(k+1)
-th iteration of (
40
), that is obtaining
\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1})
from
\widetilde{X}(t_{k})
, we know

\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1})=\exp(\widetilde{A}_{k}\Delta t)\widetilde{X}(t_{k}).

By the definition of the matrix exponential, we have

\exp(\widetilde{A}_{k}\Delta t)=I+\widetilde{A}_{k}\Delta t+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l,j=1}^{m}B^{(l)}B^{(j)}\Delta W^{(l)}_{k}\Delta W^{(j)}_{k}+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t\Delta W).

Comparing to the Milstein approximation
\widehat{X}
in (
41
), we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{X}(t_{k+1})-

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1})=\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})+A\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\Delta t+\sum_{l=1}^{m}B^{(l)}\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\Delta W^{(l)}_{k}

(42)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}(B^{(l)})^{2}(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k}))\big((\Delta W_{k}^{(l)})^{2}-\Delta t\big)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}B^{(l)}B^{(j)}(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k}))I_{k}(j,l)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}B^{(l)}B^{(j)}\widetilde{X}(t_{k})(I_{k}(j,l)-\Delta W_{k}^{(l)}\Delta W_{k}^{(j)})+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t\Delta W).

It is clear that

\left\|A\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\right\|\leq\|A\|\left\|\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\right\|,\quad\left\|B^{(l)}\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\right\|\leq\|B^{(l)}\|\left\|\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\right\|.

We will next show that
\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\|^{2}
and
\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\|
is bounded for all
k
. The boundedness of
\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\|
follows from
\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\|^{2}
by Hölder’s inequalities. A direct computation gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\exp(\widetilde{A}_{k-1}\Delta t)\exp(\widetilde{A}_{k-2}\Delta t)\cdots\exp(\widetilde{A}_{0}\Delta t)\widetilde{X}(0)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\exp(2\|\widetilde{A}_{k-1}\|\Delta t)\exp(2\|\widetilde{A}_{k-2}\|\Delta t)\cdots\exp(2\|\widetilde{A}_{0}\|\Delta t)\|\widetilde{X}(0)\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl(\|\widetilde{X}(0)\|^{2}\exp(2\sum_{\tau=0}^{k-1}\|\widetilde{A}_{\tau}\|\Delta t)\Bigr)

By the definition of
\widetilde{A}_{k}
and the fact that
\Delta W_{k}^{(l)}
are independent with each other, we have for every
k
,

\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\|^{2}\leq\|x_{0}\|^{2}\exp\Bigr[2(\|A\|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\|B^{(l)}\|^{2})T\Bigl]\prod_{\tau=0}^{N_{T}-1}\prod_{l=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Bigr[\exp(2\|B^{(l)}\||\Delta W_{k}^{(l)}|)\Bigl]<\infty.

(43)

Squaring both sides and taking expectation of (
42

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\|\widehat{X}(t_{k+1})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1})\big\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\big[(\widehat{X}(t_{k+1})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1}))^{\dagger}(\widehat{X}(t_{k+1})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k+1}))\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big\|\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\Big\|A\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\Big\|^{2}\Delta t^{2}+\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Big\|B^{(l)}\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\Big\|^{2}\Delta t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\Big\|\sum_{l=1}^{m}(B^{(l)})^{2}\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\big((\Delta W_{k}^{(l)})^{2}-\Delta t\big)\Big\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\Big\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}B^{(l)}B^{(j)}\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)I_{k}(j,l)\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\Big\|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}B^{(l)}B^{(j)}\widetilde{X}(t_{k})(I_{k}(j,l)-\Delta W_{k}^{(l)}\Delta W_{k}^{(j)})\Big\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k}\big)^{\dagger}A\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\Delta t\Big)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}\big(R_{k}^{(l,j)}+(R_{k}^{(l,j)})^{\dagger}\big)(I_{k}(j,l))^{2}\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3}).

Here, we denote

R_{k}^{(l,j)}\triangleq\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)^{\dagger}\big(B^{(j)}B^{(l)}\big)^{\dagger}B^{(l)}B^{(j)}\widetilde{X}(t_{k}).

and use the fact that

\mathbb{E}(I_{k}(j_{1},l_{1})I_{k}(j_{2},l_{2}))=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{j_{1}j_{2}}\delta_{l_{1}l_{2}}(\Delta t)^{2},\quad\mathbb{E}(I_{k}(j_{1},l_{1})\Delta W_{k}^{(l_{2})})=0.

By the fact that
\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})
is independent of
\Delta W_{k}^{(l)}
and
I_{k}(j,l)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{m}(B^{(l)})^{2}\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\big((\Delta W_{k}^{(l)})^{2}-\Delta t\big)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\Big((B^{(l)})^{2}\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\Big)^{2}\mathbb{E}\Big((\Delta W_{k}^{(l)})^{2}-\Delta t\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}_{1}\mathbb{E}\big\|\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big\|^{2}\Delta t^{2},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}B^{(l)}B^{(j)}\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)I_{k}(j,l)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}\mathbb{E}\big\|B^{(l)}B^{(j)}\big(\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big)\big\|^{2}\mathbb{E}(I_{k}(j,l))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}_{2}\mathbb{E}\big\|\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big\|^{2}\Delta t^{2}.

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}\big(R_{k}^{(l,j)}+(R_{k}^{(l,j)})^{\dagger}\big)(I_{k}(j,l))^{2}\Big)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,j=1\\
l\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}\mathbb{E}\big|R_{k}^{(l,j)}+(R_{k}^{(l,j)})^{\dagger}\big|\mathbb{E}(I_{k}(j,l))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{C}_{3}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\big\|\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big\|^{2}}\Delta t^{2}.

Let
\epsilon_{k}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\big\|\widehat{X}(t_{k})-\widetilde{X}(t_{k})\big\|^{2}}
, then we know

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k+1}^{2}\leq\epsilon_{k}^{2}+C_{1}\epsilon_{k}^{2}\Delta t+C_{2}\epsilon_{k}^{2}\Delta t^{2}+C_{3}\epsilon_{k}\Delta t^{2}+C_{4}\Delta t^{2}+C_{5}\Delta t^{3},\quad\epsilon_{0}=0.

Here
\tilde{C}_{1},\tilde{C}_{2},\tilde{C}_{3},C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}
and
C_{5}
are positive constants independent of
\Delta t
and
k
.

We will prove by induction that
\epsilon_{k}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
. Consider an auxiliary equation

\eta_{k+1}^{2}=\eta_{k}^{2}+C_{1}\eta_{k}^{2}\Delta t+C_{2}\eta_{k}^{2}\Delta t^{2}+C_{3}\eta_{k}\Delta t^{2}+C_{4}\Delta t^{2}+C_{5}\Delta t^{3},\quad\quad\eta_{0}=0.

(44)

It is clear that
\epsilon_{k}\leq\eta_{k}
and
\eta^{2}_{k}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
implies
\epsilon^{2}_{k}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
. For every integer

, assume for

\eta^{2}_{k}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
, we will prove
\eta^{2}_{m}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
. As
\eta^{2}_{k}
is an increasing sequence, there exits a constant

and

\eta^{2}_{k_{0}+1}\geq c\Delta t^{2}

\eta^{2}_{k+1}\leq\eta^{2}_{k}\left(\frac{C_{4}}{c}+1+(C_{1}+\frac{C_{3}}{\sqrt{c}}+\frac{C_{5}}{c})\Delta t+C_{2}\Delta t^{2}\right).

Then, by the fact that
\eta^{2}_{k_{0}+1}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
, we know

\eta^{2}_{m}\leq\eta^{2}_{k_{0}+1}\left(\frac{C_{4}}{c}+1+(C_{1}+\frac{C_{3}}{\sqrt{c}}+\frac{C_{5}}{c})\Delta t+C_{2}\Delta t^{2}\right)^{m}\leq C\Delta t^{2}.

Here, constant
C
are irrelevant with

\Delta t
.

\mathbb{E}\big(\widehat{X}(T)-\widetilde{X}(T)\big)^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}).

With the order
1
-strong convergence of the Milstein method under the mean square norm (see, for example,
[

), we conclude the approximate equation (

) strongly converges to the solution of the SDE (

) with at least order
1
.
∎

5
Summary

We proposed quantum algorithms for linear stochastic differential equations (SDEs) with both Gaussian noise and
\alpha
-stable Lévy noise. The algorithms are based on solving approximate equations to the corresponding SDEs by the Schrödingerisation method. Compared to the Milstein scheme, the gates complexity of our algorithms exhibits a polylogarithmic dependence on both the dimensions and the number of samples, which demonstrates the quantum advantage over their classical counterparts in scenarios involving high dimensions and large sample sizes. We also obtained the convergence rate of the algorithms for SDEs with Gaussian noise in the mean square norm.

Our algorithms were applied to several examples, including Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes, geometric Brownian motions and Lévy flights. It can be extended to general linear SDEs and reducible SDEs. Similarly as most current stage quantum algorithms, our approach can only solve linear SDEs. In practice, we also need to carefully choose the recovery region to avoid introducing additional error. Further development may be addressed to nonlinear problems. Since the gate complexity of our approach exhibits only a polylogarithmic dependence on dimension, it may also serve as an efficient approximate method for computing solutions to stochastic partial differential equations. It is also well-positioned for applications in areas such as Monte Carlo simulations and backward SDEs.
