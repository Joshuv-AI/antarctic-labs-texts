Predicting Hospital-associated complications in older adults: methodological considerations
Authors: Shukhrat Valiev, Dilnavoz Yuldasheva, Malika Khojimatova, Dilorom Saidova, Shaxlo R Sodiqova, Nizom Ermatov, Zeynab Afzali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine learning is increasingly used to predict post-surgical complications, but predictive performance does not guarantee clinical readiness. Shimizu and colleagues developed a promising model for older adults undergoing tibial fracture surgery using a large Japanese database. However, four methodological pitfalls warrant attention. First, the composite outcome was not validated in this population, and excluding in-hospital deaths introduced survivorship bias, underestimating the true burden of severe complications. Second, days to surgery, a variable not known at admission, created target leakage that inflates performance and undermines the admission-time claim. Third, using the test set for feature selection violated TRIPOD guidelines and likely overstated the scorecard’s utility. Fourth, the Super Learner exhibited substantial overfitting, with a 0.110 drop between training (AUC = 0.850) and test (AUC = 0.740) performance, while offering only marginal improvement over simpler models. We suggest redeveloping the model with proper internal validation, excluding leakage-prone predictors, treating death as a competing risk, and prioritizing interpretability when complex ensembles offer limited added value. External validation remains essential before clinical implementation.Keywords: Machine learning, Post-surgical complications, Prediction modeling, Data leakage

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article by Shimizu and colleagues, who developed a machine learning model to predict hospital-associated complications (HACs) in older adults undergoing emergency tibial fracture surgery [1]. The large sample size and rigorous ensemble approach are commendable. However, four methodological concerns warrant consideration before this tool can be translated into clinical practice.

Survivorship bias

The authors defined their primary outcome as a composite of seven HAC domains, informed by the HAC-OP framework proposed by Mudge et al. [2]. While this framework was designed as a measure of care quality in acute hospitals, it was validated in a general population of older inpatients, not specifically in fracture patients [2, 3]. The authors excluded two of the five original components, functional decline and incontinence, because they were difficult to distinguish from injury-related disability. This selective modification without revalidation raises questions about whether the composite measures what it intends to measure in this specific population. The authors themselves acknowledge that no study has tested the validity of these complications in DPC data, and that complications are often underestimated in administrative datasets [3].

More critically, the exclusion of patients who died during hospitalization introduces a substantial survivorship bias [4]. Patients who experienced fatal complications, the most severe outcomes, are simply removed from the cohort. This fundamentally alters what the model actually predicts: it forecasts non-fatal complications in survivors, not the full spectrum of hospital-associated complications as the term is clinically understood.

Previous research has consistently shown that older patients experience significantly elevated rates of iatrogenic complications. In a comprehensive review, Rothschild and Leape documented that diagnostic mishaps, therapeutic mishaps, drug complications, and falls are increased several-fold in patients aged 65 and older compared to younger patients, diagnostic mishaps (×2), therapeutic mishaps (×4), drug complications (×2.5), and falls (×9) [5]. The same review reported complication rates ranging from 29% to 45% among hospitalized older adults, substantially higher than the 16.4% reported in this study. These consistently higher estimates suggest that the 16.4% composite rate may reflect not only differences in case mix but also the systematic exclusion of the most severely affected patients.

Additionally, the composite brings together domains with very different pathophysiologies. Delirium prevention is not the same as thrombosis prevention. A single composite outcome makes it difficult to guide targeted interventions.

We suggest that future studies consider competing risk models that treat in-hospital death as a competing event rather than an exclusion criterion. Reporting individual outcomes separately would also enhance clinical utility.

Post-admission variables in admission-time prediction

The inclusion of days from admission to surgery as a predictor raises an important concern: this variable is not known at admission, yet the model is presented as an admission-time tool. The authors acknowledge this, justifying its inclusion as a proxy for the initial operative plan. However, the actual timing of surgery depends on post-admission clinical and logistical factors that are difficult to predict with certainty at presentation.

This creates a classic case of target leakage, where a model uses information that would not be available when the prediction is needed. A recent review found that 40.2% of published models predicting same-admission outcomes used features finalized only after discharge, achieving artificially high AUROCs driven entirely by such leakage-prone variables [6]. As noted in the literature, target leakage occurs when predictive features include information that will not logically be available at the time of inference [7, 8].

This matters because days to surgery emerged as one of the top five predictors. Without it, the model’s performance is unknown, a sensitivity analysis the authors did not provide. Given that the Super Learner achieved only a modest 0.016 AUC improvement over conventional logistic regression, the contribution of this variable may substantially inflate the observed performance.

We suggest the authors report a sensitivity analysis excluding days to surgery. If the AUC remains above 0.70, the tool may retain utility; if not, the model might be better framed as a post-admission assessment rather than an admission-time screening tool.

A common pitfall in prediction modeling

Using the test set to select variables, as the authors did when they chose the final 7 predictors based on the highest test-set AUC, goes against one of the most fundamental rules in prediction modeling. The TRIPOD guidelines are clear on this [9]: the test set must be kept completely untouched until the final evaluation. Its sole purpose is to give an honest, unbiased assessment of how the model will perform. Using it for feature selection is like marking your own exam paper, you lose any credible measure of real performance.

This is a classic case of data leakage [10]. When you let the test set influence model development, you can no longer truthfully assess how the model will behave with new patients. The performance you see becomes inflated, it reflects the model’s ability to fit the test set, not its ability to predict in the real world.

The right way to do this would have been to use cross-validation within the training set for feature selection, keeping the test set completely isolated until the very end [9].

This matters because the scorecard achieved an AUC of 0.736, almost identical to the Super Learner’s 0.740. This near-identity may simply reflect that the scorecard was fine-tuned on the same test set, rather than proving that a simple scorecard works as well as a complex ensemble.

The authors should redevelop the scorecard using only the training data, with cross-validation for feature selection. Only then should they test it once on the hold-out set to get a realistic estimate of how the tool would perform in practice.

Overfitting in the super learner ensemble

The Super Learner ensemble achieved a test-set AUC of 0.740, compared to a training-set AUC of 0.850, a drop of 0.110. This substantial gap suggests overfitting, where the model captures noise rather than generalizable patterns. While the ensemble incorporated 23 base learners, the modest improvement over conventional logistic regression (AUC 0.740 vs. 0.724) raises questions about whether the added complexity is justified. The authors acknowledge that some optimism in the more complex ensemble cannot be excluded. We suggest that future studies employ simpler, more interpretable models when complex ensembles offer only marginal improvement, and consider internal validation methods such as bootstrap optimism correction to obtain more realistic performance estimates.

Conclusion

Several methodological concerns should inform interpretation of this study. First, the composite outcome was not validated in this population, and excluding patients who died during hospitalization introduced survivorship bias. Second, including days to surgery, a variable not known at admission, created data leakage that artificially inflated the model’s performance. Third, using the test set to select features violated standard prediction modeling guidelines and likely overstated the scorecard’s utility. Fourth, the Super Learner showed clear signs of overfitting, with a substantial drop from training to test performance, while offering only marginal improvement over simpler models. We suggest redeveloping the model with proper internal validation, excluding leakage-prone predictors, treating death as a competing risk, and prioritizing simpler models when complex ensembles offer limited added value. External validation remains essential before clinical implementation.
