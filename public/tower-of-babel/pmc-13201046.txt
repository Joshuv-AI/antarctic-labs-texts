Association between anemia and lower socioeconomic status: The mediating role of frailty
Authors: Chaoban Wang, Xiao Yang, Jianrong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anemia is a global health issue linked to malnutrition and chronic diseases. While its impact on children’s learning is established, its broader socioeconomic implications in adults, particularly the mediating role of frailty, remain unclear. This study analyzed National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data (2007–2018) from 22,851 United States adults (18–79 years). Anemia was defined by hemoglobin levels (men: < 130 g/L; women: < 120 g/L). Frailty was assessed using a modified Fried phenotype (weakness, low activity, exhaustion, slow gait, weight loss). Socioeconomic status (SES) was evaluated using income, education, occupation, and insurance, categorized into High/Low SES via latent class analysis. Weighted logistic regression analyzed anemia-SES associations, stratified by sex, with frailty mediation assessed. Anemia was significantly associated with lower SES in both sexes (male: odds ratio [OR] = 0.57, 95% confidence interval: 0.47–0.68; female: OR = 0.78, 95% confidence interval: 0.69–0.88, P < .001). Frailty alone also strongly predicted lower SES (OR = 0.45 for both sexes, P < .001). Coexisting anemia and frailty showed the strongest negative association (male: OR = 0.32; female: OR = 0.40, P < .001). Frailty mediated a significant portion of the anemia-SES link (male: 12.8%; female: 8.88%, P < .001). Anemia is associated with lower SES in adults, with frailty potentially partly accounting for this association. This highlights the potential value of integrated anemia management addressing both physiological symptoms and socioeconomic support to improve overall outcomes.

1. Introduction

Anemia is prevalent worldwide, with the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 estimating that it affects approximately 1.92 billion people globally, corresponding to a prevalence of 24.3%. The burden is unevenly distributed, with rates exceeding 35% in regions like Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia compared to lower rates in high-income countries. Significant disparities also exist by demographics; for instance, the prevalence in women of reproductive age (33.7%) is triple that of men (11.3%), and in the United States (US), National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data indicates prevalence rises significantly in older adults (over 65 years). Its occurrence is closely related to various factors, including malnutrition, chronic diseases, and the socioeconomic environment of individuals.[1,2] Research shows that the impact of anemia on the nervous system is particularly significant, as hypoxia can lead to a decline in cognitive function, manifested as weakened attention, memory, and executive function.[3] Studies indicate that anemic children exhibit significant learning difficulties in school, resulting in their educational achievements being lower than their peers.[4,5] Some studies suggest that anemic children are at a higher risk of dropping out of school and may be unable to complete basic education or higher-level studies.[6] However, most current research focuses on anemia and children’s learning abilities. While prior adult-focused studies have largely concentrated on clinical outcomes like mortality or physical disability, the more comprehensive social impacts of anemia on adult populations (such as socioeconomic status [SES]) and how the resulting health disparities affect individual SES mechanisms remain unclear, posing challenges for developing effective public health support strategies.[7,8]

The core clinical symptoms of anemia, such as fatigue, weakness, palpitations, and shortness of breath, greatly limit patients’ daily activities and quality of life.[9] Compared to non-anemic patients, anemic patients experience a significantly increased probability of weakness, indicating a close link between anemia and frailty.[10,11] The rationale for selecting frailty as a mediator in this study lies in the physiological mechanism of anemia: chronic tissue hypoxia impairs muscle oxidative metabolism, directly potentially contributing to the physical decline and exhaustion that characterize the frailty phenotype. Frailty, reflecting cumulative physiological vulnerability, may represent a key pathway through which anemia is associated with socioeconomic disadvantage; however, this mechanism has not been adequately examined in adult populations. Generally, due to the significantly reduced physical performance of frail individuals in the workplace, they are at a disadvantage in a competitive job market.[12] Specifically, anemic patients may experience higher accident rates and work errors, declines in decision-making and problem-solving abilities, and difficulties in maintaining efficient work status, which may impair their job performance and development.[13,14] Over time, this may force patients to accept lower occupational status or frequently change jobs, thereby impacting their social status.[15,16] Therefore, managing individual anemia symptoms may lead to considerable improvements in frailty and SES benefits.

The above research reflects a potential possibility that anemic patients may be unable to fully participate in learning and work due to frailty, thereby limiting their socioeconomic development potential.[17,18] However, previous studies have mainly focused on the direct link between anemia and children’s education, as well as frailty. The relationship between anemia and SES, and the mediating role of frailty have yet to be explored. Our study contributes to the literature by novelly examining frailty not merely as a health outcome, but as a functional bridge connecting biological health (anemia) to sociological outcomes (SES). Consequently, the primary hypothesis of this study is that anemia is significantly associated with lower SES, and that frailty acts as a pivotal functional mediator in this relationship. To this end, we hope to provide supportive evidence for this direction through the NHANES large-sample population study, in order to further raise public awareness of the dangers of anemia and promote comprehensive management of anemia.

2. Methods

2.1. Data sources

The dataset utilized in this investigation was sourced from the NHANES database.[19] This extensive database offers a wealth of information encompassing demographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, health statuses, nutritional assessments, and laboratory results. The representativeness of its samples is attributed to a multistage random sampling methodology, coupled with the regular release of new data, which facilitates the monitoring of health trends over time.[20] For the present study, we focused on data spanning the years 2007 to 2018, amounting to a total of 12 years, as earlier cycles were omitted due to the absence of critical variables. Detailed descriptions of the data types and extraction methodologies are available in NHANES publications and related documentation. The research protocol received endorsement from the Ethics Review Board of the US National Center for Health Statistics, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The analysis was restricted to US residents aged between 18 and 79 years. This upper age limit was set to minimize potential bias, as certain frailty assessment components (e.g., physical activity questionnaires) have recognized measurement limitations or higher missing data rates in participants aged 80 and older. To maintain the validity of the findings, individuals lacking essential variables for evaluating SES were excluded from the study. 2.2. The primary variables of this investigation

The variables examined in this research encompassed age, gender (self-reported), race, hemoglobin concentrations (The definition of anemia is a hemoglobin level below 130 g/L in males and below 120 g/L in females),[21] mean cell volume (fL). To further characterize anemia etiologies, anemic participants were classified into 3 subtypes based on mean corpuscular volume: microcytic anemia (< 80 fL), normocytic anemia (80–100 fL), and macrocytic anemia (> 100 fL). Other covariates included smoking habits and alcohol intake.

2.3. Modified frailty assessment[22]

Frailty was defined using a modified Fried phenotype based on 5 components (weakness, low physical activity, exhaustion, slow gait speed, and unintentional weight loss) adapted to available variables, an approach serving as a valid alternative for large-scale research where direct physical measurements are unfeasible.[23,24] Specifically, weakness was operationalized as identifying “difficulty” in lifting or carrying 10 lbs (Variable: PFQ061B/C); low physical activity was identified as the highest quintile of self-reported sedentary minutes stratified by age groups (Variables: PAD680/PAQ665); exhaustion was defined as self-reported feelings of fatigue or diminished energy levels (Variable: MCQ080e); slow gait speed was assessed based on self-reported difficulty walking between rooms on the same floor (Variable: PFQ061H); and unintentional weight loss was determined by a self-reported weight loss of 10 pounds or more over the preceding year (Variables: WHD080/WHD140). Based on these operational definitions, individuals meeting 3 to 5 of these criteria were classified as frail.

2.4. Assessment of SES[23]

In the US NHANES framework, SES was evaluated using self-reported data on family income, occupation, education level, and health insurance, consistent with prior studies.[24] Each variable was categorized into 3 tiers (low, medium, and high) to facilitate practical interpretation while maintaining adequate sample sizes within each category. Family income was operationalized via the family poverty-to-income ratio, which indicates annual family income relative to the federal poverty threshold, allowing for comparability across surveys due to annual adjustments for inflation and family size.[25] Participants were classified according to their poverty-to-income ratio into low (≤ 1), middle (1–4), and high (≥ 4) categories. Education levels were classified into 3 groups: less than a high school diploma, high school graduate or equivalent, and college or higher.[26] Occupation was organized according to a widely recognized socioeconomic index in the US, with each job assessed based on earnings, educational attainment, and prestige. The socioeconomic index spanned from 13.98 to 90.45,[27] allowing for occupational classification into upper (socioeconomic index ≥ 50), lower (socioeconomic index < 50, including retirees and students), and unemployed categories. Health insurance status was delineated into private health insurance (encompassing any form of private insurance, Medigap, or single-service plans), public health insurance only (including Medicare, Medicaid, State Children’s Healthcare Plan, military health services, Indian Health Service, State-Sponsored Health Plan, or other governmental programs), and individuals lacking health insurance.

An overarching SES variable (High or Low) was derived through latent class analysis, incorporating family income level, occupation, education level, and health insurance status. To determine the optimal number of latent classes, we fitted models with 1 to 4 classes. The 2-class solution was selected as the final model based on the lowest Bayesian information criterion and the highest classification certainty (Entropy > 0.6), which provided the most parsimonious and clinically interpretable fit. Based on the item-response probabilities, the class characterized by high probabilities of top-tier income, advanced education, and private insurance was labeled as “High SES,” while the other was labeled as “Low SES” (Table S1).

2.5. Statistical analysis

Given the cross-sectional nature of the NHANES data, the mediation analysis was conducted as an exploratory investigation to generate hypotheses regarding the potential pathway involving frailty, rather than to establish definitive causal mechanisms.

In the present investigation, the analytical framework utilized both laboratory test data and questionnaire survey data. The “Full Sample 2 Year Mobile Examination Center Exam Weight” was employed as the weighting methodology. Since we combined 6 survey cycles (2007–2018), a new 12-year sample weight was constructed by dividing the original 2-year Mobile Examination Center exam weight (WTMEC2YR) by 6, in accordance with National Center for Health Statistics analytical guidelines. To ensure that the study results were representative and to mitigate bias stemming from the complexities of the sampling design, weighted regression analysis was performed utilizing the survey package. While the independent variable was hemoglobin and the dependent variable was SES, other variables were addressed through multiple imputation techniques to optimize the sample size available for analysis.

Considering the variances in anemia diagnostic criteria across genders, coupled with the potential inherent disparities in SES, the analysis was conducted on men and women as 2 distinct cohorts. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) (for non-normally distributed variables), while categorical variables were expressed as frequency (%). The variance inflation factor was employed to identify multicollinearity issues among the variables.

The association between anemia and SES was evaluated using logistic regression model applied to the comprehensive dataset. Following this, logistic regression analyses were undertaken within gender-specific groups based on the presence of anemia and frailty phenotype. Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P values. Three distinct models were utilized: model 1: unadjusted model; model 2: adjusted for age and race; model 3: further adjusted for age, race, smoking, and alcohol consumption.

Moreover, subgroup analyses were performed to ascertain the OR, 95% CI, and P values pertaining to the relationship between anemia and SES across various populations, categorized by age, race, smoking habits, and drinking behaviors. It is important to acknowledge that the reduction in sample size during subgroup analyses may result in inadequate statistical power, potentially leading to some nonsignificant findings. Additionally, mediation analyses were conducted to explore the potential mediating role of frailty in the association between anemia and lower SES. Statistical significance is denoted as follows: *P < .05, **P < .01, ***P < .001.

3. Result

3.1. Clinical characteristics of the included population

A total of 22,851 individuals were included in this study and stratified by sex and anemia status. In the male group, there were 686 patients with anemia and 10,393 non-anemic individuals; in the female group, there were 1509 cases with anemia and 10,263 non-anemic cases. Within this study population, anemia status was significantly associated with gender, age, race, lifestyle (smoking/drinking), daily function (difficulty in activities, weight loss), exhaustion status, and socioeconomic factors (income, education, occupation, insurance). Notably, a higher proportion of low income (males 26.7% vs 20.8%; females 29.6% vs 24.3%) and lower education level (males 33.5% vs 24.7%; females 26.0% vs 22.8%) were observed in the anemia groups (P < .001), providing important baseline evidence for exploring the relationship between anemia and SES (Table 1). To further characterize the population, we briefly summarized demographic characteristics across SES classes. Qualitatively, individuals in the low SES group tended to exhibit older age, higher rates of comorbidities, and a greater prevalence of both anemia and frailty compared to the high SES group. Given the differences in anemia diagnostic criteria and potential SES disparities between females and males, we treated males and females as 2 separate cohorts for subsequent analyses.

3.2. Analysis of the association between anemia and ses

Using logistic regression models, we constructed regression curves for the proportion of anemia and higher SES. It can be seen that as the proportion of anemia increased, the proportion of higher SES decreased, indicating a negative correlation between anemia and high SES. Similar effects were observed in both men and women, with the curve being smoother in women, possibly related to lower anemia thresholds (Figs. 2A and2B). Further assessment of high SES proportions based on anemia and frailty grouping revealed significant reductions in high SES likelihood whether anemia alone (male: OR = 0..57 (0.47,0.68), P < .001; female: OR = 0..78 (0.69–0.88), P < .001) or frailty alone (male: OR = 0..45 (0.38,0.54), P < .001; female: OR = 0..45 (0.39–0.51), P < .001) was present. Participants without anemia or frailty served as the reference group in analyses examining their combined effects. The coexistence of anemia and frailty was associated with the lowest likelihood of high SES (male: OR = 0..32 (0.21–0.50), P < .001; female: OR = 0..40 (0.30–0.54), P < .001), suggesting a joint association of anemia and frailty with lower SES (Table 2). These results also support that anemia has a smaller impact on SES in women, but frailty seems to have similar effects.

Further analysis stratified by anemia subtypes revealed that microcytic anemia was strongly associated with lower SES. Specifically, individuals with concurrent microcytic anemia and frailty had substantially reduced odds of high SES (total population: OR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.22–0.51, P < .001). Similar trends were observed for normocytic anemia, whereas the association for macrocytic anemia combined with frailty did not reach statistical significance (OR = 0.54, P = .307).

Variance inflation factor indicated low collinearity among variables directly included in the model, strengthening the support for the results (Table S2). Further subgroup analysis showed similar trends across subgroups in males and females(Fig. 3): relatively younger individuals (age < 60) had less impact (male: OR = 0..45 (0.38,0.54), P < .001; female: OR = 0..45 (0.39–0.51), P < .001), with some subgroups not showing significant effects, likely due to reduced statistical power from smaller sample sizes.

3.3. The mediating role of frailty

Mediation analysis of frailty was used to evaluate whether frailty plays a role in the association between anemia-related SES damage and the corresponding proportion (Fig. 4). In males, the direct effect of anemia’s SES damage was significant (average direct effect = −0.13 (−0.10–−0.17), average causal mediation effect = −0.020 (−0.01–−0.026), P < .001), with frailty accounting for 12.8% (−8.48–19%, P < .001) of the mediating factor; in females, the direct effect of anemia’s SES damage was also significant (average direct effect = −0.076 (−0.05–−0.10), average causal mediation effect = −0.0074 (−0.0032–−0.011), P < .001), with frailty accounting for 8.88% (4.19–−15%, P < .001) of the mediating factor. These results suggest that the frailty phenotype may play a part in the association between anemia and SES damage. The notably higher mediation proportion in males (12.8 vs 8.88%) suggests that frailty may act as a more prominent pathway in men, potentially reflecting distinct biological mechanisms where male anemia signals more severe physiological decline compared to the often nutritional or reproductive etiologies in women.

4. Discussion

Anemia is a common global health issue that is closely related to both physiological health and SES. The occurrence of anemia is often closely linked to factors such as malnutrition, chronic diseases, and lack of social support. Particularly in developing countries, anemia can lead to severe maternal and infant morbidity, further exacerbating health disparities and socioeconomic burdens.[28,29] Research has shown that anemia is closely associated with individuals’ quality of life, work capacity, and level of social participation, which in turn may affect their economic status and social standing.[30,31] Therefore, understanding the relationship between anemia and SES is crucial for developing effective public health policies.

Using large-sample data from the NHANES database, this study systematically analyzed the association between anemia and SES and found a significant link between anemia and low SES, with frailty phenotypes potentially partly accounting for this association. This finding suggests that the management of anemia should not only focus on physiological indicators but also incorporate social support measures to improve patients’ overall health and economic conditions. These results provide new theoretical support for public health interventions and emphasize the importance of considering individuals’ socioeconomic backgrounds in anemia management.[32,33]

In this study, we explored the relationship between anemia and SES and observed that frailty may be relevant to this association. This finding fills an important knowledge gap in the current literature, especially in understanding how physiological conditions such as anemia may be associated with socioeconomic factors. Our research is among the first to identify a significant negative association between anemia and SES in the general population and to suggest that frailty phenotypes may be relevant to this relationship.[34] By comprehensively analyzing large-sample data from the NHANES database, our results highlight that anemia and frailty are not only health issues but also factors associated with socioeconomic disadvantage; this perspective has not been adequately explored in previous literature.

From a hematological perspective, the heterogeneity of anemia etiologies may differentially influence the observed associations. In younger women, anemia is predominantly microcytic, often stemming from iron deficiency due to menstruation or pregnancy. While often considered manageable, the resulting chronic fatigue and cognitive deficits may cumulatively undermine educational attainment and labor productivity, thereby being associated with lower SES. Conversely, in older populations, anemia is frequently macrocytic or normocytic (anemia of chronic disease). These forms are typically associated with neurological degeneration and severe comorbidities, which may significantly exacerbate frailty and be related to reduced socioeconomic independence. Furthermore, the sex disparity observed warrants attention; while female anemia is often reproductive or nutritional in origin, anemia in men is less common and may signal more profound pathological underlying conditions. This suggests that for men, anemia may represent a marker of greater physiological collapse, potentially leading to a stronger association with frailty status and economic stability. However, these subtype-specific findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the absence of direct biomarkers limits precise etiological classification.

Our findings have important implications for clinical practice and policy-making. First, these findings suggest that clinicians managing patients with anemia should not only focus on physiological indicators but also consider patients’ social support and economic conditions. Early screening and intervention for anemia, combined with frailty management strategies, may help improve patients’ overall health and SES, thereby potentially reducing health disparities associated with anemia.[35] Furthermore, at the policy level, it may be beneficial to strengthen the screening and management of anemia and frailty to improve public health outcomes and reduce healthcare resource waste.

However, it is important to note that this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design cannot determine the direction of causality, which may affect the interpretation of the results. Specifically, we acknowledge the potential for reverse causation, where low SES limits access to nutrient-rich foods and healthcare services, thereby increasing the risk of anemia, rather than anemia solely contributing to lower SES. In addition, self-reported data used in the study may be subject to recall bias, so the impact on the results should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, unadjusted potential confounding factors (e.g., chronic disease burden) may also influence our findings, and future research should employ more rigorous study designs and data analysis methods, particularly in longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes and multiple centers, to further explore the temporal relationship and potential underlying mechanisms between anemia and SES. An additional limitation is the lack of specific biomarkers (e.g., ferritin, vitamin B12) to definitively confirm anemia etiologies, although we utilized mean corpuscular volume classification as a clinically relevant proxy. In subgroup analyses, some subgroups with insufficient sample sizes did not reach statistical significance, limiting the statistical power. Additionally, the assessment of frailty phenotypes was not validated through objective measurements, which may affect the reliability of the results. Furthermore, the interpretation of the mediation analysis warrants caution. Mediation modeling relies on strong assumptions that are difficult to fully verify in a cross-sectional design. Therefore, these mediation findings should be interpreted as associative and hypothesis-generating, requiring validation in longitudinal cohorts. These limitations remind us to interpret the findings cautiously and provide directions for future research improvements.

In conclusion, this study systematically reveals the association between anemia and low SES, along with the possible role of frailty in this relationship, with particular emphasis on the vulnerability of women and younger populations. Future longitudinal studies should aim to verify temporal relationships and explore effective intervention strategies for the comorbidity of anemia and frailty to break the vicious cycle of health inequality. These findings suggest that anemia management strategies may benefit from incorporating assessments of frailty and broader socioeconomic context.
