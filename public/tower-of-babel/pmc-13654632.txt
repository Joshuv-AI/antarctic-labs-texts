The Asian perspective on xenotransplantation: bridging the organ gap through regional collaboration and tailored clinical protocols
Authors: Kunxiong Wang, Xiayu Lei, Tao Li, Haiting Shi, Hidetaka Hara, Yi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractXenotransplantation using genetically engineered porcine organs has emerged as a transformative strategy to address the critical organ shortage crisis in Asia, where low deceased donation rates and high disease burden create a persistent supply-demand gap. This review examines the Asian perspective on xenotransplantation across five domains: sociocultural acceptance, technical advances in multigene-edited pig platforms, clinical protocol optimization for Asian recipients, regulatory harmonization through the Asian Xenotransplantation Association, and infrastructure development including hybrid clinical centers and capacity-building initiatives. Asian countries, particularly China, South Korea, and Japan, have demonstrated substantial progress in gene editing, designated pathogen-free breeding, and translational research. However, clinical success requires region-specific immunosuppressive protocols, pharmacogenomic adaptation, endemic infectious disease surveillance, and culturally sensitive public engagement. Regional collaboration through harmonized regulatory frameworks, multinational trial networks, and equitable access models will be essential to ensure that xenotransplantation benefits all Asian populations. This review proposes a distinct "Asian way" that integrates scientific excellence with cultural diversity and regional solidarity to shape the future of transplantation medicine.Keywords: Xenotransplantation, Organ shortage, Gene editing, Asia, International cooperation

INTRODUCTION

The Urgent Need for Xenotransplantation in Asia

The fundamental impetus for advancing xenotransplantation in Asia is the widening gap between the increasing burden of end-stage organ failure and the limited availability of human donor organs. Population aging, the rising prevalence of chronic kidney disease, liver failure, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, and improvements in critical care have expanded the number of potential transplant candidates, while the supply of transplantable human organs remains far below clinical demand [1].

This shortage is particularly pronounced in Asia. Many countries continue to face low deceased-donor rates, underdeveloped donation systems, cultural hesitation toward organ donation, and uneven access to transplant services. In China, a large number of patients require organ transplantation each year, but only a fraction ultimately undergo transplant surgery. Japan, despite its advanced healthcare system and strong expertise in transplantation medicine, has historically maintained a low deceased-donor rate, partly because of cultural, legal, and social factors related to brain-death determination and organ donation. South Korea also faces a substantial transplant gap, with many patients awaiting transplantation while deceased-donor availability remains limited [2].

These regional realities indicate that Asia has both an urgent clinical need and a compelling public health rationale for developing alternative sources of transplantable organs. Xenotransplantation, particularly the use of genetically engineered porcine organs, therefore represents a potentially transformative strategy to address the long-standing organ shortage in Asia [3].

Asia’s Unique Advantages and the Need for Regional Integration

Recent landmark clinical experiences have demonstrated that Asia is not only developing the technological infrastructure for xenotransplantation but has already begun translating these advances into human application. In 2025, a team at Xijing Hospital reported the first gene-edited pig-to-human liver xenotransplantation in a brain-dead recipient, with the graft functioning for 10 days without evidence of hyperacute rejection [4]. Subsequently, the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University performed the world’s first auxiliary pig liver xenotransplantation in a living recipient, who survived for 171 days, with graft failure ultimately attributed to xenotransplantation-associated thrombotic microangiopathy [5]. Also in 2025, the first pig-to-human lung xenotransplantation was successfully performed in China, maintaining graft viability and gas exchange for 9 days [6]. In 2026, a team from Guangxi Medical University reported the first combined whole pig liver and bilateral kidney xenotransplantation in a brain-dead recipient [7]. These pioneering achievements, together with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s authorization of the first kidney xenotransplantation trials in 2025 and the initiation of the first regulated multicenter study in November 2025, have collectively transformed the field from preclinical promise to clinical reality.

Beyond unmet clinical need, Asia possesses several region-specific strengths that may support the development of xenotransplantation. These include rapidly advancing genome-editing platforms, somatic cell nuclear transfer technologies, large-animal breeding capacity, and designated pathogen-free animal husbandry. The region also has growing expertise in transplantation surgery, infectious disease surveillance, and clinical trial infrastructure [8].

These capacities are distributed unevenly but complementarily across the region, with different countries contributing strengths in donor-animal engineering, large-animal models, transplant immunology, biomedical regulation, infectious disease surveillance, and clinical trial coordination. Rather than relying on isolated national programs, an integrated Asian framework could allow countries with different strengths to contribute to a shared xenotransplantation ecosystem.

Unlike previous reviews that have primarily focused on technical breakthroughs in North America and Europe, this review highlights the clinical demand, cultural context, technological capacity, regulatory challenges, and collaborative opportunities that are specific to Asia (Fig. 1). All transplantation-related considerations in this review adhere to the principles of the Declaration of Istanbul on Organ Trafficking and Transplant Tourism.

SOCIOCULTURAL AND RELIGIOUS LANDSCAPES

Asia’s cultural and religious landscape is highly diverse, and this diversity shapes public attitudes toward xenotransplantation. A systematic understanding of these dimensions is essential for developing regionally tailored clinical protocols, informed consent procedures, and public communication strategies.

Major religious and philosophical traditions in Asia generally do not impose absolute prohibitions on xenotransplantation, although they emphasize different ethical conditions. Islam stresses medical necessity, respect for animal dignity, and the absence of acceptable alternatives. Hinduism focuses on the overall well-being of life. Buddhism and Confucianism often adopt pragmatic and compassionate positions grounded in the relief of suffering. Christian traditions generally support transplantation when personal identity, human dignity, and the duty to preserve life are respected [9]. These positions should not be interpreted as uniform views within each tradition, because interpretations may vary according to local religious authorities, cultural practices, and individual beliefs.

These convergences suggest that xenotransplantation in Asia may not face insurmountable religious resistance. However, acceptance cannot be assumed. The use of porcine organs may raise particular concerns in Muslim communities, while broader questions regarding animal welfare, bodily integrity, family consent, and public health risk may influence social attitudes across multiple cultural settings.

Successful implementation will therefore require culturally sensitive communication, transparent risk disclosure, strong animal-welfare safeguards, and sustained engagement with patients, families, religious leaders, ethicists, and community representatives. Public trust should be built before clinical crises occur, not merely in response to adverse events. In Asia, where family-based decision-making and community values often influence medical choices, culturally informed engagement will be as important as scientific innovation.

A further concern is distributive justice. If xenotransplantation becomes clinically feasible but remains available only in a small number of wealthy urban centers, it may deepen existing inequalities in access to transplantation. Ethical development in Asia should therefore include early consideration of affordability, allocation principles, reimbursement, and integration with existing deceased- and living-donor transplant systems. Xenotransplantation should complement, rather than replace, efforts to strengthen human organ donation.

REGIONAL TECHNICAL AND CLINICAL STRENGTHS

Advances in Genetically Engineered Porcine Models

The development of genetically engineered porcine donors remains the cornerstone of clinical translation. Pigs offer anatomical and physiological compatibility with humans, a short reproductive cycle, and high amenability to genetic modification [10]. Unmodified porcine organs trigger robust human immune responses, including hyperacute rejection, acute vascular rejection, and coagulation dysregulation [11]. The historic knockout of GGTA1 eliminated galactose-α1,3-galactose–mediated hyperacute rejection [12], while subsequent deletion of CMAH and B4GALNT2 further addressed non-Gal carbohydrate antigens [13]. Beyond antigen deletion, the introduction of human complement regulators (CD46, CD55, CD59), coagulation modulators (thrombomodulin, EPCR, TFPI, CD39), and immunomodulatory molecules (CD47, HLA-E, PD-L1) has collectively improved cross-species compatibility [14,15].

Importantly, Asian countries have leveraged these universal technologies to build distinct, complementary regional strengths. China has focused on large-scale, multigene engineering capacity, rapidly establishing CRISPR/Cas-mediated platforms that integrate triple-knockout with multiple human protective transgenes (e.g., triple knockout [TKO]/hCD55/hTM/hEPCR), positioning itself as a major supplier of clinical-grade donor pigs. In contrast, South Korea has excelled in the translational integration of these donor models, building robust pathogen-controlled breeding systems and sophisticated nonhuman primate transplantation models that directly inform clinical protocol design, demonstrating a unique ability to bridge veterinary science, immunology, and regulatory science. Japan has leveraged its world-class expertise in transplantation immunology and regenerative medicine, contributing critical knowledge on organ preservation, porcine endogenous retrovirus (PERV) risk assessment, and the ethical governance frameworks necessary for high-quality clinical research. Meanwhile, Singapore and other regional biomedical hubs have added specialized capabilities in regulatory science, digital health infrastructure, and international trial coordination [14–16]. This complementary landscape underscores that Asia's strength lies not in a single national program, but in the potential synergy of its diverse technical ecosystems. However, shared biosafety challenges persist. PERVs, integrated into the porcine genome, remain a theoretical zoonotic risk despite no confirmed human transmission, necessitating combined donor screening, recipient monitoring, and standardized biobanking [17,18]. Furthermore, the production of clinical-grade donor pigs requires designated pathogen-free (DPF) facilities with stringent microbiological surveillance and traceable genetic documentation [19]. As infectious risks transcend national borders, harmonizing these minimum biosafety standards across Asia is imperative (Table 1, Fig. 2).

Optimizing Clinical Protocols for Asian Recipients

Although donor pig engineering is essential, clinical success depends on recipient-centered protocols that address cross-species immune, inflammatory, coagulation, infectious, and pharmacological barriers. Current evidence supports costimulation blockade as a cornerstone, with anti-CD40-based regimens suppressing T cell-dependent B cell activation, though the optimal combination remains under investigation [20,21]. Immunological and human leukocyte antigen considerations

Although xenotransplantation does not involve conventional donor-recipient human leukocyte antigen (HLA) matching, the recipient's HLA background remains clinically relevant. HLA class II alleles influence the repertoire of T cell responses to porcine xenoantigens, and certain HLA haplotypes common in Asian populations (such as HLA-A*11:01, HLA-B*46:01, and HLA-DRB1*09:01) may shape the magnitude and specificity of anti-pig cellular and humoral immune responses [22–24]. Additionally, HLA polymorphism affects the immunogenicity of therapeutic antibodies used in induction and maintenance regimens [25]. Baseline immune profiling (including anti-pig antibody assays, complement activity, natural killer cell phenotyping, and inflammatory biomarkers) should therefore be incorporated into early Asian trials, with particular attention to HLA-associated response variability [27].

Pharmacogenomic and metabolic differences

More directly, polymorphisms in drug-metabolizing enzymes have substantial clinical implications. The CYP3A5*3 loss-of-function allele is carried by approximately 70% to 73% of East Asians and 80% to 95% of individuals of European ancestry. Consequently, a higher proportion of East Asians are CYP3A5 expressers (carriers of the CYP3A5*1 allele), who require higher weight-based tacrolimus doses to achieve therapeutic trough concentrations, as recommended by the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) guidelines [28]. Similar population-specific differences affect mTOR inhibitors (e.g., CYP3A4/CYP3A5 polymorphisms) and mycophenolate metabolism (UGT1A9 variants) [29–31]. Body size, hepatic function, and nutritional status also differ systematically across Asian subpopulations. Therefore, immunosuppressive protocols must be genotype-guided and locally calibrated, rather than directly adopting Western dosing nomograms. Moreover, Asia's extensive experience with ABO-incompatible kidney transplantation, where desensitization and B cell immunomodulation have been refined in large patient series, provides translational insights for managing the humoral immune responses critical to xenograft survival [34].

Infectious disease risk and surveillance

The epidemiology of transplant-relevant infections varies substantially across Asia. Chronic hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) prevalence ranges from 2% to 4% in Japan and South Korea, 6% to 8% in China, and over 10% in parts of Southeast Asia, posing significant reactivation risk under intensive immunosuppression [32]. Tuberculosis incidence rates also vary widely, with many Asian countries classified as intermediate- to high-burden settings, necessitating routine latent tuberculosis screening and prophylaxis. Endemic mycoses (e.g., talaromycosis), parasitic infections (e.g., strongyloidiasis), and vector-borne diseases (e.g., dengue) present additional regional challenges rarely addressed in Western guidelines [33]. Asian protocols must therefore integrate comprehensive pretransplant screening, vaccination assessment (hepatitis B, influenza, pneumococcal), tailored prophylaxis, and lifelong surveillance extending beyond the postoperative period to capture opportunistic and zoonotic infections. Precision-medicine approaches (leveraging Asia's advances in digital health, artificial intelligence-assisted risk tools, and multicenter registries) can integrate these diverse datasets for individualized risk prediction and early rejection detection.

REGULATORY HARMONY AND REGIONAL LEADERSHIP

Regional Regulatory Harmonization and the Role of the Asian Xenotransplantation Association

The clinical translation of xenotransplantation requires a regulatory framework broader than that used for conventional allotransplantation. In addition to recipient safety and graft outcomes, regulatory systems must address genetically engineered donor animals, designated pathogen-free breeding, animal welfare, zoonotic infection control, long-term recipient surveillance, and cross-border data sharing [35]. In Asia, where healthcare systems, legal traditions, and scientific infrastructures vary widely, regulatory harmonization is essential for safe and sustainable development.

The Asian Xenotransplantation Association (AXA) can serve as a central regional platform for this process, not as a supranational regulatory authority, but as a professional and scientific body that promotes convergence among national systems through consensus standards and shared data infrastructure. AXA could develop minimum acceptable standards for donor animal qualification, clinical-grade facility certification, preclinical evidence requirements, recipient eligibility, informed consent, infection surveillance, and adverse-event reporting [8]. A regional xenotransplantation registry, collecting standardized data on donor pig characteristics, immunosuppressive regimens, graft function, rejection episodes, and infectious complications, would improve transparency and enable early detection of safety signals [1].

Linked to this harmonization effort, a multinational clinical trial network is essential. Because early xenotransplantation trials will involve small numbers of carefully selected patients, isolated single-center studies may produce fragmented evidence [36]. A coordinated Asian trial network, jointly supported by AXA and the Asian Society of Transplantation (AST), would improve patient safety, standardize protocols, and facilitate meaningful outcome comparisons across populations [37]. Participating centers should use harmonized eligibility criteria, monitoring schedules, and adverse-event definitions, while preserving national regulatory authority. Such a network could adopt a tiered structure: trial-leading centers, donor-animal production centers, reference laboratories, and registry-participating hospitals (Fig. 4).

National Regulatory Experiences and Legislative Case Studies

Although no Asian country has yet established a regulatory system dedicated exclusively to clinical xenotransplantation, several national experiences provide valuable lessons.

South Korea offers the most developed legislative framework. The Act on Safety and Support for Advanced Regenerative Medicine and Advanced Biopharmaceuticals, promulgated in August 2019 and effective from August 2020, provides a comprehensive regulatory pathway for advanced regenerative medicine products, including genetically modified cell- and tissue-based therapies [38]. Importantly, the Act was amended on February 20, 2024, effective February 21, 2025. This amendment introduced a “treatment pathway” alongside the existing clinical research pathway and removed the restriction that had previously limited clinical research to serious, rare, or intractable diseases. These changes significantly expand the scope of clinical application and make the regulatory architecture even more directly relevant to xenotransplantation. Under this Act, the Korea National Institute of Health operates a rigorous review system for preclinical data, manufacturing standards, and clinical trial design. While the Act does not explicitly address whole-organ xenotransplantation, its regulatory architecture (including risk-based classification, conditional approval pathways, and postmarket surveillance) offers a model adaptable to xenotransplantation. South Korea’s experience also highlights the importance of integrating transplantation regulation, animal biotechnology oversight, and infectious disease control into a coherent governance framework [39].

China has rapidly developed technical capacity in genome editing, donor pig production, and large-animal models, but its regulatory pathway for clinical xenotransplantation remains in evolution. In 2022, a national expert consensus on clinical trials of human xenotransplantation was published, outlining principles for donor animal qualification, preclinical evidence requirements, and patient monitoring [40]. China’s future challenge will be to translate this consensus into legally enforceable regulatory pathways with transparent, auditable standards.

Japan contributes through its long-established frameworks for transplantation ethics, regenerative medicine governance, and high-quality clinical research. The Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine (2013) and the Pharmaceutical and Medical Devices Act provide oversight for cell- and tissue-based products, though they do not directly cover vascularized xenografts [26]. Japan’s experience in risk-sensitive oversight, long-term follow-up, and public communication is particularly relevant to the societal dimensions of xenotransplantation [19].

These national experiences suggest that Asia does not require a single uniform legal system. Instead, the region needs compatible regulatory principles, shared minimum safety standards, interoperable data systems, and transparent reporting mechanisms. Ethical Governance and Risk Communication

Xenotransplantation raises distinctive ethical challenges beyond those of conventional transplantation. These include the need for lifelong infectious surveillance, restrictions on blood/tissue donation for recipients, potential implications for family contacts, and the balance between individual benefit and public health protection [41,42]. Informed consent must be more comprehensive than in standard transplantation, covering the experimental nature of the procedure, uncertain graft durability, surveillance obligations, and theoretical zoonotic risks.

Animal welfare must be treated as a core ethical component, not a secondary technical issue. Clinical-grade donor pigs must be bred, housed, and procured under standards that minimize suffering. Public accountability and transparent risk communication are essential to maintain trust, requiring sustained engagement with patient groups, religious leaders, and community representatives.

Ultimately, regulatory leadership in Asia should aim not merely to accelerate trials, but to build a trustworthy governance ecosystem that combines scientific rigor, ethical transparency, and long-term accountability.

INFRASTRUCTURE DEVELOPMENT AND GLOBAL SOLIDARITY

Building Clinical-Grade Infrastructure for Xenotransplantation

The successful implementation of xenotransplantation in Asia will require integrated infrastructure connecting donor animal production, organ procurement, clinical transplantation, immune monitoring, infectious disease surveillance, and long-term recipient follow-up. Although several Asian countries already possess strong capabilities in genome editing, large-animal research, transplant surgery, and biomedical regulation, these capacities remain unevenly distributed.

A key priority is the development of certified clinical-grade donor animal facilities operating under standardized quality-management systems, with validated protocols for breeding, genetic documentation, microbiological monitoring, quarantine, organ procurement, and traceability [43]. However, not every country needs to build a full donor pig production system independently. A regional network of accredited facilities could allow countries with advanced animal biotechnology platforms to support broader clinical translation while maintaining shared biosafety and quality standards [8].

Hybrid clinical centers may be particularly suitable for the Asian urban medical landscape [18]. These centers could integrate porcine organ procurement pathways, specialized surgical suites, immunology laboratories, infectious disease units, biobanks, and digital monitoring systems within or adjacent to major transplant hospitals. Such a model would improve coordination between animal-source organ production and human clinical care while maintaining biosafety separation and regulatory oversight [35].

Laboratory infrastructure will also be essential. Xenotransplantation requires assays not routinely used in conventional transplantation, including anti-pig antibody testing, complement activation assays, cellular immune profiling, coagulation monitoring, donor-derived molecular diagnostics, and pathogen surveillance. Regional reference laboratories could provide quality control, proficiency testing, and confirmatory diagnostics for early clinical trials [44].

Workforce Training, Capacity Building, and the “Spirit of Dana”

Infrastructure development must also include workforce training and regional capacity building. Xenotransplantation requires collaboration among transplant surgeons, nephrologists, hepatologists, cardiologists, intensivists, immunologists, infectious disease specialists, veterinarians, embryologists, genetic engineers, pathologists, ethicists, trial coordinators, and regulatory experts. Few institutions currently possess all these capabilities in an integrated form.

Regional training programs could help close this gap. AXA, AST, and partner institutions may organize workshops, fellowships, simulation-based surgical training, laboratory standardization courses, regulatory training, and multidisciplinary case conferences. Training should cover not only technical procedures but also biosafety, informed consent, animal welfare, adverse-event reporting, long-term surveillance, and communication with patients and the public [8].

The “Spirit of Dana”, rooted in South and Southeast Asian traditions of generosity and shared responsibility, provides a useful ethical and practical framework for expanding the xenotransplantation ecosystem across Asia [45]. In this context, Dana can be interpreted as a commitment to ensuring that developing countries are not excluded from the xenotransplantation era. Capacity-building efforts in donation coordination, public education, hospital networking, and transplant logistics, such as those underway in Nepal’s organ procurement organization development, illustrate how broader transplant infrastructure can be strengthened even in resource-limited settings [46]. Although xenotransplantation and human organ donation are distinct, they share foundational needs for public trust, transparent governance, trained personnel, and reliable clinical infrastructure.

An optimal Asian strategy may therefore need to balance technological innovation with regional inclusion, enabling countries at different stages of development to participate meaningfully in the field without compromising rigorous safety standards.

Equitable Access and Global Solidarity

As xenotransplantation moves from experimental trials toward possible clinical application, equitable access will become increasingly important [47]. Policymakers and professional societies should begin considering cost, reimbursement, patient selection, allocation principles, and integration with existing transplant systems [48]. Xenotransplantation should complement rather than undermine deceased donation and living donation programs.

Although this review focuses on Asia, xenotransplantation is inherently global. Zoonotic risks, donor animal standards, long-term surveillance, and data transparency cannot be treated as purely national concerns [49]. Asia can contribute to global solidarity by generating high-quality preclinical and clinical data from diverse populations, developing harmonized standards, and providing models of culturally sensitive public engagement.

Global collaboration should also be reciprocal. Asian countries are not merely recipients of technologies developed elsewhere; they are active contributors in genome editing, donor pig production, preclinical models, clinical surgery, infectious disease surveillance, and regulatory innovation. A balanced global framework should promote shared authorship, joint trials, interoperable registries, and equitable access to intellectual and clinical resources [50].

CONCLUSION

Xenotransplantation has entered a new phase of clinical possibility, and for Asia, facing the world’s largest gap between organ demand and supply, this moment carries profound significance. With rapidly expanding strengths in genome editing, donor pig production, transplant surgery, regulatory science, and digital health, Asia is positioned to become a leader rather than a follower in this field.

However, Asian development should not simply replicate Western models. A distinct “Asian way” is emerging, characterized by four pillars: (1) regionally tailored clinical protocols that account for HLA distribution, pharmacogenomic diversity, and endemic infectious risks; (2) culturally grounded ethical governance engaging religious and community values; (3) regulatory harmonization through AXA and AST to establish shared safety standards; and (4) inclusive infrastructure extending benefits beyond wealthy urban centers. This framework embeds scientific excellence within Asia’s sociocultural realities while distributing responsibilities and opportunities across countries with complementary strengths.

Looking forward, the transformative potential of xenotransplantation lies not only in alleviating the organ crisis but also in strengthening public health systems across the region. Success will require sustained investment in donor facilities, hybrid centers, workforce training, and transparent public engagement. Regional collaboration (through shared standards, joint trials, and interoperable registries) will ensure that xenotransplantation develops safely, ethically, and equitably.

With urgent clinical need, expanding technical capacity, and growing regional leadership, Asia is well positioned to define a new era in transplantation medicine, one that is inclusive, collaborative, and distinctly Asian.
