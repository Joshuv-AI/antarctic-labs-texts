A chromosome-level assembly of the alpine snow alga Chloromonas typhlos
Authors: Gai Liu, Wei Luo, Sheng Yao, Ming-kun Yang, Xiaoyu Yuan, Zhao Peng, Xuan Deng, Cuihong Wan, Chengtian Zhao, Cheng-cai Zhang, Wei Miao, Senjie Lin, Kaiyao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChloromonas typhlos is a cosmopolitan alpine snow alga distributed across continents, and its blooming accelerates snow melting by decreasing the amount of snow albedo. To elucidate the genetic traits underlying the adaptation of C. typhlos to the alpine habitat, we combined PacBio sequencing and Hi-C to generate a high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly (contig N50: 1.29 Mb; scaffold N50: 7.23 Mb) with 31 chromosomes and a genome size of 200.86 Mb. Repetitive elements constituted 11.05% of the genome, and 16,133 protein-coding genes were predicted, of which 82% were functionally annotated. This study provides a set of omics resources both for snow algae and the genus Chloromonas.Subject terms: Evolutionary biology, Evolutionary genetics, Chloroplasts

Background & Summary

Despite the harsh conditions of snowfields, snow algae survive and thrive on the snow’s surface. They maintain highly efficient carbon fixation, supporting the cold biological community comprising bacteria, archaea, fungi, worms, and insects1. Moreover, snow algae are believed to impose positive feedback on the cryosphere retreat by robustly synthesizing pigment, decreasing the surface albedo, and accelerating the melting of snow and ice2,3. Modern taxonomic classification revealed that most isolated snow algae belong to Chlamydomonadales (Chorophyta) but are widely categorized within the genera, Chloromonas, Chlainomonas, Sanguina, Limnomona, Microglena, etc.4–7.

High-quality genomes can promote the accuracy of snow algae classification, and deepen our understanding of the adaptive mechanisms. Sequencing of non-axenic snow alga Sanguina aurantia, based on Nanopore and Illumina sequencing, generated two genome variants with different sizes (96 Mb and 102 Mb)8. The snow alga cf. Sphaerocystis sp. CCCryo 101-99 is a closed relative species to the Sanguina clade6, and Çiftçi et al. assembled a genome with 122.5 Mb in length9. The psychrophilic Arctic snow alga Limnomonas spitsbergensis belongs to the Moewusinia clade, and Hulatt et al. published a 260.25 Mb sized genome10. Considering the diversity of snow algae, new snow algal models are needed to explore the molecular adaptation mechanisms, especially the genus with limited genomic resources.

The snow alga Chloromonas typhlos is a psychrotolerant species and belongs to the clade Chloromonadinia, which is far divergent from the three recently sequenced snow algae species. C. typhlos exhibits a spheroid cell morphology, with a distinguishable pyrenoid in the center (Fig. 1). C. typhlos has been used as a model strain in several snow algal studies, but limited genomic resources are available6,11–15. In this study, we combined PacBio long reads, Illumina short paired-end reads and high-throughput chromosomal conformation capture (Hi-C) sequencing to generate a chromosome-level genome for C. typhlos. The annotation of the C. typhlos nuclear genome was performed based on EST evidence integrating the full-length transcripts generated by Isoform-Sequencing (Iso-Seq) and RNA-Seq data, and protein homology-based evidence. A total of 16,133 genes were characterized, and up to 31.0% annotated genes were supported by proteomic peptide evidence, suggesting a relatively high-quality genome annotation was achieved (Fig. 1 and Table 1). Our study will provide valuable genomic resources for the future research of the snow algae C. typhlos and likely underpin the mechanisms explaining how snow algae were evolutionary adapted to the alpine habitat.

Methods

Algal strains and culture conditions

The unicellular alpine alga C. typhlos UTEX 2824 was isolated by William H. Thomas near Saddlebag Lake (37°58′22.7″N 119°16′32.5″W, ~3,100 meters above sea level (a.s.l)) (Fig. 2). The lake is in the Sierra Nevada Mountains in California, where the average day/night temperature is 6 °C /−3 °C in the spring and 22 °C /11 °C in the summer16. C. typhlos strain in this study was obtained from the Culture Collection of Algae at the University of Texas at Austin (UTEX). Axenic single algal colony was picked up from the solid (1.5% agar) Bold 1NV (B1NV, recipe details provided by UTEX) medium plate supplemented with 50 μg mL−1 ampicillin and 10 μg mL−1 chloramphenicol and maintained for genome sequencing and research in this study. The C. typhlos strain was maintained on solid B1NV medium and cultured in liquid B1NV medium shaking at 120 rpm, in diurnal (12 h light, 12 h dark) conditions at 22 °C with the light intensity of ~50 μmol photon m−1 s−2.

Genomic DNA sample preparation

C. typhlos cells (3 × 107 cells) were collected, and the genomic DNA was extracted using the cetyltrimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) method, followed by purification with Grandomics Genomic kit according to the manual. The isolated genomic DNA was run on a 1% agarose gel to check for degradation and contamination. DNA purity was also measured using a NanoDrop™ One UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with acceptable A260/A280 ratios ranging from 1.8 to 2.0 and A260/A230 ratios between 2.0 and 2.2. Finally, DNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit® 4.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA).

Library preparation and sequencing

C. typhlos genomic DNA libraries for the Illumina platform were generated using Truseq Nano DNA HT Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA was fragmented to 350 bp by sonication, polished, A-tailed, and ligated to sequencing adapters. The libraries were further sequenced using the Novaseq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and 72.89 million 150 bp clean paired-end reads were generated after filtration using fastp (0.23.2)17.

The 20 kb libraries for the PacBio Sequel I platform were constructed according to PacBio’s standard protocol (Pacific Biosciences, CA, USA). In brief, 5 µg C. typhlos genomic DNA was sheared using g-TUBEs (Covaris, USA). Single-stranded overhangs were removed, and the DNA fragments were repaired and ligated with hairpin adaptors. Link-failed fragments were eliminated using ExoIII and ExoVII exonuclease, and size selection of target fragments was performed with BluePippin (Sage Science, USA). Then, the SMRTbell library was purified using AMPure PB beads. The size distribution of the library fragments was then analyzed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Sequencing was performed on a PacBio Sequel instrument with Sequencing Primer V4 and Sequel Binding Kit 3.0 (Grandomics). A total of 12.9 Gb high-quality PacBio subreads were obtained, providing 63-fold genome coverage for genome assembly. The final clean data had a subread N50 of 10.0 kb.

For Hi-C sequencing, C. typhlos nuclei were fixed with 2% formaldehyde, digested with DpnII, and marked by incubating with biotin-14-dCTP to build the Hi-C library. Then, the Hi-C libraries were quantified and sequenced using the Illumina Novaseq platform, and a total of 32.0 Gb 150-bp paired-end Hi-C data were generated.

Estimation of genome size

A total of 10.57 Gb 150 bp paired-end reads, filtered to remove contaminated reads, and organelle genome sequences using blastn with short reads were used to estimate the C. typhlos genome size by analyzing the 21 K-mer frequency using jellyfish (v 2.3.0). The output file was used to estimate the genome size using GenomeScope (v 2.0)18. Finally, the genome size of C. typhlos was determined to be 204.18 Mb (Fig. 3).

Genome size evaluation. The genome size of C. typhlos was evaluated based on the distribution of K-mer frequency of Illumina reads using a 21-mer spectrum.

Nuclear genome assembly

A total of 12.9 Gb (subreads from 3 cells, 63.18-fold coverage) long reads generated by the PacBio Sequel I platform were de novo assembled into the preliminary contigs using the HGAP (Hierarchical Genome Assembly Process) module provided in the SMRTLink package (v5.1.0.26412, PacBio). The preliminary contigs polished by Arrow integrated in the SMRTLink package, were further polished by Pilon (v1.2) to correct single nucleotide errors19 and searched against the NCBI non-redundant nucleotide (nt) database using MegaBLAST to eliminate putative contamination. Then, Hi-C data were integrated to construct the chromosome-level genome. In brief, 207.2 million cleaned paired-end reads were generated from the libraries and mapped to the draft genome using bowtie2 (v2.3.2) with the parameter “-end-to-end–very-sensitive -L 30”. Interaction-paired reads were verified and retained by HiC-Pro (v2.8.1) from unique mapped paired-end reads for further analysis. Invalid read pairs, including dangling-end, self-cycle, re-ligation, and dumped products, were filtered by HiC-Pro (v2.8.1) (https://github.com/nservant/HiC-Pro). The scaffolds were further clustered, ordered, and oriented onto chromosomes by LACHESIS (https://github.com/shendurelab/LACHESIS), with parameters “CLUSTER_MIN_RE_SITES = 100, CLUSTER_MAX_LINK_DENSITY = 2.5, CLUSTER NONINFORMATIVE RATIO = 1.4, ORDER MIN N RES IN TRUNK = 60, ORDER MIN N RES IN SHREDS = 60”. Finally, placement and orientation errors exhibiting obvious discrete chromatin interaction patterns were manually adjusted (Fig. 4; Table 2).

Organelle genome assembly and annotation

The organelle genomes were extracted from the contigs assembled using Flyer23 (v2.8.2, meta mode), polished by Pilon, and annotated using the organelle genome annotation pipeline GeSeq.24. The maps of organellar genomes (chloroplast and mitochondria) were created by OGDRAW25. The size of mitochondrial genome is 31,308 bp (GC% = 36.13%), and the size of chloroplast genome is 502,407 bp (GC% = 33.58%), respectively (Figs. 5 and 6). The chloroplast genome of C. typhlos consisted of one short-single copy (217 kb) and one long-single copy (239 kb), divided by two 23 kb inverted repeats. The chloroplast genome of C. typhlos encoded 94 genes, including 29 tRNA, 4 rRNA, and 61 chloroplast protein genes. Notably, 16 chloroplast-encoded genes contained 24 introns, including genes coding tRNA, photosystem I/II subunits, Rubisco large subunit, ribosomal proteins, RNA polymerase and nuclear encoded chloroplast protein import machinery subunit Ycf126. The mitochondrial genome of C. typhlos encoded 15 genes, including 7 tRNA, 1 rRNA, and 7 mitochondrial protein genes.

Transcripts assembly

Total RNA was extracted from C. typhlos cells using CTAB-LiCl method. RNA integrity was evaluated with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and agarose gel electrophoresis. The purity and concentration of the RNA were determined using the Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 1 μg total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using SMARTer® PCR cDNA Synthesis Kit (PacBio). Repaired cDNAs were ligated to sequencing adapters using SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences). The SMRTbell template was annealed to Sequencing Primer V4, bound to polymerase, and sequenced on the PacBio Sequel platform using Sequel Binding Kit 3.0 (Pacific Biosciences). Long reads produced by the PacBio Sequel platform were processed using modules in the IsoSeq3 pipeline (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/IsoSeq3; PacBio). In brief, 403,953 Circular consensus sequences (CCS) were obtained using the ‘ccs’ module with parameters “–min-passes 3–min-length 50–max-length 15000–min-rq 0.9”, and barcode primers and adapters were removed using ‘lima’ module to generate 399,916 full-length non-chimeric (FLNC) reads. FLNC reads were further refined, clustered, and polished using the ‘isoseq’ module. Finally, 73,961 high-quality (accuracy rate > 99.9%) sequences were generated, which were further clustered into 19,048 unigenes using CD-hit (v 4.7)27.

In addition to IsoSeq data, RNA-Seq data generated from 40 C. typhlos samples under cyclic temperature fluctuations (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA859655) were processed by TopHat (v2.1.1)28, and C. typhlos transcripts were assembled by Cufflinks (v2.2.1) with default parameters29.

Genome annotation

Repetitive elements in C. typhlos genome were masked by combining RepeatModeler (v2.0.5) and RepeatMasker (v4.1.5)30. A de novo repeat library of C. typhlos was built by RepeatModeler. Then, repetitive sequences were identified using RepeatMasker by aligning C. typhlos genome against the RepBase library and the de novo repeat library (Fig. 1).

Protein-coding genes were predicted from the C. typhlos genome using the MAKER pipeline (v3.01.03)31, based on EST evidence, full-length transcripts generated from IsoSeq sequencing and RNA-Seq sequencing, and protein homology-based evidence, including five Chlorophyta species (Chlamydomonas reinhardtii32, Coccomyxa subellipsoidea33, Chromochloris zofingiensis34, Chlamydomonas eustigma35 and Raphidocelis subcapitata36) downloaded from JGI or NCBI.

The genomic features of psychrotolerant algal species (C. typhlos and Coccomyxa subellipsoidea33), psychrophilic algal species (Sanguina aurantia8, Limnomonas spitsbergensis10, and cf. Sphaerocystis sp. CCCryo 101-999) and mesophilic algal species (Chlamydomonas reinhardtii32, Chlamydomonas eustigma35 and Chromochloris zofingiensis36) were analyzed and compared using TBtools37 and AGAT(v0.7.0)38 (Table 1 and Fig. 7). The average intron length of alpine snow algae C. typhlos is 854 bp, almost 3-fold of that in the genome of mesophilic algal species C. reinhardtii (273 bp), while the average exon length (355 bp) of C. typhlos is similar to that of C. reinhardtii (369 bp) (Table 1). Similarly, the average intron length of snow algae S. aurantia, L. spitsbergensis, and cf. Sphaerocystis sp are significant longer than that of mesophilic algal species (C. reinhardtii, C. eustigma and C. zofingiensis). Furthermore, the intron length distribution patterns of the snow algae C. typhlos, S. aurantia, and L. spitsbergensis, in which no remarkable peaks are displayed, are significantly different to those of other algal species, while the exon and CDS length distribution patterns of these algae are similar (Fig. 7).

MS/MS analysis

C. typhlos cells at log phase were collected by centrifugation (3,000 g, 3 min). Then washed twice with 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer. Resuspended the cell pellet in lysis buffer and intermittently sonicated on ice at 100 W power for 8 minutes to disrupt the cells and extract proteins. The lysis buffer contained 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1% DDM and 1% DTT, and protease inhibitor (Roche). The extracted protein solution was then placed on ice for 1 h to fully dissolve the protein. Finally, the cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 4 °C, 12,000 g for 10 min, and the C. typhlos protein was obtained. The protein content in the solution was mainly determined by the Bradford method (Beyotime Bradford kit). Precipitated 100 μg of protein with TCA/acetone at a final concentration of 10% (−20 °C, 2 h). Precipitated proteins were collected by centrifugation (12,000 g, 4 °C, 10 min), washed twice with pre-cooled acetone, dried at room temperature and redissolved with 50 µl of NH4HCO3 to control the pH between 7-8.

A final concentration of 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 15 mM iodoacetamide (IAM) were sequentially added to the redissolved protein sample to open disulfide bonds and alkylate thiol groups. Then added 0.2 µg of trypsin for overnight digestion. Pure formic acid was added to terminate the enzymatic reaction and the sample was desalted using a C18 Stage-tips based process. The C18 column was activated and equilibrated with 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid water in sequence. After loading the sample, use 0.1% formic acid water for desalting, and finally use 75% acetonitrile to elute the sample.

Samples were analyzed using Exactive™Plus Orbitrap high-resolution LC MS/MS. The peptide fragments were separated using a 120-min linear gradient with a flow rate of 0.3 μl/min and a gradient range of 3–100% mobile phase (0.1% formic acid acetonitrile). Technical Validation

The final Hi-C scaffolding produced 31 chromosome-level scaffolds with a scaffold N50 of 7.23 Mb. To assess telomere integrity, we searched for common telomeric repeats reported in Chlorophyta, including (TTAGGG)n and (TTTAGGG)n. Telomeric repeats were clearly detected at one end of 3 scaffolds. Although no scaffold showed telomeres at both ends, the total gap length is only 28 kb (0.014% of the assembly). These results indicate high contiguity of the assembly.

The annotated proteome was assessed using the algae dataset (chlorophyta_odb10) of Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (v5.2.2) (BUSCO) with parameter “- protein”21,22. The annotation achieved 90.1% complete (81.1% single-copy + 9.0% duplicated, 1.8% fragmented and 8.1% missing) BUSCOs. The annotation was further validated using the integrated proteogenomic pipeline as described in detail in Yang et al.43. In brief, raw mass spectrometry (MS/MS) data generated by high-resolution LC-MS/MS (Q Exactive™ Plus Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap™ Mass Spectrometer Thermo Scientific™) were converted to “mgf” format by ThermoRawFileParser.exe (v1.4.0.0). After peptide searches and filtering with the stringent FDR control, the resulting map peptide spectrum matches (PSMs) from five search engines (Comet44, MSGF + 45, X!Tandem46, pFind47, and MASCOT48), were merged and the peptides were mapped to predicted C. typhlos protein sequences. Up to 31.0% annotated genes were supported by peptide evidence. An average of five peptides were identified for one gene, suggesting a relatively high-quality genome annotation was achieved (Fig. 1).
