Spatiotemporal liver dynamics: How far can we take them in vitro?
Authors: Hanyang Liu
Published: 2025

Liver, as a predominant digestive and metabolic organ, orchestrates systemic homeostasis. Intricate functions and reactions are facilitated by heterogeneous liver cells across spatial compartments, developmental stages, and ages. Such spatiotemporal dynamics remain crucial characteristics in liver biology and pathology, leading to challenges in conducting liver studies but also inspiring novel solutions. A recent study, entitled “Spatiotemporal liver dynamics shape hepatocellular heterogeneity and impact in vivo gene engineering”, clearly demonstrates how hepatocyte spatial and temporal heterogeneity influences genetic modification and therapeutic outcomes.1 Particularly, this study reveals the gradual establishment of metabolic zonation with age and elevated proteasome activity in the peri-central area in adults’ livers. Their findings emphasize the technical significance of manipulating hepatocyte zonation and functional phenotypes in vivo, which also inspires consideration of strategies to recapitulate such heterogeneity in vitro. Additionally, cell heterogeneity across liver development after weaning remains a critical but challenging process to simulate current in vitro model systems.

To date, in vitro liver models are widely used to study liver physiology and disease, including conventional two-dimensional (2D) cell culture, liver-on-chip (LoC), and organoid systems. Particularly, the LoC systems aim to mimic hepatic cell dynamics and microenvironmental modulation through extracellular matrix supplementation, cytokine signaling, or coculture with non-parenchymal cells.2 Simultaneously, recent progress of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived organoids displays preliminary lobular organization.3 Yet, despite intensive efforts, few models have achieved robust reconstruction of liver zonation, which limits their value in physiological relevance and therapeutic evaluation.

One established strategy to recapitulate zonation relies on environmental regulation. Gradients of oxygen, nutrients, and morphogens are known to shape hepatocyte identity in vivo and can be recreated on a dish or spontaneously generated in patterned hydrogels.4 These gradients can induce distinct metabolic features across hepatocyte populations, bringing models closer to their in vivo counterparts. However, one of the critical challenges aligns with maintaining stable gradients over long periods, resulting in loss of zonation phenotypes in cultivated hepatocytes and compromised reproducibility of experiments.

A second approach involves programming hepatocyte phenotypes through coculture with physiologically neighboring cell types, such as hepatic stellate cells, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, and immune cells.5 These non-parenchymal cells provide essential paracrine factors, extracellular matrix components, and direct cell–cell interactions that regulate zonation in vivo. Recent multicellular organoid and LoC models have advanced toward reproducing lobular architecture, yet achieving the correct cellular composition and spatial organization remains challenging. Moreover, controlling dynamic crosstalk among multiple lineages is still a major hurdle for long-term stability.

Milani et al.’s study also highlights opportunities for genetic manipulation to engineer hepatocyte heterogeneity in vitro.1 By identifying transcriptional regulators and signaling pathways in zonate hepatocytes, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic approaches can guide targeted gene-editing strategies. CRISPR droplet sequencing (CROP-seq) can profile the specific transcriptional outcomes after targeted CRISPR gene perturbation, for example, enabling high-throughput and precise manipulation of zone-specific gene programs in hepatocytes.6 Such genetic approaches could bypass the need for complex environmental modulations and promote in vitro liver models in more efficient drug testing and mechanistic studies. Yet, genetic modification in both primary hepatocytes and iPSC-derived cells often suffers from low efficiency. Moreover, precise spatial and temporal control of gene expression is difficult to achieve.

Essentially, the findings in this study stimulate further discussions: Shall we consider spatiotemporal liver dynamics for in vitro modeling? Ideally, yes! But how far can we take them? To address these questions, several aspects should be taken into consideration (Fig. 1). First, despite the extensive access to omics data, the key regulatory mechanisms of zonation remain incompletely understood, and systematic functional validation is needed. Second, synchronizing spatial and temporal characteristics of hepatocytes, whether by environmental gradients or genetic programming, may enhance both engineering efficiency and disease modeling relevance. Contrastingly, the utilization of spatiotemporal hepatic heterogeneity allows for diverse strategies to study dynamic functions and pathological characteristics in liver diseases. Third, spatial heterogeneity in gene-editing responsiveness, as demonstrated in vivo, could be leveraged as a valuable resource for hepatocyte subtype sorting without relying on complex reprogramming or antibody-based methods. Lastly, the choice of in vitro system is critical. 3D cell encapsulation systems, for example, organoids, allow spontaneous differentiation and self-organization but are difficult to control or to manipulate by specific phenotypes, while LoC systems provide artificial liver structures and precise manipulation of zonation features but struggle with long-term viability and stability.7 Combinative organoid-on-chip models may ultimately leverage the strengths of both, although structural integration and multicellular crosstalk could certainly be compromised.Fig. 1Spatiotemporal liver dynamics for in vitro modeling. Looking ahead, the stable and efficient in vitro modeling of liver lobular architecture that harbors hepatic heterogeneity and functions is still an unmet need, particularly for the studies of chronic liver diseases and developmental liver diseases, as well as therapeutic development. This work by Milani et al.1 provides a novel insight that spatiotemporal hepatocyte dynamics influence gene engineering in vivo, which is also a timely reminder for us to consider and utilize hepatocyte heterogeneity in establishing in vitro liver systems.
