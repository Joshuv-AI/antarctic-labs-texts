Factors associated with oral frailty among community-dwelling older adults in Chengdu, China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sikai Tang, Hongmiao Ji, Lin Chen, Yingjun Zhang, Xuemei Zhang, Xing Liu, Qiuyan Zhao, Li Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to reveal the factors associated with oral frailty among community dwelling older adults in Chengdu, China.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted. Oral frailty was assessed using the Oral Frailty Index-6, encompassing the number of natural teeth, chewing ability, tongue pressure, oral diadochokinesis, and self-reported difficulties in eating tough food and swallowing on tea or soup. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0, with modified Poisson regression employed to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs).ResultsA total of 470 community-dwelling adults aged 60 and older were included. Among them, 195 (41.5%) were identified as having oral frailty based on the OFI-6. Multivariable analysis identified that older age [70–79 years: PR:2.55, (95%CI: 1.83–3.55); ≥80 years: PR:2.89, (95%CI: 1.99–4.21)], dry mouth (PR:1.34, 95%CI: 1.00-1.80), low handgrip strength (PR:1.47, 95%CI: 1.17–1.85), and depression (PR: 1.50, 95%CI: 1.08–2.08) were associated with a higher prevalence of oral frailty, while female gender (PR: 0.79, 95%CI: 0.65–0.97) and using toothpicks (PR:0.63, 95%CI: 0.44–0.89) were associated with a lower prevalence (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThis study revealed that oral frailty is a significant concern among the community-dwelling older adults in Western China included in our sample. The factors associated with oral frailty are multifaceted, spanning sociodemographic, behavioral, physical, and psychological domains. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions and the integration of oral function assessments into community-based geriatric care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06889-3.

Background

The global challenge of an aging population is particularly pronounced in China. By the end of 2023, the population of individuals aged 60 and above in China has already reached 297 million, constituting 21.1% of the total population [1]. It is estimated that by 2050, the Chinese population aged 60 and older will surpass 400 million, accounting for a quarter of the entire population, with an estimated 150 million individuals aged 80 and above [2, 3]. The elderly population is growing, the proportion of the total population continues to rise, and a serious aging problem is emerging.

Oral health is a crucial component of overall health and well-being in older adults [4] and was recognized as an issue of global concern by the World Health Assembly in 2021 [5]. A decline in oral health, particularly in oral function, can initiate a cascade of negative health outcomes, including poor nutritional status and reduced quality of life [6]. Despite its importance, standardized and comprehensive assessments of oral function—encompassing objective clinical measures (e.g., tongue pressure, masticatory performance) and patient-reported outcomes (e.g., perceived difficulties in chewing)—are not yet routinely integrated into health surveys or geriatric clinical practice [7]. This gap hinders the early identification of older adults at risk of oral function.

Oral frailty (OF) refers to a series of ageing-related phenomena or processes that lead to changes in oral conditions (number of teeth, oral hygiene, oral function, etc.), accompanied by a decline in interest in oral health, a decline in physical and mental reserve and the development of OF leading to eating disorders [8]. The overall effect is deterioration of physical and mental function [9]. Tanaka et al. [10] reported that OF could be measured by OFI-6 (the Oral Frailty Index-6), including the number of natural teeth, chewing ability, tongue pressure, ODK (oral diadochokinesis), and self-reported difficulties in eating and swallowing. In this study, OF was defined as the coexistence of poor status in at least 3 of these 6 measures [10]. Older adults with OF had a significantly higher risk of physical frailty, sarcopenia, disability, and mortality, where their risk increased by 2.4-, 2.2-, 2.3-, and 2.2-fold, respectively. In addition, other studies confirmed that older adults with OF would lead to malnutrition and social withdrawal in older adults [11, 12]. OF may be considered a geriatric syndrome requiring routine screening [13]. Therefore, there is a need for early screening and identification of OF in older adults, to reduce the risk of adverse health outcomes and improve the quality of life.

Literature shows that other assessment tools for OF have been developed, including OFI-8 (the Oral Frailty Index-8) [14], and OFI-5 (the Oral Frailty Index-5) [15]. The choice of instrument influences the reported prevalence of OF to some extent. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Zhou et al. [16] illustrated this variation through subgroup analyses: when classified by assessment tool, studies using OFI-8 reported the highest prevalence [31%, 95%CI (confidence interval): 21–41%], followed by those using OFI-6 (21%, 95%CI: 14–28%). Furthermore, when stratified by country, they found that the prevalence in China (40%, 95% CI: 32–47%) was considerably higher than in Japan (22%, 95% CI: 11–33%). These findings suggest that the observed epidemiology of OF is shaped not only by methodological differences in measurement but also by population-specific characteristics, including variations in healthcare systems and cultural oral health practices.

Evidence from the literature has consolidated the key determinants of OF into several domains. These encompass sociodemographic factors (advanced age, gender and lower education) [14, 17, 18], comorbidities and oral health status (including dysphagia, dry mouth, and tooth loss) [19], declines in physical function [20], and psychosocial factors [21]. The multifaceted nature of these determinants suggests that OF may be effectively understood through the lens of the cumulative health deficits model of frailty [22]. This model provides a theoretical foundation for examining how health problems across multiple domains collectively contribute to adverse health outcomes. Within this framework, we conceptualize OF as the manifestation of accumulated deficits in oral health and function. Our investigation was therefore guided by this model, and we examined a comprehensive set of variables representing potential deficits across the key domains: sociodemographics factors, health behaviors, physical function, and psychological factors. This approach allows for a holistic investigation of factors associated with OF, consistent with the multidimensional nature of frailty.

However, a comprehensive investigation that concurrently examines these established domains among community-dwelling older adults in Western China is lacking. Consequently, the multidimensional nature of OF in this specific population remains inadequately characterized. To address this gap, the present study was designed to elucidate the associated factors. Guided by a conceptual framework derived from the literature, we examined a comprehensive set of variables spanning the key domains of sociodemographic factors, health behaviors, physical function, and psychological factors. We anticipate that the findings will provide a robust evidence base for developing targeted interventions to prevent and manage OF in this setting.

Methods

Study design and participants

A community-based cross-sectional investigation was carried out in Chengdu City, Sichuan Province, China, from October 2022 to February 2023. Data collection adopted a multi-stage stratified convenience sampling approach. To begin, five administrative districts (Wuhou District, Jinniu District, Chenghua District, Jinjiang District, and Qingyang District) were selected based on geographical considerations to ensure a broad and representative coverage of the central urban area of Chengdu. Subsequently, five sub-district communities were chosen from within each district. The inclusion criteria for participants encompassed: (1) urban residents aged 60 years and above, residing in the local community, (2) ability to communicate effectively in Mandarin or the local dialect. Exclusion criteria were applied to individuals meeting either of the following conditions: (1) cognitive impairment, screened using the Short Portable Mental Status Questionnaire (SPMSQ) with a cutoff of ≥ 3 errors [23], or (2) sensory impairments, (e.g., hearing or visual impairments) which were initially identified through self-report during recruitment and subsequently confirmed by the investigator’s assessment of the participant’s ability to understand verbal instructions and visually recognize assessment tools during the initial interaction, indicating a level of impairment that would impede effective communication.

The sample size was determined based on the requirements for multivariable regression analysis, using the events per variable (EPV) principle [24]. This principle recommends a minimum of 10 outcome events per independent variable (EPV ≥ 10) to ensure model stability. Planning for 6 independent variables in the final model, a minimum of 60 OF events was required. Based on a reported prevalence of 23% from a previous study [25], the minimum required sample size was calculated as (60 events/0.23) ≈ 261 participants. To account for a 20% rate of invalid questionnaires, the final target sample size was set at 313 participants. The reporting of the study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement guidelines (Table S1).

Data collection

Prior to the survey, all investigators, who were qualified registered nurses, received standardized professional training to ensure familiarity with the study protocol and assessment techniques. Participants were recruited through a multi-faceted approach. This included on-site invitations at community activity centers, the distribution of flyers and posters in residential areas, announcements through online social media groups (e.g., WeChat groups managed by neighborhood committees), and collaboration with community health centers. Data collection was conducted through structured, face-to-face interviews and assessments in designated rooms within community health centers or activity rooms in the participating communities, ensuring a quiet and controlled environment. All questionnaires were administered by the trained investigators. If participants had difficulty completing any items independently, the investigator read the questions aloud using neutral, unguided language and accurately recorded the responses. Objective measurements were performed according to standardized protocols by designated investigators. To ensure privacy and confidentiality, each participant was assessed individually in a separate area, and all collected data were anonymized using unique identification codes immediately upon collection. The quality of each completed questionnaire and data form was checked on-site by the investigators for completeness and clarity. The average time to complete the entire face-to-face investigation was approximately 20 min.

Assessments

Oral frailty

The assessment of OF was based on OFI-6 by Tanaka et al. [10], including 4 objective items and 2 subjective items: (1) number of natural and functional teeth < 20; (2) chewing ability < 14.2 for men and < 10.8 for women; (3) tongue pressure < 27.4 kPa for men and < 26.5 kPa for women; (4) an ODK < 5.2 times/s for men and < 5.4 times/s for women; (5) participants were questioned as followed: “Do you have any difficulty in eating tough foods compared to 6 months ago?”. A “yes” response indicated reduced masticatory performance; (6) another subjective question was asked: “Have you choked on your tea or soup recently?”. A “yes” response was considered indicative of impaired swallowing. OF was defined based on the number of positive results of the six items, and a score of ≥ 3 was defined as OF in our study.

Dental status

Dental status was determined by visually inspecting and counting the number of natural teeth [10], with wisdom teeth, dentures, residual roots, and loose teeth excluded from consideration.

Chewing ability

Chewing ability was evaluated by color-changing gum (Masticatory Performance Evaluating Gum XYLITOL, Lotte Co, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), a method validated in previous studies of oral function [10, 26]. The participants were asked to chew the gum for 1 min, and the gum’s color change was measured at three different points after chewing using a color difference meter (CR-20; Konica-Minolta sensing, Tokyo, Japan). The mean value of these different points was used to show chewing ability, with a higher mean value indicating better chewing ability.

Tongue pressure

Tongue pressure was measured using a tongue pressure meter (TPS100 tongue pressure meter, Saib Medical Co., Ltd., Korea). This measurement approach has been established as a valid assessment of tongue strength [10, 27]. The tongue pressure probe (air sensor) was positioned between the participants’ tongue and palate, and they were instructed to press the probe against the palate as strongly as possible using their tongue muscles. Three measurements were taken, and the mean value of tongue pressure was used for the analysis.

ODK

ODK was assessed by an automated measuring instrument (TKK-3351 Oral Function Meter, Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Japan). This objective assessment method has been validated for evaluating ODK in older populations [10, 26, 28]. The participants were asked to repeatedly pronounce the syllable /ta/ as fast as possible for 5 s, and the number of syllables per second was given by the device [29].

Related variables

The selection of independent variables was informed by the cumulative health deficits model of frailty, which provided the theoretical basis for examining variables across multiple health domains [22]. Variables were therefore categorized into four domains representing different types of potential health deficits and assessed through a structured questionnaire: (1) Sociodemographic factors included age, gender, education level, marital status, living arrangement, and personal monthly income [30]. (2) Health behaviors were assessed by self-report and encompassed drinking status, smoking status, and specific oral hygiene indicators [31] (brushing teeth, using toothpicks, using dental floss, dental checks in the last 2 years, and dental cleanings in the last 2 years). (3) Physical function included body mass index (BMI), chronic disease status [32], number of medications [33], dry mouth, handgrip strength, nutritional status, and activity of daily living (ADL). BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²), with measurements taken using calibrated instruments while participants wore light clothing without shoes. Dry mouth was measured using the Chinese version of the Summated Xerostomia Inventory translated by He et al. [34], demonstrating good reliability and validity in Chinese populations. Handgrip strength was measured using an electronic hand dynamometer (EH101, CAMRY, Guangzhou, China) following the consensus from the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia [35], ensuring appropriate categorization for our study population. Participants were instructed to hold the dynamometer at 90 degrees in a neutral position and squeeze the handle as hard as possible three times with a 60-second interval between each measurement. Handgrip strength used in our study was defined as the maximum strength of the dominant hand, expressed in kilograms (kg). For analysis, handgrip strength was categorized into two groups (< 28 kg for men; and < 18 kg for women), according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia in 2019 [35]. Nutritional status was assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment Short Form, and ADL was evaluated with the Barthel Index, all of which have demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese populations [36, 37]. (4) Psychological factors included perceived oral health, anxiety, and depression. Perceived oral health was assessed through self-report [38]. Anxiety and depression were assessed using the Chinese versions of the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-7) and the 5-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-5) respectively, both demonstrated strong reliability and validity in Chinese populations [39, 40]. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative data were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%), while continuous variables were described as Mean and SD (standard deviation) or Median with the 25th and 75th percentiles. Chi-square tests were employed to assess differences between the OF and non-OF groups. To control confounding bias and determine variables independently associated with OF in the sample, a multivariate regression was performed with selected variables showing a P value of < 0.05 in the univariate analysis. Additionally, before conducting multivariable analysis, we assessed multicollinearity among independent variables. Variables were retained in the final model only if they satisfied the following predefined criteria: variance inflation factor (VIF) < 10 and tolerance > 0.1. Given the high prevalence (41.5%) of OF in this study, the use of Logistic regression would have resulted in odds ratios (ORs) that overestimate the prevalence ratios (PRs). Therefore, we employed modified Poisson regression to directly estimate PRs, as this method provides more accurate and less biased effect estimates when dealing with common outcomes [41, 42]. PRs with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and a P value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Multivariate analysis of OF in participants

For the collinearity diagnostics, the results showed that the tolerance values were > 0.39 and the VIF were < 2.567, indicating that there was no significant covariance between the included independent variables for further analysis. Taking whether the participants had OF as the dependent variable, the value of Non-OF was 0, and OF was 1. The multivariate Poisson regression model with robust variance identified the following factors as significantly associated with the prevalence of OF. Specifically, older age [70–79 years: PR:2.55, (95%CI: 1.83–3.55), P < 0.001); ≥80 years: PR:2.89, (95%CI: 1.99–4.21), P < 0.001)], dry mouth (PR: 1.34, 95%CI: 1.00-1.80, P = 0.049), abnormal handgrip strength (PR: 1.47, 95%CI: 1.17–1.85, P = 0.001), and depression (PR: 1.50, 95%CI: 1.08–2.08, P = 0.017) were associated with a higher prevalence, whereas female gender (PR: 0.79, 95%CI: 0.65–0.97, P = 0.022) and use of toothpicks (PR: 0.63, 95%CI: 0.44–0.89, P = 0.01) were associated with a lower prevalence. Discussion

This cross-sectional study, guided by the cumulative health deficits model, identified a substantial burden of OF within our sample of community-dwelling older adults in Western China, with 41.5% of participants meeting the OFI-6. Notably, our findings demonstrate that OF is linked to a constellation of deficits across multiple health domains, encompassing sociodemographic factors (advanced age and male), health behaviors (lack of toothpick use), physical function (dry mouth and low handgrip strength), and psychological factor (depression). This pattern of results validates the multidimensional nature of OF as conceptualized by the cumulative health deficits model. Consequently, integrated management strategies are warranted: at the clinical level, routine oral function screening should be incorporated into geriatric care; community-based initiatives should deliver tailored oral health guidance; and policy efforts ought to improve financial coverage for essential dental services to systemically address this multifactorial condition.

The current study aligns with prior research by demonstrating that older age is associated with a higher prevalence of OF [9, 10, 21, 43]. A scoping review [44] defined OF as the age-related functional decline of orofacial structures. Oral function and structure in older adults show a decline with increasing age. The number of natural teeth is one of the indicators of OF, and a systematic review [45] reported that the number of missing teeth in adults in China increased with age, from 2 at the age of 40 to 12 at 65. With the missing teeth, the chewing ability of the elderly is limited, and the muscle mass of the masticatory and swallowing further decreases, aggravating the degree of OF in the elderly. In addition, older adults, along with aging, have a more pronounced decline in body functions and reduced self-care ability, making it more difficult to maintain good oral health behaviors, thus increasing the risk of OF. These findings highlight the need for community health services to integrate structured oral function assessments into routine geriatric care and to provide targeted oral hygiene support for older adults, particularly those with advanced age and natural tooth loss.

In our study, female gender was associated with a lower prevalence of OF, a finding that contrasts with some previous studies [9, 43]. This discrepancy may be partly explained by the assessment tools employed. The OFI-6 instrument used in our study incorporates gender-specific thresholds for objective measures like chewing ability, tongue pressure, and ODK. Our data indicated that female participants demonstrated better performance across several oral health indicators, including the number of natural teeth, chewing ability, tongue pressure, and ODK, alongside better general health status as reflected by handgrip strength (see Table S3. The gender differences in oral function have been confirmed by a recent study [46], which found significant associations between sex and various oral hypofunction tests, such as masticatory function and lip strength. Additionally, women often exhibit more proactive oral health-seeking behaviors, which could contribute to the maintenance of better oral function [47]. This suggests that future development of OF assessment tools for Chinese older adults should incorporate gender-specific cut-off values to enhance screening accuracy. Additionally, gender differences should be considered in health education and intervention strategies. Given that women generally show better oral health indicators and more proactive health-seeking behaviors, community health programs should implement gender-tailored approaches, with particular emphasis on targeted education and early intervention for older men to promote regular dental check-ups and timely care.

Conversely, a distinctive finding of our study is that the use of toothpicks was associated with a lower prevalence of OF. The possible reason is that food debris adheres to the tooth surface for a long time, which can form plaque, calculus, or even dental caries, causing gingival recession, loosening or falling off of the teeth, and a large number of bacteria in the oral cavity can also infect the pulpal tissues through the dental defects, triggering endodontitis, whereas the use of toothpicks can effectively remove food debris and soft tartar between the teeth, thus protecting the dental and periodontal health. It is important to note that dental professionals generally do not recommend toothpicks as the primary interdental cleaning tool due to the potential for gingival trauma. However, toothpicks are a traditional and highly accessible oral hygiene aid in China, especially among older adults. Some of the elderly people are influenced by their habits, personal preference, and dental health status, and may choose a more familiar and flexible cleaning tool - toothpicks. This variation may be attributed to the fact that oral health awareness among Chinese adults lags behind that of developed countries, and robust oral hygiene habits have not been widely established. The 4th National Oral Health Epidemiology Survey [48] showed that the proportion of people over 55 years of age using toothpicks was 45.5%, which was similar to this study (42.1%), while the proportion of flossing was only 2%. This finding underscores the need for clinicians to respect existing patient habits, while providing individualized guidance to transition gradually from toothpicks to safer and more effective interdental cleaning tools for scientifically sound oral health maintenance.

The presence of dry mouth was significantly associated with a higher prevalence of OF in our study, which aligns with previous findings [49]. The condition showed a notably high prevalence (79.8%) in our sample, consistent with the documented 20–70% prevalence range among community-dwelling older adults [50]. Saliva maintains oral health through multiple mechanisms, including acid neutralization, taste facilitation, speech articulation, and antimicrobial protection mediated by enzymes and proteins [51]. These diverse functions underscore its critical importance in the oral cavity. The frequent occurrence of reduced salivary flow in older populations, often resulting from age-related glandular decline or medication side effects (e.g., antihypertensives, anti-anxiety drugs) [52], can lead to substantial oral health impairments such as taste disorders, oral discomfort, chewing and swallowing difficulties, and periodontitis. Healthcare providers should screen older adults routinely for dry mouth, particularly those on xerogenic medications, and community programs should educate on self-management, such as adequate water intake and sugar-free gum use.

Our results confirm a significant association between low handgrip strength and a higher prevalence of OF, consistent with existing literature [43]. This relationship may be explained through the shared pathway of sarcopenia, given that muscle mass decreases by approximately 8% per decade from the age of 40 and further declines at a rate of 10–15% per decade beyond the age of 70 [53]. Skeletal muscle loss is a key determinant of physical performance and cumulative oral health issues [54]. Age-related skeletal muscle loss adversely affects masticatory, tongue, and swallowing muscles, leading to oral functional impairments such as reduced tongue pressure, diminished bite force, and tooth loss [55–57]. From a public health perspective, older adults should be encouraged to engage in resistance training and nutritional interventions aimed at preserving muscle mass, which may concurrently benefit both general and oral health.

We found that depressive symptoms were associated with a higher prevalence of OF, which is consistent with previous studies [58, 59]. Depression in older adults is closely linked to both physical frailty and poor oral health status [60]. Depressed older adults often demonstrate reduced motivation for maintaining oral hygiene, leading to deteriorated oral health conditions and delayed treatment-seeking behavior. This neglect increases susceptibility to oral diseases such as dental caries and periodontitis [61], while also contributing to reduced salivary flow, oral sensory disorders, and impaired oral immune function. In addition, older adults with depression face higher risks of malnutrition [62], potentially due to loss of eating pleasure and passive attitudes toward food intake. This nutritional deterioration can accelerate the progression of both general frailty and comorbidities [63], thereby adversely affecting oral functional status. These findings highlight the need for integrated health services that incorporate mental health screening into OF assessment protocols in community settings. Additionally, policy initiatives should promote interdisciplinary collaboration between mental health professionals and dental care providers to develop comprehensive intervention strategies for older adults with co-occurring depression and oral health concerns.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, a key strength of this study is that it represents the first attempt to assess OF using the OFI-6 tool among community-dwelling older adults in China. This provides valuable baseline data on OF and its multidimensional correlates within this population, framed by the cumulative health deficits model. Regarding limitations, the cross-sectional design is appropriate for establishing associations but precludes any causal inference. Furthermore, the use of a convenience sampling method, despite employing a multi-stage geographical framework, may limit the generalizability of our findings and introduces the potential for selection bias, as participants who were excluded for sensory or cognitive impairment. The sample size, while adequate for the primary analysis, remains relatively modest and is primarily representative of urban communities in Western China, suggesting caution in extrapolating the results to rural settings or other regions. Other potential biases should also be acknowledged. In addition, information bias might have occurred, particularly for self-reported measures such as oral hygiene behaviors and perceived oral health. Moreover, the possibility of reverse causality cannot be ruled out; for instance, OF might influence psychological well-being rather than depression solely being a risk factor for OF. Future research should aim to include larger, nationally representative samples to enhance external validity and utilize longitudinal or interventional designs to clarify causal pathways. Finally, the development and validation of native OF measurement tools and diagnostic criteria specific to the Chinese cultural and healthcare context are warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study revealed that OF is a significant concern among the community-dwelling older adults in Western China included in our sample. The identified associated factors encompassed sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, physical function, and psychological status. These findings highlight the importance of implementing multidimensional strategies for early identification and management of OF in community geriatric healthcare.

Relevance to clinical practice

Our findings underscore the urgent need to integrate OF into routine geriatric care within community health services. The high prevalence identified calls for a systematic approach to early detection and intervention. We recommend that simple and validated assessments for OF be incorporated into annual health examinations and primary care encounters for older adults. Community health workers and nurses should receive standardized training to proficiently administer these screenings, recognize early signs of oral functional decline, and provide basic oral health guidance. Additionally, policy initiatives should explore mechanisms to include essential dental check-ups and basic preventive procedures within the reimbursement scope of China’s basic medical insurance for the elderly, thereby reducing financial barriers and fostering a more proactive approach to maintaining oral health in aging populations.
