A sad farewell but the time has truly come
Authors: Stephen Touyz
Published: 2026

Having conceptualised and introduced the world’s first Open Access Journal on eating disorders (with my co-founding Editor-in-Chief Phillipa Hay) at the time that print journals reigned supreme [1], it is now time for me to stand aside and allow for the appointment of a new co-editor-in-chief. An international search has commenced to enable this.

The question often asked is, how do you start a new journal, especially one that requires the authors to pay and article processing charge, when there are established journals that are free to publish in? The answer is simple—as hard as it gets. Those very early days were fraught with difficulties with many sleepless nights. Neither myself nor my co-founding editor-in-chief, Phillipa Hay had edited a journal before, but we both had extensive publication records and were members of editorial boards such as the International Journal of Eating Disorders and European Eating Disorder Review. Journals do not survive if they do not attract seminal papers, or even if they do, fail to get cited. Although impact factors are only part of the story, they do count—as researchers world-wide share the same desire to publish their research in journals of the highest repute. A new journal has an impact factor of zero, so there is the conundrum. To get an impact factor, you need highly citeable papers, but to get them you need a respectable impact factor.

The answer was to get those highly cited published papers to attain our first impact factor. But the burning question was, who was going to pay the article processing charge? [2]. My co-editor and myself made the somewhat bold decision, that we as editors-in-chief would need to step up to the plate and generate the seed funding to pay for a series of groundbreaking papers to get the journal under way. We also needed a publisher, and are forever grateful to Sara Ho, who quickly grasped our passion and commitment, and agreed on behalf of BMC to give us a chance. She believed in us, and her attending our first ever editorial board meeting in a basement of a hotel (that’s all we could afford, but the internet failed! ) in the picturesque town of Porto, sealed the deal. There she witnessed the enthusiasm of our first editorial board who comprised many of our esteemed colleagues from around the globe.

We were also fortunate to receive financial support in kind from both the Butterfly Foundation and the National Eating Disorders Collaboration. But even this was not going to be enough to successfully launch the Journal. So Phillipa Hay and I travelled Australia, running one-day workshops and gifting all the profits to the Journal. It was indeed that early hard work and dedication that got us through. As we have said previously [3], we are so fortunate now, and not unlike Abundantia, the Roman goddess of abundance and prosperity, whose harp is overflowing with fruit, our Journal is doing likewise with submitted papers.

It is not possible to express my gratitude and the debt I owe my co-founding editor-in-chief, Phillipa Hay. We have worked tirelessly as a team for the past years and have hardly missed a week when we have not been in a communication regarding the Journal. In the early days, we would meet almost weekly in a lovely restaurant in Sydney so that we could focus our attention on the journal without disruption and order coffee, snacks or meals as required. One day, wherever we chose to sit, the new patrons, who took the tables next to us were loud and noisy. This made our Zoom conference calls to colleagues redundant—we could not hear them! So, we kept moving tables. At the end of the day, when I was paying our bill, I asked the restaurant owner what kind of day he was having. He replied “terrible”. I asked why. He told me that when the lunch time shift handed over to the evening shift, they also handed over the list of tables that has not paid for their lunch or drinks. When they went to a number of tables to collect the monies, the tables were unoccupied. The patrons had fled without paying their bills. It did not take long to conclude who the culprits were—us, as when he pointed to the tables concerned, they were all the ones that we had occupied throughout the day indulging in our editorial duties! He thanked the Journal for its honesty.

It is so important to recognise that it takes the contributions of many individuals to make a journal succeed. No journal can flourish in isolation. I am profoundly grateful to my co-founding editor, our associate editors, editorial board members, reviewers, and authors who have given their time, expertise, and intellectual generosity. Without critical peer review, which often constitutes the invisible generosity of time, the scientific integrity of the journal would not be what it is today with an impact factor of 4.5. Together, as a team we have accomplished the extra-ordinary feat, of being the highest impact factor journal in the field of eating disorders.

Editing an eating disorders journal comes with complex responsibilities and one must remain cognisant of the fact that language matters, and what gets published goes way beyond data sets. It impacts the lives of those with an eating disorder. There is not always a consensus as to the content of what is published and at times intense disagreement. We accept that. However, we have always held the view (and still do) that a healthy debate is the best way forward in dealing with highly contentious issues, and our readers should be exposed to both sides of the argument. In that vein, we continue to publish Research reports, Systematic Reviews, Comment papers and Case Reports and encourage constructive debate through our Matters Arising Section. I am proud that the journal has promoted both ethical responsibility and transparency and has warmly embraced the contributions from those with lived experience. Over the past year, the Journal has received an ever-increasing number of such papers. We recognise that most are not academics but want their voices heard. We have listened and gone beyond the call of duty to assist them in their endeavours to format their work so as to meet publication guidelines.

Co-creating this journal has been one of the most humbling experiences of my career. It has taught me that healing and understanding are collective efforts, endorsed by every researcher, clinician, and especially those with lived experience who chooses to speak. Though I’m saying goodbye to my co-editor in chief role here, my gratitude—and my belief in the eating disorder community—remain without end. We will find the answers to these often devasting and enduring disorders [4].

And so, the time has truly come.

Farewell, Adios. Adieu.
