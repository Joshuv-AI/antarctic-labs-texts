Threonine modulates the STAT3-SCD1 pathway to reduce fatty acid metabolism in duck hepatocytes☆
Authors: Zhong Zhuang, Lei Wu, Wenqian Jia, Yongpeng Li, Yijia Lu, Minghong Xu, Hao Bai, Yulin Bi, Zhixiu Wang, Shihao Chen, Guobin Chang, Yong Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDietary threonine (Thr) is known to influence fat deposition in poultry, but the precise mechanisms behind its regulatory effects on hepatic lipid metabolism remain elusive. Prior research indicated that including supplemental Thr in the feed may influence STAT3 (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3) levels in the liver of meat ducks. Numerous studies have recorded the function of STAT3 in regulating fatty acid (FA) metabolism in mammals. The primary objective of this study was to investigate whether Thr influences FA metabolism and triglycerides (TG) deposition in duck liver by regulating STAT3 expression. Primary hepatocytes were isolated from duck embryos and treated for 36 h with different doses of Thr (0, 10, 25, 50, 200 μM) in vitro or with a constructed STAT3 overexpression plasmid to examine the content of FAs and TG. RNA-seq was used to detect changes in gene expression in hepatocytes following STAT3 overexpression. The results demonstrated that both the exogenous addition of Thr and the overexpression of STAT3 significantly suppressed the capacity of hepatocytes for FAs deposition (P < 0.05). The overexpression of STAT3 also inhibited TG accumulation under conditions in response to Thr deficiency (P < 0.01). Transcriptomic analyses indicated that the overexpression of STAT3 inhibits the activity of triglyceride metabolism and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis (P < 0.01). Finally, a dual-luciferase reporter test demonstrated that STAT3 may systematically target and inhibit SCD1 transcription (P < 0.01). The present study indicates that supplemental Thr (50 μM) inhibits hepatic FA deposition via the STAT3-SCD1 pathway. This work enhances our comprehension of the functional roles of Thr and STAT3 in modulating lipid metabolism within duck livers. Moreover, it provides a partial theoretical foundation for the nutritional prevention and pharmacological intervention of lipid metabolism disorders in poultry.Keywords: Duck, Threonine, STAT3, Hepatocyte, Fatty acid metabolism

Introduction

The liver is the primary site of lipid metabolism for birds, and in poultry, especially waterfowl (ducks and geese), more than 95% of total body fat is synthesized in the liver (Hermier, 1997). Excessive lipid accumulation in the hepatic tissue of waterfowl can precipitate lipid metabolism disorders and fatty liver disease, resulting in diminished liver function, reduced feed conversion efficiency, decreased egg production, and elevated mortality rates (Cao et al., 2022). Currently, nutritional supplementation has emerged as a key approach to maintaining appropriate lipid metabolic homeostasis while improving overall production performance in the context of livestock management (Attia et al., 2013; Xie et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2022b; Zhang et al., 2022). Threonine (Thr) is an essential amino acid closely associated with lipid metabolism in ducks raised for their meat. Feed supplementation with Thr has been shown to reduce hepatic triglyceride (TG) levels and improve growth performance in ducks (Jiang et al., 2022; Jia et al., 2023). However, the precise mechanisms through which Thr functions in this context remain poorly understood. Unraveling these mechanisms could offer critical insights into identifying potential targets for preventing and treating lipid metabolism disorders and fatty liver disease in ducks.

TG acquisition in poultry primarily occurs through de novo hepatic fatty acid (FA) synthesis. During this process, saturated FAs (SFAs) are initially produced from non-lipid substrates (Acetyl-CoA, generated from glucose, pyruvate, and citrate) within hepatocytes. In contrast, the desaturation of these SFAs yields monounsaturated and polyunsaturated FAs (MUFAs and PUFAs), with TG synthesis ultimately being mediated by these FAs through the glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) pathway (Zaefarian et al., 2019). This process is primarily regulated at the transcriptional level. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) can convert acetyl-CoA into malonyl-CoA to initiate the de novo synthesis of FAs (Li et al., 2022a). The multi-enzyme FA synthase (FASN) complex then facilitated the elongation of malonyl-CoA into palmitate (Desert et al., 2018), with FA carbon chain elongation being mediated in large part by members of the elongation of very-long-chain FAs (ELOVL) gene family (Jakobsson et al., 2006). Genes in the stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD) and FA desaturase (FADS) gene family are primarily responsible for FA desaturation (Yu et al., 2023). Alongside these lipogenic enzymes, many transcription factors facilitate the regulation of FA metabolism. Sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) directly regulates the ACC, FASN, and SCD genes, affecting mammalian FA metabolism (Eberlé et al., 2004; Fang et al., 2013). In contrast, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) facilitates the β-oxidation of FA by targeting and modulating fatty acid-binding protein (FABP), FA transport protein (FATP), and members of the acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain (ACSL) family (Rakhshandehroo et al., 2007). PPARγ can similarly function as a regulator of lipid metabolism and adipocyte differentiation through its control of FASN, SCD, and FABP gene expression (Tontonoz and Spiegelman, 2008).

A growing body of evidence suggests that the transcription factor STAT3 is an essential regulator of FA metabolism in mammals (Wang et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020). Mitochondrial STAT3 knock-in mice exhibit enhanced carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1a (CPT1a)-mediated FA oxidation (Li et al., 2022b). Dietary catecholamine supplementation can suppress FA re-esterification in a STAT3-dependent manner while increasing white adipocyte oxidation in high-fat mice (Reilly et al., 2020). The antifibrotic drug pioglitazone improved hepatic FA metabolism and alleviated steatosis by STAT3-SCD1 pathway in mice (Yang et al., 2023). The relationship between STAT3 and FA metabolism in avian species has yet to be investigated. Prior research demonstrated that dietary supplementation of Thr (0.71%) led to increased expression of the STAT3 gene and protein in duck liver (Jiang et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2023). These results prompted the hypothesis that Thr can regulate FA metabolism in duck hepatocytes through a pathway mediated by STAT3. The objective of the current work was to investigate the impact of Thr and STAT3 on FA metabolic activity in duck hepatocytes and to clarify the underlying molecular pathways to test this hypothesis.

Material and methods

Ethical approval

The Animal Care and Use Committee of Yangzhou University approved all animal studies described in this article (No. YZUDWSY2020-11-07).

Cell Culture

Cherry Valley duck embryos' livers were utilized to separate primary hepatocytes for in vitro culture. All of the embryos were produced by healthy female ducks growing properly, and they were all uniformly incubated at 38°C and 65% humidity in a commercial incubator (capacity 38400, Yunfeng, China). Primary hepatocytes were prepared as described before (Bao et al., 2023). Briefly, the livers were dissected out from duck embryos of 18 days old (E18). The liver samples were cut into small pieces (1 mm × 1 mm) and digested by 0.2 mg/mL of collagenase type IV (SolarBio, Beijing, China) at 37°C for 10 minutes. The hepatocytes were collected by low-speed centrifugation (1000 r/min × 5 min). Isolated hepatocytes were cultured at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in a complete medium at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator (Thermo, MA, USA). The complete medium consists of M199 basal medium (BasalMedia, Shanghai, China), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, New Zealand), and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (PS, Gibco). Following a 24-hour incubation period, after the hepatocyte density reached 80%, the medium was exchanged for medium containing a range of Thr concentrations (there were 5 gradient concentrations: 0, 10, 25, 50, and 200 μM). After 36 hours of incubation, cell viability and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity were measured immediately. Similarly, some cells were collected and stored at -80°C for subsequent analysis of TG content, FA content, and mRNA levels. The Thr-free medium was custom-made by BasalMedia (Shanghai, China). Media with different Thr concentrations (0, 10, 25, 50, 200 μM) were prepared using custom M199 Thr-free medium (0 μM) and M199 basal medium (250 μM). Duck embryonic fibroblasts (DEFs) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 100 U/mL PS at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator.

Plasmid Transfection

GeneCreate Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) contributed the pIRES2-Egfp empty plasmid vector utilized in this investigation. The empty vector was used as a negative control during transfection since it lacks the STAT3 sequence. For STAT3 overexpression (STAT3-OE), the coding sequence (CDS) for the duck STAT3 gene (GenBank accession no. XP_038024645.1) was amplified via PCR and inserted into the pIRES2-Egfp empty plasmid vector with the BmtI and EcoRI restriction sites. PCR amplification was performed with the following primers: F, 5´-TTTAGTGAACCGTCAGATCCGCTAGCATGGCGCAGTGGA-3′ and R, 5´-CTCGAGTCAGCGACTCGAACTGGCCGCCGCGTTGGCCTC´-3′.

Throughout the transfection process, both STAT3-OE and NC underwent identical treatment. Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo, Shanghai, China) transfected STAT3-OE or NC plasmids. The hepatocytes were placed in 6-well plates and left to adhere for 24 hours. Afterward, a mixture of either STAT3-OE or NC plasmid with Lipofectamine was applied to the wells and incubated for 36 hours. After that, cells were harvested for downstream use. The ratio of the plasmid to Lipofectamine was 2000 ng to 4 μL. All transfected cells were assessed for transfection efficiency using GFP fluorescence, STAT3 quantitative PCR, and protein expression analysis.

Viability assay

A CCK-8 kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used to analyze hepatocyte viability in response to Thr treatment. Briefly, cells were added to 48-well plates (10,000/well), and 20 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added per well. After incubating for 3 h, absorbance at 450 nm was quantified using a microplate reader (TECAN, CA, USA). The cell viability assay included 6 biological replicates per treatment.

LDH release assay

A 48-well plate was utilized to collect supernatants, and an LDH test kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) was employed following the instructions. In short, the reaction mixture was mixed according to the directions given, and each well's absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The data were then adjusted for each well's cell viability. The LDH release assay included 6 biological replicates per treatment.

Oil red O staining

An Oil Red O staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used as instructed. Briefly, following fixation for 1 h at room temperature in 4% formaldehyde, cells were washed using 60% isopropanol, stained for 10 min with filtered Oil Red O solution, and rinsed with water before imaging of lipid droplets with a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). In line with earlier research (Woon et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2021), Oil Red O dye extracted from each well's cells was dissolved using 100% isopropanol to perform a quantitative examination of the lipid droplet content. After that, the mixture was put into a 96-well plate so that the absorbance at 520 nm could be utilized to evaluate the accumulation of lipid droplets. Three duplicates of each treatment were used in the Oil Red O staining.

TG analyses

Hepatocyte TG levels were measured in earlier research (Chu et al., 2022) using a commercial TG test kit (Applygen, Beijing, China). In summary, cells were lysed for 30 minutes in lysis buffer, then heated to 70°C for 10 minutes. Using a properly prepared reaction mixture, the supernatants were then collected for enzymatic tests. Standard curves were used to quantify TG levels, which were then standardized to protein concentrations measured via BCA assay (Beyotime, Beijing, China). The TG release assay included 6 biological replicates per treatment.

Lipid metabolomics

A gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (CG-MS/MS) system (Agilent, CA, USA) was used for the targeted analysis of lipid metabolites, including 48 free FAs in 8 samples of hepatocytes treated with various Thr concentrations and 8 hepatocyte samples transfected with NC or STAT3-OE plasmids. After collection, these cells were snap-frozen with liquid nitrogen and sent to YINGZI GENE Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China) for analysis. Metabolite detection and downstream analyses were performed as in prior reports (Micalizzi et al., 2020). In brief, samples were defrosted and suspended in solutions of methanol, methyl tert-butyl ether, and phosphoric acid, which had been frozen to -20°C. After ultrasonically sonicating the solution, a nitrogen blower (Shanghai Analytical Instrument, Shanghai, China) was used to dry the supernatants. After the dried samples were cooled to ambient temperature, they were suspended in 15% boron trifluoride methanol and mixed with saturated sodium chloride and n-hexane solutions. The upper n-hexane layer was then transferred, not a GC vial, for analysis. At the same time, a BCA kit (Beyotime) was used to quantify the protein levels in the remaining cell-derived suspension (Beyotime, Beijing, China). For the production of quality control samples, combine equal parts of extracts from every sample in the same group (QC). Once the instrument's high stability and quality control were established, all samples were subjected to GC analysis using an Agilent 8890 connected to quadrupole MS (Agilent 5977B). A DB-5MS capillary column was used for GC separation, using highly pure helium as the carrier GAS with the following settings: 40°C for 2 min, ramp to 200°C at 30°C/min, hold at 30°C for 1 min, ramp to 240°C at 10°C/min, hold at 240°C for 1 min, ramp to 285°C at 5°C/min, and hold at 285°C for 3 min. A 1 mL/min flow rate, a 1 μL splitless injection volume, and an inlet temperature of 250°C were used for these analyses. MS conditions were as follows: EI source temperature 230°C, ionization voltage 70 eV, transmission line temperature 280°C, and quadrupole temperature 150°C. Data were acquired in SIM mode following a 4-minute solvent delay (Tan et al., 2020).

Transcriptomic sequencing

TRIzol (Takara, Dalian, China) was used to isolate total RNA from hepatocytes following NC or STAT3-OE plasmid transfection, after which RNA-seq was performed using protocols provided (Liu et al., 2023) by GENE DENOVO (Guangzhou, China). Bowtie2 aligned high-quality reads to the duck reference genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_015476345.1/). Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed on significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs; fold-change > 2 and P < 0.05) to investigate their assigned activities. For each treatment, there were 6 biological replicates in the transcriptome sequencing.

qPCR

TRIzol (Takara, Dalian, China) was used to extract total RNA from duck hepatocytes, after which a NanoDrop 2000 instrument (Thermo, MA, USA) was used to assess RNA quantity and quality. Genomic DNA was eliminated and cDNA was prepared with the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Accurate Biology, AG11728), after which the PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green master mix (Thermo) was used to perform qPCR analyses on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, CA, USA) using the primers listed in Table 1 that were prepared by Tsingke Biotech (Beijing, China), with β-actin having served as the reference control. The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to quantify relative gene expression. All analyses were performed based on the instructions provided by the corresponding manufacturers. The qPCR analyses included 6 biological replicates per treatment.

Western immunoblotting

RIPA buffer (Beyotime) with 1% phenylmethanesulfonylfluoride (PMSF, Beyotime) was used to lyse cells, after which protein levels in the lysates were measured with a BCA kit (Beyotime). Proteins were denatured in SDS buffer (Beyotime) and separated on 8-16% SDS-PAGE gels (GenScript, Nanjing, China) at 120 V for 60 min, followed by transfer (220 mA, 90 min) onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). These blots were blocked with rapid blocking solution (NCM, Suzhou, China) for 30 min, after which they were probed at 4°C overnight with primary anti-STAT3 (A1192) and anti-β-actin (AC026) from ABclonal (China). Following a 2-hour incubation period with the corresponding secondary antibodies (1:10,000 in 1% BSA; Abcam), they were subjected to band detection using a western blot detection system (Tanon, Shanghai, China) and an enhanced chemiluminescent substrate (NCM, Suzhou, China), with quantification performed using ImageJ. Each treatment had 3 biological replicates in the WB analysis.

Luciferase assays

Predictive analyses of the 2000 bp promoter sequences upstream of the translation initiation sites for the SCD1 and FADS2 genes were performed with the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/), which identified two and one putative STAT3 binding sites in the SCD1 and FADS2 promoter regions, respectively. The complete promoter sequences for SCD1 and FADS2 (wt-SCD1, wt-FADS2) and corresponding sequences in which the highest-scoring target sequences were absent (mut-SCD1, mut-FADS2) were submitted to GeneCreate Biological Engineering Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China), and were inserted into the PGL3-basic vector. DEFs were co-transfected with these STAT3-OE/NC/wt-SCD1/mut-SCD1/wt-FADS2/mut-FADS2/PGL3-basic vectors using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermoa) along with a Renilla luciferase vector (PGL-TK). A Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was then used for the quantification of firefly (hluc) and Renilla (hRluc) luciferase intensity levels, using hluc and hRluc activity as a reference to correct for transfection efficiency. Relative luciferase activity = hRluc/hluc. The fluorescence activity obtained from all STAT3-OE transfections was normalized to the ratio of the corresponding NC treatment. Analyses were conducted using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer EnSpire, MA, USA) to ascertain the presence of STAT3 binding sites in the SCD1 and FADS2 genes. The primers utilized for the SCD1 and FADS2 mutant sequences are outlined in Table 2. The luciferase tests comprised 4 biological replicates for each treatment.

Statistical analyses

GraphPad Prism 6 and SPSS 22.0 were used to perform all statistical analyses. Results are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Unpaired Student's t-tests and one-way ANOVAs accompanied by Duncan's multiple comparison tests were employed to compare group means, with P < 0.05 as the significance threshold. Bioinformatic analyses, including PCA, Volcano Plot, GSEA, GO, and KEGG enrichment Scatter Plot, were conducted utilizing OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool), while heatmaps were produced using online bioinformatics resources (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

Results

Thr strongly suppresses hepatocyte TG and FA accumulation

Hepatocytes were treated for 36 h with a range of Thr concentrations to investigate how Thr influences lipid metabolism. Lipid droplet content was determined using Oil Red O staining. Fig. 1a and 1b show that Thr substantially inhibited lipid droplet accumulation in a dose-dependent manner (P < 0.01), and Fig. 1c shows that TG content in these cells was also reduced (P < 0.01). Variations in cell viability were observed in response to different Thr concentrations, and CCK-8 and LDH assay approaches confirmed that Thr significantly enhanced (P < 0.01) hepatocyte proliferation while reducing (P < 0.01) cell death (Fig. 1d, e). Free FA levels in hepatocytes treated with various Thr were significantly reduced, such as SFAs (C6-0, C8-0, C10-0, C11-0, C12-0, C14-0, C15-0, C16-0, C17-0, C18-0, C19-0, C20-0, C21-0, C22-0, C23-0, C24-0), MUFAs (C11-1, C15-1, C16-1T, C18-1n9c, C18-1n9t, C22-1n9), and PUFAs (C18-2n6c, C182n6t, C18-3n3, C20-2, C20-3n6, C20-4n6, C20-5n3, C22-4. C22-6n3) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1f). The key genes associated with this FA metabolism were analyzed by qPCR. Thr supplementation was found to significantly reduce the levels of SCD1, FADS1, FADS2, ELOVL2, ELOVL7, PPAR-α, PPAR-γ, and FABP1 expression (P < 0.01) without any corresponding changes in ACC or FASN expression (P > 0.05) (Fig. 1g). These results offer evidence in support of the ability of Thr to inhibit TG accumulation in hepatocytes via the regulation of FA metabolism.

Thr supplementation induces STAT3 upregulation

The addition of Thr into the medium enhances the expression of the STAT3 gene in primary hepatocytes isolated from ducks in vitro in the present study (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2a, b). We constructed a STAT3 overexpression (STAT3-OE) vector with a pIRES2 vector (Fig. 2c). The successful transfection of the STAT3-OE vector in primary hepatocytes was verified by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, observing its mRNA and protein (Fig. 2e, f) suggested that the STAT3-OE vector could be successfully expressed in primary hepatocytes.

(e-f) qPCR and Western blot analysis of STAT3 mRNA expression and protein levels after STAT3 overexpression.

STAT3 overexpression reduces TG and FA levels in hepatocytes

Hepatocytes transfected with STAT3-OE have demonstrated a significant decrease in the number of lipid droplets and a reduction in triglyceride (TG) content (P < 0.05) in comparison to cells transfected with the control vector. Furthermore, the ability of Thr-modulated lipid deposition was diminished by STAT3-OE (Fig. 3a, b). Targeted GC-MS/MS FA metabolomics analyses of these hepatocytes revealed that STAT3-OE cells exhibited significantly (P < 0.05) reduced levels of SFAs (C8-0, C10-0, C11-0, C12-0, C14-0, C15-0, C16-0, C17-0, C18-0, C19-0, C20-0, C21-0, C22-0, C23-0, C24-0), MUFAs (C11-1, C15-1, C16-1T, C18-1n9c, C18-1n9t), and PUFAs (C18-2n6c, C182n6t, C18-3n3, C20-2, C20-3n6, C20-5n3, C22-4) (Fig. 3c). The Thr addition and the STAT3-OE decreased 27 FAs (Fig. 3d, P < 0.05). However, adding Thr alone uniquely reduced these 4 specific FAs (C6-0, C22-1n9, C20-4n6, and C22-6n3) (Fig. 3d).

STAT3 overexpression induces transcriptomic changes in duck hepatocytes

Then, transcriptomic sequencing was performed on hepatocytes transfected with the NC or STAT3-OE constructs to elucidate the mechanisms by which STAT3 regulates FA metabolism. These 12 samples exhibited two distinct clustered groupings consistent with significant differences in gene expression as a function of STAT3 gene dose (Fig. 4a). In total, 488 genes were significantly differentially expressed in the STAT3-OE cells relative to NC cells, of which 272 and 216 were upregulated and downregulated (Fig. 4b). KEGG enrichment analyses of these DEGs revealed that the JAK-STAT and Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathways were the most strongly enriched (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4c). However, there was no apparent enrichment of any lipid metabolism-related pathways. Therefore, GO functional annotation was used to evaluate DEGs linked to lipid metabolism, identifying 15 enriched GO pathways involved in lipid metabolism (Fig. 4d).

Transcriptomic analysis of hepatocytes after STAT3 overexpression. (a) PCA analysis of RNA-seq data after treatment with NC STAT3-OE. (b) Volcano plot of DEGs. (c) Differential KEGG enrichment pathways. (d) Differential GO enrichment pathways related to lipid metabolism.

GSEA analyses were then performed to clarify the regulatory effect of STAT3 on lipid metabolism, revealing that the overexpression of STAT3 resulted in significant (P < 0.05) reductions in the expression of genes associated with the Response to fatty acid, Triglyceride biosynthetic process, and Lipid transport gene sets, together with significant (P < 0.01) reductions in the expression of genes involved in the Unsaturated fatty acid biosynthetic process and Triglyceride metabolic pathways. In contrast, JAK-STAT pathway-related gene expression was significantly enhanced (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5a). Heatmap analyses of the DEGs associated with these pathways were used to investigate further downstream targets of STAT3′s regulatory effects in lipid metabolism. The results showed that, after STAT3 was overexpressed, seven DEGs related to the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (including SCD1 and FADS2) were significantly downregulated (P < 0.05). However, the PLAG25 gene was unaffected (Fig. 5b). Moreover, 12 DEGs associated with the triglyceride metabolic process pathway were significantly downregulated (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5c). In comparison, the overexpression of STAT3 led to significant increases in the expression of JAK-STAT pathway-related 8 DEGs and the concomitant downregulation of 9 DEGs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5d).

STAT3 binds to the SCD1 promoter and inhibits transcription

Ten DEGs, including the upregulated genes SOCS3, IL7R, and IL12B and the downregulated genes SCD1, FADS2, ACSL6, PTGS1, LIPC, IFN, and PDGFRB, were selected for qPCR validation to perform more thorough analyses of these transcriptome data. In all cases, qPCR results yielded expression trends consistent with those from the RNA-seq analysis (P < 0.01) (Fig. 6a), confirming the reliability of these analyses. As Thr supplementation and the overexpression of STAT3 resulted in significant SCD1 and FADS2 downregulation, the relationship between STAT3 and these two genes was explored at length. Using the JASPAR database, four predicted STAT3 binding sites were identified within the 2000 bp promoter region upstream of the SCD1 gene, while one such STAT3 binding site was identified within the FADS2 promoter (Fig. 6b, c). Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed the ability of STAT3 to bind to the SCD1 promoter, as in co-transfection assays, the overexpression of STAT3 reduced the activity of a wt-SCD1 promoter construct without any corresponding effect on mut-SCD1 promoter construct (Fig. 6d). No difference in the inhibitory effect of STAT3-OE on FADS2 reporter activity was observed when evaluating a mut-FADS2 reporter construct (Fig. 6e). The data indicate that STAT3 may specifically target and regulate the transcription of SCD1.

Discussion

Thr is increasingly understood to significantly influence animal fat deposition (Çiftci and Ceylan, 2004; Zarrin-Kavyani et al., 2020). The liver is the crucial site of fat transportation and processing, functioning as a hub for the biosynthesis and breakdown of most lipids (Lu et al., 2024). Supplemental Thr intake can reduce the hepatic accumulation of lipids while inhibiting excessive fat deposition in rats (Chen et al., 2022). In Pekin ducks, Thr supplementation can also lower hepatic TG levels and minimize abdominal fat formation (Jiang et al., 2017). Following these findings, the current analysis demonstrated that Thr administration lowered FA and TG levels in duck hepatocytes in vitro.

In contrast to Thr supplementation, the exogenous administration of aromatic amino acids (AAAs, including tryptophan, phenylalanine, and tyrosin), methionine (Met), and lysine (Lys) have failed to alleviate high-fat-diet-induced obesity in mice (Ma et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2024). This could be because of the distinct catabolic mechanisms that break down Thr. AAAs, Met, and Lys are mainly broken down by transamination and deamination, resulting in essential molecules like dopamine and serotonin (from AAAs), S-adenosylmethionine (from Met), and α-ketoglutarate (from Lys), none of which are directly involved in lipid metabolism (Brosnan., 2006; Chia et al., 1984; Han., 2022; Xiao et al., 2016). In contrast, Thr is not degraded via deamination or transamination (Davis and Austic, 1982). Major enzymes associated with the metabolism of Thr include Thr dehydratase, Thr dehydrogenase, and Thr aldolase (Malinovsky, 2018). The byproducts of Thr catabolism mediated by Thr dehydratase or Thr dehydrogenase include acetyl-CoA, glycine, and aminoacetone (A J Davis, 1994). Acetyl-CoA, in turn, can serve as a substrate for de novo FA synthesis through its conversion into malonyl-CoA (Li et al., 2022a), which may provide the physiological basis for the effect of Thr supplementation on lipid accumulation in hepatocytes. In this study, however, Thr supplementation failed to affect the expression of key de novo FA synthesis-related genes (ACC, FASN), indicating that Thr may not impact de novo FA synthesis in duck hepatocytes. Given the much higher hepatocyte activity levels that were observed following Thr supplementation, acetyl-CoA may preferentially enter the tricarboxylic acid cycle as a source of energy that can fuel growth, as Thr plays vital roles in cellular growth and development (Dozier et al., 2001). Thr supplementation led to a reduction in the levels of TG and several FAs in duck hepatocytes, along with the downregulation of key FA elongation-related genes (SCD1, FADS1, FADS2, ELOVL2, and ELOVL7). This suggests that Thr may thus mitigate hepatocyte TG accumulation by inhibiting FA elongation. Further, previous studies have shown that genes in the PPAR family are crucial regulators of FA metabolism, with PPARα promoting the expression of several FA oxidation-related genes (Almanza-Perez et al., 2010). In contrast, PPARγ can activate CD36 and Nur77 to favor the uptake and storage of FAs (Yang et al., 2020). In the present study, Thr inhibited the expression of both PPARα and PPARγ. These data suggest that Thr can reduce the accumulation of triglycerides by suppressing the capacity of hepatocytes for FA metabolism.

Some earlier studies found that Thr supplementation enhanced the expression and activation of STAT3 (Jiang et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2023). Several reports have documented a link between STAT3 and the control of lipid metabolism (Li et al., 2021; Schaschkow et al., 2021). The co-treatment experiments in this work demonstrated that the overexpression of STAT3 effectively eliminated triglyceride accumulation associated with threonine deprivation. Furthermore, the alterations in FA metabolism observed in hepatocytes due to STAT3 activation were analogous to those elicited by Thr therapy. In transcriptomic analyses, STAT3 overexpression was associated with a reduction in the expression of components of the pathways related to both the synthesis and degradation of lipids, highlighting a central role for STAT3 in the Thr-mediated control of lipid metabolism. STAT3 signaling, a crucial component of the JAK-STAT system, often responds to cytokines and various growth factors that trigger its dimerization and subsequent nuclear translocation, enabling it to bind to and regulate the transcription of specific target genes. Downstream regulatory proteins, including members of the SOCS family, regulate STAT3 activity via negative feedback mechanisms (You et al., 2015; Hillmer et al., 2016). Similarly, the present study found that overexpression of STAT3 resulted in the upregulation of IL6, IL6R, JAK2, and SOCS3 together with concomitant PDGFRB, STAT1, STAT5B, SOCS1, and SOCS2 downregulation. STAT3 is most commonly regarded as a transcriptional activator, and it has previously been shown to modulate lipid metabolism in part through the activation of lipid-related genes, including FAO, FTO, and CD36 (Uri Rozovski, 2018; Zhang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021). STAT3 was also recently identified as a transcriptional repressor in certain contexts, however, as in the case of its ability to interact with HDAC11 to mediate the targeted inhibition of Nos2 expression, resulting in altered antifungal immune response induction (Wu et al., 2022a). ChIP-seq data from T cells overexpressing STAT3 further revealed the ability of this transcription factor to directly bind and inhibit the expression of genes, including TCF7 and ID3 (Sun et al., 2023). Consistent with such a model, the present data revealed that STAT3 can target and inhibit SCD1 gene expression.

SCD1 is an essential regulator of FA composition within cells by synthesizing MUFAs from SFA precursors. It primarily catalyzes C18:0 (stearic acid) and C16:0 (palmitic acid) conversion into C18:1 (oleic acid) and C16:1 (palmitoleic acid) (Ascenzi et al., 2021; Xuan et al., 2022). In this study, supplemental Thr treatment and STAT3 overexpression were associated with reductions in the C18:1n9c, C18:1n9t, and C16:1T levels observed in duck hepatocytes together with SCD1 gene downregulation, supporting a role for SCD1 in the noted Thr-induced and STAT3-mediated changes in FA metabolism. Recent investigations indicate that SCD1 significantly impacts lipid metabolism, as SCD1-deficient mice exhibit diminished hepatic expression of SREBP1c, PPARα, and PPARγ genes, consequently leading to reduced de novo fatty acid synthesis and lower expression of oxidative metabolism-related genes such as ACC, FABP, FASN, and ACSL. (Mauvoisin et al., 2010; Nakamura et al., 2014; Yao et al., 2017). Mice in which SCD1 had explicitly been knocked out in the liver presented significantly lower hepatic TG, plasma TG, and overall body fat mass levels (Miyazaki et al., 2007; Mori et al., 2024). In contrast, elevated levels of SCD1 enzymatic activity have been linked to enhanced TG and cholesterol ester biosynthesis and tissue accumulation, leading to the onset of metabolic disorders, including obesity and fatty liver disease (Piccinin et al., 2019). Therefore, these prior studies and the present results suggest that the reduced TG levels observed in response to supplemental Thr intake are attributable, at least in part, to the STAT3-mediated targeting and inhibition of SCD1. This study concludes by highlighting Thr's vital role in preserving lipid homeostasis and promoting liver health in meat ducks. Given that their liver lipid balance is more sensitive, these results imply that mule ducks, who are more likely to have fatty livers, and ducks on low-protein diets may benefit most from proper Thr supplementation in feed (Xie et al., 2017; Hu et al., 2024).

The findings of this study have to be seen in light of some limitations: First, the present research demonstrated that Thr has a significantly stronger regulatory effect on FA metabolism than STAT3, suggesting that the STAT3-SCD1 axis is just one of the molecular pathways through which Thr influences lipid deposition in hepatocytes. Other regulatory pathways still require further exploration. Second, this investigation was limited to duck hepatocytes, and it remains uncertain whether Thr and STAT3 exert similar effects on FA metabolism in other poultry species or mammals. Furthermore, fatty liver is a significant factor that negatively impacts the performance of meat ducks in current production settings. Since this study focused solely on the effects of Thr and STAT3 on normal duck hepatocytes, further research exploring the potential of this nutrient and target for the prevention and treatment of fatty liver in poultry production is warranted.

Conclusions

In summary, these results revealed that Thr can promote STAT3 upregulation together with the targeted inhibition of SCD1 transcription in duck hepatocytes, thereby reducing FA and TG accumulation within these cells. These results offer new insight into how Thr regulates lipid metabolism in duck liver while providing additional evidence in support of STAT3 as a key target for efforts to regulate hepatic lipid metabolism.
