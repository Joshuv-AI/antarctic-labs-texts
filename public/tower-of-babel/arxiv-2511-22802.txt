Birkhoff Measures, Birkhoff Sums, and Discrepancies
D. Ralston, F.M. Tangerman, J.J.P. Veerman, H. Wu

Abstract




Abstract

We study the distribution of a sequence of points in the circle generated
by rotations by a fixed irrational number
\rho
with initial condition
x_{0}
, that is:

\{x_{0}+i\rho\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The
discrepancy
as defined
by Pisot and Van Der Corput
[
5
]
, quantifies how evenly distributed such a sequence is.

Consider the ergodic or Birkhoff sum of mean zero
S(\rho,n,x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\{x+i\rho\}-1/2)
, where

\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part. This is a piecewise-linear map in the variable
x
with
n

branches, each with slope
n
. For fixed
n
and
\rho
, let
\nu(\rho,n,z)
be the number of pre-images
of
S(\rho,n,x)=z
divided by
n
. Then
\nu(\rho,n,z)
is a probability density. We call the associated
measures Birkhoff measures. We investigate how the graph of
\nu(\rho,n,z)
varies with
n
.

We prove that the length of the support of the Birkhoff measure
\nu(\rho,n,z)dz
can be expressed in
terms of the discrepancy. We also show that if
n
is a continued fraction denominator of
\rho
,
then the graph of
\nu(\rho,n,z)
an approximate isosceles trapezoid.
We also give new, brief, proofs of two classical results, one by Ramshaw

[
14
]
and one found by Kuipers-Niederreiter
[
13
]
. These results
allow efficient computation of both Birkhoff sums and discrepancies.

1
Introduction

Consider rigid rotations on the unit circle
S^{1}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}=[0,1)
by ‘angle’
\rho\in[0,1]
:
{x}\to{x+\rho}
. For much of this paper the standing assumption is that
\rho
is irrational.

1.1
Birkhoff Sums

This paper considers
partial sums of the function
\{x\}-1/2
under the rotation by angle
\rho
, where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part. This is also referred to as
ergodic sums
or
Birkhoff sums

S(\rho,n,x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\{x+i\rho\}-1/2)=nx+\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\,\rho-\dfrac{n}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\,\lfloor x+i\rho\rfloor\,.

(1.1)

We note that in the definition of
S
the summation starts at 1 (and not at 0) and ends at
n
.
When
\rho
and
n
are fixed we may abbreviate
S(\rho,n,x)
by
S(x)
. The complete inverse image
under this function of
x
(holding
\rho
and
n
fixed) will be denoted by
S^{-1}
.

1.2
Discrepancy

Discrepancy was originally introduced by Pisot and Van Der Corput
[
5
]
and later in

[
13
]
, as a way to measure the extent to which a finite set of points in a given interval is equally
distributed. The way this concept becomes interesting is given an infinite sequence and measure the
discrepancy of its first
n
elements with increasing
n
. Such finite segments can on occasion be
evenly distributed but the insertion of additional points breaks such evenness.

Let
\overline{x}:=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of points in the unit interval
[0,1)
. The
subintervals
I
of
[0,1)
we consider are half open and half closed, of the form
I=[a,b)
or its
complement (recall that
[0,1)
is our model for the unit circle).
Then denote

A(I,n):=\#\{1\leq i\leq n\ |\ x_{i}\in I\}\,.

Definition 1.1

The discrepancy of the first
n
points of
\overline{x}
is then

D_{n}(\overline{x}):=\sup_{I=[a,b)\subseteq[0,1)}\left(\frac{A(I,n)}{n}-\ell(I)\right)\,.

In fact, the quantity
nD_{n}(\overline{x})
is much more convenient. We refer to it as the clumpiness of
the sequence.

1.3
Birkhoff Measures

The aim of this work is to introduce the notion of Birkhoff measures and indicate their relation with
Birkhoff sums and discrepancy. The construction of the associated density is illustrated in Figure

1.1
. Its formal definition is then as the pushforward of the Lebesgue
measure by
S
:

\nu(\rho,n,z)|dz|=S(x)|dx|\,.

Equivalently,

\nu(\rho,n,z)=\frac{\#S^{-1}(z)}{n}\,.

(1.2)

The construction of the Birkhoff measure for
\rho
equal to the golden mean with
n=13

To illustrate what can be computed with these classical result, we finish this section by giving an
example. Let
\rho_{a}=[a,a,a,\cdots]
be its continued fraction expansion for
a\in\mathbb{N}
, such rotation
numbers are referred to as ‘metallic’. Examples are the golden mean

\rho_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}=[1,1,....]
and the silver mean

\rho_{2}=\sqrt{2}-1=[2,2,....]
. Ramshaw
[
14
]
proved that for these ‘metallic’ means,

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{S(\rho_{a},n,0)}{\ln n}
exists and is equal to

c(a)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{a}{16}\dfrac{1}{\ln\rho_{a}^{-1}}&\;\;{\rm if}\;\;\;a\;\;{\rm is\;even}\\[-8.5359pt]
\\
\dfrac{a(a^{2}+3)}{16(a^{2}+4)}\dfrac{1}{\ln\rho_{a}^{-1}}&\;\;{\rm if}\;\;\;a\;\;\rm{is\;odd}\,.\end{cases}

5.4

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{nD_{n}(\{i\rho_{a}\}_{i=1}^{n})}{\ln n}
exists and is equal to
4c(a)

[
14
]
. Nonetheless, it follows from the ergodicity of the Gauss map that for Lebesgue almost all
\rho
,

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{S(\rho,n,0)}{\ln n}=\infty

and hence
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{nD_{n}(\{i\rho\}_{i=1}^{n})}{\ln n}=\infty
.

2
Birkhoff Measures and Their Properties

Proposition 2.1

i) The density
\nu(\rho,n,z)
satisfies:

\nu(\rho,n,z)=\nu(\rho,n,-z)=\nu(1-\rho,n,z)
.

ii) The support of
\nu(\rho,n,z)
is symmetric.

iii)
0\leq\nu(\rho,n,z)\leq 1
.

Proof.
Translation by
-\rho
starting at
-x
yields the mirror image of translation by
\rho

starting at
x
. Thus we have

S(1-\rho,n,-x)=S(-\rho,n,1-x)=-S(\rho,n,x).

(2.1)

This proves that
\nu(1-\rho,n,-z)=\nu(\rho,n,z)
.

It is sufficient to show that
\nu(-\rho,n,z)=\nu(\rho,n,z)
.
From (
1.1
), one derives that

S(1-\rho,n,\{x+{(n+1)}\rho\})=S(-\rho,n,\{x+{(n+1)}\rho\})=S(\rho,n,x)\,,

(2.2)

and so we see that the number of pre-images of
S(\rho,n,\cdot)=z
equals that of
S(1-\rho,n,\cdot)=z
.
Now the desired symmetry follows from Definition
1.2
. This proves (i).

Setting
\tilde{\rho}=1-\rho
, it follows from (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) that the range
of
S(\tilde{\rho},n,x)
(varying only x) is symmetric, proving (ii). Item (iii) follows from
the fact that
S(\rho,n,x)
has at most
n
branches.

The following proposition essentially asserts that
\nu
is a
tile
.
That is: its translates over
\mathbb{Z}
give the Lebesgue measure, and
\nu
itself is (strictly) positive
on an interval and zero elsewhere (i.e. it has no ‘holes’). These properties are illustrated in
Figure
2.1
.

Figure 2.1:
The sum of seven translates of the Birkhoff measure associated with
e-2
and
n=2024
.

Theorem 2.2
(Tiling Property)

For every fixed irrational
\rho
and positive integer
n
, the density

\nu(\rho,n,z)
satisfies:

i) For every
z\in[0,1)
:
\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\nu(\rho,n,z+i)=1
,

ii) The support of the measure associated with the density
\nu(\rho,n,z)
is a closed and bounded interval.

Proof.
To prove (i), note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\nu(\rho,n,z+i)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\#S^{-1}(z+i)=n.

The right hand side says that we consider pre-images of
\pi\circ S
, where
\pi
is the canonical
projection of
\mathbb{R}
to the circle. The branches of
S
have slope
n
while at the discontinuities,

S
changes by an integer. Therefore,
\pi\circ S=c+nx\mod 1
. It follows that there are precisely

n
inverse images of
\pi\circ S
for each point on the circle.

To prove (ii), observe that
S
has the following two properties:

(a) its only discontinuities points are located at
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
such
that
\lim_{x\to x_{i}^{-}}S(x)>\lim_{x\to x_{i}^{+}}S(x_{i})
(that is: all discontinuities are ‘downward’).

(b)
S(1^{-}):=\lim_{x\rightarrow 1^{-}}S(x)\geq S(0)
.

We prove that the image of
S
is a single (connected) interval.

Let
y_{\pm}
be supremum and infimum of that image of
S
, and set
x_{\pm}
such that

\limsup_{x\rightarrow x_{+}}S(x)=y_{+}
, and similar for
x_{-}
. If
y_{+}>S(0)
, then on
[0,x_{+}]

define
F(x):=\sup_{0\leq x^{\prime}\leq x}S(x^{\prime})
. Note that
F
is continuous on this interval and so
the image of
F
equals
[S(0),y_{+})
. A fortiori, this also holds for the image of
S
. If

y_{-}<S(1^{-})
, then by the same reasoning, the image of
S
must contain all of
(y_{-},S(1^{-}))
.
Since
S(1^{-})\geq S(0)
, the image must contain all of
(y_{-},y_{+})
.

We know for any fixed irrational
\rho
, that as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
\nu(\rho,n,z)
does
not

converge pointwise as
n
tends to infinity. We will see that on the one hand the trapezoid with
support in a bounded interval occurs infinitely often (Theorem
4.6
), while on the
other hand there is also a subsequence
\{n_{i}\}
where the corresponding Birkhoff sums
S(\rho,n,x)
,
after normalization by a constant
B_{n}
that goes to infinity, converges to a Gaussian distribution

[
4
]
.

We have the following result.

Proposition 2.3

Let
n
be fixed. The function from
[0,1)
to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
given by

\rho\rightarrow\nu(\rho,n,z)
is continuous:

\lim_{\rho\rightarrow\rho_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\nu(\rho,n,z)-\nu(\rho_{0},n,z)|\,dz=0\,.

Proof.
Denote by
D
the set of points of discontinuity of
S
, consisting of the points

\{-i\rho\}_{i=1}^{n}
. For
\epsilon>0
, consider the set
D_{\epsilon}
of points within
\epsilon/2
of

D
.
D_{\epsilon}
has Lebesgue measure bounded by
n\epsilon
. Since
S
has derivative
n
(where
it exists), there is a region
J_{\rho}
of measure at most
n^{2}\epsilon
in
\mathbb{R}
where
there can be a different number of pre-images under
S(\rho,n,x)
and
S(\rho_{0},n,x)
.
And so for
\rho
sufficiently close to
\rho_{0}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\nu(\rho,n,z)-\nu(\rho_{0},n,z)|dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{J_{\rho}}|\nu(\rho,n,z)-\nu(\rho_{0},n,z)|dz+\int_{\mathbb{R}-J_{\rho}}|\nu(\rho,n,z)-\nu(\rho_{0},n,z)|dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{J_{\rho}}1\,dz+\int_{\mathbb{R}-J_{\rho}}0\,dz\leq 2n^{2}\epsilon\,,

where we have used Proposition
2.1
(iii).

3
Discrepancy and the Range of Birkhoff Measures

Recall that discrepancy measures the evenness of the distribution of sequences

\overline{x}:=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
in
S^{1}=[0,1)
.
Returning to Definition
1.1
, we note the following. In most texts
D_{n}
is defined
as
\sup_{I\subseteq[0,1)}\left|\frac{A(I,n)}{n}-\ell(I)\right|

(i.e. absolute value is taken). However, if
I
and
J
are complements in
[0,1)
, then

A(I,n)-n\ell(I)+A(J,n)-n\ell(J)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A(I,n)-n\ell(I)=n\ell(J)-A(J,n)\,.

(3.1)

and so we can omit the absolute signs without loss of generality.

Note that discrepancy is large when there are underpopulated or overpopulated intervals as will be
clear by considering two simple examples. If
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=(\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\cdots,\frac{n}{n})
,
then
D_{n}(\overline{x})=\frac{1}{n}
. At the other extreme, if
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=(x_{1},x_{1},\cdots,x_{1})
,
then
D_{n}(\overline{x})=1
.

It is clear that the most evenly distributed set of
n
points corresponds to the first example with
discrepancy equal to
\frac{1}{n}
. Obviously,
D_{n}(\overline{x})
and
D_{n+1}(\overline{x})

cannot
both be best distributed. Pursuing this observation further, one can prove that
for any
\overline{x}
, the following holds: there are infinitely many
n
such that

D_{n}(\overline{x})>c\frac{\ln n}{n}
, where
c=\max_{x>3}\frac{x-2}{4(x-1)\ln x}\approx 0.120
),

[
18
]
.

The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 3.1

The range of
S(x)
over
x\in[0,1)
is an interval of length
nD_{n}(\overline{x})
, where

\overline{x}=\{i\rho\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
.

Proof.
Let
-\overline{x}=\{-i\rho\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
. By symmetry,

nD_{n}(\overline{x})=nD_{n}(-\overline{x})
.
Denote the points
\{-i\rho\}_{i=1}^{n}
in
[0,1)
by
y_{i}
in ascending order so that

0\leq y_{1}\leq y_{2}\cdots\leq y_{n}<1\,.

Because the definition of
D_{n}
involves finitely many points
y_{i}
, its determination
is then a maximum over finitely many quantities, namely

nD_{n}(\overline{x})=nD_{n}(-\overline{x}):=\max_{0<i,j\leq n}\left((j-i+1)-n(y_{j}-y_{i})\right)=1+\max_{0<i,j\leq n}\left((j-i)-n(y_{j}-y_{i})\right)\,.

By (
3.1
), this gives

nD_{n}(\overline{x})=1+\max_{0<i,j\leq n}\left(n(y_{j}-y_{i})-(j-i)\right)\,.

(3.2)

Recall that
S(\rho,n,x)
has
n
branches with discontinuities at
\{-i\rho\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Since each
branch has slope
n
, and is reduced at every discontinuity by one, the difference of the values of
the suprema
S^{+}(y_{i})
of the successive branches can be computed (for fixed
\rho
and
n
) as

S^{+}(y_{i+1})=S^{+}(y_{i})+n(y_{i+1}-y_{i})-1=S^{+}(y_{1})+n(y_{i+1}-y_{1})-i\,.

The local minima of
S^{-}(y_{j+1})
are exactly one less than the local suprema, so

S^{-}(y_{j+1})=S^{+}(y_{j+1})-1=S^{+}(y_{1})-1+n(y_{j+1}-y_{1})-j\,.

The range of
S(x)
is the interval bounded by the largest of the former and the smallest of the latter.
Its length is the difference of those two, which together with (
3.2
) yields the theorem.

Corollary 3.2

For every positive integer
n
, any irrational number
\rho
, the support of

\nu(\rho,n,z)
, as a function of
z
, is an interval symmetric around 0 and whose length is
equal to
nD_{n}(\overline{x}_{\rho})
, where
\overline{x}_{\rho}=\{i\rho\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
.

Proof.
The support of
\nu
equals the range of
S
. The symmetry was established in Proposition

2.1
(ii).

Corollary 3.3

For fixed n, the function from
[0,1)
to
\mathbb{R}
given by
\rho\rightarrow nD_{n}(\overline{x}_{\rho})

where
\overline{x}_{\rho}=\{i\rho\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, is continuous.

Proof.
Recall that
\nu(\rho,n,z)\geq 1/n
on its support. Thus if the support of
\nu(\rho,n,z)

differs by at least
\epsilon>0
from that of
\nu(\rho_{0},n,z)
as
\rho\rightarrow\rho_{0}
, then
the integral in Proposition
2.3
would yield at least
\epsilon/n
.

This has a simple but useful corollary that we record here for future reference. Let

\widehat{S}(\rho,n,x)=nx+\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\,\rho-\dfrac{n}{2}-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\,\lfloor x+i\rho\rfloor\,.

This is simply the expression of (
1.1
), but with the sum ranging from 0 to
n-1
. Then
\widehat{S}(\rho,n,x)=S(\rho,n,\{x-\rho\})
from which we conclude that:

Lemma 3.4

The range of
S
equals the range of
\widehat{S}
.

4
The Trapezoid Theorem

In this section we show that for continued fraction approximants
q_{n}
to
\rho
that the graph of the density

\nu(\rho,q_{n},.)
is
approximately trapezoidal
, with the precise definition given in Definition

4.5
. First, we state a few convention that we will need later.

Definition 4.1

(See
[
19
]
Chapter 6.) The continued fraction coefficients of
\rho\in(0,1)

are denoted by
a_{n}
so that
\rho=[a_{1},a_{2},\cdots]
. The continued fraction convergents to
\rho
are denoted
by
p_{n}/q_{n}
with the following conventions:
p_{0}=0
,
p_{1}=1
,
q_{0}=1
, and
q_{1}=a_{1}
, and for
n\geq 2
,

\begin{array}[]{ccc}p_{n+1}&=&a_{n+1}p_{n}+p_{n-1}\\[0.0pt]
q_{n+1}&=&a_{n+1}q_{n}+q_{n-1}\;.\end{array}

Definition 4.2

(See
[
19
]
.) For given
\rho
and
n\geq 0
, define
d_{n}
as

d_{n}:=q_{n}\rho-p_{n}\,,

Note that for
n
odd, the entry
d_{n}

is negative while for
n
even
d_{n}
is positive. Note that
d_{0}=\rho
,
d_{1}=a_{1}\rho-1
, and for
n\geq 2

\begin{array}[]{ccc}d_{n+1}&=&a_{n+1}d_{n}+d_{n-1}\end{array}\;.

Proposition 4.3

Let
d=q\rho-p
with
|d|<1/(q-1)
, with
p
and
q
coprime. We have:

\nu(\rho,q,z)=1\quad\textrm{iff}\quad z\in\left[\frac{-(1-(q-1)|d|)}{2},\frac{(1-(q-1)|d|)}{2}\right)\,,

and
\nu(\rho,q,z)\in[0,\frac{q-1}{q}]
otherwise.

Figure 4.1:
Sketch of the branches of
S
with
d=0
in red and with
d>0
in blue.
Here,
S_{j}
is the third branch. The lengths of the segments indicated by
A
,
B
, and
C
,
are, respectively,
\frac{(q+1)d}{2}
,
\frac{i_{+}d}{q}
, and
\frac{(q+1-2i_{+})d}{2}
.

Proof.
Fix
p
,
q
, and
\rho
. By definition of
\nu
, its value at any point
z
equals
the number of solutions
x
of
S(x)=z
divided by
q
. Since
S
has
q
branches, then

\nu(\rho,q,z)\neq 1
implies that
\nu(\rho,q,z)\in[0,\frac{q-1}{q}]
. So all we just need to
prove the first statement of the proposition.

Our strategy is to derive explicit equations for each of those branches (see Figure

4.1
). Let
\rho=\frac{p+d}{q}
and
d
be such that
|d|<1/(q-1)
. For ease of
expressing the ideas, we think of each of the branches as including
both
endpoints.

We work out what
S
looks like when we substitute
\rho=\frac{p+d}{q}
.

S(x)=qx+\dfrac{(q+1)(p+d)-q}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{q}\,\left\lfloor x+i\left(\frac{p+d}{q}\right)\right\rfloor\,.

(4.1)

Now set
x=0
and use Proposition
6.2
(i) to evaluate
S(0)
for
d\in\left(\frac{-1}{q-1},\frac{1}{q-1}\right)
.

S(0)=\frac{(q+1)d-1}{2}-\lfloor d\rfloor=\frac{(q+1)d-\textrm{sgn}(d)}{2}\,,

(4.2)

With the proviso that in this case we understand
\textrm{sgn}(0)=+1
. For ease of argument we now
restrict to
d>0
from here on out. The case
d<0
is almost identical.

Clearly, each of the
q
branches of
S
is a linear segment with slope
q
. We label them from left to
right by
S_{0}
,
S_{1}
,…,
S_{q-1}
. So the first
S_{0}
is a segment of the line

qx+\frac{(q+1)d-1}{2}
. The discontinuity causes a reduction by one, and so
S_{1}
lies on
the line
qx+\frac{(q+1)d-1}{2}-1
, and so on. Thus

S_{j}(x)=qx+\frac{(q+1)d-1}{2}-j\,.

The locations of the discontinuities are given by

The following proposition is the main vehicle that extracts the recursive structure of the sequence

Proposition 6.1

For irrational
\rho

\forall\,\;0\leq k<q_{n+1},\;\;\;\;S(\rho,q_{n}+k,0)=S(\rho,q_{n},0)+S(\rho,k,0)+kd_{n}\,.

First place
\{i\rho\}
for

from 1 to

on the unit interval to compute the Birkhoff sum

Let

This is identical to the set of points

translated by

if and only if
each of the latter points has the property that
[i\rho,i\rho+d_{n}]
(or

[i\rho+d_{n},i\rho]
if

is negative) does
not
contain an integer. In turn, the closest
return theorem for continued fractions
[
19
]
Chapter 6 guarantees that this is the case
if and only if

.

The next proposition will be used to develop a simple expression for

Proposition 6.2

Let
\gcd(p,q)=1

\displaystyle i)\hskip 21.68121pt\sum_{i=1}^{q}\,\left\lfloor i\rho\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\frac{(q+1)p-q+1}{2}+\lfloor d\rfloor

\displaystyle\frac{(q+1)d+q-1}{2}-\lfloor d\rfloor\,.

If
\rho
is equal to
