A model of moral justice for medical universities: A structural equation modeling approach to ethical leadership and organizational integrity
Authors: Monir Ostadi, Nasrolah Ghashghaeizadeh, Narges Saeidian Khorasgani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Human resources are central to organizational progress, where moral justice plays a key role in promoting fairness, ethics, and sustainable development. In medical universities—where education, clinical care, and administration intersect—moral justice is crucial for maintaining trust and integrity. However, there is a lack of validated models tailored to this unique context.OBJECTIVE:This study aimed to develop and validate a conceptual model of moral justice tailored to the unique context of medical universities.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A sequential explanatory mixed-methods design was used. In the qualitative phase, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 14 experts (HR directors, department heads, and ethics specialists), selected via purposive and snowball sampling. The interviews lasted 45–60 minutes and were thematically analyzed. Rigor was ensured through triangulation, member checking, and audit trails. Based on qualitative findings, a 36-item questionnaire using a 5-point Likert scale was developed and administered to 256 employees across managerial, technical, and administrative roles at Qom University of Medical Sciences. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) were conducted via IBM SPSS Amos, Version 29 (IBM Corp., 2023) to validate the measurement model and assess relationships among the constructs. Model fit was evaluated via Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA).RESULTS:The study identified causal factors (e.g., ethical leadership and transparent processes), background factors (e.g., organizational culture and moral values), and intervening factors (e.g., ethical leadership development, and equitable resource distribution) as critical components of moral justice. Strategies such as increasing participation and enhancing accountability were found to drive actionable change, leading to expected outcomes such as transparency, fairness, and increased organizational accountability. The model demonstrated strong fit indices (e.g., CFI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.05), confirming its validity and reliability.CONCLUSION:The proposed model provides a robust framework for embedding moral justice into organizational structures, emphasizing the interconnectedness of leadership, culture, policies, and strategies. It offers practical recommendations for fostering a culture of trust, fairness, and integrity. Future research should explore the role of technology in enhancing moral justice and expand the model’s applicability to diverse organizational contexts.

Introduction

Human resources constitute the cornerstone of organizational success, serving as the primary driver of development, innovation, and progress.[1] They play a critical role in implementing strategies and executing activities that align with organizational goals, making them indispensable for achieving sustainable growth.[2] By leveraging their expertise, experience, and creativity, employees empower managers to make informed decisions, enhance their operational efficiency, and increase their organizational performance.[3] Moreover, motivated and engaged human resources foster a culture of productivity and innovation, which are essential for maintaining a competitive edge in today’s dynamic environment.[4]

Organizational justice within human resources emerges as a pivotal factor in this context. A workplace environment that prioritizes fairness and equality not only enhances employee satisfaction and motivation but also strengthens decision-making processes, builds trust, reduces conflicts, and ultimately drives organizational productivity.[56] By embedding justice into their core values, organizations establish a foundation for sustainable growth, fostering a culture of inclusivity and long-term success.[7]

In the unique context of medical science universities, the importance of justice takes on even greater significance. These institutions operate at the intersection of education, research, and healthcare delivery, bearing a profound responsibility to uphold moral justice. As hubs for training future healthcare professionals, advancing medical research, and delivering patient care, medical science universities must ensure that all members—students, faculty, and staff—have equitable access to resources, opportunities, and facilities. Moral justice in this setting fosters an environment of fairness, inclusivity, and ethical integrity, which is essential for maintaining public trust and advancing societal well-being.[89] Conversely, a lack of moral justice can lead to dissatisfaction, diminished motivation, and disengagement, adversely affecting the quality of education, mental health, and clinical outcomes.[10] This, in turn, undermines an institution’s ability to fulfill its mission and deliver high-quality healthcare services to society.[11]

Despite its critical importance, there is a notable gap in the literature regarding comprehensive models of moral justice tailored to the unique needs of medical science universities. Existing research predominantly focuses on justice in broader organizational contexts, leaving a void in understanding how moral justice can be systematically implemented in academic medical institutions.[121314] This gap is particularly concerning given the high-stakes nature of these environments, where ethical principles and fairness are paramount for fostering trust, advancing research, and ensuring equitable healthcare delivery.

To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to develop and empirically test a conceptual model of moral justice specifically designed for medical science universities via a mixed-methods approach and structural equation modeling (SEM). Unlike previous studies that treat justice as a generic organizational value, this research contextualizes moral justice within the complex, multidimensional environment of academic healthcare institutions. It integrates qualitative insights from expert interviews with robust quantitative validation to propose a validated, context-specific framework that reflects the ethical, administrative, and human resource realities of medical universities. The resulting model offers both theoretical contributions to the field of organizational ethics and practical guidelines for institutional leaders seeking to embed justice into academic governance and healthcare practice.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study adopted a sequential explanatory mixed-methods approach, structured in two distinct phases to provide both depth and breadth in examining ethical justice within human resource management. The initial qualitative phase was designed to explore conceptual understandings and practical experiences related to ethical justice through an interpretive lens. This phase informed the development of a theoretical framework that would later guide the quantitative investigation. In contrast, the quantitative phase sought to empirically test and validate the emerging constructs via a structured instrument and statistical modeling.

The setting for this investigation was the University of Medical Sciences, a multifaceted academic institution that combines education, research, and healthcare services. This setting provided access to diverse organizational levels and professional roles, allowing the study to capture the dynamics of ethical practices in a real-world institutional context. The university’s large-scale, hierarchical structure makes it an ideal environment for examining both the managerial and operational dimensions of human resource ethics. Furthermore, its ongoing administrative reforms and performance evaluation systems offered a timely backdrop for investigating perceptions of fairness and justice within organizational processes.

Study participants and sampling

The participant selection strategies were tailored separately for each research phase to suit its methodological purpose. In the qualitative phase, the focus was on acquiring in-depth insights from individuals with substantial expertise. Fourteen professionals—including department heads, HR directors, and senior advisors—were selected through purposeful sampling, ensuring they had a minimum of 10 years of experience in human resource functions and demonstrable engagement with ethical issues in organizational settings. Additional participants were identified via snowball sampling, which leveraged the professional networks of initial informants to include others with specialized knowledge. These participants contributed nuanced perspectives on ethical decision-making, policy enforcement, and institutional challenges.

In the quantitative phase, the emphasis shifted toward generalizability and representativeness. The sample size of 256 staff members was determined through power analysis via Morgan’s table, accounting for the University of Medical Sciences’ employee population while ensuring adequate representation across all professional levels. The participants were selected through cluster sampling from three strata: executive managers (17%), technical/administrative specialists (28%), and general employees (65%), which proportionally reflected the institution’s organizational structure. This approach enables robust statistical analysis across professional categories while maintaining methodological rigor. The participants responded to a standardized questionnaire designed based on qualitative insights, providing quantifiable data suitable for confirmatory analysis and theoretical model validation.

Data collection tool and technique

The study employed a mixed-methods approach for data collection. In the qualitative phase, semi-structured in-depth interviews with experts were conducted. The data collection process followed established qualitative methods, including the selection of participants, contacting and establishing communication, purposeful sampling, choosing the data collection approach (literature review and interviews), recording data (audio recording and note-taking), resolving difficulties, and storing, organizing, and analyzing data. In the quantitative phase, a researcher-made questionnaire (with confirmed validity) was used. The questionnaire was designed based on the indicators, components, influencing factors, core factors, and consequences of implementing ethical justice identified during the qualitative interviews. The questions were developed via the paradigmatic model of grounded theory, and responses were measured using a five-point Likert scale.

Following institutional review, additional ethical clearance was obtained from Qom University of Medical Sciences, with all necessary permissions secured from relevant authorities. Statistical analysis method

Part 1: Qualitative analysis

The qualitative phase focused on exploring the paradigmatic model related to ethical justice, identifying causal factors, contextual factors, intervening factors, strategies, and expected outcomes. The data were analyzed via theoretical coding methods (open, axial, and selective coding). After each interview, the audio file was transcribed, and the text was reviewed multiple times. Meaningful units were identified, purified, and grouped into higher-level categories using inductive and reductive methods. Rigor was ensured through Lincoln and Guba’s (1985) criteria: credibility (prolonged engagement, triangulation, and member checking), transferability (thick description and purposive sampling), dependability (audit trail and reflexivity), and confirmability (triangulation and audit trail).

Part 2: Quantitative analysis

The quantitative phase employed SEM to analyze relationships between variables. Based on factor loadings and fit indices, the relationships between factors identified in the qualitative phase were tested. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to ensure validity, and reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α =0.8). Data analysis involved SEM and path analysis using AMOS softwarever 2024.

AI policy

This study adheres to ethical guidelines regarding the responsible use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools in academic research. AI tools, including large language models such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT, were utilized exclusively to assist with language refinement, formatting, and conceptual clarification. No AI-generated content was used as a substitute for original data collection, critical analysis, or scholarly interpretation. All AI assistance was supervised by the primary researcher, ensuring that the intellectual integrity and academic rigor of the work were maintained.

Results

Phase one

In this study, 14 experts were interviewed in the qualitative section. The participants were distributed as follows: 60% were from the deputy group, 20% were managers, and 20% were specialists. In terms of work experience, 20% had 5–10 years of experience, 50% had 10–15 years of experience, and 20% had over 15 years of experience. The analysis confirmed key components of ethical justice, including causal factors (ethical leadership, employee training and awareness, fair service compensation systems, and transparent processes), contextual factors (organizational culture, maturity, moral values, and ethical commitment of employees), intervening factors (conflict of interest, organizational corruption, and monitoring systems), strategies (ethical leadership development, education initiatives, and transparent access to information), and expected outcomes (increased transparency, equitable resource distribution, and improved organizational processes). The findings were validated through member checking and triangulation, ensuring the credibility and trustworthiness of the results. The qualitative phase provided a comprehensive framework for understanding ethical justice, which informed the development of the quantitative phase.

Phase two

The results of the study are presented in three main sections: (1) assessment of data normality, (2) goodness-of-fit indices for the measurement and structural models, and (3) evaluation of direct relationships in the structural model. Each section is supported by detailed statistical analysis and findings.

Discussion

This study aimed to develop and validate a comprehensive model of moral justice tailored specifically for medical universities. The proposed conceptual framework integrates causal factors, background conditions, intervening variables, strategies, and expected outcomes, offering a multidimensional perspective on how moral justice can be systematically embedded into organizational structures within academic health institutions.

The causal factors identified—ethical leadership, employee training and awareness, and transparent processes—are foundational to promoting moral justice. These findings align with previous research indicating that ethical leadership sets the ethical tone at the top and directly influences employees’ attitudes and behaviors.[915] Ethical leaders serve as role models by consistently demonstrating fairness, accountability, and integrity, which in turn fosters a culture of trust and ethical compliance.[34] Brown et al. (2005),[35] drawing on social learning theory, argue that ethical leaders influence followers through observational learning, reinforcing ethical behavior across the organization. These findings support our finding that ethical leadership plays a pivotal role in shaping organizational morality.

Transparent systems and accessible reporting mechanisms were also found to be crucial in enabling employees to voice concerns without fear of retaliation. Research by Obiekwe[36] and colleagues (2019) emphasizes that organizations with open communication channels and clear grievance procedures experience fewer ethical violations and higher levels of employee satisfaction. Similarly, agile feedback loops ensure that ethical issues are addressed promptly, reinforcing the perception of procedural justice.[35]

The background factors—organizational culture, maturity level, and moral values—were identified as essential contextual enablers. A strong ethical culture, rooted in shared values such as respect, equity, and transparency, provides fertile ground for moral justice to thrive. Studies have shown that organizations with robust ethical climates report higher levels of employee engagement and lower turnover.[51819] Moreover, organizational citizenship behaviors—where employees voluntarily contribute beyond formal job requirements—are more prevalent in environments where ethical values are clearly articulated and practiced.[3036] These findings support our conclusion that a mature, ethically grounded culture is indispensable for sustaining moral justice initiatives.

Among the intervening factors, ethical leadership development programs, policy monitoring systems, and equitable resource distribution emerged as critical facilitators of implementation. Leadership development initiatives—especially those integrating ethics into formal training—have been shown to improve decision-making in complex healthcare settings.[37] In addition, regular evaluation of policies ensures alignment with ethical standards and allows for timely course correction. Equitable resource allocation was another key factor, which is consistent with studies showing that perceived fairness in access to opportunities and rewards significantly impacts employee motivation and organizational commitment.[62223]

The strategies proposed—justice rights and opportunities, increased employee participation, and increased accountability—serve as actionable levers for change. Promoting fairness in decision-making and resource allocation helps reduce perceptions of bias and injustice.[242526] Employee involvement in decision-making not only increases ownership and commitment but also enhances the legitimacy of decisions made.[38] This is corroborated by recent studies emphasizing participatory governance as a cornerstone of ethical public administration.

The expected outcomes—including ethical justice in management, processes, and resource distribution—highlight the transformative potential of this model. When implemented effectively, these outcomes lead to greater organizational accountability, transparency, and fairness. For instance, ethical justice in management ensures that leaders make decisions based on principles rather than personal interest, whereas ethical justice in operational procedures guarantees the unbiased implementation of rules and policies.[27] These results align with global benchmarks for ethical governance in the health and education sectors, particularly those promoted by the World Health Organization (WHO) and national regulatory bodies.[39]

Collectively, the proposed model underscores the interconnectedness of leadership, culture, policies, and strategic interventions. Ethical leadership establishes the moral direction, organizational culture provides the enabling environment, and targeted strategies translate values into action. By addressing all these dimensions, organizations—particularly medical universities—can create a sustainable ecosystem for moral justice that supports both internal integrity and external accountability.

This holistic approach not only contributes to the theoretical understanding of moral justice in complex organizations but also offers practical guidance for institutional leaders and policymakers aiming to strengthen ethical governance in healthcare and higher education settings.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive model of moral justice that integrates multiple dimensions—causal factors, background factors, intervening factors, strategies, and expected outcomes—to promote ethical principles and justice within organizations, particularly in medical universities. The findings highlight the critical role of ethical leadership, transparent processes, and employee training in fostering a culture of integrity and accountability. Additionally, organizational culture, moral values, and equitable resource distribution create a supportive environment for ethical practices, while strategies such as increasing participation and enhancing accountability drive actionable change. The expected outcomes, including transparency, fairness, and increased organizational accountability, underscore the transformative potential of systematically implementing moral justice.

The findings suggest that embedding moral justice into organizational frameworks can significantly enhance transparency, employee engagement, and institutional trust—benchmarks emphasized by international health policy frameworks such as those of the WHO and national health regulatory bodies. Specifically, strategies such as participatory decision-making and enhanced accountability support the implementation of ethical policies that uphold justice and human rights in workplace environments.

Moreover, the model provides a practical roadmap for policymakers and university administrators to strengthen internal policies related to staff management, ethics training, and resource allocation. By integrating these principles into institutional policies, medical universities can better align with health sector reforms aimed at promoting social accountability, patient-centered care, and responsible leadership.

Future studies should explore the role of technology in enhancing transparency, accountability, and ethical decision-making within organizations. As digital tools and platforms become increasingly integral to organizational operations, understanding how they can support moral justice initiatives will be critical for adapting this framework to modern workplaces.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a robust framework for understanding and implementing moral justice within medical universities. Among its strengths, the research integrates causal, background, and intervening factors through a structured modeling approach, ensuring a comprehensive exploration of moral justice. The use of a validated researcher-designed questionnaire, combined with SEM, enhances the reliability and generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the study identifies actionable strategies, such as ethical leadership and transparency, offering practical recommendations for improving organizational performance and employee satisfaction.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study’s focus on a single institution (Qom Medical University) may limit the generalizability of the results to other contexts. Second, reliance on self-reported data introduces the potential for response bias. Third, while the model incorporates key internal factors, it does not fully capture all dimensions of moral justice, particularly the role of external stakeholders such as patients, regulatory bodies, and the broader community. For instance, patient feedback, regulatory compliance, and community trust—critical elements shaping ethical practices—were not explicitly addressed. Future research should incorporate these perspectives to provide a more holistic understanding of moral justice.

Additionally, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to draw causal inferences over time. A longitudinal approach could offer deeper insights into how moral justice evolves within organizations. Furthermore, while the study effectively identifies factors and strategies, it lacks qualitative data that could enrich the understanding of employees’ experiences and perceptions of moral justice. Addressing these limitations in future research could further validate and refine the proposed model.

Implications

The SEM results indicate that ethical leadership is significantly linked to both moral justice and organizational integrity in medical universities. These findings suggest that fostering ethical leadership can drive institutional improvements in culture and ethical climate. To embed moral justice into medical education, we recommend a mandatory course—Moral Justice in Medicine —integrating bioethics, distributive justice, and patient advocacy. Case-based learning should be used to help students apply ethical principles to real-world dilemmas, with reflective essays and simulated patient encounters serving as assessment tools for ethical reasoning. Faculty development programs, particularly annual ethics training workshops, are essential to align teaching practices with SEM-derived constructs. A minimum of 10 h of annual ethics education per faculty member is recommended to maintain engagement with ethical leadership standards. Extracurricular initiatives—such as student-led ethics committees and community health partnerships—can further promote civic responsibility and health equity.

Universities should support these efforts through resource allocation and incentivizing student participation via elective credits or scholarships. For long-term impact, medical institutions must adopt policies mandating SEM-informed curricula. Accreditation bodies like the Liaison Committee on Medical Education (LCME) should incorporate moral justice competencies into their standards. Funding should prioritize faculty training and research grants to refine educational interventions. Institutional ethics boards and annual public reporting will ensure accountability. Universities should also invest in community partnerships addressing health disparities, reinforcing the practical application of moral justice. Supported by stakeholder engagement and SEM-based evaluation, these reforms will align medical education with the values of justice and equity, producing graduates capable of navigating complex ethical challenges and promoting fair, patient-centered care.
