Citrobacter youngae nosocomial bacteremia from postoperative pyogenic spondylodiscitis after a minimally invasive spinal surgery
Authors: Nguyen Sy Thau, Do Van Dong, Pham Thi Thanh Nhan, Nguyen Van Tuan, Vu Viet Sang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCitrobacter youngae (C. youngae) was first described in 1993, and data suggesting that human diseases caused by this bacterium remain scarce. Reports on C. youngae infections mostly described local infections without evidenced bacteremia, especially in immunocompromised patients. Herein, we report a rare case of bacteremia with intervertebral disc and adjacent vertebral infection due to C. youngae (detected by the Vitek® 2 Compact). A 70-year-old man with chronic lower back pain due to lumbar disc herniation underwent microdiscectomy, a minimally invasive spinal surgery. On postoperative day 4, he suffered shaking chills with worsened lower back pain compared to that before the operation, and vancomycin plus ceftriaxone were empirically administered. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine revealed osteomyelitis at L4 and L5 with L4/L5 discitis. Two blood culture sets were positive with the same pathogen, possibly C. youngae; The patient was then treated successfully with ceftazidime plus ciprofloxacin, the antibiotics were selected based on antibiotic susceptibility test results. This report suggests that C. youngae may cause severe infections in immunocompetent patients, and further surveillance should be considered to monitor antibiotic-resistant bacteria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10326-y.Keywords: Bacteremia, Spondylodiscitis, Citrobacter, Nosocomial infection, Antimicrobial resistance

Background

Citrobacter spp. is a gram-negative coliform that belongs to the Enterobacteriaceae family. The members of the genus Citrobacter are called for their ability to use citrate as their sole carbon source. These bacteria can be isolated from the natural environment and human intestine and are considered an opportunistic nosocomial pathogen infecting humans [1]. The urinary tract infection and neonatal meningitis are the most frequent conditions caused by Citrobacter spp [1, 2]. Invasive procedures are thought to be associated with Citrobacter bacteremia [3]. In 1993, Don and his colleagues used the DNA hybridization method to regroup the genus Citrobacter from three previously recognized species into eleven species. The report also proved that these novel species of the genus Citrobacter could be separated by their biochemical profiles. Citrobacter youngae belongs to genomospecies 5 with identically biochemical features, such as negative for indole and ornithine decarboxylase, and delayed positive for citrate and arginine dihydrolase. Among the Citrobacter spp., C. freundii and C. koseri are the predominant pathogens while C. youngae infections are scarce with intestinal- and genitourinary tracts being the major sources. One case of C. youngae peritonitis in immunocompromised patients has been reported, however, C. youngae was a co-pathogen causing a localized infection without bacteremia [4]. To date, a unique case with C. youngae bacteremia has been mentioned, however, the clinical manifestations could not be withdrawn from the cohort’s description [5]. Postoperative infection following spine surgery can be a devastating complication, the incidence depends on the type and approach of spine surgery [6]. Minimally invasive spinal surgery (including microdiscectomy) can minimize such infections, for instance, the postoperative infection rate in a simple lumbar decompression or microdiscectomy (~ 0.6–3%) was lower than that of an instrumented fusion (~ 6–18%) [6, 7]. Therefore, C. youngae bacteremia after a minimally invasive spine surgery should be of interest.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old retired surgeon underwent a non-urgent L4/L5 microdiscectomy at a 1,200-bed provincial hospital. He received 2 g IV cefazolin as antimicrobial prophylaxis without repeat intraoperative dosing since the operation was completed within two hours. His medical history was unremarkable with 20 years of chronic lower back pain due to lumbar disc herniation that was progressively worsening over three months before the operation. He also had a stable benign prostatic hyperplasia for the last four years. He refused to experience any medical condition or therapy that could be related to immunosuppression. Four days following the surgery, he developed a high fever with shivering and fatigue; his lower back pain was worse than it had been before the procedure. A blood culture was immediately performed and followed by the administration of intravenous vancomycin plus ceftriaxone as an empirical therapy. On day four of fever (8th postoperative day), the blood culture revealed C. youngae which was resistant to ceftriaxone (MIC ≥ 64) and susceptible to ciprofloxacin (MIC = 1), ceftazidime (MIC = 4), fourth-generation cephalosporin, gentamycin, and carbapenem (Supplementary Table S1). The tissue specimen from the surgical site could not sampled for culture and there were no documented signs of other local infection. He was transferred to 108 Military Central Hospital (108 MCH), a tertiary hospital, due to unresolved fever after four days of antibiotic treatment. On admission, an assessment of his vital signs divulged a body temperature of 38.50C, pulse of 82 beats per minute, blood pressure of 135/80 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation of 100% with a breath rate of 18 breaths per minute. His lower back including a small incision revealed no signs of inflammation except persistent pain. He was conscious and had no headache, nausea, or vomiting, no focal neurologic deficit, and no stiff neck. Laboratory findings showed a normal peripheral blood leukocyte (8930 cells per cubic millimeter) with 79.3% neutrophils (reference range, 40 to 74). Serum procalcitonin was 0.502 ng/ml (reference range, 0-0.05). The residue of the complete blood count, the comprehensive metabolic panel including liver- and kidney function tests, and urinalysis were within normal limits. Computed tomography (CT) of the lumbar region revealed fluid accumulated around the L4/L5 intervertebral disc space that was partly removed during his surgery and left psoas. Intravenous ciprofloxacin (400 mg q12h) plus ceftazidime (2 g q8h) was administered immediately after collecting blood samples for culture. Citrobacter youngae was again isolated from his blood with the same susceptibility test result while no pathogen was isolated from the urine sample. His fever was weaned and blood cultures were negative from the fourth day following this regimen, however, his lower back pain had little improvement. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar on postoperative day 14 showed osteomyelitis at L4 and L5 along with L4/L5 discitis (Fig. 1). The intravenous antibiotics regimen with ciprofloxacin and ceftazidime was then continued till completing a two-week course. He was discharged after 14 days of hospitalization (serum procalcitonin decreased to 0.115 ng/ml) and was advised to complete four weeks of oral ciprofloxacin. The patient was monitored for one more month and no episode of fever or worsened back pain was recorded.

Discussions and conclusions

We reported a rare case of nosocomial bloodstream infection from postoperative pyogenic spondylodiscitis following minimally invasive spinal surgery, the causative pathogen was possibly C. youngae detected by the Vitek® 2 Compact. The bacteremia evidentially persisted for at least four days with inappropriate empirical antibiotic therapy until the antibiogram was accessible.

Human infections, particularly bloodstream infections, due to C. youngae have been exceeding rare worldwide. In 1994 (a year after reclassification), Janda et al. revealed that C. youngae was isolated mostly from feces and genitourinary tract, rarely from blood, clinically [5]. None of the 205 Citrobacter spp. infection cases were C. youngae at an Indian hospital in 2004 [8]. In a Greek hospital (2010–2015), C. youngae was isolated from only 6/385 clinical samples. Moreover, C. youngae bloodstream infection might be scant while only 4.4% of these samples were blood [9]. A unique case out of forty-three Citrobacter spp. bacteremia was C. youngae at a South Korean hospital (2007–2017). Nevertheless, the clinical manifestations of this C. youngae bacteremia patient could not be withdrawn from the cohort’s description [10]. To date, a sole case report of C. youngae peritonitis in an immunocompromised patient with peritoneal dialysis and diabetes mellitus has been published. Nevertheless, this is a localized infection without bacteremia and C. youngae is a co-pathogen with Pantoea agglomerans [4]. Here we describe a case of bacteremia due to C. youngae, immunodeficiency which was usually seen in other Citrobacter spp. infections were not recorded in this patient. Despite the confirmation of blood culture (positive with C. youngae at two-time points, two hospitals, both used the Vitek® 2 Compact (Biomerieux-France) with very good identification at 95% probability (Supplementary Table S2)), an indication of local infection was not presented at the lumbar area where the operation was performed, hence the tissue was not collected for further microbiological tests. In addition, the species-level identification was based solely on the Vitek® 2 Compact, which is the same as previous clinical reports on C. youngae [4, 10], could be a limitation. The identical antibiograms from two hospitals showed that this C. youngae is resistant to first-, second-, and third-generation cephalosporin except for ceftazidime. Nevertheless, Citrobacter spp. has been recently accounted as a growing threat to public health mostly due to an increasing resistance to antibiotics including carbapenem [1]. The strain of C. youngae resists imipenem by receiving carbapenemase genes from other Enterobacteriaceae has been reported, thus posing a concern about the management of this pathogen [11].

Citrobacter spp. has been reported as a scant pathogen causing infection following spinal surgeries [12]. One of the most common causes of postoperative pyogenic spondylodiscitis is Staphylococcus aureus. Other pathogenic bacteria include Streptococcus spp. and gram-negative bacilli [13]. The term pyogenic spondylodiscitis refers to the infection of the disc(s) and/or adjacent vertebral [13]. The patient suffered from shaking chills and progressive lower back pain that started on postoperative day 4 of L4/L5 microdiscectomy suggesting the involvement of surgical site infection. A lumbar spinal MRI on day 14 after the surgery suggested pyogenic spondylodiscitis including the infection of L4 and L5 vertebral column and L4/L5 intervertebral disc space. Causative bacteria may reach this region via three basic routes as follows: (i) hematogenous spread from a distant site or focus of infection; (ii) contiguous spread from adjacent soft tissue infection; (iii) and direct inoculate from trauma, invasive spinal diagnostic procedure, or spinal surgery. The latter is the most appropriate for the present case since Citrobacter bacteremia was mostly disseminated from certain sources of infection [10], and his surgical site as a solely local infection was documented.

The nosocomial infections due to genus Citrobacter have been increasing worldwide, nevertheless, surgical site infections following spinal surgeries, particularly minimally invasive procedures are rarely caused by these pathogens [1, 14]. Report on the rare pathogen causing such avoidable infections does not attempt to broaden antimicrobial prophylaxis which is recommended to be active against the pathogens most likely to contaminate the surgical site. In contrast, other perioperative infection control interventions, such as hand and equipment hygiene, are more appropriate for consideration. Perspective on the possible source of the pathogen, for instance, Citrobacter spp. is usually isolated from soil, water, food, and human or animal intestines would be advantageous to address which perioperative infection control steps should be improved. Among those suspected or proven to have surgical site infection, more aggressive approaches, such as proper blood culture followed by administration of treatment antibiotics were recommended for patients with systemic signs of infection [13, 15]. Nevertheless, further researches that unravel systemic symptoms specific for disseminated infection upon surgical site infection rather than the acute response phase during the postoperative period is a prerequisite for avoiding blood culture and antibiotic overuse. For instance, shaking chills as in the present case have been reported as a specific predictor of bacteremia, it could be further evaluated in subgroups when considering the original infection [16]. Additionally, active surveillance for the antimicrobial susceptibility profile of Citrobacter spp. in each hospital setting is dispensable for guiding antibiotic therapy, concerning their increased antimicrobial resistance.

In summary, Citrobacter youngae is a rare enteric pathogen that causes nosocomial postoperative pyogenic spondylodiscitis and bacteremia. Empirical antibiotic regimens for treating surgical site infection after minimally invasive spinal surgery in certain circumstances should consider this Cefotaxime- and ceftriaxone-resistant gram-negative pathogen.
