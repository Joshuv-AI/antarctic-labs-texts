Biomechanical analysis of anatomical intramedullary nails and proximal femoral anti-rotation-II intramedullary nails for the treatment of unstable intertrochanteric fractures (AO type 31-A2.2) in older patients
Authors: Xian-wen Ma, Jian Chen, Yong Hu, Zheng-rui Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore the biomechanical properties of Zimmer natural nail (ZNN) and proximal femoral nail antirotation (PFNA)-II nails in treating older patients with unstable intertrochanteric fractures, thereby guiding the selection of internal fixation devices in clinical practice.MethodZNN and PFNA fixation models for unstable intertrochanteric fractures (AO type 31-A2.2) were established using the three-dimensional finite element method and in vitro mechanical experiments, respectively. We compared the Von Mises stress distribution, peak stress, implants and femur locations, displacement distribution, maximum displacement, location of the models, and maximum crack distance at the fracture ends between the two groups. Additionally, we compared the axial stiffness, femoral head axial displacement, and failure load for both groups.ResultFinite element analysis for AO type 31-A2.2 unstable intertrochanteric fractures showed that stresses in the ZNN and PFNA-II models concentrated at the distal locking screws, with maximum Von Mises stresses of 373.2 MPa and 811.5 MPa, respectively. The femoral stress concentrated at the distal locking screw perforation hole, with maximum Von Mises stresses of 186.5 MPa for ZNN and 1602.0 MPa for PFNA-II. According to the displacement distribution cloud map, the maximum displacements of the ZNN and PFNA-II models were 3.768 mm and 3.757 mm, respectively, both at the top of the femoral head. The maximum crack distances in the ZNN and PFNA-II models were 0.16 mm and 0.14 mm, respectively. The maximum displacements and crack distances at the fracture end were similar for both models. Biomechanical tests revealed that the axial stiffness in the ZNN group was greater than that in the PFNA-II group, with averages of 299.67 ± 27.00 N/mm and 269. 6 ± 15. 24 N/mm, respectively; the difference between the two groups was significant (T = 2. 375, P = 0. 039). In the cyclic loading test, the average axial displacement of the femoral head was 0.62 ± 0.22 mm in the ZNN group and 0.59 ± 0.23 mm in the PFNA-II group, with no significant differences (t = 0. 155, P = 0. 880). The failure load of the ZNN group significantly exceeded that of the PFNA-II group, with average failure loads of 3.95 ± 0.07 kN and 3.54 ± 0. 15 kN, respectively (t = 6.207, P < 0.001).ConclusionIn AO type 31-A2.2 unstable intertrochanteric fractures, the biomechanical properties of the ZNN group were superior to those of the PFNA-II group in terms of axial stiffness and failure load. In the cyclic loading test, the axial displacement of the femoral head was similar in both groups. The finite element analysis showed a more favorable mechanical distribution for the ZNN group. The ZNN implant demonstrated favorable biomechanical characteristics under the specific experimental conditions tested in this study.Keywords: Intertrochanteric femoral fracture, Internal fixation within fracture line

Introduction

Intertrochanteric femoral fractures are common in older patients, accounting for one-third to one-half of all hip fractures and can cause severe functional disorders [1–4]. With the increase of elderly population, hip fractures have emerged as a serious health problem, making them a leading cause of hospitalisation in this demographic [5–7]. Annually, 4.5 million people worldwide become disabled from hip fractures, which rank among the top 10 causes of disability [8]. Early surgical intervention is crucial for early recovery of limb function and reduction of postoperative complications, making it the best treatment option for these fractures, especially when combined with orthogeriatric co-management, which provides better outcomes for patients with multimorbidity by reducing the risk of adverse events [9, 10].

Although intertrochanteric fractures in the elderly present significant management challenges, surgical interventions, including extramedullary and intramedullary fixation, remain the primary treatment methods [11]. However, multiple meta-analyses indicate that, compared with extramedullary fixation, intramedullary fixation reduces the risk of implant failure and reoperation while improving functional outcomes [12, 13]. The proximal femoral nail anti-rotation (PFNA) is an intramedullary fixation device using helical blades, which offers closer bone compaction and fracture end alignment, compared with traditional screws, providing the best fixation and stability for intertrochanteric fractures [14, 15].

The optimal internal fixation method for unstable intertrochanteric fractures in older patients remains controversial. The Asian PFNA-II (Depuy Synth, USA) was designed specifically for Asian patients. It was produced based on some improvements to the standard PFNA. For example, the proximal diameter of the main nail is reduced from 17 to 16.5 mm, the diameter of the helical blade is reduced from 10.55 to 10.3 mm, and the outward inclination of the main nail is reduced from 6° to 5° [16]. Li et al. [17] compared PFNA-II and PFNA through a retrospective study. The operation time, intraoperative blood loss, total fluoroscopy time, blood transfusion volume, postoperative drainage volume, length of hospital stay, and occurrence of postoperative complications were recorded for both groups. The study found that PFNA-II aligns better with the anatomical structure of the proximal femur in Chinese patients, resulting in lower complication rates and shorter operation times in treating unstable femoral intertrochanteric fractures, compared with PFNA [18]. Comparisons of the efficacy of the InterTan nail and PFNA-II in treating unstable femoral intertrochanteric fractures showed no significant differences in terms of general complications, local complications, walking ability, Harris hip score, or hip joint range of motion at follow-up. When encountering patients with a larger radius of the anterior femoral arch, problems such as mismatch with the anatomical configuration of the proximal femur, postoperative proximal thigh pain, and stress fractures at the distal end of the main nail may occur because the short PFNA-II is a straight nail [16, 19]. The newly developed anatomical nail, the Zimmer natural nail (ZNN; Zimmer, Germany), considers the anatomical and morphological structures of the human proximal femur. The short nail has a 1275-mm anterior arch radius, a 15° forward inclination angle, and a neck-shaft angle (125°/130°). There have been relevant reports, and ZNN has achieved good clinical results [20, 21].

However, there is limited data on the biomechanical comparison between ZNN and PFNA-II. Regarding internal fixation, it is unclear which method has better mechanical stability. Surgical treatment of unstable intertrochanteric fractures (AO type 31-A2.2) has a high incidence of complications and failure rates. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the biomechanical properties of ZNN and PFNA-II in treating unstable intertrochanteric fractures (AO type 31-A2.2) using finite element analysis (FEA) and biomechanical studies to provide insights for intraoperative internal fixation selection.

Materials and methods

Finite element analysis

Three-dimensional model of the unstable intertrochanteric fracture.

Three-dimensional model of the unstable intertrochanteric fracture.

The left femur of a 67-year-old male with a height of 169 cm and a weight of 70 kg and no signs of femoral diseases or deformities was used for the geometric model of the femur. Then, the three dimensional model of human femur was reconstructed from two dimensional CT image using Mimics 17.0 software (Materialise, Belgium). The fracture model (AO classification 31-A2.2) was performed at a 40° angle to the femoral axis [22], completely remove the small trochanter and the calcar support (Fig. 1).

Building the unstable intertrochanter fracture model (AO/OTA type 31-A2.2)

Three-dimensional model of the implant.

According to the geometrical dimensions of the implant manufacturer’s catalog, we used Solidworks 2012 software (Dassault, France) to establish the three-dimensional models of ZNN (Length: 180 mm, Diameter: 10 mm) and PFNA-II (Length: 170 mm, Diameter: 10 mm). The implants were positioned separately as recommended by the manufacturer with 31-A2.2 fracture model (Fig. 2).

Establishment of geometric model of unstable intertrochanter fracture (AO/OTA 31-A2.2) fixed by ZNN, PFNA-II (A: 17.52 mm in ZNN model, B: 19.33 mm in PFNA-II model). The TAD differences between the two groups might be caused by the three-dimensional spatial measurement of virtual positioning

The entry point between the two implants were created slightly medial to the tip of the greater trochanter and anterior portion of the middle third of the trochanter. The tip-apex distance(TAD) of both groups was controlled by virtual positioning, the implant tip was placed within 10 mm of the femoral head center in all simulations (mimicking clinical TAD < 20 mm) [23].

Development of the finite element model and Convergence test.

The solid model was then imported into software Hypermesh 13.0 (Altair, USA) to generate an finite element mesh. The models were meshed using 4-node tetrahedral solid elements, and the convergence tests were performed by different sizes. This study tested tetrahedral meshes with average element lengths ranging from 0.2 to 3.5 mm. The convergence analysis revealed that the variation in maximum equivalent stress within the 0.7–1.0 mm mesh range remained within acceptable limits (less than 5%). This indicates that, within this mesh density range, the results became insensitive to mesh size, meeting convergence requirements. Based on these findings and considering computational efficiency, we ultimately selected 1 mm as the optimal mesh size for bone tissue in the model. The specific 3-D finite element models were shown in Fig. 3. The statistics of three assembly number of nodes and elements were as follows (Table 1). Calculations were done in this study using the FE analysis software Abaqus 6.14 (Dassault, USA).

Model Validation.

To validate the FE model, we reconstructed an intact femur and performed a comparative analysis with published experimental data [27]. A vertical load of 1500 N was applied to the femoral head, yielding an axial stiffness of 0.54 kN/mm in our computation, which falls within the reported measurement range (0.76 ± 0.26 kN/mm) [27]. Given individual variations, the model demonstrated satisfactory validation.

Boundary conditions, loading force settings and contact surfaces.

For the boundary conditions, the distal end of the femur was fixed in all directions. To mimic the stress acting on the hip wi thin the stance phase of walking [28], each assembly model was abducted 10°, tilted backward by10° [18], and a downward vertical force of 2000 N was applied to the femoral head [29] (Fig. 4). It was assumed that frictional contact interactions existed between different parts of the model. The friction coefficient at the fracture surface was set at 0.46, 0.3 at the bone-implant interaction [30], and 0.2 at the implant-implant interaction [31].

Biomechanical test

Sawbones femurs (Model 3403, 12 femurs, Sawbones Company, USA) are medium fourth-generation artificial synthetic femur models. The cortical bone material of this model is short fiber filled epoxy resin, while the cancellous bone material is solid rigid polyurethane foam. Its structural design closely replicates that of the human skeleton, with a femoral neck-shaft angle of 135 degrees, aligning well with the anatomical features of the thigh and femur in Asian populations. Consequently, this model is frequently employed in biomechanical testing and research. They were randomly divided into two groups: 6 in the anatomic-al intramedullary nail (ZNN) group and 6 in the proximal femoral nail (PFNA-II) group.

Modeling unstable intertrochanteric fractures (AO/OTA 31-A2.2): CT data of Sawbones femurs were imported into Mimics 17.0 to establish a three-dimensional geometric model of the Sawbones femur through threshold segmentation and region growth. In Mimics 17.0 software, an unstable intertrochanteric fracture model, conforming to Müller AO classification 31-A2.2, was established using a virtual osteotomy. Intertrochanteric osteotomy was performed at a 40° angle to the femoral axis, and the small trochanter and all medial supports were removed (Fig. 5) [32]. An osteotomy guide plate was fabricated and printed to create an unstable intertrochanteric fracture model(Fig. 5) (AO classification 31-A2.2).

In Mimics 17.0 software, virtual osteotomy is performed and a osteotomy guide is prepared A. Print the osteotomy guide plate and make the unstable intertrochanteric fracture model (B: AO/OTA 31-A2.2)

The unstable intertrochanteric fracture model (AO/OTA 31-A2.2) fixed with intramedullary nails was fabricated (Fig. 6). The entry point between the two implants were created slightly medial to the tip of the greater trochanter and anterior portion of the middle third of the trochanter. Fluoroscopy was used to aid insertion of the implant and to ensure optimal starting point. A TAD less than 20 mm was confrmed at anteroposterior and axial radiographs in all specimens. After internal fixation implantation, prior to the biomechanical test, the femur was internally rotated by 10° and tilted backward by 10°. The proximal femur was clamped, and the distal end was placed in a mold to simulate the hip force during walking. The femoral condyle was embedded with a mixture of orthodontic plaster (self-curing) and denture base resin (self-curing) in a 1:2 ratio, 2 cm above the femoral condyle, ensuring consistent angles for all specimens. Biomechanical testing was performed 1 day after embedding and fixation.

The unstable intertrochanteric fracture model (AO/OTA 31-A2.2) fixed with intramedullary nails (A: ZNN model, B: PFNA-II model)

Preloading: All specimens were preloaded before biomechanical tests, with a loading rate of 2 Hz and loading force of 400 N for 10 cycles, to eliminate creep and ensure close contact between the specimens and the biomechanical testing machine. The specimens were connected and fixed to a biomechanical testing machine using compression fixtures. A polymethylene mold was placed on the femoral head to simulate the effect of the acetabulum while ensuring uniform force distribution on the specimens during axial compression.

Axial compression test: After preloading, an axial load was applied at 10 mm/min until reaching 2000 N (Fig. 10A). A displacement-load curve was obtained, and the axial stiffness was calculated.

Mechanical loading setup for measurement of axial stiffness and axial load to failure A. Mode of failure: fracture line at the distal interlock (B: ZNN model) and the helical blade cut out (C: PFNA-II model).

Cyclic loading test: The loading rate was 2 Hz, the loading force was 1400 N for 10,000 cycles, and the axial displacement of the femoral head was recorded.

Axial compression failure test: A loading rate of 10 mm/s was applied until fixation failure, and the experiment was stopped. When the downward displacement of the head was > 15 mm, the head nail was cut out, causing fracture lines at the distal interlock, indicating fixation failure [32].

Observation indicators

The Von Mises stress distribution, peak value, and location of the plants and femurs within the two model groups were analyzed. Additionally, the displacement distribution, maximum displacement, and location of the models were analyzed, and the maximum crack distance at the fracture ends of the two model groups was measured. The axial stiffness, axial displacement of the femoral head, failure load, and internal fixation failure modes of the two groups were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 20.0 software. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, mechanical parameters were compared using Student’s t-test, with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for key outcomes (e.g., axial stiffness, failure load). Non-normally distributed data were analyzed with appropriate non-parametric alternatives (e.g., Mann-Whitney U test), with results reported as median [interquartile range]. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for all comparisons. All tests were performed under uniform conditions.

Results

FEA testing.

The Von Mises stress distribution, peak, and location of the plants within the two model groups indicated differences in the stress distribution of the two implants. In the ZNN and PFNA-II groups, stress was concentrated at the distal locking screw, with maximum stresses of 373.2 MPa and 811.5 MPa, respectively. Compared with the two implants, the ZNN implant bore less stress when fixing unstable intertrochanteric fractures (Fig. 7; Table 3).

Discussion

Reviews in the Cochrane Database indicate that intramedullary nails are recommended for treating unstable intertrochanteric fractures [33]. Owing to their theoretical and biological advantages over extramedullary fixation, such as decreased soft tissue damage and the ability to permit early weight bearing, the use of intramedullary fixation has recently increased [34, 35]. However, complications related to intramedullary nail technology can be as high as 20.5%, particularly in unstable fractures [36].

The most common complications associated with intramedullary nails are varus collapse and femoral head screw cut-outs. Mercan et al., 44 patients were in the PFNA group, cut-out occurred in 7 patients, leading to reoperation with endoprosthesis [37]. A biomechanical experiment found that the displacement of intramedullary nails in the reversed intertrochanteric fracture model is affected by the intramedullary nail diameter and fracture type [38]. The degree of displacement of the intramedullary nail is inversely proportional to its diameter and directly proportional to the instability of the fracture. Thigh pain after intramedullary fixation of femoral intertrochanteric fractures affects the quality of life of patients. Screw fixation failure, femoral shaft fracture, and other complications related to implantation failure can result in long-term pain [39], potentially due to the straight designs of PFNA/PFNA-II, Gamma 3, and InterTan short nails without an anterior arch, causing femoral complications, such as anterior cortical impingement, perforation, or fracture [19, 40].

This study used biomechanical tests to assess the biomechanical characteristics of ZNN and PFNA-II in the treatment of unstable intertrochanteric fractures of the femur (AO/OTA type 31-A2.2). Sawbone can better simulate human bone and has more homogeneous and stable mechanical properties than cadaver bone [41, 42]. Sawbone was used in this study in the place of cadaver bone for mechanical tests of internal fixation materials.

Using FEA, an important mechanical evaluation tool in biomechanical research, a previous study indicated that the combined compression-locking intramedullary nail (InterTan) provided greater stability and resistance to femoral head rotation and varus collapse than did the single-tension screw device (Gamma 3) [22]. In this study, a three-dimensional finite element model of ZNN and PFNA-II internal fixation for treating unstable intertrochanteric fractures of the femur (AO31–A2.2) was developed, and the biomechanical characteristics of the two fixation methods were compared. Under the same load, the two model groups produced different stress and displacement distributions; however, the maximum displacements were similar, both located at the top of the femoral head. The FEA results show a concerningly high stress concentration in the femur with PFNA-II (1602.0 MPa) and was concentrated in the distal screw holes. It may imply the potential risks for peri-implant fracture and cut-out, especially in osteoporotic bone. This possibly explain the complications of femoral shaft fractures after PFNA-II treatment of intertrochanteric femoral fractures, as observed by Sawaguchi et al. [19]. The difference in maximum stress within the femur between two types of implants may be attributed to several key factors related to implant design and geometry. ZNN has a 1275-mm anterior arch radius and a 15°forward inclination angle, which may enhance load transfer and thereby reduce stress shielding. In the ZNN model, the stress on the implant and femur was the lowest, possibly because the design is anatomically suitable for the femur and provides a better stress transfer distribution, potentially reducing the risk of implant and femoral fractures.

Our results demonstrated that the ZNN construct exhibited significantly higher axial stiffness and ultimate failure load compared to the PFNA-II. While these are fundamental biomechanical parameters, their clinical relevance is paramount. The enhanced stiffness likely promotes fracture healing by providing a more stable mechanical environment, reducing the risk of delayed union [43, 44]. In addition to these healing benefits, the superior axial stiffness and failure load could potentially facilitate earlier postoperative mobilization and possibly decrease risks of implant migration or subsidence [22, 25]. Earlier mobilization is a key factor in preventing debilitating complications in the elderly, such as pneumonia, deep vein thrombosis, and muscle atrophy. The biomechanical test results of this study may explain the complications after PFNA-II internal fixation reported in clinical studies: thigh or hip pain, femoral shaft fractures, femoral head internal varus and collapse, and head nail cut-out [45, 46].

Our findings regarding the biomechanical values in this study are consistent with the previous studies involving the intramedullary nails of the head. In this study, the average failure load of the ZNN was 3950 N, whereas that of PFNA-II was 3540 N. Previously recorded average values ranged from 3073 N to 4970 N [39, 47]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the synthetic bone models used or the specific loading protocol.

In the axial compression failure tests, the primary failure mode with both the groups corresponded to the head sinking displacement greater than 15 mm, as well as cut-out of the cephalic screw and femoral shaft fracture. This type of failure has been observed in previous biomechanical testing and clinical outcomes [20, 41, 48]. The occurrence of femoral head varus collapse, screw cutout, and femoral shaft fracture will inevitably increase the likelihood of reoperation.

This study has several limitations. First, The model assumes a perfect fracture reduction, which, while clinically desirable, may not be achievable in all cases and could optimistically influence the contact stresses at the fracture site. The use of a fixed boundary condition at the distal femur is a common simplification that may inflate the model’s overall stiffness. Consequently, the absolute stress values should be interpreted with caution, though the comparative trends remain valid. Second, as in other studies, the specimens should ideally be subjected to load and torsional stress simultaneously to simulate the forces experienced by patients during a fall. However, we could not accomplish this due to the limitations of existing testing machines. Third, the strength of Sawbone II is not equivalent to that of the bones of patients with osteoporosis. some older patients have osteoporosis. Osteoporosis is a key factor in these fractures, and a single model cannot adequately represent the variability in bone density and structure seen in elderly patients. If these strengths are not equal, the failure modes of internal fixation in the Sawbone may differ from those of the bones of patients with osteoporosis. Further research in this regard requires the use of cadaveric specimens to simulate actual bone mass in patients with osteoporosis. Forth, the 2000 N static load simulates the stance phase of gait but is a simplification of complex dynamic loading. Forth, our findings are limited to AO 31-A2.2 fractures and the two tested implants, and caution against overgeneralization. We agree that generalizing the results to other fracture types or implants would require further investigation. Future studies will incorporate dynamic testing to better approximate physiological conditions. Lastly, the number of specimens in this study was relatively small, and more specimens and larger clinical samples are needed for follow-up.

In conclusion, in AO type 31-A2.2 unstable intertrochanteric fractures, the ZNN implant demonstrated favorable biomechanical characteristics under the specific experimental conditions tested in this study.
