Educational inequalities and residence-based decomposition in smoking behavior
Authors: Hakan Degerli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding education-related smoking inequalities and their contributors is crucial for identifying the social determinants of health and designing targeted interventions to reduce disparities. The objectives are to identify inequalities across Türkiye and determine the factors contributing to these inequalities between urban and rural areas. To achieve the aim of the study, Global Adult Tobacco Survey Turkey datasets were employed from 2002 to 2016. The Concentration Index, a suitable technique for measuring health inequalities was used. Additionally, Oaxaca-type Decomposition approach was used to understand how the social factors affect education- related smoking inequalities by urbanicity among adults. The analysis revealed pro-educated smoking inequalities in Türkiye, with smoking disproportionately concentrated among higher-educated individuals. The Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition further showed that rural–urban differences in these education-related inequalities were driven by variations in age, employment status, and gender. These findings highlight the need for targeted public health interventions addressing both structural and behavioural factors to reduce smoking inequalities and promote equitable health outcomes in Türkiye.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains one of the most significant public health challenges worldwide, contributing to millions of deaths annually and imposing a heavy burden on healthcare systems1. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), tobacco smoking is a leading cause of preventable diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, respiratory disorders, and various cancers2. Globally, tobacco use accounts for more than 8 million deaths each year, with over 1 million of these resulting from second-hand smoke exposure2. In Türkiye, according to the 2023 Turkey Household Health Survey on the Prevalence of Risk Factors for Non-Communicable Diseases, 34.8% of individuals aged 15 years and older in Türkiye use tobacco products, and 31.3% report daily tobacco use. The prevalence of smoking is notably higher among men (46.1%) compared to women (23.6%). Among daily tobacco users, 94.1% smoke cigarettes, with an average of 15.6 cigarettes consumed per day3. Despite global efforts to reduce smoking prevalence through policies such as taxation, public smoking bans, and awareness campaigns, smoking continues to be a widespread issue, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where tobacco control measures may be less stringent4. The persistence of smoking as a public health problem underscores the need for continued research and targeted interventions to address this issue effectively5.

In Türkiye, smoking prevalence has shown varying trends over the years, with significant differences observed across demographic and socioeconomic groups. Studies have indicated that smoking behaviour is influenced by a range of factors, including age, gender, education level, and urban–rural residence6–8. While national tobacco control policies, such as the smoking ban in public places and increased taxation on tobacco products, have contributed to a decline in smoking rates in some segments of the population, disparities persist, particularly among certain socioeconomic groups9. Understanding these disparities is crucial for designing effective public health strategies that address the root causes of smoking inequalities and promote equitable health outcomes10,11.

Education has been identified as a key determinant of smoking behaviour, with studies showing a complex relationship between educational attainment and smoking prevalence12,13. While higher education is generally associated with healthier lifestyle choices, some research suggests that in certain contexts, including Türkiye, smoking prevalence may be higher among more educated individuals9,14–16. This counterintuitive finding highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the social and cultural factors that influence smoking behaviour across different educational groups. By examining the role of education in smoking inequalities, this study aims to contribute to the growing body of literature on tobacco control and inform evidence-based policies that address the unique challenges faced by different segments of the population.

The use of decomposition analysis, such as the Oaxaca-Blinder method, has become increasingly important in public health research to uncover the underlying factors contributing to health inequalities17,18. Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of this approach in understanding disparities in health behaviours, including smoking, by disentangling the effects of observable characteristics and their differential impacts across population groups14,19,20. For instance, research has shown that decomposition methods can reveal how socioeconomic factors, such as education and income, interact with regional differences to influence health outcomes18.

The aim of this study is to investigate smoking behaviour disparities in Türkiye by calculating the Concentration Index (CI) for the entire sample and applying Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition to analyse the contribution of observable characteristics and their differential effects across rural and urban areas. Understanding the contributing factors by urbanicity is essential because urban and rural areas often face distinct social, economic, and environmental conditions that influence health behaviours and outcomes. By combining both methods, the study seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of the factors driving smoking inequalities and offer insights into how regional differences contribute to these disparities, informing targeted health policy interventions.

Methodology

In this study, the data of the 2008, 2012 and 2016 waves of Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) is employed to (i) examine the education-related smoking inequalities and (ii) decompose the factors contributing to inequalities by urbanicity among adults in Türkiye. GATS is a nationally representative household survey designed to track tobacco use, related behaviours, and exposure to second-hand smoke in various countries. The GATS surveys are conducted by the World Health Organization (WHO) in collaboration with the Ministry of Health and provide a comprehensive set of indicators on tobacco use and its determinants21–23. The data are anonymized and do not contain any personally identifiable information. Therefore, ethical approval was not required for secondary data analysis.

The analysis included all respondents aged 15 years and older from the GATS survey. Current smoking status was defined as a binary variable indicating whether the respondent currently smokes tobacco products. It is coded as 1 if individuals report that they smoke. Conversely, the variable is coded as 0 for individuals who indicate that they are not smokers. The analysis includes several covariates that could influence smoking inequalities. The covariates are as follows: education status, which was categorized into illiterate, primary, secondary, high school, or university education. These categories are represented numerically from 0 to 4, respectively. Age of respondent was a continuous variable ranging from 15 years and older. Gender of respondent was a binary variable indicating female or male. Urbanicity was also a binary indicator where 1 represents urban residents and 0 represents rural residents. Working status was classified into five categories—unemployed, employed, homemaker, student, and retired—using five separate binary variables, where a value of 1 indicates that the respondent belongs to the respective category.

The Concentration Index (CI) was used to assess education-related smoking inequalities among adults. This index is widely used to measure health inequalities and analyse the distribution of health outcomes across different societal groups24. In this study respondents’ education levels were grouped, therefore the CI calculation involved estimating inequalities across ranked education groups. The calculation of CI in this study applied the group-based method proposed by Clarke and van Ourti25, using education levels as an ordinal variable. Smoking rates by education levels, the population share of each group, and cumulative rank values were incorporated into the computation. Moreover, all prevalence estimates and inequality measures (concentration indices) were weighted using the sample weight variable included in the GATS dataset, in order to account for its complex survey design and ensure nationally representative results. The Erreygers correction was applied in this study because the smoking status variable is binary, and the unadjusted Concentration Index (CI) can lead to misleading interpretations in such cases. The correction addresses the dependency of CI on the mean of the health variable, ensuring comparability across different groups or populations. By normalizing the CI, the Erreygers method provides a more accurate measure of inequality and eliminates biases caused by the distribution of the outcome variable26. This adjustment is essential for evaluating education-related inequalities in smoking behaviour effectively. The CI ranges from − 1 to + 1, where positive values indicate that the outcome, smoking status in this study, is more concentrated among higher-education groups while negative values suggest the opposite. A CI value of zero indicates perfect equality in the distribution of the outcome27.

In the second step of the analysis, the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition17 was employed to reveal rural–urban differences in education-related inequalities in smoking behaviour. Due to the binary nature of the interest variable, a linear probability model (LPM) was used within the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition framework. Although the Fairlie decomposition28 is often recommended for binary outcomes, the Oaxaca-Blinder approach was preferred due to its widespread use in health inequality research and its greater interpretability in separating the effects of group characteristics (endowments) and their coefficients. The primary advantage of using this decomposition method lies in its ability to distinguish and quantify the contributions of various factors to the observed differences in smoking rates between rural and urban populations. More specifically, this step aims to identify how rural–urban differences in sociodemographic and behavioural characteristics contribute to the gap in education-related smoking inequalities identified in the concentration index (CI) analysis. Understanding the specific contributions of characteristics and their effects is essential for developing policies that can more effectively address the health needs of rural and urban populations. It is important to note that education status was not included as an explanatory variable in the decomposition model, as it was already used as the ranking variable in the construction of the concentration indices. Including education both as the ranking variable and as a covariate in the decomposition would lead to conceptual circularity and methodological redundancy—essentially decomposing education-related inequalities by education itself. Thus, its exclusion was a deliberate and justified analytical decision to maintain clarity and avoid potential multicollinearity that could bias the results.

Results

The sample consisted of adults aged 15 years and older from Türkiye, with observations collected in 2008 (n = 8,790), 2012 (n = 9,825), and 2016 (n = 8,671). The prevalence of smoking showed some variation across the years, with 30.0% of respondents reporting smoking in 2008, decreasing to 24.5% in 2012, and increasing again to 32.0% in 2016. Education levels increased over time, with the average education status rising from 1.41 in 2008 to 1.93 in 2016. This upward trend suggests improvements in educational attainment among the surveyed population. Mean age of respondents ranged between 40.8 and 44.9 years across survey years, indicating a middle-aged adult population. The gender distribution was approximately balanced across years, with females comprising around 49–55% of the sample. The rural–urban composition shifted over time, with rural residents representing about 49% in 2008 and 2012, but decreasing to 11% in 2016, reflecting urbanization trends. Regarding working status, the proportion of employees remained relatively stable (around 36–39%) across the years. The share of homemakers increased substantially from 7.5% in 2008 to nearly 39% in 2012 and remained around 36% in 2016. By decomposing the inequalities in this way, it is gained a deeper understanding of the sources of smoking inequalities, helping to identify whether differences are driven more by the characteristics themselves, their effects, or the interaction between them. This analysis provides valuable insights for designing targeted health interventions tailored to the unique needs of rural and urban populations.

According to Table 3 the predictions for both groups (rural and urban) are statistically significant across all years (2008, 2012, and 2016), with p < 0.001. This indicates a consistent and significant inequality in smoking rates between rural and urban populations over time. The negative differences (− 0.0650, − 0.0803, − 0.0446) suggest that urban areas have higher smoking rates compared to rural areas.

The endowments component reflects differences in observable characteristics between rural and urban areas. The age variable is significant across all years (p < 0.001), with negative coefficients. This suggests that differences in age distribution between rural and urban areas contribute to lower smoking rates in rural areas. This difference thereby reduces education-related smoking inequalities between rural and urban populations. The employee variable is significant in 2008 (p < 0.05) and 2016 (p < 0.1), with negative coefficients. This implies that being employed is associated with lower smoking rates in rural areas compared to urban areas. This factor also contributes to narrowing education-related smoking inequalities. The student status is significant in 2008 (p < 0.001) and 2016 (p < 0.05), with positive coefficients. This indicates that being a student is associated with higher smoking rates in rural areas compared to urban areas. Thus, it contributes to widening education-related smoking inequalities. Being homemaker is significant in all years (p < 0.1 in 2008, p < 0.001 in 2012, and p < 0.05 in 2016), with negative coefficients. This suggests that being a homemaker is associated with lower smoking rates in rural areas compared to urban areas. Thus, it contributes to reducing education-related smoking inequalities.

The coefficients component captures differences in the effects of characteristics on smoking behaviour between rural and urban areas. The female variable is significant across all years (p < 0.001 in 2008 and 2016, p < 0.05 in 2012), with negative coefficients. This indicates that being female has a stronger negative effect on smoking rates in rural areas compared to urban areas. This differential effect contributes to reducing education-related smoking inequalities between rural and urban populations. The employee variable is significant in 2016 (p < 0.1), with a negative coefficient. This suggests that being employed has a stronger negative effect on smoking rates in rural areas compared to urban areas in 2016. Such an effect likewise helps to narrow education-related smoking inequalities. The constant term is significant in 2008 and 2012 (p < 0.001) and in 2016 (p < 0.05). The negative coefficients in 2008 and 2012 and the positive coefficient in 2016 indicate that there are unobserved factors contributing to the differences in smoking rates between rural and urban areas. These unmeasured factors may either widen or reduce education-related smoking inequalities, as indicated by the change in sign of the coefficient over time.

The interaction component assesses how differences in characteristics and their effects combine to influence smoking inequalities. Most of the interaction terms are not statistically significant, except for a few: The interaction term for homemaker status is significant in 2008 (p < 0.1), with a positive coefficient (0.0097). This suggests that the combined effect of being a homemaker and the differences in characteristics between rural and urban areas contributes to higher smoking rates in rural areas in 2008. In the context of education-related smoking inequality, this positive contribution from the interaction effect implies that this subgroup dynamic slightly widened the rural–urban inequality associated with educational differences in 2008.

As a result, the decomposition results indicate that these inequalities are shaped by a combination of several factors. For instance, age distribution plays a role, with rural areas having a younger population that tends to smoke less. This age structure, as captured in the endowments component, contributes to reducing education-related smoking inequalities between rural and urban populations. Employee variable also contributes, as being employed is associated with lower smoking rates in rural areas compared to urban areas. Both the distribution (endowments) and the effect (coefficients) of employment status help narrow the rural–urban gap in education-related smoking inequality. Additionally, gender differences are notable, with women in rural areas showing a stronger tendency to smoke less than their urban counterparts. This stronger protective effect among rural women, reflected in the coefficients component, contributes to reducing education-related smoking inequalities. Interestingly, being a student is linked to higher smoking rates in rural areas. This pattern, represented by the positive contribution of student status in the endowments component, acts in the opposite direction—widening the rural–urban inequality in education-related smoking. Similarly, homemakers in rural areas smoke less than those in urban settings. While this contributes to narrowing inequalities through endowments, interaction effects in 2008 slightly increased inequality due to complex subgroup dynamics. These results suggest that smoking behaviour is shaped not only by individual characteristics but also by the broader social and environmental context of rural versus urban living. Importantly, the decomposition analysis shows that these characteristics and their varying effects across regions contribute either to narrowing or widening education-related smoking inequalities. To address these disparities, public health interventions should be tailored to the specific needs and characteristics of each population, taking into account the unique factors that influence smoking behaviour in rural and urban areas.

Discussion

The findings of this study reveal significant education-related smoking inequalities in Türkiye, with smoking prevalence disproportionately concentrated among more educated individuals. These findings of higher smoking prevalence among more educated groups in Türkiye are consistent with previous studies29–31. Although this study does not formulate formal hypotheses, the findings suggest possible mechanisms underlying education-related smoking inequalities. For example, differences in access to health information, social norms around smoking, and socioeconomic factors may explain why smoking prevalence varies by education and residence. These interpretations align with prior literature32,33 and offer directions for future research to explore these mechanisms in more detail. This counterintuitive trend, where higher education levels are associated with higher smoking rates, contrasts with global patterns observed in many high-income countries, where higher education typically correlates with healthier behaviours, including lower smoking rates11,12. The persistence of this trend in Türkiye underscores the importance of contextual factors, such as cultural norms and social influences, in shaping smoking behaviour8,9. These patterns may reflect Türkiye’s partial alignment with Stage 3 of the smoking epidemic model34, in which smoking prevalence remains elevated among more educated and urban groups—particularly women—despite public health efforts. While national trends suggest a gradual shift toward Stage 4, the persistence of high smoking rates in specific subgroups indicates an uneven transition across population segments. The slight narrowing of education-related smoking inequality between 2008 and 2016 may reflect the relatively greater responsiveness of higher-educated individuals to tobacco control policies implemented during this period. Alternatively, shifts in the composition of smokers—such as increased uptake among less-educated groups—may have contributed to the convergence. On the other hand, Türkiye has implemented comprehensive tobacco control measures over the past decade, including the 2009 smoke-free law, significant tax increases, and mass media campaigns. While these policies have contributed to an overall decline in smoking prevalence, their effects may vary across different population groups. For instance, higher-educated individuals and urban women—groups identified in our study as having persistent or rising smoking rates—might be less influenced by general policies, potentially due to social, occupational, or cultural factors. Reflecting on these differential impacts enhances the relevance of our findings and supports the call for more nuanced, equity-focused tobacco control strategies tailored to the needs of distinct demographic groups in Türkiye.

The positive concentration indices observed in this study indicate that smoking remains more prevalent among more educated groups in Türkiye, despite a declining trend over time. This finding highlights the need for public health interventions that specifically target highly educated individuals, who may be less influenced by traditional tobacco control measures such as taxation and public smoking bans5. Educational campaigns that emphasize the health risks of smoking and promote cessation programs tailored to this demographic could be particularly effective13. Additionally, further research is needed to explore the underlying social and cultural factors that contribute to the higher prevalence of smoking among educated individuals in Türkiye.

The Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition analysis further highlights the role of demographic and socioeconomic factors in driving education-related smoking inequalities between rural and urban areas. Urban areas consistently exhibited higher smoking rates compared to rural areas across the study period (2008–2016). This disparity can be attributed to differences in age distribution, employment status, and gender roles. For instance, rural areas tend to have a younger population, which is associated with lower smoking rates, while urban areas may have more individuals in employment settings where smoking is more socially accepted6,7. Additionally, the stronger negative effect of being female on smoking rates in rural areas suggests that gender norms and cultural expectations play a significant role in shaping smoking behaviour10. It is also important to note that Türkiye’s administrative classification of rural and urban areas changed during the study period, particularly after the 2013 metropolitan law35. Many rural settlements were reclassified as urban, resulting in a sharp decline in the rural sample in the 2016 GATS wave. This shift may partly explain the observed convergence in urban–rural smoking inequalities by 2016, as some formerly rural populations were statistically redefined.

The decomposition results also reveal that employment status and student status contribute to inequalities. Being employed is associated with lower smoking rates in rural areas, possibly due to differences in workplace environments and access to health information. Conversely, being a student is linked to higher smoking rates in rural areas, which may reflect peer influence and limited access to tobacco control interventions in educational settings15,16. These findings align with previous research emphasizing the importance of targeted interventions that address the unique social and environmental contexts of rural and urban populations19,20.

The use of decomposition analysis, such as the Oaxaca-Blinder method, has proven invaluable in identifying the factors contributing to smoking inequalities. By disentangling the effects of observable characteristics and their differential impacts across population groups, this approach provides a nuanced understanding of the drivers of health disparities18. This study’s findings underscore the importance of considering both individual-level factors and broader social determinants when designing public health interventions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the persistent education-related smoking inequalities in Türkiye and the significant role of demographic and socioeconomic factors in shaping smoking behaviour. The findings of this study highlight the need for targeted public health interventions to address the persistent inequality in smoking behaviour among more educated groups. Rising smoking rates among urban women point to changing social norms and the need for gender-sensitive tobacco control. These findings underscore the importance of tailored interventions that go beyond one-size-fits-all strategies. Policymakers should consider implementing educational campaigns that specifically target highly educated individuals, emphasizing the health risks associated with smoking and promoting cessation programs tailored to this demographic. Moreover, understanding the underlying social and cultural factors contributing to the higher smoking prevalence among the educated population is essential for developing effective interventions. Investigating gender-specific patterns and potential interaction effects in education-related smoking inequalities in future studies could also yield valuable insights into subgroup differences within the Turkish population. Finally, collaboration between public health authorities, educational institutions, and community organizations is essential to create a comprehensive strategy that not only reduces smoking rates but also addresses the broader determinants of health inequalities.
