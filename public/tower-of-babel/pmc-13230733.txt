Assessing trends and forecasting meteorological drought in South Africa using Savitzky–Golay enhanced hybrid deep learning
Authors: Siphamandla Sibiya, Shaun Ramroop, Sileshi Melesse, Nkanyiso Mbatha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drought constitutes one of the most significant natural hazards worldwide, exacerbated by climate variability and change, with profound implications for ecosystems, agriculture, and livelihoods. In South Africa, particularly within the drought-prone uMkhanyakude District of KwaZulu-Natal, comprehending rainfall variability and enhancing drought prediction are imperative for sustainable water and food security planning. This study utilized daily rainfall records from six meteorological stations spanning the years 1980 to 2023 to calculate the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) at 6-, 9-, and 12-month time scales. Long-term drought trends were evaluated employing Innovative Trend Analysis (ITA) methods, which identified statistically significant decreasing trends at five stations and an increasing trend at Riverview. To augment drought forecasting, a novel hybrid model that integrates the Savitzky–Golay filter with a Temporal Convolutional Network and Long Short-Term Memory (SG–TCN–LSTM) was developed. Comparative assessments against ARIMA, LSTM, TCN, and other hybrid models demonstrated that the SG–TCN–LSTM consistently achieved the lowest Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) values (0.0349–0.1453) and the highest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document} values (0.95–0.99) across all SPI scales, indicating superior predictive accuracy and stability. The integration of signal smoothing with deep learning methodologies enhanced the robustness of forecasts, providing critical insights for proactive drought risk management. This research underscores the potential of hybrid models as reliable early-warning instruments for meteorological drought and establishes a framework that can inform national adaptation strategies. Future research should aim to extend the model to incorporate additional climatic drivers, evaluate its transferability across diverse climatic regions, and investigate its application in operational drought early-warning systems.

Introduction

Global warming has increased the frequency and severity of extreme hydroclimatic events, posing significant risks to ecosystems, agriculture, water resources, and human livelihoods1–3. Drought is particularly complex due to its gradual onset, extended duration, and socio-economic impacts4. Meteorological drought, marked by sustained precipitation deficits, often precedes agricultural and hydrological drought, making it a critical early indicator of water scarcity and food insecurity5. The assessment of meteorological drought relies on standardized indices, notably the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI)6, the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI)7, and the Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI)8. Recent studies highlight the SPEI’s relevance for assessing climate change-driven drought9,10, especially where temperature impacts are significant. However, the SPI is the only index endorsed by the World Meteorological Organization for monitoring meteorological drought due to its robustness, minimal data requirements, and comparability across climatic regimes9,11–15. Its multiscalar flexibility makes it suitable for medium- to long-term drought assessments. This study uses the SPI at 6-, 9-, and 12-month time scales to capture seasonal and interannual rainfall variability. South Africa exhibits significant vulnerability to climate variability, attributable to its semi-arid climate, reliance on rain-fed agriculture, and insufficient water storage infrastructure14,16,17. Recent drought episodes, notably the 2015–2016 El Niño event, resulted in considerable agricultural and socio-economic detriments across Southern Africa16,18,19. In this context, the uMkhanyakude District of KwaZulu-Natal serves as a particularly climate-sensitive area, characterized by its dependence on groundwater, subsistence farming practices, ecological assets such as the iSimangaliso Wetland Park, and elevated levels of socio-economic vulnerability20. Notwithstanding this vulnerability, research addressing drought in this district remains scarce, particularly concerning the development of advanced forecasting frameworks that are tailored to local hydro-climatic conditions. Traditional drought forecasting methodologies, including statistical and regression-based approaches, often struggle to accurately capture the nonlinear dynamics and temporal dependencies of hydroclimatic processes. Consequently, there has been increased adoption of soft computing and machine learning techniques in this field. Hybrid predictive models that integrate signal decomposition, optimization algorithms, and deep learning architectures have been widely used to address various environmental challenges, showing improved performance compared to standalone models in drought prediction across different climatic regions21–26. Malik et al.27 demonstrated the effectiveness of hybrid optimization-based models for meteorological drought forecasting using the Effective Drought Index (EDI), showing that SVM-HHO outperformed SVM-PSO in predicting median drought conditions in Uttarakhand, India. Similarly, Piri et al.28 conducted a comparative analysis of SVM-RSM, SVM-PSO, standalone SVM, and ANN across multiple drought indices, concluding that SVM-PSO achieved superior accuracy and trend detection, while SVM-RSM exhibited the highest operational efficiency. Advancements in deep learning highlight the importance of hybridization15,29,30. Waqas et al.31 found that wavelet-based hybrid models, especially the WBi-LSTM, outperformed standalone Bayesian Regression Forest (BRF) and Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) models at various temporal scales. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)-optimized Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models also showed improved predictive capabilities in forecasting climate-driven Cyanobacterial Harmful Algal Blooms (CyanoHAB) dynamics under multiple Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) scenarios32. Hybrid sequence-to-sequence frameworks combining Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) and Transformer architectures surpassed traditional downscaling techniques and standalone LSTM models in climate projection efforts33. In agricultural drought forecasting, Rezaei et al.34 reported that Support Vector Machine with Particle Optimization Algorithm (SVM-POA) consistently outperformed Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System with Particle Optimization Algorithm (ANFIS-POA) and Artificial Neural Network with Particle Optimization Algorithm (ANN-POA) across multiple Agricultural Standardized Precipitation Index (ASPI) timescales. Hybrid modeling has also proven effective in related environmental challenges, such as the statistical downscaling of General Circulation Models (GCMs), where integrated deep learning frameworks enhance climate projection reliability33. These advancements demonstrate the strengths of hybrid systems in modeling complex hydroclimatic processes. Nevertheless, several important gaps remain in the current literature. First, many studies focus primarily on forecasting accuracy without first examining long-term drought trend behaviour, which limits understanding of the structural evolution of drought conditions. Second, most hybrid forecasting frameworks rely heavily on signal decomposition techniques such as wavelets or CEEMDAN. Although effective in some contexts, these approaches increase computational complexity and fragment the original signal structure, potentially altering the temporal relationships embedded in the data. Third, very few studies integrate trend detection with a smoothing-based deep hybrid architecture, particularly within the hydroclimatic context of the uMkhanyakude District.

To address these limitations, this study proposes a novel Savitzky–Golay Temporal Convolutional Network Long Short-Term Memory (SG–TCN–LSTM) framework for meteorological drought prediction using SPI series spanning 1980–2023. Methodologically, this study introduces a streamlined hybrid modelling strategy that integrates trend detection and deep learning forecasting within a unified framework. From a regional perspective, it provides the first integrated trend–forecasting drought assessment for the uMkhanyakude District, offering insights that support water resource planning, agricultural risk management, and climate adaptation in one of South Africa’s most drought-prone regions.

Methods

The research was conducted in the uMkhanyakude District Municipality, situated in the northern region of KwaZulu-Natal Province, South Africa (32.01°S, 27.62°E) (see Fig. 1). The district encompasses an area of approximately 13,855 km2, bordered by Mozambique to the north, Eswatini to the northwest, and the Indian Ocean to the east. Its topography varies from low-lying coastal plains along the Indian Ocean to the Lebombo Mountains in the west, with elevations ranging from sea level to over 700 meters. The uMkhanyakude District exhibits significant ecological diversity, comprising a range of habitats from wetlands to savannah. The region experiences a subtropical climate characterized by hot, wet summers (November–March) and cool, dry winters (June–August). Mean annual rainfall fluctuates between 600 and 1,200 mm, with higher precipitation levels recorded in the eastern coastal zone and decreased amounts in the interior. Average temperatures range from 18 °C in winter to 30 °C in summer, although extreme heat events exceeding 40 °C have been documented. This district is of national significance due to its ecological and socio-economic value, as it includes the iSimangaliso Wetland Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, along with various game reserves such as Hluhluwe–iMfolozi, Ndumo, Tembe Elephant Park, and Mkuze Game Reserve. Despite its rich biodiversity and tourism potential, the district remains predominantly rural, with many communities dependent on rainfed agriculture and natural resources. The interplay of climate variability, water insecurity, and high poverty levels renders it one of the most drought-prone and socio-economically vulnerable regions in South Africa.Fig. 1Elevation Map of the uMkhanyakude District Municipality in KwaZulu-Natal Province, South Africa. Daily rainfall data from 1980 to 2023 were obtained from the South African Weather Service (SAWS) for six meteorological stations: Pongolapoort Dam, Mkuze Game Reserve, Hlabisa Mbazwana, False Bay Park, Ingwavuma Manguzi, and Riverview. The long-term rainfall variability for the six meteorological stations is presented in Supplementary Figure S1. These stations were chosen based on data completeness, spatial coverage, and record continuity. Quality control procedures were implemented to identify and rectify any missing or inconsistent observations. The resulting rainfall series was used to compute the SPI for drought characterization and modeling.

Standardized precipitation index calculation

The Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI), introduced by McKee et al.6, quantifies meteorological drought conditions. It is widely used in drought assessments due to its ability to standardize precipitation anomalies across various temporal scales, facilitating comparisons across regions and climates35. In this study, SPI values were derived by fitting a gamma probability distribution to accumulated rainfall data at specific timescales (SPI-6, SPI-9, and SPI-12) and transforming cumulative probabilities into a standard normal distribution. This process ensures that the SPI has a mean of zero and a variance of one, improving the classification of wet and dry conditions36,37. To address zero rainfall occurrences, cumulative probabilities were adjusted before normalization. SPI categorization followed the framework by McKee et al.6, where negative values indicate drought conditions and positive values indicate wet conditions, with thresholds distinguishing moderate, severe, and extreme events (see Table S1 in the Supplementary Information). For conciseness, the detailed mathematical derivation of the gamma distribution parameters, cumulative probabilities, and standardization equations are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Innovative trend analysis

The Innovative Trend Analysis (ITA) method, first introduced by Şen38, offers a key advantage over traditional approaches such as the Mann–Kendall test, as it does not require assumptions regarding serial correlation, data normality, or sample size. The procedure begins by dividing the time series into two equal sub-series, which are then independently arranged in ascending order. The first half of the series is plotted on the x-axis, while the second half is plotted on the y-axis. A graphical illustration of the ITA procedure is provided in Supplementary Figure S2. If the plotted points lie along the 1:1 reference line (45° line), the series exhibits no discernible trend. In contrast, points concentrated above the line indicate an increasing trend, whereas clustering below the line signifies a decreasing trend38,39.

Savitzky–Golay filter algorithm

The Savitzky-Golay filter, introduced by Savitzky and Golay in 196440, is a strong tool for signal denoising and smoothing. This method smooths signals by employing convolution on time-domain signals and polynomial least-squares in each convolution window41. This study utilised the Savitzky–Golay (SG) filter, a polynomial function fitting method to eliminate noise from atmospheric conditions. This method is very efficient for analysing datasets characterised by attributes that include widths, maxima, and minima, assuming the data points are evenly distributed along a specified axis42. Its superiority over other preprocessing approaches is attributed to its distinctive integration of smoothing and the retention of critical signal properties. Schafer43, Guo et al.44, and Sibiya et al.15 described the algorithm of SG in detail. The SG filter is computationally efficient and can be executed in real-time systems. For accurate analysis of the dataset, the generated curve must be continuous and smooth45. The implementation of the SG filter significantly reduced the influence of SPI outliers resulting from atmospheric disturbances.

Autoregressive integrated moving average

The Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model, originally introduced by Box and Jenkins46, was employed as a benchmark predictive model in this study. ARIMA is a stochastic linear time-series model that combines autoregressive (AR), differencing (I), and moving average (MA) components to capture temporal dependencies in stationary time series. Long and short-term memory network

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber47, is a specialized architecture within recurrent neural networks (RNNs) designed to capture long-term dependencies in sequential data. Unlike conventional RNNs, which are prone to vanishing and exploding gradient problems, LSTM networks employ gated mechanisms that regulate the flow of information through the network, enabling stable learning over extended time horizons.

An LSTM cell consists of three principal gates: the forget gate, input gate, and output gate, which collectively control the updating and propagation of the cell state. The forget gate determines which information from the previous state should be retained or discarded, the input gate regulates the incorporation of new information into the cell state, and the output gate governs the generation of the hidden state used for prediction. Through this gating mechanism, LSTM networks effectively preserve relevant historical information while filtering out noise, making them well suited for modelling nonlinear temporal dynamics in hydrological and climatological time series. In this study, the LSTM model was employed to capture nonlinear patterns in the SPI series. A schematic representation of the LSTM architecture is provided in Figure S4 in the Supplementary Material, while the detailed mathematical formulation of the gating operations is also included in the Supplementary Material to maintain the conciseness of the main manuscript.

Temporal convolutional network

The Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) is a deep learning architecture designed for sequence modelling that employs convolutional operations to capture temporal dependencies in time-series data48. Unlike RNNs, which process sequences sequentially, TCNs utilize convolutional structures that allow parallel computation during training and prediction, thereby improving computational efficiency and scalability.

The TCN architecture incorporates two key features: causal convolution and dilated convolution. Causal convolution ensures that predictions at time t depend only on current and past observations, thereby preventing information leakage from future time steps49. Dilated convolution extends the receptive field of the network by introducing a dilation factor that skips certain input elements, allowing the model to capture long-range temporal dependencies without requiring excessively deep networks50. In this way, the receptive field grows exponentially with the network depth.

To improve training stability in deep architectures, TCNs employ residual blocks, which incorporate shortcut connections between layers. These residual connections facilitate gradient propagation and mitigate the vanishing gradient problem commonly encountered in deep neural networks51. Each residual block typically consists of stacked dilated causal convolution layers followed by nonlinear activation functions and dropout regularization to prevent overfitting.

In this study, the TCN model was employed to capture complex temporal patterns in the SPI series. A schematic illustration of the TCN residual block structure is provided in Figure S5 in the Supplementary Material, while the detailed mathematical formulation of the convolution operations and residual connections is also included in the Supplementary Material to maintain the conciseness of the main manuscript.

The development of the hybrid ARIMA-LSTM model

The ARIMA–LSTM hybrid model was developed to capture both linear and nonlinear patterns in the SPI time series. In the first stage, the ARIMA model was applied to model the linear structure of the SPI data. The residuals obtained from the ARIMA forecasts were then used as inputs to the LSTM network, which was trained to learn the remaining nonlinear relationships in the series. The final prediction was obtained by combining the ARIMA forecasts with the residual forecasts generated by the LSTM model. This hybrid approach leverages the strengths of both models: ARIMA effectively captures short-term linear dependencies and autocorrelation structures, while LSTM networks are capable of learning complex nonlinear temporal dynamics. Consequently, the ARIMA–LSTM framework provides improved predictive performance compared with the individual models. A schematic representation of the ARIMA–LSTM hybrid framework is provided in Figure S6 in the Supplementary Material.

Deeper TCN configurations increased parameter complexity and computational burden without proportional gains in generalization performance. This observation suggests that convolutional receptive fields alone are insufficient to fully model the nonlinear accumulation effects characteristic of prolonged drought regimes.

To enhance the understanding of drought dynamics within the study area, key climate parameters were analysed utilizing the SPI. Figure 3 illustrates the annual means of drought-relevant variables. The SPI time series indicates multiple drought episodes throughout the study period (1980–2023), with a notably prolonged drought occurring between 2004 and 2009. A second significant drought episode was recorded from 2014 to 2016, coinciding with the intense 2015–2016 El Niño event that substantially diminished rainfall across Southern Africa. SPI time series filtering

The study introduces the SG data preprocessing methodologies for the development of a hybrid model aimed at mitigating noise and enhancing the quality of the SPI time series. Figure 5 presents the time series data for SPI-6, SPI-9, and SPI-12 plotted with their respective SG-filtered smoothed data, while the remaining results are provided in the Supplementary Materials (see Figure S7–S9). The SG filter was employed to smooth the time series, with its effectiveness dependent on the selected parameters: a window size of 21 and a polynomial order of 5, determined through a trial-and-error approach during the experimental phase. As depicted in Fig. 5, the SG filter demonstrates considerable efficacy in smoothing the SPI time series, as evidenced by the filtered curve’s close adherence to the original data. However, it is crucial to emphasize that the SG filter functions solely as a preprocessing technique aimed at noise reduction and series stabilization, without engaging in forecasting. The SG filter contributes by furnishing cleaner input data to the deep learning models, thereby enhancing their convergence and stability. Consequently, the hybrid model, which integrates SG, TCN, and LSTM, capitalizes on the strengths of each component: SG eliminates high-frequency noise, TCN extracts multi-scale features, and LSTM captures long-term memory effects. Hyperparameter tuning settings

All models were implemented in Python 3.10 utilizing Google Colab, a cloud-based platform that offers GPU acceleration to enhance computational efficiency. Various deep learning architectures, including LSTM, TCN, and the proposed SG–TCN–LSTM hybrid framework, were developed using the TensorFlow/Keras library. The ARIMA model was executed with the help of the pmdarima and statsmodels packages. Data preprocessing, normalization, and performance evaluation were conducted utilizing NumPy, Pandas, and Scikit-learn, while the SG filter was employed through the SciPy library.

The SPI time series from each meteorological station was segmented using a chronological 80/20 split, wherein the initial 80% of observations were allocated for model training, and the remaining 20% were designated for out-of-sample testing. This method preserves the temporal structure of the data and mitigates the risk of information leakage that may occur from random shuffling in time-series forecasting tasks. Forecasting models were trained within a supervised sliding-window framework for one-step-ahead \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(t+1)$$\end{document} prediction, utilizing lagged SPI observations to forecast the subsequent time step.

Hyperparameter optimization for the deep learning models was conducted using Bayesian optimization, which systematically explores the hyperparameter space to minimize validation loss and enhance predictive accuracy. The complete ranges for hyperparameter searches and optimal configurations for the ARIMA, TCN, and LSTM models are provided in Tables S3–S5 in the Supplementary Material. For the ARIMA models, optimal (p,d,q) parameters were ascertained using the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$auto\_arima()$$\end{document} procedure based on the Hyndman–Khandakar stepwise algorithm, with model selection guided by the minimization of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Prior to model estimation, the stationarity of the SPI series was assessed using the ADF test, and differencing was applied as necessary to ensure stationarity.

All models were trained independently for each SPI timescale and meteorological station, thereby ensuring methodological rigor and facilitating a consistent evaluation of forecasting performance across varying climatic conditions. Early stopping was implemented during training to prevent overfitting and enhance model generalization. While Bayesian optimization enhances model performance through the efficient identification of optimal hyperparameter combinations, it is computationally intensive. Consequently, the optimization process was meticulously designed to balance computational cost and predictive accuracy. The resulting parameter configurations represent the optimal settings for the SPI datasets analyzed in this study.

Comparative performance of forecasting models

To assess the capability of drought prediction, six forecasting models were developed: ARIMA, LSTM, TCN, ARIMA-LSTM, TCN-LSTM, and the proposed SG-TCN-LSTM hybrid model. The performance of these models was evaluated using root mean square error (RMSE), the coefficient of determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document}), and directional symmetry (DS) metrics.

The results indicate that hybrid models consistently outperform standalone models across all SPI timescales and monitoring stations. For example, Figures 6 and 7 present the results for the False Bay Park station, while the results for the remaining stations are provided in the Supplementary Materials (see Figures S10–S19). The ARIMA model, which captures linear temporal structures, provides a reasonable baseline prediction but shows limitations in representing the nonlinear relationships inherent in the SPI series. In contrast, deep learning models such as LSTM and TCN improve forecasting performance by effectively capturing nonlinear dynamics and complex temporal dependencies present in the rainfall data. Among the two deep learning architectures, the TCN model generally achieved superior prediction accuracy compared to the LSTM model. This improvement is likely attributable to the dilated causal convolution mechanism employed by TCN, which enables the model to capture multi-scale temporal patterns while maintaining computational efficiency through parallel processing. Nevertheless, both models exhibited limitations when applied independently, particularly in their ability to capture abrupt fluctuations in the SPI series.

Hybrid models that integrate different modeling strategies further enhanced forecasting accuracy. The ARIMA-LSTM model improved prediction performance by decomposing the linear and nonlinear components of the SPI series. However, the TCN-LSTM hybrid model demonstrated greater predictive capability compared to ARIMA-LSTM, suggesting that convolution-based feature extraction can effectively complement recurrent neural networks in capturing complex climatic patterns. The proposed SG-TCN-LSTM model achieved the best overall performance across all stations and timescales. The RMSE values ranged from 0.2018 to 0.2910 for SPI-6, 0.1275 to 0.2119 for SPI-9, and 0.0487 to 0.0897 for SPI-12, while the corresponding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2}$$\end{document} values ranged between 0.9456 and 0.9723 for SPI-6, 0.9785 and 0.9862 for SPI-9, and 0.9953 and 0.9991 for SPI-12. Influence of drought trends on forecasting performance

An important observation from the results is the relationship between drought trends and forecasting performance. Stations exhibiting stronger decreasing precipitation trends generally show slightly higher prediction errors compared to stations with more stable or increasing precipitation patterns. This behaviour reflects the influence of non-stationarity, which introduces structural changes in the statistical properties of the time series and increases forecasting uncertainty. For example, stations such as Ingwavuma Manguzi and Hlabisa Mbazwana, which show strong drying trends in the ITA analysis, exhibit slightly higher RMSE values compared to the Riverview station, where an increasing precipitation trend was observed. Despite this challenge, the SG-TCN-LSTM model maintains relatively stable prediction accuracy across all stations, suggesting that the hybrid framework is robust to moderate levels of non-stationarity. This robustness can be attributed to the complementary roles of the three model components. The SG filter reduces high-frequency noise and stabilizes the SPI series, allowing the deep learning models to focus on meaningful temporal structures. The TCN component extracts multi-scale temporal features using dilated causal convolutions, while the LSTM component captures long-term dependencies within the data. The integration of these mechanisms enables the hybrid model to better adapt to evolving climatic patterns compared to standalone models.

Environmental and policy implications

The increasing drought tendencies observed across the majority of monitoring stations highlight significant environmental and socio-economic risks for the uMkhanyakude district. The region is heavily dependent on rainfall-driven water resources for agriculture, domestic consumption, and ecosystem sustainability. Prolonged precipitation deficits may therefore exacerbate water scarcity, reduce crop yields, and increase pressure on already vulnerable rural communities. Accurate drought forecasting plays a crucial role in mitigating these risks by providing early warning signals that support proactive decision-making. The improved predictive performance of the SG-TCN-LSTM model suggests that advanced hybrid forecasting frameworks can contribute to more reliable drought early-warning systems. Such systems could assist water resource managers in optimizing reservoir operations, implementing timely water restrictions, and planning climate adaptation strategies. From a policy perspective, integrating machine learning-based forecasting models into national drought monitoring systems could enhance the effectiveness of climate risk management strategies in South Africa. Improved forecasting accuracy may enable government agencies to implement targeted interventions, such as drought relief programs, agricultural advisories, and water allocation policies, before severe impacts occur. Additionally, the development of robust drought prediction tools aligns with broader climate resilience objectives under national and regional climate adaptation frameworks.

Conclusion and future remarks

This study examined meteorological drought dynamics and forecasting performance in the uMkhanyakude district, South Africa, using the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) at 6-, 9-, and 12-month timescales. The trend analysis revealed a consistent drying tendency across most monitoring stations, with five stations exhibiting decreasing precipitation trends, particularly at longer accumulation periods. These results indicate an increasing risk of prolonged drought conditions in the region, which may have significant implications for agricultural productivity, water resource availability, and ecosystem stability.

To improve drought prediction, several forecasting models were evaluated, including ARIMA, LSTM, TCN, and hybrid frameworks. The findings demonstrate that hybrid approaches outperform individual models, with the proposed SG–TCN–LSTM model providing the most reliable predictions across all stations and SPI timescales. The integration of Savitzky–Golay filtering with deep learning architectures enables effective noise reduction, multi-scale feature extraction, and improved modelling of nonlinear temporal dependencies in hydroclimatic time series. From a practical perspective, the enhanced forecasting capability of the SG–TCN–LSTM framework provides a valuable tool for strengthening drought early-warning systems and supporting evidence-based water resource management in drought-prone regions. However, this study presents two primary limitations. First, the analysis is predicated on rainfall data from a limited number of stations within the uMkhanyakude District, which may constrain the generalizability of the findings to other regions. Second, the investigation exclusively utilizes precipitation-based indices, specifically the SPI, and does not account for other significant hydroclimatic variables, such as temperature or soil moisture. Furthermore, uncertainties in long-term forecasting persist due to the inherent variability and non-stationarity of climate systems. Future research should explore the integration of additional climate drivers (e.g., ENSO indices, temperature, and evapotranspiration) and multi-source datasets to further improve drought prediction accuracy and strengthen climate resilience strategies under increasing hydroclimatic variability.
