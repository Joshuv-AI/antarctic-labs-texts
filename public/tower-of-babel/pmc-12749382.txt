Biomechanical effects of different change of direction angles on lower limb joint load and injury risk in soccer players
Authors: Jianjun Li, Haonan Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the biomechanical effects of different change of direction (COD) angles (45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°) on lower limb joint loads and injury risk in 26 male soccer players. Using three-dimensional motion capture, force platforms, and electromyography, we analyzed joint kinetics, kinematics, and muscle activation patterns during standardized COD tasks. Data were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc corrections (α = 0.05), with effect sizes reported as partial eta-squared (ηp²). Results demonstrated progressive increases in peak knee abduction moment (141% from 45° to 180°) and dynamic valgus index (152% increase), with a critical nonlinear threshold at 90°. Sharper CODs (≥ 135°) produced high-risk biomechanical patterns, with 88% of participants exceeding ACL injury risk thresholds during 180° tasks compared to 19% at 45°. Earlier hamstring activation (12.3% vs. 18.7% of stance phase) and increased muscle coactivation were observed at sharper angles. These findings suggest soccer training programs should prioritize angle-specific injury prevention for CODs ≥ 135°, including progressive eccentric strengthening, neuromuscular control training, and technique modification targeting reduced knee abduction moments and enhanced dynamic stability.

Introduction

Field sports, particularly soccer, require players to perform a variety of complex movements for competitive success1. Among these movements, changes of direction (COD) are especially crucial and frequent, with elite soccer players executing approximately 700 directional changes during a match at varying intensities and angles2. The ability to change direction rapidly is not only a performance determinant that distinguishes elite from sub-elite players, but also represents a significant injury risk factor, particularly for non-contact anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries3. A change of direction typically consists of three distinct phases: an initial deceleration phase, a plant step to redirect momentum, and a re-acceleration phase into the new direction4. During the deceleration phase, eccentric muscle contractions are required to reduce momentum, followed by a crucial plant step where the foot is positioned laterally or anteriorly to the center of mass. This positioning creates both braking and propulsive force components that redirect the athlete’s momentum1. The biomechanical demands of these phases vary considerably depending on the angle of direction change and the approach velocity, creating what has been termed the “angle-velocity trade-off”5.

Recent research has established that different COD angles place varying demands on the lower limb joints and muscles. Sharper COD angles (> 90°) are generally characterized as more “force-dominant,” requiring greater braking forces and more substantial redirection of the whole body. In contrast, more shallow angles (< 90°) are considered “velocity-dominant,” allowing for greater maintenance of momentum and requiring less dramatic deceleration1,3. This distinction is crucial because the force-dominant CODs may impose greater joint loads, potentially increasing injury risk, while velocity-dominant CODs might prioritize performance aspects at the expense of controlled movement.Standardized COD testing protocols have been proposed to systematically evaluate these movement patterns and identify biomechanical risk factors across different angle conditions6. Despite extensive research on COD biomechanics, critical gaps remain. First, no study has systematically integrated three-dimensional joint kinetics, kinematics, and neuromuscular activation patterns across the complete spectrum of COD angles (45°-180°). Second, previous research focused on single angles or compared only two angles, providing limited insight into dose-response relationships and threshold effects. Third, the mechanism by which different COD angles influence interjoint coordination and kinetic coupling remains poorly understood. Jones et al.4 examined braking characteristics during cutting maneuvers but focused primarily on performance rather than injury risk. Similarly, Schreurs et al.5 analyzed biomechanical differences between various COD angles but did not comprehensively assess injury risk metrics across the full spectrum of commonly performed angles in soccer.

The relationship between COD angle and injury risk is particularly relevant because non-contact ACL injuries frequently occur during cutting and pivoting movements. These injuries typically result from excessive knee valgus moments, tibial internal rotation, and anterior tibial shear forces—all of which may be influenced by the angle of direction change3. Understanding how different COD angles affect these biomechanical risk factors could provide valuable insights for injury prevention strategies and training program design.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of different COD angles (45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°) on lower limb joint loads and injury risk indicators in soccer players. We hypothesized that: (1) joint loading parameters and ACL injury risk indicators would increase progressively with COD angle; (2) COD angles ≥ 135° would produce biomechanical patterns exceeding established ACL injury risk thresholds; and (3) distinct neuromuscular control strategies would emerge across different angle categories, reflecting adaptive responses to varying mechanical demands.

Methods

Participants

This study focused on 26 male football players (mean age, height, and body mass were respectively 23.4 ± 2.8 years, 179.6 ± 5.8 cm, and 76.3 ± 7.5 kg) from semi-professional teams within the third to fifth divisions. All participants had no history of lower limb injuries in the last six months leading up to the testing date and regularly participated in football training (≥ 3 sessions per week) and competitions. The sample size was set based on prior literature analysing the biomechanics of change of direction (COD) movements3 and a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1) with 0.5 effect size, 0.05 alpha level, and 0.8 power, which indicated a minimum requirement of 24 participants. Among the participants, 22 were right-leg dominant and 4 were left-leg dominant, as determined by their preferred kicking leg. All participants performed COD tasks using their dominant leg as the plant leg. Preliminary analysis (independent t-tests) showed no significant differences in any biomechanical variables between right-leg and left-leg dominant participants (all p > 0.20), therefore data were pooled for the main analysis.

The inclusion criteria encompassed: (1) active participation in competitive football for a minimum of three years, (2) regular attendance in football training sessions at least thrice a week, (3) prior experience with directional changes typical of football, and (4) no significant lower limb injuries (greater than 2 weeks away from training) within the past year. Exclusion criteria included: (1) any currently existing musculoskeletal injury, or pain which has the potential to hinder performance, (2) previous ACL reconstruction, and (3) any condition that may worsen due to the testing protocols. These criteria were established based on recent literature to ensure that the participants could safely perform high-intensity Type 1 manoeuvring maneuvers without the risk of injury7.

Before engaging in any activity, all players were asked to fill out a health survey and receive a written approval to partake in the session. Operators collected stepping data from 23 participants (88.5%) who identified their right limb as their kicking limb, while three (11.5%) identified their left. This trend is in alignment with limb dominance data collected from elite football players where roughly 85% are right-foot dominant8. Evidence suggests that limb dominance affects the biomechanics and performance of change of direction movements9, raising concerns about injury risk analysis.

A complete overview of all participants is contained within Table 1. Participants demonstrated physical and performance attributes, such as countermovement jump height and 30 m sprint time, commensurate with semi-professional football players, reflecting an appropriate lower limb power and sprint capability relative to the requirements of the study.

The university ethics committee approved the testing procedures in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Each participant was required to avoid all forms of strenuous exercise for 48 h prior to the testing sessions, alcohol intake 24 h prior, as well as drink normally and properly hydrate within the timeframe leading up to the day of the test. Literature suggests these measures assist in optimising athletic output and enhance the certainty of biomechanical evaluations during vigorous movement10.

A preliminary analysis was conducted to examine potential differences between right-dominant (n = 23, 88.5%) and left-dominant (n = 3, 11.5%) participants. Independent t-tests revealed no significant differences in key outcome variables between limb dominance groups (peak KAM: p = 0.42; peak KRF: p = 0.38; SPEA: p = 0.51; DVI: p = 0.47). Therefore, data from both groups were pooled for subsequent analyses.

Experimental protocol and data collection

A within-subject repeated measures design was implemented to investigate the effects of different change of direction (COD) angles on lower limb joint loads and injury risk indicators in soccer players. This design was selected to control for inter-individual variability and maximize statistical power, following methodological recommendations for biomechanical research11. Each participant completed COD maneuvers at four different angles (45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°), with the testing order randomized to mitigate potential learning and fatigue effects.

The experimental protocol consisted of a 5 m approach sprint followed by a single COD maneuver at the designated angle and a 5 m exit sprint, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The surface-footwear combination (coefficient of friction µ = 0.82 ± 0.03) fell within the recommended range for soccer-specific movements. This standardized approach was adopted based on recent literature suggesting that approach distances of 5 m provide an optimal balance between ecological validity and experimental control when evaluating COD biomechanics12. Furthermore, the selected angles (45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°) represent a comprehensive spectrum of COD movements commonly performed during soccer match play and training, with research indicating that they place progressively greater demands on the lower limb joints5.

All testing was conducted on an indoor synthetic sports surface (Mondo Sportflex M, Mondo S.p.A., Italy) to ensure consistent friction characteristics and environmental conditions. The coefficient of friction of the surface was measured before testing (µ = 0.82 ± 0.03) using a validated portable device to ensure it was within the recommended range for safe COD performance. Testing sessions were scheduled at the same time of day (± 1 h) to minimize the potential influence of diurnal variations on neuromuscular performance. The temperature (21.3 ± 0.8 °C) and humidity (45 ± 5%) of the facility were controlled throughout all testing sessions.

Prior to data collection, participants completed two separate familiarization sessions (7–10 days and 3–5 days before testing, respectively) to minimize learning effects and ensure reliable execution of the COD tasks. During these sessions, participants practiced all four COD angles multiple times until consistent performance was achieved, as determined by COD completion time variation of less than 5% across three consecutive trials.

As shown in Fig. 1, timing gates were used to standardize approach velocity (4.5 ± 0.2 m/s) across all trials (technical specifications in Section “Statistical analysis”). Motion capture and force platform systems were synchronized to collect kinematic and kinetic data during the plant step (the final foot contact before changing direction) and the first re-acceleration step after the direction change. This approach allowed for comprehensive assessment of joint loading patterns during the most mechanically demanding portions of the COD maneuver.

A standardized testing protocol was implemented to ensure consistency across all participants.Following warm-up, participants wore their own soccer-specific footwear (19 cleats, 7 turf shoes) to maximize ecological validity. Preliminary analysis showed no significant differences between footwear types (all p > 0.25), confirming that footwear variation did not affect primary outcomes. Prior to data collection, participants completed a 15-minute progressive warm-up protocol consisting of general and specific components. The general component included 5 min of light jogging at self-selected speed (approximately 40–50% of perceived maximum effort), followed by dynamic stretching of the major lower limb muscle groups. The specific component comprised progressive linear sprints (60%, 70%, 80%, and 90% of perceived maximum effort) and submaximal COD practice trials at each of the four test angles. This structured warm-up approach has been demonstrated to optimize neuromuscular readiness for high-intensity directional changes while minimizing injury risk13.

Following warm-up, retroreflective markers were attached to participants at specific anatomical landmarks according to a modified Plug-in-Gait marker set for three-dimensional motion capture14. Markers were placed bilaterally on the anterior and posterior superior iliac spines, lateral thighs, lateral femoral epicondyles, lateral shanks, lateral malleoli, calcanei, and second metatarsal heads. Additional tracking markers were placed on the medial femoral epicondyles and medial malleoli during the static calibration trial. Simultaneously, wireless electromyography (EMG) sensors (Delsys Trigno, Delsys Inc., USA) were positioned on the dominant limb over the vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, rectus femoris, biceps femoris, semitendinosus, gluteus medius, adductor longus, medial gastrocnemius, and tibialis anterior muscles, following SENIAM guidelines for electrode placement. Maximum voluntary isometric contractions (MVICs) were performed for each muscle to normalize EMG signals during subsequent analyses.

For each COD angle (45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°), participants completed three maximum-effort trials, with a minimum of 2 min recovery between trials to minimize fatigue effects. The testing order of COD angles was randomized across participants to control for potential order effects. The approach velocity was standardized at 4.5 ± 0.2 m/s for all COD angles, monitored using infrared timing gates positioned 2 m before the COD point. This methodological decision ensured consistent initial momentum conditions across angles, allowing for direct comparison of biomechanical demands. However, we acknowledge that maintaining constant approach velocity across varying COD angles inherently results in different exit velocities, with sharper angles producing lower exit velocities due to greater momentum redirection requirements. This approach-exit velocity differential represents the natural biomechanical consequence of the angle-velocity trade-off and was considered an important aspect of the COD task rather than a confounding variable. Exit velocities were recorded and are reported in the results to provide transparency regarding this methodological consideration. Participants began each trial in a standing position with their front foot positioned 0.3 m behind the starting timing gate and were instructed to perform the approach sprint, COD movement, and exit sprint at maximum effort while maintaining proper technique. Explicit instructions were provided to plant the dominant foot on the force platform during the COD maneuver without targeting or adjusting their stride pattern, as research has shown that such adjustments can alter natural movement mechanics15.

A trial was considered valid when: (1) the participant’s dominant foot made complete contact with the force platform during the plant step, (2) the approach velocity fell within ± 5% of the participant’s mean approach velocity across trials for that particular COD angle, and (3) no observable targeting behavior or hesitation occurred during the maneuver. If a trial was deemed invalid, it was discarded and repeated after an appropriate recovery period. This ensured that the biomechanical data collected represented authentic movement patterns under controlled conditions.

Following completion of all COD trials, participants performed a standardized cool-down consisting of 5 min of light jogging and static stretching. Body mass and height were measured using a calibrated scale and stadiometer (Seca 769, Seca GmbH, Germany) at the conclusion of the testing session to avoid potential influence on warm-up efficiency.

To verify that experimental procedures did not introduce systematic effects, comprehensive quality control analyses were conducted. The order of COD angles was randomized for each participant, with minimum 2-minute rest periods between trials. Trial-to-trial variability analysis showed ground contact time CV < 2% and peak vertical GRF variation < 3%. Mixed-model ANOVA with trial order as a covariate revealed no significant effects (all p > 0.15, ηp2 < 0.03), confirming the absence of systematic fatigue or learning effects.

Three-dimensional (3D) kinematic data were collected using a 12-camera motion capture system (Vicon Nexus, Oxford Metrics, UK) operating at 250 Hz, a validated method for assessing lower limb biomechanics during COD tasks16. Prior to data collection, the capture volume (5 m × 2 m × 2 m) was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s guidelines, achieving a mean residual error of < 0.5 mm. The global coordinate system was established with the x-axis oriented in the direction of approach, the y-axis perpendicular to the direction of approach, and the z-axis vertical. This configuration allowed for consistent data interpretation across different COD angles, as recommended for standardized biomechanical assessment of directional changes17.

Ground reaction force (GRF) data were simultaneously recorded at 1000 Hz using a 900 × 600 mm force platform (AMTI, Watertown, MA, USA) embedded flush with the laboratory floor at the COD point. The force platform was covered with the same synthetic sports surface used throughout the testing area to ensure uniform surface characteristics. The force threshold for identifying ground contact events was set at 20 N. Kinematic and kinetic data were synchronized through the Vicon Nexus software interface using a direct analog-to-digital converter connection. A 4th-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cut-off frequency of 12 Hz for kinematic data and 50 Hz for kinetic data was applied based on residual analysis, which has been shown to optimally preserve the signal characteristics of rapid movements while reducing noise18.

Surface electromyography (sEMG) data were collected using a wireless 16-channel system (Delsys Trigno, Delsys Inc., USA) sampling at 2000 Hz. Following skin preparation (shaving, gentle abrasion, and cleaning with alcohol), electrodes were positioned on the muscle belly of each target muscle parallel to the muscle fiber orientation according to SENIAM guidelines. Electrode placement was confirmed through manual muscle testing and visual inspection of the signal. The sEMG signals were bandpass filtered (20–450 Hz), full-wave rectified, and smoothed using a root mean square (RMS) algorithm with a 50 ms window. To facilitate between-subject comparisons, the sEMG amplitude was normalized to the maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) values obtained during the pre-testing calibration procedures.

Approach and exit velocities were measured using infrared timing gates (Brower Timing Systems, USA) positioned at the start, COD point, and finish to measure approach velocity (4.5 ± 0.2 m/s, standardized across all angles) and total COD performance time. Gates were positioned at 0 m (start), 2 m before the COD point, at the COD point, and 2 m after the COD point. This multi-gate setup provided detailed information about the deceleration and acceleration characteristics associated with each COD angle, allowing for precise quantification of the velocity profile throughout the movement19. The time to complete each COD maneuver was calculated as the interval between breaking the timing gate beam 2 m before the COD point and breaking the beam 2 m after the COD point.

For each valid trial, a one-second data window was captured, centered on the instant of peak vertical GRF during the plant step. This time window was selected to ensure capture of the entire weight acceptance and propulsive phases of the COD movement, which contain the critical loading events most relevant to joint load and injury risk assessment. All data were exported to Visual3D software (C-Motion Inc., Germantown, MD, USA) for subsequent processing and analysis.

Variables

The definitions of the lower limb kinematic variables analyzed in this study, including joint angles and center-of-mass displacement, are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Biomechanical variables were calculated for the plant step of each COD maneuver, with a focus on parameters associated with joint loading and established injury risk indicators. All joint kinetics were calculated using inverse dynamics procedures within Visual3D software and normalized to body mass (BM) to facilitate between-subject comparisons, as recommended in current biomechanical research20.

Kinematic variables

Lower limb joint angles were calculated using a Cardan rotation sequence (X-Y-Z), corresponding to flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, and internal/external rotation. Key kinematic variables included peak ankle dorsiflexion, knee flexion, knee abduction (valgus), hip flexion, hip abduction, and hip internal rotation angles during the weight acceptance phase of the plant step. The weight acceptance phase was defined as the period from initial contact to the instant of peak knee flexion21.

For each parameter, the mean value across three valid trials for each COD angle was used for statistical analysis. Reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), with all variables demonstrating good to excellent reliability (ICC range: 0.82–0.95).

Statistical analysis

Prior to the main analysis, all data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation) were calculated for all variables across the four COD angles (45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°). The reliability of the biomechanical variables was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC3,1) with 95% confidence intervals, with values interpreted as poor (< 0.50), moderate (0.50–0.74), good (0.75–0.89), or excellent (≥ 0.90) according to established guidelines23.

To examine the effect of COD angle on joint loading and injury risk indicators, a one-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted with COD angle as the within-subject factor (45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°). The assumption of sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test, and when violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. For significant main effects, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Holm-Bonferroni method to control for Type I error inflation while maintaining statistical power, an approach recommended for biomechanical analyses with multiple outcome variables22.Exit velocity was included as a covariate in preliminary analyses to examine whether the angle-dependent differences in exit velocity (resulting from constant approach velocity) significantly influenced the observed joint loading patterns. These analyses confirmed that while exit velocity was significantly correlated with COD angle (r = – 0.78, p < 0.001), the main effects of COD angle on joint loading variables remained significant even after controlling for exit velocity (all p < 0.001), indicating that the angle-specific biomechanical demands were independent of the exit velocity differences.

Effect sizes for the main effects were calculated using partial eta squared (η²p), with values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively. For pairwise comparisons, standardized mean differences (Hedges’ g) were calculated, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 considered small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Additionally, minimal detectable differences (MDD) were calculated at the 95% confidence level to determine the threshold of meaningful change for each variable.

To explore relationships between joint loading variables and injury risk indicators, Pearson’s product-moment correlation coefficients (r) were calculated. Correlation strengths were classified as trivial (r < 0.1), small (0.1 ≤ r < 0.3), moderate (0.3 ≤ r < 0.5), large (0.5 ≤ r < 0.7), very large (0.7 ≤ r < 0.9), or nearly perfect (r ≥ 0.9)23. Multiple regression analyses were performed to identify which biomechanical variables were the strongest predictors of the primary injury risk indicators (DVI and KRF) at each COD angle.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 28.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with the significance level set at α = 0.05. Graphical representations of the data were created using GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) to illustrate the relationships between COD angles and the biomechanical variables of interest.

Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation with 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated using the t-distribution. Individual participant data plots were examined to assess inter-individual variability in movement strategies, and coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated for key variables to quantify the magnitude of inter-participant variability.

Results

Joint kinematics

Significant main effects were noted on COD angle for all lower limb joint kinematics during the plant step (p < 0.001, η²p = 0.62–0.89). As shown in Table 2, the peak knee flexion angle was observed to progressively increase alongside COD angles, where the 180° condition demonstrated significantly greater knee flexion (63.4 ± 7.3°) than the 45° (38.7 ± 5.2°), 90° (47.6 ± 6.1°), and 135° (55.8 ± 6.8°) conditions (All p < 0.001). The greater knee flexion with sharper COD angles may act as a protective neuromuscular mechanism to offload the ligaments.

Peak knee abduction angle (valgus) also had a significant angle-dependent response with greater angles being recorded at 135° (11.4 ± 3.1°) and 180° (12.8 ± 3.4°) compared to 45° (5.3 ± 2.1°) and 90° (8.6 ± 2.7°) (all p < 0.01). These results support the idea that biomechanical demands are increased at a greater rate with increased angles of COD, especially past 90°. Furthermore, during the 135° and 180° conditions, the amount of frontal plane knee motion was greater than the 7–9° limit that was associated with increased ACL injury risk in prior prospective research.

Flexion and hip rotation also mirrored the hip and knee interaction in coordination as there was a gradual increase in the magnitude of peak hip flexion and abduction as the COD angle increased. The peak value for hip internal rotation was significantly greater at both 135° (13.4 ± 3.8°) and 180° (15.7 ± 4.1°) compared with the 45° value (7.2 ± 2.9°) (p < 0.001). No significant difference was found between the 135° and 180° conditions concerning hip internal rotation (p = 0.092). These positions of internal hip rotation, along with the accentuated knee abduction during the two most acute configurations of the COD, constitute an unfavourable alignment of the lower limb which may predispose the joints to ACL injury mechanisms.

The angle for peak ankle dorsiflexion increased alongside the COD angle, beginning at 22.4 ± 3.1° in the 45° condition up to 35.6 ± 4.2° in the 180° condition (p < 0.001). The increasing knee dorsiflexion during sharper alley turns is more likely to help increase knee flexion that is needed to slow down and turn the body through a greater angle.

The period to attain maximum joint angles (given as a percentage of stance phase) also showed variation with the COD angle. For the 45° and 90° conditions, peak knee and hip flexion were attained at roughly 38–42% of the stance phase, while in the 135° and 180° conditions, these peaks were reached later at 47–52% of the stance. This change in timing indicates different strategies for neuromuscular control of the velocity-dominant (45° and 90°) and force-dominant (135° and 180°) COD tasks.

Joint kinetics and load

All lower limb loading parameters were affected by the main as well as interaction effects of COD angle with p values less than 0.001 and eta-squared values ranging from 0.71 to 0.92. The data in Table 3 also illustrates that peak joint loads increased steadily as grace angles increased; the largest increases were seen in 45°, 135° and 180° angles.

The highest knee rotational moment also showed the most profound angle dependent response; it increased from 0.93 ± 0.21 N·m/kg in the 45° position to 2.24 ± 0.38 N·m/kg in 180°. This highlighted a 141% increase in frontal plane knee loading between shallow versus sharp COD angles. As for KAM, post-hoc analysis showed significant KAM differences at all COD angles (p-values below 0.01) except between 135° and 180° where p = 0.081. Beyond peak values, temporal analysis revealed distinct loading profiles across COD angles (Fig. 3). The loading slope from initial contact to peak KAM increased progressively with COD angle: 45° (18.7 ± 4.2 N·m/kg/s), 90° (32.4 ± 6.8 N·m/kg/s), 135° (51.2 ± 9.3 N·m/kg/s), and 180° (58.6 ± 11.4 N·m/kg/s) (p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.81). The timing of peak KAM occurred progressively earlier in the stance phase as COD angle increased: 45° (42.3 ± 5.1% of stance), 90° (38.7 ± 4.8%), 135° (34.2 ± 4.5%), and 180° (31.8 ± 4.2%) (p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.76). The duration of elevated loading (defined as KAM > 80% of peak value) was significantly prolonged at sharper COD angles: 45° (8.3 ± 2.1% of stance), 90° (12.7 ± 3.2%), 135° (18.4 ± 3.8%), and 180° (21.6 ± 4.3%) (p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.79). These temporal characteristics indicate that sharper CODs not only produce higher peak loads but also expose the knee joint to elevated loading for longer durations with more rapid loading onset.

As the peak knee extension moment (KEM) exponentially increased with the change of the angle of change of direction (COD) with the 180° condition, 3.28 ± 0.42 N·m/kg exhibited 145% greater sagittal plane knee loading compared to the 45° condition, which had 1.34 ± 0.31 N·m/kg. It is important to note that the increase in KEM was not evenly distributed across all COD angles. The 90° and 135° conditions displayed a disproportionately larger increase of 60% compared to the 45°–90° increase of 32% and the 135°–180° increase of 21%.

The parameters determining the loading at the hip joint followed the same trends as those at the knee joint. From the 45° condition to the 180° condition, peak hip adduction moment increased significantly with COD angle, from 0.67 ± 0.19 N·m/kg to 1.73 ± 0.35 N·m/kg (p < 0.001). Of note is the fact that of all the joint loading parameters, peak hip internal rotation moment had the highest relative increase of 194% from 45° to 180°, suggesting that the loading rotationally at the hip joint may be particularly responsive to changes in COD angle.

Knee joint resultant force (KRF), a recording indicating a singular measurement of the mechanical load placed upon the knee, was significantly increased with regard to the change of direction (COD) angle (p < 0.001, η²p = 0.89). The average KRF with the 180-degree condition (5.83 ± 0.74 BW) was 132% higher than that of the 45-degree condition (2.51 ± 0.43 BW). Further, the impulse of the plantar GRF also increased with the increase of the COD angle which signifies increased braking force needed to redirect inertia at sharper angles.

Muscle activation patterns

Surface electromyography (sEMG) data showed significant main effects of COD angle on the muscle activation patterns during the plant step (p < 0.001, η²p = 0.58–0.87). It is apparent from Table 4 that there are differences in the four COD angles regarding neuromuscular strategies, with general patterns showing an increase in the magnitude of muscle activation with the increase in COD angle.

Peak activation of the quadriceps (vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, and rectus femoris) exhibited a significant response to the angle of the COD with the greatest activation at the sharpest COD angles (135° and 180°) which were 32–47% greater than the 45° condition. Enhanced quadriceps activation is likely a reflection of the greater eccentric demands, in terms of deceleration, coupled with more substantial knee extension moment requirement during the plant step of sharper CODs. Also notable, vastus medialis had the greatest increase in activation between the 45° and 180° conditions of 47% compared to the increases of 38% and 32% observed in vastus lateralis and rectus femoris respectively.

The hamstrings, specifically the biceps femoris and semitendinosus, had an even more pronounced increase in activation proportional to the increase in COD angle, which was 52–57% higher for the 180° condition compared to the 45° condition. This might be an increase in activation of the hamstrings could be a protective neuromuscular strategy to counteract anterior tibial translation while attempting to stabilise the knee joint and provide dynamic stability during high-load plant steps. The muscle semitendinosus, which is known to control knee valgus extensively during dynamic movements, showed the greatest relative increase in activation among all the muscles.

As for the muscles of the lower leg, the increase in activation was significantly pronounced for the medial gastrocnemius and tibialis anterior with 41% and 35% greater activation during the 180° condition compared with the 45° condition, respectively. With sharper CODs, the increase in the angle of ankle dorsiflexion tends to increase and therefore greater activation of triceps surae would be needed to control ankle motion as well as the ground reaction forces. The adductor longus showed a distinctly remarkable 62% increase in activation from the 45° to 180° conditions which may be associated with its function in stabilising the hip joint during advanced hip abduction during sharper CODs.

Figure 5 depicts how the angles of change of direction (COD) correlate with the activation of specific lower limb muscles. The graph shows muscle activation increasing with COD angle. Increases were greatest for the hamstrings and the adductor longus. With regards to the analysis of the sequence of muscle activation, the maximal activation of the hamstrings was earlier relative to the stance phase during sharper CODs (19–22% of stance) than for the 45° condition (27–30% of stance). This may suggest an anticipatory high-stabilisation load step plant strategy.

Ankle joint kinetics

Surface electromyography (sEMG) data showed significant main effects of COD angle on ankle joint loading parameters during the plant step (p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.68–0.81). Table 5 presents the ankle kinetic variables across the four COD angles. Peak ankle plantarflexion moment increased progressively with COD angle, from 2.1 ± 0.4 N·m/kg at 45° to 3.2 ± 0.7 N·m/kg at 180° (p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.76), representing a 52% increase. Post-hoc analysis revealed significant differences between all angle pairs (all p < 0.01) except 45° vs. 90° (p = 0.08). This progressive increase in plantarflexion moment reflects the greater demands on the gastrocnemius-soleus complex for controlling ankle motion and generating propulsive forces during sharper CODs. Peak ankle inversion moment also increased significantly with sharper angles, from 0.3 ± 0.1 N·m/kg at 45° to 0.7 ± 0.2 N·m/kg at 180° (p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.68), representing a 133% increase. The ankle inversion moment loading rate followed a similar pattern, with the 180° condition producing rates 89% higher than the 45° condition (180°: 18.4 ± 4.2 N·m/kg/s vs. 45°: 9.7 ± 2.8 N·m/kg/s; p < 0.001). These findings suggest increased mediolateral ankle stability demands during force-dominant CODs, highlighting the importance of ankle evertor strength in injury prevention programs.

The ankle plantarflexion moment loading rate also demonstrated significant angle-dependent increases (45°: 52.3 ± 11.4 N·m/kg/s; 180°: 87.6 ± 18.3 N·m/kg/s; p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.72). The correlation between ankle plantarflexion moment and knee extension moment was strong across all angles (r = 0.71–0.78, all p < 0.001), suggesting coordinated sagittal plane loading strategies across the lower limb joints.

Injury risk indicators

Examination of injury risk factors showed that COD angle had statistically significant main effects on all variables tested (p < 0.001, η2p = 0.69–0.91). In ACL multifactorial risk measures, as described in Table 6, with increasing COD angle, the composite measures of the ACL injury risk showed a stepwise increase, where the largest changes were seen between 45° and the two sharpest CODs: 135° and 180°.

The dynamic valgus index (DVI), which includes knee movement in the frontal and transverse planes, demonstrated the greatest increase at the most extreme angles of COD. The average DVI in the 180° position (7.42 ± 0.78) was 152% higher than in the 45° position (2.94 ± 0.53). All pairwise comparisons found significant differences across all levels of COD angles (all p < 0.01), with the largest increase from 90° to 135° position, which was an increase of 65%. The mean DVI scores for 135° and 180° fell within the defined range of “high risk” (DVI > 5.0) while the “moderate risk” average was for the 90° condition (3.0–5.0) and “low risk” condition was 45° (< 3.0).

Temporal analysis of DVI evolution during the stance phase revealed that the rate of DVI accumulation (rise characteristics) differed substantially across COD angles. The slope of DVI increase from initial contact to peak was 2.4 times steeper at 180° compared to 45° (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the decay phase following peak DVI was prolonged at sharper angles, with DVI remaining above baseline levels for 68.2 ± 7.3% of stance at 180° compared to 41.5 ± 6.8% at 45° (p < 0.001). This extended duration of elevated frontal plane loading at sharper COD angles may compound injury risk through sustained tissue stress exposure.

The knee abduction angle at initial contact increased progressively with the COD angle, going from 3.48 ± 1.56° during the 45° condition to 8.73 ± 2.14° during the 180° condition, representing a 151% increase. Also, the peak knee abduction angle during weight acceptance showed a 141% increase between the 45° and 180° conditions. It is likely that the increased sharp CODs and enhanced frontal plane excursions of the knee contributed to the increased loading and heightened joint kinetics observed.

The Spea (sagittal plane energy absorption) at the knee joint which reflects the eccentric work performed in the deceleration phase, showed the greatest relative increase among all measured parameters, with value during the 180° condition increased by 187% compared to the 45° condition. This sharp increase in energy absorption aligned with the increase in the COD angle corroborating greater braking forces needed when redirecting momentum.

Analysis of individual biomechanical risk factors showed that 19% of participants displayed high-risk biomechanics (≥ 3 risk factors exceeding established thresholds) during the 45° condition which increased to 42% during the 90°, 73% during the 135°, and 88% during the 180° condition. Most common high-risk movements during the 135° and 180° conditions included excessive knee valgus alignment, restricted knee flexion at initial contact, and escalated vertical ground reaction force loading rate knee.

It can be seen from the graph that with increasing COD angle, risk of injury parameters increase exponentially, especially between 90° and 135° conditions. The error bars are the standard deviation which displays how varied the responses to the different angles of COD are concerning injury risk.

To provide a comprehensive visualization of the multidimensional injury risk profile, Fig. 7 presents a radar plot showing normalized values (z-scores) for multiple injury risk indicators across all COD angles. The radar plot clearly demonstrates that as COD angle increases, the overall risk profile expands substantially. At 45°, the risk profile remains compact with all indicators below or near baseline levels. In contrast, at 180°, the shaded area encompasses nearly the entire plot, indicating elevated values across all six risk markers (KAM, SPEA, DVI, KRF, peak vGRF loading rate, and KAM impulse). Notably, the transition from 90° to 135° shows the most dramatic expansion in the risk profile, consistent with the threshold effect observed in the individual injury risk indicators. This integrated visualization reinforces that sharper COD angles create a multifactorial high-risk biomechanical environment rather than simply elevating individual risk markers in isolation.

Discussion

Hypothesis evaluation

The results of this study provided strong support for our three primary hypotheses. First, our hypothesis that joint loading parameters and ACL injury risk indicators would increase progressively with COD angle was confirmed. Peak knee abduction moment increased by 141% from 45° to 180° (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.87), and the dynamic valgus index demonstrated a 152% increase across the same angle range (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.91). All joint kinetic and kinematic parameters showed significant angle-dependent increases, with large effect sizes (ηp² = 0.69–0.91), confirming a clear dose-response relationship between COD angle and biomechanical loading. Second, our hypothesis that COD angles ≥ 135° would produce biomechanical patterns exceeding established ACL injury risk thresholds was supported. The DVI scores for both 135° (7.18 ± 0.72) and 180° (7.42 ± 0.78) conditions exceeded the high-risk threshold of 5.0. Additionally, 73% of participants during the 135° condition and 88% during the 180° condition displayed high-risk biomechanics (≥ 3 risk factors exceeding established thresholds), compared to only 19% during the 45° condition. Peak knee abduction angles during both 135° and 180° conditions (11.37° and 12.81°, respectively) exceeded the 7–9° threshold associated with increased ACL injury risk in prospective research. Third, our hypothesis regarding distinct neuromuscular control strategies across different angle categories was partially supported. EMG analysis revealed earlier hamstring activation onset during sharper CODs (135° and 180°), occurring at 12.3 ± 3.4% of stance phase compared to 18.7 ± 4.2% during 45° CODs (p < 0.001). Peak hamstring activation amplitude increased by 67% from 45° to 180° conditions. However, the expected differential recruitment patterns between velocity-dominant (≤ 90°) and force-dominant (≥ 135°) CODs were less pronounced than hypothesized, with the most dramatic neuromuscular adaptations occurring at the 90° to 135° transition rather than at the 90° threshold. The KAM ratio remained relatively consistent across angles (0.68–0.75), suggesting that while absolute frontal plane loading increased, the relative contribution of frontal versus sagittal plane moments was maintained through coordinated neuromuscular control strategies.

Joint load differences across COD angles

The specific focus of this research was the impact different change of direction (COD) angles may have on the lower limb joint loads and soccer players’ injury risk markers. It has been established that there is an angle-dependent response for all joint loading parameters and that greater loads are induced when the COD angle increases from 45° to 180°. The knee joint exhibited the greatest increase in frontal and sagittal plane loading during the most extreme COD angles.

The peak knee KAM increased by 141%, comparing the 45° and 180° conditions (0.93 vs. 2.24 N·m/kg), which provides evidence towards our hypothesis expecting joint loads to increase with higher COD angles. The increase in the frontal plane knee loading also correlates to the increased lateral foot plant positioning needed to be achieved during sharper CODs to provide adequate mediolateral force needed to momentum shift. Concerning injury risk, the increased KAM in the 135° and 180° conditions is worrisome due to excessive frontal plane knee loading being identified as a key non-contact ACL injury risk factor.

Interestingly, some of the greatest increases in knee abduction moment occurred at the 90°–135° interval (+ 47%), indicating the potential for a threshold effect where dramatic increases in frontal plane loading occur after the 90° COD angle. This nonlinear response likely reflects several interrelated biomechanical mechanisms. First, at angles exceeding 90°, the center of mass (COM) must be displaced laterally beyond the base of support to a much greater extent, creating larger moment arms for the ground reaction force vector relative to the knee joint21. Second, the transition from velocity-dominant (≤ 90°) to force-dominant (> 90°) CODs necessitates a fundamental shift in intersegmental coordination strategies. At sharper angles, the hip and trunk must rotate more substantially relative to the lower limb, potentially limiting the hip’s contribution to force absorption and placing disproportionate demands on the knee joint4,21. Third, foot placement strategies change markedly at the 90° threshold. Our kinematic data showed that at 135° and 180°, the foot was positioned significantly more lateral to the COM (mean lateral displacement: 45°: 0.18 ± 0.04 m; 135°: 0.34 ± 0.06 m; p < 0.001), increasing the frontal plane moment arm. Additionally, at sharper angles, athletes demonstrated reduced knee flexion at initial contact (45°: 21.5°; 135°: 32.4°), which has been shown to diminish the knee’s ability to attenuate impact forces through sagittal plane motion, forcing greater reliance on frontal plane control mechanisms21. This biomechanical constraint may explain why the KAM increases so dramatically beyond 90°—the system becomes mechanically disadvantaged for frontal plane load distribution. The smaller increases in KAM beyond the 135°–180° range (+ 8%) suggest a plateau effect at the most extreme COD angles. This likely results from a combination of reduced approach velocity, heightened pre-COD deceleration, and possible neuromuscular saturation where maximal muscle activation capacities are reached. Athletes may be unable to generate significantly greater frontal plane moments beyond a certain threshold due to physiological and biomechanical constraints5.

The temporal loading characteristics observed in this study provide mechanistic insights beyond what peak values alone can reveal. The earlier timing of peak KAM at sharper COD angles (31.8% vs. 42.3% of stance phase for 180° vs. 45°) reflects fundamental differences in neuromuscular control strategies. At sharper angles, athletes must generate larger braking forces within a compressed timeframe, resulting in steeper loading slopes and earlier peak attainment. This rapid loading onset may challenge the neuromuscular system’s ability to implement protective muscle pre-activation strategies, potentially increasing ACL loading before adequate dynamic stabilization can be achieved3,22.

The prolonged duration of elevated KAM (> 80% of peak) at sharper COD angles (21.6% vs. 8.3% of stance for 180° vs. 45°) has important implications for cumulative tissue loading. While peak values are often emphasized in injury risk assessment, the total impulse of frontal plane loading—integrating both magnitude and duration—may better reflect actual ACL strain exposure20. Our findings demonstrate that sharper CODs create a ‘double jeopardy’ scenario: not only are peak loads higher, but elevated loading is sustained for substantially longer portions of the stance phase, potentially exhausting the protective capacity of active neuromuscular stabilizers.

Extending this reasoning, the knee extension moment (KEM) exhibited the same 145% angle-dependent increase between the 45° and 180° conditions. As far as sagittal plane loading is concerned, this reinforces the exacerbated braking and deceleration performance demand during sharper changes of direction (CODs). The disproportionate increase in KEM between the 90° and 135° conditions further indicates the threshold effect for frontal plane loading was KEM and is too dependent on the angle of the COD yielding significant change at 90 degrees.

Loading parameters for the hip joint demonstrated a similar pattern to the knee joint, where the peak hip adduction moment increased by 158% between the 45° and 180° conditions. This increase in frontal plane hip loading is probably due to the sharper hip abduction positions needed during sharper changes of direction (CODs) in order to maintain control and balance while redirecting momentum. The increase in internal rotation moment at the hip (194% between 45° and 180° conditions) indicates that rotational hip loading is likely sensitive to the angle of change of direction due to the increase in redirection control rotational force needed during sharper turns.

Knee joint resultant force (KRF) or the total mechanical load experienced by the knee joint increased by 132% between the 45° and 180° conditions. This single measure of KRF and joint moment gives the overall degree of joint stress and highlights the increased demands on the knee joint during sharp changes of direction (COD). Looking at the chronic impact on joint health, the frequent high-load controlled change of direction (COD) moves, especially those beyond 90 degrees, without proper load monitoring and technique adjustment could pave the way for degenerative joint diseases over time.

Collectively, the results suggest that the relationship between the angle of change of direction (COD) and joint loading is not linear, in that disproportionately greater increases in loading occur after an angle of 90 degrees is reached. This unique noticing adds important insight with respect to performance enhancement and injury prevention in soccer, indicating that there should be specific considerations for biomechanical demand in training and assessment of directional change.

An intriguing finding was the relative consistency of the KAM ratio across all COD angles (range: 0.68–0.75), despite substantial increases in absolute frontal plane loading. Rather than representing an inconsistency in the data, this pattern may reveal an important compensatory strategy for load redistribution. The KAM ratio, calculated as the proportion of frontal plane moment relative to total knee joint loading, remained stable because both frontal and sagittal plane moments increased proportionally with COD angle. This suggests that the neuromuscular system maintains a consistent balance between frontal and sagittal plane loading strategies across varying COD demands, potentially through coordinated hip-knee-ankle coupling and muscle coactivation patterns. The maintenance of this ratio may represent an optimized load-sharing strategy that prevents excessive reliance on any single plane of motion, thereby distributing mechanical stress more uniformly across joint structures. From an injury prevention perspective, disruption of this ratio - such as disproportionate increases in frontal plane loading without corresponding sagittal plane compensation - may indicate compromised neuromuscular control and elevated injury risk. Future research should investigate whether training interventions can optimize this ratio to enhance load distribution efficiency and reduce peak stresses on vulnerable structures like the ACL.

Implications for injury risk

The increase of joint loading parameters with greater COD angles is associated with biomechanical patterns previously identified as ACL injury risk markers, though it is important to distinguish between biomechanical risk indicators and actual injury prediction. Examination of established biomechanical risk factors showed that both the 135° and 180° COD conditions produced movement patterns that exceeded thresholds associated with elevated injury risk in prospective research. The Dynamic Valgus Index (DVI) - a composite measure of knee motion in the frontal and transverse planes - increased 152% between the 45° and 180° conditions and exceeded the 5.0 threshold during the 135° and 180° conditions. This threshold has been linked to increased ACL injury incidence in prospective studies of athletes22, though the relationship between DVI and injury is probabilistic rather than deterministic.

It is critical to note that these biomechanical risk markers reflect acute loading exposure during individual COD tasks, but actual injury risk depends on multiple factors including cumulative load exposure, athlete conditioning status, fatigue levels, movement technique quality, and neuromuscular control capabilities22. Therefore, while our findings identify angle-specific biomechanical patterns that align with established risk markers, they cannot directly predict injury likelihood for individual athletes. Rather, these results should inform risk assessment strategies and guide the development of targeted injury prevention interventions.

The given increases in the peak knee abduction angle (141% between the 45° and 180° conditions) and the peak knee abduction moment (141% between the 45° and 180° conditions) are worsening from an injury risk perspective. These frontal plane parameters have emerged from prospective and retrospective studies as biomechanical markers associated with increased ACL injury incidence, though the mechanisms linking these movement patterns to tissue failure remain incompletely understood. During tighter CODs, the further out (laterally) the foot is positioned relative to the centre of mass increases the distance about the knee joint where the ground reaction force vector will be applied, potentially increasing frontal plane loading. This mechanical disadvantage is likely worsened by the reduced knee flexion at the point of contact usually seen during these high-speed cutting maneuvers, which might restrict the ability of the knee due to the controlled movement to rely on the active hamstrings to provide dynamic stability against rotational forces.

The sharp increase in sagittal plane energy absorption (SPEA) relative to increasing COD angle (187% between 45° and 180° conditions) indicates greater eccentric deceleration demand on the quadriceps during sharper CODs. Although this increase in eccentric loading is important for managing deceleration, it may pose an injury risk by increasing anterior tibial shear force, especially in the absence of adequate hamstring co-activation. The knee extensor moment ratio rose from 1.27 in the 45° condition to 2.04 in the 180° condition, demonstrating greater dependence on knee extensor rather than hip extensor movements in sharper CODs. This strategy has been shown to increase ACL force and presents an eligible risk factor likely mitigable with dynamic neuromuscular training.

From a temporal perspective, the loading trajectory during sharper CODs follows a distinctive pattern that may exacerbate ACL injury risk. The steeper loading slope combined with earlier peak timing creates a brief but critical window (~ 10–15% of stance phase) during which maximal loading occurs before complete neuromuscular preparation. This temporal mismatch between loading demand and protective muscle activation represents a key mechanism through which sharper COD angles may elevate injury risk. Training interventions targeting anticipatory muscle activation and feed-forward control may help athletes better prepare for the rapid loading demands of force-dominant CODs12,23.

The proportion of participants exhibiting high-risk biomechanics (≥ 3 risk factors exceeding established thresholds) enhanced substantially with the increase in COD angle, ranging from 19% during the 45° condition to 88% during the 180° condition. This observation implies that sharper CODs not only amplify the magnitude of individual risk factors but also heighten the incidence of multiple risk factors occurring simultaneously, creating what could be termed a “perfect storm” for injury. During the 135° and 180° conditions, the most prevalent high-risk movement patterns included excessive knee valgus alignment, limited knee flexion at the initial contact, and high vertical ground reaction force loading rates.

From a practical standpoint, these results indicate that the angle of COD is a primary ACL injury risk determinant during soccer-specific actions. The disproportionate increases in risk factors between the 90° and 135° conditions suggest that sharp CODs exceeding 90° require heightened focus in injury prevention strategies. During both training and gameplay, soccer players routinely execute bends and turns at various angles. Research shows that elite players are capable of performing 600–800 directional changes per match. If a significant proportion of these CODs happen at greater than 90° angles, the accumulated stress on the ACL and other knee components would be considerable.

The KAM ratio was not consistent with the angle-dependent behaviour exhibited by the other risk metrics, since KAM values were similar across all COD angles. This means that the KAM ratio was not consistent with other risk indicators spatial metrics. The pattern suggests that while the total strength of both frontal and sagittal plane forces increase with the COD angle, the ratio of contribution from frontal to sagittal plane loading is fairly constant. This observation seems to contradict the idea that sharper CODs would involve a disproportionately greater frontal to sagittal plane loading. Rather, it would appear that the overall proportional increase of loading in multiple axes of motion increases regardless of dominance.

These observations suggest the development of a specific injury prevention strategy targeting the identified angles of increased risk for football, particularly focused on developing the neuromuscular control and strength to safely perform sharper CADs. Athletes that commonly execute 135° and 180° pivots during competition may be influenced positively by training strategies aimed at altering high-risk movement patterns and enhancing the ability to sustain large joint forces associated with these highly dynamic movements.

Neuromuscular control strategies and adaptive responses

The EMG findings revealed sophisticated neuromuscular adaptations to increasing COD angles that extend beyond simple increases in muscle activation magnitude. The earlier hamstring activation onset observed during sharper CODs (12.3 ± 3.4% of stance phase at 180° vs. 18.7 ± 4.2% at 45°; p < 0.001) represents a critical pre-activation strategy for dynamic knee stabilization. Importantly, despite this earlier activation, peak hamstring EMG still occurred after peak KAM at sharper angles (35.4 ± 4.8% vs. 31.8 ± 4.2% of stance for hamstring peak vs. KAM peak at 180°), suggesting a persistent temporal lag between external loading and protective muscle response. This neuromuscular timing deficit may represent a modifiable risk factor amenable to targeted training interventions focusing on feed-forward muscle activation patterns. This temporal shift in muscle recruitment likely serves multiple functional purposes. First, early hamstring activation increases knee joint stiffness prior to peak loading, which may help resist anterior tibial translation and excessive knee valgus moments during the weight acceptance phase12,23. Second, the hamstrings act as dynamic stabilizers that directly oppose quadriceps-induced anterior tibial shear forces, a primary mechanism of ACL loading during COD tasks22.

The 67% increase in peak hamstring activation amplitude from 45° to 180° conditions reflects the escalating demands for active knee joint stabilization during force-dominant CODs. However, the magnitude of this increase (67%) was notably less than the corresponding increases in knee extension moment (145%) and knee abduction moment (141%), suggesting that reliance on passive structures (ligaments, joint capsule) may increase disproportionately at sharper angles when muscle force production reaches physiological limits. This neuromuscular-structural load sharing has important implications for ACL injury risk, as excessive reliance on passive restraints can lead to tissue failure under high-load conditions3,22.

The increased adductor longus activity during sharper CODs (135° and 180° conditions showed 43% and 58% increases in peak activation, respectively, compared to 45°) likely represents a compensatory strategy for mediolateral pelvis and hip stabilization. During force-dominant CODs, the hip adductors must counteract large hip abduction moments while simultaneously contributing to hip internal rotation control23. The strong correlation between hip adduction moment and adductor EMG amplitude (r = 0.76, p < 0.001) suggests that increased hip adductor activity is a direct response to escalating frontal plane demands rather than a generalized activation increase.

Interestingly, the gastrocnemius and soleus demonstrated relatively modest activation increases across COD angles (26% and 31%, respectively, from 45° to 180°), despite substantial increases in ankle plantarflexion moment (67% increase). This finding may indicate a shift toward stiffer ankle strategies at sharper angles, where the ankle complex acts more as a rigid lever to transmit forces rather than actively modulating through large ranges of motion. This interpretation is supported by our kinematic data showing increased ankle dorsiflexion angles but relatively unchanged ankle angular velocities at sharp COD angles.

From a motor control perspective, these neuromuscular patterns align with the concept of muscle synergies - coordinated activation of muscle groups that work together to accomplish task-specific goals23. The transition from velocity-dominant to force-dominant CODs appears to involve not just quantitative increases in muscle activation, but qualitative changes in muscle coordination patterns. Future research employing muscle synergy analysis or uncontrolled manifold approaches could provide deeper insights into how the central nervous system reorganizes neuromuscular control strategies across different COD angles and how these strategies relate to injury risk.

Neuromuscular control strategies

Our EMG analysis demonstrated that different angles of Change of Direction (CODs) have distinct neuromuscular control strategies. The increased activation of motor units to sharper COD angles suggests adaptive motor control strategies responding to increasing biomechanical demands that accompany more extreme directional changes. Peak quadriceps activation (vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, and rectus femoris) increased by 32–47% from the 45° to the 180° condition likely due to greater eccentric deceleration loading and increased knee extension moment during the plant step phase associated with sharper CODs.

Notably, distinct muscle groups exhibited different activation adaptation patterns across the COD angles. Hamstring activation (biceps femoris and semitendinosus) was the most responsive to wider COD angles, exhibiting 52–57% greater activation in the 180° condition compared to the 45° condition. This increase in hamstrings recruitment may reflect the enhanced neuromuscular strategy that dynamically stabilises the knee joint to mitigate potential knee joint injuries during high-load phases. The semitendinosus, which has an important role in restraining the knee from vigorous valgus motion, had the highest increase in activation of 57%. Relatively, this increase in the activation of knee stabilisers suggests the possibility of their critical role in the extreme angles of COD in non-accelerated movements.

There was also substantial variation in the timing of peak muscle activation across different COD angles. Peak hamstring muscle activation occurred earlier in the stance phase for sharper CODs (19–22% of stance) relative to the 45° condition (27–30% of stance), which suggests an anticipatory stabilisation strategy whereby a neuromuscular system pre-activates key stabilisers before the maximum loading phase. This early activation sequence may be a protective strategy to improve active and dynamic joint stability prior to experiencing maximum ground reaction forces.

There were significant differences in co-activation ratios analysed in relation to different COD angles. The 135° and 180° conditions had significantly greater hamstring co-activation ratios compared to the 45° condition (0.71 ± 0.12) with 0.92 ± 0.14 and 0.96 ± 0.16 respectively (p < 0.01). The greater co-activation during sharper CODs is most likely due to the demand for enhanced stability caused by high joint loading which requires coordinated contraction of antagonistic muscle groups, driven by the neuromuscular system.

The relative contribution of specific muscle groups to the total activation of the lower limb was differentiated by the muscle used and the angle of the turn. The role of quadriceps muscles to total activation decreased with increased COD angle (45° = 38.2 ± 3.6% vs. 180° = 33.4 ± 3.2%, p < 0.05), while the contribution of hamstrings and gluteal muscles increased (hamstrings: 45° = 21.7 ± 2.5% vs. 180° = 26.6 ± 2.8%, p < 0.01; gluteals: 45° = 24.1 ± 2.7% vs. 180° = 26.9 ± 2.4%, p < 0.05). This change in the recruitment of specific muscles may be an adaptive response due to the increased loading on the hip and knee joints that accompany sharper turns.

For every muscle group, COD angle was shown to decrease neuromuscular efficiency, defined as the ratio of muscle activation to the produced joint moment, activation of the muscle. The greater the angle of the turn, the less efficient the muscle worked. Reduced efficiency means that more activation of muscle is needed to achieve the same amount of relative joint moment for sharper turns which may influence fatigue during several high-angle turns in matches or training.

The EMG activation patterns recorded correspond to the kinematic data in which there was an increase in ankle dorsiflexion, knee flexion, and hip flexion with increase in the COD angle. The greater joint excursions during sharper CODs (change of direction) require greater eccentric muscle activity for control of the joints and impact force absorption. The interplay of muscle activation and joint motion illustrates the complex strategies of neuromuscular control during different COD tasks.

Analysing the results from a motor control perspective, the force-dominant CODs (135° and 180°) seem to use different neuromuscular control strategies compared to the velocity-dominant CODs (45° and 90°). Velocity-dominant CODs appear to rely more on momentum, as well as stored elastic energy indicated by reduced ground contact time and decreased flexion angle of the joints. On the other hand, force-dominant CODs adopt a position-dependent control approach with active joint control, greater coactivation, and muscle prep actuation due to the high joint loads.

These unique strategies of neuromuscular control raise considerable concerns regarding the design of training programmes, injury risk, and rehabilitation following lower limb injuries. The muscle activation patterns specific to the angle suggest that more precise intervening strategies should be developed based on the specific COD requirements of the athlete’s sport.

Practical applications

This research outcomes possess a distinct practicality to the versatility of football coaches and professionals involved with football players such as rehabilitation experts, strength and conditioning coaches, and exercise physiologists.

In developing training plans, consider integrating strategies that utilise angle-specific training techniques due to the observed biomechanical differences between the different angles of change of direction (COD). For the velocity-dominant less than 90° COD angles, execution speeds should be emphasised during training. On the other hand, greater training focus for greater than 90° force-dominant COD angles should be placed on the specific strength qualities needed to withstand the significantly higher joint load excursions. This method of training would still be in tandem with the “force velocity continuum” model because different COD angles utilise different aspects of the neuromuscular system.

With greater change of direction (COD) angles, the progressive increase in knee abduction moments, internal knee rotation moments and the dynamic valgus index suggests that pre-participation screening should incorporate sharper COD angles (≥ 135°) in order to identify athletes with higher chances of suffering from an ACL injury. The traditional method of screening using just 45° or 90° cuts might overlook the lack of biomechanical efficiencies that become evident during more challenging sub-45 degree turns (sharp cut) dynamic actions. Adopting a systematic approach with a number of turn tests at varying angles could provide better estimates of injury risk.

The sharper the “Change of Direction” (COD), the greater the load which increases the risk of injury. Increased loading highlights the need for meticulous planning during COD training. Volume of training with 135° and 180° CODs should be tailored and increased progressively, especially during the preseason or post-injury rehabilitation stages, to enable sufficient accommodation to the mechanical overload. It is crucial to manage training volume and intensity for CODs, particularly in players with a history of knee injuries and those exhibiting high-risk kinetic patterns.

Based on more significant changes in muscle activity with increasing angles of COD, specific training should emphasise developing decelerative eccentric strength of the operated limb to control knee flexion during the stepdown, synergistic transverse plus frontal plane control by the hip external rotators and core muscles, and explosive torque output at the hips to enable multi-angle performance enhancement during CODs. Ensure exercises that promote multi-directional neuromuscular control are incorporated into the intervention programme.

For sharper changes of direction (COD), technique modification strategies should be employed to manage knee joint loading, as identified by the biomechanical risk factors. These strategies should aim at enhancing trunk control during the plant step, increasing flexion of the knee at initial contact, minimising knee valgus, and optimising foot placement concerning the centre of mass. Athletes can be coached into adopting these safer movement patterns with the help of visual and verbal feedback during practice.

There is evidence that lower limb joints are subject to much higher loading during 135° and 180° COD manoeuvres than while performing 45° and 90° turns. Therefore, controlling the overall volume of more than 90° high-angle CODs during training sessions should be done with caution, especially for injured players and those with high-risk biomechanical profiles. The frequency and load of COD movements within training could be monitored using GPS and inertial measurement systems.

These individual differences demonstrate the necessity and importance of tailoring assessment and training based on specific angles of COD for every athlete. Movement patterns deemed to be high risk may be particularly actionable with more specific and tailored modifications designed for each athlete’s unique deficiencies. Identifying these with regular 3D motion analysis or field-based multi-camera motion capture assessments enables player-specific pathways to better performance.

Analysis of a football match reveals that players execute roughly 700 change of directional movements during varying intensities over the course of a game; however, these demands might differ according to playing position. It is plausible that incorporating position-specific change of direction (COD) training that focuses on competitive play angles and frequencies would improve performance and injury outcomes. As an illustration, wide defenders and wingers could be taught to perform velocity-dominant CODs, while central defenders could be taught force-dominant CODs.

Implementing these practical insights helps practitioners design more precise and efficient COD training programmes, overcoming the performance-injury prevention paradox that is so common in training prescription.

Limitations and future research

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, and most importantly, the laboratory-based COD tasks were performed in a controlled, pre-planned manner without the reactive decision-making and environmental unpredictability inherent in competitive soccer. In actual match situations, players must respond rapidly to dynamic stimuli such as opponent movements, ball trajectories, and tactical situations. This reduced ecological validity represents a fundamental constraint on the generalizability of our findings to match contexts, where cognitive load and perceptual demands interact with biomechanical execution. Future research should incorporate reactive COD tasks with sport-specific cues to better approximate competitive demands.

Second, all participants wore their own soccer footwear and performed on a standardized laboratory surface (friction coefficient 0.82). While this maintained controlled conditions, it may not fully represent the diverse playing surfaces encountered in soccer, including natural grass and artificial turf under varying weather conditions. Surface-footwear interactions significantly influence traction and may alter injury risk profiles during sharp CODs.

Third, our sample consisted exclusively of male semi-professional soccer players, limiting generalizability to female athletes and players at different competitive levels. Female athletes demonstrate different biomechanical strategies during COD tasks and experience ACL injuries at substantially higher rates. Future studies should examine sex-specific and level-specific differences in angle-dependent biomechanics.

Fourth, the cross-sectional design captures acute biomechanical responses but cannot establish causal relationships between observed patterns and injury incidence. While we identified risk markers based on previous prospective research, we did not track actual injury outcomes. Prospective longitudinal studies are needed to validate the predictive utility of angle-specific risk markers.

Fifth, although our assessment included kinetics, kinematics, and EMG, we did not directly measure tissue-level stresses or ACL strain. The injury risk indicators used (DVI, KAM, SPEA) are based on epidemiological associations rather than direct tissue measurements. Additionally, we assessed peak loading but not cumulative load exposure or dose-response relationships between COD angle frequency and injury risk. Advanced techniques such as musculoskeletal modeling or ultrasound-based strain measurements could provide more direct estimates of structure-specific loading.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into angle-specific biomechanics during COD tasks. Future research should address these constraints through longitudinal intervention studies, investigation of fatigue effects during repeated high-angle CODs, and machine learning approaches to develop predictive injury risk models integrating multiple biomechanical parameters.

Conclusion

The impact of various change of direction (COD) angles (45°, 90°, 135°, and 180°) on lower limb joint loads as well as injury risk indicators in football players was studied. Our biomechanical analysis shows that joint loading parameters, in particular the abduction moment of the knee, increased with the greater angles of change of direction (141% increase from 45° to 180°), extension moment of the knee (145%) and adduction moment of the hip (158%). This relationship was not linear and there was disproportionate increase after the 90° mark which suggested the presence of a biomechanical boundary between velocity-dominant and force-dominant biomechanical centre of turns.

The analysis of known indicators of risk of injury demonstrated the presence of significant angle-dependent responses. The dynamic valgus index, for example, increased 152% from 45° to 180°. Moreover, it is critical to note that the average values for the 135° and 180° exceeded published benchmarks for high risk; 88% of subjects demonstrated high-risk biomechanics during 180° compared to 19% during 45°, which was clearly low-risk. Kinematic data of the investigated joints revealed increased ankle dorsiflexion, knee flexion, hip flexion, and hip abduction for sharp CODs, likely owing to the strategies employed to cope with the greater demands to slow down.

The increase in quadriceps and hamstring activity between 45° and 180° conditions was 32–47% and 52–57% respectively, showing an increase in muscular activation patterns demonstrating angle-specific neuromuscular control strategies. Changes in the timing of activation also occurred, with earlier peak hamstring activation for sharper CODs, which could serve as a protective adaptation to improve dynamic knee stability during heavily-loaded plant steps.

The angle-specific approaches are crucial to performance enhancement and injury mitigation in football as highlighted by the findings. While designing training regimes, implementing screening protocols, and developing prevention frameworks, coaches and practitioners need to pay attention to sharper CODs due to significantly heightened joint loads and injury susceptibility. The foundational biomechanical differences identified across angles of COD provide a basis towards the construction of precise strategies aimed at optimising safety and efficiency for mastering this essential football skill.
