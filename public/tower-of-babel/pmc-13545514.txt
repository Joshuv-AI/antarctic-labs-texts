Physiologically‐Guided Ultrafiltration: A Proposed Ultrasound Framework for Individualized Volume Removal During Hemodialysis
Authors: Sharad Patel, Arwa Bootwala, Adam Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUltrafiltration during hemodialysis is the most hemodynamically stressful intervention dialysis patients endure, yet its prescription relies on interdialytic weight gain and clinical gestalt. Rates exceeding 13 mL/kg/h carry substantially higher cardiovascular mortality, and recurrent intradialytic hypotension drives myocardial stunning, accelerated loss of residual kidney function, and progressive cardiac dysfunction. Existing volume management tools, including bioimpedance spectroscopy, relative blood volume monitoring, and even lung ultrasound, address fragments of this problem but none integrates the two questions that should govern every prescription: how much volume can be removed, and how fast? This review synthesizes principles from critical care hemodynamics and nephrology to propose an integrated ultrasound‐guided framework. We apply the Frank‐Starling relationship to volume removal, translating the principles of active fluid removal to maintenance hemodialysis. We approach the safety of fluid removal in terms of preload responsiveness: removal is best tolerated when the patient is preload‐unresponsive, operating on the flat portion of the cardiac function curve. The framework uses a rapid screen (inferior vena cava diameter and lung ultrasound) to identify patients near dry weight versus those requiring further assessment. When congestion is present, a two‐axis decision matrix combines venous congestion quantification (portal vein pulsatility) with preload dependence testing (passive leg raising with a stroke volume surrogate) to guide both volume target and removal rate. Preload responsiveness is defined by a rise of at least 10% to 12% in the left ventricular outflow tract velocity‐time integral after passive leg raising, and significant venous congestion by an inferior vena cava diameter of 2 cm or greater with portal vein pulsatility; preload‐unresponsive patients without congestion may tolerate rates toward the 13 mL/kg/h safety ceiling, whereas preload‐responsive or severely congested patients warrant slower removal over extended or additional sessions. We propose a two‐tiered protocol adaptable to diverse practice settings. This integrated framework has not been tested prospectively and should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating; that said, the individual components are validated and the physiological rationale is sound, and the potential to reduce ultrafiltration‐related morbidity, from intradialytic symptoms to myocardial stunning and residual function loss, is substantial.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease accounts for more than half of all deaths among patients with end‐stage kidney disease, a mortality rate that has proven stubbornly resistant to advances in dialysis technology over the past three decades [1, 2]. Among the modifiable contributors to this burden, ultrafiltration during hemodialysis is the most hemodynamically stressful event dialysis patients repeatedly endure. Data from the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study showed that ultrafiltration rates exceeding 10 mL/kg/h are associated with higher all‐cause mortality and a 30% increased risk of intradialytic hypotension [3]. Flythe and colleagues subsequently found that rates above 13 mL/kg/h carry a 59% increase in all‐cause and 71% increase in cardiovascular mortality [4]. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services now benchmarks ultrafiltration rate below 13 mL/kg/h as a quality metric, but this is a population‐level threshold, not an individualized prescription.

The current approach to ultrafiltration prescription remains largely empiric. The clinician weighs the patient, subtracts an estimated dry weight, divides by the session duration, and programs the machine. The dry weight itself is a clinical guess, refined through iterative “probing” in which the target is lowered until the patient becomes symptomatic, then raised slightly [5, 6]. No bedside tool integrates the two fundamental questions that should govern every ultrafiltration prescription: how much volume can safely be removed, and how fast can it be removed? Some patients tolerate four liters without incident while others collapse after one and a half, yet the current paradigm cannot predict which.

The tools to resolve this problem already exist, but they have developed in disciplinary silos. Critical care has built a sophisticated hemodynamic reasoning framework around passive leg raising, volume responsiveness testing, and venous congestion profiling. This work has been applied predominantly to the ICU, most often in the context of continuous renal replacement therapy [7, 8, 9]. Meanwhile, the approximately 550 000 patients on maintenance hemodialysis in the United States have not benefited from this paradigm. The measurement platforms required, principally point‐of‐care ultrasound and passive leg raising, are already available in dialysis settings and require no invasive monitoring. We have previously reviewed the individual tools available for fluid assessment in dialysis patients [10]; here, we provide the physiological approach that integrates them. The proposed framework is intended to physiologically individualize the cardiovascular response to ultrafiltration by integrating assessment of venous congestion and preload responsiveness. It is not designed to directly quantify total body water or whole‐body fluid distribution, which remain complementary aspects of volume assessment.

This review proposes an integrated, two‐axis framework for ultrafiltration during intermittent hemodialysis, pairing a rapid congestion screen with assessment of venous congestion and preload dependence to address both how much volume to remove and how fast. Each component is individually validated, but the integrated protocol has not been prospectively tested; what follows is a physiologically grounded proposal for clinical investigation.

Physiology of Ultrafiltration: Why Patients Crash

Plasma Refilling and Its Determinants

Ultrafiltration removes fluid only from the intravascular compartment, so whether a given rate is sustainable depends on the plasma refilling rate, the movement of fluid from the interstitium back into the vasculature. When ultrafiltration outpaces refilling, circulating volume contracts and venous return falls. Schneditz and colleagues measured refilling at roughly 500 mL per hour, with wide variation between patients [11, 12], and refilling fractions in maintenance hemodialysis often approach 70%, varying between and within patients over time [13]. Refilling is highest early in a session and declines as the interstitial reservoir depletes, which is why a patient stable for three hours can crash in the last forty‐five minutes [6].

The uremic vasculature compounds the problem. Under the revised Starling model, the endothelial glycocalyx governs transcapillary fluid movement and sustained interstitial absorption does not occur [14, 15]; inflammation and uremia degrade this glycocalyx, impairing refilling in precisely the population that depends on it most [16, 17]. Sodium can also be stored in osmotically inactive form in skin and muscle, bound to glycosaminoglycans [18, 19]; this reservoir, not in equilibrium with plasma, helps explain why tolerance of fluid removal varies and why post‐dialysis rebound occurs [20].

The Hemodynamic Response to Volume Removal

Why Some Patients Tolerate Ultrafiltration and Others Do Not

The cardiovascular response to ultrafiltration depends on the relationship between fluid removal and the body's compensatory reserve. Approximately 70% of total blood volume resides in the venous compartment as hemodynamically “unstressed” volume that does not itself generate venous return but can be recruited into the stressed compartment by venoconstriction to defend mean systemic filling pressure [21, 22, 23]. During ultrafiltration, the maintenance of venous return therefore depends less on the absolute volume removed than on the adequacy of this compensatory recruitment: when sympathetic venoconstriction is intact, considerable volume can be removed with little hemodynamic consequence, but when it fails, even modest removal lowers mean systemic filling pressure, reduces venous return, and depresses cardiac output [24]. In the healthy circulation, sympathetic activation buffers substantial losses. In end‐stage kidney disease, these mechanisms are systematically impaired: chronic sympathetic overactivity with vagal withdrawal leaves no reserve for acute compensation [25, 26, 27]. Baroreceptor dysfunction can contribute to hypotension in patients with preserved ejection fraction who crash during ultrafiltration [25]. The Bezold‐Jarisch reflex can produce paradoxical withdrawal of sympathetic vasoconstriction during progressive volume depletion, converting stable hemodynamics to sudden cardiovascular collapse without warning [28].

Distinguishing early‐ from late‐onset intradialytic hypotension is essential because the two have different drivers and different remedies. In an analysis of 4348 patients across 785 682 treatments, IDH occurred at a median of 120–149 min, but a substantial fraction occurred earlier, and patients prone to early‐onset events had worse all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality than those whose hypotension occurred later [29]. Early‐onset IDH typically reflects loss of vascular tone, rapid osmolar shifts, and the residual effect of antihypertensives rather than ultrafiltration‐induced hypovolemia, and is best addressed by withholding antihypertensives pre‐dialysis, adjusting dialysate sodium and temperature, and considering pharmacologic vasoconstrictors. Late‐onset IDH, by contrast, is the phenotype driven by progressive plasma volume depletion as ultrafiltration outpaces refilling, the phenotype the strategy presented here is designed to address.

Myocardial Stunning: The Hidden Cardiac Injury

Beyond acute instability, ultrafiltration inflicts subclinical cardiac injury. Burton and colleagues found that 64% of hemodialysis patients develop new segmental wall motion abnormalities during dialysis, confirmed by McIntyre with positron emission tomography [30, 31]; repeatedly stunned segments develop fixed dysfunction over 12 months [30]. More frequent hemodialysis reduces stunning by lowering per‐session volume [32], but only 12% of patients accept additional sessions [33]. If we cannot add sessions, each session must be made safer.

From Resuscitation to Active Fluid Removal: Inverting the Question

Passive Leg Raising as the Reference Standard for Volume Responsiveness

The passive leg raising maneuver has become the reference standard for predicting volume responsiveness in spontaneously breathing patients. By transitioning a patient from semi‐recumbent to supine with legs elevated to 45 degrees, approximately 300–500 mL of blood is mobilized from the lower extremities and splanchnic circulation, simulating a reversible volume challenge [34]. A meta‐analysis of 21 studies demonstrated a pooled area under the curve (AUC) of 0.95the curve of 0.95 for predicting fluid responsiveness [35]. The response must be assessed by measuring changes in cardiac output (through LVOT VTI or carotid Doppler) rather than blood pressure, which lacks sensitivity [36]. A reduction‐based provocation such as head‐up tilt, lower‐body negative pressure, or extracorporeal volume sequestration would be physiologically symmetric to ultrafiltration and has been studied in hemodialysis [37, 38], but each requires specialized equipment or maneuvers that are difficult to perform in the dialysis chair and carry safety considerations in volume‐sensitive patients; passive leg raising remains the operationally practical reference standard with the largest validation base, and the most direct evidence in dialysis patients also uses passive leg raising [39]. Orthostatic maneuvers feasible in the dialysis unit, including head‐up positioning achieved by adjusting the dialysis chair and, in ambulatory patients, active standing, offer an alternative venous‐return challenge and merit study. Bedside estimation of jugular venous pressure is likewise informative and inexpensive, but its interpretation carries substantial inter‐observer variability, whereas the ultrasound measures described here are more reproducible [40].

The Inversion: Passive Leg Raising Predicts the Response to Fluid Removal

In 2016, Monnet and colleagues demonstrated that passive leg raising could predict which patients would tolerate fluid removal [7]. In 39 critically ill patients requiring renal replacement therapy, patients whose cardiac index increased by 9% or more with passive leg raising were significantly more likely to develop hemodynamic instability during subsequent fluid removal (AUC 0.89).

This inverts the usual clinical question. In resuscitation, we ask: “Will fluid help this patient?” When removing volume, the question is: “Will fluid removal hurt this patient?” The physiology is the same Starling curve read in the opposite direction. If passive leg raising increases cardiac output, the patient is on the steep portion of the curve, in preload‐dependent territory where small changes in venous return produce large swings in stroke volume. Removing volume from such a patient will produce a mirror‐image decline in cardiac output, with predictable consequences: hypotension, organ hypoperfusion, and myocardial stunning.

Preload Responsiveness Applied to Fluid Removal

The relevant physiology is preload responsiveness, and it operates identically in the intensive care unit and the dialysis unit, independent of the direction in which venous return is changed. A patient on the flat portion of the cardiac function curve is preload‐unresponsive: a change in preload produces little change in stroke volume, so fluid removal is well tolerated. A patient on the steep portion is preload‐responsive (preload‐dependent), and removing volume will lower stroke volume and precipitate hemodynamic instability. What differs in dialysis is not the physiology but the clinical question: rather than asking whether fluid will help, we ask whether fluid removal will harm.

From the Intensive Care Unit to the Dialysis Unit: The Translation Gap

The supporting evidence has continued to grow on the critical care side. Biscarrat and colleagues extended the concept to continuous renal replacement therapy using a 250 mL net ultrafiltration challenge in critically ill patients [8], Melo and colleagues subsequently described ultrafiltration intolerance as a multidimensional construct encompassing not only preload dependence but also cardiac dysfunction, vascular tone, and impaired refilling [9, 41].

What remains is the translation to intermittent hemodialysis, which poses its own challenges: faster ultrafiltration rates, ambulatory patients with chronic autonomic dysfunction, and cumulative hemodynamic stress over years rather than days. Passive leg raising indicates only whether fluid can be safely removed; it does not by itself specify the rate of removal or how much total volume should ultimately come off. A patient may be preload‐unresponsive with minimal residual congestion (ultrafiltration is safe but unnecessary) or preload‐unresponsive with massive congestion (ultrafiltration is both safe and urgently needed). The missing piece is venous congestion quantification.

The Two‐Axis Assessment: Tools and Evidence

Venous Congestion Assessment: From the Venous Excess Ultrasound Score to a Simplified Profile

The Venous Excess Ultrasound Score (VExUS) provides a graded assessment of systemic venous congestion using inferior vena cava (IVC) diameter as an entry gate (greater than 2 cm suggests congestion), followed by Doppler interrogation of the hepatic vein, portal vein, and intrarenal vein [42]. For hemodialysis patients, we recommend a simplified congestion profile. The intrarenal vein is unreliable in end‐stage kidney disease because of parenchymal atrophy and fibrosis; modified VExUS omitting this component performs comparably to the full score for detecting elevated right atrial pressure [43]. The hepatic vein adds diagnostic value but requires waveform morphology analysis that is technically demanding. Portal vein pulsatility, by contrast, is technically straightforward: the vessel is superficial, consistently accessible, and requires only pattern recognition. Portal pulsatility is independently associated with acute kidney injury after cardiac surgery (hazard ratio 2.09) [44]. Our recommendation is therefore IVC diameter plus portal vein pulsatility index as the practical congestion assessment for dialysis, with hepatic vein Doppler as an optional addition when operator skill permits. We acknowledge that portal vein pulsatility has not been prospectively validated in chronic hemodialysis patients specifically. However, portal pulsatility reflects right atrial pressure transmission through the hepatic sinusoids, a mechanical phenomenon governed by venous compliance and central venous pressure regardless of the clinical setting [45]. What remains to be established is the precise grading thresholds that optimally discriminate congestion severity in the chronic dialysis population. Absolute vein diameter thresholds also do not account for body size or sex, and indexing IVC and portal vein measures to body surface area or height may improve discrimination, particularly across populations of differing stature.

Lung ultrasound quantifies extravascular lung water and predicts cardiovascular events and mortality in hemodialysis patients [46, 47]. Pairing it with IVC assessment provides a rapid bilateral cardiac screen: IVC diameter less than 2 cm indicates low central venous pressure and absence of significant right‐sided congestion, while bilateral A‐lines carry a high probability of a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure below 18 mmHg, screening for left‐sided congestion. When both are present, neither side of the heart is significantly volume overloaded. When IVC is 2 cm or greater and/or B‐lines are present, the full two‐axis assessment is warranted.

Validation in hemodialysis patients is growing. A single‐center observational study showed that VExUS scores tracked fluid removal during hemodialysis sessions [48]. A subsequent prospective study confirmed that VExUS captures dynamic volume shifts during hemodialysis, with median scores decreasing from 1 pre‐dialysis to 0 post‐dialysis [49]. Portal vein changes were noted to be more sensitive than IVC changes in monitoring real‐time decongestion [50].

Stroke Volume Surrogates: Determining a Safe Removal Rate

The second axis requires a bedside measure of stroke volume response to passive leg raising. The left ventricular outflow tract velocity‐time integral (LVOT VTI) is the most direct: from the apical five‐chamber view, pulsed‐wave Doppler yields a velocity envelope that tracks stroke volume, since outflow tract diameter is fixed within an individual [51]. An increase of 10%–12% or more after passive leg raising indicates volume responsiveness, placing the patient on the steep portion of the curve, where aggressive removal is poorly tolerated (Figure 1).

The Frank‐Starling relationship and preload responsiveness. The steep portion of the curve corresponds to preload‐responsive (preload‐dependent) patients, in whom a passive leg raising‐induced increase in venous return produces a substantial rise in stroke volume; these patients are predicted to tolerate ultrafiltration poorly. The plateau corresponds to preload‐unresponsive (preload‐independent) patients, in whom the same maneuver produces little change in stroke volume because the heart is operating near the top of its function curve; these patients are predicted to tolerate ultrafiltration.

However, LVOT VTI acquisition requires advanced echocardiographic skill and is difficult to obtain serially during hemodialysis with suboptimal acoustic windows. Carotid artery Doppler is a compelling alternative. The common carotid artery is superficial, consistently accessible, and does not require cardiac windows. Carotid Doppler velocity‐time integral tracks stroke‐volume change and has been validated against transesophageal echocardiography [52, 53, 54]. A wearable, FDA‐cleared continuous‐wave Doppler patch can automate carotid VTI measurement, potentially eliminating operator dependence [55].

The most directly relevant validation in dialysis patients is from Bou Chebl and colleagues, who studied 54 end‐stage kidney disease patients [39]. Before dialysis, the change in VTI with passive leg raising was only 1.83%, indicating preload unresponsiveness. After dialysis, the same patients demonstrated a 15.05% increase; they had crossed over to volume responsiveness. Whether measured by LVOT VTI or carotid Doppler, the physiological question is the same: does preload augmentation via passive leg raising increase stroke volume? The choice of measurement platform is pragmatic.

Why the Matrix Matters When the Screen Is Positive

Why not rely on a single parameter? Because single parameters consistently fail. Inferior vena cava diameter is unreliable in numerous clinical scenarios and is nearly always dilated before hemodialysis [56]. Central venous pressure does not predict fluid responsiveness [57, 58]. No single number can capture what is fundamentally multidimensional physiology. Consider a patient with biventricular failure, anasarca, and an ejection fraction of 15%: massively congested and simultaneously volume responsive, a combination invisible to any single measurement but immediately obvious on the matrix (Figure 2).

The matrix is deployed when the rapid screen (inferior vena cava [IVC] diameter ≥ 2 cm and/or B‐lines) indicates congestion is present. The vertical axis represents venous congestion assessed by portal vein pulsatility (moderate‐to‐severe pulsatility = significant congestion, mild pulsatility = minimal congestion). The horizontal axis represents the hemodynamic response to passive leg raising assessed by a stroke volume surrogate (a change in the velocity‐time integral [ΔVTI] ≥ 10%–12% = volume responsive/preload‐dependent, ΔVTI < 10% = preload‐unresponsive). Each quadrant defines a distinct clinical scenario. Upper‐right (significant congestion, preload‐unresponsive): patient needs substantial removal and tolerates it well; probe dry weight, removal likely exceeds interdialytic weight gain, rates can approach the conventional population‐level ceiling. Lower‐right (minimal congestion, preload‐unresponsive): remove interdialytic weight gain, cautious dry weight probe possible, rates can be standard or higher; session may be shorter. Lower‐left (minimal congestion, preload‐responsive): near target but preload‐dependent; conservative rates, longer sessions or multi‐session removal if probing dry weight. Upper‐left (significant congestion, preload‐responsive): highest‐risk phenotype; substantial removal needed but poorly tolerated, requiring conservative rates, extended treatment times, multi‐session decongestion. Positioning Against Existing Volume Management Tools

Several technologies address parts of this problem and are reviewed in detail in a companion paper [10]. In brief, bioimpedance spectroscopy estimates total body water but provides no hemodynamic information; relative blood volume monitoring is reactive rather than predictive and has not improved outcomes in randomized trials [59, 60]; and lung ultrasound quantifies congestion without assessing tolerance of removal, which may explain why lung ultrasound‐guided dry‐weight reduction did not reduce mortality in a randomized trial [61]. None integrates the two questions of how much volume to remove and how fast. Bioimpedance, absolute blood volume monitoring, and arterial pressure are complementary rather than competing modalities, and integrating them with the present framework is a priority for future work.

The Stakes of Getting It Wrong

Immediate Morbidity

The daily costs of empiric ultrafiltration fall on patients. Muscle cramping, nausea, dizziness, and post‐dialysis fatigue are among the most common complaints of hemodialysis patients, and all are tied to intravascular volume depletion during ultrafiltration [33, 62]. Recovery averages six to eight hours. These symptoms drive non‐adherence, early termination, and missed sessions, each independently associated with increased mortality.

Cardiac Injury and Residual Kidney Function: The Long Game

The case for physiologically‐guided ultrafiltration is strongest for residual kidney function. In the CHOICE Study, urine output above 250 mL per day at one year was associated with a 30% reduction in all‐cause mortality, better quality of life, less inflammation, and lower erythropoietin requirements [63]. Even 1 to 2 mL per minute of residual filtration improves survival.

Overaggressive ultrafiltration accelerates residual function loss through a vicious cycle: recurrent intradialytic hypotension causes renal hypoperfusion and ischemic nephron injury; as residual function declines, interdialytic weight gain increases, pushing ultrafiltration rates higher, which causes more hypotension and more nephron loss [30, 64].

The incremental hemodialysis movement advocates for twice‐weekly sessions in patients with substantial residual function [65, 66, 67, 68], but requires a real‐time assessment tool to flag when residual function is failing. As the rapid screen becomes persistently positive and portal pulsatility increases, the clinician has objective evidence that the current dialysis frequency is no longer adequate.

A Tiered Clinical Protocol

Who Needs This and When

The full two‐axis assessment is not needed for every patient at every session. Its value is greatest where uncertainty is highest: heart failure, recurrent intradialytic hypotension, autonomic neuropathy, ejection fraction below 40%, or the first year on dialysis, when residual function is most vulnerable. Hospitalization, new or worsening intradialytic symptoms, and scheduled reassessment every three to six months are additional triggers.

Implementation Tiers

Not every dialysis unit has Doppler‐capable ultrasound or echo‐trained staff, and any framework that adds a lengthy hemodynamic evaluation to every patient at every session will not be adopted. We therefore propose a two‐layer architecture: a 30‐s rapid screen requiring no Doppler as the universal entry point, with the full matrix triggered only when clinically indicated. We recognize that pre‐dialysis assessments are commonly performed by trainees or nursing staff who may lack point‐of‐care ultrasound skills, and any rollout will need to account for credentialing, training time, and supervision; the tiered architecture is designed in part to keep the universal screen achievable within the existing workforce.

Tier 1, the rapid screen (IVC plus lung ultrasound), is universal and feasible at every session on any portable device, without Doppler, in about thirty seconds. It updates the clinician's prior estimate from interdialytic weight gain, vintage, and residual urine output. An IVC below 2 cm with bilateral A‐lines indicates a patient near physiological dry weight, for whom ultrafiltration targets interdialytic weight gain only; discordant findings warrant investigation rather than dismissal of either source. An IVC of 2 cm or greater and/or B‐lines confirm congestion and triggers Tier 2.

Tier 2 deploys the full two‐axis matrix. Portal vein pulsatility index quantifies the congestion axis, and a stroke volume surrogate (LVOT VTI or carotid artery Doppler) with passive leg raising determines the preload dependence axis; emerging wearable Doppler devices may further simplify this measurement [55]. This tier requires 20 to 30 h of focused competency‐based training. The resulting quadrant yields specific guidance for both volume target and removal rate (Figure 2).

The congestion axis (portal vein pulsatility) informs how much volume to target; the preload dependence axis (passive leg raising response) indicates whether removal will be tolerated and how cautiously to proceed. The highest‐risk quadrant, significant congestion with preload dependence, is a mismatch between what the patient needs and what the patient tolerates, calling for slow removal over extended or multiple sessions. The rate ranges in Figure 2 and Table 1 are anchored to the 13 mL/kg/h mortality threshold [4] and are proposed pending prospective testing.

Redefining Dry Weight

The textbook definition of dry weight, the lowest post‐dialysis weight a patient tolerates without symptoms, belongs to an era without bedside hemodynamic tools. We propose a physiological definition instead: dry weight is the post‐dialysis weight at which the pre‐dialysis rapid screen at the subsequent session is negative (IVC < 2 cm, bilateral A‐lines), portal vein pulsatility has normalized, and volume responsiveness is beginning to emerge with passive leg raising (ΔVTI approaching 10%). This is a functional definition, grounded in physiology rather than symptoms.

Under this method, dry weight is not a fixed number but an estimate refined across sessions, converging over weeks on a physiologically justified target that symptom‐based probing alone cannot reliably identify. This iterative endpoint is also how the framework answers the question of how much volume should ultimately be removed: not as a fixed liter target set in advance, but as the cumulative removal across sessions that brings the patient to the negative‐screen, emerging‐PLR‐positive state.

This replaces symptom‐driven probing with physiology‐driven probing, catching both subclinical overhydration and overshooting [5, 69]. Once a dry weight target has been validated by this model, the rapid screen alone is sufficient for ongoing monitoring, with the full two‐axis reassessment repeated on a scheduled basis or when clinical triggers arise. Patients with marked congestion may require reassessment over multiple dialysis sessions and, when clinically indicated, repeat physiologic assessment during treatment if substantial decongestion is achieved. The framework is intended as an iterative process rather than a single‐session prescription.

Limitations and Future Directions

The framework is built from individually validated components, but its integration has not been prospectively tested, and the rate ranges in Table 1 are expert‐derived rather than the product of randomized comparison. Portal vein pulsatility thresholds also require validation in the chronic hemodialysis population. The realistic path is a staged program: pilot feasibility studies to confirm that teams can perform the two‐axis assessment reliably and to generate variance estimates for powering; modest randomized trials with intradialytic hypotension endpoints; and larger pragmatic trials powered for major adverse cardiovascular events, hospitalization, and residual kidney function. Quality‐improvement collaboratives could run alongside to refine workflows and build the imaging registries such trials will require. We hope this review encourages the dialysis community to help generate that evidence.

Sex‐ and gender‐based differences in vascular compliance, body composition, and autonomic function likely influence both preload responsiveness and ultrasound thresholds, and future validation studies should be designed to capture these effects.

The framework deliberately addresses two questions: how fast volume can be safely removed within a session, and when serial removal has converged on dry weight. It does not specify how many liters to remove in a given session; per‐session targets remain guided by interdialytic weight gain and the validated dry weight estimate. Bioimpedance and absolute blood volume monitoring offer complementary views of total body water and continuous intravascular state, and a future iteration could integrate them, though this requires prospective validation rather than a priori inclusion.

The main barrier to adoption is training, not equipment cost, and it is shrinking. The simplified congestion profile (IVC diameter and portal vein pulsatility) requires far less training than full VExUS, and the rapid screen needs no Doppler. Carotid artery Doppler requires less skill than LVOT VTI, and emerging wearable Doppler devices may eventually automate the measurement [55]. Nephrology training programs should consider point‐of‐care hemodynamic assessment a core competency, as critical care and emergency medicine already have.

Technology will continue to lower the implementation barrier. AI‐automated congestion grading and VTI measurement can reduce operator dependence [70]. Integration of ultrasound data with dialysis machine feedback loops could enable semi‐automated ultrafiltration rate adjustment [71]. But the bedside framework described here is available now.

Conclusion

Ultrafiltration prescription during hemodialysis should be physiologically individualized, not empiric. A rapid screen using IVC diameter and lung ultrasound identifies patients likely near dry weight and those who need further assessment. When congestion is present, combining portal vein pulsatility with passive leg raising creates a two‐axis decision matrix that addresses both how much volume can be removed and how fast it can safely come off. Framing fluid removal in terms of preload responsiveness provides the conceptual foundation for a decongestion‐oriented approach to dialysis fluid management. Structured as an iterative workflow where clinical estimates are updated by ultrasound data at the start of each session, the framework turns ultrafiltration from a static prescription into a physiologically informed process. This integrated approach remains a hypothesis that has not been prospectively validated; however, the physiological rationale is sound and the individual tools are validated, and the potential to reduce ultrafiltration‐related morbidity across its full spectrum is substantial. Point‐of‐care ultrasound puts these tools in the nephrologist's hands without invasive monitoring. It is time to move beyond “weigh and divide.”
