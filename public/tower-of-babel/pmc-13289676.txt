Can pre-disaster community resilience enhance post-disaster recovery among older adults? A natural experiment in Iwanuma City after the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and Tsunami
Authors: Yuto Shiozaki, Rika Ohtsuka, Hiroyuki Hikichi, Katsunori Kondo, Ichiro Kawachi, Jun Aida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs large-scale disasters occur more frequently worldwide, understanding how pre-disaster community resilience influences post-disaster recovery has become imperative for disaster risk-reduction policy and practice. This study investigated whether pre-disaster community resilience helped explain post-disaster subjective recovery following the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and Tsunami. We conducted a baseline survey of older residents in Iwanuma City in 2010 and a follow-up survey 2.5 years after the disaster, yielding an analytic sample of 3523 respondents nested within 98 districts. Ten resilience indicators reflecting social capital and economic development were measured at both individual and community levels before the disaster. Using multilevel logistic regression models, we examined the association between these indicators and two outcomes: self-reported recovery of daily life and perceived recovery of the neighborhood. Housing damage, bereavement, and high community-level housing destruction substantially reduced the participants’ perceived recovery. Pre-disaster community-level social participation was significantly associated with better recovery of individual daily life, whereas local activities and interactions mitigated the negative impact of severe housing damage. By contrast, respondents with severe damage living in districts with higher pre-disaster evaluations of administrative services reported poorer neighborhood recovery. This natural experimental evidence demonstrates that community environments characterized by high social participation and frequent local interactions before a disaster foster more favorable recovery perceptions. The findings provide prospective evidence on the associations between the selected community resilience indicators and post-disaster recovery, and highlight the importance of strengthening social connectedness in ordinary times as a practical strategy for enhancing disaster resilience.Keywords: Community resilience, Social capital, Disaster recovery, Natural experiment, 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and Tsunami, Multilevel model, Self-reported recovery

Introduction

Over the past two decades, as large-scale disasters have begun to occur more frequently worldwide, community resilience has become a central concept in disaster risk reduction research, reflecting a shift from vulnerability-based frameworks to those emphasizing community coping and adaptive capacity [1–3]. This shift was prompted by empirical findings that communities with similar exposure to hazards often experience divergent recovery trajectories, a pattern not fully explained by physical damage alone (e.g. Refs. [4–6]). Resilience has been conceptualized as the capacity of communities to absorb, recover from, and even transform themselves in response to disasters [7,8]. A growing body of literature has identified multiple dimensions of resilience—social, economic, institutional, infrastructural, and environmental—that interact to shape post-disaster response and recovery processes [1,7,9,10].

To measure and operationalize community resilience, researchers have developed diverse indicators to assess this multidimensional construct. These indicators are premised on the assumption that communities with higher resilience experience less severe impacts and recover more rapidly after a disaster. However, despite the growing use of these indicators in policy and research [11,12], there is a lack of rigorous evidence demonstrating that higher pre-disaster scores on resilience indicators are consistently associated with improved post-disaster outcomes [13–16].

Recent studies have attempted to validate resilience indicators using post-disaster recovery data, particularly those constructed from secondary sources (e.g. Refs. [14,17–20]). For instance, Burton [14] identified a subset of Disaster Resilience of Place (DROP) indicators [9] that predicted built environment recovery after Hurricane Katrina. Song et al. [18] showed that infrastructural and sociodemographic characteristics explained variations in industrial recovery after flooding in China. While these studies provide important insights, secondary data indicators often fail to capture the socially embedded dimensions of resilience, such as trust, informal support, and social participation, that may be critical for community-level recovery.

To address these limitations, several researchers have used primary post-disaster data to incorporate social and perceptual dimensions into resilience measurement [21–23]. These studies have shown that leadership, civic engagement, social support, and trust can predict subjective recovery and well-being. However, these measures capture only post-disaster conditions; they do not measure pre-disaster resilience, nor do they rely on the retrospective recall of pre-disaster conditions to avoid recall bias after a disaster. Consequently, they have not been able to reveal whether communities’ social characteristics before a disaster influence their recovery trajectories. This gap is fundamental because disaster resilience research (e.g. Refs. [1,7]) assumes that pre-existing community capacities shape post-disaster recovery; however, this assumption has rarely been tested using causal or longitudinal evidence. Only a few studies have attempted to examine whether pre-disaster community resilience, including its socially embedded dimensions, such as social capital, can predict post-disaster outcomes.

A unique exception is the Iwanuma Study [24], a field site of the Japan Agency for Gerontological Evaluation Study (JAGES) (https://www.jages.net/). The baseline of the Iwanuma Study was established in August 2010, seven months before the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and Tsunami (GEJET), when a population-based survey of all residents aged 65 years or older was conducted. The Iwanuma Study included questionnaire-based measures of individual- and community-level social capital. These pre-disaster data were linked to a follow-up survey conducted approximately 2.5 years after the disaster, offering a unique opportunity to examine the relationship between pre- and post-disaster community characteristics and post-disaster outcomes [24]. Previous analyses of the Iwanuma Study demonstrated that pre-disaster social capital predicted a range of post-disaster health outcomes, including a lower risk of depression [25], post-traumatic stress disorder [26], functional decline [27], and cognitive decline [28]. These studies provide rare longitudinal evidence of the protective effects of pre-existing social environments among older adults. However, no study has examined whether pre-disaster community resilience affects disaster recovery at the household and neighborhood levels.

The current study directly addresses this gap by examining the prospective associations between pre-disaster community resilience indicators, measured months before the 2011 GEJET, and subjective post-disaster recovery. Using the unique natural experimental setting of the Iwanuma Study, we assess whether these pre-disaster community resilience factors help explain variation in two recovery outcomes obtained 2.5 years after the disaster—self-reported recovery of individual daily life and self-reported recovery of the neighborhood—conditional on realized disaster exposure and damage severity. This study provides a rare longitudinal test of resilience theory, evidence on which components of community resilience matter for disaster recovery, and practical insights for strengthening communities in preparation for future disasters.

Study area

Iwanuma City was selected because it is the site of the Iwanuma Study, a rare longitudinal cohort with both pre-disaster and post-disaster data that enables a natural experimental examination of community resilience and recovery.

Pre-disaster condition of Iwanuma City

According to Japan’s Statistics Bureau [29], Iwanuma City, located in Miyagi Prefecture in northeastern Japan, had a population of 44,187 residents and 15,519 households as of October 2010. The city’s economy was primarily based on agriculture, small- and medium-scale manufacturing, and service industries. Its coastal areas featured agricultural land and fishing activities, while the inland zones were more urbanized. Although not a major industrial hub, Iwanuma City benefited from its proximity to Sendai City, the regional capital, and Sendai Airport, contributing to its moderate economic stability. Demographically, the city was experiencing gradual population aging. In 2010, 19.8% of Iwanuma City’s population was aged 65 or older [30].

Disaster damage

On March 11, 2011, Iwanuma was severely affected by the GEJET (Fig. 1). The tsunami inundated approximately 48% of the city’s area, resulting in 187 fatalities. The disaster also caused substantial damage to housing. Altogether, 5428 houses were damaged, including 736 completely destroyed houses, corresponding to the “major” category in the government-defined damage assessment (see Table A1 in the Appendix). In addition to the extensive housing destruction, large areas of farmland and several manufacturing facilities were inundated, causing widespread disruption across the city [31,32].

Iwanuma City and the tsunami inundation area of the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and Tsunami.

Post-disaster recovery context (2011–2013)

Immediately after the disaster, 26 evacuation shelters accommodated evacuees in Iwanuma City, the number of which peaked at approximately 6700. Subsequently, to help evacuees stay with their pre-disaster neighbors and preserve community ties among affected residents, the municipal government consolidated the evacuees into a limited number of larger shelters. This principle of maintaining pre-disaster community ties was also applied to temporary housing provision [33]. In collaboration with Iwanuma City, the Miyagi Prefectural Government located prefabricated temporary housing in a small number of consolidated sites, enabling residents from the same districts to be housed together. In parallel, the prefecture secured private rental units and provided leased temporary housing [31].

At the time of the October 2013 follow-up survey conducted by the Iwanuma Study, some residents had already moved out of temporary housing, whereas many others remained. As described in Section 4, even among individuals who had experienced similar levels of disaster-related damage, notable differences emerged in their subjective perceptions of recovery, in both their daily lives and neighborhoods. Understanding these differences, with particular attention paid to pre-disaster community resilience, constitutes the central research question of this study.

Methods

Study population

The Iwanuma Study is a subset of the nationwide JAGES cohort conducted in 2010 to investigate the social determinants of health among older community-dwelling adults. We used Iwanuma City’s 99 administrative districts (gyousei-ku) as community units, which serve as the foundation for pre-existing neighborhood organizations for disaster preparedness activities. The geographical size of these districts varies considerably (4631–5,519,550 m2).

Before the disaster, in August 2010, a population-based mail survey using the municipal resident registry was conducted, targeting all residents aged 65 years or older (n = 8576) (Fig. 2). The mailed questionnaire assessed pre-disaster characteristics, including health status, sociodemographic factors, and subjective evaluations of community conditions (e.g., trust, administrative service, local activities), which were used to construct resilience indicators. The baseline survey achieved a response rate of 59.0% (n = 5058).

Participants’ flow (n = 3523).

A follow-up survey was conducted in October 2013, approximately 2.5 years after the disaster, using face-to-face interviews to collect information on disaster experiences, current health, community conditions, and self-reported recovery. Among the baseline respondents, 3594 were contacted (follow-up rate: 82.1%). After excluding respondents who lacked valid content (n = 27), whose answers showed internal inconsistency (n = 40), and those residing in districts with fewer than five respondents to avoid biased multilevel estimations (n = 4) [34], the analytic sample comprised 3523 respondents nested within 98 districts (district sample size range: 6–132; median: 35.9).

Outcome variables

The outcome variables were self-reported (i.e., perceived) recovery of daily life and self-reported recovery of the neighborhood assessed in October 2013. Respondents rated recovery on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from “not at all” to “completely” (see Table 1 for the question items). For the analysis, the responses were dichotomized into completely/mostly versus halfway/slightly/not at all. This dichotomization reflects a substantive threshold in the disaster recovery context, where reporting “completely” or “mostly” recovered indicates that individuals have largely regained stable living conditions, whereas the remaining categories represent incomplete recovery. In addition, because Likert scales are ordinal and the intervals between categories cannot be assumed equal, regression results based on a five-point scale require cautious interpretation; dichotomization, therefore, facilitates a more straightforward interpretation.

Subjective recovery indicators are increasingly used in disaster research (e.g. Refs. [21–23,35,36]) to capture individuals’ holistic appraisals across multiple domains, such as housing, livelihoods, access to services, and social relationships. In severely inundated areas of Iwanuma City, large-scale land use changes and collective relocation fundamentally altered the built environment, making objective recovery indicators (e.g., population size or housing stock) unable to return to meaningful pre-disaster baselines. Therefore, we interpret self-reported (perceived) recovery as a valid and contextually appropriate proxy for assessing post-disaster recovery in Iwanuma while acknowledging that it does not directly measure objective reconstruction.

Explanatory variables

We selected 10 pre-disaster resilience indicators from the 2010 baseline survey based on the established resilience theory [1] and prior empirical studies [23,27,28]. This operationalization of community resilience is necessarily partial rather than comprehensive. While prior theoretical frameworks conceptualize community resilience as a multidimensional construct, including domains such as physical exposure, infrastructural robustness [7,9], communication systems, and institutional coordination [1], these dimensions were not directly measured in the 2010 baseline survey. Therefore, this study focuses on the social and economic dimensions of resilience measurable prior to the disaster. In line with the study’s objective to examine whether pre-existing social and economic conditions influence post-disaster recovery, hazard intensity and disaster damage were treated as exposure variables and controlled for in the statistical models rather than conceptualized as components of resilience (see Section 3.4 for details). This approach allows us to assess the explanatory role of pre-disaster community resilience conditional on disaster severity.

All indicators in this study represent the respondents’ subjective evaluations of their economic and social conditions prior to the disaster. Economic indicators captured the respondents’ assessments of local conditions: perceived changes in the local economy, income inequality, and administrative services. These three indicators complement the broader construct of economic development, particularly resource equity. Within the theoretical framework, equitable distribution and access to services and opportunities are central to community resilience [1]. The social capital indicators comprised cognitive dimensions (trust, mutual help, and place attachment) and structural dimensions, including emotional and instrumental support, social participation (the number of community groups respondents engaged in), and perceived changes in local activities and interactions. Previous empirical studies have also shown that these indicators influence individual health [27,28] and recovery processes [23].

Individual-level indicators were measured directly for each respondent in the 2010 baseline survey using the items described in Table 1. Consistent with the method used in previous research (e.g. Refs. [27,28]), community-level indicators were calculated as district-level means of the corresponding individual responses.

Covariates

Disaster exposure and individual characteristics were adjusted to isolate the association between pre-disaster community resilience and recovery outcomes. Disaster-related variables were obtained in the 2013 follow-up survey and included housing damage, district-level rate of housing damage, and bereavement. Housing damage was determined based on government-defined inspections and categorized into five levels (0 = no damage, 1 = minor damage, 2 = moderate−, 3 = moderate+, and 4 = major damage) (see Table A1 in the Appendix for the definition) [37,38]. Households with moderate− or greater housing damage were eligible for temporary housing support provided by the government because their residential functionality was considered substantially impaired. We also constructed a district-level indicator of destruction, defined as the proportion of respondents whose houses sustained moderate− or greater damage. This measure captures the broader environmental impact experienced by the respondents, such as damage to local infrastructure, disruption of services, and the loss of familiar neighborhood surroundings, beyond damage to any single household. Bereavement was defined as the loss of relatives or close friends (0 = no, 1 = yes).

Individual covariates from the 2010 baseline survey included age (continuous variable in years), sex (male or female), education (score range: 1 (<6 years) to 4 (≥13years)), homeownership (0 = no, 1 = yes), equalized income (score range: 1 (<1.2 million yen) to 4 (≥3.6 million yen)), and depression tendency (score range: 0–15). Equalized income was calculated as reported gross household income divided by the square root of the number of household members. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Japanese version of the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), a self-administered instrument comprising 15 yes/no questions, with higher scores indicating greater levels of depressive tendency [39].

Statistical analysis

We used two-level multilevel logistic regression models to estimate the association between pre-disaster resilience indicators and two binary outcomes: self-reported recovery of (i) neighborhood and (ii) individual daily life. In our models, individuals (level 1) were nested within districts (level 2: community level). For individual i in district j, the binary outcome variable Yij equals 1 if the respondent reports being “completely or mostly recovered” regarding the neighborhood or daily life, and 0 otherwise.

For Models 3–5, we added one resilience indicator at a time to assess its independent explanatory association with recovery. We therefore estimated parallel models for each resilience indicator rather than mutually adjusting all indicators within a model. This approach was chosen to minimize multicollinearity among conceptually related resilience measures and maintain model stability given the limited number of Level-2 units (n = 98). In addition to Model 5, we tested the interactions between community-level resilience and the district-level rate of severe housing damage (i.e., the proportion of houses with moderate− or greater damage). However, none were statistically significant; therefore, these results are not reported here.

We generated 50 imputed datasets using multiple imputations by chained equations (MICE) to address potential bias due to missing data. Because trust, mutual help, and place attachment are highly correlated, we excluded trust and place attachment from the imputation model to avoid multicollinearity. Mutual help was retained because it had the lowest VIF among the three indicators. The imputation model also included all other analytical variables and 15 GDS items. As a sensitivity analysis, we tested stratified multilevel logistic regression models by tsunami inundation status (inundated vs. non-inundated districts) to examine whether the observed associations differed between tsunami-affected and non-affected areas. We also estimated multilevel ordered logistic regression models using the original five-point recovery scale to examine the robustness of the results to the dichotomization of the recovery outcome variables. All models were constructed using Stata (version 18.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Comparisons with 2010 census data for older residents in Iwanuma City [29] indicated that our analytic sample was broadly representative in terms of sex and employment status, although the respondents were slightly younger and more likely to be married (see Table A2 in the Appendix).

In 2013, 74.9% of the respondents perceived their neighborhood as completely or mostly recovered, and 79.4% reported the same levels of recovery in their daily lives. More than half of the participants experienced housing damage, and one-third reported the loss of family members or close friends. Pre-disaster individual-level resilience indicators generally reflect favorable social conditions, as shown in Table 2. The respondents reported moderately high levels of trust, mutual help, and place attachment, and more than 90% had access to emotional or instrumental support. However, social participation averaged less than one organized group per person. Economic development, income inequality, and administrative service evaluations suggest relatively stable conditions before the disaster. At the community level, the aggregated indicators showed mean values similar to those observed at the individual level. Across the 98 districts, the standard deviations of all indicators were roughly 5–10% of each variable’s theoretical range, and no indicators exhibited unusually limited variability.

In the null model (Model 0), as shown in Table 3, substantial between-district variation—random intercepts at the community-level—was observed for both outcomes (Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) = 0.34 for neighborhood recovery and ICC = 0.29 for daily life), supporting the use of multilevel modeling.

Associations between pre-disaster resilience indicators and the two recovery outcomes were examined using models that incorporated individual- and community-level variables (Models 3–5). At the individual level, mutual help (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.06–1.33) and place attachment (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.02–1.31) were significantly associated with higher odds of perceiving neighborhood recovery (see Table A6 and A7 in the Appendix). Community-level social participation was positively associated with perceived recovery of individual daily life (OR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.28–3.43) (Table 4). Sensitivity analyses stratified by tsunami inundation status yielded patterns of associations that were broadly consistent with those observed in the main multilevel regression analysis. Because stratification reduced the number of districts at the community level and thus statistical power, we focused on the direction of associations rather than statistical significance. The key community resilience indicators—social participation, administrative services, and local activities and interactions—showed consistent directions of association in both inundated and non-inundated districts with those observed in the main analysis (see Tables A10–12 in the Appendix). Additionally, sensitivity analyses using multilevel ordered logistic regression models with the original five-point recovery scale yielded similar patterns to those observed in the main (dichotomized) models for the key community resilience indicators (see Tables A13–15). At the same time, several associations that were not statistically significant in the main models became significant in the ordered models (see Tables A16–18). These include negative associations of community-level trust and mutual help with individual recovery, as well as a positive interaction between community-level emotional support and housing damage for neighborhood recovery. However, as these associations were not observed in the main models, they may be dependent on model specification and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Taken together, these sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the main findings. Therefore, the following discussion focuses on the results from the dichotomized models. Additional associations identified in the ordered models are not considered central to the main conclusions, as they were not consistent across specifications.

Discussion

This study examined whether pre-disaster community resilience helped explain self-reported recovery 2.5 years after the GEJET. As expected, both individual-level housing damage and bereavement adversely affected perceived recovery. Importantly, district-level severe housing damage also showed a substantial negative impact on perceived recovery, especially at the neighborhood level, suggesting that broader environmental damage beyond personal loss shapes residents’ perceptions of recovery. Against this backdrop, two pre-disaster community-level factors—social participation and local activities and interactions—were associated with more favorable recovery. Social participation had a direct positive effect on daily life recovery, whereas local activities and interactions buffered the adverse effects of severe housing damage. Conversely, respondents in districts with higher pre-disaster evaluations of administrative services experienced a stronger negative impact of housing damage on neighborhood recovery.

A major strength of this study is its prospective natural experimental design, which enabled us to investigate the effect of community resilience as it existed before the GEJET, addressing a major gap in prior research that relied on either pre-disaster secondary data or post-disaster surveys to measure resilience. Using pre-disaster social survey data, we tested the explanatory associations of resilience indicators rooted in residents’ social environments with post-disaster recovery. The multilevel modeling framework allowed us to evaluate the contextual effects of community-level resilience while adjusting for individual characteristics and disaster exposure, thereby reducing potential confounding.

However, this study had several limitations. The operationalization of community resilience was partial, as only selected social and economic dimensions were measurable in the baseline survey. Therefore, the findings do not imply that other theoretically proposed resilience domains are unimportant for recovery. Furthermore, the study population comprised older adults living in a single city, which limits the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the findings should be interpreted in light of the specific socio-institutional context of Iwanuma City, a relatively small municipality where neighborhood-based administrative districts (gyousei-ku) functioned as well-established community units prior to the disaster and where post-disaster evacuation and temporary housing policies purposively sought to preserve original neighborhood groupings. Such structural conditions may have strengthened the role of pre-disaster social ties. In larger and more densely populated metropolitan settings, where residential mobility is higher and neighborhood cohesion may be weaker, the mechanisms through which community resilience operates may differ. Therefore, the applicability of the findings to major disasters in highly urbanized contexts requires careful consideration.

Methodologically, several additional considerations warrant attention. All resilience indicators and recovery outcomes were based on self-reported perceptions. Although subjective recovery captures individuals’ holistic appraisals, it may not fully correspond to objective indicators of physical reconstruction, and psychological adaptation or shifting expectations may have affected responses. Moreover, the resilience indicators comprised binary, ordinal, and count variables, which were modeled as linear predictors without formally testing nonlinearity or scale effects. While this approach preserves interpretability and aligns with our explanatory modeling framework, it may limit strict comparability across indicators and should be interpreted with caution. In addition, sensitivity analyses using the original five-point recovery scale also identified a limited number of additional statistically significant associations not observed in the dichotomized models. These associations may reflect effects on more nuanced variation in perceived recovery. However, since they were not consistent across model specifications, they are interpreted cautiously and are not considered part of the central conclusions of this study.

Another limitation is that, although community resilience is inherently multidimensional, we evaluated each resilience indicator separately and did not examine interactions among multiple community-level indicators, as doing so would substantially increase model complexity relative to the number of level-2 units (n = 98). Future research may explore the structural interrelationships among resilience components using theoretically specified models. Furthermore, unmeasured post-disaster contextual factors, such as variations in governmental response or external assistance, may have influenced recovery. Finally, individuals who experienced severe damage may have been more likely to die, relocate outside the city, or move into long-term care facilities, potentially leading to attrition bias and affecting the estimated associations.

Our findings align with the previous evidence from the Iwanuma Study, indicating the protective effects of community social participation on health outcomes after the GEJET (e.g., cognitive and functional decline [27,28]). These findings are broadly consistent with international research on social capital and disaster recovery. Qualitative studies from Kobe (following the 1995 Great Hanshin-Awaji Earthquake), Gujarat (after the 2001 Gujarat Earthquake), and Hurricane Katrina in 2005 have emphasized that communities with strong social ties recover more effectively [4,40]. A recent intervention study in Ofunato demonstrated that strengthening social connectedness after a disaster can enhance self-reported recovery [23]. Most international evidence relies on qualitative accounts or post-disaster measurements of social capital. This study advances the literature by providing prospective, quantitative evidence that pre-disaster community conditions, specifically social participation and local activities and interactions, shape post-disaster recovery. The absence of significant associations with other resilience indicators supports the hypothesis that not all dimensions of community resilience operate similarly across contexts [21], underscoring the need for a more nuanced empirical validation of resilience indicators.

There are several possible explanations for these findings. As summarized in Table 7, the indicators that showed meaningful associations with recovery outcomes exhibited varying types of effects. The differing effects of community-level social participation and local activities and interactions may reflect distinct forms of social capital and recovery mechanisms. Social participation, measured by involvement in hobbies, sports, and volunteer groups, likely reflects organized and outward-looking forms of engagement. Such participation connects individuals to broader networks beyond their immediate neighborhood and may provide opportunities to access diverse information, resources, and support that facilitate recovery processes, consistent with prior studies that highlight the role of broader social networks in disaster recovery [5,40]. In communities with such high levels of participation, the distribution of information and supplies for disaster recovery can be carried out efficiently, and the benefits may even extend to those who are not directly part of the network. This may explain why community-level social participation was associated with better recovery outcomes, largely independent of the level of individual damage.

By contrast, local activities and interactions may reflect more informal, place-based social ties embedded in everyday neighborhood life. These ties are characterized by frequent contact, familiarity, and mutual awareness among nearby residents, which may facilitate informal caregiving and practical assistance during times of disruption, as highlighted in prior studies on the role of place-based social ties in disaster recovery [5,40]. The preservation of pre-existing social networks has been noted as a critical factor in post-disaster recovery in previous studies [23,41]. In the context of the GEJET, Iwanuma City implemented temporary housing allocation strategies that preserved pre-disaster community groupings, allowing residents to stay together with their original neighbors. This continuity likely helped maintain pre-existing support networks even after displacement, thereby facilitating recovery among individuals who experienced substantial disaster impacts.

Community-level administrative services showed a negative interaction with housing damage, suggesting that higher pre-disaster evaluations of public services may have amplified dissatisfaction with neighborhood recovery among residents whose houses were severely damaged. One possible explanation is that residents in areas with more favorable perceptions of administrative services may have developed higher expectations regarding post-disaster recovery, and when these expectations were not met, the perceived gap between pre-disaster standards and post-disaster realities intensified dissatisfaction. This interpretation is consistent with prior research showing that even among beneficiaries of public reconstruction housing, individuals who perceived higher-quality pre-disaster neighborhood environments tend to report lower satisfaction with post-disaster housing conditions [41]. This suggests that higher baseline living standards and service expectations increase sensitivity to discrepancies between expected and actual recovery conditions. In addition, the district-level severe housing damage rate likely reflects a broader disruption to infrastructure, local services, familiar landscapes, and place identity, thereby exerting a distinct adverse influence on perceived neighborhood recovery. Taken together, these findings suggest that pre-disaster social capital, administrative context, and district-level damage shape recovery perceptions through different pathways.

The findings of this study have important implications for community resilience indicators and their relevance to disaster recovery policies. Among the examined indicators, two pre-disaster community-level factors—social participation and local activities and interactions—were valid and meaningful components of community resilience. These results indicate that enhancing such forms of social connectedness before disasters may contribute to more favorable recovery trajectories. By contrast, the negative interaction observed between community-level administrative services and housing damage suggests that this indicator warrants reconsideration as a measure of resilience because higher pre-disaster evaluations did not translate into better recovery perceptions among severely affected residents. Finally, the strong adverse association between district-level housing destruction and perceived neighborhood recovery underscores that recovery cannot be achieved through household-level assistance alone; rather, it highlights the need for the collective recovery of shared landscapes and environments, along with efforts to maintain and rebuild community social capital.

Leveraging the unique natural experimental setting of the Iwanuma Study, this research provides rare longitudinal evidence supporting the core assumption of community resilience theory that communities with higher pre-disaster resilience tend to achieve more favorable post-disaster recovery outcomes. At the same time, the findings show that community resilience is composed of multiple social elements that operate in distinct ways: different aspects of communities relate to recovery through different pathways. By empirically examining the pre-disaster community resilience indicators that were significantly associated with self-reported recovery, this study contributes to the validation of resilience indicators and highlights the importance of strengthening social connectedness during ordinary times.
