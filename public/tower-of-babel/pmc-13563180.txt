Transient neonatal hypothyroidism due to maternal seaweed soup ingestion: a report of 3 cases
Authors: Sofya Ilmer, Vivienne Lopez-Cabreza, Parissa Salemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeaweed soup (miyeok-guk) is traditionally consumed daily by Korean women during pregnancy and postpartum, often resulting in maternal iodine intake exceeding 2000 µg/day, which can induce neonatal hypothyroidism (NH) in breastfed infants. Neonatal hypothyroidism refers to transient thyroid dysfunction from extrinsic iodine exposure, distinct from congenital hypothyroidism (CH) due to intrinsic thyroid defects. We present 3 infants of Korean descent with NH associated with maternal seaweed soup consumption. Case 1 is a preterm infant with severe hypothyroidism resolving rapidly after maternal discontinuation of seaweed soup, with levothyroxine discontinued at 6 months. Case 2 presented with subclinical hypothyroidism on day of life 11 and an atypical course requiring dose adjustments before proving transient at age 3.5 years. Case 3 had severe hypothyroidism at birth and was treated for 6 years as presumed CH; however, levothyroxine dose remained unchanged despite significant growth, and therapy was discontinued after maternal dietary history was obtained, confirming transient disease. These cases demonstrate that initial thyroid-stimulating hormone severity poorly predicts permanence and that levothyroxine dose trajectory is more reliable. Culturally sensitive history-taking and systematic reevaluation at age 3 can prevent unnecessary lifelong therapy.

Introduction

Neonatal hypothyroidism (NH) due to maternal seaweed soup consumption is a recognized phenomenon in East Asian cultures, where it is traditionally consumed during pregnancy and postpartum to promote lactation. We use “neonatal hypothyroidism” to reflect its acquired nature from extrinsic iodine exposure, distinct from congenital hypothyroidism (CH), which typically results from intrinsic thyroid defects such as thyroid dysgenesis (absent, ectopic, or hypoplastic gland) or dyshormonogenesis (defects in thyroid hormone synthesis). While CH is most often permanent and detected on newborn screening (NBS), NH caused by iodine excess is characteristically transient and resolves once the iodine source is removed, though the 2 entities may be clinically indistinguishable at initial presentation.

Seaweed contains extremely high iodine concentrations, ranging from 16 µg/g in nori to over 8000 µg/g in certain kelp products, with substantial variability depending on species, geographic origin, and processing conditions [1]. Excessive maternal intake elevates breast milk iodine levels, potentially inducing hypothyroidism in susceptible neonates through the Wolff–Chaikoff effect. The American Thyroid Association recommends women ingest approximately 250 µg of iodine daily during pregnancy and lactation, cautioning that sustained intake above 500 to 1100 µg/day should be avoided [2]. However, Korean postpartum women often consume over 2000 µg/day [3-6]. Importantly, initial thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) severity does not reliably predict whether NH will be transient or permanent, and failure to obtain a thorough maternal dietary history may result in years of unnecessary treatment.

US case reports of seaweed-induced NH remain rare, and prolonged unnecessary treatment resulting from missed dietary history has not been well described. We present 3 cases illustrating the clinical spectrum of iodine-induced NH and highlight the importance of culturally sensitive history-taking and systematic reassessment.

Case presentation

Case 1

A female infant born at 31 weeks gestation to a Korean-American mother presented on day of life (DOL) 29. Initial NBS on DOL 3 was normal; repeat screening on DOL 23 revealed TSH 158.9 μIU/mL (SI [International System of Units]: 158.9 mIU/L) (reference range, 0.70-11.00 μIU/mL [SI: 0.70-11.00 mIU/L]). Maternal dietary history consisted of seaweed soup 1 to 2 times daily following delivery while predominantly breastfeeding.

Case 2

A full-term female infant born to a Korean-American mother who consumed seaweed soup prenatally was predominantly breastfed. Newborn screening showed slightly elevated TSH (numeric value not reported by the New York State screening program).

Case 3

A full-term female infant born to a Korean-American mother had abnormal NBS showing TSH 460.3 μIU/mL (SI: 460.3 mIU/L) and total thyroxine (T4) 6.1 μg/dL (SI: 78.5 nmol/L).

Diagnostic assessment

Case 1

Confirmatory testing showed TSH 295 μIU/mL (SI: 295 mIU/L), free thyroxine (FT4) 0.7 ng/dL (SI: 9.0 pmol/L) (reference range, 0.9-1.8 ng/dL [SI: 11.6-23.2 pmol/L]), and T4 5.9 μg/dL (SI: 75.9 nmol/L) (reference range, 4.6-12.0 μg/dL [SI: 59.2-154.4 nmol/L]). The mother immediately discontinued seaweed soup.

Case 2

Confirmatory testing on DOL 11 revealed TSH 53 μIU/mL (SI: 53 mIU/L) with normal FT4 1.2 ng/dL (SI: 15.4 pmol/L).

Case 3

Evaluation on DOL 7 confirmed severe primary hypothyroidism (TSH 456 μIU/mL [SI: 456 mIU/L], T4 4.3 μg/dL [SI: 55.3 nmol/L]).

Treatment

Case 1

Levothyroxine 25 µg daily (12 µg/kg/day) was initiated.

Case 2

Levothyroxine 37.5 µg daily (11.4 µg/kg/day) was initiated on DOL 12. Two weeks later, TSH was suppressed (0.68 μIU/mL [SI: 0.68 mIU/L]) with elevated FT4 and T4, prompting dose reduction to 25 µg daily. By 3 months, the dose was further reduced to alternating 12.5/25 µg daily (3.6 µg/kg/day). However, at 15 months, TSH rose to 6.09 μIU/mL (SI: 6.09 mIU/L), requiring a dose increase to 25 µg daily (2.0 µg/kg/day). At 33 months, TSH rose again to 8.85 μIU/mL (SI: 8.85 mIU/L), necessitating continuation of 25 µg daily (1.8 µg/kg/day).

Case 3

Levothyroxine 37.5 µg daily (13 µg/kg/day) was initiated. One month later, TSH was normalized with elevated thyroid hormone levels (T4 17.7 μg/dL [SI: 227.7 nmol/L], FT4 3.3 ng/dL [SI: 42.5 pmol/L]). Her levothyroxine dose remained unchanged for 6 years despite significant growth.

Outcome and follow-up

Case 1

Two weeks later, TSH normalized to 0.65 μIU/mL (SI: 0.65 mIU/L) with elevated T4 and FT4, indicating rapid recovery and iatrogenic hyperthyroxinemia. Levothyroxine was progressively reduced and discontinued at 6 months. She remained euthyroid postdiscontinuation (Fig. 1A).

Case 2

At age 3.5 years, levothyroxine was reduced to 12.5 µg daily (0.9 µg/kg/day). Given the low-dose requirement and maternal iodine exposure history, levothyroxine was discontinued after 5 months of stable thyroid function tests (TFTs) (TSH 3.61 μIU/mL [SI: 3.61 mIU/L], FT4 1.4 ng/dL [SI: 18.0 pmol/L]). Two months postdiscontinuation, TSH rose to 7.39 μIU/mL (SI: 7.39 mIU/L) (reference range for age, 0.70-6.00 μIU/mL [SI: 0.70-6.00 mIU/L]); however, repeat testing 4 months later showed spontaneous normalization (TSH 4.16 μIU/mL [SI: 4.16 mIU/L], FT4 1.3 ng/dL [SI: 16.7 pmol/L]). She remains euthyroid off therapy (Fig. 1B).

Case 3

At 6 years 2 months, after transfer to a new provider, detailed dietary history revealed maternal consumption of seaweed soup up to 4 times weekly during pregnancy and postpartum. This history, combined with lack of dose escalation, raised suspicion for transient etiology. Levothyroxine was initially tapered and eventually discontinued. Follow-up at 2 and 4 months postdiscontinuation confirmed normal TFTs. She remains euthyroid off medication (Fig. 1C).

Discussion

High iodine concentrations from maternal seaweed soup consumption can induce the Wolff–Chaikoff effect, where excessive intrathyroidal iodine inhibits thyroid hormone synthesis [7]. Normally, iodine is transported into thyroid cells via the sodium/iodide symporter (NIS) and organified by thyroid peroxidase (TPO) to form thyroid hormones. However, when intrathyroidal iodine levels rise sharply, organification is inhibited through suppression of hydrogen peroxide generation, which is essential for TPO activity [8].

In healthy adults, the thyroid typically “escapes” from the Wolff–Chaikoff effect within 1 to 2 weeks through downregulation of NIS expression, which reduces iodine uptake and allows hormone synthesis to resume. However, the fetal and neonatal thyroid may fail to escape this inhibitory effect, particularly in preterm infants with immature hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axes [9].

During pregnancy, excessive maternal iodine from seaweed soup crosses the placenta, directly exposing the fetus. Postpartum, iodine is concentrated in breast milk, leading to sustained exposure in the nursing infant and prolonged suppression of thyroid hormone synthesis [10, 11]. This explains the variable timing of presentation: prenatal exposure may cause an abnormal initial newborn screen, whereas postpartum-onset consumption may result in a normal initial screen followed by delayed hypothyroidism as iodine accumulates via breast milk. Discontinuation of maternal seaweed intake and temporary levothyroxine therapy typically leads to resolution once the iodine load clears [10]. Understanding the source of this iodine excess requires recognition of the cultural practices that drive maternal intake. Despite guideline recognition of iodine excess as a cause of transient hypothyroidism, seaweed consumption is not specifically addressed, and US case reports remain rare [10, 12].

The postpartum consumption of seaweed soup is deeply rooted in Korean and other East Asian cultures, believed to promote maternal recovery and lactation [5, 13]. While traditionally emphasized postpartum, many women begin consumption in the third trimester. Studies show over 93% of Korean postpartum women consume seaweed soup daily, with median iodine intakes exceeding 2000 to 3000 µg/day—far above the recommended upper limit of 500 to 1100 µg/day for lactating women [2, 4-6, 13]. This practice is often continued by immigrant families in the United States. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) emphasizes that culturally effective care requires inquiry into traditional health practices, as they may not be spontaneously disclosed [14]. A culturally sensitive approach helps identify modifiable risk factors otherwise overlooked [15]. The cases presented illustrate this principle and demonstrate the heterogeneous outcomes of iodine-induced NH.

Case 1 exemplifies preterm infant vulnerability to iodine excess. This infant had a normal initial newborn screen but developed severe hypothyroidism by DOL 23—consistent with delayed TSH elevation seen in preterm infants due to immaturity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis. Preterm infants have reduced thyroid reserve, diminished TSH surge at birth, and cannot escape iodine-induced inhibition as efficiently as term infants [12, 16]. This environmental trigger and developmental vulnerability caused profound but transient hypothyroidism that resolved rapidly after removing the iodine source [12, 17]. This case highlights how environmental exposures can interact with developmental immaturity to produce a phenotype mimicking permanent hypothyroidism but is reversible. Importantly, when seaweed soup consumption begins postpartum, a normal initial newborn screen does not exclude subsequent hypothyroidism. Unlike preterm infants, full-term infants do not routinely undergo repeat screening, raising concern that cases may go undiagnosed.

Case 3 illustrates how delayed recognition of transient etiology can result in years of avoidable treatment. This patient received levothyroxine replacement therapy for 6 years learning of maternal seaweed soup consumption. Notably, her levothyroxine dose remained consistent, which should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis of CH [12, 18]. The AAP recommends that if permanent CH has not been confirmed by imaging demonstrating thyroid dysgenesis or TSH elevation above 10 mIU/L during treatment, a trial off levothyroxine should be considered at 3 years of age, particularly in patients on low doses (<2 µg/kg/day) [12]. In contrast, case 2 demonstrates that prenatal iodine exposure can cause an abnormal initial newborn screen. When dietary history is obtained at presentation, it should alert clinicians to possible transient disease and prompt systematic reevaluation rather than assuming permanence. Earlier reassessment guided by maternal dietary history could have identified the transient nature of case 3 sooner.

Initial TSH concentration is not predictive of permanence; permanent hypothyroidism should not be assumed solely based on very elevated TSH at diagnosis. Predictors of transient disease include low levothyroxine requirement, stable dosing over time, and absence of TSH elevation during treatment. Pediatricians should obtain detailed dietary and cultural history at diagnosis, and pediatric endocrinologists should revisit this when the clinical course suggests transient disease.

Culturally sensitive history-taking, including specific inquiry about seaweed consumption, should be incorporated into routine prenatal, newborn, and postpartum care—particularly in East Asian families. Inquiry should include timing of consumption (prenatal vs postpartum) to anticipate the clinical presentation. When maternal iodine excess is identified, repeat thyroid function testing should be considered even if the initial newborn screen is normal, as delayed TSH elevation may occur as iodine accumulates via breast milk. For infants diagnosed with hypothyroidism in this context, consideration of a trial off levothyroxine at age 3 years can prevent years of unnecessary treatment.
