The lung immune prognostic index as a predictive biomarker in urological malignancies undergoing immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy
Authors: Yan He, Yuan Yuan, Xiao Wei, Lili Li, Pengcheng Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe conducted a meta-analysis to evaluate the prognostic utility of the Lung Immune Prognostic Index (LIPI) in patients with urological malignancies treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs).MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and EMBASE up to March 3, 2025. Clinical outcomes included overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), objective response rate (ORR), and disease control rate (DCR). Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), with a threshold score ≥6 defining high-quality studies.ResultsThis meta-analysis incorporated seven studies comprising 2498 patients. Our findings demonstrated that patients with good LIPI index exhibited significantly prolonged OS (good vs. intermediate: HR = 0.51, 95% CI 0.42–0.62, p < 0.001; good vs. poor: HR = 0.15, 95% CI 0.11–0.20, p < 0.001; good vs. Intermediate and poor: HR = 0.48, 95% CI 0.38–0.61, p < 0.001). The data further showed that patients with good LIPI index exhibited significantly prolonged PFS; (good vs. intermediate: HR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.57–0.76, p < 0.001; good vs. poor: HR = 0.23, 95% CI 0.14–0.37, p < 0.001; good vs. Intermediate and poor: HR = 0.73, 95% CI 0.66–0.81, p < 0.001). Additionally, we found that the good LIPI index correlated with higher ORR (good vs. intermediate: OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 0.98–2.28, p = 0.061; good vs. poor: OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.01–3.83, p = 0.047). The good LIPI index correlated with higher DCR (good vs. intermediate: OR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.53–3.01, p < 0.001; good vs. poor: OR = 5.08, 95% CI: 2.85–9.05, p < 0.001). No publication bias existed, and sensitivity analysis confirmed stable results.ConclusionThe LIPI emerges as a valuable prognostic biomarker in patients with urological malignancies treated with ICIs therapy.

Introduction

Urological malignancies, encompassing renal cell carcinoma, urothelial carcinoma, and related cancers, represent a global health burden with suboptimal outcomes under conventional chemotherapies and targeted therapies [1]. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has revolutionized treatment paradigms for advanced-stage diseases [2], yet therapeutic responses exhibit marked heterogeneity - while a subset of patients achieve durable survival benefits, others experience primary resistance or rapid disease progression [3,4]. This underscores the urgent need for universally applicable and cost-effective prognostic biomarkers to guide personalized therapeutic strategies.

The Lung Immune Prognostic Index (LIPI), a composite scoring system incorporating peripheral inflammatory markers (derived neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, dNLR) and serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), was initially validated for predicting immunotherapy efficacy and survival outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer [5]. Stratified into three tiers (0–2), recent evidence suggests LIPI’s prognostic utility across diverse malignancies [6–9]. Its simplicity has facilitated widespread application in prognosticating ICI-treated non-small cell lung cancer. Systemic inflammation plays a pivotal role in tumor progression, substantially reducing survival outcomes [10–12]. The dNLR serves as a robust surrogate for inflammatory status across malignancies. Neutrophils, central to inflammatory responses, exhibit dual functionality in direct tumor cytotoxicity and microenvironment modulation [13]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte density positively correlates with immunotherapy response rates and survival benefits [14]. Both LDH and dNLR have been identified as independent prognostic factors associated with adverse outcomes in diverse solid tumors [15,16]. However, its predictive value in urological oncology remains controversial due to inconsistent validation studies.

ICIs therapy has achieved remarkable progress in personalized cancer immunotherapy, frequently integrated into standard regimens alongside conventional therapies. However, not all patients derive clinical benefit from ICIs. Current predictive biomarkers for ICI efficacy encompass tumor mutational burden (TMB), PD-L1 expression, gut microbiome diversity, hypoxic microenvironment-driven interferon-γ signaling-mediated antigen presentation pathways, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [17]. Nevertheless, clinical implementation of these biomarkers faces challenges including insufficient standardization of detection methods, tissue acquisition difficulties, and high economic burdens.

This meta-analysis synthesizes data from seven clinical trials involving 2498 ICI-treated patients, systematically evaluating LIPI’s predictive capacity in urological malignancies. Our pooled analysis provides high-grade evidence to optimize risk-stratified immunotherapy protocols.

Methods

Literature search strategy

This meta-analysis adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [18]. A comprehensive literature search was systematically conducted using PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library on March 3, 2025. The search strategy employed Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms including: “Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors”. Terms searched in [All Fields], including “PD-1 inhibitor”, “PD-L1 inhibitor”, “CTLA-4 inhibitor”, “Pembrolizumab”, “Nivolumab”, “Atezolizumab”, “Ipilimumab”, “Avelumab”, “Tremelimumab”, “Durvalumab”, “Cemiplimab”, LIPI-related terms: “Lung Immune Prognostic Index”, “Respiration Immune Prognosis Index”, “Pulmonary Immune Prognosis Index”. The complete Boolean logic syntax and search filters are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) original clinical studies (cohort studies or clinical trials) involving patients with histologically confirmed urological malignancies; (2) all patients receiving at least one dose of ICI therapy; (3) explicit reporting of LIPI scores and risk stratification. The LIPI is determined by measuring blood levels of neutrophils, white blood cells, and lactate dehydrogenase. It is defined by a neutrophil/(white blood cells - neutrophils) ratio > 3 and lactate dehydrogenase exceeding the upper normal limit, categorizing patients into three groups: good (point 0), intermediate (point 1), and poor (point 2) and (4) availability of quantitative data for at least one clinical outcome, including overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), objective response rate (ORR), or disease control rate (DCR), hazard ratio (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) or extractable survival curve data. Exclusion criteria encompassed non-original research (e.g. reviews, meta-analyses, case reports), studies with incomplete data (e.g. undefined LIPI scoring methodology or irretrievable survival outcomes), trials with independently evaluated ICI efficacy, duplicate publications, and studies with unavailable full texts.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extracted from the selected studies included first author, publication year, study region, population characteristics, ICI agent types, tumor subtypes, sample size, and clinical outcomes (Table 1). Due to the high accuracy of multivariate analyses, priority was given to extract data from multivariate analyses of HR and OR over univariate analyses in the data extracting process. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), with a threshold score ≥6 defining high-quality studies.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, USA). Heterogeneity across studies was quantified using chi-square test and the I2 statistic. A random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method) was applied if high heterogeneity was detected (p < 0.10 or I2 > 50%); otherwise, a fixed-effects model (Mantel-Haenszel method) was employed. Publication bias was evaluated through funnel plot symmetry by Egger’s regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test. Significant bias (p < 0.10) prompted trim-and-fill adjustment to estimate corrected effect sizes. A sensitivity analysis was implemented to assess the stability of the results by excluding each study independently. For sensitivity analysis, the leave-one-out method was used to systematically recalculate pooled estimates after sequential exclusion of individual studies. All hypothesis testing was two-tailed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of study

Through a systematic screening process involving duplicate removal and relevance assessment, 58 initially identified articles underwent full-text evaluation, as a result seven studies meeting the inclusion criteria. All included studies utilized retrospective cohort designs, with two investigations (Pauline et al. and Lucia et al.) deriving cohorts from secondary analyses of prospective clinical trials. The literature selection process followed PRISMA guidelines (Figure 1). The NOS scores for studies ranging from 6 to 8, indicating moderate-to-high quality with a low risk of bias. Among the seven studies, three studies focused on metastatic urothelial carcinoma, two studies on metastatic renal cell carcinoma, one on renal cell carcinoma, and one on bladder cancer (Table 1).

Meta-analysis of OS

The association between LIPI levels and OS in urological malignancies patients ICIs was estimated using survival data from seven studies comprising 2498 patients. Based on LIPI index, the patients were grouped into three groups: good, intermediate, poor. In this study, we focused on three comparisons, between good and poor, between good and intermediate, between good and intermediate-poor. The results showed that there was significant heterogeneity between the good and intermediate-poor (I2 = 57.9%, p = 0.05), so a random-effects model was used. The results revealed that patients with good LIPI index had significantly better OS (HR: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.38–0.61, p < 0.001) (Figure 2A). We found that there was no significant heterogeneity between the good and intermediate (I2 = 14.2%, p = 0.324), so a fixed-effects model was used. The results revealed that patients with good LIPI index had significantly better OS (HR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.42–0.62, p < 0.001) (Figure 2B). We have found that there was no significant heterogeneity between the good and poor (I2 = 0%, p = 0.805), so a fixed-effects model was used. The results revealed that patients with good LIPI index had significantly better OS (HR: 0.15, 95% CI: 0.11–0.20, p < 0.001) (Figure 2C). We conducted a subgroup analysis of urinary tract tumor types and ICI agents. The subgroup analysis indicated that in patients with four types of urinary tract tumors-mUC, mRCC, RCC, and BC-a good LIPI score continued to demonstrate superior advantages in OS (Figure S1). This pattern was similarly observed in the ICI subgroup analysis, where monotherapy or combination therapy with ICI agents appeared to yield no significant difference in patient prognosis (Figure S2). Overall, the results demonstrated that good LIPI index had significant prolonged OS.

Meta-analysis of PFS

To evaluate the predictive value of LIPI for disease progression risk, this study further analyzed the association between LIPI scores and PFS. The LIPI good vs. intermediate-poor comparison showed low heterogeneity (I2 = 7.6%, p = 0.355), therefore the fixed-effects model was used to confirm that elevated progression risk in the combined group intermediate-poor (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.66–0.81, p < 0.001) (Figure 3A). For the LIPI good vs. intermediate comparison, no heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 0%, p = 0.788), and the fixed-effects model indicated shortened PFS in the intermediate group (HR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.57–0.76, p < 0.001) (Figure 3B). In the LIPI good vs. poor comparison, substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 70.7%, p = 0.009), therefor a random-effects model was used for the analysis. The pooled analysis revealed that a significantly increased progression risk in the poor LIPI group (HR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.14–0.37, p < 0.001) (Figure 3C). Notably, high heterogeneity in the good vs. poor comparison might potentially attribute to interstudy variations in metastatic disease management. Collectively, lower LIPI index correlated with superior PFS benefits. The concordant HR direction and effect magnitude (HR < 0.3) strongly supported LIPI’s predictive consistency for PFS. Subgroup analysis revealed that good LIPI demonstrated better prognostic correlation with PFS across three distinct urinary tract tumors and different ICIs (Figure S3 and S4). However, no significant statistical differences were observed between monotherapy and combination ICI regimens [19–21].

Meta-analysis of ORR and DCR

To further assess the predictive value of LIPI for treatment response, this study analyzed the correlation between LIPI scores and ORR. For the LIPI good and intermediate comparison, low heterogeneity was shown (I2 = 0%, p = 0.621), then we used the fixed-effects model. The results indicate a non-significant trend toward higher ORR in the good LIPI group (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 0.98–2.28, p = 0.061) (Figure 4A). Comparing the LIPI good with poor, no heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 0%, p = 0.607), so a fixed-effects model was used. The results demonstrated that patients with good LIPI index had a high ORR (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.01–3.83, p = 0.047) (Figure 4B). Furthermore, we explored the predictive capacity of LIPI index for DCR.

Similarly, the comparison of LIPI good and intermediate exhibited no homogeneity (I2 = 0%, p = 0.706), so the fixed-effects model was used. The results confirmed that significantly higher DCR in the LIPI good group (OR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.53–3.01, p < 0.001) (Figure 4C). The LIPI good vs. poor comparison showed minimal heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, p = 0.378), so we used a fixed-effects model as the statistical method, which revealed patients with good LIPI index had a lower DCR compared to patients with poor LIPI index (OR = 5.08, 95% CI: 2.85–9.05, p < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings by leave-out method.

Our analysis indicated that the pooled HR for OS did not significantly change after exclusion of any individual study (Figure 5). The comparison of LIPI good with intermediate-poor was within range of 0.38–0.61 (original HR = 0.48) (Figure 5A), the comparison of LIPI good with intermediate was within range of 0.42–0.62 (original HR = 0.51) (Figure 5B), and the comparison of the LIPI good with poor was within range of 0.11–0.20 (original HR = 0.15) (Figure 5C).

Publication bias

Potential publication bias was assessed via Egger’s and Begg’s tests combined with funnel plot symmetry. For OS analysis, no potential publication bias was observed, including comparation of good with intermediate-poor (Egger’s test p = 0.372, Begg’s test p = 0.806) (Supplementary Figure S5A), comparation of good with intermediate (Egger’s test p = 0.603, Begg’s test p = 1.000) (Supplementary Figure S5B), comparation of good with poor (Egger’s test p = 0.232, Begg’s test p = 0.800) (Supplementary Figure S5C). For PFS analyses, publication bias was near the threshold comparing good with intermediate-poor groups (Egger’s test p = 0.043; Begg’s test p = 0.080) (Supplementary Figure S6A). The preserved funnel plot symmetry and stable pooled HR upon sensitivity analysis (variation magnitude <15%) suggested this finding might stem from heterogeneity in intermediate/poor group definitions or small-sample effects. Despite the marginal bias signal in the PFS good vs. intermediate-poor group, the consistent effect direction (HR = 0.73, p < 0.001) supported result robustness. For other groups PFS analyses, no potential publication bias was observed, including comparation of good with intermediate (Egger’s test p = 0.297, Begg’s test p = 1.000) (Supplementary Figure S6B), comparation of good with poor (Egger’s test p = 0.413, Begg’s test p = 1.000) (Supplementary Figure S6C). ORR analyses demonstrated no detectable publication bias across comparisons (good vs. intermediate: Egger’s test p = 0.779, Begg’s test p = 1.000; good vs. poor: Egger’s test p = 0.105, Begg’s test p = 1.000) (Supplementary Figure S7A-B). DCR analyses demonstrated no detectable publication bias across comparisons (good vs. intermediate: Egger’s test p = 0.764, Begg’s test p = 1.000; good vs. poor: Egger’s test p = 0.962, Begg’s test p = 1.000) (Supplementary Figure S7C-D). Thus, our results are stable and reliable.

Discussion

Our meta-analysis incorporating these seven studies demonstrated that the LIPI holds significant predictive value for clinical outcomes in patients with urological malignancies receiving ICI therapy. Specifically, patients with good LIPI scores (0 points) exhibited superior clinical outcomes across multiple endpoints - including OS, PFS, ORR, and DCR - compared to those with intermediate or poor LIPI scores. This prognostic stratification pattern closely aligns with the original validation of LIPI in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [5], where lower LIPI scores similarly correlated with enhanced immunotherapy efficacy.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors are currently the standard first-line treatment for mRCC; however, not all patients benefit from ICI therapy [21]. Our findings indicate that patients with higher LIPI scores exhibit poorer survival outcomes following ICI treatment, primarily reflected in OS. For example, comparing good with poor LIPI scores yielded a HR of 0.15. Similarly, consistent trends were observed across other prognostic indicators: PFS (HR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.14–0.37, p < 0.001), ORR (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.01–3.83, p = 0.047), DCR (OR = 5.08, 95% CI: 2.85–9.05, p < 0.001). A good LIPI score reflects superior host immune status in patients. For such individuals, enhancing treatment efficacy by maintaining immune balance may be feasible and potentially beneficial [26]. Patients with higher LIPI scores may exhibit increased systemic inflammation due to the cumulative effects of dNLR and LDH. Inflammation is recognized as a driver of cancer progression and a contributor to immune resistance in cancer patients. Neutrophils participate in systemic inflammatory responses and cancer development, while LDH serves as another inflammatory marker reflecting tumor burden. Adverse LIPI (intermediate and poor LIPI) is closely associated with the occurrence of immune checkpoint inhibitor pneumonia (CIP). Therefore, patients with adverse LIPI may be unsuitable for treatment with ICIs [27]. We recommend employing combination immunotherapy targeting multiple immune checkpoint inhibitors or combining immunotherapy with chemotherapy to rapidly reduce tumor burden and modify the tumor microenvironment. Concurrently, monitor changes in LIPI status and closely prevent the occurrence of CIP to improve prognosis in patients with intermediate and poor LIPI outcomes.

This meta-analysis provides the first consolidated evidence supporting LIPI’s predictive utility in urological malignancies receiving ICIs. LIPI offers unique clinical advantages: its components (dNLR and LDH) are readily accessible, reproducible, and amenable to low-cost assessment. As a composite biomarker reflecting systemic inflammation and tumor metabolic activity, LIPI captures both host immune status and microenvironmental characteristics, serving as a practical tool for predicting ICI efficacy in urologic oncology. For patients with favorable LIPI scores, early interventions targeting inflammatory pathways or metabolic reprogramming may represent promising strategies to enhance clinical outcomes. Although recent studies have confirmed the predictive value of PNI and Blood biomarkers for immunotherapy in gastrointestinal cancers and hepatocellular carcinoma, the present study found that LIPI showed a unique prognostic stratification ability in urologic tumors, suggesting that the immune microenvironment of different cancer types may need to be assessed by specific biomarker systems [28,29].

Notable limitations were considered: First, the predominance of retrospective studies in our analysis introduces potential selection bias and residual confounding. Second, heterogeneity in ICI regimens across studies complicates cross-trial comparisons. In patients with urological malignancies receiving ICI therapy, good LIPI predict significantly better clinical outcomes compared to intermediate or poor LIPI scores. Therefore, future multicenter prospective cohort studies with standardized therapeutic protocols and longitudinal biomarker monitoring frameworks are imperative to validate these findings and facilitate clinical translation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this meta-analysis validates the LIPI emerges as a valuable prognostic biomarker in patients with urological malignancies treated with ICIs therapy. The results demonstrate that patients with good LIPI scores exhibited superior clinical outcomes across multiple endpoints compared to those with intermediate or poor LIPI scores, offering a novel insight into the prognostic biomarker and providing new strategies for clinical management.
