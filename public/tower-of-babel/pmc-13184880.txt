Gestational Trophoblastic Neoplasia With Intracardiac Metastatic Masses
Authors: Luz Clemencia Zarate-Correa, María Camila Bejarano-Oliveros, María Juliana Reyes-Cardona, Carlos Enrique Vesga-Reyes, Pastor Olaya, Jorge Alexander Zambrano-Franco, Jairo Sánchez-Blanco, Camilo Andrés Calderón-Miranda

ABSTRACT

History of Presentation

A 28-year-old woman presented to an external institution with a 2-day history of abdominal pain and a positive pregnancy test (β-human chorionic gonadotropin [β-hCG] >225,000 mIU/mL). Transvaginal ultrasound showed no evidence of intrauterine or extrauterine pregnancy, but revealed a hyperechoic lesion in the left ovary.

During emergency department evaluation, she developed acute oppressive chest pain radiating to the left arm, dyspnea, hemoptysis, and palpitations; cardiac troponin was initially positive, and the patient was classified as having intermediate-risk pulmonary embolism. Focused cardiac ultrasound demonstrated a velocity time integral of 23 cm and multiple mobile masses within the left-sided cardiac chambers.

The patient was transferred to our institution. On admission, she reported persistent abdominal and chest pain; vital signs were stable. Cardiovascular examination revealed regular rhythm without murmurs, and lung auscultation was unremarkable.

Past Medical History

The patient’s obstetric history was gravida 5, para 3, abortions 1, molar pregnancy 1. A complete hydatidiform mole was diagnosed in 2022 with posterior normalization of β-hCG. Last obstetric event occurred 16 months prior, resulting in live birth, without placental histopathology or postpregnancy surveillance.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the acute onset of cardiopulmonary symptoms and the presence of left-sided intracardiac masses, primary differential diagnoses included intracardiac thrombus and infective endocarditis. Acute pulmonary embolism was also considered in the context of hemoptysis and chest pain.

In parallel, the markedly elevated β-hCG level in the absence of intrauterine or ectopic pregnancy raised strong suspicion for gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN), with potential metastatic involvement of the pulmonary vasculature and intracardiac extension.

Investigations

Initial laboratory evaluation revealed β-hCG 268,342 mIU/mL, hemoglobin 11.5 g/dL, leukocytes 9,060/μL, prothrombin time 11.9 seconds, partial thromboplastin time 35.5 seconds, and international normalized ratio 1.06. Renal function and electrolyte levels were normal, and repeat troponin testing was negative.

Transesophageal echocardiography revealed multiple filiform and mobile masses extending from the left superior pulmonary vein into the left atrium, left ventricle, and ascending aorta (Video 1). Computed tomography pulmonary angiography showed chronic segmental thromboembolism in the lingular artery, multiple bilateral pulmonary nodules, and a 6.2 × 5.3 cm solid hypervascular mass in the anterior segment of the left upper lobe with tortuous vascular structures and possible aneurysmal dilation. Infiltration of the left superior pulmonary vein with intraluminal thrombus extending into the left atrium was also observed (Figure 1).

Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed no evidence of acute ischemia, hemorrhage, or central nervous system metastases. Abdominal computed tomography revealed bilateral renal masses, consistent with metastatic disease.

Management

Based on the clinical presentation, imaging findings, and markedly elevated β-hCG levels, a diagnosis of International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics stage IV GTN with pulmonary and left-sided intracardiac involvement was established (World Health Organization prognostic score 17). Tissue biopsy was deferred, as β-hCG is a validated diagnostic and prognostic biomarker, and biopsy posed a high risk of hemorrhagic complications and treatment delay. Full anticoagulation with enoxaparin was started for pulmonary thromboembolism.

The patient was admitted to the oncologic intensive care unit and initiated on induction chemotherapy with etoposide and cisplatin, followed by multiagent etoposide, methotrexate, and actinomycin D, alternating with cyclophosphamide and vincristine. Serial transthoracic echocardiography was used for close surveillance of the intracardiac masses during treatment.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient has completed 7 cycles of chemotherapy, with normalization of β-hCG levels, sustained over 2 consecutive cycles. Follow-up imaging demonstrated a marked reduction in the size of pulmonary and renal lesions without evidence of new metastases (Figure 2).

Abdominal Computed Tomography Demonstrating Renal Metastatic Involvement

(A) Coronal contrast-enhanced computed tomography. Red arrows show heterogeneous masses involving both kidneys, consistent with metastatic involvement at baseline. (B) Interval post–systemic chemotherapy contrast-enhanced computed tomography. Green arrows show marked reduction in size of renal lesion.

Serial transthoracic echocardiography showed near-complete resolution of the left atrial masses and significant reduction of the mitral valve lesions (Video 2). The patient remains hemodynamically stable, without major hemorrhagic or thromboembolic complications, and is awaiting consolidation chemotherapy as part of standard treatment protocol (Figure 3).

Discussion

Gestational trophoblastic disease represents one of the most curable malignancies in oncology, owing to its extreme chemosensitivity and the availability of β-hCG as a reliable biomarker for diagnosis and follow-up. Nevertheless, the disease remains clinically heterogeneous, and atypical presentations, particularly cardiac involvement, pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges that may obscure timely recognition and management.1,2

Although GTN most commonly develops within 6 to 12 months following evacuation of a complete hydatidiform mole, it may arise after any gestational event, including term pregnancy. In this case, the prolonged interval after β-hCG normalization and the later normal term pregnancy suggest that the current metastatic disease more likely represents a postterm GTN rather than late persistent postmolar disease.3

Cardiac involvement in GTN is striking, with evidence limited to isolated case reports. This low frequency complicates guideline development and creates diagnostic ambiguity. Reported cases illustrate 2 important clinical dilemmas. First, intracardiac thrombi can mimic metastatic GTN, particularly in patients with indwelling central venous catheters or patients in a hypercoagulable state driven by high β-hCG levels. Second, when true cardiac metastases occur, they may be associated with advanced, high-risk disease, often coexisting with pulmonary, hepatic, or cerebral metastases.1,2,4

Multimodality imaging plays a central role in differentiating these entities. Echocardiography remains the first-line modality for detection, whereas cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography provide complementary information regarding tissue characteristics, vascularity, and extracardiac extension. Structured echocardiographic assessment tools, such as the differential echocardiographic malignancy score, integrate features including mass mobility, attachment, echogenicity, and vascularity, improving diagnostic accuracy and reducing unnecessary interventions.4, 5, 6

Management of cardiac involvement in GTN must be individualized. Chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment, whereas the role of anticoagulation, surgery, or radiotherapy depends on the nature of the cardiac lesion and its hemodynamic consequences. Thrombi require anticoagulation and catheter management. Surgical intervention is generally reserved for refractory cases or cases complicated by obstruction of embolization, whereas radiotherapy has a limited but supportive role, particularly for central nervous system metastases.4,6, 7, 8, 9

This case illustrates the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for GTN in patients with elevated β-hCG and atypical cardiopulmonary presentations. Early recognition and multidisciplinary management are essential to preserve the excellent prognosis associated with this otherwise highly curable disease.

Conclusions

Cardiac involvement in GTN is rare but clinically significant, often mimicking more common intracardiac pathologies. Multimodality imaging and structured echocardiographic assessment are critical for accurate diagnosis. Prompt initiation of systemic chemotherapy guided by International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics staging and World Health Organization risk stratification remains the cornerstone of management and can lead to favorable outcomes even in advanced disease.
