Amino acids and mineral composition of normal and felted wool of the ukrainian carpathian mountain sheep breed
Authors: Vitalii Tkachuk, Nataliia Motko, Nataliia Ohorodnyk, Bogdan Kyryliv, Ihor Tkachuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe felting of wool directly on the sheep largely affects its value as a raw material for the textile industry. In this regard, the aim of this study was to investigate the amino acid and mineral composition of wool affected by this defect in sheep of the Ukrainian Carpathian Mountain breed. Experimental wool samples were divided into guard and down fibers. The amino acid composition was determined using an AAA-400 amino acid analyzer, the mineral composition was determined using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific iCE 3500), and sulfur was determined by the nephelometric method based on the turbidity of a barium sulfate suspension stabilized with glycerin. It has been shown that the process of wool felting is accompanied by partial degradation of fibers resulting from the destruction of disulfide, ionic, and hydrogen bonds, as indicated by a significant decrease in the total amino acid content, due to reductions in aspartic (P < 0.01) and glutamic (P < 0.01) acids, arginine (P < 0.05), as well as cystine (P < 0.01) and histidine (P < 0.05) in down fibers, and lysine (P < 0.01) in guard fibers. The observed decrease in calcium and copper content in felted wool indicates a disruption of ionic interactions with the functional groups of amino acids, which play a key role in stabilizing the structural organization of wool fibers, while the decrease in sulfur content in down fibers confirms the destruction of disulfide bonds. Therefore, the results of the study indicate that wool felting is the result of biochemical processes leading to disruption of the keratin structure of the fiber. In the future, the obtained data may be used to develop comprehensive approaches aimed at preventing and eliminating this wool defect.Keywords: Felted wool, Gguard and down fibers, Amino acid composition, Mineral elements, Disulfide and ionic bondsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry

Introduction

Sheep wool is a valuable textile raw material, the quality characteristics of which are formed during fiber growth and depend on a complex of genetic, physiological, nutritional, and technological factors1,2. The main component of the wool fiber is the fibrillar protein keratin, the chemical and structural features of which determine the physicomechanical and, consequently, technological properties of wool3. An important role in the formation of these properties is played by the amino acid composition of keratin and the mineral elements present in the fiber4,5.

The amino acid composition of wool determines the nature of inter- and intramolecular bonds and the degree of ordering of keratin molecules, and therefore the integrity and stability of the fiber6. As reported by Puqi et al.7, sulfur-containing amino acids, primarily cysteine, play a special role in the formation of wool fibers, ensuring the formation of disulfide bonds between keratin molecules, which determine the strength, resilience, and elasticity of the fibers. Amino acids with small and polar (hydrophilic) side chains participate in the tight packing of secondary structures and form a system of hydrogen bonds, while aromatic and hydrophobic amino acids stabilize the internal structure of keratin8,9.

The mineral composition of wool complements and modulates the action of the amino acid matrix, influencing the physical properties of the fibers10. Sulfur is a component of cysteine, which forms disulfide bonds in keratin, determining the strength, elasticity, and resistance of fibers to mechanical impacts. Copper participates in enzymatic processes, particularly through copper-containing oxidases, which contribute to the formation of cross-links in keratin, partially affecting the tensile strength of the fibers. Calcium plays a regulatory role in the functioning of hair follicles, thereby affecting the rate of growth and the quality of wool11. However, recent studies have shown significant regional differences in the concentrations of biogenic elements in sheep wool, which correlate with environmental conditions and the mineral supply in the animals’ diet12.

Semi-coarse-wool sheep, including the Ukrainian Carpathian Mountain breed, occupy an important place in livestock production in the Carpathian region of Ukraine due to the versatility of their productivity13. In particular, they provide the population with mutton, milk, and wool14. The latter serves as a raw material for the production of a wide range of felt and felted materials and products, the manufacture of which is possible due to the ability of wool fibers to felt15. Felting ability refers to the property of wool to form a dense and strong mass — felt — as a result of the irreversible convergence and interlocking of fibers16.

However, sheep wool is strongly influenced by unfavorable management conditions, which may cause the felting process to begin directly on the animal. According to Patrucco et al.17, felting occurs when humidity increases significantly and fibers rub against each other, as a result of which the scales of the hair cuticle interlock, forming a dense layer. As a result of the disruption of structural uniformity, such wool is practically unsuitable for primary processing, and the value of wool raw material with this defect decreases significantly18.

Traditionally, felting is considered the result of exogenous factors19; however, the biochemical prerequisites for the development of this defect remain insufficiently understood. Available scientific data indicate that changes in the amino acid composition of keratin and the mineral profile of wool may affect fiber hydrophilicity20, thereby enhancing surface interactions between fibers and creating conditions for felting. However, systematic studies aimed at the comparative analysis of the amino acid and mineral composition of normal and felted wool in semi-coarse-wool sheep are scarce in the available literature.

In this regard, the study of the amino acid and mineral composition of normal and felted wool of semi-coarse-wool sheep of the Ukrainian Carpathian Mountain breed is a relevant research task. The results of such studies will deepen the understanding of the biochemical mechanisms underlying the formation of wool fiber quality, clarify the role of individual amino acids and mineral elements in the development of this defect, identify potential markers of susceptibility to felting, and provide a scientific basis for the development of effective measures for the prevention and elimination of this defect, thereby improving the quality of wool raw materials.

Materials and methods

Selection and preparation of wool samples

For the research, samples of normal and felted wool were used, taken from adult lactating ewes of the Ukrainian Mountain Carpathian breed. The animals belonged to the Prometey Farm (Kolomyskyi district, Ivano-Frankivsk region, Ukraine) and were kept on pasture. Wool for the research was taken from 20 ewes from the fleece area behind the shoulder blade (10 samples of normal and 10 samples of felted wool). All animals were kept under the same conditions of maintenance and feeding.

The obtained wool samples were washed in a neutral detergent solution. Then, in order to remove grease, they were extracted with carbon tetrachloride (Penta s.r.o., Czech Republic) for 5 h in a Soxhlet apparatus, rinsed and dried to air-dry state. After that, manually, using tweezers, taking into account external signs, individual fibers were divided into down and guard.

Research on the amino acid composition of wool

The amino acid composition of wool was determined by ion chromatography using an amino acid analyzer of the AAA-400 brand from Ingos (Czech Republic). The principle of operation of the analyzer is based on the acid hydrolysis of keratin proteins with the formation of free amino acids and their subsequent separation by ion-exchange liquid chromatography and quantitative determination by the intensity of the color of the reaction products with ninhydrin.

Samples of wool weighing 10 mg were hydrolyzed with 2 ml of 6 N hydrochloric acid solution (Lach-Ner s.r.o., Czech Republic) at a temperature of 1100 C for 24 h. For the correct determination of cystine, wool samples were subjected to oxidative treatment with perfomic acid before acid hydrolysis in order to convert disulfide and thiol groups into a stable form - cysteic acid. The separation of amino acids was carried out on a cation-exchange column filled with Ostin Ingos sorbent, with a mobile phase from a combination of four citrate buffer solutions with pH 2.7–8.0, with column regeneration at pH 14.0, and subsequent post-column derivatization of amino acids with ninhydrin in solution with the formation of chromophoric complex compounds, with dual-channel photometric detection at 570 nm. The identification of amino acids was carried out by retention time relative to a standard mixture, the quantitative content was calculated by peak areas by calibration coefficients using the CHROMuLAN 0.82 software (Ingos s.r.o., Czech Republic).

Research on the mineral composition of wool

Determination of the content of mineral elements was carried out using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer Thermo Scientific iCE 3500 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA), the principle of which is based on the selective absorption of resonant radiation by free atoms of elements in a flame. Preparation of wool samples was carried out by wet mineralization with a mixture of concentrated nitric acid (Penta s.r.o., Czech Republic) and perhydrol (Lach-Ner s.r.o., Czech Republic) in a ratio of 5:1 until the mineralization was completely clarified. Measurements were carried out using an acetylene-air mixture. Calculation of the concentration of elements in the sample was carried out using the standard SOLAAR software according to the constructed calibration graphs. The accuracy and reproducibility of the results were confirmed by a series of parallel determinations.

Determination of sulfur content in wool

The sulfur content in wool was determined by the nephelometric method using the turbidity of a barium sulfate suspension stabilized with glycerin. The method is based on the formation of a finely dispersed suspension of barium sulfate during the interaction of sulfate ions with barium ions in an acidic medium. The intensity of nephelometry depends on the concentration of sulfate ions in the sample. Glycerin was used as a stabilizer to increase the stability of the suspension and reduce particle aggregation.

A wool sample weighing 10 mg was placed in a 50 ml round-bottom flask, filled with 3 ml of perchloric acid (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd, China) and 2 ml of magnesium nitrate (Loba Chemie, India). The flasks were placed in a sand bath heated to 2000 C and mineralization was carried out for 4 h, periodically mixing their contents. After completion of mineralization, the liquid in the flasks should be completely transparent, and upon cooling, solidify into a solid ice-like mass. After cooling, 3.4 ml of barium chloride (System Optimum PE, Ukraine) in glycerin (Istok-Plus LLC, Ukraine) was added to each flask and the resulting solidified mass was dissolved by stirring, which was nephelometrically measured using a Specol-11 spectrophotometer (Carl Zeiss Jena, Germany) at a wavelength of 420 nm. A solution of anhydrous sodium sulfate (System Optimum PE, Ukraine) containing from 50 to 500 µg of sulfur was used to construct the calibration curve.

Compliance with ethical standards

Manipulations with animals were carried out in accordance with the international principles of the Council of Europe Convention “For the Protection of Vertebrate Animals Used for Experimental and Other Scientific Purposes”, resolutions of the National Congress of Ukraine on Bioethics (2010), and the Law of Ukraine No. 3447-IV “On the Protection of Animals from Cruelty” as amended on 15.11.2024. Wool samples were collected from animals with the permission of the farm owners. The sampling procedure was non-invasive and did not cause harm or distress to the animals, in accordance with standard animal welfare guidelines. The study was approved by the bioethical commission of the S.Z. Gzhytsky Lviv National University of Veterinary Medicine and Biotechnology (protocol No. 2 dated March 25, 2025).

Statistical analysis

The data obtained as a result of the experiment were analysed using Statistica 12.0 software (StatSoft Inc., USA). The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). Due to the small sample size (n = 4 per group), both parametric (Student’s t-test) and non-parametric (Mann-Whitney U test) methods were used to compare two independent groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Due to the decrease in these amino acids, a lower total amino acid content was observed in felted wool. The conducted studies also showed that the mineral composition of wool fibers depends on their type, since guard fibers, compared with down fibers, contain lower amounts of sulfur, zinc, and copper.

Discussion

Sheep wool, due to its unique combination of physicochemical properties, is a valuable environmentally friendly raw material for the textile industry. The high competitiveness of wool under conditions of the widespread use of synthetic fibers is ensured by its ability to retain heat, absorb moisture, and exhibit a wide range of hygienic properties21,22. In view of this, the demand for high-quality wool raw materials is steadily increasing. However, a significant proportion of wool is characterized by various defects that complicate further processing and reduce its value. One of such defects is felting of wool directly on the sheep.

Wool felting is studied mainly as a surface process, investigating the effects of friction, the structure of cuticular scales, the crimp of fibers, their hygroscopicity, and contamination with urine and microorganisms, thereby overlooking the biochemical aspect of this problem. However, felting is not only the result of exogenous factors. Its development is a complex process in which endogenous structural and biochemical transformations play a significant role, and elucidating these transformations is not possible without studying the amino acid and mineral composition of wool fibers.

Wool felting is most commonly observed in coarse-wool and semi-coarse-wool sheep23,24. This defect is characteristic of semi-coarse-wool sheep of the Ukrainian Mountain Carpathian breed, which are characterized by a heterogeneous fleece.

Our studies of the amino acid composition of the wool of this breed first showed that down and guard fibers contain almost identical total amounts of amino acids; however, they differ in their amino acid composition. In particular, down fibers are characterized by a higher content of cystine, which is responsible for the formation of disulfide bonds that stabilize the hair structure25,26, as well as polar hydrophilic amino acids (serine, threonine, and tyrosine), which participate in the formation of hydrogen bonds stabilizing the α-helix of keratin27,28. Guard fibers, in contrast, are characterized by higher contents of hydrophobic amino acids (valine, leucine, isoleucine, and phenylalanine), which contribute to increased structural heterogeneity of keratin29.

Felting of wool leads to partial degradation of the fibers, as evidenced by a decrease in the total amino acid content, which is observed in both down and guard fibers. The most pronounced changes in down fibers involve cystine, due to a decrease in sulfur content, which indicates the destruction of disulfide bonds30. As shown by Li et al.31 in the development of anti-felting treatments for wool, the destruction of these bonds and the oxidation of cystine to cysteic acids significantly increase the susceptibility of the cuticle layer to proteolysis, followed by fiber degradation.

The significant decrease in aspartic and glutamic acids in both down and guard fibers of felted wool may indicate the degradation of keratin-associated proteins (KAPs), which is consistent with the data reported by Tissera et al32. The simultaneous decrease in arginine and histidine in down fibers, and in arginine and lysine in guard fibers, indicates a weakening of ionic interactions that stabilize the keratin structure33,34, which is partially confirmed by the observed changes in the mineral composition of the fibers.

Our studies also showed that down fibers, compared with guard fibers, contain significantly higher amounts of zinc, copper, and especially sulfur. The higher sulfur content is expected given the greater concentration of cystine and high-sulfur proteins in these fibers, as reported by Czibula et al35 and Belbeoch et al.36.

Felting of wool is accompanied by a complex of physicochemical effects that lead to partial demineralization of the fiber. The stable association of mineral ions, particularly Ca²⁺, with the amino acid matrix of keratin becomes disrupted. In wool, calcium is retained mainly through ionic interactions with the carboxyl groups of acidic amino acids37, localized in interfibrillar regions38 These Ca²⁺-mediated bonds function as ionic “bridges” that further stabilize the fiber structure39. Our data indicate disruption of the ionic interactions between Ca²⁺ and the carboxyl groups of aspartic and glutamic acids, which is consistent with the findings reported by Mowafi & El-Sayed40.

The decrease in copper observed in felted wool may be associated with disruption of copper ion-coordination bonds with the functional groups of amino acids in lipid–protein complexes of the cuticle layer, followed by its leaching from the fiber as a result of cuticle damage caused by keratinolytic enzymes of the fleece microflora41,42. Similar results were reported by Vikash et al.43, as well as in our previous studies44.

Thus, the results of the present study revealed characteristic changes in the amino acid and mineral composition of guard and down fibers of felted wool in sheep of the Ukrainian Carpathian Mountain breed.

Conclusions

Felting of wool in sheep of the Ukrainian Carpathian Mountain breed is accompanied by pronounced structural and biochemical alterations in both down and guard fibers, which are manifested by changes in their amino acid and mineral composition. Felted wool is characterized by a decrease in total amino acid content, mainly due to reductions in aspartic and glutamic acids, arginine, as well as cystine and histidine in down fibers and lysine in guard fibers, which indicates the disruption of disulfide, ionic, and hydrogen bonds responsible for the internal structural organization of wool fibers. This is further evidenced by the reduced contents of calcium and copper in such wool, as well as a decrease in sulfur in down fibers. The combined changes indicate that wool felting is associated with a complex destabilization of the molecular organization of the fiber structure, affecting both protein and mineral components, which ultimately leads to deterioration of the physicochemical and technological properties of wool raw materials and highlights the need for further in-depth investigation of the mechanisms underlying this process.
