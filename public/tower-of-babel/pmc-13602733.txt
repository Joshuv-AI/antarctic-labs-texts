Humanitude care techniques for sit-to-stand assistance: a comparative observational study
Authors: Qi An, Akito Tanaka, Miwako Honda, Kazuto Nakashima, Ryo Kurazume
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSupporting care recipients to actively participate in sit-to-stand movements helps preserve autonomy and self-efficacy while engaging residual physical capacity. Such approaches may reduce caregiver burden and prevent burnout. Humanitude, a person-centred care methodology, includes specific techniques for assisting standing that may promote active involvement of care recipients.ObjectiveTo investigate how sit-to-stand assistance differs between Humanitude-trained caregivers, general nurses, and nursing students, and to examine its effects on the biomechanics and muscle activity of care recipients.DesignA comparative observational study.SettingTwo clinical training rooms in hospitals.ParticipantsThe study included 59 caregivers (9 Humanitude-trained experts, 40 general nurses, and 10 nursing students) and two simulated care recipients.MethodsEach caregiver performed 10 repeated sit-to-stand assistance trials. Biomechanical metrics, including the care recipients’ center of mass trajectory, independent weight-bearing ratio, and electromyographic activity of the knee and hip extensors, were measured. Trial data were averaged per pair. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the relationship between independent weight-bearing ratio and muscle activity. Additional analyses compared assistance strategies across groups with different levels of independent weight bearing ratio.ResultsHumanitude-trained caregivers facilitated significantly higher independent weight-bearing and hip extensor activity than both general nurses and nursing students, as well as higher knee extensor activity than general nurses. Across all caregivers, the independent weight-bearing ratio was positively correlated with knee extensor activity (Spearman’s ρ = .56, 95% CI: 0.36–0.72, p< .001) and hip extensor activity (ρ = .34, 95% CI: 0.09–0.55, p= .008). Care recipients in higher weight-bearing conditions were assisted by strategies that first prioritized anterior translation of the center of mass closer to the base of support.ConclusionsHumanitude-based sit-to-stand assistance promotes greater independent weight bearing and voluntary lower limb muscle activation in care recipients. These findings suggest that Humanitude techniques may enhance functional engagement and provide a biomechanical basis for person-centred nursing care.Registrationnot registered

Background

Globally, rapid demographic aging has led to an increasing number of older adults requiring assistance with daily activities (United Nations, 2020). The sit-to-stand transfer is a fundamental prerequisite for functional mobility and independence, essential for activities such as eating, dressing, and toileting (Janssen et al., 2002; Azharuddin and Zia, 2021). Difficulty in performing this fundamental movement is one of the most powerful predictors of subsequent functional decline, leading to a loss of independence and reduced quality of life (Park et al., 2021). Furthermore, in clinical settings, early mobilization—encouraging patients to stand and walk rather than remain bedridden (Zisberg et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2024)—is recognized as critical for preventing deconditioning and accelerating recovery (Adler and Malone, 2012; de Sousa et al., 2019). Sit-to-stand transfers are among the most frequently performed nursing tasks. They also pose substantial safety risks, with epidemiological data indicating that a significant proportion of falls in healthcare facilities occur during transfers (Rapp et al., 2012; Komisar et al., 2023).

This duality underscores the clinical imperative for effective and safe sit-to-stand assistance. Providing such assistance is physically demanding; improper techniques significantly increase the risk of work-related musculoskeletal disorders for caregivers (Waters et al., 2007; Hsieh et al., 2022). To mitigate these risks, many healthcare facilities have adopted “no-lift” policies or mechanical aids (Andersen et al., 2014; Sousa et al., 2023). These measures protect caregivers, but over-reliance on passive mechanical assistance or improper manual lifting may inhibit the care recipient’s active participation, depriving them of the opportunity to utilize their residual physical activity.

Conventional nursing assistance, while prioritizing safety and efficiency, often contradicts theoretical models that emphasize active motor involvement (Hignett, 2003; Noble et al., 2018). Biomechanically, a successful sit-to-stand transfer requires the generation of forward momentum via trunk flexion and its effective transition to vertical movement at seat-off—a phase demanding significant knee and hip extensor torque (Schenkman et al., 1990; Sadeh et al., 2023). Yet, observational studies indicate that assistance methods vary widely (Yip, 2004; Kjellberg et al., 2004), and under clinical time constraints, caregivers frequently resort to manual lifting rather than facilitating the patient’s own movement (Nelson & Baptiste, 2004). This passive approach bypasses critical momentum phases, suppressing voluntary muscle activation (Burnfield et al., 2012, 2013). Such suppression of residual abilities hinders biomechanically efficient strategies and potentially contributes to a cycle of functional decline and learned helplessness (Scott et al., 2018).

To address these issues, research has explored assistive methods that preserve the care recipient’s motor function. Studies of expert therapists demonstrate that integrating verbal and manual cues elicits lower-limb muscle activation in care recipients compared to verbal cues alone (Nelson et al., 2025). Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that the level of support provided by assistive devices significantly modulates the care recipient’s movement duration, joint angles, and joint torques (Piersol & Riemann, 2020), and that care recipient’s muscle activation can be increased even with devices if they are verbally encouraged to exert their maximal effort (Burnfield et al., 2012). These findings indicate that the specific assistance technique directly modulates the care recipient’s active participation (Burnfield et al., 2012; Werner et al., 2017).

In pursuit of optimized care, and aligning with the global shift towards person-centred care (Edvardsson et al., 2010), multimodal care methodologies have gained significant attention. Originally developed in 1979 by Yves Gineste and Rosette Marescotti (Gineste & Pellissier, 2007), Humanitude is a pioneering care methodology that strongly resonates with this modern person-centred approach. Humanitude emphasizes four pillars—gaze, speech, touch, and standing—and integrates multimodal communication with facilitative physical assistance to promote autonomy and active participation of care recipients. In this framework, “standing” represents the concept of verticality, which emphasizes supporting individuals to achieve and maintain an upright posture as a means of preserving functional ability, dignity, and autonomy (Sumioka et al., 2021). Its clinical efficacy is emerging across multiple domains, including medication-related, behavioral, and functional outcomes. Specifically, it has been associated with significant reductions in the use of psychotropic medications (Kobayashi et al., 2024), as well as the mitigation of behavioral symptoms and the maintenance of activities of daily living (Figueiredo et al., 2018; Henriques et al., 2019).

Within its framework, physical assistance is designed to encourage the patient’s own efforts. The conceptual linkage between this relational, person-centred approach and biomechanical outcomes lies in the integration of psychological security and motor facilitation. Relational techniques—such as continuous eye contact and non-coercive, wide-area touch—help reduce the care recipient’s anxiety, fear of falling, and involuntary passive resistance. By establishing this trusting environment, the caregiver can smoothly guide the recipient’s center of mass forward rather than lifting them forcefully. Consequently, the recipient feels sufficiently safe to actively recruit their own lower limb extensor muscles, theoretically translating into a higher independent weight-bearing ratio and improved biomechanical efficiency during the transition to standing. While the full Humanitude approach inherently integrates visual, auditory, and tactile communication, evaluating all elements simultaneously makes it difficult to quantify the specific physical mechanisms. Therefore, the present study deliberately excluded verbal communication to strictly isolate and evaluate the purely biomechanical assistance techniques (e.g., non-coercive touch and postural guidance) derived from the Humanitude framework, rather than the broader approach as a whole.

Nevertheless, these hypothesized biomechanical benefits have not been rigorously quantified, as evidence for physical improvements has largely relied on qualitative assessments or caregivers' subjective evaluations (Figueiredo et al., 2018; Henriques et al., 2019). A specific research gap remains regarding the objective biomechanical mechanisms during sit-to-stand assistance. Although sit-to-stand is the foundational transition required for achieving verticality and maintaining activities of daily living independence, it is still unclear how relational care techniques directly translate into physical motor facilitation during this critical movement. Previous quantitative analyses have focused on caregiver posture and muscle activity (Keir & MacDonell, 2004; Hellmers et al., 2022) or used robotic simulations to clarify joint torque changes (Lin et al., 2021), but the direct impact on human care recipients during expert intervention has not been fully investigated.

Theoretically, effective techniques should promote the care recipient’s active participation, quantifiable by their voluntary muscle activation (Burnfield et al., 2012) and independent weight-bearing (Piersol & Riemann, 2020). Yet, few studies have used these specific metrics to validate expert techniques compared to standard practice. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether these biomechanical strategies arise from cumulative clinical experience or specialized conceptual training. To comprehensively clarify these characteristics, it is valuable to evaluate a spectrum of proficiency, comparing not only specialized experts and general professionals but also individuals with zero practical clinical longevity. Therefore, the exploratory purpose of this study was to use objective biomechanical measures to compare sit-to-stand assistance provided by Humanitude experts with that of general nurses and nursing students. We hypothesized that Humanitude expert assistance would be characterized by (1) a significantly higher independent weight-bearing ratio, and (2) muscle activation patterns that more closely resemble those of independent, unassisted movement.

Methods

Study design

This study utilized a comparative observational design to evaluate the biomechanical differences in sit-to-stand assistance provided by caregivers from diverse professional backgrounds and levels of expertise. The primary objective was to characterize the assistance strategies employed by Humanitude experts, general nurses, and nursing students, and specifically to quantify their impact on the care recipient’s active motor engagement. This engagement was assessed using objective metrics, including the independent weight-bearing ratio, voluntary muscle activation, and the whole-body center of mass trajectory of simulated care recipients. Given its exploratory nature as a biomechanical characterization study rather than a confirmatory clinical trial, this study was not prospectively registered in a public clinical trial registry.

Setting and participants

Setting

The experiment was conducted in clinical training rooms at two sites: National Hospital Organization Tokyo Medical Center, a national academic medical center in a major metropolitan area, and Koriyama Medical Care Hospital, a private regional hospital specializing in long-term medical and nursing care. Data were collected on a single day at National Hospital Organization Tokyo Medical Center in September 2022 and over a five-day period at Koriyama Medical Care Hospital in November 2022. These facilities were selected based on the availability of the required biomechanical equipment and space, as well as access to the participant pool.

Participants

A total of 59 caregivers (6 males, 53 females) were recruited for this study. The inclusion criterion for all participants was experience with sit-to-stand assistance, gained through clinical practice or academic training. Individuals with a history of musculoskeletal injuries, particularly lower back pain, that could affect their ability to perform assistance tasks were excluded from the study. Importantly, to preserve the ecological validity of our findings and reflect the natural diversity of the active healthcare workforce, no screening, selection, or restrictions were applied regarding the caregivers’ physical stature (such as height, weight) or baseline physical strength. The caregivers were then categorized into three distinct groups:I.Humanitude Experts (n = 9): Certified instructors who are also licensed healthcare professionals; notably, all nine experts recruited for this study were registered nurses. To obtain their instructor certification, they had completed a 10-week comprehensive training program covering theoretical principles, practical skills, clinical application, and instructional methods. After passing rigorous certification exams, they maintained their status through annual professional development and teaching. We recruited all available certified Humanitude experts who could be reached during the study period, representing a unique and specialized cohort.II.General Nurses (n = 40): Licensed nurses currently employed in clinical hospitals who were aware of Humanitude as a concept but had no prior formal technical training or participation in official Humanitude workshops. A larger sample size was prioritized for this group because they represent the primary clinical standard against which the specialized techniques of Humanitude experts were evaluated, thereby robustly capturing the variance in active clinical practice.III.Nursing Students (n = 10): Students enrolled in a nursing program with no prior professional work experience. This group was recruited not to achieve broad generalizability for the student population, but to serve as a baseline benchmark (a “clinical experience = zero” control) to illustrate how individuals with theoretical knowledge but no practical longevity naturally perform the assistance. Their sample size was secondary to the primary study objective and was logistically bound by their availability within the academic calendar between intensive clinical practicums.

Given the exploratory nature of this initial biomechanical investigation, a formal a priori power analysis for sample size calculation was not conducted. Instead, we employed a convenience sampling approach to recruit as many eligible participants as possible within the study period. Participants were recruited based on the pre-defined inclusion criteria. A total of 59 eligible individuals (9 experts, 40 nurses, and 10 students) were invited to participate, and all of them successfully completed the experimental protocol. As described above, the disproportionate group sizes directly reflect the practical availability of the rare expert cohort relative to the need to capture broad clinical variance among general nurses. Consequently, no participants were excluded, and data from all 59 individuals were included in the final analysis.

Two female healthcare professionals (a 57-year-old nurse, 50 kg; and a 58-year-old medical doctor, 58 kg) served as simulated care recipients (hereafter referred to as “care recipients”). Both individuals had no underlying musculoskeletal or neurological conditions, possessed normal cognitive function, and were fully independent in all activities of daily living. These two recipients were selected for their similar stature and body mass to maintain experimental consistency and ensure the comparability of data. By minimizing the potential confounding influence of the recipient’s physique, we were able to precisely measure the biomechanics of the assistance itself, independent of the recipient’s physical size or pathological movement constraints. They were instructed to relax and transfer their body weight to the caregiver during the sit-to-stand assistance, minimizing their own voluntary effort.

Importantly, to prevent any specific recipient-caregiver pairing bias, these two simulated care recipients were assisted by caregivers from all three groups, with no fixed assignment between specific recipients and caregiver groups. Because the same two individuals participated as recipients across all experimental conditions, any potential baseline differences regarding the care recipient's physical characteristics were effectively controlled across the three caregiver groups.

The decision to use healthy individuals as simulated care recipients was based on the need to ensure high internal validity by minimizing confounding variables such as unpredictable resistive movements or varying baseline physical capacities typical of clinical populations. This methodological approach is consistent with previous biomechanical studies evaluating assistive devices and techniques (Burnfield et al., 2012; Daynard et al., 2001), which utilized healthy participants to isolate and clarify the specific effects of the assistance itself. By standardizing the recipient’s responses, we were able to precisely quantify the differences in the caregivers’ facilitation strategies and their direct impact on independent weight-bearing and muscle activation patterns.

A preliminary analysis focusing on a subset of the data (expert group) and different metrics was previously reported in a conference proceeding (An et al., 2022). The current study significantly expands upon this prior work by: (1) increasing the total sample size to 59 caregivers across three experience levels, (2) introducing the independent weight-bearing ratio as a primary outcome measure, and (3) providing a comprehensive comparative analysis of center-of-mass trajectories and muscle activation across these groups.

Experimental procedure

At the start of each experimental day, the care recipients conducted 10 independent (unassisted) sit-to-stand trials to establish a baseline for their functional capacity.

Following this baseline measurement, the main assisted trials began. Each caregiver, upon arrival, was given a standardized instruction script. They were informed that the care recipient was able to stand but required assistance due to difficulty initiating the movement and a potential risk of their knees buckling. To isolate the biomechanical impact of the physical assistance technique itself, caregivers were strictly instructed to provide no verbal cues or encouragement during the trials. Each caregiver then assisted a care recipient in rising from a standardized chair to a full standing position for a total of 10 repetitions. To standardize the task difficulty, the chair height was adjusted to the height of the care recipient’s knee joint (popliteal height). Prior to these trials, the care recipients were instructed to refrain from attempting to stand up independently or pre-emptively, but rather to follow the caregiver’s guidance and engage their muscles naturally if the assistance facilitated the movement.

The mean of the 10 trials was used for analysis to minimize the impact of intrasubject variability and transient fluctuations in physical response. To minimize the effects of fatigue on the simulated care recipients, a rest period of a minimum of 30 minutes was provided between sessions with different caregivers.

Assistance Procedures and Instructions

To evaluate the differences in assistance techniques, specific instructions were provided to each caregiver group. The General Nurses were instructed to perform the sit-to-stand assistance as they would in their routine clinical practice (“Please assist as you usually do”). The Nursing Students were asked to apply the techniques they had learned in their basic nursing education (“Please assist as you were taught in academic training”). The Humanitude Experts were instructed to apply the standardized Humanitude methodology.

Measures

Independent weight-bearing ratio

To investigate the extent to which the simulated care recipient supported their own weight during assistance, one force plate (Tec Gihan Co., Ltd., TFG4060) was placed under the care recipient’s buttocks and another under their feet. Data were recorded at 1,000 Hz and processed using a second-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 10 Hz. The vertical ground reaction forces obtained from the buttocks and feet were summed and normalized by the care recipient’s body weight to obtain the independent weight-bearing ratio.

Muscle activity

The activity of the knee and hip extensor muscles (vastus lateralis and gluteus maximus) of the simulated care recipient was measured using surface electromyography (Cometa, Wave Infinity) at 1,000 Hz. The muscle activity data were band-pass filtered between 40–400 Hz using a fourth-order Butterworth filter, rectified, and further smoothed with a fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filter at 4 Hz (Clark et al., 2010; Gizzi et al., 2011).

Kinematic data

The whole-body center of mass trajectory of the simulated care recipient was measured at a sampling frequency of 60 Hz using a wearable inertial sensor system (Movella Inc., MVN Xsens Awinda). The system calculated the center of mass position of the care recipient based on its integrated biomechanical model, and this trajectory was used as the sole kinematic variable for analysis. To normalize the data across participants of different statures, the horizontal and vertical displacements of the center of mass were divided by each recipient’s body height. These normalized values were expressed as a percentage of height.

Data analysis

Data processing and normalization

For each trial, the point of seat-off was defined as the moment the vertical ground reaction force measured at the chair fell below a threshold of 5 Newtons (N). This point was set as the central time reference. All time-series data, including independent weight-bearing and muscle activity data, were then segmented into a 4-second epoch, spanning from 2.0 seconds before seat-off to 2.0 seconds after seat-off. All subsequent analyses were performed on these segmented data. There were no missing data for the primary outcomes.

To allow for comparison across trials and participants, both the independent weight-bearing ratio and muscle activity data were normalized. The data from the 10 independent (unassisted) sit-to-stand trials served as the 100% baseline for each care recipient. The normalized ground reaction force (F˜) and normalized muscle activity (m˜) for each assisted trial were calculated as follows:(1)F˜(t)=F(t)1T∑t=1t=TF^(t),(2)m˜(t)=m(t)1T∑t=1t=Tm^(t),where F(t) and m(t) represent the time-series data during an assisted trial, and F^(t) and m^(t) represent the mean waveforms across the 10 independent baseline trials, and T is the number of samples in the analysis epoch.

Statistical comparison among caregiver groups

Statistical comparisons of primary outcome variables (e.g., independent weight-bearing ratio and muscle activity) across the three caregiver groups were performed using a Kruskal-Wallis H test, as the assumption of normality, assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, was violated. This non-parametric test was also chosen for its robustness against outliers and unequal group sizes. To evaluate the magnitude of these differences alongside the hypothesis tests, effect sizes (η2) were calculated for the Kruskal–Wallis H tests. We interpreted the magnitude of the effect size following established benchmarks: η2≥0.14 was considered a large effect, while 0.06≤η2<0.14 was considered a moderate effect (Fiel Peres, 2026). When a significant difference was found, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Bonferroni correction. Primary outcome data are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs).

Relationship between independent weight-bearing and muscle activity

To test the hypothesis that Humanitude-based assistance facilitates active participation, we analyzed the relationship between the care recipient’s independent weight-bearing (represented by independent weight-bearing, F˜) and their voluntary muscle activation (represented by m˜).

The data processing involved a two-step averaging procedure to yield a single representative value for each of the 59 caregiver-recipient pairs. First, for each of the 10 assisted sit-to-stand trials, the normalized independent weight-bearing ratio and muscle activity data were averaged over the duration of the movement. Second, these 10 time-averaged values were then averaged together to create one mean value of the independent weight-bearing ratio and one mean muscle activity value for that specific caregiver. Finally, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (ρ) were calculated to assess the strength and direction of the monotonic relationship between these 59 pairs of representative independent weight-bearing ratios and muscle activity values.

To evaluate whether the quality of assistance was influenced by the length of professional practice, we examined the relationship between the caregivers’ clinical experience (years) and the primary outcome measures. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated between the years of experience and the representative values of independent weight-bearing ratio and muscle activity for all 59 caregivers. This analysis aimed to distinguish the effects of specialized Humanitude training from the potential confounding effect of general clinical longevity.

Classification by level of independent weight-bearing ratio and time-series analysis

To clarify the specific biomechanical characteristics associated with different levels of facilitation, we categorized the assisted trials into three performance-based groups according to the observed distribution of independent weight-bearing ratios. Specifically, we divided the dataset into tertiles (the 33.3rd and 66.7th percentiles) to ensure strictly balanced group sizes. The groups were defined as High weight-bearing (the top tertile), representing expert-level performance; Mid weight-bearing (the middle tertile); and Low weight-bearing (the bottom tertile), representing varying degrees of physical lifting. For each group, ensemble-averaged waveforms of the independent weight-bearing ratio and muscle activity were calculated to visualize the synchronization between weight-bearing support and the recipient’s muscle activity.

Center of mass of care recipient during assistance

To explore the movement patterns associated with different levels of care recipient participation, we categorized the assisted sit-to-stand trials into groups based on their measured independent weight-bearing ratios. For each of these groups, the center of mass trajectories were time-normalized and then ensemble-averaged. This comparative analysis aimed to identify the specific center of mass kinematic patterns that characterized trials with higher levels of active motor engagement from the care recipient.

Ethical considerations and informed consent

The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Information Science and Electrical Engineering, Kyushu University (Approval Number: 2021-04-1). All participants were provided with a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential risks, both orally and in writing. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study.

The Humanitude Expert strategy (Fig. 4A) was characterized by the caregiver lowering their own center of gravity and guiding the recipient’s center of mass significantly forward prior to seat-off. This handling brought the recipient’s center of mass closer to the base of support, facilitating a more stable and autonomous transition to standing.

In contrast, the strategy typical of the Low weight-bearing group (Fig. 4B), which primarily consisted of General Nurses and Nursing Students, involved a dominant upward lifting motion. In these cases, caregivers often initiated the movement without providing sufficient forward translation of the recipient’s trunk.

The ensemble-averaged center of mass trajectories (Fig. 4C) provide a quantitative confirmation of these strategic differences. All trajectories were normalized to the recipient’s body height to ensure comparability. The unassisted (independent) movement was characterized by the deepest forward lean, reaching a maximum horizontal displacement of approximately 15% of body height before transitioning to upward movement. The High weight-bearing group exhibited a similar trajectory pattern, although the maximum forward displacement was smaller (approximately 5% of body height) compared to the unassisted trials. The Mid weight-bearing and Low weight-bearing groups showed almost no forward translation, exhibiting a nearly vertical trajectory from the onset of the movement. This indicates that in these groups, the caregiver’s upward lifting force dominated the movement before the recipient could initiate a natural forward shift of their center of mass, which is essential for autonomous standing.

Discussion

Biomechanical mechanism of Humanitude facilitation

The superior performance of the Humanitude Expert group does not appear to be solely a result of clinical experience, as evidenced by our correlation analysis. Instead, it is associated with the intentional application of biomechanical principles. By adjusting the foot position and guiding the trunk forward, the experts align the recipient’s center of mass with their base of support. This pre-positioning reduces the mechanical work required for the legs to lift the body, allowing even recipients with limited strength to contribute effectively. The observed delay and reduction in muscle activity in the Low weight-bearing group suggest that standard lifting techniques may inadvertently suppress care recipients’ abilities by depriving them of the opportunity to use their residual physical capacities.

The positive correlation between the independent weight-bearing and muscle activation indicates that expert facilitation optimizes the natural motor program. From the perspective of motor control theories and neuromuscular facilitation principles, appropriate physical assistance plays a crucial role in regulating muscle activation. For instance, previous research has demonstrated that therapeutic interventions, such as Neuro-Developmental Treatment, can significantly improve the muscle synergy structure and temporal activation during the extension phase of the sit-to-stand motion in hemiplegic patients (Yang et al., 2020). Similarly, the expert techniques observed in this study likely function as an effective neuromuscular facilitation strategy, normalizing the motor control of the hip and knee extensors to mirror unassisted movement. This contrasts with the lifting approach seen in the Mid and Low weight-bearing groups, where excessive external support led to unnatural movement and suppressed muscle activation, aligning with the principles reported by Piersol and Riemann (2020). While previous studies used robotic models to clarify these mechanical principles (Lin et al., 2021), our study validates these strategies through actual physiological responses in human interactions.

Clinical and practical implications: functional independence, safety, and caregiving practice

Promoting independent weight-bearing and active extensor muscle recruitment during routine care transitions has profound clinical and practical implications, particularly for vulnerable populations such as frail geriatric patients, stroke survivors, and intensive care unit patients undergoing early mobilization. Regarding functional independence, encouraging active extensor recruitment during routine transitions is vital for preventing disuse syndrome and learned helplessness (Scott et al., 2018). By consistently utilizing residual strength, recipients can prolong their ability to perform activities of daily living independently.

Furthermore, it is noteworthy that substantial within-group variability in biomechanical performance was observed, particularly among the general nurses. This variance likely reflects the diverse range of routine care practices, individual handling habits, and varying levels of informal training present in general clinical settings, further underscoring the critical need for standardized, biomechanically sound facilitation protocols.

In terms of safety, this approach benefits both parties. When recipients actively coordinate their center of mass movement, the risk of balance loss or slipping—common in forceful passive lifting—is minimized. Simultaneously, the physical load on the caregiver is reduced, offering a practical way to align with "no-lift" policies (Waters et al., 2007) while avoiding the functional decline associated with over-reliance on passive mechanical assistance.

Consequently, our results highlight the potential benefits of a paradigm shift in caregiving practice from lifting to facilitating. Integrating these techniques may help transform routine care into an opportunity to promote patient autonomy while protecting the caregiver’s musculoskeletal health.

Educational implications: beyond communication to physical interaction

The inclusion of three caregiver groups allowed us to consider proficiency across a spectrum ranging from no professional clinical experience to general clinical practice and specialized Humanitude expertise. The findings did not indicate a simple progression in biomechanical proficiency with increasing clinical experience. Instead, they suggest that effective facilitation may depend on acquiring structured, technique-specific strategies, such as guiding anterior translation of the center of mass rather than lifting the recipient vertically. Routine exposure to caregiving alone may therefore be insufficient for developing these skills, highlighting the need for explicit instruction and competency-based assessment. The observational design does not permit causal attribution of the observed differences to Humanitude training, and this interpretation should be examined in future longitudinal or interventional studies.

Building on this interpretation, our findings highlight an important area for professional development. Recent educational advances, such as augmented reality, have successfully enhanced caregiver empathy (Nakazawa et al., 2023); our results additionally emphasize the importance of structured training in the mechanics of physical interaction. As Hodder et al. (2010) argued, expert biomechanical strategies are learned competencies. Integrating the quantitative metrics identified in this study, including center-of-mass trajectory control and the independent weight-bearing ratio, into educational frameworks alongside communication training may enable caregivers to acquire and assess facilitation skills more explicitly. Combining empathetic communication with biomechanically sound facilitation may transform routine daily assistance into high-quality rehabilitative care and contribute to the prevention of hospital-associated disability (Zisberg et al., 2011).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the experimental protocol involved only two healthy and independent individuals serving as simulated care recipients. This design was essential for experimental control, standardization, and safety, but it limits the direct generalizability of our findings to clinical populations, such as frail older adults or patients with neurological or cognitive impairments. Crucially, we did not measure direct clinical outcomes, such as actual fall rates or longitudinal changes in activities of daily living independence. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as controlled biomechanical evidence regarding the physical impact of the technique. Therefore, we strongly recommend that future research employ longitudinal designs involving actual clinical populations to directly investigate the impact of these techniques on concrete clinical outcomes, including the reduction of falls and the preservation of activities of daily living independence.

Second, we did not collect quantitative data on the caregivers’ physical characteristics, such as height, weight, or baseline physical strength. Although we examined the association between overall years of clinical experience and biomechanical outcomes across the full sample, we did not collect detailed information on prior patient-handling experience or conduct within-group or multivariable analyses of these potential confounding factors. To prioritize comparability across a large number of caregivers (N = 59), we utilized simulated recipients with consistent body types. Although allowing this natural variation in caregiver physique and experience helps preserve the ecological validity of the study by mirroring real-world clinical environments, these factors inherently influence performance during physical assistance, and individuals with greater height, physical strength, or extensive clinical background might have had a biomechanical advantage. Since a significant discrepancy in body size and experience may substantially affect biomechanical demands and safety, future research should systematically record both caregiver physical characteristics and detailed professional histories to evaluate and control for these confounding factors.

Third, because this was an exploratory investigation, the sample sizes for the Humanitude Experts (n = 9) and nursing students (n = 10) were relatively small and highly unequal compared to the general nurse group (n = 40), which represents a key limitation affecting the external validity of our subgroup estimates. The substantial group imbalance and the lack of an a priori power analysis inherent to this convenience sampling approach mean that our data may not fully capture the wider variance in physical characteristics or individual caregiving styles present in the broader population of healthcare professionals. To address these sample characteristics transparently, we utilized robust non-parametric tests to mitigate the impact of outliers and unequal variances. Additionally, to provide a secondary methodological reference despite the retrospective design, a post hoc power analysis is presented. The post hoc analysis yielded estimated power values of 0.88–0.98. Given its retrospective nature, these values should be regarded only as supplementary descriptive indices rather than evidence of sample-size adequacy. Future research should involve larger, more balanced cohorts across a broader range of clinical conditions and investigate the longitudinal effects of Humanitude-based assistance on the recovery of independent mobility in actual patient populations.

Conclusion

This study quantitatively demonstrated that sit-to-stand assistance provided by Humanitude experts has the potential to promote significantly higher active participation and more natural biomechanical patterns in care recipients compared to standard nursing techniques. Our findings reveal that expert caregivers do not merely lift the recipient but strategically guide the center of mass, which contributes to optimizing independent weight-bearing ratio and muscle activation.

In an era where “no-lift” policies are essential for caregiver safety, this study provides evidence for a third way: a facilitatory approach that is expected to ensure safety by reducing physical load while simultaneously addressing the risks of functional decline and learned helplessness. By integrating these expert biomechanical strategies into nursing education and clinical practice, there is a significant opportunity to transform routine daily assistance into meaningful rehabilitative interventions. Further clinical trials are needed to confirm long-term outcomes, such as reduced fall risk and improved activities of daily living. The present study provides a biomechanical foundation for these future investigations and for enhancing the quality of care and autonomy of older adults.
