Geospatial Discrepancy of Chronic Hepatitis B and Hepatitis D Testing in Alberta: A Population‐Based Spatial Analysis
Authors: Bryce Tkachuk, Isabelle Couloigner, Carla S. Coffin, Jason Jiang, Stefania Bertazzon, Abdel‐Aziz Shaheen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic hepatitis B (CHB) and hepatitis D virus (HDV) coinfection are major causes of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. The geographic distribution of CHB and alignment of anti‐HDV testing with CHB burden remain poorly defined. We aimed to examine if anti‐HDV testing and seropositivity align with areas of highest CHB burden. Adults with CHB (2014–2022) were identified using a validated serologic algorithm in the provincial laboratory database capturing HBV/HDV serology. Cases were geolocated to Aggregate Dissemination Areas (ADAs), linked to Alberta Health Services zones and rural–urban continuum levels. Age‐ and sex‐standardized CHB prevalence was calculated per ADA using the 2016 Canadian Census. Descriptive mapping, global spatial autocorrelation and local hotspot analysis were used to assess spatial clustering. Among 8317 persons with CHB, the provincial age‐ and sex‐standardized prevalence was 1.70 per 1000 population (95% CI, 1.66–1.74). Prevalence ranged from 2.15 per 1000 in metropolitan areas to 0.40 per 1000 in remote regions and was higher in Calgary than Edmonton (2.53 vs. 1.73 per 1000). Overall, 17.5% of CHB patients were tested for anti‐HDV, and 4.1% of those tested were anti‐HDV positive. CHB prevalence hotspots were concentrated in metropolitan ADAs, whereas hotspots of anti‐HDV testing and positivity showed only partial overlap with these high‐burden areas. CHB burden in Alberta is geographically clustered, particularly within specific neighbourhoods of metropolitan cities, whereas anti‐HDV testing remains infrequent and only partially aligned with high‐burden areas. Reflex or systematic anti‐HDV testing, paired with geographically informed outreach, may improve case detection.

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major global health problem, with an estimated 296 million people living with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) [1]. International guidelines emphasize HBV screening, care, monitoring and antiviral therapy for elimination [2]. The World Health Organization's 2030 viral hepatitis elimination targets remain unmet by many countries, particularly regarding diagnosis and treatment coverage [3, 4].

Hepatitis D virus (HDV) requires HBV for its life cycle and causes more rapid progression to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma than HBV infection alone [5, 6]. Approximately 12 million people worldwide are anti‐HDV positive, representing 4.5% of HBsAg‐positive individuals, with higher prevalence in certain regions [7]. Hepatology guidelines recommend HDV testing in HBsAg‐positive individuals with risk factors or advanced liver disease [2], while recent guidance supports broader HDV screening in high‐income countries [5, 8].

In low‐endemic, high‐income settings like North America, CHB burden is primarily driven by migration from endemic regions. In 2018, approximately 1.47 million foreign‐born individuals in the United States had CHB, accounting for most national cases [9]. CHB prevalence remains highest in Asia and sub‐Saharan Africa, with migration shaping epidemiology in destination countries [1]. Gaps in vaccination, diagnosis and treatment persist across many countries, threatening HBV elimination targets [3, 4].

In Canada, while CHB is in a low‐endemic status overall, data reveals inequities in immigration and care access. A recent Alberta population‐based cohort study showed age‐ and sex‐adjusted CHB incidence declined between 2015 and 2020, while prevalence doubled, with patients experiencing a mortality ratio of 3.9 compared to the general population [10]. However, that study did not examine neighbourhood‐level spatial clustering or hotspot/coldspot patterns of CHB prevalence or HDV testing. Geography significantly affects access to viral hepatitis diagnosis and treatment, and spatial epidemiology has mapped high‐burden areas for HCV testing and treatment in Alberta [11]. Despite the clinical importance of HDV and need to strengthen HBV/HDV care cascades, little is known about CHB and HDV testing distribution in Canada. Recent data from tertiary centers showed low HDV testing rates among CHB patients and substantial under‐diagnosis, even in high‐risk populations [12, 13]. Studies suggest that reflex or universal HDV testing could improve case detection and be cost‐effective in high‐income settings [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. However, it remains unclear whether communities with higher CHB burden in North America receive more HDV testing, or if HDV positivity creates clusters in specific neighbourhoods. Therefore, we conducted a province‐wide spatial analysis to characterize the neighbourhood‐level distribution and clustering of CHB prevalence and anti‐HDV. Specifically, we calculated ADA‐level age‐ and sex‐standardized CHB prevalence using Alberta population denominators and assessed whether hotspots of anti‐HDV testing and anti‐HDV seropositivity among persons with CHB spatially aligned with CHB prevalence hotspots, to inform targeted testing and implementation strategies.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a population‐based, retrospective spatial analysis of adults with CHB residing in Alberta, Canada, between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2022. Alberta has a population of approximately 4 million [19] and is administered by Alberta Health Services (AHS) through five geographic zones (North, Edmonton, Central, Calgary and South).

Data Sources and Case Definitions

We used the AHS provincial laboratory database, which captures all HBV and HDV serology and HBV DNA tests for Alberta residents, to identify CHB cases and anti‐HDV testing. The laboratory data were linked to the Alberta Health Care Insurance Plan (AHCIP) Registry using unique provincial health numbers to confirm residency and to obtain age and sex. CHB was defined using the same laboratory‐based algorithm employed in our previous epidemiology study [10]. Briefly, a person was classified as having CHB if they had persistent detection of HBsAg or HBeAg for at least 6 months, or if their HBV serology pattern was consistent with chronic infection in the absence of anti‐HBc IgM [10]. We included individuals aged 20 years or older at the time of first qualifying test. People who were non‐residents of Alberta, non‐registrants in AHCIP, or whose records could not be geolocated were excluded.

For each person with CHB, we identified all HDV antibody (anti‐HDV) tests and extracted their results. ‘HDV tested’ was defined as having at least one anti‐HDV test during the study period. ‘HDV positive’ was defined as having at least one reactive anti‐HDV test. HDV RNA results were not available in the provincial laboratory database used for this analysis; therefore, anti‐HDV positivity was interpreted as serologic evidence of HDV exposure and not as confirmation of active or chronic HDV infection.

Geographic Units and Rural–Urban Continuum

Residential postal codes were obtained from the AHCIP Registry and linked to geographic units using AHS Postal Code Translator Files. Individuals with CHB were geolocated to Aggregate Dissemination Areas (ADAs), small standard geographic units defined by Statistics Canada that represent neighbourhood‐scale areas [20]. ADA‐level population counts and age/sex distributions were derived from the 2016 Canadian Census. ADAs with zero population or missing linkage were excluded. Each ADA was assigned to one of four categories along a rural–urban continuum based on adapted rural–urban continuum area (RUCA): metropolitan, urban, rural, or remote. Metropolitan ADAs include the two major cities (i.e., Calgary and Edmonton) and their highly urbanized surroundings. Urban ADAs represent five second‐tier urban centres (e.g., Medicine Hat) and their surrounding suburban regions. Rural ADAs include small towns and hinterland. Remote ADAs are sparsely populated northern regions. Each ADA was also assigned to an AHS zone (North, Edmonton, Central, Calgary, South) [21].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was age‐ and sex‐standardized CHB prevalence per 1000 population at the ADA level over the 2014–2022 period. For each ADA, CHB prevalence was calculated as the number of CHB cases divided by the ADA population and directly standardized to the 2016 Canadian population by 5‐year age and sex strata. Secondary outcomes were defined among individuals with CHB. For each ADA, we calculated: (1) the proportion who had at least one anti‐HDV test; and (2) among those tested for anti‐HDV, the proportion who were anti‐HDV positive. These measures were summarized for the province, for each AHS zone and across the rural–urban continuum.

Statistical and Spatial Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize CHB counts, crude prevalence and standardized prevalence across the rural–urban categories. Provincial estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests applied to ADA‐level rates were used to compare CHB prevalence and anti‐HDV indicators across rural–urban categories and between metropolitan regions, mirroring the approach from the HCV spatial analysis. Descriptive maps were generated to visualize the spatial distribution of age‐ and sex‐standardized CHB prevalence, anti‐HDV testing and anti‐HDV positivity across Alberta. Geographic data are known to exhibit spatial autocorrelation (i.e., greater self‐similarity over short distances) [22]. The extent of spatial autocorrelation in a dataset can help assess its spatial pattern (i.e., clustered, dispersed, or random). Among the spatial autocorrelation indices in the literature, Moran's I is one of the most widely used. Its value ranges from −1 to +1. Positive I values indicate positive spatial autocorrelation, i.e., clustering of similar values (e.g., low values near low values); negative I values indicate negative spatial autocorrelation, that is, a dispersed pattern (e.g., low values near high values); null I values indicate lack of spatial autocorrelation, i.e., spatial randomness. In our study, Moran's I was used to assess the spatial pattern of age‐ and sex‐standardized CHB prevalence, anti‐HDV testing and anti‐HDV positivity at the ADA level. The analysis was conducted at the spatial level (aka analytical scale, or spatial resolution) of ADA. Hotspot analysis uses a local indicator of spatial autocorrelation, that is, Gi*, to compare the clustering of a variable applying a moving window with a defined neighbourhood over the study area [23]. Thus, hotspot analysis identifies spatially contiguous and statistically significant areas of high prevalence (i.e., hotspots) and of low prevalence (i.e., cold spots). It differs from Moran's I spatial autocorrelation index in that: (1) it uses a different statistic; (2) Moran's I (in the form defined above) is a global index, that is, it yields a single value for a whole region (e.g., Alberta, Urban Central), whereas Gi* is a local indicator, that s, it yields one value for each ADA within any region; (3) Moran's I assesses positive vs. negative spatial autocorrelation, that is, clustered vs. dispersed (or random) pattern, whereas Gi*, further to identifying positive spatial autocorrelation, shows clusters of high values (i.e., hotspots) vs. clusters of low values (i.e., cold spots). Analyses were conducted using R and ArcGIS Desktop/Pro. Ethics approval and data‐sharing agreements were obtained through the University of Calgary and AHS, consistent with approvals for the previous CHB and HCV work.

Results

Cohort and Overall CHB Prevalence

We identified 8317 adults with laboratory‐confirmed CHB who were residents of Alberta and could be geolocated to an ADA. The overall cohort description and age‐ and sex‐standardized prevalence estimates by AHS zone and rural–urban category were summarized in Table 1. Provincial crude CHB prevalence was 1.95 per 1000 population, while the age‐ and sex‐standardized prevalence was 1.70 per 1000 (95% CI, 1.66–1.74). CHB burden was concentrated in metropolitan areas. As shown in Table 1, 7510 cases (90.3% of all CHB) resided in metropolitan ADAs, corresponding to a crude prevalence of 2.53 per 1000 and an age/sex standardized prevalence of 2.15 per 1000 (95% CI, 2.10–2.20). In contrast, standardized prevalence was 0.82 per 1000 (95% CI, 0.74–0.90) in urban ADAs, 0.49 per 1000 (95% CI, 0.44–0.55) in rural ADAs and 0.40 per 1000 (95% CI, 0.24–0.56) in remote ADAs (p < 0.001 for metropolitan versus non‐metropolitan). Within metropolitan Alberta, CHB prevalence differed between Calgary and Edmonton. Metro Calgary had 4613 CHB cases among 1,547,735 residents (crude prevalence 2.98 per 1000) and a standardized prevalence of 2.53 per 1000 (95% CI, 2.45–2.61). Anti‐HDV Testing and Positivity Among People With CHB

Anti‐HDV testing and positivity among people with CHB are summarized in Table 1. Of the 8317 CHB patients, 1455 (17.5%) were tested for anti‐HDV. Among those tested, 59 (4.1%) were anti‐HDV positive. Across the rural–urban continuum, anti‐HDV testing remained modest. In metropolitan ADAs, 1322 of 7510 CHB patients (17.6%) were tested for anti‐HDV; in urban ADAs, 83 of 428 (19.4%); in rural ADAs, 48 of 355 (13.5%); and in remote ADAs, 2 of 24 (8.3%), p = 0.09. Among those tested, anti‐HDV positivity was 4.1% in metropolitan ADAs, 4.8% in urban ADAs, 2.1% in rural ADAs, and 0.0% in remote ADAs, although denominators were small outside metropolitan areas.

Within‐City Patterns in Calgary and Edmonton

Within the city of Calgary, Table 2 presents anti‐HDV testing and positivity by city quadrant. Among 4417 CHB cases, 783 individuals (17.7%) were tested for anti‐HDV, and 36 (4.6%) were anti‐HDV positive. Proportions tested were similar across quadrants (16%–18%), but positivity varied from 3.5% in the northeast to 9.0% in the southeast. Within the city of Edmonton, among 2787 CHB cases, 481 individuals (17.3%) were tested for anti‐HDV and 18 (3.7%) were anti‐HDV positive. Proportions tested were broadly similar across quadrants (16%–20%), while positivity varied from 3.1% in the southwest to 5.5% in the northwest.

Spatial Autocorrelation

Global Moran's I demonstrated strong positive spatial autocorrelation (clustering) for age/sex‐standardized CHB rates at the provincial level (I = 0.75; p < 0.001) and within metropolitan regions (Metro Edmonton I = 0.55; p < 0.001; Metro Calgary I = 0.69; p < 0.001; combined Metro I = 0.67; p < 0.001), Table S1. At a finer geographic scale, clustering was also observed in the combined Urban stratum (I = 0.13; p = 0.028) and Urban South (I = 0.23; p = 0.011), whereas rural and remote strata were consistent with spatial randomness (e.g., Rural I = 0.01; p = 0.54; Remote (North) I = 0.06; p = 0.30; Rural Central I = −0.09; p = 0.43; Rural North I = −0.08; p = 0.57), Table S1. Moran's I was not reported for the smallest strata when estimation was not stable or informative (e.g., Urban North, Urban Central, Rural Calgary, Rural South), Table S1.

For anti‐HDV, Moran's I showed moderate clustering for the spatial distribution of anti‐HDV testing at the provincial level (I = 0.42; p < 0.001) and within metropolitan Edmonton (I = 0.24; p < 0.001), metropolitan Calgary (I = 0.27; p < 0.001) and the combined metropolitan region (I = 0.27; p < 0.001), Table S2. Most non‐metropolitan strata were consistent with spatial randomness (e.g., Urban South I = 0.00; p = 0.60; Rural I = 0.02; p = 0.23; Remote (North) I = 0.04; p = 0.19; Rural Central I = −0.01; p = 0.82), while the combined Urban stratum showed borderline evidence of clustering (I = 0.08; p = 0.050), Table S2. Spatial autocorrelation for anti‐HDV positivity was weak: clustering was present at the provincial level (I = 0.09; p < 0.001) and in the combined metropolitan region (I = 0.05; p = 0.046), but not within Edmonton or Calgary when analyzed separately (both I = 0.04, p = 0.21 and p = 0.17, respectively). Outside metropolitan strata, anti‐HDV positivity autocorrelation could not be evaluated due to small counts (< 30).

Hotspot Patterns

Hotspot analysis (Gi*) identified statistically significant, spatially contiguous clusters that were concentrated within the two metropolitan regions (Figures 1, 2, 3). For CHB prevalence, Metro Calgary exhibited a large hotspot extending from north Calgary through the northeast to Chestermere, with smaller adjacent hotspots on the west side of the primary cluster; Metro Edmonton exhibited a single large hotspot extending from St. Albert to the city centre (north of the river) (Figure 1).

Discussion

This province‐wide, population‐based spatial analysis demonstrates that CHB in Alberta is geographically concentrated and statistically clustered at neighbourhood level, and that anti‐HDV screening is not yet embedded as a routine component of CHB care. The provincial age‐ and sex‐standardized prevalence of CHB was 1.70 per 1000 population; 90.3% of cases resided in metropolitan Calgary or Edmonton, with higher prevalence in Calgary than Edmonton. Only 17.5% of individuals underwent anti‐HDV testing and 4.1% of those tested were anti‐HDV positive. Together, these findings identify a high‐yield implementation gap. HDV exposure is present at a clinically meaningful frequency, but the majority of the CHB population is not being screened.

The metropolitan concentration of CHB we observed is not unexpected and aligns with contemporary HBV epidemiology in high‐income settings, where prevalent infection increasingly reflects migration from endemic regions rather than ongoing domestic transmission. Global syntheses emphasize that diagnosis and linkage to care remain the dominant barriers to HBV elimination [1]. Recent Canadian population‐based work similarly indicates that immigrants and refugees account for a substantial share of HBV burden [24]. These realities are reflected in current European guidance, which emphasizes systematic case‐finding and care pathways that can reach populations with barriers to conventional clinic‐based screening [25].

A key contribution of this study is that it moves beyond expected metropolitan concentration by identifying statistically significant within metropolitan hotspots and cold spots that translate into actionable target geographies. Hepatitis B rates showed very strong positive spatial autocorrelation at the provincial level (Moran's I 0.75; p < 0.001) and remained significantly clustered in both metropolitan regions (Edmonton Moran's I 0.55; Calgary Moran's I 0.69; p < 0.001), while most rural/remote strata were consistent with spatial randomness. Hotspot mapping further localized burden to contiguous neighbourhoods, including a large Calgary hotspot extending from the north through the northeast towards Chestermere and an Edmonton hotspot extending from St. Albert towards the city centre and north of the river. The operational value of identifying such clusters is that it enables targeted, place‐based intervention design, an approach that has been used in other viral hepatitis contexts, where hotspot detection was proposed as a basis for prioritizing future screening and prevention efforts [26].

In contrast to the strong spatial signal for CHB burden, anti‐HDV‐related outcomes showed weaker and less consistent clustering, likely reflecting both biology and implementation. Anti‐HDV testing demonstrated spatial autocorrelation at the provincial level and within Edmonton, Calgary and the combined metropolitan region (Moran's I 0.42, 0.24, 0.27 and 0.27, respectively; all p < 0.001). Anti‐HDV positivity showed only weak autocorrelation (Moran's I 0.09; p < 0.001) and did not demonstrate consistent within‐city clustering, consistent with smaller numbers. These findings reinforce a key point for interpretation: the HBV burden signal is robust and highly clustered, whereas the anti‐HDV signal is weaker and more vulnerable to under‐ascertainment driven by incomplete testing. This distinction is clinically important because anti‐HDV testing identifies serologic exposure, whereas HDV RNA testing is required to confirm active infection. In our setting, HDV RNA results were not available in the provincial laboratory database, limiting assessment of the full diagnostic cascade. Reliable RNA confirmation is also increasingly important given the genetic diversity of HDV. Recent international sequencing data demonstrated substantial HDV diversity, including greater genotype 1 diversity than previously recognized, with implications for molecular diagnostic assay design and performance [27].

The clinical importance of closing this ascertainment gap is underscored by guideline statements recognizing HDV as the most aggressive form of chronic viral hepatitis and recommending structured diagnostic pathways that include anti‐HDV testing and confirmatory HDV RNA testing [8]. The same direction is emphasized in U.S. expert guidance, which highlights broad (often one‐time universal) anti‐HDV testing for people with CHB as the most reliable way to reduce underdiagnosis [28].

Our observed anti‐HDV positivity (4.1% among those tested) is directionally consistent with global estimates of HDV exposure among HBsAg‐positive individuals derived from a large meta‐analysis [7]. However, because only 17.5% of our CHB cohort underwent anti‐HDV testing, the absolute burden of undiagnosed HDV in Alberta may be materially higher than the number of identified cases. This interpretation is supported by multiple health‐system studies documenting selective and incomplete HDV testing, which can bias prevalence estimates downward. In the Veterans Health Administration, incomplete testing raised concern about underestimation of prevalence and reinforced the need for systematic approaches [29]. More recently, persistently low testing performance across two large U.S. CHB cohorts further underscored that under‐testing remains common even in well‐resourced health systems [30].

The spatial results also suggest why physician‐driven or risk‐based testing may produce uneven ascertainment. Hotspot analysis suggested that anti‐HDV testing hotspots were smaller, less contiguous and more sensitive to analytic choices than the HBV burden hotspots, and that anti‐HDV testing overlapped only partially with areas of highest HBV prevalence. Testing patterns may reflect provider ordering behaviour, clinic or facility location, service availability and clinician awareness, in addition to neighbourhood‐level risk. In Metro Calgary, anti‐HDV testing hotspots appeared within the city and overlapped an inner‐city area rather than the suburban extension (Chestermere), while in Metro Edmonton testing hotspots included a small cluster near the city centre and a larger cluster in the southwest metro region. Moreover, anti‐HDV positivity hotspots were sparse: a small hotspot was described in southwest Edmonton, and the Calgary hotspot stretched from east of downtown towards Chestermere. In this context, the quadrant‐level analyses provide an additional signal that the distribution of positivity may not mirror the distribution of testing: while testing proportions were broadly similar across quadrants, anti‐HDV positivity varied (e.g., Calgary 3.5% in the northeast quadrant vs. 9.0% in the southeast quadrant). Taken together, the results suggest that testing location may reflect service availability as much as neighbourhood‐level risk and that a default testing strategy (rather than physician‐driven risk‐based testing) is likely required for equitable detection.

These observations support evaluation of systematic and reflex anti‐HDV testing for people with CHB. Multiple implementation studies demonstrate that reflex anti‐HDV testing, automatically adding anti‐HDV when HBsAg is positive, can increase testing coverage and identify additional cases that would otherwise remain undiagnosed. In Spain, implementation of anti‐HDV testing in all HBsAg‐positive individuals was associated with a five‐fold increase in HDV diagnoses [14]. In Italy, introduction of an anti‐HDV reflex testing strategy increased identification of HDV infection among HBsAg‐positive individuals [15]. More recently, reflex anti‐HDV testing at a Central European tertiary centre demonstrated high diagnostic yield among patients with a first positive HBsAg [31]. Importantly, reflex approaches also reduce reliance on clinician awareness and improve equity by making the diagnostic pathway less dependent on where a patient receives care.

Although modelling studies suggest that one‐time universal HDV testing among adults with CHB can be cost‐effective in some settings, our study did not conduct an Alberta‐specific cost‐effectiveness or budget‐impact analysis [32]. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as identifying an implementation gap and supporting evaluation of reflex or systematic anti‐HDV testing, rather than proving that universal reflex testing is cost‐effective in Alberta. The clinical value of diagnosis is increasing as therapeutic options evolve. In addition, the availability of more effective HDV therapies increases the clinical value of diagnosis: a Phase 3 study of bulevirtide demonstrated clinically meaningful virologic and biochemical outcomes in chronic hepatitis D [33]. As therapeutic options expand, failing to diagnose HDV increasingly represents a missed opportunity for risk stratification and intervention.

From a policy and operations perspective, the central implication of this work is that geography can be used as a pragmatic lever to strengthen the HBV‐HDV cascade. Evaluation of reflex or systemic anti‐HDV testing for all HBsAg‐positive individuals would address the most immediately modifiable determinant of underdiagnosis: incomplete screening. In parallel, identification of contiguous CHB hotspots provides an evidence base for allocating outreach resources, community‐based screening, culturally tailored education, and linkage‐to‐care navigation to neighbourhoods with the highest expected yield. This ‘dual strategy’ (system‐level reflex testing plus geographically targeted outreach) is particularly relevant for micro‐elimination approaches in low‐endemic settings, where the burden is concentrated in specific urban communities and where one size fits all approaches risk diluting resources without meaningfully improving case finding.

This study has several strengths relevant to hepatology practice and health‐system planning. We leveraged a province‐wide laboratory repository capturing HBV and anti‐HDV serologies across Alberta, reducing referral bias and enabling a population‐based view of testing practices. We linked cases to small‐area neighbourhood units and applied complementary spatial statistics, translating an administrative dataset into actionable geographic signals. Several limitations warrant consideration. First, HDV RNA results were not available in the provincial laboratory database used for this analysis. Anti‐HDV seropositivity therefore indicates serologic evidence of HDV exposure and cannot distinguish resolved exposure from active or chronic HDV infection. Consequently, this study should not be interpreted as estimating the prevalence of chronic hepatitis D in Alberta. Anti‐HDV testing was incomplete and likely selective; therefore, the observed 4.1% anti‐HDV seropositivity among tested individuals should not be generalized directly to all persons with CHB. Our estimates reflect diagnosed CHB captured through laboratory testing and therefore underestimate the total community burden by excluding undiagnosed infections. Use of residential postal codes and 2016 Census denominators could introduce potential misclassification related to mobility and demographic change over time. Finally, ecological analyses cannot identify individual‐level determinants (e.g., country of birth, household transmission, or behavioural exposures) driving hotspots; nonetheless, the observed mismatch between CHB burden hotspots and anti‐HDV testing hotspots is itself a meaningful implementation signal.

In conclusion, CHB burden in Alberta is geographically clustered, particularly in metropolitan Calgary and Edmonton, whereas anti‐HDV testing remains infrequent and only partially aligned with high‐burden areas. These findings support evaluation of systematic or reflex anti‐HDV testing, paired with confirmatory HDV RNA pathways and geographically informed outreach, to improve HBV/HDV case detection and implementation efforts.
