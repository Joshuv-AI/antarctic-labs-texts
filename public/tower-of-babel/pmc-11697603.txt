Accelerating access to human genomics for public health: perspectives from the Western Pacific region
Authors: Expert Meeting on Accelerating Access to Human Genomics for Public Health Participants
Published: 2024

Over the past two decades, awareness has grown about the wide-ranging applications of genomic technologies in human health and beyond.1 This awareness was further heightened during the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic, which underscored the critical role of genomics while revealing global disparities in its adoption and benefit sharing. Recognising the potential of genomics and its associated implementation challenges, the World Health Organization (WHO) Science Council produced its inaugural report2 in 2022, outlining a roadmap to accelerate access to genomic technologies and their applications, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In these settings, advancements in genomic initiatives have contributed to improved infectious disease surveillance, aiding in the detection and monitoring of emerging respiratory pathogens, zoonotic diseases, tuberculosis, and antimicrobial resistance.3 The report led to the launch of the WHO genomics programme of work to advance the understanding and implementation of genomic technologies in research and clinical practice, with the goal of contributing to the improvement of the health of communities and populations.

To raise awareness of the benefits and risks of implementing human genomics technologies, develop a joint vision and identify pragmatic approaches to accelerate access to human genomics for public health in the Western Pacific region, an expert consultation meeting was convened on 29–30 April 2024 in Manila, Philippines.4 Forty-seven experts from 14 countries and areas in the region came together. They shared the challenges and priorities in the rollout of large-scale genomic initiatives, cancer genomics, and rare disease and congenital disorder genomics and discussed crosscutting issues including bioethics, health economics, bioinformatics, and research policy.

The meeting took an important step in mapping a regional landscape of human genomics. Numerous programmes were presented at the meeting. Japan's Initiative on Rare and Undiagnosed Diseases has established a nationwide diagnostic and research system for previously unreached populations with rare and undiagnosed diseases, covering entire geographic areas and across clinical subspecialty fields, and has provided final diagnoses in more than 2200 pedigrees.5 Australia's National Centre for Indigenous Genomics is building the national Indigenous genomic resource, governed by Indigenous communities.6 By enrolling East Asian, Southeast Asian, and South Asian populations that have long been underrepresented in existing genomic databases, the National Precision Medicine Strategy of Singapore aims to increase the diversity in population health research and leverage the resulting research outcomes to inform the development of clinical pathways for precision medicine.7,8

Though most examples shared at the meeting were situated in high- and upper middle-income countries in the region, the meeting revealed growing interest among stakeholders from less resourced settings in exploring applications of genomic technologies such as precision cancer care and rare disease diagnostics and demonstrating the respective public health value to policymakers at home. There was also broad agreement among experts that the implementation of human genomics would benefit from a pragmatic, stepwise approach that is focused on use cases with high impact for public health. This is particularly relevant for less resourced settings where governments are seeking to launch modest yet impactful initiatives.

Building on examples shared during the discussions (Table 1), the consensus from the meeting proposes a set of entry points for governments and institutions interested in pursuing large-scale human genomic initiatives.

Building an investment case: align human genomics initiatives with local healthcare needs and research priorities

Securing health policymakers’ support for human genomics initiatives requires identifying use cases that address local public health priorities and strategically aligning with policy landscape. For instance, evidence generated by the National Cancer Centre Singapore challenged existing age-based screening recommendations, highlighting the transformative potential of a cancer genomics programme to influence healthcare practices.9 Similarly, the multidimensional evaluation of the Hong Kong Genome Project at various stages of implementation has already generated early indication of benefits of the programme to other aspects of the local health system.4 The MyGenom Project, which aims to sequence at least 10,000 Malaysian genomes, finally secured buy-in from stakeholders after years of repeated proposals by aligning the project with the national policies across multiple sectors and ministries (Table 1).

Addressing implementation hurdles: start with what can be availed and evolve

A wide range of enablers were identified as essential to the implementation success of large-scale genomic programmes in the meeting, including the presence of policy frameworks and guidelines, access to genetic counselling services, education and training for health workers and medical providers, and sustainable financing solutions such as reimbursement strategies and provision of subsidies. While it is important to acknowledge the large systemic and multi-sectoral efforts need for human genomic initiatives, implementation can start small, with what is available and what can be done within current means. The Korea National Institute of Health commenced its 20-year national human genome research programme by first launching large scale cohort studies, closely followed by developing capacity in genomic data analysis and gradually building biobanks. Now its operational scale has evolved to include big data sharing platforms.4 The Tohoku Medical Megabank Project began as community-based cohorts in prefectures heavily affected in the Great East Japan Earthquake of 2011. It has grown to become one of largest multi-omics information platforms in Asia and an important resource to inform local policy decisions.10

Fostering an enabling regional ecosystem: collaborate in research and workforce development, build trust with the public and align multisectoral efforts

Institutions planning on embarking on human genomic programmes may begin by identifying areas of pathogen genomics infrastructure and capacity enhanced during the COVID-19 pandemic that can be repurposed, such as in Cambodia where the enhanced genomic sequencing capacity, facilitated in large part by international cooperation, has been leveraged to study other infectious diseases (Table 1). Implementation efforts can also be aided by recognising known enablers as exemplified in the efforts to enact the Rare Diseases Act of the Philippines which built upon the network of actors that supported the Newborn Screening Act more than a decade prior.4 New implementers can explore regional partnerships and participate in the training and educational activities provided by institutions in the region to build local expertise. Examples include the University of the Philippines Manila's genetics counselling programme, the Asia Pacific Bioinformatics Network's workshops on bioinformatics and analytics, and the Summer Internship for Indigenous Genomics, a capacity-building initiative for Indigenous communities implemented in New Zealand, Australia and Micronesia.4 The Philippine Genome Center conducts community and school outreach programmes such as genomics caravans and educational activities including symposia and workshops to build public trust in genomics, to train future scientists, and to promote participation in initiatives such as the Filipino Genome Sequencing Project (Table 1).

Collaboration between academia, government and industry is essential for expanding affordable access to genomic technologies, including sequencing facilities, diagnostic platforms and technical expertise. The Advanced Genomics Collaboration, a partnership between University of Melbourne and Illumina, built a hub within Melbourne Medical Precinct for accelerating translation of biomedical research to precision cancer care.11 Partnerships between hospitals and industry have been central to screening for drug responsiveness in around 6000 patients and families with epilepsy, cardiovascular disease, cancer and diabetes in Viet Nam.4 Furthermore, Japan's Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics data sharing platform with research institutions and industry has been instrumental in study participant selection and drug development.12 While these strategic partnerships are driven by local context, coordinated dialogues among parties at the regional level can help raise awareness and steer the direction of such collaborations towards equitable human genomics.

Partnerships between genomics experts, legislators, and patient advocacy groups are especially pivotal in advancing human genomics. In the Philippines, overcoming challenges in communicating the value of genomics to policymakers has been made possible to continuous engagement, not only by clinicians and researchers but more importantly by patients and their families, who have played a critical role in advocating for genomic solutions in rare diseases and congenital disorders.4

International collaboration is essential to advancing human genomics in the region. Institutions with a wealth of experience4 and resources can coordinate regional research collaborations, engage in workforce development opportunities, and lead collective efforts to build and share not only harmonized data systems but also inclusive genomic reference data. The Asian Thoracic Oncology Research Group contributes to advancing translational research in the region by coordinating multicentre clinical trials, molecular profiling projects and continuing education programmes (Table 1). The Centre for Population Genomics’ efforts to purposefully include underrepresented populations in genomic programmes through OurDNA exemplifies the need to collaborate on building inclusive genome reference databases.4

Addressing the ethical, legal and social implications of human genomics to realise transformative potential for all

To fully harness human genomics for public health, solidarity and inclusivity are indispensable. National and regional policies should focus on involving patients, families and underrepresented groups, including indigenous populations in the conception, implementation, and evaluation of genomic initiatives. It is also crucial to expand beyond conventional biomedical ethics to a broader public health ethics that emphasizes achievement of equity through collective action.

At present, the legal and political response to genomic activities is varied, and many countries are attempting to match their policies with the rapidly emerging genomic advancements. Since the enactment of the Bioethics and Safety Act of 2004 in the Republic of Korea, significant amendments tackling security, anonymization, and informed consent, among others, have been implemented but criticisms about their adequacy vis-à-vis emerging issues persist.4 In New Zealand, different aspects of genomic activities have been governed by different legislations, and efforts to regulate genomic initiatives under unified legal instruments are shaped by the political landscape.4 Regional collaboration can help by sharing expertise and practices, addressing ethical and legal challenges and improving genomic data governance to address the risk of genetic discrimination and privacy breeches.

While much needs to be done to accelerate access to human genomics for public health across countries, regardless of their socioeconomic status, significant momentum exists to build upon. The WHO is working to collaborate with meeting participants, partners, and Member States to address the priorities and to realise the collective vision for human genomics in the Western Pacific.
