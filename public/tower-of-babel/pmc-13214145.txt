Automatic and explainable assessment for Parkinson’s disease by video-based human motion understanding
Authors: Mengqi Liu, Yixing Ye, Haolun Li, Yijing Guo, Ping Liang, Xuemei Li, Chunyu Yin, Xinguang Yu, Xinyuan Dong, Yiqin Yao, Chi-Man Pun, Jing Xie, Kun Yang, Rui Zong, Dongsheng Kong, Feng Xu, Hao Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe assessment of Parkinson’s disease depends heavily on neurologist experience and involves significant clinical workload, where standard motor examinations require substantial time and face-to-face evaluation, limiting accessibility for patients with mobility constraints. Furthermore, current rating scales contain subjective definitions that lead to rating inconsistencies among clinicians. Existing automated methods exhibit severe problems, including being applicable to only single symptoms, lacking clinical interpretability, and insufficient accuracy.MethodsWe proposed an AI-based, fully automatic, and explainable PD assessment technique using videos. The system detects keypoints on face, body, hands, and feet, then extracts motion features including amplitude, frequency, velocity, and acceleration that directly correspond to MDS-UPDRS rating criteria, enabling explainable assessment. We evaluate all 16 vision-based items in the MDS-UPDRS motor examination, covering symptom categories such as masked face, bradykinesia, postural instability, and tremor symptoms.ResultsOur automated system achieved accuracies of 97.2%, 90.1%, 96.6%, and 96.2% for the four symptom categories respectively in our clinical experiments. When used as clinical support in the real clinical assessment process, the system improved clinician accuracy from 78.7 to 85.3% overall, with correction rates of 15.1%, 8.0%, 36.9%, and 80.1% for each symptom.ConclusionsThis AI-based video assessment provides accurate, automatic, objective, and interpretable Parkinson’s disease evaluation while supporting clinical decision-making. The system enables remote monitoring and reduces healthcare resource strain, particularly benefiting regions with limited neurological expertise.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD), including symptoms such as masked face, bradykinesia, postural instability, and tremor [1, 2], leads to a significant decline in motor function and in life quality for patients [3, 4]. Among populations aged 65 and older, the incidence of PD is approximately 108 to 212 per 100,000 person-years [5]. The increasing prevalence of PD imposes substantial social and economic burdens on patients’ families and healthcare systems [6]. Although therapeutic approaches such as neuromodulation devices have been developed to manage PD symptoms [7], accurate assessment of disease severity remains essential for treatment planning and monitoring. The clinical diagnosis of PD primarily relies on the observation and assessment of motor symptoms, which requires experienced neurologists to identify characteristic movement patterns [8]. At present, the Movement Disorder Society-sponsored revision of the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) is the primary tool utilized by neurologists to evaluate the severity and progression of PD [9]. The rating for the MDS-UPDRS Part III (motor examination) is directly performed by neurologists through a face-to-face interview with a patient one at a time. This assessment process requires the clinicians to guide and observe patients in the hospital, demanding considerable time and effort from both parties (approximately 20 to 50 min). Due to the limited mobility of PD patients, regular hospital visits for monitoring their condition may be impractical, which can easily lead to untimely medication and even potential exacerbation of the disease. Furthermore, the definitions for some item ratings in the MDS-UPDRS motor examination are ambiguous and subjective, and some ratings are based on quantitative values of movement amplitude, which are difficult to distinguish [10]. Even experienced neurologists may make rating errors. Studies have shown that clinical raters often disagree on exact MDS-UPDRS scores due to inter-rater variability, which may introduce uncertainty into disease assessment [11].

Nowadays, mobile devices like smartphones have strong capabilities in visual sensing and computing, which opens up the potential for telemedicine in assessing patients with PD [12–14]. The vision-based MDS-UPDRS motor examination consists of 16 items, including 1 for masked face (facial expression), 6 for bradykinesia (finger tapping; hand movements; pronation-supination movements of hands; toe tapping; leg agility; global spontaneity of movement or body bradykinesia), 5 for postural instability (arising from chair; gait; freezing of gait; postural stability; posture), and 4 for tremor (postural tremor of the hands; kinetic tremor of the hands; rest tremor amplitude; constancy of rest tremor). It can undertake nearly 90% of the total assessment work defined in the MDS-UPDRS Part III. Therefore, it is important to leverage cameras of mobile devices and develop video analysis techniques to perform MDS-UPDRS motor examination automatically, which will provide objective and reliable evaluations of PD, improve the consistency of clinical ratings, and enable patients to monitor their condition at home while allowing clinicians to assess remotely and reduce their workload.

Advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Computer Vision (CV) [15–21] have paved the way for the automatic evaluation of items in the MDS-UPDRS motor examination. Several studies [22–32] have explored how to use automatic methods to measure the severity of Parkinson’s disease through video data. For instance, Guarín et al. [24] used video-based kinematic analysis and machine learning to detect early-stage PD from finger tapping, hand movements, and leg agility tasks, achieving an 86% detection accuracy. Lange et al. [25] applied computer vision to analyze over 1200 clinical videos of hand movements and identified three kinematic domains (e.g., speed, consistency, and timing/scale) that reliably respond to levodopa therapy. Other studies investigated gait [26], sit-to-stand transitions [27, 28], whole-body bradykinesia [29], and tremor severity from videos [30, 31]. Additionally, learning-based systems such as FastEval [32] were developed for online motor symptom evaluation of multiple items. A recent systematic review by Pecoraro et al. [33] summarized 45 eligible studies using computer vision for PD assessment. This review confirmed that most existing studies focus on individual motor tasks, with gait and bradykinesia being the most investigated symptoms, while comprehensive multi-symptom assessment remains limited.

Based on the above literature, we summarize three main limitations of existing techniques. First, they typically focus on a single symptom or even a single item within the MDS-UPDRS motor examination, and the multi-person items (3.12, 3.16) and comprehensive items (3.13, 3.14, 3.18) are never considered by previous works due to the high technical challenge. Second, for the considered items, they only train AI models to estimate the scores from videos, and they do not extract critical interpretability information like tremor amplitude of body joints for clinicians and patients to understand and trust. Third, existing AI-based methods usually exhibit low accuracy when estimating human motions in videos, which hinders clinical trials and clinical assessment support, especially when assessing tremor-related items (3.15–3.18) that require centimeter-level accuracy. Given all these drawbacks, existing techniques are incomplete and inaccurate to use in real clinical practice.

In this study, we propose an AI-based, explainable assessment method for Parkinson’s disease using videos to calculate ALL vision-based items of the MDS-UPDRS motor examination. These assessment results are obtained from the calculated time-varying movement frequency, amplitude, velocity, acceleration and other interpretable indicators of the body parts of a patient, which makes clinicians better understand and trust the assessment results and thus use them in their real clinical assessment. We further develop a clinical support system based on common mobile devices like phones or tablets to provide the aforementioned information to aid the clinicians’ assessment. As shown in Fig. 1a, this system estimates ratings for all 16 vision-based items in MDS-UPDRS motor examination. Technically, our AI system first detects the coordinates of keypoints on the face, body, hands, or feet in each video frame. Then, the system extracts a series of clinically explainable objective indicators for different tasks before calculating the final assessment results. For example, for tremor-related items, we directly quantify tremor amplitude and score it according to the MDS-UPDRS standards. For items with subjective rating descriptions (e.g., bradykinesia), the system provides temporal trend curves of the extracted features, such as the amplitude and frequency of finger tapping, to present severity-related changes to clinicians. Please note that our system first detects the coordinates of keypoints on different body-parts in each video frame, rather than directly estimating the ratings in end-to-end manners. This enables us to further calculate various understandable motion features and objective indicators to help clinicians in their assessment process. This understandable information increases the clinicians’ trust in the AI techniques and enables the fusing of AI’s knowledge and human knowledge together in the clinical assessment process. Notice that item 3.1 (speech) involves audio data, which has been successfully validated in our previous research [34], while item 3.3 (rigidity) requires tactile assessment of major joints, which can be supplemented by pressure sensors and will be solved in our future work. In general, the accurate extraction of joint motion trajectories and the proposed explainable objective indicators provide guarantees for the feasibility of clinical auxiliary assessment.

To demonstrate the accuracy and feasibility of the proposed method, we collected video data of PD patients from public hospitals in various regions in China. These videos cover all vision-based items in the MDS-UPDRS motor examination. We designed a clinical trial in which a group of clinicians and our proposed AI system independently rate these Parkinson’s patients on various items. Our automated assessment method achieved accuracies of 97.2%, 90.1%, 96.6%, and 96.2% for the masked face, bradykinesia, postural instability, and tremor symptoms, respectively. Compared to the clinician’s assessment, our automatic assessment system improved by 5.8%, 22.2%, 8.5%, and 16.0% for the four symptoms. Additionally, we recorded the clinicians’ new ratings after they referred to the objective indicators provided by our auxiliary system. The correction rates of our clinical support system for clinicians in these four symptoms were 15.1%, 8.0%, 36.9%, and 80.1%, respectively. From the average performance of the 16 items, the overall accuracy increased from 78.7% to 85.3%. The results show that our automatic assessment system surpasses the average accuracy of clinicians in grading symptoms. The proposed objective indicators also reduce the rating errors among clinicians.

Our vision-based system is entirely non-contact, eliminating the need for patients to visit hospitals for in-person consultations while maintaining high-quality comprehensive healthcare. The system not only expands access for patients constrained by location or movement, but also conserves critical medical resources. Additionally, the solution decreases diagnostic costs for patients and enables higher-frequency monitoring of disease progression. This is particularly vital for developing nations and regions, where traditional healthcare access remains challenging.

Results

Patient population and data collection

With informed consent from patients and ethics committee approval, we collected data from patients with Parkinson’s disease. The data was gathered from the neurology departments of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (in northern China), Southeast University Zhongda Hospital and Nanjing Lishui People’s Hospital (both in southern China), between December 2021 and March 2024. The dataset contained patient videos for the MDS-UPDRS Part III (motor examination) assessments and the corresponding gold standard (ground truth) assessment results. The videos were recorded using smartphone cameras at 30 frames per second with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, which is a normal configuration for smartphones. We collected a total of 1731 videos of 112 patients, including 66 men and 46 women, aged between 47 and 71. During data collection, patients typically recorded one video per assessment item at each clinical visit. For bilateral items (e.g., item 3.4 for finger tapping, item 3.7 for toe tapping, and item 3.8 for leg agility), valid recordings for the left and right sides were processed as separate video samples. Some patients completed two clinical assessments, once before and once after surgery. As detailed in Methods, the system trains an independent classifier for each assessment item that involves machine learning. The data splitting was therefore performed at the patient level within each item. Specifically, for every item that involves machine learning, all videos of a given patient for that item were assigned exclusively to either the training set or the test set. For the tremor-related items (3.15, 3.16, 3.17, and 3.18), the severity is scored by rule-based MDS-UPDRS amplitude thresholds without machine learning training. The detailed numbers of training and test videos for each item are shown in Table 1. To evaluate the accuracy of clinicians or our system in assessing the severity of patients’ symptoms, a gold standard was required for all the collected videos. For the tremor-related items (3.15–3.18) in MDS-UPDRS Part III, according to the definitions, the gold standard was associated with tremor amplitude recorded by optical markers placed on bodies. For the other items (3.2, 3.4–3.14), five trained and certified neurologists are asked to conduct the evaluations, and we used majority vote to determine the final assessment results as the gold standard. The items were categorized as “Normal” (score 0), “Slight” (score 1), “Mild” (score 2), “Moderate” (score 3), or “Severe” (score 4) according to the MDS-UPDRS. These videos and the corresponding assessment results were used in both model development and evaluation.

Automatic assessment system

The system can evaluate 16 vision-based items in the MDS_UPDRS motor examination, which are classified into four symptoms: masked face, bradykinesia, postural instability, and tremor. The system utilized the collected videos (totally 1731 videos from 112 patients) for both training and verification. Following the patient-level splitting protocol described above, 1107 videos were used for training and 624 for testing. We used four widely accepted metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score) to comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of our automatic assessment system for Parkinson’s disease across multiple symptoms. The results, presented in Table 2, demonstrate considerable promise for the automatic assessment with high performance across multiple symptoms. In the following, we analyze the assessment results in detail.

Comprehensive PD symptoms automatic scoring results

As defined in the MDS-UPDRS [9], for the masked face symptom (item 3.2), it is required to observe patients’ blinking, masked face or loss of facial expressions, spontaneous smiling and lip parting. Our system rates the severity of these symptoms by extracting the patient’s facial expressions, lip-parting duration, and eye-blink frequency from the video. There are 98 videos (in the total of 1731 videos) recorded for evaluating this symptom. We use 55 of them for training and 43 for tests. As shown in Table 2, the system achieved overall accuracy of 97.2%, indicating that it can accurately identify different scores in Parkinson’s assessment, has a low prediction error rate, and maintains a good balance between precision and recall, demonstrating strong robustness. The high performance across all severity level scores further demonstrates the system’s robustness in both detecting the presence of symptoms and distinguishing between different levels of severity.

For the bradykinesia symptom (items 3.4–3.8 and 3.14), the MDS-UPDRS requires observing the speed, amplitude, hesitations, halts, and decrementing amplitude of the patient’s hands and feet movements. Our system extracts the amplitude, frequency, velocity, and acceleration of the key movement parts of the patient in the video to rate the severity of symptoms. The dataset includes 1169 videos (952 for training and 217 for test) for this symptom. As shown in Table 2, the system maintained an overall accuracy of 90.1%. While this is lower than the performance for the masked face symptom, it is expected due to the highly subjective nature of bradykinesia. Nevertheless, the system is still able to discern fine variations in motor control and speed. Notably, the accuracy for “Severe” level (score 4) reaches 94.1%, indicating that the system is particularly adept at identifying more pronounced motor impairments.

For evaluating the postural instability symptom (items 3.9–3.13), it is required to observe the patient’s posture, fluency, stability, and ability to complete the item independently during standing and walking. The system extracts item-related objective indicators such as gait frequency, step width, and standing time to rate the severity of symptoms. The dataset provides 165 training videos and 72 test videos for this symptom. The overall accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score of the system reached 96.6%, 94.7%, 95.6%, and 95.1%, respectively, demonstrating excellent performance across all scores, especially for higher severity score categories. Specifically, the system achieved 100% accuracy for “Severe” level (score 4) and 98.2% accuracy for “Moderate” level (score 3), indicating its strong capacity to identify significant motor impairments.

For the tremor symptom (items 3.15–3.18), we need to observe whether the patient exhibits tremors while remaining still or moving, as well as the duration of the tremors. Since computer vision techniques can estimate 3D body/hand joints and key points from videos directly, we do not need to use our video to do any training but directly use the techniques to extract the tremor amplitude and duration from the fingertip joints of both hands, the left and right knee joints, and the jaw point, and then calculate the severity based on these measurements according to the definition in the MDS-UPDRS. All the 337 test videos are used for testing, and as shown in Table 2, the overall accuracy of the system reached 96.2%. The high accuracy and precision demonstrate that the system is effective in distinguishing tremor levels.

To complement the existing accuracy-based evaluation by accounting for the ordinal nature of the severity scale, we computed Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) for each symptom category, as shown in Table 3. To better evaluate the system’s performance, we also computed the same metrics for the clinicians in the without-reference condition (detailed assessment protocol described in the Clinical Support System section below), with all values calculated against the gold standard. For the automated system, the QWK values ranged from 0.883 to 0.973, indicating almost perfect agreement on the ordinal severity scale. The MAE values ranged from 0.070 to 0.295, confirming that prediction errors predominantly occur between adjacent severity levels. In comparison, the clinicians obtained lower QWK values (0.698 to 0.859) and higher MAE values (0.112 to 0.434). Across all four symptom categories, the automated system consistently achieved higher QWK and lower MAE than the clinicians, highlighting its superior accuracy in severity categorization.

In summary, our vision-based automatic assessment system demonstrates high performance across all major Parkinson’s disease symptoms, with notable strengths in masked face, postural instability, and tremor symptoms.

Clinical support system

Given the high accuracy of our automatic assessment system, we further applied it in real clinical practice. Here, we verified the effectiveness of our defined motion features and our objective indicators in assisting clinicians. We invited 11 clinicians from three hospitals to rate 16 vision-based items from 616 videos according to the MDS-UPDRS guidelines. We conducted this evaluation twice with and without the system-provided auxiliary information. The interval between the two scoring sessions was four to six weeks to eliminate any influence from the previous scores. Notice that the order of the two settings is decided randomly to make a fair comparison. Table 4 shows the assessment accuracy for each of the 16 MDS-UPDRS items with and without auxiliary information. The average accuracy increases from 78.7 to 85.3%, which demonstrates that the inclusion of auxiliary information improves the clinicians’ assessment accuracy. By reducing human subjective error, the auxiliary information helps ensure the assessments are more reliable and contribute to the assessment process. Notably, certain symptom items, such as 3.12 and 3.15, already exhibited high accuracy (more than 90%) without auxiliary information. However, even in these cases, auxiliary information further optimized the assessment outcomes.

For the masked face symptom, the average assessment accuracy without auxiliary information was 91.4%. After introducing the auxiliary information, the average accuracy increased to 92.7%. This improvement was statistically significant (paired t-test, P = 3.9 × 10⁻⁴). As shown in Table 5, all clinicians showed improvements in accuracy after referring to the auxiliary information, with increases ranging from 0.9 to 3.7%. Notably, in the “score 0” group (representing no symptom), the accuracy of all clinicians increased to 100%, demonstrating that the auxiliary information provided by our system helps clinicians identify asymptomatic patients and those with mildly masked faces. For scores 1 and 2, the accuracy of clinicians improved by 3.4% and 2.3%, respectively, with the aid of our auxiliary information. This indicates that our clinical support system is useful for identifying minor facial expression changes that are less obvious in scores 1 and 2. Additionally, Table 7 shows that the standard deviation of the 11 clinicians’ ratings for this symptom decreased from 0.19 to 0.16, suggesting that the aided system improves assessment reliability by reducing the variability in assessments. As shown in Table 8, the ICC for masked face improved from 0.79 (95% CI [0.69, 0.87]) without auxiliary information to 0.86 (95% CI [0.79, 0.92]) with auxiliary information, indicating improved inter-rater reliability among clinicians.

Bradykinesia, a more complex motor symptom characterized by slowness and reduced amplitude of voluntary movements, showed an assessment accuracy of 67.9% without referring to our aided system. This accuracy increased to 70.5% after using our system. Statistical analysis confirmed the significance of this improvement (paired t-test, P = 3.4 × 10⁻⁴). As shown in Tables 5 and 10 out of 11 clinicians showed improvements in accuracy after using the auxiliary information. The standard deviation of accuracy across clinicians slightly increased from 3.1% to 3.4% as shown in Table 6, indicating that individual clinicians benefited differently from the auxiliary information. Meanwhile, the inter-rater consistency on individual cases improved substantially. As shown in Table 7, the standard deviation for bradykinesia without auxiliary information was 0.78, which reflects the variability in the initial ratings across clinicians. After using our system, the standard deviation decreased to 0.65, suggesting that the system not only improved the overall accuracy but also helped reduce inter-clinician variability. As shown in Table 8, the ICC for bradykinesia improved from 0.67 (95% CI [0.60, 0.73]) without auxiliary information to 0.69 (95% CI [0.63, 0.75]) with auxiliary information, reflecting a modest improvement in inter-rater reliability. It is worth noting that the rating descriptions of bradykinesia in the MDS-UPDRS are highly subjective. There is little distinction between adjacent scores, and descriptions such as “normal,” “mild hesitation,” and “moderate slowing” are often vague. This vagueness makes it difficult for clinicians to distinguish the boundaries between ratings. Although our automatic assessment system achieves an accuracy of 90.1% by utilizing the extracted motion features, when these motion features are provided to clinicians, they are still unable to distinguish the subjective-defined ratings. This also suggests the need to replace the subjective-defined ratings with objective-defined ones in the future to achieve more accurate and consistent evaluation of the disorders, since there are techniques to calculate objective values automatically.

Regarding the postural instability symptom, the average assessment accuracy was 88.1% without auxiliary information and improved to 92.5% with the information. This improvement of 4.4% points was statistically significant (paired t-test, P = 1.1 × 10⁻³). As shown in Table 5 and 9 out of 11 clinicians showed improvements in accuracy, while the remaining 2 maintained their original high accuracy. In addition, the inter-rater consistency improved as the average standard deviation of ratings for patients decreased from 0.55 to 0.39. As shown in Table 8, the ICC for postural instability improved from 0.87 (95% CI [0.82, 0.90]) without auxiliary information to 0.97 (95% CI [0.96, 0.98]) with auxiliary information, indicating near-perfect inter-rater reliability among clinicians. This improvement shows that the auxiliary information helps clinicians with different experience levels reach more consistent assessments.

For the tremor symptom, the average assessment accuracy without using our aided system was 80.2%. After incorporating the auxiliary information, this accuracy increased dramatically to 96.2%. This improvement of 16.0% points was highly significant (paired t-test, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁶). As shown in Table 5, the accuracy improvements for the tremor symptom were especially pronounced at the “Slight” (score 1) severity and “Mild” (score 2) severity levels, with increases of 18.8% and 21.2%, respectively. This indicates that our aided system was particularly effective in rating subtle tremors, where manual observation often falls short due to their low amplitude. The standard deviation was 0.72 without the auxiliary information, indicating considerable variability across clinicians. However, as the accuracy of our auxiliary information was recognized by all clinicians and they chose to trust it in their assessment, the standard deviation dropped to 0. This high degree of consistency strongly supports the system’s ability to standardize tremor assessments, ensuring that all clinicians reach the same conclusions, regardless of their individual experience levels. As shown in Table 8, the ICC for this symptom increased from 0.76 (95% CI [0.66, 0.84]) to 1.00, as all clinicians adopted identical ratings based on the system-provided tremor amplitude measurements.

In summary, the auxiliary information significantly improved assessment accuracy for all four symptom categories (P < 0.05) and reduced inter-clinician variability. These results provide strong evidence for incorporating auxiliary information into clinical practice, especially for subtle symptoms. The reduction in human error and the improvement in accuracy demonstrate the value of using advanced auxiliary information to support clinicians, making the assessment process more robust and reliable.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a new vision-based automatic assessment and clinical support system for motor examination in Parkinson’s disease (PD). Leveraging artificial intelligence and computer vision, the system scores all 16 vision-based items in the MDS-UPDRS motor examination, including symptoms such as masked face, bradykinesia, postural instability, and tremor. It also extracts the patient’s motion trajectories and objective indicators (covering the face, body, hands, and feet) to assist clinicians in assessment. A comparison in clinical assessments demonstrated that our system not only achieves more accurate assessments than clinicians but also provides the auxiliary information to help clinicians effectively correct some erroneous clinician scores. This new technology enables accurate and comprehensive online evaluation, making the tracking of Parkinson’s disease more routine and providing patients with detailed insights into their disease progression, and patients with limited mobility or those in underserved areas with a shortage of neurologists will greatly benefit from our method.

In addition to vision-based systems, wearable sensors such as gyroscopes and accelerometers [38–43] are also used to quantify joint motion trajectories and pressure changes. However, these techniques have several limitations: (1) Most wearable sensors require direct contact with the patient’s body, which can cause discomfort and slightly impede movement. (2) Different motor examinations often require additional specialized hardware, increasing both inconvenience and cost. For example, accelerometers are used to monitor tremors, pressure insoles assess gait stability, and electromyography helps assess muscle stiffness and bradykinesia. (3) Existing wearable sensors are typically worn in easily accessible locations, like the wrists or waist, limiting their ability to record necessary movements from other body parts, such as the face, toes, etc. In contrast, vision-based systems only require a standard mobile device with a camera, such as a smartphone or a tablet, which is widely available and eliminates the need for additional purchase. Furthermore, vision-based systems do not require physical contact with the patient, do not impede movement, and can capture desired motion trajectories from different view directions.

The high performance of our system is driven by four key factors. First, for human keypoint detection, we conducted extensive experiments on the face, body, hands, and feet, to select the most accurate algorithm for each body part. Second, based on the accurate human keypoint detection, we designed a deep learning-based motion trajectory refinement network to eliminate abnormal jitters while preserving the patient’s natural movement. Third, we developed stable, clinically interpretable objective indicators tailored to different items of the MDS-UPDRS. Finally, in the classification phase, we employed a hybrid approach combining the objective indicators and machine learning algorithms. For those objective ratings in MDS-UPDRS, we performed direct classification using the MDS-UPDRS-defined indicators. For those subjective ratings in MDS-UPDRS, we conducted thorough experiments to implement and identify the most suitable machine learning models.

In assessing Parkinson’s disease motor symptoms, scores for symptoms such as masked face, bradykinesia, postural instability, and tremor often manifest as subtle differences that are difficult to accurately assess using visual or traditional examination methods. These slight variations in symptoms pose challenges to clinical judgment, particularly in patients with mild or borderline manifestations. While traditional deep learning methods can extract features that are difficult for humans to detect, their “black box” nature limits clinicians’ understanding and trust. In contrast, our approach uses deep learning to extract interpretable and quantifiable features that are understandable to humans, rather than relying solely on end-to-end classification. This enhances clinicians’ trust in the technology and facilitates a more seamless integration of deep learning insights with human clinical knowledge.

It is notable that, compared to other symptoms, bradykinesia shows lower accuracy in our automatic assessment system. This is due to the highly subjective nature of the rating descriptions for bradykinesia-related items (items 3.4–3.8, 3.14) in the MDS-UPDRS. The descriptions for different scores are often vague, such as “normal,” “mild hesitation,” and “moderate slowing of movements.” The subjectivity affects the classifier’s judgment and presents a significant challenge for clinicians. In this case, even our objective indicators cannot contribute too much for the clinical assessment. So, more objective ratings are expected for these symptoms. In contrast, for tremor symptoms, unlike previous work, we did not use machine learning methods to directly produce the final rating. Instead, we proposed a more interpretable method by calculating tremor amplitude to determine the final rating. This approach not only helps clinicians improve assessment stability but also increases their confidence and reduces assessment difficulty.

In addition to the automatic motor assessment and auxiliary clinical support for Parkinson’s disease, our vision-based system can also be extended to other neurological movement disorders, such as Essential Tremor (ET) and stroke, which share similar evaluation scales and also require quantitative analysis of the patient’s movement data. While the specific movements and observed body parts may vary, our system provides a robust technical foundation that is applicable to a wide range of neurological movement disorders.

Methods

Overview

In this study, we propose an automated Parkinson’s disease (PD) assessment method and develop a clinical support system to assist clinicians in assessment, as shown in Fig. 1a. This system can estimate ratings for all 16 vision-based items in the MDS-UPDRS motor examination, including 1 masked face item, 6 bradykinesia items, 5 postural instability items, and 4 tremor items, involving observations of the face, body, hands, and feet. Our AI system first detects the coordinates of keypoints on different body-parts in each video frame. It introduces a Motion Trajectory Refinement Network (MTR-Net) to eliminate the abnormal jitter caused by the original keypoints detection algorithm and estimate the kinematic features accurately. The system then extracts a series of clinically explainable objective indicators that are consistent with MDS-UPDRS rating criteria for different tasks. These objective indicators are provided to the classifier for rating or to clinicians for auxiliary assessment.

Subjects and protocol

The vision-based items in MDS-UPDRS motor examination are as follows: 3.2 (facial expression), 3.4 (finger tapping), 3.5 (hand movements), 3.6 (pronation-supination movements of hands), 3.7 (toe tapping), 3.8 (leg agility), 3.9 (arising from chair), 3.10 (gait), 3.11 (freezing of gait), 3.12 (postural stability), 3.13 (posture), 3.14 (global spontaneity of movement or body bradykinesia), 3.15 (postural tremor of the hands), 3.16 (kinetic tremor of the hands), 3.17 (rest tremor amplitude), and 3.18 (constancy of rest tremor). It is worth noting that item 3.1 (speech) involves audio data, which has been successfully validated in our previous research. Item 3.3 (rigidity) requires tactile assessment of major joints, which can be supplemented with pressure sensors. To ensure system stability, operators are advised to use a tripod and maintain ambient lighting as the primary light source. It is also essential to keep the patient fully visible within the video frame. During recording, items 3.2, 3.4, 3.5, 3.6, 3.15, and 3.16 focus on the upper body, while the other items require full-body shots.

Keypoints trajectory refinement

While keypoint detection methods show high accuracy for single-frame PD patient image, they encounter significant challenges when applied to video data. These challenges include abnormal jitter, which fails to accurately represent actual patient movements and compromises the extraction of precise motion data. Simply enhancing pose estimator accuracy is insufficient to resolve the issue of jitters in skeleton sequences during video predictions.

Low-pass filters, such as exponential moving average filters and Kalman filters, are widely used for data smoothing. However, these filters require careful parameter tuning to balance noise reduction and signal preservation. To mitigate manual tuning and improve temporal consistency, learning-based methods such as SmoothNet [47] have been proposed, which leverage data-driven temporal modeling to refine pose sequences. Nevertheless, both traditional filters and learning-based approaches still struggle to differentiate between jitter caused by algorithmic errors and tremors inherent to Parkinson’s disease patients. Applying stronger smoothing to suppress jitter may also mask important PD symptoms that should be detected.

To effectively distinguish between algorithmic jitters and patient tremors, and to reconstruct true motion symptom trajectories, we propose the Motion Trajectory Refinement Network (MTR-Net), as shown in Fig. 1b. The MTR-Net extracts position, velocity, acceleration, and jerk from skeleton trajectories and integrates these features into four parallel inference branches. It is worth noting that PD patients often experience bradykinesia (slowness of movement) and tremors, both of which can be captured by the jerk component in motion analysis. Jerk represents the rate of change of acceleration and can provide insights into the smoothness of movements. Each branch of the MTR-Net follows a similar structure, consisting of Fully Connected (FC) layers and multiple Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) in series. Then, the features from these branches are then fused to output the optimized joint motion trajectories. Whole-body motion feature extraction

After obtaining the locations of keypoints across the whole body of patients, the system extracts key motor features and objective indicators for each MDS-UPDRS item. These features are used for automatic scoring and provide clinicians with valuable auxiliary information to assist in assessment, reducing subjectivity.

For UPDRS item 3.2 (facial expression), the system evaluates symptoms such as reduced eye-blink frequency, lip-parting, and a masked face. It extracts motion features, including eye-blink frequency, lip-parting duration, as well as facial Action Units (AU) to serve as objective indicators. These indicators help clinicians in assessment and monitoring, facilitating more precise treatment and management plans.

For UPDRS item 3.18 (constancy of rest tremor), the system assesses the duration of the patient’s rest tremor. The system recorded the duration of any tremors in items 3.15 and 3.17 and calculated its proportion to the inspection time.

Motor function score

The classification system generates integer assessment scores for each item based on the MDS-UPDRS motion examination scoring criteria. The system uses a coexistence mode of objective indicators and machine learning algorithms. For highly objective ratings, the system directly applies objective indicators for classification. For more subjective ratings, extensive experimentation was conducted to select the most suitable machine learning model.

For UPDRS item 3.2 (facial expression), based on experimental observations, if the objective indicator “lip-parting distance” exceeds 0.5 cm, the patient is rated within {3, 4} (open mouth). If not, the rating falls within {0, 1, 2}. For ratings within {3, 4}, if the “lip-parting duration” exceeds half the observation time, the rating is 4; otherwise, it is 3. For ratings within {0, 1, 2}, the “eye-blink frequency” and extracted facial action unit features are input into a Logistic Regression (LR) classifier to distinguish between ratings 0, 1, and 2.

For UPDRS items 3.4 (finger tapping), 3.5 (hand movements), 3.6 (pronation-supination movements of hands), 3.7 (toe tapping), and 3.8 (leg agility), the system inputs the amplitude, velocity, acceleration, and frequency of the specially designed motion parts (Euclidean distance between the tip of index finger and thumb for item 3.4, the average Euclidean distance between the five fingertip joints and the wrist joint for item 3.5, the distance in the horizontal coordinates of the metacarpophalangeal joints between the little finger and the thumb for item 3.6, the vertical coordinates of the toe joint, and the vertical coordinates of the ankle joint for item 3.7.) into a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) for classification, respectively.

For UPDRS item 3.9 (arising from a chair), the model first assesses whether the patient used the chair arms during the stand-up process based on the objective indicator ‘ratio of wrist to shoulder distance and shoulder width’ to classify the rating into {0, 1} or {2, 3, 4}. For ratings within {0, 1}, objective indicators ‘stand-up duration, ‘number of attempts’, and ‘forward leaning degree’ are input into a Support Vector Machine (SVM) for binary classification. For ratings within {2, 3, 4}, the SVM performs three-class classification.

For UPDRS item 3.10 (gait), ratings 3 (needs assistance with a device) and 4 (needs assistance from another person) are accurately judged by clinicians, so the classification model focuses on distinguishing ratings 0–2. Given the limited variation in the ‘gait frequency’ objective indicator, which is relatively unaffected by patient height and age, the model uses ‘gait frequency’ as input and a Random Forest model for classification. The same approach is used for UPDRS item 3.11 (freezing of gait).

For UPDRS item 3.12 (postural stability), the model first uses the objective indicator “distance between patient and clinician” to determine whether assistance is required, classifying ratings into {0, 1, 2} or {3, 4}. If the final distance exceeds the initial distance, the rating is within {0, 1, 2}; otherwise, it is within {3, 4}. For ratings within {0, 1, 2}, the final rating is based on the objective indicator ‘alternating steps’ with less than 3 steps for rating 0, 3 to 5 steps for rating 1, and more than 5 steps for rating 2. For ratings within {3, 4}, if alternating steps are present, the rating is 3; otherwise, it is 4.

For UPDRS item 3.13 (posture), the system inputs spinal curvature angles during standing and walking into a Random Forest model for classification.

For UPDRS item 3.14 (global spontaneity of movement or body bradykinesia), the system inputs the amplitude, velocity, acceleration, and frequency of the specially designed motion parts (e.g., Euclidean distance between the tip of index finger and thumb for item 3.4, the average Euclidean distance between the five fingertip joints and the wrist joint for item 3.5, the distance in the horizontal coordinates of the metacarpophalangeal joints between the little finger and the thumb for item 3.6, Euclidean distance between the head joint and feet joints for item 3.9, step width for item 3.10.) into a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) for classification, respectively.

For UPDRS item 3.15 (postural tremor of the hands), 3.16 (kinetic tremor of the hands), and 3.17 (rest tremor amplitude), the Tremor Amplitude (TA) obtained from the model is used for rating. For facial tremors, if TA = 0 cm, the rating is 0; if 0 < TA < 1 cm, the rating is 1; if 1 ≤ TA < 2 cm, the rating is 2; if 2 ≤ TA < 3 cm, the rating is 3; and if TA ≥ 3 cm, the rating is 4. For other tremors, if TA = 0 cm, the rating is 0; if 0 < TA < 1 cm, the rating is 1; if 1 ≤ TA < 3 cm, the rating is 2; if 3 ≤ TA < 10 cm, the rating is 3; and if TA ≥ 10 cm, the rating is 4.

For UPDRS item 3.18 (constancy of rest tremor), the Tremor Time Ratio (TTR) obtained from the model is used for rating. If TTR = 0%, the rating is 0; if 0 < TTR ≤ 25%, the rating is 1; if 25 < TTR ≤ 50%, the rating is 2; if 50 < TTR ≤ 75%, the rating is 3; and if TTR > 75%, the rating is 4.

These motor function scoring methods ensure that each UPDRS item is evaluated with tailored criteria, leveraging both objective indicators and advanced machine learning techniques to provide accurate and clinically relevant assessments of Parkinson’s disease symptoms.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.9. Paired two-tailed t-tests were used to compare the assessment accuracy of 11 clinicians (n = 11) before and after using auxiliary information for each of the four symptom categories (masked face, bradykinesia, postural instability, and tremor). The significance level was set at \alpha = 0.05, and exact P values are reported. Descriptive statistics including mean and standard deviation (s.d.) were calculated for each comparison.

Conclusion

This study presents an AI-based and explainable video assessment system for Parkinson’s disease, successfully evaluating all 16 vision-based MDS-UPDRS items with accuracies of 90.1–97.2% across symptom categories. Clinical validation demonstrated significant improvements in assessment accuracy, with clinician correction rates of 8.0–80.1%. The system also provides motion features that directly correspond to MDS-UPDRS rating criteria, which enhances trust and enables consistent assessments. This non-contact approach improves healthcare accessibility in underserved regions, reduces costs, and enables frequent remote monitoring, demonstrating considerable potential for advancing Parkinson’s disease management.
