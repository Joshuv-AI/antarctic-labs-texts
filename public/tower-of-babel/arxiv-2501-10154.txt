Complexity of the Virtual Network Embedding with uniform demands
Amal Benhamiche, Pierre Fouilhoux, Lucas Létocart, Nancy Perrot, Alexis Schneider

Abstract
We study the complexity of the Virtual Network Embedding Problem (VNE), which is the combinatorial core of several telecommunication problems related to the implementation of virtualization technologies, such as Network Slicing. VNE is to find an optimal assignment of virtual demands to physical resources, encompassing simultaneous placement and routing decisions. The problem is known to be strongly NP-hard, even when the virtual network is a uniform path, but is polynomial in some practical cases. This article aims to draw a cohesive frontier between easy and hard instances for VNE. For this purpose, we consider uniform demands to focus on structural aspects, rather than packing ones. To this end, specific topologies are studied for both virtual and physical networks that arise in practice, such as trees, cycles, wheels and cliques. Some polynomial greedy or dynamic programming algorithms are proposed, when the physical network is a tree or a cycle, whereas other close cases are shown NP-hard. Keywords: Virtual Network Embedding, Complexity, Graph topologies, Dynamic Programming, Polynomial cases

Proof.

(u,v)
hosts no virtual edge.

Let
W^{*}(u,k)
denote the minimum mapping costs induced by the utilization of subtree
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u)
, when
k
consecutive nodes are placed on it. It includes the (placement and routing) costs of using all the substrate (node and edge) resources in the subtree.
Note that this mapping cost is the same for any
k
consecutive nodes considered. Indeed, given a mapping that places nodes
\overline{u}_{i},\overline{u}_{i+1},\ldots,\overline{u}_{i+k-1}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n_{r}\}
, on
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u)
, then a mapping of same restricted cost, and overall cost, that places
\overline{u}_{j},\overline{u}_{j+1},\ldots,\overline{u}_{j+k-1}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{r}\}
, on
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u)
can be obtained simply by shifting the indices of the virtual node in the placement by
j-i
.
This minimum restricted cost is denoted
W^{*}(u,k)
.
If the capacities are insufficient, the cost is again set to
+\infty
.

If
u
is a leaf node, clearly
W^{*}(u,k)=k*w(u)
(if
k\leq c(u)
). Otherwise,
u
has a children
u_{a}
and
u_{b}
, the following recurrence formula holds:

W^{*}(u,k)=\min_{l_{a}+l_{b}=k}W^{*}(u_{a},l_{a})+W_{E}(u_{a},u,l_{a})+W^{*}(u_{b},l_{b})+W_{E}(u_{b},u,l_{b})

(1)

Indeed, consider the mapping
m=(m_{V},m_{E})
that has minimum restricted costs
W^{*}(u,k)
on
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u)
. Let us denote
l_{a}
(resp.
l_{b}
) the number of virtual nodes placed on
\mathcal{T}(u_{a})
(resp.
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u_{b})
). Recall that only leaf nodes have capacity, thus
u
host no nodes and
l_{b}=k-l_{a}
. We can consider, from Lemma
10
, that both these set of nodes are consecutive. Moreover, the mapping of the
l_{a}
(resp.
l_{b}
) consecutive nodes placed on
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u_{a})
(resp.
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u_{b})
) must have minimum restricted costs on
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u_{a})
(resp.
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u_{b})
). Otherwise, there is another mapping
m^{\prime}=(m^{\prime}_{V},m^{\prime}_{E})
with better restricted costs for
l_{a}
nodes on
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u_{a})
. Then, we could construct another mapping that is
m
, except for the placement on
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u_{a})
, that is from
m^{\prime}
. That mapping would then have a better restricted cost over
\mathcal{T}_{s}(u)
, which is a contradiction.

Theorem 11
.

C-
\langle\mathcal{C}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{s}\rangle
is in
\mathsf{P}
and is solvable in
\mathcal{O}({n_{r}}^{2}n_{s})

Proof.

Consider the following leaf-to-root algorithm. First, the values of
W^{*}
are computed for each leaf node, which takes
\mathcal{O}(n_{r}n_{s})
. Then, for a non-leaf node, the values of
W^{*}
are computed in
\mathcal{O}({n_{r}}^{2})
, using formula
1
. When
W^{*}(u_{r},n_{r})
is computed, a solution is found. From Lemma
10
, this solution is optimal. Since the root node can only be visited by the dynamic algorithm at the last iteration, the overall computational time is
\mathcal{O}({n_{r}}^{2}n_{s})
.
∎

Virtual path

The property given in Lemma

Theorem 12
.

C-
\langle\mathcal{P}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{s}\rangle
is in
\mathsf{P}
, and is solvable in
\mathcal{O}({n_{r}}^{2}n_{s})

10
holds for the outer cycle of a wheel, and we can construct by dynamic programming a minimum cost feasible mapping so that each subtree contains consecutive nodes of the outer cycle.

However, in a given mapping where
k
(consecutive) nodes of the outer cycle are placed on a subtree

Theorem 13
.

C-
\langle\mathcal{W}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{s}\rangle

\mathsf{P}
, and is solvable in
\mathcal{O}({n_{r}}^{2}n_{s})

Theorem 14

4.3
Virtual tree

Recall that C-
\langle\mathcal{S}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{s}\rangle

[
20
]
and C-
\langle\mathcal{P}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{s}\rangle
(as a corollary of Theorem
12
) are in
\mathsf{P}
. We will prove the opposite result for virtual trees. Consider the following problem.

Bin Packing Problem
(
BPP
)

Input:

A set of
n
integers
A=a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
, two integers
K
(the number of bins) and
B
(the size of each bin)

Question:

Is there a partition of
A
into
K
disjoint sets
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{K}
, such that, for each subset
A_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,
\sum_{a_{l}\in A_{i}}a_{l}\leq B
?

In what follows, we reduce
BPP
, which is
\mathcal{NP}
-hard
[
14
]
, to E-
uniVNE
.
This reduction use similar gadgets that those used in proofs done by
[
18
]
, where
BPP
is also reduced to a
VNE
case with a virtual tree (more specifically a
tied-star
), and a substrate tree. However, their work consider that edge demands are non-uniform, and that
n_{r}=n_{s}
. Thus it needs to be adapted.

Theorem 15
.

E-
\langle\mathcal{T}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{s}\rangle
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.

Proof.

Consider a
BPP
instance
(A,K,B)
with
K\geq 3
. We assume w.l.o.g. that
\sum_{a_{l}\in A}a_{l}=K*B
, as adding some elements of unit weight to
A
in any instance of
BPP
makes it true.
In this proof, we will use a gadget which is a star
\mathcal{S}(k)
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, of
k
leafs.
An instance of E-
uniVNE
can be constructed as follows.
The virtual graph is a tree
\mathcal{T}_{r}
, that we construct starting from a single node, the root
\overline{u}_{r}
.
For each element
l\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the center of a star gadget
\mathcal{S}^{l}_{r}=\mathcal{S}(a_{l})
is connected to
\overline{u}_{r}
.
Furthermore,
K*B-|A|

singleton nodes
are connected to
\overline{u}_{r}
.
The substrate graph is the star
\mathcal{S}_{s}=\mathcal{S}(K)
. The center of
\mathcal{S}_{s}
is denoted
u_{c}
and has a unit capacity. The leafs of
\mathcal{S}_{s}
, denoted
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{K}
, have
2*B
capacity, and the edges
(u_{i},u_{c})
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, have a
B
.

We now show that there exists a solution to the
BPP
instance if and only if there exists a feasible mapping of
\mathcal{T}_{r}
on
\mathcal{S}_{s}
:

(\Rightarrow)
: Consider a solution
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}
of the
BPP
instance.
Consider the following mapping
m=(m_{V},m_{E})
:
\overline{u}_{r}
is placed on
u_{c}
; all the nodes of
\mathcal{S}^{l}_{r}
, for
a_{l}\in A_{i}
, are placed on
u_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
. Furthermore
B-|A_{i}|
singleton nodes are placed on
u_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
. The routing of a virtual edge is the unique path between the two substrate nodes hosting the two virtual nodes connected by the edge.
This mapping is feasible, as
u_{c}
hosts exactly one node, each substrate leaf hosts exactly
2B
virtual nodes, and each substrate edge hosts
B
virtual edges.

(\Leftarrow)
: Suppose there is a feasible mapping
m=(m_{V},m_{E})
of
\mathcal{T}_{r}
on
\mathcal{S}_{s}
.

In a feasible mapping, only
u_{c}
can host
\overline{u}_{r}
. Indeed, a leaf
u_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, can only host a virtual node with at most
3B-1
children, as
c(u_{i})=2B
and
c(u_{i},u_{c})=B
. However
\overline{u}_{r}
has
KB\geq 3B
children, thus it can only be placed on
u_{c}
.

Furthermore, each leaf
u_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, hosts exactly
B
children of
\overline{u}_{r}
. Indeed,
\overline{u}_{r}
, which is placed on
u_{c}
, has
K*B
children and
\sum_{i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}c(u_{i},u_{c})=KB
.
This indicates that, if the center of
\mathcal{S}^{l}_{r}
, for
l\in A
, is placed on
u_{i}
, the leafs of
\mathcal{S}^{l}_{r}
are also placed on
u_{i}
, since there remains no capacity on edge
(u_{i},u_{c})
.
There remains, on
u_{i}
, a capacity of
B
for those leafs. Thus the partition of
A
into
A_{i}
, where
A_{i}
are the elements whose stars are placed on
u_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, is a valid solution for the
BPP
instance.
∎

This shows that, even in graphs with uniform demands, packing aspects remain important for
VNE
problems, because of nodes connectivity in the virtual graph.

The previous proof can be modified to show the result in the case where
n_{r}\leq n_{s}
for a general substrate tree. Given an instance of
BPP
, the instance of
VNE
constructed has the same virtual tree, but the substrate graph is a tree
\mathcal{T}_{s}
, constructed from a root node
u_{r}
. For each of the
K
bins, the center of a star
\mathcal{S}(B-1)
is connected to
u_{r}
. All nodes have a unit capacity, and all edges have a capacity of

. The rest of the proof is analogous to the above one.

E-
\langle\mathcal{T}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}_{s}\rangle

\mathsf{NP}

However, the reduction technique does not work for a substrate star when

4.4
General virtual graph on a substrate path

For a substrate path, the polynomial results obtained above holds, but not the
\mathsf{NP}
-completness proof. However a reduction can be done using the following problem, which is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete for planar and series-parallel graphs
[

]

Minimum Cut Linear Arrangement
(
MCLA
)

Input:

An undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, a positive integer
K

Question:

Is there a linear ordering of
\mathcal{G}
, i.e. a one-to-one function
f:V\rightarrow\{1,2,\ldots,|V|\}
such that, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,|V|-1\}

|\{(\overline{u},\overline{v})\in E:f(\overline{u})\leq i<f(\overline{v})\}|\leq K
?

Theorem 18
.

E-
\langle\mathcal{G}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{s}\rangle
is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete

Proof.

Consider a
MCLA
instance
(\mathcal{G},K)
. An instance of E-
uniVNE
can be constructed as follows. The virtual graph is
\mathcal{G}
. The substrate graph
\mathcal{P}_{r}
is a path with
|V|
nodes
u_{1},\ldots,u_{|V|}
. Every node has a capacity of one, and every edge has a capacity of
K
.

Let us show that there is a valid linear ordering of
\mathcal{G}
if and only if there is a feasible mapping of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{P}_{s}
.

(\Rightarrow)
: Suppose that there is a linear ordering
f:V\rightarrow\{1,2,\ldots,|V|\}
of
\mathcal{G}
. A mapping
m=(m_{V},m_{E})
can be constructed as follows:

m_{V}(\overline{u})=u_{f(\overline{u})}
, for
\overline{u}\in V
and
m_{E}(\overline{u},\overline{v})=p(u_{f(\overline{u})},u_{f(\overline{v})})
, for
(\overline{u},\overline{v})\in E
.

We now show that this mapping is feasible. Since every substrate node host exactly one virtual node,
m_{V}
is feasible. Note that a substrate edge
\{u_{i},u_{i+1}\}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,|V|-1|\}
, hosts an edge
\{\overline{u},\overline{v}\}
if and only if
f(\overline{u})\leq i
and
f(\overline{v})\geq i+1
. Hence the edge
\{u_{i},u_{i+1}\}
hosts

|(\overline{u},\overline{v})\in E:\{u_{i},u_{i+1}\}\in m_{E}(\overline{u},\overline{v})|=|\{(\overline{u},\overline{v})\in E:f(\overline{u})\leq i<f(\overline{v})\}|\leq K
virtual edges. Thus

is feasible.

(\Leftarrow)
: Suppose that there is a feasible mapping
m=(m_{V},m_{E})
of
\mathcal{G}_{r}
on
\mathcal{P}_{s}
. Consider the following linear ordering
f:V\rightarrow\{1,2,\ldots,|V|\}
where
f(\overline{u})
is the index of the substrate node that hosts
\overline{u}
.
Since the substrate nodes have unit capacities,
f
is a one-to-one function. Moreover, with the same argument on edge capacity as above,
f
is a valid linear ordering.
∎

\mathcal{G}
is a tree,

\mathsf{P}

[

Substrate cycle

Unlike to a tree, in a substrate cycle two paths connect two substrate nodes
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
. Hence the algorithm for tree does not adapt for this case.
In what follows, the path
u_{i},u_{i+1},\ldots,u_{j-1},u_{j}
(reps.
u_{i},u_{i-1},\ldots,u_{j+1},u_{j}
) is said to be the
clockwise
(resp. counterclockwise) path from
u_{i}
to
u_{j}
, and is denoted
p^{+}(u_{i},u_{j})
(resp.
p^{-}(u_{i},u_{j})
).

5.1
Virtual path and cycle

As a cycle is a Hamiltonian graph, from Proposition
4
, we have that E-
\langle\mathcal{P}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{s}\rangle
and E-
\langle\mathcal{C}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{s}\rangle
are in
\mathsf{P}
.
We prove in the following that the cost variants is also in
\mathsf{P}
.

Virtual path

A mapping of a virtual path
\mathcal{P}_{r}
on a substrate cycle
\mathcal{C}_{s}
is said to be an
elementary path
if the concatenation of the routing of edges,
m_{E}(\overline{u}_{1},\overline{u}_{2})\cup m_{E}(\overline{u}_{2},\overline{u}_{3})\cup\ldots\cup m_{E}(\overline{u}_{n-1},\overline{u}_{n})
, is an elementary path of
\mathcal{C}_{s}
, i.e. substrate nodes appear at most once in it. This case happens only when substrate edges host at most one virtual edge and (at least) one substrate edge hosts none (i.e. the mapping does not go around the whole cycle).
The following lemma shows that elementary path mappings are the structure of optimal solutions.

Lemma 20
.

There is a minimum cost feasible mapping of
\mathcal{P}_{r}
on
\mathcal{C}_{s}
that is an elementary path.

Proof.

Let
m=(m_{V},m_{E})
be a feasible mapping of
\mathcal{P}_{r}
on
\mathcal{C}_{s}
that is not an elementary path. We will construct an elementary path mapping
m^{\prime}=(m^{\prime}_{V},m^{\prime}_{E})
from
m
of lower or equal cost, which will prove the lemma.

We introduce a procedure that constructs an elementary clockwise path mapping, given the imput parameters
u_{a}\in V_{s}
and
k:V_{s}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, such that, for
u_{i}\in V_{s}
,
k(u_{i})\leq c(u_{i})
.
The construction starts at a substrate node
u_{a}
where it places the first
k(u_{a})
nodes. It then moves in a clockwise manner on the cycle, and places on each node
u_{i}
the next
k(u_{i})
virtual nodes.
The routing of
(\overline{u}_{j},\overline{u}_{j+1})
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,n_{r}-1\}
, is set to the clockwise path between the placement of
\overline{u}_{j}
and that of
\overline{u}_{j+1}
(it is an empty path when they are placed on the same node). By construction, this mapping is an elementary path and is feasible.

We now show that the cost of
m^{\prime}
is lower or equal than the one of
m
. The mapping
m^{\prime}=(m^{\prime}_{V},m^{\prime}_{E})
is obtained with parameters
k(u_{i})
,
u_{i}\in V_{s}
set to the number of nodes hosted by
u_{i}
in
m
, while the choice of
u_{a}
depends on the configuration of
m
as follows. If
m
uses all substrate edges,
u_{a}
can be set to any nodes that hosts some virtual nodes, say
u_{1}
. Otherwise, there is a node
u_{j}\in V_{s}
such that, in
m
,
(u_{j-1},u_{j})
is not used, and
(u_{j},u_{j+1})
is used. We set
u_{a}=u_{j}
.

The mapping
m^{\prime}
has the same node placement cost than
m
, since both mapping place the same number of virtual nodes on any substrate nodes.
It can also be shown that a substrate edge in
m^{\prime}
hosts at most the number of edges that were hosted in
m
. Hence the routing cost (and hence the overall cost) of
m^{\prime}
is lower or equal than the one of
m
. ∎

The following lemma proposes a polynomial algorithm to construct an elementary path mapping.

Lemma 21
.

For
u_{s},u_{t}\in V_{s}
, a minimum cost feasible mapping that is a clockwise elementary path from
u_{s}
to
u_{t}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n_{s}\log(n_{s})+n_{r})

Proof.

We will use the procedure detailed in proof of Lemma
20
with
u_{a}
set to
u_{s}
, and the parameters
k(u_{i})
,
u_{i}\in V_{s}
, computed greedily as follows.
First, all
k(u_{i})
,
u_{i}\in V_{s}
, are set to zero, except for
k(u_{s})
and
k(u_{t})
which are set to one. Then, the nodes of
u_{s},\ldots,u_{t}
are sorted by increasing costs. Finally, substrate nodes are chosen greedily to host the remaining
n_{r}-2
virtual nodes. A unit is iteratively added to
k(u_{i})
where
u_{i}
is the cheapest node with some capacity left (i.e. such that
k(u_{i})<c(u_{i})
).
Overall, producing the mapping takes
\mathcal{O}(n_{r}+n_{s}log(n_{s}))
.

The mapping obtained has minimum cost among those that are clockwise elementary paths from
u_{s}
to
u_{t}
. Indeed, any mapping that is a clockwise elementary path from
u_{s}
to
u_{t}
has the same routing cost, using edges
(u_{s},u_{s+1}),\ldots,(u_{t-1},u_{t})
exactly once. However, the resulting mapping minimizes placement costs, as it uses, with respect to their capacities, the minimum cost substrate nodes of the subpath.
∎

Applying Lemma
21
, for any
u_{s},u_{t}\in V_{s}
, we obtain the final result:

Corollary 22
.

C-
\langle\mathcal{P}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{s}\rangle
is in
\mathcal{P}
and can be solved in
\mathcal{O}({n_{s}}^{2}(n_{s}\log(n_{s})+n_{r}))

For a virtual tree, Lemma
20
does not seem to be adaptable. The complexity of this case is an open problem.

Open Problem 23
.

Is C-
\langle\mathcal{T}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{s}\rangle

\mathsf{NP}
-complete ?

Virtual cycle

In the case of a virtual cycle
\mathcal{C}_{r}
, there is a minimum cost feasible mapping of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
on
\mathcal{C}_{s}
where the partial mapping of the virtual subpath
\overline{u}_{1},\ldots,\overline{u}_{n_{r}}
is a clockwise elementary path. To show this, one can consider a mapping
m
for which this is not true. The procedure of Lemma

is used with input parameters derived as previously from

to produce a partial mapping of the subpath
\overline{u}_{1},\ldots,\overline{u}_{n_{r}}
, which is then completed by routing
(\overline{u}_{1},\overline{u}_{n_{r}})
as in

. The mapping obtained has a lower or equal cost.

Similarly, for
u_{s},u_{t}\in V_{s}
, a minimum cost feasible mapping for which the partial mapping of
\overline{u}_{1},\ldots,\overline{u}_{n_{r}}
is an elementary path from

to

. Thus:

Corollary 24
.

C-
\langle\mathcal{C}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{s}\rangle
is in
\mathsf{P}
and can be solved in
\mathcal{O}({n_{s}}^{2}(n_{s}\log(n_{s})+n_{r}))

5.2
Virtual wheel

We have shown that C-
\langle\mathcal{C}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{s}\rangle
is in
\mathsf{P}
. It is known that C-
\langle\mathcal{S}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{s}\rangle
is in
\mathsf{P}

[
20
]
, through a reduction of the problem to a series of
n_{s}
integer flow problem, which is solvable in polynomial time
[
2
]
.
In a graph
G=(V,E)
, let
P_{s,t}
be the set of paths connecting
s,t\in V
. The problem can be defined as follows:

Minimum Cost Single Integer Flow Problem
(
SIFP
)

Input:

A graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, two nodes
s,t\in V
, capacities
c:E\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
and cost
w:E\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, integers
N,R\in\mathbb{N}

Question:

Is there a
N
-flow function
f:P_{s,t}\Rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that (a)
\sum_{p\in P_{s,t}}f(p)=N
, (b) for
e\in E
,
\sum_{p\in P_{s,t},e\in p}f(p)\leq c(e)
and
(c)

\sum_{p\in P_{s,t}}\sum_{e\in p}w(e)f(p)\leq R
?

In what follows, we will show that C-
\langle\mathcal{W}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{s}\rangle
can be solved in polynomial time too, by combining ideas from the two algorithms.

First, let us show that there is a minimum cost feasible mapping of

The algorithm is clearly impracticable. The simple fact that there is two paths between two nodes in a cycle makes the dynamic algorithm approach much more expensive than on a tree. Note that this approach can be further used for any virtual topology on a substrate cycle, but the algorithm in the general case becomes exponential. In some cases, such as paths or wheels, it might be adapted to be polynomial, but at the cost of high computation complexity. Hence the hardness of the problem in the general case remain an open problem.

Open Problem 30

Is E-

\mathsf{NP}

We have proposed a large study of the frontier between polynomial and
\mathsf{NP}
-hard cases of
uniVNE
. These complexity results give more insights about the computational challenges of
VNE
.
Since the considered network topologies correspond to real use-cases, our polynomial algorithms can be directly used in practice, particularly those on trees, such as in data-centers
[
5
]
.
They are directly linked to practical applications as the considered topologies correspond to networks encountered in practice.

The problems that are left opened raise several questions on intriguing cases, like E-
\langle\mathcal{W}_{r}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{s}\rangle
. Other topologies may also be of significant interest for further study, like star of stars (called 2-stars by
[
18
]
) or cactuses.

Another important direction is how the results evolve when several virtual graphs have to be mapped simultaneously on the substrate graph. Dynamic programming algorithms on substrate trees, for instance, might be extended for embedding several virtual paths.

Implementing real virtualized networks requires further dedicated optimization methods. Among possible approaches, Mathematical Programming has been shown to be efficient for solving network design problems
[
15
,
6
]
.
To this aim, finding polyhedral characterizations for the polynomial cases we have proposed could help to improve branch-and-cut approaches. Another perspective is to use decomposition schemes for large integer programs for which the pricing subproblems can be solved in polynomial time.
