Curcumin modulates targeted gut bacterial populations and NF-κB/NRF2 immune-redox responses in Eimeria-challenged broilers fed soybean or canola oil
Authors: Hussein Maytham Abdulhusein, Kamran Taherpour, Hossein Ali Ghasemi, Hassan Shirzadi, Fatemeh Tavakolinasab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAvian coccidiosis compromises growth performance and gut health in broiler chickens. We investigated whether the dietary fat source (soybean oil vs. canola oil) and curcumin supplementation (0.02%) could enhance resilience to a mixed Eimeria challenge. In a 42-day trial, 660 one-day-old male Ross 308 broiler chicks were allocated to five treatments, with six replicates of 22 birds each: an unchallenged control group fed a soybean oil-based diet and four challenged groups fed soybean oil, soybean oil plus curcumin, canola oil, or canola oil plus curcumin. Challenged birds received an oral gavage of mixed Eimeria oocysts on day 14. Relative to the non-supplemented challenged control groups, the clearest pairwise recovery was observed in the canola oil plus curcumin group, whereas responses in the soybean oil plus curcumin group were more endpoint-dependent. Among challenged birds in the factorial analysis, curcumin improved body weight gain and feed efficiency; reduced abdominal fat and relative liver and spleen weights; improved lipid and hepatobiliary indices; increased post-challenge immunoglobulin levels; reduced NF-κB and IL-6 expression; increased IL-10 expression; enhanced total antioxidant capacity, superoxide dismutase and catalase activities; lowered malondialdehyde (MDA) and corticosterone levels; and upregulated NRF2 and SOD1. Culture-based enumeration showed that curcumin increased ileal and cecal Lactobacillus spp. counts and reduced ileal E. coli, with a tendency to reduce cecal E. coli. Canola oil produced limited endpoint-specific effects, mainly on feed intake, cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, alanine aminotransferase, MDA, and IL-6. Introduction

Avian coccidiosis remains one of the most consequential enteric diseases in modern broiler production because Eimeria spp. disrupt the intestinal epithelium, impair digestion and absorption, and increase susceptibility to dysbiosis and opportunistic infections. Beyond performance losses, the condition is increasingly framed as an “intestinal health syndrome,” in which epithelial injury, immune activation, and microbiome instability reinforce one another, thereby reducing feed efficiency and growth persistency1–3. Although chemoprophylaxis and vaccination remain central control tools, commercial management is challenged by heterogeneous field exposure, economic constraints, and a shift toward production systems that restrict antibiotic-class anticoccidials—thereby increasing reliance on vaccination, husbandry, and supportive nutrition4–6. Accordingly, research is moving toward resilience-oriented strategies, including next-generation vaccines and nutrition programs designed to preserve intestinal integrity during challenge, and is increasingly evaluating integrated endpoints spanning performance, systemic biochemistry, immune mediators, oxidative status, and microbial ecology7–10.

A central mechanism underlying Eimeria-induced dysfunction is the imbalance between pro-inflammatory and antioxidant signaling. Infection-associated epithelial injury activates innate immune pathways, including NF-κB signaling, which promotes inflammatory cytokine expression and contributes to tissue damage when excessively activated11,12. Concurrently, reactive oxygen species generated during infection increase lipid peroxidation and impair antioxidant defenses. The KEAP1–NRF2 pathway is a major regulator of endogenous antioxidant and cytoprotective responses and is closely connected to inflammatory signaling through NRF2–NF-κB crosstalk13,14. Stress physiology may further amplify this gut–immune–redox disturbance because corticosterone-related metabolic changes can affect hepatic–intestinal metabolism and microbiota structure15,16. Thus, the NF-κB/NRF2 axis provides a mechanistic framework for evaluating whether dietary interventions can reduce inflammatory pressure while strengthening antioxidant resilience during coccidiosis.

Curcumin, a bioactive polyphenol from Curcuma longa, is a promising phytogenic additive within this framework because it has been associated with the inhibition of inflammatory responses, activation of NRF2-linked antioxidant defenses, improved gut microbial balance, and enhanced intestinal resilience under stress or infection17–20. In broilers, curcumin-based interventions, including conventional and nanoparticle forms, have been reported to improve antioxidant status, immune responses, serum lipid profiles, intestinal morphology, and performance under different stress conditions21,22. In Eimeria-challenged models, curcumin and other plant-derived extracts have also been shown to reduce infection-associated inflammation, oxidative stress, and intestinal dysfunction23,24.

However, the biological expression of curcumin may depend on the dietary matrix in which it is supplied. Curcumin is lipophilic, and dietary fats can influence the intestinal milieu through effects on solubilization, bile-mediated digestion, lipid metabolism, and microbial ecology25. In addition, lipid sources differ in fatty acid composition and oxidative susceptibility, which may independently affect inflammatory tone, membrane composition, microbiota structure, and redox status26. Soybean oil is relatively rich in linoleic acid and has a higher n-6 profile, whereas canola oil contains more oleic acid and a relatively lower n-6:n-3 ratio; these differences may alter the host inflammatory-redox environment in which curcumin acts27–29.

Accordingly, the combination of curcumin with different lipid sources is scientifically relevant because both factors may converge on the same biological targets, intestinal integrity, microbiota stability, inflammatory regulation, and antioxidant defense, but through partly distinct mechanisms. Curcumin may directly modulate NF-κB/NRF2 signaling, whereas the dietary oil source may modify lipid metabolism, oxidative load, and the intestinal environment that supports curcumin function. We therefore hypothesized that curcumin would mitigate the biological costs of mixed Eimeria challenge by reducing NF-κB-associated inflammatory responses and enhancing NRF2-related antioxidant defenses, with concurrent improvements in gut microbiota, humoral immunity, corticosterone concentration, and systemic biochemical status. The objective of this study was to evaluate the individual and combined effects of dietary curcumin and lipid source on growth performance, carcass traits, serum biochemistry, selected bacterial indicators, immunoglobulins, corticosterone concentration, antioxidant status, and NF-κB/NRF2-related gene expression in broilers challenged with mixed Eimeria species.

Results

Comparisons among the five treatments were performed to identify specific group differences among NC (unchallenged negative control fed a soybean oil-based diet), PC + SO (Eimeria-challenged group fed a soybean oil-based diet), PC + SO+CUR (Eimeria-challenged group fed a soybean oil-based diet supplemented with 0.02% curcumin), PC + CO (Eimeria-challenged group fed a canola oil-based diet), and PC + CO+CUR (Eimeria-challenged group fed a canola oil-based diet supplemented with 0.02% curcumin). By contrast, the factorial analysis was restricted to challenged birds and used to estimate the main effects of oil source, curcumin, and their interaction. Accordingly, the effects of curcumin and oil source were interpreted as factorial effects conditional on challenge status, whereas specific treatment differences were interpreted only when supported by pairwise comparisons.

Productive performance and slaughter traits

Table 1 summarizes productive performance across the experimental period (0–42 d) and carcass traits at day 42 in broilers exposed to a mixed Eimeria challenge at 14 d of age. Average daily gain was higher in the Control and CO + CUR groups than in the SO and CO groups, and body weight gain in the Control group was also higher than in SO + CUR. For feed intake, all challenged dietary treatments exhibited lower cumulative intake than the Control group. Among the challenged groups, the canola-oil treatments (CO and CO + CUR) had higher feed intake than SO, whereas SO + CUR was intermediate and did not differ significantly from the other challenged treatments. Feed efficiency in the curcumin-supplemented diets (SO + CUR and CO + CUR) was comparable with the Control and higher than CO; SO was intermediate and differed significantly only from the Control. Factorial analysis indicated a fat source × curcumin interaction for feed intake: curcumin increased intake in the soybean-oil diet, whereas no such effect was observed with canola oil. Regarding main effects, oil source significantly affected feed intake (canola oil > soybean oil), and curcumin supplementation improved body weight gain and feed efficiency.

For carcass traits, carcass yield was higher in the Control and CO + CUR groups than in SO and CO, and thigh yield was higher in the Control group than in SO and CO. Abdominal fat was lower in the Control and CO + CUR groups than in SO. Relative liver weight was higher in all challenged treatments except CO + CUR compared with the Control, and relative spleen weight was lower in SO and CO than in the Control. No significant differences were detected among treatments for breast yield or for relative weights of the heart, thymus, and bursa of Fabricius. Factorial analysis showed no significant main effects of oil source and no oil source × curcumin interaction for carcass parameters; however, curcumin increased carcass and thigh yields and reduced relative abdominal fat and relative liver and spleen weights. Notably, breast yield showed only a tendency toward significance in the five-treatment comparison (P = 0.061), and this result should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Serum biochemistry

Table 2 summarizes the serum biochemical profile of broilers under coccidiosis challenge. Serum total cholesterol (CHOL) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were significantly lower in the Control and CO + CUR groups than in SO (P < 0.05). Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activity was lower in CO + CUR than in SO and CO (P < 0.05). Similarly, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activities were lower in the Control and CO + CUR groups than in SO (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed among treatments for glucose, triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), total protein, albumin, globulin, or uric acid.

Targeted ileal and cecal bacterial counts

Because only Lactobacillus spp. and Escherichia coli were enumerated using selective culture methods, these data represent selected culturable bacterial indicators rather than a comprehensive gut microbiota profile. Table 3 summarizes ileal and cecal bacterial counts (day 24) in broilers under coccidiosis challenge. In the ileum, the Control group had higher Lactobacillus counts than the SO and CO groups (P < 0.05). Ileal E. coli counts were highest in SO, whereas the lowest counts were observed in the Control and CO + CUR groups (P < 0.05). In the cecum, the Control group exhibited the highest Lactobacillus counts and differed significantly from all other treatments (P < 0.05); additionally, CO + CUR had higher cecal Lactobacillus counts than SO. Cecal E. coli counts were higher in all challenged treatments except CO + CUR than in the Control group (P < 0.05). Based on the 2 × 2 factorial analysis (excluding the Control), no fat source × curcumin interaction was detected. For main effects, canola oil tended to reduce ileal E. coli counts compared with soybean oil, whereas curcumin increased Lactobacillus counts in both the ileum and cecum and reduced ileal E. coli counts; curcumin also tended to reduce cecal E. coli counts (P < 0.05).

Immunity

Table 4 presents serum immunoglobulin concentrations in broilers on day 13 (1 d before challenge) and day 24 (10 d post-challenge). On day 13, experimental treatments did not significantly affect serum IgA, IgM, or IgG. On day 24, the unchallenged Control group exhibited the lowest IgG and IgA concentrations and differed significantly from all challenged groups. Among the coccidiosis-challenged treatments, broilers fed canola oil plus curcumin (CO + CUR) had higher IgG and IgA concentrations than those fed SO. In contrast, serum IgM did not differ significantly among the five treatments in the CRD comparison. Factorial analysis (excluding the Control) indicated no fat source × curcumin interaction for serum immunoglobulins before or after challenge and no main effect of fat source; however, curcumin supplementation increased IgG, IgM, and IgA concentrations after challenge.

Antioxidant status, corticosterone, and NRF2-related genes

Table 5 summarizes serum antioxidant indices and corticosterone in broilers under coccidiosis challenge. Relative to SO, the curcumin-supplemented treatments (SO + CUR and CO + CUR) exhibited higher total antioxidant capacity (TAC) and lower malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations (P < 0.05). The CO + CUR group also showed higher superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and a tendency toward higher catalase (CAT) activity than SO (P < 0.05 for SOD; trend for CAT). Among challenged birds, serum corticosterone was highest in SO and CO and differed from the unchallenged Control group (P < 0.05). Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity did not differ among treatments. Factorial analysis (excluding the Control) indicated no fat source × curcumin interaction; however, fat source had a significant main effect on MDA (canola oil < soybean oil; P < 0.05), and curcumin exerted significant main effects by increasing TAC, SOD, and CAT and decreasing MDA and corticosterone (P < 0.05).

Discussion

To improve clarity, two statistical levels are distinguished in the following interpretation. Pairwise treatment comparisons refer to the completely randomized five-treatment design and identify specific differences among the Control, SO, SO + CUR, CO, and CO + CUR groups. In contrast, factorial main effects and fat source × curcumin interactions refer only to the four coccidiosis-challenged treatments and should be interpreted as dietary effects conditional on the mixed Eimeria challenge. Accordingly, conclusions about curcumin or fat source are based primarily on the factorial analysis, whereas specific group differences are discussed only when supported by Tukey’s post hoc comparisons.

Across the coccidiosis-challenged diets, the most consistent signal was the main effect of curcumin on growth and efficiency, rather than a fat source–dependent response. Using factorial contrasts within the challenged groups, curcumin increased body weight gain (BWG) by 138 g per bird (+ 5.3%) relative to the non-curcumin average (2740 vs. 2602 g) and improved feed efficiency (FE) by 4.1% (0.568 vs. 0.545). In contrast, the fat source × curcumin interaction was not significant for BWG or FE, supporting a relatively stable curcumin effect across oil sources. Biologically, this pattern is consistent with the current view that Eimeria infection compromises epithelial integrity, increases endogenous nutrient losses, and depresses nutrient-use efficiency30,31. Accordingly, improvements in FE under challenge often reflect barrier protection and modulation of inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways, rather than simply increased feed intake. Curcumin has repeatedly been associated with reduced intestinal inflammatory signaling and improved antioxidant status, alongside improved performance in broilers19,32, and experimental coccidiosis studies similarly support its capacity to attenuate challenge-induced performance losses and gut dysfunction23,33. In our CRD means, the practical relevance was evident: CO + CUR partially restored performance toward the Control, narrowing the BWG deficit to 3.65% (2774 vs. 2879 g) while maintaining FE at near-Control levels (0.571 vs. 0.576; −0.87%). This pattern is consistent with a damage-limiting, rather than growth-promoting, mode of action during enteric infection. By contrast, oil source primarily influenced feed intake (FI) and showed a fat source × curcumin interaction for FI, consistent with evidence that fatty-acid profile can modulate appetite regulation, inflammatory mediators, and host responses during infection28,29. Although the present study focuses on systemic immune-redox responses, the phenotypic validity of the mixed-Eimeria model was confirmed in our companion study, in which the challenge impaired growth performance across different rearing periods, increased mortality, lesion scores, and oocyst shedding, whereas curcumin improved performance while reducing duodenal and cecal lesions and fecal oocyst output34. Thus, the current findings provide complementary mechanistic evidence supporting the curcumin-associated resilience phenotype observed under a coccidiosis challenge.

Carcass fatness provides a second, mechanistically informative anchor point, suggesting that curcumin exerted a directionally favorable metabolic effect under coccidiosis challenge. Within the canola-oil challenged diet, curcumin reduced abdominal fat by 15.9% (1.22 vs. 1.45% of body weight), and across challenged diets the factorial main effect indicated a 16.5% reduction (1.265 vs. 1.515% of body weight). These shifts are consistent with reports linking curcumin to altered lipid deposition and improved metabolic regulation (e.g., reduced lipogenesis and/or enhanced lipid utilization), often accompanied by a lower inflammatory tone in broilers32,35. Importantly, because carcass traits showed neither significant main effects of fat source nor fat source × curcumin interactions in the factorial model, the carcass and thigh-yield responses in our dataset are best interpreted as curcumin-driven rather than oil-driven; however, breast yield is described only as a tendency because the five-treatment comparison did not reach significance. Mechanistically, this effect may reflect attenuation of systemic immune activation—supported by the reduced relative liver and spleen weights with curcumin—and a consequent shift in energy allocation away from inflammatory costs toward productive partitioning1,36. This interpretation also accords with challenge-model evidence that Eimeria disrupts microbiota structure and barrier function, two domains in which phytogenic additives and lipid strategies may plausibly converge33,37,38.

The serum biochemical pattern supports the view that the Eimeria challenge can extend beyond the intestine, altering systemic metabolism and imposing a measurable burden on hepatic function. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports showing that coccidiosis-associated intestinal injury, inflammation, and oxidative stress can impair barrier function, increase systemic metabolic load, and influence hepatic enzyme activity and lipid metabolism39–41. Importantly, the absence of treatment differences in glucose and major protein fractions suggests that, at the sampling age, the primary systemic effects involved lipid metabolism and hepatobiliary integrity rather than broad disruption of energy or protein homeostasis. This pattern supports the interpretation that inflammation- and oxidative stress–linked metabolic modulation, rather than generalized malnutrition, was the dominant systemic signal.

Mechanistically, the main effects of fat source (canola vs. soybean oil) are biologically plausible because dietary fatty-acid composition can influence hepatic lipid trafficking, lipoprotein assembly, and inflammatory tone. Recent studies support this interpretation by showing that dietary fat source can influence broiler lipid metabolism, immune-related responses, and cecal microbial composition, although these effects are often endpoint-specific and depend on the physiological context26,28. Therefore, the canola-oil effects observed here should be interpreted as limited and endpoint-specific rather than as uniform improvements across all measured traits. Diets relatively richer in monounsaturated fatty acids and with a different n-6:n-3 balance—often characteristic of canola compared with soybean oil—may reduce lipogenic drive and improve lipoprotein profiles, helping to explain why canola oil was associated with lower CHOL and LDL-C in the factorial analysis. The absence of consistent oil-source effects for many immune, microbial, carcass, and antioxidant gene-expression variables indicates that lipid source acted as a secondary modifier rather than as the dominant protective factor. Comparable oil-type effects on plasma lipids and metabolic resilience have been reported in broilers exposed to physiological stressors42. In parallel, the main effect of curcumin—improved lipid-related endpoints and reduced enzyme activities—aligns with its documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory actions, including attenuation of NF-κB–linked inflammatory cascades and support of cytoprotective redox signaling that may stabilize intestinal barrier function and reduce secondary hepatic load19,43. Notably, the absence of a fat source × curcumin interaction indicates that the curcumin response was broadly consistent across lipid sources (i.e., additive rather than strongly synergistic), which is advantageous for practical formulation because benefits may persist even when basal dietary fat sources vary.

The selected bacterial indicators measured here are consistent with the concept that coccidial infection can disturb intestinal microbial ecology44–46; however, the present culture-based data are limited to Lactobacillus spp. and E. coli and therefore cannot define whole-community shifts in the gut microbiota. Mechanistically, epithelial damage, leakage of luminal proteins, and altered mucus and goblet-cell dynamics during Eimeria infection can shift substrate availability and local redox status, thereby promoting Proteobacteria and other inflammation-associated bacteria while weakening colonization resistance1,47. The fat source signal in the factorial analysis—showing only a trend toward lower ileal E. coli with canola compared with soybean oil—suggests that lipid-matrix effects on microbial selection were modest under this disease pressure, although biologically plausible. Differences in fatty-acid profiles can influence bile secretion and bile-acid composition, exposure to antimicrobial lipids and bile acids, and inflammatory tone, all of which can secondarily shape gut communities25,26. Consistent with this premise, replacing conventional ingredients with canola-derived components has been reported to alter cecal community structure and functional outputs (e.g., fermentation end-products), supporting the view that canola-based lipid/protein matrices can modulate the microbiota even when growth responses are subtle48. Collectively, these findings fit a model in which infection-driven dysbiosis predominates, whereas dietary fat source exerts smaller, context-dependent selective pressure on specific taxa.

A key outcome of the present study was the consistent, directionally favorable association between curcumin supplementation and selected bacterial indicators: Lactobacillus counts increased in both intestinal segments, whereas E. coli counts decreased (or tended to decrease), without a detectable fat source × curcumin interaction. This pattern suggests that curcumin-associated changes in these selected bacterial indicators were comparatively consistent across lipid sources under coccidiosis challenge. Several complementary mechanisms could account for these findings. Curcumin has documented anti-inflammatory and barrier-supporting actions, which could indirectly limit E. coli expansion by reducing epithelial leakage and inflammatory niches that favor Proteobacteria22,49. In addition, curcumin may exert selective antimicrobial activity and/or prebiotic-like effects, thereby supporting lactic acid bacteria and shifting community structure toward taxa associated with improved mucosal homeostasis22. Curcumin-mediated activation of cytoprotective pathways (e.g., NRF2/ARE) and mitigation of oxidative injury may further stabilize the gut environment, which is relevant given that oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling are central drivers of dysbiosis during enteric disease50. Finally, because host–microbiota crosstalk can amplify pathology through macrophage recruitment and pro-inflammatory programming during E. tenella infection, a curcumin-associated shift away from E. coli dominance and toward Lactobacillus enrichment may plausibly dampen downstream immune escalation9.Overall, the bacterial-enumeration results suggest that curcumin may partially support a more favorable pattern in these selected bacterial indicators under coccidiosis, whereas dietary fat source appears to play a secondary—though potentially meaningful—modulatory role. Because only Lactobacillus spp. and E. coli were quantified by culture-based enumeration, the present bacterial data should be interpreted as selected microbial indicators rather than as evidence of comprehensive microbiome profiling. This limitation is important because recent sequencing-based studies have shown that dietary interventions can affect microbial richness, diversity, and community structure beyond a few culturable taxa51,52. Therefore, the present findings support curcumin-associated shifts in selected beneficial and opportunistic bacterial groups, but they do not allow conclusions regarding whole-community diversity or global microbiota remodeling.

The absence of treatment effects on serum immunoglobulins before challenge (d 13) indicates comparable baseline humoral status across groups, whereas the marked divergence after challenge (d 24) is consistent with the expected immune activation following Eimeria exposure53. Lower IgA and IgG concentrations in the unchallenged Control group likely reflect minimal antigenic stimulation, whereas elevated IgA and IgG in challenged birds probably represent a coordinated mucosal–systemic response aimed at limiting parasite replication and promoting clearance54. Notably, within challenged birds, curcumin supplementation supported humoral responsiveness (factorial main effect) without evidence of a fat source × curcumin interaction, aligning with reports that curcumin can enhance immune competence under stress or infection through redox-sensitive signaling and improved intestinal homeostasis21,23. The higher IgA and IgG in CO + CUR relative to SO may reflect a more favorable inflammatory–regulatory balance and/or lipid-mediated differences in immune-cell membrane composition and antigen presentation—an interpretation consistent with the observed cytokine and NF-κB patterns. However, the absence of significant main effects of fat source for immunoglobulins suggests that this advantage is most evident at the combined-treatment level rather than as a uniform oil effect.

At the transcriptional level, reduced NF-κB expression and attenuation of downstream pro-inflammatory signaling (particularly IL-6, with a trend toward lower IL-1β) in curcumin-supplemented groups support a central mechanistic interpretation: curcumin can constrain NF-κB–driven inflammatory cascades while preserving, or potentially enhancing, protective immunity. NF-κB is a pivotal transcriptional hub linking pattern-recognition receptor inputs (e.g., TLR/MyD88) to cytokine production and inflammatory tissue remodeling12, and Eimeria infections are well known to engage NF-κB–associated pathways in intestinal tissues55. In this context, the concomitant increase in IL-10—a key regulatory cytokine that limits immunopathology—suggests that curcumin shifted host responses toward a more controlled, resolution-oriented profile. Such a shift is biologically advantageous during coccidiosis, in which excessive inflammation compromises barrier function and nutrient utilization17,23. The main effect of canola oil on IL-6 expression (without interaction) further suggests that altering dietary fatty-acid profiles can independently modulate inflammatory tone, potentially complementing curcumin’s anti-inflammatory actions. Finally, the broader literature indicates that infection outcomes reflect integration of immune signaling with gut ecosystem dynamics; for example, host resistance phenotypes to E. tenella are accompanied by distinct gut microbial patterns10. Accordingly, interventions that temper NF-κB activity while supporting regulatory circuits (e.g., IL-10) may reduce collateral tissue damage while maintaining effective immune containment54,56.

Coccidiosis can impose a substantial oxidative load through epithelial injury and inflammation, promoting reactive oxygen species generation and lipid peroxidation and thereby weakening systemic antioxidant defenses4,40. Within this framework, the pattern observed here—curcumin-associated improvements in circulating antioxidant status (higher total antioxidant capacity and antioxidant enzyme activities, alongside lower lipid peroxidation)—is consistent with curcumin acting not only as a radical scavenger but, more importantly, as a regulator of endogenous redox signaling19,57. The concurrent increase in NRF2 expression (and in the NRF2-linked enzyme gene SOD1) supports a mechanistic interpretation centered on the KEAP1–NRF2–ARE axis, a master pathway governing cytoprotective and antioxidant gene programs13. This is biologically meaningful because NRF2 activation can enhance antioxidant capacity while also constraining inflammation through NRF2–NF-κB crosstalk, thereby limiting oxidant amplification driven by inflammatory cytokine signaling58. Consistent NRF2-oriented antioxidant benefits of curcumin have been reported in broilers exposed to oxidative challenges (e.g., diquat or aflatoxin), in which curcumin improved redox biomarkers and upregulated NRF2-related targets18,50. Recent studies further support the interpretation that curcumin-containing interventions can support broiler resilience through coordinated improvements in intestinal barrier integrity, inflammatory control, attenuation of oxidative stress, and microbiota modulation. For example, Liu et al.52 reported that Lonicerae flos and turmeric extracts improved growth performance, reduced intestinal lesion scores and coccidial oocysts, enhanced barrier-related gene expression, lowered inflammatory cytokine levels, and modulated cecal microbiota in challenged broilers. Similarly, He et al.59 showed that curcumin enhanced antioxidant defenses in broilers under oxidative stress, supporting its role as a functional redox-modulating feed additive. Moreover, anticoccidial nutrition reviews highlight phytogenics as practical tools to mitigate oxidative stress and support intestinal resilience under Eimeria challenge60. Overall, the concordance between serum antioxidant indices and NRF2/SOD1 expression provides a coherent molecular rationale for the observed redox improvements61. The selective upregulation of NRF2 and SOD1, with unchanged GPx1 and CAT expression, suggests that curcumin did not uniformly activate all antioxidant genes but instead preferentially enhanced specific components of the NRF2-associated antioxidant network. This gene-specific response may be explained by differences in antioxidant response element accessibility, promoter sensitivity, tissue-specific regulation, and sampling time after the oxidative challenge13. Functionally, SOD1 may respond earlier to superoxide-related oxidative pressure, whereas GPx1 and CAT can be regulated more strongly by substrate availability, cofactor-dependent enzyme maturation, protein stability, or post-translational mechanisms than by mRNA abundance alone62,63. Thus, the lack of GPx1 and CAT mRNA induction does not exclude their functional contribution, particularly because curcumin improved serum antioxidant indices, including TAC, SOD, and CAT activities, and reduced MDA and corticosterone levels. Overall, curcumin appears to strengthen the NRF2–SOD1 transcriptional axis, while other antioxidant defenses may be modulated mainly at enzymatic or post-transcriptional levels.

The stress-marker response complements this redox narrative. Lower corticosterone concentrations with curcumin suggest attenuation of systemic stress signaling, which is relevant because corticosterone elevation is closely linked to oxidative dysfunction and metabolic disturbance in poultry, including mitochondrial perturbations and increased oxidant pressure64,65. Experimental corticosterone models in broilers indicate that sustained corticosterone exposure can promote oxidative injury and organ dysfunction, and curcumin has been reported to blunt these adverse outcomes. Together, these findings support a plausible “stress–redox loop” in which curcumin dampens corticosterone-associated oxidative escalation64,65. The selective nature of the responses (i.e., improvements in TAC and SOD-related signals without uniform shifts across all antioxidant nodes) is also consistent with pathway- and tissue-specific regulation, including post-transcriptional control and cofactor dependence61. Regarding fat source, the direction of the canola-oil effect on oxidative endpoints is compatible with evidence that dietary lipid matrices influence peroxidative susceptibility and antioxidant dynamics66. However, the absence of a fat source × curcumin interaction suggests these factors operated largely additively, rather than synergistically, under the factorial model—an outcome frequently observed when phytogenic additives are evaluated alongside dietary formulation strategies under enteric stress4,60. Taken together, the serum and gene-expression data support curcumin as a consistent modulator of NRF2-linked antioxidant defense and stress physiology during coccidiosis, with dietary fat source exerting a secondary, independent influence13,40.

A limitation of the present study is that intestinal morphometric indices are not presented here. These endpoints are central to verifying intestinal barrier recovery during coccidiosis; however, they were comprehensively analyzed in a companion article from the same experimental trial34. That study reported that the mixed Eimeria challenge disrupted villus–crypt architecture and goblet cell counts, whereas dietary curcumin restored intestinal morphology by increasing villus height, villus surface area, the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio, and goblet cell counts, alongside improved barrier-related responses. Therefore, the gut-health interpretation in the present study should be considered complementary to those previously reported intestinal morphology and barrier-biology findings, while the current article specifically extends the mechanistic framework toward microbiota, humoral immunity, corticosterone, and NF-κB/NRF2-linked immune-redox regulation. An additional limitation is that the study included only one unchallenged reference group, which received the soybean-oil diet; therefore, the study was not designed as a full challenge × oil source × curcumin factorial experiment. Consequently, the dietary effects should be interpreted as conditional on Eimeria challenge and should not be extrapolated to unchallenged birds.

Conclusions

Under a mixed Eimeria challenge, dietary curcumin was associated with endpoint-specific improvements in resilience-related responses, with the most consistent responses observed for immune-redox regulation, antioxidant status, corticosterone levels, and selected serum biochemical indices. These effects were evaluated through both pairwise treatment comparisons and a factorial analysis restricted to challenged birds; therefore, they should not be interpreted as uniform across all endpoints or lipid backgrounds. Culture-based enumeration indicated favorable shifts in selected bacterial indicators, namely Lactobacillus spp. and E. coli, but did not characterize the whole gut microbial community. Canola oil produced limited endpoint-specific effects, mainly on feed intake, lipid and hepatobiliary indices, MDA, and IL-6. Overall, curcumin was the primary dietary factor associated with partial mitigation of coccidiosis-related immune-redox disturbances, whereas lipid source acted as a secondary and context-dependent modifier.

Methods

Animal ethics

All procedures involving birds were conducted in accordance with internationally accepted guidelines for the care and use of animals in research and were approved by the Ilam University’s Animal Ethics Committee (approval number: IR.ILAM.REC.1405.001). Husbandry, handling, and sampling were performed to minimize distress. To standardize handling-related stress across treatments, the unchallenged Control group received a sham oral gavage.

Birds, treatments, and management

A total of 660 Ross 308 broiler chicks were purchased from a commercial hatchery and used in a 42-d trial arranged as a completely randomized design with five dietary treatments. Male broiler chicks were selected as a uniform, fast-growing commercial broiler phenotype to minimize sex-related variation in growth, metabolism, immune response, and redox status. Upon arrival, chicks were clinically inspected for general health and viability, and only active birds without visible abnormalities were included in the experiment. Birds were then randomly allocated to treatments, with six replicate pens per treatment and 22 birds per pen. Birds were reared in floor pens (1.5 × 1.8 m) on litter, with ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the study. The house environment was maintained under standard commercial conditions with uniform lighting and adequate ventilation. Temperature was set at 34 ± 1 °C during the first week and gradually reduced to approximately 23 ± 1 °C by the later rearing period. Birds were reared under a controlled lighting schedule, with 24 h of light from days 1 to 3 and 23 h of light and 1 h of darkness from days 4 to 42.Five experimental treatments were applied: (1) an unchallenged Control fed a soybean-oil–based diet (n-6–rich) and not exposed to coccidiosis; (2) a coccidiosis-challenged soybean-oil diet; (3) a coccidiosis-challenged canola-oil diet (characterized by a higher monounsaturated fatty acid content and a relatively higher n-3 fraction than soybean oil); (4) a coccidiosis-challenged soybean-oil diet supplemented with curcumin (0.02%); and (5) a coccidiosis-challenged canola-oil diet supplemented with curcumin (0.02%).

Curcumin, a 95% natural turmeric extract, was procured from Karen Company (Yazd, Iran) for this study and incorporated into the feed at 0.02% (200 mg/kg). This inclusion level was selected based on previous broiler research showing that 200 mg/kg dietary curcumin improved growth performance, intestinal morphology, antioxidant capacity, immune responses, and anticoccidial indices under stress or enteric challenge conditions17,21,22. Diets were formulated for three feeding phases (starter, grower, and finisher) to meet or exceed breeder recommendations for Ross 308 broilers. Table 6 presents the ingredient composition and calculated chemical composition of the basal diets for 0–10 d, 11–28 d, and 29–42 d. Dietary oil was included at 40 g/kg in each phase, using soybean oil or canola oil according to treatment allocation. Oil sources and characterization

Antioxidant-free soybean and canola oils were provided by Oil Seeds Co. (Tehran, Iran). Both oils used in the experimental diets were characterized for physicochemical quality and fatty acid composition (Table S1). Oil samples were collected from each batch used in feed manufacture and analyzed for moisture (%), impurities (%), unsaponifiable matter (%), free fatty acids (%), peroxide value (mEq/kg), and gross energy (kcal/kg) using standard oil-quality methods. Moisture content was determined by controlled oven drying; impurities were quantified gravimetrically after filtration; unsaponifiable matter was measured following alkaline saponification and solvent extraction; free fatty acids were quantified by titration and expressed as a percentage (typically as oleic acid equivalents); and peroxide value was determined by iodometric titration as an index of primary oxidation products. Gross energy was measured using an adiabatic bomb calorimeter. Fatty acid profiles were determined as fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) after transesterification and analyzed by gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC-FID), with results expressed as the relative percentage of total fatty acids.

As shown in Table S1, soybean oil had a higher polyunsaturated fatty acid content, dominated by linoleic acid (C18:2n-6), and a higher n-6:n-3 ratio, whereas canola oil contained a higher proportion of monounsaturated fatty acids (notably oleic acid, C18:1), higher α-linolenic acid (C18:3n-3), and a lower n-6:n-3 ratio. These compositional differences were considered relevant for interpreting treatment effects on inflammation–redox balance and host–microbiota responses under coccidiosis challenge.

Coccidia species challenge

To induce coccidiosis, birds in the challenged treatments were orally gavaged on day 14 with 1 mL of a sporulated oocyst suspension containing a mixed Eimeria inoculum (approximately 50,000 oocysts of Eimeria acervulina, 10,000 oocysts of E. maxima, and 5,000 oocysts of E. tenella per bird). Oocysts were obtained from the Parasitology Laboratory, University of Tehran, and were originally recovered as field isolates from feces/litter and intestinal material collected from commercial flocks56. For sporulation and preservation, oocysts were maintained in potassium dichromate solution and handled using standard parasitological procedures to ensure viability before dosing. The unchallenged Control group received a sham gavage of 1 mL sterile saline on the same day to standardize handling stress between challenged and unchallenged birds67.

Growth performance, carcass traits measurements, and sampling

Body weight and feed intake were recorded on a pen basis throughout the 42-d trial. Birds were weighed by replicate pen at placement and again on day 42, and overall BWG(1–42 d) was calculated as the difference between final and initial pen body weight. Overall FI(1–42 d) was determined for each pen by subtracting feed refusals from total feed offered over the experimental period. FE was calculated as BWG/FI for each replicate pen. Performance indices (BWG, FI, and FE) were corrected for mortality.

Carcass evaluation was performed on day 42. Two male birds per replicate pen (12 birds per treatment) were randomly selected, individually weighed to obtain pre-slaughter body weight, and humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation. Birds were defeathered and eviscerated, and carcass yield (eviscerated carcass) was calculated relative to pre-slaughter body weight. Breast and leg portions (thigh + drumstick) were excised and weighed, with yields expressed as percentages of pre-slaughter body weight. Abdominal fat (fat surrounding the gizzard, proventriculus, cloaca, and abdominal musculature) was collected and weighed. The liver, heart, spleen, thymus, and bursa of Fabricius were excised, blotted dry, and weighed. Abdominal fat and organ weights were expressed as relative weights (% of pre-slaughter body weight) for statistical analysis.

Serum, intestinal microbiota, and jejunal gene-expression analyses were performed on day 24, corresponding to 10 days after Eimeria inoculation on day 14. This sampling point was chosen because it captures the post-acute phase of coccidiosis, during which inflammatory activation, oxidative stress, microbial disturbance, and early recovery of intestinal function are evident, thereby allowing a mechanistic assessment of host responses56,67,68.

Serum metabolic profile, immunoglobulins, and antioxidant/stress markers

On day 24, two birds per replicate pen (selected with body weight close to the pen mean) were sampled for blood collection from the wing vein. Blood was collected into gel separator tubes without anticoagulant, and serum was obtained by centrifugation (2,500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C). To assess humoral immunity at baseline, blood sampling was also conducted on day 13 (pre-challenge) using the same procedure and replicate-level sampling strategy.

For the serum metabolic profile (day 24), concentrations of glucose, TG, CHOL, HDL-C, LDL-C, total protein, albumin, and uric acid, as well as activities of AST, ALT, and ALP, were measured using commercial biochemical kits (Pars Azmoun Co., Tehran, Iran) and an automated spectrophotometer (Chem 200; Gesan Production Srl, Campobello, Italy), as previously described56,69. Serum globulin concentration was calculated as total protein − albumin.

For serum immunoglobulins (days 13 and 24), IgA, IgG, and IgM concentrations were quantified by ELISA using flat-bottom 96-well plates and species-appropriate quantitation kits (Bethyl Laboratories Inc., Montgomery, TX, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For antioxidant status and corticosterone (day 24), TAC, MDA, and the activities of SOD, GPx, and CAT were assessed using commercial assay kits. TAC was measured using a TAC kit (Randox Laboratories Ltd., Crumlin, UK), and SOD, GPx, CAT, and MDA were measured using kits from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA), according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Serum corticosterone was determined using a chicken corticosterone ELISA kit (ZellBio GmbH, Ulm, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions, consistent with Ghasemi and Nari70.

Targeted ileal and cecal bacterial counts

On day 24, two birds per replicate pen (selected with body weight close to the pen mean) were used for intestinal bacterial enumeration. Following euthanasia, the gastrointestinal tract was aseptically removed, and the ileum (from Meckel’s diverticulum to the ileocecal junction) and ceca (from the ostium to the tip) were identified and ligated to prevent cross-contamination. Approximately 1 g of digesta from each segment was collected into sterile containers, homogenized, and serially diluted (10-fold) in sterile diluent (phosphate-buffered saline, PBS). Appropriate dilutions were plated in duplicate according to conventional selective culture methods commonly used in broiler studies71,72. Selective media were used to enumerate Lactobacillus spp. on de Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar (anaerobic incubation at 37–39 °C for 48–72 h) and E. coli on eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar (aerobic incubation at 37–39 °C for 24 h). Colony counts from duplicate plates were averaged for each sample, and bacterial populations were expressed as log10 CFU/g digesta.

Jejunal mRNA expression of immune- and antioxidant-related genes

On day 24, two birds per replicate pen (selected with body weight close to the pen mean) were euthanized, and a mid-jejunal segment was excised, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until molecular analysis56. Total RNA was extracted from jejunal tissue using a commercial RNA extraction kit (Pars Tous Co., Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To minimize genomic DNA contamination, extracted RNA was treated with DNase I before reverse transcription. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a cDNA synthesis kit (Pars Tous, Iran) following the manufacturer’s protocol69.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using an ABI 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) with SYBR Green Master Mix (Pars Tous, Iran) to quantify jejunal mRNA expression of immune-related genes (NF-κB, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10) and antioxidant-related genes (NRF2, GPx1, SOD1, and CAT), with GAPDH used as the reference (housekeeping) gene69,73. Primer sequences, amplicon sizes, and GenBank accession numbers are provided in Table S2. Assays included appropriate quality control (e.g., no-template controls and technical replicates) and were reported in accordance with MIQE recommendations for qPCR experiments74,75. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method by normalizing each target Ct to GAPDH and expressing values relative to the Control group75=.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Prior to analysis, data were screened for outliers, and normality of model residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Percentage data, including carcass yield, cut-up part yields, abdominal fat, and relative organ weights, were arcsine-transformed; however, means are presented as untransformed values for ease of interpretation. The experiment was analyzed first as a five-treatment completely randomized design to compare the unchallenged Control group with the four challenged dietary treatments. The unchallenged Control group was included as a healthy physiological reference and was not intended to form part of a full challenge × fat source × curcumin factorial arrangement. Therefore, to evaluate dietary effects specifically under a coccidiosis challenge, a secondary 2 × 2 factorial analysis was conducted using only the four challenged treatments, with fat source, curcumin supplementation, and their interaction included as fixed effects. Accordingly, the main effects of fat source and curcumin, as well as the fat source × curcumin interaction, should be interpreted as effects conditional on the mixed Eimeria challenge rather than as responses in unchallenged birds. Replicate pen was considered the experimental unit for performance variables, whereas the individual bird sampled within a pen was considered the experimental unit for blood, tissue, microbiological, and carcass variables. When a significant treatment effect was detected, least-squares means were separated using Tukey’s test. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05, and trends were discussed when 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10.
