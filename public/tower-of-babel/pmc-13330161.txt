Prediction of intrinsic solubility for drug-like organic compounds using automated network optimizer (ANO) for physicochemical feature and hyperparameter optimization
Authors: You Kyoung Chung, Seung Jin Lee, Junho Lee, Himchanvit Cho, Sung-Jin Kim, Joonsuk Huh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate prediction of aqueous solubility remains a critical challenge in the chemical and pharmaceutical industries, significantly influencing drug development and delivery. This study revisits this well-explored area by leveraging the advanced capabilities of modern computational resources. We apply an automated network optimizer model that integrates dual optimization processes for molecular features and hyperparameters, streamlining the traditionally complex hyperparameter search while providing an efficient interpretation of molecular properties. By employing feature optimization techniques, our deep neural network model demonstrates improvements in both the speed and accuracy of molecular property predictions, achieving an average performance of R2 = 0.991. This result outperforms conventional hyperparameter optimization methods such as grid search and random search in predicting the intrinsic solubility of 3,745 compounds across four external experimental datasets. Based on feature importance analysis, we identified key molecular features and structures that significantly influence solubility. Additionally, combining three molecular fingerprints (Morgan, MACCS key, and Avalon) with molecular descriptors enhances model performance, providing a deeper understanding of the relationship between molecular structure and solubility within the physicochemical feature optimization process. These findings underscore the potential of machine learning models to improve predictive modeling of physical properties, apply automated modeling and feature selection to new chemical datasets, and offer explainable insights into the principles driving solubility predictions. Scientific contributions This article focuses on recent advancements in the prediction of molecular properties through the application of a Quantitative Structure-Property Relationship (QSPR)-based deep neural network (DNN) model. This model employs a dual optimization approach that integrates both molecular feature selection and hyperparameter tuning. A review of publicly available datasets for drug-sized molecules is presented, highlighting the contributions of automated modeling and feature selection in enhancing the predictive accuracy of physical properties. Introduction

Solubility, defined as the process of dissolving a solute in a solvent to form a homogeneous system, is crucial for achieving desired drug concentrations in systemic circulation and ensuring pharmacological efficacy [1]. Understanding the solubility of chemical compounds is vital for predicting their physicochemical behavior and facilitating the design and synthesis of next-generation drug-like compounds [2]. As a key property, solubility influences interactions in various environments, making its prediction an essential research focus for decades, with substantial progress made through pivotal discoveries and advancements [3]. The advent of computational methods has transformed solubility research by enabling in silico predictions, which are faster and more cost effective than experimental approaches. Several empirical modeling methods have been developed using nonexperimental structural parameters. A groundbreaking discovery in this field was the use of the octanol–water partition coefficient (logP), introduced by Hansch et al., to predict solubility [4, 5]. This discovery laid the foundation for further advancements, including the development of the general solubility equation (GSE) by the Yalkowsky group [6–8]. The GSE provided a more comprehensive framework by integrating molecular structure descriptors, while group contribution methods [9–11] established correlations between water solubility and functional groups by decomposing molecules into subunits and estimating their solubility contributions. However, the GSE’s reliance on experimental data, such as the melting point (Tm), and the group contribution method’s need for numerous parameters to achieve accurate predictions have led to the development of alternative approaches. These methods focus solely on structural features for estimating logP, as explored in subsequent research [12, 13]. Further advancements were made by Jorgensen and Duffy [14], who utilized Monte Carlo simulations and various molecular descriptors, including molecular weight (MW), volume, solvent accessible surface area (SASA), hydrogen bond (HB) counts, and physical descriptors like solute–water Coulomb (ESXC) and Lennard–Jones (ESXL) interactions, along with hydrophobic and hydroPhilic components. These refinements have made predictive models more accurate and reliable, contributing significantly to the field of solubility prediction [1, 7, 8, 15–18]. Despite these advancements, traditional methodologies still face inherent limitations, particularly in high-throughput settings. These methods are often time-consuming and expensive, making them impractical for efficiently screening large libraries of compounds. In addition, their predictive accuracy can sometimes be suboptimal, limiting their applicability in real-world scenarios. Consequently, there is a critical need for more robust computational approaches that enhance both the efficiency and reliability of solubility prediction in drug development.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have opened new avenues for improving QSPR models [12]. Over the past decades, AI-based QSPR models [19–22] have demonstrated promise in predicting the solubility of drug-like molecules [23–34]. These models leverage molecular descriptors—quantitative representations of molecular properties—to describe and predict molecular behavior. By integrating such descriptors, AI models can construct highly accurate predictive frameworks. The descriptors serve as inputs to machine learning (ML) algorithms, which learn the relationships between molecular properties and solubility outcomes. A key advantage of AI-driven solubility prediction is its capacity to manage large datasets [3, 16, 17] and capture complex relationships that may elude traditional methods. In addition, AI models can dynamically adjust to new data, enhancing their predictive accuracy over time. However, traditional ML models, such as ridge regression (RR), support vector machines (SVM), random forest regression (RFR) struggle with scalability and accuracy in high-throughput settings due to their reliance on manual feature selection and limited hyperparameter tuning capabilities. While more advanced models such as multi-layer perceptrons (MLP) and deep neural networks (DNN) offer improved flexibility and performance by learning complex, non-linear relationships between molecular features, they still face significant challenges. These include the need for extensive manual tuning of numerous hyperparameters, such as learning rates, dropout rates, and layer sizes, which can drastically affect model performance. Furthermore, the manual selection of optimal architectures and hyperparameters often leads to time-consuming trial-and-error processes, making it difficult to consistently achieve high predictive accuracy across diverse datasets. In this study, we propose a physicochemical deep learning model that utilizes an automated network optimizer (ANO) to improve the prediction of intrinsic solubility for drug-sized compounds. Our model employs Bayesian optimization (BO) with tree-structured Parzen estimators (TPEs) to efficiently explore the hyperparameter space, identifying the optimal model configuration. By integrating a DNN model with the advanced HPO capabilities of the autoML method [35, 36], we enable the simultaneous optimization of molecular features and model parameters from both chemical and machine learning perspectives. This dual optimization strategy aims to not only enhance predictive accuracy and performance but also reduce the time and cost required to achieve optimal solutions. Our approach achieved high performance, with an R2 = 0.991, surpassing the results obtained using traditional standalone methods such as ridge regression (RR), support vector machine (SVM), and multilayer perceptron (MLP). Additionally, through an analysis of feature importance using the Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method [37] and a correlation heatmap, we identified key molecular descriptors that significantly influence solubility are molecular weight (Mol_Weight), octanol/water partition coefficient (MolLogP), total polar surface area (Mol_TPSA), polarizability (MolMR), van der Waals surface area, and topological atomic connectivity. Understanding the role of these descriptors not only boosts model performance but also provides valuable insights into the molecular determinants of solubility. These findings demonstrate that the proposed ML method significantly enhances the accuracy of predictive modeling for physical properties. Moreover, it facilitates automated modeling and feature selection for new chemical data, offering a clearer understanding of the principles underlying solubility predictions. This advancement holds the potential to drive the discovery of new materials and improve drug development processes.

Results and discussion

Data overview

An appropriate dataset is essential for constructing a reliable prediction model. We compiled our dataset from well-curated and experimentally validated literature sources, ensuring structural diversity across a broad range of compounds. The dataset consists of 3,745 aqueous solubility measurements, including small organic molecules, drugs, and drug-like species, collected from four external sources. Specifically, it includes 1,129 drug-like compounds from the ESOL (Estimated SOLubility) dataset by Delaney [16], 1291 organic compounds from Huuskonen [38], 829 drug-like compounds from Lovric et al. [39], and 496 compounds from Willighagen et al. [40].

Figure 1 illustrates the distribution of the aqueous solubility dataset from these external sources. The solubility values, expressed as log units of molar solubility (mol/L), range from − 12 to + 2, representing aqueous solubilities measured at 20–25 °C. LogS quantifies the aqueous solubility of a compound, indicating how much of the compound can dissolve in water under specific conditions of temperature and pH. A lower logS value signifies lower solubility in water, while a higher value reflects greater solubility. Compounds with 0 and higher solubility value are highly soluble, those in the range of 0 to − 2 are soluble, those in the range of − 2 to − 4 are slightly soluble and insoluble if less than − 4. Notably, around 85% of drugs exhibit logS values between − 1 and − 5, with virtually no values below − 6 [14, 15]. This range appears to balance the polarity needed for adequate aqueous solubility and the hydrophobicity required for membrane permeability [41]. Most of the collected data fall within this target range, which is typically associated with pharmacological efficacy. Feature engineering

In this study, we employed the SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) notation [42, 43] to represent chemical structures for the input dataset. While SMILES provides a concise, text-based representation of molecular structures, its use of special characters (e.g., parentheses, numbers) and complex combinations (e.g., ring structures) poses challenges for direct processing by ML models. To address these limitations, we utilized molecular fingerprints (FPs) from the RDKit library [44]. We incorporated two types of features to build our ML model: molecular fingerprints and physicochemical molecular descriptors. Specifically, 2,727 bits of fingerprints, including Morgan (2048 bits) [45], MACCS keys (167 bits) [46], and Avalon (512 bits), as well as 49 molecular descriptors, were utilized for all compounds.

The Morgan fingerprint, also known as extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFPs) [47], is developed based on circular environments around each atom. It encompasses the connectivity of atoms (e.g. elements, neighbors, hydrogen counts, charges, isotopes, ring counts) and chemical features such as donor, acceptor, aromatics, halogens, basic, and acidic groups. The Morgan fingerprint allows adjustment of the size of atomic environments through a radius parameter, and in this study, we used a radius of 2 to capture both local and global structural information in a balanced manner. MACCS keys provide a powerful representation by encoding 166 predefined structural patterns or fragments commonly observed in organic molecules into 167-bit strings. These keys indicate the presence of specific functional groups, ring systems, and atomic combinations, and have been extensively validated in structure–activity relationship studies of drug-like molecules. The Avalon fingerprint, similar to the Morgan fingerprint, focuses on atomic bonds, distance paths, hydrogen counts, and ring paths, additionally including atom pairs, atom triplets, and extended connectivity patterns to capture topological properties of molecules. The combination of these three fingerprints captures complementary aspects of molecular structures, improving predictive performance compared to using individual fingerprints alone.

For physicochemical descriptor calculation, we computed 49 molecular descriptors reflecting 1D, 2D, and 3D chemical properties. Generating stable conformers, which represent specific arrangements of atoms in three-dimensional space, can be challenging for complex molecules. To address this, we used RDKit's ETKDG (Experimental Torsion Distance Geometry with basic Knowledge) algorithm [48] to generate initial 3D structures, generated up to 50 conformers per molecule, and performed energy minimization using the MMFF94 (Merck Molecular Force Field) force field [49]. The lowest energy conformer was selected for 3D descriptor calculation, and for molecules where conformer generation failed (less than 5% of the entire dataset), only 2D descriptors were utilized. To handle multiple values for 3D descriptors (e.g., dihedral angles, surface areas), we developed a multidimensional data frame structure that aggregates values using means and standard deviations, in contrast to the single values typically associated with 2D descriptors.

For the feature integration and preprocessing process, we implemented a standardized protocol to effectively combine 2,727 fingerprint bits and 49 molecular descriptors. Binary features (fingerprints) maintained their 0 or 1 values. This ensured that features with different scales could contribute equally to model training. The combined feature vector was structured as 2,776 dimensions (FP_bits(2,727) + Descriptors(49) dimensions). This preprocessing was performed using only training data statistics to prevent data leakage into validation and test sets.

ML model development

Prior research in solubility prediction has utilized computational approaches spanning statistical methodologies (multilinear regression, MLR), conventional machine learning techniques (RR, SVM, RFR, GSE), and neural network architectures. Within neural network methodologies, traditional artificial neural networks (ANNs) employing shallow architectures have generally shown only moderate predictive performance. Although these methods exhibit some predictive capability, their performance is often constrained by architectural limitations. Statistical approaches such as MLR are restricted to linear relationships, and shallow ANNs with limited hidden layers cannot adequately capture complex nonlinear patterns. These limitations motivated our implementation of deep neural networks (DNNs), a specialized subset of ANNs characterized by multiple hidden layers, for comparative evaluation. DNN models, distinguished from conventional shallow ANNs through architectural depth, not only extract and represent complex hierarchical information from datasets but also generate lower-dimensional latent representations. These latent spaces serve multiple purposes including complex data pattern visualization, synthetic data generation, and anomaly detection. We assessed DNN model performance using two distinct optimization strategies: one incorporating hyperparameter optimization (HPO) exclusively, and another utilizing the Automated Network Optimizer (ANO), which simultaneously optimizes both features and hyperparameters.

The ANO model, while introducing additional complexity beyond the fundamental multi-layer structural advantages of DNNs, emphasizes critical selection of optimized descriptors. Selected descriptors account for inherent data variance and errors, yielding enhanced performance outcomes that further improve upon baseline DNN capabilities. Our methodology incorporates rigorous model optimization procedures conducted across two rounds within the ANO framework, resulting in substantial performance improvements compared to basic DNN implementations. Furthermore, our investigation revealed that using either the full descriptor set or only a small subset of top-ranking descriptors reduces the ability to effectively identify and distinguish noisy data or subtle variations. This effect was observed consistently across different architectural approaches, from linear methods such as MLR to shallow ANNs and our DNN implementation. As a result, this limitation leads to reduced R2 values and degrades overall predictive performance, regardless of the specific computational method employed.

Figure 2 illustrates our study workflow, outlining three stages of the machine learning pipeline: data curation, network optimization, and prediction outcomes. Network optimization emerges as the central process in this investigation, intricately integrating feature selection and HPO. For feature selection optimization, we adopted an "exploration-based" methodology, systematically investigating 49 distinct combinations derived from available features. This comprehensive exploration addressed an extensive search space containing 249 potential combinations. This approach prioritizes variance and bias minimization during optimization procedures, ultimately maximizing overall DNN model performance. A notable limitation of this methodology involves significant computational time and resources required for thorough exploration of multiple feature combinations. To address this challenge, we implemented specialized optimization techniques, including vectorization and logarithmic transformations, for each of the 49 features. Hyperparameter optimization (HPO) was conducted using Bayesian optimization (BO) with Tree-structured Parzen Estimators (TPEs) within the Optuna framework, a widely adopted AutoML platform. BO, as a sequential model-based optimization algorithm, leverages results from previous iterations to intelligently identify promising hyperparameter candidates, consistently seeking improved scores over earlier iterations. This systematic approach enables our DNN-based ANO implementation to surpass performance limitations inherent in basic DNN architectures and previous statistical methodologies.

ANO model performance

Data split method

The reliability of machine learning models in molecular property prediction fundamentally depends on appropriate data partitioning strategies. To address this critical aspect, we implemented nine complementary splitting approaches using the Advanced Data Splitter framework, each designed to evaluate different aspects of model generalization capability. All datasets were partitioned maintaining an 80:20 ratio for training and test sets, respectively. Our splitting strategies encompass: (i) stratified random split (rm), providing baseline performance metrics with balanced solubility distribution; (ii) enhanced scaffold split (sc), ensuring structural novelty through Bemis-Murcko framework separation; (iii) hierarchical cluster split (cl), maximizing chemical space separation via Ward linkage clustering; (iv) chemical space coverage split (cs), employing MaxMin diversity selection; (v) time-series split (ti), simulating prospective applications through complexity-ordered allocation; (vi) physicochemical split (pc), utilizing property-based PCA clustering; (vii) activity cliff split (ac), targeting challenging structure–activity relationships; (viii) solubility-aware split (sa), incorporating domain-specific measurement challenges; and (ix) ensemble split (en), optimizing multi-method integration.

The stratified random splitting approach distributes molecules stochastically while preserving solubility distribution balance across predefined bins (very poor < -6, poor -6 to -4, moderate -4 to -2, good -2 to 0, excellent > 0 log S). This enhanced conventional method resulted in a Tanimoto similarity of 0.307 (± 0.15), providing a robust statistical baseline. While maintaining distributional properties, such moderate overlap serves as an appropriate reference point for evaluating more challenging splitting strategies.

The enhanced scaffold-based partitioning employs Bemis-Murcko decomposition with size-balanced allocation to prevent extreme imbalances while maintaining scaffold integrity. Despite enforcing strict scaffold exclusivity between training and test sets, the observed Tanimoto similarity (0.261 ± 0.16) achieved good separation levels. This result indicates that while core molecular frameworks differ substantially, the method successfully balances structural novelty with statistical power requirements.

The hierarchical cluster splitting methodology demonstrated exceptional performance in creating chemically distinct partitions. Through Ward linkage clustering of Morgan fingerprints followed by maximum distance-based cluster assignment, this approach achieved the lowest Tanimoto similarity (0.139 ± 0.15). The substantial reduction in structural overlap validates this method's effectiveness as the most stringent test of model extrapolation capability, challenging models to generalize across maximum chemical space gaps.

Chemical space coverage splitting employs the Kennard-Stone MaxMin algorithm, combining molecular fingerprints (70% weight) with key physicochemical properties (30% weight) to ensure optimal diversity in test sets. This balanced approach yielded a Tanimoto similarity of 0.326 (± 0.13), demonstrating effective coverage across both structural and property dimensions while maintaining chemical relevance.

The activity cliff splitting strategy specifically targets challenging prediction scenarios by identifying structurally similar molecules with significantly different solubility values using the Structure–Activity Landscape Index (SALI). With preferential allocation of cliff molecules to test sets (maximum 30%), this method achieved a Tanimoto similarity of 0.229 (± 0.12), successfully creating demanding validation conditions that probe model robustness at structure–activity relationship boundaries.

Temporal splitting, implemented through BertzCT complexity-based ordering when publication dates were unavailable, yielded intermediate separation (0.325 ± 0.14). This approach simulates the practical challenge of predicting properties for newly synthesized, potentially more complex compounds based on historical training data, providing realistic prospective validation scenarios.

The solubility-aware splitting incorporates domain-specific knowledge by calculating compound-specific difficulty scores based on measurement uncertainty factors (aggregation tendency, polymorphism risk, pH sensitivity, temperature effects). This specialized approach resulted in a Tanimoto similarity of 0.301 (± 0.14), reflecting the practical challenges encountered in experimental solubility determination.

The ensemble splitting method employs differential evolution optimization to determine optimal weights for combining multiple splitting strategies, achieving a Tanimoto similarity of 0.265 (± 0.13). Through multi-criteria optimization balancing chemical space coverage, physicochemical diversity, and measurement challenges, this approach provides comprehensive model validation.

The physicochemical property split utilizes 17 + solubility-relevant descriptors with correlation-based feature selection, PCA dimensionality reduction, and agglomerative clustering, yielding a Tanimoto similarity of 0.326 (± 0.12). This method ensures property space coverage while maintaining interpretability through chemically meaningful descriptor selection.

The remarkable consistency of similarity values within the 0.139—0.326 range across all splitting methods confirms their collective effectiveness in preventing data leakage while maintaining chemical relevance. Notably, all methods achieved "good" or "excellent" separation levels according to established validation criteria (< 0.4), with the variation in similarity scores demonstrating that each method poses distinct generalization challenges. The hierarchical cluster splitting's superior separation (0.139), combined with activity cliff splitting's challenge targeting (0.229) and ensemble optimization's balanced approach (0.265), ensures robust assessment of model applicability domains across diverse deployment scenarios. This comprehensive evaluation framework directly addresses fundamental concerns regarding overoptimistic performance estimates in molecular machine learning, providing realistic expectations and confidence intervals for deployment in pharmaceutical discovery pipelines.

Application to molecular solubility prediction

The synthetic experiments translate directly to practical gains. On the Delaney ESOL dataset for aqueous solubility, standard hyperparameter optimization achieved R2 = 0.985 using Bayesian Optimization. Further improvements through dual optimization (simultaneously optimizing features and hyperparameters) pushed performance to R2 = 0.991 while identifying 21—28 key molecular descriptors from an initial pool of 49. Although a 0.6% absolute improvement may appear modest, in pharmaceutical development such refinements translate into substantial cost savings.

Performance assessment and molecular descriptor analysis

Model performance was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R2), mean squared error (MSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean square error (RMSE). To ensure a fair and consistent comparison across models, all comparative algorithms (RR, SVR, RFR, MLP, and the standard DNN) and ANO were evaluated under an identical experimental protocol. Specifically, all models used the same input representation, consisting of 2727-bit fingerprints concatenated with 49 physicochemical descriptors, and the same preprocessing procedure, in which fingerprint features were retained in binary form while continuous descriptors were standardized using statistics computed only from the training set. For each dataset, we generated fixed train, validation, and test indices once and reused the identical splits across all models. Accordingly, all metrics reported were computed on the same held-out test set.

Hyperparameter tuning for each baseline model was performed exclusively on the training and validation data using predefined search spaces (Methods "Baseline models and comparative evaluation protocol" section). During tuning, we applied the same evaluation metric (R2) and the same cross-validation protocol to avoid optimistic estimates. After selecting the optimal configuration, each final model was trained using the training data and evaluated on the corresponding test set. Feature importance analysis and SHAP-based interpretation

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis

To better understand the influence of physicochemical molecular descriptors on the prediction of intrinsic solubility, we conducted feature importance analysis using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), an explainable AI tool [50]. SHAP analysis provides insights into the relative importance of individual features in predicting compound solubility, offering a deeper understanding of the internal workings of the ML model. SHAP values quantify the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions, providing valuable information for feature engineering in HPO. Higher SHAP values indicate more significant contributions to prediction accuracy. Plots of the mean absolute SHAP values (mean|SHAP|) highlight the overall influence of each feature on the predictions, demonstrating strong correlations with the chemical descriptors applied to the data. Figure 7 shows the top 20 most important chemical descriptors for predicting aqueous solubility, based on their average SHAP values for four external datasets.Fig. 7SHAP analysis of the ANO model trained on molecular descriptors: the average SHAP values, showing the impact of each descriptor on solubility predictions for each dataset: a WS496, b Delaney, c Lovric, and d Huuskonen

SHAP analysis of the ANO model trained on molecular descriptors: the average SHAP values, showing the impact of each descriptor on solubility predictions for each dataset: a WS496, b Delaney, c Lovric, and d Huuskonen

From the SHAP value analysis, molecular properties that significantly influence solubility prediction include MW (molecular weight), MolMR (molecular polarizability), TPSA (total polar surface area), and MolLogP (partition coefficient ratio of substances in water to octanol solvent). On the other hand, the top molecular features slightly differ in order across datasets. PEOE_VSA (partial equal O-atom-weighted van der Waals surface area) is ranked as a key molecular property for the WS496 dataset, as it calculates a molecule’s van der Waals surface area, specifically focusing on oxygen atoms for hydrogen bonding and interactions with environments to dissolve. In addition, Labute_ASA (Labute Accessible Surface Area) and valence electrons are also highlighted as significant for Lovrics and Delaney dataset, respectively. Labute_ASA is associated with a compound's solubility, as a larger accessible surface area typically correlates with improved solvation and increased solubility in aqueous media. Valence electrons contribute to the overall charge distribution in a molecule, as charged or partially charged sites on a molecule can lead to favorable electrostatic interactions with solvent molecules, thus increasing solubility. Furthermore, data-specific series of features appear for each dataset: SlogP_VSA (Surface area weighted by logP_van der Waals surface area) for the Lovric dataset. It combines information about lipophilicity with the van der Waals surface area of a compound, providing the molecular interactions with biological membranes and its potential solubility in different environments and predicting how well a compound might permeate cell membranes or remain soluble in aqueous solutions. SMR_VSA (scaled molecular refractivity-weighted van der Waals surface area) for the Delaney dataset. It captures the geometric and spatial characteristics of a molecule. It is valuable for optimizing the three-dimensional characteristics of compounds, ensuring that they have the appropriate properties for effective interaction with biological systems while maintaining a balance between hydroPhilicity and lipoPhilicity. VSA_Estate (Volume-Surface Area Estate) quantifies the polar surface area of a molecule, providing insights into its hydroPhilicity and potential interactions with solvents [51] for the [51] dataset.

From the feature important analysis, we also found that solubility is inversely correlated with molecule size and molecular polarizability. This relationship arises because solute molecules must overcome strong intermolecular attractions (e.g., van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding) to interact with solvent molecules. As MW increases, more energy is needed to overcome intermolecular forces, reducing the likelihood of solute dissolution. This is also true for MolMR (molecular polarizability), where stronger intermolecular interactions, particularly in molecules with a dipole moment, can reduce solubility. In addition, this aligns with MolLogP, commonly known as the partition coefficient. It assesses a compound’s distribution between an organic solvent (typically octanol) and water based on molecular descriptors. This measure provides insight into a compound’s lipophilicity or hydrophobicity, describing its tendency to dissolve in nonpolar solvents rather than polar solvents like water. A higher LogP indicates increased lipophilicity, suggesting the compound is more likely to remain in nonpolar environments and thus may exhibit lower solubility in aqueous solutions. While most of the top features exhibit a negative correlation with molecular solubility, TPSA shows a positive correlation, as it reflects a molecule's ability to interact with the solvent, especially in aqueous environments. Higher TPSA often indicates a greater number of polar functional groups such as hydroxyl, amine, and carboxyl groups, which can interact favorably with water molecules. This increases the molecule's hydrophilicity, facilitating dissolution. Molecules with a larger TPSA can form more hydrogen bonds with the solvent. These interactions can stabilize the solute in solution, promoting solubility. Furthermore, certain topological properties, such as NumHeteroatoms(number of heteroatoms), NOCount (number of nitrogen and oxygen atoms), NumHAcceptors(number of hydrogen bond acceptors), and numHDonor (number of hydrogen bond donors), can positively impact solubility by enhancing interactions with the solvent, potentially resulting in higher solubility. Figure S1 presents the correlations between specific chemical features and logS values.

In addition to the qualitative descriptor-level interpretations above, the SHAP patterns can be further contextualized using established solubility frameworks. From a thermodynamic standpoint, intrinsic solubility reflects a balance between the energetic cost of disrupting solute–solute interactions in the solid phase and the stabilization gained from solute–solvent interactions in water. In this regard, the systematic negative SHAP contributions associated with larger and more polarizable molecules are consistent with an increased propensity for cohesive intermolecular interactions and crystal packing, which effectively raises the energetic barrier to dissolution. This interpretation aligns with the General Solubility Equation view, in which solubility tends to decrease when lipophilicity increases and when the effective lattice contribution becomes more dominant.

Moreover, the descriptor groups that contribute positively to solubility can be interpreted through the Hansen solubility parameter perspective. Because water exhibits strong polar and hydrogen-bonding character, features that enhance polar and hydrogen-bonding compatibility can increase the stabilizing solvation free energy and thereby improve aqueous solubility. Conversely, features capturing extensive nonpolar surface exposure or strongly hydrophobic surface character indicate a reduced match to water’s polar and hydrogen-bonding components, leading to less favorable solvation and lower solubility. Overall, these theory-guided interpretations support that the SHAP-identified descriptors are not merely statistical correlates but reflect physically meaningful drivers of intrinsic solubility.

The distribution of data in relation to the Rule of Five across the datasets

According to the rule, a compound is more likely to be an orally active drug if it meets the following criteria: first, it should have no more than 5 hydrogen bond donors, particularly emphasizing hydroxyl and amine groups; second, it should have no more than 10 hydrogen bond acceptors, typically involving oxygen and nitrogen atoms. Additionally, the compound should have a molecular weight of less than 500 Da and a calculated LogP not exceeding 5. This set of guidelines is established to assess the drug likeness of potential pharmaceutical compounds and provides a straightforward approach for predicting the solubility and permeability of compounds based on their molecular properties. We also analyzed the TPSA as a key factor, and most datasets align with the established guidelines.

We further examined model residuals after grouping compounds into solubility bins (very poor < − 6, poor − 6 to − 4, moderate − 4 to − 2, good − 2 to 0, and excellent > 0), following the same binning scheme used in the stratified splitting procedure. This analysis indicates that the “very poor” region (LogS < − 6) is represented by relatively few samples and is therefore a particularly challenging regime, where both limited data coverage and elevated experimental variability can affect predictive reliability. Consistent with this observation, the magnitude of prediction errors tends to increase in this extreme tail compared with the more densely populated central range (approximately − 1 to − 5), which contains the majority of drug-like compounds. Accordingly, we explicitly note that predictions for compounds in the LogS < − 6 region should be interpreted with lower confidence, and we identify improved modeling of this sparse, low-solubility tail as an important limitation of the current work and a key direction for future research.

To further validate the reliability of the model, we conducted a comprehensive analysis based on the applicability domain (AD), which was defined through the integration of structural similarity and descriptor-space distance. The thresholds for the AD were established using percentile-based rules derived from the training distribution, specifically utilizing the 5th percentile for k-nearest-neighbor Tanimoto similarity and the 95th percentile for Mahalanobis distance.

In the Delaney dataset, approximately 94.5% of the test compounds were identified as falling within the AD. For these in-domain compounds, the model maintained high predictive accuracy with an R2 of 0.991. Conversely, molecules flagged as being outside the AD primarily consisted of those with extreme low solubility, specifically where LogS < − 6, or those characterized by complex and bulky ring systems. These out-of-domain molecules exhibited significantly higher absolute residuals that often exceeded 3%. This quantitative distinction confirms that while the ANO model is highly robust for drug-like chemical spaces, predictions for compounds situated outside the defined AD should be interpreted with lower confidence.

Overall, this study reaffirms the established relationship between molecular structure and properties, as defined by the QSPR. Developing advanced ML models that can effectively differentiate factors affecting solubility between molecules holds significant potential. Such advancements could narrow the gap between model predictions and experimental data, enhancing the reliability and predictive power of these models. While acknowledging the current model’s limitations, this study demonstrates the feasibility of constructing a practical solubility-predicting model for a structurally diverse set of drug compounds using DNN modeling with feature optimization.

Conclusion

This study investigates the prediction of drug molecule solubility through two developed models: a DNN model optimized solely with hyperparameters (HPs) and an ANO model incorporating both feature engineering and HP optimization. The ANO model demonstrates superior performance, with high predictive accuracy across diverse datasets. Additionally, the regression model offers insights into the relationships between specific features and solubility, underscoring the importance of MW, molecular polarizability, refractivity, total polar surface area, and van der Waals surface area. The best results were achieved on the Delaney ESOL dataset using a combination of three fingerprints and 49 molecular descriptors, yielding an R2 score of 0.991 for predicting solubility in drug-like compounds. A dual-round optimization process was implemented to identify the best models, considering performance and feature count. This study also examined the impact of molecular features on the ML model’s predictions through SHAP analysis, uncovering correlations between features and their influence on predicting intrinsic solubility. The integration of the ANO model with feature analysis represents a promising direction for future research, providing insights into the complex interactions between molecular properties and solubility. We note that predictive reliability is reduced in sparsely represented regions of the data distribution, particularly at extreme low-solubility values (e.g., LogS < − 6), as well as for molecules that fall outside the defined applicability domain. Expanding the availability of high-quality measurements in these challenging regimes and incorporating uncertainty-aware learning strategies therefore represent important and promising directions for future work.

Pruning strategies and caching system

To enhance optimization efficiency and reduce computational cost, we implemented a multi-tiered pruning strategy within the ANO framework combining median-based pruning with aggressive early stopping rules. The median pruner algorithm maintains a running history of trial performances at each evaluation checkpoint and automatically terminates trials performing below the median of historical trials at the same step, effectively focusing computational resources on promising hyperparameter regions while maintaining exploration diversity. This approach offers several advantages: it adapts dynamically to the performance distribution without requiring predetermined thresholds, remains robust to outliers unlike mean-based methods, and balances exploration with exploitation by allowing sufficient initial trials before pruning begins. We augmented this baseline strategy with aggressive custom termination rules based on absolute performance thresholds: immediate termination for trials with R2 < − 10.0 in the first fold (indicating fundamental model failure), termination when cumulative average R2 < − 5.0 after multiple folds (persistent poor performance), and conditional pruning when average R2 < 0.0 based on the median pruner's recommendation. Additionally, we implemented an intelligent caching system using NPZ format for fingerprints (uncompressed for speed) and compressed PKL format for molecular descriptors, organized in a hierarchical directory structure with automatic cache invalidation through configuration flags. The cache system maintains separate stores for fingerprints (2727 bits) and chemical descriptors (up to 882 additional features), enabling rapid feature retrieval across modules. Cache files include metadata for version control and are shared across optimization modules to prevent redundant calculations. The combination of aggressive pruning and intelligent caching prevents wasted computation on failing models while ensuring thorough exploration of promising hyperparameter spaces, crucial for the high-dimensional optimization problem inherent in our dual optimization framework.

Dual cross-validation strategy

We implemented a dual cross-validation approach to balance rigorous research validation with practical deployment assessment, addressing the common discrepancy between academic performance metrics and real-world applicability. Type 1 (Research Pipeline) performs K-fold cross-validation exclusively on training data, with each fold independently predicting the held-out test set, providing an unbiased performance estimate that serves as the primary optimization target for the ANO framework. This approach ensures no data leakage and maintains statistical rigor required for scientific publication. Type 2 (Production Pipeline) splits training data into train/validation subsets for cross-validation, then trains the final model on the complete training set before a single evaluation on the hold-out test set, simulating real-world deployment scenarios where the model must generalize to truly unseen data.

ANO algorithm validation

The accurate molecular solubility prediction requires advanced computational strategies capable of navigating the intricate landscape of molecular descriptors. Our study introduces a Bayesian optimization-based feature selection methodology within the Automated Network Optimization (ANO) framework, designed to systematically identify and prioritize the most informative molecular properties while eliminating redundancies.

To ensure that the final model retained only the most predictive descriptors, ANO’s Feature Optimization (FO) stage conducted multiple Bayesian optimization trials. Each iteration refined the feature set based on two guiding principles: (1) descriptors that minimally contributed to predictive performance were excluded, and (2) consistently high-impact descriptors were preferentially retained. This process led to a carefully curated subset of 24–28 descriptors, achieving an optimal R2 score of 0.988 and demonstrating the critical importance of precise feature selection.

By systematically refining the descriptor set, the framework reduces computational redundancy, minimizes overfitting, and improves the generalizability of the model across diverse molecular datasets. Baseline models and comparative evaluation protocol

Baseline hyperparameters were tuned using the training and validation data only within predefined search spaces: RR (alpha), SVR (C, epsilon, gamma), RFR (n_estimators, max_depth, min_samples_split), and MLP (hidden_layer_sizes, alpha, learning_rate). Optimization was performed with Optuna (TPE sampler) under a matched evaluation budget, and the best configuration was selected by five-fold cross-validation on the combined training and validation data. Final performance was reported on the held-out test set. To ensure a fair comparison, all baseline models and the standard DNN used the same features and identical data splits as ANO, with continuous descriptors standardized using training-set statistics and concatenated with binary fingerprints.

Applicability domain estimation

The applicability domain (AD) was defined using a dual criterion based on structural similarity and descriptor-space distance. For each query, we computed k-nearest-neighbor (k = 5) Tanimoto similarities to the training set in fingerprint space and the Mahalanobis distance in standardized continuous descriptor space. Percentile-based thresholds were derived from the training distribution, and compounds exceeding the distance threshold or lacking sufficient similarity support were flagged as outside-domain.
