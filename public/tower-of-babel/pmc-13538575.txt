Enhanced lung cancer detection using BiLSTM attention and transformer models on clinical text data
Authors: Bodicherla Siva Sankar, D Natarajasivan, M Purushotham Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer continues to be a major cause of death globally, making early and precise detection crucial for improving patient outcomes. In this research, we introduce an enhanced method for predicting and detecting lung cancer by combining Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks with an attention mechanism and transformer-based models. This hybrid model effectively tackles common issues such as overfitting, data imbalance, and long-sequence dependencies in textual clinical data. A well-curated dataset of patient medical records, including demographics, symptoms, radiology reports, and other clinical information, was used to assess the model’s performance. The experimental results showed a marked improvement in prediction accuracy, with the proposed model achieving 99.8% accuracy and significantly lowering validation loss to 0.3112, outperforming existing systems. The incorporation of attention mechanisms with BiLSTM and transformer models improved the model’s ability to focus on critical clinical features, thus enhancing its generalization capabilities. Additionally, the model’s faster training time (13.99 s) demonstrates its practicality for clinical use, particularly in resource-constrained settings. While the results are promising, potential overfitting due to the small dataset suggests further research with larger datasets and advanced regularization methods is needed. Introduction

Background

Lung cancer is a major global health issue, being the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Each year, it is responsible for about 1.8 million deaths, which constitutes nearly 18% of all cancer fatalities1. The high mortality rate is primarily due to late diagnosis, where limited treatment options and poor survival rates become prevalent. In Canada, lung cancer continues to be the leading cause of cancer mortality, surpassing deaths from breast, colorectal, and prostate cancers combined2. This imposes a significant emotional and financial strain on individuals, families, and healthcare systems, underlining the urgent need for improved methods of early detection. Detecting lung cancer at an early stage can drastically reduce mortality, as treatment is more effective when the disease is diagnosed early3. One of the major obstacles in early lung cancer detection is the absence of specific symptoms in its early stages and the complexity of clinical data. Patients often exhibit generic symptoms like coughing, weight loss, and shortness of breath, which are common in many other respiratory diseases4. Furthermore, the sheer volume of clinical records, radiology reports, and diagnostic results adds to the difficulty in accurately identifying high-risk patients5. Advanced deep learning methods, especially those that can process large amounts of clinical text data, offer a promising solution to enhance the accuracy of early detection6.

These clinical cues match the symptom fields extracted from the records and were used as key predictive features for our BiLSTM-Attention and Transformer models.

Problem statement

Traditional machine learning models face challenges when it comes to processing and interpreting large text-based datasets such as clinical reports. Classical algorithms like Decision Trees, Support Vector Machines, and basic Neural Networks tend to struggle with the complexity of unstructured clinical text, which often contains nuanced medical language and evolving patient conditions7. These models rely heavily on manual feature extraction and shallow learning approaches, limiting their ability to capture the broader context within medical narratives. As a result, important indicators of lung cancer may be missed, leading to delayed or inaccurate diagnoses8. Another key limitation of traditional models is their inability to scale effectively with large datasets. Clinical data is vast, including diverse elements like demographics, symptoms, radiology results, and medical histories9. As dataset size increases, traditional models struggle with performance degradation because they are not equipped to capture long-term dependencies or contextual relationships within sequential text data10. This limitation highlights the need for more advanced methods, such as Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks and transformer models, which are better suited for handling the complexity and scale of medical text data11.

Research gap

Despite advancements in machine learning, models like recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and transformers still present challenges when applied to healthcare text data. One prominent issue is overfitting, where a model performs well on training data but fails to generalize to new, unseen data12. This problem is especially common when using limited clinical datasets, which often arise due to privacy concerns and difficulties in collecting comprehensive data13. Overfitting compromises the reliability of the model in real-world scenarios, reducing its effectiveness in critical areas like lung cancer detection14. A further challenge involves managing long sequence dependencies within clinical records. Healthcare text data often includes lengthy, detailed narratives of patient history, symptoms, and treatments15. Traditional RNNs and basic LSTMs struggle to capture these long-term dependencies due to the vanishing gradient problem, leading to a loss of important context from earlier parts of the text16. This negatively impacts prediction accuracy. To address this, more sophisticated models like BiLSTM with attention mechanisms and transformer-based models, such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), are required to effectively process long text sequences while maintaining contextual relevance17.

Objective

The primary objective of this study is to improve the accuracy and reliability of lung cancer prediction and detection by using advanced deep learning techniques, specifically BiLSTM with attention mechanisms and transformer-based models like BERT18. These models are designed to address the limitations of traditional machine learning techniques by efficiently processing large text datasets and capturing complex relationships within clinical narratives19. BiLSTM networks provide the ability to process text in both directions, ensuring that the model captures both past and future contexts, which is crucial in understanding the complex nature of clinical reports20. Additionally, transformer-based models like BERT introduce self-attention mechanisms that excel at capturing long-range dependencies in text, making them highly effective in managing unstructured data like clinical reports21. By leveraging these cutting-edge techniques, this study aims to overcome challenges such as overfitting, data imbalance, and long-sequence dependencies, ultimately improving the accuracy and timeliness of lung cancer detection22. The results of this research could provide more reliable and efficient diagnostic tools, aiding clinicians in making informed and timely decisions for patient care23.

Preprocessing

Data preprocessing is a critical step in preparing the dataset for machine learning models. Handling missing values is a common requirement in clinical datasets, and imputation techniques were employed based on the type of data (e.g., mean imputation for numerical fields, mode imputation for categorical fields)20. Text data was tokenized, breaking down unstructured clinical narratives into words or tokens suitable for input into the model. Further preprocessing involved removing stopwords and applying text normalization methods such as lowercasing and stemming to reduce dimensionality19. To address class imbalance, where non-cancer cases are more frequent than cancer cases, techniques such as oversampling the minority class (lung cancer cases) and undersampling the majority class were used18. This ensured the model could learn effectively from both classes without bias. Data augmentation was also applied to increase the size of the minority class by creating synthetic examples, further enhancing the model’s ability to generalize across the dataset. To ensure effective handling of clinical text data, we applied a robust normalization pipeline including lowercasing, lemmatization, and stopword removal using the SpaCy library. Furthermore, medical abbreviations were expanded using the Unified Medical Language System (UMLS) to preserve domain-specific context. In addition, the BERT component of our model was fine-tuned using biomedical text, incorporating BioBERT vocabulary embeddings to enhance semantic understanding in clinical narratives. These preprocessing steps improved both the interpretability and the performance of the model in real-world applications.

Model architecture

The core architecture in this study is based on Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks, augmented with an attention mechanism17. BiLSTM allows the model to process text in both forward and backward directions, enabling it to capture context from the entire sequence. This is especially useful in medical narratives, where key information may be scattered throughout the report16. The attention mechanism enhances the model’s performance by enabling it to focus on the most relevant parts of the input, improving prediction accuracy and interpretability. Alongside BiLSTM, transformer-based models such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) were utilized15. BERT’s self-attention mechanism excels at managing long text sequences and maintaining context, making it ideal for handling large amounts of unstructured clinical text. By combining the strengths of BiLSTM and BERT, the model is able to capture both short-term dependencies and long-range relationships, offering a more robust approach to lung cancer prediction.

Initially, raw documents are tokenized and embedded into continuous vectors (Eq. (1)), with positional information optionally added using sinusoidal encodings (Eq. (2)). (3)), governed internally by gated operations as described in Eq. (4). The outputs are concatenated to produce a unified contextual state (Eq. (5)). An additive attention mechanism then computes token importance scores (Eq. (6)), normalizes them into probability weights (Eq. (7)), and aggregates them into a weighted context vector (Eq. (8)). This final sequence representation is passed through a dense projection layer with sigmoid activation to estimate the probability of the target class (Eq. (9)). Our model first reads each clinical note in both directions with a BiLSTM to capture surrounding context, then uses an attention layer to highlight the most informative words and phrases for easier interpretation. A Transformer encoder then builds on this signal to model long-range relationships and richer semantics, and the combined representation feeds a single classifier tuned for lung-cancer prediction. The designed the BiLSTM-Attention-Transformer to scale in both compute and clinic. In our setup, training took about 13.99 s per epoch, showing it runs efficiently on mid-range GPUs or modest CPU clusters-no exotic hardware required. The modular late-fusion design also makes it straightforward to grow: you can distribute training across nodes or retrain incrementally as new data arrives. For deployment, the model can be containerized (e.g., TensorFlow Serving or ONNX Runtime) and integrated with EHR systems, though practical hurdles remain-privacy compliance, IT integration, and uneven hospital hardware. To address these, we outline federated learning for privacy-preserving training and model quantization to cut inference latency, improving scalability and real-world feasibility across diverse clinical settings.

Training procedure

The models were trained using an 80–20 split, with 80% of the data used for training and 20% for validation. This ensures that the model learns from the majority of the data while being evaluated on unseen cases14. To prevent overfitting, early stopping was implemented, terminating the training when validation performance plateaued. The Adam optimizer was used due to its efficiency in handling large datasets and sparse gradients13. The loss function applied was binary cross-entropy, suited for binary classification tasks like lung cancer detection. It helped minimize the difference between predicted probabilities and true labels. Regularization techniques like dropout were also introduced to further reduce the likelihood of overfitting, ensuring the model could generalize well to new, unseen data.

Evaluation metrics

The model’s performance was assessed using several key metrics. Accuracy was the primary measure, indicating the overall correctness of the model’s predictions32. Precision (the proportion of correctly predicted positive cases), recall (the proportion of actual positive cases correctly identified), and the F1-score (which balances precision and recall) were also used to evaluate the model’s effectiveness, particularly in handling imbalanced data33. Additionally, the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC) was employed to evaluate the model’s ability to distinguish between lung cancer and non-cancer patients34. A high AUC-ROC score is crucial in medical diagnostics as it indicates a model’s capability to make correct classifications in a clinical setting. The performance of the proposed model is evaluated using a set of complementary metrics to ensure robustness and interpretability. Precision, recall, and F1-score are computed as in Eq. (17), while macro-F1 (Eq. (18)) provides a class-balanced evaluation by averaging across positive and negative classes. To further validate robustness under imbalance, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) is applied (Eq. (19)). Discriminative ability is measured by the area under the ROC curve (AUC) (Eq. Together, these evaluation metrics provide a comprehensive picture of model accuracy, calibration, and robustness in real-world medical decision-making contexts.

Handling overfitting and imbalance

Overfitting, a common issue in machine learning, was addressed through regularization and dropout techniques. These methods reduced the models’ tendency to overfit the training data, ensuring that they generalize well to new, unseen data. Additionally, early stopping was employed to halt training when improvements in the validation set stopped, further preventing overfitting. The models also effectively addressed the issue of class imbalance, which is common in medical datasets where healthy individuals outnumber patients with lung cancer. Oversampling of the minority class (lung cancer patients) and undersampling of the majority class were applied to balance the data, ensuring that the models would not be biased towards predicting the majority class. As a result, both the BiLSTM and transformer-based models showed improved performance on imbalanced datasets, which was evident in their high precision and recall values. Discussion

Interpretation of results

This study highlights the practical effectiveness of integrating Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks with attention mechanisms and transformer-based models for lung cancer prediction and detection. The use of BiLSTM with attention resulted in a notable improvement in capturing sequential patterns from clinical text data, leading to highly accurate predictions. This architecture allowed the model to focus on critical features within medical narratives, which is essential when dealing with unstructured and complex text data. Achieving a validation loss as low as 0.3112 and an accuracy of 100%, the model performed remarkably well, demonstrating that the hybrid approach effectively mitigates issues such as overfitting and data imbalance. The addition of transformer-based models, such as BERT, enabled the system to manage longer clinical text sequences while maintaining context over extended ranges, which is crucial in medical reports where key details may be dispersed. The attention mechanism further enhanced model performance by allowing it to emphasize important features, improving generalization and robustness. These results suggest that the proposed model not only improves lung cancer detection accuracy but also has valuable potential for clinical applications where processing large volumes of textual data is required efficiently and accurately.

Comparison with prior studies

In comparison to previous research on lung cancer detection, the hybrid BiLSTM-transformer model in this study offers superior performance, particularly in handling long-sequence dependencies and imbalanced datasets. Traditional machine learning models, such as decision trees, support vector machines, and basic neural networks, often struggled with unstructured clinical text data. For example, hybrid deep learning approaches used by Kesiku and Garcia-Zapirain1 and Nair et al.2 improved accuracy but did not achieve the same level of precision in processing complex medical narratives as seen in this study, largely due to the lack of attention mechanisms and transformer-based models. Additionally, transformer-based models like those used by Wang et al.3 excel at processing long sequences of text data from electronic health records. However, these models still faced challenges related to overfitting and generalization, especially with smaller datasets. The current study’s combination of BiLSTM and transformers addresses these challenges, offering a more balanced solution for lung cancer detection by handling both short- and long-term dependencies while effectively tackling overfitting and data imbalance—outperforming earlier approaches in these critical areas.

Limitations

Despite the promising results, certain limitations of this study should be acknowledged. One of the primary limitations is the relatively small dataset used for model training and validation. Although the dataset was well-curated and contained both structured and unstructured data, its limited size may have contributed to potential overfitting, which was addressed through techniques such as regularization and dropout. Expanding the dataset in future studies would likely improve the model’s generalization and allow for more comprehensive performance evaluations across various clinical settings. Another limitation is related to computational resources. Transformer-based models, like BERT, are known for their substantial computational requirements, which may present challenges in resource-limited environments, such as smaller healthcare facilities. Although this study demonstrated a fast training time of 13.99 s, deploying the model in real-world scenarios with larger datasets and more complex configurations might require significant computational power. Future research could focus on optimizing the model’s efficiency across different hardware settings to ensure broader clinical applicability. Furthermore, we conducted an ablation study to evaluate the contribution of each component in the proposed model architecture. The study involved systematically removing the BiLSTM layer, attention mechanism, and BERT module, and observing the resulting performance changes. As shown in Table 4, the complete model with all components achieved the best performance in terms of accuracy, F1-score, and AUC. Removing any one component led to a noticeable performance drop, underscoring the importance of each architectural element in achieving robust lung cancer prediction.

The validation metrics and their formulas used in this research.

Accuracy

Accuracy is the proportion of correctly predicted cases out of the total number of predictions made. In this study, the model reached 99.8% accuracy, indicating that all cases were classified correctly.

Precision

Precision represents the fraction of true positive predictions out of all positive predictions made.

A high precision value indicates that the model effectively avoids classifying non-cancer patients as having lung cancer (i.e., few false positives).

Recall (sensitivity)

Recall measures the proportion of actual positive cases that were correctly identified by the model. A high recall ensures that the model captures most of the true lung cancer cases, minimizing missed diagnoses.

F1-score

The F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, balancing the two metrics, especially when there is a trade-off. An F1-score of 1.0 in this research reflects that the model effectively balances precision and recall, achieving excellent performance in detecting lung cancer while minimizing false positives.

Validation loss

Validation loss measures how well the model performs on unseen data. In classification tasks like this one, binary cross-entropy is commonly used. Conclusion

This research demonstrates substantial progress in using machine learning for lung cancer detection, particularly with the development of a hybrid model combining Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks with attention mechanisms and transformer-based models like BERT. The model effectively captures both short- and long-term dependencies in clinical text data, leading to highly accurate predictions. Achieving 100% accuracy and significantly reducing validation loss to 0.3112, this model addresses common challenges in medical datasets, such as overfitting and class imbalance. Its hybrid design improves the interpretability and performance of lung cancer detection systems, providing practical solutions suitable for clinical use. The major contribution of this study is overcoming the limitations of earlier models, which struggled to manage long text sequences, unstructured data, and imbalanced datasets. The incorporation of attention mechanisms allowed the BiLSTM model to concentrate on the most important sections of clinical narratives, while transformer-based models like BERT excelled at processing longer and more complex text sequences. Together, these methods provided a powerful framework for lung cancer detection, outperforming traditional machine learning techniques. The model’s fast training time of 15.11 s further underscores its potential for use in real-world clinical environments with limited computational resources. For future work, the model’s generalizability can be improved by incorporating more diverse and larger datasets. Additionally, exploring federated learning for privacy-preserving data sharing between healthcare institutions could be a fruitful direction for enhancing model performance while maintaining patient confidentiality. This approach would enable broader access to insights from decentralized datasets without compromising data security. Furthermore, optimizing the model’s performance on different hardware setups would expand its applicability across a wider variety of clinical settings, from large research institutions to smaller healthcare facilities.

Limitations and future work

Bias, dataset integration, and future validation plans

We acknowledge that the current study utilizes a relatively homogeneous and limited dataset, which may introduce bias and limit generalizability. To address this, we are actively collaborating with two regional hospitals to collect a more diverse set of clinical records. This expanded dataset will allow us to validate the robustness of our hybrid model across varied demographics and clinical settings. Such efforts aim to mitigate dataset-specific biases and improve the system’s applicability in real-world, heterogeneous healthcare environments.
