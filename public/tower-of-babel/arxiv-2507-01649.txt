GradMetaNet : An Equivariant Architecture for Learning on Gradients
Yoav GelbergUniversity of Oxford yoav@robots.ox.ac.uk, Yam EitanTechnion yam.eitan@campus.technion.ac.il Aviv NavonIndependent ReseracherAviv ShamsianBar-Ilan UniversityTheo (Moe) PuttermanUC BerkeleyMichael BronsteinUniversity of Oxford, AITHYRAHaggai MaronTechnion/NVIDIA

Abstract
Gradients of neural networks encode valuable information for optimization, editing, and analysis of models. Therefore, practitioners often treat gradients as inputs to task-specific algorithms, e.g. for pruning or optimization. Recent works explore learning algorithms that operate directly on gradients but use architectures that are not specifically designed for gradient processing, limiting their applicability. In this paper, we present a principled approach for designing architectures that process gradients. Our approach is guided by three principles: (1) equivariant design that preserves neuron permutation symmetries, (2) processing sets of gradients across multiple data points to capture curvature information, and (3) efficient gradient representation through rank-1 decomposition. Based on these principles, we introduce GradMetaNet , a novel architecture for learning on gradients, constructed from simple equivariant blocks. We prove universality results for GradMetaNet , and show that previous approaches cannot approximate natural gradient-based functions that GradMetaNet can. We then demonstrate GradMetaNet ’s effectiveness on a diverse set of gradient-based tasks on MLPs and transformers, such as learned optimization, INR editing, and estimating loss landscape curvature.

Our approach.
In this paper, we introduce
GradMetaNet
, an architecture designed for learning on gradients of deep models such as
MLPs
and
transformers
.
GradMetaNet
’s design is guided by the following principles:
(1)
Respecting symmetries:
Neural parameter spaces exhibit inherent symmetries, leading to redundancies in gradient representations.
GradMetaNet
’s design is derived to respect these symmetries, reducing the number of parameters and improving sample efficiency. As demonstrated in previous work
[
12
,
22
,
90
,
43
,
16
,
47
,
78
,
11
]
, equivariant design is crucial for learning on data with symmetries.
(2)
Processing sets of gradients:
Many applications, such as curvature-aware optimization, pruning, and uncertainty estimation, require access to
collections
of gradients on different datapoints which encode the local geometry of the loss.
GradMetaNet
is thus designed to efficiently handle sets of gradients computed on different datapoints. (3)
Efficient representation:
As gradients are extremely high-dimensional, we encode them efficiently. Gradients of neural networks, evaluated on a single data point, admit a rank-1 decomposition which provides a compact representation that scales linearly (rather than quadratically) with the number of neurons.

Decomposed gradients have a simpler symmetry structure compared to the raw weight representation, allowing us to construct
GradMetaNet
using simple equivariant building blocks
[
90
,
31
,
75
]
and to incorporate attention mechanisms. This enables us to prove universality results still unknown for weight-space models. Additionally, we formally demonstrate the necessity of processing sets of gradients, proving that several fundamental gradient-based algorithms cannot be approximated based on a single averaged gradient.

We evaluate
GradMetaNet
on several gradient learning tasks, comparing to equivariant weight-space architectures and other natural baselines. First, we demonstrate
GradMetaNet
’s ability to predict local curvature information using a small sample of gradients, achieving a 26.3% improvement over standard approximations, and outperforming other learned approaches. We then integrate
GradMetaNet
into learned optimizer architectures and apply it to train image classifiers and transformer language models, achieving up to a 4.63× reduction in steps compared to Adam, and a 1.78× improvement over other learned baselines. Finally, we use
GradMetaNet
for model editing, where we improve on current
state-of-the-art
results in editing MNIST and CIFAR10 INRs by up-to 22.5%. Across all tasks,
GradMetaNet
consistently outperforms baselines, highlighting the value of efficient gradient representations and equivariant processing of sets of gradients.

Background

Notation.

Throughout the paper, we denote models by
{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
, where
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}
are the parameters. When
{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}
is a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), we write the input dimension as

d_{L}

d_{1},\dots,d_{L-1}
, and denote the activation function by
\sigma

{\bm{\theta}}=({\bm{W}}_{1},{\bm{b}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{W}}_{L},{\bm{b}}_{L})
. Given a dataset
{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
, a loss function
\ell:{\mathcal{Y}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}({\bm{\theta}}):=\frac{1}{|{{\mathcal{B}}}|}\sum_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in{{\mathcal{B}}}}\ell({\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}}).

The parameters gradients of the loss on the batch are denoted by
\nabla_{{\mathcal{B}}}:=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}({\bm{\theta}})

({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})

{\mathcal{L}}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}({\bm{\theta}}):=\ell({\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}})

Rank-1 decomposition of gradients.
While general parameter gradients have the same shape as parameters, for many neural architectures, the gradient

(1)

Applying the chain rule yields the following expressions for the weight and bias gradients:

\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{l}}{\mathcal{L}}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{g}}^{(l)}({\bm{a}}^{(l-1)})^{\top},\quad\nabla_{{\bm{b}}_{l}}{\mathcal{L}}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{g}}^{(l)}.

Symmetries of Decomposed Gradients

Weight-space symmetries.

Many neural architectures exhibit parameter space symmetries: parameter transformations that leave the network’s function unchanged. In particular, MLPs exhibit well-documented permutation invariance
[
33
,
76
,
1
,
60
,
92
]
: permuting the neurons of a hidden layer, while keeping track of the connections to the neighboring layers, alters the weight matrices but preserves the function represented by the network. This parameter space symmetry can be expressed by an action of the permutation symmetry group
G:=S_{d_{1}}\times\cdots\times S_{d_{L-1}}
. For
{\bm{\theta}}=({\bm{W}}_{1},{\bm{b}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{W}}_{L},{\bm{b}}_{L})\in{\bm{\Theta}}
and
h=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{L-1})\in G
, the action
h\cdot{\bm{\theta}}=({\bm{W}}_{1}^{\prime},{\bm{b}}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,{\bm{W}}_{L}^{\prime},{\bm{b}}_{L}^{\prime})
is given by

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{1}^{\prime}={\bm{P}}_{\tau_{1}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}_{1},

\displaystyle{\bm{b}}_{1}^{\prime}={\bm{P}}_{\tau_{1}}^{\top}{\bm{b}}_{1},

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{l}^{\prime}={\bm{P}}_{\tau_{l}}^{\top}{\bm{W}}_{l}{\bm{P}}_{\tau_{l-1}},

\displaystyle{\bm{b}}_{l}^{\prime}={\bm{P}}_{\tau_{l}}^{\top}{\bm{b}}_{l},\quad l\in\{2,\dots,L-1\}

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{L}^{\prime}={\bm{W}}_{L}{\bm{P}}_{\tau_{L-1}},

\displaystyle{\bm{b}}_{L}^{\prime}={\bm{b}}_{L},

where
{\bm{P}}_{\tau_{l}}\in\{0,1\}^{d_{l}\times d_{l}}
is the permutation matrix corresponding to
\tau_{l}\in S_{d_{l}}
(see Figure
4
for an illustration). The action of
G
preserves the function represented by the network:
{\bm{f}}_{g\cdot{\bm{\theta}}}\equiv{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}
. Permutation symmetries naturally extend to many other neural architectures, see
Lim et al. 2023
,
Kofinas et al. 2024
,
Zhou et al. 2024a
for a detailed discussion.

Decomposed gradient symmetries.
Weight-space symmetries naturally extend to decomposed gradients. Following the discussion in Section
3
, we define the decomposed gradient space of an MLP
{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}
as

{\bm{\Gamma}}:={\bm{\Gamma}}^{(0)}\oplus\cdots\oplus{\bm{\Gamma}}^{(L)},

(4)

where
{\bm{\Gamma}}^{(l)}:={\mathbb{R}}^{d_{l}\times 2}
, referred to as the
neuron space
of the
l
-th layer, contains pairs
{\bm{\gamma}}^{(l)}=({\bm{a}}^{(l)},{\bm{g}}^{(l)})
of activations and pre-activation gradients.
\oplus
denotes a direct sum of vector spaces.

G
’s action extends naturally to
{\bm{\Gamma}}
. For a decomposed gradient
{\bm{\gamma}}=({\bm{\gamma}}^{(0)},\dots,{\bm{\gamma}}^{(L)})\in{\bm{\Gamma}}
and
h=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{L-1})\in G
, the action
h\cdot{\bm{\gamma}}=(h\cdot{\bm{\gamma}}^{(0)},\dots,h\cdot{\bm{\gamma}}^{(L)})
is given by:

h\cdot{\bm{\gamma}}^{(l)}=({\bm{P}}_{\tau_{l}}^{\top}{\bm{a}}^{(l)},{\bm{P}}_{\tau_{l}}^{\top}{\bm{g}}^{(l)}),\text{ for }l=1,\dots,L-1,\quad h\cdot{\bm{\gamma}}^{(l)}={\bm{\gamma}}^{(l)},\text{ for }l=1,L.

(5)

Let
{\bm{\Phi}}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}:{\bm{\Theta}}\to{\bm{\Gamma}}
be the function that maps parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
to the decomposition of the gradient
\nabla_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}({\bm{\theta}})
.
{\bm{\Phi}}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}
is
G
-
equivariant
, that is:

{\bm{\Phi}}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}(h\cdot{\bm{\theta}})=h\cdot{\bm{\Phi}}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}({\bm{\theta}}).

(6)

This equivariance applies to any transformation that modifies or extracts information from the function represented by
{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}
using its gradients. As illustrated in Figure
4

G
’s action on
{\bm{\Gamma}}
is simpler than its action on
{\bm{\Theta}}

GradMetaNet

In this section, we introduce
GradMetaNet
, an architecture for learning on gradients designed to process
sets
of
decomposed
gradients in a
G_{b}
-
equivariant
way. As the symmetry structure of
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}
is simpler than that of
{\bm{\Theta}}_{b}
, we can design
GradMetaNet
using simpler equivariant layers compared to its weight-space counterparts
[
61
,
92
,
91
]
. Specifically,
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}\in{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}
can be viewed as a set of decomposed gradients
\{{\bm{\gamma}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\gamma}}_{b}\}
, each of which is a concatenation of sets of neuron-level features
{\bm{\gamma}}_{i}=({\bm{\gamma}}^{(0)}_{i},\dots,{\bm{\gamma}}^{(L)}_{i})
. To further simplify the symmetry structure, we incorporate a positional encoding map that enables us to treat each
{\bm{\gamma}}_{i}
as a
single
bag of neuron-level features. This allows us to implement
GradMetaNet
using simple, well-established equivariant layers for sets
[
67
,
90
,
31
,
75
]
. As illustrated in Figure
5

GradMetaNet

(9)

(I)

Similarly to

that concatenates a
layer identifier
each neurons in the intermediate layers and a

to each neuron in the first and last layers. We use sinusoidal PE
[
79
]
.

(II)

{L_{{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}}}:{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f_{\text{in}}]\to{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f_{\text{out}}]

Navon et al. 2023b
,
{L_{\mathrm{Prod}}}:{\bm{\Gamma}}[f_{\mathrm{in}}]\to{\bm{\Theta}}
applies a pointwise MLP to the features associated with the neurons connected to each weight:
{\bm{W}}^{(l)}_{i,j}=\mathrm{MLP}_{1}([{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}^{(l)}_{i,:},{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}^{(l+1)}_{j,:}])
,
{\bm{b}}^{(l)}_{i}=\mathrm{MLP}_{2}({{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}^{(l)}_{i,:})
.

For detailed descriptions and implementation details for all layers, a
G
-invariant head for invariant tasks, and other design choices see Appendix
D.1
.

pipeline: gradients are decomposed into rank-1 factors and positional encoding is applied. The input is then transformed by a stack of

{L_{\mathrm{Pool}}}
pools these representations into
{\bm{\Gamma}}[f]

{L_{\mathrm{Prod}}}

Extension to transformers.
As formally detailed and motivated in Appendix
B
, decomposed transformer gradients have an additional sequence dimension with a set symmetry structure and the neuron spaces across the residual stream are tied together. Therefore, when applying
GradMetaNet
, we treat the sequence dimension as the batch dimension (with optional sequence PE), stack all the neuron features across the residual stream together to a single neuron space, and extend our positional encoding to include the attention head indices. For an extended discussion see Appendix
B.2
and
B.3
.

GradMetaNet
++.
Similarly to
Lee et al. 2019
,
Romero et al. 2020
,
Kasten et al. 2024
, we can preserve equivariance by replacing summation in steps (II) and (VI) with attention mechanisms. Therefore, we introduce an attention-based variant of
GradMetaNet
, termed
GradMetaNet
++, where
{L_{{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}}}
and
{L_{{\bm{\Gamma}}}}
are implemented using attention across the neuron and batch dimensions. This variant demonstrates significant performance improvements on some tasks, consistent with findings in previous studies. For a detailed description of
GradMetaNet
++ refer to Appendix
D.2
.

6
Theoretical Analysis

Importance of processing sets of gradients.
Instead of processing the gradient of the average loss as in other gradient learning methods
[
18
,
41
,
91
]
,
GradMetaNet
processes sets of (decomposed) gradients computed on individual datapoints. This approach is motivated by the fact that a set of gradients encodes strictly more information than the corresponding average gradient, enabling e.g. curvature estimations that cannot be computed using the average alone. This intuition is formalized in Appendix
E.1
, leading to Proposition
9
whose informal statement appears below.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\{\nabla_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}\}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in{{\mathcal{B}}}}
be gradients computed on on a set of datapoints
{{\mathcal{B}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{D}}
. There exist functions–such as natural gradient approximations or pruning saliency scores–that cannot be reconstructed from the average gradient
\nabla_{{\mathcal{B}}}
alone.

Expressive power.

Restricting a model to be equivariant with respect to a specific group action can potentially reduce its expressive power
[
59
,
51
,
52
]
. However, we demonstrate that
GradMetaNet
does not suffer from such limitations. Specifically, we prove a universal approximation property for equivariant functions defined on a compact domain that doesn’t intersect a certain low-dimensional subset
{{\mathcal{E}}}\subset{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}
. Formally:

Theorem 2
.

Let
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}
be a compact domain such that
{\mathcal{K}}=\cup_{g\in G_{b}}g\cdot{\mathcal{K}}
and
{\mathcal{K}}\cap{{\mathcal{E}}}=\emptyset
.
GradMetaNet
models are universal approximators (in the
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
-sense) of continuous
G_{b}
-equivariant functions from
{\mathcal{K}}
to
{\bm{\Theta}}
.

Figure 7:
Comparison of gradient-learning models trained to predict the FIM diagonal from a sample of
128
gradients. Results are averaged over 5 seeds; shading represents standard deviation.

{{\mathcal{E}}}
is the set of neuron features that have identical activations and backpropagated signals for at least two neurons (see Appendix
E.2
for a precise definition). Similarly to
Maron et al. 2020
,
Finkelshtein et al. 2025
, the inclusion of
{{\mathcal{E}}}
in Theorem
2
is essential (see Appendix
E.3
). However,
{{\mathcal{E}}}
is a union of subspaces of co-dimension
2
, has Lebesgue measure
0
, and the conditions for membership in
{{\mathcal{E}}}
are highly unlikely in practice, making this assumption mild.

Corollary 3
.

Let
{{\mathcal{B}}}
and
\{\nabla_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}\}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in{{\mathcal{B}}}}
be as in Proposition
1
. Several natural functions–such as natural gradient approximations and pruning saliency scores–can be effectively approximated by
GradMetaNet
, which has access to
\{\nabla_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}\}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})\in{{\mathcal{B}}}}
, but cannot be approximated by methods that rely solely on
\nabla_{{\mathcal{B}}}
.

For formal statements and proofs of all of the results in this section see Appendix
E
. In summary,
GradMetaNet
incorporates meaningful inductive biases for processing sets of gradients while retaining high expressive power, enabling it to represent all continuous functions on sets of gradients under mild assumptions.

7.2
Learned Optimizers

Optimizing deep neural networks is one of the fundamental challenges of deep learning. While classical optimizers
[
62
,
19

80
,
40
]
have become standard tools, their design largely relies on intuition and empirical validation. A promising alternative is to

[

6

Most optimizers (learnable and hand-crafted) only process the averaged batch gradient, and therefore don’t have access to local curvature information. In this experiment we integrate
GradMetaNet
into learned optimizer architectures, providing it with the raw information required for computing the curvature in the form of sets of gradients on individual datapoints across batches.

Setup.
Following
Harrison et al. 2022
,
Zhou et al. 2024a
, we parametrize our learned optimizer rules as

{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{t}+\alpha\left({\bm{v}}_{t}^{\mu}+\beta{\bm{F}}_{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t},\nabla_{t},\{{\bm{v}}_{t}^{\mu_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k},t)\right),

(10)

where
\nabla_{t}
is the current gradient, and
\{{\bm{v}}_{t}^{\mu_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k}
are momentum terms with different decay rates. The standard architecture for
{\bm{F}}_{\bm{\phi}}
is DeepSets (DS)
[
90
]
which applies a per-parameter MLP to the input features. More recently, researchers have explored using equivariant weight-space architectures like Universal Neural Functionals (UNF)
[
91
]
to implement
{\bm{F}}_{\bm{\phi}}
. For
GradMetaNet
-based learned optimizers we parametrize
{\bm{F}}_{\bm{\phi}}
as

{\bm{F}}_{\bm{\phi}}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t},\nabla_{t},\{{\bm{v}}_{t}^{\mu_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k},t,{\bm{F}}_{\bm{\psi}}^{\mathrm{\text{GradMetaNet}}}\left({{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{t},\{{\bm{\mathsfit{v}}}_{t}^{\mu_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{k}\right)\right),

(11)

where
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{t}\in{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}
is the set of decomposed gradients across the current batch, and
\{{\bm{\mathsfit{v}}}_{t}^{\mu_{i}}\}_{i}
are exponential moving averages of past
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{t}

\alpha

\beta
,

{\bm{\phi}}
, and

[
84
]
) to minimize the training loss after
T

GradMetaNet
, UNF +
GradMetaNet

10

Tasks.

We use three types of optimization tasks: (1) optimizing a 2-parameter linear regression, constructed to have non-diagonal curvature, (2) optimizing MLPs for classifying CIFAR10, CIFAR100
[

[
89

[

Results and discussion.
As Figure

Implicit Neural Representations (INRs)
[

Data.

Following previous works
[

For each INR, we compute the parameter gradients with respect to the MSE loss between the INR output and the target edited image, evaluated over randomly sampled points. The input data consists of both the parameters of the INR and the corresponding gradients.

Baselines.

We evaluate
GradMetaNet
and
GradMetaNet
++ in combination with several weight-space architectures.
GradMetaNet
and
GradMetaNet
++ process the gradients to produce outputs in
{\bm{\Theta}}
, which are then used as additional weight features for the base weight-space network. This hybrid approach is compared against two baselines: (1) the base weight-space network, and (2) the base weight-space network augmented with probing features. Following
Kofinas et al. 2024

8
Conclusion and Limitations

Conclusion.
We introduce
GradMetaNet
, an equivariant architecture for processing sets of decomposed gradients, supported by both theoretical and experimental results. Theoretically, we demonstrate that under mild assumptions
GradMetaNet
can approximate any function processing sets of gradients, and that average gradient methods are unable to approximate several natural gradient-based functions. Experimentally, we demonstrate
GradMetaNet
’s ability to predict local curvature, enhance learned optimizers, and achieve state-of-the-art performance in model editing.

Limitations and future work.
The current implementation of
GradMetaNet
is limited to MLPs and transformers, in future work we hope to extend
GradMetaNet
to support other neural architectures. Additionally,
GradMetaNet
++’s use of attention mechanisms limits its usage in large-scale settings. Finally, scaling
GradMetaNet
,
GradMetaNet
++ or their variants to larger models, such as state-of-the-art LLMs and generative models, is an interesting direction for future work.

(13)

\nabla_{{\bm{W}}_{l}}{\mathcal{L}}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{g}}^{(l)}({\bm{a}}^{(l-1)})^{\top},\quad\nabla_{{\bm{b}}_{l}}{\mathcal{L}}_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{g}}^{(l)}

as discussed in Section

A.3
The Fisher Information Matrix and Its Uses

Many gradient-based algorithms
[
55
,
27
,
87
,
82
,
17
]
use the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) to approximate the curvature of the loss landscape. Below, we define the FIM and present two common gradient-based algorithms that utilize it. The FIM has numerous other applications, this section serves only as a basic introduction. For a more comprehensive overview, we refer readers to
[
54
,
64
]
.

The Fisher information matrix.

Consider a supervised learning problem of predicting outputs
{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
from inputs
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
. We assume a probabilistic model of the form
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})=p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}))
, where
p
is called the
likelihood
. For classification tasks we may assume a softmax likelihood,
p({\bm{y}}=k|{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}))=\mathrm{softmax}({\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}))_{k}
, and for regression, we usually take a Gaussian likelihood
p({\bm{y}}|{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}))={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}};{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{I}})
.
p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})
is called the
predictive distribution
. The FIM is defined by

{{\bm{F}}}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{y}}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log(p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}))\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log(p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}))^{\top}\right)\in{\bm{\Theta}}\otimes{\bm{\Theta}}

(15)

The FIM is a second order approximation of the change in the model’s predictive distribution with respect to the parameters

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\parallel p_{{\bm{\theta}}+\bm{\delta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})\right)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\delta}^{\top}{{\bm{F}}}\bm{\delta}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(\lVert\bm{\delta}\rVert^{3}\right)

(16)

and thus contains information about the geometry of the space distributions and the loss landscape. Additionally, when
{\bm{\theta}}
is a local minimum, the FIM is identical to the Hessian of the loss. The FIM is computed using gradients of the model on
a single datapoint
and specifically using only gradients of the output
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}_{i})
. For regression

\begin{split}{{\bm{F}}}&=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{y}}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log(p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}))\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log(p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}))^{\top}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{y}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}};{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{I}})}\left(\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\frac{1}{2}\lVert{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{y}}\rVert^{2}\right)\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\frac{1}{2}\lVert{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{y}})\rVert^{2}\right)^{\top}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{y}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{y}};{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{I}})}\left(\lVert{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})-{\bm{y}}\rVert^{2}\right)}_{\equiv{\bm{I}}}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})^{\top}\right),\end{split}

(17)

and for classification

\begin{split}{{\bm{F}}}&=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}},{\bm{y}}\sim p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log(p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}))\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log(p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}))^{\top}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{C}p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}=k|{\bm{x}})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log(p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}=k|{\bm{x}}))\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log(p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}=k|{\bm{x}}))^{\top}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{C}\frac{1}{p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}=k|{\bm{x}})}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}=k|{\bm{x}})\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{y}}=k|{\bm{x}})^{\top}\right)\end{split}

(18)

The natural gradient.

The natural gradient is defined by preconditioning the gradient using the FIM

{\nabla_{\mathrm{nat}}}:={{\bm{F}}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\theta}})

(19)

Motivated from the perspective of information geometry
[
4
]
, the natural gradient defines the direction in parameter space that gives the largest change in the loss per unit of change in the
predictive distribution
of the model, measured by KL-divergence. This is to be contrasted with the standard gradient, which is the direction that gives the largest change in the loss per unit of change in
parameters
. Natural gradients are a fundamental tool in optimization and can be used to accelerate training
[
55
,
27
,
25
]
.

Natural gradient optimization requires dynamic computation, storing and inversion the FIM whose size grows quadratically with the number of parameters, making it intractable at scale. This necessitates the use of approximations, such as K-FAC
[
55
,
27
,
26
]
.

FIM usage in pruning.
In pruning, we want to assign each weight of a trained model a saliency score that indicates its importance to the model’s performance. The goal is to remove a certain percentage of weights with the lowest saliency, resulting in a compressed model with fewer parameters that still performs relatively well. The original OBD
[
45
]
and OBS
[
32
]
algorithms use the Hessian to compute saliency scores. However, since the model is trained, the parameters are likely close to a local minimum, and the the FIM is often used as an approximation of the Hessian.

Given parameters of a trained model
{\bm{\theta}}
, the OBD pruning saliency scores are given by

{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{OBD}}:={\bm{\theta}}^{2}\odot\mathrm{diag}({{\bm{F}}}_{\bm{\theta}}),

(20)

where
{\bm{\theta}}^{2}
is a matrix of the square of each parameter, and
\odot
stands for point-wise product. The optimal brain surgeon (OBS) pruning saliency scores are given by

{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{OBS}}:={\bm{\theta}}^{2}\oslash\mathrm{diag}({{\bm{F}}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}),

(21)

where
\oslash
denotes point-wise division.

We begin by presenting the gradient decomposition for transformers, and then analyze its symmetry structure and the applicability of
GradMetaNet

B.2
Transformer (Decomposed) Gradient Symmetries

The permutation symmetry groups of the parameter spaces of general architectures, and transformers in particular, were analyzed in
Lim et al. 2023
,
Kofinas et al. 2024
,
Zhou et al. 2024a
.
Lim et al. 2023
,
Kofinas et al. 2024
identify permutation symmetries with automorphisms of the computation graph of
{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}
, and
Zhou et al. 2024a
analyzes the permutation symmetries of multi-dimensional tensors. To give a flavor of the adaptations needed in the transformer case, we first look at the effects of the residual connections. Intuitively, we need to tie together the neuron spaces of dimension
d
(i.e.
{\bm{H}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}l}}-{{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}1}})}
,
{\bm{O}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}l}})}
,
\widehat{{\bm{H}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}l}})}}
, and
{\bm{U}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}l}})}
) under the same symmetry group (
S_{d}
) because of the residual connections. With this “symmetry tying” the residual connections and LayerNorms preserve permutation symmetries, since if
{\bm{\Phi}},{\bm{\Psi}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are
S_{d}
-equivariant functions, we have

({\bm{\Phi}}+{\bm{\Psi}})(\sigma\cdot{\bm{x}})={\bm{\Phi}}(\sigma\cdot{\bm{x}})+{\bm{\Psi}}(\sigma\cdot{\bm{x}})=\sigma\cdot{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{x}})+\sigma\cdot{\bm{\Psi}}({\bm{x}})=\sigma\cdot({\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{x}})+{\bm{\Psi}}({\bm{x}}))=\sigma\cdot({\bm{\Phi}}+{\bm{\Psi}})({\bm{x}}),

and

\mathrm{LayerNorm}(\sigma\cdot{\bm{x}})=\sigma\cdot\mathrm{LayerNorm}({\bm{x}}).

The analysis of the permutation symmetry group of transformer weight spaces provided in
Lim et al. 2023
,
Kofinas et al. 2024
,
Zhou et al. 2024a
follows similar observations. The resulting symmetry group is

G=\underbrace{S_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}T}}}_{\text{tokens}}\times\underbrace{S_{d}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{residual}\\
\text{stream}\end{subarray}}\times\overbrace{\underbrace{(S_{d_{k}})^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}h}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{MHA}\\
\text{output}\end{subarray}}\times\underbrace{S_{d_{\mathrm{ff}}}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{FFN}\\
\text{hidden dim}\end{subarray}}}^{\times{{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}L}}\text{ transformer blocks}}.

(28)

Where, in our decomposed gradients case,
S_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}T}}
acts on the sequence dimension of all spaces,
S_{d}
acts on the second axis (the
d
-dimension) of all
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}T}}^{\mathrm{res}}
s, each
(S_{d_{k}})^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}h}}
acts independently on the
V^{({{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}l}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}j}})}
and
O^{({{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}l}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}j}})}
components of each head in
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}T}}^{\mathrm{attn}}
, and each
S_{d_{\mathrm{ff}}}
acts on the hidden dimension of the FFN represented in
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}T}}^{\mathrm{hidden}}
.

We note that transformer parameter spaces exhibit other neural symmetries that are not modeled by the
permutation
symmetry group
G
. These symmetries include ReLU scaling symmetries
[
38
]
and general attention symmetries (the transformation
({\bm{Q}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}l}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}j}})},{\bm{K}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}l}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}j}})})\mapsto({\bm{Q}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}l}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}j}})}{\bm{R}},{\bm{K}}^{({{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}l}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}j}})}{\bm{R}}^{-\top})
for
{\bm{R}}\in\mathrm{GL}_{d_{k}}({\mathbb{R}})
results in the same attention matrix). Accounting for these symmetries is left for future work.

B.3
Adapting
GradMetaNet
to Transformers

To implement a

First, we treat the sequence dimension as a batch dimension, optionally with additional positional encoding for the token index. {\bm{\Gamma}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}T}}^{\mathrm{res}}

{\bm{\Gamma}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}T}}^{\mathrm{attn}}

{\bm{\Gamma}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,1,0}T}}^{\mathrm{hidden}}

{L_{{\bm{\Gamma}}}}

{{\color[rgb]{0.4,0,0}L}}

D.4
Computational Complexity.

We analyze the space and runtime complexity of
GradMetaNet
and
GradMetaNet
++, comparing them to alternative approaches. Throughout this discussion we denote by
P
the number of parameters in the underlying MLP
{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}
, whose gradients are being processed, and denote the number of neurons by
N
. Since the gradient-batch-to-gradient-batch update is the most computationally intensive component in both architectures, we focus our analysis on this operation.

GradMetaNet
.

The gradient-batch-to-gradient-batch update in
GradMetaNet
consists of a stack of layers
{L_{{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}}}:{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f_{\text{in}}]\to{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f_{\text{out}}]
, as defined in Section
5
. Each of these layers has both space and runtime complexity of
O(N\cdot b\cdot f_{\text{in}}\cdot f_{\text{out}})
. Thus, assuming a fixed hidden dimension and number of layers,
GradMetaNet
has a complexity of
O(N\cdot b)
.

Gradient concatenation and averaging in weight space.

Both gradient concatenation and averaging methods process sets of gradients by utilizing weight-space architectures, such as those introduced by
Navon et al. 2023a
,
Lim et al. 2023
. These architectures employ layers
L_{w}:{\bm{\Theta}}[f_{\text{in}}]\to{\bm{\Theta}}[f_{\text{out}}]
with time and space complexity
O(P\cdot f_{\text{in}}\cdot f_{\text{out}})
.

In the
concatenation
approach, gradients are concatenated, producing an input element in
{\bm{\Theta}}[b]
, resulting in an overall complexity of
O(P\cdot b)
for a fixed hidden dimension and number of layers. This approach scales poorly compared to
GradMetaNet
when
b\cdot P>b\cdot N
. In contrast, the
averaging
approach reduces gradients to a single representation in
{\bm{\Theta}}[1]
, yielding a complexity of
O(P)
. However, this method is also suboptimal in the overparameterized regime (
P>b\cdot N
). In addition, even in cases where the batch size is sufficiently large such that
P<b\cdot N
, gradient-averaging methods may still be suboptimal due to their expressivity limitations (see Section
6
).

GradMetaNet
++.

The gradient-batch-to-gradient-batch update in
GradMetaNet
++ is implemented using a stack of layers
{U_{{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}}}:{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f_{\text{in}}]\to{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f_{\text{out}}]
, as detailed in Appendix
D.2
. Each layer has a time complexity of
O((N^{2}\cdot b+b^{2}\cdot N)\cdot f_{\text{in}}\cdot f_{\text{out}})
and can be designed to achieve a space complexity of
O(N\cdot b\cdot f_{\text{in}}\cdot f_{\text{out}})
. While these layers have the highest time complexity among the approaches considered so far, their space complexity remains efficient. Moreover, constructing an attention-based variant for weight-space architectures would scale quadratically with
P
, making it far less practical in terms of scalability.

Appendix E
Theory

In this section we provide proofs and further discussion for the results presented in Section
6
. Throughout this section we use
\nabla_{i}
to denote gradients of the networks computed on a single datapoint.

E.1
Importance of Processing Collections of Gradients

In Section
6
, we discuss the expressivity limitations of processing the gradient of the average loss on the batch compared to the collection of gradient at each of the datapoints. In this section we formalize these limitations. We start with some notation and definitions. As we saw in Appendix
A.3
, the FIM can be computed using gradients on individual datapoints. Given a set of such gradients
{\mathcal{G}}=\{\nabla_{1},\dots,\nabla_{b}\}
, the FIM computed using the gradients in
{\mathcal{G}}
is denoted by
{{\bm{F}}}_{\mathcal{G}}
. As we saw in the main text
{\mathcal{G}}
can be thought of as an element of
{\bm{\Theta}}_{b}
.

Definition 4
.

Let
{\bm{\Phi}}:{\bm{\Theta}}\times{\bm{\Theta}}_{b}\to{\bm{\Theta}}
be a function whose inputs are parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
and a set of gradients
{\mathcal{G}}=\{\nabla_{1},\dots,\nabla_{b}\}
. We say that
{\bm{\Phi}}
non-trivially depends on the FIM if for some function
{\bm{\Psi}}:({\bm{\Theta}}\otimes{\bm{\Theta}})\times{\bm{\Theta}}\times{\bm{\Theta}}\to{\bm{\Theta}}
,

{\bm{\Phi}}\left({\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}}\right)={\bm{\Psi}}\left({{\bm{F}}}_{\mathcal{G}},{\bm{\theta}},\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla_{i}\right)

(43)

and there exists a pair of inputs
{\bm{\theta}}
,
{\mathcal{G}}=\{\nabla_{1},\dots,\nabla_{b}\}
and
{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
,
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}=\{\nabla_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\nabla_{b}^{\prime}\}
where
{\mathcal{G}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
are admissible gradient sets
2
2

2

Here, by “admissible”, we mean that the elements of
{\mathcal{G}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}
are actual MLP gradients, rather than arbitrary elements of
{\bm{\Theta}}
.
and such that
{\bm{\theta}}={\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
,
\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla_{i}=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla^{\prime}_{i}
but

{\bm{\Phi}}\left({\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}}\right)={\bm{\Psi}}\left({{\bm{F}}}_{\mathcal{G}},{\bm{\theta}},\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla_{i}\right)\neq{\bm{\Psi}}\left({{\bm{F}}}_{{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}},{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla^{\prime}_{i}\right)={\bm{\Phi}}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}\right).

(44)

Many commonly used functions over sets of gradients non-trivially depend on the FIM. before providing such examples, we first state the following trivial proposition.

Proposition 5
.

Let
{\bm{\Phi}}:{\bm{\Theta}}\times{\bm{\Theta}}_{b}\to{\bm{\Theta}}
be a function that non-trivially depends on the FIM. There exist an
\epsilon>0
such that for any continuous function
{\bm{\Lambda}}:{\bm{\Theta}}\times{\bm{\Theta}}\to{\bm{\Theta}}
it holds that:

\max_{{\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}}}\left\lVert{\bm{\Phi}}\left({\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}}\right)-{\bm{\Lambda}}\left({\bm{\theta}},\frac{1}{b}\sum\nabla_{i}\right)\right\rVert>\epsilon.

(45)

In other words, functions which non-trivially depend on the FIM cannot be approximated (in the
\ell_{\infty}
-sense) by continuous functions that rely only on the average gradient.

Proof.

The proof follows trivially from Definition
4
. Let
{\bm{\theta}}
,
{\mathcal{G}}=\{\nabla_{1},\dots,\nabla_{b}\}
and
{\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
,
{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}=\{\nabla_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\nabla_{b}^{\prime}\}
be a pair of inputs such that
{\bm{\theta}}={\bm{\theta}}^{\prime}
and
\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla_{i}=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\nabla^{\prime}_{i}
, but

{\bm{\Phi}}\left({\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}}\right)\neq{\bm{\Phi}}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}\right)

(46)

For any

we have

{\bm{\Lambda}}\left({\bm{\theta}},\frac{1}{b}\sum\nabla_{i}\right)={\bm{\Lambda}}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},\frac{1}{b}\sum\nabla_{i}^{\prime}\right).

(47)

Therefore, if we choose
0<\epsilon<2\lVert{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}})-{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime})\rVert
we have

\lVert{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}})-{\bm{\Lambda}}\left({\bm{\theta}},\frac{1}{b}\sum\nabla_{i}\right)\rVert+\lVert{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime})-{\bm{\Lambda}}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},\frac{1}{b}\sum\nabla^{\prime}_{i}\right)\rVert\geq\lVert{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}})-{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime})\rVert>2\epsilon.

(48)

This implies that either
\lVert{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}})-{\bm{\Lambda}}\left({\bm{\theta}},\frac{1}{b}\sum\nabla_{i}\right)\rVert>\epsilon
or
\lVert{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime})-{\bm{\Lambda}}\left({\bm{\theta}}^{\prime},\frac{1}{b}\sum\nabla^{\prime}_{i}\right)\rVert
and so

\max_{{\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}}}\lVert{\bm{\Phi}}({\bm{\theta}},{\mathcal{G}})-{\bm{\Lambda}}\left({\bm{\theta}},\frac{1}{b}\sum\nabla_{i}\right)\rVert>\epsilon

(49)

completing the proof.

{\bm{\Theta}}_{b}

Definition 6

(Natural gradient map)
.

The natural gradient map

is defined by

{\bm{\Phi}}_{\mathrm{nat}}(\nabla,{\mathcal{G}})=({{\bm{F}}}_{\mathcal{G}}+\epsilon{\bm{I}})^{-1}\nabla,

(50)

where
{\bm{I}}
is the identity matrix and

is a damping factor. These are added since while positive-definite, the FIM is not guaranteed to be invertible.

Definition 7

(OBD/OBS pruning saliency maps)

E.3
Importance of
{{\mathcal{E}}}
in Universality

Recall that Theorem
2
proves the universality of
GradMetaNet
over compact domains which do not intersect the set
{{\mathcal{E}}}\subset{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f]
defined by:

{{\mathcal{E}}}=\bigcup_{l=1}^{L-1}\bigcup_{i_{1}=1}^{l}\bigcup_{i_{2}=i_{1}+1}^{l}\left\{{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}\in{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f]\middle|\sum_{j=1}^{b}{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{i_{1},j,:}^{(l)}=\sum_{j=1}^{b}{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{i_{2},j,:}^{(l)}\right\}

(97)

In this section, we prove that there exist compact sets
{\mathcal{K}}\subset{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f]
with
{\mathcal{K}}\cap{{\mathcal{E}}}\neq\emptyset
over which
GradMetaNet
is not universal. We emphasize that, as discussed below, sets intersecting
{{\mathcal{E}}}
consist of highly regular gradient values, which deviate significantly from typical gradient sets. Consequently, the requirement
{\mathcal{K}}\cap{{\mathcal{E}}}=\emptyset
is a mild and realistic assumption.

Proposition 23
.

There exist a pair of elements
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{1},{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{2}\in{{\mathcal{E}}}
such that for any
\tau\in G_{b}
,
\tau\cdot{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{1}\neq{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{2}
but for every
GradMetaNet
model
{\bm{F}}
it holds that
{\bm{F}}({{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{1})={\bm{F}}({{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{2})
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we focus in this proof on the invariant version of
GradMetaNet
,
{\bm{F}}:{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{f^{\prime}}
. The proof can be easily extended to the equivariant version of
GradMetaNet
,
{\bm{F}}:{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f]\to{\bm{\Theta}}[f^{\prime}]
. Let the underlying MLP used to define the spaces
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f]
have an input dimension
d_{0}=1
, a single hidden layer of dimension
d_{1}=n
, and an output dimension
d_{2}=1
. In this case,
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f]
, along with the action of
G_{b}
, is isomorphic to the space of “sets of sets” defined in
Hartford et al. 2018
. Moreover,
GradMetaNet
is equivalent to the architecture proposed in
Hartford et al. 2018
. This architecture is known to be unable to distinguish between certain highly regular, non-equivalent inputs. For example, incidence matrices of graphs can be viewed as sets of sets and, therefore, as elements of
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f]
. It was shown in
Albooyeh et al. 2019
that the ability of the architecture in
Hartford et al. 2018
, and consequently
GradMetaNet
, to separate graphs based on their incidence matrices is equivalent to the 1-WL test
[
88
]
.

Consider an example where
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{1}
represents the incidence matrix of a graph consisting of two disconnected cycles of length 3, and
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{2}
represents the incidence matrix of a single cycle of length 6. For any
GradMetaNet
model
{\bm{F}}
, it will hold that
{\bm{F}}({{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{1})={\bm{F}}({{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{2})
. A straightforward check shows that for this example,
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{1},{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}_{2}\in{{\mathcal{E}}}
, completing the proof.
∎

F.1
Curvature Information Estimation

Dataset.

To construct the dataset for this experiment, we first generate 3000 SIREN models
[
77
]
, commonly used for INRs. Each model has three layers with 32 hidden features, i.e.
1\to 32\to 32\to 1
. The weights and biases of these models are randomly initialized using a standard Gaussian distribution. Each data point in the resulting dataset corresponds to a single SIREN model and consists of an input gradient set of size 128,
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}\in{\bm{\Gamma}}_{128}[2]
, and a target vector,
{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{FIM}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}
. The input gradient set corresponding to a SIREN model
{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}
is computed by sampling 128 random points
S_{{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{128}\}\subset[-1,1]
. To simulate diverse datasets, the points in
S_{{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}}
are sampled from a distribution
X_{p}
defined by

X_{p}=\begin{cases}\text{U}([0,1]),&\text{with probability }p,\\
\text{U}([-1,0]),&\text{with probability }1-p.\end{cases}

(98)

Here the value of
p
is randomly and uniformly selected over the unit interval. The set of input gradients is then given by
\{\nabla_{1},\dots,\nabla_{128}\}
,
\nabla_{i}=\nabla{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i})
. Thus the input vector
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}\in{\bm{\Gamma}}_{128}[2]
is given by
[\nabla_{1},\dots,\nabla_{128}]
.

To compute the target vector
{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{FIM}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}
, we first randomly sample 1024 random points from
X_{p}

S^{\prime}_{{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}}=\{x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{1024}\}\subset[-1,1]
(Note that the sets
S_{{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}}
and
S^{\prime}_{{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}}
are independent). We then compute the true FIM (see Section
A.3
) by

{{\bm{F}}}=\frac{1}{1024}\sum_{i=1}^{1024}\nabla{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\prime}_{i})\nabla{\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(x^{\prime}_{i})^{\top}.\in{\bm{\Theta}}\otimes{\bm{\Theta}}

(99)

The target vector
{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{FIM}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}
is then given by
{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{FIM}}=\mathrm{diag}{{\bm{F}}}
. The task is then to predict
{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{FIM}}
based on the gradient set vector
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}
and is trained using
l_{2}
loss. For baselines which process data in parameter space
{\bm{\Theta}}[f]
(rather than gradient set space
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{128}[2]
), the gradient vector
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}
computed from gradient set
\{\nabla_{i}\}
is replaced by representing each gradient in parameter space
\nabla_{i}\in{\bm{\Theta}}
, and then either concatenating them resulting in a vector
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}[128]
or averaging them, resulting in a vector in
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}
.

Baselines.

We compare
GradMetaNet
and
GradMetaNet
++ against multiple baselines.

MLP + concat:
This baseline takes the gradient vector
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}
described above as input, flattens it, and feeds it into a standard MLP.

Neuron Asymmetric
GradMetaNet
:
This variant respects the batch symmetries but not the gradient space symmetries of
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f]
. It uses
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}
as input and applies a Deep Sets architecture, as described in
Zaheer et al. 2017
. Specifically, the vector feature for element
i
in the set is constructed as
[{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}^{(0)}_{i,:,:},\dots,{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}^{(L)}_{i,:,:}]
.

Batch Asymmetric
GradMetaNet
:
This variant closely resembles
GradMetaNet
but respects the gradient space symmetries while disregarding the batch symmetries of
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[f]
. It takes
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}
as input and applies a
GradMetaNet
model defined over
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{1}[256]
, where the batch axis is “flattened”, resulting in a single gradient with a feature dimension of
256=2\cdot 128
.

Weight Space Models:
The variants “DWS+Concat”, “DWS+Average”, “GMN+Concat”, and “GMN+average” take as input the gradients represented as
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}[f]
. Here,
{\bm{\theta}}
is either the average of the gradients in the set, in which case
f=1
, or the concatenation of all gradients in the set, in which case
f=128
. These variants then apply either the deep weight space (DWS) architecture described in
Navon et al. 2023b
or the graph metanetwork (GMN) architecture introduced in
Lim et al. 2023
to the corresponding input vector.

Standard Estimator:
Finally, to highlight the benefits of learning to approximate algorithms rather than relying on fixed, non-learnable approximations, we compare
GradMetaNet
and
GradMetaNet
++ to a standard estimation of the target. Specifically, we compute
\mathrm{diag}{{\bm{F}}}_{{{\mathcal{B}}}_{1}}
based on the 128 gradients provided as input and evaluate its
\ell_{2}
distance to the target, which, as a reminder, is
\mathrm{diag}{{\bm{F}}}_{{{\mathcal{B}}}_{2}}
. Here,
{{\mathcal{B}}}_{2}
is computed using the gradients of a larger set of 1024 points, independently generated from the data points in
{{\mathcal{B}}}_{1}
.

Data preparation.

We use 500 examples as a test dataset and 500 examples as a validation dataset, with the size of the training set varying between 10 and 2000 examples. As a preprocessing step, all data, including the target vectors, is normalized based on the statistics of the training dataset. The following are the three normalization methods used, based on the vector space to which the data belongs:

1.

For input vectors of the form
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}\in{\bm{\Gamma}}_{128}[2]
: for each layer
l=0,\dots,3
, we compute the mean
\mu^{(l)}
and standard deviation
\sigma^{(l)}
over the set of values:

\{{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}^{(l)}_{i,j,k}\mid i=0,\dots,b;\,j=1,\dots,d_{l};\,k=0,1;\,{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{train}}\}.

We then normalize the data by:
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}^{(l)}\leftarrow\frac{{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}^{(l)}-\mu^{(l)}}{\sigma^{(l)}}
.

2.

For input vectors of the form
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}[f]

{\bm{\theta}}=({\bm{W}}^{(1)},{\bm{b}}^{(1)},{\bm{W}}^{(L)},{\bm{b}}^{(L)},)
(where
f=1
for averaging or
f=128
for concatenation): we follow the normalization scheme suggested in
Navon et al. 2023a
, where for each layer
l=1,\dots,3
, we compute the means
\mu_{w}^{(l)},\mu_{b}^{(l)}
and standard deviations
\sigma_{w}^{(l)},\sigma_{b}^{(l)}
over the sets of values:

\{{\bm{W}}^{(l)}_{i,j,k}\mid i=1,\dots,d_{l-1};\,j=1,\dots,d_{l};\,k=0,\dots,f;\,\\
{\bm{W}}^{(l)}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{train}}\}.

\{{\bm{b}}^{(l)}_{i,k}\mid i=1,\dots,d_{l};\,k=0,\dots,f;\,{\bm{b}}^{(l)}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{train}}\}.

We then normalize the data as follows:

{\bm{W}}^{(l)}\leftarrow\frac{{\bm{W}}^{(l)}-\mu_{w}^{(l)}}{\sigma_{w}^{(l)}},\quad{\bm{b}}^{(l)}\leftarrow\frac{{\bm{b}}^{(l)}-\mu_{b}^{(l)}}{\sigma_{b}^{(l)}}.

3.

For target vectors
{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{FIM}}\in{\bm{\Theta}}
: recall that
{\bm{\Theta}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{P}
for some integer
P\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{FIM}}(i)
denote the
i
-th entry of the vector
{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{FIM}}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
. We compute a single mean
\mu
and a single standard deviation
\sigma
over the set of values:
\{{\bm{\theta}}_{\text{FIM}}(i)\mid i=1,\dots,P;\,{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{FIM}}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{train}}\}
. The data is then normalized as:
{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{FIM}}\leftarrow\frac{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{FIM}}-\mu}{\sigma}
.

Navon et al. 2023a

All models were trained for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-3}
and a batch size of 32. We report the test loss corresponding to the best validation performance and repeat the experiment with random seeds 1 through 5.

The following are descriptions of the optimization tasks the learned optimizers were evaluated on. Across all tasks, the training loss is negative log-likelihood and batch size is 128 except for the transformers task.

(1)

Learned optimizers are tasked with training a three-layer MLP classifier on a downsized (
8\times 8

[
89
]

(2)

MLP on CIFAR10.
Learned optimizers are tasked with training a three-layer MLP classifier on a downsized (
8\times 8
) flattened version of CIFAR10. The MLP has a single hidden layer of dimension 32 and ReLU activations.

(3)

MLP on CIFAR100.
Learned optimizers are tasked with training a three-layer MLP classifier on a downsized (
8\times 8

(4)

Transformer on LM1B.
Learned optimizers are tasked with training a transformer language model on LM1B
[
14
]

(5)

2-parameter linear regression.
Learned optimizers are tasked with training a linear regression model with two inputs and a single output. The input training data is a mixture of two Gaussians centered around
(1,2)
and
(2,1)
and the target is always
0

.

Learned optimizer architectural details.

In each inner training iteration, all learned optimizers are provided with the current parameters
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
, current gradient
\nabla_{t}
, six momentum value
\{{\bm{v}}_{t}^{0.1},{\bm{v}}_{t}^{0.5},{\bm{v}}_{t}^{0.9},{\bm{v}}_{t}^{0.99},{\bm{v}}_{t}^{0.999},{\bm{v}}_{t}^{0.9999}\}
, iteration number
t
as an 11-dimensional sinusoidal encoding. All of these inputs are concatenated across the feature dimension, to get a 19-dimensional vector per-parameter. I.e. the learned optimizers inputs are in
{\bm{\Theta}}[19]
and outputs are in
{\bm{\Theta}}[1]
.

For
GradMetaNet
based learned optimizers, inputs also include current set of individual gradients
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}\in{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}
and six momentum value
\{{\bm{\mathsfit{v}}}_{t}^{0.1},{\bm{\mathsfit{v}}}_{t}^{0.5},{\bm{\mathsfit{v}}}_{t}^{0.9},{\bm{\mathsfit{v}}}_{t}^{0.99},{\bm{\mathsfit{v}}}_{t}^{0.999},{\bm{\mathsfit{v}}}_{t}^{0.9999}\}

{\bm{\Gamma}}_{b}[7]
. These inputs are processed by
GradMetaNet
, that outputs an embedding in
{\bm{\Theta}}[14]
, i.e. a 14-dimension embedding vector per parameter. This embedding is concatenated to the other inputs of the learned optimizer, so the DeepSets/UNF based learned optimizer gets an input in
{\bm{\Theta}}[33]
.

The DeepSets
[
90
]
based learned optimizers process the inputs by applying a per-parameter MLP with three hidden layers of size 32. The UNF
[
91
]
based learned optimizers apply a per parameter MLP with two hidden layers of size 32 and output dimensions of 32, followed by a singe UNF layer. As a baseline compared against all learned optimizers we used a standard Adam optimizer
[
40

with learning rate tuned by grid-search over the values
\{0.0005*j\}_{j=1}^{20}
(i.e. 20 equidistant values in the range
[0.0005,0.01]

F.3
INR Editing

Dataset.

We utilized two previously proposed INR datasets: MNIST INRs
[
60
]
and CIFAR10 INRs
[
92
]
. Each INR represents an image from the original image datasets, and consists of three layers with 32 hidden features. The target images are produced using the image processing library OpenCV
[
10
]
, by dilating or increase the contrast of the MNIST and CIFAR-10 images, respectively. To construct the input gradient set,
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}\in{\bm{\Gamma}}_{64}
, we sample 64 random input coordinates in
[0,1]^{2}
and compute the gradients of the editing loss w.r.t the original INR parameters. Specifically, if
\theta
are the INR parameters and
{\bm{I}}_{i}:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the target image, then for an input coordinates
(x_{i},y_{i})
, the gradient
\nabla_{i}
is given by
\nabla_{i}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left({\bm{f}}_{\bm{\theta}}(x_{i},y_{i})-{\bm{I}}_{i}(x_{i},y_{i})\right)^{2}
. We use the same set of random coordinates for all INRs.

Combining
GradMetaNet
and weight-space methods.

We combine
GradMetaNet
and the weight-space architectures as follows: First,
GradMetaNet
processes the gradients
{{\bm{\mathsfit{g}}}}\in{\bm{\Gamma}}_{64}
to produce outputs in
{\bm{\Theta}}[f]
. In this experiment we choose
f=8
. Next, the output is used as additional weight features for the weight-space network, i.e., the input to the weight-space network is in
{\bm{\Theta}}[9]
.

Additional experimental details.

We train all methods for 150K steps using the AdamW
[
50
]
optimizer with a batch size of 64. We search over learning rates in
\{0.01,0.005,0.0001\}
. For ScaleGMN and GMN we use the official implementation provided in
Kalogeropoulos et al. 2024
with the same parameter configuration provided by the authors. We use the bidirectional variant (ScaleGMN-B,
Kalogeropoulos et al. 2024
) with 10 layers and a hidden dimension of 128. The DWS
[
60
]
network consists of 8 layers with 128 hidden features. Finally,
GradMetaNet
consists of 10 layers with 256 hidden features, while
GradMetaNet
++ uses 3 blocks with 8 heads and 64 hidden features.
