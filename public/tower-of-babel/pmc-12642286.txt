The effect of repeated simulation-based disaster education on nursing students’ self-efficacy in disaster response
Authors: Ebru Konal Korkmaz, Aynur Uysal Toraman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to its geographic and climatic characteristics, Turkey faces a high risk of natural disasters such as earthquakes, floods, and landslides. Therefore, nurses must be competent in all phases of the disaster management cycle. However, the current state of nursing education in Turkey is inadequate, leaving students feeling unprepared and uncertain about their roles and responsibilities in disaster situations. Furthermore, no studies have evaluated the use of standardized patient simulations in disaster nursing in Turkey. The objective of the present study is to evaluate the effect of repeated standardized patient simulation-based disaster training on nursing students’ self-efficacy.MethodsA quasi-experimental study was carried out with 44 fourth-year nursing students. The disaster nursing course includes theory and practice. Within the scope of the study, theoretical training and laboratory practices were carried out on obstetric evaluation and pregnancy follow-up in disasters, care practices after abortion, family planning counselling in disasters, sexual and reproductive health. After training, two repeated standardized patient simulations were conducted. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0.ResultsThe median score for disaster nursing practice skills increased among students as a result of repeated simulations, and significant differences were found between applications (p < 0.001). Additionally, a significant difference was found in the median scores on the Disaster Response Self-Efficacy Scale before and after the first and repeated standardized patient simulations s(p < 0.001).ConclusionsSimulation-based education improved nursing students’ disaster nursing skills and increased their disaster response self-efficacy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Disaster nursing, Simulation, Patient simulation, Nursing education

Introduction

Turkey is the 116th most risky country among 181 countries in terms of natural disasters. Turkey ranks 112th in terms of vulnerability, 93rd in terms of coping inadequacy and 119th in terms of adaptation inadequacy [1]. Due to geological, meteorological, topographical structure and climate characteristics, natural disasters such as earthquakes, landslides, floods and avalanches are frequently experienced in Turkey [2].

The intricate nature of disasters, their pervasive impact on all segments of society, and the necessity for prompt intervention demand the involvement of health professionals at every stage of the disaster process [3]. The 2022 edition of the Nursing National Core Education Program in Turkey stipulates that undergraduate nursing education programs must encompass the inclusion of topics pertaining to the provision of care in exceptional situations circumstances. Additionally, students are expected to develop the competencies required for the planning, implementation, management, and evaluation of such situations [4]. Although, disaster-related courses are offered at 36 universities in our country, intended for undergraduate nursing education (designated by various titles, “Disaster Nursing,” Disaster Culture,” and “Nursing in Disasters”), studies indicate that students and nurses have limited knowledge regarding their roles and responsibilities before, during, and after disasters, and thus require training in disaster management [3, 5, 6].

It is important to provide an experiential learning environment for training students with disaster nursing competencies. In realizing this, it is recommended to integrate practices such as role-playing, video-assisted training, and simulation into the disaster nursing education process [5–8]. It is stated that simulation-based disaster nursing education increases students’ knowledge levels, motivation and self-awareness, and positively affects their intention to participate in disaster planning, response and management [7, 9–11].

Most of the studies in the literature are focused on the preparation and intervention stages of the disaster cycle, and high valid simulation methods were used [7, 10–12]. Current valid simulation methods used in disaster nursing education are mainly focused on the prevention and response phases. In particular, the use of standardized patient methods has been shown to enhance students’ cognitive, affective, and psychomotor skills, increase their learning motivation, and foster teamwork awareness [11, 13]. However, it is also essential that nurses play an active role not only during the response phase but also in promoting individual and community well-being. These roles include ensuring food safety, conducting surveillance studies, maintaining environmental health, and planning and delivering services according to the needs of disadvantaged groups [14, 15]. Considering that the rehabilitation and recovery of individuals and society after a disaster may take months or even years, the contribution of nurses to these long-term processes is critical for achieving sustainable community health. Therefore, nurses should be provided with professional knowledge and skills for the stages of disaster prevention and recovery [15]. Nursing students should know each stage of the disaster management cycle and be competent in the necessary skills. In this context, the aim of the study was to examine the effect of repeated standardized patient simulation-based disaster training on nursing students’ disaster response self-efficacy.

Methods

Study design

A quasi-experimental simulation study was conducted with pre- and post-test repeated measurements without a control group.

Sample

The study was conducted between March and May 2023 with fourth year nursing students studying at the Nursing School in Turkey. Using G*Power (V3.1.9.7), a minimum sample size of 40 was calculated having as a reference the effect size of a previous study [10]. We included 45 people in the study to allow for withdrawals and losses. The study was concluded with 44 students. The post hoc power analysis confirmed that the adequacy sample size (effect size = 1.735, α = 0.05, power = 0.98). The inclusion criteria for the study were determined as follows: The subject is currently enrolled in their fourth year of studies at the Nursing School. They have volunteered to participate in the study and have attended the training program eliminate on a regular basis.

Procedure

The research was conducted in two stages: preparation and intervention.

Preparation phase

Training materials, data collection tools and scenarios were prepared for the disaster nursing training program. The training program was created within the scope of the services carried out in the recovery phase of the disaster management cycle and aimed to provide students with the skills of health assessment and improvement, counseling and re-planning in the post-disaster period. In line with these objectives, “Disaster Nursing, Intercultural Competent Approach in Disasters, Sexual and Reproductive Health in Disasters” topics were discussed and presentations were prepared in line with the learning objectives.

The scenarios were prepared in line with the criteria in the International Nursing Association for Clinical Simulation (INACSL) standards within the scope of theoretical training and skills checklist for the recovery phase of the disaster management cycle [16]. In the post-disaster period, there are difficulties in accessing reproductive health services, abortions and premature births may develop as a result of trauma or shock due to disaster, pregnant and puerperium women experience, sanitation problems, access to family planning services is limited, security problems, violence and rape incidents are frequently seen in this period [17, 18].

Three distinct scenarios were devised, each incorporating a specific demographic: a pregnant disaster victim, an adult woman with a four-month-old infant, and an adult woman with a history of abortion as a consequence of being trapped beneath the rubble in the earthquake in a container city. With these scenarios, it is aimed to raise students’ awareness about the effects of disasters on the health of adult women, problems that may develop based on gender, determining the needs of women in sexual and reproductive health and ensuring the coordination of services for these needs. The scenarios were designed to enable students to perform physical examination, take sexual and reproductive health history, provide family planning counseling, assess immunization status, evaluate environmental health, socioeconomic, cultural characteristics and lifestyle habits, and assess psychological status. For the content validity of the scenarios, three experts in the fields of Public Health, Medical Education, and Public Health Nursing, with expertise in disaster management, sexual and reproductive health, and disadvantaged groups, were consulted. As a result of the expert opinion, it was determined that the scenarios were valid and no changes were made.

A pilot study was conducted with a convenience sample of nine students who volunteered to participate and met the inclusion criteria. Prior to the simulation, students underwent disaster nursing training and laboratory exercises. Subsequently, the students were organized into groups of three and then subjected to a series of three simulated scenarios. A lottery method was employed to determine the students’ participation in each scenario. Subsequent to the implementation, no alterations were made to the scenario content or skill checklist. The pilot study’s data were not incorporated into the research data set.

Intervention phase

Simulations were carried out in the Public Health Nursing Standard Patient Laboratory in the Nursing School. Data was gathered outside of teaching hours. At all stages of the study, students worked one-on-one with the researchers and each simulation experience was conducted with one student. Each simulation experience lasted approximately 15–20 min. The debriefing sessions were conducted in groups of five and each session lasted 30 min.

The “Student Information Form and Disaster Response Self-Efficacy Scale” were administered to the students who had volunteered to participate in the study prior to the commencement of the Disaster Nursing Training and Laboratory Practice [19]. The theoretical training was conducted in three sessions, with visual presentations prepared within the scope of the Disaster Nursing Training programme, comprising question and answer sessions and video demonstrations. Each session lasted 45 min. The training program schedule was determined according to the availability of the students, and training and laboratory practices were carried out in groups of 5–8 students. Following the conclusion of the theoretical training programme, the participants were required to undertake skills training in the following areas: obstetric evaluation and pregnancy follow-up in disasters, care practices after abortion, and family planning counselling. One week after the completion of the skills training, the first standardized patient simulation (SPS) was performed. In the first SPS experience, two scenarios were used: an adult woman with a four-month-old baby who was rendered homeless as a consequence of earthquake (Scenario 1) and an adult woman with a history of abortion as a result of being trapped under debris in the earthquake (Scenario 2) were placed in a container city. In order to reduce bias and minimize information sharing between students, which student would apply which scenario was determined by lottery method. The scenario was done individually. The student developed his/her practice for 15–20 min. The practices were monitored by the facilitator who managed the simulation in the control room and two independent observers. The observers evaluated the students according to the “Disaster Nursing Practice Skills Checklist” [12, 14]. After the completion of the practice, the students were asked to fill in the “Disaster Response Self-Efficacy Scale” and then they were taken to the debriefing session conducted with the diamond model in groups of five [19, 20].

The second simulation was conducted 15 days after the first SPS was completed. Within the scope of this simulation, a scenario (Scenario 3) about the placement of a pregnant disaster victim in a container city was used. The implementation lasted approximately 15 min. The simulation was monitored by the facilitator and two independent observers in the control room. The students were evaluated by the observers according to the “Disaster Nursing Practice Skills Checklist” [12, 14]. Following the completion of the practice, the students were asked to fill in the “Disaster Response Self-Efficacy Scale” and then they were taken to the analysis session conducted with the diamond model in groups of five [19, 20]. Research process

Measurements

Student information form prepared by the researchers consisted of three questions about the students’ age, gender, and education on disaster nursing practices.

Disaster nursing practice skills checklist was prepared in line with the literature and in accordance with the content of the scenarios used in the research [12, 14, 21]. A check list consisting of 30 questions was prepared to assess skills such as communication, taking general health history, assessing psychological status, socioeconomic and cultural characteristics, lifestyle habits and environmental factors, taking sexual and reproductive health history and counseling. For each step of the expected skills, students were evaluated as “Accurate/Fully Applied (2 points)”, “Incomplete/Inpartially Applied” (1 point), “Did not apply (0 points)”. A minimum score of 0 and a maximum score of 60 points can be obtained from the checklist. The checklist was completed by two different observers specialized in the field of public health nursing by observing the students during the practices. The observers made their evaluations independently of each other by watching the students during simultaneous video recording in the simulation laboratory. In order to evaluate the inter-observer agreement, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated over the scores given in the first and second practices, and it was determined that the agreement between the two observers was high, with a value above 0.80.

Disaster response self-efficacy scale (DRSES) developed by Li et al. [19], the Turkish validity and reliability of the scale was conducted by Uysal Toraman and Konal Korkmaz [22]. The Cronbach Alpha value of the scale is 0.94 and the Cronbach Alpha values of the sub-dimensions are between 0.86 and 0.89. The scale consists of three sub-dimensions and 19 items: search and rescue and psychological assessment competence (items: 1–10), disaster assessment competence (items: 11–15) and adaptation competence (items: 16–19). The responses are evaluated according to a five-point Likert-type scale. A high score on the scale indicates a high level of disaster response self-efficacy [22].

Data analysis

SPSS 25.0 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) package program trial version was used for data analysis. Statistical significance was evaluated at 95% confidence interval at p < 0.001 level. The findings were expressed as numbers, percentages, means, and medians. The data was assessed for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, with non-parametric tests employed (K = 0.123, p = 0.000). Wilcoxon test was used for comparisons of two dependent groups, Friedman test was used for comparisons of more than two dependent groups and Bonferroni Dunn multiple comparison test was used to see from which group the difference originated. Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated over the scores given in the first and second simulation and correlation analysis was performed to evaluate interobserver agreement.

Discussion

The study demonstrated that repeated SPS enhanced students’ abilities in various domains, including communication, health history taking, socioeconomic and cultural characteristics, assessment of lifestyle habits, environmental assessment, physical examination, and sexual and reproductive health history taking skills in disasters. The results of numerous studies on this topic also indicate that the utilization of simulation techniques in disaster nursing education enhances students’ abilities in emergency intervention, triage, critical thinking, and cognitive and psychomotor skills [7, 20]. In contrast to the findings of previous studies, this research is the first to examine the development of students’ skills in making sexual and reproductive health assessments in disasters. The results demonstrate that repeated SPS is an effective method for fostering the acquisition of these skills.

It is also necessary to evaluate the self-efficacy of nursing students to determine their knowledge and skill levels and readiness for disaster intervention [23]. Hwang and Lee [9] found that simulation-based training increased students’ perceptions of biological disaster preparedness and disaster nursing competencies. In this study, it was concluded that experiencing a situation closest to reality positively affected students’ proficiency levels. It is emphasized that repeating the simulation experience strengthens the student’s learning outcomes and the importance of repeating simulation sessions many times to increase self-efficacy [24–26]. In our study, in addition to theoretical education, laboratory practices and then repeated SPS were performed, and it was determined that students’ self-efficacy improved after each experience. The findings of the study are consistent with the literature data. In a study conducted in Oman, it was determined that as the level of knowledge and frequency of experience of students regarding disaster nursing practices increased, their confidence and willingness to intervene increased and their competencies improved [27]. As a result of our study, the significant increase in self-efficacy after repeated SPS supports that the experience of simulation practice and the frequency of this experience are very important and effective in skills training.

International Council of Nurses (ICN) emphasizes that all nurses graduating from basic nursing education programs should have the skills and competence to perform first aid practices, triage, psychological first aid and rehabilitation in disasters [14]. In order for students to gain proficiency in these skills, the scenarios developed as part of the study addressed topics such as post-traumatic physical and psychological assessment, bleeding control, and follow-up. In both simulation, students were expected to communicate with disaster victims who exhibited high levels of fear and anxiety due to the earthquake and who had sleep problems with a supportive and empathic approach. Additionally, they were required to determine the victims’ urgent and basic needs. It was observed that their skill scores increased as a result of repeated simulation. Fernandez- Pacheco et al. [28] examined the triage skills of students in a mass disaster simulation and found that the application improved students’ disaster triage skills. Similarly, in a study conducted in China, it was determined that students receiving simulation-based disaster triage training had better triage skills than students receiving classical training [29]. In a study in which the standard patient method was applied in psychological first aid training, the psychological first aid competence of the experimental group was significantly higher than the control group in which the standard training method was applied [30]. Zhang et al. [31] found that there was an increase in students’ self-efficacy post-test total score after simulation-based psychological first aid training.

In the scenarios designed to facilitate students’ acquisition of competence in disaster assessment and adaptation, the theme of the placement of earthquake victims in a container city was addressed. In this context, practices were carried out, addressing issues such as the provision of follow-up care for those who have sustained injuries, access to contraceptive methods, immunization, the assessment of environmental risks, and the provision of basic necessities such as nutrition and clean water. It was expected that students would manage the situation in a supportive and compassionate manner, controlling their individual anxieties and fears. It was observed that competencies in adaptation and disaster assessment exhibited improvement following repeated SPS. The findings of this study are corroborated by previous research [32, 33]. Satoh et al. [34] examined the disaster nursing competencies of nursing students who participated in relief activities following the Kumamoto earthquake. Their findings indicated that students who engaged in relief activities demonstrated enhanced awareness and adaptation competencies in performing disaster nursing practices that respected human rights, aligned with ethical principles, and facilitated empathetic communication and effective teamwork with both disaster victims and their colleagues. Additionally, numerous studies have indicated that direct experience with a disaster, involvement in volunteer activities, and participation in relief teams are conducive to students’ comprehension of their responsibilities and roles, thereby facilitating the adaptation process [35, 36]. In studies examining disaster response competencies, it was determined that student nurses were not aware of their responsibilities in local and national institutions during disaster response, their organizational duties in the disaster management process, and that they were not ready to participate in activities such as disaster preparedness, response and post-disaster evaluation [27, 37]. It was observed that transitioning from the role of a student to that of a team member proved an efficacious strategy for the acquisition of knowledge, the development of skills, and the attainment of competence in the domain of disaster assessment. The findings demonstrate the significance of simulation-based training in attaining proficiency in disaster assessment and adaptation.

Conclusion

As a result of the simulation-based disaster nursing training program, students’ disaster nursing practice skills, disaster intervention, search and rescue and psychological assessment, disaster assessment and adaptation competencies improved. The findings of the study show that standardized patient simulation and repetitive simulation is an effective method to improve the disaster response skills and competencies of nursing students. It is recommended to conduct randomized controlled trials evaluating different teaching methods such as standardized patient and repetitive simulation to improve students’ self-efficacy in disaster intervention in nursing education and to examine the long-term effects of the method with follow-up studies to evaluate the impact of simulation-based disaster nursing education on students’ disaster intervention competencies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the research was conducted with a relatively small sample size from a single nursing school in Turkey, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other institutions or countries. Second, the study employed a quasi-experimental design without a control group, which restricts the ability to establish causal relationships and may introduce potential confounding factors. One of the limitations of this study is that the target population was restricted to women who were pregnant, had recently given birth, or had experienced miscarriage, rather than including all individuals affected by disasters. Finally, the follow-up period was short, and the long-term retention of skills and self-efficacy improvements was not assessed. Future studies employing randomized controlled trials, larger and more diverse samples, and long-term follow-up measurements are recommended to validate and extend these findings.
