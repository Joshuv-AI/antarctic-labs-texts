The threshold for loose Hamilton cycles in random hypergraphs
Alan Frieze, Xavier Pérez-Giménez

Abstract
We show that w.h.p. the random r r -uniform hypergraph H n , m H_{n,m} contains a loose Hamilton cycle, provided r ≥ 3 r\geq 3 and m ≥ ( 1 + ϵ ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ n r m\geq\frac{(1+\epsilon)n\log n}{r} , where ϵ \epsilon is an arbitrary positive constant. This is asymptotically best possible, as if m ≤ ( 1 − ϵ ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ n r m\leq\frac{(1-\epsilon)n\log n}{r} then w.h.p. H n , m H_{n,m} contains isolated vertices.

Introduction

The thresholds for the existence of Hamilton cycles in the random graphs
G_{n,m},G_{n,p}
have been known for many years, see
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
and
[

. There have been many generalisations of these results over the years and the problem is well understood. It is natural to try to extend these results to hypergraphs.

An
r
-uniform hypergraph is a pair
H=(V,E)
where
E\subseteq\binom{V}{r}
. Let
H_{n,p}
be the binomial random
r
-uniform hypergraph on
n
vertices with edge probability
p
. (The uniformity is
r
thoughout and so we drop
r
from some of the notation.) Let
H_{n,m}
be the random
r
-uniform hypergraph on
n
vertices with
m
randomly chosen
E_{m}
edges from
\binom{V}{r}
. We are also interested in oriented hypergraphs in which each edge has one distinguished vertex called its
tail
and
r-1

heads
. Let
\vec{H}_{n,p}
be the oriented counterpart of
H_{n,p}
in which each one of the
r\binom{n}{r}
possible oriented edges is picked independently with probability
p
. Note that if we take
\vec{H}_{n,p}
and ignore the orientations, then the outcome is distributed as
H_{n,p^{\prime}}
with
p^{\prime}=1-(1-p)^{r}
, so
p^{\prime}\sim rp
when
p=o(1)
.

We say that an
r
-uniform sub-hypergraph
C
of
H
is a Hamilton cycle of type
\ell
, for some
1\leq\ell\leq r-1
, if there exists a cyclic ordering of the vertices
V
such that every edge consists of
r
consecutive vertices and for every pair of consecutive edges
e_{i-1},e_{i}
in
C
(in the natural ordering of the edges) we have
|e_{i-1}\setminus e_{i}|=\ell
. When
\ell=r-1
we say that
C
is a
loose
Hamilton cycle. Frieze
[
10
]
, Dudek and Frieze
[
5
]
and Dudek, Frieze, Loh and Speiss
[
6
]
established the threshold for the existence of a loose Hamilton cycle in
H_{n,m}
up to a constant factor. (See also, Ferber
[
7
]
and Frankston, Kahn, Narayanan and Park
[
8
]
.) We will prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be an arbitrary small positive constant. If
r\geq 3
and
(r-1)\mid n
and
m\geq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)n\log n}{r}
then w.h.p.
H_{n,m}
contains a loose Hamilton cycle.

If
m\leq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)n\log n}{r}
then w.h.p.
H_{n,m}
contains isolated vertices and so this is asymptotically best possible. (The constraint
(r-1)\mid n
is necessary for the existence of a loose Hamilton cycle. We should also remark that Narayanan and Schacht
[
16
]
obtained the precise threshold for type
\ell
cycles, except for the case of loose Hamilton cycles.)

We need the following result of Altman, Greenhill, Isaev and Ramadurai
[
2
]

H_{n,d}
denote a random

r
-uniform hypergraph on vertex set

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
r\mid dn
and

. Then,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(H_{n,d}\text{ contains a loose Hamilton cycle})=\begin{cases}1&d>\rho(r).\\
0&d\leq\rho(r).\end{cases}

Here

is the unique real in

such that

(\rho-1)(r-1)\left(\frac{\rho r-d-r}{\rho r-\rho}\right)^{(r-1)(\rho r-\rho-r)/r}=1.

We also need (the proof of) the following theorem of Kahn
[
13
]

Theorem 3

Let

be an arbitrary positive constant. If

and

then w.h.p.
H_{n,m}
contains a perfect matching.

Proof of Theorem
1

Let
{\mathcal{K}}=\binom{[n]}{r}
denote the edges of the complete
r
-uniform hypergraph with vertex set
[n]
. Let
N=|{\mathcal{K}}|=\binom{n}{r}
. Let
\hat{H}_{n,d}
be the random (multi)-hypergraph in which every vertex
v
picks a random set of
d
edges that contain
v
. Each edge is picked independently of other choices and with replacement, so in principle one vertex could pick the same edge twice or two different vertices may pick the same edge. However, neither of these situations will occur w.h.p for
d=d^{*}=\varepsilon^{2}\log n
, which is what we are interested in. We can think of
\hat{H}_{n,d}
as a hypergraph generalization of the
d
-out model of random graphs. For convenience, we may sometimes regard
\hat{H}_{n,d}
as oriented by making each vertex be the tail of all the edges that it picked.

We will be concerned with
H_{n,p}
for
p=(1+\varepsilon)p_{*}
where
p_{*}=\log n/\binom{n-1}{r-1}
and
\varepsilon>0
is a small constant.

Theorem 4
.

Let
d^{*}=\varepsilon^{2}\log n
,
p=(1+\varepsilon)\log n/\binom{n-1}{r-1}
and
\rho\in\mathbb{N}
constant. Then, there is a coupling in which
H_{n,p}
contains
\rho
independent copies
{\widehat{\Gamma}}_{1},{\widehat{\Gamma}}_{2},\ldots,{\widehat{\Gamma}}_{\rho}
of
\hat{H}_{n,d^{*}}
w.h.p.

Proof.

We will consider three types of edges:
1
,
2
and
3
. Edges of types
1
and
3
are oriented, i.e. they have a distinguished vertex called tail and
r-1
heads. Edges of type
2
are not oriented.
\vec{E}_{1},E_{2},\vec{E}_{3}
will be auxiliary random hypergraphs on
[n]
with edges of types
1,2,3
, respectively, and built independently of each other.
\vec{E}_{1}
is built by picking each one of the
r\binom{n}{r}
possible oriented edges with probability
p_{1}=1-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}p_{*}\right)^{1/r}\sim\frac{\varepsilon}{2r}p_{*}
independently from other choices. So
\vec{E}_{1}
is distributed as
\vec{H}_{n,p_{1}}
.

E_{2}
is distributed like
H_{n,p_{2}}
(unoriented) with
p_{2}=(1+\varepsilon/2)p_{*}
.
Finally,
\vec{E}_{3}
is distributed as
\vec{H}_{n,p_{3}}
with
(1-p_{3})^{r}=1-p_{2}
, so
p_{3}\sim p_{2}/r
.
Note that if
E_{i}
(
i\in\{1,3\}
) is obtained from
\vec{E}_{1},\vec{E}_{3}
by forgetting the orientation of the vertices, then it is distributed as
H_{n,\tilde{p}_{i}}
, where
\tilde{p}_{i}=1-(1-p_{i})^{r}\sim rp_{i}
(more precisely,
\tilde{p}_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}p_{*}
and
\tilde{p}_{3}=p_{2}=(1+\varepsilon/2)p_{*}
). Therefore, there is no hope of embedding
E_{1}\cup E_{2}\cup E_{3}
inside of
H_{n,p}
since each one of
E_{2}
and
E_{3}
have almost the same number of edges as
H_{n,p}
, so the union contains almost twice the needed edges. So our approach will be to consider all edges of type 1 together with some of type 2 and some of type 3 in a way that we can build the desired coupling.

We generate
\vec{E}_{1}
as the union of
\rho
independent copies
\vec{E}^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,\vec{E}^{(\rho)}_{1}
of
\vec{H}_{n,p^{\prime}_{1}}
with
(1-p^{\prime}_{1})^{\rho}=1-p_{1}
, so
p^{\prime}_{1}\sim p_{1}/\rho=\frac{\varepsilon}{2r\rho}p_{*}
.
Given a vertex
v
in an oriented hypergraph, its out-edges are the edges that have
v
as a tail. Out-degrees and other related notions are defined the obvious way. We say
v
is
good
if its out-degree is at least
d^{*}
in every
\vec{E}^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,\vec{E}^{(\rho)}_{1}
. Otherwise we call vertex
v

bad
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{\rho}
be independent copies of
\vec{H}_{n,\alpha p_{*}}
for some
\alpha>0
. Let
V_{\alpha}
denote the set of vertices of degree less than
d^{*}
in at least one of the
L_{i}
. Then
\mathbb{E}(|V_{\alpha}|)\leq n^{\theta_{\alpha}}
where
\theta_{\alpha}=\varepsilon^{2}(\log 1/\varepsilon^{2}+1+\log\alpha)-\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
p_{\alpha}=\alpha p_{*}
. Then,

\displaystyle E(|V_{\alpha}|)

\displaystyle\leq n\rho\sum_{k=0}^{d^{*}}\binom{\binom{n-1}{r-1}}{k}p_{\alpha}^{k}(1-p_{\alpha})^{\binom{n-1}{r-1}-k}

\displaystyle\leq 2n\rho\left(\frac{\binom{n-1}{r-1}p_{\alpha}e}{\varepsilon^{2}\log n}\right)^{\varepsilon^{2}\log n}\exp\left\{-\left(\binom{n-1}{r-1}-k\right)p_{\alpha}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 3n\rho\left(\frac{e\alpha}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{\varepsilon^{2}\log n}n^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle=O(n^{1+\theta_{\alpha}}).

∎

Putting
\alpha=\varepsilon/(2r\rho)
in Lemma
5
and using the Markov inequality, we see that w.h.p. all but at most a
1/n^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
fraction of the vertices are good, where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon^{\prime}(\varepsilon,r,\rho)>0
. We call this event
\mathcal{F}_{1}
.

Now we will consider a hypergraph that contains all edges of type
1
, those edges of type
2
with at most one bad vertex and those of type
3
with more than one bad vertex. More precisely, let
V_{G}
be the set of good vertices and
V_{B}=[n]\setminus V_{G}
the set of bad ones. Let
E^{\prime}_{2}=\{e\in E_{2}:|e\cap V_{B}|\leq 1\}
. We can orient each edge in
E^{\prime}_{2}
with exactly one bad vertex by making that bad vertex the tail, and edges with no bad vertices are oriented following any arbitrary deterministic rule. The resulting oriented hypergraph is denoted
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{2}
be the event that every bad vertex has degree at least
\rho d^{*}
in
E^{\prime}_{2}
(or equivalently out-degree at least
\rho d^{*}
in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}
). For good vertices we have already found enough edges in
E_{1}
so we don’t care about their degrees in
E_{2}
or
E_{3}
. Putting
\alpha=1+\varepsilon/2
in Lemma
5
, we see that
\theta_{\alpha}<-1
and so
V_{\alpha}=\emptyset
w.h.p and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
holds.

Then define
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}=\{e\in\vec{E}_{3}:|e\cap V_{B}|>1\}
and let
E^{\prime}_{3}
be the unoriented version of
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}
resulting from ignoring orientations. Edges in
E^{\prime}_{2}
appear with probability
p_{2}
among those edges containing at most one bad vertex. Edges in
E^{\prime}_{3}
appear with probability
1-(1-p_{3})^{r}=p_{2}
among the other edges that are not elegible for
E^{\prime}_{2}
. In other words, even though each one of
E^{\prime}_{2}
and
E^{\prime}_{3}
depend on the set of good vertices (and thus on
E_{1}
),
E^{\prime}_{2}\cup E^{\prime}_{3}
is distributed as
H_{n,p_{2}}
and is independent of
E_{1}
. Then
E_{1}\cup E^{\prime}_{2}\cup E^{\prime}_{3}
is distributed as
H_{n,\tilde{p}_{1}+p_{2}-\tilde{p}_{1}p_{2}}
which can be trivially included inside of

since
\tilde{p}_{1}+p_{2}-\tilde{p}_{1}p_{2}\leq\tilde{p}_{1}+p_{2}=p
. Hence, all it remains to show is that in the event that
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}_{2}
holds, we can find
\rho
independent copies of
\hat{H}_{n,d^{*}}
(regarded as oriented) contained in
\vec{E}_{1}\cup\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}\cup\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}
, which after forgetting orientations we just showed is contained in
H_{n,p}
. We call such copies of
\hat{H}_{n,d^{*}}

K_{1},\ldots,K_{\rho}
. If
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}_{2}
fails, just pick
K_{1},\ldots,K_{\rho}
independently from everything else so the coupling fails, but this occurs with probability
o(1)
.

Suppose we reveal the out-degrees of
\vec{E}^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,\vec{E}^{(\rho)}_{1}
and degrees of
E^{\prime}_{2}
(but nothing else) and suppose that
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}_{2}
holds. In particular, we know which vertices are good or bad.
For every vertex
v\in[n]
and “layer”
i=1,\ldots,\rho
, we want to pick (with replacement)
d^{*}
out-edges of
v
uniformly at random and independently of all other choices. These will be the out-edges of
v
in
K_{i}
. If
v
is good, this is straightforward, since
v
has out-degree
d_{v,i}\geq d^{*}
in
E^{(i)}_{1}
and all sets of

out-neighbours are equally likely and independent of those of other vertices and layers. Incorporating replacement is also straightforward. (In other words, we are coupling
d^{*}
random independent samples with replacement from a universe with a uniformly chosen random subset of size
d_{v,i}\geq d^{*}
.)

If
v
is bad, picking its
d^{*}
out-edges in each

is trickier. We will be using edges in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}\cup\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}
with tail
v
. In particular, we will only use edges in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}
with exactly one bad vertex, and ignore those with no bad vertices. Recall that edges of type 2 were originally unoriented, but we oriented them in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}
so that their only bad vertex (if they have one) is the tail. We will also use edges in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}
that have tail
v
. Most bad vertices will not pick vertices from
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}
, but some will. Note that the edge densities of
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}
and
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}
are different since each out-edge of
v
with only one bad vertex appears in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}
with probability
p_{2}
(before conditioning on degrees of
E_{2}
) while each out-edge of
v
with more bad vertices appears in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}
with probability
p_{3}\sim p_{2}/r
. This is a delicate issue that complicates things and is possibly the crux of the argument. We cannot make the density of
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}
smaller a priori since we want to make sure that bad vertices have enough out-edges and we cannot make the density of
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}
larger since otherwise the coupling with
H_{n,p}
would not work.

Let us proceed to do this carefully. Let
v
be a bad vertex. Let
X=X_{v}
be the set of all possible out-edges of
v
in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}

v
and
r-1
good vertices as heads. Note
x=|X|=\binom{|V_{G}|}{r-1}\sim\binom{n-1}{r-1}
. Let
Y=Y_{v}

be the set of all possible out-edges of
v
in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}
, i.e. edges with tail at
v
that contain at least one additional bad vertex. Clearly,
y=|Y|=\binom{n-1}{r-1}-x\sim\frac{|V_{B}|}{n}x\leq n^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}}x
by
\mathcal{F}_{1}
.
Since we are also assuming that
\mathcal{F}_{2}
holds, the out-degree of
v
in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}
is
s_{v}\geq\rho d^{*}
, so the set
S=S_{v}
of out-edges of
v
in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{2}
is a random subset of
X
of size
s_{v}\geq\rho d^{*}
. Let
S^{\prime}
be a random subset of
S
of size exactly
\rho d^{*}
(which is still uniformly chosen among all subsets of
X
of that size). Also, let
T=T_{v}
be the set of out-edges of
v
in
\vec{E}^{\prime}_{3}
. Now
t=|T|
is distributed as
\Bin(y,p_{3})
. Our goal is to find a coupling
U\subseteq S^{\prime}\cup T
where
U=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{\rho d^{*}}\}
is obtained by uniformly and independently sampling
\rho d^{*}
random elements of
X\cup Y
(where recall

is the set of all possible out-edges of

) with replacement (i.e.
|U|\leq\rho d^{*}
and could be strictly smaller if the same edge is sampled twice).

To do this, for each
i=1,\ldots,\rho d^{*}
, let
a_{i}
be an integer in
[x+y]=\left[\binom{n-1}{r-1}\right]
choosen uniformly at random (and independent of other choices). We can think of the edges in
X\cup Y
as having distinct labels in
[x+y]
so we pick
u_{i}
by first picking its label

, and later revealing what edge corresponds to that label. However, all we know so far is that edges in
Y
have labels in
[y]
and edges in
X
have labels in
[x+y]\setminus[y]
. The label of each individual edge is still undecided. The reason to do this is to see: 1) how many different edges are in
U
after possible edge repetitions, and 2) how many of these fall in
X
or
Y
.
If
a_{i}\leq y
, that means that
u_{i}
will land in
Y
(which has probability

X
. Let
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\rho d^{*}}\}

A_{X}=A\setminus[y]

. Then
u=|U|=|A|

, or in other words
\Bin(\rho d^{*},y/(x+y))\leq\Bin(y,p_{3})

\Bin\left(\rho\varepsilon^{2}\log n,1/n^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\right)\leq\Bin\left(n^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\binom{n-1}{r-1},\frac{1+\varepsilon/2}{r}\frac{\log n}{\binom{n-1}{r-1}}\right).

(1)

The coupling in (

Lemma 6
.

Let
0\leq P\leq 1/2
and
N,K,L\in\mathbb{N}
with
L\geq 2
. Then there is a coupling

\Bin(N,P)\leq\Bin(LKN,P/K)

Proof.

Let
Y=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{LK}Y_{i,j}
with
Y_{i,j}
i.i.d.
\text{Bernoulli}(P/K)
, so
Y
is
\Bin(LKN,P/K)
. We can write
Y=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y^{\prime}_{i}
where
Y^{\prime}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{LK}Y_{i,j}
. Putting
Z=\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}
with
Z_{i}=1_{Y^{\prime}_{i}\geq 1}
, we have that
Z_{i}\leq Y^{\prime}_{i}
and thus
Z\leq Y
. Note that
Z
is distributed as
\Bin(N,P_{0})
with

1-P_{0}=(1-P/K)^{LK}\leq e^{-LP}\leq e^{-2P}\leq 1-P

(where we used that
L\geq 2
and
0\leq P\leq 1/2
). Since
P\leq P_{0}
, we can easily find a
\Bin(N,P)
random variable
X
and a coupling such that
X\leq Z
. As a result,
X\leq Y
as desired.
∎

Shamir problem with a lower bound on minimum degree

It will be convenient to replace
\varepsilon
by
2\varepsilon
and work with
H_{n,p},p=(1+2\varepsilon)p_{*}
. This can be coupled to contain the union of
\rho=\lfloor\rho(r)\rfloor+1
random hypergraphs
H_{0}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{\rho}H_{i}

H_{0}=H_{n,(1+\varepsilon)p_{*}}
and
H_{i}=H_{n,\varepsilon m_{*}/\rho}
where
m_{*}=Np_{*}
. Next let
\widehat{\Gamma}_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,\rho
, be subhypergraphs of
H_{0}
as in Theorem
4
and let
\Gamma_{i}=\widehat{\Gamma}_{i}\cup H_{i},i=1,2,\ldots,\rho
.
The aim of this section is to prove the following:

Theorem 7
.

\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\ldots,\Gamma_{\rho}
contain (independent uniform) perfect matchings (assuming that
r
divides
n
), w.h.p.

Theorem
1
will follow from Theorem
7
. Symmetry implies that the perfect matchings in the
\Gamma_{i}
are uniform and independence follows from the construction and Theorem

with the matchings in
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\ldots,\Gamma_{\rho}
to obtain Theorem

that

. This restriction will be removed in Section

[

We continue to the proof of Theorem
7
and concentrate on
\Gamma_{1}
. Put quite simply, our proof is a minor modification of Kahn’s proof of Theorem
3
giving the correct constant in Shamir’s problem, see
[
13
]
. We let
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{N}
be a random permutation of
{\mathcal{K}}
. Then for
t\geq 1
, we let
R_{t}=\left\{e_{t+1},e_{t+2},\ldots,e_{N}\right\}
. We let
{\mathcal{H}}_{t}
denote the hypergraph with edge-set

where
E_{1}=E(\widehat{\Gamma}_{1})
. Thus, we begin with
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}={\mathcal{K}}
and in a step of the process we obtain

from

by deleting the edge

from

. We leave

untouched by this process, so that

where
0\leq k<r
. We can use
k=0
as the base case for an inductive proof. So, suppose that
{\mathfrak{X}}_{k}
is the statement that
H_{n,m}
contains a loose hamilton cycle w.h.p. when
(r-1)\mid n
and
n/(r-1)\equiv k\mod r
and
m\geq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)n\log n}{r}
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
. Assume then that
{\mathfrak{X}}_{k}
is true. We will show using Ferber’s idea that if
n=(r-1)(r\hat{n}+k+1)
then there exists a positive constant
\alpha
such that if
m\geq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)n\log n}{r}
then
H_{n,m}
contains a loose Hamilton cycle with probability at least
\alpha
. (In fact, we will show that
\alpha=1/(2r)
works.) After this, we can use a result of Friedgut
[
9
]
that says that there are no coarse thresholds with
m\sim n\log n
. In particular, we apply the second remark following Theorem 2.1 of that paper to obtain w.h.p.

Following Ferber, we let
p=m/N
and then let
p_{1}=\log n/N
and define
p_{2}
by
1-p=(1-p_{1})(1-p_{2})
. Fix an arbitrary edge
e=\left\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}\right\}\in E(H(n,p_{1}))
and note that such an edge exists w.h.p. Now let
V^{*}=([n]\setminus e)\cup\left\{e^{*}\right\}
, where
e^{*}
is a new vertex. Note that
|V^{*}|=n-r+1=(r-1)(r\hat{n}+k)
. Now consider the hypergraph
H^{*}
with vertex set
V^{*}
derived from
H_{n,p_{2}}
by replacing an
x_{i}
in any edge by
e^{*}
and accepting it with probability
\frac{1-(1-p_{2})^{1/r}}{p_{2}}\sim 1/r
. The edge density of
H^{*}
is such that we can apply the induction hypothesis to argue that
H^{*}
contains a loose Hamilton cycle
C^{*}
w.h.p. Now by symmetry, there is a probability of at least
1/r
that
\left\{e^{*}\right\}=e_{1}^{*}\cap e_{2}^{*}
for two consecutive edges of
C^{*}
. Suppose that
e_{i}^{*}
,
i=1,2
, are derived from
e_{1},e_{2}
of
H_{n,p_{2}}
. Given this there is a further probability of at least
1-1/r\geq 1/2
that we can replace
e_{1}^{*},e_{2}^{*}
by
e_{1},e,e_{2}
to obtain a loose Hamilton cycle in
H_{n,p_{2}}
.
∎

5
Final remarks

Kahn
[
14
]
also proved a hitting time version for perfect matchings. It is not obvious how to tighten the current approach to get a hitting time version for loose hamilton cycles. This is because we need a minimum degree of
\rho>2
to be able to apply Theorem
2
.
