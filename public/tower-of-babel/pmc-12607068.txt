Proficiency testing for urinary iodine and its impact on laboratory research related to iodine deficiency disorders (IDD) in the central Africa subregion
Authors: Julius Nsawir Bonglaisin, S M K Wangue, F L Yenika, L Manet, M G Kamini, B N Tata, G Nkeunen, P Sakava, C Matchawe, G N Medoua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe urinary iodine concentration (UIC) in humans is an essential component of the different phases of the fight against iodine deficiency disorders (IDD). As the etiology of goiter is diverse and the enlargement of the thyroid gland does not necessarily reflect the functional state or even the degree of iodine deficiency, the urinary iodine level reflects the true situation. Ambition for quality results pushed the iodine laboratory of the Centre for Food Safety and Nutrition Research, Yaounde, Cameroon, to participate in a proficiency testing (PT) programme initiated and coordinated by the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) of the United States of America. Background This study investigated the impact of PT on research related to IDD in Central Africa. Materials and methods This study uses z scores and trends to evaluate the PT results of laboratories obtained with the CDC from 2001 to 2018. Values of z scores above 2.0 (or below − 2.0) were considered unusual values, whereas z scores above 3.0 (or below − 3.0) were outliers. Acceptable values of z scores were expected to fall between ± 2.0. Data were categorized into very low iodine (0–30 µg/l), low iodine (40–110 µg/l), medium iodine, (120–240 µg/l) and high iodine (250–600 µg/l) contents. Results The results of the acceptable z scores within ± 2.0 interval were 63.2%, 68.4%, 52.6% and 84.4% for very low, low, medium and high iodine contents, respectively. The overall trend indicates no significant difference between the iodine laboratory results and CDC target values (p > 0.05 in all cases). Conclusion Participation in the PT program enabled the laboratory to introduce a better standard operating procedure, improve sample treatment and reduce personal errors to the minimal level, achieving great progress in IDD research. Background

The concentration of iodine in urine is easily obtainable, cost-efficient and well-accepted biochemical marker for iodine intake or status. Over 90% of the body’s iodine is excreted in the urine, where it is stable, reflecting the subject’s recent intake of this essential mineral. Its laboratory test is thus an essential part of the survey, monitoring and evaluation of iodine deficiency disorders (IDD) programs [1–4].

Studies have shown that clinical examinations of goiters and cases of cretinism can provide neither an exact appreciation of the deficiency nor the extent of IDD without the urinary iodine situation [5]. As the etiology of goiter is diverse and the enlargement of the thyroid gland does not necessarily reflect the functional state or even the degree of iodine deficiency, only urinary iodine reflects the true situation [6, 7]. In the same light, other forms of cretinisms exist and endemic cretinism caused by iodine deficiency can occasionally be exacerbated by naturally occurring goitrogens [8–10].

The production of accurate and precise measurements provides data integrity and facilitates evidence-based policy. It is thus imperative and even mandatory that laboratory estimation of iodine in urine be carried out with care to ensure that appropriate data obtained by the service provider are reliable in terms of their reproducibility and accuracy. Monitoring the quality of the urinary iodine results by using reliable quality control (QC) tools can thus be used to check the discarding of good reports and prevent nonconformance of data before they are released.

The Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) played the role of the mother laboratory in a proficiency testing (PT) program entitled “Ensuring the Quality of Urinary Iodine Procedures (EQUIP)” [11], put in place as a tool to control the quality of Urinary Iodine Estimation (UIE). Specifically, the CDC ensured monitoring of UIE of the International Resource Laboratory for Iodine (IRLI) of the Centre for Research in Food Safety and Nutrition (CRASAN) through this PT program, helping it to assess the accuracy of urinary iodine analyses as well as providing technical support.

IRLI of CRASAN with the acronym “IRLI-CRASAN” participated in this program by receiving, analyzing and sending back to this mother laboratory, the results of samples prepared through spiking by the CDC laboratory itself and shipped for analysis three times per year. This EQUIP scheme has been carried out since 2001, and this paper presents IRLI-CRASAN data from 2001 to 2018 received from the CDC. Although PT was used in conjunction with the Internal Control and Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) requirements, this quality control tool has effectively determined the performance of IRLI-CRASAN, in guaranteeing the laboratory reliability of its results. Although there have been variations at times, the IRLI-CRASAN in Yaounde does enjoy the fact that her results are around the mean of results from several world laboratories. Over the years, laboratories have been able to track progress in identifying the presence of possible analytical errors, enabling them to implement corrective actions to eliminate the causes of the errors.

Although the IRLI-CRASAN has not participated consistently in this PT since 2001, highlighting its impact on results is necessary to encourage the participation of potential IDD laboratory in PT programs. As IRLI-CRASAN continues to improve its standard operating procedure, corrective actions taken as a prerequisite of achieving accreditation to ISO/IEC 17,025 on general requirements for the competence of testing and calibration laboratories [12], demonstrate its commitment to meeting and maintaining high standards.

Methodology

This is a retrospective impact study that compares IRLI-CRASAN participation data over the years to the CDC target values, examining the different corrective measures put in place for better performance.

All PT data from urine samples prepared by spiking and shipping three times per year by the CDC to the IRLI-CRASAN for UIE from 2001 to 2018 were retrieved for both the CDC target values and the IRLI-CRASAN on Excel sheets. The quantitative data were initially analyzed and categorized to facilitate assessment. Every shipment package of spiked samples from the CDC, Atlanta, Georgia, USA, contained urine with very low iodine, low iodine, medium iodine and high iodine contents, in line with the WHO/UNICEF recommendation [13–15]. With this in mind, the retrieved data in archives were categorized as samples with (1) very low iodine content (0–30 µg/l); (2) low iodine content (40–110 µg/l); (3) medium iodine content (120–240 µg/l); and (4) high iodine content (250–600 µg/l).

Inconsistent data and even outliers were taken into account in order to identify areas requiring improvement but missing data due to participation interruption of IRLI-CRASAN were not factored in the performance assessment.

Trends or patterns with the CDC target values were established for each group, and z scores were calculated.

The value of the z score was calculated via the following formula: z = (X - µ)/σ, where z is the z score, X is the mean value of the IRLI-CRASAN, µ is the CDC mean or target value, and σ is the standard deviation of the IRLI-CRASAN value, which was calculated via Excel.

Values of z scores above 2.0 (or below − 2.0) were considered unusual values, whereas z scores above 3.0 (or below − 3.0) were outliers. Acceptable values of z scores were expected to fall between − 2.0 and 2.0.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed according to ISO 13528:2005 on statistical methods used in PT by interlaboratory comparisons [16]. The statistical measurement was based on each IRLI-CRASAN mean value’s relationship to the CDC mean or target value. This was done via the z score to assess how the IRLI-CRASAN result deviated from the CDC target value.

Trends were analyzed via Excel. The mean values of IRLI-CRASAN and those of CDC were subjected to one-way analysis of variance within a statistical package, according to the procedure of Steel and Torrie (1980) using Statgraphic 5.0. Significantly different means were separated via the methods of Duncan [17]. The values compared are presented on the basis of their t tests and p values provided by the statistical package.

Results

From 2001 to 2018, IRLI-CRASAN participated intermittently in the proficiency testing (PT) program. In a total of 19 participations there were three participations in 2007 and 2008, two from 2003 to 2005 and, 2018, one in 2001, 2006 and 2010 to 2012. Notably, there was no participation in 2009 and between 2013 and 2017.

Overall IRLI-CRASAN demonstrated a performance above 50% across the study groups, as shown by the z scores (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). IRLI-CRASAN performance was strongest in samples with high iodine concentrations, as demonstrated in Figs. 7. Conversely, apart from the latter, there is a need for improved performance with samples having very low to medium iodine contents (Figs. 1, 2, and 3). This indicates that the IRLI-CRASAN Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) on iodine is less sensitive.

Discussion

The available methods for UIE have been reviewed by Dunn et al. [4]. Mostly, the methods involve mild acid digestion and colorimetric procedures at specified time intervals. These procedures are less time consuming than the traditional method of dry ashing [18]. These available methods first of all use acid digestion under mild conditions and iodide ion is determined from its catalytic reduction of cerium ammonium sulfate in the presence of arsenic acid in the Sandell-Koltoff reaction [4]. IRLI-CRASAN has over the years been using the traditional dry alkaline ashing method [18] and this PT has enabled this laboratory to envisage transitioning to a more sensitive and accurate method for improved performance on samples having very low to medium iodine contents.

The accuracy and precision of iodine measurements are important for monitoring the status of iodine nutrition at the population level, and for ensuring quality in efforts to eliminate IDD in the Central Africa sub-region. Urinary iodine value is used as a guide to determine, give evidence, observe and monitor IDD and even its treatment [15]. To maintain reliable data, it is mandatory that tools for high precision be used to assess IDD or urinary iodine (UI) results to monitor iodine nutrition adequately in a country [19], in addition to adopting a stable and reliable laboratory method for the program. In the context of PTs, it is important to observe how the use of other tools, such as z score (used by the National Metrology Institute of South Africa, NMISA in its PT program [20]), which seems to be more stringent than trends/patterns or coefficients of variance, can contribute to the process of monitoring the elimination of IDD through quality results. The z scores calculated from the EQUIP data of the IRLI-CRASAN and CDC target or mean values are also satisfactorily good tests for accuracy and precision for UIC, as outlying results are indicated by specific z scores. Although the coefficient of variance was incorporated into CDC’s EQUIP PT program in 2001 and has provided important feedback as well as a basis for improving accuracy and precision over time to laboratories collaborating with this PT, it has certain limitations. An obvious problem arises when the mean of the variance is zero, making it impossible to calculate the CV for such as in the case of samples with iodine concentrations close to zero. In other words, the measurement scale should have a nonarbitrary zero value since the coefficient of variance cannot be calculated for zero values, but the z score can provide information in such a case. Secondly, no specific value for the coefficient of variation is a “good” value. It depends on the situation. However, in most cases, a low coefficient of variance could be better because it would mean that the spread of data values is low relative to the mean. However, z scores can provide specific information independent of zero values in the data. Specifically, z scores above 2.0 (or below − 2.0) are considered unusual values, whereas z scores above 3.0 (or below − 3.0) are outliers. Acceptable values of z scores are expected to fall between − 2.0 and 2.0 [21, 22].

Therefore, the z score is another useful tool that can be used for interlaboratory comparisons to assess laboratory performance, especially for results with values close to zero. This approach can significantly improve the quality of data on low iodine content from laboratories measuring iodine in different IDD laboratories. The value of it cannot be overemphasized, its utility has been extensively documented in the literature, demonstrating its role in performance evaluation and data visualization. For example, authors have used z scores in PT [22] and as a tool to enable the presentation of a clinical supervisor or practitioner to monitor a client’s multiple factors over time [23] and for the display and analysis of data [24].

As concern trends or patterns, similar approaches have been used in the development of proficiency testing for chemical analysis by authors [25]. The monotonic nonlinear relationship observed for the values of both laboratories, adds value and reinforces confidence in the IRLI-CRASAN’s findings between 2001 and 2018. Although IRLI-CRASAN used a laborious, less cost-effective, and time-intensive standard operating procedure (SOP) published in 1986 [18], it consistently produced reliable results for samples containing high iodine concentration like modern methods [26, 27]. This reliability is evident in its contributions to iodine sufficiency monitoring and public health interventions that affect millions of people across several Central and West African countries such as Gabon, Equatorial Guinea, the Central African Republic, Benin, Mali, Mauritania, and Chad [28]. More than 80% of the urine samples received from the sub-region for UIE by IRLI-CRASAN were observed to be very high in urinary iodine concentration and iodine excess has been revealed especially in big cities like Yaounde where median urinary iodine values have been diagnosed to be at the toxicity significant level [29]. Similarly, UIC data for the Cameroon population from 2002 to 2018 that was concomitantly obtained with data from refinery iodization, iodine content of salt at the port of entry in Cameroon, and the household-level consumption of iodized salt [30, 31] were also reliable.

Conclusion

Proficiency testing (PT) using tools such as the z score can adequately assess the accuracy and precision during UIE, especially in samples containing low iodine concentrations.

It is recommended that IRLI-CRASAN resume regular participation in the CDC proficiency testing program to support ongoing quality assurance. It is also recommended that the IRLI-CRASAN creates a network of laboratories at the industrial and academic levels and initiates PT for quality assurance at the level of the country and Central Africa subregion to improve performance in UIE. This can occur on the condition that the IRLI-CRASAN works to ensure its competence according to specific international standards, issued on the basis of the general requirements for the competence of PT providers and its implementation.
