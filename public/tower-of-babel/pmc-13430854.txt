Visceral leishmaniasis in leukemia patients, a great neglected disease in endemic countries: report of a retrospective study and systematic literature review
Authors: Shabnam Asfaram, Zahra Mashhadi, Manouchehr Barak, Soheila Molaei, Akbar Molaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVisceral leishmaniasis (VL) is an emerging infectious disease caused by the genus Leishmania and shares clinical features with some types of leukemia.MethodsIn a retrospective study (2020–2022), bone marrow aspirates (BMAs) from 52 children with leukemia in Ardabil Province, northwestern Iran, were examined for the presence of VL via microscopy and specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) methods. Additionally, the results of a systematic review of the literature on VL in these patients were reported.ResultsIn our retrospective study, microscopic investigations of BMA slides from 52 children with leukemia did not reveal Leishmania amastigotes. However, PCR detected amastigotes in three BMAs (5.8%). Simultaneously, a systematic review was designed, and 53 cases of VL with leukemia were retrieved. Half of these cases were from Brazil. Most cases were observed in children and adolescents (77.3%), with a higher prevalence in men (67.8%). The most common clinical and laboratory findings were: splenomegaly (96%), fever (90.5%), and anemia (92%). Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) was the most prevalent type of leukemia (66%). The systematic review on diagnostic accuracy showed that the serological method (sensitivity: 85%) proved to be more reliable than the microscopic method (sensitivity: 55%), while the PCR method detected all cases of leishmaniasis. Patients treated with anti-Leishmania drugs improved, except for one patient whose VL was reactivated one year later.ConclusionVL should be added to the list of possible differential diagnoses of leukemia patients, particularly in endemic areas, and physicians’ awareness of this condition prevents unnecessary invasive diagnostic procedures.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Visceral leishmaniasis, Leukemia, Diagnosis, Bone marrow

Introduction

Visceral leishmaniasis (VL) is an emerging infectious disease caused by the Leishmania genus and is transmitted to humans via the bite of Phlebotomus sandflies [1]. In Iranian patients, the disease can manifest in two primary forms, depending on the specific species of the parasite: VL and cutaneous leishmaniasis. VL is caused mainly by Leishmania donovani (L. donovani) in Africa and Asia, and by L. infantum/chagasi in the Mediterranean Basin, Central Asia, southern Europe, America, and parts of Africa [2]. In Iran, the main agent of VL is L. infantum, which is often reported in children. The parasite is transmitted to blood cells within the reticuloendothelial system via the skin, where it causes hepatomegaly and splenomegaly, and is life-threatening in more than 95% of untreated cases [3, 4]. As Leishmania is a specific pathogen that is not commonly present in the environment, the patient’s epidemiological history and nonspecific manifestations may be easily overlooked. Additionally, serological or PCR tests for this pathogen are not routinely performed in the laboratory, which may delay the diagnosis of this infection or lead to the misdiagnosis of VL as an autoimmune disease or malignancy [4]. Leukemia, a type of blood cancer, is more prevalent among white people and men, and is categorized into four types: acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), acute myeloid leukemia (AML), chronic lymphoblastic leukemia (CLL), and chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). All types of leukemia increase with age, except ALL, which peaks during infancy and early childhood [5]. Clinically, VL often coexists with ALL, complicating the diagnosis of the most prevalent childhood malignancy. Generally, VL-affected children exhibit symptoms similar to those of leukemia patients, including fever, anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, splenomegaly, hepatosplenomegaly, disseminated intravascular coagulation, an abnormal increase in the number of lymphocytes, and the presence of atypical cells and abnormal blasts in bone marrow aspirate (BMAs) [6]. Previously reported cases of VL in patients with various forms of leukemia treated with long-term anticancer chemotherapy indicate an overlap between malignant disease and leishmaniasis transmission [7]. In addition, a large number of studies suggest a possible link between leishmaniasis and the subsequent appearance of malignancy [8]. Despite this overlap, there is currently no systematic evidence on the frequency of VL in children diagnosed with leukemia, and how often it is missed by routine diagnostic methods in endemic regions. Existing reports primarily consist of individual case studies, which create a knowledge gap regarding the prevalence of VL among leukemia patients and the performance of diagnostic tests in this immunocompromised group. This study was therefore designed to detect VL in slides from children with leukemia in an endemic region of Iran and to compile all published cases of concurrent VL and leukemia through a systematic review. The goals are to identify shared clinical features, highlight diagnostic challenges, and sensitivities of available diagnostic methods.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this study, in addition to presenting a retrospective study of the coexistence of VL and leukemia by examining slides obtained from children with leukemia during two years (January 2020–December 2022) in Ardabil Province, northwestern Iran, a systematic review of the literature was conducted. In total, 52 BMA slides from children with leukemia in this province, where visceral leishmaniasis is highly endemic, were examined via microscopic and specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) methods. With permission from the children’s hospital, we utilized patients’ medical records to gather demographic, clinical, and laboratory information. Inclusion criteria included a confirmed leukemia diagnosis, and exclusion criteria involved slides that were inadequate or unreadable. The ethical committee of research at Ardabil University of Medical Sciences (IR-ARUMS-EC-1399-137) approved this study.

Microscopic examination

In our cross-sectional retrospective study, VL cases were screened on Giemsa-stained BMA slides in children aged 3 to 14 years with leukemia. Leishmania amastigotes were examined by light microscopy under an oil objective (100×). At least 200 fields per slide were fully assessed by two independent parasitologists and a third reviewer for resolution. A maximum of four slides was scanned daily to prevent exhaustion. Finally, the slides were scraped, and DNA was extracted.

Conventional PCR assay

Total DNA was extracted from Giemsa-stained BMA slides according to the protocol and stored separately in 2 mL tubes at − 20 °C for the PCR test [9, 10]. The PCR mixture was set up in an absolute volume of 25 µL with 12.5 µL of Master Mix (Fermentas, Hundred pM), 1µL each of the sense and antisense primers RV1 (59-CTTTTCTGG TCC CGC GGGTAG G-39) and RV2 (59-CCA CCTGGC CTATTT TAC ACC A-39), respectively, and 6 µL of extracted DNA and 4.5 µL of sterile distilled water. The cycling program consisted of an initial stage at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Then, a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min was performed. L. infantum DNA and water without DNA were included as positive and negative controls, respectively. Amplification of the products was verified on a 1.5% agarose gel, and a 145-base pair (bp) band of kDNA that belongs to L. infantum was identified by ultraviolet transillumination after staining with SYBR Green [10].

Systematic review of the literature

This review aimed to identify all reported cases of VL occurring in individuals with various types of leukemia. The systematic literature review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Additionally, the protocol for this review was registered in the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (registration code CRD 42024572247). We selected all publications on leukemia and leishmaniasis based on their titles and abstracts. Articles related to leukemia and other diseases were excluded from the study. We applied no limitations on publication language or status. Records were gathered from Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, PubMed, Scopus, the Scientific Information Database (SID), and the Cochrane Library. The non-MeSH/MeSH terms and keywords used for the search were: “visceral leishmaniasis,” “leishmaniasis,” or “Leishmania,” in combination with “leukemia,” “malignancy,” “acute lymphoblastic leukemia,” “chronic lymphocytic leukemia,” “acute myeloid leukemia,” and “chronic myeloid leukemia.” Three authors (SM, ShA, and AM) performed screening, data extraction, risk-of-bias assessment, and certainty of evidence rating independently and in duplicate. ZM and MB resolved any disagreements. Data from each article, including the first author, year of publication, geographical area, patient demographics, diagnostic methods, clinical features that led to delays in diagnosis, and outcomes, were extracted from the reviewed studies and tabulated (Table 1).

Results

Retrospective study

Microscopic examination

In the present study, microscopic examination of leukemia slides revealed no Leishmania amastigotes.

Conventional PCR assay

Conventional PCR on 52 BMA smears revealed a 145 bp band of kinetoplast DNA (kDNA) belonging to the genus Leishmania in three children (5.8%), which included two cases of ALL in females aged 6 and 14 years and one case of AML in a 6-year-old male. The causative Leishmania species, L. infantum, was identified according to epidemiological criteria (Fig. 2).

Systematic review of the literature

Figure 1 illustrates the search and selection procedures for our systematic literature review. We included 24 articles reporting 50 cases of VL in patients with leukemia. The first report in the world was published in 1983 in Spain, and the most recent report appeared in 2023 in China. Additionally, we included three positive smear samples from leukemia patients in Ardabil, resulting in a total of 53 positive samples. In the systematic review, the causative species of Leishmania were identified in 40 cases based on epidemiological information, such as a history of contact, residence, or travel in endemic areas, and in some instances through PCR testing. The identified species included: L. donovani in 8 cases, L. chagasi in 24 cases, and L. infantum in 8 cases. However, the species was not identified in the remaining 13 cases. In detail, 25 cases were acquired in Brazil, eight in Italy, five in Iran, three in China, two each in Nepal, Morocco, and India, and one each in Spain, France, Portugal, the United Kingdom, Belgium, and Malta. Nearly half of the cases were reported in Brazil, where L. infantum chagasi is endemic. In terms of age, 41 patients (77.3%) were children and adolescents under 20 years of age (range 4 months–20 years), whereas the remaining patients were middle-aged and older adults (range, 29–80 years). Among the 28 patients with known sex, 19 (67.8%) were male, and 9 (32.2%) were female. Of the 53 patients with known clinical presentations, splenomegaly was observed in 51 (96%), fever in 48 (90.5%), hepatomegaly in 37 (69.8%), lymphadenopathy in 32 (64%), pallor in 34 (64%), weight loss in 32 (60%), and anorexia in 30 (56.6%) patients. Among the laboratory data documented in all patients except one, anemia and pancytopenia were observed in 49 (92%) and 17 (32%) patients, respectively. Of these, 66% (35 of 53) were classified as ALL, and 51 cases underwent BMA; in 55% (28/51) of the cases, amastigotes were detected microscopically, whereas serological tests were positive in 85% (12/14) of the cases. PCR-based BMA identification of Leishmania spp. was performed on seven samples, and positive results were obtained in all the samples. Regarding antileishmanial therapy, the treatment details were unspecified for 29 patients. Out of the 24 patients who received treatment, five patients (12.5%) were treated with amphotericin B (AMB), eight patients (33.3%) received liposomal amphotericin B (LAMB), two patients (8.3%) were given pentamidine, four patients (16.6%) received glucantime, two patients (8.3%) were treated with pentostam, and three patients (12.5%) received a combination of allopurinol and glucantime. Three patients (12.5%) died after treatment due to refractory leukemia, pulmonary infection, and septic shock. Two patients (8.3%) dropped out of treatment, and one patient (4.2%) was not treated with glucantime, leading to reactivation of the VL one year later.

Discussion

In this study, the analysis of BMA was negative for Leishmania amastigotes. However, we detected three positive smears of VL in children with leukemia using the PCR method and systematically reviewed the previous literature. All three positive smear samples were under 15 years old and were from cities labeled as provincial endemic for VL. In some studies, BMA analysis failed to detect Leishmania amastigotes, whereas the PCR method was positive for Leishmania identification [30, 31]. Also, in a Brazilian study of 24 children with leukemia, VL was microscopically detected in only five cases [22]. However, in studies conducted in Iran, amastigotes were observed in BMA, and these findings were confirmed through PCR and serological methods, which differed from the results of the current study [18, 19]. The most important endemic areas of VL in Iran are the northwestern and southern regions, with almost 100 new symptomatic cases reported annually. Approximately 99% of kala-azar cases in these areas primarily affect children under 12years of age and rarely occur in adults [35]. In areas where VL is endemic, many healthy adults have antibodies against Leishmania, even if they do not recall any clinical signs of this infection. In these areas, a latent infection can progress to overt disease under the influence of stresses that upset the immune system, or immunosuppressive therapy can activate latent leishmaniasis, and Leishmania can play an opportunistic role in immunosuppressed patients. Although immune suppression from treatments or diseases is rarely described as a risk factor for VL, the most common underlying cause of immunodeficiency in patients with VL, besides HIV infection, is hematological malignancies [34]. Race and age are the main factors affecting leukemia incidence. ALL is the most common type of pediatric cancer and has a poorer outcome in adults compared to children [36]. Exposure to chemical agents through breastfeeding, contact with contaminated clothing and environments, or use of pesticides and insecticides in homes has been associated with increased risk of leukemia in the pediatric population [37]. Compared with those in children, the incidence, prevalence, and mortality of AML are greater in elderly individuals. This type of leukemia has an inferior prognosis and relatively short survival, accounting for nearly 80% of leukemia cases [38]. A review indicated that leukemia incidence is most prevalent among white, black, Hispanic, and Asian/Pacific Islander populations. Although Leukemia affects both sexes, it is more common in males and has a higher mortality rate [39]. Coinfections are a serious issue in patients with hematological malignancies, as chronic infection can promote cancer progression and vice versa [25]. In both diseases, innate immunity, including natural killer cells and macrophages, is involved, along with adaptive immunity, such as CD4/CD8 cells and regulatory T cells. The development of a Th1 immune response by CD4+ T cells usually helps control both conditions, while a weakened Th1 response can lead to parasite growth in target organs or uncontrolled cancer progression. The presence of Leishmania and tumor cells, combined with prolonged inflammatory signaling and deficiencies in anti-inflammatory mechanisms, can result in chronic inflammation. This chronic inflammation can promote tumor development and enhance parasite proliferation. Ultimately, the environment created by Leishmania infection can support cancer progression, and, conversely, cancer can also affect the infection [40]. However, immunosuppression caused by leukemia increases the risk of leishmaniasis, and chemotherapy can inhibit the proliferation of intracellular amastigotes, complicating the detection of amastigotes in the BMA. Therefore, in most cases, the traditional observation method for identifying amastigotes in BMA, lymph nodes, or spleen, which is considered the gold standard for diagnosing leishmaniasis, is not effective, as its sensitivity and specificity are limited owing to its dependence on operator skill and parasite load [1]. Hence, serological tests can sometimes be used to diagnose VL in patients with leukemia [41]. The indirect immunofluorescence assay has been noted for its high sensitivity and specificity, approaching 100%. This test is particularly useful for diagnosing Leishmania, and our experience supports these findings. Additionally, this serological test is effective for monitoring the progression of VL, because during treatment, the antibody titer decreases, and it becomes negative if the treatment is successful. However, anticancer drugs such as miltefosine have strong leishmanicidal effects, suggesting that the mechanisms supporting parasite and cancer growth are similar, can interfere with antibody production, and may result in missed or delayed serological diagnosis of VL [42, 43]. As a result, advanced molecular techniques are reliable for disease follow-up when morphological characteristics and serological methods are uncertain or when fresh samples are unavailable. However, this approach is not commonly used in resource-limited countries [44]. VL may develop in leukemia patients because of the transfer of polluted blood, reactivation of latent infections due to immunosuppression caused by ALL or its treatment, or as a new infection [18]. Considering the low number of VL cases in leukemia patients in this endemic region, reactivation seems unlikely, yet it remains a possibility. Therefore, physicians, especially in endemic areas, should be vigilant for the clinical signs of VL and consider the possibility of VL in cases of relapse in individuals with complete remission of leukemia or antibiotic-resistant fever. Thus, it is prudent to keep infectious causes as a differential diagnosis in patients with worsening leukemia. This study had several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the results. First, the relatively small sample size may not have accurately represented the populations in other regions of Iran. Additionally, a convenience sampling method was used, which could have introduced a selection bias, ultimately limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence that VL may occur in a small subset of pediatric leukemia patients in regions where the disease is endemic. In a low burden of parasites, it may go undetected when diagnosis relies solely on microscopy. By combining a retrospective review with a systematic compilation of published cases, we highlight the diagnostic limitations of conventional methods in immunocompromised cases and demonstrate the added value of PCR testing. However, the small number of PCR-positive cases in our cohort and the retrospective nature of the analysis limit the generalizability of our findings. Therefore, visceral leishmaniasis should not be routinely assumed in all leukemia patients but may be considered selectively when clinical features are unexplained, treatment-resistant, or atypical for leukemia progression, especially in endemic settings. Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, the incorporation of species-specific molecular assays or sequencing to confirm Leishmania species, and the evaluation of diagnostic algorithms that integrate serology, PCR, and clinical indicators. Strengthening molecular diagnostic capacity could enhance early detection, minimize misdiagnosis, and facilitate more informed clinical decision-making for immunocompromised pediatric populations.
