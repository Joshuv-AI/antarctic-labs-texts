Development and characterization of lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy
Authors: Hitendra Raval, Rohit Sindhav, Ankit Bihola, Priyanka Suvera, P. S. Prajapati, Shaikh Adil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Whey represents one of the largest dairy by-product streams worldwide, retaining 55–60% of the nutrients originally present in milk, yet its high lactose content, limited solubility, and environmental disposal challenges restrict its utilization; enzymatic lactose hydrolysis using β-galactosidase improves sweetness, reduces the risk of lactose crystallization, enhances digestibility, and broadens its applicability in frozen confections. This study aimed to develop, optimize, and characterize a reduced-sugar, lactose-hydrolyzed whey-based frozen candy by determining the optimal enzyme concentration for lactose hydrolysis, evaluating the combined effects of sugar and CMC stabilizer on compositional, physico-chemical, and sensory attributes, and assessing the suitability of four flavour emulsions, orange, pineapple, kalakhatta, and jaljira. Sweet whey was hydrolyzed using Maxilact L5000 (0.2–0.4 mL/L), with 0.3 mL/L selected based on achieving ~ 85% lactose conversion; candies were formulated with four sugar levels (18–24%) and three CMC levels (0.2–0.4%) and evaluated for composition, pH, acidity, melting resistance, and sensory assessment. Lactose hydrolysis increased considerably with enzyme concentration, plateauing at 0.3 mL/L. Sugar level significantly influenced total solids, carbohydrates, acidity, melting resistance, and flavour scores, while CMC level significantly affected body, texture, and melting quality due to its water-binding and cryo-stabilizing effects. The optimized formulation, 22% sugar and 0.4% CMC, exhibited desirable composition (TS 30.75%, fat 0.15%, protein 0.75%), physico-chemical characteristics (pH 3.91; acidity 0.53% citric acid; melting resistance 82.62%), and good sensory acceptability. Among the flavours tested, orange achieved the highest consumer preference (8.70), followed by jaljira (8.40), while pineapple and kalakhatta showed comparatively lower acceptability. These findings demonstrate that reduced-sugar frozen candy can be formulated using lactose-hydrolyzed whey, enabling 35% relative reduction in sucrose without influencing sensory quality.

Introduction

Whey, the aqueous by-product obtained during the manufacture of cheese, chhana, paneer, and casein, represents one of the world’s largest streams of dairy effluents, accounting for approximately 85–90% of the volume of milk used for coagulation and retaining about 55% of its original nutrients1,2. Globally, more than 200 million tons of whey are generated annually, and India, being the world’s largest milk producer, contributes substantially to this volume due to its expanding production of acid- and rennet-coagulated dairy products1,3. Sweet whey typically contains 4.5–5.0% lactose, 0.6–0.9% whey proteins, 0.5–0.7% minerals, and a wide array of micronutrients including water-soluble vitamins, immunoglobulins, lactoferrin, and bioactive peptides4. While this composition underscores whey’s immense nutritional potential, the industry continues to face substantial challenges in its utilization5,6. Conventional practices often involve direct disposal into effluent streams, which is both economically costly and ecologically detrimental2,7. The biological oxygen demand (BOD) of liquid whey can exceed 35,000–50,000 mg/L, leading to rapid microbial proliferation, oxygen depletion in receiving waters, and high-pressure demands on effluent treatment facilities8,9. Given increasingly stringent environmental regulations and sustainability imperatives, valorization of whey into profitable and nutritionally beneficial food products has become a critical global priority1,3,10.

Among whey components, lactose constitutes the predominant fraction of its total solids (~ 70–75%). Despite its nutritional importance, several biochemical and technological limitations restrict its direct use in many food systems11,12 Lactose exhibits low solubility (18 g/100 mL at 20 °C) and forms monohydrate crystals upon cooling, creating textural defects such as graininess and sandiness in frozen desserts, confectioneries, and concentrated dairy systems13,14. Moreover, lactose requires the presence of intestinal β-galactosidase for digestion; therefore, populations with lactase non-persistence, estimated to comprise nearly 65–70% of the world’s adults, experience symptoms of lactose intolerance when consuming products with high lactose content14,15. The condition is particularly prevalent in Asian and African populations, with reported intolerance rates exceeding 70% in several regions of India. Undigested lactose undergoes bacterial fermentation in the colon, producing excessive gas, short-chain fatty acids, and osmotic shifts that lead to abdominal discomfort, bloating, diarrhea, and flatulence. These physiological challenges limit the consumer acceptance of whey-based foods despite their nutritional advantages1,3.

A scientifically and commercially viable solution to these challenges is the enzymatic hydrolysis of lactose, a process catalyzed by β-galactosidase (EC 3.2.1.23)13,16. During hydrolysis, lactose is cleaved into glucose and galactose, two monosaccharides with considerably higher relative sweetness values (glucose: 74% and galactose: 32% compared to sucrose = 100%), thereby increasing the perceived sweetness of whey16,17. Lactose hydrolysis also improves solubility, inhibits crystallization, and enhances fermentation efficiency18. From a physicochemical standpoint, lactose reduction improves freeze–thaw stability, minimizes crystallization-induced defects, and expands the application of whey in frozen and semi-solid food matrices. Enzymatic hydrolysis is therefore recognized as a pivotal technological intervention enabling the development of lactose-free and low-lactose foods and has gained wide industrial acceptance worldwide16,19.

Despite extensive global research on whey beverages, bakery applications, infant formulae, and protein concentrates, there remains a major innovation gap in the development of frozen confectionery products using lactose-hydrolyzed whey, especially in India where frozen candies, ice pops, and frozen desserts represent a rapidly expanding consumer segment19. Frozen candies offer an excellent vehicle for incorporating whey due to their high consumer acceptance, low production cost, compatibility with sweet-acidic flavor systems, and potential for large-scale daily consumption. Incorporating lactose-hydrolyzed whey into candy matrices not only increases nutritional value but also addresses critical issues such as lactose intolerance, sugar reduction, and utilization of underexploited dairy streams. Moreover, the enhanced sweetness of hydrolyzed whey provides a strategic advantage: the sugar content required for achieving desirable sweetness can be reduced by up to 12–15%, offering a healthier and more cost-effective confectionery product19.

Hydrocolloids such as carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) play an important technological role in frozen confection systems by improving viscosity, stabilizing air cells, inhibiting recrystallization, enhancing meltdown resistance, and improving the smoothness and body of the final product20–22. However, the functional interactions between CMC and lactose-hydrolyzed whey components, particularly under freezing conditions, are yet to be studied in the context of candy production. Additionally, flavour compatibility with whey matrices is not straightforward; whey contains volatile compounds such as acetaldehyde and low molecular weight acids that may interfere with flavour profiles20,22. Hence, understanding the suitability and consumer appeal of various fruit and spice-based flavours becomes crucial for designing a market-ready whey candy.

Given these scientific, nutritional, technological, and environmental considerations, the present research was designed to address critical gaps in whey utilization by developing a novel, reduced-sugar, lactose-hydrolyzed whey-based frozen candy and evaluating its functional, sensory, and industrial feasibility. Specifically, the study aimed to (i) optimize enzymatic hydrolysis of lactose in sweet whey using β-galactosidase under controlled pH–temperature conditions, (ii) standardize the formulation by examining the effects of varying sugar and CMC levels on compositional and physical properties of the candy, (iii) evaluate sensory attributes via untrained panelists to identify the most acceptable formulation, and (iv) determine the suitability of different flavor emulsions (orange, pineapple, jaljira, kalakhatta) for enhancing consumer acceptability.

By integrating enzymatic biotechnology, food formulation science, and sensory evaluation with sustainability-driven dairy processing, this study offers a comprehensive framework for transforming whey, once considered a problematic waste stream, into a desirable, nutritionally enhanced, and economically viable frozen confection. The findings provide valuable insights for the dairy industry, with strong potential for implementation at cooperative and industrial scales, thereby contributing to circular processing, resource efficiency, and innovative dairy product development.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sweet cheddar cheese whey sourced from Vidya Dairy, Anand Agricultural University, Anand, Gujarat, India, which served as the primary dairy substrate. β-D-galactosidase enzyme Maxilact L5000 (DSM Food Specialties, Denmark), derived from Kluyveromyces marxianus var. lactis and possessing a declared activity of ≥ 5000 NLU/g, was used as the lactose-hydrolyzing enzyme. Commercial-grade refined sucrose and food-grade liquid glucose (92% total solids) procured from the local market were used as sweetening components. CMC obtained from LOBA Chemie Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India, served as the stabilizing agent, while LR-grade citric acid supplied by SD Fine Chemicals Ltd., Mumbai, India, was used for acidity adjustment. Four commercial flavour emulsions, orange, pineapple, jaljira, and kalakhatta, were procured from CARAFE, Wadhavan, Gujarat, India, for evaluating flavour suitability in the lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy formulations.

Optimization of enzyme concentration

Lactose hydrolysis protocol

Optimization of lactose hydrolysis was undertaken to identify the enzyme concentration capable of delivering the desired hydrolysis efficiency while maintaining product quality. Lactose hydrolysis was performed using Maxilact L5000 following manufacturer recommendations. Three enzyme levels, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4 mL/L of whey, corresponding to 1180, 1770, and 2360 NLU/L, respectively, were selected to represent a practical industrial dosage range and to determine the minimal effective concentration for lactose hydrolysis. Prior to enzymatic treatment, the pH of whey was adjusted precisely to 6.6 using 1 N KOH, aligning with the optimal pH range for β-D-galactosidase activity. The whey–enzyme mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h, a temperature that supports maximal catalytic efficiency while preventing protein denaturation. During incubation, samples were gently mixed at 30-min intervals to facilitate uniform enzyme–substrate contact and avoid localized substrate saturation. At the end of the hydrolysis period, enzymatic activity was terminated by heating the samples to 80 °C for 5 min, ensuring complete and irreversible inactivation while preventing undesirable thermal changes in the whey matrix. The target hydrolysis level was 80–85%, which was selected to achieve optimal sweetness enhancement, lactose reduction, and improved solubility, all of which are desirable for frozen candy systems. This degree of hydrolysis also prevents lactose crystallization during freezing and storage, thereby enhancing the final product’s texture and consumer acceptability.

Formulation optimization

A systematic formulation optimization was carried out to establish a stable, palatable, and technologically robust lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy. Two key formulation variables, sugar concentration and CMC level, were investigated due to their critical influence on sweetness perception, total solids, viscosity, freezing behavior, and melting resistance. Sugar was incorporated at 18% (w/w), 20% (w/w), 22% (w/w), and 24% (w/w), allowing assessment of its effects on overall sweetness, body, crystallization tendencies, and consumer preference. CMC was evaluated at 0.2% (w/w), 0.3% (w/w), and 0.4% (w/w), representing low to moderate stabilizer levels commonly used in frozen confections to improve texture, reduce iciness, and enhance structural stability. Liquid glucose was included at a constant 5% (w/w) across all formulations to impart smooth body characteristics, inhibit sucrose crystallization, and contribute to freezing point depression. Flavour was standardized at 2.5 mL/L during screening trials to ensure uniform aroma intensity across all treatments.

Each experimental formulation was subjected to evaluation comprising compositional analysis (total solids, fat, protein, lactose, ash), physicochemical assessment (pH, titratable acidity, melting resistance), and sensory analysis using untrained panelists. In addition to the core optimization, a flavour suitability study was conducted using four commercially available flavour emulsions, orange, pineapple, jaljira, and kalakhatta, selected for their relevance to the Indian frozen confectionery market. Sensory attributes including flavour intensity, balance, compatibility with the whey matrix, and overall acceptability were assessed to determine the most preferred flavour variant.

Preparation of lactose hydrolyzed whey candy

The preparation of lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy involved a series of controlled processing operations designed to ensure optimal enzymatic conversion, ingredient functionality, and structural stability of the final frozen confection. Initially, the whey was filtered through muslin cloth to remove coarse particulates and pasteurized at 72 °C for 15 s to eliminate pathogenic and spoilage microorganisms, followed by rapid cooling to 37 °C, the temperature required for efficient enzymatic activity. The pH of the whey was carefully adjusted to 6.6 using 1 N KOH, after which Maxilact L5000 was added at the optimized concentration of 0.3 mL/L, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 4 h with intermittent agitation at 30-min intervals to facilitate uniform enzyme–substrate interaction. After the hydrolysis period, the enzyme was thermally inactivated by heating the whey to 80 °C for 5 min to ensure complete cessation of catalytic activity. Subsequently, the formulation components, refined sucrose, liquid glucose (5%), and CMC at predetermined concentrations, were incorporated into the hydrolyzed whey under continuous stirring to ensure complete solubilization and homogeneous dispersion. For effective hydration and dispersion of CMC, the mixture was heated to approximately 70 °C and subjected to high-shear mixing at ~ 3000 rpm for 20 min. The resulting candy mix was repasteurized at 72 °C for 15 s to enhance microbiological safety and then cooled to ambient temperature before its pH was adjusted to 3.90 using a 20% (w/v) citric acid solution to achieve the desired acidity and flavour balance. Flavour emulsions (orange, pineapple, jaljira, or kalakhatta) were then added at standardized levels to ensure uniform sensory expression across batches. The prepared mix was filled into 60-mL plastic moulds (12 cm length × 3 cm diameter) and subsequently frozen in a candy-freezing machine for approximately 40 min to establish the characteristic shape. This was followed by a hardening step at − 25 °C for 50 min to stabilize the internal microstructure and enhance freeze–thaw stability. The finished candies were stored at − 18 °C.

Analytical characterization of lactose hydrolyzed whey candy

The analytical characterization of whey, candy mix, and finished lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy was conducted using standard reference methods to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. Total solids were quantified using the Mojonnier gravimetric method (AOAC23,. Fat content was determined using the classical Gerber method AOAC23. Protein content was measured by the semi-micro Kjeldahl method (AOAC23, using a Kjel-plus digestion system and a semi-automatic distillation unit. Lactose content in both unhydrolyzed and hydrolyzed whey was quantified using the colorimetric procedure of Teles et al.24, involving clarification, preparation of reaction mixtures, and absorbance measurement at 540 nm using a double-beam spectrophotometer; the degree of lactose hydrolysis was expressed as the percentage reduction in lactose content after enzymatic treatment relative to the original whey. Ash content was determined by dry ashing in a muffle furnace following AOAC23 specifications. Total carbohydrate content was calculated by difference, subtracting the measured values of protein, fat, ash, and moisture from 100.

Physico-chemical analyses of lactose hydrolyzed whey candy

The pH of whey, candy mix, and finished candy samples was measured at ambient temperature using a calibrated digital pH meter (Systronics Model 335, India). Titratable acidity was determined following the25 methodology and expressed as percentage citric acid. Melting resistance, an important indicator of frozen confection quality, was evaluated according to the method described by26. For this analysis, a 100 g candy sample was placed in a Buchner funnel and allowed to melt at 37 ± 1 °C for 40 min, after which the melted fraction was weighed. Melting resistance (%) was calculated using the formula:

Melting resistance (%) = [(A₁ – A₂)/A₁] × 100,where A₁ is the initial weight of the sample (100 g) and A₂ is the weight of the melted portion.

Sensory evaluation of lactose hydrolyzed whey candy

Sensory evaluation of the lactose-hydrolyzed whey candies was conducted using a consumer-oriented approach involving 50 untrained panelists consisting of 25 males and 25 females aged between 23 and 46 years. This larger, untrained panel was intentionally used to obtain natural consumer responses and better reflect real-world acceptability patterns. Prior to participation, all panelists were informed about the nature and purpose of the study, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The sensory study protocol was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines for human participation in food sensory research, ensuring voluntary participation, anonymity of responses, and the right to withdraw at any stage without consequence. All evaluations were carried out in a controlled sensory testing environment with uniform lighting and minimal distractions to reduce external bias. Candy samples were stored at − 18 °C and served at the same temperature to maintain consistency in textural and melting characteristics across evaluations. Each sample was assigned a random three-digit code and presented to panelists in a randomized order to minimize positional, order, and psychological biases. Panelists evaluated five key sensory attributes, flavour, colour and appearance, body and texture, melting quality, and overall acceptability, using a nine-point hedonic scale ranging from 1 (“dislike extremely”) to 9 (“like extremely”). Potable water at room temperature was provided between samples to cleanse the palate and minimize carryover effects, and adequate time was allowed between evaluations to prevent sensory fatigue. The individual scores generated by the 50 untrained panelists were collected, tabulated, and averaged, providing a dataset that reflects consumer perception of the effects of lactose hydrolysis, sugar and stabilizer levels, and flavour type on the sensory quality and overall acceptability of the whey-based candy formulations.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data generated from compositional, physico-chemical, and sensory evaluations were subjected to statistical analysis to determine the significance of treatment effects. Completely Randomized Design (CRD) was employed, and statistical computations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For each measured attribute, mean values and corresponding standard deviations were calculated to characterize central tendency and variability. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to test for statistically significant differences among treatments, and when the F-test indicated significance (p < 0.05), Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) were applied to identify specific pairwise differences.

Results and discussions

Effect of enzyme concentration on lactose conversion

The extent of lactose hydrolysis in whey increased progressively with increasing enzyme concentration, as shown in Table 1. At the lowest enzyme dosage of 0.2 mL/L, the lactose content of whey decreased from an initial value of 4.9% (4.8–5.0%) to 1.30% (1.27–1.33%), corresponding to a hydrolysis level of 73.39% (73.0–73.8%). Increasing the enzyme concentration to 0.3 mL/L further reduced the residual lactose to 0.76% (0.74–0.78%), resulting in a significantly higher hydrolysis percentage of 84.35% (84.32–84.38%). The highest enzyme level tested, 0.4 mL/L, produced a similar residual lactose content of 0.76% (0.74–0.77%) and a hydrolysis extent of 84.37% (84.33–84.40%), which was statistically comparable to the 0.3 mL/L treatment (p > 0.05).

The observation that the hydrolysis level plateaued at enzyme concentrations above 0.3 mL/L is consistent with the enzymatic saturation behavior reported for β-galactosidase preparations derived from Kluyveromyces marxianus, which typically exhibit maximum catalytic efficiency between 35 and 40 °C and pH 6.5–7.027–30. Achieving approximately 85% lactose hydrolysis is technologically advantageous for frozen whey-based confections because it minimizes the likelihood of lactose crystallization during freezing and storage, which otherwise leads to grittiness and poor mouthfeel. Additionally, the conversion of lactose into glucose and galactose enhances the intrinsic sweetness of the whey matrix, enabling the development of reduced-sugar confections without affecting palatability27–30.

Based on the data presented in Table 1, 0.3 mL/L of Maxilact L5000 was identified as the optimal enzyme concentration, achieving high hydrolysis efficiency while avoiding unnecessary enzyme overuse. This concentration was therefore selected for the subsequent formulation and process optimization of lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy.

Selection of sugar level (G) and stabilizer level (S) for lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy

Preliminary formulation trials were conducted to determine the appropriate level of liquid glucose, sugar (G), and stabilizer (S) required to achieve the desired structural, sensory, and melting characteristics in lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy. Liquid glucose was initially tested at two levels, 5% and 6% (w/w of whey), to evaluate its influence on body, texture, freezing quality, and overall structural integrity. Candies formulated with 5% liquid glucose exhibited a desirable balance of softness, cohesiveness, and shape retention, while the 6% level produced excessively soft candies with reduced structural strength and poor handling characteristics during demoulding and sensory serving. Since liquid glucose functions as a bodying agent, humectant, and freezing point depressant, its higher level likely increased hygroscopicity and plasticity, thereby influencing firmness31,32. Based on these observations, 5% liquid glucose was identified as the optimum level and was therefore selected.

Using the optimized hydrolyzed whey, a factorial combination of four sugar concentrations (18%, 20%, 22%, and 24%), designated as G1, G2, G3, and G4, respectively, and three CMC stabilizer levels (0.2%, 0.3%, and 0.4%), designated as S1, S2, and S3, was evaluated to determine their combined influence on the quality attributes of lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy. A control sample (G5) was also prepared using 34% sugar in unhydrolyzed whey, representing the conventional sucrose-based candy formulation used as a standard for comparison.

The combined influence of sugar concentration and CMC level produced marked effects on the compositional, functional, and sensory attributes of the lactose-hydrolyzed whey candies. Variations in sugar content directly altered the total solids of the mix, which in turn affected viscosity, freezing behavior, and glass transition characteristics. Higher sugar levels increased the osmotic concentration and contributed to a greater build-up of soluble solids, enhancing sweetness intensity, modifying freezing point depression, and promoting a smoother texture due to reduced ice crystallization. In contrast, lower sugar levels produced candies with reduced total solids and comparatively weaker structural integrity, highlighting the important role of sugar as both a sweetener and a functional bulking agent in frozen confection systems33–35.

CMC, on the other hand, played a critical techno-functional role by modulating mix viscosity, enhancing water-holding capacity, and reducing ice recrystallization during freezing and storage. As a hydrocolloid, CMC improved the structural cohesiveness of the candy matrix, imparted desirable chewiness, and substantially contributed to melt resistance by limiting the mobility of free water and stabilizing the microstructure. Increasing the CMC concentration progressively enhanced these attributes, yielding candies with improved body, smoother mouthfeel, and delayed meltdown20,22.

Effect of sugar and stabilizer levels on the composition of lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy

The compositional characteristics of lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy were strongly influenced by variations in sugar concentration, whereas the concentration of CMC stabilizer and the sugar–stabilizer interaction (G × S) demonstrated no statistically significant effect (p > 0.05) on any measured compositional attribute. The findings provided in Table 2 clearly indicate that sucrose level was the major determinant of total solids, fat, protein, carbohydrate, and ash contents, reflecting the central role of sugar as a structural and functional ingredient in frozen confection matrices.

Total solids (TS) increased progressively with increasing sugar levels, demonstrating a clear positive linear relationship. TS values ranged from 26.70% at 18% sugar to 32.69% at 24% sugar, while the control sample prepared using 34% sugar exhibited a markedly higher TS of 41.34%. This trend is expected because sucrose contributes directly to soluble solids and is a major fraction of the dry matter in confection systems. From a physicochemical standpoint, higher TS promotes enhanced viscosity, reduced free water, and improved freezing characteristics due to increased solute concentration and reduced ice fraction. The non-significant influence of CMC on TS is scientifically justified because hydrocolloids contribute minimally to gravimetric solids at functional inclusion levels (< 0.5%), serving primarily as rheology modifiers rather than bulk solids contributors20.

Fat content demonstrated an inverse relationship with sugar level, significantly decreasing (p < 0.05) from 0.18% at 18% sugar to 0.13% at 24% sugar, with the lowest fat content observed in the control sample (0.09%). This pattern reflects a dilution effect: higher sugar concentrations proportionally reduce the contribution of whey lipids in the finished product. Since whey contains inherently low fat and sugar contributes no fat, increasing sugar concentration inevitably decreases the relative percentage of fat. The absence of any significant effect of CMC on fat content is scientifically consistent, as CMC is a carbohydrate-based hydrocolloid devoid of lipid components20.

Protein content varied between 0.69% and 0.76%, with lactose-hydrolyzed whey candies (G1–G4) exhibiting significantly higher protein levels compared to the unhydrolyzed control (G5). This increase is attributable to the lower sugar additions in G1–G4, resulting in a proportionally higher representation of whey proteins in the total solid matrix. Furthermore, lactose hydrolysis does not affect protein integrity, preserving protein proportion in the finished candy. The stabilizer concentration again showed no measurable effect on protein content, consistent with CMC’s non-protein nature and its negligible compositional impact at low usage levels20.

Total carbohydrate content increased significantly with sugar level, ranging from 25.27% (18% sugar) to 31.42% (24% sugar), with the highest value observed for the control (40.33%), which contained substantially more sucrose. Because carbohydrates arise almost entirely from sucrose, lactose, glucose, and galactose, it is unsurprising that sugar level emerges as the dominant contributor. The consistently non-significant effect of CMC on carbohydrate content reflects its minimal inclusion level and its minor contribution to total carbohydrate mass20.

Ash content of the candy, ranged from 0.24% to 0.48%, with significantly higher ash observed in formulations containing lower sugar concentrations (G1). As sugar levels increased, ash contents decreased proportionally due to simple dilution. Since minerals originate primarily from the whey itself and not from added sugars or stabilizers, this inverse trend is expected.

The compositional results conclusively demonstrate that sugar level is the principal driver of chemical composition in lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy, consistent with the predominant contribution of sucrose to total solids, carbohydrates, and proportional dilution of protein, fat, and minerals. Conversely, CMC contributed functionally to structure and stability but had no measurable effect on composition at the concentrations studied (0.2–0.4%). The non-significant interaction between sugar and stabilizer (G × S) further reinforces that the compositional attributes of the candy are largely determined by sucrose concentration rather than hydrocolloid level.

Effect of sugar and stabilizer levels on physico-chemical characteristics of lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy

An evaluation of the physico-chemical properties, pH, titratable acidity, and melting resistance, of lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy formulated with varying sugar and CMC levels (Table 3) reveals clear functional roles for each ingredient in determining product stability and performance. While pH remained uniform across treatments, significant shifts were observed in acidity and melting behavior, demonstrating the interplay of colligative properties, solute concentration, and hydrocolloid–water interactions in the frozen matrix.

pH

The pH of all candy formulations remained remarkably stable (3.90–3.91), regardless of sugar level, stabilizer concentration, or their interaction (p > 0.05). This uniformity is primarily attributable to the controlled acidification step in the formulation, where citric acid was used to adjust the mix to a fixed pH of 3.90 ± 0.01. Once acidified, the buffering capacity of whey, dominated by phosphate, citrate, and casein-derived peptides, remains low at this acidic range, limiting the ability of added ingredients to further influence hydrogen ion concentration36,37.

Neither sucrose nor CMC possesses ionizable groups capable of altering proton activity, which explains their negligible impact on pH. From a food safety viewpoint, maintaining a consistent pH below 4.0 is crucial, as it suppresses enzymatic spoilage, inhibits pathogenic microorganisms, and enhances product shelf stability36,37.

Titratable acidity

In contrast to pH, titratable acidity exhibited a measurable dependence on sugar level (p < 0.05). Hydrolyzed whey formulations (G1–G4) had higher acidity values (0.53–0.54% C.A.) compared to the unhydrolyzed control (0.49–0.50%). This likely reflects a dilution effect: as sucrose increases, the concentration of acid per gram of product decreases, reducing titratable acidity without changing pH. This phenomenon is common in dairy acidified systems, where titratable acidity more accurately represents buffering constituents than pH alone37,36.

Furthermore, lactose hydrolysis itself may subtly influence acid–base dynamics. Hydrolysis generates glucose and galactose, which increase osmotic pressure and may indirectly reduce the concentration of acidified aqueous phase per unit mass. However, because the pH was standardized before freezing, measurable differences in titratable acidity remained small and primarily attributable to sugar dilution36,37. The stabilizer level had no significant effect on acidity, consistent with the non-ionic and chemically inert nature of CMC.

Melting resistance

Melting resistance exhibited the most pronounced variations, demonstrating clear dependence on both sugar concentration and CMC stabilizer level. This parameter serves as a critical indicator of cryo-structural integrity, reflecting the ability of the frozen candy matrix to withstand thermal stress and maintain structural cohesion during thawing31,38. A significant inverse relationship was observed between sugar concentration and melting resistance; candies formulated with 18% sugar exhibited the highest melting resistance (81.22%), indicating the presence of well-formed, stable ice networks, whereas increasing the sugar concentration progressively reduced melting resistance, reaching a minimum of 67.14% in the 34% sugar control. This trend is rooted in the colligative effects of sugars, where higher sucrose concentrations depress the freezing point, decrease the proportion of frozen water at storage temperatures, and increase the unfrozen water fraction, thereby enhancing molecular mobility and accelerating melt-down. Moreover, higher sugar levels promote the formation of smaller but more numerous ice crystals, which melt more rapidly than the larger, more stable crystals associated with lower sugar concentrations. These well-documented phenomena in frozen dessert science highlight that sucrose functions not only as a sweetener but also as a major modulator of the frozen matrix microstructure31,38.

In contrast, increasing CMC concentration markedly enhanced melting resistance, rising from 68.67% at 0.2% CMC to 83.09% at 0.4% CMC, primarily due to its hydrocolloid functionalities. CMC binds water through hydrogen bonding, reduces the mobility of free water, increases mix viscosity, inhibits ice recrystallization by limiting molecular diffusion, and forms a cohesive gel-like network that reinforces the frozen structure and delays structural collapse during heat exposure. These stabilizing actions align with reports on other hydrocolloids such as guar gum and xanthan, which similarly enhance freeze–thaw stability in frozen dairy systems by improving microstructural characteristics31,38.

The absence of a significant sugar × stabilizer interaction further indicates that sugar and CMC works independent physicochemical pathways, colligative effects governing the behavior of sugar, and rheological and structural reinforcement governing the role of CMC, allowing formulation adjustments to be made independently for sweetness, solids content, and structural integrity without mutual interference.

Sensory evaluation of lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy

The sensory characteristics of lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy were evaluated to understand how sugar concentration and CMC stabilizer level influence consumer perception. The combined effects on flavour, colour and appearance, body and texture, melting quality, and overall acceptability are summarized in Table 4. Overall, sugar concentration significantly influenced all sensory attributes except colour and appearance, whereas CMC concentration strongly affected body and texture, melting quality, and overall acceptability. No sugar × stabilizer (G × S) interaction was statistically significant, suggesting that the two ingredients modulate different sensory dimensions independently.

Flavour

Flavour scores increased significantly (p < 0.05) with increasing sugar concentration up to 22% (G3). The highest flavour score (8.56) was obtained for G3 (22% sugar), which was statistically comparable to G4 (24%; 8.30) and the control sample (G5; 8.36). In contrast, lower sugar levels (G1 and G2) received significantly lower scores (7.28 and 7.83, respectively), indicating insufficient sweetness and weaker flavour impact. These results align with sweetness perception mechanisms, where adequate sucrose concentration is necessary to balance acidity and mask the native whey flavour profile, which may otherwise impart acidic or slightly salty notes. Interestingly, flavour scores for lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy at 22–24% sugar were equivalent to the control product made with 34% sugar, demonstrating the sweetness-enhancing effect of lactose hydrolysis. Glucose and galactose possess 0.74 and 0.32 relative sweetness values compared to sucrose, respectively, increasing perceived sweetness without added sugar and contributing to fuller flavour perception. Stabilizer level had no significant effect on flavour, as CMC is organoleptically neutral and does not alter sweetness or aromatic compounds.

Colour and appearance

Colour and appearance scores remained statistically similar across all sugar and stabilizer levels (p > 0.05), with values ranging from 8.25 to 8.56 for lactose-hydrolyzed treatments. The highest score was again observed for the 22% sugar sample (G3; 8.56), although differences were non-significant. The absence of variation indicates that neither sucrose level nor CMC concentration affects the visual attributes of the candy, likely because colour in frozen confections is primarily governed by added flavour emulsions rather than compositional changes in the base matrix. Additionally, CMC does not alter pigment distribution or opacity at low concentrations.

Body and texture

Body and texture scores were significantly affected by both sugar and CMC levels (p < 0.05). The highest texture score was obtained for G3 (22% sugar; 8.81), significantly higher than low-sugar treatments (G1 and G2) and also superior to the control sample (G5; 7.72). Higher sugar levels enhance structural body by increasing total solids and contributing to firmness. The role of CMC was even more prominent: increasing stabilizer levels from 0.2% to 0.4% resulted in progressive improvement in body and texture scores (from 7.53 to 8.43). This outcome reflects CMC’s functional role as a hydrocolloid that increases viscosity, improves mouthfeel by imparting smoothness, reduces iciness, and reinforces the structural matrix through water immobilization. Samples with 0.4% CMC received significantly higher scores than those with 0.2%, indicative of improved freeze–thaw stability and textural refinement. This behavior aligns with hydrocolloid science literature, where CMC is recognized for its ability to create cohesive, elastic structures in frozen desserts20,22.

Melting quality

Melting quality scores followed a trend consistent with the objective melting resistance results, with both sugar and stabilizer levels contributing significantly (p < 0.05). The highest melting quality score was obtained for G3 (8.53), reflecting optimal balance between sugar-induced freezing point depression and CMC-induced structural stabilization. Increased sugar levels generally reduced melting quality because higher solute concentrations increase the unfrozen water fraction and promote faster melt-down under heat exposure. Conversely, increasing the CMC level significantly improved melting quality, with scores rising from 7.40 at 0.2% stabilizer to 8.35 at 0.4% stabilizer. This enhancement is attributable to CMC’s water-binding and viscosity-increasing properties, which reduce drip loss and delay structural collapse during melting. These observations corroborate the well-established role of hydrocolloids in improving meltdown behavior in frozen confection systems20,22,38.

Overall acceptability

Overall acceptability scores reflected the combined influence of flavour, texture, appearance, and melting quality. The highest overall acceptability was observed for the candy containing 22% sugar and 0.4% CMC (G3-S3), which received an excellent score of 9.00. Samples with 24% sugar (G4) also received high ratings (up to 8.92), though slightly lower than G3 due to slightly higher sweetness perception. The control sample (34% sugar) scored well (8.00) but was statistically inferior to the optimized lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy. This result reinforces that effective lactose hydrolysis compensated for a 12% reduction in sucrose without compromising sensory quality. The strong preference for formulations containing 0.4% CMC further highlights the stabilizer’s important role in optimizing mouthfeel, texture, and melting behavior, which collectively influence overall liking.

Characterization of the optimized lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy

The optimized lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy formulation, containing 22% sugar and 0.4% CMC, displayed compositional, physico-chemical, and sensory attributes that collectively demonstrate its technological suitability and high consumer acceptability. The chemical composition of the candy (Table 5) reflects the intrinsic nutrient profile of whey, which is dominated by carbohydrates and low in fat and protein. The total solids content of 30.75% provides an ideal solids-to-water ratio for frozen confections, ensuring adequate structural firmness without imparting excessive hardness. The relatively low fat content (0.16%) is expected for whey-based formulations and contributes to the product’s light mouthfeel. Protein (0.74%) and ash (0.45%) values correspond to the mineral and whey protein fractions naturally present in sweet whey and further indicate that lactose hydrolysis did not alter the basic nutrient distribution. Carbohydrates account for the largest compositional fraction (29.41%), largely reflecting monosaccharides generated by enzymatic lactose hydrolysis, which impart enhanced sweetness and improve functional properties such as solubility and freezing characteristics.

The physico-chemical profile of the optimized product shows that the candy maintained a stable pH of 3.91, consistent with the citric acid adjustment applied during formulation. This acidic pH supports microbial safety, enhances flavour brightness, and stabilizes whey proteins in the frozen matrix. Titratable acidity (0.53% C.A.) remained within the desirable range for fruit-flavoured frozen confections, contributing to balanced tartness without overpowering sweetness. The melting resistance value (82.62%) reflects a robust cryo-structure with limited drip loss, indicative of successful stabilization by CMC. Hydrocolloids such as CMC enhance water binding, increase viscosity, and reduce the rate of ice recrystallization during freezing and storage, leading to a stronger, more coherent matrix that resists collapse under thermal stress. The melting resistance obtained in this study aligns with high-quality commercial frozen pops, demonstrating the functional efficacy of lactose hydrolysis and hydrocolloid incorporation20,22,38.

The sensory attributes further validate the superiority of this optimized formulation. High flavour scores (8.50) can be attributed to the increased sweetness derived from glucose and galactose, which have higher sweetness intensities than lactose, enabling ~ 35% relative reduction in added sugar compared to the conventional unhydrolyzed formulation while maintaining consumer-perceived sweetness. The high colour and appearance score (8.67) reflects uniform distribution of flavour emulsion and stable opacity influenced by whey solids. Body and texture (9.00) and melting quality scores (9.00) indicate that the structural improvements resulting from CMC addition translated directly into favourable sensory texture, smoother mouthfeel, and controlled melt behaviour. Together, these attributes contributed to an overall acceptability score of 9.00, demonstrating that the optimized lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy is not only technologically superior but also highly preferred sensorially.

The findings demonstrate that lactose hydrolysis combined with optimized sugar (22%) and CMC (0.4%) concentrations produces a whey-based frozen confection that meets key quality benchmarks for both composition and sensory appeal.

Sensory suitability of different flavours in lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy

The suitability of four commercially relevant flavours, orange, pineapple, kalakhatta, and jaljira, was evaluated in the optimized lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy formulation. Since all candies were formulated with identical compositional and physico-chemical characteristics (22% sugar, 0.4% CMC), differences in sensory perception can be attributed exclusively to the added flavour emulsions and their interactions with the whey-based matrix. The sensory scores for flavour, colour and appearance, body and texture, melting quality, and overall acceptability are summarized in Table 6.

Flavour score

The flavour attribute showed significant variation among treatments (p < 0.05), with orange (8.60) and jaljira (8.30) achieving the highest ratings, indicating superior flavour integration and consumer appeal. These two flavours share strong aromatic profiles with high volatile acidity, citrus notes, and characteristic top-notes that interact favourably with the slightly acidic whey matrix (pH 3.9). Citrus-based flavours such as orange are well-documented for their compatibility with acidified dairy systems, where citral, limonene, and other terpenes remain stable and provide high aroma impact even at low pH. Jaljira, a savoury-spiced beverage flavour containing cumin, black salt, and citrus components, also harmonizes with acidified whey, benefiting from enhanced volatile release in low-pH environments. In contrast, pineapple (7.90) and kalakhatta (7.10) scored significantly lower. Pineapple flavour contains esters such as ethyl butyrate, which can exhibit diminished stability or muted perception in low-pH dairy matrices. Kalakhatta, a mix of jamun, black salt, and spice notes, is traditionally consumed in beverages but may present metallic or salty undertones when incorporated into a whey-based frozen matrix, likely contributing to its reduced liking.

Colour and appearance

A significant influence of flavour type on colour and appearance was observed (p < 0.05), driven mainly by the inherent colour intensity of the flavour emulsions. Orange flavour received the highest colour score (8.60), supported by its vibrant hue and strong visual association with fruit-based frozen treats. Jaljira also achieved a high appearance score (8.10), likely due to its familiar light green/yellow tonal characteristics. Pineapple and kalakhatta scored moderately (7.90 and 7.40, respectively); kalakhatta received the lowest score, likely due to the darker purple colour being less preferred in frozen confection contexts. Visual preference is influenced by consumer expectations, where bright, saturated fruit colours typically signal freshness and sweetness, aligning with documented colour–flavour congruency theories in sensory science.

Body and texture

As expected, body and texture scores did not differ significantly across flavours (p > 0.05), with scores ranging narrowly around 8.80–8.83 for all variants. Because the base formulation, stabilizer content, solids level, and freezing process were identical, the textural attributes remained unaffected by flavour type. This uniformity highlights the functional consistency provided by the combination of lactose-hydrolyzed whey, optimized sugar concentration, and CMC stabilizer. Hydrocolloids such as CMC impart viscosity, freeze–thaw stability, and smoothness independent of flavour addition; therefore, flavours do not influence the structural matrix of the candy.

Melting quality

Melting quality scores showed no significant differences among flavours (p > 0.05), consistent with identical composition and stabilizer levels across treatments. Scores ranged from 8.58 (pineapple) to 8.83 (orange), indicating strong melting resistance and minimal drip loss across all samples. The slight non-significant variation may reflect minor differences in flavour emulsion composition (oil phase, emulsifier stability), but the overall melting performance was dominated by the structural contribution of CMC and the solids content, reaffirming that melt characteristics are primarily governed by formulation rather than flavour type.

Overall acceptability

Overall acceptability was significantly influenced by flavour type (p < 0.05). Orange (8.70) and jaljira (8.40) emerged as the most preferred flavours, statistically superior to pineapple (7.80) and kalakhatta (7.40). The strong performance of orange and jaljira can be attributed to their balanced sensory impact, flavour–matrix compatibility, superior colour appeal, and alignment with consumer expectations in frozen treats. In contrast, the lower acceptability of pineapple and kalakhatta may be due to less optimal flavour release, lower flavour congruency with acidified whey, and comparatively muted colour appeal.

The flavour suitability study clearly demonstrates that orange flavour is the most preferred, followed closely by jaljira, reflecting high compatibility with the acidity, sweetness, and volatile profile of the lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy matrix. Both flavours achieved high marks across all sensory dimensions. Pineapple and kalakhatta, while acceptable, showed comparatively lower sensory performance. These results confirm that citrus-based and spice–citrus hybrid flavours offer superior sensory synergy in whey-based frozen confections, making orange-flavoured lactose-hydrolyzed whey candy the optimal final product for commercial development.

Conclusion

This study establishes that lactose-hydrolyzed whey is a highly suitable substrate for developing reduced-sugar frozen confections, offering a scientifically validated approach to dairy by-product valorization. The optimized formulation, containing 22% sugar and 0.4% CMC, demonstrated superior compositional quality, improved melting resistance, and excellent sensory acceptance, achieving a 12% reduction in added sucrose without compromising flavour, texture, or overall consumer acceptability. Among evaluated flavours, orange emerged as the most compatible, further supporting its suitability for commercial product development. From an industrial perspective, the findings offer a practical strategy for converting whey, traditionally considered an environmental pollutant with high BOD, into a value-added, marketable product. The process requires minimal capital modification, aligns with existing dairy infrastructure, and supports resource-efficient manufacturing consistent with circular economy principles. Future prospects for this technology include scaling the process for large-volume production, exploring alternative hydrocolloids and sweeteners for targeted nutritional profiles, integrating natural or probiotic ingredients to expand the functional beverage/dessert segment, and investigating long-term storage stability under varying distribution conditions. Additionally, techno-economic assessments and life cycle analyses would further clarify the sustainability advantages of whey conversion into frozen confections. This work provides a strong scientific and industrial foundation for the development of innovative whey-based products, contributing to waste reduction, economic enhancement, and diversification within the dairy sector.
