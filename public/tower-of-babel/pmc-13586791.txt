Cognitive Function Influences Longitudinal Motor Function Recovery after Proximal Femoral Fracture: A Longitudinal Analysis
Authors: Takehiro Kaneoka, Tomohiro Kawazoe, Kazuhiro Yamazaki, Gen Shiraishi, Takashi Sakai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:As the incidence of proximal femoral fractures (PFFs) continues to increase, optimizing patient-specific treatment strategies is essential for the efficient use of healthcare resources. The aim of this study was to examine longitudinal postoperative motor function recovery in patients with femoral neck fractures (FNF) and intertrochanteric fractures (ITFs) and to identify which factors, including fracture type and patient-related characteristics, predominantly influence recovery trajectories to inform individualized rehabilitation strategies.Methods:This retrospective study included patients who underwent bipolar hip arthroplasty for FNF (201 hips) or internal fixation for ITF (224 hips) and completed convalescent rehabilitation. Motor function was assessed using the motor Functional Independence Measure (mFIM) at admission to and discharge from the convalescent rehabilitation ward. Longitudinal mFIM score changes were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM), with time, fracture type, cognitive function, pre-fracture mobility status, age, and other covariates as fixed effects.Results:In the LMM, only cognitive function showed a significant interaction with time (P <0.001, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.38 to 0.66), whereas fracture type did not (P = 0.055, 95% CI −0.06 to 5.13). Fracture type (P <0.001, 95% CI −8.28 to −2.56) and pre-fracture mobility status (P <0.001, 95% CI −6.22 to −3.28) demonstrated only significant main effects.Conclusions:Postoperative functional recovery trajectories after PFFs were significantly influenced by cognitive function. The use of LMM facilitated detailed assessment of longitudinal recovery patterns beyond single time-point evaluations. These findings highlight the importance of cognitively tailored rehabilitation strategies in optimizing functional recovery.

INTRODUCTION

The incidence of proximal femoral fractures (PFFs) is projected to increase substantially by 2029, driven by ongoing global population aging.1) PFFs occur predominantly in older adults and are associated with increased healthcare and nursing care costs, as well as elevated mortality rates.2) These fractures are expected to become a growing contributor to the burden on medical resources in coming years. Under these circumstances, it is increasingly important to predict functional prognosis at an early stage for each patient and to implement appropriately tailored rehabilitation goals and individualized interventions. Such strategies are essential for optimizing the use of limited healthcare resources.

Several factors influence postoperative functional outcomes in patients with PFFs, including age, cognitive function, and pre-fracture activities of daily living (ADLs).3,4,5,6) Among these, perioperative cognitive function has a particularly strong impact on walking ability.7) Moreover, patient characteristics differ between femoral neck fractures (FNFs) and intertrochanteric fractures (ITFs), and these differences—together with fracture type—may influence functional outcomes.8,9,10,11,12) However, the relative contribution of these factors to longitudinal changes in postoperative motor function has not been fully elucidated, and detailed analyses using robust longitudinal approaches remain limited.

We hypothesized that longitudinal postoperative motor function recovery should be influenced by multiple factors, including both fracture type and patient-related characteristics. The objective of this study was to explore which factors most strongly influenced recovery trajectories over time, and whether these trajectories were primarily driven by fracture type or by patient-related factors such as cognitive function and pre-fracture mobility status.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective study was conducted at a regional hospital in Japan with an integrated care system encompassing acute care and convalescent rehabilitation, including 250 total beds and 50 beds dedicated to the convalescent rehabilitation ward (CRW). The study period spanned from January 2018 to December 2024, and all patients who underwent surgery for PFFs at our institution were included. To minimize the influence of differences in surgical procedures and postoperative weight-bearing protocols, only patients who underwent bipolar hip arthroplasty were included among those with FNF, whereas patients with FNF treated with internal fixation were excluded because of their small number and the requirement for restricted weight-bearing. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 195 patients (201 hips) who underwent bipolar hip arthroplasty for FNFs and 217 patients (224 hips) who underwent internal fixation for ITFs were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Patient selection flowchart.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shunan Memorial Hospital (Approval No. R07-05). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data.

Surgical Procedures and Postoperative Rehabilitation

All surgeries were performed by a dedicated orthopedic team consisting of three surgeons. For bipolar hip arthroplasty, a taper wedge stem was used via a posterolateral approach. Among the short external rotators, only the inferior gemellus and obturator externus were detached, whereas the other surrounding muscles were preserved. For internal fixation of ITFs, a short femoral nail was inserted using an intramedullary technique. In all cases included in the final analysis, full weight-bearing was permitted immediately after surgery, and no patient required postoperative weight-bearing restrictions or modifications to rehabilitation protocols because of fixation instability.

Postoperatively, patients received physical therapy in the acute care ward up to 6 days per week, with individual sessions lasting 20–40 min. During this phase, rehabilitation primarily emphasized early mobilization, toileting practice, and gait training. Once adequate wound healing was confirmed and weight-bearing and ambulation on the affected limb were deemed feasible, patients were transferred to the CRW.

In the CRW, all patients received multidisciplinary care provided by a team consisting of physicians, nurses, physical therapists, occupational therapists, and social workers. Physical therapy was administered eight times per week, with each session lasting 40–60 min, focusing on transfer training, gait and stair training, and joint range-of-motion exercises. Occupational therapy was provided six times per week for 40–60 min per session, targeting improvement of basic ADLs, instructions on bathing and bed mobility to prevent dislocation, cognitive assessment and stimulation, and guidance on the use of assistive devices and safety education. To determine the continuation and appropriate adjustment of the rehabilitation program, weekly multidisciplinary team conferences were held. Patients were discharged from the CRW when they achieved a level of functional independence sufficient for safe return to their living environment. The discharge destination was determined through multidisciplinary team discussion, considering the patients’ ADL during rehabilitation, with participation of the patients and their families.

Outcome Measures

Data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records. Motor function was assessed using the motor component of the Functional Independence Measure (mFIM).13) The FIM consists of 18 items across three domains: ADLs, mobility, and cognitive function, with each item rated on a 7-point scale. The mFIM comprises 13 items related to ADLs and mobility, with a maximum score of 91 points. Higher scores indicate greater independence. FIM assessments were performed at the time of CRW transfer and at discharge, based on multidisciplinary team conferences. The mFIM was evaluated in terms of both absolute score and improvement rate. To account for ceiling effects, the potential improvement rate was calculated as follows: (mFIM at discharge − mFIM at CRW admission)/ (91 − mFIM at CRW admission). Pre-fracture mobility status was categorized into four levels: independent ambulation, cane-assisted walking, walker-assisted walking, and wheelchair use.

The Hasegawa Dementia Scale-Revised (HDS-R) was used to evaluate cognitive function and was administered by nursing staff upon hospital admission. The HDS-R is a widely used screening tool for dementia, consisting of items that assess orientation and various aspects of memory, including qualitative differences.14) In this study, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and heart disease were considered comorbidities, because these are common chronic conditions among older adults and are likely to affect the course of rehabilitation and functional outcomes. Additional patient background characteristics included age at the time of surgery, body mass index, and the number of days from admission to surgery. Statistical Analysis

To evaluate the effects of each factor on longitudinal changes in mFIM scores, a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) was employed. Fixed effects included time, fracture type (FNF or ITF), HDS-R score, age, sex, time from admission to surgery, and pre-fracture mobility status. In addition, the length of hospital stay, including days in the acute care ward and the CRW, was collected and included in the analyses as a covariate to evaluate its influence on functional recovery. To investigate whether fracture type influenced the course of functional recovery, the interaction between time and fracture type was considered the primary effect of interest. In addition, to account for patient-related factors potentially affecting recovery trajectories, interaction terms between time and HDS-R score and between time and pre-fracture mobility status were included in the model. To account for repeated measurements within individuals, a random intercept for each patient was included, and parameter estimation was performed using restricted maximum likelihood. The significance of fixed effects was assessed using type III tests.

Given the strong mathematical dependency between the mFIM improvement rate and preoperative mFIM score, the improvement rate was not included in the LMM to avoid multicollinearity. Instead, a separate multiple regression analysis (MRA) was performed with the mFIM improvement rate as the dependent variable, serving as a sensitivity analysis. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables are presented as number (percentage).

mFIM and HDS-R scores were treated as continuous variables in the analyses. Summed ordinal scales comprising multiple items, such as the FIM and cognitive assessment scales, are commonly analyzed as continuous variables in rehabilitation research and clinical statistical analyses.15,16,17) All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), with a significance level set at P <0.05.

Power Analysis

An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 (Düsseldorf, Germany), based on a repeated-measures analysis of variance design (within–between interaction), with two groups (FNF and ITF), two time points, an assumed effect size f of 0.15, α = 0.05, power = 0.95, and an assumed correlation of 0.5 among repeated measures. The estimated total sample size required was 196, and the final sample size in this study exceeded this requirement.

RESULTS

Univariate Analysis

In the univariate analysis of patient characteristics, the ITF group included significantly older patients (P <0.001), a higher proportion of women (P = 0.006), and a greater prevalence of comorbidities (P = 0.008) compared to the FNF group. In addition, pre-fracture mobility was significantly lower in the ITF group (P = 0.007), and the length of stay in the CRW was significantly longer in the ITF group (P <0.001). Regarding functional outcomes, the FNF group demonstrated significantly higher mFIM scores both at the time of CRW transfer (P <0.001) and at discharge (P =0.008), as well as a significantly greater improvement rate (P = 0.018). However, no significant between-group difference was observed in the absolute gain in mFIM score (P = 0.188; Table 1).

At discharge, the mFIM score was significantly higher in the FNF group than in the ITF group (65.0 ± 24.0 vs. 58.8 ± 22.8, P = 0.008); however, absolute gain in mFIM score did not differ significantly between groups (18.7 ± 13.6 vs. 20.5 ± 14.9, P = 0.188). The mFIM improvement rate was significantly higher in the FNF group than in the ITF group (50.7 ± 30.2% vs. 43.9 ± 29.0%, P = 0.018).

Linear Mixed-effects Model Analysis

The LMM revealed a significant change in mFIM scores over time (B = 12.10, P <0.001). Among the interaction terms with time, the HDS-R score showed a significant interaction, indicating that cognitive function influenced the trajectory of recovery (B = 0.52, P <0.001). In contrast, fracture type (B = 2.54, P = 0.055) and pre-fracture mobility status (B = −0.30, P = 0.672) did not exhibit significant interactions with time; however, both parameters had significant main effects (B = 5.09, P <0.001 and B = −4.66, P <0.001, respectively). The length of hospital stay was significantly associated with mFIM recovery. Days of acute care ward stay showed a positive association with mFIM scores (B = 0.30, P <0.001), whereas days of CRW stay showed a negative association (B = −0.07, P = 0.030; Table 2).

Multiple Regression Analysis

In the MRA with mFIM improvement rate as the dependent variable, HDS-R score (B = 1.97, P <0.001) and pre-fracture mobility status (B = −6.46, P <0.001) were identified as significant explanatory variables. In contrast, fracture type (B = 4.07, P = 0.068) and length of hospital stay, including days of acute care ward stay (B = −0.18, P = 0.235) and CRW stay (B = 0.07, P = 0.198), were not significantly associated with the mFIM improvement rate (Table 3).

Post-hoc Analysis

A post-hoc power analysis was performed based on a repeated-measures analysis of variance design (within–between interaction). Given the total sample size of 425, an effect size f of 0.09, α = 0.05, and two time points, the achieved power was calculated to be 0.959.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated longitudinal postoperative functional recovery after PFFs and the factors influencing recovery trajectories. In the LMM, only cognitive function demonstrated a significant interaction with time in relation to mFIM recovery, whereas fracture type did not show a significant interaction. Furthermore, in the MRA with mFIM improvement rate as the dependent variable, cognitive function and pre-fracture mobility status were identified as significant predictors. These findings suggest that patient-related factors, rather than fracture type, play a more important role in the recovery trajectory of mFIM scores after hip fracture surgery.

In the LMM analysis, only cognitive function showed a significant interaction with time, indicating that cognition was a key determinant of the trajectory of postoperative functional recovery in patients with PFFs. The present findings are consistent with those presented in previous systematic reviews identifying multiple factors associated with functional recovery after hip fracture, which have reported that age, comorbidities, cognitive status, and pre-fracture functional capacity are key determinants of recovery outcomes.18,19) Furthermore, previous longitudinal studies using mixed-effects models have demonstrated that cognitive function significantly influences physical function and activity levels after hip fracture.20) In particular, cognitive function assessed in the early postoperative period has been shown to predict subsequent recovery over time, in line with the findings of the present study. In contrast, fracture type, pre-fracture mobility status, and age at the time of surgery showed significant main effects but no significant interactions with time. This suggests that although older age, lower pre-injury mobility, or sustaining an ITF may be associated with a lower final functional level, these factors do not appear to influence the longitudinal pattern of recovery itself. This tendency was further supported by the univariate analysis: patients in the FNF group had higher mFIM scores, were younger, and had better pre-fracture mobility; however, there was no significant between-group difference in the absolute gain in mFIM score. In addition, in the LMM analysis, the length of hospital stay was associated with mFIM scores. A longer stay in the acute care ward was associated with higher mFIM scores, whereas a longer stay in the CRW was associated with lower mFIM scores. These findings may reflect differences in patient condition, suggesting that patients requiring prolonged rehabilitation in the CRW may have more severe functional impairment. However, hospital stay was not significantly associated with the mFIM improvement rate. Importantly, adjustment for hospital stay did not alter the main findings of this study, and cognitive function remained the only factor significantly associated with recovery trajectories over time.

Cognitive function and pre-fracture mobility status were identified as significant factors associated with the improvement rate of mFIM score following surgery. This finding was derived from an MRA conducted to explore the impact of background variables in more detail, prompted by the observed significant between-group difference in mFIM improvement rates in the univariate analysis. These results suggest that patients with preserved cognitive function may benefit more from rehabilitation owing to better comprehension of instructions and enhanced learning ability, leading to more efficient functional gains. Additionally, patients with lower pre-fracture mobility status may have limited physiological reserve, making it more difficult to achieve substantial recovery, thereby influencing the extent of functional improvement.

In this study, an LMM was employed to evaluate longitudinal changes in mFIM scores. Unlike conventional approaches that compare functional status at a single time point, the LMM enables assessment of within-subject changes over time, providing a more dynamic understanding of recovery. Previous studies have relied on simple comparisons of FIM scores at discharge or at fixed follow-up intervals, which may not adequately account for individual variability in baseline function or recovery rate. In contrast, the LMM used in this study adjusts for individual differences in baseline scores as random effects while modeling time-related changes as fixed effects. This approach allowed us to clearly distinguish between initial differences in functional status and longitudinal recovery patterns associated with fracture type. Although initial mFIM scores were significantly different between fracture types, no significant difference was observed in the trajectory of recovery over time, suggesting that the time-dependent course of functional improvement may not be determined by fracture type.

Crucially, the results of this study suggest that postoperative recovery patterns after hip fracture are influenced more by patient-related factors than by fracture type itself. Notably, cognitive function was the only factor that showed a significant interaction with time, suggesting that it plays a central role in shaping the longitudinal pattern of functional recovery. Arcolin et al.10) reported that fracture type influenced the FIM improvement rate in relatively younger patients with higher baseline ADL function. Other studies have also described associations between fracture type and clinical outcomes.10,11,12) In contrast, our study not only performed group comparisons through univariate analysis but also applied an LMM to identify factors affecting the time-dependent trajectory of recovery. In addition, we performed MRA to examine determinants of mFIM improvement rate. The cohort in this study also showed a tendency for patients with ITF to be older than those with FNF—a finding consistent with those of previous studies8,9)—supporting the external validity of our sample. Furthermore, the observed association between HDS-R score and postoperative recovery aligns with prior literature suggesting that cognitive function is a key predictor of rehabilitation outcomes in older adults.7,17)

The findings of this study suggest that patient-related factors such as cognitive function and pre-fracture mobility status have a greater influence on postoperative functional recovery than fracture type itself. Although there were differences in final functional levels between the FNF and ITF groups, no significant difference was observed in their longitudinal recovery trajectories. This indicates that a uniform rehabilitation approach based solely on fracture type may not be appropriate and instead supports the need for individualized rehabilitation strategies tailored to cognitive and physical baseline function. Importantly, the use of an LMM allowed us to visualize and differentiate longitudinal recovery processes that may otherwise be overlooked when relying solely on discharge scores or improvement rates, highlighting a clinically valuable methodological perspective. Based on these findings, postoperative rehabilitation goals should be set according to cognitive function rather than surgical procedure or fracture classification. Developing rehabilitation strategies that reflect individual cognitive profiles may be essential for improving outcomes in older adults with PFFs.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective analysis conducted at a single institution, which may limit generalizability. However, a major strength is that all phases of care—from surgery through discharge—were completed within a single institution, allowing for standardized perioperative management, rehabilitation protocols, and outcome assessments. Future studies incorporating multicenter data are warranted to enhance external validity. Second, the study population was limited to patients who completed convalescent rehabilitation. In addition, patients with FNFs treated with internal fixation were excluded because of their small number and the difficulty in standardizing postoperative rehabilitation protocols, particularly because of weight-bearing restrictions. These exclusion criteria may have introduced selection bias by narrowing the study population. Nevertheless, the primary aim of this study was to compare postoperative recovery trajectories and clarify the influence of fracture type and patient-related factors on functional recovery. By restricting the analysis to patients who underwent consistent rehabilitation protocols, we minimized the confounding effects of variability in rehabilitation interventions. As a result, the present findings reflect recovery patterns in older adults receiving standardized postoperative rehabilitation in routine clinical practice and provide clinically relevant insights into functional recovery under specific care conditions. Third, delirium in the early postoperative period may have influenced cognitive assessments such as the HDS-R score and potentially affected rehabilitation outcomes; however, because of the retrospective design of this study, the presence of delirium was not consistently documented in the medical records and could not be reliably evaluated. Therefore, its potential impact could not be fully assessed. Because HDS-R was assessed only once at admission, transient cognitive impairment related to delirium may have resulted in lower cognitive scores than the patients’ true baseline cognitive function, potentially leading to an overestimation of the association between cognitive function and postoperative recovery trajectories. However, because delirium may occur across a broad spectrum of cognitive function in older adults with PFFs, the overall impact on the primary findings remains uncertain. Future prospective studies should incorporate validated delirium screening tools, such as the Confusion Assessment Method,21) together with serial cognitive assessments to better distinguish delirium-related transient impairment from baseline cognitive decline. Fourth, although the mFIM and HDS-R are ordinal scales, they were treated as continuous variables in the present study. Both measures are derived from the sum of multiple items and have been commonly used as quantitative indices in previous studies.15,16,17) Nevertheless, the ordinal nature of these scales may limit strict interpretation as interval-level data. Finally, this study included only Asian (Japanese) patients; therefore, caution is required when generalizing the results to other racial or ethnic populations.

CONCLUSION

The findings of this study suggest that cognitive function is a key factor influencing longitudinal changes in motor function among patients with PFFs. These results highlight the importance of assessing patient-related characteristics for determining recovery trajectories.
