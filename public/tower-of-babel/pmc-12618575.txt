Geotechnical evaluation of aeolian sand and gravel soil as subgrade backfill materials in desert lake regions
Authors: Jiangyang Hu, Jun Mao, Wei Huang, Bingxiang Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the constraints of transportation conditions and cost, using traditional gravel soil with favorable properties for backfilling is often impractical in remote desert highway construction. Consequently, aeolian sand, which is abundant in these regions, provides a promising alternative for roadbed backfill. This study investigates the feasibility of substituting gravel soil with aeolian sand in typical desert highway construction. Comprehensive laboratory experiments were conducted to evaluate the physical and mechanical properties of both materials. The results indicate that although aeolian sand exhibits high shear strength and permeability, it poses compaction challenges due to poor particle gradation. The engineering parameters applied in the finite element numerical simulation model were systematically verified through field tests and compared with experimental results. It is used to simulate the behavior of aeolian sand, focusing on predicting foundation settlement and embankment slope stability under different compaction conditions. The analysis identifies the key factors influencing roadbed settlement and slope stability under applied loads. Based on these findings, the study offers theoretical guidance for optimizing the application of aeolian sand in desert road construction, aiming to reduce costs and logistical difficulties while ensuring a stable and durable roadbed.Keywords: Desert region, Mechanical properties, Numerical models, Roadbed settlement and slope stability

Introduction

Western China is rich in aeolian sand and gravel soil, both of which are considered potential materials for roadbed fillers in regional highway construction. Aeolian sand, however, consists of fine, uniformly sized, and poorly graded particles, which limit its effectiveness as a natural roadbed material without supplementary technical measures. In contrast, gravel soil, characterized by its high strength and stability, is widely regarded as an excellent natural backfill and is commonly used in roadbed construction across Western China.

Currently, ground improvement techniques for aeolian sand and gravel subgrades in western China are mainly focused on shallow reinforcement above the water table. Several studies have confirmed the feasibility of using aeolian sand as subgrade fill. Dong1 explored its potential as embankment fill in desert environments. Huang et al.2 applied modified aeolian sand as a waterproof layer in collapsible loess regions, which significantly enhanced subgrade stability. Li et al.3 introduced a “wet compaction” technique that improved the performance of aeolian sand during backfilling. He et al.4 demonstrated its suitability in cement-stabilized gravel bases. However, actual construction projects often traverse desert interiors and shallow lake zones. These areas typically involve soft foundations below the water level that require deeper treatment. Although Existing research has provided valuable insights into the physical and mechanical properties of aeolian sand, as well as its engineering behavior and stability as a non-cohesive soil. Nevertheless, its application in deep-desert subgrade construction remains insufficiently explored. In particular, challenges persist in route optimization and subgrade treatment methods. To address these challenges, researchers have begun modifying aeolian sand to enhance its engineering properties. For instance, in the United Arab Emirates, sand barriers and vegetation have been implemented along the Sheikh Zayed desert highway to control sand movement5. In Saudi Arabia and other Middle Eastern countries, aeolian sand has been successfully used in desert railway projects6. At the same time, structural reinforcement techniques have received increasing attention. Geocells7 and mesh confinements8 have shown promising results in improving the stability of aeolian sand subgrades in arid regions. To further enhance mechanical performance, various reinforcing materials have been introduced. Fiber-reinforced aeolian sand has been widely studied and found to significantly improve both strength and deformation resistance9–12. Pozzolanic binders such as fly ash and cement have also been commonly used to increase durability and load-bearing capacity13–15. Despite these advancements, the long-term performance and deep foundation reinforcement of aeolian sand in shallow desert lake regions remain insufficiently investigated. Further research is needed to address these gaps and develop more effective engineering solutions.

Gravel soil, due to its high strength and stability, is a preferred material for roadbed construction. Extensive studies have examined its physical and mechanical properties, with a focus on compaction characteristics1617, gradation impact18,19, pore structure, permeability coefficient20–22, and shear strength23. In their research on gravel soil foundations for desert roadbeds, He et al.24 identified an optimal single-point ramming frequency of approximately nine hits for effective compaction. Huang25 developed an evaluation method based on in-situ test indicators to accurately assess gravel soil liquefaction potential, and Shan et al.26 showed that dynamic compaction effectively mitigates uneven settlement from groundwater liquefaction and structural damage. Additionally, Wei27 found that gravel soil serves as a viable anti-seepage material in engineering applications. Gravel soil is increasingly applied not only in roadbeds but also in railway foundations, where higher speeds demand precise track flatness. Luo28 studied deformation curves in geogrid-reinforced gravel samples under varying pressures, underscoring gravel soil in railway construction.

This study evaluates the suitability of aeolian sand and gravel soil as subgrade backfill materials in desert shallow lake areas. Beyond conventional physical property testing, it examines shear strength, compaction behavior, and permeability under varying moisture and compaction conditions. Numerical simulations are also employed to predict foundation settlement and slope stability. By integrating laboratory data with modeling, the study develops a more comprehensive understanding of how these materials perform under field-like conditions. The findings are expected to serve three key purposes. First, they identify the mechanical limits and advantages of aeolian sand when used independently as a roadbed material. Second, they provide comparative performance data against gravel soil, which serves as a benchmark for high-performance backfill. Third, they offer practical guidance on managing compaction and moisture conditions in desert lake environments, where water scarcity and ground saturation present dual challenges. By addressing both dry and wet conditions, this research fills a critical knowledge gap and delivers technical recommendations for engineers tasked with designing stable, cost-effective, and sustainable roadbeds in remote desert areas with complex terrain. Ultimately, the study contributes to improving infrastructure resilience in arid regions through the use of locally available materials.

Research on engineering characteristics of backfill in the desert lake area

To comprehensively assess the engineering attributes of aeolian sand and gravel soil as subgrade backfill materials, a series of foundational laboratory tests was initially performed. These tests included evaluations of natural moisture content, compaction characteristics, and permeability, providing essential insights into the fundamental engineering behavior of the backfill. Subsequently, direct shear tests were carried out under varying moisture content conditions, enabling the determination of the shear strength index of the filler across different levels of moisture. The results of these laboratory tests formed the basis for subsequent analysis. Numerical methods were then applied to examine the relationship between the engineering properties of aeolian sand and gravel soil backfill and their effects on subgrade performance. The analysis focused on how these properties influence bearing capacity and settlement behavior. The primary objective of this study was to identify the key control parameters governing the bearing capacity and settlement of subgrades constructed with these materials.

It can be seen from Table 1 and Fig. 1, aeolian sand is predominantly composed of particles in the 0.25–0.075 mm range, which account for over 85% of the total. This narrow distribution reflects poor gradation, resulting in insufficient particle size diversity for effective interlocking and void filling. Compacting aeolian sand is problematic because fine particles cannot effectively bridge the void spaces. This results in low dry density and makes it difficult to achieve high degrees of compaction. The compaction curve of aeolian sand often shows a bimodal shape with both dry and wet compaction zones. However, excessive uniformity reduces overall compaction effectiveness, especially under field conditions with limited moisture control. In contrast, gravel soil demonstrates a well-graded particle composition, with distribution across all gradation ranges and a high coefficient of uniformity (Cu > 5). This diversity in particle sizes facilitates dense packing, as smaller particles occupy the voids between larger ones, improving both mechanical interlocking and overall compactness. Improved gradation allows the material to attain higher maximum dry density with less effort, ensuring a more stable and durable fill. Moreover, the higher internal friction angle of gravel soil increases its resistance to shear deformation, contributing to superior load-bearing capacity and slope stability. Therefore, the favorable gradation and packing characteristics of gravel soil make it an excellent backfill material for subgrade applications, particularly in areas requiring high compaction and mechanical stability.

To investigate the permeability characteristics of aeolian sand and gravel soil, permeability tests were conducted on selected backfill samples to determine their respective coefficients of permeability. Concurrently, to evaluate the permeability of the underlying soil strata, samples were collected from the construction site and subjected to similar tests. According to the geological borehole map, the subgrade reinforcement depth mainly consists of a silt layer, with certain sections containing higher silt content. For the permeability test, representative silt samples from the subsurface stratum were selected. Considering the focal point of this study revolves around the design of backfill embankments, it becomes imperative to adhere to specific compaction thresholds. In particular, the roadbed of the subgrade requires a minimum compaction degree of 97%, while the upper and lower embankments should not be less than 95% and 93%, respectively. Based on these requirements, three experimental groups were established using aeolian sand, gravel soil, and silt. Permeability tests were then performed on each material at compaction degrees of 93%, 95%, and 97%.

As shown in Table 3, the permeability coefficient of gravel soil is approximately 2.0 × 10⁻2 cm/s, whereas that of aeolian sand is about 3.0 × 10⁻3 cm/s. In comparison, the underlying silt layer exhibits a much lower permeability coefficient of roughly 8.5 × 10⁻4 cm/s. Standard backfill materials, such as silty or clayey soils, typically exhibit low permeability and poor drainage capacity. In contrast, aeolian sand and gravel soil possess superior hydraulic properties that facilitate the rapid dissipation of excess pore water pressure. This advantage is particularly significant in desert lake regions such as the Taitema Lake area, where shallow groundwater and fine-grained, low-permeability soils are prevalent. Under these conditions, the risk of pore water accumulation during construction increases, potentially compromising subgrade stability if not adequately drained. Gravel soil, with its coarse particles and well-graded structure, not only provides excellent compaction and shear strength but also exhibits high permeability, making it an ideal backfill material in challenging environments. Although aeolian sand is finer and more uniform in particle size, it still exhibits relatively high permeability compared with conventional cohesive soils. In addition, its abundance and accessibility in desert regions provide logistical advantages, helping to reduce transportation costs. Moreover, the permeability of all three soils decreases with increasing compaction, as denser soil structures restrict water flow paths. This characteristic is advantageous in roadbed construction, where controlled permeability is required to balance drainage capacity with structural stability.

The test results indicate that the optimal moisture content of the aeolian sand sample is approximately 16%, while the maximum dry density is 1.632 g/cm3. The compaction characteristics of aeolian sand are notably different from those of conventional soils. The test curve of aeolian sand compaction is shown in Fig. 2.

The test curve of different sand compaction.

The compaction test curve of aeolian sand clearly exhibits two distinct peaks. The first peak appears at a water content of approximately 4%, corresponding to a dry density of 1.538 g/cm3. The second, more prominent peak occurs at the optimal water content of about 16%, where the maximum dry density of 1.632 g/cm3 is achieved. This bimodal compaction behavior reflects the unique mechanical response of aeolian sand, which exhibits two distinct compaction modes: dry compaction at low moisture levels and wet compaction at optimal moisture content. The initial peak results from the efficient rearrangement of dry particles under mechanical energy when interparticle lubrication is minimal, leading to a relatively dense packing state. However, at low moisture levels, weak particle cohesion combined with frictional resistance hinders further rearrangement. As the water content increases, a transition zone is reached in which moisture reduces internal friction and facilitates particle movement, allowing more effective reorientation and packing. This process produces the second peak, corresponding to wet compaction. At this stage, the sand reaches its highest achievable density. The dry compaction peak, which reaches approximately 94% of the maximum dry density, indicates that aeolian sand can still achieve a relatively high degree of compaction even under low-moisture conditions. This characteristic is particularly advantageous in arid regions, such as desert lake areas, where water availability is limited. Thus, adopting a dry compaction strategy not only conserves water but also ensures acceptable engineering performance. Moreover, within the intermediate moisture content range of 4–12%, the variation in dry density remains relatively moderate, suggesting that aeolian sand exhibits lower compaction sensitivity to moisture changes in this interval. This stability enhances its practical applicability, providing a degree of construction tolerance under variable site conditions. In summary, the two-peak phenomenon reflects aeolian sand’s transition from friction-dominated dry compaction to lubrication-assisted wet compaction. Recognizing and utilizing this behavior is essential for optimizing field compaction strategies, particularly in water-scarce environments. Research on the effects of tamping on backfill materials

To evaluate the suitability of gravel soil and aeolian sand as backfill materials in desert lake areas, compaction and permeability tests were conducted on soil layer samples. These tests aimed to investigate compaction characteristics and the capacity for excess pore water pressure dissipation under tamping loads. Two compaction energy levels, 1500 kN m and 2500 kN m, were applied, and their reinforcement effects were compared. Based on engineering practice, the typical spacing between dynamic compaction points is 1.5 to 2.5 times the hammer diameter, corresponding to 3.0–5.0 m. In this experiment, spacings of 3.5 m and 4.5 m were adopted.

The relationship curves between the single-point settlement, cumulative settlement, and the number of blows is shown in Fig. 5. As the number of blows increases, cumulative settlement gradually increases, while single-point settlement progressively decreases. After a certain number of blows, the cumulative settlement curve begins to flatten. The observations show that during single-point compaction, settlement approaches zero after approximately nine blows, which was taken as the criterion for terminating the experiment. Compaction energies of 1500 kN m and 2500 kN m were selected to represent typical and enhanced field compaction levels, respectively. Both energy levels achieved optimal densification at around nine blows, providing a practical balance between experimental efficiency and engineering relevance for desert roadbed applications.

Relationship between tamping amount and tamping number.

Multiple pore water pressure gauges were installed around the compaction points to analyze the impact of tamping on excess pore water pressure. As shown in Fig. 6, the cumulative excess pore water pressure increases with the number of blows. Initially, excess pore water pressure increases significantly with each blow, but the incremental rise diminishes in the later stages. After approximately nine blows, the excess pore water pressure stabilizes. At both energy levels, the excess pore water pressures measured at 4.5 m and 3.5 m from the compaction point center were essentially the same, indicating that the backfill material provided effective reinforcement. Considering construction costs, a larger compaction spacing can therefore be adopted.

Research on the relationship between the engineering characteristics of backfill and foundation settlement and embankment slope stability

Numerical analysis methods were applied to systematically investigate the key control parameters influencing subgrade settlement and embankment slope stability in subgrade filling applications. Finite element software Midas and the slope stability analysis tool Geo-Slope were used to develop numerical models for aeolian sand and gravel soil as filling materials, based on empirical data from laboratory tests. These models allowed manipulation of material properties such as deformation modulus, internal friction angle, and water content. By applying specific load conditions to the calibrated models, the behavior of aeolian sand and gravel soil under different compaction characteristics was simulated. This analysis identified the primary variables governing subgrade settlement and embankment slope stability under the design load conditions.

In contrast, raising the compaction degree beyond 95% results in a reduction in slope stability. Although increasing compaction generally enhances soil strength and reduces settlement, slope stability may decline when compaction exceeds 95% due to structural changes within the soil matrix. Excessive compaction can lead to particle crushing and loss of interparticle friction, particularly in granular soils such as aeolian sand and gravel soil. This over-compaction disrupts the natural interlocking of particles, reducing the internal friction angle, which is a critical parameter for slope stability. In addition, overly dense soils may become brittle, making them more susceptible to sudden failure under shear stress. In sloped embankments, these effects can collectively decrease the factor of safety, leading to a paradoxical reduction in stability despite higher compaction levels. Moreover, given the relatively gentle slope ratio of 1:2 for the first-level slope, the most vulnerable sliding surface develops along the second-level slope above the waterline. As a result, the slope stability coefficients remain consistent regardless of whether water is present.

Conclusions and discussion

This study began by examining the fundamental physical properties of aeolian sand and gravel soil backfill materials in a desert lake region. Compaction and direct shear tests were conducted to evaluate their basic mechanical characteristics. Subsequently, extensive numerical simulations were performed to analyze the settlement behavior and slope stability of aeolian sand and gravel soil backfills. These simulations accounted for roadway loads under both wet and dry conditions, with compaction levels set at 93%, 95%, 97%, and the specified design compaction degree. The principal findings of this study are summarized as follows:

Aeolian sand is classified as fine sand and is characterized by a low unevenness coefficient, typically less than 5, which reflects its uniform particle size distribution. However, its poor gradation makes achieving adequate compaction challenging. In contrast, gravel soil, classified as gravel sand, has a higher unevenness coefficient, indicating a broader particle size range and improved gradation. This diversity in particle sizes facilitates more effective compaction, making gravel soil a more suitable backfill material. In addition, gravel soil exhibits high permeability, which aids in dissipating excess static pore water pressure. Aeolian sand and silt also demonstrate favorable permeability, enabling efficient pore water pressure dissipation.

Both aeolian sand and gravel soil exhibit negligible cohesion in their shear strength index, whereas silt shows moderate cohesion, ranging from approximately 3 to 5 kPa. For all three materials, shear strength is governed primarily by the internal friction angle. Aeolian sand, with its relatively higher internal friction angle, demonstrates greater shear strength than gravel soil.

Under load, the subgrade settlement patterns of aeolian sand and gravel soil backfills are broadly similar, with the deformation modulus emerging as the primary factor influencing settlement. As the deformation modulus increases, settlement decreases, with the greatest concentration occurring at the center of the roadway. However, beyond a certain threshold modulus, additional increases yield only marginal reductions in settlement, indicating diminishing returns.

In typical operational scenarios, subgrade compaction and the presence or absence of external water vary. Both aeolian sand and gravel soil exhibit improved slope stability as the compaction degree increases from 93 to 97%. However, the extent of this improvement remains limited. Notably, the safety factor for aeolian sand embankments improves significantly in the absence of water on the slope exterior compared to wet conditions. Conversely, the safety and stability of gravel soil embankments remain largely unaffected by the presence or absence of water.

In summary, aeolian sand and gravel soil exhibit distinct yet complementary geotechnical characteristics. Gravel soil demonstrates superior compaction efficiency, higher permeability, and a greater internal friction angle, contributing to enhanced slope stability and rapid dissipation of pore water pressure. By contrast, aeolian sand, with its bimodal compaction behavior and moderate permeability, can achieve adequate dry density and a favorable internal friction angle, making it a viable alternative in water-scarce desert environments. Both materials display similar settlement behavior under design loading, with the deformation modulus serving as the key control factor. Overall, gravel soil is preferable when performance is prioritized, whereas aeolian sand offers logistical and economic benefits where resource availability and water conservation are critical. These findings provide practical guidance for material selection and compaction strategies in roadbed construction projects located in desert lake regions. The high permeability and excellent compaction properties of gravel soil make it particularly suitable in areas with shallow groundwater or frequent water level fluctuations, as it facilitates rapid drainage and sustains slope stability even under saturated conditions. Meanwhile, aeolian sand, due to its wide availability and favorable dry compaction performance, offers a cost-effective option in arid regions where water resources are limited. Its moderate sensitivity to moisture variations further enhances construction flexibility. Collectively, these insights support a performance-based and resource-efficient approach to road embankment design in challenging desert environments.

Despite the comprehensive experimental and numerical analysis presented, this study has several limitations that warrant further investigation. First, although compaction and slope stability under different moisture and loading conditions were evaluated, the environmental impacts of large-scale extraction and utilization of aeolian sand and gravel soil were not considered. Second, the simulated compaction process did not account for construction variability, such as uneven site compaction, equipment constraints, or weather-related disturbances; future refinements of numerical models incorporating real-time field data would therefore be valuable. Third, reinforcement strategies were not explored, including the use of geosynthetics or chemical stabilizers, which could further improve the performance and sustainability of these materials. Future research should include environmental assessments, field-scale construction validation, and reinforcement optimization to expand the applicability of these findings to infrastructure projects in the desert lakes region.
