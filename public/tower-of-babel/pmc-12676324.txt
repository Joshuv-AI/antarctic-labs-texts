Mitigation of the Anodic Shift of the Onset Potential in a TiO2/α-Fe2O3 Photoanode by Inserting a Nickel Ferrite (NiFe2O4) Blocking Layer
Authors: Seong Kyu Jang, Seung Hyeon Jeong, Aram Hong, Woon Yong Sohn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
It is widely recognized that the insertion of TiO2 as a blocking layer into a photoanode in a PEC (photoelectrochemical) system can retard the electron–hole back recombination. However, it has been also reported that it can induce an anodic shift in the onset potential in the current density–voltage curve. In this study, we introduced a nickel ferrite (NiFe2O4) layer as an additional layer to offset the adverse effect of the TiO2 layer by preventing Ti diffusion from TiO2. We demonstrated that the α-Fe2O3 photoanode in which both NiFe2O4 and TiO2 layers were inserted not only maintained the high current density but also exhibited a cathodic shift in the onset potential, compared to that of α-Fe2O3 with the TiO2 layer. Furthermore, by depositing an additional NiFeO x passivation layer, we utilized the combined benefits of the NiFe2O4 underlayer and the passivation layer, ultimately achieving a cathodic shift of 0.12 VRHE. Using various electrochemical analyses and time-resolved spectroscopic techniques, we investigated the origin of the improvement in the performance of the photoanode by inserting a NiFe2O4 layer.

Introduction

Photoelectrochemical (PEC) water splitting is a promising and environmentally friendly technology for hydrogen production that utilizes semiconductor materials to convert solar energy into chemical energy. Since its first demonstration in 1972, various semiconductor materials have been extensively investigated as photoanodes for PEC oxygen evolution, such as TiO2, , Ta3N5, − α-Fe2O3, − and BiVO4. , Hematite (α-Fe2O3) has great potential as a photoanode due to its abundance, nontoxicity, relatively narrow band gap (2.1 eV), and high photostability. − Despite its theoretical expectation, the PEC performance of α-Fe2O3 is still poor due to low carrier mobility (10–2 cm2 V–1 s–1), sluggish oxygen evolution kinetics, and a high electron–hole recombination rate. −

One of the recombination pathways for photogenerated electrons and holes is back recombination. − This occurs when photogenerated electrons transferred from α-Fe2O3 to the conductive substrate recombine with holes existing on the semiconductor surface. Back recombination competes with the water oxidation reaction, leading to a decrease in the photocurrent density. , To overcome such drawbacks, many researchers have suggested the introduction of a blocking layer such as TiO2, − SnO2, SiO2, , NiO x , , and NiFe2O4. The insertion of the blocking layer between the conductive substrate and α-Fe2O3 can prevent the back transfer of photogenerated electrons to α-Fe2O3 after migration to the conductive substrate, resulting in the retardation of the back recombination. , The blocking layer must have an appropriate thickness, enabling electron tunneling.

The introduction of TiO2 into α-Fe2O3-based photoanodes has been well-known for improving conductivity, which can enhance PEC performance. ,− However, it has been reported that the TiO2 blocking layer could result in an anodic shift of the onset potential in the current density with respect to the applied voltage curve. ,− In our previous study, we demonstrated that the anodic shift took place in the α-Fe2O3-based photoanodes with the TiO2 blocking layer, which was attributed to Fermi-level pinning. Because of that, an insufficient amount of holes on the photoanode surface under the low applied bias voltage could not react at the interface efficiently.

To address the abovementioned issue, the introduction of NiFe2O4 could be one solution, which was demonstrated in our previous study, which indicated that inserting NiFe2O4 as an underlayer in the α-Fe2O3-based photoanode effectively prevented the back recombination as a blocking layer, leading to a slight cathodic shift in the onset potential. However, the growth of the α-Fe2O3 nanorods was insufficient due to the poor interface between the NiFe2O4 and α-Fe2O3 nanorods, resulting in low photocurrent density. Here, we fabricated TiO2/NiFe2O4/α-Fe2O3 photoanodes by sequentially depositing TiO2, NiFe2O4, and α-Fe2O3 on a fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrate and deposited NiFeO x known as a passivation layer on the surface of the TiO2/NiFe2O4/α-Fe2O3 photoanode. Using the time-resolved spectroscopy technique, we determined that the two layers beneath the α-Fe2O3 act as an effective blocking layer. In this study, the current density did not decrease owing to the sufficient growth of α-Fe2O3 nanorods on NiFe2O4. As evidenced by the cathodic shift in the onset potential, we demonstrated that the overall PEC performance was improved compared with the TiO2/α-Fe2O3 photoanode.

Experimental Section

Synthesis

The FTO was cleaned for 30 min with distilled water and ethanol to remove any impurities. α-Fe2O3 was prepared using a solution-derived method for growing β-FeOOH on the FTO substrate. The film was heated at 100 °C for 1 h in a solution of 0.15 M iron­(III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, 99%, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1 M sodium nitrate (NaNO3, 99.0%, Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in distilled water. Following the procedures, the film was rinsed with distilled water and then annealed at 700 °C for 35 min.

To introduce the TiO2 blocking layer to the film, a solution consisting of titanium diisopropoxide bis­(acetylacetonate) ([(CH3)2CHO]2–Ti­(C5H7O2)2, 75 wt % in isopropanol, Sigma-Aldrich) in 1-Butanol (CH3(CH2)3OH, ≥99.4%, Sigma-Aldrich) was spin-coated on the FTO substrate. The spin-coated film was annealed at 500 °C for 30 min. To introduce the NiFe2O4 blocking layer to the film, iron­(III) 2-ethylhexanoate (50% w/w in mineral spirits, Alfa Aesar) and nickel­(II) 2-ethylhexanoate (78% w/w in ethyl hexanoic acid, Sigma-Aldrich) were mixed in hexane. This metal complex solution was spin-coated onto the film. The film was then exposed to UV light (254 nm) for 5 min and annealed at 700 °C for 35 min. By sequentially following this process, α-Fe2O3 (H), TiO2/α-Fe2O3 (T/H), NiFe2O4/α-Fe2O3 (N/H), and TiO2/NiFe2O4/α-Fe2O3 (T/N/H) films were fabricated.

To deposit NiFeO x , a metal complex solution was prepared by mixing iron­(III) 2-ethylhexanoate (50% w/w in mineral spirits, Alfa Aesar) and nickel­(II) 2-ethylhexanoate (78% w/w in ethyl hexanoic acid, Sigma-Aldrich) in hexane, followed by a 10-fold dilution with hexane. The solution was spin-coated onto the α-Fe2O3 electrode under optimized conditions, and the coated sample was exposed to UV light for 5 min. Finally, NiFeO x was obtained by being annealed at 100 °C for 1 h.

Characterization

The crystal structures of the fabricated films were analyzed by using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Rigaku Miniflex 600 diffractometer. The surface morphologies were examined through scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Carl Zeiss, Gemini 560). UV–vis absorption spectra were recorded in the 400–800 nm range with a UV–vis spectrometer (Agilent Technologies Cary 8454) to assess the optical properties of the films. The surface elemental composition was confirmed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Ulvac-PHI, PHI Quantera-II), and the cross sections of each sample were analyzed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI company, Titan cubed G2 60-300).

PEC Measurements

The photoelectrochemical properties were evaluated under AM 1.5 G light (100 mW/cm2) by using a potentiostat (CHI650E). The J–V curves, J–t curves, photoelectrochemical impedance spectra (PEIS), and open-circuit potential (OCP) were obtained with the same instrument. The PEC cell setup consisted of an α-Fe2O3-based photoanode as the working electrode, a Pt counter electrode, a Hg/HgO reference electrode, and an aqueous NaOH solution (pH 13.6) as the electrolyte. To standardize the voltage values, all applied voltages (vs Hg/HgO) were converted using the Nernst equation, with the standard potential of Hg/HgO. Time-Resolved Measurement

To investigate the dynamics of photogenerated charge carriers in the fabricated films, a near-field heterodyne transient grating (NF-HD-TG) technique was used. This technique provides high sensitivity and effectiveness in time-resolved spectroscopic studies at solid/liquid interfaces. − The detail of the NF-HD-TG method was described in the previous studies. − Briefly, we employed the third harmonic output of a Nd:YAG (YAG) laser as the pump light (355 nm, 1.0 mJ/pulse, 10 Hz) and a continuous wave (CW) diode laser (637 nm) as the probe light. The pump light was irradiated on the sample to generate a transient grating. In the presence of the transient grating, the signal and reference (0th order diffraction) were both detected at the zeroth order spot. The signal intensity, resulting from interference between the signal and reference beams, was optimized by adjusting the sample to the transmission grating distance. A photodiode was used as a detector (Thorlabs, model PDA36A2). To suppress the scattered light and fluorescence generated from the excited sample, a long pass filter (Thorlabs, FELH0600, 600 nm) was installed in front of the detector. The NF-HD-TG responses were monitored and recorded by using an oscilloscope (Tektronix, TDS5054). All measurements were conducted in a three-electrode configuration (α-Fe2O3-based photoanode, Pt, and Hg/HgO) at 0 VRHE.

Results and Discussion

To identify the crystal structures of the fabricated films, we measured X-ray diffraction patterns, as shown in Figure . The diffraction peaks labeled with a hash (#) and an asterisk (*) were attributed to α-Fe2O3 (JCPDS 01-080-5413) and SnO2 from the FTO substrate (JCPDS 01-079-6887), respectively. As shown in Figure , the diffraction patterns of all types of films indicated the successful synthesis of α-Fe2O3. To confirm their existence, we measured the XRD patterns of the T/H/N sample by producing thicker layers under the same conditions and successfully assigned them (TiO2: JCPDS 84-1286, NiFe2O4: JCPDS 01-086-2667), as shown in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information (SI), consistent with the former studies. ,,− Thus, we believe that the TiO2 and NiFe2O4 layers existed in the fabricated films, as well. Even after a 10 h J–t stability test at 1.23 VRHE, the crystalline structure of the NiFe2O4 layer remained well preserved, as shown in Figure S2.

Figure presents the top views of the SEM images of each film, which show the structure of the α-Fe2O3 nanorods. The TiO2 and NiFe2O4 layers were not observed in the images because these layers were deposited between α-Fe2O3 and the FTO substrate. As shown in Figure c, the size of the α-Fe2O3 nanorods in N/H was smaller than those of the others, which could be attributed to the insufficient growth of the α-Fe2O3 nanorods. In contrast, in the case of T/N/H film, the α-Fe2O3 nanorods were well-fabricated, as shown in Figure d, meaning that the introduction of TiO2 facilitated the adequate growth of α-Fe2O3 on the NiFe2O4 layer. Although the exact reason the TiO2 layer affects the growth of α-Fe2O3 is not clear, there is a possibility that Ti diffusion plays a role in this effect. SEM top view images of each film after the 10 h J–t stability test at 1.23 VRHE (Figure S3) show that no morphological changes were observed even after the water oxidation reaction.

XPS was employed to analyze the electronic and chemical environments of the prepared films and to further verify their fabrication. Detailed information about the photoelectron peak positions is provided in Tables S1–S3 in SI. As shown in Figures S5–S8, the survey spectra of all films showed Fe 2p1/2 and Fe 2p3/2 peaks at 724 and 711 eV, ,, respectively, along with the O 1s peak at 529 eV, , indicating the successful synthesis of α-Fe2O3. The high-resolution Fe 2p spectra of each film (Figures S5b and S8b) reveal contributions from both Fe2+ and Fe3+ species. Likewise, the high-resolution O 1s spectra of each film (Figures S5c and S8c) show peaks corresponding to lattice oxygen (OL), oxygen vacancies (Ov), and chemisorbed oxygen (OA). In addition, Ti signals were detected in the XPS spectra of the T/H and T/N/H films (Figures S7 and S8), with Ti 2p1/2 and Ti 2p3/2 peaks observed at 463 and 458 eV, , respectively, confirming the presence of a TiO2 layer as well as Ti diffusion from it to the surface. High-resolution Ti 2p spectra (Figures S7d and S8d) reveal contributions from Ti3+ and Ti4+ species. By contrast, no Ni peaks were detected in the N/H and T/N/H films, suggesting that the NiFe2O4 layer is located beneath α-Fe2O3 and that Ni did not diffuse to the surface.

To examine the surface characteristics after long-term stability, XPS measurements were performed following a 10 h J–t test (Figures S9–S12). For the H film, as shown in Table S1–S3, both Fe 2p and O 1s peaks exhibited a slight shift (∼0.4 eV) to higher binding energy, likely due to surface charging or changes in surface potential rather than a chemical change in the α-Fe2O3 structure. For the other films, no significant changes in the Fe 2p, O 1s, or Ti 2p peaks were observed after the 10 h stability test. In the N/H film, minor variations in the Fe 2p1/2 of the Fe3+ peak were detected, which are likely attributable to fitting uncertainty or surface charging effects. Overall, these results indicate that no substantial peak shifts occurred after the 10 h stability test, suggesting that all films remained stable without leaching or structural degradation.

To evaluate the PEC performance of the photoelectrodes, we conducted J–V curves using linear sweep voltammetry (LSV). The concentrations of TiO2 and NiFe2O4 were optimized based on the photocurrents at 1.23 VRHE in the J–V curves shown in Figure S13 in SI. As shown in Figure S13d and SI, we also confirmed that the inversion of the TiO2 and NiFe2O4 layers did not affect the PEC performance. Figure a displays the averaged J–V curve obtained by measuring the curves under the same conditions as shown in Figure S14 and in SI. The average photocurrent values at 1.23 VRHE with error bars were also presented in Figure S15 in SI. The photocurrent densities of the films with the TiO2 layer at 1.23 VRHE were significantly increased, as presented in Figure a (H: 0.24 mA/cm2, T/H: 0.38 mA/cm2, N/H: 0.24 mA/cm2, T/N/H: 0.42 mA/cm2), which is consistent with other studies. , In addition, we also observed that the onset potential of the photoanodes with the TiO2 layer was shifted anodically, as expected. The T/H and T/N/H photoanodes exhibited lower photocurrent densities at lower applied voltages compared to the H and N/H photoanodes, while the photocurrent density increased significantly at higher applied voltages. As shown in Figure b, the onset potentials of the N/H and T/N/H films were slightly shifted cathodically (H: 0.69 VRHE, T/H: 0.79 VRHE, N/H: 0.67 VRHE, T/N/H: 0.76 VRHE), but the effect of inserting the NiFe2O4 layer on photocurrent density was negligible.

To alleviate Fermi level pinning by reducing the defect states existing on the surface of the films, a passivation layer (NiFeO x ) was deposited on the T/N/H photoanode surface, achieving a significant cathodic shift of the onset potential (0.67 VRHE) and a relatively high photocurrent density (0.43 mA/cm2 at 1.23 VRHE).

Using the J–V curves shown in Figure a, we calculated the applied bias photon-to-current efficiencies (ABPEs) in accordance with the previous studies. , As shown in Figure S16 and SI, the ABPE % values of the films with the TiO2 layer were lower than those of the H and N/H at lower applied voltages. On the other hand, at higher applied voltages, the ABPEs % for the films with the TiO2 layer increased. Moreover, at 1.0 VRHE, the measured ABPEs % values were 0.033% (H), 0.037% (T/H), 0.034% (N/H), and 0.041% (T/N/H), showing that the ABPE increases with the incorporation of NiFe2O4.

To confirm the charge separation (ηsep) and transfer efficiencies (ηtrans) of all the prepared films, the J–V curves were measured, with 0.5 M Na2SO3 added as a hole scavenger, shown in Figure S17 in SI. Figure c and d show ηsep and ηtrans, respectively, which were calculated by eqs and from previous studies. ,

As seen in Figure c, the ηsep of the films with a TiO2 layer was lower than those of the H and N/H films at low applied voltages, whereas they significantly increased at the high applied voltages. In particular, the ηsep of T/N/H was highest at higher applied voltages. As suggested previously, the low ηsep of the films with a TiO2 layer was attributed to Fermi level pinning caused by the diffusion of Ti4+ originating from the TiO2 layer, hindering the efficient separation of the photogenerated charge carriers. Under the high applied bias voltage, the Fermi level could be unpinned, allowing for effective separation of the charge carriers. In addition, when the applied voltage exceeded approximately 1.1 VRHE, the ηsep of the films with the NiFe2O4 layer increased.

ηtrans, shown in Figure d, was determined by the number of the photogenerated holes trapped at the photoanode surface and the overpotential for charge transfer at the interface between the photoanode and the electrolyte. Since the surfaces of the films were not treated in this study, the ηtrans of all the films was directly correlated with the quantity of the holes trapped on the α-Fe2O3 surface. At lower applied voltages, the films with the TiO2 layer exhibited lower ηtrans values compared to the other films. However, as the applied voltage increased, ηtrans improved as well, exhibiting the highest efficiency in the case of the T/H film. It implies that, if the TiO2 layer existed, the amount of the photogenerated holes trapped at the surface would be smaller when the applied voltage was low but it increased with the increase in the applied bias voltage.

To evaluate the long-term photostabilities of the photoanodes, we measured J–t curves at 1.23 VRHE for 10 h. As shown in Figure , the photocurrents remained stable for all types of photoanodes, which indicates that the TiO2 and NiFe2O4 layers did not affect the stability of α-Fe2O3.

To investigate the kinetics of photogenerated charge carriers in both the bulk and interface regions, photoelectrochemical impedance spectroscopy (PEIS) was performed across a voltage range from 0.7 to 1.2 VRHE, as presented in Figure . The impedance values were derived from the Nyquist plots, along with the fitting parameters and the equivalent circuit diagram shown in Figure S20 in SI. In the circuit, R s represents the series resistance, which includes both the resistance of the conductive substrate (FTO) and any external circuit components. R 1 corresponds to the bulk transport resistance of the photoanode and is affected by the conductivity. R 2 denotes the interfacial charge transfer resistance between the photoanode surface and the electrolyte, influenced by either the number of photogenerated holes remaining on the photoanode surface or the overpotential.

Applied voltage vs resistance graphs of R 1 (bulk) and R 2 (between photoanode and electrolyte interface resistance).

As shown in Figure a, the R 1 values of the photoanodes with the inserted TiO2 layer were consistently lower than those of the H and N/H films across all applied voltages, which indicates that the introduction of the TiO2 layer was able to enhance the conductivity of the photoanode. In contrast, when the NiFe2O4 layer was inserted into the T/H film, the conductivity decreased, suggesting that the presence of the NiFe2O4 layer could be unfavorable for charge transport. However, the increase in photovoltage (Figure , Table ) and the significant reduction in R 2 at low voltage (Figure b) outweigh this drawback, resulting in an overall enhancement of PEC performance. On the other hand, as shown in Figure b, the R 2 values of the films with the TiO2 layer were significantly higher than those of others under the low applied bias voltage, meaning that the charge transfer efficiencies of the samples with the TiO2 layer were lower than those of others as already reported in the previous study. However, it was noticeable that the R 2 values could be drastically reduced by the introduction of NiFe2O4, which implies that it could mitigate Fermi level pinning originating from the insertion of the TiO2 layer, resulting in an increase in the number of holes on the photoanode surface at lower voltages.

In order to further confirm whether the insertion of NiFe2O4 indeed suppressed the Ti diffusion originating from the TiO2 layer, we measured the XPS spectra of Ti 2p, as shown in Figure S21 in SI. As illustrated in the figure, the intensity of the Ti 2p peaks significantly decreased in the presence of the NiFe2O4 layer. Since XPS can sensitively detect elements present at a depth of approximately 10–20 nm from the surface, indicating that NiFe2O4 acted as an effective barrier against Ti diffusion from TiO2. As mentioned above, in our previous work, we established that Fermi level pinning was induced by Ti diffusion from the TiO2 layer. Therefore, it is reasonable to infer that the Fermi level in the T/N/H sample was also unpinned as a result of reduced Ti diffusion.

Under this condition, it has been understood that the slow decay of long-lived holes can be assigned to back recombination. ,

This observation supports the conclusion that both TiO2 and NiFe2O4 can act as the blocking layers effectively, which significantly suppressed the back recombination. Moreover, as shown in Figure a, c, and d, the presence of the TiO2 layer prevented the band bending at the low applied voltages, resulting from Fermi level pinning, which induced the anodic shift of the onset potential. At higher applied voltages, Fermi level unpinning took place, which enhanced photocurrent density, charge separation, and transfer efficiencies ultimately, resulting in improved water splitting efficiency. As shown in Figure S21, the incorporation of NiFe2O4 effectively suppresses the surface diffusion of Ti. This suppression alleviates Fermi level pinning, thereby improving the charge separation efficiency, as demonstrated in Figure c. Moreover, the increase in the photovoltage observed in the OCP curves (Figure and Table ) provides evidence of Fermi level unpinning. Consequently, the introduction of NiFe2O4 into the films leads to a cathodic shift of the onset potential. Additionally, we demonstrated that the TiO2 layer enhanced the conductivity as shown in the PEIS results in Figure with the marked reduction in the R 2 resistance of T/N/H, compared to T/H at the low applied voltage (0.8 VRHE), supporting the claim that the presence of the NiFe2O4 layer induces Fermi level unpinning. We proposed that the NiFe2O4 layer can alleviate Fermi level pinning, thereby facilitating more effective band bending at relatively low applied voltages. In addition, as observed in the PEIS results, the R 1 value of the sample including NiFe2O4 indicated a decrease in conductivity. However, we believe that the increase in photovoltage and decrease in R 2 (Figure b) help compensate for this drawback. The combined effect likely contributes to the relatively small shift in the onset potential observed in the T/N/H sample. In previous study, we utilized NiFe2O4 as a blocking layer. In contrast, in this study, we utilized the NiFe2O4 layer to specifically mitigate the increase in the onset potential induced by the TiO2 underlayer.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced NiFe2O4 to enhance the PEC performance of the T/H photoanode. Using the NF-HD-TG method, we demonstrated that both TiO2 and NiFe2O4 effectively suppress the back recombination between the photogenerated electrons in the conductive substrate and the surface trapped holes. The insertion of the TiO2 layer in the photoanode resulted in an increased photocurrent density and an anodic shift of the onset potential, as expected. The insertion of the NiFe2O4 layer in the T/H photoanode increases the charge separation efficiency along with the enhanced photovoltage, which could be attributed to Fermi level unpinning, giving rise to the cathodic shift of the onset potential with the aid of strong band bending, ultimately improving the PEC performance. Further enhancements in the PEC performance could be achieved through surface modification using a passivation layer (NiFeO x ).
