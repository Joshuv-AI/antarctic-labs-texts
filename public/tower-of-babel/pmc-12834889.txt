HOMER3 and its correlation with immune-infiltrating M2 macrophages in colorectal cancer
Authors: Ruirui Luan, Changjiang Yang, Xianglian Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHomer scaffold protein 3 (HOMER3) is a member of the HOMER family of postsynaptic density scaffolding proteins, but its function in CRC remains unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the role of HOMER3 in CRC by analyzing extensive databases and evaluating real-world clinical samples. Additionally, multivariate Cox regression analysis and functional enrichment analysis were utilized to identify the pathways and functions potentially regulated by HOMER3 and its co-expressed genes. Database analysis unveiled a significant upregulation of HOMER3 in CRC, correlating with diminished survival metrics and adverse clinical indicators such as advanced pT stage and pN stage. Experimental analysis of real-world samples showed that HOMER3 expression was elevated in CRC and was associated with advanced pN and pT stages. Elevated HOMER3 expression emerged as an independent prognostic marker for poorer outcomes in CRC patients. The functional enrichment analysis underscored HOMER3’s oncogenic potential, implicating it in key regulatory mechanisms of tumorigenesis and its association with diverse immune cell infiltration, notably macrophages. We observed a positive correlation between M2 macrophages and HOMER3 expression, a finding that was further corroborated by immunofluorescence staining. HOMER3 may serve as a valuable prognostic biomarker in CRC, and its expression correlates with the infiltration of M2-polarized macrophages within the tumor immune microenvironment. These findings underscore the necessity for further investigations into the functional role of HOMER3 in CRC progression and its underlying immune regulation mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04247-8.Keywords: Homer scaffold protein 3, Colorectal cancer, Prognosis, Immune microenvironment

Introduction

CRC remains one of the most prevalent malignant tumors worldwide, ranking third in incidence and second in mortality [1]. Furthermore, there is a concerning trend toward earlier onset of the disease, with around 45% of cases developing metastasis or recurrence after surgical treatment [2].

In-depth investigations into CRC pathogenesis have expanded therapeutic options for both localized and advanced-stage diseases, leading to a doubling of overall survival (OS) in advanced-stage patients. However, surgical intervention remains the cornerstone of CRC management, highlighting the critical importance of early diagnosis and timely intervention in significantly improving patient prognosis. These findings underscore the urgent need to identify novel biomarkers for improving prognostic outcomes in CRC patients.

Homer scaffold protein 3 (HOMER3) plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of various diseases. It is a gene encoding a protein that belongs to the Homer family of postsynaptic density scaffold proteins [3]. The encoded protein contains a domain mediating protein-protein interactions, consisting of an N-terminal Enabled/vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein homology 1 (EVH1) domain facilitating protein-protein interactions, along with a carboxy-terminal coiled-coil domain and two leucine zipper motifs involved in self-oligomerization. Functionally, this multifunctional protein participates in numerous essential biological processes [3–6] and has been strongly associated with the development of several disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease [7–9].

Notably, HOMER3 expression is significantly upregulated in multiple human malignancies, including NSCLC, bladder cancer, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), LIHC Clinically, elevated HOMER3 expression correlates with poor prognosis in these cancer types. Mechanistically, in NSCLC, elevated levels of HOMER3 activate the expression of downstream mitochondrial genes via GA-binding protein subunit beta-1 (GABPB1), thereby promoting mitochondrial anabolism [10]. In bladder cancer, nutrient deprivation can increase HOMER3 expression, thereby promoting tumor growth [11]. HOMER3 can enhance the invasion of TNBC through its interaction with β-catenin [5]. In the context of LIHC, HOMER3 facilitates the progression of LIHC via the EZH2/miR-361/GPNMB axis. HOMER3 has significant diagnostic implications. Elevated expression levels of HOMER3 may indicate the presence of LIHC [12]. Additionally, the serological detection of anti-HOMER3 antibodies in patients with cerebellar atrophy can aid in the diagnosis of BRCA [13]. Knocking out HOMER3 can reduce the growth and migration capacity of glioma cells [14]. HOMER3 is also highly expressed in acute myeloid leukemia and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [15, 16]. Given its multifaceted roles in tumor biology, HOMER3 inhibition has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer diagnosis and treatment [5, 10–12]. However, the functional role and molecular mechanisms of HOMER3 in CRC pathogenesis remain poorly understood. To date, only a single study has partially addressed this issue, and notably, this investigation lacked validation in human clinical samples. Therefore, the precise mechanisms through which HOMER3 promotes CRC development and progression remain to be fully elucidated, warranting further comprehensive investigation.

Interactions between malignant and nonmalignant cells within the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) are crucial for cancer development, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic outcome [17]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) play a key role in the TIME and are generally the most abundant population of immune cells [18].

We hypothesize that HOMER3 expression is significantly upregulated in CRC and is clinically associated with poor prognosis in CRC patients. Furthermore, we propose that HOMER3 serves as a critical regulator of the TIME in CRC.

We utilized the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) databases, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) to compare the expression of HOMER3 between CRC and normal samples, further assessing the relationship between HOMER3 expression profiles and patient clinical characteristics and survival outcomes. Additionally, we evaluated the correlation between HOMER3 mRNA levels and the TIME, employing immunofluorescence methods to elucidate the relationship between HOMER3 and macrophages. These findings offer new insights into the involvement of HOMER3 in CRC progression and anti-tumor immune responses.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

On January 3, 2024, we accessed the Xiantao Academic Online Analysis Tool (https://www.xiantao.love/) to retrieve gene expression RNA-seq data and related clinical information from the TCGA database, including 647 CRC patients (COAD and READ) and 51 normal tissue samples.

Survival analysis

We used Kaplan Meier (KM) technique and log-rank test for survival analysis, with HOMER3 median expression level as the cutoff value. We used univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis to examine the impact of clinical pathological variables on patient prognosis. We included prognostic variables with p values < 0.1 in the univariate analysis into subsequent multivariate analyses. Subsequently, multivariate Cox analysis was used to detect independent prognostic indicators.

Enrichment analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), Gene Ontology (GO), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were performed using the ClusterProfiler package (v3.6.3) in R. The GO analysis systematically evaluated three ontologies: cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes. GSEA was implemented as described by Subramanian et al. (2005) to assess the statistical significance and coordinated expression patterns of predefined gene sets across biological conditions. Significance thresholds were set at FDR < 0.25 and |NES| >1.5 according to GSEA best practices, with additional filtering using adjusted p-values (Benjamini-Hochberg method, p < 0.05). The reference gene sets were obtained from MSigDB v7.5.1, specifically utilizing the h.all.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt (Hallmark gene sets) and C5.All.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt (Gene Ontology collections) as biological annotation databases.

Analysis of immune infiltration

The ESTIMATE algorithm was employed to calculate the stromal and immune scores in CRC samples [19]. The relationship between HOMER3 expression and immune cell infiltration was assessed using multiple methods, including MCPCOUNTER [20], quanTIseq [21], EPIC [22], CIBERSORT [23], and TIMER [24]. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the association between HOMER3 expression and immune cell infiltration. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to compare immune cell infiltration between HOMER3 low- and high-expression groups.

Patients

This study enrolled 77 patients diagnosed with adenocarcinoma. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged between 18 and 80 years, no history of other malignant tumors, no prior radiotherapy or chemotherapy, no neoadjuvant therapy before surgery, and availability of complete clinical and pathological data. For IHC analysis, tumor tissues from surgical resections and adjacent normal tissues (located > 5 cm from the tumor) were fixed in 4% formalin and embedded in paraffin blocks. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Outdo Biotech Company. All participants provided written informed consent after being fully informed about the study’s content and procedures.

IHC staining

CRC and adjacent normal tissue sections were fixed in paraffin, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated using a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer under microwave heating at 95 °C. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min. After blocking with 10% fetal bovine serum for 1 h, sections were incubated with the primary antibody against HOMER3 (1:400; 16624-1-AP) at 4 °C overnight. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated universal anti-rabbit secondary antibody was applied, followed by detection with diaminobenzidine (DAB) and counterstaining with hematoxylin. Two independent pathologists evaluated HOMER3 expression based on the proportion of positively stained cells and staining intensity. The proportion of positive cells was scored as 1 (≤ 30%), 2 (31–70%), or 3 (≥ 70%), and staining intensity was scored as 1 (weak) or 2 (strong). The IHC score was calculated by summing these scores, with final HOMER3 expression defined as low (0–3) or high (4–6).

Immunofluorescence staining

CRC tissue samples were selected for immunofluorescence staining. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated to inactivate endogenous peroxidase. After antigen retrieval, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against HOMER3 (1:300; 16624-1-AP) and CD163 (1:100; ab156769) at 4 °C for 48 h. After washing with Tris-buffered saline (TBS), sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The percentage of positive cells was quantified, and results were validated by two pathologists.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 3.6.3). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used to compare HOMER3 expression between tumor and normal tissues. The TISIDB online platform (Ru et al., 2019) was utilized to analyze the relationship between HOMER3 expression and pathological staging or patient prognosis. Welch’s one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc test (or t-test) was applied to assess associations between HOMER3 expression and clinicopathological variables. The Pearson chi-square test was used to evaluate relationships between HOMER3 expression and clinicopathological parameters; Fisher’s exact test was applied when theoretical frequency < 5 or total sample size < 40. KM curves were constructed to assess the predictive value of HOMER3. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate progression-free interval (PFI), disease pecific survival (DSS), and OS. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate analysis. A two-tailed p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The expression level of HOMER3 is upregulated in CRC

Our initial analysis of HOMER3 mRNA expression patterns across multiple tumor types in the TCGA database demonstrated significant upregulation in various malignancies compared to their corresponding normal tissues, including COAD, BLCA, BRCA, CESC, READ, and STAD. To enhance the reliability of our findings, we subsequently analyzed paired tumor-normal samples from the TCGA database. The results were consistent with our initial unpaired sample analysis (Fig. 1A, B). In the TCGA database, CRC is classified into two distinct subtypes: COAD and READ. Notably, HOMER3 mRNA expression levels were significantly elevated in both subtypes compared to matched adjacent normal tissues. To validate these findings, we systematically examined HOMER3 expression in CRC tissues, confirming significant upregulation in both paired and unpaired samples relative to normal controls (Fig. 1C-D). To further investigate the relevance of HOMER3 in CRC, we performed IHC analysis on 77 clinically annotated CRC samples. Our findings revealed that the protein levels of HOMER3 are also elevated in the tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1E-F). High HOMER3 expression predicts poor prognosis in CRC patients

To investigate the association of HOMER3 with clinicopathological features and prognosis in cancer patients, we analyzed correlations between HOMER3 mRNA expression levels, clinical characteristics, and survival outcomes. Utilizing the TISIDB database, our study demonstrated that elevated HOMER3 mRNA expression inversely correlated with OS in multiple malignancies [25]. Further analysis of tumor staging revealed that higher HOMER3 mRNA levels were significantly associated with advanced tumor stages (Fig. 2A, B) in multiple malignancies. Focusing on CRC, we assessed the prognostic relevance of HOMER3. Welch’s one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction indicated that HOMER3 mRNA expression was significantly linked to N staging, DSS, and PFI, but not with age, gender, primary tumor (T) staging, or distant metastasis (M) staging (Fig. 2C-K). Stratifying patients into high- and low-expression groups (n = 322 each) based on median HOMER3 mRNA levels confirmed associations between HOMER3 expression and N staging, DSS, and PFI (Supplementary Table 1). Logistic regression analysis revealed that high HOMER3 expression correlated with advanced N staging, shorter DSS, and reduced PFI compared to low expression (Table 1).

Survival analyses demonstrated that patients with high HOMER3 expression exhibited significantly worse OS, DSS, and PFI (Fig. 2L-N). Univariate Cox analysis identified T staging, N staging, M staging, pathological stage, age, and high HOMER3 expression as risk factors for shorter OS. Subsequent multivariable analysis confirmed age, T staging, M staging, pathological stage, and high HOMER3 expression as independent predictors of poor OS (Supplementary Table 2). Similarly, univariate analysis linked shorter DSS to T staging, N staging, M staging, pathological stage, and high HOMER3 expression, while multivariable analysis identified M staging, pathological stage, and high HOMER3 expression as independent prognostic factors (Supplementary Table 3). Finally, high HOMER3 expression, alongside T staging and M staging, emerged as an independent predictor of reduced PFI in both univariate and multivariable analyses (Supplementary Table 4).

HOMER3 May be involved in the initiation and progression of CRC

To further explore the role of HOMER3 in the occurrence and development of CRC, this study analyzed the differential genes between the high and low expression groups of HOMER3. The analysis revealed that compared to the low expression group, the high expression group showed 252 downregulated genes and 658 upregulated genes (Fig. 3A. Supplementary Tables 5 and 6 for the top 50 significantly changed genes). Furthermore, GO and KEGG pathway analyses showed that the downregulated genes were mainly enriched in pathways related to the detection of chemical stimulus involved in sensory perception, DNA packaging complex, olfactory receptor activity, and Neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation. NETs have been shown to exhibit chemotactic effects on circulating tumor cells, promoting the progression of colorectal cancer [26]. On the other hand, the upregulated genes were mainly enriched in pathways associated with external encapsulating structure organization, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, and signaling receptor activator activity (Fig. 3B, C). Additionally, GSEA revealed that in patients with upregulated HOMER3 expression, there was an enrichment of genes involved in pathways related to Neuroactive Ligand Receptor Interaction, Pathways in Cancer, Leukocyte Transendothelial Migration, Chemokine Signaling Pathway, and Cell Adhesion Molecules Cams. Conversely, in patients with decreased HOMER3 expression, gene changes were associated with pathways such as Interferon Gamma Response, Angiogenesis, Tnfα Signaling Via Nf-κb, and Hypoxia (Fig. 3D and E). The enrichment of these genes indicates that HOMER3 may play a role in the initiation and progression of CRC.

HOMER3 expression correlates with infiltration levels of macrophages

Utilizing the ESTIMATE algorithm to evaluate tumor microenvironment scores (ESTIMATE, Stromal, and Immune scores), we observed significantly elevated scores in CRC patients with high HOMER3 expression (Fig. 4A-C). Multiplatform immune profiling (MCPCOUNTER, QUANTISEQ, EPIC, CIBERSORT, TIMER) consistently demonstrated a strong positive correlation between HOMER3 expression and macrophage infiltration, specifically driven by the M2 subtype (MCPCOUNTER: r = 0.318, p < 0.001; QUANTISEQ M2: r = 0.141, p < 0.001; EPIC: r = 0.370, p < 0.001; CIBERSORT M2: r = 0.125, p < 0.01; TIMER: r = 0.167, p < 0.001), while no significant association was found with M1 macrophages (QUANTISEQ M1: r=-0.015, p = 0.72; CIBERSORT M1: r = 0.048, p = 0.23), suggesting HOMER3 promotes tumor associated macrophage (TAM) infiltration and M2 polarization in CRC. This conclusion was further supported by marker analysis showing stronger correlation with M2 marker CD163 (r = 0.38, p < 0.001) than panmacrophage marker CD68 (r = 0.18, p < 0.01) (Fig. 4I-J), and immunofluorescence validation revealing significant colocalization of HOMER3 and CD163 in CRC tissues (p < 0.05, Fig. 4K-L). Mechanistically, CRC with high HOMER3 expression exhibited upregulated GABPB1 (r = 0.21, p < 0.001, Fig. 4M)and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) levels (r = 0.63, p < 0.001, Fig. 4N), which showed a positive association with CD163 expression (r = 0.59, p < 0.001), GABPB1, r = 0.18, p < 0.001, TGF-β, r = 0.59, p < 0.001, Figure 4O, P). These correlations suggest that HOMER3 promotes the M2 polarization of macrophages, which may be associated with GABPB1 and TGF-β.

Discussion

CRC is a highly prevalent gastrointestinal tumor and remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality globally, with a low five-year survival rate [1]. Research on the pathogenesis and treatment of CRC, spanning both clinical and basic sciences, has yielded tangible therapeutic outcomes, resulting in significant improvements in both the survival duration and quality of life for CRC patients. However, despite these advancements, the mortality rate remains alarmingly high. While prognoses for CRC patients have seen enhancements, the mortality rate continues to rank second globally.

HOMER3 is widely expressed in normal tissues and plays crucial physiological roles. Emerging evidence has demonstrated its significant involvement in the pathogenesis of multiple malignancies, including NSCLC, bladder cancer, TNBC, and LIHC [12]. Notably, current literature reveals limited studies investigating the functional role and molecular mechanisms of HOMER3 in CRC [27].

Bioinformatics approaches have become indispensable tools for identifying novel cancer biomarkers, with validated clinical relevance in recent studies [28]. In this study, we analyzed HOMER3 mRNA expression patterns across pan-cancer datasets from TCGA. Our results demonstrated significantly elevated HOMER3 mRNA levels in CRC and other malignancies compared to adjacent normal tissues. To validate these findings, IHC analysis of 77 paired CRC and normal tissues revealed consistent upregulation of HOMER3 protein in tumor specimens. In real word, elevated HOMER3 protein correlated with advanced pathological N staging, higher TNM staging, and increased risk of distant metastasis in CRC. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified HOMER3 as an independent prognostic factor for OS, DSS, and PFI. Importantly, high HOMER3 expression levels showed significant associations with shortened DSS/PFI. These findings highlight the potential of HOMER3 as a prognostic biomarker in CRC.

Notably, GSEA specifically highlighted the chemokine signaling pathway as demonstrating strong positive correlation with HOMER3 expression levels. This association provides mechanistic evidence supporting our hypothesis that HOMER3 may facilitate tumor progression through chemokine network modulation within the TIME. Our GSEA analysis further revealed that epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT) processes showed significant association with HOMER3 expression patterns. Of particular note, EMT enables malignant cells to acquire aggressive phenotypic characteristics critical for metastasis [29]. Significantly, TGF-β, a potent EMT inducer that plays dual roles in colorectal cancer pathogenesis by initiating EMT programming while simultaneously regulating metastatic processes, microenvironment remodeling, and immune evasion mechanisms [29], showed coordinated expression patterns with HOMER3. Our data suggest that HOMER3-mediated regulatory mechanisms within the TIME may constitute a key molecular pathway contributing to unfavorable clinical outcomes in CRC patients.

The dynamic relationship between tumor cells and immune cell infiltration is pivotal in cancer progression [30]. The quantification of tumor-infiltrating immune cells is anticipated to elucidate the multifaceted role of the immune system in human cancer, particularly its involvement in tumor escape mechanisms and response to treatment. Tumor-infiltrating immune cells can be quantified from RNA sequencing data of human tumors using bioinformatics methods [31]. Multi-omics integration research has distinct advantages in the field of tumor research [32]. MCPCOUNTER is a method designed to quantify tumor-infiltrating immune cells, fibroblasts, and epithelial cells based on a rigorous and robust set of marker genes [33]. QUANTISEQ and quanTIseq are specifically developed for RNA-seq data analysis [34]. EPIC estimates have demonstrated high consistency with immune cell fractions calculated by flow cytometry [22]. CIBERSORT is a widely used method for assessing immune cell infiltration; it employs the principle of linear support vector regression to deconvolve the expression matrix of immune cell subtypes and estimate their abundance [23]. These algorithms utilize different approaches and emphasize distinct aspects of immune cell quantification. TAMs serve as accessible biomarkers in the TIME and have been shown to correlate with cancer prognosis [35]. Various platforms, were utilized to analyze the relationship between HOMER3 expression and immune cells, Various platforms, were utilized to analyze the relationship between HOMER3 expression and immune cells, which make results more reliable.

HOMER3 plays a critical role in regulating immune homeostasis [4, 36]. The analysis across multiple platforms consistently indicates an association between macrophages and HOMER3 expression. Research has shown that M1 macrophages play a role in enhancing the immune system’s ability to combat tumors, exerting anti-cancer effects during tumorigenesis. Conversely, M2 macrophages not only promote tumor growth by directly or indirectly stimulating angiogenesis but also facilitate immune suppression, assisting tumors in evading immune surveillance, thus exhibiting pro-tumorigenic effects[37]. We observed a positive correlation between type M2 macrophages and HOMER3 expression, a finding that was further corroborated by immunofluorescence staining. To further investigate how HOMER3 influences the regulation of macrophage chemotactic differentiation, our study conducted an analysis of differentially expressed genes. The results revealed a positive correlation between the expression of HOMER3 and TGF-β. Further literature search, we found GABPB1 deletion results in mitochondrial abnormalities and dysfunction, characterized by reduced mitochondrial oxygen consumption and impaired mitochondrial protein synthesis[38]. In NSCLC, elevated levels of HOMER3 activate the expression of downstream mitochondrial genes via GABPB1, consequently increasing oxygen consumption [10]. Tumor hypoxia has been shown to promote the expression of TGF-β [39]. Our study presume that in colorectal cancer, increased HOMER3 levels may enhance oxygen consumption through GABPB1 and subsequently elevate TGF-β production. Our study proposes a hypothesis that HOMER3 may facilitate the transition of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype by promoting TGF-β expression.

To further investigate how HOMER3 regulates macrophage chemotactic differentiation, we analyzed differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The results demonstrated a positive correlation between HOMER3 expression and levels of TGF-β and GABPB1. Notably, prior studies indicate that GABPB1 deficiency induces mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by reduced oxygen consumption and impaired mitochondrial protein synthesis [38]. In non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), HOMER3 upregulation activates GABPB1-dependent mitochondrial gene expression, thereby enhancing oxygen consumption [10]. Since tumor hypoxia is known to promote TGF-β production [39], we hypothesize that in colorectal cancer, elevated HOMER3 may augment oxygen consumption via GABPB1, potentially driving TGF-β upregulation. Additionally, both TGF-β and GABPB1 showed positive correlations with CD163 (an M2 macrophage marker). Based on these observations, we hypothesize that HOMER3-associated macrophage polarization in CRC may be mechanistically linked to TGF-β. This study may provide a new direction for both basic and clinical research in CRC.

While this study provides novel insights into the role of HOMER3 in colorectal cancer progression, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size in the validation cohort (n = 70) may compromise the statistical power of our conclusions. Second, although we systematically proposed hypotheses through bioinformatic analyses, the absence of experimental validation using both in vivo and in vitro models weakens the evidence level for the proposed mechanisms. Future investigations will focus on: (1) expanding clinical cohorts to validate the prognostic value of HOMER3 expression in CRC; (2) performing comprehensive in vivo and in vitro functional experiments to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying HOMER3-mediated CRC progression; (3) investigating the mechanism of HOMER3-associated macrophage phenotypic transition by monitoring dynamic changes in TAMs using live-cell imaging. These proposed studies aim to establish a theoretical foundation for developing HOMER3-targeted therapies and optimizing immunotherapeutic strategies in CRC management.

Conclusions

In summary, our study findings indicate elevated expression of HOMER3 in CRC tissues compared to normal tissues, with higher levels of HOMER3 expression associated with later stage disease and poorer prognosis. HOMER3 may promotes the differentiation of macrophages into the M2 phenotype, thereby promoting tumor progression, a process that may be mediated by TGF-β. However, due to the limited experimental conditions, there are still some shortcomings. In our future research, we will supplement relevant experiments to further elucidate the underlying mechanism.
