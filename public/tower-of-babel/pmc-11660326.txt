Adenoviral-vectored neoantigen vaccine augments hyperexpanded CD8+ T cell control of tumor challenge in mice
Authors: Gabriel Dagotto, Alessandro Colarusso, Robert C Patio, David Li, Tochi Anioke, Victoria Giffin, Ruoran Guan, Trisha Anand, Esther Mbiwan, Malika Aid, Dan Barouch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoantigens are promising immunogens for cancer vaccines and are often delivered as adjuvanted peptide vaccines. Adenoviral (Ad) vectors have been shown to induce strong CD8+ T cell responses as vaccines against SARS-CoV-2, Ebola, and Zika, but their utility as neoantigen delivery vectors remains largely unexplored. In this study, we examine how an Ad-vectored neoantigen vaccine would impact tumor immunity compared with a peptide neoantigen vaccine.MethodsWe generated Ad serotype 26 (Ad26) vaccine candidates encoding B16-F10-ovalbumin (OVA) and MC38-specific neoantigens. Ad26 vaccines were compared with adjuvanted peptide delivery as prophylactic vaccines in B16-F10-OVA and MC38 challenge models. Immune responses induced by the best Ad26 vaccine (Ad26.VP22.7Epi) were compared with peptide vaccination systemically and within the tumor. Following vaccination with Ad26.VP22.7Epi, peptide, or sham, tumor-infiltrating CD45+ cells were analyzed using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and T cell receptor sequencing (TCR-seq) to identify vaccine-induced differences in the tumor microenvironment.ResultsSingle-shot Ad26 vaccines induced greater neoantigen-specific interferon-γ CD8+ T cell immune responses than two-shot adjuvanted peptide vaccines in mice, and Ad26.VP22.7Epi also provided superior protective efficacy compared with the peptide vaccine following tumor challenge. Ad26.VP22.7Epi induced a robust immunodominant CD8+ T cell response against the Adpgk neoantigen, while the peptide vaccine-induced lower responses against both Adpgk and Reps1 neoantigens. scRNA-seq analysis of CD45+ tumor-infiltrating cells demonstrated that both Ad26.VP22.7Epi and peptide vaccine-induced similar numbers of infiltrating CD8+ T cells. However, Ad26.VP22.7Epi induced CD8+ T cells showed more upregulation of T cell maturation, activation, and Th1 pathways compared with peptide vaccine induced CD8+ T cells, suggesting improved functional T cell quality. TCR-seq of these tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes also demonstrated that Ad26.VP22.7Epi generated larger T cell hyperexpanded clones compared with the peptide vaccine.ConclusionsThese results suggest that the Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccine led to improved tumor control compared with the peptide vaccine due to increased T cell hyperexpansion and functional activation. Our data suggest that future cancer vaccine development strategies should focus on inducing functional hyperexpanded CD8+ T cell responses and not only maximizing tumor infiltrating CD8+ T cell numbers.Keywords: Vaccine, T cell

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study demonstrates that Ad serotype 26 (Ad26) neoantigen delivery induces more activated and functional CD8+ T cells as well as increased T cell hyperexpansion within the tumor microenvironment compared with long peptide vaccination. Introduction

Cancer vaccines have become a promising new approach as prophylactic or therapeutic regimens for different cancers. Several immunogens, including neoantigens,1 have been used to direct the immune response against tumor cells. Neoantigen vaccines focus the immune response on spontaneous mutations in tumor cells, thereby selectively targeting cancer cells rather than host cells.2 3 Neoantigen cancer vaccines have shown promise in multiple clinical trials using adjuvanted long peptides.4 5

Neoantigen vaccines induce CD8+ T cells for tumor clearing.6 Other cell types such as CD4+ T cells have been found to be important7 8 but the ability of a cancer vaccine to generate strong antigen specific CD8+ T cell responses appears to be critical.9 Currently, adjuvanted peptides are a common vaccine platform for neoantigen vaccines due to manufacturing ease, stability, and easy administration,10 but peptide vaccines are often weak inducers of CD8+ T cells.10 11

The limitations of the long peptide platform present an opportunity for further optimization using viral vectors or messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines. Adenoviral (Ad) vector vaccines have shown promise for Ebola,12 Zika,13 and most recently SARS-CoV-2 with the J&J Ad serotype 26 (Ad26).CoV2.S14 and the AstraZeneca ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 vaccines.15 16 Ad vectors are promising in the cancer field due to their immunogenicity and limited need for cold storage.17 In particular, Ad vectors have been shown to induce robust CD8+ T cell responses.16 Previous work has shown that Ad vector vaccines with checkpoint inhibitors can limit or even prevent tumor growth in mice,18 but there has been limited data to date investigating how Ad vectors induce immune response within the tumor microenvironment. The objective of our study was to compare immune responses induced by an Ad vector vaccine and an adjuvanted long peptide vaccine with the goal of identifying critical cell populations and immune responses for future cancer vaccine design.

In this study, we generated several candidate Ad26 vaccines and tested immune responses systemically and within the tumor microenvironment in mice following challenge with two different cancer cell lines. We then used single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and T cell receptor sequencing (TCR-seq) to identify differences in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in mice vaccinated with Ad26 or long peptide vaccines to inform future cancer vaccine design.

Results

Ad26 vaccination in B16-F10-OVA model

To assess the potency of Ad vector vaccines we created an Ad26 vaccine that encodes ovalbumin (Ad26.OVA). We tested this vaccine in both a prophylactic and therapeutic context. Four weeks prior to the challenge (figure 1A), we vaccinated mice with either single shot Ad26.OVA, single shot OVA protein (with anti-CD40 antibody and Polyinosinic acid:polycytidylic acid (Poly I:C) as adjuvants), or sham. One day prechallenge we assessed the immunogenicity of each vaccine platform using whole-blood intracellular cytokine staining (WB-ICS). Mouse peripheral blood mononuclear cells were stimulated with a commercially available OVA MHC-I peptide, and immune responses were measured as %interferon (IFN)γ+ among CD8+ T cells. The Ad26.OVA vaccine generated CD8+ T cell responses that were one order of magnitude higher than the peptide vaccine (figure 1B). Mice were then challenged with 105 B16-F10-OVA cells implanted in the right flank. Sham mice showed rapid tumor growth from day 10 to day 40 (figure 1C). By day 50 all sham mice reached humane endpoints (figure 1D), whereas both vaccines provided complete protection in this model. We then tested vaccine protection against B16-F10-OVA cells in a therapeutic context (figure 1A). Mice were challenged with 105 B16-F10-OVA cells implanted in the right flank. Mice were vaccinated 4 days after the challenge. Both Ad26.OVA and OVA protein demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in this model (figure 1E,F).

Screening Ad26 neoantigen constructs to identify candidate vaccine

We next compared Ad26 and peptide vaccines in a more stringent tumor challenge model. To develop MC38 cancer vaccines, we concatenated seven previously identified MHC-I MC38 neoantigens,19 termed “7Epi”. We then generated four candidate Ad26 vectors expressing the 7Epi immunogen either alone or together with other carrier proteins reported to boost immune signaling (figure 2A). The 7Epi immunogen was expressed alone (Ad26.7Epi), connected to herpes viral protein 22 on either the N terminus (Ad26.VP22.7Epi) or on the C terminus (Ad26.7Epi.VP22) to improve CD8+ T cell responses,20 21 or expressed with the shark invariant chain22 that has been shown to increase CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses in hepatitis B vaccines23 (Ad26.Shark.7Epi). All candidate vaccines were screened for in vitro expression levels which were comparable in A549 cells (figure 2B). Mice were immunized with 109 vp of each vector. Four weeks later, splenocytes were harvested and ICS was performed following stimulation with a pool of all seven neoantigen peptides. All four candidate vaccines showed comparable antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses, and no responses were observed in the sham and vector control (Ad26.Luciferase) groups (figure 2C). We did not observe any significant differences between our candidate vaccines despite differences in co-expressed proteins.

Testing Ad26 candidate vaccines with standard peptide vaccines

All four Ad26 vaccine candidates were immunogenic after a single dose and were evaluated for protective efficacy. We compared the Ad26 vaccines with the 7Epi immunogen delivered as peptides with poly I:C and an anti-CD40 monoclonal antibody (mAb) mAb. A single shot of the adjuvanted peptide vaccine did not provide any detectable protection against tumor growth compared with naïve mice, suggesting that two shots would be required for the peptide vaccine (online supplemental figure S1). Mice therefore received a single immunization of the Ad26 vaccines 28 days before challenge or two immunizations of the adjuvanted peptide vaccine 21 and 7 days before challenge, as described by Yadav et al 2014 (figure 3A). One day before the challenge, mice were bled and WB-ICS was performed to determine immune responses and identify correlates of protection. The four Ad26 vaccines showed higher 7Epi-specific immune responses than the peptide vaccine in both %IFNγ+ and %CD107a+ CD8+ T cells (figure 3B,C).

Protective efficacy of Ad26 and peptide vaccines against MC38 tumor challenge

Following vaccination (figure 3A), mice were challenged with 3×105 MC38 tumor cells implanted subcutaneously in the right flank. Tumor volumes were measured three times per week, and tumor volumes were plotted (figure 3, online supplemental figure S2). Sham vaccination (Naïve, Ad26.Luciferase, and Adjuvant alone) demonstrated the fastest tumor growth, and all vaccine groups demonstrated reduced tumor growth compared with sham. The best protection was observed with the Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccine, which was significantly better at limiting tumor growth than sham at every time point measured (p<0.0001 at all time points). The peptide vaccine was only better than the sham at the first three time points (D12; p=0.0028, D15; p<0.0001, D19; p<0.0001, D22; p=0.0824, D25; p=0.2652). All Ad26 vaccines were able to fully protect 15–20% of mice out to day 100 (figure 3E). By comparing the WB-ICS data prior to the challenge, we observed that increased CD8+ T cells expressing CD107a and IFNγ negatively correlated with tumor growth, but regulatory T cells (Treg) cells showed no correlation to tumor growth at any time point (figure 3F).

Therapeutic efficacy was tested with and without immune checkpoint blockade (online supplemental figure S3a). Ad26.VP22.7Epi alone did not have an effect in a therapeutic context, likely due to the speed of the MC38 tumor model and the time required for Ad26 vectors to induce immune responses. When used in tandem with anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) antibody administration we observed a trend towards increased therapeutic efficacy in Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccinated mice compared with sham mice (online supplemental figure S3b,c).

Deconvoluting vaccine immune responses

To further understand the differences in immune responses between vaccine platforms, we compared the best Ad26 vaccine candidate, Ad26.VP22.7Epi, to the adjuvanted peptide vaccine. We sought to deconvolute the immune response and study recall effects postchallenge. To accomplish this goal, we vaccinated two cohorts of mice with the vaccines or sham following the same schedule as used in the prior study (figure 4A). One cohort of mice was used to evaluate CD8+ T cell responses to each peptide comprizing the 7Epi immunogen. The second cohort of mice was challenged with 3×105 MC38 cells and tumor growth was measured. Consistent with the prior study, the Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccine showed superior protection than both the sham and the adjuvanted peptide vaccine (figure 4B). Ad26.VP22.7Epi induced CD8+ T cell responses to Adpgk and Irgq, whereas the adjuvanted peptide vaccine induced detectable CD8+ T cell responses against only Reps1 on day 0 prior to challenge (figure 4C). The second cohort of mice was sacrificed on day 14, and both the spleen and tumor were harvested to assess recall immune responses in those tissues. In the spleen, we observed a large increase in Adpgk-specific CD8+ T cells in Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccinated mice (from median 0.08 at D0 to 0.63 at D14; p=0.0003 Mann-Whitney test) (figure 4D). In the adjuvanted peptide-vaccinated mice, we observed lower responses to both Adpgk and Reps1.

Immune responses were much stronger within the tumor than in the spleen, suggesting a robust local tissue response. The CD8+ T cell responses increased from a median of 0.63% in the spleen to a median of 15.44% in the tumor for Ad26.VP22.7Epi (figure 4E; p=0.0001 Mann-Whitney test). Peptide Adpgk (median 4.11%) and Reps1 (median 4.84%) responses were also higher than sham. No Irgq-specific CD8+T cell responses were observed in the TILs. Thus, Ad26.VP22.7Epi induced a stronger immunodominant response largely against the single peptide Adpgk while the peptide had weaker responses against two of the seven peptides. This prompted us to investigate Adpgk alone as an Ad vector immunogen, but this immunogen alone was not sufficiently immunogenic to merit further testing (online supplemental figure S4).

Day 14 spleen and tumor ICS responses were correlated to tumor growth (figure 4F). The percent of Adpgk-specific CD8+ T cells in both the spleen and the tumor negatively correlated with tumor growth. Higher numbers of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells also negatively correlated with tumor growth. Increased spleen Adpgk-specific CD8+ T cells correlated with increased tumor infiltrating Adpgk-specific CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S5).

scRNA-seq analysis of TILs

Our data suggested key differences in immune responses induced by Ad26.VP22.7Epi and peptide vaccination in the tumor microenvironment. To analyze these differences in greater detail, we used scRNA-seq on TILs from sham, peptide, and Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccinated mice. Mice were immunized as shown (figure 5A). From day 18 to day 20 postchallenge, 1–2 mouse tumors were harvested per treatment group (sham, peptide, Ad26.VP22.7Epi) per day. Tumors were processed and magnetic-assisted cell sorting (MACS) was used to selectively isolate CD45+ cells; thereby, excluding tumor cells from our scRNA-seq analysis. 16 mouse tumors were used for this analysis (sham=5, peptide=5, Ad26=6). A minimum of 10,000 CD45+ cells were harvested from each tumor for sequencing using the 10x Genomics scRNA-seq platform.

Individual cells were analyzed, and cell types were identified using the Immunological Genome Project (ImmGen) cell classifications. All cells from 16 different mouse tumors were integrated to generate a consensus uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and identify different cell types (figure 5B). The majority of cells identified in the tumor were macrophages or monocytes. Cell type population sizes were similar across treatment groups, with the exception of T cells and their subpopulations (figure 5B). We generated a gene heatmap to evaluate our cell classification (figure 5C). This heatmap showed good agreement between our clusters and cell type specific gene expression. To further confirm our labeling, we generated graphs overlaying cell type specific genes on our UMAP. We displayed CD3e (T cells) (figure 5E), CD8a (CD8+ T cells) (figure 5F), and CCR2 (monocyte) (figure 5G), which showed agreement with our clustering. Both vaccine types showed increased tumor CD8+ T cells compared with the sham group (figure 5D; Ad26 vs sham; p=0.0260, peptide vs sham; p=0.0079), with a trend towards the greatest number of tumor infiltrating CD8+ T cells in the adjuvanted peptide-vaccinated mice.

Differential gene expression analysis of tumor infiltrating CD8+ T cells

Differences in the percentage of infiltrating CD8+ T cells prompted us to investigate differential gene expression (DGE) between CD8+ T cells from sham, peptide, and Ad26.VP22.7Epi mice. 251 genes were upregulated in the CD8+ T cells from Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccinated mice and 130 genes were upregulated in the peptide-vaccinated mice (figure 6A). QIAGEN Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA)24 was used to identify differentially upregulated and downregulated relevant immunological pathways in CD8+ T cells (figure 6B). Comparing Ad26.VP22.7Epi to sham vaccinated mice, we observed an upregulation in Th1 pathway, NUR77 Signaling in T lymphocytes (implicated in antigen receptor signaling,25 Granzyme A signaling, and a slight activation in PD-1, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) signaling, as well as a downregulation in interleukin (IL)-10 signaling, IL-8 signaling, and IFN signaling. When comparing Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccinated mice to peptide vaccinated mice, we observed similar results to the comparison with sham, although the Th1 signaling, NUR77 signaling, and TNFR2 signaling pathways were more highly upregulated in the Ad26.VP22.7Epi immunized mice compared with peptide immunized mice. We also found that IL-10 signaling and PD-1, PD-L1 signaling were more highly upregulated in peptide mice compared with the Ad26.VP22.7Epi mice.

We used IPA to investigate disease and biological function pathways that were differentially modulated (figure 6C). We observed an upregulation in differentiation of helper T lymphocytes, T cell development, differentiation of T lymphocytes, and maturation of leukocytes and a downregulation in T cell migration, cell movement of T lymphocytes, and proliferation of T lymphocytes when comparing Ad26.VP22.7Epi to sham. When comparing Ad26.VP22.7Epi to peptide vaccination, we saw further upregulation of the same pathways.

To confirm the mechanistic importance of CD8+ T cells, we CD8+ depleted immunized mice prior to challenge (online supplemental figure S6) and compared MC38 tumor growth in CD8+ depleted mice compared with control mice (figure 6D). CD8+ depletion led to much faster tumor growth in all groups with all CD8+ depleted groups exceeding the growth of the sham, isotype control tumors. These results demonstrate that CD8+ T cells are critical for tumor control following vaccination. Interestingly, we observed a short-term, transient delay in tumor growth between the Ad26.VP22.7Epi anti-CD8+ group and the other CD8+ depleted groups. This indicates that other cells in addition to CD8+ T cells may also contribute to tumor control following Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccination.

TCR-seq analysis of TILs

To further understand the impact of vaccines on T cell responses, we conducted TCR sequencing on the same cells from figures5 6. Using TCR sequencing, we identified 12,051 cells with complete TCRs across all mice.26 The ProjecTILs package was used to analyze T cell subpopulations.27 We identified multiple CD8+ subsets as well as Treg populations and other minor T cell subtypes (figure 7A). The total number of tumor-infiltrating TCR positive cells for both peptide (4,772) and Ad26.VP22.7Epi (4,809) was nearly double that of sham (2,470). Investigating specific T cell subsets, we observed differences in the Treg populations with sham having significantly more tumor Tregs than Ad26.VP22.7Epi. Both vaccine groups had larger amounts of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells than sham, and sham mouse tumors contained significantly more naïve CD8+ T cells compared with vaccinated mouse tumors (figure 7B; Ad26 vs sham; p=0.0173, peptide vs sham; p=0.0317).

Using TCR sequencing we explored variability-diversity-joining(VDJ) gene usage within each group and identified T cell populations that share VDJ gene usage. We determined T cell clonotypes as cell populations sharing the same VDJ genes to allow intermouse and intergroup comparisons. We overlayed the top T cell clonotypes for each condition onto a UMAP of all T cells (figure 7C). The population of cells from sham immunized mice was much smaller than from vaccinated mice (figure 7C). Both the peptide and sham groups showed mostly CD8+ exhausted and CD8+ pre-exhausted T cells. In contrast, the Ad26.VP22.7Epi group showed many cells in the CD8+ effector memory region of the UMAP. We also saw this difference in CD8+ effector memory cells when comparing the top five clonotypes (online supplemental figure S7). We compared the top 10 TCR gene clonotypes to the total number of T cells infiltrating the tumor normalized per animal (figure 7D). Sham mice showed considerably fewer T cells than peptide or Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccinated mice. Moreover, peptide-vaccinated mice had significantly less expansion of clonotypes than the Ad26.VP22.7Epi mice. Peptide-vaccinated mice had about five moderately expanded TCR populations, whereas the Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccinated mice had two large TCR populations and multiple smaller populations. Interestingly, many of the same VDJ genes were used for both vaccines (online supplemental table S1). We considered a T cell population to be hyperexpanded when it occupied 10–100% of TCRs sequenced in an individual mouse. In the sham group, one out of five mice (20%) had a hyperexpanded TCR clonotype population (figure 7E). In the peptide vaccinated group, this number increased to three out of five mice (60%), but in the Ad26.VP22.7Epi group this number expanded to six out of six mice (100%) of mice showing hyperexpanded TCR clonotypes (figure 7E).

Correlating tumor infiltrating cell populations to tumor growth

Results from scRNA-seq and TCR-seq were correlated with tumor growth of each mouse to identify potential correlates of protection (figure 7F). A larger percentage of all tumor-infiltrating T cells being CD8+ T cells negatively correlated with tumor growth (online supplemental figure S8a; R=−0.5499; p=0.0273). Hyperexpanded CD8+ T cells also showed a negative trend with tumor growth (online supplemental figure S8c). Treg and naïve CD8+ T cell populations both positively correlated with tumor growth (online supplemental figure S8b,d; R=0.5714; p=0.0208, d; R=0.5488; p=0.0277).

Discussion

Comparing different vaccine platforms and regimens allows the identification of critical immune responses important for tumor control. We compared Ad vector-delivered neoantigens to adjuvanted peptide neoantigens in tumor challenge models in mice. We observed superior immunogenicity when vaccinating with single-shot Ad26 vaccines compared with two-shot peptide vaccines in the MC38 model. The Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccine showed a narrow but robust CD8+ T cell response against one neoantigen epitope (Adpgk), whereas the adjuvanted peptide vaccine showed a weaker but broader immune response against both Adpgk and Reps1. Both vaccines resulted in increased T cell infiltration into tumors but led to the upregulation of different transcriptomic pathways in the TILs. Ad26.VP22.7Epi-elicited T cells showed upregulation of T cell activation and maturation pathways, whereas the peptide-elicited T cells showed upregulation of T cell exhaustion pathways. Ad26.VP22.7Epi also induced more robust hyperexpanded T cell populations than the adjuvanted peptide vaccine. These data suggest that the superior efficacy of the Ad26.VP22.7Epi vaccine compared with the adjuvanted peptide vaccine may relate to the more robust function and hyperexpansion of Adpgk-specific CD8+ T cells in tumors, rather than simply increased numbers of CD8+ T cells in tumors.

Previous work has shown that, when combined with checkpoint blockade inhibitors, a gorilla Ad vector is able to induce stem-like CD8+ T cells.28 In the future, it will be critical to determine if the creation of large, high-quality, hyperexpanded T cell populations is intrinsic to the Ad vector platform as well as understand why this occurs. Future studies could further optimize the induction of functional, hyperexpanded neoantigen-specific CD8+ T cells. Both peptide and Ad vectors as well as potentially other platforms (mRNA, DNA, dendritic cell (DC), etc) could give different important immune responses.29,31 mRNA neoantigen vaccines have been used to provide multiple boosts without induction of anti-vector immune responses and are currently being explored in clinical trials.32

Both peptide and Ad-vector based vaccines are in clinical trials.33 34 Peptide vaccines are currently the predominant neoantigen vaccine modality with both short peptides and long peptides being tested.33 The NeoVax vaccine has been a prominent peptide neoantigen vaccine tested in clinical trials against chronic lymphocytic leukemia (NCT03219450) with cyclophosphamide and pembrolizumab and against melanoma (NCT01970358) following tumor resection. NeoVax showed promising results particularly in the melanoma trial where six of eight patients were found to have no signs of active disease 4 years after treatment.35 Peptide vaccines have also been tested for prevention against malignancy in spreading pancreatic or colorectal cancers (NCT02600949). Ad vector-based therapeutics have recently begun entering the clinic: Gritstone bio using a chimpanzee Ad vector prime followed by a self-amplifying mRNA booster in combination with nivolumab and ipilimumab showed safety in phase I clinical trials.36 Nouscom’s personalized neoantigen cancer vaccine (NOUS-PEV) consisting of a great ape Ad vector prime and a modified Vaccinia Ankara vaccine with pembrolizumab was tested in a phase Ib study and showed safety and immunogenicity.37 Other vaccine modalities such as mRNA and DCs are also being tested in the clinic, and results from these studies will help inform future vaccine design.38

Our work compared different vaccine platforms primarily in a prophylactic model. In most real-world scenarios, neoantigen vaccines will be generated and delivered after the patient has developed cancer.39 This is difficult to model with MC38 tumors, as this model leads to lethal tumors in 2–3 weeks and is too rapid for induction of optimal vaccine responses. Patient tumors generally do not grow as rapidly as the MC38 cancer cell line and oftentimes patients will have tumors surgically removed before receiving neoantigen treatment.40 Future work could use genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) as an alternative for slower-growing tumors. These mouse models contain mutations that lead to the development of tumors and certain models are slower growing than the B16-F10-OVA or MC38 models.41 GEMMs can also be engineered to produce tumors with known neoantigens which would allow a consistent neoantigen vaccine to be tested in these models instead of generating a new vaccine for each mouse.42 Another area of improvement could be the location of the tumor. The MC38 syngenic cell line is useful for its widespread adoption and ease of measurement, but implanting tumors subcutaneously in the flank can lead to translatability issues as the tumor is no longer located in its native environment leading to differences in the tumor microenvironment compared with a naturally occurring tumor. Using orthotopically implanted tumors could allow testing of the Ad26 vaccine in a more natural tumor microenvironment.43 44 The route of immunization could also be tested for an effect on efficacy. All Ad26 vaccines in this work were administered intramuscularly while peptide vaccines were given intraperitoneally. These routes were selected to match previous work using each platform,19 45 but there is extensive evidence demonstrating route of immunization can lead to different immune responses.46,48

In conclusion, our work showed that the Ad26.VP22.7Epi platform induces robust, functional, hyperexpanded CD8+ T cell responses in tumors against the dominant tumor neoantigen Adpgk. These data suggest the potential importance of large, hyperexpanded activated CD8+ T cell clones, rather than just the number of tumor-infiltrating T cells.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

MC38 and B16-OVA cell lines were a generous gift from Dr Ferry Ossendrop at Universiteit Leiden. Both cell lines were grown in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) supplemented with 8% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% Pen-Strep, and 30 µM 2-Mercaptoethanol. B16-OVA cell media was additionally supplemented with 1 mg/mL geneticin. Cells were maintained at 37°C with 10% CO2. Cells were expanded, aliquoted, and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Before each tumor experiment, cells were thawed and passaged two times to expand before the challenge.

Ad26 vectors

Ad26 viral vector transgenes were generated from the 7Epi Immunogen along with vaccine-specific carrier proteins (figure 2). All transgene sequences were codon optimized and synthesized through Thermo Fisher Scientific GeneArt. Ad26 non-replicating vectors lacking E1/E3 regions were grown in HEK 293B-55k.TetR cells.49 The E1 gene region was replaced with the transgene of interest. All vectors were sequenced and tested for infectivity before use.

Mouse immunizations and immunogenicity work

Female C57BL/6 mice (The Jackson Laboratory) were randomly distributed into groups. Mice received a single shot of 109 vp Ad26 replication-incompetent vaccine suspended in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), single shot peptide vaccine consisting of: 50 µg of each of the seven peptides in the 7Epi immunogen with 100 µg Poly(I:C) (InvivoGen: 31852-29-6) and 50 µg anti-CD40 antibody (Bio X Cell: BP0016-2, clone FJK45), prime-boost peptide vaccine with the same adjuvants, or sham vaccines. Mice receiving Ad vector or sham vaccines were vaccinated through the intramuscular route by administering 100 µl to each mouse divided evenly between both mouse quadriceps muscles. Sham mice were vaccinated with the same route and dose as the Ad26 vaccines but were administered only 1× PBS. Peptide vaccinations were given through the intraperitoneal route at a volume of 100 µl per mouse. Peptide sequences were as follows: Aatf: SKLLSFMAPIDHTTMSDDARTELFRS; Reps1: GRVLELFRAAQLANDVVLQIMELCGATR; Irgq: KARDETAALLNSAVLGAAPLFVPPAD; Adpgk: GIPVHLELASMTNMELMSSIVHQQVFPT; Cpne1: DFTGSNGDPSSPYSLHYLSPTGVNEY; Dpagt1: EAGQSLVISASIIVFNLLELEGDYR; Med12: DIDPSSSVLFEYMEKPDFSLFSP. To collect peripheral blood for WB-ICS, mice were bled through the submandibular route.

For tumor challenge studies, C57BL/6 mice (The Jackson Laboratory) were randomly allocated into groups. 1×105 B16-F10-OVA cells or 3×105 MC38 tumor cells in PBS were implanted subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse. For prophylactic work, Ad26 or sham vaccinated mice received 109 vp of Ad26 vaccine or sham 28 days before the challenge. Peptide-vaccinated mice received 50 µg of each of the seven peptides in the 7Epi immunogen with 100 µg Poly(I:C) and 50 µg anti-CD40 antibody at 21 days and 7 days before the challenge. 10 days after tumor inoculation, tumors were measured at a minimum of 3 days per week. Tumor volume was calculated as the length (longest dimension) × width2/2. Tumor length and width were measured with a digital caliper. Animals that showed adverse clinical signs were observed more frequently and euthanized if moribund. Mice were euthanized if tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3. All animal studies were conducted in compliance with all relevant local, state, and federal regulations and were approved by the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees.

Anti-PD-1 antibody administration

Anti-PD-1 antibody (Bio X Cell #BE0146) and isotype control antibody (Bio X Cell #BE00890) were administered on days 7, 10, 14, and 17 postchallenge at 200 µg per mouse per dose. These antibody doses were administered as 100 µl intraperitoneal injections.

CD8+ T cell depletion

Mice were immunized prophylactically as previously described. Two days and 1 day prior to the challenge mice were administered 500 µg of CD8+ depletion antibody (Bio X Cell #BE0061) or an isotype control antibody (Bio X Cell #BE0090) as a 100 µl intraperitoneal injection.

Immune assays

ICS assay

Tumors were processed following the Miltenyi Biotec Tumor Dissociation kit instructions, mouse (130-096-730) protocol. In brief, tumors were cut into small 2–4 mm pieces and were placed into Miltenyi gentleMACS C-Tubes (130-093-237) containing 2.35 mL of Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 media. 100 µL Enzyme D, 50 µL Enzyme R, and 12.5 µL Enzyme A were added to the C-tube. Tumors were processed on the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator using the “m_impTumor_02” protocol followed by a 40 min incubation at 37°C. After incubation, tumors were processed on the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator using the “m_impTumor_03” program once. Cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 7 min. Cells were resuspended in 3 mL of R10 (500 mL RPMI, 50 mL FBS, 5 mL Pen/Strep) and then passed through a 70 µm filter (Miltenyi: 130-110-916) followed by a 30 µm filter (Miltenyi: 130-041-407). After passing through a 30 µm filter tumor cells were treated the same as splenocytes.

Resuspended splenocytes (100 µL) were added to a 96-well plate and incubated with 100 µL media, a stimulation pool containing peptides of interest at a concentration of 2 µg/mL, or Leukocyte Activation Cocktail with BD GolgiPlug from BD Biosciences (550583) (positive control). Cells were incubated at 37°C for 1.5 hours. Following incubation 0.2 µL of GolgiPlug and GolgiStop in 50 µL of R10 was added to each well. The plate was then incubated for 5 hours at 37°C then moved to 4°C overnight. The next day, the cells were washed two times with MACS buffer, stained with Aqua live/dead dye and (for TILs cells were also incubated with anti-mouse CD16/32 Fc Block BD Biosciences (553141) then stained with an antibody cocktail consisting of predetermined titers of the following antibodies: CD8a (clone: 53–6.7; PE/Dazzle594), CD4 (clone: GK1.5; BUV496), CD3 (clone: 17A2; BUV737), CD44 (clone: IM7; BV711), CD45 (clone: 30-F11; BV785), CD25 (clone: 3C7; PerCP/Cyanine5.5). Cells were incubated at 4°C protected from light for 1 hour. Afterward, cells were washed with MACS buffer twice and then incubated with 100 µL of Cytofix/Cytoperm solution for 15 min at 4°C. Cells were then washed two times with 1× Perm/Wash Buffer (BD Perm/Wash Buffer 10× in Cytofix/Cytoperm Fixation/Permeabilization kit diluted with Milli-Q water and passed through 0.22 µm filter) (for antibody cocktails containing FoxP3 ab BD Pharmigen Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BD Biosciences 562574) was used per manufactures instructions) and stained with an antibody cocktail consisting of the following abs: FoxP3 (clone: R16-715; APC), IFNγ (clone: XMG1.2; BV421), TNFα (clone: MP6-XT22; PE/Cyanine7), IL-2 (clone: JES6-5H4; AF700). Cells were then washed two times with 1× Perm/Wash Buffer and once with MACS Buffer before being fixed with MACS Buffer containing 2% formaldehyde. The fixed cells were then analyzed by the BD LSR II system.

scRNA-seq sample collection

15 mice for each group (sham, peptide+adj, Ad26.VP22.7Epi) were vaccinated and implanted as previously described in the methods sections. At day 18 post-tumor implantation, the five smallest tumors (six smallest for Ad26.VP22.7Epi group) by volume in each group were selected to be harvested over the next 3 days. On day 18, two tumors of the five were harvested from each group. The same was done on day 19. On day 20, one tumor was harvested from each group except the Ad26.VP22.7Epi group which harvested two tumors. This resulted in five tumors for sham and peptide+adj, respectively and six tumors for Ad26.VP22.7Epi.

Tumors were collected and processed as discussed in the ICS portion of the methods. After resuspending the tumor cells and generating a single cell suspension TILs were selected by using MACS for CD45+ cells. TILs were isolated following the Miltenyi CD45 (TIL) MicroBeads mouse protocol. Briefly, tumor cells were resuspended in 90 µL of MACS Buffer. After resuspension 10 µL of CD45 (TIL) MicroBeads mice were added. Cells were incubated at 4°C for 15 min in the dark. Buffer was then added to the cells to reach 500 µL total volume. The cell suspension was then put through the Miltenyi LS column on a Miltenyi QuadroMACS separator and washed two times with 1 mL MACS Buffer. After washing the LS column was removed from the QuadroMACS separator and cells were flushed out using the Miltenyi LS column plunger.

scRNA-seq library prep was provided by the Single Cell Core at Harvard Medical School, Boston, Massachusetts, USA. For each library, cells were resuspended in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) with 0.04% FBS to achieve a target concentration of 200–1,500 cells per µL. Samples with more than 85% viability were processed. For each library, ∼16,000 cells were loaded onto a 10× chromium controller to target a recovery of 10,000 cells. Both gene expression and TCR libraries using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kits (v2) were prepared following the 10x Genomics’ user guide. Briefly, the single cells were encapsulated and barcoded within nanoliter-sized droplets along with the reagents necessary for cell lysis and reverse transcription. Within the droplet, cells are lysed and the mRNA molecules are captured and barcoded during reverse transcription. Subsequently, full-length complementary DNA was made, followed by enzymatic fragmentation. P5, P7, i7 and i5 sample indexes, and TruSeq Read 2 (read 2 primer sequence) were added via end repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation, and PCR. The libraries were sequenced at the Biopolymers Facility at Harvard Medical School using a NovaSeq 6000 V1.5 S4 full-flow cell following sequencing guidelines recommended by 10x Genomics protocol.

scRNA-seq data analysis

All operations were performed on Harvard Medical School’s Linux-based high-performance computing cluster (O2), and locally, in R (V.4.3.1).

Preprocessing and integration

Raw sequencing reads for gene expression and TCR sequencing were respectively mapped to the GRCm38 mouse reference genome (mm10, v.2020-A) and GRCm38 VDJ reference (V.7.0.0), using Cell Ranger multi V.7.0.0 (10x Genomics). Downstream analysis was conducted following the single-cell clustering pipeline in Seurat V.4.4.0. Quality control was applied to all 16 animals, with initial filtering to remove cells expressing fewer than 200 genes as well as genes that were expressed in three cells or fewer. Furthermore, cells whose mitochondrial reads comprised more than 5% of total reads were excluded. Raw counts for each animal were normalized using Seurat’s log-normalization function, and for exploratory purposes, the top 3,000 variable features were identified using the variance stabilizing transformation selection method.

Cells were annotated based on normalized counts using SingleR V.2.2.0. Annotation labels were assigned according to reference labels in the ImmGen database, using Celldex V.1.10.1. Integration was then performed according to the default integration pipeline in Seurat version V.4.4.0. This involves identifying integration features and anchors, followed by integrating all 16 mouse Seurat objects into one data set. This approach was used in order to mitigate batch effects between animals and study our mouse population as a cohesive data set. More specifically, integration was performed for two distinct cell groups across all mice: All cells and only cells defined as T cells, that is, cells identified to have a productive TCR sequence by VDJ TCR sequencing (10x Genomics).

Clustering and visualization

For visualization purposes, the integrated counts were scaled, and dimensionality reduction was performed using Seurat V.5.0.1. An elbow plot was used to assess that significant variance of the dataset was largely captured by the first 16 principal components. Principal component analysis (PCA) and UMAP for the first 20 principal components were run on the integrated data set to ensure comprehensive data representation. Following PCA and UMAP, nearest neighbors were computed, and clustering was performed to identify shared clusters within the data set, and facilitate the visualization of UMAP plots.

To evaluate the accuracy of the ImmGen labeling, we used feature plots in Seurat to visualize expression levels of singular genes across cells. Additionally, we used SingleR’s heatmap score plot to gage the overall concordance between cell types and their assignment scores. For this heatmap, we created the catchall “Rare Cells” category by averaging the scores of the following cells, which we determined to be present in low numbers in our mice: “Stem cells”, “Microglia”, “Mast cells”, “Endothelial cells”, “B cells, pro”, “Basophils”, “Endothelial cells”, “Eosinophils”, “Epithelial cells”, “Stromal cells”, “B cells”, “NK cells”, and “Tgd”.

T cell analysis

As stated previously, raw sequencing reads for TCR sequencing were mapped to the mouse GRCm38 V(D)J reference (V.7.0.0), using Cell Ranger multi V.7.0.0 (10x Genomics). T cell metadata, including VDJ usage and complete TCR sequence, were combined with each animal’s corresponding Seurat object metadata using scRepertoire V.1.10.1. Here, we used combineTCR’s removeNA option to add metadata to only those cells identified to have both valid TCR-α, and TCR-β chains. T cells not meeting this criterion were filtered out, and quality control (QC) and integration were performed as described above. To meet the needs of our experimental design in characterizing T cell populations in mouse tumors, T cells were annotated using ProjecTIL’s (V.3.1.0) classifier and mouse TIL atlas as a reference.

PCA and UMAP were performed similarly to above, and a UMAP plot was made for all T cells with clustering based on T cell subpopulations as determined by ProjecTILs. Further, UMAP plots for each treatment (sham, peptide, Ad26) were made to highlight the cells expanded with the most prevalent clonotype in each treatment. Similarly, scRepertoire’s clonal homeostasis function was used to plot T cell hyperexpansion in each animal. Here, the CTgene option was used to observe shared VDJ gene usage across treatment types. Furthermore, the top 10 clonotypes were plotted as proportions of total T cell numbers for each treatment group.

For DGE, we focused solely on CD8+ T cells, as defined by ProjecTILs. DGE was performed with the FindMarkers function in Seurat V.5.0.1. Seurat V.5 was chosen over Seurat V.4 due to the former’s change in behavior, which allows for a larger number of significant (ie, corrected p value≤0.05) differentially expressed genes (DEGs) to be detected, as compared with previous releases of Seurat. DGE was performed for the following contrasts: Ad26 versus sham, Ad26 versus peptide, and peptide versus sham. Volcano plots were generated in ggplot2 (V.3.4.4) to highlight the spread of DEGs for each contrast.

DGE analysis

DEGs for each cell-specific vaccine comparison were uploaded to QIAGEN IPA24 with log2fold change, p value, and q value. The data were analyzed with the use of QIAGEN IPA (QIAGEN, https://digitalinsights.qiagen.com/IPA). A core expression analysis based on the “Expr Log Ratio” was conducted on each data set to determine differentially expressed pathways. Analysis was combined using the comparison analyses tool. Pathways were selected by significance and relevance to the cancer disease model.
