The Token Games: Evaluating Language Model Reasoning with Puzzle Duels
Simon Henniger, Gabriel Poesia

Abstract
Evaluating the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models is increasingly challenging as models improve. Human curation of hard questions is highly expensive, especially in recent benchmarks using PhD-level domain knowledge to challenge the most capable models. Even then, there is always a concern about whether these questions test genuine reasoning or if similar problems have been seen during training. Here, we take inspiration from 16th-century mathematical duels to design The Token Games (TTG): an evaluation framework where models challenge each other by creating their own puzzles. We leverage the format of Programming Puzzles — given a Python function that returns a boolean, find inputs that make it return True — to flexibly represent problems and enable verifying solutions. Using results from pairwise duels, we then compute Elo ratings, allowing us to compare models relative to each other. We evaluate 10 frontier models on TTG, and closely match the ranking from existing benchmarks such as Humanity’s Last Exam, without involving any human effort in creating puzzles. We also find that creating good puzzles is still a highly challenging task for current models, not measured by previous benchmarks. Overall, our work suggests new paradigms for evaluating reasoning that cannot be saturated by design, and that allow testing models for other skills like creativity and task creation alongside problem solving.

Introduction

In the 16th century, mathematicians during the Italian renaissance rose to fame not for publishing breakthrough results but instead by winning public mathematical duels
(
1
)
. In these duels, two competitors presented a series of mathematical problems to each other: to win, one should successfully solve the problems posed by the other participant and stump them with one’s own challenges, revealing their solutions afterwards to the public. In a famous duel where Niccolò Tartaglia’s won against Antonio Maria Fior, it became clear that Tartaglia had found a general solution to the cubic polynomials, at a time when a general method was not yet publicly known
(
17
)
. Even then, the public could validate Tartaglia’s victory without having the mathematical knowledge necessary to solve them.

Seen as a way to compare mathematical prowess, these renaissance-era duels held many interesting properties. First, since solutions were expected to be verifiable, the public could confirm that a contestant had successfully solved a problem without the need to know or understand their reasoning. Moreover, given that arbitrarily difficult mathematical problems were acceptable, as long as they were well-defined and the proposers could solve them, duels would always allow a new mathematician to showcase their brilliance – as an evaluation of aptitude, duels cannot be saturated as long as there are always harder problems. Finally, duels were also implicitly a test of creativity: posing hard but already known problems would not work against opponents that have memorized the literature even if they lack in competence to solve new problems.

Figure 1

We can thus verify both proposer’s and solver’s solution attempts, decide the outcome of each round and of the overall duel after a fixed number of turns.

In this paper, we take inspiration from mathematical duels to design a reasoning evaluation for Large Language Models that overcomes several limitations of current benchmarks. As LLMs improve in their capability for reasoning, it has become steadily harder for humans to design new questions that can measure progress as new models are released
(
13
;
15
)
. We instead propose
The Token Games
(TTG), a reasoning evaluation based on duel-style competitions between pairs of LLMs where models themselves propose challenge puzzles for each other. TTG inherits several of the properties that we outlined above: it cannot be saturated (new, stronger models can in principle always surpass previous ones), it allows us to evaluate models on arbitrarily difficult problems without the need for expensive human curation, and it also tests creativity: repeating hard problems seen during training data is sub-optimal, since other models might also have been trained on them. TTG allows us to continuously evaluate new models with minimal effort and much lower cost compared to sourcing new problems from human experts.

In a TTG duel, as illustrated in Figure
1
, two language models take turns where they alternate between the roles of “proposer” and “solver”. Each turn might end either in a draw or in one of them scoring a point. At the beginning of the turn, the proposer first designs a puzzle and gives its own solution. To ensure that we can verify solutions, we adopt the format of Programming Puzzles
(
16
)
, proposed in prior work as a standard, flexible representation for mathematical and coding challenges. A Programming Puzzle is specified by the source code of a function that takes arbitrary parameters and returns a boolean value. The solution is an example of inputs for which the function returns true. Programming Puzzles are extremely flexible and allow for a arbitrarily hard problems to be encoded: for instance, NP-complete problems can be specified by implementing a verifier (e.g., the evaluator of a SAT formula), without revealing the solution (e.g., an assignment for propositional variables that satisfies the formula). If the proposer’s solution is incorrect, the solver scores. If it is correct but the solver succeeds, the turn ends in a draw. Otherwise, if the solver fails, the proposer scores. The duel goes on for a specified number of turns, and whoever scores more points wins the match.

We use the outcomes of a set of duels to compute Elo ratings for each language model, allowing us to rank models while always leaving space for new, more capable models to outperform. We empirically evaluate whether the TTG ranking agrees with other public benchmarks, such as GPQA and HLE. We find a significant positive correlation between our Elo ratings and both HLE (
\rho=0.58
) and GPQA Diamond (
\rho=0.63
). Moreover, we also separately evaluate model’s abilities in terms of the fraction of turns they won as a solver and as a proposer. We find that win rates as solver correlate even more strongly with existing benchmarks (
\rho=0.75
for HLE;
\rho=0.74
for GPQA Diamond). This indicates that TTG serves as a strong proxy for traditional reasoning performance when evaluating models for problem solving, but additionally tests a skill — problem creation — that is less predicted by existing benchmarks. We show several analyses of the capabilities and failure modes that TTG reveals, including many cases of overconfidence of frontier models when designing hard puzzles (and failing to solve their own problems). Overall, our work contributes new ideas for designing scalable evaluations of LLM reasoning to stand the challenges posed by rapidly improving models.

Related Work

Language Model Reasoning.

There has been rapid progress in improving the reasoning capabilities of large language models. Early experiments with GPT-3 and PaLM
(
18
)
showed that prompting models to provide a
chain-of-thought
, or fine-tuning them on scratchpads
(
12
)
, substantially improves performance on multi-step reasoning problems. Recent models have increasingly been trained or fine-tuned with reinforcement learning specifically targeted at improving their ability to reason in language
(
20
)
, yielding a line of “reasoning models” starting with OpenAI o1
(
11
)
and DeepSeek r1
(
9
)
, and now becoming common practice across frontier models. The development of ever more capable reasoning models has led to rapid and sometimes discontinuous jumps in performance on benchmark tasks, and measuring and understanding the capabilities of the most recent models has become a challenge of its own.

LLM Reasoning Benchmarks.

Early benchmarks of mathematical problems used to evaluate LLMs included GSM8K
(
6
)
and MATH
(
10
)
, extending to broader tasks such as BIG-Bench
(
2
)
and ARC-Challenge
(
5
)
. With rapid improvement, and sometimes near saturation, of performance in these benchmarks, recently benchmark developers turned to curating problems from experts, with benchmarks like GPQA
(
15
)
, Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE,
13
) and Frontier Math
(
8
)
recruiting participants explicitly to design questions requiring highly specialized (e.g., PhD-level) knowledge in various fields. Relying on domain experts to design questions that are difficult for frontier models comes at a substantial cost: constructing HLE, for instance, involving sourcing experts by using a prize pool of 500k USD for the top high-quality submissions. Thus, constructing benchmarks with this combination of quality and difficulty is done only sparingly. Moreover, even then these benchmarks still risk being saturated by rapidly improving models: even one year after its release, for instance, the most performant models already achieved close to 90% accuracy on GPQA. Moreover, without having access to model’s training data, it is impossible to disentangle factors such as potential data contamination (e.g., GPQA is available online) or similarity to previously existing problems (even if human authors were unaware of them).

Problem Generation with LLMs.

Several recent works explore using language models themselves to generate problems, besides solving them. However, due to the general concern that LLM-generated problems might be unreliable (e.g., have incorrect answers, missing hypotheses, be ambiguous, etc), these problems are generally only used as training data, but not as a target for evaluation. Notable examples include MetaMath
(
19
)
, which uses LLMs to generate synthetic mathematical problems for training, and models that generate and prove formal mathematical conjectures
(
14
;
7
)
. Our work uses LLMs to propose
Programming Puzzles

(
16
)
for one another, in contrast with previous work where puzzles were extracted from human sources.

LLM Arenas

Several recent LLM benchmarks have turned to competitions between pairs of LLMs as a way to compare them. These include Chatbot Arena
(
4
)
, ranking LLM responses to user prompts, as well as Copilot Arena
(
3
)
, where users compare LLMs as coding assistants. In existing “arenas”, however, the judges are human participants, and the prompts used to elicit model responses are also created by users. In contrast, The Token Games is a competition where the judge is simply based on executing Python code to test solutions to puzzles, and problem authors are LLMs themselves.

3
The Token Games

We now describe
The Token Games
, our proposed evaluation framework for LLM reasoning. TTG is based on the concept of a
programming puzzle
introduced in prior work as a unified representation for programming challenges
(
16
)
. A programming puzzle (or simply “puzzle”, for short) is specified by a function
f(x)
that receives input
x
and returns a boolean value (true or false). Then,
y
is a solution to the puzzle if
f(y)=\texttt{true}
.

Puzzles are a compelling representation for reasoning problems given the flexibility of this encoding. Specifying a verifier
f
is a natural way to encode a wide variety of arbitrarily difficult reasoning challenges, including NP-complete problems such as boolean satisfiability. Similarly, any mathematical equation between numerical quantities can be encoded as a function that evaluates both sides of the equation for the given values of the variables in
x
, and then compares both results for equality. Even several open problems in mathematics, such as finding a cycle in the Collatz
3n+1
sequence, can be represented as a simple puzzle that simply
verifies
the given solution, without
f
revealing how to solve the problem. Instead of collecting human-written puzzles to evaluate LLMs, our key idea here is to design TTG around “reasoning duels” where two LLMs propose puzzles to challenge each other in a match.

3.1
Reasoning duel

In TTG, pairs of LLMs in a pool compete in a series of
duels
, which are conducted programatically as described in Algorithm
1
. In a duel, the two models play a fixed number of rounds where they alternate between the roles of
proposer
and
solver
. Each round can end with one of the two models scoring a point, or in a draw, where scores remain unchanged. Round
i
starts with the proposer first designing a puzzle,
f_{i}
, and providing a solution,
s_{i}
, to its own puzzle. The proposer is given a private scratchpad for reasoning about their puzzle design before committing to a final puzzle and its answer; this allows models to engage in chain-of-thought reasoning when constructing puzzles. The puzzle is accepted only if the proposer’s solution is correct: that is,
f_{i}(s_{i})=\texttt{true}
. If this is not the case, the proposer immediately loses this round, and models change sides. Otherwise, if the proposer’s solution is correct, the solver receives
f_{i}
(but not the proposer’s private reasoning) and is challenged to find some solution,
x_{i}
(which need not be equal to
s_{i}
). We then test whether
f_{i}(x_{i})=\texttt{true}
: if not, the proposer wins this round, and otherwise the round ends in a draw. At the end of all rounds, the model with most points is declared the winner. The match ends in a draw if both models have the same number of points after the last round.

Solutions are verified by executing the puzzle code in a sandboxed Python environment. If evaluation of
f_{i}(s_{i})
or
f_{i}(x_{i})
results in a runtime error (e.g., due to malformed code, type errors, or timeout), the solution is treated as incorrect. A puzzle input can be any Python value that can be constructed in-place,
including strings, numbers and many data structures.

As the duel progresses, both models receive a history of all previous rounds. This history allows models to adapt their strategies dynamically: they can reflect on their own failed puzzle attempts to avoid repeating mistakes, learn from successful solutions to propose harder puzzles in future rounds, and adjust to their opponent’s difficulty level. Crucially, each model only has access to partial information about their opponent’s rounds: they see the puzzle code
f_{i}
, their own solution, and the round’s outcome, but not the opponent’s private reasoning or explanation of their puzzle’s intended solution. For their own rounds, models retain full access to their explanations, enabling them to learn from their own design choices, or failures when designing previous puzzles.

Reasoning duels have several interesting properties from a benchmarking perspective. For instance, duels in principle incentivize models to creatively come up with
novel questions
: if a model only recalls a difficult problem from its training data, its opponent might also have already seen its solution. Second, the fact that models design the puzzles themselves allows them to show capabilities not explicitly asked for during benchmark design. For instance, if a model happens to be able to solve a problem that is open to humans, it can win duels by proposing puzzles around that problem. This cannot happen in a benchmark where humans design questions and also need to provide answers to test model capabilities: the problem’s author cannot be surprised by a model’s answer if they had to know it in the first place to design the benchmark. Access to the history allows a model to incorporate ideas from its opponent, leading to greater diversity of puzzles. Finally, a duel requires a model to have a good self-evaluation of its own capabilities: it should not propose puzzles that it itself cannot solve, since that leads to losing a round. As our analysis in Section 4 shows, we indeed find that even the most capable reasoning models are very often overconfident about what they know.

3.2
Rating models

To obtain a single scalar measure of reasoning ability across multiple models, we run duels between model pairs and fit ratings by applying the Bradley-Terry (BT) model on the set of outcomes, as in prior work on pairwise preference evaluation of LLMs
(
3
;
4
)
. We parameterize the BT equation using the same exponential scoring function used in the Elo rating system, popular in chess and other competitive sports. Under the standard Elo model, the probability that model
A
defeats
B
in a match is

P(A\succ B)=\frac{1}{1+10^{(E_{B}-E_{A})/\sigma}},

(1)

where
E_{A}
and
E_{B}
denote the Elo ratings of models
A
and
B
, respectively, and
\sigma
is a scale parameter (we use
\sigma=400
, the standard choice in chess).

We fit Elo ratings simultaneously on the final outcomes from all pairwise duels. Formally, let
w_{AB}
,
\ell_{AB}
, and
d_{AB}
denote the number of wins, losses, and draws for model
A
against model
B
across all duels. Following the standard convention in Elo-based systems, we treat each draw as half a win for each player, defining effective wins
\tilde{w}_{AB}=w_{AB}+d_{AB}/2
and effective losses
\tilde{\ell}_{AB}=\ell_{AB}+d_{AB}/2
. The negative log-likelihood of the observed outcomes is then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\{E_{m}\})=-\sum_{(A,B)}\Big[

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{AB}\log P(A\succ B)+

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{AB}\log P(B\succ A)\Big]\kern 5.0pt.

Since Elo ratings are only meaningful in relative terms, we fix the rating of an arbitrarily chosen model to
E_{1}=1000
(our implementation uses the model whose name comes first in lexicographical order) and optimize the remaining ratings
\{E_{2},\cdots,E_{n}\}
to minimize
\mathcal{L}
via gradient descent. The resulting ratings admit a simple interpretation: a difference of
\sigma
points corresponds to an expected win probability of approximately 91% for the higher-rated model; each 100-point difference represents a
\approx
64% expected win rate.

3.3
Turn win rates as solver and proposer

Besides ranking models on their overall performance, in principle the ability of LLMs to solve hard puzzles need not be identical to their ability to propose good challenges. Thus, we also separately estimate each model’s ability as a solver and as a proposer. Tos this end, we simply measure the fraction of
rounds
won as a proposer (i.e., when playing as a proposer, how often did the model find a valid puzzle that its opponent could not solve), and fraction of rounds successful as a
solver
(how often did the model successfully solve the opponent’s puzzle, counting a trivial victory of the solver if the opponent failed to propose one). We call these turn-level measures the model’s
Proposer Win Rate
and
Solver Win Rate
. Note that the Solver Win Rate considers the solver successful when it is able to solve the opponent’s puzzle, whereas for computing the duel-level outcome we consider such situation a draw.

4.1
RQ1: Correlation between TTG and expert-authored benchmarks

We now assess whether performance on TTG, a benchmarking framework with no human-designed problems, is indicative of strong performance on two widely used reasoning benchmarks where questions were authored by diverse domain experts. Table
1
shows three performance metrics based on TTG duels, as well as publicly reported accuracies on Humanity’s Last Exam and GPQA Diamond. Prop% and Solv% indicate the turn-level Proposer Win Rate and Solver Win Rate metrics respectively, as described in Section
3.3
.

Despite the complete absence of human-authored problems in TTG, we observe that model rankings are generally consistent across benchmarks. Notably, the top two models on TTG (GPT-5.2 Pro and Gemini 3 Pro) are also the top two on HLE, with GPT-5.2 Pro ranking first across all three benchmarks. At the other end of the spectrum, most models that struggle on TTG also tend to perform poorly on the expert-authored benchmarks: Gemini 2.5 Flash, for instance, ranks in the bottom three on all rankings, with Claude Sonnet 4.5 only slightly above it across all three benchmarks.

However, we find one notable outlier in our evaluation: GPT-5.2 (non-Pro, and with its default “thinking” setting), which ranks last on TTG but third on HLE and second on GPQA Diamond. We hypothesize that the high-scoring GPT-5.2 model on HLE and GPQA were ran not with its default thinking mode, but perhaps in “high thinking”, even though that is not explicitly mentioned in the leaderboard. We observe that, without extended thinking enabled, this model in particular struggles in reasoning about Programming Puzzles both as a proposer and solver — across all duels it never managed to generate a valid puzzle that its opponent could not solve, and even as a solver performs worse than Gemini 2.5 Flash, a much weaker model in HLE and GPQA Diamond. GPT-5.2 Pro, on the other hand, performed best in our evaluation, consistent with its high ranking on other benchmarks.

In summary, we find a positive answer to our first research question:
problem-solving performance in TTG, as measured by solver win rates, is highly indicative of strong performance on expert-authored reasoning benchmarks
. This is striking due to the minimal cost in designing TTG, with no human-authored problems. Furthermore, we find that problem generation is a qualitatively different capability with weaker correlation to previous evaluations, highlighting TTG’s ability to separately assess these reasoning skills.

). As shown in Table
1
, models that rank highly as solvers tend to also rank highly as proposers: for instance, GPT-5.2 Pro leads in both roles, followed by Gemini 3 Pro. This suggests that the reasoning capabilities required to solve difficult puzzles overlap with those needed to construct them.

However, we also observe a notable asymmetry when examining absolute performance levels. Across all rounds, proposers either fail to generate a valid puzzle, or to stump their opponents, in 87.7% of the time. Even the strongest proposer, GPT-5.2 Pro, wins only 43.3% of rounds as a proposer, compared to a perfect 100% as solver. At the other extreme, Gemini 2.5 Flash and GPT-5.2 fail to win a single round as proposer (0%), despite still GPT-5.2 solving 52.6% of valid puzzles proposed by its opponents, and Gemini 2.5 Flash solving 73.8% of them (see Table
3
).

This disparity reveals that
creating good puzzles is substantially harder than solving them
. Models frequently either propose puzzles that their opponents can solve, or fail to solve their own problems. The proposer role thus also represents a highly discriminating test of reasoning capability, with a wider spread in performance across models.

4.3
RQ3: Can proposers increase difficulty using the duel’s history?

In TTG duels, proposers can see the whole history of interactions. This design decision was based on the hypothesis that proposers should adapt the difficulty of their problems as they observe either their own failures in proposing valid puzzles with correct solutions (likely indicating they should be less ambitious) or seeing the opponent’s successes as solvers (pressuring them to attempt to propose harder puzzles). To assess whether such an adaptation is taking place, we use the performance of GPT-5-mini and GPT-5.2 on all puzzles extracted from all duels as a uniform assessment of puzzle difficulty beyond the two competing models. When solving a puzzle, as also happens during duels, models are only given general instructions on format and the puzzle itself, but no other previous context. We evaluate both models’ success rates for puzzles proposed across turns from 1 to 10 in all duels.

Figure
5
shows the results. We find that
proposers steadily increase difficulty across turns
.
Solve rates decrease on average by 2.54% (GPT-5.2) and 1.07% (GPT-5 mini) with each of the ten turns.
For reference, GPT-5-Mini’s solve rate of the tenth puzzle of a game is only 77.4%, while it is 86.8% for the game’s first puzzle. We find that the solve rate decrease is greater for GPT-5.2, which also exhibited overall worse performance as a solver.

4.4
RQ4: Are failing proposers too conservative or too ambitious?

When playing as a proposer, a model can fail to win the turn in one of two ways: (1) its own solution is wrong (giving a point to its opponent), or (2) the opponent’s solution is correct (ending the turn in a draw). In the former case, the proposer was arguably overly confident in its ability to solve the puzzle it proposed. In the latter case, the puzzle was not difficult enough to stump the opponent. How do models behave with respect to these two failure modes as proposers?

Figure
3
shows the number of failures as proposers of each of these two categories and Table
3
shows overall performance as a proposer. Across all models, we see that failing at providing hard-enough puzzles is slightly more common (47.8% of all puzzle attempts) than providing a too hard puzzle with an incorrect sample solution (39.8%).

Data, however, differs a lot by model. GPT-5.2 exhibits extraordinary overconfidence (97.8% penalties for an incorrect solution to its own puzzle). An example for what GPT 5.2’s overconfidence looks like can be found in Appendix
C.11
. In that puzzle, GPT 5.2 hallucinates a number of hexadecimal numbers which are supposed to correspond to the string
hard-to-guess-42
. It then uses a subtly broken XOR scheme to turn them into a SHA-256 digest. Claude Opus 4.5, on the other hand, usually writes puzzles that are too easy – its competitors can solve Opus’ puzzles 74.4% of the time. Gemini 2.5 Flash is an interesting case: it never managed to produce a correct puzzle that its opponent cannot solve. Among its attempts, about half are overambitious (52.5%) and the other half is too easy. Moreover, we see that models become more ambitious with each passing round: penalty rates increase 24.4% in the first round to 41.1% in the last (Table
4
).

Easy puzzles sometimes have just one of those constraints. For instance, Claude Sonnet 4.5 proposes a puzzle that requires the sum of the ASCII representation of a string be a perfect square. The solver model can recognize the pattern and easily solves the puzzle (see Appendix

Most hard puzzles have multiple constraints that must all be satisfied simultaneously. GPT-5.2 Pro successfully proposed a puzzle consisting of 13 different constraints (see Appendix
C.7

The very hardest puzzles are combinations of different ideas and can go beyond lists of constraints. For instance, a puzzle implemented a Brainfuck virtual machine. The solution was to write a Brainfuck program that left the tape in a certain state, which was obscured in the code by a number of cryptographic operations. We also often see models behave naïvely even though they are placed in a competition. For instance, GPT-5-mini’s often writes interesting puzzles but puts through comments, and sometimes even the expected answer, in the function’s documentation. Stronger models were simply less informative in the comments they wrote. deception.

Overall, we find a highly rich diversity of puzzle ideas, including mathematical challenges, logic puzzles, hard computational problems, and often a mixture in the same puzzle. As models progress, the flexible format of programming puzzles ensures that TTG leaves an unbounded room for new models to propose interesting and harder puzzles, and potentially demonstrate their capabilities in interesting ways.

5
Conclusion

We propose a competition-style evaluation of LLM reasoning with no human-authored problems. When comparing ten of the currently strongest LLMs, performance on TTG correlates significantly with other standard reasoning benchmarks that were highly expensive to design.

With 10 models, we were able to run duels between all pairs of models. This is not necessary, however, since the Bradley-Terry ratings we compute can be inferred from relatively sparse subsets of all duels. This will become important as we add new models to TTG and dueling against all previous models becomes infeasible.

Overall, we believe TTG to be a flexible paradigm to measure and understand model reasoning that will stand the challenge of evaluating increasingly more capable models, avoiding saturation by design. Moreover, we hope its design contributes ideas for evaluating other important capabilities besides verifiable reasoning.

Impact Statement

As machine learning and LLMs improve at a fast pace, benchmarks
need to keep up to help us understand limitations and capabilities of modern models. Quick saturation has been a longstanding issue with benchmarks based on sourcing new, ever more challenging human-authored problems, which are also expensive to obtain. Our work presents a benchmark that can in principle stand this challenge, as well as assess other orthogonal skills not present in previous evaluations, such as the ability of models to create problems and correctly estimate their own capabilities. Our evaluation does not intend to replace, but rather complement, existing benchmarks that test models for more specific skills. Although currently TTG correlates with existing reasoning benchmarks, it is possible that they could diverge in the future with more capable models exploiting puzzles that deviate more and more from problems that humans create. Also, evaluating models on TTG involves executing AI-written code, which has to always be done with caution.
