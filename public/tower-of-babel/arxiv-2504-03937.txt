Abstract
This educational article highlights the geometric and algebraic complexities that distinguish tensors from matrices, to supplement coverage in advanced courses on linear algebra, matrix analysis, and tensor decompositions. Using the case of real-valued 2 × 2 × 2 2\times 2\times 2 tensors, we show how tensors violate many well-known properties of matrices: – The rank of a matrix is bounded by its smallest dimension, but a 2 × 2 × 2 2\times 2\times 2 tensor can be rank 3. – Matrices have a single typical rank, but the rank of a generic 2 × 2 × 2 2\times 2\times 2 tensor can be 2 or 3—it has two typical ranks. – Any limit point of a sequence of matrices of rank r r is at most rank r r , but a limit point of a sequence of 2 × 2 × 2 2\times 2\times 2 tensors of rank 2 can be rank 3 (a higher rank). – Matrices always have a best rank- r r approximation, but no rank-3 tensor of size 2 × 2 × 2 2\times 2\times 2 has a best rank-2 approximation. We unify the analysis of the matrix and tensor cases using tools from algebraic geometry and optimization, providing derivations of these surprising facts. To build intuition for the geometry of rank-constrained sets, students and educators can explore the geometry of matrix and tensor ranks via our interactive visualization tool.

Introduction

Tensors have emerged as powerful tools for modeling complex relationships across multiple dimensions.
In this educational article, we aim to highlight key
characteristics that distinguish tensor analysis from matrix analysis, with a focus the geometry of sets of matrices and tensors of particular ranks.
We have created an interactive tool that students and educators can use to visualize and build intuition for the results in this paper.
This article provides material suitable for a numerical linear algebra, data science, or first algebraic geometry course.
It is peppered with helpful examples and exercises designed to deepen understanding.
We assume some knowledge of linear algebra and use examples based on linear algebra to motivate the discussion.

In contrast to matrix rank,
there is no efficient algorithm for computing tensor rank,
which is known to be NP-hard in general
[
hillar_most_2013
]
.
However, the rank of any
2\times 2\times 2
tensor can be determined
[
kruskal_rank_1983
,
kruskal_rank_1989
,
ten_berge_kruskals_1991
,
de_silva_tensor_2008
]
as we discuss in
Section

2
,
and so we focus on
2\times 2\times 2
tensors to give a glimpse of the geometric
and algebraic structure of tensors.
We compare the properties of matrix and tensor rank, and delve into why
tensors have the properties that they do.

We call a rank
typical
if it occurs on a set of positive (Lebesgue) measure.
One interesting property of tensors is that there is not necessarily a single typical rank for a tensor of a given size.
For example, the typical rank of a
2\times 2
matrix is 2; in other words,
every
2\times 2
matrix has rank 2 except for a set of measure zero.
In contrast, the typical rank of a
2\times 2\times 2
tensor is 2 or 3; that is, both ranks 2 and 3 occur on sets of positive measure.
(Additionally, the reader may have observed that we are implying the tensor rank can be larger than the maximum dimension of the tensor.
For tensors of size
2\times 2\times 2
, the maximum possible rank is 3.)

A special polynomial in tensor entries, called the
hyperdeterminant
and discussed in detail in
Section

2
, can be used to determine
if a
2\times 2\times 2
tensor has one of the two typical ranks. A tensor is rank-2 if its
hyperdeterminant is positive and rank-3 if its hyperdeterminant is negative.
The hyperdeterminant is zero on a 7-dimensional hypersurface which
contains
2\times 2\times 2
tensors of all possible ranks (0, 1, 2, and 3).
A three-dimensional slice of the surface is visualized in
Fig.

2
using
https://ghbrown.net/deter/hdet
which contains a button to generate this figure.

Figure 2
:

2\times 2\times 2
tensors showing the 7-dimensional zero set of the hyperdeterminant.

A matrix or tensor is said to have a given
canonical form
if it can
be transformed into that form by an invertible transformation;
see
Section

3
.
There are eight canonical forms for
2\times 2\times 2
tensors, even though there are only four
possible ranks (0, 1, 2, and 3).
In contrast, the number of canonical forms of a matrix is equal to
the number of possible ranks.

An
orbit
of a canonical form is a set of matrices or tensors
that are equivalent under invertible transformations.
The simplicity of the matrix case is evident from the topology and
tesselation of its orbits in the ambient space.
The set of rank-2 matrices of size
2\times 2
is a single orbit of
dimension 4.
Its closure contains the set of rank-1 matrices, which is a single
orbit of dimension 3.
The closure of rank-1 matrices contains the rank-0 (all zero entries) matrix.
This leads to a simple hierarchical structure on the orbits.
The situation for
2\times 2\times 2
tensors is markedly different.
There are two distinct orbits of sets of rank-2 and rank-3 tensors, each of which have dimension 8.
There is another orbit of rank-3 tensors of dimension 7 and multiple distinct
orbits of rank-2 tensors, each of dimension 5.
The closure of rank-3 tensors (the maximum possible rank) does not
contains all tensors of lower rank, so the orbits are no longer
hierarchical.
See
for dimensions of matrix and tensor orbits, and
for visualizations.

These results on algebraic and geometric structure of
2\times 2\times 2
tensors give insight into ill-posed instances of low-rank tensor approximation.
Unlike matrices, for which the best rank-
r
approximation is well-defined
according to the Eckart-Young theorem
[
eckart_approximation_1936
]
,
no rank-3 tensor of size
2\times 2\times 2
has a best rank-2 approximation
[
de_silva_tensor_2008
]
.
In
, we provide a geometric proof for this
surprising property and provide intuitive explanations with
the aid of singular geometry and computational visualizations.
The singularities (e.g., self-intersections) of the zero hyperdeterminant surface in
Fig.

2
play an
important role in the ill-posedness of low-rank approximation.

In
, we overview how the
algebraic and geometric structure of tensors changes when we go from

2\times 2\times 2
tensors to larger scales.
At the same time, we explain how several of the qualitative phenomena observed in this article persist in higher dimensions.
Open research problems for larger-sized tensors are also mentioned.

2
(Hyper)determinant and rank

The algebraic notion of determinant characterizes matrix rank, and an analogous concept plays a role in the characterization of tensor rank for
2\times 2\times 2
tensors.
Before we discuss this, we recall some definitions.

A
semi-algebraic
subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a set of all points satisfying a finite Boolean combination of polynomial equalities and inequalities
[
bochnak_real_1998
, Definition 2.14]
.
This definition may seem limited, but there are a variety of ways to build up complicated semi-algebraic sets.
Indeed, finite combinations of semi-algebraic sets via set unions, intersections, complements, set differences, and Cartesian products also result in semi-algebraic sets.
Furthermore, the image of a semi-algebraic set under any
polynomial map
(that is, a function whose output entries are polynomial functions of the input entries) is again a semi-algebraic set
[
bochnak_real_1998
, Proposition 2.2.7]
, which is a consequence of the Tarski-Seidenberg principle
[
bochnak_real_1998
, Section 1.4]
.

Informally, the
local dimension
of the set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
at a point in
S
refers to number of continuous degrees of freedom in
S
around the point.
The
dimension
of the set
S
is then simply the maximum over all local dimensions.
For instance, a point
has dimension zero, a line has dimension 1, a plane
has dimension 2, the surface of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has dimension 2, the set of
m\times n
matrices has dimension
mn
, and the set of
2\times 2\times 2
tensors has dimension 8.
Formally, since we will be working exclusively with sets defined by polynomials, the dimension refers to the
semi-algebraic dimension
(defined precisely in
[
bochnak_real_1998
, Section 2.8]
).

A set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called (Lebesgue)
measure-zero
if it can be covered by a countable union of
n
-dimensional cubes whose total volume can be made arbitrarily small; for a complete definition see, e.g.,
[
rudin_principles_1976
]
.
For example, a point is a set of measure zero with respect to a line, a curve is a set of measure zero with respect to a plane, and any set of dimension
\leq r-1
is measure zero with respect to a set of dimension
r
.
Additionally, any countable union of measure zero sets is measure zero
[
rudin_principles_1976
]
, including finite unions.
A set that is not measure zero has
positive volume
or
positive measure
.
We say a property of a point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
generic
if it holds everywhere except for a set of measure zero.
For example, being nonzero is a generic property in
\mathbb{R}
.
If a property holds on a set of positive measure, then we say it is
typical
.
For example, being positive is a typical property in
\mathbb{R}
.
Any generic property is typical, by definition.

2.1
Matrix determinant

The
determinant
of a square matrix is a polynomial function of the matrix entries.
The determinant for the
2\times 2
matrix

\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}

is
ad-cb
.
A square matrix is non-singular (and hence full rank) if and only if its determinant is nonzero

[
horn_matrix_2017
]
.
More generally, an
m\times n
matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}
is rank
r
if and only if
the determinants of all square submatrices of size
(r+1)\times(r+1)

are zero and
there exists a square submatrix of size
r\times r
whose determinant is nonzero
[
horn_matrix_2017
]
.

The determinant connects invertibility from linear algebra
to algebraic equations.
It provides an algebraic way to show that full-rank matrices
are generic, as we now explain:
Observe that the set of
2\times 2
matrices
of rank 2 is a semi-algebraic set because it is the set

\set{\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}:ad-cb\neq 0}
.
At any matrix of rank 2, the local dimension is 4, so the set has dimension 4 and positive volume.
This is because the determinant is a continuous function, so at every rank-2 matrix there exists an open ball where the determinant is nonzero.
On the other hand, the set of
2\times 2
matrices
of rank
\leq 1
is the set

\set{\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}:ad-cb=0}.

The dimension of this set is 3 since it must be the case that

a=cb/d
for generic
d\neq 0
.
Hence one of the coordinates is constrained, and there are only three continuous degrees of freedom.
Therefore the set of
2\times 2
matrices of rank
\leq 1

is dimension 3 and measure zero; consequently, the set of
2\times 2
matrices of rank 2 is generic.
More generally, since any rank-deficient matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has a submatrix with determinant zero and
the zero set of any nonzero polynomial on
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has dimension at most
mn-1

[
bochnak_real_1998
, Section 2.8]
, it holds that the set of low-rank matrices has dimension at most
mn-1
.
Thus the property of being full rank is generic.

2.2.1
Rank-2 and rank-3 tensors are typical

From

there is no generic rank; instead, both ranks 2 and 3 are typical for
2\times 2\times 2

In other words, the set of rank-2 tensors and the set of rank-3 tensors both have positive volume in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2\times 2}
, as we argue below.

We begin by showing that the set of rank-2 tensors is positive measure.
Observe that

\Delta\left(\resizebox{}{846504}{$\begin{bmatrix}[rr|rr]4&2&1&1\\
4&3&1&1\end{bmatrix}$}\right)=1,

(5)

and so this tensor is rank 2.
Since the hyperdeterminant is a continuous function of the tensor entries, there exists an open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2\times 2}
centered about this tensor
such that every point in the ball has strictly positive hyperdeterminant.
Such an open ball has positive measure, concluding the argument.
The argument for rank-3 tensors is analogous, based on the observation that

\Delta\left(\resizebox{}{846504}{$\begin{bmatrix}[rr|rr]1&0&0&1\\
0&1&-1&0\end{bmatrix}$}\right)=-4,

(6)

and so this tensor is rank-3 by the characterization (

2.3
Rank-constrained sets are semi-algebraic

We will make heavy use of sets of matrices or tensors of fixed or bounded rank, e.g., the set of
2\times 2\times 2
tensors of rank
\leq 2
.
A fundamental but simple result is that all such sets are semi-algebraic (described by a finite number of polynomial equalities and inequalities), which can be shown using the minimal amount of technology for semi-algebraic sets introduced at the start of
Section

2
.

We previously discussed how matrices are semi-algebraic using the determinant. Another way to
see that matrices of bounded rank are semi-algebraic is by the definition of matrix rank:
\rank(\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}^{\intercal})\leq r
if
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}
and
\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}
have
r
columns.
Since the set

\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}

is semi-algebraic and that the map

(\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}},\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}})\mapsto\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}^{\intercal}

is polynomial, we have that the image (all rank
\leq r
matrices) is a semi-algebraic set
[
bochnak_real_1998
, Proposition 2.2.7]
.
Sets of fixed matrix rank can be realized as set differences of sets with bounded matrix rank, and hence are also semi-algebraic.
The proof for tensors uses an analogous factorization to parameterize bounded rank tensors, but is otherwise identical
[
de_silva_tensor_2008
, Theorem 6.2]
.

3
Orbits and canonical forms

In this section we explain how to use invertible transformations, or more precisely an appropriate group action, to partition the space of matrices or tensors into subsets of constant rank.

3.1
Groups and group actions

We begin by recalling the mathematical concepts of groups, group actions, and their orbits.

A
group

G
is a set along with a binary operation

\ast:G\times G\rightarrow G
such that the following properties hold:

•

associativity:
a\ast b\ast c=(a\ast b)\ast c=a\ast(b\ast c)
for all
a,b,c\in G
,

•

identity: there exists
e\in G
such that

e\ast a=a\ast e=a
for all
a\in G
, and

•

inverse: for all
a\in G
there exists
b\in G

such that
b\ast a=a\ast b=e
.

We are interested in groups where the elements are matrices and the operator
\ast
is matrix multiplication.
Three important matrix groups are the general linear, special linear, and orthogonal groups,
defined respectively as

\displaystyle\operatorname{GL}(n)

\displaystyle=\set{\bm{{\mathbf{{M}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:\det(\bm{{\mathbf{{M}}}})\neq 0},

\displaystyle\operatorname{SL}(n)

\displaystyle=\set{\bm{{\mathbf{{M}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:\det(\bm{{\mathbf{{M}}}})=1},

\displaystyle\operatorname{O}(n)

\displaystyle=\set{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}:\mathbf{M}^{\intercal}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}\bm{{\mathbf{{M}}}}^{\intercal}=\mathbf{I}_{n}}.

In addition to the binary operation
\ast
occurring within the group, we can define an operation of group elements on objects
outside
the group.
If we have a group
G
and a set
X
, we call a map
\cdot:G\times X\rightarrow X
a
group action
if for all
x\in X
and
g,h\in G
it holds

g\cdot(h\cdot x)=(g\ast h)\cdot x

and
e\cdot x=x
.
For example, if
G=\operatorname{O}(n)
and
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can define a group action via matrix-vector multiplication.

Given a group
G
with operator
\ast
and action
\cdot
on
X
,
the
orbit
of
x\in X
under
G
is denoted as
G\cdot x
and
defined to be the set of images of
x
under elements of
G
, i.e.,

G\cdot x=\set{g\cdot x:g\in G}\subset X.

Each point in
X
gives rise to an orbit under a group action. Furthermore, it is straightforward to prove that two orbits are either disjoint or equal.
Therefore, the action of a group
G
induces a partition of
X
into orbits.
To make the concept of an orbit concrete we give a simple example.

Example 3.2
.

Consider the vector

\mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

and the action of
\operatorname{O}(2)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
via matrix-vector multiplication.
Then the orbit is
\operatorname{O}(2)\cdot\mathbf{x}=\set{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert_{2}=1}
as shown in
Fig.

3
. Following the same logic, other orbits under the group action are circles of other radii centered at the origin, as well as the singleton
\{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix}\}
.

Given groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
with respective operators
\ast_{1}
and
\ast_{2}
, one can define the
product group

G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
.
As a set, the product group is the Cartesian product.
The group operation
\ast
for the product group
G
is simply the operator
\ast_{1}
in the first argument and
\ast_{2}
in the second:

(g_{1},g_{2})\ast(h_{1},h_{2})=(g_{1}\ast_{1}h_{1},g_{2}\ast_{2}h_{2})
.
A product group is itself a group.
For example, the orbit of
\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
under
\operatorname{GL}(m)\times\operatorname{GL}(n)
is

\left(\operatorname{GL}(m)\times\operatorname{GL}(n)\right)\cdot\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\equiv\set{\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{V}}}}^{\intercal}:\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}\in\operatorname{GL}(m),\bm{{\mathbf{{V}}}}\in\operatorname{GL}(n)},

where the group action is defined to be two-sided matrix multiplication with a transpose on the right side.

3.2
Orbits and matrix rank

Now we examine the role of orbits in matrix rank.
Without loss of generality (since rank is unaffected by transposition),

m\geq n
.

Proposition 3.4
(Matrix orbits).

If
\bm{{\mathbf{{M}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has rank
r
and
m\geq n
, then

\left(\operatorname{GL}(m)\times\operatorname{GL}(n)\right)\cdot\bm{{\mathbf{{M}}}}=\left(\operatorname{GL}(m)\times\operatorname{GL}(n)\right)\cdot\bm{{\mathbf{{C}}}}_{r}\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{{\mathbf{{C}}}}_{r}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\bm{{\mathbf{{I}}}}_{r}&\bm{{\mathbf{{0}}}}\\
\bm{{\mathbf{{0}}}}&\bm{{\mathbf{{0}}}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.

Noting that
(\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}},\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}})\in\operatorname{GL}(m)\times\operatorname{GL}(n)
implies
\rank(\bm{{\mathbf{{A}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{C}}}}_{r}\bm{{\mathbf{{B}}}}^{\intercal})=r
, the set of rank-
r
matrices is precisely the orbit of the partial identity matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{C}}}}_{r}
under the action of
\operatorname{GL}(m)\times\operatorname{GL}(n)
.

We call the matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{C}}}}_{r}
in
Proposition

3.4
a
canonical form
, as it
represents a simple rank-
r
matrix and any other rank-
r
matrix can be transformed into
\bm{{\mathbf{{C}}}}_{r}
.
This particular choice of orbit representative is simply a convention, and any other rank-
r
matrix would also work.
Since every matrix is in the orbit with canonical form
\bm{{\mathbf{{C}}}}_{r}
for some
r\in\set{0,...,n}
, the space
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is partitioned into
n+1
orbits.

3.3
Orbits and tensor rank

Here we consider the orbits of real
2\times 2\times 2
tensors under the group action of

\operatorname{GL}(2)\times\operatorname{GL}(2)\times\operatorname{GL}(2)
.
We will demonstrate that this partitions the space of
2\times 2\times 2
tensors into a finite number of orbits.

3.3.1
Tensor times matrix (TTM)

The
tensor-times-matrix (TTM)
product,
denoted
\bm{{\mathscr{{T}}}}\times_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}

transforms a tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{T}}}}
by multiplying it in mode-
k
by the
matrix
\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}
. If we say

\bm{{\mathscr{{S}}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{{T}}}}\times_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}
, this is equivalent to saying that their
mode-
k
unfoldings, defined in
Eq.

8
, are related as

\bm{{\mathbf{{S}}}}_{(k)}=\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}\bm{{\mathbf{{T}}}}_{(k)}.

The TTM has the following properties:

\displaystyle(\bm{{\mathscr{{T}}}}\times_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}})\times_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{Y}}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{{T}}}}\times_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{YX}}}},

(10)

\displaystyle(\bm{{\mathscr{{T}}}}\times_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}})\times_{\ell}\bm{{\mathbf{{Y}}}}=(\bm{{\mathscr{{T}}}}\times_{\ell}\bm{{\mathbf{{Y}}}})\times_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{{T}}}}\times_{k}\bm{{\mathbf{{X}}}}\times_{\ell}\bm{{\mathbf{{Y}}}}\quad\text{if}\quad k\neq\ell.

(11)

A TTM product computed in multiple modes is the
multi-TTM
operation, also known as the Tucker product.
For a tensor

\bm{{\mathscr{{T}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n\times p}
and matrices

\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m}
,

\bm{{\mathbf{{V}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}
, and

\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times p}
,
the multi-TTM operation is written as

\bm{{\mathscr{{S}}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{{T}}}}\times_{1}\bm{{\mathbf{{U}}}}\times_{2}\bm{{\mathbf{{V}}}}\times_{3}\bm{{\mathbf{{W}}}}.

The resulting tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{{S}}}}
is of size
q\times r\times s
and its

(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
-entry is defined explicitly by

s_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{p}t_{ijk}u_{\alpha i}v_{\beta j}w_{\gamma k}.

The multi-TTM gives a group action of
\operatorname{GL}(2)\times\operatorname{GL}(2)\times\operatorname{GL}(2)
on
2\times 2\times 2
tensors.
