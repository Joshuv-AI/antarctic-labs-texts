Introduction

In 2020, in Asia and the Pacific, nearly one billion young people aged 10–24 years—60% of the world’s young people1—were experiencing, or soon to be transitioning through, key life experiences (sexual debut, intimate relationships, having children, marriage) and complex physical, neurocognitive and social transitions that occur during adolescence. Across this diverse region, there are substantial challenges related to the provision and access to sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services for girls and young women in ways that are responsive to their needs. This is evidenced by the high prevalence of unintended pregnancy, increasing adolescent fertility rates and the high unmet need for SRH services and modern contraceptives because of limited access to information and education, health services and financial resources1 (see Box 1). In qualitative studies,2 adolescent girls described social and service-based barriers inhibiting their access to modern contraception. Such SRH inequities are compounded by international global shortages of health workers and a tendency to exclude adolescent SRH services from universal health coverage programmes regionally.

We need to think differently about how to elicit a step change in responses to unintended pregnancy among girls and young women in this heterogeneous region. A potential opportunity lies in youth-led SRH self-care and contraceptive innovation. Practiced socially and culturally around the world for millennia, self-care is a way of maintaining SRH for people who have limited or no access to formal healthcare or where access to health services is stigmatised.3 The WHO’s recent publication of self-care guidelines4 has highlighted how self-care interventions might enhance SRH. Recent innovation in contraceptive products and technologies has centred on female-controlled strategies offering women more choice and agency in reproductive decisions and actions.

If situated in and evolving from the realities of adolescents’ lives, self-care and contraceptive innovation could: help overcome social, cultural and gender norms around SRH that prevent unmarried girls and young women from accessing services; respond to the heightened need for privacy and confidentiality that becomes so prominent during adolescence; and support young people’s agency and self-determination through access to modern contraception that aligns more closely to their sexual and social lives. In this commentary, we reflect on opportunities associated with self-care and contraceptive innovation for pregnancy prevention among girls and young women aged 10–24 years in Asia and the Pacific.

YOUTH-CENTRED SELF-CARE

Adolescent SRH self-care comprises activities, capacities and interventions that enable young people to identify and assess their own needs, access and use appropriate health products and technologies, and seek support from health services and professionals as needed.5 SRH self-care interventions include ‘evidence-based, quality drugs, devices, diagnostics and/or digital technologies which can be provided fully or partially outside of formal health services and can be used with or without the support of a health worker’.4 In addition to condom use, self-care recommendations include access to self-administered injectable contraception; over-the-counter oral contraceptive pills and emergency contraceptive pills without a prescription; provision of up to 1 year’s supply of oral contraceptive pills, supported by flexible resupply systems so women can obtain pills easily at the time required; and pregnancy self-testing.4

SRH self-care has focused on increasing access to and use of modern contraceptives among populations in African settings.6 To date, there is a lack of evidence among adolescents in Asia and the Pacific settings. A programme of thoughtful youth-led research7 across Asia and the Pacific—that centres girls’ and young women’s views and lived experiences of self-care for pregnancy prevention—would help inform new solutions to early and unintended pregnancy. Item 1 in Box 2 would help unpack how self-care for pregnancy prevention might impact positively the lives of girls and young women vulnerable to unintended pregnancy.

Throughout this exploration, it would be important to recognise two issues: while it is tempting to focus on new digital and physical self-care solutions, it is the heterogeneous SRH needs of girls and young women that should determine self-care innovation8; self-care is complementary to, not a replacement for, sustainable, high-quality adolescent-responsive health service provision.3 8

Youth-led contraceptive innovation

Effective SRH self-care requires innovation to expand the range of contraceptive products available to young people. Examples of products at various stages of development include self-injectable contraception,6 the Caya diaphragm9 and a pericoital contraceptive pill.10 Other ideas in the pipeline include biodegradable contraceptive implants, longer acting injectable methods and user-administered micro-needle (or micro-array) patches.11

For contraceptive innovation to be relevant to adolescent girls and young women—as opposed to adult women—we will need to be attentive to the varied contraceptive requirements that exist for girls and young women in different societal circumstances. Without collaborating with young people from diverse parts of the world as expert partners throughout key stages of contraceptive innovation, we will not know whether progress aligns with what girls and young women want or need to navigate their sexual lives. Items 2–3 in Box 2 would help guide how contraceptive innovation could be more suited to the sexual lives of girls and young women.

Securing sustainable financing for the co-design and market assessment of contraceptive products and technologies underscores the importance of investing in evidence-based strategies that prioritise the needs and preferences of young people. Public and private sector donors, philanthropic organisations and development agencies will play a vital role in supporting these initiatives, ensuring that resources are allocated effectively and equitably to advance adolescent SRH outcomes globally.

Effective SRH self-care also requires innovation in the way that new and existing contraceptive products are delivered to young people living in urban, peri-urban and rural settings across Asia and the Pacific. WHO self-care guidelines4 support peer-to-peer action, mentoring and counselling, as well as shifting of tasks traditionally undertaken by health workers to lay people. This creates opportunities to explore the potential for young people with lived experience of pregnancy—often excluded from education, training and livelihood opportunities due to these experiences—to become trained and paid to work as trusted youth suppliers of contraceptives and SRH information. Early ideas around community-led contraceptive injectable and implant provision have been explored with adults in Kenya.12 Item 4 in Box 2 would enable the development of contraceptive care models led by girls and young women.

Enabling environments

Supportive social, legal, policy and health system environments are essential for self-care interventions.4 Prior analyses document complex SRH policy and legal frameworks for young people across Asia and the Pacific.1 For example, the legal age of consent to medical treatment (including SRH services) in most countries is 18. Legislation concerning the age of consent to sex varies in the region (between 12 and 19 years) and differs for boys and girls within the same country in some settings. Some countries have a lower age of consent or allow exemptions for consensual sex between young people who are close in age. In five countries, premarital sex is prohibited and criminalised. Unintended pregnancies occur in marriages involving adolescents; laws in seven countries still allow the marriage of girls less than 18 years, and six countries have exemptions (usually on religious or cultural grounds) that allow the marriage of girls before the age of 18. In combination, these contradictions fail to recognise the agency and lived experience of young people with respect to their SRH and sexuality, creating substantial barriers to accessing SRH information and services, with disproportionate impacts on girls.1

New developments in contraceptive self-care and innovation can occur with careful efforts to eliminate contradictions across SRH laws and policies, especially regarding access to services based on age. The aim is to work towards evidence-informed legal and policy frameworks that reflect the rights, agency and evolving capacities of adolescents and young people to make independent decisions regarding their own health13 and align with the realities of young people’s lives. The UN Committee on the Rights of the Child states, ‘for preventive or time-sensitive SRH services, states should consider a legal presumption that adolescents are competent to seek and access these services’.13 Top-down national legislative and regulatory support would be required to permit the use of the full range of contraceptive products and technologies in any national setting and access to these via health facilities and pharmacies, and other digital, private and non-governmental providers. Further policy support would be gained by replicating efforts being undertaken in Kenya, Nigeria, Senegal and Uganda,14 to establish national self-care guidelines and networks, and accompanying legislative and policy frameworks to support implementation. While working in support of change to national policies and laws, advocacy would also be needed throughout community settings across the region to build local support for young people’s access to contraceptive care services at an earlier age, in time for their first sexual experiences, rather than just child spacing processes in marriage.

Conclusion - For young people, by young people

We should not try to achieve these changes without partnering with young people as co-leaders of change,7 especially those with lived experience of unintended pregnancy. There is value in replicating strategies in other health areas—HIV, mental health, TB—where adults with lived experience of health challenges have been leading solutions to these problems. We need to do the same here—enabling, supporting and working side by side with girls and young women to lead change through research and service delivery, and monitoring, evaluation and advocacy (see Box 2, item 5) to tackle the persistent challenge of early and unintended pregnancy.
