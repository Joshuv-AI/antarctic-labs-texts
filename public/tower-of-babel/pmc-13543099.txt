Problematic social media use is associated with altered directed exploration under uncertainty
Authors: Xinqi Zhou, Xiaolong Liu, Haoran Dou, Haichao Zhao, Yong Liu, Xi Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProblematic social media use (PSMU) has been linked to altered decision-making, yet the specific mechanisms governing how individuals with PSMU navigate uncertainty-driven exploratory choices remain poorly understood. This study investigated whether PSMU is associated with distinct patterns of directed exploration (strategic information-seeking) and random exploration (decision noise).Methods148 young adults (55 PSMU, 93 controls) completed a two-armed bandit task that independently manipulates relative and total uncertainty. A validated hybrid computational model was fitted to choices to estimate individual parameters for directed and random exploration. Mediation analysis tested whether intolerance of uncertainty (IU) mediated the relationship between PSMU and exploration strategies.ResultsThe PSMU group exhibited significantly stronger directed exploration than controls (p = 0.029, d = 0.33), indicating an increased drive to actively seek information to resolve uncertainty. No group differences were found in random exploration or value-based choice. Mediation analysis revealed a significant negative indirect effect of IU on the PSMU-directed exploration relationship (indirect effect = −0.038, 95% CI [−0.078, −0.003]), suggesting that while PSMU broadly promotes information-driven exploration, IU may paradoxically dampen this tendency.ConclusionsPSMU is characterized by heightened directed exploration in response to explicit uncertainty signals. The opposing influences of PSMU and IU highlight the nuanced role of uncertainty sensitivity in shaping decision-making. Computational phenotyping of exploration strategies offers a promising avenue for understanding the cognitive mechanisms underlying problematic social media engagement.

Introduction

The contemporary digital environment presents individuals with an unprecedented stream of choices under uncertainty. Social media platforms, with their algorithmically curated feeds, infinite scroll features, and unpredictable social feedback, exemplify this environment (Scharlach & Hallinan, 2023; Meshi et al., 2015). For most users, navigating these platforms involves a constant, often unconscious, resolution of the explore-exploit dilemma. This means deciding when to continue engaging with known, rewarding content and when to seek out novel information, profiles, or interactions (Scharlach & Hallinan, 2023). Understanding how this fundamental decision-making process operates and potentially breaks down in individuals who develop problematic patterns of social media use is important for advancing both theoretical models of behavioral addictions and practical intervention strategies.

Problematic social media use (PSMU) is a widespread hallmark of the digital age, with recent meta-analyses placing its global prevalence among young adults between 5% and 15%, depending on assessment tools and cultural settings (Boer et al., 2020; Cheng et al., 2021). This high prevalence establishes PSMU as a significant public health priority and calls for a rigorous understanding of its cognitive foundations. Crucially, unlike substance use disorders, where pharmacological properties confound cognitive measurements, PSMU permits a relatively pure investigation of the explore-exploit trade-off, precisely because it arises from behavioral rather than chemical dependencies. PSMU represents a pattern of excessive, compulsive engagement with social media that leads to functional impairment, has been increasingly examined through the lens of decision-making deficits. Empirical evidence has begun to link PSMU to decision-making alterations. For instance, Meshi et al. (2021) demonstrated that individuals with higher PSMU severity showed altered evaluation of both risk and ambiguity during decision-making, suggesting that PSMU is associated with a general sensitivity to uncertain outcomes. Similarly, Pan et al. (2025) found that greater time spent on social networking sites was associated with more impulsive decision-making patterns, as measured by delay discounting tasks. These findings indicate that PSMU-related decision-making deficits extend beyond social contexts to general decision processes. Theoretical models such as the I-PACE (Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution) framework posit that addictive behaviors emerge from dynamic interactions among predisposing personality traits, affective and cognitive responses to stimuli, and executive control processes (Brand et al., 2025). Within this framework, a core driver of PSMU is the individual's response to uncertainty. Social media platforms leverage several design features that generate and exploit uncertainty to sustain user engagement. Algorithmically curated feeds create informational uncertainty by continuously presenting novel, unpredictable content (Nadkarni & Hofmann, 2012; Oberst et al., 2017). The variable ratio reinforcement schedule of social rewards, such as likes, comments, and shares, generates uncertainty about outcomes, which drives dopamine release and reinforces checking behavior (Lindstrom et al., 2021). Additionally, the continuous availability of platforms enables users to act on uncertainty-driven impulses in real-time, facilitating the development of habitual checking patterns. These features collectively create a potent environment for fostering problematic use, as individuals are repeatedly faced with uncertainty about whether new rewarding content exists, whether others have engaged with their posts, and whether they might be missing out on socially rewarding experiences. This fear of missing out (FoMO) has been identified as a key driver of PSMU, motivating compulsive checking and information-seeking behavior (Elhai et al., 2025; Fioravanti et al., 2021). Yet, the specific manner in which individuals with PSMU resolve this uncertainty through exploration has not been directly examined using formal computational models. Computational models formalize the latent decision processes that give rise to those choices, thereby enabling a mechanistic interpretation of observed behavior. Critically, this approach yields individualized parameter estimates that index meaningful variations in decision-making strategies across participants. It allows us to determine whether PSMU-related alterations reflect strategic information-seeking, decision noise, or some combination of these processes.

The exploration-exploitation trade-off is a well-established framework in cognitive neuroscience and computational psychiatry for studying adaptive and maladaptive decision-making (Daw et al., 2006; Gershman, 2018). This framework has been increasingly applied to understand decision-making in psychiatric disorders, including substance use disorders and behavioral addictions (for a comprehensive review, see Jami et al., 2025). For instance, Morris et al. (2016) demonstrated that individuals with alcohol dependence exhibited altered exploration-exploitation trade-offs characterized by avoidance of uncertainty, while other studies have linked addiction severity to biases in exploratory choice patterns (Jami et al., 2025). However, despite the growing interest in decision-making alterations in addictive disorders, the specific computational mechanisms, specifically the dissociation between directed and random exploration, have not been systematically examined in the context of PSMU. This represents a significant gap, as distinguishing between strategic information-seeking and stochastic decision noise is critical for understanding the precise cognitive alterations that characterize PSMU and for developing targeted interventions. Critically, this framework has recently been refined to distinguish between two computationally and behaviorally dissociable forms of exploration, i.e. directed exploration and random exploration (Gershman, 2018; Wilson et al., 2014). Directed exploration is a strategic, goal-directed behavior in which an individual actively biases their choices toward options with higher uncertainty because the information gained can improve future decisions. It is often formalized as an “uncertainty bonus” added to an option's subjective value. Random exploration, by contrast, reflects non-strategic behavioral variability or decision noise that leads to exploration by chance rather than by intent (Fan et al., 2023; Gershman, 2019). This methodological advance allows researchers to move beyond simply asking whether individuals explore to precisely characterizing how they explore.

A key individual difference construct implicated in PSMU is intolerance of uncertainty (IU), defined as a dispositional tendency to perceive uncertain or ambiguous situations as threatening and to respond with heightened anxiety and avoidance (Carleton et al., 2007). The relationship between IU and PSMU is theoretically complex. On one hand, individuals high in IU may engage in excessive social media checking as a maladaptive strategy to reduce uncertainty and obtain reassuring information, thereby increasing the risk of PSMU (Rozgonjuk et al., 2019). On the other hand, the aversive experience of uncertainty itself may paradoxically lead to avoidance of situations that involve informational ambiguity, even when exploration could be beneficial (Bauer et al., 2020; Carleton et al., 2007; Grupe & Nitschke, 2013). This avoidance pattern has been well-documented in individuals high in IU, who tend to interpret uncertain situations as threatening and engage in behaviors aimed at reducing exposure to uncertainty (Carleton, 2016). This tension suggests that IU may exert both approach and avoidance influences on exploration depending on the motivational context. Critically, existing evidence offers important but fragmented clues. Fan et al. (2023) demonstrated that trait anxiety, a construct closely associated with IU, reduces directed exploration in laboratory bandit tasks. Yet Chou et al. (2024) subsequently showed that this anxiety-exploration relationship is highly context-sensitive, differing between directed and random exploration and shifting with task design. Meanwhile, Wyatt et al. (2024) established that prefrontal and frontostriatal circuits, neural regions also linked to PSMU, serve as the functional backbone of directed exploration. These reviews jointly underscore that a meaningful understanding of PSMU requires both a dissociation of exploration modes and a careful consideration of affective moderators such as IU. However, this integrative perspective has not yet been translated into empirical investigation; the combined influence of PSMU, IU, and computationally specified exploration parameters remains entirely unexplored, leaving the field without a unified account of how these factors interact.

First, do individuals with PSMU exhibit altered directed or random exploration compared to non-PSMU controls? Drawing on the literature linking PSMU to heightened fear of missing out and compulsive information-seeking (Elhai et al., 2025; Fioravanti et al., 2021), we hypothesized that the PSMU group would show elevated directed exploration. While compulsive checking in daily life may be driven by both habitual and goal-directed mechanisms, our computational task specifically isolates the goal-directed component of exploration by modeling choices as a function of computed uncertainty and expected value. If PSMU involves a fundamental alteration in how uncertainty is valued and resolved, we would expect this to manifest as a strategic bias toward uncertain options—even in an abstract, non-social context. The directed exploration parameter captures precisely this strategic information-seeking component, distinguishing it from habitual responses, which would instead manifest as random exploration or value-independent choice. Thus, finding heightened directed exploration in PSMU would suggest that the tendency to seek information under uncertainty is a domain-general feature of the disorder, rather than merely a context-specific habit. In contrast, we did not have strong predictions regarding random exploration, as this parameter has been less directly tied to motivational accounts of PSMU and may instead reflect domain-general cognitive noise or impulsivity.

Second, does IU mediate the relationship between PSMU and exploration strategies? Based on the I-PACE framework and prior findings that IU is elevated in PSMU (Sang et al., 2024), we tested a mediation model in which PSMU status predicts exploration parameters through IU. Importantly, we anticipated a nuanced pattern. While PSMU status would be positively associated with directed exploration overall, showing a positive total effect, the unique contribution of IU might be in the opposite direction. Specifically, higher IU might predict less directed exploration when controlling for PSMU. Such a pattern, known as a statistical suppression effect, would suggest that two opposing mechanisms are simultaneously at play, namely a general PSMU-linked drive toward information-seeking and a specific IU-linked aversive response to uncertainty that dampens strategic exploration. This hypothesis aligns with evidence that IU is associated with avoidance behaviors and reduced tolerance for the informational ambiguity inherent in exploration (Carleton et al., 2007).

Third, we aimed to examine whether any observed effects are specific to directed versus random exploration. Given that directed exploration reflects a cognitively demanding, strategic process, whereas random exploration reflects behavioral variability, differential associations with PSMU and IU would provide mechanistic specificity, pinning the locus of any dysfunction to strategic information-seeking rather than to a generalized increase in decision noise.

The present study aims to address this gap by investigating how PSMU severity relates to directed and random exploration under uncertainty. We recruited a sample of young adults and classified them into a PSMU group and a non-PSMU control group. By integrating a well-validated behavioral paradigm with computational modeling and psychometric assessment, this study offers a novel contribution to the literature on PSMU. It moves beyond self-report measures of exploratory tendencies to characterize the latent decision processes that may underlie maladaptive social media engagement. Understanding whether and how PSMU is associated with distinct forms of exploration can inform mechanistic theories of problematic digital behavior and may ultimately guide the development of targeted interventions that address specific cognitive biases rather than treating PSMU as a monolithic problematic behavior.

Method

Participants and procedure

Participants were recruited from university and online student communities. The study was conducted in a laboratory setting where participants completed the behavioral task on a computer. Sample size was determined based on previous computational modeling studies using the two-armed bandit task, which typically recruit between 100 and 200 participants to obtain stable parameter estimates in hierarchical Bayesian models (Gershman, 2018; Wilson et al., 2014). We therefore aimed to recruit approximately 150 participants after exclusions. All participants provided written informed consent before participating. The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee. Exclusion criteria were: (1) age < 18 years, (2) failure to meet the task accuracy criterion (choosing the objectively more rewarding option in <60% of trials, following Fan et al., 2023, to ensure effective learning), or (3) incomplete questionnaires. The accuracy criterion was applied to ensure that participants had sufficiently learned the task structure and were not responding randomly, as computational model parameters would be uninterpretable in participants who failed to demonstrate basic learning. Exclusion steps were applied sequentially. Participants were first excluded based on age, then based on task accuracy, and finally based on incomplete questionnaires. After exclusions (n = 13), the final sample comprised 148 participants. Based on the BSMAS cut-off, 55 participants were classified into the PSMU group and 93 into the non-PSMU group. This screening-based cut-off, following established guidelines (Andreassen et al., 2012; Griffiths, 2009), was defined as endorsing at least four of the six items at a frequency of often or very often, reflecting the six core components of addiction (i.e., salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse). Participants completed the Chinese versions of BSMAS and the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS) (Yam et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2017), and provided demographic information including time spent on social media daily, assessed using a single categorical item adapted from prior research.

The two-armed bandit task

The task is designed to disentangle the influences of relative and total uncertainty on exploration (Fan et al., 2023; Gershman, 2019). Participants completed 30 blocks of 10 trials each and were instructed to accumulate as many points as possible. In each block, participants chose between two options labeled either “Stable” (S) or “Fluctuating” (F). This resulted in four block types (FS, SF, FF, SS), which remained constant within a block. The labels were explicitly instructed, with a stable option's mean reward fixed within a block while a fluctuating option's mean changed unpredictably through a Gaussian random walk. This means the fluctuating option is always more uncertain than the stable option. The key manipulation is that the comparison between conditions FS and SF isolates sensitivity to relative uncertainty (which option is more uncertain), while the comparison between FF and SS isolates sensitivity to total uncertainty (how much overall uncertainty is present, Fig. S1). On each trial, participants saw the two options, made a choice, and received a reward (points drawn from a Gaussian distribution around the chosen option's current mean). A detailed description of the Bayesian belief-updating model (Kalman filter) and the computational choice model is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Computational modeling of choice behavior

Choices were analyzed using a validated hybrid model that combines directed and random exploration (Fan et al., 2023). The probability of choosing the left option was modeled via a probit regression:Pat=1W=Φw1Vt+w2RUt+w3Vt/TUt

Here, Vt is the estimated value difference between left and right options (from the Kalman filter), RUt is relative uncertainty (left uncertainty minus right uncertainty), and TUt is total uncertainty (root sum square of both options' uncertainties). The parameter w2 captures directed exploration: a positive w2 means the participant adds a bonus to an option's value proportional to its relative uncertainty, biasing choice toward the more uncertain option. The parameter w3 captures random exploration: a positive w3 indicates that as the environment becomes more uncertain overall, choices become more random (less dependent on value Vt). This model was fitted using hierarchical Bayesian estimation in R with the brms package (Bürkner, 2017), yielding posterior parameter estimates for each participant.

Statistical analyses

Group differences in demographics and model parameters (w1 for value, w2 for directed exploration, w3 for random exploration) were assessed using Welch's two-sample t-tests, with effect sizes reported as Cohen's d. To test the mediating role of IU, an exploratory mediation model based on group differences was specified with PSMU group (0 = non-PSMU, 1 = PSMU) as the predictor, IUS total score as the mediator, and the individual participant's w2 parameter as the outcome. Unstandardized coefficients are reported, and the significance of the indirect effect was assessed using 95% confidence intervals (CIs) based on bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5000 resamples (Preacher & Hayes, 2008). Post-hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted.

Conditional effects and the hybrid model

Analysis of psychometric curves (see Supplementary Fig. S3) replicated core task effects. Participants were sensitive to relative uncertainty, as evidenced by an intercept shift between conditions FS and SF. They exhibited random exploration under high total uncertainty, as shown by a steeper value-dependence slope for condition FF compared to SS.

Participants in the analysis sample performed well, choosing the most rewarding option 81.19% (SE: 0.3%) of the time on average (Fig. S2). There was a significant intercept shift between the psychometric curves for condition FS and SF (median = −0.09, 95% CI = [−0.17, −0.01]), reflecting the participants' sensitivity to uncertainty. The signed relative uncertainty showed that people directed their exploration avoids the uncertain option (Fig. S3a). Additionally, the intercept of FS was significantly negative (median = −0.07, 95% CI = [−0.14–0.01], see Table S1 for the full regression result table), indicating that people formed a bias toward choosing option 2 when option 1's relative uncertainty was high. The intercept of SF was not significantly different from zero (median = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.04 0.07]). There was no significant difference in intercept between conditions FF and SS (median = −0.002, 95% CI = [−0.09 0.08]), suggesting that people did not adjust directed exploration according to total uncertainty. Evidence for random exploration was also found. There was a significant steeper curve for FF than SS (median = −0.16, 95% CI = [−0.30, −0.03], Fig. S3). This suggests that people chose more randomly when facing high total uncertainty, that is, random exploration is sensitive to total uncertainty. There was no significant difference in slope between SF and FS (median = 0.02, 95% CI = [−0.10 0.14]), corroborating that random exploration is not sensitive to relative uncertainty.

The model with three regressors (‘hybrid model’) outperformed model candidates that only allowed for one exploration strategy, other nested model candidates, and reinforcement learning models with constant learning rates (Table S2). The hybrid model demonstrated good parameter recovery capability (correlation between fitted and simulated parameters: all rs > 0.95, all ps < 0.001). Participants were sensitive to V, RU, and V/TU, with fixed effect coefficients were all significant from zero (V: Estimate = 0.90, 95% CI = [0.83 0.97]; RU: Estimate = −0.14, 95% CI = [−0.19–0.09]; V/TU: Estimate = 0.07, 95% CI = [0 0.14]; Table S3). Together, our data support previous studies that people use a hybrid of directed and random exploration strategies, which are respectively sensitive to relative and total uncertainty induced by volatility (Fan et al., 2023; Gershman, 2018, Gershman, 2019).

Group differences in exploration strategies

To address our first research question regarding group differences in exploration strategies, we compared computational parameters between PSMU and non-PSMU groups. The PSMU group exhibited a significantly higher directed exploration parameter (w2) than the non-PSMU group (t = 2.20, p = 0.029, d = 0.33; see Table 2). This indicates that the PSMU group was more strategically biased toward choosing the more uncertain option. No significant group differences were observed for the random exploration parameter (w3) or value-guided choice (w1), suggesting the effect is specific to directed exploration. Sensitivity analysis indicated that the minimal detectable effect size for 80% power with this sample is d = 0.41. It indicates that while our sample size is consistent with prevailing practices in computational modeling studies, it is a little underpowered. The mediating role of intolerance of uncertainty

To test our remaining research questions, mediation analysis was conducted to test whether IUS mediated the relationship between PSMU status and directed exploration (w2). The results are summarized in Table 3. The total effect of PSMU on w2 was positive and significant (c = 0.066, p = 0.028). The direct effect of PSMU on w2, controlling for IUS, became larger (c’ = 0.104, p = 0.003). Critically, a significant negative indirect effect through IUS was observed (indirect effect = −0.038, 95% CI [−0.078, −0.003]). This constitutes a suppressor effect, as PSMU and IUS are positively correlated on the a path, but IUS is negatively associated with directed exploration on the b path. Discussion

This study provides the computational characterization of exploratory decision-making in individuals with problematic social media use. By employing a two-armed bandit task that independently manipulates relative and total uncertainty and by fitting a validated hybrid model of choice behavior, we were able to dissociate directed and random exploration strategies and examine their unique relationships with PSMU severity and intolerance of uncertainty. Three principal findings emerged from this investigation.

First, individuals classified with PSMU exhibited significantly greater directed exploration compared to non-PSMU controls. Specifically, the PSMU group showed a stronger positive weight on relative uncertainty in the computational choice model, indicating a heightened strategic bias to select the more uncertain of two options in order to gather information. This effect was specific to directed exploration, and no group differences were observed in random exploration or in the weight placed on expected value. These findings suggest that PSMU is not characterized by a global increase in behavioral variability or decision noise but rather by a specific amplification of goal-directed information-seeking behavior. In the context of the explore-exploit dilemma, individuals with PSMU appear to adopt a more “exploration-heavy” policy, prioritizing the resolution of uncertainty over the exploitation of known rewards. Several social media-specific characteristics may contribute to this heightened directed exploration. Algorithmic personalization creates an environment of informational uncertainty by continuously presenting novel content tailored to user preferences, essentially encouraging users to explore rather than exploit known content. Variable social rewards, in which the timing and magnitude of social feedback (likes, comments, shares) are unpredictable, create a powerful uncertainty signal that may drive strategic exploration (Lindstrom et al., 2021). The infinite scroll feature further promotes exploratory behavior by removing natural stopping cues, making it easier to continue seeking new information. Importantly, the distinction between active use (e.g., posting, commenting) and passive use (e.g., browsing, scrolling) may moderate these effects, with passive use being more strongly associated with uncertainty-driven checking behaviors (Verduyn et al., 2015). These platform characteristics may create a reinforcing cycle: individuals with a pre-existing tendency for directed exploration are drawn to the uncertainty-rich environment of social media, and the platform features in turn amplify and sustain this exploratory tendency, contributing to problematic use.

This pattern is consistent with descriptions in the literature that emphasize the role of compulsive information-seeking and fear of missing out (FoMO) in driving PSMU (Elhai et al., 2025; Fioravanti et al., 2021). The constant need to check social media feeds, refresh notifications, and monitor social updates can be conceptualized as a form of directed exploration in a socially and informationally uncertain environment. Our findings provide a formal, computational instantiation of this idea, suggesting that the same cognitive machinery that supports adaptive exploration in abstract laboratory tasks may, when amplified or dysregulated, contribute to maladaptive patterns of social media use. Importantly, our task used non-social stimuli and abstract rewards, suggesting that the observed tendency reflects a domain-general alteration in decision processes rather than a response specific to social content. This raises the possibility that heightened directed exploration constitutes a cognitive marker or vulnerability factor for PSMU that generalizes across contexts.

The second key finding concerns the complex role of intolerance of uncertainty. Exploratory mediation analysis revealed a significant negative indirect effect of IU on the relationship between PSMU and directed exploration. This suppressor effect indicates that, while PSMU status was positively associated with directed exploration overall, the specific pathway through IU exerted an opposing influence, such that higher levels of IU predicted lower directed exploration. In other words, the unique contribution of IU was to dampen, rather than promote, strategic information-seeking. This finding appears paradoxical at first glance but aligns with a nuanced understanding of IU as a multifaceted construct. Although IU involves a desire for predictability and a motivation to reduce uncertainty, the affective experience of uncertainty as aversive and threatening may simultaneously trigger avoidance of situations where uncertainty is explicitly salient (Carleton et al., 2007). In the bandit task, choosing the more uncertain option requires tolerating the anxiety of not knowing the outcome. For individuals high in IU, this anxiety may outweigh the informational benefit of exploration, leading to a preference for the safer, known option.

This dissociation between PSMU-related approach toward uncertainty and IU-related avoidance of uncertainty has significant theoretical implications. It suggests that the drive to explore in PSMU is not primarily fueled by IU itself, which would predict a positive mediation pathway, but by other, co-occurring factors. These might include heightened reward sensitivity, greater FoMO, or altered dopaminergic signaling associated with compulsive behavior (Lindstrom et al., 2021; Meshi et al., 2015). IU, by contrast, may function as a brake on exploration, one that is overcome or counterbalanced by these other forces in the context of PSMU. This interpretation is consistent with the I-PACE model (Brand et al., 2025), which emphasizes that addictive behaviors arise from the interaction of multiple predisposing, affective, and executive factors rather than from a single trait. Our findings can be understood within the I-PACE framework by mapping computational parameters onto specific components of the model. The heightened directed exploration observed in PSMU may reflect alterations in the cognitive component of the model, specifically in how uncertainty-related information is processed and weighted during decision-making. The positive association between PSMU and directed exploration suggests that in individuals with PSMU, uncertainty signals may be more salient or more positively valenced, leading to a greater tendency to seek information even when this comes at a cost to immediate reward. Importantly, our computational approach extends the I-PACE model by providing formal, quantifiable measures of these latent cognitive processes. Future research that simultaneously measures IU, FoMO, reward sensitivity, and computational parameters of exploration will be critical for disentangling these influences and mapping them onto specific neural systems.

The third finding is the specificity of the observed effects to directed, as opposed to random, exploration. This dissociation provides mechanistic insight into the nature of the decision-making alterations associated with PSMU. Directed exploration is a cognitively demanding, prefrontal-dependent strategy that requires individuals to track uncertainty, compute its value, and integrate it with reward expectations (Gershman, 2018; Wilson et al., 2014). This dissociation aligns with the neural evidence reviewed by Wyatt et al. (2024), which implicates prefrontal-dependent processes in directed exploration, whereas random exploration may reflect lower-level processes such as decision noise or exploration triggered by basal ganglia circuits. The specificity of our findings to directed exploration is also consistent with the nuanced pattern described by Chou et al. (2024), who found that anxiety, a construct related to IU, primarily affects directed rather than random exploration. Our results suggest that PSMU is associated with alterations in the higher-order, strategic aspects of exploration rather than with a general increase in behavioral stochasticity. This finding aligns with neuroimaging evidence implicating prefrontal control systems and frontostriatal circuits in both PSMU and decision-making under uncertainty (Lin et al., 2025; Schmitgen et al., 2025). Our findings suggest several clinical implications, with the specificity to directed exploration favoring strategic interventions over broad impulsivity-focused ones, including metacognitive and cognitive-behavioral approaches targeting uncertainty-driven checking urges, while psychoeducation on platform design may foster mindful use, prevention could address cognitive biases in uncertainty-driven exploration especially for high-IU individuals, and computational parameter estimates may serve as sensitive outcome measures in intervention trials.

However, it is important to note that evidence regarding executive functions and cognitive control in PSMU is mixed. While some studies have reported impairments in inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility in individuals with PSMU (e.g., He et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2024), others have found no significant differences or even enhanced performance on certain cognitive tasks (e.g., Meshi et al., 2021). This heterogeneity suggests that PSMU may not be characterized by a global deficit in executive function, but rather by more specific alterations in how cognitive resources are deployed. Our finding of heightened directed exploration, which requires intact prefrontal function to track uncertainty and strategically bias choices, is consistent with the view that PSMU involves alterations in information-seeking priorities rather than fundamental executive dysfunction. Indeed, the ability to engage in directed exploration itself requires intact cognitive control; the issue in PSMU may be that this capacity is directed toward maladaptive goals (e.g., excessive social media checking) rather than being fundamentally impaired. This interpretation is consistent with the I-PACE framework's distinction between impaired and intact executive functions.

An additional consideration is the relatively high social media use in the control group, with 48% of controls reporting more than 3 h of daily use. This suggests that our control group represents a non-problematic but still heavy-use population, which is consistent with the high prevalence of social media use among young adults. This may actually strengthen our findings, as we observed significant differences in computational parameters despite relatively high baseline use in the controls. However, it also raises the possibility that our group differences might be attenuated compared to what would be observed in a more conservative control group (e.g., low-frequency users). Future studies should consider including a broader range of use levels to examine whether the relationship between use frequency and exploration parameters is linear or threshold-dependent.

Several limitations of the present study warrant discussion. First, the cross-sectional, quasi-experimental design precludes causal inference. The mediation model tested here, in which PSMU status predicts directed exploration through IU, is statistically equivalent to alternative models with different causal orderings. Given that IU is often conceptualized as a stable, trait-like vulnerability factor, it is plausible that high IU predates and contributes to the development of PSMU, rather than functioning as a mediator of PSMU's effects. Longitudinal studies with multiple waves of assessment are needed to determine the temporal precedence of these variables and to test for reciprocal influences over time. Second, our sample consisted of Chinese university students, and the generalizability of these findings to other cultural contexts, age groups, and clinical populations remains to be established. Cultural variation in uncertainty avoidance, social media norms, and the expression of problematic use could moderate the observed relationships. Third, while the BSMAS is a widely used screening tool, the cut-off employed for group classification yields a relatively high prevalence of PSMU compared to population estimates. Replication using more conservative diagnostic criteria or continuous dimensional analyses would strengthen confidence in these results. Future research should consider using multiple assessment approaches, including collateral reports, behavioral tracking, and functional impairment measures, to provide a more comprehensive characterization of PSMU. Additionally, the ongoing debate about the diagnostic status of PSMU and other behavioral addictions (Perales et al., 2024) highlights the need for continued refinement of assessment instruments and operational definitions. Fourth, the bandit task used abstract monetary rewards and non-social uncertainty, which, while enabling rigorous experimental control, may not fully capture the motivational salience of social media stimuli. Future studies could adapt this paradigm to incorporate social rewards (e.g., “likes,” social approval) and uncertainty about social outcomes to test whether the observed exploratory tendencies are more pronounced in socially relevant contexts. Fifth, the study's sample size was determined based on normative practices in computational modeling research rather than a formal a priori power analysis. The minimal detectable effect size at 80% power was d = 0.41, slightly larger than our observed effect. Future studies with larger samples, ideally determined through formal a priori power analysis, are needed to confirm and extend our findings.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that problematic social media use is associated with a distinct computational phenotype characterized by heightened directed exploration, a strategic bias toward uncertain options for information gain, and that this relationship is complexly moderated by intolerance of uncertainty. These findings extend the literature on PSMU by moving from self-reported traits to formal models of latent decision processes, providing a mechanistic bridge between psychological constructs and observable behavior. The suppressor effect of IU highlights the importance of considering multiple, potentially opposing motivational forces when modeling the cognitive architecture of problematic digital behavior. Future research integrating computational modeling with neuroimaging and longitudinal designs holds promise for further unraveling these mechanisms and translating insights into targeted, mechanism-based interventions for individuals whose social media use has become compulsive and impairing.
