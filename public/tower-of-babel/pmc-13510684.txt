IDE-mediated GLP-1 degradation as the basis for designing long-acting and CNS-stable GLP-1 receptor agonists
Authors: Lu Zhang, Xinyi Liu, Chenfang Si, Xianglong Hou, Xintian Hu, Yelin Chen, Junhao Hu, Christoph W. Turck, Bing Shan, Yaoyang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) is a key incretin hormone rapidly degraded by circulating proteases such as DPP-4. The metabolism of GLP-1 by other proteases, particularly tissue-resident proteases, remains largely unexplored. Here, we identify insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) as a previously unknown GLP-1-degrading protease with two cleavage sites. We show that IDE-mediated degradation of GLP-1, but not insulin, represents a major mechanism regulating glucose control. To resist IDE, we engineered GLP-1 and Semaglutide with D-amino acid substitutions at these sites. These peptides exhibit enhanced stability in plasma, liver and intestinal secretomes, peritoneal fluid, and central nervous system (CNS). D-Ser18-Semaglutide shows prolonged plasma retention and sustained glucose-lowering effects in mice. Moreover, IDE knockdown and intracerebral injection of D-Ser18-Semaglutide confirm IDE’s physiological role in GLP-1 degradation, particularly in the CNS. These findings reveal a previously unidentified regulatory mechanism of GLP-1 metabolism and provide a strategy for designing long-acting agonists with improved metabolic and CNS therapeutic potential.

INTRODUCTION

GLP-1 is an incretin peptide hormone that plays a central role in glucose homeostasis by enhancing glucose-dependent insulin secretion (1), suppressing glucagon release, delaying gastric emptying, and promoting satiety (2, 3). Beyond its role in metabolic regulation, GLP-1 and activation of its receptor have been shown to function across multiple body organs, particularly promoting neurogenesis and exerting neuroprotective effects in the central nervous system (CNS) (4). Therefore, GLP-1-based therapies have emerged as the most prominent and effective treatments for diabetes and obesity in current clinical practice and also hold great potential for the treatment of neurological disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, multiple sclerosis, and beyond.

However, the therapeutic potential of native GLP-1 is severely limited by its short half-life (5, 6), which is primarily due to rapid degradation by proteolytic enzymes such as dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) and neutral endopeptidase 24.11 (NEP 24.11) (7, 8), as well as rapid renal clearance owing to its small molecular size. To overcome these limitations, DPP-4 inhibitors such as Sitagliptin (9), Saxagliptin (10), and Linagliptin (11) have been developed to prevent the enzymatic degradation of endogenous GLP-1. Moreover, GLP-1 analogues or GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have been designed to resist enzymatic degradation and extend their circulating half-life through various modifications. For instance, mutations at the DPP-4 cleavage site can render the peptide resistant to DPP-4-mediated degradation; fatty acid modifications can promote binding to albumin, thereby protecting the peptide from rapid renal clearance; and fusion with albumin or Fc fragments prolongs plasma half-life by increasing molecular size and bloodstream stability. Successful examples include Semaglutide (12), Liraglutide (13), Albiglutide (14), and Dulaglutide (15). However, these strategies primarily improve the stability of GLP-1RAs in the circulatory system, but do not necessarily address their metabolism in target organs. Considering that DPP-4 is not a highly abundant protease in many organs (16) (fig. S1A), this highlights the importance of a comprehensive understanding of GLP-1 metabolism mechanisms, particularly across different organs, in guiding the development of the next generation of GLP-1-based therapeutics.

Insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE), a zinc metalloprotease, is widely recognized for its role in the degradation of insulin, glucagon, and amyloid-β, with implications for both metabolic and neurodegenerative diseases (17–19). Given that both IDE and GLP-1 are closely associated with these diseases, we hypothesized that IDE could play a role in GLP-1 degradation, thereby contributing to its short half-life under physiological and pathological conditions. More importantly, compared to DPP-4, IDE is not only a circulating enzyme but is also highly expressed in tissues such as the CNS (fig. S1A). Furthermore, studies have reported that IDE is expressed and secreted in both intracellular and extracellular compartments (20). IDE-mediated GLP-1 metabolism may therefore have a critical role in GLP-1 function in the CNS.

To test this hypothesis, in this study, we demonstrate that IDE is a potent degradative enzyme for GLP-1, with cleavage sites identified at Ser18-Tyr19 (IG-1) and Ala25-Lys26 (IG-2). Using multiple in vitro and in vivo settings, we show that IDE-mediated degradation significantly reduces GLP-1 stability and bioactivity. Interestingly, we demonstrate that IDE-mediated degradation of GLP-1, rather than insulin, is the principal mechanism through which IDE regulates glucose homeostasis. Based on this mechanism, we engineered GLP-1 analogs with D-amino acid substitutions at the IDE cleavage sites, which resulted in enhanced stability and increased resistance to proteolysis. Furthermore, we developed D-Ser18-Semaglutide, a Semaglutide analog with a D-amino acid substitution at the Ser18 site, which demonstrated superior pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties compared to Semaglutide. The single D-amino acid substitution allows for better blood glucose control even with less frequent dosing. By bypassing peripheral metabolism, direct stereotaxic injection into the hypothalamus demonstrated that D-Ser18-Semaglutide elicits a superior weight loss effect in obese mice. In summary, our findings provide mechanistic insights into IDE-mediated GLP-1 degradation and offer a previously unexplored approach to engineering next-generation long-acting GLP-1RAs with improved therapeutic efficacy. These strategies hold substantial potential for treating CNS disorders with the help of GLP-1 analogues.

RESULTS

IDE degrades GLP-1 to modulate glucose homeostasis

IDE has been genetically linked to diabetes susceptibility and is known to regulate glucose homeostasis through the degradation of peptide hormones, including insulin and glucagon (21–23). However, whether IDE degrades additional substrates and thereby contributes more broadly to glycemic homeostasis remains unclear. Given that the substrate-binding chamber of IDE is relatively small (estimated to accommodate peptides of <50 amino acids), limiting its catalytic activity largely to short peptides (19), we hypothesized that IDE may target other secreted peptide hormones. To test this, we analyzed secreted peptides from the conditioned media of IDE-knockdown and wild-type NCI-H716 cells. This cell line is widely recognized for its ability to endogenously express proglucagon and process it into mature GLP-1 (24), making it a suitable cellular model for studying native GLP-1 biosynthesis and secretion. Notably, IDE knockdown resulted in a marked increase in GLP-1 levels (Fig. 1, A and B), exceeding the accumulation observed for established IDE substrates such as glucagon and insulin (Fig. 1, A and B and data S1).

To directly assess whether GLP-1 is an IDE substrate, we incubated purified human GLP-1 (7-37) (20 μM) with recombinant human IDE (200 nM) at 37°C for 30 min. Under these conditions, IDE efficiently degraded GLP-1, with a similar effect observed for glucagon (Fig. 1C). The addition of 6bk, a known IDE inhibitor, slightly reduced the degradation caused by IDE (Fig. 1C). In contrast, insulin showed minimal degradation by IDE (Fig. 1C), which became detectable only when the IDE concentration or incubation time was substantially increased (fig. S1, B and C). To validate a direct physical interaction, BS3 crosslinking followed by SDS–PAGE revealed an upward mobility shift consistent with IDE–GLP-1 complex formation (fig. S1D). Mass spectrometry mapping identified crosslinks between Lys899 of IDE and Lys34 of GLP-1 (fig. S1E), providing molecular evidence for direct substrate engagement. These results identify GLP-1 as a substrate of IDE in vitro. Additionally, we determined the kinetic parameters of IDE-mediated GLP-1 degradation using a fluorogenic MCA/DNP-GLP-1 substrate. Recombinant IDE (20 nM) was incubated with increasing concentrations of substrate (5–100 μM) at 37°C. Fluorescence was continuously monitored over the initial 300 s to calculate initial velocities (V0) (fig. S1F). The reaction followed classical Michaelis–Menten kinetics, yielding a Km of ∼30 μM and a Vmax of ∼1211 RFU·s−1 (Fig. 1D and fig. S1F).

Because both GLP-1 and insulin contribute to glucose lowering, with GLP-1 known to promote insulin secretion (2, 3), we next asked which IDE-regulated substrate predominates in vivo. We generated a liver-specific IDE knockdown mouse model by intravenous administration of an AAV8 vector encoding IDE-targeting shRNA (Fig. 1E). Intraperitoneal glucose tolerance testing (IPGTT) revealed that IDE knockdown mice displayed a more pronounced acute reduction in blood glucose compared with wild-type controls (Fig. 1F). Notably, this enhanced glucose-lowering effect was abolished by co-treatment with the GLP-1 receptor antagonist Exendin (9-39) (100 nmol/kg), resulting in comparable glycemic profiles between knockdown and control animals (Fig. 1F and fig. S1G).

Moreover, we performed oral glucose tolerance tests (OGTT), which provide a more physiologically relevant context for assessing endogenous GLP-1 action. In this setting, the enhanced glucose-lowering effect observed after IDE knockdown was abolished by pre-treatment with the GLP-1 receptor antagonist Exendin (9-39) (fig. S1, H and I).

Similarly, the increased insulin response in IDE-knockdown mice was reversed by GLP-1 receptor blockade (Fig. 1G and fig. S1, J and K), indicating that the observed insulin secretion is dependent on GLP-1 receptor signaling rather than reflecting altered insulin clearance. We further measured circulating glucagon and GIP levels during the OGTT and observed no significant differences between groups (fig. S1, L and M). Together, these results support the conclusion that IDE regulates glucose homeostasis primarily through modulation of GLP-1 degradation.

IDE contributes to GLP-1 degradation in addition to DPP-4 under physiologically relevant conditions

To directly compare the GLP-1–degrading activities of IDE and DPP-4, a well-established major GLP-1–degrading enzyme, we performed in vitro degradation assays under defined conditions. In a reaction system (50 μL) containing 20 μM GLP-1 and either IDE (20 nM) or DPP-4 (20 nM), mass spectrometry analysis demonstrated that IDE degrades GLP-1 more efficiently than DPP-4 at equivalent enzyme concentrations (Fig. 1H).

To better approximate physiological circulating conditions, we established a reconstituted in vitro system based on reported plasma protease concentrations (https://www.proteinatlas.org), with GLP-1 maintained at an elevated concentration suitable for quantitative detection (DPP-4, ∼8 nM; IDE, ∼0.01 nM; GLP-1, 0.22 nM). Under these conditions, despite an ∼800-fold difference in enzyme concentration, 8 nM DPP-4 induced only marginally greater GLP-1 degradation than 0.01 nM IDE after 30 min (Fig. 1I). When both enzymes were present, GLP-1 inactivation was further accelerated (Fig. 1I). By 60 min, GLP-1 was fully cleared by either protease alone (Fig. 1I).

Collectively, these results establish IDE, alongside DPP-4, as a physiologically relevant GLP-1 protease and demonstrate its contribution to the control of glucose homeostasis through regulation of GLP-1 stability.

Knockdown of IDE increases circulating GLP-1 and improves glycemic control in mice

To determine whether IDE regulates GLP-1 and glucose homeostasis under physiological conditions, mice were injected with an AAV8 vector expressing IDE-targeting shRNA to achieve liver-specific knockdown of IDE (Fig. 1J and fig. S1N). Ten days after AAV8 administration, a positive control group was treated with Sitagliptin (0.3 mg/kg), a selective inhibitor of DPP-4, the canonical enzyme responsible for GLP-1 degradation. Thirty minutes later, all mice received exogenous GLP-1 (1 mg/kg), and plasma GLP-1 concentrations were measured at 10 and 30 min after injection.

Liver-specific knockdown of IDE significantly increased circulating GLP-1 levels compared with control animals, with an effect that was more pronounced than sitagliptin treatment and most evident at the 30-min time point (Fig. 1K). Moreover, combined IDE knockdown and DPP-4 inhibition further stabilized plasma GLP-1, demonstrating a synergistic effect (Fig. 1K).

Consistent with these biochemical changes, IDE knockdown mice exhibited markedly improved glucose-lowering responses during glucose tolerance testing following GLP-1 pre-administration (Fig. 1L). In contrast, Sitagliptin treatment alone did not significantly improve acute glycemic responses compared with controls (Fig. 1L). Together, these findings support the conclusion that IDE is an endogenous, physiologically relevant regulator of GLP-1 stability and contributes to the control of glucose homeostasis.

GLP-1 primary cleavage sites mediated by IDE

Next, we aimed to identify the primary cleavage sites on GLP-1 mediated by IDE. Using mass spectrometry, we analyzed all GLP-1 fragment peptides resulting from IDE treatment in vitro. Our data identified two major cleavage sites: Ser18-Tyr19 and Ala25-Lys26, termed IDE-cut-GLP-1 site 1 (IG-1) and site 2 (IG-2), respectively (Fig. 2A). Further analysis showed that the abundance of GLP-1 fragments generated from these two cleavage sites increased over time (fig. S2A). Notably, the reduced intensity of the “YLEGQAAKEFIAWLVKGRG” fragment at later time points likely reflects its secondary cleavage by IDE (fig. S2A). We next evaluated the effect of the IDE inhibitor 6bk on GLP-1 degradation. Consistent with our previous observations, 6bk failed to fully inhibit IDE activity, particularly at the IG-2 site (fig. S2B). Using the same approach, we mapped DPP-4 mediated cleavage of GLP-1 and found that proteolysis was largely restricted to the Ala8- Glu9 bond (Fig. 2B), consistent with prior reports (7).

To assess IDE’s role in more physiologically relevant conditions, we incubated GLP-1 with mouse plasma (Fig. 2C), mouse peritoneal fluid (Fig. 2D) and rhesus monkey CSF (Fig. 2E). Mass spectrometry analysis confirmed significant GLP-1 degradation in all matrices (Fig. 2, C to E), with cleavage occurring at the known DPP-4 site (Ala8-Glu9) as well as at the IDE-specific IG-1 and IG-2 sites (Fig. 2F and fig. S2C), demonstrating that IDE serves as a bona fide physiological contributor to GLP-1 degradation. In peritoneal fluid, GLP-1 was also cleaved at Phe28-Ile29, likely due to the activity of other enzymes such as NEP 24.11 (8, 25) (fig. S2C). Furthermore, we detected smaller peptide fragments derived from both IDE- and DPP-4-mediated cleavage products, indicating that the initial cleavage products remain susceptible to further proteolytic processing (fig. S2D). Our findings support the fact that GLP-1 metabolism involves multiple proteases and identify two primary cleavage sites on GLP-1 mediated by IDE.

Based on the identification of IDE’s primary cleavage sites, we synthesized two hexapeptides (VSSYLE and QAAKEF) targeting these sites as competitive inhibitors. These peptides, particularly when combined, effectively inhibited IDE-mediated GLP-1 degradation (fig. S2E).

Decreased IDE levels enhance GLP-1 stability

To further investigate IDE’s role in GLP-1 degradation in a cellular environment, we collected and concentrated the culture medium from JHH7 hepatocellular cells and added 5 μM GLP-1 for a 30-minute incubation. JHH7 cells were selected because they are a well-differentiated human hepatoma cell line with high endogenous IDE expression and secretion (fig. S2F), making them suitable for establishing a semi-physiological system to investigate the role of IDE in GLP-1 degradation. In addition to wild-type (WT) cells, JHH7 cells were transfected with shIDE (in pLKO.1) using PolyJet, followed by puromycin selection (8 μg/mL, 7 days) to generate stable IDE-knockdown lines. IDE expression in this cell line was subsequently partially restored to approximately 30% of WT levels by transfecting an IDE overexpression plasmid (fig. S2F). First, we confirmed that the IDE secreted by WT JHH7 cells is a major protease responsible for degrading extracellular GLP-1, with cleavage occurring primarily at the IG-1 and IG-2 sites (Fig. 2, G and H). Strikingly, when IDE levels were decreased, GLP-1 stability significantly increased, and the degradation resumed after IDE was reintroduced (Fig. 2, G and H and fig. S2F).

Next, we examined the stability of endogenous GLP-1 secreted by NCI-H716 colorectal cells. The abundance of cell-secreted GLP-1 was measured using mass spectrometry. Consistent with results from exogenous GLP-1, IDE knockdown in NCI-H716 cells significantly increased the extracellular levels of GLP-1 (Fig. 2I and fig. S2G), further supporting IDE as a key regulator of extracellular GLP-1 levels (Fig. 2I and fig. S2G).

D-amino acid substitution at IG-1 and IG-2 sites enhances GLP-1 stability

To enhance resistance to IDE digestion while maintaining the ability to activate GLP-1 receptors, we engineered the IG-1 and IG-2 sites by substituting one or two residues with their corresponding D-amino acid residues (Fig. 3A). Additionally, we switched the DPP-4 cleavage site (Ala) to a non-natural amino acid, 2-Aminoisobutyric acid (Aib), in these GLP-1 variants. These GLP-1 analogs were incubated with IDE in vitro. Our data showed that GLP-1 variants with D-amino acid substitutions exhibited significantly improved stability. The Ser18 mutant as well as the double mutant at both IG-1 and IG-2 sites exhibited minimal degradation (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, mass spectrometry analysis revealed that the D-amino acid substitutions only protected cleavage at the targeted site, leaving the other IG site unaffected. The double mutant at both IG-1 and IG-2 sites prevented cleavage at both sites (Fig. 3, B and C).

Subsequently, we tested the double D-amino acid GLP-1 (dSA-GLP-1) mutant and found that it exhibited enhanced stability in cell culture medium (Fig. 3D and fig. S3A), mouse plasma (Fig. 3E and fig. S3B) and mouse peritoneal fluid (Fig. 3F and fig. S3C). To further assess its potential for CNS applications, we examined dSA-GLP-1 stability in CNS-relevant matrices, including mouse brain lysate (Fig. 3G and fig. S3D) and rhesus monkey CSF (Fig. 3H). Consistent with previous findings, dSA-GLP-1 exhibited markedly improved stability in these environments, suggesting its potential for CNS drug development efforts. Moreover, stability assays in human serum demonstrated that dSA-GLP-1 exhibited significantly prolonged stability compared with native GLP-1 after both 30 min and 24 h of incubation (Fig. 3I and fig. S3, E and F). In summary, by targeting the IDE-induced cleavage sites of GLP-1, we designed several D-amino-acid-substituted GLP-1 analogues with enhanced stability towards IDE digestion.

dSA-GLP-1 exhibits activity in activating GLP-1R

To assess the agonistic activity of these GLP-1RAs, we employed a GLP-1 activity assay that measures cAMP production in GLP-1R-overexpressing 293 T cells (fig. S3G). Our results revealed that dSA-GLP-1 exhibited comparable agonistic activity to its native form (Fig. 3, J and K). When IDE was added to the assay, GLP-1 lost its ability to stimulate cAMP production due to rapid degradation by the enzyme, whereas dSA-GLP-1 maintained its ability to activate cAMP production due to its greater stability (Fig. 3L).

Next, GLP-1 and dSA-GLP-1 were administered to WT mice via intraperitoneal injection. Our data revealed that dSA-GLP-1 exhibited superior glucose-lowering effects compared to native GLP-1 (Fig. 3M and fig. S3H). Moreover, plasma samples were collected 30 min after glucose challenge for hormone analysis. Both GLP-1 and dSA-GLP-1 increased insulin levels and decreased glucagon levels relative to saline controls, with dSA-GLP-1 showing a more pronounced effect (fig. S3, I and J). GIP levels were elevated only in the dSA-GLP-1 group relative to saline controls (fig. S3K). These findings support the enhanced glucose-lowering efficacy of dSA-GLP-1 in vivo.

D-Ser18-Semaglutide exhibits enhanced stability

Fatty acid modification is a well-established approach to enhance peptide stability in the bloodstream (26). Building on the widely used GLP-1 receptor agonist Semaglutide, we designed a D-form variant, termed D-Ser18-Semaglutide, by introducing a D-serine at position 18 (Fig. 4A). Notably, lysine 26, which is selected for fatty acid attachment in Semaglutide, is also a key residue in the IG-2 cleavage site. Therefore, by modifying only Ser18 to its D-configuration, both IG-1 and IG-2 sites can be simultaneously protected, resulting in improved resistance to IDE. In vitro experiments demonstrated that D-Ser18-Semaglutide showed greater resistance to IDE-mediated degradation compared to Semaglutide (Fig. 4, B and C). This enhanced stability was also confirmed in cell culture medium (Fig. 4D and fig. S4A), mouse peritoneal fluid (Fig. 4E and fig. S4B), plasma (fig. S4, C and D), mouse brain lysate (Fig. 4F and fig. S4E), and rhesus monkey CSF (Fig. 4G). Notably, the two Semaglutide analogues showed comparable stability in mouse plasma and human serum, suggesting that lipid modification and albumin binding contribute to enhanced resistance to proteolysis (fig. S4F). Furthermore, D-Ser18-Semaglutide retained its agonistic activity for GLP-1R (Fig. 4, H and I), showing much higher cAMP production in the presence of IDE compared to Semaglutide (fig. S4G).

D-Ser18-Semaglutide enhances glucose tolerance in mice

Building on evidence that engineering at the IG-1 and IG-2 cleavage sites can enhance resistance to IDE-mediated degradation of GLP-1 and its analogues, we sought to evaluate the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of D-Ser18-Semaglutide in vivo. To assess its metabolism and stability, mice were intraperitoneally administered 1 mg/kg of either Semaglutide or D-Ser18-Semaglutide once, and blood samples were analyzed by mass spectrometry at several post-injection time points (0, 0.08, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 hours) (fig. S4H). While plasma concentrations of D-Ser18-Semaglutide and Semaglutide were comparable during the first 4 h post-injection (fig. S4H), D-Ser18-Semaglutide exhibited slower clearance during the elimination phase. As a result, at 48 h post-injection, the plasma concentration of D-Ser18-Semaglutide was approximately fourfold that of Semaglutide, indicating prolonged systemic exposure (Fig. 4J and fig. S4H). This increased plasma concentration after two days of metabolism highlights the effectiveness of the D-amino acid substitution at Ser18 in conferring resistance to IDE-mediated proteolysis, thereby enhancing the peptide’s stability in vivo.

To assess whether the improved pharmacokinetic profile of D-Ser18-Semaglutide translates into enhanced therapeutic efficacy, we conducted three-phase acute glucose tolerance tests in mice. Animals received 50 nmol/kg of Semaglutide or D-Ser18-Semaglutide at 1.5 hours before glucose administration (time − 1.5 hours), followed by intraperitoneal glucose injections at three subsequent time points (0, 12, and 24 hours). The mice were fasted throughout the entire experiment, and blood glucose levels were monitored during each test phase (Fig. 4K). In the first phase, both Semaglutide variants exhibited comparable glucose-lowering effects. However, in the later two phases, mice treated with D-Ser18-Semaglutide exhibited a significantly attenuated rise in blood glucose following glucose challenges compared to those treated with Semaglutide (Fig. 4L and fig. S4I). Furthermore, long-term glucose tolerance tests were conducted over four days. Mice received a single administration of the respective Semaglutide variants at 0 hours, followed by intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests at 24-, 48-, 72-, and 96-hours post-administration. Prior to each test, mice were fasted for 8 hours (Fig. 4M). Notably, D-Ser18-Semaglutide significantly reduced blood glucose compared with Semaglutide at multiple time points during the 98-hour experimental period (Fig. 4N and fig. S4J). These results indicate that the enhanced stability of D-Ser18-Semaglutide confers prolonged glucose-lowering activity, underscoring its potential as a long-acting therapeutic agent (Fig. 4, L and N).

D-Ser18-Semaglutide exhibits enhanced glucose-lowering and weight-reducing efficacy in obese mice

To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of D-Ser18-Semaglutide in a pathological context, we conducted a study using DIO mice. In the first experimental scheme, animals were administered low doses (25 nmol/kg) of either Semaglutide or D-Ser18-Semaglutide for four consecutive days, and their blood glucose levels and body weights were monitored daily for 10 days (Fig. 5A). Both drugs effectively reduced body weight to a similar extent, but body weight was gradually regained after treatment cessation (fig. S4K). During the four-day treatment period, both Semaglutide variants successfully lowered blood glucose levels to comparable extents (Fig. 5B). However, after treatment cessation, blood glucose levels remained significantly lower in the D-Ser18-Semaglutide group. Three days post-treatment, the glucose-lowering effect of Semaglutide was completely diminished, whereas D-Ser18-Semaglutide maintained a prolonged effect (Fig. 5, B and C). These findings emphasize the long-lasting nature of D-Ser18-Semaglutide in achieving durable blood glucose control.

D-Ser18-Semaglutide improves glucose-lowering efficacy in obese mice.

(A to C) DIO mice treated with D-Ser18-Semaglutide for 4 consecutive days exhibited more sustained glycemic control than Semaglutide-treated mice (n = 8, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s test for (B); n = 8, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test for C; mean ± SEM). (D to F) DIO mice treated with less frequent dosing of D-Ser18-Semaglutide exhibited more sustained glycemic control than Semaglutide-treated mice. Mice received Semaglutide every 2 days (7 doses) or D-Ser18-Semaglutide every 3 days (5 doses) at 50 nmol/kg (n = 7–10, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s test for E; n = 7–10, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test for F; mean ± SEM). (G) D-Ser18-Semaglutide exhibits superior weight loss efficacy following direct intracerebral injection (n = 7–10, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s test, mean ± SEM). (H) Circulating Semaglutide exhibits enhanced stability in IDE knockdown mice. Plasma Semaglutide concentrations were quantified 48 hours after a single intraperitoneal injection (1 mg/kg) (n = 5, use unpaired two-tailed t test, data presented as mean ± SEM). (I to K) Long-term glucose tolerance testing shows that IDE knockdown enhances Semaglutide efficacy in mice. Across four experimental phases (I), blood glucose monitoring (J) and AUC analysis (K) demonstrated more potent and sustained glycemic control throughout the study period (n = 8, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test for (J); n = 8, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test for K; mean ± SEM). (L to N) IDE knockdown in DIO mice enhances Semaglutide efficacy in glycemic control. DIO mice received five doses of Semaglutide (every other day). Blood glucose monitoring (M) and AUC analysis (N) demonstrated enhanced glucose-lowering efficacy in IDE knockdown mice (n = 8, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s test for (M); n = 8, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test for (N); mean ± SEM).

Given the prolonged stability and extended activity of D-Ser18-Semaglutide, we investigated whether it could also achieve satisfactory therapeutic efficacy with reduced dosing frequency. To test this, 50 nmol/kg of Semaglutide was administered every two days, while 50 nmol/kg of D-Ser18-Semaglutide was administered every three days (Fig. 5D). Despite the reduced dosing frequency, D-Ser18-Semaglutide demonstrated comparable weight-reducing effects to Semaglutide (fig. S4L). In addition, D-Ser18-Semaglutide exhibited superior glucose-lowering efficacy at multiple time points despite the lower dosing frequency (Fig. 5, E and F). Consistently, quantitative area under the curve (AUC) analysis demonstrated significantly improved glycemic control with D-Ser18-Semaglutide compared to Semaglutide (Fig. 5F). This finding suggests that the enhanced stability of D-Ser18-Semaglutide allows for less frequent administration without compromising therapeutic outcomes, offering a potential advantage in clinical settings.

Direct stereotaxic injection of D-Ser18-Semaglutide into the brain enhances weight-reducing efficacy in obese mice

As the hypothalamus is the central brain region critically involved in appetite regulation (27), and given that Semaglutide cannot efficiently cross the blood-brain barrier to directly act on the central nervous system regulation, it is believed to suppress appetite indirectly via peripheral pathways, such as vagal nerve signaling (28). Considering that IDE is highly expressed in the brain, we sought to evaluate whether D-Ser18-Semaglutide could exert direct central effects when delivered into the hypothalamus.

To test this hypothesis, we administered 6.25 nmol/kg of Semaglutide variants via stereotaxic injection directly into the hypothalamus of DIO mice and compared their effects on body weight regulation. The dose was chosen based on published studies demonstrating that central administration of GLP-1 receptor agonists within this dose range is sufficient to produce measurable pharmacological effects (29). Notably, D-Ser18-Semaglutide exhibited superior metabolic stability in murine brain tissue, leading to a more sustained anti-obesity effect (Fig. 5G). In contrast, mice treated with unmodified Semaglutide experienced significant weight rebound within 48 hours postadministration, suggesting rapid degradation by IDE-mediated proteolysis in the brain (Fig. 5G). These data not only highlight the therapeutic potential of D-Ser18-Semaglutide for metabolic diseases, but more importantly, suggest that the underlying mechanism involving IDE-mediated regulation of GLP-1, and the subsequent development of GLP-1RAs based on this mechanism, may have broader applications for a range of other CNS disorders.

Knockdown of IDE in mice enhances the therapeutic effect of Semaglutide

To further elucidate the role of IDE in the degradation of Semaglutide and other GLP-1 analogues in vivo, we reduced IDE expression levels using AAV8-shIDE, which primarily targets the liver, in both normal and DIO mice. To evaluate the impact of IDE on Semaglutide metabolism and stability in vivo, both IDE knockdown mice and wild-type mice were intraperitoneally administered 1 mg/kg of Semaglutide once, and plasma samples were analyzed by mass spectrometry at 48 hours (Fig. 5H). Notably, the plasma concentration of Semaglutide in IDE knockdown mice remained approximately 1.5 times higher than that of wild-type mice (Fig. 5H). Similar to our previous experiment, long-term glucose tolerance tests were conducted following a single administration of Semaglutide. Mice were fasted for 8 hours prior to each intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test, and glycemic responses were monitored at 24-, 48-, 72-, and 96-hours post-administration (Fig. 5I). Our results demonstrate that IDE knockdown significantly enhances the glucose-lowering effect of Semaglutide in vivo (Fig. 5, J and K). To investigate the hormonal basis underlying the enhanced glucose-lowering effect of Semaglutide in IDE knockdown mice, we measured plasma insulin, GIP, and glucagon levels during the glucose tolerance test. IDE knockdown mice treated with Semaglutide exhibited significantly higher glucose-stimulated insulin levels and reduced glucagon levels compared with control mice (fig. S4, M and N). In contrast, GIP levels were not significantly affected by IDE knockdown (fig. S4O). These data suggest that IDE knockdown enhances the therapeutic efficacy of Semaglutide through increased Semaglutide stability, leading to augmented insulin secretion and suppression of glucagon release (fig. S4, M to O). Moreover, in DIO mice (Fig. 5L and fig. S4P), while Semaglutide induced comparable weight loss regardless of IDE levels (fig. S4Q), its glucose-lowering efficacy was markedly enhanced in IDE-knockdown mice (Fig. 5, M and N). Collectively, these data confirm that IDE-mediated degradation limits the therapeutic potential of Semaglutide in vivo and underscore the importance of targeting IDE cleavage sites to enhance the stability and efficacy of GLP-1 receptor agonists (Fig. 5, J, K, M, and N).

DISCUSSION

GLP-1RAs are a class of therapeutics widely used for the treatment of metabolic diseases such as diabetes and obesity. The development of GLP-1 receptor agonists has focused on improving resistance to enzymatic degradation (e.g., DPP-4-mediated cleavage) and enhancing therapeutic efficacy through multi-receptor agonism strategies, including GLP-1/GIP/glucagon receptor co-agonists such as Retatrutide (30). However, understanding GLP-1 clearance beyond the circulatory system is critical for the rational design of next-generation GLP-1RAs.

In this study, we establish IDE as a potent GLP-1 degrading protease. Our in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that IDE-mediated degradation significantly reduces GLP-1 stability and bioactivity. Furthermore, IDE knockdown in cell cultures and mice led to a substantial increase in GLP-1 levels, underscoring the pivotal role of IDE in GLP-1 metabolism. The identification of IDE cleavage sites IG-1 and IG-2 provides important mechanistic insights into IDE’s substrate specificity and offers principles for therapeutic design.

The D-amino acid substitution strategy employed in this study is relatively straightforward and provides the first direct evidence highlighting the importance of the identified IDE cleavage sites IG-1 and IG-2. Notably, widely used GLP-1RAs on the market, such as Liraglutide and Semaglutide, attach a fatty acid chain at the Lys26 residue. It is generally believed that this modification site was selected primarily due to the high chemical reactivity of lysine. However, our findings reveal that this lysine also coincides with an IDE cleavage site, suggesting that the lipidation not only facilitates albumin binding but may also confer partial resistance to IDE-mediated proteolysis. Although D-amino acid substitution at the cleavage site resulted in a slight decrease in receptor activation potency compared to the parent molecule, the therapeutic efficacy was still significantly improved due to enhanced stability. This observation underscores the crucial importance of improving IDE proteolytic resistance in GLP-1RA design. Nevertheless, more comprehensive chemical engineering and screening at these cleavage sites could generate GLP-1RAs with both enhanced stability and higher biological activity, thereby further improving therapeutic efficacy.

While DPP-4 is highly abundant in the bloodstream, IDE is widely distributed across multiple organs, from the intestine, where abundant GLP-1 is secreted, to GLP-1 target organs such as the brain. Therefore, IDE-mediated GLP-1 degradation possibly represents a critical regulatory mechanism across the whole body, particularly within the CNS.

Notably, the GLP-1RA Exenatide (31, 32) recently failed to show significant clinical benefits in Parkinson’s disease trials, exhibiting minimal effects on movement, symptoms, and brain imaging outcomes (33). We have confirmed that Exenatide, which contains an IDE cleavage site (IG-1), is a substrate for IDE, suggesting that IDE-mediated degradation may have contributed to its limited efficacy. Moreover, agents like Semaglutide and Liraglutide exhibit poor blood-brain barrier penetration, making them less suitable for CNS disorders. In contrast, our data using mouse brain lysates and monkey cerebrospinal fluid suggest that engineering GLP-1RAs such as exenatide to resist IDE degradation can increase brain concentrations of GLP-1RAs, potentially enhancing their therapeutic effects for neurological diseases.

Furthermore, our findings could facilitate the design of orally bioavailable peptide-based GLP-1RA formulations. IDE is abundantly expressed in the digestive tract and peritoneal cavity (34, 35), and GLP-1RAs engineered to resist IDE-mediated degradation have the potential for improved stability and efficacy in case of oral administration. Notably, recent advances in orally active small-molecule GLP-1RAs, such as Orforglipron (36), offer an alternative strategy to overcome the enzymatic instability of peptide-based GLP-1RAs, as these non-peptide agonists are intrinsically resistant to DPP-4- and IDE-mediated degradation.

Modulating IDE activity using small molecules has long been pursued as a strategy to stabilize insulin for diabetes treatment (37). However, progress has been limited due to IDE’s broad substrate specificity. Inhibiting IDE could simultaneously elevate undesirable substrates such as glucagon (an insulin antagonist) and impair amyloid-β clearance, potentially worsening neurodegenerative conditions. Accordingly, IDE knockout studies have produced controversial outcomes in mice, with some studies reporting benefits, including the improvements in body mass, fasting blood glucose, and insulin tolerance at two months of age IDE-KO mice (38), and others showing deleterious effects (39). Selective allosteric inhibitors targeting specific IDE substrates have been proposed, but remain extremely challenging to develop (37). An alternative strategy could involve selectively reducing IDE expression in specific tissues using approaches such as small interfering RNA (siRNA) or antisense oligonucleotides. For instance, targeting IDE in hepatocytes or intestinal cells could significantly enhance endogenous GLP-1 secretion and amplify GLP-1–centered metabolic benefits without globally disrupting IDE function.

Our experiments combining IDE knockdown with DPP-4 inhibition revealed a synergistic effect in stabilizing GLP-1 levels in vivo. This finding suggests that targeting multiple degradative pathways could further improve the therapeutic efficacy of GLP-1-based therapies. While DPP-4 inhibitors are already widely used in clinical practice, their effectiveness may be limited by alternative degradative enzymes like IDE. Therefore, our results highlight the promise of dual-target strategies for maximizing GLP-1RA stability and bioactivity.

In conclusion, this study elucidates the critical role of IDE in GLP-1 degradation and demonstrates the feasibility of engineering IDE-resistant GLP-1RAs via targeted amino acid substitutions. Our findings not only deepen the mechanistic understanding of GLP-1 metabolism but also lay the foundation for developing next-generation GLP-1RAs with improved therapeutic efficacy for diabetes, obesity, and potentially CNS disorders.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell lines and cell culture

JHH-7 and HEK293T cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, NCI-H716 were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. NCI-H716 cells were kindly provided by Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Purification of human IDE

Recombinant N-terminal His6-tagged human IDE (residues 42–1019) was expressed in E. coli BL21 cells transformed with the pET28a-hIDE expression plasmid. Protein expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG when cultures reached an OD600 of approximately 0.6 and continued overnight at 37°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 1% Triton X-100, and 1 mM TCEP, pH 8.0). Following cell lysis at 4°C and clarification by centrifugation (10,000g, 25 min, 4°C), the supernatant was incubated with Ni2+-charged IMAC resin for 3 h at 4°C. The resin was washed twice with lysis buffer and three times with wash buffer (50 mM phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, and 1 mM TCEP, pH 8.0). Bound proteins were eluted with buffer containing 250–500 mM imidazole, and eluted fractions were pooled and exchanged into IDE buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl). Purified IDE routinely yielded approximately 1 mg/mL protein with >90% purity as assessed by Coomassie-stained SDS–PAGE.

IDE-mediated GLP-1 degradation in vitro

Synthetic human GLP-1 (7-37) (1 mg, Sangon Biotech, T510039–0001) was dissolved in 25% DMSO (1 mL total volume) and used as a stock solution for all in vitro degradation assays, cell-based experiments, and in vivo studies unless otherwise stated. GLP-1 (7-37) corresponds to the bioactive form of the peptide, lacking the N-terminal propeptide (residues 1–6) present in proglucagon. The reaction system (15 μL) contained 20 μM GLP-1, 200 nM IDE, 200 nM 6bk (IDE inhibitor), 200 nM DPP-4 (Abcam, ab79138), and 200 nM Sitagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor). The reaction buffer consisted of 20 mM Tris-HCl and 100 mM NaCl (pH 7.7). In the inhibitor involved groups, enzymes and inhibitors were pre-incubated for 30 min before the reaction. In the hexapeptides groups, VSSYLE (GenScript) and QAAKEF (GenScript) were added at 1 mM concentration. The reaction was carried out at 37°C with shaking at 750 rpm and terminated by adding 5× SDS-loading buffer with DTT. Following SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, the gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue until protein bands were visible. The gel was then destained with deionized water until the background was clear.

Mass spectrometry analysis of GLP-1 and its degraded peptides

C18 desalting columns (Empore, Lot#321069D) were activated with 100 μL acetonitrile (ACN) and equilibrated with 100 μL 0.1% FA solution. Samples were loaded onto the C18 column, washed three times with 100 μL 0.1% FA, and eluted with 100 μL 90% ACN + 0.1% FA. The eluate was dried under vacuum at 30°C and reconstituted in 20 μL 0.1% FA for mass spectrometry analysis using a Thermo Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) with EASY-nLC 1000 liquid chromatography (Thermo Fisher). The analysis column was 15 cm long, 100 μm inner diameter, and packed with 1.9 μm C18 resin. Mobile phase A consisted of 98% H2O, 2% ACN, and 0.1% FA, while mobile phase B contained 98% ACN, 2% H2O, and 0.1% FA. The gradient was as follows: 0 min, 8% B; 2 min, 20% B; 5 min, 45% B; 9 min, 55% B; 28 min, 80% B; 30 min, 95% B. Flow rate was 300 nL/min. Data acquisition was performed in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, selecting the top 5 precursor ions for fragmentation. MS1 scans were acquired in the Orbitrap with a resolution of 60,000, an AGC target of 3e6, and a maximum injection time of 20 ms. Fragmentation was performed using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) at 32 eV, and MS2 spectra were acquired with a resolution of 30,000, an AGC target of 1e5, and a maximum injection time of 45 ms. Data analysis was performed using MaxQuant (version 2.1.4.0) or PD 2.1 (Thermo Fisher) with sequence matching to GLP-1.

Degradation of GLP-1 and its variants in cell culture medium and biological fluids

JHH7 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, NCI-H716 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin until reaching confluency. The culture medium was replaced with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and cells were incubated for 1 h. The supernatant was collected and concentrated using a 3 kDa molecular weight cutoff filter to a final volume of 80 μL. Western blot analysis was performed to detect IDE levels in the conditioned medium.

For degradation analysis in culture medium, GLP-1, its variants (dSA-GLP-1 and others), Semaglutide, and D-Ser18-Semaglutide (Synpeptide) were dissolved at 5 μM concentration in 100 μL of JHH7 conditioned medium and incubated at 37°C for 30 min.

To assess degradation in biological fluids, mouse plasma was collected and diluted 1:4 with PBS. Human serum was purchased (Topgene Biotechnology). GLP-1 and its variants were incubated at 5 μM in 50 μL of diluted plasma at 37°C for 30 min. Similarly, peritoneal fluid was collected from mice by injecting 1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) into the peritoneal cavity, followed by fluid collection. Samples (100 μL) containing 5 μM GLP-1, its variants, Semaglutide, or D-Ser18-Semaglutide were incubated at 37°C for 30 min.

Reactions in all conditions were terminated by heating at 95°C for 5 min, followed by C18-based peptide extraction. The eluted peptides were dried under vacuum, reconstituted in 20 μL 0.1% FA, and analyzed using a Thermo Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer with identical parameters as previously used.

For endogenous GLP-1 secreted by NCI-H716 cells, NCI-H716 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 106 cells per well in a six-well plate. Cells were washed and incubated in 2 mL of PBS for 1 h. The medium was processed using a C18 column. The column was first activated with acetonitrile (ACN) and equilibrated with 0.1% formic acid (FA). The sample was loaded onto the column, washed with 0.1% FA, and eluted with 90% ACN + 0.1% FA. The eluate was dried and analyzed using Thermo Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer with identical parameters as previously used.

Crosslinking of IDE and GLP-1

For crosslinking experiments, 1 mg of GLP-1 was dissolved in 1 mL of 25% DMSO. The reaction system (50 μL) contained 10 μg GLP-1, 10 μg IDE, and 1 mM BS3 crosslinker. The mixture was incubated at 37°C with shaking at 750 rpm for 60 min and terminated by adding 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8). Samples were prepared in SDS loading buffer, heated at 95°C for 10 min, and subjected to 7.5% SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes under constant voltage (25 V, 0.3A, 60 min). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, incubated overnight with primary antibodies, washed, and incubated with secondary antibodies before visualization.

In addition, cross-linked products were separated by SDS-PAGE, and the bands corresponding to crosslinked GLP-1 and IDE were excised for in-gel digestion. Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using a Thermo Q-Exactive HF mass spectrometer. Data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, with MS1 scans in the Orbitrap and MS2 fragmentation using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD). The data were processed using p-Link.

Knockdown of IDE

For IDE knockdown in cell lines, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting IDE were designed and cloned into the pLKO.1 vector. The forward oligonucleotide sequence was 5’-CCGG-TAGTTTCTGATTCACTATTAG-CTCGAG-CTAATAGTGAATCAGAAACTA-TTTTTG-3′, and the reverse oligonucleotide sequence was 5’-AATTCAAAAA-TAGTTTCTGATTCACTATTAG-CTCGAG-CTAATAGTGAATCAGAAACTA-3′. The recombinant constructs were transfected into HEK293T cells for lentiviral packaging. Viral supernatants were harvested and used to infect JHH-7 cells. After 72 h, cells were selected using 8 μg/mL puromycin to establish stable IDE-knockdown cell lines. Knockdown efficiency was validated by Western blot analysis.

For in vivo IDE knockdown, shRNA sequences targeting IDE were designed and cloned into the pscAAV-U6-shRNA-CBh-EGFP-tWPA vector. The forward oligonucleotide sequence was 5’-CCGG-CGGCTGCATATTGAAGCACTT-CTCGAG-AAGTGCTTCAATATGCAGCCG-TTTTTG-3′, and the reverse oligonucleotide sequence was 5’-AATTCAAAAA-CGGCTGCATATTGAAGCACTT-CTCGAG-AAGTGCTTCAATATGCAGCCG-3′. Recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV8) particles were produced and purified. Mice were injected intravenously with 3 × 1011 viral genomes (v.g.) of the AAV8 construct for liver-specific IDE knockdown. Knockdown efficiency was assessed via Western blot analysis.

Control cells or mice were treated with the empty pLKO.1 vector (for cell lines) or AAV8 particles containing the empty pscAAV-U6-CBh-EGFP-tWPA vector (for in vivo studies) processed identically to the shIDE constructs.

GLP-1 receptor activation assay

HEK293T cells were transfected with a GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R) expression plasmid (flag-pCMV-GLP-1R) and incubated for 24 h to ensure receptor overexpression. Cells were then harvested, resuspended at a density of 400 cells/μL, and plated at 2000 cells per well in a 384-well plate.

GLP-1RAs, including GLP-1, dSA-GLP-1, Semaglutide, and D-Ser18-Semaglutide, were prepared in serial concentrations ranging from 0.2 pM to 2 μM. Each well received 5 μL of the agonist solution and was incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 30 min to stimulate cAMP production.

Following incubation, the cAMP levels were measured using the cAMP-GS DYNAMIC KIT (PerkinElmer, 62AM4PEB), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) was used to determine cAMP levels, and emission signals were detected at 665 nm and 620 nm upon excitation at 337 nm. The cAMP response was calculated as the ratio of 665 nm to 620 nm fluorescence intensity.

Quantitative analysis of hormone levels in mouse plasma using ELISA

To measure plasma GLP-1 levels, mice fasted for 8 h were pretreated with a DPP-4 inhibitor (0.3 mg/kg, oral gavage) 30 min prior to intraperitoneal GLP-1 administration (1 mg/kg). Blood samples were collected at 10 and 30 min post-injection into EDTA-coated tubes and immediately centrifuged (2,000g, 5 min) to obtain plasma. GLP-1 concentrations were quantified using a commercially available ELISA kit (Abcam, ab184857).

To measure plasma insulin, glucagon and GIP levels, mice fasted for 8 h received an intraperitoneal injection of Exendin (9-39) (100 nmol/kg) 30 minutes prior to glucose challenge (2 g/kg, i.p. or oral gavage). Blood samples were collected at 30 minutes post-glucose administration into EDTA-coated tubes and immediately centrifuged (2,000g, 5 min) to obtain plasma. Plasma hormone concentrations were determined using commercially available ELISA kits (Sangon, NO. D721159, NO. D721189 and NO. D721075), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

In vivo glucose-lowering study in mice

Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (No. ECSIOC-2023-20) and conducted following ethical guidelines. Male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were maintained under a 12 h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. Mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups.

For pharmacokinetic analysis, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 1 mg/kg Semaglutide or D-Ser18-Semaglutide, and blood samples were collected at 0, 0.08, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h post-injection. Semaglutide or D-Ser18-Semaglutide and its internal standard diclofenac were extracted from plasma by solid phase column extraction, and quantitative analysis was performed using the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode of a triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometer (Waters XEVO TQ-XS). The components in the sample were separated using a Thermo Hypersil GOLD C18 50 × 2.1 mm, 3 μm column and a specific gradient elution method. The column temperature was 40°C, and the mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, respectively. The flow rate was 0.350 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL.

For glucose tolerance tests, mice were fasted for 8 h before receiving an intraperitoneal injection of 50 nmol/kg GLP-1RAs. 2 g/kg glucose was injected and blood glucose levels were measured at 1.5, 13.5, and 25.5 h postinjection using a glucometer (Accu-Chek, Roche).

For long-term glucose tolerance tests, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 50 nmol/kg GLP-1RAs. Before each glucose tolerance test, mice were fasted for 8 hours. 2 g/kg glucose was injected and blood glucose levels were measured at 24, 48, 72 and 96 h post-injection using a glucometer (Accu-Chek, Roche). To measure plasma insulin, glucagon and GIP levels, blood samples were collected at 30 minutes post-glucose administration into EDTA-coated tubes and immediately centrifuged (2,000g, 5 min) to obtain plasma. Plasma hormone concentrations were determined using commercially available ELISA kits (Sangon, NO. D721159, NO. D721189 and NO. D721075), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of D-Ser18-Semaglutide in a pathological context, diet-induced obese (DIO) mice were treated with 25 nmol/kg Semaglutide or D-Ser18-Semaglutide for 4 consecutive days. Blood glucose levels and body weight were monitored over time. To assess long-term efficacy, Semaglutide was administered 50 nmol/kg every two days, while D-Ser18-Semaglutide was administered 50 nmol/kg every three days. Blood glucose levels and body weight were monitored over time.

Stereotaxic administration of Semaglutide variants to the hypothalamus in DIO mice

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane followed by 1–1.5% maintenance (in 100% O2 at 0.8 L/min) using a calibrated vaporizer. Animals were positioned in a digital stereotaxic frame with non-rupture ear bars and adjustable nose clamp, maintaining body temperature at 37 ± 0.5°C via feedback-controlled heating pad. Following scalp disinfection, a midline incision exposed bregma and lambda sutures. Bilateral craniotomies (0.5 mm diameter) were drilled at ARC coordinates. Semaglutide and its variants were delivered through a needle connected to a microprocessor-controlled injector at 50 nL/min (total 1000 nL/side). Post-injection, the needle remained for 10 min to ensure diffusion before slow retraction. Skull surfaces were irrigated with sterile saline before suture closure.

Statistical analysis

Quantifications of immunoblots and Coomassie blue staining were performed using ImageJ (version 1.8.0). The statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0). Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted. The level of significance was determined as P < 0.05.
