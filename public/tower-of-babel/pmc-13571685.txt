Effect Of Arginine And Spray-Dried Chitosan Pretreatment On Microshear Bond Strength To Bleached Enamel
Authors: Priscila Oliveira Miranda, Leonardo Santos Barros, Núbia Inocencya Pavesi Pini, Milagros Falcon Aguilar, Flávio Henrique Baggio Aguiar, Cecilia Pedroso Turssi, Roberta Tarkany Basting, Flávia Lucisano Botelho do Amaral, Waldemir Francisco Vieira-Junior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study evaluated the effect of experimental solutions containing arginine or spray-dried chitosan on the microshear bond strength of bleached bovine enamel.MethodologyEnamel blocks (5×5×3 mm) were allocated into four groups (n=12): I) unbleached control; II) bleaching only; and bleaching followed by treatment with III) 8% arginine or IV) 2.5% spray-dried chitosan solutions for 5 min prior to adhesive procedures. Bleaching consisted of two sessions of 35% hydrogen peroxide (Whiteness HP, FGM) at a 7-day interval. Two restorations were built per block using a universal adhesive (Scothbond Universal Plus, 3M) and a nanofilled resin composite (Filtek Z350 XT, 3M). Specimens were tested for microshear bond strength (MPa), and failure mode analysis (%). Representative samples of predominant failure, as well as the arginine and chitosan powders, were evaluated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).ResultsData were analyzed using generalized linear models, chi-square and Fisher’s exact test. The unbleached group showed higher MPa values than the bleached groups, which did not differ from each other (p = 0.004). Adhesive failure was the predominant fracture pattern with no significant differences among the groups (p = 0.203).ConclusionTreatments with arginine or spray-dried chitosan did not differ from the control bleaching group and did not reduce adhesive failures. Neither solution effectively mitigated the immediate reduction in bond strength following bleaching.

Introduction

Dental bleaching is a widely adopted technique, largely due to its effectiveness, affordability, and aesthetic benefits in managing teeth with color alterations.1,2 The bleaching procedure involves applying peroxide-based gels to dental surfaces, with high-concentration formulations typically used in-office by dental professionals and lower concentrations recommended for at-home application.3,4 The active agent presents in these gels, the hydrogen peroxide, possess low molecular weight enabling them to diffuse through the enamel and reach the dentin.5 Once penetrated, the bleaching agents act by cleaving chromogens into smaller, less pigmented molecules, with the potential for complete removal from the dental substrate.5,6

During oxidation-reduction reactions, free radicals generated from the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide persist within the dental tissues.2–4,6 Factors such as the presence of these free radicals, the pH of the bleaching gel, and the application time have been associated with several adverse effects, including tooth sensitivity;1,7 alterations in enamel surface morphology and microhardness;8–10 increased dental permeability;11 changes in the physicochemical properties of the tooth structure;12,13 and reduced bond strength.4,14 The decrease in bond strength is primarily attributed to enamel surface alterations, pore formation, and the entrapment of residual oxygen, which interferes with the polymerization of adhesive systems.4,14 Consequently, an extended interval is required before performing restorative procedures.4

In response to these challenges, several strategies have been proposed to mitigate the negative effects of bleaching on adhesion.3,4 Among these, the application of antioxidants immediately after the bleaching procedure aims to accelerate the elimination of residual oxygen within the dental substrate.4 By reversing the oxidative effects, antioxidants can enhance the bond strength to dental tissues, thereby facilitating the subsequent adhesion of restorative materials.4 In this context, natural antioxidants act as free radical stabilizers, potentially enabling earlier initiation of restorative interventions.4 Examples of such agents include catalase, sodium ascorbate, proanthocyanidins (grape seed extract), green tea, and lycopene.4,15 However, the clinical validation of these agents remains limited, and other biomaterials and amino acids commonly used in dentistry may also contribute to free radical neutralization and bond strength improvement.

Arginine is an essential amino acid that remains stable in aqueous solutions and exhibits an alkaline pH, allowing for the physical obliteration of dentinal tubules through calcium and phosphate deposition and precipitation.16 Although the isolated effects of arginine on dentin targeted for restorations are described,16,17 beyond tubule occlusion,18 this amino acid may also protect the tooth against mineral loss and help reverse erosive or demineralizing processes, especially when used in combination with fluoride.19 Additionally, arginine has been suggested to reduce hydroxyl radicals formation20 and may therefore act as an effective free radical scavenger.21 Whether applied as a solution or incorporated into dentifrices, arginine has shown promising results in mitigating the adverse effects associated with dental bleaching.8,12,22 However, to date, no studies have investigated its potential to reverse the deleterious effects of bleaching agents on adhesive performance.

Concurrently, chitosan is a biopolymer derived from chitin, which is obtained through the partial deacetylation of this naturally abundant polysaccharide found in the exoskeleton of invertebrates.23,24 Its key properties include biocompatibility and biodegradability,23,25 and it has also been reported to exhibit antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activities.24,25 The functionality of chitosan varies considerably depending on its formulation and molecular weight,26 with low-molecular weight chitosan demonstrating greater antioxidant potential compared to its high-molecular weight counterpart.27 Among the techniques used to solubilize chitosan, ionic crosslinking and spray-drying28,29 has been proposed for the production of microparticles, which may enhance its bioavailability.24,28 However, no studies to date have examined the potential antioxidant effect of spray-dried chitosan when applied to bleached enamel.

The development of materials and clinical protocols capable of mitigating the adverse effects of tooth bleaching is clinically relevant, particularly for subsequent adhesive procedures involving enamel and dentin. The waiting period commonly recommended before definitive restorative treatment may interfere with treatment continuity and patient adherence, as patients may not return for follow-up or may discontinue treatment during this period. This limitation becomes particularly relevant when existing restorations exhibit a noticeable color mismatch with the newly bleached tooth structure, potentially resulting in an unfavorable esthetic condition until definitive restorative treatment can be performed. Based on this rationale, the application of arginine and spray-dried chitosan could offer beneficial effects on the immediate bond strength of bleached enamel. Therefore, the objective of this in vitro study was to evaluate the influence of arginine and spray-dried chitosan application on the bond strength of bleached bovine enamel. Specifically, the null hypothesis tested was that no significant differences would be observed in bond strength or failure mode among groups treated or not treated with different antioxidant agents.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study followed a randomized experimental design, with enamel pre-treatment as independent variable, divided into four levels: Negative control (no bleaching);Positive control – dental bleaching with 35% hydrogen peroxide (Whiteness HP, FGM);Dental bleaching and treatment with 8% arginine;Dental bleaching and treatment with 2.5% spray-dried chitosan.

The experimental units consisted of bovine enamel specimens (n = 12), with two resin cylinders independently fabricated on the surface of each enamel specimen. The sample size was determined based on an effect size recommended by Zieliński and Więckiewicz (2025)30 for studies categorized as “treatment”. Using the software G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany) and considering a statistical power of 80%, a significance level of 5%, and four experimental groups, the minimum required sample size was estimated at 12 specimens per group. The response variables were microshear bond strength (MPa) and failure mode, classified as adhesive, cohesive within resin, cohesive within enamel or mixed.

Specimen Preparation

Following approval by the local Animal Ethics Committee (Comissão de Ética no Uso de Animais da Faculdade São Leopoldo Mandic; process #2023/32), forty-eight bovine incisors were obtained from a commercial slaughterhouse, where they would otherwise have been discarded. As the bovine teeth were obtained from a commercial slaughterhouse, the method of euthanasia was not controlled by the researchers. However, all procedures were performed in accordance with Brazilian governmental standards and regulations. The study was conducted in accordance with Brazilian Law No. 11,794/2008, Decree No. 6899/2009, and the guidelines established by the National Council for the Control of Animal Experimentation (CONCEA). The teeth were cleaned and frozen and stored for no longer than five months before use. The crowns were separated from the roots using a diamond disc mounted on a low-speed handpiece (NSK, Tokyo, Japan). Bovine enamel blocks (5 x 5×3 mm) were sectioned from the middle third of the buccal surfaces using the same diamond disc. After sectioning, the specimens were embedded in polystyrene resin (Piraglass, Piracicaba, SP, Brazil) to facilitate polishing and microshear bond strength testing. Enamel surfaces were then flattened and polished for 15 seconds using an automated polishing machine (Arotec, Cotia, SP, Brazil) with #600 and #1200 silicon carbide papers. Finally, the specimens were sonicated (Marconi, Piracicaba, SP, Brazil) for 10 minutes to remove any residual abrasive particles.

The concentrations of arginine (8%) and chitosan (2.5%) were selected based on previous studies investigating these agents in different dental applications, formulations and vehicles, including dentifrices and gels, for instance.8,12,16,32,33

Throughout the experiment, all specimens were stored in a remineralizing solution proposed by Featherstone et al,34 and modified by Serra and Cury,35 containing 1.5 mM Ca, 50 mM KCl, 0.9 mM PO4, and 20 mM Tris buffer (pH = 7).

Adhesive Procedures, Microshear Bond Strength Test and Failure Mode Analysis

The universal adhesive system Scotchbond Universal Plus (3M, St. Paul, MN, USA) was applied considering the manufacturer’s instructions (Table 1), with selective enamel etching using 37% phosphoric acid for 30 seconds.31 Light curing was performed using an LED unit (VALO, Ultradent, South Jordan, UT, USA) at an irradiance of 1000 mW/cm2 for 10 seconds. After adhesive application, two Tygon tubes (1 x 1 mm; diameter x height) were positioned on each enamel surface, and a single increment of nanofilled resin composite (Z350 XT, shade A2; 3M, St. Paul, MN, USA) was inserted into each tube. Using the same LED device, light curing was performed for 20 seconds. Details of all materials used are presented in Table 1.

After 24 hours of storage in distilled water at 37°C, the restored specimens were subjected to microshear bond strength testing using a universal testing machine (EZ Test, EZS; Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min. The values obtained (Kgf) and the restoration diameter were used to calculate the bond strength (load/area; MPa). The bond strength values obtained from the two restorations within each specimen were then averaged, and the resulting mean value was considered for statistical analysis.

The fractured surfaces were visually examined under a stereomicroscope (EK3S3, Eikonal Equip. Ópticos e Analíticos, São Paulo, SP, Brazil), at a 30× magnification to classify the failure mode as follows: adhesive (failure at the adhesive interface); cohesive within enamel (failure restricted to enamel, with the adhesive interface preserved); cohesive within resin (failure occurring within the resin composite restoration, with the adhesive interface preserved) and mixed (failure involving the adhesive interface and either enamel and/or the resin composite restoration).

Scanning Electron Microscopy Analysis

Representative specimens corresponding to the predominant failure mode of each group were selected and prepared for scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JEOL JSM 5600 PV, Tokyo, Japan) at 15kV and 50× magnification. Additionally, for characterization purposes, the arginine and the spray-dried chitosan particles were examined under SEM at magnifications that best illustrated the morphological features, and measured with the ImageJ software (ImageJ, US National Institutes of Health; Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical Analysis

After verification of normality and homoscedasticity, microshear bond strength data were analyzed using generalized linear models with a gamma distribution and log link function. Pairwise comparisons of means were performed using Wald tests with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. Failure mode data were evaluated using the chi-square test (excluding premature failures) and Fisher’s exact test (including premature failures). All analyses were performed using the R program (R Core Team, 2024, Vienna, Austria), with the significance level set at 5%.

Discussion

A satisfactory bond strength between the restorative material and dental enamel is essential for achieving effective and long-lasting restorations.14 However, after dental bleaching, this adhesion may be significantly reduced due to the presence of free radicals generated from the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, which remain within the dental tissues.4,31 The present study investigated strategies to mitigate these adverse effects, specifically the use of arginine and chitosan to accelerate the elimination of residual oxygen from the dental structure.4 The null hypothesis-predicting no differences among the groups-was tested and rejected. Notably, only the non-bleached group demonstrated statistically higher bond strength values compared with the experimental groups. This finding aligns with previous studies indicating that residual oxygen remaining after bleaching procedures compromises adhesive performance.4,14

The use of antioxidants and other bioactive agents has been explored as a strategy to reduce the waiting period required before restorative procedures can be safely performed after bleaching,4 particularly due to their potential to enhance the stability of the bond between resin composites and dental substrates.23 However, in the present study, pretreatments with arginine and spray-dried chitosan were not effective in reestablishing the immediate bond strength compromised by the bleaching process.

Similarly, previous research reported that the use of arginine as a dentin pretreatment did not improve either the immediate or the long-term bond strength (after six-months) when combined with different adhesive systems.16 Although it has been suggested that arginine could reduce the formation of hydroxyl radicals,20 act as a free radical scavenging,21 or minimize deleterious effects during in-office bleaching when incorporated into dentifrices,12 the arginine-based solution applied in this study did not enhance an increase nor preserve the immediate bond strength of bleached enamel. This finding may be related to the low pH of the adhesive system used,36 which could have promoted the solubilization and removal of arginine from the enamel surface.37 Additionally, as observed under SEM analysis, arginine particles presented as irregular macroparticles, a morphology that may have hindered their effective adsorption onto the enamel surface.

Regarding the other experimental agent, chitosan is a biopolymer widely recognized for its biocompatibility and biodegradability25 and can be obtained through methods such as ionic crosslinking or spray-drying.28,29 Although the chitosan solution used in this study did not prevent the reduction in bond strength, the synthesis method-confirmed by SEM images-successfully produced small, uniformly distributed particles, which may enhance the material’s bioavailability for various applications. Chitosan has been employed in multiple purposes in dentistry,23 yet its effectiveness depends on factors such as molecular weight, the vehicle or solvent used for its dissolution, degree of hydrophilicity, and chemical interactions with the target substrate.23,26,27,38 Moreover, similar to arginine, any potential antioxidant activity cannot be completely dismissed, as the adhesive system employed may have inactivated its active properties.36,37

Although enamel bond strength recovery after bleaching is known to be time-dependent,39 the immediate time point was intentionally selected because the primary aim of this study was to determine whether the pretreatment agents could enable immediate adhesive restoration after bleaching, thereby avoiding the waiting period traditionally recommended before restorative procedures. Nevertheless, the recovery of bond strength over longer periods with the tested pretreatment agents was not investigated and should be addressed in future studies. In addition, although the present study was specifically designed to investigate the immediate effect of the selected pretreatment agents, the inclusion of an additional positive control, such as sodium ascorbate, could have provided a direct comparison with an established antioxidant known to promote bond strength recovery,4,40 thereby providing an additional reference for interpreting the effects of the experimental agents and strengthening the assessment of the sensitivity of the experimental model to detect improvements in adhesion following bleaching.

The relevance of the present approach relies on the potential of arginine and chitosan to interact with dental tissues through distinct mechanisms while being investigated as independent solutions rather than as components of commercially available formulations. An 8% arginine concentration was selected based on its established use in dentifrices and reported effects on dentin permeability, mineral loss, and adhesive performance.8,12,41,42 Likewise, 2.5% chitosan was selected based on evidence of favorable effects of similar concentrations on enamel mineral loss and resin-dentin interface durability,32,33,43 as well as evidence of protective effects during tooth bleaching.44–47 More recently, chitosan has also shown potential to preserve adhesive performance following bleaching.48 Importantly, the present study extends this evidence by investigating spray-dried chitosan as an independent 2.5% solution, a formulation not previously investigated in this specific context. Although no significant effects were observed under the conditions tested, the present findings do not preclude potential effects at other concentrations or application protocols, warranting further investigation of these compounds under different experimental conditions.

The present findings suggest that the short-term application of arginine or spray-dried chitosan, under the protocols evaluated, is unlikely to restore the immediate bond strength of bleached enamel. Nevertheless, the rationale for investigating these agents remains supported by previous evidence reporting beneficial effects of arginine and chitosan on dental tissues and on bleaching-related outcomes.8,12,45–47,49,50 Therefore, the absence of an immediate effect on bond strength should not preclude further investigation of these compounds. Future studies should evaluate different concentrations and formulations, as well as the incorporation of cross-linking agents or catalysts to enhance their interaction. Further research should also investigate their effects on bleached dentin, alternative delivery vehicles, and modified application protocols, including different application times and intervals between bleaching and adhesive procedures. Such approaches may help determine whether arginine- or chitosan-based strategies could provide clinically relevant benefits under different experimental conditions.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, neither arginine nor spray-dried chitosan pretreatment restored the immediate bond strength to bleached enamel.
