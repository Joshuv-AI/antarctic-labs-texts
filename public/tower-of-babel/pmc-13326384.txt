Is negative coping necessarily "bad"? State anxiety and college student adjustment: a three-way interaction model
Authors: Ji Yang, Minglu Zheng, Tianmu Liu, Xuejun Xie, Feng Zhen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe transition to university life presents significant developmental challenges, and state anxiety has been widely recognized as a key factor undermining college student adjustment. However, the psychological mechanisms through which anxiety relates to adjustment remain insufficiently understood. From a resource-regulation perspective, this study investigates how positive and negative coping moderate the anxiety-adjustment relationship. We also examine whether perceived social support further conditions these moderating effects.MethodsA total of 1105 college students were surveyed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire, Perceived Social Support Scale, and Chinese College Student Adjustment Scale.ResultsState anxiety was negatively associated with college adjustment. Both positive coping and negative coping significantly moderated this relationship, and these moderating effects were further conditioned by perceived social support. Specifically, negative coping weakened the negative association between anxiety and adjustment primarily among students with high levels of perceived social support. For positive coping, the combination of high positive coping and low perceived social support was associated with the weakest negative association between anxiety and adjustment.ConclusionThese findings indicate that coping strategies and perceived social support jointly shape the association between anxiety and adjustment. Notably, individuals with high positive coping maintain relatively good adjustment even under low social support, suggesting greater reliance on internal regulatory resources. In contrast, negative coping is not inherently beneficial but may serve a context-dependent regulatory function under supportive conditions. Overall, this study provides a more nuanced understanding of college student adjustment and informs targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04708-x.Keywords: State anxiety, College student adjustment, Coping styles, Perceived social support, Three-way interaction effect

Introduction

Adjustment to university life represents a critical developmental challenge for students, demanding the integration of emotional, cognitive, and behavioral resources to meet the new academic, social, and personal demands [19]. This adjustment has been linked to positive academic performance, psychological well-being, and long-term career success [1, 8]. However, modern university students face substantial stress from academic pressures, social transitions, and rapidly changing societal contexts, all of which complicate their adjustment [17]. Understanding the psychological mechanisms underlying college student adjustment therefore remains an important research priority.

State anxiety, a prevalent emotional response during the transition to university, plays a key role in college student adjustment. From an evolutionary perspective, anxiety serves as an adaptive alarm system, alerting individuals to potential threats in the environment [26]. However, prolonged or excessive anxiety may impair adaptive functioning by depleting cognitive and emotional resources [20, 22]. In this context, the impact of anxiety on adjustment depends not only on its intensity but also on the availability of coping resources.

Theories of stress and coping emphasize that coping styles moderate the relationship between anxiety and adjustment. Coping refers to the cognitive and behavioral strategies individuals use to manage stress [15]. Coping strategies, such as problem-solving and cognitive reappraisal, are associated with better adjustment, while avoidance and emotional suppression (negative coping) are typically linked to poorer outcomes [3, 16]. However, coping strategies are not inherently positive or negative; Indeed, their effectiveness is context-dependent [14]. For instance, empirical research has found that in situations of high stress, emotion-focused or avoidant coping can offer short-term relief by preventing resource depletion [31, 33].

According to Folkman and Moskowitz [15], coping effectiveness is determined not only by the strategy itself but also by the broader resource environment in which it is enacted. Therefore, social support constitutes another crucial factor influencing college student adjustment. Perceived social support, which refers to individuals' subjective evaluations of the availability and adequacy of support from their social networks [37], can replenish emotional resources and buffer the negative effects of stress [18]. Perceived support has been shown to enhance emotional regulation and social adjustment [10, 36]. However, the relationship between social support and coping is complex: while individuals with strong coping resources may rely less on external support, those with weaker coping mechanisms benefit more from supportive environments (e.g., emotional reassurance, informational guidance, and instrumental assistance) [5, 25]. Therefore, perceived social support is expected to condition the moderating role of coping styles in the relationship between state anxiety and college student adjustment, rather than functioning solely as an independent moderator.

In light of these theoretical perspectives, the present study proposes an integrative framework that conceptualizes state anxiety as both an adaptive signal of potential environmental threats and a factor associated with the consumption of individuals' cognitive and emotional regulatory resources. Importantly, state anxiety is expected to have a direct and robust association with college student adjustment, as supported by prior research. However, the strength of this association may vary depending on the availability and configuration of regulatory resources. Coping styles and perceived social support are conceptualized as two complementary forms of regulatory resources. Coping styles represent internal regulatory mechanisms that shape how individuals allocate and utilize their limited cognitive and emotional resources when responding to stress (e.g., engagement vs. disengagement strategies). In contrast, perceived social support reflects an external resource context that can buffer resource depletion and facilitate recovery.

From this perspective, the relationship between state anxiety and college student adjustment is not uniform but conditional upon the interaction between internal coping strategies and external social support. Specifically, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1: State anxiety is negatively associated with college student adjustment.

H1: State anxiety is negatively associated with college student adjustment.

H2: Positive and negative coping moderate the association between state anxiety and adjustment.

H2: Positive and negative coping moderate the association between state anxiety and adjustment.

H3: Perceived social support moderates the moderating effects of positive and negative coping on the relationship between state anxiety and adjustment.

H3: Perceived social support moderates the moderating effects of positive and negative coping on the relationship between state anxiety and adjustment.

By clarifying how internal coping resources and external social resources jointly shape the anxiety-adjustment relationship, this study aims to contribute to a more nuanced understanding of adaptive processes in university contexts and to inform the development of resource-sensitive psychological interventions.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 1186 questionnaires were distributed through an online survey across four universities in Jiangxi, Guangdong, and Shanxi provinces in China, and 1105 valid responses were collected, resulting in an effective response rate of 93.17%. Participants received a participation.

Fee of 5 RMB (approximately US$0.77) after completing the questionnaire. Invalid questionnaires were excluded based on two criteria: (1) responses to Instructed Response Items (e.g., Please select "Strongly Agree" for this question.) that were not answered correctly, and (2) surveys with unusually short or long completion times, indicating insufficient attention to the questionnaire.

The average age of participants was 21.34 ± 2.91 years. Among them 479 were male (43.35%), 626 were female (56.65%); 936 were undergraduates (84.71%), 169 were postgraduate students or above (15.29%); 449 were only children (40.63%), 656 were non-only children (59.37%); 529 were from urban areas (47.87%), and 576 were from rural areas (52.13%).

Measures

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI)

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) was developed by Spielberger [29], consisting of 40 items and two subscales: the State Anxiety Inventory (S-AI) and the Trait Anxiety Inventory (T-AI). This study utilized the state anxiety subscale, which contains 20 items (e.g., "I am tense"). The scale is scored using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 4 = very much so), with higher scores indicating a more pronounced state of anxiety at the present time. The internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach's α) used in this study was 0.90.

Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ)

The Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire, compiled by Xie [35], was employed. This questionnaire was developed by adapting and simplifying existing Western coping measures (e.g., Ways of Coping Questionnaire) to better fit the Chinese cultural context. It consists of 20 items, containing two dimensions: positive coping and negative coping. Positive coping reflects active, adaptive strategies when facing stress, such as problem-solving, active distraction, and cognitive reappraisal (e.g., "I try to distract myself by working, studying, or other activities"). Negative coping refers to passive or avoidant responses, including avoidance, wishful thinking, substance use, and dependence on others (e.g., "I rely on others to solve the problem"). A 4-point Likert scale was employed, with higher scores indicating more frequent use of that coping style. In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficients were 0.85 for the positive coping subscale and 0.78 for the negative coping subscale.

Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS)

The Perceived Social Support Scale, developed by Zimet et al. [37], was used. It consists of 12 items, containing three dimensions: family support, friend support, and other support (e.g., "My family really tries to help me"). A 7-point Likert scale was employed, with higher scores indicating higher levels of perceived social support. In this study, the Cronbach's α for the overall scale was 0.92, and the coefficients for each subdimension ranged from 0.85 to 0.86.

Chinese College Student Adjustment Scale (CCSAS)

The Chinese College Student Adjustment Scale, compiled by Fang et al. [12], was used. It consists of 60 items, containing seven dimensions: interpersonal relationship adjustment, learning adjustment, campus life adjustment, career adjustment, emotional adjustment, self-adjustment, and satisfaction (e.g., "I am very satisfied with my current college life"). A 5-point Likert scale was employed, with higher scores indicating better environmental adjustment ability. In this study, the Cronbach's α for the overall scale was 0.92, and the coefficients for each subdimension ranged from 0.60 to 0.79.

Data analysis

All scores reported for the scales in this study are mean scores of the individual items. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0), while moderation and three-way interaction analyses were conducted with Hayes' PROCESS macro (Version 5.0). Specifically, Model 1 was used to test the moderating effects of positive and negative coping styles on the relationship between state anxiety and adjustment. Model 3 was applied to examine the three-way interaction among state anxiety, coping styles, and perceived social support.

Prior to the regression analyses, all continuous variables were mean-centered to reduce multicollinearity and facilitate the interpretation of interaction effects. Demographic variables, including gender, age, academic level, and place of residence, were entered as covariates to control for their potential confounding effects. These variables were included as covariates because prior research has shown that demographic characteristics such as gender, age, and academic level may be associated with anxiety levels and student adjustment outcomes among college students.

Bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was employed to estimate confidence intervals for interaction effects. Effects were considered statistically significant when the 95% bias-corrected confidence interval did not include zero. The significance level was set at p ≤ 0.05. Simple slope analyses and conditional effect analyses were conducted to further interpret significant interaction effects at high and low levels (± 1 SD) of the moderator variables.

Results

Preliminary analyses

Harman's single-factor test was used to assess common method bias. An unrotated exploratory factor analysis was conducted on all items of the four research variables. The results showed that 17 factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, and the first factor explained 21.82% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. Therefore, it can be concluded that there was no significant common method bias in this study.

To further verify the absence of severe common method bias, we adopted the unmeasured latent method construct (ULMC) approach. We compared the baseline model (χ2 = 553, df = 90, χ2/df = 6.14, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.07, SRMR = 0.04) with the ULMC model in which a latent method factor was added to all items (χ2 = 1123.95, df = 85, χ2/df = 13.22, CFI = 0.88, TLI = 0.83, RMSEA = 0.11, SRMR = 0.07). Notably, adding the latent method factor resulted in worse model fit rather than an improvement, and all changes in fit indices were below the critical thresholds. Taken together, these findings confirmed that common method bias did not pose a serious threat to the present study.

To examine potential multicollinearity among the predictors, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for all variables included in the regression models. VIF values were obtained using ordinary least squares regression analyses in SPSS. The results indicated that all VIF values ranged from 1.27 to 1.79, which were well below the commonly accepted threshold of 5.0. These findings suggest that multicollinearity was not a serious concern in the present study.

Model testing

Testing the moderating effects

First, the direct effect of state anxiety on college student adjustment was examined (Model 0). Results indicated a significant negative association (β = –0.54, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [–0.58, –0.50]). Model 1 in the PROCESS macro (Version 5.0) was used to test the moderating effects of positive and negative coping styles on the relationship between state anxiety and adjustment. After controlling for demographic variables, the results showed that the interaction terms between state anxiety and both positive coping (β = 0.08, p < 0.05, 95% CI = [0.01, 0.15]) and negative coping (β = 0.26, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [0.19, 0.33]) were significantly associated with adjustment. The Johnson–Neyman technique was used to probe the interaction effects. The results indicated that the association between state anxiety and adjustment remained significant across the observed range of positive coping. For negative coping, the association was significant when negative coping was below 1.53, representing 99.19% of the observed sample values.

Testing the three-way interaction moderating effects

Further analysis using Model 3 in the PROCESS macro tested the three-way interaction effect among state anxiety, positive coping, and perceived social support. The results indicated that the three-way interaction term was significantly associated with adjustment (β = –0.07, p < 0.05, 95% CI = [–0.13, –0.01]), as detailed in Table 2.

Discussion

The present study investigated the interactive roles of state anxiety, coping styles, and perceived social support in college student adjustment from a resource-regulation perspective. By integrating evolutionary, transactional, and resource-based perspectives, this research aimed to clarify how anxiety-related resource depletion and regulatory mechanisms jointly shape adaptive functioning. Overall, the findings support the proposed three-way interaction model and demonstrate that the effects of emotional states on college student adjustment depend on both coping strategies and social contexts.

State anxiety and college student adjustment

The current study substantiates the hypothesis that state anxiety is negatively associated with college student adjustment. This result aligns with prior research indicating that heightened anxiety is linked to poorer academic, social, and emotional functioning [21, 34]. From an evolutionary perspective, anxiety functions as an early warning signal that promotes vigilance toward potential threats [26]. However, excessive or prolonged activation of state anxiety may impair adaptive performance.

From a cognitive-resource perspective, the repetitive worry triggered by state anxiety occupies working memory capacity, consuming limited cognitive resources. This leads to attentional fixation on threatening stimuli (e.g., risks of academic failure), reducing cognitive resources available for adapting to new environments, thereby impairing an individual's adaptive regulatory capacity [2, 22]. Moreover, sustained emotional regulation efforts associated with anxiety may accelerate psychological depletion, limiting tolerance for stress and increasing vulnerability to maladjustment [11]. Although causal direction cannot be established, these mechanisms together suggest that state anxiety may impair adjustment by depleting cognitive and emotional regulatory resources. The present findings extend this literature by demonstrating that such detrimental associations remain robust even after controlling for individual differences and contextual factors.

Moderating role of positive coping

In line with the study's hypothesis, positive coping moderated the association between state anxiety and college student adjustment. Specifically, higher levels of positive coping attenuated the negative relationship between anxiety and adjustment. This finding is consistent with prior research suggesting that problem-solving, cognitive reappraisal, and other adaptive strategies can enhance perceived control and facilitate effective resource utilization under stress [13, 16].

From a stress and coping perspective, positive coping reflects active engagement with stressors and efforts to regulate emotional response effectively [15]. These strategies are associated with increased self-efficacy and a greater sense of control, which are critical for maintaining adaptive functioning in challenging environments [30]. When individuals experience state anxiety, the use of positive coping may help redirect attention from threat-related stimuli toward goal-relevant information, thereby reducing cognitive interference and preserving regulatory resources [2].

Moreover, evidence from student populations suggests that effective coping and supportive regulatory processes are associated with improved emotional regulation and psychological well-being [10]. From a resource-regulation perspective, positive coping can thus be understood as an internal regulatory resource that facilitates efficient allocation and recovery of cognitive and emotional resources. Accordingly, students who frequently employ positive coping strategies may be better able to maintain adaptive functioning under conditions of elevated anxiety.

Moderating role of negative coping

The present study found that negative coping significantly moderated the association between state anxiety and college student adjustment, such that higher levels of negative coping were associated with a weaker negative relationship between anxiety and adjustment. This pattern was most evident under high perceived social support, suggesting that the adaptiveness of negative coping—if any—is highly context-dependent.​

From a stress-regulation perspective, avoidant and disengagement-oriented strategies may​ temporarily reduce emotional arousal by limiting direct engagement with stressors [15]. When state anxiety is high, such strategies could be interpreted as serving a short-term regulatory function, possibly preventing further escalation of distress in the moment. From a resource-based perspectives, state anxiety consumes substantial cognitive and emotional resources, leading to attentional narrowing and reduced regulatory capacity [2, 22]. Temporary withdrawal might help conserve limited cognitive resources under acute anxiety, though this remains a speculative interpretation given the cross-sectional design.​

Furthermore, coping flexibility frameworks suggest that individuals dynamically adjust their coping strategies based on situational demands [7, 31]. Within this framework, the observed pattern might reflect that negative coping operates as a provisional response under high-anxiety conditions, particularly when external support is available. However, it is important to emphasize that this does not imply negative coping is generally beneficial; prolonged reliance on avoidance may still undermine long-term adjustment, and our data cannot establish temporal precedence or causality.

Conditional effects of perceived social support

The present findings indicate that the role of perceived social support in the relationship between anxiety and adjustment is conditional upon individuals' coping strategies. Rather than exerting a uniformly protective effect, social support appears to function differently depending on the availability of internal regulatory resources.

Specifically, the present study found that individuals with high levels of positive coping maintained relatively favorable adjustment even under conditions of low perceived social support, suggests that positive coping may function as a robust internal regulatory resource, enabling individuals to manage stress effectively without relying heavily on external support. This interpretation is consistent with prior research indicating that individuals with strong coping competencies demonstrate greater self-efficacy and regulatory flexibility [7, 4].

Importantly, this finding can be further understood through the lens of Self-Determination Theory [28], which emphasizes the role of autonomy in psychological functioning. Individuals with strong positive coping skills may engage in more autonomous self-regulation, thereby maintaining adaptive functioning even in less supportive environments. In such cases, external support may become less critical for maintaining adjustment. In addition, the matching hypothesis of social support [9] provides further explanatory insight. This perspective suggests that the effectiveness of social support depends on the extent to which it aligns with individuals' needs. For individuals with strong internal coping resources, externally provided support may offer limited additional benefit, particularly if it does not match their regulatory demands.

In contrast, For students who predominantly employed negative coping strategies, high perceived social support substantially weakened the negative association between state anxiety and adjustment. This finding suggests that the adaptiveness of negative coping may depend critically on the broader resource context in which it is embedded.

From the perspective of the stress-buffering hypothesis [6], social support may mitigate the adverse effects of anxiety by providing emotional reassurance, informational guidance, and instrumental assistance. These external resources may compensate for the limitations of disengagement-oriented coping, thereby enabling individuals to maintain relatively adaptive functioning despite less effective internal regulation. From a resource-regulation perspective [19], social support may compensate for the limitations of maladaptive coping strategies by replenishing depleted resources and facilitating recovery. In this sense, negative coping may function as a provisional or transitional strategy, allowing individuals to temporarily withdraw from stressors while relying on external support systems to maintain overall functioning.

Nevertheless, it is important to interpret these findings with caution. The buffering role of social support does not imply that negative coping is inherently beneficial. Prolonged reliance on avoidance or disengagement may still hinder problem resolution and increase the risk of long-term maladjustment [3]. Therefore, the observed protective effect of negative coping should be understood as context-dependent and potentially time-limited.

Theoretical and practical implications

The present study provides a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between state anxiety and college student adjustment by demonstrating that this association is conditional rather than uniform. Specifically, positive and negative coping moderate this relationship, and that these moderating effects are further conditioned by perceived social support.

These results suggest that the effectiveness of coping strategies depends on both individual and contextual factors. Positive coping appears to play a relatively consistent buffering role, whereas the function of negative coping is more context-dependent. In particular, negative coping may attenuate the negative association between anxiety and adjustment under conditions of high perceived social support, highlighting the importance of external resources in shaping coping effectiveness.

From a practical perspective, these findings underscore the need for more targeted interventions in university settings. Enhancing positive coping strategies may help students better manage anxiety and maintain adjustment. At the same time, increasing perceived social support may be especially beneficial for students who rely more on negative coping strategies, as supportive environments can help mitigate the negative effects of anxiety. Overall, intervention efforts should consider both individual coping tendencies and the availability of social resources, rather than adopting uniform approaches.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationships among anxiety, coping, social support, and adjustment. Longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify temporal dynamics and directional effects. Second, reliance on self-report measures may introduce response biases, despite statistical controls for common method variance. Future research should incorporate multi-method approaches, including behavioral and physiological indicators. Third, the sample consisted exclusively of Chinese college students, which may limit generalizability to other cultural contexts. Cultural norms regarding emotional expression, help-seeking, and social interdependence may influence coping patterns and support utilization. Cross-cultural comparative studies are therefore warranted. Future research may also explore potential mediating mechanisms, such as emotion regulation efficacy, cognitive appraisal processes, and resilience, to further clarify how coping strategies and perceived social support jointly influence adjustment. Additionally, examining domain-specific stressors, such as academic versus interpersonal challenges, may provide more fine-grained insights into adaptive processes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that state anxiety is negatively associated with college student adjustment, yet this relationship is moderated in nuanced ways by positive and negative coping styles and perceived social support. Notably, individuals with high positive coping maintained relatively favorable adjustment even under low perceived social support, suggesting greater reliance on internal regulatory resources. In contrast, negative coping attenuated the negative association between anxiety and adjustment primarily among students with high levels of perceived social support, indicating a compensatory role of external resources when internal coping is less adaptive. These findings underscore the complex and context-dependent nature of the adaptation process. They emphasize the need for a more differentiated and resource-sensitive approach in supporting college students, one that goes beyond simplistic coping categorizations and leverages both internal coping flexibility and external social resources to foster psychological well-being and successful adjustment.
