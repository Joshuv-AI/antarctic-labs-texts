Euclidean coordinate-space perturbation theory with a single mass scale Preprint: CERN-TH-2025-239, MITP-25-074
Christoph L. Schröder, Harvey B. Meyer

Abstract
We develop elements of coordinate-space perturbation theory for massive quantum field theories in general d d -dimensional Euclidean space. Using the expansion in Gegenbauer polynomials, we provide analytic expressions for several three-point correlation functions in theories with one massive and one massless field. To this end, a class of antiderivatives of products of two Bessel functions multiplied by a power of their common argument are studied systematically. We expect these results to be useful in perturbative calculations involving vertices of high degree, at finite temperature and/or in finite volume, as well as in auxiliary perturbative computations for a lattice QCD treatment of hadronic effects in precision observables. As an illustration, we compute the one-loop coordinate-space propagator of a massive particle coupled to a massless one, both in the vacuum and at finite temperature.



1
Introduction

In a seminal paper published in 1980, Chetyrkin, Kataev and Tkachov developed the Gegenbauer-polynomial coordinate-space technique
Chetyrkin:1980pr
for computing dimensionally regularized massless Feynman diagrams.
In this paper, we proceed to generalize the technique to diagrams with massive propagators. More precisely, most of the examples considered in the following consist of a number of propagators with mass
m
and others that are massless, a type of kinematics we refer to as ‘QED-like’. A necessary ingredient in these calculations, the Gegenbauer expansion of the massive scalar propagator was already given in
Groote:2005ay
.
As compared to the original publication
Chetyrkin:1980pr
, in which multi-loop momentum-space amplitudes are computed with coordinate-space techniques, we focus more strongly on intrinsically coordinate-space amplitudes.
As a typical application, the Gegenbauer-polynomial technique has been used
Asmussen:2022oql
in
d=4
to compute the QED part of the diagrams yielding the hadronic light-by-light contribution to the muon
(g-2)
.
A second difference in emphasis is that with the massive propagators being Bessel functions, the radial integrals become non-trivial, and we will devote a substantial portion of this article to computing precisely such integrals.

Textbooks on quantum field theory often begin with formulating Feynman rules in position-space perturbation theory, before moving swiftly to the corresponding momentum-space rules. There is a simple reason for that. Recall that in position space, the integration variables correspond to the coordinates of vertices
1

Note that, due to translation invariance, the naive number of required position-space integrals is one less than the number of vertices.
, whereas in momentum space they are the loop momenta. Now, for a connected graph made of
n
-legged vertices, we have the following relation (see e.g.
Weinberg:1995mt
) between the number of loops
L
, the number of vertices
V
and the number of external legs
E
:

(n-2)V=2(L-1)+E.

(1)

For
n=3
, for instance, as in QED and for most vertices in the Standard Model, at high orders the number of vertices grows twice as fast as the number of loops. For contact interactions,
n=4
, the growth rate breaks even, and higher-order vertices favour the position-space approach in terms of the naive number of integrals to be performed. Thus position-space methods have potential in effective field theories with contact interactions involving four or more legs per vertex. Increasing the number of external legs carrying definite momenta, on the other hand, clearly favours the momentum-space methods, as this corresponds to computing Fourier integrals for vertices attached to external legs.
Of course, these simple arguments do not account for the possibility that integrals may factorize or otherwise simplify. In fact, we shall see instances of position-space amplitudes that can be computed and re-used as building blocks in later calculations. In momentum space, too, certain amplitudes are re-usable in more extended diagrams, the simplest example being the self-energy correction to a propagator.

Besides applications in field theories with contact interactions and for evaluating the electroweak parts of Feynman diagrams involving non-perturbative ‘QCD blobs’ to be computed in lattice QCD, perturbative position-space methods have been known for a long time to be useful in calculations performed in a finite volume
Collins:1984xc
;
Luscher:1985dn
, the thermal boundary conditions of the Matsubara formalism being an important special case
Gasser:1987zq
. In high-order perturbative calculations of the QCD free energy, the frequency/spatial-coordinates representation was found to be particularly well suited
Arnold:1994ps
;
Arnold:1994eb
. Indeed the ‘vacuum diagrams’ to be computed, having no external legs (
E=0
), lend themselves naturally to a coordinate-space treatment.

Before proceeding, we briefly mention a few directions tangential to the present work.
(a) Calculations in coordinate space have been used to give a different perspective on the renormalization of a quantum field theory
Freedman:1991tk
. In particular, a central idea in this approach is to give a precise meaning to products of distributions.
(b) Coordinate-space techniques have also found applications in other regularizations. They have been used in lattice perturbation theory calculations
Luscher:1985wf
as a way to reduce the number of numerical sums/integrals to be computed.
(c) The Gegenbauer-polynomial technique has been considered in
d
-dimensional momentum space
Terrano:1980af
.
For
d=4
, the propagator
((p-q)^{2}+m^{2})^{-1}
has a relatively simple expansion in the relevant polynomials
C_{n}^{(1)}(\hat{p}\cdot\hat{q})
,
a fact exploited already in

, when dimensional regularization had not been discovered yet.

Consider then for concreteness a Euclidean quantum field theory with a massive complex scalar field
\phi(x)
and a massless real scalar field
\chi(x)
, interacting via the Yukawa-like Lagrangian term
{\cal L}_{\rm int}=g\chi\phi^{*}\phi

G_{m}(x)

m

An important building block in coordinate-space perturbation theory is the three-point function illustrated at leading order (LO) in

\langle\phi(x)\chi(y)\phi^{*}(0)\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm LO}}{{=}}-g\int d^{d}z\;G_{m}(z)\,G_{0}(y-z)\,G_{m}(x-z).

(2)

Applying the Gegenbauer polynomial expansion method to perform the angular integrals leads to radial integrals of the form (
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\lambda=(d-2)/2
)

\displaystyle\int dz\,z^{n+1}\,K_{\lambda}(mz)\,{\cal D}_{\lambda+n}(mz),

(3)

\displaystyle\int dz\,\frac{1}{z^{2\lambda+n-1}}\,K_{\lambda}(mz)\,{\cal D}_{\lambda+n}(mz),

(4)

where

stands for a modified Bessel function, either of the first or the second kind. The integrals are needed for arbitrary upper or lower bound, in other words the full anti-derivative is needed.
This leads us to study systematically certain classes of anti-derivatives, such as

\int dz\,z^{\ell}\,{\cal D}_{\lambda}(mz)\,\bar{\cal D}_{\lambda+n}(mz),

(5)

with
n,\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
{\cal D},\bar{\cal D}\in\{I,K\}

2
Elements of coordinate-space perturbation theory

We work in
d=2\lambda+2
dimensions.
The volume of the
S_{d-1}
unit sphere, which has codimension one in
d
dimensions, is given by

\Omega_{d}=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}=\frac{2\pi^{\lambda+1}}{\Gamma(\lambda+1)}\;.

(6)

The average of a function
f
of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
over the unit sphere is defined as

\langle f(x)\rangle_{\hat{x}}\equiv\frac{1}{\Omega_{d}}\int d\Omega_{\hat{x}}\;f(x),

(7)

where
d\Omega_{\hat{x}}
is the solid angle element associated with point
\hat{x}
on the unit sphere, where
\hat{x}=x/r
with
r=|x|
. For instance, for
d=3
we have
d\Omega_{\hat{x}}=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi
, where
(r,\theta,\phi)
are the spherical coordinates of point
x
.

2.1
Propagators

The massive propagator solves the
‘screened Poisson equation’

(-\triangle+m^{2})\,G_{m}^{(\lambda)}(x)=\delta^{(d)}(x),

(8)

with
\triangle=\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
denoting the
d
-dimensional Laplacian and
\delta^{(d)}(x)
the Dirac delta distribution.
Explicitly, the propagator reads

G_{m}^{(\lambda)}(x)=\frac{m^{\lambda}}{(2\pi)^{\lambda+1}}\;\frac{K_{\lambda}(m|x|)}{|x|^{\lambda}}\,.

(9)

In the limit
m\to 0
, one obtains the massless propagator

G_{0}^{(\lambda)}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\lambda)}{4\,\pi^{\lambda+1}|x|^{2\lambda}}\,.

(10)

Propagators in
d
and
(d+2)
dimensions are related through differentiation,

-\frac{d}{d(x^{2})}G_{m}^{(\lambda)}(x)=\pi\,G_{m}^{(\lambda+1)}(x).

(11)

Differentiating with respect to the mass, instead, lowers the index
\lambda
by one unit,

-\frac{d}{d(m^{2})}G_{m}^{(\lambda)}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}G_{m}^{(\lambda-1)}(x).

(12)

Exploiting the fact that
K_{-\lambda}(z)=K_{\lambda}(z)
, we also note the formal property

G_{m}^{(-\lambda)}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{2\pi|x|}{m}\right)^{\lambda}K_{\lambda}(m|x|)=(2\pi)^{2\lambda}G_{|x|}^{(\lambda)}(m\,\hat{\epsilon})\,,

(13)

with
\hat{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
an arbitrary unit vector, which interchanges the role of the mass and the distance of propagation.

The propagator of a Dirac fermion reads

\displaystyle S^{(\lambda)}_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-\partial_{\mu}^{(x)}\gamma_{\mu}+m)G_{m}^{(\lambda)}(x)

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{\mu}\gamma_{\mu}\,2\pi\,G_{m}^{(\lambda+1)}(x)+mG_{m}^{(\lambda)}(x).

(15)

2.2
Convolution of two propagators

Here we recall an elementary result on
the convolution of two propagators, illustrated by the diagram in Fig.
2
. It is most easily obtained via the detour into Fourier space, since the convolution then becomes a simple product and we can decompose the latter according to

\frac{1}{k^{2}+m^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{k^{2}+M^{2}}=\frac{1}{M^{2}-m^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}+m^{2}}-\frac{1}{k^{2}+M^{2}}\right).

(16)

Thus one finds

(G^{(\lambda)}_{m}*G_{M}^{(\lambda)})(x)\equiv\int d^{d}y\;G^{(\lambda)}_{m}(x-y)G^{(\lambda)}_{M}(y)=\frac{1}{M^{2}-m^{2}}\left(G^{(\lambda)}_{m}(x)-G^{(\lambda)}_{M}(x)\right).

(17)

The convolution can be obtained for
M=m
as a limiting case. Using Eq. (
12
) in the last step, one finds

(G^{(\lambda)}_{m}*G^{(\lambda)}_{m})(x)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial m^{2}}G^{(\lambda)}_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}G^{(\lambda-1)}_{m}(x).

(18)

An alternative way to derive this result is to note that in a free massive scalar field theory,

-\frac{\partial}{\partial m^{2}}G_{m}^{(\lambda)}(x)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial m^{2}}\langle\phi(x)\phi^{*}(0)\rangle=\int d^{d}y\,\left\langle\phi(x)\phi^{*}(y)\phi(y)\,\phi^{*}(0)\right\rangle_{c}=(G^{(\lambda)}_{m}*G^{(\lambda)}_{m})(x).

2.3
Gegenbauer polynomials

The Gegenbauer polynomials can be defined via the expansion of a generating function,

\frac{1}{(1-2xt+t^{2})^{\lambda}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}^{\lambda}(x)\,t^{n}.

(19)

The first three polynomials read

C_{0}^{\lambda}(x)=1
,

C_{1}^{\lambda}(x)=2\lambda\,x
and

C_{2}^{\lambda}(x)=\lambda(2(\lambda+1)x^{2}-1)
.
The lower index of the polynomial determines whether it is even or odd,

\displaystyle C_{n}^{\lambda}(-x)=(-1)^{n}C_{n}^{\lambda}(x),

(20)

and its overall normalization is such that

C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)=\frac{(2\lambda)_{n}}{n!}\;,

(21)

where
(a)_{n}\equiv\Gamma(a+n)/\Gamma(a)
is the Pochhammer symbol.
In the present context, the most useful property of this set of polynomials is their orthogonality on the
S_{d-1}
unit sphere in
d=2\lambda+2
dimensions,

\displaystyle\Big\langle C_{n}^{\lambda}(\hat{x}\cdot\hat{u})\;C_{\ell}^{\lambda}(\hat{y}\cdot\hat{u})\Big\rangle_{\hat{u}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{n+\lambda}\,\delta_{n\ell}\;C_{n}^{\lambda}(\hat{x}\cdot\hat{y}).

(22)

For
\lambda=1
these polynomials are known as Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind
U_{n}
.

The Gegenbauer expansion of the massive propagator reads
Groote:2005ay

\displaystyle G_{m}^{(\lambda)}(x-y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{(m,\lambda)}_{n}(|x|,|y|)\;C^{(\lambda)}_{n}(\hat{x}\cdot\hat{y})

(23)

\displaystyle\gamma^{(m,\lambda)}_{n}(|x|,|y|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\lambda)(\lambda+n)}{2\,\pi^{\lambda+1}(|x||y|)^{\lambda}}\Big(\theta(|x|-|y|)\,I_{\lambda+n}(m|y|)\,K_{\lambda+n}(m|x|)

(24)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\theta(|y|-|x|)\,I_{\lambda+n}(m|x|)\,K_{\lambda+n}(m|y|)\Big),

a result that goes back to a contribution of the year 1900 by Macdonald according to
Watson’s
classic treatise on Bessel functions
Watson
.
An important aspect of the expansion coefficients is that for
|x|>|y|
, the expression factorizes in a function of
|x|
times a function of
|y|
, and similarly for the opposite ordering.
As a special case, the expansion coefficients for the massless propagator read
Chetyrkin:1980pr

\displaystyle\gamma^{(0,\lambda)}_{n}(|x|,|y|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\lambda)}{4\,\pi^{\lambda+1}}\left[\theta(|x|-|y|)\frac{1}{|x|^{2\lambda}}\left(\frac{|y|}{|x|}\right)^{n}+\theta(|y|-|x|)\frac{1}{|y|^{2\lambda}}\left(\frac{|x|}{|y|}\right)^{n}\right].

(25)

We also note that, from the relation (
18
) between propagators in
d
and
(d+2)
dimensions,
the following expansion is immediately obtained,

\displaystyle G_{m}^{(\lambda-1)}(x-y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Big(-4\pi\frac{\partial}{\partial m^{2}}\gamma_{n}^{(m,\lambda)}(|x|,|y|)\Big)\,C_{n}^{(\lambda)}(\hat{x}\cdot\hat{y}).

(26)

An alternative derivation of the coefficients of
G_{m}^{(\lambda-1)}(x-y)
in the polynomials
C_{n}^{(\lambda)}(\hat{x}\cdot\hat{y})
proceeds via the relation between Gegenbauer polynomials corresponding to
d
and
d-2
dimensions (polynomials of negative degree are defined to vanish here),

\displaystyle C_{n}^{(\lambda-1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda+n-1}\left(C_{n}^{(\lambda)}(x)-C_{n-2}^{(\lambda)}(x)\right).

(27)

In this way, one finds the relation

\displaystyle-4\pi\frac{\partial}{\partial m^{2}}\gamma_{n}^{(m,\lambda)}(|x|,|y|)=(\lambda-1)\left(\frac{\gamma_{n}^{(m,\lambda-1)}(|x|,|y|)}{\lambda+n-1}-\frac{\gamma_{n+2}^{(m,\lambda-1)}(|x|,|y|)}{\lambda+n+1}\right).

(28)

Finally, the following Gegenbauer-polynomial expansion is useful for Fourier transformations,

e^{ik\cdot x}=2^{\lambda}\Gamma(\lambda)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\lambda+n)i^{n}\frac{J_{\lambda+n}(|k||x|)}{(|k||x|)^{\lambda}}C^{(\lambda)}_{n}(\hat{k}\cdot\hat{x}).

(29)

3
Antiderivatives of products of two modified Bessel functions

In the preceding section, we reviewed the fact that in coordinate-space, the massive scalar propagator is mainly given by a modified Bessel function (of the second kind), and the propagator with a difference of two vectors as argument can be written as an expansion with modified Bessel functions in its coefficients. Hence, when evaluating Feynman diagrams in coordinate space, the usual integrations over the vertices, post applying the Gegenbauer expansion and the orthogonality relation of the corresponding polynomials, yield radial integrals with integrands consisting of products of a monomial and two modified Bessel functions. Because the expression for the expansion coefficients depends on which input vector is larger (cf. Eq. (
24
)), the radial integrals have variable integration bounds, i.e. they amount to indefinite integrals. It is hence worthwhile investigating this class of antiderivatives thoroughly, which we will do in this section. In the case of integrals appearing in calculations performed in
d=4
dimensions, a systematic study was presented in
BachelorThesisSchroder
.

3.1
Known results due to Lommel, et al.

In this subsection, we summarize a class of known results for the antiderivatives in question. From Watson
Watson
, two important results attributed to Lommel exist. We have transcribed them from ordinary to modified Bessel function. Watson refers to the first as
Lommel’s first method
, which we shall simply call
Lommel 1
. For modified Bessel functions, it states that

\displaystyle\int\frac{I_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}(z)}{z}\,dz=z\frac{I_{\mu+1}(z)K_{\nu}(z)-I_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu+1}(z)}{\mu^{2}-\nu^{2}}+\frac{I_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}(z)}{\mu+\nu},

(30)

\displaystyle\int\frac{I_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}(z)}{z}\,dz=\frac{z}{\mu^{2}-\nu^{2}}\left(I_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)K_{\nu}(z)-I_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\right),

(31)

\displaystyle\int\frac{I_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)}{z}\,dz=z\frac{I_{\mu+1}(z)I_{\nu}(z)-I_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu+1}(z)}{\mu^{2}-\nu^{2}}+\frac{I_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)}{\mu+\nu},

(32)

\displaystyle\int\frac{I_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)}{z}\,dz=\frac{z}{\mu^{2}-\nu^{2}}\left(I_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)I_{\nu}(z)-I_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\right),

(33)

\displaystyle\int\frac{K_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}(z)}{z}\,dz=-z\frac{K_{\mu+1}(z)K_{\nu}(z)-K_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu+1}(z)}{\mu^{2}-\nu^{2}}+\frac{K_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}(z)}{\mu+\nu},

(34)

\displaystyle\int\frac{K_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}(z)}{z}\,dz=\frac{z}{\mu^{2}-\nu^{2}}\left(K_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)K_{\nu}(z)-K_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\right).

(35)

For equal orders one could evaluate the limit e.g. by L’Hôpital’s rule, but since this involves derivatives with respect to the order, we will see a simpler way to come to a result in a moment.

Watson also includes what he calls
Lommel’s second method
, which we will refer to as
Lommel 2
, and this comprises integral recursion relations that read

\displaystyle(\rho+\mu+\nu)\int z^{\rho-1}I_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle-(\rho-\mu-\nu-2)\int z^{\rho-1}I_{\mu+1}(z)I_{\nu+1}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=z^{\rho}\left(I_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)-I_{\mu+1}(z)I_{\nu+1}(z)\right),

(36)

\displaystyle(\rho+\mu+\nu)\int z^{\rho-1}K_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle-(\rho-\mu-\nu-2)\int z^{\rho-1}K_{\mu+1}(z)K_{\nu+1}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=z^{\rho}\left(K_{\mu}(z)K_{\nu}(z)-K_{\mu+1}(z)K_{\nu+1}(z)\right),

(37)

and finally, for the mixed case,

\displaystyle(\rho+\mu+\nu)\int z^{\rho-1}K_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle+(\rho-\mu-\nu-2)\int z^{\rho-1}K_{\mu+1}(z)I_{\nu+1}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=z^{\rho}\left(K_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)+K_{\mu+1}(z)I_{\nu+1}(z)\right).

(38)

One observes that all these recursion relations collapse in the following two cases, and then present the solution to one antiderivative:
\rho=\mu+\nu+2
and
\rho=-\mu-\nu
. For example, for the mixed case this gives the results

2(\mu+\nu+1)\int z^{\mu+\nu+1}K_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)\,dz=z^{\mu+\nu+2}\left(K_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)+K_{\mu+1}(z)I_{\nu+1}(z)\right),

(39)

and,

2(\mu+\nu-1)\int K_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)\frac{dz}{z^{\mu+\nu-1}}=-\frac{1}{z^{\mu+\nu-2}}\left(K_{\mu-1}(z)I_{\nu-1}(z)+K_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)\right).

(40)

Similarly, results can be found for the other two combinations of Bessel functions.

Also from Watson, one may now deduce integrals of the type
\int z^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{p}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{p}(z)\,dz
in an elegant way, where
p
is a non-zero integer. These are

\int I_{p}^{2}(z)\frac{dz}{z}=\frac{1}{2p}\left\{I_{p}^{2}(z)+(-1)^{p}\left[-I_{0}^{2}(z)-2\sum_{k=1}^{p}(-1)^{k}I_{k}^{2}(z)\right]\right\},

(41)

\int K_{p}^{2}(z)\frac{dz}{z}=\frac{1}{2p}\left\{K_{p}^{2}(z)+(-1)^{p}\left[-K_{0}^{2}(z)-2\sum_{k=1}^{p}(-1)^{k}K_{k}^{2}(z)\right]\right\},

(42)

and finally,

\int K_{p}(z)I_{p}(z)\frac{dz}{z}=\frac{1}{2p}\left\{-K_{0}(z)I_{0}(z)-2\sum_{k=1}^{p}K_{k}(z)I_{k}(z)+K_{p}(z)I_{p}(z)\right\}.

(43)

It shall be noted that for non-integer
p
we at least have a result (cf.
Watson
) for functions of the first kind, namely

2\nu\int_{0}^{z}\frac{I_{\nu}^{2}(t)}{t}dt=I_{\nu}^{2}(z)+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}I_{\nu+n}^{2}(z),

(44)

but there seems to be no simple result for the indefinite integrals
\int z^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{0}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{0}(z)\,dz
.

3.2
Extension of Schafheitlin’s recursion relation and further special results

In the following, we provide an extended version of a recursion relation, which in its simpler form is originally due to Schafheitlin, among indefinite integrals of products of Bessel functions.
In contrast to Lommel’s recursion relation it involves a recursion with respect to the monomial instead of the order of Bessel functions.

First, consider Bessel’s modified differential equation,

z^{2}y^{\prime\prime}(z)+zy^{\prime}(z)-(z^{2}+\nu^{2})y=0,

(45)

fulfilled by both modified cylinder functions
\mathcal{D}_{\nu}
,
\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\nu}
. From this,

\displaystyle\int z^{\rho}

\displaystyle(z^{2}+\nu^{2})\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\int z^{\rho}\left\{z^{2}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}(z)+z\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\right\}\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)\,dz,

(46)

where the differential equation was applied on
\mathcal{D}_{\nu}
. Then, from a partial integration, we get

\displaystyle\int z^{\rho}

\displaystyle(z^{2}+\nu^{2})\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=z^{\rho+2}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)-(\rho+1)\int z^{\rho+1}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)\,dz-\int z^{\rho+2}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)\,dz.

(47)

Now let us repeat the process, letting the differential equation act on
\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}
this time:

\displaystyle\int z^{\rho}

\displaystyle(z^{2}+\mu^{2})\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=z^{\rho+2}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)-(\rho+1)\int z^{\rho+1}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)\,dz-\int z^{\rho+2}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)\,dz.

(48)

Symmetrizing both expressions yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle\equiv(\rho+1)\left\{\int z^{\rho}(z^{2}+\nu^{2})\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)\,dz+\int z^{\rho}(z^{2}+\mu^{2})\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)\,dz\right\}

(49)

\displaystyle=(\rho+1)z^{\rho+2}S_{\mu\nu}(z)-(\rho+1)^{2}\int z^{\rho+1}S_{\mu\nu}(z)\,dz-2(\rho+1)\int z^{\rho+2}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)\,dz,

(50)

where the symmetric combination is defined as

S_{\mu\nu}(z):=\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)+\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\Bigl(\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)\Bigr).

(51)

We will now consider the last integral appearing in Eq. (
50
) and perform integration by parts. We obtain

\displaystyle(\rho+3)\int z^{\rho+2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=z^{\rho+3}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)-\left\{\int z^{\rho+3}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}^{\prime}(z)\,dz+\int z^{\rho+3}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}^{\prime\prime}(z)\,dz\right\}.

(52)

From the differential equation obeyed by the modified Bessel functions, we infer

\displaystyle z^{2}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime\prime}(z)=(z^{2}+\nu^{2})\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)-z\mathcal{D}_{\nu}^{\prime}(z),

(53)

\displaystyle(\rho

The integrals involving the symmetric combination are easily handled.
Performing integration by parts gives

In this case, we obtain explicit results for a two-parameter class of integrals:

3.3
An algorithm for finding antiderivatives for non-negative integer powers

Here we want to investigate integrals of the form

\int z^{\ell}\mathcal{D}_{\mu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu+n}(z)\,dz,

(74)

where
\ell
and
n
are non-negative integers,
\mu
is a real number. Notice that by shifting
\mu

\int z^{\ell}\mathcal{D}_{\nu}(z)\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\nu-n}(z)\,dz=\int z^{\ell}\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}(z)\mathcal{D}_{\mu+n}(z)\,dz
on defining
\mu=\nu-n
.

Let us investigate the integral expression in the
\ell
-
n
-plane. We have two straight lines for which we know an easy expression for the antiderivative (from now on referred to as shorelines). One is given by the formulae of the last section, namely eqs. (
70

\ell=n+1
. The other known result applies to the case
\ell=-1
(cf. Lommel 1). When both orders of Bessel functions are equal (i.e.
n=0
), one can simplify this according to eq. (
44

\mu
further simplifies to a finite summation, see the equations above eq. (

\mu=0
and
n=0
there seems to be no easy expression. This leads to a diagram as in Fig.

We will now show that the existence of a simple form for the antiderivative in question depends on the parity of
\ell+n
. By simple we mean an expression that is of the same form as the integrand: a (finite) sum of a monomial multiplied by two modified Bessel functions. To see this, we develop an algorithmic way to obtain the antiderivative. Pictorially spoken, we will show how to reach only the shorelines from any point in the grid of the
\ell
-
n
-plane if
\ell+n
is odd for any non-zero real
\mu

\mu
is a non-zero integer or we start from region I, only finite summations are involved.

To develop our algorithm, let us investigate the allowed ways to move in the grid in the
\ell
-
n
-plane. Firstly, there is the recursion relation of Schafheitlin developed in the last section. Here, the power of the monomial is shifted by two while the orders of Bessel functions remain unchanged. There are two important caveats with this relation: First, it is not valid to go from power
+1
to
-1
, hence one cannot reach the
\ell=-1
-magic-line using this relation. Secondly, the relation collapses when approaching the
\ell=n+1
-magic-line from below, hence, such a step is also forbidden.

Secondly, there is the usual recursion relation for the Bessel functions. This can be used to create a sum of two terms, one with the same power but the
n
changed by two, and the other with both the power and the
n
changed by one unit. Thus, this almost always creates two distinct strands that have to be followed. The exception is the case
n=1
, where one can derive a recursion with only one strand,

\int z^{s}I_{\delta}(z)I_{\delta+1}(z)\,dz=-\left(\delta+\frac{s}{2}\right)\int z^{s-1}I_{\delta}^{2}(z)\,dz+\frac{z^{s}}{2}I_{\delta}^{2}(z),

(75)

valid for real

and
\delta
.

We start the proof by writing

\int z^{s}I_{\delta}(z)I_{\delta+1}(z)\,dz=\int z^{s-1}I_{\delta}(z)\Bigl(zI_{\delta+1}(z)\Bigr)\,dz.

(76)

Then, we can use the well-known identity
zI_{\delta}^{\prime}(z)=zI_{\delta+1}(z)+\delta I_{\delta}(z)
to obtain

\int z^{s}I_{\delta}(z)I_{\delta+1}(z)\,dz=\int z^{s}I_{\delta}(z)I_{\delta}^{\prime}(z)\,dz-\delta\int z^{s-1}I_{\delta}^{2}(z)\,dz.

(77)

For the first integral, use integration by parts, which yields

\int z^{s}I_{\delta}(z)I_{\delta}^{\prime}(z)\,dz=z^{s}I_{\delta}^{2}(z)-\int\Bigl(sz^{s-1}I_{\delta}^{2}(z)+z^{s}I_{\delta}^{\prime}(z)I_{\delta}(z)\Bigr)\,dz,

(78)

or on rearrangement

\int z^{s}I_{\delta}(z)I_{\delta}^{\prime}(z)\,dz=\frac{z^{s}}{2}I_{\delta}^{2}(z)-\frac{s}{2}\int z^{s-1}I_{\delta}^{2}(z)\,dz.

(79)

Hence the final result is obtained on insertion.

\displaystyle\int z^{s}I_{\delta}(z)K_{\delta+1}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=\left(\delta+\frac{s}{2}\right)\int z^{s-1}I_{\delta}(z)K_{\delta}(z)\,dz-\frac{z^{s}}{2}I_{\delta}(z)K_{\delta}(z)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\begin{cases}\log|z|,&s=0,\\

Example 2

As an example for region II consider the integral

We now come to the generalization of these examples. We say an antiderivative is
like the integrand
, if it is a finite sum of terms of the same form as the integrand, that is a product of a monomial and two modified Bessel functions. If the exponent in the monomial of the integrand is non-negative, so is the exponent in all summands. If the antiderivative is an infinite summation of such terms, we say that it possesses the property of being like the integrand
weakly
.

Claim 1

Let us now formulate our first claim: the antiderivative has the property of being like the integrand, in the case that the combination
(n,\ell)
belongs to the odd sub-lattice, i.e.
n+\ell
is odd, and is part of region I, i.e.
\ell\geq n+1
, or belongs to the odd sub-lattice, is part of region II (i.e.
\ell<n+1
) and
\mu
is a non-zero integer. If in the latter case only functions of the first kind are involved and
\mu
is non-integer, then the antiderivative has the property of being like the integrand weakly.

For the proof, we will use the well-established concept of the Manhattan metric on the grid of integer
(n,\ell)
-values. We note that each component of all steps has a Manhattan norm of two and thus the parity of the sub-lattice is preserved under all these operations. The shoreline
\ell=n+1
belongs to the odd sub-lattice. Therefore, it can be reached by any point of the odd sub-lattice in region I where there are no restrictions to the allowed operations. For region II one has to be a bit more careful since one may not move along the grid as freely due to the restrictions of Schafheitlin. As these include that it is not possible to reach the
\ell=n+1
-shoreline from below, one has to use also the regular recursion relation of the Bessel functions. Now again the same argument as before applies, but in addition to reaching the shoreline of
\ell=n+1
there are strands that move parallel to this shoreline in region II. These strands obey
\ell=n-(2k+1)
,
k=0,\dots
, where there will always be the
k=0
-strand when starting from region II. This strand includes the point
(n,\ell)=(1,0)
, which by eq. (
75
) can be recursed down to the point
(n,\ell)=(0,-1)
. The corresponding antiderivative is like the integrand in the case of non-zero
\mu
and has this property weakly in the case of non-integer
\mu
and functions of the first kind according to eqs.
41
and following. All other parallel strands terminate at the
\ell=-1
-shoreline and give antiderivatives like the integrand.

Consider now points of the even sub-lattice. First, it is obvious that one can reach region II from any point of the even sub-lattice in region I, simply by making repeated use of the regular recursion relation for the modified Bessel functions. This will create numerous strands, which either terminate on a point of the even sub-lattice in region II or end on the
\ell=n+1
-shoreline. Hence, let us assume without loss of generality that we start from a point
(n,\ell)
of the even sub-lattice in region II.

3.6
Summary of our findings on antiderivatives

In this section we were able to use the method of recursion relations to compute several general types of antiderivatives involving products of two modified Bessel functions. Apart from the well-known recursion relations of the Bessel functions themselves, we could extend a recursion relation originally due to Schafheitlin and thus state a new and far more general integral recursion relation; see Eqs. (
68
–
69
). As an additional tool, we proved the set of recursion relations Eqs.(
75
,
80
–
82
).

Starting from results that were derived by Lommel more than a hundred years ago, we investigated several classes of indefinite integrals using our recursive method. This led us to discover the closure property that, under a condition derived here on the parity of the sum of the degree of a monomial and the orders of the two Bessel functions, the antiderivative can be written as a finite sum of terms of the same form as the integrand. For example, for a monomial with non-negative integral exponent and two modified Bessel functions of the first kind and integer order, this is possible if the parity of the sum of the degree of the monomial and the two orders of Bessel functions is odd. Moreover, in the other cases our approach still allows for a systematic way to simplify other antiderivatives to a common master integral, such as
\int I_{0}^{2}(z)\,dz
, that is to our understanding only expressible in terms of more general special functions. On the way, we have given many practical examples of finding antiderivatives, including cases which are not readily found in tables or using a computer algebra system. In particular, Eq. (
90
) is, to our knowledge, a new result.

After this in-depth inspection of a class of indefinite integrals, we can now apply the obtained results to those cases that arise in coordinate-space perturbation theory.

Extension to finite temperature

The scalar propagator at finite temperature can be written in several useful forms,

\displaystyle G_{m}^{(T,\lambda)}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv T\sum_{p_{0}}\int\frac{d^{d-1}p}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\frac{e^{ip\cdot x}}{p^{2}+m^{2}}

(168)

\displaystyle=T\sum_{p_{0}}e^{ip_{0}x_{0}}\,G_{\sqrt{p_{0}^{2}+m^{2}}}^{(\lambda-1/2)}(\vec{x})

(169)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{m}^{(\lambda)}(x+\beta n_{0}\hat{e}_{0}),

(170)

where the sum over
p_{0}
extends over the Matsubara modes,
p_{0}=2\pi Tn
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
;
\beta=1/T
is the inverse temperature and
\hat{e}_{0}
is a unit vector pointing in the Euclidean time direction.

The relations (
17
)-(
18
) carry over straightforwardly to finite temperature. Using the shorthand
\int_{\beta}d^{d}y\equiv\int_{0}^{\beta}dy_{0}\int d^{d-1}y
,

\displaystyle(G^{(T,\lambda)}_{m}*G_{M}^{(T,\lambda)})(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\beta}d^{d}y\;G^{(T,\lambda)}_{m}(x-y)G^{(T,\lambda)}_{M}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M^{2}-m^{2}}\left(G^{(T,\lambda)}_{m}(x)-G^{(T,\lambda)}_{M}(x)\right),

(171)

\displaystyle(G^{(T,\lambda)}_{m}*G^{(T,\lambda)}_{m})(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\beta}d^{d}y\;G^{(T,\lambda)}_{m}(x-y)G^{(T,\lambda)}_{m}(y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial m^{2}}G^{(T,\lambda)}_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}G^{(T,\lambda-1)}_{m}(x)\,.

(172)

As an application, we consider the finite-temperature version of the one-loop correction to the scalar propagator (
132

Here we generalize the expression to a wider set of masses,

\displaystyle\delta_{2}G_{m_{1},m_{2},m}^{(T,\lambda)}(x)

(173)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\;\int_{\beta}d^{d}y\int_{\beta}d^{d}z\;G_{m}^{(T,\lambda)}(x-y)G_{m_{1}}^{(T,\lambda)}(y-z)G_{m_{2}}^{(T,\lambda)}(y-z)G_{m}^{(T,\lambda)}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\beta}d^{d}w\;G^{(T,\lambda)}_{m_{1}}(w)\,G^{(T,\lambda)}_{m_{2}}(w)\,G^{(T,\lambda-1)}_{m}(x-w)

(174)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2},n\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\beta}d^{d}w\;G^{(\lambda)}_{m_{1}}(w+n_{1}\beta\hat{e}_{0})\,G^{(\lambda)}_{m_{2}}(w+n_{2}\beta\hat{e}_{0})\,G^{(\lambda-1)}_{m}(x-w+n\beta\hat{e}_{0})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\int d^{d}w\;G^{(\lambda)}_{m_{1}}(w+\nu_{1}\beta\hat{e}_{0})\,G^{(\lambda)}_{m_{2}}(w+\nu_{2}\beta\hat{e}_{0})\,G^{(\lambda-1)}_{m}(x-w)

\displaystyle\delta_{2}G_{m,0,m}^{(T,\lambda)}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\widetilde{W}(x+\nu_{1}\beta\hat{e}_{0},x+\nu_{2}\beta\hat{e}_{0}),

8
Conclusion

We have shown that, in the Gegenbauer-polynomial position-space approach to perturbative, dimensionally regularized quantum field theory, the radial integrals are still largely tractable in QED-like kinematics, i.e. with one mass scale present. To this end, we reviewed and extended classic results on modified Bessel functions, deriving conditions under which a certain class of antiderivatives takes the same form as the function under the integral.

As an illustration of calculations performed in position space, we computed the leading-order three-point function defined in Eq. (
103
), with the result given by Eq. (
115
), as well as the one-loop correction to a two-point function, defined by Eq. (
130
) and given in its final form in Eq. (
149
), keeping the number of spacetime dimensions
d=2\lambda+2
general. Similarly, we have provided an expression for the position-space two-point function at non-zero temperature, to one-loop order (Eqs. (
173
) and (
178
)). In this context, we have illustrated that the underlying O(
d
) spacetime symmetry can still be exploited, despite its breaking through the thermal boundary conditions in time, the price to pay being remaining sums over the winding numbers of propagators.

In position-space perturbation theory, one can define and compute re-usable ‘building blocks’ such as the coordinate-space three-point function illustrated by Fig.
1
or the mixed coordinate/momentum space function defined by the diagram of Fig.
11
.
Once a suitable representation has been found for them, these building blocks can be used in a more extended diagram, reducing the actual number of integrals to be performed for such a diagram with
V
vertices to a number smaller than
V-1
.
Obviously, as one moves to building blocks with additional external vertices, i.e. ones that are not integrated over, they become functions of more and more variables and thereby more complex.

In this article, we have focused on the direct evaluation of the required angular and radial integrals. However, as stated in several instances throughout the manuscript, most of these integrals amount to solving an inhomogeneous second-order linear differential equation with the help of its Green’s function. It is conceivable that other methods could be successfully applied to solving these differential equations. In particular, if the form of the answer is foreseeable on physics grounds, e.g. on the basis of a dispersive representation, making an ansatz with coefficients to be adjusted is a promising approach.

A.1
Solving the integrals for
\lambda=1

We will now derive antiderivatives for the integrals in four dimensions. First, we use the substitution
u=m|w|
in each, we thus arrive at

\displaystyle\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1}=\frac{1}{2^{6}\pi^{4}}\Biggl\{

\displaystyle K_{0}(m|x|)\int_{0}^{m|x|}\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{0}(u)+

\displaystyle I_{0}(m|x|)\int_{m|x|}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)K_{0}(u)-

\displaystyle K_{2}(m|x|)\int_{0}^{m|x|}\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{2}(u)-

\displaystyle I_{2}(m|x|)\int_{m|x|}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)K_{2}(u)\Biggr\}.

(182)

For the first of the four integrals use
K_{1}(z)=-K_{0}^{\prime}(z)
and integration by parts,

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{0}(u)

\displaystyle=-\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{0}^{\prime}(u)I_{0}(u)

\displaystyle=-K_{0}(u)I_{0}(u)+\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{0}(u)I_{1}(u).

(183)

Now on using the identity
K_{0}(z)I_{1}(z)+K_{1}(z)I_{0}(z)=1/z
we arrive at

\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{0}(u)=-K_{0}(u)I_{0}(u)+\int\mathrm{d}u\,\frac{1}{u}-\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{0}(u),

(184)

which can be rearranged to

2\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{0}(u)=-K_{0}(u)I_{0}(u)+\log|u|.

(185)

This gives the first antiderivative.

For the third we first use the integral recursion relation in Lommel 2 to rewrite the integral as

\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{2}(u)=-\frac{u}{2}\Bigl(K_{0}(u)I_{1}(u)+K_{1}(u)I_{2}(u)\Bigr)+\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{0}(u)I_{1}(u).

(186)

Next, we again make use of the identity
K_{0}(z)I_{1}(z)+K_{1}(z)I_{0}(z)=1/z
,

\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{2}(u)=-\frac{u}{2}\Bigl(K_{0}(u)I_{1}(u)+K_{1}(u)I_{2}(u)\Bigr)+\log|u|-\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{0}(u),

(187)

and then can insert the result from above for the first antiderivative,

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{2}(u)=-\frac{u}{2}\Bigl(K_{0}(u)I_{1}(u)+K_{1}(u)I_{2}(u)\Bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(K_{0}(u)I_{0}(u)+\log|u|\Bigr).

(188)

On may now still use the identity
K_{0}(u)I_{1}(u)+K_{1}(u)I_{0}(u)=1/z
and the recursion relation for the Bessel function to simplify this, which yields

\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)I_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\{\log|u|-1+2I_{1}(u)K_{1}(u)+K_{0}(u)I_{0}(u)\Bigr\}.

(189)

The second antiderivative can again be computed by the same procedure used for the first: it follows that

\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)K_{0}(u)=-\frac{1}{2}K_{0}^{2}(u).

(190)

Finally, the fourth antiderivative can be obtained again by an application of Lommel 2,

\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)K_{2}(u)=-\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{0}(u)K_{1}(u)+\frac{u}{2}\Bigl(K_{0}(u)K_{1}(u)-K_{1}(u)K_{2}(u)\Bigr).

(191)

Using the above result for the third antiderivative finally yields

\int\mathrm{d}u\,K_{1}(u)K_{2}(u)=\frac{1}{2}K_{0}^{2}(u)-K_{1}^{2}(u).

(192)

A.1.1
Evaluation of the definite integrals

Now one may plug in the integral boundaries into the antiderivatives. For the last three integrals this is straight-forward, since they all converge, as we will now see. For the second integral we have a contribution of

\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2^{6}\pi^{4}}I_{0}(m|x|)\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2}K_{0}^{2}(u)\Bigr]_{u=m|x|}^{\infty}.

(193)

Since
K_{\nu}(z)\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}e^{-z}
for large

\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2^{7}\pi^{4}}I_{0}(m|x|)K_{0}^{2}(m|x|).

(194)

For the contribution from the fourth integral we have

\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1,4}=-\frac{1}{2^{6}\pi^{4}}I_{2}(m|x|)\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}K_{0}^{2}(u)-K_{1}^{2}(u)\Bigr]_{u=m|x|}^{\infty}.

(195)

Again, the upper boundary does not contribute because of the asymptotic behaviour of the modified Bessel function of the second kind. Hence,

\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1,4}=\frac{1}{2^{6}\pi^{4}}I_{2}(m|x|)\Bigl[\frac{1}{2}K_{0}^{2}(m|x|)-K_{1}^{2}(m|x|)\Bigr].

(196)

For the contribution of the third integral it is not as clear that it converges. But observe that from the limiting form of the modified Bessel functions for small arguments, one can deduce that for
\nu>0
and
\mu>0

nist

K_{\mu}(z)I_{\nu}(z)\sim\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(\mu)\left(\frac{2}{z}\right)^{\mu}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{\nu}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Gamma(\mu)}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\left(\frac{2}{z}\right)^{\mu-\nu},

(197)

whereas for
\mu=0
we have
nist

K_{0}(z)I_{\nu}(z)\sim-\log z\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{\nu}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}.

(198)

and more precisely, by using the series expansions,
Watson

K_{0}(z)I_{0}(z)=(-\gamma_{E}+\log 2-\log z)+\mathcal{O}(z).

(199)

Then, we find that

\displaystyle\lim_{u\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\Bigl(\log u-1+2I_{1}(u)K_{1}(u)+K_{0}(u)I_{0}(u)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\lim_{u\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\log u-1+1-\gamma_{E}+\log 2-\log u\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log 2-\gamma_{E}}{2}.

(200)

Now one may compute the contribution of the third integral,

\displaystyle\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1,3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2^{6}\pi^{4}}K_{2}(m|x|)\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[\log|u|-1+2I_{1}(u)K_{1}(u)+K_{0}(u)I_{0}(u)\Bigr]_{u=0}^{m|x|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{7}\pi^{4}}K_{2}(m|x|)\Bigl\{1+\log 2-\gamma_{E}-\log(m|x|)

\displaystyle\qquad-2I_{1}(m|x|)K_{1}(m|x|)-K_{0}(m|x|)I_{0}(m|x|)\Bigr\}.

(201)

Finally, the first integral does not converge. We may still plug in the boundaries and treat the infinite limit as an (improper) constant. This gives

\displaystyle\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{7}\pi^{4}}K_{0}(m|x|)\Biggl\{\log(m|x|)-K_{0}(m|x|)I_{0}(m|x|)

\displaystyle\qquad-\gamma_{E}+\log 2-\lim_{u\downarrow 0}\Bigl(2\log u\Bigr)\Biggr\}.

(202)

\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1}=\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1,1}+\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1,2}+\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1,3}+\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1,4}.

(203)

nist

K_{\nu}(z)\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2z}}e^{-z},\qquad I_{\nu}(z)\sim\frac{e^{z}}{\sqrt{2\pi z}},\qquad(z\to\infty).

\Bigl(\delta_{2}G_{m}^{(1)}(x)\Bigr)_{1}\sim\frac{1}{2^{6}\pi^{4}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\Biggl\{\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\log 2-\gamma_{E}-\lim_{u\downarrow 0}\log u\Bigr)\frac{e^{-m|x|}}{\sqrt{m|x|}}-\frac{e^{-m|x|}}{(m|x|)^{3/2}}\Biggr\}.

The two-point function at one loop order for

(215)

(216)

Appendix C
Derivation of the Gegenbauer expansion for
K_{0}(|x-u|)

We start by taking Neumann’s addition theorem (resp. a special case of Graf’s addition theorem) in the form

Let us now adopt this to our situation and set

to be positive values. Set

u

\varpi=|x-u|
.

Now we can start to prove the actual formula in question. First, transform the summation,

K_{0}(|x-u|)=K_{0}(|u|)I_{0}(|x|)+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{k}(|x|)K_{k}(|u|)\cos(k\varphi).

(221)

Then, introduce the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind
T_{n}
by the identity

T_{n}(\cos\theta)=\cos(n\theta),

(222)

and combine this with the well-known interrelation between the Chebyshev polynomials,

T_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(U_{n}(x)(1+\delta_{n0})-U_{n-2}(x)\Bigr).

(223)

Here, the convention is used that the Chebyshev polynomial vanishes for negative orders. Upon insertion we get

\displaystyle K_{0}(|x-u|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}I_{k}(|x|)K_{k}(|u|)U_{k}(\cos\varphi)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}I_{k}(|x|)K_{k}(|u|)U_{k-2}(\cos\varphi).

(224)

The second summation actually starts at
k=2
and on an index shift this becomes

K_{0}(|x-u|)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}U_{k}(\cos\varphi)\Bigl\{I_{k}(|x|)K_{k}(|u|)-I_{k+2}(|x|)K_{k+2}(|u|)\Bigr\}.

(225)
