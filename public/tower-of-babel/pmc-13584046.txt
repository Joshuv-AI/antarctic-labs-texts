Case report on complex jewelry foreign body ingestion in pediatric population: When watchful waiting prevails over intervention
Authors: Suraj KC
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractForeign body ingestion (FBI) is a frequent pediatric emergency that often requires immediate, invasive endoscopic or surgical extraction when the object is large, multi-linked, or has sharp components. This case report highlights the safety and efficacy of an evidence-based watchful waiting protocol for a complex jewelry ingestion in an asymptomatic older child. An asymptomatic 8-year-old male presented to the emergency department 2 hours after accidentally swallowing a multi-linked metallic bracelet. Initial abdominal radiography 2 hours post-ingestion localized the radiopaque object to the proximal small bowel. Due to the complete absence of clinical symptoms or peritoneal signs, a strict conservative protocol was initiated, utilizing serial clinical examinations and radiographic tracking. Follow-up imaging at 8 hours post-ingestion demonstrated rapid transit to just below the left hemidialhragm suggesting the descending colon while Xray done 14 hours post ingestion localized the radiopaque object midline lower pelvis near the rectum. The patient passed stool along with the bracelet after 14 hours post ingestion, which was photographed. While up to 90% of pediatric foreign bodies pass spontaneously, multi-linked jewelry often prompts premature intervention due to concerns over mechanical entrapment or mucosal injury. Utilizing objective post-ingestion time anchors is crucial for accurately reconstructing gastrointestinal transit sequences and validating conservative pathways. Watchful waiting paired with precise, time-anchored serial radiography is a safe, highly cost-effective strategy for managing complex jewelry ingestion in pediatric populations, provided the patient remains entirely asymptomatic and imaging confirms continuous distal transit.Keywords: Conservative, Endoscopic, Foreign body, Pediatric, Surgical

Introduction

Foreign body ingestion (FBI) is a frequent pediatric emergency, particularly in children under 5 years old [1]. These young children often explore objects with their mouths and may accidentally swallow small non-food items out of curiosity. Infants and toddlers are at a higher risk of swallowing objects due to their immature teeth compared to older children [2]. Older children who ingest foreign bodies often have psychiatric or developmental conditions. A case study of a child who spontaneously passed a metallic foreign body without intervention highlights the importance of watchful waiting in such situations. This case underscores the potential for favorable outcomes without immediate medical intervention, emphasizing the need for careful observation and monitoring in similar cases. The work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2023 criteria [3].

Case presentation

An 8-year-old boy was brought to the emergency department by his parents with a history of ingesting a bracelet 2 hours prior. They stated that he was playing with the bracelet and accidentally swallowed it while inserting it into his mouth. According to the parents, the patient had achieved normal developmental milestones, and there was no psychiatric illness in their child. Following the ingestion, they were worried and gave him about 500 mL of water to drink. Upon presentation, the boy had no major complaints, no abdominal pain, abdominal distension, vomiting, neck crepitus, slurring of speech, or neck pain. He was passing flatus but not stool. On clinical examination, the patient was well-oriented to time, place, and person with normal hemodynamic parameters. Abdominal examination revealed no tenderness or distension. His laboratory parameters showed normal complete and differential counts, as well as normal renal and liver function tests. Xray abdomen and pelvis was done 2 hours post ingestion which showed radiopaque object localized in left upper quadrant likely at proximal bowel (Fig. 1). Since there were no signs of complications, he was admitted to the ward and advised for serial imaging. He was instructed to consume only clear liquids and to report if he passed any foreign body in stool or had black stool. Serial imaging taken 8 hours post ingestion showed the foreign body demonstrated rapid transit to just below the left hemidiaphragm suggesting the descending colon (Fig. 2) while Xray done 14 hours post ingestion localized the radiopaque object midline lower pelvis near the rectum (Fig. 3). The patient passed stool along with the bracelet after 14 hours post ingestion, which was photographed (Fig. 4). X-ray of the abdomen and pelvis was done after complete passage, and it was normal. He was discharged on the same day with oral analgesics and lactulose. The patient revisited on the third day after discharge and was doing well.

Discussion

The incidence of foreign body ingestion peaks in adolescent boys compared to girls, though both sexes show a similar prevalence in younger age groups [4]. Spectrum of clinical feature is broad, with children frequently swallowing coins, small toys, or objects of various shapes and sizes. It is estimated that 80–90% of these cases pass through the digestive system naturally, while 10–20% require endoscopic removal, and only 1% necessitate surgical intervention [5]. The esophagus remains the most common site of foreign body impaction, particularly at its physiologic narrowings [6]. While most objects transit within 4–6 days without issue, symptomatic obstruction or delay is highly localized to narrow or angulated segments. These critical check-points include the esophagus, pre-pyloric region, ligament of Treitz, ileocecal valve, and rectosigmoid junction, where intestinal perforation occurs in a rare 1% of total cases [7].

Symptomatic patients may present with vomiting, a globus sensation, dysphagia, or throat pain, though a clear history of ingestion provided by the patient or parents is invaluable. A meticulous history and physical examination are vital during initial assessment to screen for emergency conditions. Key aspects of history-taking include symptoms, type of foreign body, timing of presentation, and associated conditions. Clinicians must recognize the underlying mechanisms of emergency signs during the physical examination [8]. Sharp, long, or pointed objects perforating the stomach or intestines present with peritonitis and radiographic pneumoperitoneum. Conversely, foreign bodies migrating or impacted in the pharynx or esophagus track air along deep fascial planes into the neck or chest wall, presenting as subcutaneous emphysema and pneumomediastinum. Sharp foreign bodies—such as razor blades, needles, bones, or glass fragments—carry a high 15–35% risk of structural gastrointestinal damage. They directly puncture the mucosa or impale themselves at angulated boundaries like the pylorus, ileocecal valve, or rectosigmoid junction. Sewing needles are notorious for rapid gastric and bowel perforations, demanding early surgical or endoscopic intervention to prevent severe complications [9,10]. Button batteries pose an equally severe emergency risk when lodged in moist mucosal environments like the esophagus, nose, or ears. Even discarded, partially depleted batteries can generate electrical currents sufficient to cause extensive liquefactive necrosis within an hour. While steadily moving batteries rarely cause harm, a missed esophageal battery can rapidly culminate in airway destruction or fatal hemorrhage via an aorto-esophageal fistula [11].

Pathophysiological risks vary significantly by object type, making magnet ingestion protocols strictly dependent on the piece count and tracking times [12]. A history of sharp foreign body ingestion mandates immediate radiographic assessment. Plain radiography serves as the primary, first-line tool to identify the size, type, and location of radiopaque objects and monitor their transit. It is highly effective for metallic objects, but possesses a poor sensitivity for glass (43%) or fish bones (26%), and leaves wood entirely invisible; if clinical suspicion persists despite negative plain X-rays, emergency endoscopy is warranted [13].

For truly radiolucent materials like food boluses, plastics, or wood, non-contrast computed tomography (CT) with 3-dimensional reformations acts as the superior secondary modality, offering high sensitivity for object localization, preprocedural mapping, and identifying extraluminal air or wall thickening without the unnecessary treatment delays associated with oral or intravenous contrast protocols [14]. Other imaging techniques must be used with strict clinical justification to avoid dangerous delays: ultrasound possesses a highly specific, established utility for diagnosing water-absorbing polymer beads (water beads) which are otherwise invisible on X-rays, whereas magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or barium swallows have no defined role in acute foreign body ingestion algorithms and should be avoided [13].

Unwitnessed and radiolucent object ingestions can create uncertainty surrounding the nature of ingestion and may lead to significant delay in diagnosis and management [5]. Sharp objects, animal bones, blister pack foil, and magnets carry the highest risks of retention and perforation. Swallowed magnets are particularly hazardous because they can attract other iron-containing objects or adjacent magnets across bowel walls, causing volvulus, complete obstruction, pressure necrosis, and perforation [15]. Asymptomatic single magnet ingestions can be safely managed on an outpatient basis with daily plain radiographs to confirm distal progression. If a single magnet fails to progress within 24 hours, multi-view lateral radiographs are indicated to rule out overlapping identical pieces before attempting site-specific retrieval. Conversely, multiple magnet ingestions are highly dangerous and require intensive monitoring via serial radiographs every 4 to 6 hours. Expectant management is strictly contraindicated unless imaging definitively confirms that the multiple magnets are physically bound together as a single, cohesive unit moving progressively. If multiple pieces fail to advance over a 6-hour window, immediate endoscopic or surgical intervention is mandatory to prevent delayed complications, including localized ulceration, stricture, obstruction, volvulus, hemorrhage, sepsis, death, or complex enteroenteric, arteriogastrointestinal, or tracheoesophageal fistula formation [12].

Ultimately, foreign body management is categorized into conservative, endoscopic, and surgical approaches, with choices dictated by object type, anatomic location, progression, and active complications. Esophageal impactions and sharp objects retained in the stomach require urgent endoscopic retrieval via gastroscopy or colonoscopy, boasting excellent success rates [16]. Surgical intervention is rare and strictly reserved for cases showing an absolute arrest of bowel progression, developing acute symptoms, or failing multiple endoscopic attempts [4]. Surgery is typically considered when multiple endoscopic treatments have failed or when there are complications that cannot be resolved with endoscope [17].

Conclusion

Foreign body ingestion is frequently observed in younger children, but older individuals with psychiatric issues or developmental delays may also ingest foreign objects. The consequences of foreign body ingestion vary depending on the size, shape, sharpness, and chemical composition of the object. In most cases, foreign bodies pass through the alimentary canal without complications. While expectant management can be effective, certain cases may require endoscopic or surgical intervention.
