CT-based 3D planning-supported stem-free screw-cement augmentation versus 2D radiograph-planned short-stem reconstruction for AORI type I–II medial tibial defects in TKA: a two-year retrospective study
Authors: Zhenbao Lu, Yuhua Feng, Qiujin Xia, Hongkuan Lin, Jiliang Chen, Xu Wang, Jianfu Zhu, Xiaohong Fan, Chengshou Lin, Qingshan Xu, Qijin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedial tibial defects in varus knee osteoarthritis (KOA) challenge alignment and tibial support in primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA). A stem-free metaphyseal screw-cement strategy supported by computed tomography (CT)-based three-dimensional (3D) planning, with artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted segmentation used for model generation in the planning software, may be a bone-preserving alternative to conventional two-dimensional (2D) radiograph planning with short tibial stems. This study compared early tibial alignment and clinical outcomes between two reconstructive pathways in Anderson Orthopaedic Research Institute (AORI) type I–II medial tibial defects.MethodsIn this single-centre retrospective cohort, 97 patients with varus KOA and AORI type I–II medial tibial defects undergoing TKA were managed using either a CT-based 3D planning-supported stem-free screw-cement pathway (n = 38) or a 2D radiograph-planned short-stem pathway (n = 59). Given non-randomised pathway allocation, analyses assessed associations. The primary outcome was the absolute deviation of the mechanical medial proximal tibial angle (mMPTA) from 90° at 4–8 weeks. Secondary outcomes included achievement of mMPTA within 90° ± 3°, intraoperative visible blood loss, pain and function measured by the visual analogue scale (VAS), Knee Society Score (KSS), and Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) at 3 and 24 months, and complications. Between-pathway comparisons used standard univariable tests, findings were interpreted as associative given non-randomised allocation.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between pathways (all P > 0.05). Postoperative tibial alignment did not differ, with comparable absolute deviation of mMPTA from 90° and similar rates within 90° ± 3°. Operative time was similar, but visible blood loss was lower in the CT-3D screw-cement pathway than in the 2D short-stem pathway (262.7 ± 75.3 vs 381.9 ± 71.3 mL, P < 0.001). At 3 months, the CT-3D screw-cement pathway showed lower VAS and better KSS/WOMAC, whereas outcomes were comparable at 24 months. No deep infection, radiographic loosening, periprosthetic fracture, or revision was observed within 24 months.ConclusionsThe CT-3D screw-cement pathway was associated with tibial alignment and 24-month function comparable to the 2D short-stem pathway, with lower blood loss and better early recovery. Fixation-related inferences are limited to early radiographic stability.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is one of the most common musculoskeletal disorders worldwide and a leading cause of chronic pain and disability in middle-aged and older adults, affecting approximately 4–5% of the global population and imposing an increasing disease burden with population ageing and rising obesity prevalence [1, 2]. Patients with end-stage KOA often require total knee arthroplasty (TKA) to relieve pain and restore function, and the annual volume of primary TKA has continued to rise over the past two decades [3]. In patients with long-standing varus KOA, medial tibial plateau bone defects, particularly posteromedial cavitary defects, are common and are closely associated with medial deviation of the lower limb mechanical axis, knee joint instability, and prosthetic failure [4, 5], making reconstruction of the tibial plateau a key challenge in this subgroup.

Medial tibial defects weaken the bony support of the tibial baseplate. Even focal defects only a few millimetres deep can, if not managed appropriately, lead to uneven load distribution and residual varus alignment, causing the mechanical medial proximal tibial angle (mMPTA) to deviate from neutral and increasing the risk of micromotion and loosening at the prosthesis-bone interface [4, 6]. The Anderson Orthopaedic Research Institute (AORI) classification is commonly used to evaluate tibial bone defects and to guide reconstruction, and in clinical practice most medial plateau defects are classified as type I or II [7, 8]. For these type I–II defects, commonly used reconstructive strategies include screw-cement augmentation, modular metal augments, structural bone grafts, and tibial stem extensions [4, 9, 10]. Each option involves trade-offs between preserving bone stock, providing stable support, and facilitating future revision, and there is still no consensus between bone-preserving screw-cement techniques and stem-based constructs that emphasise diaphyseal support [5, 10]. A recent retrospective study of Rand IIb medial tibial plateau defects compared screw-cement filling with extensive tibial resection combined with a thick polyethylene insert and reported comparable 2-year clinical and radiographic outcomes, with slightly better correction of limb alignment and preservation of bone stock in the screw-cement group [11]. However, that study did not include tibial stem extensions or evaluate how different preoperative planning approaches influence reconstructive pathway selection and alignment accuracy, and pathway-level comparisons that include both screw-cement augmentation and tibial stem extensions in AORI type I–II medial tibial defects remain scarce.

Conventional preoperative planning for TKA mainly relies on two-dimensional (2D) radiographs to assess deformity and bone defects [7, 11]. However, 2D assessment is limited by magnification errors, overlapping bony contours, and a lack of depth information, and tends to underestimate the extent and volume of defects; lesions judged preoperatively as “mild” may intraoperatively prove more extensive, necessitating unplanned metal augments or tibial stem extensions [7]. Computed tomography (CT)-based three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction allows more accurate quantification of defect depth and area and depicts their spatial relationship with the tibial shaft axis and joint line, providing a more reliable morphological basis for individualised osteotomy and selection of the reconstructive pathway [7]. Building on CT-based 3D reconstruction, CT-based 3D preoperative planning platforms combine segmentation, 3D modelling, and virtual TKA simulation to inform implant sizing, osteotomy planes, and alignment targets within a single workflow; the segmentation step may be artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted depending on the system. Previous studies have reported that CT-based 3D planning platforms, some incorporating AI-assisted segmentation, may improve the accuracy of implant sizing and selected alignment parameters and may reduce unexpected intraoperative adjustments [12–14]. However, data on the use of these tools in patients with medial tibial defects classified as AORI type I–II and direct comparisons between different reconstructive pathways remain limited. Studies that define postoperative coronal mechanical alignment of the tibial component, such as the absolute deviation of mMPTA from neutral, as the primary radiographic outcome are also scarce [12, 15].

Against this background, high-quality comparative data remain limited for patients with end-stage varus KOA and AORI type I–II medial tibial defects, particularly regarding pathway-level comparisons between a CT-based 3D planning-supported stem-free screw-cement pathway and a 2D radiograph-planned short-stem pathway. To our knowledge, real-world comparisons between these two clinically used pathways in this population have not yet been reported. Therefore, a single-centre retrospective cohort study was conducted to compare the two predefined pathways. The absolute deviation of postoperative mMPTA from the neutral target of 90° was selected as the primary radiographic endpoint, and perioperative blood loss and early and mid-term pain and functional outcomes were prespecified as key secondary outcomes, to generate pathway-level evidence for reconstructive decision-making in this subgroup.

Methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This single-centre retrospective cohort study with 2-year follow-up was conducted at a tertiary hospital to compare two integrated, real-world reconstructive pathways in primary TKA for end-stage KOA with AORI type I–II medial tibial defects: a CT-3D screw-cement pathway and a 2D radiograph-planned short-stem pathway. In this pragmatic, pathway-level comparison, the unit of analysis was the reconstructive pathway as a whole rather than any single technical factor (planning modality, implant system, or defect reconstruction method). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (Approval No. K2025112002), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. All procedures involving human participants complied with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. This study did not include any animal experiments. Owing to the retrospective observational design and non-randomised pathway assignment, the analyses were intended to evaluate associations rather than establish causality. In addition, the planning modality (CT-based 3D software with AI-assisted segmentation) was bundled with implant system and reconstruction philosophy across predefined pathways. Therefore, observed differences should be interpreted as pathway-level associations reflecting differing fixation philosophies (stem-free metaphyseal reconstruction vs stem-based support), with AI-assisted segmentation representing an implementation detail of the CT-3D planning software rather than an isolated exposure.

Patient selection

Between January 2019 and November 2023, consecutive patients with end-stage varus KOA who underwent primary TKA at our institution were retrospectively identified. All patients presented with medial tibial defects classified as AORI type I or II. Based on the reconstructive pathway selected preoperatively, patients were divided into two groups: a CT-based 3D planning-supported stem-free screw-cement pathway (planning performed using CT-based 3D software with AI-assisted segmentation) and a 2D radiograph-planned short-stem pathway. In routine practice, pathway selection was based on the preoperative imaging assessment (including defect morphology) and surgeon judgement, which may introduce pathway-selection bias.

Inclusion criteria (1) diagnosis of end-stage KOA requiring primary TKA, with varus deformity and medial tibial defects classified as AORI type I–II based on preoperative imaging (radiographs and CT); (2) availability of complete preoperative radiographs or CT scans adequate for evaluating tibial defect morphology and lower-limb alignment; (3) undergoing TKA with either CT-based 3D preoperative planning (implemented using AI-assisted segmentation) or 2D radiograph-based planning using line drawing and angle measurements; (4) completion of at least 24 months of clinical and radiographic follow-up.

Exclusion criteria (1) AORI type III tibial defects or cases requiring structural bone grafts, large metal augments, or long tibial stems as predetermined reconstruction strategy; (2) prior ipsilateral knee surgery other than diagnostic arthroscopy; (3) inflammatory arthritis, severe extra-articular deformity, or valgus knees; (4) active infection, compromised soft-tissue condition, or systemic comorbidities contraindicating TKA; (5) incomplete imaging or follow-up data insufficient for alignment analysis or functional evaluation.

Preoperative planning

CT-3D screw-cement pathway All cases were processed using a CT-based 3D planning system (AIJOINT, Changmugu Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; version 1.0) that generates patient-specific 3D models via AI-assisted segmentation and enables virtual TKA simulation for implant sizing and osteotomy planning [16]. Model performance was verified through internal cross-validation with expert-labeled datasets and externally evaluated across independent multicentre cohorts before clinical release [17–19]. The software generates patient-specific implant recommendations and allows surgeons to review, modify, or override AI outputs at any stage prior to final confirmation. Patients in this pathway were scheduled to receive the Johnson & Johnson Attune tibial baseplate combined with screw-cement augmentation for AORI type I–II medial tibial defects. This combination of CT-3D planning, the Attune tibial system, and screw-cement augmentation constituted the predefined CT-3D screw-cement pathway.

2D short-stem pathway Planning was performed directly on anteroposterior and lateral knee radiographs. The surgeon manually drew reference lines to estimate tibial resection thickness, defect involvement, and posterior slope. When medial metaphyseal support was considered insufficient based on defect depth and varus alignment, a tibial short extension stem from the Aikang (AK) knee system was preselected as the primary reconstruction method. Conventional 2D line-drawing planning together with the AK tibial baseplate and short extension stem constituted the predefined 2D short-stem pathway.

Surgical procedure

All surgeries were performed by the same senior arthroplasty surgeon using a standard medial parapatellar approach under general anesthesia. Distal femoral resection was performed using an intramedullary guide with a valgus angle of 6° to the mechanical axis, and femoral rotation was set with reference to the posterior condylar axis and Whiteside’s line. All knees received a posterior-stabilized femoral component.

In the CT-3D screw-cement pathway, tibial resection parameters—including resection depth, coronal alignment perpendicular to the mechanical axis, and posterior slope—strictly followed the 3D planning output. Medial tibial defects (AORI type I–II) were reconstructed using screw-cement augmentation according to defect size, and the Attune tibial component was implanted without metal augments or tibial stems.

In the 2D short-stem pathway, tibial resection was performed using an extramedullary alignment guide based on the preoperative radiographic measurements. All knees received a tibial baseplate with a short extension stem from the AK knee system to provide additional medial support. Femoral preparation and soft-tissue balancing were completed using conventional measured resection and gap-balancing techniques. All components were cemented with high-viscosity polymethylmethacrylate.

Perioperative management, including use of a pneumatic tourniquet, tranexamic acid, prophylactic antibiotics, postoperative rehabilitation, and thromboprophylaxis, was identical in both pathways and followed the institutional enhanced recovery protocol.

Data collection and outcome measures

The primary outcome was the absolute deviation of the mMPTA from the neutral mechanical target of 90° on the first routine postoperative full-length anteroposterior radiograph obtained between 4 and 8 weeks after surgery, when early fixation has typically stabilized sufficiently for radiographic coronal assessment and soft-tissue swelling has usually subsided to allow reliable coronal alignment assessment. The mMPTA was measured on standardized full-length anteroposterior radiographs of the lower limb, using the mechanical axis of the tibia as the reference. When more than one eligible radiograph was available within this time window, the earliest standardized image was selected for analysis. All radiographic measurements were independently performed by two observers who were blinded to pathway allocation and preoperative planning mode, using predefined measurement protocols. Key secondary outcomes were (1) the proportion of knees with an mMPTA of 90° ± 3° at 4–8 weeks postoperatively and (2) intraoperative visible blood loss (mL). Other secondary outcomes included: (1) pain and functional outcomes at 3 and 24 months, assessed by the visual analogue scale (VAS), the Knee Society Score (KSS), and the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC); and (2) perioperative and follow-up complications, including thromboembolic events, infection, wound complications, radiographic loosening, and any reoperation or revision.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analysed using independent-samples t tests, whereas non-normally distributed variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) and were analysed using Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. In addition, exploratory multivariable linear regression models were fitted for the primary outcome and for selected secondary outcomes, with the reconstructive pathway as the main independent variable and age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, AORI tibial defect type, and the corresponding preoperative scores as covariates. These adjusted models reduce but cannot eliminate confounding; therefore, results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

The primary analysis compared the primary outcome, defined as the absolute deviation of mMPTA from 90° on the first standardized full-length radiograph obtained 4–8 weeks postoperatively, between groups. A post hoc power analysis based on the observed variability of the primary outcome and the realised group sample sizes was performed to contextualise the likelihood of detecting between-group differences and suggested that the study was reasonably powered to detect clinically meaningful effects, whereas smaller differences may have gone undetected. Analyses of secondary outcomes, including both unadjusted comparisons and adjusted multivariable models, were considered exploratory, and no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. For these endpoints, the magnitude and clinical relevance of effect estimates were interpreted alongside P values. All tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

After excluding four patients (two due to incomplete records, one lost to follow-up, and one who declined participation), a total of 97 patients were included in the analysis: 38 in the CT-3D screw-cement pathway and 59 in the 2D short-stem pathway. Baseline characteristics, including sex, age, BMI, ASA classification, operative side, and AORI tibial defect category, showed no significant differences between the CT-3D screw-cement pathway and the 2D short-stem pathway. Other preoperative clinical indices, such as VAS, KSS, and WOMAC scores, were also comparable between the groups (all P > 0.05). Functional outcomes

At 3 months postoperatively, the CT-3D screw-cement pathway had lower VAS pain scores (2 [1–3] vs 3 [2– 3]; P = 0.006), higher KSS (79.7 ± 5.0 vs 77.5 ± 4.1; P = 0.019), and lower WOMAC total scores (20 [16–24] vs 23 [19–27]; P = 0.017) than the 2D short-stem pathway. At 24 months, VAS, KSS, and WOMAC scores were comparable between the two groups (all P > 0.05). Exploratory multivariable linear regression analyses, adjusting for age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, AORI tibial defect type and the corresponding preoperative scores, yielded effect estimates that were broadly consistent with the unadjusted comparisons. After covariate adjustment, pathway assignment was not independently associated with the absolute deviation of mMPTA from 90° (β = − 0.49°, 95% CI –1.24 to 0.27; P = 0.202), whereas the early advantages of the CT-3D pathway in 3-month KSS (β = 2.70, 95% CI 1.49–3.92; P < 0.001) and WOMAC scores (β = − 2.98, 95% CI − 4.35 to − 1.62; P < 0.001) remained evident. For 24-month functional outcomes, adjusted models suggested only small between-group differences for KSS (β = − 0.29, 95% CI –1.21 to 0.64; P = 0.537) and WOMAC (β = 1.07, 95% CI 0.06–2.09; P = 0.039). Given the exploratory nature of these models and the lack of multiplicity adjustment, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously.

Complications and reoperations

No cases of deep infection, radiographic loosening, or periprosthetic fracture were observed in either pathway during the 24-month follow-up. Minor complications were infrequent and comparable between groups (1/38 vs 2/59, P > 0.05). One patient in the CT-3D screw-cement pathway developed superficial wound exudation on postoperative day 4, and one patient in the 2D short-stem pathway developed superficial wound exudation on postoperative day 9; both resolved after prolonged local dressing. In addition, one patient in the 2D short-stem pathway was found to have postoperative stiffness at the 1-month follow-up and was subsequently readmitted for manipulation under anesthesia and continuous passive motion exercises, with subsequent improvement. No revision surgery was required in either pathway within 24 months.

Representative cases

One representative case was selected from each pathway (CT-3D screw-cement pathway and 2D short-stem pathway).

CT-3D screw-cement case

A 69-year-old patient with end-stage varus KOA and an AORI type II medial tibial defect underwent primary TKA in the CT-3D screw-cement pathway. CT-3D planning quantified the uncontained medial defect and supported selection of a standard posterior-stabilized femoral component and a tibial baseplate with screw-cement augmentation, targeting an mMPTA of 90° and a physiological posterior tibial slope while preserving metaphyseal bone stock. Intraoperatively, the tibial resection depth, coronal alignment, and posterior slope closely followed the CT-3D plan, and screw-cement augmentation was performed at the medial plateau without the use of a tibial stem. 2D short-stem case

A 72-year-old patient with end-stage varus KOA and an AORI type II medial tibial defect underwent primary TKA in the 2D short-stem pathway. Preoperative planning was performed on full-length anteroposterior radiographs by drawing the mechanical axis of the tibia and the planned tibial resection line to restore a neutral mMPTA. Because of the large varus deformity, extensive tibial resection was planned to remove the collapsed medial plateau and obtain a flat tibial cut without residual bone defect. The posterior tibial slope and resection level were further adjusted on the lateral knee radiograph. A posterior-stabilized femoral component and a tibial baseplate with a short cemented extension stem were selected to provide additional metaphyseal-diaphyseal support in the setting of severe varus deformity. Intraoperatively, the tibial resection thickness, coronal alignment, and posterior slope followed the 2D plan, and the final tibial cut surface showed no residual medial defect. The short extension stem was used to provide additional metaphyseal-diaphyseal support and load transfer. Discussion

In this cohort, two predefined reconstructive pathways for primary TKA in end-stage KOA with AORI type I–II medial tibial defects were evaluated: a CT-3D screw-cement pathway and a 2D short-stem pathway with more extensive tibial resection. Both strategies achieved similar tibial alignment, as reflected by postoperative mMPTA, its absolute deviation from 90°, and the proportion of knees within 90° ± 3°, and operative time was also comparable. The stem-free metaphyseal screw-cement pathway supported by CT-3D planning, however, was associated with substantially lower intraoperative blood loss (by approximately 120 mL) and better pain and function at 3 months, whereas functional outcomes were similar by 24 months. This pattern of early clinical benefit without clear mid-term divergence is consistent with reports on CT-based 3D planning-assisted workflows and screw-cement reconstruction [14, 20, 21]. Accordingly, the between-pathway differences observed here should be interpreted as associations reflecting bundled reconstructive strategies, and the independent contribution of AI-assisted planning cannot be isolated in this study.

In primary TKA for AORI type I–II medial tibial defects, the CT-3D screw-cement pathway achieved coronal tibial alignment comparable to that of the 2D short-stem pathway based on postoperative mMPTA outcomes. In addition, no radiographic loosening or revision was observed within 24 months in either pathway. These observations suggest that, when defect morphology and volume are carefully assessed and the osteotomy plane and target alignment are predefined, a bone-preserving screw-cement construct may achieve adequate early metaphyseal support, allowing stem omission without an apparent loss of coronal alignment accuracy, as assessed by mMPTA and radiographs within the 24-month follow-up. In the AORI type I–II cases included in this study, no radiographic signs suggestive of early failure (e.g., subsidence or loosening) were observed within 24 months, while metaphyseal bone stock was preserved by avoiding stem extension and metal augments. This is broadly consistent with previous clinical follow-up studies in patients with moderate tibial defects, which reported that screw-cement techniques achieve favourable short- to mid-term radiographic findings and clinical outcomes comparable to those obtained with metal augments or long-stemmed components [21–23]. Finite element analyses have also shown that, when screw number, trajectory, and diameter are appropriately configured, this construct can improve stress distribution within the defect region and produce a loading pattern around the tibial baseplate that approximates an intact tibia, which in theory may reduce local stress concentration and the risk of early subsidence [9, 24]. In contrast, metal augments and diaphyseal stem extensions provide more rigid structural support but often require additional bone resection or rely on distal canal fixation. Previous studies have suggested that these designs may be associated with a higher incidence of radiolucent lines beneath the metal, distal stress concentration, and stem tip pain [8, 25]. Building on existing work in Rand IIb defects, and according to the currently available literature, this investigation appears to be among the few studies that directly compare short tibial stem extension and bone-preserving screw-cement augmentation within the same cohort. The findings indicate that, in AORI type I–II medial tibial defects with adequate metaphyseal bone stock, screw-cement reconstruction can achieve a level of alignment control and early radiographic stability comparable within the observed follow-up to that of short tibial stem extension, thereby extending previous work on screw-cement and stemmed constructs and providing preliminary pathway-level clinical evidence to guide reconstructive strategy selection for this pattern of defect [11].

At 3 months postoperatively, the CT-3D screw-cement pathway had significantly lower VAS pain scores (2 [1–3] vs 3 [2– 3], P = 0.006), higher KSS (79.7 ± 5.0 vs 77.5 ± 4.1, P = 0.019), and lower WOMAC scores (20 [16–24] vs 23 [19–27], P = 0.017) than the 2D short-stem pathway. By 24 months, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for VAS, KSS, or WOMAC (all P > 0.05). The early advantage is likely multifactorial and may reflect differences in fixation philosophy and reconstruction workflow (stem-free metaphyseal screw-cement support vs short-stem support, along with differing resection and instrumentation strategies), which could influence perioperative blood loss and early rehabilitation [14, 26]. With ongoing rehabilitation, patients in the 2D planning plus stem extension group gradually improved and approached the functional level of the CT-3D screw-cement pathway, consistent with previous reports that different TKA techniques often show only modest mid-term functional differences [27, 28]. Whether CT-3D planning provides incremental benefit beyond the bundled pathway (including implant system and fixation strategy) remains uncertain and will require longer follow-up.

Across the 97 knees included in this study, no deep infection, radiographic loosening, periprosthetic fracture or revision surgery occurred within the 24-month follow-up. This absence of loosening at 24 months is reassuring but not unexpected after primary TKA, and it does not establish mechanical equivalence or long-term fixation durability between constructs. This low event rate suggests that both strategies are safe in the short to midterm for AORI type I–II defects, although the cohort was not powered to detect rare complications. In the screw-cement group, radiographs showed no plateau collapse, screw migration or component subsidence, and periprosthetic bone structure remained stable, consistent with prior reports describing favourable early- to mid-term radiographic performance of screw-cement constructs [20–22]. The stem extension group also showed no radiographic failure, although a few patients reported dull pain around the distal stem tip, a symptom that has been linked to stress concentration at the stem-bone interface in revision cohorts [29]. This discomfort rarely required further surgery but may influence early subjective satisfaction. The present follow-up is too short to assess late fatigue failure or loosening of screw-augmented constructs, especially in patients with high body mass index or severe osteoporosis, and these risks need to be clarified in longer-term studies [20, 22].

Clinically, the present data suggest that a stem-free metaphyseal screw-cement strategy supported by CT-based 3D planning is most suitable for patients with AORI type I–II medial tibial defects, preserved metaphyseal bone stock, and defect depths usually within about 20 mm. In this setting, CT-3D planning helps to delineate the true extent of the defect and the residual metaphyseal support, allowing a 3D reconstruction that avoids metal augments and long stems while keeping a favourable bone bed for potential future revision [20, 30]. For these moderate defects, screw-cement augmentation is technically straightforward and relatively economical, and several series have reported clinical outcomes comparable to those of stemmed and augmented constructs [20, 30]. By contrast, in AORI type III defects, extensive metaphyseal bone loss, severe osteoporosis, or multiply operated knees, metaphyseal support is often inadequate and long stems, large metal augments, or structural grafts remain the preferred options [6, 30]. The present results should not be extrapolated to these high-risk patterns.

CT-based 3D planning has practical implications for both imaging and implant costs. It requires preoperative CT scans and access to dedicated planning software, which increases upfront expenditure. However, more precise defect assessment may prevent unnecessary use of stem extensions or large metal augments and may simplify future revision, partially offsetting the initial expense [31]. Combined with published experience in complex arthroplasty [15, 32, 33], the current findings support a practical role for CT-3D planning as a preoperative decision tool for managing tibial defects. Defining its cost-effectiveness and optimal indications across different defect patterns will require prospective multicentre studies with longer follow-up.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. This was a single-centre retrospective cohort with a modest sample size, particularly in the CT-3D screw-cement pathway (n = 38), which increases the risk of selection bias, limits generalisability, and reduces statistical power to detect rare complications or revisions. Treatment allocation followed two predefined reconstructive pathways rather than randomisation, and although adjusted analyses were performed, residual confounding by preoperative deformity, defect size, bone quality, and other clinical factors is likely. Importantly, defect morphology and intraoperative judgement may have influenced pathway selection, and these factors may not be fully captured by available covariates; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. The comparison was intentionally pathway-level: planning modality (CT-3D vs 2D radiographs), implant system (Attune vs AK), and reconstruction method (bone-preserving screw-cement vs short tibial stem extension) were coupled, so the data inform decisions between these bundled strategies rather than the isolated effects of CT-3D planning, a specific implant design, or a single reconstruction technique. Radiographic measurements were obtained by two independent observers using predefined protocols, but formal inter- and intra-observer reliability statistics were not calculated, and the 2-year follow-up provides only mid-term radiographic and clinical information. Therefore, statements regarding ‘stability’ are limited to early radiographic stability within the observed period, and long-term fixation durability or mechanical equivalence cannot be inferred, particularly in patients with high body mass index or severe osteoporosis [34]. Finally, all cases involved AORI type I–II medial tibial defects, and the sample size did not permit robust subgroup analyses by defect severity; the results should therefore not be extrapolated to AORI type III defects or more extensive bone loss, in which long stems, large augments, or structural grafts are still often required.

These findings support the feasibility of a stem-free screw-cement construct implemented within a CT-3D planning pathway for selected AORI type I–II medial tibial defects with adequate metaphyseal bone stock. Given the non-randomised allocation and the bundled nature of the comparison (planning modality, implant system, and fixation strategy were coupled), the results are associative and warrant longer-term multicentre prospective validation with prespecified indications and implant-related endpoints.

Conclusions

In this single-centre two-year retrospective cohort of primary TKA for end-stage KOA with AORI type I–II medial tibial defects, the CT-3D screw-cement pathway and the 2D short-stem pathway achieved comparable postoperative mMPTA alignment and 24-month functional outcomes. The CT-3D pathway was associated with lower intraoperative blood loss and better pain and function at 3 months, and no deep infection, revision, or radiographic loosening was observed within 24 months, which is consistent with early radiographic stability during the observed follow-up. In selected patients with preserved metaphyseal bone stock, a CT-based 3D planning-implemented stem-free screw-cement construct (with AI-assisted segmentation as a software implementation step) may be a bone-preserving option. Multicentre prospective confirmation with longer follow-up is warranted.
