Physics-Constrained Learning of Energy-Preserving Stencils for Maxwell’s Equations
Victory C. Obieke

Abstract
We study data-driven construction of spatial discretizations for the one-dimensional Maxwell system. Starting from high-fidelity spectral training data, we learn a linear convolution stencil that approximates the spatial derivative in Maxwell’s equations. The stencil coefficients are obtained from a convex quadratic program with linear constraints that enforce skew-adjointness of the discrete derivative; these constraints guarantee a semi-discrete electromagnetic energy identity and yield a CFL condition expressed directly in terms of the stencil’s Fourier symbol. We show that the learned operator has a purely imaginary symbol, induces purely oscillatory modal dynamics, and preserves the discrete Maxwell energy exactly under Crank–Nicolson time-stepping. Numerical experiments with smooth traveling-wave solutions demonstrate that the energy-constrained, ADMM-learned stencil with radius R = 1 R=1 achieves approximately second-order spatial convergence in the PDE error, matching the expected order of the standard centered finite-difference stencil. We compare projected gradient, Nesterov-accelerated gradient, ADMM, and an interior-point solver for the resulting quadratic program, and we illustrate extensions to nonstandard target operators and noisy training data. Overall, the learned stencils achieve accuracy comparable to classical central differences while exactly preserving a discrete electromagnetic energy, making them viable drop-in, structure-preserving spatial operators for finite-difference time-domain methods and data-driven PDE solvers.



1
Introduction

Finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) methods are a standard tool for
solving Maxwell’s equations in computational electromagnetics

[
25
,
26
]
. Classical schemes, such as the Yee
method
[
28
]
, are derived directly from the continuous
equations: spatial derivatives are approximated using fixed
central-difference stencils, and stability is enforced via analytical
CFL conditions. These schemes are carefully designed so that the
discrete curl operator is skew-adjoint, which yields exact
semi-discrete energy conservation and compatible discrete divergence
constraints.

Recently, there has been growing interest in
data-driven
discretizations
, in which discrete operators are learned from
numerical or experimental data, for example via regression or neural
networks. Examples include data-driven finite-difference
discretizations for Burgers’ equation and the Navier–Stokes equations

[
1
]
, data-driven closures for turbulent flows

[
21
,
14
]
, and sparse-regression or
neural approaches for discovering governing equations and hidden
dynamics
[
24
,
22
,
7
]
. These
methods typically aim to minimize approximation error on a chosen
training distribution, but they do not, in general, explicitly enforce
structural properties such as stability, conservation, or energy
balance.

For Maxwell’s equations, this is a significant limitation. Classical
FDTD schemes are constructed so that discrete versions of divergence
constraints and energy identities hold at the semi-discrete level,
ensuring that numerical solutions respect key physical invariants.
Learned operators that ignore these structures risk exhibiting
uncontrolled numerical dissipation or growth, even if they fit the
training data well.

In this work we address this gap in a simple but concrete setting: the
one-dimensional Maxwell system with periodic boundary conditions. Our
goal is to learn a discrete spatial derivative operator

D:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N},

represented as a convolution stencil, directly from data. We impose
linear constraints on the stencil that enforce
skew-adjointness

of
D
with respect to the discrete
L^{2}
inner product. This structural
constraint guarantees exact conservation of discrete electromagnetic
energy at the semi-discrete level, independently of the details of the
training data or the optimization procedure.

Our approach is inspired by two lines of work. On the one hand,
classical structure-preserving discretizations for Maxwell and related
PDEs use staggered grids and skew-adjoint discrete curl operators to
obtain discrete energy identities and long-time stability

[
4
,
8
,
19
]
. High-order and
operator-splitting variants maintain these properties by design

[
4
,
3
]
. On the other hand,
data-driven PDE schemes learn local stencils or coarse-grid operators
from high-fidelity simulations, often achieving accuracy comparable to
or better than hand-crafted schemes on specific tasks

[
1
,
11
,
6
]
. However,
these learned operators usually encode physical structure only
implicitly, through the choice of loss function, training data, or
architecture, rather than via hard constraints.

Related ideas also appear in recent neural-operator surrogates for
electromagnetics and other time-dependent PDEs. Dynamic-convolution
neural operators for FDTD-like EM propagation impose causal receptive
fields and material-dispersion structure

[
17
,
13
]
. Domain-decomposition neural
operators couple learned subdomain solvers through interface penalties
or consistency conditions
[
18
,
10
]
.
Other frameworks embed conservation laws via soft constraints or
corrective layers
[
16
,
15
,
27
]
. These
approaches can significantly improve stability and physical fidelity,
but they generally enforce conservation or energy balance only
approximately, depending on optimization and hyperparameters.

Building on this context, our main contributions are as follows.
We formulate a convex quadratic optimization problem for
learning convolution stencils for the 1D Maxwell system from spectral
training data. The objective is a least-squares misfit between the
learned discrete derivative and high-fidelity derivatives, subject to
linear constraints that encode skew-adjointness of the stencil
coefficients together with simple box bounds.
We show that these linear constraints are exactly equivalent
to skew-adjointness of the corresponding convolution operator with
respect to the discrete
L^{2}
inner product, which in turn yields an
exact semi-discrete electromagnetic energy identity and expresses the
CFL condition directly in terms of the stencil’s Fourier symbol

\mu(\theta)
. This yields, to our knowledge, the first learned
Maxwell stencil with a
guaranteed
semi-discrete energy
conservation law.
We characterize the numerical wave speed and associated CFL
restriction of the learned discrete curl operator via this symbol.
We implement projected gradient, Nesterov-accelerated
gradient, ADMM, and an interior-point method to solve the constrained
quadratic program, and we benchmark the resulting stencils in
time-dependent 1D Maxwell simulations. We also illustrate how the same
constrained learning framework can recover nonstandard, coarse-grid
effective operators and regularize synthetic spectral training data
contaminated by noise or mild inconsistencies. In particular, the
ADMM-learned radius-
R=1
stencil exhibits approximately second-order
spatial convergence for smooth traveling-wave solutions, matching the
expected order of the standard centered finite-difference stencil it
approximates. The experiments compare PDE error, energy-constraint
residuals, and energy evolution across stencil radii, and demonstrate
that ADMM and the interior-point solver produce nearly identical,
highly accurate stencils, with ADMM offering a favorable tradeoff
between accuracy, constraint satisfaction, and runtime. Our results
illustrate how even simple linear constraints in a data-driven
discretization can encode important physics and restore structural
properties such as energy conservation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first data-driven convolution stencil for the one-dimensional Maxwell system whose learned coefficients are constrained to guarantee a semi-discrete electromagnetic energy identity.

The paper is organized as follows. Sections 2–3 introduce the
semi-discrete 1D Maxwell system, derive the corresponding discrete
energy identity, and analyze the numerical wave speed and CFL
restriction associated with the learned operator. Sections 4–6
describe the learning formulation and optimization algorithms, and
present numerical experiments that evaluate accuracy, energy behavior, dispersion,
and robustness to nonstandard targets and noisy training data.

2
Semi-discrete Maxwell system and energy identity

We consider the 1D Maxwell system in vacuum,
written in non-dimensional form with
\varepsilon_{0}=\mu_{0}=1
:

\partial_{t}E=\partial_{x}H,\qquad\partial_{t}H=\partial_{x}E,

(1)

on the periodic domain
[0,L]
.

We discretize space using a uniform grid
x_{i}=i\Delta x
,

i=0,\dots,N-1
, with
\Delta x=L/N
, and collect the nodal values
of the electric and magnetic fields into vectors

\mathbf{E}(t)=(E_{0}(t),\dots,E_{N-1}(t))^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{H}(t)=(H_{0}(t),\dots,H_{N-1}(t))^{\top}.

Let
D:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
denote a linear discrete derivative operator.
In this work,
D
is a
convolution operator
defined by a stencil

w\in\mathbb{R}^{2R+1}
,

(D\mathbf{u})_{i}=\sum_{\ell=-R}^{R}w_{\ell}\,u_{i+\ell},\qquad i=0,\dots,N-1,

(2)

with periodic wrap-around in the index
i+\ell
.
The semi-discrete Maxwell system then reads

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{E}(t)=D\mathbf{H}(t),\qquad\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{H}(t)=D\mathbf{E}(t).

(3)

We equip
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with the standard discrete
L^{2}
inner product

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle:=\Delta x\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}u_{i}v_{i},\qquad\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}:=\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}\rangle.

(4)

The corresponding discrete electromagnetic energy is

\mathcal{E}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{E}(t)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{H}(t)\|^{2}.

(5)

Theorem 1

(Semi-discrete energy identity)
.

Let
D
be any linear operator on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and let

\mathbf{E}(t),\mathbf{H}(t)
satisfy
(
3
). Then

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t)=\langle D\mathbf{H}(t),\mathbf{E}(t)\rangle+\langle D\mathbf{E}(t),\mathbf{H}(t)\rangle.

(6)

In particular, if
D
is skew-adjoint with respect to
(
4
), i.e.,

\langle D\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle=-\langle\mathbf{u},D\mathbf{v}\rangle\quad\forall\,\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},

(7)

then the discrete energy is exactly conserved:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t)=0.

(8)

Proof.

Differentiating (
5
) in time and using
(
3
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{E}(t),\mathbf{E}(t)\right\rangle+\left\langle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{H}(t),\mathbf{H}(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle D\mathbf{H}(t),\mathbf{E}(t)\rangle+\langle D\mathbf{E}(t),\mathbf{H}(t)\rangle,

which is (
6
).
If
D
is skew-adjoint, then

\langle D\mathbf{H},\mathbf{E}\rangle=-\langle\mathbf{H},D\mathbf{E}\rangle=-\langle D\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H}\rangle,

so the two terms in
(
6
) cancel and

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(t)=0
.
∎

2.1
Skew-symmetric convolution operators and stencil constraints

We now specialize to convolution operators of the form
(
2
) with periodic boundary conditions.
Let
w=(w_{-R},\dots,w_{-1},w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{R})^{\top}
be the stencil.

Lemma 1
.

Let
D
be defined by (
2
) with periodic wrap-around.
Then the matrix entries of
D
satisfy

D_{ij}=w_{i-j},\qquad i,j=0,\dots,N-1,

(9)

where the difference
i-j
is taken modulo
N
.

Proof.

By definition,

(D\mathbf{u})_{i}=\sum_{\ell=-R}^{R}w_{\ell}\,u_{i+\ell}=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}w_{i-j}\,u_{j},

where
j=i+\ell
(modulo
N
). Hence
D_{ij}=w_{i-j}
; see, e.g.,
[
4
]
.
∎

Theorem 2

(Characterization of skew-symmetric stencils)
.

Let
D
be the convolution operator (
2
) with
periodic boundary conditions and stencil
w\in\mathbb{R}^{2R+1}
.
Then
D
is skew-adjoint with respect to (
4
),
i.e.,
D^{\top}=-D
, if and only if the stencil satisfies

w_{0}=0,\qquad w_{-\ell}=-w_{+\ell},\quad\ell=1,\dots,R.

(10)

Proof.

By Lemma
1

the entries of
D
are
D_{ij}=w_{i-j}
, so

(D^{\top})_{ij}=D_{ji}=w_{j-i}.

The condition
D^{\top}=-D
is therefore equivalent to

w_{j-i}=-w_{i-j}\quad\text{for all }i,j,

or, writing
\ell=i-j
,

w_{-\ell}=-w_{+\ell}\quad\text{for all }\ell.

In particular, taking
\ell=0
yields
w_{0}=-w_{0}
, hence
w_{0}=0
,
and for
\ell=1,\dots,R
we obtain
w_{-\ell}=-w_{+\ell}
.
Conversely, if (
10
) holds, then the above
computation shows
D^{\top}=-D
, i.e.,
D
is skew-adjoint.
∎

We encode the conditions (
10
) as a set of linear
constraints on the stencil vector
w
.
Let
C\in\mathbb{R}^{(R+1)\times(2R+1)}
be defined by

(Cw)_{0}=w_{0},\qquad(Cw)_{\ell}=w_{-\ell}+w_{+\ell},\quad\ell=1,\dots,R,

and let
d\in\mathbb{R}^{R+1}
be the zero vector.
Then the constraint
Cw=d
is simply

w_{0}=0,\qquad w_{-\ell}+w_{+\ell}=0,\quad\ell=1,\dots,R,

(11)

which is equivalent to skew-symmetry.

Corollary 1
.

Let
C
and
d
be defined in (
11
).
Then
Cw=d
if and only if the stencil
w
satisfies
(
10
), i.e., if and only if the corresponding
convolution operator
D
is skew-adjoint.

Proof.

Immediate from (
11
) and Theorem
2
.
∎

Corollary
1
shows that the linear constraint
Cw=d
used
in our optimization problem is
exactly
the condition required for
semi-discrete energy conservation via Theorem
1
.

3
Wave speed and CFL for the learned operator

The discrete curl operator
D
constructed in
Section
2.1
is a periodic convolution with stencil

w=(w_{-R},\dots,w_{0},\dots,w_{R})
satisfying the skew-symmetry
constraints of Theorem
2
. For such an operator it
is convenient to work with its Fourier symbol

\mu(\theta):=\sum_{k=-R}^{R}w_{k}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\theta},\qquad\theta\in[-\pi,\pi],

(12)

which characterizes the response of
D
to discrete Fourier modes

\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}j\theta}
.

For the semi-discrete Maxwell system

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}\\
\mathbf{E}\end{bmatrix}=A\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}\\
\mathbf{E}\end{bmatrix},\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}0&D\\
D^{T}&0\end{bmatrix},

the eigenvalues of
A
are purely imaginary and can be written as

\lambda(\theta)=\pm\mathrm{i}\,|\mu(\theta)|
for

\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]
. In particular, the quantity

|\mu(\theta)|
plays the role of a
numerical wave speed
for
the mode
\theta
, and the maximal value

c_{\max}:=\max_{\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]}|\mu(\theta)|

(13)

is the largest speed supported by the learned operator. For the
standard centered difference stencil of width three one recovers

c_{\max}\sim h^{-1}
, corresponding to the usual CFL restriction for
explicit wave propagation schemes.

If one were to apply the classical leapfrog
scheme to the semi-discrete system, stability of the scalar test
equation
\dot{u}=\lambda u
with
\lambda=\mathrm{i}\omega

requires
\Delta t\,|\omega|\leq 2
, see, e.g.,

[
23
,
20
,
12
]
. Substituting

\omega=\pm|\mu(\theta)|
and maximizing over
\theta
yields the
CFL condition

\Delta t\;\leq\;\frac{2}{c_{\max}},

(14)

which expresses the admissible time step solely in terms of the
learned symbol (
12
). In this way the learned
stencil can be interpreted within the familiar CFL framework for
explicit FDTD schemes, although in our numerical experiments we will
use an implicit time integrator. In the present work we focus on CN,
which is unconditionally stable for the skew-adjoint semi-discrete
operator. A systematic comparison with leapfrog using the derived CFL
bound is left for future work.

3.1
Crank–Nicolson time integration

For the actual time-dependent simulations in
Section
6
we use the Crank–Nicolson (CN) method
applied to the semi-discrete system

\frac{d\mathbf{U}}{dt}=A\mathbf{U},\qquad\mathbf{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}\\
\mathbf{E}\end{bmatrix},\qquad A^{T}=-A.

The CN update reads

\frac{\mathbf{U}^{n+1}-\mathbf{U}^{n}}{\Delta t}=A\,\frac{\mathbf{U}^{n+1}+\mathbf{U}^{n}}{2},

(15)

which coincides with the implicit midpoint rule for linear systems.
Since
A
is skew-adjoint, the discrete energy

\mathcal{E}^{n}:=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{U}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\|\mathbf{H}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{E}^{n}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)

is preserved exactly by (
15
) for any time
step
\Delta t>0
; see, e.g.,
[
8
,
9
]
for
background on such structure-preserving schemes.

In particular, CN does not require the CFL restriction
(
14
) for stability or energy conservation in this
linear, skew-adjoint setting. In the numerical experiments we
nevertheless choose a time step
\Delta t
proportional to the grid
spacing
h
and small enough that (
14
) would be
satisfied for the corresponding centered-difference operator. This
ensures that temporal discretization errors remain small relative to
the spatial error while allowing us to interpret the learned stencil
in terms of an effective wave speed
c_{\max}
.

4
Data-driven stencil learning

We now formulate the quadratic optimization problem used to
learn stencils from data generated by a high-fidelity discretization.

4.1
Training data from spectral 1D Maxwell

We generate training data by sampling random Maxwell
states and computing their time derivatives exactly (up to spectral
discretization error).
We work on the periodic domain
[0,L]
with
N
grid points and
spacing
\Delta x=L/N
.
For each training sample we draw a random truncated Fourier series

\displaystyle E(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{m_{\max}}a_{m}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi mx}{L}+\phi_{m}\right),

\displaystyle H(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{m_{\max}}b_{m}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi mx}{L}+\psi_{m}\right),

with amplitudes
a_{m},b_{m}
and phases
\phi_{m},\psi_{m}
drawn from simple
Gaussian and uniform distributions.
We then compute the spatial derivatives
\partial_{x}E
and

\partial_{x}H
spectrally using FFTs:

\partial_{x}E=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left(\mathrm{i}k\,\widehat{E}\right),\qquad\partial_{x}H=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left(\mathrm{i}k\,\widehat{H}\right),

where
k
is the discrete wavenumber.
Finally, we obtain the time derivatives from the PDE
(
1
):

\partial_{t}E=\partial_{x}H,\qquad\partial_{t}H=\partial_{x}E.

We denote by

U\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{sims}}\times 2\times N}

the array of training states, where

U[s,0,i]=E^{(s)}(x_{i})
and
U[s,1,i]=H^{(s)}(x_{i})

denote the electric and magnetic fields of the
s
-th training sample
evaluated at the grid point
x_{i}
.
Similarly,

\frac{dU}{dt}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{sims}}\times 2\times N}

stores the corresponding time derivatives, with

(dU/dt)[s,0,i]=\partial_{t}E^{(s)}(x_{i})
and

(dU/dt)[s,1,i]=\partial_{t}H^{(s)}(x_{i})
.
Here
n_{\mathrm{sims}}
denotes the number of independent training
samples (or snapshots), each consisting of a randomly generated Maxwell
state
(E,H)
and its corresponding time derivative.
The spatial domain
[0,L]
is discretized using
N
uniformly spaced
grid points
x_{i}=i\Delta x
,
i=0,\dots,N-1
, where
\Delta x=L/N
.

4.2
Least-squares formulation

Given training pairs
(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H})
and

(\partial_{t}\mathbf{E},\partial_{t}\mathbf{H})
, we wish to learn a
single convolution stencil
w
such that

D\mathbf{H}\approx\partial_{x}\mathbf{H}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{E},\qquad D\mathbf{E}\approx\partial_{x}\mathbf{E}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{H},

in a least-squares sense. Because we assume periodic boundary
conditions, the stencil is applied identically at every grid point,
with indices interpreted modulo
N
.

For each grid index
i
we extract local patches

\mathbf{h}_{i}=(H_{i-R},\dots,H_{i+R})^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{e}_{i}=(E_{i-R},\dots,E_{i+R})^{\top},

and define scalar targets

y^{(H)}_{i}=\partial_{t}E_{i},\qquad y^{(E)}_{i}=\partial_{t}H_{i}.

The desired relationships are

w^{\top}\mathbf{h}_{i}\approx y^{(H)}_{i},\qquad w^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{i}\approx y^{(E)}_{i}.

Stacking all patches and targets over space and all simulations, we
obtain a linear system

Aw\approx b,

(16)

where each row of
A
is either
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\top}
or

\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}
, and
b
collects the corresponding targets.
This leads to the unconstrained least-squares problem

\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2R+1}}\frac{1}{2}\|Aw-b\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|_{2}^{2},

(17)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is a small Tikhonov regularization parameter.

4.3
Energy constraint and box bounds

To enforce skew-adjointness and hence discrete energy conservation, we
impose the linear constraint
Cw=d
with
C
and
d
defined as in
Section
2.1
.
By Corollary
1
, any stencil satisfying
Cw=d

defines a skew-adjoint convolution operator
D
and therefore an
energy-conserving semi-discrete Maxwell system.

We also impose simple box bounds
|w_{\ell}|\leq M
, i.e.,

-M\leq w_{\ell}\leq M,\qquad\ell=-R,\dots,R,

(18)

where
M>0
is chosen large enough to contain the true derivative
stencil but small enough to control numerical conditioning.

Our final optimization problem is the convex quadratic program

\displaystyle\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2R+1}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|Aw-b\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|_{2}^{2},

(19)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle Cw=d,

\displaystyle-M\leq w\leq M.

This problem is strictly convex for
\lambda>0
and admits a unique
solution.
For our first-order methods we use a slightly simplified variant of
(
19
) in which only the linear energy constraint is enforced.
Specifically, projected gradient (PG) and Nesterov-accelerated gradient
(NAG) are applied to the equality-constrained problem

\displaystyle\min_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2R+1}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|Aw-b\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|_{2}^{2},

(20)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle Cw=d,

while the full equality-plus-box-constrained problem (
19
) is
handled by ADMM and the interior-point solver in CVXPY. In our
experiments the solutions of (
20
) produced by PG and NAG
remain well inside the box
[-M,M]^{2R+1}
, so the difference between
(
20
) and (
19
) is negligible in practice.
In the presence of noisy or mildly inconsistent training data, the least-squares
system can become ill-conditioned and produce stencils with very large
coefficients; the Tikhonov term and box bounds mitigate this behavior, as
illustrated in Section
6.7
.

5
Convex solvers

We briefly summarize the algorithms used to solve (
19
).
All methods operate on the same design matrix
A
, constraint matrix

C
, and bounds.

5.1
Projected gradient methods

Our first two solvers are projected gradient (PG) and
Nesterov-accelerated gradient (NAG), applied to the
equality-constrained

problem (
20
). In particular, for PG/NAG we enforce only the linear
energy constraint
Cw=d
and do not explicitly impose the box bounds

-M\leq w\leq M
. In our experiments the PG/NAG iterates remain strictly inside the
box, so the resulting solutions agree (to numerical tolerance) with those of the
fully constrained problem (
19
).

Define the feasible affine subspace

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{eq}}=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2R+1}:Cw=d\}.

The orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{eq}}
with respect to
the Euclidean inner product is given explicitly by

P_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{eq}}}(z):=z-C^{\top}(CC^{\top})^{-1}(Cz-d),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2R+1},

(21)

assuming that
C
has full row rank.

Let

f(w):=\tfrac{1}{2}\|Aw-b\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\nabla f(w)=A^{\top}(Aw-b)+\lambda w.

We choose a stepsize
\alpha=1/L
, where one may take

L=\|A\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda=\|A^{\top}A\|_{2}+\lambda
.

For PG, the update is

w^{k+1}=P_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{eq}}}\!\left(w^{k}-\alpha\nabla f(w^{k})\right),

with
P_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{eq}}}
given by (
21
).

For NAG we use the extrapolated point

y^{k}=w^{k}+\beta_{k}(w^{k}-w^{k-1}),

and then update

w^{k+1}=P_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{eq}}}\!\left(y^{k}-\alpha\nabla f(y^{k})\right),

with the standard parameters

t_{k+1}=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(1+\sqrt{1+4t_{k}^{2}}\right),\qquad\beta_{k}=\frac{t_{k}-1}{t_{k+1}},\qquad t_{0}=1.

We track the objective
f(w^{k})
, wall-clock time, and the equality
residual
\|Cw^{k}-d\|_{2}
at each iteration.

Remark 1
.

Projecting sequentially onto
\{Cw=d\}
and the box
[-M,M]^{2R+1}
is not, in
general, the exact Euclidean projection onto their intersection. If the box
constraint becomes active and one wishes to enforce both constraints within a
projected-gradient framework, one should compute
P_{\{Cw=d\}\cap[-M,M]^{2R+1}}

using an intersection projection method such as Dykstra’s algorithm, or solve the
projection step as a small quadratic program.

5.2
ADMM

To better handle the combination of equality and box constraints, we
also implement an alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
[
5
]

scheme.
We introduce a copy
z
of
w
and enforce the box constraint on
z

only:

w\in\mathbb{R}^{2R+1},\qquad z\in[-M,M]^{2R+1},\qquad w=z,\qquad Cw=d.

The augmented Lagrangian splits into a quadratic
w
-problem with
equality constraints and a simple proximal
z
-update given by box
projection.

\begin{bmatrix}A^{\top}A+(\lambda+\rho)I&C^{\top}\\

7
Conclusion

We proposed an energy-conserving, data-driven discretization framework for
the one-dimensional Maxwell system. By learning convolution stencils from
spectral training data under a linear skew-symmetry constraint, we obtain
discrete derivative operators that are guaranteed to be skew-adjoint and
therefore exactly conserve semi-discrete electromagnetic energy. Numerical
experiments show that the resulting schemes match the accuracy of standard
central differences while preserving these structural properties. Using
Crank–Nicolson time integration, we further confirmed that the discrete
energy remains constant to roundoff for learned
stencils satisfying the skew-adjoint constraint.

8
Discussion and extensions

Our results show that simple linear constraints can encode key physics in
data-driven discretizations: enforcing
Cw=d
yields skew-adjoint spatial
operators that exactly preserve a semi-discrete energy identity, while remaining
close to standard centered differences in the homogeneous 1D setting.

To probe more realistic regimes, we also considered a nonstandard target
operator whose stencil does not match any classical centered-difference formula
and showed that the constrained learner accurately recovers its space–time
behavior, while the matching CD stencil incurs noticeable error, and a
noisy regression experiment in which unconstrained least squares produces large
coefficients and severe energy drift, whereas the energy- and box-constrained
stencil stays well-conditioned, energy-stable, and faithful to the clean
baseline
E(x,t)
.

These examples suggest that physics-motivated constraints can regularize noisy
or mildly mis-specified regression problems and make it feasible to learn
effective Maxwell operators in settings where analytic stencils are difficult to
derive or tune (e.g., heterogeneous media or band-limited/experimental data).
A natural next step is to extend the same constrained-learning philosophy to
staggered curl operators on Yee grids in higher dimensions, enforcing block skew-symmetry to
preserve a discrete Poynting theorem, and to couple the learned operators with
simple dispersive material models and absorbing layers in more realistic FDTD
setups.

Appendix: Spectral structure of the learned stencil

Theorem 3

(Spectral structure and modal energy conservation for the learned stencil)
.

Let
w^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{2R+1}
be any solution of the quadratic program
(
19
) (or the equality-constrained variant (
20
)), and let

D^{\star}
be the associated periodic convolution operator defined by
(
2
). Let
\mu^{\star}(\theta)
denote the Fourier symbol of

D^{\star}
,

\mu^{\star}(\theta):=\sum_{k=-R}^{R}w_{k}^{\star}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k\theta},\qquad\theta\in[-\pi,\pi].

Then the following hold:

(i)

Because
w^{\star}
satisfies the linear constraint
Cw^{\star}=d
,
it is skew-symmetric in the sense of (
10
), i.e.

w_{0}^{\star}=0,\qquad w_{-\ell}^{\star}=-w_{+\ell}^{\star},\quad\ell=1,\dots,R.

Consequently, the symbol
\mu^{\star}(\theta)
is purely imaginary and odd:

\mu^{\star}(\theta)\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R},\qquad\mu^{\star}(-\theta)=-\mu^{\star}(\theta)\quad\text{for all }\theta\in[-\pi,\pi].

(ii)

Consider the semi-discrete Maxwell system with the learned operator

D^{\star}
,

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}\\[2.5pt]
\mathbf{E}\end{bmatrix}=A^{\star}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}\\[2.5pt]
\mathbf{E}\end{bmatrix},\qquad A^{\star}=\begin{bmatrix}0&D^{\star}\\
(D^{\star})^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix},

together with the discrete
L^{2}
inner product (
4
) and
energy (
5
). Then
A^{\star}
is skew-adjoint and
diagonalizable in the discrete Fourier basis. For each discrete wavenumber

\theta
, the corresponding
2\times 2
block has eigenvalues

\lambda_{\pm}^{\star}(\theta)=\pm\,\mathrm{i}\,|\mu^{\star}(\theta)|.

In particular, each Fourier mode evolves as a harmonic oscillator with
frequency
|\mu^{\star}(\theta)|
.

(iii)

The discrete electromagnetic energy associated with the learned
stencil can be written as a sum of modal contributions and is conserved

mode by mode
. If
\widehat{E}^{\star}(\theta,t)
and

\widehat{H}^{\star}(\theta,t)
denote the discrete Fourier coefficients of

\mathbf{E}(t)
and
\mathbf{H}(t)
, then

\mathcal{E}(t)=\frac{\Delta x}{2}\sum_{\theta}\bigl(|\widehat{E}^{\star}(\theta,t)|^{2}+|\widehat{H}^{\star}(\theta,t)|^{2}\bigr),

and for each
\theta
one has

\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(|\widehat{E}^{\star}(\theta,t)|^{2}+|\widehat{H}^{\star}(\theta,t)|^{2}\bigr)=0.

Thus the total discrete energy and each modal energy contribution are
preserved exactly by the semi-discrete learned Maxwell system.

Proof.

Because
w^{\star}
is a solution of (
19
) or (
20
), it
satisfies the linear constraint
Cw^{\star}=d
by construction. By
Corollary
1
, this is equivalent to the skew-symmetry
conditions (
10
), which immediately imply the structure of

\mu^{\star}(\theta)
and the identities in part (i) via the same calculation as
in Theorem
2
.

Skew-symmetry of
w^{\star}
implies
(D^{\star})^{\top}=-D^{\star}
, so the block
matrix
A^{\star}
is skew-adjoint with respect to the discrete inner product
(
4
). Since
D^{\star}
is a periodic convolution,
it is diagonalized by the discrete Fourier transform; the
2\times 2
block
for each mode
\theta
has the form

A^{\star}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mu^{\star}(\theta)\\
-\mu^{\star}(\theta)&0\end{bmatrix},

whose eigenvalues are
\lambda_{\pm}^{\star}(\theta)=\pm\,\mathrm{i}\,|\mu^{\star}(\theta)|
,
yielding part (ii).

Finally, unitarity of the discrete Fourier transform implies that the
discrete
L^{2}
norms of
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{H}
are equal to the sums
of squared modal amplitudes, so the energy
\mathcal{E}(t)
decomposes as in
part (iii). For each fixed
\theta

2\times 2
system governed by the
skew-Hermitian matrix

preserves the Euclidean norm of

(\widehat{H}^{\star}(\theta,t),\widehat{E}^{\star}(\theta,t))
, which implies
modal energy conservation and hence conservation of the total discrete energy.
