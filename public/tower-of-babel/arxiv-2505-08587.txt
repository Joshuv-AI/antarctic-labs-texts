Two-Level Sketching Alternating Anderson acceleration for Complex Physics Applications
Nicolas A. Barnafi, Massimiliano Lupo Pasini, Nicolás A. Barnafi, Massimiliano Lupo Pasini

Abstract
We present a novel two‐level sketching extension of the Alternating Anderson–Picard (AAP) method for accelerating fixed‐point iterations in challenging single‐ and multi‐physics simulations governed by discretized partial differential equations. Our approach combines a static, physics‐based projection that reduces the least‐squares problem to the most informative field (e.g., via Schur–complement insight) with a dynamic, algebraic sketching stage driven by a backward stability analysis under Lipschitz continuity. We introduce inexpensive estimators for stability thresholds and cache‐aware randomized selection strategies to balance computational cost against memory‐access overhead. The resulting algorithm solves reduced least‐squares systems in place, minimizes memory footprints, and seamlessly alternates between low‐cost Picard updates and Anderson mixing. Implemented in Julia, our two‐level sketching AAP achieves up to 50% time‐to‐solution reductions compared to standard Anderson acceleration—without degrading convergence rates—on benchmark problems including Stokes, p p ‐Laplacian, Bidomain, and Navier–Stokes formulations at varying problem sizes. These results demonstrate the method’s robustness, scalability, and potential for integration into high‐performance scientific computing frameworks.

Introduction

The
aa
(
aa
) method
[
And65
]
is a multi-secant method
[
BRZS18
,
BCRZS21
]
that has been widely used either to improve the convergence rate of convergent fixed-point schemes or to restore convergence when the original fixed-point scheme is not convergent
[
FS09
,
WN11
,
PE13
,
ZOB20
,
BCRZS21
]
. In particular, the convergence of
aa
has been studied with respect to specific properties of different scientific applications
[
TK15
,
TEE+17
,
MJ19
,
BCK21
]
.

aa
requires solving a least-squares (LS) problem at each fixed-point iteration where up to all previous iterations are used. This can be computationally expensive for scientific applications that involve large-scale calculations, especially when such calculations are distributed on high-performance computing (HPC) platforms.
To reduce the computational cost of
aa
for the solution of linear systems, a recently proposed variant of
aa
called
aar
(
aar
)
[
BSP16
,
PSP16
,
SPP19
]
performs multiple (inexpensive) Richardson iterations between two consecutive Anderson mixing corrections. This can be naturally extended to the non-linear setting by using Picard iterations, and is known as the
aap
(
aap
) method
[
FLS25
]
. The number of fixed-point iterations between two consecutive Anderson mixing corrections can be arbitrarily set by the user. When no such iterations are performed,
aap
becomes
aa
. Recent theoretical results establish that for linear systems
aar
is more robust than the standard
aa
against stagnations
[
LP19
]
. However, even if performed intermittently, computing the Anderson mixing via least-squares can still be computationally expensive when the number of rows in the tall and skinny matrix that defines the left-hand side (LHS) of the LS problem is large.

Various literature contributions assessed the computational advantages of replacing accurate and expensive computations with inaccurate and inexpensive ones in
aap
, either by approximating the evaluation of the fixed-point operator or by reducing the accuracy to solve the LS problem.
For specific problems, the accuracy and computational costs are controlled using physics information, which results in computational savings without affecting the final convergence of the fixed-point scheme
[
FZB20
,
OPY+20
]
.
For situations where physics-driven guidelines are difficult (and often impossible) to determine,
[
LPL24
]
proposed more general guidelines motivated by forward and backward error analysis, which propose to judiciously subselect the rows of the tall and skinny matrix associated with the LS problem via adaptive sketching
[
Woo14
,
Mah11
,
MDM+23
]
.

In this work, we extend the backward stability analysis performed in
[
LPL24
]
to non-linear fixed-point iterations under the assumption of Lipschitz continuity of the fixed-point operator, and we propose a two-level sketching technique to project the LS problem in a subspace and thereby reduce the computational cost of solving it while still ensuring convergence of
aap
to the desired accuracy. The first sketching is motivated by the physics, and consists in projecting the residual onto a subset of the fields that characterize the physics of the phenomenon of interest. The use of the first sketching level is motivated by the fact that (i) multi-physics problems can be recast as a system of only one of their variables without losing information (such as the Schur complement in linear systems), and (ii) different fields can attain values with very different scales, so that combining them can hinder the overall conditioning of the LS problems solved to compute the Anderson mixing, which in turn can affect the convergence of
aap
. The first level physics-based sketching operator is static and determines a fixed projection subspace throughout the solution process, which allows to conveniently pre-allocate the memory needed once at the beginning of the execution of the algorithm. The second sketching level of our approach coincides with the adaptive sketching already used in
[
LPL24
]
, for which we propose sharper indicators to activate an adaptive selection of the rows of the tall an
d
skinny matrix according to our backward stability analysis.

While each of the two components—projection of Anderson acceleration onto a reduced or physically meaningful subspace and approximate or sketched solution of the associated least-squares problem—has appeared separately in prior work (e.g., projection and variable-aware formulations of Anderson acceleration
[
WN11
,
PR21
]
and backward-stable inexact or perturbed Anderson least-squares solves
[
LPL24
]
), the contribution of this paper is not merely their juxtaposition. Rather, the manuscript introduces a structured two-level decomposition of approximation error in Anderson acceleration, in which the static physics-based restriction and the dynamic algebraic sketching address distinct and complementary stability mechanisms. The backward stability analysis clarifies that physics-based projections act on the conditioning and scale of the least-squares problem, while algebraic sketching introduces controlled perturbations whose admissibility depends on residual-dependent bounds. This separation leads to an algorithmic structure in which the two sketching levels play fundamentally different roles and cannot be interchanged or merged without loss of robustness. We highlight that the resulting adaptive scheme can still suffer from instabilities that are inherent to the method, as the backward stability analysis only ensures that the sketching does not result in
additional
instability. Other improvements have been considered in the literature, such as better memory management in the QR factorization through a Truncated Gram-Schmidt approach
[
TXH+24
]
, as well as enriching the method with safeguarding mechanisms that provide a strict improvement on convergence
[
GCG22
]
.

When the sketching at the second level is performed by randomly selecting the entries to retain, there is an increased risk that the entries retained are physically stored far from each other in the computer memory, which in turn increases the chances of cache misses and slows down the execution of the code. To address this issue, we propose simple but effective guidelines for the randomized sketching that reduce the overhead of cache-misses in two specific operations: matrix-vector products and the QR factorization. To define a sketching range, we compare the time elapsed to perform each operation using sequential memory access against a random mask, and from there obtain the maximum sketching allowed to have faster operations for various problem sizes. Based on benchmark tests that we performed in this work, we concluded that a reasonable percentage of entries to retain in the second (adaptive) level sketching is 10% for matrix-vector products and 80% for QR factorizations. While the arithmetic complexity of the least-squares solve scales linearly with problem size, the practical performance is dominated by memory traffic and cache coherency due to the non-contiguous memory access patterns of the masks. Consequently, in this work we rely on empirical benchmarks rather than asymptotic analysis to characterize the method’s efficiency.

We implemented our approach in the coding language
Julia

[
BEKS17
]
, and illustrated the performance of our numerical scheme on different single- and multi-physics problems: Stokes,
p
-Laplacian, Bidomain, and stationary Navier-Stokes. We additionally considered an increasing number of
dof
in all test, in order to ensure that the proposed methods do not present deterioration for increasing problem sizes. Our numerical results confirmed the efficacy of our sketching approach in both reducing memory usage and computational time in
aap
while still maintaining its convergence to the desired accuracy. Our implementation is open-source and available at the following GitHub repository
https://github.com/ORNL/Restricted-Alternating-Anderson-Picard
.

From a methodological perspective, this work contributes a stability-driven framework for sketching Anderson acceleration, in which backward error bounds for the Anderson least-squares problem are used to derive admissible and adaptive row-selection strategies for nonlinear fixed-point iterations.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we review
aap
. In Section
3

aap
, together with the relevant notation and memory requirements. In Section
4
we perform the backward stability analysis of the LS problem solved to computed the Anderson mixing vector in
aap
. Based on the conclusions of the backward stability analysis we propose two sketching strategies, subselection and randomized, which extend the ones proposed in
[
LPL24
]
to non-linear fixed point problems under the assumption that the fixed-point operator is Lipschitz. Finally, we provide several numerical tests in Section

Remark 1

The mathematical formulation of
aap
that we adopted so far follows the residual formulation described in (
1

\mathbfit{x}
such that
\mathbfit{T}(\mathbfit{x})=\mathbf{0}
. An alternative formulation of
aap
follows the definition of a fixed-point problem, that is find
\mathbfit{x}
such that
\mathbfit{x}=\mathbfit{S}(\mathbfit{x})
, for some given operator
\mathbfit{S}
. Problems written in residual form allow one to compute the required vectors
\mathbfit{f}^{k}
,
\mathbfit{g}^{k}
in the following order:

\mathbfit{x}^{k}\to\mathbfit{f}^{k}=\mathbfit{T}(\mathbfit{x}^{k})\to\mathbfit{g}^{k}=\mathbfit{x}^{k}-\omega\mathbfit{f}^{k}\to\mathbfit{x}^{k+1}=\mathbfit{g}^{k}.

(11)

If one considers an operator given in fixed-point form, then the second and third operations are executed in inverted order:

\mathbfit{x}^{k}\to\mathbfit{g}^{k}=\mathbfit{S}(\mathbfit{x}^{k})\to\mathbfit{f}^{k}=\mathbfit{x}^{k}-\mathbfit{g}^{k}\to\mathbfit{x}^{k+1}=\mathbfit{x}^{k}-\omega\mathbfit{f}^{k}.

(12)

Although our implementation in
Julia
supports both variants, the mathematical discussion presented henceforth relies only on the residual form, since most non-linear problems of interest in physics and engineering applications are described in residual form, thus justifying it to be the standard choice for most non-linear solver libraries. For readers interested in mathematical formulations of the
aa
scheme for fixed-point iterations that commonly arise for solving non-linear
pde
, we point to the
[
PRX19
]
where
aa
is presented and analyzed as an acceleration technique to speed up the Picard iterations for Navier-Stokes equations.

Sketching-based
aap

To perform controlled perturbations of the Anderson least-squares problem whose admissibility is governed by the backward stability bounds derived in Section
4
of this work, we consider two different levels of sketching in our algorithm, represented by restriction operators
\mathbfit{\Pi}
that project the solution onto a lower-dimensional space. To illustrate this approach, we focus on projections that extract a subset of indices, commonly referred to as
masking
operators.

The first level of restriction is physics based, and is primarily motivated by the multi-physics nature of certain problems such as the interaction between velocity and pressure in fluid dynamics. This approach is based on two key insights: (1) in many cases, one field can be effectively reduced in terms of another, which is rigorously justified in linear problems through the computation of the Schur complement matrix
[
Zha06
]
. This suggests that it is possible to solve the least squares problem using only one of the sub-physics without loss of information. (2) Differences in scale between the various physical components of a model can negatively affect the conditioning of the monolithic problem. We denote this subspace identified by the first level restriction as
\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{1}}
, with the corresponding restriction operator
\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{1}}
, where
\ell_{1}\leq n
. This formulation allows us to determine the size of the reduced least squares matrix
\mathbfit{F}_{\Pi}\coloneqq\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}\mathbfit{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{1}\times n}
at the allocation phase.

The second level of restriction is purely algebraic (not driven considerations on the physics) and is also dynamic (it can change across iterations). It is motivated by adaptive sketching techniques, such as the one proposed in
[
LPL24
]
, based on the observation that precision in the least squares problem becomes less critical as the residual approaches zero. We define this second subspace as
\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{2}}
, with the corresponding restriction operator
\mathbfit{\Pi}_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{2}}
. Consequently, the overall restriction operator is given by
\mathbfit{\Pi}\coloneqq\mathbfit{\Pi}_{2}\circ\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{2}}
.

4
Projection-based sketching stability analysis

In
aap
, it is crucial to compute the Anderson mixing vector
\mathbfit{\alpha}
accurately. However, it has been observed that as iterations progress, the requirement for precision becomes increasingly less critical
[
LPL24
]
. This observation can be rigorously justified through a backward stability analysis of the perturbed least squares problem, where the perturbations considered in practice arise from applying the restriction operators
\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}
and
\mathbfit{\Pi}_{2}
to reduce the dimensionality of the LS problem, as described in Section
3
.

In the following, we extend the proof provided in
[
LPL24
]
to the non-linear case, under the assumption that the non-linear function
\mathbfit{T}
is Lipschitz continuous. That is, there exists a positive constant
L
such that for all
\mathbfit{x}_{1},\mathbfit{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\|\mathbfit{T}(\mathbfit{x}_{1})-\mathbfit{T}(\mathbfit{x}_{2})\|\leq L\|\mathbfit{x}_{1}-\mathbfit{x}_{2}\|.

(13)

Since restriction operators return vectors of reduced dimensionality, in the backward stability analysis we will use instead the induced projection operators, defined as
\mathbfit{P}=\mathbfit{\Pi}^{T}\mathbfit{\Pi}
,
\mathbfit{P}^{1}=\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}^{T}\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}
, and
\mathbfit{P}^{2}=\mathbfit{\Pi}_{2}^{T}\mathbfit{\Pi}_{2}
respectively. These operators preserve the dimension of the vectors/matrices but zero-out the unused rows.
For simplicity, we will drop the
\Pi
subindex and thus use the matrix
\mathbfit{F}^{k}
instead of
\mathbfit{F}^{k}_{\Pi}=\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}\mathbfit{F}^{k}
, so that on iteration
k
the problem of interest yields the following solution:

\mathbfit{\alpha}^{k}=\argmin_{\mathbfit{\alpha}}\|\mathbfit{F}^{k}\mathbfit{\alpha}-\mathbfit{f}^{k}\|^{2}.

(14)

Therefore, the optimal backward error to solve (
14
) is given by

\min_{\delta\mathbfit{F},\delta\mathbfit{f}}\left\{\|\delta\mathbfit{F},\delta\mathbfit{f}\|:(\delta\mathbfit{F},\delta\mathbfit{f})=\argmin_{(\delta\mathbfit{F},\delta\mathbfit{f})}\|(\mathbfit{F}+\delta\mathbfit{F})\mathbfit{\alpha}^{k}-(\mathbfit{f}^{k}+\delta\mathbfit{f})\|^{2}\right\}.

(15)

In particular, we assume that the error introduced on the right-hand side (RHS), represented by the vector
\delta\mathbfit{f}
, can be controlled. Moreover, this error is used to bound the error introduced by
\delta\mathbfit{F}
on the left-hand side (LHS). Specifically, we employ projection operators
\mathbfit{P}_{k}
acting on each column, such that the perturbed matrix
\hat{\mathbfit{F}}^{k}
is given by

\hat{\mathbfit{F}}^{k}\coloneqq\mathbfit{F}^{k}+\delta\mathbfit{F}^{k}=\mathbfit{F}^{k}-\left[(\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{1})\Delta\mathbfit{f}^{1},\ldots,(\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{k})\Delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}\right],

(16)

and similarly, we define the perturbed vector as

\hat{\mathbfit{f}}^{k}\coloneqq\mathbfit{f}^{k}+\delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}=\mathbfit{f}^{k}-(\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{k})\mathbfit{f}^{k}.

(17)

In what follows, we consider the
QR
factorizations
\mathbfit{F}^{k}=\mathbfit{Q}^{k}\mathbfit{R}^{k}
and
\hat{\mathbfit{F}}^{k}=\hat{\mathbfit{Q}}^{k}\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k}
for the original and perturbed problems respectively. We can now study the perturbation norm, defined as

\delta^{k}=|\delta\mathbfit{F}^{k}\hat{\mathbfit{\alpha}}^{k}|_{2}=\left|\left[(\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{1})\Delta\mathbfit{f}^{1},\ldots,(\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{k})\Delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}\right]\hat{\mathbfit{\alpha}}^{k}\right|_{2}.

(18)

Resorting to the Lipschitz continuity of the fixed-point operator
\mathbfit{T}
, we obtain
|\Delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}|=|\mathbfit{f}^{k}-\mathbfit{f}^{k-1}|\leq L|\Delta\mathbfit{x}^{k}|
, and thus we can bound the perturbation as

\delta^{k}\leq\sum_{i}^{k}|\hat{\mathbfit{\alpha}}^{k}|_{2}\|\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{i}\||\Delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}|_{2}\leq\sum_{i}^{k}L|\hat{\mathbfit{\alpha}}^{k}|_{2}\|\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{i}\||\Delta\mathbfit{x}^{k}|_{2}.

(19)

Using this estimate we can show the following result, which aids in devising adaptivity strategies for a judicious sketching in Algorithm
2
.

Theorem 2
.

Consider
\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k}
the R factor from the QR factorization of the perturbed matrix
\hat{\mathbfit{F}}^{k}=\mathbfit{F}^{k}+\delta\mathbfit{F}^{k}
,
\epsilon
a positive scalar representing the tolerable perturbation magnitude,
\mathbfit{f}^{k}
the
k
-th residual of
aap
, and
(\eta_{j})_{j}
an arbitrary sequence such that
\sum_{j}^{\infty}\eta_{j}=C
for some positive scalar
C
. We will further denote, for a given matrix
\mathbfit{Z}
,
\sigma_{\text{min}}(\mathbfit{Z})
as the smallest singular value of
\mathbfit{Z}
. Consider the perturbations induced by the sketching operators as defined in (
16
) and (
17
), for which we assume the following hypotheses:

1.

The perturbation
\delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}
of the k-th residual, defined by the relation
\hat{\mathbfit{f}}^{k}=\mathbfit{f}^{k}+\delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}
, is such that

|\delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}|_{2}\leq\epsilon|\mathbfit{f}^{k}|_{2}.

(20)

2.

The projection operators
(\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{j})
satisfy that

\|\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{j}\|\leq\eta_{j}\frac{\sigma_{\text{min}}(\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k})}{L|\mathbfit{f}^{k}|_{2}|\Delta\mathbfit{x}^{j}|_{2}(1+\epsilon)}\hskip 11.49994pt\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}.

(21)

Under these assumptions, the perturbation norm
\delta^{k}
defined in (
18
) satisfies that

\delta^{k}\leq C.

(22)

Proof.

The proof closely resembles the one presented in
[
LPL24
]
. We first bound the weights in terms of the perturbed vector. Using the relation
\left[\hat{\mathbfit{F}}^{k}\right]^{\dagger}=[\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k}]^{-1}[\hat{\mathbfit{Q}}^{k}]^{T}
, we obtain that

|\hat{\mathbfit{\alpha}}^{k}|_{2}=\left|[\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k}]^{-1}[\hat{\mathbfit{Q}}^{k}]^{T}\hat{\mathbfit{f}}^{k}\right|_{2}\\
\leq\left\|[\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k}]^{-1}\right\|\left|\hat{\mathbfit{f}}^{k}\right|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{min}}(\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k})}\left(\left|\mathbfit{f}^{k}\right|_{2}+\left|\delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}\right|_{2}\right)\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{\sigma_{\text{min}}(\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k})}\left|\mathbfit{f}^{k}\right|_{2},

(23)

where we used that
\|\mathbfit{Z}^{-1}\|=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{min}}(\mathbfit{Z})}
. Putting this in (
19
) we obtain

\delta^{k}\leq\sum_{i}^{k}\frac{L(1+\epsilon)}{\sigma_{\text{min}}(\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k})}\left|\mathbfit{f}^{k}\right|_{2}\left\|\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{i}\right\|\left|\Delta x^{i}\right|_{2}.

(24)

From this equation we can then conclude that, if for every
j\leq k
(
21
) holds, then we have

\delta^{k}\leq\sum_{j}^{k}\eta_{j}\leq C,

(25)

which establishes the result.

Using this theoretical result, we can provide simple but still effective strategies to adaptively project the LS problem onto a subspace defined by reducing the
dof
retained from the original LS problem. Designing an adaptive strategy needs to consider, on one hand, Theorem
2
in order to ensure that the approximated calculations to solve the LS problem at each mixing step do not affect the convergence of
aap
to the desired accuracy. On the other hand, it needs to consider the slowdown associated to non-sequential memory access due to a highly frequent cache miss. We have studied what is the maximum percentage of sketching that still yields faster matrix-vector products and faster QR factorizations in Section
4.2
with respect to a dense matrix with sequential memory access.

4.1
Ensuring LHS stability

We start by observing that, as
\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{k}
are projection operators, they have unit norm. Therefore, the second hypothesis on Theorem
2
becomes

\|\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}_{j}\|=1\leq\eta_{j}\frac{\sigma_{\text{min}}(\hat{\mathbfit{R}^{k}})}{L|\mathbfit{f}^{k}||\Delta\mathbfit{x}^{j}|(1+\epsilon)}\hskip 11.49994pt\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,k\},

(26)

which can be recast as a bound on
\epsilon
as it must hold that

\epsilon\leq\frac{\eta_{j}\sigma_{\text{min}}(\hat{\mathbfit{R}^{k}})}{L|\mathbfit{f}^{k}||\Delta\mathbfit{x}^{j}|}-1=:\epsilon_{\text{LHS}}.

(27)

Since
\sigma_{\text{min}}(\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k})
and
L
are impractical to compute, we adopt the following computationally inexpensive approximations:

•

Estimate
\sigma_{\text{min}}
by performing a few (e.g., between 1 and 5) iterations of the inverse power method to the matrix
[\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k}]^{T}\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k}

[
Kel95
]
.

•

Estimate
L
by keeping track of the increment norms so that

L\approx L_{k}=\max\left(L_{k-1},\frac{|\Delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}|_{2}}{|\Delta\mathbfit{x}^{k}|_{2}}\right).

(28)

4.1.1
Ensuring RHS stability

To enforce this constraint, we will use that the perturbation vector
\delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}=(\mathbfit{I}-\mathbfit{P}^{k})\mathbfit{f}^{k}
is given by the entries that are removed from the vector, and ensure that
|\delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}|<\epsilon|\mathbfit{f}^{k}|
. An effective way to use this is to compute some indices, and with that simply define the quantity

\epsilon_{\text{RHS}}\coloneqq\frac{|\delta\mathbfit{f}^{k}|_{2}}{|\mathbfit{f}^{k}|_{2}}.

(29)

To establish a connection between
\epsilon_{\text{LHS}}
and
\epsilon_{\text{RHS}}
, we impose that
\epsilon=\epsilon_{\text{RHS}}
. From Equation
27
we have that
0<\epsilon_{\text{RHS}}\leq\epsilon_{\text{LHS}}
.

4.2
Choosing the index set

To determine the rows to remove in the tall-and-skinny matrix
\mathbfit{F}^{k}_{\Pi}
and its corresponding right hand side
\mathbfit{f}^{k}
, we consider that, in practical terms, accessing random indices can be slower than accessing the full vector due to frequent cache misses in the random access memory of the computer. For this reason, we estimated the maximum number of random indices that can be used while still ensuring a faster matrix-vector product and QR factorization. We present the benchmarking code to compare the computational time for matrix-vector product and QR factorization with and without randomized sketching in Figure
1
. We performed the benchmarking using a random matrix with 50 columns and a number of rows ranging from
10^{3}
up to
10^{6}
, and show the maximum allowed percentages in Figure
2
. To make these estimates precise, we ran every test and averaged the results until the relative change in the computed average was below
10^{-5}
.

From this benchmarking test, we conclude that: our sketching operators should retain no more than 10% of the original rows to achieve a faster matrix-vector product and no more than 80% of the original rows to ensure a faster QR factorization. Following the approach in
[
LPL24
]
, we consider two sketching strategies for a given percentage
S
:

•

Subselection
: We retain the
S\%
of entries corresponding to the largest current residual
\mathbfit{f}^{k}
.

•

Randomized
: We retain a randomly selected
S\%
of the entries.

The subselection strategy follows the intuition that the portions of the residual with larger residuals are where computational efforts should be concentrated to effectively reduce the error.

4.3
Overall strategy

Under all previous considerations, the final adaptivity strategy requires the following steps:

1.

Fix a sketching percentage
S
,

2.

estimate
\epsilon_{\text{LHS}}
and assert it is positive,

3.

compute the index set defining the mask,

4.

use the mask to compute
\epsilon_{\text{RHS}}
, and

5.

if
0\leq\epsilon_{\text{RHS}}\leq\epsilon_{\text{LHS}}
, use mask. Otherwise, do not use
\mathbfit{\Pi}_{2}
.

We show the detailed steps in Algorithm
4
. We have added the problem dimension
N
to the
\epsilon_{\text{LHS}}
estimator in order to consider vector norms that are less prone to dimensionality problems with
|\cdot|^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}(\cdot)^{2}
. Without this, adaptivity is seldom activated for larger problems. A more general strategy to avoid this kind of dimensionality problems would be to frame the method in terms of the infinite dimensional spaces where the
pde
are formulated and compute the corresponding infinite dimensional norms. This is beyond the scope of this work and part of what we plan to address in future work.

We consider two choices for the subselection parameter
\eta_{j}
: either
\eta_{j}=j^{-1.1}
(termed
power
adaptivity) or
\eta_{j}=1
(termed
constant
adaptivity). Combining these variants yields five adaptivity strategies used in our numerical experiments:

•

No adaptivity
:
\mathbfit{\Pi}_{2}=\mathbfit{I}
.

•

Subselect power
: Residual subselection with
\eta_{j}=j^{-1.1}
.

•

Subselect constant
: Residual subselection with
\eta_{j}=1
.

•

Randomized power
: Random index selection with
\eta_{j}=j^{-1.1}
.

•

Randomized constant
: Random index selection with
\eta_{j}=1
.

Our analysis requires that series given by
(\eta_{j})_{j}
is convergent, and thus the disctinction between the
constant
and
power

\eta_{j}\sigma_{\text{min}}(\hat{\mathbfit{R}}^{k})/L|\mathbfit{f}^{k}||\Delta x^{j}|
and thus artificially prevent adaptivity. We present a sensitivity study of adaptivity on the exponent
\ell
of
\eta^{-\ell}
in Appendix
A
, where we show for a specific problem that it is not significative for the random masks, and negligible for subselection.

5.1
Stokes problem

The Stokes problem is formulated as follows: Given
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, find a velocity
\mathbfit{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and pressure
p_{S}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathbfit{u}+\grad p_{S}

\displaystyle=\mathbfit{f}

\displaystyle\text{ in $\Omega$},

(30)

\displaystyle\dive\mathbfit{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle\mathbfit{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{D}$},

\displaystyle\left(\grad\mathbfit{u}-p_{S}\mathsfbfit I\right)\mathbfit{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle\text{on $\Gamma_{N}$}.

We considered
\Omega=(0,1)^{3}
, a constant volume force
\mathbfit{f}(x)=(1,1,1)
, homogeneous Dirichlet conditions on
\Gamma_{D}=\{\mathbfit{x}=(x,y,z)\in\partial\Omega:y\in\{0,1\},z\in\{0,1\}\}
, and homogeneous Neumann conditions on
\Gamma_{N}=\{\mathbfit{x}=(x,y,z)\in\partial\Omega:x\in\{0,1\}
. This problem is discretized with inf-sup stable Taylor-Hood elements
\mathbb{P}_{2}\times\mathbb{P}_{1}

[
ESW14
]
, which gives rise to the block linear system

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{K}&\mathbfit{B}^{T}\\
\mathbfit{B}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{u}\\
p_{S}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{F}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(31)

We use a preconditioned Richardson iteration

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{u}^{k+1}\\
p_{S}^{k+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{u}^{k}\\
p_{S}^{k}\end{bmatrix}-\mathbfit{P}^{-1}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{K}&\mathbfit{B}^{T}\\
\mathbfit{B}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{u}^{k}\\
p_{S}^{k}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{F}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(32)

and accelerate it with our implementation of
aap
. We use a block diagonal preconditioner
\mathbfit{P}
with the stiffness matrix on the velocity block and a mass matrix in the pressure block. This is known to be a scalable preconditioner for this problem
[
ESW14
]
, and we approximate the inverses with an Algebraic Multigrid for both blocks as provided by HYPRE
[
FY02
]
. We study the effect of physics-based masks and algebraic masks obtained using adaptivity in terms of number of iterations and time-to-solution, and we present also the performance of the approach identified as best performing among all the combinations of masking and adaptive selection that we tested. A relative tolerance of
10^{-6}
is used to determine convergence.

5.1.1
Stokes: Physics-based mask test

The physics-based restriction operator we consider is the one induced by the physics of the problem, meaning either

\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{u}\\
p_{S}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\mathbfit{u}\hskip 22.99988pt\text{ or }\hskip 22.99988pt\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{u}\\
p_{S}\end{bmatrix}\right)=p_{S},

(33)

which we refer to as
velocity
and
pressure
masks, respectively. We show in Table
1
the total iterations and solution time in seconds required to converge with
\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}
being an identity (no mask), a velocity mask, and a pressure mask.

We note that the velocity mask yields more iterations and higher time-to-solution than the unmasked case, whereas the pressure mask converges in fewer iterations and in less time than the unmasked case. More specifically, the use of the pressure mask results in a 24% reduction in the number of iterations and a 33% reduction in the solution time in the largest problem when compared to the unmasked version. The conclusion we draw from these results is that the pressure seems to capture the complexity of the problem more effectively than the velocity, which seems to be a reasonable conclusion given that the Stokes equation can be reformulated as a pressure-only problem by means of the Schur complement.

p

The
p
-Laplacian problem is given by: Given
p_{L}\geq 1
and
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, find
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle F(u)\coloneqq-\dive|\grad u|^{p_{L}-2}\grad u-f

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(34)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\partial\Omega$},

where
\Omega
is the unit cube,
f(x)=1
, and
p_{L}=1.5
. We used
p_{L}
instead of
p
to avoid confusion with the alternation parameter
p
in
aap
. We chose
p_{L}<2
as it is a challenging scenario for which there are fewer efficient strategies in the literature
[
Loi20
]
. To solve the problem, we discretized it with first order Lagrangian finite elements and then used a quasi-Newton scheme to solve the nonlinear problem. The solver is given as follows: Consider a harmonic initial guess
u^{0}
and then iteratively solve a Laplace-stabilized iteration:

-\beta\Delta\delta u^{k}=-F(u^{k-1}),

(35)

where we have found the value
\beta=10
to behave robustly. The increment
\delta u^{k}
is used as a residual, i.e.

\mathbfit{T}(u^{k})=\delta u^{k}=\frac{1}{\beta}\Delta^{-1}\left(F(u^{k-1})\right),

(36)

that allows for acceleration using
aap
. Instead of inverting the Laplacian exactly in (
36
), we consider the action of one iteration of a smoothed aggregation AMG preconditioner, as provided by the PCGAMG preconditioner in PETSc
[
BAA+21
]
. For this problem, we test the adaptivity strategies and then look for the overall best performing method. There are no sub-physics in this problem, so we simply set
\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}=\mathbfit{I}
, and a relative tolerance of
10^{-6}
was used to establish convergence.

5.5
Bidomain equations

The Bidomain model is given by the following system: Given an ionic model
I_{\text{ion}}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and applied current
I_{\text{app}}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, find extracellular and intracellular potentials
u_{e},u_{i}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle F_{e}(u_{e},u_{i})\coloneqq\frac{\partial(u_{e}-u_{i})}{\partial{t}}-\dive D_{e}\grad u_{e}+I_{\text{ion}}(u_{e}-u_{i})-I_{\text{app}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

(37)

\displaystyle F_{i}(u_{e},u_{i})\coloneqq\frac{\partial(u_{i}-u_{e})}{\partial{t}}-\dive D_{i}\grad u_{i}-I_{\text{ion}}(u_{e}-u_{i})+I_{\text{app}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle\grad u_{i,e}\cdot\mathbfit{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\partial\Omega$},

where
D_{e,i}
are symmetric and positive definite conductivity tensors. This is a degenerate parabolic problem that is commonly used to describe the propagation of an electric potential in soft tissue, such as the one that generates a heartbeat in cardiac tissue. We consider the same parameter setting as in
[
BHPS24
]
, with a Fitz-Hugh Nagumo ionic current. We discretize it with an implicit Euler scheme in time, and with first order finite elements in space. All the numbers we report are related to the solution of the first time step. It has been observed in
[
BHPS24
]
that
aa
yields a robust and scalable solver for this model.

To solve it, we consider the fixed point map given by the following Picard iteration:

\begin{bmatrix}u_{e}^{k+1}\\
u_{i}^{k+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{e}^{k}\\
u_{i}^{k}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}F_{e}(u_{e}^{k},u_{i}^{k})\\
F_{i}(u_{e}^{k},u_{i}^{k})\end{bmatrix}

(38)

and accelerate it with
aap
with a window size of
m=50
. We have observed that smaller window sizes do not yield a convergent scheme. A relative tolerance of
10^{-4}
was used to establish convergence.

\mathbfit{\Pi}_{1}\left(\begin{bmatrix}u_{e}\\

(39)

which we refer to as
extracellular
and
intracellular
masks, respectively. We solved the problem using an identity, extracellular, and intracellular masks, and report the iteration count and CPU time in seconds obtained in Table

In this section we solve the stationary Navier-Stokes equation for the lid driven cavity benchmark: Given a Reynolds number
Re>0
, find a velocity
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a pressure
p_{NS}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{Re}\Delta\mathbfit{u}+[\grad\mathbfit{u}]\mathbfit{u}+\grad p_{NS}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ in $\Omega$},

(40)

\displaystyle\dive\mathbfit{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle\mathbfit{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbfit{u}_{D}

\displaystyle\text{ on $\partial\Omega$},

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p_{NS}\,dx

\displaystyle=0.

We discretize this problem using inf-sup stable Taylor-Hood elements, and solve it using an accelerated Picard iteration as in
[
PRX19
]
: Consider
Re>0
and a given velocity
\mathbfit{u}^{k}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, then find a velocity
\mathbfit{u}^{k+1}
and a pressure
p_{NS}^{k+1}
such that

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{Re}\Delta\mathbfit{u}^{k+1}+[\grad\mathbfit{u}^{k+1}]\mathbfit{u}^{k}+\grad p_{NS}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ in $\Omega$},

(41)

\displaystyle\dive\mathbfit{u}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle\mathbfit{u}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbfit{u}_{D}

\displaystyle\text{ on $\partial\Omega$},

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p_{NS}^{k+1}\,dx

\displaystyle=0.

The solution map
(\mathbfit{u}^{k+1},p_{NS}^{k+1})=T(\mathbfit{u}^{k})
yields a fixed point iteration whose fixed point is the solution of the stationary Navier-Stokes equation. This map
T
is the one we accelerate with
aap
. For this problem, we consider a physical mask study, an adaptivity study, and an overall best strategy computation. In all cases we considered

, a div-div stabilization with constant

6
Conclusions

In this work we proposed a two-level
aap
algorithm. The two-level nature of the method is motivated by the possibility of using two separate restriction operators, where one of them is physics-based and known before execution, and the other one is purely algebraic, adaptive, and dynamically computed at each iteration. This first operator is advantageous as it reduces the memory requirements of the method, and in some cases it was observed to yield a significant reduction in the number of iterations. The second operator further reduces the size of the least-squares problem by adaptively choosing
dof
. The adaptivity strategy that we proposed was directly motivated by the backward stability analysis, and we verified in all tests that the strategies were in fact stable, as the difference in iterations between the method with and without adaptivity was typically not very significant, and proved beneficial in most cases. In addition, the Stokes and Navier-Stokes tests reveal that the physics-based mask considered depends heavily on the structure of the fixed-point operator that one accelerates, as both models are based on similar variables but were solved with very different strategies.

We emphasize that the purpose of the present analysis is not to establish global convergence rates for Anderson acceleration under sketching, but to characterize admissible approximations that preserve the convergence behavior of the underlying exact method. A general convergence theory for nonlinear Anderson acceleration remains an open problem, even in the absence of sketching.

The proposed algorithm was shown to be effective for certain problems, where we observed time savings between a
40\%
and a
50\%
with respect to standard
aa
. These savings are not consistent among all tests, which shows that performance is problem dependent, as one would expect. The problems where our algorithm was most advantageous are the Stokes and the Bidomain equations, which are the ones where residual evaluation was the cheapest. This shows that our methodology can improve the performance of
aa
most reliably in problems where the computation of the residual is not excessively dominant, as otherwise the least-squares problems solved in
aap
provides no significant overhead, and a minimal increase in iterations can be disadvantageous. Despite this, in all cases we could obtain a method that was comparable or better than the classic one in terms of iterations, and it remained robust with respect to the problem size despite the reduction of the least-square problem.

Although the observed speedups are moderate, such reductions still significantly accelerate the convergence of the numerical scheme in large-scale PDE simulations where AA is applied repeatedly and the least-squares solve constitutes a non-negligible fraction of the total runtime. Moreover, the proposed approach achieves these gains without altering the underlying nonlinear solver or preconditioner, making it complementary to existing algorithmic improvements.

Future work will integrate our Julia package with more advanced adaptive sketching techniques provided in RandBLAS, the Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra (RNLA) package in BLAS
[
MDM+23
]
currently under development. This work has been implemented in Julia, and will be released in the AAP.jl library, available to the community upon the publication of this work.
