1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite group and let
x
be an element of
G
. A central question in group theory is whether
x
lies in a small characteristic subgroup of
G
. This problem has been the subject of extensive research, and numerous papers have addressed various aspects of it. Below, we highlight two of the most well-known results related to this topic.
Recall that for a prime
p
, the element
x
is called
p
-singular
if its order is divisible by
p
; it is called
p
-regular
(or a
p^{\prime}
-element) if its order is not divisible by
p
; and it is called a
p
-element
if its order is a power of
p.

The Baer-Suzuki theorem provides a useful characterization of the Fitting subgroup
F(G)
of a finite group
G
, which is the largest nilpotent normal subgroup of
G
. It states that an element
x\in G
lies in
F(G)
if and only if
\langle x,x^{g}\rangle
is nilpotent for every
g\in G.
Various versions of this result have been established by Baer, Suzuki, Alperin and Lyons (see
[
5
,
40
,
1
]
).
When
x
is a
p
-element for some prime
p
, the theorem can be restated as follows:
x\in O_{p}(G)
if and only if
\langle x,x^{g}\rangle
is a
p
-group for every
g\in G
, where
O_{p}(G)
is the largest normal
p
-subgroup of
G
. This result was further strengthened by Guralnick and Robinson
[
25
, Theorem A]
, who showed that if
x
is an element of prime order
p
and the commutator
[x,g]=x^{-1}g^{-1}xg
is a
p
-element for every
g\in G
, then
x\in O_{p}(G)
. Later, Guralnick and Malle extended this result to all
p
-elements
[
21
, Theorem 1.4]
. For further generalizations of the Baer-Suzuki theorem, see
[
22
,
26
,
41
]
.

In the opposite direction, Glauberman’s
Z_{p}^{*}
-theorem provides a criterion for determining when a
p
-element of a finite group lies in the center of the group modulo a certain normal subgroup. Recall that the element
x\in G
is said to be central modulo a normal subgroup
N
of
G
if the image
xN
lies in the center of the quotient group
G/N
. Equivalently, this means that the subgroup
[x,G]
, generated by all the commutators
[x,g]
for
g\in G
, is contained
N
. For a prime
p
, we define
Z_{p}^{*}(G)
to be the full inverse image in
G
of the center of
G/O_{p^{\prime}}(G)
, where
O_{p^{\prime}}(G)
is the largest normal
p^{\prime}
-subgroup of
G
. Glauberman’s
Z_{p}^{*}
-theorem states that if
x
is a
p
-element of a finite group
G
, then
[x,g]
is a
p^{\prime}
-element for every
g\in G
if and only if
x
is central modulo
O_{p^{\prime}}(G)
. Equivalently, it can be rephrased as follows:
x^{G}\cap C_{G}(x)=\{x\}
if and only
x\in Z_{p}^{*}(G)
, where
x^{G}
denotes the conjugacy class of
G
containing
x
and
C_{G}(x)
is the centralizer of
x
in
G
. (See
[
26
, Theorem 5.1]
).

Glauberman originally proved his theorem for
p=2
using the modular representation theory in
[
19
]
. For odd primes, the result was independently established by Guralnick and Robison
[
25
]
and by Artemovich
[
3
]
. We refer the readers to
[
26
]
for various equivalent formulations of this theorem. As an application, Guralnick and Robinson proved the following generalization (
[
25
, Theorem D(ii)]
): if
x\in G
is an element of prime order
r
and
p\neq r
is a prime such that
[x,g]
is a
p
-element for every
g\in G,
then
x
is central modulo
O_{p}(G)
.
We now present our first result, which generalizes several of the theorems discussed above.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group, let
x\in G
and let
p
be a prime. Then
[x,g]
is a
p
-element for every
g\in G
if and only if
x
is central modulo
O_{p}(G)
.

Theorem
1.1
generalizes both Theorem D(ii) in
[
25
]
and Theorem 1.4 in
[
21
]
, as discussed earlier. First, if we take
x\in G
to be an element of prime order
r\neq p
, then Theorem
1.1
yields directly Theorem D(ii) from
[
25
]
. Next, suppose
x\in G
is a
p
-element and the commutator
[x,g]
is a
p
-element for every
g\in G
, where
p
is a prime. Then, by Theorem
1.1
,
\langle x\rangle O_{p}(G)
is a normal
p
-subgroup of
G
. Since
O_{p}(G)
is the largest normal
p
-subgroup of
G
, it follows that
\langle x\rangle O_{p}(G)=O_{p}(G)
and hence
x\in O_{p}(G)
. This recovers Theorem
1.4
from
[
21
]
.

For the proof of Theorem
1.1
, a standard reduction argument allows us to reduce the proof of the theorem to the case where the group is almost simple. To address this case, we require the following result, which is of independent interest. Recall that a finite group
G
is called almost simple with socle
S
if there exists a finite nonabelian simple group
S
such that
S\unlhd G\leqslant\operatorname{Aut}(S)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a finite almost simple group, let
x
be a nontrivial element of
G
and let
p
be a prime. Then there exists
g\in G
such that
[x,g]
is a nontrivial
p^{\prime}
-element.

For the proof of Theorem
1.2
, a direct calculation eliminates the cases when the socle of the almost simple group
G
is an alternating group. For sporadic and finite groups of Lie type cases, we use character theory, in particular, the structure constant formula to determine whether an element can be written as a commutator of the form
[x,g]
or not.

Remark 1.3
.

We record here some remarks on Theorem

.

(i)

If

is either

or

-singular for every

then it is not true that

is central modulo

. For a counterexample, consider

and

of order

. Then the commutators
[x,g]
has order
1,4
or

for every

but
G/O_{2}(G)\cong\mathrm{S}_{3}
and so

is not central modulo

. This is taken from
[

]
.

(ii)

It is conjectured in
[

,
26
]
that if
x\in G
is an element of order

and

is either
1

-singular for every

, then

).

We next present some applications of the preceding theorems to problems concerning products of conjugacy classes.
Let
G
be a finite group and let
X\subseteq G
be a nonempty set. For any
g\in G
, we define the conjugate of
X
by
g
as

X^{g}=\{x^{g}:=g^{-1}xg:x\in X\}
, and the inverse of
X
as
X^{-1}=\{x^{-1}:x\in X\}
. We say that
X
is a normal subset of
G
if it is invariant under conjugation by
G
; that is,
X^{g}=X
for all
g\in G.
Clearly, every conjugacy class of
G
is a normal subset of
G
, and conversely, every normal subset of
G
is a disjoint union of conjugacy classes of
G
. Given two nonempty subsets
X,Y\subseteq G
, we define their (set) product as
XY=\{xy:x\in X,y\in Y\}
. If
X
and
Y
are normal subsets of
G
, then
X^{-1}
and
XY
are normal subsets of
G
and we have the equality
XY=YX
. Let
K=x^{G}
be the conjugacy class of
G
containing
x
. Theorem
1.1
can be equivalently reformulated in terms of conjugacy class products as follows: ‘
K^{-1}K
consists entirely of
p
-elements if and only if
x
is central modulo
O_{p}(G)
.’ We now turn to investigating the structure of finite groups under various natural restrictions on how the product of two conjugacy classes decomposes.

The study of products of conjugacy classes in finite groups has become an active area of research in finite group theory, garnering significant attention in recent years. For an overview of this topic, we refer the readers to the survey by Arad and Herzog
[
2
]
and the more recent exposition in
[
6
]
. In 1985, Arad and Herzog
[
2
]
proposed the following conjecture: In a finite nonabelian simple group, the product of any two nontrivial conjugacy classes is never a single conjugacy class.
Despite considerable interest, this conjecture remains open, particularly in the case of finite simple classical groups. Various partial results have been established in support of the conjecture (see
[
2
,
17
,
23
,
24
,
36
]
), but a general proof remains elusive.
In a related direction, Thompson conjectured that if
G
is a finite nonabelian simple group, then there exists a conjugacy class
C\subseteq G
such that
C^{2}=G
. This conjecture has also attracted considerable attention, and significant progress has been made recently by Larsen and Tiep
[
32
]
, where they confirm the conjecture for many families of simple groups.

An element
g\in G
is real if it is conjugate to its inverse, that is, there exists an element
t\in G
such that
g^{t}=g^{-1}
. A conjugacy class
C
of
G
is called a real class if it contains a real element; in this case, we have
C=C^{-1}.

In
[
7
]
, the authors proposed the following conjecture: if a finite group
G
possesses a conjugacy class
K
such that
K^{-1}K=1\cup D\cup D^{-1}
for some conjugacy class
D
of
G
, then the subgroup
\langle K\rangle
generated by
K
is solvable. In the same paper, it is shown that
G
cannot be a simple group. Furthermore, under the assumption that
D=D^{-1}
, the authors prove that
\langle K\rangle
is solvable provided certain conditions on the sizes of
D
and
K
are met. When
K
is real,
D
is real and hence
K^{2}=1\cup D
. In this case, the solvability of
\langle K\rangle
follows directly from
[
8
, Theorem A]
. In the present work, we confirm that this conjecture holds in full generality.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
K
be a conjugacy class of
G
. Suppose
K^{-1}K=1\cup D\cup D^{-1}
for some conjugacy class
D
of
G
. Then
\langle K\rangle
is solvable.

In Theorem
1.4
, let
x\in K
and
y\in D
. Then
y^{-1}\in D^{-1}
. For each
g\in G
, either
[x,g]=x^{-1}x^{g}=1
, or
[x,g]
is conjugate to either
y
or
y^{-1}
. Consequently, for each
g\in G,

[x,g]=1
or
o([x,g])=m
, where
m=o(y)=o(y^{-1})\geq 1
is fixed. (Here, for
g\in G
,
o(g)
denotes its order.)
This leads to the following natural question: If
x\in G
and there exists an integer
m\geq 1
such that for all
g\in G
, either
[x,g]=1
or
o([x,g])=m
, is
\langle x^{G}\rangle
necessarily solvable?

When
m=1
, the element
x
lies trivially in
Z(G)
, so we may assume
m>1
. If
m
is a prime power, the conclusion follows from Theorem
1.1
. Now suppose
m
is not a prime power. If
x
is a
p
-element for some prime
p
, then we may invoke a result due to Shult
[
37
]
, whose proof is independent of the classification of finite simple groups. Consequently, our proof of Theorem
1.5
below is classification-free.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
x\in G
be a
p
-element for some prime
p
. Assume that there exist two distinct primes
r
and
s
such that either
[x,g]=1
or the order of
[x,g]
is divisible by
rs
for every
g\in G
. Then
x\in Z(G)
.

Theorem
1.5
can be viewed as a special case of Conjecture 2 in
[
26
]
. For arbitrary elements (not necessarily
p
-elements), we obtain the following variant of the Baer-Suzuki theorem, which provides an affirmative answer to the question above.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
x\in G
. Suppose that there exist distinct primes
r
and
s
such that for every
g\in G
, either
[x,g]=1
or the order of
[x,g]
is divisible by
rs
. Then
x\in F(G)
.

Remark 1.7
.

In Theorem
1.6
, the conclusion

does not necessarily holds if

does not have prime power order. For instance, let

in MAGMA
[

Our proof of Theorem

relies heavily on results from an on-going project by Guralnick and Tracey in

]
, which classifies all pairs

such that the element

lies in a unique maximal subgroup of the finite group

.
As a consequence of Theorems

and

, we obtain.

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let

be an element. Assume that there exists an integer

such that either
[x,g]=1
or the order of
[x,g]
is

We note that Corollary

provides an alternative proof of Theorem

.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
G
be a finite group. A component of
G
is a finite quasisimple subnormal subgroup of
G
. Recall that a finite group
L
is quasisimple if it is perfect, that is,
L=L^{\prime}
, and
L/Z(L)
is a finite nonabelian simple group. The layer of
G
, denoted by
E(G)
, is a normal subgroup of
G
generated by all components of
G
. The generalized Fitting subgroup of
G
, denoted by
F^{*}(G)
, is defined as the product
F^{*}(G)=F(G)E(G)
. As usual,
Z(G)
denotes the center of
G
. For a subgroup
H
of
G
, the centralizer and normalizer of
H
in
G
are denoted by
C_{G}(H)
and
N_{G}(H)
, respectively. We denote by
R(G)
the solvable radical of
G
, that is, the largest normal solvable subgroup of
G
.

The famous Burnside

-lemma (
[

) states that if
G
is a finite nonabelian simple group, then the trivial class is the only conjugacy class of

which has prime power class size. In particular, if a finite group

has a nontrivial conjugacy class of prime power size, then

is not simple. Kazarin extended this further by proving the following.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let

If
|x^{G}|
is a power of a prime, then
\langle x^{G}\rangle
is a solvable group.

Proof.

This is
[
30
, Theorem]
.
∎

We need the following result due to Shult.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

be a solvable subgroup of a finite group

lies in the center of every solvable subgroup of

. Then

lies in the center of

Proof.

This is Theorem 1 in
[
37
]
.

S
has irreducible characters
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
of degree
(q^{2}-1)^{2}
and
q^{4}-1
. It is readily to see that one of these two characters will have
p
-defect zero as wanted.

If
S
is one of the remaining simple groups in the list
\mathcal{L}
, then we can check using GAP
[
18
]
that the lemma holds in these cases as well.

Assume that
S\not\in\mathcal{L}
. Assume first that
S
is an exceptional simple group of Lie type in characteristic
r
.
It follows from the proof of Proposition 2.4 in
[
34
]
that
G^{*}
has two maximal tori

and
T_{2}
such that

d=\gcd(|T_{1}|,|T_{2}|)=|Z(G)|,\text{and }\gcd(|T_{1}|/d,|T_{2}|/d)=1

and that each
T_{i}
has a regular semisimple element
s_{i}\in[G^{*},G^{*}]
of prime order. Thus the corresponding Delighne-Lusztig characters
R_{i}:=R_{T_{i},s_{i}}
are irreducible characters of

, up to sign. Furthermore, for
i=1,2,

R_{i}(1)=\frac{|G|_{r^{\prime}}}{|T_{i}|}=\frac{|S|_{r^{\prime}}}{|T_{i}|/d}.

Hence either
R_{1}
or
R_{2}
has
p
-defect zero for each prime
p\neq r
dividing
|S|
.

Finally, assume that
S
is a simple classical group of Lie type in characteristic

. Since
S
is not in the set
\mathcal{L}
, it follows from the proof of Proposition 2.5 in
[
34
]
that

will possess two maximal tori

and

which satisfy the same properties as in the exceptional cases. Therefore, the conclusion of the lemma follows as before.
∎

We also need the following result about the centralizers of Sylow
r
-subgroups of almost simple groups whose socle is a finite simple group of Lie type in characteristic
r
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a finite almost simple group with socle
S
, where
S
is a finite simple group of Lie type in characteristic
r
. Let
R
be a Sylow
r
-subgroup of
G
. Then
C_{G}(R)\leqslant R.

Proof.

Let
R_{0}=R\cap S
and
H=N_{G}(R_{0})
. Then
R_{0}
is a Sylow
r
-subgroup of
S
and
R_{0}\unlhd R
as
S\unlhd G
. It follows that
R\leqslant H
and thus
R_{0}\leqslant O_{r}(H)\leqslant R\leqslant H
. Now
C_{G}(R)\leqslant C_{G}(R_{0})\leqslant H
and thus
C_{G}(R)=C_{H}(R).
By
[
20
, Corollary 3.1.4]
,
F^{*}(H)=O_{r}(H)
. By
[
4
, 31.13]
,
C_{H}(O_{r}(H))\leqslant O_{r}(H)
and hence

C_{G}(R)=C_{H}(R)\leqslant C_{H}(O_{r}(H))\leqslant O_{r}(H)\leqslant R.

The proof is now complete.
∎

The following lemma can be checked using the information on the orders of the Schur multipliers of finite simple groups. We will give a proof using the Schur-Zassenhaus theorem.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
L
be a finite quasisimple group. If
p
is a prime divisor of
|Z(L)|
, then
p
is also a divisor of
|L/Z(L)|.

Proof.

Let
p
be a prime divisor of
|Z(L)|
.
Assume by contradiction that
p\nmid|L:Z(L)|
. Let
P
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
L
. Then
P\leqslant Z(L)
and
|L:P|
is coprime to
|P|
. By the Schur-Zassenhaus theorem (
[
4
, Theorem 18.1]
),
L
has a subgroup
H
such that
L=HP
, where
H
is a
p^{\prime}
-group. As
P\leqslant Z(L)
while
L
is perfect, we have
L=[L,L]=[H,H]\leqslant H
whence
L=H
is a
p^{\prime}
-group, a contradiction. Therefore,

divides
|L/Z(L)|
as wanted.
∎

3.
Orders of commutators

Let
G
be a finite group. If
C
is a conjugacy class of
G
, we denote its class sum by
\widehat{C}=\sum_{y\in C}y
. The set of all class sums
\widehat{C}
, where
C
runs over the set of all conjugacy classes of
G
, forms a basis for the center
Z(\mathbb{C}G)
of the complex group algebra
\mathbb{C}G
. For each
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, there exists a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra homomorphism
\omega_{\chi}:Z(\mathbb{C}G)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by

\omega_{\chi}(\widehat{C})=\frac{|C|\chi(g)}{\chi(1)}

where
g\in C
(see
[
28
, Chapter 3]
). By
[
28
, Theorem 3.7]
,
\omega_{\chi}(\widehat{C})
is an algebraic integer.

Note that
\mathrm{S}_{4}(2)^{\prime}\cong\mathrm{A}_{6}
. For a nonempty set
\Omega
of size
n
, we denote the alternating group and the symmetric group of degree
n
acting on the set
\Omega
by
\mathrm{A}_{n}=\mathrm{Alt}(\Omega)
and
\mathrm{S}_{n}=\mathrm{Sym}(\Omega)
, respectively.
We now prove the following theorem which is equivalent to Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a finite almost simple group with simple socle
S
. Let
p
be a prime and let
x\in G.
If
[x,g]=1
or
[x,g]
is
p
-singular for every
g\in G
, then
x=1
.

Proof.

Let the pair
(G,x)
be a counterexample to the theorem with
|G|
minimal. Then
1\neq x\in G
and
[x,g]=1
or
[x,g]
is
p
-singular for every
g\in G.
Suppose that
H
is a finite almost simple subgroup of
G
containing
x
. Clearly,
[x,h]=1
or it is
p
-singular for every
h\in H.
By the minimality of
|G|
,
x=1
, which is a contradiction. Thus
x
does not lie in any proper almost simple subgroup of
G
. In particular,
G=\langle S,x\rangle=S\langle x\rangle
and hence
G^{\prime}=S
. Let
K=x^{G}.
Then

(1)

K^{-1}K=C_{0}\cup C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup\dots\cup C_{a}

where
C_{0}=1
and for
1\leqslant i\leqslant a,

C_{i}
is a conjugacy class of
G
containing
y_{i}
with
y_{i}\in G
a nontrivial
p
-singular element. Taking the class sums, we get

(2)

\widehat{K^{-1}}\widehat{K}=\sum_{i=0}^{a}n_{i}\widehat{C_{i}}

where the structure constant
n_{i}=n(K^{-1},K,C_{i})
can be computed by

(3)

n(K^{-1},K,C_{i})=\dfrac{|K|^{2}}{|G|}\sum_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\dfrac{|\phi(x)|^{2}\overline{\phi(y_{i})}}{\phi(1)}

(see Exercise (3.9) in
[
28
]
). For
i=0,
by the second orthogonality relation
[
28
, Theorem 2.18]
, we have

n_{0}=\dfrac{|K|^{2}}{|G|}\sum_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\dfrac{|\phi(x)|^{2}\overline{\phi(1)}}{\phi(1)}=\dfrac{|K|^{2}}{|G|}\sum_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}{|\phi(x)|^{2}}=\dfrac{|K|^{2}|C_{G}(x)|}{|G|}=|K|.

Let
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
. Applying the homomorphism
\omega_{\chi}
to the class sum Equation (
2
), we get

(4)

\dfrac{|K|^{2}|\chi(x)|^{2}}{\chi(1)^{2}}=|K|+\sum_{i=1}^{a}n_{i}\dfrac{|C_{i}|\chi(y_{i})}{\chi(1)}.

(a) Assume first that
S
is a sporadic simple group, the Tits group or an alternating group
\mathrm{A}_{n}
with
5\leqslant n\leqslant 19
. For these cases, the character tables of the almost simple groups
G
with socle
S
are available in GAP
[
18
]
. For each
1\neq x\in G,
we can compute the structure constant
n(K^{-1},K,C)
for the conjugacy classes
C=y^{G}
for every
y\in G
, where
K=x^{G},
and one can check that there exist two nontrivial elements
z_{1},z_{2}
of coprime orders with nonzero structure constants. Hence
z_{1}^{G}
and
z_{2}^{G}
appear in the decomposition of the product
K^{-1}K
, which is a contradiction.

(b) Assume next that
S\cong\mathrm{A}_{n}
with
n\geq 20.
For these cases,
G\in\{\mathrm{A}_{n},\mathrm{S}_{n}\}.
Let
\Omega=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. If
\sigma=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{s})
is an
s

\ell(\sigma)
the length of
\sigma
, which is
s
, and the support of
\sigma
is the subset
\mathrm{supp}(\sigma)=\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{s}\}
of
\Omega.
Let
x=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\dots\sigma_{k}
, where
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
are pairwise disjoint cycles in
\mathrm{S}_{n}
, including cycles of length
1
. Denote
\ell_{i}=\ell(\sigma_{i})
for the length of each cycle, and assume without loss of generality that
\ell_{1}\geq\ell_{2}\geqslant\dots\geqslant\ell_{k}.
Note that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\ell_{i}=n.

Assume

has a fixed point on
\Omega
. Then
\ell_{k}=1
. Without loss of generality, assume
\sigma_{k}=(n)
. Then
x=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\dots\sigma_{k-1}\in G\cap\mathrm{Sym}(\Omega_{1})
with
\Omega_{1}=\Omega\setminus\{n\}
. Now

is a finite almost simple group with socle

Assume
G=\mathrm{S}_{n}
. Suppose that

Then
\ell_{k}\leqslant n/2
Let

, where

and

and
\Omega_{2}=\Omega\setminus\Omega_{1}.
Here

. Let

, we see that

and thus

is either
1
or

-singular. Thus

satisfies the hypothesis of the theorem. By the minimality of
|G|
, we have
u=1
, a contradiction. Suppose that
k=1
so
x
is an
n

. Let

and

. Then both

and

lie in
\mathrm{A}_{n}\unlhd G
. By computation, we get
[x,g_{1}]=(1,3,n)
and
[x,g_{2}]=(1,3,4,2,n)
, violating the hypothesis of the theorem.

Assume
G=\mathrm{A}_{n}.
Observe that if
k=1
, then
x\in G
is an
n
-cyclic and
n
must be odd. The argument above also shows that this is impossible. Thus, we assume
k\geq 2.
Then
2\leqslant\ell_{k}\leqslant n/2
. Let
\Omega_{1}
be the support of
\sigma_{k}
and
\Omega_{2}=\Omega\setminus\Omega_{1}
. Then
m=|\Omega_{2}|=n-\ell_{k}\geq n/2\geq 10.
Let

. Then
H
is a finite nonabelian simple subgroup of
G
and
H
centralizes

. Note that

Assume
\ell_{k}
is odd. Then
v
is an even permutation. So

and

. Let

. Then

and thus

is either 1 or

-singular and thus

as

, a contradiction.

Assume
\ell_{k}
is even. Then

is an odd permutation and so

is odd as

is an even permutation. Let

. If

; and if

. As

; and if

. Thus

, which is a contradiction.

(c) Assume that
S\not\in\{\mathrm{S}_{4}(2)^{\prime},{}^{2}\textrm{F}_{4}(2)^{\prime}\}

Assume first that

. Let

is

-singular since

). For each

with

, we know that

for some

. As

. Moreover,

Assume that

,

It follows that

|\chi(x)|^{2}=\frac{\chi(1)^{2}}{|K|}.

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Suppose firsst that
x
is central modulo
O_{p}(G)
. Then for every
g\in G
, we have
[x,g]\in O_{p}(G)
, and the result follows.
We now prove the converse. Assume that
[x,g]
is a
p
-element for every
g\in G
. We will show, by induction on
|G|
, that
x
is central modulo
O_{p}(G)
; that is,
[x,g]\in O_{p}(G)
for all
g\in G.

Let
\overline{G}=G/O_{p}(G)
and adopt the ‘bar’ notation. Note that
O_{p}(\overline{G})=1
. For every
\overline{g}\in\overline{G}
, we have
[\overline{x},\overline{g}]=[x,g]O_{p}(G)
, which is a
p
-element in
\overline{G}
. Suppose
O_{p}(G)>1
. Then
|\overline{G}|=|G/O_{p}(G)|<|G|
, so by the induction hypothesis,
\overline{x}\in Z(\overline{G})
. Hence,
{x}
is central in
{G}
modulo
O_{p}(G)
as desired. Now assume
O_{p}(G)=1
. Our goal is to show that
x\in Z(G)
.

Let
M=\langle x^{G}\rangle<G
. Then
M\unlhd G
and since
O_{p}(G)=1
, we have
O_{p}(M)\leqslant O_{p}(G)=1
, so
O_{p}(M)=1
. For every
g\in M
, the commutator
[x,g]
is a
p
-element by hypothesis. Thus, by the induction hypothesis,
x\in Z(M)
. Since
Z(M)
is a normal
p^{\prime}
-subgroup of
G
, we conclude that
x\in O_{p^{\prime}}(M)\leqslant O_{p^{\prime}}(G)
. Therefore, for all
g\in G
,
[x,g]\in O_{p^{\prime}}(G)
. But since
[x,g]
is a
p
-element by assumption, it must be trivial. Thus
[x,g]=1
for all
g\in G
, and hence
x\in Z(G)
.

Thus we may assume that
\langle x^{G}\rangle=G
. Let
N
be a minimal normal subgroup of
G
.

Case
1
.
N
is abelian. Then
N
is an elementary abelian
r
-group for some prime
r\neq p
. Since
[x,n]\in N
is an
r
-element for all
n\in N
, it follows that
[x,N]=1
, so
x\in C_{G}(N).
As
C_{G}(N)\unlhd G
and
\langle x^{G}\rangle=G
,
C_{G}(N)=G
, so
N\leqslant Z(G).
Now
xN\in G/N
satisfies the condition that
[xN,gN]
is a
p
-element for all
gN\in G/N.
We claim that
O_{p}(G/N)=1
. Once this is established, we may apply the induction hypothesis to conclude that
xN\in Z(G/N)
, which implies
[x,g]\in N
for all
g\in G
. Since
[x,g]
is a
p
-element for every
g\in G
by hypothesis, it follows that
[x,g]=1
, so
x\in Z(G)
as wanted. To prove the claim: let
U/N=O_{p}(G/N)
. Since
N\leqslant Z(G)
,
N
is a central Sylow
r
-subgroup of

. It follows that
U
has a normal Sylow
p
-subgroup
P
. Since
U\unlhd G
, we have
P\unlhd G
as well. As
O_{p}(G)=1
,
P=1
. Hence
O_{p}(G/N)=1
, completing the proof of the claim. Thus, we may henceforth assume that
R(G)=1
.

Case
2
.
Assume that
N
is nonabelian. Then
N\cong S^{k}
, where
S
is a nonabelian simple group and
k\geq 1
.
Suppose that
N
is a
p^{\prime}
-group. Then
[x,N]=1
, so
N\leqslant Z(G)
, a contradiction. Hence
p
divides
|S|
. In particular,
O_{p^{\prime}}(G)=R(G)=1.
Let
H=\langle x\rangle N
, and suppose
H<G
. By induction,
x
is central in
H
modulo
O_{p}(H)
, so
\langle x\rangle O_{p}(H)\unlhd H
, and hence

. Since
R(H)\cap N=1
, we have
[R(H),N]=1
, and so
x\in C_{G}(N)\unlhd G
, contradicting the fact that
\langle x^{G}\rangle=G
and
N
is nonabelian. Thus
G=\langle x\rangle N.

Let
C=C_{G}(N).
Then
C\unlhd G.
Assume
C>1
. Then
[C,N]=1
and
C\cap N=1
. As
G=\langle x\rangle N
,
C\cong CN/N\leqslant G/N
and so
C
is a cyclic normal subgroup

. In particular,
C\leqslant R(G)=1
, a contradiction. Therefore,
N
is the unique minimal normal subgroup of
G
. Write
N=S_{1}\times S_{2}\times\cdots\times S_{k}
, where
S_{i}\cong S
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
. Note that
\langle x\rangle
acts transitively on the set
\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
.
Let
L=S_{1}
. Assume that
k\geq 2.
Then
L\neq L^{x}
and thus
[L,L^{x}]=1
by
[
4
, 31.5]
. Let
s\neq p
be a prime divisor of
|L|
and let
a\in L
be an
s
-element. Then
a^{x}\in L^{x}
commutes with
a
. It follows that
a^{x}a^{-1}=[x,a^{-1}]
is both an
s
-element and a
p
-element. Hence it is trivial, so
x
commutes with
a
and thus
x
commutes with all
s
-elements of
L
. Since
L
is nonabelian simple,
L
is generated by a nontrivial conjugacy class of
s
-elements, hence
x
centralizes
L
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
x
normalizes
L
which forces

. Therefore,
G
is a finite almost simple group with simple socle
S
.
By Theorem
3.1
, we conclude that
x=1\in Z(G)
, completing the proof of the theorem.
∎

4.
Products of conjugacy classes

Let
G
be a finite group. We denote the last term of the derived series of
G
by
G^{(\infty)}
.
We now prove Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
G
be a counterexample to the theorem with
|G|
minimal. Then there exists a conjugacy class
K
of
G
such that
K^{-1}K=1\cup D\cup D^{-1}
for some conjugacy class
D
of
G
, but
H=\langle K\rangle
is not solvable. Let
x\in K
and
y\in D
. Note that
D=y^{G}
and
D^{-1}=(y^{-1})^{G}.

Let
N
be a nontrivial normal subgroup of
G
. The image
\overline{K}=(xN)^{G/N}
in
G/N
also satisfies the hypothesis of the theorem. By the minimality of
|G|
, we conclude that
HN/N\cong H/H\cap N
is solvable. In particular, if
N
is solvable, then so is
H.
Therefore, we may assume
R(G)=1
.

Suppose
G
has two distinct minimal normal subgroups
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
. Then
N_{1}\cap N_{2}=1
and the previous argument implies that
H^{(\infty)}\subseteq N_{i}
for each
i=1,2
. Hence
H^{(\infty)}\subseteq N_{1}\cap N_{2}=1
, so
H
is solvable, a contradiction. Thus,
G
has a unique minimal normal subgroup
N\cong S^{k}
, where
S
is a finite nonabelian simple group and
k\geq 1
. It follows that
C_{G}(N)=1
, and so
F^{*}(G)=E(G)=N
.

Recall that
K^{-1}K=1\cup D\cup D^{-1}
and let
m=o(y)
. For every
g\in G
,
[x,g]=x^{-1}x^{g}\in K^{-1}K
, so either
[x,g]=1
or
[x,g]
is conjugate in
G
to
y
or
y^{-1}
. In particular,
[x,g]=1
or
o([x,g])=m
for all
g\in G.
If
m
is a power of a prime
p
, then Theorem
1.1
yields that
\langle x\rangle O_{p}(G)\unlhd G
, and hence
H
is solvable, a contradiction. So we assume that
m>1
is not a prime power.

Let
L
be a component of
G
. Then
L\leqslant N
and
L\cong S.
Assume that
L^{x}\neq L
. By
[
4
, 31.4]
,
[L^{x},L]=1
. Let
p
be a prime divisor of
|L|
and let
a\in L
be a
p
-element. Then
[a,a^{x}]=1
and thus
a^{x}a^{-1}=[x,a^{-1}]
is a
p
-element. Since
m
is not a power of
p
, we must have
[x,a^{-1}]=1
. In particular,
x
commutes with all
p
-elements in
L
. Since
L
is generated by its
p
-elements,
x
centralizes
L
which implies that
L^{x}=L
, a contradiction. Hence
L^{x}=L
for every component
L
of
G
. If
x
centralizes all components of
G
, then
x\in C_{G}(F^{*}(G))=C_{G}(N)=1
, a contradiction. Therefore,
x
normalizes but does not centralize a component
L
of
G
.

Let
M=\langle x\rangle L
and let
A=C_{M}(L)\unlhd L
. Then
L\cap A=1
and
\overline{M}=M/A
is almost simple with socle
\overline{L}=LA/A\cong L
. Choose
g\in M
such that
\overline{g}\in\overline{M}\setminus C_{\overline{M}}(\overline{x})
. Then
z=[x,g]=x^{-1}x^{g}\neq 1
. Since
1\neq z\in K^{-1}K=1\cup D\cup D^{-1}
, we have
z\in D\cup D^{-1}
. Thus
o(z)=m>1
and
t=o(\overline{z})
dividing
m
. Since
M=\langle x\rangle L
,
M^{\prime}=L
whence
z\in L
. On the other hand,
z^{t}\in A
. So
z^{t}\in L\cap A=1
, which implies
t=m
. We have shown that for all
\overline{g}\in\overline{M}
, either
[\overline{x},\overline{g}]=\overline{1}
or
[\overline{x},\overline{g}]
has order
m
. Since
m>1
is divisible by some prime, Theorem
3.1
implies
\overline{x}=\overline{1}
. Therefore,
x\in A
centralizes
L
, contradicting our earlier conclusion. This contradiction completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
G
be a counterexample to the theorem with
|G|
minimal. Then
x
is a
p
-element for some prime
p
and there exist two distinct primes
r
and
s
such that for every
g\in G
, either
[x,g]=1
or
rs
divides
o([x,g])
; yet
x\not\in Z(G)
.
By the minimality
G
, if
H
is a proper subgroup of
G
containing
x
, then
x\in Z(H)
.

Let
M=\langle x^{G}\rangle
, and suppose
M<G.
Then by the minimality of
|G|
, we have
x\in Z(M)\subseteq F(G)
. Since
x
is a
p
-element,
x\in O_{p}(G)
. However, since
x\not\in Z(G)
, there exists some
g\in G\setminus C_{G}(x)
, so
1\neq[x,g]\in O_{p}(G)
. This contradicts the assumption that
rs
divides the order of every nontrivial commutator
[x,g]
, because
O_{p}(G)
contains only
p
-elements. Hence, we must have

Assume that
G
is nonsolvable. Then
x\in Z(H)
for every solvable subgroup
H
of
G
containing
x
. By Lemma
2.2
,
x\in Z(G)
, contradicting our assumption. Therefore,
G
is solvable.

Now consider the normal subgroups
O_{r}(G)
and
O_{r^{\prime}}(G)\}
. Suppose
g\in O_{r}(G)\setminus C_{O_{r}(G)}(x)
. Then
[x,g]\neq 1
is an
r
-element, hence

, a contradiction. The same argument applies to
O_{r^{\prime}}(G)
. Therefore,
x
centralizes both
O_{r}(G)
and
O_{r^{\prime}}(G)
. Since

generates

, both

and
O_{r^{\prime}}(G)
are central in

. In particular,

. By Fitting’s lemma (
[

, 31.10]
),

, so it follows that

. As
x
is a

-element and

, it follows that

is a
p
-group. Now apply the same reasoning as in the second paragraph: if

is a

-group and

is not central, then there exists

such that

is a

-element. But this contradicts the assumption that

. Thus, we conclude that

∎

5.
Some variants of the Baer-Suzuki theorem

In this section, we complete the proofs of Theorem
1.6
and Corollary
1.8
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
q\geq 2
be integers. A prime divisor
p
of
q^{n}-1
is called a primitive prime divisor if
p
divides
q^{n}-1
but
p
does not divide
q^{m}-1
for all integers
1\leqslant m<n
. By Zsigmondy’s theorem
[
43
]
, such a prime exists for all pairs
(q,n)\neq(2,6)
. Moreover, if
p
is a primitive prime divisor of
q^{n}-1
, then
n
is the multiplicative order of
q
modulo
p
, so
n
divides
p-1
, and hence
p\geq n+1.

Let
G
be a finite almost simple group with socle
S
. Suppose that
S
is a classical group over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, where
q=\ell^{f}
for some prime
\ell
, and let
V
be the natural module for
S
of dimension
n
. According to Aschbacher’s theorem
[
31
, Theorem 1.2.1]
, if
H
is a subgroup of
G
which does not contain
S
, then
H
is either contained in one of the geometric collections
\mathcal{C}_{i}(G)
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant 8
, or
H\in\mathcal{S}(G)
, where
\mathcal{S}(G)
denotes the collection of almost simple subgroups acting irreducibly on
V
(see
[
31
, p. 3]
).

If
S
is a nonabelian simple group, we denote its Schur multiplier by
M(S)
. Throughout, we use the notation
\mathrm{L}_{n}^{+}(q)
for
\mathrm{L}_{n}(q)
and
\mathrm{L}_{n}^{-}(q)
or
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Let
G
be a counterexample to the theorem with minimal order. Then there exist distinct primes
r
and
s
such that either
[x,g]=1
or
rs
divides
o([x,g])
for every
g\in G
, yet
x\not\in F(G)
. Observe that the hypothesis of the theorem is inherited by any subgroup of
G
containing
x
.
By the minimality of
|G|
, if
H
is a proper subgroup of
G
containing
x
, then
x\in F(H)
. In particular,
X=\langle x\rangle
is subnormal in every proper subgroup of
G
containing
X
but
X
is not subnormal in
G
, since
x\not\in F(G)
. By Wielandt’s zipper lemma
[
29
, Theorem 2.9]
,
G
has a unique maximal subgroup
M
containing
x
and
x\in F(M).

We next show that
G=\langle x^{G}\rangle
. Suppose, for contradiction, that
A=\langle x^{G}\rangle
is a proper subgroup of
G.
Then
x\in F(A)
, by the observation above. Since
A\unlhd G
, we have
F(A)\subseteq F(G)
, implying
x\in F(G)
, contradicting the choice of
x
. Hence,
G=\langle x^{G}\rangle
as wanted.

Let
p\in\{r,s\}
and let
N\in\{O_{p}(G),O_{p^{\prime}}(G)\}
. Let
g\in N\setminus C_{N}(x)
. Then
[x,g]\neq 1
is a
p
-element or a
p^{\prime}
-element. Therefore,
rs\nmid o([x,g])
, contradicting the assumption on
x
. Thus
x
centralizes both
O_{p}(G)
and
O_{p^{\prime}}(G)
and since
x^{G}
generates
G
, we deduce that both
O_{p}(G)
and
O_{p^{\prime}}(G)
are central in
G
. In particular,
F(G)\leqslant Z(G)
. By Bender’s theorem (
[
4
, 31.13]
), we have

C_{G}(E(G))=C_{G}(F^{*}(G))\leqslant Z(F(G))\leqslant Z(G)

and thus
E(G)>1
.

Let
L
be a component of
G
and suppose
L^{x}\neq L.
By Burnside’s
p^{a}q^{b}
-theorem (
[
28
, Theorem 3.10]
),
|\pi(L)|\geq 3
. Thus there exists a prime
p
different from both
r
and
s
dividing
|L|
. Choose a
p
-element
a\in L\setminus Z(L)
such that
[x,a]\neq 1
and
[a^{x},a]=1
. Then
[x,a^{-1}]=a^{x}a^{-1}
is a nontrivial
p
-element. Hence
rs
does not divide the order of
[x,a^{-1}]
. Thus
x
normalizes every component of
G
. If
x
centralizes every component of
G
, then
x\in C_{G}(E(G))\leqslant Z(G)
, which is impossible. Hence there exists a component
L
of
G
such that
L^{x}=L
but
[x,L]\neq 1.

Suppose
\langle x\rangle L<G
. Then
\langle x\rangle L\leqslant M
, the unique maximal subgroup of
G
containing
x
. Since
L
is a component of
G
, it is also a component of
M
. By
[
4
, 31.4]
, we have
[F(M),L]=1
. Since
x\in F(M)
,
[x,L]=1
, a contradiction. Hence
G=\langle x\rangle L
, so
E(G)=L
and
C_{G}(L)=Z(G)=F(G)
.

Let
\pi=\{r,s\}
. For every
g\in G\setminus C_{G}(x)
,
rs
divides
o([x,g])
and since
[x,g]\in G^{\prime}=L
, it follows that
rs
divides
|L|
. Moreover,
O_{\pi^{\prime}}(G)\leqslant O_{r^{\prime}}(G)\leqslant Z(G)
. We claim that
O_{\pi^{\prime}}(G)=1
. Suppose by contradiction that
O_{\pi^{\prime}}(G)>1
. Then
G/O_{\pi^{\prime}}(G)
satisfies the hypothesis of the theorem and thus by the minimality of
|G|
,
xO_{\pi^{\prime}}(G)\in F(G/O_{\pi^{\prime}}(G))=F(G)/O_{\pi^{\prime}}(G)
and so
x\in F(G)
, a contradiction. Therefore,
O_{\pi^{\prime}}(G)=1.
It follows that

F(G)=Z(G)=O_{r}(G)\times O_{s}(G).

In particular,

Z(L)=O_{r}(L)\times O_{s}(L)\leqslant F(L)\leqslant F(G)=Z(G).

We next claim that the order of
xZ(G)
in
G/Z(G)
is divisible by
rs
. Fix
p\in\{r,s\}
and write
x=x_{p}x_{p^{\prime}}
, where
x_{p}
and
x_{p^{\prime}}
are the
p
-part and
p^{\prime}
-part of
x
, respectively. It suffices to show that
x_{p}\not\in Z(G)
. Since
x\in F(M)
,
x_{p}\in O_{p}(M)
and
x_{p^{\prime}}\in U:=O_{p^{\prime}}(F(M))
. Note that
C_{G}(x)<M
, for otherwise
x\in Z(G)
by Lemma
2.2
. Choose
g\in M\setminus C_{G}(x)
. Since
[O_{p}(M),U]=1
,

[x_{p},g]^{x_{p^{\prime}}}=[x_{p},g]\in O_{p}(M)\text{ and }[x_{p^{\prime}},g]\in U.

Hence

1\neq[x,g]=[x_{p}x_{p^{\prime}},g]=[x_{p},g]^{x_{p^{\prime}}}[x_{p^{\prime}},g]=[x_{p},g][x_{p^{\prime}},g].

As
p
divides
o([x,g])
, we conclude that
[x_{p},g]\neq 1
and thus
x_{p}\not\in Z(G)
. Therefore, the order of
xZ(G)
in
G/Z(G)
is divisible by
rs
as claimed.

We now prove that
|Z(L)|
is divisible by
rs
. Suppose by contradiction that
p
does not divide
|Z(L)|
for some
p\in\{r,s\}
. Then
O_{p}(L)=1
. Let
g\in G\setminus C_{G}(x)
and write
y=[x,g]\in L=G^{\prime}
, which is nontrivial with
rs\mid o(y)
. Write
y=y_{p}y_{p^{\prime}}
, where
y_{p}
and
y_{p^{\prime}}
are the
p
-part and
p^{\prime}
-part of
y
, respectively. Let
\widetilde{G}=G/Z(G)
and denote
\widetilde{y}=yZ(G)
. Suppose
p
does not divide
o(\widetilde{y})
, so
y_{p}\in Z(G)
. Hence
y_{p}\in O_{p}(G)
since it is a
p
-element. Therefore,
y_{p}\in O_{p}(G)\cap L=O_{p}(L)=1
, and hence
y_{p}=1
, a contradiction to the fact that
p\mid o(y)
. Therefore,
|Z(L)|
is divisible by
p
as wanted. Hence
|Z(L)|
is divisible by
rs
.

From the previous discussion, we see that if
S\cong L/Z(L)
, then
rs\mid|Z(L)|
and so
rs
divides
|M(S)|
. The Schur multipliers of the finite nonabelian simple groups are listed in Theorems 5.1.3 and 5.1.4 and Tables 5.1.C and 5.1.D in
[
31
]
. Examining these, we find that the only simple groups

such that

for distinct primes

and

are the follwoing:

(1)

and

(2)

and

(3)

and

and

(5)

(6)

and

with

and

For cases listed in

, we have

. In cases

and

,

which forces

and

Let
\widetilde{x}\in\widetilde{G}=G/Z(G)
be the image of

in
\widetilde{G}
. Then
\widetilde{x}
has order divisible by
rs
, and
\widetilde{G}=\langle\widetilde{x}\rangle S
is a finite almost simple group with simple socle
S\cong\widetilde{L}=LZ(G)/Z(G)\cong L/Z(L)
, where

is one of the simple groups listed above. Furthermore,
\widetilde{G}
has a unique maximal subgroup
\widetilde{M}
containing
\widetilde{x}
such that
\widetilde{x}\in F(\widetilde{M})
. Hence
|F(\widetilde{M})|
is divisible by
rs
. Using
[
27
]
and MAGMA
[
10
]
, we can eliminate the cases when

is one of the simple groups in
(1)-(5)
except for the case
S={}^{2}\textrm{E}_{6}(2).

Assume that
S=\mathrm{L}_{n}^{\epsilon}(q)
, where
q=\ell^{f}
for some some prime
\ell
and
\epsilon=\pm
.
Since
r\neq s
are primes and
rs
divides
|M(S)|=d=\gcd(n,q-\epsilon)
, we deduce that

and
q\geq 7
if
\epsilon=+
and
q\geq 5
if
\epsilon=-
. Furthermore, as
rs
divides

,
\widetilde{x}
is nontrivial in
\widetilde{G}
. In particular,
\widetilde{x}
is not unipotent.

By
[
27
, Proposition 6.2]
,
\widetilde{x}
does not normalize a parabolic subgroup of
S
. By Borel-Tits theorem
[
9
, Proposition 3.12]
(or
[

, Theorem 3.1.3]
),
C_{S}(\widetilde{x})
is a
\ell^{\prime}
-group. In fact
C_{\text{Inndiag(S)}}(\widetilde{x})
is an
\ell^{\prime}
-group, where

denotes the group of inner-diagonal automorphisms of

. Since

, we deduce that
\ell\not\in\{r,s\}.

Let
\widetilde{M}
be the image of
M
in
\widetilde{G}
. Then
\widetilde{M}
is the unique maximal subgroup of
\widetilde{G}
containing
\widetilde{x}
and
\widetilde{x}\in F(\widetilde{M}).
Since
\widetilde{G}=\langle\widetilde{x}\rangle S
, we can write
\widetilde{M}=\langle\widetilde{x}\rangle M_{0}
, where

By
[

,
\widetilde{x}
centralizes

. As

,
\widetilde{x}
centralizes

. In other words,

. Since
C_{S}(\widetilde{x})
is an
\ell^{\prime}
-group,

is an
\ell^{\prime}
-group.

Suppose that
\widetilde{M}
is of type
(II)
as defined in
[

, Remark 4.5]
. Then
\widetilde{M}\in\mathcal{C}_{5}(\widetilde{G})
. By Propositions

in
[

]
, since

, the structure of

implies that

,
\textrm{PSp}_{n}(q^{1/\alpha})
or
\mathrm{P}\Omega_{n}^{\pm}(q^{1/\alpha})
, for some prime divisor

of

. As

is nonabelian simple, it is a component of

and hence contained in

. However, this contradicts the earlier conclusion that

is an

-group.

By
[

, Proposition 6.1 (1)]
,
\widetilde{x}
is semisimple and

is a maximal torus. By
[

, Proposition 7.1]
, either

is a prime power or

with

, where

are odd, and

Since

,

cannot be a prime power. Hence the second case holds. Note that

divides
|C_{S}(\widetilde{x})|
. Since

, a primitive prime divisor

of

|M_{0}|
since

is divisible by a prime

By Proposition 6.1 in

]
again and

\widetilde{M}
is in one of the Aschbacher collections

or
\mathcal{S}
of
\widetilde{G}
. In the latter case, the socle of

is a finite simple group of Lie type in characteristic

Consequently,
M_{0}
also lies in one of those corresponding collections of
S
. We now examine each of these possibilities, noting from earlier that
M_{0}
is not a parabolic subgroup of
S
and

is an
\ell^{\prime}
-group.

(a)
M_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}(S)
. Since
M_{0}
is not a parabolic subgroup,
M_{0}
is of type
\mathrm{GU}_{m}(q)\perp\mathrm{GU}_{n-m}(q)
with
1\leqslant m<n/2.
Since
n\geq 6
and
m<n/2
, we deduce that

By Proposition 4.1.4 in
[
31
]
,
M_{0}
has a component isomorphic to a central quotient of
\mathrm{SU}_{n-m}(q)
, which is not an
\ell^{\prime}
-group.

(b)
M_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}(S)
. Then
M_{0}
has type
\mathrm{GU}_{m}(q)\wr\mathrm{S}_{t}
, where
n=mt,t\geq 2
or
\mathrm{GL}_{n/2}(q^{2}).2
.
If the latter case occurs, then
E(M_{0})
contains a central quotient of
\mathrm{SL}_{n/2}(q)
, not an
\ell^{\prime}
-group. Assume that the former case holds. If
m\geq 2
, then clearly
E(M_{0})
is not an
\ell^{\prime}
-group (see
[
31
, Proposition 4.2.9]
). If

, then

divides

is divisible by a primitive prime divisor

(c)
M_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{3}(S)

with

is a prime. Since

. By
[
31

M_{0}
has a component which is a central quotient of
\mathrm{SU}_{m}(q^{t})

\ell^{\prime}
-group.

(d)
M_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{4}(S)

\mathrm{GU}_{m_{1}}(q)\otimes\mathrm{GU}_{m_{2}}(q)

[
31
, Proposition 4.4.10]

-group.

(e)
M_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{7}(S)

\mathrm{GU}_{m}(q)\wr\mathrm{S}_{t}

\textrm{soc}(M_{0})=\mathrm{U}_{m}(q)^{t}

(f)
M_{0}\in\mathcal{S}(S)
. Since
M_{0}

\ell

Finally, assume

Then

In all cases, we obtain a contradiction. This completes the proof.
