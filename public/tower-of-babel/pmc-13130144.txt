Human Alpha1‐Antitrypsin Demonstrated Protective Effects Against Auditory Impairment in Murine Model of Noise‐Induced Hearing Loss
Authors: Benyamin M. Kaminer, Sabri El‐Saied, Ismael Abu Freh, Amit Amar, Oren Ziv, Daniel M. Kaplan, Eli C. Lewis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe pathophysiology of noise‐induced hearing loss (NIHL) encompasses excessive inflammation, tissue damage, production of reactive oxygen species, and apoptotic processes, culminating in irreversible damage to hair cells. Currently, no clinical interventions are available to mitigate this condition. Alpha1‐antitrypsin (AAT) is a circulating molecule with tissue‐protective properties that increases during inflammatory states and facilitates the resolution of inflammation. Transgenic mice expressing elevated levels of human AAT have demonstrated enhanced tissue repair in various in vivo models, including restoration of vestibular function following inner ear trauma. Noise exposure can result in either temporary or permanent changes in hearing sensitivity. A temporary threshold shift (TTS) reflects a reversible elevation in auditory thresholds, typically recovering over hours to days as cochlear structures regain function. In contrast, a permanent threshold shift (PTS) indicates irreversible injury to cochlear hair cells (HC) or synapses, leading to sustained hearing impairment. Repeated or severe noise exposure increases the likelihood that an initially temporary loss progresses to permanent damage, underscoring the importance of interventions that support tissue repair and recovery. This underscores the importance of implementing effective noise control measures and adhering to hearing protection protocols in environments with excessive noise. 1

The primary mechanism underlying noise‐induced hearing loss (NIHL) and associated synaptopathy is damage to the cochlear HC. TTS is attributed to irreversible damage to stereocilia or synapses in the HC, whereas moderate TTS results from a protective purinergic hearing adaptation. Damage to HC is partially mediated by the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and subsequent activation of intracellular stress pathways, leading to programmed and/or necrotic cell death. 2 , 3

Alpha1‐antitrypsin (AAT) is a circulating glycoprotein with tissue‐protective properties that are elevated under inflammatory conditions and is reversibly neutralized by local ROS. It is currently used to treat genetic AAT deficiencies. 4 Transgenic mice expressing human AAT (hAAT+/+) show accelerated tissue repair in several in vivo models. 5

Building on existing research, this study aimed to further explore the protective role of AAT in the inner ear, specifically focusing on its potential to mitigate the damage caused by excessive noise exposure. The correlation between AAT deficiency and hearing disorders as well as its positive impact on vestibular rehabilitation suggests that AAT may have broader applications in preserving auditory function. 6 , 7 By examining the effects of AAT in NIHL, this study sought to expand our understanding of its protective mechanisms and potential therapeutic applications.

We hypothesized that the transgenic overexpression of human AAT would offer protection against PTS following noise exposure in mice, as compared to wild‐type controls.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Each experimental group comprised 5 mice (10 ears total). Both male and female mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks, were included in each group. The experimental protocols and animal care complied with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Ben Gurion University. Animal experiments were performed using wild‐type C57BL/6 mice (6‐8 weeks old, Envigo, Israel) and homozygote hAAT transgenic mice (6‐8 weeks old, C57BL/6 background) that express lung‐specific human AAT and display high levels of circulating human molecules. All animals were housed in standard animal cages under conventional laboratory conditions, and all behavioral experiments were conducted during the light phase.

NIHL Model In Vivo

Wild‐type C57BL/6 mice (n = 5) and homozygote hAAT transgenic mice (n = 5) were housed in separate cages and subjected to broadband noise (2‐4 kHz) at 100 dB SPL for 2 hours, as described elsewhere. 8 Briefly, mice were placed in a specialized sound chamber equipped with a loudspeaker powered by an amplifier (ARTOS). During the experiment, the mice were allowed to move freely without restraint.

Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR) Measurement

ABR were recorded at baseline, 24 hours, 4 days, and 7 days after noise exposure, as described elsewhere. 9 Briefly, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine and xylazine. The ABRs were recorded at 4 kHz for each mouse. Responses were then obtained and averaged. Threshold was defined as the lowest stimulus intensity at which a positive wave was evident in the evoked response trace. The ABR results were analyzed by 2 evaluators. ABR were measured at specific time points to assess auditory function after noise exposure. The 4 kHz frequency was selected due to its susceptibility in this strain.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Welch's t‐test for unequal variances (two‐tailed). Welch's t‐test was used due to unequal variance and small sample size. The data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with statistical significance set at P < .05. Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software).

Results

Hearing Loss Induced by Noise Exposure in Wild‐Type and Transgenic hAAT Mice

The baseline ABR results indicated that the average hearing threshold in the hAAT group was 44 decibels (dB). In contrast, the WT group exhibited an average hearing threshold of 42.8 decibels (dB).

Twenty‐four hours after noise exposure, all mice exhibited significant impairment in hearing thresholds. The extent of impairment differed between the hAAT and WT groups. In the hAAT group, the mean auditory threshold increased markedly to 59 dB, indicating more severe hearing loss. The WT group also experienced a significant increase in auditory threshold, reaching 48.9 dB. The difference between the 2 groups was statistically significant (P < .05), as shown in Figure 1.

Expanded auditory threshold measurements on the fourth day revealed notable differences between the hAAT and WT groups. The hAAT group exhibited a higher average threshold of 51 dB, indicating a greater degree of hearing impairment compared to the WT group, which demonstrated a slightly lower average threshold of 47.2 dB (P < .05). Between Days 1 and 4, thresholds improved in both groups, and by Day 7 the mean auditory threshold was 47.5 dB in the hAAT group versus 53.3 dB in the WT group (Figures 2 and 3).

Discussion

Tissue repair following noise‐induced trauma is of paramount importance, as any failure in this process results in irreversible damage to the cochlea. Although corticosteroids are the most frequently employed therapeutic agents, their effectiveness is limited, and their systemic administration is associated with significant adverse effects.

Our findings indicate that hAAT initially exacerbates the threshold shift, but subsequently facilitates recovery, thereby differentiating TTS in hAAT mice from PTS in WT mice. This pattern suggests an enhanced capacity for tissue repair rather than protection from the initial acute insult. Excessive noise exposure has been shown to significantly affect cellular function and viability by elevating ROS levels. This increase in ROS can lead to cellular dysfunction and ultimately trigger apoptotic cell death, as demonstrated in previous studies. 10 , 11 The interplay between oxidative stress and inflammation is further exemplified by the contrasting roles of AAT and its oxidized form. Oxidized AAT, which results from direct exposure to ROS produced and released by neutrophils, exhibits proinflammatory properties. In contrast, native AAT exhibits anti‐inflammatory effects, highlighting the complex relationship between oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. 12

Furthermore, the marked increase in threshold observed in the AAT group compared with that in the WT group is intriguing. This increase could be a direct result of more than a single function of AAT, possibly affecting neural processing or other aspects of auditory perception. These observations highlight the complex interplay between AAT and auditory function, warranting further investigation of the specific mechanisms underlying these effects and their potential implications for understanding and treating AAT‐related auditory disorders.

Corticosteroids are the most prevalent treatment for inflammatory conditions in the inner ear and are administered both locally and systemically. While corticosteroids effectively reduce inflammation, their systemic administration is associated with significant side effects. Furthermore, systemic use increases the risk of bacterial infections and does not provide tissue protection. 12 , 13 Sendowski et al observed significantly reduced threshold shifts 48 hours postnoise exposure and notably lower outer hair cell (OHC) and inner hair cell (IHC) losses in ears treated locally with methylprednisolone. However, 14 days after noise exposure, the differences were minimal and not statistically significant. 14

In a separate experiment, Mg²⁺ supplementation was shown to reduce threshold shifts at 48 hours and 7 days following noise exposure, although this effect was no longer evident at 14 days in guinea pigs. 14 Likewise, antioxidant agents such as glutathione and N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) have demonstrated partial protective effects in rats and chinchillas when administered before or after noise exposure; however, these treatments do not prevent the development of PTS. Moreover, it is important to note that NAC can be ototoxic at high doses. 15 , 16

As previously discussed elsewhere, 5 these findings indicate that aggravation serves as a primary response in the environment characterized by overexpression of AAT and TTS in the AAT group, in contrast to PTS in the WT group. This study establishes a basis for re‐evaluating the role of tissue recovery in local compensatory mechanisms. Patients experience such significant changes in quality of life due to delayed or partial tissue recovery processes irrespective of direct neuronal injury and should provide an incentive for refocusing research on the loss of complex neuronal functions back to the immediate tissue environment and its potential capacity to act as a fundamental facilitator of functional restoration. The limitations of this study include the small cohort size, consisting of 5 mice per group and 10 ears per group analyzed, the assessment conducted at only one frequency (4 KHz), and the lack of histological correlation. These factors constrain the generalizability of the findings and should be addressed in future studies.

This represents preliminary data as proof of concept, and future studies should address the histological and vestibular status changes after excessive noise exposure and the role of systemic and topical application of AAT.

Conclusion

This study provides a promising foundation for future work on tissue‐protective agents such as AAT in the treatment of NIHL. These results highlight the importance of focusing on tissue recovery processes in addition to direct neuronal injury when developing strategies to preserve and restore auditory function.
