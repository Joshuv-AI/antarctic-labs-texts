A unified framework for identification of cell-type-specific spatially variable genes in spatial transcriptomic studies
Authors: Zhiwei Wang, Yeqin Zeng, Ziyue Tan, Yuheng Chen, Xinrui Huang, Hongyu Zhao, Zhixiang Lin, Can Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCharacterizing spatial variations in gene expression with cell type specificity is crucial for understanding complex traits and diseases, yet it remains challenging. Here, we introduce the Mixture of Mixed Models (MMM), a unified framework that effectively identifies cell-type-specific spatially variable genes (SVGs) in spatial transcriptomics. MMM achieves superior performance because of our innovations in model and algorithm design. In the mouse brain study, MMM reveals that these SVGs are significantly enriched for heritability in brain-related phenotypes, highlighting their importance in complex traits and diseases. When analyzing the cancer datasets, our findings also shed light on the role of cell-type-specific SVGs in cell–cell communications and microenvironment regulation.

Spatial transcriptomic (ST) technologies (1–8) enable the measurement of gene expression and physical locations simultaneously, providing unprecedented opportunities to reveal the diversity and complexity of biological systems within the tissue context (9, 10). To fully harness the power of ST technologies, it is crucial to characterize spatially variable genes (SVGs) (11–14) in a cell-type-specific manner—essentially, how the gene expression changes across space within a cell type of interest. The cell-type-specific SVGs may exhibit distinct spatial patterns such as clustering and periodicity, reflecting different cell states. These spatial patterns can be attributed to particular tissue structures or local microenvironment niches and indicate certain biological processes in various contexts (15), including functional programs, pathogenic processes (16), cell–cell communications (17–19), and tumor ecosystems (8, 20). Further investigations into the cell-type-specific SVGs have great potential to advance our understanding of the mechanisms underlying complex diseases (16, 21), and subsequently help identify potential therapeutic targets.

However, the current limitations of ST technologies present several challenges in characterizing cell-type-specific SVGs. First, cell type composition is a major confounding factor in practice (15). As the composition of cell types varies spatially, the expression of their marker genes also changes accordingly. Without effectively accounting for this confounding factor, the SVGs identified by most existing methods, such as SpatialDE (22), SPARK (23), SPARK-X (24), and HEARTSVG (25), may simply be marker genes of a cell type rather than cell-type-specific SVGs. In the ST data with low resolution, such as Slide-seq (3) and 10x Visium (4), the gene expression measurements at spatial spots originate from mixtures of multiple cells. Although cell type deconvolution (26, 27) can provide estimates of cell type proportions at each spot, the presence of cell type mixtures complicates the identification of cell-type-specific SVGs. Even in high-resolution ST data like MERFISH (1), Stereo-seq (6), 10x Xenium (7), and 10x Visium HD (8), uncertainties in cell type identification persist due to diffusion or imperfect cellular segmentation (15), and overlooking this uncertainty can lead to inflated type I errors and unreplicable results. Second, single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data are often used as the reference for cell type deconvolution and identification in ST studies. However, the expression profiles observed in ST data may not fully align with scRNA-seq reference data due to platform differences (26). Without accounting for the platform effects, spurious spatial variations in ST data might be misidentified as SVGs. Third, the expression patterns of cell-type-specific SVGs in real data remain largely unknown and possibly diverse, posing a challenge for using parametric methods that may have limited statistical power. There is still a need for the development of flexible methodologies capable of addressing these challenges and capturing a wide spectrum of spatial patterns.

In this paper, we introduce a unified statistical framework called Mixture of Mixed Models (MMM), standing for the Mixture of Mixed Models, to identify cell-type-specific SVGs. The success of MMM relies on its probabilistic model, which accounts for cell type composition and corrects for platform effects between ST and scRNA-seq data explicitly. In the MMM framework, cell-type-specific gene expression in ST data is decomposed into three components: the average gene expression of the same cell type obtained from scRNA-seq reference data, spatial variations, and nonspatial variations. The statistical significance of spatial variations is examined using a powerful nonparametric test (23, 28) capable of detecting diverse spatial patterns. While methods such as CTSV (29), C-SIDE (15), spVC (30), Celina (31), and STANCE (32) can be used to identify cell-type-specific SVGs, MMM achieves superior performance that benefits from our innovations in model and algorithm design (detailed comparison in SI Appendix, Methods). Through a comprehensive simulation study, we showed that MMM can produce well-calibrated P-values; thus, it can effectively control type I errors and achieve high statistical power. We then applied MMM to eight publicly available ST datasets of different tissues from various species. These datasets were generated by different platforms with a wide range of resolutions, including 10x Visium (4), Spatial Transcriptomics (2), 10x Xenium (7), 10x Visium HD (8), and Stereo-seq (6). In our study of the mouse brain, we demonstrated that MMM can identify cell-type-specific SVGs that are replicable across different technologies. We further conducted an integrative analysis with genome-wide association studies (GWASs) and found that the identified cell-type-specific SVGs are significantly enriched in brain-related phenotypes. This suggests that MMM has great potential to elucidate the mechanisms underlying complex traits and diseases. We also used MMM to analyze a high-resolution Xenium human breast cancer dataset by accounting for the uncertainties from imperfect cell segmentation and annotation, showing that certain cell-type-specific SVGs given by MMM may contribute to cell–cell communications and regulate the tissue microenvironment. Furthermore, we applied MMM to the 3D tissue models constructed from multiple ST slices, demonstrating its utility in 3D data analysis.

Results

Method Overview.

Given the common presence of cell type mixtures across space in the spatially resolved transcriptomic data, identifying cell-type-specific SVGs requires not only separating the contributions of different cell types from overall gene expression measurements but also inferring whether each contribution varies across spatial locations. This appears to be a challenging task; however, with the rapid advancement of cell type deconvolution methods (26, 27, 33, 34), their reliable performance offers opportunities to explore cell-type-specific signals. Hence, we propose a probabilistic framework called MMM that enables the statistical characterization of cell-type-specific SVGs by accounting for the cell type mixtures in the spatial context.

Specifically, we begin the model design with raw count data rather than transformed data to avoid information loss. Then, we use a log-mixture structure to account for cell type composition while simultaneously correcting for the spot and platform effects between ST and scRNA-seq data. To characterize the gene expression profile of each cell type at a spot, we decompose it on the log scale into three components: the average gene expression of the certain cell type, spatial variations, and nonspatial variations.

To handle the above complicated model, we propose a powerful score testing framework that can evaluate the significance of cell-type-specific spatial variations. We first develop an approximate algorithm based on penalized quasi-likelihood (PQL) (35, 36) to fit the null model efficiently. Then, we construct a cell-type-specific test statistic in quadratic form (28) to examine the significance of the variance component related to spatial locations. This test statistic incorporates a cell-type-specific membership matrix to account for the contributions from individual cell types, effectively addressing the confounding issues arising from the cell type mixtures in the ST data. Since the true expression patterns of genes are largely unknown and can vary across genes and cell types in practice, we use the Cauchy combination method (37, 38) to aggregate the information from diverse spatial kernels that cover a wide spectrum of common spatial patterns observed in real datasets. The technical details are provided in Materials and Methods, and SI Appendix, Methods.

Simulation Study.

We first conducted a comprehensive simulation study to benchmark MMM and related methods (15, 23–25, 31). To mimic the cell type mixtures in the ST data, we simulated two cell types, termed cell type 1 and cell type 2, which are colocalized at each spot (Fig. 1A). Simulated gene expression data were generated with various cell-type-specific spatial patterns, including “null” (randomly distributed), “gradient” (change along the x-axis), “hotspot” (highly expressed in the central circular region), and “streak” (highly expressed in the middle streak region) (Fig. 1B). For each experiment, we simulated 2,000 genes, where all of them were null genes for both cell types in the null case, while 10 of them were SVGs specific to cell type 1, and the rest were null genes for both cell types in the alternative case. Details are provided in SI Appendix, Simulation Design.

To highlight the essential role of each component in the MMM model, we simulated five scenarios, where Scenarios 1 and 2 were designed to study the influence of cell type mixtures on SVG identification, Scenario 3 was designed to study the spot and platform effects, Scenario 4 combined all the above settings to mimic the real ST data, and Scenario 5 was designed to study the method’s sensitivity to prior assumptions. Specifically, in Scenario 1, the proportions of cell types 1 and 2 were uniformly distributed across space (Fig. 1C). We found that the P-values of MMM and non-cell-type-specific methods were well calibrated in the null case (Fig. 1D), and all methods could successfully identify SVGs in the alternative case; however, non-cell-type-specific methods could not clarify which cell type the spatial variations belonged to (Fig. 1E). In Scenario 2, we varied the cell type composition spatially, with the proportion of cell type 1 increasing from left to right and cell type 2 in contrast (Fig. 1F). Due to the heterogeneity in gene expression profiles between the two cell types, non-cell-type-specific methods identified their respective marker genes as SVGs (Fig. 1H), while MMM controlled type I errors by accounting for the cell type composition (Fig. 1G). In Scenario 3, we kept the cell type composition randomly distributed, but varied the spot effects αi’s spatially (Fig. 1I) and added gene-specific platform effects γg’s (Fig. 1J). This was designed to examine the robustness of the methods in the presence of batch effects and platform differences. Under the null condition, MMM still produced well-calibrated P-values for both cell types by explicitly correcting for these two effects, while Celina yielded slightly inflated results (Fig. 1K). Celina’s unsatisfactory control of type I errors in this scenario is likely due to its modeling of normalized data instead of raw count data. The data transformation step before analysis can lead to an inadequate characterization of the mean–variance relationship inherent in raw count data and introduce systematic errors (23, 39), thus preventing the full elimination of confounding bias. In Scenario 4, we combined the settings of Scenarios 2 and 3 so that the cell type composition varied spatially, as well as varying spot effects and gene-specific platform effects. We should note that Scenario 4 was the most challenging and also the closest to real ST data. We then set the false discovery rate cutoff at 0.05 and measured the power of MMM and other cell-type-specific methods in the alternative case (Fig. 1L). We found that MMM was more powerful than the other two methods across all settings of spatial pattern and sample size because of its ability to aggregate information from multiple kernels corresponding to diverse spatial patterns and correction for spot effects and platform effects. Compared to Celina, the power gain of MMM is due to its modeling of raw count data, which avoids the loss of information (23, 40). C-SIDE suffered from low statistical power since its nonparametric mode is not effective in combining the P-values corresponding to multiple basis functions (SI Appendix, Methods). However, we should emphasize that its performance here does not reflect the effectiveness in the task for which it was originally designed. In Scenario 5, we generated data with varied prior distributions, including “flat top,” “skew,” and “bimodal” (Fig. 1M), to evaluate whether MMM is sensitive to prior assumptions (more prior settings in SI Appendix, Fig. S1). We examined MMM under the null condition first (Fig. 1N) and then in the alternative case (Fig. 1O). The obtained P-values were well calibrated in all settings, showing that MMM is robust to prior assumptions. In summary, MMM outperformed all existing methods in various scenarios, both in terms of statistical power and type I error control.

MMM Distinguishes Cell-Type-Specific SVGs from Cell Type Marker Genes in the 10x Visium Human Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex Dataset.

Here, we evaluated our method on a human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) dataset generated by 10x Visium (41), which consists of two pairs of adjacent slices with indices 151673–151676 from a neurotypical adult donor. Throughout the analysis of the four Visium slices, a single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset of human DLPFC (42) was used as reference.

By comparing with the methods that do not consider cell type composition, we demonstrated MMM’s ability to distinguish cell-type-specific SVGs from cell type marker genes. We began with a single slice with index 151673 and used STitch3D (34) to estimate cell type proportions (β^i,k’s; Fig. 2A), spot effects (α^i’s; Fig. 2B), and platform effects (γ^g’s; Fig. 2C). Clearly, there existed layer-specific spot effects and heterogeneous gene platform effects, and ignoring these effects can lead to confounded results when identifying cell-type-specific SVGs. To highlight the differences between MMM and non-cell-type-specific methods, we applied MMM alongside SPARK, SPARK-X, and HEARTSVG. Comparing the obtained P-values, genes MAG, KRT17, and HS6ST3 were flagged as SVGs by SPARK, SPARK-X, and HEARTSVG (Fig. 2D, first row), while MMM’s results indicated that these genes were not cell-type-specific SVGs (Fig. 2D, second row). Since these three genes were the marker genes of Oligos_3, Ex_8_L5_6, and Ex_10_L2_4, respectively, their spatial expression patterns were mainly affected by the distribution of cell type proportions across space (Fig. 2E, first and second rows). In contrast, MMM successfully revealed that their spatial variations were evenly distributed within a specific cell type by accounting for the cell type composition (Fig. 2E, last row), as indicated by the maximum a posteriori estimate of the variations u^i,k,g compared to the logarithm of mean expression (SI Appendix, Methods).

Furthermore, we analyzed the four integrated slices together from the 3D perspective (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) and noted that MMM identified more SVGs since considering more sample spots jointly improved the statistical power.

MMM Produces Replicable Results Across Multiple Adult Mouse Brain Datasets and Reveals the Heritability Enrichment in Brain-Related Phenotypes.

In this section, we applied MMM to multiple adult mouse brain ST datasets, including two slices profiled by the 10x Visium platform (43) and a 3D model constructed using 35 slices profiled by Spatial Transcriptomics (44). By comparing the results from several datasets, we showed MMM’s robustness and consistency across different sources and perspectives. We further showed that the heritability of cell-type-specific SVGs was significantly enriched in certain brain-related traits and diseases. Throughout the study, we used STitch3D to perform cell type deconvolution with a mouse brain snRNA-seq dataset containing 59 cell types (27) as reference.

We first focused on the two Visium slices of the adult mouse brain across the antero-posterior (AP) axis (43), which have relatively well-defined morphological characteristics and thus allow a reasonable and distinct comparison (Fig. 3A). For example, the thalamus is a paired structure of gray matter situated near the brain center, and the hippocampus exhibits consistent thickness and comprises curve-shaped subregions, including three cornu ammonis (CA) areas and the dentate gyrus (DG). Compared to existing methods, the P-values obtained from MMM were more consistent between two slices (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Then, we focused on four cell-type-specific SVGs identified by MMM, including Ext_Thal_1-specific SVGs Pcp4 and Nrxn3 (Fig. 3C), and Inh_1-specific SVGs Calb1 and Gnas (Fig. 3E). We found that their expression variations revealed by MMM within respective cell types showed replicable spatial patterns between the two slices (Fig. 3 D and F). Next, we applied MMM to a 3D model constructed from 35 coronal slices of a mouse brain hemisphere along the AP axis. The original dataset was generated by Spatial Transcriptomics technology (44). A significant challenge in analyzing the 3D model was the presence of batch effects across the numerous slices. However, the cell-type-specific SVGs identified by MMM exhibited spatial patterns that closely aligned with previous findings (Fig. 3 G and H). This suggests that MMM is effective in correcting batch effects across the slices. To further demonstrate the robustness of MMM, we evaluated its sensitivity to different deconvolution methods and rotation of spatial coordinates. First, by integrating MMM with estimated cell type proportions by RCTD and STitch3D, respectively, we found that it produced consistent results for most cell types (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Then, we applied MMM to the two slices rotated by 45 degrees and compared the results before and after rotation, showing its rotational invariance (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Overall, the cell-type-specific SVG identification results from MMM were stable and reliable in practice.

In many recent studies, the integration of the risk genetic variants identified by GWASs with other omics data provides valuable insights into mechanisms that underlie complex traits and diseases (16, 46–53). For example, the integration of GWAS and scRNA-seq data is a promising way to reveal functional programs and pathogenic processes at the single-cell resolution (49–52). However, the heterogeneity within the same cell type and lack of spatial location information severely limit accurate investigations of cellular origins in the tissue context. Although some methods attempt to utilize the ST data to tackle the above challenges (16), the common cell type mixtures confound the results and prevent finer resolution analyses. Here, we conducted an integrative analysis of the GWAS summary statistics data from European ancestry with the cell-type-specific SVGs identified by MMM. Unlike the heritability enrichment analysis for highly expressed genes in certain tissues, our analysis provides a unique angle to characterize risk genes within a spatially variable and cell-type-specific context.

To do so, we aligned mouse genes with human genes, which is a commonly adopted strategy for studying human traits and diseases (16, 46, 50). Despite the biological differences between humans and mice, arguments in favor of using mouse RNA-seq data have been well discussed (50). First, gene expression profiles are highly conserved between humans and mice (54), which is supported by a series of large-scale projects (55–57). Second, high-quality RNA-seq data are more readily obtainable from mice than from humans. Third, the key findings from mouse data have been successfully replicated in human data in previous studies (50). After aligning mouse genes with human genes, we mapped these genes to single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the human genome based on reference annotations (58), and generated a unique set of SNP annotations for each cell type. By treating a cell-type-specific SNP set as the annotation set, we evaluated whether the corresponding cell-type-specific genes can explain a higher proportion of heritability for a phenotype using the stratified linkage disequilibrium score regression (S-LDSC) (46, 59, 60). Here, we considered GWAS summary statistics data for 30 phenotypes (SI Appendix, Table S1), which can be roughly grouped into two categories: brain-related, including intelligence (IQ), memory, reaction time (RT), verbal-numerical reasoning (VNR), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), epilepsy, Parkinson’s disease (PD), anorexia nervosa (AN), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), bipolar disorder (BD), cannabis use disorder (CUD), depression, insomnia, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), obsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCS), posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), schizophrenia (SCZ), and Tourette syndrome (TS); and non-brain-related, including coronary artery disease (CAD), high blood pressure (HBP), pulse rate (PR), free thyroxine (FT4), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), type 2 diabetes (T2D), body mass index (BMI), waist-hip ratio (WHR), bone mineral density (BMD), height, and osteoarthritis (OA).

Note that we have two groups of genes for heritability enrichment analysis: cell-type-specific highly expressed genes identified from scRNA-seq reference data and cell-type-specific SVGs identified by MMM in ST data (SI Appendix, Table S3). To differentiate the contributions of two groups of genes, we first examined the heritability enrichment of genes that are highly expressed in each brain cell type. After mapping these brain-expressed genes to the human genome, we conducted S-LDSC using its baseline model, which includes 52 functional categories (such as genic regions, enhancer regions, and conserved regions) along with a category encompassing all SNPs. For each cell type, we incorporated the SNP annotation set derived from cell-type-specific highly expressed genes into the baseline model and estimated their heritability enrichment across the 30 phenotypes. We summarized the results of the enrichment analysis in SI Appendix, Fig. S9 and observed significant enrichment of these highly expressed genes in most brain-related phenotypes. In contrast, no cell types in this mouse brain study exhibited statistically significant enrichment in non-brain-related traits or diseases, serving as a negative control. To characterize the contribution from cell-type-specific SVGs, we further added an SNP annotation set derived from the cell-type-specific SVGs to the baseline model of S-LDSC, conditional on the cell-type-specific highly expressed gene sets. The results of the heritability enrichment analysis are shown in Fig. 3I. We found that the SVGs of certain neuronal subtypes were significantly enriched in cognitive traits, such as IQ and VNR, as well as in psychiatric disorders, including BD, depression, insomnia, OCD, and TS. This indicates that within these neuronal subtypes, only a subset of cells in specific regions drives the corresponding traits and diseases by regulating the expression of genetics-informed genes. For example, Nrxn3 was an SVG within Ext_Thal_1 cells (Fig. 3D) while its human homolog NRXN3 links synaptic cell adhesion to cognition and its disorders (61–63). Mutations in NRXN3 may cause developmental delay, learning disability, movement disorder, and behavioral problems (64, 65). We further performed a gene analysis using MAGMA (66) with GWAS summary statistics data as input and found that NRXN3 was significantly associated with IQ (adjusted P-value 1.284×10−4). In summary, the cell-type-specific SVGs identified by MMM have great potential to elucidate the mechanisms underlying complex traits and diseases in the spatial context through integrative analysis with GWAS hits.

MMM Yields Robust Results on the 10x Xenium Human Breast Cancer Dataset by Accounting for the Uncertainties in Cell Segmentation and Annotation.

To demonstrate the effectiveness and stability of our method for high-resolution ST data, we applied MMM to a human breast cancer dataset (7) generated by 10x Xenium, a recently developed imaging-based ST technology that enables measurement of gene expression at the subcellular resolution. The reference data were generated using Chromium Single Cell Gene Expression Flex applied to FFPE tissues (scFFPE-seq) in the same study (7).

Typically, analyzing the Xenium data at the single-cell level requires two preprocessing steps: cell segmentation (67–69) and cell type annotation (70, 71) (Fig. 4A), both of which introduce significant uncertainties in the subsequent investigations. For example, in the cell segmentation step, imperfect boundary division can lead to the inclusion of transcripts from surrounding cells into the target cell, thus causing cell mixtures. More importantly, these transcripts may not belong to the target cell type (Fig. 4B). Second, cell type annotation in a deterministic way can also bring uncertainty due to imperfect segmentation. To demonstrate this, we applied RCTD to estimate the probabilities of the most likely cell type of each cell segmented by UCS (69). Notably, many cells exhibit a probability of less than 0.75 for their most likely cell type (Fig. 4C). Ignoring these uncertainties can lead to inflated type I error rates when identifying cell-type-specific SVGs. Moreover, outcomes from different segmentation and annotation methods can vary a lot (Fig. 4D), posing a huge challenge for reproducibility. Within the MMM framework, we use the log-mixture structure to account for these uncertainties in the probabilistic view, producing consistent P-values when using segmented cells from different methods as input (Fig. 4E, first row); in contrast, ignoring these uncertainties may lead to substantially less consistent results (Fig. 4E, second row).

We also visualized the expression levels of cell-type-specific SVGs identified by MMM (Fig. 4F), some of which exhibited distinct expression patterns across different spatial domains, suggesting that these spatial variations were driven by the activities of other cells in niches with high probability. For example, the expression levels of CEACAM6 within DCIS 2 cells were significantly different between the area surrounded by invasive tumor cells and other areas (Fig. 4G). To further verify this conjecture, we performed spatial clustering to assign cells to clusters based on their niches using CellCharter (72) (Fig. 4H). We found that DCIS 2 cells distributed in different areas were assigned to different clusters (blue and pink), indicating the differences in their microenvironment structures. We then applied niche-DE (19) to perform the niche-differential expression analysis. We noted that the identified cell type DCIS 2-specific niche-associated genes, which were differentially expressed within DCIS 2 cells in the context of specific spatial niches, had a considerable degree of overlap with the SVG results from MMM (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). For example, CEACAM6 was a niche-associated gene of DCIS 2 cells regulated by the presence of invasive tumor cells with adjusted P-value 5.550×10−3 reported by niche-DE, suggesting certain signaling molecule interactions. This was also validated by applying CellChat (73), whose results revealed that CEACAM signaling pathway was an important component of the cell–cell communication network in this breast cancer dataset (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Notably, CEACAM6 is a cell surface receptor that can influence intracellular signaling events and plays an important role in tumorigenesis, invasion, and metastasis in vivo (74–77). It was also found that CEACAM6 could potentially contribute to the regulation in the tumor-stroma boundary, which was correlated with the efficacy of immunotherapy (78). Further investigations, such as CEACAM6-targeted antibody–drug conjugate, has shown promising improvement in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma therapy (79). Finally, we used g:Profiler (80) to perform a functional enrichment analysis for the DCIS 2 cell-type-specific SVG set (SI Appendix, Fig. S11) and found that it was enriched in gene ontology (GO) terms including anchoring junction (adjusted P-value 7.621×10−6), apical junction complex (adjusted P-value 8.208×10−6), cell–cell junction (adjusted P-value 2.957×10−4), apoptotic process (adjusted P-value 8.942×10−4), and cell adhesion (adjusted P-value 2.604×10−3).

MMM Provides Consistent Results Across Multiple Colorectal Cancer Datasets from Different ST Technologies.

To evaluate whether MMM can obtain consistent results between ST data with varied resolutions, we applied MMM to the human colorectal cancer (CRC) datasets (8) generated by multiple ST technologies, including Visium, Visium HD, and Xenium. Throughout the study, we used the paired scRNA-seq dataset provided in the same study (8) as reference. We chose the CRC data for two reasons: First, the extreme sparsity and high noise level in the Visium HD data make the analysis particularly challenging, allowing us to highlight MMM’s advantage in statistical power; Second, the paired Visium and Xenium data could be used for verifying the identified cell-type-specific SVGs in the absence of ground truth.

In the tumor boundary region, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are often the most abundant stromal cell type, and macrophages are the most abundant immune cell type, both playing crucial roles in shaping the tumor microenvironment (TME). Therefore, we focused on CAFs and macrophages to make the comparison between MMM and existing methods. Since there was no ground truth on cell-type-specific SVGs, we first applied these methods to the Visium HD data and then used the identified cell-type-specific SVGs in the paired Visium data for verification. In the comparison with Celina and C-SIDE, we found that MMM not only identified more cell-type-specific SVGs but also achieved greater consistency between the Visium HD and Visium data (SI Appendix, Figs. S12 and S13). This showed that MMM enjoys higher statistical power when handling sparse and noisy data and thus could obtain more reliable results. We also visualized the spatial patterns of the identified SVGs for CAFs and macrophages using the datasets generated by Visium, Visium HD, and Xenium (Fig. 5A). We found that the spatial patterns revealed by MMM were highly consistent between different ST technologies. Specifically, MMM identified the colocalization of two subpopulations, MMP11+ CAFs and SPP1+ macrophages, around the tumor boundary region, providing insights into the tumor microenvironment.

MMM Reveals 3D Spatial Patterns of the Cell-Type-Specific SVGs in the Drosophila Embryo Model Constructed from the Stereo-Seq Dataset.

In this section, we used MMM to analyze a 16 to 18 h Drosophila embryo dataset within certain spatial domains to present more distinct spatial patterns of the identified cell-type-specific SVGs in the organ context. The dataset comprises multiple Stereo-seq ST slices (81), where spatial bins were created by aggregating 20×20 DNA nanoballs into 10 µm × 10 µm-sized bins as described in the original study. The reference data were built upon a three-level single-cell combinatorial-indexing RNA-sequencing (sci-RNA-seq3) Drosophila embryo atlas (82) that matches the same developmental stage as the ST data.

We began by applying STitch3D to estimate cell type proportions and reconstruct a 3D whole organism spatial atlas based on 13 ST slices. STitch3D successfully reconstructed the central nervous system (CNS) and salivary gland, which fully recapitulated the morphology and position of the corresponding organ structures (Fig. 6 A and C). We then zoomed in on these two specific regions and applied MMM to reveal the spatial patterns of cell-type-specific SVGs in the organ context. Compared to other tasks in which MMM was examined, the detection of cell-type-specific SVGs for the Drosophila embryo faced additional challenges. First, the Stereo-seq dataset is highly sparse with more than 95% genes in most spatial bins having zero counts. Second, only a small subset of spots are available for analysis when we focus on a specific region. Both data sparsity and the limited sample size severely restrict statistical power. Moreover, batch differences among multiple ST slices largely confound the results and may cause false discoveries (Fig. 6 B and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). Despite the difficulties, MMM successfully identified cell-type-specific SVGs and enjoyed high statistical power by directly working on raw count data rather than transformed data while simultaneously correcting for slice-specific effects (SI Appendix, Methods). We further visualized the expression variations of the identified cell-type-specific SVGs and found that these genes varied significantly at different locations within certain organs (Fig. 6 B and D), suggesting that they may correspond to different developmental states or functions. For example, jim is an important transcription factor in neuronal diversity (83) and elimination (84), and fabp overexpression enhances autophagy and suppresses amyloid- pathology, leading to potential therapeutic interventions for Alzheimer’s disease (85). We also used g:Profiler (80) to perform a functional enrichment analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). In the analysis of the CNS-specific SVGs, we noted that the GO terms related to CNS development were significantly enriched, such as neuron differentiation (adjusted P-value 2.050×10−4) and generation (adjusted P-value 4.979×10−4).

Discussion

Characterizing spatial variations in gene expression with cell type specificity is an important but challenging task for investigating the landscape of complex biological systems in ST studies. In this paper, we have presented MMM, a unified statistical framework for identifying cell-type-specific SVGs in ST datasets. The success of MMM relies on its three carefully designed features. First, MMM uses a log-mixture structure to decompose the measured gene expression at each spot into a linear combination of fractions attributed to different cell types on the log scale, so that the cell-type-specific SVG identification results will not be dominated by the marker genes of certain cell types. Second, MMM explicitly corrects for platform differences between ST and scRNA-seq data, which are commonly found in real data. In contrast, overlooking this difference can lead to misidentification of spatial variations. Third, MMM can aggregate the information across multiple kernels corresponding to various spatial patterns. Therefore, the overall assessment output by MMM is effective in detecting unknown and potentially diverse spatial patterns in real ST data. Through comprehensive numerical experiments, we showed that MMM enjoys high statistical power while appropriately controlling type I error. We also used MMM to analyze eight publicly available ST datasets of different tissues generated from various platforms with a wide range of resolutions. The experimental results indicate that even for high-resolution ST data, considering cell type mixtures contributes to obtaining more stable, reliable, and replicable results. MMM is also easily applicable to 3D datasets, providing more biological insights from the 3D perspective.

MMM directly models the raw RNA counts to avoid information loss, resulting in an appreciable power gain (23, 40). In contrast, ignoring the count nature of ST data can lead to an inadequate characterization of the mean–variance relationship and introduce systematic errors (23, 39). Since the normal distribution cannot approximate the sparse data appropriately, such a power gain is apparent in data with low counts, especially for the recently developed high-resolution ST technologies like Stereo-seq and Visium HD. However, when handling data with high counts, the Gaussian approximation approach is attractive since a more computationally efficient algorithm can be derived.

Finally, we discuss the limitations and potential extensions of MMM. First, MMM requires annotated scRNA-seq data for reference-based deconvolution of cell types. The quality of scRNA-seq reference data and how well it matches the ST data have a notable influence on the deconvolution results and, subsequently, on the cell-type-specific SVG identification. Although single-cell atlases are increasingly available, they may contain missing cell types or substantial platform effects. More importantly, some ST tissues may lack the corresponding scRNA-seq reference data. Second, the cell types of interest may be present in extremely low quantities, which can make MMM unreliable for such rare cell types due to limited sample sizes and significant confounding effects from other cell types mixed together. These limitations may not be reflected in simulations because of the idealized settings of cell type composition but have essential impacts in practice as shown in our analysis of the Visium adult mouse brain dataset (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Third, while our nonparametric statistical testing framework makes MMM powerful in characterizing general spatial gene expression patterns, their clear biological interpretations still rely on specific scientific questions and further investigations, as illustrated in our real data analyses. Compared to the methods that work on the predefined covariates, such as C-SIDE and niche-DE (SI Appendix, Methods), MMM is more suitable for preliminary exploration in cases where prior understanding of ST data is limited. Fourth, MMM was designed to capture spatially correlated cellular heterogeneity by considering each gene separately. However, combining all genes together could provide gene-set-level information. For example, representing the variations of spots across all genes through the low-rank approximation (86–89) generates interpretable factors that can provide deeper biological insights, such as the identification of metagenes, and gene–gene covariation and coregulation analysis.

Materials and Methods

The MMM Method.

Let Y=Yi,g∈NI×G denote the observed gene expression count matrix with the spot index i∈1,⋯,I and the gene index g∈1,⋯,G. To account for cell type mixtures, we first need to determine the cell type proportions βi=βi,1,⋯,βi,KT∈RK of each spot i, where K represents the total number of cell types. This can be estimated using deconvolution methods (26, 34) with the paired scRNA-seq data as reference. We further introduce two effects, denoted as α=αi∈RI and γg∈R, to account for spot-specific effects and gene-specific platform effects between ST and scRNA-seq data. Specifically, α captures the variations in total transcript counts between spots while γg accounts for the expression differences of gene g between scRNA-seq and ST platforms. As we are interested in cell-type-specific spatial gene expression patterns, we next introduce the cell-type-specific gene expression profiles ηi,g=ηi,1,g,⋯,ηi,K,gT∈RK for spot i. Then, we consider the following hierarchical probabilistic model (26, 90) for the observed RNA counts in ST data:[1]Yi,g∣λi,g∼PoissonNiλi,g,[2]logλi,g=xiTξg+log∑k=1Kβi,kηi,k,g+αi+γg,

where Ni is the total transcript count for spot i, λi,g represents the underlying expression level of gene g for spot i, xi=xi,1,⋯,xi,CT∈RC is the covariate vector, and ξg=ξ1,g,⋯,ξC,gT∈RC is the vector of corresponding fixed effects shared by all spots. The log-mixture structure (Eq. 2) separates the contributions of different cell types by representing λi,g as the weighted sum of K cell-type-specific gene expressions ηi,k,g’s after adjusting for the multiplicative effects xiTξg, αi, and γg. To characterize cell-type-specific gene expression profiles, we decompose ηi,k,g into three components (15, 23) on its log scale as[3]logηi,k,g=logη0,k,g⏟log mean expression+bi,k,g⏟spatial variation+ϵi,k,g⏟nonspatial variation,

where logη0,k,g is the logarithm of mean expression of cell type k, calculated from scRNA-seq data. The term bi,k,g captures the cell-type-specific spatial correlation pattern among spots at different locations, and ϵi,k,g represents nonspatial variation. Given the mixed-effects model (Eq. 3), we now focus on how to distinguish the spatial variation term from the nonspatial variation term. For gene g, we use a stationary Gaussian process to characterize the spatial variations from the same cell type k across all spots and an independent and identical Gaussian prior for all nonspatial variations (23)[4]b1,k,g,⋯,bI,k,gT∼N0,τk,gK,ϵi,k,g∼N0,τϵ,g,

where matrix K∈RI×I is a prespecified kernel function of the spatial locations ℓ=ℓ1,⋯,ℓIT with the (i,i′)-th element being Ki,i′=Kℓi,ℓi′, τk,g is the scaling factor of spatial variance component, and τϵ,g is the variance of nonspatial variance component. In contrast to existing methods, our formulation presents several unique advantages for characterizing spatial gene expression patterns in a cell-type-specific manner. First, we begin the model design with raw count data rather than transformed data to avoid information loss. Second, the log-mixture structure (Eq. 2) accounts for cell type composition while simultaneously correcting for the spot and platform effects between ST and scRNA-seq data. Third, the mixed-effects model (Eq. 3) enables a statistically rigorous way to examine the significance of spatial variations. Furthermore, our model can be extended to identify cell-type-specific SVGs in the multiple slices case and allow for batch effect correction by modeling the slice-specific effects (SI Appendix, Methods). In summary, these features enable MMM to seamlessly integrate with the count-based deconvolution methods, establishing it as a unified framework for identifying cell-type-specific SVGs.

To determine whether gene g is an SVG of cell type k, we can formulate it as a statistical testing problem with the null hypothesis H0:τk,g=0 against the alternative one H1:τk,g>0. To evaluate the significance of the spatial variance component of each cell type, we propose a powerful score testing framework while considering the cell type mixtures. Specifically, we first develop an approximate algorithm based on PQL (35, 36) to fit the null model efficiently. After the algorithm converges, we obtain the pseudo data vector Y~g∈RI in the linear mixed-effects form with the marginal distribution Y~g∼NXξg,Σg under the null condition. Here, X=x1,⋯,xIT∈RI×C is the covariate matrix of all spots, Σg=Wg−1+∑k=1KMk,gτϵ,gIMk,gT is the covariance matrix, Wg∈RI×I is the diagonal weight matrix, and Mk,g∈RI×I is the membership matrix indicating the contribution of cell type k to each spot. Motivated by the marginal distribution of pseudo data, we construct a cell-type-specific test statistic in quadratic form (28)[5]Qk,g=12PgY~gTMk,gKMk,gTPgY~g,

which follows a mixture of χ12 distributions under the null hypothesis. Here, Pg is the symmetric projection matrix defined as Pg=Σg−1−Σg−1XXTΣg−1X−1XTΣg−1. Note that Mk,g contains cell-type-specific information to help address the confounding issue due to the mixture of cell types. The power of the above hypothesis testing scheme is obviously determined by how well the kernel matrix K matches the true spatial variations exhibited by the gene of interest within a certain cell type. Hence, we use the Cauchy combination method (38) to aggregate information from multiple kernels capturing various spatial patterns and calculate a calibrated P-value as the overall assessment. The technical details are provided in SI Appendix, Methods.

Seamless Integration with Cell Type Deconvolution.

Through our numerical experiments, we found that the cell type proportions estimated by the deconvolution methods play an important role in the cell-type-specific SVG identification, and thus keeping the deconvolution and SVG models consistent in terms of the definition of cell type proportion is essential to controlling the false positive rates and yielding reliable and interpretable results. Within the MMM model, cell type proportion βi,k represents the ratio of the transcript count from cell type k to all transcripts at spot i. Here, we highlight its difference with another definition based on the number of cells rather than transcripts. Therefore, we chose RCTD (26) and STitch3D (34) to estimate the cell type proportions for MMM throughout the study. Specifically, we used RCTD for the single ST slice case and STitch3D for the multiple ST slices case. For a fair comparison in simulation studies, we used RCTD for C-SIDE (15) and CARD (33) for Celina (31) as suggested in their tutorial websites. The above deconvolution methods all require the ST data with paired scRNA-seq data as input.

MMM offers a seamless integration with the results from the deconvolution step. For example, the cell type proportion βi,k, spot-specific effect αi, and gene-specific platform effect γg can be directly inherited from the cell type deconvolution step. After plugging in these estimates, no identifiable issue exists when fitting the MMM model. While the first two are necessary (βi,k and αi), users can choose not to provide the platform effect γg, and our software would estimate it automatically by adding a column of ones in the covariate matrix X and treating γg as an intercept term. Comparing to fixing γg the same as the deconvolution result, we observed better model fits by leaving it unconstrained in practice. The reason can be that the deconvolution model may not be exactly the same as the SVG model, and the latter fitted one should therefore be more appropriate. As a result, we allow γg to be a free parameter in our software.

Computational Efficiency.

We analyze the computational complexity of the MMM method to assess its scalability. Given the RNA count matrix Y∈NI×G containing I sample spots and G genes, we focus on one gene at a time since all genes can be processed in parallel in our software implementation. To fit the null model, the MMM algorithm requires OI2 computations. In the statistical testing step, we need OI2 computations to evaluate the test statistics. Therefore, the overall computations are quadratic to the sample size I and linear to the number of genes G.

We implemented the MMM method in the open-source R package MCube, which employs a carefully designed parallel computing framework to achieve high scalability and efficient memory control with user-configurable parameters. Notably, users can flexibly specify the number of worker processes along with the number of threads on Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms per worker. This hierarchical control empowers the maximal utilization of available computational resources. In addition, to address memory constraints inherent in large-scale kernel matrix operations, we handle matrices that are too large to load in memory through memory-mapping to binary files on disk (91). This approach enables the kernel matrices that exceed physical memory limits while maintaining efficient data access.

We also conducted a runtime benchmark on simulated datasets (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) and recorded the computation time for all the real data analyses (SI Appendix, Table S7).
