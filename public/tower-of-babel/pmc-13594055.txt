A Cost-Effectiveness Analysis for Treatments of Patients with Early Stage Huntington’s Disease in the USA
Authors: Divya Patil, Julia F. Slejko, Joseph M. Savitt, T. Joseph Mattingly II
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/ObjectiveHuntington’s disease (HD) is a rare neurodegenerative condition caused by mutations in the huntingtin gene. Emerging therapies such as Tominersen and AMT-130 have potential to treat HD, highlighting the importance of evaluating their cost-effectiveness. This study evaluates the cost-effectiveness of Tominersen and AMT-130 versus standard of care (SoC) for early stage HD in the US.MethodsA Markov model with four health states (early, middle, late, and death) was developed to estimate lifetime costs and benefits from a modified societal perspective, with death as an absorbing state. The model used 1-year cycle length with half-cycle correction, and an annual discount rate of 3% was used for all future costs and utilities. Health benefits were measured in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). All costs were adjusted to 2024 US dollars. As per the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) value assessment framework, a willingness-to-pay threshold (WTP) of $500,000/QALY gained was considered given that HD is a rare disease. Scenario analysis assessed the impact of adding value of hope to the base-case utility values. Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to capture uncertainty across all model parameters.ResultsCompared with SoC, Tominersen increased costs by $2.11 million and QALYs by 0.93, resulting in an ICER of $2.28 million per QALY gained, whereas AMT-130 increased costs by $1.49 million and QALYs by 5.23, resulting in an ICER of $285,703 per QALY gained in the base case. Probabilistic analysis showed AMT-130 had 80% probability of being cost-effective at $350,000/QALY gained.ConclusionsFrom a modified US societal perspective, this analysis suggests that AMT-130 may be cost-effective relative to SoC, whereas Tominersen exceeds the WTP threshold of $500,000 per QALY gained. Further evidence on long-term effectiveness and durability is needed to inform policy and reimbursement decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s41669-026-00668-5.

Key Points for Decision-Makers

This study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of two investigational treatments for early stage HD—Tominersen and AMT-130—from a modified societal perspective in the US. Introduction

Huntington’s disease (HD) is a rare neurodegenerative condition caused by mutations in the huntingtin gene. HD is a devastating neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the progressive development of psychiatric manifestations, cognitive decline, and uncontrolled jerky movements known as chorea that include bothersome and potentially disabling excessive movements in the fingers, feet, face, and torso [1]. Early signs of HD can vary widely, but often involve symptoms such as personality changes, mood swings, depression, forgetfulness, impaired judgment, unsteady gait, involuntary movements (chorea), slurred speech, difficulty in swallowing, and significant weight loss [2, 3].

The prevalence rate of HD in predominantly white populations averages around 10 cases per 100,000 individuals. The diagnosed prevalence of HD has exhibited a global increase of approximately 10–20% in each decade. However, the actual prevalence of HD is probably underestimated, as some individuals with HD and those genetically predisposed to the disease remain undiagnosed owing to social stigma and/or a lack of genetic testing [4].

As of today, there is no cure available for this disease; however, symptoms can be managed using a range of options, including chorea medication, antipsychotic medication, antidepressants, mood-stabilizing medication, as well as non-drug therapies [5]. Tominersen, a symptom-managing genetic treatment, may be considered broadly applicable within the HD population because it targets the underlying genetic mutation and can be administered repeatedly to manage symptoms over time. Tominersen is an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) that binds to the messenger RNA (mRNA) instructions that cells use to make the huntingtin (HTT) protein. Once bound, the ASO causes the message to be destroyed, leading to lower levels of the HTT protein overall [6]. In contrast, AMT-130 is a one-time gene therapy delivered through a single neurosurgical procedure. It uses a viral vector to carry microRNA that lowers the harmful HTT protein by silencing the gene that produces it [7]. Data from ongoing trials of these investigational drugs suggest that they may contribute to slowing the disease progression and potentially even disease reversal, particularly in the case of AMT-130 [6, 8–10].

Huntington’s disease has a significant financial impact on patients, caregivers, and the healthcare system [4]. However, a limited number of attempts have been made to quantify this impact. There have been hardly any studies to determine the cost of care for HD and to understand how costs may vary as the disease progresses through the early, middle, and late stages. Moreover, the emergence of these promising drugs, such as Tominersen and AMT-130, which have the potential to cure HD, further underscores the need for economic evaluation. While early clinical evidence suggests these therapies may slow disease progression, their relative cost-effectiveness remains unknown. To date, no study has compared the cost-effectiveness of these treatments relative with standard of care. This study aimed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of Tominersen and AMT-130 compared with SoC for the treatment of patients with HD in the US, using a modified societal perspective.

Methods

Treatment Strategies

The model compared three treatment strategies: standard of care (SoC), Tominersen, and AMT-130. SoC consisted of currently available symptomatic management, including pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic interventions aimed at managing motor, psychiatric, and cognitive symptoms. Tominersen was modeled as a symptom-managing genetic treatment with repeated administrations, while AMT-130 was modeled as a one-time gene therapy with sustained treatment effects. Differences in treatment costs, administration costs, and clinical effectiveness were incorporated into the model on the basis of available evidence and published literature.

Model Structure

A health-state transition Markov model with four health states was used to determine the cost-effectiveness of the potential genetic treatments compared with SoC for patients with early stage HD in the US. Patients progressed through early, middle, and late stages, with death as an absorbing state. The model was used to estimate the incremental costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) from each treatment option over a lifetime horizon. A modified societal perspective was adopted to capture direct healthcare costs and non-healthcare costs such as caregiver burden. The model consisted of three stages: early, middle, and late stage. These stages were based on disease markers using an algorithm that was developed and validated in the Divino et al. study [11]. The cycle length was 1 year with an annual discount rate of 3% used for all future costs and utilities. A half-cycle correction method was used for this model. The model was built and analyzed using TreeAge Pro Healthcare 2023 software. Data synthesis and mathematical calculations were carried out using Microsoft Excel.

The mean age of 50 years for patients with early stage HD was determined using the estimates reported in the Crowell et al. study [12]. Given that the median survival following HD diagnosis is estimated to be approximately 15–18 years [13], a total of 30 annual cycles were considered sufficient to capture lifetime costs and health outcomes associated with the disease. For the purpose of our study, we limited our key assumptions to patients with early stage HD, as the efficacy of these treatments is expected to be restricted to this group. Key assumptions were made using a best-case scenario approach based on clinical trial information and manufacturer data to estimate annual costs and disease progression probabilities for these treatments.

Model Parameters

Disease Progression Probabilities and Adverse Event Rates

All patients entered the model in the early stage, and at the end of each cycle, they could transition into a different health stage, stay in the early stage, or experience death (Fig. 1). Transition to a more severe HD stage could only happen one cycle at a time and only to the next severe HD stage (i.e., patients in the early stage could not skip the middle stage and move directly to late stage HD). Disease stage transition probabilities were derived from the study by Mohan et al. [14], which developed and validated a data-driven HD progression model using longitudinal clinical measures, including UHDRS-based outcomes. This study provides empirically estimated annual transition probabilities across clinically meaningful disease stages that can be mapped to early, middle-, and late-stage HD, consistent with the structure of our analysis. However, a complete cure cannot be guaranteed, and hence the probability of transition from the early to the middle stage was assumed to decrease from 0.16 to 0, accounting for the remaining possibility of remaining in the early stage. Owing to limited published data, adverse event rates were informed by interim clinical trial evidence and conservative assumptions. Interim results from the AMT-130 trial reported no adverse drug reactions or suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions; however, two of ten participants (20%) experienced serious perioperative adverse events unrelated to the investigational product. To conservatively reflect procedure-related risk, an adverse event probability of 0.20 was assumed for AMT-130 [15].

For Tominersen, adverse event rates were based on preliminary findings from the GENERATION HD-1 trial [6], which reported adverse events potentially related to the study drug or lumbar puncture. Given differences in dosing frequency and study design relative to the GENERATION HD-2 trial [6, 8–10], an adverse event probability of 0.40 was assumed for Tominersen. These parameters were explored in sensitivity analyses.

Costs

Costs associated with HD were identified from the Patil et al. study [16]. The current standard of care costs for HD were identified from existing literature. Costs identified were categorized as direct healthcare costs (inpatient, outpatient, and drug costs) and non-healthcare costs (caregiver costs), further stratified by early, middle, and late-stage HD [16]. All costs in the model were adjusted to represent them in 2024 US dollars using the US Consumer Price Index [17]. Willingness-to-pay threshold (WTP) of $500,000 per QALY gained was applied, given that HD is a rare disease [18–21] (Table 1).

Since Tominersen and AMT-130 are currently in clinical trials, we lacked established cost data and therefore relied on cost estimates from treatments for spinal muscular atrophy (SMA). The rationale behind using these SMA cost estimates was the similarity of the existing SMA treatment with these HD treatments. Nusinersen (SPINRAZA®) is a prescription medicine used to treat SMA in children and adult patients [22]. Both Tominersen and Nusinersen are antisense oligonucleotide drugs. They have the same mechanism of action. Both drugs aim to reduce the expression of disease-causing proteins by promoting the degradation of the targeted mRNA or altering its splicing pattern. Additionally, both drugs are administered through intrathecal injection into the cerebrospinal fluid [6, 8, 22].

Considering the similarities between Tominersen and Nusinersen, we assumed that the unit price of 60 mg of Tominersen would be equivalent to that of Nusinersen. A cost-effectiveness study found that the wholesale acquisition cost for a single intrathecal injection of 12 mg of Nusinersen was $125,000 [19, 23]. However, to account for typical markups observed for hospital outpatient treatments, we applied a 15% regular payer discount [23], to estimate the potential cost associated with administering Tominersen in a hospital setting. After accounting for the discount, the estimated cost of a single 60 mg injection of Tominersen was $106,250. According to the clinical trial protocol for Tominersen, each injection is administered every 4 months [10], leading to three injections annually for a patient with early stage HD. Based on this schedule, the estimated annual cost of Tominersen was $318,750. Moreover, we assumed that the cost of administering one dose of Tominersen would be the same as that of Nusinersen, which was determined to be $1,209 [23]. On the basis of this assumption, we estimated the annual cost of administering Tominersen to be $3,627.

Similar to AMT-130, Zolgensma® is a one-time-only dose gene therapy designed to target the genetic root cause of SMA by replacing the function of the missing or nonworking SMN1 gene [24]. AMT-130 and Zolgensma® both use viral vectors to deliver genetic material to the patient’s cells to treat a genetic disease. Considering these similarities, the cost for AMT-130 was assumed to be the same as the list price for Zolgensma®, which was determined to be $2,125,000 [19, 21]. The regular payer discount of 15% was then applied to the list price, for a one-time dose of AMT-130, which was estimated to be $1,806,250. The estimated national average cost to perform the complex stereotactic neurosurgery required to administer a single intrastriatal injection of AMT-130 was found to be $23,516 per patient [25]. Drug and administration costs were incorporated into the model on a per-cycle basis. For Tominersen, the unit drug cost and administration cost were applied per injection, with three injections per year. For AMT-130, the one-time drug and administration costs were applied in the first cycle only. Adverse events costs were estimated using data from existing literature [26–29] (Table 2).

Effectiveness

Effectiveness in the model was measured as health utilities in the form of QALYs with 0 indicating death and 1 indicating full health. Health utilities for SoC were derived from the Hawton et al. study [30] that used SF-6D preference-based measures to estimate stage-specific utilities for HD. Utilities for Tominersen and AMT-130 were assumed equal to SoC because trial-based utility data are not yet available. Scenario analysis was carried out to assess the effect of an additional 0.07 value of hope [31] to the base-case health-state utility.

Sensitivity Analysis

A scenario analysis was performed to examine the effect of incorporating an additional value of hope [32] to the base-case utility values for both treatments, reflecting preference for therapies offering a greater probability of long-term survival. The concept of value of hope in health economics literature refers to the notion that in specific circumstances, a rational individual may deviate from their typical risk-averse behavior and undertake calculated risks that have the potential to yield favorable outcomes, such as a cure [33].

Additionally, deterministic sensitivity analysis and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) were performed on relevant model parameters to capture the overall uncertainty in the model. For the PSA, beta distributions were used for health-state utilities, while gamma distributions were applied to cost parameters. A total of 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations were conducted to generate the joint uncertainty around costs, QALYs, and ICERs. This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) 2022 guidelines [34] (Appendix Table 1).

Results

Base-Case Results

For the patients with early stage HD, the total cost associated with Tominersen was approximately $2.79 million, which was nearly four times greater than that of the SoC, which had a total cost of approximately $685,662. However, Tominersen was found to have higher QALYs (7.03 QALYs) compared with the SoC (6.11 QALYs). As a result, the ICER for Tominersen over the SoC was approximately $2.28 million per QALY gained, exceeding the WTP threshold of $500,000 per QALY gained (Table 3). Scenario Analysis

We performed a scenario analysis to test an additional 0.07 value of hope to the early stage health-state utility for Tominersen and AMT-130 treatment arms. As the effectiveness of the two treatment arms was the only parameter that was altered, there were no variations in the incremental cost estimates.

After considering the additional value of hope to the base-case utility value, the total QALYs for Tominersen increased from 7.03 to 7.49, and for AMT-130, it increased from 11.34 to 12.43. The incremental QALYs increased by 0.45 for Tominersen and by 1.09 for AMT-130. As a result, the ICER value decreased significantly from $2.28 million per QALY gained to $1.52 million per QALY gained for Tominersen, and for AMT-130, it changed from $285,703 per QALY gained to $236,540 per QALY gained. Sensitivity Analyses

Deterministic Sensitivity Analysis

One-way sensitivity analyses indicated that the ICERs were most sensitive to the cost of the drugs, particularly AMT-130 and Tominersen, as well as health-state utility values for early and middle-stage HD. Costs associated with adverse events had a moderate impact, whereas variations in caregiver, indirect, and adaptation costs had comparatively smaller effects on the ICERs. Overall, drug costs and early and middle-stage utility values were the primary drivers of model results. The list of parameters and their impact on ICER are listed in Appendix Table 1. The results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in tornado diagrams in the Appendix (Figs. 1 and 2).

Probabilistic Sensitivity Analysis

The cost-effectiveness acceptability curve revealed that the probability of AMT-130 being considered cost-effective at a WTP threshold of $250,000 per QALY gained was 30%, and at a WTP threshold of $350,000 per QALY gained, it was 80%. Discussion

The results of this study provide valuable insights into the incremental cost-effectiveness of Tominersen and AMT-130 compared with the current SoC for patients with early stage HD. The results indicated that Tominersen, when compared with the SoC, was associated with markedly higher costs but offered greater health benefits than the current SoC for HD. However, since the ICER exceeded the WTP threshold, the current SoC would be considered preferable over Tominersen for patients with early stage HD.

In contrast, the results of the analysis showed that AMT-130 was not only associated with higher costs but also provided greater health benefits compared with Tominersen and the SoC. The ICER for AMT-130 compared with the SoC was within the range of what is considered cost-effective at a WTP threshold of $500,000 per QALY gained, indicating that AMT-130 treatment may be a better option compared with the current SoC for patients with early stage HD.

Moreover, Tominersen, when compared with AMT-130, was found to be dominated, meaning that it was more expensive and less effective than AMT-130. These findings suggest that AMT-130 may be a cost-effective option compared with the current SoC and Tominersen; however, further research is needed to determine its relative value compared with other treatment options for HD.

Sensitivity analyses showed that our conclusions were not sensitive to changes in most of the input parameters, except for the cost and early stage utility values of AMT-130 and Tominersen. Varying the cost of these treatments resulted in significant changes in the ICER, suggesting that the cost and early stage utility values of these treatments are the key drivers of the model’s results. The ICER for AMT-130 remained below the WTP threshold of $500,000 per QALY gained, whereas for Tominersen, the ICER exceeded the WTP threshold across all tested scenarios. Moreover, in the scenario analysis, we found that the effect of additional value of hope had a significant impact on the ICERs for both the treatments compared with SoC. These findings highlight the potential impact of including nonclinical benefits in the analysis.

It is important to interpret the reported QALY differences with caution. QALYs are derived from utility values that may not fully reflect variations in all aspects of health-related quality of life in HD. Consequently, the absolute differences in incremental QALYs observed between treatment arms are relatively modest. While small differences can have meaningful implications in a chronic, progressive condition, these results should be considered in the context of the limitations inherent in utility measurement and the assumptions applied in this model. Additionally, it should be noted that the Tominersen trial recently updated its protocol to continue only the 100-mg dose [40]. Our analysis used the 60-mg dose reported in the published trial data [10], and future studies may consider evaluating higher doses as additional evidence becomes available.

While our model incorporated a value of hope adjustment to account for potential additional benefit from transformative therapies, it does not explicitly capture heterogeneity in patient preferences regarding treatment risk or the relative importance of quality-of-life improvements. Patients may value certain outcomes differently or tolerate risks differently, which could influence real-world decisions. Future research could consider preference-weighted methods or discrete choice experiments to explore this heterogeneity and its impact on cost-effectiveness estimates.

A higher WTP threshold of $500,000 per QALY gained was used in this analysis, given that HD is an ultrarare condition. The Institute for Clinical and Economic Review has previously reported that treatments for rare diseases, such as Spinraza® and Zolgensma® for spinal muscular atrophy, often exceed conventional US cost-effectiveness thresholds of $100,000–150,000 per QALY gained [19]. It has been observed that payers may consider broader impacts and adopt higher thresholds when assessing the value of treatments for ultrarare diseases [20]. In recognition of these factors, cost-effectiveness analyses conducted by Institute for Clinical and Economic Review often include WTP thresholds of up to $500,000 per QALY gained for rare diseases [19–21]. Following this precedent, we applied a WTP threshold of $500,000 per QALY gained in our study. Findings from the PSA also indicated that the probability of both of the treatments being considered cost-effective was 0% at the conventional WTP threshold of $100,000–150,000 per QALY gained.

Additionally, the model was subject to extensive sensitivity and scenario analyses to test the robustness of the results and identify the key drivers of model outcomes. This approach provides a rigorous and transparent framework for evaluating the potential value of emerging therapies in a complex disease such as HD. Moreover, this study adds to the growing body of literature on the economic evaluation of HD treatments, and its findings may inform future decision-making regarding the allocation of healthcare resources for patients with HD.

Strengths and Limitations

The model relied on many assumptions and simplifications owing to limited clinical trial evidence and the lack of established cost data for Tominersen and AMT-130. While the assumptions were based on expert opinion and published literature, the results may not fully represent the real-world situation. As a result, model outcomes could be subject to uncertainty if the assumptions do not accurately reflect actual clinical practice. This study was based on the clinical and economic evidence available at the time of analysis, and emerging data may further refine estimates of effectiveness, safety, and cost-effectiveness in future research.

Moreover, there is limited evidence on the durability of the treatment effect for AMT-130 and Tominersen. The model relied heavily on the assumption of sustained benefit for both treatments beyond the data. It was assumed that patients responding to treatment would maintain the highest functional status observed in the early stage for the remainder of the model time horizon until their death. Furthermore, the short-term comparative clinical efficacy of AMT-130 and Tominersen with the SoC is uncertain. The statistical generalizability or the comparative efficacy of AMT-130 and Tominersen with the SoC cannot be assessed. These limitations highlight the need for further research and evaluation of the long-term clinical and economic outcomes of AMT-130 and Tominersen for the treatment of early stage HD.

One of the key strengths of this study is its use of a comprehensive economic model to assess the cost-effectiveness of Tominersen and AMT-130 compared with the current SoC for patients with early stage HD. To our knowledge, this is the first cost-effectiveness analysis study comparing AMT-130 and Tominersen with the current SoC for early stage HD. The model incorporated a range of inputs, including clinical trial data, published literature, and expert opinion, to simulate disease progression and estimate the long-term costs and health outcomes associated with each treatment.

Conclusions

From a modified US societal perspective, this analysis suggests that AMT-130 may be cost-effective relative to standard of care, whereas Tominersen exceeds the WTP threshold of $500,000 per QALY gained. In light of our findings, we recommend that future research should focus on the long-term clinical effectiveness and durability of these treatments, compare their value with other HD interventions, and explore the cost-effectiveness of additional strategies to improve patient outcomes.
