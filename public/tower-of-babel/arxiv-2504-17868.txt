Color Fault-Tolerant Distance Preservers: Õptimal Size in Conditionally Õptimal Time
Merav Parter, Asaf Petruschka

Abstract
We revisit the problem of fault-tolerant (FT) distance preservers , when failure events in the network admit a form of correlation modeled as color faults . FT distance preservers are sparse subgraphs that preserve distances between specified pairs of vertices, even after some edge or vertex failures occur. In the classical fault model, any set of at most k k edges or vertices might fail (where k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 is a given parameter). Despite extensive research, the classical model admits significant and tantalizing gaps, both in terms of sparsity bounds and of algorithmic efficiency. In this work, we study the problem in the recently introduced color fault-tolerant (CFT) model: the given graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) has arbitrary colors on its edges/vertices where each color appears at most k k times, and is susceptible to color faults , where the failure of color c c causes all the c c -colored elements to crash. Our main contribution is in the multi-source setting, where G G has a source-set S ⊆ V S\subseteq V , and the CFT preserver should preserve S × V S\times V distances under any single color fault. We show the following results (where n = | V | n=|V| , m = | E | m=|E| ): • There exists a CFT distance preserver H H of G G with O ~ ​ ( n 2 − 1 k + 1 ⋅ | S | 1 k + 1 ) \tilde{O}(n^{2-\frac{1}{k+1}}\cdot|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}}) edges. 1 1 1 The O ~ ​ ( ⋅ ) \tilde{O}(\cdot) notation suppresses poly-logarithmic factors in n n . • The above sparsity bound is worst-case optimal up to polylogarithmic terms. • There is a combinatorial randomized algorithm that produces a preserver H H whose size meets the above optimal sparsity bound, with running time of O ~ ​ ( m ⋅ n 1 − 1 k + 1 ⋅ | S | 1 k + 1 ) \tilde{O}(m\cdot n^{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}\cdot|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}}) . • The above running time is conditionally optimal: a polynomial improvement would refute the combinatorial Boolean Matrix Multiplication (BMM) conjecture. Furthermore, the running time remains optimal even if we only require mild sparsification to m 1 − ϵ m^{1-\epsilon} edges. Our (conditionally) tight algorithm relies on a new approach for compressing fault-tolerant distance information in the presence of nearby faults, using the tool of sparse neighborhood covers. We believe that this technique may have further applications in the study of fault-tolerant graph sparsification.

3.1
Framework and Overview

To build a valid CFT

Observation 3.1

(Last edge observation)

Suppose

.
Then

is a CFT

Proof.

We show that if

contains an

.
The base case

When

of a shortest

As
\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(s,t^{\prime})=d-1

Concatenating

∎

Thus, the algorithm’s goal is to include
\mathtt{LastE}(s,t\mid c)
in

(s,t,c)\in S\times V\times C
.

We define decreasing

d_{i}:=(n/|S|)^{1-\frac{i}{k+1}}
for

.
Additionally, we define two “trivial thresholds”

We use the following terminology:
Two vertices

The factor

3.4

Next, for each nontrivial threshold

with the following property:

(P)

For every

This is achieved by taking

We call the vertices in

.
Note that

.
We also define the

.

Let

Recall that

Let

The main tool we use to exploit the nice distances structure of

3.4

Intuitively, it allows us to convert a level-

Instead of treating level-

While this approach requires us to consider a larger number of sources (

First, for a given

Second, we will also only care about colors that appear at most

Furthermore, all these appearances of color

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(s,t,c)\in S\times V\times C

.
Then,

(1)

(2)

-near to

(3)

If

.

.

Proof.

If

found on the

-suffix of

, which exists by property

.
Then (1) clearly holds.
For (2), observe that

-near to

.
Since

is in level

-near to

(i+1)
-near to

.
Item (3) follows immediately from (2) by triangle inequality.
Finally, (4) follows from (2): indeed, because

is in level

, there are

that are

-far from

.
As the

are
(i+1)
-near to

, they can be at most

.
∎

We are now ready to give an overview of the algorithm.
First, we explain how the last level is easily dealt with, and afterwards we only focus on the other levels.
As a first step, we give a simple algorithm that achieves near-optimal size for the output, but has suboptimal running time.
Then, we explain the high-level ideas for improving the running time to near-optimal.

Handling the Last Level.

Item 4 in

3.4

We simply compute the SSSP (single-source shortest path) trees rooted at each

This adds

To see the correctness, consider some level-

(s,t,c)
.
By

, there is some

on the

-suffix of
\pi(s,t\mid c)
(item 1), and

contains no

Hence, the

-length suffix of

path in the tree rooted at

.

Achieving Near-Optimal Size.

Let us fix some non-last level

.
As in the last level, we still compute SSSP trees from the

For every

-source

, we execute SSSP from

in

, but trim it at distance

;
The resulting rooted tree

inside the ball

We add all trees

to the output

.
Again, this adds
O(n\cdot|A_{i}\cup S|)
edges and takes
O(m\cdot|A_{i}\cup S|)
time, which is within budget.

Now, fix an
i
-source
z\in A_{i}\cup S
.
By
Lemma

(1), we only care about adding
\mathtt{LastE}(z,t\mid c)
for triplets
(z,t,c)
where

, i.e.,

.
In this case,
\pi(z,t)
, which is the

-path from the root

to

, is already in

.
So, if
\pi(z,t)
avoids the color
c
, then
(z,t,c)
is already taken care of.
We therefore assume that
t
has some ancestor
x
of color
c
in
T(z)
.
But by
Lemma

3.4
(2) we have
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(x,t)\leq 2d_{i+1}
, meaning

is in fact within the
2d_{i+1}

nearest

.

In other words, to treat the

-source

, it’s enough to add
\mathtt{LastE}(z,t\mid c)
whenever the target

sees the color

among its

nearest ancestors in

.
Thus, each target

only causes the insertion of up to
2d_{i+1}
edges when processing a specific

|A_{i}\cup S|\cdot 2d_{i+1}
edges overall (in level

).
Summing over

, the total number of added edges in level

is at most
n\cdot|A_{i}\cup S|\cdot 2d_{i+1}=\tilde{O}(n^{2}\cdot\frac{d_{i+1}}{d_{i}})=\tilde{O}(n^{2-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})
, as required.

By the discussion above, we get the following simple algorithm for level
i
:
For each

-source
z\in A_{i}\cup S
and color

, run SSSP from

in

, but add to

only the last edges of the shortest paths

such that

has the color

among its

nearest ancestors in

.
This simple algorithm is already enough to ensure the output

is a CFT

However, the running time is
O(m\cdot|A_{i}\cup S|\cdot n)
, which in level

becomes
\tilde{O}(mn^{2-\frac{2}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{2}{k+1}})
.
This is larger than our desired running time by a factor of
n^{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}}
.

Achieving Near-Optimal Time.

The main challenge lies in improving the running time to near-optimal.
Roughly, the idea is to harness the same argument we used for bounding the
size
of the output
H
, to also effectively bound the
running time
: if we could restrict each edge to participate in only
\tilde{O}(d_{i+1})
SSSP computations from a specific
i
-source
z
, we would get total running time of
\tilde{O}(m\cdot|A_{i}\cup S|\cdot d_{i+1})=\tilde{O}(mn^{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})
which is precisely our budget.
Intuitively, to achieve this, we would like the SSSP computation from
z
in
G-c
to only use edges incident on vertices
t
such that the color
c
appears on their nearest
\tilde{O}(d_{i+1})
ancestors; these “allowed” edges are the only ones we might actually need to add into
H
as a result of this computation.
Clearly, this intuition doesn’t work as is: e.g., it could be that all edges incident on
z
are not allowed to be used during the SSSP computation from
z
, which is absurd.

To make it work, instead of executing the SSSP directly on
G-c
, we construct a
shortcutted graph

\hat{G}(z,c)
that has the allowed original edges, plus extra weighted
shortcut edges
which represent safe paths avoiding the color
c
.

We put two types of shortcuts in
\hat{G}(z,c)
.
The simpler ones represent paths in
T(z)
that are unaffected by the failure of color
c
.
Roughly speaking, these simple shortcuts let the SSSP computation “get inside” the subtrees of the
c
-colored vertices.
However, the allowed edges are found only in the
shallow
layers of these subtrees (the first
\tilde{O}(d_{i+1})
layers), while shortest paths from
z
avoiding
c
may need to travel into
deep
layers, even if they end in the shallow ones.

The second, more complicated type of shortcuts is designed
to represent such traversals in the deep layers.
These use the fact that the
(i+1)
-landmarks
A_{i+1}
must appear on the path at least once every
d_{i+1}
steps.
The idea is to create a
shell
of additional
\tilde{O}(d_{i+1})
layers beneath the original shallow layers, which we now call the
kernel
layers.
We also allow the edges touching the shell to be used, which is within budget.
Now, to go from between the kernel layers and the deep layers beneath the shell, one must walk through the shell for at least
d_{i+1}
steps, and hit some
(i+1)
-landmark there.
Thus, we can add shortcuts to the
(i+1)
-landmarks in the shell, which represent paths that entirely avoid the kernel (and hence the color
c
); these take care of representing the traversal into deep layers.

As it turns out, the shortcut edges are few enough so that they do not hinder the overall running time of the SSSP computations in the shortcutted graphs
\hat{G}(z,c)
.
However, the second-type shortcuts present a problem: how do we compute their weights in the first place?
We need those weights just to
construct

\hat{G}(z,c)
.
A priori, this requires computing distance in
G
minus the kernel layers, which
depend on the color

c
, and is therefore too costly.
We overcome this obstacle by using (a variant of) the well-known tool of
sparse neighborhood covers
.
Essentially, each cluster in the neighborhood cover will be used to define the kernel layers and shell layers for many different colors
c
, and thus we can compute the shortcuts for avoiding the kernels of the different clusters (instead of different colors), which can be done efficiently.

3.2
Algorithm Description for Level
i\leq k-1

From now on, we focus on the algorithm for level
i
, which ensures that
\mathtt{LastE}(s,t\mid c)
is included in
H
for every level-
i
triplet
(s,t,c)
.
In its analysis, we will show that it takes
\tilde{O}(mn^{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})
time while adding
\tilde{O}(n^{2-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})
edges to
H
, so summing over all
k=O(\log n)
levels yields the required running time and size bounds of the entire algorithm.

Step 1: Shortest-Path Trees from
i
-Sources.

For each
i
-source
z\in A_{i}\cup S
, we execute SSSP in
G
from the root
z
.
Let
T(z)
be the resulting shortest-path tree, but trimmed at depth
d_{i}
.
We add all trees
T(z)
to the output

.
This adds
O(n\cdot|A_{i}\cup S|)
edges and takes
O(m\cdot|A_{i}\cup S|)
time, which is easily verified to be within our budget.
Note that this step also yields the distance
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(z,v)
between any
i
-source
z\in A_{i}\cup S
and any vertex
v
.
We will later use these distances as weights for shortcuts in the shortcutted graphs, as explained in the overview.

Step 2: Neighborhood Cover.

An
r
-neighborhood cover
is a collection of vertex-subsets called
clusters
, such that every vertex has its
r
-radius ball
contained in
some cluster.
The quality of a neighborhood cover is measured by two parameters that one wishes to minimize:
The
diameter
of the clusters, and their
overlap
which is the maximum number of clusters having some vertex common to all of them.
For our purposes, we need a variant of neighborhood cover, where each cluster consists of an inner kernel and shell, and the kernels themselves already cover all the
r
-neighborhoods.
Formally, we use the following result:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer.
There is an
\tilde{O}(m)
time algorithm that computes a collection of
kernels

K_{1},\dots,K_{\ell}\subseteq V

with corresponding
clusters

X_{j}:=B_{G}(K_{j},r)
and
shells

Y_{j}:=X_{j}-K_{j}
, such that:

•

(Covering)
Each
v\in V
has a
covering kernel

K_{j}
with
B_{G}(v,r)\subseteq K_{j}
.

•

(Diameter)
Each cluster has
weak diameter
4
4

4

A vertex set
W\subseteq V
is said to have weak diameter
d
if
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(w,w^{\prime})\leq d
for every
w,w^{\prime}\in W
.

O(r\log n)
.

•

(Overlap)
Each
v\in V
belongs to
O(\log n)
clusters.

Lemma

3.5
follows as a corollary from the classical near-linear time algorithm for neighborhood covers (without kernels) of
[
ABCP98
]
. We provide the proof in
Appendix

C
.

For level
i
, we apply
Lemma

3.5
with

, where this choice of radius stems from item 3 of
Lemma

3.4
.
From now on,
K_{1},\dots,K_{\ell}
,
X_{1},\dots,X_{\ell}
and
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{\ell}
denote the kernels, clusters and shells resulting from this application.

As explained in the overview, the shortcutted graphs will have weighted shortcut edges based on the neighborhood cover.
These shortcuts will correspond to distances from the following set:

\big\{\operatorname{dist}_{G-K_{j}}(a,v)\mid 1\leq j\leq\ell,~a\in A_{i+1}\cap Y_{j},~v\in V-K_{j}\big\}.

Namely, for each
(i+1)
-landmark
a\in A_{i+1}
and each

such that

belongs to the shell
Y_{j}
, we need to compute SSSP in

, i.e., avoiding the kernel

.
By the overlap guarantee in
Lemma

3.5
, each
(i+1)
-landmark appears in

shells, and hence triggers only
O(\log n)
SSSP calls.
Thus, the total time spent for computing these distances is
\tilde{O}(m\cdot|A_{i+1}|)\leq\tilde{O}(m\cdot|A_{k}|)=\tilde{O}(m\cdot n^{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}\cdot|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})
, which is within budget.

Step 3: Finding Relevant Colors.

We also use the neighborhood cover to define the notion of

:

Definition 3.6

(Relevant Color for
i
-Source)
.

Color

-colored vertex

(i.e., the kernel with

) contains
all

-colored vertices in

.
In this case, we choose one such arbitrary

, and denote the kernel, cluster and shell for the pair

by
K(z,c):=K_{j}
,

and

, respectively.

The reasoning behind the name “relevant” stems from

3.4
(3):
it says that an

such that the

To check if some color

Thus, computing all pairs of

Step 4: Constructing Shortcutted Graphs.

Fix an
i
-source
z\in A_{i}\cup S
and relevant color

.
We now give the construction of the corresponding shortcutted graph

.
We first introduce some notations and terminologies.
To avoid clutter, in the following we may omit

from the notations when they are clear from context.

•

The ball
refers to

, and

from

in

, which was computed in Step 1.

•

A vertex

.

•

The
kernel
,
cluster
, and

refer to
K:=K(z,c)
,
X:=X(z,c)
, and
Y:=Y(z,c)
from
Definition

, respectively.
Recall that the cluster is the
4d_{i+1}
-radius ball around the kernel, i.e.,
X=B_{G}(K,4d_{i+1})
, and the shell is the part of the cluster outside the kernel, i.e.,

.

•

The
affected kernel
,
affected cluster
and

are the intersections of the kernel, cluster and shell with the affected set

,
denoted

,

and

•

The

are all those

.

•

The

are all those vertices in

that have some neighbor from the affected cluster, but are not affected themselves. That is,
O=N_{G}(X_{D})\cap(B-D)
.

We define the shortcut graph for
z
and
c
as follows (see verbal description following the formal definition below).

The Shortcutted Graph
\hat{G}(z,c)

For
z\in A_{i}\cup S
and relevant color
c
, the shortcutted graph
\hat{G}=\hat{G}(z,c)
is defined by

\displaystyle V(\hat{G})

\displaystyle:=B,

\displaystyle E(\hat{G})

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{Original-Edges}(\hat{G})\cup\operatorname{Landmark-Shortcuts}(\hat{G})\cup\operatorname{Root-Shortcuts}(\hat{G}),

where the edges and their weights are defined by

\displaystyle\operatorname{Original-Edges}(\hat{G})

\displaystyle:=\{\text{$(u,v)$ of weight $1$}\mid(u,v)\in E,~u\in B,~v\in X_{D}\}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Landmark-Shortcuts}(\hat{G})

\displaystyle:=\{\text{$(a,v)$ of weight $\operatorname{dist}_{G-K}(a,v)$}\mid a\in L,~v\in B-K\}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Root-Shortcuts}(\hat{G})

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\text{if $L\neq\emptyset$:}&\{(z,v)\text{ of weight }\operatorname{dist}_{G}(z,v)\mid v\in O\text{ or }v\in B-(K\cup D)\}\\
\text{if $L=\emptyset$:}&\{(z,v)\text{ of weight }\operatorname{dist}_{G}(z,v)\mid v\in O\}\end{cases}

The vertex set of
\hat{G}
is just the ball
B
.
We include in
\hat{G}
every
original edge
from
G
that has some endpoint in the affected cluster
X_{D}
(and the other endpoint can be anywhere in
B
).
These original edges are given weight
1
.
Then, we add two types of
shortcut edges
to
\hat{G}
.
One type is the
landmark shortcuts
:
From each designated landmark
a\in L
, we add edges to every vertex
v
outside the kernel
K
, with weight
\operatorname{dist}_{G-K}(a,v)
.
The other type is the
root shortcuts
:
First, we add edges from the root
z
to every vertex
v
in the outer layer
O
, with weight
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(z,v)
.
Additionally, in the “non-degenerate” case where
L\neq\emptyset
, i.e., there is
some
designated landmark, we add such root shortcuts from
z
to every
v\in B-(K\cup D)
, i.e.,
v
is a non-affected vertex outside the kernel.
(The reason behind this slight complication is rather technical. Essentially, if
L=\emptyset
, then the latter kind of root shortcuts are not required for us, but including them might damage the running time.)

We defer the analysis of the time it takes to construct the shortcut graphs, as it is more convenient to analyze together with the time to execute the next Step 5.

Step 5: SSSPs in Shortcutted Graphs.

For every pair of
i
-source
z\in A_{i}\cup S
and relevant color
c
, we consider the corresponding shortcutted graph
\hat{G}=\hat{G}(z,c)
.
We execute (weighted) SSSP from

in
\hat{G}-c
.
Then, for each target vertex

in the affected kernel

, we add the last edge of the
z\rightsquigarrow t
shortest path computed by this SSSP into the output

Note that this edge must be original, since shortcuts do not touch the affected kernel.

This concludes the description of the algorithm for level

.
Next, we provide an analysis of the output size and running time (for steps 4 and 5).
Afterward, we provide the correctness proof for the algorithm.

3.3
Size and Running Time Analysis

The analysis of the output size and the algorithm’s running time hinges on the following:

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
z\in A_{i}\cup S
and color
c
relevant for
z
.
Then, every vertex in the affected cluster
X_{D}(z,c)
must have a
c
-colored vertex among its
O(d_{i+1}\log n)
nearest ancestors in
T(z)
.

Proof.

Let
v\in X_{D}(z,c)
.
Then, because
v
is affected, it has some
c
-colored ancestor
x
in
T(z)
.
Since
c
is a relevant color for
z
, the corresponding cluster
X(z,c)
(in fact, even its kernel
K(z,c)
) contains every
c
-colored vertex in
T(z)
, and particularly
x
.
As clusters have weak diameter
O(d_{i+1}\log n)
, the distance between
x
and
v
in
G
, and hence also in the shortest-path tree
T(z)
, is
O(d_{i+1}\log n)
.
∎

First, we analyze the number of edges added to the output
H
during level
i
.
Each subroutine for pair
z,c
adds as many edges as the size of the affected kernel.
So, by
Lemma

3.7
, the total number of added edges during level
i
is

|V|\cdot|A_{i}\cup S|\cdot O(d_{i+1}\log n)=\tilde{O}(n^{2}\cdot\frac{d_{i+1}}{d_{i}})=\tilde{O}(n^{2-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})
,
which is within our budget.

We now focus on the running time.
To start, let us consider the time required to
construct
the shortcutted graphs.
The primary task here is to identify the vertices in the affected kernel/cluster/shell.
By
Lemma

3.7
, this can be done by scanning the subtrees rooted in the
c
-colored vertices in
T(z)
, but only up to depth
O(d_{i+1}\log n)
from the root of the subtree, and checking if the scanned vertices belong to the corresponding kernel/cluster/shell.
Thus, fixing
z
and letting
c
vary, each edge in
T(z)
is scanned only
O(d_{i+1}\log n)
times.
Now summing over
z
, we bound the running time for identifying all affected clusters by
m\cdot|A_{i}\cup S|\cdot O(d_{i+1}\log n)=\tilde{O}(mn\frac{d_{i+1}}{d_{i}})=\tilde{O}(mn^{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})
, which is within budget.
Once the affected kernel/cluster/shell (and the designated landmarks inside the affected shell) are identified, the rest of the time to construct the shortcutted graph
\hat{G}(z,c)
is linear in the number of edges (original or shortcuts) that
\hat{G}(z,c)
has.
The SSSP we execute in this
\hat{G}(z,c)
is also near-linear in the number of edges; hence, it remains to account for the time required to run the SSSPs in the shortcutted graphs.

Observe that the number of edges in
\hat{G}(z,c)
is dominated by its original edges and its landmark shortcuts.
Indeed, a root shortcut of the form
(z,v)
with
v\in O
can be charged to some original edge
(v,u)
with
u\in X_{D}
.
Also, a root shortcut of the form
(z,v)
with
v\in B-(K\cup D)
can be charged to a landmark shortcut
(a,v)
with
a\in L
.
Now, recall that original edges and landmark shortcuts have at least one endpoint in the affected cluster.
Thus, by
Lemma

3.7
, each original edge
e\in E
can appear in at most
|A_{i}\cup S|\cdot O(d_{i+1}\log n)=\tilde{O}(n\frac{d_{i+1}}{d_{i}})=\tilde{O}(n^{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})
shortcutted graphs.
Similarly, each pair in
A_{i+1}\times V
can appear as a landmark shortcut (with varying weight) in
\tilde{O}(n^{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})
shortcutted graphs.
So, summing over all pairs
z,c
, the total time for level
i
(other than the preprocessing, which we already accounted for) is

(m+|A_{i+1}|\cdot n)\cdot\tilde{O}(n^{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})

Note that

|A_{i+1}|\cdot n\leq|A_{k}|\cdot n=\tilde{O}(n^{2-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})\leq\tilde{O}(m)
,
so the total running time of level
i
is

\tilde{O}(mn^{1-\frac{1}{k+1}}|S|^{\frac{1}{k+1}})

as needed.

3.4
Correctness

We now prove the correctness of the algorithm in level
i
, meaning that it includes in the output
H
all of the required last edges in this level.
Let
(s,t,c)\in S\times V\times C
be a level-
i
triplet; our goal is to show that
\mathtt{LastE}(s,t\mid c)\in H
.
To this end, let
z\in A_{i}\cup S
be the
i
-source from
Lemma

3.4
for the triplet
(s,t,c)
.
By
Lemma

3.4
(1), we have
\mathtt{LastE}(s,t\mid c)=\mathtt{LastE}(z,t\mid c)
, and we will show that the latter has been added to

during level

.

We first handle a trivial case, when

doesn’t have the color

; then
\mathtt{LastE}(z,t\mid c)
is just the last edge of
\pi(z,t)
.
Note that
\pi(z,t)
has length at most

by

(1), and hence it is found in the shortest-path tree

of the ball

, which we’ve added to

.
Thus, we may assume

has some
c
-colored ancestor

in the tree

.
By

(3) we have
V_{c}\cap B\subseteq B_{G}(x,4d_{i+1})
, hence the covering kernel of

.
Therefore, color

is relevant for the

-source

.
We thus focus on the subroutine executed for the pair

(and again, omit them from notations to avoid clutter).

Consider

,

, and

, which are, respectively, the kernel, cluster, and shell we have chosen for

4.2
Size Lower Bound

We now prove the tight lower bound on the worst-case size of CFT multi-source distance preservers.

Theorem 4.2
.

For any integers

,

We construct the lower bound instance as follows:

•

\sigma

•

Define

as the
\sigma

of

(respectively).

•

•

Proof (of claim).

If there is a

So,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{G^{\prime}-c_{i}}(s_{\ell},k)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}_{T_{\ell}-c_{i}}(s_{\ell},i^{\prime})+\operatorname{dist}_{G^{\prime}-c_{i}}(i^{\prime},k)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{dist}_{T_{\ell}-c_{i}}(s_{\ell},i)+\operatorname{dist}_{G^{\prime}-c_{i}}(i^{\prime},k)

as

,

(4)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{dist}_{T_{\ell}-c_{i}}(s_{\ell},i)+2

(5)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{dist}_{G^{\prime}-c_{i}}(s_{\ell},i)+2

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{dist}_{G^{\prime}-c_{i}}(s_{\ell},k)

By the equality in

Now, by the equality in

∎

We now analyze the algorithm’s running time.
Denote the size and running time guarantees of the assumed sparsification algorithm by

s=m^{1-\epsilon}=n^{1+a-\epsilon+o(1)},\quad t=n^{2+a-\frac{1-b}{k+1}-\epsilon}=n^{2+a-\epsilon+o(1)}/r.

Consider the time spent on block
I^{\prime}
:

•

Constructing
G^{\prime}
from
G
amounts to constructing the trees
T_{1},\dots,T_{\sigma}
.
Each such tree is constructed in linear time in its size, and the total size of the trees is
O(n)
.

•

Applying the sparsification to get the preserver
H^{\prime}
takes up to
O(t)
time.

•

Then, for each
i\in I^{\prime}
, we run one BFS procedure in (a subgraph of)
H^{\prime}
and check
n
inequalities involving the output distances, which takes
O(s+n)
time.

Thus, the total running time of our BMM algorithm is

\underbrace{r}_{\text{\#blocks}}\cdot\left(\underbrace{O(n)}_{\text{construct $G^{\prime}$}}+\underbrace{O(t)}_{\text{construct $H^{\prime}$}}+\underbrace{(n/r)}_{|I^{\prime}|}\cdot\underbrace{O(s+n)}_{\text{BFS in $H^{\prime}$}}\right)=O(rt+ns+n^{2})=O(n^{2+a-\epsilon+o(1)}+n^{2}).

Since the sparsification algorithm for constructing preservers is combinatorial,
our resulting BMM algorithm is also combinatorial, and its running time (which is faster than
\Omega((mn)^{1-o(1)}+n^{2})
) contradicts
Conjecture

4.3
.
∎

.

Appendix A
Other Types of CFT Preservers

A.1
Applications of Multi-Source: CFT Pairwise Preservers and
+2
Spanners

The first application is for
pairwise
CFT distance preservers with arbitrary demand pairs:

Theorem A.1
.

For any
n
-vertex colored graph
G=(V,E)
with color classes of size at most
k
, and any
P\subseteq V\times V
, there exists a CFT
P
-distance preserver
H
with

\tilde{O}(n^{2-\frac{2}{2k+3}}\cdot|P|^{\frac{1}{2k+3}})

edges.

Proof.

Let
L
be a parameter to be set later;
a path is
long
if its length is
\geq L
, and
short
otherwise.
The strategy is to separately take care of triplets
(s,t,c)
where
(s,t)\in P
and
c
is some color, depending on whether
\pi(s,t\mid c)
is long (“long triplet”) or short (“short triplet”).

•

Long triplets:

These are handled using CFT multi-source preservers.
Let
S\subseteq V
be a hitting set for all
\pi(s,t\mid c)
with
(s,t,c)
a long triplet.
By standard hitting set arguments,
|S|=O(\frac{n}{L}\cdot\log n)
.
We add to
H
the CFT
S\times V
distance preservers of
Theorem

1.2
, which has
\tilde{O}(n^{2}\cdot L^{-\frac{1}{k+1}})
edges.
Now, if
(s,t,c)
is a long triplet, there is some
x\in S\cap\pi(s,t\mid c)
, so

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{H-c}(s,t)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{dist}_{H-c}(s,x)+\operatorname{dist}_{H-c}(x,t)

by triangle inequality,

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(s,x)+\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(x,t)

as
H
is a CFT
(S\times V)\cup(V\times S)
dist. pres.,

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(s,t)

\displaystyle\text{since $x\in\pi(s,t\mid c)$}.

•

Short triplets:

These are handled using “hit-miss sampling”.
We work in
r=40L\ln n
iterations.
In iteration
i
, we sample each color independently with probability
\frac{1}{L}
into a color set
F_{i}
, and add to
H
every
short
path of the form
\pi(s,t\mid F_{i})
with
(s,t)\in P
.
This adds
\leq|P|\cdot L
edges in each iteration, hence
\tilde{O}(|P|\cdot L^{2})
edges over all iterations.

Now, suppose
\pi(s,t\mid c)
is short.
We say iteration
i
is
good
for the triplet
(s,t,c)
if
c\in F_{i}
, and no color from
\pi(s,t\mid c)
is in
F_{i}
.
In this case, iteration
i
adds to
H
a path of length
\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(s,t)
which avoids the color
c
, so
(s,t,c)
is taken care of.
The probability of a single iteration to be good is
\frac{1}{L}(1-\frac{1}{L})^{L}\geq\frac{1}{4L}
.
So, the probability that
(s,t,c)
has some good iteration is at least
1-(1-\frac{1}{4L})^{r}\geq 1-e^{-\frac{r}{4L}}\geq 1-n^{-10}
. By a union bound, with high probability every short triplet
(s,t,c)
has some good iteration.

Choosing
L
s.t.
|P|\cdot L^{2}=n^{2}\cdot L^{-\frac{1}{k+1}}
, i.e.,

L=(\frac{n^{2}}{|P|})^{\frac{k+1}{2k+3}}
,
yields the stated size bound for
H
.
∎

The second application is for CFT
+2

spanners
: this is a subgraph
H
of
G
such that
\operatorname{dist}_{H-c}(u,v)\leq\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(u,v)+2
for every
u,v\in V
and color
c
.
The proof idea is based on
[
BGPW17
]
.

Theorem A.2
.

For any
n
-vertex colored graph
G=(V,E)
with color classes of size at most
k
, there exists a CFT
+2
spanner
H
with

|E(H)|=\tilde{O}(n^{2-\frac{1}{k+2}})

edges.

Proof.

We show the proof for the case where
G
has colored edges, and the case of colored vertices is very similar.
Let
L
be a parameter to be set later.
We say a vertex is
colorful
if it has at least
L
edges of different colors incident to it, and
dull
otherwise.

Let
S\subseteq V
be such that every colorful vertex has at least two incident edges of different colors with the other endpoint in
S
.
By standard hitting set arguments,
|S|=O(\frac{n}{L}\cdot\log n)
.
Let
H_{dull}\subseteq G
consists of all the edges incident to dull vertices.
Let
H_{src}
be a CFT
S\times V
distance preserver of
Theorem

1.2
.
We define
H=H_{dull}\cup H_{src}
.

We first prove correctness.
Fix
u,v\in V
and color
c
, and we show that
\operatorname{dist}_{H-c}(u,v)\leq\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(u,v)+2
.
If
\pi(u,v\mid c)
is contained in
H_{dull}
, we are done.
Otherwise, there is some colorful
x\in\pi(u,v\mid c)
, with at least two incident edges of different colors connecting
x
to
S
.
At least one of those is not of color
c
.
Denote it by
e=(x,s)
with
s\in S
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{H-c}(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{dist}_{H-c}(u,s)+\operatorname{dist}_{H-c}(s,v)

by triangle ineq.,

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(u,s)+\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(s,v)

as
H
is a
1
-CFT
S\times V
preserver,

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(u,x)+\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(x,v)+2\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(x,s)

by triangle ineq. (splitting both distances at
x
),

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}_{G-c}(u,v)+2

since
x\in\pi(u,v\mid c)
, and
e=(x,s)
is in
G-c
.

We now analyze the size.
Note that a dull vertex sees at most
L
different colors on its incident edges, so its degree can be at most
Lk
.
Thus,
H_{dull}
contains at most
nLk
edges.
As for
H_{src}
, by
Theorem

1.2
it has
O(n^{2}\cdot L^{-\frac{1}{k+1}}\cdot k\log n)
edges.
We set

so that

, namely
L=(n\log n)^{1-\frac{1}{k+2}}
.
Plugging this choice of
L
yields the stated size bound for
H
.
∎

A.2
Single-Pair Distance Preservers in Weighted Graphs

In this section, we study CFT
single-pair
preservers, required to preserve the distance between a specific pair of vertices
s,t
under any color fault.
Here, we focus on
weighted
graphs with
failing edges
(i.e., edge-colored in the CFT setting), and show a notable gap between
k
-FT and CFT with color classes of size
\leq k
, already for
k=2
.
The
k
-FT case was studied by
[
BGPW17
]
, who showed that
1
-FT single pair distance preservers only need

k
-FT for any

may require
\Omega(n^{2})

Our bounds for the CFT case are obtained by reductions to (fault-free) pairwise distance preservers.
Let
\mathtt{DP}(n,p)
denote the worst-case size of (fault-free) distance preservers for at most
p

n
-vertex weighted undirected graphs.
Coppersmith and Elkin
[

]
proved that
\Omega(n^{2/3}p^{2/3})\leq\mathtt{DP}(n,p)\leq O(\min\{\sqrt{n}p+n,n\sqrt{p}\})

We show:

Theorem A.3
.

Let
\mathtt{CFT\text{-}SP}(n,k)
be the worst-case size of a CFT single-pair distance preserver, over
n
-vertex undirected weighted edge-colored graphs
s.t. each color appears at most
k

Then,

\Omega(\mathtt{DP}(n,n))\leq\mathtt{CFT\text{-}SP}(n,k)\leq O(\mathtt{DP}(n,kn)).

When

\mathtt{CFT\text{-}SP}(n,k)=\widetilde{\Theta}(\mathtt{DP}(n,n))
;
plugging the bounds of
[
CE06
]
, this is between
\tilde{\Omega}(n^{4/3})
and
\tilde{O}(n^{3/2})
.

We note that unlike other preservers in the paper, in this section we only consider failing edges rather than vertices; this is because our results use the well-known
restoration lemma

[

]
which only holds for edge failures.

Lemma A.4

(Weighted Restoration Lemma
[

]
)
.

After

We now show the upper bound in
Theorem

As noted in

A.3
Single Source Reachability and Bounded Flow in Directed Graphs

Single-source
k
-FT reachability preservers were introduced by Baswana, Choudhary and Roditty
[
BCR18
]
.
Here, the given graph
G=(V,E)
is
directed
and comes with a single source vertex
s
, and the goal is to find a sparse subgraph
H=(V,E^{\prime})
such that for every
t\in V
and every
F\subseteq V
(
F\subseteq E
) with
|F|\leq k
, there is a directed
s\rightsquigarrow t
path in
G-F
iff there is such a path in
H-F
.
The extension of this definition to the CFT setting is obvious (replacing the failure of the set
F
with the failure of a color
c
in the above definition).
The work of
[
BCR18
]
showed that any
n
-vertex
directed
graph has a
k
-FT single-source reachability preserver with
O(2^{k}\cdot n)
edges, and gave a matching lower bound.
We strengthen their lower bound and show it holds also in the CFT setting with color classes of size
\leq k
:

Theorem A.6
.

The worst-case size of CFT single-source reachability preservers for
n
-vertex directed colored graphs with color classes of size
\leq k

(s.t.
2^{k}\leq n
) is

\widetilde{\Theta}\big(2^{k}\cdot n)
.

Hence, here there is essentially no gap between
k
-FT and CFT with color classes of size
\leq k
, i.e., the brute-force approach of using a solution for the
k
-FT to also solve CFT is optimal.

The problem has been further generalized to
k
-FT single-source
\lambda
-bounded flow preservers
, where
H-F
is not only required to preserve the reachability from the source
s
in
G-F
, but the
flow value
from
s
to every other vertex up to a threshold of
\lambda

[
LMSZ19
,
BCDW24
]

(the reachability case is just
\lambda=1
).
Bansal, Choudhary, Dhanoa and Wardhan
[
BCDW24
]
have recently generalized the results of
[
BCR18
]
and proved that the optimal (worst-case) sparsity in
k
-EFT is
\Theta(\lambda 2^{k}\cdot n)
.
Our CFT lower bound easily generalizes as well, giving another instance of where the CFT setting does not provide a benefit over the corresponding
k
-FT setting:

Theorem A.7
.

The worst-case size of CFT single-source
\lambda

Appendix C
Neighborhood Cover

Here, we give the proof of
Lemma

3.5
from the classical
\tilde{O}(m)
time algorithm for neighborhood covers by Awerbuch, Berger, Cowen and Peleg
[
ABCP98
]
.
First, by using their algorithm to construct a “standard”
2r
-neighborhood cover, we can compute a collection of clusters
Z_{ij}\subseteq V
for
1\leq i\leq p
,
1\leq j\leq q
, and a mapping
\operatorname{cov}:V\to\{Z_{ij}\}_{1\leq i\leq p,1\leq j\leq q}
, such that:

•

(Covering) For each
v\in V
,
B_{G}(v,2r)\subseteq\operatorname{cov}(v)
.

•

(Diameter) Each
Z_{ij}
has (strong) diameter
O(r\log n)
.

•

(Overlap)
p=O(\log n)
and, for every
i=1,\dots p
, the sets
\{Z_{ij}\}_{1\leq j\leq q}
are mutually disjoint.

We construct our “shelled” neighborhood cover for balls of radius
r
as follows:
For each
Z_{ij}
we create the kernel
K_{ij}=B_{G}(\operatorname{cov}^{-1}(Z_{ij}),r)
and cluster
X_{ij}=B_{G}(K_{ij},r)=B_{G}(\operatorname{cov}^{-1}(Z_{ij}),2r)
(and the shell
Y_{ij}=X_{ij}-K_{ij}
).
Let us now prove the required properties:

•

(Covering) For
v\in V
, if
\operatorname{cov}(v)=Z_{ij}
, then
B_{G}(v,r)\subseteq K_{ij}

by definition of
K_{ij}
.

•

(Diameter) If
u,v\in X_{ij}
, then by definition of
X_{ij}
there are
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in\operatorname{cov}^{-1}(Z_{ij})\subseteq Z_{ij}
such that
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,u^{\prime}),\operatorname{dist}_{G}(v,v^{\prime})\leq 2r
.
By triangle inequality,
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq 4r+\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
.
But
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in Z_{ij}
which has diameter
O(r\log n)
, so we obtain
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)=O(r\log n)
.
Thus,

has weak diameter

.

•

(Overlap) We assert that for each
1\leq i\leq p

are mutually disjoint, so in particular each vertex can belong to at most
p=O(\log n)

Seeking contradiction, suppose

Then there exist

, which is impossible as

and
Z_{i\ell}
are disjoint.
