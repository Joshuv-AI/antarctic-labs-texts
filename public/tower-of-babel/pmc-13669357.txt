Difunctionalization of unsaturated hydrocarbons via EDA complexes to access pentafluorosulfanyl thioethers
Authors: Xiaofeng Zhang, Yuanyang Jiang, Hui-Yi Yang, Yifan Ma, Jiangshan Zhang, Ning Zhang, Gang Wu, Donglei Sun, Yi Shi, Wei Zhang, Shuaishuai Yue, Shuo Guo, Jianbo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecently, the pentafluorosulfanyl (SF5) group has attracted considerable interest from medicinal chemists owing to its capacity to significantly enhance the pharmaceutical properties of small-molecule drug candidates. However, a general method for three-component pentafluorosulfanylation of electron-deficient alkenes remains elusive. Herein, we present an electron donor-acceptor (EDA) complex photoactivation strategy for the thiopentafluorosulfanylation of unsaturated hydrocarbons, including electron-deficient alkenes, alkynes, 1,6-enynes, and even acetylene gas. The utility of this approach is demonstrated through the synthesis of a diverse array of pentafluorosulfanyl thioethers and the late-stage functionalization of complex molecules, including pharmaceuticals, natural products, and amino acid derivatives. This work establishes a framework for designing atom-economical difunctionalization reactions that employ green LEDs under mild conditions. Moreover, mechanistic studies support an EDA complex-mediated pathway and identify ArSCl as a key intermediate formed upon generation of the thiyl radical.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Synthetic chemistry methodology, Reaction mechanisms

Introduction

Fluorinated organic molecules play a significant role in biomedicine, agrochemicals, and materials science1–5. Among emerging fluorinated motifs, the pentafluorosulfanyl (SF5) group has attracted increasing attention due to its strong electronegativity and high lipophilicity (SF5: Hammett σp = 0.68, Hansch πp = 1.23 vs CF3: Hammett σp = 0.54, Hansch πp = 0.88). As a versatile bioisostere of trifluoromethyl and tert-butyl groups, the SF5 group has unique steric and electronic characteristics, leading to growing opportunities in pharmaceuticals and functional materials6–12 (Fig. 1a). Furthermore, aromatic SF5 compounds have been suggested to improve environmental profiles13, whereas analogous studies on aliphatic SF5 derivatives remain scarce. Consequently, the incorporation of SF5 into organic frameworks has gained popularity, with growing applications in agrochemistry14, optoelectronics15 and medicinal chemistry9,16.

In recent years, the synthesis of SF5-containing molecules has witnessed remarkable breakthroughs and rapid progress, driven by the development of diverse radical pentafluorosulfanylation strategies17–19. For instance, Pitts, Tlili, Qing, Qin, and colleagues introduced strain-release functionalization strategies that facilitate direct C-SF5 and N-SF5 bond formation, enabling access to SF5-based “hybrid bioisosteres” that complement conventional aryl-SF5 motif20–25. Paquin and co-workers reported the first hydropentafluorosulfanylation of alkynes using SF5Cl as the SF5 source, yielding Z-configured SF5-alkenyl products26. Meanwhile, Qing’s group demonstrated the use of alkyl azides as substrates in radical pentafluorosulfanylation reactions to access various SF5 compounds27,28. Notably, several structurally tailored, shelf-stable SF5 reagents have recently been developed by Paquin29, Tlili23,30, Qin25,31, and co-workers. These reagent platforms have enabled a variety of transformations for the synthesis of SF5-containing molecules25,32 Recently, our group has developed efficient radical pentafluorosulfanylation protocols that facilitate migration reactions, hydroxypentafluorosulfanylation of alkenes, allylic pentafluorosulfanylation, and other transformations through precise control of radical intermediates32–35. Despite these advances, current methods for three-component pentafluorosulfanylation of alkenes or alkynes remain largely limited to electron-rich or strained substrates24–26,30–33,36–39 (Fig. 1b). In particular, thiopentafluorosulfanylation has been limited only to the strained ring substrates, while its extension to electron-deficient alkenes or alkynes remains a significant challenge.

SF5Cl is among the most widely used reagents for radical pentafluorosulfanylation. Reported activation strategies for SF5Cl include radical initiation, THF-induced activation, photochemical excitation, and base-promoted pathways20,26,40,41 (Fig. 1c). In most systems, the pentafluorosulfanyl radical is generated and undergoes an atom-transfer radical addition (ATRA) across unsaturated C = C bonds42–44. To address the persistent issue of undesired chlorinated byproducts, recent studies have focused on substrate design and radical modulation32,33,35,45,46. Drawing inspiration from the electron donor–acceptor (EDA) complex chemistry47–57, including pioneering work by Paquin and co-workers58, we hypothesized that SF5Cl could undergo EDA complex-mediated activation (Fig. 1d, top). This mechanism would generate SF5 radicals and chloride anions simultaneously, with the latter functioning as a nucleophile rather than participating in ATRA processes. Moreover, in the presence of alkenes or alkynes, the resulting alkyl radicals could be intercepted by a donor-derived radical species, thus enabling a three-component reaction.

In this work, we report a straightforward EDA complex photoactivation strategy employing SF5Cl as the electron acceptor and aryl thiols or diaryl disulfides as the electron donor to achieve thiopentafluorosulfanylation of diverse unsaturated hydrocarbons, including alkenes, alkynes, enynes, and BCBs (Fig. 1d, bottom).

Results

The feasibility of the direct thiopentafluorosulfanylation of phenyl acrylate (1a) was evaluated using thiophenol (2a) and SF5Cl (3) as model substrates. First, a range of solvents, including DCM, THF, and EtOAc, gave inferior results relative to toluene, likely due to the stability of the radicals (Table 1, entries 1–3; see supplementary information Table S1 for details). Screening of light sources revealed that green light is sufficient and effective to activate thiophenol/SF5Cl EDA complex to produce SF5 radicals (entries 4-7). It is believed that the screening of light sources and solvents plays a significant role in controlling the reaction. A more dilute solution of SF5Cl gave lower yields, likely because it is less conducive to EDA complex formation28,59,60 (entry 8). Likewise, no improvement was observed when SF5Cl was added dropwise, suggesting that high concentrations of SF5Cl promote the formation of EDA complexes (entry 9). The addition of excess thiophenol reduced the yield of 4a, leading to disulfide-substituted by-products at the alkene double bond (entry 10; see the supplementary information for detailed characterization). Therefore, using the optimal conditions (green LEDs irradiation in toluene at room temperature for 4 h), the desired thiopentafluorosulfanylated product 4a was obtained in 92% isolated yield (entry 11).

With the optimized conditions in hand, we aimed to define the substrate scope of the method. As shown in Fig. 2, a wide range of differently substituted phenyl acrylates were readily converted into the corresponding thiopentafluorosulfanylated products in good to excellent yields. Electron-neutral substituent substrates were smoothly converted to the corresponding products in good yields (4a and 4b). For these substrates bearing electron-withdrawing substituents, the expected products 4c-4h were obtained in 49-88% yields. Notably, both OCF3 and CF3-substituted phenyl acrylates smoothly afforded the corresponding products 4i and 4j in 61% and 89% yields, respectively.

Moreover, 2-naphthyl acrylate also afforded 4k in 70% yield. The protocol was also applicable to the late-stage modification of complex molecules, such as raspberry ketone (4l), 4-tert-octylphenol (4m), and carvacrol (4n), providing the target products in moderate yields. Substrates with two substituents on the aromatic ring also performed smoothly (4o, 4p). Furthermore, the mild reaction conditions of this method enable the thiopentafluorosulfanylation of protected amino acids, affording target product 4q in 73% yield. The substrate scope can also be extended to other electron-deficient alkenes, leading to the desired products in moderate yields (4r-4t). Notably, [1.1.0]bicyclobutanes were also suitable substrates for this transformation, affording 4u and 4v in good yields. The thiophenol substrates bearing various substituents on the aromatic ring were also examined. Fluoro (4w, 4x), chloro (4y, 4z), ester (4aa), and methyl (4ab) groups were all tolerated, affording the corresponding products in 46-89% yields.

Subsequently, alkyne substrates were investigated (Fig. 3). Simple phenylacetylene could be easily converted to its derived thiopentafluorosulfanylated product 7a in 75% yield with excellent stereoselectivity. For phenylacetylene derivatives substituted with phenyl and halogen groups on the aryl ring (7b-7g), the corresponding thiopentafluorosulfanylated alkenes were obtained in 43–69% yields. Furthermore, the system was further extended to trifluoromethyl-, bis(trifluoromethyl)- and trifluoromethoxy-substituted phenylacetylenes, providing the desired products (7h-7j) in good yields. The crystal structure of 7h (CCDC 2494785) confirmed the E-configuration of the product. A variety of functional groups are tolerated, including ester (7k), ketone (7l), nitrile (7 m, 7n), sulfone (7o), aldehyde (7p), and naphthalene (7q), affording the corresponding products in good to excellent yields (52–98%). It is worth mentioning that all the substrates had stereoselectivity for the E-configuration of products. Phenylacetylene bearing electron-donating substituents, such as methyl (7r, 7s), tert-butyl (7t), and n-pentyl (7u), suppressed the formation of the products. As a result, the corresponding thiopentafluorosulfanylated alkenes were obtained in diminished yields (40–63%). To further demonstrate the generality of this protocol, the alkyl-substituted alkynes were investigated, affording the corresponding products in 40–65% yields (7v-7y). The practicability of this approach was further demonstrated by the conversion of the simplest acetylene, affording the corresponding products in 61% and 55% yield, respectively (7z, 7aa), further demonstrating the efficiency and tolerance of this approach toward substrates.

Furthermore, we explore the application of this transformation to include a tandem radical cyclization system. Thus, a broad scope of 1,6-enynes were examined and we found these substrates could be successfully converted into the expected cyclization products in good yields, with the E-isomer as the major product in all the cases (Fig. 4). The 1,6-enynes bearing N-phenyl imide containing various halo-groups such as F, Cl, and Br performed well to deliver desired products in good yields (9b-9f). The crystal structure of 9b (CCDC 2388327) confirmed the E-configuration of the product. Switching from electron-donating (p-OMe, p-tBu) to electron-withdrawing (p-CN and p-CF3) groups of the substituents on the arenes exhibited good compatibility for the transformation, affording the products along with excellent E/Z ratios (9g-9j). Furthermore, N-benzyl imide was well tolerated, leading to the target product 9k in 91% yield. We also examined the reactions of 8a with various aryl thiols. Both electron-neutral and electron-donating groups were efficiently transformed into the corresponding products (9l-9o) with up to 90% isolated yield. Subsequently, an analogous investigation was conducted on a range of derivatives of amino acids and other bioactive compounds. Notably, for this class of substrates, pharmaceutical substrate benzocaine afforded the desired product in 77% yield (9p), amino acids derivatives bearing alkyl groups were also readily converted to E-thiopentafluorosulfanylated succinimides in good to excellent yields along with excellent E/Z ratio, including L-alanine (9q), L-leucine (9r), L-aspartate (9s), D-valine (9t), and D-phenprobamate (9u).

To further demonstrate the utility of this method, a series of derivatization reactions of the SF5 thioether products was conducted to access diverse SF5-substituted derivatives (Fig. 5). A scale-up reaction using 4.0 mmol of 5l under standard conditions smoothly afforded 1.11 g of compound 7l. The resulting 7l was successfully subjected to further transformations. Partial oxidation with m-CPBA afforded the corresponding sulfoxide 10 in 84% yield61, whereas complete oxidation provided sulfone 11 in 85% yield. Further oxidation of 11 with m-CPBA/TFA generated p-quinol 12 in 46% yield34. Additionally, treatment of 7l with allylmagnesium bromide reagent led to the formation of homo-allylic alcohol-substituted SF5-thioether 13 in 85% yield. Oxidation of 7l using (diacetoxyiodo)benzene (PIDA) and ammonium carbamate afforded the phenylsulfonimide-substituted SF5-product 14 in 57% yield. These results highlighted the robustness of the alkenyl-substituted pentafluorosulfanyl groups under strong oxidizing conditions. Similarly, oxidation of brominated SF5-thioether 7c with m-CPBA yielded a sulfone, which then underwent Suzuki cross-coupling to give arylated product 15. Moreover, reduction of 4a with NaBH4 afforded alcohol 16 in 56% yield without compromising the SF5 group. These transformations expand the synthetic versatility of SF5 thioethers and provide practical guidance for the development of SF5 frameworks. In addition, preliminary biological evaluation revealed that SF5-thioether 4j exhibited greater activity against cancer cells than irinotecan (see Section 6 of the Supplementary Information).

To gain insights into the reaction mechanism of this photoinduced thiopentafluorosulfanylation process, mechanistic experiments were performed (Fig. 6). Under the EDA complex conditions, in situ generation of SF5Cl in the presence of thiophenol 2a or PhSSPh 6a delivered the desired EDA complex I or EDA complex II, which displayed a bathochromic displacement in the visible region. As a result, the solution developed a bright-yellow color49 (Fig. 6a, b). Additionally, the formation of this intermediate EDA complex was also confirmed by the Job’s Plot experiment62,63, where a 1:1 stoichiometric relation between SF5Cl and 2a was confirmed (see Supplementary Information Fig. S9). Subsequently, we employed time-dependent density functional (TD-DFT) to understand the optical and electronic properties of the EDA complex and molecular orbitals (Fig. 6c, see Source data for details). The computational results indicate that in the EDA complex, photoinduced electron transfer occurred from the thiophenol (donor) to the SF5Cl (acceptor). The lowest-energy excited state, comprising πHOMO-πLUMO transitions, exhibits a notable degree of charge transfer character. In addition, we employed the 1H NMR spectroscopy to monitor the spectral changes upon the gradual addition of the diphenyl disulfide to SF5Cl (Fig. 6d). As the amount of PhSSPh increased, the 1H NMR signals of diphenyl disulfide shifted downfield, indicating the formation of an electron donor-acceptor (EDA) complex between diphenyl disulfide and SF5Cl. We thus hypothesized that the reaction occurred through radical intermediates. We next employed the EPR spectroscopy to probe the radical intermediates under standard conditions to synthesize SF5-thioethers (Fig. 6e, Section 4 in the Supplementary Information for details). N-Benzylidene-tert-butylamine N-oxide (PBN) was used as a spin trap to identify radicals as long-lived PBN-radical adducts64,65. A purple-line signal with g = 2.0071 and hyperfine coupling constants AN = 12.35 G, AH = 46.41 G, and AF = 1.48 G was detected, which formed upon addition of the SF5 radical on the nitrone (Radical 1); another weak green-line signal with (g = 2.0079, AN = 12.22 G and AH = 5.85 G) was also detected, attributed to addition of the ArS radical on the nitrone (Radical 2). Consistently, control experiments were conducted to confirm the involvement of a radical process. Under standard conditions, the reaction was completely suppressed by TEMPO, BHT, or DPE, and the corresponding adducts were detected by HRMS (see Supplementary Information for details). Interestingly, PhSCl was identified as a key intermediate in the thioetherification process of radical conditions (Fig. 6f). Under dark conditions, PhSSPh did not react with SF5Cl to form PhSCl; in contrast, irradiation with green LEDs led to its formation, suggesting that PhSCl is generated via a photoinduced EDA complex. Consistent with this observation, HRMS analysis under the standard conditions revealed the presence of PhSCl. Consistent with its UV–vis absorption profile (see Fig. S10 in the Supplementary Information), PhSCl undergoes homolytic cleavage more readily than PhSSPh to generate the PhS radical upon irradiation. Accordingly, replacement of PhSSPh with PhSCl (1.2 equiv) under the standard conditions afforded product 7a in 60% yield (Fig. 6f, bottom). These observations support the intermediacy of PhSCl in the radical thiopentafluorosulfanylation reaction.

Consequently, the proposed reaction mechanism is depicted in Fig. 6g. Initially, the electron-deficient reagent SF5Cl 3 interacts with the electron-rich aryl thiol 2 or diaryl disulfide 6 to generate the corresponding ground-state electron donor-acceptor (EDA) complex (I or II). Upon irradiation with green light, this EDA complex undergoes a photoinduced electron transfer process, leading to the formation of SF5 radical, thiyl radical III, and ArSCl IV or HCl as byproducts. The SF5 radicals subsequently add to the unsaturated hydrocarbons, resulting in the generation of carbon-centered radicals (V), which are intercepted by the ArS radical to furnish the desired SF5-thioethers (4 or 7). Importantly, ArSCl is efficiently converted to the PhS radical under green LED irradiation.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a general strategy for the radical difunctionalization of unsaturated hydrocarbons via a photoactive EDA complex with SF5Cl and an aryl thiol or a diaryl disulfide. Upon irradiation with low-energy visible light, SF5 radicals are generated under mild conditions. This approach offers a clean reaction profile, and in certain cases, the reagent itself provides both radicals needed for the difunctionalization. The method demonstrates broad substrate scope and excellent functional group tolerance, accommodating a diverse range of unsaturated systems, including alkenes, alkynes, [1.1.0]bicyclobutanes, 1,6-enynes, and even acetylene gas, highlighting its versatility and practical value. The high robustness of this strategy facilitates direct radical functionalization of abundant functional groups and enables late-stage modification of biorelevant compounds. Moreover, post-functionalization of the resulting pentafluorosulfanyl thioethers enables efficient, novel routes to complex SF5-containing architectures.

Methods

General procedure A for compound 4

A 10 mL vial equipped with a magnetic stir bar was charged with unsaturated hydrocarbons 1 (1.0 equiv, 0.1 mmol). Then anhydrous toluene (2 mL) and aryl thiol 2 (1.2 equiv, 0.12 mmol) were added successively. The vial was then sealed using a cap with a rubber bottle stopper and removed from the glovebox. Then, a solution of SF5Cl (in nhexane, ~0.5 M, 7.0 equiv, 0.7 mmol, 1.4 mL) was added by a syringe, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 4 hours at room temperature under the green LEDs irradiation. Then, the reaction was concentrated in vacuo, and the residue was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel to afford the product 4.

General procedure B for compound 7

A 10 mL vial equipped with a magnetic stir bar was charged with alkyne 5 (1.0 equiv, 0.1 mmol) and disulfide ether (0.6 equiv, 0.06 mmol). Then anhydrous toluene (2 mL) was added successively. The vial was then sealed using a cap with a rubber bottle stopper and removed from the glovebox. Then, a solution of SF5Cl (in nhexane, ~0.5 M, 5.0 equiv, 0.5 mmol, 1.0 mL) was added by a syringe, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 6 h at room temperature under the green LEDs irradiation. Then, the reaction was concentrated in vacuo, and the residue was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel to afford the product 7.

General procedure C for compound 9

A 10 mL vial equipped with a magnetic stir bar was charged with 1,6-enyne 8 (0.1 mmol, 1.0 equiv) and NaHCO3 (0.2 mmol, 2.0 equiv, 17 mg) in a glovebox. Then anhydrous toluene (4 mL) and aryl thiol 2 (0.2 mmol, 2.0 equiv) were added successively. The vial was then sealed using a cap with a rubber bottle stopper and removed from the glovebox. Then, a solution of SF5Cl (in nhexane, ~0.5 M, 5.0 equiv, 0.2 mmol, 0.4 mL) was added by a syringe, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 12 hours at room temperature under the green LEDs irradiation. Then, the reaction was concentrated in vacuo, and the residue was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel to afford the product 9.
