Strata of Ecological Coexistence via Grassmannians
Türkü Özlüm Çelik, Pierre A. Haas, Georgy Scholten, Kexin Wang, Giulio Zucal

Abstract
We study the Lotka–Volterra system from the perspective of computational algebraic geometry, focusing on equilibria that are both feasible and stable. These conditions stratifies the parameter space in ℝ n × ℝ n × n \mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n} with the feasible-stable semialgebraic sets. We encode them on the real Grassmannian Gr ℝ ⁡ ( n , 2 ​ n ) \operatorname{Gr}_{\mathbb{R}}(n,2n) via a parameter matrix representation, and use oriented matroid theory to develop an algorithm, combining Grassmann–Plücker relations with branching under feasibility and stability constraints. This symbolic approach determines whether a given sign pattern in the parameter space ℝ n × ℝ n × n \mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n} admits a consistent extension to Plücker coordinates. As an application, we establish the impossibility of certain interaction networks, showing that the corresponding patterns admit no such extension satisfying feasibility and stability conditions, through an effective implementation. We complement these results using numerical nonlinear algebra with HypersurfaceRegions.jl to decompose the parameter space and detect rare feasible-stable sign patterns.

Introduction

Let
n
be a positive integer,
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
a real vector, and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
a real matrix. The generalized Lotka–Volterra system
[
23
,
18
,
20
]
for the time-varying vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by the system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs):

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}),

(1)

where
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the diagonal matrix with the components of
\mathbf{x}
on the diagonal. In components, we write
\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})
,
\mathbf{B}=(b_{ij})
,
\mathbf{x}=(x_{i})
, so that (
1
) becomes

\dot{x}_{i}=x_{i}\left(a_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij}x_{j}\right),\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,n.

(2)

At a
steady state
of the system,
\dot{\mathbf{x}}={\bf 0}
. If
\mathbf{B}
is nonsingular, the Lotka–Volterra system has a steady state,

\mathbf{x}_{\star}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{a}.

(3)

Of particular interest to us will be steady states with all coordinates positive, i.e., steady states of
coexistence
. Borrowing ecological parlance, we term these states
feasible

[
24
,
20
]
. The steady state in (
3
) is the only possible steady state of coexistence in the Lotka–Volterra system
[
20
]
. Beyond feasibility, we study
stability
: a feasible equilibrium is (locally asymptotically) stable if small perturbations decay over time, which occurs exactly when all eigenvalues of the Jacobian at
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
have negative real parts
[
2
]
.
Explicitly, this Jacobian is

J_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{\star})=-\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{x}_{\star})\mathbf{B}.

(4)

A necessary condition for the equilibrium to be stable is
(-1)^{n}\mathrm{det}({J}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}})>0
. This is a straightforward consequence of the Routh–Hurwitz conditions for stability
[
23
]
. Now feasibility requires
\mathrm{det}(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{x}_{\star}))>0
, so a necessary condition for stability and feasibility is
{\det(\mathbf{B})>0}
. Writing
\mathbf{B}^{-1}
in (
3
) in terms of the adjugate matrix
\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{B})
, we can therefore express the conditions of feasibility and stability equivalently using the normalized forms of
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
and
J_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}:=\mathrm{adj}(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{a},

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\star})=-\mathrm{diag}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\star})\mathbf{B}.

(5)

In theoretical ecology, the Lotka–Volterra system is the simplest model for the dynamics of a vector
\mathbf{x}
of abundances of
n
species
[
23
,
18
]
. In this context,
\mathbf{a}
represents the growth rates of these species in the absence of interactions, which are encoded by
\mathbf{B}
. In more detail,
a_{i}>0
for a species that grows on its own, but
a_{i}<0
for a species that dies in the absence of other species. The diagonal elements
b_{ii}
describe intraspecies interactions, and it is usual to assume
b_{ii}>0
to impose a notion of carrying capacity. Different types of interspecies interactions are defined by
b_{ij}
(
i\neq j
): the interaction between species
i
and
j
is
competitive
if
b_{ij},b_{ji}>0
; it is
mutualistic
if
b_{ij},b_{ji}<0
; and it is a
directed predator-prey interaction
, with species
i
predating on species
j
, if
b_{ij}<0
,
b_{ji}>0
.

Coexistence of species in the Lotka–Volterra system corresponds to the existence of a stable and feasible steady state
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
. It is straightforward to decide, numerically, whether
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
is stable and feasible for given
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{B}
, but theoretical ecology ultimately seeks to describe the general principles that determine the possibility of stable and feasible coexistence. In particular, one asks: How does the possibility of stable and feasible coexistence depend on the network of types of ecological interactions, i.e., on the sign patterns of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{B}
?

The explicit conditions for feasibility and stability are too complex, however, to allow immediate insights into this question beyond the textbook case
n=2

[
23
]
. Much recent work in theoretical ecology, exemplified by
[
3
,
22
,
9
,
8
,
17
]
, has therefore followed the approach pioneered by Robert May
[
19
]
, of studying large ecological communities statistically, using results from random matrix theory
[
4
]
. This approach has revealed, for example, the generic effects of competition, mutualism, and predation on stability and feasibility of coexistence, but it cannot capture the full network structure of ecological interactions. Very recent work
[
20
]
has therefore taken a complementary approach, of exhaustive analysis of all networks of types of ecological interactions for small communities with
2\leqslant n\leqslant 5
. This revealed that the probability of stable and feasible coexistence depends hugely on the structure of this network. In particular, this work discovered that a very small subset of these networks are
impossible ecologies
in which stable and feasible coexistence is nontrivially impossible
[
20
]
, but the identification of impossible ecologies with
n>3
remained a numerical conjecture.

Here, we therefore bring the perspective of computational and real algebraic geometry
[
21
]
to the study of the feasible (
3
) and stable (
4
) equilibria in the parameter space
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
of the Lotka–Volterra system
1
. We show how the requirements of feasibility and stability translate into a collection of polynomial inequalities in the entries of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{B}
, so that the regions of parameter space corresponding to feasible-stable equilibria are
semialgebraic sets
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+n^{2}}
, the
feasible-stable strata of ecological coexistence
. The boundaries of these sets encode natural degeneracies such as equilibria with vanishing coordinates or marginally stable dynamics with eigenvalues on the imaginary axis. Our primary focus is on understanding the structure of these semialgebraic sets, in particular to identify those sign patterns for
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})
that admit no feasible-stable equilibrium, which are precisely the impossible ecologies of
[
20
]
.

To address this question, we recast the problem in an algebraic-combinatorial framework on the real Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}_{\mathbb{R}}(n,2n)
. Through the matrix representation
{[\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{a})\mid\mathbf{B}]}
, feasibility and stability conditions become sign constraints on the Plücker coordinates, which can be analyzed via oriented matroid theory
[
5
]
.
This reformulation allows us to replace parts of the semialgebraic feasibility-stability problem by purely combinatorial constraints derived from the Grassmann–Plücker relations, providing a systematic route to proving impossibility results.
The Grassmannian perspective also lends itself to effective computation: we develop a search algorithm, combining propagation via Grassmann–Plücker relations with branching steps constrained by ecological feasibility and stability checks. This approach not only certifies impossibility for certain sign patterns, but also organizes the feasible cases into strata indexed by their oriented matroid type, giving a combinatorial stratification of the parameter space that mirrors its semialgebraic decomposition.

Example 1.1

(
n=2
)
.

As a motivating example, we consider the two-species Lotka–Volterra system. Feasibility corresponds to
\mathbf{x}_{\star}>0
, or equivalently
\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{a}>0
, and stability requires both
\Tr(-\mathrm{diag}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\star})\mathbf{B})>0
and
\det(\mathbf{B})>0
. For instance, with

\mathbf{B}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{11}&b_{12}\\
b_{21}&b_{22}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{a}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}\\
a_{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{a}=\begin{bmatrix}b_{22}a_{1}-b_{12}a_{2}\\
-b_{21}a_{1}+b_{11}a_{2}\end{bmatrix},

feasibility and stability translate into explicit polynomial inequalities. Embedding the system into the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(2,4)
via the
2\times 4
matrix representation
M=[\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{a})|\mathbf{B}]
,
we recover the entries of
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{B}
and
\det(\mathbf{B})
as Plücker coordinates, i.e., two-by-two minors of
M
:

p_{13}=a_{1}b_{21},\quad p_{14}=a_{1}b_{22},\quad p_{23}=-a_{2}b_{11},\quad p_{24}=-a_{2}b_{12},\quad p_{34}=\det(\mathbf{B}),

and feasibility becomes the inequalities

p_{14}+p_{24}>0
and
-p_{13}-p_{23}>0
. Each possible combination of signs in the off-diagonal entries of
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{a}
corresponds to a total of 10 different networks
[
20
]
of types of ecological interactions (i.e., competition, mutualism, predation), four of which are incompatible with feasibility.
For instance, under
obligate mutualism
(
a_{i}<0
,
b_{ij}<0
for
i\neq j
),
p_{14}<0,p_{24}<0
, so the feasibility inequalities cannot hold, making this network impossible.

In Section
2
, we show that the feasibility and stability conditions of the Lotka–Volterra system define a semialgebraic set, which we refer to as the
feasible–stable stratum
(Definition
2.2
), defined by polynomial inequalities in the input parameters
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Section
3
introduces the
ecological Grassmannian
, where we propose the real Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}_{\mathbb{R}}(n,2n)
as an algebraic framework for studying these semialgebraic constraints.
In Proposition
3.1
, we reformulate the feasible-stable stratification over the Grassmannian, and in Corollary
3.3
we present a purely combinatorial relaxation of this reformulation in terms of sign patterns on the input parameters. In Section
4
, we develop algorithms that exploit the real Grassmannian and its oriented matroid stratification to explore the feasible-stable semialgebraic sets efficiently, and we describe their implementation in
Maple

[
25
]
based on our findings in Section
3
. Section
5
reports the results of our computational experiments. We answer questions left open in
[
20
]
in Theorem
5.1
, which establishes the impossibility of certain ecological networks with
n=4
species, as illustrated in Figure
2
. Finally, Section
6
uses tools from numerical algebraic geometry to study the feasible-stable parameter space via hypersurface arrangements, yielding explicit region counts, representative feasible–stable points, and combinatorial statistics on sign patterns.

2
Feasible Stable Ecologies as Semialgebraic Sets:

Routh–Hurwitz Polynomials

The condition that all eigenvalues of an
n\times n
matrix
M
have negative real parts can be reformulated as positivity conditions on the coefficients of its characteristic polynomial. This formulation, known as
Routh–Hurwitz criterion
, goes back to the classical works of Routh (1877) and Hurwitz (1895). For modern treatments and applications, we refer the reader to
[
2
,
15
,
23
]
. We adopt this criterion as our point of departure. Let the characteristic polynomial of
M
be given by

\displaystyle p_{M}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\det(\lambda I-M)=\lambda^{n}+c_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+\ldots+c_{1}\lambda+c_{0}.

(6)

The Hurwitz polynomial of
M
of degree
k
(for
k=1,\dots,n
) is defined as the determinant of the following
k\times k
matrix, where
c_{k}=0
if
k<0
:

\begin{pmatrix}c_{n-1}&c_{n-3}&c_{n-5}&\cdots&c_{n-(2k-1)}\\
1&c_{n-2}&c_{n-4}&\cdots&c_{n-(2k-2)}\\
0&c_{n-1}&c_{n-3}&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&1&c_{n-2}&\cdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&c_{n-k}\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

Theorem 2.1

(Routh–Hurwitz criterion)
.

The polynomial in (
6
) has all roots with negative real part if and only if all the Hurwitz polynomials (
7
) are positive.

Alternative formulations of this criterion can be found in
[
15
]
.
Indeed, the stability condition can be expressed in several equivalent ways: for example, in addition to the positivity of the Hurwitz determinants, one may require that all coefficients
c_{i}
of the characteristic polynomial be positive. In particular, a necessary condition for stability is
0<c_{0}=p_{M}(0)=(-1)^{n}\det(M)
. In our setting, we may choose among these formulations to suit the algebraic manipulations and computational tools applied in later sections.

From (
6
), it follows that the coefficients
c_{i}
are polynomials in terms of the entries of the matrix
M
.
For a given
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, we
take
M
to be the Jacobian matrix
J_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}(\textbf{x}_{\star})
in (
4
) evaluated at the steady state
\textbf{x}_{\star}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{a}
.
Although these constraints are rational in
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})
, we can use the interdependence between stability and feasibility to pass to a semialgebraic formulation of the constraints, as noted above around Equation (
4
): with
\det(\mathbf{B})>0
, feasibility
\mathbf{x}_{\star}>0
is equivalent to
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\star}>0
, and
J_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}
is Hurwitz-stable if and only if
\tilde{J}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}
is.
Thus our goal is to study the parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
for ecologies via polynomial positivity conditions in
a_{i}
,
b_{ij}
and
c_{i}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})
using tools from computational algebraic geometry.
With this in place, we define the following semialgebraic set as the geometric object corresponding to the feasible-stable ecologies
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

Definition 2.2

(Feasible-stable stratum)
.

Fix an ordered sign pattern

\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n},\sigma_{11},\sigma_{12},\dots,\sigma_{nn})\;\in\;\{\pm\}^{\,n+n^{2}}.

The
feasible-stable stratum
associated to
\sigma
is the semialgebraic set

\mathcal{S}_{n}(\sigma)\;=\;\left\{\,(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\;\Bigg|\;\begin{aligned} &\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})=\sigma,\quad\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{B})\,\mathbf{a}>\mathbf{0},\\[2.0pt]
&\text{all the Hurwitz polynomials of }\tilde{J}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}>0\end{aligned}\right\},

(8)

where
\tilde{J}_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}
is defined in (
4
).
Throughout, the polynomial constraints in
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})
ensuring
\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{a}>0
are called the
feasibility conditions
.
The polynomial constraints induced by the Routh–Hurwitz criterion are called the
stability (Hurwitz) conditions
.

In Definition
2.2
, we impose strict inequalities in the feasibility and stability conditions, so that each
\mathcal{S}_{n}(\sigma)
is an open basic semialgebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
From the lens of real algebraic geometry, it would be more natural to define a closed analogue by replacing the strict inequalities with non-strict ones, thereby including boundary points where one or more feasibility or stability polynomials vanish.
Such boundary cases correspond to degenerate steady states (in which some coordinates of
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
are equal to zero) or to marginally stable dynamics (for which the Jacobian has eigenvalues on the imaginary axis).
Since our aim is to characterize strictly positive equilibria that are asymptotically stable, we restrict attention to the open formulation and exclude these limiting configurations.

Definition 2.3

(Feasible-stable stratification)
.

The collection of strata
\bigl\{\mathcal{S}_{n}(\sigma)\bigr\}_{\sigma}

provides a pairwise disjoint, locally closed partition of the subset of the parameter space

\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
in which the feasibility and stability conditions are satisfied.
We call this decomposition the
feasible-stable stratification
of
the Lotka–Volterra parameter space. Each stratum is labeled by its
sign pattern
\sigma
and records precisely those parameters that realize
a feasible and asymptotically stable equilibrium contained in the orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
designated by
\sigma
. The
coexistence locus
in parameter space is the union of all

nonempty
feasible-stable strata. A Lotka–Volterra network with sign pattern
\sigma
is called

impossible
if and only if its feasible-stable stratum is empty,

\mathcal{S}_{N}(\sigma)=\varnothing
.
Thus, no choice of parameters with the prescribed signs yields a strictly
positive, asymptotically stable equilibrium.

Problem 2.4

(Realizability)
.

Given a sign pattern
\sigma\in\{\pm\}^{\,n+n^{2}}
, decide whether the feasible-stable stratum
\mathcal{S}_{n}(\sigma)
is empty. This is precisely the impossible-ecology problem studied in
[
20
]
.

To illustrate the semialgebraic formulation, we examine the following example.

Example 2.5

(
n=3
and the impossibility of obligate mutualism)
.

We consider
obligate mutualism

[
20
]
of
n=3
species, corresponding to the sign pattern
\sigma
with
\sigma_{i}=-
for all
i
,
\sigma_{ii}=+
, and
\sigma_{ij}=-
for
i\neq j
. All off-diagonal interactions are mutualistic and all intrinsic growth rates are negative. The feasibility inequality
\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{a}>0
under this
\sigma
forces all
2\times 2
principal minors of
\mathbf{B}
to be negative. This contradicts the Hurwitz stability condition
c_{1}>0
, which follows from the expression of
c_{1}
in terms of all principal minors. Hence
\mathcal{S}_{3}(\sigma)=\varnothing
, so this network is an impossible ecology.

We conclude the section by relating our framework to a broader context. Population dynamics have been studied not only in ecology but also in the context of game theory
[
18
,
27
]
, where similar mathematical structures arise.
In particular, the equations governing steady states in the Lotka–Volterra system share the same algebraic form as equilibrium conditions in game theory
[
1
, Equation (2.3)]
.
In both cases, the defining relations are polynomial constraints involving an interplay matrix, viz., the payoff matrix in game theory and the interaction matrix in ecology.
From this viewpoint, the ecological steady-state condition can be interpreted as an analogue of the best-response equilibrium condition, suggesting that computational algebraic geometry methods developed for game-theoretic equilibria may be applied to feasibility problems in ecological models.
A similar connection exists with other disciplines: the Lotka–Volterra system has actually been studied in the contexts of chemical reaction networks
[
13
]
and integrable systems
[
6
,
26
]
, both of which employ algebraic and geometric techniques central to nonlinear algebra. Formulating these connections precisely, within the perspective of nonlinear algebra, is a promising avenue for future research.

3
Feasible Stable Ecologies via Grassmannians

In this section, we map the parameters
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
into the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}_{\mathbb{R}}(n,2n)
, the set of all
n
-dimensional linear subspaces of the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. We study the feasible-stable strata
\mathcal{S}_{n}(\sigma)
of the Lotka–Volterra system (
1
) via the algebra and geometry of the Grassmannian. Throughout this section, we omit the subscript
\mathbb{R}
, assuming that we are working only over
\mathbb{R}
. We refer the reader to
[
21
, Chapter 5]
for a perspective on Grassmannians and projective spaces from nonlinear algebra.
We first outline the construction of the map from the parameters
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})
to the real Grassmannian and defer the algebraic details to later in the section.
The following construction defines the pipeline of our work:

(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})\longmapsto\mathcal{L}=\operatorname{rowspan}(M)\longmapsto p_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}=\operatorname{Pl}(\mathcal{L}),

(9)

where
\operatorname{Pl}
denotes the
Plücker embedding
of the Grassmannian into projective space and the parameters
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})
are encoded into the block matrix

M=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&\cdots&0&b_{11}&\cdots&b_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&a_{n}&b_{n1}&\cdots&b_{nn}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2n}.

(10)

We will denote by
M_{S}
the square matrix obtained by selecting the columns indexed by the ordered set
S
. (Hereinafter, we fix the ordering of the elements in set notation because of the alternating sign properties of the determinant.) We start by observing that positivity of the steady-state vector
\mathbf{x}_{\star}=\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{a}
can be expressed in terms of the minors of
M
,

(\mathbf{x}_{\star})_{i}=(-1)^{i+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\det(M_{j\cup S_{i}})/\det(M_{[n+1,\dots,2n]}),

(11)

where
S_{i}=[n+1,\ldots,2n]\setminus\{n+i\}
. From this observation, it is natural to attempt to reformulate Problem
2.4
in terms of the
n\times n
minors of
M
.

Since linear row operations on
M
scale all
n\times n
minors by a common nonzero factor, we work projectively: we identify vectors of minors up to
v\sim\lambda v
(
\lambda\neq 0
). The
n
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{L}=\operatorname{rowspan}(M)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2n}
can be uniquely determined by the vector of minors. The Plücker embedding is the map

\operatorname{Pl}\colon\operatorname{Gr}(n,2n)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\binom{2n}{n}-1}
into the real
projective space
of dimension
{\binom{2n}{n}-1}
which takes
\mathcal{L}\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,2n)
to its
Plücker coordinates

p\coloneq(p_{S})=\left(\det(M_{S})\mid{S\in\binom{[2n]}{n}}\right)
, wherein
\binom{[2n]}{n}
is the set of subsets of
[1,2,\dots,2n]
of size
n
. The image
\operatorname{Pl}(\operatorname{Gr}(n,2n))
of the Plücker embedding is the zero locus of the classical
Grassmann–Plücker relations
, which are homogeneous quadratic relations on the Plücker coordinates.

Using (
11
), the
feasibility
of
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
can be expressed in Plücker coordinates as

(\mathbf{x}_{\star})_{i}=(-1)^{i+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{p_{j\cup S_{i}}}{{p_{[n+1,\dots,2n]}}}>0\quad\text{for all }i=1,\ldots,n.

(12)

The
stability
inequalities from Theorem
2.1
can translate into Plücker coordinates but require additional information from the vector
\mathbf{a}
.

We recall from (
4
) that
J_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{\star})
is the Jacobian at the steady state
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
of system (
1
).
Its characteristic polynomial is

\displaystyle P_{J_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{\star})}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\det\bigl(\lambda I-J_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x}_{\star})\bigr)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}\lambda^{i},

(13)

with coefficients

\displaystyle c_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[n]\\
|I|=i\end{subarray}}\prod_{j\in I^{\text{c}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\star})_{j}\det(\mathbf{B}_{I^{\text{c}}}),

(14)

where
I^{\text{c}}=[n]\setminus I
and
\mathbf{B}_{I^{\text{c}}}
is obtained from
\mathbf{B}
by deleting the rows and columns indexed by
I
.
For a subset
I\subseteq[n]
, we denote
\pi(I)
as the inversion number of the set
[I^{c},I]
, i.e., the minimal number of pairwise swaps required to sort it in ascending order. Then

p_{[I^{\text{c}},n+I]}=(-1)^{\pi(I)}\prod_{j\in I^{\text{c}}}a_{j}\det(\mathbf{B}_{I^{\text{c}}}).

(15)

Combining (
15
) and (
14
), we obtain the expression

c_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[n]\\
|I|=i\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\pi(I)}\prod_{j\in I}a_{j}\prod_{k\in{I^{\text{c}}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\star})_{k}\cdot\frac{p_{[I^{\text{c}},n+I]}}{p_{[1,\dots,n]}}.

(16)

Thus from (
16
) and (
12
), the stability conditions in Definition
2.2
can be expressed in terms of the Plücker coordinates and
\mathbf{a}
.
The condition
c_{0}>0
is equivalent to feasibility and
p_{[n+1,\dots,2n]}=\det(\mathbf{B})>0
and it is the only among the Routh–Hurwitz conditions that is independent of
\mathbf{a}
.
In projective space, a point
(p_{S})\in\operatorname{Gr}(n,2n)
is defined only up to a nonzero scalar; multiplying any row of
M
by a negative factor flips the sign of every Plücker coordinate
p_{S}
. Feasibility fixes the sign of
p_{[1,\dots,n]}=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}
, which makes the sign of the Plücker coordinates well-defined. The positivity of
p_{[n+1,\dots,2n]}=\det(\mathbf{B})
is then a necessary condition for the steady state to be feasible and stable.

Our interest is now to study the sign patterns of the Plücker coordinates from the sign patterns of
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})

The theoretical framework developed in Sections

k=|\{B\mid\chi(B)=\mbox{?}\}|

n

Let

Propagation
, Grassmann–Plücker relations can determine at most
|U|

|U|
.
In
Branching
, every recursive call assigns a sign to at least one unknown basis, again strictly reducing
|U|

Thus, along any path of the search tree,

Because both

, it was shown in
[
20
]
that exactly four ecological sign patterns are impossible.
Two of these four configurations are symmetric, and our algorithm correctly certifies them to have no valid sign completions, i.e., to be impossible. For the remaining two (asymmetric) configurations, the algorithm returns a single completion each. However, these completions violate additional sign constraints involving smaller minors that are not yet incorporated into our implementation, see Example
5.2
. Hence, all four ecologies identified in
[
20
]
remain impossible.

In addition to the three
n=4
ecological patterns that our code proves to be impossible, we observe a striking difference in the completion counts for the remaining two symmetric patterns also claimed to be impossible in
[
20
]
(Figure
2
). While the code returns only 64 completions for each of these symmetric cases, the corresponding asymmetric patterns yield over 1000 completions. Even when stability and feasibility checks are disabled, and only the chirotope rule is applied, the symmetric ecologies yield only 256 completions, substantially fewer than the generic case. This demonstrates that the chirotope rule alone imposes strong combinatorial restrictions on the sign patterns of Plücker coordinates for symmetric interaction structures. These observations suggest that symmetry in ecological networks introduces intrinsic combinatorial obstructions to coexistence. We anticipate that further analysis, such as incorporating additional stability sign conditions, or examining signs of smaller minors, could rule out these cases completely.

6
Computations via
HypersurfaceRegions.jl

In this section, we study feasibility and stability by computing regions in the complement of a hypersurface arrangement. As discussed in Section
2
, the feasibility and stability conditions of the Lotka–Volterra system are polynomial inequalities in the parameters
(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.
The feasibility conditions require
\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{B})\mathbf{a}>0
, while stability is determined by the positivity of the Routh–Hurwitz polynomials. We consider the hypersurface arrangement that consists of the hypersurfaces defined by these polynomials i.e., the zero sets of the polynomials, together with the coordinate hyperplanes given by
a_{i}=0,b_{ij}=0
for
i,j\in[n]
.

In
[
11
]
, the authors introduced an algorithm that combines Morse theory with numerical solution of ODEs to compute the number of connected regions in a hypersurface arrangement, along with their Euler characteristics and a representative point from each region. This algorithm was later adapted and implemented in Julia as the package
HypersurfaceRegions.jl

[
7
]
.
We use this package to compute possible ecologies for
n=2,3,4
. Related work using methods from numerical algebraic geometry has recently appeared in
[
10
]
.

To simplify computations, we impose the conditions
b_{ii}=1
for each
i\in[n]
. This assumption is without loss of generality since we always have
b_{ii}>0
. Moreover, replacing
\mathbf{B}
by
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{D}
for some diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}
with positive entries rescales the equilibrium
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
to
\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{\star}
maintaining its positivity, and transforms the corresponding Jacobian
J_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}
in (
4
) to
\mathbf{D}^{-1}J_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{D}
, which preserves its eigenvalues.

We start with the case
n=2
. Without loss of generality, we set
b_{11}=b_{22}=1
. Thus, there remain four free parameters, viz.,
a_{1},a_{2},b_{12},b_{21}
. The feasibility and stability conditions are then expressed by the positivity of the polynomials
a_{1}-a_{2}b_{12}
,
a_{2}-a_{1}b_{21}
,
a_{1}+a_{2}-a_{1}b_{21}-a_{2}b_{12}
,
1-b_{12}b_{21}
. Using
HypersurfaceRegions.jl
, we compute the regions of the hypersurface arrangement defined by these four polynomials along with the coordinate hyperplanes given by
a_{1}=0
,
a_{2}=0
,
b_{12}=0
, and
b_{21}=0
. There are 72 regions in total, each contractible.

Next is the case
n=3
. Without loss of generality, we set
b_{11}=b_{22}=b_{33}=1
, leaving nine free variables:
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
and
b_{12},b_{13},b_{21},b_{23},b_{31},b_{32}
.
From the feasibility and stability conditions, we obtain six polynomial constraints of degrees
3,3,3,3,8,3
. In principle, one could compute the regions defined by the hypersurface arrangement formed by these six polynomials together with the nine coordinate hyperplanes defined by the free variables. However, the computational complexity of this task is prohibitive: the number of critical points in these arrangements typically scales as
O(d^{n})
, where
d
is the total degree of the hypersurfaces and
n
is the number of variables. In our setting, this leads to an estimated
O(10^{12})
critical points, beyond current computational limits. To circumvent this difficulty, we instead fix the equilibrium values
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
to be
(1,1,1)
. Under this assumption, feasibility is automatically satisfied, and our free variables reduce to
b_{12},b_{13},b_{21},b_{23},b_{31},b_{32}
, with
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{\star}
. We then only need to consider the three polynomial constraints arising from stability conditions. Using the package
HypersurfaceRegions.jl
, we compute these regions in approximately three minutes, obtaining 1207 distinct regions. Among these, 343 regions correspond to feasible and stable solutions. In
[
20
]
, the authors report six
irreducible ecologies
(for which any two-species subecology is impossible or trivial), for which feasible and stable steady states are found very low probability under random sampling. In our computations, we realize all six irreducible ecologies, which together account for 30 out of the 343 feasible stable regions. This highlights the benefit of our deterministic region-computation approach over traditional random sampling methods. Finally we turn to the case
n=4
. As in the previous cases, we set the diagonal entries
b_{11}=b_{22}=b_{33}=b_{44}=1
, leaving 16 free variables: the four entries of
\mathbf{a}
and the 12 off-diagonal entries of the matrix
\mathbf{B}
. Our hypersurface arrangement consists of eight polynomial constraints arising from the feasibility and stability conditions, together with 16 hyperplanes corresponding to the variables. To reduce complexity, we first fix the equilibrium
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
to a positive vector, as in the case
n=3
. However, even after this reduction, the remaining
12
free parameters make a complete region computation infeasible. We therefore introduce an additional simplification: we fix the signs of several off-diagonal entries of
\mathbf{B}
in advance. Rather than computing all regions of the arrangement, we restrict attention to verifying the realizability of specific sign patterns. The computation performed by
HypersurfaceRegions.jl
proceeds in two stages: First, it computes critical points of a suitable rational function chosen so that each region of the arrangement contains at least one critical point. Second, it tracks paths between these critical points to determine region membership. For the task of verifying the possibility of a particular sign pattern, we can restrict the computation to the first step.
We thus randomly select a positive
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
, randomly choose and fix four off-diagonal entries of
\mathbf{B}
with predetermined signs, and then numerically compute critical points in one minute using numerical algebraic geometry.

[+,+,-,-,\,+,+,+,+,\,+,+,+,+,\,-,+,+,-].
