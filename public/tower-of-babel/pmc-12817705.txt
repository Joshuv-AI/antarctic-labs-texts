Lessons learned from responses to COVID-19 pandemic in rural, Indigenous, and isolated populations in the US and India: a qualitative study
Authors: Austin Henderson, Tenzin Namdul, Gary Ferguson, Sara London, Richard MacLehose, Dedra Buchwald
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRural, Indigenous, and isolated communities faced unique challenges and opportunities during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. Identifying lessons from these communities, which are rarely studied, is crucial for improving future crisis responses similar populations. This study aims to inform similar communities by centralizing lessons learned and improving preparedness for future health crises.MethodsWe examined three geographically and culturally distinct self-governing communities: a Tibetan Buddhist monastic community in southern India, Alaska Native communities in Interior Alaska, and the Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe in South Dakota. Through semi-structured interviews conducted from 2021 to 2022 with community members working in healthcare, education, law enforcement, and governance, we analyzed the policies implemented, the decision-making processes behind them, and the psychological experiences of community members during the pandemic.ResultsThe themes identified from the interviews included the creative mobilization of internal resources amid limited external support, such as community members assuming new roles like quarantine enforcement and using shared culture as a resource to change health behaviors. All three communities implemented travel restrictions. Reported stress on a personal and community level was high, particularly due to deaths caused by the pandemic.ConclusionsRural, Indigenous, and isolated communities responded to the COVID-19 pandemic with a range of strategies, including strict shutdowns, travel restrictions, community-driven support, and traditional healing practices. Despite demonstrating resilience and adaptability, they faced challenges such as resource limitations, enforcement difficulties, and community stress, highlighting the need for ongoing support and tailored approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13719-0.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic inflicted substantial socio-economic and human costs worldwide [1], challenging governments to respond rapidly with a wide variety of public health policies [2], One general framework for understanding these varied policies is that state responses were a function of the ability of governments to identify and implement appropriate public health policies, abilities which were in turn due to factors such as experience with prior pandemics, the structure of their governments, and availability of material and non-material resources such as expertise [3]. Within this framework in which states have imperfect knowledge of what policies they should pursue, examining the experiences of similar political bodies can be useful for helping governments improve their response to future health crises. While much research has been done on the public health response of countries like those of India [4, 5] and the United States [6, 7], at every stage of the COVID-19 pandemic, comparatively little research has examined smaller political bodies within those countries, particularly rural, Indigenous, and isolated (RII) communities, revealing a need for better understanding of what such communities can learn from each other.

For the purposes of this study, we define that the key distinguishing characteristics of RII communities are that they are self-governing sovereign institutions with unique powers and responsibilities, widely shared cultural beliefs and practices within each community, distance from major urban centers, small populations, and low wealth. These unique factors influenced how these communities experienced the COVID-19 pandemic, including what public health policies were implemented and the manner of their implementation. Previous research has found that such communities in disparate locations, including rural Zimbabwe [8] and northern India [9] had a heightened need to be self-reliant during the pandemic, emphasizing the importance of improving local decision-making in this context.

This study examined three highly geographically and ethnically diverse communities, including a Tibetan Buddhist Monastic community in South India, an Alaska Native community, and the Cheyenne River Sioux Nation. Rather than focus on the particularities of any one community, we strove to identify broader themes that may be more applicable to RII communities. We conducted a qualitative study using primary source semi-structured interviews with leaders and members of these communities, including healthcare workers, educators, and individuals whose jobs changed in response to the conditions of the pandemic. We examined common themes that these communities experienced and public health policies that they implemented in response to the pandemic with the aim of producing a resource for similar communities facing future public health crises.

Methods

Design

We conducted 15 semi-structured interviews in three diverse communities based on longstanding cultural and research connections. One author (TN), a medical anthropologist and a Tibetan Medicine doctor, interviewed 6 members of the Tibetan community in the Drepung Loseling monastery in southern India. Another author (GF), who is Unangax̂ (Aleut) and has worked extensively in the CATG region, interviewed 4 Indigenous community members residing in XX communities in the Arctic. Lastly, a researcher deeply embedded in American Indian health (AH) interviewed 5 members of the Cheyenne River Sioux Nation.

Setting

Tibetan monastic community

The Doeguling Tibetan Settlement is one of the largest Tibetan refugee settlements and is located in Mundgod in Karnataka state of South India. The settlement is one of the main cultural repositories of Tibetan life in India. This settlement, ~ 250 miles from Bengaluru, was established in 1966 on 4,000 acres of land allocated by the Government of Karnataka. Today, it contains one of India’s largest concentrations of Tibetan refugees, exceeding 16,000 Tibetans [10]. The settlement contains a unique integration of a Tibetan monastic school system and Tibetan medical clinics, surrounded by a large lay Tibetan community – a settlement structure similar to many of the great population centers in Tibetan civilization in pre-1959 Tibet. Drepung Monastic School was initially established in Tibet in 1416 and was re-established in India in 1969. Drepung Monastery is one of the key cultural centers in the settlement and houses 4,500 monks. Though affiliated with the Tibetan Central Administration (de facto Tibetan Government-in-Exile), the monastery is largely autonomous in its administration. It provides comprehensive education in Buddhist epistemology, philosophy, and psychology. This study was conducted within Drepung Loseling monastic community.

Council of Athabascan Tribal Governments

The Council of Athabascan Tribal Governments (CATG) is a regional organization representing nine federally recognized Tribes in the interior of Alaska. Founded in 1978, CATG’s is a collective voice in advocating for resources, policy changes, and services to promote the social, cultural, economic, and political well-being of its member Tribes. This includes advocating for Native rights, fostering self-determination, and addressing the unique needs of its members that are often underrepresented or overlooked in state and federal policies. CATG serves nine member Tribes in the Yukon-Koyukuk and Tanana regions of Interior Alaska that share common Athabascan cultural and historical ties, primarily based on the Athabascan language and subsistence lifestyles. Member Tribes include Koyukuk Tribal Council, Rampart Village Council, Huslia Tribal Council, Allakaket Tribal Council, Nikolai Tribal Council, Grayling Tribal Council, Shageluk Tribal Council, McGrath Tribal Council, and Kaltag Tribal Council. These CATG Tribes are located in some of the most remote parts of Alaska, with few roads connecting them to larger population centers. Like many rural Indigenous communities, they face challenges such as persistent poverty, with much of the population living below the federal poverty line, and a reliance on culturally-centered seasonal work, such as subsistence hunting and fishing. The communities are close-knit and inter-related with strong family and cultural ties.

Cheyenne River Sioux Nation

Approximately 8,000 enrolled Tribal members of Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe (CRST) reside on the Cheyenne River Indian Reservation in South Dakota. At 4,266.987 square miles, the reservation is the fourth-largest Indian reservation in the United States. The economic conditions of the CRST are poor, with an estimated 26.4% unemployment rate, a per capita income of $14,706 per year, and poverty rate of 35.4%, all substantially worse than state and national averages [14]. The Tribe is a federally recognized sovereign entity that has meaningful governmental relations with other Tribal Nations, the state of South Dakota, and the United States Government. Leadership is comprised of a 15-member Tribal Council and 4-member elected Executive Committee. The Tribe has maintained a strong sense of tradition, which has been an animating force in organizing against other contemporary challenges, such as the Dakota Access Pipeline [11].

Recruitment and participants

Having either lived in or worked with these communities previously, we used existing contacts to reach out to potential study participants. Then, we built upon these contacts using “friendship pyramiding and snowball sampling” techniques [15] to obtain sample groups that were as varied as possible. While reaching out to potential participants, we sent a fact sheet that clearly explained the goal and purpose of our study, as well as set expectations for what study participation would entail (see Appendix A.2). If an individual was interested, a meeting time was scheduled. Participants received a $100 incentive for participation.

The study population consisted of community leaders, healthcare providers, and community members, with at least one person representing each role from each community. Some participants had multiple roles either simultaneously or at different points during the pandemic. Participants needed to be 18 years of age and capable informed consent. We chose these roles to gather diverse perspectives on the decision-making processes in these communities, how different pandemic policies were implemented, and how those policies were experienced.

Informed consent

Before beginning data collection, we obtained participants’ verbal consent to conduct the interview and disseminate study results. All interview materials were approved by the Washington State University Institutional Review Board (application #18918), and by the Tribal Leadership Council of the Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe. Using an approved script, each interviewer explained the study objectives to participants and verbally obtained consent to record conversations and interviews. We only obtained verbal consent for two reasons. First, written informed consent may be sensitive in the Tibetan and Indigenous community contexts, where individuals be skeptical or suspicious about signing written agreements. Second, verbal rather than written consent facilitated our efforts to ensure the confidentiality of participants. We also emphasized that participants could ask the interviewers to cease recording at any time and refuse to answer questions they felt uncomfortable answering. No participants refused or later revoked consent.

Procedures and data collection instruments

Semi-structured interviews scripts were developed in an iterative process in which drafts were first reviewed by community members not included in the study then modified according to their feedback. Interviews, which lasted 60 to 90 min, were conducted by Zoom or telephone and digitally recorded, accompanied. Interviewers also took detailed notes. Interviews asked questions about the policies implemented, the challenges faced, and how each community made decisions. Interviewers asked each question listed on the script but would re-order them or pose additional follow-up questions as appropriate. The interviews were conducted via Zoom or on a phone call. Interviews were digitally recorded, and interviewers also took detailed notes. Each interview typically lasted 1 h, and none took more than 1.5 h. Full copies of our interview scripts in both English and Tibetan are presented in Appendix A.1.

The interviews were divided into four sections. The first section asked about the interviewees’ involvement in pandemic responses to ascertain their specific involvement (if any) in the community’s response. The second section asked about their perceptions of the community’s policies, strategies, and resources. The third section centered on the process used by the community to decide upon pandemic responses, the types of information community leaders and members acted upon, actions that were helpful in implementing strategies to contain COVID-19, and challenges they faced. The fourth section asked open-ended questions about personal and community-level stressors faced, what the interviewee wished their community would have done differently, any COVID-related incidents that stood out in their mind, and advice they would like to share with communities facing similar challenges. Interviewers asked each question listed on the script but would occasionally change the order in which questions were asked and asked specific follow-up questions as appropriate. The interviews were conducted via Zoom or phone call. Interviews were digitally recorded, and interviewers also took detailed notes. Each interview typically lasted 1 h, and none took more than 1.5 h.

Data management and monitoring

Semi-structured interviews were recorded on digital voice recorders and transcribed into text documents. Voice recordings were erased once transcriptions were completed. Transcriptions from interviews were coded to ensure anonymity, with the code key stored in a password-protected file on the computer. Written notes were stored in Microsoft Word documents.

Material from field notes, interviews, and image-elicitations were maintained in Microsoft Word and Excel documents. These files were stored on the computers of each interviewer. Any notes and transcriptions from interviews and image elicitations were coded with pseudonyms to ensure informant anonymity, with the code key stored in a password-protected file on the computer. Details from interviews (such as names and place of origin) that might reveal the informant’s identity were altered to protect the participants’ identity.

Data analysis

The study data generated was comprised of handwritten notes, and recorded, transcribed, and de-identified interviews. Data analysis centered on how the communities responded to the COVID-19 pandemic, including strategies devised and implemented, challenges faced, resources used, emphasizing any unique, common, and culturally informed themes. The lead analyst used thematic analysis; deductive codes were derived from the research questions and interview protocol and inductive codes applied open coding, axial coding, then selection of themes [12, 13]. SL, an experienced qualitative researcher, conducted the analysis using the Dedoose software [14]. We highlight essential themes with exemplary quotes in the results section.

Results

Preventing transmission: shutdowns, travel restrictions, quarantines and curfews

Shutdowns were implemented in all three sites, but the specifics of implementation varied. Shutdowns involved mandatory closing of schools, businesses, community centers, and other public meetings, restrictions on the number of people who could gather in any given place, and sudden decreases in access to healthcare facilities for non-healthcare workers. All sites also implemented travel restrictions that shared common features. Community-level travel restrictions prohibited the entrance of outsiders and also did not allow community members to enter or leave for weeks or months without special dispensation. Those restrictions were enforced by physical checkpoints on roads and often staffed by community members who had worked in non-law enforcement occupations prior to the pandemic. These individuals were authorized to handle reports of individuals who broke quarantine measures, with the authority to fine, jail, and/or quarantine those who did not adhere to regulations. It is also worth noting that receipt of vaccination often allowed members to no longer be subject to stringent quarantine and travel restrictions.

In contrast, although the monasteries articulated a strict policy to shut down the whole monastery campus, enforcement of movement restrictions was voluntary. In general, monks were motivated to protect the vulnerable older members of the monastic population. For instance, a COVID-19 Committee member shared his and others concern for older monks.I am usually calm, but I got nervous and even scared when I felt like we might not be able to protect our older monks who are very important for all of us. These older monks are the beholder of Buddhist culture and wisdom. I also thought about my teacher. Moreover, I think it is important to think about your community rather than just yourself in a situation like this. (Tibetan monastery)

So, from March to July, it was pretty well shut down. One notable policy was the implementation of mandatory quarantines when entering the community, a program integrated with other quarantine mandates for those who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2. This involved, in the case of CRST, also creating specific quarantine areas for community members so they would not have to live with and potentially expose household members. One participant from the Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe reflected on their quarantine strategy, mentioning that they provided care and supplies while their community members isolated themselves.…if they had symptoms or close contact or tested positive, then they would be given the option to stay at home, depending on their severity of their symptoms. Then, they would have to quarantine for that long, for about 14 days, 10 to 14 days. They would have CHRs [Community Health Representatives, a new role created to support quarantine enforcement and support of those quarantined] come and check, our community health representatives, come and check their temperature, and bring them supplies or just necessities that would keep them at their house, like food and hygiene products and stuff like that. Mostly everyone didn’t mind doing that, because the Tribe gave paid leave for people who did test positive to stay at home and quarantine. Other than that, then you would have to check into a shelter and stay at one of the community shelters, or you would have to go to jail and sit in a quarantine cell by yourself. (Cheyenne River Sioux)

COVID-19 vaccines: using culture and incentives to increase uptake

A culturally-congruent desire to protect their Elders was a driving motivation of vaccination across all sites. They prioritized vaccinating Elders, used the example set by elders as a positive model, and promoted the vaccine by spreading the message that protecting the Elders in their community was essential, both from SARS-CoV-2 itself and from the isolation resulting from stringent quarantine measures. Some communities vaccinated Elders and community leaders first to show the importance of community vaccinations. An Alaska Native participant shared that younger people were willing to be vaccinated because of the example set by elders.Yeah, they’ve seen our elders do it. So, they said that they need to do it as well to protect our elders. Our culture really, really thrives on protecting our elders. When COVID-19 broke out our main goal was to protect them… We lost a lot of them, not from COVID, but I think because being isolated, they’re not used to that. (Alaska)

COVID-19 testing: easy access and mobile testing

All communities took a direct role in increasing access to testing, thus, participants from all communities reported that COVID-19 testing was widely and easily available. To prevent spread in their clinics, COVID-19 testing spots was designated in the community and healthcare workers administered COVID-19 tests to people in their cars. Other outreach strategies included distributing flyers and information about vaccines to their community members and personal telephone calls to promote vaccinations. One participant from Alaska described a multi-pronged approach to testing:We [provided COVID-19 tests at homes] for the elders …we took swabs and brought [results] back. There’s no reason for them to get out of their house and get, probably even be around somebody that is positive. So we didn’t want to take that chance. But everybody else, we told them stay in their vehicles and we took the COVID test outside to them. (Alaska)

Leadership, Decision-making, and communication

Leadership during the pandemic came from many sources, including new decision-making bodies created to respond to the crisis. Across all three sites, healthcare workers and administrators were prominent in decision-making bodies; in Alaska and South Dakota, not surprisingly, Tribal chiefs were notable leaders, and in India, Abbotts and teachers played key roles in decisions. A common thread was these leaders frequently met to discuss the number of COVID-19 positive cases, containment goals, and practical strategies to address the community’s needs, maintain communication, and ensure effective leadership. The formation of a COVID-19 Committee occurred quickly in the Tibetan community.COVID-19 started to spread in India in early March of 2020. On March 17, 2020, I reached out to our monastery disciplinary head, administration board members, and hospital director to form a committee and to meet to discuss how best to prepare ourselves in case COVID-19 spreads around our area. We had the meeting every month to update on everything. (Tibetan Monastery)

In Alaska Native and CRST communities, doctors, psychiatrists, and Tribal leaders often shared updates and information on the radio. Direct communication kept community members appraised of the local COVID-19 situation and what policy changes were being implemented, as exemplified in an Alaskan community:We had daily radio shows. It was at a certain hour, and they will tell us exactly what’s going on, the CDC guidelines. I’d watch the news every morning and see how far, how much it is spreading in the United States, how close it’s getting to us. (Alaska)

Resources: shortages in the medical systems, sufficient personal protective equipment (PPE) and food

Most shortages reported by participants occurred within the healthcare system, including needing new or different doctors, especially those with expertise in treating critically ill patients, more healthcare providers such as nurses and physician assistants, more beds in their housing and quarantine facilities, and specific medical supplies. One Tibetan interviewee felt well-supported, only lacking high-level medical professionals.I think we were able to get all the resources that we need to prepare ourselves. I think our “emergency team” worked hard to get all the necessary things arranged – necessary items and PPE, especially masks because we distributed lots of masks among our monks as well as local Tibetans in the settlement. We did have problem of not having a doctor at our monastery hospital at one point of time. But we have a very efficient nurses and they worked hard with our volunteer group. We managed to get a doctor after a few months and that was good. (Tibetan Monastery)

These Tibetan herbal medicines were all very helpful. (Tibetan Monastery)

Challenges

Participants from the United States discussed challenges they faced during the pandemic. These included difficulty enforcing quarantines, disrespect by non-Natives for Tribal sovereignty and policies, drug and alcohol abuse hindering adherence to safety practices, and the stigmas of both acquiring COVID-19 and adhering to COVID-19 protective strategies.

Advice

Participants shared advice they believed should be communicated to other communities about effective ways to address a pandemic. The advice included having a unified community to address their needs together and efficiently. They cited prioritizing preventative measures, preparing for a pandemic, encouraging volunteering, encouraging caring for their communities and for their patients, and ensuring their community takes disease seriously and heeds their leaders. A Tibetan monk noted the following.

I think one most important thing was despite all challenges and difficulties we faced in serving our community by providing necessary items, we stayed focused on our goal and persevere to continue our work. And when we see how much we were able to help, it felt good. (Tibetan Monastery)

Participants were asked if they would change anything with their strategies in addressing the pandemic. They spoke about the need for more funding and better adherence to social distancing, masking, and protective processes. Other needs were better doctors, improved screening of people at high-risk for infection and mental health issues, more widespread testing in the community, and more community education on COVID-19 protection.

Discussion

We investigated the experiences of geographically and culturally diverse RII communities during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly their shared challenges and opportunities. The communities studied included a Tibetan Buddhist monastic community in southern India, Indigenous communities in the Arctic, and the Cheyenne River Sioux Nation. Despite their diversity, common themes emerged in their COVID-19 experiences, which we hope may be valuable to similar communities in managing future public health crises.

The participants described various policies and strategies they adopted during the pandemic to contain the spread of SARS-CoV-2. As noted by others, all of these strategies were adopted in some form in other communities [3], but the shared characteristics of our communities helped them identify the best strategies to implement for their local capacity and cultural context. One shared strategy was the implementation of shutdowns and travel restrictions that changed dynamically in response to local conditions in ways that may not be feasible in larger, less geographically remote locations. For instance, in the Alaska Native communities, a shutdown was enforced once the first local COVID-19 case was reported, restricting movement and gatherings. In the Cheyenne River Sioux community, curfew policies changed in direct response to local transmission rates.

Each community had some form of quarantine as part of a travel restriction regime or as a direct result of a person’s positive COVID-19 test. While involuntary quarantines were sometimes quite contentious and unpopular, they are a prime example of a policy with public health value but substantial costs to individuals and the political system that enforces them. Hence, local decision-makers must judiciously weigh the costs and benefits. Communities rapidly created systems to support the quarantines, including housing and a steady supply of food for quarantined people, showing the feasibility of such policies, at least for some period of time, in low-resource environments.

Communities prioritized and tailored creative strategies to increase vaccination rates based on their local and cultural values and resources. The importance of protecting elders was a particularly salient touchpoint shared by all three communities. By emphasizing that vaccination was not simply an act to protect the recipient but also to minimize elders’ risks of infection and need for isolation, these communities were able to leverage cultural values as a strength. Other American Indian Tribes combined Tribal ceremonies with vaccination drives, emphasizing that there are myriad ways which culture can be creatively leverage [15]. Addressing the challenges of rural and low-density living, especially when travel may be difficult, availability of tests and vaccines was improved by minimizing test and vaccine administration in busy clinics. One important policy was the use of substantial monetary incentives to encourage vaccination. While evidence in other populations suggests that incentives for COVID-19 vaccines are effective [16] and do not cause unintended negative consequences [17]. the incentives used by the community in our study were orders of magnitude larger and purportedly effective in convincing opponents to vaccinations to become vaccinated. As vaccination remains a contentious topic, policies to increase vaccination rates that can feasibly be implemented in remote settings should be further investigated.

The role of strong leadership in handling the pandemic was central in all three communities. Regular meetings among community leaders helped coordinate strategies and maintain communication. Participants in all communities noted efforts by leaders were essential in addressing needs and ensuring effective management of the pandemic. In addition, a notable, but not unexpected, finding was that RII communities could not always rely upon larger governmental bodies, including those nearby, for aid. The less a community can count on outside help, the more it must pivot towards self-help and maximize use of its internal resources. Broadly, the RII communities we studied did not report dramatic shortages of resources such as food and PPE, but did experience shortages of medical supplies, and particularly, medical expertise, such as doctors. During crises like the COVID-19 pandemic, in which demand for healthcare workers is high everywhere, it may not be feasible for RII communities to recruit additional medical personnel. However, online training for healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic and other technologies may present opportunities for RII communities to increase internal medical capacity, an approach that has been demonstrated to be feasible and effective [18].

Our research contributes to understanding how religion played a role in community responses to the COVID-19 pandemic. Qualitative interviews with religious leaders from Christian, Catholic, Jewish, and Muslim communities across the United States has documented how faith communities both adopted their practices to meet COVID-19 health guidelines and leveraged their shared faith to encourage COVID-19 responsible behavior [19]. Others have documented how Buddhist monks in northern India took it upon themselves to implement many of the policies described in this paper in the absence of aid from state-level sources, in turn legitimizing traditional governance [9]. We also found that traditional practices were greatly by the pandemic affected in all three RII communities. Some ceased religious and cultural practices, while others, like the Tibetan monastic community, integrated traditional healing practices such as herbal teas, meditation, yoga, and breathing practices to maintain health and well-being into response strategies. Together, these observations highlight the cultural costs of the pandemic, as well as the importance of culturally tailored approaches to health crises.

The pandemic caused considerable personal and community stress. Participants expressed fears of transmitting the virus to family members and described divisions within families over safety practices. The inability to hold funerals and limitations on other cultural practices also caused distress. Increased alcohol consumption was noted as a coping mechanism for some community members, reflecting the broader impact of the pandemic on mental health and well-being. Increased alcohol consumption during the pandemic was widely noted in populations in the United States [20].

The findings of this study align with similar research on the response of rural and Indigenous communities to the COVID-19 pandemic. Previous studies have highlighted the unique challenges faced by such communities, including limited access to healthcare, higher rates of preexisting conditions, and the importance of culturally informed approaches. In a study of community perspectives on the response to COVID-19 in rural Zimbabwe, the importance of community engagement and local leadership in the effective management of the pandemic was underscored, especially to address the lack of trusted information sources [8]. This study identified several challenges in enforcing quarantines and travel restrictions, particularly when non-Native individuals disrespected Tribal sovereignty and policies. The stigma associated with contracting COVID-19 further complicated adherence to protective measures. Interviewees emphasized the need for more funding, better adherence to safety practices, improved medical resources, and enhanced education about COVID-19 protection and pandemic mitigation strategies.

Advice to similar communities centered on thoughtfully preparing for future pandemics. This included better material readiness (e.g. more medical supplies) but also preparedness to handle unforeseen consequences of policies enacted in the early pandemic, particularly those related to mental health. A common piece of advice was successful responses to the pandemic were attributable to leveraging communities’ unique strengths and cultures.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of 15 participants is small and may not capture the full diversity of experiences and responses within each community. Future research could benefit from a larger and more varied sample to gather perspectives, particularly from those who did not play a role in healthcare and policymaking. Second, because we used a snowball sampling approach, we may have biased our findings towards perspectives of individuals who are open to participating in a research project, and may not reflect options and perspectives of other community members. Third, though our goal was to identify themes that would be useful to other RII communities, we only involved three such communities, thus, our findings do not present a comprehensive view of such themes. Fourth, this study captures a snapshot of the community responses during a specific period of the pandemic. As the consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic evolve, additional lessons about the long-term management of associated health conditions will require new and innovative approaches. Fifth, and relatedly, we conducted interviews over a year after the pandemic began, meaning that participants may have misremembered or forgotten some important details. Sixth, participants may have experienced social desirability bias, and provided answers that they thought the researchers would like to hear. We did not guide participants towards providing any specific answers during our interviews, but nevertheless, participants may have preferred to give answers that, for instance, cast their community in a positive light. Seventh, because we did not collect data on COVID-19 related outcomes in these communities, we are not able to offer a quantitative assessment of the COVID-19 responses. Future research would strongly benefit from including a quantitative component which could directly assess which policies worked.

Conclusion

The response to the COVID-19 pandemic by RII communities was characterized by a range of strategies, from strict shutdowns and travel restrictions to community-driven support and traditional healing practices. These communities demonstrated resilience and adaptability, leveraging local leadership and resources to manage the crisis effectively. However, the challenges they faced, including resource limitations, enforcement difficulties, and personal and community stress, highlight the need for ongoing support and tailored approaches. Future research should build on these findings, expanding the scope and depth of study to ensure comprehensive understanding and improved preparedness for future health crises. This study aims to inform and support similar communities in their response to current and future pandemics, fostering a more resilient and informed public health landscape by centralizing and sharing the lessons learned from these communities.
