User perceptions of an artificial intelligence-based computer-aided detection system in colonoscopy: a single-center exploratory survey study
Authors: Seon Yeong Ko, Byung Chang Kim, Chang Won Hong, Bun Kim, Kyung Su Han, Dae Kyung Sohn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeArtificial intelligence (AI)-based computer-aided detection (AI-CADe) systems can improve adenoma detection during colonoscopy. However, successful clinical implementation depends on diagnostic performance and user acceptance, usability, and workflow integration. This study evaluated user perceptions before and after the clinical implementation of AI-CADe.MethodsThis single-center, repeated, cross-sectional descriptive survey evaluated the endoscopy unit staff perceptions of AI-CADe at the National Cancer Center. Two anonymous online surveys were administered before and 1 month after the system installation. The surveys assessed the perceived diagnostic benefits, workflow impact, user satisfaction, concerns regarding false-positive detection, dependence on AI, and factors influencing acceptance and continued use. The analyses were descriptive and exploratory.ResultsTwenty-nine and 25 participants completed the pre- and post-installation surveys, respectively. Positive responses regarding the interest in AI-CADe, perceived adenoma detection rate improvement, expected lesion removal, patient satisfaction, procedural satisfaction, and workflow impact were reported in both survey phases. Concerns regarding overdetection, unnecessary biopsies, and increased dependence on AI were also reported. Accuracy and sensitivity were the most frequently selected adoption factors, followed by cost and false-positive rates. Open-ended feedback included positive comments regarding lesion recognition, procedural support, and limitations related to repeated alerts, false-positive alarms, delayed detection, and system responsiveness.ConclusionEndoscopy unit staff members showed favorable perceptions of AI-CADe and recognized its potential value in colonoscopy practice. However, concerns regarding false-positive detection, workflow integration, and dependence on AI indicate the need for user-centered optimization and long-term real-world evaluation.Keywords: Colonoscopy, Artificial intelligence, Colorectal neoplasms, Early detection of cancer, Computer-assisted diagnosis

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent malignancy worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality [1]. Early detection and endoscopic removal of premalignant colorectal lesions effectively reduce CRC incidence and CRC-related deaths [2]. Colonoscopy is crucial in the diagnosis and prevention of CRC and is regarded as the gold standard screening and surveillance modality.

However, colonoscopy is operator-dependent, potentially overlooking clinically relevant lesions. Lesion miss rates are influenced by multiple factors, including endoscopist experience, bowel preparation quality, withdrawal technique, and lesion-specific characteristics, such as size, morphology, and anatomical location. Several landmark studies have demonstrated that adenomas are frequently missed even during back-to-back colonoscopies performed by experienced endoscopists [3]. A meta-analysis of tandem colonoscopy studies has reported an average adenoma miss rate of approximately 22%, underscoring the intrinsic limitations of visual detection alone [4]. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown that adenoma miss rates are associated with lesion size, proximal colon location, and non-polypoid morphology, highlighting persistent diagnostic gaps even in high-quality examinations [5].

Adenoma detection rate (ADR) has emerged as a key quality indicator for colonoscopy and is strongly associated with long-term clinical outcomes. Large population-based studies have demonstrated that higher ADRs are associated with a lower risk of interval CRC and CRC-related mortality [6,7]. Consequently, improving ADR is prioritized to enhance colonoscopy quality and patient outcomes. However, traditional approaches for improving ADR, such as training, audits, feedback, and procedural standardization, have shown variable effectiveness and may be limited by human factors and interoperator variability [8].

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning-based image recognition, have enabled the development of real-time computer-aided detection (CADe) systems for colonoscopy. These systems continuously analyze endoscopic video streams and automatically highlight suspected lesions, thereby functioning as secondary observers. Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that AI-assisted CADe significantly improves ADR compared to conventional colonoscopy by increasing the detection of diminutive and subtle lesions [9,10]. Meta-analyses have confirmed that CADe enhances detection performance and reduces interoperator variability, suggesting its potential role in standardizing colonoscopy quality across different practice settings [9–11].

Despite robust evidence supporting the diagnostic efficacy of AI-CADe, its translation into routine clinical practice has been slower than expected. The successful implementation of AI-based systems in real-world settings depends heavily on user acceptance, perceived usability, and integration into existing clinical workflows. Although many endoscopists express strong interest in AI-CADe technology and anticipate its clinical benefits, concerns about increased procedural costs, prolonged examination times, false-positive alerts, and potential overreliance on AI assistance were reported [12]. These concerns may act as barriers to clinical adoption in high-volume endoscopy units, in which efficiency and workflow stability are critical.

Most existing studies evaluating AI-CADe focused more on objective performance metrics, such as ADR, polyp detection rate, or miss rate reduction, than the subjective experiences of end users. User-centered factors, including perceived usefulness, ease of use, workflow impact, and trust in AI recommendations, are increasingly recognized as important determinants of the sustainable implementation of digital health technologies [13]. Moreover, user perceptions may evolve after a hands-on experience with AI-CADe. However, real-world data comparing user perceptions before and after implementation are limited.

Evaluating real-world user perceptions among endoscopy unit staff is important to understand the facilitators and barriers to the clinical adoption of AI-CADe systems. Such evaluations are relevant in tertiary referral centers in which diverse professionals interact with AI systems within complex clinical workflows. Therefore, this study aimed to explore user perceptions of the usability and perceived clinical impact of an AI-CADe system among the endoscopy unit staff at the National Cancer Center. Using structured surveys administered before and after system installation, we assessed the overall interest, perceived benefits, concerns related to workflow and false-positive detection, and factors influencing the acceptance of AI-CADe in routine practice.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This single-center, repeated, cross-sectional descriptive study aimed to explore user perceptions of the usability and perceived clinical impact of an AI-CADe system in a real-world endoscopy unit setting, focusing on user-centered outcomes, including perceived effectiveness, workflow impact, acceptability, and adoption-related concerns.

Study population and procedure

The study involved physicians, fellows, and nurses working in the endoscopy unit of the National Cancer Center. Eligible participants were staff members who received an explanation of the study objectives and procedures and voluntarily provided informed consent. Individuals who declined to participate were excluded, and participants were allowed to withdraw at any time without consequences.

To assess unit-level perceptions before and after early clinical exposure to the AI-CADe system, surveys were administered before system installation (September 1–12, 2025) and 1 month after installation (October 27–31, 2025). The AI-CADe software was formally installed on September 30, 2025. Prior individual exposure to AI-CADe among some staff members before the baseline survey was attributable to participation in clinical research trials or temporary exposure to other research-oriented AI-CADe devices evaluated within the center. Thus, the system installation on September 30, 2025, represented the formal institutional implementation of the dedicated system into routine daily operations across the unit, rather than the initial individual exposure to AI technology for all participants. To ensure complete anonymity, no personal identifiers or tracking codes were collected. Consequently, individual respondents could not be paired between the pre- and post-installation surveys, and the data were analyzed as repeated cross-sectional samples.

Participants were recruited via announcements posted on an institutional bulletin board and website. Access to the online questionnaire was provided via a QR code or web link included in the recruitment materials. Electronic informed consent was obtained before survey initiation.

Questionnaire and data collection

The same questionnaire structure (version 1.0) was used in both survey phases. The questionnaire was newly developed for this study by adapting constructs from established technology acceptance frameworks and relevant literature to reflect the specific clinical context of colonoscopy AI-CADe, and its content validity was reviewed and refined by two expert endoscopists and study investigators.

The survey comprised four conceptual domains: demographics and baseline characteristics; perceived usability, diagnostic benefits, and trust; clinical experience and intention for continued use; and workflow, integration, and adoption factors. Key items evaluated anticipated benefits pre-installation versus perceived actual impacts post-installation, with items regarding procedural satisfaction after use answered in the pre-installation phase specifically by respondents reporting prior hands-on experience with AI-CADe. The complete survey instrument is provided in Supplementary Material 1. Open-ended questions were included to collect additional feedback on positive experiences, inconveniences, and areas requiring improvement.

All survey data were automatically collected using a secure online survey platform. Personal information was minimized and any contact information collected for participant compensation was deleted after compensation was provided.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were primarily descriptive and exploratory. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Survey responses were summarized using predefined response categories and the selected items were dichotomized for summary presentation. Positive responses were defined as “strongly agree” or “agree” for perception-related items, “greatly improved” or “slightly improved” for procedural satisfaction, and “very positive” or “positive” for workflow impact. Concern responses were defined as “very concerned” or “slightly concerned.” Between-survey comparisons were performed using the Fisher exact test based on the original response categories. Considering the exploratory nature of the study and limited sample size, no formal power calculation was performed, and the p-values were interpreted descriptively. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

User perceptions before and after artificial intelligence-based computer-aided detection implementation

Positive responses regarding interest in AI-CADe technology were reported by 25 (86.2%) and 22 participants (88.0%) in the first and second surveys, respectively. Positive responses regarding perceived ADR improvement were reported by 19 (65.5%) and 20 participants (80.0%), respectively. Positive responses regarding the expected increase in lesion removal were reported by 20 (69.0%) and 19 participants (76.0%), respectively. Positive responses regarding patient satisfaction were reported by 21 (72.4%) and 20 participants (80.0%), respectively. Positive responses regarding the workflow impact were reported by 22 (75.9%) and 21 participants (84.0%), respectively. Among the respondents with actual AI-CADe use, improved procedural satisfaction was reported by 9 (47.4%) and 13 participants (59.1%) in the first and second surveys, respectively. The willingness to continue using AI-CADe was reported by 13 (68.4%) and 15 participants (68.2%), respectively. There were no statistically significant differences between the two survey phases (Table 2). The full response distributions for each survey item are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Key concerns and determinants of artificial intelligence-based computer-aided detection adoption

Concern about overdetection or unnecessary biopsy was reported by 17 (58.6%) and 20 participants (80.0%) in the first and second surveys, respectively. Concerns about increased dependence on AI were reported by 19 (65.5%) and 17 participants (68.0%), respectively (Table 2, Fig. 1A). Among the factors considered important in deciding whether to adopt AI-CADe, accuracy and sensitivity were selected by 25 (86.2%) and 21 participants (84.0%) in the first and second surveys, respectively. Cost was selected by 16 (55.2%) and 14 participants (56.0%), respectively. The false-positive rate was selected by 8 (27.6%) and 14 participants (56.0%) in the first and second surveys, respectively. Other adoption-related factors included the user interface, increased procedure time, and patient trust or satisfaction (Fig. 1B).

Open-ended feedback

Open-ended feedback was collected to further describe user experiences with AI-CADe. Positive comments included reports that the system helped highlight subtle lesions, supported mucosal inspection, provided a double-checking effect during real-time observation, and facilitated the recognition of suspicious lesions. The reported limitations include repeated alerts for already identified lesions, false-positive alarms, delayed detection, and occasional system interruptions or overloads.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the endoscopy unit staff’s perceptions of an AI-CADe system before and after its initial clinical implementation. Overall, the respondents demonstrated a high level of interest in AI-CADe and frequently reported positive perceptions of its potential clinical benefits, including improvements in ADR, lesion detection, procedural satisfaction, and workflow impact. Some perception-related items showed higher proportions of positive responses after short-term exposure; however, none of the between-survey differences were statistically significant. Simultaneously, concerns regarding overdetection or unnecessary biopsies and increased dependence on AI were reported in both survey phases. These findings indicate that favorable perceptions of AI-CADe and practical operational concerns were observed in both survey phases.

The overall positive attitude toward AI-CADe is consistent with previous survey studies and clinical trials conducted in different healthcare settings. Multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated that CADe systems can improve ADR by enhancing the detection of diminutive or subtle lesions. Although this study did not evaluate objective colonoscopy outcomes, such as ADR or polyp detection rate, our findings show that the endoscopy unit staff recognized the potential clinical value of AI-CADe during the early phase of real-world implementation. The inclusion of both physicians and nurses provides an overall endoscopy unit-level perspective on AI-CADe implementation. However, because nursing staff constituted the vast majority of respondents (75.9% and 84.0% in the pre- and post-installation surveys, respectively), these findings predominantly reflect nursing staff perceptions and should not be directly generalized to endoscopists or physicians alone.

However, concerns regarding false-positive detections were prominent. Concerns about overdetection or unnecessary biopsies were reported in both survey phases, and the false-positive rate was more frequently selected as an adoption-related factor after system implementation. The open-ended feedback included repeated alerts for previously identified lesions and false-positive alarms. Excessive or redundant alerts may increase the cognitive burden, distract operators, and reduce trust in AI systems over time [14]. These findings suggest that the sustainable implementation of AI-CADe requires optimization for improved alert specificity and reduction of redundant signals.

The workflow impact has emerged as a key consideration. Positive responses regarding workflow impact were frequent in both surveys; however, open-ended feedback described delayed detection, occasional interruptions, and system overload. These findings suggest that the perceived usability of AI-CADe is influenced by diagnostic support, system responsiveness, and integration into routine procedural flow. Optimizing CADe systems to support established endoscopic routines may be important in high-volume endoscopy units.

Concerns regarding increased dependence on AI assistance were reported in both survey phases. This finding reflects the broader issues of automation bias and overreliance on clinical decision-support systems [15]. Although reliance on AI may reflect a growing trust in system performance, it may raise concerns about operator vigilance and skill maintenance. Therefore, the implementation of AI-CADe should be accompanied by appropriate user training, a clear role delineation between human operators and AI assistance, and safeguards to ensure that the AI output remains supportive.

This study had several limitations. First, this study was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size. Additionally, the questionnaire was developed for exploratory purposes without undergoing formal prior psychometric validation or reliability testing, which may limit the standardized measurement of staff perceptions. Second, because the surveys were fully anonymous without individual identifiers, paired longitudinal analysis was not possible, although some individuals likely participated in both survey phases. Third, the short interval between system installation and the second survey may have limited the extent to which the perceptions evolved with sustained use. Fourth, because of the limited sample size and the predominance of nursing staff over physicians, this study was not powered to evaluate differences in perceptions according to professional roles, endoscopy experience, or procedural volume. Fifth, endoscopy experience represented procedural execution for physicians but unit support experience for nurses, which limits direct functional comparability of this variable across roles. Finally, this study focused on subjective user perceptions and did not directly evaluate objective clinical outcomes, such as ADR, polyp detection rate, procedure time, or post-colonoscopy CRC.

However, this study provided real-world insights into the early user experience of AI-CADe implementation in a tertiary referral endoscopy unit. By describing the perceived clinical benefits and concerns, our findings indicate that technical performance alone may be insufficient for successful clinical adoption. User-centered design, workflow integration, targeted training, and iterative feedback mechanisms are important components for sustainable AI-CADe implementation.

Overall, favorable perceptions of AI-CADe technology and recognition of its potential value for supporting procedural quality were observed in both survey phases. However, concerns regarding false-positive detection, workflow integration, and dependence on AI were also reported, indicating the need for balanced optimization that addresses both algorithmic accuracy and practical usability. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up periods, and the integration of objective clinical, workflow, and economic outcomes are warranted to define the role of AI-CADe in routine colonoscopy practice more comprehensively.
