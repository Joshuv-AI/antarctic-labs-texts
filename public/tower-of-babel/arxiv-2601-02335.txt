Polynomial-order oscillations in geometric discrepancy
Thomas Beretti

Abstract
Let C ⊂ ℝ 2 C\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} be a convex body, and let 𝒫 ⊂ [ 0 , 1 ) 2 \mathcal{P}\subset[0,1)^{2} be a set of N N points. The discrepancy of 𝒫 \mathcal{P} with respect to C C is defined as 𝒟 ⁡ ( 𝒫 , C ) = ∑ 𝐩 ∈ 𝒫 ∑ 𝐧 ∈ ℤ 2 𝟙 C ​ ( 𝐩 + 𝐧 ) − N ​ | C | . \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\,C)=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{C}(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{n})-N|C|. A standard approach for estimating how a point distribution deviates from uniformity is to average the latter quantity over a family of sets; in particular, when considering quadratic averages over translations and dilations, one obtains 𝒟 2 ( 𝒫 , C ) = ∫ 0 1 ∫ [ 0 , 1 ) 2 | 𝒟 ( 𝒫 , 𝝉 + δ C ) | 2 d 𝝉 d δ . \mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{[0,1)^{2}}\left|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta C)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\delta. This paper concerns the behaviour of optimal homothetic quadratic discrepancy inf # ​ 𝒫 = N 𝒟 2 ​ ( 𝒫 , C ) as N → + ∞ . \inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\quad\text{as}\quad N\to+\infty. Beck and Chen [ 1 ] showed that the optimal h.q.d. of convex polygons has an order of growth of log ⁡ N \log N . More recently, Brandolini and Travaglini [ 6 ] showed that the optimal h.q.d. of planar convex bodies has an order of growth of N 1 / 2 N^{1/2} if their boundary is 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} , and of N 2 / 5 N^{2/5} if their boundary is only piecewise- 𝒞 2 \mathcal{C}^{2} and not polygonal. We show that, in general, no order of growth is required. First, by an implicit geometric construction, we show that one can obtain prescribed oscillations from a logarithmic order of log ⁡ N \log N to polynomial orders of N 2 / 5 N^{2/5} and N 1 / 2 N^{1/2} , and vice versa. Secondly, by using Fourier-analytic methods, we show that prescribed polynomial-order oscillations in the range N α N^{\alpha} with α ∈ ( 2 / 5 , 1 / 2 ) \alpha\in(2/5,1/2) are achievable. These results provide the first examples of sets for which an optimal averaged discrepancy does not admit a single order of growth, and to the best of our knowledge, a similar phenomenon has not been reported before in the literature.



1
Introduction

We define the two-dimensional torus as
\mathbb{T}^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
and we identify
[0,1)^{2}
with
\mathbb{T}^{2}
via the quotient map
\boldsymbol{\tau}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\tau}+\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Let
C
be a planar convex body (that is, a compact convex set with non-empty interior), and let
\mathds{1}_{C}
stand for its characteristic (indicator) function. Consider the periodization functional
{\mathfrak{P}}\colon L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
defined by

{\mathfrak{P}}\{\mathds{1}_{C}\}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{C}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n}).

The theory of irregularities of distribution, often referred to as discrepancy theory, quantifies how point distributions in a space deviate from uniformity (see
[
8
]
and
[
5
]
for a general overview and applications). In geometric discrepancy theory, historically initiated by Roth’s seminal work
[
11
]
, one measures such a difference using sets as test functions and compares the number of points that fall within them with the expected value. We introduce the following notions of discrepancy.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body, and let
\mathcal{P}_{N}\subset\mathbb{T}^{2}
be a set of
N
points. We define the
discrepancy
of
\mathcal{P}_{N}
with respect to
C
as

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{N},\,C)=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}}{\mathfrak{P}}\{\mathds{1}_{C}\}(\mathbf{p})-N|C|.

(1.1)

Let
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in[0,1)^{2}
be a translation parameter and
\delta\geq 0
be a dilation parameter. We define the
homothetic quadratic discrepancy
(in short,
h.q.d.
) of
\mathcal{P}_{N}
with respect to
C
as

\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P}_{N},\,C)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P}_{N},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta C)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\delta,

(1.2)

where
\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}
is the normalised Haar measure on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and
\,\mathrm{d}\delta
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
.

It is a classic question in the theory of irregularities of distribution to determine the optimal behaviour of the
h.q.d.
as
N
(the number of samples per unit square) goes to infinity. In other words, we are concerned with the quantity

\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\quad\text{as}\quad N\to+\infty.

Surprisingly, the qualitative behaviour of this quantity depends strongly on the geometry of
C
: for instance, when
C
is the unit square one obtains a logarithmic order of growth, while when
C
is the unit disk one obtains a polynomial order. We now present sharp estimates for more general bodies, but first, we introduce suitable notation for orders of growth.

Let
f
and
g
be two functions defined on the same domain, and let
q
be an object; we write
f\lesssim_{q}g
to indicate that there exists a positive value
c
, that may depend on
q
, such that
f\leq cg
in the whole domain. If we omit
q
in the subscript, then
c
is a positive constant. If the
\lesssim_{q}
(or
\lesssim
) holds in both senses, then we write
f\approx_{q}g
(or
f\approx g
).

First, we state a result of Beck and Chen
[
1
]
on the optimal
h.q.d.
with respect to polygons.

Theorem 1.2

(Beck-Chen)
.

Let
C
be a convex polygon. Then, it holds

\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\approx_{C}\log N.

Secondly, we present a recent result of Brandolini and Travaglini
[
6
]
that, notably, recovers a polynomial order of growth across an interval of exponents.

Theorem 1.3

(Brandolini-Travaglini)
.

Let
C
be a planar convex body. If the boundary of
C
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
(regardless of curvature), then it holds

\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\approx_{C}N^{1/2}.

If
C
is not a polygon and its boundary is piecewise-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
with at least one corner, then it holds

\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\approx_{C}N^{2/5}.

Moreover, for
\alpha\in(2/5,1/2)
, there exists a planar convex body
C_{\alpha}
such that

\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C_{\alpha})\approx_{\alpha}N^{\alpha}.

In the current paper, we aim to show that, in general, the optimal
h.q.d.
does not need to exhibit an order of growth. This is achieved by presenting two general methods for constructing special sets whose optimal
h.q.d.
may continuously switch between different orders of growth (logarithmic or polynomial).

Our first method employs a simple geometric argument. This allows prescribed oscillations from a logarithmic order of
\log N
to polynomial orders of
N^{2/5}
and
N^{1/2}
, and vice versa. We state our result as follows.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be in
\{0,2/5,1/2\}
, let
\left\{\varepsilon_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be in
(0,\varepsilon]
for some small positive
\varepsilon
, and let
\left\{q_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be positive integers. Then there exists a planar convex body
C
and an increasing sequence
\left\{N_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha_{i}=0
, it holds

\log^{1-\varepsilon_{i}}N\leq\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\leq\log^{1+\varepsilon_{i}}N\quad\text{for every}\quad N\in[N_{i},N_{i}+q_{i}],

while, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\alpha_{i}\in\{2/5,1/2\}
, it holds

N^{\alpha_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}\leq\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\leq N^{\alpha_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}}\quad\text{for every}\quad N\in[N_{i},N_{i}+q_{i}].

This first method obtains the planar convex body
C
as the limit of a nested sequence of planar convex bodies; thus,
C
is constructed implicitly. The simplicity of our first method comes at the cost of limited knowledge on the geometry of the boundary of
C
.

On the other hand, our second method provides a direct construction. This employs harmonic analysis techniques and subtle geometric estimates on the Fourier transform of sets. We obtain prescribed polynomial-order oscillations in the range
N^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in(2/5,1/2)
, and state our second result as follows.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be in
(2/5,1/2)
, let
\left\{\varepsilon_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be in
(0,\varepsilon]
for some small positive
\varepsilon
, and let
\left\{q_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be positive integers. Then there exists a planar convex body
C
and an increasing sequence
\left\{N_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds

N^{\alpha_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}\leq\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\leq N^{\alpha_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}}\quad\text{for every}\quad N\in[N_{i},N_{i}+q_{i}].

The second method involves constructing the boundary of
C
“by hand”; as we will see, it suffices to design the boundary only in a small neighbourhood of a point, while leaving the remaining part as
\mathcal{C}^{2}
.

These two results settle the first examples of sets for which the optimal
h.q.d.
does not exhibit an order of growth. Interestingly, if one further considers rotations in the averaging process (therefore, averaging over the whole group of affine transformations), it is a classic result of Beck
[
2
]
that one always obtains an order of growth of
N^{1/2}
. The reader may consult a recent work by Gennaioli and the author
[
3
]
for further details on the interplay between discrepancy and geometric measure theory.

In the pages that follow, Section
2
contains the implicit geometric construction that leads to Theorem
1.4
, while Section
3
contains the direct construction that proves Theorem
1.5
. In particular, the proof of Theorem
1.5
requires different techniques to establish lower and upper bounds, and therefore, these are treated in distinct subsections.

2
The first method

Recalling the hypotheses of Theorem
1.4
, we have that
\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of exponents with values in
\{0,2/5,1/2\}
,
\left\{\varepsilon_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a (possibly decreasing) sequence in
(0,\varepsilon]
for some small positive
\varepsilon
, and
\left\{q_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a (possibly increasing) sequence of positive integers. We remark that
\alpha_{i}
prescribes the target order of growth, while
\varepsilon_{i}
and
q_{i}
quantify, respectively, how closely and for how long we approximate it.

We begin by presenting a simple but essential auxiliary result.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{T}^{2}
be a set of
N
points, and let
A
and
B
be two planar sets such that
\rm{diam}(A)\leq 1
,
A\supset B
, and
|A\setminus B|\leq\eta\leq 1
. Then, it holds

\left|\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},A)-\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},B)\right|\leq 2N^{2}\eta^{1/2}.

Proof.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, it holds

\left|\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},A)-\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},B)\right|^{2}\leq 2N\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta A)-\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta B)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\delta.

(2.1)

Now, since
A\supset B
, it holds

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta A)-\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta B)=\mathcal{D}\left(\mathcal{P},\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta(A\setminus B)\right).

(2.2)

Since
\rm{diam}(A)\leq 1
, then, for any
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
, the set of
\boldsymbol{\tau}
’s such that

\mathds{1}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta(A\setminus B)}(\mathbf{p})\neq 0

has measure
\delta^{2}|A\setminus B|=\delta^{2}\eta
. Therefore, it holds

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|\mathds{1}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta(A\setminus B)}(\mathbf{p})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\delta^{2}\eta.

Now, by applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality again, we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{D}\left(\mathcal{P},\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta(A\setminus B)\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\delta&\leq 2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathds{1}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta(A\setminus B)}(\mathbf{p})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\delta+2N^{2}\eta^{2}\\
&\leq 2N\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|\mathds{1}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta(A\setminus B)}(\mathbf{p})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\delta+2N^{2}\eta^{2}\\
&\leq 2N^{2}\eta+2N^{2}\eta^{2}\leq 4N^{2}\eta.\end{split}

Combining the latter with (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) yields the claim.
∎

The latter result is all we need to proceed with the proof of Theorem
1.4
. We start by considering a sequence of convex planar bodies
\left\{C_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
to be chosen later, but such that each diameter is less than
1
and

\partial C_{i}\quad\text{is}\quad\begin{cases}\text{a polygon}&\text{if}\quad\alpha_{i}=0\\
\text{non-polygonal and piecewise-$\mathcal{C}^{2}$ with at least one corner}&\text{if}\quad\alpha_{i}=2/5\\
\mathcal{C}^{2}&\text{if}\quad\alpha_{i}=1/2\\
\end{cases}.

Now, let
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a (decreasing) sequence with values in
(0,1]
, but to be chosen later as well. Moreover, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we further impose that
C_{i}\subset C_{i+1}
and that
|C_{i+1}\setminus C_{i}|\leq\eta_{i+1}
.

The construction in the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

For the sake of notation, consider the function

H_{i}(N)=\begin{cases}\log N&\text{if}\quad\alpha_{i}=0\\
N^{2/5}&\text{if}\quad\alpha_{i}=2/5\\
N^{1/2}&\text{if}\quad\alpha_{i}=1/2\\
\end{cases}.

We recall that, by Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds

\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C_{i})\approx_{C_{i}}H_{i}(N).

(2.3)

We now construct the sequence
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
recursively. Suppose
\left\{\eta_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{i-1}
and
\left\{C_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{i-1}
have already been chosen. Then, let
N_{i}
be such that

H_{i}^{1-\varepsilon_{i}}(N)\leq\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C_{i})\leq H_{i}^{1+\varepsilon_{i}}(N)\quad\text{for every}\quad N\in[N_{i},N_{i}+q_{i}].

(2.4)

In particular, notice that such an
N_{i}
exists, otherwise (
2.3
) would not hold. Then, choose
\eta_{i}
in such a way that
\eta_{i}\leq\eta_{i-1}/2
and

\eta_{i}\leq(N_{i}+q_{i})^{-4}.

By choosing the whole sequence
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in such a fashion, it follows that the nested sequence of the planar convex bodies
\left\{C_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
converges (in measure) to a planar convex body
C
such that, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds

|C\setminus C_{i}|\leq\sum_{j=i+1}^{\infty}\eta_{j}\leq\eta_{i}.

Therefore, by applying Lemma
2.1
, we obtain that, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, for every
N\in[N_{i},N_{i}+q_{i}]
, and for every set
\mathcal{P}\subset[0,1)^{2}
of
N
points, it holds

|\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)-\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C_{i})|\leq 2N^{2}\eta_{i}^{1/2}\leq 2.

Finally, by (
2.4
) and a bit of rearrangement, the estimates in Theorem
1.4
follow.

The second method

The harmonic analysis arguments we are to employ in this section have been recently developed in
[
6
]
and
[
4
]
. These make use of a geometric quantity that we define as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a convex body. For an angle
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and value
\lambda>0
, we define the
chord
of
C
in direction
\mathbf{u}(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
at distance
\lambda
as

K_{C}(\theta,\lambda)=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in C\,\colon\,\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u}(\theta)=\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in C}(\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{u}(\theta))+\lambda\right\}.

Further, we consider its length
\left|K_{C}(\theta,\lambda)\right|
, and we define the quantity

{\gamma}_{C}(\theta,\lambda)=\max\{\left|K_{C}(\theta,\lambda)\right|,\left|K_{C}(\theta+\pi,\lambda)\right|\}.

One may notice that the decay of
|K_{C}(\theta,\lambda)|
as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
much depends on the local geometry of the (if just one) point
b\in\partial C
whose inner normal is
\mathbf{u}(\theta)
. As examples, if
\partial C
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
and has positive curvature at
b
, then
|K_{C}(\theta,\lambda)|
ultimately decays as
\lambda^{1/2}
, while if
b
is the vertex at a corner, then
|K_{C}(\theta,\lambda)|
ultimately decays as
\lambda
.

Now, we define the Fourier transform of
\mathds{1}_{C}
as

\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{C}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\int_{C}e^{-2\pi i\mathbf{x}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

and state a result that relates averages of the Fourier transform of
\mathds{1}_{C}
to the chords of
C
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
C
be a planar convex body. Then, there exists a positive value
\lambda_{C}
such that, for every angle
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and every
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{C}]
, it holds

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\lambda^{-1}\,\mathbf{u}(\theta))\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta\approx\lambda^{2}\gamma^{2}_{C}(\theta,\lambda).

We refer to
[
4
, Lem. 3.3]
for a detailed proof of the latter, but we mention that it relies upon the one-dimensional results in
[
10
]
and
[
6
]
.

We now show, through standard calculations, how quadratic discrepancy and the Fourier transform are intertwined. We start by exploiting the convolutional structure of (
1.1
). By setting

{\mu}=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}}\mu_{\rm D}(\mathbf{p})-N\mu_{\rm H},

where
\mu_{\rm H}
is the normalised Haar measure on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and
\mu_{\rm D}(\mathbf{p})
is the Dirac delta centred at
\mathbf{p}
, one may write

\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}+C)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\mathfrak{P}\{\mathds{1}_{C}\}({\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}{\mu}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\mathfrak{P}\{\mathds{1}_{C}\}\ast{\mu}\right)(\boldsymbol{\tau}).

Now, if
f
is an integrable function or a finite measure on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, we denote by

{\mathcal{F}}\{f\}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}

the function of the Fourier coefficients of
f
. In particular, we notice that it holds
\mathcal{F}\{{\mu}\}(\mathbf{0})=0
, and moreover, for every
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, it holds

{\mathcal{F}}\circ\mathfrak{P}\{\mathds{1}_{C}\}(\mathbf{n})=\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{C}(\mathbf{n}).

Hence, by applying Parseval’s identity, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta C)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|(\mathfrak{P}\{\mathds{1}_{\delta C}\}\ast{\mu})\right|^{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left|{\mathcal{F}}\circ\mathfrak{P}\{\mathds{1}_{\delta C}\}({\mathbf{n}})\right|^{2}\left|{\mathcal{F}}\{{\mu}\}({\mathbf{n}})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}({\mathbf{n}})\right|^{2}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}}e^{2\pi i\mathbf{p}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}}\right|^{2}.

Finally, we may rewrite (
1.2
) as

\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},C)=\sum_{{\mathbf{n}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}}e^{2\pi i\mathbf{p}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}}\right|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\mathbf{n})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta.

Geometric construction and Fourier estimates

Recalling the hypotheses of Theorem
1.5
, we have that
\left\{\alpha_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of exponents with values in
(2/5,1/2)
,
\left\{\varepsilon_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

(0,\varepsilon]

, and

\mathbf{0}\in\partial C

\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}\,\colon\,P_{i}(x)=y,\,a_{i}<x<b_{i},\,\kappa_{i}(a_{i})=k_{i},\,\kappa_{i}(b_{i})=k_{i-1}\right\},

and in the end, we obtain a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
curve segment of finite length. By a final rigid motion, we may place this curve segment so that it is tangent to the
x
-axis and its left endpoint is the origin; see Figure
3
for an illustration of the resulting curve segment
P
. By construction,
P
has strictly decreasing continuous curvature (and this goes to infinity as one approaches the origin). Finally, we let
\partial C
coincide with
P
for its length, and then we design the rest of
\partial C
in such a way that it is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
with signed curvature bounded from above by
\kappa_{1}(1)
and from below by, say,
1/10
.

The purpose of the latter construction is the following:
\partial C
has to look like the graphs of different
P_{i}
’s at increasingly smaller neighbourhoods of the origin. Ultimately, this is achieved by ensuring that each
k_{i+1}
is much larger than
k_{i}
. Indeed, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, mark by
(x_{i},y_{i})
the left endpoint of
P_{i}
after being assembled into
P
. Then, since all the curvatures
\kappa_{i}
in (
3.2
) are uniformly bounded from above by
2x^{-1}
for
x\in(0,1]
, it follows that we may get any
(x_{i},y_{i})
arbitrarily close to the origin. The fact that
k_{i}
may be chosen arbitrarily large ensures that we may get
P_{i+1}^{\prime}(b_{i+1})
arbitrarily close to
0
, and this, in turn, ensures that we may get the inner normal at
(x_{i},y_{i})
arbitrarily close to
(0,1)
.

Then, we are interested in how the chords
K_{C}(\theta+\pi/2,\lambda)
behave for small values of
\theta
. To this purpose, let
\beta\in(1,2)
, and consider a second planar convex body
A(\beta)
whose boundary coincides with the graph of
y=|x|^{\beta}
in
[-1,1]^{2}
. Moreover, suppose that
A(\beta)
has a centre of symmetry and is symmetric with respect to the
y
-axis. Hence, it holds the following estimates on the chords of
A(\beta)
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
A(\beta)
be as previously defined. Then, there exists a positive small constant
c
(independent of
\beta
) such that it holds

\left|K_{A(\beta)}(\theta+\pi/2,\lambda)\right|\approx_{\beta}\begin{cases}\lambda^{1/\beta}&\textnormal{if}\quad 0\leq|\theta|<\lambda^{\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}<c\\
\lambda^{1/2}|\theta|^{\frac{2-\beta}{2(\beta-1)}}&\textnormal{if}\quad\lambda^{\frac{\beta-1}{\beta}}\leq|\theta|<c\end{cases}.

We leave the proof of the latter in the Appendix, and we mention that similar calculations had previously been carried over in
[
4
, Prop. 8.4]
.

Now, recall that, by a suitable choice of
k_{i}
, we may get
(x_{i},y_{i})
arbitrarily close to the origin, as we may get its inner normal to make an angle arbitrarily close to
\pi/2
. Ultimately, this guarantees that, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we may obtain an interval
[\lambda_{i,1},\lambda_{i,2}]
and a positive value
\theta_{i}
such that, for every
\theta
such that
|\theta|\leq\theta_{i}
and every
\lambda
such that
\lambda\in\left[\lambda_{i,1},\lambda_{i,2}\right]
, it holds

\left|K_{C}(\theta+\pi/2,\lambda)\right|\approx\left|K_{A(\beta_{i})}(\theta+\pi/2,\lambda)\right|.

Notice that, as
\theta_{i}
and
\lambda_{i,2}
may depend on
\left\{\beta_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{i-1}
and
\left\{k_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{i-1}
, the choice of
\lambda_{i,1}
can be made arbitrarily small at every
i
-th step; this will be key for the estimates in the following subsection.

Finally, Proposition
3.3
and Lemma
3.2
may be applied to
C
to obtain the following result. From now on, we write
\rho^{-1}
in place of
\lambda
, to prioritise the argument of the Fourier transform.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
C
be as previously defined. Then, one may choose the sequence
\left\{k_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in such a way that, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an interval
[\rho_{i,1},\rho_{i,2}]\subset[1,+\infty)
(with
\rho_{i+1,1}>\rho_{i,2}
and
\lim_{i\to+\infty}\rho_{i,1}=+\infty
) and a positive value
\theta_{i}
(with
\theta_{i+1}<\theta_{i}
and
\lim_{i\to+\infty}\theta_{i}=0
) such that, for
\rho\in[\rho_{i,1},\rho_{i,2}]
and
|\theta|\leq\theta_{i}
, it holds

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}\left(\rho\,\mathbf{u}(\theta+\pi/2)\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta\approx_{\beta_{i}}\begin{cases}\rho^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}&\textnormal{if}\quad\left|\theta\right|\leq\rho^{\frac{1-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}}}\\
\rho^{-3}\left|\theta\right|^{\frac{2-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}&\textnormal{if}\quad\rho^{\frac{1-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}}}<\left|\theta\right|\leq\theta_{i}\end{cases}.

In particular, once
\rho_{i,1}
and
\theta_{i}
are fixed, the endpoint
\rho_{i,2}
can be made arbitrarily large.

Starting from the fact that we have designed
P
so that its curvature is strictly decreasing, we obtain the following auxiliary result.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
C
be as previously defined. Then, there exist
\lambda_{0}>0
and
\theta_{0}>\pi/2
such that, for
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{0})
and for
\pi/2\leq\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\theta_{0}
, it holds

|K_{C}(\theta_{1},\lambda)|\leq 2|K_{C}(\theta_{2},\lambda)|.

The proof of the latter requires a technical geometric argument, which we leave to the Appendix. By pairing Lemma
3.2
with Lemma
3.5
, we obtain that, for every
\rho>\max\{\lambda_{C}^{-1},\lambda_{0}^{-1}\}
and every angle
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
such that
\pi/2\leq\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\theta_{0}
, it holds

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}\left(\rho\,\mathbf{u}(\theta_{1})\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}\left(\rho\,\mathbf{u}(\theta_{2})\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta.

(3.3)

3.2
Lower bound

We show how we may construct the sequence
\left\{k_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
in such a way that, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds the lower bound

N^{\alpha_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}\leq\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\quad\text{for}\quad N\in[N_{i},N_{i}+q_{i}].

To this purpose, we need an argument of Cassels
[
7
]
and Montgomery
[
9
, Ch. 6]
for estimating exponential sums from below. We formulate it in a version specialised to balls, and the reader may find a short proof either in
[
6
]
or
[
4
]
. In what follows,
B(r)
stands for the ball centred at the origin with radius
r
.

Lemma 3.6

(Cassels-Montgomery)
.

For every origin-symmetric convex body
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and for every finite set of points
\{\mathbf{p}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{T}^{2}
, it holds

\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in(C\setminus B(r))\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{2\pi i\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{j}}\right|^{2}\geq\frac{|C|}{4}N-2\pi(r^{2}+1)N^{2}.

We proceed with the geometric argument that will pair with the latter lemma. For the sake of notation, we write

\|\theta\|_{\pi}=\min_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|\theta-j\pi|.

First, notice that, since we have constructed
\partial C
in such a way that its curvature is uniformly bounded from below and above when excluding a neighbourhood of the origin (and its symmetric counterpart), then for every
\lambda\in[0,\lambda_{0})
and for every
\theta
such that
\|\theta\|_{\pi}>1/10
, it holds

|K_{C}(\theta+\pi/2,\lambda)|\approx\lambda^{1/2}.

(3.4)

Now, fix an
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, by the latter consideration paired with Lemma
3.2
, and by Lemma
3.4
and (
3.3
), we get the lower bound

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}\left(\rho\,\mathbf{u}(\theta+\pi/2)\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta\gtrsim_{\beta_{i}}\begin{cases}\rho^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}&\textnormal{if}\quad\rho\in\left[\rho_{i,1},\rho_{i,2}\right]\;\land\;\|\theta\|_{\pi}\leq 1/10\\
\rho^{-3}&\textnormal{if}\quad\rho\in\left[\rho_{i,1},\rho_{i,2}\right]\;\land\;\|\theta\|_{\pi}>1/10\\
0&\textnormal{else}\end{cases}.

Let
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\in(0,1]
be a value to be chosen later. Moreover, let
N
,
X
, and
Y
be positive parameters to be chosen later, but such that
\rho_{i,1}\ll Y\ll X\ll\rho_{i,2}
and
XY=N^{1+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}}
. Then, consider a rectangle
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that it is symmetric with respect to the axes and has a vertex in
(X/2,Y/2)
; consequently, it also holds
|R|=N^{1+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}}
. Denote by
R(\omega)
the same rectangle rotated counterclockwise by an angle
\omega
, and define the function
\Phi\colon\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to[0,+\infty)
by

\Phi(\mathbf{m})=\int_{-1/10}^{1/10}\mathds{1}_{R(\omega)\setminus B(\rho_{i,1})}(\mathbf{m})\,\mathrm{d}\omega,

Now, in order to apply Cassels-Montgomery lemma effectively, we need to find a positive parameter
Z=Z(N)
such that in the annulus
|\mathbf{m}|\in\left[\rho_{i,1},\rho_{i,2}\right]
it holds

Z\Phi(\mathbf{m})\leq\begin{cases}|\mathbf{m}|^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}&\textnormal{if}\quad|\mathbf{m}|\in\left[\rho_{i,1},\rho_{i,2}\right]\;\land\;\|\arg(\mathbf{m})+\pi/2\|_{\pi}\leq 1/10\\
|\mathbf{m}|^{-3}&\textnormal{if}\quad|\mathbf{m}|\in\left[\rho_{i,1},\rho_{i,2}\right]\;\land\;\|\arg(\mathbf{m})+\pi/2\|_{\pi}>1/10\\
0&\textnormal{else}\end{cases}.

First, we describe two regions where
\Phi
vanishes. By construction, for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
|\mathbf{m}|\in\left[\rho_{i,1},X\right]^{\mathsf{c}}
, it holds
\Phi(\mathbf{m})=0
. Secondly, for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
|\mathbf{m}|\geq Y
and
\|\arg(\mathbf{m})+\pi/2\|_{\pi}\leq 1/10
, it holds
\Phi(\mathbf{m})=0
as well.

Now, for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
|\mathbf{m}|\leq Y
, we have the trivial estimate
\Phi(\mathbf{m})\leq 1/5
. Since in this region we are aiming for
Z\Phi(\mathbf{m})\leq|\mathbf{m}|^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}
, then we settle for

Z\lesssim Y^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}.

Last, we consider the remaining region defined by
|\mathbf{m}|\in[Y,X]
and
\|\arg(\mathbf{m})+\pi/2\|_{\pi}>1/10
. By some elementary geometry, we find that
\Phi(\mathbf{m})\leq 2\alpha
where
\alpha
is such that
|\mathbf{m}|\sin\alpha=Y/2
. Hence, it holds
\Phi(\mathbf{m})\leq\pi Y/(2|\mathbf{m}|)
, and since in this region we are aiming for
Z\Phi(\mathbf{m})\leq|\mathbf{m}|^{-3}
, then we settle for

Z\lesssim Y^{-1}X^{-2}.

Having considered all the possible regions, we are left with the requirement

Z\lesssim\min\left(Y^{-2-2/\beta_{i}},Y^{-1}X^{-2}\right),

and if, by an optimisation argument, we equalise the two terms in the minimum and use the constraint
XY=N^{1+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}}
, then we obtain

X\approx N^{(1+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i})(\beta_{i}+2)/(3\beta_{i}+2)},\quad Y\approx N^{(1+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i})2\beta_{i}/(3\beta_{i}+2)},\quad\text{and}\quad Z\approx N^{-(1+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i})(4\beta_{i}+4)/(3\beta_{i}+2)}.

Starting from (
3.1
), and by Parseval’s identity and Cassels-Montgomery lemma, we get that, for any set of
N
points
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{p}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{T}^{2}
, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta C)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\delta

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{2\pi i\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{j}}\right|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}\left(\mathbf{m}\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta

\displaystyle\gtrsim_{\beta_{i}}\sum_{|\mathbf{m}|\geq\rho_{i,1}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{2\pi i\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{j}}\right|^{2}Z\Phi(\mathbf{m})

\displaystyle=Z\int_{-1/10}^{1/10}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in R(\omega)\setminus B(\rho_{i,1})}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{2\pi i\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{j}}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\omega

\displaystyle\gtrsim Z\left(N^{2+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}}-2\pi(\rho_{i,1}^{2}+1)N^{2}\right).

By construction,
\rho_{i,2}
can be chosen arbitrarily large, and this also applies to
N
since our only constraint is
X\ll\rho_{i,2}
. Hence, by taking
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}
in such a way that
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}(\beta_{i}+2)/(3\beta_{i}+2)<\varepsilon_{i}
, we find an
N_{i}
such that

N_{i}^{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}}\geq 4\pi(\rho^{2}_{i,1}+1)\quad\text{and}\quad(N_{i}+q_{i})^{(1+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i})(\beta_{i}+2)/(3\beta_{i}+2)}\ll\rho_{i,2}.

Therefore, it follows that, for every
N\in[N_{i},N_{i}+q_{i}]
, it holds

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\,\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta C)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\delta\gtrsim_{\beta_{i}}N^{-(1+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i})(4\beta_{i}+4)/(3\beta_{i}+2)}N^{2+\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}}=N^{\frac{2\beta_{i}}{3\beta_{i}+2}-\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\frac{\beta_{i}+2}{3\beta_{i}+2}}>N^{\alpha_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}.

Notice that, since
N^{-\varepsilon_{i}}
can be made arbitrarily small, we can ultimately get the implicit constant before
N^{\alpha_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}
to be
1
.

3.3
Upper bound

For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we proceed to show the upper bound

\inf_{\#\mathcal{P}=N}\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\lesssim N^{\alpha_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}}\quad\text{for}\quad N\in[N_{i},N_{i}+q_{i}].

This is done by employing suitable samplings, and we will make use of the kind that appear in
[
6
, Thm. 8]
.

Fix
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and again, we remind that at every
i
-th step of our construction,
\rho_{i,2}
serves as a free parameter that may be chosen arbitrarily large. By pairing Lemma
3.2
with (
3.4
), and by Lemma
3.4
and (
3.3
), in the annulus
\rho\in[\rho_{i,1},\rho_{i,2}]
it holds the upper bound

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}\left(\rho\,\mathbf{u}(\theta+\pi/2)\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta\lesssim_{\beta_{i}}\begin{cases}\rho^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}&\textnormal{if}\quad\|\theta\|_{\pi}\leq\rho^{\frac{1-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}}}\\
\rho^{-3}\|\theta\|_{\pi}^{\frac{2-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}&\textnormal{if}\quad\rho^{\frac{1-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}}}<\|\theta\|_{\pi}\leq\theta_{i}\\
\rho^{-3}&\textnormal{if}\quad\theta_{i}<\|\theta\|_{\pi}\par\end{cases}.

(3.5)

Since we have constructed
\partial C
in such a way that its curvature is uniformly bounded from below, then by Lemma
3.2
, for every
\rho>\lambda_{C}^{-1}
and any angle
\theta
, it holds

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}\left(\rho\,\mathbf{u}(\theta)\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta\lesssim\rho^{-3}.

(3.6)

Again, let
\tilde{N}\in\mathbb{N}
be a free large parameter to be chosen later, and set

G=\lfloor\tilde{N}^{\frac{2+\beta_{i}}{2+3\beta_{i}}}\rfloor,\quad L=\lfloor\tilde{N}^{\frac{2\beta_{i}}{2+3\beta_{i}}}\rfloor,\quad J_{G}=[0,G-1]\cap\mathbb{N},\quad\text{and}\quad J_{L}=[0,L-1]\cap\mathbb{N}.

Set
N=GL
(trivially,
N/2<\tilde{N}<2N
), and consider the set of
N
points
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{T}^{2}
defined by

\mathcal{P}=\left\{\mathbf{p}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{N}=\{\mathbf{p}_{\ell,g}\}_{g\in J_{G},\,\ell\in J_{L}}\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{p}_{\ell,g}=\left(\frac{g}{G},\,\frac{\ell}{L}\right).

Recall that, by Parseval’s identity, we get

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{P},\boldsymbol{\tau}+\delta C)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\neq(0,0)}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\mathbf{m})\right|^{2}\left|\sum_{g\in J_{G}}\sum_{\ell\in J_{L}}e^{2\pi i\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{\ell,g}}\right|^{2},

and in particular, it holds the identity

\sum_{g\in J_{G}}\sum_{\ell\in J_{L}}e^{2\pi i\left(m_{1}\frac{g}{G}+m_{2}\frac{\ell}{L}\right)}=\begin{cases}GL&\text{if}\quad m_{1}\in G\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{and}\quad m_{2}\in L\mathbb{Z}\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}.

Hence, consider the set
\mathcal{R}=(G\mathbb{Z}\times L\mathbb{Z})\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
, and split it into the regions

\begin{split}V_{1}&=\left\{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{R}\,\colon\,|m_{1}|\leq|m_{2}|^{1/\beta_{i}}\right\},\\
V_{2}&=\left\{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{R}\,\colon\,|m_{2}|^{1/\beta_{i}}<|m_{1}|\leq\theta_{i}|m_{2}|\right\},\\
V_{3}&=\left\{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{R}\,\colon\,\theta_{i}|m_{2}|<|m_{1}|\right\},\end{split}

so that we may write

\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)=G^{2}L^{2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{1}}+\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{2}}+\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{3}}\right)\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\mathbf{m})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta.

(3.7)

Now, we constrain
\tilde{N}
to be so large that
L>\rho_{i,1}
, and as a consequence, it follows that
\mathcal{R}\cap B(\rho_{i,1})=\varnothing
. Moreover, we constrain
\rho_{i,2}
to be so large that

\mathcal{Q}_{i}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{R}\cap B^{\mathsf{c}}(\rho_{i,2})}|\mathbf{m}|^{-3}<N^{-2}.

We exploit (
3.5
) to study the three sums in (
3.7
). In this case, we must consider

\rho=|\mathbf{m}|\quad\text{and}\quad\tan\theta=-\frac{m_{1}}{m_{2}}.

For the sake of notation, we write the annulus
\mathcal{A}=\left\{\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{R}\,\colon\,|\mathbf{m}|\in[\rho_{i,1},\rho_{i,2}]\right\}
. Notice that for small
\theta
it holds
\tan\theta\approx\theta
, and consequently, with a bit of rearrangement, we can rewrite the estimates in (
3.5
) as: for every
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{A}
it holds

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}\left(\mathbf{m}\right)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta\lesssim_{\beta_{i}}\begin{cases}|m_{2}|^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}&\text{if}\quad|m_{1}|\leq|m_{2}|^{1/\beta_{i}}\\
|m_{1}|^{\frac{2-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}|m_{2}|^{\frac{1-2\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}&\text{if}\quad|m_{2}|^{1/\beta_{i}}\leq|m_{1}|<\theta_{i}|m_{2}|\\
|m_{1}|^{-3}&\text{if}\quad\theta_{i}|m_{2}|<|m_{1}|\end{cases}.

(3.8)

For
j\in\{1,2,3\}
, notice that by the constraints we have imposed before, it holds
V_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}=V_{j}\cap B(\rho_{i,2})
and
G^{2}L^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{i}=N^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{i}<1
, and by further applying (
3.6
), we obtain

\begin{split}G^{2}L^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{j}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\mathbf{m})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta&=G^{2}L^{2}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}}+\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{j}\cap B^{\mathsf{c}}(\rho_{i,2})}\right)\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\mathbf{m})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta\\
&\lesssim G^{2}L^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\mathbf{m})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta+G^{2}L^{2}\mathcal{Q}_{i}\\
&\lesssim G^{2}L^{2}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\mathbf{m})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta.\end{split}

(3.9)

Then, by applying (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), for the first sum in the last term of (
3.7
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{1}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\mathbf{m})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\beta_{i}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{1}}|m_{2}|^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}\lesssim\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{+\infty}\,\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{(Ln_{2})^{1/\beta_{i}}/G}(Ln_{2})^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}

\displaystyle\lesssim L^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}\sum_{n_{2}=1}^{+\infty}n_{2}^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}\left(1+(Ln_{2})^{1/\beta_{i}}/G\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim L^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}.

Similarly, for the second sum in the last term of (
3.7
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\mathbf{m})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\beta_{i}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{2}}|m_{1}|^{\frac{2-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}|m_{2}|^{\frac{1-2\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{+\infty}\,\sum_{n_{2}=Gn_{1}/(\theta_{i}L)}^{(Gn_{1})^{\beta_{i}}/L}(Gn_{1})^{\frac{2-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}(Ln_{2})^{\frac{1-2\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim G^{\frac{2-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}L^{\frac{1-2\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{+\infty}n_{1}^{\frac{2-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}\sum_{n_{2}=Gn_{1}/(\theta_{i}L)}^{+\infty}n_{2}^{\frac{1-2\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim G^{\frac{2-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}L^{\frac{1-2\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{+\infty}n_{1}^{\frac{2-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}\left(Gn_{1}/(\theta_{i}L)\right)^{\frac{-\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i}-1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim G^{-2}L^{-1}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{+\infty}n_{1}^{-2}\lesssim G^{-2}L^{-1}.

For the last sum in the last term in (
3.7
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{3}}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{\delta C}(\mathbf{m})\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\delta

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\beta_{i}}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in V_{3}}|m_{1}|^{-3}\lesssim\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{+\infty}\,\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{Gn_{1}/(\theta_{i}L)}(Gn_{1})^{-3}

\displaystyle\lesssim G^{-3}\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{+\infty}n_{1}^{-2}G/(\theta_{i}L)\lesssim G^{-2}L^{-1}/\theta_{i}.

Finally, since
G^{2}L^{-2/\beta_{i}}=L\approx N^{\frac{2\beta_{i}}{2+3\beta_{i}}}=N^{\alpha}_{i}
, and by comparing the previous three estimates with (
3.7
), we obtain

\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\lesssim_{\beta_{i}}G^{2}L^{2}\left(L^{-2-2/\beta_{i}}+G^{-2}L^{-1}/\theta_{i}\right)\lesssim G^{2}L^{-2/\beta_{i}}+L/\theta_{i}\lesssim N^{\alpha_{i}}/\theta_{i}

Hence, since we may choose
\rho_{i,2}
and
\tilde{N}
arbitrarily large, then we can ultimately obtain
N
such that
N^{\varepsilon_{i}}\geq\theta_{i}^{-1}
, and for the same reason, we may ultimately write

\mathcal{D}_{2}(\mathcal{P},\,C)\leq N^{\alpha_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}}.

At this stage, by the constraints we have imposed, we may only conclude that the upper bound holds for all the integers
N
of the form

N=\lfloor n^{\frac{2+\beta_{i}}{2+3\beta_{i}}}\rfloor\,\lfloor n^{\frac{2\beta_{i}}{2+3\beta_{i}}}\rfloor,\quad\text{with}\quad n^{\alpha_{i}}>\rho_{i,1}\quad\text{and}\quad n^{2}<\rho_{i,2}.

In order to prove that there is a suitable choice of points for some
q_{i}
consecutive integers in the interval
(\rho_{i,1}^{1/\alpha_{i}},\rho_{i,2}^{1/2})
, consider the following recursive definition

n_{j}=\max\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}\;\colon\lfloor n^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\,\lfloor n^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\leq N-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\,\lfloor n_{k}^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\,\lfloor n_{k}^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\right\}\quad\text{for}\quad j\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\},

whereas improper sums are conventionally considered as zeros.
Now, notice that the latter definition implies that

\begin{split}N-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\,\lfloor n_{k}^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\,\lfloor n_{k}^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor&\leq\lfloor(n_{j}+1)^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\,\lfloor(n_{j}+1)^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\\
&\leq\lfloor n_{j}^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\,\lfloor n_{j}^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor+\lfloor n_{j}^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor+\lfloor n_{j}^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor+1,\end{split}

and therefore, it follows that

N-\sum_{k=1}^{j}\,\lfloor n_{k}^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\,\lfloor n_{k}^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\leq 2n_{j}^{1-\alpha_{i}}.

Again, by the definition of
n_{j}
, we point out that

\frac{n_{1}}{2}\leq N\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{n_{j+1}}{2}\leq N-\sum_{k=1}^{j}\,\lfloor n_{k}^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\,\lfloor n_{k}^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor,

and therefore, by induction, we obtain

N-\sum_{k=1}^{j}\,\lfloor n_{k}^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\,\lfloor n_{k}^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\leq 2n_{j}^{1-\alpha_{i}}\leq 2^{4}n_{j-1}^{(1-\alpha_{i})^{2}}\leq 2^{2j}N^{\left(1-\alpha_{i}\right)^{j}}.

In particular, notice that

N-\sum_{j=1}^{5}\lfloor n_{j}^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\,\lfloor n_{j}^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\leq 2^{10}N^{\left(1-\alpha_{i}\right)^{5}}\ll\bigl(N^{\alpha_{i}/2}\bigr).

Then, if we further impose that
N^{\alpha_{i}^{2}/2}>\rho_{i,1}
, we may associate a choice of points as in the previous construction to every
N_{j}=\lfloor n_{j}^{1-\alpha_{i}}\rfloor\,\lfloor n_{j}^{\alpha_{i}}\rfloor
such that
N_{j}\gtrsim N^{\alpha_{i}/2}
, and we don’t bother the contribution of the remaining points since, by the latter equation, it is certainly much smaller than
N^{\alpha_{i}}
. Finally, it is harmless to sum the contribution of our (at most) five point-sets and the remainder points, since, for
a_{1},\ldots,a_{6}\geq 0
, it holds

\left(\sum_{j=1}^{6}a_{j}\right)^{2}\leq 6\sum_{j=1}^{6}a_{j}^{2}.

By the argument we have just carried over, we may find an integer
N_{i}\gg q_{i}
for which the upper bound in Theorem
1.5
holds, and by choosing
\rho_{i,2}
large enough, we ensure that the same construction may be carried over for the next consecutive
q_{i}
integers after
N_{i}
.

Remark 3.7
.

Notice that all the constraints needed in the proof of the lower bound and in the proof of the upper bound are ultimately a matter of choosing
\rho_{i,2}
large enough; this guarantees that we may get the
N_{i}
obtained at the end of the two proofs to be the same integer.

\lambda=\int_{0}^{b(\theta_{i},\lambda)-p(\theta_{i})}\mathbf{u}(\theta_{i})\cdot\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{\prime}(p(\theta_{i})+s)\,\mathrm{d}s

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be positive numbers, and let
g\colon[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
be such that

g(x)\approx\begin{cases}x^{\alpha}&\textnormal{if}\quad 0\leq x<1\\
x^{\beta}&\textnormal{if}\quad x\geq 1\end{cases}.

If
x_{y}
is such that
g(x_{y})=y
, then it holds

x_{y}\approx\begin{cases}y^{1/\alpha}&\textnormal{if}\quad 0\leq y<1\\
y^{1/\beta}&\textnormal{if}\quad y\geq 1\end{cases}.

We proceed with the proof of Proposition
3.3
, and by symmetry, we may restrict ourselves to the case
\theta\geq 0
. Since the curvature of the graph of

decreases as one moves away from the origin, then, by arguing as in the proof of Lemma
3.5

\tilde{\lambda}
and

such that, for every

\left|K^{+}_{A(\beta)}(\theta+\pi/2,\lambda)\right|\geq\left|K^{-}_{A(\beta)}(\theta+\pi/2,\lambda)\right|.

In this same range, the endpoints of the chord

are bounded to the part of
\partial A(\beta)
that coincides with the graph of
y=|x|^{\beta}
. Thus, it is enough to establish the estimates in Proposition
3.3

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Without loss of generality, let
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{A(\beta)}\colon[-1,1)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the counter-clockwise arc-length parametrisation of
\partial A(\beta)
such that
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{A(\beta)}(0)=\mathbf{0}
, and let
b
and
p
be defined analogously as in the proof of Lemma
3.5
. For the sake of notation, we set
x_{o}(\theta)
to be the abscissa of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{A(\beta)}(p(\theta))
and
x_{+}(\theta,\lambda)
to be the abscissa of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{A(\beta)}(b(\theta,\lambda))
. First, by some basic geometric calculations, we notice that, for
\lambda\in[0,\tilde{\lambda})
and for
\theta\in[0,\tilde{\theta})
, it holds

\left|K_{A(\beta)}^{+}(\theta+\pi/2,\lambda)\right|\approx x_{+}(\theta+\pi/2,\lambda)-x_{o}(\theta+\pi/2).

For the sake of brevity, we write
x_{0}=x_{o}(\theta+\pi/2)
and
x_{+}=x_{+}(\theta+\pi/2,\lambda)
. Now, we notice that
x_{+}
is the abscissa of the (right) intersection of the curve
y=|x|^{\beta}
with the straight line

y=(x-x_{o})\beta x_{o}^{\beta-1}+x_{o}^{\beta}+\frac{\lambda}{\cos\theta}.

Equalizing, and with the normalisation
z=\frac{x-x_{o}}{x_{o}}
, we get to the equation

f(z)=|z+1|^{\beta}-z\beta-1=\frac{\lambda}{x_{o}^{\beta}\cos\theta},

(A.5)

and we note that, for
\theta\in[0,\tilde{\theta})
, both
x_{o}
and
\cos\theta
are non-negative. By applying Taylor’s formula with integral remainder to
f
, we obtain

f(z)=\beta(\beta-1)\int_{0}^{z}(1+t)^{\beta-2}(z-t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Notice that for
z\in[0,1)
it holds

\int_{0}^{z}(1+t)^{\beta-2}(z-t)\,\mathrm{d}t\approx\int_{0}^{z}(z-t)\,\mathrm{d}t\approx z^{2}.

On the other hand, for
z\in[1,+\infty)
it holds

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{z}(1+t)^{\beta-2}(z-t)\,\mathrm{d}t&=\int_{0}^{z/2}(1+t)^{\beta-2}(z-t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\frac{z}{2}}^{z}(1+t)^{\beta-2}(z-t)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\approx z\int_{0}^{z/2}(1+t)^{\beta-2}\,\mathrm{d}t+z^{\beta-2}\int_{\frac{z}{2}}^{z}(z-t)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\frac{z}{\beta-1}\left(\left(1+z/2\right)^{\beta-1}-1\right)+z^{\beta-2}\frac{z^{2}}{8}\approx_{\beta}z^{\beta}.\end{split}

Hence, we get

f(z)\approx_{\beta}\begin{cases}z^{2}&\text{if}\quad 0\leq z<1\\
z^{\beta}&\text{if}\quad z\geq 1\end{cases},

and if we consider (
A.5
), by applying Lemma
A.1
, and by the fact that for
\theta\in[0,\tilde{\theta})
it holds
\cos\theta\approx 1
, then it follows that

\frac{x_{+}-x_{o}}{x_{o}}\approx_{\beta}\begin{cases}\lambda^{1/2}x_{o}^{-\beta/2}&\text{if}\quad 0\leq\lambda x_{o}^{-\beta}<1\\
\lambda^{1/\beta}x_{o}^{-1}&\text{if}\quad\lambda x_{o}^{-\beta}\geq 1\end{cases}.

(A.6)

Finally, by the definition of
x_{o}
, we have

\beta x_{o}^{\beta-1}=\left.\frac{d}{dx}x^{\beta}\right|_{x=x_{o}}=\tan\theta,

and therefore, we get that for
\theta\in[0,\tilde{\theta})
it holds

x_{o}\approx\theta^{\frac{1}{\beta-1}}.

The conclusion hence follows by a rearrangement of the terms in (
A.6
).
