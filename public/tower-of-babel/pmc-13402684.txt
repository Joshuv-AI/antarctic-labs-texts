An ubiquitous antibacterial toxin from human gut bacteria engenders neonatal colonization advantage
Authors: Ezequiel Valguarnera, Jessica Tung, Jesse J. Pak, Seongmi K. Russell, David W. Hecht, Drew J. Schwartz, Juliane Bubeck Wardenburg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacteroides fragilis is a common member of the human colonic microbiota. In children, the B. fragilis Type 6 Secretion System (T6SS) has been suggested to mediate dominance between coexisting strains. We sequenced nearly 900 clinical isolates and characterized their T6SS toxin repertoire. The T6SS effector Bte3 was identified as one of the most prevalent effectors within this human isolate cohort. Bte3 exhibited the ability to intoxicate both B. fragilis and Enterobacteriaceae in vitro, and was defined as a periplasmic-intoxicating effector that employs previously unknown adaptor proteins for its secretion. The role of bte3 in early life competition was interrogated via a murine model in which neonates were exposed upon birth to two T6SS-isogenic strains. We observed that T6SS-engineered strains and human isolates containing bte3-based toxin combinations displaced competing B. fragilis strains unless these strains manifest immunity to bte3. These studies suggest that the landscape of T6SS effectors in the human population may be shaped not only by temporal exposure to distinct strains, but by potency of effector function. Our work underscores the relevance of T6SS diversity in B. fragilis early life competition and provides a potential roadmap to design microbial interventions in the context of neonatal acquisition of colonic commensals.

Introduction

Mammals are exposed to gut microbes upon birth, establishing microbiota assembly and composition1,2. While the mechanistic aspects of initial microbiota assembly are ill-defined, microbial composition is linked to health and disease3,4. The adult microbiome is stable until disruptive events, such as exposure to antibiotics, infectious diseases, or dietary changes occur5,6, whereas bacterial diversity and abundance changes throughout infancy are common7,8. Bacteria from the genus Bacteroides are ubiquitous in Western microbiomes, constituting up to 50% of the total gut bacteria9. Within the genus, Bacteroides fragilis is a commensal that confers immune tolerance towards gut bacteria, yet is also the most commonly isolated anaerobe from extraintestinal infection in humans10. Moreover, a subset of B. fragilis strains termed enterotoxigenic B. fragilis (ETBF) carries a metalloprotease toxin linked to infantile diarrhea, extraintestinal infections, and colon cancer risk11.

The role of B. fragilis in disease engenders interest in understanding fundamental attributes of niche acquisition and strain dominance. We have previously shown that the type VI secretion system (T6SS) confers dominance to non-toxigenic B. fragilis (NTBF) in vivo when in competition with ETBF, protecting from ETBF-associated injury12. The T6SS is a multimeric structure that injects toxin effectors into bacterial target cells, exhibiting homology to bacteriophages and contractile injection systems13. A key feature of the T6SS is the co-occurrence of effector-coding genes with specific immunity protein-coding genes to protect kin cells14. The importance of the T6SS in microbiota development is suggested by stool metagenomic analyses of T6SS loci from healthy humans15 which revealed that infants are colonized with T6SS-positive B. fragilis at a higher frequency than adults. In addition, children display dominance transitions over time between strains that carry distinct T6SS effector-coding genes, while adults are stably colonized by a single strain15,16.

In this work, we sought to test the hypothesis that the B. fragilis T6SS mediates strain transitions during early life predicated on effector function hierarchy. We leverage a unique B. fragilis clinical isolate collection to generate sequence-based data on ~900 isolates, providing insight into the genetic landscape and evolution of B. fragilis T6SS toxin repertoires in humans. Through this effort, we identify B. fragilis T6S effector 3, or Bte3, as a widespread effector capable of targeting Bacteroidales and Enterobacteriaceae. As this target range is unprecedented for the B. fragilis T6SS, we define the genetic and functional requirements for secretion of Bte3-based T6SS toxin combinations, revealing the ability of Bte3 to increase competitive fitness in the neonatal gut. Moreover, we show the temporal and genetic restrictions by which a single T6SS effector gains a colonization advantage in the neonatal colon via sequential exposure of pre-weaned mice to Bte3-harboring strains and susceptible or immune (Bti3-harboring) strains. Together, these findings underscore the prominent role of T6SS in strain dominance during enteric microbiota assembly, and suggest that the overall stable prevalence of T6SS locus variants in adults is a combination of effector hierarchical toxicity, effector-specific immunity repertoires, and primary occupancy of the neonatal colonic niche.

Sequence analysis confirmed that the majority of isolates carry at least one of three known T6SS genomic architectures (GA) (Supplementary Fig. 1b)15. We focused on B. fragilis-restricted GA3 effector toxins, present in 80.1% (n = 707) of isolates. GA3 effector and immunity (ei) pairs are encoded in two separate loci (locus I/II) within the T6SS cluster (Fig. 1b)18. Sequence searches using previously proposed effector and immunity genes allowed for the identification of known and novel effector-immunity genes15. Consequently, we developed a nomenclature for GA3 effectors based on the distribution of locus I/II combinations and previously established ei conventions (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data 1)12,15,19,20. Locus I repertoire contained 7 ei pairs (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1a, and Supplementary Data 1), dominated by the characterized bfe1_bfi1 (B.fragilis effector_immunity 1) and bte1_bti1 (B. fragilis T6S effector_immunity 1)19–21. Divergent effectors were named ble1 (B. fragilislocus I effector 1) and the Division II-restricted cte1 (Clade II effector 1) (Fig. 1c). We also identified lower-prevalence locus I variants: bfe1b (84.9% identity to bfe1), bte1b (77.5% identity to bte1), and cte1b (81.4% identity to cte1b) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data 1). Locus II contained 17 ei pairs, primarily bfe2_bfi2, bte2_bti219,20, and the uncharacterized bte3_bti3 and bte4_bti4 (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 1d, and Supplementary Data 1). These effectors were named in two series, bfe3- bfe8b and bte3-bte7, primarily based on their co-occurrence frequency with specific locus I effectors (Fig. 1c). The second criteria for nomenclature of locus II effectors was based on the four configurations in which ei genes appeared: (1) a single ei pair with a canonical paar tip gene; (2) two ei pairs flanking three open reading frames of unknown function; (3) two or three ei pairs, an effector-encoded N-terminal PAAR tip, and a conserved orf located between vgrG and bte5/bfe5; and (4) a single ei pair (cte2), where Cte2 contains a predicted N-terminal PAAR domain (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Novel locus II genes included bfe5 (63.7% identity to bte5), bfe8b (74.5% identity to bfe8), and cte3 (56.4% identity to bfe2) among others. While all locus II clusters carry a vgrG spike ortholog, VgrG C-terminal sequences are highly divergent, suggesting that specific VgrGs establish highly selective interactions with their cognate locus II effectors (Supplementary Fig. 1e)22–27.

Combinatorial analysis identified 33 unique locus I/II combinations, with bte1_bte3_bte4 being the most abundant (23% of GA3+ isolates, n = 157) (Fig. 1c, green bars). The breadth of this clinical repository enabled us to identify three Division II-restricted combinations: cte1_cte2, bte1_cte3, and cte1_cte3. Analysis of 816 public B. fragilis genomes from NCBI revealed similar ei distribution and abundance patterns (Supplementary Fig. 1f). Temporal distribution analysis of locus I/II combinations throughout the timespan of the collection showed few significant associations that were not informative of specific GA3 combinations as drivers of transmission (Supplementary Data 1, Temporal associations).

To validate GA3 ei frequency across distinct host developmental ages and disease states, we surveyed human microbiome datasets (preterm infants, term infants/children, and adults) for B. fragilis and the locus II effector bte3 gene (Supplementary Data 2)28–38. Consistent with community-acquired colonization, B. fragilis prevalence was low among hospitalized preterm infants (7.95%), yet significantly higher in term-delivered infants and children (19.3–89.5%) (Fig. 1d). Prevalence among healthy adults (56.5–57.3%) mirrored that of term infants and children, reflecting B. fragilis stability in the human gut (Fig. 1d)16. The bte3 effector was detected in a similar percentage of individuals across age groups (13.6–38.1%), suggesting T6SS effector repertoires are established early in life (Fig. 1e). Moreover, the prevalence of bte3 in B. fragilis-positive metagenomes matched our adult isolate data (20.2%; Fig. 1e). Thus, our adult isolate collection likely reflects T6SS ei diversity during neonatal colonization.

To examine the relationship between lineage divergence and GA3 diversity, we performed a phylogenetic analysis of the 882 isolates, including six reference strains as controls for T6SS content and genotypic context (Fig. 1f, control strains 1–6). The tree confirmed ancestral divergence into Division I (Fig. 1f, green and blue clusters) and Division II clades (Fig. 1f, red cluster). Within Division I, an early branching event further distinguished a major (n = 707, blue cluster) and minor (n = 126, green cluster) clade. Mapping GA3 combinations onto this phylogeny revealed distinct evolutionary patterns: (1) lineage-specific divergence: Division II isolates consistently harbor clade-specific combinations, mainly cte1_cte2, bte1_cte3, and cte1b_cte2. This suggests that a prototypical GA3 was acquired by the last common ancestor of Divisions I and II, with subsequent branching events driving lineage-restricted repertoires. (2) Differential GA3 retention: the Division I Minor clade exhibited low GA3 prevalence (18.3%) compared to the Major clade (93.4%) (Fig. 1g). This suggests an early GA3 loss event in the minor clade, with rare GA3-positive isolates representing recent, independent acquisitions. This clade is significantly enriched for bft genes (24.4%, odds ratio = 3.66, p < 0.0001), suggesting a selective advantage via bft acquisition (Fig. 1f, outer color strip). (3) Clonal expansion and horizontal gene transfer (HGT): in the Major clade, most terminal branches associate with a single GA3 combination, indicative of clonal expansion following HGT. Notably, several instances of independent GA3 acquisition were identified, such as the repeated gain of the bte1_bte3_bte4 combination (Fig. 1f, black asterisks) within Cluster 19 (n = 559, Fig. 1f, blue asterisk). (4) Metabolic constraints: sporadic GA3 loss occurred even within Major clade branches dominated by single combinations (Fig. 1F, gray bars in inner color strip), likely reflecting the metabolic burden of T6SS maintenance. Rare isolates with divergent combinations within similar clusters suggest ongoing genetic plasticity via combination switching or GA3 loss and re-aquisition (Fig. 1f, green asterisk; e.g., isolate 16075 in Cluster 19.9.5.5).

B. fragilis strains harbor multiple orphan immunity proteins encoded outside of the T6SS cluster, termed acquired immunity defense (AID) loci39. Mapping AID sequences onto the phylogeny revealed that AID acquisition events are skewed towards Division I Major clade clusters (Clusters 6, 15, 18, 19.8, and 19.9.5; 151/240, 62.9% of AID+ isolates) (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Data 1). AID enrichment biased GA3 combinations towards bfe1_bfe2, bfe1_bte2, and bfe1_bfe5_bfe6_bfe7 (Supplementary Fig. 1g). GA3-negative isolates exhibited low AID abundance (6.8% AID+; n = 12/175), driven by enrichment of GA3- isolates in Division I Minor and Division II (73.7% of GA3-) clades, suggestive of acquisition and diversification of AID repertoires posterior to divergence between Division I Major and Minor clades. Thus, our data suggest the B. fragilis GA3 ei and orphan immunity landscape has primarily been driven by ancestral HGT events, followed by lineage-specific clonal expansion and diversification.

We also analyzed the repository for T6SS genes corresponding to genomic architectures GA1 and GA2. We found no association between the presence of GA1 and GA3, but a positive association between GA2 carriage and the absence of GA3 (Supplementary Fig. 1g and Supplementary Data 1). Specifically, GA1 positively associated with the GA3 combination ble1_bfe2, and GA2 with bfe1_bte2 (Supplementary Fig. 1g and Supplementary Data 1). The generalized lack of association between GA1/GA2 and GA3 is consistent with GA1/GA2 dissemination via HGT18,40,41 (Supplementary Fig. 1g and Supplementary Data 1). Together, these findings highlight the complexity of GA3 toxicity and immunity repertoires, underscoring the potential competitive advantage in the mammalian gut of strains with highly diverse effector-immunity arsenals.

A genetic platform for effector-immunity protein functional studies

The differential abundance of effector-immunity loci combinations suggests these proteins play discrete roles in competitive fitness. However, testing this hypothesis in clinical isolates is confounded by significant intraspecies genomic diversity, even among strains sharing identical locus I/II combinations (e.g., bte1_bte3_bte4 clusters; Fig. 1f). To isolate the selective advantage of T6SS effectors from other genetic determinants of colonization, we established an isogenic B. fragilis T6SS platform to examine the functional attributes of distinct ei pairs and locus I/II combinations. Deletion of locus I/II effector-immunity genes btei1_2 from strain NCTC 9343 (N1) rendered a Δt6-1 Δt6-2 mutant strain (Fig. 2a) with confirmed loss of T6SS activity as N1 Δt6-1 Δt6-2 showed blunted Hcp secretion similar to the N1 ΔtssC mutant (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In contrast, the parental N1 Δtdk and individual locus deletion strains (N1 Δt6-1 and N1 Δt6-2) permitted Hcp secretion (Supplementary Fig. 2a)42. Complementation of the Δt6-1 Δt6-2 mutant to examine effector hierarchy was approached by episomal expression of effector-immunity pairs in the pAH2 vector12, testing representative ei pairs found in locus I (bfei1, blei1, and btei1) and locus II (bfei2, btei2, and btei3). We were unable to clone intact bfe2, in agreement with a previous report19. Complementation of Hcp secretion (Supplementary Fig. 2c) and in vitro toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 2d) was achieved by ei pairs btei1 and btei2 that are native to N1, but not with those locus I/II effector-immunity pairs that are not found in N1. This prompted the hypothesis that additional genetic information was required for intoxication by non-naive N1 effectors. Within the locus I neighborhood, there are four hcp1 gene variants encoding the primary needle component (hcp2, hcp3, hcp4, and hcp5). Sequence alignments of hcp2/3/4/5 genes specific to bfe1, bfe1b, ble1, bte1, and cte1 revealed lower similarities from hcp2 and hcp3 (83.2% and 75.3% average identity, respectively) across the different loci I, in contrast to highly conserved hcp4 and hcp5 (92.1% and 91.6% average identity, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Thus, we tested the hypothesis that effector-specific hcp2 and/or hcp3 genes are required for intoxication. Complementation of the Δt6-1 Δt6-2 mutant with bfei1 showed no toxicity, which was reverted by co-expression with the cognate hcp2 and hcp3 genes (Fig. 2b). In the case of locus II effectors, we tested if specific vgrG spike-coding genes are necessary for toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Complementation of the Δt6-1 Δt6-2 strain with bte1 or locus I/II combination btei1_3 together with bte3-specific orf1/2/3 genes indicated that a specific vgrG gene is required for bte3-dependent toxicity (Fig. 2b). Bte1 and Bte3-specific toxicity was achieved only when btei3, accessory orfs and vgrG were co-expressed. Hence, in B. fragilis T6SS, effector toxins require specific accessory protein-coding genes for secretion/activity in vitro22–27.

Based on these findings, we modified the Δt6-1 Δt6-2 strain by deleting the hcp2/3, paar, and vgrG loci to generate the Δt6petit (Δt6p, Supplementary Fig. 2e) strain to permit investigation of ei function in the context of native accessory genetic loci. We used an established chromosomal integration plasmid for T6SS complementation constructs consisting of effector-immunity gene pairs or locus I/II combinations with corresponding accessory genes (Supplementary Fig. 2f)43. The Δt6p strain only exhibited T6SS activity when complemented with locus I/II combinations, confirming the role of effector-specific accessory genes in T6SS function (Supplementary Fig. 2g). In an initial exploration of this system, we demonstrated that each of the locus I effectors in the N1 Δt6-1 Δt6-2 strain (Supplementary Fig. 2h) and a series of locus I/II combinations (Fig. 2c) in the N1 Δt6p strains exhibit in vitro killing against the N1 Δt6p strain and two Bacteroidales species, Phocaeicola vulgatus and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron. Hcp secretion was confirmed for all locus I effectors (Supplementary Fig. 2i) and locus I/II combinations (Supplementary Fig. 2j), mirroring killing activity observed. We then extended these studies to examine whether the native T6SS activity of clinical B. fragilis isolates representing the 10 most abundant locus I/II combinations would exhibit killing of the N1 Δt6p strain. Up to three distinct B. fragilis isolates per effector combination were confirmed to manifest T6SS function (Supplementary Fig. 3a), then evaluated as the attacker in a competitive fitness assay against the N1 Δt6p strain. Similar to the control N1 Δt6p complemented with locus I/II combinations bte1_2 and bte1_3 (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3b), almost all clinical isolates demonstrated killing of the N1 Δt6p defender (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Nevertheless, observed intraspecies antagonism of B. fragilis clinical isolates against the N1 Δt6p defender is likely confounded by other non-T6SS mechanisms. Thus, through a rigorous investigation of the bte1_2 and bte1_3_4 effector configurations as the most abundant in our strain collection, we demonstrated that N1 Δt6p killing was dependent on T6SS function by deletion of tssC in representative bte1_2 and bte1_3_4 strains (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3c).

Bte3 exhibits broad species targeting

Previous studies evaluated T6SS effector toxicity from locus I/II combinations bfe1_2 and bte1_2 against non-Bacteroidales target cells, observing no competitive disadvantage of E. coli defenders19,20. Based on the T6SS genetic diversity in our B. fragilis repository, we hypothesized that one or more effectors may exhibit increased breadth of target cell killing. Analysis of the parental N1 Δt6p strain against an E. coli S17λ-1 pir strain revealed that E. coli survival was not impaired by the N1 Δt6p strain harboring an empty vector (pEV1) or bte1_2 (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 3d). Upon bte1_3 expression in N1 Δt6p, the parental E. coli S17-1 strain exhibited ~3 log survival reduction (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 3d, black circles) while E. coli S17-1 with pEV2 plasmid encoding Bti3 (pEV2/bti3) was protected from N1-mediated killing (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 3d, gray circles), defining bte3 as the first B. fragilis T6SS effector-coding gene to show in vitro toxicity beyond Bacteroidales19,20. We then examined if endogenous bte3 enables E. coli S17-1 targeting by clinical B. fragilis isolates. Killing phenotypes were only found for B. fragilis isolates harboring bte1_3_4 (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 3d, black circles), and relieved by using a ΔtssC bte1_3_4 attacker or by expression of Bti3 in E. coli (S17λ-1 pEV2/bti3, gray circles). Bte3-dependent E. coli killing was also shown for other laboratory and commensal strains, with varying degrees of toxicity as likely the resultant of attacker T6SS payload and growth rate (Supplementary Fig. 3e, bte1_3_4 isolate 13051 vs. N1) relative to defender growth rate and tolerance to anaerobiosis (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c, S17-1 versus CFT073). Extending these studies, we observed that the locus I/II combination bte1_3 displayed toxicity against S. Typhimurium (Fig. 2f, filled circles) and V. cholerae (Fig. 2g, filled circles) that was not observed with bte1_2, and was prevented by using a ΔtssC bte1_3_4 attacker and in defender strains harboring the bti3 gene (Fig. 2f, g, gray circles). While the vast majority of T6SS effectors have been demonstrated to be toxic in a contact-dependent manner, we cannot fully rule out that the broad toxicity of Bte3 is occurring in a contact-independent manner44,45. Examination of in vitro antagonism in liquid media between bte1_3_4 isolate 13051 and E. coli S17-1 strain showed no significant reduction in E. coli CFU, consistent with Bte3 as a contact-dependent effector (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Toxicity was not conferred against Gram-positive bacteria or Candida albicans (Supplementary Fig. 4e), suggesting that Bte3 specifically targets diverse Gram-negative bacteria.

Inspection of the btei3 locus led to identification of three hypothetical orfs downstream of the bti3 gene (Supplementary Fig. 1d). To examine the hypothesis that these loci are required for Bte3 secretion and/or toxicity, we complemented the N1 Δt6-1 Δt6-2 strain with constructs carrying pEV1-encoded bte1_3_orf1_2_3, bte1_3 alone, or each permutation of the orf1/2/3 genes in the context of bte1_3 expression (Fig. 3c, lower panel). While bte1_3 alone showed no bte3-based killing, the presence of orf1, orf2, and orf3 genes enabled bte3-specific intoxication (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, constructs containing orf2 and orf3 genes were sufficient to elicit a killing phenotype indistinct from the construct with orf1/2/3 genes (Fig. 3c). These findings prompted the hypothesis that Bte3 forms a pre-secretion complex with its specific VgrG and the proposed accessory proteins encoded by orf2 and orf3. To test this, we generated functional pEV1/bte1_3(orf2/3) derivatives on the N1 Δt6-1 Δt6-2 background in which we HA-tagged Bte3 and alternatively Flag-tagged VgrG, Orf2, or Orf3 (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Reciprocal Flag-tag and HA-tag immunoprecipitations revealed protein-protein interactions between Bte3 and both Orf2 and Orf3 (Supplementary Fig. 6a). We hypothesized that VgrG-Bte3 interactions were impacted due to anti-HA or anti-Flag antibody binding to their respective epitopes, as we did not observe reciprocal immunoprecipitations of VgrG and Bte3 upon expression of VgrG-Flag/Bte3-HA (Supplementary Fig. 6a). However, we hypothesize this is a technical artifact of pulldown on the C-terminally tagged VgrG interactome, as this construct rendered a T6SS functional strain that permitted Hcp, VgrG, and Bte3 secretion (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). When anti-Flag immunoprecipitates of VgrG, Orf2, or Orf3 were subjected to mass spectrometry (MS), protein interactions supportive of a pre-secretion complex formed by Bte3, VgrG, Orf2, and Orf3 were detected in Orf2 and Orf3-tagged samples (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Data 3). MS analysis of the VgrG-Flag strain immunoprecipitates showed blunted binding to secretion complex components and an enrichment in Hcp1 and members of the T6SS baseplate complex, including TssE, TssF, TssG, and TssK (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Data 3)46. Thus, our data is suggestive of a pre-secretion complex formed by Bte3, VgrG, Orf2, and Orf3.

To elucidate the identity of Bte3 accessory proteins Orf2 and Orf3, we generated structural predictions using ColabFold47 (Fig. 3e, f). Analysis of the Orf2 structural model revealed an 11-strand open β-barrel with a putative hydrophobic cavity (Fig. 3e), while similarity-based searches of the Orf2 model using Foldseek48 showed no matches with previously characterized proteins or folds. Following nomenclature used for a broad array of T6SS accessory proteins, Orf2 was thus named Adaptor for Bte3, or Ate3 (Fig. 3e)22,26,49. Protein domain predictions of Orf3 informed the presence of a domain of unknown function 4280 (DUF4280), described as a T6SS PAAR-like tip protein in Francisella spp and in the free-living Bacteroidota species Flavobacterium johnsoniae50–52. In agreement, the Orf3 predicted structure and Foldseek analysis strongly suggested that this domain functions as the PAAR tip protein for Bte3, which we thus designate as Pte3 (Fig. 3f).

The sum of the data generated so far supported the hypothesis of specific interactions between Bte3, Ate3, and Pte3 as required for T6SS function and secretion of Bte3. Thus, we were prompted to examine interactions between Bte3 and Ate3, or Bte3 and Pte3, in the presence or absence of Pte3 or Ate3, respectively. Using tagged versions of Bte3, Ate3, and Pte3, our data highlighted that while the lack of pte3 rendered partial loss of Bte3-Ate3 interactions, the lack of ate3 fully disrupted Bte3–Pte3 interactions (Fig. 3g). Altered protein interaction profiles were accompanied by a complete loss of Bte3 and Hcp secretion together with no T6SS-based reduction in defender CFUs (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). Our findings on the necessity of Ate3 and Pte3 for Bte3 secretion and T6SS function led us to investigate the degree of conservation of the members of this atypical spike complex. Structural analysis of Ate3 via Foldseek identified orthologs in T6SS gene clusters restricted to commensal (Bacteroides uniformis) (Supplementary Fig. 6f, BuAte3) and free-living (Flavobacterium columnare) (Supplementary Fig. 6f, FcAte3) Bacteroidota species, including the B. fragilis Bfe3-specific adaptor Afe3 (Supplementary Fig. 6f). Moreover, an Ate3 ortholog was found to be encoded by a fully conserved hypothetical orf upstream of locus II effectors bfe5 and bte5, thereby named as Afe5 and Ate5, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6f). Structure-sequence-based alignments via PROMALS3D53 of Pte3 and experimentally determined PAAR proteins from E. coli (PDB: 4JIW)54 and B. fragilis (PAAR for Bte2, or Pte2) (PDB: 8GRA)55 revealed striking differences (Supplementary Fig. 6g). E. coli PAAR and Pte2 presented three conserved β-sheets each, shown to establish interactions with VgrG monomers for spike assembly54,55 (Supplementary Fig. 6g). This observation was extended for the PAAR specific to the locus II effector Bfe2 (Pfe2) (Supplementary Fig. 6g). On the other hand, Pte3 presented two VgrG-interacting β-sheets (Supplementary Fig. 6g), a structural motif that was conserved in the PAAR-like ortholog specific to the locus II effector Bfe3 (Pfe3) (Supplementary Figs. 1d and 6g). Structure-based sequence alignments of Bte3- and Bfe3-specific PAAR-like proteins with PAAR-like proteins IglG from Francisella and Fjoh_3275 from F. johnsoniae indicated conservation of two VgrG-interacting β-sheets, and four cysteine residues essential for Francisella IglG-dependent T6SS phenotypes51(Supplementary Fig. 6g). Identification of structural orthologs of PAAR-like and adaptor proteins in divergent species suggests a broadly conserved T6SS effector delivery mechanism in Bacteroidota, in which coevolution of accessory proteins ensures exclusive secretion of cognate effectors.

Bte3 and Bti3 localization and orthologs in divergent Gram-negative bacteria

To shed more light on the mechanistic basis of Bte3-mediated intoxication, we examined Bti3 subcellular localization as a correlate to define Bte3-targeted cellular compartments. We separated periplasmic and outer membrane (OM) fractions from whole cells via a hyperosmotic cell envelope preparation method. Fraction purity was assigned using RNA Polymerase subunit alpha (Fig. 3h: RpoA, cytosolic) and Fragipain (Fpn, periplasm and OM), an outer membrane protease that undergoes self-cleavage upon surface exposure (Fig. 3h: Fpn, full length; Fpn*, cleaved)56–58. Flag-tagged Bti3 was restricted to the periplasm and outer membrane enriched fractions, consistent with the presence of a predicted lipoprotein signal peptide (Fig. 3h). While an N-terminal Flag tag appended to Bti3 showed periplasmic and outer membrane localization (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 7a) and functional immunity against Bte3 (Supplementary Fig. 7c, red circles), cellular localization and Bte3 protection was blunted with a C-terminal Bti3 Flag tag (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c, gray circles). Thus, our data on Bti3 localization-dependent immunity was indicative of Bte3 as an effector whose target is comprised in the periplasm and/or outer membrane.

HA-tagged Bte3 was found exclusively in cytosolic fractions when expressed alone (Fig. 3h), as expected for a protein lacking predicted signal peptides. Bte3 showed partial localization in periplasmic fractions when co-expressed with Bti3 (Fig. 3h). This unexpected finding also occurred when expressing Bte3 together with Bti3 and accessory protein genes in the N1 ΔtssC strain (Supplementary Fig. 7d), defining Bte3 localization as bti3-dependent and unrelated to Bte3 secretion or T6SS activity. We cannot discard that partial periplasmic localization of Bte3 was due to expression of bte3 from a non-GA3 genomic locus lacking cognate expression regulation. Notably, the Bte3 signal in whole cells appears as both an ~75 kDa band and a SDS-resistant high-molecular-weight aggregate, distinct from periplasm- and supernatant-enriched fractions that only display a single Bte3 band (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 6c, e). To further investigate proposed periplasmic/outer membrane toxicity by Bte3, we tested inducible expression of bte3 in E. coli as a cytosolic protein or fused to the signal peptide of the periplasmic protein PelB (Fig. 3i). Upon induction by IPTG, Bte3 showed a lethality phenotype only when expressed as a PelB signal peptide fusion (Fig. 3i, pelB-bte3), supporting our hypothesis that Bte3 targets the periplasmic space.

We then utilized an AlphaFold2 model of Bte3 to gain structural insights that may inform effector function. Bte3 displayed three N-terminal β-sheet sandwiches (β120–134, β2135–236, and β3273–411) and a C-terminal open β-barrel (aa 412–768) (Fig. 3j). β-barrel-containing proteins are known as stereotypical integral outer membrane proteins, leading us to hypothesize that Bte3 could interact with the outer membrane through its C-terminal domain59. The Bti3 structural model showed a unique combination of an N-terminal β-sandwich and four C-terminal alpha-helixes (Fig. 3k), rendering no predictive information on the immunity mechanism against Bte3. Foldseek searches of the Bte3 model identified structural orthologs with conserved domain composition and arrangement in commensal and free-living Bacteroidota, including locus II effector Bfe3 (Supplementary Fig. 7e). We also identified Bte3 orthologs in Pseudomonadota species, including E. coli, which present a single N-terminal β-sandwich (β322–155) (Fig. 3j, EcBte3). Bti3 structural orthologs co-occur with all Bte3 orthologs, suggestive of a broadly conserved immunity mechanism (Fig. 3k, EcBti3, and Supplementary Fig. 7f). Every bte3 and btei3 ortholog pair was found within a clear T6SS genetic arrangement (Supplementary Fig. 7g). Common to Bacteroidota ortholog operons was the presence of PAAR-like and Adaptor-coding genes (Supplementary Fig. 7g, gray boxes), which differed from orthologs in Enterobacteria that present a DUF4123-containing putative chaperone within the T6SS locus60 (Supplementary Fig. 7g). We tested expression in E. coli of the E. coli ortholog of bte3 (Ecbte3) as a cytosolic or signal peptide-containing protein and, similarly to Bte3 from B. fragilis, lethality was only observed upon periplasmic expression (Supplementary Fig. 7h, pelB-Ecbte3). Our observations suggest, in conjunction with the broad species killing phenotype of Bte3 in vitro (Fig. 2e–g), that Bte3-like proteins are periplasmic effector toxins that likely target interphylum-conserved elements of the bacterial outer membrane.

T6SS GA3 effectors define neonatal competitive advantage in mice

The versatility of ei pairing in naturally occurring B. fragilis strains, together with mechanistic studies that define the specificity of GA3 function, positioned us to test the hypothesis that T6SS diversity is central to in-niche competition hierarchy. We then started with the simplest genetic model possible, using two wild-type (bte1_2) isogenic N1 strains for competition. We assessed in vivo neonatal colonization of N1 pEV2 vs. N1 pEV1 isogenic strains at postnatal day 21 (Fig. 4b, left group, pEV1), revealing a slight dominance phenotype of the N1 pEV1 strain based on colony-forming unit (CFU) enumeration from feces (Fig. 4b, left panel) and cecum (Fig. 4b, right panel). The other group of neonatal mice were exposed to N1 pEV2 and T6SS-engineered N1 pEV1 strains, the latter with an added locusI/II combination bte1_3_4 (N1 pEV1 + bte3_4). Colonization studies showed displacement of N1 pEV2 at postnatal day 21 from feces and cecum when N1 pEV1 + bte3_4 was the competitor strain (Fig. 4b, right group, bte3_bte4). To examine competitive phenotypes as the sole result of bte3-associated gain of killing, we introduced Bti3 into a group of defender strains, providing protection in vitro against Bte3 attacks by a N1 pEV1 strain containing additional bte3 (N1 pEV1 + bte3) (Supplementary Fig. 8d, gray circles). Co-colonization of neonates with N1 pEV1 + bte3 resulted in displacement of the N1 pEV2 strain at postnatal day 21 (Fig. 4c, pEV2 group), whereas a defender strain equipped with bti3 (N1 pEV2 + bti3) was able to colonize similarly to N1 pEV1 + bte3, demonstrating that this immunity protein is both necessary and sufficient for Bte3 resistance in vivo (Fig. 4c, bti3 group).

Previous data from our group has shown that neonatal colonization by B. fragilis in mice occurs around postnatal day 1061. Thus, we tested if colonization phenotypes of N1 pEV1 + bte3 and N1 pEV2 were affected by niche pre-occupation due to a competitor strain. To this end, we maintained separate mono-colonized litters with N1 pEV1 + bte3 or N1 pEV2 (N1 pEV2 or pEV2 + bti3) strains until litters were co-housed from postnatal day 10 to postnatal day 21 (Supplementary Fig. 9a and Fig. 4d). Non-immune strain N1 pEV2 was readily displaced from the neonatal gut of most animals, regardless of the primary mono-colonization strain, as determined by differential ear punching of neonates (Fig. 4d (pEV2 group) and Supplementary Fig. 8b (top panels)). Bti3-containing N1 (N1 pEV2 + bti3) achieved high levels of colonization in animals originally colonized by N1 pEV2 or N1 pEV1 + bte3 as primary strains (Fig. 4d (bti3 group) and Supplementary Fig. 9b (bottom panels)), suggesting that the colonic niche remained open for T6SS-dependent occupancy at postnatal day 10. When co-housing was tested from postnatal day 14 to postnatal day 21, we observed no differences in N1 pEV1 + bte3 or N1 pEV2 colonization between non-immune and immune groups (Fig. 4e), indicative of primary strain occupancy effects on colonization output. While P14 co-housing showed that T6SS-independent dominance of N1 pEV2 over N1 pEV1 + bte3 occurred in neonates exposed to N1 pEV2 as primary strain (Supplementary Fig. 9c, right panels), we observed Bti3-dependent cross colonization of N1 pEV2 into neonates exposed to N1 pEV1 + bte3 as primary strain, similar to postnatal day 10 co-housing data (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c, left panels). Thus, at postnatal day 14, the neonatal niche still appeared as partially influenced by T6SS effects on stable colonization. This set of experiments revealed a discrete time window for T6SS-based superiority during neonatal colonization by isogenic strains with a larger effector repertoire, confirming the role of immunity proteins in early life colonization resistance.

We then interrogated if the same T6SS-based colonization advantage applied to strains with dissimilar genetic backgrounds, examining whether a T6SS-engineered strategy could be employed to restrict colonization of commensals with pathogenic potential during neonatal microbiome assembly. Our lab and others have been interested in understanding colonization and pathogenesis principles of enterotoxigenic B.fragilis (ETBF), a subset of B. fragilis strains that carry a gut-damaging protease known as B. fragilis toxin (BFT)57,62. Recent findings informed of BFT-dependent bacterial access to the colonic lamina propria in neonatal mice exposed to a T6SS-negative ETBF strain, a phenotype that could be reverted by introduction of a T6SS-positive non-toxigenic B.fragilis (NTBF) competitor strain during postnatal days 12 and 1661. We thus sought to test if increased NTBF effector load could preclude colonization by T6SS-active ETBF, protecting the host from neonatal gut damage12. We leveraged our clinical isolate collection and re-evaluated locus I/II combinations distribution based on absence (NTBF, 794/882 isolates) or presence of the bft gene (ETBF, 88/882 isolates) (Supplementary Fig. 10a). A bft-dependent skew in the locus I/II combination repertoires became apparent, highlighting an enrichment of bte1_bte5_bte6 and ble1_bfe2/ble1_bte2 combinations in ETBF isolates (Supplementary Fig. 10a). In line with the findings from our phylogenetic analyses, isolates showing association of bft with specific locus I/II combinations are grouped in genetically similar clusters. We consolidated candidate GA3-only NTBF/ETBF competitors matched for identical locus I/II combination (Supplementary Fig. 10b). To minimize confounding factors in competition outcomes, candidate strains were selected for absence of AIDs (Fig. 1f), and subgrouped based on their repertoire of non-T6SS antibacterial toxins known as Bacteroidales secreted antibacterial proteins63–67 (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Concomitantly, phylogenetic analysis of our pool of NTBF/ETBF isolates showed that most candidate pairs were binned in the same clusters of genetic similarity (Supplementary Fig.10c, except 13051NTBF/13360ETBF). We performed in vitro competition assays for candidate NTBF/ETBF pairs and established permutations in which NTBF could antagonize ETBF in vitro in a T6SS-specific manner (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Examination of in vitro competition between 13387NTBF/10968ETBF combinations, with endogenous ei pairs bfe1_8, revealed strain dominance by 13387NTBF engineered to express bte1_bte3 (Supplementary Fig. 10c, leftmost panel). BFT secretion phenotypes confirmed isolate 10968 as a bonafide ETBF strain (Supplementary Fig. 10d). Extension of these in vitro studies to the neonatal model to examine co-colonization (Fig. 4f) revealed no significant differences between strains in fecal and cecum CFU in T6-isogenic strain competition (Fig. 4g, left group, pEV1). Conversely, expression of bte1_bte3 in 13387NTBF positioned this strain to outcompete the 10968ETBF strain (Fig. 4g, right group, bte1_bte3). T6SS-dependent colonization restriction of the BFT-expressing isolate 10968ETBF was evident as a reduction in ETBF-dependent colonic damage in neonates, manifest as maintenance of cecum weight (Fig. 4h), colon length (Fig. 4i), and overall colonic architecture (Fig. 4j). In conclusion, this series of in vivo studies defines T6SS-effector diversity as a biological determinant of colonization advantage in the context of competitive interactions between genetically similar organisms during neonatal microbiota assembly. Our data is supportive of the role of Bte3 as a bona fide GA3 effector toxin that is capable of presenting a fitness advantage in the gut during early life, potentially accounting for the overrepresentation of bte3 within the T6SS repertoire from B. fragilis.

Discussion

In this study, we provide mechanistic support for the hypothesis that the B. fragilis T6SS mediates an effector-specific colonization advantage prior to weaning in neonatal microbiota-enabled mice. Utilizing a B. fragilis genetic platform in which we were able to assess both native and engineered T6SS locus I/II combinations, we advance a functional understanding of the array of T6SS effectors and effector-specific accessory proteins. The foundation of these studies is the largest single-source data set of B. fragilis clinical strains and corresponding genomic data through which we captured the genetic diversity and abundance of effector toxins to reveal several notable attributes of the T6SS. First, the bte1_3_4 combination was demonstrated to be the most prevalent effector combination, suggesting that these effectors may confer a fitness advantage. Second, we identified both novel and lineage-restricted locus I/II combinations, revealing ancestral HGT events that shaped T6SS effector diversity and clonal expansion. Third, our studies highlight the role of genetic context in informing the functional requirements for effector-specific assembly. Finally, in vivo studies during B. fragilis niche assembly illustrates the genetic and temporal specificity of distinct effectors and immunity proteins in ecologic fitness. Thus, while encounters of distinct B. fragilis isolates within the enteric microbiome of a given individual result from the stochastic nature of environmental exposures, it is possible that a hierarchy of effector-based outcomes may be predicted, informing of T6SS-based strain superiority.

Focusing on the role of Bte3 as a ubiquitous B. fragilis T6SS effector, we demonstrate that this protein intoxicates Gram-negative bacteria in vitro, including Bacteroidales and Enterobacteriaceae species. While these studies have not defined an intoxication mechanism for Bte3, data on Bti3 localization and Bte3 heterologous expression in E. coli indicate that Bte3 is a periplasmic toxin. Structural models of Bte3 and orthologs are defined by a C-terminal open β-barrel fold, distinct from a typical outer membrane-bound β-barrel protein68. The identification of structural orthologs of Bte3/Bti3 pairs in Bacteroidota and Pseudomonadota enabled us to appreciate T6SS genetic arrangements containing ortholog ei pairs that would otherwise have appeared unrelated due to their low sequence identity. Our overall data is suggestive that Bte3 intoxicates an unknown yet highly conserved moiety in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. Future directions to uncover the target of Bte3 could be guided by transposon screenings for resistant mutants in Bacteroides and Enterobacteriaceae species in the context of Bte3-expressing attackers, as well as proximity labeling techniques followed by mass spectrometry upon periplasmic Bte3 expression in E. coli.

Detailed studies of the bte3 genomic context led to the identification and characterization of a unique set of accessory proteins composed of the PAAR-like tip Pte3 and the novel adaptor Ate3. Conservation of key predicted structural features for Pte3 and Ate3 orthologs in Bacteroidota suggested that multiple species share a similar mechanism of spike complex assembly for Bte3. Recent studies have provided a functional and structural definition of a secretion complex comprising the Ate3 ortholog Afe5 (coined as Bacteroides T6SS adaptor protein C, BtapC) together with Bfe5 and Bfe7 (coined as BtpeA and BtaeB, respectively)69. In that context, deletion mutants of bfe5 and/or bfe7 displayed complete loss of T6SS function. In light of these timely findings, our data on the necessity of Bte3 for T6SS-based competition supports a hypothetical model in which Bte3 and orthologs are not only effector toxins but essential components of the spike complex.

Early studies suggest that harnessing the T6SS for deliberate perturbation of the microbiota may be a viable strategy for disease modification61,70. To this end, we have utilized in vivo competition studies in neonatal SPF mice as this model poses a rigorous scenario that entails initial colonization and establishment by B. fragilis in the context of a maternally-inherited community61. Within this context, we hypothesized that competition between isogenic organisms (N1 vs. N1) will be dictated mainly by T6SS function, whereas even closely related strains (13387NTBF/10968ETBF, cluster 19.9.5.8) will likely carry differential genetic determinants of stable colonization outcomes. Our in vivo data revealed that a competitive fitness advantage was awarded to any attacker when complemented with a bte3-based locus I/II combination, regardless of the T6SS isogenic competition phenotype. This finding suggests that T6SS effectors may exhibit a hierarchy of killing potency, wherein single effectors, such as Bte3 may confer a competitive advantage in isolation. In contrast, less potent effectors may require co-expression with other effector toxins to exhibit either additive or potentially even synergistic killing capability. At present, it remains possible that hierarchical differences in toxicity form the basis for the emergence of bte1_3_4-dominated clusters of genetic similarity as the most abundant from B. fragilis human isolates. A complementary hypothesis to account for the prevalence of bte1_3_4 relies on the generalized lack of bti3 and bti4 orphan genes across our isolate collection (Supplementary Data 1; 3/882 isolates), which could render most B. fragilis and Bacteroidales strains susceptible to intoxication by Bte3 and Bte4. Our findings on modified B. fragilis strains with increased functional payload diversity suggest that the T6SS machinery itself does not limit effector pairing. Phylogenetic analyses have pointed towards early founder events within the Div. I Major clade as the drivers of effector content and diversity. An intriguing possibility for the naturally limited number of ei pairs and combinations per isolate (bfe1_bfe5_bfe6_bfe7, n = 4 ei pairs) is that the mechanisms governing acquisition of GA3 through HGT and/or recombination-mediated “shuffling” of locus I and locus II are either currently absent or rare.

Our data on NTBF and ETBF strain diversity and T6SS-related competition is, through the lens of B. fragilis phylogeny, likely a product of evolutionary bottlenecks for expansion of bft in B. fragilis. We hypothesize that early branching of Division I Minor clade was accompanied by a GA3 loss founder event and followed by acquisition of bft and/or other clade-specific genetic determinants of colonization (Fig. 1g). In the case of GA3+ isolates within Div. 1 Major clade (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 10a), bft-enriched clusters also consisted of NTBF isolates harboring identical locus I/II combinations, as represented in our pool of competitor NTBF/ETBF pairs (Supplementary Fig. 10c). The pathogenicity island that comprises the bft gene is thought to be transmitted between B. fragilis strains via an integrative conjugative element, in a contact-dependent manner71,72. T6SS, also a contact-dependent mechanism, has been demonstrated to inhibit inter- and intra-species conjugation in P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii through effector-dependent antagonism73,74. Hence, we hypothesize that clustered dissemination of bft could be partially explained by increased transmission between genetically similar isolates, either lacking GA3 or harboring the same effector-immunity repertoire.

The use of a neonatal transmission model enables co-exposure of mice to distinct strains that may exhibit competitive interactions during early life, providing a system to test a plethora of T6SS-repertoires in vivo. Additional temporal dissection of our model through delayed co-housing revealed host developmental-specific time restrictions to T6SS effects, underscoring the dynamic nature of bacterial genetic determinants of colonization in stable community assembly. We hypothesize that this neonatal murine model, in combination with our T6SS genetic platform, will allow us to conduct studies to discern whether there is a functional hierarchy of locus I/II combinations. These studies must incorporate a comprehensive temporal analysis with sampling of neonates beyond weaning to monitor stable colonization by T6SS-based dominant lab strains and clinical isolates, which is of particular importance to strain combinations that have not shown competitor displacement at the time of weaning. Together, these studies are expected to provide a roadmap to incorporate active locus I/II effector combinations into lab-engineered and clinical B. fragilis strains. The present study has thus engendered new questions on the importance of T6SS from gut commensals while providing novel mechanistic insights and a set of tools that will allow us to further understand genetic determinants of inter- and intraspecies dominance in the neonatal murine gut. Our work underscores the relevance of T6SS diversity in B. fragilis early life competition and provides a potential roadmap to design microbial interventions in the context of neonatal acquisition of colonic commensals.

Methods

All experiments were carried out in compliance with regulations using protocols approved by the Institutional Biological and Chemical Safety Committee (IBC) at Washington University in Saint Louis.

Microbial strains, media, and growth conditions

Strains used in this study are detailed in Supplementary Data 4. Bacteroides strains were grown in brain heart infusion75 broth for liquid cultures and in BHI 1.5% agar for solid growth, supplemented with hemin 1 µg/ml and vitamin K1 0.5 µg/ml. Bacteroides cultures were incubated at 37 °C inside an anaerobic chamber (Coy Laboratories) with a 5% H2, 10% CO2, 85% N2 atmosphere. Antibiotics used with Bacteroides strains: gentamycin (Gm) 200 µg/ml, clindamycin (Cln) 5 µg/ml, rifampicin (Rif) 50 µg/ml, chloramphenicol (Cm) 10 µg/ml. E. coli, K. pneumoniae, S. typhimurium, and V. cholerae strains were grown in lysogeny broth (LB) and LB 1.5% agar media at 37 °C. Antibiotics used with E. coli strains: ampicillin 100 µg/ml, kanamycin 50 µg/ml. S. aureus and B. subtilis strains were grown in tryptic soy broth (TSB) and TSB 1.5% agar76 at 37 °C. L. monocytogenes and E. faecalis strains were grown in BHI and BHI 1.5% Agar at 37 °C. C. albicans strain SN425 was grown in yeast-extract peptone adenine dextrose (YPAD) or YPAD 2% agar at 30 °C.

Generation of a clinical isolate collection (David Hecht, Loyola University)

Bacteroides fragilis strains used for this study were from a collection of approximately 5000 clinical isolates of the Bacteroides fragilis group obtained from >10 medical centers and hospital systems in the Chicago metropolitan area over the period of 1989–2018 for the purposes of antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Anatomic sites of infection were provided by the respective medical center laboratory at the time the culture was received. All strains were purified and then identified using ANA IDENT II (Innovative Diagnostic Systems) with additional biochemical testing to determine species, as needed, and then frozen in double skim milk at −70 °C77 to establish a permanent collection at the Hecht Laboratory, Loyola University Chicago. The entire collection was donated to the Bubeck-Wardenburg laboratory at Washington University in 2018.

Generation of a clinical isolate sub-collection and genomic assemblies

A sub-collection of isolates was selected based on the most common B. fragilis body isolation sites (Peritoneal, abdominal, deep-seating, and blood infections)11. We purposefully enriched our subset of isolates in various isolation sites to potentially uncover a broader diversity of isolates than selecting isolates on the most prevalent sites (Supplementary Data 1, Isolation Site Table). gDNA was purified using a 96-well plate Blood and Tissue DNA Kit (Qiagen), and PCR-based sequencing libraries (iGenomX, Twist Biosciences) were sequenced in an Illumina Hi-Seq 3000 run (Illumina). Unicycler v0.4.7 and Prokka v1.13.3 were used for contig assembly and annotation, respectively78,79. Additional details in Supplementary Information.

Phylogenetic analysis of clinical isolates

Predicted hypothetical proteomes for each genomic assembly (919 isolate Fasta amino acid files + 8 control strains Fasta amino acid files) were analyzed for genetic distance using Prot-Spam v1.0, an alignment-free method for phylogeny of bacterial genomes80. Diagonal distance matrix was converted to a condensed vector with the Python v3.12.12 Scipy library v1.16.3 (https://scipy.org/), and agglomerative clustering was performed with UPGMA (unweighted pair-group method using arithmetic means) using average linkage. UPGMA tree was exported to Newick format and visualized using iTOL v7.5 (interactive Tree of Life, https://itol.embl.de/)81,82. For the tree representing all species (Supplementary Fig. 1A), the tree was displayed as unrooted and labeled with colored ranges for the clusters corresponding to Division I Major (blue), Division I Minor (green), and Division II clades (red). Taxonomy of non-B. fragilis isolates was determined using GTDB-tk v2.0, a tool built on the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB)83,84. For the B. fragilis-restricted tree, we processed a Prot-Spam matrix for the 888 B. fragilis proteomes (882 isolates + 6 control strains) and generated a UPGMA tree in Newick format. Three color strips were generated using the iTOL format, assigning each genetic attribute (GA3, AID, bft) per isolate and control strain. Fifty one clusters were generated by cutting the UPGMA dendrogram at a height of t = 0.00570. Cluster 19 (n = 556) was further cut into ten subclusters (19.1–19.9; t = 0.005419), and a subset of subclusters were cut into third (e.g., 19.9.5; t = 0.005166) and fourth degree clusters (e.g., 19.9.5.5; t = [0.002674, 0.002676]). Fisher’s exact test was used to determine significance (p < 0.05) of enrichment for each of the different genetic attributes across clusters from all degrees.

Survey of B. fragilis bte3 in human stool metagenomes

Publicly available datasets were downloaded from the DIABIMMUNE consortium (term-delivered infants and children; three cohorts referred to here as Vatanen, Kostic, Yassour)32–34, the Human Microbiome Project (HMP)28,85, and MetaHIT29,35 consortia (adults), and additional published fecal microbiome datasets from community term infants (Deveaux)36 and hospitalized preterm infants (Gibson, Gasparrini, Schwartz, Thänert; collectively abbreviated as GGST)30,31,37,38. Metagenomic read quality was assessed using FastQC v0.12.1 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Residual sequencing adapters were removed using Trimmomatic v0.3986, except for 40 metagenomes from the HMP, where “Illumina Universal Adapters” were detected, but no corresponding documentation could be found online. Residual contaminating human reads were removed using DeconSeq v0.4.387, referencing the human genome. Disordered reads were repaired using the repair.sh script from BBMap v39.01 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/). MetaPhlAn4 v4.0.288 was used to survey B. fragilis prevalence and quantify its relative abundance in metagenomes. Bowtie2 v2.5.2 was validated for the detection of bte3 in representative bte3 clinical isolate sequencing reads and used to align metagenomic reads to bte315,89. Detailed results of the metagenomic analysis are provided in Supplementary Data 2.

DNA reagents and constructs

Constructs and oligonucleotides are detailed in Supplementary Data 4. All constructs were generated using standard techniques, consisting of assemblies of PCR inserts with restriction enzyme-digested vector backbones. Additional technical details about constructs generated in this study are available in the Supplementary Information.

Genetic manipulation of Bacteroidales and non-Bacteroidales

For our studies in B. fragilis NCTC 9343 N1, we performed deletion mutants using pExchange-tdk, a standard negative selection system based on the thymidine kinase gene (Δtdk)90. For deletion of tssC in clinical isolates 10765 and 13051, we used pSIE1, an anhydrotetracycline (aTc)-based negative selection system reliant on inducible T6SS effectors91. For deletion of bte1, bte3, bte4, and bte1/bte4 in isolate 13051, we developed pK-bes, a pKNOCK-derivative allelic exchange vector with sucrose-based negative selection92. Deletion and complementation constructs were introduced into Bacteroidales strains via conjugation using the E. coli S17-1 strain as widely described. E. coli, S. typhimurium, and V. cholerae were transformed with constructs via electroporation using established protocols93. Additional details on vectors and construct generation, including pK-bes protocol for selection, are available in Supplementary Information and Supplementary Data 4.

T6SS effector toxicity in vitro experiments in E. coli

E. coli BL21 (DE3) electrocompetent cells were freshly transformed with pET22b and pET22b-derived constructs and plated in LB Amp 0.1% glucose plates and grown overnight at 37 °C. Individual colonies were used to inoculate liquid LB Amp 0.1% glucose cultures and grown overnight at 37 °C. Serial dilutions of stationary phase cultures were plated onto LB Amp 0.1% glucose and LB Amp 0.1 mM IPTG plates, and grown overnight at 37 °C. Growth inhibition was measured by visual inspection of plates.

Subcellular fractionations

B. fragilis N1 strains were grown to the stationary phase in 50–100 ml liquid cultures and used for subcellular fractionation with the Tris-sucrose-EDTA (TSE) method94. One hundred OD600 were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 20 min, and pellets were gently resuspended in 1 ml of TSE buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM sucrose, 1 mM EDTA) with protease inhibitors. Cells were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Supernatant containing cell envelope-enriched fractions (periplasm + outer membrane) was either analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blot or ultracentrifuged for 1 h at 100,000 × g. Supernatants enriched in periplasm were concentrated using TCA as described in this study, and outer membrane pellets were resuspended in 100 µl of Laemmli buffer. For whole cell controls, 1 ml of culture was pelleted and resuspended in 100 µl of Laemmli buffer.

In vitro competition assays and T6 secretion analysis

Competitions between Bacteroidales strains were carried out using log-phase-grown bacteria at a 5:1 attacker:target ratio for 16–24 h. For competitions with non-Bacteroidales, target cells were grown to the stationary phase and mixed with B. fragilis attackers at a 50:1 attacker:target ratio for 5 h. For all assays, equal volumes of attacker (normalized to 5 OD600) and Target (normalized to 1 OD600 for Bacteroidales, 0.1 OD600 for non-Bacteroidales) cells were mixed, and 5 µl were spotted onto a BHI plate. Agar spots were excised with a fine spatula or inverted sterile 1000 µl tip and deposited into a 2 ml tube with 1 ml of BHI. Tubes were vortexed for 10 s, and 100 µl of BHI was recovered, diluted, and plated. For Bacteroidales targets, viable cells were selected in BHI Rif (B. fragilis) or BHI Tet (non-B. fragilis). For non-Bacteroidales targets, survivors were aerobically grown in plates of the preferred target-specific media. For T6 secretion analysis of B. fragilis strains, 1 ml of log-phase-grown cultures was centrifuged for 5 min at 4000 × g. Pellets were resuspended in 100 μl of Laemmli buffer; 900 μL of culture supernatants were precipitated with 20% trichloroacetic acid95 for 15 min at 4 °C, centrifuged at 20,000 x g for 15 min, washed twice with acetone, and resuspended in 40 μL of Laemmli buffer. Typically, 1 µl of 0.1 M NaOH was added to neutralize the pH of TCA resuspended pellets. 10 µl of whole cells and supernatants were used for SDS-PAGE and Western blots. Liquid media competitions were set up in the same ratios and growth conditions as solid media competitions; strains were OD600-normalized, and 100 µl of each strain were mixed and seeded to a 96-well plate. Plates were covered with sterile breathable film and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 16–20 h. Each competition was serially diluted and plated in selective media as described above. Limitations to our study: As we have been unsuccessful at recombinant expression of Bte3 for antisera generation, we have used a non-GA3 locus genomic insertion to drive expression of bte3 tagged with HA under regulation of the rpoD (bt1311) promoter. Future studies will incorporate in situ gene tagging to study endogenous expression levels of bte3 and other T6SS-relevant genes.

Immunoprecipitations

Immunoprecipitations were carried out in standard conditions using 100 ml of B. fragilis log phase cultures. Additional details in Supplementary Information.

Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis

Technical details on the processing of TCA-precipitated samples for MS are available in Supplementary Information. Label-free quantification (LFQ) values from Supplementary Data 3 were plotted on a heatmap as log10 values to appreciate differences in the intensity of the peptides pulled down by the different anti-Flag immunoprecipitations. Data represents a single biological and technical replicate per condition; n = 1. To calculate sample enrichment versus the negative control, intensities were normalized, and log2 fold change (log2FC) was calculated for each protein in every pairwise comparison. Corresponding Z-score values were calculated as a measure of confidence to determine if enrichment of any given protein between a set condition and the control is an outlier in comparison to other proteins in the same sample (Z = 2 means a log2FC value of exactly 2 standard deviations over the average protein in that sample). Crossed boxes in the heatmaps represent eluates with low or non-detectable signal to allow for log10 value comparison.

Structure modelling and analyses

De novo protein structure models were generated with Alphafold2 via ColabFold (Alphafold2_advanced notebook) using MMseqs2 and no templates47. Structure models generated using ColabFold or available through UniProt (Supplementary Data 1) were used as input to search for similar structures using Foldseek48. For structure alignments, we used the super method for superimposition in PyMOL (version 2.5.8), with 5 cycles and a cutoff = 2 Å for outlier rejection. For Bte3, models were split into separate domains (β1–β2, β3, C-term open β-barrel) prior to alignment. For Bti3, signal peptides were trimmed from the models prior to alignment. For PAAR PyMOL comparisons, models were trimmed to a minimal tip domain for improved alignments. For PAAR structure-sequence analysis, models were loaded into the PROMALS3D server v1.0, and alignments were processed for Supplementary Fig. 6 using Geneious. Additional details on alignment metrics are available in Supplementary Data 1.

Animal modelling

All animal studies were conducted in accordance with ethical regulations using protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees and Institutional Biosafety Committees at Washington University School of Medicine. C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory or bred and maintained in-house in a specific pathogen-free facility under standard dark/light cycle, ambient temperature, and humidity conditions. Sample size estimates for animal experiments were based on prior animal modeling studies utilized within the laboratory for the investigation of colonization61. Vertical transmission modeling. B. fragilis strains for animal inoculation were grown overnight under anaerobic conditions to reach the stationary phase (16 h). Bacteria from 35 mL of culture were sedimented at 5000 x g for 8 min at 4 °C, decanted, and resuspended in 2 mL of 0.1 N sodium bicarbonate. For vertical transmission competition studies, two co-housed pregnant dams that were 8–10 weeks old at the time of mating received water containing 100 mg/L clindamycin from E14 through E17. On E15, each dam was gavaged with 100 μL of 0.1 N sodium bicarbonate containing 5 × 109 CFU of different B. fragilis strains. Dams were co-housed immediately after gavage, or single-housed until postnatal day 10 or 14 for delayed co-housing experiments. Induction and stability of colonization was assessed by collecting independent stool samples at multiple time intervals from dams and from pups at weaning age (Supplementary Figs. 7c, e–g, and 9e). Stool and cecal tissue homogenates (details in Supplementary Information) were then serially diluted and plated for CFU enumeration on BHI agar containing Gm and Cln, or BHI Gm Cln Rif added for differential selection of defender strains. Differential strain growth was also confirmed by verifying CFU fluorescence in a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System. CFU counts were log10 transformed prior to plotting, with a limit of detection set at 1000. In our neonatal model, most pregnant dams are colonized with >108 cfu/g of B. fragilis in feces; some dams spontaneously lose colonization during the pre-weaning period. For this reason, we systematically exclude data from cages in which dams were colonized below 1 × 107 CFU at the post-inoculation day 15–18, equivalent to postnatal day 10–13 (Supplementary Figs. 7c, e–g, and 8e). Our experience suggests that neonates in such cages will not become B. fragilis-colonized. Neonate cohorts included both males and females, and sex was not a considered variable in our colonization outcomes. Data is the combination of two or three independent experiments; all attempts at replication were successful. Pregnant dams were allocated randomly into the different experimental groups for co-housing at the time of birth, as well as postnatal co-housing. Neonates for post-natal cross-fostering were randomly allocated to their fostering cages. Blinding was not considered relevant for these studies because the primary experimental outcomes are strictly quantitative measurements. The assessment of competitive fitness relies on standard microbiological enumeration of bacterial CFUs on selective media, and the experimental groups were strictly defined by the precise genetic identity of the bacterial strains used, which were continuously verifiable via antibiotic resistance profiling and fluorescence markers (GFP/mCherry). Similarly, in vivo macroscopic assessments, such as cecum weight and colon length, are standard quantitative physical measurements, rendering the potential for investigator subjectivity to influence data collection or analysis negligible.

Statistics and reproducibility

For microbiome studies, two-tailed Fisher’s exact tests with Bonferroni correction were used for pairwise comparisons between groups. For cecum weight measurements, an unpaired, parametric, two-tailed Student’s t test was used for pairwise comparisons of means. For all other experiments, two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons was used when comparing the means of two groups. One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s comparisons was used when comparing two or more groups to a control group. Error bars represent SD, and horizontal lines represent comparisons between means. In the Fig. legends, n = X represents the number of biological replicates used for analysis. For all statistical analyses utilizing parametric tests (e.g., Student’s t tests and ANOVAs), the normal distribution of the data and homogeneity of variances were assumed without conducting formal normality tests. This assumption is based on well-established precedents and standard experimental practices within the field for in vitro type VI secretion system (T6SS) interbacterial competition assays and in vivo murine gut colonization models. Furthermore, to better approximate a normal distribution and satisfy parametric assumptions, bacterial CFU counts from both in vitro and in vivo experiments were log10-transformed prior to analysis, consistent with standard methodologies for analyzing microbiological enumeration data. All experiments presented in Figs. 2b–g, 3a–c, g–i, 4b–e, g–j, Supplementary Figs. 2a–d, g–j, 3a–e, 4a–e, 5a–d, 6a–e, 7a–d, h,8a–g, 9b, c, and 10c–e were independently reproduced at least once with similar results.
