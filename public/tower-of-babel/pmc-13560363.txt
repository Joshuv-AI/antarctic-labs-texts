Metagenomic-based quantification of Pseudomonas aeruginosa burden links microbiome collapse to mortality in severe community-acquired pneumonia
Authors: Junjie Zhao, Xinxin Chen, Xinyi Wang, Yidan Cui, Jiancheng Zhuge, Yi Zhang, Lu Zhang, Youqin Yan, Honglong Fang, Zhidan Hua, Gaoxiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere community-acquired pneumonia (sCAP) remains a major cause of mortality in critically ill patients, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) is a frequent pathogen associated with poor prognosis in this population. While metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) is widely used for pathogen detection, its value in quantifying pathogen abundance and linking it to lung microbiome alterations remains unclear.ObjectivesThis study investigated the association between P. aeruginosa abundance quantified by mNGS and lung microbiome alterations and clinical outcomes in sCAP patients.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included 130 patients with sCAP caused by P. aeruginosa from five hospitals (September 2021–June 2025). Patients were stratified into low, medium, and high abundance groups according to mNGS-derived reads per ten million (RPTM) values of P. aeruginosa. Lung microbiome diversity and community structure were analyzed, and differences between groups were assessed using appropriate statistical methods. The association between P. aeruginosa abundance and clinical outcomes was evaluated using correlation analysis, sankey diagram, receiver operating characteristic curve, grey zone analysis and logistic regression.ResultsA total of 130 patients with sCAP due to P. aeruginosa were stratified into low, medium, and high abundance groups based on mNGS-derived RPTM value. Microbial diversity decreased progressively with increasing abundance, and community structures differed significantly among groups (all P < 0.05). P. aeruginosa became increasingly dominant, accounting for up to 95.99% of the microbiota in the high abundance group. Higher P. aeruginosa abundance was associated with increased disease severity, including longer mechanical ventilation, prolonged hospital stay, and higher 28-day mortality. Sankey diagram showed a progressive decline in treatment effectiveness and an increase in mortality with increasing P. aeruginosa abundance. P. aeruginosa_RPTM showed moderate predictive value for mortality (AUC = 0.761, Sens = 69.40%, Spec = 75.30%, cutoff: 41122, grey zone: 2287–220339) and remained independently associated with 28-day mortality in multivariable analysis [2.219 (1.509 to 3.262), P < 0.001].ConclusionIn patients with sCAP, higher P. aeruginosa_RPTM measured by mNGS was associated with reduced lung microbiome diversity and unfavorable clinical outcomes. RPTM-based risk stratification may help identify patients at increased risk of poor prognosis.

Introduction

Severe community-acquired pneumonia (sCAP) remains a leading cause of mortality in critically ill patients worldwide [1, 2]. Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and supportive care, outcomes in patients with severe pneumonia, particularly those admitted to intensive care units, remain suboptimal [3]. Early identification of the causative pathogen and assessment of disease severity are critical for guiding appropriate treatment and improving prognosis [4].

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a well-recognized opportunistic pathogen associated with sCAP [4–6]. It is characterized by intrinsic and acquired resistance to multiple antibiotics, rapid adaptation to the host environment, and the ability to dominate the pulmonary microbiota under pathological conditions [7]. Previous studies have shown that infection with P. aeruginosa is associated with increased disease severity, prolonged hospitalization, and higher mortality in critically ill patients [8]. However, most conventional diagnostic approaches focus on pathogen detection in a qualitative manner, which limits their ability to reflect pathogen abundance and its relationship with disease progression.

The lung microbiome is increasingly recognized as a dynamic ecosystem that plays an important role in respiratory health and disease [9]. Disruption of microbial diversity, or dysbiosis, has been observed in patients with severe pneumonia and is often associated with the overgrowth of dominant pathogens [10, 11]. Prior studies have suggested that reduced microbial diversity is linked to worse outcomes in critically ill patients, highlighting the importance of understanding host–microbiome interactions in respiratory infections [12].

In this context, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has emerged as a powerful tool for comprehensive characterization of respiratory pathogens and the lung microbiome [13, 14]. Studies have demonstrated that mNGS has been increasingly applied in community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) [15, 16], particularly in severe cases [17, 18], and has been reported to improve pathogen detection compared with conventional microbiological methods. However, beyond pathogen identification, mNGS also provides semi-quantitative metrics, such as reads per ten million (RPTM), which have been used as indicators of microbial abundance in clinical samples. In our previous study, we observed that mNGS-derived RPTM was positively associated with length of hospital stay (LOHS) in patients with pulmonary mycobacterial diseases, and concurrent changes in lung microbiome diversity were also noted, suggesting its potential to reflect pathogen abundance, microbial community alterations, and disease severity [19]. Nevertheless, the clinical relevance of mNGS-derived quantitative metrics in sCAP, particularly in severe cases due to P. aeruginosa, remains poorly understood.

Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association between mNGS-derived P. aeruginosa abundance (as estimated by RPTM) and lung microbiome characteristics, as well as its relationship with clinical outcomes in patients with sCAP.

Methods

Study design and population

This multicenter retrospective study included patients with sCAP caused by P. aeruginosa who were admitted to five hospitals between September 2021 and June 2025. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Quzhou People’s Hospital (approval No. 2025 − 162). Due to the retrospective design and use of anonymized data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

The diagnosis of sCAP was established according to the ATS/IDSA criteria, defined by the presence of one major criterion or at least three minor criteria [4]. Identification of P. aeruginosa infection was based on conventional microbiological methods, including culture of respiratory specimens such as bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) or sputum. mNGS results were used for additional pathogen detection and quantitative analysis, but were not used as the sole basis for etiological diagnosis.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) mNGS results with detection of P. aeruginosa. Exclusion criteria included: (1) age < 18 years; (2) incomplete clinical data.

Data collection

Demographic characteristics, immunocompromised, laboratory parameters, and clinical outcomes were collected from electronic medical records. Disease severity was assessed using the APACHE-II score. Clinical outcomes included duration of mechanical ventilation (MV), LOHS, and 28-day mortality.

Definition of treatment effectiveness and immunocompromised status

To facilitate visualization of the relationship between microbial abundance, treatment response, and clinical outcomes in the Sankey diagram, treatment effectiveness was introduced as an intermediate clinical variable.

Effective treatment was defined as the achievement of clinical stability following therapy within 72 h. Clinical stability was considered when all of the following criteria were met: (1) body temperature ≤ 37.8 °C; (2) heart rate ≤ 100 beats per minute; (3) respiratory rate ≤ 30 breaths per minute; (4) systolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg without vasopressor support; and (5) oxygen saturation ≥ 90% or arterial partial pressure of oxygen ≥ 60 mmHg while breathing ambient air.

Ineffective treatment was defined as either failure of symptom improvement after treatment adjustment requiring a change to alternative antibiotics, or continued clinical deterioration and disease progression despite therapeutic modification [20, 21].

Immunocompromised status was included as a clinically relevant variable based on prior evidence demonstrating its association with increased risk and poor outcomes in sCAP [22, 23]. Patients were classified as immunocompromised if they had underlying conditions or treatments known to impair immune function, including but not limited to hematologic malignancies, solid organ transplantation, long-term corticosteroid use, immunosuppressive therapy, or other conditions affecting host immunity [24].

mNGS and pathogen quantification

BALF samples were processed using mNGS according to standard protocols. Sequencing data were analyzed to identify microbial taxa and calculate RPTM values for each detected organism. All BALF samples were processed and sequenced at a central laboratory (WillingMed, Beijing, China). Laboratory personnel performing the mNGS experiments and primary microbiome analyses were blinded to patients’ clinical outcomes.

The abundance of P. aeruginosa was quantified using RPTM values. Patients were stratified into three groups based on RPTM values: low abundance (< 10000), medium abundance (10000–100000), and high abundance (> 100000).

mNGS procedures

Sample processing and DNA/RNA extraction: BALF samples (1.5–3 mL) were obtained via bronchoscopy following routine clinical procedures. Samples were promptly centrifuged at 12,075 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and 500 µL of the supernatant was collected for nucleic acid extraction. Genomic DNA was extracted using the PathoXtract® Universal Pathogen Enrichment Extraction Kit (WYXM03202S; WillingMed, Beijing, China), while total RNA was isolated using the PathoXtract® Virus DNA/RNA Isolation Kit (WYXM03009S; WillingMed, Beijing, China), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Construction of DNA/RNA libraries and sequencing: For DNA-targeted detection, sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina DNA Prep (M) Tagmentation Kit (20018705; Illumina, San Diego, USA). When both DNA and RNA pathogens were analyzed, extracted RNA was first reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the SuperScript Double-Stranded cDNA Synthesis Kit (11917020; Invitrogen), followed by pooling with genomic DNA for library construction. Library quality and concentration were evaluated using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 550Dx platform, generating no fewer than 20 million reads per sample. Bioinformatic analysis: Sequencing data were processed using a standardized bioinformatics workflow. Raw reads in FASTQ format were subjected to quality control using Trimmomatic (v0.40) [25] to remove low-quality bases, adapter sequences, and short fragments. After quality filtering, host-derived sequences were eliminated by aligning reads to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.3) [26]. The remaining non-human reads were taxonomically classified using Kraken2 (v2.1.0) [27] against a curated microbial reference database comprising over 24,000 pathogenic organisms. For each detected organism, read counts were normalized to RPTM for downstream analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1), and figures were generated using OriginPro (version 2025b) and R (version 4.4.1). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for laboratory parameters and as median interquartile range (IQR) for other continuous variables. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. For comparisons among the three groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

Alpha diversity was evaluated using the Shannon and Simpson indices because these complementary metrics capture different aspects of microbial community diversity. The Shannon index reflects both species richness and evenness, whereas the Simpson index places greater emphasis on community dominance and evenness, providing a more comprehensive assessment of microbial diversity. Alpha diversity indices were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and beta diversity was assessed based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed to visualize differences in microbial community structure, and statistical significance was evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) and analysis of similarities (ANOSIM). Differentially abundant taxa were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), with an LDA score > 2.0 and a P ≤ 0.05 were used as thresholds for statistical significance. Associations between microbial features and clinical outcomes, including duration of MV, LOHS, and 28-day mortality, were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the predictive value of P. aeruginosa_RPTM for 28-day mortality, and grey zone analysis was further applied to determine the range of inconclusive cutoff values [28]. Sankey diagram were constructed to visualize the distribution of microbial abundance groups across treatment effectiveness and corresponding clinical outcomes. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with 28-day mortality, and results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Missing values were handled using multiple imputation via the mice package (version 3.12.0), with 10 imputed datasets generated. The proportion of missing data was low and did not involve key variables. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 148 patients with sCAP caused by P. aeruginosa were initially screened from five hospitals between September 2021 and June 2025. After excluding 7 patients aged < 18 years and 11 patients with incomplete clinical data, 130 patients were finally included in the analysis. Based on the mNGS-derived RPTM of P. aeruginosa, patients were stratified into low (n = 61), medium (n = 30), and high (n = 39) abundance group (Fig. 1).

Fig 1. Study flow chart

Study flow chart

Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. No significant differences were observed among the three groups in age, sex, or immunocompromised status. However, markers of inflammation and disease severity showed a stepwise increase across groups. Specifically, white blood cell count (WBC) and procalcitonin (PCT) levels were significantly higher in the high abundance group (both P < 0.001). Similarly, clinical outcomes worsened with increasing RPTM levels, as reflected by longer duration of mechanical ventilation (MV) and LOHS (both P < 0.001). The 28-day mortality rate increased progressively from 21.31% in the low abundance group to 43.33% in the medium abundance group and 58.97% in the high abundance group (P = 0.001).

Microbiome analysis

Alpha diversity analysis demonstrated a significant decline in microbial diversity with increasing P. aeruginosa abundance. Both Shannon and Simpson indices were highest in the low abundance group, followed by the medium abundance group, and lowest in the high abundance group (all P < 0.05, Fig. 2A).

Beta diversity analysis based on Bray–Curtis distance revealed distinct microbial community structures across the three groups. PCoA analysis showed clear separation with minimal overlap (PC1: 26.64%, PC2: 14.95%, Fig. 2B), which was further supported by PERMANOVA (R2 = 0.343, P < 0.001) and ANOSIM (R2 = 0.393, P < 0.001, Fig. 2C).

At the compositional level, the low abundance group exhibited the most diverse microbial distribution, whereas P. aeruginosa progressively dominated in the medium and high groups (Fig. 2D). In the high abundance group, P. aeruginosa accounted for 95.99% of the total microbial abundance. LEfSe analysis identified Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans as significantly enriched taxa in the low abundance group, while P. aeruginosa was significantly enriched in the high abundance group, with no differential taxa detected in the medium abundance group (Fig. 2E).

Clinical and prognostic analysis

Spearman correlation analysis showed that P. aeruginosa_RPTM was positively correlated with duration of MV, LOHS, APACHE II score, and WBC level (Fig. 3A). Sankey diagram further demonstrated that, as P. aeruginosa_RPTM increased from low to high levels, the proportion of patients achieving effective treatment decreased, whereas treatment failure increased. Among patients with treatment ineffective, mortality rates also increased progressively (Fig. 3B).

ROC curve showed that P. aeruginosa_RPTM had moderate predictive performance for 28-day mortality, with an area under the curve of 0.761 (Fig. 3C). The optimal cutoff value was 41,122, with the sensitivity of 69.6% and specificity of 70.2%. Grey zone analysis indicated that P. aeruginosa_RPTM below 2287 were associated with approximately 90% survival, whereas values above 220,339 were associated with approximately 90% mortality (Fig. 3D). In univariate analysis, age (P = 0.081), APACHE II score (P = 0.018), immunocompromised status (P = 0.032), and P. aeruginosa_RPTM (P < 0.001) were associated with 28-day mortality. In multivariate analysis, only P. aeruginosa_RPTM remained an independent predictor (ORs = 2.219, P < 0.001), while immunocompromised status showed a non-significant trend (OR = 2.287, P = 0.098) (Fig. 3E).

Discussion

In this study, we found that increasing P. aeruginosa_RPTM, quantified by mNGS, was associated with a progressive loss of microbial diversity, dominance of a single pathogen, and significantly worse clinical outcomes in patients with sCAP. Importantly, P. aeruginosa_RPTM remained an independent predictor of 28-day mortality, suggesting that quantitative microbial abundance provides prognostic information beyond conventional clinical variables.

Previous studies have consistently shown that alterations in the lung microbiome are closely linked to disease severity in pneumonia. For example, a large cohort study reported that CAP is characterized by greater microbiota disruption, pathogen dominance, and altered microbial interactions, which were associated with worse clinical status [29]. Similarly, studies in severe CAP populations have demonstrated that reduced microbial diversity and compositional shifts are associated with disease severity and organ dysfunction [30]. Our findings are consistent with these observations but extend them by demonstrating a clear gradient effect, in which increasing P. aeruginosa abundance is accompanied by stepwise reductions in alpha diversity and progressive separation in beta diversity. This suggests that higher microbial abundance is associated with progressive alterations in microbiome structure rather than merely reflecting disease severity.

Another notable finding is the near-complete dominance of P. aeruginosa in the high abundance group (Fig. 2D). Similar ecological patterns have been described in other respiratory infections. For instance, in pulmonary tuberculosis, pathogen-positive samples are often dominated by a single organism with markedly reduced diversity, in contrast to more diverse communities in non-infected individuals [31]. In CAP patients with invasive pulmonary aspergillosis, significant reductions in alpha diversity and distinct microbial community structures have also been reported [32]. Together, these findings support the concept that severe infections are frequently associated with a transition toward low-diversity, pathogen-dominated microbiomes, which is consistent with the ecological collapse observed in our high abundance group.

We also found that P. aeruginosa_RPTM was strongly correlated with clinical severity indicators, including APACHE II score, inflammatory markers, and key outcomes such as duration of MV and LOHS (Fig. 3A). This suggests that quantitative pathogen abundance, as captured by mNGS, may provide additional clinical insight beyond microbiome structure alone. While previous studies have linked microbiome alterations to host immune dysregulation and adverse outcomes [32], our findings further indicate that quantitative mNGS-derived RPTM may serve as a more direct and clinically actionable marker reflecting both microbial dynamics and host response.

A novel aspect of our study is the integration of microbial abundance, treatment effectiveness, and prognosis using a Sankey diagram (Fig. 3B). While prior studies have demonstrated that early clinical response is closely associated with mortality in sCAP [33, 34], few have incorporated microbiological parameters into this trajectory. Our findings indicate that higher RPTM is associated with reduced likelihood of effective treatment and increased mortality among patients with treatment failure, suggesting that higher microbial abundance is associated with poorer treatment response and increased mortality.

From a prognostic standpoint, P. aeruginosa_RPTM showed moderate predictive performance for 28-day mortality (AUC = 0.761, Sens = 69.40%, Spec = 75.30%) (Fig. 3C). Notably, grey zone analysis further improved its clinical interpretability by defining ranges associated with markedly different outcomes (Fig. 3D). Patients with P. aeruginosa_RPTM values below 2287 were highly likely to survive, whereas those with values above 220,339 were at substantially increased risk of death. These findings suggest that P. aeruginosa_RPTM may be useful for early risk stratification in clinical practice.

In subsequent regression analysis, P. aeruginosa_RPTM remained an independent predictor of 28-day mortality, whereas traditional risk factors such as immunocompromised status did not retain statistical significance (Fig. 3E). This finding suggests that the association between host immune status and clinical outcomes may be partially explained by differences in microbial abundance and ecological disruption. Indeed, previous studies have shown that immunocompromised patients exhibit significantly higher mortality and distinct disease trajectories in CAP [22]. In addition, emerging evidence from critically ill populations indicates that microbial dominance and dysbiosis are linked to host inflammatory responses and mortality [35].

In this context, RPTM as a quantitative mNGS-derived metric, may capture these downstream host–pathogen–microbiome interactions more directly, thereby serving as a more proximal indicator of disease progression. This association may account for the independent prognostic value of P. aeruginosa_RPTM observed in our multivariable analysis. These findings suggest that mNGS-derived P. aeruginosa RPTM may complement, rather than replace, established clinical severity assessment tools, such as APACHE II and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score. Integrating quantitative mNGS-derived pathogen abundance with conventional clinical scores may improve early risk stratification and support more individualized clinical decision-making in patients with sCAP. Future prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate this integrated approach.

It should also be acknowledged that P. aeruginosa exhibits substantial genomic and phenotypic heterogeneity. Previous studies have shown that different strains may harbor diverse plasmids, virulence determinants, and antimicrobial resistance genes, resulting in considerable variation in pathogenicity, antimicrobial susceptibility, and clinical outcomes [36–39]. Therefore, although higher P. aeruginosa_RPTM reflects greater microbial abundance and was strongly associated with disease severity in our cohort, microbial abundance alone cannot fully capture strain-specific biological characteristics that may also influence prognosis. Integrating quantitative mNGS results with genomic information, including resistance and virulence determinants, may provide a more comprehensive assessment of pathogen behavior and facilitate precision antimicrobial therapy in future studies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although RPTM provides a semi-quantitative estimate of pathogen abundance, it does not directly reflect the absolute bacterial load. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted in terms of relative microbial abundance rather than absolute pathogen load. Second, its retrospective design may introduce selection bias and residual confounding. Third, the sample size was relatively limited, and the RPTM cutoff and grey zone identified in this study require external validation. Fourth, mNGS was performed at a single time point, and dynamic changes in microbial abundance were not assessed. Finally, although RPTM reflects the relative abundance of P. aeruginosa, it does not provide information regarding strain-specific genomic features, such as virulence determinants, plasmid carriage, or antimicrobial resistance genes, all of which may influence pathogenicity and clinical outcomes. Future studies combining quantitative mNGS with genomic characterization of P. aeruginosa are warranted.

Conclusion

mNGS has been widely used as a diagnostic tool for pathogen detection, but its quantitative potential remains underexplored. In this study, we demonstrate that mNGS-derived RPTM of P. aeruginosa is closely associated with microbiome disruption and adverse clinical outcomes in patients with sCAP. Importantly, P. aeruginosa_RPTM showed prognostic value and may serve as a useful tool for early risk stratification. These findings highlight the potential of quantitative mNGS metrics to extend beyond pathogen identification toward clinically meaningful interpretation. Nevertheless, prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the clinical utility and generalizability of RPTM-based risk stratification before its routine clinical implementation.
