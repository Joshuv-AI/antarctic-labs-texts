Highest weight vectors of tensors
Alimzhan Amanov and Damir Yeliussizov

Abstract
. We study highest weight vectors for symmetric and alternating spaces of tensors, whose dimensions are given by generalized Kronecker coefficients. We describe the algebraic relations for classical constructions of corresponding spanning sets of highest weight vectors. The proof is based on important duality that we discover for these highest weight spaces and vectors. As applications of duality, we also give conceptual interpretations to power expansions of Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant and its dual exterior form.

One of the main problems that we resolve in this paper, refers to description of all algebraic relations among these highest weight vectors. As we show, to solve this problem it is important to study both symmetric and alternating spaces and dual highest weight vectors. The explicit forms

In particular, we describe all linear relations for these spanning sets of highest weight vectors.
Note that this problem is related to the construction of bases for the spaces (
1

Our main results are summarized as follows:

1. Algebraic relations.

We describe the algebraic relations among highest weight vectors
\{\Delta\}
and
\{\nabla\}
spanning the spaces (
1
). The proof is based on the next two important results.

2. Duality isomorphism of highest weight spaces.

With highest weight vectors
\{\Delta\}
and
\{\nabla\}
, we construct explicit isomorphisms for dual (equidimensional) highest weight spaces.

3. Duality of highest weight vectors.

We discover a natural yet nontrivial duality for expansions of highest weight vectors

\{\nabla\}

The proof of this property relies on constructing some invariants of certain double cosets of Young subgroups.
The duality is crucial for proving both of the above results.
In special cases, this duality also gives conceptual interpretations to power expansions of Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant and its dual exterior Cayley form; these expansions are related to evaluations of fundamental SL-invariants of tensors and higher-dimensional analogues of the Alon–Tarsi conjecture on Latin hypercubes, and have important implications to positivity of Kronecker coefficients.

Theorem 1.3

(Duality for
\Delta,\nabla

Let

(i) For odd

\displaystyle\Delta_{T}

\displaystyle=\pm\sum_{S\in\mathsf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}~\langle\nabla_{S},\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T}\rangle\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S},\quad T\in\mathsf{T}_{01}(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{S}

\displaystyle=\pm\sum_{T\in\mathsf{T}_{01}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}\langle\Delta_{T},\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S}\rangle\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T},\quad S\in\mathsf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}}).

(ii) For even

\displaystyle\Delta_{S}

\displaystyle=\pm\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\mathsf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}~\langle\Delta_{S^{\prime}},\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S}\rangle\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S^{\prime}},\quad S\in\mathsf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}}),

2.7.

All the groups we consider are over

The group

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}

and

For the

(where

is given by

(G_{1},\ldots,G_{d})\cdot v_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{d}=G_{1}v_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes G_{d}v_{d},

where
v_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
G_{i}\in\mathrm{GL}(n)
. This action induces the action on the tensor space
\bigotimes^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
.
For the group
\mathrm{GL}(n)^{\times d}
the definitions above can be rewritten similarly, where

is replaced with

,

with

and the weights
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}

.
We denote by
\mathrm{WV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}V
and

of weight

2.10.
Kronecker coefficients

The dimensions of the highest weight subspaces that we consider are given by generalized Kronecker coefficients. The
generalized Kronecker coefficient

g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, indexed by a
d
-tuple
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
of partitions of
m
, is the multiplicity of the trivial
S_{m}
-irreducible representation
[1\times m]
in the tensor product
[\lambda^{(1)}]\otimes\ldots\otimes[\lambda^{(d)}]
of
S_{m}
-irreducibles (tensor product is again a representation with diagonal
S_{m}
-action). Alternatively,
g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is the multipcity of
[\lambda^{(1)}]
in
[\lambda^{(2)}]\otimes\ldots\otimes[\lambda^{(d)}]
.
It is known, that
[\lambda]\otimes[\mu]\cong[\lambda^{\prime}]\otimes[\mu^{\prime}]
, which, together with the above, implies the following properties:

(a)

g(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(d)})=g(\lambda^{(\sigma(1))},\ldots,\lambda^{(\sigma(d))})
for any permutation
\sigma\in S_{d}
.

(b)

g(\lambda^{(1)},\lambda^{(2)},\ldots,\lambda^{(d)})=g((\lambda^{(1)})^{\prime},(\lambda^{(2)})^{\prime},\lambda^{(3)},\ldots,\lambda^{(d)})
.

(c)

g(1\times m,\boldsymbol{\lambda})=g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
.

For
d=2
, we have
g(\lambda,\mu)=\delta_{\lambda,\mu}
is the Kronecker delta and for
d=3
we recover the usual Kronecker coefficients.
Then we have

\dim\mathrm{HWV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}=g(1\times m,\boldsymbol{\lambda})=g(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\quad\text{for any }d\geq 2,

and

\dim\mathrm{HWV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}=g(m\times 1,\boldsymbol{\lambda})=g(1\times m,(\lambda^{(1)})^{\prime},\lambda^{(2)},\ldots,\lambda^{(d)}),

where the latter is equal to
g((\lambda^{(1)})^{\prime},\lambda^{(2)},\ldots,\lambda^{(d)})
, which is rewrites to
g(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})
for odd
d
.

Remark 2.1
.

The Kronecker coefficients can also be defined via representations of
\mathrm{GL}(n)
.
Consider
\mathrm{GL}(n^{d})
-irreducible representation
V_{n^{d}}(1\times m)
(the subscript emphasizes the group) of degree
m
polynomials. Under the group homomorphism
\mathrm{GL}(n)^{\times d}\to\mathrm{GL}(n^{d})
, given by
g_{1}\times\ldots\times g_{d}\to g_{1}\boxtimes\ldots\boxtimes g_{d}
(where
\boxtimes
is Kronecker product of matrices), this representation restricts to the representation of its subgroup
\mathrm{GL}(n)^{\times d}
and
g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is the multiplicity of irreducible
V_{n}(\lambda^{(1)})\otimes\ldots\otimes V_{n}(\lambda^{(d)})
in this decomposition.

Definition 3.1

(Signature function)

Let

\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(\sigma):=\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma(\mathrm{block}_{1}))\cdots\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma(\mathrm{block}_{\ell}))

(3)

where for a set

we denote
\sigma(B)=(\sigma(b_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(b_{k}))
and

\mathrm{sgn}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{sgn}(a),&\text{if }a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\text{ is a permutation of $[k]$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4)

For example, for

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sgn}(1234)\,\mathrm{sgn}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}123})\,\mathrm{sgn}({\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}12})=+1,

\displaystyle\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(23{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}32}{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}1}14{\color[rgb]{1,0.5,0}2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1})

\displaystyle P^{\prime}_{T}

Definition 4.3
.

For a word

a pair

i<j
and

We choose this notation to indicate that

can be zero.

We are now ready to define the highest weight vectors.

(HWV polynomials and forms)

For a

\displaystyle\Delta_{T}

Lemma 4.6

(Column swap rule)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
be a
d
-tuple of partitions of
m
and
S,T\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})
. Then for any
\pi\in S_{m}
we have:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\pi T}=\mathrm{sgn}(\pi)^{d}\Delta_{T}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nabla_{\pi S}=\mathrm{sgn}(\pi)^{d+1}\nabla_{S}.

Proof.

It is enough to show the result for a transposition
\alpha=(i,i+1)
of
S_{m}
for some
i\in[m-1]
. We bijectively map the term
\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S}
of
\Delta_{T}
in (
15
) to the term
\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{\alpha S}
of
\Delta_{\alpha T}
. Let us compare their coefficients and show that

(-1)^{T}\mathrm{sgn}_{T}(X)=(-1)^{d}(-1)^{\alpha T}\mathrm{sgn}_{\alpha T}(\alpha X).

Each signature above is the product of signs over the rows.
For a fixed
k
-th row
t_{k}
, the sign
\mathrm{sgn}_{\alpha t_{k}}(\alpha X)
changes by
-1
only whenever we change adjacent letters of the same block of
t_{k}
. On the other hand,
(-1)^{\alpha T}
changes by
-1
if distinct letters were swapped. Hence, the product changes by
-1
in any case in each row, implying the total change by
(-1)^{d}
. The same holds for
\nabla_{\alpha S}
, but there is an extra sign change due to the property of the wedge product. Since
S_{m}
is generated by transpositions of adjacent letters, the result follows.
∎

On the other hand, by Lemma

,
\Delta_{(1,2)T}=(-1)^{d}\Delta_{T}=-\Delta_{T}
for odd
d
and
\nabla_{(1,2)T}=(-1)^{d+1}\nabla_{T}=-\nabla_{T}
for even
d
, which implies the statement.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.5
.

First, let us show that
\Delta_{T}
and
\nabla_{S}
are indeed highest weight vectors. By symmetrization of
P_{T}
and alternation of
P_{S}
we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sym}(P_{T})

\displaystyle=\sum_{X\in A(\lambda)}\mathrm{sgn}_{T}(X)\ \mathrm{Sym}\left(\bigotimes e_{X}\right)=\sum_{X\in A(\lambda)}\mathrm{sgn}_{T}(X)\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{X}=\widetilde{\Delta}_{T}\cdot||T||,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Alt}(P_{S})

\displaystyle=\sum_{Y\in A(\lambda)}\mathrm{sgn}_{S}(Y)\ \ \mathrm{Alt}\left(\bigotimes e_{Y}\right)\ \ =\sum_{Y\in B(\lambda)}\mathrm{sgn}_{S}(Y)\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{Y}=\widetilde{\nabla}_{S}\cdot||S||.

By Lemma
4.7
,
\Delta_{R}=0
for odd

and
\nabla_{R}=0
for even

whenever

. Since the family of tensors

(or

) span the highest weight subspace of the tensor space (Proposition

), the projection of this family (of highest weight tensors) to the corresponding subspace of polynomials (or forms) spans the highest weight subspace of polynomials (or forms) of the corresponding weight.
∎

Note that the sets

of lattice tables and

of lex-tables are not subsets of one another. Using Lemma

.

The statement of Theorem
4.5
is true if the sets

and

are replaced with

and

, i.e. if lattice tables are replaced with lex-tables.

4.2.
Some useful properties

We now discuss several properties of these highest weight vectors and forms.

4.2.1.
Internal symmetries

Some pairs of tables produce the same polynomial or form, up to a constant. This happens due to the existence of some internal symmetries. Let us show a straightforward one.

Proposition 4.9

(Slice symmetry)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
be a
d
-tuple of partitions of
m
and
1<i<j\leq\ell(\lambda^{(1)})
be two rows of its first partition. Fix
T\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
and let
T^{\prime}
be the table obtained by swapping the letters
i
and
j
in the first row, i.e. relabelling of blocks. Then the weight of
T^{\prime}
is
((i,j)\lambda^{(1)},\lambda^{(2)},\ldots)
, but the vectors

\Delta_{T}=(-1)^{\lambda_{i}^{(1)}\cdot\lambda_{j}^{(1)}}\Delta_{T^{\prime}},\qquad\nabla_{T}=(-1)^{\lambda_{i}^{(1)}\cdot\lambda_{j}^{(1)}}\nabla_{T^{\prime}},

are still highest weight vectors of weight
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.

Proof.

By definition of polynomials and forms, the numbering of the blocks only affect the signature
(-1)^{T}
. Since
\lambda_{i}^{(1)}\cdot\lambda_{j}^{(1)}
swaps are performed, the signature changes by this amount.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

In terms of hypermatrices, the swap of block labels means slice swaps. Thus, when we consider index set of the highest weight vectors, we may narrow it to the orbits of hypermatrices
A^{\mathrm{lex}}_{d}
and
B^{\mathrm{lex}}_{d}
, by considering only orbits under the swaps of certain slices.

4.2.2.
Horizontal concatenation

If we have two tables
P,Q\in A_{d}
, it is natural to consider some operations on the tables.

Definition 4.10

(Horizontal concatenation)
.

Let
\lambda\vdash m
be a partition. For a positive integer sequence
\alpha
, which is a permutation of
\lambda
, let
w\in A(\alpha)
be a word of weight
\alpha
. By
w+x
for
x\in\mathbb{N}
denote the word with each
w_{i}
increased by
x
, i.e.
(w+x)_{i}=w_{i}+x
.

For a pair of words
(p,q)\in A(\alpha)\times A(\beta)
of weights
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell})
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r})
(not necessarily partitions), define the new word

p\oplus q:=p.(q+\ell)\in A(\alpha.\beta)

where ‘.’ denotes the concatenation of words. For example, if

p=11122,

q=122333,
then

p\oplus q=p.(q+2)=11122\mathbf{344555},

where
\alpha=(3,2),\beta=(1,2,3)
and the resulting word has weight
(3,2,1,2,3)
. If
\alpha
and
\beta
are partitions, then the resulting weight is
w(p\oplus q)=\alpha\uplus\beta:=(\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime})^{\prime}
, i.e. vertical sum.

Similarly, we extend this operation to
d
-tables. Fix
d
and let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
be
d
-tuples of partitions of
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, respectively. Let
P=(p_{1},\ldots p_{d})\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
and
Q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{d})\in A_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mu})
be
d\times m_{1}
and
d\times m_{2}
tables. Define
d\times(m_{1}+m_{2})
table
P\oplus Q\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\uplus\boldsymbol{\mu})
by setting
i
-th row
(P\oplus Q)_{i}=p_{i}\oplus q_{i}
. For example, for
d=2
we have:

P=\begin{pmatrix}11122\\
11122\end{pmatrix},Q=\begin{pmatrix}111123\\
112323\end{pmatrix},P\oplus Q=\begin{pmatrix}11122\textbf{333345}\\
11122\textbf{334545}\end{pmatrix},

which in terms of matrices corresponds to

P=\begin{tabular}[]{ | c | c | }\hline\cr 3&0\\
\hline\cr 0&2\\
\hline\cr\end{tabular},\quad Q=\begin{tabular}[]{ | c | c| c | }\hline\cr 2&1&1\\
\hline\cr 0&1&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&1\\
\hline\cr\end{tabular},\quad P\oplus Q=\begin{tabular}[]{ |c|c|c|c|c| }\hline\cr 3&0&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&2&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\bf{2}&\bf{1}&\bf{1}\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\bf{0}&\bf{1}&\bf{0}\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\bf{0}&\bf{0}&\bf{1}\\
\hline\cr\end{tabular},

i.e. block-diagonal concatenation of (hyper)matrices.

Proposition 4.11

(Ring product rule)
.

For a
d\times m_{1}
table
P\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})
and a
d\times m_{2}
table
Q\in A(\boldsymbol{\mu^{\prime}})
as above, we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{P}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\displaystyle\vee$}}}}\Delta_{Q}=\Delta_{P\oplus Q}\in\mathrm{HWV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}^{m_{1}+m_{2}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}

\displaystyle\nabla_{P}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\displaystyle\wedge$}}}}\nabla_{Q}=\nabla_{P\oplus Q}\in\mathrm{HWV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}^{m_{1}+m_{2}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d},

where
n\geq\max(\ell(\lambda),\ell(\mu))
.

Proof.

First note that the vectors
\Delta_{P\oplus Q}
and
\nabla_{P\oplus Q}
are indeed highest weight vectors of weight
\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\mu}
by Proposition
4.9
.
For
(\Lambda,{\textstyle\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{2}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\textstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}}})=(\Delta,\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\displaystyle\vee$}}}})
or
(\Lambda,{\textstyle\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{2}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\textstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}}})=(\nabla,\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\displaystyle\wedge$}}}})
, by definition we have:

\Lambda_{P\oplus Q}=(-1)^{P\oplus Q}\sum_{R\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\mu})}\mathrm{sgn}_{P\oplus Q}(R){\textstyle\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{2}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\textstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}}}_{i=1}^{m_{1}+m_{2}}e_{R(i)},

Note that

\mathrm{sgn}_{P\oplus Q}(R)=\mathrm{sgn}_{P}(R(1),\ldots,R(m_{1}))\cdot\mathrm{sgn}_{Q}(R(m_{1}+1),\ldots,R(m_{1}+m_{2})),

since the first
m_{1}
columns are independent of the rest
m_{2}
columns.
Thus, substituting
R_{1}:=(R(1),\ldots,R(m_{1}))
and
R_{2}:=(R(m_{1}+1),\ldots,R(m_{1}+m_{2}))
and taking out the brackets we obtain:

\Lambda_{P\oplus Q}=(-1)^{P\oplus Q}(-1)^{P}(-1)^{Q}\Lambda_{P}{\textstyle\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{2}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\textstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}}}\Lambda_{Q}.

Since,
(-1)^{P\oplus Q}=(-1)^{P}(-1)^{Q}
, the statement follows.
∎

Let us show how this implies the well-known
semigroup
property of Kronecker coefficients for general
d
, which was proved in
[
CHM09
]
(for
d=3
).

Corollary 4.12

(Semigroup property)
.

If
g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cdot g(\boldsymbol{\mu})>0
, then
g(\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\mu})\geq\max(g(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),g(\boldsymbol{\mu}))
.

Proof.

Let
a=g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\geq g(\boldsymbol{\mu})
. By Theorem
4.5
there is a basis

\mathrm{span}\{\Delta_{P_{1}},\ldots,\Delta_{P_{a}}\}=\mathrm{HWV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}^{m_{1}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}

for
P_{i}\in B(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})
. Let
Q\in B(\boldsymbol{\mu^{\prime}})
be such that
\Delta_{Q}\neq 0
. Then

\mathrm{span}\{\Delta_{P_{1}\oplus Q},\ldots,\Delta_{P_{a}\oplus Q}\}\subseteq\mathrm{HWV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{\mu}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}^{m_{1}+m_{2}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}

and the polynomials
\{\Delta_{P_{i}\oplus Q}\}
are linearly independent, since the polynomial ring is an integral domain.
∎

5.
Duality of highest weight vectors

In the section we prove duality for highest weight polynomials and forms.

For
S\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
denote by
\mathrm{stab}(S):=\{\pi\in S_{m}:\pi S=S\}
the stabilizer w.r.t. column permutation and by
||S||:=|\mathrm{stab}(S)|
its size.
For a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
we also define the sign
(-1)^{\lambda}:=(-1)^{w}
where
w=\underbrace{12\ldots\lambda_{1}}_{1\text{-st block}}\ldots\underbrace{12\ldots\lambda_{\ell}}_{\ell\text{-th block}}
, and
(-1)^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(-1)^{\lambda^{(i)}}
for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(d)})
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
be a
d
-tuple of partitions of
m
.

(i) For odd
d
we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{T}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\sum_{S\in A^{\mathrm{lex}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}\langle\nabla_{S},\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T}\rangle\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S},\quad T\in B(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{S}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\sum_{T\in B^{\mathrm{lex}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})}\langle\Delta_{T},\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S}\rangle\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T},\quad S\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),

(ii) For even
d
we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{S}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\sum_{S^{\prime}\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})}\langle\Delta_{S^{\prime}},\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S}\rangle\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S^{\prime}},\quad S\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{T}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\sum_{T^{\prime}\in B(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})}\langle\nabla_{T^{\prime}},\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T}\rangle\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T^{\prime}},\quad T\in B(\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

To prove this, we first introduce the coefficients
a(T,S)
and
b(S,T)
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
(S,T)\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\times A(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})
. After symmetrization (or alternation) of
P_{T}
(or
P_{S}
) (see Section
3
) we obtain
\Delta_{T}
(or
\nabla_{S}
) with some collinear terms in the sum. Let us collect them to obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{T}=\frac{1}{||T||}\sum_{S\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}(-1)^{T}\mathrm{sgn}_{T}(S)\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S}=

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in A^{\mathrm{lex}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}a(T,S)\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S},

\displaystyle\qquad\text{where }a(T,S):=

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{T}}{||T||\cdot||S||}\sum_{\pi\in S_{m}}\mathrm{sgn}_{T}(\pi S),

\displaystyle\nabla_{S}=\frac{1}{||S||}\sum_{T\in B(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})}(-1)^{S}\mathrm{sgn}_{S}(T)\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T}=

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in B^{\mathrm{lex}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})}b(S,T)\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T},

\displaystyle\text{where }b(S,T):=

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{S}}{||S||}\sum_{\pi\in S_{m}}\mathrm{sgn}(\pi)\cdot\mathrm{sgn}_{S}(\pi T).

Then
\langle\Delta_{T},\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S}\rangle=a(T,S)\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\langle\nabla_{S},\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T}\rangle=b(S,T)\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 5.1
.

For
\nabla_{S}
it is straightforward, since columns of
T
do not repeat. For
\Delta_{T}
, each term in the sum of
a(T,S)
repeats
||S||=|\mathrm{Stab}(S)|
times, which then cancels out.

It is clear that the sums in the definition of
a(T,S)
or
b(S,T)
may contain vanishing terms. We are going to describe these coefficients with nonzero terms.

Definition 5.3
.

For
d\times m
tables
X,Y,
define the following set of permutations

G(X,Y):=\{w\in S_{m}:\mathrm{sgn}_{X}(wY)\neq 0\}.

Using this set we can rewrite the coefficients
a(T,S)
and
b(S,T)
as follows:

a(T,S)=\frac{1}{||T||\cdot||S||}\sum_{\pi\in G(T,S)}\mathrm{sgn}^{\prime}_{T}(\pi S),\quad b(S,T)=\frac{1}{||S||}\sum_{\pi\in G(S,T)}\mathrm{sgn}(\pi)\cdot\mathrm{sgn}^{\prime}_{S}(\pi T).

(17)

where we write:

\mathrm{sgn}^{\prime}_{X}(\pi Y):=(-1)^{X}\mathrm{sgn}_{X}(\pi Y)

to shorten the notation.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
be an integer sequence with
|\alpha|=m
. For
t\in A(\alpha)
, let
Y_{t}\subseteq S_{m}
be the stabilizer of the word
t
, known as the
Young subgroup
.

5.1.
Single row

We first show properties of
G(s,t)
for single row tables
s,t
. We write lowercase letters for single row tables.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\lambda\vdash m
be a partition of length
\ell=\ell(\lambda)
. For any pair of words
(s,t)\in A(\lambda)\times A(\lambda^{\prime})
the following properties hold:

(a)

G(s,t)\in Y_{s}\backslash S_{m}/Y_{t}
is a double coset. Moreover, for
h\in G(s,t)
and double coset decomposition
w=xhy\in Y_{s}hY_{t}
we have
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(wt)=\mathrm{sgn}(x)\cdot\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(ht)
.

(b)

\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(t)\neq 0\iff\mathrm{sgn}_{t}(s)\neq 0\iff\{(s_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=1,\ldots,m}=\{(i,j)\in D(\lambda)\}
is the Young diagram of
\lambda
, where
s=s_{1}\ldots s_{m}
and
t=t_{1}\ldots t_{m}
.

(c)

\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(t)\neq 0\iff\mathrm{sgn}_{hs}(ht)\neq 0
for any
h\in S_{m}
.

(d)

G(s,t)^{-1}=G(t,s)
.

(e)

The product
(-1)^{G(s,t)}:=\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(\pi t)\cdot(-1)^{\pi}\cdot\mathrm{sgn}_{t}(\pi^{-1}s)
does not depend on
\pi\in G(s,t)
, i.e. it is invariant on the double coset.

(f)

The product
f(s,t):=(-1)^{s}(-1)^{G(s,t)}(-1)^{t}

does not depend on a pair
(s,t)
, but only on
\lambda
and is equal to
(-1)^{\lambda}:=(-1)^{t_{0}}
for
t_{0}=\underbrace{12\ldots\lambda_{1}}_{1\text{-st block}}\ldots\underbrace{12\ldots\lambda_{\ell}}_{\ell\text{-th block}}
.

Proof.

We prove the statements one by one.

(a) Due to the weight of the words
s
and
t
we have
G(s,t)\neq\varnothing
. Let us fix
h\in G(s,t)
, i.e.
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(ht)\neq 0
.
For an arbitrary permutation
w\in G(s,t)
with
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(wt)\neq 0
, there is a unique permutation
x\in Y_{s}
which permutes elements only in blocks of
s
so that the words
x\cdot ht=w\cdot t
are equal. For
y:=(xh)^{-1}w
we have
y\cdot t=t
, and hence
y\in Y_{t}
. Therefore, the decomposition
w=xhy\in Y_{s}hY_{t}
holds.

Conversely, suppose that
w=xhy\in Y_{s}hY_{t}
. Then
y
leaves
t
unchanged,
h
makes
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(ht)\neq 0
. Since each transposition of
x
to the word
hy\cdot t
changes the sign by
-1
, we have
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(wt)=\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(xht)=(-1)^{x}\cdot\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(ht)\neq 0
and
w\in G(s,t)
.

(b) Let us present
s
and
t
as a
2\times m
table with the first row
s
and the second row
t
, which we denote by

(s,t):=\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}&\ldots&s_{m}\\
t_{1}&\ldots&t_{m}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(t)\neq 0
if and only if the set of columns of the table
(s,t)
is equal to the set of cell coordinates of the diagram
\lambda
.

(c) Swapping rows or columns does not violate this property.

(d) Let
w^{-1}\in G(s,t)^{-1}
, i.e.
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(w^{-1}t)\neq 0
. Then by (b) we have:

\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(w^{-1}t)\neq 0\iff\mathrm{sgn}_{w^{-1}t}(s)\neq 0\iff\mathrm{sgn}_{t}(ws)\neq 0,

i.e.
w\in G(t,s)
and all steps are reversible.

(e) By (a) we can write
G(s,t)=Y_{s}hY_{t}
for an arbitrary element
h\in G(s,t)
. Since Young subgroups are generated by transpositions, this double coset can also be generated with multiplying
h
by transpositions of
Y_{s}
from the left and by transpositions of
Y_{t}
from the right.
For any transposition
\alpha\in Y_{s}
, replacing
h\to\alpha h
we have:
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(\alpha ht)=-\mathrm{sgn}(ht)
(transposition within
s
-block),
\mathrm{sgn}_{t}((\alpha h)^{-1}s)=\mathrm{sgn}_{t}(h^{-1}s)
(transposition of equal letters) and
(-1)^{\alpha h}=-(-1)^{h}
, thus the product is unchanged.
Similarly, for a transposition
\beta\in Y_{t}
replacing
h\to h\beta
we have:
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(w\beta t)=\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(wt)
,
\mathrm{sgn}_{t}((h\beta)^{-1}s)=-\mathrm{sgn}_{t}(hs)
and
(-1)^{h\beta}=-(-1)^{h}
. Thus, the product is indeed invariant on the double coset.

(e) Let
s_{0}=1^{\lambda_{1}}\ldots\ell^{\lambda_{\ell}}
and
t_{0}=12\ldots\lambda_{1}\ldots 12\ldots\lambda_{\ell}
.
We will show consequently that
f(s,t)=f(s_{0},t_{0})
. We will do that in two steps: first, we replace
t\to ht
so that
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(ht)\neq 0
, i.e find a nonzero shift; then we sort the columns of
(s,ht)
to achieve
(s_{0},t_{0})
. Diagrammatically we have:

\begin{pmatrix}s\\
t\end{pmatrix}\xlongrightarrow{t\to ht}\begin{pmatrix}s\\
ht\end{pmatrix}\xlongrightarrow{\text{ sort columns }}\begin{pmatrix}s_{0}\\
t_{0}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1^{\lambda_{1}}&\ldots&\ell^{\lambda_{\ell}}\\
12\ldots\lambda_{1}&\ldots&12\ldots\lambda_{\ell}\end{pmatrix}

Let
h\in G(s,t)
be the permutation of minimal length satisfying
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(ht)\neq 0
. This permutation does not permute equal letters of
t
(otherwise the length can be reduced). Let us show that
f(s,t)=f(s,ht)
. By (c) we have:

\displaystyle f(s,t)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{s}\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(ht)\cdot(-1)^{h}\cdot\mathrm{sgn}_{t}(h^{-1}s)(-1)^{t},

\displaystyle f(s,ht)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{s}\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(ht)\cdot(-1)^{\mathrm{id}}\cdot\mathrm{sgn}_{ht}(s)(-1)^{ht}.

Note that
(-1)^{ht}=(-1)^{h}(-1)^{t}
, since each transposition of
h
swaps only distinct letters of
t
. It remains to show that
\mathrm{sgn}_{t}(h^{-1}s)=\mathrm{sgn}_{ht}(s)
. Indeed, the table
(t,h^{-1}s)
is obtained from the table
(ht,s)
by permuting its columns according to the permutation
h
, which does not permute equal letters of
t
. Hence, the relative order of
t
-block permutations in the bottom row does not change in both tables; this implies that
\mathrm{sgn}_{t}(h^{-1}s)=\mathrm{sgn}_{ht}(s)
and
f(s,t)=f(s,ht)
.

Now we may assume that
\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(t)\neq 0
(i.e. replace
ht\to t
), then the table
(s,t)
contains distinct columns forming the set
\{(i,j)\in D(\lambda)\}
. By (b) we can reach the table
(s_{0},t_{0})
from the table
(s,t)
by column swaps. Let us consequently apply adjacent transpositions of the form
(i,i+1)
to the columns of
(s,t)
to obtain
(s_{0},t_{0})
and observe that
f(s,t)
does not change. By (d), put
\pi=e
to rewrite

f(s,t)=(-1)^{s}\mathrm{sgn}_{s}(t)\cdot\mathrm{sgn}_{t}(s)(-1)^{t}.

Let
\alpha=(i,i+1)
be the transposition we apply. Let us keep track of the sign changes after applying
\alpha
. If
i
-th and
(i+1)
-th columns are:

•

\begin{bmatrix}xx\\
yz\end{bmatrix}
then the changes are

5.3.
Interpretation of coefficients

Let us now give a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients
a(T,S)
(and
b(S,T)
), for
T\in A^{\mathrm{lex}}(m)
and
S\in A^{\mathrm{lex}}(m)
.

Let
\sigma
be a bijective map
\sigma:T\to S
(where
T,S
are viewed as
multisets
of columns), which is read as follows.
Consider
T\subseteq[k]^{d}
as a choice of some cells of a cube, which are going to be filled with columns from
S
. Each cell (column)
p
of
T
is filled by certain entry (column)
q
of
S
, namely if
\sigma(p)=q
. This way, each cell
p\in[k]^{d}
has
T_{p}
entries and entry
q\in S
is present
S_{q}
times. The entries within same cell are ordered.

We call bijection
\sigma:T\to S
a
(T,S)
-filling
if additionally
\mathrm{sgn}_{T}(\sigma(T))\neq 0
, i.e. for each direction
i\in[d]
and for each
j
-slice
E^{(i)}_{j}
in
i
-th direction: the entries of
\sigma
in slice
E^{(i)}_{j}
must have a permutation if all coordinates except the
i
-th one are ignored. More formally, if
T\cap E=\{q^{1}<\ldots<q^{\ell}\}
and
\sigma(q^{k})=p^{k}\in[n]^{d}
, then we must have
(p^{1}_{i},\ldots,p^{\ell}_{i})\in S_{\ell}
.

The
sign

\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)
of
(T,S)
-filling
\sigma
is then the product of signs
\mathrm{sgn}(p^{1}_{i}\ldots p^{\ell}_{i})
over all slices
E^{(i)}_{j}
for
i=1,\ldots,d
and
j=1,\ldots,k
.
This describes the following interpretation.

Proposition 5.6
.

For
(T,S)\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})\times A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
we have

a(T,S)=(-1)^{T}\sum_{\sigma:T\to S}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)

where
\sigma
runs over all
(T,S)
-fillings.

Proof.

We have

a(T,S)=\frac{(-1)^{T}}{||S||\cdot||T||}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{m}}\mathrm{sgn}_{T}(\sigma S)=(-1)^{T}\sum_{\sigma:T\to S\text{ - injective}}\mathrm{sgn}_{T}(\sigma(T)),

where we match
\mathrm{sgn}(p^{1}_{i}\ldots p^{\ell}_{i})
with the sign of the corresponding block in
i
-th row of
T
(each slice
E
corresponds to certain row-block). The factor
||T||
ensures that the order within the cells does not matter.
∎

7.1.
Raising and lowering operators

Let
\mathfrak{g}:=\mathfrak{gl}(n)^{\times d}
be the Lie algebra of
G:=\mathrm{GL}(n)^{\times d}
acting on
V=(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
diagonally. Then the corresponding action of Lie algebra for
v=v_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{m}\in\bigotimes^{m}V
and
A\in\mathfrak{g}
is

A\cdot v=Av_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes v_{m}+\ldots+v_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes Av_{m},

where
Av_{i}
is an ordinary matrix multiplication. The action on
\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}V
and
\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}V
is analogous.
Let
E_{i,j}
be the
n\times n
matrix with a single entry
1
at
(i,j)
and zero elsewhere. For
i<j
the operator
E^{(1)}_{i,j}:v\mapsto(E_{i,j},0,0,\ldots)v
is called a
raising operator
in direction
1
, and for
i>j
it is called
lowering operator
. Raising (or lowering) operators are consistent with the weight lattice: if
v\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
of weight
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda,\ldots)
, then the vector
E^{(1)}_{i,j}(v)
is of weight

\alpha^{(1)}_{ij}\boldsymbol{\lambda}:=(\lambda+e_{i}-e_{j},\ldots)
,
i.e. increased by a positive root.
For other directions, the operators
E^{(\ell)}_{ij},\alpha^{(\ell)}_{ij}
are defined similarly.
We also denote
(E^{(\ell)}_{ij})^{*}=E_{ji}^{(\ell)},(\alpha^{(\ell)}_{ij})^{*}=\alpha_{ji}^{(\ell)}
the pair corresponding to negative roots.

This allows us to recover criteria of being highest weight vector. For vector
v
of weight
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
we have

\displaystyle v\in\mathrm{HWV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}~\bigotimes^{m}V\iff\forall\ell\in[d],i<j:E^{(\ell)}_{ij}(v)=0,

(23)

i.e. weight vector
v\in V
vanishing by all raising operators is highest weight vector.

The following easy fact allows us to map action of raising matrix units to
\partial
operations.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
be a
d
-tuple of partitions of
m
. For
S\in A(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
we have

\partial^{(\ell)}_{ij}(S)=\{S^{\prime}\in A(\alpha^{(\ell)}_{ij}\boldsymbol{\lambda}):\langle E^{(\ell)}_{ij}\cdot e_{S(1)}\otimes\ldots\otimes e_{S(m)},e_{S^{\prime}(1)}\otimes\ldots\otimes e_{S^{\prime}(m)}\rangle=1\}.

In particular,
E^{(\ell)}_{ij}\left(\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{S}\right)=\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}\partial^{(\ell)}_{ij}(S)
and
E^{(\ell)}_{ij}\left(\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S}\right)=\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}\partial^{(\ell)}_{ij}(S)
.

Lemma 7.4

(HWV relations)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
be a
d
-tuple of partitions of
m
and

H=\sum_{X\in C^{\mathrm{lex}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}h_{X}\ \cdot{\textstyle\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{2}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\textstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}}}e_{X}\in\mathrm{WV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\textstyle\mathop{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{2}{$\displaystyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\textstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.2}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Diamond$}}}}}}V,

Proof.

be operators for fixed

We are enough to rewrite (

\displaystyle E(H)=\sum_{T\in B^{\mathrm{lex}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})}h_{T}\cdot E\left(\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T}\right)=\sum_{T\in B^{\mathrm{lex}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}})}h_{T}\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\partial(T)}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T^{\prime}}.

Note, that

∎

8.
Power expansions of the Cayley form for odd

Let

be odd,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
be
d

. In this section we consider the dual spaces

\mathrm{HWV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\prime}}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{k})^{\otimes d}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{HWV}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}^{m}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}

where we replaced

for wedge space to distinguish the spaces. This imposes restriction
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\subseteq(n\times k)^{\times d}
.

8.1.
\mathrm{SL}
-invariants

If we specialize to the rectangular weights
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\to(n\times k)^{d}
, the highest weight polynomials (w.r.t.
\mathrm{GL}(n)^{\times d}
) and forms (w.r.t
\mathrm{GL}(k)^{\times d}
) become invariants w.r.t.
\mathrm{SL}(n)^{\times d}
and
\mathrm{SL}(k)^{\times d}
, respectively. With these weights we will use the following notation for hypermatrices:

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{d}(n,k)

\displaystyle:=A^{\mathrm{lex}}((n\times k)^{d})=\mathsf{T}_{\mathbb{N}}((n\times k)^{d}),

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}_{d}(k,n)

\displaystyle:=B^{\mathrm{lex}}((k\times n)^{d})=\mathsf{T}_{01}((k\times n)^{d}),

i.e. the first argument corresponds to the length of the cube and the second corresponds to the sum of entries in each slice.
These set equalities are identified via
d
-line notation. Note that the
(0,1)
-hypermatrix
T\in\mathsf{B}_{d}(n,k)
is determined by its support
\mathrm{supp}(T)\subseteq[k]^{d}
.

Denote the special class of Kronecker coefficients at rectangular shapes:

g_{d}(n,k):=g(\underbrace{n\times k,\ldots,n\times k}_{d\text{ times}})

and recall that
g_{d}(n,k)=\dim\mathrm{HWV}_{(n\times k)^{d}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}^{nk}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}=\dim\mathrm{HWV}_{(k\times n)^{d}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}^{nk}(\mathbb{C}^{k})^{\otimes d}
.
The ring of
\mathrm{SL}
-invariant polynomials over
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
has the grade decomposition

\mathbb{C}[(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}]^{\mathrm{SL}(n)^{d}}\cong\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}\mathrm{HWV}_{(n\times k)^{d}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}^{nk}(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d},

but we fix
k
(multiple of a degree) and iterate over
n
instead. The resulting sequence of rectangular Kronecker coefficients
\{g_{d}(n,k)\}_{n\geq 0}
counts grades’ dimension of the following subspace of
\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}(\mathbb{C}^{k})^{\otimes d}
:

\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{HWV}_{(k\times n)^{d}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}^{nk}(\mathbb{C}^{k})^{\otimes d}.

Remark 8.1
.

We studied the sequence
g_{d}(n,k)
in
[
AY23
]
. In particular, we showed the symmetry
g_{d}(n,k)=g_{d}(k^{d-1}-n,k)
for
n\in[0,k^{d-1}]
(note that
g_{d}(n,k)=0
for
n>k^{d-1}
) and conjectured that the sequence is unimodal for even
k
. In
[
AY25
]
we refined this unimodality conjecture for arbitrary weights
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and proved refined version of this conjecture for
k=2
.

It is known that
g_{d}(1,k)=g_{d}(k^{d-1},k)=1
, which suggests the existence of unique invariants in the corresponding graded pieces of polynomials and forms. These invariants are particularly important. Let us discuss them.

The first identity
g_{d}(1,k)=1
for polynomials is trivial: there is a unique homogenious polynomial of degree
k
in
\mathbb{C}[(\mathbb{C}^{1})^{\otimes d}]
. While for forms, there is a special highest weight vector
\omega\in\mathrm{HWV}_{(k\times 1)^{d}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}(\mathbb{C}^{k})^{\otimes d}
, which we call the
Cayley form
given by:

\omega=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\pi_{1},\ldots\pi_{d}\in S_{k}}\mathrm{sgn}(\pi_{1}\cdots\pi_{d})\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}_{i=1}^{k}e_{\pi_{1}(i),\ldots,\pi_{d}(i)}=\nabla_{\begin{pmatrix}1^{k}\\
\vdots\\
1^{k}\end{pmatrix}}\in\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}^{k}(\mathbb{C}^{k})^{\otimes d},

which is (up to a scale) unique highest weight vector of weight
(k\times 1)^{d}
.
Note that the product in wedge space corresponds to the direct sum operation
\bigoplus
on tables by Proposition
4.11
, and therefore, for each
n\leq k^{d-1}
we have

\displaystyle\omega^{n}=\nabla_{\begin{pmatrix}1^{k}\ldots n^{k}\\
\vdots\\
1^{k}\ldots n^{k}\end{pmatrix}}=:\nabla_{I_{n,k}}\in\mathrm{HWV}_{(n\times k)^{d}}\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}^{nk}(\mathbb{C}^{k})^{\otimes d}

where we denote the corresponding
d\times nk
table by
I_{n,k}\in\mathsf{A}_{d}(n,k)
.

For the second identity
g_{d}(k^{d-1},k)=1
, there is a unique invariant polynomial
\Delta_{F_{d,k}}
, where
F_{d,k}\in\mathsf{B}_{d}(k,k^{d-1})
is the
d\times k^{d}
table with all distinct columns. In particular,
\mathrm{supp}(F^{(d)}_{k})=[k]^{d}
. For example, for
d=3
we have the following table:

F_{3,k}=\begin{pmatrix}~&1^{k^{2}}&~&\ldots&~&k^{k^{2}}&~\\
1^{k}&\ldots&k^{k}&\ldots&1^{k}&\ldots&k^{k}\\
12\ldots k&\ldots&12\ldots k&\ldots&12\ldots k&\ldots&12\ldots k\end{pmatrix}.

Remark 8.2
.

This invariant was introduced by Bürgisser and Ikenmeyer in
[

]
.
This polynomial is a

invariant, in the sense that it is the smallest degree invariant in the ring

Note that the unique invariant in

\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{[k]^{d}}
.

8.2.
Expansion of the Cayley form

Using the main duality from Section
5
we give a conceptual explanation to power expansions of the Cayley form
\omega
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
d\geq 3
be odd. For each
n\leq k^{d-1}
we have:

\omega^{n}=(-1)^{n\times k}\sum_{T\in\mathsf{B}_{d}(n,k)}\Delta_{T}(\mathsf{I}_{n})\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T}

where sum runs over all
d\times nk
tables of weight
(n\times k)^{d}
. In particular, for
n=k^{d-1}
we have:

\omega^{k^{d-1}}=(-1)^{k^{d-1}\times k}\Delta_{F_{d,k}}(\mathsf{I}_{k^{d-1}})\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{[k]^{d}}.

Proof.

Since
\omega^{n}=\nabla_{I_{n,k}}
as noted above, we have

\nabla_{I_{n,k}}=\sum_{T\in\mathsf{B}_{d}(k,n)}b(T,I_{n,k})\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{T}.

By Theorem
5.1
we have
b(I_{n,k},T)=(-1)^{n\times k}a(T,I_{n,k})
.
Since evaluation of the polynomial
\Delta_{T}
at the unit tensor
\mathsf{I}_{n}
is equal to the coefficient
a(T,I_{n,k})
, the result follows.
∎

Remark 8.3
.

We first proved this result about power expansions of
\omega
in
[
AY23
]
by a different rather long combinatorial argument analyzing signs of Latin hypercubes. Now we have a conceptual proof of this result. Note also that this result is important for showing positivity of Kronecker coefficients.

8.3.
Latin hypercubes

Evaluations of invariants at unit tensors has a nice combinatorial interpretation. For
T\in\mathsf{B}_{d}(n,k)
, let
L
be a hypermatrix with
\mathrm{supp}(L)=T\subseteq[k]^{d}
.

We call
L
a
partial Latin hypercube
, if non-zero entries of any fixed slice form a permutation, conventionally collected in lexicographical order of
[k]^{d}
. When
\mathrm{supp}(T)=[k]^{d}
, we call
L
a
Latin hypercube
. Let
\mathsf{L}_{d}(T)
and
\mathsf{L}_{d}(k)
be the sets of partial Latin hypercubes with support
T
and
[k]^{d}
, respectively.

The
sign
or
signature

\mathrm{sgn}(L)
of a partial Latin hypercube
L
with support
T
is defined to be the product over all slices (in all directions) of the slice permutation signs. We then define the sums

\mathrm{AT}_{d}(T):=\sum_{L\in\mathsf{L}_{d}(T)}\mathrm{sgn}(L),\qquad\mathrm{AT}_{d}(k):=\sum_{\mathsf{L}_{d}(k)}\mathrm{sgn}(L),

which we call
d
-dimensional Alon–Tarsi numbers.
The following proposition is straightforward from Proposition
5.6
.

Proposition 8.2
.

We have

\Delta_{T}(\mathsf{I}_{n})=a(T,I_{n,k})=(-1)^{T}\mathrm{AT}_{d}(T)

for any
d
and
T\in\mathsf{B}_{d}(k,n)
. Moreover,
\mathrm{AT}_{d}(T)=0
for odd
k
.

Remark 8.4
.

We refer to
[
AY23
]
for more on Latin hypercubes.

Remark 8.5
.

While it is evident that
\Delta_{F_{d,k}}\neq 0
and
\Delta_{F_{d,k}}(\mathsf{I}_{n})=\pm\mathrm{AT}_{d}(k)
, there are other nonzero coefficients in the fundamental invariant
\Delta_{F_{d,k}}
. Similarly,
\nabla_{I_{k^{d-1},k}}=\mathrm{AT}_{d}(k)\cdot\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigwedge$}}}}e_{[k]^{d}}
and there are other indices
S\in\mathsf{A}_{d}(k^{d-1},k)
for which
\nabla_{S}\neq 0
. For such
S
, in fact,
\nabla_{S}\neq 0
if and only if
\langle\Delta_{F_{d,k}},\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.1}{$\displaystyle\vee$}}}}e_{S}\rangle\neq 0
.

Remark 8.6

(On the Alon–Tarsi conjecture and Kronecker positivity)
.

Recall that the Alon–Tarsi conjecture on Latin squares
[
AT92
]
(cf.
[
HR93
]
) states that
\mathrm{AT}_{2}(k)\neq 0
for even
k
. For
d=3
, a similar problem about Latin cubes was posed by Burgisser–Ikenmeyer
[
BI17
]
asking if
\mathrm{AT}_{3}(k)\neq 0
for even
k
(which holds for
k=2,4
). As we studied in
[
AY23
]
, this problem can be generalized for any
d
given that
\mathrm{AT}_{d}(2)\neq 0
and that the statement
\mathrm{AT}_{d}(k)\neq 0
for even
k
and odd
d
implies positivity of Kronecker coefficients; namely, using the power expansions of the Cayley form
\omega
we showed that
\mathrm{AT}_{2}(k)\neq 0
implies that
g_{d}(n,k)>0
for all
n\leq k
, while
\mathrm{AT}_{3}(k)\neq 0
implies that
g_{d}(n,k)>0
for all
n\leq k^{d-1}
; furthermore, unconditionally for even
k
and odd

we have
g_{d}(n,k)>0
for
n\leq\max\{2^{d-1},\sqrt{n}/2-1\}

[
AY23
,
AY25
]
, which uses the facts that
\mathrm{AT}_{2}(p\pm 1)\neq 0
for odd primes
p

[

,
Gly10
]
and

.

9.1.
Expansion of Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant

We are going to show the next application of the duality on invariant polynomials. We will use the tables
I_{n,k}
and
F_{d,k}
defined in the previous section.

Let us recall Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant
\delta=\delta_{d,k}
defined as follows:

\delta:=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma\in(S_{k})^{d}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}_{i=1}^{k}e_{\sigma(i)}=\Delta_{I_{1,k}}\in\mathbb{C}[(\mathbb{C}^{k})^{\otimes d}]_{k},

where
\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)=\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{1})\cdots\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{d})
for
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d})
. Note that this formula becomes identical zero for odd
d
. More recognized form would be:

\delta^{*}(X)=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}\in S_{k}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{d})\prod_{i=1}^{k}X_{\sigma_{1}(i)\ldots\sigma_{d}(i)}

Remark 9.1
.

The invariant
\omega
(discussed in previous section) can be regarded as a
k
-form version of Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant
\delta
: for tensor
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\otimes X_{i}\in(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes d}
:

\delta^{*}(X)=\omega^{*}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})

for even
d
. This invariant was introduced by Cayley
[
Cay43
,
Cay45
]
. Notably, it is the smallest degree
\mathrm{SL}(k)^{\times d}
-invariant polynomial over
(\mathbb{C}^{k})^{\otimes d}
for even
d
and is unique up to a scalar. For odd
d
, the above formula gives identical zero; moreover, the smallest degree of an invariant is always at least
n\lceil n^{1/(d-1)}\rceil

[
AY23
]
which corresponds to the degree of the fundamental invariant
\Delta_{F_{d,k}}
for
n=k^{d-1}
.

Remark 9.2
.

The hyperdeterminant
\delta
can also be characterized as the unique function (up to a scalar) satisfying multi-linearity and skew-symmetry in argument’s slices in any fixed direction, see
[
AY23c
]
.

In particular, the powers of
\delta
can be written as

\delta^{n}=\Delta_{I_{n,k}},

since the product in the polynomial ring is translated to
\oplus
operation on tables by Proposition
4.11
. It is known that for
d=2
, the coefficient of the power of the ordinary determinant
\det^{k}
at
e_{11}e_{12}\ldots e_{kk}
is equal to the Alon–Tarsi number
(-1)^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\mathrm{AT}_{2}(k)
. We prove the following generalization of this fact, which becomes an analogue Theorem
8.1
about the power expansions of
\omega
for even
d
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
be even. For any
n\geq 1
we have:

\delta^{n}=\sum_{S\in\mathsf{A}_{d}(k,n)}\Delta_{S}(\mathsf{I}_{n})\cdot\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S},

In particular, the coefficient of
\delta^{k^{d-1}}
at
\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{[k]^{d}}
is equal to
\pm\mathrm{AT}_{d}(k)
.

Proof.

Let us rewrite the power of the hyperdeterminant in the form of
\Delta
-polynomial:
\delta^{n}=\Delta_{I_{n,k}}
for
I_{n,k}\in\mathsf{A}_{d}(n,k)
. For each
S\in\mathsf{A}_{d}(k,n)
, the coefficient at
\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{S}
in
\Delta_{I_{n,k}}
is the number
a(I_{n,k},S)
, which by the symmetry of coefficients from Lemma
5.5
(d) satisfies

a(I_{n,k},S)=(-1)^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}a(S,I_{n,k})=(-1)^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\Delta_{S}(\mathsf{I}_{n}),

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(n\times k)^{d}
. Note, that
(-1)^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=((-1)^{n\times k})^{d}=1
, since
d
is even.
In particular, for
n=k^{d-1}
and
F=F_{d,k}
we have

a(I_{k^{d-1},k},F)=a(F,I_{k^{d-1},k})=\Delta_{F}(\mathsf{I}_{n})=(-1)^{F}\mathrm{AT}_{d}(k),

and the proof follows.
∎

Power expansions
\delta^{n}
contain other curious coefficients.
It turns out that the appropriate power of
\delta^{n}
contains both the number of
c
-dimensional Latin hypercubes and the signed sum of Latin hypercubes
\mathrm{AT}_{c}(k)
for any even
c\leq d/2
.

Proposition 9.2
.

Let
d
and
c\in[2,d/2]
be both even. Consider the following
d\times k^{c}
tables:

T_{odd}=\begin{pmatrix}~&~&~\\
~&F_{c,k}&~\\
~&~&~\\
\hline\cr 12\cdots k&\cdots&12\cdots k\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
12\cdots k&\cdots&12\cdots k\end{pmatrix},\qquad T_{even}=\begin{pmatrix}~&~&~\\
~&F_{c,k}&~\\
~&~&~\\
\hline\cr~&~&~\\
~&F_{c,k}&~\\
~&~&~\\
\hline\cr 12\cdots k&\cdots&12\cdots k\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
12\cdots k&\cdots&12\cdots k\end{pmatrix}.

Then the coefficients of
\delta^{k^{c-1}}
at
\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{T_{even}}
is
|\mathsf{L}_{c}(k)|
and at
\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.9}{$\displaystyle\bigvee$}}}}e_{T_{odd}}
is
\mathrm{AT}_{c}(k)
.
In fact, the same holds for
T_{odd}
if
c>d/2
.

Proof.

Let
I=I_{k^{c-1},k}
. The desired coefficients are then
a(I,T_{even})
and
a(I,T_{odd})
. Let us look into their expansions by definition (
17
). Note that

\mathrm{sgn}_{T_{odd}}(\pi I)=\mathrm{sgn}_{F_{c,k}}(\pi I)\text{ and }\mathrm{sgn}_{T_{even}}(\pi I)=|\mathrm{sgn}_{F_{c,k}}(\pi I)|

for any
\pi\in S_{k^{c}}
, since each row is repeated
parity
(i.e. odd or even) number of times in the table
T_{parity}
. Thus,

a(I,T_{odd})=a(T_{odd},I)=a(F_{c,k},I^{\prime})=\pm\mathrm{AT}_{d}(k),

where
I^{\prime}
is formed by the first
c
rows of
I
. Similarly, for
a(T_{even},I)
each non-vanishing term in the sum has a positive sign, giving
|\mathsf{L}_{c}(k)|
.
∎
