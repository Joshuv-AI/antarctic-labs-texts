Duplicial functors, descent categories and generalized Hopf modules
Ivan Bartulović, John Boiquaye, Ulrich Krähmer

Abstract
. Böhm and Ştefan have expressed cyclic homology as an invariant that assigns homology groups HC i χ ​ ( N , M ) \mathrm{HC}^{\chi}_{i}(\mathrm{N},\mathrm{M}) to right and left coalgebras N \mathrm{N} respectively M \mathrm{M} over a distributive law χ \chi between two comonads. For the key example associated to a bialgebra H H , right χ \chi -coalgebras have a description in terms of modules and comodules over H H . The present article formulates conditions under which such a description is simultaneously possible for the left χ \chi -coalgebras. In the above example, this is the case when the bialgebra H H is a Hopf algebra with bijective antipode. We also discuss how the generalized Hopf module theorem by Mesablishvili and Wisbauer features both in theory and examples.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Simplicial and
cyclic objects from comonads

The classical homology theories
studied in algebra and geometry such
as group, Lie algebra, Hochschild,
or singular homology can
be expressed as the homology of
a suitable comonad
\mathbb{T}
on some
category
\mathcal{B}
,
see
[
21
, Section 8.7]
:
given any functors

\mathrm{M}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{B}
and
\mathrm{N}\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{Z}
, one builds a
simplicial functor

\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathrm{N},\mathrm{M})_{n}:=\mathrm{N}\mathrm{T}^{n+1}\mathrm{M}
, where
\mathrm{T}
is the
endofunctor underlying
\mathbb{T}
.
Typically,

\mathcal{Y}
is the terminal category and

\mathcal{Z}
is the category of
abelian groups, so

\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathrm{N},\mathrm{M})
is just a
simplicial abelian group.
The homology theory is defined in
terms of the associated chain
complex
[
21
, Definition 8.3.6]
.

Cyclic homology theories
[
6
]

follow a more general pattern:
as shown by Böhm and Ştefan
[
3
]
,
they define invariants of
a comonad distributive law
\chi\colon\mathbb{T}\mathbb{S}\Rightarrow\mathbb{S}\mathbb{T}
, or,
equivalently, of a comonad
with a given factorization as
a composition
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{S}
of two
comonads (see
Section
2.4
).
The coefficients of the theory
are left respectively
right
\chi
-coalgebras
\lambda\colon\mathrm{N}\mathrm{S}\Rightarrow\mathrm{N}\mathrm{T}

and
\rho\colon\mathrm{T}\mathrm{M}\Rightarrow\mathrm{S}\mathrm{M}

(see
Definition
2.15

for this notion). In the
presence of these extra
structures, the simplicial functor

\mathrm{C}_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathrm{N},\mathrm{M})
becomes a
duplicial functor
(see
[
7
]
for this
generalization of
Connes’ cyclic objects due to Dwyer
and Kan), and
the associated chain complex
becomes simultaneously a cochain complex.
Thus it is the presence of the
second comonad
\mathbb{S}
that leads to the coboundary map which is used to define cyclic homology.

1.2.
Background and
aim

Assume

\mathrm{F}\colon\mathcal{A}\rightleftarrows\mathcal{B}\colon\mathrm{U}
is an adjunction for

\mathbb{T}
, meaning that

\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{F}\mathrm{U}

(see
[
21
, Main
Application 8.6.2.]
).
If
\mathbb{S}
is a lift of a comonad

\mathbb{C}
on
\mathcal{A}
through this
adjunction, there is a
canonical distributive law

\chi\colon\mathbb{T}\mathbb{S}\Rightarrow\mathbb{S}\mathbb{T}
(see
Section
2.6
);
in the case of an
Eilenberg–Moore adjunction,
this yields a
bijective correspondence
between lifts of comonads and
distributive laws
[
23
, Theorem
2.4]
.
In this setup,
right
\chi
-coalgebra
structures
\rho
on a functor

\mathrm{M}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{B}
correspond
bijectively to

\mathbb{C}
-coalgebra structures on

\mathrm{U}\mathrm{M}

[
9
, Proposition 3.5]
,
which are much easier to describe in
the most important examples of the
theory (see
Section
4

for the explicit
example obtained from
a bialgebra
H
).
The aim of the present article is to
provide an analogous description of
left
\chi
-coalgebra structures on
a functor
\mathrm{N}\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{Z}
.

1.3.
Main result

The classification of left

\chi
-coalgebras
is obtained by expanding

[
9
, Remark 3.6]
, where it
is suggested that in the presence of
an adjunction
\mathrm{V}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightleftarrows\mathcal{B}\colon\mathrm{G}
for

\mathbb{S}
and a comonad
\mathbb{Q}
which
extends
\mathbb{T}
through
\mathrm{V}\dashv\mathrm{G}
, left
\chi
-coalgebra
structures on
\mathrm{N}
should
correspond
bijectively to
\mathbb{Q}
-opcoalgebra
structures on
\mathrm{N}\mathrm{V}
. We depict the resulting setup in the
following diagram (see Section
2.6
for the
more details):

1.4.
Hopf

The probably most natural instance
of Theorem
1.1
is
constructed as follows:
suppose we are initially given a
mixed distributive law
\theta

between a monad
\mathbb{B}
and a comonad
\mathbb{C}
on a category
\mathcal{A}
.
First, we define

\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}
to be the
category of
\mathbb{B}
-algebras. As
mentioned above,

[

determines a
lift
\mathbb{S}
of
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathcal{B}
.
Secondly, we define

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{S}}

\mathbb{S}

[

If

\textstyle{{\mathcal{A}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{K}}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{F}}

\scriptstyle{\mathrm{V}}

correspond to comonad morphisms

\kappa\colon\mathbb{T}\Rightarrow\mathbb{S}

(see
[
16
, Section 2.2]
). The
latter appears in different contexts
and particular cases under the
names

map

[

]
,

map

[

]
,

[

]
,

[

]
,

, and

The

can be generalized

Notation and
conventions

We denote categories by
calligraphic capitals, functors by
Roman capitals, and natural
transformations by small Greek letters.
As in this introduction, blackboard
bold letters are used for
(co)monads, with the same letter in
Roman font denoting the underlying
endofunctors.
The class of objects of a category
\mathcal{C}
is denoted by
\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
.
Throughout the paper, the composition of functors and the horizontal composition of natural transformations is denoted by juxtaposition, while we use
\circ
for vertical composition of natural transformations.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we review
some prerequisites and fix
notation, which closely
follows the one used
in
[
9
]
:
we recall in particular
the notions of
(co)monads, (co)algebras over
these, of distributive laws
between these and of
(co)algebras over distributive
laws.
Throughout,
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}
are categories.

2.1.
(Co)monads

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category.
Recall that a monad on
\mathcal{C}

is a monoid in the category of
endofunctors on
\mathcal{C}
:

Definition 2.1
.

A
monad

\mathbb{B}
on
\mathcal{C}
is a
triple
(\mathrm{B},\mu^{\mathbb{B}},\eta^{\mathbb{B}})
, where
\mathrm{B}\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
is
an endofunctor and

\mu^{\mathbb{B}}\colon\mathrm{B}\mathrm{B}\Rightarrow\mathrm{B}
and
\eta^{\mathbb{B}}\colon\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}\Rightarrow\mathrm{B}

are natural transformations
(the
multiplication

and the
unit
of
\mathbb{B}
) satisfying the associativity and unitality conditions

\mu^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\mathrm{B}\mu^{\mathbb{B}}=\mu^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\mu^{\mathbb{B}}\mathrm{B}

and
\mu^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\mathrm{B}\eta^{\mathbb{B}}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{B}}=\mu^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\eta^{\mathbb{B}}\mathrm{B}
.

Here and elsewhere, we follow
the convention to write
\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{B}}
for the
identity morphism of an object
in a category (here the
identity natural
transformation of the
endofunctor
\mathrm{B}
), but when
forming a horizontal composition
(“whiskering” a natural
transformation with a functor)
or take a monoidal product
with an identity morphism,
we just write
\mathrm{B}
. The
symbol
\circ
is reserved
for the vertical composition
of natural transformations.

A comonad on
\mathcal{C}
is a
monad on the opposite
category
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}
:

Definition 2.2
.

A
comonad

\mathbb{C}
is an
endofunctor
\mathrm{C}
together
with natural transformations

\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}\colon\mathrm{C}\Rightarrow\mathrm{C}\mathrm{C}
and
\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}}\colon\mathrm{C}\Rightarrow\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{C}}

that are called
comultiplication

and
counit
and satisfy
the coassociativity and
counitality conditions
\mathrm{C}\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}\circ\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}=\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{C}\circ\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}
and

\mathrm{C}\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}}\circ\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{C}}=\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{C}\circ\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}
.

As monoids in a monoidal category,
monads naturally form a category.
However, for what we will discuss,
the category on which a monad acts
needs to be viewed as part of
the monad that may change under a
morphism of monads, and this leads
to two choices:

Definition 2.3
.

A
lax morphism

from a monad
\mathbb{A}
on
\mathcal{C}
to
a monad
\mathbb{B}
on
\mathcal{D}
is a
pair
(\mathrm{G},\sigma)
consisting of a functor
\mathrm{G}\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
and a natural transformation
\sigma\colon\mathrm{A}\mathrm{G}\Rightarrow\mathrm{G}\mathrm{B}
such
that
\sigma\circ\mu^{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{G}\mu^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\sigma\mathrm{B}\circ\mathrm{A}\sigma
and
\sigma\circ\eta^{\mathbb{A}}\mathrm{G}=\mathrm{G}\eta^{\mathbb{B}}
;
a
colax morphism

from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
is a pair
(\mathrm{F},\tau)
consisting of a functor
\mathrm{F}\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
and a natural transformation
\tau\colon\mathrm{F}\mathrm{A}\Rightarrow\mathrm{B}\mathrm{F}
such that
\tau\circ\mathrm{F}\mu^{\mathbb{A}}=\mu^{\mathbb{B}}\mathrm{F}\circ\mathrm{B}\tau\circ\tau\mathrm{A}
and
\tau\circ\mathrm{F}\eta^{\mathbb{A}}=\eta^{\mathbb{B}}\mathrm{F}
.

These two choices are about
switching from
\mathsf{Cat}
to

\mathsf{Cat}^{\mathrm{op}}
(reverse the
direction of functors between
categories). This is entirely
independent from switching
from a specific category

\mathcal{C}\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathsf{Cat})
to

\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathsf{Cat})
which turns
monads into comonads. So by viewing
comonads as monads on te opposite
categories, there are analogously
two types of morphisms between
comonads; we spell this out to make
clear which type we call lax and
which one colax, and which symbols
we will typically use:

Definition 2.4
.

A
lax morphism
of from
a comonad
\mathbb{C}
on
\mathcal{C}

to a comonad
\mathbb{D}
on
\mathcal{D}

is a pair
(\mathrm{G},\sigma)

consisting of a functor
\mathrm{G}\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
and a natural
transformation
\sigma\colon\mathrm{C}\mathrm{G}\Rightarrow\mathrm{G}\mathrm{D}
such that

\mathrm{G}\Delta^{\mathbb{D}}\circ\sigma=\sigma\mathrm{D}\circ\mathrm{C}\sigma\circ\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{G}
and

\mathrm{G}\varepsilon^{\mathbb{D}}\circ\sigma=\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{G}
; a
colax morphism

from
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{D}
is a pair
(\mathrm{F},\sigma)
, where
\mathrm{F}\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is a functor and
\tau\colon\mathrm{F}\mathrm{C}\Rightarrow\mathrm{D}\mathrm{F}
is a natural
transformation such that

\Delta^{\mathbb{D}}\mathrm{F}\circ\tau=\mathrm{D}\tau\circ\tau\mathrm{C}\circ\mathrm{F}\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}
and

\varepsilon^{\mathbb{D}}\mathrm{F}\circ\tau=\mathrm{F}\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}}
.

Remark 2.5
.

It follows from
these definitions that if
\mathrm{G}\colon\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
is a functor and if
\sigma\colon\mathrm{C}\mathrm{G}\Rightarrow\mathrm{G}\mathrm{D}
is an invertible natural transformation such that
(\mathrm{G},\sigma)
is a lax morphism of comonads from
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathbb{D}
, then
(\mathrm{G},\sigma^{-1})
is a colax morphism of comonads from
\mathbb{D}
to
\mathbb{C}
.

2.2.
(Co)algebras over
(co)monads

Let
\mathbb{B}
be a monad on
\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 2.6
.

A
\mathbb{B}
-algebra
on a
category
\mathcal{X}
is a pair

(\mathrm{M},\alpha)
consisting of
a functor
\mathrm{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{C}
and a natural
transformation
\alpha\colon\mathrm{B}\mathrm{M}\Rightarrow\mathrm{M}
(called

action
) such that

\alpha\circ\mu^{\mathbb{B}}\mathrm{M}=\alpha\circ\mathrm{B}\alpha
and

\alpha\circ\eta^{\mathbb{B}}\mathrm{M}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{M}}
.

When it comes to the notion of
morphism between algebras,

\mathcal{X}
and
\mathbb{B}
will always be
fixed in this paper:

Definition 2.7
.

A morphism between

\mathbb{B}
-algebras
(\mathrm{M},\alpha)

and
(\mathrm{N},\beta)
on
\mathcal{X}
is a natural transformation
f\colon\mathrm{M}\Rightarrow\mathrm{N}
such that
f\circ\alpha=\beta\circ\mathrm{B}f
.
The
Eilenberg–Moore
category of

\mathbb{B}
is the
category
\mathcal{C}^{\mathbb{B}}
of

\mathbb{B}
-algebras on the terminal
category.

Thus the objects of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathbb{B}}
are pairs
(X,\alpha)

of an object
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
and a morphism
\alpha\colon\mathrm{B}(X)\to X
in
\mathcal{C}
such
that
\alpha\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X}=\alpha\mathrm{B}(\alpha)

and
\alpha\eta^{\mathbb{B}}_{X}=\mathrm{id}_{X}
;
here
the concatenation
denotes the composition of
morphisms in
\mathcal{C}
(recall
we only use
\circ
to
denote the
vertical composition of
natural transformations).
A morphism from
(X,\alpha)
to
(Y,\beta)
is a morphism
f\colon X\to Y
in
\mathcal{C}
such that
f\alpha=\beta\mathrm{B}(f)
.

As with all other notions,
these definitions have the
obvious dual version:

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\mathbb{C}
be a comonad on
\mathcal{C}
.
A
\mathbb{C}
-coalgebra
on a
category
\mathcal{X}
is a
pair
(\mathrm{M},\gamma)
,
where
\mathrm{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{C}
is a functor and
\gamma\colon\mathrm{M}\Rightarrow\mathrm{C}\mathrm{M}
is a
natural transformation (called

coaction
) which
satisfies

\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{M}\circ\gamma=\mathrm{C}\gamma\circ\gamma
and

\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{M}\circ\gamma=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{M}}
.
A morphism between coalgebras
(\mathrm{M},\gamma)
and
(\mathrm{N},\delta)
is a natural transformation
f\colon\mathrm{M}\Rightarrow\mathrm{N}
such that
\delta\circ f=\mathrm{C}f\circ\gamma
.
If the category
\mathcal{X}
is
the terminal category, then
the category formed by
coalgebras over
\mathbb{C}
is
called the

Eilenberg–Moore
category
of
\mathbb{C}
and is denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

So an object in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}}

consists of an object
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})
together with
a morphism
\gamma\colon X\to\mathrm{C}(X)
in
\mathcal{C}

such that
\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}_{X}\gamma=\mathrm{C}(\gamma)\gamma

and
\epsilon^{\mathbb{C}}_{X}\gamma=\mathrm{id}_{X}
, and a morphism
from
(X,\gamma)
to
(Y,\delta)
is a morphism
f\colon X\to Y
in
\mathcal{C}
such
that
\delta f=\mathrm{C}(f)\gamma
.

Remark 2.9
.

In
[
9
]
,

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{C}}
was called the

coKleisli category
of

\mathbb{C}
.

In Section
3
, we
will furthermore use the
concept of an opcoalgebra over
a comonad
\mathbb{C}
on
\mathcal{C}
(one similarly
defines opalgebras over a
monad, but we we will not use
this term):

Definition 2.10
.

A
\mathbb{C}
-opcoalgebra
on

\mathcal{X}
is
a functor
\mathrm{M}\colon\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{X}
together with a natural
transformation
\gamma\colon\mathrm{M}\Rightarrow\mathrm{M}\mathrm{C}
satisfying

\mathrm{M}\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}\circ\gamma=\gamma\mathrm{C}\circ\gamma
and
\mathrm{M}\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}}\circ\gamma=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{M}}
.
A morphism between
opcoalgebras
(\mathrm{M},\gamma)

and
(\mathrm{N},\delta)
on
\mathcal{X}
is a natural transformation
f\colon\mathrm{M}\Rightarrow\mathrm{N}
such that
\delta\circ f=f\mathrm{C}\circ\gamma
.

Remark 2.11
.

Some authors call (co)algebras

left (co)modules
and
op(co)algebras
right
(co)modules
. This is
motivated by the
examples of (co)monads on
monoidal categories that are
given by tensoring with
(co)monoids therein.
However, we stick
to the original terminology
which is more suggestive in
examples in which
the (co)algebras are algebraic
structures such as
semigroups, rings etc.

2.3.
From (co)monads to adjunctions and back

We recall:

Definition 2.12
.

An
adjunction
is a pair of functors

\mathrm{F}\colon\mathcal{A}\rightleftarrows\mathcal{B}\colon\mathrm{U}
,
together with natural
transformations
\eta\colon\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{A}}\Rightarrow\mathrm{U}\mathrm{F}
and
\varepsilon\colon\mathrm{F}\mathrm{U}\Rightarrow\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{B}}
such that

\varepsilon\mathrm{F}\circ\mathrm{F}\eta=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{F}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{U}\varepsilon\circ\eta\mathrm{U}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{U}}.

(2.1)

We call
\mathrm{F}
the
left adjoint
and
\mathrm{U}
the
right adjoint
, which we shortly denote
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
.
The natural transformations
\eta

and
\varepsilon
are respectively
called the
unit
and the

counit
of the adjunction
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
.

In this situation, the
endofunctor
\mathrm{B}=\mathrm{U}\mathrm{F}
on
\mathcal{A}
is part of the monad
\mathbb{B}=(\mathrm{B},\mu^{\mathbb{B}},\eta^{\mathbb{B}})
,
where
\mu^{\mathbb{B}}=\mathrm{U}\epsilon\mathrm{F}

and
\eta^{\mathbb{B}}=\eta
, and
the endofunctor
\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{F}\mathrm{U}
on
\mathcal{B}
is part of the comonad
\mathbb{T}=(\mathrm{T},\Delta^{\mathbb{T}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{T}})
, where
\Delta^{\mathbb{T}}=\mathrm{F}\eta\mathrm{U}
and
\varepsilon^{\mathbb{T}}=\varepsilon.

Conversely, any monad
\mathbb{B}
on
\mathcal{A}
induces the
Eilenberg–Moore
adjunction

\mathrm{F}\colon\mathcal{A}\rightleftarrows\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}\colon\mathrm{U}
. Here, the
free algebra
functor

\mathrm{F}
is given by setting
\mathrm{F}(X)=(\mathrm{B}(X),\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X})
on objects
and
\mathrm{F}(f)=\mathrm{B}(f)
on morphisms,
while
\mathrm{U}
is the forgetful
functor defined by
\mathrm{U}(X,\alpha)=X
on objects and by
\mathrm{U}(f)=f
on morphisms.
The unit
\eta
of the adjunction is given by setting
\eta_{X}=\eta^{\mathbb{B}}_{X}
for
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})
.
The counit
\varepsilon
of the adjunction is defined by setting
\varepsilon_{(X,\alpha)}=\alpha
for
(X,\alpha)\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})
.
For a comonad
\mathbb{S}
on
\mathcal{B}
, the Eilenberg–Moore
adjunction
\mathrm{V}\colon\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{S}}\rightleftarrows\mathcal{B}\colon\mathrm{G}

is formed by the

cofree coalgebra
functor
, which is
given by
\mathrm{G}(X)=(\mathrm{S}(X),\Delta^{\mathbb{S}}_{X})
on objects
and by
\mathrm{G}(f)=\mathrm{S}(f)
on morphisms,
while
\mathrm{V}
is the forgetful
functor.
The unit
\eta^{\prime}
of the adjunction
is defined by
setting
\eta^{\prime}_{(X,\gamma)}=\gamma

for
(X,\gamma)\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{S}})
.
The counit
\varepsilon^{\prime}
of the
adjunction is given by
setting
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{X}=\varepsilon^{\mathbb{S}}_{X}
for
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{B})
.

2.4.
Distributive
laws

Let

\mathbb{B}
be a monad and

\mathbb{C},\mathbb{D}
be comonads on
\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 2.13
.

A
distributive law
of
\mathbb{C}
over
\mathbb{D}
is a natural transformation
\chi\colon\mathrm{C}\mathrm{D}\Rightarrow\mathrm{D}\mathrm{C}
such that
(\mathrm{D},\chi)
is a lax endomorphism of
\mathbb{C}
and
(\mathrm{C},\chi)
is a colax endomorphism of
\mathbb{D}
.
A
mixed distributive
law of
\mathbb{B}
over
\mathbb{C}
is a natural transformation
\theta\colon\mathrm{B}\mathrm{C}\Rightarrow\mathrm{C}\mathrm{B}
such that
(\mathrm{C},\theta)
is a lax endomorphism of the monad
\mathbb{B}
and
(\mathrm{B},\theta)
is a colax endomorphism of the comonad
\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 2.14
.

Some authors call (mixed)
distributive laws

entwinings
.

Finally, we recall the definition of
a coalgebra over a distributive law.
For this, let
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathbb{S}
be
comonads on a category
\mathcal{B}

and
\chi
be a distributive law of

Definition 2.15
.

A

\chi

on a category
\mathcal{Y}
is a pair

\varepsilon^{\mathbb{S}}\mathrm{M}\circ\rho=\varepsilon^{\mathbb{T}}\mathrm{M}
.
A
left
\chi
-coalgebra
on a category
\mathcal{Z}
is a pair
(\mathrm{N},\lambda)
, where
\mathrm{N}\colon\mathcal{B}\to\mathcal{Z}
is a
functor and
\lambda\colon\mathrm{N}\mathrm{S}\Rightarrow\mathrm{N}\mathrm{T}
is a natural transformation such that

\mathrm{N}\Delta^{\mathbb{T}}\circ\lambda=\lambda\mathrm{T}\circ\mathrm{N}\chi\circ\lambda\mathrm{S}\circ\mathrm{N}\Delta^{\mathbb{S}}

and
\mathrm{N}\varepsilon^{\mathbb{T}}\circ\lambda=\mathrm{N}\varepsilon^{\mathbb{S}}
.

\chi_{(X,\alpha)}=\Lambda_{\mathrm{U}(X,\alpha)}\mathrm{F}(\Omega^{-1}_{(X,\alpha)})=\theta_{X},

which proves equation (
3.6
).

(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
of coalgebras over the comonad
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
on
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}
, which is called the category of
entwined coalgebras
.
By Section
2.3
, we have the forgetful-cofree adjunction
\mathrm{V}\colon(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}\rightleftarrows\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}\colon\mathrm{G}
, whose associated comonad is precisely
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
.
By
[
22
, Section 4.9]
, one defines a comonad
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
on
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
such that
\mathrm{V}\mathrm{T}^{\theta}=\mathrm{T}\mathrm{V}
as follows.
For any object
((X,\alpha),\delta)
and any morphism
f
in
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
, set

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}^{\theta}((X,\alpha),\delta)=(\mathrm{T}(X,\alpha),\chi_{(X,\alpha)}\mathrm{T}(\delta))=((\mathrm{B}(X),\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X}),\theta_{X}\mathrm{B}(\delta)),

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}^{\theta}(f)=\mathrm{T}(f),\quad\Delta^{\mathbb{T}^{\theta}}_{((X,\alpha),\delta)}=\Delta^{\mathbb{T}}_{(X,\alpha)},\quad\varepsilon^{\mathbb{T}^{\theta}}_{((X,\alpha),\delta)}=\varepsilon^{\mathbb{T}}_{(X,\alpha)}.

Remark 3.5
.

Beware that
\mathrm{T}^{\theta}
is not to
\mathrm{T}
, what
\mathrm{C}^{\theta}
to
\mathrm{C}
is.
While the comonad
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
extends the comonad
\mathbb{T}
through
\mathrm{V}\dashv\mathrm{G}
, the comonad
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
lifts
\mathbb{C}
through
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
(see Definition
2.18
).
The choice of this slightly inconsistent notation is motivated by the formulas (
3.4
) and (
3.8
).
Note that
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
stands for
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
stands for
\mathbb{S}
in the more general setup of Section
3.2
.

We can use the
identity natural
transformation

to exhibit the comonad extension in the sense of Definition
2.18
. Explicitly, we set
\tilde{\Lambda}_{((X,\alpha),\delta)}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{T}(X,\alpha)}=\mathrm{id}_{(\mathrm{B}(X),\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X})}
for all
((X,\alpha),\delta)
in
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
.
We have the following lemma, which is similar to Lemma
3.4
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The unique natural transformation
\tilde{\Omega}\colon\mathrm{T}^{\theta}\mathrm{G}\Rightarrow\mathrm{G}\mathrm{T}
whose mate equals
\tilde{\Lambda}
is computed by setting

\tilde{\Omega}_{(X,\alpha)}=\theta_{X}

for all
(X,\alpha)\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})
.
In particular,
\tilde{\Omega}
implements a comonad extension
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
of
\mathbb{T}
if and only if the morphism
\theta_{X}
is an isomorphism from
\mathrm{T}^{\theta}(\mathrm{G}(X))
to
\mathrm{G}(\mathrm{T}(X))
for any
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

We first note that for any
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})
, the morphism
\theta_{X}
is a morphism in
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
from
\mathrm{T}^{\theta}(\mathrm{G}(X))
to
\mathrm{G}(\mathrm{T}(X))
. Indeed, this fact follows from the hypothesis that
(\mathrm{B},\theta)
is a colax endomorphism of
\mathbb{C}
. Next, denote by
\varepsilon^{\prime}
and
\eta^{\prime}
the counit and unit of the adjunction
\mathrm{V}\dashv\mathrm{G}
.
By equation (
2.2
) and by snake relations (
2.1
), we have for any
(X,\alpha)\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})
,

\tilde{\Omega}_{(X,\alpha)}=\mathrm{G}(\mathrm{T}(\varepsilon^{\prime}_{(X,\alpha)}))\mathrm{G}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{G}(X,\alpha)})\eta^{\prime}_{\mathrm{T}^{\theta}(\mathrm{G}(X,\alpha))}.

By definitions and functoriality of
\mathrm{G}
, it follows that

\tilde{\Omega}_{(X,\alpha)}=\mathrm{C}(\mathrm{B}(\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}}_{X}))\theta_{\mathrm{C}(X)}\mathrm{B}(\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{C}}),

which by naturality of
\theta
and counitality of
\mathbb{C}
implies the statement.
∎

We have the following corollary of Theorem

Corollary 3.7
.

If the mixed distributive law

\chi
-coalgebra structures on

\mathrm{N}
correspond bijectively to
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
-opcoalgebra structures on
\mathrm{N}\mathrm{V}
.

Proof.

By using Lemma
3.6
and the invertibility of
\theta
, we have that the diagram

\textstyle{\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{G}}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{T}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\tilde{\Omega}}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{T}^{\theta}}
\textstyle{(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}}

commutes up to the natural isomorphism
\tilde{\Omega}
.
It follows from the definition of

, functoriality, and equation (

) that
(\mathrm{V},\tilde{\Lambda})
is a colax morphism of comonads from
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
to
\mathbb{T}
.
By using Remark

, this implies that
(\mathrm{G},\tilde{\Omega})
is a lax morphism of comonads from
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
to
\mathbb{T}
.
Finally, since

is an isomorphism, the induced distributive law

3.6.
Generalized Hopf module
theorems

Assume now
that we are in the setup of Section
3.5
.
An even simpler classification of
left
\chi
-coalgebra structures on

\mathrm{N}
is possible in the case when
the category
\mathcal{A}
is equivalent
to the category
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
of entwined
coalgebras.
To this end, we recall a result from
[
13
]
(see also
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
).
Let
\mathrm{K}\colon\mathcal{A}\to(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
be a
functor such that the following
diagram (strictly) commutes

\textstyle{{\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}}}
\textstyle{{\mathcal{A}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{K}}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{F}}
\textstyle{(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}.\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{V}}

In other words, we have for any
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})
,

\mathrm{K}(X)=(\mathrm{F}(X),\gamma_{X}),

where
\gamma\colon\mathrm{F}\Rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{\theta}\mathrm{F}
is a natural transformation giving the functor
\mathrm{F}
a coalgebra structure over
\mathrm{C}^{\theta}
.
The latter induces the
Galois map

\kappa
associated to
\mathrm{K}
and the adjunction
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
, which is a natural transformation from
\mathrm{T}
to
\mathrm{C}^{\theta}
defined by

\kappa=\mathrm{C}^{\theta}\varepsilon\circ\gamma\mathrm{U}\colon\mathrm{T}\Rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{\theta}.

We recall the following result obtained by combining
[
13
, Theorem 4.4]
with
[
16
, Proposition 2.5]
.

Theorem 3.8
.

The functor
\mathrm{K}
is an equivalence if and only if

(1)

the natural transformation
\kappa\mathrm{F}
is an isomorphism and

(2)

the functor
\mathrm{F}
is comonadic, that is, the functor
\mathrm{L}\colon\mathcal{A}\to(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{T}}
, which is the unique functor such that
\mathrm{V}\mathrm{L}=\mathrm{F}
and
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{U}=\mathrm{G}
, is an equivalence.

Remark 3.9
.

Note that the category
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{T}}
appears under the name
descent category
in
[
2
]
, or
category of descent data with respect to the monad

\mathbb{B}
in
[
12
]
.

If
\mathrm{K}
is an equivalence with a quasi-inverse
\bar{\mathrm{K}}
, then
\mathrm{K}\dashv\bar{\mathrm{K}}
. If
\xi
is the counit and
\phi
the unit of the adjunction, then the conjugate comonad
\overline{\mathbb{T}^{\theta}}
on
\mathcal{A}
of the comonad
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
on
{(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}}
(see Section
3.3
) is defined by

\overline{\mathbb{T}^{\theta}}=(\bar{\mathrm{K}}\mathrm{T}^{\theta}\mathrm{K},\bar{\mathrm{K}}\mathrm{T}^{\theta}\xi^{-1}\mathrm{T}^{\theta}\mathrm{K}\circ\bar{\mathrm{K}}\Delta^{\mathbb{T}^{\theta}}\mathrm{K},{\phi}^{-1}\circ\bar{\mathrm{K}}\varepsilon^{\mathbb{T}^{\theta}}\mathrm{K}).

By combining Corollary
3.7
with Corollary
3.3
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.10
.

If the mixed distributive law
\theta
is invertible and if
\mathrm{K}
is an equivalence, then (in the above notation) the left
\chi
-coalgebras on
\mathrm{N}
correspond to
\overline{\mathbb{T}^{\theta}}
-opcoalgebras on
\mathrm{N}\mathrm{F}
.

3.7.
An example from bimonads

In this section, we apply the theory described in Sections
3.5
and
3.6
to bimonads in the sense of
[
15
]
and opmonoidal monads (called bimonads in
[
4
]
and Hopf monads in
[
17
]
).

Definition 3.11
.

A
bimonad
on
\mathcal{A}
(in the sense of
[
15
]
) is a sextuple
\mathbb{B}=(\mathrm{B},\mu^{\mathbb{B}},\eta^{\mathbb{B}},\Delta^{\mathbb{B}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}},\theta)
, where
\mathbb{B}=(\mathrm{B},\mu^{\mathbb{B}},\eta^{\mathbb{B}})
is a monad,
\mathbb{C}=(\mathrm{B},\Delta^{\mathbb{B}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}})
is a comonad and the natural transformation
\theta\colon\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}\Rightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{B}
is a mixed distributive law of
\mathbb{B}
over
\mathbb{C}
such that
\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\mu^{\mathbb{B}}=\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}}\mathrm{B}
,
\Delta^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\eta^{\mathbb{B}}=\eta^{\mathbb{B}}\mathrm{B}\circ\eta^{\mathbb{B}}
,
\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\eta^{\mathbb{B}}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{A}}
, and

\Delta^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\mu^{\mathbb{B}}=\mathrm{B}\mu^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\theta\mathrm{B}\circ\mathrm{B}\Delta^{\mathbb{B}}.

(3.10)

Remark 3.12
.

In particular, any bimonad
\mathbb{B}
on
\mathcal{A}
in the sense of the above definition satisfies
\theta\circ\eta^{\mathbb{B}}\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{C}\eta^{\mathbb{B}}
and
\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}}\mathrm{B}\circ\theta=\mathrm{B}\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}}
. Besides, we note that it follows from axioms for
\mathbb{B}
that
\Delta^{\mathbb{B}}=\theta\circ\mathrm{B}\eta^{\mathbb{B}}
and
\mu^{\mathbb{B}}=\mathrm{C}\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}}\circ\theta
.

Let
\mathbb{B}=(\mathrm{B},\mu^{\mathbb{B}},\eta^{\mathbb{B}},\Delta^{\mathbb{B}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}},\theta)
be a bimonad in the sense of Definition
3.11
. The comonad
\mathbb{C}=(\mathrm{B},\Delta^{\mathbb{B}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}})
then lifts to a comonad
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
on
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}
(see equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
)). If
\theta
is invertible, then the comonad
\mathbb{T}
on
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}
induced by the Eilenberg–Moore adjunction
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
is a lift of the comonad
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
on
(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
(see equations (
3.7
) and (
3.8
)). In this case, according to Corollary
3.7
, the left
\chi
-coalgebras over a functor
\mathrm{N}\colon\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}\to\mathcal{Z}
correspond bijectively to
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
-opcoalgebras over
\mathrm{N}\mathrm{V}
.
Following
[
15
, Section 4.3]
, consider a functor
\mathrm{K}\colon\mathcal{A}\to(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
, given by setting for any object
X
and any morphism
f
in
\mathcal{A}
,

\mathrm{K}(X)=(\mathrm{F}(X),\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{B}})=((\mathrm{B}(X),\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X}),\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{B}})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{K}(f)=\mathrm{F}(f).

Note that it follows from equation (
3.10
) that for any
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})
, the morphism
\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{B}}
is a morphism from the algebra
\mathrm{F}(X)
to the algebra
\mathrm{C}^{\theta}\mathrm{F}(X)
.
Thus the functor
\mathrm{K}
is well-defined and induces a coalgebra
(\mathrm{F},\gamma)
over
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
by setting
\gamma_{X}=\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{B}}
for all
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})
.
We clearly have
\mathrm{V}\mathrm{K}=\mathrm{F}
and the theory from Section
3.6
applies.
The Galois map associated to
\mathrm{K}
and
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
is given by setting for all
(X,\alpha)\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})
,

\kappa_{(X,\alpha)}=\mathrm{B}(\alpha)\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{B}}\colon(\mathrm{B}(X),\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X})\to(\mathrm{B}(X),\mathrm{B}(\alpha)\theta_{X}).

We recall from Theorem
3.8
that the functor
\mathrm{K}
is an equivalence if and only if the natural transformation
\kappa\mathrm{F}
is an isomorphism and if the free functor
\mathrm{F}
is comonadic.
Finally, if
\theta
is invertible and if
\mathrm{K}
is an equivalence, then Corollary
3.10
is applicable and one gets a simpler classification of the left
\chi
-coalgebras on
\mathrm{N}
.

Remark 3.13
.

We note that a bimonad
\mathbb{B}=(\mathrm{B},\mu^{\mathbb{B}},\eta^{\mathbb{B}},\Delta^{\mathbb{B}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}},\theta)
on
\mathcal{A}
such that
\kappa\mathrm{F}
is a natural isomorphism in the above sense is called a
Hopf monad
in
[
15
]
.
This is different than notion of a Hopf monad in the sense of
[
4
]
, which is considered in the next section.

3.8.
An example from opmonoidal monads

In this section, we outline (following
[
14
, Section 5]
) a particular case of the theory described in Sections
3.5
and
3.6
in the setting of monoidal categories.
An
opmonoidal monad
(see
[
11
]
, or
[
17
,
4
]
under different names)
\mathbb{B}
on a monoidal category
(\mathcal{A},\otimes,\mathbb{1})
is an opmonoidal functor and a monad on
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mu^{\mathbb{B}}
and
\eta^{\mathbb{B}}
are opmonoidal natural transformations.
This means in particular that the functor
\mathrm{B}
comes with opmonoidal constraints

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\mathrm{B}_{2}(X,Y)\colon\mathrm{B}(X\otimes Y)\to\mathrm{B}(X)\otimes\mathrm{B}(Y)\}_{X,Y\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}_{0}

\displaystyle\colon\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{1})\to\mathbb{1}\

satisfying several axioms. For example, one has for any
X,Y\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})
,

\mathrm{B}_{2}(X,Y)\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X\otimes Y}=(\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X}\otimes\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{Y})\mathrm{B}_{2}(\mathrm{B}(X),\mathrm{B}(Y))\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{B}_{2}(X,Y)).

(3.11)

Following
[
4
]
, an opmonoidal monad
\mathbb{B}
gives rise to natural transformations

\displaystyle H^{l}=

\displaystyle\{H^{l}_{X,Y}\colon\mathrm{B}(X\otimes\mathrm{B}(Y))\to\mathrm{B}(X)\otimes\mathrm{B}(Y)\}_{X,Y\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle H^{r}=

\displaystyle\{H^{r}_{X,Y}\colon\mathrm{B}(\mathrm{B}(X)\otimes Y)\to\mathrm{B}(X)\otimes\mathrm{B}(Y)\}_{X,Y\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})},

which are called
left
and
right fusion operators
and are respectively defined by setting for any
X,Y\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})
,

\displaystyle H^{l}_{X,Y}=

\displaystyle(\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{B}(X)}\otimes\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{Y})\mathrm{B}_{2}(X,\mathrm{B}(Y)),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle H^{r}_{X,Y}=

\displaystyle(\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{B}(Y)})\mathrm{B}_{2}(\mathrm{B}(X),Y).

Definition 3.14
.

A
left
(respectively, a
right
)
pre-Hopf monad
on
\mathcal{A}
is an opmonoidal monad on
\mathcal{A}
such that
H^{l}_{\mathbb{1},-}
(respectively,
H^{r}_{\mathbb{1},-}
) is an isomorphism.
A
pre-Hopf monad
on
\mathcal{A}
is a left and a right pre-Hopf monad. A
Hopf monad
on
\mathcal{A}
is an opmonoidal monad on
\mathcal{A}
such that both left and right fusion operators are isomorphisms.

For more details and algebraic properties of Hopf monads in the sense of the above definition, see
[
4
]
.
Now let
\mathbb{B}
be an opmonoidal monad on a monoidal category
(\mathcal{A},\otimes,\mathbb{1})
and let
\mathbb{C}
be a comonad on
\mathcal{A}
given by setting for all
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})
and every morphism
f
in
\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}(X)=\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{1})\otimes X,\quad\mathrm{C}(f)=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{1})}\otimes f,

\displaystyle\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}_{X}=\mathrm{B}_{2}(\mathbb{1},\mathbb{1})\otimes X,\quad\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}}_{X}=\mathrm{B}_{0}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{X}.

Following
[
14
, Section 5]
, we define a mixed distributive law of
\mathbb{B}
over
\mathbb{C}
by setting for
X\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A})
,

\theta_{X}=(\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{\mathbb{1}}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{B}(X)})\mathrm{B}_{2}(\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{1}),X)\colon\mathrm{B}\mathrm{C}(X)\to\mathrm{C}\mathrm{B}(X).

Using equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) and the above formulas we have that the lift
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
of
\mathbb{C}
to
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}
is given by setting for any
(X,\alpha)\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}})
and any morphism
f
in
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{B}}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}^{\theta}(X,\alpha)=(\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{1})\otimes X,(\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{\mathbb{1}}\otimes\alpha_{X})\mathrm{B}_{2}(\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{1}),X)),

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}^{\theta}(f)=\mathrm{C}(f),\qquad\Delta^{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}_{(X,\alpha)}=\Delta^{\mathbb{C}}_{X},\qquad\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}_{(X,\alpha)}=\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}}_{X}.

(3.13)

Remark that
\mathrm{C}^{\theta}(X,\alpha)
is the monoidal product of
\mathbb{B}
-algebras
(\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{1}),\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{\mathbb{1}})
and
(X,\alpha)
(see
[

).
From Section
3.5
and particularly Lemma

, we conclude that the natural isomorphism
\Omega\colon\mathrm{C}\mathrm{U}\Rightarrow\mathrm{U}\mathrm{C}^{\theta}
implementing the lift
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
of
\mathbb{C}
, its mate
\Lambda\colon\mathrm{F}\mathrm{C}\Rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{\theta}\mathrm{F}
, and the distributive law

induced by

and

are given by the formulas

\displaystyle\Omega_{(X,\alpha)}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{1})\otimes X},\quad\Lambda_{X}=(\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{\mathbb{1}}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{B}(X)})\mathrm{B}_{2}(\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{1}),X),

\displaystyle\chi_{(X,\alpha)}=(\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{\mathbb{1}}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{B}(X)})\mathrm{B}_{2}(\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{1}),X).

Following equations (

) and (

\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
on

\displaystyle\mathrm{T}^{\theta}((X,\alpha),\delta)=((\mathrm{B}(X),\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X}),(\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{\mathbb{1}}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{B}(X)})\mathrm{B}_{2}(\mathrm{B}(\mathbb{1}),X)\mathrm{B}(\delta)),

As in Section

of

\tilde{\Omega}_{(X,\alpha)}
equals to

Following

4.1.
A bimonad from
bialgebras

In what follows,
we consider the bimonad

\mathbb{B}=(\mathrm{B},\mu^{\mathbb{B}},\eta^{\mathbb{B}},\Delta^{\mathbb{B}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}},\theta)

on
{}_{k}\mathrm{Mod}
(in the sense
of
[
14
]
), which is
defined by the formulas

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}=H\otimes{-},\quad\mu_{X}^{\mathbb{B}}=\mu\otimes\mathrm{id}_{X},\quad\eta_{X}^{\mathbb{B}}=1\otimes\mathrm{id}_{X},\quad\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{B}}=\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id}_{X},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{X}^{\mathbb{B}}=\varepsilon\otimes\mathrm{id}_{X},\quad\theta_{M}(a\otimes b\otimes m)=a_{1}b\otimes a_{2}\otimes m.

4.2.
The first
adjunction

First, we have
that

{}_{k}\mathrm{Mod}^{\mathbb{B}}={}_{H}\mathrm{Mod}

and that the Eilenberg–Moore
adjunction
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}

for
\mathbb{B}
consists of the free
module functor
\mathrm{F}=H\otimes-\colon{}_{k}\mathrm{Mod}\to{}_{H}\mathrm{Mod}
and the forgetful
functor
\mathrm{U}
.
For a left
H
-module
M
, the
H
-action on
\mathrm{F}(M)=H\otimes M
is induced by multiplication
in
H
.
The unit and the counit of the adjunction
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
are defined by the formulas

\eta_{M}^{\prime}(m)=1\otimes m,\qquad\varepsilon_{(M,\alpha)}^{\prime}(h\otimes m)=hm.

Thus the induced comonad
\mathbb{T}
on
{}_{H}\mathrm{Mod}
is given by the formulas

\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{F}\mathrm{U}=H\otimes-,\quad\Delta^{\mathbb{T}}_{(M,\alpha)}(h\otimes m)=h\otimes 1\otimes m,\quad\varepsilon^{\mathbb{T}}_{(M,\alpha)}(h\otimes m)=hm.

The second comonad
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
on
{}_{H}\mathrm{Mod}
is a lift of the comonad
\mathbb{C}=(\mathrm{B},\Delta^{\mathbb{B}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{B}})
on
{}_{k}\mathrm{Mod}
. It is explicitly given by
\mathrm{C}^{\theta}(M,\alpha)=(H\otimes M,\beta)
, where

\beta(a\otimes b\otimes m)=a_{1}b\otimes\alpha(a_{2}\otimes m)=a_{1}b\otimes a_{2}m

is the diagonal action. The comultiplication and the counit of the comonad
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
are defined by

\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}=\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id}_{X},\quad\varepsilon_{X}^{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}=\varepsilon\otimes\mathrm{id}_{X}.

The natural isomorphism
\Omega\colon\mathrm{C}\mathrm{U}\Rightarrow\mathrm{U}\mathrm{C}^{\theta}
which implements this lift is given by
\Omega_{(M,\alpha)}=\mathrm{id}_{H\otimes M}
for any
H
-module
M
.
Its mate
\Lambda\colon\mathrm{F}\mathrm{C}\Rightarrow\mathrm{C}^{\theta}\mathrm{F}
is given by setting for any
k
-module
M
,

\Lambda_{M}(a\otimes b\otimes m)=a_{1}b\otimes a_{2}\otimes m,

which is an
H
-module map from the free
H
-module
H\otimes H\otimes M
to the
H
-module
H\otimes H\otimes M
with the diagonal action on first two tensorands.

4.3.
The second adjunction

We clearly have that
({}_{H}\mathrm{Mod})_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}={}_{H}^{H}\mathrm{Mod}
. The Eilenberg–Moore adjunction
\mathrm{V}\dashv\mathrm{G}
for
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
consists of the forgetful functor

\mathrm{V}\colon{}_{H}^{H}\mathrm{Mod}\to{}_{H}\mathrm{Mod}
(which is left
adjoint!) and the cofree comodule functor
\mathrm{G}\colon{}_{H}\mathrm{Mod}\to{}_{H}^{H}\mathrm{Mod}
.
More precisely,
\mathrm{G}
is defined by setting

\mathrm{G}=H\otimes-
, such that for an
H
-module
M
, the
H
-action on
\mathrm{G}(M)
is the diagonal action
and the
H
-coaction on
\mathrm{G}(M)
is given by
\Delta\otimes\mathrm{id}_{M}
.
The unit and the counit of the
adjunction
\mathrm{V}\dashv\mathrm{G}

are defined by the formulas

\eta^{\prime\prime}_{(M,\alpha,\gamma)}=\gamma,\qquad\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}_{(M,\alpha)}=\varepsilon^{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}_{(M,\alpha)}.

The comonad
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}

such that
\mathrm{V}\mathrm{T}^{\theta}=\mathrm{T}\mathrm{V}
is given by

\mathrm{T}^{\theta}=H\otimes-,\quad\Delta^{\mathbb{T}^{\theta}}_{(M,\alpha,\gamma)}(h\otimes m)=h\otimes 1\otimes m,\quad\varepsilon^{\mathbb{T}^{\theta}}_{(M,\alpha)}(h\otimes m)=hm.

For a Hopf module
M
, the
H
-action on
\mathrm{T}^{\theta}(M)
is the free action and the
H
-coaction
\gamma
on
\mathrm{T}^{\theta}(M)

is given by the formula

\gamma(h\otimes m)=h_{1}m_{-1}\otimes h_{2}\otimes m_{0}.

We have the natural
transformation
\tilde{\Lambda}\colon\mathrm{V}\mathrm{T}^{\theta}\Rightarrow\mathrm{T}\mathrm{V}
, which is
given by setting
\tilde{\Lambda}_{(M,\alpha,\gamma)}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathrm{F}(M)}
for any

H
-module
M
. The following
result allows us to define

\tilde{\Omega}\colon\mathrm{T}^{\theta}\mathrm{G}\Rightarrow\mathrm{G}\mathrm{T}
,
which will be part of that
data for the extension
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
of
\mathbb{T}
through
\mathrm{V}\dashv\mathrm{G}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

The unique
natural transformation
\tilde{\Omega}\colon\mathrm{T}^{\theta}\mathrm{G}\Rightarrow\mathrm{G}\mathrm{T}
whose mate
equals
\tilde{\Lambda}
is
given for any
(M,\alpha)
in

{}_{H}\mathrm{Mod}
by

\tilde{\Omega}_{(M,\alpha)}(a\otimes b\otimes m)=\theta_{M}(a\otimes b\otimes m)=a_{1}b\otimes a_{2}\otimes m.

(2)

The morphism
\tilde{\Omega}_{(M,\alpha)}
is a morphism of Hopf modules for any
(M,\alpha)
in
{}_{H}\mathrm{Mod}
.

(3)

If
H
is a Hopf algebra with a bijective antipode, then
\theta_{M}
is invertible with inverse

\theta^{-1}_{M}(a\otimes b\otimes m)=b_{2}\otimes S^{-1}(b_{1})a\otimes m.

Moreover,
\tilde{\Omega}
implements an
extension of
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
of

\mathbb{T}
through
\mathrm{V}\dashv\mathrm{G}
.

Proof.

This is mostly a corollary of statements seen in Section
3.5
which apply to bimonads in the sense of Mesablishvili and Wisbauer.

(1)

This statement follows from Lemma

3.6
.

(2)

Following
(1)
, one
needs to verify that

\theta_{M}
is a morphism from
the free
H
-module
H\otimes H\otimes M
to the
H
-module

H\otimes H\otimes M
, where
H

acts diagonally on the first
two tensorands. Also, one
needs to check that it is a
morphism from the

H
-comodule
H\otimes H\otimes M
with coaction

a\otimes b\otimes m\mapsto a_{1}b_{1}\otimes a_{2}\otimes b_{2}\otimes m

to
the cofree comodule
H\otimes H\otimes M
. Both verifications can be done by hand but they also follow from the fact that
\theta
is a mixed distributive law.

(3)

The first part of the statement is well-known and left to the reader. See
[
4
, Example 2.10]
for the expression of the inverse
in the case of Hopf algebras (with invertible antipode) in braided monoidal categories.
Finally, once
\theta
is an
isomorphism, it follows from

(1)
and
(2)
that
\tilde{\Omega}_{(M,\alpha)}
is
an isomorphism of the
aforementioned Hopf modules for any
H
-module
(M,\alpha)
. ∎

Let

\mathrm{K}\colon{}_{k}\mathrm{Mod}\to{}_{H}^{H}\mathrm{Mod}
be the functor given by the formulas

\mathrm{K}(X)=(\mathrm{F}(X),\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{B}})=((\mathrm{B}(X),\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{X}),\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{B}})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{K}(f)=\mathrm{F}(f).

It follows from Section
3.7

that the functor
\mathrm{K}
is well-defined
and induces a coalgebra
(\mathrm{F},\gamma)

over
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
by setting
\gamma_{X}=\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{B}}
for all
k
-modules
X
.
The Galois map associated to
\mathrm{K}
and
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}

is given by setting for all
H
-modules

(M,\alpha)
,

\kappa_{(M,\alpha)}=\mathrm{B}(\alpha)\Delta_{M}^{\mathbb{B}}\colon(\mathrm{B}(M),\mu^{\mathbb{B}}_{M})\to(\mathrm{B}(M),\mathrm{B}(\alpha)\theta_{M}).

For any
k
-module
X
, the map
\kappa_{\mathrm{F}(X)}\colon H\otimes H\otimes X\to H\otimes H\otimes X
is given by setting for any
h,\ell\in H
and
x\in X
,

\kappa_{(H\otimes X,\mu_{H}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{X})}(h\otimes\ell\otimes x)=h_{1}\otimes h_{2}\ell\otimes x.

It is well-known that
H
is a
Hopf algebra if and only if the
latter map is for all
X
an
isomorphism; in this case, its
inverse is

h\otimes\ell\otimes x\mapsto h_{1}\otimes S(h_{2})\ell\otimes x
.
By the fundamental
theorem on Hopf modules from

[
10
]
, this is also
equivalent to the condition
that the category
\mathcal{C}={}_{H}^{H}\mathrm{Mod}
of Hopf modules is equivalent
to
\mathcal{A}={}_{k}\mathrm{Mod}
by
means of the functor that
assigns to a Hopf module
M

the space of coinvariants
k\Box_{H}M=\{x\in M\mid x_{-1}\otimes x_{0}=1\otimes x\}
.

If
H
is a bialgebra, right-
\chi
-coalgebras are by
[
9
, Proposition 3.6]
in bijection with
H
-modules
which are simultaneously
H
-comodules (without a compatibility condition between them).
However, if
H
is
a Hopf algebra with bijective
antipode, thanks to Lemma
4.1
(3) we can apply Corollaries
3.7
and
3.10
to obtain a
completely dual classification
of the left
\chi
-coalgebras as modules and
comodules.

Remark 4.2
.

The example of this section
can be explained in terms of
the opmonoidal monad (see Section
3.8
)
\mathrm{B}=H\otimes-
on
{}_{k}\mathrm{Mod}
, where

\mathrm{B}_{2}(X,Y)\colon H\otimes X\otimes Y\to H\otimes X\otimes H\otimes Y,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{B}_{0}\colon H\to k

are given by

\mathrm{B}_{2}(X,Y)(h\otimes x\otimes y)=h_{1}\otimes x\otimes h_{2}\otimes y\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{B}_{0}(x)=\varepsilon(x).

5.
A nonexample: The list bimonad

In this section, we explore the non-empty list monad
on the category
\mathsf{Set}
of
sets. This is a bimonad in the
sense of
[
15
]
. However, we show that in this case the important Corollaries
3.7
and
3.10
from Section
3
are not applicable.

5.1.
The non-empty list bimonad

In order to fix the notation, we recall the following.

Definition 5.1
.

A
list
on a set

X
is an element of the set

\coprod_{n\geq 0}{X^{n}}
, that is,
a pair
(l,n)
, where
l\in X^{n}
is an ordered
n
-tuple of elements
in
X
and
n\geq 0
is an
integer called the
length
of
the list
l
. The list is

non-empty
if
n>0
.
In what follows, we denote such a list
((x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),n)
by
[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.

Following

[
9
, Section 6.3]
, one
defines the
non-empty
list bimonad

\mathbb{L}^{+}=(\mathrm{L}^{+},\mu^{\mathbb{L}^{+}},\eta^{\mathbb{L}^{+}},\Delta^{\mathbb{L}^{+}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{L}^{+}},\theta)
on
\mathsf{Set}
as follows. First, for any set
X
and
any function
f\colon X\to Y
,

\mathrm{L}^{+}(X)=\coprod_{n>0}{X^{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{L}^{+}(f)([x_{1},\dots,x_{n}])=[f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{n})].

Next, the multiplication

\mu^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}_{X}\colon\mathrm{L}^{+}(\mathrm{L}^{+}(X))\to\mathrm{L}^{+}(X)
on
\mathbb{L}^{+}

is given by sending a list

[[x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{1,n_{1}}],\ldots,[x_{m,1},\ldots,x_{m,n_{m}}]]

to the concatenated list

[x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{1,n_{1}},\ldots,x_{m,1},\ldots,x_{m,n_{m}}].

The unit map

\eta^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}_{X}\colon X\to\mathrm{L}^{+}(X)
sends an
element
x\in X
to the list

[x]
. The comultiplication
and counit of
\mathbb{L}^{+}
are
given by the formulas

\Delta^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}_{X}([x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])=[[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}],\ldots,[x_{n}]],\quad\varepsilon^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}_{X}([x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])=x_{1}.

To shorten the notation, we sometimes refer to the comonad
(\mathrm{L}^{+},\Delta^{\mathbb{L}^{+}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{L}^{+}})
as
\mathbb{C}
.
Finally, the mixed
distributive law

\theta
is defined as follows.
For any set
X
, the function
\theta_{X}
sends a list

[[x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{1,n_{1}}],\ldots,[x_{m,1},\ldots,x_{m,n_{m}}]]

in

\mathrm{L}^{+}(\mathrm{L}^{+}(X))
to the list

\displaystyle\Big[[x_{1,1},x_{2,1},x_{3,1}\ldots,x_{m,1}],\ldots,[x_{1,n_{1}},x_{2,1},x_{3,1},\ldots,x_{m,1}]

,

\displaystyle[x_{2,1},x_{3,1}\ldots,x_{m,1}],\ldots,[x_{2,n_{2}},x_{3,1},\ldots x_{m,1}]

,

\displaystyle\ldots

,

\displaystyle[x_{m,1}],[x_{m,2}],\ldots,[x_{m,n_{m}}]\Big]

in
\mathrm{L}^{+}(\mathrm{L}^{+}(X))
.

Proposition 5.2
.

For any set
X
, the function
\theta_{X}
is injective. However, for a non-empty set
X
, the function
\theta_{X}
is not surjective.

Proof.

The injectivity follows from the definition of the mixed distributive law
\theta
.
To see that
\theta_{X}
is not a surjection we remark that for any non-empty set
X
, there is a list
y\in\mathrm{L}^{+}(\mathrm{L}^{+}(X))
which has no preimage.
For instance, take
y=[[y_{1},y_{2}]]\in\mathrm{L}^{+}(\mathrm{L}^{+}(X)).

Assume that there is an
x\in\mathrm{L}^{+}(\mathrm{L}^{+}(X))
with
\theta_{X}(x)=y
.
The list
x
is of the form

[[x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{1,n_{1}}],\ldots,[x_{m,1},\ldots,x_{m,n_{m}}]]

for some
m\geq 1
and
n_{1},\dots,n_{m}\geq 1
.
Since
\theta_{X}(x)=y
, we necessarily have
\mathrm{length}(\theta_{X}(x))=\mathrm{length}(y),
which is equivalent with

n_{1}+\dots+n_{m}=1.

Now since the left hand side of the above equation is at least
m\geq 1
, we have that
m=1
and
n_{1}=1
. Contradiction.
∎

5.2.
Entwined coalgebras

The Eilenberg–Moore category
\mathsf{Set}^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}
of the monad
\mathbb{L}^{+}=(\mathrm{L}^{+},\mu^{\mathbb{L}^{+}},\eta^{\mathbb{L}^{+}})
is isomorphic to the category
\mathsf{SemiGp}
of semigroups (see
[
9
, Proposition 6.4.]
).
The Eilenberg–Moore adjunction
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
for the monad
\mathbb{L}^{+}
consists of the free functor, which is given by

\mathrm{F}(X)=(\mathrm{L}^{+}(X),\mu_{X}^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}),\qquad\mathrm{F}(f)=\mathrm{L}^{+}(f)

and the forgetful functor
\mathrm{U}\colon\mathsf{SemiGp}\to\mathsf{Set}
.
The unit and the counit of the adjunction
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
are given by the formulas

\eta^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}_{X}(x)=[x],\qquad\varepsilon^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}_{(X,\alpha)}=\alpha.

Clearly,
\mathrm{U}\mathrm{F}=\mathrm{L}^{+}
, but by Section
2.3
, we also have that the endofunctor
\mathrm{F}\mathrm{U}
is part of a comonad
\mathbb{T}
on
\mathsf{SemiGp}
.
Next, following Section
3.5
, there is a comonad lift
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
of
\mathbb{C}=(\mathbb{L}^{+},\Delta^{\mathbb{L}^{+}},\varepsilon^{\mathbb{L}^{+}})
through the adjunction
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
so that

\textstyle{\mathsf{SemiGp}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{C}^{\theta}}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{U}}
\textstyle{\mathsf{SemiGp}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{U}}
\textstyle{\mathsf{Set}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Omega}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{C}}
\textstyle{\mathsf{Set}}

commutes up to the natural isomorphism
\Omega=\mathrm{id}
, i.e. commutes strictly. Further, let
\mathrm{V}\dashv\mathrm{G}
be the Eilenberg–Moore adjunction for
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
. As described in Section
3.5
, there is a comonad
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
on the category
\mathsf{SemiGp}_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
such that
\mathrm{V}\mathrm{T}^{\theta}=\mathrm{T}\mathrm{V}
.
This defines the identity natural transformation
\tilde{\Lambda}\colon\mathrm{V}\mathrm{T}^{\theta}\Rightarrow\mathrm{T}\mathrm{V}
.
We have by Lemma
3.6
, that the unique natural transformation
\tilde{\Omega}\colon\mathrm{T}^{\theta}\mathrm{G}\Rightarrow\mathrm{G}\mathrm{T}
whose mate equals
\tilde{\Lambda}
is given by
\tilde{\Omega}_{(X,\alpha)}=\theta_{X}
.
Since
\theta
in not an isomorphism (see Proposition
5.2
),
\tilde{\Omega}
cannot implement an extension
\mathbb{T}^{\theta}
of
\mathbb{T}
. Thus the Corollary
3.7
is not applicable.

5.3.
Non-invertibility of the Galois map

Let

\mathrm{K}\colon\mathsf{Set}\to\mathsf{SemiGp}_{\mathbb{C}^{\theta}}
be the functor given by the formulas

\mathrm{K}(X)=(\mathrm{F}(X),\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{L}^{+}})=((\mathrm{L}^{+}(X),\mu^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}_{X}),\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{L}^{+}})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{K}(f)=\mathrm{F}(f).

It follows from Section
3.7
that the functor
\mathrm{K}
is well-defined and induces a coalgebra
(\mathrm{F},\gamma)
over
\mathbb{C}^{\theta}
by setting
\gamma_{X}=\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}
for all sets
X
.
The Galois map associated to
\mathrm{K}
and
\mathrm{F}\dashv\mathrm{U}
is given by setting for all semigroups
(X,\alpha)
,

\kappa_{(X,\alpha)}=\mathrm{L}^{+}(\alpha)\Delta_{X}^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}\colon(\mathrm{L}^{+}(X),\mu^{\mathbb{L}^{+}}_{X})\to(\mathrm{L}^{+}(X),\mathrm{L}^{+}(\alpha)\theta_{X}).

Explicitly,
\kappa_{(X,\alpha)}([x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}])=[x_{1}\cdots x_{n},x_{2}\cdots x_{n},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
In the following proposition we characterize semigroups
(X,\alpha)
with the property that
\kappa_{(X,\alpha)}
is bijective.

Proposition 5.3
.

For a nonempty semigroup
(X,\alpha)
, the semigroup morphism
\kappa_{(X,\alpha)}
is bijective if and only if
(X,\alpha)
is a group.

Proof.

It is straightforward to show that if
(X,\alpha)
is a
group, then

\kappa_{(X,\alpha)}
is
bijective.
Conversely, let
(X,\alpha)
be a semigroup such that
\kappa_{(X,\alpha)}
is bijective.
An elementary argument shows that the bijectivity of
\kappa_{(X,\alpha)}

is equivalent to the bijectivity of the map
[x,y]\mapsto[xy,y]
.
The latter in turn
is injective if and only if
the semigroup is right cancellative,
that is, if for all
x,y,z\in X
,
we have
xz=yz\Rightarrow x=y
.

First, since

[x,y]\mapsto[xy,y]
is
bijective, we have that for every

y\in X
there is
a unique element
e_{y}\in X

with
\kappa_{(X,\alpha)}([e_{y},y])=[y,y]
, that is, such that
e_{y}y=y
.
Note that
(e_{y}e_{y})y=e_{y}(e_{y}y)=e_{y}y=y
, so the uniqueness
implies
e_{y}e_{y}=e_{y}
.
Furthermore, the equality

(ye_{y})y=y(e_{y}y)=yy
combined with the fact that
X
is right cancellative
shows
ye_{y}=y
. In other words, each
y\in Y
has a unique idempotent
local unit
element
e_{y}\in X
with

e_{y}y=ye_{y}=y
.
Next, for all
y\in X
there is a unique

\bar{y}\in X

with

\kappa_{(X,\alpha)}([\bar{y},y])=[e_{y},y]
, that is, such that

\bar{y}y=e_{y}
.
It follows that
(y\bar{y})y=y(\bar{y}y)=ye_{y}=y=e_{y}y
, so
y\bar{y}=e_{y}
.
Thus
y\in X

has a unique local inverse

\bar{y}\in Y
such that
\bar{y}y=y\bar{y}=e_{y}
.

Now observe that

(e_{y}\bar{y})y=e_{y}(\bar{y}y)=e_{y}e_{y}=e_{y}=\bar{y}y
,
so

e_{y}\bar{y}=\bar{y}
