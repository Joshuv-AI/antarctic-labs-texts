Abstract
. In this paper, we study C ∗ C^{*} -envelopes of finite-dimensional operator algebras arising from constrained interpolation problems on the unit disc. In particular, we consider interpolation problems for the algebra H node ∞ H^{\infty}_{\text{node}} that consists of bounded analytic functions on the unit disk that satisfy f ⁡ ( 0 ) = f ⁡ ( λ ) f(0)=f(\lambda) for some 0 ≠ λ ∈ 𝔻 0\neq\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}} . We show that there exist choices of four interpolation nodes that exclude both 0 0 and λ \lambda , such that if I I is the ideal of functions that vanish at the interpolation nodes, then C e ∗ ​ ( H node ∞ / I ) C^{*}_{e}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I) is infinite-dimensional. This differs markedly from the behavior of the algebra corresponding to interpolation nodes that contain the constrained points studied in the literature. Additionally, we use the distance formula to provide a completely isometric embedding of C e ∗ ​ ( H node ∞ / I ) C^{*}_{e}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I) for any choice of n n interpolation nodes that do not contain the constrained points into M n ​ ( G n ​ c 2 ) M_{n}(G^{2}_{nc}) , where G n ​ c 2 G^{2}_{nc} is Brown’s noncommutative Grassmannian.



1.
Introduction

Let
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{D}=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid|z|<1\right\}
be a finite set of points and
\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{C}
. The Pick interpolation problem is the following: does there exist a holomorphic function
f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\overline{\mathbb{D}}
(called an
interpolating function
) such that
f(z_{i})=w_{i}
? The solution to this problem is well known - a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of an interpolating function is that the
Pick matrix

\Bigg[\frac{1-w_{i}\overline{w_{j}}}{1-z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}}\Bigg]_{i,j=1}^{n}

is positive semi-definite. This question was solved independently by Pick and Nevanlinna (See, for example,
[
32
]
). More generally, one can formulate the matricial Pick interpolation problem, where the
w_{i}
’s are replaced by
s\times r
matrices
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}\}
. Analogously, an interpolating function
f
with
f(z_{i})=W_{i}
and
\|f\|\leq 1
exists if and only if

\Bigg[\frac{I-W_{i}W_{j}^{*}}{1-z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}}\Bigg]\geq 0.

The operator algebraic approach to the Pick interpolation problem was pioneered by Koranyi and Sz-Nagy
[
38
]
, and a breakthrough was achieved by Sarason
[
36
]
. The key ingredient is the relationship between
H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
and
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
. Recall that
H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
is the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS for short) of all analytic functions on
{\mathbb{D}}
with square summable MacLaurin coefficients. The multiplier algebra of
H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
is
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
, the algebra of all bounded analytic function on
{\mathbb{D}}
. One encodes the fact that one can test the existence of solutions by the positivity of the corresponding Pick matrix for all sizes of matrices by saying that
H^{2}({\mathbb{D}})
is a complete Pick space. Complete Pick spaces are well-studied and important objects in function theory. A celebrated theorem of Agler and McCarthy
[
3
]
tells us that under some mild assumptions, every complete Pick space is equivalent as an RKHS to a quotient of the Drury-Arveson space. Namely, the RKHS of analytic functions on the unit ball of
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
(where we allow
d=\infty
) with the reproducing kernel
k(z,w)=\frac{1}{1-\langle z,w\rangle}
.

However, even in the case of the annulus, the Pick interpolation problem does not have such a simple and elegant solution. Abrahamse
[
1
]
has discovered that to solve the scalar Pick interpolation problem on a multiply-connected domain of genus
g
, one requires a family of kernels parametrized by the
g
-dimensional torus. In
[
6
]
, Ball has solved the matricial interpolation problem on multiply-connected domains. However, a sufficient family of kernels described by Ball is parametrized by
g
-tuples of unitaries of all sizes. In
[
2
]
, Agler formulated a general approach to the Pick interpolation problem based on families of kernels (see also
[
25
]
).

Another generalization is the constrained Pick interpolation problem, where the interpolating function is to satisfy an additional condition. In
[
15
]
, the authors solve an interpolation problem on
{\mathbb{D}}
with the additional constraint that the interpolating function
f
satisfies
f^{\prime}(0)=0
. The algebra of all such functions is denoted
H^{\infty}_{1}
. In this case, a family of kernel functions

\{k^{\alpha,\beta}\}_{|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1}

is required. Ragupathi
[
33
]
generalized this result for subalgebras of
H^{\infty}
with
predual factorization
. One main family of examples of subalgebras with predual factorization consists of subalgebras of
H^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
of the form

H_{B}=\mathbb{C}\cdot 1+BH^{\infty},

where
B
is a Blaschke product. The constrained interpolation problem is analogous to the interpolation problem in multiply-connected domains. To be more precise, if one considers multiply-connected domains, the corresponding algebro-geometric object is a curve of genus
g
. In the constrained case (with a finite Blaschke product), the algebro-geometric object is a singular rational curve. One of the first examples of the interplay between dilation theory and singular rational curves is the Neal parabola
[
17
]
. The Neal parabola is the planar curve cut out by the equation
y^{2}=x^{3}
. The map
z\mapsto(z^{2},z^{3})
gives a parametrization of the Neal parabola. It is rather straightforward that the algebra
H^{\infty}_{1}
corresponds to the Neal parabola. More generally,
H_{B}
corresponds to a parametrization of a rational curved ramified at the zeroes of
B
with multiplicities prescribed by
B
. In
[
7
]
, Ball, Bolotnikov, and ter Horst solved the matrix-valued constrained interpolation problem for
H^{\infty}_{1}
. An approach to the constrained interpolation problem through test functions was taken in
[
19
,
20
]
. In
[
13
]
, an approach was taken using the
{\mathbb{A}}_{1}(1)
property of dual operator algebras, which was subsequently developed in
[
16
]
with the introduction of the conductor ideal.

The minimality of the corresponding family of kernels can be studied using boundary representations. Arveson introduced boundary representations in
[
4
]
. Given an operator algebra
A
, one would like to find the smallest
C^{*}
-algebra generated by
A
. This
C^{*}
-algebra is called the
C^{*}
-envelope of
A
. Its existence was conjectured by Arveson and proved by Hamana
[
22
]
. In
[
29
]
, Paulsen and McCullough calculate the
C^{*}
-envelopes of two operator algebras - the first one is

H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{2})/{I}
with

I=\left\{f\in H^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{2})\mid f(0,0)=f\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},0\right)=f\left(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=0\right\}.

This operator algebra arises from an interpolation problem studied by Solazzo
[
37
]
. The second algebra is
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{A})/{I_{F}}
, which arises from an interpolation problem in finitely connected domains, studied by Abrahamse
[
1
]
. Here,
F\subset\mathbb{A}
is the finite set of interpolation nodes and
I_{F}
is the ideal of functions in
H^{\infty}(\mathbb{A})
that vanish on
F
. It turns out that
C^{*}_{e}(H^{\infty}(\mathbb{A})/I_{F})\cong M_{n}(C({\mathbb{T}}))
, where
3\leq n=|F|
and
{\mathbb{T}}=\partial{\mathbb{D}}
is the unit circle. Since, in the case of the annulus, the kernel family is parametrized by
{\mathbb{T}}
, we see that this family is minimal and solves the complete Pick interpolation problem on the annulus. In
[
15
]
, the authors show that if
0\in F
, then
C^{*}_{e}(H^{\infty}_{1}/I_{F})\cong M_{n+1}
. More generally, Ragupathi
[
33
]
calculated
C^{*}_{e}(H_{B}/I_{F})
in the special case that
F
contains some of the zeroes of
B
. The
C^{*}
-envelopes always turned out to be algebras of matrices.

In this paper, we study constrained interpolation algebras arising from nodal cubics. Namely, for
0\neq\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
, we consider the algebra

H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}=\{f\in H^{\infty}\mid f(0)=f(\lambda)\}=\mathbb{C}\cdot 1+\frac{z(z-\lambda)}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}H^{\infty}.

We describe the solution to the matricial Pick interpolation problem and the corresponding family of kernels in Section
2
. The family is analogous to the one obtained by Ball, Bolotnikov, and ter Horst
[
7
]
. The first main result of the paper is in Section
3
.

Theorem A

(Theorem
3.11
and Theorem
3.20
)
.

For
\lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, there exists
F=\{z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4}\}\subset{\mathbb{D}}
, such that
C^{*}_{e}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I_{F})
is infinite-dimensional.

To this end, we produce a family of pairwise unitarily inequivalent dilation maximal irreducible representations parametrized by the circle without a finite set of points.
This result shows that the behavior of the
C^{*}
-envelope when the set of interpolation nodes does not contain the ramification points is markedly different from the case studied in the literature so far. In Section
4
, we provide a candidate for the
C^{*}
-envelope of
C^{*}_{e}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I_{F})
for
F
that does not contain
0
and
\lambda
.

Theorem B

(Theorem
4.3
)
.

Let
0,\lambda\notin F\subset{\mathbb{D}}
be a finite set. Then, there exists a completely isometric embedding
\Gamma\colon H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I_{F}\to M_{n}(G_{2}^{nc})
.

Here
G^{2}_{nc}
is the noncommutative Grassmannian introduced by Brown
[
11
]
. The noncommutative Grassmannian
G^{2}_{nc}
is the noncommutative analog of the Riemann sphere. As we will see, the latter parametrizes the family of kernels for the scalar Pick problem.

2.
Pick family for
H^{\infty}_{\operatorname{node}}

The content of this section is inspired by the work of Ball, Bolotnikov, and ter Horst
[
7
]
. Though some proofs are similar to
[
7
]
, we include them for completeness. Our method differs from the approach in
[
7
]
, and we aim to obtain a distance formula.

For
k,\,l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we denote by
M_{k\times l}(L^{2})
the Hilbert space of
k\times l
matrices with entries in
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}})
, with the inner product given by

\left\langle F,G\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\operatorname{tr}[FG^{*}].

Similarly,
M_{k\times l}(L^{1})
is the Banach space of
k\times l
matrices with entries in
L^{1}
, with the norm given by

\|F\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\operatorname{tr}[(F^{*}F)^{\frac{1}{2}}],

and
M_{k\times l}(L^{\infty})
is the Banach space of
k\times l
matrices with entries in
L^{\infty}
and the sup norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
.
We will also need the Hilbert space
M_{k\times l}(H^{2})
and the Banach spaces
M_{k\times l}(H^{1})
and
M_{k\times l}(H^{\infty})
, which are the subspaces of analytic functions in
M_{k\times l}(L^{2})
,
M_{k\times l}(L^{1})
and
M_{k\times l}(L^{\infty})
, respectively.
We recall that the Riesz representation theorem identifies
M_{k\times l}(L^{1})^{*}
with
M_{l\times k}(L^{\infty})
via

\varphi_{F}(G)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\operatorname{tr}[FG],

for
F\in M_{k\times l}(L^{1})
and
G\in M_{l\times k}(L^{\infty})
. Moreover, the preannihilator of
M_{k\times l}(H^{\infty})
is given by
M_{l\times k}(zH^{1})
.

For a fixed
0\neq\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
, consider the norm-closed, co-dimension
1
subalgebra of
H^{\infty}
defined by

H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}=\{f\in H^{\infty}\mid f(0)=f(\lambda)\}={\mathbb{C}}\cdot 1+B_{\lambda}H^{\infty},

where
B_{\lambda}(z)=z\frac{z-\lambda}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}
is the Blaschke product vanishing at
0
and
\lambda
.
Moreover, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
let
k_{w}(z)=\frac{1}{1-z\overline{w}}
be the function in
H^{2}
satisfying
\left\langle f,k_{w}\right\rangle=f(w)
for all
f\in H^{2}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
separates points in
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0,\lambda\}
.

Proof.

Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0,\lambda\}
. By Lagrange interpolation, there are polynomials
\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset H^{\infty}
satisfying

f_{i}(z_{j})=\delta_{i,j},\,f_{i}(0)=f_{i}(\lambda)=0.

In particular,
f_{i}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
such that
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
and define

H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}=\{f\in H^{2}\mid\exists c\in{\mathbb{C}},\,f(0)=c\cdot\alpha,\,f(\lambda)=c\cdot(\alpha+f_{\lambda}(\lambda)\beta)\}.

Here
f_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{\sqrt{1-|\lambda|^{2}}}{|\lambda|}(k_{\lambda}(z)-1)
. Then,
H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with kernel function

k^{\alpha,\beta}(z,w)=\overline{(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}(w))}(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}(z))+\frac{B_{\lambda}(z)\overline{B_{\lambda}(w)}}{1-z\overline{w}}.

Proof.

Note that
f_{\lambda}(\lambda)=\frac{|\lambda|}{\sqrt{1-|\lambda|^{2}}}
. Hence, for every
g\in H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
,

\langle g,1\rangle=c\alpha,\,\langle g,f_{\lambda}\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{1-|\lambda|^{2}}}{|\lambda|}(g(\lambda)-g(0))=c\beta.

In other words,
H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}=\operatorname{span}\{\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}(z)\}\oplus B_{\lambda}H^{2}
, where the sum is direct since
B_{\lambda}H^{2}
is orthogonal to
\operatorname{span}\{1,f_{\lambda}\}=\operatorname{span}\{1,k_{\lambda}\}
, as
B_{\lambda}(0)=B_{\lambda}(\lambda)=0
.
Therefore,
H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}\subset H^{2}
is a closed subspace. Hence,
H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
is a RKHS with kernel functions
P_{H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}}k_{w}
. Moreover,

k^{\alpha,\beta}(z,w)=\langle P_{H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}}k_{w},P_{H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}}k_{z}\rangle=\langle k_{w},\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}\rangle(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}(z))+\langle M_{B_{\lambda}}M_{B_{\lambda}}^{*}k_{w},k_{z}\rangle=\\
\overline{(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}(w))}(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}(z))+\frac{B_{\lambda}(z)\overline{B_{\lambda}(w)}}{1-z\bar{w}}.

∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For every
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
,
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}\subseteq\operatorname{Mult}(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})
. Moreover, if
\alpha\neq 0,-f_{\lambda}(\lambda)\beta
, then we have equality. As a result, for every
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
and every
f\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
,
M_{f}^{*}k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}=\overline{f(z_{i})}k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}.

Proof.

Fix arbitrary
(\alpha,\beta)
on the unit sphere. Let
f\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
and
f(0)=f(\lambda)=\tilde{c}
. Then, for every
g\in H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
,

(fg)(0)=cf(0)\alpha=c\tilde{c}\alpha,\,(fg)(\lambda)=c\tilde{c}(\alpha+f_{\lambda}(\lambda)\beta).

That is
fg\in H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
, hence
f\in\operatorname{Mult}(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})
. The last part then follows automatically by properties of
\operatorname{Mult}(\mathcal{H})
for a general RKHS.

On the other hand, for every
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0,\lambda\}
,

k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{0}}(z_{0})=\|k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{0}}\|^{2}=|\alpha+f_{\lambda}(z_{0})\beta|^{2}+\frac{|B_{\lambda}(z_{0})|^{2}}{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}>0.

Let
f\in\operatorname{Mult}(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})
. Since
f
is a multiplier,
\underset{z\in{\mathbb{D}}}{\sup}|f(z)|\leq\|M_{f}\|_{\text{mult}}<\infty
.
Let
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0,\lambda\}
. Since
k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{0}}(z_{0})>0
and is analytic,
\frac{1}{k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{0}}(z)}
is analytic in a neighborhood of
z_{0}
. Moreover,
fk_{z_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}\in H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
, and thus,
f=\frac{fk_{z_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}}{k_{z_{0}}^{\alpha,\beta}}
is analytic in a neighborhood of
z_{0}
. By Riemann’s removable singularity theorem,
f
is analytic in all of
{\mathbb{D}}
. Therefore,
f\in H^{\infty}
. Assume, in addition, that
\alpha\neq 0,-f_{\lambda}(\lambda)\beta
. Then, let
p\in H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
be a polynomial satisfying

p(0)=\alpha,\,p(\lambda)=\alpha+f_{\lambda}(\lambda)\beta.

By assumption,

f(0)p(0)=c\cdot\alpha,\,f(\lambda)p(\lambda)=c\cdot(\alpha+f_{\lambda}(\lambda)\beta).

Therefore,
f(0)=f(\lambda)=c
, that is
f\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
.

∎

For fixed
k,\,l^{\prime}\geq l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
M_{k}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}})
denote the space of
k\times k
matrices with entries in
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
and define

{\mathbb{G}}(l^{\prime}\times l)=\{\alpha,\beta\in M_{l^{\prime}\times l}({\mathbb{C}})\mid\alpha\alpha^{*}+\beta\beta^{*}=I_{l}\}.

Furthermore, for
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{G}}(l^{\prime}\times l)
, define

(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k,l,l^{\prime}}=\{F\in M_{k\times l}(H^{2})\mid\exists M\in M_{k\times l^{\prime}}({\mathbb{C}})\mid F(0)=M\alpha,\,F(\lambda)=M(\alpha+f_{\lambda}(\lambda)\beta)\}.

To simplify notations, we will shorten this by
(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k}
, if
k=l=l^{\prime}
and if
k=l=l^{\prime}=1
, we will simply write
H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}
.
By an argument similar to the one in the proof of Lemma
2.2
,

(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k,l,l^{\prime}}=M_{k\times l^{\prime}}({\mathbb{C}})(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}(z))\oplus B_{\lambda}M_{k\times l}(H^{2}).

Proposition 2.4
.

(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k,l,l^{\prime}}
is a matrix-valued RKHS with kernel function

k^{\alpha,\beta}(z,w)=(\alpha^{*}+\overline{f_{\lambda}(w)}\beta^{*})(\alpha+f_{\lambda}(z)\beta)+\frac{B_{\lambda}(z)\overline{B_{\lambda}(w)}}{1-z\overline{w}}I_{l}.

To be more precise, for every
f\in(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k,l,l^{\prime}}
, every
w\in{\mathbb{D}}
, and every
X\in M_{k\times l}({\mathbb{C}})
,

\operatorname{tr}(f(w)X^{*})=\langle f,Xk^{\alpha,\beta}_{w}\rangle.

Moreover, for every
F\in M_{k}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}})
,

M_{F}^{*}Xk^{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,w)=F(w)^{*}Xk^{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,w).

Proof.

Let
f\in(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k,l,l^{\prime}}
and write
f=U(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda})+B_{\lambda}\tilde{f}
for some
\tilde{f}\in M_{k\times l}(H^{2})
. Then, for every

X\in M_{k\times l}({\mathbb{C}})
,

\displaystyle\left\langle f,Xk^{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,w)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle U(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda})+B_{\lambda}\tilde{f},Xk^{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,w)\right\rangle=

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left\langle U(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}),X(\alpha^{*}+\beta^{*}\overline{f_{\lambda}(w)})(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda})\right\rangle}_{(1)}+\underbrace{\left\langle B_{\lambda}\tilde{f},\overline{B_{\lambda}(w)}X{B_{\lambda}k(\cdot,w)}\right\rangle}_{(2)}.

Here, we write
k(z,w)=\frac{1}{1-z\bar{w}}
. We treat each summand separately.

\displaystyle\left\langle U(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}),X(\alpha^{*}+\beta^{*}\overline{f_{\lambda}(w)})(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda})\right\rangle=

\displaystyle=\left\langle U\alpha,X(\alpha^{*}+\beta^{*}\overline{f_{\lambda}}(w))\alpha\right\rangle+\left\langle U\beta f_{\lambda},X(\alpha^{*}+\beta^{*}\overline{f_{\lambda}(w)})\beta f_{\lambda}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left[U\alpha\alpha^{*}(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}(w))X^{*}\right]+\operatorname{tr}\left[U\beta\beta^{*}(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}(w))X^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left[U(\alpha+\beta f_{\lambda}(w))X^{*}\right].

For the second summand, we observe that the operator of multiplication by
B_{\lambda}
is an isometry. Therefore,

\left\langle B_{\lambda}\tilde{f},X\frac{B_{\lambda}\overline{B_{\lambda}(w)}}{1-z\overline{w}}\right\rangle=B_{\lambda}(w)\left\langle\tilde{f},X\frac{1}{1-z\overline{w}}\right\rangle=B_{\lambda}(w)\operatorname{tr}(\tilde{f}(w)X^{*}).

Combining the two, we get our result. For the last claim of the lemma, note that for every
G\in(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k,l,l^{\prime}}
,

\left\langle G,M_{F}^{*}Xk^{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,w)\right\rangle=\left\langle FG,Xk^{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,w)\right\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(X^{*}F(w)G(w))=\operatorname{tr}((F(w)^{*}X)^{*}G(w))=

=\left\langle G(w),F(w)^{*}Xk^{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,w)\right\rangle=\left\langle G,F(w)^{*}Xk^{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,w)\right\rangle.

∎

Now we are ready to present the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 2.5
.

For a fixed
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{D}}
and
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}\}\in M_{k}({\mathbb{C}})
. There exists a function
F\in M_{k}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}})
satisfying
\|F\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
F(z_{i})=W_{i}
, for all
1\leq i\leq n

if and only if for every
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{G}}(k\times k)
and for every

(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in(M_{k}({\mathbb{C}}))^{n}
,

(2.1)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\operatorname{tr}[X_{j}k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{i},z_{j})X_{i}^{*}-W_{j}^{*}X_{j}k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{i},z_{j})X_{i}^{*}W_{i}]\geq 0.

Before proving this theorem, we need a few general results about
(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k,l,l^{\prime}}
. To this end, fix some points
F=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{D}}
, let
B_{F}
be the Blaschke product with simple zeroes at
F
and define the ideal

I=\{f\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}\mid\forall 1\leq i\leq n,\,f(z_{i})=0\}=H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}\cap B_{F}H^{\infty}.

Lemma 2.6
.

For a fixed
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
set

\mathcal{I}=\{G\in M_{k}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}})\mid\forall 1\leq i\leq n\,\ G(z_{i})=0\}=M_{k}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}})\cap B_{F}M_{k}(H^{\infty}).

Then,

\mathcal{I}_{\perp}=M_{k}(I)_{\perp}\subset\overline{\varphi_{\lambda}B_{F}}M_{k}(H^{1}),

with
\varphi_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{z-\lambda}{1-\overline{\lambda}z}=\frac{B_{\lambda}(z)}{z}
.

Proof.

It is known that
(H^{\infty})_{\perp}=H^{1}_{0}=zH^{1}
. Since
B_{\lambda}B_{F}H^{\infty}\subset I
and since taking pre-annihilators is inclusion reversing,

I_{\perp}\subset(B_{\lambda}B_{F}H^{\infty})_{\perp}=\overline{B_{\lambda}B_{F}}zH^{1}=\overline{\varphi_{\lambda}B_{F}}H^{1}.

Therefore,

\mathcal{I}_{\perp}=M_{k}(I)_{\perp}=M_{k}(I_{\perp})\subset\overline{\varphi_{\lambda}B_{F}}M_{k}(H^{1}).

∎

Lemma 2.7
.

The set

S=\{h\in\mathcal{I}_{\perp}\mid\exists s\in{\mathbb{D}},\,\det(h(s))\neq 0\}\subset\mathcal{I}_{\perp}

is dense in
\mathcal{I}_{\perp}
.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
f\in\mathcal{I}_{\perp}
be such that for every
s
,
\det(f(s))=0
. Note that

f(\frac{1}{2})
has a finite spectrum. Hence there exists
0<\delta
such that
\frac{\delta}{2}\not\in\sigma(f(\frac{1}{2}))
and
\delta\cdot k<\varepsilon
. Now,
f-\delta zI_{k}
is invertible at
\frac{1}{2}
and
f-\delta zI_{k}\in S
as
f,\,\delta zI_{k}\in\mathcal{I}_{\perp}
. Moreover,

\|f-(f-\delta zI_{k})\|_{1}=\delta\cdot\|I_{k}\|_{1}=\delta\cdot k<\varepsilon.

∎

Recall from
[
31
]
, that a function
f\in M_{k\times\ell}(H^{\infty})
is called
inner
if
f(e^{i\theta})
is an isometry almost everywhere on
{\mathbb{T}}
(in particular,
\ell\leq k
). Additionally, we will say that
g\in M_{s\times t}(H^{2})
is
right outer
(respectively,
left outer
), if
\overline{M_{s}(H^{\infty})g}=M_{s\times t}(H^{2})
(respectively,
\overline{gM_{t}(H^{\infty})}=M_{s\times t}(H^{2})
.) More generally, by
[
34
, Definition B in Section 5.2]
, a holomorphic operator-valued function
g
is outer, if there exists a bounded analytic (in fact, outer) function
\varphi
, such that
\varphi g
is bounded and outer. Though the definition in
[
34
]
of an outer function slightly differs from ours, they boil down to the same thing, if one restricts attention to the subspace they call
M_{out}
. Moreover, by
[
34
, Theorem A in Section 5.4]
, the definition is independent of
\varphi
and by
[
34
, Theorem B in Section 5.4]
, an outer function has pointwise dense range. Since we only consider finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, the latter is just surjectivity.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
g\in M_{k}(H^{\infty})
, such that
g^{T}
is left outer. Then,
\det(g(z))\neq 0
for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
. A similar result holds for the Nevanlinna class outer functions.

Proof.

Let
K(z,w)=\frac{1}{1-z\overline{w}}I
be matricial Szego kernel.
As
g^{T}
is left outer, there exists a sequence
\{f_{n}\}\subset M_{k}(H^{\infty})
, such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}g^{T}f_{n}=I_{k}
in
M_{k}(H^{2})
. Hence, for every
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
and
1\leq i,j\leq k
,

(g^{T}(z)f_{n}(z))_{i,j}=\left\langle g^{T}(z)f_{n}(z),E_{i,j}\right\rangle=\left\langle g^{T}f_{n},E_{i,j}K(\cdot,z)\right\rangle\to\left\langle I_{k},E_{i,j}K(\cdot,z)\right\rangle=\left\langle I_{k},E_{i,j}\right\rangle=(I_{k})_{i,j},

i.e.,
g^{T}(z)f_{n}(z)\to I_{k}
entry-wise. By continuity of the determinant,

\det(g^{T}(z)f_{n}(z))\to\det(I_{k})=1.

Therefore,
\det(g^{T}(z))=\det(g(z))\neq 0
. The second claim follows immediately by multiplying by a non-vanishing scalar-valued bounded function that will make
g
bounded.
∎

The following factorization result in
M_{k}(H^{1})
is, probably, well-known to experts. However, since we do not have a good reference, we include the proof here.

Lemma 2.9
.

Every function
h\in M_{k}(H^{1})
can be factored as

h=g\cdot f

with
f\in M_{l\times k}(H^{2})
,
g\in M_{k\times l}(H^{2})
,
l\leq k
,
g^{T}
left outer, and
\|g\|_{2}^{2}=\|f\|^{2}_{2}=\|h\|_{1}
.

Proof.

By
[
34
, Theorem in Section 5.7]
,
h^{T}
can be
factored as

h^{T}=h_{i}^{T}h_{o}^{T}
, with
h_{i}^{T}
a
k\times l^{\prime}
inner function and
h_{o}^{T}\in M_{l^{\prime}\times k}(H^{1})
(for some
l^{\prime}\leq k
) a left outer function. Note that, though the theorem of Rosenblum and Rovnyak yields a factorization in the Nevanlinna class, by
[
34
, Theorem A in Section 4.7]
, the Hardy classes are the intersection of the Nevanlinna class with the corresponding
L^{p}
space. Since the inner is almost everywhere an isometry on the unit circle, we get that the outer function is in the corresponding Hardy class. Next, by
[
36
, Theorem 4]
we can factor
h_{o}
as

h_{o}=g\tilde{f}

with
g\in M_{k\times l}(H^{2})
,
\tilde{f}\in M_{l\times l^{\prime}}(H^{2})
and

\tilde{f}^{*}\tilde{f}=(h_{o}^{*}h_{o})^{\frac{1}{2}},\,g^{*}g=\tilde{f}\tilde{f}^{*}
on
\mathbb{T}
. Note that
[
36
, Theorem 4]
is for the square case. Since
h_{o}\in M_{k\times l^{\prime}}(H^{1})
, we can complete it with zeroes to a square matrix. Factor it and then take from
\tilde{f}
only the first
l^{\prime}
columns. Moreover, we can take the inner-outer factorization of
g^{t}=vg^{\prime}
and replace
\tilde{f}
by
v^{t}\tilde{f}
. Note that since
v
is almost everywhere an isometry on
\mathbb{T}
, we have that for almost every
z\in\mathbb{T}
,
v(z)^{t}\tilde{f}(z)(v(z)^{t}(z)\tilde{f}(z))^{*}=(g^{\prime}(z))^{t*}g^{\prime}(z)^{t}
. Namely, the same condition holds. Moreover, by the definition of the norms in
H^{2}
, we have that
\|g\|_{2}^{2}=\|g^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}
. Hence, if we set
f=\tilde{f}h_{i}
, the decomposition

h=h_{o}h_{i}=g\tilde{f}h_{i}=g\cdot f

satisfies the desired properties.
∎

Theorem 2.10
.

For every
h\in S
, there exists some
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{G}(k\times k)
, such that

h=gf^{*}

with
g\in(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k}
,
f\in M_{k}(L^{2})\ominus((H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k}\cap B_{F}M_{k}(H^{2}))
,
g^{T}
left outer and
\|g\|^{2}_{2}=\|f\|^{2}_{2}=\|h\|_{1}
.

Proof.

Write
h=\overline{\varphi_{\lambda}(z)B_{F}}h^{\prime}
with
h^{\prime}\in M_{k}(H^{1})
. By Lemma
2.9
,
h^{\prime}
can be factored as
gf_{0}
with
f_{0},\,g\in M_{k}(H^{2})
,
g^{T}
outer and
\|g\|_{2}^{2}=\|f_{0}\|_{2}^{2}=\|h\|_{1}
. Note that all matrices are square, since
h\in S
and, therefore,
\det h
is not identically zero.
We first factor
h
as

h=\overline{\varphi_{\lambda}B_{F}}gf_{0}.

Taking
f=\varphi_{\lambda}(z)B_{F}f_{0}^{*}
and observing that
\|f\|_{2}^{2}=\|f_{0}\|_{2}^{2}
(as both
\varphi_{\lambda}
and
B_{F}
are inner functions and
\|f_{0}\|^{2}_{2}=\|f_{0}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

Now let

\tilde{\alpha}=I_{k},\,\tilde{\beta}=\frac{1}{f_{\lambda}(\lambda)}\left(g(0)^{-1}g(\lambda)-I_{k}\right),\,\tilde{U}=g(0)

and define

\alpha=\left(I_{k}+\tilde{\beta}\tilde{\beta}^{*}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},\,\beta=\left(I_{k}+\tilde{\beta}\tilde{\beta}^{*}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{\beta},\,U=\tilde{U}\left(I_{k}+\tilde{\beta}\tilde{\beta}^{*}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Note that
U,\,\alpha,\,\alpha+f_{\lambda}\beta
are invertible (as
h\in S
and
g^{t}
is left outer) and that
\alpha\alpha^{*}+\beta\beta^{*}=I_{k}
and
U\alpha=g(0),\,U\left(\alpha+f_{\lambda}(\lambda)\beta\right)=g(\lambda)
, thus
g\in(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k}
. Lastly,
to prove
f
satisfies the desired property, we first observe that for every
\phi\in\mathcal{I}
,

\left\langle\phi g,f\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\phi gf^{*}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\phi h=0,

that is
f\in(\mathcal{I}g)^{\perp}
. Therefore, it is enough to prove that
\overline{\mathcal{I}g}=(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k}\cap B_{F}M_{k}(H^{2})

\mathcal{I}g\subset(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k}\cap B_{F}M_{k}(H^{2}).

For the other direction, let
\psi\in(H^{2}_{\alpha,\beta})_{k}\cap B_{F}M_{k}(H^{2})

\psi(\lambda)=V(\alpha+f_{\lambda}(\lambda)\beta),\,\psi(0)=V\alpha.

As
g^{T}
is left-outer,
g
is right-outer, and therefore
\overline{M_{k}(H^{\infty})g}=M_{k}(H^{2})
. So there exists a sequence
\{\psi_{m}\}_{m}\subset M_{k}(H^{\infty})

\psi_{m}=\psi_{m}(0)+\left(\psi_{m}(\lambda)-\psi_{m}(0)\right)\frac{f_{\lambda}}{f_{\lambda}(\lambda)}+B_{\lambda}\tilde{\psi}_{m},

with

.
Therefore,

\varphi_{m}=\psi_{m}-\left(\psi_{m}(\lambda)-\psi_{m}(0)\right)\frac{f_{\lambda}}{f_{\lambda}(\lambda)}\in M_{k}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}).

Moreover,

\|\varphi_{m}g-\psi\|\leq\|\psi_{m}g-\psi\|+\left\|\frac{f_{\lambda}}{f_{\lambda}(\lambda)}\right\|\left\|\psi_{m}(\lambda)-\psi_{m}(0)\right\|\to 0,

where in the last step we used that

\lim_{m\to\infty}(\psi_{m}(\lambda)-\psi_{m}(0))=\psi(\lambda)g(\lambda)^{-1}-\psi(0)g(0)^{-1}=VU^{-1}-VU^{-1}=0.

Lastly, we can always assume that at most one of

and

\varphi_{m}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{m}(z_{i})P_{i}

\varphi_{m}(z_{i})g(z_{i})\to\psi(z_{i})=0

\|(\varphi_{m}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{m}(z_{i})P_{i})g-\psi\|\leq\|\varphi_{m}g-\psi\|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\varphi_{m}(z_{i})\|\|P_{i}g\|\to 0.

(\varphi_{m}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{m}(z_{i})P_{i})g\in\mathcal{I}g
, concluding the proof.

F\in M_{r}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}),\,G\in\mathcal{I}
,

\|\varphi(F)\|=\|\varphi(F+G)\|\leq\|F+G\|.

Hence,

\|P_{(\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}})_{r,m}}M_{F}|_{(\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}})_{r,m}}\|\leq\inf_{G\in\mathcal{I}}\|F+G\|=\|F+I\|.

As this is independent of
m
and
(\alpha,\beta)
,

\sup_{m}\sup\left\{\left\|P_{(\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}})_{r,m}}M_{F}|_{(\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}})_{r,m}}\right\|\mid(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{G}(m\times m)\right\}\leq\|F+I\|,

as desired.
∎

3.
Boundary representations from scalar interpolation

We begin this section with some results on representations of
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
for general
0\neq\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
and
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0,\lambda\}
. Here
I
is again the ideal of functions that vanish on the
z_{i}
. By
[
9
, Proposition 2.3.4]
,
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
is an operator algebra when equipped with the quotient operator space structure. By a representation of an operator algebra on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
, we mean a unital completely contractive homorphism to
B({\mathcal{H}})
. The spectral structure of
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
is quite simple and is described in the following proposition.

Lemma 3.1
.

The algebra
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
is generated by
h+I
, with
h\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
being the degree
n+1
polynomial satisfying
h(z_{i})=z_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
and
h(0)=h(\lambda)=0
. The spectrum of
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
consists of the characters of evaluation at
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
. Therefore, for every finite-dimensional representation
\varphi\colon H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I\to B({\mathcal{H}})
and every
f\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
,
\varphi(f+I)=f(\varphi(h+I))
is diagonalizable.

Proof.

Let
f\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
. There exists a polynomial
p(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}a_{k}z^{k}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
satisfying

p(z_{i})=f(z_{i})
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Define
\tilde{f}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}a_{k}h^{k}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
. Then,

\tilde{f}(z_{i})=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}a_{k}h^{k}(z_{i})=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}a_{k}z_{i}^{k}=p(z_{i})=f(z_{i}).

So
f+I=\tilde{f}+I=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}a_{k}h^{k}+I=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}a_{k}(h+I)^{k}
. We conclude that every character of
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
is determined on
h
. It is also easy to see that for every
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}
,
h-\alpha+I
is invertible in
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
. The claim on the spectrum now follows.

For the claim that for every
f\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
,
\varphi(f+I)=f(\varphi(h+I))
, we use a density argument. Firstly, since
I
is weak
∗
closed, the quotient map

\pi\colon H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}\to H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I

is weak
∗
continuous. Therefore,
\varphi\circ\pi
is weak
∗
continuous. Since polynomials are weak
∗
dense, it is enough to prove it for polynomials. However, this follows from the fact that
\varphi
is an algebra homomorphism.
Now consider
p(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z-z_{i})\cdot z(z-\lambda)\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
. Then,
p+I=0+I
. Since
\varphi
is a representation,
\varphi(p+I)=0
. On the other hand,
p+I=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(h-z_{i})\cdot h(h-\lambda)+I
, and since
\varphi
is a representation,

\varphi(p+I)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\varphi(h+I)-z_{i}I_{d}\right)\varphi(h+I)\left(\varphi(h+I)-\lambda I_{d}\right)=0.

Therefore, the minimal polynomial of
\varphi(h+I)
factors into different linear factors, i.e.,
\varphi(h+I)
is diagonalizable. The general claim follows.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{G}}(1\times 1)
, the map

\rho_{\alpha,\beta}\colon H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I\to B(\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}})

given by
f+I\to P_{\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}}M_{f}|_{\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}}
is an operator algebra representation.

Proof.

The claim follows at once from the Sarason Lemma
[
35
, Lemma 0]
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

For every
(\alpha,\beta)
on the unit sphere of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, let
T=\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(h+I)
. Then, the minimal polynomial of
T
is precisely,
p(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z-z_{i})
. This is since
T^{*}
is an operator on
n
dimensional space with eigenvalues
\overline{z_{1}},\ldots,\overline{z_{n}}
.

Now we need to recall some notations and terminology from the theory of
C^{*}
-covers and boundary representations of operator algebras. Recall, that a
C^{*}
-cover of an operator algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is a
C^{*}
-algebra
B
together with a completely isometric homomorphisms
\iota\colon{\mathcal{A}}\to B
, such that
B=C^{*}(\iota({\mathcal{A}}))
. For every operator algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
, there are two extremal
C^{*}
-covers. Let
C^{*}_{\max}({\mathcal{A}})
denote its maximal
C^{*}
-cover. The maximal
C^{*}
-cover for an operator system was constructed by Kirchberg and Wassermann
[
26
]
. The maximal
C^{*}
-cover of an operator algebra is described in
[
9
, Proposition 2.4.2]
. The maximality of this
C^{*}
-cover is encoded in its universal property. For every
C^{*}
-algebra
B
and a completely contractive homomorphism
\varphi\colon{\mathcal{A}}\to B
, there exists a
*
-homomorphism
\pi\colon C^{*}_{max}({\mathcal{A}})\to B
extending
\varphi
. The minimal
C^{*}
-cover of
{\mathcal{A}}
is the
C^{*}
-envelope that we will denote by
C^{*}_{e}({\mathcal{A}})
. The existence of the
C^{*}
-envelope was conjectured by Arveson
[
4
]
and proved by Hamana
[
22
]
. The
C^{*}
-envelope of
{\mathcal{A}}
has the universal property that for every
C^{*}
-cover
\iota\colon{\mathcal{A}}\to B
, there exists a surjective homomorphism
\pi\colon B\to C^{*}_{e}({\mathcal{A}})
, such that
\pi\circ\iota=\iota_{e}
, where
\iota_{e}
stands for the completely isometric embedding of
{\mathcal{A}}
in
C^{*}_{e}({\mathcal{A}})
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{K}
be Hilbert spaces,
\mathcal{A}
an operator algebra and

\psi_{1}\colon\mathcal{A}\to B(\mathcal{H}),\,\psi_{2}\colon\mathcal{A}\to B(\mathcal{K})

be two representations. We say that
\psi_{2}
is
dilation
of
\psi_{1}
, if
P_{\mathcal{H}}\psi_{2}|_{\mathcal{H}}=\psi_{1}
. Moreover,
\psi_{1}
is called
dilation maximal
if every dilation of
\psi_{1}
is trivial, i.e., if
\psi_{2}
dilates
\psi_{1}
, then
\psi_{2}=\psi_{1}\oplus\psi
for some representation
\psi
.

It follows from Sarason’s lemma that
{\mathcal{H}}
is semi-invariant for
\psi_{2}(A)
. The following definition is due to Arveson
[
4
]
.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an operator algebra. A representation

\pi\colon C^{*}(\mathcal{S})\to B(\mathcal{H})

is said to have the unique extension property if for every unital completely positive map (ucp for short)
\varphi\colon C^{*}(\mathcal{S})\to B(\mathcal{H})
, such that
\pi|_{\mathcal{A}}=\varphi|_{\mathcal{A}}
, we have that
\pi=\varphi
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
(B,\iota)
be a
C^{*}
-cover of an operator algebra
\mathcal{A}
. A representation

\psi\colon B\to B(\mathcal{H})

is said to be a boundary representation, if it is irreducible and has the unique extension property.

It follows from results of Muhly and Solel
[
30
]
(see also
[
18
]
and
[
5
]
) that
\pi
has the unique extension property if and only if
\pi|_{{\mathcal{A}}}
is dilation maximal. The following theorem is a particular case of
[
14
, Proposition 6.5.1]
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an operator algebra and
\psi\colon C^{*}_{\max}(\mathcal{A})\to B(\mathcal{H})
be a boundary representation of
\mathcal{A}
. Let
\pi\colon C^{*}_{\max}(\mathcal{A})\to C^{*}_{e}(\mathcal{A})
be the surjective
*
-homomorphism guaranteed by the universal properties. Then, there is a representation

\sigma\colon C^{*}_{e}(\mathcal{A})\to B(\mathcal{H})

such that
\sigma\circ\pi=\psi
(that is,
\psi
factors through
C^{*}_{e}(\mathcal{A})
).

Before presenting the main result of this section, we need two technical lemmas.

Lemma 3.8
.

Fix
0\neq\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
and let
\omega\in\mathbb{D}
.

(1)

If there exist
\zeta\neq\omega\in{\mathbb{D}}
, such that

B_{\lambda}(\zeta)=B_{\lambda}(\omega)
,
then

B_{\omega,\zeta}(z):=\frac{z-\zeta}{1-\overline{\zeta}z}\cdot\frac{z-\omega}{1-\overline{\omega}z}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}.

(2)

If
B_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\omega)=0
, then

B_{\omega}^{2}(z)=\left(\frac{z-\omega}{1-\overline{\omega}z}\right)^{2}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{D})
be such that
\varphi\left(B_{\lambda}(\omega)\right)=0
.

(1)

In the first case,

\varphi\circ B_{\lambda}
is a Blaschke product with

2
zeroes that vanishes at
\zeta
and
\omega
, hence

\varphi\circ B_{\lambda}=e^{i\theta}\frac{z-\zeta}{1-\overline{\zeta}z}\cdot\frac{z-\omega}{1-\overline{\omega}z}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}.

(2)

In the second case, by the chain rule

\left(\varphi\circ B_{\lambda}\right)^{\prime}(\omega)=\varphi^{\prime}\left(B_{\lambda}(\omega)\right)B^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\omega)=0,

i.e.,
\varphi\circ B_{\lambda}
is a Blaschke product with at most
2
zeroes with a double zero at
\omega
. Therefore

\varphi\circ B_{\lambda}(z)=e^{i\theta}B_{\omega}^{2}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}.

∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
0\neq\lambda\in{\mathbb{D}}
. Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0,\lambda\}
be such that
B_{\lambda}(z_{1})=B_{\lambda}(z_{4})
and
B_{\lambda}(z_{2})=B_{\lambda}(z_{3})
. Assume, furthermore, that there exists
\omega\in{\mathbb{D}}
such that
B_{z_{1},z_{2},\omega}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
.
Then, there exists some
\zeta\in\mathbb{D}
such that

B_{z_{3},z_{4},\zeta}(z)=\frac{z-z_{3}}{1-\overline{z_{3}}z}\cdot\frac{z-z_{4}}{1-\overline{z_{4}}z}\cdot\frac{z-\zeta}{1-\overline{\zeta}z}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}.

Moreover, if
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, then
\omega,\,\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.8
and our assumption,

B_{z_{1},z_{4}},\,B_{z_{2},z_{3}}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}.

If
B_{\lambda}^{\prime}(\omega)=0
, then
\left(\frac{z-\omega}{1-\overline{\omega}z}\right)^{2}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
. However,

B_{z_{3},z_{4},\omega}(0)=\frac{B_{z_{1},z_{2}}(0)\cdot B_{z_{3},z_{4}}(0)\cdot B_{\omega}^{2}(0)}{B_{z_{1},z_{2},\omega}(0)}=\frac{B_{z_{1},z_{2}}(\lambda)\cdot B_{z_{3},z_{4}}(\lambda)\cdot B_{\omega}^{2}(\lambda)}{B_{z_{1},z_{2},\omega}(\lambda)}=B_{z_{3},z_{4},\omega}(\lambda).

Here we used the fact that

B_{z_{1},z_{2}}\cdot B_{z_{3},z_{4}}=B_{z_{1},z_{4}}\cdot B_{z_{2},z_{3}}.

Otherwise, since
B_{\lambda}
is
2
to
1
, there exists some
\zeta\neq\omega
such that
B_{\lambda}(\zeta)=B_{\lambda}(\omega)
. By Lemma
3.8
,
B_{\zeta,\omega}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
. By similar arguments,

B_{z_{3},z_{4},\zeta}(0)=\frac{B_{z_{1},z_{2}}(0)\cdot B_{z_{3},z_{4}}(0)\cdot B_{\zeta,\omega}(0)}{B_{z_{1},z_{2},\omega}(0)}=\frac{B_{z_{1},z_{2}}(\lambda)\cdot B_{z_{3},z_{4}}(\lambda)\cdot B_{\zeta,\omega}(\lambda)}{B_{z_{1},z_{2},\omega}(\lambda)}=B_{z_{3},z_{4},\zeta}(\lambda).

Lastly, assume
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4},\lambda
are real. By plugging
0
and
\lambda
into
B_{z_{1},z_{2},\omega}
we obtain the equality

-z_{1}z_{2}\omega=\frac{\lambda-z_{1}}{1-z_{1}z}\cdot\frac{\lambda-z_{2}}{1-z_{2}z}\cdot\frac{\lambda-\omega}{1-\overline{\omega}\lambda}.

Therefore,

\omega\cdot\frac{1-\overline{\omega}\lambda}{\lambda-\omega}=\frac{\omega-|\omega|^{2}\lambda}{\lambda-\omega}\in\mathbb{R}.

Consequently, there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(c+1)\omega=\lambda(c+|\omega|^{2})\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
\lambda\neq 0
and
|\omega|<1
. we must have
c\neq-1
. Therefore, we conclude that
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
.
A similar argument shows that
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

From here on out,
0\neq\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\cap\mathbb{D}
is fixed. The following definition encodes the technical properties that we need to ensure the abundance of boundary representations.

Definition 3.10
.

Four points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\in\mathbb{D}\cap\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{0,\lambda\right\}
will be called
good points
with respect to
\lambda
, if there exists an infinite family
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{T}\subset\mathbb{G}(1\times 1)
such that the following conditions hold:

(1)

B_{\lambda}(z_{1})=B_{\lambda}(z_{4}),\,B_{\lambda}(z_{2})=B_{\lambda}(z_{3})
.

(2)

There exists
\omega\in\mathbb{D}\cap\mathbb{R}
such that

B_{z_{1},z_{2},\omega}(z):=\frac{z-z_{1}}{1-\overline{z_{1}}z}\cdot\frac{z-z_{2}}{1-\overline{z_{2}}z}\cdot\frac{z-\omega}{1-\overline{\omega}z}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}.

(3)

For every
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}
, the matrix

\left[k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{i},z_{j})\right]_{i,j=1}^{4}

has a column (equivalently, row) with no zeroes.

(4)

For every
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}
there exists a pair
z_{i}\neq z_{j}
, such that

(3.1)

\left|\frac{k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{j},z_{i})}{\sqrt{k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{i},z_{i})\cdot k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{j},z_{j})}}\right|^{2}\neq\left|\frac{k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}(z_{j},z_{i})}{\sqrt{k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}(z_{i},z_{i})\cdot k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}(z_{j},z_{j})}}\right|^{2}.

(5)

For every
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}
,

\begin{cases}k_{z_{\ell}}\not\in\operatorname{span}\{k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{3}},k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{4}},\,P_{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}k_{\zeta}^{\alpha,\beta}\}&\ell\in\{1,2\}\\
k_{z_{\ell}}\not\in\operatorname{span}\{k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{1}},k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{2}},\,P_{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}k_{\omega}^{\alpha,\beta}\}&\ell\in\{3,4\}\end{cases},\,\begin{cases}f_{\ell}\not\in\operatorname{span}\{k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{1}},k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{2}},\,P_{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}k_{\omega}^{\alpha,\beta}\}&\ell\in\{1,2\}\\
f_{\ell}\not\in\operatorname{span}\{k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{3}},k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{4}},\,P_{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}k_{\zeta}^{\alpha,\beta}\}&\ell\in\{3,4\}\end{cases},

where
\zeta
is some point satisfying
B_{z_{3},z_{4},\zeta}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
that exists by Lemma
3.9
, and
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{4}\}\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha,\beta}
is the dual basis of
B=\{k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}\mid 1\leq i\leq 4\}
(i.e.,
f_{i}(z_{j})=\delta_{i,j}
).

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}\in{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\{0,\lambda\}
be good points and let
I\subset H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
be the ideal of functions that vanish on
z_{1},\ldots,z_{4}
. Then, the representations
\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}\colon C^{*}_{max}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I)\to B({\mathcal{M}}_{\alpha,\beta})
that extend the representations
\rho_{\alpha,\beta}
for
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}
form an infinite family of unitarily inequivalent boundary representations. In particular,
C^{*}_{e}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I)
is infinite-dimensional.

We break the proof of Theorem
3.11
into two parts. First we show that for every
(\alpha,\beta),\,(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}
, the representations
\rho_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\rho_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}
are unitarily inequivalent and irreducible. We then proceed to show that they are boundary representations for
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
.

Proposition 3.12
.

The family of representations
(\sigma_{\alpha,\beta})_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}}
of
C^{*}_{max}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I)

is a family of irreducible unitarily inequivalent representations.

Proof.

Fix
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{A}
. Recall that in the scalar case, a basis for
\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}
is

B=\left\{k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{1}},k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{2}},k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{3}},k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{4}}\right\}.

For every
f\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
, by Lemma
2.3
,
M_{f}^{*}
is diagonal with respect to
B
. Let
G
be the Gram matrix of
B
, namely,

(G)_{i,j}=\left\langle k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{j}},k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}\right\rangle=k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{i},z_{j}).

Then,

[P_{\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}}M_{f}|_{\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}}]_{C}=\left([M_{f}^{*}|_{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\beta}}]_{C}\right)^{*}=G^{-1/2}\begin{pmatrix}f(z_{1})&0&0&0\\
0&f(z_{2})&0&0\\
0&0&f(z_{3})&0\\
0&0&0&f(z_{4})\end{pmatrix}G^{1/2}=G^{-\frac{1}{2}}D_{f}G^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
C
is the orthonormal basis
\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{4}(G^{-\frac{1}{2}})_{j,i}k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{j}}\mid 1\leq i\leq 4\right\}
.

Since
B(\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}})\cong M_{4}(\mathbb{C})
as
C^{*}
-algebras via
T\to[T]_{C}
, it is enough to show that

C^{*}(\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I))\cong C^{*}(\underbrace{\left\{[P_{\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}}M_{f}|_{\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}}]_{C}\mid f\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}\right\}}_{\mathcal{S}})=M_{4}(\mathbb{C}).

To see that, for
1\leq i\leq 4
let
f_{i}\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}
be such that
f_{i}(z_{j})=\delta_{i,j}
and let

A_{i}=P_{\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}}M_{f_{i}}|_{\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}}=G^{-\frac{1}{2}}E_{ii}G^{\frac{1}{2}}\in C^{*}(\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I)).

Then,

\sum_{i=1}^{4}\frac{1}{k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{i},z_{i})}A_{i}A_{i}^{*}=G^{-1}\in C^{*}(\mathcal{S}).

As
C^{*}
algebras are closed under square-roots,

G^{1/2}\in C^{*}(\mathcal{S}).

Moreover, note that,

A_{i}A_{j}^{*}=G^{-\frac{1}{2}}D_{f_{i}}GD_{f_{j}}G^{-\frac{1}{2}}=k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{i},z_{j})G^{-\frac{1}{2}}E_{i,j}G^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

By
(3)
in Definition
3.10
, we can assume without loss of generality that

k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{1},z_{2}),\,k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{1},z_{3}),\,k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{1},z_{4})\neq 0.

Hence,

\frac{1}{k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{i},z_{j})}G^{\frac{1}{2}}A_{i}A_{j}^{*}G^{\frac{1}{2}}=G^{1/2}G^{-1/2}E_{i,j}G^{-1/2}G^{1/2}=E_{i,j}\in C^{*}(\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I))

for
1\leq i=j\leq 4
and
i=1,\,j=2,3,4
. Consequently,

E_{2,3}=E_{2,1}E_{1,3},\,E_{2,4}=E_{2,1}E_{1,4},\,E_{3,4}=E_{3,1}E_{1,4}\in C^{*}(\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I)),

i.e.,
\left\{E_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i,j\leq 4\right\}\subset C^{*}(\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I))
, concluding the proof of irreducibility. For the second part, recall that

\sigma_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\sigma_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}
are unitarily equivalent if there exists a unitary
U\colon\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}\to M_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}
, such that for every
f+I\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
,

U\rho_{\alpha,\beta}(f+I)^{*}=\rho_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}(f+I)^{*}U.

Equivalently, there exists some unitary
U
such that for all
f+I\in H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
,

UM_{f}^{*}|_{\mathcal{M_{\alpha,\beta}}}=M_{f}^{*}|_{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}}U.

In particular, if
f_{i}
is the polynomial satisfying
f_{i}(z_{j})=\delta_{i,j}
, then for every
1\leq i\leq 4

f_{i}(z_{i})Uk_{z_{i}}^{\alpha,\beta}=Uk^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}=UM_{f_{i}}^{*}(k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}})=M_{f_{i}}^{*}(Uk^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}).

Therefore,
Uk^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}\neq 0
is an eigenvector of
M_{f_{i}}^{*}
as an operator on
\mathcal{M_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}}
with eigenvalue

1
. By Lemma
2.3
,
Uk^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}\in\operatorname{\operatorname{span}}\left\{k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}_{z_{i}}\right\}
(one dimensional eigenspace), i.e.,
Uk^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}=\gamma_{i}k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}_{z_{i}}
. Moreover, as
U
is unitary,

\|k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}\|=|\gamma_{i}|\|k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}_{z_{i}}\|

and

\left\langle k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}},k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{j}}\right\rangle=\gamma_{i}\overline{\gamma_{j}}\left\langle k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}_{z_{i}},k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}_{z_{j}}\right\rangle.

Taking the absolute value on both sides, we get

\left|\left\langle k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}},k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{j}}\right\rangle\right|=\frac{\|k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{i}}\|}{\|k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}_{z_{i}}\|}\frac{\|k^{\alpha,\beta}_{z_{j}}\|}{\|k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}_{z_{j}}\|}\left|\left\langle k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}_{z_{i}},k^{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}_{z_{j}}\right\rangle\right|.

Equivalently,

(3.2)

Lemma 3.22
.

Let
\left\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\right\},\,\left\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\right\}\subset\mathbb{D}\setminus\left\{0,\lambda\right\}
be two sets of points such that
z_{i}\neq z_{j}
and
w_{i}\neq w_{j}
for all

P=\Bigg[\frac{B_{\lambda}(z_{i})\overline{B_{\lambda}(w_{j})}}{1-z_{i}\overline{w_{j}}}\Bigg]_{i,j=1}^{n}

Proof.

As
B_{\lambda}(z_{i})\neq 0
and
B_{\lambda}(w_{j})\neq 0
by assumption and

P=\begin{pmatrix}B_{\lambda}(z_{1})&0&\ldots&0\\
0&B_{\lambda}(z_{2})&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&B_{\lambda}(z_{n})\end{pmatrix}\underbrace{\Big[\frac{1}{1-z_{i}\overline{w_{j}}}\Big]}_{\tilde{P}}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{B_{\lambda}(w_{1})}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&\overline{B_{\lambda}(w_{2})}&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&\overline{B_{\lambda}(w_{n})}\end{pmatrix},

it is enough to prove

is invertible. We prove this by induction on

, the claim is clear. Assume that this is true for

Calculating the determinant of

as a function of

\det(\tilde{P})=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}(-1)^{1+j}\det((\tilde{P})_{1,j})\cdot\frac{1}{1-z_{1}\overline{w_{j}}},

with

(by induction hypothesis,

\det(\tilde{P})(z_{2})=\ldots=\det(P)(z_{n+1})=\det(\tilde{P})(\infty)=0.

i.e., it has

. Therefore,
\det(\tilde{P})=\det(\tilde{P})(z_{1})\neq 0

Lemma 3.23
.

Let
\left\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\right\},\,\left\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}\right\}\subset(\mathbb{D}\cap\mathbb{R})\setminus\left\{0,\lambda\right\}
be two sets of points such that
z_{i}\neq z_{j}
and
w_{i}\neq w_{j}
for all
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
. Then, the set of all pairs
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{T}
for which the matrix

\left[k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{i},w_{j})\right]_{i,j=1}^{4}
is singular is either
\mathbb{T}
or finite.

Proof.

Rewrite the matrix as

\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\alpha+f_{\lambda}(z_{1})\beta\\
\vdots\\
\alpha+f_{\lambda}(z_{n})\beta\end{pmatrix}}_{u_{\alpha,\beta}}\cdot\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\alpha+f_{\lambda}(w_{1})\beta\\
\vdots\\
\alpha+f_{\lambda}(w_{n})\beta\end{pmatrix}^{*}}_{v^{*}_{\alpha,\beta}}+\underbrace{\Bigg[\frac{B_{\lambda}(z_{i})\overline{B_{\lambda}(w_{j})}}{1-z_{i}\overline{w_{j}}}\Bigg]}_{P}.

This matrix is a rank-one perturbation of the invertible matrix
P
. By the determinant lemma, its determinant is given by

\det(P)(1+v^{*}_{\alpha,\beta}P^{-1}u_{\alpha,\beta})
.
As everything is real-valued, the polynomial
1+v^{*}_{\alpha,\beta}P^{-1}u_{\alpha,\beta}
is a real polynomial of degree
2
in
(\alpha,\beta)
. Note that the polynomial
1-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}
cuts out the unit circle. A straightforward computation shows that this polynomial is irreducible. The zero sets of two polynomials of degree
2
can have either a finite intersection (including the empty set) or have a component in common. The circle is the set of real points in the complex variety cut out by the polynomial
1-\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}

2
vanishes on an infinite set in the circle, it must vanish on the entire complex variety. We conclude that the level-set

\left\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{T}\mid 1+v^{*}_{\alpha,\beta}P^{-1}u_{\alpha,\beta}=0\right\}

is either finite or is equal to
\mathbb{T}
.

4.
A
C^{*}
-cover candidate

The non-commutative Grassmanian,
G_{n}^{nc}
, introduced by Brown
[
11
]
, is the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by the identity element and
\{P_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i,j\leq n\}
, such that the matrix

P=[P_{i,j}]_{i,j=1}^{n}

is a projection. In
[
27
]
, McClanahan proved that
G_{2}^{nc}
is a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of the free product
M_{2}*_{{\mathbb{C}}}{{\mathbb{C}}}^{2}
. Consequently, by a theorem of Exel and Loring
[
21
, Theorem 3.2]
,
G_{2}^{nc}
is RFD. In this section, we present another characterization of
G_{2}^{nc}
, and define a completely isometric embedding of
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
into
M_{n}(G_{2}^{nc})
, providing a candidate for the
C^{*}-
envelope of
H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be the universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by two elements
x,y
such that
x^{*}x+y^{*}y=1
.
Note that such universal
C^{*}
-algebra exists by
[
8
, Section
2
, 8.3.1]
, since the relation is of the form

\|p(x,y,x^{*},y^{*})\|=0

for the non-commutative polynomial
p(x,y,x^{*},y^{*})=x^{*}x+y^{*}y-1
, this relation is realizable by bounded operators on a Hilbert space, and it places an upper bound on
\|x\|
and
\|y\|
. This algebra is also known as the universal Pythagorean algebra (see, for example,
[
10
]
and
[
12
]
). Moreover, it was shown in
[
12
, Theorem 6.7]
that
\mathfrak{A}
is RFD.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

\mathfrak{B}=C^{*}(1,xx^{*},yy^{*},xy^{*})\subset\mathfrak{A}
.
Then,
G_{2}^{nc}\cong\mathfrak{B}
.

Proof.

As
\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}
is a column isometry, the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}xx^{*}&xy^{*}\\
yx^{*}&yy^{*}\end{pmatrix}

is a projection. By universality, if
a,\,b,\,c
are the generators of
G_{2}^{nc}
,
there exists a unital surjective
*
-homomorphism

\rho\colon G_{2}^{nc}\to\mathfrak{B},\,a\to xx^{*},\,b\to xy^{*},\,c\to yy^{*}.

It is enough to prove that
\rho
is injective. To this end, let
x\in\ker(\rho)
. As
G_{2}^{nc}
is RFD, to show
x=0
it is enough to prove that for every finite-dimensional representation
\pi
of
G_{2}^{nc}
,
\pi(x)=0
.
Fix a finite-dimensional representation

\pi\colon G_{2}^{nc}\to M_{n}({\mathbb{C}}).

Then, the matrix

P=\begin{pmatrix}\pi(a)&\pi(b)\\
\pi(b)^{*}&\pi(c)\end{pmatrix}

is a projection. Let
m=tr(P)
. We have two cases.

(1)

If
m\leq n
, let
\chi_{1}\colon G_{2}^{nc}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be the character satisfying

\chi_{1}(a)=\chi_{1}(c)=1,\,\chi_{1}(b)=0

and consider the representation
\pi^{\prime}=\pi\oplus\chi_{1}^{\oplus(n-m)}
, with
\chi_{1}^{\oplus(n-m)}
being the
(n-m)
-fold direct sum of
\chi_{1}
with itself. Then,

P^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\pi^{\prime}(a)&\pi^{\prime}(b)\\
\pi^{\prime}(b)^{*}&\pi^{\prime}(c)\end{pmatrix}

is a projection in
M_{2(2n-m)}({\mathbb{C}})
with
tr(P^{\prime})=2(n-m)+m=2n-m
, hence there exists a column isometry

V\colon{\mathbb{C}}^{2n-m}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2(2n-m)},\,V=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{x}\\
\tilde{y}\end{pmatrix},

such that
VV^{*}=P^{\prime}
. By universality of
\mathfrak{A}
, we have a representation

\tilde{\pi}\colon\mathfrak{A}\to M_{2n-m}({\mathbb{C}}),\,\tilde{\pi}(x)=\tilde{x},\,\tilde{\pi}(y)=\tilde{y}.

Therefore,
\pi^{\prime}=\tilde{\pi}|_{\mathfrak{B}}\circ\rho
. As
x\in\ker(\rho)
,
\pi^{\prime}(x)=0
and consequently
\pi(x)=0
.

(2)

If
n<m
, let
\chi_{0}\colon G^{nc}_{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be the character that maps
a
,
b
, and
c
to zero, and consider the representation
\pi^{\prime}=\pi\oplus\chi_{0}^{\oplus(m-n)}
.
Then,

P^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\pi^{\prime}(a)&\pi^{\prime}(b)\\
\pi^{\prime}(b)^{*}&\pi^{\prime}(c)\end{pmatrix}

is a projection in
M_{2m}({\mathbb{C}})
with
tr(P^{\prime})=m
. By similar arguments, again
\pi(x)=0
.

Since this holds for an arbitrary finite-dimensional representation
\pi
, we conclude that
x=0
. Hence,
\rho
is an isomorphism of
C^{*}
-algebras.
∎

For every

and every

\begin{pmatrix}\alpha^{t}\\
\beta^{t}\end{pmatrix}
is a column isometry. By universality of
\mathfrak{A}
, there exists a representation

\pi_{\alpha,\beta}\colon\mathfrak{A}\to M_{m}({\mathbb{C}})

given by

dimensional representation of
\mathfrak{A}
is of this form for some pair

\Psi=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\underbrace{\left(\left(x+f_{\lambda}(z_{i})y\right)\left(x^{*}+\overline{f_{\lambda}(z_{j})}y^{*}\right)+\frac{B_{\lambda}(z_{i})\overline{B_{\lambda}(z_{j})}}{1-z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}}\right)}_{l_{i,j}}F_{i,j},

m,r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and consider the space
M_{r,mn}({\mathbb{C}})
, thought of as the space of row block matrices with
n
blocks of size
r\times m
. In other words, we identify
M_{r,mn}\cong M_{r,m}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Fix some
W=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n})\in M_{r}({\mathbb{C}})^{\oplus n}
and define two linear operators

\psi_{\alpha,\beta},\,\varphi_{W}\colon M_{r\times mn}({\mathbb{C}})\to M_{r\times mn}({\mathbb{C}})

by

\psi_{\alpha,\beta}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&\ldots&X_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&\ldots&X_{n}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\left[k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{j},z_{i})^{t}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}

and

\varphi_{W}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&\dots&X_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}X_{1}&\dots&W_{n}X_{n}\end{pmatrix}

Furthermore, define an inner product on
M_{r\times mn}({\mathbb{C}})
by

\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}\\
\vdots\\
X_{n}\end{pmatrix}^{T},\begin{pmatrix}Y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
Y_{n}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\right\rangle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}Y_{i}^{*}\right).

It is straightforward that with respect to this inner product

\varphi_{W}^{*}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&\ldots&X_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}^{*}X_{1}&\ldots&W_{n}^{*}X_{n}\end{pmatrix}

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
E_{i,j}
be the matrix units in
M_{r\times mn}
, and define the ordered basis

\varepsilon=\left\{E_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq r,\,1\leq j\leq mn\right\}

ordered by
E_{i,j}<E_{k,l}
if and only if
j<l
or
j=l
and
i<k
. Then, in Kronecker’s notation
[
24
]
, for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and every
W=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{n})\in M_{r}({\mathbb{C}})^{\oplus n}
,

[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}]_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}F_{j,i}\otimes k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{j},z_{i})^{t}\otimes I_{r},\,[\varphi_{W}]_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i,i}\otimes I_{m}\otimes W_{i}

with
F_{i,j}
the matrix units of
M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
.

Proof.

By definition,
\psi_{\alpha,\beta}
is given by right multiplication by the matrix
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}F_{i,j}\otimes k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{j},z_{i})
. Hence, by
[
24
, Lemma 4.31]
,
[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}]_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}F_{j,i}\otimes k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{j},z_{i})^{t}\otimes I_{r}
.

For the second part, by definition,
\varphi_{W}
acts on the
i
’th block by multiplication on the left by
W_{i}
. Therefore, again by
[
24
, Lemma 4.31]
,
[\varphi_{W}]_{\varepsilon}
is a block diagonal matrix, with the
i
’th block equal to
I_{m}\otimes W_{i}
. Therefore,

[\varphi_{W}]_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i,i}\otimes I_{m}\otimes W_{i}.

∎

Remark 4.5
.

Immediately,
\varepsilon
is an orthonormal basis with respect to the inner product defined on
M_{r\times mn}({\mathbb{C}})
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For every
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{G}}(m\times m)
,
\pi_{\alpha,\beta}^{(n)}(\Psi)\otimes I_{r}=[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}]_{\varepsilon}
. In particular,
[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}]_{\varepsilon}
is positive definite.

Proof.

(\pi_{\alpha,\beta}^{(n)}(\Psi))_{i,j}=\pi_{\alpha,\beta}(\ell_{i,j})=(\alpha^{t}+f_{\lambda}(z_{i})\beta^{t})(\overline{\alpha}+\overline{f_{\lambda}(z_{j})\beta})+\frac{B_{\lambda}(z_{i})\overline{B_{\lambda}(z_{j})}}{1-z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}}I_{m}=

=k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{i},z_{j})^{t}.

Therefore,

\pi_{\alpha,\beta}^{(n)}(\Psi)\otimes I_{r}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}F_{j,i}\otimes k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{j},z_{i})^{t}\otimes I_{r}=[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}]_{\varepsilon}.

For the second part, by
4.2
and since
\pi^{(n)}
is a homomorphism of unital
C^{*}-
algebras,
\pi^{(n)}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Psi)
is positive definite. By properties of Kronecker product and since
I_{r}
is positive definite,
[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}]_{\varepsilon}
is positive definite. Lastly, by remark
4.5
,
\psi_{\alpha,\beta}
is positive definite.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

The generalized trace condition for fixed
m,r\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{G}}(m\times m)
and
W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}\in M_{r}({\mathbb{C}})
is equivalent to

\left\|\left[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right]_{\varepsilon}\cdot\left[\varphi_{W}\right]_{\varepsilon}\cdot\left[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]_{\varepsilon}\right\|\leq 1.

Proof.

By our previous notation, the generalized trace condition is

(4.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left[\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&\dots&X_{n}\end{pmatrix}\left[k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{j},z_{i})\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{*}\\
\vdots\\
X_{n}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}^{*}X_{1}&\dots&W_{n}^{*}X_{n}\end{pmatrix}\left[k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{j},z_{i})\right]_{i,j=1}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{*}W_{1}\\
\vdots\\
X_{n}^{*}W_{n}\end{pmatrix}\right]\geq 0,

for all
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in M_{r\times m}({\mathbb{C}})
.
Denoting
X=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&\ldots&X_{n}\end{pmatrix}
, this is the same as

\left\langle\psi_{\alpha,\beta}(X)-\varphi_{W}(\psi_{\alpha,\beta}(\varphi_{W}^{*}(X))),X\right\rangle\geq 0,

for all
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in M_{r\times m}({\mathbb{C}})
. By definition, this is the same as

\psi_{\alpha,\beta}\geq\varphi_{W}\circ\psi_{\alpha,\beta}\circ\varphi_{W}^{*}.

Using Lemma
4.6
, we can conjugate both sides by
\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. Consequently, the trace condition is equivalent to

1\geq\left(\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\circ\varphi_{W}\circ\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left(\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\circ\varphi_{W}\circ\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{*}.

The latter, in turn, is equivalent to

\left\|\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\circ\varphi_{W}\circ\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|\leq 1.

∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
4.3
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

First, it is clear that the range is contained in
M_{n}(\mathfrak{B})
, as
\Psi\in\mathfrak{B}
. Throughout this proof, we will use the notation

\widetilde{\psi_{\alpha,\beta}}:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}F_{j,i}\otimes k^{\alpha,\beta}(z_{j},z_{i})^{t}.

Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and consider the homomorphism
\Gamma^{(r)}\colon M_{r}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I)\to M_{r}(M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}))

given by

\Gamma^{(r)}([f_{i,j}+I])=\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}E_{i,j}\otimes\Gamma(f_{i,j}+I).

To show
\Gamma^{(r)}
is an isometry, it is enough to prove

\|[f_{i,j}+I]\|\leq 1\iff\|\Gamma^{(r)}([f_{i,j}+I])\|\leq 1
.
As
\mathfrak{A}
is RFD, our claim is equivalent to showing that

\|[f_{i,j}+I]\|\leq 1\iff\|\tilde{\rho}(\Gamma^{(r)}([f_{i,j}+I]))\|\leq 1

for every finite-dimensional representation

\tilde{\rho}\colon M_{r}(M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}))\to M_{k}({\mathbb{C}})
.
Fix a finite-dimensional representation
\tilde{\rho}
of
M_{r}(M_{n}(\mathfrak{A}))
. It is well known that

\tilde{\rho}
is unitarily equivalent to
\rho^{(r)}
for some representation

\rho\colon M_{n}(\mathfrak{A})\to M_{s}({\mathbb{C}}).

Hence,
\left\|\rho^{(r)}(\Gamma^{(r)}([f_{i,j}+I]))\right\|=\left\|\tilde{\rho}(\Gamma^{(r)}([f_{i,j}+I]))\right\|
.
Again,
\rho
is unitarily equivalent to
\hat{\rho}^{(n)}
where
\hat{\rho}
is a representation of
\mathfrak{A}
. As we noted,
\hat{\rho}=\pi_{\alpha,\beta}
for some
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{G}}(m\times m)
. Hence,
\rho
is unitarily equivalent to
\pi_{\alpha,\beta}^{(n)}
. Let
U
be the unitary satisfying
\rho([f_{p,q}])=U\pi^{(n)}_{\alpha,\beta}([f_{p,q}])U^{*}
.
Then,

\rho^{(r)}\left(\Gamma^{(r)}([f_{i,j}+I])\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}E_{i,j}\otimes\rho\left(\Gamma\left(f_{i,j}+I\right)\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}E_{i,j}\otimes\left(U\pi_{\alpha,\beta}^{(n)}\left(\Gamma\left(f_{i,j}+I\right)\right)U^{*}\right)=

=\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}E_{i,j}\otimes\left(U\pi_{\alpha,\beta}^{(n)}\left(\Psi^{-\frac{1}{2}}D_{f_{i,j}}\Psi^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)U^{*}\right)=

=\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}E_{i,j}\otimes\left(U\widetilde{\psi_{\alpha,\beta}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{k,k}\otimes f_{i,j}(z_{k})I_{m}\right)\widetilde{\psi_{\alpha,\beta}}^{\frac{1}{2}}U^{*}\right)=

=\left(I_{r}\otimes U^{*}\right)\left(I_{r}\otimes\widetilde{\psi_{\alpha,\beta}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}E_{i,j}\otimes\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{k,k}\otimes f_{i,j}(z_{k})I_{m}\right)\right)\left(I_{r}\otimes\widetilde{\psi_{\alpha,\beta}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left(I_{r}\otimes U\right).

Note that the last equality of the second line holds since
\pi_{\alpha,\beta}^{(n)}(f_{i,j}(z_{k}))=f_{i,j}(z_{k})I_{m}
. Since
I_{r}
and
U
are unitaries,
I_{r}\otimes U
is unitary (with
(I_{r}\otimes U)^{*}=I_{r}\otimes U^{*}
) by properties of Kronecher product, that is
\rho^{(r)}\left(\Gamma^{(r)}([f_{i,j}])\right)
is unitarily equivalent to

(4.3)

\left(I_{r}\otimes\widetilde{\psi_{\alpha,\beta}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}E_{i,j}\otimes\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{k,k}\otimes f_{i,j}(z_{k})I_{m}\right)\right)\left(I_{r}\otimes\widetilde{\psi_{\alpha,\beta}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right).

Conjugating (
4.3
) by the unitary that interchanges the first two tensor products and then by the unitary that interchanges the last two tensor products, we get

\left(\widetilde{\psi_{\alpha,\beta}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\otimes I_{r}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}F_{k,k}\otimes I_{m}\otimes W_{k}\right)\left(\widetilde{\psi_{\alpha,\beta}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\otimes I_{r}\right)=\left[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\left[\varphi_{W}\right]_{\varepsilon}\cdot\left[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}=

=\left[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\circ\varphi_{W}\circ\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]_{\varepsilon},

with
W_{k}=[f_{i,j}](z_{k})
.
Now assume that
\left\|\tilde{\rho}(\Gamma^{(r)}(f))\right\|\leq 1
. By unitary equivalence, and since
\varepsilon
is an orthonormal basis,

\left\|\left[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\circ\varphi_{W}\circ\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]_{\varepsilon}\right\|=\left\|\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\circ\varphi_{W}\circ\psi_{\alpha,\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|\leq 1.

We have observed that this is equivalent to the generalized trace condition (
4.1
) for
W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}
being true for
r
and
m
.
As
M_{r}\left(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}}/I\right)
and
M_{r}(H^{\infty}_{\text{node}})/\mathcal{I}
are isometrically isomorphic, by (
4.1
) this is equivalent to

\left\|[f_{i,j}]+\mathcal{I}\right\|=\left\|[f_{i,j}+I]\right\|\leq 1
.
Therefore,
\tilde{\rho}(\Gamma^{(r)})
is an isometry, hence
\Gamma^{(r)}
is an isometry.
∎
