Rehabilitation of Complex Iatrogenic Prosthodontic Failures: A Case Report on Comprehensive Full-Mouth Reconstruction
Authors: Rawan Alrethia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe management of partial edentulism with severe occlusal disharmony and failed prosthodontic restorations requires careful multidisciplinary treatment. A 48-year-old male presented with defective restorations, marginal discrepancies, excess cement, poor contours, a collapsed occlusal plane, missing teeth, hyper-erupted posterior teeth, occlusal interferences, and localised stage II grade B periodontitis. After written informed consent, the treatment was completed in four phases: non-surgical periodontal therapy, endodontic retreatment with post-core reconstruction, selective extractions, and definitive fixed prosthodontic rehabilitation. A mounted diagnostic wax-up established the restored vertical dimension and group-function occlusal scheme. IPS e.max lithium disilicate crowns were bonded anteriorly, while monolithic zirconia crowns were cemented posteriorly. At the 4-week follow-up, the patient demonstrated complete resolution of periodontal inflammation, elimination of occlusal interferences, stable group function, and high satisfaction with masticatory function and smile aesthetics.

Introduction

Partial edentulism is a common condition in clinical dentistry and requires careful treatment planning to restore function, aesthetics, and oral health.1,2 Management becomes more challenging in patients with severe occlusal disharmony, failed prosthodontic restorations, and periodontal involvement, requiring combined periodontal, endodontic, and prosthodontic care.3–5 Occlusal irregularities with over-erupted teeth and premature contacts may compromise masticatory efficiency, restoration stability, and stomatognathic function.6,7 Previous restorations with marginal gaps, poor contours, and loss of marginal integrity may worsen plaque accumulation and functional imbalance, making complete rehabilitation necessary instead of simple repair.8 Periodontal evaluation also plays an important role in treatment planning, since periodontal breakdown may reduce the long-term prognosis of abutment teeth and prosthodontic restorations.2,4 Proper diagnosis and sequential interdisciplinary management are therefore essential for achieving functional stability, aesthetic rehabilitation, and preservation of the remaining dentition.

Multidisciplinary full-mouth rehabilitation is widely used for managing complex partial edentulism associated with multiple clinical problems.3–6 This treatment protocol includes detailed diagnosis, treatment planning, periodontal therapy, oral hygiene improvement, endodontic or surgical procedures when indicated, and definitive prosthodontic rehabilitation.9–12 Diagnostic wax-up helps evaluate occlusion and aesthetic outcome before treatment, while provisional restorations permit assessment of function, phonetics, and patient comfort before final prosthesis placement.13,14 Current restorative materials and clinical techniques have improved the quality of full-mouth rehabilitation. Zirconium oxide is commonly used for posterior restorations, whereas IPS e.max is preferred for anterior aesthetic restorations.15–17 Hydrofluoric acid etching and silane application improve ceramic bonding.15,16,18 Proper occlusal design and occlusal guards help maintain prosthetic stability and function.10,19

Iatrogenic prosthodontic failure refers to periodontal, endodontic, or occlusal complications caused by improper clinical procedures, poor biomechanical principles, or inadequate pre-prosthetic diagnosis.1,8,20,21 Open margins, over-contoured restorations, an uncorrected occlusal plane, and retained subgingival cement can cause local tissue damage and functional problems.8,22 Full-mouth rehabilitation is commonly described for generalised attrition or tooth loss,9,11,12 but systematic clinical methods for managing extensive multi-quadrant iatrogenic prosthodontic failures are limited. Re-treating these cases is vastly more complex than primary rehabilitation due to irreversible loss of tooth structure, compromised abutments, altered vertical dimension, and severe occlusal interferences caused by previous improper restorations.6,8,22

Recent clinical reports emphasize that managing iatrogenic failure requires a rigorous diagnostic protocol to distinguish between salvageable abutments and those needing extraction, followed by sequential occlusal re-establishment.8,22 This case report contributes distinct clinical value by presenting a step-by-step interdisciplinary framework to correct extensive iatrogenic damage in a 48-year-old partially edentulous male. By documenting the diagnosis, phased management, material selection (anterior lithium disilicate and posterior zirconia), and post-treatment outcomes through 4 weeks, this report provides actionable guidelines for preventing biological and mechanical complications and correcting severe restorative failure.

Case Presentation

Patient Information

A 48-year-old male presented to the postgraduate prosthodontic clinic with a chief complaint of an uncomfortable bite and a poor appearance of the teeth shape. The patient was a Colonel in National Guard and a chronic smoker. His medical history revealed unremarkable, with no known drug allergies, no systemic disease, and no significant family medical history. Both parents were reported to be healthy. His vital signs at the initial visit were within normal limits, with a blood pressure of 127/78 mmHg, pulse of 65 beat/minute, and a respiration rate of 17/minute.

The dental history of patient revealed irregular dental visits, usually for emergency treatment. Orthodontic treatment was completed seven years prior to the presentation. He had congenitally missing maxillary right lateral incisor (#12), which was managed through space closure and canine repositioning. The patient had undergone previous extractions of teeth #18, #28, #38, #37, #44, #47, and #48 and root canal treatment of tooth #16. All restorations were clinically and radiographically defective, with periodontal involvement, an uneven occlusal plane, occlusal discrepancies, and poor marginal integrity. These findings indicated inadequate adherence to standard clinical procedures during the previous restorative treatment.

Clinical Findings

Extraoral Examination

The extraoral examination showed an oval facial form without asymmetry, swelling, or lymphadenopathy. Mandibular movements were normal without deviation. The TMJs were asymptomatic without clicking, crepitus, or tenderness. Lip support, competency, mobility, and neuromuscular coordination were satisfactory. Vertical facial height and nasolabial angle were within normal range. Smile analysis demonstrated a right-sided deviation of the dental midline from the facial midline, low smile line according to Tjan classification, absence of buccal corridors, and an incisal curve that did not parallel the contour of the lower lip.

Maxillary Arch and Mandibular Arch-Occlusion View

Intraoral examination revealed U-shaped maxillary and mandibular arches with a normal palatal vault. Maxillary and mandibular labial and buccal frenal attachments were within normal limits. Oral mucosa and pharyngeal tissues showed no visible pathology. Generalized callus deposition and multiple defective crowns were present throughout the dentition. Teeth #18, #28, #37, #38, #44, #47, and #48 were missing. Congenitally missing tooth #12 had been managed by space closure and canine repositioning. Nicotine stomatitis was observed on the maxillary palate.

Panoramic and Full-Mouth Radiograph

Panoramic radiograph showed normal TMJ anatomy and a normal trabecular bone pattern in both arches. Mild alveolar ridge resorption was present in the maxillary and mandibular arches, with intact bilateral maxillary sinus floors. Multiple defective crowns and missing teeth were identified throughout the dentition. Radiographic over-eruption of teeth #17 and #27 was evident. Full-mouth radiographs demonstrated dense maxillary and mandibular bone with heavy trabeculation. Crown-root ratios were favourable in the remaining teeth. Clinical and radiographic examination showed satisfactory general oral health with no contraindications to dental treatment. Periodontal evaluation revealed generalized periodontitis stage II, grade B with fair oral hygiene. Existing restorations consisted of multiple defective full-coverage crowns suggestive of previous prosthodontic failure. Maxillary and mandibular partial edentulism was associated with severe occlusal irregularities, working and non-working interferences, over-eruption of teeth #17 and #27, and an uneven occlusal plane. Based on clinical findings, the patient was categorized as PDI Class IV for partial edentulism, indicating a highly complex prosthodontic condition requiring multidisciplinary rehabilitation. The patient showed good cooperation and motivation throughout treatment.

Therapeutic Plan

Based on the detailed clinical and radiographic examination, the treatment plan was formulated into four phases and presented to the patient. All treatment options, objectives, limitations, and expected outcomes were thoroughly discussed with the patient, and written informed consent was obtained prior to the commencement of treatment. This case report was prepared and structured in accordance with the CARE (case report) guidelines to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting. The treatment plan was organised as follows: Phase I: Oral hygiene instructions, full-mouth scaling, a complete diagnostic wax-up, and provisionalization.Phase II: Surgical and endodontic phase, which included extraction of #17, and #27, root canal therapy for #11, #23, #25, #41, #42, #43, #45, and #46, endodontic retreatment of tooth #16, and post and core of teeth #16, #23, #41, #42, #43, and #45.Phase III: Definite prosthodontic rehabilitation through the fabrication and delivery of single crowns for all remaining teeth.Phase IV: Maintenance, re-evaluation, and supportive care through 4 weeks post-delivery, with long-term recall scheduled thereafter.

Treatment

Preliminary Records

After obtaining written informed consent from the patient, preliminary impressions were made with irreversible hydrocolloid and poured in type III gypsum stone. A Slidematic facebow transfer was recorded using an ear bow with the anterior third reference point positioned 43 mm from the lateral incisor edge (Figure 5A). Centric relation was recorded using a Lucia jig and bilateral manipulation. Diagnostic casts were mounted on a Denar Mark II articulator for occlusal evaluation and treatment planning (Figure 5B). Diagnostic Wax-Up

Two sets of diagnostic casts were prepared using irreversible hydrocolloid and dental stone. A complete diagnostic wax-up was performed on the mounted casts to establish the planned occlusal plane and occlusal pattern according to clinical findings and aesthetic requirements. The wax-up served as a guide for provisionalization and definitive prosthodontic rehabilitation (Figure 6). Mounted wax-up.

Provisional Restorations

Based on the diagnostic wax-up, teeth preparation was completed for zirconia crowns in the posterior region and IPS e.max all-ceramic crowns in the anterior region. Milled polymethyl methacrylate provisional crowns were fabricated and cemented with temporary luting cement in both arches to establish group function occlusion. After placement, aesthetics, phonetics, and occlusal function were evaluated and accepted by the patient. Irreversible hydrocolloid impressions of the provisional restorations were then made for fabrication of a custom anterior guide table and to assist in construction of the definitive prostheses.

Teeth Preparation and Final Impression

Final preparation of the maxillary teeth was completed, followed by gingival retraction using retraction cord soaked in Hemodent solution containing 25% aluminium chloride. A full-arch maxillary impression was made with polyvinyl siloxane additional silicone impression material using an acrylic resin custom tray (Figure 7). Centric Relation Record and Cross-Mounting

A centric relation record was obtained between the maxillary provisional restorations and mandibular prepared teeth at the established occlusal vertical dimension to permit cross-mounting of the casts. Centric relation was recorded using a leaf gauge and polyvinyl siloxane registration material. Definitive and provisional casts were mounted on a Denar Mark II articulator. The articulator, mounted working casts, provisional restoration casts, and detailed laboratory instructions were then sent to the dental laboratory for fabrication of the definitive restorations.

Definitive Restoration-Maxillary and Mandibular

Zirconia copings were fabricated for posterior teeth, while IPS e.max all-ceramic copings were prepared for anterior teeth. Clinical try-in was performed to evaluate internal fit, centric relation, and vertical dimension before porcelain application. Restorations were later evaluated in the bisque stage for interproximal contacts, contour, and occlusion. Posterior crowns were cleaned and cemented using self-adhesive transparent resin cement (RelyX Unicem, 3M ESPE). Anterior restorations were bonded after conditioning with 5% hydrofluoric acid for 20 seconds, followed by rinsing, drying, monobond application for 60 seconds, and luting with Variolink Esthetic LC warm shade resin cement. Final restorations were clinically assessed for marginal adaptation, occlusal stability, and aesthetic outcome before completion of treatment (Figure 9). Follow-Up and Outcomes

The patient was reviewed at 24 hours, 48 hours, two weeks, and four weeks after definitive cementation. Minor occlusal adjustments were completed at the 24-hour visit, and a maxillary thermoplastic soft-hard occlusal nightguard was delivered at 48 hours for restoration protection. Soft tissue examination showed healthy gingival adaptation around all restoration margins. Post-treatment photographs confirmed stable group function occlusion with complete removal of the pre-treatment occlusal interferences (Figure 10). Discussion

This case report describes multidisciplinary rehabilitation of a 48-year-old partially edentulous patient with severe occlusal irregularities, failed prosthodontic restorations, and generalised periodontitis. Periodontal, endodontic, occlusal, and esthetic findings were assessed to develop a four-phase treatment plan based on prosthodontic principles.1,23 Sequential treatment planning can improve function and long-term clinical outcomes.9,10 Examination showed generalised stage II grade B periodontitis, multiple missing teeth, defective full-mouth crowns, posterior over-eruption, and severe working and non-working side interferences, resulting in a PDI Class IV classification. Similar clinical findings have been described in patients with collapsed occlusion requiring full-mouth rehabilitation.6 Defective restorations and compromised marginal integrity observed in this patient contribute to plaque accumulation, periodontal destruction, occlusal instability, restoration failure, and progressive bone loss, as previously described in prosthodontic literature.8,21,22

Periodontal examination showed generalized rolled edematous gingival margins with bleeding on probing at multiple sites, requiring periodontal therapy before definitive prosthodontic rehabilitation. Solanki et al (2025) reported that combined periodontal and prosthodontic management improves treatment stability in complex partially edentulous cases.4 In managing the severe over-eruption of teeth #17 and #27, alternative treatment options were carefully evaluated. Endodontic therapy combined with aggressive clinical crown lengthening or intentional intrusion via skeletal anchorage (orthodontic mini-screws) were considered to re-establish the occlusal plane. However, given the advanced localized periodontal pocketing (4 mm distal pockets), severe supra-eruption into the opposing edentulous ridge space, and the presence of severe working/non-working occlusal interferences, endodontic/orthodontic intrusion presented an unpredictable long-term periodontal prognosis. Therefore, selective extractions of #17 and #27 were chosen as the most predictable approach to eliminate dynamic occlusal obstacles and establish a stable posterior occlusal plane.2,19 Compared to similar full-mouth retreatment cases reported by Cheong et al (2019)6 and Lanza et al (2017),3 where removable or implant-supported prostheses were utilized, the present case successfully restored function and aesthetics entirely with tooth-supported fixed dental prostheses. This decision preserved residual sound tooth structure and relied on rigid post-core foundation restorations on heavily compromised abutments.

Diagnostic wax-up served as the foundation for treatment planning by helping correct the occlusal plane, evaluate vertical dimension, eliminate occlusal interferences, and assess the planned aesthetic outcome. Balavadivel et al (2020) reported that diagnostic wax-up and custom templates improve treatment accuracy and reduce chairside adjustments during full-mouth rehabilitation.7 Lee et al (2019) observed greater patient satisfaction with aesthetic outcome when diagnostic wax-up was combined with comprehensive treatment planning.13 Mounting the diagnostic casts on a semi-adjustable articulator also helped evaluate static and dynamic occlusion and assisted in selection of the occlusal pattern.

The endodontic phase included root canal treatment and retreatment procedures, followed by post-and–core rehabilitation of structurally compromised teeth. Fiber-reinforced posts were selected because of their favourable adhesive and biochemical properties compared to metallic posts.3 The definitive restorations included lithium disilicate crowns for anterior teeth and zirconia crowns for posterior teeth because of their superior aesthetic, mechanical, and survival characteristics.15–17 Biocompatible and marginal fit were important considerations during material selection, as inaccuracies may adversely affect surrounding oral tissues and restoration longevity.23,24 Proper adhesion protocol and careful removal of excess cement were essential to minimize periodontal complications associated with subgingival cementations.8

Group function occlusion was selected during provisional and definitive rehabilitation because of the patient’s periodontal condition and the need for balanced occlusal force distribution. Group function distributes lateral occlusal forces among multiple posterior teeth and reduces excessive loading on individual teeth.19 Occlusal adjustment and contour correction eliminated working and non-working side interferences in tooth pairs #17/#46, #26/#27/#36, and #13/#43, resulting in stable occlusal rehabilitation. The patient reported improvement in aesthetics, mastication, comfort, self-confidence, and quality of life following treatment.20,25 Similar findings were reported by Sinha et al (2025), who observed favorable clinical outcome following full-mouth rehabilitation, with 91.4% treatment success and 96.8% restoration survival after three years.20 This case highlights the significance of comprehensive diagnosis and assessment of the periodontal, endodontic, and aesthetic elements before making a treatment plan, along with diagnostic wax-up and provisionalization in guiding definitive outcomes. The success of treatment of partially edentulous patients is dependent on the coordination of various disciplines of dentistry in a systematic manner for obtaining predictable, stable and long-term functional and aesthetic results.

The clinical findings describe the treatment outcome of a single patient and cannot be generalised to a larger population. The post-treatment evaluation was conducted at four weeks; therefore, prosthetic survival, ceramic chipping, and periodontal stability require evaluation over a longer follow-up period. Patient satisfaction was assessed subjectively without validated Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs), such as the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP-14). Occlusal changes were assessed using clinical articulation and excursive movement checks rather than quantitative T-Scan digital occlusal analysis. Future prospective studies should include larger samples, longer follow-up periods, validated patient-reported measures, and quantitative occlusal assessment to determine the clinical performance of extensive fixed prosthodontic rehabilitation.

Conclusion

Multidisciplinary full-mouth rehabilitation requires careful diagnosis and phased treatment planning. Correction of the occlusal plane using a diagnostic wax-up and provisional restorations can help manage occlusal interferences and improve masticatory function. Appropriate periodontal care, prosthodontic procedures, and material selection may provide satisfactory functional and aesthetic rehabilitation and reduce prosthetic complications.
