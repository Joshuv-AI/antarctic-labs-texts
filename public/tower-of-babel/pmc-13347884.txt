Family relationship, environmental, and cognitive correlates of adolescents' drug use in Cameroon: a school-based cross-sectional study across three secondary schools
Authors: Jean Pierre Yves Awono Noah, Shulika Leocadia Tangwa, Rogacien Kana, Wepnyu Njamnshi Yembe, Justin Ndié, Marileine Kemme Kemme, Fadimatou Mfiya, Conrad Duvivier Angoe, Rodrigue Bilè Komokcheua, Paul Martin Jonas Kana, Julienne Louise Ngo Likeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychoactive substance use among adolescents represents a growing public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa. This study examined alcohol and illegal psychoactive substance use among secondary school adolescents in Yaoundé, Cameroon, with particular attention to associated factors, including family relationships.MethodsA school-based cross-sectional study was conducted among adolescents enrolled in Forms 3 to 5 in three secondary schools in Yaoundé. Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire. Quantitative analyses estimated 12-month prevalence and associated factors using robust Poisson regression for alcohol use and complementary log–log regression for illegal psychoactive substance use, complemented by bootstrap resampling and Firth’s penalised regression to assess robustness in a rare-event context. Qualitative open-ended responses were analysed using thematic and lexical co-occurrence approaches (Jaccard C and T coefficients) to explore adolescents’ representations of substances and perceived consequences.ResultsA total of 352 adolescents were included (54.3% girls). The median age was 15 years (IQR: 14–17; observed range: 12–20). The 12-month prevalence of alcohol use was 32.7% (95% CI: 27.8–37.8), while 3.7% (95% CI: 2.0–6.2) reported illegal psychoactive substance use. Marijuana and cocaine emerged as the most salient illicit substances within adolescents’ cognitive representations, forming the strongest co-occurrence pattern (Jaccard C = 0.24; T = 0.389), while other substances such as tramadol, heroin, tobacco, and alcohol were mentioned less prominently. Perceived consequences were predominantly negative, encompassing neuropsychological, somatic, behavioural, and social effects, although limited positive representations persisted. Multivariable analyses showed that alcohol use was independently associated with increasing age, availability of drugs around the home, and illegal substance use. When illegal psychoactive substance use was the outcome, alcohol use was inversely associated. Family relationship variables were not independently associated with either outcome after adjustment.ConclusionThese findings underscore the central and asymmetric role of alcohol use in adolescent substance involvement. In parallel, they indicate that alcohol use among school-going adolescents is more strongly influenced by age and environmental exposure than by family relationship factors. They support multi-level prevention strategies focusing on early school-based psychoeducation, regulation of alcohol availability in home and community settings, and longitudinal research to clarify substance-use trajectories.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27702-9.Keywords: Family relationship, Adolescent, Substance-Related Disorders, Alcohol Drinking, Illicit Drugs, Prevalence, Cameroon

Background

Adolescence is a critical developmental stage marked by growing autonomy, identity formation, and heightened vulnerability to risk-taking behaviours, including the use of psychoactive substances [1, 2]. Globally, substance use during this period has been linked to adverse outcomes ranging from school dropout and unsafe sexual practices to long-term psychiatric and behavioural disorders such as anxiety, depression, and substance dependence [3, 4]. In low- and middle-income countries, this vulnerability is often magnified by inadequate mental health infrastructure, weak regulatory frameworks, and social norms that trivialize the consumption of substances such as alcohol, tobacco, and prescription drugs [5, 6]. Within this context, alcohol use and illegal psychoactive substance use represent distinct but interrelated public health concerns during adolescence, both of which are examined in the present study.

Recent evidence from sub-Saharan Africa highlights the widespread nature of adolescent substance use. A meta-analysis by Ebrahim, Adams, and Demant [7] estimated a lifetime prevalence of 21% among youth aged 10–24 years, with alcohol being the most commonly consumed substance. Similarly, a review by Jumbe et al. [8] identified substance use as the result of interacting factors operating at individual (e.g. male gender, older age), interpersonal (peer influence), familial (poor parental supervision), and community levels (easy drug access), consistent with ecological and social development frameworks. Rather than reiterating these determinants exhaustively, the present study builds on this literature by focusing on the family environment as a central and modifiable context shaping adolescent substance use behaviours. In Cameroon, adolescent substance use remains a growing concern. In Yaoundé, Ekwoge et al. [9] reported a 12-month prevalence of 33.7% among high school students, with alcohol (24.7%) and tobacco (7.9%) being the most common substances, and strong associations with male gender, age over 16 years, and permissive family environments. In Douala, Kingsley et al. [10] found lifetime experimentation rates as high as 91.0% and current use at 42.8%, largely influenced by family or peer use and home availability of substances. In the South-West Region, Nkouonlack et al. [11] observed that 15.3% of secondary school students misused prescription drugs, often in combination with alcohol or tobacco. Other studies further underscore the contribution of structural and psychosocial factors: Mekone et al. [12] linked adolescent substance use in Yaoundé to single-parent or grandparent care, alcohol availability at home, and emotional distress. Together, these findings point to the importance of examining not only prevalence, but also the relational and emotional contexts in which substance use occurs.

Family relationships play a pivotal role in adolescent development and substance use, acting as either protective or risk-enhancing factors. Parenting style, emotional availability, communication quality, and household substance exposure have been consistently associated with substance-related behaviours [13–15]. Within an ecological framework, family relationships constitute a key proximal context shaping adolescent substance-use behaviours. Adolescents exposed to neglectful or conflictual family environments, or to parental and sibling psychoactive substance use, are at increased risk of early substance-use initiation. Conversely, supportive family dynamics, characterised by parental monitoring, emotional warmth, and relational closeness, have been associated with delayed initiation and lower frequency of alcohol and other substance use [4, 16–18].

Despite this well-established evidence, relatively few quantitative, school-based studies in sub-Saharan Africa have explicitly operationalised and examined the quality of family relationships as a core explanatory factor alongside peer and environmental influences.

In Cameroon in particular, existing studies are often qualitative, urban-centric, or limited to university populations, with limited attention to adolescents’ perceptions of substances and their emotional coping strategies within the family context. Moreover, few studies have combined prevalence estimation, analysis of substance-related perceptions, and multivariable modelling of familial and contextual correlates within a single analytical framework.

To address these gaps, this study investigated alcohol use and illegal psychoactive substance use and their familial correlates among adolescents attending three secondary schools in Yaoundé, Cameroon. Specifically, the study aimed to: (i) determine the 12-month prevalence of alcohol use and illegal psychoactive substance use among school-going adolescents in Yaoundé; (ii) describe the types of drugs known by adolescents and explore their perceptions of the consequences and observable effects of substance use among peers; and (iii) examine sociodemographic, emotional, familial, and environmental factors associated with alcohol use and illegal psychoactive substance use. By placing family relationships at the centre of the analysis, this study seeks to strengthen evidence on adolescent mental health in Cameroon and inform public health interventions targeting relational determinants of substance use.

Methodology

Study design, location and period

A school-based cross-sectional study was conducted in the Djoungolo health district of Yaoundé, Cameroon, over a three-month period (April–June 2021). The district is urban, with numerous public and denominational secondary schools enrolling adolescents from varied socioeconomic backgrounds.

Target population

The study population comprised students in Forms 3–5 (equivalent to 4e, 3e, and 2nde in the French Cameroonian educational system), typically aged 13–16 years. These grades were selected as they represent mid-adolescents at a critical stage of social and behavioural development and mature enough to complete structured questionnaires. Only students who provided assent and had prior parental or guardian consent were included. Students who were unable to understand the study information or complete the self-administered questionnaire independently were not included, as identified through observable difficulties during the consent and questionnaire administration process.

During the COVID-19 period, most secondary schools had shifted to distance learning; therefore, only secondary schools maintaining face-to-face teaching were eligible for in-person data collection.

Sample size and sampling

The minimum required sample size was 350 students, calculated using the World Health Organisation (WHO) formula for prevalence studies [19], assuming a 35% prevalence of psychoactive substance (PAS) use, a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level. This estimate was informed by recent school-based studies in Cameroon reporting prevalence rates of 27.5–42.8% [9, 10, 12].

A multistage convenience–purposive–random sampling design was used [20–22]:

At the first stage, secondary schools located in the Djoungolo health district of Yaoundé were selected by convenience, based on feasibility criteria including accessibility, administrative authorisation, and willingness to participate. This convenience–purposive component was dictated by COVID-19-related operational constraints: at the time of data collection, the vast majority of public secondary schools in Yaoundé remained under distance-learning arrangements in line with Ministry of Secondary Education directives, while a limited number of private institutions in the Djoungolo health district had resumed face-to-face teaching under sanitary protocols. Schools were approached sequentially, and recruitment stopped after the first three eligible schools that had resumed in-person teaching granted administrative authorisation and agreed to participate; these were private institutions, and additional eligible schools in the district were not approached.

At the second stage, grade levels were purposively selected, restricting the study population to students enrolled in Forms 3 to 5, corresponding to mid- and late adolescence, a period during which substance use behaviours are more likely to emerge and be reliably reported.

At the third stage, one class was selected per eligible grade level (Forms 3–5) within each participating school. When more than one class was available within a grade, simple random sampling was used to select the class, based on a random number generator applied to the list of eligible classes provided by the school administration. In one school, only Forms 4 and 5 were available due to institutional structure. All students enrolled in the selected classes were exhaustively invited to participate, and no further sampling was performed at the student level. Differences between the total number of students enrolled in the selected classes and the number included in the study reflect absenteeism on the day of data collection and refusal to participate, partly attributable to disruptions related to the COVID-19 pandemic. Of the 547 students enrolled in the selected classes, 352 were included in the study, corresponding to an overall class coverage rate of 64.3% (Supplementary Information 1). Data collection

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire available in French and English, administered during school hours under standardised classroom conditions. Trained research assistants with prior experience in school-based public health surveys supervised data collection, provided clarification when needed while ensuring confidentiality, and teachers were not present during administration. Research assistants received a one-day training covering study objectives, ethical considerations (assent, confidentiality, and non-coercion), standardised questionnaire administration, identification of comprehension difficulties, and appropriate handling of participants’ questions to avoid influencing responses. The questionnaire was pre-tested among 30 students in a non-participating classroom school, resulting in minor wording adjustments to improve clarity and comprehension.

The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study and informed by selected sub-items of the DEP-ADO screening tool. The DEP-ADO instrument has been widely used in adolescent populations and has demonstrated good diagnostic performance, with high sensitivity and specificity reported in prior validation studies in French-speaking adolescent populations [23–26]. In the present study, the DEP-ADO substance list and the frequency-based response format (item 1 of the full DEP-ADO scale) were used as references to assess alcohol and illegal psychoactive substance use; the full DEP-ADO instrument was not administered.

In addition, the questionnaire covered sociodemographic characteristics, family context (including parental substance use history and perceived relationship quality), emotional responses and coping with life difficulties, substance-related knowledge and perceptions, peer and media influence, environmental availability of substances at home and at school, and perceptions of school guidance and counselling services (Supplementary Information 2).

Variables of study

Dependent variables

The primary outcome was the use of at least one illegal psychoactive substance, and the secondary outcome was alcohol use. Both alcohol use and illegal psychoactive substance use were assessed using a 12-month recall question asking participants whether they had used each substance and, if so, how often. Response options ranged from “never” to “every day.”

For illegal psychoactive substances, participants reported use for each listed substance (marijuana, cocaine, glue/solvents, hallucinogens, opioids including heroin and tramadol, amphetamines, and other non-prescribed substances). Illegal psychoactive substance use was measured using a composite score obtained by summing responses across substance-specific items. This composite score was subsequently dichotomised to define a binary outcome (yes/no), with participants coded as “yes” if they reported any use of at least one listed substance in the past 12 months and “no” otherwise.

Alcohol use was defined as any reported alcohol consumption in the past 12 months including beer, wine, and spirits. Alcohol use was similarly recoded into a binary variable (yes/no).

In this study, the term “psychoactive substances” (PAS) refers to substances that affect the central nervous system and includes both legal substances (alcohol and tobacco) and illegal psychoactive substances. The primary outcome, illegal psychoactive substance use, excluded alcohol and tobacco and referred specifically to the use of illicit substances as defined above.

Reliability and content validation of study measures

To the best of our knowledge, although the full DEP-ADO instrument has been previously validated in adolescent populations, no prior standalone validation studies exist for the specific 7-item scale or for the contextual and family-related variables as operationalised in this study. In the present work, the DEP-ADO was not used as a diagnostic or screening tool; rather, only selected sub-items (substance list and frequency-based response format) were adapted as references. Consequently, psychometric properties relevant to diagnostic instruments, such as sensitivity, specificity, or inter-rater agreement (κ), were not applicable.

Reliability assessment therefore focused on the internal consistency of the 7-item scale assessing illegal psychoactive substance use, evaluated prior to dichotomisation using McDonald’s Omega, Guttman’s Lambda-6, and Cronbach’s Alpha [27–32]. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.917; McDonald’s Omega = 0.919; Guttman’s Lambda-6 = 0.955), all exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70. A full description of the seven items composing this scale is provided in Supplementary Information (Supplementary information 3).

As the independent variables represented descriptive and contextual characteristics rather than latent constructs, formal psychometric validation was not applicable. Instead, their selection and operationalisation were validated through expert consensus to ensure content relevance and metrological coherence. This process involved a multidisciplinary panel comprising two addiction specialists, one clinical psychologist, one epidemiologist, one neuropsychologist, and two public health experts. The expert review ensured conceptual clarity, contextual appropriateness, and alignment with the study objectives.

Data management and analysis

Quantitative data

No missing data were observed. Adolescent characteristics were summarised using medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables.

Prevalence of alcohol use and illegal PAS use were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

To test the association between independent variables and illegal PAS use, given the very low prevalence of illegal PAS use in the study population (3.7%) and the highly asymmetric distribution of this binary outcome (Supplementary information 4), regression analyses were conducted using a Robust Complementary Log–Log (CLogLog) link, which is particularly appropriate for highly imbalanced binary outcomes and rare events [33–39]. Unlike the logit link, which is symmetric and may underestimate event probabilities in rare events settings, the cloglog link provides an asymmetric response function better suited for modelling probabilities concentrated near 0 or 1 [36, 37, 40, 41]. Quasi-complete separation was addressed through category aggregation for selected categorical variables exhibiting sparse data and random zero cells, including “status of life” and “feelings when facing life’s frustrations and difficulties”. Variables with structural zero cells due to institutional design (school) were described but excluded from regression analyses. To strengthen inference in this rare-events context, Bootstrap resampling with 5,000 replications was applied to assess the stability of estimates with respect to sampling variability, reduce reliance on asymptotic assumptions, and mitigate sensitivity to potentially influential observations [42–45]. Regression coefficients (B) with corresponding 95% CIs were also reported. In addition, a sensitivity analysis using Firth’s penalised logistic regression with profile likelihood confidence intervals [46] was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings to rare-event bias and potential separation.

Associations between independent variables and alcohol abuse were examined using Robust (modified) Poisson regression because alcohol use was a common outcome in the study population (32.7%). In this context, logistic regression is known to overestimate effect sizes when odds ratios are interpreted as prevalence ratios. The modified Poisson approach allows direct estimation of prevalence ratios with robust variance correction, offers improved numerical stability compared with log-binomial models, and is widely recommended for cross-sectional studies with binary outcomes and high outcome prevalence [45, 47–53]. RPR does not require the Poisson distribution assumption [52, 53] and remains reliable with sample sizes as small as 100 [49]. All independent variables were first examined in univariate analyses, and full univariate results are reported. Univariate analyses included hypothesized predictors (sociodemographic, individual and harm reduction factors) and a priori predictors (family relationships). Variables associated with the outcome at p < 0.20 [54] in univariate analyses were subsequently entered simultaneously into the multivariable models using the entry method. No automatic forward or backward selection procedure was applied. Results were reported as adjusted prevalence ratios (aPR) with 95% confidence intervals.

In order to ensure full control over the reference category, categorical predictors with more than two categories were dummy coded, with one category serving as the reference. This strategy allows estimation of category-specific effects without imposing arbitrary ordering and was applied consistently in both univariate and multivariable analyses, with selection performed at the level of the original categorical variable [55–57].

Concerning Diagnostics, Multicollinearity was checked using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance [58, 59], and results shown no evidence of collinearity (Supplementary information 5). Linearity between “age” and each outcome was assessed using the Box–Tidwell procedure [60], a standard diagnostic method for testing non-linearity in continuous predictors. Results indicate that the linearity assumption was reasonably met (Supplementary information 5).

All tests were two-sided with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Results were presented in tables and figures. Statistical analyses were primarily conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27, including Robust Poisson Regression and Complementary Log–Log models with robust standard errors and Bootstrap resampling. Firth’s penalised logistic regression, used to address rare-event bias, was performed using XLSTAT version 19 (Premium), as this procedure is not natively supported in standard SPSS. Relevant syntax and output files are provided in the Supplementary information (Supplementary Information 6–7).

Qualitative data

Open-ended responses related to types of drugs known and perceived effects of use were analysed using a combined thematic–lexical co-occurrence approach.

An initial inductive thematic coding process was conducted through repeated reading of responses, allowing codes to emerge directly from participants’ discourse. During this process, synonymous and conceptually equivalent expressions were systematically harmonised into unified codes, ensuring semantic consistency across the corpus. The coding framework was iteratively refined through team discussions to clarify code definitions, merge overlapping categories, and ensure contextual relevance. Coding validity was ensured through a consensus-based validation process involving multiple members of the research team, rather than formal inter-coder reliability statistics. This approach was considered appropriate given the exploratory nature of the analysis and the short, open-ended textual responses. Findings from the lexical co-occurrence analysis were subsequently triangulated with thematic interpretation, allowing convergence between quantitative co-occurrence patterns and qualitative meaning.

Lexical co-occurrence analysis focused on the co-occurrence of thematic codes within textual units. The C-coefficient (Jaccard coefficient), a symmetric normalised co-occurrence index [61–63], was used to assess thematic proximity by quantifying how often and how consistently two codes co-occur across the corpus. Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [66] and the ethical guidelines outlined by the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) [67]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee for Research on Human Health of the University of Douala, Cameroon (Ref. N° 2748 IEC-UD/07/2021/T).

Written informed parental consent was obtained for all participants prior to enrolment. In addition, each adolescent provided written informed assent after receiving a clear explanation of the study objectives, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality safeguards. No personally identifiable information was collected from either students or their parents.

To ensure ethical follow-up, a toll-free helpline funded by the principal investigator was made available throughout data collection and for one additional month. This service provided accurate information on psychoactive substances and allowed participants to seek guidance or referral if needed.

Thematic co-occurrence of psychoactive substance knowledge among adolescents

Co-occurrence analysis matrix (Supplementary Information 8–9) revealed a structured pattern in adolescents’ knowledge of psychoactive substances, characterised by associations of varying strength and thematic integration. The strongest association was observed between marijuana and cocaine, with 64 co-mentions and a high Jaccard coefficient (C = 0.24), indicating a robust and stable cognitive linkage. This pair constitutes the central hub around which knowledge of other illicit substances is organised.

More moderate co-occurrence patterns were identified between cocaine and tramadol (16 co-mentions; C = 0.09) and between marijuana and tramadol (11 co-mentions; C = 0.05), suggesting that tramadol is cognitively associated with major illicit substances in adolescents’ representations. In contrast, associations involving alcohol and tobacco/cigarettes exhibited low Jaccard coefficients (C ranging from 0.02 to 0.06), despite relatively high frequencies of mention. These patterns indicate that alcohol and tobacco are widely known but function primarily as contextual reference substances rather than as structuring elements within the co-occurrence network.

Substances that were infrequently mentioned overall, such as hallucinogens, heroin, and sedatives, showed very low or negligible co-occurrence values (C ≤ 0.01), reflecting a marginal and weakly structured knowledge of these substances among adolescents.

Complementary analysis using the T coefficient (Supplementary Information 9) strengthened this interpretation by highlighting directional thematic structuring beyond simple co-occurrence frequency. Notable directional associations were observed for cocaine–tramadol (T = 0.219) and marijuana–heroin (T = 0.339). The latter association, despite the low frequency of heroin mentions, suggests that heroin is primarily conceptualised through its linkage with marijuana in adolescents’ cognitive frameworks. Moderate T values for alcohol–marijuana (T = 0.206) and alcohol–cocaine (T = 0.167) further support the interpretation of alcohol as a background or gateway reference within substance-related knowledge. Additionally, associations involving tobacco with cocaine (T = 0.182) and tramadol (T = 0.133) indicate its integration into broader themes related to exposure, risk-taking, and initiation.

Overall, the combined interpretation of C and T coefficients indicates that adolescents’ knowledge of psychoactive substances is organised around a central marijuana–cocaine axis, with secondary associations involving tramadol, while licit substances serve as contextual anchors and rarer illicit substances remain peripheral within the cognitive structure of substance-related representations.

Perceived consequences and motivations for use drugs

Adolescents most frequently associated drug use with madness, death, mental disorder, cancer, and lung disease. Other cited effects included addiction, bad behaviour, stress, school dropout, and organ damage, while a few responses (e.g., “powerful,” “courage”) suggested perceived benefits (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of substance use among school-going adolescents in Yaoundé by combining prevalence estimates, cognitive representations of drugs, and multivariable analyses of associated factors. Approximately one in three adolescents reported alcohol use in the past 12 months, whereas illegal psychoactive substance use remained relatively uncommon. Beyond prevalence, adolescents organised psychoactive substances through shared cognitive representations, with marijuana and cocaine emerging as dominant conceptual hubs and alcohol occupying a distinct position within these representations. Although perceived consequences were predominantly negative, limited positive representations were also reported. Multivariable analyses revealed an asymmetric cross-sectional statistical association between alcohol use and illegal psychoactive substance (PAS) use among school-going adolescents. When illegal PAS use was modelled as the outcome, alcohol use was inversely associated with the prevalence of illegal substance use (aPR = 0.39). Conversely, when alcohol use was modelled as the outcome, illegal PAS use was positively associated with the prevalence of alcohol consumption (aPR = 2.16). Because this analysis is cross-sectional, these bidirectional estimates must be interpreted as correlational rather than as evidence of direction, sequence, or mechanism, and they are fully compatible with the co-existence of the two behaviours within the same small subgroup of adolescents (11 of 13 illicit-substance users also reported alcohol use). Sensitivity analyses using bootstrap resampling and Firth’s penalised regression showed that alcohol use was the only association consistently retained for illegal PAS use, whereas associations with educational level observed in the primary complementary log–log model were not consistently reproduced. For alcohol use, increasing age, availability of drugs around the home, and illegal PAS use remained independently associated. Family relationship variables were not independently associated with either alcohol use or illegal PAS use after adjustment.

Environmental context, substance-use trajectories, and adolescents’ cognitive representations

The high prevalence of alcohol use likely reflects its legal status, widespread availability, and cultural normalisation within households and neighbourhoods [68]. In Cameroon, alcohol consumption among adolescents is often embedded in social and sports-related youth settings, where binge drinking practices have been reported, contributing to early exposure and a reduced perception of risk [69]. This contextual normalisation plausibly explains why availability around the home, rather than at school, emerged as a key determinant of alcohol use in the adjusted model. In contrast, the relatively low prevalence of illegal psychoactive substance use may be explained by stricter social sanctioning, legal consequences, and underreporting in school settings, where disclosure of illegal behaviours may be particularly sensitive. Age-related increases in alcohol use are consistent with growing autonomy, peer exposure, and experimentation during adolescence [70]. The apparent protective association between alcohol use and illegal psychoactive substance use should not be interpreted as a causal protective effect. Adolescence is a critical period for the initiation of substance use, during which alcohol tend to appear earlier than illicit drugs, reflecting distinct substance-use trajectories rather than systematic overlap with illegal substance use [71]. Although higher illegal substance use was observed among students in lower grades in the primary model, this association was not robust to bootstrap resampling and penalised regression, suggesting sensitivity to sampling variability and limited statistical support rather than a stable underlying association. The independent associations observed for alcohol use likely reflect the predominance of proximal and contextual determinants over more distal relational factors [72]. Increasing age is commonly associated with greater autonomy, expanded peer networks, and increased exposure to drinking opportunities [71], while the availability of drugs around the home directly facilitates access and normalisation of alcohol use [73]. The inverse cross-sectional association between alcohol use and illegal psychoactive substance use should not be interpreted as a causal protective effect. One hypothesis consistent with existing longitudinal evidence is that of trajectory differentiation, whereby adolescents in alcohol-dominant trajectories remain within socially regulated patterns less likely to extend to illegal substance use [74]; however, the present cross-sectional design does not allow this hypothesis to be formally tested. By contrast, the absence of independent associations with family relationship variables after adjustment indicates that, once age, environmental exposure, and concurrent substance use are accounted for, reported family relational factors do not exert a measurable independent effect on alcohol or illegal substance use in this population. This finding suggests that, in this setting, immediate environmental availability and behavioural contexts may outweigh family relational characteristics in shaping adolescent substance-use behaviours.

The co-occurrence patterns observed may reflect the way adolescents are exposed to and socialised into substance-related narratives. Alcohol, marijuana, and cocaine are often discussed together in peer interactions, media content, and prevention messages, which may contribute to their cognitive clustering in adolescents’ mental representations. Alcohol’s frequent presence in family and community settings likely reinforces its role as a familiar reference point, facilitating its symbolic linkage with other psychoactive substances even when patterns of use differ.

More broadly, these cognitive associations suggest that adolescents conceptualise psychoactive substances as part of a shared behavioural and symbolic continuum, rather than as isolated categories. This structuring may shape how risks, experimentation, and transitions between substances are perceived, highlighting the importance of addressing adolescents’ interpretive frameworks, not only behaviours, in prevention efforts.

Alcohol dominance, substance-use trajectories, and the limited role of family relationships among adolescents

Evidence from Cameroon consistently shows that alcohol use is more prevalent than illegal psychoactive substance use among school-going adolescents, although estimates vary by study and recall period. Isabelle et al. reported very high alcohol use (91%) alongside low illegal substance use (5%) [12] while Kingsley et al. found similarly high six-month alcohol use (74.4%) but low cannabis and tramadol use (2.45%) [10]. In contrast, Ekwoge et al. observed more moderate lifetime alcohol prevalence (32% and 45%) across two Yaoundé schools, with consistently low lifetime prevalence of tramadol, marijuana, and inhalants (1.1–1.6%) [9]. Despite this variability, alcohol remains consistently far more prevalent than illegal psychoactive substances among Cameroonian adolescents. Comparable patterns are reported elsewhere in Africa, with pooled data from eight sub-Saharan countries showing higher current alcohol use (11.3%) than marijuana (2.0%) or lifetime amphetamine use (2.6%) [75], reinforcing the view that alcohol is the most socially embedded adolescent substance in school settings, while illegal substances remain comparatively marginal.

Regarding perceived consequences, our findings are consistent with qualitative evidence from Colombia showing that adolescents largely perceive psychoactive substance use as harmful to academic performance, peer relations, and health [76]. Our study further highlights the cognitive and emotional structuring of these perceptions, with adolescents linking substance use to internal psychological processes in addition to observable outcomes. The presence of limited positive representations suggests ambivalence rather than lack of awareness, illustrating the added value of lexical approaches for capturing the multidimensional nature of adolescents’ perceptions.

The inverse cross-sectional association between alcohol use and illegal psychoactive substance use observed here should not be interpreted as evidence against a gateway pathway per se, nor as a causal protective effect; rather, it is consistent with the increasingly documented heterogeneity of adolescent substance-use trajectories. Classical linear gateway models [77] have been substantially refined by latent class and latent transition analyses showing that alcohol-dominant trajectories — characterised by regular alcohol use without progression to illicit substances — coexist with distinct high-risk polysubstance trajectories [74, 78, 79]. In sub-Saharan African adolescent populations specifically, pooled data from eight countries document markedly higher current alcohol use than marijuana or amphetamine use [75], consistent with a dominant alcohol-only trajectory in school-going samples. Within this framework, our cross-sectional findings are compatible with membership in alcohol-dominant trajectories rather than with linear progression, although, as a cross-sectional study, we cannot formally distinguish these possibilities and our interpretation therefore remains hypothesis-generating.

Across settings, adolescent alcohol use has been associated with older age, male sex, peer and parental alcohol use, school-related factors, and engagement in other risk behaviours, including concurrent illicit drug use [80–88]. Our study specifically operationalised family relationship quality (father, mother, siblings) alongside parental substance-use history, an approach rarely implemented in prior SSA school-based studies, which typically focus on parental alcohol use or monitoring rather than on relational quality [80–88]. Despite this analytical focus, none of the family relationship variables remained independently associated with alcohol use or illegal psychoactive substance use in the adjusted models, even though several were associated at the univariable level. This empirical pattern indicates that, in this urban Cameroonian sample, proximal contextual and behavioural factors, age, home availability of substances, and concurrent alcohol/illicit use, statistically dominated relational factors once joint adjustment was performed. We interpret this cautiously: family relationships remain conceptually and theoretically central to adolescent development and substance use [89–101], but our cross-sectional adjusted findings suggest that, under the specific conditions sampled here (urban, private schools, COVID-19 context, small number of illicit users), proximal environmental exposure carries the bulk of the observable statistical signal. Longitudinal and multi-site designs will be needed to re-examine the role of relational family variables once environmental exposure is held constant.

Public health implications for adolescent substance-use prevention

These findings have important implications for adolescent substance-use prevention in urban Cameroon. First, alcohol use should be prioritised as a central entry point for intervention, given its high prevalence and central position within adolescent substance-related knowledge structures and its statistical centrality in our multivariable models. Previous longitudinal research has identified alcohol as an early substance in many adolescent trajectories, supporting this prioritisation [102–104]; in our cross-sectional data, the central statistical position of alcohol use within adolescent substance-related patterns is consistent with this literature but does not itself establish a temporal sequence.

Second, because home availability of substances, not family relationship quality, emerged as the principal proximal environmental correlate of alcohol use in our adjusted analyses, regulatory and community-level strategies aimed at reducing household alcohol accessibility and visibility should be emphasised. Family-based prevention programmes such as the Strengthening Families Program [105–107] can complement these environmental approaches by strengthening parental monitoring and communication, although the added value of family-focused interventions in contexts comparable to ours should be evaluated empirically rather than assumed from our cross-sectional findings.

Third, preventive interventions should begin early in secondary school, targeting younger adolescents before risk behaviours become established. School-based programmes that integrate social-skills training, emotional regulation, and peer-norm correction, such as the Life Skills Training model through Cooperative Learning (CL), have shown effectiveness in reducing substance-use initiation among adolescents [108, 109].

Finally, the cognitive clustering of substances observed in this study suggests that prevention initiatives should address substance use holistically rather than focusing on individual drugs. Integrated prevention frameworks recommended by international public health agencies emphasise addressing multiple substances simultaneously within broader behavioural and social contexts [110, 111].

Limitations and strengths

Given the very small number of illegal psychoactive substance users observed in this sample (n = 13), the adjusted estimates for this outcome should be interpreted as exploratory. Although the complementary log–log link, BCa bootstrap resampling (5,000 replications) and Firth’s penalised logistic regression were applied specifically to mitigate rare-event bias and potential quasi-complete separation, the limited number of events inherently constrains statistical precision. This is particularly evident for categorical predictors with sparse cells, for which bootstrap confidence intervals were wide and the associations observed in the primary model (e.g., educational level) attenuated under sensitivity analyses. Effect sizes and confidence intervals reported here for illegal psychoactive substance use should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, and replication in larger adolescent samples, ideally multi-site and longitudinal, is warranted before any firm inference is drawn.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and all reported associations should be interpreted as non-causal relationships. Second, reliance on self-reported data may have introduced recall and social desirability bias, particularly for illegal psychoactive substance use in a school context. Third, the sample was drawn from only three private secondary schools within an urban district of Yaoundé using a hybrid convenience–purposive–random sampling approach dictated by COVID-19-related school closures. This introduces a clear urban and private-school selection bias: adolescents from public schools, rural areas, and out-of-school populations, who typically differ in socioeconomic background, parental education, and exposure to home and community substance availability, are not represented in our sample. As a consequence, our prevalence estimates may under-represent illicit-substance exposure relative to public or out-of-school adolescents, while possibly over-representing alcohol use patterns associated with higher disposable income, and the observed associations should be generalised with caution beyond comparable urban private-school settings. Furthermore, despite achieving the required sample size, COVID-19-related absenteeism on data-collection days may have introduced additional, though likely limited, selection bias. Because several constructs were assessed using adapted or non-validated items, measurement error and non-differential misclassification cannot be excluded and may have attenuated observed associations.

This study has several strengths, including the combined use of quantitative and qualitative approaches, the application of robust and complementary regression methods, and the explicit consideration of family and environmental factors. Finally, the low prevalence of illegal psychoactive substance use constrained statistical power to detect weaker associations. To address this, complementary sensitivity analyses, including bootstrap resampling and Firth’s penalised regression, were conducted to assess the robustness of findings.

Conclusion

This study provides an overview of psychoactive substance use among secondary school adolescents in Yaoundé, Cameroon. Alcohol use was common, while illegal psychoactive substance use was less frequent but remains a concern given early initiation. Adolescents’ representations reflected both prevention messages and peer-driven meanings, highlighting the symbolic dimension of substance use. Multivariable analyses identified alcohol use as statistically central to adolescent substance-use patterns in this sample, associated primarily with age and environmental availability rather than with family relationship variables, although the cross-sectional design precludes inference about temporality or causation. These findings support prevention strategies that prioritise early, school-based interventions targeting alcohol use, address environmental and household exposure beyond schools, and incorporate adolescents’ cognitive and emotional frameworks, while retaining family-informed components as theoretically justified complements. Longitudinal, multi-site prospective studies are needed to clarify substance-use trajectories, re-examine the role of family relationship quality once environmental exposure is accounted for, and evaluate the short- and long-term effects of integrated interventions such as the Strengthening Families Program and Life Skills Training through Cooperative Learning in the Cameroonian context.
