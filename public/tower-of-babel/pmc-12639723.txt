Food-induced phenotypic plasticity in echinoid larvae is inducible and reversible throughout development
Authors: Ariana Lee, Isabel Villafuerte, Sarah Kasem, Ethan Nguyen, Bruno Pernet, Douglas A Pace
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEchinoid larvae are known to display food-induced phenotypic plasticity, where larvae alter their morphology and physiology to reflect available food levels. The aim of this study was to describe the induction and reversal of morphological and physiological plasticity through the entirety of larval development of the sand dollar Dendraster excentricus.ResultsWhen larvae fed a low algal ration were switched to high food conditions at 10-, 20-, and 30-day post-fertilization, we observed rapid induction of the high-fed phenotype as indicated by decreases in postoral arm length (POAL) as well as changes in assimilation and growth efficiencies. Switched larvae required more time to develop, but due to physiological changes in assimilation efficiency, they expended the same total amount of energy to achieve metamorphic competence as constantly high-fed larvae. These morphological results were also confirmed by tracking individual larvae in separate experiments. When larvae were switched from high to low food conditions, short-armed larvae rapidly experienced significant increases in POAL.ConclusionsThese results establish that plasticity responses in echinoid larvae are both reversible and inducible throughout larval development and result in significant adaptive benefits in terms of energy use. Our single larval experiments facilitate future analyses that can explore the genetic and molecular underpinnings of phenotypic plasticity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02447-w.Keywords: Echinoderm, Inducible plasticity, Dendraster excentricus, Energetics, Plasticity, Larva, Physiological efficiency

Background

Phenotypic plasticity is a ubiquitous phenomenon that allows individual organisms to alter their phenotype in response to environmental changes [1]. Given the importance of this phenomenon as an adaptive response mechanism to changes in biotic and abiotic conditions, understanding its regulation and downstream consequences is of great interest to the fields of evolution [2], ecology [3], and physiology [4]. There is evidence that plasticity responses during development (i.e., developmental plasticity) are relegated to specific developmental periods called critical windows [5–8]. During such critical windows, organisms have the capacity to sense and respond to environmental changes by altering attributes of their phenotype. A question that emerges from these observations is if organisms can reverse their phenotype after switching or do they exhibit irreversible plasticity [9]. Irreversible plasticity could lead to maladaptive consequences if subsequent environmental conditions changed in a way that resulted in the switched phenotype being mismatched with the new environment.

Phenotypic plasticity in feeding marine invertebrate larvae has been studied for more than 30 years [10–17]. These studies have revealed that when larvae develop the ability to feed, they can sense the amount of food available and adaptively change their morphology to match food levels. There is additional evidence that larvae can also detect food levels prior to feeding ability and adjust their morphology in anticipation of food levels when they reach a feeding stage [18]. These morphological responses typically involve changes in the relative size of the larval feeding structure where low food conditions result in larger feeding structures to increase feeding capacity. The majority of work on this topic has focused on the pluteus larvae of echinoid echinoderms (e.g., [12, 13, 18–23]). Pluteus larvae develop from relatively small eggs and sequentially grow up to four pairs of ciliated appendages (arms). These larval arms are used for locomotion and the generation of a feeding current so that unicellular algae can be directed to the mouth for ingestion [24]. In these larvae, the food-induced plasticity response is characterized by larvae in low food conditions growing longer arms, supporting a longer ciliated band, relative to larvae in high food conditions [12, 13]. Increased ciliary band length in low food conditions is considered adaptive because it increases particle capture rate, partially compensating for reduced food availability [12]. In conjunction with these morphological changes, low-fed larvae also have higher physiological growth efficiencies compared to high-fed larvae [25, 26]. In an important study examining plasticity in purple urchin (Strongylocentrotus purpuratus) larvae [27], it was established that morphological plasticity is mediated by a dopamine signaling pathway. If dopamine signaling was inhibited, larvae exhibited the long-arm, low-fed phenotype, regardless of available food levels. Thyroid hormone signaling also acts as an important regulator of both larval and juvenile skeletal structures during echinoderm development [28, 29]. Heterochronic shifts in development that mimic the food-induced plasticity response have been observed in echinoid larvae exposed to exogenous thyroid hormone [30]. How dopamine and thyroid hormone signaling relate to each other in coordinating an integrated plasticity response remains unknown.

Despite numerous descriptions of food-induced larval plasticity (especially morphological) in echinoid larvae (sea urchins and sand dollars) (e.g., [18–23, 25, 26, 31–34]), many critical questions remain regarding its adaptive significance. The period of analysis for many of these studies is significantly shorter than the length of time required for the larvae to achieve metamorphic competence (defined as the ability to respond to environmental settlement cues by undergoing metamorphosis). Without assessing plasticity responses throughout the entire larval period, it is impossible to assess their full adaptive significance. Additionally, the extent of phenotypic switching in these larvae is unknown as most studies (including those of the current authors) only examine plasticity responses to differential feeding treatments established at the onset of feeding ability (e.g., [12, 13, 21, 25–27, 31]). Previous research [18] has shown that prefeeding stages can sense food levels, resulting in adaptive changes to larval morphology upon reaching the feeding stage. Despite these important observations, how long larvae can sense and respond to changes in food level after feeding commences is unknown. Given that algal abundance in marine habitats is known to vary both spatially and temporally [35, 36], the adaptive potential of food-induced phenotypic plasticity must be considered against its ability to respond (i.e., induce the long arm phenotype into the short arm phenotype or reverse the short arm phenotype into the long arm phenotype) when feeding conditions change. Otherwise, the inability for phenotypic induction or reversal after feeding ability is attained might result in phenotype-environment mismatches [3, 37].

The goal of this study was to examine the potential for the induction and reversal of phenotypic plasticity as a result of changing food levels throughout larval development of the sand dollar Dendraster excentricus. Sand dollar larvae, in particular, have been useful for studying food-induced plasticity responses [12, 13, 18, 21, 25, 26, 33, 34, 38]. In order to examine phenotypic switching, our experimental design relied on the finding of Adams et al. [27] which defined the developmental “default” pathway being low-fed larvae with long arms and the induced pathway (by way of dopamine signaling linked to algal food ingestion) being the short-armed phenotype (as described above). We had three hypotheses: (1) larvae possess the ability to induce the short arm, high-fed phenotype throughout development, (2) the induction of the short-armed phenotype is part of a larger integrated response that also involves simultaneous changes in physiological growth efficiency, and (3) high-fed, short-armed larvae are able to reverse to the long-armed phenotype when food levels are reduced. Hypotheses 1 and 2 were pursued in large-scale cultures following population-level responses in both morphology and physiology. Due to the importance of the first hypothesis, we also tracked the morphological responses of individually cultured larvae to confirm population-level data. Despite the rich history of morphological plasticity studies in pluteus larvae (referenced above), we are unaware of any studies following plasticity responses in individually tracked larvae. Given the increased capacity to track physiological, biochemical, and molecular attributes of microorganisms, the ability to assess plasticity responses in single larvae would represent a powerful and novel direction for future studies of the mechanistic basis of larval plasticity. Hypothesis 3 was pursued in independent cultures where only morphological changes in the population were assessed.

Our results show that food-induced plasticity is inducible and reversible throughout larval development, and that these changes involve both morphological remodeling and significant changes in physiological growth efficiency. We also demonstrate that the culture and tracking of individual larvae are feasible, thereby making experimental approaches that address phenotypic variation on the level of individual larvae accessible. This study expands our knowledge of food-induced plasticity in echinoid larvae and provides new experimental approaches for studying its ecological and evolutionary significance and its mechanistic underpinnings.

Results

Inducible plasticity response: switching from low to high food concentrations

Morphological plasticity and metamorphic competence

Postoral arm length (POAL) in low-fed larvae increased faster than in high-fed larvae (Fig. 1A), while midline body length (MBL) increased faster in high-fed larvae (Fig. 1B). The different growth patterns of low- and high-fed larvae were clearer when POAL growth was standardized to midline body length (MBL) (Fig. 1C). These data illustrate the canonical plasticity response in pluteus larvae, where low food conditions result in larvae growing longer arms, both absolutely and relative to midline body length, compared to high-fed larvae [12, 25, 26]. Stomach size (SS) showed a similar pattern to MBL, with low-fed larvae maintaining small stomachs compared to high-fed larvae, whose stomachs grew rapidly over development (Fig. 1D).

Larvae switched from low to high food concentrations at 10-, 20-, and 30-day post-fertilization (DPF) (hereafter referred to as SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30, respectively) were at progressively more advanced developmental stages at the time of the switch (labels on Fig. 1A and images in Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Larvae switched at 10 DPF (SW-10) were mostly at the early six-arm stage and had no visible rudiment. Larvae at 20 and 30 DPF (SW-20 and SW-30, respectively) were at the eight-arm stage and possessed significant rudiment development (Additional file 1: Fig. S1, green arrows; comparable to stages ii–iii as defined by [39]). Comparison of rudiment size for the SW-20 and SW-30 treatments at the time of switching from low food to high food conditions revealed similar rudiment sizes, despite the 10-day difference in switch times (Additional file 2: Table S1, t [4] = − 0.37; P > 0.05). Larvae in all switched treatments displayed a similar set of morphological responses to increased food levels: POAL (Fig. 1A) rapidly decreased, while MBL and SS increased (Fig. 1B and D). In the span of 10 days from switching food levels for each of the switch treatments, the average decrease in POAL was 84 µm for SW-10 (16% decrease from 10 DPF of 522-µm POAL), 180 µm for SW-20 (31% decrease from 20 DPF of 583 µm), and 215 µm for SW-30 (37% decrease from 30 DPF of 578 µm). Changes in POAL-MBL ratios demonstrated that not only did absolute POAL decrease but also POAL became smaller relative to MBL (Fig. 1C). Comparison of POAL-MBL ratios between switched larval treatments 5 days after switching and same-age low-fed larvae revealed that switched SW-10 (15 DPF) and SW-20 (25 DPF) larvae had significantly lower ratios than constant low-fed larvae (Table 1). Despite a more than 65% reduction in POAL-MBL ratio, larvae switched at 30 DPF were not significantly different from low-fed larvae at 35 DPF, due to the high amount of among-culture variation in low-fed larvae at 35 DPF. Visual inspection of switched larvae (from all treatments) confirmed that larval arms were becoming shorter, and that growth was focused on the larval body where the stomach and rudiment were located. An example of this change is given in Fig. 2 showing the morphological changes in the SW-10 treatment relative to the constant low- and high-fed controls. Overall, when larvae were switched from low to high food concentrations, POAL decreased both absolutely and relatively, resulting in larvae that increasingly resembled constant high-fed larvae. Assessment of morphology when larvae reached 63% metamorphic competence (Table 2) revealed that despite larvae possessing differences in size and developmental stage when they were switched, upon reaching 63%, metamorphic competence larvae exhibited similar morphometric values. POAL was similar among high-fed, SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30 larvae (ANOVA, df = 3, 11; P > 0.05; Table 2). MBL values were similar in all treatments except for SW-10, which was significantly higher (ANOVA, df = 3, 11; P < 0.05; Table 2). However, comparison of POAL-MBL ratios found no significant differences among all four treatments. Likewise, SS was similar in all four treatments.

Changes in morphology in individually tracked larvae switched from low to high food conditions at 13 and 20 DPF (Fig. 3) showed the same pattern as in the population analysis. For larvae switched at 13 DPF, there was a significant decrease in POAL 6 days after switching (paired t-test for 13 and 19 DPF; t = 2.47, P < 0.05, n = 10). Likewise, larvae switched at 20 DPF showed significant decrease in POAL 6 days after being in high food conditions (paired t-test for 20 and 26 DPF; t = 3.22, P < 0.05, n = 8). The average decrease in POAL was 55 µm and 170 µm for the 13 and 20 DPF switches, respectively (Fig. 3A, blue lines; Additional file 1: Fig. S2 shows individual morphometric data for all larvae as well as sample photographs). MBL increased for all individuals with an average increase of 68 µm (n = 10) and 67 µm (n = 8) for 13 and 20 DPF switches, respectively. While a few individually tracked larvae exhibited no or slight positive change in POAL after the switch (Fig. 3A), all individuals from both switches exhibited a decrease in POAL-MBL ratio (Fig. 3C). The decrease in POAL-MBL ratio was significant for both switches (paired t-test: 13–19 DPF, t = 6.08, P < 0.001, n = 10; 20–26 DPF, t = 5.70, P = 0.001; n = 8). The average decrease in POAL-MBL ratio was 0.71 and 0.92 for 13 and 20 DPF switches (Fig. 3C), respectively.

Time to metamorphic competence

Time to metamorphic competence was dependent on feeding treatment. High-fed larvae and all low- to high-switched treatments reached almost 100% metamorphic competence within the timeframe of our experiments (up to 50 DPF). Low-fed larvae reached an average level of competence of 30% + / − 7.1% (SE; n = 4) by 50 DPF. Due to the change in rate processes associated with advanced rudiment development (i.e., decrease in ingestion rates), a developmental benchmark of 63% metamorphic competence was used for comparison of size and physiological rates among larval feeding treatments. High-fed larvae were at 63% competence at 16 DPF (dark horizontal line in Fig. 4). Similar competence levels for SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30 were attained at 25, 32, and 39 DPF, respectively (Fig. 4). Based on logistic regression, low-fed larvae were predicted to reach 63% competence at 58 DPF.

Physiological plasticity

Rates of algal ingestion (Fig. 5A), respiration (Fig. 5B), and protein growth (Fig. 5C) were assessed in the same high-fed, SW-10, SW-20, SW-30, and low-fed cultures as those used for the population morphology and competence assessments (Figs. 1, 2, and 4). For all three physiological measurements, constantly high- and low-fed larvae exhibited rates similar to those seen in previous analyses [25, 26]. All switched treatments (SW-10, SW-20, SW-30) exhibited immediate increases in ingestion rates upon increasing food levels from 1000 to 10,000 cells mL−1. Ingestion rates continued to increase after switching and were in a similar range as those of constantly high-fed larvae (Fig. 5A). Respiration rates for all low- to high-switched treatments increased rapidly when food concentrations were increased, reaching levels like those of constantly high-fed larvae (Fig. 5B). Protein biomass exhibited a similar trend, with switched larvae increasing immediately upon being switched to high food conditions and reaching protein biomass levels like those of high-fed larvae. Overall, larvae switched from low (1000 cells mL−1) to high (10,000 cells mL−1) demonstrated a rapid response to the increased food availability regardless of when the switch occurred.

Primary physiological data (Fig. 5A, B, C) were used to construct models of energy acquisition (ingestion), energy to fuel metabolism (respiration), and energy invested in growth (protein biomass). Additional file 1: Fig. S3 shows these models for all treatments and cultures. These models were used to determine energetic parameters for all replicate treatments and cultures at a standard developmental benchmark of 63% metamorphic competence (Fig. 4). The energetic cost of development (calculated by summing the daily metabolic energy used until 63% metamorphic competence was achieved; Fig. 6A) depended on feeding treatment (ANOVA, df = 4, 14; F = 30.4; P < 0.001; Additional file 2: Table S2). However, despite SW-10 requiring 9 additional days of development and SW-20 requiring 16 additional days of development, their total energetic costs of development were similar to those of constantly high-fed larvae (Tukey simultaneous test for difference in means; Additional file 2: Table S2) (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, all treatments that received high food concentrations at any point in their development (including SW-30) had lower costs of development than constantly low-fed larvae (Fig. 6A; Additional file 2: Table S2). Despite the large differences in time to achieve 63% metamorphic competence (16 DPF for high-fed, 39 DPF for SW-30, and 58 DPF for low-fed), comparison of protein biomass at 63% competence revealed no significant differences among treatments, including low-fed larvae (ANOVA, df = 4, 14; F = 1.61; P > 0.05) (Fig. 6B). The average protein biomass at this standardized developmental benchmark was 2405 ± 162 ng of protein larva−1 (mean ± SEM, n = 15).

Assimilation efficiency (AE) and net growth efficiency (NGE) in constantly high- and low-fed treatments were similar to those previously found using the same feeding treatments [25, 26]. High-fed larvae had an average assimilation efficiency (AE) for all of development of 27% ± 3.5% (mean ± SEM, n = 3), and low-fed larvae had an AE of 59% ± 9.7% (mean ± SEM, n = 3) (Fig. 6C). All switched treatments displayed significant changes in both AE and NGE values when switched from low to high food conditions (Additional file 2: Table S3). AE values prior to switching were similar at 43%, 40%, and 42% for SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30, respectively. When switched to high food conditions, these efficiencies decreased to similar levels for the remainder of development (i.e., time of switching until 63% metamorphic competence) to 17%, 23%, and 32% (Additional file 2: Table S3). Net growth efficiency (NGE) exhibited the opposite trend, with high-fed larvae having higher developmental efficiency values than low-fed larvae: 67% + / − 11.6% (SE) and 45% + / − 6.5% (SE), respectively. All switched treatments had relatively low NGE values when reared in low food conditions (44%, 40%, and 40% for SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30, respectively). After being switched to high food conditions, switched larvae had significantly higher NGE values (68%, 65%, 62% for SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30, respectively) (Fig. 6D and Additional file 2: Table S3). We also checked if changes in AE or NGE after switching were related to developmental time and not an outcome of changes in food amount. Comparison of constantly low-fed larvae analyzed at 10-, 20-, and 30-DPF (the times in which switched treatments were changed from low- to high-fed conditions) demonstrated that pre- and post-switched values were either in the opposite direction as those in the switched larval treatments (AE) or were similar to each other (NGE) (Additional file1: Fig. S4). Therefore, the changes observed in switched larvae were not an outcome of developmental time or stage but resulted from larvae being switched from low to high food conditions.

Reversible plasticity response: switching from high to low food concentrations

Regardless of culture method, larvae in high to low Exp-1 (Fig. 7 A and B) and in high to low Exp-2 (Fig. 7 C, D, and E) exhibited similar morphological responses when switched from high to low food conditions. Larvae from low-fed treatments had higher POAL than high-fed larvae both on an absolute scale and when standardized to MBL. In both experiments, larvae switched from high to low food conditions immediately increased POAL, both on an absolute scale (Fig. 7A and C) and when standardized to MBL (Fig. 7B and D). Repeated measures ANOVA (Additional file 2: Table S4) revealed that POAL values were not only significantly higher than the constant high-fed larvae but were also significantly higher than the constant low-fed larval treatment (ANOVA, Exp-1: df = 2, 98, F = 111, P < 0.001; Exp-2: df = 2, 220, F = 315, P < 0.001). Representative constant high-fed, constant low-fed, and high- to low-switched larvae at 13 DPF are shown in Fig. 7E.

Discussion

Induction of the high-fed phenotype

Previous research [27, 29, 30] on echinoid food-induced larval plasticity has discovered that both dopamine and thyroid hormone signaling are linked to sensory pathways that induce morphological plasticity, especially during early development when larvae first become able to feed on algae. Our objectives were to determine if (1) larvae possess the ability to induce the short arm, high-fed phenotype throughout development, (2) the induction of the short-armed phenotype is part of a larger integrated response that also involves changes in physiological growth efficiency, and (3) high-fed, short-armed larvae are able to reverse to the long-armed phenotype when food levels are reduced. Maintaining the ability to induce or reverse phenotype would be highly adaptive given the spatial and temporal variability of food abundance in marine habitats [36, 40]. Additionally, we sought to understand the plasticity phenomenon from both morphological and physiological perspectives during the entirety of larval development (functionally defined as the attainment of metamorphic competence).

Our results clearly demonstrate that there is no distinct critical window limiting when the induction of the high-fed phenotype can occur. Larvae switched from low to high food conditions either prior to or after significant rudiment development displayed a similar induction of the short-armed, high-fed phenotype. This induction of the high-fed phenotype involved rapid changes both in morphology and physiology, providing evidence that they work in tandem and are part of a unified phenotypic response system. Furthermore, switching high-fed larvae to low food conditions resulted in a morphological reversal from the high-fed phenotype to the low-fed phenotype.

The long-armed, low-fed phenotype enables larvae to increase their feeding capacity by increasing the length of their ciliated band [12]. We expected that once larvae were switched from low to high food concentrations, they would stop investing in further growth of their larval arms but continue taking advantage of increased feeding capacity provided by the already long arms. However, upon switching, the larvae exhibited rapid and significant decreases in postoral arm length. Thyroid hormone (TH) is a critical regulator of both larval and juvenile development in echinoids [28–30]. It has been observed that the use of exogenous TH can mimic the high food response resulting in the larvae with short arms and large rudiments [30]. This response is similar to our results in larvae switched from low to high food conditions (shortening arms and reaching metamorphic competence faster than constantly low-fed controls), thereby supporting the possibility that the TH in the ingested algae is serving as a signaling mechanism regulating the changes in larval morphology and developmental rate. However, it is difficult to know if this is the case since, as pointed out in other studies [12, 32], the presence of food is a confounding variable in this response. Also, unlike the TH response in which stomach size decreased [30], the switched larvae in our study experienced an increase in stomach size.

We further assessed the unexpected population-level result of significant arm shortening when switched to high food conditions by conducting an independent experiment in which individual larvae were tracked after being switched from low to high food concentrations. The results of this analysis are consistent with those of the population analysis: low-fed larvae reduced their postoral arm length when placed in high food conditions whether the switch was early (e.g., 10 DPF, no rudiment development) or late (e.g., 30 DPF with significant rudiment development) in larval development. Of particular interest is that while the overall morphological response in individually tracked larvae was similar to that seen at the population level, there was appreciable variation among individual larvae. If attributable to additive genetic variability, this individual variation in response suggests that plasticity may be susceptible to natural selection.

Previous research has indirectly demonstrated that arm growth in prefeeding larvae is energetically costly [27]. Our analysis extends this observation, demonstrating that even after attaining a given arm length, larvae quickly reduce arm length when placed in a high food environment. This indicates that the energetic expense of larval arms is not just a matter of their initial construction but also includes maintenance. This is also observed in the SW-30 treatment which, at the time it was switched from low to high food conditions (30 DPF), exhibited no increase in rudiment size compared to the SW-20 treatment when it was switched (20 DPF). This lack of rudiment size increase, despite having 10 extra days to grow, is likely related to the energy required to maintain relatively long postoral arms. High maintenance costs for long arms may result from increased ion regulatory needs due to the high surface area exposure in low-fed larvae. A positive correlation of Na+,K+-ATPase (NKA) activity and surface area has been observed in a range of biological systems [41–43]. The NKA pump is critical for ion regulation in echinoid larvae [44–46]. For temperate and Antarctic echinoids, the amount of total NKA enzyme and its in vivo activity increased significantly during development, in parallel with increasing larval surface area [46, 47]. Increases in postoral arm length in low-fed larvae likely involve a trade-off between greater feeding capacity [12] and increased energy use for ion regulation. Once low-fed larvae encounter a high food environment, the need for increased feeding capacity is alleviated, and the induction of the high-fed phenotype results in the reduction of arm length, perhaps as an energy-saving mechanism. It is also possible that the high rates of protein turnover previously observed in low-fed larvae [26] are related to the maintenance of long arms. Therefore, by reducing arm length upon switching to high food conditions, there is a reduction in energy use going to protein synthesis and degradation, both of which require large amounts of energy [48, 49].

Physiological responses to switching to high food conditions were also clear. The three primary physiological metrics in this study (ingestion rate, metabolic rate, and protein growth rate) all increased immediately upon switching. Efficiencies prior to switching were similar to those of constantly low-fed larvae. However, upon switching, efficiency values resembled those of high-fed larvae. The lower NGE values (the proportion of energy available for growth after metabolic expenses are met) for pre-switch low-fed larvae may result from the higher protein turnover rates, as previously observed [26], and ion regulatory costs of the long arms used to increase feeding capacity. Low-fed larvae had high assimilation efficiency even though they had small stomachs. Prior research has identified a negative correlation between postoral arm length and stomach size, where high-fed larvae have short arms and large stomachs and low-fed larvae have long arms and small stomachs [32, 50]. This pattern was also observed in the current study for all low- to high-switched treatments. This is sometimes interpreted as a trade-off where increasing ciliary band length occurs at the expense of digestive power [51]. Our data [25, 26] demonstrates that while stomach size is smaller in low-fed larvae, digestive capacity, as measured by assimilation efficiency, is not sacrificed commensurately. For a given amount of ingested food, low-fed larvae are able to use more of that energy to fuel growth and metabolism than high-fed larvae. Together, these responses have significant consequences for the overall energetic efficiencies of low-fed larvae (below). Upon switching to high food concentrations, stomach size increases, and assimilation efficiency decreases. This may reflect a lower investment in digestion and absorption per unit surface area of the digestive system due to benefits of having greater access to food.

The adaptive benefit of the relatively high assimilation efficiencies in low-fed treatments prior to switching may be the driving factor that results in a similar total cost of growth among the different feeding treatments (i.e., high-fed and SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30). Despite requiring an additional 9 and 16 days to achieve 63% metamorphic competence (high-fed = 16 DPF), the total energy consumed for the SW-10 and SW-20 treatments was similar to that of constantly high-fed larvae. Since NGE of low-fed larvae was lower than that of high-fed larvae, the energy savings were likely linked to increased assimilation efficiency values: for the same number of algal cells ingested, low-fed larvae were able to assimilate a greater fraction of that energy into growth and metabolism. The energy gained through increased assimilation efficiency was likely used to support major housekeeping metabolic processes, such as ion regulation and protein turnover [26] resulting from increased larval arm length. These results highlight that the low-fed plasticity response does not simply increase food acquisition from the environment (albeit modestly [12]) but also increases the efficient use of whatever food is acquired. Therefore, even though increases in arm length are not enough to compensate for the lower amount of food, there are physiological support mechanisms to further alleviate the consequences of the food-limiting environment. That morphological and physiological responses for all three switch treatments occurred simultaneously suggests that the food-induced plasticity response among these phenotypic levels is part of a coordinated response.

Reversible plasticity response: switching from high to low food levels

The response of high-fed larvae when switched to low food concentrations further demonstrates that regardless of developmental stage and previous nutritional history, echinoid larvae remain sensitive to changes in available food levels throughout their growth and development. Both high to low food switch experiments yielded similar results: not only did larvae respond to the switch by increasing arm length but also these responses resulted in greater POAL than that of the constantly low-fed treatments. This result suggests that there is not a developmentally predetermined maximum POAL. Lipids are critical biochemical reserves for fueling embryonic and larval development [45, 52–54]. Larvae (including those of D. excentricus) reared at high food concentrations can build up their lipid reserves [25, 45, 55]. The “overcompensation” of the postoral arm response in high-fed larvae switched to low food concentrations may be possible due to the buildup of energetic reserves during the high-fed period. A negative correlation between arm length and use of maternal lipid reserves has been observed in echinoid larvae, demonstrating both the cost of arm length growth and the use of high-energy lipid molecules to fuel it [27]. The rapid increase in arm length when switched to low food concentrations is likely supported by the lipids gained through past feeding. A previous study [33], examining plasticity responses from an evolutionary perspective, demonstrated that echinoid species with smaller eggs exhibit a greater degree of food-induced plasticity that occurred earlier in development relative to echinoid larvae developing from larger eggs (e.g., arm length negatively correlated with food availability). Our results show that plasticity responses within a single species are not entirely dependent on maternal endowment to the egg but can also be affected by larval nutritional history. The use of non-maternal resources to fuel plasticity adds further complexity to the relationship between egg size and the capacity for plasticity [51]. While the current study looked at plasticity reversal when food was switched early in larval development, it would be useful to know if such morphological changes occur when food is switched after significant rudiment development.

Ecological consequences of induction and reversal of phenotype

While morphological plasticity responses in echinoid larvae have been well-characterized (e.g., [12, 21–23, 31]), their significance in nature remains poorly understood. Food levels in the marine environment are variable over time and space [36, 40]. The adaptive value of food-induced plasticity responses is highly dependent on the response time required to change phenotype when algal abundance changes. A delayed response may result in a phenotype-environment mismatch (e.g., short arms in a low food environment), which could be maladaptive [3, 27, 37]. However, most prior research has focused on morphological effects of consistent food levels starting at the onset of feeding or just prior to the onset of feeding (reviewed in 51). A more thorough understanding of the plasticity response in larvae must include assessment of how larvae respond to changes in algal abundance at any given point during their development. To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to assess echinoid larval responses to increases and decreases in algal concentrations at different developmental time-points. It demonstrates that larvae are capable of food-induced plasticity throughout their development. These responses occurred at a variety of developmental stages (e.g., six-armed and eight-armed) including before and after the initiation of rudiment growth.

Miner and Vonesh [56] determined that sea urchin larvae were unable to adjust their morphology to match food concentrations that were varied every 2 days. Our approach precluded us from determining if significant changes occurred within 2 days of switching food levels. However, for both the induction (low to high switch) and the reversal (high to low switch) experiments, we observed clear changes in morphology. In the induction experiments, we also observed changes in physiological rates and efficiencies. These observations suggest that larval form and physiology respond to large changes in algal abundance range relatively quickly. It is clearly important to determine how larval response time compares with temporal variation in algal abundance in nature. If larvae experience variation in food levels at frequencies greater than their response time, their morphology and physiology may often not reflect current food conditions but those that the larvae encountered days ago. Further, the lag in larval response to changes in food level may complicate interpretation of studies that make inferences about food limitation in nature from the morphology of field-collected larvae (e.g., [57]) or of larvae reared in field-collected seawater (e.g., [38]).

An important question regarding plasticity in indirect developing organisms (i.e., organisms that possess a larval stage) is if (and when) plasticity in time to metamorphic competence is lost [58]. Different models have been developed to predict variation in time to and size at metamorphosis for a range of organisms with complex life cycles (amphibians: [59], copepods: [60], insects: [61], barnacles: [11]). Our data on sand dollars indicates that the timing of metamorphic competence remains resource dependent for most of the larval phase, meaning that this trait remains highly plastic throughout development. However, the similarity in both morphology (POAL, MBL, and POAL-MBL ratios) and protein biomass at metamorphic competence regardless of feeding history suggests that size at metamorphic competence is relatively fixed. These results demonstrate that while nutritional history will have consequences on the ability to achieve metamorphosis to the juvenile stage, its consequences are not carried over after attaining the juvenile stage (i.e., all juveniles are at a similar starting point in terms of size). Regardless of the variation a larva encounters during its development, there is a convergence upon a permissible size range in order to achieve metamorphic competence. Given that the rudiment makes up the majority of tissue biomass in late pluteus larvae, the similar value of protein in metamorphically competent larvae likely reflects the minimum rudiment size required for successful metamorphosis. Due to this convergence on a common size, our results suggest, for larvae of D. excentricus, that delayed settlement is not a factor that negatively impacts juvenile success.

Conclusions

The current study significantly expands our understanding of food-induced phenotypic plasticity during echinoid larval development, demonstrating the ability of larvae to rapidly change in both morphology and physiology throughout development. Importantly, this study quantitatively defines the energetic value of these changes relative to a common finish line in terms of metamorphic competence. While the low-fed phenotype does not fully compensate for reduced food levels, increased arm length in combination with increased assimilation efficiency allows these larvae to extend their larval duration, increasing their chances of encountering a better feeding environment. The adaptive value of this response is demonstrated in the similar total costs of development for constantly high-fed larvae and larvae that were fed 1/10th the food for much of their development. This research further highlights the usefulness of the echinoid larval system as a model for developmental plasticity. The ability to induce or reverse the plastic phenotype throughout development will be particularly helpful in exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying plasticity (e.g., [27, 62]) as well as the ability to track these changes in individual larvae.

Methods

Adult sand dollar collection and spawning

Adults of Dendraster excentricus were collected from San Pedro, CA, USA (33°42′ 30.2″ N, 118°16′ 39.1″ W). They were injected through the peristomial membrane with 1–2 mL of 0.5-M potassium chloride (KCl) to induce gamete release [19, 63]. Each culture was initiated using the sperm of three males and the eggs of three females to create a genetically diverse population. Concentrated sperm were diluted (1:1000) and added to the suspension of eggs until fertilization success (as determined by the presence of a fertilization envelope) was ≥ 90%. After fertilization, zygotes were initially stocked at five per milliliter in 0.2-µm pore-size filtered seawater (FSW) and kept at 16 °C (± 1 °C). This temperature was chosen as it represents temperatures characteristic of their spring spawning season [17] and is the same temperature used in previous studies of plasticity in these larvae [19, 25, 26]. At the feeding stage, 2-day post-fertilization (DPF), larvae were split into six 20-L vessels at a concentration of one individual per milliliter.

Larval culturing and sampling

All feeding treatments were kept in 20-L vessels with FSW at 16 °C (± 1 °C). Cultures were mixed using acrylic paddles rotated at 5 rpm by DC gear motors (Buehler Products, Kinston, NC, USA). Water changes were completed three times weekly by gently collecting larvae on a submerged 50-µm-pore mesh. Larvae were then resuspended in a small volume of FSW where their concentration was estimated by counting larvae in multiple samples of 100 µL. These values were used to estimate larval survivorship as well as the necessary volume to acquire a specific number of larvae for respiration or protein biomass measurements (described below).

Larvae were fed Rhodomonas sp., a unicellular alga commonly used for rearing echinoid larvae (e.g., [12, 25]). Algae were cultured in f/2 medium in Erlenmeyer flasks with sponge stoppers on a light cycle of 16 light-8 dark. Algae were harvested for feeding at the end of their logarithmic growth phase. Algal concentrations were determined using a BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences San Jose, CA, USA) [25, 64]. Algal concentrations in each culture vessel were checked by flow cytometry and restocked daily to the respective feeding treatment concentrations.

Inducible plasticity response (switching larvae from low to high food conditions)

Three independent cultures were used to assess the morphological and physiological consequences of switching larvae from low to high food conditions. Each independent culture consisted of five different feeding treatments: constant high-fed (10,000 cells of Rhodomonas sp. per mL), constant low-fed (1000 cells per mL), and three low to high food switched treatments. The switched treatments received a low food diet (1000 cells mL−1) before switching to a high food diet (10,000 cells mL−1) at 10, 20, or 30 DPF (hereafter referred to as SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30, respectively) (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A). The low- and high-fed treatment levels were used as they represent food availability levels that result in the typical plasticity response where low-fed have long arms and high-fed have short arms, and the low-fed treatment still results in positive biomass growth [25, 26]. Prior to switching food levels, larvae in switch treatments were pooled with constant low-fed treatments. All treatments were reared in 20-L vessels. For all cultures, both morphology and physiology were monitored (described below). Cultures were terminated either at 50 DPF or when the majority of the culture reached metamorphic competence (as determined by competency assays, described below). Our objective was to find a standard developmental benchmark that reflected, for the population of each treatment, the near completion of larval development, thereby allowing for appropriate comparisons of morphological and physiological traits.

Larval morphometrics and development

To assess larval growth and morphological plasticity in response to feeding conditions, photos of at least 10 larvae were taken for each treatment every 3–5 days with additional photos taken 2-day post-switch. The larvae were relaxed with an equal volume of 7.5% MgCl2 and fixed with 10–15 µL of 16% paraformaldehyde before being placed on glass slides with a raised cover slip. Larvae were immediately photographed using an Olympus EP50 camera mounted on a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope. Postoral arm length (POAL) and midline body length (MBL) were measured as previously described [13, 25] (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). POAL length was defined as the length from the intersection of the postoral arm rod with the transverse rod to the end of the arm (i.e., where tissue stopped, which was always longer than the end of the arm rod) [30]. POAL was used as a proxy for ciliated band length [12, 25]. POAL-MBL ratios were calculated so that changes in POAL could be standardized to larval body size [25, 31]. Stomach size (SS) was determined by measuring the stomach length (SL) and stomach width (SW) (including the epithelial lining surrounding the stomach cavity) (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). Stomach size was then determined by taking the square root of the stomach area (assuming the shape of an ellipsoid where the stomach length and width are the ellipsoid axes) [30]. Rudiment size was measured for SW-20 and SW-30 treatments when they were switched from low to high food conditions (20 and 30 DPF, respectively). Rudiment size was not considered for SW-10 because at 10 DPF, this treatment had no rudiment. Rudiment size was calculated as the square root of the cross-sectional area using the rudiment length and width as the main axes values [30]. Larval dimensions were measured using FIJI [65] calibrated using an image of a stage micrometer taken under identical conditions as the larval images. To ensure no artifacts were introduced by way of fixing larval tissue, in a separate experiment, we compared all morphometric characters (POAL, MLB, POAL-MBL, SL, SW) in live larvae and in fixed larvae. No significant differences were observed for all measurements (P > > 0.05; data not shown). To further reduce the introduction of artifacts, all images were taken within 5 min of fixation.

T-tests were used to compare POAL-MBL ratios 5 days after switching from low to high food concentrations for each switched treatment (SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30). Each treatment was compared with the same-aged larvae from the constantly low-fed treatment (e.g., SW-10 was compared at 15 DPF with low-fed larvae at 15 DPF). A t-test was also used to compare rudiment size in SW-20 and SW-30 larvae when switched from low to high food conditions on the day each treatment was switched to high food conditions. Prior to these comparisons, assumptions of normality and equality of variances were confirmed (P > 0.05) using an Anderson–Darling test [66] and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance [67], respectively. T-tests were conducted using Minitab Statistical Software (v20.3).

Protein biomass

Triplicate aliquots of 300–500 larvae were collected from each treatment every 3–5 days for protein biomass analysis. Larvae were placed into 1.5-mL tubes and spun down at 1000 g at 4 °C for 2 min; excess seawater was then aspirated from the sample before storing the pelleted larvae at − 80 °C. Protein biomass was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) [68]. Frozen samples were resuspended in nanopure water and sonicated at 30% amplitude for three pulses of 3 s each while on ice (Fisher, Sonic Dismembrator). Assays were conducted per manufacturer’s instructions and absorbance quantified at 562 nm using a BioTek Synergy H1 Hybrid Plate reader. A standard curve using bovine serum albumin was used to relate absorbance with protein amount. Data were log10-transformed for statistical analyses to correct for non-linearity, non-normality, or unequal variances. Protein biomass at 63% metamorphic competence was compared among all treatments using a one-way ANOVA in which culture was a random factor and treatment was a fixed factor (Minitab Statistical Software v20.3). Assumptions of normality were confirmed (P > 0.05) using an Anderson–Darling test.

Ingestion rates

Daily ingestion rates (IR) were measured for all three cultures as previously described [25, 26]. Algal concentrations were measured daily by flow cytometry and used to record the number of algae eaten and to estimate the number of algal cells required to bring cultures back to treatment conditions. Algal concentration was determined by taking two samples from each culture and determining the average. Samples were acquired through a 50-μm-pore Nitex mesh to ensure no larvae were removed. “Ci” and “Cf” represent the initial and final algal concentration (algal cells mL−1) for each treatment. Initial algal concentration was measured after restocking to ensure algal concentrations were within 10% of the target. “t” is time between the initial and final algal measurements, “V” is the total volume in the culture vessels (20,000 mL), and n is the number of larvae in the experimental vessel. The number of larvae in each vessel was determined during every water change using volumetric sampling. For IR calculations on non-water change days, larval numbers were estimated by interpolating between sampling days using linear regression.

Respiration rates

Larval respiration rates were measured for all three low to high switch experiments using the µBOD method of Marsh and Manahan [69]. Larvae were enumerated and resuspended in oxygen-saturated FSW equilibrated to 16 °C. They were immediately loaded into seven µBOD vials (~ 500 µL), capped, and placed in a water bath at 16 °C. An additional three µBOD vials contained only seawater to account for non-larval oxygen consumption. Samples were incubated for 2–4 h depending on developmental stage. Oxygen was then measured using a polarographic oxygen sensor (Strathkelvin Instruments) calibrated by injecting oxygen-saturated FSW (16 °C) to set the maximum O2 concentration (249.8 µmol L−1). After incubation in the µBOD vials, larvae were centrifuged to the bottom of the µBOD vial and a water sample acquired using a 500-µL gas-tight Hamilton syringe. Approximately, 400 µL of sample was immediately injected into a Strathkelvin oxygen electrode chamber which held a volume of ~ 70 µL (injected volume was more than five times the sample port volume, ensuring adequate displacement of seawater already in the sample port). After injection, the electrode was given 1 min to stabilize before recording oxygen concentration (pmol O2 L−1). After oxygen measurements, the number of larvae in each vial was counted. Total oxygen consumed for each treatment (larval rates — control rates) was linearly regressed against the number of larvae in each vial; the resulting slope represented respiration rate in units of pmol O2 h−1 larva−1 [69].

Energetics analyses

Estimates of total cost of growth, assimilation efficiency (AE), and net growth efficiency (NGE) for all three cultures were made following the protocols of previous studies examining physiological energetic efficiencies in larvae of D. excentricus [25, 26]. AE and NGE were calculated using the following equations:

“I” represents exogenous energy acquired by the larvae, “M” represents the energy used to fuel growth and development, and “G” represents the energy invested into larval growth. Budgets were constructed using the following assumptions: (1) Energetic intake (I) was only through algal consumption, (2) rates of oxygen consumption represent total larval energy expenditure through metabolism (M), and (3) changes in total protein content throughout development represent energy allocated to growth (G). Daily rates of algal ingestion were converted to units of energy acquired (“I”) using a value of 2.25 µJ (algal cell)−1 based on the specific energetic content of Rhodomonas sp. [70]. Respiration rates were converted to energic units (“M”) using an oxyenthalpic conversion of 484 kJ mol O2−1 [71]. Protein growth rates were converted to energetic units using a conversion factor of 24 kJ g−1 of protein [71]. Growth in larvae of D. excentricus is predominantly through increasing protein biomass; carbohydrates represent less than 1% of biomass in marine invertebrate larvae [52, 53]. Lipid growth in larvae of D. excentricus is significantly slower relative to protein and only represents 5–7% of total larval biomass in low- and high-fed larvae [25]. From an energetic perspective, proteins are one of the most expensive types of macromolecules to synthesize and deposit, regardless of environmental conditions [48, 49]. Therefore, only protein biomass was considered for energetic efficiency calculations.

The primary physiological data of algal ingestion, respiration, and protein growth were used to create best-fit models for I, M, and G, respectively. For each replicate culture (N = 3) and treatment, primary physiological data were log10-transformed to correct for nonlinearity, non-normality, or unequal variances. These linear regression values (slope and y-intercept) were then used to model the change in each energetic variable (I, M, and R) throughout larval development. Time of development considered in these models ranged from the start of feeding (2 DPF) until the larval age when the treatment reached 63% metamorphic competence (this age varied by treatment). For constant high-fed and low-fed treatments, single continuous models were created. For switched larval treatments, two models were generated. The first model described the changes in the respective energetic parameter while being fed a low food ration (1000 cells mL−1). The endpoint of this model was the DPF when food was switched from low to high conditions (e.g., for SW-10, the pre-switch model ended at 10 DPF). The second model described changes when switched to a high food ration (10,000 cells mL−1). The endpoint for each model was when a metamorphic competence of 63% was achieved (determined through an induction assay, see below) or, in the case of low-fed larvae, when it was estimated to occur based on a linear extrapolation. For example, the first part of the SW-10 model ranged from 2 to 10 DPF (when they were receiving a low algal ration of 1000 cells mL−1) and the second part of the model from 10 to 25 DPF (when they were receiving a high algal ration of 10,000 cells mL−1). The individual models (I, M, and G) created from these estimates are shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S3. The final model for each variable was created by using the average of each of the three independent models.

The total cost of development was calculated by using the cumulative amount of energy consumed through metabolism throughout larval development (M). Efficiency values were calculated by summing the daily amounts of energy apportioned to each process (I, M, and R). These cumulative energetic values were then used to calculate AE and NGE. For constant low- and high-fed larvae, this required only one calculation (for each replicate culture) as the feeding treatments were constant throughout development. For the switched treatments (SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30) these energetic values were calculated twice throughout development: once while they were receiving the low algal ration of 1000 cell mL−1 (“pre-switch efficiency”) and once for the remaining period of development when they were receiving the high algal ration of 10,000 cells mL−1 (“post-switch efficiency”). For example, for SW-10, pre-switched estimate of M was the total amount of energy used at from 2 to 10 DPF; the post-switch estimate of M was from 10 to 25 DPF (the larval age when 63% metamorphic competence was achieved). To ensure that any pre- and post-switch differences were not driven by developmental changes independent of changes in food level, efficiency calculations were made in the constant low-fed treatment as well, using the same switch time points as the SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30 treatments. Similar approaches to modeling the cost of growth and growth efficiency have been used in numerous studies in a wide range of organisms (e.g., [68, 72–76]).

Comparison of the total energetic cost of development was conducted using a one-way ANOVA in which algal feeding treatment (constant low- or high-fed and larvae switched from low to high food conditions at 10, 20, or 30 DPF) was a fixed factor and culture was a random factor. The ranking of differences in cost of development was conducted using post hoc Tukey simultaneous tests for differences in means. AE and NGE were compared among larval treatments in which food was switched from low to high rations at different larval ages (SW-10, SW-20, and SW-30) using an ANOVA where feeding level (pre-switch = low algal ration and post-switch = high algal ration) and age at switch (10, 20, or 30 DPF) were fixed factors and culture was a random factor. Prior to analysis, assumptions of normality were confirmed (P > 0.05) using an Anderson–Darling test. Statistical analyses were conducted using Minitab Statistical Software v20.3.

Larval metamorphic competence

Metamorphic competence was evaluated starting at 16 DPF (when high-fed larvae showed significant rudiment development and a decrease in algal feeding rates) every 2–5 days for all treatments until a minimum of 80% of larvae sampled achieved metamorphic competence or after 50 days had elapsed (for the constant low-fed treatment). Metamorphic competence was based on the previously published definition as the stage where larvae exhibit obvious retraction of tissue surrounding the skeletal rods of the arms when exposed to settlement cues [77–79]. We used excess KCl as a settlement cue, which has been used previously in echinoid larvae [29, 79, 80] and in the case of competent larvae of D. excentricus results in a settlement response within 1 h, resulting in 95% of larvae completing metamorphosis with no mortality [77]. We therefore considered the arm tissue retraction response to excess KCl as indicative of larvae being metamorphically competent. Larvae (50–100) were placed in a 6-well plate with 2 mL of 40-mM excess KCl in FSW. Larvae were then incubated for 1 h at 16 °C and then inspected for arm tissue retraction using a stereomicroscope. Logistic regressions were carried out in R 1.4.1717 (R Core [81]) to estimate the age (DPF) when each treatment reached 63% competence. A metamorphic competence of 63% (meaning 63% of the population responded to the induction assay by showing obvious tissue retraction) was used as a standard developmental benchmark for physiological measurements. The reason for this was that (1) physiological analyses required measurements on populations of larvae; therefore, a common population end-point was required for reasonable comparisons between feeding treatments, and (2) as larval populations approach advanced stages where settlement is imminent, physiological parameters change and become more representative of juvenile physiology (e.g., rates of unicellular algal ingestion decrease, thereby confounding estimates of growth and assimilation efficiencies during larval development).

Individual larval morphometric analysis

Morphological plasticity in individually tracked larvae that were switched from low to high food concentrations was assessed in an independent culture. The same spawning, culturing, and morphological measurement methods described above were used except that larvae were kept alive during photography. Larvae were initially grown in population (1 larva mL−1) and given a low food diet (1000 cells mL−1) that was replenished daily. For this assessment, we choose to examine phenotypic induction at two different points, both approximately halfway through their larval development (based on the results of the population level analyses described above). In the first experiment, 10 individual larvae were randomly chosen at 13 DPF. These larvae were photographed alive for measurement of POAL and MBL. Each larva was then placed in a separate 500-mL beaker containing FSW and a high food diet (10,000 cells mL−1). Each beaker was stirred using a swinging paddle system operating at a frequency of 4 strokes per min−1 [82]. After 6 days of development (19 DPF) in high food conditions, each larva was removed from its 500-mL beaker and photographed again. This experiment was repeated using larvae from the same initial low-fed culture. This time, 10 larvae were randomly selected at 20 DPF, photographed alive for morphological measurements, and moved to high-fed conditions in individual 500-mL beakers. These larvae were then measured again after 6 days of feeding at 26 DPF. A paired t-test was used to compare pre- and post-switch larval morphology, with each larva representing an independent replicate. Prior to analysis, assumptions of normality were confirmed (P > 0.05) using an Anderson–Darling test. Statistical analysis was conducted using Minitab Statistical Software v20.3.

Reversible plasticity response (switching larvae from high to low food conditions)

Two experiments were conducted to examine the morphological consequences of switching from high to low food conditions (hereafter called a reversible plasticity response) (Additional file 1: Fig. S5B). Both experiments were independent cultures initiated as described above with gametes from three males and three females. The two experiments differed in the volume of the culture and the method of mixing of the cultures (so as to keep larvae and algal food evenly distributed in the respective culture vessels). The first experiment, high to low Exp-1, was conducted in 20-L vessels (the same culturing methods used for the low- to high-switched population experiments described above). The second experiment, high to low Exp-2, was conducted in 1.8-L beakers where mixing was achieved using a swinging paddle system operating at a frequency of 4 strokes min−1 [82] (same as the culture system for tracking individual larval responses described above). For both experiments, constant low- and high-fed larvae were fed 1000 and 10,000 algal cells mL−1, respectively. Switched larvae were fed 10,000 algal cells mL−1 until 7 DPF when the algal concentration was reduced to 1000 algal cells mL−1. This earlier age for switching was chosen because high-fed larvae developed faster than the low-fed larvae in the inducible plasticity experiments (above), and we wished to test the phenomenon of reversible plasticity in larvae with little to no rudiment development. For both experiments, morphology was measured throughout development (as described above) to 20 DPF (high to low Exp-1) and 15 DPF (high to low Exp-2). To test for a reverse plasticity response in POAL (i.e., an increase), a repeated measures ANOVA was conducted comparing all three treatments (constant low- and high-fed larvae and high- to low-switched larvae) starting from 10 DPF (first measurement after switching from high to low food conditions) to end of period of observation. Prior to analysis, normality of data was confirmed using an Anderson–Darling test.
