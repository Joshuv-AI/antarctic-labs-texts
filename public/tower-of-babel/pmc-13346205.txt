Relief of bHD1 autoinhibition and modular coupling support MAT-A pathway activation in Flammulina filiformis
Authors: Jinwei Zhang, Yu Tian, Xuyang Xiang, Qinghao Fan, Ruiying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractFlammulina filiformis is a commercially important edible mushroom whose sexual compatibility is determined by genes at two unlinked mating-type loci, A and B. However, the molecular basis of MAT-A activation in this species remains unclear. Here, we established a pair of compatible monokaryotic strains, F11 and F22, derived from the dikaryotic parent ACCC50407, and used them to investigate the genomic and functional basis of MAT-A activation. Genome analysis showed that the MAT-A locus is bipartite, comprising the Aa and Ab subloci. Five HD genes were identified, including one Aa-encoded gene (aHD2-1) and four Ab-encoded genes. In yeast assays, aHD2-1 showed no detectable partner interaction or transcriptional activation activity, whereas specific interactions were detected between the Ab-encoded bHD1 and bHD2 proteins. Deletion analysis further indicated that bHD1-1 contains a functional C-terminal transcriptional activation domain whose activity is constrained in the full-length protein. In vivo, introduction of the compatible allele bHD1-2 into F11 induced pseudoclamp formation and increased clp1 expression. Guided by these results, we constructed a chimeric HD gene, F11ChiHD, by combining the N-terminal region of bHD2-1 with the C-terminal activation region of bHD1-1, which similarly promoted pseudoclamp formation and clp1 upregulation in F11. Together, these findings indicate that MAT-A activation in F. filiformis depends primarily on the Ab sublocus and provide a genetic and functional framework for understanding mating-pathway regulation in this species.Key points• MAT-A pathway activation in Flammulina filiformis is driven primarily by the Ab sublocus.• Relief of bHD1 autoinhibition is associated with pseudoclamp formation and clp1 upregulation in monokaryotic F11.• A chimeric HD protein supports modular coupling between HD2-associated DNA targeting and bHD1-associated transcriptional activation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13839-5.Keywords: Flammulina filiformis, MAT-A locus, Homeodomain proteins, Pseudoclamp connection, Monokaryotic fruiting

Introduction

Flammulina filiformis is a tetrapolar heterothallic basidiomycete with sexual compatibility determined by two unlinked mating-type loci, MAT-A and MAT-B, located on different chromosomes (van Peer et al. 2011; Wang et al. 2016). Although this commercially important enokitake mushroom was long classified as Flammulina velutipes (Curtis) Singer, multigene phylogenetic and morphological analyses later recognized it as a distinct species, F. filiformis (Wang et al. 2018). The species is widely distributed across Europe, Asia, and North America (Ingold 1980) and is valued for both its nutritional quality and reported bioactive properties. In addition to essential amino acids, vitamins, and minerals, it contains compounds with reported anticancer, antibacterial, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory activities (Tang et al. 2016). Accordingly, F. filiformis is widely cultivated commercially, and the development of cultivars with improved yield, quality, and disease resistance remains an important breeding objective.

In heterothallic tetrapolar basidiomycetes, mating compatibility at the mating-type A (MAT-A) locus is determined by homeodomain (HD) transcription factors encoded at this locus (Kües et al. 1992; Stankis et al. 1992). Current understanding of this regulatory system has been derived mainly from the model mushrooms Coprinopsis cinerea and Schizophyllum commune. In C. cinerea, the MAT-A locus comprises two physically separated subloci, Aα and Aβ, and different allelic combinations at these subloci determine sexual development (Mutasa et al. 1990; May et al. 1991; Kües et al. 1992). Subsequent studies further showed that multiple A mating specificities arise from combinations of multiallelic HD1 and HD2 genes, including members of three paralogous HD1/HD2 gene pairs within the A mating-type system (Kües et al. 1994c; Pardo et al. 1996). Mating-type homeodomain proteins are divided into two classes, HD1 and HD2, which are distinguished by conserved residues in the recognition helix: HD1 proteins carry the WFI-AR motif, whereas HD2 proteins carry the WFQN-R motif (Kües and Casselton 1992; Kües et al. 1994a). Mating brings together compatible non-self HD1–HD2 pairs, and one compatible pair is sufficient to trigger A-regulated development (Kües et al. 1992; Banham et al. 1995). Compatible partner choice further depends on an N-terminal dimerization domain in the HD proteins (Banham et al. 1995). Mechanistic studies in C. cinerea suggested that heterodimerization combines the stronger DNA-binding capacity of HD2 with the transactivation function and nuclear localization signals (NLSs) of HD1, thereby generating an active transcription factor (Asante-Owusu et al. 1996; Spit et al. 1998). Consistent with this modular view, a chimeric homeodomain protein was shown to confer self-compatibility and constitutive sexual development in C. cinerea (Kües et al. 1994b). Activation of this HD-dependent switch is required for dikaryotic development, and downstream A-regulated factors such as pcc1 and clp1 are essential for this process (Murata et al. 1998; Inada et al. 2001; Kamada 2002). Related studies in S. commune likewise showed that compatible non-self combinations of Aα-encoded homeodomain proteins determine mating-type specificity and activate sexual development, supporting a conserved role for HD-based compatibility control in mushroom-forming basidiomycetes. (Specht et al. 1992; Stankis et al. 1992; Magae et al. 1995; Robertson et al. 1996). Coordinated with this MAT-A-controlled dikaryotic program, the mating-type B (MAT-B) locus encodes pheromone receptors and pheromone precursors, whose recognition activates pheromone-responsive G-protein and MAPK signaling pathways to promote cell fusion, nuclear migration, and dikaryon establishment (Raudaskoski and Kothe 2010; Kües 2015).

Building on these general models, the overall organization of the mating-type loci in F. filiformis was first described using monokaryotic genome resources. Early work identified a bipartite MAT-A locus comprising two subloci, Aa and Ab (corresponding to the HD-a and HD-b subloci in earlier studies), separated by ~ 73 kb. It also identified a MAT-B locus containing two linked subloci separated by a large intervening region (van Peer et al. 2011). Analyses of additional monokaryons further supported this two-sublocus organization of the MAT-A locus and suggested that Aa is a functionally active, at least di-allelic sublocus involved in mating-type determination, carrying either a conserved HD1 or HD2 gene depending on the strain (Wang et al. 2016, 2019). For the MAT-B locus, a third unlinked sublocus was later proposed based on genome annotation and mating analyses (Zhao et al. 2020). Functional studies have also begun to link these loci to development in F. filiformis. For example, the A-pathway-associated regulator clp1 has been characterized and shown to affect mycelial growth and fruiting body development (Lyu et al. 2021). More recently, the introduction of a compatible HD gene into monokaryotic mycelia was reported to activate the A pathway, induce pseudoclamp morphogenesis, and increase clp1 expression (Liu et al. 2024).

Although previous studies have outlined the organization of the mating-type loci in F. filiformis and provided initial evidence for A-pathway activation, the molecular basis of HD-mediated compatibility in this species remains unclear. In particular, how compatible HD proteins assemble into an active regulatory complex has not been directly examined. Because interaction principles inferred from model basidiomycetes may not be fully transferable to F. filiformis, direct experimental analysis in this species is needed.

Here, we established a pair of compatible monokaryotic strains, F11 and F22, derived from the dikaryotic parent ACCC50407, and characterized their MAT-A loci. Using yeast-based assays, we examined HD1–HD2 interaction patterns and mapped their transcriptional activation domains (TADs). We then performed in vivo functional validation in F. filiformis by introducing a compatible native HD allele, bHD1-2. Guided by the domain-mapping results, we further designed a chimeric HD gene to probe A-pathway activation. Together, these analyses connect locus organization, HD interaction specificity, and pathway activation in F. filiformis, and provide a species-specific framework for understanding mating-related dikaryotic development in this mushroom.

Materials and methods

Strains and medium

The F. filiformis dikaryotic strain ACCC50407, obtained from the Agricultural Culture Collection of China (ACCC), and its derived monokaryotic strains F11 and F22 were used in this study. All strains were routinely maintained on CYM agar containing 10 g/L maltose, 20 g/L glucose, 2 g/L tryptone, 2 g/L yeast extract, 0.5 g/L MgSO₄·7H₂O, 4.6 g/L KH₂PO₄, and 20 g/L agar.

Preparation of monokaryons from the dikaryotic parent strain

The dikaryotic strain ACCC50407 was cultured on CYM agar at 23 °C in the dark for 7 days. Approximately 0.1 g of mycelia was suspended in 1 mL of 0.5 M sucrose and digested with 2% (w/v) lywallzyme (Guangdong Institute of Microbiology, Guangzhou, China) at 30 °C for 3 h, with gentle mixing every 30 min. The digestion mixture was filtered through sterile cotton to remove undigested debris and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min. The resulting protoplast pellet was resuspended in 0.5 M sucrose and spread onto CYM agar supplemented with 0.5 M sucrose to maintain osmotic stability. Plates were incubated at 23 °C in the dark until visible colonies formed, and regenerated colonies were then transferred to fresh CYM plates.

To determine the nuclear status of regenerated isolates, mycelia were examined microscopically using an EVOS FLoid imaging system (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Isolates lacking clamp connections were classified as monokaryotic, whereas isolates with clamp connections were classified as dikaryotic.

Mating-type assignment by pairwise crossings

To assign mating types, one randomly selected monokaryotic isolate was designated as the AxBx reference strain. Isolates that formed clamp connections when paired with this reference were assigned to the compatible AyBy mating type. Isolates that did not form clamp connections with the AxBx reference were subsequently paired with a confirmed AyBy tester strain. Those that formed clamp connections in this second pairing were assigned as AxBx.

DNA extraction, genome sequencing, and assembly

The F. filiformis monokaryotic strains F11 and F22 were cultured on CYM agar at 23 °C in the dark for 8 days. Mycelia were harvested, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB)-based method (Porebski et al. 1997). Whole-genome sequencing was carried out by Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq (San Diego, CA, USA) and PacBio Sequel II (Menlo Park, CA, USA) platforms.

For genome assembly, PacBio subreads were converted to circular consensus sequencing (CCS) reads and assembled de novo using hifiasm v0.19.9 (Cheng et al. 2021). The resulting draft assemblies were then polished with Illumina short reads using Pilon v1.24 to improve consensus accuracy (Walker et al. 2014). To generate chromosome-level scaffolds, Hi-C libraries with insert sizes of 300–700 bp were prepared from F11 and F22 mycelia and sequenced on an Illumina paired-end platform. Raw Hi-C data were processed using HiC-Pro v2.8.1 (Servant et al. 2015), and contigs were clustered, ordered, and oriented into chromosomes using LACHESIS (version-201701) (Burton et al. 2013). Assembly completeness was assessed using BUSCO v2.0 against the fungi_odb9 dataset.

Identification and sequence analysis of HD genes at the MAT-A locus

HD genes at the MAT-A locus were identified in the F. filiformis F11 and F22 genome assemblies using a two-step BLAST-based approach. First, local tBLASTn searches were performed against the genome assemblies with an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10–5. Query sequences included HD proteins from F. filiformis (FfaHD2, AGG68681.3; FfbHD1, AGG68679.1; FfbHD2, AGG68680.1), C. cinerea (CcHD1, AAD33326.1; CcHD2, CAA56132.1; CcHD1, CAA56133.1), and S. commune (ScHD1, AAB01372; ScHD2, AAB01373). Candidate gene models were then evaluated by BLASTp searches against the NCBI non-redundant (nr) protein database to confirm homology and support annotation.

Conserved regions, including the homeodomain, were annotated using the InterProScan web server (Jones et al. 2014). Nuclear localization signals (NLSs) were predicted with the PSORT II server (Nakai and Horton 1999), and coiled-coil regions were identified using the NCOILS web tool with a window size of 14 (Lupas 1996). Pairwise sequence alignments were generated using Needle from the EMBOSS package v6.6.0 (Rice et al. 2000), and multiple sequence alignments were generated using T-Coffee v13.46.1 (Notredame et al. 2000). Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the maximum-likelihood method in MEGA11 (Tamura et al. 2021). The identified HD gene sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PQ480328–PQ480332.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Mycelia were harvested and ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle. Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Universal Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HiScript III First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA Wiper) (Vazyme).

Yeast two-hybrid analysis of HD protein interactions

Protein-protein interactions were assessed using the Y2HGold GAL4-based yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) system (Yeast Two-Hybrid Interaction Verification Kit, Coolaber, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The pGADT7 and pGBKT7 vectors were linearized by digestion with EcoRI and BamHI (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). For fusion to the GAL4 activation domain (AD), the full-length coding sequences (CDSs) of bHD1-1, bHD2-1, bHD1-2, and bHD2-2 were cloned in frame into pGADT7. For fusion to the GAL4 DNA-binding domain (BD), the full-length CDSs of aHD2-1, bHD1-1, and bHD2-1, together with the N-terminal fragments of bHD1-1 (amino acids 1–305) and bHD2-1 (amino acids 1–195), were cloned into pGBKT7. Primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

The indicated AD and BD construct pairs were co-transformed into the yeast strain Y2HGold (Saccharomyces cerevisiae). For autoactivation tests, each BD construct was co-transformed with the empty pGADT7 vector. Co-transformations with pGBKT7-53/pGADT7-T and pGBKT7-Lam/pGADT7-T were used as the positive and negative controls, respectively. Transformants were first selected on double-dropout medium (SD/− Leu/− Trp). Protein interactions were then evaluated on high-stringency quadruple-dropout medium (SD/− Leu/− Trp/− Ade/− His) supplemented with 20 µg/mL X-α-Gal. Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 3 days and then examined for growth and blue coloration.

Transcriptional activation assay of HD proteins

To assess the intrinsic transcriptional activation activity of HD proteins, the full-length CDSs of the respective HD genes were cloned in frame into the EcoRI and BamHI sites of pGBKT7 to generate fusions with the GAL4 DNA-binding domain. Based on the predicted domain architecture of these proteins, a series of truncated constructs was further generated to map transcriptional activation regions. These included two fragments of aHD2-1 (amino acids 126–475 and 199–475), six fragments of bHD1-1 (1–305, 267–602, 306–602, 348–602, 421–602, and 456–602), and six fragments of bHD2-1 (1–195, 135–676, 196–676, 278–676, 418–676, and 506–676). All fragments were cloned in frame into pGBKT7. Primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

The resulting constructs, together with the empty pGBKT7 vector as a negative control, were individually transformed into the yeast strain Y2HGold. The empty pGBKT7 vector was used to exclude background activation. Transformants were first selected on single-dropout medium (SD/− Trp) and then transferred to high-stringency triple-dropout medium (SD/− Trp/− Ade/− His) supplemented with 20 µg/mL X-α-Gal. Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 3 days, and growth and color development were recorded. The α-Galactosidase (α-GAL) Activity Assay Kit (Biobox, Beijing, China) was used to detect α-galactosidase activity according to the instruction manual.

Transformation vector construction

The backbone plasmid pBHg-FfMnSOD1-OE was provided by the Mycological Research Center of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University. In this vector, the hygromycin resistance gene (HygR) is driven by the F. filiformis glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (gpd) promoter. To construct the overexpression vector, the bHD1-2 coding sequence was amplified from strain F22 cDNA using the primer pair bHD1-2-F/R. The amplified fragment was inserted into the SpeI and PspOMI sites of pBHg-FfMnSOD1-OE using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme), generating pGPD-bHD1-2.

To generate the chimeric construct, the N-terminal region of bHD2-1 (1–758 bp, encoding amino acids 1–220) and the C-terminal region of bHD1-1 (1090–1985 bp, encoding amino acids 348–602) were amplified from gDNA of strain F11 using the primer pairs bHD2-1(1–758)-F/R and bHD1-1(1090–1985)-F/R, respectively. These two fragments were assembled and inserted into the SpeI and PspOMI sites of pBHg-FfMnSOD1-OE using the ClonExpress MultiS One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme), generating pGPD-F11ChiHD. Primers used for vector construction are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation

A. tumefaciens-mediated transformation (ATMT) was performed as previously described (Ding et al. 2011), with minor modifications. Induction medium (IM) was prepared according to the same protocol. The F. filiformis strain F11 was cultured on CYM agar at 23 °C for 8 days. Mycelial discs (5 mm in diameter) were excised from the actively growing colony margin and pre-cultured in liquid CYM at 25 °C for 3 days without shaking. A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 carrying the target plasmid was grown in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with kanamycin (100 µg/mL) and rifampicin (50 µg/mL) at 28 °C and 200 rpm for at least 16 h. Cells were collected by centrifugation (4000 × g for 10 min), resuspended in IM containing 200 µmol/L acetosyringone, and incubated at 28 °C and 90 rpm for 5 h. The induced A. tumefaciens culture was then co-cultivated with the mycelial discs at 28 °C for 6 h without shaking.

After co-cultivation, the mycelial discs were blotted on sterile filter paper, placed on IM agar, and incubated at 25 °C for 3 days. The discs were then transferred to CYM agar containing hygromycin (18 µg/mL) for transformant selection and cefotaxime (400 µg/mL) to eliminate Agrobacterium, and incubated at 23 °C for 25 days. Hygromycin-resistant colonies were isolated and subcultured twice on the same selective medium. Putative transformants were first screened by PCR amplification of HygR using the primer pair HygR-F/R with gDNA as the template. Transcription of bHD1-2 and the chimeric F11ChiHD construct was then assessed by reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) using cDNA as the template and the primer pairs T-bHD1-2-F/R and T-F11ChiHD-F/R, respectively (Supplemental Table S1).

RT-qPCR and gene expression analysis

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed in a 20 µL reaction volume using an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). The expression of clp1 (GenBank accession no. MH234398) was quantified using the primer pair q-clp1-F/R. The β-actin gene (GenBank accession no. OP354401), amplified with the primer pair q-act-F/R, was used as the internal reference. The RT-qPCR program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melt-curve analysis was performed using the instrument’s default program. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Three biological replicates were analyzed, each with three technical replicates. Primer sequences used in this assay are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

Observation of mycelial morphology and measurement of mycelial growth rate

For observation of mycelial morphology, strains were inoculated onto CYM agar plates, and sterile glass slides were inserted diagonally into the medium. After hyphae had grown onto the glass surface, the slides were removed and examined using an EVOS FLoid imaging system (Thermo Fisher). For measurement of radial growth rate, mycelial discs (5 mm in diameter) excised from the actively growing colony margins were inoculated at the center of fresh CYM agar plates. Plates were incubated at 23 °C in the dark for 8 days. Colony diameters were measured, and the radial growth rate was calculated as colony radius divided by the incubation time (days). Growth assays were performed with three biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v26.0 (www.ibm.com/products/spss). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent biological replicates. Differences between the two groups were analyzed using the independent-samples Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as P ≤ 0.05 (*), P ≤ 0.01 (**), and P ≤ 0.001 (***).

Results

Isolation and morphological characterization of monokaryons

Monokaryons were obtained from the F. filiformis dikaryotic strain ACCC50407 by protoplast regeneration. A total of 40 regenerated isolates were recovered, including 25 monokaryons and 15 dikaryons. Pairwise-crossing assays separated the 25 monokaryons into two mating-type classes, designated here as AxBx (n = 10) and AyBy (n = 15). Two representative isolates with stable growth and typical colony morphology, F11 (AxBx) and F22 (AyBy), were selected for subsequent analyses.

The parental dikaryon and the derived monokaryons showed distinct colony morphologies. On CYM agar, the parental strain ACCC50407 formed colonies with abundant white aerial mycelia (Fig. 1A). In contrast, the monokaryon F11 showed thinner and sparser aerial hyphae (Fig. 1B). The monokaryon F22 produced thicker and more abundant aerial hyphae than F11 and appeared denser than the parental strain (Fig. 1C). Microscopic observation further showed that hyphae of both monokaryons were narrower than those of the dikaryotic parent (Fig. 1B, C).

Analysis of the mating-type loci in compatible monokaryotic strains

High-quality genome assemblies were obtained for strains F11 (38.3 Mb) and F22 (41.8 Mb) using PacBio HiFi, Illumina, and Hi-C data, enabling analysis of their mating-type loci (GenBank accession nos. JBIGLK000000000 and JBIGLL000000000, respectively).

Annotation of the MAT-A loci identified five HD genes in total: aHD2-1 (PQ480328), bHD1-1 (PQ480329), bHD2-1 (PQ480330), bHD1-2 (PQ480331), and bHD2-2 (PQ480332). Among these, aHD2-1 was located in the Aa sublocus adjacent to the mitochondrial intermediate peptidase (mip) gene, whereas bHD1-1, bHD1-2, bHD2-1, and bHD2-2 were clustered in the Ab sublocus (Fig. 2A). The Aa and Ab subloci were separated by 72 kb in both genomes (Fig. 2A). The detailed genomic coordinates of these HD genes in F11 and F22 are provided in Supplemental Table S2. Comparative analysis showed that the genomic region containing aHD2-1, including introns, was identical between F11 and F22. Within the Ab sublocus, the genomic sequences of the bHD1 alleles (bHD1-1 and bHD1-2) shared 89% identity, whereas those of the bHD2 alleles (bHD2-1 and bHD2-2) shared 81% identity (Fig. 2A). The HD proteins ranged from 475 to 688 amino acids in length. Domain analysis indicated that all five proteins contained a canonical homeodomain and predicted NLSs (Fig. 2B). Multiple sequence alignment showed that the HD2 proteins from both F11 and F22 contained the conserved WFQN-R motif, consistent with reference HD2 proteins from C. cinerea and S. commune (Fig. 2C). In contrast, the HD1 proteins of F. filiformis contained a conserved WLTSTR motif rather than the WFI-AR motif found in the reference HD1 proteins (Fig. 2C). Phylogenetic analysis separated the HD1 and HD2 proteins into two clades, and within the HD2 clade, proteins encoded by the Aa and Ab subloci formed distinct sub-branches (Fig. 2D).

Interactions between HD1 and HD2 proteins

Y2H assays were used to examine interaction patterns among the HD proteins encoded by the MAT-A locus. Initial tests showed autoactivation of the full-length bHD1-1 and bHD2-1 proteins (Supplemental Fig. S1). To avoid this effect, truncated constructs containing their N-terminal regions, designated bHD1-1N (amino acids 1–305) and bHD2-1N (amino acids 1–195), were used in subsequent assays. The BD constructs were generated by fusing full-length aHD2-1 and the N-terminal regions of bHD1-1 and bHD2-1 to the GAL4 DNA-binding domain in pGBKT7. The AD constructs were generated by fusing the full-length bHD1-1, bHD2-1, bHD1-2, and bHD2-2 proteins to the GAL4 activation domain in pGADT7.

As shown in Fig. 3, the positive control grew and developed blue coloration on quadruple-dropout medium (SD/−Leu/−Trp/−Ade/−His supplemented with X-α-Gal), whereas the negative control grew only on double-dropout medium (SD/−Leu/−Trp). No interaction is detected between aHD2-1 and any of the tested prey proteins, as yeast co-expressing pGBKT7-aHD2-1 with the respective prey constructs fail to grow on quadruple-dropout medium (Fig. 3). Likewise, no interaction is observed between HD1 and HD2 proteins derived from the same parental strain, such as bHD1-1N and bHD2-1. In contrast, interactions were detected between Ab-encoded HD1–HD2 pairs: yeast co-expressing bHD1-1N with bHD2-2 or bHD2-1N with bHD1-2 grew and developed blue coloration on selective medium (Fig. 3). Together, these results indicate that mating compatibility between F11 and F22 is associated with heterodimerization between the bHD1 and bHD2 proteins encoded by the Ab sublocus, whereas aHD2-1 from the Aa sublocus does not show detectable interaction in this assay.

Analysis of the transcriptional activation domains of HD proteins

Transcriptional activation activity of the five HD proteins from F. filiformis was examined in yeast. Full-length CDSs were fused to the GAL4 DNA-binding domain in pGBKT7 and introduced into Y2HGold. Autoactivation assays showed that full-length bHD1-1, bHD2-1, bHD1-2, and bHD2-2 displayed transcriptional activation activity (Supplemental Fig. S1). To further map the TADs, deletion analyses were performed based on predicted structural features, including the homeodomain, NLS, and coiled-coil regions. A series of truncated constructs was generated for aHD2-1 (three fragments), bHD1-1 (seven fragments), and bHD2-1 (seven fragments) (Fig. 4A).

All transformants grew comparably on single-dropout medium (SD/− Trp), indicating successful transformation (Fig. 4B). On selective triple-dropout medium (SD/− Trp/− His/− Ade supplemented with X-α-Gal), none of the truncated aHD2-1 fragments supported yeast growth. Consistently, quantitative α-galactosidase assays showed that all aHD2-1 fragments had activities below that of the negative control (101 U/mg protein), indicating no detectable autonomous activation activity (Fig. 4C, D). For bHD1-1, yeast expressing the N-terminal or more central fragments (amino acids 1–305, 267–602, and 306–602) did not grow on the selective medium and showed α-galactosidase activities lower than the negative control (58, 72, and 61 U/mg protein, respectively). In contrast, yeast expressing the isolated C-terminal fragments (amino acids 348–602, 421–602, and 456–602) grew and developed blue coloration (Fig. 4C). The full-length bHD1-1 construct showed moderate activity (125 U/mg protein), whereas the isolated C-terminal fragments showed higher activities, including 353 U/mg protein for residues 348–602 and 945 U/mg protein for residues 456–602 (Fig. 4D). For bHD2-1, all truncations except the N-terminal fragment (amino acids 1–195) supported growth on the selective medium and showed X-α-Gal reporter activity (Fig. 4C). The C-terminal region of bHD2-1 showed high α-galactosidase activities, reaching 2,715 and 6,021 U/mg protein for fragments 418–676 and 506–676, respectively (Fig. 4D).

Together, these results indicate that aHD2-1 lacks detectable autonomous activation activity, whereas both bHD1-1 and bHD2-1 contain functional C-terminal TADs. In addition, deletion of upstream regions increased the activation activity of bHD1-1, whereas bHD2-1 showed stronger overall activation activity.

Effects of bHD1-2 introduction into strain F11

Because aHD2-1 was identical between the compatible strains F11 and F22 and showed no detectable interaction in the Y2H assays (Fig. 3), the Aa sublocus was unlikely to account for compatibility in this strain pair. By contrast, specific interactions were detected between the compatible Ab-encoded combinations bHD1-1N/bHD2-2 and bHD2-1N/bHD1-2. We therefore introduced bHD1-2 from strain F22 into the F11 monokaryon to test whether a compatible bHD1–bHD2 pairing could activate the MAT-A pathway in vivo.

The overexpression vector pGPD-bHD1-2, carrying the HygR, was constructed (Fig. 5A) and transformed into strain F11. Eleven putative transformants were obtained after two rounds of screening on CYM medium containing 18 µg/mL hygromycin, a concentration selected based on the hygromycin sensitivity assay of strain F11 (Supplemental Fig. S2). Genomic PCR using HygR-specific primers (HygR-F/R) confirmed the presence of HygR in representative transformants, including T4-bHD1-2, T5-bHD1-2, T6-bHD1-2, and T9-bHD1-2 (Fig. 5B). Because bHD1-2 is absent from F11, RT-PCR was used to examine its transcription in the transformants. Transcripts of bHD1-2 were detected in all positive transformants examined (Fig. 5C).

Phenotypic changes were observed after the introduction of bHD1-2. No pseudoclamp connections were detected in wild-type F11 hyphae. In contrast, pseudoclamp structures were observed in transformants such as T4-bHD1-2 and T5-bHD1-2 (Fig. 5D). These structures appeared as short rod-like outgrowths emerging from the hyphae and bending toward the basal part of the cell, with visible septa at the corresponding positions (Fig. 5D). In addition, the transformants showed sparser hyphal growth at the colony margins than wild-type F11. Radial growth rate also differed among transformants. The growth rate of T4-bHD1-2 (5.22 mm/day) did not differ significantly from that of wild-type F11 (5.5 mm/day), whereas T5-bHD1-2 showed a significantly reduced growth rate of 4.5 mm/day (Fig. 5E).

To further assess whether these phenotypic changes were associated with A-pathway activation, clp1 expression was examined by RT-qPCR. Compared with wild-type F11, clp1 expression increased 2.1-fold in T4-bHD1-2 and 3.7-fold in T5-bHD1-2 (Fig. 5F). Together, these results indicate that introduction of bHD1-2 into F11 was associated with pseudoclamp formation and increased clp1 expression, supporting MAT-A pathway activation in F11.

Activation of the MAT-A mating pathway by the chimeric F11ChiHD gene

Our Y2H and TAD-mapping analyses, together with the in vivo effects of bHD1-2 introduction into F11, suggested that MAT-A-encoded HD proteins make distinct contributions to complex formation and transcriptional activation. In the F11/F22 pair, detectable interactions are observed only between the bHD1-bHD2 combinations bHD1-1N/bHD2-2 and bHD2-1N/bHD1-2 (Fig. 3). Deletion analysis further suggested that the N-terminal region of bHD2-1 retains the homeodomain but lacks autonomous transcriptional activation activity, whereas the C-terminal region of bHD1-1 contains a functional TAD together with a predicted NLS (Fig. 4). Consistent with a DNA-targeting role for the HD2 component, bHD2 proteins of F. filiformis retain the conserved WFQN-R motif found in reference HD2 proteins from C. cinerea and S. commune (Fig. 2C). Together, these observations provided the rationale for testing whether a single chimeric protein could bypass the usual requirement for heterodimerization and activate the MAT-A pathway.

To this end, a chimeric gene, designated F11ChiHD, was constructed by fusing a 1–758 bp fragment of bHD2-1 (encoding amino acids 1–220) to a 1090–1985 bp fragment of bHD1-1 (encoding amino acids 348–602). The bHD2-1-derived region contains the HD2 homeodomain and a short downstream segment, whereas the bHD1-1-derived region contains the functional TAD and a predicted NLS. The resulting chimeric gene retained three native introns (50, 48, and 128 bp), yielding a 1,654 bp genomic construct predicted to encode a 475-aa chimeric protein (Fig. 6A). F11ChiHD was then placed under the control of the gpd promoter in the expression vector pGPD-F11ChiHD (Fig. 6B) and introduced into the F11 monokaryon by ATMT. After two rounds of selection on CYM medium containing 18 µg/mL hygromycin, nine positive transformants carrying HygR were identified by genomic PCR: T1-F11ChiHD, T4-F11ChiHD, T5-F11ChiHD, T14-F11ChiHD, T18-F11ChiHD, T34-F11ChiHD, T35-F11ChiHD, T39-F11ChiHD, and T42-F11ChiHD (Fig. 6C). RT-PCR further detected transcription of the chimeric construct. In eight transformants (T1, T5, T14, T18, T34, T35, T39, and T42), cDNA-derived amplicons (expected size: 1,428 bp) were shorter than the corresponding product amplified from the plasmid template (1,654 bp), consistent with removal of the three native introns (50, 48, and 128 bp) from the F11ChiHD transcript (Fig. 6D). No amplification was detected in wild-type F11.

Introduction of F11ChiHD was associated with marked phenotypic changes. Similar to strains expressing bHD1-2, transformants carrying the chimeric construct, such as T1-F11ChiHD and T35-F11ChiHD, formed pseudoclamp structures on their hyphae (Fig. 6E). These transformants also showed reduced radial growth relative to wild-type F11 (5.5 mm/day). The growth rate of T1-F11ChiHD was 4.2 mm/day, whereas T35-F11ChiHD grew at 5.1 mm/day (Fig. 6F). Consistent with these phenotypic changes, RT-qPCR showed increased clp1 expression in both transformants relative to wild-type F11, with 2.6-fold and 1.3-fold increases in T1-F11ChiHD and T35-F11ChiHD, respectively (Fig. 6G). Together, these results indicate that the F11ChiHD chimera can promote pseudoclamp formation and increased clp1 expression in F11, supporting the view that it can functionally mimic key features of the native HD1–HD2 heterodimer in activating the MAT-A pathway.

Discussion

The genetic organization of mating-type loci in basidiomycetes is highly diverse, reflecting complex evolutionary histories (Maia et al. 2015; Coelho et al. 2017). Consistent with previous reports in F. filiformis (van Peer et al. 2011), genomic mapping of our strains showed that the MAT-A locus is bipartite, comprising the Aa and Ab subloci separated by ~ 72 kb (Fig. 1A). Previous studies further showed that the Aa sublocus can be functionally active in other F. filiformis strains, carrying either a conserved Hd2 gene, such as the FvHd2-1-type found in L11 and W23, or a conserved Hd1 gene shared by Fv01-1 and NJ6-21, and that allelic differences at this sublocus alone can confer compatibility and clamp connection formation (Wang et al. 2019). In the F11/F22 background, however, the aHD2-1 alleles were identical to each other and grouped with the L11/W23-type sequences. Relative to the longer aHD2-1 allele of strain KACC42780 (1646 bp), all of these shorter alleles shared a 66-bp deletion in the 5′ region (1580 bp; Supplemental Fig. S3), while the remaining sequence was highly conserved, with only a few nucleotide differences. However, in our yeast assays, aHD2-1 showed neither detectable partner interaction nor transcriptional activation activity (Figs. 3 and 4). Together, these observations indicate that compatibility between F11 and F22 is determined primarily by the Ab sublocus, whereas the Aa sublocus made no detectable contribution in the assays used here. Because mating-type determinants are generally associated with allelic polymorphism linked to self/non-self discrimination, this pattern is more consistent with a reduced contribution to mating specificity than with a fully functional recognition locus (Kothe 1996; Casselton and Olesnicky 1998; van Diepen et al. 2013; Kües 2015). In this context, the reduced allelic polymorphism and loss of detectable activity of aHD2-1 support the view that the Aa sublocus may make a diminished contribution to mating specificity in at least a subset of F. filiformis lineages relative to Ab.

Given that compatibility between F11 and F22 is governed primarily by the Ab sublocus, the HD proteins encoded by this sublocus likely require tight control to prevent inappropriate activation in the monokaryotic state. Our Y2H and transcriptional activation analyses support a relief-of-autoinhibition model for bHD1. In the Y2H assays, detectable interaction is observed only between the Ab-encoded combinations bHD1-1N/bHD2-2 and bHD2-1N/bHD1-2 (Fig. 3). When examined individually in yeast, full-length bHD1-1 showed only weak transcriptional activation activity, whereas the N-terminal and extended central fragments (amino acids 1–305, 267–602, and 306–602) failed to support yeast growth or blue coloration on selective medium and showed no detectable α-galactosidase activity. By contrast, isolated C-terminal fragments lacking these upstream regions (amino acids 348–602 and 456–602) show strong transcriptional activation activity (Fig. 4). This truncation pattern is consistent with the presence of a potent C-terminal TAD that is constrained in the full-length protein by an inhibitory determinant located upstream of residue 348. Consistent with this interpretation, clp1, a major downstream effector of the A pathway, was strongly repressed in the monokaryotic state, with transcript levels in wild-type F11 and F22 being approximately 276-fold and 195-fold lower, respectively, than in the dikaryotic strain ACCC50407 (Supplemental Fig. S4). This model is further supported by in vivo functional validation in the native F11 background: expression of the F22-derived bHD1-2 protein in F11 provides a compatible partner for the resident bHD2-1 protein, inducing pseudoclamp formation and increasing clp1 expression (Fig. 5D, F). A similar pattern was recently reported in Flammulina, where introduction of a non-self HD gene into monokaryotic hyphae activated the A pathway, induced pseudoclamp morphogenesis, and upregulated clp1 expression (Liu et al. 2024). Classical work in C. cinerea established that heterodimerization assembles complementary functional modules into an active regulator (Kües et al. 1994b; Asante-Owusu et al. 1996). Our data extend this framework by suggesting that in F. filiformis, the release of bHD1 from intramolecular inhibition may represent an additional layer of control in A-pathway activation.

An additional layer of complexity arose from the observation that bHD2 also exhibited strong, independent transcriptional activation activity in the yeast reporter assay (Fig. 4). This raises an important question: despite possessing a potent activation domain, why does bHD2 fail to activate the A pathway on its own in the native monokaryotic state? One possible explanation is that activation-domain strength alone is insufficient to determine A-associated developmental outputs. Recent systematic studies and reviews indicate that activation domains can be encoded by diverse sequence-feature combinations and often function in a strongly context-dependent manner, consistent with differences in coactivator recruitment and regulatory environment (Udupa et al. 2024; Morffy et al. 2024). Our sequence and functional data are broadly consistent with this view. bHD2 in F. filiformis retains the conserved WFQN-R motif within its homeodomain (Fig. 2C), consistent with the preservation of the HD2-associated DNA-targeting role described in model basidiomycetes (Kües and Casselton 1992). By contrast, bHD1 shows motif divergence, carrying a WLTSTR sequence, yet retains pathway-associated activation capacity. Consistent with this functional separation, bHD1-1 and bHD2-1 share very low overall sequence identity across their full-length protein sequences (17%) (Supplemental Fig. S5), further suggesting that their activation-domain-containing regions are compositionally distinct. Further support comes from the F11ChiHD chimera (Fig. 6). By combining the bHD2-derived N-terminal region, which includes the homeodomain, with the bHD1-derived C-terminal region containing the activation domain and a predicted NLS, this construct was able to promote pseudoclamp formation and increase clp1 expression in F11. Together, these observations suggest that productive A pathway activation depends not simply on strong transcriptional activation activity, but on the correct coupling of the HD2-derived DNA-targeting module with a context-compatible activation module from bHD1. This interpretation is also consistent with classical studies in C. cinerea showing that HD1–HD2 heterodimerization functionally combines complementary modules and regulates subcellular localization (Kües et al. 1994b; Asante-Owusu et al. 1996; Spit et al. 1998).

The need for tight gating of A-pathway activation is likely related to the physiological cost of uncoordinated development. In basidiomycetes, successful sexual development generally depends on coordinated activities of the MAT-A and MAT-B-regulated pathways (Kamada 2002; Kües 2015). Activation of the A pathway alone, in the absence of complementary B-pathway signals, may therefore divert resources toward incomplete developmental outputs, including pseudoclamp connections, which are morphologically distinct from both ordinary hyphal branching and true clamp connections (Supplemental Fig. S6), without enabling full sexual development. In our study, ectopic activation of the A pathway in the F11 monokaryon, achieved either by introduction of the F22-derived bHD1-2 allele or by expression of the F11ChiHD chimera, is associated with pseudoclamp formation, increased clp1 expression, reduced radial mycelial growth, and weakened colony vigor (Figs. 5D–F and 6E–G). This developmental trade-off is consistent with previous studies. In Flammulina, overexpression of clp1 reduced vegetative mycelial growth while promoting later fruiting potential (Lyu et al. 2021). Similarly, in Lentinula edodes, forced mating between strains of incompatible mating type by synthetic pheromones induced clamp connection formation and clp1 expression, but the resulting dikaryon showed very poor growth on PDA (Ha et al. 2018). In addition, in Pleurotus eryngii, introduction of compatible A and B mating-type genes into monokaryons was reported to upregulate clp1 and promote fruiting body development, but the resulting fruiting bodies remained non-sporulating (Shang et al. 2023). Together, these observations suggest that deployment of mating-associated developmental modules outside their normal physiological context can impose broader physiological and developmental costs, including impaired vegetative growth and incomplete reproductive output. This trade-off may help explain why A-pathway outputs, particularly clp1 induction, remain strongly repressed in the monokaryotic state until compatible mating signals are present.

Given that MAT-A activation is tightly gated and associated with vegetative growth costs, how can wild-type monokaryons occasionally bypass these checkpoints and form fruiting bodies? Monokaryotic fruiting has also been reported in several mushroom-forming fungi, including S. commune (Esser et al. 1979), Cyclocybe (Agrocybe) aegerita [Cyclocybeaegerita] (Esser and Meinhardt 1977), Mycoleptodonoides aitchisonii (Riffiani et al. 2019b), and Amanita phalloides (Wang et al. 2023), although the molecular basis of this phenomenon remains poorly understood (Kües and Teichert 2026). In our system, Monokaryotic fruiting is likewise observed in F11 and F22 under prolonged cultivation (Supplemental Fig. S7). One possible factor lies in the subcellular regulation of HD proteins. Our sequence and functional analyses suggest that these proteins collectively retain key features required for transcriptional regulation, including homeodomain-mediated DNA targeting, transcriptional activation capacity, and predicted NLSs, although access to these functions appears to be constrained in the monokaryotic state. In particular, predicted NLSs were identified in both bHD1 and bHD2. This is consistent with observations in Pholiota microspora and M. aitchisonii, which suggest that monomeric HD proteins may enter the nucleus prior to heterodimerization (Yi et al. 2009; Riffiani et al. 2021). Spatial exclusion is therefore unlikely to be the sole barrier. A more plausible explanation is that release of bHD1 autoinhibition constitutes a major checkpoint, while bHD2 may also contribute through recruitment or other context-dependent regulatory functions. Evidence from other mushroom-forming fungi suggests that this checkpoint can sometimes be relaxed through changes in HD dosage. In M. aitchisonii, monokaryotic clamp formation was reported not to be genetically linked to mating type, and clamp-forming monokaryons showed elevated expression of the HD2-type gene Mahd2 (Riffiani et al. 2019a, 2021). Similarly, in P. microspora, artificial elevation of A-mating-type HD gene expression was sufficient to drive widespread pseudoclamp and clamp morphogenesis in monokaryons (Yi et al. 2010). Together, these studies point to HD dosage as one plausible route by which the normal heterodimerization checkpoint can be relaxed, providing a useful conceptual context for our own observation of monokaryotic fruiting. However, whether prolonged cultivation in F. filiformis acts through altered HD expression, partial release of bHD1 autoinhibition, bHD2-assisted recruitment of cofactors, or other regulatory changes remains unknown.

While this study provides a genetic and functional framework for MAT-A activation in F. filiformis, several mechanistic questions remain open. Our current model is supported primarily by genetic and yeast-based evidence, and direct validation of HD-complex binding and in vivo localization will be needed to further refine it. In addition, the molecular basis of the proposed developmental bypass remains unresolved. Addressing these questions will not only improve our understanding of mating-pathway regulation in mushroom-forming fungi but may also support future genetic improvement and breeding of edible mushrooms.
