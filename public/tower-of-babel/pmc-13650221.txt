Real-World Use of Adjuvant Nivolumab for Resected Stage III–IV Melanoma: 2-Year Analysis of AdjuMel
Authors: Stéphane Dalle, Philippe Saiag, Céleste Lebbé, Jean-Jacques Grob, Brigitte Dréno, Sophie Dalac, Bernard Asselain, François Skowron, Sophie Darras, Nicolas Jovenin, Gaëlle Quereux, Amir Khammari, Capucine Rieuf, Florence Brellier, Laurent Mortier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFollowing the positive findings of the phase 3 CheckMate 238 trial, adjuvant nivolumab has become a standard therapy in patients with completely resected stage III–IV melanoma. The prospective, ongoing AdjuMel study is evaluating the real-world use, effectiveness, and safety of adjuvant nivolumab in patients with resected melanoma in France. Here we report 2-year results from the third interim analysis of AdjuMel.MethodsThis observational, prospective study is evaluating data from patients with resected stage III–IV melanoma treated with adjuvant nivolumab in France. Study outcomes include effectiveness, safety, and treatment patterns of anti-programmed cell death-1 (PD-1, nivolumab) under routine clinical practice in the adjuvant setting. The primary endpoint was relapse-free survival (RFS). This third interim analysis (data cut-off: 3 November 2023) included patients with ≥ 1 follow-up visit, using data collected up to the 24-month visit.ResultsOverall, 343 patients (median [range] age 65 [27–95] years; 57.7% male; 41.7% BRAF-mutation positive) were evaluable in this interim analysis, with a median (range) follow-up of 23.4 (2.1–27.3) months. At treatment initiation, 86.8% and 13.2% of patients had stage III and IV disease, respectively. The 2-year RFS rate was 52.8% (95% confidence interval 47.0–58.2). The risk of recurrence or death was lower in patients with stage IIIA disease versus IIIB–D (hazard ratio [HR] 0.43) and in those aged < 75 versus ≥ 75 years (HR 0.56), and higher in patients with versus without tumor ulceration (HR 1.99). The safety profile of adjuvant nivolumab was consistent with that reported in CheckMate 238, and patient quality of life did not appear to be adversely affected.ConclusionsThese real-world data are in line with those reported in randomized clinical trials, despite the broader inclusion criteria of the study population.Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04550247.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains Supplementary Material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13555-026-01853-4.

Introduction

Ranked as the sixth most common cancer in 2023 [1], melanoma has an estimated overall incidence rate in Europe of up to 35 new cases per 100,000 people, although some Northern European countries have a higher incidence than others [2–5]. In France, the incidence of melanoma is approximately 28 and 22 cases per 100,000 in men and women, respectively [1]. The incidence of melanoma is expected to increase over time, partially due to improvements in early detection, improved awareness, and an aging population [2, 3]. While 5-year survival rates for melanoma are estimated at 60–90% across Europe [2], median overall survival (OS) is typically reduced in patients initially diagnosed at more advanced disease stages [5, 6]. In general, melanoma mortality rates are increasing in Europe, particularly in older patients [2, 4, 7].

Treatment options for advanced melanoma have improved significantly in recent years with the introduction of immunotherapies (e.g., checkpoint inhibitors) and targeted therapies (e.g., B-Raf proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase [BRAF] inhibitors, or mitogen-activated extracellular kinase [MEK] inhibitors) [1, 8]. The combination of immunotherapy and targeted therapy has shown further improvements in outcomes [8]. Although used less frequently now, conventional chemotherapy agents may still be administered in selected patients (such as those refractory to immunotherapy and/or targeted therapy), often in later lines and combined with other agents [8]. Radiotherapy can also be used in the adjuvant setting in cases of incomplete surgical resection or bulky disease, or as palliative therapy [4].

Nivolumab is a selective human monoclonal antibody against the programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) receptor [9]. Adjuvant nivolumab is now an approved and guideline-recommended therapy for patients with completely resected stage III–IV melanoma [10, 11], based on data from the phase 3 CheckMate 238 study [12]. CheckMate 238 demonstrated superior clinical benefit of nivolumab versus ipilimumab when administered as single agents, including improved relapse-free survival (RFS) in patients with completely resected stage IIIB/C or IV melanoma (hazard ratio [HR] 0.68; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.56–0.82; p < 0.0001) [12–14]. In landmark analyses, estimated RFS rates were higher with nivolumab versus ipilimumab through to 5 years [12–16].

Given their less restrictive patient inclusion criteria, data from real-world studies complement those obtained in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [17, 18]. Real-world outcomes provide valuable insight into the routine clinical use and effectiveness of treatment, and help to confirm reported outcomes from the more controlled RCT environment [17, 18]. The prospective, ongoing AdjuMel study is currently evaluating the real-world use and effectiveness of adjuvant nivolumab therapy in patients with resected melanoma in France. Here, we describe the results from the third interim analysis of AdjuMel, providing data at 2 years of follow-up in the evaluable population (i.e., participants who received ≥ 1 dose of adjuvant nivolumab and with ≥ 1 follow-up visit and time-to-event analyses of censored data beyond 24 months).

Methods

Study Design

The prospective, observational AdjuMel study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT04550247) is evaluating the effectiveness, safety, and treatment patterns of adjuvant nivolumab in patients with resected stage III–IV melanoma in routine clinical practice in France. The study design is summarized in Fig. 1. Eligible patients were identified from 49 general and university hospital-based dermatology or oncology care facilities in France. These sites are assumed to be representative of the most active melanoma centers. Patients receiving adjuvant nivolumab therapy (240 mg every 2 weeks [Q2W] or 480 mg every 4 weeks [Q4W]) are being followed for 5 years from the date of the first nivolumab infusion until death, withdrawal of consent, loss of follow-up, or end of the study, whichever is earliest. Patient selection was based on the systematic sampling technique, with participating physicians being asked to include all consecutive eligible patients. In this interim analysis, data from patients who provided consent were analyzed from the start of the first nivolumab infusion to 3 November 2023.Fig. 1AdjuMel study design. The study protocol and any amendments were approved by the necessary institutional review boards/independent ethics committees. The study protocol was reviewed by an accredited Comités de Protection des Personnes (CPP), whose approval covered all participating centers (CPP approval: ID RCB 2020 A00257 32, 31 January 2020; substantial modification approval: 13 November 2024; SI no. 20.00896.002011 MS19). The study also received authorization from the national regulatory authority, ANSM (no. 1478652, 3 March 2020). The study was conducted with the support of a clinical research organization and the French Clinical Database of Melanoma Patients (RIC-Mel), and in accordance with regulatory authority and local governance body guidance. All patients provided oral informed consent prior to participating in the study.

Patients

The study included adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with a primary diagnosis of melanoma and involvement of lymph nodes or resectable metastatic disease. Patients had undergone complete resection and were eligible for adjuvant nivolumab. The physician’s decision to start nivolumab treatment was taken independently of the study (i.e., before the patient was invited to participate).

Patients who received ≥ 1 dose of adjuvant nivolumab prior to enrollment were excluded from the study. Other exclusion criteria were: a current diagnosis of persisting advanced melanoma or cancer other than advanced melanoma; current participation in an interventional clinical trial; or pregnancy (female patients only).

Study Assessments

The primary endpoint of the study was RFS (i.e., time from the start of adjuvant treatment [index date] until the earliest of either disease recurrence or death). If a patient was either still alive without experiencing any disease recurrence or lost to follow-up, RFS was censored on the last date the patient was known to be alive (i.e., time from start of adjuvant treatment [index date] until date of last contact).

Secondary endpoints of the study were patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and previous treatments; OS (i.e., time from the start of adjuvant therapy [index date] until date of death, or, if lost to follow-up, date patient was last known to be alive); and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS; i.e., time from index date until the earliest of either distant metastasis or death). Similarly, if a patient was either still alive without experiencing distant metastasis or loss to follow-up, DMFS was censored on the date of last contact. Other secondary endpoints were adjuvant nivolumab patterns of use (i.e., dosing, treatment duration, and reasons for discontinuation); safety (i.e., incidence and severity of adverse events [AEs], immune-related AEs, serious AEs [SAEs], and treatment-related AEs [TRAEs]); health-related quality of life (HRQoL) using the self-administered 12-item Short Form (SF-12) questionnaire completed at baseline and then every 6 months; and subsequent therapy effectiveness (i.e., RFS2, progression-free survival [PFS; defined as the time from first-line systemic treatment initiation until disease progression or death, whichever occurs first], and OS) and safety. Clinical factors predictive of recurrence or death were analyzed as an exploratory endpoint, as well as subsequent treatments administered after disease recurrence.

During the follow-up period, assessments were performed at baseline (day 0), every 3 months up to 1 year, then every 6 months thereafter until up to 5 years after initiation of adjuvant nivolumab. Data were entered into electronic case report forms.

Statistical Analyses

The study was descriptive in nature, and no formal hypotheses were tested. The sample size was determined from the precision of the estimations, measured as the range of their 95% CIs. With an overall sample size of 350 patients, the precision of the 95% CI for the RFS rate would be within 5%, which was considered sufficient to obtain relevant conclusions.

The primary objective (RFS) was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Other outcome variables were summarized using descriptive statistics, with continuous variables described using numbers and ranges, means, standard deviations (SDs), 95% CIs, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQR), and discrete variables described using numbers and proportions of patients. Effectiveness outcomes were evaluated in the evaluable population, defined as all eligible patients who received ≥ 1 dose of adjuvant nivolumab and with ≥ 1 follow-up visit. Safety was evaluated in the safety population, defined as all patients who received ≥ 1 dose of nivolumab.

Regular interim analyses were scheduled after each year of follow-up. This report presents outcomes from the third interim analysis, i.e., with a data cut-off date 2 years after the last patient was included.

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS release 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patients

At the data cut-off date (3 November 2023), 361 patients had been identified, and 351 fulfilled eligibility criteria and received at least one dose of nivolumab (i.e., the safety population), among whom 343 had ≥ 1 follow-up visit (i.e., the evaluable population; see Supplementary Fig. S1 in the electronic Supplementary Materials). Data were collected between 30 July 2020 and 3 November 2023, representing the period between the index date (first nivolumab infusion) and the 24-month follow-up visit (Fig. 1). Median (range) follow-up was 23.4 (2.1–27.3) months.

The patient baseline demographics and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. The median (range) age at baseline was 65 (27–95) years, and 57.7% of patients were male. The resected stage at treatment initiation was stage III in 86.8% of patients and stage IV in 13.2%. Of 295 patients with known BRAF status, 123 (41.7%) had a BRAF-mutated tumor. Among 264 patients with stage III disease, 141 (53.4%) had tumor ulceration. Real-World Effectiveness of Nivolumab

Relapse-Free Survival

Overall, 155 patients (45.2%) experienced an RFS event, of whom 146 had disease recurrence and 9 died without recurrence. At this third interim analysis, the median RFS was 25.8 months (95% CI 21.0, not reached), and the estimated 2-year RFS rate was 52.8% (95% CI 47.0–58.2; Fig. 2). For analysis of RFS by disease stage, please see Supplementary Table S1 in the electronic Supplementary Materials. Among the patients with disease recurrence, the first recurrence was locoregional only for 66/146 patients (45.2%) and distant (with or without locoregional relapse) for 79/146 (54.1%); the type of recurrence was unknown for 1 patient. Disease recurrence occurred before the end of treatment or within 6 months after stopping treatment in 122 patients and 6 months after stopping treatment in 20; the time of disease recurrence was unknown for 4 patients.Fig. 2Kaplan–Meier curve of relapse-free survival. Overall Survival

Overall, death was recorded for 46 patients (13.4%), but the median OS had not been reached after 2 years of follow-up. The 2-year OS rate was 85.7% (95% CI 81.2–89.1).

Distant Metastasis-Free Survival

DMFS events were reported in 110 patients (32.1%), but the median DMFS had not been reached after 2 years of follow-up. The 2-year DMFS rate was 67.2% (95% CI 61.6–72.1).

Clinical Factors Predictive of Recurrence or Death

Using a multivariate Cox regression model, the risk of disease recurrence or death was significantly lower in patients with stage IIIA disease at baseline than in those with stage IIIB–D disease (HR 0.43; 95% CI 0.24–0.78; p = 0.0051) and in patients aged < 75 versus ≥ 75 years (HR 0.56; 95% CI 0.40–0.79; p = 0.0009; Fig. 3). Conversely, the risk of recurrence or death was significantly higher in patients with versus without tumor ulceration (HR 1.99; 95% CI 1.38–2.89; p = 0.0003) and in patients who had received prior adjuvant radiotherapy than in those who had not (HR 2.07; 95% CI 1.04–4.10; p = 0.0381; Fig. 3).Fig. 3Multivariate Cox model of the risk of disease recurrence or death. *Denotes a statistically significant difference between subgroups. Subsequent Treatments in Patients with Disease Relapse

Among 146 patients with disease recurrence, 109 (74.7%) received subsequent systemic treatment, 51 (34.9%) underwent surgery, and 34 (23.3%) received radiotherapy. The first subsequent systemic treatment was considered to be curative (82/109 [75.2%]), adjuvant (26/109 [23.9%]), or palliative (1/109 [0.9%]). The median time from discontinuation of adjuvant nivolumab to initiation of subsequent systemic therapy was 35.5 days. Overall, immunotherapy was the most common first subsequent treatment administered post-adjuvant nivolumab (63.3%) and for the management of both locoregional (55.6%) and distant recurrences (67.1%; Table 3). Safety

In the safety population (n = 351), 68.7% of patients had ≥ 1 AE of any grade (grade 3–4 AEs, 20.8%; Table 4). Among these, 52.7% of the patients had ≥ 1 AE that was related to adjuvant nivolumab therapy. Health-Related Quality of Life

Overall, 343 patients completed ≥ 1 SF-12 questionnaire during follow-up, including at baseline (n = 334) and at 6 (n = 260), 12 (n = 228), 18 (n = 176), and 24 months (n = 159). Among evaluable patients who completed a questionnaire at baseline and who had available follow-up data, 157 patients completed an SF-12 questionnaire at 24 months. In these patients, the mean (SD) change from baseline in the aggregate physical health score was +0.44 (8.81) at 24 months, with the greatest improvement observed in the bodily pain subscore (+2.06 [11.68]). Similarly, the mean (SD) change from baseline in the aggregate mental health score was +0.67 (10.80) at 24 months, with the greatest improvement observed in the role emotional subscore (+0.85 [10.33]). While the magnitude of these changes may not be clinically meaningful, these results suggest that adjuvant nivolumab treatment does not adversely affect HRQoL.

Discussion

This interim analysis of the AdjuMel study data demonstrated a 2-year RFS rate of 52.8% with adjuvant nivolumab administered in real-world clinical practice, with a safety profile appearing consistent with that reported in CheckMate 238 [12]. Lower disease stage and younger age at baseline were associated with a lower risk of disease recurrence and death with adjuvant nivolumab, whereas the presence of tumor ulceration in patients with stage III disease was associated with a higher risk. Immunotherapy was the most commonly used treatment for the management of locoregional or distant recurrences that occurred after adjuvant nivolumab, and, similar to CheckMate 238 [12], nivolumab treatment did not appear to adversely affect HRQoL. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as long-term HRQoL data were available for only a subset of patients, and the magnitude of observed changes over time was small and may not be clinically meaningful. As such, these results are descriptive and limited by incomplete follow-up and the observational nature of the study.

The 2-year RFS rate in the current study was lower than that in the nivolumab arm of the phase 3 CheckMate 238 study (52.8% versus 62.6%); however, this observation is not unexpected given the inclusion of more high-risk patients in AdjuMel owing to the broader eligibility criteria than those of the phase 3 clinical trial (see Supplementary Table S2 in the electronic Supplementary Materials) [12]. Compared with CheckMate 238, the patient population of AdjuMel was older (median age 65 versus 56 years) and included more patients with stage IIIA disease (17.2% versus 0%). In addition, a greater proportion of patients with stage III disease had tumor ulceration (53.4% versus 39.2%). Moreover, fewer patients with stage IV disease in AdjuMel had metastasis status M1a (44.4% versus 59.8%) and more had M1b (33.3% versus 16.0%) than in CheckMate 238, and fewer patients had BRAF-mutated tumors (35.9% versus 42.1%). Further subgroup analyses (data not shown) indicated that the BRAF mutation was not associated with greater benefit from adjuvant nivolumab. Furthermore, AdjuMel included patient types that were not enrolled in CheckMate 238, such as those pretreated in the adjuvant or neoadjuvant settings (6.4%), patients with concomitant radiotherapy at baseline (3.7%), and patients with ocular melanoma (0.6%) [12]. While equivalence between fixed- and weight-based dosing of nivolumab has been demonstrated in a population pharmacokinetics model [19], it is also important to note that all patients in the nivolumab arm of the CheckMate 238 study received nivolumab at a dosage of 3 mg/kg Q2W rather than the fixed dose of 480 mg Q4W received by the majority of patients in AdjuMel. The dosing used in AdjuMel may mostly be owing to efforts to reduce congestion in hospital day units, allow for fewer hospital visits for people who live more remotely, improve comfort of use for the patient, and address the preference for less frequent visits to the hospital that was implemented during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Importantly, cross-study comparisons should be interpreted cautiously given these differences and the lack of randomization.

Similar to CheckMate 238, KEYNOTE-054 was another phase 3 study evaluating immunotherapy in patients with resected high-risk stage III melanoma (including stage IIIA but no stage IV disease); however, this study used another PD-1 inhibitor (pembrolizumab) [20]. While the KEYNOTE-054 study data cannot be directly compared with those of the current analysis, it is important to note that KEYNOTE-054 reported that pembrolizumab did not negatively impact patient HRQoL versus placebo [21], similar to that observed with nivolumab in AdjuMel and CheckMate 238 [12]. As with other cross-trial comparisons, these observations are descriptive.

The current study findings were generally consistent with those of another observational study evaluating the real-world effectiveness and safety of adjuvant nivolumab. The PRESERV MEL study in Belgium and Luxembourg included 152 patients with completely resected stage III or IV melanoma who received adjuvant nivolumab therapy [22]. After a median follow-up of 18.5 months, the 18-month RFS rate was 68.4%, and the median RFS was not reached. AEs and TRAEs were reported in 96% and 86% of patients, respectively, with 14% of patients experiencing ≥ 1 grade 3–4 TRAEs [22]. However, one important difference compared with the current study is that PRESERV MEL did not include pretreated patients. These similarities should be interpreted cautiously given differences in study populations and methodology.

Data from the AdjuMel study could help prescribers, patients, and health authorities in France better understand the real-world effectiveness and safety of adjuvant nivolumab for the treatment of metastatic melanoma, and thereby inform decision-making. The safety profile of adjuvant nivolumab and effects on HRQoL are reassuring and almost the same as in clinical trials, despite the patient population not being heavily selected. However, these observations should be interpreted cautiously given the study design.

The strengths of the study are its prospective design, which enabled the collection of robust data of interest, the large sample size, the less selected patient population, and the inclusion of patients from university hospitals. The acknowledged limitations of the study include its lack of a control arm and observational design, which meant that patients may not have necessarily followed the planned visit schedule, potentially leading to missing data and underreporting of AEs. In addition, the absence of randomization precludes causal inference and limits the ability to directly compare outcomes with those from clinical trials. Subgroup analyses, including by BRAF status, were exploratory and not powered for formal comparisons. Similarly, prior neoadjuvant and/or adjuvant treatment history did not reach statistical significance in the multivariate model (data not reported in this manuscript); however, this likely reflects limited statistical power owing to the small proportion of pretreated patients (6.4%) and heterogeneity in prior treatment regimens. The observed effect estimate (HR 1.74) and borderline p-value (p = 0.0581) suggest a potentially clinically relevant signal that may be underpowered rather than absent. Further, some patients may not have been followed for the entire study period owing to switching to a smaller center or to a specialist private practice after responding to adjuvant nivolumab therapy. Finally, because all participating sites are located in France, generalizability of these results to other countries should be made with caution.

Conclusions

These interim 2-year data from the AdjuMel study provide insights into the real-world effectiveness and safety of adjuvant nivolumab in patients with resected stage III–IV melanoma in France. The estimated 2-year RFS rate and real-world safety results are generally consistent with those reported in CheckMate 238, although differences in patient characteristics and the noncomparative, observational design should be considered when interpreting these findings. As a single-arm study, no causal inferences can be drawn, and the results should be viewed as complementary to existing trial evidence. The safety profile of adjuvant nivolumab in this real-world setting appeared consistent with prior reports, and HRQoL outcomes were generally stable over time. However, interpretation of HRQoL findings is limited by the availability of long-term data for only a subset of patients, and the magnitude of observed changes was small and may not be clinically meaningful. The findings suggest that approved indications of adjuvant nivolumab, as established in RCTs, may be extended to real-life clinical practice, providing improved outcomes in patients with melanoma who would otherwise be treated with surgery alone. Overall, these findings contribute to a better understanding of treatment patterns and outcomes with adjuvant nivolumab in routine clinical practice, but longer follow-up and comparative analyses are warranted.
