Saar Huberman, Or Patashnik, Omer Dahary, Ron Mokady, Daniel Cohen-Or

Abstract
. Text-to-image diffusion models excel at generating high-quality, diverse images from natural language prompts. However, they often fail to produce semantically accurate results when the prompt contains concept combinations that contradict their learned priors. We define this failure mode as contextual contradiction , where one concept implicitly negates another due to entangled associations learned during training. To address this, we propose a stage-aware prompt decomposition framework that guides the denoising process using a sequence of proxy prompts. Each proxy prompt is constructed to match the semantic content expected to emerge at a specific stage of denoising, while ensuring contextual coherence. To construct these proxy prompts, we leverage a large language model (LLM) to analyze the target prompt, identify contradictions, and generate alternative expressions that preserve the original intent while resolving contextual conflicts. By aligning prompt information with the denoising progression, our method enables fine-grained semantic control and accurate image generation in the presence of contextual contradictions. Experiments across a variety of challenging prompts show substantial improvements in alignment to the textual prompt.

1.
Introduction

Text-to-image diffusion models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in generating high-quality and diverse visual content from natural language prompts
(
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Ramesh et al. 2021
;
Ho et al. 2020
)
. However, achieving precise semantic alignment between the generated image and the conditioning prompt remains an open challenge. This issue becomes particularly pronounced when the target prompt lies outside the model’s training distribution, such as prompts that combine semantically plausible yet statistically uncommon or unconventional concepts.
For example, as shown in Figure
1
, generating an image from the prompt “a rooster in a nest” often results in colorful birds sitting in a nest. This is due to the model’s strong prior entanglement between bird species and nests, which implicitly contradicts the notion of a rooster.

We refer to this phenomenon as
Contextual Contradiction
, a conflict between two concepts that arises not from direct semantic opposition, but from the model’s associations learned during training.
More precisely, we say that concept
A
contextually contradicts concept
B
if the model’s prior entangles
A
with concept
C
, and
B
contradicts
C
.
In Figure
1
, we illustrate this with a blowing dragon, which stands in contextual contradiction with the water due to its entanglement with fire.

The phenomenon of contextual contradiction in text-to-image models relates to the broader issue of spurious correlations in deep learning. Models often rely on shortcuts, which are predictive patterns in the training data that do not reflect true semantics
(
Geirhos et al. 2020
)
. In NLP, models exploit syntactic heuristics rather than reasoning
(
McCoy et al. 2019
)
, while in computer vision, they may depend on background cues rather than object features
(
Xiao et al. 2021
;
Singh et al. 2020
)
. For example, classifiers may fail to recognize cows on beaches due to background biases
(
Beery et al. 2018
)
.
In this paper, we identify similar biases in generative models, where contextual contradictions arise when prompts include plausible but uncommon concept combinations.

To address this issue, we build on the observation that the denoising process follows a coarse-to-fine progression, during which different semantic attributes (e.g., background, pose, shape, and texture) emerge at distinct stages
(
Chefer et al. 2023
;
Balaji et al. 2022
;
Patashnik et al. 2023
)
.
Our key idea is to guide the model at each stage of denoising with the information most relevant to the type of content being formed at that point. To achieve this, we decompose the original prompt into a sequence of proxy prompts, each aligned with the attributes expected at a specific stage and designed to avoid contextual contradictions.

Ensuring that proxy prompts preserve the original intent while avoiding contextual contradictions requires a broad understanding of the real world. For example, it involves understanding that a bear is entangled with specific poses, such as walking on all fours or standing upright, which in turn, contradicts the handstand pose. Our method leverages a large language model (LLM) to analyze the target prompt, identify contextual contradictions, and construct suitable proxy prompts.

We demonstrate that, by using in-context examples and prompting the LLM to follow a reasoning process through a brief explanation, it can identify contextually contradictory concepts in a prompt and determine the appropriate stage of denoising at which each attribute should be introduced.
It can also suggest alternative, non-conflicting concepts that preserve the intended attributes and use them to construct stage-specific proxy prompts.
In doing so, the LLM effectively guides the model toward the intended meaning of the original prompt.

Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our method in generating images from contextually contradicting text prompts.
By introducing prompt information at targeted stages, our approach generates
precise combination of semantic attributes while avoiding undesired entanglement of unrelated attributes.
Compared to previous methods, our stage-dependent prompt decomposition leads to more faithful and semantically aligned generations.

2.
Related Work

Learned Spurious Correlations

Machine learning models are known to be sensitive to spurious correlations in their training data
(
Geirhos et al. 2020
;
McCoy et al. 2019
;
Ye et al. 2024
)
. This sensitivity often leads to a significant drop in performance when training-time associations between concepts do not hold at test time. Prior work has extensively studied this issue in discriminative computer vision tasks, showing, for example, that recognition models tend to rely heavily on background cues
(
Singh et al. 2020
;
Xiao et al. 2021
;
Beery et al. 2018
)
.

In our work, we show that text-to-image models exhibit similar behavior.
When given contextually contradictory prompts – combinations of concepts that conflict with correlations seen during training – diffusion models often fail to generate images that accurately reflect the prompt. We evaluate this behavior using the Whoops! dataset
(
Bitton-Guetta et al. 2023
)
, which contains prompts constructed by first describing two co-occurring elements, and then replacing one with a less compatible alternative. This results in scenarios that are unlikely to occur in the real world.

Semantic Alignment in Text-to-Image Synthesis

Text-to-image models often struggle to fully capture the semantic intent of input prompts,
particularly when prompts involve complex or internally conflicting concepts.
Previous works have analyzed common failure cases and proposed targeted improvements across various stages of the generation pipeline, including enhanced text embedding representations
(
Rassin et al. 2022
;
Feng et al. 2022
;
Tunanyan et al. 2023
)
, refined attention mechanisms
(
Dahary et al. 2024
)
and guidance strategies that leverage attention maps for loss heuristics
(
Chefer et al. 2023
;
Rassin et al. 2023
;
Meral et al. 2024
;
Agarwal et al. 2023
)
.
Despite these advances, existing methods often fail to handle prompts containing contradictory concepts arising from the model’s learned associations. Our work directly addresses this underexplored challenge, focusing on contextually contradictory prompts.

Multi-Prompt Conditioning Techniques

Conditioning diffusion models on multiple prompts has emerged as an effective strategy for improving control and compositionality.
One line of work, primarily focused on personalization, introduces distinct learned tokens at different layers of the model and at various denoising timesteps
(
Alaluf et al. 2023
;
Voynov et al. 2023
)
. This design allows each token to capture different attributes of the personalized concept, leading to improved identity preservation.
Other approaches vary the prompt across timesteps to modulate specific visual properties, such as object shape
(
Liew et al. 2022
;
Patashnik et al. 2023
)
, or alternate between rare and frequent object descriptions to improve attribute binding
(
Park et al. 2024
)
.
Fine-grained spatial control has also been achieved by assigning sub-prompts to separate image regions
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
.
Additionally, some methods leverage multiple diffusion models, each conditioned on different prompt attributes, and combine their outputs into a unified prediction
(
Liu et al. 2022
;
Bar-Tal et al. 2023
)
.
Unlike most prior works,
we focus on utilizing multiple prompt to settle internal semantic tension, where concept combinations lead to contextual contradictions.

LLM-Guided Diffusion

LLMs have demonstrated strong capabilities in language understanding. Moreover, they possess a broad, implicit grasp of world knowledge, acquired through large-scale training on diverse textual data.
Recent approaches have leveraged these capabilities to guide diffusion model generation, often incorporating planning and reasoning to improve semantic alignment
(
Yang et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2024
;
Hu et al. 2024
)
.
In our work, we employ LLMs with in-context learning to identify contextual contradictions, generate proxy prompts, and determine the corresponding timestep ranges for conditioning, while promoting a reasoning process through brief, explanatory outputs.

3.
Method

In this section, we begin by analyzing the coarse-to-fine behavior of the denoising process (Section
3.1
). Building on the insights from our analysis, we introduce our training-free framework for resolving contextual contradictions in text-to-image generation. As illustrated in Figure
2
, our approach consists of two main components:
(i) prompt decomposition (Section
3.2
, top part of the figure), and
(ii) stage-aware prompt injection (Section
3.3
, bottom part of the figure). In the following, we describe each of these components.

3.1.
Coarse-to-Fine Denoising

As shown by previous works
(
Hertz et al. 2022
;
Balaji et al. 2022
;
Chefer et al. 2023
;
Patashnik et al. 2023
)
, the denoising process of diffusion models operates in a coarse-to-fine manner: early steps determine low-frequency structures, while high-frequency details emerge in later steps.

This behavior is reflected in the training process, in which varying noise levels are applied across different timesteps, and the model is trained to reconstruct the original clean image from the noisy input.
High noise levels used at early timesteps yield low signal-to-noise ratios, restricting reconstruction to spatially smooth, low-frequency components, such as background or layout, which are recoverable even when the image is heavily corrupted.
At smaller timesteps, where the signal-to-noise ratio is higher, high-frequency features become more reliably reconstructible.

During inference, the noise is gradually removed as the model generates the image in a coarse-to-fine manner.
This coarse-to-fine nature of the image generation leads to two key observations:
(i)
Irreversibility of details.

At each stage of denoising, the model commits to a certain level of structural detail. Once a given attribute (e.g., layout, coarse shape) has been established, it becomes effectively fixed and cannot be revised in later steps, even if it misaligns with the prompt.
(ii)
Flexibility in high-frequency details.

In early stages, high-frequency details have not yet emerged and thus are unaffected by the prompt. This allows greater flexibility in guiding the model without impacting the final generated fine details.

We demonstrate these observations in Figure
3
, by examining the model’s
x_{0}
predictions throughout the denoising process.
In the top row, the prompt is fixed to “A howling wolf and a flying bat at midday”. In the middle row, we use the proxy prompt “A howling
dog
and a flying
bird
at midday” during the early steps of denoising, and then switch to the same target prompt used in the top row. We replaced “wolf” with “dog” because the two animals are semantically similar and share similar low-frequency visual features. Moreover, “a howling dog” is not contextually associated with nighttime. For similar reasons, we replaced “bat” with “bird”.

The first row illustrates the irreversibility of details. Night colors and a large moon are established early in the denoising process, and although they do not match the prompt, the model is unable to correct them in later steps.

The second column shows the flexibility of high-frequency details.
Although different prompts were used for each row, the layout and object shapes are visually similar, reflecting the fact that distinguishing information between the target and proxy prompts – specifically regarding layout and object shape – has not yet emerged at this early stage of the denoising process.
Yet, since a howling dog, unlike a howling wolf, is not entangled with night, the background in the second row adheres more closely to the prompt.
By switching to the target prompt at this point in the process, we are still able to generate a wolf and a bat rather than a dog and a bird, while also aligning with the word “midday”.

The bottom row illustrates the importance of selecting an appropriate proxy prompt. Snakes and rats are associated with nighttime, which conflicts with the “midday” specification. In addition, their lack of visual similarity to the target objects leads to visual leakage between the bat and the wolf.

These observations motivate a stage-aware prompting strategy that enables precise control over early structural decisions, while allowing more flexible conditioning for attributes that are not yet generated.
Building on this, we now describe the two main components of our method: prompt decomposition and stage-aware prompt injection.

3.2.
Prompt Decomposition

Given a prompt
P
that contains contextually contradictory concepts, we aim to construct a sequence of proxy prompts
\{p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{n}\}
and corresponding timestep intervals
\{I_{1},I_{2},...,I_{n}\}
that together reflect the intended semantics of
P
.
Each proxy prompt
p_{i}
is designed to preserve the relevant semantics of
P
for attributes or concepts that typically form during its assigned interval
I_{i}
. This is crucial due to the
irreversibility of details
in the generation process. At the same time,
p_{i}
is chosen to avoid contradictory combinations that tend to emerge at that stage, leveraging the
flexibility in high-frequency details
.
This decomposition enables conditioning the diffusion model on contextually coherent content that evolves in tandem with the coarse-to-fine structure of the denoising process (see Figure
3
).

To generate these proxy prompts and their associated intervals, we employ a large language model (LLM) with two complementary roles. First, we leverage the LLM’s broad world knowledge and reasoning capabilities to detect semantic contradictions and to propose contextually appropriate substitutes for conflicting concepts. Second, we use its instruction-following and in-context learning capabilities to infer the temporal staging of concepts, guided by examples and instructions that reflect the denoising behavior of diffusion models.

We implement this using a structured prompt template that includes both instructional text and representative input-output examples, along with textual explanations of the contextual contradictions in the original prompts. These examples illustrate successful decompositions that resolve contextual conflicts, as well as cases that require no decomposition, enabling the LLM to generalize from both scenarios. The full instruction prompt is provided in the appendix (see Table
4
).

In-context Examples

Our in-context examples for guiding the LLM include the target prompt as input and the corresponding proxy prompts, each paired with its associated timestep interval, as output. Each example also includes a brief explanation describing the contextual contradiction in the prompt, which is part of the expected output. By requiring the LLM to produce this explanation, we encourage it to engage in a reasoning process, improving its ability to identify contradictions and generate coherent proxy prompts.
The examples cover a range of contradiction types, including semantic mismatches, spatial violations, behavioral implausibilities, and stylistic inconsistencies. We provide these examples in the appendix (see Table
5
).

These examples were constructed by first identifying contextually-contradictory prompts that produce implausible generations in the base model (FLUX), particularly when concept conflicts appear early in the denoising process. Drawing on our coarse-to-fine analysis, we manually decomposed such prompts into proxy prompts sequences that stabilize low-frequency structure before gradually introducing high-frequency details. We used an LLM to generate natural language explanations and validate that each substitution preserve visual and contextual plausibility while resolving conflicts in a temporally coherent manner.

In total, we include 20 such examples, covering both effective decompositions and cases where minimal intervention was sufficient to preserve coherence. These examples guide the LLM in learning successful decomposition strategies, such as proxy substitution, temporal staging, and contradiction deferral.

Concept Substitution

A core mechanism of our prompt decomposition is concept substitution, which promotes contextual coherence by temporarily replacing a conflicting concept in the prompt with a structurally appropriate alternative (see Figure
3
).
A simpler alternative for avoiding contextual contradictions is to remove the conflicting concept entirely. However, we observe that introducing an object only at a later stage, without a placeholder proxy, can affect the perceived size of that object (see Figure
4
). In extreme cases, introducing the object too late may result in it being omitted from the generated image altogether. Therefore, we prefer the concept substitution strategy.

As demonstrated in Figure
3
, choosing the right substitution is key to the method’s success.
When the substituted concept is semantically appropriate and visually similar to the original object during the interval in which it is applied, the model correctly associates it with the original concept.
In contrast, poor substitutions – due to mismatches in scale, pose, location, or to inappropriate timing – can result in extra objects, layout errors, or failure to follow the prompt (see Figure
3
, bottom row, and Figure
5
).

3.3.
Stage-aware Prompt Injection

Given a sequence of proxy prompts
{p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{n}}
, their corresponding timestep intervals
{I_{1},I_{2},...,I_{n}}
, and a text-to-image (T2I) diffusion model, we condition the model using different prompts throughout the denoising process. At each timestep
t
, we apply the prompt
p_{i}
such that
t\in I_{i}
. By aligning each proxy prompt with a specific timestep interval, the denoising process is guided at each stage by concepts relevant to the level of detail emerging at that point. This enables the gradual construction of the image while avoiding conflicts with the model’s learned priors.

This injection mechanism integrates seamlessly into existing inference pipelines without any architectural modifications, and is compatible with a range of pretrained diffusion models. Differences in denoising schedules across models are handled entirely during prompt decomposition (Section
3.2
). We show examples with SD3 in the appendix (see Figure
11
).

Implementation Details.

We use the state-of-the-art text-to-image model FLUX.1 [dev]
(
Labs 2024
)
and GPT-4o as LLM to demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach. Inference is performed using
T=50

We use the baselines hyperparameters as appeared in their original papers or implementation. To ensure a fair comparison, we also use
T=50

Baselines

Since our method builds upon FLUX.1 [dev], we directly compare our results against this base model to clearly demonstrate the improvements achieved by our approach. Additionally, we include a comparison to the training-free method R2F
(
Park et al. 2024
)
, which similarly employs a large language model (LLM) to alternate between different prompts during generation. As R2F is implemented based on SD3
(
Esser et al. 2024
)
, we also compare our results to this model. Lastly, we compare against the fine-tuned model Ella
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
, which is currently publicly available with SD 1.5 as its base model.

Datasets

We evaluate our method using three datasets: Whoops!
(
Bitton-Guetta et al. 2023
)
, Whoops-Hard, and ContraBench.
Whoops!
consists of 500 prompts paired with commonsense-defying images, designed to test visual reasoning and compositional understanding. While relevant to our task, many of its prompts are relatively easy for modern T2I models and fail to consistently reveal their limitations. To address this, we curate a more challenging dataset
Whoops-Hard
, a subset comprising 100 particularly difficult prompts from Whoops!
(
Bitton-Guetta et al. 2023
)
. This challenging benchmark provides a stronger basis for evaluating text-to-image generation given the current performance of state-of-the-art methods. Furthermore, to test more challenging cases of semantic alignment, we introduce a new benchmark
ContraBench
, a curated set of 40 prompts crafted to induce strong contextual contradictions, including background-object mismatches, semantic tension, and scale conflicts. ContraBench serves as a focused, high-difficulty benchmark for evaluating text-image alignment on prompts that contain contextual contradictions. We will make both ContraBench and the Whoops-Hard subset publicly available.

Ablation Studies

To evaluate the contribution of each component in our method, we perform an ablation study on the Whoops-Hard benchmark by selectively removing or modifying individual elements of the method, and present the results in Table

Each configuration isolates a specific design choice to quantify its effect on alignment performance.

Conclusions

We have introduced a training-free framework for resolving contextual contradictions in text-to-image generation – that is, cases where seemingly plausible prompts are internally inconsistent because of strong, hidden biases in the model. Our method identifies and strategically separates such contradictions over time, enabling faithful rendering of prompts that would otherwise yield semantically inconsistent outputs.

At its core, our approach leverages the broad real-world understanding embedded in large language models to reason about the semantic plausibility of prompts. Rather than modifying the training data or fine-tuning the model, we harness this reasoning capacity at inference time to decompose prompts into a sequence of contextually coherent stages. These proxy prompts guide the generative process in alignment with the model’s prior expectations, helping it circumvent conflicts and maintain semantic fidelity.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that our stage-aware decomposition substantially improves alignment and image quality – especially for prompts that challenge the model’s default associations.
Future directions include refining our method using VLM-generated responses to improve proxy prompts generation dynamically during the generation process, and applying our method to text-to-video generation models.

You are an expert assistant in time step dependent prompt conditioning for diffusion models.

Your task is to decompose a complex or contextually contradictory prompt into up to
three
intermediate prompts that align with the model’s denoising stages — from background layout to object identity to fine detail.
Only introduce prompt transitions when needed.

Diffusion Semantics (Low
\rightarrow
High Frequency Progression):

Steps 0–2:
Scene layout and dominant color regions (e.g., sky, forest, sand tone)

Steps 3–6:
Object shape, size, pose, and position

Steps 7–10:
Object identity, material, and surface type (e.g., glass vs. rubber)

Steps 11–13+:
Fine features and local details (e.g., tattoos, insects, facial detail)

Since denoising progresses from coarse to fine, it is crucial to stabilize large-scale visual structures (such as body shape, pose, and background) before introducing small or semantically charged elements (such as facial details, objects in hand, or surreal features).

Substitution Strategy:

1. Begin with high-level layout (background, geometry).

2. Use
placeholder concepts
if needed to stabilize layout before detailed insertions.

3. Substitutes must match in shape, size, and visual function.

4. Replace placeholders as soon as fidelity permits.

5. Do not maintain substitutions longer than needed.

6. If the prompt is visually coherent, return a
single prompt
with no decomposition.

Output Format:

a. Explanation:
A short sentence explaining why decomposition is needed.

b. Final dictionary:

{
"prompts_list": [
"<prompt1>",
"<prompt2>",
"...",
"<target prompt>"
],
"switch_prompts_steps": [<step1>, <step2>, ...]
}

- Do not include any text outside this structure.

A.3.
ContraBench

To evaluate image generation under prompts containing contextual contradictions, we introduce
ContraBench
, presented in Table
7
. This dataset consists of 40 prompts generated using ChatGPT, each intentionally constructed to include visual or semantic inconsistencies challenge standard diffusion models. The prompts span a range of contradiction types, including physical implausibility, role violation, object misplacement, and surreal transformations. ContraBench serves as a targeted benchmark for assessing a model’s ability to produce visually coherent and semantically plausible images in the presence of contradictory concepts.

Our method’s proxy prompt injection mechanism integrates seamlessly into existing inference pipelines without requiring any architectural modifications, and it is compatible with a range of pretrained diffusion models.
