From Evidence to Action: Implementing Evidence-Based Interventions for Youth Substance Use Prevention Through Drug-Free Communities and CDC's ENGAGE Framework
Authors: Douglas R. Roehler, Karrie R. Thompson, Emily Rayburn, Gregory Smith, Julie Guarnizo, Ilenia Morales
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Youth substance use continues to be a persistent public health concern in the United States, worsened by an evolving substance landscape that includes high-potency cannabis, nicotine vaping devices, and exposure to multiple substances. Although substantial evidence supports the effectiveness of evidence-based interventions (EBIs) for preventing youth substance use, many communities encounter difficulties in translating prevention science into coordinated and sustainable practices.Program:The Drug-Free Communities (DFC) Support Program provides critical infrastructure for coalition-based prevention efforts but does not mandate specific interventions. To facilitate the implementation of EBIs by both DFC coalitions and non-DFC coalitions, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) developed ENGAGE: Evidence-Based Strategies to Prevent Youth Substance Use. This framework systematically categorizes prevention evidence into 6 complementary strategies targeting individual, family, school, peer, community, and service-system factors.Implementation:An overview of the ENGAGE framework and case studies showing how strategies can be put into action within DFC-supported coalitions are provided. Two rural coalition case studies demonstrate how ENGAGE facilitates data-driven planning, cross-sector collaboration, and the integration of EBIs across different settings. The coalitions used local data to prioritize strategies, implemented evidence-based curricula and youth engagement initiatives, coordinated community and environmental approaches, and adapted implementation processes to local contexts while maintaining attention to implementation fidelity.Discussion:The case studies highlight common implementation challenges, such as limited workforce capacity, funding instability, data constraints, and the necessity to balance fidelity with cultural and contextual adaptation. ENGAGE supports coalitions in overcoming these obstacles by offering a systems-level framework that aligns activities, mitigates fragmentation, and enhances accountability.Implications for Policy and Practice:Integrating ENGAGE within DFC coalitions can enhance the implementation of EBIs into sustainable community prevention systems. Policy initiatives that bolster coalition infrastructure, workforce development, data management systems, and multiyear funding are essential for advancing effective youth substance use prevention on a broader scale.

Introduction

Youth substance use remains an evolving public health challenge in the United States, despite decades of progress in prevention science, surveillance, and policy. Although recent national data show a leveling out or even declines in some areas of youth substance use, substance use among youth remains widespread, particularly for alcohol, cannabis, and nicotine products.1,2 Youth substance use also differs across communities, with many rural, tribal, and under-resourced areas still experiencing disproportionately high rates of youth substance use.1

The burden of youth substance use is complicated by a rapidly changing and more dangerous substance landscape. Products accessible to youth today are very different from those used by previous generations, both in potency and route of administration. High-potency cannabis concentrates, flavored and disposable nicotine vaping devices, and illicitly manufactured substances contaminated with synthetic opioids or other toxic adulterants have introduced new and complex risks.3,4 This new environment increases the risk for overdose, makes designing prevention messaging more difficult, and burdens prevention and health systems that were originally designed to address a narrower and more stable range of substances. As the substance environment continues to change, prevention strategies must remain flexible while grounded in evidence.

Early initiation of substance use is consistently linked to a greater risk of developing substance use disorders, co-occurring mental health issues, poor academic performance, and negative physical and social outcomes that can persist throughout life.4 Adolescence is a critical developmental stage marked by rapid brain and biological growth, increased susceptibility to social influences, and growing independence.4 During this stage, factors such as the environment, including family dynamics, peer norms, and school environment have a particularly strong influence on behavior.4,5 Prevention efforts that focus on early intervention, boosting protective factors (eg, family and school connectedness, positive extracurricular involvement), and minimizing exposure to risks are key to decreasing substance-related harms.4–6

Evidence-based interventions (EBIs) can delay substance use initiation, decrease usage frequency, and enhance protective factors across individual, family, school, and community settings.5–7 Despite great progress in understanding EBIs, translating prevention research into lasting and widely disseminated change remains challenging. Many communities face limited workforce capacity, fragmented funding sources, and uncertainty about how to select, implement, and maintain EBIs. Implementing such programs with fidelity while adapting to local contexts proves further challenging. Consequently, evidence-based prevention has been unevenly implemented into practice, and promising programs are often short-lived or implemented in isolation rather than integrated into comprehensive and robust prevention systems.7

The Drug-Free Communities (DFC) Support Program was created to tackle these challenges by boosting community prevention efforts through coalition-based approaches. Authorized by Congress in 1997, DFC has focused on cross-sector collaboration, data-driven planning, and evaluation.8 DFC coalitions serve as essential infrastructure by bringing together stakeholders from education, public health, law enforcement, healthcare, and community organizations to plan, coordinate strategies, and leverage resources. Intentionally, while DFC funding supports coalition infrastructure and accountability, it does not mandate specific programs or strategies, leaving coalitions responsible for implementing prevention curricula and programs.

The flexibility provided to coalitions in programming and strategy, although beneficial, also brings challenges. Navigating a complicated and often contradictory research literature, deciding how to prioritize among a multitude of strategies, and integrating programs across different settings can be difficult without a strong and detailed guiding framework. To address this issue, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) developed ENGAGE: Evidence-Based Strategies to Prevent Youth Substance Use, a practical framework designed to help communities translate data into coordinated actions.9 The framework is centered around 6 prevention strategies designed to collectively address individual, family, school, peer, community, and service-system factors associated with youth substance use [Figure 1]. The resource complements DFC by organizing the strongest available evidence into 6 integrated prevention strategies that promote comprehensive, systems-level prevention.8,9

The objective of this article is to illustrate the role of EBIs in preventing youth substance use within DFC-supported community coalitions. We accomplish this by introducing the ENGAGE framework, detailing its 6 separate but complementary strategies, and providing real-world examples from DFC-funded prevention efforts

Evidence-Based Approaches to Youth Substance Use Prevention

Evidence-based approaches to youth substance use are rooted in prevention science, developmental research, and implementation studies. EBIs are defined as programs, practices, or policies that have proven effective through rigorous evaluation and replication across populations and settings.5 In the current context, these interventions target modifiable risk and protective factors known to influence substance use throughout development.

Youth substance use results from a combination of factors operating across multiple levels of the social-ecological model. Individual characteristics such as self-regulation, coping skills, and mental health interact with family dynamics, peer norms, school climate, and broader community conditions, such as substance availability and local policies.9 School-based EBIs typically focus on enhancing knowledge, social-emotional skills, and resistance and refusal training, equipping youth to handle peer pressure and make healthier choices. Family-based programs tend to improve parenting skills, communication techniques, and youth supervision. Community-level strategies generally work to shift norms, improve enforcement, and modify environmental factors that affect access and risk perception of substances. When these approaches are used alone, they are more likely to have small effects; when coordinated and reinforced across different levels and settings, they are more likely to contribute to meaningful reductions in youth substance use.9

Against this backdrop, DFC coalitions are uniquely positioned to implement coordinated, multilevel approaches to substance use prevention.10 By bringing together schools, families, public health agencies, law enforcement, and community organizations through structured partnerships and shared accountability, coalitions help align prevention strategies across different settings and reinforce consistent messages over time.5 However, translating coordination into results requires more than simply selecting evidence-based interventions. Effective coalition-led prevention depends on purposeful planning, clear prioritization, and ongoing monitoring to ensure strategies are executed with quality and adapted appropriately to local contexts.4,5

Ensuring the fidelity of EBI core components is essential for achieving the intended change.7 Fidelity can be assessed by monitoring adherence to core program components, intervention dosage, and the quality of intervention delivery.7 Concurrently, adaptation and nimbleness are necessary to ensure local relevance, feasibility, and community buy-in. Implementation science emphasizes planned adaptation, documenting changes, and continuous quality improvement to balance fidelity with responsiveness to local needs.6,7 These principles are integral to both the DFC model and the ENGAGE framework, emphasizing the importance of implementation quality alongside choosing the right program for the right situation.

The Drug-Free Communities Support Program

Administered by the Office of National Drug Control Policy in partnership with CDC's Division of Overdose Prevention, DFC provides multiyear funding to local coalitions that show broad sector representation, sustained community engagement, and a commitment to data-driven prevention.8

At the core of the DFC model is the understanding that no single organization or intervention can effectively tackle youth substance use alone. DFC stresses coalition governance, shared responsibility, and strategic planning tailored to local contexts and data. Coalitions must include key sectors, such as schools, law enforcement, health care, youth-serving organizations, parents, and faith-based institutions, to ensure prevention efforts align with community priorities, boost local support, leverage diverse expertise, and serve as a sustainability mechanism.

Importantly, DFC funding supports prevention infrastructure rather than prescribing specific programs. The program provides funding for up to 10 years, contingent upon successful reapplication after the first 5 years. This approach allows coalitions to choose EBIs that suit local needs, local context, and capacity. At the same time, it places the responsibility on coalitions to interpret evidence, set priorities, and ensure effective implementation. Without a clear framework, coalitions may struggle to coordinate programs across different settings or to demonstrate how individual activities support broader prevention goals. ENGAGE was developed partly to address this challenge. By organizing prevention evidence into a clear set of strategies, ENGAGE offers DFC coalitions a practical tool to turn infrastructure and funding into coordinated, evidence-based actions.

CDC's ENGAGE Framework

ENGAGE was developed by the CDC to assist communities in translating prevention science into coordinated, actionable strategies to combat youth substance use across all levels of influence.9 ENGAGE organizes the best available evidence into a framework that supports community decision making while aligning with prevention science principles. ENGAGE emphasizes that no single intervention can create lasting change at the population level on its own. Instead, meaningful change happens when complementary strategies are implemented together and reinforced across different settings. ENGAGE closely aligns with the DFC planning process by emphasizing partner involvement, youth participation, use of local data, and ongoing monitoring. For DFC coalitions, ENGAGE provides a common language and organizational structure to support the strategic implementation of EBI, improve program fidelity, and increase accountability across sectors. In this way, ENGAGE serves as a practical framework to strengthen coalitions' capacity to implement and expand evidence-based prevention efforts.

ENGAGE Prevention Strategies

ENGAGE provides a practical, systems-oriented framework for coordinating substance-use prevention across sectors and settings. Rather than representing discrete or sequential program options, the 6 ENGAGE prevention strategies are designed to function collectively as an integrated prevention system, reinforcing protective factors and addressing risk across multiple levels of influence.

Strategy 1: enhance knowledge and skills

This strategy focuses on strengthening individual protective factors by building youth knowledge, decision-making, emotional regulation, coping, and refusal skills—abilities that develop during adolescence.4 ENGAGE promotes interactive, developmentally appropriate approaches that promote critical thinking and healthy behaviors. Evidence-based, curriculum-driven programs such as LifeSkills Training exemplify this approach and have shown long-term reductions in alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, and other drug use.11 While building individual skills is foundational, ENGAGE highlights that these efforts are most successful when supported by positive family, school, and community environments.

Strategy 2: nurture family environments

ENGAGE emphasizes the important role families play in shaping youth attitudes, norms, and behaviors related to substance use. Family-centered prevention strategies seek to strengthen parenting practices, communication, supervision, and emotional support, especially during late childhood and early adolescence when family influence is still strong. Evidence-based programs such as the Strengthening Families Program have been shown to decrease youth substance use and related behavioral issues while enhancing family functioning.12 Within the ENGAGE framework, nurturing family environments reinforce skills introduced through individual and school-based efforts and help ensure consistent expectations across different settings.

Strategy 3: give youth access to resources and activities

This strategy focuses on increasing youth access to positive, prosocial opportunities that foster connection, belonging, and healthy development. ENGAGE frames access to include safe spaces, mentoring, enrichment activities, academic supports, and youth-serving programs that encourage engagement and resilience. By broadening access to supportive resources and structured activities, this strategy creates opportunities for prosocial involvement and strengthens protective social bonds.5,9

Strategy 4: amplify protective community environments

ENGAGE recognizes that community norms, peer influences, and social conditions greatly influence youth substance use behaviors. This strategy aims to create environments that promote healthy norms, correct misconceptions about substance use, and amplify youth voice and leadership in prevention efforts. Involving youth as partners helps ensure prevention messages are credible, locally relevant, and responsive to local realities. By strengthening community connectedness and encouraging positive peer influence, this approach reduces pressures that contribute to initiation and escalation of substance use.

Strategy 5: guide efforts to lessen immediate and long-term harms

ENGAGE recognizes that youth who have experienced violence and other adverse experiences or who have begun using substances may require additional services and support to prevent immediate and long-term harms. This strategy includes services and support for youth exposed to violence and other adverse experiences, as well as treatment and services for youth demonstrating early substance use behaviors. Within DFC coalitions, advancing this strategy may involve identifying youth immediate needs, strengthening connections to appropriate community-based services, and coordinating referral pathways across schools, behavioral health providers, and other youth-serving organizations.

Strategy 6: encourage provider and health system engagement

The final ENGAGE strategy emphasizes the role of health care providers and health systems in preventing youth substance use through routine interaction, screening, and early intervention. Primary care visits provide opportunities for providers to discuss substance use and associated risks with youth, while screening combined with appropriate intervention can identify youth at elevated risk and connect them with needed services. Within DFC coalitions, advancing this strategy may involve strengthening partnerships with health care providers, promoting evidence-based screening and brief intervention, and improving referral pathways between health care and community-based prevention and treatment services.

Case Studies

The following case studies demonstrate how DFC coalitions implement the ENGAGE framework. These examples highlight how ENGAGE strategies are combined, adapted to local contexts, and maintained. The case studies highlight coordinated, systems-level prevention approaches that integrate individual, family, school, and community strategies.

Case study 1: Drug-Free Dickson Coalition (DFDC)

The Drug-Free Dickson Coalition (DFDC) serves Dickson County, a predominantly rural community in Middle Tennessee characterized by geographic dispersion, limited public transportation, and constrained access to behavioral health services. Like many rural areas, Dickson County faces structural challenges that complicate prevention efforts, including economic stress, limited youth-serving infrastructure, and reduced access to prevention and treatment resources. At the same time, the community benefits from strong local relationships and institutional partnerships that provide a foundation for coalition-based prevention.

DFDC was established in 2012 in response to growing concerns about alcohol, tobacco, and other drug use among local youth. Early coalition activities focused primarily on awareness-raising, community education, and relationship-building across sectors. While these efforts helped establish visibility and trust, coalition leadership increasingly recognized the need to transition toward more structured, evidence-based prevention strategies capable of producing measurable, population-level impact.

This shift aligned closely with the principles of the DFC Program, which emphasizes data-driven planning, cross-sector collaboration, and accountability for outcomes.8 DFDC began formalizing governance structures, strengthening sector representation, and prioritizing systematic use of local data to guide decision making. Local youth survey data revealed early initiation of alcohol use, rising nicotine vaping among middle school students, and increasing cannabis use by high school. Although these trends mirrored national patterns, they were amplified locally by limited enforcement capacity, normalization of vaping, and increased exposure to substance-promoting content through social media.

Local data also revealed elevated levels of anxiety, stress, and psychological distress among youth, underscoring the need for prevention strategies that addressed both substance use and underlying risk factors. In response, DFDC adopted a comprehensive prevention approach aligned with CDC's ENGAGE framework, recognizing that no single intervention could address the full range of risks facing local youth.

Receipt of a DFC grant in 2021 marked a turning point for the coalition. Funding supported expanded staffing, more formal strategic planning, and deeper partnerships with schools, health care providers, law enforcement, and youth-serving organizations. The coalition refined its strategic plan using local data and aligned prevention activities with evidence-based approaches later reflected in the ENGAGE strategies, supporting coherence and accountability.

Guided by a focus on enhancing knowledge and skills and promoting healthy school environments, DFDC selected LifeSkills Training11 as a core school-based prevention strategy. Program selection was informed by the evidence base, alignment with identified risk factors, and feasibility within local school schedules. Implementation challenges included licensing costs, facilitator training, and competing academic demands. DFDC addressed these barriers through supplemental funding, early engagement with school administrators, and alignment with existing health education standards.

Implementation fidelity was supported through structured facilitator training, ongoing coaching, and routine monitoring. DFDC used implementation checklists, observation tools, and feedback mechanisms to ensure core components were delivered as intended while allowing limited, planned adaptations to enhance local relevance. These efforts reflect ENGAGE's emphasis on balancing fidelity with responsiveness and illustrate the application of implementation science principles in a real-world setting.5–7

Consistent with a systems-level approach, DFDC integrated school-based programming with broader coalition activities. The coalition partnered with the juvenile justice system to deliver prevention education for youth participating in diversion programs, linking skill-building with early intervention. Community awareness campaigns and parent education initiatives reinforced school-based messaging and began to shift perceived normative beliefs about vaping across the community.

Ongoing use of data remained central to DFDC's prevention efforts. The coalition regularly reviewed youth survey results, implementation metrics, and qualitative feedback from partners to assess progress and identify emerging trends. When data indicated increased use of disposable vaping devices, DFDC adapted messaging and outreach strategies accordingly, demonstrating the adaptive capacity emphasized by both DFC and ENGAGE. In 2024, DFDC was designated a CADCA Blue Ribbon Coalition, recognizing excellence in coalition functioning, data-driven decision making, and the implementation of evidence-based prevention strategies.13

Together, the DFDC experience illustrates how a DFC-supported coalition can evolve from awareness-based activities to a coordinated, evidence-based prevention system.

Case study 2: Gratiot County Substance and Suicide Prevention Program (GCSSPP)

The Gratiot County Substance and Suicide Prevention Program (GCSSPP) serves Gratiot County, a rural Mid-Michigan community of approximately 40,000 residents characterized by small towns, unincorporated townships, and a strong agricultural identity. The county includes a mix of generational farming families and longstanding local institutions that shape community norms and relationships. Like many rural areas, Gratiot County faces structural challenges that hinder public health efforts, such as limited public transportation, shortages of behavioral health providers, elevated poverty rates, and relatively few extracurricular opportunities for youth. Yet, the county benefits from a strong school identity, a culture of volunteerism, and durable cross-sector relationships that provide a foundation for collective action.

Youth substance use prevention is a key part of GCSSPP's mission. GCSSPP has offered evidence-based curricula across all 6 school districts, supports alternatives to suspension for vaping and tobacco use, and maintains a countywide youth coalition that engages middle and high school students in peer-led prevention campaigns and leadership development. These activities collectively reflect efforts across multiple ENGAGE strategies, including improving knowledge and skills, fostering positive peer norms, promoting healthy school environments, and creating supportive community conditions.

In line with the DFC model, GCSSPP depends on cross-sector collaboration as the key prevention infrastructure. The coalition unites schools, health care providers, law enforcement, local government, Alma College, and grassroots organizations to synchronize prevention priorities and coordinate actions. In a rural setting where formal service systems are scarce, these relationships are vital for maintaining efforts.

GCSSPP relied on local data to guide its prevention efforts. Student survey data from 2022 to 2024 showed shifting patterns of substance use and risk perception among youth. Vaping increased among seventh- and ninth-graders, while alcohol and cannabis use rose among ninth-graders. By contrast, cigarette use continued to decline across grade levels. Risk perception data also indicated a decline in perceived risk related to regular alcohol and cannabis use, along with changes in perceived access. These findings together suggested a changing risk environment that required adaptive, data-driven prevention responses.

In response, GCSSPP refined its strategy to focus on skill development and strengthening protective norms across schools and community settings. Evidence-based curricula such as Too Good for Drugs14 and Too Good for Violence15 were offered across all 6 school districts. To date, implementation has occurred in 3 of the 6 schools, promoting consistent prevention messaging. These programs emphasize decision-making, coping, and resistance skills, targeting individual risks and strengthening behavioral expectations at school. In addition to DFC funding, GCSSPP has received strong, ongoing support from the state's Medicaid Managed Care Organization, allowing for expanded reach and careful fidelity planning.

Youth engagement is a key part of GCSSPP's prevention approach. A countywide youth coalition and the Gratiot County Youth Leadership Summit provide structured opportunities for students from the 3 schools to get involved in leadership development, peer education, and prevention planning. By positioning youth as active participants rather than passive receivers, the coalition promotes positive peer norms while strengthening the relevance and credibility of prevention messages.

Implementation challenges reflected the realities of rural coalition work and school-based systems. Early staff turnover disrupted continuity of youth engagement, requiring repeated efforts to rebuild trust with students and school staff. In response, GCSSPP refined its hiring practices to focus on selecting new candidates with strong interpersonal skills, adaptability, and personal characteristics that align with the school culture, resulting in greater stability and consistency in the program. Academic and extracurricular demands sometimes affected student participation, prompting the coalition to integrate prevention activities within existing school structures, such as advisory periods and health classes. In addition, relationships with school leadership proved essential for successful implementation. Differences in administrative priorities, disciplinary philosophies, and leadership turnover influenced how prevention efforts were adopted across districts. GCSSPP addressed these challenges through consistent communication, strategic use of local data to highlight needs, and aligning prevention efforts with school goals. These strategies helped build trust and foster deeper integration of prevention programming.

Overall, the GCSSPP case study shows how a DFC-supported coalition can turn strategies within ENGAGE into a coordinated prevention system rather than just separate programs. By combining evidence-based curricula, youth leadership, cross-sector collaboration, and ongoing data use, the coalition has developed a prevention approach that adapts to changing substance use patterns and reflects the challenges of a rural community.

Together, the DFDC and GCSSPP cases highlight the flexibility of the ENGAGE framework across rural contexts and in different community settings and emphasize the importance of coalition infrastructure in turning evidence-based prevention into actionable practice.

Challenges for Practice

Despite significant progress in prevention science and the availability of EBIs, the implementation of youth substance use prevention remains inconsistent across communities. The gap between what is proven effective and what is actually practiced highlights ongoing structural, organizational, and contextual challenges that limit the reach and long-term impact of prevention efforts. Understanding these obstacles is essential for translating frameworks like ENGAGE into sustainable public health outcomes, especially within DFC coalitions.

One of the most common issues is limited workforce capacity. Many DFC coalitions and local public health agencies operate with minimal staff, often relying on a small number of people to coordinate partnerships, manage grants, deliver programs, oversee data collection, and report results, among other critical roles. These challenges are especially severe in rural and under-resourced communities, where prevention activities are often added to existing roles rather than supported as dedicated positions. High staff turnover makes these problems worse by disrupting continuity, eroding institutional knowledge, and increasing dependence on short-term or volunteer workers. Without sufficient capacity, even well-chosen EBIs may be implemented inconsistently or abandoned entirely.

Funding instability is another related obstacle. Although EBIs are cost-effective over time, they often require initial investments for training, licensing, materials, and technical support. Short-term grant cycles and categorical funding streams hinder coalitions' ability to plan for sustainability and discourage the long-term integration of EBIs into schools, community organizations, and service systems. When funding ends, programs are often scaled back or discontinued despite proven effectiveness, which damages trust among partners and reduces the return on previous investments. This is an important reason why DFC allows for up to 10 years of funding. This pattern underscores the mismatch between short-term funding and the long-term goals of prevention.

Data and evaluation capacity also pose significant challenges. While the DFC model emphasizes data-driven planning and accountability, many coalitions lack access to strong data systems or the expertise necessary to monitor and assess implementation fidelity, track outcomes, and support ongoing quality improvement. Youth surveys may be conducted infrequently, data might not be available at the appropriate level of detail, and results may not arrive in time to inform decisions. Without actionable data, prevention efforts might continue without clear evidence of impact, making it harder to justify ongoing investment or respond effectively to emerging substance use trends. DFC coalitions are required to regularly collect core measurement data—allowing for the tracking of program success and needed modifications.

Adaptation adds additional complexity. Many EBIs were created and tested in specific populations and may not fully represent the cultural norms, languages, or lived experiences of all communities. What might be a successful program in one setting could be ineffective in another without proper tailoring. When adaptations are made without proper guidance, program fidelity can be compromised; when adaptations are avoided, engagement and effectiveness may decline. For successful implementation, it is necessary to plan adaptations, document changes, and involve the community meaningfully to balance fidelity with responsiveness.5,7 Addressing these issues is crucial to ensure prevention efforts are set up to succeed.

ENGAGE offers a practical framework for addressing these challenges by rethinking prevention as a coordinated, systems-level effort instead of a collection of isolated programs. For DFC coalitions, ENGAGE provides a common structure for aligning activities across sectors, clarifies how individual programs support larger prevention goals, and helps prioritize strategies based on local data. By emphasizing integration across individual, family, school, peer, community, and service-system levels, ENGAGE assists coalitions in moving beyond fragmented efforts toward comprehensive prevention systems.

Conclusion

Youth substance use prevention requires coordinated systems, not standalone activities. The DFC model provides coalition infrastructure, and CDC's ENGAGE framework strengthens that infrastructure by organizing evidence-based strategies across individual, family, school, peer, community, and service-system levels. Using evidence-based frameworks (eg, ENGAGE) can strengthen local planning and coordination by helping coalitions translate local data into complementary prevention strategies. The case studies illustrate how coalitions use local data, cross-sector partnerships, and implementation supports to integrate prevention efforts and adapt to emerging substance use trends. Persistent challenges, such as workforce, funding, and uneven data capacity, continue to hamper sustainability.
