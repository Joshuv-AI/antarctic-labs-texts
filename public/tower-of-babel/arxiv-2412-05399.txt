Approximating Analytic Spectra of Hyperbolic Systems with Summation-by-Parts Finite Difference Operators
Brittany A. Erickson

Abstract
In this work we explore the fidelity of numerical approximations to the analytic spectra of hyperbolic partial differential equation systems with variable coefficients. We are particularly interested in the ability of discrete methods to accurately discover sources of physical instabilities. By considering the perturbed equations that arise in linearized problems, we study systems in which a lower-order term can act as a source of internal energy within the system. We apply high-order accurate summation-by-parts finite difference operators, with weak enforcement of boundary conditions through the simultaneous-approximation-term technique, which leads to a provably stable numerical discretization with formal order of accuracy given by p = 𝟐 , 𝟑 , 𝟒 p=2,3,4 and 𝟓 5 . We derive analytic solutions using Laplace transform methods, which provide important ground truth to ensure numerical convergence at the correct theoretical rate. We derive the analytic spectrum and find that it is better captured with mesh refinement, although dissipative strict stability (where the growth rate of the discrete problem is bounded above by the analytic) is not obtained. We also find that sole reliance on mesh refinement can be a problematic means for determining physical growth rates as some eigenvalues emerge (and persist with mesh refinement) based on spatial order of accuracy but are non-physical. We suggest that numerical methods be used to approximate the spectra when numerical stability is guaranteed and convergence of the numerical spectra is evident with both mesh refinement and increasing order of accuracy.

Systems of hyperbolic partial differential equations emerge across vast application areas, from gravitational waves to drug delivery targeting cancerous tissues, to unsteady volcanic eruptions

When nonlinearities are present, the governing equations can be notoriously challenging to analyze and solve and are often linearized around a background and/or steady state solution. Even so, analytic solutions can be difficult to obtain and one often turns to numerical methods in order to explore the underlying physics and/or origin of unstable regimes. However, there are several stumbling blocks when using numerical methods to explore physical instabilities. If one observes an instability in the numerical simulation it can be difficult to determine if it is numerical or physical in origin. In addition, the spectrum of the discretization could appear to converge to eigenvalues that are artifacts of the numerical method but are non-physical.

In this work we explore the fidelity of numerical approximations to analytic spectra, focusing on variable-coefficient hyperbolic systems containing a lower-order term, which arise in perturbation studies (e.g. when nonlinear equations are linearized, for example in

3
Governing Equations

We consider hyperbolic systems of the form

U_{t}+A(x)U_{x}=B(x)U+F(x,t),\quad x\in[0,L],\quad t\geq 0,

(1)

where
F
is a known source function,
A(x),B(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
are spatially varying coefficient matrices. Here
A(x)
is diagonalizable with real eigenvalues and
B(x)U
is a lower order term often referred to as the “damping” term, when it does not contribute to growth of the system’s internal energy
[
22
]
. We assume
A=V\Lambda V^{-1}
, with
\Lambda=\text{diag}(-\bar{c}(x),\bar{c}(x))
, which pertains to applications with information flowing in two directions at speed
\bar{c}(x)
, for example the Euler equations in fluid dynamics where
\bar{c}(x)
is the fluid sound speed.
U=[u_{1},u_{2}]^{T}
is the vector of physical variables. For the 1D Euler equations, for example,
U
would correspond to the vector of fluid velocity and pressure.

An energy estimate for (
1
) can be obtained through the diagonalization

W_{t}+\Lambda(x)W_{x}=\tilde{B}(x)W+\tilde{F}(x,t),\quad x\in[0,L],\quad t\geq 0,

(2)

where
W=V^{-1}U
,
\tilde{B}=-\Lambda V^{-1}V_{x}+V^{-1}BV
,
\tilde{F}=V^{-1}F
. We consider the general case where
\tilde{B}(x)=[a(x),b(x);c(x),d(x)]\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and derive conditions in which
\tilde{B}
contributes to internal energy growth. Boundary conditions for (
2
) are motivated by the energy estimate we derive shortly and are given by

\displaystyle w_{2}(0,t)

\displaystyle=R_{0}w_{1}(0,t)+h_{0}(t),

(3a)

\displaystyle w_{1}(L,t)

\displaystyle=R_{L}w_{2}(L,t)+h_{L}(t),

(3b)

where
R_{0},R_{L}
are reflection coefficients constrained by the energy estimate. Note that a non-diagonal
\tilde{B}
matrix in (
2
) couples the components within the interior of the domain, whereas conditions (
3
) couple components at boundaries for
R_{0},R_{L}\neq 0
.

Supplementing (
2
)-(
3
) with the initial condition

W(x,0)=f(x),

(4)

taking
\tilde{F}=h_{0}=h_{L}=0
(not required but eases the analysis) an energy estimate can be obtained by multiplying (
2
) by
W^{T}
, adding the transpose, and integrating by parts. This process yields the estimate

\displaystyle\dot{E}

\displaystyle=\bar{c}(L)(R_{L}^{2}-1)w_{2}^{2}(L)+\bar{c}(0)(R_{0}^{2}-1)w_{1}^{2}(0)+

\displaystyle(W,\Lambda_{x}W)+(W,\tilde{B}W)+(\tilde{B}W,W),

(5)

where we define the energy
E=||W||^{2}
using the
L_{2}
scalar product and norm

(U,W)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{L}U^{T}Wdx,\quad||U||=\sqrt{(U,U)}.

(6)

Estimate (
5
) illustrates the well-known result that reflection coefficients should satisfy
-1\leq R_{0},R_{L}\leq 1
for there to be no energy growth from the boundaries. Assuming this and letting
\alpha=||\tilde{B}||=\underset{||U||=1}{\text{max}}||\tilde{B}U||
and
\gamma=||\Lambda_{x}||
,
applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to (
5
) further reduces it to

\displaystyle\dot{E}

\displaystyle\leq(W,\Lambda_{x}W)+(W,\tilde{B}W)+(\tilde{B}W,W)

\displaystyle\leq(\gamma+2\alpha)E.

(7)

Equation (
7
) can be integrated in time to yield the final result

E(t)\leq\exp\left[(\gamma+2\alpha)t\right]E(0)=\exp\left[(\gamma+2\alpha)t\right]||f||^{2},

(8)

which shows that energy in the system is bounded in terms of the data of the problem, i.e. the problem is well posed. Some exponential growth must be tolerated, however, due to the effects of
\tilde{B}
and
\Lambda_{x}
(the latter of which includes those from spatially varying wave speeds), see chapter 3 in
[
23
]
.

4
Analytic Spectra

Specific rates of growth or decay associated with the initial boundary value problem (
2
)-(
4
) can be determined by computing the
analytic spectrum. We denote the Laplace transform of a locally integrable function
h(t)
by

\mathcal{L}[h]=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}h(t)\exp\left(-st\right)dt,\quad s\in\mathbb{C}.

(9)

Taking the initial data
f=0
(and still considering
h_{0},h_{L},F=0
as in the energy estimate) and Laplace transforming in time yields

s\hat{W}+\Lambda(x)\hat{W}_{x}=\tilde{B}(x)\hat{W},\quad x\in[0,L],\quad s\in\mathbb{C},

(10)

where we use a hat to denote the Laplace transform. Equation (
10
) can be re-written as

\hat{W}_{x}=M(x,s)\hat{W},\quad x\in[0,L],\quad s\in\mathbb{C},

(11)

where

\displaystyle M(x,s)

\displaystyle=\Lambda^{-1}(x)(\tilde{B}(x)-sI)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-(a(x)-s)/\bar{c}(x)&-b(x)/\bar{c}(x)\\
c(x)/\bar{c}(x)&(d(x)-s)/\bar{c}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

In this work we compute the associated analytic spectrum, considering several cases for the spatially varying coefficients. Case 1 assumes constant coefficients and that
\tilde{B}
is a diagonal matrix. This is a particular case of the more general, but is useful for illustrative purposes. In Case 2 we still assume constant coefficients, but assume a slightly more general case where
\tilde{B}
is not diagonal. We then include some results for special cases of variable coefficients, namely Case 3 when
\tilde{B}
is still a diagonal matrix. For these three cases the solution to
(
11
) is given by

\hat{W}(x,s)=\exp\left(\int^{x}M(\xi,s)d\xi\right)C(s),

(13)

where
C(s)=[C_{1}(s),C_{2}(s)]
are coefficients determined by the boundary conditions. We also include the set-up for Case 4 (the most general case) when
\tilde{B}
is not diagonal, which will be explored in numerical studies.

4.1
Case 1: Constant coefficients;
\tilde{B}
is a diagonal matrix

In this case we have that
b=c=0
, thus the eigenvalues of
M
are given by

\lambda_{1,2}(s)=-(a-s)/\bar{c},\quad(d-s)/\bar{c},

(14)

with eigenvectors
v_{1},v_{2}
the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus (
13
) reduces to

\hat{W}(x,s)=C_{1}(s)\exp\left[\lambda_{1}(s)x\right]v_{1}+C_{2}(s)\exp\left[\lambda_{2}(s)x\right]v_{2}.

(15)

Substituting the Laplace transform of boundary conditions (
3
) into (
15
) yields the linear system
A(s)C(s)=0
where

A(s)=\begin{pmatrix}-R_{0}&1\\
\exp\left[-(a-s)L/\bar{c}\right]&-R_{L}\exp\left[(d-s)L/\bar{c}\right]\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

As we are interested in non-trivial solutions to (
11
), we must have that
\text{det}(A)=0
, i.e.

R_{0}R_{L}\exp\left[L\lambda_{2}(s)\right]-\exp\left[L\lambda_{1}(s)\right]=0.

(17)

The above condition (
17
) is met at discrete
s_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
which define the analytic spectrum, namely

s_{n}=\frac{(a+d)L+\bar{c}\ln(R_{0}R_{L})+2\bar{c}\pi in}{2L},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(18)

Note that this result implies that the spectrum only exists if
R_{0}R_{L}\neq 0
, namely, the two characteristic variables must be coupled at domain boundaries; a non-reflecting boundary condition will imply no analytic spectrum. Furthermore, values of
s_{n}
with positive real part can exist if
a+d
is sufficiently positive. For example, if
R_{0}=R_{L}\in\{-1,1\}
, then
\text{Re}(s_{n})>0
if
a+d>0
, leading to physical growth of the system. However, the term
\ln(R_{0}R_{L})
will counteract positive values of
a+d
if it is sufficiently negative, leading to energy decay.

4.2
Case 2: Constant coefficients;
\tilde{B}
is not diagonal

Here the eigenvalues of
M
are

\lambda_{1,2}(s)=\frac{-(a-d)}{2\bar{c}}\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\text{disc}(M)},

(19)

where the discriminant of
M
is

\text{disc}(M)=\left(\frac{a-d}{\bar{c}}\right)^{2}-4\left[\frac{bc-(a-s)(d-s)}{\bar{c}^{2}}\right].

(20)

We first look for
s
-values corresponding to distinct eigenvalues (where
\text{disc}(M)\neq 0
, i.e.
s
-values where
s\neq\frac{a+d}{2}\pm\sqrt{bc})
, each with multiplicity 1. Since by assumption both
b
and
c
cannot both be zero, we assume WLOG that
b\neq 0
. Then the linearly independent eigenvectors of
M
corresponding to each eigenvalue are given by

v_{1,2}=[1,\quad[-(a-s)-\lambda_{1,2}\bar{c}]/b].

(21)

In this case,
\text{det}(A)=0
yields the equation

\displaystyle\exp\left(\lambda_{1}L\right)(-1+R_{L}v_{1}^{(2)})(-R_{0}+v_{2}^{(2)})+

\displaystyle\exp\left(\lambda_{2}L\right)(1-R_{L}v_{2}^{(2)})(-R_{0}+v_{1}^{(2)})=0.

(22)

If
R_{0}=R_{L}=R\in\{-1,\,\,1\}
then (
22
) simplifies to

[\exp\left(\lambda_{1}L\right)-\exp\left(\lambda_{2}L\right)](-1+Rv_{1}^{(2)})(-R+v_{2}^{(2)})=0.

(23)

We note that by inspection, the second two terms in (
23
) yield the real root

s_{0}=\frac{a+R(b+c)+d}{2}.

(24)

Complex roots also exist when

\exp\left[\lambda_{1}(s)L\right]=\exp\left[\lambda_{2}(s)L\right],

(25)

(although by assumption,
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
)
and one can check that solutions to (
25
) are the roots

s^{\pm}_{n}=\frac{a+d}{2}\pm\sqrt{bc-\pi^{2}n^{2}\bar{c}^{2}/L^{2}},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\char 92\relax\{0\},

(26)

which produces purely real roots for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\char 92\relax\{0\}
such that

bc\geq\pi^{2}n^{2}\bar{c}^{2}/L^{2}
.

We now look for
s-
values corresponding to a repeated eigenvalue
\lambda=\frac{-(a-d)}{2\bar{c}}
(i.e. where
s=\frac{a+d}{2}\pm\sqrt{bc}
, so that
\text{disc}(M)=0
) with multiplicity 2 and only one eigenvector. The general solution (
13
) reduces to

\displaystyle\hat{W}(x,s)=

\displaystyle C_{1}(s)\exp\left[\lambda(s)x\right](I+x(M-\lambda(s)I))v_{1}+

\displaystyle C_{2}(s)\exp\left[\lambda(s)x\right](I+x(M-\lambda(s)I))v_{2},

(27)

where
v_{1},v_{2}
are linearly independent solutions of
(M-\lambda I)^{2}v=0
, namely
v_{1}=[1,0]^{T}
and
v_{2}=[0,1]^{T}
. Imposing boundary conditions (
3
) and seeking non-trivial solutions as before yields the equation

\displaystyle\exp\left[\lambda(s)L\right]

\displaystyle\left[-R_{0}\left(-\frac{bL}{\bar{c}}-R_{L}\left[1+\frac{L}{\bar{c}}\left(\frac{a+d}{2}-s\right)\right]\right)-\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left(1+\frac{L}{\bar{c}}\left[s-\frac{a+d}{2}\right]-R_{L}\frac{cL}{\bar{c}}\right)\right]=0.

(28)

In the case that
R_{0}=R_{L}=R\in\{-1,1\}
, (
28
) reduces to

\exp\left[\lambda(s)L\right]\left[R\frac{L}{\bar{c}}\left(b+c\right)+\frac{L}{\bar{c}}\left(a+d-2s\right)\right]=0.

(29)

The second term in (
29
) yields the single real root
s_{0}
.

For other values of
R_{0},R_{L}
, roots of (
22
) are more challenging to compute analytically but could be determined numerically.

4.3
Case 3: Variable coefficients;
\tilde{B}
is a diagonal matrix

We first define the function

I_{\bar{c}/\delta}(x)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\delta(y)}{\bar{c}(y)}dy,

(30)

with
I_{\bar{c}}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{\bar{c}(y)}dy
corresponding to the time it takes to travel a distance
x
at speed
\bar{c}(x)
. We can write the general solution (
13
) as

\displaystyle\hat{W}(x,s)=

\displaystyle C_{1}(s)\exp\left[-I_{\bar{c}/a}(x)+sI_{\bar{c}}(x)\right]v_{1}+

\displaystyle C_{2}(s)\exp\left[I_{\bar{c}/d}(x)-sI_{\bar{c}}(x)\right]v_{2}.

(31)

Application of boundary conditions and the desire for non-trivial solutions requires again solving
\text{det}(A)=0
, which yields the equation

R_{0}R_{L}\exp\left[I_{\bar{c}/d}(L)-sI_{\bar{c}}(L)\right]-\exp\left[-I_{\bar{c}/a}(L)+sI_{\bar{c}}(L)\right]=0,

(32)

which can be solved for the discrete eigenvalues

s_{n}=\frac{\ln(R_{0}R_{L})+I_{\bar{c}/d}(L)+I_{\bar{c}/a}(L)+2\pi ni}{2I_{\bar{c}}(L)},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(33)

which reduces to (
18
) in the case of constant coefficients.

Case 4: Variable coefficients;
\tilde{B}

For this case it is more challenging to compute an analytic spectrum since the general solution to the differential equation (

5
SBP-SAT Finite Difference Approximation

Analytic solutions to the governing equations (
2
) - (
4
) can often be difficult to obtain and we turn to numerical methods. In this work we consider a class of high-order accurate finite difference methods known as summation-by-parts (SBP), with weak enforcement of boundary conditions through the simultaneous-approximation-term (SAT) technique. As opposed to traditional finite difference methods that “inject” boundary data by overwriting grid points with the given data, the SAT technique imposes boundary conditions weakly through penalization, so that all grid points approximate both the PDE and the boundary conditions up to a certain level of accuracy. The combined approach is known as SBP-SAT and provides a provably stable semi-discretization in space
[
11
,
12
,
25
]
. The spatial derivative operators involve centered difference approximations within the domain interior, with a special transition to one-sided differences near domain boundaries. We use the diagonal norm SBP-SAT operators from
[
10
]
that have a formal order of accuracy given by
p=2;3;4
and
5
, with interior order of accuracy
2p-2
and boundary accuracy of
p-1
.

5.1
Discrete Operators

The spatial domain
0\leq x\leq L
is discreteized with
N+1
evenly spaced grid points
x_{i}=0+ih,i=0,\dots,N
with grid spacing
h=1/N
. A function
u
projected onto the computational grid is denoted by
{\boldsymbol{{u}}}=[u_{0},u_{1},\dots,u_{N}]^{T}
and is often taken to be the interpolant of
u
at the grid points. The grid basis vector
{\boldsymbol{{e}}}_{j}
is defined to be a vector with value 1 at grid point
j
and 0 otherwise, which allows us to extract the jth component, namely
u_{j}={\boldsymbol{{e}}}_{j}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{u}}}
.

We say that matrix
{\boldsymbol{{D}}}
is an SBP approximation to the first derivative operator
\partial/\partial x
if it can be decomposed as
{\boldsymbol{{H}}}{\boldsymbol{{D}}}={\boldsymbol{{Q}}}
with
{\boldsymbol{{H}}}
being symmetric positive-definite and
{\boldsymbol{{Q}}}
satisfying
{\boldsymbol{{u}}}^{T}({\boldsymbol{{Q}}}+{\boldsymbol{{Q}}}^{T}){\boldsymbol{{v}}}=u_{N}v_{N}-u_{0}v_{0}
. Here,
{\boldsymbol{{D}}}
is the standard central finite difference operator in the interior which transitions to one-sided at boundaries and
{\boldsymbol{{H}}}
is a diagonal, positive-definite matrix allowing us to define a discrete inner-product and norm, namely

({\boldsymbol{{U}}},{\boldsymbol{{W}}})_{\boldsymbol{{H}}}={\boldsymbol{{U}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{H}}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{{W}}},\quad||{\boldsymbol{{U}}}||^{2}_{\boldsymbol{{H}}}=({\boldsymbol{{U}}},{\boldsymbol{{U}}})_{\boldsymbol{{H}}},

(34)

where
{\boldsymbol{{H}}}_{2}=({\boldsymbol{{I}}}_{2}\otimes{\boldsymbol{{H}}})
and
{\boldsymbol{{U}}}=[{\boldsymbol{{u}}}_{1}^{T}\quad{\boldsymbol{{u}}}_{2}^{T}]^{T},{\boldsymbol{{W}}}=[{\boldsymbol{{w}}}_{1}^{T}\quad{\boldsymbol{{w}}}_{2}^{T}]^{T}
.

5.2
Discretized Governing Equations

Equation (
2
) and boundary conditions (
3
) are discretized with the skew-symmetric SBP-SAT method
[
26
,
27
]
as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{W}}}_{t}+{\boldsymbol{{\Lambda}}}_{D}{\boldsymbol{{W}}}=

\displaystyle\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}}+\alpha_{0}{\boldsymbol{{H}}}_{2}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{{w}}}_{2,0}-R_{0}{\boldsymbol{{w}}}_{1,0}-h_{0}(t))(E_{2}\otimes e_{0})+

\displaystyle\quad\alpha_{L}{\boldsymbol{{H}}}_{2}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{{w}}}_{1,N}-R_{L}{\boldsymbol{{w}}}_{2,N}-h_{L}(t))(E_{1}\otimes e_{N})+\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{F}}}},

(35)

where

{\boldsymbol{{\Lambda}}}_{D}=0.5({\boldsymbol{{\Lambda}}}{\boldsymbol{{D}}}_{2}+{\boldsymbol{{D}}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{{\Lambda}}})-0.5{\boldsymbol{{M}}}_{\bar{c}},

(36)

for
{\boldsymbol{{\Lambda}}}=\text{diag}(-{\boldsymbol{{\bar{c}}}},{\boldsymbol{{\bar{c}}}})
and
{\boldsymbol{{D}}}_{2}=({\boldsymbol{{I}}}_{2}\otimes{\boldsymbol{{D}}})
. Here

{\boldsymbol{{M}}}_{\bar{c}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{{D}}}{\boldsymbol{{\bar{c}}}})&{\boldsymbol{{0}}}\\
{\boldsymbol{{0}}}&\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{{D}}}{\boldsymbol{{\bar{c}}}})\end{pmatrix},\quad\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}=\begin{pmatrix}\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{{a}}})&\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{{b}}})\\
\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{{c}}})&\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{{d}}})\end{pmatrix},

(37)

which reduces to

{\boldsymbol{{\Lambda}}}_{D}=(\Lambda\otimes{\boldsymbol{{D}}}),\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}=(\tilde{B}\otimes{\boldsymbol{{I}}})
for constant coefficients.

{\boldsymbol{{I}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{{I}}}_{2}
are the
N\times N
and
2\times 2
identity operators, respectively. Here
E_{j}
is a
2\times 1
vector of zeros with a 1 in the
j^{th}
position and
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{L}
are scalar penalty parameters. A discrete energy estimate determines values of the penalty parameters that yield a stable scheme, which may be obtained by taking
h_{0}=h_{L}=\tilde{F}=0
(not required but eases the computation). To aid the following analysis we rewrite (
35
) as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{W}}}_{t}+{\boldsymbol{{\Lambda}}}_{D}{\boldsymbol{{W}}}=

\displaystyle\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}}+{\boldsymbol{{P}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}},

(38)

where

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{{P}}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}{\boldsymbol{{H}}}_{2}^{-1}[(E_{2}E_{2}^{T}\otimes e_{0}e_{0}^{T})-R_{0}(E_{2}E_{1}^{T}\otimes e_{0}e_{0}^{T})]+

\displaystyle\alpha_{L}{\boldsymbol{{H}}}_{2}^{-1}[(E_{1}E_{1}^{T}\otimes e_{N}e_{N}^{T})-R_{L}(E_{1}E_{2}^{T}\otimes e_{N}e_{N}^{T})].

(39)

Multiplying (
38
) by
{\boldsymbol{{W}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{H}}}_{2}
and adding the transpose yields the estimate

\displaystyle\dot{{\boldsymbol{{E}}}_{h}}

\displaystyle=-\bar{c}_{0}w_{1,0}^{2}+\bar{c}_{N}w_{1,N}^{2}+\bar{c}_{0}w_{2,0}^{2}-\bar{c}_{N}w_{2,N}^{2}+0.5({\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{M}}}_{\bar{c}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+0.5({\boldsymbol{{M}}}_{\bar{c}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+

\displaystyle\quad({\boldsymbol{{W}}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+({\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{P}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+({\boldsymbol{{P}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{W}}})

\displaystyle=-\bar{c}_{0}w_{1,0}^{2}+\bar{c}_{N}w_{1,N}^{2}+\bar{c}_{0}w_{2,0}^{2}-\bar{c}_{N}w_{2,N}^{2}+0.5({\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{M}}}_{\bar{c}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+0.5({\boldsymbol{{M}}}_{\bar{c}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+

\displaystyle\quad({\boldsymbol{{W}}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+

\displaystyle\quad 2\alpha_{0}w_{2,0}(w_{2,0}-R_{0}w_{1,0})+2\alpha_{L}w_{1,N}(w_{1,N}-R_{L}w_{2,N}),

(40)

where
{\boldsymbol{{E}}}_{h}={\boldsymbol{{W}}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{{H}}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{{W}}}
. Taking
\alpha_{0}=-\bar{c}_{0},\quad\alpha_{L}=-\bar{c}_{N}
, estimate (
40
) reduces to

\displaystyle\dot{{\boldsymbol{{E}}}_{h}}=-

\displaystyle\bar{c}_{0}[w^{2}_{1,0}(1-R_{0}^{2})+(w_{2,0}-R_{0}w_{1,0})^{2}]+

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\bar{c}_{N}[w_{2,N}^{2}(1-R_{N}^{2})+(w_{1,N}-R_{L}w_{2,N})^{2}]+

\displaystyle 0.5({\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{M}}}_{\bar{c}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+0.5({\boldsymbol{{M}}}_{\bar{c}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+({\boldsymbol{{W}}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}})+(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}{\boldsymbol{{W}}},{\boldsymbol{{W}}})

\displaystyle\leq(||{\boldsymbol{{M}}}_{\bar{c}}||_{\boldsymbol{{H}}}+2||\tilde{{\boldsymbol{{B}}}}||_{\boldsymbol{{H}}}){\boldsymbol{{E}}}_{h},

(41)

and thus a stable discretization, where the rate of change of the discrete energy mimics that of continuous case as in (
5
).

We note that the discrete energy estimate (
41
), places bounds on the solution at any final time
T
, for the case of zero boundary and source data. This means that the numerical solution converges as the grid is refined but only on a compact domain in both space and time. Because we are interested in how a discretization approximates analytic growth/decay rates it is important to underscore that such stability estimates do not guarantee the temporal evolution of the discrete system matches that of the of the continuous (which is ensured by a stronger notion known as strict stability, see
[
12
]
), which we will discuss in more detail in
Section

7
.

Analytic Solutions and Numerical Verification

To verify convergence of our numerical methods, analytic solutions are desirable.
When
\tilde{B}
is a diagonal matrix and model data (e.g.
\tilde{F},h_{0}
etc.) are mostly zero, analytic solutions may be obtained via Laplace transform methods as in
[
21
]
without computations becoming too untractable. For more general cases of
\tilde{B}
and non-zero model data however, we may obtain analytic solutions using the method of manufactured solutions
[

]

To illustrate the construction of an analytic solution via Laplace methods, we consider case 3 with
b(x)=c(x)=0
,
h_{0}=h_{L}=F=0
, and initial data
f_{1}(x)=0,f_{2}(x)\neq 0
. The solution to (
2
)-(

) is thus

\hat{W}=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{x}M(\xi,s)d\xi\right)C(s)+G(x,s),

(42)

where

G(x,s)=[0,\quad G_{2}(x,s)]^{T},

(43)

for

G_{2}(x,s)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\frac{f_{2}(\xi)}{\bar{c}(\xi)}\exp\left[I_{\bar{c}/d}(x)-I_{\bar{c}/d}(\xi)-s(I_{\bar{c}}(x)-I_{\bar{c}}(\xi))\right]d\xi,

(44)

or in component form

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{1}(x,s)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-I_{\bar{c}/a}(x)+sI_{\bar{c}}(x)\right]C_{1}(s),

(45a)

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{1}(x,s)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[I_{\bar{c}/d}(x)-sI_{\bar{c}}(x)\right]C_{2}(s)+G_{2}(x,s).

(45b)

Using similar techniques as in
[
21
]
, we observe that

G_{2}(x,s)=\exp\left[I_{\bar{c}/d(x)}\right]\mathcal{L}[f_{2}(\xi)H(\xi)\exp\left[-I_{\bar{c}/d}(\xi)\right]],

(46)

where
H
is the Heaviside function and
\xi
is the characteristic variable defined implicitly by

t=I_{\bar{c}}(x)-I_{\bar{c}}(\xi).

(47)

We note that closed forms solutions therefore require that
I_{\bar{c}}(x)
be invertible, which allows us to solve for
\xi
, namely

\xi(x,t)=I_{\bar{c}}^{-1}(I_{\bar{c}}(x)-t),

(48)

as in
[
21
]
. Applying boundary conditions allows us to solve for the coefficients, namely, we have

\displaystyle C_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=R_{L}R(s)G_{2}(L,s),

(49)

\displaystyle C_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=R_{0}R_{L}R(s)G_{2}(L,s),

(50)

where

\displaystyle R(s)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\exp\left[-I_{\bar{c}/a}(L)+sI_{\bar{c}}(L)\right]-R_{0}R_{L}\exp\left[I_{\bar{c}/d}(L)-sI_{\bar{c}}(L)\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[I_{\bar{c}/a}(L)-sI_{\bar{c}}(L)\right]\cdot

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(R_{0}R_{L})^{n}\exp\left[n(I_{\bar{c}/d}(L)+I_{\bar{c}/a}(L)-2sI_{\bar{c}}(L))\right],

(52)

where the geometric series in (

\text{Re}(s)>\frac{\ln(R_{0}R_{L})+I_{\bar{c}/a}(L)+I_{\bar{c}/d}(L)}{2I_{\bar{c}}(L)}.

(53)

Thus we have solutions in Laplace space given by

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{1}(x,s)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}R_{L}(R_{0}R_{L})^{n}

\displaystyle\exp\left[y_{n}(x)-s\alpha_{n}(x)\right]\mathcal{L}[f_{2}(\xi)H(\xi)\exp\left[-I_{\bar{c}/d}(\xi)\right]],

(54a)

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{2}(x,s)=

\displaystyle G_{2}(x,s)+\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(R_{0}R_{L})^{n+1}\cdot

\displaystyle\exp\left[z_{n}(x)-s\beta_{n}(x)\right]\mathcal{L}[f_{2}(\xi)H(\xi)\exp\left[-I_{\bar{c}/d}(\xi)\right]],

(54b)

where

\displaystyle y_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=(-I_{\bar{c}/a}(x)+I_{\bar{c}/a}(L)+n[I_{\bar{c}/d}(L)+I_{\bar{c}/a}(L)]+I_{\bar{c}/d}(L),

(55)

\displaystyle z_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=(I_{\bar{c}/d}(x)+I_{\bar{c}/a}(L)+n[I_{\bar{c}/d}(L)+I_{\bar{c}/a}(L)]+I_{\bar{c}/d}(L),

(56)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=-I_{\bar{c}}(x)+(2n+1)I_{\bar{c}}(L),

(57)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=I_{\bar{c}}(x)+(2n+1)I_{\bar{c}}(L)

(58)

which reduces to

\displaystyle y_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=(-ax+aL+nL(a+d)+dL)/\bar{c},

(59)

\displaystyle z_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=(dx+aL+nL(a+d)+dL)/\bar{c},

(60)

(61)

for constant coefficients.

\displaystyle w_{1}(x,t)=

Applying the Laplace transform to discretization (

8
Conclusions

In this work we derived analytic spectra and solutions to systems of hyperbolic partial differential equations with variable coefficients that arise in perturbed problems, for example when linearizing a nonlinear system around a steady-state solution. We developed a provably stable semi-discretization based on high-order SBP-SAT finite difference schemes in order to explore numerical investigations of physical instabilities. While we did not obtain a strictly stable method of dissipative type (so that the growth rate of the discretization is bounded by that of the analytic) we did observe convergence of the numerical spectrum.

We conclude by stating that caution should be taken when using discrete methods to approximate analytic spectra. A method that is provably stable is desirable, and demonstration of numerical convergence will ensure that positive eigenvalues are physical and not a result of a numerical instability. When the analytic spectra and/or solutions cannot be computed in closed form, discrete approximations to the spectra should exhibit convergence with both mesh-refinement and with higher-order spatial accuracy in order to gain assurance that one is well-capturing the underlying physics.
