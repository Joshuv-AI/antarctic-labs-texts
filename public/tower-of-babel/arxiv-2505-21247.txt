Kernel Ridge Regression for conformer ensembles made easy with Structured Orthogonal Random Features
Konstantin Karandashev

Abstract
A computationally efficient protocol for machine learning in chemical space using Boltzmann ensembles of conformers as input is proposed; the method is based on rewriting Kernel Ridge Regression expressions in terms of Structured Orthogonal Random Features, yielding physics-motivated trigonometric neural networks. The resulting method is tested on experimental datasets of oxidation potentials in acetonitrile and hydration energies, using several popular molecular representations to demonstrate the method’s flexibility. Despite only using computationally cheap forcefield calculations for conformer generation, we observe systematic decrease of machine learning error with increased training set size in all cases, with experimental accuracy reached after training on hundreds of molecules and prediction errors being comparable to state-of-the-art machine learning approaches. We also present novel versions of Huber and LogCosh loss functions that made hyperparameter optimization of the new approach more convenient.

Introduction

Optimization in chemical compound space is an important part of chemical industry, with machine learning (ML) models emerging at the forefront of accelerating it
[
1
,
2
]
. This has led to creation of a large number of

,

]

21

[
22
]
. NN architectures sharing such characteristics also arise
[

,

,
25
,

2
Theory

2.1
Kernel Ridge Regression in space of molecular conformers

Given a training set of molecules
\mathrm{A}_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{train}}
), which we for now assume to be represented by atomic coordinates and nuclear charges, and the corresponding quantities of interest
q_{i}
, the KRR estimate of the quantity of interest for a query molecule
\mathrm{B}
is

q_{\mathrm{KRR}}(\mathrm{B})=\sum_{t=1}^{N_{\mathrm{train}}}\alpha_{i}^{\mathrm{KRR}}k(\mathrm{A}_{i},\mathrm{B}),

(1)

where
k
is the kernel function and
\vec{\alpha}^{\mathrm{KRR}}
are regression coefficients solving the equation

(\mathbf{K}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{N_{\mathrm{train}}})\vec{\alpha}^{\mathrm{KRR}}=\vec{q},

(2)

where
\mathbf{K}
is a matrix such that
K_{ij}=k(\mathrm{A}_{i},\mathrm{A}_{j})
,
\mathbf{I}_{N_{\mathrm{train}}}
is the
N_{\mathrm{train}}\times N_{\mathrm{train}}
unity matrix,
\lambda>0
is a hyperparameter introduced for regularization. Equations (
1
) and (
2
) can be viewed as linear interpolation of quantities
q_{i}
in a vector space of infinite dimensionality, with
k(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B})
being the dot product of vectors in that space where
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
are mapped
[
36
]
.

We will now outline three strategies for representing molecular conformers and defining the kernel function
k
in terms of those representations which were used in this work. The first strategy is to represent each molecule
\mathrm{A}
with a vector
\vec{X}^{(\mathrm{A})}
and then calculate the kernel function
k(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B})
using the
(global) Gaussian kernel function

k_{\mathrm{global}}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B}):=\exp\left(-\frac{|\vec{X}^{(\mathrm{A})}-\vec{X}^{(\mathrm{B})}|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

(3)

where
|\ldots|
is a vector’s
l^{2}
norm and
\sigma
is a hyperparameter. Examples of vector molecular representations considered in this work are Coulomb Matrix (CM)
[
37
,
38
]
and Spectrum
of London and Axilrod-Teller-Muto potential (SLATM)
[
4
]
.

The second strategy is to represent each atom in molecule
\mathrm{A}
with a vector, the resulting molecular representation being a matrix
\mathbf{X}^{(\mathrm{A})}
. We consider two popular choices for defining the corresponding kernel function, the first one being the
local Gaussian kernel function

k_{\mathrm{local}}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B}):=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{A})}}\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{B})}}k_{\mathrm{global}}(\vec{X}_{j^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})},\vec{X}_{j^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})}),

(4)

where
N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{A})}
and
N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{B})}
are number of atoms in molecules
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
,
n_{j^{\prime}}^{(A)}
and
n_{j^{\prime\prime}}^{(B)}
are nuclear charges of atoms
j^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime\prime}
of molecules
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
, while
\vec{X}_{j^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})}
and
\vec{X}_{j^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})}
are the corresponding atomic representation vectors.
k_{\mathrm{local}}
is particularly useful for ML of extensive molecular quantities (
e.g.
total potential energy); an example of a representation that can be used with
k_{\mathrm{local}}
is Smooth Overlap of Atomic Orbitals (SOAP) descriptors
[
39
,
3
]
. Another popular kernel expression is

k_{\mathrm{dlocal}}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B}):=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{A})}}\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{B})}}\delta(n_{j^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})}-n_{j^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})})k_{\mathrm{global}}(\vec{X}_{j^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})},\vec{X}_{j^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})}),

(5)

where
\delta
is the Kroneker delta function. Examples of representations used with
k_{\mathrm{dlocal}}
considered in this work are revised version of Faber-Christensen-Huang-Lilienfeld representation (FCHL19)
[
40
,
41
]
, atomic version of SLATM (aSLATM)
[
4
]
, and convolutional version of Many Body Distribution Functional (cMBDF)
[
42
,
43
]
.

The third kernel expression we will consider is specific to the FJK representation
[
44
]
(named so due to Fock
\mathbf{F}
, Coulomb
\mathbf{J}
, and exchange
\mathbf{K}
used to generate it). The representation of molecule
\mathrm{A}
takes results of cheap Hartree Fock calculations, among them properties of localized orbitals
\psi_{o}^{(\mathrm{A})}
(
o=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{orb}}^{(\mathrm{A})}
, where
N_{\mathrm{orb}}^{(\mathrm{A})}
is the number of occupied orbitals in molecule
\mathrm{A}
), then assigns a weight
w_{j}(\psi_{o}^{(\mathrm{A})})
and vector representation
\vec{v}_{j}(\psi_{o}^{(\mathrm{A})})
to each contribution to the localized orbital from atomic orbitals centered on an atom with index
j
(
j\in 1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{A})}
). The corresponding expression for kernel function between molecules
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
can be written as

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{FJK}}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{o^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{orb}}^{(\mathrm{A})}}\sum_{o^{\prime\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{orb}}^{(\mathrm{B})}}k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{Gauss}}(\psi_{o^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})},\psi_{o^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})}),

(6)

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{Gauss}}(\psi_{o^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})},\psi_{o^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})})

\displaystyle:=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{global}}^{2}}\left[1-\frac{k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{lin}}(\psi_{o^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})},\psi_{o^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})})}{\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{lin}}(\psi_{o^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})},\psi_{o^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})})k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{lin}}(\psi_{o^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})},\psi_{o^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})})}}\right]\right\},

(7)

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{lin}}(\psi_{o^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})},\psi_{o^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{A})}}\sum_{j^{\prime\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{B})}}w_{j^{\prime}}(\psi_{o^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})})w_{j^{\prime\prime}}(\psi_{o^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})})\exp\left[\frac{|\vec{v}(\psi_{o^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})})-\vec{v}(\psi_{o^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})})|^{2}}{2\vec{\sigma}\odot\vec{\sigma}}\right]

(8)

where
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
and
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{Gauss}}
are linear and Gaussian kernel functions for localized orbitals,
\sigma_{\mathrm{global}}
is a hyperparameter,
\vec{\sigma}
is a vector of hyperparameters of the same dimensionality as
\vec{v}_{j}(\psi_{o}^{(\mathrm{A})})
(the implied division by this vector is element-wise), and
\odot
stands for element-wise multiplication.
1
1

1

The definition of
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
(
8
) differs from the original one in Ref
[
44
]
by rescaling
\vec{\sigma}
by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
.
Note that the number of weights
w_{j^{\prime}}(\psi_{o^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})})
and
w_{j^{\prime\prime}}(\psi_{o^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})})
that are not exactly zero does not grow with system size, meaning the same can be said about the computational cost of evaluating
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
(
8
).
\vec{v}(\psi_{o}^{(\mathrm{A})})
consists of entries corresponding to momentum distribution,
\mathbf{F}
,
\mathbf{J}
, and
\mathbf{K}
matrices; in this work these four different types of entries were given a separate
\sigma
value each (which differs from the
\vec{\sigma}
used in Ref.
[
44
]
).

While
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}
was used for ML of extensive quantities, Ref.
[
44
]
additionally considered mapping properties associated with changes of energy due to changes of electronic structure (such as HOMO and LUMO energies) onto pairs of Slater determinants, each given a separate FJK representation. The kernel function for such Slater determinant pairs
\mathrm{A}_{1\rightarrow 2}
and
\mathrm{B}_{1\rightarrow 2}
reads

k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{pair}}(\mathrm{A}_{1\rightarrow 2},\mathrm{B}_{1\rightarrow 2})=k_{\mathrm{FJK}}(\mathrm{A}_{1},\mathrm{B}_{1})+k_{\mathrm{FJK}}(\mathrm{A}_{2},\mathrm{B}_{2})-k_{\mathrm{FJK}}(\mathrm{A}_{1},\mathrm{B}_{2})-k_{\mathrm{FJK}}(\mathrm{A}_{2},\mathrm{B}_{1}),

(9)

where
\mathrm{A}_{1}/\mathrm{A}_{2}
and
\mathrm{B}_{1}/\mathrm{B}_{2}
are the Slater determinants constituting
\mathrm{A}_{1\rightarrow 2}
and
\mathrm{B}_{1\rightarrow 2}
. The expression features cancellation between localized orbitals that are not affected by the change of electronic structure represented by the Slater determinant pair, which makes physical sense.

2.2
Rewriting kernels in terms of Structured Orthogonal Random Features

SORF is based on Random Fourier Features (RFF) formalism
[
16
]
, which approximates the infinite dimensional vectors on which molecules are mapped in KRR with finite dimensional
feature vectors

\vec{z}
of dimensionality
N_{\mathrm{f}}
, resulting in the kernel function

k_{\mathrm{SORF}}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B})=\vec{z}(\mathrm{A})\cdot\vec{z}(\mathrm{B}),

(10)

where “
\cdot
” is dot product. Rewriting Equations (
1
) and (
2
) in terms of
k_{\mathrm{SORF}}
results in the following expression for the SORF estimate of a quantity

q_{\mathrm{SORF}}(\mathrm{B})=\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{z}(\mathrm{B})

(11)

with the regression coefficients
\vec{\alpha}
found by solving the equation

\left(\mathbf{Z}^{\mathbf{T}}\mathbf{Z}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{N_{\mathrm{f}}}\right)\vec{\alpha}=\mathbf{Z}^{\mathbf{T}}\vec{q},

(12)

where
\mathbf{Z}
is a matrix such that
\vec{Z}_{i}=\vec{z}(\mathrm{A}_{i})
. Though the expression implies finding
\vec{\alpha}
by solving a
N_{\mathrm{f}}\times N_{\mathrm{f}}
system of linear equations, in our work we avoided that by rewriting Equation (
12
) in terms of Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
[
45
]
of the
\mathbf{Z}
matrix, making the cost of solving (
12
)
\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{train}}^{2}N_{\mathrm{f}})
if
N_{\mathrm{train}}<N_{\mathrm{f}}
(which was always the case in this work). There are two significant advantages of Equations (
11
) and (
12
) over their KRR versions (
1
) and (
2
). The first one is that for larger datasets it is possible to choose
N_{\mathrm{f}}
smaller than
N_{\mathrm{train}}
, decreasing the computational time cost and memory requirements of finding
\vec{\alpha}
. The second one is most relevant for kernel functions whose evaluation scales bilinearly w.r.t.
N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{A})}
and
N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{B})}
, such as
k_{\mathrm{local}}
(
4
),
k_{\mathrm{dlocal}}
(
5
), and
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}
(
6
); as will be demonstrated later evaluating the corresponding feature vectors
\vec{z}
is linear w.r.t. the size of molecule of interest, eliminating computational cost scaling w.r.t. training set system size for calculating ML predictions and improving said scaling for finding the regression coefficients.

If the system of interest is represented by a single vector of size
N_{\mathrm{init}}
(which is a power of
2
) and the global Gaussian kernel (
3
) is used, the vectors
\vec{z}
are generated with the standard SORF expression

\vec{\mathcal{F}}(\vec{X}^{(\mathrm{A})},\mathrm{D},\upphi,\sigma):=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N_{\mathrm{f}}}}\left\{\cos\left[\frac{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{init}}}}{\sigma}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{N_{\mathrm{tr}}}\mathbf{H}_{N_{\mathrm{init}}}\mathbf{D}_{st}\right)\vec{X}^{(\mathrm{A})}+\vec{\phi}_{s}\right]\right\}_{s=1}^{N_{\mathrm{stack}}}.

(13)

Here
\mathbf{H}_{N_{\mathrm{init}}}
is
N_{\mathrm{init}}\times N_{\mathrm{init}}
Hadamard matrix
[
22
]
,
\mathrm{D}
is an
N_{\mathrm{stack}}\times N_{\mathrm{tr}}
array of
N_{\mathrm{init}}\times N_{\mathrm{init}}
diagonal matrices
\mathbf{D}_{st}
(
s=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{stack}}
,
t=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{tr}}
), each diagonal element randomly chosen between
1
and
-1
,
\upphi
is an
N_{\mathrm{stack}}
array of
N_{\mathrm{init}}
long vectors
\vec{\phi}_{s}
, each value randomly chosed from the interval
[0,2\pi)
,
\{\ldots\}_{s=1}^{N_{\mathrm{stack}}}
stands for concatenating the
N_{\mathrm{stack}}
vectors into a single vector; the implied calculation of the cosine function is element-wise. Products of the Hadamard matrix with a vector are evaluated with fast Walsh-Hadamard transform (hence the name of the
N_{\mathrm{tr}}
parameter) whose computational cost is
\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{init}}\ln N_{\mathrm{init}})
. Note that
N_{\mathrm{f}}=N_{\mathrm{init}}N_{\mathrm{stack}}
. It can be demonstrated
[
17
]
that

\vec{\mathcal{F}}(\vec{X}^{(\mathrm{A})},\mathrm{D},\upphi,\sigma)\cdot\vec{\mathcal{F}}(\vec{X}^{(\mathrm{B})},\mathrm{D},\upphi,\sigma)\approx k_{\mathrm{global}}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B}).

(14)

We note at this point that all the applications discussed in this work in the context of SORF can be analogously performed with other procedures
[
19
,
20
]
for generating feature vectors that approximate
k_{\mathrm{global}}
analogously to Equation (
14
). However, in this work we used SORF due to its balance of good numerical performance and algorithmic simplicity.

Using SORF can be considered
[
26
]
using a single-layer NN (the corresponding layer will be called
\mathcal{F}
from now on), and we will now add layers to it to accommodate more complex kernel expressions. We first introduce the
sum layer

\mathcal{S}
, its combination with
\mathcal{F}
, which we will refer to as
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}
, is defined as

\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{X}^{(\mathrm{A})},\mathrm{D},\upphi,\sigma):=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{a}}}\mathcal{F}(\vec{X}_{j}^{(\mathrm{A})},\mathrm{D},\upphi,\sigma).

(15)

It is evident that dot product of
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}
features reproduces
k_{\mathrm{local}}
(
4
). To reproduce
k_{\mathrm{dlocal}}
(
5
) we additionally introduce the
switch layer

\mathcal{T}
, applicable if a vector
\vec{X}
corresponding to an atom is augmented with the atom’s nuclear charge
n
. Writing
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{F}
means a separate
\mathcal{F}
layer is initialized for each possible nuclear charge, with
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{F}
written as

\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{X}^{(\mathrm{A})},\sigma):=\sum_{j}^{N_{\mathrm{a}}}\mathcal{F}[\vec{X}_{j}^{(\mathrm{A})},\mathrm{D}(n_{j}^{(\mathrm{A})}),\upphi(n_{j}^{(\mathrm{A})}),\sigma],

(16)

where
\mathrm{D}(n_{j}^{(\mathrm{A})})
and
\upphi(n_{j}^{(\mathrm{A})})
are tensors analogous to
\mathrm{D}
and
\upphi
in Equation (
13
) that have been initialized specifically for atoms with nuclear charge
n_{j}^{(\mathrm{A})}
.
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{F}
features reproduce
k_{\mathrm{dlocal}}
(
5
) as their dot product and were previously used in Ref.
[
21
]
.

To reproduce the nested exponential expressions appearing in
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}
(
6
)-(
8
) we use
\mathcal{F}
(
13
) to generate vectors whose dot product reproduces
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
(
8
) and note that applying
k_{\mathrm{global}}
(
3
) to a pair of such vectors (after normalization) approximates
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{Gauss}}
(
7
). We introduce weighted sum layer
\mathcal{W}
that allows us to write features corresponding to
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
(
8
) as

\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\psi^{\mathrm{A}}_{o}):=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{A})}}w_{j}(\psi_{o}^{(\mathrm{A})})\mathcal{F}\left[\vec{v}_{j}(\psi_{o}^{(\mathrm{A})})/\vec{\sigma},\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{orb}},\upphi^{\mathrm{orb}},1,\right].

(17)

Additionally defining normalization layer
\mathcal{N}
that divides a vector by its
l^{2}
norm allows us to reproduce
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}
(
6
) with features generated by
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}
, formally written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{A}):=

\displaystyle\sum_{o=1}^{N_{\mathrm{orb}}^{(\mathrm{A})}}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\psi_{o}^{(\mathrm{A})})

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\psi_{o}^{(\mathrm{A})}):=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left[\frac{\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\psi^{\mathrm{A}}_{o})}{\left|\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\psi^{\mathrm{A}}_{o})\right|},\mathrm{D}^{\mathrm{mol}},\upphi^{\mathrm{mol}},\sigma_{\mathrm{global}}\right]

(19)

\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}
features allow to reproduce
k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{pair}}
(
9
) as

k_{\mathrm{FJK}}^{\mathrm{pair}}(\mathrm{A}_{1\rightarrow 2},\mathrm{B}_{1\rightarrow 2})\approx[\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{A}_{2})-\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{A}_{1})][\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{B}_{2})-\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{B}_{1})].

(20)

We calculated
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{A}_{2})-\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}(\mathrm{A}_{1})
by assigning representations of localized orbitals in
\mathrm{A}_{1}
and
\mathrm{A}_{2}
weights of
-1
and
1
and applying
\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}
to them. Note that contributions to the feature vectors from localized orbitals that do not change between
\mathrm{A}_{1}
and
\mathrm{A}_{2}
cancel out, which makes physical sense. To simplify discussion, we will assume that for the single determinant case
\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{N}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}
is used as well, with each localized orbital given a weight of
1
(which is also the way FJK representations were processed in our code implementation).

2.3
Applications to ensembles of conformers

Consider doing ML of an extensive quantity of a molecule
\mathrm{A}
about which we know conformers with indices
c=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{conf}}^{(\mathrm{A})}
, each assigned a matrix of atomic representations
\mathbf{X}_{c}^{(\mathrm{A)}}
and a nonnegative Boltzmann weight
w_{c}^{\mathrm{b}(\mathrm{A})}
. A natural way to extend
k_{\mathrm{local}}
(
4
) to ensembles would be with the kernel function

k_{\mathrm{local}}^{\mathrm{ens}}=\sum_{c^{\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{A}}}\sum_{c^{\prime\prime}=1}^{N_{\mathrm{conf}}^{\mathrm{B}}}w_{c^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{b}(\mathrm{A})}w_{c^{\prime\prime}}^{\mathrm{b}(\mathrm{B})}k_{\mathrm{local}}(\mathbf{X}_{c^{\prime}}^{(\mathrm{A})},\mathbf{X}_{c^{\prime\prime}}^{(\mathrm{B})}).

(21)

The cost of evaluating this kernel function is
\mathcal{O}(N_{\mathrm{conf}}^{(\mathrm{A})}N_{\mathrm{conf}}^{(\mathrm{B})}N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{A})}N_{\mathrm{a}}^{(\mathrm{B})})
, making the motivation to instead use SORF even stronger than for
k_{\mathrm{local}}
; in terms of Subsection
2.2
, the combination of layers corresponding to
k_{\mathrm{local}}^{\mathrm{ens}}
is
\mathcal{W}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}
. Using
\mathcal{W}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}
though leaves the ML algorithm unable to correct for
\vec{w}^{\mathrm{b}}
potentially inaccurately representing relative importance of different conformers. This motivated introduction of an alternative expression,
\mathcal{E}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{F}
, where the
mixed-extensive
layer
\mathcal{E}
is defined as

\vec{\mathcal{E}}(\vec{X},\mathrm{D},\upphi,\sigma,\sigma_{\mathrm{mix}}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{stack}}(1+\sigma_{\mathrm{mix}}^{-1})}}\left\{\vec{X}\right\}_{s=1}^{N_{\mathrm{stack}}}+\frac{|\vec{X}|}{\sqrt{1+\sigma_{\mathrm{mix}}}}\vec{F}\left(\frac{\vec{X}}{|\vec{X}|},\mathrm{D},\vec{\phi},\sigma\right),

(22)

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{mix}}
is a nonnegative hyperparameter.
\sigma_{\mathrm{mix}}
controls switching between
k_{\mathrm{local}}^{\mathrm{ens}}
(
21
) and a different kernel that is related to
k_{\mathrm{local}}^{\mathrm{ens}}

3
Numerical details

For each learning curve presented in this work we divided a dataset into a training set (approximately 80% of the total number of points, making it 474 for LES and 514 for FreeSolv) and a test set. For each training size we picked a random subset of the training set, used it to optimize hyperparameters as described in
A
, trained the model using these hyperparameters, and then calculated Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of model’s predictions for the test set molecules. For each dataset and method the procedure was repeated 4 times, providing both the average MAEs and their dispersions presented in Section
4
.

By default we ran calculations with
N_{\mathrm{f}}=32768
at all
\mathcal{F}
layers, since the value had performed well in Ref.
[
21
]
, and chose
N_{\mathrm{init}}
as the smallest power of two integer greater or equal to representation vector size, padding the “extra” values of the vector with zeros. This could not be done for SLATM, aSLATM, and (in the case of LES) SOAP since their representation vectors corresponded to
N_{\mathrm{init}}
larger than
32768
. For SLATM, the issue was resolved by projecting all representation vectors onto linear space spawned by representation vectors of conformers in the training set, and then appending to the resulting vectors the
l^{2}
distance between the projected and non-projected representation vector; note that using thus defined new representation vectors would not affect training and predictions of a KRR model. The procedure decreased
N_{\mathrm{init}}
to
4096
for SLATM, but could not be used for aSLATM and SOAP due to large number of atomic environments in the training set. Ref.
[
21
]
used Principal Component Analysis to fit atomic representations into a small enough
N_{\mathrm{init}}
, but we wanted to avoid that in order to accurately observe prediction errors of aSLATM and SOAP relative to other methods. Therefore, for aSLATM we used
N_{\mathrm{init}}=131072
, for SOAP we used
N_{\mathrm{init}}=65536
for LES and
N_{\mathrm{init}}=32768
for FreeSolv.

On a closing note, we evaluate how much representing a molecule with a conformer ensemble rather than a single conformer actually affected ML results presented in this work, using as examples combinations of MSORF representations that had demonstrated best and worst performance, namely aSLATM, FCHL19, and CM. 5
Conclusions and outlook

We have devised a general SORF-based procedure for generating physically motivated molecular representations from Boltzmann ensembes of conformers and demonstrated its utility for predicting experimental values for molecular properties. The method both demonstrates systematic decrease of prediction error with increased training set size and allows to reproduce experimental accuracy with hundreds of molecules as training set. At the same time, we did not observe a significant difference between representing a molecule with an ensemble of conformers or a single conformer, implying that, at least for the quantities considered in this work, how conformers are represented is a more important question than how many of them are used.

Prediction errors observed in this work were comparable to state-of-the-art achieved with other ML approaches, despite generating the underlying conformer representations using parameters that (with the exception of SOAP) had not been optimized for the quantities of interest. One possible way to improve on this is to generate conformer representations with another NN, train the combined model on computational data, and then re-optimize the MSORF part of the model on experimental data, essentially finetuning the lowest layers of the combined model and bridging MSORF’s gap with transfer learning techniques, that have shown promise for FreeSolv in Refs.
[
83
,
77
,
84
,
79
]
. Another possible direction could be going beyond the simple expressions for weighting contributions of different conformers, atoms, or localized orbitals used in this work to more complicated expressions gleaned from explainable artificial intelligence applied to
ab initio
calculations
[
85
]
. In both cases, it would be possible to capitalize on additional
ab initio
data while keeping MSORF grounded in KRR formalism. Lastly, while we focused on combining representations of conformers into representations of molecular ensembles, MSORF could analogously combine representations of molecular ensembles into representations of mixtures of molecules. This could make MSORF models useful for exploring chemical spaces of mixtures, a problem relevant for several industrial applications that involve using organic liquids
[
86
,
87
,
88
]
.
